Effects of weather factors and maternal characteristics on preterm birth: a six-year retrospective study
Authors: Büsra Korkmaz, Fatma Ocakoglu, Ayse Aydın
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreterm birth represents a leading cause of neonatal morbidity and fatality globally. Recent research has increasingly focused on the potential influence of meteorological and ecological variables on adverse pregnancy outcomes, particularly in the context of environmental change. Despite this attention, the evidence is still inconsistent, and there is a paucity of data from humid subtropical regions. The present study investigates the association between weather variables and preterm birth in a humid subtropical region of Türkiye.MethodsA retrospective observational study was carried out using hospital birth records from January 2018 to December 2023 at a tertiary referral center in Rize, Türkiye. Preterm birth was defined as delivery before 37 completed weeks of gestation. Meteorological variables, including monthly mean air temperature, relative humidity, precipitation, wind speed, surface atmospheric pressure, and solar radiation, were obtained from the NASA POWER database and matched to birth records by month and year of delivery. Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with preterm birth.ResultsThe study included 1,767 deliveries, of which 681 (38.5%) were preterm births. Preterm birth occurred more frequently among women aged 35 years or older, male infants, and nulliparities. Univariate analyses indicated that relative humidity was significantly higher in preterm births than in term births (p = 0.004), whereas other meteorological variables did not show significant differences.In multivariable analysis, maternal age ≥ 35 years was associated with a higher likelihood of preterm birth compared with age 25–34 years, and nulliparity was also associated with an increased likelihood of preterm birth. Among weather factors, higher relative humidity was associated with an increased likelihood of preterm birth, whereas higher surface atmospheric pressure, wind speed, and solar radiation were associated with a decreased likelihood. Temperature and precipitation were not significantly associated with preterm birth. The regression model was statistically significant (p < 0.001) and explained approximately 9% of the variance in preterm birth.ConclusionsCertain weather factors, particularly relative humidity, surface atmospheric pressure, and wind speed, may be associated with preterm birth in humid subtropical regions. These findings suggest that certain meteorological factors may be associated with preterm birth; however, these associations should be interpreted with caution and do not imply causal relationships.

Background

Preterm birth is one of the major obstetric problems that currently exerts significant adverse effects on both individual and public health [1–3]. As a leading cause of neonatal mortality and morbidity worldwide, preterm birth results in persistent health consequences not only during the perinatal period but also throughout later stages of life [1]. Individuals born preterm are at increased risk for a wide range of chronic conditions, including cardiovascular and pulmonary diseases, as well as nephrological and metabolic disorders. The magnitude of this risk increases proportionally with the degree of prematurity [4, 5].

In addition, preterm birth has been associated with impairments in attention, motor and language skills, and elevated mental functions such as planning, problem-solving, and mental flexibility, leading to poorer academic achievement [5–7]. Consequently, preterm birth is a substantial burden not only in the acute period but also in the long term, influencing individuals, families, and medical systems alike [4, 8].

The etiology of preterm birth is multifactorial, and maternal sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics play a significant role [9–11]. Factors such as advanced maternal age, nulliparity, a history of previous preterm birth, obstetric complications, folic acid deficiency, medication use, and tobacco use have all been associated with an increased risk of preterm birth [9].

However, individual and obstetric characteristics alone are not always sufficient to fully explain the risk of preterm birth, as different pregnancy outcomes may be observed among women with similar maternal profiles [12]. This observation suggests that, in addition to maternal factors, environmental and contextual determinants should also be considered when assessing the risk of preterm birth [13].

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward the effects of environmental and weather factors on pregnancy outcomes [14]. In particular, meteorological conditions such as ambient temperature, humidity, seasonal variability, and extreme weather events have been reported to be associated with pregnancy outcomes, including the timing of birth [15]. Several studies have suggested that rising environmental temperatures and heatwaves may be associated with a higher likelihood of preterm birth [16].

However, the magnitude and direction of this association vary throughout regions and climatic conditions, and existing evidence yields heterogeneous findings. Therefore, it has been emphasized that the relationship between preterm birth and meteorological factors should be evaluated while accounting for regional characteristics and contextual conditions [15].

The effects of weather variables on preterm birth have been explored mainly through pregnancy-specific bodily changes [17]. During pregnancy, increases in metabolic rate, body weight, and adipose tissue are accompanied by a reduction in thermoregulatory capacity, rendering pregnant women more susceptible to environmental heat stress [18, 19]. Exposure to high ambient temperatures has been proposed to be associated with maternal dehydration, which may be related to increased blood viscosity and reduced uteroplacental blood flow [20].

In addition, heat stress may enhance activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, resulting in elevated levels of corticotropin-releasing hormone and cortisol. These endocrine alterations have been reported to be associated with uterine contractions and a higher likelihood of preterm birth [20]. Nevertheless, these biological mechanisms have not yet been fully elucidated, and the available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous.

Although the number of studies examining the association between weather variables and preterm birth has increased in recent years, the consistency of findings in the existing literature remains limited, and results vary according to geographical region, climatic characteristics, and research methods [18, 21]. In particular, studies that incorporate long-term data, jointly evaluate multiple meteorological variables, and simultaneously account for maternal characteristics are relatively scarce. Moreover, the limited number of studies conducted across regions with varied climatic conditions, such as Türkiye, limits the generalizability of current evidence.

In this context, there is a clear need for studies that assess the relationships among meteorological conditions, maternal characteristics, and preterm birth at the regional level and over prolonged time periods. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between weather parameters, maternal characteristics, and preterm birth using six years of retrospective data in Rize, located in the Eastern Black Sea region of Türkiye, under humid Black Sea climatic conditions.

The objective of this study was to examine the relationships among weather variables, maternal characteristics, and preterm birth using six years of retrospective data.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study analyzed hospital birth records to investigate the association between weather variables and preterm birth. The study included deliveries that occurred between January 2018 and December 2023.

Setting

The study was conducted in Rize, a province located in the Eastern Black Sea region of Türkiye. The region has a humid subtropical climate defined by high annual precipitation, mild winters, and warm, humid summers. Due to the strong maritime influence and mountainous terrain, periodic temperature variations are relatively moderate, while humidity remains high throughout the year. These climatic characteristics make the region suitable for investigating the relationship between meteorological conditions and adverse birth outcomes. Data were obtained from Recep Tayyip Erdoğan University Rize Training and Research Hospital, a tertiary referral center that provides obstetric care to the surrounding population. This center serves as a regional referral unit for high-risk pregnancies and receives complicated obstetric cases from surrounding healthcare facilities.

Participants

The study population consisted of women who delivered at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology of Recep Tayyip Erdoğan University Rize Training and Research Hospital between January 2018 and December 2023. All eligible deliveries recorded in the hospital database during the study period were considered for inclusion. Women with complete clinical and obstetric data were included in the analysis. Pregnancies ending before 20 completed weeks of gestation were classified as miscarriages and excluded. Records with missing key variables required for the analysis were also excluded.

Data sources

Clinical and obstetric data were obtained retrospectively from the electronic medical records of Recep Tayyip Erdoğan University Rize Training and Research Hospital. These records included maternal age, obstetric history, parity, gestational age at birth, and infant sex. Meteorological data for the study period (January 2018–December 2023) were retrieved from the NASA POWER (Prediction Of Worldwide Energy Resources) database as monthly averages. The environmental variables included mean air temperature (°C), average relative humidity (%), total precipitation (mm/month), mean wind speed (m/s), surface atmospheric pressure (kPa), and all-sky surface photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) (kW·h/m²/month). Meteorological measurements were matched to individual birth records based on the month and year of delivery.

Exposure assessment

Exposure to weather parameters was assessed using monthly environmental data obtained from the NASA POWER database for the entire study period. For each birth record, meteorological exposure values were assigned based on the corresponding month and year of delivery, and each participant was linked to the relevant monthly environmental conditions. All meteorological variables were treated as continuous variables in the statistical analyses. Monthly aggregation was used to better represent sustained environmental exposure and to reduce short-term variability associated with single-day measurements. However, this approach does not allow the identification of biologically relevant exposure windows during pregnancy, such as trimester-specific effects or short-term triggers immediately preceding labor. Therefore, the exposure definition reflects general environmental conditions rather than precise individual-level exposure and may not correspond to the timing of physiological processes leading to preterm birth.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome of the study was preterm birth, defined as delivery before 37 completed weeks of gestation (< 37 weeks) according to World Health Organization criteria. Gestational age at birth was obtained from hospital medical records based on obstetric assessment documented in the clinical files. Births occurring at or after 37 completed weeks of gestation were classified as term births. For statistical analyses, the outcome variable was treated as binary, coded 1 for preterm birth and 0 for term birth.

Covariates

Maternal and neonatal characteristics that could influence the association between weather factors and preterm birth were included as covariates. Maternal age was categorized into three groups: <25 years, 25–34 years, and ≥ 35 years. Parity was categorized as nulliparity, second pregnancy, and three or more pregnancies. Infant sex was categorized as female or male. To avoid conceptual overlap with continuous meteorological variables, the temperature-extreme period variable (cold vs. warm months) was not included in the final regression model. All covariates were included in the regression analyses to control for potential confounding effects.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study sample and the meteorological variables, with continuous variables presented as means and standard deviations and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using skewness and kurtosis values. Group comparisons between preterm and term births were performed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables. Effect sizes were expressed using Cohen’s d, and standardized residuals were examined in chi-square analyses to identify cells contributing to statistically significant differences.

Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with preterm birth. The dependent variable was birth outcome (0 = term birth, 1 = preterm birth). Independent variables included meteorological variables (mean temperature, relative humidity, precipitation, solar radiation, wind speed, and surface atmospheric pressure) and maternal/neonatal characteristics (maternal age, parity, and infant sex). Mode of delivery and the temperature-extreme period variable (cold vs. warm months) were not included in the final model due to concerns regarding reverse causality and conceptual overlap with continuous meteorological variables. Model significance was evaluated using the Omnibus Test of Model Coefficients, and model fit was assessed using Nagelkerke R². Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. In addition, the regression model structure was determined based on existing literature, biological plausibility, and conceptual relevance of the included variables. Variables considered likely to introduce conceptual overlap or reverse causality were excluded from the final model to improve interpretability and reduce redundancy.

Ethics approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Atatürk University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: B.30.2.ATA.0.01.00/292, Date: 03 May 2024).

In addition, institutional permission was granted by the Rize Provincial Directorate of Health and Recep Tayyip Erdoğan University Rize Training and Research Hospital (Permission No: E-64960800-799-247818694, Date: 04 July 2024).

Due to the study’s retrospective nature and the use of anonymized, routinely collected data, informed consent was waived.

Results

The study sample consisted of 1,767 births, including 681 (38.5%) preterm and 1,086 (61.5%) term deliveries. Statistical analysis of maternal age revealed significant differences between the two groups (x2 = 23.879, p = 0.001), with mothers aged ≥ 35 years being more frequently represented in the preterm cohort (57.6%) compared to the term cohort (46.9%). Significant associations were also identified regarding infant sex and parity. Male infants were more prevalent in the preterm group than in the term group (53.3% vs. 48.3%, x2 = 4.121, p = 0.042). Furthermore, adhering to the standardized terminology for nulliparity as requested, a higher proportion of preterm births occurred among nulliparous women compared to term births (46.5% vs. 40.7%, x2 = 6.518, p = 0.038; Table 1).

Regarding meteorological parameters, variables recorded during the delivery month were compared across the cohorts. No statistically significant differences were observed for mean surface atmospheric pressure (100.33 ± 0.41 kPa vs. 100.37 ± 0.43 kPa, p = 0.067), monthly precipitation (3.21 ± 1.62 mm vs. 3.17 ± 1.49 mm, p = 0.613), or mean temperature (12.56 ± 9.18 °C vs. 12.20 ± 9.38 °C, p = 0.432). Mean relative humidity was identified as the only environmental factor demonstrating a statistically significant difference between groups (t = 2.896, p = 0.004), with higher average levels recorded in the preterm group (80.20 ± 2.61%) compared to the term group (79.85 ± 2.38%). Solar radiation levels remained equivalent between the two cohorts (1.72 ± 0.80 vs. 1.73 ± 0.80 kW-hr/m^2/month, p = 0.873; Table 1).

A multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with preterm birth. The overall model was statistically significant (Omnibus Test of Model Coefficients, χ² = 119.139, p < 0.001) and explained approximately 8.9% of the variance in preterm birth (Nagelkerke R² = 0.089) (Table 2).

Maternal age was significantly associated with preterm birth (p = 0.001). Women aged ≥ 35 years had a higher likelihood of preterm birth compared with those aged 25–34 years (OR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.17–1.80), whereas the < 25 age group did not show a statistically significant difference.

Parity was also significantly associated with preterm birth (p = 0.033). Nulliparous women had a higher likelihood of preterm birth compared with those with three or more pregnancies (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.02–1.65), while second pregnancies were not significantly associated.

Among meteorological variables, surface atmospheric pressure (OR = 0.045, p < 0.001), mean wind speed (OR = 0.156, p < 0.001), and solar radiation (OR = 0.278, p < 0.001) were associated with a decreased likelihood of preterm birth. In contrast, higher relative humidity was associated with an increased likelihood of preterm birth (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.03–1.17, p = 0.002).

Infant sex, precipitation, and mean temperature were not significantly associated with preterm birth in the multivariable model (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Overall, advanced maternal age, nulliparity, and increased relative humidity were associated with a higher likelihood of preterm birth, whereas higher surface atmospheric pressure, wind speed, and solar radiation were associated with a decreased likelihood of preterm birth. To provide a clearer interpretation of the magnitude and direction of these associations, the key meteorological findings from the multivariable model are summarized as follows: mean relative humidity showed a significant positive association with preterm birth (OR: 1.104, 95% CI: 1.035–1.176, p = 0.002), whereas surface atmospheric pressure (OR: 0.045, 95% CI: 0.018–0.117, p = 0.001), mean wind speed (OR: 0.156, 95% CI: 0.088–0.276, p = 0.001), and solar radiation (OR: 0.278, 95% CI: 0.146–0.530, p = 0.001) were inversely associated with preterm birth. These findings are consistent with the main results presented above.

Discussion

The present study assessed the effects of weather factors and maternal characteristics on preterm birth over six years. The findings suggest that advanced maternal age, nulliparity, and certain meteorological conditions—particularly higher relative humidity, lower surface atmospheric pressure, and lower wind speed—may be associated with preterm birth. The relatively modest explanatory power of the regression model (Nagelkerke R² = 0.089) underscores the multifactorial etiology of preterm birth and the need to consider both individual obstetric risk factors and environmental stressors. Importantly, the absence of several well-established obstetric and lifestyle confounders—such as prior preterm birth, maternal comorbidities, body mass index, smoking status, and hypertensive disorders—introduces a substantial risk of residual confounding. These unmeasured factors may have influenced the observed associations, potentially leading to biased or overestimated effect sizes. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and cannot be considered as evidence of independent effects.

Maternal characteristics and preterm birth

In the present study, a substantial proportion of women who experienced preterm birth were aged 35 years or older, and advanced maternal age was independently associated with preterm birth in the logistic regression analysis. This finding is consistent with previous literature. Large-scale cohort studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated a significantly increased risk of preterm birth among pregnancies in women aged 35 and older [22–24]. Data from Türkiye similarly indicate a higher prevalence of preterm birth and perinatal complications among women of advanced maternal age [25, 26]. This association may be partially explained by age-related increases in maternal comorbidities, impaired placental vascular function, heightened oxidative stress, and the cumulative effects of long-term environmental exposures [27–29]. Furthermore, reduced physiological reserve in older pregnant women may increase susceptibility to environmental stressors, including humidity and atmospheric variability, which may contribute to the risk of preterm birth.

The finding that nulliparity may be associated with an increased risk of preterm birth is also consistent with previous studies [30, 31]. Large cohort studies have reported that women experiencing their first pregnancy have a higher risk of preterm birth compared with multiparous women [32, 33]. This elevated risk has been attributed to biological mechanisms such as incomplete cervical and uterine adaptation during the first pregnancy, a higher incidence of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, and differences in the vaginal microbiota [34–36]. These biological vulnerabilities may interact with climatic conditions characterized by increased humidity and meteorological variability, thereby potentially increasing the risk of preterm birth among nulliparous women.

Weather factors and preterm birth

In this study, multivariable analyses showed that several meteorological parameters, particularly lower surface atmospheric pressure, higher relative humidity, and lower wind speed, were associated with preterm birth. This finding suggests that preterm birth may be associated with multiple meteorological factors rather than a single environmental indicator. Taken together, these findings may suggest that variations in environmental conditions may be associated with differences in gestational duration. These associations may be related to potential physiological mechanisms. Increased relative humidity may impair maternal thermoregulation and contribute to dehydration and cardiovascular strain, while low atmospheric pressure may influence uterine activity through biomechanical and hormonal pathways. Reduced wind speed may exacerbate heat and humidity accumulation, further increasing environmental stress on pregnant individuals [18–20]. However, it should be noted that the inability to distinguish between spontaneous and medically indicated preterm births may have influenced these associations, as medically indicated cases are often driven by clinical decision-making rather than environmental exposures, potentially introducing heterogeneity into the observed relationships.

The findings of this study are consistent with the existing literature on the impact of meteorological exposures during pregnancy on perinatal outcomes. While our findings support this body of evidence, they also suggest that risk cannot be attributed solely to temperature; rather, meteorological factors may act in combination, contributing to environmental stress and increased physiological burden. These mechanisms may represent possible pathways linking environmental conditions and pregnancy outcomes.

Conversely, some studies have reported a potential association between low ambient temperatures and preterm birth, which may be related to mechanisms such as vasoconstriction and elevated blood pressure [37, 38]. Such heterogeneous findings in the literature may reflect differences in climate types, population-level adaptation, exposure metrics, and lag structures used across studies.

In contrast to some previous studies, temperature was not significantly associated with preterm birth in our model. This finding may be related to the use of monthly averaged data, which may smooth short-term temperature extremes, as well as regional climatic characteristics such as high humidity and relatively moderate temperature variability in the study area [15, 18, 21]. Given that preterm birth is often triggered by acute physiological and inflammatory processes occurring over short time intervals, the use of monthly aggregated meteorological data may introduce a temporal mismatch between exposure and outcome. As a result, the observed associations may reflect broader environmental patterns rather than direct or immediate effects, and should therefore be interpreted with caution in terms of biological plausibility.

In the present study, the association between increased relative humidity and a higher likelihood of preterm birth further supports the concept of combined environmental stress related to humidity and atmospheric conditions [18]. A large cohort study conducted in China demonstrated that exposure to elevated relative humidity, particularly in the period close to delivery, was associated with a significantly increased risk of preterm birth. In contrast, conditions characterized by lower humidity were associated with a relatively lower risk [39]. Similarly, recent studies have emphasized that humid environmental conditions and atmospheric variability which are becoming more frequent with climate change, may be associated with perinatal health outcomes [18, 19]. In this context, changes in humid environmental conditions may be related to changes in uteroplacental balance, particularly during the third trimester of pregnancy.

In this study, lower surface atmospheric pressure and lower wind speed were found to be associated with preterm birth, suggesting that weather factors may operate within a complex network associated with preterm birth. The potential role of changes in surface pressure in the onset of labor has long been discussed in the literature; several studies have reported that rapid decreases in barometric pressure may be related to membrane rupture and spontaneous labor onset [40]. Possible physiological explanations include mechanical and biophysical changes accompanying reduced external pressure, which may be related to changes in membrane integrity and uterine activity [40].

Under conditions of low wind speed and atmospheric stagnation, the accumulation of humidity, increased atmospheric pollution levels, and impaired maternal thermoregulation represent additional plausible mechanisms [16, 41]. This evidence suggests that climate change–related atmospheric instability may be associated with preterm birth risk through complex combinations of meteorological conditions.

The inverse association observed for all-sky surface radiation may be related to mechanisms such as vitamin D synthesis and circadian rhythm regulation and beneficial effects on maternal psychological well-being [42]. However, the loss of statistical significance of radiation in some models suggests that this finding could be influenced by collinearity among meteorological variables. Large-scale studies have predominantly focused on temperature and humidity as primary exposures, while evidence regarding solar radiation and sunshine duration remains relatively limited [43].

Overall, the meteorological findings of this study are consistent with the limited number of investigations that have jointly evaluated relative humidity, surface pressure, and wind speed. A multicenter European study reported that surface pressure and other meteorological variables were associated with preterm birth risk with pressure fluctuations identified as a key determinant [44]. Similarly, a recently published multicenter study from China identified increased relative humidity and low surface pressure as potential factors associated with pregnancy complications, findings that are partially consistent with our results [45].

Climate change is expected to increase environmental variability affecting women of reproductive age, particularly in regions with high humidity. The associations observed in this study between relative humidity, surface atmospheric pressure, and wind speed may be consistent with emerging evidence on environmental influences on pregnancy outcomes. These findings may suggest that certain maternal groups, such as women of advanced maternal age and nulliparous women, could be more vulnerable to environmental stressors; however, this interpretation should be approached with caution. Overall, the results should be interpreted as associative, and no causal inferences can be drawn.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the retrospective design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between meteorological factors and preterm birth. Second, the study was conducted in a single tertiary care center, which may limit the generalizability of the results. In addition, the relatively high preterm birth rate observed in this study may reflect the tertiary referral nature of the study center, which receives high-risk and complicated pregnancies, and this may further restrict external validity.

Third, meteorological data were obtained as monthly averages rather than daily or trimester-specific measurements. While this approach allows for the evaluation of general environmental conditions, it may obscure short-term fluctuations and critical exposure windows that are more biologically relevant to the initiation of preterm labor. Given that preterm birth may be influenced by acute triggers occurring shortly before delivery, this temporal aggregation may lead to exposure misclassification and introduce a temporal mismatch between exposure and outcome. As a result, the ability to establish biologically plausible associations is limited.

Furthermore, environmental exposures were assigned based on the month and year of delivery, and individual-level differences in exposure (e.g., time spent indoors, occupational conditions, or use of climate control) could not be accounted for.

In addition, although several maternal and environmental variables were included in the analysis, important confounding factors such as prior preterm birth, maternal comorbidities, body mass index, smoking status, and hypertensive disorders were not available in the dataset. The absence of these variables may have introduced substantial residual confounding, potentially influencing both the direction and magnitude of the observed associations. This limitation significantly restricts the interpretability of the findings and precludes conclusions regarding independent or causal relationships.

Finally, preterm birth was analyzed as a single outcome, and it was not possible to distinguish between spontaneous and medically indicated preterm births. This represents a major limitation of the study, as these subtypes have distinct etiologies, risk factors, and underlying mechanisms. Medically indicated preterm births are often driven by clinical decision-making processes rather than environmental exposures, and their inclusion alongside spontaneous cases may have introduced substantial heterogeneity. This limitation may have influenced the observed associations and significantly restricts the interpretability of the findings.

Additionally, formal sensitivity analyses were not conducted to evaluate the stability of the regression model under alternative model specifications. Therefore, the robustness of the observed associations across different analytical approaches could not be assessed.

Conclusions

The findings of this study suggest that certain meteorological factors may be associated with preterm birth alongside maternal characteristics. However, given the methodological limitations, particularly related to exposure assessment and residual confounding, these associations should be interpreted with caution. The results do not imply causal relationships but rather highlight potential patterns that warrant further investigation.

Future studies using more precise exposure measurements and comprehensive adjustment for confounding variables are needed to better understand the relationship between environmental factors and preterm birth.
