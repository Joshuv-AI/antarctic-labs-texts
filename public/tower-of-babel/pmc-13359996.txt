A case report of triquetral osteoid osteoma: from diagnostic challenge to complete recovery
Authors: Marwa Zahdeh, Rami Akwan, Mahmoud R Manasra, Munther J Shawar, Azzam Zrineh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoid osteoma (OO) is a benign bone tumor that rarely affects the carpal bones, with triquetral involvement being exceptionally uncommon. Background

Osteoid osteoma (OO) is a benign bone-forming tumor that accounts for approximately 10–12% of all benign bone tumors and 2–3% of all primary bone tumors [1, 2]. First described by Bergstrand in 1930 and subsequently characterized as a distinct entity by Jaffe in 1935, this tumor typically presents as a small radiolucent nidus (usually < 2 cm) surrounded by reactive osteosclerosis [3]. It predominantly affects children and young adults, with approximately 70% of cases occurring in patients younger than 20 years, and demonstrates a male predominance with a male-to-female ratio of 2:1 to 3:1 [3].

The appendicular skeleton, particularly the long bones of the lower extremities, is the most common location for osteoid osteoma, with the femur and tibia accounting for more than half of all cases [3]. In contrast, involvement of the hand and wrist is relatively uncommon, representing only 5–15% of cases [4]. The phalanges represent the most common location for osteoid osteoma in the hand, followed by the carpal bones, whereas involvement of the metacarpals is the least common [5, 6]. In their systematic review of 401 hand osteoid osteomas, Meyer et al. reported that the phalanges accounted for 52.9% of cases, the metacarpals for 14.5%, and the scaphoid was the most common carpal bone at 7.7%, whereas the triquetrum was among the least frequently affected sites (2.2%), underscoring the exceptional rarity of triquetral involvement [7]. Osteoid osteoma arising in the triquetrum is exceptionally rare, with only a handful of cases documented in the literature [8–10].

The classic clinical presentation of osteoid osteoma includes localized pain that is characteristically worse at night and dramatically relieved by nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or salicylates [3]. This may be attributed to elevated prostaglandin levels, reported to be 100–1000 times higher than normal, which are believed to play a fundamental role in pain processing through vascular and inflammatory mechanisms [3, 11, 12]. However, when the tumor arises in atypical locations such as the carpal bones, the clinical presentation may mimic a variety of conditions, including inflammatory arthritis, tenosynovitis, or infection [13, 14]. Despite advances in imaging technology, the diagnosis of osteoid osteoma in atypical locations or with unusual clinical features remains challenging. Such diagnostic difficulties often lead to significant delays, resulting in prolonged patient suffering, reduced quality of life, increased healthcare costs, and secondary complications associated with certain cases [13].

Plain radiography is usually the initial imaging modality performed when OO is suspected, often revealing a small radiolucent nidus surrounded by reactive sclerosis, occasionally containing central calcification. However, CT scan remains the preferred imaging technique, as it accurately localizes the nidus and often reveals the characteristic target-shaped appearance [15]. Although MRI imaging can detect cancellous lesions, prominent bone marrow edema may obscure the nidus, making it less useful for tumor evaluation [16]. Notably, when findings remain inconclusive, three-phase skeletal scintigraphy can provide additional diagnostic information [17]. Furthermore, modern single-photon emission computed tomography combined with CT (SPECT-CT) may further aid localization by combining the high physiological sensitivity of scintigraphy with precise anatomical localization, potentially reducing diagnostic delay when conventional imaging findings are equivocal.

Conservative management of OO with NSAIDs can provide symptom relief, with resolution reported after approximately 33 months; however, the adverse effects of long-term NSAID use and the lack of clear data on recurrence after discontinuation limit its role as a definitive treatment [16, 18]. Surgical excision has traditionally been considered curative; however, open en bloc resection may result in significant bone loss, prolonged activity restriction, risk of pathological fracture, and the need for bone grafting and internal fixation, particularly in weight-bearing bones [16]. To minimize these potential consequences, less invasive approaches have been developed, with CT-guided percutaneous radiofrequency ablation now regarded as the treatment of choice, achieving success rates of up to 90% [12].

We present a challenging case of osteoid osteoma arising in the triquetrum in a young male patient, with a two-year diagnostic delay attributed to atypical presentation, equivocal imaging findings, and the rarity of triquetral bone involvement. This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of clinical suspicion for osteoid osteoma in the differential diagnosis of chronic wrist pain in young patients, even when initial investigations are equivocal.

Case Presentation

A 20-year-old male presented with a two-year history of progressively worsening pain and swelling of the right wrist. The pain was severe, deep, and persistent, with marked nocturnal exacerbation. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), particularly celecoxib, provided transient relief, which led to prolonged and frequent use.

Around four years prior to presentation, the patient sustained trauma to his right wrist during a bicycle fall. Initial management consisted of immobilization with splinting and casting for one month; however, suboptimal stabilization necessitated recasting. He achieved full recovery and remained asymptomatic for approximately one year before a painless swelling developed over the right wrist. This swelling progressively enlarged and was eventually accompanied by worsening pain and functional impairment. No systemic symptoms were reported throughout this period.

Physical examination revealed a dorsolateral wrist swelling with mild skin discoloration and a scar related to the previous biopsy. Focal tenderness was elicited over the triquetrum, with severe discomfort even on minimal pressure. Thenar and hypothenar muscle wasting were noted. Additionally, the right wrist range of motion (ROM) was markedly restricted. Pinch and grip strength were reduced. The neurologic examination, however, was unremarkable. Sensation remained intact over the median and ulnar nerve distributions, and there was no evidence of vascular compromise. Family history was notable for bone cancer in the patient’s grandfather, who was diagnosed at age 63 and died three months later (other documentation unavailable), as well as psoriasis in the patient’s grandmother.

Over the preceding two years, the patient was evaluated by multiple specialists, including rheumatologists and orthopedic surgeons. Despite thorough assessments, no consensus diagnosis was reached, and the case remained a diagnostic challenge. Laboratory investigations throughout this period were unremarkable; rheumatoid factor and other autoimmune markers were negative, and inflammatory parameters, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein, remained within normal limits. White blood cell counts were occasionally mildly elevated but predominantly normal, and all other blood tests showed no abnormalities.

Initial imaging studies in 2022 following the trauma, including plain radiographs and CT scans of the right wrist, revealed only linear, non-displaced fractures of the trapezium and trapezoid bones without any focal bony lesion (Fig. 1). As symptoms developed and progressed, a contrast-enhanced MRI of the right wrist was performed in August 2024, which demonstrated features suggestive of synovitis with diffuse bone marrow edema (Fig. 2). These findings prompted a synovial biopsy with culture. The synovial fluid was yellow and turbid with a pH of 8, and the leukocyte differential indicated minimal chronic inflammation without evidence of malignancy. Culture revealed no microbial growth.

Given the ongoing suspicion of inflammatory arthritis, empirical treatment was initiated with methotrexate (20 mg weekly), prednisolone (20 mg daily), a COX-2 inhibitor, and folic acid supplementation. The patient discontinued treatment after one month due to a lack of clinical improvement. Two ultrasound-guided intra-articular corticosteroid injections were then administered, providing only temporary symptomatic relief.

Alternative diagnoses were considered, including complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) and seronegative inflammatory diseases, but were later ruled out following the appropriate clinical and radiological assessments. Another contrast-enhanced MRI obtained in 27th of March 2025 demonstrated persistent bone marrow edema with synovial thickening (Fig. 3). Upon careful re-reviewing of serial MRI studies, a hand surgery specialist found a focal enhancing lesion in the triquetral bone that had not been previously appreciated, and a CT scan of the wrist was subsequently ordered to improve visualization.

Contrast-enhanced MRI of the right wrist (coronal, axial, and sagittal views) performed in March 2025 shows features suggestive of persistent synovitis along with a triquetral enhancing lesion (red arrows)

The CT scan performed in May 2025 clearly demonstrated a lucent nidus with a peripheral sclerotic rim and a central sclerotic dot within the triquetrum, measuring approximately 0.8 × 0.7 × 0.8 cm, findings pathognomonic of osteoid osteoma (Fig. 4). A presumptive diagnosis of triquetral osteoid osteoma was made. Additional imaging sections from multiple planes for the MRI and CT are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Multidisciplinary consultations were undertaken to determine the optimal management strategy. An arthroscopic approach involving synovial debridement, calcification removal, and bone grafting was initially considered; however, it was deemed technically challenging with a high rate of potential complications. Radiofrequency ablation was also considered, but the lesion’s proximity to the ulnar nerve raised concerns for neurovascular injury. Eventually, the hand surgery specialist recommended an excisional biopsy without bone grafting to confirm and remove the lesion. This approach was endorsed by another hand surgeon, and surgery was expedited, given the patient’s development of flank pain and the presence of 2 + proteinuria on urinalysis, findings attributed to prolonged NSAID use.

Preoperatively, right wrist motion was severely restricted, with flexion limited to 10° and no extension or ulnar/radial deviation. Surgery was performed via a dorsal ulnar longitudinal incision centered over the fifth extensor compartment to expose the triquetrum. A cortical window was created, and a well-circumscribed lesion was resected. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of osteoid osteoma. Partial synovectomy was also performed. Postoperatively, the patient reported immediate pain relief with gradual improvement in wrist function. The wrist was immobilized in a volar splint for two weeks to protect the surgical site and allow initial soft tissue healing. The patient was advised to avoid forceful gripping, heavy lifting, and impact activities for six weeks to minimize the risk of fracture at the excision site. A physiotherapy program was initiated, including transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation and range-of-motion exercises, along with vitamin supplementation. At two-month follow-up, clinical assessment revealed flexion of 60° with full extension and full radial and ulnar deviation, as well as grip strength comparable to the contralateral side. Additionally, the patient reported no pain at rest or during activity and no longer required analgesics. The surgical wound healed without complications. At the six-month follow-up, the patient remained pain-free with full functional recovery. Figure 7 presents a visual timeline outlining the sequence of clinical events. Clinical photographs demonstrated restoration of near-symmetric wrist range of motion, including extension, flexion, radial and ulnar deviation, pronation, and supination, compared to the contralateral side (Fig. 8).

Patient Perspective

The patient reported that the two-year period of undiagnosed, progressively worsening pain had a major impact on his daily life and mental well-being. He described persistent sleep disruption due to severe nocturnal pain and difficulty carrying out routine manual tasks. The repeated inconclusive evaluations and failed treatments left him frustrated and anxious, especially as he worried about the possibility of a serious underlying condition. His grandfather’s history of bone cancer, which led to death within three months of diagnosis, made these concerns harder to dismiss. Following surgical excision and complete resolution of symptoms, he expressed substantial relief and gratitude, reporting a return to full daily activities and a marked improvement in his quality of life. He noted that the period of diagnostic uncertainty was the most distressing aspect of his illness. He also shared that the prolonged diagnostic course, including repeated imaging, multiple specialist visits, and investigations that yielded no answers, left him with a strong aversion to further radiological examinations. Despite acknowledging his significant clinical improvement, he firmly declined any postoperative imaging, stating that the experience of undergoing repeated investigations without answers had caused lasting frustration and emotional exhaustion.

Discussion

This case illustrates the significant diagnostic challenge posed by osteoid osteoma occurring in atypical locations such as the carpal bones. A limited number of triquetral osteoid osteomas have been reported in the literature. Murray et al. identified a single case of triquetral osteoid osteoma among 44 primary carpal neoplasms in a large series of 26,800 bone tumors reviewed at the Mayo Clinic over an 83-year period [19]. Our present case adds to this limited body of literature and highlights the difficulties of identifying this rare lesion in uncommon anatomical locations.

The two-year delay in diagnosis experienced by our patient is consistent with the literature, where the average time to diagnosis was 20.5 months (range 7–62 months) in a recently published case series [20]. Taylor et al. [8] reported a similar case of triquetral OO with a 4-year diagnostic delay, during which the patient underwent multiple unsuccessful empirical interventions, including synovectomy, curettage, bone grafting, stellate ganglion blocks, and even thoracic sympathectomy for presumed complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS). Herzberg et al. [9] also described a subperiosteal OO of the triquetrum with a six-year diagnostic delay. In our case, the patient experienced two years of progressive pain to reach a definitive diagnosis, during which he was evaluated by multiple specialists and received empirical treatment for inflammatory arthritis without benefit. This diagnostic delay was likely obscured by the history of remote wrist trauma, which directed initial clinical attention toward post-traumatic sequelae rather than a primary bone lesion.

Additionally, the clinical presentation mimicked inflammatory arthritis, with prominent wrist swelling, restricted motion, and pain that responded temporarily to NSAIDs. Intra and juxta-articular osteoid osteomas may present with features suggestive of inflammatory monoarthritis, including synovitis and joint effusion [21]. Such misleading presentations may lead to prolonged diagnostic delays and inappropriate treatments, including intra-articular corticosteroid injections and immunosuppressive drugs, as occurred in our case [21, 22].

The pathophysiology of OO-related pain is well characterized; osteoblasts within the tumor nidus overexpress cyclooxygenase enzymes, leading to markedly elevated local concentrations of prostaglandins [23]. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) sensitizes peripheral nociceptive nerve fibers and induces vasodilation, resulting in increased intraosseous pressure. Nocturnal exacerbation of pain is attributed to resting venous pooling during recumbency, which further elevates intraosseous pressure. This proposed mechanism may explain the characteristic dramatic response to COX-2 inhibitors, which directly suppress the prostaglandin cascade at its enzymatic source. The muscle wasting was attributed to disuse atrophy secondary to chronic pain and prolonged functional impairment rather than nerve compression, as supported by the unremarkable neurologic examination; this interpretation was subsequently confirmed by normalization of grip strength following surgical excision.

The imaging characteristics of carpal osteoid osteoma can also be misleading. Conventional radiographs failed to identify the lesion in our patient. Small carpal bones pose a particular challenge due to overlapping anatomy and the frequent absence of periosteal reaction, unlike in long bones. MRI, which is increasingly used as a first-line imaging modality for evaluating joint pathology, has recognized limitations in diagnosing osteoid osteoma and can result in misdiagnosis [24–26].

Osteoid osteomas are classified based on their anatomical location within bone as cortical (most common, accounting for approximately 75% of cases), cancellous (medullary), or subperiosteal [11]. This classification has important diagnostic and therapeutic implications. Cortical lesions typically demonstrate the classic radiographic appearance of a nidus surrounded by reactive sclerosis, whereas cancellous and subperiosteal lesions frequently lack this characteristic periosteal reaction, making radiographic identification more challenging [27].

In our case, although the lesion was intracortical and present on the initial MRI, it was overlooked due to low clinical suspicion for osteoid osteoma in the wrist, focusing on synovial inflammation, the lesion’s small size, and being obscured by bone marrow edema. Even on a subsequent contrast-enhanced MRI, where the tumor was more conspicuous, it was misinterpreted until careful re-evaluation of the imaging. On the other hand, CT imaging clearly demonstrated a well-defined lucent nidus with a sclerotic rim and central calcification, a feature characteristic of osteoid osteoma. Research supports the superiority of CT over MRI in detecting OO, and it remains the most reliable imaging modality [3, 28, 29]. Our experience reinforces the recommendation that CT should be performed promptly in patients with persistent joint pain unresponsive to conventional treatment, especially when there is even a remote suspicion of osteoid osteoma. Moreover, MRI of the wrist should be carefully evaluated, ensuring that subtle bony lesions are not overlooked while attention is directed toward inflammatory changes.

The history of preceding trauma in our patient adds another layer of complexity to the diagnostic evaluation. Patients sometimes attribute their symptoms to minor injuries, which can further delay consideration of a neoplastic process. However, it is important to recognize that osteoid osteoma is rarely reported after trauma, but should nonetheless be suspected [30].

Treatment of osteoid osteoma has evolved significantly over the past decades. While conservative management with NSAIDs remains a reasonable initial approach given the potential for spontaneous regression, surgical intervention is indicated for patients with refractory symptoms, intolerance to prolonged NSAID use, or severely restricted range of motion. Percutaneous radiofrequency ablation has emerged as the gold standard minimally invasive treatment for OO [31]. However, its application in small juxta-articular bones such as the carpal bones is constrained by several factors. It requires a thermal safety margin of 1.0 to 1.5 cm from the skin surface and major neurovascular structures to prevent thermal necrosis and nerve injury. Given the small dimensions of the triquetrum and the 0.8 cm lesion size, achieving adequate safety margins from the adjacent ulnar nerve and artery was technically infeasible. Additional limitations in the hand include the risk of thermal damage to articular cartilage, tendons, and the thin overlying soft tissues, as well as higher technical failure rates compared to long bone applications. Open surgical excision, while more invasive, offers the advantages of direct visualization, histopathological confirmation through complete nidus removal, and the ability to perform concurrent procedures such as synovectomy. In our patient, the open surgical excision approach was utilized and was further justified by the urgent need to discontinue NSAIDs due to renal complications.

Furthermore, surgical excision of osteoid osteoma in carpal bones presents unique technical challenges distinct from long bone surgery. The small dimensions of the triquetrum demand precise preoperative localization and meticulous intraoperative technique to ensure complete nidus removal, as incomplete excision is the primary cause of recurrence. Concurrently, excessive bone removal risks weakening the structural integrity of the triquetrum, potentially leading to pathological fracture or destabilization of the proximal carpal row with consequent alteration of wrist biomechanics. In our case, a carefully planned cortical window was created to achieve complete lesion resection while preserving the structural integrity of the bone and maintaining carpal alignment. Intraoperative specimen examination confirmed intact nidus removal, and the absence of postoperative fracture or carpal instability supports the adequacy of this approach.

The decision to forgo bone grafting was based on several considerations. The triquetrum is a non-weight-bearing bone, and the excised lesion (0.8 cm) constituted a relatively small cortical defect. Bone grafting may not be necessary for small defects in carpal bones, as satisfactory osseous regeneration and favorable clinical outcomes have been achieved without graft augmentation [20, 32]. Grafting also adds operative time and carries its own risks, including donor-site morbidity with autograft, graft resorption, and infection. In our patient, the absence of pathological fracture and complete functional recovery at six months support the decision not to graft. That said, grafting remains appropriate in cases involving larger bony defects, significant compromise to the structural integrity of the host bone, or bones subject to higher mechanical demands.

The development of flank pain and 2 + proteinuria in our patient is a notable consequence of prolonged NSAID use that deserves discussion. Prostaglandins, particularly PGE2 and prostacyclin, are essential for maintaining renal perfusion through afferent arteriolar vasodilation. In our patient, chronic COX-2 inhibition with celecoxib likely compromised this protective mechanism, resulting in renal ischemia. The clinical picture is compatible with acute interstitial nephritis (AIN) or renal papillary necrosis (RPN), both of which are established complications of long-term NSAID therapy. This case demonstrates that relying on prolonged NSAID use as a management strategy is not without consequence, even in young patients, and reinforces the need to pursue a definitive diagnosis early rather than continuing indefinite symptomatic treatment.

The dramatic functional recovery observed in our patient, from severely restricted wrist motion preoperatively to near-complete restoration of range of motion on follow-ups, along with discontinuation of analgesics due to pain resolution, underscores the importance of accurate and timely diagnosis and intervention.

Conclusions

This case highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by osteoid osteoma when it occurs in rare anatomical locations such as the triquetrum. The key lessons from this case include: 1- osteoid osteoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of persistent wrist pain, particularly in young patients, even when clinical features suggest inflammatory arthritis; 2- early CT imaging is recommended in cases of atypical or prolonged wrist pain when initial evaluation fails to establish a diagnosis, as subtle bony lesions may otherwise be overlooked; 3- MRI can detect an osteoid osteoma, but careful evaluation of imaging is essential; 4- open surgical excision remains an effective treatment option when percutaneous techniques may be risky; and 5- complete removal of the nidus results in excellent outcomes with immediate pain relief and functional recovery.
