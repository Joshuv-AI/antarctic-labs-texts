The relationship between palliative psychiatry and assisted dying: a scoping review
Authors: Micaela Forte, Lucy Panko, Kate Tsiandoulas, Terri Rodak, Lucy Costa, Sarah Levitt, Daniel Z. Buchman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundPalliative psychiatry applies palliative care principles to the mental health care of people experiencing severe and persistent mental illness (SPMI), with the aim of improving quality of life and alleviating suffering. As debates about assisted dying (AD) for mental illness expand globally, questions have emerged about how palliative psychiatry relates to AD.MethodsThis scoping review, guided by Arksey and O’Malley’s scoping review framework, maps the scholarly and grey literature on the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD. Database searches were run on April 30, 2025, and updated on September 2, 2025. Data were analyzed using descriptive numerical analysis and qualitative content analysis.FindingsAmong the 50 included publications, 15 characterized palliative psychiatry and AD as separate practices with distinct goals, while 13 proposed palliative psychiatry as an alternative care pathway to AD and care-as-usual.InterpretationThe literature positions the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD along a spectrum, from equivalent to differentiated, though most publications treat them as distinct. Future research should clarify the definition and practice of palliative psychiatry, and examine whether, and how, it may offer a quality of life-oriented alternative care pathway.FundingThe Canadian Institutes of Health Research provided financial support for this review. The funder had no role in study design; data collection, analysis, and synthesis; report writing; and the decision to submit the paper for publication.

Research in contextEvidence before this studyPalliative psychiatry has been discussed in the literature since a seminal publication in 2016 which conceptually explored the application of palliative care principles to psychiatry. Prior literature on palliative psychiatry has largely consisted of conceptual publications that theorize about its definitions and practices, with comparatively limited empirical evidence. The literature on AD is much more developed. Although palliative psychiatry and AD for mental illness are often considered in overlapping discussions, no previous reviews were identified that described the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD.Added value of this studyThis scoping review fills an evidence gap in the literature to map the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD. Our review demonstrates how the literature clarifies the conceptual and clinical boundaries between two frameworks that both seek to address intolerable psychiatric suffering but rest on divergent goals. The findings can help contribute to the broader literature by demonstrating that palliative psychiatry is understood in most cases as a potential alternative to AD, which can help inform more coherent, ethically grounded, and clinically consistent approaches to intolerable psychiatry suffering in people living with SPMI.Implications of all the available evidenceTogether with prior evidence, this scoping review advances conceptual clarity regarding the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD. The findings underscore the heterogeneity in how these concepts are defined and related across the literature, highlighting the need for further research about whether and how they should inform clinical decision-making for individuals with SPMI, particularly for those experiencing poor quality of life and substantial psychiatric suffering despite trying best available treatments. This evidence may inform mental health clinicians’ treatments and goals of care discussions, while informing policymakers’ understandings about the current scholarly landscape, particularly in jurisdictions considering AD legalization for individuals with SPMI and where palliative psychiatry approaches may provide an appropriate alternative.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Palliative psychiatry has been discussed in the literature since a seminal publication in 2016 which conceptually explored the application of palliative care principles to psychiatry. Prior literature on palliative psychiatry has largely consisted of conceptual publications that theorize about its definitions and practices, with comparatively limited empirical evidence. The literature on AD is much more developed. Although palliative psychiatry and AD for mental illness are often considered in overlapping discussions, no previous reviews were identified that described the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD.

Added value of this study

This scoping review fills an evidence gap in the literature to map the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD. Our review demonstrates how the literature clarifies the conceptual and clinical boundaries between two frameworks that both seek to address intolerable psychiatric suffering but rest on divergent goals. The findings can help contribute to the broader literature by demonstrating that palliative psychiatry is understood in most cases as a potential alternative to AD, which can help inform more coherent, ethically grounded, and clinically consistent approaches to intolerable psychiatry suffering in people living with SPMI.

Implications of all the available evidence

Together with prior evidence, this scoping review advances conceptual clarity regarding the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD. The findings underscore the heterogeneity in how these concepts are defined and related across the literature, highlighting the need for further research about whether and how they should inform clinical decision-making for individuals with SPMI, particularly for those experiencing poor quality of life and substantial psychiatric suffering despite trying best available treatments. This evidence may inform mental health clinicians’ treatments and goals of care discussions, while informing policymakers’ understandings about the current scholarly landscape, particularly in jurisdictions considering AD legalization for individuals with SPMI and where palliative psychiatry approaches may provide an appropriate alternative.

Introduction

In 2016, Trachsel and colleagues published a seminal paper that described palliative psychiatry as an approach focused on quality of life for individuals experiencing severe and persistent mental illness (SPMI).1 Their work highlighted the risks of therapeutic neglect and overly aggressive treatment, which can lead to unmitigated suffering for this population. Discussions of palliative psychiatry have since suggested that this approach is likely relevant to individuals contending with treatment-resistant symptoms, meaning many trials of best-available treatments fail to improve, and in some cases worsen, the suffering associated with their mental disorder.2

Growing discussions about palliative psychiatry in the literature intersect with the debates around the practice of assisted dying (AD) for mental illness in jurisdictions where its implementation may be forthcoming or is currently being debated.3 The move towards potentially legalizing AD access for people with mental illness as the sole underlying medical condition has sparked contentious ethical debates about the potential for structural discrimination (including ableism, racism, sexism, and classism).4, 5, 6 AD legislation forefronts controversial concepts such as the irremediability of psychiatric disorders and intolerable suffering.7 Since these concepts are also relevant to palliative psychiatry, debates about palliative psychiatry have intensified in the context of possible AD expansion.2

The paucity of literature describing whether or how palliative psychiatry approaches are related to AD for mental illness further obscures this complexity. To date, there are no scholarly reviews of the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD for mental illness. This scoping review aims to address the evidence gap concerning the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD for mental illness. This review is timely and important to clarify the scopes of both palliative psychiatry and AD policies and practices, and to highlight areas that necessitate further inquiry.

We aimed to answer the research question: “What does the scholarly and grey literature say about the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD?” We used the term AD as an internationally recognized umbrella term for voluntary euthanasia, and for our purposes includes assisted suicide or death, physician-assisted suicide or death, and medical assistance in dying (MAiD) for mental illness.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

We applied scoping review methods as described by Arksey and O’Malley.8 In collaboration with the research team, a health sciences librarian developed the search strategies and conducted searches in MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), APA PsycInfo (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCO), Philosopher’s Index (ProQuest), and Academic OneFile (Gale). To capture newspaper content, we searched Global Newsstream Collection (ProQuest). All database searches were run on April 30, 2025, and again on September 2, 2025, to capture literature published during the review process.

All strategies used database-specific subject headings, keywords in natural language, and advanced search operators to capture literature on palliative psychiatry, regardless of whether AD terms appeared in the metadata. This allowed us to broadly scope how AD is discussed in the palliative psychiatry literature, even if it was not a main topic in an article. Search queries used Boolean and adjacency operators to look for the terms “palliative” or “non-curative” with or near terms related to serious mental illness (e.g., “severe∗ mental∗”, “treatment resistant psychiatr∗”, “SPMI”). No limits were placed on study type, publication type, publication date, or language. See Appendix A for the complete search strategies.

To capture how palliative psychiatry and AD were discussed in the public sphere beyond scholarly journals, we searched the Global Newsstream Collection database. We were aware of online news and magazine articles relevant to our review that were not identified by this database search, so we searched Psychiatric Times Online and Google by hand. We input simplified search terms in the search bars of Psychiatric Times Online and Google, and screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts of the first 10 pages of results. We included relevant publications in our screening and charting.

We developed narrow inclusion and exclusion screening criteria using the PCC mnemonic (population, concept, context, and study characteristics),9 to ensure our reviewed articles remained specific to our research question (Table 1). Our population of interest was individuals with SPMI and mental health clinicians. Our concepts of interest were AD, primarily in the contexts of mental illness, and palliative psychiatry. We conceptualized palliative psychiatry as applying principles of palliative care to addressing psychiatric symptoms and suffering associated with SPMI.1 The focus of a palliative approach with this population is on quality of life and relief of suffering, as opposed to mitigating or curing the illness or symptoms.i We excluded publications that only discussed palliative care, palliative care psychiatry, and psychiatry in palliative care since they describe distinct concepts from palliative psychiatry. Including them may have conflated the delivery of psychiatric care within palliative care for physical conditions with our question about palliation as applied to psychiatric conditions. Contextually, we included studies from all countries, in all languages, from all years. We included scholarly and grey literature, including news and magazine articles. Data extraction and synthesis

Two authors worked in parallel to extract data related to ten a priori categories reflecting the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD from the included publications (see Fig. 2 for a full list of categories). We developed the categories as an author team in response to our research question based on the various ways the publications could in theory describe the relationship. We then created definitions for each category prior to data extraction.Fig. 2Results.

Results.

Due to the limited literature and often indirect engagement with our research question in the literature, we extracted one illustrative quote per publication to directly address our research question. We coded each quote with one or more a priori categories. Some publications discussed multiple ways in which AD and palliative psychiatry are related, to which we ascribed more than one category of relationships. Each extractor assigned the quote to the most similar categor(ies) and its definition.

We synthesized data using both descriptive numerical summaries and qualitative content analysis to deepen understanding.12 We conducted numerical analysis on the frequency of each code to rank relationships from most to least prevalent (Fig. 2) and qualitatively examined quotes to refine the codes. We supported qualitative analysis through discussion between the two extractors, and then the author team. After discussions, we recoded 25 quotes with a different category and removed additional code(s) from 14 publications due to lack of relevance. We further collapsed the a priori categories into broader categories given conceptual similarity and overlap, resulting in a final spectrum of six categories. This informed a mapped spectrum of relationships between palliative psychiatry and AD, from differentiated (i.e., not the same) to equivalent (i.e., the same). We produced a visualization of the analysis, seen in Fig. 3. The registered protocol for our scoping review is available on Open Science Framework’s registries under the DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/FR6A8.Fig. 3Palliative psychiatry and assisted dying: a spectrum of relationships.

Relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD

We categorized publications across ten relationship categories (Fig. 2). We identified a spectrum of described relationships between palliative psychiatry and AD (Fig. 3). Our spectrum ranges from differentiated (and most prevalent) to equivalent (and least prevalent). Findings are reported in this order, including numerical analysis to illustrate their prevalence.

Palliative psychiatry and AD are discussed separately

12 publications discussed both AD and palliative psychiatry in separate sections of the publication.15,18,21,23,24,35,42, 43, 44,46,48,55 These publications included both terms without discussing them together. The extracted quotes in this category only included how the publication discussed AD, not palliative psychiatry. In some publications, the authors stated that AD would not be discussed or that discussions about AD were outside of the scope of the publication.35,43,46,48,55 For example, in their survey study, Stoll and colleagues46 stated that survey questions about AD would not be included. These publications did not clearly state whether palliative psychiatry and AD were related or not.

Palliative psychiatry and AD are distinct from one another

We collapsed the categories ‘palliative psychiatry and AD are not related’ (n = 11) and ‘palliative psychiatry does not advocate for AD’ (n = 4) into this category (n = 15) since we found conceptual similarities between the arguments. 11 publications proposed that palliative psychiatry and AD are mutually exclusive, with separate goals.10,13,28,29,33,38,54,56, 57, 58, 59 Most of these publications distinguished the two by highlighting the differences between life-affirming approaches and interventions hastening or leading to death.2,10,37,38,54,56,59 For instance, Sousa and colleagues10 stated.“Currently, there are few studies linking PC [(palliative care)] with psychiatric disorders and/or mental health issues. This impacts the effort to better define the concept of PP [(palliative psychiatry)], distinguishing it from the general approach to PC. Thus, its differentiation serves to position PP against the controversial issue of assisted death.” (p. 6)

“Currently, there are few studies linking PC [(palliative care)] with psychiatric disorders and/or mental health issues. This impacts the effort to better define the concept of PP [(palliative psychiatry)], distinguishing it from the general approach to PC. Thus, its differentiation serves to position PP against the controversial issue of assisted death.” (p. 6)

As these authors claimed that rehabilitation falls within palliative psychiatric goals, they situated palliative psychiatric goals separately from the goals of palliative care and AD (which do not include rehabilitation). Furthermore, Moran38 stated that palliative psychiatry’s goals oppose patient abandonment and discontinuation of medical care. Gieselmann and Vollmann29 emphasized that palliative psychiatry approaches do not justify the permissibility of AD. Attia and colleagues13 posited that mental healthcare practitioners ‘draw the line’ at AD. Specifically, they suggested that eating disorder specialists report incorporating palliative care approaches in their clinical practices but not assisting in their patients’ death.

Some publications made distinctions by outlining understandings of irremediable suffering. One publication proposed that suffering can be remedied in life,57 which directly contrasts AD philosophy that assumes suffering is only remedied through death. Geppert28 distinguished palliative psychiatry from AD by claiming that, although they both center on patient wishes and values, palliative psychiatry is rooted in being flexible: individuals can rescind their choice of treatment pathways and resume aggressive treatment at any time, whereas AD is irreversible. Similarly, Kirby33 proposed that they can be differentiated by understanding the values and wishes of individuals with SPMI. If their wishes are to “maintain or enhance their quality of life without pursuing and achieving symptom-free recovery”, palliative psychiatry is the ideal approach (p. 65). Individuals who wish to pursue AD would thus follow a different approach. Xu and Sisti58 suggested that even individuals who desire AD should be guided to palliative psychiatry until there is greater conceptual clarity for both. Overall, this suggests that palliative psychiatry and AD are considered separate due to their foundational principles, goals of care, and end results.

Four publications argued that discussions about palliative psychiatry do not equate to support for AD,16,17,37,58 thereby distinguishing palliative psychiatry from AD, but thematically warrants specific attention as it describes relationships of discourse and advocacy. These publications asserted that conflating palliative psychiatry and AD misrepresents the goals of palliative care and may increase barriers to palliative psychiatry. Furthermore, some authors cautioned practitioners to use precise language regarding beneficiaries and goals of palliative psychiatry to avoid confusion with AD advocacy.17,37 For example, Linblad and colleagues37 emphasized that terminology around palliative psychiatry, namely its goals about practicing “without the intention of either hastening or postponing death, […] could be interpreted as a statement against physician-assisted death.” (p. 29). Similarly, Xu and Sisti’s58 recommendation that palliative psychiatry be employed instead of AD for individuals experiencing SPMI echoes this positioning. Their argument explicitly demonstrates that advocating for palliative psychiatry does not always lead to advocacy for AD. In fact, in many cases, advocacy for palliative psychiatry appears to oppose that for AD. These four publications generally supported palliative psychiatry and framed it in opposition to AD.

Palliative psychiatry is an alternative to AD

13 publications specified that palliative psychiatry should be offered as an alternative or substitute to address mental suffering for individuals experiencing SPMI who seek AD.22,27,30,32,34,45,49, 50, 51, 52,58 We separated this category from the prior, since framing palliative psychiatry as an alternative to AD advances ethical and clinical claims regarding the appropriate response to irremediable suffering that go beyond distinguishing the two. These publications proposed that palliative psychiatry enhances quality of life by alleviating suffering associated with SPMI without resorting to AD. In jurisdictions permitting AD, the only two currently available options to this population are coercive suicide prevention (CSP) and AD, both of which inadequately address their diverse needs. To respond to this dichotomy, van Veen and van Delden49 argued that palliative psychiatry offers an alternative ‘middle ground’ care path for those with treatment-resistant SPMI, warranting further exploration.

Publications differed in the way that palliative psychiatry could serve as an alternative to AD, from completely replacing AD to having both available. Regarding the former, Xu and Sisti58 suggested that palliative psychiatry approaches should replace MAiD (i.e., AD) for individuals with SPMI, rather than an approach that combines the two. They stated that:“[g]iven current conceptual limitations, palliative psychiatry, and not MAID, is recommended in severely treatment-resistant cases of mental illness.” (p. 44)

“[g]iven current conceptual limitations, palliative psychiatry, and not MAID, is recommended in severely treatment-resistant cases of mental illness.” (p. 44)

Conversely, several publications suggested that palliative psychiatry and AD should be alternatives to care-as-usual for individuals with SPMI.22,31,32,53 These publications raised ethical concerns about AD legislation, particularly given barriers to evidence-based mental health interventions, while simultaneously calling for further development and integration of palliative psychiatry. For instance, Hazif-Thomasa and colleagues31 stated that, if AD is legal and reified in practice, individuals with SPMI must also have timely access to multidisciplinary mental health care through an unparalleled increase in palliative psychiatry development and resources. Furthermore, de Winter22 emphasized that palliative psychiatry should never be understood as justifying a lower threshold for AD. Verhofstadt and colleagues53 raised similar concerns about poor mental health care contributing to MAiD requests, rather than irremediable suffering:“[I]ntegrating recovery-oriented and palliative approaches in psychiatry is urgent, especially where MAID legislation includes psychiatric conditions. [MAID on psychiatric grounds] requests should stem from unalleviated suffering despite comprehensive and adequate care, not due to incomprehensive and inadequate care” (p. 7)

“[I]ntegrating recovery-oriented and palliative approaches in psychiatry is urgent, especially where MAID legislation includes psychiatric conditions. [MAID on psychiatric grounds] requests should stem from unalleviated suffering despite comprehensive and adequate care, not due to incomprehensive and inadequate care” (p. 7)

Additionally, Verhofstadt and colleagues52 described palliative psychiatry as an alternative approach to AD through a two-track approach with psychological, physical, and social approaches on the life track, and discussing motivations and eligibility for AD on the death track. Similarly, Vanhie and colleagues50 described the Reakiro model as a palliative psychiatry approach that explores individuals’ wishes for AD and alternative avenues that could meet those needs. Havenaar and colleagues30 suggested that, when individuals experiencing SPMI broach conversations about AD, palliative psychiatry should also be discussed in tandem with AD procedures. Importantly, some publications raised that palliative psychiatry could reduce the number of individuals experiencing SPMI who request AD by addressing the disparities in palliative care for these individuals.45,51

There is a divide in the reviewed publications about how palliative psychiatry may operate as an alternative to AD; either as a replacement for AD, or as an ethically necessary third option beyond AD or CSP. Accordingly, palliative psychiatry may offer alternative care options to address individuals who request AD because they may feel they have no other options due to inadequate, inaccessible, or nonexistent mental health care services.

Palliative psychiatry and AD are related but not the same

Nine publications indicated that palliative psychiatry and AD might be related.1,2,19,20,36,39, 40, 41,47 These publications suggested that palliative psychiatry and AD fall along the same spectrum of interventions for individuals experiencing SPMI, but are not interchangeable.2 Publications in this category diverged from others that clearly stated palliative psychiatry and AD are distinct, since we interpreted their discussions to indicate that both are related in some way. For example, Trachsel and colleagues1 claimed that palliative psychiatry is “intricately linked” to AD legality (p. 3).

In many instances, however, authors raised ambiguities in the relationship; publications discussed the relationship as poorly defined, unclear, or requiring further clarification.20,36 Publications also stressed the need to further examine and reflect on the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD. For instance, Moureau and colleagues41 called for further reflection on the relationship between palliative psychiatry approaches such as the Oyster Care Model, suicidality, and AD.

Some publications referenced both palliative psychiatry and AD in the same sentence without drawing explicit connections between them.19,40,47 While these publications did not clarify the nature of the relationship, they implied that palliative psychiatry and AD are part of the same broader discourse about interventions in SPMI healthcare. For example, Moureau and colleagues40 considered questions about AD crucial to their research on palliative psychiatric care for individuals experiencing SPMI, while Decorte and colleagues19 noted that caregivers and researchers from psychiatric and palliative fields discussing AD for SPMI also inquired about palliative options. Collectively, these findings suggest that discussions about palliative psychiatry often include AD, but do not prescribe how the two should relate.

AD is one example of palliative psychiatry

Two publications discussed AD as one (but not every)j palliative psychiatric intervention.2,14 Bauschka and O’Melia14 described AD, where legal, as a possible palliative psychiatric intervention. Westermair and colleagues2 suggested that AD could potentially become understood as the “end of the spectrum” of palliative psychiatry interventions for people experiencing SPMI (p. 1538).

Palliative psychiatry and AD are equivalent

We collapsed the categories ‘palliative psychiatry advocates for AD’, ‘palliative psychiatry necessarily leads to AD’, ‘palliative psychiatry and AD are interchangeable’, and ‘AD is the sole palliative psychiatry intervention’ under this broader type of relationship, given their conceptual similarities. Only two publications argued that advocacy for palliative psychiatry necessarily leads to advocacy for AD. One author25,26 claimed that discussions about palliative psychiatry have been used to advocate for AD policies, including MAiD expansion in Canada, and that the use of the term “palliative” in these discussions has connoted that individuals experiencing SPMI are approaching end-of-life.

We did not identify any publications which claimed that AD is the sole palliative psychiatry intervention, that palliative psychiatry and AD are interchangeable, or that palliative psychiatry necessarily leads to AD.

Discussion

This scoping review mapped the scholarly and grey literature discussing palliative psychiatry and AD as a preliminary effort in clarifying their relationship. Across 50 publications, we identified a spectrum of relationships from differentiated and most prevalent to equivalent and least prevalent (Fig. 3). Most publications (78%) fell towards the ‘differentiated’ end of the spectrum while the fewest viewed them as equivalent, suggesting that most authors consider palliative psychiatry and AD as different. Since 2016, scholarly interest in the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD has increased, indicating the importance of this conversation alongside AD policy changes. Key findings highlight ethical imperatives for advancing palliative psychiatry in response to AD expansion, the challenge of distinguishing the relationship between the two while exploring death wishes, and contextual nuances across jurisdictional and diagnostic contexts.

We found that 13 papers positioned palliative psychiatry as an alternative pathway for individuals experiencing SPMI, broadening the dichotomy of care-as-usual and AD while distinguishing it from AD. This framing underscores calls to develop palliative psychiatry pathways amid concerns that mental health care-as-usual can be inaccessible and inadequate for individuals experiencing SPMI, particularly as AD could become increasingly accessible after potential expansion in jurisdictions such as Canada. To ensure AD requests are not based on a lack of access to mental health care, and rather are based on suffering from symptoms and experiences associated with SPMI, our findings indicate that possible expanded access to AD to include mental illness as the sole underlying condition may necessitate further research on and implementation of palliative psychiatry approaches. Most publications were contextualized by jurisdictions with socialized medicine. Further research is necessary to examine how AD expansion is understood in publicly-funded healthcare systems, particularly given critiques that AD may be framed as a means of reducing government healthcare spending rather than being developed alongside palliative psychiatry approaches.60

Importantly, authors suggested that greater development of palliative psychiatry could reduce the number of individuals experiencing SPMI requesting AD and might, in some cases, replace AD. Care models that could be understood as palliative psychiatry, such as Oyster Care and Reakiro,41,50 were described as ethically preferable since they centre patient values and wishes, minimize paternalism, and do not cause or hasten death. These findings suggest a clinical and ethical imperative to develop life-affirming alternatives to AD. Further research is important to identify autonomy-promoting approaches to SPMI that both do not hasten death and also address the deficiencies in the current inadequate mental health care system.

Publications coded under palliative psychiatry as an alternative to AD suggested that addressing death wishes may serve as a supportive intervention within palliative psychiatry, distinct from including AD as part of its framework.50,52 Evidence suggests that discussing and providing access to AD assessments may help alleviate suffering without leading to AD, suggesting a potential palliative effect.61 Other publications rejected recommending AD for individuals experiencing SPMI, reflecting broader debates about whether AD should be a form of palliative care for physical illnesses.62, 63, 64

These discussions hinge on interpretations of the scope, goals, and ethical obligations of palliative care.65,66 Our analysis suggests that palliative psychiatry may represent a clinical and philosophical paradigm, whereas AD is a specific intervention that may not align with this approach. The finding that palliative psychiatry may involve conversations about death wishes, but not necessarily AD, prompts further inquiry into whether or how conversations about AD should be integrated into palliative psychiatry.

Among the nine publications positioning palliative psychiatry and AD as related but not the same, authors more frequently raised questions about the relationship than provided answers. They highlighted uncertainty, conceptual ambiguity, and confusion about whether, how, and to what extent the two practices intersect. Although these authors did not specify the nature of the relationship, our findings in this category suggest that palliative psychiatry and AD are related through broader efforts to respond ethically and clinically to SPMI. While this scoping review brings initial clarity to the relationship, our findings highlight the need for further research to better understand this relationship from a clinical and ethical standpoint.

12 other publications did not directly discuss palliative psychiatry and AD together, indicating that, since the discussions were separate, the authors were distinguishing palliative psychiatry and AD by not engaging in their comparison. Publications that specified that AD would not be discussed may indicate that palliative psychiatry can be explored and advanced without AD.

Finally, our demographic findings suggest that the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD is nuanced. The majority of included publications were commentaries and case studies (n = 16)7,14,15,17,20,22,28,30,33,36,40,42, 43, 44,47,59 and theoretical and conceptual publications (n = 8), which suggests an opportunity for future empirical investigation of the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD, and the issues that these two raise. Most publications were authored by psychiatrists and clinicians reflecting on their own practices, highlighting a predominance of clinically grounded perspectives and raising questions about the motivations for discussing suffering, mental illness, and AD within healthcare provision. The relative scarcity of contributions from those theorizing about the relationship (e.g., bioethicists, professors, and researchers) suggests that this conversation is informed primarily by clinical experience rather than theoretical frameworks. Our findings about specific SPMI diagnoses, notably SE-AN, further underscore the need for targeted research into how this relationship may vary across professional expertise, legislative context, and diagnosis.

The legal and cultural context in which the publication was written may have influenced how authors described the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD. For example, countries where psychiatric AD is legalized (e.g., Belgium, the Netherlands) may conceptualize the relationship within treatment options along a spectrum, whereas countries where it is illegal (e.g., US, Canada, Germany) may describe the relationship as standalone interventions, as one-or-the-other alternatives, or as justifications for sides of the legalization debates. Even within countries, legal frameworks are inconsistently applied (e.g., Ontario and Quebec, Canada). However, these publications can still be understood as contributing to an emerging international discourse about palliative psychiatry and AD.

The findings of this scoping review represent a first step in clarifying contested clinical conceptualizations regarding the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD. Our spectrum has implications for clinician and policymakers’ conceptual understandings about the relationship – namely, that it is heterogeneous across the literature and thus may require conceptual clarity.

Our findings suggest possible boundaries of palliative psychiatry and AD that may inform mental health clinicians’ understandings of the two when considering goals of care and treatment recommendations for patients with SPMI. For policymakers, our findings may deepen understandings about where current discussions about palliative psychiatry and AD lie on a spectrum. It is important to understand the current landscape when considering mental health policies that could impact the provision of palliative psychiatry and AD, including for jurisdictions currently debating legalization and expansion.

Limitations of this study include financial barriers to translation services. Seven reviewed publications written in a language other than English were translated using Microsoft Word’s free machine-translation tool. It is possible that some words were mistranslated or misinterpreted after translation. We examined the translated texts in their entirety to contextualize our reading of the terms and determine, to the best of our abilities, that the text met our criteria. Furthermore, we analyzed one quote from each publication. To avoid deducing generalizations from one quote, we contextualized the quotes in our extraction with the entire paper and returned to the full texts during writing to understand the perspective within the quote in its entirety.

Finally, palliative psychiatry is a small but growing field. Some authors of this review have also published on the topic of palliative psychiatry and received federal grant funding to study it. Author overlap with some of the included literature was, therefore, unavoidable. The review authors who have published on palliative psychiatry have noted its potential. At the time of writing, one author was involved on the MAiD policy committee at their institution, but palliative psychiatry was not a focus of this group. Consistent with PRISMA-ScR guidance on transparency and methodological rigour, we addressed potential bias by assigning study selection, data charting, and analysis to two independent reviewers, with all decisions guided by pre-established inclusion and exclusion criteria. We followed rigorous scoping review methods to support reproducibility and auditability of the process. In accordance with recommendations for reflexivity in evidence synthesis,67,68 the author group explicitly considered how their roles as scholars, students, clinicians, and individuals with lived experience of mental illness might influence interpretation about the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD.

To our knowledge, this scoping review is the first to map the literature examining the relationship between palliative psychiatry and AD. We found that discussions about the relationship exist along a spectrum from differentiated to equivalent. Seventy-eight percent of sources assert their differentiation. Palliative psychiatry is often described as rejecting the dichotomy of care-as-usual versus AD, proposing a third approach that limits coercive interventions and avoids a hastened death. Some publications viewed palliative psychiatry as ethically necessary in contexts wherein AD or inadequate care-as-usual are the only options, positioning palliative psychiatry as an alternative for individuals experiencing SPMI. Our findings suggest a recognized need for life-affirming alternatives to AD, with palliative psychiatry offering a potential care pathway that prioritizes quality of life and may supplant pathways that lead to death. We hope that future literature is expansive and considers a diversity of situated and social positions within these discussions, including individuals with lived experience of mental illnesses and of the systems that govern their care.
