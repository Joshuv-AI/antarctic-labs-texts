Effect of peri-interventional music on postictal agitation in electroconvulsive therapy patients (MUSE): protocol for an open-label multicentre randomised controlled trial in the Netherlands
Authors: Emilie S van der Valk Bouman, Melissa Ertman, Michel R Koopmans, Joris J B van der Vlugt-Molenaar, Willemijn T C J Heijnen, Jasmijn C van Groen, Jan-Wiebe Korstanje, Tom K Birkenhager, Markus Klimek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPostictal agitation (PIA) is a common adverse effect following electroconvulsive therapy (ECT). Current pharmacological treatments for PIA have undesirable side effects, and interventions to prevent PIA are unsatisfactory. The aim of this study is to assess the effect of peri-interventional music on PIA for patients undergoing ECT. Additionally, the study will assess the impact of music on pretreatment anxiety and post-treatment cognitive impairment.Methods and analysisThis multicentre, open-label, parallel randomised controlled trial (RCT) aims to include 92 patients from two centres in Rotterdam, the Netherlands. Participants will be randomised into two groups: a music intervention group and a control group. The music group listens to recorded music 30 min before and 12 min after each of the first six ECT sessions of the full ECT course, while the control group will receive standard care. The primary outcome is the presence of PIA, measured using the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS). Secondary outcomes include the severity and duration of PIA, pretreatment anxiety, recovery duration, peri-treatment medication requirements, cognitive impairment and depression severity. Data will be analysed according to an intention-to-treat principle.Ethics and disseminationThis study protocol has been approved by the Medical Ethical Review Committee of the Erasmus Medical Centre on 28 January 2025 (MEC-2024–0467) and subsequently received local approval at Antes Parnassia group. The trial will be carried out following the Declaration of Helsinki principles. Study results will be reported in a peer-reviewed journal according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines.Trial registration numberNCT06817330.Keywords: Randomized Controlled Trial, PSYCHIATRY, Adult psychiatry, Adult anaesthesia

Introduction

Postictal agitation (PIA) is a notable adverse effect occurring shortly after electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), a highly effective treatment for severe major depressive disorder. Characterised by symptoms such as anxiety, disorientation, clouded consciousness, motor agitation and sometimes violent behaviour, PIA can last up to an hour after ECT treatment.1 PIA-like behaviour can also be observed in patients recovering from general anaesthesia only and is termed emergence agitation (EA) in such scenarios.2 As ECT patients are recovering from the ECT-induced seizure as well as from general anaesthesia, effective interventions must consider factors such as incidence rates, influencing factors and underlying mechanisms of PIA. Although PIA incidence is estimated to be 12%–18%,3 the interchangeable use of terms such as delirium, interictal confusion and postictal restlessness in the literature complicates precise estimation. Therefore, PIA incidence may be higher, with some studies reporting rates as high as 62%.4 Furthermore, patients who have experienced PIA have an increased risk of recurrence, estimated to be 43%.5

Despite the prevalence and risks of PIA after ECT and EA after general anaesthesia, effective prevention and treatment options remain limited and inadequately standardised. PIA and EA are potentially dangerous adverse effects, as patients can harm themselves and others.6 Its presence can be a predictor of other ECT-related side effects, especially retrograde amnesia.7 Notably, up to 75% of ECT patients experience anxiety about the risk of treatment-related memory loss or impairment, a concerning statistic, given that pretreatment anxiety is a risk factor for PIA.18,10 Similarly, a significant proportion of patients experience anxiety in anticipation of anaesthesia or the ECT treatment itself.11 Although the exact neurophysiological mechanisms of PIA after an ECT-induced seizure remain unknown, proposed theories involve an imbalance in the recovery rates of excitatory and inhibitory brain processes, as well as between different brain structures.2 12 Proposed post-ECT prevention and treatment of PIA therefore includes the use of benzodiazepines or antipsychotics. However, antipsychotics may cause undesirable side effects,13 and benzodiazepines—being anticonvulsants—are less suitable for ECT procedures.14 Some pre-ECT preventive strategies include avoiding bilateral ECT, reducing pre-treatment anxiety and using dexmedetomidine.1 5 While benzodiazepine treatment could mitigate pretreatment anxiety, it should be avoided pretreatment since its use may raise the seizure threshold.15 Furthermore, the absence of a standardised clinical approach to managing ECT-related anxiety limits the options for preventing PIA via this pathway.16 17 Although Feenstra et al. reported a moderate effect of dexmedetomidine, a recent meta-analysis failed to identify significant prognostic factors to affect the likelihood of developing PIA.3 5 Therefore, there is a critical need for alternative prevention and (non-pharmacological) treatment strategies for PIA.

Apart from EA, PIA shares similarities with postoperative delirium, a condition often observed in patients recovering from surgery. Postoperative delirium is characterised by acute dysfunction of the brain, fluctuating consciousness, inattention and disorganised thinking, alongside hyperactive symptoms such as agitation and restlessness.18 Understanding these similarities may aid in developing comprehensive strategies to manage and prevent these conditions. One promising approach is the use of peri-interventional music listening. Various studies have shown that music interventions can significantly reduce preoperative anxiety, as well as the need for opioids in the context of surgery.19,22 Moreover, studies indicated that music exposure reduces delirium incidence compared with non-exposure in post-surgical and critically ill intensive care unit (ICU) patients,23,25 as well as reducing anxiety and stress in these contexts.26,28 The observed benefits of music-listening in surgical and ICU populations therefore warrant consideration in the ECT context, particularly whether it could also prevent or mitigate PIA as a delirium-like state. However, music is a strong stimulus and may also provoke adverse reactions, especially if the genre is disliked or linked to negative experiences.29 In ECT settings, replacing standard care elements such as verbal reassurance from nurses with recorded music could unintentionally reduce emotional support, which is important to take into account.30

Although its exact mechanisms of action are still unknown, music may reduce agitation through modulation of the autonomic nervous system (ANS) and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis—key systems involved in stress regulation. In delirium, elevated cortisol, sympathetic over-activation and neuroinflammatory processes are key contributors to its onset and severity.31 32 Music has been associated with increased parasympathetic activity via vagal stimulation, reduced cortisol levels and potential anti-inflammatory effects in various clinical contexts.2533,35 These mechanisms provide a neurobiological basis for music’s therapeutic potential in modulating stress-related pathways implicated in delirium—and may similarly apply to PIA in ECT.

While few studies have examined the effect of music during the peri-interventional period of ECT, the potential benefits of music for patients undergoing psychiatric therapies, including ECT, have been explored since the 1940s. Early investigations indicated that music, particularly familiar tunes, evoked memories and proved to be a useful tool in reducing the symptoms of psychiatric patients.36 Previous studies also showed that music could serve different functions for ECT patients.37 Furthermore, positive (psychological) effects of music on both patients and hospital staff during ECT treatment have been observed.38 Despite the observational nature of these studies, without quantification of patient responses or assessing outcomes in a systematic way, a more recent randomised controlled trial (RCT) by Graff et al. had similar findings.39 They investigated patient preferences for music played before ECT and demonstrated not only that a large majority of ECT patients are willing to listen to music, but also that music is generally well-received by ECT patients, is easy to implement and lacks the side effects typical of pharmacological treatments.39

Given the promising results and positive effects of music interventions in ECT and other related clinical contexts, we hypothesise that peri-interventional music listening may be a feasible and well-tolerated strategy for preventing PIA in patients undergoing ECT. As this approach has not yet been directly compared with standard care in the context of PIA prevention, we propose an RCT protocol to evaluate the effect of peri-interventional music on PIA in ECT patients. The study will also assess the impact of music on pretreatment anxiety and post-treatment cognitive impairment.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This is a multicentre, open label, parallel RCT with two arms, designed to evaluate the effect of a music-listening intervention (intervention group) on PIA as compared with standard preoperative care (control group). Figure 1 provides an overview of the study design. We aim to include a total of 92 patients from two centres: Maasstad Ziekenhuis in Rotterdam (Antes Parnassia group) and Erasmus University Medical Centre in Rotterdam (Erasmus MC), both located in the Netherlands. A list of participating study sites can be found in the patient informed consent forms (online supplemental file 1 and online supplemental file 2). Study coordination will take place from Erasmus MC. After screening, recruitment and informed consent, participants will be randomised by the research team to the music intervention or control group using a secure web-based, computerised randomisation system with concealed allocation in a 1:1 ratio. No stratification will be used during randomisation. Baseline measurement will take place within 7 days before the start of the ECT course. During the first six sessions of the ECT course, the music group will receive recorded music 30 min before and 12 min after each session. The control group receives standard care and will not receive music or wear headphones. Patients will be followed up for 90 days after completing the full ECT course. The study is scheduled to commence in September 2025 and is expected to conclude in February 2027. Eligibility, recruitment and consent

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All adult patients undergoing ECT treatment for major depression at Erasmus MC and Maasstad Ziekenhuis are eligible. In both centres, etomidate is used as the anaesthetic agent, to minimise confounding effects. Exclusion criteria include significant hearing impairment, severe neurological conditions that interfere with the ability to process music (as determined by the treating physician) and patients receiving ECT for diagnoses other than major depression (such as schizophrenia and catatonia). Recruitment and consent

The physicians performing ECT will identify eligible patients for participation in the study and inform the research team. A member of the research team will then approach the patient or their legal representative to introduce the research study and provide and explain participation in the trial as part of the informed consent process. Patients will be included in the study once their signed informed consent form is obtained, as completed by the patient or their legal representative. Sample informed consent forms translated into English from Dutch for both the patient and legal representative can be found in online supplemental file 1 and online supplemental file 2, respectively. Furthermore, flyers and posters for recruitment, approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee, will be provided to medical staff.

Sample size calculation

A systematic review by our team revealed significant variability in PIA incidence across countries, along with differing levels of bias in outcome measurement.3 Due to a lack of specific internal data, our sample size calculation was based on the PIA incidence rate of 25% reported in the only Dutch (cohort) study included in this review.40 Furthermore, this incidence rate aligns with the estimates of the research teams from the participating centres. While no trials have investigated the effect of music on PIA in ECT patients, other non-pharmacological interventions for postoperative delirium, a similar adverse effect, have shown a relative reduction of 36%–77%.41 42 A recent RCT on the effect of music on delirium after neurosurgery used a 60% relative reduction as its target.24 43 Therefore, we will consider the music intervention clinically relevant if it reduces PIA incidence by 60%, corresponding to an absolute reduction from 25% to 10%. For the sample size calculation, we considered the design as a cluster RCT,44 45 where patients are seen as the clusters, and music treatments as single observations. Data will be analysed at the ECT session level, accounting for within-patient correlation. Based on a PIA incidence of 25%, a power of 80%, a two-sided significant p value of less than 0.05 in a 1:1 randomisation, a cluster size of 6 with minimal variation allowed (0.1) and an intra-cluster correlation of 0.3, we determined a required sample size of 41 patients per group. We expect a loss to follow-up of 10%, and therefore will include 92 patients. With six ECT sessions per patient, we expect a total of 360 music treatments/observations.

Intervention

Participants in the music group will be provided with a tablet to choose music from Spotify playlists curated by the research team, consisting of the following genres: classical, Dutch popular, 60s–80s, 90s–10s, lofi/mellow, global, hip hop/R&B, electronic/dance, rock/metal, jazz/blues and religious music. In addition to the curated genre-based playlists, participants will also have the option to select their own preferred playlist on Spotify. Participants will be instructed to select music they believe will be most effective in helping them reduce anxiety and agitation. The music selection in the different genre-based playlists is based on music previously used in a RCT targeting delirium in the perioperative context,25 supplemented with expert input of a certified music therapist to ensure clinical relevance and safety. The music therapist’s input was leading in the composition of music playlists, to ensure appropriateness and safety of the intervention. While music is widely recognised as a promising tool for emotional regulation, there is currently no consensus on which specific genres or music characteristics are most effective for reducing anxiety or agitation—particularly in clinical populations. However, recent research suggests that self-selected music, especially when individuals retain control over their choices, may be particularly effective in managing anxiety.46 Moreover, neurobiological research indicates that individual music preferences can have a greater impact on emotional regulation than specific musical features or genres themselves.47 Our approach aligns with this by offering a broad range of music genres, which also is in line with Rentfrow’s five-factor model of musical preferences,48 enhancing the likelihood of resonating with personal music preferences and emotional needs across our heterogeneous patient population. Since patients in this study are severely depressed and vulnerable and whose decision-making is often impaired,49 a fully individualised approach for the music selection process is not feasible. Therefore, we have opted for balancing patient preference with structured pre-curated, recorded music playlists on Spotify. Participants may switch between playlists during the session, and these choices will be recorded.

Patients start with music listening in the pre-ECT holding area or ECT treatment room according to standard care, a typically quiet space where one or more members of the treatment and research team will be present to administer the music. In this room, patients select a playlist. They are also able to choose their desired volume, with a maximum volume of 80 dB to prevent hearing damage.50 Then, they will start listening to the music via headphones for 30 min before each ECT session. Studies on the optimal duration of music listening to achieve an effect are scarce, and earlier clinical studies into the effect of music on preprocedural anxiety used music listening interventions with variable durations between 15 and 40 min.51 52 However, in one RCT looking at music to reduce preoperative anxiety, stronger associations were found when using durations of 30 min over durations of 15-min interventions. Following this evidence, this study will use a duration of 30 min of music prior to ECT.

The headphones will then be removed and the music paused during ECT. Directly after ECT, the headphones will be placed back on while the patient is recovering and moved to the recovery room, a similarly quiet space where they are accompanied by one or more members of the treatment and research team. Music will then resume for another 12 min. This duration was chosen for feasibility within the ECT workflow and was the longest period comfortably possible without disrupting routine care. Both music listening durations were determined in consultation with psychiatry teams at both centres, researchers from the Music as Medicine group and the Medical Ethical Review Committee of the Erasmus MC Rotterdam.

This procedure will be repeated during each session. In cases where patients decline the music or headphones, the researcher may ask the patient to continue to listen, but when this is not possible, the researcher will make note of and record the duration of the discontinuation. In case a patient reacts adversely to the music and/or headphones, several safety measures are in place. Both anaesthesiologists and psychiatrists are present during the study procedures and can intervene if needed. If a patient experiences increased agitation or distress because of the music and/or headphones, emergency medication such as midazolam can be administered, following the same standard care protocols as for ‘standard’ agitation during ECT. This will be documented accordingly. No form of coercion will be employed. Patients can request to stop the music or remove the headphones at any time. If a patient becomes visibly agitated because of the music and/or headphones without explicitly requesting discontinuation, the music will be stopped and the headphones removed immediately. Such instances will be documented accordingly. The control group will receive standard care and will not listen to music or wear headphones. All participants will be asked to refrain from listening to music directly before or after ECT, except as part of the intervention period. Music listening at other times is permitted but not encouraged and should be reported in the pre-session questionnaire. Both groups will receive standard clinical care, with no treatments withheld. In order to ensure the music intervention is performed correctly, all interventionists (nurses, medical students and/or members of the research team) will be trained on the correct operating procedures in line with the protocol. This training will be documented in the training log and site delegation file. One interventionist performs the music intervention procedure for an individual patient at the individual ECT session.

Due to the nature of the intervention, patients cannot be blinded, as they are conscious and aware of whether they are listening to music. Outcome assessors will also not be blinded, as the control group receives standard care without headphones or music, which is visibly different from the intervention. This decision was made to keep the control group as close to standard care as possible and to preserve routine interpersonal care practices—such as verbal reassurance—which may be compromised when a patient is wearing headphones. These practices are considered an important component of standard care for this vulnerable population, as supported by expert opinion and existing literature.30

Outcome measurements

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of this study is the presence or absence of PIA. All patients will be screened for PIA during the first 30 min after the ECT session using the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS), a validated 10-point scale indicating patient alertness and responsiveness, and taking less than 20 s to perform.53 54 It has been used previously in ECT settings30 and has high inter-rater reliability.55 The RASS ranges from −5 (deep sedation) to +4 (extreme agitation). Positive scores indicate agitation relevant for diagnosing PIA, with +1 suggesting restlessness and +4 indicating violent behaviour. Screening for PIA will be conducted directly after each of the first six ECT sessions by a nurse or medical student, monitoring the patient for 30 min. PIA is defined as a RASS score of +1 or greater during this period. Patients scoring below +1 will be recorded as no PIA for that session. Nurses, medical students and/or members of the research team will receive RASS training from the research team or site psychiatrists to be documented in the training log and delegation file.

Assessed at the same time as the primary outcome, mild PIA is characterised by a RASS score of +1, while moderate and severe PIA are indicated by scores of ≥ +2. A RASS score of at least +2 is associated with a greater risk of danger to the patient and/or staff.56

Other study parameters

The following parameters will be assessed from the electronic patient records as part of standard care: patient baseline characteristics, including age, American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) classification, body mass index, education, sex, time since depression or bipolar disorder diagnosis and other medication use during the ECT course. The details of the ECT procedure will also be recorded, including electrode placement, stimulus dose, stimulus type (brief or ultra-brief pulse), seizure duration and the number of sessions.

Data management and statistical methods

Data will be managed using Castor EDC (Ciwit B.V., Amsterdam), a certified data capture tool that complies with all applicable laws and regulations and includes skips, validation checks and an audit trail. Information on coding, security and storage of data can be found in the online supplemental file 1 and online supplemental file 2, and additional data management procedures are outlined in the Data Management Plan for this study, available on request to the principal investigator. Statistical analysis will be conducted based on the intention-to-treat principle, analysing patients according to their assigned treatment arm regardless of the treatment received. Patient retention will be promoted by providing the patient clear study information before the start of the study (both verbal and written), conducting live assessments and intervention initiation by a dedicated research team member per patient, a friendly reminder by phone before the out-of-ward follow-up appointment 90 days post-ECT course and using standard care questionnaires where possible to minimise patient burden. In the case of patient withdrawal, all data collected are to be included in analyses up until the moment of discontinuation. The primary endpoint is the occurrence of a RASS score of +1 or higher for each of the first six ECT sessions, analysed using a logistic mixed-effects model. This model accounts for multiple treatments per patient by including treatment arm as a fixed effect and patient ID as a random effect. A two-sided p value of 0.05 or less will be considered statistically significant.

Secondary outcomes, such as the presence and severity of PIA, time to reorientation, pretreatment anxiety, music listening behaviour, duration of recovery, peri-treatment medication requirements, cognitive impairment and severity of depression, will be analysed using linear, logistic and ordinal mixed-effects models, as appropriate. These models will include treatment arm as a fixed effect and subject ID as a random effect, with residuals evaluated for normality and homoscedasticity. Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise population characteristics for each treatment arm, including medians and IQRs for continuous variables and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. Future exploratory risk stratification may involve multivariable logistic regression analysis, where multiple independent variables (eg, baseline characteristics, disease-specific factors and ECT treatment details) will be considered simultaneously to predict the presence of PIA in each subject. The final dataset will be available only for the research team or on reasonable request to the principal investigator. Data analysis will be performed using R (R Core Team (2023)) V.4.3 or higher.

Changes to the protocol

In the development of this study, the protocol was amended after initial approval by the Medical Ethical Review Committee (MERC). No changes were made to the study design itself, but a modification concerning the control group was submitted. In the initial study protocol, participants of the control group were instructed to wear noise-cancelling headphones without music. However, during preparations for the study, the clinical staff emphasised the importance of interaction between the patient and the healthcare personnel before undergoing ECT. With short and comforting interactions with the clinical staff, possible anxiety was intended to be reduced. Concerns were raised on the effect of absolute silence in the control group, possibly increasing anxiety and moreover preventing patients from these comforting interactions. Therefore, the use of noise-cancelling headphones without music was considered substandard care, and it was decided to relieve the control group from wearing headphones in general, replacing the control group to standard care with no music. The amendment was resubmitted and subsequently approved by the MERC.

Potential limitations

This study has several potential limitations to take into account. First, this is a multicentre trial, with potential differences in standard ECT care per participating centre which could introduce variability in the outcome measurements. However, all participating centres are in the same city and country, following the same national guidelines. They also all use the same anaesthetic agent, etomidate, minimising confounding effects. A second limitation is the lack of blinding. Patients cannot be blinded due to the nature of the intervention, as they are conscious and aware of whether they are listening to music. Outcome assessors will also not be blinded, since the control group receives standard care without headphones or music, making the intervention visibly distinct. This choice preserves routine interpersonal care practices—such as verbal reassurance—which are considered essential for this vulnerable population. However, the absence of blinding may introduce bias and should be acknowledged as a limitation of the study. Similar challenges have been acknowledged in previous RCTs investigating perioperative music interventions, where blinding of patients or outcome assessors was impossible for feasibility reasons.24 66 Third, individual differences in music preference and emotional response to music may influence outcomes, despite the use of a broad range of genre-based curated playlists and the option to self-select a playlist, designed to balance freedom of choice with structure for this vulnerable patient group in which decision-making is often impaired.

Ethics and dissemination

The study protocol received ethical approval from the MERC of the Erasmus Medical Centre (V.1.1, MEC-2024–0467) on 28 January 2025 and subsequently received local approval at Antes Parnassia group Rotterdam, before the start of the study. Patient retention will be promoted by providing the patient clear study information before the start of the study (both verbal and written), conducting live assessments and intervention initiation by a dedicated research team member per patient, a friendly reminder by phone before the out-of-ward follow-up appointment 90 days post-ECT course and using standard care questionnaires where possible to minimise patient burden. If applicable, any protocol amendments will be submitted to the MERC and local research committees of participating centres. This clinical trial will be conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (64th WMA General Assembly, Fortaleza, Brazil, October 2013) and in accordance with the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act. The trial was registered in the National Institute of Health on February 10, 2025 (www.clinicaltrials.gov, ID: NCT06817330).

All participants will be asked for written informed consent by the research team. Participants will receive no (financial) compensation. Since participants will have no increased risks, the MERC of Erasmus MC has given dispensation from statutory obligation to provide insurance for subjects participating in medical research (article 7, subsection 6 of the WMO and Medical Research (Human Subjects) Compulsory Insurance Decree of 23 June 2003). Because of the negligible risks, the study has been exempted from forming a data monitoring committee by the MERC of the Erasmus MC. No interim analysis or audits are required for the same reason. The MERC has also exempted this study from registering adverse events or serious adverse events because the music intervention cannot be deemed connected to any such event taking place. For the same reason, no data safety monitoring board is installed. Confidentiality will be maintained through secure (password-controlled) storage of data as described in the Informed Consent Form (online supplemental file 1 and online supplemental file 2) and Data Management Plan, as well as will follow the requirements outlined in the General Data Protection Regulation.

This study protocol (V.1.2, 21 November 2024) followed the SPIRIT (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials) guideline67 and the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines for non-pharmacological treatments.68

Ancillary and post-trial care

Due to the nature of the study, no provisions for ancillary or post-trial care are made.

Dissemination policy

The investigators are thoroughly committed to publishing the study results in a peer-reviewed scientific journal. Results will be published in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines.69 The principal investigator and research team will have access to the password-protected final dataset. Access to the Dutch study protocol and data collection forms, final trial participant dataset and statistical code will be available upon request to the principal investigator.
