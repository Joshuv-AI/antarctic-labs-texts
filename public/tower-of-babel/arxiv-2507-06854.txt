Proof-Theoretic Functional Completeness for the Connexive Logic C
Sara Ayhan & Hrafn Oddsson

Abstract
We show the functional completeness for the connectives of the non-trivial negation inconsistent logic C by using a well-established method implementing purely proof-theoretic notions only. Firstly, given that C contains a strong negation, expressing a notion of direct refutation, the proof needs to be applied in a bilateralist way in that not only higher-order rule schemata for proofs but also for refutations need to be considered. Secondly, given that C is a connexive logic we need to take a connexive understanding of inference as a basis, leading to a different conception of (higher-order) refutation than is usually employed. Keywords : Functional completeness, Bilateralism, Connexive logic, Proof-theoretic semantics, Refutations, Higher-order derivability

Proof-theoretic semantics is the view that it is the rules of logical connectives governing their use in a proof system that determine their meaning.

In this paper von Kutschera is concerned with giving a concept of refutation that is -- other than in intuitionistic logic -- not derivative on a concept of proof but on a par with it.
This is a very bilateralist stance, since bilateralism is the view that in proof-theoretic semantics it is not only the rules governing proofs of complex formulas but also the rules governing their refutations that need to be considered as meaning-determining and, importantly, that these concepts should be considered both as primitive, not one reducible to the other.

The proof of functional completeness for

There is one important conceptual point worth mentioning, though.
When discussing under what conditions we can give rules for refutations of arbitrary connectives, von Kutschera says that “[o]bviously, these rules [for refutation] cannot be devised independently of [the rules for proofs] if the consistency of the Gentzen calculi is to be maintained when these rules are added”
[
17
, p. 108, (translated by the authors)]

What he fails to mention in this context is that our conception of how precisely to refute inferences plays a crucial role here.
We also give the refutation rules dependently on the proof rules in the same manner as it is done in
[
17

and we do get the completeness result for the
inconsistent
logic
C
.
So, in light of our adjustments of von Kutschera’s approach for
C
(Section 4), the dependence of the refutation rules on the proof rules seems to be an independent matter, separate from any considerations about consistency.
It is rather the underlying notion of refutation that is important here and with respect to
that
our approaches are obviously essentially different.
While von Kutschera (without any further explanation or justification) assumes a notion of refutation that captures the, in many logics, usual way of interpreting a negated implication, i.e., the refutability of a derivation going from
A
to
B
is defined by the provability of
A
and the refutability of
B
, our notion will capture the connexive understanding of implication.
Thus, the refutability of a derivation going from
A
to
B
will be defined by the refutability of
B
given the assumed provability of
A
.
So, it is rather von Kutschera’s underlying assumption that derivability should basically behave as implication does in

that is critical for consistency, not the factor of dependent definitions between proofs and refutations.

Higher-Order Derivability

Now we want to define the notion of derivability for
R
-expressions of arbitrary
R
-degree.
Therefore, we define a calculus in which not only sequents but
R
-expressions of arbitrary degree are allowed.
The following structural rules hold:

S\Rightarrow S

\Delta,T\Rightarrow S\Delta\Rightarrow S

\Delta,T,S,\Gamma\Rightarrow U\Delta,S,T,\Gamma\Rightarrow U

\Delta,S\Rightarrow T\Delta,S,S\Rightarrow T

\Delta\Rightarrow T\Delta\Rightarrow S\qquad\Delta,S\Rightarrow T

We add two rules, which tell us how to introduce an
R
-expression of arbitrary degree on the right and on the left side of

\Gamma\Rightarrow(\Delta\Rightarrow S)\Gamma,\Delta\Rightarrow S

\Gamma,(\Delta\Rightarrow S)\Rightarrow T\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta\quad\quad\Gamma,S\Rightarrow T

The expression

In line with our connexive understanding of inference, we define the refutability of

To formulate this as one rule, we can fix that

Then we have the following rule:

\Delta\Rightarrow-(\Gamma\Rightarrow S)\Delta,\Gamma\Rightarrow-S

If the reverse is demanded to hold as well, i.e.:

then we also obtain a condition on how to derive something
from

R

\Delta,-(\Gamma\Rightarrow S)\Rightarrow T\Delta\Rightarrow\Gamma\quad\quad\Delta,-S\Rightarrow T

We will write

Now that we have our deductive framework, let us briefly outline the remainder of the paper.
First, we adopt von Kutschera’s generalized introduction rules for arbitrary connectives.
We then give the introduction rules for the usual connectives for

and follow von Kutschera’s argument step by step to obtain functional completeness for

Finally, we provide a way to translate this framework into a standard sequent calculus of

6
Functional completeness

The problem of functional completeness for a given logic
\mathscr{L}
involves identifying a set
\Theta
of logical connectives of
\mathscr{L}
such that every logical connective of
\mathscr{L}
can be explicitly defined through a finite number of compositions using elements of
\Theta
.
We will show that the system of operators
\{{\sim},\wedge,\vee,\rightarrow\}
of
C
is functionally complete.

Following
[
17
]
, we start by noting that we can express the structural symbols of our system in terms of the connectives from
\{{\sim},\wedge,\vee,\rightarrow\}
of
C
in the following way.

For every
R
-expression
S
we have a mapping onto a formula

according to the following rules:

1.) If
S
is a formula, then
\overline{S}=S
.

2.)
\overline{-S}={\sim}\overline{S}
.

3.) If
\Delta
is the series of
R

, then
\overline{\Delta}=\overline{S_{1}},...,\overline{S_{n}}

\overline{\overline{\Delta}}=\overline{S_{1}}\wedge...\wedge\overline{S_{n}}
.

4.)
\overline{\Delta\Rightarrow S}=\overline{\overline{\Delta}}\rightarrow\overline{S}
.

5.)
\overline{\Rightarrow S}=\overline{S}
.

Then the following holds:

Theorem 6.1

\vdash\overline{S}\Leftrightarrow^{s}S
.

The proof is a simple induction on the complexity of

, p. 109f.]

\vdash F(A_{1},...,A_{n})\Leftrightarrow^{s}(\overline{S_{11}}\wedge...\wedge\overline{S_{1s_{1}}})\vee...\vee(\overline{S_{t1}}\wedge...\wedge\overline{S_{ts_{t}}}).

can be replaced in all contexts by the formula
(\overline{S_{11}}\wedge...\wedge\overline{S_{1s_{1}}})\vee...\vee(\overline{S_{t1}}\wedge...\wedge\overline{S_{ts_{t}}})

and the connectives

.

is an initial sequent, i.e., of the form
T\Rightarrow T
, then
\Delta^{*}\Rightarrow S^{*}
is of the form
T^{*}\Rightarrow T^{*}
,
which is easily derivable in
G3C
.
The induction step for the remaining structural rules (

,
PL
,
CL
,
Cut
) is trivial.

If the last step of the derivation is an application of
RI^{+}
, then
\Delta\Rightarrow S
is of the form
\Gamma\Rightarrow(\Sigma\Rightarrow T)
and
\vdash_{\texttt{SC}_{\infty}}\Gamma,\Sigma\Rightarrow T
.
By the induction hypothesis, we have that
\vdash_{\texttt{G3C}}\Gamma^{*},\Sigma^{*}\Rightarrow T^{*}
. Now,
\Delta^{*}\Rightarrow S^{*}
is
\Gamma^{*}\Rightarrow(\Sigma^{*}\rightarrow T^{*})
which gives
\vdash_{\texttt{G3C}}\Delta^{*}\Rightarrow S^{*}
.
A similar argument applies to
LI^{+}
,
RI^{-}
, and
LI^{-}
.

Now, suppose that the last step is an application of the rule (I).
Then
\Delta\Rightarrow S
is of the form
\Delta\Rightarrow F(A_{1},...,A_{n})
and
\vdash_{\texttt{SC}_{\infty}}\Delta\Rightarrow S_{i1}
, and … , and
\vdash_{\texttt{SC}_{\infty}}\Delta\Rightarrow S_{is_{i}}
for some
i\leq t
.
By the induction hypothesis,
\vdash_{\texttt{G3C}}\Delta^{*}\Rightarrow S^{*}_{i1}
, and … , and
\vdash_{\texttt{G3C}}\Delta^{*}\Rightarrow S^{*}_{is_{i}}
.
So,
\vdash_{\texttt{G3C}}\Delta^{*}\Rightarrow S^{*}_{i1}\wedge...\wedge S^{*}_{is_{i}}
and therefore
\vdash_{\texttt{G3C}}\Delta^{*}\Rightarrow(F(A_{1},...,A_{n}))^{*}
.
Similar arguments apply to (II), (III), and (IV).

∎

Theorem 6.3

If
\Gamma\cup\{A\}
is a set of formulas containing only connectives from
\{\wedge,\vee,\rightarrow,{\sim}\}
, then

\vdash_{\texttt{SC}_{\infty}}\Delta\Rightarrow A\text{ iff }\vdash_{\texttt{G3C}}\Delta\Rightarrow A.

Proof.

We have
\vdash_{\texttt{G3C}}\Delta\Rightarrow A
iff
\vdash_{\texttt{SC}_{\infty}}\Delta^{*}\Rightarrow A^{*}
iff
\vdash_{\texttt{SC}_{\infty}}\Delta\Rightarrow A.

∎

7
Conclusion

We used a purely proof-theoretical method of showing functional completeness for the connectives of the connexive logic
C
.
This is a desirable approach from a perspective in the spirit of proof-theoretic semantics, which demonstrates that model-theoretic notions are not needed to determine the meaning of logical connectives.
Furthermore, by considering not only proof but also refutation rule schemata for connectives our approach follows the bilateralist principles of treating proofs and refutations on a par.
The essential difference from similar results for other systems has its source in the connexive understanding of inferences, leading to a different understanding of (meta-)refutation than is usually taken for granted.
With this understanding, though, and according adjustments, we can get the same completeness result for
C
, i.e., a non-trivial negation inconsistent logic, as we get for other ‘well-behaved’ systems.
Thus, this paper provides a contribution to furthering the understanding and appreciation of the system
C
and of connexive logics in general by bringing in a bilateralist proof-theoretic semantics perspective.
Also, it shows that having a dependency between definitions of proofs and definitions of refutations is not the key criterion for consistency of a system.
Rather, our background assumptions on how refutation of inferences work are decisive here, namely whether we assume a ‘classical’ or a connexive understanding.
