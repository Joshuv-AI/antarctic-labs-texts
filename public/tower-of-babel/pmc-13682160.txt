Comparative evaluation of Mannheim Peritonitis Index and Mansoura Scoring System for risk stratification in perforation peritonitis: a prospective study from a resource-limited setting of North India
Authors: Hazique Jameel, Mohammad Habib Raza, Afzal Anees, Mohammad Sadik Akhtar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerforation peritonitis is one of the most common surgical emergencies encountered in clinical practice and is associated with considerable morbidity and mortality. Prognostic scoring systems are important for predicting outcomes, guiding management strategies and facilitating surgical audits. The Mannheim Peritonitis Index (MPI) is a widely used intraoperative scoring system, while the Mansoura Scoring System (MSS) is a newer model based on preoperative inflammatory parameters. The aim of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of MPI and MSS in predicting mortality and postoperative complications in patients with perforation peritonitis.Materials and methodsA prospective observational cohort study was conducted in the Department of Surgery at Jawaharlal Nehru Medical College, Aligarh Muslim University. A total of 200 patients diagnosed with perforation peritonitis and undergoing surgical intervention were included. MPI and MSS scores were calculated for all patients. Patients were followed up for 30 days postoperatively to evaluate outcomes including mortality, postoperative complications and hospital stay. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 29. Diagnostic performance of the scoring systems was evaluated using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsThe mean age of patients ranged widely, with the highest proportion belonging to the 31–40 year age group. Males constituted 69.5% of the study population. The ileum was the most common site of perforation (56.5%), followed by gastric perforation (15%). Higher MPI and MSS scores were significantly associated with increased postoperative morbidity and mortality. MPI demonstrated higher specificity in predicting mortality, whereas MSS showed comparable sensitivity and allowed earlier preoperative risk assessment.ConclusionBoth MPI and MSS are effective prognostic tools for predicting outcomes in perforation peritonitis. While MPI has a better discriminatory ability for predicting mortality due to its incorporation of intraoperative findings, MSS offers the advantage of early preoperative risk stratification using simple laboratory parameters.Keywords: Perforation peritonitis, Mannheim Peritonitis Index, Mansoura Scoring System, Mortality prediction, Surgical outcomes

Introduction

Perforation peritonitis is a life-threatening surgical emergency caused by perforation of a hollow viscus, resulting in contamination of the peritoneal cavity with gastrointestinal contents. Despite major advances in surgical techniques, antimicrobial therapy and critical care management, perforation peritonitis continues to be associated with high morbidity and mortality, particularly in developing countries. Early diagnosis and prompt surgical intervention remain crucial for improving outcomes in these patients [1].

The etiology and clinical spectrum of perforation peritonitis vary significantly across geographic regions. In developing countries, gastroduodenal and small bowel perforations secondary to peptic ulcer disease, enteric fever, tuberculosis and appendicitis are commonly encountered, whereas colonic perforations due to malignancy, diverticulitis, or ischemia are more frequently reported in developed nations [2].

Accurate prognostic assessment plays an important role in patients presenting with perforation peritonitis. It assists surgeons in clinical decision-making, helps in prioritizing intensive care resources and enables early identification of high-risk patients who may require aggressive management. Several prognostic scoring systems have been developed to predict mortality and morbidity in patients with peritonitis, including the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), Physiological and Operative Severity Score for the Enumeration of Mortality and Morbidity (POSSUM), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, and the Mannheim Peritonitis Index (MPI) [3].

Among these, the Mannheim Peritonitis Index (MPI), introduced by Wacha and Linder in 1987, is one of the most widely used and validated scoring systems for predicting the prognosis of patients with peritonitis. It incorporates clinical and intraoperative variables such as age, organ failure, duration of peritonitis and the nature of peritoneal exudate to stratify mortality risk [4]. Several studies have demonstrated that MPI correlates well with patient outcomes and remains a useful prognostic tool in surgical practice [5].

However, a major limitation of the Mannheim Peritonitis Index is its reliance on intraoperative findings, which restricts its applicability for early preoperative risk stratification. In recent years, attention has shifted toward prognostic tools based on readily available laboratory parameters that allow risk prediction before surgical intervention [6].

The Mansoura Scoring System is a recently proposed prognostic tool that utilizes preoperative variables such as age, mean platelet volume (MPV), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and C-reactive protein (CRP). These parameters are easily obtained from routine laboratory investigations and allow early risk stratification in patients with peritonitis [7].

In view of the importance of early prognostic assessment in perforation peritonitis, the present study was undertaken to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the Mannheim Peritonitis Index and the Mansoura Scoring System in patients presenting with perforation peritonitis and contrast their clinical utility at different stages of management.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This prospective observational study was conducted in the Department of Surgery, Jawaharlal Nehru Medical College, Aligarh Muslim University, India, from February 2024 to March 2026.

Study population

A total of 200 patients diagnosed with perforation peritonitis and undergoing surgical intervention were included in the study based on feasibility and institutional requirements. Ethical clearance was granted by the institutional board and written informed consent was obtained from all patients after explaining the voluntary nature of participation and strict confidentiality of patient data.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of the study was 30-day postoperative mortality. Secondary outcomes included postoperative complications such as surgical site infection, ventilatory complications, cardiac events, burst abdomen and duration of ICU stay. The predictive performance of the Mannheim Peritonitis Index (MPI) and Mansoura Scoring System (MSS) was evaluated for both primary and secondary outcomes.

Management protocol

Patients reporting to the Emergency Department of JNMCH with history of acute abdomen are examined. A complete history and thorough clinical examination is done to ascertain the clinical diagnosis of perforation peritonitis. Resuscitative Measures are initiated alongside Clinical assessment. Vitally stable patients are shifted to the Emergency X-ray Room for Chest X-ray and X-ray Abdomen Erect and Supine. USG Abdomen/CECT-Whole abdomen may be done according to the patients’ stability and diagnostic dilemma.

After the Clinical and Radiological Assessment, if the diagnosis of perforation peritonitis is made, informed consent is taken for inclusion in the study and necessary surgical intervention is planned. All the relevant investigations for the Mannheim Peritonitis Index and Mansoura Scoring System are done after obtaining the informed consent. Routine post-operative care is provided. Empirical broad spectrum antibiotics are started and later changed based on the culture and sensitivity reports. Patients are discharged when they are able to accept oral feeds, have adequate pain control and without any undue local and systemic complications. They are followed up for a period of 30 days for any complications.

Data collection

Detailed demographic data, clinical findings, laboratory parameters, intraoperative findings and postoperative outcomes were recorded. Patients were followed for 30 days postoperatively to document complications and mortality.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 29. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Normality of continuous data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Comparisons between categorical variables were performed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where expected cell counts were < 5. Continuous variables were compared using the Independent samples t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data.

The association between clinical variables, scoring systems (MPI and MSS) and post-operative outcomes (mortality and morbidity) was assessed using univariate analysis, followed by multivariate logistic regression analysis to identify independent predictors of adverse outcomes. To improve model stability and avoid structural multicollinearity, separate multivariate logistic regression models were constructed. Composite scoring systems (MPI and MSS) were analyzed independently of their component physiological variables. The number of predictors in each model was restricted to maintain an acceptable events-per-variable ratio.The diagnostic performance of the Mannheim Peritonitis Index and Mansoura Scoring System for predicting mortality and postoperative complications was evaluated using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index. Literature-supported thresholds were retained for clinical interpretability. Comparison of ROC curves was performed using the DeLong method for correlated curves. The study was not blinded, which may introduce observer bias; however, treatment decisions were not influenced by study scoring systems. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated for selected cut-off scores.

Discussion

Perforation peritonitis continues to represent a major surgical emergency with significant morbidity and mortality, particularly in developing countries. The present study evaluated the clinical profile of patients with perforation peritonitis and compared the prognostic performance of the Mannheim Peritonitis Index (MPI) and Mansoura Scoring System (MSS) in predicting postoperative outcomes.

Age distribution

In the present study, majority of patients belonged to the young and middle-aged adult population, with peak incidence observed in the 31–40 year age group (25%), followed by the 21–30 year age group (22.5%). This age distribution is consistent with findings reported in Indian studies by Jhobta et al. [1], Dorairajan et al. [8] and Sharma et al. [9], which documented the highest incidence of perforation peritonitis in patients aged 21–40 years.

In contrast, studies from Western countries have reported a higher incidence among elderly populations. This difference in age distribution is largely attributed to varying etiological patterns.

Sex distribution

A clear male predominance was observed in the present study, with males constituting 69.5% of the study population. This finding is consistent with most Indian studies, which report male predominance ranging between 65% and 80%. Jhobta et al.¹ reported a male-to-female ratio of 3.2:1, similar to the trend observed in our study. Several factors may contribute to this predominance, including higher prevalence of smoking, alcohol consumption and occupational exposure among males, which increase the risk of gastrointestinal pathologies leading to perforation.

Most common site of perforation

The ileum was the most common site of perforation (56.5%) in the present study. Similar findings have been reported in Indian studies by Jhobta et al. [1] and Dorairajan et al. [8], which documented ileal perforation in 40–60% of cases. These findings reflect the epidemiological pattern of developing countries where infectious etiologies such as enteric fever and intestinal tuberculosis remain prevalent causes of small bowel perforation. In contrast, Western literature reports colonic and gastric perforations as more common sites due to higher incidence of diverticular disease, malignancy and ischemic colitis.

The stomach was the second most common site of perforation (15%) in the present study. Older Indian studies reported a higher proportion of gastric and duodenal perforations. However, recent studies such as those by Sharma et al. [9] and Thorsen et al. [10] have documented a declining trend in peptic ulcer perforations.

Colonic perforations, although relatively uncommon in our study, were associated with significantly higher morbidity and mortality. Western studies by Biondo et al. [11] and Teixeira et al. [12] have reported colonic perforations as common surgical emergencies with mortality rates ranging from 20% to 40%.

Etiology of perforation

In the present study, intestinal tuberculosis was the most common etiology (38.5%), followed by enteric fever (22.5%) and peptic ulcer disease (19%).

Indian studies by Jhobta et al. [1], Dorairajan et al. [8], and Kapoor et al. [13] have reported tuberculosis as the underlying cause in 30–45% of perforation cases. In contrast, tuberculosis is rarely encountered as a cause of perforation in Western countries.

Enteric fever was the second most common cause in the present study. Typhoid ileal perforation is widely reported in South Asian literature and typically affects young adults, as described by Jhobta et al. [1] and Eggleston et al. [14]. Peptic ulcer disease accounted for 19% of cases in our study. Earlier Indian studies reported higher proportions of peptic ulcer perforation. However, recent studies have demonstrated a declining incidence due to improved medical therapy and eradication of H. pylori infection, as noted by Svanes et al. [15].

MPI score distribution

In the present study, 60% of patients had MPI scores ≥ 22, while 24.5% had MPI scores ≥ 30, placing them in the high-risk category.

These findings are comparable to studies by Sharma et al. [9] and Notash et al. [16], which reported that a large proportion of patients in developing countries present with moderate to severe MPI scores, largely due to delayed presentation and advanced disease at the time of diagnosis as shown by Kologlu et al. [17]

MSS score distribution

The majority of patients in our study had MSS scores ≥ 3, with score 4 being the most common category. Literature evaluating MSS remains limited; however, Abdel-Razik et al. [7] reported similar clustering of higher MSS scores in patients with diffuse peritonitis and systemic inflammatory response.

Optimal MPI cut-off value

The optimal MPI cut-off value for predicting mortality in the present study was ≥ 29, corresponding to the highest Youden index. Previous studies by Wacha et al. [4] and Billing et al. [5] have also recommended MPI values between 26 and 29 as thresholds for identifying high-risk patients.

Optimal MSS cut-off value

The optimal MSS cut-off value in our study was ≥ 3, which demonstrated higher sensitivity in predicting adverse outcomes. Studies evaluating the Mansoura scoring system and other sepsis-based scoring models have similarly suggested lower cut-off values to maximize sensitivity, enabling early identification of high-risk patients.

Comparison between MPI and MSS

In the present study, MPI demonstrated higher specificity, whereas MSS showed higher sensitivity and negative predictive value.

This finding suggests that MSS may have limited granularity for nuanced risk stratification. However, its strength lies in early preoperative identification of high-risk patients using simple laboratory parameters. In contrast, the Mannheim Peritonitis Index demonstrated a more graded distribution of risk, reflecting its incorporation of intraoperative findings. This trade-off between sensitivity and specificity has been observed in several studies evaluating risk stratification scores in surgical patients. Although the AUC values were numerically similar, formal comparison using the DeLong test showed no statistically significant difference between the two models. The performance of MPI and MSS observed in this study may not be directly applicable to settings with different epidemiological profiles.

Multivariate analysis for mortality prediction

The score-based model confirmed that both MPI and MSS are independently associated with mortality, reflecting their validity as prognostic tools. In contrast, the physiological model demonstrated that preoperative shock and renal dysfunction are strong clinical determinants of adverse outcomes.

This distinction highlights that composite scores and physiological variables capture different yet complementary dimensions of disease severity as highlighted by Singh SR et al. [18] and Khan S et al. [19]

Site of perforation and postoperative complications

In the present study, ileal and colonic perforations were associated with significantly higher postoperative complications, including wound infections, respiratory complications, cardiac failure and prolonged ICU stay.

This observation is consistent with studies by Jhobta et al. [1] and Biondo et al. [11], which reported increased morbidity in patients with distal bowel perforations. The high bacterial load and severe fecal contamination associated with distal gastrointestinal perforations contribute to increased risk of sepsis and postoperative complications.

MPI and postoperative complications

The mean MPI score exceeded 25 in most patients with postoperative complications, indicating a strong association between higher MPI scores and increased morbidity. Previous studies by Wacha et al. [4] Billing et al. [5] Gupta S et al. [20] demonstrated a sharp rise in morbidity and mortality when MPI scores exceed 26, supporting the findings of the present study.

MSS and postoperative complications

Higher MSS scores were observed in patients with systemic complications, including sepsis and organ dysfunction requiring intensive care support. MSS incorporates inflammatory markers such as CRP and NLR, which reflect the systemic inflammatory response and therefore correlate well with septic complications. Previous studies evaluating sepsis-based prognostic scores have similarly reported higher scores in patients with organ dysfunction and ICU admission.

Conclusion

Both the Mannheim Peritonitis Index and Mansoura Scoring System demonstrated acceptable predictive performance in assessing mortality risk in perforation peritonitis.

However, these findings should be interpreted in the context of a single-center study with region-specific etiological patterns and their generalizability to other populations may be limited. Further multicenter studies are required to validate these results across diverse clinical settings.

Limitations

This was a single-center study conducted in a resource-limited setting and region-specific etiological spectrum may limit the external validity of the findings. The sample size, although adequate, may not be sufficient for detailed subgroup analyses. Additionally, the follow-up period was limited to 30 days, precluding assessment of long-term outcomes and late complications. Selection bias may have been introduced as only patients undergoing surgical intervention were included, while those who expired prior to surgery were excluded. Furthermore, variations in surgical technique and perioperative management could have influenced patient outcomes. The study was not blinded, which may introduce observer bias; however treatment decisions were not influenced by study scoring systems. Lastly, although the Mansoura Scoring System demonstrated promising results, it remains relatively new and requires further external validation across diverse clinical settings.
