A Case Report of Tubal Pregnancy Complicated with Partial Hydatidiform Mole
Authors: Ning Wu, Yuting Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPartial hydatidiform mole (PHM) is a rare pathological subtype of gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD). Tubal pregnancy complicated with PHM is an extremely rare clinical condition with atypical preoperative manifestations, which easily leads to clinical misdiagnosis. Definitive diagnosis primarily relies on intraoperative exploration combined with postoperative histopathological and immunohistochemical verification. This study reports a case of tubal PHM in a 31-year-old nulliparous female presenting with 54 days of amenorrhea and persistent lower abdominal distending pain. Preoperative examinations revealed markedly elevated serum β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) levels and a unilateral adnexal mixed mass. The patient underwent emergency laparoscopic left salpingectomy, and the final diagnosis of tubal PHM was confirmed by postoperative pathological examination. The patient achieved uneventful postoperative recovery, with serum β-hCG declining steadily in a logarithmic pattern and no metastatic lesions identified during follow-up. This article systematically elaborates the clinical manifestations, pathological features, diagnostic challenges, individualized treatment strategies, and standardized follow-up principles of tubal PHM, aiming to improve clinicians’ awareness of differential diagnosis and standardized management of this rare disease, and provide a credible clinical reference for the diagnosis and treatment of rare ectopic gestational trophoblastic lesions.Keywords: tubal pregnancy, partial hydatidiform mole, ectopic pregnancy, gestational trophoblastic disease

Introduction

Gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD) refers to a group of heterogeneous disorders caused by abnormal proliferation and differentiation of placental trophoblast cells, mainly including complete hydatidiform mole (CHM), partial hydatidiform mole (PHM), invasive hydatidiform mole, choriocarcinoma, and placental site trophoblastic tumor. As a benign subtype of GTD, hydatidiform mole is pathologically characterized by trophoblastic hyperplasia, stromal edema, and vesicular degeneration of placental villi, and is classified into CHM and PHM according to villous morphological features and the presence or absence of embryonic tissue.1

The early clinical manifestations of hydatidiform mole lack specificity, overlapping significantly with those of normal early intrauterine pregnancy and multiple gestation, which frequently causes preoperative missed diagnosis or misdiagnosis.2 Delayed clinical intervention may trigger severe complications including acute pelvic hemorrhage and secondary pelvic infection, threatening maternal safety.3 Although histologically benign, hydatidiform mole carries a malignant transformation potential: 10–20% of complete hydatidiform mole (CHM) and 1–5% of PHM cases may progress to invasive gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN) with metastatic capacity.3 Therefore, standardized postoperative follow-up and timely targeted intervention are essential to ensure favorable patient prognosis.4

Hydatidiform mole predominantly occurs in intrauterine pregnancies, while ectopic hydatidiform mole implanted in the fallopian tube, ovary, or broad ligament is extremely rare.5 Epidemiological data indicate that fewer than 150 confirmed tubal PHM cases have been reported globally to date, rendering it an ultra-rare GTD subtype.2,6 The overall incidence of ectopic molar pregnancy is approximately 1.5 per 1,000,000 pregnancies, and existing clinical data are mainly derived from sporadic case reports without systematic summary and analysis.3,6 Tubal PHM shares highly consistent clinical and ultrasonographic features with conventional tubal ectopic pregnancy, resulting in an extremely high preoperative misdiagnosis rate.2 Definitive diagnosis depends primarily on postoperative histopathological examination and molecular auxiliary testing.7 This study presents a confirmed tubal PHM case with a comprehensive literature review, aiming to supplement clinical data of this rare disease and improve clinicians’ diagnostic accuracy and standardized management capacity.8

Case Presentation

Clinical Data

A 31-year-old nulliparous female (G1P0) was admitted to the emergency department with a chief complaint of “54 days of amenorrhea and 1 day of persistent lower abdominal distending pain”. The patient had regular menstrual cycles (28–30 days/cycle, 5–7 days duration), with the last menstrual period occurring on April 15, 2025. A urine pregnancy test was positive after amenorrhea, and the patient had no typical early pregnancy symptoms such as nausea and vomiting. One day prior to admission (June 8, 2025), she developed persistent lower abdominal distending pain without radiating pain, vaginal bleeding, dizziness, or fatigue, and thus presented for emergency treatment. The patient had no history of chronic systemic diseases, surgery, trauma, pelvic infection, genetic diseases, or adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Physical examination revealed stable vital signs with normal body temperature, pulse, respiration, and blood pressure. Cardiopulmonary auscultation showed no abnormal findings. The abdomen was soft and flat, without tenderness, rebound tenderness, or muscular tension; the liver and spleen were not palpable, and shifting dullness was negative. Gynecological examination showed normally developed external genitalia consistent with nulliparous status. The vagina was unobstructed with a small amount of colorless, odorless vaginal discharge. The cervix was smooth and normal in size, with no bleeding or motion tenderness. The uterus was anteverted with normal size, texture, and mobility, and no tenderness was detected. Mild thickening and tenderness were noted in the left adnexal area, while no masses or tenderness were found in the right adnexal area.

Auxiliary examinations showed that the patient’s serum β-hCG level was 48,948 IU/L one day before admission, which increased sharply to 130,290 IU/L on the day of surgery (the result was reported postoperatively due to laboratory testing cycle limitations).3 Preoperative chest X-ray, routine blood tests, coagulation function, liver and renal function, and electrocardiogram showed no abnormalities. Gynecological color Doppler ultrasound (Figure 1) demonstrated a normal-sized anteverted uterus with an endometrial thickness of 6 mm and heterogeneous endometrial echogenicity.9 A 58 mm × 43 mm ill-defined mixed echogenic mass was detected in the left adnexal region, containing a 32 mm × 24 mm × 21 mm gestational sac with a visible yolk sac, a 9.6 mm embryo, and viable fetal heart activity (Figure 1b). An irregular 90 mm × 33 mm anechoic area with flocculent reticular echoes was observed in the rectouterine pouch, indicating pelvic effusion (Figure 1a).9 Although the real-time preoperative β-hCG result was unavailable, the drastically elevated β-hCG level and large adnexal mass strongly indicated impending tubal rupture and intra-abdominal hemorrhage, prompting urgent surgical exploration.3 After full communication with the patient and her family regarding surgical risks, prognostic benefits, and fertility impacts of conservative medication and surgical intervention (tubal preservation versus salpingectomy),10 the patient voluntarily underwent emergency laparoscopic left salpingectomy.

Intraoperative Findings

A 10-mm umbilical incision was made to establish a laparoscopic operating channel, and carbon dioxide pneumoperitoneum was maintained at 12 mmHg. Laparoscopic exploration revealed a large number of blood clots and hemorrhagic effusion in the pelvic cavity, extending to the perihepatic and perisplenic spaces, with an estimated total pelvic hemorrhage of approximately 500 mL (Figure 2a). Further adnexal exploration showed blood clots adhering to the fimbrial end of the left fallopian tube (Figure 2b). The ampulla-to-fimbria segment of the left fallopian tube was significantly edematous and cyanotic, with a 0.5-cm local rupture and active bleeding at the rupture site (Figure 3). Combined with preoperative evaluation and intraoperative findings of ruptured tubal hemorrhage that precluded tubal preservation, laparoscopic left salpingectomy was performed. The operation proceeded smoothly with an intraoperative blood loss of approximately 50 mL, and no injury to adjacent pelvic organs occurred. Gross observation of the resected fallopian tube specimen confirmed villous tissue within the tubal lumen.

Postoperative Pathology and Follow-Up

Postoperative histopathological examination confirmed typical morphological features of PHM. Microscopic observation of the left fallopian tube specimen showed heterogeneous villous morphology, with coexisting normal small villi and diffusely edematous, enlarged villi. Pathognomonic scalloped villous borders and focal stromal lacuna formation were observed, accompanied by mild focal trophoblastic atypia without diffuse or severe trophoblastic proliferation.11 No trophoblastic invasion of the tubal wall or surgical margins was detected, with complete tubal tissue structure and negative resection margins (Figure 4a and b). Immunohistochemical staining showed a Ki-67 proliferation index of 90% and positive p57 expression, suggesting active trophoblastic proliferation.12

Postoperative pathological images of the left salpingectomy specimen (HE staining, 100× magnification). (a and b) The resected fallopian tube tissue presents coexisting normal villi and edematous scalloped villi, with focal mild trophoblastic atypia and no tubal wall invasion, consistent with the pathological diagnosis of partial hydatidiform mole.1

Notably, isolated positive p57 expression cannot support a definitive diagnosis of PHM, and its core clinical value is limited to excluding classic CHM.7 Due to the technical limitations of our institution, molecular confirmatory tests including short tandem repeat (STR) genotyping and flow cytometric ploidy analysis were not performed, and genetic verification of the diandric triploid karyotype of PHM was therefore unavailable, which constitutes a major limitation of this case study.6

Standardized postoperative follow-up was implemented with weekly serum β-hCG detection using unified reagents and testing standards to dynamically evaluate trophoblastic tissue regression.3 The patient’s serum β-hCG level decreased steadily in a logarithmic trend, returning to the normal range (<5 IU/L) at 8 weeks postoperatively and maintaining a stable level during the subsequent 3-month follow-up period (Figure 5).13 According to the 2001 FIGO GTD Risk Scoring System, the patient was stratified into the low-risk group.14 No metastatic manifestations such as irregular vaginal bleeding, persistent abdominal pain, cough, or hemoptysis were observed throughout the follow-up period. Consistent with the management guidelines for low-risk GTD, routine chest imaging was not performed.14 No postoperative complications, disease recurrence, or adverse events occurred. Considering the patient’s nulliparous status and future fertility requirements, individualized fertility guidance was provided: low-risk PHM patients do not require 1–2 years of strict contraception, and safe pregnancy can be attempted 6 months after sustained normalization of serum β-hCG levels.10 Regular follow-up is still required for 6 months after β-hCG remission to screen for potential persistent trophoblastic lesions.15

Discussion

GTD is a heterogeneous proliferative disease of trophoblast cells, among which hydatidiform mole is the most common subtype.1 The global incidence of hydatidiform mole presents obvious geographical differences: the incidence is lower than 1/1000 pregnancies in European and American populations, while it rises to approximately 2/1000 pregnancies in Asian populations including China.3 The incidence of CHM is approximately 1/695 pregnancies, while PHM is rarer, with an incidence of 1/1945 pregnancies.1

Amenorrhea is the most common initial symptom of hydatidiform mole, followed by first-trimester irregular vaginal bleeding, severe gestational emesis, and inconsistent uterine enlargement relative to gestational age in partial patients.1 Different from common intrauterine hydatidiform mole, ectopic hydatidiform mole implanted in extrauterine sites (cervix, fallopian tube, ovary, abdominal cavity, etc) is extremely rare.5 The extremely low incidence of tubal PHM leads to insufficient clinical recognition and a high misdiagnosis rate in emergency gynecological practice.3 As a rarely reported GTD subtype, tubal PHM lacks systematic clinical data and unified standardized diagnostic protocols.8

The pathogenesis of ectopic hydatidiform mole is closely related to fertilization errors and chromosomal ploidy abnormalities, which is consistent with the genetic mechanism of intrauterine PHM.11 Typical PHM originates from dispermic fertilization of normal haploid oocytes, forming a diandric triploid karyotype (69,XXY or 69,XYY), which is the core genetic basis for abnormal villous proliferation and vesicular degeneration.5,11 Compared with intrauterine PHM, tubal PHM undergoes abnormal extrauterine implantation and persistent ischemic-hypoxic stimulation, which further aggravates villous edema and reactive trophoblastic hyperplasia, resulting in significant morphological overlap with simple ectopic pregnancy complicated by villous hydropic degeneration.16 Its clinical manifestations are highly consistent with conventional tubal ectopic pregnancy, including amenorrhea, abdominal pain, and vaginal bleeding, and may be complicated by acute intra-abdominal hemorrhage, which is consistent with the clinical characteristics of this case. The patient in this study only presented with simple lower abdominal distending pain without specific characteristic manifestations of molar pregnancy, fully reflecting the difficulty of preoperative differential diagnosis.2 At present, the definitive diagnosis of ectopic hydatidiform mole relies on the combination of postoperative histopathological examination and molecular genetic verification.8 Therefore, routine pathological screening of all surgically resected ectopic pregnancy specimens is essential to avoid missed diagnosis and misdiagnosis of rare molar lesions.17

Dynamic monitoring of serum β-hCG is a critical auxiliary indicator for the clinical diagnosis of ectopic hydatidiform mole.3 Serum β-hCG levels in uncomplicated conventional ectopic pregnancy are generally below 10,000 IU/L, while levels exceeding 100,000 IU/L suggest a higher risk of abnormal trophoblastic proliferation and malignant transformation of trophoblastic lesions.3 Although no unified diagnostic threshold of β-hCG has been established for tubal PHM, drastically elevated serum β-hCG in patients with ectopic pregnancy should arouse high clinical suspicion of combined molar lesions, requiring comprehensive diagnosis combining ultrasonographic features and postoperative pathological results.18 Ultrasound is the first-line imaging technique for preliminary screening of tubal PHM.9 However, the ultrasonographic features of tubal PHM are non-specific and easily confused with multiple benign gestational lesions, necessitating rigorous differential diagnosis.9 (1) Ectopic pregnancy with villous hydropic degeneration: Ischemic injury caused by abnormal ectopic implantation leads to reversible villous edema and mild reactive trophoblastic hyperplasia, which mimics PHM in morphology but lacks pathognomonic scalloped villous borders and stromal lacunae, with no persistent β-hCG elevation after lesion resection;16 (2) Conventional tubal pregnancy with high β-hCG: Viable simple ectopic pregnancy may present with elevated β-hCG but has no typical molar pathological changes;3 (3) Heterotopic pregnancy: Combined intrauterine and ectopic pregnancy can also cause abnormal β-hCG elevation, which requires ultrasonic exclusion.18

Histopathological examination is the gold standard for the diagnosis of hydatidiform mole.1 As shown in Figure 4, CHM is pathologically characterized by diffuse hydropic degeneration of all placental villi, absence of embryonic tissue, and diffuse circumferential trophoblastic hyperplasia, which is derived from androgenetic monospermic or dispermic fertilization of empty oocytes.11 In contrast, PHM is characterized by mixed normal and edematous villi, focal mild trophoblastic hyperplasia, typical scalloped villous borders, stromal trophoblastic inclusions, detectable embryonic tissue, and diandric triploid genotypes.11,16 Notably, villous tissue of ectopic pregnancy often develops secondary hydropic degeneration due to long-term ischemic-hypoxic damage after tubal implantation, resulting in significant morphological overlap with true PHM and greatly increasing the risk of pathological misdiagnosis.5,16 Therefore, experienced pathological review combined with molecular biomarker detection is indispensable for accurate classification and diagnosis.12

p57 immunohistochemistry is a core biomarker for the differential diagnosis of molar subtypes. Diandric triploid PHM retains maternal p57 gene expression and presents positive p57 staining, while androgenetic CHM shows negative p57 expression, enabling effective differentiation between the two diseases.12 Nevertheless, STR genotyping, which confirms the diandric triploid karyotype (one maternal and two paternal haploid sets), remains the definitive gold standard for PHM diagnosis.19 The lack of molecular genetic verification in this case is a key research limitation.11

Molecular diagnostic techniques effectively resolve the pathological diagnostic dilemma caused by secondary villous degeneration and realize precise etiological classification of hydatidiform mole.11 Common auxiliary molecular detection methods include DNA ploidy analysis, p57 immunohistochemistry, and STR genotyping.7 Recent GTD molecular studies have confirmed that p57 immunohistochemistry is suitable for rapid preliminary screening, while STR genotyping can accurately identify chromosomal ploidy and parental genetic components, effectively distinguishing CHM, dispermic/monospermic PHM, and non-molar triploid villous degeneration.6,11 At present, STR genotyping has become the definitive diagnostic standard for atypical and ectopic PHM, significantly reducing the misdiagnosis rate caused by simple morphological judgment.8 Due to institutional technical constraints, molecular genetic confirmation was not performed in this case, which affects the accuracy of etiological diagnosis and is an unavoidable limitation of this study.7

The clinical treatment of tubal PHM requires individualized stratified management based on the patient’s hemodynamic status, fertility intention, and disease severity. Two core clinical scenarios need strict differentiation to avoid inappropriate treatment. First, conservative medical treatment: For hemodynamically stable patients with unruptured tubal lesions, no intra-abdominal hemorrhage, continuously declining low-level β-hCG, and strong fertility demand, single-agent methotrexate treatment can be attempted to preserve tubal function.10 Although sporadic successful cases of conservative treatment have been reported,20 the clinical indications are extremely rigorous. Second, postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy: It is only applicable to patients with postoperative β-hCG plateau, persistent elevation, or malignant transformation suggestive of GTN,3 and is not recommended as routine prophylaxis for primary ectopic PHM. In this case, due to tubal rupture and active intra-abdominal bleeding (Figures 2 and 3), the patient was not a candidate for conservative treatment, and emergency laparoscopic salpingectomy was the safest and most optimal intervention.5

Hydatidiform mole has a potential risk of progressing to invasive mole or choriocarcinoma, and PHM is not a purely low-risk disease. All confirmed cases require individualized risk stratification to guide clinical management. Based on the 2001 FIGO Risk Scoring System for Gestational Trophoblastic Disease, this case was classified as low-risk PHM without adverse prognostic factors.21

Stratified follow-up strategies are recommended for different GTD subtypes, and the traditional unified 1–2 year long-term follow-up protocol is no longer advocated in the latest global consensus. The 2025 updated FIGO GTD management guidelines clearly emphasize individualized risk-stratified follow-up and fertility guidance.4 High-risk CHM and confirmed GTN cases require 1–2 years of strict follow-up due to the high risk of persistent lesions and distant metastasis.3 In contrast, low-risk intrauterine PHM and ectopic PHM have a significantly lower malignant transformation risk (persistent GTN incidence <1%), and individualized shortened follow-up protocols are clinically feasible.4,8 As shown in Figure 5, this patient’s β-hCG levels declined steadily, and she was low-risk, with serum β-hCG decreasing logarithmically and returning to normal 8 weeks after surgery.13 For low-risk patients with sustained β-hCG remission and no metastatic symptoms, follow-up can focus on dynamic β-hCG monitoring without routine long-term systemic imaging to avoid excessive medical intervention. In terms of fertility management, recent cohort studies confirm that low-risk PHM patients do not require strict 2-year contraception, and safe pregnancy can be attempted 6 months after stable β-hCG normalization without increasing the risk of recurrence or adverse pregnancy outcomes.10 Despite the favorable prognosis of this case, regular follow-up for 6 months after β-hCG remission is still necessary for early detection of potential persistent trophoblastic lesions.22

Conclusion

In summary, tubal PHM is an extremely rare GTD subtype with non-specific clinical and imaging manifestations, which is frequently misdiagnosed as conventional tubal ectopic pregnancy or ectopic pregnancy with reactive villous hydropic degeneration. This rare typical case systematically summarizes the diagnostic and therapeutic principles of tubal PHM, and puts forward the following core clinical insights.

Improved Diagnostic Vigilance

For ectopic pregnancy patients with abnormally elevated serum β-hCG (exceeding 100,000 IU/L), clinicians should highly suspect combined molar lesions, and strictly differentiate them from villous hydropic degeneration, high-β-hCG conventional ectopic pregnancy, and heterotopic pregnancy. Extremely high β-hCG levels in tubal ectopic cases are important warning indicators of abnormal trophoblastic proliferation and molar transformation.

Standardized Multimodal Pathological Diagnosis

Single routine HE morphological observation is insufficient for accurate PHM diagnosis. Clinically, p57 positivity only excludes CHM and cannot independently confirm PHM. The combined application of Ki-67 proliferation index detection, p57 immunohistochemistry, and STR molecular genotyping is the current standardized diagnostic procedure, which should be actively carried out when clinical conditions permit to eliminate morphological diagnostic errors and improve diagnostic accuracy. As demonstrated in Figure 4, the histopathological features are the foundation of diagnosis.

Individualized Stratified Treatment

Therapeutic strategies should be formulated based on the patient’s hemodynamic stability and lesion status. Methotrexate conservative therapy is only applicable to stable, unruptured low-risk patients with fertility intentions. Emergency surgical resection is mandatory for ruptured lesions with active bleeding (Figures 2 and 3). Clinicians should strictly distinguish conservative treatment for primary lesions and adjuvant chemotherapy for secondary GTN to avoid excessive treatment.3

Precision Follow-Up and Fertility Guidance

The traditional unified long-term follow-up and strict contraception model should be abandoned. For low-risk ectopic PHM patients, regular postoperative β-hCG monitoring is sufficient, and pregnancy can be attempted 6 months after stable β-hCG remission without mandatory 2-year contraception (Figure 5).

This case supplements the limited clinical data of rare tubal PHM, standardizes the core procedures of differential diagnosis, pathological evaluation, individualized treatment, and stratified follow-up for this disease, and provides a valuable clinical reference for the standardized management of similar rare gestational trophoblastic diseases.
