Comment on “Precision-guided heart failure interventions: The expanding role of advanced cardiac imaging and artificial intelligence”
Authors: B. Selvapriya, B.N. Deepak Kumar
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the study by Albulushi and Al-Farhan et al. [1], which examined imaging and AI-guided heart failure interventions. The proposed precision care pathway organizes a broad literature into a clinically usable structure, and three aspects of that synthesis warrant further comment.

The proposed precision-care framework lacks empirical validation

The stepwise pathway, modality-selection table, and seven-step implementation blueprint are original constructs assembled by the authors, rather than schemas drawn from a validated source [2]. No pilot program, retrospective audit, or comparison against existing society algorithms accompanies these tools; however, the text recommends them in prescriptive language, instructing clinicians to define the dominant problem, select a test, and build longitudinal monitoring through this specific sequence [3]. A framework this detailed, appearing in a peer-reviewed article, risks adoption as though it carries the same evidentiary weight as trial data cited elsewhere in the review. Labeling the pathway as a proposed conceptual tool pending prospective evaluation, rather than a practical blueprint ready for implementation, would set the reader's expectations correctly.

Post-hoc subgroup evidence for remote pressure monitoring is treated as confirmatory

The review describes GUIDE-HF as neutral for its primary endpoint and states that pre-pandemic analyses suggested a benefit in reducing heart failure events [4]. This subgroup finding depends on splitting the trial period around an unplanned, pandemic-driven disruption to care delivery, a temporal division defined after the data were observed rather than specified in advance. Post-hoc subgroups generated around an external shock are hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory, regardless of how biologically plausible the results appear. Presenting this analysis alongside the positive MONITOR-HF trial without distinguishing pre-specified from exploratory evidence risks overstating the confidence in pulmonary artery pressure-guided therapy outside a dedicated confirmatory trial.

Divergent transcatheter edge-to-edge repair trial results are explained without an operational selection algorithm

The review attributes the different outcomes of COAPT and MITRA-FR to whether mitral regurgitation is a treatable driver of heart failure or a marker of advanced ventricular disease and states that imaging should identify which category a patient falls into. This proportionality concept is not accompanied by specific volumetric or regurgitant fraction thresholds, derived from the two trials' baseline characteristics, that would allow a reader to replicate the distinction in an individual patient. Without these operational cutoffs, the practical takeaway asks clinicians to apply a conceptually sound principle that the review itself does not translate into a reproducible bedside test.

Conclusion

This review provides a wide-ranging and clinically relevant synthesis of imaging and artificial intelligence in heart failure intervention. Distinguishing the authors' original organizational proposals from validated evidence, flagging post-hoc analyses explicitly where they inform practice recommendations, and converting conceptual selection principles into operational thresholds would strengthen the translational value of an already timely contribution.
