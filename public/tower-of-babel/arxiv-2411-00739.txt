1.
Introduction

A non-trivial element
g
in a group
\Gamma
is called
reversible
if there exists
h\in\Gamma
such that
hgh^{-1}=g^{-1}
. An element
h\in\Gamma
conjugating
g
to
g^{-1}
is called a
reciprocator
of
g
.
If the reciprocator
h
is an involution (i.e.,
h^{2}=1
), then
g
is said to be
strongly reversible
. Every strongly reversible element, except involutions themselves, can be written as a product of two involutions. For various perspectives on reversibility, see
[
BR
]
,
[
LR
]
,
[
Sa
]
,
[
OS
]
.
In Fuchsian groups, a torsion-free element is reversible if and only if it is strongly reversible; see
[
DG
, Theorem 1.1]
. Moreover, if
hgh^{-1}=g^{-1}
for some
g\in\Gamma
, then
h
must be an involution. In the modular group
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, such elements are referred to as
reciprocal
(following Sarnak
[
Sa
]
), and we adopt this terminology. A conjugacy class that contains a reciprocal element is called a
reciprocal class
.
If
g
is a hyperbolic reciprocal element in a Fuchsian group
\Gamma
, then the translation axis of
g
projects to a closed geodesic on
\mathbb{H}^{2}/\Gamma
. A reciprocal element implies that this closed geodesic passes through an even-order cone point; such geodesics are called
reciprocal geodesics
. Thus, hyperbolic reciprocal classes bijectively correspond to the reciprocal geodesics on
\mathbb{H}^{2}/\Gamma
.

Reciprocators of a reciprocal element are unique up to multiplication by elements of the centralizer
Z_{\Gamma}(g)
; see
[
BR
]
. If a reciprocal element
g\in\Gamma
is hyperbolic and admits a reciprocator
h\in\Gamma
, then
\langle g,h\rangle
is an infinite dihedral subgroup of
\Gamma
. Thus, classifying reciprocal geodesics reduces to classifying such dihedral subgroups up to conjugacy. In particular, each equivalence class of reciprocal geodesics corresponds to the conjugacy class of a reciprocal element, equivalently to the conjugacy class of the infinite dihedral subgroup generated by a hyperbolic reciprocal element
g
and its reciprocator
h
.

In recent years, the asymptotic growth and distribution of reciprocal geodesics have been studied in a variety of settings, in the modular group
[
Sa
]
,
[
BK
]
, on hyperbolic surfaces
[
ES
]
, and more generally on negatively curved orbifolds
[
PP
]
. Sarnak investigated reciprocal classes in the modular group
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, obtaining asymptotic growth and equidistribution results; see
[
Sa
]
. Bourgain and Kontorovich further studied the growth rate of
low-lying
reciprocal geodesics on the modular surface
[
BK
]
. Erlandsson and Souto extended these results to arbitrary orientable two-dimensional hyperbolic orbifolds; see
[
ES
]
.
Parkkonen and Paulin
[
PP
]
have recently generalized the results of Sarnak and Erlandsson–Souto to strongly reversible periodic orbits of the geodesic flow on pinched negatively curved Riemannian orbifolds. In all these works, the hyperbolic length of geodesics serves as the measure for asymptotic growth.

Another approach, based on the free product structure of the modular group, was used by Basmajian and Suzzi Valli. They derived asymptotic growth rates for reciprocal classes using the lengths of the associated combinatorial words; see
[
BV
]
. Following this approach, Marmolejo, in
[
Ma
]
, obtained the spherical growth for reciprocal classes in a Hecke group
\Gamma_{p}
for
p
odd. Here, by
spherical growth
, we mean the asymptotic count of the number of distinct reciprocal classes represented by words of exactly a given length.

Recall that the Hecke group
\Gamma_{p}
is generated by the Möbius transformations acting on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
,

\iota:z\mapsto-\frac{1}{z}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{p}:z\mapsto z+\lambda_{p},

p\geq 3
,
where

\lambda_{p}=2\cos\pi/p
. Geometrically we can see it as a free product of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, where
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
is generated by
\iota
and
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
is generated by
\gamma_{p}=\iota\alpha_{p}
.
In particular,
\Gamma_{3}
is the modular group. Note that the map
\gamma_{p}:z\mapsto-\dfrac{1}{z+\lambda_{p}}
has order
p
. We denote
\gamma_{p}=\iota\alpha_{p}
. We will fix this presentation for the Hecke group, i.e.,
\langle~\iota,\gamma_{p}~|~\iota^{2}=\gamma_{p}^{p}=1~\rangle
throughout the paper. If
p=2r
with
r\in\mathbb{N}-\{1\}
,
\gamma_{p}^{r}
is an involution. We denote
\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma_{p}^{r}
.

As follows from
[
DG
]
, the combinatorics of the reciprocal words are relatively simpler for
p
odd. This is due to the presence of a unique involution up to conjugacy that conjugates a reciprocal element
g
to
g^{-1}
. For even
p
, this is not the case as there are two choices for such an involution, up to conjugacy. Accordingly, it requires further analysis to resolve the complication when
p
is even. In this paper, we resolve this complication and obtain the corresponding spherical growth for reciprocal classes in the even case.

The spherical growth of the reciprocal classes for odd
p
follows from the work of Marmolejo
[
Ma
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
Ma
, Theorem 5.4.1]

Let
P(z)=z^{m+1}-2z^{m-1}-\cdots-2z-2
where
m=\frac{k-1}{2}
.
The conjugacy classes of primitive reciprocal words of length
2t
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{k}
for
k
odd, are asymptotic to
c_{k}\rho_{k}^{t}
where
\rho_{k}
is the unique positive root of
P(z)
in the interval
[\sqrt{2},2]
.
Moreover, the
\rho_{k}
are strictly increasing and
\rho_{k}\to 2
as
k\to\infty
.

We extend Marmolejo’s result for a Hecke group
\Gamma_{p}
, with even
p
. In particular, we obtain an asymptotic estimate for the size of the reciprocal classes in
\Gamma_{p}
when
p
is even.

Thus, throughout the paper we assume that
p=2r
is even, and we write
\gamma_{r}
in place of
\gamma_{p}
. Let
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}
denote the set of conjugacy classes of reciprocal elements of word length
\ell
in
\Gamma_{p}
. Thus, the primary objects of interest are conjugacy classes, not individual words.

Every conjugacy class in
\Gamma_{p}
contains a cyclically reduced representative, and the length of the class is defined as the length of any such representative (a formal definition will be provided later). Moreover, any two cyclically reduced representatives of the same conjugacy class differ by a cyclic permutation of their letters.

We prove the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let

P(x)=x^{r+1}-2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}x^{\,r-j}-1,

and let
\rho\in(\sqrt{2},2)
be its unique positive real root. Let
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}
denote the set of conjugacy classes of reciprocal elements in the Hecke group
\Gamma_{2r}
of word length
\ell
. Then there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that, as
\ell\to\infty
,

|\mathcal{N}_{2\ell}|\sim C_{1}\rho^{\ell},\qquad|\mathcal{N}_{2\ell-1}|\sim C_{2}\rho^{\ell},

where
C_{2}=\rho^{-u-1}C_{1}
. Here, reciprocal classes of odd length occur only if
r
is even, i.e.,
r=2u
.

This yields the following corollary.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{p}
denote the set of conjugacy classes of primitive reciprocal elements in the Hecke group
\Gamma_{2r}
of word length
\ell
. Then the primitive reciprocal conjugacy classes satisfy the same asymptotic formulas as in Theorem
1.2
.

(1)

If
r
is odd, then

|\mathcal{N}_{2\ell}^{p}|\sim C_{1}\rho^{\ell}\qquad(\ell\to\infty),

and there are no primitive reciprocal conjugacy classes of odd word length.

(2)

If
r
is even, then

|\mathcal{N}_{2\ell}^{p}|\sim C_{1}\rho^{\ell},\qquad|\mathcal{N}_{2\ell-1}^{p}|\sim C_{2}\rho^{\ell}\qquad(\ell\to\infty).

In addition to the above asymptotic count, we provide explicit combinatorial formulas for the number of conjugacy classes in each reciprocal type, see Theorem
3.6
. We have used Marmolejo’s counting function
\Psi^{r}_{n}(x)
, cf.
[
Ma
]
, which counts the number of integer solutions to the equation
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=x
, with
0<p_{i}\leq r
. This extends Marmolejo’s count to reciprocal classes in
\Gamma_{2r}
for all values of
r>1
.

We prove the results using the combinatorial structure of
\Gamma_{2r}\approx\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\ast\mathbb{Z}/2r\mathbb{Z}
to determine the spherical growth.
Each reciprocal class contains some cyclically reduced words, which can be expressed in terms of the generators
\iota
and
\gamma_{r}
. Then a reciprocal element in
\Gamma_{2r}
is conjugated to one of the following forms:
\iota K\iota K^{-1}
,
\tilde{\gamma}K\tilde{\gamma}K^{-1}
, or
\iota K\tilde{\gamma}K^{-1}
, for some word
K\in\Gamma_{2r}
. We refer to a word of the form
\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{1}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{2}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{n}}
, that is, starting with
\iota
and ending with
\gamma_{r}
, as an
\iota-\gamma_{r}
word
. Using these conjugacy representatives, we derive a summation formula for their word lengths, which in turn yields a recurrence relation associated with each reciprocal class. Finding a closed-form solution for this recurrence relation provides the desired estimate.

We briefly outline the structure of this paper. After recalling some preliminary notions, we note preliminary observations about the counting function in Section
2
. We estimate counts for the reciprocal classes in Section
3
. Finally, we prove Theorem
1.2
and Corollary
1.3
in Section
4
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Reciprocal elements in Hecke groups

In the case of Hecke groups, elements of order two (i.e., involutions) are also reciprocal; however, since they are finite up to conjugacy, we restrict our discussion to infinite reciprocal elements. In
[
DG
]
, several types of reciprocal elements for Hecke groups were introduced; we recall them below.

Definition 2.1
.

The reciprocal element is said to be
symmetric
if it can be seen as a product of
\iota
and conjugates of
\iota
.

Definition 2.2
.

The reciprocal element is said to be
p
-reciprocal
if it can be seen as a product of
\tilde{\gamma}
and conjugates of
\tilde{\gamma}
.

Definition 2.3
.

The reciprocal element is said to be
symmetric
p
-reciprocal
if it can be seen either as a product of
\iota
and conjugates of
\tilde{\gamma}
or as a product of
\tilde{\gamma}
and conjugates of
\iota
.

2.2.
Words

In this subsection, we define word length in free products and in conjugacy classes, cf.
[
MKD
]
for details.

Let
G=\langle S\mid R\rangle
be a group with finite generating set
S
and relators
R
. A
word

W
in
G
is a finite sequence of generators and their inverses:

W=s_{1}^{\epsilon_{1}}s_{2}^{\epsilon_{2}}\cdots s_{n}^{\epsilon_{n}},\quad\text{where }\epsilon_{i}=\pm 1\text{ and }s_{i}\in S.

A
subword
is a consecutive subsequence of a word. A word is
reduced
if it contains no subword of the form
s_{i}^{\pm 1}s_{i}^{\mp 1}
.

A reduced word is
cyclically reduced
if no cyclic permutation of its letters produces a non-reduced word. For example, in
G=\langle a,b\rangle
, the word
ab^{2}a^{3}ba^{-1}
is not cyclically reduced; its cyclically reduced form is
b^{2}a^{3}b
.

We can also define word in free products of groups. Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are two groups with the presentations
\langle S_{1}|R_{1}\rangle
and
\langle S_{2}|R_{2}\rangle
respectively. Then the free product of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
is
\tilde{G}=G_{1}\ast G_{2}=\langle S_{1}~\sqcup~S_{2}~|~R_{1}~\sqcup~R_{2}\rangle
and
G_{1},G_{2}
are the free factors of
\tilde{G}.

An element
g=a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{n}
for all
a_{i}\in S_{1}\sqcup S_{2}
, is a
reduced word
in
\tilde{G}
if any of
a_{i}
’s not
Id
and any two consecutive
a_{i}
’s do not belong to the same
G_{j}
for
j\in\{1,2\}.
For example, if
\rm{PSL(2,\mathbb{Z})}

=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\ast\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}=\langle~x,y~|~x^{2},y^{3}~\rangle
, then
Id,x
are the
elements of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and
Id,y,y^{2}
are the elements of
\rm PSL(2,\mathbb{Z)}
the sequence
xyxy^{2}
is reduced ; the sequence
yy^{2}
is not reduced since the consecutive terms
y
and

y^{2}
are in the same free factor.

Similarly we can also define
cyclically reduced word
. The element
g
in
\tilde{G}
is cyclically reduced if any cyclic permutation of
a_{i}
’s produce a reduced word.

Theorem 2.4
.

[
MKD
, Theorem 4.1]

Each element
g
of
G_{1}*G_{2}
can be uniquely expressed as a
product

g=g_{1}g_{2}\dots g_{t}
, where
g_{1}g_{2}...g_{t}
is a reduced sequence.

From this theorem we can define the length of a word in free products.

Definition 2.5
.

The
length
of a word in a free product of two cyclic groups is the number of generators in its reduced form.

For the Hecke group
\Gamma_{2r}=\langle\iota,\gamma_{r}\mid\iota^{2},\,\gamma_{r}^{2r}\rangle
, the length of the reduced word
\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{1}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{2}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{n}}\iota
is

1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(1+|k_{i}|\bigr),

where
-r<k_{i}\leq r
for all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.

The length is defined only for reduced words. If
\omega
is chosen to be cyclically reduced in a group
G
, then its length is minimal among all words in its conjugacy class. Hence we obtain the following definition of the length of a conjugacy class.

Definition 2.6
.

The
length
of a conjugacy class in
\Gamma_{p}
is the minimum length of a cyclically reduced word in that conjugacy class.

If a conjugacy class contains a cyclically reduced word with minimal length
l
, we loosely call it a
conjugacy class of length
l
.

2.3.
A counting function:

We shall use the following function for counting reciprocal classes of a given word length
\ell
.

For a natural number
n
, the function
\Psi_{n}
from
\mathbb{N}
to
\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, defined by
\Psi_{n}(x)
, is the number of solutions of the equation,

(2.1)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=x,

where all
p_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
x
are positive integers. It is easy to see that
\Psi_{n}(x)={x-1\choose n-1}
.
Let
\Psi_{n}^{r}(x)
denote the number of solutions of (
2.1
) with constraints
1\leq p_{i}\leq r
, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, for some
r<x
. We have the following proposition.

Proposition 2.7
.

If
x<n
, then
\Psi^{r}_{n}(x)=0
. If
x\geq n
, then we have

\Psi^{r}_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{x-n}{r}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}\binom{x-rk-1}{n-1}.

Proof.

If we choose
x<n
, there is no positive integral solution for the Equation (
2.1
). That implies
\Psi^{r}_{n}(x)=0
.

Now, we consider the Equation (
2.1
) for
x\geq n
.
Let
S
be the set of all solutions with
p_{i}\geq 1
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and let
A_{i}
be the set of solutions, where
p_{i}\geq r+1
. Let
S^{\prime}
be the set of all required solutions. Then we have

S^{\prime}=S-\Big(\bigcup_{i}A_{i}\Big).

Then the number of required solutions is

|S^{\prime}|=|S|-\sum_{i}|A_{i}|+\sum_{i<j}|A_{i}\cap A_{j}|-\cdots+(-1)^{k}\sum_{i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}}|A_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap A_{i_{k}}|+\cdots,

by the inclusion-exclusion principle. The set
A_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap A_{i_{k}}
is the set of the solutions of the Equation (
2.1
) such that
p_{i_{j}}\geq r+1
for all
j\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
. To deduce the cardinality of this set, we need to rewrite the equation into the following equation:

(2.2)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}p^{\prime}_{i}=x-rk\quad\text{(where all $p^{\prime}_{i}\geq 1$).}

The cardinality of the set
A_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap A_{i_{k}}
, is the number of the solutions of the Equation (
2.2
),
i.e.,
\Psi_{n}(x-rk)=\binom{x-rk-1}{n-1}
.

There are
\binom{n}{k}
ways to choose such
p_{i}\geq r+1
.

Hence, the total number is:

\Psi^{r}_{n}(x)=|S^{\prime}|=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{x-n}{r}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}\binom{x-rk-1}{n-1}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
r,x
be given natural numbers. Then the total number of the solutions
\{n;k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}\}
,
n\in\mathbb{N},~{k}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
-r<k_{i}\leq r
for all
k_{i}
and
q
be the number of
k_{i}
’s which are equal to
r
of the equation
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|k_{i}|+n=x
is given by the following sum after considering all possibilities for
n
and
k_{i}
’s,

\sum_{q=0}^{\lceil\frac{x}{r+1}\rceil-1}\sum_{n=\lceil\frac{x-q}{r}\rceil}^{\lfloor\frac{x-(r-1)q}{2}\rfloor}\Psi_{n}^{r-1}(x-n-rq)2^{n-q}{n\choose q}.

Proof.

Since some of the
k_{i}
’s are equal to
r
, we have two choices depending upon their sign for those
k_{i}
’s such that
k_{i}\neq r
. So, the number of solutions for the equation, for fixed
n
and a fixed
q
is:

(2.3)

\Psi_{n}^{r-1}(x-n-qr)2^{n-q}.

Now,
n
will be maximum when all
k_{i}\neq r
have absolute values
1
. Therefore,

n-q+n+rq\leq x

\Rightarrow 2n+(r-1)q\leq x

\Rightarrow n\leq\frac{x-(r-1)q}{2}.

Similarly,
n
will reach the lower bound if the absolute value of each
k_{i}\neq r
is
r-1
. Therefore,

(n-q)(r-1)+rq+n\geq x

\Rightarrow nr+q\geq x

\Rightarrow n\geq\frac{x-q}{r}.

So, the number of all the solutions of the equation for fixed
q,

\sum_{n=\lceil\frac{x-q}{r}\rceil}^{\lfloor\frac{x-(r-1)q}{2}\rfloor}\Psi_{n}^{r-1}(x-n-rq)2^{n-q}.

Note that
q
has its minimum value
0
when all
k_{i}\neq r
, and
q
has maximum value
\lceil\frac{x}{r+1}\rceil-1
when all
k_{i}
’s except one, are equal to
r
. Also note that there are
{n\choose q}
possibilities of having
q
number of
k_{i}
to be equal to
r
in the tuple
(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{n})
.
Now summing up for
q
in between
0
and
\lceil\frac{x}{r+1}\rceil-1
, the total number of solutions is given by:

\sum_{q=0}^{\lceil\frac{x}{r+1}\rceil-1}\sum_{n=\lceil\frac{x-q}{r}\rceil}^{\lfloor\frac{x-(r-1)q}{2}\rfloor}\Psi_{n}^{r-1}(x-n-rq)2^{n-q}{n\choose q}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

3.
Counting the reciprocal conjugacy classes

To count the reciprocal classes in the Hecke group, we need to specify the representations of the reciprocal classes. We recall the following theorem from
[
DG
]
,

Theorem 3.1
.

[
DG
]

For any reciprocal hyperbolic element in
\Gamma_{2r}
, the following possibilities can occur.

(1)

Suppose in the reciprocal class, the reciprocators are conjugate to
\iota
. Then, there are exactly four symmetric elements in that class.

(2)

Suppose in the reciprocal class, the reciprocators conjugate to
\gamma_{p}^{r}
. Then, the number of
p
-reciprocal elements is exactly
4r
.

(3)

Suppose in the reciprocal class, each reciprocal element has two types of reciprocators; one type of reciprocator conjugates to
\iota
, and another type of reciprocator conjugates to
\gamma_{p}^{r}
.

(a)

If the reciprocal class does not contain any nonzero power of
\iota\gamma_{r}^{r}
, then the number of symmetric elements and the number of
p
-reciprocal elements contained in the class are exactly two and exactly
2r
, respectively.

(b)

If the reciprocal class contain any non-zero power of
\iota\gamma_{r}^{r}
, then
the number of symmetric
p
-reciprocal elements and the number of
p
-reciprocal elements contained in the class are exactly two and exactly
2r-2
, respectively.

3.1.
Counting reciprocal classes

We have seen that a reciprocal class
[\alpha]
in
\Gamma_{p}
can be divided into three types depending on whether it has all the reciprocators conjugate to only one of
\iota
and
\tilde{\gamma}
, or both of them. In the following, we obtain a ‘ normal form’ for such reciprocal classes.
Note that a reciprocal element
\alpha
with the form
\iota\tilde{\gamma}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{1}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{2}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{n}}\iota\tilde{\gamma}\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{n}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{2}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{1}}
, is a symmetric
p
-reciprocal element, but any even power of
\alpha
i.e.
\alpha^{2m}
for
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, may not remain in the same form. It can take the form of a reduced expression like
\iota K\iota K^{-1}
or
\tilde{\gamma}K\tilde{\gamma}K^{-1}
, up to conjugacy. Although reciprocal elements have distinct forms in the primitive case, they may coincide in the general case.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
[\tilde{\alpha}]
be a reciprocal class in
\Gamma_{2r}

Lemma 3.3
.

Consider the set of all reciprocal classes in

with length

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{q=0}^{\lceil\frac{l}{r+1}\rceil-1}\sum_{n=\lceil\frac{l-q}{r}\rceil}^{\lfloor\frac{l-(r-1)q}{2}\rfloor}\Psi_{n}^{r-1}(l-n-rq)2^{n-q}{n\choose q}.

Consider the symmetric reciprocal word

\gamma_{r}^{-k_{n}}\iota\dots\gamma_{r}^{-k_{1}}\iota R\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{1}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{2}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{n}}=\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{n}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{2}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{1}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{1}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{2}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{n}}.

Let
R^{\prime}=\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{n}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{2}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{1}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{1}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{2}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{n}}
. Then

and

are of the form mentioned in Lemma

and

\sum^{n}_{i=1}2|k_{i}|+2n=2l~\Rightarrow~\sum_{i=1}^{n}|k_{i}|=l-n,\hbox{ where }-r<k_{i}\leq r.

Let

be the number of

,

Lemma 3.4
.

Consider the set of all

-reciprocal classes

Let

be a

-reciprocal element of this form:

2\sum^{n-q}_{i=1}k_{i}+2n+2rq+2(r+1)=2l

4.
Spherical growth of the Reciprocal Classes

Let
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}
denote the number of conjugacy classes of reciprocal elements of word length
\ell
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
[\alpha]
be a reciprocal conjugacy class in
\Gamma_{2r}
.

(1)

If
r
is odd, then
[\alpha]
has even word length.

(2)

If
r
is even, then
[\alpha]
has odd word length if and only if it is symmetric
p
-reciprocal. In particular, the symmetric and
p
-reciprocal classes have even word length.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
, every reciprocal conjugacy class admits a cyclically reduced representative of one of the following three types: symmetric,
p
-reciprocal, or symmetric
p
-reciprocal.

In the symmetric case, the representative has the form

\alpha=\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{1}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{2}}\cdots\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{n}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{n}}\cdots\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{2}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{1}},

and hence

\ell(\alpha)=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}|k_{i}|+2n,

which is always even.

In the
p
-reciprocal case, by Lemma
3.4
, one has

\ell(\alpha)=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}|k_{i}|+2n+2(r+1),

which is again always even.

In the symmetric
p
-reciprocal case, by Lemma
3.5
, one has

\ell(\alpha)=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}|k_{i}|+2n+(r+1).

Therefore the parity of
\ell(\alpha)
is exactly the parity of
r+1
.

If
r=2u+1
is odd, then
r+1=2u+2
is even, so in all three cases the word length is even.

If
r=2u
is even, then
r+1=2u+1
is odd, so the symmetric
p
-reciprocal case has odd word length, while the other two cases remain even.

This proves both assertions.
∎

4.1.

.

In order to prove the Theorem we shall show that

and
q
must be
1
, and all
k_{i}
’s are
0.
Then
R=\iota\tilde{\gamma}\iota\tilde{\gamma}
. So, there is only one element for the case
q>0.
Then the required number is

\frac{1}{6}(2^{l}+2(-1)^{l})+\frac{1}{6}(2^{u+1}+2(-1)^{u+1}).

It proves
(2)
.

For
(3)
, We emphasize that the three reciprocal types are not, in general, mutually disjoint: a reciprocal class may contain both symmetric and
p
-reciprocal elements. Nevertheless, the corresponding primitive representatives are disjoint. Therefore, we decompose
\mathcal{N}_{2l}
into three disjoint subsets
A_{2l},B_{2l},C_{2l}
, where
A_{2l}
,
B_{2l}
, and
C_{2l}
denote the sets of conjugacy classes whose primitive representatives are symmetric reciprocal,
p
-reciprocal, and symmetric
p
-reciprocal, respectively. We can see that the set
A_{2l}
is a collection of the reduced words, which are symmetric and of the form
\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{1}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{2}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{n}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{n}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{2}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{1}}
, where
-r<k_{i}\leq r
. Then, the set
A_{2l,j}=\iota\gamma_{r}^{j}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{2}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{n}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{n}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{2}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{-j}
gives the equation,

2+2|j|+2\sum_{i=2}^{n}|k_{i}|+2n=2l,

where
-r<k_{i},~j\leq r
.
This implies

\sum_{i=2}^{n}|k_{i}|+n=l-|j|-1.

So,
|A_{2l,j}|=|A_{2(l-|j|-1)}|
. Now, varying
|j|
over the set
\{r,r-1,\dots,1\}
, we get the following condition hold,

|A_{2l}|=2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}|A_{2(l-|j|-1)}|+|A_{l-r-1}|.

Here,
2
appears for both signs of
k_{i}
’s.
Similarly, we have the following equalities.

|B_{2l}|=2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}|B_{2(l-j-1)}|+|B_{l-r-1}|,~~|C_{2l}|=2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}|C_{2(l-j-1)}|+|C_{l-r-1}|.

Therefore, we have

|\mathcal{N}_{2l}|=2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}|\mathcal{N}_{2(l-j-1)}|+|\mathcal{N}_{l-r-1}|.

This proves
(3)
.
∎

Now, we recall the following well known results.

Theorem 4.3

(
Perron
)
.

The polynomial

f(x)=x^{n}+ax\pm 1

is irreducible for
|a|\geq 3
. When
|a|=2
,
f(x)
is either irreducible or has a factor
x\pm 1
. In the latter case, the second factor of
f(x)
is irreducible.

Theorem 4.4
.

Every real root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficients is either an integer or irrational.

Definition 4.5
.

[
RS
, Definition 8.1.2]

Let
f(z)=z^{n}+a_{n-1}z^{n-1}+a_{n-2}z^{n-2}+\dots+a_{1}z+a_{0}
be a monic with complex coefficients. Then, the
Cauchy bound
of
f
, say
\xi
, is the unique positive root of the equation
z^{n}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\lvert a_{k}\rvert z^{k}
=0.

Theorem 4.6

(
Cauchy
)
.

Let
f(z)=z^{n}+a_{n-1}z^{n-1}+a_{n-2}z^{n-2}+\dots+a_{1}z+a_{0}
be an
n
-degree polynomial with complex coefficients. Then all zeros of
f(z)
have modulus less than or equal to the Cauchy bound
\xi.

We use these results to analyze the characteristic equation corresponding to

|\mathcal{N}_{2l}|=2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}|\mathcal{N}_{2(l-j-1)}|+|\mathcal{N}_{2(l-r-1)}|,

namely,

x^{l}=2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}x^{l-j-1}+x^{l-r-1}.

For
x\neq 0
, we have the following equation:
x^{r+1}-2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}x^{r-j}-1
. Let

P(x)=x^{r+1}-2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}x^{r-j}-1.

Since
x=-1
is a root of
P(x)
, then

P(x)=(x+1)Q(x),\qquad Q(x):=x^{r}-x^{r-1}-\cdots-x-1.

Let
\widetilde{Q}(x)=x^{r}Q(1/x)=1-x-\cdots-x^{r}
be the reciprocal polynomial of
Q
. Then

-(x-1)\widetilde{Q}(x)=x^{r+1}-2x+1.

By Theorem
4.3
, the polynomial
x^{r+1}-2x+1
has the factor
x-1
, and its other factor
\widetilde{Q}(x)
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
. Hence
Q(x)
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
. Since we are in characteristic
0
,
Q
has no repeated roots; moreover,
Q(-1)\neq 0
, so
P(x)=(x+1)Q(x)
also has no repeated roots.

By Descartes’ rule of signs,
Q
has exactly one positive real root. That is also a root of
P
. We now show that this root lies in
(\sqrt{2},2)
and is the unique root of maximal modulus.

Lemma 4.7
.

The recurrence relation

(4.3)

|\mathcal{N}_{2l}|=2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}|\mathcal{N}_{2(l-j-1)}|+|\mathcal{N}_{2(l-r-1)}|

has a characteristic polynomial with a unique positive real root
\rho
lying in the interval
(\sqrt{2},2)
.

Moreover,

|\mathcal{N}_{2l}|\sim C_{1}\rho^{l}\quad\text{as }l\to\infty,

where
\rho
is the root of the recurrence polynomial with the largest absolute value and
C_{1}
is a constant.

Proof.

If we define
P
as a map from real line to real line, then
P(\sqrt{2})<0
:

P(\sqrt{2})=2^{\frac{r+1}{2}}-2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}2^{\frac{r-j}{2}}-1

=2^{\frac{r+1}{2}}-2^{\frac{r+1}{2}}-2\sum_{j=2}^{r-1}2^{\frac{r-j}{2}}-1

=-2\sum_{j=2}^{r-1}2^{\frac{r-j}{2}}-1<0.

Also,

P(2)=2^{r+1}-2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}2^{r-j}-1

=2^{r+1}-2(2^{r-1}+2^{r-2}+\dots+2^{2}+2+1)+1

=2^{r+1}-2(2^{r}-1)+1=3.

That means,
P(2)>0.

Then, by Bolzano’s theorem, there is at least one zero of the polynomial in the interval
[\sqrt{2},2]
, denote it as
\rho.
Since, there is no integer in
(\sqrt{2},2)
,
\rho
must be an irrational number by Lemma
4.4
. This is the Cauchy bound for
P(x)
. From the earlier discussion and Theorem
4.3
, there are no repeated roots in
P(x)
.

Let
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\dots,\rho_{r+1}
be the roots of
P(x)=0
. Since
P
has no repeated roots, the general solution of the recurrence is

(4.4)

|\mathcal{N}_{2l}|=c_{1}\rho_{1}^{l}+c_{2}\rho_{2}^{l}+\cdots+c_{r+1}\rho_{r+1}^{l},

where
c_{1},\dots,c_{r+1}
are constants determined by the initial conditions. Let the real positive root
\rho
be
\rho_{k}
. For simplicity, we denote the root
\rho_{k}
by
\rho
and the coefficient
c_{k}
of
\rho^{l}_{k}
by
C_{1}
.

Since
|\rho_{i}|<\rho
for all
i\neq k
, we have

\frac{|\mathcal{N}_{2l}|}{\rho^{l}}=C_{1}+\sum_{i\neq k}c_{i}\left(\frac{\rho_{i}}{\rho}\right)^{l}.

The second term tends to
0
as
l\to\infty
.
the asymptotic follows once Lemma
4.9
proves
C_{1}>0
. ∎

To extract the exact coefficient
C_{1}
, we rely on the standard rational expansion theorem for distinct roots. We recall the precise theorem given in
[
GKP
]
.

Theorem 4.8

(Rational Expansion Theorem for distinct roots)
.

If

F(z)=\frac{B(z)}{R(z)},

where

R(z)=q_{0}\prod_{k=1}^{s}(1-\rho_{k}z),

and the numbers
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{s}
are distinct, and
B(z)
is a polynomial of degree less than
s
, then

[z^{n}]F(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{s}c_{k}\rho_{k}^{n},\qquad\text{where}~~c_{k}=-\frac{\rho_{k}\,B(1/\rho_{k})}{R^{\prime}(1/\rho_{k})}.

Lemma 4.9
.

The coefficient of
\rho^{l}
, i.e.,
C_{1}
is a positive real number.

Proof.

For simplification, let us consider
|\mathcal{N}_{2k}|
as
a_{k}
.

So, we rewrite (
4.3
) in terms of
a_{k},

a_{k}=2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}a_{k-j-1}+a_{k-r-1}.

This implies

(4.5)

a_{k}=0\cdot a_{k-1}+2a_{k-2}+2a_{k-3}+\cdots+2a_{k-r}+a_{k-r-1}.

Let us consider the power series

f(z)=\sum_{k\geq 1}a_{k}z^{k-1}.

We know the characteristic polynomial of the recurrence relation,

P(z)=z^{r+1}-2z^{r-1}-\cdots-2z^{2}-2z-1.

Then the reciprocal of
P
as follows,

(4.6)

\tilde{P}(z)=z^{r+1}P\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)=1-0\cdot z-2z^{2}-\cdots-2z^{r-1}-2z^{r}-z^{r+1}.

Now, we consider another polynomial
N(z)
such that

\tilde{P}(z)f(z)=N(z).

Then, we have

N(z)=a_{1}+(a_{2}-0\cdot a_{1})z+(a_{3}-0\cdot a_{2}-2a_{1})z^{2}+\cdots

+(a_{k}-0\cdot a_{k-1}-2a_{k-2}-\cdots-2a_{1})z^{k-1}+\cdots

+(a_{n+1}-0\cdot a_{n}-2a_{n-1}-\cdots-2a_{n-r+1}-a_{n-r})z^{n}+\cdots.

The coefficients of
z^{n}
for
n\geq r+1
are vanished, since we have the relation from (
4.5
),

a_{n+1}-\left(0\cdot a_{n}+2a_{n-1}+\cdots+2a_{n-r+1}+a_{n-r}\right)=0.

Therefore,

N(z)=b_{0}+b_{1}z+b_{2}z^{2}+\cdots+b_{r}z^{r},

where

b_{0}=a_{1},

b_{1}=a_{2}-0\cdot a_{1},

b_{2}=a_{3}-0\cdot a_{2}-2a_{1},

\vdots

b_{r}=a_{r+1}-0\cdot a_{r}-2a_{r-1}-\cdots-2a_{1}.

Now, we have
f(z)=\dfrac{N(z)}{\widetilde{P}(z)},
where the degree of
N(z)
less than the degree of
\widetilde{P}(z)
. In our situation, we apply Theorem
4.8
with

F(z)=f(z),~B(z)=N(z),~R(z)=\widetilde{P}(z).
Then, we have

[z^{n}]f(z)=a_{n+1}=\sum_{k=1}^{r+1}c_{k}\rho_{k}^{n+1},\qquad\text{where}~~c_{k}=-\frac{\rho_{k}\,N\!\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}\right)}{\widetilde{P}^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}\right)}.

If
\rho_{k}=\rho
, then the coefficient corresponding to the term
\rho^{n+1}
is
c_{k}:=C_{1}
.
To show
C_{1}
is positive, we first show that

\widetilde{P}^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\right)<0
.
From (
4.6
), we have

\widetilde{P}^{\prime}(z)=-2\cdot 2z-\cdots-2(r-1)z^{r-2}-2rz^{(r-1)}-(r+1)z^{r}.

Since all coefficients of the terms
z^{k}
for
k\geq 1
in
\widetilde{P}(z)
are non-positive and strictly negative for
k\geq 2
, it follows that
\widetilde{P}^{\prime}(z)<0
for all
z>0
. This implies
\widetilde{P}^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\right)<0
.

Now, our claim is that every
b_{k}
in
N(z)
is non-negative.
To prove that, we use the initial conditions on
a_{l}
from Lemma
4.2
.
Then

b_{0}=a_{1}=|\mathcal{N}_{2}|=\frac{1}{6}(2-2)=0,

b_{1}=a_{2}-0\cdot a_{1}=a_{2}=\frac{1}{6}(2^{2}+2),

b_{2}=a_{3}-0\cdot a_{2}-2a_{1}=\frac{1}{6}\left(2^{3}-2-(2^{2}+2)\right),

=\frac{1}{6}\left(2^{3}-2-2^{2}-2\right)>0.

In general,
if
m
is even and
m\neq r+1
, then

b_{m}=\frac{1}{6}\left(2^{m}+2-2\big(2^{m-2}+2+2^{m-3}-2+\cdots+2^{2}+2+2-2\big)\right).

Summing the geometric series,

b_{m}=\frac{1}{6}\left(2^{m}+2-2^{2}(2^{m-2}-1)\right)>0.

Similarly, if
m
is odd and
m\neq r+1
, then

b_{m}=\frac{1}{6}\left(2^{m}-2-2\big(2^{m-2}+2^{m-3}+\cdots+2^{2}+2\big)\right)>0.

In the case
m=r+1
, an extra term

\frac{1}{6}\left(2^{u+1}-2(-1)^{u+1}\right)

appears, that is non negative. That means,
b_{r+1}>0
.
Therefore, in
N(z)
there are no negative coefficients. In fact, except for the constant term, every coefficient is positive.

Thus, it follows that

N(z)>0\quad\text{for all }z>0.

In particular,

N\!\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\right)>0.

Hence,

C_{1}=-\frac{\rho\,N\!\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\right)}{\widetilde{P}^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\right)}>0.

∎

4.2.
When
r
is even

Assume that
r=2u
is even. By Lemma
4.2
, reciprocal conjugacy classes of odd word length occur, and these arise precisely from the symmetric
p
-reciprocal classes. Therefore, in this subsection we restrict to this setting.

Lemma 4.10
.

Suppose
\mathcal{N}_{2l-1}
be the set of conjugacy classes of reciprocal words of word length
2l-1
. Then we have :

(1)

If
l\leq r+u+1
, then,
|\mathcal{N}_{2l-1}|=\frac{1}{6}(2^{l-u-1}+2(-1)^{l-u-1})
.

(2)

If
l=r+u+2
, then
|\mathcal{N}_{2l-1}|=\frac{1}{6}(2^{l-u-1}+2(-1)^{l-u-1})+\frac{1}{6}(2^{u+1}+2(-1)^{u+1})
.

(3)

If
l\geq r+u+3
,

|\mathcal{N}_{2l-1}|=|\mathcal{N}_{2(l-u-1)}|=2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}|\mathcal{N}_{2(l-u-j-2)}|+|\mathcal{N}_{2(l-u-r-2)}|.

Proof.

To prove
(1)
, it suffices to show that

|\mathcal{N}_{2l-1}|=|\mathcal{N}_{2(l-u-1)}|.

Consider a reciprocal reduced word of length
2l-1
of the form

\big(\iota\tilde{\gamma}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{1}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{2}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{k_{n}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{n}}\dots\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{2}}\iota\gamma_{r}^{-k_{1}}\big)^{\epsilon},

where
\epsilon
is an odd positive integer.

Since the base word is symmetric
p
-reciprocal, its length is

L=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}|k_{i}|+2n+(r+1),

and therefore
L\geq r+1
. If
\epsilon>1
is odd, then
\epsilon\geq 3
, so

\epsilon L\geq 3L\geq 3(r+1).

But the given word has length
2l-1\leq 3r
, a contradiction. Hence necessarily
\epsilon=1
.

Then, we get this equation for the word length
2l-1
after rewriting the
k_{i}
’s,

2\sum_{i=1}^{n}|k_{i}|+2n+2qr+2u+1=2l-1

\Rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{n}|k_{i}|+n+qr=l-1-u.

(1)
follows from the above equality.

(2)
follows from the previous case and from the arguments in the proof of Lemma
4.2
and the fact that
[(\iota\tilde{\gamma})^{3}]
also belongs to
\mathcal{N}_{3(r+1)}.

For
(3)
, this will follow from the proof of Lemma
4.2
. ∎

Recall the Big
O
-notation, defined in Section
1.1
. To establish Corollary
1.3
, we observe the following lemmas.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
\beta=\lfloor\tfrac{l}{2}\rfloor
. Then,

|\mathcal{N}^{np}_{l}|=O\left(\rho^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{\beta+1}{2}\right\rfloor}\right),

where
\mathcal{N}^{np}_{l}
denotes the set of all non-primitive reciprocal classes in
\Gamma_{2r}
with word length
l
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{N}^{p}_{l}
denote the set of all primitive reciprocal classes in
\Gamma_{2r}
with word length
l
. Clearly, both
\mathcal{N}^{np}_{l}
and
\mathcal{N}^{p}_{l}
are sub collections of
\mathcal{N}_{l}
. Note that a non-primitive reciprocal class of word length
l
arises from a primitive class of word length
q
dividing
l
, where
q<l
. Therefore,

|\mathcal{N}^{np}_{l}|\leq\sum_{q\mid l,~q<l}|\mathcal{N}^{p}_{q}|\leq\sum_{q=1}^{\lfloor\frac{l}{2}\rfloor}|\mathcal{N}_{q}|.

Using the fact that
|\mathcal{N}_{q}|\sim C\rho^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{q+1}{2}\right\rfloor}
(where
C
is either
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
, depending on
q
), implies
\lvert\mathcal{N}_{q}\rvert=O\left(\rho^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{q+1}{2}\right\rfloor}\right)
, we obtain

\sum_{q=1}^{\lfloor\frac{l}{2}\rfloor}|\mathcal{N}_{q}|=\sum_{q=1}^{\lfloor\frac{l}{2}\rfloor}O\left(\rho^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{q+1}{2}\right\rfloor}\right).

Since the dominant term in this sum corresponds to the largest value of
q
, which is
\lfloor\tfrac{l}{2}\rfloor=\beta
, we conclude that

|\mathcal{N}^{np}_{l}|=O\left(\rho^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{\beta+1}{2}\right\rfloor}\right).

This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{l}
be the set of all reciprocal classes in
\Gamma_{2r}
with word length
l
, and
\mathcal{N}^{p}_{l}
be the subset of primitive reciprocal classes. Then

|\mathcal{N}^{p}_{l}|\sim|\mathcal{N}_{l}|.

Proof.

Let
\rho
be the unique positive root (mentioned in Theorem
4.6
) with maximum absolute value among the roots of the recurrence relation defined by the characteristic equation

x^{r+1}-2\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}x^{r-j}-1=0.

By Theorem
1.2
, we have positive integers
M^{\prime}
and
l_{0}
, such that the total number of reciprocal classes satisfies

|\mathcal{N}_{l}|\geq M^{\prime}\rho^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{l+1}{2}\right\rfloor},\qquad\text{
for all $l\geq l_{0}$.
}

and for positive integers
M
and
l_{1}
the number of non-primitive classes satisfies the upper bound

|\mathcal{N}^{np}_{l}|\leq M\rho^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{\beta+1}{2}\right\rfloor}\quad\text{for all $l\geq l_{1}$},

where
\text{for }\beta=\left\lfloor\tfrac{l}{2}\right\rfloor.

Also, by definition,

|\mathcal{N}_{l}|=|\mathcal{N}^{np}_{l}|+|\mathcal{N}^{p}_{l}|\leq M\rho^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{\beta+1}{2}\right\rfloor}+|\mathcal{N}^{p}_{l}|.

We obtain the inequality

|\mathcal{N}_{l}|-M\rho^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{\beta+1}{2}\right\rfloor}\leq|\mathcal{N}^{p}_{l}|\leq|\mathcal{N}_{l}|.

Dividing by
|\mathcal{N}_{l}|
, we get

1-\frac{M\rho^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{\beta+1}{2}\right\rfloor}}{|\mathcal{N}_{l}|}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{N}^{p}_{l}|}{|\mathcal{N}_{l}|}\leq 1

1-\frac{M\rho^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{\beta+1}{2}\right\rfloor}}{M^{\prime}\rho^{\lfloor\frac{l+1}{2}\rfloor}}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{N}^{p}_{l}|}{|\mathcal{N}_{l}|}\leq 1.

Since
\beta=\lfloor\tfrac{l}{2}\rfloor<l
, the denominator dominates as
l\to\infty
, and the ratio tends to zero. Thus,

\frac{|\mathcal{N}^{p}_{l}|}{|\mathcal{N}_{l}|}\to 1\quad\text{as }l\to\infty,

which implies

|\mathcal{N}^{p}_{l}|\sim|\mathcal{N}_{l}|.

This proves the lemma.
∎

The corollary follows from Lemma
4.13
and Theorem
1.2
. ∎
