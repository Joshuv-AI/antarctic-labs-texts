OC43 clinical isolate spike proteins have distinct carbohydrate-binding properties
Authors: Zaky Hassan, Min Jin, Ying Liu, Zhijie Li, Alan H M Wong, Marc Desforges, Adam Forman, Mark Nitz, Tarini Gunawardena, Theo J Moraes, Masahiro Narimatsu, Jeffrey L Wrana, Hai Yu, Xi Chen, James M Rini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe human coronavirus HCoV-OC43 (OC43) is the most widespread of the four common cold-causing seasonal coronaviruses, and tissue culture-adapted strains of it have been used for ~50 years. Nevertheless, clinical isolates of OC43 differ from tissue culture-adapted OC43 in ways that call into question the value of the latter as a model. Among these are differences in their entry mechanisms and the activities of their hemagglutinin-esterases (HE). We now show that the spike proteins of OC43 clinical isolates differ from that of the tissue culture-adapted reference strain (OC43-Lab) in their carbohydrate-binding properties and ability to bind mucins, decoy receptors cleaved by the HE. We also show that, unlike HCoV-HKU1 (HKU1), they do not bind with high affinity and specificity the 9-O-acetylated α2–8-linked disialic acid moiety implicated in viral entry for OC43-Lab and HKU1. The spike proteins of the OC43 clinical isolates possess two inserts, not found in OC43-Lab, that flank the carbohydrate-binding site. Our structural analysis of a representative clinical isolate shows that insert-2 is a determinant of these specificity differences and that the carbohydrate-binding site undergoes conformational changes on carbohydrate binding. These structural features are shared by HKU1 and suggest common mechanisms for adaptation to the human sialoglycome.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Fluorescence imaging, Surface plasmon resonance, Virus-host interactions

Introduction

Four seasonal coronaviruses are responsible for a significant percentage of common cold cases in humans. They typically cause mild upper respiratory tract symptoms, but they can have more serious outcomes in high-risk groups1,2. Of the four, HCoV-OC43 (OC43) and HCoV-HKU1 (HKU1) are betacoronaviruses of the embecovirus subgenera characterized by their use of carbohydrate receptors containing a 9-O-acetylated N-acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5,9Ac2)3–5, one of the several O-acetylated sialic acids found in mammalian tissues6–8. In the late 1960s, OC43 was adapted to tissue culture and since then tissue culture-adapted strains have been widely used in the study of this virus9. In 2006 an infectious cDNA clone10 which we have termed OC43-Lab was generated using the VR-759 virus stock obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The genome sequence of OC43-lab serves as the NCBI reference sequence and has formed the basis for the key structural and biochemical experiments characterizing the OC43 spike3,4,11,12. Moreover, the ability to generate mutant viruses using the cDNA clone has greatly facilitated structure-function analysis13–15. The neuroinvasive properties of OC43-Lab have been well studied in mice and clinical isolates found to infect the central nervous system in human patients have also been described16–20. More recently, lab-adapted OC43 has emerged as a biosafety level 2 model for the development of therapeutics against SARS-CoV-2 and the finding that prior exposure to OC43 and the other seasonal coronaviruses can influence the immune response to SARS-CoV-2 has further stimulated research on this seasonal coronavirus21–23.

Despite widespread use, lab-adapted OC43 differs from the clinical isolates of OC43 in important ways. OC43-Lab, for example, can be propagated in several established cell lines but like HKU1, the OC43 clinical isolates require primary airway epithelial cells differentiated at the air-liquid interface24–27. The VR-1558 viral stock (ATCC) was derived from VR-759 and work using these stocks and OC43-Lab provides evidence that tissue culture-adapted OC43 uses an endosomal entry route while the clinical isolates studied enter at the cell surface15,28–31. OC43-Lab lacks the optimal spike protein S1/S2 furin cleavage site implicated in viral entry that is found in many betacoronaviruses32,33 including the OC43 clinical isolates32,34,35. The adaptation of OC43 to tissue culture has also been associated with a dependence on proteoglycans for entry, an outcome linked to the loss of an optimal furin cleavage site35–37. Interestingly, the suboptimal furin cleavage site in OC43-Lab has also been associated with its neurovirulence in mice14,15. Taken together, these observations underscore the importance of characterizing the clinical isolates of this widespread human coronavirus.

OC43 and HKU1 entered the human population independently38,39 and their glycan receptors have not yet been identified. Glycan microarray analysis, however, shows that both OC43-Lab and HKU1 bind the 9-O-acetylated α2–8-linked disialic acid moiety characteristic of complex gangliosides11, a class of sialic acid-containing glycosphingolipids40. Moreover, overexpression of the ganglioside glycosyltransferase ST8SIA1 (GD3 synthase) leads to increased cell staining and pseudotyped viral entry for both viruses11. The GD3 ganglioside is characterized by the presence of an α2–8-linked disialic acid moiety and is one of several gangliosides known to be 9-O-acetylated41,42. Although OC43-Lab and HKU1 both bind the 9-O-acetylated form of this disialic acid moiety, recent work has shown that the HKU1 spike protein possesses a 6-amino acid insert (insert-2), not found in OC43-Lab, which confers increased affinity and specificity for this carbohydrate determinant43. The spike protein of OC43-Lab also differs from HKU1 in its glycoprotein binding, erythrocyte agglutination and cell binding properties3–5. OC43-Lab is capable of agglutinating rat red blood cells and binding bovine submaxillary mucin (BSM), for example, while HKU1 does so only weakly3,4,44. As mucins serve as decoy receptors, their binding by viruses play critical roles in viral entry, escape from infected cells and tissue tropism45,46. Together, these observations suggest that OC43-Lab differs from HKU1 in important ways.

Embecoviruses possess hemagglutinin-esterase (HE), a viral membrane protein akin to the neuraminidases of the influenza A and B viruses. It contains both lectin-binding properties and acetylesterase activity. It removes the O-acetyl group from acetylated sialic acids, including those found on decoy receptors such as mucins, and it plays roles in both cell binding and entry as well as virus release from infected cells47,48. Work on OC43-Lab, for example, has shown that the catalytic activity of the HE strongly enhances viral production13, an outcome attributed to efficient viral dissemination in tissue culture. Forced evolution studies using the bovine coronavirus (BCoV) from which OC43 is thought to have originated39 have also established that a balance between the HE carbohydrate binding affinity and that of the spike protein is a determinant of viral fitness48. Notably, the lectin activity of the OC43 HE was lost after transmission to humans, an indication of changing interactions with host carbohydrate receptors49. HKU1 also lacks HE lectin activity, and this parallel suggests common mechanisms for adaptation to the human sialoglycome49. Indeed, the HE lectin activity facilitates the de-O-acetylation of the clustered sialoglycans found on mucins, but how its loss might be linked to viral fitness is not yet understood.

The coronavirus spike protein can be divided into the S1 and S2 subunits, which are responsible for receptor binding and fusion of the viral and host cell membranes, respectively. S1 contains four domains (D1-D4), and the canonical 9-O-acetyl sialic acid-binding site of the embecoviruses is located in the D1 domain (also known as the NTD and S1A) (Fig. 1a–c). The D2 domain (also known as the CTD and S1B, and where appropriate, the receptor-binding domain, RBD) is typically responsible for binding the host protein receptor when one exists50,51. Large conformational changes in the S2 subunit on conversion from the pre-fusion to the post-fusion forms of the spike protein are responsible for driving membrane fusion52,53. The D2 domain can be found in both the down and up conformations, and the latter is required when D2 is used to bind a protein receptor. In the simplest of models, protein receptor binding promotes the D2 domain up conformation leading to S1-S2 dissociation and conversion to the postfusion conformation required to drive membrane fusion33,52–55.

In addition to a carbohydrate receptor containing 9-O-acetyl sialic acid, HKU1 has also been found to recognize a protein receptor, the cell surface protease TMPRSS256. TMPRSS2 can only be bound when D2 is in the up conformation, and it has also been found that carbohydrate binding to the D1 domain promotes the D2 domain up conformation43,57–62. Indeed, saturating concentrations of the glycan moiety of the 9-O-acetylated GD3 ganglioside led to a ~ 7-fold increase in the affinity shown by the HKU1 spike protein for TMPRSS243, a demonstration of the interplay between its carbohydrate and protein receptors. It is currently unknown whether OC43-Lab, other tissue culture-adapted strains, or the OC43 clinical isolates employ a protein receptor.

We now show that compared to OC43-Lab, the clinical isolates of OC43 tested all show reduced cell staining and decreased affinity for both small soluble saccharides and BSM. The spike proteins of the OC43 clinical isolates contain two short inserts (insert-1 and insert-2) not found in OC43-Lab, and we show that insert-2 is a determinant of these specificity differences. We have also found that unlike that reported for HKU143, the OC43 clinical isolates do not bind the 9-O-acetylated α2–8-linked disialic acid moiety with high affinity and specificity. This carbohydrate moiety has been implicated in pseudoviral entry for OC43-Lab and HKU111, and this represents an important difference between the OC43 clinical isolates tested and both OC43-Lab and HKU1. HKU1 also possesses an insert-2, and our structural analysis now shows that how insert-2 modulates carbohydrate binding is different in these viruses. Finally, we have shown that the carbohydrate-binding site of a representative OC43 clinical isolate undergoes concerted conformational changes on carbohydrate binding, a feature not shared by OC43-Lab. The presence of insert-2, carbohydrate-dependent conformational changes in the canonical 9-O-acetyl sialic acid binding site and the loss of the HE lectin activity are all shared by HKU1. Taken together this suggests that common mechanisms have been used by these viruses for adaptation to the human sialoglycome.

Results

The OC43 clinical isolates possess inserts that flank the carbohydrate-binding site

Sequence analysis shows that the clinical isolates of OC43 possess two short inserts (insert-1 and insert-2) that flank the canonical embecovirus carbohydrate-binding site and that are not found in OC43-Lab. Insert-1 and insert-2 are typically 5 and 4 amino acids in length, respectively, and they show considerable sequence variation (Fig. 1c,d). The four amino acids prior to insert-2, found in all embecoviruses, are also variable, and we have named them “preinsert-2”. Phylogenetic analysis of the OC43 D1 domain alone showed that it segregates into three classes which correlate with the sequence of preinsert-2. Class 1 contains OC43-Lab and its preinsert-2 sequence is typically CNSK, while in Classes 2 and 3 it is typically CIRR and CISR, respectively (Fig. 1e,f). To explore the possibility that the inserts found in the OC43 clinical isolates might be determinants of carbohydrate-binding specificity, we selected four representatives for further study: OC43-C1 (Class 3), OC43-C2 (Class 2), OC43-C3 (Class 3) and OC43-C5 (Class 1) (Fig. 1d,f).

The OC43 clinical isolates differ from OC43-Lab in their cell-binding properties

To assess cell binding properties, we generated tetravalent streptavidin-phycoerythrin complexes of the various D1 domains (SAPE-D1) and assessed binding by fluorescence microscopy and/or flow cytometry. The non-binding mutant OC43-Lab N27A12 was used as a non-binding control. Clinical isolates of both OC43 and HKU1 have been found to infect the ciliated cells of primary human airway epithelia differentiated at the air-liquid interface24–27, and we tested these cells first. We found that all the SAPE-D1 complexes tested stained these cells at the apical surface just below that of the tubulin marker for cilia (Fig. 2a–f). The intensity of staining observed among the OC43 clinical isolates was similar but less than that observed for OC43-Lab and HKU1 (Supplementary Table 1).

We next tested LAN-5 cells, a neuroblastoma cell line previously used for studying OC43-Lab13–15. The SAPE-D1 complex of OC43-Lab stained LAN-5 cells well while that of the four clinical isolates did so only weakly if at all (Fig. 2i-m; Supplementary Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 1). Mutants of the OC43-C2 D1 domain lacking both inserts (OC43-C2∆1∆2) or insert-2 alone (OC43-C2∆2) showed only small increases in staining relative to the unmutated form (Supplementary Fig. 2), evidence that their presence does not account for the weak staining observed. The SAPE-D1 complex of HKU1 stained LAN-5 cells at a level between that of OC43-Lab and the clinical isolates (Fig. 2h,i).

Overexpression of the ganglioside glycosyltransferase ST8SIA1 (GD3 synthase) has been shown to increase both HEK293T cell staining (using S1A-Fc fusion proteins) and pseudoviral entry for OC43-Lab and HKU111. Consistent with these observations, our SAPE-D1 complexes of both OC43-Lab and HKU1 also showed large increases in cell staining after ST8SIA1 overexpression in HEK293F cells (Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 1). Moderate increases were also observed for OC43-C1, but little if any increase was observed for the other OC43 clinical isolates. Both OC43-C2∆1∆2 and OC43-C2∆2 stained at levels similar to that of OC43-C2, a further indication that the inserts are not the basis for the weak staining observed relative to OC43-Lab in these cell staining experiments (Supplementary Fig. 3).

HEK293 cells are deficient in a number of ganglioside glycosyltransferases63, and we repeated the cell staining experiments using Madin Darby canine kidney cells (MDCK), a cell line reported to have high levels of 9-O-acetylated sialic acids on its cell surface64. ST8SIA1 overexpression in these cells led to large increases in staining for both OC43-Lab and HKU1 as well as small increases for all the OC43 clinical isolates except OC43-C3 (Fig. 2o–u; Supplementary Table 1). Notably, OC43-C1 stained wild-type MDCK cells at levels similar to that of OC43-Lab and HKU1 (Supplementary Fig. 4), a further indication of specificity differences among the OC43 clinical isolates. Taken together, the increased staining observed after ST8SIA1 overexpression suggests the ability to bind 9-O-acetylated GD3 or other 9-O-acetylated gangliosides downstream of it in the ganglioside biosynthetic pathway.

The OC43 clinical isolates differ from OC43-Lab in their red blood cell agglutination and BSM binding properties

It has been shown that the D1 domain and the S1 subunit of OC43-Lab, expressed as an Fc-fusion protein, is more effective at agglutinating rat and mouse red blood cells than the D1 domain of HKU13,4. We have now extended the analysis to include the OC43 clinical isolates using tetravalent streptavidin-D1 complexes (SA-D1) as the agglutinin. OC43-C3 was unable to agglutinate mouse red blood cells at the concentrations tested and it agglutinated rat red blood cells weakly (16-fold weaker than OC43-Lab). The other OC43 clinical isolates were at least 2-fold weaker agglutinins than OC43-Lab for both red blood cell types (Supplementary Fig. 5; Supplementary Table 1). Relative to OC43-C2, OC43-C2∆1∆2 and OC43-C2∆2 showed a 2-fold improvement in their ability to agglutinate. Consistent with that previously reported, HKU1 was a significantly weaker agglutinin than OC43-Lab for both mouse and rat red blood cells (Supplementary Fig. 5).

It has also been reported that the D1 domain of OC43-Lab binds BSM, a model mucin widely used in the study of these human coronaviruses, with higher affinity than the D1 domain of HKU1 does3,4. To assess the OC43 clinical isolates, we used a biolayer interferometry (BLI)-based binding assay, with BSM immobilized on the biosensor and the monovalent D1 domain in solution. Notably, the D1 domains of the OC43 clinical isolates bound BSM 14.9- to 63.3-fold weaker than that of OC43-Lab (KD = 4.8 ± 1.8 µM) (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. 6–7; Supplementary Table 1). OC43-C2∆2 bound 4.1-fold tighter than OC43-C2, an indication that insert-2 is an impediment to BSM binding. These results establish that, like HKU1, the D1 domains of the OC43 clinical isolates bind BSM more weakly than OC43-Lab does. The very weak red blood cell agglutination and BSM-binding properties shown by OC43-C3 sets it apart from both OC43-Lab and the other OC43 clinical isolates tested.

The OC43 clinical isolates bind a range of 9-O-acetylated sialoglycans weakly

Using a surface plasmon resonance-based assay, we next measured the affinities of the various D1 domains for a small set of soluble oligosaccharides (Fig. 3; Supplemenatry Fig. 8–13). Included are the 9-O-acetylated forms of the glycan moieties of GM2, GM3 and GD3 gangliosides, as well as both α2–6- and α2–3-linked sialylated N-acetyllactosamine. The latter are oligosaccharide fragments found on both N- and O-glycans. Striking was the observation that the OC43 clinical isolates bind the methyl glycoside of 9-O-acetyl sialic acid (9OAc-Sia) with an affinity 1.9- to 17.2-fold lower than that observed for OC43-Lab (KD = 29.6 ± 1.7 µM) (Supplementary Table 1). The results also show that with few exceptions, the affinity of the OC43 clinical isolates for all the compounds tested was equivalent to or weaker than that for the monosaccharide 9OAc-Sia, an indication that they are being bound through the terminal 9-O-acetylated sialic acid moiety alone. As shown for OC43-C2, deletion of insert-2 led to small increases in affinity (<2.6-fold) for all the compounds tested, an indication that it is a weak impediment to binding in these soluble saccharide binding studies.

Also notable were the results obtained for the aminophenyl glycoside of the 9-O-acetylated GD3 glycan moiety (9OAc-GD3ap), the only compound tested containing the α2–8-linked disialic acid moiety. OC43-Lab bound 9OAc-GD3ap with a KD of 12.1 ± 0.6 µM, an affinity 2.4-fold higher than that observed for 9OAc-Sia. This was the highest affinity shown by OC43-Lab for any of the compounds tested, confirming its specificity for the 9-O-acetylated disialic acid moiety as previously reported11. In contrast, the OC43 clinical isolates bound 9OAc-GD3ap relatively weakly with affinities ranging from 47.2 ± 5.8 µM to 131.2 ± 16.4 µM. By comparison HKU1 bound 9OAc-GD3ap with a KD of 5.1 ± 0.3 µM, an affinity 13.3- to 25.3-fold higher than its affinity for the other glycans in this panel43.

In broad terms, the invariably lower affinity shown by the OC43 clinical isolates for this panel of 9-O-acetylated α2–3-, α2–6- and α2–8-linked sialic acid-containing compounds provides an explanation for why they stained cells, agglutinated mouse and rat red blood cells, and bound BSM less effectively than OC43-Lab did. The data also showed that, as a group, they did not show high binding affinity or specificity for 9OAc-GD3ap, a compound containing the 9-O-acetylated disialic acid moiety implicated in binding and pseudoviral entry for both OC43-Lab and HKU111.

OC43-Lab recognizes the 9-O-acetylated α2–8-linked disialic acid moiety

To provide structural insight into the affinity and specificity differences observed between OC43-Lab and the OC43 clinical isolates, we determined the cryo-EM structures of the spike proteins of both OC43-Lab and OC43-C2 in complex with 9OAc-GD3ap. OC43-C2 is a Class 2 member, and the sequence of its preinsert-2 and insert-2 together (C261IRRPKDG268) represents the largest block (26.4%) of the unique sequences deposited (Fig. 1f). Also reported is the cryo-EM structure of the OC43-C2 spike in the apo form. In all cases, the D2 domains of the trimers were found to be in the all-down conformation. All three structures were determined with C3 symmetry imposed, and the overall resolution obtained ranged from 1.68-1.93 Å (Supplementary Table 2; Supplementary Fig. 14). Local refinement of the D1 domains led to increased local resolution (Supplementary Table 2; Supplementary Fig. 14) and an improved description of the canonical embecovirus 9-O-acetyl sialic acid-binding site in all cases.

In the OC43-Lab complex with 9OAc-GD3ap, cryo-EM density for only the two sialic acid moieties of the tetrasaccharide is observed (Fig. 4a–d). The terminal 9-O-acetylated α2–8-linked sialic acid sits in the canonical carbohydrate binding site, and its 9-O-acetyl, 5-N-acetyl and carboxyl groups all make key interactions with the protein (Supplementary Fig. 15). The interactions observed are very similar to those previously reported for OC43-Lab and HKU1 and key among these are interactions with Trp-90, Asn-27, Thr-31, Lys-81 and Ser-83 (OC43-Lab numbering), residues all shown to be critical for pseudoviral entry12,43,62. The exceptional quality of the D1 domain in the locally refined map has also provided new detail and shows that several water molecules are found to bridge the interaction between the protein and the carbohydrate (Supplementary Fig. 15; Fig. 4a–d; Fig. 5a). The density for the α2–3-linked sialic acid is weaker than that of the 9-O-acetylated α2–8-linked sialic acid, and it sits in a pocket (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Fig. 16a) formed in part by preinsert-2 (C256NSK259). The NH of its 5-N-acetyl group makes the sole interaction with the protein, donating a hydrogen bond to the side chain of Ser-83, a residue that is also hydrogen-bonded to the α2–8-linked sialic acid. The ability to accommodate and interact with the α2–3-linked sialic acid provides a rationalization for the 2.4-fold increase in affinity observed for 9OAc-GD3ap over that of 9OAc-Sia alone (Fig. 3).

Preinsert-2 and insert-2 modulate 9OAc-GD3ap binding in OC43-C2

In contrast to that observed for OC43-Lab, strong density for only the terminal 9-O-acetylated α2–8-linked sialic acid moiety is observed in the OC43-C2 – 9OAc-GD3ap complex (Fig. 4e–h), and the interactions with it are essentially identical to that observed in the OC43-Lab complex. Density for the glycerol tail of the α2–3-linked sialic acid can also be observed, and it indicates that the conformation of the α2–8 glycosidic linkage differs significantly from that observed in the OC43-Lab complex (Fig. 5a,b; Supplementary Fig. 16). The observation that OC43-C2 recognizes only the terminal 9-O-acetylated sialic acid moiety provides a rationalization for why it does not bind any of the compounds tested with an affinity greater than that observed for the monosaccharide 9OAc-Sia alone (Fig. 3).

The α2–3-linked sialic acid cannot be accommodated as it is in the OC43-Lab complex, as what would otherwise be a steric conflict with OC43-C2 preinsert-2 (C261IRR264) leads to its disordering. However, it is the well-defined conformation of preinsert-2 and insert-2 as a unit—anchored at both ends to the D1 domain framework—that leads to this outcome. In all embecoviruses, the first residue of preinsert-2 is a cysteine (Cys-256 in OC43-Lab; Supplementary Fig. 17), which is disulfide-bonded to a framework residue (Cys-176 in OC43-Lab) and this constitutes the N-terminal anchor. The C-terminal anchor results from the burial of an apolar sidechain in a conserved apolar pocket. In OC43-C2, it is the sidechain of Phe-269, the residue following insert-2 that is buried in the pocket. This is to be contrasted with OC43-Lab (which lacks insert-2) where the sidechain of Leu-260, the residue following preinsert-2, is buried in the pocket (Fig. 5a,b; Supplementary Fig. 18). As such, it is preinsert-2 that is anchored at both ends in OC43-Lab, while in OC43-C2, it is preinsert-2 and insert-2 as a unit. These anchored structures are conformationally well-defined and dictate the different ways that 9OAc-GD3ap is engaged by these two viruses. In all the OC43 clinical isolates, the last residue of insert-2 is invariably a glycine (Fig. 1d) and its conformation ensures that Phe-269 can access the apolar pocket (Fig. 5b). Given that Phe-269 is also highly conserved, it is likely that preinsert-2 and insert-2 will be anchored to the framework in the other OC43 clinical isolates as described here for OC43-C2.

The OC43-C2 carbohydrate-binding site accesses both an open and a closed conformation

Comparison of the OC43-C2 apo structure with that of its 9OAc-GD3ap complex shows that the canonical 9-O-acetyl sialic acid-binding site can assume what we have termed the open and closed conformations. In the open conformation observed in the apo structure, residues Asn-32 to Thr-36 are significantly displaced relative to that observed in the carbohydrate-bound closed conformation (Fig. 6a,b,e; Supplementary Fig. 19b). Thr-36 is not hydrogen bonded to Trp-95 in the open conformation, and it must move 10.9 Å to do so on conversion to the closed conformation. Moreover, in the open conformation, the sidechain of Asn-32 (Asn-27 in OC43-Lab) is not in position to make the key hydrogen bond with the 9-O-acetyl group that is observed when carbohydrate is bound. The structures also show that conversion from the open to the closed conformation is correlated with a two-residue register shift in downstream residues such that Ile-41 now occupies the volume formerly filled by Ile-43 (Fig. 6e; Supplementary Fig. 19b). The equivalent open to closed conformational change and register shift are also observed in HKU1 (Fig. 6c,d,f; Supplementary Fig. 19a) where they are associated with an additional 15-degree rotation of the D1 main domain. On carbohydrate binding, these conformational changes in HKU1 serve to promote the D2 domain down to up transition required to bind its protein receptor, TMPRSS243,62. This is to be contrasted with OC43-C2 where carbohydrate binding promotes the carbohydrate-binding site open to closed conformation and register shift but does not lead to the D1 main domain rotation or the D2 domain up conformation as we now show. Comparison of our OC43-Lab–9OAc-GD3ap complex with that of the previously reported apo structure12 shows that OC43-Lab is yet again different. In both the absence and presence of bound carbohydrate, its 9-O-acetyl sialic acid binding-site is in the closed conformation and the downstream residues are found in the register-shifted position (Supplementary Fig. 20). As with OC43-C2, the binding of carbohydrate does not lead to the D1 main domain rotation or the D2 domain up conformation. For OC43-C2, the role played by the open to closed transition and the correlated register shift on carbohydrate binding is not clear, but the parallels with HKU1 suggest a common function as discussed below.

Discussion

Sequence analysis shows that a subset of embecoviruses possess short amino acid inserts that flank the canonical 9-O-acetyl sialic acid-binding site that are not found in OC43-Lab, the OC43 reference strain derived from the tissue culture-adapted VR-759 viral stock. Among clinical isolates, insert-1 is found only in OC43, while insert-2 is found in both OC43 and HKU1. Notably, insert-2 is also found in some murine embecoviruses, including the well-studied Murine Hepatitis Virus (Supplementary Fig. 17). As we now show, preinsert-2 and insert-2 function as a unit to mediate carbohydrate-binding specificity and affinity, roles that establish them as determinants of viral entry. Although we have not identified a role for insert-1, its proximity to the carbohydrate-binding site suggests the potential to impact on the binding of extended sialoglyconjugates and/or the approach of virus to host cell surfaces. Insert-1, insert-2 and preinsert-2 all show considerable sequence variation, an observation that may reflect mutational changes driven by escape from receptor-blocking neutralizing antibodies. Indeed, a subset of neutralizing antibodies that bind the D1 domain of OC43-Lab are unable to bind D1 of selected OC43 clinical isolates, an outcome proposed to result from sequence variation in the vicinity of the carbohydrate-binding site65.

Although OC43-Lab lacks both inserts, the absence of inserts is not strictly correlated with tissue culture adaptation. Both insert-1 and insert-2 were observed in early sequencing efforts that also used the VR-759 virus stock39,66 and inserts were recently observed in viruses originating from VR-155867,68 (Supplementary Fig. 21), an ATCC virus stock derived from VR-759 and which now replaces it. In all cases, the inserts observed in these tissue-culture adapted strains are very similar to those of OC43-C5 (Supplementary Fig. 21), an observation consistent with the fact that like OC43-Lab they are Class 1 viruses possessing a CNSK presinsert-2 (Fig. 1e,f). That viruses with and without inserts have originated from the VR-759 stock may reflect differences in adaptation to tissue culture prior to sequencing, a suggestion consistent with our observation that insert-2 is a determinant of carbohydrate binding affinity and specificity. Taken together, these results underscore the importance of carefully interpreting and qualifying the results derived from the study of both clinical isolates and tissue-culture adapted strains of OC43.

Among our findings is the observation that the OC43 clinical isolates bound 9OAc-GD3ap with affinities 3.6- to 10.8-fold lower than OC43-Lab does and 8.5- to 25.7-fold lower than HKU1 does. The significance of the relatively weak binding shown by the OC43 clinical isolates stems from the fact that the 9-O-acetylated α2–8-linked disialic acid moiety characteristic of GD3 has been implicated in cell binding and pseudoviral entry for both OC43-Lab and HKU111. We have also found that OC43-Lab and OC43-C2 engage the α2–8-linked disialic acid moiety differently and that this is the result of differences in the sequence and structure of preinsert-2 and insert-2 and how these elements are anchored to the D1 domain framework (Fig. 5a,b). In OC43-Lab which lacks insert-2, the residue following preinsert-2 sits in an apolar pocket to form the second anchor point (Fig. 5a,b), while in OC43-C2 it is the residue following insert-2. This is to be further contrasted with HKU1, where the final residue of preinsert-2 (C242NAI245) sits in the apolar pocket and this positions insert-2 after the second anchor point, not before it, as seen in OC43-C2 (Fig. 5c). As a result, OC43-C2 engages only the terminal α2–8-linked 9-O-acetyl sialic acid of the disialoside, while HKU1 engages both sialic acid moieties43. Sequence analysis suggests that these structural differences will be respectively conserved in the clinical isolates of OC43 and HKU1, and that preinsert-2 and insert-2 have evolved to modulate carbohydrate binding affinity and specificity in this way.

Despite differences, we have also found striking parallels between the OC43 clinical isolates and HKU1. Among these is relatively weak affinity for BSM compared to that observed for OC43-Lab. Moreover, as we have shown for OC43-C2, insert-2 serves as an impediment to BSM binding as does insert-2 and its associated N-glycan in HKU13. Mucins serve as viral decoy receptors45,46 and low affinity for them would be expected to confer a fitness advantage on the clinical isolates of both OC43 and HKU1. It might also explain why the HE lectin activity of the clinical isolates of both viruses has been lost49. The lectin activity facilitates the deacetylation of the clustered glycans typical of mucins, and low affinity for them would be consistent with a reduced need to do so efficiently. Relevant to this discussion, HE and spike protein carbohydrate-binding affinity as well as HE catalytic activity have all been linked to viral fitness in tissue culture and suggest parallels with influenza virus13,45,48.

In a further parallel with HKU1, we have found that the canonical embecovirus 9-O-acetyl sialic acid-binding site of OC43-C2 can be found in both the open and closed conformations and that carbohydrate binding promotes the open to closed transition (Fig. 6e,f; Supplementary Fig 18). In addition, the transition is found to be correlated with a two-residue register shift in downstream residues in both viruses. In HKU1 these changes are further correlated with a rotation of the D1 main domain and promotion of the D2 domain down to up transition43,62. Promoting the up conformation on carbohydrate binding in this way has been proposed to facilitate TMPRSS2 binding in all the HKU1 genotypes59,60,62. In OC43-C2, however, the conformational changes beyond that of the register shift do not ensue and carbohydrate binding does not lead to the D2 domain up conformation as we have now shown. This establishes that the open to closed transition and register shift are not solely linked to promoting the D2 domain up conformation and suggests that HKU1 might simply have co-opted them to do so. It further suggests that these viruses differ in how the postfusion conformation is triggered and whether the OC43 clinical isolates or OC43-Lab will be found to employ a protein receptor is an open and important question.

Conversion of the carbohydrate-binding site from the open to the closed conformation would be expected to occur at the expense of carbohydrate-binding affinity and we posit that this might serve a common functional role in the clinical isolates of both OC43 and HKU1. Indeed, it would be expected to lead to relatively weak affinity for 9-O-acetylated sialoglycans that are not strengthened by additional interactions and this might provide a route to evading decoy receptors such as mucins. As shown by HKU1, additional interactions mediated by insert-2 lead to affinity and specificity for the 9-O-acetylated α2–8-linked disialoside43. Although the significance of this specificity is not yet clear, it is notable that this carbohydrate determinant is not found on at least some mucins types8,44. A mechanism for generating weakened affinity is also well aligned with current thinking on how dynamic low-affinity interactions promote virus mobility at the cell surface while permitting penetration through the glycocalyx to access high-affinity receptors45,46. Although the analysis of additional clinical isolates will be required, it is tempting to speculate that the open and closed conformations, the presence of insert-2, and the loss of HE lectin activity—features shared by OC43-C2 and HKU1—are all associated with adaptation to the human sialoglycome. In any case, our results establish the importance of studying the OC43 clinical isolates in efforts aimed at characterizing their relevant receptors and the basis for how they have evolved to infect their human hosts.

Methods

Expression and purification of the soluble forms of the OC43 spike proteins and the D1 domain alone

Soluble secreted forms of the spike proteins of OC43-Lab (NCBI accession AAT84354.1) and OC43-C2 (NCBI accession ARA15421.1) were produced in HEK293F cells following that previously described43,69,70. In brief, HEK293F cells stably expressing an rtTA helper plasmid (pB-RN) were co-transfected in a 24 well plate with 0.1 μg of a transposon helper plasmid (pB-ase) and 0.9 μg of a plasmid containing the spike cDNA cloned into an inducible piggyBac transposon-based expression plasmid (pB) using 2 μL of 293Fectin (Invitrogen, Catalog Number: 12347019) as a transfection reagent. Cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 3% FBS, and cells stably expressing the plasmids were selected and maintained by culturing in 2 µg/mL puromycin. Cells were then trypsinized and expanded into a T75 flask, followed by expansion into a T175 flask after reaching confluence. Finally, cells in the T175 flask were trypsinized, and expanded into shaker flasks in suspension culture using Freestyle media (Gibco, Catalog Number: 12338018) with 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Catalog Number: 15140122). At a density of 1 million cells/mL, cells were supplemented with 1 μg/mL aprotinin and induced with 1 μg/mL doxycycline for 2 days.

The cDNA for residues 15-1295 (OC43-Lab) and 15-1300 (OC43-C2) were codon-optimized and modified to introduce three proline residues (T1025P, A1070P, L1071P and T1030P, A1075P, L1076P, respectively), and a C-terminal Fibritin foldon domain to stabilize the prefusion conformation. Following the foldon domain, 6-His and Avi-tags were incorporated. The secreted protein was purified by Ni2+-NTA affinity chromatography followed by gel filtration (Superose 6 Increase, Cytiva). Depending on the application, the secreted protein was further purified by HIC (HiTrap Butyl HP, Cytiva) and ion exchange chromatography (HiTrap Q, Cytiva).

Soluble secreted forms of the D1 domains of OC43-Lab, OC43-C1 (NCBI accession AMK59677.1), OC43-C2 (NCBI accession ARA15421.1), OC43-C3 (NCBI accession AGT51790.1), and OC43-C5 (NCBI accession AIX10749.1) were produced in HEK293F GnT1- cells. In brief, HEK293F GnT1- cells were transfected in a 24 well plate with 0.75 μg of pB-D1, 0.15 μg pB-RN and 0.1 μg pB-ase per well using 2 μL of 293Fectin as the transfection reagent. Stable cells were selected and maintained with 2 µg/mL puromycin and 200 µg/mL neomycin (G418) and expanded as described above.

The cDNA for residues; 15-307 (OC43-Lab), 15-312 (OC43-C1), 15-316 (OC43-C2), 15-316 (OC43-C3), and 15-309 (OC43-C5) were codon-optimized, and a C-terminal 6-His and Avi-tag was incorporated. The secreted proteins were purified by Ni2+-NTA affinity chromatography followed by gel filtration (Superdex 200 Increase, Cytiva). The OC43-C2Δ1Δ2, OC43-C2Δ2 and OC43-Lab N27A D1 domain mutants were produced using standard mutagenesis methods and purified in the same way. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing. The amino acid sequences for all the constructs can be found in Supplementary Fig. 22.

Sequence and phylogenetic analysis of OC43 spike sequences

HCoV-OC43 viral sequences were accessed from the Bacterial and Viral Bioinformatics Resource Center (https://www.bv-brc.org). Human coronavirus OC43 entries were filtered by host, and the entries marked human (1526 in total) were exported. NCBI accession numbers were extracted, and the coding sequences of all the entries were downloaded from the NCBI using Batch Entrez as FASTA protein files. NCBI sequences were filtered by aligning all entries to the reference HCoV-OC43 spike protein sequence (NCBI accession AAT84354.1), discarding entries with less than 60% sequence identity using the Bio.Align.PairwiseAligner module (Biopython) to yield 380 spike protein sequences. Sequences were further manually filtered to remove entries from viruses that have been propagated in animals or cell culture before submission to the NCBI, resulting in 376 spike protein sequences. A multiple sequence alignment was conducted using MUSCLE v571. Phylogenetic analysis using the D1 domain alone was conducted using IQ-TREE v2.3.4 by maximum likelihood inference. The best-fit model was automatically determined by ModelFinder72 within IQ-TREE, and branch support was assessed with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates73.

Synthesis of the 9-O-acetylated sialic acid-containing saccharides

Each of the following compounds: (i) methyl 9-O-acetyl-N-acetyl-α-neuraminic acid (Neu5,9Ac2αOMe), (ii) Neu5Acα2–8Neu5Acα2–3Galβ1–4Glcβ-AP (GD3-AP), (iii) Neu5,9Ac2α2–8Neu5Acα2–3Galβ1–4Glcβ-AP (9OAc-GD3ap), (iv) Neu5,9Ac2α2–3(GalNAcβ1–4)Galβ1–4GlcβProN3, (v) Neu5,9Ac2α2–3Galβ1–4GlcNAcβProN3, and (vi) Neu5,9Ac2α2–6Galβ1–4GlcNAcβProN3 were synthesized, purified and stored as previously described43.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) binding studies

The wild-type and mutant D1 domains were biotinylated by the BirA enzyme as previously described43,74. In short, 400 to 500 µg of the D1 domain was prepared in a reaction mixture containing 330 µM biotin, 7.3 µg/mL BirA, 1 mM ATP, 1 mM MgCl2, and protease inhibitor (Roche, Catalog Number: 11836170001) in PBS, pH 7.4. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by size-exclusion chromatography in PBS, pH 7.4 (Superdex 200 Increase, Cytiva) to remove the unreacted biotin.

The binding affinities of the OC43 D1 domains for the various sialoglycans were determined by surface plasmon resonance spectroscopy using a Biacore X100 or a Biacore 8K instrument (Cytiva). Solutions of the biotinylated D1 domains at concentrations of up to 1.5 mg/mL were flowed over the streptavidin sensor chip (Cytiva), on flow cell 2 (FC2), at a flow rate of 5 µL/min for 60 s, in PBS, pH 7.4. The biotinylated OC43 D1 domain N27A mutant was used as a non-binding reference and was captured on the same SPR sensor chip on flow cell 1 (FC1) in the same way. Serial dilutions of the various sialoglycan analytes were prepared, and binding studies were conducted at 25 °C, in PBS, pH 6.3, containing 0.2% Tween 20, at a flow rate of 10 µL/min. Association and dissociation times were 60 s each. SPR data was collected at 1 Hz and double referenced during processing. Response versus concentration was plotted at the steady state (15 s before the end of sample injection), and the KD was determined by fitting to a 1:1 model using Graphpad Prism (v. 10).

Biolayer interferometry (BLI)-based binding studies

3.8 mg of Bovine Submaxillary Mucin, BSM, (Sigma, Catalog Number: M3895) was dissolved in 15 mL PBS, pH 6.3 using sonication. The solution was filtered using a 0.2 µm PES syringe filter (Basix/Fisherbrand, Catalog Number: 13100106) and then concentrated using a 10 kDa concentrator (Amicon/Merck, Catalog Number: UFC901024) to 250 µL (1.4 mg/mL). The concentrated material was biotinylated using NHS-PEG4-Biotin (ThermoFisher, Catalog Number: A39259) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, NHS-PEG4-biotin was dissolved in DMSO to produce a stock solution at a concentration of 20 mM. 6 µL of the stock solution was added to 250 µL of the BSM solution in PBS, pH 6.3. The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min, followed by removal of excess NHS-PEG4-biotin by gel filtration chromatography (Superose 6 Increase, Cytiva) in PBS, pH 6.3. Biotinylated BSM (6.5 µg/mL) was immobilized on streptavidin biosensors (Octet Streptavidin Biosensor, Sartorius) at levels up to 1 nm. The D1 proteins were serially diluted, and the binding study was conducted in kinetics buffer (PBS, pH 6.3, 0.1% BSA, 0.02% Tween 20). Response versus concentration was plotted at the steady state (15 s before the end of sample injection), and the KD was determined by fitting to a 1:1 model using Graphpad Prism (v. 10).

Hemagglutination assay

The SA-D1 complexes were produced by conjugating BirA biotinylated D1 domains with streptavidin (Roche, Catalog Number: 11721666001). Biotinylated D1 was produced as described above. 40 µL of streptavidin (1 mg/mL) in PBS, pH 7.4, was mixed with 70 µL of biotinylated D1 (4.3 mg/mL) in the same buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The tetravalent SA-D1 complex was purified by size exclusion chromatography (S200 Increase, Cytiva). Two-fold serial dilutions of the SA-D1 complexes (starting at 320 ng/mL, 50 µL per well) were prepared in a round-bottom 96-well plate (Sarstedt, Catalog Number: 83.3925) using a buffer containing PBS pH 6.3 and 0.5% BSA. 50 µL of mouse (0.5%, C57BL/6, Charles River) or rat (0.5%, CD Sprague Dawley, Charles River) erythrocyte suspension was added to each well with mixing. Plates were incubated overnight at 4 °C before hemagglutination was scored.

Stock cell lines

HEK293F cells (Invitrogen) and HEK293F GnT1- cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 with 3% fetal bovine serum (FBS) as previously described. LAN-5 cells were a gift from Dr. Michael Ohh (University of Toronto) and were cultured in RPMI1640 (Wisent, Catalog Number: 350030CL) with 10% FBS. MDCK cells were a gift from Dr. Daniela Rotin (The Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto) and were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Wisent, Catalog Number: 319085CL) with 10% FBS. All media were supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin.

Production of ST8SIA1-overexpressing cells

HEK293F cells stably expressing ST8SIA1 (NCBI Accession NM_003034.4) were generated using a piggyBac transposon-based mammalian expression system74. HEK293F cells stably expressing pB-RN were seeded in a 24 well plate with 2 × 105 cells in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 3% FBS. The cells were grown overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and transfected the next day with 0.9 μg of pB-ST8SIA1 and 0.1 μg pB-ase per well using 2 μL of 293Fectin as a transfection reagent. Stable cells were selected and maintained by culturing in 2 µg/mL puromycin. Cells were induced with 100 ng/mL doxycycline for 24 hours before all experiments.

MDCK cells stably expressing ST8SIA1 (NCBI Accession NM_003034.4) were generated by nucleofection using the cell line nucleofector kit L (VCA-1005 from Lonza Bioscience). MDCK cells were trypsinized from a confluent P100 plate, and 5 × 105 cells were resuspended in 100 μL nucleofector solution and 10 μl sterile water containing 1.88 µg pB-ST8SIA1, 0.38 µg pB-RN, and 0.25 µg pB-ase were added. The suspension was transferred to a certified nucleofection cuvette and electroporated using the A-24 program. Cells were recovered post nucleofection by culturing in a 6 well plate with DMEM/F12 with 10% FBS at 37 °C for 48 h. Stable cells were selected and maintained by culturing in 2 µg/mL puromycin and 200 µg/mL neomycin (G418). Cells were induced with 100 ng/mL doxycycline for 24 h before all experiments.

Human nasal epithelial cells

Human nasal epithelial cells (HNECs) were isolated as previously described from nasal brushes (nasal inferior turbinate) of healthy donors75. The HNECs at passage 3 were seeded on collagen IV-coated 24 transwell inserts (Corning, Catalog Number: 3470) at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and were differentiated at the air-liquid interface (ALI) for 21–28 days using PneumaCult-ALI basal differentiation media (STEMCELL Technologies, Catalog Number: 05001) to obtain fully differentiated nasal epithelial cultures. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of the Hospital for Sick Children Toronto, ON, Canada (REB 1000061106-informed consent and biobanking cells, and REB 1000071125-secondary use for coronavirus related research).

Fluorescence microscopy and the production of the SAPE-D1 complexes

Streptavidin R-Phycoerythrin OC43-D1 domain complexes (SAPE-D1) were used as probes for the fluorescence microscopy studies. Briefly, 100 µg of premium-grade Streptavidin R-Phycoerythrin (Invitrogen, Catalog Number: S21388) was incubated with 100 µg of BirA-biotinylated D1, as described above, at 1:8 molar ratio in ~200 µL PBS, pH 7.4 for 2 h at room temperature. The SAPE-D1 complexes were purified by gel filtration chromatography in PBS, pH 7.4 (Superdex 200 Increase, Cytiva) (Supplementary Fig. 23). The complexes were stored in PBS, pH 7.4 with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Catalog Number: 11836170001) at 4 °C until used.

HEK293F, LAN-5, and MDCK cells were seeded on coverslips in 24-well plates at a density of 2.0 × 105, 2.0 × 105, and 1.8 × 105 per well respectively and cultured overnight. Cells were fixed with 3.7% formaldehyde in PBS, pH 7.4, for 10 min at room temperature. The HEK293F and LAN-5 coverslips were pretreated with 100 µg/mL poly-D-lysine according to manufacturer instructions (Sigma Catalog Number #7280). In brief, coverslips were incubated with 100 µg/mL poly-D-lysine for 30 min at room temperature in a biosafety cabinet, washed 3 times with sterile water, and left to dry for 2 h. Coverslips were prepared immediately prior to use. Cells were stained with 50 nM SAPE-D1 in PBS, pH 7.4, containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at 4˚C. After washing with PBS, pH 7.4 containing 0.05% Tween 20 (PBST), cells were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and the coverslips were mounted using ProLong Gold antifade fluorescent mounting medium (Invitrogen, Catalog Number: P36930).

HNEC ALI inserts were washed three times with PBS, pH 7.4 and embedded in Tissue-Tek optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Sakura, Catalog Number: 4583). 10-µm cross-sections were processed by a cryotome and stored at −80 ˚C. Cryosections were fixed with 3.7% formaldehyde in PBS, pH 7.4, for 20 min at room temperature and blocked with 1% BSA (PBS, pH 7.4) for 30 min. After blocking, sections were stained with 50 nM SAPE-D1 in 0.5% BSA (PBS, pH 7.4) for 1 hour at 4˚ C. Sections were washed with PBST and stained with β-tubulin IV antibody (1:500, Sigma, Catalog Number: T7941) in 2% BSA/PBST for 1 h at RT, and tubulin was detected by staining with Alexa-488-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:500, Invitrogen, Catalog Number: A11001) for 1 h at RT. The nucleus was stained with DAPI, and sections were mounted as described above.

Confocal and widefield microscopy

Confocal Images were acquired on a WAVE-FX-X1 system (Quorum Technologies) using a modified CSU-X1 scanhead (Yokogawa Electric) attached to a Zeiss Axiovert Z1 (Carl Zeiss) and EMCCD camera (Hamamatsu photonics, C9100-13). Flow cytometry

HEK293F and MDCK cells were trypsinized and passed through a 40 µm cell strainer (Corning, catalog number 352340) to remove large aggregates. For live cell staining 500,000 to 1,000,000 cells in 96 well-U-shaped bottom plates (Corning, Catalog Number: 3799) were incubated with 50 nM of the SAPE-D1 conjugates in 100 µL FACS buffer (0.5% BSA, 1 mM EDTA, PBS, pH 7.4) for 60 min at 4 °C. The cells were washed twice with FACS buffer and stained with DAPI (1:1000) to allow for the identification and exclusion of dead cells. The stained cells were analyzed on a BD LSRFortessa X-20 analyzer, and the data were processed using the FlowJo 10.1 software. The gating strategy is shown in Supplementary Fig. 24.

Cryo-EM data collection

Holey gold film-coated EM grids with 2 µm holes were prepared as previously described43. For the apo OC43-C2 spike data set, the sample solution was prepared by mixing an equal volume of water and a spike protein solution containing 2.1 mg/mL protein in 10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, buffer containing 50 mM NaCl. For the OC43-C2 spike – 9OAc-GD3ap complex data set, the sample solution was prepared by mixing an 870 µM 9OAc-GD3ap solution in water with an equal volume of spike protein solution containing 2.1 mg/mL protein in 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 buffer containing 50 mM NaCl. For the OC43-Lab spike – 9OAc-GD3ap complex data set, the sample solution was prepared by mixing an 870 µM 9OAc-GD3ap solution in water with an equal volume of spike protein solution containing 1.8 mg/mL protein in 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0 buffer containing 50 mM NaCl. Both spike – 9OAc-GD3ap complex solutions were incubated for 10 min at room temperature before vitrification using a Vitrobot Mark IV device.

Grid screening and optimization were done with a Talos L120C 120 kV microscope equipped with a Ceta 16 M CMOS camera. The final high-resolution data collection was performed on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Titan Krios G3 300 kV microscope equipped with a Falcon 4 direct electron detector. For the apo OC43-C2 spike and OC43-C2 Spike – 9OAc-GD3ap data sets, movies were collected at 75,000 × nominal magnification, resulting in a calibrated pixel size of 1.03 Å. Each movie was recorded in counting mode with a 7.5 s exposure time and saved in EER format. The exposure rate was adjusted to 5.09 electrons per pixel per second, resulting in a total exposure of 36 electrons per Å2. The data were collected with a 1000 nm to 2000 nm defocus. A total of 4809 movies were collected for the apo OC43-C2 spike dataset, and 9010 movies were collected for the OC43-C2 spike – 9OAc-GD3ap dataset. For the OC43-Lab spike – 9OAc-GD3ap data sets, movies were collected at 120,000 x nominal magnification, resulting in a calibrated pixel size of 0.64 Å. Each movie was recorded in counting mode with a 7.5 s exposure time and saved in EER format. The exposure rate was adjusted to 5.09 electrons per pixel per second, resulting in a total exposure of 36 electrons per Å2. The data were collected with a 1000 nm to 2000 nm defocus. A total of 24,277 movies were collected.

OC43-C2 – 9OAc-GD3ap complex Cryo-EM Data Processing (Supplementary Fig. 25)

All data analysis steps were performed with cryoSPARC 4.3.176. The OC43-C2 – 9OAc-GD3ap dataset was processed with 2x super-resolution mode. Movies were aligned with patch-based motion correction, and contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated with patch-based CTF estimation. Particle selection was done with Topaz. The Topaz model for particle selection was generated from 2D classification jobs of particle images selected by automatic picking with a Gaussian blob from 200 randomly selected micrographs. Multiple rounds of 2D and 3D classification were used to remove low-quality particles from the Topaz-picked particles. The final dataset included 1,479,347 particles. Further improvements in CTF parameter estimation and motion correction were obtained by performing global CTF refinement and local motion correction. Homogeneous refinement was carried out with C3 symmetry imposed, yielding a global resolution of 1.89 Å. To improve the resolution of the D1 domain, local refinement with a D1 domain mask was carried out after symmetry expansion with C3 symmetry, yielding a map with a global resolution of 1.98 Å.

Model building and refinement

The OC43-Lab – 9OAc-GD3ap spike protein model was built manually into the map using UCSF ChimeraX77 (v. 1.10) and Coot78 (v. 0.9.8.96). The cryo-EM structure of the OC43 spike protein trimer (PDB: 6NZK) was used as a reference during model building. In an iterative process, model building in Coot, molecular dynamics-assisted refinement using the ISOLDE tool (v.1.10.1) in UCSF ChimeraX and Real-Space refinement in Phenix79 were performed. 15 N-glycans per chain (45 in total) were also built and refined using Coot and ISOLDE80. A restraint file in cif format for the terminal disialic acid moiety (Neu5,9Ac2α2–8Neu5Ac) was built using Jligand81. The models for the OC43-C2 apo spike and the OC43-C2 – 9OAc-GD3ap spike complex, were built in a similar fashion, using the OC43-Lab spike model as a reference. All atomic models were validated using the comprehensive validation (cryo-EM) tool in Phenix (v.1.20.1-4487). The molecular graphics were generated in UCSF ChimeraX82. Where indicated, maps were sharpened using the Sharpening Tools in cryoSPARC 4.3.176.
