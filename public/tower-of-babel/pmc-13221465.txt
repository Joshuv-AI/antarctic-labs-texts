Genotypic challenges in implementing broadly neutralizing antibody-based long-acting HIV-1 therapies
Authors: Kaiming Tao, Alon Herschhorn, Daniela Fera, Rebecca M. Lynch, Boris D. Julg, Bette Korber, Robert W. Shafer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs) against HIV-1 are promising components of long-acting antiretroviral treatment (ART) due to their prolonged activity and potential for recruiting additional immune effector functions. Their use is most advanced in virologically suppressed people living with HIV-1 switching from a standard daily ART regimen to a long-acting regimen comprising one or two bnAbs combined with one of the currently approved long-acting small-molecule inhibitors: cabotegravir or lenacapavir. Implementation of bnAb-based therapies, however, requires accurate assessment of viral susceptibility. Although phenotypic assays provide the most direct and reliable measures of bnAb activity, their limited scalability has prompted interest in genotypic approaches. Here we summarize evidence linking HIV-1 Env sequence characteristics to reduced bnAb susceptibility, including in vivo selection data in animal models and human studies, and in vitro selection and susceptibility data. Although genotypic analysis of HIV-1 Env may reliably identify resistance in some viruses, more research is required before genotypic methods can reliably predict susceptibility in clinical settings.

Introduction

The necessity for about 40 million individuals to adhere to daily, lifelong therapy is an obstacle to controlling the HIV-1 pandemic. This challenge has stimulated the development of long-acting therapies for HIV-1 treatment. Among these, a regimen combining long-acting injectable forms of the integrase strand transfer inhibitor cabotegravir and the nonnucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor rilpivirine is available as a bimonthly treatment for people living with HIV-1 (PLWH) with stable virological suppression on antiretroviral therapy (ART)1–3. Additionally, lenacapavir, an inhibitor of HIV-1 capsid function, is a long-acting antiretroviral administered subcutaneously (SC) every 6 months and has been approved for use in combination with other ARVs in heavily ART-experienced patients4,5.

With the limited number of long-acting ARVs, broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs) have emerged as a promising option for HIV-1 treatment due to their extended half-lives and ability to engage the host immune system through Fc-mediated effector functions. The use of bnAbs for ART, however, is challenged by the extraordinary variability of HIV-1 Env and the continual selection pressures imposed during natural infection6–9. Despite these limitations, bnAbs have shown encouraging results in phase II studies in PLWH with stable virological suppression when combined with lenacapavir or cabotegravir and when resistant variants have not been detected by proviral DNA susceptibility testing10–15.

In this review, we summarize the expanding literature defining genotypic correlates of resistance to bnAbs, drawing on data from in vitro studies, animal models, and human clinical trials. These data provide insights into bnAb mechanisms of action and inform the design of newer bnAbs with broader and more consistent activity. Genotypic analysis of HIV-1 Env can be useful for the identification of viral resistance to bnAbs; however, additional research is needed to reliably predict viral susceptibility from sequence data in clinical settings.

HIV-1 Env

HIV-1 Env is a fusion protein sparsely expressed on the virus surface, and it enables fusion and cell entry16,17. It is synthesized as a single gp160 protein and assembled into a trimer with each protomer post-translationally cleaved to form a gp120 and gp41 heterodimer. The complex is anchored to the virus membrane via the gp41 transmembrane domain, while gp120 associates non-covalently with the gp41 ectodomain. Each protomer of the Env trimer typically contains 18 to 32 N-linked glycans that shield most of the polypeptide surface. The glycans are added post-translationally at most of the predicted N-linked glycosylation sites (PNGS) in the Env sequence18–20. Although the extent of glycan shielding is relatively conserved, the precise number, location, and processing of glycans are strain-dependent20,21 and consequently different regions of the protein surface can be exposed across strains, impacting the accessibility of distinct epitopes22,23.

There are five variable loops in gp120 termed V1–V5, which differ substantially across HIV-1 strains, although the V3 loop is far less variable than the other four. V1 and V2 are sometimes referred to as the V1/V2 loop because they are encoded by a continuous linear sequence. Both contribute structurally to the apex of the Env trimer, and the combination of their properties is a better predictor of their susceptibility to bnAbs that target this region24. Disulfide bonds demarcate each of the variable regions except V517. These variable regions evolve rapidly with evolution within V1, V2, V4, and V5, often characterized by insertions and deletions leading to heterogeneity in their length and the location of N-linked glycosylation sites24.

HIV-1 gp120 has an inner domain located close to the trimer axis and an outer domain that includes most variable loops17. The CD4 binding site (CD4bs) lies in a cavity formed at the interface of the inner and outer domains, surrounded by the V1/V2, V3, and V5 loops, as well as multiple highly conserved N-linked glycans25–27. HIV-1 gp120 binding to the CD4 receptor initiates cell entry and triggers V1/V2 and V3 rearrangements that mediate the transition of gp120 from a closed conformation to a more open state, exposing the parts of Env that bind to the CCR5 or CXCR4 coreceptors on target cells28. Coreceptor binding may induce gp120 shedding and result in the extension of gp41 and its fusion peptide toward the target cell membrane29,30. HIV-1 Env amino acid diversity between subtypes is about 30%, whereas intra-subtype diversity ranges from 18% to 24%31,32. By comparison, inter-subtype amino acid diversity in capsid, protease, RT, and integrase is about 10%, while intra-subtype diversity is not much higher than 5%33. Moreover, insertions and deletions are common within Env but exceedingly rare in the other targets of therapy.

The three-dimensional structures of HIV-1 Env in complex with many bnAbs have been determined using disulfide-stabilized Env trimers known as SOSIPs, derived from the soluble Env gp140 ectodomain. SOSIPs incorporate a disulfide bond (“SOS”) between gp120 residue 501 and gp41 residue 605 and an isoleucine-to-proline (“IP”) substitution at gp41 residue 559 in the first heptad repeat, which stabilizes Env34,35. Cryo-EM has been particularly valuable in elucidating the configuration of the N-linked glycans on the Env trimer34.

bnAb identification and characterization

The development of single B-cell culture and sorting technologies combined with high-throughput neutralization screening has made it possible to efficiently identify bnAbs in the sera of individuals with high titers of neutralizing activity36,37. bnAbs are defined by their potency and breadth of neutralization measured using standardized pseudovirus panels developed over the past 15 years38–43. Neutralizing data for many bnAbs tested using such panels have been compiled by the Los Alamos National Laboratory CATNAP (Compile, Analyze, and Tally Neutralizing Antibody Panels) database and by computational tools in HIResist44,45.

The breadth and potency of bnAbs against pseudotyped virus panels are likely higher than against current circulating primary virus isolates for at least three reasons. First, many Envs in the large pseudovirus panels were isolated two to three decades earlier in the HIV-1 pandemic, and there is evidence for continuous drift of HIV-1 isolates toward reduced bnAb susceptibility39,40,46–48. Second, pseudotyped virus panels comprise single HIV-1 variants rather than the diverse quasispecies in clinical isolates. Finally, primary isolates cultured in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) may have more Env spikes and different glycosylation patterns than pseudotyped viruses49–51.

Most neutralization assays use HeLa-derived TZM-bl cells expressing CD4, CXCR4, CCR5, and HIV-1 entry-dependent Tat to activate beta-galactosidase or luciferase reporter genes. In these assays, TZM-bl cells are infected with HIV-1 env-pseudotyped viruses with increasing bnAb concentrations. Dose-response curves are used to calculate IC50s, IC80s, or IC90s during a single round of replication52,53. A widely used commercial variation, the PhenoSense Monoclonal Antibody Assay (Labcorp-Monogram Biosciences), has been used to assess the susceptibility of amplified env genes in clinical trials54.

Early bnAb definitions were relatively inclusive, encompassing antibodies that neutralized >30% of viruses in a panel with an IC50 < 50 µg/ml55. As bnAbs have progressed into animal model studies and clinical trials, stricter potency criteria have been applied, reflecting observations that protective efficacy in various settings requires plasma concentrations >100-fold higher than their IC₅₀ or IC₈₀ values56–59. bnAbs target several sites of vulnerability on HIV-1 Env: the CD4bs, the V3 glycan supersite, the V2 apex, the membrane-proximal external region (MPER), a series of epitopes spanning gp120 and gp41, and a conserved gp120 region referred to as the “silent face” because it elicits minimal antibody responses in most PLWH60–63. X-ray crystallography has typically been used to identify contacts between CD4bs bnAbs and gp120 “core” constructs, which were truncated and lacked variable loops. Cryo-EM has usually been more effective at visualizing bnAbs bound to the prefusion SOSIP trimer, particularly for the V3 glycan and V2 apex bnAbs. bnAb lineages recognizing the same epitope often share common genetic and structural features, including extensive somatic hypermutation and long heavy-chain complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR-H3) sequences61,64–66.

Prior to study in humans, bnAbs are often evaluated in human immune system (HIS) mice or in the nonhuman primate (NHP) macaque model67–69. Advantages of HIS mice are their simplicity and that they typically do not produce anti-human Abs. A disadvantage is their lack of functional innate effector cells57. In NHPs, chimeric SHIVs expressing HIV-1 Env in an SIV backbone are used to challenge animals via routes modeling natural transmission70. The number of SHIVs available has expanded due to the discovery that substituting Env residue S375 with a bulky aromatic residue enhances binding to rhesus CD4, enabling more HIV-1 Envs to efficiently replicate in rhesus macaques71,72.

bnAbs targeting the CD4bs, V2 apex, V3 glycan supersite, and MPER have been studied in clinical trials73. Single bnAbs or bnAb combinations have been evaluated primarily for their ability to reduce HIV-1 viral load in ART-naïve individuals with viremia and to delay virus rebound after discontinuing suppressive ART—an intervention termed analytic treatment interruption (ATI). In the absence of therapy following an ATI, the median time to virological rebound above 200 copies/ml is about 3 weeks, with a small proportion of participants remaining suppressed for up to 12 weeks74–78. The Antibody Mediated Prevention (AMP) trials assessed bnAbs for pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)79, but this approach is unlikely to be pursued because of limited effectiveness compared with long-acting drugs such as cabotegravir and lenacapavir80–83.

Most bnAbs evaluated clinically contain the “LS” Fc mutations M428L and N434S to enhance binding affinity to the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), which helps maintain immunoglobulin levels84. The LS modification increases bnAb half-lives regardless of epitope specificity and does not affect innate and adaptive immune function85,86. LS-modified bnAbs typically have half-lives of about 2 months, although they are generally longer in PLWH with suppressed viral loads than in those with ongoing replication87–89.

In addition to their direct neutralizing activity, HIV-1 bnAbs can engage FcγR pathways to stimulate natural killer cells, induce antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), and promote phagocytosis and complement deposition90,91. Fc-mediated functions may also facilitate a “vaccinal effect,” in which immune complexes formed between bnAbs and Env are taken up by antigen-presenting cells, enhancing adaptive immunity and potentially accelerating clearance of virus-infected cells92. In studies using HIS mice and NHP, FcγR-mediated functions were shown to enhance bnAb activity, leading to greater viral suppression, improved HIV-1-specific cellular immunity, and lower proviral DNA levels93–98.

Findings from human clinical trials have been variable. Several ATI studies have reported extended viral suppression beyond that expected from plasma bnAb levels, sometimes with increases in HIV-1–specific CD8+ T-cell responses75,99–104. However, other studies have not observed consistent increases in T-cell responses105–107. The impact of bnAbs on the HIV-1 reservoir has been limited. While some trials have reported reductions in replication-competent proviral DNA levels75,108, most have not demonstrated statistically significant changes whether bnAbs were given alone or with immunostimulatory or latency-reversing agents99,102,105,109–113.

The genotypic basis of bnAb resistance

Testing for resistance to bnAbs is essential at the population level to define their breadth of activity, and at the individual level, to guide enrollment in clinical trials. Should bnAbs become part of ART, determining viral susceptibility will likely be required before treatment. Although phenotypic assays are the most direct method for assessing susceptibility, genotypic testing is also important for research and potential clinical use.

Phenotypic testing of clinical samples presents two main challenges. First, because HIV-1 exists as a quasispecies, low-abundance resistant variants may be masked by susceptible strains. Second, in virologically suppressed individuals, testing relies on proviral DNA from PBMCs rather than plasma. PBMC viral outgrowth assays are labor-intensive and tend to recover only the most replication-competent viruses, potentially missing resistant clones less likely to replicate in culture53,88,99. Recombinant virus assays using amplified proviral DNA molecules are simpler but may be dominated by expanded lymphocyte clones carrying defective viruses unlikely to reactivate114. Nonetheless, one study has shown that these two approaches were concordant in 84% of 74 paired assays115.

Genotypic assays complement phenotypic testing by providing mechanistic insights that inform bnAb design and resistance prediction. They are also more accessible for clinical use. However, predicting bnAb susceptibility from Env sequences is challenging. Ongoing efforts to improve these predictions integrate data from structural studies, in vitro neutralization assays, in vitro selection experiments, and analyses of Env sequence changes emerging in animal models and human clinical trials.

Structural studies have mapped bnAb epitopes and identified key amino acids involved in binding. However, bnAb–Env contacts can vary by HIV-1 subtype, and structural analyses do not capture many allosteric escape mutations or glycosylation changes that can block bnAb access to their epitopes116,117. Additionally, because HIV-1 Env is synthesized in a metastable prefusion state, mutations that alter conformational control points or increase trimer flexibility can confer resistance28,118–120.

In vitro neutralization studies have been performed following site-directed mutagenesis or alanine screening, approaches that strengthen the link between a particular amino acid change and a change in susceptibility. However, most of the neutralization assays in the CATNAP database (about 180,000 results for 2700 pseudoviruses) have been performed on independent pseudotyped viruses44.

Several computational approaches have been developed to predict bnAb susceptibility from Env sequences121. Unlike the relatively simpler models used to predict susceptibility to drugs targeting HIV-1 RT, protease, integrase, and capsid, bnAb susceptibility prediction is complicated by the strong context dependence of Env, where the effects of individual mutations depend on the sequence background, and the influence of Env conformational dynamics on epitope accessibility. These challenges are compounded by the limited size and diversity of available training datasets relative to the underlying biological complexity.

Most models have integrated genotype–phenotype correlations from the CATNAP database with structural and molecular features, including bnAb contact residues24,122,123, HIV-1 subtype or other evidence for shared ancestry24,124, predicted glycan occupancy at PNGS125, and bnAb variable region sequences126. The latter approach models antibody-virus susceptibility as a function of both virus and antibody sequences, thereby leveraging a large proportion of the CATNAP dataset126.

Some approaches aim to predict IC50 values122,123,125,127, whereas others classify viruses as susceptible or resistant according to predefined thresholds117,126,128,129. Several models are interpretable, quantifying the effects of individual mutations on susceptibility24,117,123,128,129, whereas others function as “black boxes”122,125,126. Due to the complexity of Env structure and conformation dynamics, accurate predictions of bnAb susceptibility from genotype–phenotype datasets have been limited. In addition, these models have not systematically integrated empirical evidence from bnAbs selection studies or site-directed mutagenesis experiments.

In vivo selection studies have compared Env sequences before and after bnAb treatment in animal models and clinical trials. These studies, however, involve few participants and may not detect some resistance mechanisms. They are also usually short and often show only subtle shifts in amino acid frequencies at a position rather than complete amino acid replacements. Finally, in a few bnAb trials, no Env changes emerged despite virological rebound, possibly due to insufficient tissue bnAb levels or because rebound occurred via direct cell-to-cell transmission59,130,131.

In vitro selection studies have typically evaluated libraries of Env pseudovirus clones carrying single amino acid substitutions. Following exposure to bnAbs, deep sequencing identifies mutations enriched under selective pressure116,132–134. Correlations with IC₅₀ values have supported their biological relevance116, though results are clone dependent and many escape mutations are rarely found in circulating viruses, suggesting that in vivo evolutionary constraints are not fully replicated in these systems116,133,134.

CD4bs bnAbs

CD4bs bnAbs generally have greater breadth than bnAbs targeting other sites135–137. CD4bs bnAbs contact residues are found in loop D, the CD4 binding loop, and the β23 strand in the V5 loop (Fig. 3). The CD4bs was among the earliest bnAb targets to be characterized, beginning with the identification of b12 in the 1990s and VRC01 in 2010138–140. These bnAbs typically recognize conserved Env epitopes while avoiding steric clashes with glycans62. VRC01

VRC01 has an IgG heavy chain encoded by VH1-2*02, high levels of somatic hypermutation, and a rare short five-amino-acid light chain complementarity-determining region 3 (L-CDR3)35. VRC01 neutralizes 35% of strains with an IC80 ≤ 1.0 μg/ml (Fig. 2A)44,45. It has been studied for PrEP in PLWH with active virus replication and in those with stable virological suppression.

Active virus replication

Among eight ART-untreated viremic participants, six infused with an intravenous (IV) 40 mg/kg dose of VRC01 experienced 1.1 to 1.8 log reductions in plasma viremia109. Among participants in whom virus selection was detected, the residues undergoing the most frequent changes were within or near VRC01 contact sites at positions 268, 280, 429, 455, 460, 462, 463, and 465 (Fig. 3; Table S1)109. In three participants, changes in the length of the V5 loop were also observed109.

Stable virological suppression

In an open-label study of 24 participants, those receiving 40 mg/kg IV at two different schedules were significantly more likely to maintain virological suppression at week 4 than historical controls141. In eight participants, the mutations selected by therapy included E275K, I277L, T278S, D279N, and K282R (Fig. 3; Table S1). In contrast, a trial of 13 participants with CRF01_AE infection who initiated ART during acute infection found that VRC01 (40 mg/kg IV every 3 weeks for up to 24 weeks) modestly delayed viral rebound and did not result in VRC01 resistance130,142.

In the Tatelo study, in which VRC01LS plus the V3 glycan supersite bnAb 10-1074 was administered to 25 children on ART since birth, 11 children maintained virological suppression, while 14 experienced virological rebound143,144. Archived provirus susceptible to 10-1074, a smaller proviral DNA reservoir, and sustained virological suppression throughout early life were predictors of maintaining virological suppression144.

PrEP

was evaluated in the two AMP trials79. Although VRC01 did not prevent infection overall, a prespecified sieve analysis pooling data across both trials found that the incidence of infection in those with VRC01 susceptible isolates (IC80 < 1.0 μg/ml) was significantly lower among VRC01 recipients than placebo recipients (0.20 versus 0.86 per 100 person-years) for an estimated prevention efficacy against susceptible viruses of 75.4%. Transmitted variants with VRC01 resistance usually exhibited minimal cross-resistance to VRC07-523 and N6145.

In site-directed mutagenesis studies, one or more of the following mutations alone or in combination were associated with markedly reduced VRC01 susceptibility: N97E, N197E/S, N279I/K, N280D/Y, A281G/H/L/T, T455E, G458DY, G459D, and G471Q137,146,147. In statistical analyses of in vitro susceptibility data, changes at positions N234, E275, N276, N280, A281, K282, S364, S365, R456, G458, G459, and G471 were also linked to reduced susceptibility24,117,123,129,149.

3BNC117

3BNC117 was identified in a PLWH displaying high neutralization titers150. Like VRC01, it arose from the VH1-2*02 germline gene but has different somatic mutations contributing to greater breadth and potency. It neutralizes 68% of 582 isolates in the CATNAP database at an IC80 < 1 µg/ml (Fig. 2B)44,45. It has been studied alone and in combination with 10-1074 in PLWH with active virus replication and stable virological suppression.

Active virus replication

In eight ART-naïve viremic PLWH receiving 3BNC117 30 mg/kg IV, plasma viral load reductions of 0.8 to 2.5 log copies/ml were observed and were proportional to baseline 3BNC117 susceptibility151. Viremia rebounded by day 28 in five individuals, including three with viruses displaying >3-fold reduced susceptibility151. Among ten individuals receiving 10 mg/kg or 30 mg/kg, emergent mutations at day 28 included S274Y, K282R, Q363H, G459D, S461D, and a longer V5 loop (Fig. 3; Table S2)151,152.

Two trials evaluated 3BNC117 plus 10-1074 in viremic PLWH88,107. In one of seven participants receiving both bnAbs (30 mg/kg each at baseline, week 2, and week 4), the four with dual-sensitive virus had a mean 2.05 log₁₀ reduction in plasma HIV-1 RNA lasting up to 3 months88. One non-responder harbored pre-existing 280S and 281H/T. Viruses from two participants with virologic rebound developed N276D and G471E. In a second trial of five PLWH with virus loads between 200 and 5000 copies/ml treated with eight infusions of both bnAbs over 24 weeks, two maintained virological suppression for a mean of 42 weeks107.

Stable virological suppression

There have been two studies of 3BNC117 monotherapy110,153, four studies of 3BNC117 plus 10-1074, including one in which the LS forms of both bnAbs were administered75,99,107,108, and two studies in which 3BNC117-LS plus 10-1074-LS were combined with lenacapavir in individuals with stable virological suppression10,11,154.

In 13 individuals with susceptible baseline outgrowth cultures receiving 3BNC117 monotherapy (30 mg/kg), there was a mean delay in viral rebound of 6.7 weeks in those receiving two infusions separated by 3 weeks and of 9.9 weeks for those receiving four infusions separated by 2 weeks153. Four individuals maintained virological suppression until 3BNC117 concentration fell below 20 µg/ml. Viruses from 6 of 8 individuals undergoing sequencing developed variants with marked reductions in 3BNC117 susceptibility, including one with R456S, two with A281D, and two with K282N/E (Fig. 3; Table S2).

In a second study describing participants in the same trial, those with proviruses having an IC50 > 2.0 μg/ml had a mean time to rebound of 3.6 weeks, while those with an IC50 < 2.0 μg/ml viruses had a mean time to rebound of 9.2 weeks. With few exceptions, the amino acids observed in rebounding viruses were present at low levels in proviral DNA. Amino acid changes were reported at positions 275, 277–279, 281, 283, 365, 369, 371, 373, 455, 459–465, and 467 (Fig. 3; Table S2)110.

Among the four trials of 3BNC117 plus 10-1074 in PLWH with stable virological suppression, genotypic testing at the time of virological rebound was performed in two trials99,108. Although 10-1074 resistance developed in nearly all participants in these trials, 3BNC117 resistance rarely developed in part because its mean serum concentrations at virological rebound were consistently lower than those of 10-107499,108.

The phase 1 study of 3BNC117-LS plus 10-1074-LS in combination with lenacapavir comprised two cohorts of participants10,11. The first cohort included 20 individuals with proviral susceptibility to both bnAbs, defined as an IC90 < 2.0 μg/ml, who received either a single dose of 10 mg/kg or 30 mg/kg of each bnAb10. This group of 20 was derived from 55 (44.4%) of 124 screened individuals with virus susceptible to both bnAbs10. The second cohort included ten individuals with susceptibility to just one of the two bnAbs11. Among 20 individuals in the first cohort followed for 26 weeks, one experienced virological rebound and developed a lenacapavir-resistance mutation but no reduction in susceptibility to either bnAb155. Among 10 individuals in the second cohort followed for 26 weeks, two experienced virological rebound but no evidence for lenacapavir resistance or reduced bnAb susceptibility155.

In a phase 2 trial, 53 participants with baseline susceptibility to both bnAbs were treated with 2550 mg each of 3BNC117LS and 10-1074LS plus lenacapavir at baseline and week 2615,154. Three participants developed virological rebound, including two who developed 10-1074 resistance and one who developed lenacapavir resistance15,154. Each of the participants with virological rebound weighed >100 kg and had lower bnAb and/or lenacapavir exposures.

In vitro studies (Fig. 3; Table S2)

In site-directed mutagenesis studies, one or more of the following mutations alone or in combination were associated with >10-fold reduced susceptibility, including N97E, N279I/K, N280D/Y, A281G/H/L/T, T455E, R456S, G458D/Y, G459D, and G471Q24,137,146,147,156.

In engineered libraries containing large numbers of subtype A HIV-1BG505 variants, amino acids at seven 3BNC117 contact sites were selected by 3BNC117: N197, K207, N279, A281, K282, R308, and A316116. However, in a different neutralization escape platform using a different subtype A pseudovirus, changes at just A281, R456, and G471 were selected133. In this assay, loss of the highly conserved PNGS at position 276 surprisingly increased 3BNC117 susceptibility133, consistent with the context-dependent role of this PNGS in modulating CD4bs bnAb activity—enhancing susceptibility in some viral backgrounds while reducing it in others157,158. In statistical analyses of in vitro neutralizing data, mutations at the conserved positions R456, G458, and G459, and at the polymorphic positions 462 and 471 were associated with reduced 3BNC117 susceptibility24,117,149.

VRC07-523

VRC07-523 is an engineered VRC07 variant, a bnAb isolated from the same donor as VRC01. It has a four-amino-acid insertion in CDR H3, enabling it to make more gp120 contacts159. It is 5- to 8-fold more potent than VRC01 in vitro, and it prevented infection in NHPs at a 5-fold lower dose than VRC01159. It neutralizes 72% of 769 pseudoviruses in the CATNAP database at an IC80 < 1.0 µg/ml (Fig. 2C)44,45. VRC07-523LS has been evaluated as monotherapy in PLWH with active viremia160,161. It has also been administered as part of a triple bnAb regimen in both viremic and virologically suppressed PLWH106,162 and in combination with long-acting cabotegravir in PLWH with stable virological suppression14.

Active virus replication

In a Phase I trial, a single infusion of VRC07-523LS at 40 mg/kg was administered to nine ART-naïve viremic PLWH160. Prior to infusion, eight of nine individuals had virus populations susceptible to VRC07-523, defined as an IC80 < 1.0 μg/ml. The mean reduction in viral load was 1.7 log copies/ml. Four participants developed one or more gp120 mutation including D197N, D297H, N279K, N280D, D356I, D398G, G459D, and N460S160,161.

Triple bnAb combination

VRC07-523LS 20 mg/kg IV was studied in combination with the V3 loop (PGT121) and V1/V2-apex (PGDM1400) bnAbs in three viremic PLWH not receiving ART and in 12 ART-suppressed individuals106,162. One of three viremic individuals experienced a six-fold reduction in susceptibility to VRC07-523 linked with the emergence of the relatively common PNGS at position 234 and an additional PNGS in the V5 loop (Fig. 3; Table S3)162. Among the ten ART-suppressed PLWH, one individual with virological rebound developed G459D, a VRC07-523 contact site mutation106.

In combination with cabotegravir in PLWH with stable virological suppression

In the Phase II single-arm, open-label A5357 trial, VRC07-523LS administered every 2 months was studied in combination with cabotegravir 300 mg IM administered monthly in 71 virologically suppressed individuals whose isolates cultured from PBMCs were required to have an IC50 ≤ 0.25 μg/ml. Over the course of 48 weeks, five individuals developed protocol-defined virological failure, including two who resuppressed and one who developed a cabotegravir-associated resistance mutation14.

Animal model and in vitro studies (Fig. 3, Table S3)

In the SHIV model, six NHPs consistently developed N279S, N280D, and R456S, with N280D predominating156. In site-directed mutagenesis studies, N280D resulted in a complete lack of VRC07-523 activity, while N279S and R456S were each associated with ~15-fold reduced susceptibility156. Statistical analyses have indicated that loss of the position 276 PNGS and mutations at N280, R456, G459, and G471 were associated with reduced VRC07-523 susceptibility24.

N6

N6 contains features similar to VRC01 but is about ten times more potent and has substantially more breadth135. It neutralizes 85% of 426 isolates at an IC80 < 1 μg/ml (Fig. 2D)44,45. Unlike the other clinical CD4bs bnAbs, N6 depends less on the V5 loop and has a binding surface shifted toward the more conserved loop D163.

N6LS was studied in 14 ART-naïve viremic participants in part 1 of the BANNER trial164. The median viral load reduction was 1.72 log copies/ml in the eight individuals receiving 40 mg/kg IV. In part 2 of the trial, which evaluated different IV and SC doses in 48 persons, the antiviral response was proportional to baseline N6 susceptibility and the N6 dose164. N6LS was also studied in the phase 2 open-label EMBRACE study conducted in virologically suppressed individuals with a proviral DNA baseline IC90 ≤ 2.0 μg/ml. Among 50 participants receiving 60 mg/kg IV in combination with monthly IM cabotegravir, 48 maintained virological suppression at month 612. Among 49 participants receiving N6LS 3000 mg SC plus recombinant human hyaluronidase (to enhance subcutaneous absorption), 46 participants maintained virological suppression12. Among four participants with virological rebound, two in the IV group developed either high (IC90 > 50 μg/ml) or low (IC90 > 2 μg/ml) level N6 resistance, while one in the SC group developed a major cabotegravir-resistance mutation165.

Animal model and in vitro studies (Fig. 3; Table S4)

In the SHIV model, one of eight NHPs receiving N6 developed D279E166. Alanine scanning mutagenesis revealed that substitutions at positions 279, 282, 283, 365, 366, 367, 370, 371, and 457 conferred >3-fold reduced N6 binding affinity135. In site-directed mutagenesis studies, one or more of the following mutations alone or in combination were associated with significantly reduced N6 susceptibility, including N97E, D278G, N/D279E/G/K, N280D/Y, and A281T/Y132,137,147.

V3 glycan supersite bnAbs

The most critical glycan for susceptibility to this class of bnAbs is the N332 glycan at the base of the V3 loop. This epitope also contains a highly conserved 324G(DN)IR327 motif and various high mannose glycans, particularly the N332 glycan, contacted by most bnAbs targeting this site167. N332 is present in ~75% of group M viruses and is mostly absent from CRF01_AE viruses, a major variant in Asia24,45,168. The two V3 glycan bnAbs in clinical trials, 10-1074 and PGT121, are clonal variants that arose in the same individual infected with a subtype A virus169,170. V3 glycan supersite bnAbs are as potent as the CD4bs bnAbs in clinical trials but have a narrower neutralization breadth.

10-1074

10-1074 neutralizes 35% of 922 HIV-1 isolates at an IC80 < 1.0 μg/ml (Fig. 2E)44,45. It has been studied as monotherapy in PLWH with active virus replication in one trial87, and, as indicated above, in combination with 3BNC117 in two trials of PLWH with active virus replication88,107, in four trials of PLWH with stable virological suppression75,99,107,108, and with lenacapavir in two additional trials of individuals with stable virological suppression10,11,154.

In the monotherapy trial, 11 of 13 viremic PLWH with 10-1074 susceptible variants receiving 30 mg/kg IV, had a mean 1.52 log reduction in plasma HIV-1 RNA levels87. Four weeks after infusion, 91% of sequences showed recurrent mutations, most of which eliminated the PNGS at position 332 by mutation at either N332 or S334. Several sequences contained changes from either D or N at position 325 to E or K (Fig. 4; Table S5)87. In 10 of 11 individuals from whom pseudotyped viruses were tested, those with resistance to 10-1074 were also resistant to PGT12187. In vitro studies

In high-throughput in vitro selection studies with subtype A and B pseudoviruses, escape mutations occurred at the following 10-1074 contact sites: D325, R327, H330, N332, S334, and T415116,134. In site-directed mutagenesis experiments, loss of the PNGS at N332 and D325E/S, R327A, H330R, and T415R was each associated with significant reductions in 10-1074 susceptibility (Fig. 4; Table S5)116,137.

PGT121

PGT121 neutralizes 45% of 830 variants at an IC80 < 1.0 μg/ml (Fig. 2F)44,45. Neutralization depends largely on accessibility to N332, although PGT121 possesses some ability to recognize other glycans in the absence of N332171. In a monotherapy trial, PGT121 30 mg/kg IV was administered to 13 viremic participants not receiving ART, including nine with a virus load between 2000 and 100,000 copies/ml and four with a virus load <2000 copies/ml172. PGT121 reduced plasma HIV-1 RNA levels by a median of 1.77 log copies/ml in the four individuals with a high virus load and baseline PGT121 susceptibility172. Similar to 10-1074, emergence of resistance was caused primarily by the loss of the PNGS at position 332 and, in a few cases, by mutations at positions 325, 328, 330, and 413 (Fig. 4; Table S6)172.

In the trial of PGT121 in combination with VRC07-523 and V2 apex bnAb PGDM1400, all trial participants either had reduced susceptibility to PGT121 at baseline or at virological rebound162. Six individuals, including four with late virological rebound, experienced loss of the PNGS at position 332 or developed G234R or H330Y (Fig. 4, Table S6)106.

In site-directed mutagenesis studies, mutations at positions 156, 301, 323, 325, 327, 330, 415, and 441 were associated with reduced PGT121 susceptibility (Fig. 5; Table S6)116,167. V2 apex bnAbs

The V1/V2 loops at the Env trimer apex are highly variable in terms of sequence, length, and extent of glycosylation. Surrounding these loops is a conserved region covered by glycans, most notably the highly conserved N156 and N160 glycans. V2 bnAbs target a quaternary epitope at the apex by binding and crosslinking the three Env protomers173. They have an extraordinarily long CDRH3, typically 26 to 30 amino acids long, that allows penetration of the glycan shield to reach the core protein epitope174,175. V2 bnAbs have limited potency and breadth against subtype B viruses24,48. PGDM1400 and CAP256-V2 (originally CAP256-VRC26.25) are V2 bnAbs in clinical trials.

PGDM1400 neutralizes 42% of 1161 strains at an IC80 < 1 μg/ml (Fig. 2G)44,45. In 10 HIS mice infected with HIV-1 isolates susceptible to PGDM1400, viruses developed mutations associated with loss of the PNGSs at positions 130 or 160176. PGDM1400 has been studied in two clinical trials in combination with PGT121 and VRC07-523LS. One trial included five viremic ART-naïve participants162 and the other included 12 participants undergoing an ATI106. In the trial with viremic participants, emergent resistance occurred in four in association with loss of the position 160 PNGS162. In the ATI trial, resistance was associated with loss of the position 130 PNGS in three participants (Fig. 5; Table S7)106.

In YU2 subtype B site-directed mutants, N160K/T and T162I/N reduced PGDM1400 susceptibility137. Alanine mutations associated with reduced PGDM1400 susceptibility included N134A, N160A, T162A, R166A, D167A, and Y177A (Fig. 5; Table S7)177.

CAP256-V2 neutralizes 50% of 293 strains at an IC80 < 1 μg/ml (Fig. 2H)44,45. It neutralizes approximately 70% of subtype C viruses but only 15% of subtype B viruses, and, among subtype B viruses in the AMP trials, only 6%48,178. The CAP256 parent antibody depends on the following Env residues: F159, N160, L165, R166, D167, K169, and K171179,180. Viruses susceptible to CAP256-V2 are enriched for 164D/E and 169 R/K (Fig. 5; Table S8)178. CAP256-V2LS was studied in combination with VRC07-523LS and a TLR7 agonist in 20 South African women from the FRESH trial who had initiated ART during acute infection and achieved stable suppression181. Fourteen participants met ART restart criteria during 43 weeks of follow-up. Genotypic correlates of virologic rebound have not yet been reported.

Membrane-proximal external region (MPER)

The gp41 MPER is a highly conserved region of the Env gp41 subunit that bridges the extracellular domain and transmembrane region of Env and extends from position 659 to 683. Most MPER-targeting bnAbs neutralize more efficiently when Env is in an open configuration182. MPER bnAbs have unusually long and hydrophobic CDRH3183 and often display some degree of autoreactivity associated with the hydrophobic character of CDRH3 regions used by many of these Abs62.

In its original description, the MPER-directed bnAb 10E8 neutralized 98% of tested pseudoviruses at an IC50 < 50 μg/ml184. More recent analyses using CATNAP and HiResist show similar IC50 coverage (95% < 50 μg/ml), but substantially lower coverage (19% of 430 pseudoviruses) at the IC80 < 1 μg/ml threshold44,45. Escape from 10E8 results primarily from mutations in the structurally defined gp41 contact sites116. Several modifications led to improved solubility, stability, potency, and a prolonged half-life, resulting in 10E8V and 10E8v4185,186. Six healthy participants receiving 10E8VLS in a phase I trial experienced moderate to severe local reactogenicity187. However, reactogenicity did not occur in a phase 1 trial of a trispecific bnAb containing the 10E8v4 Fab region, which is missing two heavy-chain amino acids present in 10E8VLS187,188.

Engineered bnAbs with enhanced neutralization potency and breadth

The variable regions of naturally arising bnAbs can be modified using in silico structure- and sequence-based design approaches to enhance potency and breadth while reducing autoreactivity159,189–191. Among the bnAbs currently in advanced clinical development, VRC07-523 and 10E8v4 were modified to optimize Fab engagement with their epitopes159,186. Another highly potent engineered bnAb, N49P9.6-FR, was generated by introducing affinity-enhancing somatic mutations into the naturally occurring CD4bs bnAb N49P9.3191. Similar strategies have been applied to engineer bnAbs targeting other Env epitopes190,192.

Several groups have developed bi- and trispecific HIV-1 bnAbs targeting multiple epitopes within a single antibody molecule. These constructs employ formats such as dual-Fab IgGs or tandem single-chain variable fragments fused to an Fc domain. A major advantage of bi- and trispecific bnAbs is that they function as single therapeutic products, which may streamline manufacturing and regulatory approval. Their potential for synergistic potency, however, is constrained by the low density of Env spikes on the HIV-1 surface, which limits the engagement of multiple epitopes simultaneously193. One trispecific bnAb SAR441236, targeting the CD4bs, MPER, and V1V2 apex, has demonstrated protection against a mix of SHIVs in NHPs194, and a construct targeting the same epitopes has entered clinical studies188.

Predictive value of molecular features for bnAb susceptibility

Of the most frequently reported molecular features associated with reduced susceptibility to CD4bs bnAbs, eight included variant amino acids at relatively conserved residues: absence of an N276 PNGS, 279 not D/N, 280 not N, 281T/H/D/Y, 282 not K, 456 not R, 459 not G, and 471E/I/Q (Fig. 3). In the presence of at least one of these features, 47.2%, 64.1%, and 78.0% of pseudoviruses in the CATNAP database had an IC80 < 1.0 μg/ml for 3BNC117, VRC07-523, and N6, respectively. Two features, the absence of a PNGS at N332 and 325 not D/N, were the most predictive of reduced susceptibility to the V3-targeting bnAbs (Fig. 4). In their presence, just 0.5% and 11.1% of viruses in the CATNAP database had an IC80 < 1.0 μg/ml for 10-1074 and PGT121, respectively. In their absence, 77.6% and 67.7% of viruses had an IC80 < 1.0 μg/ml for 10-1074 and PGT121 (Fig. 7B). Using an expanded list of features that included these two features with six others led to minimal improvement in predicting susceptibility.

Six molecular features—absence of PNGS at positions 130, 156, and 160 and 166 not R/K, 167 not D/N, and 169E—had a modest effect on predicting susceptibility to the V2-apex bnAb PDGM1400. In the presence of one or more features, 29.6% of pseudoviruses had an IC80 < 1.0 μg/ml (Fig. 7C). In their absence, 58.0% had an IC80 < 1.0 μg/ml.

Conclusions

The use of bnAbs for HIV-1 treatment presents unique challenges due to the extraordinary variability of HIV-1 Env and the intrinsic resistance of many circulating variants. Susceptibility testing is therefore essential to ensure that a selected bnAb or bnAb combination is active against an individual’s proviral DNA population. Although phenotypic testing provides the most direct and reliable assessment of susceptibility, genotypic analysis offers complementary insights into bnAb mechanisms of action and can inform the design of newer bnAbs with broader and more consistent activity. Although genotypic analysis of HIV-1 Env may identify resistance in some viruses, further research is required before it can reliably predict bnAb susceptibility in clinical settings.

In this review, we synthesized evidence linking HIV-1 gp120 molecular features to reduced bnAb susceptibility from four types of data: (1) selection of amino acid substitutions during bnAb treatment in animal models and clinical trials; (2) identification of escape mutations in in vitro passage and high-throughput mutagenesis experiments; (3) direct measurement of susceptibility in site-directed mutants; and (4) statistical analyses of large genotype–phenotype datasets. Although gp41 residues influence susceptibility to the MPER-binding bnAb 10E8 and can modulate gp120 epitope conformation195–197, consistent associations between gp41 variation and susceptibility to bnAbs targeting gp120 epitopes have been limited.

Predicting bnAb activity from genotypic data is challenging for several reasons. First, bnAbs interact with multiple Env residues, and these interactions vary across subtypes and conformational states; therefore, both selected mutations and their phenotypic effects are often context dependent134,158. Second, amino acids outside defined binding sites influence bnAb activity24,116,117,198. Finally, factors beyond amino acid substitutions, including glycan composition19,21,125 and variable loop length99,151,199,200, contribute to bnAb resistance.

Three main challenges limit the broader implementation of bnAb-based treatment regimens: (1) the incomplete breadth of bnAbs across circulating HIV-1 variants; (2) the difficulty detecting low-abundance resistant variants within the proviral DNA reservoir using either genotypic or phenotypic methods; and (3) the limited ability to predict bnAb susceptibility from Env genotypes.

Addressing the first challenge will require the clinical development of bnAbs with greater breadth, such as the recently described CD4bs bnAbs 1-18, N49P9.6, and 04_A6137,191,201, as well as the identification of bnAbs targeting diverse epitopes to enable more effective combination strategies. Because bnAbs can exert immunomodulatory effects that extend viral suppression beyond that predicted from plasma concentrations75,93–102, it is possible that a single active bnAb, when combined with a potent long-acting small-molecule agent, may be sufficient for maintaining durable suppression in PLWH transitioning to long-acting therapy.

The second challenge may be mitigated by assessing bnAb susceptibility using plasma samples obtained at the time of initial HIV-1 diagnosis, prior to ART, thereby avoiding the difficulty of detecting low-abundance resistant variants in the proviral DNA reservoir202. Consistent with this approach, bnAb-based maintenance strategies have shown more efficacy when ART is initiated early, such as in infants treated at birth or adults treated during acute infection75,143,144.

Overcoming the third challenge will require continued expansion of the types of data reviewed here. The predictive performance of genotypic approaches is currently constrained by the limited availability of in vitro and in vivo selection data and by the relatively small number of viruses tested for many bnAbs in clinical development. Systematic collection of selection data and expansion of genotype–phenotype datasets should improve the performance of predictive algorithms and may ultimately enable more reliable prediction of bnAb susceptibility.
