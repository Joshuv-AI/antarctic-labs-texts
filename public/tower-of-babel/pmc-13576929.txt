Prolonged Headache in a 13‐Year‐Old Girl With Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia: A Case of Possible Neuroinflammatory Complication Responsive to Intravenous Immunoglobulin
Authors: Amereh Hadizadeh, Hossein Esfahani, Firozeh Hosseini, Afshin Fayyazi, Nasibeh Ghalandari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNeurological complications in pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) may result from disease, CNS involvement, or treatment. We report a 13‐year‐old girl with persistent headache and blurred vision suggestive of raised intracranial pressure. MRI findings and clinical evaluation raised suspicion for a possible immune‐mediated or demyelinating process, improved following IVIG therapy.

Key Clinical MessageMessage Prolonged headache in children with acute lymphoblastic leukemia may indicate possible demyelinating/immune‐mediated neurological complication. This case highlights the importance of considering immune‐mediated etiologies in atypical neurological presentations and demonstrates a rapid, sustained response to intravenous immunoglobulin therapy, offering a potentially effective treatment approach in similar cases.

Introduction

Neurological complications occur in approximately 10%–20% of children undergoing treatment for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and represent a major source of morbidity. These may result from direct leukemic infiltration of the CNS, infections due to immunosuppression, cerebrovascular events, or treatment‐related toxicities such as posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES), methotrexate‐induced leukoencephalopathy, or drug‐induced encephalomalacia. Headache is one of the most common neurological manifestations, reported in up to 18.9% of cases with neurological involvement, and may signal raised intracranial pressure (ICP), CNS infiltration, or inflammatory processes [1, 2, 3, 4].

Immune‐mediated encephalopathies, including acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), are less commonly described in ALL but can mimic relapse or treatment toxicity. ADEM typically presents with multifocal neurological deficits, encephalopathy, and characteristic white‐matter hyperintensities on MRI, often triggered by infection or, rarely, as a paraneoplastic phenomenon. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is a recognized first‐line or adjunctive therapy for ADEM and other autoimmune encephalitides, modulating the immune response and rapidly improving symptoms in many cases [5, 6, 7].

We describe a 13‐year‐old girl with longstanding ALL who developed prolonged headache and signs of raised ICP that proved refractory to acetazolamide but responded dramatically to IVIG, supporting a suspected diagnosis of demyelinating/immune‐mediated neurological complication pending further confirmatory workup.

Case Presentation

A 13‐year‐old girl with a 3‐year history of standard‐risk B‐cell ALL (in remission on maintenance therapy) presented with a several‐week history of prolonged headache and blurred vision. The maintenance regimen for these patients was as follows: Methotrexate po every week, vincristine every month, Mercaptopurine every day and IT therapy every 3 months. Symptoms were consistent with raised ICP, including papilledema on fundoscopy, headache that woke her up in the morning and nausea/vomiting. MRI imaging and LP were performed. (Table 1 and Figure 1). She had been treated with acetazolamide 375 mg daily for presumed IIH with partial improvement, but headache persisted.

Although the measured CSF opening pressures were only mildly elevated or within the upper normal range (initial 10 cmH2O; subsequent values 5 cmH2O and 2 cmH2O), the clinical diagnosis of raised ICP rested on the combination of characteristic morning headache, nausea/vomiting, blurred vision, and confirmed papilledema. The patient had already been receiving acetazolamide before the first recorded lumbar puncture, which may have lowered the measured pressures. Serial pressures declined further in parallel with clinical improvement after IVIG.

Brain MRI

Non‐contrast and contrast‐enhanced MRI demonstrated non‐specific hyperintense signals on T2‐weighted and FLAIR sequences in the deep white matter, basal ganglia, and periventricular regions (Figure 1). These findings were interpreted as possibly related to metabolic derangements or encephalomalacia/leukoencephalopathy, common in long‐term ALL survivors exposed to CNS‐directed chemotherapy. No mass lesions, enhancement, or restricted diffusion suggestive of acute ischemia or PML were noted.

The affected white matter looks bright on the ADC map rather than dark; thus, no obvious diffusion restriction argues against an acute ischemic or densely cellular (e.g., PML, lymphomatous) process and is more consistent with vasogenic edema/demyelination.

The imaging appearance alone cannot reliably distinguish an immune‐mediated demyelinating process from chemotherapy‐related leukoencephalopathy; the diagnosis was therefore guided by the inflammatory CSF profile, negative infectious work‐up, absence of blasts, and the subsequent clinical response to IVIG.

Initial CSF showed elevated protein and hypoglycorrhachia (low glucose), suggestive of an inflammatory or infectious process, which normalized on subsequent analyses (Table 1). No blasts were identified, ruling out overt CNS relapse.

Neurological consultation considered differentials of ADEM, CNS vasculitis, or PML given the MRI appearance and clinical context in an immunocompromised patient. Extensive infectious workup (including viral PCRs) was negative.

Autoimmune encephalitis antibody panels (including MOG‐IgG, AQP4‐IgG, and NMDAR antibodies), oligoclonal bands, and IgG index were not obtained at the time of presentation due to limited resources and high price.

Given the lack of response to acetazolamide and the inflammatory CSF profile with demyelinating‐like MRI changes, a trial of immunomodulatory therapy was initiated. The patient received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a dose of 2 g/kg (divided over 3 days, standard protocol). Within days, her headache and blurred vision improved significantly, with sustained resolution of symptoms at follow‐up. No further interventions (e.g., steroids or plasmapheresis) were required. Repeat imaging or CSF was not performed immediately post‐treatment, but clinical recovery was excellent.

Discussion

Persistent headache and raised ICP in a child with ALL years into treatment pose a broad differential that includes CNS relapse, treatment‐related leukoencephalopathy, infection, vascular complications, and immune‐mediated processes. In this case, the absence of blasts in CSF, negative HSV PCR, and non‐specific MRI hyperintensities argued against active leukemic infiltration or viral encephalitis. The initial CSF profile (elevated protein with variably low glucose) suggested an inflammatory or immune‐mediated process, while the dramatic response to IVIG was suggestive of a possible demyelinating/immune‐mediated neurological complication, most consistent with possible ADEM or an ADEM‐like syndrome [2, 4].

ADEM and ADEM‐like presentations have been reported in pediatric ALL, both at diagnosis as a possible paraneoplastic phenomenon and during/after treatment. In one reported case, a child with newly diagnosed B‐ALL presented with seizures and multifocal white‐matter lesions on MRI consistent with ADEM in the absence of CNS leukemia; symptoms and imaging improved with chemotherapy (including steroids). Another case described ADEM mimicking late CNS relapse in ALL, highlighting the diagnostic challenge in treated patients. MRI findings of T2/FLAIR hyperintensities in white matter, basal ganglia, and periventricular areas overlap with those of chemotherapy‐induced leukoencephalopathy, yet the clinical course and IVIG responsiveness in our patient pointed toward an immune rather than purely toxic etiology [2, 6, 7].

IVIG is an established therapy for ADEM and other autoimmune neurological syndromes, modulating immune responses and rapidly improving symptoms in many cases. PML was considered but deemed less likely due to the rapid response to IVIG (PML typically progresses despite supportive care) and absence of characteristic asymmetric, confluent white matter lesions without enhancement. The excellent response in this case supports considering empiric IVIG when a demyelinating/immune‐mediated neurological complication is suspected, especially when steroids are relatively contraindicated or when symptoms persist despite standard ICP management [5, 9, 10].

This case underscores the value of a multidisciplinary approach involving hematology, neurology, and radiology in ALL patients with atypical neurotoxicity. Early consideration of immunomodulation when standard ICP management fails can prevent long‐term morbidity.

Although high‐dose intravenous corticosteroids (typically methylprednisolone 30 mg/kg/day for 3–5 days) are considered first‐line therapy for ADEM and related conditions [7], corticosteroids were deliberately avoided in this case for two principal reasons. First, the patient was already receiving maintenance chemotherapy that includes corticosteroids; additional high‐dose systemic steroids would further increase immunosuppression and the risk of opportunistic infection. Second, corticosteroids exert direct cytolytic effects on lymphoblasts and are integral to CNS‐directed prophylaxis; any clinical improvement after steroids could therefore not be reliably attributed to resolution of an immune‐mediated process rather than suppression of an undetected early CNS leukemic relapse—particularly important given the absence of CSF flow cytometry. IVIG was selected because it modulates the immune response (via Fc‐receptor blockade, cytokine regulation, and autoantibody neutralization) without antileukemic cytotoxicity, thereby preserving diagnostic clarity while providing comparable efficacy and a favorable safety profile in immunocompromised patients [10].

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this case report. First, the diagnosis of immune‐mediated neurological complications (likely ADEM‐like) remains presumptive, as confirmatory investigations including autoimmune encephalitis antibody panels (MOG‐IgG, AQP4‐IgG, and NMDAR antibodies), oligoclonal bands, and IgG index were not obtained due to limited resources and high cost. Secondly, as a single‐case report, the findings lack statistical generalizability, and the dramatic response to IVIG, while suggestive, does not establish causality; the improvement could reflect the natural course of a self‐limiting inflammatory process rather than a direct therapeutic effect. Finally, the absence of long‐term follow‐up data precludes assessment of potential relapse or delayed neurological sequelae, which are particularly relevant in immunocompromised pediatric ALL patients. Future studies with larger cohorts and comprehensive immunological workups are needed to validate these findings and establish evidence‐based protocols for immune‐mediated neurological complications in pediatric ALL.

Conclusion

Persistent headache and visual symptoms in a child with ALL require thorough evaluation beyond routine IIH management. In this case, a suspected demyelinating/immune‐mediated neurological complication was successfully treated with IVIG, highlighting its therapeutic potential in selected post‐chemotherapy neurological complications. Long‐term follow‐up is essential to monitor for relapse or sequelae.
