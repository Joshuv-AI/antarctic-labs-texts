Ataxin-2-like promotes translation of nonpolyadenylated reovirus mRNA
Authors: Xayathed Somoulay, Gavin S Treadaway, Stefanie Hedayati, Myles E Steed, Nathan E Adam, Monico S Williams, John S L Parker, John L Woolford, Laurie A Silva, Gwen M Taylor, Terence S Dermody
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMammalian orthoreovirus (reovirus) nonstructural protein μNS nucleates viral factories (VFs), which are sites of viral genome replication, protein synthesis, and particle assembly. Reovirus mRNAs are not polyadenylated, yet these transcripts are efficiently translated. To identify host factors associated with translation in VFs, we conducted a proteomics screen and identified ataxin-2-like (ATXN2L) as a μNS-interacting protein. CRISPR-mediated gene knockout (KO) of ATXN2L impairs reovirus replication. The ATXN2L RNA-binding domains are required for reovirus replication and association with the 3′ terminus of nonpolyadenylated reovirus mRNAs. Synthesis of viral proteins is diminished in ATXN2L-KO cells following reovirus infection. Translation of a reovirus reporter construct is diminished following transfection of ATXN2L-KO cells with nonpolyadenylated mRNA but not with polyadenylated mRNA. These data identify ATXN2L as an essential mediator of translation of nonpolyadenylated reovirus mRNA.Subject terms: Cell biology, Translation, Virus-host interactions

Introduction

Eukaryotic translation is made efficient by the presence of a 5′ cap and 3′ polyadenylated (poly[A]) tail appended to mRNAs. During translation initiation, the 5′ cap is bound by cap-binding protein eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E (eIF4E), which interacts with eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4A (eIF4A) and scaffolding protein eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4G (eIF4G) to form the eIF4F complex1. Poly(A) binding protein (PABP) engages the 3′ poly(A) tail and simultaneously scaffolds the eIF4F complex by interacting with eIF4G1. The eIF4F-PABP-mRNA interaction circularizes the mRNA and promotes recruitment of the 40S ribosomal subunit to the 5′ end of the mRNA to stimulate translation initiation in a closed loop1,2. Circularization of mRNA enhances translation by promoting translation reinitiation using the same RNA transcript1,2, underscoring the importance of the 5′ cap and 3′ poly(A) tail. Nearly all cellular mRNAs have a 5′ cap. However, some cellular and viral mRNAs lack 3′ poly(A) tails3–8. Mechanisms by which nonpolyadenylated mRNAs are translated are not well understood.

Mammalian orthoreovirus (reovirus) forms nonenveloped virions that package ten segments of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) in two concentric shells, called the outer capsid and core9. Reovirus spreads by the enteric and respiratory routes and causes age-restricted disease in a variety of mammals10–12. Reovirus genome synthesis, translation, and capsid assembly occur within neoorganelles called viral factories (VFs), which are nucleated by viral nonstructural protein μNS13–15. The μNS protein interacts with viral structural and nonstructural proteins and the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase16–20. Viral cores also interact with μNS and are embedded within VFs, where they transcribe viral mRNAs that are capped at the 5′ end but lack 3′ poly(A) tails3,16,21,22. Despite viral mRNAs lacking poly(A) tails, reovirus proteins are efficiently expressed in infected cells3. In translation experiments conducted in vitro, free poly(A) impairs translation of polyadenylated mRNAs but does not affect translation of reovirus mRNAs23, suggesting that reovirus translation does not require PABP and instead depends on undetermined viral or host factors.

In this study, we used a proximity-based biotinylation identification (BioID) screen to identify host interactants of VF-forming protein μNS. Ataxin-2-like (ATXN2L), a stress granule (SG) protein implicated in posttranscriptional regulation of some cellular mRNAs24, was identified by μNS BioID in a network of SG and translation proteins. ATXN2L functions with its paralog, ataxin-2 (ATXN2), to promote the translation of circadian rhythm mRNAs25. While circadian rhythm transcripts are polyadenylated, an ATXN2-independent translation function for ATXN2L has not been elucidated. We found that ATXN2L interacts with μNS and localizes to VFs in infected cells. Reovirus replication is impaired in cells lacking ATXN2L but not in those lacking ATXN2. Deleting the ATXN2L RNA-binding LSm domain alters the capacity of the protein to associate with both reovirus mRNA and μNS protein. ATXN2L associates with the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of reovirus mRNAs but not with those lacking a conserved terminal pentanucleotide sequence. Translation of nonpolyadenylated reovirus mRNA but not polyadenylated mRNA is dampened in cells lacking ATXN2L. Together, these data suggest that ATXN2L is required for reovirus replication by stimulating the translation of nonpolyadenylated reovirus mRNAs. This study identifies a new proviral host protein for reovirus and enhances understanding of factors used to translate mRNAs lacking 3′ poly(A) tails.

Results

Identification of an SG network interacting with reovirus nonstructural protein μNS

Reovirus nonstructural protein μNS forms the matrix for reovirus replication factories in infected cells13,17,26. Since viral translation is compartmentalized within VFs27, we hypothesized that an unbiased approach to identify host protein interactants of μNS would uncover proviral factors that function directly in viral translation. To identify VF-associated proteins, we used a proximity-based BioID screen with μNS from prototype reovirus strain T3D (Fig. 1a). A promiscuous BirA biotin ligase28 was fused to the amino-terminus of μNS (BirA-μNS) and placed under the control of a doxycycline-inducible promoter. BirA-μNS expression was confirmed in infected cells following doxycycline (Dox) induction (Fig. 1b). For the μNS BioID screen, we employed a strategy similar to a BioID screen we conducted previously to identify host proteins that interact with viral structural protein σ329 and used uninduced (−Dox) cells lacking BirA-μNS expression as a negative control. Relative to uninduced (-Dox) control cells, induction (+Dox) led to expression of BirA-μNS, which localized to cytoplasmic VFs marked by viral nonstructural protein σNS (Fig. 1b). In the presence of exogenous biotin, BirA-μNS promoted biotinylation of proteins at VF assemblies (Fig. 1c), with a greater abundance of biotinylated species observed in BirA-μNS-expressing cells (+Dox) relative to uninduced (−Dox) control cells (Fig. 1d).

Affinity capture and mass spectrometry of biotinylated proteins in BirA-μNS-expressing cells identified 50 unique candidates, including reovirus proteins and host proteins that distribute to VFs27,30–32 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Using Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, we observed that the identified cellular proteins are enriched in biological processes related to posttranscriptional regulation, translation regulation, SG assembly, and organization and biogenesis of cellular components (Fig. 1e). STRING protein-protein interaction cluster analysis33 grouped candidates into four interaction networks. Cluster 1 is enriched for proteins involved in protein folding and cytoskeletal organization (Supplementary Fig. 1b), including chaperonin containing tailless complex polypeptide 1 (TCP1) subunit 3 (CCT3), a subunit of the T-complex ring complex (TRiC) chaperonin, which folds viral outer-capsid protein σ330. Consistent with previous studies suggesting that VFs recruit SG proteins31 and compartmentalize translation27, Cluster 2 is enriched for SG-associated proteins and proteins involved in translation initiation (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 1c). This network includes Ras-GTPase-activating protein binding protein 1 (G3BP1), an SG-nucleating protein that associates with VFs during reovirus infection31. Clusters 3 and 4 include cadherin-binding proteins and proteins involved in nuclear reorganization, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e).

Reovirus infection induces SG formation34,35, but at later stages of replication, SGs are dissipated, and SG-associated proteins are recruited to VFs31,32,36. Precise functions of SG proteins during reovirus replication are unknown. Since GO analysis revealed processes such as SG assembly and regulation of translation in the top five most enriched terms (Fig. 1e), we chose to focus on candidates in STRING Cluster 2 (Fig. 1f). To identify VF-associated candidates that promote reovirus replication, we cross-referenced proteins in Cluster 2 with top candidates from two previous genetic screens for reovirus proviral host factors, a whole genome CRISPR screen conducted using murine BV-2 cells and reovirus strains T1L and T3D37 (Fig. 1g) and a whole genome siRNA screen conducted using HeLa cells and reovirus strain T3SA+37,38 (Fig. 1h). Comparison of the three independent screens revealed ATXN2L, an SG-associated protein implicated in posttranscriptional regulation of gene expression24, as a single common candidate (Fig. 1f–h). These data suggest that ATXN2L functions in reovirus replication.

ATXN2L interacts with μNS

To validate ATXN2L as a VF-associated protein, we first probed for ATXN2L and μNS interactions using BirA-μNS-expressing cells and found that ATXN2L co-precipitates with BirA-μNS (Fig. 2a). We verified this interaction by conducting the reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation using reovirus-infected cells and observed co-precipitation of μNS with ATXN2L (Fig. 2b). Since μNS scaffolds VFs in infected cells14,17,26, we speculated that μNS-ATXN2L interactions would occur at VFs. To assess this possibility, we visualized the subcellular distribution of ATXN2L in mock- and reovirus-infected cells using confocal microscopy. In mock-infected cells, ATXN2L distribution was diffuse in the cytoplasm. However, in reovirus-infected cells, ATXN2L distributed to VFs marked by μNS (Fig. 2c, d). Thus, ATXN2L interacts with μNS and localizes to VFs during reovirus infection.

The carboxy-terminal region of μNS is required for interactions with ATXN2L

To identify sequences of μNS required for ATXN2L interactions, we co-expressed ATXN2L and HA-tagged full-length μNS or truncated μNS in 293T Lenti-X cells (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Truncation of the carboxy-terminal third of μNS abolished interactions with ATXN2L, while deletion of residues in the amino-terminus maintained interactions, although slightly diminished compared with full-length μNS (Fig. 2f, g). Additionally, expression of only the carboxy-terminal third of μNS (residues 472–721) was sufficient to promote interactions with ATXN2L (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c).

ATXN2L is required for reovirus replication

To determine whether ATXN2L is required for reovirus replication, we used siRNA-mediated gene silencing in HeLa cells. At 24 h post-infection, we observed an approximately sixfold decrease in viral titer in ATXN2L siRNA-treated cells compared with control siRNA-treated cells (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). However, siRNA-mediated gene silencing of ATXN2L paralog, ATXN2, did not affect viral titers (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d), suggesting that ATXN2 and ATXN2L lack functional redundancy in the context of reovirus infection. ATXN2L expression is not completely abrogated using siRNA-mediated gene silencing (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Therefore, to further investigate a requirement for ATXN2L in reovirus replication, we used CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing to engineer a clonal HeLa cell line with a nonfunctional ATXN2L gene. As a control, ATXN2L-knockout (KO) cells were complemented with wild-type ATXN2L (KO+). Isogenic cell lines were engineered by complementing wild-type HeLa cells and ATXN2L-KO cells with an empty vector (WT and KO, respectively) (Fig. 3a). WT, KO, and KO+ cells express comparable levels of ATXN2 (Fig. 3b) and maintain similar viability and proliferation rate (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Following inoculation of WT, KO, and KO+ cells with reovirus, we assessed infectivity by viral antigen staining at a late time point and observed significantly diminished infectivity of KO cells relative to WT cells (Fig. 3c, d), consistent with diminished reovirus titers in ATXN2L siRNA-treated cells (Supplementary Fig. 3b). In contrast, infection of WT and KO+ cells was comparable and robust (Fig. 3c, d), indicating that restoring ATXN2L expression in KO cells rescues reovirus infectivity. To verify that ATXN2L is required for viral replication, WT, KO, and KO+ cells were inoculated with reovirus, and viral titers in cell lysates were determined. Viral replication in KO cells at 24 h post-infection was decreased by ~100-fold compared with that in WT cells. Replication was restored to WT levels following ATXN2L complementation (Fig. 3e), substantiating ATXN2L as a proviral host factor for reovirus. In contrast, ATXN2L is not required for replication of chikungunya virus (CHIKV) (Fig. 3f), indicating that KO cells are viable and can support the replication of an unrelated RNA virus.

ATXN2L is dispensable for early reovirus replication steps

To identify steps in reovirus replication that require ATXN2L, we first evaluated early replication steps, including virus attachment and capsid disassembly. Using a flow-cytometry-based binding assay, reovirus attachment to WT, KO, and KO+ cells was comparable (Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). Following internalization of reovirus into cells, virions traffic through the endocytic pathway, where outer-capsid proteins are removed by cathepsin proteases39. The μ1C fragment of outer-capsid protein μ1 is cleaved to form δ40. There were no significant changes in the kinetics of μ1C to δ formation in WT, KO, and KO+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 5d, e). Together, these data indicate that ATXN2L is dispensable for reovirus attachment and disassembly.

Reovirus co-opts SGs during replication31,35, and ATXN2L contributes to efficient SG assembly41. Using sodium arsenite and reovirus infection to induce SG formation in WT, KO, and KO+ cells, we observed that SG formation is not impaired in KO cells relative to WT and KO+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 5f, g). These data suggest a function for ATXN2L at a replication step that follows SG assembly.

Reovirus replication requires the ATXN2L LSm and LSmAD domains

ATXN2L encodes two amino-terminal RNA-binding domains, Sm-like (LSm) and Sm-like associated domain (LSmAD) (Fig. 4a). Additionally, the carboxy-terminus of ATXN2L has a PAM2 motif (Fig. 4a), which interacts with PABP42. To identify domains in ATXN2L required for reovirus replication, we complemented ATXN2L-KO cells with constructs expressing single domain deletions of individual RNA-binding domains (ΔLSm and ΔLSmAD), deletion of both RNA-binding domains (Δ2X-LSm), or deletion of the PAM2 motif (ΔPAM2) (Fig. 4a, b). Deletion of either individual or both RNA-binding domains disrupted ATXN2L interactions with μNS in infected cells (Fig. 4c, d). However, ATXN2L interactions with μNS were less diminished in ΔLSmAD cells than in ΔLSm or Δ2X-LSm cells (Fig. 4c, d), suggesting that the LSm domain is the primary mediator of ATXN2L interactions with μNS. In contrast to the LSm and LSmAD domain deletions, interactions of ATXN2L with μNS in ΔPAM2 cells were comparable to WT (Fig. 4c, d). Since μNS expression was diminished in reovirus-infected ΔLSm, ΔLSmAD, and Δ2X-LSm cells, we tested ATXN2L-μNS interactions following transfection of a plasmid encoding HA-tagged μNS and observed similar interaction patterns (Supplementary Fig. 6).

To assess a requirement for ATXN2L domains in reovirus replication, WT, KO, KO+, and mutant ATXN2L-KO+ cells were inoculated with reovirus, and viral titers were determined at 0, 24, and 48 h incubation (Fig. 4e, f). Following 24 h incubation, viral yields were significantly diminished in ΔLSm, ΔLSmAD, and Δ2X-LSm cells relative to WT cells, while viral yields were not diminished in ΔPAM2 cells (Fig. 4e). These data are consistent with a requirement for the ATXN2L LSm and LSmAD domains for interactions with μNS (Fig. 4c, d and Supplementary Fig. 6). Following 48 h incubation, viral yields in ΔLSmAD and WT cells were comparable (Fig. 4f), suggesting that LSmAD contributes only modestly to replication efficiency. These data suggest that an mRNA-binding function of ATXN2L is required for reovirus replication.

Reovirus 3′UTR sequences associate with ATXN2L

Since the ATXN2L LSm and LSmAD domains bind RNA24,43 and removing these domains impairs reovirus replication, we hypothesized that ATXN2L interacts with reovirus RNA. To evaluate the capacity of ATXN2L to associate with reovirus RNA, we captured ATXN2L in infected cells by immunoprecipitation and probed the immunoprecipitates for reovirus s4 mRNA as a surrogate using RT-qPCR. ATXN2L immunoprecipitated from WT cells efficiently engaged reovirus s4 mRNA compared with an IgG control (Fig. 5a). In contrast, ATXN2L association with s4 mRNA was significantly impaired in ΔLSm and Δ2X-LSm cells but not in ΔLSmAD or ΔPAM2 cells (Fig. 5a), suggesting that the LSm domain is required for interactions with reovirus mRNA. We also assessed the capacity of ATXN2L to interact with FAM199X mRNA, an RNA target of ATXN243 (Fig. 5b). ATXN2L interaction with FAM199X mRNA was not changed following deletion of either LSm or LSmAD domains. The binding of FAM199X to ΔPAM2 ATXN2L was slightly diminished, albeit not statistically significant.

Since ATXN2 interacts with sequences within the 3′UTRs of target RNAs25, we hypothesized that a sequence in the 3′UTRs of reovirus mRNAs was required for ATXN2L association. Reovirus 3′UTRs are highly variable in length and sequence3, but there is a conserved terminal pentanucleotide sequence (5′-UCAUC-3′) present in all reovirus mRNAs (Fig. 5c). To assess the requirement for the 3′ terminal sequence for interactions with ATXN2L, we used biotinylated s4 3′UTR or s4 3′UTR with a deletion of ten nucleotides from the 3′ terminus (s4 3′UTRΔ10) to capture ATXN2L from mock-infected and reovirus-infected cells (Fig. 5d). We chose to delete ten nucleotides from s4 due to a near-tandem 5′-UCAUC-3′ sequence at the 3′ end (Fig. 5d). As a negative control, we used a scrambled s4 3′UTR sequence. We found that the s4 3′UTR precipitated ATXN2L from infected cells, but the scrambled RNA control did not (Fig. 5e, f). However, there was minimal association of ATXN2L with s4 3′UTR in mock conditions (Fig. 5e, f), suggesting that reovirus infection influences ATXN2L function or that an unidentified host or viral protein interacts with viral RNA in complex with ATXN2L. Deletion of the terminal ten nucleotides of s4 3′UTR significantly diminished ATXN2L association (Fig. 5e, f), providing additional evidence that ATXN2L engages viral RNAs in a sequence-specific manner. To determine whether ATXN2L interacts with other reovirus RNAs, we used 3′UTRs from s4, m3, and l3 as representatives from each class of reovirus RNA and observed ATXN2L association with each (Fig. 5g, h). Together, these data suggest that ATXN2L interacts with terminal sequences of reovirus mRNA, possibly in complex with additional viral or host proteins.

Viral factory enlargement is abrogated in the absence of ATXN2L

ATXN2L is implicated in the translation of some cellular mRNAs25. To determine whether ATXN2L functions in the translation of reovirus mRNAs, we first visualized VF formation in infected WT, KO, and KO+ cells. Large, globular VFs occupied most of the cytoplasm in WT and KO+ cells (Fig. 6a, b), consistent with VFs observed at later stages of reovirus replication19. In contrast, VFs in KO cells remained punctate (Fig. 6a, b). The morphology of VF-like structures was comparable in uninfected WT, KO, and KO+ cells overexpressing GFP-μNS (Supplementary Fig. 7), suggesting that VF formation in infected KO cells is impaired due to diminished μNS protein expression (Fig. 6c) and not an intrinsic function of ATXN2L to promote VF assembly.

ATXN2L associates with polysomes in reovirus-infected cells

To further evaluate the capacity of ATXN2L to promote reovirus translation, we conducted polysome analysis using mock and reovirus-infected cells. A substantial increase in the 80S ribosome peak from infected cells compared with mock was observed (Fig. 7a), consistent with stalled monosomes. We collected fractions along the polysome profile and used immunoblotting to determine ATXN2L abundance in each fraction. We found that ATXN2L redistributes to polysomes in infected cells compared with mock (Fig. 7b, c).

The poly(A)-binding protein engages poly(A) tails of mRNAs and circularizes these mRNAs by interacting with eIF4G to enhance translation initiation efficiency44,45. To evaluate whether ATXN2L interacts with eIF4G during reovirus replication, we immunoprecipitated ATXN2L from infected cells and probed the immunoprecipitates for selected translation factors. Both eIF4G1 and eIF4G3 co-precipitated with ATXN2L (Supplementary Fig. 8), consistent with their identification in the μNS BioID screen (Fig. 1f). Together, these data suggest that ATXN2L is present in translation complexes during reovirus replication.

ATXN2L promotes translation of nonpolyadenylated reovirus mRNA

Despite lacking a 3′ poly(A) tail, reovirus mRNAs are translated efficiently in infected cells34. To test a function for ATXN2L in reovirus translation, we used RT-qPCR to determine the abundance of viral s4 mRNA along the polysome profiles from infected WT and KO cells (Fig. 7d). We observed a decrease in s4 associated with polysome fractions (Fig. 7e) from KO cells compared with WT, suggesting translation of reovirus RNA is diminished in the absence of ATXN2L. To verify that the decrease in translation was not due to a reduction in global translation, we conducted a puromycin incorporation assay using WT, KO, and KO+ cells and observed comparable translation levels in each of the cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Additionally, we did not observe significantly altered ACTB mRNA association with polysome fractions in KO cells compared with WT cells (Supplementary Fig. 9c), although we cannot exclude the possibility that translation of other host mRNAs is diminished. These data provide evidence that ATXN2L promotes translation of specific mRNAs.

To further evaluate an ATXN2L requirement in reovirus translation, we infected WT, KO, and KO+ cells with reovirus and quantified expression of the s4 translation product, outer-capsid protein σ3. Throughout a single cycle of replication, σ3 levels were diminished in ATXN2L-KO cells relative to those in WT and KO+ cells (Fig. 7f and Supplementary Fig. 10a, b), suggesting that ATXN2L is required for efficient σ3 expression. We also observed diminished expression of other viral structural proteins in KO cells relative to WT and KO+ cells following immunoblotting using a reovirus-specific polyclonal antiserum (Supplementary Fig. 10c–f). In contrast, transfection of WT, KO, and KO+ cells with a σ3-encoding plasmid, which yields capped and polyadenylated mRNA when transcribed46, led to comparable levels of σ3 (Fig. 7g). Transcription of σ3-encoding s4 mRNA was not impaired in infected cells lacking ATXN2L (Fig. 7h). Additionally, we evaluated the expression of the core histone proteins, H2A, H2B, H3, and H4, which are encoded by nonpolyadenylated mRNA and found comparable levels of these histone proteins in WT, KO, and KO+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). Thus, ATXN2L specifically promotes translation of nonpolyadenylated reovirus transcripts.

To directly compare ATXN2L-mediated translation of nonpolyadenylated and polyadenylated reovirus mRNAs, we engineered NanoLuciferase translation reporter constructs that terminate with either the native reovirus s4 3′UTR or the s4 3′UTR with an appended poly(A) tail (Fig. 7i). In vitro transcribed mRNAs (Fig. 7j) were transfected into infected WT, KO, and KO+ cells. Translation of mRNA with a native reovirus s4 3′ terminus (poly[A]-) was diminished in KO cells relative to that in WT cells but restored in KO+ cells (Fig. 7k). However, translation of reporter mRNA encoding a 3′ poly(A) tail (poly[A]+) was comparable in WT, KO, and KO+ cells (Fig. 7k). Together, these data substantiate a function for ATXN2L in translation of nonpolyadenylated reovirus mRNAs.

Discussion

In this study, we discovered a reovirus replication factory interaction network enriched with SG proteins using a BioID screen. The cellular integrated stress response is activated as an intrinsic defense mechanism against many viruses, several of which have mechanisms to subvert and benefit from this response47. Stress pathways also function in reovirus replication31,34–36,48, and our findings substantiate the interplay between these processes. Within the SG network, we identified ATXN2L as a proviral host factor required for reovirus replication. We provide evidence that ATXN2L is recruited to VFs by interactions with viral nonstructural protein μNS and promotes efficient translation of the nonpolyadenylated reovirus mRNAs. Our findings enhance an understanding of reovirus replication and illuminate a function for ATXN2L in translation of mRNAs that lack integral RNA elements required for translation initiation.

ATXN2L is an SG-associated protein that functions in mRNA metabolic pathways43,49–52, although evidence linking these functions to ATXN2L is limited. ATXN2L shares functional domains with its paralog ATXN2, and both proteins are conserved in most vertebrates25,53. Thus, many of the proposed functions of ATXN2L are inferred from studies of ATXN2. Our data demonstrate that ATXN2L lacks functional redundancy with ATXN2 in certain circumstances, as silencing of ATXN2 does not affect reovirus replication (Supplementary Fig. 3), and endogenous ATXN2 expression in ATXN2L-KO cells is not sufficient to restore viral titers (Fig. 3b). Additionally, ATXN2 and ATXN2L may not completely share the same RNA targets, as deletion of the ATXN2L LSm domain does not diminish binding of ATXN2 RNA target, FAM199X (Fig. 5b). Differences in ATXN2 and ATXN2L functions also have been observed in murine development. Genetic ablation of ATXN2 in mice does not perturb viability51, but attempts to engineer ATXN2L-null mice have not been successful, as these mice are embryonic lethal54. These data provide support for the importance of ATXN2L functions independent of those of ATXN2.

ATXN2 and ATXN2L share three functional domains: the RNA-binding LSm domain, the LSmAD domain, which harbors a clathrin-mediated trans-Golgi signal, and the PAM2 motif, which interacts with PABP41,43. Deletion of the ATXN2L LSm domain, but not the LSmAD domain, significantly diminishes reovirus replication and the capacity to engage reovirus mRNA. These observations are consistent with differential RNA-binding activity by the two domains, as observed in studies using recombinant LSm and LSmAD domains of ATXN243. In contrast to LSm, the PAM2 motif is dispensable for reovirus replication, providing additional support for a mechanism of reovirus translation independent of PABP23.

The LSm domain is found in the broader family of LSm proteins, which are expressed in all eukaryotes and highly conserved55. These proteins are required for the formation of spliceosomal snRNP complexes by interactions with uridine-rich regions at the 3′ end of U snRNAs56,57. Specific ATXN2 mRNA targets containing AU-rich elements have been identified in 3′UTRs43, although interactions between these motifs and paralog ATXN2L have yet to be validated. The 3′UTRs of reovirus mRNAs are highly variable in length, ranging from 35 to 73 nucleotides, with conservation of only the five 3′-terminal residues (5′-UCAUC-3′)3. Our findings suggest that this sequence is required for association with ATXN2L as the deletion of ten nucleotides including the conserved pentanucleotide at the terminus of the s4 3′UTR significantly reduces ATXN2L interaction (Fig. 5e). The NSP3 protein of rotavirus, which also transcribes nonpolyadenylated mRNAs, interacts with the conserved 3′-terminal 5′-GACC-3′ sequence of rotavirus mRNAs and binds eIF4G to establish 5′−3′ communication58–60. However, there is little apparent sequence or structural similarity between the two proteins. NSP3 is a highly structured 36 kDa protein that functions as a homodimer59, while ATXN2L is a 113 kDa protein predicted to include several disordered regions25. We hypothesize that these proteins use different biochemical mechanisms to serve analogous functions. In addition to identifying a reovirus sequence required for RNA interactions, we found that ATXN2L has the capacity to interact with viral RNAs of each class, small, medium, and large (Fig. 5h). However, our study is limited in that purified interactants were not used to assess the possibility of direct interactions. Thus, it is possible that ATXN2L functions in complex with other viral or host proteins to engage reovirus RNAs. We observed minimal interaction of reovirus RNA with ATXN2L in mock-infected cells compared with infected cells, suggesting that ATXN2L has a greater affinity for RNA targets in infected conditions. Polysome profiles from reovirus-infected cells demonstrate a substantially larger 80S ribosome peak compared with those from mock-infected cells (Fig. 7a). This spike in the 80S peak suggests a greater abundance of stalled monosomes, which may result from cellular stress61. However, we do not think these monosomes are SG-associated, since we obtained the polysome profiles at times that correlate with SG disassembly during reovirus replication35. Instead, we hypothesize that the stalled monosomes in infected cells are host transcripts and that there may be a shift in translation from host to reovirus mRNAs during infection, as reovirus proteins are efficiently expressed in infected cells3. We also observed a modest increase in the 80S fraction and slightly lower polysome abundance in ATXN2L-KO cells relative to WT cells (Fig. 7d), suggesting a shift in ATXN2L-mediated translation. However, we did not detect changes in global translation using puromycin labeling (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). These data suggest that ATXN2L is required for translation of additional unidentified host transcripts.

Using a luciferase reporter translation system, we determined that ATXN2L specifically promotes translation of nonpolyadenylated reovirus mRNAs. ATXN2 positively regulates translation of various mRNAs using diverse mechanisms62–64. Both ATXN2 and ATXN2L drive rhythmic translation of circadian mRNAs by recruitment to translation-competent biomolecular condensates25. We did not observe distinct ATXN2L condensates at times that correlate with SG protein recruitment to VFs31, but we did detect ATXN2L staining at VFs. Reovirus VFs appear to coalesce as biomolecular condensates13,17,19,27,65. Since viral translation is localized within these compartments27, we think it is possible that ATXN2L forms nested droplets66 within VFs that compartmentalize sites dedicated to translation. Such a process would minimize viral mRNA competition between translation factors and other viral mRNA-binding proteins19,20 and separate mRNA-dependent processes, including viral transcription, genome replication, and gene segment assortment into progeny virions.

Results from our μNS BioID screen identified both eIF4G1 and eIF4G3 in the same network as ATXN2L. Using co-immunoprecipitation, we validated ATXN2L interactions with both eIF4G isoforms during viral infection (Supplementary Fig. 8). These data suggest that ATXN2L is present in translation complexes during reovirus replication. We verified this possibility using polysome profiling and demonstrated that ATXN2L distributes to polysome fractions in infected cells (Fig. 7b, c). We propose a mechanism by which ATXN2L engages viral mRNAs at the 3′ terminus and mediates interactions with eIF4G to initiate translation. However, whether ATXN2L directly interacts with eIF4G and promotes the formation of an mRNA closed-loop is unclear. We hypothesize that ATXN2L-mediated translation is not required for all nonpolyadenylated transcripts, as levels of some histone proteins are not altered in the absence of ATXN2L (Supplementary Fig. 11). The mRNAs encoding the core histone proteins are nonpolyadenylated, highly regulated, and do not rely on PABP for translation. Instead, the 3′UTRs of these mRNAs harbor a stem-loop structure67 that is bound by stem-loop binding protein (SLBP)68,69. The SLBP-interacting protein (SLIP1) bridges an interaction between SLBP and eIF4G to initiate translation70. Thus, it is not surprising that ATXN2L is dispensable for histone mRNA translation, although we cannot exclude the possibility that ATXN2L targets other nonpolyadenylated transcripts.

Mechanisms of translation for double-stranded RNA viruses that transcribe mRNAs lacking poly(A) tails have been elusive. Our findings establish a new function for SG protein ATXN2L in promoting translation of nonpolyadenylated reovirus mRNAs. As most cellular mRNAs have poly(A) tails, we provide insight into cellular strategies used for specialized translation of mRNAs lacking canonical features.

Methods

Cell lines

HeLa S3 cells (ATCC CCL-2.2) and 293T Lenti-X cells (Takara Bio) were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (GIBCO) supplemented to contain 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (VWR). Spinner-adapted murine L929 fibroblast cells (ATCC CCL-1) were maintained in Joklik’s Modified Eagle’s Minimal Essential Medium (JMEM, United States Biological) supplemented to contain 5% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 U ml−1 penicillin and streptomycin, and 250 ng ml−1 amphotericin B in suspension (35 °C) or monolayer (37 °C) cultures. BHK-21 cells (ATCC CCL-10) were maintained in alpha minimal essential medium (αMEM; Gibco) supplemented to contain 10% FBS and 10% tryptose phosphate. Vero cells (ATCC CCL-81) were maintained in αMEM supplemented to contain 5% FBS, L-glutamine, penicillin, streptomycin, and amphotericin B.

HeLa-derived ATXN2L-KO cells were engineered by transfection with either control pLentiCRISPRv2 or pLentiCRISPRv2-ATXN2LsgRNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) followed by selection with 20 μg ml−1 blasticidin (InvivoGen) administered at 48 h post-transfection. Monoclonal cell populations were obtained by seeding resistant cells at low density (20 cells ml−1) in a 10 cm dish. After 3 weeks, cell colonies were dispersed using cloning rings and trypsin and expanded in a 96-well plate. Clones were validated for ATXN2L protein expression using immunoblotting. ATXN2L-KO cells were complemented by transduction with pCSIB-derived empty vector (control) or vectors expressing full-length or mutant forms of ATXN2L71, followed by selection with 10 μg ml−1 blasticidin. HeLa-cells expressing doxycycline-inducible BirA-μNS and GFP-μNS were engineered by transduction with LKO-derived lentiviral vector72, followed by selection with 1 μg ml−1 puromycin (InvivoGen) for one week.

Viruses

Recombinant reovirus strain T3D was recovered using plasmid-based reverse genetics73 and purified74. Reovirus titers were quantified by plaque assay using L-cell monolayers74. CHIKV stocks were propagated from full-length WT infectious cDNA clones75. Plasmids encoding CHIKV cDNAs were linearized by digestion with NotI-HF (NEB). Capped, full-length CHIKV RNAs were transcribed in vitro using mMessage mMachine SP6 transcription kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and introduced into BHK-21 cells by electroporation using a Gene Pulser electroporator (Bio-Rad). Culture supernatants were harvested 24 h after electroporation, clarified by centrifugation at 855×g for 10 min, and stored in aliquots at −80 °C.

Plasmid construction

To engineer plasmid encoding doxycycline-inducible BirA-μNS, T3D μNS cDNA was amplified using PCR from pcDNA3.1-μNS19 and cloned into a mycBioID2-13XLinker-MCS (Addgene) plasmid using restriction enzymes XhoI/KpnI (NEB). The resulting plasmid was subcloned into pLKO72 using restriction enzymes NheI/NotI (NEB). To engineer plasmid encoding doxycycline-inducible GFP-μNS, the moxGFP-μNS sequence from pCI-moxGFP-μNS65 was cloned into the pLKO vector using restriction enzymes NheI/NotI.

To engineer a CRISPR construct encoding sgRNA targeting human ATXN2L (pLentiCRISPRv2-ATXN2LsgRNA), pLentiCRISPRv2-Blast (Addgene) was amplified using PCR (Supplementary Table 1) to generate linear fragments encoding ATXN2L-targeting sgRNA sequence 5′-CCCTCGTCGGGAGGACATTG-3′ from the human GeCKO v2 library76 and ligated into pLentiCRISPRv2-Blast using a NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly kit (NEB).

To engineer pcDNA3.1-ATXN2L, pcDNA-ATXN2L248-3189 was amplified by PCR using specific primers (Supplementary Table 1) to generate a linear fragment, encoding nucleotides 248-3189 of ATXN2L amplified from pOTB7-ATXN2L (Horizon Discovery MHS6278-202833076, Accession: BC082760) and ligated into pcDNA3.1 using HiFi Assembly. ATXN2L nucleotides 1-247 were synthesized as a gBlock fragment (Integrated DNA Technologies) (Supplementary Table 2) and subcloned into pcDNA-ATXN2L248-3189 using restriction enzymes NheI/KpnI to yield pcDNA3.1-ATXN2L. To engineer pcDNA3.1-ATXN2L ΔLSm, ΔLSmAD, and ΔPAM2, specific primers (Supplementary Table 1) were used to amplify linear fragments using pcDNA3.1-ATXN2L as template. HiFi Assembly was used to ligate the fragments encoding each mutant. WT and mutant forms of ATXN2L were subcloned into the lentivirus pCSIB vector71 using PCR amplification and HiFi Assembly.

To engineer pcDNA3.1-HA-μNS, specific primers (Supplementary Table 1) were phosphorylated using T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB) and used to amplify linear fragments using pcDNA3.1-μNS as a template. Linear fragments were ligated using T4 DNA ligase (NEB).

To engineer nanoluciferase constructs for in vitro transcription, a unique BspQI restriction site was introduced using PCR amplification of pT7-S4T3D (Addgene) to obtain plasmid pT7-S4-p(A)0. An existing BspQI restriction site located 5′ to the T7 promoter sequence of pT7-S4T3D was removed by replacing the nucleotide sequence spanning PciI-NsiI restriction enzyme sites with a synthesized gBlock fragment containing an ablated BspQI site (Integrated DNA Technologies) (Supplementary Table 2). This fragment was cloned into pT7-S4T3D using restriction enzymes PciI-NsiI (NEB) to obtain pT7-Δ5′BspQI-S4T3D. To engineer a nanoluciferase-encoding construct flanked by the S4 5′UTR and terminal 3′ 200 nucleotides, pT7-S4-p(A)0 and CHIKV181/25-nLuc77 plasmids were used as templates for PCR amplification and ligated using HiFi Assembly to obtain pT7-S4-nLuc-p(A)-. To engineer sequences encoding a poly(A) tail 3′ the S4 3′UTR, pT7-S4-nLuc-p(A)- and pFFLuc-IVT (Addgene) were used as templates for PCR amplification and ligated using HiFi Assembly to obtain plasmid pT7-S4-nLuc-p(A)+. Plasmids used in the study are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Lentivirus transduction

Lentiviruses for transduction were prepared by co-transfection of Lenti-X 293T cells (Takara Bio) in T25 tissue-culture flasks with 8.7 μg lentiviral vector, 1.1 μg VSV-G expression plasmid (Addgene), and 10.9 μg psPAX2 lentiviral packaging plasmid (Addgene) using TransIT-LT1 transfection reagent (Mirus Bio). Cell supernatants were harvested at 72 h post-transfection and filtered using a 0.45-μm filter. WT and derivative HeLa cells (106 cells) were centrifuged to form pellets, resuspended in a mixture of 2 ml lentivirus, 2 ml complete DMEM, and polybrene (15 μg ml−1 final concentration), and cultivated in T25 tissue-culture flasks. Following incubation at 37 °C overnight, the transduction medium was removed and replaced with fresh medium. At 48 h post-transduction, cells were selected with 10 μg ml−1 blasticidin for pCSIB-derived lentiviral vectors and 1 μg ml−1 puromycin for pLKO-derived lentiviral vectors.

BioID screen

HeLa-pLKO-BirA-μNS cells were adsorbed with 500 PFU cell−1 of reovirus at room temperature for 1 h. The inoculum was removed and replaced with DMEM supplemented with doxycycline (Sigma) (50 ng ml−1 final concentration). Cells were also not induced with doxycycline in parallel to control for naturally biotinylated proteins. At 6 h post-induction, the medium was supplemented with biotin (Sigma) (50 μM final concentration) for 18 h. Biotinylated proteins were affinity-purified as described30 with several modifications. Cells were washed twice with PBS, pelleted, and resuspended in 600 μL of BioID lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 0.2% sodium dodecyl sulfate [SDS], 1 mM phenylmethylsulphonyl fluoride [PMSF; Sigma], 1 mM dithiothreitol [DTT], protease inhibitor (Roche), and supplemented with 5% Triton X-100). Lysates were sonicated twice using a VirSonic 100 probe at an output level of 3 with 30 pulses each. Tris-HCl, pH 7.4 (22.5 mM final concentration) was added to each sample and sonicated with 30 pulses at an output level of 3. Cell debris was collected by centrifugation at 16,500×g at 4 °C for 10 min, supernatants were incubated with streptavidin-coated Dynabeads (Invitrogen), and samples were incubated at 4 °C overnight on an end-to-end rotator. Beads were washed twice with Wash Buffer 1 (2% SDS), once with Wash Buffer 2 (0.1% deoxycholic acid [DOC; DIFCO], 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA, 500 mM NaCl, and 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5), once with Wash Buffer 3 (0.5% DOC, 0.5% NP40, 1 mM EDTA, 250 mM LiCl, and 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4), and twice with 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4. Beads were resuspended in Laemmli sample buffer with 10 mM EDTA and incubated at 95 °C for 10 min. Eluted proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE.

Mass spectrometry

Affinity-purified proteins were digested with trypsin, and tryptic peptides were analyzed by nanoflow liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (nLC-MS/MS) using a NanoAcquity UPLC (Waters Corporation) interfaced with a Thermo LTQ Velos Pro Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For each analysis, a 1 µL volume of protein digest was injected onto a C18 column (PicoChip™column packed with Reprosil C18 3 μm 120 Å chromatography media in a 10.5 cm long, 75 μm ID column with a 15 μm tip, New Objective, Inc.) and then eluted off to the mass spectrometer using a 37-min linear gradient of 3–35% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The Velos Pro was operated in positive ionization mode with a spray voltage of 1.95 kV and capillary temperature of 275 °C. Acquisition consisted of cycles of one full-scan MS1 (automatic gain control [AGC] of 3 × 104, 75 ms maximum ion accumulation time, and m/z range of 375–1800) followed by eight MS/MS spectra recorded sequentially for the most abundant ions in the ion trap (minimum signal required 1000 counts, 1 × 104 AGC target, 100 ms maximum injection time, isolation width 2 m/z, normalized collision energy 35, and activation time 10 ms). Dynamic exclusion (30 s) was enabled to minimize redundant selection of peptides previously selected for MS/MS.

The tandem mass spectra (MS/MS) were analyzed using the MASCOT v2.4.0 (Matrix Science) search engine, and identified peptides and proteins were further statistically validated with Scaffold® v4.11.0 (Proteome Software) software using the following parameters to narrow proteins of interest to those specifically biotinylated in BirA-μNS-expressing cells: protein threshold of 99.0%, minimum of two peptides, and 90% peptide threshold. Furthermore, only proteins with a probability of greater than 95% accurate identification were included in subsequent analysis. Three naturally biotinylated carboxylases (pyruvate carboxylase, methylcrotonoyl-CoA carboxylase subunit alpha, and propionyl-CoA carboxylase alpha chain)78, common mass spectrometry contaminants such as keratin79, and all biotinylated proteins expressed in control uninduced HeLa-BirA-μNS cells were excluded. Spectral count ratios ≥2 for candidates identified from BirA-μNS cells relative to control cells were scored as positive. Following application of these filters, unique protein candidates identified in two biological replicates were subjected to STRING network analysis33 and MCL network clustering with an inflation parameter of 2.

Assessment of ATXN2L requirement for CHIKV replication

WT, KO, or KO+ HeLa cells were adsorbed with CHIKV strain 181/25 at an MOI of 1 PFU cell−1 at 37 °C for 1 h. The inoculum was removed, cells were washed once with PBS, and complete medium was added. After incubation at 37 °C for various intervals, 10% of the culture supernatant was collected and replaced with fresh medium. Viral titers were determined by plaque assay using Vero cells.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), permeabilized using 1% Triton X-100, and blocked with PBS-BGT (0.5% bovine serum albumin [BSA; RPI], 0.1% glycine, and 0.05% Tween 20 in PBS). Cells were incubated with primary antibody (Supplementary Table 4), incubated with species-specific Alexa Fluor 488- or 647-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen), and counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Invitrogen) to label nuclei. Confocal micrographs were captured using a Leica SP8 laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a 63X oil objective. Images were processed using Leica LAS X software. Areas corresponding to viral factories were quantified using the ROI tool in Icy software.

For reovirus infectivity, cells were fixed with methanol at −20 °C, washed twice with PBS, and blocked with 5% BSA in PBS. Cells were incubated with rabbit reovirus-specific antiserum followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 488 anti-rabbit secondary antibody, and counterstained with DAPI. Immunofluorescence images were captured using a Lionheart FX automated microscope (BioTek) equipped with a 20X air objective. Images were processed, and signals were quantified using Gen5 software (BioTek).

Immunoblotting

Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE using Mini-Protean TGX precast gels (Bio-Rad) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad) using a Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked using 5% BSA in tris-buffered saline (TBS) and incubated overnight with primary antibody (Supplementary Table 4) diluted in TBS supplemented to contain 0.1% Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich) (TBS-T) at 4 °C. Membranes were washed with TBS-T and incubated with species-specific secondary antibodies conjugated with IRDye800CW or IRDye680RD (LI-COR) diluted 1:7500 in TBS-T. Immunoblots were visualized using an Odyssey CLx imager (LI-COR).

Co-immunoprecipitation

WT or ATXN2L-KO HeLa cells complemented with ATXN2L domain deletion mutants were adsorbed with 100 PFU cell−1 of reovirus at RT for 1 h. The inoculum was removed and replaced with complete DMEM, and cells were incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Cells were lysed with Co-IP lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 137 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP40, and 2 mM EDTA) at 4 °C for 30 min using an end-to-end rotator. Cell lysates were passed through a 25-gauge needle five times, and debris was collected by centrifugation at 17,000×g for 10 min. Clarified lysates were incubated with 2 μg ATXN2L-specific antiserum (Proteintech) or 2 μg rabbit IgG isotype antibody (Invitrogen) conjugated to Protein G Dynabeads (Thermo). ATXN2L interactions with μNS and eIF4G isoforms were assessed using SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting with ATXN2L-specific antiserum (Proteintech), μNS-specific antiserum, anti-eIF4G1 rabbit polyclonal antiserum (Proteintech), and anti-eIF4G3 rabbit antiserum (Invitrogen). Signal intensities were determined using ImageJ, and μNS abundance in ATXN2L immunoprecipitants was calculated relative to input signal.

For experiments to identify μNS sequences required for ATXN2L interactions, plasmids encoding full-length HA-μNS or truncation mutants were co-transfected with ATXN2L plasmid into 293T Lenti-X cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Cell lysates were immunoprecipitated using HA-specific monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology), and immunoblotted using μNS-specific antiserum and ATXN2L-specific polyclonal antibody. ATXN2L signal intensity was conducted using ImageJ, and fold enrichment was calculated as the ratio of anti-HA to isotype control, normalized to input.

HeLa-BirA-μNS cells were induced with doxycycline (50 ng ml−1 final concentration) for 24 h prior to cell lysis and immunoprecipitation of BirA-μNS. BirA-μNS interactions with ATXN2L were assessed using immunoprecipitation with 2 μg myc-specific mouse monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology) or 2 μg mouse IgG2a isotype antibody (BioLegend) conjugated to Protein G Dynabeads, followed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting using ATXN2L-specific antiserum and μNS-specific antiserum.

Viral RNA quantification

WT, KO, and KO+ HeLa cells were adsorbed with 10 PFU cell−1 of reovirus and incubated at 37 °C. Cells were lysed at various intervals using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and RNA was purified using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Viral S480 and human ACTB (Thermo Fisher Scientific) Taqman primer and probe sets were used to amplify cDNA using a qScript XLT 1-Step RT-qPCR ToughMix kit (Quanta Bio). cDNA was quantified using a ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Reovirus S4 levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method relative to human ACTB RNA.

For RNA-immunoprecipitation RT-qPCR experiments, WT, KO, and ATXN2L deletion mutant-expressing HeLa cells were adsorbed with 100 PFU cell−1 of reovirus and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Cell lysates were prepared and incubated with 2 μg ATXN2L-specific antiserum at 4 °C for 2 h. Antibody-bound lysate was incubated with Protein G Dynabeads at 4 °C for 1 h. Fold enrichment was calculated as described81. The ΔCt of S4 RNA and FAM199X RNA was calculated by subtracting the Ct value of the co-immunoprecipitation input samples and the dilution factor of input from the Ct value of ATXN2L-associated RNA. ΔΔCt was calculated as the difference between the ΔCt of the immunoprecipitated samples and the isotype control samples. Fold enrichment was calculated as a linear transformation of the ΔΔCt and normalized to WT.

Biotinylated RNA pulldown

HeLa cells were adsorbed with reovirus at 100 PFU cell−1 and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Polysome profiling

WT and KO HeLa cells were adsorbed with 100 PFU cell−1 of reovirus at RT for 1 h. The inoculum from virus-adsorbed cells was replaced with DMEM supplemented to contain 10% FBS and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Cells were incubated with 5 mg/mL cycloheximide at 37 °C for 2 min, washed with ice-cold PBS supplemented to contain 0.1 mg mL−1 cycloheximide, and lysed in polysome lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 7.5, 140 mM KCl, 12 mM MgCl2, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5 mM DTT, and 100 μg μL−1 cycloheximide) supplemented with protease inhibitor (Roche), 0.2 U uL−1 SUPERase-In (Invitrogen), and murine RNase inhibitor (NEB). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 20,000×g at 4 °C for 10 min. A BioComp Gradient Station density gradient fractionator was used to fractionate and analyze gradients with continuous monitoring at A260 using a TRIAX flow cell (BioComp Inc.). Nine fractions along the polysome gradient were collected for subsequent analysis of RNA and proteins.

RNA from each fraction of reovirus-infected WT and KO HeLa cells was purified by phenol-chloroform extraction and precipitated in isopropanol in the presence of GlycoBlue Co-precipitant (Invitrogen) and 500 pg EGFP RNA to normalize for variation in purification efficiency. Purified RNAs were used as templates for cDNA synthesis using Superscript IV Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen), and relative levels of viral s4 and human ACTB were evaluated using RT-qPCR. Fold change of RNA in each fraction was calculated using the ΔΔCt method.

Proteins from each fraction of mock and reovirus-infected WT HeLa cells were precipitated in ethanol in the presence of GlycoBlue Co-precipitant. Proteins were resolved using SDS-PAGE and immunoblotted using ATXN2L-specific antibody and RPL17-specific antibody (R&D Systems).

σ3 expression assay

WT, KO, and KO+ HeLa cells were adsorbed with 10 PFU cell−1 of reovirus or transfected with pcDNA3.1-σ3 using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) and incubated at 37 °C. After incubation for various intervals, cells were lysed, and protein in cell lysates was resolved by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotted using σ3-specific monoclonal antibody 4F230 and actin-specific monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology). Signal intensities for σ3 and actin were quantified using ImageJ, and σ3 protein levels were calculated using the ratio of σ3 intensity to actin intensity.

Assessment of ATXN2L requirement in nonpolyadenylated mRNA translation

pT7-s4-nLuc-poly(A)- and pT7-s4-nLuc-poly(A)+ plasmids were linearized using restriction enzyme BspQI at 50 °C for 2 h and used as templates for in vitro transcription with a HiScribe T7 ARCA mRNA kit (NEB). Transcribed RNA was purified using a Monarch RNA Cleanup kit (NEB). Molecular weights of RNAs were verified by denaturing gel electrophoresis.

WT, KO, and KO+ HeLa cells were adsorbed with 100 PFU cell−1 of reovirus and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Cells were transfected with 50 ng of s4-nLuc-poly(A)- or s4-nLuc-poly(A) + RNA using Lipofectamine MessengerMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Luciferase activity was quantified using a Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay (Promega). Luciferase reactions were diluted 8-fold in complete DMEM to obtain a signal in the linear range. Luminescence was assessed using a Synergy H1 (BioTek) microplate reader.

Sequence logo of reovirus 3′UTR termini

RNA sequences from 21 mammalian orthoreovirus isolates were obtained from NCBI using Entrez Direct. Partial sequences and coding region sequences were removed. A sequence logo was derived from the terminal 15 nucleotides of the remaining sequences using the ggseqlogo package in R82.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with a minimum of three independent replicates. Data were presented as the mean ± s.d. unless otherwise indicated. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests were used with an α = 0.05. Where indicated, ordinary one-way and two-way ANOVA analyses were conducted with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test (α = 0.05), Sidak’s multiple comparisons test (α = 0.05), or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test (α = 0.05). For all violin plots, upper and lower lines indicate the 25th and 75th percentiles, respectively, and the center denotes the median value. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 data analysis software and reported in Supplementary Table 5.
