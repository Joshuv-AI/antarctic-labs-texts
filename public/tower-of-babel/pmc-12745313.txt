The Peripheral Epigenome Predicts White Matter Volume Contingent on Developmental Stage: An ECHO Study
Authors: Taena Hanson, Sophie Spencer, Samantha A Harker, Fatoumata Barry, Jennifer Beauchemin, B Blair Braden, Phoebe Burton, Viren D’sa, Daphne Koinis-Mitchell, Sarah E Mennenga, Sean C L Deoni, Candace R Lewis, on behalf of program collaborators for Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhite matter (WM) development is crucial for efficient neural connectivity, and disruptions in its maturation are associated with neurocognitive and psychiatric disorders. Emerging evidence suggests that epigenetic mechanisms, particularly DNA methylation (DNAm), regulate WM structure, yet little is known about these relationships in pediatric populations. Using data from the Environmental Influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) study, we investigated the association between peripheral DNAm of WM-related genes and WM volume (WMV) across development. Salivary DNAm profiles were obtained from 205 children (2 months–15.5 years) using the Infinium MethylationEPIC array, and T1-weighted MRI was used to quantify WMV. A targeted-epigenome analysis identified 1867 CpG sites across 352 genes significantly predicting WMV (FDR q < 0.05). Notably, 11,611 CpG sites exhibited age-dependent associations, with effects strongest in early childhood. Further, DNAm in saliva significantly correlated with DNAm in brain tissue (r = 0.915). These findings suggest that peripheral epigenetic markers may serve as biomarkers of neurodevelopmental trajectories, with potential applications in early detection of WM-related disorders. Our study highlights the importance of developmental timing in DNAm-WMV associations and underscores the utility of saliva-based DNAm in pediatric neuroimaging research. The formation and maintenance of white matter (WM) is crucial for neural efficiency, and disruptions in its development can have lasting neurocognitive and psychiatric implications [1]. WM volume (WMV) increases markedly in early childhood and then levels off but remains plastic throughout life [2]. Oligodendrocytes are glial cells responsible for the formation of the sheath partially comprising WM, which insulates axons to support efficient neuronal communication. WM degradation, as measured by MRI hyperintensities, is associated with stressful life events, early life socioeconomic status (SES), sexual assault, psychiatric and neurodegenerative diseases, and cognitive decline in aging [3–10]. Growing evidence suggests that epigenetic mechanisms play a key role in regulating WM development and maintenance in response to environmental influences [11, 12].

Epigenetics encompasses biological mechanisms, such as histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, regulatory RNAs, and DNA methylation (DNAm), that regulate gene expression without altering the underlying nucleotide sequence [13]. Most human studies linking DNAm to behavior or brain-based disorders rely on peripheral samples, including blood, saliva, and buccal cells [14]. Furthermore, monozygotic twin studies highlight the utility of peripheral DNAm for assessing early-life exposures, mental health, and cognitive outcomes in childhood while controlling for genetic influences, reinforcing its relevance as a biomarker for environmental impacts on brain-related processes [15–18]. Blood and brain DNAm exhibit high global correlations; however, individual CpG site correlations are more variable, depending on genomic context and regulatory function [19–22]. Importantly, two studies have reported that saliva may serve as a better proxy for brain DNAm than blood, with saliva–brain correlations exceeding those of blood–brain [23, 24]. However, because brain DNAm can only be extracted in rare cases (such as epileptic surgeries) or post-mortem, there is a significant interest in understanding whether peripheral epigenetic markers reflect brain structure [12, 25].

Studies investigating the link between white matter and peripheral epigenetics have revealed strong associations, primarily in clinical adult or infant populations. In adult major depressive disorder (MDD) populations, DNAm of BDNF (N = 77) and SLC6A4 (N = 96) correlates with reduced fractional anisotropy (FA) in key white matter tracts such as the corpus callosum and corona radiata [26, 27]. Similarly, DNAm profiles are associated with white matter alterations and cognitive (N = 80) and psychopathological symptoms (N = 39) in first-episode schizophrenia [28, 29]. Longitudinal research, such as the Whitehall II imaging study, has identified differentially methylated regions associated with cognitive impairment and found that accelerated epigenetic aging was linked to measures of white matter integrity (N = 48) [30]. Collectively, these findings highlight strong associations between peripheral epigenetic markers, white matter structure, and mental health in adults.

Stressors during the perinatal period can induce widespread DNAm changes, which correlate with reduced WM connectivity and atypical brain maturation. In preterm infants, differential DNAm has been linked to altered white matter connectivity and microstructure, highlighting potential epigenetic mechanisms underlying neurodevelopmental vulnerabilities (N = 72; N = 258) [31, 32]. Further, such associations can be moderated by genetic variations [33].

Infants born to mothers with obesity display reduced fractional anisotropy (FA) in multiple WM regions, accompanied by alterations in DNAm at 68 CpG sites (N = 14) representing genes critical to neurodevelopment [34]. Similarly, maternal depression and anxiety correlate with epigenetic changes detectable in umbilical cord blood (N = 52), which are linked to sex-specific differences in WM structure at 1 month of age [35]. Together, these studies indicate that peripheral DNAm markers may serve as biomarkers for WM development and reveal how early life stress can shape neurodevelopmental trajectories through epigenetic pathways.

A recent 2023 review reported that only 21% of neuroimaging epigenetic studies explored developmental cohorts, and only 5% in childhood [12]. Because DNAm is dynamic throughout life, especially in the early years, associations with the brain may differ across developmental stages [36, 37]. As such, studying DNAm associations with WM during development provides a unique opportunity to assess dynamic peripheral epigenetic correlates that may change over time and provide valuable biological insights for neurodevelopment. Most infant DNAm studies rely on perinatal tissues, such as cord blood or placenta, which are only accessible at birth, limiting longitudinal assessments. Using saliva as a scalable and noninvasive alternative allows for tracking DNAm changes beyond the perinatal period, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of how epigenetic markers relate to white matter development across childhood.

Rather than exploring the potential relationship between WMV and DNAm of a few specific candidate genes or a broad epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) that includes irrelevant genes not expressed in WM brain tissue, we employed a targeted-epigenome approach. We extracted all CpG sites on, or near, 441 genes previously associated with WM microstructure from a GWAS of 43,802 individuals [38]. This approach improves upon both classical analytical approaches by expanding from the limitations of candidate gene models and reducing the scope of our analyses from EWAS to improve statistical power. The current study extends the field of neuroimaging epigenetics by examining the following: (1) the relationship between DNAm of WM-related genes and WMV in a healthy pediatric population, (2) if the relationship between DNAm of WM-related genes and WMV differs across development, and (3) correlations between WM-related gene DNAm measured in saliva and brain tissue. We hypothesize that DNAm of WM-related genes measured from saliva samples will predict WMV in a healthy pediatric population, dependent on age.

Methods

Parent Study

This study was based on a subset of participants prospectively followed as part of the Environmental Influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) Program. MRI data from this cohort has been used in prior studies [39–41]. ECHO is a consortium of 69 established pediatric cohort studies collecting new data under a common protocol since 2019 [42], with the primary aim of studying the effects of early-life environmental exposures on child health. The current study included one ECHO cohort in the analyses. Single and cohort-specific Institutional Review Boards monitored human subject activities and the centralized ECHO Data Analysis Center. All participants provided written, informed consent.

Eligibility criteria for the parent study included: mothers > 18 years old, term gestation of 37–41 weeks, healthy singleton pregnancy, no evidence of uncontrolled medical conditions (i.e., hypertension, pre-eclampsia, uncontrolled diabetes) or medical conditions that could potentially impact the safety of a participant during a study visit, no history of major psychiatric illness, English speaking, consent to baby brain imaging, and the longitudinal nature of the study; infants had no significant congenital anomalies; and infants had no history of neurological trauma or disorder (e.g., epilepsy). The inclusion criteria for the subset used in this study included participants who provided a saliva sample and an MRI scan within 365 days (Mdays_between = 3.54; SDdays_between = 5.23).

Demographics

Demographic information was collected by parent reports. The study sample included youth (N = 205; females = 90; age range 2 months–15.5 years; Mage = 5.68, SDage = 3.69]. Age distribution across 2-year blocks is as follows: ages 0–1 (n = 45), 2–3 (n = 23), 4–5 (n = 45), 6–7 (n = 37), 8–9 (n = 26), and over 10 (n = 29). Sample demographics are summarized in Table 1. A flowchart on how the analytic sample was derived can be found in Fig. 1. SES was calculated using the Hollingshead Four Factor Index of Social Status [43]. Maternal and paternal education scores were multiplied by three and averaged. This led to weighted average SES scores for each participant.

Saliva Collection and DNA Isolation

Saliva was collected from participants in the lab or at home using Oragene (DNA Genotek, Ottawa, Ontario, Canada) saliva collection kits. DNA was extracted with a standard isolation kit (DNA Genotek’s PT-L2P-5). Sample yield and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically using NanoDrop ND-1000 methods (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE).

DNA Methylation

The DNA was bisulfite treated with the EZ-96 DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research) according to Illumina’s recommended deamination protocol. Genomic annotations and probes for the EPICv1 array were acquired through the support website of Illumina Inc. (https://support.illumina.com/). The Illumina Infinium MethylationEPICv1 array BeadChip (850 K) was carried out at either the home institution (n = 151) or by the Epigenomic Services from Diagenode (n = 271; Cat nr. G02090000). Batch and array were used as covariates in all analyses. Illumina describes that the location of the CpG is relative to the CpG island with the sequence of annotation: shores being 0–2 kb from islands, shelfs being 2–4 kb from islands, and open sea regions isolating CpG sites in the genome that do not have a specific designation. North refers to upstream (5′) of the CpG island and south refers to downstream (3′) of the CpG island.

Genotype Array and Data Preprocessing

Genotyping was conducted using a Multi-Ethnic Global Array (MEGA, > 1.7 million markers) on an Illumina iScanSystem at the Translational Genomic Research Institute in Phoenix, AZ. PLINK v1.9 [44] was used to conduct data preprocessing and quality control. Thresholds were applied for minor allele frequency (MAF, ≥ 5%), Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (p ≥ 1.0 × 105), SNP genotyping rate (≥ 95%), and sample genotyping rate (≥ 95%). Relatedness detection and sex mismatch analyses were conducted. Principal component analyses (PCA) were conducted to remove outliers. The TOPMed Imputation Server utilized VCF files for imputation. PLINK v1.9 was also used to calculate the first ten principal components and corresponding eigenvalues using default minor allele frequency (--maf) and call rate (--geno) filters. The eigenvalue scree plot confirmed that the first three ancestry principal components provided the majority of variance and therefore were tested for subsequent model fitting analyses.

DNA Methylation Data Preprocessing

Raw intensity data (IDAT) files were preprocessed using the minfi package (1.52.1) in R (4.3.2). Detection p-values were computed for each probe to assess signal quality, and samples with detection p-value > 0.01 were excluded [45]. Sample-wide quality control was further evaluated using median signal intensity distributions, with low-quality samples identified using the plotQC() function and a badSampleCutoff threshold of 10. Samples with median log2 intensity values below this threshold were excluded from further analysis. All samples demonstrated effective bisulfite conversion, as evidenced by high signal intensity in BISULFITE CONVERSION I and II control probes for both red and green channels, with no outliers. We excluded probes with fewer than 3 beads in more than 5% of samples, consistent with published thresholds [46]. Probes overlapping known SNPs, probes located on sex chromosomes, and cross-reactive probes were removed [47], as these can introduce technical variability unrelated to biological effects. To minimize technical variation across samples, quantile normalization was applied to adjust for differences in signal intensity distributions. Additionally, predicted sex based on methylation patterns was compared to reported sex to identify and exclude any mismatched samples. Because interindividual cell-type heterogeneity fluctuates with age and impacts pediatric research [48], we generated estimated cell proportions using the Middleton reference panel [49, 50], which was developed to estimate in whole saliva pediatric studies. Using the R package EpiDISH (Epigenetic Dissection of Intra-Sample Heterogeneity, 3.8) and the robust partial correlation (RPC) method, we estimated the proportion of epithelial cells per sample. Epithelial cell proportion was included as a covariate in all analyses. M-values were used for DNAm analysis as recommended by previous research [51].

MRI

Imaging was performed at the Lifespan facility at Brown University. All data was collected without sedation. For children under 4 years of age, MRI was performed during natural non-sedated sleep using previously described methods [52]. Older children were scanned awake while watching TV. Pediatric (MedVac) immobilizers were used along with foam cushions and padding to minimize motion. Image quality was monitored during and following scanning. If motion artifacts were observed (e.g., ghosting, blurring, or other signal variations), scans were repeated or removed from analysis. T1-weighted anatomical MRI data were acquired using a three-dimensional MP-RAGE sequence on a 3 T Siemens Trio scanner with the following parameters: echo time = 5.6 ms, repetition time = 1400 ms, inversion time = 950 ms, flip angle = 15°, and 1.1 × 1.1 × 1.1 mm resolution. Image matrix and field of view were adjusted by infant size to achieve the desired image resolution. Structural T1 images were processed using FreeSurfer software, utilizing infant FreeSurfer atlases for children less than 4 years old [53, 54]. FreeSurfer delineates 33 distinct brain regions per hemisphere, in addition to total WMV. Total white matter measurements were divided by 10,000 to adjust the scale of the variable and enable easier interpretation.

Statistics

Model Fits

To validate appropriate covariate inclusion in our models, we compared Akaike inclusion criteria (AIC) and adjusted R2 across models with several different covariate combinations. For this model comparison, we generated the first principal component derived from the top 100 most variable CpG sites within the sample. The first principal component (PC1) was utilized as the main independent variable in these model comparison analyses. Covariates tested include age at brain scan, sex, days between brain scan and genetic collection, epithelial cell type proportion, batch, array, the first three ancestry PCs, and SES.

White Matter Targeted-Epigenome Association Study

To assist with statistical power and engage a theory-driven approach in our analysis, we performed a targeted-epigenome-wide analysis testing only sites annotated to genes previously associated with white matter structure [38]. The referenced study conducted a genome-wide association analysis using diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) data from 43,802 individuals to identify common genetic variants influencing white matter microstructure. The researchers performed a multivariate genome-wide association study (GWAS) on 215 dMRI phenotypes. In the supplementary materials of this article, Table S17 presents significant gene-level associations identified through MAGMA (Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation). Specifically, the analysis revealed 2772 significant gene-level associations (p < 1.2 × 10−8, adjusted for 215 phenotypes) across 441 unique genes. After filtering the full EPIC data to only include probes on, or near, these 441 genes, as well as removing highly correlated sites (r > 0.85), we retained 24,412 CpG sites for this analysis.

Multivariate linear regression models were run separately for each CpG site with WMV as the outcome variable, with appropriate covariates determined by model fit analysis (see “Results”). Additionally, we investigated age and sex interaction terms in separate models to explore changes in DNAm-WMV associations across these relevant biological variables.

Saliva DNA Methylation Correlated with Brain DNA Methylation

DNAm levels of CpG sites annotated to WM-related genes were compared to the same CpG sites measured in brain tissue. Brain DNAm values were obtained from the Allen Brain Atlas BrainSpan data, a publicly available dataset based on the Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip. The BrainSpan dataset provided DNAm values (N = 177 samples from 16 individuals; Mage = 8.3, SDage = 10.0) from 16 brain regions per participant (11 cortex regions, hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, striatum, and cerebellum). To conduct correlation analyses, we calculated a DNAm value averaged across participants for each CpG site assessed in this study. DNAm values for brain data were averaged across participants and across brain regions. Separate correlation analyses were performed for different CpG location annotations, including islands, shores, shelves, and open seas.

The addition of all of our relevant covariates produced the best model fit even when penalized for additional parameters, as evidenced by the lowest AIC and higher adjusted R2 values. The exception to this was the addition of SES, which produced slightly higher AIC values and a similar adjusted R2 compared to a model with all other covariates included. Due to the established impact of SES on DNAm [55] and the close to negligible difference in model fit with the inclusion of SES, we chose to include SES in the final model. Thus, a final model including age at brain scan, sex, days between brain scan and genetic collection, epithelial cell type proportion, batch, array, the first three ancestry PCs, and SES as covariates was utilized in all further analyses.

Out of 24,412 CpG sites, 1,867 sites, annotated to 352 unique genes, passed multiple corrections (FDR q < 0.05) to significantly predict WMV (Fig. 2, Online Resource 1). Overall, promoter sites had the lowest percentage of significant predictors for WMV compared to other site locations. A breakdown of significant sites by location is provided in Table 3. CpG Site by Age Interactions

To further characterize age-related effects, we created a categorical age variable based on the well-established neuroimaging literature indicating rapid and consistent WM development during the first 3 years of life [1, 2], categorizing participants into two groups: younger (below age 3) and older (age 3 and above, Online Resource 3). Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the top 100 sites with a significant main effect (PC1main, 81.12% variance explained) and the top 100 sites with a significant age interaction (PC1inter, 88.65% variance explained). There was a significant interaction of PC1inter with this categorical age variable (β = 8.35, p = 9.84 × 10−8), with PC1inter significantly predicting WMV in the 0–3-year-olds (β = 17.23, p = 7.85 × 10−6) but not in 3–15-year-olds (β = 0.35, p = 0.89). Alternatively, there was no significant interaction between PC1main and categorical age (β = 0.08, p = 0.96). PC1main was a significant predictor of WMV in younger children (β = 9.63, p = 0.01) and in older children (β = 12.77, p = 7.42 × 10−5). Both models are visualized in Fig. 4.

Discussion

This study provides novel evidence that peripheral DNAm of WM-related genes is significantly associated with WMV throughout childhood, with developmental stage playing a critical moderating role. Specifically, our findings reveal that DNAm measured in saliva predicts WMV in younger children but not in older children for a subset of CpG sites, whereas other sites remain predictive across development. These results emphasize the importance of assessing DNAm at individual CpG sites rather than relying solely on global methylation measures, aligning with previous research demonstrating that site-specific DNAm patterns better capture functional epigenetic modifications relevant to brain structure and function [20, 21].

The association between DNAm and WMV has been previously explored in both clinical and developmental contexts, primarily in adults and infants. Prior research has linked peripheral DNAm to WM alterations in MDD, schizophrenia, and cognitive aging [26, 27, 29, 30]. While these studies have been instrumental in identifying epigenetic correlates of WMV in psychiatric conditions, our study is one of the first to examine these relationships across early childhood and adolescence in a non-clinical sample. Importantly, our findings suggest that peripheral epigenetic markers may hold promise for the early detection of individuals at risk for later psychiatric disorders or other WM-related conditions [56, 57]. For example, DNAm signatures of childhood trauma measured in blood can predict psychiatric disorders and other adverse health outcomes 17 years after exposure [57]. Alternatively, peripheral epigenetic markers have also been used for robust diagnostic risk prediction systems to classify anxiety disorders from healthy controls, resulting in an impressive area under the curve of 0.876 [58]. Given that WM health plays a crucial role in cognitive and emotional development [4], identifying epigenetic signatures associated with WMV during these formative years could provide valuable insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying neurodevelopmental trajectories and psychiatric vulnerability. Alternatively, WM-related peripheral epigenetic signatures may serve as a means to distinguish between psychiatric disorders, offering a potential tool for differential diagnosis and personalized intervention strategies.

In infancy, DNAm modifications have been linked to perinatal stressors that impact WM development. For example, studies have identified associations between preterm birth, maternal obesity, maternal depression, and DNAm signatures predictive of white matter alterations in newborns [31, 32, 34, 35]. Collectively, these findings highlight early-life sensitivity to environmental factors and epigenetic regulation, supporting our observation that WMV-DNAm associations are strongest in early childhood. The developmental specificity of our results also aligns with prior work showing that DNAm patterns change dynamically over time [59, 60] and may exhibit different functional relevance at different stages of neurodevelopment [37]. However, it is important to recognize that sensitivity to early-life stress extends beyond the perinatal period.

Adverse childhood experiences can lead to long-lasting epigenetic modifications, affecting stress-related genes and potentially contributing to various health outcomes [61, 62]. For example, lifetime trauma burden is associated with accelerated epigenetic age, which was also associated with cortical atrophy in brain areas associated with emotion regulation and threat regulation [63]. Since WM is also vulnerable to such stressors [10, 64], peripheral DNAm may serve as a valuable biomarker for understanding the impact of trauma on white matter health. Therefore, monitoring peripheral DNAm, especially in vulnerable populations such as children in state custody, could provide insights into how traumatic experiences influence WM across development. Identifying these epigenetic markers may also help determine individuals who could benefit from early interventions to mitigate long-term neurodevelopmental consequences [4].

Our results demonstrate that DNAm-WMV associations are most pronounced during early childhood, a period marked by rapid WM expansion and heightened sensitivity to environmental influences. This aligns with the broader literature indicating that epigenetic regulation of neural pathways is particularly dynamic in the first few years of life [65]. Given that WMV stabilizes in later childhood and adolescence, it is plausible that epigenetic associations decline as structural development reaches a plateau [1, 2]. An important contribution of our study is the integration of peripheral DNAm as a potential biomarker for WM structure, in addition to relating it to brain DNAm. Our analysis using publicly available brain DNAm data revealed that some sites with high cross-tissue DNAm correlations were also significantly associated with WMV, particularly in non-island regions. Such results support the notion that certain peripheral epigenetic markers may serve as proxies for brain methylation patterns and brain structure [19, 20]. This finding aligns with prior research showing that approximately 3% of CpG sites exhibit high correlation between matched prefrontal cortex and buccal cells [66]. However, while previous studies reported the strongest correlations in promoter regions, our results indicate the highest cross-tissue correlation across shore CpG sites [66].

A key strength of this study is the targeted-epigenome analyses, which balance the specificity of candidate gene studies with the breadth of EWAS by using a GWAS-informed approach. By leveraging a curated list of 441 WM-related genes identified in a large GWAS (N = 43,802) [38], we were able to focus on biologically relevant CpG sites while maintaining robust statistical power. Our results, showing that DNAm across 352 genes significantly predicted WMV and 433 genes exhibited age-dependent associations, underscore the extensive role of epigenetic regulation in white matter development and the potential of epigenetic biomarkers for WM development.

Despite these contributions, several limitations should be considered. While our pediatric sample is larger than many previous WM-epigenetic association studies, longitudinal designs are needed to track DNAm-WMV associations over time and establish causal relationships. While we accounted for key covariates such as ancestry and SES, additional environmental exposures (e.g., stress, nutrition, toxins) likely interact with DNAm to influence WM trajectories. Future studies should incorporate more granular environmental measures to better capture these influences. Further, because this is an association study, causality in either direction cannot be assumed. Since DNAm profiles are distinct across various peripheral samples, these results cannot generalize across blood or buccal swabs. Finally, while this is the first pediatric study to compare salivary and brain DNAm correlations for WM-associated genes, paired samples would further strengthen these findings and clarify the extent to which salivary-DNAm can serve as a proxy for central nervous system DNAm.

Our findings provide strong evidence that peripheral DNAm of WM-related genes is associated with WM development in early childhood, with distinct age-dependent effects. By demonstrating robust epigenetic associations with WMV, particularly in developmental windows characterized by rapid brain maturation, this study contributes to the growing field of neuroepigenetics and highlights the potential for epigenetic markers to inform future research on neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders. Future work should continue to refine our understanding of how environmental exposures interact with DNAm in peripheral biospecimens and their relation to brain structure and function.
