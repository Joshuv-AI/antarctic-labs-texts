High-accuracy photoacoustic gas sensor enabled by deep learning-based concentration inversion from full waveforms
Authors: Pengbo Chen, Mingyang Feng, Mu Liang, Qianwen Wang, Yingying Qiao, Mingqi Jiao, Lei Li, Chongxin Shan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In photoacoustic spectroscopy (PAS) gas sensors, existing concentration inversion methods typically rely on either a single scalar or a feature vector. In principle, the complete signal waveform contains richer concentration-related information, and its utilization is expected to further improve inversion accuracy, which remains unverified to date. To achieve this, this study proposes an end-to-end method based on a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) that directly maps the full second-harmonic (2f) waveform to gas concentration. Experimental validation with chloroform (CHCl3) showed strong predictive robustness under stochastic waveform perturbations, achieving a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.9999; among the noise-augmented evaluation samples, 95.95% exhibited absolute errors below 1 ppm, and 61.8% exhibited relative errors below 1%. This method autonomously extracts concentration information from the full waveform and establishes a complex nonlinear mapping to gas concentration. Furthermore, this framework may provide a strategy for concentration inversion in other gas sensors employing second-harmonic wavelength modulation spectroscopy (WMS-2f) detection.

Introduction

Chloroform (CHCl3) functions as a versatile organic solvent across multiple industries, driven by its high solubility and volatility. Specifically, this compound serves as a reaction medium in organic synthesis [1], enables active pharmaceutical ingredient extraction, facilitates precision cleaning in semiconductor manufacturing, and supports textile dyeing processes [2]. Nevertheless, CHCl3 exhibits documented toxicological hazards [3]. Acute exposure suppresses central nervous function, inducing dizziness or coma, whereas chronic inhalation correlates with hepatic injury and potential carcinogenicity. To satisfy OSHA Permissible Exposure Limit (PEL) standards, workplace CHCl3 concentrations must remain below 50 ppm [4]. Consequently, precise CHCl3 monitoring becomes essential for mitigating occupational hazards and maintaining regulatory compliance.

Trace CHCl3 monitoring primarily employs spectroscopic [5], chromatographic, and electrochemical techniques [6]. Among these, electrochemical methods suffer from poor selectivity and are easily affected by environmental interference [7], while chromatography is limited by complex equipment and long analysis cycles [8]. In contrast, spectroscopic techniques based on Beer–Lambert law [9], such as Tunable Diode Laser Absorption Spectroscopy (TDLAS) [10], [11], Quartz-enhanced photoacoustic spectroscopy (QEPAS) [12], [13], [14], Cavity Ring-Down Spectroscopy (CRDS) [15], Direct Absorption Spectroscopy (DAS) [16], and Photoacoustic Spectroscopy (PAS) [17], [18], offer significant advantages by measuring the absorption characteristics of gas molecules at their characteristic wavelengths to determine concentration. These techniques are highly selective, non-invasive, and capable of real-time monitoring [19], [20]. Specifically, PAS has become the most suitable method for CHCl3 gas detection due to its simple structure, fast response time, background-free detection, strong interference resistance, and high sensitivity [21], [22].

To fully capitalize on the inherent advantages of PAS, second-harmonic (2f) signals via wavelength modulation spectroscopy (WMS) enable quantitative gas analysis. Frequency modulation shifts the detection bandwidth to circumvent 1/f noise and suppress low-frequency baseline drifts. The 2f waveform originates from the nonlinear variation of the absorption coefficient during laser wavelength modulation, establishing a linear correlation between signal amplitude and gas concentration [23]. Because the 2f peak aligns with the molecular absorption maximum and maximizes the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), inversion algorithms routinely extract the peak amplitude, as shown in Fig. 1, to establish a linear relationship with gas concentration [24].

Despite its theoretical reliability, the practical accuracy of peak-based concentration inversion is compromised by optical disturbances, environmental fluctuations, and non-ideal system responses. Laser instabilities and the residual amplitude modulation (RAM) inherent to wavelength modulation introduce background interference at the detection frequency, thereby degrading the SNR [25]. Environmental fluctuations, primarily driven by temperature and pressure variations, perturb gas absorption line intensities and collisional broadening coefficients, ultimately distorting the absorption lineshape profiles [26], [27]. These variations simultaneously alter the acoustic-pressure distribution and thermal relaxation time within the photoacoustic cell, introducing baseline drift. Non-ideal system responses further distort the temporal waveform, as thermal boundary layer effects and nonlinear detector–amplifier characteristics generate phase shifts, asymmetric broadening, baseline drifts, and harmonic aliasing [28]. Consequently, the peak amplitude deviates from the true value, directly compromising the quantitative accuracy of concentration inversion.Fig. 1Two concentration inversion methods in PAS: using the full signal and using only the signal peak.Fig. 1

Current research on concentration inversion accuracy divides into system optimization and signal processing algorithm enhancement. System optimization suppresses noise and enhances signal fidelity via laser source upgrading [29], [30], photoacoustic cell resonance enhancement [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], high-sensitivity microphone integration [36], [37], and laser nonlinearity compensation [38]. In parallel, signal processing algorithms benefit from cost-effectiveness and robust generalization to enable digital real-time correction, attracting substantial research attention [39]. Based on the dimensionality of mapping signals to concentrations, existing methods can be classified into two primary paradigms, scalar and vector mapping. The first category frames concentration inversion as a scalar mapping, establishing a linear relationship between the 2f peak amplitude and gas concentration. To enhance the accuracy of this peak-based model, such studies prioritize noise suppression to restore signal fidelity. Current methods to enhance this scalar mapping rely on both SNR improvement and baseline correction. Techniques for suppressing noise include Kalman filtering [40], wavelet thresholding [41], variational mode decomposition (VMD) [42], and neural network-based denoising [43]. Techniques addressing baseline drifts range from 2f/1f normalization [44] to adaptive self-calibration via neural network fitting [45]. Although single-scalar concentration inversion is computationally straightforward and facilitates real-time processing, it discards the majority of spectral data, yielding fundamentally limited information utilization. The second category approaches inversion as a vector mapping, where concentration is retrieved from multi-dimensional features that can be either explicitly defined or implicitly extracted. Explicit features encompass directly measurable spectral attributes, including waveform profiles [46], integral areas [47], and multi-peak amplitudes [48]. Implicit features are derived through dimensionality reduction and feature extraction techniques, such as empirical mode decomposition (EMD) [49] and principal component analysis (PCA) [50]. Leveraging these features, the concentration inversion is extended from a single scalar to a finite-dimensional vector. According to WMS theory, the complete 2f waveform encodes concentration through a complex nonlinear mapping. However, current approaches inherently perform lossy compression on this full waveform, thereby discarding a wealth of hidden concentration features. It remains unclear whether using the complete waveform can further improve inversion accuracy. Specifically, extending concentration inversion from selected features to the entire waveform remains an ideal objective. Although this approach may theoretically maximize information utilization, its practical feasibility has yet to be demonstrated.

This paper proposes a full waveform-based concentration inversion method that utilizes a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) to achieve end-to-end gas concentration inversion. The proposed approach leverages the fact that the entire waveform contains informative features correlated with gas concentration, as shown in Fig. 1. To enhance feature representation, multi-scale convolution (Conv) layers were designed to capture hierarchical signal patterns. A residual structure was incorporated to promote feature reuse and stability, while an attention mechanism was integrated to adaptively focus on critical regions of the signal. These architectural components jointly improved the accuracy and robustness of concentration inversion. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method significantly improves detection precision and provides an effective solution for advancing the practical implementation of PAS-based sensing technologies.

Methods

Photoacoustic spectroscopy

PAS is a highly sensitive trace gas detection technique based on the photoacoustic effect and the Beer–Lambert law [51]. The operating principle involves a modulated laser beam at a specific wavelength passing through the target gas, where gas molecules absorb the optical energy. This absorbed energy undergoes non-radiative relaxation, converting to heat and producing periodic pressure fluctuations. These pressure waves are subsequently detected using high-sensitivity microphones or piezoelectric sensors.

Consider a detection system in which a gas absorption peak is located at wavelength λ0, with absorption cross-section σ(λ), concentration C, and optical path length L. In practice, the laser output wavelength consists of three main components: a sawtooth wave term λs(t) for scanning the absorption peak, a sinusoidal modulation term λmsin(ωmt), and higher-order terms introduced by laser nonlinearity. Therefore, the actual output wavelength can be expressed as shown in Eq. (1), where α1 and α2 are the nonlinear coefficients. In addition to the concentration dependence described above, the measured 2f waveform is affected by non-ideal characteristics of the practical sensing system. Nonlinear laser wavelength tuning causes the actual wavelength modulation to deviate from an ideal sinusoidal waveform and introduces mixing between different derivative components of the absorption line shape. Residual amplitude modulation produces additional background and harmonic components at the detection frequency. Moreover, baseline drift, environmental noise, and non-ideal responses of the microphone, lock-in amplifier, and acquisition chain may alter the amplitude, symmetry, sidelobe distribution, and baseline of the measured waveform. These factors do not necessarily generate intrinsic higher-order concentration terms, but they introduce additional waveform distortions and background variations. Consequently, a concentration-inversion method based solely on a single 2f peak amplitude and a strictly linear calibration may not fully account for the weak higher-order Beer–Lambert contributions and system-induced variations contained in the measured waveform, thereby limiting its inversion accuracy under practical measurement conditions.

Concentration inversion models

This study presents a gas concentration inversion network, which integrates multi-scale Conv layers [52], residual connections, and attention mechanisms for 2f signal analysis. As shown in Fig. 2, the input layer initially extracts 2f signal features, subsequently applies batch normalization (BN) to normalize the activations, and finally employs Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation functions to introduce nonlinearity. The network architecture is designed with the principle of deep feature extraction and optimization synergy, comprising three core modules: the multi-scale Conv layers, which enable multi-level signal representation; the residual module, which stabilizes the training of the deep network [53]; and the convolutional block attention module (CBAM) [54], which enhances the feature selection capability. Finally, the mapping of the 2f signal to concentration values is accomplished using a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) [55].

The selection of the multi-scale convolutional layers, residual connections, and attention mechanism was guided by the characteristics of the present concentration-inversion task. Each input is a fixed-length one-dimensional 2f waveform acquired over one wavelength-scanning cycle, while the output is a single gas concentration value. The concentration-related information is distributed across waveform characteristics at different scales, including local peak morphology, sidelobe variations, waveform width, and broader baseline or envelope variations. Therefore, parallel convolution kernels with different receptive-field sizes were employed to simultaneously extract local and relatively global features. This design provides a multi-resolution representation of the waveform features without requiring an excessive increase in network depth. Residual connections preserve the original waveform information during deep feature extraction and facilitate stable gradient propagation, whereas the attention mechanism adaptively emphasizes concentration-sensitive feature channels and sampling positions.

In the multi-scale Conv layers, parallel 1 × 3, 1 × 5, and 1 × 7 Conv kernels are used to capture signal features at different scales. The concentration-related information is nonuniformly distributed along the sampled 2f waveform, while practical system non-idealities and measurement noise introduce additional waveform distortions. By employing receptive fields of different sizes, this module effectively integrates both local features (e.g., signal peak details) and global trend features (e.g., baseline trends). The diversity of receptive fields enhances the network’s robustness to noise interference, enabling the extraction of deep features related to concentration from the signal.

The introduction of the residual module establishes a stable framework for deep network training. This structure incorporates an identity mapping between the original input and the Conv-transformed features, which mitigates the vanishing-gradient problem in deep networks by facilitating gradient flow, thus allowing the model to learn more complex concentration features. Simultaneously, this method preserves the original signal features while maintaining information integrity, thereby enhancing the model’s robustness.

The CBAM employs a channel–spatial attention mechanism for adaptive feature weighting. The channel attention layer learns feature channel weights to emphasize concentration-sensitive features, while the spatial attention layer identifies key regions within the signal that are strongly correlated with concentration. This dual attention mechanism optimizes both feature and spatial dimensions collaboratively, effectively suppressing environmental noise and baseline drift, thereby enhancing feature selection capability.

The MLP employs a progressive feature-compression strategy, using fully connected (FC) layers to map the extracted abstract features to gas concentration. Nonlinear activation functions are employed to learn the composite mapping between the measured waveform features and gas concentration, including the approximately linear concentration response, weak higher-order Beer–Lambert corrections, and waveform variations introduced by practical system non-idealities. Dropout layers with rates of 0.3 and 0.2 are inserted between successive FC layers to reduce overfitting and improve the generalization capability of the model. This process reduces feature redundancy while retaining concentration-relevant information. During data preprocessing, the concentration labels within the calibrated range of 0–100 ppm are normalized to [0, 1] using min–max normalization. The final FC layer generates a continuous scalar output, which is subsequently passed through a sigmoid activation function to constrain the normalized prediction to [0, 1]. The predicted concentration is then recovered through inverse Min–Max transformation. Because the model is trained only using samples within 0–100 ppm, predictions outside this calibrated range are not supported and may be unreliable.

Experimental system

Absorption line selection

Based on the analysis using the HITRAN database [56], as shown in Fig. 3, CHCl3 exhibits the strongest absorption peak at 1683.07 nm in the near-infrared region, at a temperature of 298 K and a nitrogen background pressure of 1 atm. Consequently, a laser operating at this wavelength is selected as the light source for this study. The laser is stabilized at an operating temperature of 20 °C, and its emission wavelength can be tuned continuously by adjusting the driving current in the range of 70–100 mA. This ensures that the laser wavelength spans the target absorption peak and remains within the stable operating range.

Experimental setup

The experimental setup for detecting CHCl3 gas concentration using PAS technology is shown in Fig. 4. The system utilizes a distributed feedback laser (NEL, NLK1U5EAAA) as the core light source, which is precisely controlled by a current controller (ILX Lightwave, LDX-3232) and a temperature controller (ILX Lightwave, LDX-5525B). The laser current controller was operated at a bias current of 70 mA. A positive-going sawtooth waveform with a frequency of 0.04 Hz, an amplitude setting of 2.2 V, and a DC offset of 1.1 V was used to scan across the CHCl3 absorption line. Meanwhile, a sinusoidal waveform with an amplitude setting of 9 V, zero DC offset, and a frequency of 710 Hz was superimposed on the scanning waveform for wavelength modulation. Since second-harmonic detection was employed, the corresponding 2f detection frequency was 1420 Hz, matching the resonant frequency of the photoacoustic cell. The laser output power was approximately 15 mW. During the experiment, the total gas flow rate was maintained at 100 sccm by mass flow controllers (INHA, MF-200DC), which adjusted the flow ratios to generate samples with specific concentrations. The photoacoustic signal, produced in the photoacoustic cell, was captured by a capacitive microphone (BSWA, MPA416) and demodulated in a phase-sensitive manner using a lock-in amplifier (Stanford Research Systems, SR830). The signal was then digitized by a high-speed data acquisition card (National Instruments, NI-9775) at a sampling rate of 1 kHz and sent to a computer for further processing.

Results and discussion

Dataset acquisition

This study generated 21 CHCl3 concentration levels ranging from 0 to 100 ppm at intervals of 5 ppm by adjusting the mass flow controllers (MFCs). The 2f signals obtained from the experimental system are shown in Fig. 5. At each concentration level, 48 experimentally measured 2f waveforms were recorded, with each waveform consisting of 25000 sampling points. Of these, 38 waveforms were assigned to the training set, while the remaining 10 waveforms were assigned to the validation set.

The detection performance of the experimental system was evaluated by performing linear regression between the 2f peak amplitude and gas concentration. As shown in Fig. 8(a), the linear fitting yielded a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.9987, demonstrating an excellent linear relationship between the signal amplitude and CHCl3 concentration over the range of 0–100 ppm. Concentration inversion

Following the dataset preparation procedure described above, the proposed model was trained on the augmented training set and evaluated on the test set. The proposed model was trained using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU with 12 GB of graphics memory. To accelerate the model’s convergence, min–max normalization was applied to scale the input signals to the range of [0, 1]. The log-cosh loss behaves approximately quadratically for small residuals and linearly for large residuals. It therefore provides smooth gradients while reducing sensitivity to large errors and outliers, facilitating stable model optimization. This ensures smooth convergence toward the optimal solution. As shown in Fig. 6, the model’s training process consists of distinct stages. In the early phase, the loss decreases sharply, indicating that the loss function effectively guides the model in capturing key features related to concentration. In the later phase, the loss stabilizes and reaches optimal performance after 775 epochs. To examine the reproducibility of the training process, the model was independently trained three times using different random initializations. The optimal checkpoints were obtained at epochs 751, 858, and 775, with corresponding validation losses of 6.05 × 10−7, 8.87 × 10−7, and 7.01 × 10−7, respectively. The loss curve in Fig. 6 and the subsequent inversion results correspond to the third run, whose validation loss was intermediate among the three runs, rather than to the run with the lowest validation loss.

To further verify the contribution of the main architectural components, module-ablation experiments were conducted under the same dataset partition and training settings. The multi-scale convolution blocks were replaced with 1 × 5 convolutional layers while preserving the corresponding input and output channel dimensions, whereas the residual blocks and CBAM modules were replaced with identity mappings. A plain CNN was further constructed by applying all three replacements simultaneously. As summarized in Table 1, the complete network achieved the best concentration-inversion performance. Replacing the multi-scale convolution blocks with single-scale convolutions caused a moderate reduction in accuracy, demonstrating the benefit of extracting waveform features over different receptive-field sizes. Removing the residual blocks produced a more pronounced performance degradation, indicating that residual connections facilitate feature preservation and effective deep-feature learning. Among the individual ablations, removing CBAM resulted in the largest prediction error, supporting its role in adaptively emphasizing concentration-relevant feature responses. The plain CNN also performed worse than the full model, confirming that the multi-scale convolution, residual learning, and attention-based feature weighting provide complementary contributions to the final prediction performance.

For the final evaluation, 10 additional 2f waveforms were independently acquired at each concentration level and were not used for model training or validation. These independently acquired signals were augmented with random noise to generate 100 noise-augmented evaluation samples per concentration level, thereby enabling assessment of the variability of repeated predictions under stochastic waveform perturbations and of the repeatability and robustness of the model predictions. The augmented waveforms were subsequently downsampled to 100 sampling points per waveform using the same preprocessing procedure. Both the proposed method and the peak-fitting model were evaluated using these noise-augmented evaluation samples. The resulting concentration-inversion results are shown in Fig. 7(a). The experiments demonstrate that the proposed method provides results closer to the true concentrations than the peak fitting model across all concentration levels. To further quantify the accuracy difference, Fig. 7(b) presents the absolute inversion errors for all data in the test set, visually highlighting the deviation of both methods from the true values. The model’s performance in gas concentration inversion was evaluated using the noise-augmented evaluation samples. As shown in Fig. 8(a), the average concentration predicted by the model is highly consistent with the true concentration, with an R2 of 0.9999. The inversion deviations across all concentration levels remain within a minimal range, indicating that the model can effectively suppress noise interference in the 2f signal and achieve high-precision concentration inversion. In contrast, the R2 of the peak fitting model is 0.9987, but it exhibits relatively larger deviations in the low concentration range. As shown in Fig. 8(b), within the concentration range of 0–25 ppm, the R2 for the proposed method remains stable, while the peak fitting model drops to 0.9693. At low concentrations, the attenuation of the 2f signal intensity leads to a significant reduction in peak amplitude. This results in fixed-amplitude environmental noise components – such as mechanical vibration, acoustic background, and electronic noise – becoming the dominant sources of interference. Because these noise components have approximately constant amplitudes, they have a greater impact on the weak signals, causing the peak region to lose its inherent noise-resistance advantage and leading to significant deviations in the concentration inversion results of the peak fitting model.Fig. 9Box plots of the inversion-error distributions across the noise-augmented evaluation samples at different concentrations. In the high-concentration range, the deviations between the mean values obtained by both concentration inversion methods and the true values are small, and the results are generally consistent. However, as shown in Fig. 7(b), the absolute error range of sample inversions increases with rising gas concentration. Fig. 9 illustrates the absolute-error distributions of the noise-augmented evaluation samples at different concentrations using box plots. The analysis reveals that the proposed method yields a smaller mean inversion error compared to the peak fitting model. Specifically, the interquartile range (IQR) of the errors for samples at each concentration is typically within 1 ppm. Although the error range of the proposed method increases with higher concentrations, the extent of this increase remains limited. In contrast, the error range of the peak fitting method increases significantly. This phenomenon can be attributed to the rise in 2f signal amplitude at higher gas concentrations, which intensifies noise components, such as shot noise and phase noise, that are proportional to the signal strength. By utilizing the full signal data for concentration inversion, the proposed method effectively suppresses the impact of these noise components. In contrast, the peak fitting model relies on a single peak point, and although the averaged results of multiple signals are close to the true value, the absolute peak error of individual samples increases more significantly with higher concentrations.

A statistical analysis of the absolute errors for the two inversion methods is presented in Fig. 10. At the augmented-sample level, 95.95% of the noise-augmented evaluation samples using the proposed method have absolute errors below 1 ppm, which is much higher than the 67.75% achieved by the peak-fitting model. In terms of relative error, 61.8% of these samples using the proposed method exhibit errors below 1%, compared to only 31.05% for the peak-fitting model. These results indicate that the proposed method achieves lower prediction errors and greater robustness than the peak-fitting model under the applied stochastic waveform perturbations. This difference occurs because the proposed method utilizes the complete 2f harmonic signal for concentration determination, whereas the peak-fitting model extracts information solely from individual peak characteristics.Fig. 10Comparison of the distributions of absolute and relative errors calculated from the noise-augmented evaluation samples.Fig. 10

Conclusion

Conventional concentration inversion methods are grouped into two classes of peak-based scalar and multi-feature vector paradigms, both of which apply lossy compression to the full 2f waveform. By utilizing the complete 2f waveform for concentration inversion, this study improves the accuracy of concentration estimation. Moreover, the results presented in Supplementary Material S1 provide preliminary evidence that incorporating sidelobe information contributes to further enhancing the inversion accuracy. To address the complex nonlinear mapping between the full waveform and gas concentration, alongside inevitable noise interference in measurements, an end-to-end 1D-CNN is employed. This method not only autonomously extracts concentration features but also exhibits robust nonlinear fitting and exceptional noise resilience. Across the full 0–100 ppm test range, the proposed method achieved an R2 of 0.9999. Moreover, at the augmented-sample level, 95.95% of the noise-augmented evaluation samples exhibited absolute errors below 1 ppm, and 61.8% of these samples exhibited relative errors below 1%. In the low-concentration range of 0–25 ppm, the proposed method maintained an R2 of 0.9996, whereas the R2 of the peak fitting method decreased to 0.9693. More broadly, since the proposed model performs concentration inversion using the complete WMS-2f waveform, its framework can potentially be adapted to other WMS-2f-based gas sensors.
