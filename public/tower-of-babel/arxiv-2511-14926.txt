Finite-Horizon LQR for General Markov Jump Linear Systems: Deterministic Reformulation and Reduced-Order Solution Thanks: The authors thank the Vice-Rectory for Research, Extension and Social Projection (VIEPS) of Universidad del Atlántico for support.
Alfredo R. R. Narváez, Jeinny Peralta, M. A. C. Candezano

Abstract
. This paper studies the Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) problem for continuous-time Markov Jump Linear Systems (MJLS) governed by general finite-state Markov chains that may include transient, absorbing, or non-communicating states. The problem, posed over a finite time horizon, is reformulated deterministically by expressing the cost functional in terms of a collection of second-moment matrices of the system state. A projection operator is introduced to restrict the analysis to the subspace of visited states, namely those with positive probability of being reached within the time horizon. The solution of the resulting deterministic problem is obtained from a reduced-order system of coupled matrix Riccati differential equations involving only the visited states, which define a quadratic value function satisfying a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman-type equation. The structure of this equation is formally justified in the matrix setting via the Riesz–Fréchet representation theorem, establishing a rigorous foundation for the deterministic reformulation and resolving an open aspect in previous literature. Several numerical examples, including cases with non-communicating states, validate the theoretical results and illustrate the practical relevance of the proposed generalization.

Introduction

It is common to observe that many natural phenomena undergo abrupt changes in their behavioral dynamics; that is, phenomena whose
modus operandi
changes at each instant and for which it is not possible to have prior knowledge of when or how these changes will occur, even though there may be some information about their behavior in each mode of operation. A phenomenon or process that exhibits this type of behavior is known in the literature as a stochastic process. Of course, it is also possible to fit such a process into a mathematical model describing another phenomenon, for example, via a system of differential equations, such that the dynamics of the model parameters are governed by the behavior of the stochastic process. According to the literature, the equations that describe such models are known as jump dynamical systems; and in particular, when the system evolves linearly in its state variable and the parameter jumps are governed by a Markov chain, the system is referred to as a Markov Jump Linear System (MJLS), which can be written as:

The problem addressed in this paper lies at the intersection of stochastic systems and applied optimal control. The formulation and analysis developed here are motivated not only by theoretical interest but also by the wide range of applications in engineering, economics, and systems subject to regime-switching dynamics. In this sense, the methods employed contribute to the broader field of applied mathematics and align with ongoing research efforts that seek to bridge stochastic modeling, control theory, and functional analysis. Given its relevance in numerous applications, optimal control theory has naturally extended to the framework of MJLS. However, since solving a deterministic problem is generally less complex than addressing its stochastic counterpart, and motivated by the need to relax the communicating states assumption, the purpose of this work is to reformulate the finite-horizon LQR problem with jumps using deterministic optimal control results, suitably adapted to a new state space of a different nature than that of the original system state.

(4)

\displaystyle 0=\min_{\mathbf{L}}\Big\{\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t,\mathbf{L}),\mathbf{X}\rangle+\partial_{t}J(t,\mathbf{X})+\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}J(t,\mathbf{X}),\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A+BL}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{X})\rangle\Big\},

Although the approach developed here follows the general line of reasoning in
[
16
]
, that earlier work did not provide a formal derivation of the HJB equation in terms of second-moment matrix collections. Instead, the structure of the HJB equation was assumed without rigorous justification, and the origin of such an equation in the Hilbert space setting remained unclear. In contrast, the present work makes two main contributions. First, we establish the mathematical foundation for the HJB formulation by invoking the Riesz–Fréchet representation theorem, which provides a rigorous justification for the form of the HJB equation in the context of Hilbert spaces of matrix-valued collections. This closes an important conceptual gap left open in
[
16
]
and enhances the theoretical soundness of the deterministic reformulation. Second, building upon the generalization initiated in
[
16
]
, we provide a comprehensive treatment of the LQR problem for arbitrary finite-state Markov chains by systematically introducing the projection operator
\mathcal{P}
and restricted operators that naturally handle non-visited states. This allows us to derive a reduced-order system of coupled Riccati equations involving only the
|{Z}|
visited states, rather than all
N
states, and to fully characterize the solution structure in this general setting.

This conceptual clarification, in turn, enables the formulation of a sufficiency verification theorem that establishes a fundamental connection between the HJB equation in (
4
) and the minimization of the original cost functional in (
2
). Specifically, under suitable regularity conditions, we show that if a continuously differentiable function
V(t,\mathbf{X})
satisfies the HJB equation in (
4

\mathbf{L}^{*}(t)
exists for all

, then
V
coincides with the value function and the feedback law
\mathbf{L}^{*}(t)
solves the finite-horizon LQR problem with jumps. Furthermore, we prove that this minimizer

can be computed explicitly by solving a system of
|{Z}|

The organization of this work is as follows. Section 2 presents a review of the classical finite-horizon LQR problem with jumps under the irreducibility assumption, introduces the necessary mathematical framework including the projection operator and restricted operators, and develops the deterministic reformulation in terms of second-moment matrices that naturally handles general Markov chains. Section 3 develops the solution of the reformulated problem using deterministic optimal control techniques, including the rigorous derivation of the HJB equation via the Riesz–Fréchet representation theorem and the reduced-order coupled Riccati system. Section 4 provides illustrative numerical examples that validate the theoretical results, including cases with non-communicating states that demonstrate the practical relevance of our generalization. One example involves an application that models the optimal trajectory design required to keep a satellite in orbit despite potential failures in its thrusters, where failure modes are modeled as absorbing states. Finally, Section 5 contains the conclusions derived from the results presented.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we present a review of the finite-horizon LQR problem with Markovian jumps. We begin by recalling its classical formulation in the stochastic setting under the restrictive assumption that all states of the underlying Markov chain communicate with each other (i.e., irreducibility). We then introduce its deterministic reformulation based on the second-moment matrix framework, which serves as the foundation for removing this assumption and developing a general theory applicable to arbitrary finite-state Markov chains.

2.2.
Reformulation of the LQR Problem with Jumps

To set the stage for this alternative treatment of the problem that will enable us to handle general Markov chains, we introduce a reformulation of the LQR problem in which the main variables are elements of a Hilbert space of finite collections of matrices. We begin by establishing the necessary mathematical framework.

Let us consider the set
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n,m}

\mathbf{V}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{N})

and
\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for brevity). The space
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n,m}
is also a vector space, equipped with the operations:

\mathbf{V}+\mathbf{W}:=(V_{1}+W_{1},\ldots,V_{N}+W_{N}),\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha\mathbf{V}:=(\alpha V_{1},\ldots,\alpha V_{N}),

for all
\mathbf{V}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{N}),\mathbf{W}=(W_{1},\ldots,W_{N})\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n,m}
and any scalar
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, for
\mathbf{V},\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n,m}
as above, we write

\mathbf{V}\mathbf{W}:=(V_{1}W_{1},\ldots,V_{N}W_{N}).

Additionally, we define the subspaces
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n*}
and
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n+}
as

\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n*}:=\left\{\mathbf{V}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{N})\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\;:\;V_{i}=V_{i}^{\prime},\,\forall i=1,\ldots,N\right\}

and

\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n+}:=\left\{\mathbf{V}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{N})\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n*}\;:\;V_{i}\geq 0,\,\forall i=1,\ldots,N\right\}.

Moreover, we introduce the following bilinear mapping
\langle\cdot\,;\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, defined by

(14)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{V};\mathbf{W}\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tr(V_{i}^{\prime}W_{i}),\quad\forall\>\mathbf{V},\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

which satisfies the axioms of an inner product on
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

{rmrk}

Note that the space
\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is isomorphic to the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
via the isomorphism
\textbf{{vec}}:\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
defined as

\textbf{{vec}}(A)=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&\cdots&a_{n1}&a_{12}&\cdots&a_{n2}&\cdots&a_{1n}&\cdots&a_{nn}\end{bmatrix}^{\prime},\quad\forall A\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

with
A=(a_{ij})
for
i,j=1,\ldots,n
, see
[
4
]
. Moreover, it is easy to verify that
\textbf{{vec}}(A)^{\prime}\textbf{{vec}}(B)=\tr(A^{\prime}B)
for all
A,B\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where
\tr(A^{\prime}B)
defines the inner product between the matrices
A
and
B
in
\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Hence, equipping
\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with the Frobenius norm
\|A\|_{\text{F}}^{2}:=\langle A,A\rangle_{F}:=\tr(A^{\prime}A)
turns it into a Banach space, or equivalently,
\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
becomes a Hilbert space when endowed with the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{F}
. This, in turn, implies that the space
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, equipped with the inner product defined in (
14
), is also a Hilbert space.

2.2.2.
Visited States and Projection Operator

Before proceeding with the characterization of the second-moment matrices, we introduce the notion of visited states and a projection operator that will play a crucial role in our general formulation.

{dfntn}

We define the set of
visited states
over the time horizon
[0,T]
as:

{Z}:=\left\{i\in\mathcal{S}:\pi_{i}(t)>0\text{ for some }t\in[0,T]\right\},

where
\pi_{i}(t)=\text{Pr}(\theta(t)=i)
.

States
i\notin{Z}
are never visited in the sense that
\pi_{i}(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
. By standard properties of continuous-time Markov chains, if
\pi_{i}(t)>0
for some
t>0
, then
\pi_{i}(s)>0
for all
s\geq t
. Therefore,
{Z}
depends only on the initial distribution
\phi
and the generator
\Lambda
and it can be characterized as consisting of all states
i
for which there exists a state
j
with
\phi(j)>0
and a finite sequence
i_{0}=j,i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}=i
such that

\lambda_{i_{0},i_{1}}\lambda_{i_{1},i_{2}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{k-1},i_{k}}>0.

{rmrk}

When the Markov chain is irreducible and
\phi(i)>0
for all
i\in\mathcal{S}
, we have
{Z}=\mathcal{S}
. This is precisely the setting covered by the classical results in Theorems
2.1
and
2.1
. In the general case,
{Z}
may be a proper subset of
\mathcal{S}
, and we write
|{Z}|
to denote the cardinality of the set of visited states.

{dfntn}

Let
{\mathcal{P}}:\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
denote the projection operator defined componentwise by:

(15)

({\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{U}))_{i}=\begin{cases}U_{i},&i\in{Z},\\
0,&i\notin{Z},\end{cases}

for all
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. We denote the image of
\mathcal{P}
by

\mathbb{V}:=\text{Im}(\mathcal{P})=\left\{\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:U_{i}=0\text{ for all }i\notin{Z}\right\}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

Note that
\mathcal{P}
is indeed an orthogonal projection since:

(1)

\mathcal{P}^{2}=\mathcal{P}
(idempotence),

(2)

\mathcal{P}
is self-adjoint:
\langle\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{U});\mathbf{V}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{U};\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{V})\rangle
for all
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Furthermore, if
{\mathcal{I}}:\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
stands for the identity operator, any
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
admits the orthogonal decomposition

\mathbf{U}={\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{U})+(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P})(\mathbf{U}),

where
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbf{U})\in\mathbb{V}
and
(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P})(\mathbf{U})\in\mathbb{V}^{\perp}
, with

\mathbb{V}^{\perp}=\left\{\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:U_{i}=0\text{ for all }i\in{Z}\right\}.

2.2.3.
Operators on Matrix Collections

Now, for a given element
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we introduce the following operators acting on
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
:
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}}:\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U}}:\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, defined componentwise by:

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U},i}(\mathbf{Q}):=U_{i}Q_{i}+Q_{i}U_{i}^{\prime}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda_{ji}Q_{j},\quad\forall\,\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\;i\in\mathcal{S},

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U},i}(\mathbf{Q}):=U_{i}^{\prime}Q_{i}+Q_{i}U_{i}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda_{ij}Q_{j},\quad\forall\,\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\;i\in\mathcal{S}.

{rmrk}

The operators defined in (
16
) and (
17
) are adjoint to each other with respect to the inner product (
14
) (see
[
4
, Lemma 3.5]
). That is, for all
\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\langle\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{Q});\mathbf{P}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{Q};\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{P})\rangle.

This adjointness is a key property in deriving the solution to the finite-horizon LQR problem with jumps, as will be shown in subsequent sections.

Since we are working with general Markov chains where not all states may be visited, it is natural to introduce restricted versions of these operators that act only on the subspace
\mathbb{V}
of collections whose components vanish for non-visited states.

{dfntn}

For a given element
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we define the
restricted operators

\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}}^{{Z}}:\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{V}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U}}^{{Z}}:\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{V}
by:

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{Q}):=\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{Q})\right),\quad\forall\,\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{V},

(19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{Q}):=\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{Q})\right),\quad\forall\,\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{V}.

Equivalently, in component form, for
i\in{Z}
:

(20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U},i}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle:=U_{i}Q_{i}+Q_{i}U_{i}^{\prime}+\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ji}Q_{j},

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U},i}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle:=U_{i}^{\prime}Q_{i}+Q_{i}U_{i}+\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ij}Q_{j},

and for
i\notin{Z}
:

\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U},i}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{Q})=\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U},i}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{Q})=0.

{rmrk}

The restricted operators have the following important properties:

(1)

Invariance of
\mathbb{V}
:
They map
\mathbb{V}
into itself. That is, if
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{V}
, then
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{Q}),\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{Q})\in\mathbb{V}
. This is immediate from the definition since they are obtained by composing with
\mathcal{P}
.

(2)

Adjointness:
The operators
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}}^{{Z}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U}}^{{Z}}
remain adjoint to each other when restricted to
\mathbb{V}
: for all
\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{V}
,

\langle\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{Q});\mathbf{P}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{Q};\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{P})\rangle.

This follows from the fact that
\mathcal{P}
is self-adjoint and the adjointness of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U}}
.

(3)

Reduced sums:
In component form, the sums in (
20
)–(
21
) run only over
j\in{Z}
rather than over all
j\in\mathcal{S}
. This reflects the fact that for
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{V}
, we have
Q_{j}=0
for all
j\notin{Z}
, so the terms with
j\notin{Z}
contribute nothing to the sums. This observation is crucial: it shows that the dynamics of visited states decouple from non-visited states.

(4)

Reduction to classical case:
When the Markov chain is irreducible and all states have positive initial probability (i.e.,
{Z}=\mathcal{S}
), we have
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{I}
and
\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, so the restricted operators coincide with the original ones:

\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}}^{{Z}}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}},\quad\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U}}^{{Z}}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{U}}.

Thus, our general framework recovers the classical setting as a special case.

(5)

Computational efficiency:
In practical implementations, when
|{Z}|\ll N
, working with the restricted operators allows us to solve a reduced-order system involving only
|{Z}|
matrix equations rather than
N
equations. This can lead to significant computational savings.

2.2.4.
Second-Moment Matrices

Let us define a collection
\mathbf{X}(t)=(X_{1}(t),\ldots,X_{N}(t))
of
N
second-moment matrices of the state trajectory
x(t)
, where each component is given at time
t\geq 0
by:

(22)

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)={E}(x(t)x(t)^{\prime}\cdot 1_{\{\theta(t)=i\}}),\quad i\in\mathcal{S}.

{rmrk}

The definition (
22
) is well-posed for all
i\in\mathcal{S}
and all
t\in[0,T]
. When
\pi_{i}(t)>0
(i.e.,
i\in{Z}
), the matrix
X_{i}(t)
can be equivalently written as

X_{i}(t)=\pi_{i}(t){E}(x(t)x(t)^{\prime}\mid\theta(t)=i),

relating it to the conditional second moment. This conditional expectation is well-defined precisely because
\pi_{i}(t)>0
. However, when
\pi_{i}(t)=0
(i.e.,
i\notin{Z}
), the conditional expectation
{E}(x(t)x(t)^{\prime}\mid\theta(t)=i)
is not uniquely defined, as we are conditioning on an event of probability zero. Nevertheless, the definition (
22
) remains well-posed and yields
X_{i}(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
, since the event
\{\theta(t)=i\}
has probability zero.
This observation has an important consequence:
\mathbf{X}(t)\in\mathbb{V}
for all
t\in[0,T]
. That is, the collection of second-moment matrices naturally lives in the subspace
\mathbb{V}
of collections that vanish on non-visited states. Equivalently,
\mathbf{X}(t)=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}(t))
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

When the system in (
1
) is a homogeneous MJLS, i.e., when the dynamics reduce to

(23)

\dot{x}(t)=A_{\theta(t)}(t)x(t),

the collection of second-moment matrices defined in (
22
) evolves according to the following result.

{thrm}

[
[
7
]
, adapted]
For a homogeneous MJLS as in (
23
), the collection
\mathbf{X}(t)\in\mathbb{V}
satisfies, for all
t>0
,

(24)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(t)}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{X}(t)).

Equivalently, in component form, for all
i\in{Z}
,

(25)

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{i}(t)=A_{i}(t)X_{i}(t)+X_{i}(t)A_{i}^{\prime}(t)+\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ji}X_{j}(t),

while for
i\notin{Z}
, we have
X_{i}(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

For
i\in{Z}
, the result follows from
[
7
, Proposition 4.4(b)]
, which establishes that

\dot{X}_{i}(t)=A_{i}(t)X_{i}(t)+X_{i}(t)A_{i}^{\prime}(t)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda_{ji}X_{j}(t).

However, since
X_{j}(t)=0
for all
j\notin{Z}
(by Remark
2.2.4
), the sum reduces to
\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ji}X_{j}(t)
, yielding (
25
).

For
i\notin{Z}
, since
\pi_{i}(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
, the definition (
22
) immediately yields
X_{i}(t)=0
, and consequently
\dot{X}_{i}(t)=0
.

Therefore,
\mathbf{X}(t)\in\mathbb{V}
for all
t\in[0,T]
, and the compact form (
24
) emphasizes that the dynamics evolve entirely within the subspace
\mathbb{V}
of visited states. The use of the restricted operator
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(t)}^{{Z}}
ensures that the right-hand side also belongs to
\mathbb{V}
, making the equation consistent.
∎

{rmrk}

The form (
25
) reveals an important structural property: the evolution of
X_{i}(t)
for
i\in{Z}
depends only on
X_{j}(t)
for
j\in{Z}
. In other words, the second-moment dynamics for visited states decouple completely from non-visited states. This justifies working with a reduced-order system of dimension
|{Z}|
rather than the full system of dimension
N
.

2.2.5.
Deterministic Reformulation of the LQR Problem

Next, we rewrite the cost functional in (
9
) in terms of the second-moment matrix
\mathbf{X}(t)
, assuming a priori that the minimizing control input is of the form
u(t)=L_{\theta(t)}(t)x(t)
for
t\in[0,T]
. In fact, we claim that the finite-horizon LQR problem with jumps in (
10
) can be equivalently rewritten as:

(26)

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{L}}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t,\mathbf{L});\mathbf{X}(t)\rangle dt+\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(T);\mathbf{X}(T)\rangle

\displaystyle\text{s.t.:}\;\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}(t)}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{X}(t)),\\
\mathbf{X}(0):=\mathbf{X}^{0}\in\mathbb{V},\end{cases}

where the components of the initial collection
\mathbf{X}^{0}\in\mathbb{V}
are given by

X_{i}(0)={E}(x(0)x(0)^{\prime}1_{\{\theta(0)=i\}})=\phi(i)x_{0}x_{0}^{\prime},\quad i\in{Z},

and
X_{i}(0)=0
for
i\notin{Z}
. The variable
\mathbf{L}(t)=(L_{1}(t),\ldots,L_{N}(t))\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{m,n}
, defined for
t\in[0,T]
, is the decision variable of the problem, and the collection
\mathbf{\Gamma}(t,\mathbf{L})\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is defined componentwise as

\Gamma_{i}(t,\mathbf{L}):=Q_{i}(t)+L_{i}^{\prime}(t)R_{i}(t)L_{i}(t),\quad 0\leq t<T,\quad i\in\mathcal{S},

and
\Gamma_{i}(T):=Q_{i}(T)
for each
i\in\mathcal{S}
.

We now verify that the cost functional in (
9
) coincides with that in (
26
).
For the terminal term, using the definition
X_{i}(T)={E}(x(T)x(T)^{\prime}1_{\{\theta(T)=i\}})
, we have

(27)

\displaystyle{E}\!\left[x(T)^{\prime}Q_{\theta(T)}(T)x(T)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\tr\left({E}\left[x(T)x(T)^{\prime}1_{\{\theta(T)=i\}}\right]Q_{i}(T)\right)

(28)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\tr(X_{i}(T)Q_{i}(T))=\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(T);\mathbf{Q}(T)\rangle,

and since
\mathbf{\Gamma}(T)=\mathbf{Q}(T)
, this equals
\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(T),\mathbf{X}(T)\rangle
. Similarly, if
u(t)=L_{\theta(t)}(t)x(t)
for
t\in[0,T)
, then

x(t)^{\prime}(Q_{\theta(t)}(t)+L_{\theta(t)}^{\prime}(t)R_{\theta(t)}(t)L_{\theta(t)}(t))x(t)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}x(t)^{\prime}\Gamma_{i}(t,\mathbf{L})x(t)\,1_{\{\theta(t)=i\}},

and hence, by linearity of expectation and the definition of
X_{i}(t)
,

\displaystyle{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}x(t)^{\prime}(Q_{\theta(t)}(t)+L_{\theta(t)}^{\prime}(t)R_{\theta(t)}(t)L_{\theta(t)}(t))x(t)\,dt\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\!\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\tr(\Gamma_{i}(t,\mathbf{L})X_{i}(t))\,dt

(29)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\!\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t,\mathbf{L}),\mathbf{X}(t)\rangle\,dt.

Thus, the stochastic cost functional (
9
) admits the deterministic representation (
26
).
Since
X_{i}(t)=0
for all
i\notin Z
and
t\in[0,T]
(see Remark
2.2.4
), the inner products effectively reduce to the visited states:

\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t,\mathbf{L}),\mathbf{X}(t)\rangle=\sum_{i\in Z}\tr(\Gamma_{i}(t,\mathbf{L})X_{i}(t)).

This observation is crucial: it shows that the optimal control problem naturally decouples into a reduced-order problem over the visited states
{Z}
, which may be strictly smaller than the full state space
\mathcal{S}
when the Markov chain is not irreducible. Moreover, since the constraint
\dot{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}(t)}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{X}(t))
preserves the property that
\mathbf{X}(t)\in\mathbb{V}
(as the restricted operator maps
\mathbb{V}
into itself), the entire optimization problem can be formulated over the reduced-order space
\mathbb{V}
of dimension
n^{2}|{Z}|
rather than the full space
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of dimension
n^{2}N
. To formalize the consistency between the full-order and reduced-order formulations when control is present, we establish the following result.

{prpstn}

[Consistency of restricted and full systems]
Consider the full-order system of controlled dynamics

(30)

\dot{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}(t)}(\mathbf{X}(t)),\quad\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{X}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

and the restricted system

(31)

\dot{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}(t)}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{X}(t)),\quad\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{0}).

Then, if
\mathbf{X}_{\text{full}}(t)
solves (
30
) and
\mathbf{X}_{\text{res}}(t)
solves (
31
), we have

\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{full}}(t))=\mathbf{X}_{\text{res}}(t),\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

In other words, the projection of the solution to the full system coincides with the solution to the restricted system.

Proof.

Define
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}(t):=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{full}}(t))
. Clearly,
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}(0)=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{0})
. Since
\mathcal{P}
is a linear and time-independent operator,

\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathcal{P}\left(\dot{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{full}}(t)\right)=\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}(t)}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{full}}(t))\right).

By Definition
2.2.3
, for any
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\mathcal{K}^{Z}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}(t)}(\mathbf{Q})=\mathcal{P}\big(\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}(t)}(\mathbf{Q})\big).

Writing
\mathbf{Q}=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Q})+(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P})(\mathbf{Q})
with
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Q})\in\mathbb{V}
and
(\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{P})(\mathbf{Q})\in\mathbb{V}^{\perp}
, and noting that for
i\in Z
we have
\lambda_{ji}=0
whenever
j\notin Z
, it follows that

\mathcal{P}\!\left(\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{Q})\right)=\mathcal{P}\!\left(\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Q}))\right)=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{U}}^{Z}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Q})).

Applying this to
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{X}_{\text{full}}(t)
gives

\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathcal{K}^{Z}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}(t)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}(t)),

which shows that
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}(t)
satisfies (
31
). By uniqueness of solutions to ODEs,
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathbf{X}_{\text{res}}(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
∎

{rmrk}

An important question arises: what about the control gains
L_{i}(t)
for
i\notin{Z}
? Since states
i\notin{Z}
are never visited, the values of
L_{i}(t)
for such states do not affect the cost functional or the state dynamics. They can be chosen arbitrarily (or set to zero for definiteness). The optimization problem (
26
) effectively determines only
L_{i}(t)
for
i\in{Z}
.

The reformulated version, expressed in terms of the matrix-valued trajectory
\mathbf{X}(t)\in\mathbb{V}
and the operator-based structure with explicit use of the restricted operators, provides a rigorous foundation for extending the LQR theory to general Markov chains without the communicating states assumption. This framework will serve as the basis for deriving the main results in the following sections, where we will obtain a reduced-order system of coupled Riccati differential equations involving only the visited states
{Z}
.

3.
Solution to the Finite-Horizon LQR Problem with Jumps: A Deterministic Approach

In this section, we present the deterministic approach to solving the finite-horizon LQR problem with Markovian jumps, previously reformulated in terms of second-moment matrices. A key advantage of this reformulation is that it naturally accommodates general Markov chains without the restrictive assumption of irreducibility. By working within the subspace
\mathbb{V}
of collections that vanish on non-visited states and employing the restricted operators introduced in Section 2, we derive a reduced-order system of coupled Riccati differential equations involving only the visited states
{Z}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
. By discretizing the time interval and applying dynamic programming principles, we derive a HJB-type equation associated with the cost-to-go functional. Under suitable regularity assumptions, we first establish the differentiability of the value function and validate the sufficiency theorem that ensures that a solution to the HJB equation also solves the original optimization problem.

3.1.
A Sufficient Condition via the HJB Equation

We consider a discretization of the problem in (
26
), to which, under certain conditions, we apply the dynamic programming algorithm in order to obtain an HJB-type equation. To this end, let us take a partition
\{0=t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{\ell}=T\}
of the interval
[0,T]
and assume
t_{k+1}-t_{k}=\delta>0
for all
k=0,\ldots,\ell-1
, so that
t_{k}=k\delta
, for
k=0,\ldots,\ell
. We also denote
\mathbf{X}(t_{k})=\mathbf{X}(k\delta)
by
\mathbf{X}^{k}
and
\mathbf{L}(t_{k})=\mathbf{L}(k\delta)
by
\mathbf{L}^{k}
. With this, the dynamics of the second-moment matrices is approximated by:

\mathbf{X}^{k+1}=\mathbf{X}^{k}+\delta\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}^{k}+\mathbf{B}^{k}\mathbf{L}^{k}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{X}^{k}),

where we recall that
\mathbf{X}^{k}\in\mathbb{V}
(i.e., components corresponding to non-visited states remain zero), and the cost functional to be minimized, by finding suitable collections of matrices
\mathbf{L}^{0},\ldots,\mathbf{L}^{\ell-1}
of appropriate dimensions, is approximated by:

(32)

\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t_{k},\mathbf{L}^{k});\mathbf{X}^{k}\rangle\delta+\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t_{\ell});\mathbf{X}^{\ell}\rangle.

Now, for any time
t
in
[0,T]
and any collection
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{V}
, we define the cost-to-go associated with problem in (
26
) as:

(33)

J^{*}(t,\mathbf{X})=\min_{\mathbf{L}(\tau),\,\tau\in[t,T]}\left\{\int_{t}^{T}\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(\tau,\mathbf{L}(\tau));\mathbf{X}(\tau)\rangle d\tau+\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(T);\mathbf{X}(T)\rangle\right\},

where the minimization is understood over
\mathbf{X}(\tau)\in\mathbb{V}
satisfying the constraint
\dot{\mathbf{X}}(\tau)=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(\tau)+\mathbf{B}(\tau)\mathbf{L}(\tau)}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{X}(\tau))
with
\mathbf{X}(t)=\mathbf{X}
, and whose discretized version, for any
t_{k}
and
\mathbf{X}^{k}=\mathbf{X}
, is given by:

\tilde{J}^{*}(t_{k},\mathbf{X})=\min_{\mathbf{L}^{k},\ldots,\mathbf{L}^{\ell-1}}\left\{\sum^{\ell-1}_{i=k}\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t_{i},\mathbf{L}^{i});\mathbf{X}^{i}\rangle\delta+\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t_{\ell});\mathbf{X}^{\ell}\rangle\right\}.

Therefore, the dynamic programming algorithm yields:

(34)

\hskip-113.81102pt\tilde{J}^{*}(t_{\ell},\mathbf{X})=\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t_{\ell});\mathbf{X}^{\ell}\rangle

(35)

\tilde{J}^{*}(t_{k},\mathbf{X})=\min_{\mathbf{L}}\{\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t_{k},\mathbf{L});\mathbf{X}\rangle\delta+\tilde{J}^{*}(t_{k+1},\mathbf{X}^{k+1})\},\quad\quad k=\ell-1,\ldots,0,

and hence
\tilde{J}^{*}(0,\mathbf{X}^{0})
is the minimum value of the discretized cost functional in (
32
). Now suppose that, for any
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{V}
, the function
\tilde{J}^{*}(\cdot,\mathbf{X}):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable, and that for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
its derivative is denoted by
\partial_{t}\tilde{J}^{*}(t,\mathbf{X})
. Also, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we assume that
\tilde{J}^{*}(t,\cdot):\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is differentiable. Then, by the Riesz-Fréchet representation theorem (see e.g.
[
2
]
), there exists a unique element
\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{J}^{*}(t,\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{V}
such that
\partial_{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{J}^{*}(t,\mathbf{X})(\mathbf{H})=\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{J}^{*}(t,\mathbf{X});\mathbf{H}\rangle
for all
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{V}
. Hence,
\tilde{J}^{*}(\cdot,\cdot)
is differentiable at any
(t,\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{V}
. Indeed, defining

r(\delta,\mathbf{H}):=\tilde{J}^{*}(t+\delta,\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{H})-\tilde{J}^{*}(t,\mathbf{X})-\partial_{t}\tilde{J}^{*}(t,\mathbf{X})\delta-\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{J}^{*}(t,\mathbf{X});\mathbf{H}\rangle,

for all
(\delta,\mathbf{H})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{V}
, and using the mean value theorem and differentiability of
\tilde{J}^{*}(t+\delta,\cdot)
, we get:

r(\delta,\mathbf{H})=\left[\partial_{t}\tilde{J}^{*}(t+h\delta,\mathbf{X})-\partial_{t}\tilde{J}^{*}(t,\mathbf{X})\right]\delta+r_{1}(\mathbf{H}),

where
0<h<1
and
r_{1}(\mathbf{H}):=\tilde{J}^{*}(t+\delta,\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{H})-\tilde{J}^{*}(t+\delta,\mathbf{X})-\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{J}^{*}(t,\mathbf{X});\mathbf{H}\rangle
satisfies
\lim_{\mathbf{H}\to\mathbf{0}}r_{1}(\mathbf{H})/||\mathbf{H}||=0
. As a consequence, the continuity of
\partial_{t}\tilde{J}^{*}(\cdot,\mathbf{X})
and the fact that

\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\to 0\\
\mathbf{H}\to\mathbf{0}\end{subarray}}\dfrac{r(\delta,\mathbf{H})}{|\delta|+||\mathbf{H}||}=0,

confirm that
\tilde{J}^{*}(\cdot,\cdot)
is differentiable at
(t,\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{V}
. Therefore, the term
\tilde{J}^{*}(t_{k+1},\mathbf{X}^{k+1})
in (
35
) can be written as:

(36)

\displaystyle\tilde{J}^{*}(t_{k+1},\mathbf{X}^{k+1})

\displaystyle=\tilde{J}^{*}(t_{k}+\delta,\mathbf{X}+\delta\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}^{k}+\mathbf{B}^{k}\mathbf{L}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{X}))

\displaystyle=\tilde{J}^{*}(t,\mathbf{X})+\partial_{t}\tilde{J}^{*}(t_{k},\mathbf{X})\delta+\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{J}^{*}(t_{k},\mathbf{X});\delta\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}^{k}+\mathbf{B}^{k}\mathbf{L}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{X})\rangle+r(\delta),

where
\lim_{\delta\to 0}r(\delta)/\delta=0
. Substituting into (
35
), we obtain:

0=\min_{\mathbf{L}}\{\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t_{k},\mathbf{L});\mathbf{X}\rangle\delta+\partial_{t}\tilde{J}^{*}(t_{k},\mathbf{X})\delta+\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\tilde{J}^{*}(t_{k},\mathbf{X});\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}^{k}+\mathbf{B}^{k}\mathbf{L}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{X})\rangle\delta+r(\delta)\}.

Dividing by
\delta
and taking the limit as
\delta\to 0
(assuming that
\tilde{J}^{*}(t_{k},\mathbf{X})\to J^{*}(t,\mathbf{X})
as
k\to\infty
), we obtain:

(37)

0=\min_{\mathbf{L}}\{\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t,\mathbf{L});\mathbf{X}\rangle+\partial_{t}J^{*}(t,\mathbf{X})+\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}J^{*}(t,\mathbf{X});\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{X})\rangle\},\quad\forall(t,\mathbf{X})\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{V},

which is an HJB-type equation with terminal condition
J^{*}(T,\mathbf{X})=\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(T);\mathbf{X}\rangle
.

{rmrk}

It is well known that, due to the isomorphism
\textbf{{vec}}:\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
, for a function
\psi:\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to be differentiable, the partial derivatives
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{ij}}
must exist and be continuous for every matrix
X\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with entries
x_{ij}
, where
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Furthermore, if
\psi
is differentiable and
X\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is arbitrary, then for every
H\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we have:

\partial_{X}\psi(X)(H)=\nabla_{\textbf{{vec}}(X)}\psi(X)^{\prime}\textbf{{vec}}(H)=\tr\left(\textbf{{vec}}^{-1}\left(\nabla_{\textbf{{vec}}(X)}\psi(X)\right)^{\prime}H\right),

where

\textbf{{vec}}^{-1}(\nabla_{\textbf{{vec}}(X)}\psi(X))=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{11}}(X)&\cdots&\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{1n}}(X)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{n1}}(X)&\cdots&\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{nn}}(X)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Thus, defining
\nabla_{X}\psi(X):=\textbf{{vec}}^{-1}(\nabla_{\textbf{{vec}}(X)}\psi(X))
, we get:

\partial_{X}\psi(X)(H)=\langle\nabla_{X}\psi(X),H\rangle_{F}.

Similarly, consider the isomorphism
\widehat{\textbf{{vec}}}:\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}N}
(see
[
7
]
) defined by:

\widehat{\textbf{{vec}}}(\mathbf{X})=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{{vec}}(X_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\textbf{{vec}}(X_{N})\end{bmatrix},\quad\forall\;\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

and let
\widehat{\psi}:\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function. Then, for any
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\mathbf{H}=(H_{1},\ldots,H_{N})\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we have:

\partial_{\mathbf{X}}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{X})(\mathbf{H})=\nabla_{\widehat{\textbf{{vec}}}(\mathbf{X})}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{X})^{\prime}\widehat{\textbf{{vec}}}(\mathbf{H})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_{\textbf{{vec}}(X_{i})}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{X})^{\prime}\textbf{{vec}}(H_{i}).

By Riesz-Fréchet representation theorem, there exists a unique element
\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that, defining
\nabla_{X_{i}}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{X}):=\textbf{{vec}}^{-1}(\nabla_{\textbf{{vec}}(X_{i})}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{X}))
, we obtain:

\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{X});\mathbf{H}\rangle=\partial_{\mathbf{X}}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{X})(\mathbf{H})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tr(\nabla_{X_{i}}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{X})^{\prime}H_{i}),\quad\forall\mathbf{H}\;\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

so that
\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{X})=(\nabla_{X_{1}}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{X}),\ldots,\nabla_{X_{N}}\widehat{\psi}(\mathbf{X}))\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

{xmpl}

Let
A\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a given matrix, and define the functions
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{m})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\psi_{3},\psi_{4}:\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\psi_{1}(X)=\tr(AX),\quad\psi_{2}(X)=\tr(XA),\quad\psi_{3}(Y)=\tr(AY^{\prime}),\quad\psi_{4}(Y)=\tr(Y^{\prime}A),

for all
X\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
Y\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{n})
, respectively. Then we claim that

\nabla_{X}\psi_{1}(X)=\nabla_{X}\psi_{2}(X)=A^{\prime},\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{Y}\psi_{3}(Y)=\nabla_{Y}\psi_{4}(Y)=A.

Indeed, we have:

(38)

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=\tr(AX)=\tr\left(\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}a_{1\ell}x_{\ell 1}&\cdots&\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}a_{1\ell}x_{\ell n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}a_{n\ell}x_{\ell 1}&\cdots&\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}a_{n\ell}x_{\ell n}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}a_{k\ell}x_{\ell k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{\ell k}a_{k\ell}=\tr\left(\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{1k}a_{k1}&\cdots&\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{1k}a_{km}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{mk}a_{k1}&\cdots&\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{mk}a_{km}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\tr(XA)=\psi_{2}(X).

Therefore, for
i=1,\ldots,m
and
j=1,\ldots,n
, we obtain:

\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial x_{ij}}(X)=\frac{\partial\psi_{2}}{\partial x_{ij}}(X)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{ij}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}a_{k\ell}x_{\ell k}\right)=a_{ji},

hence
\nabla_{X}\psi_{1}(X)=\nabla_{X}\psi_{2}(X)=A^{\prime}
. Similarly,
\psi_{3}(Y)=\psi_{4}(Y)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}a_{k\ell}y_{k\ell}
, so that

\frac{\partial\psi_{3}}{\partial y_{ij}}(Y)=\frac{\partial\psi_{4}}{\partial y_{ij}}(Y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{ij}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}a_{k\ell}y_{k\ell}\right)=a_{ij},

for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and
j=1,\ldots,m
. Therefore,
\nabla_{Y}\psi_{3}(Y)=\nabla_{Y}\psi_{4}(Y)=A
, as claimed.

{xmpl}

Let
A\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
be given, and define the function
\psi:\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{m})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
\psi(X)=\tr(X^{\prime}AX)
for all
X\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{m})
. We aim to show that
\psi
is differentiable and that
\nabla_{X}\psi(X)=(A+A^{\prime})X
for all
X\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Indeed, we compute:

(39)

\displaystyle\psi(X+H)

\displaystyle=\psi(X)+\tr(H^{\prime}AX)+\tr(X^{\prime}AH)+\tr(H^{\prime}AH)

\displaystyle=\psi(X)+\tr(H^{\prime}(AX+A^{\prime}X))+\tr(H^{\prime}AH)

\displaystyle=\psi(X)+\langle AX+A^{\prime}X,H\rangle_{F}+\tr(H^{\prime}AH),

for any
H\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Let
r(H):=\tr(H^{\prime}AH)
, then

(40)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|r(H)|}{\|H\|_{F}}=\frac{|\tr(H^{\prime}AH)|}{\|H\|_{F}}=\frac{|\tr(AHH^{\prime})|}{\|H\|_{F}}=\frac{|\langle HH^{\prime},A\rangle_{F}|}{\|H\|_{F}}\leq\frac{\|HH^{\prime}\|_{F}\|A\|_{F}}{\|H\|_{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\|H^{\prime}\|_{F}\|A\|_{F},

so that
\lim_{H\to 0}\frac{r(H)}{\|H\|_{F}}=0
, and therefore
\nabla_{X}\psi(X)=AX+A^{\prime}X=(A+A^{\prime})X
, as claimed. Note that the penultimate and last inequalities in (
40
) follow from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the submultiplicativity of the Frobenius norm, respectively.

We have seen that in order for the cost-to-go to satisfy the HJB-type equation as in (
37
), we imposed differentiability conditions on every
(t,\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{V}
over a discretized version of it. However, we may not possess any information about such property for the functional. In any case, the fact that the HJB-type equation in (
37
) was obtained allows us to raise the following question: assuming we have a solution
V(t,\mathbf{X})
to the HJB-type equation in (
37
), is it necessarily equal to the cost-to-go in (
33
)? More specifically, is
V(0,\mathbf{X}^{0})
the value of the optimal cost in (
26
)? As in
[
1
]
, we shall show that solving the HJB-type equation (
37
) yields, at each time
t
, an optimal collection of matrices
\mathbf{L}^{*}(t)
that minimizes the cost functional in (
26
). This result is known as the
Sufficiency Theorem
(also referred to as a
Verification Theorem
), and the following is an adaptation of a similar result found in
[
1
]
.

{thrm}

Suppose that
{V}(t,\mathbf{X})
is continuously differentiable for all
(t,\mathbf{X})\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{V}
and satisfies the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation:

(41)

\displaystyle 0=\min_{\mathbf{L}}\Big\{\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t,\mathbf{L});\mathbf{X}\rangle+

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{V}(t,\mathbf{X})+\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}{V}(t,\mathbf{X});\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}(t)}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{X})\rangle\Big\},

\displaystyle{V}(T,\mathbf{X})=

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{Q}(T),\mathbf{X}\rangle,\quad\forall\ \mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{V}.

Assume also that for every
t\in[0,T]
, the collection
\mathbf{L}^{*}(t):=(L_{1}^{*}(t),\ldots,L_{N}^{*}(t))
achieves the minimum in the HJB equation. Let
\mathbf{X}^{*}(t)\in\mathbb{V}
be the corresponding trajectory of second-moment matrices associated with
\mathbf{L}^{*}(t)
, initialized by
\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{X}^{0}\in\mathbb{V}
. Then,
{V}
is the unique solution to the HJB equation, and

(42)

\displaystyle{V}(t,\mathbf{X})=J^{*}(t,\mathbf{X}),\quad\forall\;(t,\mathbf{X})\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{V}.

Moreover,
\mathbf{L}^{*}(t)
,
t\in[0,T]
, solves the finite-horizon MJLS-LQR problem in (
26
).

Proof.

We proceed analogously to the proof of Proposition 2.1 in
[
1
]
. Fix
t\in[0,T)
and consider any admissible control collection
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}(\tau)
for
\tau\in[t,T]
, with the corresponding state trajectory
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau)\in\mathbb{V}
satisfying:

(43)

\dot{{\mathbf{X}}}(\tau)=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(\tau)+\mathbf{B}(\tau)\widehat{\mathbf{L}}(\tau)}^{{Z}}({\mathbf{X}}(\tau)),\quad\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(t)=\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{V}.

Since
V
satisfies the HJB equation, for every
\tau\in[t,T]
, we have:

(44)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(\tau,\widehat{\mathbf{L}});\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau)\rangle+\partial_{\tau}{V}(\tau,\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau))+\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}{V}(\tau,\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau));\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(\tau)+\mathbf{B}(\tau)\widehat{\mathbf{L}}(\tau)}^{{Z}}(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau))\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(\tau,\widehat{\mathbf{L}});\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau)\rangle+\partial_{\tau}{V}(\tau,\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau))+\nabla_{\widehat{\textbf{{vec}}}(\mathbf{X})}{V}(\tau,\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau))^{\prime}\widehat{\textbf{{vec}}}(\dot{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}(\tau))

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(\tau,\widehat{\mathbf{L}});\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau)\rangle+\frac{d}{d\tau}V(\tau,\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau)),

where the second equality uses the chain rule and the fact that
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau)\in\mathbb{V}
for all
\tau
, so that
\dot{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}(\tau)\in\mathbb{V}
by the invariance property of the restricted operator
\mathcal{K}^{{Z}}
. Integrating over
[t,T]
gives:

(45)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t}^{T}\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(\tau,\widehat{\mathbf{L}}(\tau));\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau)\rangle d\tau+V(T,\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(T))-V(t,\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{T}\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(\tau,\widehat{\mathbf{L}}(\tau));\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau)\rangle d\tau+\langle\mathbf{Q}(T);\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(T)\rangle-V(t,\mathbf{X}).

Thus,

(46)

V(t,\mathbf{X})\leq\int_{t}^{T}\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(\tau,\widehat{\mathbf{L}}(\tau));\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(\tau)\rangle d\tau+\langle\mathbf{Q}(T);\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(T)\rangle.

Now, take
\mathbf{L}^{*}(\tau)
as the minimizing collection in the HJB equation, and let
\mathbf{X}^{*}(\tau)\in\mathbb{V}
be the corresponding state trajectory. Then, the inequality in (
44
) becomes an equality for
\mathbf{L}^{*}(\tau)
, and therefore:

(47)

V(t,\mathbf{X})=\int_{t}^{T}\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(\tau,\mathbf{L}^{*}(\tau));\mathbf{X}^{*}(\tau)\rangle d\tau+\langle\mathbf{Q}(T);\mathbf{X}^{*}(T)\rangle.

Hence,
V(t,\mathbf{X})=J^{*}(t,\mathbf{X})
for all
(t,\mathbf{X})\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{V}
, and in particular
V(0,\mathbf{X}^{0})
is the optimal value of the finite-horizon problem in (
26
). ∎

3.2.
Riccati-Based Approach to the Jump LQR Problem

We now address the solution to the LQR problem with jumps reformulated as in (
26
), using the HJB-type equation in (
41
). We show that the solution of this equation, and hence the cost-to-go function
J^{*}(t,\mathbf{X}(t))
in (
33
), is given by
\langle\mathbf{X}(t);\mathbf{Y}(t)\rangle
, where
\mathbf{Y}(t)
is a collection of matrices in
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n+}
for all
t\geq 0
. Moreover, we demonstrate that the solution
V(t,\mathbf{X})
in (
41
) exists and is of the form
\langle\mathbf{X}(t);\mathbf{Y}(t)\rangle
, with
\mathbf{Y}(t)\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n+}
, if and only if
\mathbf{Y}(t)
satisfies a system of nonlinear matrix differential equations, specifically a system of coupled Riccati matrix differential equations involving only the visited states
{Z}
.

{thrm}

Consider the system of coupled Riccati matrix differential equations in the variable
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N})\in\mathbb{V}
:

(48)

\displaystyle-\dot{Y}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=A_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)+Y_{i}(t)A_{i}(t)+\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j}(t)+Q_{i}(t)-Y_{i}(t)B_{i}(t)R^{-1}_{i}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t),

\displaystyle\text{for }i\in{Z},

\displaystyle Y_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{for }i\notin{Z},

\displaystyle Y_{i}(T)

\displaystyle=Q_{i}(T),\quad\text{for }i\in{Z},

\displaystyle Y_{i}(T)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{for }i\notin{Z}.

Then,
V(t,\mathbf{X}):=\langle\mathbf{Y}(t);\mathbf{X}\rangle
, with
\mathbf{Y}(t)\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n+}\cap\mathbb{V}
,
0\leq t\leq T
, differentiable, is a solution to the HJB-type equation in (
41
) if and only if
\mathbf{Y}(t)
satisfies the coupled Riccati matrix differential equations in (
48
). For each
t\in[0,T]
and
i\in{Z}
, the minimizing collection
\mathbf{L}^{*}
in the HJB-type equation is given by:

(49)

\displaystyle L_{i}(t)=-R_{i}^{-1}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t),\;\;0\leq t\leq T.

For
i\notin{Z}
, the value of
L_{i}(t)
is immaterial since such gains are never used.

Proof.

Let
V(t,\mathbf{X})=\langle\mathbf{Y}(t);\mathbf{X}\rangle
, with
\mathbf{Y}(t)=(Y_{1}(t),\ldots,Y_{N}(t))\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n+}\cap\mathbb{V}
,
0\leq t\leq T
, differentiable, and
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{V}
arbitrary, be a solution to the HJB-type equation in (
41
). Then, since
\partial_{t}V(t,\mathbf{X})=\partial_{t}\langle\mathbf{Y}(t);\mathbf{X}\rangle=\sum_{i\in{Z}}\tr(\dot{Y}_{i}(t)X_{i})=\langle\dot{\mathbf{Y}}(t);\mathbf{X}\rangle
, where the sum runs only over
i\in{Z}
since
X_{i}=0
for
i\notin{Z}
, and
\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}V(t,\mathbf{X})=\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\langle\mathbf{Y}(t);\mathbf{X}\rangle=\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\sum_{i\in{Z}}\tr(Y_{i}(t)X_{i})
, where

\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\sum_{i\in{Z}}\tr(Y_{i}(t)X_{i})=\left(\nabla_{X_{1}}\sum_{i\in{Z}}\tr(Y_{i}(t)X_{i}),\ldots,\nabla_{X_{N}}\sum_{i\in{Z}}\tr(Y_{i}(t)X_{i})\right)=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Y}(t))=\mathbf{Y}(t),

and also

\langle\mathbf{Y}(t);\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{X})\rangle=\langle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{Y}(t));\mathbf{X}\rangle
,
by the adjointness property of the restricted operators (Remark
2.2.3
), the equation in (
41
) becomes:

(50)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0=&\min_{\mathbf{L}}\left[\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t,\mathbf{L});\mathbf{X}\rangle+\langle\dot{\mathbf{Y}}(t);\mathbf{X}\rangle+\langle\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{Y}(t));\mathbf{X}\rangle\right]\\
=&\min_{\mathbf{L}}\left[\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t,\mathbf{L})+\dot{\mathbf{Y}}(t)+\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{Y}(t));\mathbf{X}\rangle\right].\end{split}

Since only components
X_{i}
with
i\in{Z}
contribute to the inner product, to determine the minimizing collection
\mathbf{L}^{*}
, we differentiate the bracketed expression in (
50
) with respect to
\mathbf{L}
and equate to zero:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{L}}\langle\mathbf{\Gamma}(t,\mathbf{L})+\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{A}(t)+\mathbf{B}(t)\mathbf{L}}^{{Z}}(\mathbf{Y}(t));\mathbf{X}\rangle=0,

which yields, for each
i\in{Z}
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{L_{i}}\sum_{j\in{Z}}\tr\big(X_{j}\big[L_{j}^{\prime}R_{j}(t)L_{j}+(A_{j}(t)+B_{j}(t)L_{j})^{\prime}Y_{j}(t)

\displaystyle+Y_{j}(t)(A_{j}(t)+B_{j}(t)L_{j})\big]\big)=0.

Since the terms involving
L_{j}
for
j\neq i
are independent of
L_{i}
, this reduces to:

\displaystyle\nabla_{L_{i}}\tr\big(X_{i}\big[L_{i}^{\prime}R_{i}(t)L_{i}+(A_{i}(t)+B_{i}(t)L_{i})^{\prime}Y_{i}(t)+Y_{i}(t)(A_{i}(t)+B_{i}(t)L_{i})\big]\big)=0,

which leads to:

\displaystyle 2(R_{i}(t)L_{i}+B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t))X_{i}=0,

for arbitrary
X_{i}\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
i\in{Z}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle R_{i}(t)L_{i}+B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)=0,

and therefore,

(51)

\displaystyle L_{i}=L_{i}^{*}(t)=-R_{i}^{-1}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t),

for all
i\in{Z}
and
0\leq t\leq T
. Substituting this optimal gain into (
50
) yields:

(52)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in{Z}}

\displaystyle\tr\big(X_{i}\big[Q_{i}+Y_{i}(t)B_{i}(t)R_{i}^{-1}(t)R_{i}(t)R_{i}^{-1}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)+\dot{Y}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle+(A_{i}(t)-B_{i}(t)R_{i}^{-1}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t))^{\prime}Y_{i}(t)+Y_{i}(t)(A_{i}(t)-B_{i}(t)R_{i}^{-1}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t))

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j}(t)\big]\big)=0,

which simplifies to:

(53)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in{Z}}\tr\big(X_{i}\big[\dot{Y}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle+A_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)+Y_{i}(t)A_{i}(t)+\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j}(t)

\displaystyle+Q_{i}(t)-Y_{i}(t)B_{i}(t)R^{-1}_{i}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)\big]\big)=0,

for arbitrary
X_{i}\in\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
i\in{Z}
. Thus, the equation in (
53
) implies:

\dot{Y}_{i}(t)+A_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)+Y_{i}(t)A_{i}(t)+\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j}(t)+Q_{i}(t)-Y_{i}(t)B_{i}(t)R^{-1}_{i}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)=0,

which is exactly the matrix differential equation in (
48
) for
i\in{Z}
.

For
i\notin{Z}
, since
X_{i}(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
by Remark
2.2.4
, these components do not contribute to the inner product
\langle\mathbf{Y}(t);\mathbf{X}(t)\rangle
and therefore do not affect the cost functional. Consequently, the values of
Y_{i}(t)
for
i\notin{Z}
are immaterial to the optimization problem, and we can prescribe
Y_{i}(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
without loss of generality, as stated in (
48
). The mathematical consistency of working with this reduced-order formulation (where components corresponding to non-visited states are set to zero) versus the full-order system is rigorously established by Proposition
3.2
below.

Finally, by Theorem
3.1
, we have
\langle\mathbf{Y}(T);\mathbf{X}\rangle=V(T,\mathbf{X})=J^{*}(T,\mathbf{X})=\langle\mathbf{Q}(T);\mathbf{X}\rangle
, and hence
\langle\mathbf{Y}(T)-\mathbf{Q}(T);\mathbf{X}\rangle=0
, for any
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{V}
. Since this equality must hold for all
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{V}
, and recalling that components
X_{i}
with
i\notin{Z}
are zero for any such
\mathbf{X}
, we conclude that the equality determines
Y_{i}(T)=Q_{i}(T)
for all
i\in{Z}
. For
i\notin{Z}
, the value of
Y_{i}(T)
does not affect the cost functional
\langle\mathbf{Y}(T);\mathbf{X}\rangle
for any
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{V}
, and we prescribe
Y_{i}(T)=0
for definiteness, as stated in (
48
). This completes the derivation of the terminal condition.

Conversely, suppose that
\mathbf{Y}(t)=(Y_{1}(t),\ldots,Y_{N}(t))\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n+}\cap\mathbb{V}
,
0\leq t\leq T
, differentiable, is a solution to the terminal value problem in (
48
), and let us show that
V(t,\mathbf{X})=\langle\mathbf{Y}(t);\mathbf{X}\rangle
is a solution of the HJB-type equation in (
41
). The terminal condition in (
48
) prescribes
Y_{i}(T)=Q_{i}(T)
for
i\in{Z}
and
Y_{i}(T)=0
for
i\notin{Z}
. Since for any
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{V}
we have
X_{i}=0
for
i\notin{Z}
, the boundary condition
V(T,\mathbf{X})=\langle\mathbf{Q}(T);\mathbf{X}\rangle
in (
41
) is satisfied, as

\langle\mathbf{Y}(T);\mathbf{X}\rangle=\sum_{i\in{Z}}\tr(Y_{i}(T)X_{i})=\sum_{i\in{Z}}\tr(Q_{i}(T)X_{i})=\langle\mathbf{Q}(T);\mathbf{X}\rangle.

Substituting
\langle\mathbf{Y}(t);\mathbf{X}\rangle
into the right-hand side of (
41
) gives:

(54)

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{L}}\bigg[\sum_{i\in{Z}}\tr\big(X_{i}\big[\dot{Y}_{i}(t)+Q_{i}(t)+

\displaystyle L_{i}^{\prime}R_{i}(t)L_{i}+(A_{i}(t)+B_{i}(t)L_{i})^{\prime}Y_{i}(t)

\displaystyle+Y_{i}(t)(A_{i}(t)+B_{i}(t)L_{i})+\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j}(t)\big]\big)\bigg].

Thus, choosing
L_{i}=L_{i}^{*}(t)=-R_{i}^{-1}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)
for
i\in{Z}
, with
\mathbf{Y}(t)
satisfying (
48
), we obtain:

(55)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in{Z}}\tr

\displaystyle\big(X_{i}\big[\dot{Y}_{i}(t)+A_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)+Y_{i}(t)A_{i}(t)+\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j}(t)+Q_{i}(t)

\displaystyle-Y_{i}(t)B_{i}(t)R^{-1}_{i}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)\big]\big)=\sum_{i\in{Z}}\tr\big(X_{i}\big(\dot{Y}_{i}(t)-\dot{Y}_{i}(t)\big)\big)=0,

for all
t\in[0,T)
and
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{V}
. This shows that
V(t,\mathbf{X})=\langle\mathbf{Y}(t);\mathbf{X}\rangle
satisfies the HJB-type equation in (
41
), provided that
\mathbf{Y}(t)
is a solution of the coupled Riccati matrix differential equations in (
48
). Consequently, the optimal cost
J^{*}
, solution to the problem in (
26
), is given by:

(56)

J^{*}=J^{*}(0,\mathbf{X}(0))=\langle\mathbf{Y}(0);\mathbf{X}(0)\rangle=\sum_{i\in{Z}}\tr(Y_{i}(0){X}_{i}(0)),

where the sum involves only visited states since
X_{i}(0)=0
for
i\notin{Z}
.
∎

The coupled Riccati equations in (
48
) constitute a reduced-order system compared to formulations that naively include all
N
states. Specifically, for
i\in{Z}
, the differential equations involve only couplings with other visited states
j\in{Z}
, as indicated by the sum
\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j}(t)
—a direct consequence of using the restricted operator
\mathcal{H}^{{Z}}
rather than the unrestricted operator
\mathcal{H}
. For
i\notin{Z}
, we have
Y_{i}(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
, eliminating the need to solve differential equations for these components. The optimal cost formula in (
56
) involves only the visited states
i\in{Z}
, since
X_{i}(0)=0
for
i\notin{Z}
. When
|{Z}|<N
, this represents a genuine order reduction: instead of solving
N
coupled Riccati equations, we need only solve
|{Z}|
equations. This order reduction is mathematically justified by the fact that unvisited states have zero probability throughout the time horizon and therefore do not contribute to either the system dynamics or the cost functional. To formalize this reduction and establish its consistency with the full-order formulation, we present the following result.

{prpstn}

[Consistency of the reduced Riccati system]
Let
\mathbf{Y}_{\text{full}}(t)=(Y_{1}(t),\ldots,Y_{N}(t))
be a solution to the full-order Riccati system

(57)

\begin{split}-\dot{Y}_{i}(t)&=A_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)+Y_{i}(t)A_{i}(t)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j}(t)+Q_{i}(t)\\
&\quad-Y_{i}(t)B_{i}(t)R^{-1}_{i}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t),\quad Y_{i}(T)=Q_{i}(T),\end{split}

for all
i\in\mathcal{S}
, and let
\mathbf{Y}_{\text{red}}(t)
be the solution to the reduced-order system (
48
). Then

\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Y}_{\text{full}}(t))=\mathbf{Y}_{\text{red}}(t),\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

That is, the projection of the full solution coincides with the reduced solution.

Proof.

Define
\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t):=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Y}_{\text{full}}(t))
.
For each
i\in{Z}
, we have
\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t)=Y_{i,\text{full}}(t)
, and therefore

\begin{split}-\dot{\widetilde{Y}}_{i}(t)&=-\dot{Y}_{i,\text{full}}(t)=A_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i,\text{full}}(t)+Y_{i,\text{full}}(t)A_{i}(t)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j,\text{full}}(t)+Q_{i}(t)\\
&\quad-Y_{i,\text{full}}(t)B_{i}(t)R^{-1}_{i}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i,\text{full}}(t).\end{split}

For
j\notin{Z}
, the transition rates
\lambda_{ij}
from
i\in{Z}
to
j\notin{Z}
may be nonzero in the generator
\Lambda
. However, since
\widetilde{Y}_{j}(t)=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Y}_{\text{full}}(t))_{j}=0
for all
j\notin{Z}
, the sum becomes

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda_{ij}\widetilde{Y}_{j}(t)=\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ij}\widetilde{Y}_{j}(t).

Hence, for
i\in{Z}
we obtain

-\dot{\widetilde{Y}}_{i}(t)=A_{i}^{\prime}(t)\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t)+\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t)A_{i}(t)+\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ij}\widetilde{Y}_{j}(t)+Q_{i}(t)-\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t)B_{i}(t)R^{-1}_{i}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t),

which is precisely the
i
th equation of the reduced system (
48
).
For
i\notin{Z}
, the projection enforces
\widetilde{Y}_{i}(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

At the terminal time
t=T
, we have

\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Y}_{\text{full}}(T))=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Q}(T))
,
whose components satisfy
Y_{i}(T)=Q_{i}(T)
for
i\in{Z}
and

Y_{i}(T)=0
for
i\notin{Z}
.
Hence, the projection preserves the terminal data, and the boundary
condition of the reduced system (
48
) coincides exactly
with the projected terminal condition of the full-order system.
Therefore, no inconsistency arises between the full-order and
reduced-order formulations.

Since
\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)
satisfies both the differential equations
and the terminal condition of the reduced system, and this system has a
unique solution on
[0,T]
under standard assumptions, it follows that

\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}(t)=\mathbf{Y}_{\text{red}}(t)
for all

t\in[0,T]
. Consequently,

\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Y}_{\text{full}}(t))=\mathbf{Y}_{\text{red}}(t),\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

∎

{rmrk}

Existence and uniqueness of solutions to the reduced-order Riccati system (
48
) follow from standard results for coupled matrix differential equations (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
), applied to the subsystem involving only states
i\in{Z}
. The key observation is that for
i\in{Z}
, the system

\begin{split}-\dot{Y}_{i}(t)&=A_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)+Y_{i}(t)A_{i}(t)+\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j}(t)+Q_{i}(t)\\
&\quad-Y_{i}(t)B_{i}(t)R^{-1}_{i}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)\end{split}

forms a well-posed system of
|{Z}|
coupled Riccati equations with terminal conditions
Y_{i}(T)=Q_{i}(T)
for
i\in{Z}
. The coupling through the sum
\sum_{j\in{Z}}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j}(t)
involves only the visited states, ensuring that the reduced system is properly decoupled from the trivial components
Y_{i}(t)=0
for
i\notin{Z}
. The existence and uniqueness theory for such systems (under standard assumptions on the coefficient matrices) guarantees a unique positive semidefinite solution
\mathbf{Y}(t)\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n+}\cap\mathbb{V}
on
[0,T]
.

{rmrk}

When the Markov chain is irreducible and
\phi(i)>0
for all
i\in\mathcal{S}
, we have
{Z}=\mathcal{S}
, the projection operator reduces to the identity
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{I}
, and the restricted operators coincide with the unrestricted ones:
\mathcal{K}^{{Z}}=\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{H}^{{Z}}=\mathcal{H}
. In this case, the reduced-order Riccati system (
48
) recovers exactly the classical coupled Riccati equations from Theorem
2.1
:

-\dot{Y}_{i}(t)=A_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t)+Y_{i}(t)A_{i}(t)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\lambda_{ij}Y_{j}(t)+Q_{i}(t)-Y_{i}(t)B_{i}(t)R^{-1}_{i}(t)B_{i}^{\prime}(t)Y_{i}(t),

for all
i\in\mathcal{S}
. Thus, our general framework strictly extends the classical theory while remaining consistent with it in the special case of irreducible chains.

The derivation presented above exemplifies the power and elegance of the proposed methodology. The transition from the original stochastic control problem to a deterministic reformulation in terms of the second-moment matrix not only streamlines the analysis, but also leads naturally to a system of coupled Riccati differential equations whose structure encapsulates the essential dynamics and optimality principles of the problem. Moreover, the generalization to arbitrary Markov chains—including those with transient states, absorbing states, or non-communicating classes—reveals that the problem naturally admits a reduced-order formulation involving only the visited states
{Z}
. By introducing the projection operator
\mathcal{P}
and the restricted operators
\mathcal{K}^{{Z}}
and
\mathcal{H}^{{Z}}
, and leveraging the orthogonal decomposition of collections in
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
into the subspace
\mathbb{V}
and its orthogonal complement
\mathbb{V}^{\perp}
, we have established that components corresponding to unvisited states remain identically zero and do not affect the optimal solution. The clarity and cohesion of this approach underscore the mathematical coherence inherent in the theory, offering a unified, tractable, and conceptually transparent framework for addressing optimal control under regime-switching dynamics with general Markov chains. This completes the rigorous mathematical foundation that was initiated in
[
16
]
but left incomplete regarding the formal derivation of the HJB equation and the systematic treatment of non-communicating states.

5.
Conclusions

This work presents a comprehensive analysis of the Linear Quadratic Regulator problem with Markovian jumps in a finite-horizon setting, making a significant departure from classical assumptions by removing the requirement that all states of the underlying Markov chain communicate. The LQR problem with jumps arises within the framework of stochastic optimal control theory and typically requires specialized tools from this field, including stochastic processes, the infinitesimal generator of Markov processes, Itô stochastic integrals, stochastic differential equations, martingale processes, stochastic dynamic programming, and the stochastic maximum principle, among others, as discussed in
[
13
,
4
]
.

A central contribution of this work is the reformulation of the jump LQR problem through a deterministic model whose state variable differs fundamentally from that of the original stochastic formulation. This reformulation enables the recovery and adaptation of results from deterministic optimal control theory in the pursuit of a solution. The mathematical rigor of this approach is established through several key theoretical results.

Theorem
3.1
provides a verification theorem that establishes sufficient conditions for optimality via the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation, validating that any solution to the HJB equation yields the optimal cost-to-go function. Theorem
3.2
constitutes the main result of this work, characterizing the optimal solution through a reduced-order system of coupled Riccati differential equations and demonstrating that the value function takes the quadratic form
V(t,\mathbf{X})=\langle\mathbf{Y}(t);\mathbf{X}\rangle
if and only if
\mathbf{Y}(t)
satisfies these equations with appropriate terminal conditions. These theorems provide the complete characterization of the finite-horizon LQR problem for general Markov jump linear systems.

To properly account for states that may never be visited, we introduced a projection operator that naturally restricts the problem to the subspace of visited states—those states with positive probability of being reached during the time horizon—thereby addressing configurations where the Markov chain contains transient states, absorbing states, or multiple non-communicating classes. The consistency of this reduction is rigorously justified through two formal results: Proposition
2.2.5
demonstrates that solving the restricted system is equivalent to projecting the solution of the full system, ensuring consistency of the reduced-order formulation for the controlled dynamics; and Proposition
3.2
confirms that the reduced-order Riccati system yields the same solution (via projection) as the full-order system. These propositions establish that the restriction to visited states is not merely a heuristic simplification but rather a mathematically consistent and equivalent reformulation.

The present approach was inspired by
[
16
]
, which proposes a similar deterministic reformulation strategy for a jump LQR problem with a time-reversed switching process under general (non-irreducible) Markov chains, and by
[
9
]
, which applies a comparable idea in the discrete-time setting also without assuming irreducibility. However,
[
16
]
does not provide formal justification for the validity of presenting the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation in the context of collections of matrices conditioned by the mode of a Markov chain. In contrast, the present work makes these foundational aspects explicit and provides rigorous theoretical justification for the proposed deterministic reformulation via the Riesz-Fréchet representation theorem, thereby completing a key step that had remained open in the literature.

The validation of theoretical results through numerical examples is also of notable relevance. In Example
4
, we demonstrated the effectiveness of the formula in (
56
) for computing the minimum cost
J^{*}
by comparing it with results obtained via Monte Carlo simulation based on 10,000 sample trajectories. The similarity percentage consistently exceeded 95% across different time horizons
T
, with relative errors on the order of
10^{-2}
. This type of quantitative verification had not been commonly reported in previous publications. Similarly, Example
4
confirmed the validity of the computed
J^{*}
in a deterministic context, showing that the formula in (
56
) reduces to the standard deterministic formulation when the transition rate matrix is zero and both system parameters and cost weighting matrices remain constant across all modes. Examples
4
and
4
demonstrate the practical applicability of the framework to scenarios involving non-communicating states. Example
4
illustrates how the projection operator naturally handles a chain with multiple isolated classes, while Example
4
addresses a realistic satellite control problem where thruster failures lead to an absorbing state. These examples highlight the importance of the generalization presented in this work and its relevance to real-world applications in fault detection systems, hierarchical control architectures, and reliability analysis.

Finally, while this work focuses on the finite-horizon setting, the results suggest interesting directions for future research, particularly regarding the extension to infinite-horizon problems and the investigation of sufficient conditions, such as notions of controllability and observability adapted to the general (non-irreducible) case, that ensure existence, optimality, and stability of solutions.
