Abstract
This paper establishes that, under the appropriate range of values of the parameters involved in the formulation of the model, a diffusive predator-prey system with saturation can have an arbitrarily large number of coexistence states for sufficiently large saturation rates. Moreover, it ascertains the global structure of the set of coexistence states in the limiting system as the saturation rate blows-up. MSC Numbers : 35J57, 92D40, 34C23, 70K05 Keywords : Predator-prey systems of Holling–Tanner type; Multiplicity of coexistence states; Bifurcation Theory; Stability and Phase plane analysis

Introduction

This paper studies the existence, multiplicity and global structure of the coexistence states of the following diffusive one-dimensional Holling–Tanner predator-prey model with Neumann boundary conditions

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u^{\prime\prime}=\lambda u-a(x)u^{2}-b\frac{uv}{1+\gamma u}\qquad\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[6.0pt]
-v^{\prime\prime}=\mu v-dv^{2}+c(x)\frac{uv}{1+\gamma u}\qquad\,\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[7.0pt]
u^{\prime}(0)=u^{\prime}(1)=0,\;\;v^{\prime}(0)=v^{\prime}(1)=0,\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

where
a,c\in\mathcal{C}([0,1];\mathbb{R})\setminus\{0\}
are non-negative,
b>0
and
d>0
are positive constants, and
\lambda
,
\mu
and
\gamma>0
are viewed as real parameters. This model is a special one-dimensional prototype of the generalized heterogeneous diffusive Holling–Tanner model introduced by two of the authors in
[
22
]
and
[
23
]
, whose simplest prototypes go back to Blat and Brown
[
2
]
and Casal et al.
[
4
]
, where some necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of coexistence states was established in its multidimensional counterpart under Dirichlet boundary conditions. The limiting problem when
\gamma=0
provides us with a classical diffusive predator-prey model of Lotka–Volterra type. In Population Dynamics,
\gamma>0
represents the saturation rate of the predator population, whose density is measured by
v
, in the presence of prey abundance, whose density is measured by
u
. The coexistence states are the componentwise positive solutions,
(u,v)
, of (
1.1
). According to the strong maximum principle, they satisfy
u\gg 0
and
v\gg 0
in the sense that
u(x)>0
and
v(x)>0
for all
x\in[0,1]

(see Chapters 1 and 2 of
[
17
]
, if necessary).

Whereas in the special case when
\gamma=0
the set of values of the parameters
(\lambda,\mu)
where (
1.1
) has a coexistence state coincides with the region where both semitrivial positive solutions are linearly unstable, when
\gamma>0
, (
1.1
) can have coexistence states even in the region where one of the semitrivial positive solutions becomes linearly stable. Actually, in this region one might have multiple coexistence states, as documented numerically by Casal et al.
[
4
]
. The rigorous existence of an
S
-shaped bifurcation diagram of coexistence states was established later by Du and Lou
[
8
]

and two of the authors in
[
23
]
. A similar
S
-shaped bifurcation diagram has also been obtained in a quasilinear predator-prey steady-state model with cross-diffusion,
which, after a suitable change of variables, is reduced to a system resembling the Holling–Tanner type, as shown by Kuto and Yamada
[
13
]
(see also
[
12
]
).

The multiplicity results in the Holling–Tanner model (
1.1
) contrast dramatically with the
one-dimensional uniqueness theorem of López–Gómez and Pardo
[
25
]
,
[
26
]
. Based on this uniqueness theorem, it was shown in
[
4
]
and
[
22
]
that (
1.1
) has a unique coexistence state for sufficiently small
\gamma>0
.

The main goal of this paper is to show that (
1.1
) can have an arbitrarily large number of coexistence states for sufficiently large
\gamma>0
. This result sharpens, substantially, a previous finding of Du and Lou
[
9
]
for a multidimensional counterpart of (
1.1
), where the existence of two non-constant positive coexistence states was established via variational methods.

Precisely, by introducing the change of variables

w:=\gamma\,u,\qquad\varepsilon=\frac{1}{\gamma},

(1.2)

(
1.1
) can be equivalently expressed as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-w^{\prime\prime}=\lambda w-\varepsilon a(x)w^{2}-b\frac{wv}{1+w}\qquad\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[6.0pt]
-v^{\prime\prime}=\mu v-dv^{2}+\varepsilon c(x)\frac{wv}{1+w}\qquad\,\,\,\,\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[7.0pt]
w^{\prime}(0)=w^{\prime}(1)=0,\;\;v^{\prime}(0)=v^{\prime}(1)=0.\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

Thanks to (
1.2
), analyzing (
1.1
) as
\gamma\uparrow+\infty
is equivalent to study (
1.3
) as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. Moreover, it is natural to think of (
1.3
) as a perturbation of the underlying limiting system obtained from (
1.3
) by switching off to zero the parameter
\varepsilon
as follows

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-w^{\prime\prime}=\lambda w-b\frac{wv}{1+w}\qquad\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[6.0pt]
-v^{\prime\prime}=\mu v-dv^{2}\qquad\quad\,\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[7.0pt]
w^{\prime}(0)=w^{\prime}(1)=0,\;\;v^{\prime}(0)=v^{\prime}(1)=0.\end{array}\right.

Thus, as this limiting system is uncoupled, and
v=\frac{\mu}{d}
is the unique positive solution of
the
v
-equation, which exists if and only if
\mu>0
, this paper will focus special attention into the positive solutions of the problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-w^{\prime\prime}=\lambda w-\frac{b\mu}{d}\frac{w}{1+w}\qquad\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[6.0pt]
w^{\prime}(0)=w^{\prime}(1)=0,\end{array}\right.

(1.4)

with
\mu>0
and
\lambda>0
, since
w=0
is the unique non-negative solution of (
1.4
) if

\lambda\leq 0
. Indeed, suppose that (
1.4
) admits a positive solution,
w
. Then,
w(x)>0

for all
x\in[0,1]
. Moreover, there exists
x_{m}\in[0,1]
such that

w(x_{m})=\max_{x\in[0,1]}w(x).

Necessarily,
w^{\prime\prime}(x_{m})\leq 0
. Thus,

\lambda-\frac{b\mu}{d}\frac{1}{1+w(x_{m})}\geq 0,

which implies
\lambda>0
. Therefore, throughout this paper we will assume that

\lambda>0\quad\hbox{and}\quad\mu>0.

(1.5)

Since (
1.4
) is an autonomous second order problem, phase plane analysis and time-map techniques can be used to get the next result, which is our main multiplicity theorem for (
1.4
). Throughout this paper,
we set

\mu_{\kappa}:=\frac{d}{b}(2\kappa\pi)^{2},\qquad\kappa\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
\mu\in(0,\mu_{1}]
. Then, the constant

w_{0}:=\frac{b\mu}{d\lambda}-1

is the unique positive solution of (
1.4
).

Suppose
\mu\in(\mu_{\kappa},\mu_{\kappa+1}]

the set of positive solutions of
(
1.4

plus
\kappa

,

at a family of critical values

0<\lambda_{1}^{-}<\lambda_{2}^{-}<\cdots<\lambda_{\kappa}^{-}<\tfrac{b\mu}{2d}<\lambda_{\kappa}^{+}<\cdots<\lambda_{2}^{+}<\lambda_{1}^{+}<\tfrac{b\mu}{d},

as illustrated in Figure
4

\kappa=2
. Moreover, for every

,

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\mathscr{C}_{n})=[\lambda_{n}^{-},\lambda_{n}^{+}],

where

denotes the

\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\lambda,w)=\lambda,

and, for every
\lambda\in(\lambda_{n}^{-},\lambda_{n}^{+})
,

\mathscr{C}_{n}
consists of two positive solutions,
(\lambda,w_{n})
and
(\lambda,\tilde{w}_{n})
, where
w_{n}
and
\tilde{w}_{n}
are the only positive solutions of (
1.4
),
w
, such that
w-w_{0}
has
n
zeros in
(0,1)
.
Therefore, (
1.4
) has exactly
2\kappa+1
positive solutions for each
\lambda\in(\lambda_{\kappa}^{-},\lambda_{\kappa}^{+})
.

Furthermore,
the loop
\mathscr{C}_{\kappa}
bifurcates from
\mathscr{C}_{0}
at the single point

(\mu,\lambda,w_{0})=\big(\mu_{\kappa},\tfrac{b\mu}{2d},1\big)

as

, and its existence interval,
\lambda\in(\lambda_{\kappa}^{-},\lambda_{\kappa}^{+})

\mu
.

As it will become apparent in Section 2,
\lambda_{\kappa}^{\pm}
are the real roots of the eigencurves of the linearized problem at the constant solution
w_{0}
.

The second aim of this paper is to show that the high multiplicity result established by Theorem
1.1

is mimicked by the perturbed problem (
1.3
) for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, except, at most, at finitely many values of the parameter
\lambda
. This is an extremely delicate issue based on the non-degeneration of
the coexistence states of (
1.3
) for
\varepsilon=0
, which relies on the structure of the solution
set of (
1.4
) and the analyticity of the eigenvalues of the associated variational problems. As a consequence of their non-degeneration, the Implicit Function Theorem shows that (
1.3
) has an arbitrarily large number of coexistence states for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0

This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 delivers the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Section 3 first ascertains the dimension of the unstable manifolds of the solutions of (
1.3
) for
\lambda
sufficiently
close to
\lambda_{n}^{\pm}
through the Exchange Stability Principle of Crandall and Rabinowitz

6
]
, and then combines the underlying analyticity with respect to
\lambda

to get the dimensions of the unstable manifolds of the positive solutions of (
1.4
) for all value of
\lambda
outside a certain finite set. Finally, in Section 4 the multiplicity theorem for the problem (
1.3
) is stated and proven.

The limiting system

In this section, we analyze the constant and nonconstant coexistence states of the limiting system derived in Section
1

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-w^{\prime\prime}=\lambda w-b\frac{wv}{1+w}\qquad\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[4.0pt]
-v^{\prime\prime}=\mu v-dv^{2}\qquad\quad\,\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[4.0pt]
w^{\prime}(0)=w^{\prime}(1)=0,\;\;v^{\prime}(0)=v^{\prime}(1)=0.\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

As (
2.1
) is uncoupled and the unique positive solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}-v^{\prime\prime}=\mu v-dv^{2}\qquad\,\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[2.0pt]
v^{\prime}(0)=v^{\prime}(1)=0,\end{array}\right.

is the constant function

v_{0}:=\frac{\mu}{d},

the problem (
2.1
) reduces to the analysis of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-w^{\prime\prime}=\lambda w-\frac{b\mu}{d}\frac{w}{1+w}\qquad\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[4.0pt]
w^{\prime}(0)=w^{\prime}(1)=0,\end{array}\right.

(2.2)

which coincides with (
1.4
). Thus, throughout this section we will deal with (
2.2
), whose unique constant positive solution is given by

w_{0}:=\tfrac{b\mu}{d\lambda}-1,

for all
\mu>0
and
\lambda\in(0,\tfrac{b\mu}{d})
.
Regarding
\lambda
as the main bifurcation parameter, Figure

plots the curve of constant positive solutions of (

\mathscr{C}_{0}:=\left\{(\lambda,w_{0})\;:\;\lambda\in(0,\tfrac{b\mu}{d})\right\}.

It should be noted that, for every
\mu>0
,
\lambda\mapsto w_{0}
is decreasing with respect to
\lambda

\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}w_{0}=+\infty,\quad\lim_{\lambda\uparrow\tfrac{b\mu}{d}}w_{0}=0.

Actually, as it will become apparent in Lemma

2.1
Spectrum of the linearization around
\mathscr{C}_{0}

This section analyzes the spectrum of the linearized problem of (
2.2
) at the constant positive solution
w_{0}
. This local analysis will provide us with some pivotal necessary and sufficient conditions for the bifurcation of nonconstant positive solutions from
\mathscr{C}_{0}
. Subsequently,
\lambda
will be the main bifurcation parameter of (
2.2
), while
\mu
will play the role of a secondary parameter. The bigger is
\mu
, the more complex is the global bifurcation diagram in
\lambda
of the problem (
2.2
).

Setting
D^{2}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}
, it is folklore that, for every
V\in L^{\infty}(0,1)
, the linear
eigenvalue problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(-D^{2}+V(x))\varphi=\tau\varphi\quad\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[7.0pt]
\varphi^{\prime}(0)=\varphi^{\prime}(1)=0,\end{array}\right.

possesses an increasing sequence of simple eigenvalues, which will be denoted by

\{\tau_{\ell}(V)\}_{\ell=0}^{\infty},

whose associated eigenfunctions have exactly
\ell
zeros in
(0,1)
. In particular, in the special case when
V(x)\equiv V
is constant, this sequence is given by

\tau_{\ell}(V):=\tau_{\ell}(0)+V=(\ell\pi)^{2}+V,\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

(2.3)

The next result provides us with a necessary condition for the existence of a positive solution of
(
2.2
). Note that, throughout this paper we are imposing (
1.5
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that (
2.2
) admits a positive solution. Then,
\lambda\in(0,\tfrac{b\mu}{d})
.

Proof.

Let
w
be a positive solution of (
2.2
). Then,

\left(-D^{2}+\tfrac{b\mu}{d}\tfrac{1}{1+w}\right)w=\lambda w\quad\hbox{in}\;\ (0,1).

Thus, since
w(x)>0
for each
x\in[0,1]
, we find that

\lambda=\tau_{0}\left(\tfrac{b\mu}{d}\tfrac{1}{1+w}\right).

Hence, by the monotonicity of the principal eigenvalue with respect to the potential (see
[
17
, Sec. 8.1]
, if necessary), it follows that

\lambda=\tau_{0}\left(\tfrac{b\mu}{d}\tfrac{1}{1+w}\right)<\tau_{0}\left(\tfrac{b\mu}{d}\right)=\tfrac{b\mu}{d}.

Therefore,
\lambda\in(0,\tfrac{b\mu}{d})
.
∎

Since

\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\frac{w}{1+w}=\frac{1}{(1+w)^{2}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\frac{1}{(1+w_{0})^{2}}=\left(\frac{d\lambda}{b\mu}\right)^{2},

the spectrum of the linearization of (
2.2
) at the constant positive solution
w_{0}
consists of the eigenvalues of the linear problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-w^{\prime\prime}=\lambda w-\frac{d}{b\mu}\lambda^{2}w+\tau w\qquad\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[7.0pt]
w^{\prime}(0)=w^{\prime}(1)=0.\end{array}\right.

(2.4)

According to (
2.3
), the problem (
2.4
) has the following sequence of eigencurves

\tau_{0,\ell}(\lambda)\equiv\tau_{0,\ell}(\lambda,\mu):=\tau_{\ell}\left(-\lambda+\tfrac{d}{b\mu}\lambda^{2}\right)=\tfrac{d}{b\mu}\lambda^{2}-\lambda+(\ell\pi)^{2},\quad\ell\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},

(2.5)

which is a sequence of polynomials of degree two in
\lambda

whose roots are given by

\lambda_{\ell}^{\pm}\equiv\lambda_{\ell}^{\pm}(\mu):=\tfrac{b\mu}{2d}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-\tfrac{4d}{b\mu}(\ell\pi)^{2}}\right),\quad\ell\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

(2.6)

Thus, setting

\mu_{\kappa}:=\frac{d}{b}(2\kappa\pi)^{2},\qquad\kappa\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},

(2.7)

it becomes apparent that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda_{\kappa}^{-}=\overline{\lambda_{\kappa}^{+}}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R},\;\;\tau_{0,\kappa}(\lambda)>0\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\lambda\in[0,\frac{b\mu}{d}],&\;\hbox{if}\;\,\mu<\mu_{\kappa},\\[15.0pt]
\lambda_{\kappa}^{-}=\lambda_{\kappa}^{+}=\frac{b\mu}{2d},&\;\hbox{if}\;\;\mu=\mu_{\kappa},\\[10.0pt]
\lambda_{\kappa}^{\pm}\in(0,\frac{b\mu}{d}),\;\;\tau_{0,\kappa}(\lambda)\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\!\!\!<0\;\;\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda\in(\lambda_{\kappa}^{-},\lambda_{\kappa}^{+}),\\[5.0pt]
\!\!\!>0\;\;\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda\in(0,\frac{b\mu}{d})\setminus[\lambda_{\kappa}^{-},\lambda_{\kappa}^{+}],\end{array}\right.&\;\hbox{if}\;\;\mu>\mu_{\kappa}.\end{array}\right.

Moreover, for every integer
\ell\geq 0
, the spectral polynomials
\tau_{0,\ell}(\lambda)
satisfy

\begin{split}\tau_{0,\ell}(0)=(\ell\pi)^{2}=\tau_{0,\ell}\left(\tfrac{b\mu}{d}\right)=\tau_{0,\ell}\left(\tfrac{b\mu}{2d}\right)+\tfrac{b\mu}{4d},\quad\dot{\tau}_{0,\ell}\left(\tfrac{b\mu}{2d}\right)=0,\end{split}

(2.8)

where
\dot{\tau}_{0,\ell}
stands for the derivative of
\tau_{0,\ell}(\lambda)
with respect to
\lambda
. Note that, thanks to (
2.8
),

\lim_{\mu\uparrow+\infty}\tau_{0,\ell}\left(\tfrac{b\mu}{2d}\right)=-\infty.

As (

) provides us with an increasing sequence of values of

\lim_{\kappa\uparrow\infty}\mu_{\kappa}=+\infty
, the larger is

, the larger is the number of

Moreover,

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\uparrow+\infty}\lambda_{\kappa}^{-}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\uparrow+\infty}\left[\tfrac{b\mu}{2d}\frac{\left(1-\sqrt{1-\tfrac{4d}{b\mu}(\kappa\pi)^{2}}\right)\left(1+\sqrt{1-\tfrac{4d}{b\mu}(\kappa\pi)^{2}}\right)}{1+\sqrt{1-\tfrac{4d}{b\mu}(\kappa\pi)^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mu\uparrow+\infty}\left(\tfrac{b\mu}{2d}\frac{\tfrac{4d}{b\mu}(\kappa\pi)^{2}}{1+\sqrt{1-\tfrac{4d}{b\mu}(\kappa\pi)^{2}}}\right)=(\kappa\pi)^{2}

\lim_{\mu\uparrow+\infty}\frac{\lambda_{\kappa}^{+}}{b\mu/d}=1.

In particular,

\lim_{\mu\uparrow+\infty}\lambda_{\kappa}^{+}=+\infty.

Furthermore, for every integer

\tfrac{\partial\lambda_{\ell}^{-}}{\partial\mu}<0<\tfrac{\partial\lambda_{\ell}^{+}}{\partial\mu},

which is reinforced by the fact that

is decreasing with respect to

. The right picture in Figure

-plane. Finally, note that, for every

,

\begin{split}\dot{\tau}_{0,\kappa}(\lambda_{\kappa}^{-})&=\tfrac{2d}{b\mu}\lambda_{\kappa}^{-}-1=-\sqrt{1-\tfrac{4d}{b\mu}(\kappa\pi)^{2}}\\
&<0<\sqrt{1-\tfrac{4d}{b\mu}(\kappa\pi)^{2}}=\tfrac{2d}{b\mu}\lambda_{\kappa}^{+}-1=\dot{\tau}_{0,\kappa}(\lambda_{\kappa}^{+}).\end{split}

(2.10)

From the previous spectral analysis one can easily count the number of negative eigenvalues of
the linearization of (
2.2
) at
w_{0}=\frac{b\mu}{d\lambda}-1
, given by (

), according to the values of the two parameters
\lambda\in(0,\frac{b\mu}{d})
and

. Such a number, coincident with the dimension of the unstable manifold of

, will be denoted by
\mathscr{M}(w_{0})

\tau_{0,0}(\lambda)=\left(\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}-1\right)\lambda<0\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;\lambda\in(0,\tfrac{b\mu}{d}),

we have that
\mathscr{M}(w_{0})\geq 1
for all

. As it will become apparent from the next discussion, the value of

\lambda

Suppose
\mu<\mu_{1}
. Then,
\tau_{0,\ell}(\lambda)>0

\lambda\in(0,\frac{b\mu}{d})
. Thus,

\lambda\in(0,\frac{b\mu}{d})

\tau_{0,1}(\tfrac{b\mu}{2d})=0

Suppose
\mu\in(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
. Then, since
\mu<\mu_{2}
, we have that
\tau_{0,\ell}(\lambda)>0
for all
\ell\geq 2
and
\lambda\in(0,\frac{b\mu}{d})
. Consequently,
1\leq\mathscr{M}(w_{0})\leq 2

\lambda\in(0,\frac{b\mu}{d})
. Moreover, since

\tau_{0,1}(\lambda)\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}>0&\;\;\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{1}^{-})\cup(\lambda_{1}^{+},\tfrac{b\mu}{d}),\\
<0&\;\;\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda\in(\lambda_{1}^{-},\lambda_{1}^{+}),\end{array}\right.

it becomes apparent that

\mathscr{M}(w_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\;\;\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{1}^{-}]\cup[\lambda_{1}^{+},\frac{b\mu}{d}),\\[2.0pt]
2&\;\;\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda\in(\lambda_{1}^{-},\lambda_{1}^{+}).\end{array}\right.

(2.11)

When
\mu=\mu_{2}
, we have that
\tau_{0,2}(\lambda)>0
for each
\lambda\in(0,\frac{b\mu}{d})\setminus\{\frac{b\mu}{2d}\}
, but
\tau_{0,2}(\frac{b\mu}{2d})=0
. Consequently,
(
2.11
) remains valid.

More generally, when
\mu\in(\mu_{\kappa},\mu_{\kappa+1})
for some integer
\kappa\geq 2
, then

\tau_{0,\ell}(\lambda)>0\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;\ell\geq\kappa+1\;\;\hbox{and}\;\;\lambda\in(0,\tfrac{b\mu}{d}),

and

\mathscr{M}(w_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\;\;\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{1}^{-}]\cup[\lambda_{1}^{+},\frac{b\mu}{d}),\\[2.0pt]
2&\;\;\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda\in(\lambda_{1}^{-},\lambda_{2}^{-}]\cup[\lambda_{2}^{+},\lambda_{1}^{+}),\\
\cdots&\\
\kappa&\;\;\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda\in(\lambda_{\kappa-1}^{-},\lambda_{\kappa}^{-}]\cup[\lambda_{\kappa}^{+},\lambda_{\kappa-1}^{+}),\\[2.0pt]
\kappa+1&\;\;\hbox{if}\;\;\lambda\in(\lambda_{\kappa}^{-},\lambda_{\kappa}^{+}).\end{array}\right.

(2.12)

The change of the Morse index by one when
\lambda
crosses
\lambda_{\ell}^{\pm}
for every
\ell\in\{1,....,\kappa\}
if
\mu\in(\mu_{\kappa},\mu_{\kappa+1})
entails the bifurcation of a branch of positive solutions of (
2.2
) bifurcating
from
\mathscr{C}_{0}
at
\lambda=\lambda_{\ell}^{\pm}
,
\ell\in\{1,....,\kappa\}
, as it will become apparent in the forthcoming analysis.

2.2
Phase plane analysis

In this section, we are using some non-standard phase plane techniques to ascertain the fine global structure of the set of nonconstant positive solutions of (
2.2
). As a consequence of this analysis, every nonconstant positive solution of (
2.2
) will be characterized from its nodal behavior with respect to
w_{0}
. Precisely, for every integer
n\geq 1
, it is said that
w_{n}
is a positive
(n,w_{0})
-nodal solution of (
2.2
) if
w_{n}
is a positive solution of (
2.2
) and
w_{n}-w_{0}
has exactly
n
zeroes in
(0,1)
, i.e.
w_{n}
is a positive solution of (
2.2
) crossing
n
times the constant solution
w_{0}
.

Since the problem (
2.2
) is autonomous, phase plane analysis and map-time techniques can be used.
As its associated first order system is given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}w^{\prime}=z,\\[5.0pt]
z^{\prime}=-\lambda w+\frac{b\mu}{d}\frac{w}{1+w},\end{array}\right.

(2.13)

the unique nontrivial equilibrium of (
2.13
) is
(w_{0},0)=(\frac{b\mu}{d\lambda}-1,0)
, and
the associated total energy is

\mathscr{E}(w,z):=\tfrac{1}{2}z^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}w^{2}-\tfrac{b\mu}{d}\left[w-\ln(1+w)\right].

Thus, we can take as potential energy the function

F(w):=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}w^{2}-\tfrac{b\mu}{d}\left[w-\ln(1+w)\right],\qquad w>-1.

(2.14)

As the function
F
satisfies, for every
w>-1
,

F^{\prime}(w)=\lambda w-\frac{b\mu}{d}\frac{w}{1+w},\qquad F^{\prime\prime}(w)=\lambda-\frac{b\mu}{d}\frac{1}{(1+w)^{2}},

where
′
stands for the derivative with respect to
w
here, it is apparent that

(F^{\prime})^{-1}(0)=\{0,w_{0}\},\quad F^{\prime\prime}(0)<0,\quad F^{\prime\prime}(w_{0})>0,\quad\lim_{w\uparrow+\infty}F(w)=+\infty.

Thus, in the
(w,z)
phase plane, the equilibrium
(0,0)
is a local saddle point, while the equilibrium
(w_{0},0)
is a local center surrounded by periodic orbits enclosed by the homoclinic connection

\mathscr{E}(w,z)=\mathscr{E}(0,0)=0.

Therefore, the phase plane portrait of (
2.13
) for
w\geq 0
is the one sketched in Figure
3
.
By simply having a glance at Figure
3
it is easily realized that the positive solutions of (
2.2
) should have their trajectories in the negative energy levels of (
2.13
), which have been plotted using green orbits in Figure
3
. Let denote by
(w_{\pm},0)
the crossing points of a generic periodic orbit with the
w
-axis. Then,

\begin{split}\mathscr{E}(w,z)&=\tfrac{1}{2}z^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}w^{2}-\tfrac{b\mu}{d}\left[w-\ln(1+w)\right]\\
&=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}w_{\pm}^{2}-\tfrac{b\mu}{d}\left[w_{\pm}-\ln(1+w_{\pm})\right]=F(w_{\pm})<0.\end{split}

(2.15)

Figure 3
:
Phase plane portrait of (
2.13
).

Subsequently, for every
w_{-}\in(0,w_{0})
, we denote by

T(w_{-}):=T(w_{-},w_{+}(w_{-}))>0

the necessary time used for the solution of (
2.13
) to link
(w_{-},0)
with
(w_{+},0)
through the orbit (
2.15
). As
\mathscr{E}(w,z)=0
is a homoclinic connection of
(0,0)
, we find that

\lim_{w_{-}\downarrow 0}T(w_{-})=+\infty.

(2.16)

Moreover, as established by the next lemma,
\lim_{w_{-}\uparrow w_{0}}T(w_{-})
can be calculated from the corresponding time map
for the linearized system of (
2.13
) at
(w_{0},0)
, which is given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}w^{\prime}=z,\\[2.0pt]
z^{\prime}=-\lambda\Big(1-\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}\Big)w.\end{array}\right.

(2.17)

As it becomes apparent from the proof of the lemma, this is equivalent to compute
T(w_{-})
, linearize the potential energy appearing in the
T(w_{-})
formula and letting
w_{-}\uparrow w_{0}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For every
w_{-}\in(0,w_{0})
,
T(w_{-})
coincides with half of the period of the periodic orbit of (
2.13
) through the point
(w_{-},0)
. Moreover,

T(w_{0}):=\lim_{w_{-}\uparrow w_{0}}T(w_{-})=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\lambda\left(1-\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}\right)}}

(2.18)

coincides with half of the period of the corresponding orbit of (
2.17
).

Proof.

According to (
2.15
),
T(w_{-})
can be expressed in terms of the potential energy
F(w)
as

T(w_{-})=\int_{w_{-}}^{w_{0}}\frac{dw}{\sqrt{2\left[F(w_{-})-F(w)\right]}}+\int_{w_{0}}^{w_{+}}\frac{dw}{\sqrt{2\left[F(w_{+})-F(w)\right]}}.

The fact that
T(w_{-})
equals half of the period of the periodic orbit of (
2.13
) through the point
(w_{-},0)
follows from the symmetry of (
2.13
) with respect to the
w
-axis.
For the rest of this proof, we set

\displaystyle T_{1}(w_{-})

\displaystyle:=\int_{w_{-}}^{w_{0}}\frac{dw}{\sqrt{2\left[F(w_{-})-F(w)\right]}},

\displaystyle T_{2}(w_{+})

\displaystyle:=\int_{w_{0}}^{w_{+}}\frac{dw}{\sqrt{2\left[F(w_{+})-F(w)\right]}}.

Then, introducing the changes of variable

w=w_{0}+\theta(w_{-}-w_{0})\quad\hbox{and}\quad w=w_{0}+\theta(w_{+}-w_{0})

in
T_{1}(w_{-})
and
T_{2}(w_{+})
, respectively, it follows that

\displaystyle T_{1}(w_{-})

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(w_{-}-w_{0})\,d\theta}{\sqrt{2\left[F(w_{-})-F(w_{0}+\theta(w_{-}-w_{0}))\right]}},

\displaystyle T_{2}(w_{+})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(w_{+}-w_{0})\,d\theta}{\sqrt{2\left[F(w_{+})-F(w_{0}+\theta(w_{+}-w_{0}))\right]}}.

Moreover, for
w_{\pm}\approx w_{0}
, the differences inside the square roots can be developed as

\begin{split}&F(w_{\pm})-F(w_{0}+\theta(w_{\pm}-w_{0}))\\
&=F(w_{\pm})-F(w_{0})+F(w_{0})-F(w_{0}+\theta(w_{\pm}-w_{0}))\\
&=F^{\prime}(w_{0})(w_{\pm}-w_{0})+\tfrac{1}{2}F^{\prime\prime}(w_{0})(w_{\pm}-w_{0})^{2}+O((w_{\pm}-w_{0})^{3})\\
&-\left(F^{\prime}(w_{0})\theta(w_{\pm}-w_{0})+\tfrac{1}{2}F^{\prime\prime}(w_{0})\theta^{2}(w_{\pm}-w_{0})^{2}+O((w_{\pm}-w_{0})^{3})\right)\\
&=(1-\theta^{2})\tfrac{1}{2}F^{\prime\prime}(w_{0})(w_{\pm}-w_{0})^{2}+O((w_{\pm}-w_{0})^{3}).\end{split}

Thus,

\begin{split}\lim_{w_{-}\uparrow w_{0}}T(w_{-})&=\lim_{w_{-}\uparrow w_{0}}T_{1}(w_{-})+\lim_{w_{+}\downarrow w_{0}}T_{2}(w_{+})\\
&=2\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{(1-\theta^{2})F^{\prime\prime}(w_{0})}}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{F^{\prime\prime}(w_{0})}}.\end{split}

(2.19)

Therefore, taking into account that

F^{\prime\prime}(w_{0})=\lambda-\frac{b\mu}{d}\frac{1}{(1+w_{0})^{2}}=\lambda\left(1-\frac{d\lambda}{b\mu}\right),

(
2.18
) holds. Finally, since (
2.17
) can be expressed as

w^{\prime\prime}+\lambda\left(1-\tfrac{b\lambda}{d\mu}\right)w=0,

it becomes apparent that
T(w_{0})
equals half of the period of the solutions of (
2.17
). The proof is complete.
∎

Thanks to the continuity of the period map, the next result follows from (
2.16
) and (
2.18
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
\mu>\mu_{\kappa}
for some integer
\kappa\geq 1
. Then, for every
\lambda\in(\lambda_{\kappa}^{-},\lambda_{\kappa}^{+})
, the problem (
2.2
) possesses, at least, two positive
(\kappa,w_{0})
-nodal solutions.

Proof.

Since
\mu>\mu_{\kappa}
, we have that
\tau_{0,\kappa}(\lambda)<0
for all
\lambda\in(\lambda_{\kappa}^{-},\lambda_{\kappa}^{+})
. Thus, thanks
to (
2.5
), we find that

(\kappa\pi)^{2}-\lambda\left(1-\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}\right)<0\quad\hbox{for all}\;\;\lambda\in(\lambda_{\kappa}^{-},\lambda_{\kappa}^{+}).

Consequently, thanks to Lemma
2.2
,

\kappa T(w_{0})=\frac{\kappa\pi}{\sqrt{\lambda\left(1-\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}\right)}}<1\quad\hbox{for all}\;\;\lambda\in(\lambda_{\kappa}^{-},\lambda_{\kappa}^{+}).

(2.20)

Hence, by (
2.16
), there exists
w_{-}\in(0,w_{0})
such that

\kappa T(w_{-})=1.

By construction, the unique solution of the Cauchy problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-w^{\prime\prime}=\lambda w-\frac{b\mu}{d}\frac{w}{1+w},\\[2.0pt]
w(0)=w_{-},\;\;w^{\prime}(0)=0,\end{array}\right.

denoted by
w_{\kappa}
, provides us with a positive
(\kappa,w_{0})
-nodal solution of (
2.2
). As (
2.2
)
is autonomous, considering the extension of
w_{\kappa}(x)
to
[0,2]
,

\hat{w}_{\kappa}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}w_{\kappa}(x)&\quad\hbox{if}\;\,x\in[0,1],\\[4.0pt]
w_{\kappa}(2-x)&\quad\hbox{if}\;\,x\in(1,2],\end{array}\right.

the shift

\tilde{w}_{\kappa}(x):=\hat{w}_{\kappa}(x+\kappa^{-1}),\qquad x\in[0,1],

(2.21)

provides us with another positive
(\kappa,w_{0})
-nodal solution of (
2.2
). Note that
w_{\kappa}\neq\tilde{w}_{\kappa}
because

\tilde{w}_{\kappa}(0)=w_{\kappa}(\kappa^{-1})\equiv w_{+}>w_{0}>w_{-}=w_{\kappa}(0).

This argument shows that positive
(\kappa,w_{0})
-nodal solutions always arise by pairs, and ends the proof.
∎

However, to make precise the number and location of the non-constant positive solutions, some monotonicity properties of the period map are needed. Those are direct consequences of the next result of technical nature.

Lemma 2.3
.

The kinetic of the differential equation of (
2.2
),

f(w):=\lambda w-\frac{b\mu}{d}\frac{w}{1+w},

is an
A-B
-function, as discussed in Definition 1.4.1 of Schaaf
[
29
]
. Thus,
T(w_{-})
is decreasing with respect to
w_{-}
, i.e.

T^{\prime}(w_{-})<0\quad\hbox{for all}\;\;w_{-}\in(0,w_{0}),

(2.22)

where
′
stands for derivation with respect to
w_{-}
.

Proof.

Let us prove that
f
is an A-B-function in the interval
J=[0,w_{h}]
, where
w_{h}>0
is the unique positive zero of the potential energy
F(w)
. Since

f^{\prime}(w)=\lambda-\tfrac{b\mu}{d(1+w)^{2}}\quad\hbox{for all}\;\;w\in J,

setting
\alpha:=\sqrt{\tfrac{b\mu}{d\lambda}}-1
, it becomes apparent that

f^{\prime}(w)\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}<0&\quad\hbox{if}\;\,w\in[0,\alpha),\\[3.0pt]
=0&\quad\hbox{if}\;\,w=\alpha,\\[3.0pt]
>0&\quad\hbox{if}\;\,w>\alpha.\end{array}\right.

According to Definition 1.4.1 of Schaaf
[
29
]
,
f
is an A-B function if

•

f^{\prime}
has only simple zeros on
J
;

•

f
is an A-function for
w>\alpha
, i.e.

f^{\prime}(w)f^{\prime\prime\prime}(w)-\tfrac{5}{3}(f^{\prime\prime}(w))^{2}<0\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;w>\alpha;

•

f
is a B-function for
w\in[0,\alpha)
, i.e.

f(w)f^{\prime\prime}(w)-3(f^{\prime}(w))^{2}\leq 0\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;w\in[0,\alpha].

Since

f^{\prime\prime}(w)=\frac{2b\mu}{d(1+w)^{3}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad f^{\prime\prime\prime}(w)=-\frac{6b\mu}{d(1+w)^{4}}\;\;\hbox{for all}\;\;w>0,

we find that
f^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)>0
and

f^{\prime}(w)f^{\prime\prime\prime}(w)-\frac{5}{3}(f^{\prime\prime}(w))^{2}=-\frac{6b\mu\lambda}{d(1+w)^{4}}+(6-\tfrac{20}{3})\frac{b^{2}\mu^{2}}{d^{2}(1+w)^{6}}<0

for all
w>0
; in particular, for all
w>\alpha
. Thus, it remains to check that
f
is a B-function. Indeed, by the definition of
\alpha
,

\frac{1}{1+\alpha}=\sqrt{\frac{d\lambda}{b\mu}},

and, since we are taking
\lambda<\tfrac{b\mu}{d}
, it becomes apparent that, for every
w\in[0,\alpha]
,

\displaystyle f(w)f^{\prime\prime}(w)-3(f^{\prime}(w))^{2}

\displaystyle<f(w)f^{\prime\prime}(w)=w\left(\lambda-\tfrac{b\mu}{d}\tfrac{1}{1+w}\right)\tfrac{2b\mu}{d(1+w)^{3}}

\displaystyle\leq w\left(\lambda-\tfrac{b\mu}{d}\tfrac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)\tfrac{2b\mu}{d(1+w)^{3}}

\displaystyle=w\sqrt{\lambda}\left(\sqrt{\lambda}-\sqrt{\tfrac{b\mu}{d}}\right)\tfrac{2b\mu}{d(1+w)^{3}}<0.

Therefore,
f(w)
is an A-B-function. Consequently, by Theorem 2.1.3 of Schaaf
[
29
]
(see also Opial
[
27
]
), (
2.22
) holds. The proof is complete.
∎

(n,w_{0})
-nodal solutions,

This section analyzes the local bifurcation of
(n,w_{0})
-nodal solutions from the constant solution curve
\mathscr{C}_{0}
. Although, according to the phase plane analysis of Section
2
, we already know that the loops of
(n,w_{0})
-nodal solutions,
\mathscr{C}_{n}
, bifurcate supercritically from
\lambda_{n}^{-}
and subcritically from
\lambda_{n}^{+}
, the combined use of the bifurcation theorem by Crandall–Rabinowitz,
[
5
]
, together with the Exchange Stability Principle
[
6
]
, provides us, in addition, with the dimensions of the unstable manifolds of these solutions, which are needed to obtain the appropriate non-degeneration conditions for getting the coexistence states of (
1.3
) perturbing from the solutions of (
1.4
).

To perform the local bifurcation analysis from the positive constant solution curve
w_{0}
, we make the change of variables

u:=w-w_{0}

in (
1.4
), transforming
w_{0}
into
0
. Then, instead of looking for positive
(n,w_{0})
-nodal solutions of (
1.4
), we are searching for
n
-nodal solutions of the equivalent problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u^{\prime\prime}=\lambda u\left(1-\frac{d\lambda}{b\mu}\frac{1}{\frac{d\lambda}{b\mu}u+1}\right)\quad\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[7.0pt]
u^{\prime}(0)=u^{\prime}(1)=0.\end{array}\right.

(3.1)

Since
w\geq 0
, we have that
u\geq-w_{0}
and, hence,

\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}u+1\geq 1-w_{0}\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}=1-\left(\tfrac{b\mu}{d\lambda}-1\right)\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}=\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}>0.

Thus, the right-hand side of (

) is analytic in
\lambda
and
u
.

3.1
Local bifurcations from
(\lambda,u)=(\lambda_{n}^{\pm},0)
,
n\geq 1
.

Subsequently, for any given integer
j\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}_{N}^{j}[0,1]
the Banach space of the functions
u:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
of class
\mathcal{C}^{j}
in
[0,1]
such that
u^{\prime}(0)=u^{\prime}(1)=0
with the norm of
\mathcal{C}^{j}[0,1]
. Then, the solutions of (
3.1
) are given by the zeroes of the operator

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}\,:(0,\tfrac{b\mu}{d})\,\times\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{N}^{2}[0,1]\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}[0,1]

\displaystyle\quad(\lambda,u)\mapsto\mathscr{F}(\lambda,u):=-u^{\prime\prime}-\lambda u\Big(1-\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}\tfrac{1}{\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}u+1}\Big),

which is an analytic Fredholm operator that can be decomposed as

\mathscr{F}(\lambda,u)=\mathscr{L}(\lambda)u+\mathscr{N}(\lambda,u),

where

\mathscr{L}(\lambda)u:=D_{u}\mathscr{F}(\lambda,0)u=-u^{\prime\prime}-\lambda\left(1-\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}\right)u

(3.2)

is a linear Fredholm operator of index
0
, and
\mathscr{N}(\lambda,u)
is a nonlinear operator satisfying

\mathscr{N}(\lambda,u)=o(\|u\|)\quad\hbox{as}\;\;\|u\|\to 0.

The null-space
N[\mathscr{L}(\lambda)]
is the linear space generated by the nontrivial solutions of the linear problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}-\varphi^{\prime\prime}=\lambda\left(1-\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}\right)\varphi\quad\hbox{in}\;\;(0,1),\\[7.0pt]
\varphi^{\prime}(0)=\varphi^{\prime}(1),\end{array}\right.

(3.3)

in
(0,1),

and the generalized spectrum of
\mathscr{L}(\lambda)
is given by

\Sigma(\mathscr{L}):=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\;:\;\dim N[\mathscr{L}(\lambda)]\geq 1\}.

Recalling (
2.5
), we have that, for every integer
\ell\geq 0
,

\lambda\left(1-\tfrac{d\lambda}{b\mu}\right)=(\ell\pi)^{2}-\tau_{0,\ell}(\lambda).

(3.4)

Thus, since
(\ell\pi)^{2}
,
\ell\geq 0
, provides us with the set of eigenvalues of
-D^{2}
in
(0,1)

under Neumann boundary conditions, it becomes apparent that

\Sigma[\mathscr{L}(\lambda)]=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\;:\;\tau_{0,\ell}(\lambda)=0\;\;\hbox{for some integer}\;\ell\geq 0\}.

Consequently, according to the analysis of Section
2
, we find that
\Sigma[\mathscr{L}(\lambda)]=\{0\}
if
\mu\in(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
, and, whenever
\mu\in(\mu_{\kappa},\mu_{\kappa+1})
for some integer
\kappa\geq 1
,

\Sigma[\mathscr{L}]:=\{\lambda_{n}^{\pm}\;:\;1\leq n\leq\kappa\}\quad\hbox{and}\quad N[\mathscr{L}(\lambda_{n}^{\pm})]=\mathrm{span\,}[\cos(n\pi x)]

(3.5)

for all
n\in\{1,\dots,\kappa\}
.

) in a left-neighborhood of
\lambda_{n}^{+}

The proof of Part (b) relies on the analyticity of the eigenvalues
\tau_{n,\ell}(\lambda)
with respect to

\lambda
for each pair of integers
n\geq 0
and
\ell\geq 0
. This is obvious for

. So, suppose

and note that the potential energy introduced in (

),

is analytic with respect to
\lambda
and
w>-1
. Thus, also is analytic the function

where
0<w_{n,-}<w_{0}<w_{n,+}
are the unique values of
w
satisfying

\mathscr{E}(w_{n},z)=F(\lambda,w_{n,\pm}).

Moreover, since
G(\lambda,w_{n,\pm})=0
and

\frac{\partial G}{\partial w}(\lambda,w_{n,\pm})=\frac{\partial F}{\partial w}(\lambda,w_{n,\pm})=\lambda w_{n,\pm}-\frac{b\mu}{d}\frac{w_{n,\pm}}{1+w_{n,\pm}}\neq 0,

by the Implicit Function Theorem,
w_{n,\pm}=w_{n,\pm}(\lambda)
is analytic with respect to
\lambda
. Therefore, since either
w_{n}(0)=w_{n,-}(\lambda)
, or
w_{n}(0)=w_{n,+}(\lambda)
, are the initial values of the underlying Cauchy problem, by Peano’s Differentiability Theorem, we can infer that the
(n,w_{0})
-nodal solution
w_{n}:=w_{n}(\cdot,\lambda)
also is an analytic function of
\lambda
in
\mathcal{C}_{N}^{2}[0,1]
. Therefore, by a classical result on perturbation from simple eigenvalues of Kato
[
11
]
, it follows from (
3.25
) that, for every integer
n\geq 1
and any
(n,w_{0})
-nodal solution,
w_{n}
, of (
2.2
),
\tau_{n,\ell}(\lambda)
also is an analytic function of
\lambda\in(\lambda_{n}^{-},\lambda_{n}^{+})
.

According to Part (a), we already know that

\tau_{n,n-1}(\lambda)<0<\tau_{n,n}(\lambda)

for
\lambda>\lambda_{n}^{-}
sufficiently close, and
\lambda<\lambda_{n}^{+}
sufficiently close. In particular,
(
3.28
) holds for these
\lambda

\tau_{n,n-1}(\lambda)
and

\tau_{n,n}(\lambda)
vanish, at most, at finitely many points in the interval
(\lambda_{n}^{-},\lambda_{n}^{+})
.
Moreover, (
3.28
) must be respected for all
\lambda\in(\lambda_{n}^{-},\lambda_{n}^{+})
, as, otherwise, either

\tau_{n,n-1}(\lambda)
, or
\tau_{n,n}(\lambda)
, must change sign in
(\lambda_{n}^{-},\lambda_{n}^{+})
, and this is impossible. Indeed, suppose that, e.g.,
\tau_{n,n-1}(\lambda)
changes sign at some
\lambda_{0}\in(\lambda_{n}^{-},\lambda_{n}^{+})
. Then, by analyticity, there exists an odd
\nu=2m+1\geq 1
such that

\frac{{\rm d}^{\nu}\tau_{n,n-1}}{{\rm d}\lambda^{\nu}}(\lambda_{0})\neq 0.

Thus, according to Theorem 4.4.3 of
[
21
]
, the generalized algebraic multiplicity
of
[
16
]
(going back to Esquinas and López-Gómez
[
10
]
for transversal eigenvalues), satisfies

\chi[\mathscr{L}_{n}(\lambda);\lambda_{0}]=2m+1\in 2\mathbb{N}+1,

where

\mathscr{L}_{n}(\lambda):=-D^{2}-\lambda+\tfrac{b\mu}{d}\tfrac{1}{(1+w_{n}(\lambda))^{2}}.

Therefore, by Theorem 4.3.4 of
[
16
]
, a bifurcation to non-constant positive solutions of (
1.4
) from
(\lambda,w)=(\lambda_{0},w_{n})
occurs, which contradicts the uniqueness established by Theorem

and ends the proof of Part (b). Finally, Part (c) holds by analyticity.
∎
