Reply of the authors: Response to Letter to the Editor
Authors: Peter Lindner, Kerry Flannagan, Howard J Li, Jerry Wang, Luis R Hoyos, Meike Uhler, Michael Homer, Kate Devine, Micah Hill, Phillip Romanski
Published: 2026

We appreciate Dr. Lamba’s thoughtful commentary on our recent article evaluating live birth outcomes following euploid frozen embryo transfer in women aged >35 years using either autologous or donor oocytes (1). As highlighted in the letter, the primary objective of our study was to explore whether the well-established decline in reproductive outcomes with advancing maternal age persists even when analysis is restricted to embryos confirmed to have a normal chromosome copy number through preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A).

In our multicenter cohort, we observed that live birth declined with increasing maternal age among patients undergoing transfer of autologous euploid embryos, whereas outcomes remained relatively stable when donor oocyte euploid embryos were used. These findings support the concept that reproductive aging influences outcomes through mechanisms that extend beyond the increasing prevalence of embryonic aneuploidy alone. While PGT-A addresses chromosome abnormalities of the embryo, it does not fully account for other age-related changes in oocyte biology that may influence embryo developmental potential and implantation success.

The Letter-to-the-Editor appropriately highlights several potential biological explanations, including mitochondrial dysfunction, cytoplasmic alterations, and epigenetic changes that may accumulate with advancing oocyte age. These factors likely contribute to differences in developmental competence that may persist even in a euploid embryo. Therefore, our findings reinforce the multifactorial nature of reproductive aging and suggest that euploid status, although an important determinant of success, does not entirely mitigate age-related declines in reproductive potential.

We also agree that retrospective analyses must be interpreted with appropriate caution. As noted in our discussion, variations in laboratory conditions, embryo assessment, and evolving assisted reproductive technology practices over time may introduce heterogeneity that cannot be controlled fully in observational datasets. However, several of these concerns were considered in our analysis, as baseline characteristics between study groups were comparable, including embryo grade, day of blastulation, endometrial thickness, and the proportion of embryo transfers performed with or without a corpus luteum. Future prospective studies incorporating additional markers of embryo competence, such as morphokinetic parameters, metabolic profiling, and improved assessments of endometrial receptivity, may further clarify the mechanisms underlying these observations.

From a clinical standpoint, the findings underscore an important counseling consideration. Although PGT-A reduces the impact of embryonic aneuploidy, maternal age remains a relevant prognostic factor when patients pursue treatment with their own oocytes. Communicating this nuance may help patients develop realistic expectations regarding treatment outcomes while preserving the clear value that PGT-A can provide in appropriate clinical contexts while emphasizing the utility of donor oocyte as a treatment option.

We thank Dr. Lamba for contributing to the ongoing discussion surrounding reproductive aging and embryo competence and appreciate the opportunity to further contextualize our findings.
