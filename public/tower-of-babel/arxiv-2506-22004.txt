GKNet: Graph Kalman Filtering and Model Inference via Model-based Deep Learning
Mohammad Sabbaqi, Riccardo Taormina, Elvin Isufi

Abstract
Inference tasks with time series over graphs are of importance in applications such as urban water networks, economics, and networked neuroscience. Addressing these tasks typically relies on identifying a computationally affordable model that jointly captures the graph-temporal patterns of the data. In this work, we propose a graph-aware state space model for graph time series, where both the latent state and the observation equation are parametric graph-induced models with a limited number of parameters that need to be learned. More specifically, we consider the state equation to follow a stochastic partial differential equation driven by noise over the graphs’ edges accounting not only for potential edge uncertainties but also for increasing the degrees of freedom in the latter in a tractable manner. The graph structure conditioning of the noise dispersion allows the state variable to deviate from the stochastic process in certain neighborhoods. The observation model is a sampled and graph-filtered version of the state capturing multi-hop neighboring influence. The goal is to learn the parameters in both state and observation models from the partially observed data for downstream tasks such as prediction and imputation. The model is inferred first through a maximum likelihood approach that provides theoretical tractability but is limited in expressivity and scalability. To improve on the latter, we use the state-space formulation to build a principled deep learning architecture that jointly learns the parameters and tracks the state in an end-to-end manner in the spirit of Kalman neural networks. The proposed models are evaluated on controlled and four real-world scenarios, showing the effectiveness of the proposed approach when the model lacks training data. Finally, we conduct a case study on water networks, where the proposed model is used to predict the water levels in the network when the data is extremely scarce.



I
Introduction

Inference of time-varying signals over graphs plays a key role in network-based systems to interpolate missing values, extrapolate a certain horizon, and detect anomalies, to name a few
[
kolaczyk_statistical_2014
,
ortega_overview_2018
,
marisca_learning_2022
,
yi_fouriergnn_nodate
,
li_deep_2023
]
.
This is a challenging problem because the relatio-temporal coupling in the data needs to be exploited in a computationally affordable manner due to the problem dimensions involving large graphs and long time horizons.
The challenge increases when the graph is imperfect, which is often the case in almost all physical networks (water, power, transport) but also when the said graph is estimated from a finite amount of data
[
giannakis_topology_2018
,
dong_learning_2019
,
mateos_connecting_2019
,
buciulea_learning_2022
,
shen_kernel_2017
]
.
The impact of the topological uncertainties has been studied in different settings including graph filtering
[
isufi_graphfilters_2024
]
, graph signal processing
[
ceci_graph_2020
]
, graph neural networks
[
sabbaqi_gtcnn
,
gao_learning_2023
,
gao_stochastic_2021
,
gao_stability_2021
]
, and PDE-based graph machine learning
[
chamberlain_grand_2021
,
song_robustness_2022
]
.

A natural way to infer time-varying signals on graphs is to extend graph kernels
[
smola_kernels_2003
]
into a relatio-temporal form, where a separable kernel is favored due to computational aspects
[
romero_kernelbased_2017
,
lu_probabilistic_2021
]
.
Another approach is to design a latent space model where independent Gaussian processes pass through a graph filter to account for both the temporal and the spatial connections in the data
[
lu_spatiotemporal_2021
,
zhi_gaussian_2023
]
.
The work in
[
nikitin_non_2022
]
also builds around Gaussian processes but designs a joint (non-separable) kernel based on stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs).
The latter is more interpretable and expressive than the separable kernels, but it is computationally heavier.
In general, these statistical approaches are limited in scalability, and rely on statistical assumptions that may not hold.

Another approach to model time-varying signals over graphs is to use temporal variants of graph neural networks (GNNs).
One stream of works focuses on sequentially combining GNNs with recurrent neural networks or their variants
[
jin_survey_2024
]
.
Another stream of works, related to this paper, designs graph neural networks that can capture both relational and temporal dependencies simultaneously.
For example, the authors in
[
ruiz_gated_2020
]
insert graph convolutions within a GRU architecture to consider spatial connections while exploiting temporal patterns.
The work in
[
sabbaqi_gtcnn
]
propose an architecture performing on a product graph of spatial connections and temporal dependencies.
The main drawback of these approaches is that they require a large amount of data to learn the model parameters, and treat the graph structure as solely the domain where graph convolutions are performed, ultimately, limiting the interpretability of the process dynamics.

To overcome the limitations of the above approaches, one needs to combine the interpretability and reliability of statistical approaches with the flexibility and scalability of machine learning approaches.
Model-based deep learning
[
shlezinger_model-based_2022
,
shlezinger_model-based_2023
]
is a promising direction in this regard, which aims to develop data-driven solutions where particular models or respective optimization problems are used as inductive biases to design principled architectures that allow a degree of tractability
[
hadou_robust_2023
]
.
Model-based deep learning for graphs has been proposed in
[
sabbaqi_gtfunrolling
,
chen_graph_2021
,
nagahama_graph_2022
,
chen_time_2021
,
vu_Unrolling_2021
]
.
However, these models are restricted to a certain task, typically time-invariant, and data.
The latent perspective is often key to model the time series evolution as the driving factors may not be observed
[
isufi_observing_2020
]
.
Moreover, they work either on static graph signals or short temporal windows.

State space model-based deep learning solutions are a powerful alternative in this regard.
In this context, the authors in
[
revach_kalman_2022
]
proposed KalmanNet for tracking state variable which is a deep learning architecture that learns the Kalman gain parameters via a recurrent neural network.
Later on,
[
sagi_extended_2023
]
proposed a spectral-based approach for extended Kalman filtering in non-linear graph state space models.
Based on this work, the GSP-KalmanNet
[
buchnik_gspkalman_2023
]
is proposed to track graph time-varying signals in state space models similar to KalmanNet.
However, they consider both the observation and transition models known which is rarely the case.

In this work we propose a graph-based state space model for inferring time varying signals over graphs where both the state and observation models are unknown.
This is a high-dimensional ill-posed problem, and, to make it tractable, we consider the state equation based on a graph SPDE kernel that accounts for the uncertainties in the graph structure.
The observation model is a locally graph-filtered version of the latent graph signals to prevent over-smooth predictions.
We use said parametric state space formulation to model the process evolution on two fronts.
First, both the state and observation models are learned through an optimization problem in a maximum likelihood setting alongside the latent time series.
This method is theoretically grounded and interpretable, but it is computationally heavy and lacks expressivity.
Thus, we extend the proposed algorithm into a model-based deep learning architecture with a recurrent inference model to enhance its robustness, expressive power, and scalability even in the presence of topological uncertainties and few data samples.
Our main contributions are fourth fold:

•

Graph-based state space model with uncertainties:

We propose a graph-based state space model for inferring time-varying signals over graphs that accounts for uncertainties.
This state model is inspired by topological SPDE-based kernels over graphs where the dispersion of the system noise is closely tied to the graph structure.
Then, the state passes through a graph filter to obtain the observations.

•

Learning via Kalman filter and maximum likelihood:

We propose an inference algorithm for time series over graphs.
The Kalman filter is used alternatively with a maximum likelihood estimation algorithm to learn the observation and state model parameters, as well as to infer the latent time series simultaneously, within an expectation maximization setting.

•

Model-based deep learning architecture:

We extend the proposed model into a model-based deep learning architecture to enhance its robustness, expressive power, and scalability.
To do so, we use a recurrent recognition model to learn the model parameters and topological uncertainties in an end-to-end manner.

•

Numerical experiments:

Extensive experiments on tasks such as forecasting and time series interpolation over a synthetic and four real-world datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed model.
The results show a favorable performance when compared with both statistical and machine learning approaches.

•

Water network systems case study:

A case study on water network systems is conducted to show the effectiveness of the proposed model in real-world applications beyond the benchmark datasets.
The method is modified for the case study to account for the specific characteristics of the water network systems.

II
Problem Formulation

Consider an undirected graph
{\mathcal{G}}=\{{\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{W}}\}
with the node set
{\mathcal{V}}
, the edge set
{\mathcal{E}}
, and the edge weights
{\mathcal{W}}
.
Let the graph
{\mathcal{G}}
have
|{\mathcal{V}}|=N
nodes and
|{\mathcal{E}}|=M
edges, and let it be represented by its adjacency matrix
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
.
The Laplacian matrix
{\mathbf{L}}=\textrm{diag}({\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{1}})-{\mathbf{A}}
is another representation matrix behaving similarly to a difference operator where
{\mathbf{1}}
is an all-one vector and
\textrm{diag}(\cdot)
transforms a vector into a diagonal matrix.
Matrix
{\mathbf{B}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times M}
is the node-to-edge incidence matrix of graph
{\mathcal{G}}
which indicates the nodes connected by each edge.
One can show that the graph Laplacian matrix
{\mathbf{L}}={\mathbf{B}}{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}
while
{\mathbf{L}}_{1}={\mathbf{B}}^{\top}{\mathbf{B}}
is defined as the edge Laplacian matrix.
A time series over the nodes of the graph
{\mathcal{G}}
is a sequence of data points
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
at time
t
with the
i
-th entry
x_{it}
being time series evolving over the node
i
.

Our ultimate goal is to model the time series data
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
over graph
{\mathcal{G}}
for downstream tasks such as prediction, anomaly detection, and signal reconstruction.
Under the assumption that the graph
{\mathcal{G}}
plays a role in the time series generation process, or it captures the data structure, the temporal dependencies of
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
are related to it.
In the simplest case, the time series can be generated by a Markovian process over the graph where the data at time
t
is a transformation of the data at time
t-1
constrained to the graph structure as

{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=f({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1})+{\mathbf{w}}_{t},

(1)

where
f(\cdot)
is an arbitrary function that depends on the adjacency matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
is the noise term.
Identifying function
f(\cdot)
from signal observations is the central problem in different disciplines as we elaborate next.

II-A

Problem Motivation

Modeling the time series over graphs is considered in social networks, transportation systems, and power grids where the data is collected over a network of nodes and the evolution is influenced by the network structure.
However, the observed data might not strictly follow the Markovian process in (
1
) and the evolution might be more complex.
Hence, modeling the data using such strong statistical assumptions might be insufficient to capture the underlying process dynamics.

State space models help to overcome this issue and consider the observed data
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
is a local aggregation of a latent state variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
that evolves over the graph.
The state variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
follows a Markovian process influenced by the graph structure as in (
1
).
The observation model locally combines the state variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
considering the graph structure as

{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=g({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{x}}_{t})+{\mathbf{v}}_{t},

(2)

where
g(\cdot)
is an arbitrary function and
{\mathbf{v}}_{t}
is the observation noise.

Given the observation model
g(\cdot)
and the state model
f(\cdot)
are known, the state variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
can be estimated via graph Kalman filtering.
This problem was initially addressed in
[
shi_kalman_2009
]
for state estimation in sensor networks with distributed computations.
Authors in
[
isufi_observing_2020
]
proposed the graph Kalman filter for band-limited processes over graphs given the network structure and sampled measurements.
Later on, the unscented graph Kalman filter
[
li_unscented_2023
]
and the extended graph Kalman filter
[
sagi_extended_2023
]
were proposed to handle nonlinearities in the dynamics and observation model.
Moreover, the work in
[
su_graph-frequency_2024
]
proposed a graph-frequency domain Kalman filter to enhance the tracking performance subject to outlier measurements.
However, all of these methods suffer from instability against model mismatches and require a good initialization of the state variable.
Authors in
[
buchnik_gspkalman_2023
]
proposed a model-based neural network called GSP-KalmanNet to tackle this issue by learning the Kalman gain via a recurrent neural network from the data itself.
This method is more robust to model mismatches and leverages the graph structure, to improve the estimation performance.
Yet, both the observation and state models
g(\cdot)
and
f(\cdot)
are assumed known.

Observation model.

Unlike the abovementioned works, we aim to learn both the state model
f(\cdot)
and the observation model
g(\cdot)
to map the time series data into a well-behaved latent space.
More specifically, we consider the observation model to be a linear local aggregation of the state variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
over the graph combined with the measurement noise as

{\mathbf{y}}_{t}={\mathbf{M}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K}h_{k}{\mathbf{L}}^{k}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\right)+{\mathbf{v}}_{t}={\mathbf{M}}\tilde{{\mathbf{H}}}({\mathbf{L}}){\mathbf{x}}_{t}+{\mathbf{v}}_{t},

(3)

where
\tilde{{\mathbf{H}}}({\mathbf{L}})=\sum_{k=0}^{K}h_{k}{\mathbf{L}}^{k}
is a graph convolutional filter of order
K
with coefficients
{\mathbf{h}}=[h_{0},\dots,h_{K}]^{\top}

[
isufi_graphfilters_2024
]
.
The masking matrix
{\mathbf{M}}\in\{0,1\}^{N_{o}\times N}
accounts for missing values in the observation with
N_{o}
as the number of observed nodes.
In the rest of the paper, we use
{\mathbf{H}}={\mathbf{M}}\tilde{{\mathbf{H}}}({\mathbf{L}})
as the observation model for notational convenience.

State model.

We consider the linear approximation of heat diffusion over the graph as the evolution process in the latent space as

d{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=-c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}dt

(4)

where
c
is the diffusivity factor.
This is motivated by the fact that the heat diffusion process is the simplest process over a graph and can be represented with a minimum number of parameters.
However, directly using (
4
) can be too strict as it limits the feasible space for the learnable filter parameters
{\mathbf{h}}
in the observation model.
Moreover, the evolution process relies on the exact graph structure which might not be the case in real-world applications.
To account for the uncertainties in the graph structure, and to allow for a more flexible model, we propose a state space model where the state variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
follows a SPDE-based graph state equation.
One can define the stochastic heat diffusion process over the graph as
[
nikitin_non_2022
]

d{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=-c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}dt+{\mathbf{S}}d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}
is an
F
-dimensional standard Brownian motion and
{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times F}
is the dispersion matrix that distributes the noise over the graph throughout the process.
The solution of this SPDE hovers around the deterministic heat diffusion process over graphs defined in (
4
) up to the covariance of the noise term
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}
.
The intensity of the noise term around each node is controlled by the dispersion matrix
{\mathbf{S}}
through graph and time which allows the model to be arbitrarily close to a heat diffusion process at each node independently.
This property is crucial for modeling time series over graphs as it allows the model to be more flexible and generic.

However, using a general diffusion matrix
{\mathbf{S}}
comes with drawbacks:

1.

The high degree of freedom leads to model overfitting.

2.

The interpretability of the model is lost as the system noise disperses without any structure.

3.

It is computationally expensive and memory demanding as the dimension of
{\mathbf{S}}
is dependent on the problem size.

4.

The model is non-scalable and non-transferable to other graphs as
{\mathbf{S}}
is tailored to a fixed structure.

Thus, we propose a local uncertainty model for the latent variable which considers the graph structure as an inductive bias in the noise dispersion matrix as

\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=-c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\mathrm{d}t+{\mathbf{B}}\textrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t},

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M}
is the local uncertainty vector that allows the process
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
to deviate from a diffusion process around a certain edge
m
if the corresponding
\alpha_{m}
is high.
Discretizing the continuous-time state equation (
6
) via the first-order Euler method, we get the linear state model

{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}=-c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}+{\mathbf{B}}\textrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}){\mathbf{w}}_{t},

(7)

where
{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{I}})
is the standard additive noise term.
As (
7
) suggests, a white Gaussian noise
{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
is considered over the edges of the graph where the variance of each edge is controlled by the corresponding learnable parameter
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.
Hence, the parameter
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
can be considered as the edge uncertainty vector which allows the process
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
to deviate from a heat diffusion process around a certain edge proportional to the intensity of uncertainty
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
in that edge.
This property has two main advantages: first, it provides more
degrees of freedom for estimating the observation model
{\mathbf{H}}
in (
3
) as the span of valid latent diffusions
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
is increased;
second, it allows the model to be more robust to the structural uncertainties in the graph as the corresponding uncertainty term
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is learned from the data itself.

The following remarks are in order to link the proposed state space model with Gaussian processes and to interpret the uncertainty modeling capabilities of the model.

Remark 1
.

Under initial Gaussian conditions, i.e.,
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})
, the solution of (
5
) is a Gaussian process of the form
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\sim{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{P}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t,s}({\mathbf{L}}))

[
nikitin_non_2022
]
.
The mean process
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}
is a heat diffusion on graphs
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t}=\exp(-c{\mathbf{L}}t)\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
.
The covariance function is
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t,s}({\mathbf{L}})={\mathbf{V}}{\mathbf{C}}_{t,s}{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
, where matrix
{\mathbf{C}}_{t,s}
has as
(i,j)
-th entry

[{\mathbf{C}}_{t,s}]_{ij}=\frac{[{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{S}}^{\top}{\mathbf{V}}]_{ij}}{c(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j})}(e^{-c\lambda_{i}|t-s|}-e^{-c(\lambda_{i}t+\lambda_{j}s)}).

(8)

This covariance matrix defines a relatio-temporal kernel that suppresses higher graph frequency content and imposes low-pass properties.
If the matrix
{\mathbf{S}}{\mathbf{S}}^{\top}
is analytic in
{\mathbf{L}}
, this kernel shrinks into a spectral Gaussian process over the graph
[
zhi_gaussian_2023
]
.

This parallel shows that the kernel in (
8
) has in general two main limitations.
First, considering a time series of length
T
, its complexity is of order cubic
{\mathcal{O}}(N^{3}T^{3})
, limiting its applicability to small graphs and time horizons.
Second, the Brownian motion diffuses over the network regardless of the topology via a general diffusion matrix
{\mathbf{S}}
.
We tackle the first issue by implementing the Gaussian process recursively in a state space model as in (
3
); and the second issue by matching the dispersion matrix
{\mathbf{S}}
with the graph sparsity as in (
7
).

Remark 2
.

To further shed light on the graph uncertainty modeling capabilities of (
7
), we can consider the observed graph
{\mathbf{L}}
matches the support of the true graph
{\mathbf{L}}^{\star}
which drives the diffusion, but its edge weights differ slightly because of an estimation error.
This could be seen as a relative estimation error
[
gama_stability_2020
,
sabbaqi_gtcnn
,
levie_transferability_2019
]
, and it is common in many applications involving physical networks such as water, transportation, and power networks.
This error can be modeled as

{\mathbf{L}}^{\star}={\mathbf{L}}+{\mathbf{B}}\textrm{diag}({\mathbf{e}}){\mathbf{B}}^{\top},

(9)

where
{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M}
is a standard noise modeling the uncertainties over each edge independently.
A heat diffusion model over the underlying graph
{\mathbf{L}}^{\star}
can then be written as

\textstyle\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=-c{\mathbf{L}}^{\star}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\mathrm{d}t=-c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\mathrm{d}t-c{\mathbf{B}}\textrm{diag}({\mathbf{e}}){\mathbf{B}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\mathrm{d}t,

(10)

where process
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
is the superposition of the diffusion over the observed graph
{\mathbf{L}}
and of the relative error graph
{\mathbf{B}}\textrm{diag}({\mathbf{e}}){\mathbf{B}}^{\top}
.
Note that
{\mathbf{L}}^{\star}
,
{\mathbf{L}}
, and
{\mathbf{B}}\textrm{diag}({\mathbf{e}}){\mathbf{B}}^{\top}
share the same sparsity pattern.

This SPDE is time-variant because of the term
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}={\mathbf{B}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
.
Our proposal could be seen as defining
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}
to get the linear time-invariant (LTI)-SPDE.
Although this approximation is not exact, except in the converging limit where the graph signals are smooth, it allows us to interpret the model as an uncertainty model over the edges that links this work with graph perturbation models.
The LTI-SPDE reads as

\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=-c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\mathrm{d}t+{\mathbf{B}}\textrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t},

(11)

with
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}={\mathbf{w}}\mathrm{d}t
.
This means that the Brownian motion
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}
has independent entries with different energies
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
that depend on the graph signal differences
{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
.
Thus, the uncertainty will play a bigger role on edges with a bigger signal difference.
In turn, this shows how we can generalize the heat diffusion process over graphs to allow for uncertainties in the structure.

II-B

Problem Statement

Given the partially observed data
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
over
T
consecutive timestamps, and considering the state space model

\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=-c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}+{\mathbf{B}}\textrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}){\mathbf{w}}_{t},\\
&{\mathbf{y}}_{t}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}+{\mathbf{v}}_{t}\coloneq{\mathbf{M}}{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}}({\mathbf{L}}){\mathbf{x}}_{t}+{\mathbf{v}}_{t},\end{aligned}\right.

(12)

with initial condition
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{0}},\sigma_{0}^{2}{\mathbf{I}})
, system noise
{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{I}})
, and observation noise
{\mathbf{v}}_{t}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{0}},\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})
, our goal is to estimate the filter parameters
{\mathbf{h}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K+1}
in
{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}}({\mathbf{L}})
and edge uncertainties
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M}
to recover the missing entries in the observation
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
and perform other down-stream tasks such as forecasting or anomaly detection.

The challenge here is that the latent variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
is not directly observed and a trade-off between the uncertainty in the latent space
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and the degrees of freedom for the observation model
{\mathbf{h}}
should be considered.
This problem can be linked to hidden Markov models
[
cappe_hmm_2005
]
, but the graph structure adds an extra layer of complexity.
We solve this problem in two ways.
First, by maximizing the likelihood function obtained from the solution of the state space model (
12
) through the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm as it performs comparatively well when the number of observations in (
12
) is low
[
dempster_maximum_1977
,
baum_statistical_1966
]
.
However, in some cases, the statistical approaches are limited by the linearity of the model, strong statistical assumptions on the data, and scalability.
To overcome the latter, we build on the developed EM algorithm and propose a model-based deep learning approach to address these issues at the price of theoretical guarantees and interpretability.

III
Learning Graph State-Space Model

In this section, we derive an algorithm for learning the graph state-space model parameters based on the expectation-maximization framework.

III-A

Maximum Likelihood Estimation

The joint likelihood of (
12
) is the joint probability of observations
\{{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{y}}_{T}\}
and states
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{0},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{T}\}
given the model parameters, namely, the system noise
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, the observation noise
\sigma^{2}
, the filter coefficients in
{\mathbf{h}}
, and the initial variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
.
It reads as

\displaystyle p({\mathbf{y}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{y}}_{T},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}_{0},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{T}|{\mathbf{h}},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\sigma^{2},\sigma_{0}^{2})

(13)

\displaystyle=p({\mathbf{x}}_{0}|\sigma_{0}^{2})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\mathbf{x}}_{t},{\mathbf{h}},\sigma^{2})p({\mathbf{x}}_{t}|{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),

due to the Markovian property of the state-space model.
From (
12
), the transition and observation densities can be written respectively as

\displaystyle p({\mathbf{x}}_{t}|{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}|-c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1},{\mathbf{Q}}),

(14)

\displaystyle p({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\mathbf{x}}_{t},{\mathbf{h}},\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{N}}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t},\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}}),

(15)

with system noise covariance matrix
{\mathbf{Q}}={\mathbf{B}}\textrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{2}){\mathbf{B}}^{\top}
and graph filter
{\mathbf{H}}
as in (
3
).

Maximizing the likelihood, or equivalently minimizing the negative log-likelihood
{\mathcal{L}}
, w.r.t. the model parameters (graph filter coefficients
{\mathbf{h}}
, edge uncertainties
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, observation noise
\sigma^{2}
, and initial variance
\sigma_{0}^{2}
) is the goal of the learning algorithm to fit the best state space model into the data.
The negative log-likelihood has the closed-form expression

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=\frac{NT}{2}\log\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|{\mathbf{y}}_{t}-{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{T}{2}\log|{\mathbf{Q}}|+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}+c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1})^{\top}{\mathbf{Q}}^{-1}({\mathbf{x}}_{t}+c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1})

\displaystyle+\frac{N}{2}\log\sigma_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}\|{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}.

(16)

Here, the first line is a data fidelity term, whereas the second line measures how well the state variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
fits a diffusion process up to an edge-wise uncertainty term
{\mathbf{Q}}
, finally, the last line is the term for the initial state and will be fixed to avoid numerical instability.
We initialize the state as the Normal distribution for simplicity, however, one can use the Riccati solution for more accurate initial conditions
[
borovych_data-driven_2022
]
.
Notice that the likelihood function
{\mathcal{L}}
is differentiable and can be minimized via gradient-based methods after inferring the latent state variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
.

III-B

Expectation-Maximization (EM) Algorithm

To minimize (
16
), we consider the EM algorithm, which is a widely-used two-step iterative algorithm for finding the maximum likelihood estimates of parameters in statistical models in the presence of latent variables
[
dempster_maximum_1977
,
baum_statistical_1966
]
.
The EM algorithm maximizes a lower bound on the likelihood function given any distribution of the latent variables.
Then, it iteratively refines the distribution of the latent variables (E-step) and the model parameters (M-step) until convergence.

E-step:

Given the model parameters
{\Theta}=\{{\mathbf{h}},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\sigma^{2}\}
and a batch of observations
\{{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, the state estimation is the minimum-variance Bayesian inference of the state-space model.
This solution can be derived via Kalman smoothing
[
kalman_new_1961
]
.

Filtering.

The Kalman filtering is a closed-form solution of Bayesian inference of states given causal observations.
The algorithm is as follows:

\displaystyle\texttt{prediction}\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{-}=-c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}\\
&{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-}=c^{2}{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{P}}^{+}_{t-1}{\mathbf{L}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{Q}}\end{aligned}\right.

(17)

\displaystyle\texttt{correction}\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\mathbf{K}}_{t}={\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}({\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}\\
&{\mathbf{x}}^{+}_{t}={\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{-}+{\mathbf{K}}_{t}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}-{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{-})\\
&{\mathbf{P}}^{+}_{t}=({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{K}}_{t}{\mathbf{H}}){\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-}\end{aligned}\right.

(18)

where
{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
is the Kalman gain and incorporates the information from a new observation in the raw prediction considering the Bayesian structure of the state-space model.

{\mathbf{P}}_{t}=\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}_{t}{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{\top}]
is the covariance matrix of the state variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
.
For the sake of simplicity, we use
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
instead of
\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{x}}_{t}]
to indicate the mathematical expectation of the state variable.

Smoothing.

The Kalman smoothing moves backward over the trajectory and re-corrects the Kalman filter estimations having the information from all the observed data in the batch.
The equations for the Kalman smoother are

\displaystyle{\mathbf{J}}_{t-1}=-c{\mathbf{P}}^{+}_{t-1}{\mathbf{L}}({\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-})^{-1}

(19)

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}={\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}+{\mathbf{J}}_{t-1}(\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}+c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1})

(20)

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t-1}={\mathbf{P}}_{t-1}^{+}+{\mathbf{J}}_{t-1}(\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t}-{\mathbf{P}}_{t-1}^{+}){\mathbf{J}}_{t-1}^{\top}

(21)

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbf{V}}}_{t-1}={\mathbf{P}}_{t-1}^{+}{\mathbf{J}}_{t-2}^{\top}+{\mathbf{J}}_{t-1}(\hat{{\mathbf{V}}}_{t}+c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{P}}_{t-1}^{+}){\mathbf{J}}_{t-2}^{\top}

(22)

where
\hat{(\cdot)}=\mathbb{E}[(\cdot)|\{{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}]
,
{\mathbf{J}}_{t}
is the smoothing gain helping the backward recursion over mean
{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}
and covariance
{\hat{\mathbf{P}}}_{t}
, and
{\hat{\mathbf{V}}}_{t}=\text{cov}[{\mathbf{x}}_{t},{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}|\{{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}]
is the covariance of the estimated state variable
{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}
between two consecutive time instants which is required for the computation of the intermediate distribution in the EM algorithm.

Having the state variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
and its covariance matrix
{\mathbf{P}}_{t}
, the intermediate distribution of the likelihood can be computed in the E-step and used in the M-step to update the model parameters as we discuss next.

M-step:

To apply EM on (
16
), the expectation of the negative log-likelihood conditioned to observations is required.
This intermediate distribution can be computed as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[{\mathcal{L}}|\{{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}]

(23)

\displaystyle=\frac{NT}{2}\log\sigma^{2}+\frac{T}{2}\log|{\mathbf{Q}}|

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\text{tr}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\mathbf{y}}_{t}{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{\top}-{\mathbf{H}}\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{\top}-{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}+{\mathbf{H}}\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left({\mathbf{Q}}^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t}+c{\mathbf{L}}\hat{{\mathbf{V}}}_{t}+c\hat{{\mathbf{V}}}_{t}{\mathbf{L}}^{\top}+c^{2}{\mathbf{L}}\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t-1}{\mathbf{L}}^{\top}\right).

To minimize
{\mathcal{Q}}
w.r.t. the model parameters, one can compute the gradients of (
23
).
The model parameters can be obtained via closed-form solution of the gradients or be updated using gradient-based optimization methods.

The EM algorithm iterates between the E-step, and the M-step until convergence.
The computational complexity of the E-step in each iteration is of order
{\mathcal{O}}(TN^{3})
due to the inverse matrix operation computation of the Kalman gain in (
18
) where
N
is the number of nodes in the graph.
The M-step is computationally less expensive, and it is governed by
N
-by-
N
matrix-matrix products in each timestamp, hence, the computations can be done in
{\mathcal{O}}(TN^{2})
.

Figure 1
:
Recurrent Graph Kalman Network block diagram.
Encoder: takes the observations as input and transfer them into the latent domain.
The Kalman module: updates the data in the state space assuming a diffusion process over the graph.
Inference module: estimates the parameters of the Kalman module given the observations indirectly and a hidden recurrent layer.
Decoder: receives the output of Kalman module and transfers it back to the observation domain.

The proposed Kalman-based approach is mathematically tractable and optimal, but it has three main limitations: (i) low expressivity due to the linearity of the model; (ii) strict mathematical assumptions on the data; and (iii) high computational complexity in the number of nodes.
To address these issues, we propose a novel graph Kalman network (GKNet) which is a model-based deep inference and learning framework for data on graphs.
GKNet consists of four modules.
First, there are the encoder and the decoder modules which are graph convolutional neural networks (GCNNs)
[
isufi_graphfilters_2024
]
that transform the observations into the state domain and vice-versa with a nonlinear mapping to increase the model expressivity.
Second, there are prediction and correction modules, forming the Kalman module, which are graph filters that account for the prediction and correction steps in Kalman filter and smoother, respectively. Parametrizing the Kalman gain as a graph filter in the Kalman module relaxes the statistical assumptions of the model and expands its applicability.
Finally, there is a recurrent module as an inference model estimating the parameters of the prediction and correction modules that reduces the number of parameters and recursions of the model drastically.

IV-A

Encoder and Decoder

The observation equation (
3
) connects the observations
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
to the state variable
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
via a linear graph filter
{\mathbf{H}}
.
The encoder and decoder modules are responsible for transforming the data into the latent space and vice-versa similar to the graph filter
{\mathbf{H}}
.
To preserve the graph filter principle but improve its expressivity, we consider graph convolutional neural networks (GCNNs) as the encoder and decoder modules.
A GCNN layer can be defined as

{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}^{(l+1)}=\text{ReLU}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K}h_{kl}{\mathbf{L}}^{k}{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}^{(l)}\right),\quad l=0,\dots,L-1

(24)

where the trainable parameters
{\mathbf{h}}_{l}=[h_{0l},\dots,h_{Kl}]^{\top}
are the coefficients of the graph filter in each layer
l
,
\text{ReLU}(\cdot)
is the chosen non-linear activation function, and
{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}^{(l)}
is the feature vector of the
l
-th layer
[
gama_convolution_2020
]
.

The encoder transforms the observations
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
into the state domain
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
via a graph convolutional neural network.
This module receives the observation
{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}_{t}={\mathbf{y}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
(the input is zero-padded at nodes with missing value) and returns
{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}={\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}^{(L)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
as a rough estimation of the state mean and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
as the estimation of observation noise.
Hence, the encoder function
\Phi_{\text{enc}}(\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times 2}
is defined as

[{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t}]=\Phi_{\text{enc}}({\mathbf{y}}_{t},{\mathbf{L}};{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{enc}})

(25)

where
\Phi_{\text{enc}}(\cdot)
is a GCNN with
L
layers and
F
features per layer and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{enc}}=[{\mathbf{h}}_{l}]_{l=0}^{L}
contains the learnable parameters of the GCNN.

The decoder is also a GCNN followed by a readout layer based on the application and desired targets.
The decoder’s input is the processed latent state
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
and the output is the estimation of desired targets
{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}
.
The decoder is defined as

{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}=\Phi_{\text{dec}}({\mathbf{x}}_{t},{\mathbf{L}};{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{dec}})

(26)

where
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{dec}}
contains the learnable parameters of
\Phi_{\text{dec}}(\cdot)
,
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{(0)}={\mathbf{x}}_{t}
is the input, and
{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}={\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{(L)}
is the output.

IV-B

Kalman Module

The Kalman module accounts for the statistical computations done via the Kalman filter inside the state space.
The module consists of two main parts: prediction and update.
The prediction sub-module estimates the state variable’s statistics based on the previous state and the edge uncertainty vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
while the update sub-module corrects this estimation based on the observation
1
1

1

For the sake of simplicity, the Kalman smoothing is removed in the presented neural-aided model.
However, it can be added explicitly in the Kalman module where the matrix
{\mathbf{J}}_{t}
is a graph filter with parameters derived from the inference module.
Implicitly, the reverse recursions can be accounted for the inference module by using a bi-directional recurrent network.
.

Prediction sub-module.

This module accounts for the prediction step in Kalman filter in (
17
).
Given the diffusivity factor
c
and edge uncertainties
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, this module predicts the mean
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{-}
and covariance matrix
{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-}
of the state variable based on Equation (
17
).
The initial state
({\mathbf{x}}_{t-1},{\mathbf{P}}_{t-1})
is either provided by the previous recurrent layer or initialized by
({\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{P}}_{0})=({\mathbf{0}},{\mathbf{I}})
.
Thus, this module can be defined as

({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{-},{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-})=\Psi_{\text{pred}}(({\mathbf{x}}_{t-1},{\mathbf{P}}_{t-1}),{\mathbf{L}};c,\boldsymbol{\alpha})

(27)

where
\Psi_{\text{pred}}(\cdot)
recurrently predicts the state variable based on Kalman equations (
17
).

Correction sub-module.

This accounts for the update steps of Kalman filter in (
18
).
We replace the Kalman gain
{\mathbf{K}}_{t}
with a graph filter
{\hat{\mathbf{K}}}_{t}({\mathbf{L}},\sigma^{2})
and define the correction module as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}_{t}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{-}+{\hat{\mathbf{K}}}_{t}({\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}-{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{-})

(28)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}_{t}

\displaystyle=({\mathbf{I}}-{\hat{\mathbf{K}}}_{t}){\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-}

(29)

where
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{-}
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-}
are the predicted mean and covariance matrix of the state variable from the prediction module,
{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}
is the output of the encoder, and
{\hat{\mathbf{K}}}_{t}
is a graph filter approximating the Kalman gain
{\mathbf{K}}_{t}
.
This graph filter aims to mimic the Kalman gain
{\mathbf{K}}_{t}
in a scalable and data efficient manner with fewer parameters.

To justify the module, assume the encoder has estimated the graph filter
{\mathbf{H}}
well and
{\mathbf{y}}_{t}={\mathbf{H}}{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}
holds. Then we can write

{\mathbf{x}}_{t}={\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{-}+{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}({\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-}{\mathbf{H}}^{\top}+{\sigma}^{2}{\mathbf{I}})^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}({\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}-{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{-}).

(30)

As the graph filter
{\mathbf{H}}
is a function of
{\mathbf{L}}
, the update equation can be approximated with a graph filter up to the Eigenspace mismatch between
{\mathbf{L}}
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-}
as

{\mathbf{x}}_{t}={\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{-}+{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-}{\hat{\mathbf{K}}}_{t}({\mathbf{L}},\sigma^{2})({\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}-{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{-})

(31)

where
{\hat{\mathbf{K}}}_{t}(\cdot)
is a function of the graph Laplacian and the observation noise variance.
We consider a graph filter as
{\hat{\mathbf{K}}}_{t}({\mathbf{L}},\sigma^{2})=\sum_{k=0}^{K}h_{kt}(\sigma^{2}){\mathbf{L}}^{k}
where the parameters
h_{kt}(\sigma^{2})
are dependent on the estimated observation noise variance.
Similarly, the update equation for the covariance matrix can be rewritten as

{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{+}=({\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-}{\hat{\mathbf{K}}}_{t}({\mathbf{L}},\sigma^{2})){\mathbf{P}}_{t}^{-}.

(32)

IV-C

Inference Module

To estimate the parameters in the predictions and correction modules, one can unroll the Kalman equations and learn the parameters in an end-to-end manner.
However, this limits the model to a fixed temporal window
T
, high computational complexity, and linear time-invariant systems.
The inference module encodes the parameters of the prediction and correction modules into the state of a recurrent neural network (RNN) that is responsible to capture temporal behavior of the state variable through its model parameters.
The recurrent unit is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}_{t}=\text{ReLU}(\text{BN}({\mathbf{U}}_{in}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t})+{\mathbf{U}}_{z}{\mathbf{h}}_{t-1})

\displaystyle{\hat{\mathbf{h}}}_{t}=\text{ReLU}(\text{BN}({\mathbf{U}}_{out}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t})+{\mathbf{U}}_{h}{\mathbf{h}}_{t-1})

\displaystyle{\mathbf{h}}_{t}={\mathbf{z}}_{t}\odot{\mathbf{h}}_{t-1}+(1-{\mathbf{z}}_{t})\odot{\hat{\mathbf{h}}}_{t}

(33)

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid activation function,
\text{BN}(\cdot)
is the batch normalization,
\odot
is the element-wise multiplication,
and
{\mathbf{U}}_{in}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\tilde{K}\times N}
,
{\mathbf{U}}_{out}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\tilde{K}\times N}
,
{\mathbf{U}}_{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\tilde{K}\times\tilde{K}}
, and
{\mathbf{U}}_{h}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\tilde{K}\times\tilde{K}}
are the learnable parameters of the RNN where
\tilde{K}
is the number of parameters in Kalman module.

The Kalman gain filter
{\hat{\mathbf{K}}}_{t}
is completely defined via its coefficients
{\mathbf{h}}_{t}^{(0)}=[h_{0t}^{(0)},\dots,h_{Kt}^{(0)}]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K+1}
, and the diffusivity factor
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
is a scalar.
However, the edge uncertainties
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M}
increase with graph size and can be limiting for model scalability.
To address this issue, we consider the edge uncertainties
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
to be the output of an innovation process with an edge convolutional filter
{\mathbf{H}}_{e}({\mathbf{L}}_{1})
defined as

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}h_{kt}({\mathbf{B}}^{\top}{\mathbf{B}})^{k}{\mathbf{w}}_{t}={\mathbf{H}}_{e}({\mathbf{L}}_{1}){\mathbf{w}}_{t},

(34)

where
{\mathbf{H}}_{e}({\mathbf{L}}_{1})=\sum_{k=0}^{K}h_{kt}({\mathbf{B}}^{\top}{\mathbf{B}})^{k}
is an edge convolutional filter of order
K
operating on edge Laplacian
{\mathbf{L}}_{1}={\mathbf{B}}^{\top}{\mathbf{B}}
,
{\mathbf{h}}_{t}=[h_{0t},\dots,h_{K_{1}t}]^{\top}
are trainable filter coefficients, and
{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
is the innovation process input.
The input signal
{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
can be any known signal and holds prior knowledge about the edge uncertainties.
For example,
{\mathbf{w}}_{t}={\mathbf{B}}^{\top}{\mathbf{d}}
, where
{\mathbf{d}}
is the degree vector of the graph, is a prior enforcing the edges connected to the nodes with higher degrees more susceptible to uncertainty which is tied to relative perturbations of graphs.
Note that in the GKNet, the edge uncertainties
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}
are varying over time, unlike the EM-based approach which needed them static over time for the time invariance of the system.

In summary, the inference module computes the parameters required in the Kalman module
{\mathbf{h}}_{t}=[c_{t},{\mathbf{h}}_{t}^{(0)\top},{\mathbf{h}}_{t}^{\top}]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2K+3}
through the state of the RNN in (
33
).
The size of the RNN state
{\mathbf{h}}_{t}
is independent of the size of the graph as the number of filter coefficients in the graph filters is fixed.
However, the size of filters
{\mathbf{U}}_{in}
and
{\mathbf{U}}_{out}
are dependent on the number of nodes in the graph, as they are incorporating the observation statistics effects, indirectly computed in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t}
, into the model parameters
{\mathbf{h}}_{t}
.
Although this operation is still computationally efficient, it confines the model transferability.
To tackle this one can remove this dependency, use a global aggregation of the observation statistics, or fine-tune the model on the target graph domain.
The computational comlexity of the inference module is
{\mathcal{O}}(NT(2K+3))
due to the matrix-vector multiplications in the RNN, the encoder and decoder modules are
{\mathcal{O}}(MKFL)
, and the Kalman module is
{\mathcal{O}}(MNK)
.
Hence, the computational cost of GKNet reduced significantly compared to the EM approach which is
{\mathcal{O}}(TN^{2})
.

IV-D

Training

The loss function is inspired by the likelihood function (
16
) and enforces the state to follow an evolution over the graph.
The loss function reads as

{\mathcal{L}}_{t}=\|{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}-{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|{\mathbf{x}}_{t}+c{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}\|_{{\mathbf{Q}}^{-1}}^{2}

(35)

where
\lambda
is the regularization weight and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{A}}^{2}=(\cdot)^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}(\cdot)
is the quadratic form over the matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
.
The first term is the data fidelity term and the second term is the smoothness term enforcing the state to follow a diffusion process over the graph up to the edge uncertainty measure
{\mathbf{Q}}^{-1}
.
The inverse operation is computationally cheap as the matrix
{\mathbf{Q}}={\mathbf{B}}\textrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}){\mathbf{B}}^{\top}
only changes via the diagonal edge uncertainty term
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and the rest can be pre-computed.
It means we can write the inverse of
{\mathbf{Q}}^{-1}={\mathbf{B}}^{\dagger_{L}}\textrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-1}){\mathbf{P}}^{\dagger_{R}}
and pre-compute the left and right psuedo-inverses of
{\mathbf{B}}
as
{\mathbf{B}}^{\dagger_{L}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}^{\dagger_{R}}
, respectively
[
bapat_graphs_2010
]
.

V
Numerical Results

We evaluate GKNet in different scenarios compared with state-of-the-art and baseline alternatives.
In all experiments, the ADAM optimizer is used to train the model.
The learning rate is set to
10^{-3}
with a batch size of 32.
The training is performed for 100 epochs with early stopping based on the validation loss.
The reported results are the average of 20 runs with different random seeds.
The standard variations are omitted as they are all the same order of magnitude
{\mathcal{O}}(10^{-3})
.
Our experiments aim to provide insights into the model components by answering the following three main research questions:

RQ.1
:

How does the proposed model perform in high data regime for interpolation and extrapolation tasks when compared with alternatives?

RQ.2
:

Can the proposed model generalize well in a low data regime for interpolation and extrapolation tasks?

RQ.3
:

Can the GKNet perform well in applications with missing values?

RQ.4
:

How do the different components of the proposed model affect the performance such as the regularization term and uncertainty model?

We answer these questions in three tasks: multivariate time-series forecasting; spatiotemporal signal interpolation; and tracking dynamic systems.
For further details, the codes are available here.
