Intraoperative use of sodium oxybate to prevent postoperative delirium in older patients undergoing major orthopedic surgery
Authors: Fu-Shan Xue, Dan-Feng Wang, Xiao-Chun Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

With a great interest, we read the recent article “Prophylactic effect of intraoperative sodium oxybate on postoperative delirium (POD) in older patients undergoing major orthopedic surgery: a randomized clinical trial” by Cui et al. published in BMC Medicine on December 31, 2025 [1]. The trial demonstrated that intraoperative administration of sodium oxybate 30 mg·kg−1 provided a possible surgery time-specific efficacy on the development of POD, that is, it only significantly reduced POD incidence in participants undergoing morning surgery but not in those with afternoon surgery. The strengths of this trial include a large sample size, the efforts to control perioperative factors affecting POD occurrence, and the prespecified subgroup analysis for primary outcome based on the stratification by surgical timing (morning versus afternoon). Despite of these strengths, we still wish to discuss with Cui et al. about several methodological issues in this trial to promote the interpretation of their results.

Methodological considerations

Incomplete control of perioperative risk factors

Pain alleviation and physical function improvement are the common indications of major orthopedic surgery. However, in baseline characteristics of participants, Cui et al. [1] did not clearly state if two groups were comparable with respect to preoperative pain and functional status of participants. Available evidence indicates that preoperative pain severity and functional dependence are significant risk factors for POD in orthopedic patients [2, 3]. Furthermore, preoperative sleep quality was assessed by the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS), with a score of ≥ 6 indicating the presence of sleep disturbance. Given the wide interquartile range (0–8) of AIS scores reported in the Cui et al. study, simply comparing median scores may mask imbalances in the proportion of patients with clinically significant sleep disturbances, a known risk factor of POD in elderly patients with major orthopedic surgery [4].

Mostly important, the timeframe of interest for this study was 7 days postoperatively, but Cui et al. [1] did not clearly state if same early postoperative rehabilitation program was used in two groups and whether postoperative complications were comparable between groups. It has been shown that postoperative immobility and complications are significantly associated with an increased risk of POD in the orthopedic patients [2, 3, 5]. In contrast, early mobilization and rehabilitation interventions may reduce the incidence and duration of POD [6]. We are concerned that any significance between-group imbalance in above unknown factors would have biased the primary outcome of this study.

Underestimated incidence of POD

As primary outcome of this study, the occurrence of POD within 7 days postoperatively was evaluated twice daily using the Confusion Assessment Method. As Cui et al. [1] did not report the specific times for two daily POD screenings, it was unclear whether some of participants undergoing afternoon surgery would have missed the pre-set afternoon POD screening on day of surgery. Furthermore, European Society of Anesthesiology recommends that POD screening should be started in the postanesthesia care unit as early as possible, as POD may occur soon after emergence from anesthesia [7]. However, Cui et al. [1] did not describe whether POD occurred in the period from the completion of anesthesia emergence to the first pre-set POD screening was included in the results. It has been reported that emergence delirium occurring in the postanesthesia care unit is common among elderly orthopedic patients with general anesthesia, with an incidence of up to 35.0% [8]. In addition, POD is a fluctuating condition that commonly appears late at night. Only two daily screenings in this study may have also missed the POD occurred at night. It is reported that application of a Confusion Assessment Method form interview combined with a chart review may greatly improve the strengths for identification of incident POD, especially cases occurring during the night shift [9]. We argue that above issues would have underestimated the incidence of POD in elderly patients undergoing major orthopedic surgery, a known high-risk population for POD [2, 3].

Subgroup analysis without multiple adjustments of confounding factors

The prespecified subgroup analysis for primary outcome was performed based on the stratification by procedural timing (morning versus afternoon). Cui et al. [1] provided no significant differences in duration of surgery between intervention and control groups of morning and afternoon surgery populations, but did not state whether preoperative factors affecting the occurrence of POD, such as age, Mini-Mental State Examination score, comorbidities (especially cardiovascular and central nervous diseases), Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale scores, and AIS score [2, 3], were comparable between two groups of morning and afternoon surgery populations. We believe that not adjusting these preoperative confounding factors would have biased the findings of subgroup analysis.

Concerns about generalizability of the findings

Postoperative pain was mainly managed using a patient-controlled intravenous analgesia with sufentanil, and a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug was administered as rescue analgesic. According to the data provided in Tables 1 and 3 of Cui et al.’s article, only about 62% of patients received patient-controlled intravenous analgesia. Furthermore, median maximal pain score was 4 with large interquartile ranges of 2–7, indicating that a significant proportion of patients experience moderate to severe postoperative pain. Actually, current enhanced recovery after orthopedic surgery protocols recommend the use of multimodal opioid-sparing analgesic strategies including regional blocks and regular non-opioid analgesics. Furthermore, opioids are used as rescue analgesics only when postoperative pain is not well controlled with other methods [10]. As postoperative analgesic strategy of this study is inconsistent with the requirements of current enhanced recovery after orthopedic surgery protocols, the generalizability of study findings may be limited.

Recommendations for future studies

It is indisputable that the work of Cui et al. provides a clue for new clinical use of sodium oxybate, that is, preventing the occurrence of POD. To enhance the strength of evidence provided by Cui et al., we agree with them that the multi-center randomized controlled trials with large sample sizes are still needed to verify their findings. In further studies, however, it must refine the methodology for addressing above potential issues raised by us in order to avoid potential bias of results. Especially, a robust model of subgroup analysis with multiple adjustments for perioperative confounding factors should be established to further determine if sodium oxybate can really produce a surgery time-specific efficacy on POD. In addition, all efforts in clinical research works should be aimed at improving final outcomes of patients, rather than a single indicator. Thus, the design of further studies should also include the assessments on the key outcome variables of the current enhanced recovery after orthopedic surgery practice, such as times to early mobilization and gastrointestinal function recovery, quality of recovery, patient satisfaction, and complication rates [10]. If the results of further studies demonstrate clinically significant benefits of intraoperative sodium oxybate by decreasing POD, it will have significant clinical values and prospects.

Conclusions

Cui and colleagues provided evidence suggesting that sodium oxybate may reduce POD occurrence in elderly patients with major orthopedic surgery. The methodological considerations discussed by us aim at improving their work to obtain more robust evidence by an overview of pathways for future studies. The implementation of multi-center designs, strict control of perioperative confounding factors, precise assessment of primary outcome with the Confusion Assessment Method form interview plus a chart review, and inclusion of subgroup analysis with multiple adjustments may provide more definitive data about prophylactic efficacy of intraoperative sodium oxybate on POD. Such methodological refinements may facilitate the translation of this evidence into clinical practice and potentially improve outcomes of elderly patients undergoing major orthopedic surgery.
