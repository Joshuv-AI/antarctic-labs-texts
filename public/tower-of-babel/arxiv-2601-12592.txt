Abstract
. In the setting of constructive reverse mathematics, we analyse the downward Löwenheim-Skolem (DLS) theorem of first-order logic, stating that every infinite model has a countable elementary submodel. Refining the well-known equivalence of the DLS theorem to the axiom of dependent choice (DC) over classical base theories, our constructive approach allows for several finer logical decompositions: Just assuming countable choice (CC), the DLS theorem is equivalent to the conjunction of DC with a newly identified fragment of the excluded middle (LEM) that we call the blurred drinker paradox (BDP). Further without CC, the DLS theorem is equivalent to the conjunction of BDP with similarly blurred weakenings of DC and CC. Independently of their connection with the DLS theorem, we also study BDP and the blurred choice axioms on their own, for instance by showing that BDP is LEM without a contribution of Markov’s principle and that blurred DC is DC without a contribution of CC. The paper is hyperlinked with an accompanying Coq development.

Introduction

The Löwenheim-Skolem theorem
1
1

1

Usually attributed to Löwenheim
[
lowenheim1915moglichkeiten
]
and Skolem
[
Skolem1920-SKOLUB
]

[

]

is a central result about first-order logic, entailing that the formalism is incapable of distinguishing different infinite cardinalities.
In particular the theorem’s so-called downward part, stating that every infinite model (over a countable signature) can be turned into a countably infinite model with otherwise the exact same behaviour, can be considered surprising or even paradoxical:
2
2

2

Discovered and discussed by Skolem
[
skolem1922einige
]
. See also the discussion by McCarty and Tennant
[
mccarty1987skolem
]
for a constructivist perspective.
even axiom systems like ZF set theory, concerned with uncountably large sets like the reals or their iterated power sets, admit countable interpretations.
This seeming contradiction in particular and its metamathematical relevance in general led to an investigation of the exact logical assumptions under which the downward Löwenheim-Skolem (DLS) theorem applies.

From the perspective of (classical) reverse mathematics
[
friedman1976systems
,
simpson2009subsystems
]
, there is a definite answer: the DLS theorem is equivalent to the dependent choice axiom (DC), a weak form of the axiom of choice, stating that there is a path through every total relation
[
boolos2002computability
,
espindolalowenheim
,
karagila2014downward
]
.
To argue the first direction, one can organise the usually iterative construction of the countable submodel such that a single application of DC yields the desired result.
For the converse direction, one uses the DLS theorem to turn a given total relation
R
into a countable sub-relation
R^{\prime}
, applies a (classically available) instance of the axiom of countable choice (CC) to obtain a path
f^{\prime}
through
R^{\prime}
, which is then reflected back as a path
f
through
R
.
In total, that is:

\mathsf{DLS}~\leftrightarrow~\mathsf{DC}

However, the classical answer is insufficient if one investigates the computational content of the DLS theorem, i.e. the question how effective the transformation of a model into a countable submodel really is.
A more adequate answer can be obtained by switching to the perspective of
constructive
reverse mathematics
[
ishihara_reverse_2006
,
diener2018constructive
]
, which is concerned with the analysis of logical strength over a constructive meta-theory, i.e. in particular without the law of excluded middle (LEM), stating that
p\lor\neg p
for all propositions
p
, and ideally also without CC
[
richman2001constructive
]
.
In that setting, finer logical distinctions become visible and one can analyse the computational content of the DLS theorem by investigating two questions:

(1)

Does the DLS theorem still follow from DC alone, without any contribution of LEM?

(2)

Does the DLS theorem still imply all of DC, without any contribution of CC?

\mathsf{DLS}\,(+\,\mathsf{CC})~\overset{?}{\leftrightarrow}~\mathsf{DC}\,(+\,\mathsf{LEM})

In this paper, after giving a fully constructive proof of a weak form of the DLS theorem sharing the same computational content as constructivised model existence theorems
[
herbelin2024analysis
,
forster2020completeness
]
, we observe that neither 1) nor 2) is the case.
Instead, we clarify which exact fragment of LEM is needed on top of DC to prove the DLS theorem and, conversely, which exact fragment of DC it implies.

Regarding (1), note that the DLS theorem requires LEM in the form of the drinker paradox:
3
3

3

Polularised as a logic puzzle by Smullyan
[
smullyan1990name
]
and studied in relation to other principles of constructive mathematics by Escardó and Oliva
[
escardo2010searchable
]
.
in every (non-empty) bar there is a particular person, such that if this person drinks, then everybody in the bar drinks.
The classical explanation for that phenomenon is simple, either everyone drinks anyway, in which case we can choose just an arbitrary person, or there is someone not drinking, in which case we choose that person and obtain a contradiction to the assumption they would drink.
The role of the drinker paradox in the proof of the DLS theorem now is to ensure that the constructed model correctly interprets universal quantification:
4
4

4

Incidentally, a similar requirement is needed in Henkin-style completeness proofs
[
henkin_completeness_1949
]
, connecting to our favoured strategy to establish the DLS theorem.
given a formula
\forall x.\,\varphi(x)
one can find a special domain element
a
such that
\varphi(a)
implies
\forall x.\,\varphi(x)
, thereby reducing a test over the whole domain to a test of a single point and easing the correctness proof.
However, we observe that one actually does not need to know
a
concretely but only that it is contained somewhere in the countable model we construct, more formally, that there is a countable subset
A
such that
\forall a\in A.\,\varphi(a)
implies
\forall x.\,\varphi(x)
.
Seen computationally, this means that we reduce testing over the whole domain to testing only a countable part of it.

On a more abstract level, this observation corresponds to a constructively weaker form of the drinker paradox: in every bar, there is a countable group, such that if everyone in this group drinks, then everybody in the bar drinks.
We call this principle the
blurred drinker paradox
as it continues the bar situation at a later point when everyone’s vision got blurred and clear identifications of persons become impossible.
That it corresponds to the DLS theorem is suggestive since both statements in a sense collapse arbitrary to countable cardinality and indeed we can show that, with CC still assumed in the background, the DLS theorem is equivalent to the conjunction of DC with the blurred drinker paradox.
On top of this equivalence, we study the principle (and its dual needed for existential quantification) in a more general setting with arbitrary blurring cardinalities and in relation to other non-constructive principles, unveiling a hierarchy of classically invisible logical structure.

Turning to question (2), we observe that DC becomes underivable from the DLS theorem if we further give up on CC in the background.
This suggests that the actual fragment of DC at play is a weakening without the contribution of CC, i.e. a principle that follows from DC but does not imply CC.
By a deeper analysis of the proof of the DLS theorem, we actually identify several weakenings of DC that happen to include similar blurring techniques as in the case of the blurred drinker paradox, again connected to the indistinguishability of countable and uncountable cardinalities.
In particular, we show that the DLS theorem is equivalent to the conjunction of a strong blurred form of DC and the blurred drinker paradox, with the former further decomposing into a weaker blurred form of DC conjoined with a blurred form of CC.

Orthogonal to its use for the constructive reverse analysis of the DLS theorem, our discussion of blurred choice axioms contributes to the constructive understanding of the logical structure of choice principles in general, thereby complementing related work by Brede and Herbelin
[
brede2021logical
]
.
For instance, we show that in the absence of CC, the core of DC actually states that every total relation has a total countable sub-relation or, alternatively, that every directed relation has a directed countable sub-relation.
These and similar classically equivalent but constructively weaker reformulations of DC are in visible connection to the DLS theorem.

The idea of constructive reverse mathematics is to identify non-constructive logical principles and their equivalences to well-known theorems, thereby classifying logical strength and computational content

In preparation of upcoming similar results, we review some well-known connections of logical principles like

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_lem]{\mathsf{LEM}}}}~:=~

\displaystyle\forall p:\mathbb{P}.\,p\lor\neg p

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_lem]{\mathsf{LPO}}}}~:=~

can be equivalently stated for arbitrary
x_{0}:A
as

\forall R:A{\rightarrow}A{\rightarrow}\mathbb{P}.\,\mathsf{tot}(R)\to\exists f.\,f\,0=x_{0}\land\forall n.\,R\,(f\,n)\,(f\,(n+1))

by restricting
R
to the sub-relation
R^{\prime}
reachable from
x_{0}
.
Then a path
f
through
R
induces a path
f^{\prime}
through
R^{\prime}
by first taking the path from
x_{0}
to
f\,0
and by then continuing with
f
.

Now to show
CC
, assume a total relation
\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}A{\rightarrow}\mathbb{P}
on
A
with some element
a_{0}
and consider
A^{\prime}:=\mathbb{N}\times A
and

R^{\prime}\,(n,x)\,(m,y)~:=~m=n+1\land R\,n\,y

which is total since
R
is total.
The modified version of
DC
for
R^{\prime}
and the choice
x_{0}:=(0,a_{0})
then yields a path
f^{\prime}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\times A
through
R^{\prime}
and it is straightforward to verify that
f\,n:=\pi_{2}\,(f^{\prime}\,(n+1))
is a choice function for
R
.
∎

We write
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_ac]{\mathsf{DC}}}}^{\Delta}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_ac]{\mathsf{CC}}}}^{\Delta}
for
DC
and
CC
restricted to decidable relations, respectively.
We assume that
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_ac]{\mathsf{CC}}}}^{\Delta}
holds in our meta-theory, as is the case in most formulations of constructive mathematics, while
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_ac]{\mathsf{DC}}}}^{\Delta}
is usually unprovable.

Also, while in (extensional) set-theoretic systems
AC
implies
LEM
, this is not the case in most (intensional) type-theoretic systems, and in neither of those does
DC
imply
LEM
.

2.3.
First-Order Logic

We summarise the concepts for first-order logic (FOL) needed to state the downward Löwenheim-Skolem (
\mathsf{DLS}
) theorem.
The
syntax
of FOL is represented inductively by terms
t:\mathbb{T}
and formulas
\varphi:\mathbb{F}
depending on signatures of function and relation symbols
f
and
P
:

\displaystyle t:\mathbb{T}

\displaystyle~::=~\mathsf{x}_{n}\mid f\,\vec{t}\hskip 94.00008pt(n:\mathbb{N})

\displaystyle\varphi,\psi:\mathbb{F}

\displaystyle~::=~\dot{\bot}\mid P\,\vec{t}\mid\varphi\dot{\to}\psi\mid\varphi\dot{\land}\psi\mid\varphi\dot{\lor}\psi\mid\dot{\forall}\varphi\mid\dot{\exists}\varphi\hskip 17.07182pt

The term vectors
\vec{t}
are required to have length matching the specified arities
{|{f}|}
and
{|{P}|}
of
f
and
P
.
The negative fragment of FOL referred to in
Sections

2.3
and
3
comprises formulas only constructed with
\dot{\bot}
,
\dot{\to}
, and
\dot{\forall}
.
Crucially, we assume that the signatures of function and relation symbols are countable, which induces that so are
\mathbb{T}
and
\mathbb{F}
.

Variable binding
is expressed using de Bruijn indices
[
de_bruijn_lambda_1972
]
, where a bound variable is encoded as the number of quantifiers shadowing its relevant binder.
Capture-avoiding instantiation with parallel substitutions
\sigma:\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{T}
is defined both for terms as
t[\sigma]
and formulas as
\varphi[\sigma]
.
Notably,
(\dot{\forall}\varphi)[\sigma]
is defined by
\dot{\forall}\varphi[\uparrow\!\sigma]
where
\uparrow\!\sigma
is a suitable shifting substitution.
We denote by
t:\mathbb{T}^{c}
and
\varphi:\mathbb{F}^{c}
the closed terms and formulas, respectively, i.e. those that do not contain free variables.
The latter are also called sentences.

The standard notion of
Tarski semantics
is obtained by interpreting formulas in models
\mathcal{M}
identified with their underlying domain, providing interpretation functions
\mathcal{M}^{{|{f}|}}\to\mathcal{M}
for each
f
and relations
\mathcal{M}^{{|{P}|}}\to\mathbb{P}
for each
P
.
Given an environment
\rho:\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{M}
, we define term evaluation
\hat{\rho}\,t
and formula satisfaction
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\varphi
recursively.
For instance,

\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\dot{\forall}\varphi~:=~\forall x:\mathcal{M}.\,\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\varphi[x]

with
\varphi[x]
being a notational shorthand expressing that we consider
\varphi
in the updated environment mapping the first variable to the domain element
x
.

While we will mostly be concerned with semantic considerations, to illustrate the connection of the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem to completeness, we also briefly use
deduction systems
.
Deduction systems are represented by inductive predicates
\Gamma\vdash\varphi
relating predicates
\Gamma:\mathbb{F}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{P}
on formulas with derivable formulas
\varphi
, for instance by rules in the style of natural deduction.
A classical system is obtained by incorporating a rule like double negation elimination, which in a constructive meta-theory is only sound for classical models, i.e. models satisfying
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\varphi
or
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\dot{\neg}\varphi
for all
\varphi
.

Fact
\the\ifcsdef{countsame}{dummy}{coqfact}
(Soundness).

If
\Gamma\vdash\varphi
, then
\mathcal{M}\vDash\varphi
for every classical model
\mathcal{M}
with
\mathcal{M}\vDash\Gamma
.

Proof.

By induction on the derivation of
\Gamma\vdash\varphi
, most cases are straightforward.
To show the classical derivation rule sound, the classicality of the model is required.
∎

The converse property of soundness is completeness, stating that semantic validity implies syntactic provability.
In full generality, completeness cannot be proven constructively
[
KreiselMP
,
henkin1954metamathematical
,
krivtsov2015semantical
,
espindola2016semantic
,
simpson2009subsystems
]
, but the intermediate model existence theorem is constructive for the negative fragment only containing the logical connectives
\dot{\bot}
,
\dot{\to}
, and
\dot{\forall}

[
herbelin2024analysis
,
forster2020completeness
]
.

Fact
\the\ifcsdef{countsame}{dummy}{coqfact}
(Model Existence).

In the negative fragment of FOL, for every consistent context
\Gamma
of sentences one can construct a syntactic model
\mathcal{M}
over the domain
\mathbb{T}
such that
\mathcal{M}\vDash\Gamma
.

Proof.

We outline the main construction as it will be relevant for similar syntactic models used in
Sections

3
and
3.7
.
In a first step, a constructive version of the Lindenbaum Lemma is used to extend
\Gamma
into a consistent context
\Delta\supseteq\Gamma
with suitable closure properties.
Next, a model over domain
\mathbb{T}
with

f^{\mathcal{M}}\,\vec{t}~:=~f\,\vec{t}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptP^{\mathcal{M}}\,\vec{t}~:=~P\,\vec{t}\in\Delta

is constructed, for which the so-called Truth Lemma

\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\sigma}\varphi~\leftrightarrow~\varphi[\sigma]\in\Delta

is verified by induction on
\varphi
for all
\sigma:\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{T}
, acting both as substitution and environment in
\mathcal{M}
.
Then since
\Gamma\subseteq\Delta
, in particular
\mathcal{M}\vDash\Gamma
follows.
∎

We will see in
Section

3
that the model existence theorem yields a weak but fully constructive formulation of the
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem.
This formulation will be based on the notion of elementary equivalence.

Definition 2.1
(Elementary Equivalence).

[elementary_equivalence]
Two models
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
are
elementarily equivalent
if they satisfy the same sentences, i.e. if for every
\varphi:\mathbb{F}^{c}
we have
\mathcal{M}\vDash\varphi
iff
\mathcal{N}\vDash\varphi
.

Note that elementarily equivalent models only satisfy the same closed formulas but otherwise may behave extremely differently.
A much stronger requirement is that of elementary embeddings, taking all formulas into account and therefore completely aligning the behaviour of the models.

Definition 2.2
(Elementary Submodel).

[elementary_homomorphism]
Given models
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
, we call
h:\mathcal{M}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}
an
elementary embedding
if

\forall\rho\varphi.\,\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\varphi\leftrightarrow\mathcal{N}\vDash_{h\circ\rho}\varphi.

If such an
h
exists, we call
\mathcal{M}
an
elementary submodel
of
\mathcal{N}
.

The
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem then states that every model has a countable elementary submodel.

3.
Constructive Downward Löwenheim-Skolem Theorem

We begin with a comparison of different constructive proof strategies for the
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem at various strengths, mostly to identify the underlying concepts in preparation of upcoming results.
First, a weak formulation only yielding an elementarily equivalent model but not necessarily an elementary submodel is obtained as a by-product of a Henkin-style completeness proof via model existence
[
henkin_completeness_1949
]
.
Since the Henkin construction is fully constructive in the negative fragment
[
herbelin2024analysis
,
forster2020completeness
]
, so is the derived
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem.

Fact
\the\ifcsdef{countsame}{dummy}{coqfact}
(DLS via Model Existence).

In the negative fragment of FOL, for every classical model one can construct an elementarily equivalent syntactic model.

Proof 3.3
.

Given that
\mathcal{M}
is classical, we can use soundness to show that the set
\mathsf{Th}(\mathcal{M}):=\{\varphi:\mathbb{F}^{c}\mid\mathcal{M}\vDash\varphi\}
of closed formulas satisfied by
\mathcal{M}
is consistent.
Then by model existence (
Section

2.3
), there is a model
\mathcal{N}
with (countable) domain
\mathbb{T}
and
\mathcal{N}\vDash\mathsf{Th}(\mathcal{M})
.
This already establishes the first implication showing
\mathcal{M}
elementarily equivalent to
\mathcal{N}
. For the converse, assuming a closed formula
\varphi
with
\mathcal{N}\vDash\varphi
, we obtain
\mathcal{M}\vDash\varphi
by using the classicality of
\mathcal{M}
and the observation that, if it were
\mathcal{M}\vDash\dot{\neg}\varphi
instead, also
\mathcal{N}\vDash\dot{\neg}\varphi
would follow, contradiction.

The model existence proof can be extended to the full syntax using
LEM
alone
[
forster2020completeness
]
, so the derived version of the
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem notably does not rely on any form of choice axioms.
In fact, already the weak law of excluded middle (
\forall p.\,\neg p\lor\neg\neg p
) is sufficient
[
herbelin2023new
]
but we are not aware of a proof showing it necessary for this form of the
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem.

Also note that the Lindenbaum extension used in the proof of
Section

2.3
ensures that quantified formulas have associated Henkin witnesses in form of unused variables.
In the second variant, this intermediate step is not necessary, since we restrict to models that address all Henkin witnesses by closed terms.

Definition 3.4
(Witness Property).

[witness_prop]
Given a model
\mathcal{M}
with environment
\rho
, we call
w:\mathcal{M}
a
Henkin witness
for
\dot{\forall}\varphi
if

\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\varphi[w]~\to~\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\dot{\forall}\varphi

and, symmetrically, a Henkin witness for
\dot{\exists}\varphi
if

\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\dot{\exists}\varphi~\to~\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\varphi[w].

We say that
\mathcal{M}
has the
witness property
if Henkin witnesses for all formulas can be expressed by closed terms
t:\mathbb{T}^{c}
.

For models with the witness property, we can then derive the stronger conclusion yielding a countable elementary submodel by means of a simplified syntactic model construction.

Fact
\the\ifcsdef{countsame}{dummy}{coqfact}
(DLS via Witnesses).

For every model with the witness property one can construct a syntactic elementary submodel.

Proof 3.5
.

Given
\mathcal{M}
with the witness property and an arbitrary environment
\rho
, we consider the syntactic model
\mathcal{N}
constructed over the (countable) domain
\mathbb{T}
by setting

f^{\mathcal{N}}\,\vec{t}~:=~f\,\vec{t}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptP^{\mathcal{N}}\,\vec{t}~:=~P^{\mathcal{M}}\,(\hat{\rho}\,\vec{t}).

We prove that
\hat{\rho}
is an elementary embedding of
\mathcal{N}
into
\mathcal{M}
, i.e. that
\mathcal{N}\vDash_{\sigma}\varphi
if and only if
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\hat{\rho}\circ\sigma}\varphi
for all
\sigma:\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{T}
and
\varphi
by induction on
\varphi
.
The only cases of interest are the quantifiers, we explain universal quantification as example.

Let
t:\mathbb{T}^{c}
denote the Henkin witness for
\dot{\forall}\varphi

and assume
\mathcal{N}\vDash_{\sigma}\dot{\forall}\varphi
.
Then in particular
\mathcal{N}\vDash_{\sigma}\varphi[t]
and by inductive hypothesis
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\hat{\rho}\circ\sigma}\varphi[t]
, which implies
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\hat{\rho}\circ\sigma}\dot{\forall}\varphi
by the Henkin property of
t
.
That conversely
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\hat{\rho}\circ\sigma}\dot{\forall}\varphi
implies
\mathcal{N}\vDash_{\sigma}\dot{\forall}\varphi
is straightforward.

Many proofs of the
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem proceed by extending the signature with enough fresh constants such that a model satisfying the witness property can be constructed
[
conant_notes
]
.
Alternatively, as a the third variant, we replace the condition to represent Henkin witnesses syntactically with environments collecting them semantically.

Definition 3.6
(Henkin Environment).

[Henkin_env]
Given a model
\mathcal{M}
, we call
\rho:\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{M}
a
Henkin environment
if it collects Henkin witnesses for every formula
\varphi
as follows:

\displaystyle\exists n.\,\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\varphi[\rho\,n]~\to~\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\dot{\forall}\varphi

\displaystyle\exists n.\,\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\dot{\exists}\varphi~\to~\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\varphi[\rho\,n]

Note that if
\mathcal{M}
has the witness property, then
\mathcal{M}
admits a Henkin environment by enumerating the evaluations of closed terms, but not vice versa.

The use of Henkin environments then allows to conclude the
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem without extending the signature or domain, which is a particularly suitable strategy for mechanisation.

Theorem 3.7
(DLS via Environments).

[Henkin_LS]

For every model admitting a Henkin environment one can construct a syntactic elementary submodel.

Proof 3.8
.

Given a model
\mathcal{M}
with Henkin environment
\rho
, we proceed as in the previous proof, i.e. we consider the syntactic model
\mathcal{N}
induced by
\rho
.
Again, inductively verifying that
\hat{\rho}
is an elementary embedding of
\mathcal{N}
into
\mathcal{M}
is only non-trivial for quantifiers, for illustration assume
\mathcal{N}\vDash_{\sigma}\dot{\forall}\varphi
for some environment
\sigma:\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{T}
and formula
\varphi
.
We aim to show
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\hat{\rho}\circ\sigma}\dot{\forall}\varphi
which is equivalent to
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\dot{\forall}\varphi[\uparrow\!\sigma]
and thus reduces to
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\varphi[\uparrow\!\sigma][\rho\,n]
using a witness
\rho\,n
guaranteed by the Henkin property of
\rho
.
The latter then follows from
\mathcal{N}\vDash_{\sigma}\dot{\forall}\varphi
instantiated to
\rho\,n
and the inductive hypothesis.

All upcoming proofs of the
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem will factor through
Theorem

3.7
or a strengthening thereof (
).

4.
Downward Löwenheim-Skolem Theorem using DC and LEM

In this section, we use the proof strategy induced by
Theorem

3.7
to reconstruct the well-known connection of the
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem to
DC
over a classical meta-theory
[
espindolalowenheim
,
karagila2014downward
,
boolos2002computability
]
, providing both
CC
and
LEM
.
First, we show that in this context,
DC
can be used to construct a Henkin environment and therefore to conclude the
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem.
As the later, constructively refined, proofs will follow the same pattern, we give the construction here in full detail.

Theorem 4.9
.

[LS_downward_with_DC_LEM]

Assuming
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_ac]{\mathsf{DC}}}}+{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_lem]{\mathsf{LEM}}}}
, the
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem holds.

Proof 4.10
.

By
Theorem

3.7
, it is enough to show that under the given assumptions every model admits a Henkin environment.
Given a model
\mathcal{M}
, the construction of Henkin environment is done in three steps, each making use of a different logical assumption, thereby explaining the respective non-constructive contributions.
The high-level idea is to describe an extension method by which Henkin witnesses are accumulated stage by stage, where
LEM
is needed to guarantee the existence of Henkin witnesses,
CC
(as a consequence of
DC
) is needed to pick such witnesses simultaneously for every formula in every stage, and finally
DC
is needed to obtain a path through all stages such that its union constitutes a Henkin environment.

Formally, we define a step relation
S:(\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{M}){\rightarrow}(\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{M}){\rightarrow}\mathbb{P}
to express the extension of environments, such that
S\,\rho\,\rho^{\prime}
captures that
\rho^{\prime}
contains all witnesses with respect to
\rho
:

S\,\rho\,\rho^{\prime}~:=\rho\subseteq\rho^{\prime}\land\forall\varphi.\bigwedge\begin{array}[]{ll}\exists n.\,\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\varphi[\rho^{\prime}\,n]~\to~\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\dot{\forall}\varphi\\[2.84544pt]
\exists n.\,\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\dot{\exists}\varphi~\to~\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\varphi[\rho^{\prime}\,n]\end{array}

Clearly every fixed point of
S
, i.e.
\rho
with
S\,\rho\,\rho
, is a Henkin environment so we now explain how such a fixed point is obtained by the aforementioned three steps.

(1)

Given any environment
\rho
, the assumption of
LEM
guarantees Henkin witnesses to exist for all formulas by its connection to the drinker paradoxes:
For
\dot{\forall}\varphi
, the existence of a Henkin witness is exactly the instance
{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_lem]{\mathsf{DP}}}}_{\mathcal{M}}
for the predicate
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{\rho}\varphi[\_]
and for
\dot{\exists}\varphi
exactly the corresponding instance
{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_lem]{\mathsf{EP}}}}_{\mathcal{M}}
.

(2)

We now use
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_ac]{\mathsf{CC}}}}_{\mathcal{M}}
to show that
S
is total, i.e. given some
\rho
we construct
\rho^{\prime}
with
S\,\rho\,\rho^{\prime}
.
By the previous step, we know that every formula
\dot{\forall}\varphi
has a Henkin witness with respect to
\rho
.
So by fixing some enumeration
\varphi_{n}
of formulas, we know that for every
n
the formula
\dot{\forall}\varphi_{n}
has a Henkin witness and thus
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_ac]{\mathsf{CC}}}}_{M}
yields a function
\rho_{\forall}
such that
\rho_{\forall}\,n
is the Henkin witness to
\dot{\forall}\varphi_{n}
.
Analogously, another application of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_ac]{\mathsf{CC}}}}_{M}
yields a function
\rho_{\exists}
such that
\rho_{\exists}\,n
is the Henkin witness to
\dot{\exists}\varphi_{n}
.
We then set
\rho^{\prime}:=\rho\cup(\rho_{\forall}\cup\rho_{\exists})
and obtain
S\,\rho\,\rho^{\prime}
by simple calculation.

(3)

We apply
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_ac]{\mathsf{DC}}}}_{\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{M}}
to get a path
F:\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}(\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{M})
through
S
, yielding a cumulative sequence of environments
F_{0}\subseteq F_{1}\subseteq F_{2}\subseteq\dots
of Henkin witnesses.
To collect the sequence into a single environment, we define

\rho\,\langle n_{1},n_{2}\rangle~:=~F_{n_{1}}\,n_{2}

and verify that
S\,\rho\,\rho
, i.e. that
\rho
is Henkin.
This is obtained by several properties of
\rho
:

•

F_{k}\subseteq\rho
for every
k
:
Given
n
we need to find
n^{\prime}
with
F_{k}\,n=\rho\,n^{\prime}
, pick
n^{\prime}:=\langle k,n\rangle
.

•

S\,F_{k}\,\rho
for every
k
:
By the previous fact, we know
F_{k}\subseteq\rho
, so we just need to show that
\rho
contains all Henkin witnesses relative to
F_{k}
.
Since
F
is a path through
S
, we know
S\,F_{k}\,F_{k+1}
, so
F_{k+1}
contains these witnesses, but then so does
\rho
given
F_{k+1}\subseteq\rho
.

•

S\,\rho\,\rho
:
Since
\rho\subseteq\rho
, we just need to show that for given
\varphi
both Henkin witnesses relative to
\rho
are contained in
\rho
.
Since
\varphi
contains only finitely many variables and therefore, since
\rho
is constructed in cumulative stages, we can find
k
with
\rho\subseteq_{\varphi}F_{k}
, meaning
\rho
is included in
F_{k}
on all free variables of
\varphi
.
Then in particular there is a permutation substitution
\sigma
such that evaluation of
\varphi
in
\rho
coincides with evaluation of
\varphi[\sigma]
in
F_{k}
.
But then, since
S\,F_{k}\,\rho
by the previous fact,
\rho
contains the witnesses for
\varphi[\sigma]
relative to
F_{k}
and thus for
\varphi
relative to
\rho
itself.

We remark that the forthcoming constructive refinements will weaken the respective logical assumptions in each of the three steps above, making precise which independent sources of non-constructivity are at play.
For the converse direction, the necessity for dependent choice relies on the presence of countable choice.

Fact
\the\ifcsdef{countsame}{dummy}{coqfact}
.

Assuming
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_ac]{\mathsf{CC}}}}_{\mathbb{N}}
, the
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem implies
DC
.

Proof 4.11
.

The high-level idea is that the
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem reduces
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_ac]{\mathsf{DC}}}}_{A}
to
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_ac]{\mathsf{CC}}}}_{\mathbb{N}}
by transforming
A
into a countable domain.

Formally, assuming a total relation
R:A{\rightarrow}A{\rightarrow}\mathbb{P}
, we consider the model
\mathcal{M}
with domain
A
and interpretation
P^{\mathcal{M}}_{R}\,x\,y:=R\,x\,y
for some binary relation symbol
P_{R}
.
The
\mathsf{DLS}
theorem then yields an elementary submodel
\mathcal{N}
over a countable domain, say
\mathbb{N}
itself for simplicity, witnessed by an elementary homomorphism
h:\mathcal{N}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{M}
.
Since totality is a first-order property with
\mathcal{M}\vDash\mathsf{tot}(P_{R})
by assumption, in particular
\mathcal{N}\vDash\mathsf{tot}(P_{R})
, so the interpretation
P^{\mathcal{N}}_{R}:\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{P}
must be total, too.

But then
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_ac]{\mathsf{CC}}}}_{\mathbb{N}}
yields a choice function
f:\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{N}
for
P^{\mathcal{N}}_{R}
and we can verify that
g:\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}A
defined by
g\,n:=h\,(f^{n}\,0)
is a path through
R
:
to justify
R\,(g\,n)\,(g\,(n+1))
for any
n
, consider an environment
\rho:\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}
with
\rho\,0:=f^{n}\,0
and
\rho\,1:=f^{n+1}\,0
, so
R\,(g\,n)\,(g\,(n+1))
can be equivalently stated as
\mathcal{M}\vDash_{h\circ\rho}P_{R}(\mathsf{x}_{0},\,\mathsf{x}_{1})
.
By elementarity of
h
this reduces to
\mathcal{N}\vDash_{\rho}P_{R}(\mathsf{x}_{0},\,\mathsf{x}_{1})
, which translates to
P^{\mathcal{N}}_{R}\,(f^{n}\,0)\,(f\,(f^{n}\,0))
and holds since
f
is a choice function for
P^{\mathcal{N}}_{R}
.

Corollary 4.12
(Classical Decomposition).

5.
The Blurred Drinker Paradox

In this section, we introduce the concept of
blurring
, by which we refer to replacing existential quantifiers by quantification over subsets.
By this transformation, logical principles can be obtained with constructively slightly reduced information content, as concrete witnesses are hidden in a blur of computationally indistinguishable elements.
Here, we study that concept at the example of the drinker paradoxes, in
we will extend it to choice principles.
A summary diagram will be given at the end of this section.

We introduce the following blurred forms of
DP
and
EP
:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_blem]{\mathsf{BDP}}}}^{B}_{A}~:=~

\displaystyle\forall P:A{\rightarrow}\mathbb{P}.\exists f:B{\rightarrow}A.\,(\forall y.\,P\,(f\,y))\to\forall x.\,P\,x

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_blem]{\mathsf{BEP}}}}^{B}_{A}~:=~

\displaystyle\forall P:A{\rightarrow}\mathbb{P}.\exists f:B{\rightarrow}A.\,(\exists x.\,P\,x)\to\exists y.\,P\,(f\,y)

Building on the intuition from before, for instance the principle
{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_blem]{\mathsf{BDP}}}}^{B}_{A}
states that a Henkin witness for
P:A{\rightarrow}\mathbb{P}
in the sense of
{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_lem]{\mathsf{DP}}}}_{A}
is contained in a blur of size at most
B
, represented by a function
f:B{\rightarrow}A
.
In that situation, we call
f
a
blurred
Henkin witness or simply a
Henkin blur
and require that
B
is inhabited.

Note that, while
{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_lem]{\mathsf{DP}}}}_{A}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_lem]{\mathsf{EP}}}}_{A}
are duals in the sense that
{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_lem]{\mathsf{DP}}}}_{A}
also yields
{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_lem]{\mathsf{EP}}}}_{A}
for negative predicates
\{x:A\mid\neg P\,x\}
and vice versa, even in that sense
{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_blem]{\mathsf{BEP}}}}^{B}_{A}
is still slightly stronger than
{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_blem]{\mathsf{BDP}}}}^{B}_{A}
as it concludes with a constructively strong existential quantifier.
This will play a role in the slightly different connection to Kripke’s schema subject to
Section

5
.

Intuitively, the blurred drinker paradoxes allow to test quantified properties on a large domain by considering restrictions to smaller domains, especially countable ones.
In this perspective, they resemble Kripke’s schema
[
van1977use
]
, stating that every proposition can be tested by considering the solvability of Boolean functions over countable domain:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_lem]{\mathsf{KS}}}}~:=~

\displaystyle\forall p:\mathbb{P}.\exists f:\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{B}.\,p\leftrightarrow\exists n.\,f\,n=\mathsf{true}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_lem]{\mathsf{KS}}}}^{\prime}~:=~

\displaystyle\forall p:\mathbb{P}.\exists f:\mathbb{N}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{B}.\,(p{\rightarrow}\neg(\forall n.\,f\,n=\mathsf{false}))\land((\exists n.\,f\,n=\mathsf{true}){\rightarrow}p)

Note that
KS
expresses that every proposition is
\Sigma_{1}
, where the logical complexity class
\Sigma_{1}
refers to the syntactic form of a single existential quantifier over a decidable predicate.
In comparison, the slightly weaker
{\color[rgb]{0.6094,0.1055,0.1875}{\hyperref@@ii[defs_lem]{\mathsf{KS}}}}^{\prime}
replaces the existential quantifier in one direction by a negated universal quantifier.
