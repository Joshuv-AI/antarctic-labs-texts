When the night is not long enough: soil drying constrains nighttime tree rehydration, setting a physical limit to stomatal regulation
Authors: Julian Schoch, Lorenz Walthert, Peter Lehmann, Andrea Carminati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlants are often assumed to optimize carbon assimilation against water loss through stomatal regulation, responding instantaneously to varying atmospheric demand and soil water supply. However, a growing body of work highlights the importance of nighttime recovery processes for understanding plant water use, yet nighttime processes are not considered in existing supply–demand models. Here, we extend an existing supply–demand model by including flux imbalances between atmospheric demand and soil supply buffered by plant water storage. The flux imbalances accumulate during the day and are compensated overnight. Applying the conceptual model to field data from Quercus pubescens Willd., we show that progressive soil drying constrains the rate of nighttime rehydration. Precisely, below a critical soil water potential threshold (ψsoil,crit), nighttime rehydration begins to decline because rehydration rates become too low relative to the duration of the night (‘the night is not long enough’). This threshold marks a transition beyond which maintaining high transpiration rates becomes increasingly risky. The observations show that stomatal regulation begins near this threshold, pointing to the concomitance between daytime stomatal regulation and limited nighttime rehydration. These results suggest that progressive soil drying constrains nighttime rehydration and reveal a critical hydraulic threshold that coincides with the onset of stomatal regulation and highlight that plant water use is constrained not only by daytime fluxes but also by nighttime recovery.

Introduction

Climate change intensifies hot and dry conditions, increasing tree mortality (Hammond et al. 2022) and reshaping forest structure and carbon storage (McDowell and Allen 2015). Forests act as a major carbon sink (Pan et al. 2011) and, together with other terrestrial ecosystems, contribute substantially to evapotranspiration over land (Jasechko et al. 2013), thereby playing a critical role in regulating global carbon and water cycles. This regulation occurs to a great extent through stomata, which control the exchange of vapour and CO₂ between vegetation and the atmosphere. Rising vapour pressure deficit (VPD) and drying soils reduce vegetation productivity by inducing stomatal closure (Jung et al. 2010, Novick et al. 2016, Liu et al. 2020). However, understanding the mechanisms behind the physiological and environmental drivers of stomatal regulation, including the interaction between VPD and soil drying, remains challenging (Carminati and Javaux 2020, Grossiord et al. 2020). Specifically, there is a mismatch between: (i) studies predicting stomatal closure using the instantaneous water supply–demand framework, which focus on midday leaf water potential (ψleaf) and hydraulic conductance of soil and plants (Sperry and Love 2015, Sperry et al. 2017, Carminati and Javaux 2020, Joshi et al. 2022); and (ii) studies emphasizing the role of water storage capacity (i.e., the total amount of internally stored water that can be mobilized to support transpiration; distinct from hydraulic capacitance, which describes the change in water storage per unit change in water potential), nighttime stem rehydration and predawn water potential (ψleaf,pd) as the key state variables for predicting stomatal closure (Zweifel et al. 2021b, Peters et al. 2023, 2025, Potkay and Feng 2023). The aim of our study is to provide a conceptual model linking the midday and the night view of stomatal regulation of water use.

We begin with the midday approach. Several successful models of stomatal response to soil moisture and VPD are based on the water supply–demand framework (Sperry and Love 2015, Sperry et al. 2017, Carminati and Javaux 2020, Joshi et al. 2022, Carminati et al. 2026). These models describe water flow from the soil to the leaf, driven by gradients in water potential, and controlled by the hydraulic conductance of key plant elements (soil, root, stem and leaves). These models assume that plants optimize carbon assimilation over a water cost, which has been associated with the midday leaf water potential (Sperry et al. 2017, Joshi et al. 2022). According to these models, plants minimize the risk of excessively negative leaf water potential (e.g., to prevent embolism formation in the xylem) by downregulating stomatal conductance when the atmospheric water demand exceeds the plant’s capacity to transport water and/or the soil’s ability to supply water to the roots.

However, these models neglect water storage capacity, which can buffer transient diurnal imbalances between water supply and transpirational demand and is replenished during periods of low transpiration (e.g., at night; Čermák et al. 1976). Specifically, water dynamics in trees reflect the interaction between root water uptake and atmospheric demand, resulting in alternating phases of storage depletion and rehydration (Steppe et al. 2006, Brum et al. 2026). Importantly, these processes can be observed through variations in stem diameter across both daily and seasonal timescales (Hinckley and Bruckerhoff 1975, Zweifel et al. 2001). While stem diameter changes in this study are primarily interpreted as reflecting fluctuations in water storage, they do not exclusively represent reversible water storage dynamics (Brum et al. 2026). Notably, the physiologically relevant variable is turgor pressure (Bartlett et al. 2012). Turgor is essential for irreversible cell expansion and thus growth (Lockhart 1965, Steppe et al. 2006). Trees that recover faster from daytime dehydration and turgor loss have greater potential for nighttime growth (Zweifel et al. 2021b). Consistently, temperate tree species reduce their stomatal conductance in order to maintain nighttime turgor, which supports cell expansion (Peters et al. 2023, Potkay and Feng 2023). Along this line, it was shown that stomatal conductance during the day is better predicted by ψleaf,pd than ψleaf,md (Peters et al. 2025). These results highlight the importance of nighttime recovery processes for plant water use.

While the effects of soil drying on daytime plant water transport are well established (Sperry and Love 2015, Carminati and Javaux 2020), its consequences for nighttime recovery remain poorly understood. This knowledge gap arises because existing hydraulic supply–demand models neglect internal water storage and its depletion during the day and replenishment at night. To address this limitation, we incorporate mass conservation into an established supply–demand framework to develop a conceptual model that links daytime water depletion with nighttime recovery, providing a hydraulic basis for understanding how soil drying constrains both processes. The approach is grounded in physical principles and provides an upper constraint on plant water use while recognizing that plant adjustments also contribute to shaping hydraulic responses.

To test this hypothesis in the field, we link ΔVday and ΔVnight to observable quantities. We define peak diurnal shrinkage (PDS; often referred to as maximum daily shrinkage, e.g., Ortuño et al. 2010, Fernández and Cuevas 2010, Dietrich et al. 2018) as the realized outcome of ΔVday, derived from the difference between predawn and midday stem radius. Similarly, nighttime rehydration (NR), defined as the increase in stem radius from midday minimum to the subsequent predawn, represents the realized ΔVnight. Both PDS and NR represent integrated responses to the underlying balance between water supply and demand, without explicitly resolving them. In the field, based on the conceptual model, we expect increasing PDS and NR values with progressive drying, followed by a sharp decline at potentials concomitant with the onset of stomatal regulation.

These sites are covered by mixed downy oak (Quercus pubescens Willd.) forests that have remained unmanaged for decades. Table 1 provides an overview of their key climatic characteristics. Sap flow measurements

In March 2023, at each site, two trees were instrumented with one sap flow sensor (UGT SFM-4, Müncheberg, Germany) installed at breast height. For installation, the bark around the sensor was carefully thinned to a remaining thickness of 5 mm. For the three needles of the sensor (two needles with a pair of temperature sensors and a heater probe in the middle), three holes were drilled, and the needles with temperature sensors at 10 and 20 mm were inserted into the sapwood. Data were recorded at 15-min intervals, and sap flux density of inner and outer sensor position was calculated following the method described in Forster (2020). In short, the sap flow of the entire stem was estimated by scaling the inner and outer sap flux densities according to sapwood area. Sapwood thickness was taken as 49 mm based on literature for this species (Poyatos et al. 2007), with the inner and outer sapwood layers separated at 15 mm below the cambium. To correct for potential zero-flow offsets, data from 22 March to 5 April (when sap flow was minimal) were used to correct baseline offset (Clearwater et al. 2009). To then derive daily peak sap flow, we calculated the 95th percentile of 15-min sap flow measurements for each day. This approach reduces sensitivity to outliers while preserving the peak. Furthermore, sap flow was normalized by potential evapotranspiration (PET) to assess stomatal regulation. This approach reflects the fact that transpiration (sap flow) is governed by the combined effects of stomatal conductance and atmospheric demand (e.g., Jarvis et al. 1976). Specifically, we divided by the daily sap flow with the daily potential evapotranspiration (PET; MeteoSwiss), which integrates radiation, temperature, humidity and wind, and serves as a proxy for atmospheric demand.

Soil matric potential measurements

Soil matric potential (ψsoil) was measured using two or three MPS-2 dielectric sensors (Decagon Devices, Pullman, WA, USA) installed in the soil profile wall at defined depth layers (Table 3) in the year 2014. These sensors determine dielectric permittivity of two porous ceramic discs, which varies with their water content. Matric potential was derived from the known relationship between water content and matric potential of the discs. Data were logged hourly using Decagon EM-50 data loggers. Sensor readings were corrected following established protocols (Walthert and Schleppi 2018, Walthert et al. 2023). As all four soil depths exhibited similar drying dynamics, we used the arithmetic mean across depths as a representative estimate of ψsoil for comparison with plant physiological parameters.

Tree water storage dynamics derived from dendrometer measurements

To measure stem radius changes, we used automated high-precision point dendrometers (ZN12-T-WP, Natkon, Oetwil am See, Switzerland). At each site, two trees were instrumented with one dendrometer each, installed at breast height. The processing followed established methods (Knüsel et al. 2021, Luković et al. 2022). Data selection criteria

To minimize the influence of phenological changes (e.g., leaf unfolding and senescence) on transpiration measurements, we restricted the analysis to the summer months (June–August), corresponding to full canopy development with relatively stable leaf area and consistently high potential transpiration rates. To ensure consistency with the conditions under which leaf water potential was calibrated (Table 4), only days with potential evapotranspiration (PET) ≥ 3 mm day−1 and precipitation ≤1 mm were included. This restricts the analysis to remain within the range of environmental conditions represented in the calibration data.

Statistical analysis

To identify threshold behaviour in hydraulic variables, segmented regression analysis was applied using the R package ‘segmented’ (Muggeo 2008; Version 2.1-4). For each tree and response variable, linear models were first fitted to describe the relationship between the response (e.g., PDS, NR, normalized sap flow, ψleaf,pd and the predictor ψsoil. These models were then extended to segmented regressions to estimate breakpoints ψsoil,crit representing shifts in system behaviour. Breakpoint estimates and their 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the ‘segmented’ package based on the fitted model. Confidence intervals were derived using the default option implemented in the package. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2; R Core Team, 2024).

Conceptual model

To estimate daytime and nighttime storage dynamics (ΔVday and ΔVnight), the model requires both a soil water supply S(t) and an atmospheric demand D(t). For the supply term S(t), we used the model of Carminati and Javaux (2020), with parameter values listed in Table 5. This model describes water flow from the bulk soil to the leaf as a function of ψsoil and ψleaf through a series of hydraulic resistances. For conceptual simplicity, we assumed a constant ψleaf of −1.5 MPa and a stepwise decreasing ψsoil from −0.01 to −1.4 MPa. As the conceptual model purposely neglects stomatal regulation, transpiration was assumed to follow atmospheric demand. Accordingly, D(t) was derived from potential evapotranspiration data obtained from a nearby meteorological station (MeteoSwiss). Across all trees, the measured variables exhibited characteristic responses to soil drying (Figure 2). The PDS and NR initially increased with decreasing ψsoil, reached a maximum and subsequently declined under drier conditions. In contrast, normalized sap flow remained relatively constant under wet conditions before declining with further soil drying. Likewise, ψleaf,pd initially followed ψsoil, but transitioned to a steeper decline under drier conditions. These response patterns were quantified using segmented regression (see Materials and methods). Breakpoint estimates were remarkably consistent among variables within each tree and exhibited overlapping 95% confidence intervals (Table 6). Within sites, breakpoint estimates were also similar, averaging 1.1 and 0.9 MPa for Trees A and B, respectively, and ~0.7 MPa for Trees C and D. Consequently, variation among sites exceeded variation among trees within a site. In all trees, maximum nighttime rehydration remained lower than maximum daytime depletion.

We further examined the relationship between ψleaf,pd and ψsoil to assess whether equilibrium between plant and soil persists under progressive drying (Figure 2, bottom panels). Under wet conditions, ψleaf,pd broadly follows the expected 1:1 relationship with ψsoil (dashed black line). However, beyond the breakpoint, ψleaf,pd deviates systematically from this equilibrium across all trees. We interpret this breakpoint as the point when nighttime soil water flow can no longer refill the trees up to the point that the bulk soil water potential would allow. This breakpoint occurs at soil water potentials comparable to those identified for PDS, NR and normalized sapflow (Table 6), supporting our interpretation that declines in nighttime rehydration are rate and soil limited. Together, these observations indicate that changes in storage dynamics, transpiration and predawn equilibration are concomitant and occur within a similar range of a critical soil water potential (ψsoil,crit).

Discussion and conclusions

ψsoil,crit as critical threshold for onset of stomatal regulation

We hypothesized that the onset of stomatal regulation is concomitant with the hydraulic threshold defined by the maximum in nighttime rehydration (ψsoil,crit). The field results are consistent with this expectation: Figure 2 shows that the onset of reduction in normalized sapflow (reflecting stomatal regulation) is concomitant with the decline in both PDS and NR. We interpret ψsoil,crit as an upper operating boundary, rather than a strict failure point, as trees may reduce transpiration earlier. Similarly, some species might also move beyond this operating boundary, but this would be a risky strategy, as these trees would face accelerating dehydration.

The value of ψsoil,crit is determined by factors that influence the balance between water supply and atmospheric demand. Increased atmospheric demand enhances daytime shrinkage, while reduced soil water availability and declining soil–root hydraulic conductance limit water supply. In addition, tree physiological traits influence both depletion and recovery rates, including adjustments in hydraulic conductance, leaf area, turgor loss point and root properties observed under drought conditions (Limousin et al. 2009, Bartlett et al. 2012, Brunner et al. 2015, Moreno et al. 2021). These responses suggest that the hydraulic functioning of the system reflects not only current environmental conditions but also prior exposure to drought. As a result, ψsoil,crit should be understood as a dynamic operating boundary emerging from the interaction between atmospheric demand, soil properties and plant hydraulic traits. While this threshold theoretically might vary across ecosystems, for temperate tree species it may converge toward similar values (Peters et al. 2025), likely reflecting constraints imposed by declining soil hydraulic conductivity on water extraction as soils dry (Carminati et al. 2026).

The dynamic operating boundary, in combination with the conceptual model, provides a mechanistic basis for the well-established role of turgor in controlling growth (Lockhart 1965, Steppe et al. 2006). Within this framework, the timing of nighttime growth and the reduction in growth under high atmospheric demand (Zweifel et al. 2021b) can be understood as consequences of the diurnal water balance. Increased daytime depletion elevates the demand for nighttime recovery, which may be limited by hydraulic constraints, thereby delaying the restoration of turgor and constraining growth.

ψsoil,crit, together with ΔVday and ΔVnight, provides a unifying framework that links daytime demand–supply dynamics with nighttime recovery processes. In that sense, the model reconciles perspectives focusing on hydraulic limitation during the day (Sperry and Love 2015, Sperry et al. 2017, Carminati and Javaux 2020, Joshi et al. 2022) with those emphasizing nighttime recovery and growth (Zweifel et al. 2021b, Peters et al. 2023, 2025, Potkay and Feng 2023).

Nighttime equilibrium

Under wet conditions (ψsoil > ψsoil,crit), ψleaf,pd closely approaches ψsoil,pd, indicating effective equilibration between plant and soil over the night (Figure 2, last row). Importantly, this balance does not imply complete rehydration, as progressive soil drying lowers ψsoil and ψleaf,pd thereby shifting the plant to an increasingly drier state. As soils dry, NR initially increases, suggesting that plants can enhance nighttime rehydration in response to greater daytime depletion. However, upon reaching ψsoil,crit, this trend reverses. Recovery no longer increases despite continued drying, and the plant fails to equilibrate with the soil, despite PDS declining. Below this threshold (ψsoil < ψsoil,crit), plant water potential declines more rapidly than ψsoil, indicating that nighttime rehydration becomes insufficient to offset daytime losses. Predawn disequilibrium between plant and soil water potentials is well documented and often attributed to nighttime transpiration (e.g., Donovan et al. 2001, 2003, Bucci et al. 2004). While such losses can contribute to incomplete recovery, our analysis suggests a complementary mechanism in which the rate of nighttime rehydration itself becomes limiting, thereby constraining NR. In this view, predawn disequilibrium reflects both ongoing water losses and limitations in nighttime rehydration. Alternatively, the observed decline in NR and emergence of predawn disequilibrium may also be interpreted within the broader context of stem rehydration dynamics and hydraulic strategies proposed for temperate tree species during drought (Peters et al. 2023, 2025, Brum et al. 2026).
