Postbiotic derived from Lacticaseibacillus paracasei CNCM I-5220 as a novel approach to improve ageing-induced skin damage
Authors: Francesca Algieri, Silvia Pimazzoni, Nina Tanaskovic, Daniele Braga, Giuseppe Penna, Maria Rescigno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ageing process impacts skin health by reducing cell turnover and altering skin microbiota, which plays a crucial role in maintaining homeostasis and protection against pathogens. Microbiota-derived metabolites, including postbiotics generated through nutrient fermentation, may contribute to these protective functions. In this context, topical application of postbiotics represents a promising strategy to mitigate visible signs of skin ageing. We investigated the effects of PB-SKF, a postbiotic derived from the fermentation of fructooligosaccharides (FOS) by Lacticaseibacillus paracasei strain CNCM I-52201on skin functional properties. Gene expression profiling of PB-SKF-treated keratinocytes suggested the modulation of pathways associated with age-related processes. Consistent with these findings, in vitro experiments showed increased hyaluronic acid and pro-collagen production by keratinocytes and fibroblasts, respectively, following PB-SKF treatment. In addition, PB-SKF promoted fibroblast migration, supporting a potential role in wound healing-related processes. These findings supported the evaluation of PB-SKF in a clinical setting. After six weeks of topical application of cream containing 2% PB-SKF, treatment with the postbiotic significantly reduced skin roughness and increased hydration, elasticity, and echogenicity compared to placebo. Overall, PB-SKF exhibits anti-ageing-like properties both in vitro and in the clinical evaluation, supporting its role in maintaining skin homeostasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52294-0.

Introduction

Skin ageing is a multifactorial process that affects both structure and function of the skin2. Chronological ageing is driven by intrinsic factors, like genetic background, metabolism, ethnicity, and hormonal status, while extrinsic factors such as ultraviolet (UV) exposure, smoking, nutrition, and air pollution, further contribute to skin deterioration. As a result, aged skin becomes more susceptible to mechanical damage, infections, and inflammatory and degenerative disorders3.

Intrinsic ageing is associated with reduced regenerative capacity and impaired skin homeostasis, characterized by decreased cell proliferation and accumulation of senescent cells4. These changes affect both major skin cell populations, fibroblasts and keratinocytes, leading to epidermal thinning and altered tissue organization5. Keratinocytes play a central role in maintaining barrier integrity, immune responses, and hydration6 whereas dermal fibroblasts are responsible for extracellular matrix (ECM) production and tissue remodelling7.

Among extrinsic factors, chronic UV exposure is a major contributor to skin ageing and accounts for a large proportion of visible skin changes8. UV radiation promotes the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the activation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), leading to a collagen degradation and fragmentation of ECM components9. The resulting reduction in collagen and elastin contribute to decreased skin elasticity and wrinkles formation10. Moreover, aged skin is characterized by reduced in hydration and increased transepidermal water loss11, partly due to decreased levels of hyaluronic acid (HA), a key molecule involved in water retention and structural stability12.

Skin ageing is also associated with alterations in the composition of the skin microbiota13, which plays an important role in maintaining barrier function and protecting against pathogens. Age-related changes in microbial composition have been reported, although a general decline in microbial diversity is commonly observed in elderly skin..

Several strategies have been explored to improve skin health and restore microbiota balance, including the use of probiotics, prebiotics, and bacterial lysates. However, the formulation of products containing live microorganisms presents stability and safety challenges. In this context, postbiotics, defined as microbial-derived metabolites, represent a promising alternative for topical applications.

Fructooligosaccharides (FOS) are commonly used as fermentation substrates for Lacticaseibacillus species, supporting the production of metabolites with potential relevance for skin homeostasis, while providing a translationally relevant alternative to standard bacterial culture media14,15.

In 2012, postbiotics were defined as “any factor resulting from the metabolic activity of a probiotic or any released molecule capable of conferring beneficial effects to the host in a direct or indirect way”14,16–18. More recently, this concept has been further refined by the International Probiotics Association (IPA), which proposed a harmonized framework for postbiotics and classified them into well-defined categories, including microbial metabolites (MM), defined as metabolic products released by microorganisms18–22. Among these, metabolites derived from lactic acid bacteria, such as short-chain fatty acids and lactate, have been associated with skin barrier function and hydration23. In addition, postbiotic derived from Lacticaseibacillus have been linked to the modulation of pathways involved in hyaluronic acid production and extracellular matrix regulation24. Consistent with this concept, recent evidence has identified microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolites as key regulators of epithelial barrier function, underscoring the role of host–microbe metabolic interactions in barrier homeostasis25. These microbial metabolites may contribute to the maintenance of dermal structure and function, highlighting their potential relevance for anti-ageing and skin-protective applications26–28.

Considering the emerging potential of postbiotics, the recent conceptual advances in their definition, as a mixture of metabolites during the fermentation process and not pure metabolites as previously used which fall in the category of ingredients or drugs, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the activity of a novel Lacticaseibacillus paracasei CNCM I-5220-derived postbiotic (formerly Lactobacillus paracasei1, classified as a MM19 (hereafter called PB-SKF) on the skin. Its effects were investigated in keratinocytes and fibroblasts representing complementary cellular models of skin physiology, and further evaluated in a clinical study on healthy volunteers.

Results

Postbiotic chemical characterization

To provide an initial characterization of PB-SKF composition, analytical data generated during product development were considered together with metabolomic analyses. PB-SKF consists of a complex mixture of metabolites generated during the fermentation of FOS by L. paracasei CNCM I-5220. Available data indicate the presence of multiple classes of fermentation-derived compounds, including organic acids and fatty acid derivatives. Among these, fatty acids such as oleic acid and decanoic acid have been reported in the fermented fraction, as described in patent literature (WO2019149941). In addition, previous metabolomic analyses identified several low-molecular-weight microbial metabolites associated with microbial metabolism, including phytosphingosine, tyramine, tryptamine and other intermediates of microbial metabolism29. Metabolomic profiling of different PB-SKF batches revealed a distinct biochemical signature compared with the corresponding control medium, as evidenced by the clear separation of clusters in principal component analysis (Supplementary data, Figure S1A). Moreover, among the metabolites contributing to this distinct clustering, tryptophan and indolelactate were identified (Supplementary data, Figure S1B). Several tryptophan-derived microbial metabolites, including indolelactate, have been reported to act as physiological agonists of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR)30.

Analytical testing revealed lactate as one of the major metabolites produced during fermentation, along with surface-active compounds in the fermented preparation, including anionic, cationic and non-ionic surfactant fractions. Analysis of 50 batches showed that the median lactate content was 72,60 mg/g. The median surfactant content across the batches was 288,3 mg/kg (Supplementary data, Figure S1D).

Overall, these findings indicate that PB-SKF is characterized by a complex and reproducible metabolite profile, which may contribute to the biological effects observed in this study.

PB-SKF treatment shows anti-ageing-like signature on keratinocytes

The epidermis, mainly composed of keratinocytes, represents the primary interface between our body and the external environment. Keratinocytes play a crucial role in maintaining barrier integrity, hydration, and protection against environmental stressors. To investigate the molecular signature of PB-SKF, keratinocytes were treated with three different concentrations of the postbiotic or the vehicle and subjected to RNA-Seq analysis. Transcriptomic profiling was used as an exploratory approach to identify biological processes modulated by PB-SKF treatment. The transcriptomic analysis therefore provided an initial overview of the biological pathways potentially modulated by PB-SKF, which were subsequently supported by functional assays targeting key extracellular matrix–related processes. PB-SKF induced a dose-dependent transcriptional response (Fig. 1A). Differential expression analysis identified 426/213, 1385/884, and 2448/1949 up- and downregulated genes at 1, 3, and 10 mg/mL, respectively (padj < 0.05) (Fig. 1A). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed a clear separation of PB-SKF-treated samples from vehicle-treated controls, particularly at the 3 and 10 mg/mL concentrations, while the 1 mg/mL condition showed a transcriptional profile closer to control samples (Fig. 1B). Given the highest transcriptional response at 10 mg/mL, subsequent analyses focused on this concentration. Among the most upregulated genes by PB-SKF, HAS3 (log2FC 2.70, padj 1.66E-104), encoding hyaluronic acid synthase 3, was prominently induced. Pathway enrichment analysis identified several PB-SKF upregulated pathways associated with epidermal barrier function and tissue repair, including mTORC1 signaling31 and cholesterol metabolism, as well as IL-2 and TGF-β signaling pathways32,33 (Supplementary data, Figure S2A). These processes are known to be altered during skin ageing, including reduced lipid synthesis and impaired wound healing34,35. In addition, genes associated with UV-response pathways were significantly enriched. These pathways are typically involved in cellular responses to oxidative and environmental stress and are linked to photoaging-related mechanisms8, suggesting the induction of a broad spectrum protective response. Detailed information on the genes contributing to each enriched Hallmark and Gene Ontology (GO) term, along with the associated enrichment metrics, is provided in Supplementary document “Gene Set Enrichment Analysis_Gene List”.

PB-SKF treatment did not induce relevant cytotoxicity, as cell viability remained above 70% under all tested conditions (Supplementary data, Figure S2B), although a modest but statistically significant reduction was observed at 24 h compared with the vehicle. To further explore the relationship with age-related processes, gene expression data were compared with the “Human Ageing Genomic Resources (HAGR)” databases36. PB-SKF treatment modulated a subset of genes associated with ageing-related signatures (Fig. 1C), including genes involved in extracellular matrix regulation, lipid metabolism and cellular stress responses. Notably, COL4A1 expression, encoding collagen type IV α1 chain, was increased while genes involved in cholesterol and lipid metabolism, such as HMGCS1 and GRAMD1C, were also modulated. These processes are known to be altered in aged skin and contribute to changes in epidermal barrier function and dermal structure37–40. Overall, these results indicate that PB-SKF modulates gene expression patterns associated with pathways relevant to skin ageing and to an oxidative stress protective response at large.

PB-SKF treatment increases the release of hyaluronic acid in keratinocytes and modulates pro-collagen production in fibroblasts under oxidative stress

One of the hallmarks of skin ageing is the reduction in hydration, associated with increased transepidermal water loss and skin dryness. HA, a hydrophilic polymer synthesized by keratinocytes, is a major component of the ECM and plays a pivotal role in water retention and, skin hydration12. HAS3 is one of the most active enzymes involved in HA synthesis and secretion in keratinocytes41. Based on the transcriptomic data showing HAS3 upregulation, we assessed its expression at the mRNA levels. PB-SKF treatment induced a dose-dependent increase in HAS3 expression after 6 h (Fig. 2A). Besides HA, collagen is a major ECM that is progressively decreased in aged skin42. Collagen is mostly produced by fibroblasts in the skin43, thus we switched to this cell type to address whether PB-SKF treatment may favor the production of collagen, Oxidative stress which characterizes both aged and UV-exposed skin, contributes to collagen degradation through increased ROS production6. To model oxidative stress conditions, fibroblasts were exposed to hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). As expected, H₂O₂ treatment reduced both cell viability and pro-collagen type I α1 levels after 24 h (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Figure S3A). Under these conditions, PB-SKF treatment increased pro-collagen compared to vehicle-treated cells. Importantly, when pro-collagen levels were normalized to the percentage of viable cells, the stimulatory effect of PB-SKF remained evident, indicating that the observed modulation was not solely attributable to differences in cell number. Pre-treatment with PB-SKF for 24 h before H2O2 stimulation resulted in a dose-dependent increase in pro-collagen release (Figs. 2D, E). PB-SKF treatment did not alter fibroblast viability (Supplementary data, Figure S3B).

Taken together, these results show that PB-SKF modulates extracellular matrix-related processes, including HA release in keratinocytes and pro-collagen in fibroblasts under oxidative stress conditions.

PB-SKF treatment promotes fibroblast migration in a wound healing-related model

Skin ageing is associated with impaired regenerative capacity, including delayed wound healing. This process is influenced by several factors, such as increased inflammatory signaling, reduced immune cell recruitment, and impaired fibroblast proliferation, migration and extracellular matrix production34. Dermal fibroblasts play a central role in tissue repair by migrating to the wound site, produce ECM components, and contributing to tissue remodeling44–46. To assess the effect of PB-SKF on fibroblast migration, a scratch assay was performed. NHDF cells were pre-treated with PB-SKF or vehicle prior to scratch induction, and wound closure was monitored over 72 h (Fig. 3A). PB-SKF treatment increased the percentage of wound closure compared to vehicle-treated cells. After 24 h, wound closure reached 58% in PB-SKF-treated cells versus 41% in controls (Fig. 3B). As shown in Fig. 3C, PB-SKF treatment induced an effect comparable to the positive control epidermal growth factor (EGF), with a significant increase in wound closure observed over 72-h treatment. PB-SKF treatment improves skin parameters in a clinical evaluation

Skin ageing is associated with reduced collagen and hyaluronic acid levels, leading to reduced elasticity and hydration10,47.

Based on the in vitro findings showing modulation of HA and pro-collagen production, a clinical evaluation was conducted to assess the effects of PB-SKF on skin functional parameters. The study was performed as a single-center, randomized, placebo-controlled, intra-individual (split-face) cosmetic evaluation involving 20 healthy volunteers. Participants applied a cream containing PB-SKF to one side of the face and a placebo formulation to the contralateral side twice a day for six weeks (Fig. 4A). Instrumental measurements were performed at baseline and after treatment using validated non-invasive techniques. Compared with the placebo-treated side, PB-SKF treatment significantly increased skin hydration (Fig. 4B) and elasticity (Fig. 4C) and promoted skin extensibility (Fig. 4D). Moreover, a significant reduction in skin roughness was observed, including both average roughness (Ra) (Fig. 4E) and maximum roughness (Rz) (Fig. 4F). These findings were further confirmed by the 3D skin surface analysis, which showed a visible reduction of wrinkle depth at the end of the treatment (Fig. 4G). Discussion

Skin ageing is a multifactorial process affecting tissue regeneration, ECM organization and skin homeostasis4. Among the most relevant changes are reduced cell proliferation5 and the decreased synthesis of ECM components, including collagen, elastin and HA, which contribute to reduced elasticity, increased dryness and wrinkle formation47–49.

In the present study, we investigated the effects of PB-SKF, a postbiotic derived from L. paracasei CNCM I-5220, using a multi-level approach combining transcriptomic analysis, in vitro functional assays and a clinical evaluation. Several strategies have been proposed to improve the health of aged skin. Certainly, the use of living or tyndallized probiotics and synbiotics, as well as prebiotics and bacterial lysates, has been increasing in skin products to reestablish the skin microbiota homeostasis50. Topical application of probiotics has also been explored for their ability to counteract oxidative stress and modulate inflammatory responses in the skin. In particular, Lacticaseibacillus species have been associated with improved skin hydration and reduced wrinkle formation50–52. However, the formulation of topical products containing live microorganisms remains challenging due to stability and safety concerns, as well as the requirement for microbial viability at the time of application14,21,53.

In this context, postbiotics represent a promising alternative for topical application. Unlike probiotics, postbiotics do not rely on viable microorganisms and are therefore less sensitive to environmental conditions such as temperature and humidity, offering advantages in terms of formulation stability and safety. The definition of postbiotics has evolved over time and has been recently refined by the International Probiotics Association, which classifies microbial metabolites (MM) as a distinct category22. Within this classification, PB-SKF can be categorized as a microbial metabolite–based postbiotic, consisting of bioactive compounds generated during the fermentation of L. paracasei.

FOS act as selective substrates for Lacticaseibacillus species, supporting their metabolic activity and the production of bioactive metabolites. These metabolites have been associated with several processes relevant to skin homeostasis, including maintenance of an acidic skin environment, support of epidermal barrier function, enhancement of hydration, and modulation of local inflammatory response15,54,55. Among these, short-chain fatty acids and lactate have been reported to contribute to skin barrier integrity and hydration, while Lacticaseibacillus-derived postbiotics have been associated with the modulation of hyaluronic acid–related pathways and extracellular matrix regulation, including collagen and elastin deposition through growth factor–dependent signaling. These mechanisms are consistent with the effects observed in the present study, including modulation of HA-related pathways, pro-collagen production under oxidative stress, and wound healing-related model23,24,27,28,56–60.

Firstly, we evaluated the activity of PB-SKF in human keratinocytes, which play a central role in maintaining epidermal barrier integrity and hydration. Transcriptomic analysis indicated that PB-SKF modulates pathways associated with epidermal barrier, wound healing and response to environmental stressors. These processes are known to be altered during ageing, which is characterized by reduced levels of HA10. In particular, PB-SKF treatment induced upregulation of genes involved in extracellular matrix regulation, including HAS3 and COL4A1, as well as genes related to lipid metabolism9. The strong upregulation of HAS3 was validated at the mRNA level and supported by increased hyaluronic acid production, suggesting a role of PB-SKF in pathways related to skin hydration. The transcriptomic analysis should be considered exploratory, and further validation of additional targets will be required to clarify the underlying molecular mechanisms. Consistently, PB-SKF increased HA release in keratinocytes and modulates pro-collagen production in fibroblasts under oxidative stress. Notably, the magnitude of these effects is comparable to that reported for established modulators of extracellular matrix processes, such as ascorbic acid and TGF-β –dependent pathways, which are known to induce moderate but biologically relevant changes in matrix components61–64. Although direct benchmarking with reference anti-ageing compounds was not included in our experimental design, the magnitude of the effects observed with PB-SKF on pro-collagen and hyaluronic acid production is consistent with those reported in the literature for established modulators of extracellular matrix processes. In this context, the ability of PB-SKF to promote HA and pro-collagen release supports the biological relevance of its activity and places this postbiotic within mechanistic frameworks typically associated with established anti-ageing reference compounds. In addition, PB-SKF enhanced fibroblast migration in a wound healing-related model, further supporting its role in processes associated with tissue repair.

These in vitro findings were supported by the clinical evaluation, where PB-SKF treatment improved skin hydration, elasticity and surface parameters compared to placebo. A reduction in skin roughness and an increase in dermal echogenicity was also observed, suggesting changes in dermal structural organization. However, given the limited sample size and the exploratory nature of the study, these results should be considered preliminary.

The observed comprehensive biological effects may be related to the complex composition of PB-SKF, which includes multiple classes of fermentation-derived metabolites such as organic acids, fatty acid derivatives, lactate and other low-molecular-weight microbial compounds. Several of these metabolites have been associated with biological processes relevant to skin physiology. For example, phytosphingosine has been linked to epidermal barrier function and anti-inflammatory responses65, while microbial tryptophan and indole derivatives can act as signaling molecules involved in host–microbe interactions, and enhance barrier function66. In addition, lactate and fatty acid derivatives contribute to skin hydration and barrier function67.

Microbial biosurfactants produced by lactic acid bacteria have been reported to modulate microbial adhesion, biofilm formation and host–microbe interactions and have been proposed as potential functional ingredients in dermatological and cosmetic applications 68. These components may therefore contribute, at least in part, to the biological activities observed in this study.

A limitation of the present study is that, although several classes of fermentation-derived metabolites have been identified in PB-SKF, a comprehensive chemical characterization of PB-SKF and the contribution of individual components remain to be elucidated. The observed effects are likely the result of additive or synergistic interactions among multiple metabolites. Further studies will be required to better define the postbiotic composition and its mechanisms of action.

In conclusion, PB-SKF modulates pathways and functional process relevant to skin physiology and improves multiple skin parameters in a clinical setting, supporting its potential role in maintaining skin homeostasis.

Materials and methods

Postbiotic chemical characterization

The chemical composition of PB-SKF was assessed using analytical data generated during product development and metabolomic analyses. PB-SKF is obtained through the fermentation of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei CNCM I-5220 and consists of a complex mixture of fermentation-derived metabolites.

Targeted analytical measurements (HPLC–DAD) were used to quantify lactate as a major fermentation-derived metabolite. Untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed to identify low-molecular-weight microbial metabolites associated with bacterial metabolic pathways.

In addition, external analytical testing (colorimetric assays) was conducted to detect surface-active compounds, including anionic, cationic and non-ionic surfactant fractions.

In vitro experimental design

Immortalized human keratinocytes (HaCaT were purchased from Cell Lines Service GmbH, Eppelheim, Germany(#300493) and primary Normal Human Dermal Fibroblasts (NHDF, Promocell #C-12302) were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum, 1% Glutamine, 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For gene expression evaluation, HaCaT cells were seeded in 6-well plates (2 × 105 cells/well). For protein analysis, cell were seeded in 96-well plates (4 × 104 cells/well). After 24 h, cell were incubated overnight in starvation medium (DMEM with 2% FBS). Cells were treated with PB-SKF (1, 3, and 10 mg/mL) or vehicle control. For gene expression evaluation, cells were collected after 6 h of treatment, while for protein evaluation supernatants were collected after 48 h of treatment. NHDF were 96-well plates (6 × 103 cells/well) in complete medium for 24 h. Then, cells were pre-treated for 24 h with PB-SKF using a five-point 1:3 serial dilution (10, 3.33, 1.11, 0.37, and 0.12 mg/mL) or with the vehicle control in starvation medium , followed by stimulation with 125 µM H2O2 for 24 h. Supernatants were collected to measure the levels of human pro-collagen I α1 by ELISA.

Cell viability was assessed in NHDF seeded 96-well plates (6 × 103 cells/ well) under the same experimental conditions. For the wound healing assay, NHDF were seeded in 6-well (1.8 × 105 cells /well), cultured for 24 h and treated with PB-SKF or vehicle prior to scratch induction. Wound closure was monitored over time as described below.

Postbiotic production

PB-SKF was produced by Postbiotica S.r.l. using the PBTech® technology (described in patent WO 2019/149941). PB-SKF was obtained starting from L. paracasei CNCM I-5220, a strain selected based on previous in vitro studies showing low pro-inflammatory activity and protective effects in inflammatory models69. The strain belonged to a well-characterized L. paracasei lineage, for which phenotypic and genotypic characterization has been previously reported in the literature70,71. The bacterial culture was grown at 37 °C in fermentation medium. The collected bacterial biomass was then transferred to a minimal solution media supplemented with prebiotic fibers (short-chain FOS) and was incubated for an additional 24 h to allow fermentation. After fermentation (4000 rpm at 4 °C), the supernatant was separated from the biomass by centrifugation, pasteurized at 90 °C for 10 min subsequently spray-dried in the presence of maltodextrin. Therefore, PB-SKF contained microbial metabolites released during bacterial fermentation. Maltodextrin was therefore used as the vehicle control in all experiments. In addition, for the in vitro experiments, the postbiotic preparation was further filtered (0.22 μm) to ensure the absence of any residual bacterial components that could interfere with cell-based assays. Targeted quantification of lactate and surface-active compounds, including anionic, cationic and non-ionic surfactant fractions were performed by an external analytical laboratory using standard analytical methods.

Metabolomics analysis

Global metabolomics profiling was performed by Metabolon Inc. In brief, samples were prepared using an automated MicroLab STAR system (Hamilton Co). Sample preparation was conducted using methanol extractions to remove the protein fraction while allowing maximum recovery of chemically diverse metabolites. The resulting extract was divided into five fractions: two for analysis by two separate reverse phase (RP)/UPLC-MS/MS methods with positive ion mode electrospray ionization (ESI), one for analysis by RP/UPLC-MS/MS with negative ion mode ESI, one for analysis by HILIC/UPLC-MS/MS with negative ion mode ESI, and one sample was reserved for backup. Samples were placed briefly on a TurboVap (Zymark) to remove the organic solvent. All methods utilized a Waters ACQUITY ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) and a Thermo Scientific Q-Exactive high resolution/accurate mass spectrometer interfaced with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI-II) source and Orbitrap mass analyzer operated at 35,000 mass resolution. The sample extract was dried then reconstituted in solvents compatible to each of the four methods. Each reconstitution solvent contained a series of standards at fixed concentrations to ensure injection and chromatographic consistency. Raw data were extracted, peak-identified and QC processed using Metabolon’s hardware and software. Compounds were identified by comparison to library entries of purified standards or recurrent unknown entities that contains the retention time/index (RI), mass to charge ratio (m/z), and chromatographic data (including MS/MS spectral data) for all molecules present in the library. For each metabolite, the raw values in the experimental samples were divided by the median of those samples in each instrument batch, giving each batch and thus the metabolite a median of one. Batch-normalized data, simply reflecting the median-scaled raw data, were divided by the value of the normalizer. Then, each metabolite has been normalized to osmolarity and was rescaled to have median 1/4 1 (divide the new values by the overall median for each metabolite). Data were expressed as a scaled intensity value. Welch’s 2-sample t-test is used to test whether 2 unknown means are different from 2 independent populations29.

RNA sequencing data analysis in HaCaT keratinocytes

Quality control of raw data was performed with fastqc v0.11.8. Quality filtering was performed with trimmomatic v0.39 setting the following parameters: CROP:74 MINLEN:50 AVGQUAL:25. High-quality reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38 primary assembly genome) using STAR (v2.7.6a). Reads were assigned to genes with featureCounts (v2.0.1) using the gencode.v42.annotation.gtf gene transfer file (GTF) as a reference annotation file for genomic feature boundaries. Processing of raw counts, exploratory data analysis and differential expression analysis were performed using DESeq2 (v1.38.3) R package. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to investigate the changes induced by PB-SKF treatment72. Only list of differentially expressed genes with following criteria were used: padj < 0.05 and |log2 Fold Change|> 0.5. The complete list of genes driving each enriched Hallmark/GO term is provided as Supplementary document “Gene Set Enrichment Analysis_Gene List”.

The files used as input for pre-ranked GSEA analysis were the list of differentially expressed genes of the experiment ranked by log2 Fold Change and the reference files h.all.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt and c5.all.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt. Heatmap was generated using the pheatmap R package: normalized data obtained with DESeq2 were log2 transformed, given as input to the pheatmap() function and row-wise scaled (padj < 0.05).

Protein evaluation in HaCaT keratinocytes and NHDF fibroblasts

Human HA and pro-collagen protein evaluation were performed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (#DY3614, #DY6220 R&D System, Inc. Bio-Techne) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Optical density was measured at 450 nm on CLARIOstar microplate reader (BMG Labtech).

Cell viability assay in NHDF fibroblasts

Cell viability of NHDF fibroblasts was determined using the Thiazolyl Blue Tetrazolium Bromide (MTT) reduction assay. After 24 h of H2O2 treatment, supernatants were removed and 0.45 mg/mL MTT (#ab146345, Abcam) was added in each well in starvation medium, and incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C in 5% CO2. After 3 h, the MTT solution was removed, and the formazan crystals were diluted with pure dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The absorbance was measured at 570 nm with a reference wavelength of 640 nm on CLARIOstar microplate reader (BMG Labtech).

Wound healing assay in NHDF fibroblasts

NHDF fibroblasts were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1.8 × 105 cells/well in a complete medium for 24 h. Then, cells were maintained in starvation medium overnight and then pre-treated with 0.1 mg/mL PB-SKF or its vehicle for 24 h. EGF (Recombinant Human EGF #AF-100–15, Peprotech) (0.1 µg/mL) was used as positive control. Scratches were made across the well in a cross pattern, incubated for an additional 72 h with the treatment in the starvation medium, and monitored using microscopic images every 24 h. Scratch closure was calculated for each time point as percentage relative to the scratch area at T0. The distance between the margins of the wound was measured with MRI_Wound Healing Tool of ImageJ.

Instrumental evaluations of anti-ageing efficacy of PB-SKF

The clinical test was performed by Institute of Skin and Product Evaluation (ISPE s.r.l. in Milan, Italy) as a single-center exploratory cosmetic clinical evaluation to assess instrumental skin parameters under real-use conditions. Formal approval was not required under local regulations; however, the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and GCP. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Table 2. 20 female volunteers (average age 61.3 ± 4.9 years) were enrolled and completed the study. A randomized split-face design was applied, in which each participant received the PB.SKF formulation on one side of the face and the placebo on the contralateral side, twice a day for six weeks. The active and placebo creams were identical in appearance with the active formulation consisted of the placebo cream supplemented with 2% PB-SKF. The allocation of the active or placebo cream to the right or left side of the face was randomly assigned according to a predefined randomization list, ensuring blinding of the participants to treatment assignment. Participants were instructed to avoid the use of other facial cosmetic products and excessive UV exposure during the study period. Compliance with study instructions was monitored during scheduled visits at baseline and after 6 weeks. Skin hydration was measured using a Corneometer CM 825 (Courage & Khazaka, Germany), which assesses the capacitance of the stratum corneum and expresses results in corneometric units (c.u.). Skin mechanical properties were evaluated using a Cutometer MPA 580 (Courage & Khazaka, Germany), which measures vertical skin deformation induced by negative pressure. Three suction–release cycles were performed at each measurement site. The parameters analyzed were R0 (skin extensibility; lower values indicate higher firmness) and R2 (gross elasticity; ratio of recovery to final deformation). Skin roughness was assessed using silicone replicas (SILFLO, J&S Davis Ltd., UK), analyzed with Quantilines software (Monaderm) under standardized illumination conditions.
