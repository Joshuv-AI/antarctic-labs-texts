The ternary nanocomposite g-C3N4/TiO2@polyaniline embedded in PVDF based triboelectric nanogenerator for energy harvesting
Authors: Ashok Kumar Swami, Nelapati Avinash, Deepak Verma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a materials-engineering strategy to enhance triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG) output by embedding a ternary g-C₃N₄/TiO₂@polyaniline (GTP) nanocomposite into PVDF tribopositive electrodes and pairing them with PTFE tribonegative layers of systematically varied composition from 30 to 60 wt%). XPS, TEM, FESEM/EDX, UV–Vis, and DSC confirm that TiO₂ nanoparticles are well distributed on g-C₃N₄ nanosheets and interconnected through a conducting PANI network, with evidence of interfacial Ti–O–C/Ti–N bonding, Ti3⁺ associated defect states, and band-tail absorption (Urbach energy, EU = 0.31 eV), indicating localized electronic states that support charge storage/transfer.” In vertical contact–separation mode, the devices exhibit a composition-dependent trade-off: the 30 wt% PTFE configuration delivers the highest short-circuit current (Isc = 2.86 μA) and peak power (Pmax = 10.3 μW), whereas the 60 wt% PTFE device yields the maximum open-circuit voltage (Voc = 4.56 V). These results demonstrate that voltage–current characteristics can be tuned through interfacial state engineering and controlled adjustment of the tribonegative layer composition. To confirm repeatability and stability under manual tapping, the best-performing 30 wt% and 60 wt% devices has been validated using an automated cycle-resolved statistical analysis.Keywords: Ternary nanocomposite, g-C3N4/TiO2@PANI, Triboelectric nanogenerator, PVDF based TENG. Introduction

Triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs), which transform mechanical energy such as that found in motion, vibration, and human activity into electrical energy, have become a particularly appealing option for low-power electronics due to their straightforward device architectures, wide range of materials, environmental friendliness, high instantaneous power density, and inexpensive manufacturing methods, and ability to deliver a significant electrical output appropriate for powering sensors and low-energy devices. The triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG), an effective device that can transform mechanical stimulation into electrical output, was initially developed by Z. L. Wang1 and his team in 20122. The triboelectric nanogenerator works on triboelectrification and that is the combined effect of contact electrification and electrostatic induction. The process of bringing two distinct electron affinities of triboelectric materials into physical contact either by pressing, sliding, or separating is known as triboelectrification. Both electrodes separate when the external force is released, causing the potential to drop suddenly. Electrons move between the electrodes to counterbalance this, completing the half-cycle of the TENG’s AC output. This process repeatedly occurs and provide positive and negative output voltage and current. The current output of TENG device is called displacement current3. The output performance of TENG device critically depend on surface functionalization, interfacial charge trapping and transport, and the dielectric and mechanical properties of the contacting layers4,5. TENG performance and stability can therefore be enhanced by designing materials that increase charge creation, inhibit recombination, and optimize charge extraction.

Most commonly utilized polymer materials include conducting polymers like polyaniline (PANI) and poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS), water-soluble/processible polymers like polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), and electroactive and dielectric polymers like poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF), polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), and polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)6,7. Due to their inherent propensity for transferring or accept electrons, polymers like PTFE, PDMS, and PVDF hold crucial positions in the triboelectric series. Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) is considered a highly tribo-negative material due to its remarkable chemical and thermal durability, low surface friction, and great electrical insulation. Haiyang Zou et al.8 stated that PTFE has a triboelectric charge density of −113 μCm−2. Although poly (vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) combines triboelectric and piezoelectric responses in a unique way, its low electrical conductivity prevents direct charge transport to external circuits9,10. To get around this, PVDF is frequently combined with conductive pathways or charge-transfer agents (such as conductive fillers, interfacial electrodes, or dopants) to enable effective charge extraction11,12. PVDF was selected as the host polymer for the ternary g-C₃N₄/TiO₂@PANI (GTP) filler because of its excellent film-forming ability, mechanical flexibility, and ferroelectric β-phase, which can enhance interfacial polarization and charge storage in composite triboelectric layers. Functional fillers provide a practical way for modifying microstructure, surface affinity, and dielectric constant, which in turn can modify charge generation, storage, and retention. In this regard, conductive or redox-active polymers have garnered widespread interest in hybrid and composite materials that include high-dielectric or charge-trapping inorganic components. In the polymer matrix incorporation of two dimensional (2D) material and inorganic nanoparticles can effectively (i) increase charge trapping and capturing capability, (ii) enhance the effective permittivity to boost stored charge, and (iii) alter surface roughness to enlarge real contact area13,14. When appropriately balanced to prevent leakage, conductive polymers can simultaneously enhance charge transport to electrodes. Despite these alluring synergies, a significant obstacle still lies in creating ternary systems that take advantage of the complementing qualities of several fillers while preventing aggregation, phase separation, and mechanical stiffening15.

Polyaniline (PANI), titanium dioxide (TiO₂), and graphitic carbon nitride (g-C₃N₄) make up an attractive trio of useful elements for TENG electrodes. In exfoliated forms, g-C₃N₄ nanosheets have a high specific surface area, intrinsic trap states that can localize charge, and a large number of lone-pair electrons on nitrogen atoms. Because of these characteristics, when applied to the polymer surface, it functions as a surface modifier and a charge reservoir16. The chemical stability, polarization mediated by defects, high density of oxygen-vacancy-related trap states, and comparatively large dielectric constant of TiO2 nanoparticles are well known; even very low loadings can raise the composite permittivity and produce deep traps that lessen charge recombination17.

Polyaniline (PANI) is a conducting polymer with strong electron-donating properties, which is due to abundant amino groups18. Protonation (doping) can be used to tune PANI, which provides polaron/bipolaron states that enable quick lateral conduction of charges to the electrode and enable charge extraction19. PANI offers regulated conductivity for charge collection, while g-C₃N₄ and TiO₂ provide trapping and dielectric enhancement when embedded in a compliant polymer binder such as PVDF. A potent tactic for promoting the enhanced TENG performance is to combine the special benefits of g-C3N4, TiO2, and PANI to create effective tribo-positive materials. In the GTP ternary composite, protonated (amine-rich) PANI serves three complementary functions: (i) it creates a polaronic/conductive bridge between g-C₃N₄ and TiO₂, which promotes interfacial charge transfer and stabilises trapped charges (σ₀ t); (ii) it improves the composite’s local polarizability, favouring stored charge and Voc; and (iii) it is chemically and processing-compatible with PVDF (solvent, spin-coating, annealing), enabling a continuous, repeatable composite film20. Regretfully, composite materials made of g-C3N4, TiO2 and PANI have not yet been investigated for application in TENGs.

This systematic study addresses the tribo-positive layer in contact-separation TENGs by synthesizing and systematically characterizing a ternary g-C₃N₄/TiO₂@PANI (GTP) nanocomposite embedded in PVDF. In this work we have developed a facile and reproducible in-situ composite synthesis route that yields well-dispersed g-C₃N₄ nanosheets and TiO₂ nanoparticles attached with PANI matrix. After synthesis of ternary GTP nanocomposite was embedded in 3 wt% PVDF dispersion in dimethylformamide (DMF) by simple mixing method. g-C3N4, TiO2, PANI, and ternary GTP nanocomposite’s structural, thermal and chemical properties (SEM, TEM, XRD, FTIR, DSC and UV-Vis) were analysed with triboelectric behaviour. And systematic investigation clarifies the ways in which each component—g-C₃N₄, TiO₂, and PANI—affects charge generation, trapping, and extraction in order to determine composition and microstructure windows that optimize electrical output and stability. We compare the device’s performance to that of PTFE-based tribonegative equivalents with different PTFE contents from 30 wt% PTFE to 60 wt% PTFE dispersion in Milli Q water. The primary novelty of this work lies in purposeful combination of three complementary functional fillers (g-C₃N₄, TiO2 and PANI) within PVDF matrix and systematic characterization of structure, property and device relationships. Although single- or binary-component fillers have been demonstrated to improve TENG output in previous research, but there is limited thorough investigation of ternary nanocomposite systems, especially those that take advantage of both trapping (g-C₃N₄, TiO₂) and regulated conductivity (PANI) in a polymer binder. This work aims to provide mechanistic insight and useful guidelines for engineering composite tribo-layers for high-performance TENGs by combining advanced characterization with device-level electrical measurements (open-circuit voltage and short-circuit current). In the present study, the objective is not to benchmark absolute TENG output relative to pristine PVDF systems but rather to systematically investigate the influence of ternary g-C₃N₄/TiO₂@PANI interfacial engineering within the PVDF matrix and the effect of varying PTFE concentration in the tribonegative layer. Therefore, comparisons are restricted within the GTP–PVDF/PTFE compositional framework.

Experimental section

Materials

Aniline (C6H5NH2), Hydrochloric acid (HCl), Ammonium per Sulphate (APS), Thiourea (CH₄N₂S), Dimethylformamide (DMF) {(CH₃)₂NC(O)H}, Isopropyl Alcohol (IPA) and Ethanol (C2H5OH) was purchased from LOBA Chemie, Mumbai INDIA. Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) 60 wt% dispersion in Milli-Q water has been purchased from Sigma Aldrich. TiO2 nanoparticles (rutile phase) purchased from Techinstro industries Maharastra, INDIA.

Synthesis of g-C3N4 nanosheets

The g-C3N4 nanosheets were produced using thiourea powder as a precursor. Five grams of thiourea were put in a round quartz crucible, enclosed with a lid, and heated for three hours at 500 °C in an air-filled muffle furnace. The yellow bulk g-C3N4 powder was gathered once the furnace had cooled. The bulk g-C3N4 was then heated for thermal exfoliation for three hours at 500°C in a muffle furnace to create g-C3N4 nanosheets.

Synthesis of ternary g-C₃N₄/TiO₂@PANI (GTP) nanocomposite and Polyaniline (PANI)

A straightforward in-situ chemical oxidative polymerization procedure was used to synthesize the ternary g-C₃N₄/TiO₂@PANI (GTP) nanocomposite, with the precursor weight percentage ratio being 70:25:5, respectively. After 30 minutes of ultrasonication, a homogenous mixture consisting of g-C₃N₄ (1g) and TiO2 NPs (357mg) was prepared in a Milli Q water and ethanol (1:1) solvent. The dispersion was then moved to an ice bath (0–5 °C) with a 1 M HCl solution, and it was agitated for five minutes. Afterwards, 0.107g of aniline was added to the cold mixture. The polymerization process involved the dropwise addition of oxidant APS (315 mg in 15 ml water) while being continuously stirred. For three hours, the color changed from pale yellow to dark blue and was stirred. The solution was then filtered, extensively cleaned with water and IPA until it reached a pH between 6 to 7 (slightly acidic), and then dried at 70 °C in hot air oven. PANI was synthesized using a similar technique without the presence of TiO2 and g-C₃N₄.

Preparation of g-C₃N₄/TiO₂@PANI (GTP) nanocomposite embedded PVDF dispersion

A homogenous suspension of PVDF embedded in GTP nanocomposite was made by dissolving 150 mg of PVDF powder in 5 mL of N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) at 60 °C while being stirred magnetically until the powder was completely dissolved. After that, 100 mg of GTP nanocomposite was added to the PVDF solution, and stirred for 30 minutes, the mixture was constantly swirled to ensure that the nanoparticles were evenly distributed throughout the polymer matrix. In order to eliminate any agglomerates and provide a uniform nanocomposite solution appropriate for film preparation, the resultant suspension was further passed through a 0.45 μm PTFE syringe filter.

Preparation of 30wt%, 40wt%, 50wt% and 60wt% PTFE dispersions

Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) dispersions were made in different weight percentages (30, 40, 50, and 60 wt%). The stock solution was a PTFE aqueous dispersion that was commercially available (60 wt% in water, Sigma-Aldrich). The stock dispersion was diluted using Milli-Q water (18.2 MΩ·cm) to reach the desired weight percentages, yielding the lower concentration dispersions (30 wt%, 40 wt%, and 50 wt%). Before use, every dispersion was well dispersed in order to ensure uniformity.

Spin-coating of triboelectric layers

Prior to spin coating, the ITO substrates were thoroughly cleaned by subsequent ultrasonication in detergent solution, Milli-Q water, isopropanol, and acetone to remove surface contaminants and improve film adhesion. The ITO-coated glass substrates were used as electrodes due to their smooth surface and high electrical conductivity, providing a rigid support for the active layer under low to moderate contact forces typical of low-power triboelectric energy harvesting. The deposited GTP–PVDF and PTFE films were thermally treated at 120 °C and 250 °C, respectively, to promote solvent removal and enhance interfacial bonding with the substrate. The resulting coatings exhibited stable adhesion during device assembly and repeated contact–separation operation without observable peeling or delamination. An NXG-P1 spin coater was used to fabricate all the functional films for the TENG device. The substrates were commercially available 2.5 × 2.5 cm2 ITO coated glass slides (Sigma-Aldrich), with specification of surface resistivity 70-100 Ω/sq, and transmittance 86% (nominal at 550nm). In order to create electrical contacts, silver conductive ink was applied to the exposed ITO surface and allowed to cure in a hot air oven. To avoid coating, Kapton (polyimide) tape was used to mask the ITO substrate’s contact area. The NXG-P1 spin coater was then used to spin-coat the produced nanocomposite solutions onto the ITO substrates that had been cleaned. Cross-section SEM was utilised to determine the thickness of prepared tribo-positive and negative layers (films) coated on a clean glass substrate. The average thickness of the spin-coated GTP mixed PVDF composite positive layer is 11.7 ± 2.1 μm (n = 5 measurement points) and the PTFE negative layer is 0.7 μm (700 ± 40 nm, n = 5).

TENG device assembly

In order to achieve controlled vertical displacement, the TENG device has been assembled together in a vertical contact-separation mode arrangement using two plastic platforms supported by four helical springs placed at the corners. The tribo-positive and tribo-negative electrodes were kept 3 cm apart to provide sufficient contact-separation movement while the device was in function. With a free length of 4 cm, an outer coil diameter of 5 mm, and a wire diameter of roughly 0.8 mm, the springs used offered reliable contact-separation behavior and structural stability even after repeated cycling. The plastic platforms were attached to the ITO substrates with deposited triboelectric coatings so that the active surfaces faced one another. Statistical data analysis

Data processing and analysis

In order to preserve optimum measurement circumstances and suitable impedance matching for each signal type, electrical output signals, such as voltage and current, were measured in independent experimental runs. This method is consistent with established procedures for characterizing triboelectric nanogenerators21. Using pandas, numpy, and scipy tools, a custom Python analysis script was created to handle raw data with automatic cycle-level quantification, maintaining analytical consistency and reproducibility across datasets.

Signal pre-processing

Raw time-series data was processed systematically before analysis. In order to correct sampling abnormalities such as duplicate timestamps and non-monotonic time steps, time vectors were processed and standardized to a strictly monotonic seconds-based format. Such anomalies are known sources of artifacts in numerical differentiation and integration processes carried out on high-impedance signals, which is why these modifications were required22. To maintain signal integrity throughout the characterization procedure, separate analytical pipelines were used to process the voltage and current datasets.

Cycle detection and segmentation

An automatic valley-to-valley identification technique was used to segment individual electrical cycles. This approach ensures that every extracted cycle includes a full active pulse without truncation by locating local minima in the signal that match to boundaries between subsequent contact-separation events. In triboelectric nanogenerator waveform analysis, valley-to-valley segmentation techniques of this kind are frequently used, especially for measuring per-cycle electrical outputs under non-periodic or transient excitation conditions23. An interquartile-range outlier detection criterion was used to rigorously reject non-physical cycles caused by electrical breakdown, irregular sparking, or acquisition artifacts in order to assure data quality. The interquartile range is defined as IQR = Q3 − Q1, where Q1 is the metric distribution’s first quartile (25th percentile) and Q3 is its third quartile (75th percentile). Cycles with metric values beyond the range [Q1 − 1.5×IQR, Q3 + 1.5×IQR] were identified as outliers and eliminated from further examination24. Note that the transmitted electrical charge, which is represented separately in this work, should not be confused with the quartile notation Q1 and Q3, which only pertains to statistical percentiles used for outlier identification.

Metric calculation

For open-circuit voltage measurements, the peak voltage (Voc, peak) was directly calculated from each segmented voltage cycle. Short-circuit current and transferred charge measurements required more sophisticated processing to address baseline drift and spurious charge accumulation, which are well-documented challenges in triboelectric nanogenerator current characterization25,26. Three successive phases were included in the current analysis methodology to ensure precise charge quantification. To eliminate systematic offsets, a cycle-specific baseline current was first calculated by calculating the median current value during each cycle’s quiescent pre-pulse region. This baseline was then removed from the entire waveform. Second, the temporal interval where the absolute current magnitude above five percent of the peak pulse amplitude was isolated to determine the active pulse window. By removing inactive areas where drift predominates, this threshold-based window definition assures that charge integration only records the actual triboelectric response. Third, the baseline-corrected current over the specified active window was numerically trapezoidally integrated to determine the transferred charge per cycle (ΔQ). In order to produce stable and repeatable cycle-resolved charge estimations, this pulse-resolved integration technique efficiently suppresses non-physical charge drift that builds up during inactive intervals between contact-separation events27. Because cumulative drift can seriously skew charge quantification if ignored, the methodology is especially important for measurements carried out under varied contact circumstances or long sample times.

Statistical reporting and visualization

From time segments exhibiting dense and sustained cycle activity, representative waveforms were automatically picked. Following phase normalization, cycle-ensemble waveform statistics are displayed as median waveforms with interquartile range shading. In order to allow morphological comparison independent on tapping rate variations, current pulses underwent further peak alignment28. Instead of reporting the mean and standard deviation, all distribution and stability graphs display the sample size, median, and interquartile range. Because manual tapping conditions naturally result in sporadic extreme events that would disproportionately affect parametric statistics, this option of robust statistics is suitable29. Under these experimental conditions, the median and interquartile range offer more trustworthy measurements of central tendency and spread because they hold steady even in the presence of outlier events during lengthy measurement sequences. It should be noted that in order to preserve optimal measurement circumstances for each type of signal, voltage and current measurements were carried out in several experimental runs. As a result of the separate acquisition and processing paths used for each measurement modality, the number of approved cycles following quality filtering varies across voltage datasets and current or charge datasets.

Characterization techniques

The structural, morphological, optical, and electrical properties of the produced PANI and GTP nanocomposites were assessed using a thorough characterization process. A Bruker AXS D8 Focus diffractometer was used to obtain X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns over a 2θ range of 10–80° using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å). Shimadzu UV1800ENG spectrophotometers were used to record UV–Vis absorption spectra in the 200–800 nm wavelength range. A PerkinElmer Spectrum 65 FTIR spectrophotometer was used to obtain Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra between 4000 and 400 cm−1. The thermal stability of the sample was determined through differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), and elemental mapping with a JEOL, JSM-7900F microscope were used to examine the surface morphology and elemental composition. Using a JEOL JEM-2100 microscope, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) pictures were captured. A Thermo Fisher Scientific device was used to measure X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) in order to analyze the elemental and chemical states. A Keithley 2604B source meter was used for all electrical characterisation measurements.

Although these trap states can increase the amount of charge that can be stored, which is advantageous for triboelectric applications, they can additionally make it more straightforward for non-radiative recombination to occur if there are electronic transport channels, which could lower the device’s overall efficiency44.

FTIR, XRD and DSC analysis

FTIR spectra of TiO2, g-C3N4, PANI and the GTP nanocomposite samples were recorded; to investigate alterations in the local bonding environment, the complete and magnified between 400 to 2000 cm−1 spectra are displayed in Fig. 4a and b, respectively. FTIR spectrum shows distinctive vibrational bands of TiO2 nanoparticles in fingerprint region. Vibrational modes of the Ti–O bond in the TiO2 crystal lattice are responsible for the broad absorption band in the 700–430 cm−1 region. In particular, the rutile phase’s characteristic Ti–O stretching vibration can be seen at about 517 cm−1. This band at 434 cm−1 is associated with the deformative vibration of the Ti–O bending mode45. Furthermore, the Ti–O–Ti bridging links, which are indicative of the TiO2 network structure, are blamed for the strong and wide band seen at 700 cm−146. Bare g-C₃N₄ nanosheets’ FTIR spectra show distinctive vibrational bands that validate the graphitic carbon nitride structure (Fig. 4b). Since triazine (or tri-s-triazine) units are the basic building blocks of g-C₃N₄, the absorption peak at 800 cm−1 corresponds to their distinctive breathing mode47. The aromatic heterocyclic rings’ C=N double bond stretching vibration is attributed to the peak at 1562 cm−1, whilst the aromatic C–N stretching vibrations are associated with the peak at 1246 cm−148. Polyaniline has strong characteristic peaks at 1562 cm−1, 1465 cm−1, 1246 cm−1, and 1129 cm−1 (Fig. 4b), respectively49,50. The characteristic peaks of the PANI backbone are the N=Q=N (Q, quinonoid structure) and N–B–N (B, benzenoid structure) stretching modes, which corresponded to the peaks at 1562 cm−1 and 1465 cm−1, respectively; the peaks at 1246 cm−1 are attributed to the C=N stretching vibration peaks of aromatic amines51. The observed shifts and broadening of the 1200–1600 cm−1 bands in the GTP nanocomposite as compared to pristine PANI and g-C3N4 confirm the presence of hydrogen bonding, π–π interactions, and electronic coupling, all of that support in the emergence of interfacial states52. PANI polaronic bands and C–N/C=N vibrations are tightly correlated with the appearance or strengthening of peaks around 1246 cm−1, 1314 cm−1, 1129 cm−1, 1465 cm−1, and 1562 cm−1. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of GTP nanocomposite

The chemical composition and interfacial bonding of the components present in the as-prepared GTP nanocomposite have been examined using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), as shown in (Fig. 7). The survey spectra of ternary nanocomposite g-C₃N₄/TiO₂@PANI (GTP) displayed in (Fig. 7b). Three separate peaks centred at 284.7, 286.6, and 288.6 eV are observed in the deconvoluted C 1s XPS spectra of the GTP nanocomposite (Fig. 7b), which reveals information about the carbon atoms’ chemical bonding conditions within the ternary nanocomposite. The predominant signal at 284.7 eV is associated with sp2-hybridized C–C/C=C bonds from the graphitic domains of g-C₃N₄ and the aromatic rings of polyaniline60. Carbon atoms bound to heteroatoms (C–N⁺/C–O/C=N) within the protonated PANI backbone and at the g-C₃N₄–TiO2 interface produce the intermediate peak at 286.6 eV, which may represent partial oxidation and electronic interaction between the organic and inorganic phases. The N–C=N species in the tri-s-triazine (heptazine) framework of g-C₃N₄ are responsible for the high-binding-energy peak at 288.6 eV, indicating that the structural integrity of graphitic carbon nitride is maintained in the composite61. Two separate components are visible at 398.7 eV and 400.1 eV in the deconvoluted N 1s XPS spectra (Fig. 7c). The strong signal at 398.7 eV is associated with sp2-hybridized nitrogen atoms (C–N=C) in the aromatic tri-s-triazine (heptazine) units of g-C₃N₄, indicating the preservation of the conjugated heterocyclic framework during composite synthesis62. Tertiary or protonated nitrogen species (C–N–H/C₃–N⁺H), which originate from doped polyaniline and edge or defect sites of g-C₃N₄, are responsible for the component at 400.1 eV63. This peak may also comprise nitrogen coupled to titanium (Ti–N–C) at the organic–inorganic interface. These two nitrogen states show a hybrid bonding environment where g-C₃N₄ and PANI are chemically linked with TiO₂, allowing for enhanced electronic coupling and interfacial charge transfer pathways that could increase triboelectric performance.

The maximum Voc is produced by the 60 wt% PTFE-based TENG, whereas the 30 wt% PTFE TENG produces the maximum Isc and peak power. This phenomenon can be understood in terms of PTFE’s extreme negative triboelectric polarity, its low dielectric constant, and the composite’s interfacial properties. When the electrodes come into contact or separate, the 30% PTFE based TENG with PVDF and conductive GTP (g‑C3N4/TiO2@PANI) nanocomposite provides channels that may exchange charges more quickly, resulting in high Isc67. Specifically, PTFE easily collects surface charge upon contact and has a very high electronegativity. Therefore, films with higher PTFE amount hold more net surface charge, boosting Voc, as explained by equations 3 and 4. Conversely, compared to the highly PTFE-rich films, the 30% PTFE composite has a greater effective permittivity and better charge mobility due to the fact that it contains more PVDF (ε ≈8–10) and GTP filler (g–C3N4/TiO2@PANI)68. The GTP nanocomposite generates several deep interfaces where charges can localize. Charge recombination is slowed by these interfacial states, also known as trap states. In fact, current research demonstrates that deep charge-trap sites (such as MOFs) in a TENG layer significantly increase retained surface charge and decrease charge dissipation69. The doped PANI and g-C3N4/TiO2 semiconductor heterojunctions in the composite probably introduce trap states that trap ions or tribo-electrons. Since the composite’s trapping and insulating properties are at their peak at 60% PTFE, more charge is retained (increasing Voc), but charge movement is sluggish. Rapid charge transfer (increased Isc) before recombination is made possible by trap-mediated retention, which is still present but less prominent at 30% PTFE. The visualization is further supported by spectroscopic evidence; the GTP composite’s chemical bonding environment is confirmed by XPS spectra (C1s, O1s, N1s, Ti2p, and Cl2p). Subgap trap states in the GTP nanocomposite are consistent with a moderate bandgap and an extended Urbach tail in UV-Vis absorption’s Tauc-plot analysis of the GTP composite. More localized defect/trap states that are capable of capturing charges under friction are implied by a greater Urbach energy (tail). The 30 wt% PTFE TENG produces the highest current and power because higher permittivity generally boosts TENG output by increasing capacitance and charge production. The device containing 30 wt% PTFE exhibited a median crest factor of 3.2 (IQR: 2.7–4.3, n = 113 cycles), whereas the 60 wt% PTFE device showed a higher median crest factor of 4.3 (IQR: 3.7–6.1, n = 34 cycles). The larger CF observed for the 60 wt% PTFE device indicates a more impulsive current waveform with sharper discharge peaks relative to its RMS level. In contrast, the 30 wt% PTFE device exhibits a lower crest factor and a smaller interquartile range, indicating more distributed current pulses and improved cycle-to-cycle stability. This behavior is consistent with the higher short-circuit current and power output observed for this composition. Because triboelectric nanogenerators generate electrical output through displacement current induced by periodic electrostatic potential variation during the contact–separation process, their electrical signals typically appear as transient current pulses rather than steady conduction currents3. Furthermore, material-dependent variations in triboelectric charge density can influence electrostatic field development and discharge behavior, as demonstrated in quantitative studies of the triboelectric series8. These observations, therefore, provide waveform-level evidence supporting the voltage–current trade-off governed by PTFE composition within the GTP–PVDF/PTFE triboelectric system.

Individual contact-separation events transfer tens of nanocoulombs of charge every pulse, as shown by representative time-domain measurements. For a representative occurrence (Fig. 10 c), numerical integration of the current trace of 30 wt% PTFE TENG results in a transferred charge of ΔQ = 25.0 nC. The related voltage and current waveforms for 30 wt% PTFE TENG (Fig. 10a,b) show that peak Isc(t) appear as short microsecond-scale spikes with microampere-level peak amplitudes that coincide with mechanical contact, whereas peak Voc(t) reaches roughly 3 V. The analytical contact-separation mode triboelectric nanogenerator model is in line with these findings. The immediate electrode gap distance and the net stored charge control the open-circuit voltage, whereas the charge transfer rate (dQ/dt) and contact mechanics control the electrode separation’s temporal evolution and control the short-circuit current. While the current pulses record the quick charge redistribution that takes place during contact and separation transitions, the voltage waveform depicts quasi-static charge storage dynamics.

For representative contact-separation occurrences, the integrated short-circuit current results in a transferred charge of ΔQ = 200.4 nC per pulse (Fig. 10f). Peak open-circuit voltages around ≈3.3 V and μA-scale current spikes (I = 1.0–1.2 μA) are displayed in simultaneous voltage and current windows (Fig. 10d, e). The integrated charge plateau, which is consistent with trap/charge-transfer states in the GTP filler that increase retained charge, shows that the accessible transferable charge was saturated during that event.

A clear materials-dependent trade-off is revealed by ensemble cycle analysis. The voltage envelope for the 60 wt% PTFE device (Fig. 11a) exhibits a wide IQR and a persistent median voltage through separation, whereas the current pulses are tiny but steady across time. The 30 wt% PTFE device (Fig. 11b), Contrary, generates significantly larger median current pulses and increased pulse-to-pulse amplitude, indicating more efficient transient charge transfer. These patterns can be explained by competing effects: smaller PTFE fractions result in softer contacts and higher effective capacitance, which boost dQ/dt and short-circuit current, while increasing PTFE raises triboelectric surface charge (favouring higher retained Voc).

A distinct composition-dependent trade-off between voltage amplitude and charge transfer capacity in electrical output characteristics is shown by the cycle-resolved study. The 60 wt% PTFE device often produces higher peak voltages, with median values in the 3.6–3.8 V range and sporadic excursions to roughly 4.5 V under vigorous contact conditions, as seen in Figures 10, 11, and 12. The 30 wt% PTFE device, on the other hand, generates significantly more current and transmitted charge per cycle but has slightly lower median voltage levels, usually 3.2–3.4 V. This redistribution implies that the triboelectric interface is altered by lower PTFE loading in a way that prioritizes charge mobility and transfer efficiency over voltage accumulation. As the composite microstructure changes with filler content, variations in surface charge trapping density, interfacial capacitance, or charge dissipation pathways may be reflected in the measured voltage-charge trade-off. Optimization for certain application requirements is made possible by this composition-tuneable output distribution: optimum charge transfer for energy harvesting or actuation against high-voltage production for sensing applications.

This increase in charge transfer is consistently demonstrated by several types of analytical methods. Time-domain studies reveal that the 30 wt% PTFE device generates higher peak current amplitudes, with median values of around 1.1–1.3 µA compared to 0.3–0.5 µA for the 60 wt% PTFE device. The transmitted charge per cycle shows an even more striking compositional dependence, with median values of over 200 nC for the 30 wt% PTFE device—nearly an order of magnitude higher than the 20–30 nC measured for the 60 wt% PTFE composition. Complementary visualization techniques validate these tendencies’ resilience. Consistent composition-dependent behavior can be shown in representative waveforms (Fig. 10), ensemble median profiles with interquartile range shading (Fig. 11), and stability plots with violin distributions (Fig. 12). This multifaceted agreement indicates persistent changes in triboelectric performance, rather than occasional high-amplitude outlier occurrences, are responsible for the observed increase in current and charge output for the 30 wt% PTFE device. The conclusion that PTFE loading systematically modifies the voltage-charge trade-off in these composite triboelectric surfaces is strengthened by the agreement between various analytical viewpoints. This study’s observation of the decoupling between voltage amplitude and charge transfer is in line with the behavior of triboelectric nanogenerators, which has been extensively documented to show different responses to changes in material composition, device geometry, and operating conditions3,21. The current findings show that while lowering PTFE loading improves charge-dominant performance, increasing PTFE content favors voltage-dominant output, as shown by higher integrated charge per cycle and current amplitudes in the 30 wt% PTFE device.

Conclusion

We report a successful synthesis of ternary nanocomposite GTP (g-C₃N₄/TiO₂/PANI) and GTP-enabled PVDF/PTFE TENG whose performance is governed by a balance between surface charge generation and dynamic charge transfer. Structural and spectroscopic analyses (TEM/SEM, XPS, UV–Vis, EDX) confirm well-dispersed TiO₂ nanoparticles anchored on g-C₃N₄ sheets and bridged by polyaniline, producing a high-surface-area, N-rich organic matrix with abundant interfacial sites. Protonated N and PANI-related species (C 1s at 286.6 eV, N 1s at 400.1 eV), Ti–O–C/Ti–N interfacial bonding signals, and a small Ti3+/oxygen vacancy population are all preserved, according to XPS investigation. Polaronic and sub-bandgap absorption characteristics with a high Urbach energy (EU = 0.31 eV) are revealed by UV-Vis spectroscopy, suggesting significant trap states and charge transfer pathways. Polarizability and charge retention at the organic–inorganic heterojunction interfaces are improved by these features. A basic trade-off between Voc and Isc output is shown by electrical measurement of TENGs with different PTFE contents (30–60 wt%). Due to PTFE’s high surface charge density and low dielectric constant, which boost surface potential, the 60 wt% PTFE device reaches the maximum open-circuit voltage (Voc = 4.56 V). On the other hand, due to better mechanical contact, increased effective permittivity, and faster charge transfer kinetics, the 30 wt% PTFE device generates the maximum short-circuit current (Isc(t) = 2.86 μA) and peak power under matched load conditions (Pmax = 10.32 μW). Best performed TENG device’s (30wt% and 60wt% based TENG) were further analysed by statistical calculations. In order to create interfacial electronic states and surface morphology for improved triboelectric nanogenerator performance, this work presents a scalable materials design technique that combines g-C₃N₄, TiO2, and doped PANI.
