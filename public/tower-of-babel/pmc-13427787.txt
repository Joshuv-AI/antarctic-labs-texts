Advancing skull base surgery training in Sub-Saharan Africa: lessons from the IRCAD Africa 360° Skull Base Course
Authors: Claire Karekezi, James Balogun, King Kayondo, Arianna Fava, Sebastien Froelich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn many low- and middle-income countries (LMCIs), particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), skull base surgery is limited by inadequate infrastructure, scarce surgical tools, and few structured training opportunities. Given its technical complexity, requiring mastery of intricate neurovascular and craniofacial anatomy, as well as a coordinated multidisciplinary approach, these gaps pose major barriers to safe and effective care. To sustain the training of LMICs neurosurgeons, the authors propose a practical framework for establishing skull base skills laboratories in resource-limited settings.MethodsWe describe the microsurgical laboratory at IRCAD Africa, Kigali, inaugurated in October 2023, providing training for African surgeons in minimally invasive surgery (MIS) across multiple surgical disciplines; this includes skull base surgery through both open microsurgical and endoscopic techniques. Training included theoretical and practical modules, live demonstrations, and supervised cadaveric dissections. Trainees reflected daily on their learning via structured questionnaires.ResultsThe IRCAD Africa 360° Skull Base Course, launched in May 2024 and repeated in May 2025, trained 75 surgeons from 12 African countries. The course featured 11 wet lab stations with preserved human specimens, covering six modules: antero-lateral corridor, temporal fossa floor, petrous bone and posterior petrosal approach, cranio-cervical junction, endoscopic endonasal, and extended endonasal approaches. Essential tools and practical guidelines were provided. Participants reported high satisfaction with both theory and hands-on training.ConclusionSSA neurosurgeons show growing interest in developing skull base surgery skills. Cadaveric dissection remains the most effective training method and the IRCAD Africa 360° Skull Base Course demonstrates that high-level skull base education can be successfully delivered in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Skull base surgery is among the most complex and technically demanding subspecialties within the fields of neurosurgery and otolaryngology [3]. It requires accessing deep-seated and critical regions of the cranial base to manage diverse pathologies; these include benign and malignant tumors, vascular anomalies, and traumatic lesions. Successful skull base surgery demands mastery of intricate skull base anatomy, advanced imaging, specialized instruments and equipment, and the support of a multidisciplinary team. These requirements are often limited or entirely absent in low-resource settings such as Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [9, 24].

In most low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), the implementation of skull base surgery is still hindered by multiple challenges. These include inadequate infrastructure, a scarcity of trained personnel particularly neurosurgeons and essential supporting subspecialties, restricted access to high-resolution imaging and intraoperative navigation systems, as well as limited availability of microscopes and endoscopes. Financial constraints place an additional burden on both patients and healthcare systems. The insufficiency of post-operative critical care facilities and follow-up services further compounds morbidity and mortality risks [9, 22, 24]. Consequently, many patients who require skull base surgery in these settings remain untreated or are referred abroad at high financial cost. Even where skull base surgery is available, service capacity is far below demand, and complication rates remain high [8, 25].

Addressing these barriers requires innovative, context-specific solutions, including structured training programs, simulation platforms, affordable technologies, and strengthened international collaborations [19, 21]. Cadaveric skull base dissections represent a cornerstone in this process, providing a low-risk environment to learn complex anatomy, refine technical skills, and practice surgical decision-making [2, 6, 27, 29].

This paper examines the feasibility and challenges of establishing skull base skills laboratories in resource-limited settings. It focuses on the design, implementation, and early experience of cadaveric Skull Base Courses at IRCAD Africa offering a reproducible framework for similar initiatives in other LMICs.

Methods

This study is a descriptive educational program evaluation of the first two Skull Base Courses at IRCAD Africa based on prospective participant feedback and institutional experience.

IRCAD (Research Institute Against Digestive Cancer) Africa, established in Kigali, Rwanda in October 2023, is a pioneering surgical training and research center located in Masaka, near Kigali city, Rwanda [18, 26]. It is the first IRCAD facility on the African continent and the fifth globally, following centers in France, Taiwan, Brazil, and Lebanon. This state-of-the-art institute is dedicated to advancing minimally invasive surgery (MIS) across Africa through comprehensive training, cutting-edge technology, and collaborative research. IRCAD Africa boasts advanced facilities, including a specialized skill laboratory equipped with high-definition imaging systems with a 16 operating tables capacity and a 226-seat auditorium (Fig. 1 a-b), the center offers training in various surgical disciplines including neurosurgery and ENT, on both open microsurgical and endoscopic techniques. At IRCAD Africa, we have undertaken a comprehensive needs assessment to prepare for the launch of our first Skull Base Course which was scheduled for May 2024. A detailed list of essential equipment including surgical microscopes, endoscopic towers, microsurgical and endoscopic sets, head holders and audiovisual infrastructure was developed to ensure that the training environment mirrors real operative conditions. Recognizing that access to advanced surgical training remains limited in Africa, our objective was to create a sustainable platform that would equip young neurosurgeons and ENT surgeons with world-class skills in both microsurgical and endoscopic skull base approaches with affordable tools.

The laboratory and all its activities are approved by the ethical committee of IRCAD Africa. The training program combined structured theoretical modules and hands-on cadaveric dissections, all conducted under expert supervision. This approach allowed trainees to integrate foundational knowledge with practical skills, reinforcing safe techniques and complex anatomical understanding in skull base surgery.

Each morning, trainees participated in a structured evaluation session to review and reflect on the previous day’s activities. This allowed participants to consolidate key learning points, clarify challenging concepts, and provide immediate feedback on both the theoretical sessions and cadaveric dissections. At the conclusion of the course, a comprehensive evaluation was conducted, enabling trainees to share their overall impressions, highlight strengths, and suggest areas for improvement.

Results

Course implementation and participation

The 360° Skull Base Course at IRCAD Africa is a highly intensive four-to-five-day program covering microanatomy, advanced micro-endoscopic dissection techniques, and complex skull base approaches. Training is delivered in a state-of-the-art wet lab with 16 fully equipped workstations, taking up to 30 trainees. The lab provides training on complex skull base approaches on preserved human tissue and this activity is approved by the ethical committee of IRCAD Africa.

The first IRCAD Africa 360° Skull Base Course was conducted in May 2024, it enrolled 43 surgeons from across Africa. It provided instruction on a wide spectrum of microsurgical and endoscopic skull base approaches. The second edition followed in May 2025 with 32 participants, and the course is now scheduled as an annual fixture. Trainees represented various African countries including Burundi, Cameroon, Chad, Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), Ghana, Kenya, Ivory Coast, South Africa, Rwanda, Zambia, Zimbabwe and Uganda.

Laboratory infrastructure and context adaptations

To realize this vision, we engaged industry partners, academic institutions, and philanthropic organizations to secure sponsorships for trainees and equipment. Companies supporting the course included Karl Storz, Medtronic LTD, Carl Zeiss, and Bissinger Medizintechnik. Their collaboration enabled the establishment of a cutting-edge training program and strengthened regional capacity in complex neurosurgical care.

We obtained 11 table mounted technoscopes from Zeiss, shipped in and returned after each course, this limited functional stations to 11, though the lab has a 16-stations capacity and can host up to 30 trainees. Therefore, the course was open for a maximum of 22 trainees, distributed 2 per station and the rest of the participants were observers but participated equally in all theoretical education sessions. The main central station was dedicated for demonstration. Each station included an endoscopic tower system with 2 screens, a technoscope fixed at the table, a suction and a drilling system. In the absence of rigid head fixation, padded basins fixed to tables were used to stabilize the head specimens (Fig. 1c). The lab has basic surgical sets which have scissors, clamps, different forceps and suction tubes; the rest of microsurgical and endoscopic instruments were supplied and shipped by Karl Storz and Bissinger Medizintechnik, 11 drills with different attachments and burrs were provided by Medtronic LTD. Some optional technologies like navigation systems, ultrasonic aspiration, micro-debriders or shavers and rigid Mayfield head-holders, were not available deemed not necessary. Trainees were provided with gowns, caps, shoe covers, and scrubs. Essential tools and LMICs-specific adaptations for a cadaveric Skull Base Course are summarized in Table 1.

Eleven cadaveric head specimens were prepared by the Department of Anatomy of the University of Rwanda for each course edition. IRCAD Africa facilities offer a specimen preservation room equipped with fridges where specimens are kept during the week of the course. All specimens were fixed and embalmed; however, they were not vascularly injected, as the department was not familiar with head injection technique. These specimens provided the foundation for hands-on training across the 11 functional stations, each fully equipped with the necessary instruments and tools, summarized in Table 2. This list highlights essential microsurgical and endoscopic tools that helped conduct the course, their sources, and availability status. A final station set-up is shown in Fig. 1d.

Curriculum design and faculty organization

A 4 to 5-day theoretical and practical program shown in Table 3, designed for neurosurgery and ENT residents as well as junior consultants, was carefully planned in advance and reviewed by the Course Director (SF) and Co-Director (CK). Faculty with recognized expertise in skull base surgery were selected, with particular emphasis on strengthening and empowering skull base practice within Africa. Of the seven to eight faculty members engaged in each course, approximately half were African surgeons actively practicing skull base surgery, ensuring both one on one high-quality teaching and regional relevance. The course dedicates three and a half days to open transcranial approaches, progressing systematically from anterior to posterior corridors, and one and a half days to endoscopic endonasal techniques. To provide a comprehensive and progressive learning pathway, six dedicated theoretical and practical modules were developed, each addressing key anatomical regions and surgical strategies to the skull base. Each technical approach was introduced through a dedicated lecture in the auditorium, followed by an expert-led demonstration in the skill lab and one-on-one supervised hands-on cadaveric dissection using microsurgical or endoscopic techniques, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Together, these modules form a structured curriculum that integrated theoretical knowledge with cadaveric dissection, enabling participants to achieve both anatomical mastery and technical proficiency.

Educational outcomes and participant feedback

Daily evaluations consistently reflected high participant satisfaction, with trainees rating the course as highly valuable for both its theoretical content and hands-on dissection components. At the program’s conclusion, overall evaluations showed that participants found the balance between microsurgical and endoscopic modules appropriate, faculty engagement exceptional, and cadaveric laboratory facilities well suited for advanced training.

Participant feedback further emphasized the course’s strong educational impact. Overall, 74.3% of participants rated the sessions as excellent or very good, while 20.1% rated them as satisfactory. Among the 13 respondents, 92.3% indicated a high likelihood of recommending the course to a colleague, assigning a score of 9 or 10 on a 10-point scale. Many participants highlighted the importance of continued access to structured skull base courses within Africa, reporting increased confidence in anatomical orientation, dissection skills, and surgical decision-making. Objective measures of technical skill acquisition and post-course clinical impact after trainees returned in their countries were not assessed.

Five months after the second course, a team from Lariboisière University Hospital, visited to train the Anatomy Department of the University of Rwanda, on cadaveric latex head injection technique. This hands-on training aimed to enhance anatomical preservation and visualization of vascular structures, providing valuable skills for future skull base courses and microsurgical dissections. The collaboration marked an important step in strengthening local capacity for high-quality anatomical research and surgical training.

Discussion

Global neurosurgery and global skull base surgery

According to The Lancet Commission on Global Surgery 2030, an estimated 5 billion people worldwide lack access to basic surgical care, a crisis that disproportionately affects LMICs [17]. Within this gap, neurosurgery constitutes a significant unmet need, yet it is often overshadowed in LMICs’ healthcare agendas, which tend to prioritize communicable diseases. A recent report indicates that approximately 160 million people are left without essential surgery each year, most of them in these countries [20].

Delivery of neurosurgical care in these regions is constrained by even more multiple barriers, including a shortage of trained specialists, limited training opportunities, and inadequate infrastructure. Many hospitals lack access to even fundamental neurosurgical technologies such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), surgical microscopes, and high-speed drills while advanced tools such as neuronavigation systems, Ultrasonic aspirators, intraoperative Neuromonitoring (IONM) systems remain rare or entirely absent. Even within neurosurgery itself, disparities persist across subspecialties. Neurotrauma, for example, has received substantial attention due to its high disease burden, while other subspecialties such as skull base surgery remain underdeveloped [5, 22]. Meanwhile, brain tumors represent a significant and growing challenge with meningiomas, gliomas, and sellar region tumors among the most common surgically treatable lesions requiring increased focus and resources [9, 24].

The skull base is among the most complex anatomical regions, comprising the floor of the anterior cranial fossa, orbit, sellar region, sphenoid wing, temporal bone, clivus, and occipital bone. Pathologies in this region range from benign and malignant tumors to non-neoplastic conditions such as trigeminal neuralgia, hemifacial spasm, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks, encephaloceles, and osteomyelitis. Skull base neurosurgery requires not only technical mastery but also a multidisciplinary approach, involving otolaryngology, ophthalmology, endocrinology, radiation oncology, radiology, and critical care. In LMICs, where such collaborative frameworks are rarely established, the absence of these teams increases both the difficulty of surgery and the risk of complications [9]. Training in skull base surgery is exceptionally demanding, requiring additional fellowship-level expertise and a lifelong learning curve. Cadaveric courses have been described as the “second pillar” of skull base training after formal clinical training; however, such opportunities remain scarce across Africa [11–13].

A recent survey of neurosurgery residents and early-career neurosurgeons in SSA revealed that nearly 90% lacked access to cadaveric dissections, highlighting the scale of unmet training needs [7, 11], therefore, the establishment of skull base laboratories provides a critical solution. Such labs recreate the operating room environment and allow these trainees to practice microsurgical and endoscopic techniques in a safe, controlled setting before applying them to patients. Despite their critical importance in neurosurgical education, very little literature exists describing the logistics of starting and maintaining these labs, particularly in SSA [19, 21, 27]. The scarcity of needed infrastructure, equipment, and trained personnel, poses additional challenges to the development of such facilities in these settings. In addition, the establishment of a skull base skills laboratory in a resource-limited context necessitates careful strategic, cost-effective planning and prioritization of high-impact training interventions. Both microsurgical and endoscopic techniques require dedicated facilities that replicate the operating theater as closely as possible, this environment provides an indispensable platform for developing technical expertise, mastering complex anatomical dissections, and fostering multidisciplinary collaboration [3, 13, 16].

Documenting the process of setting up a functional skull base laboratory in resource-limited environments is therefore essential, not only to guide similar initiatives but also to expand opportunities for high-level surgical training where the need is greatest.

The IRCAD Africa Skull Base Courses’ trainees represented a broad cross-section of SSA, reflecting both the diversity and the shared workforce challenges across the region. Neurosurgical services in these countries remain heterogeneous, ranging from relatively well-established academic centers with expanding subspecialty training to low-resource settings where workforce shortages, limited access to advanced imaging, and constrained operative infrastructure continue to restrict the full development of complex subspecialties such as skull base surgery [22].

Despite incremental progress in training capacity and regional collaborations, the neurosurgical burden of disease remains disproportionately high compared to workforce density, resulting in significant unmet need for both basic and advanced neurosurgical care [11, 12]. In this context, regional training initiatives serve as critical platforms for skills transfer, standardization of care, and the gradual development of sustainable subspecialty capacity across the continent.

Equipment and training modules for skull base training

Essential microsurgical equipment for a skull base course includes high-resolution visualization systems such as surgical microscopes, microsurgical instrument sets, high-speed drills with diamond and cutting burrs and suction with irrigation systems. For endonasal endoscopic approaches, a complete tower system is required, comprising a high-definition monitor, camera, light source, and rigid endoscopes of varying angles (0°, 30°, 45°). These are complemented by specialized endoscopic instruments such as dissectors, suctions, curettes, and forceps. Each cadaveric station should ideally be equipped with an adjustable surgical table, a head-holding device (e.g., Mayfield clamp), and standard craniotomy tools. In addition, the training auditorium should benefit from audiovisual systems that allow live projection and recording of dissections, thereby enhancing both demonstration and documentation [1, 3, 6, 13, 16].

Establishing such facilities in resource-limited settings, however, presents significant challenges. Access to cadaveric specimens is still restricted by cultural, legal, and logistical barriers, while the costs of preservation facilities, advanced equipment, and infrastructure can be prohibitive. Navigation systems, surgical microscopes, and other high-end tools are not always affordable or accessible, and long-term sustainability relies on institutional commitment, consistent funding, and trained faculty [13, 29].

Fava et al. demonstrated the advantages of a structured, milestone-based curriculum in a well-resourced European laboratory, highlighting the educational rigor possible when infrastructure and specimens are readily available [6]. In contrast, Konan et al. describe the first skull base laboratory in West Africa, where innovation through refurbished equipment, regional collaboration, and external partnerships enabled feasibility despite significant limitations [13]. While Fava et al.’s model represents an educational ideal, it is not easily transferable to resource-limited contexts, and Konan et al.’s initiative, though groundbreaking, raises questions of sustainability and outcome measurement. Together, the studies underscore that future efforts should merge structured curricula with context-adapted, cost-effective solutions to ensure that high-quality neurosurgical training is globally accessible and locally sustainable [6, 13, 16, 21].

Lessons from IRCAD Africa

The experience of IRCAD Africa adds an important dimension to skull base training initiatives, complementing the structured milestone-driven model described by Fava et al. [6] and the resource-adapted approach of Konan et al. [13]. Unlike Fava’s high-resource European setting or Konan’s pioneering but resource-limited West African lab, IRCAD Africa demonstrates that large-scale, state-of-the-art training centers can be successfully established within LMICs. By training more than 400 surgeons from 27 countries including over 70 in skull base surgery and integrating cadaveric dissection with theoretical teaching, the institute has shown the feasibility of delivering advanced, multidisciplinary education in Africa. The flagship “360° Skull Base Course,” supported by modern infrastructure and international faculty, underscores the scalability and sustainability of such programs when anchored by strong institutional commitment and regional collaboration [18, 26]. Nevertheless, similar to other training models, challenges remain regarding equitable access, long-term funding, and outcome measurement, highlighting the need to balance high-tech facilities with cost-effective, locally adaptable strategies to ensure broader regional impact.

Implications for other LMICs’ initiatives

Several lessons from this experience may be applicable to other LMICs settings. First, partnerships with industry can serve as temporary infrastructure multipliers during the early phases of program development, helping to bridge critical resource gaps. Second, deliberate investment in local faculty development is essential for long-term sustainability and reduced reliance on external expertise. Third, cadaver acquisition remains a major challenge in many settings due to cultural perceptions, legal restrictions, logistical barriers, and limited body donation systems. Ethical considerations are equally important and require transparent consent processes, respectful handling of donors, compliance with regulatory frameworks, and adherence to best practices in anatomical education [15, 28]. Finally, structured curricula that combine theoretical teaching, expert demonstrations, and supervised cadaveric dissections is essential to optimize educational outcomes.

Barriers, challenges, and the way forward

Although basic neurosurgical training remains a priority in Sub-Saharan Africa, skull base pathologies are increasingly diagnosed due to improved imaging access, longer life expectancy, and evolving referral patterns. Conditions such as skull base meningiomas, pituitary adenomas, chordomas, chondrosarcomas, cranial base schwannomas and other complex skull base tumors are being recognized more frequently, yet local capacity to manage them safely remains limited. Shortages of trained faculty and the lack of structured training programs continue to restrict sustainable skull base education in LMICs, resulting in delayed care, costly referrals abroad, and suboptimal outcomes [4, 12, 20]. Advanced skull base courses should therefore be viewed not as a luxury, but as a necessary component of comprehensive neurosurgical capacity building, with benefits extending to improved anatomical knowledge, microsurgical skills, and complication avoidance.

Addressing these challenges requires affordable tools, local capacity building, and strategic partnerships, while long-term success depends on regional ownership, ethical cadaver acquisition, structured training, and outcome evaluation [10, 14, 23, 28]. Importantly, improving patient outcomes also requires strengthening perioperative care, intensive care, rehabilitation, and overall health system capacity.

Limitations

This study did not include objective measures of technical skill acquisition post-course like operative performance, or long-term patient-level outcomes. These endpoints were beyond the scope of the current evaluation but represent important areas for future research to more comprehensively assess educational effectiveness and clinical impact of the course.

Conclusion

The IRCAD Africa 360° Skull Base Course initiative demonstrates that advanced skull base surgical training can be effectively implemented within a resource-constrained African environment through structured, collaborative models. To strengthen the evidence base for such programs, future studies should incorporate objective assessments of transmitted technical skills, evaluate translation into independent clinical practice, and include patient-level outcome measures. The development and consolidation of sustainable subspecialty training pathways remain critical to addressing inequities in access to safe, high-quality neurosurgical care. Ultimately, such capacity-building efforts are instrumental in advancing neurosurgical expertise across the continent, enabling neurosurgeons to safely manage complex skull base pathology and improve long-term patient outcomes.
