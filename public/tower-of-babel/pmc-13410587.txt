Elevated ST2+ Tregs in RA-ILD: correlation with pulmonary fibrosis and the IL-33/ST2/AREG axis
Authors: Peiyan Zhang, Jiawei Wang, Haoyang Sun, Jinlin Miao, Zhaohui Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterstitial lung disease (ILD) represents a significant extra-articular complication associated with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), contributing substantially to the morbidity and mortality observed in affected patients. Despite its clinical relevance, the underlying mechanisms driving the pathogenesis of RA-ILD remain poorly understood, necessitating further investigation into the immunological factors that may play a role in this condition.MethodsThis study aimed to elucidate the role of ST2+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the progression of RA-ILD. To achieve this, we developed a composite murine model that integrated collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) with intratracheal bleomycin (BLM) administration, thereby simulating the pathophysiological features of RA-ILD. Concurrently, using flow cytometric and artificial intelligence-based quantitative analysis of high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT), we analyzed a clinical cohort consisting of healthy controls, RA patients without ILD (RA-nonILD), and RA patients with ILD (RA-ILD).ResultsHistopathological assessment confirmed that the composite model exhibited exacerbated lung inflammation and fibrosis. Notably, flow cytometry revealed a marked expansion of ST2+ Tregs in both the lungs of CIA + BLM mice and the peripheral blood of RA-ILD patients. Moreover, quantitative HRCT analysis indicated a positive correlation between the frequency of ST2+ Tregs and fibrotic lung volume in patients. RA-ILD patients exhibited elevated serum levels of IL-33 and amphiregulin (AREG), which correlated with disease severity. In vitro studies further demonstrated that IL-33 stimulation significantly upregulated AREG expression at both the mRNA and protein levels in Tregs.ConclusionThese results highlight the potential involvement of the IL-33/ST2 axis in mediating a pathogenic Tregs response characterized by AREG production, thereby linking systemic autoimmunity to the progression of lung fibrosis in RA-ILD. Importantly, this study identifies the IL-33/ST2/AREG axis and ST2+ Tregs as promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets for RA-ILD, laying the groundwork for future mechanistic studies and translational research endeavors.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD), Regulatory T cells (Tregs), IL-33/ST2 axis, Amphiregulin (AREG), Pulmonary fibrosis, Biomarker

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune disorder characterized by chronic synovitis and progressive joint destruction [1]. However, its morbidity and mortality are increasingly driven by severe extra-articular manifestations [2]. Among these, interstitial lung disease (ILD) represents one of the most common and serious complications, significantly impairing quality of life and is a leading cause of death in the RA population [3]. The clinical burden of RA-ILD is substantial, with reported prevalence rates vary considerably, yet collectively affecting a significant proportion of patients [4]. This high prevalence underscores a critical unmet need, as RA-ILD is associated with a poor prognosis, particularly when fibrotic progression occurs [5]. Notably, longitudinal studies indicate that approximately half of RA-ILD patients exhibit radiographic progression of fibrosis over time, a process strongly associated with increased mortality [6]. Identifying reliable biomarkers and effective therapeutic targets for RA-ILD continues to post a paramount challenge in rheumatology and pulmonology.

The pathogenesis of RA-ILD is complex, characterized by a maladaptive interplay between dysregulated systemic autoimmunity, persistent inflammation, and aberrant tissue repair mechanisms culminating in pulmonary fibrosis [7, 8]. Recent studies have begun to delineate shared and distinct pathways between RA-ILD and other fibrotic lung diseases, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [9]. For instance, proteomic profiling has revealed overlapping serum biomarker signatures, while molecular studies highlight the involvement of pathways such as TGF-β signaling, oxidative stress, and impaired autophagy in fibrogenesis [10–12]. Furthermore, specific mediators like matrix metalloproteinase-7 (MMP-7) have been implicated in driving subpleural fibrosis in experimental models [13]. Despite these advances, a mechanistic understanding of how the systemic inflammatory milieu of RA predisposes the lung to fibrosis remains elusive. A pivotal gap lies in elucidating the role of immune regulatory circuits, particularly those involving regulatory T cells (Tregs), which are essential for maintaining peripheral tolerance and limiting collateral tissue damage [14, 15]. While Treg dysfunction is a hallmark of autoimmunity [16], their specific functional states and subset dynamics within the unique immunopathological landscape of RA-ILD are poorly characterized.

Notably, the IL-33/ST2 axis has emerged as a crucial signaling pathway at the nexus of type 2 immunity, tissue injury response, and fibrosis [17, 18]. IL-33, an alarmin released by damaged epithelial and endothelial cells, signals through its receptor ST2 (IL1RL1) to drive pro-fibrotic responses in structural cells and modulate immune cell function [19]. Notably, a subset of Tregs expresses ST2, and IL-33 signaling can expand this population and potentiate their suppressive capacity in certain contexts [20]. This establishes ST2+ Tregs as a unique cellular node potentially capable of integrating tissue-derived damage signals (IL-33) with immunoregulatory output [21]. In fibrotic diseases lsuch as systemic sclerosis, alterations in ST2+ Tregs have been observed, suggesting a role in fibrotic pathogenesis [22]. However, their contribution to RA-ILD is entirely unexplored. A significant gap in knowledge exists in directly investigating this specific Treg subset in the context of concurrent arthritis and lung fibrosis. Most preclinical studies utilize models of isolated lung injury or arthritis [23], failing to recapitulate the systemic-to-local disease continuum seen in clinical settings. Consequently, there is a pressing need for integrated models and focused studies to determine whether ST2+ Tregs act as a protective, homeostatic buffer or acquire a dysregulated, pro-fibrotic phenotype in RA-ILD, thereby connecting systemic autoimmune inflammation with organ-specific fibrosis.

To address these fundamental questions, the present study adopts a translational and multi-modal approach. We employed a novel composite murine model combining collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) with intratracheal bleomycin (BLM) administration to simulate the co-occurrence of autoimmune joint inflammation and progressive pulmonary fibrosis, thereby offering a more clinically relevant platform than single-insult models. This in vivo work was paralleled by a well-characterized clinical cohort study comprising healthy controls (HC), RA patients without ILD (RA-nonILD), and RA-ILD patients. Our methodological framework integrated advanced techniques across multiple scales: artificial intelligence-based quantitative analysis of high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) scans provided objective and precise metrics of lung involvement; multi-parameter flow cytometry enabled detailed immunophenotyping of Tregs and their ST2+ subset in both murine and human samples; and functional in vitro assays using sorted Tregs dissected the direct effects of IL-33 stimulation. This combination of an innovative disease model, robust clinical correlation, and mechanistic cellular experiments represents a key methodological advantage, allowing for a comprehensive exploration from whole-organ pathology to specific molecular events.

This investigation had three primary objectives: First, we aimed to characterize the frequency, distribution, and phenotypic features of ST2+ Tregs in the lungs and systemic circulation in our composite CIA + BLM model and in patients with RA-ILD compared to relevant controls. Second, we sought to determine the clinical relevance of these cells by assessing correlations between them and established indices of disease activity, including arthritis scores and, notably, quantitative HRCT measures of fibrotic burden. Third, we investigated the functional consequences of IL-33/ST2 pathway engagement on Treg biology, with a specific focus on the expression of amphiregulin (AREG), an epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) ligand implicated in tissue repair and remodeling. Elucidating whether and how the IL-33/ST2 axis regulates AREG production in Tregs may uncover a novel mechanistic link between a key tissue alarmin system and a potent effector molecule within the immunoregulatory compartment. These findings highlight potential new therapeutic targets for mitigating fibrosis in RA-ILD.

Methods

Animals and treatment

The study utilized 120 male C57BL/6J mice, aged between 6 and 8 weeks and weighing from 18 to 22 g, obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of the Air Force Military Medical University. The mice were categorized into four separate groups: the control group receiving normal saline, the collagen-induced arthritis group (CIA), the bleomycin (BLM) treatment group, and the group receiving both collagen-induced arthritis and bleomycin (CIA + BLM). Strict adherence to national standards for the management of medical laboratory animals was maintained during animal husbandry; all mice were kept under carefully regulated conditions with free access to water and standard laboratory rodent feed.

On day − 25, an emulsified solution of chicken type II collagen (Chondrex) at a volume of 100µL, combined with complete Freund’s adjuvant (Chondrex), was subcutaneously administered at the tail’s base to the CIA and CIA + BLM groups. This was succeeded by a booster shot of the same concentration employing incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (Chondrex) on day − 11. The control group, along with the BLM group, received simultaneous intradermal injections of saline. Arthritis assessments and body weight measurements were carried out biweekly. A four-paw arthritis scoring system was employed, categorized as follows: 0 = normal joint; 1 = minor swelling or redness in the ankle or midfoot; 2 = slight swelling present in both the ankle and foot; 3 = moderate swelling along with erythema; 4 = significant swelling and erythema [24]. The highest achievable score was 16. On day 0, mice were anesthetized using sodium pentobarbital at a dosage of 80 mg/kg. Following the preparation of the neck, exposure of the trachea, and incision, a microsyringe with a volume of 25 µL was positioned around 1 cm beneath the cricoid cartilage. The BLM and CIA-BLM groups received a slow intratracheal instillation of bleomycin hydrochloride solution (5 mg/kg, Nippon Kayaku Co., Ltd.), whereas the control and CIA groups were administered saline without bleomycin through bronchial administration. On day 14 (14d) and month 6 (6 m), euthanasia of the mice was performed to gather peripheral blood and lung tissue samples. Portions of the lung tissues were preserved in formaldehyde for pathological evaluation, while the remainder were processed into single-cell suspensions for flow cytometry analysis. Because the incidence rate of CIA in C56BL/6 mice ranges from 40% to 60%, only those mice exhibiting arthritis were selected for further lung experiments. This experimental protocol received approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of the National Translational Science Center for Molecular Medicine, Air Force Military Medical University (Approval No.: 2023-NTSCMM-ID011).

Lung histopathology

Lung tissues were preserved using 4% formaldehyde, subsequently embedded in paraffin, and sliced into sections measuring 3 μm. These sections underwent staining with both an HE staining kit and a Masson’s trichrome staining kit (Solarbio), followed by scanning and analysis via the Unna digital slide scanning system. To evaluate lung tissue inflammation in a semi-quantitative manner, the Smith scoring system was utilized, assigning points based on the affected area: 0 points for no injury, 1 point for lesion areas less than 25%, 2 points for 25%-50% lesion areas, 3 points for 50%-75% lesion areas, and 4 points for areas exceeding 75%. Each section was examined in five high-power fields, and the average score was computed. The assessment of collagen deposition was conducted using Masson staining. ImageJ software facilitated the isolation of blue-stained collagen regions to determine the percentage of collagen area, calculated as: collagen area ratio (%) = (number of blue area pixels / total tissue area pixels) × 100%. Ultimately, the average was compiled for each tissue sample, yielding the final score.

Lung tissue single-cell suspension

Prepare digestion solution with 200U/mL Collagenase Type IV (Gibco) + 0.5U/mL DNase I (Saiguo Biotech). After cutting lung tissue into 1 mm fragments, digest in 2mL digestion solution at 37 °C with 300 rpm for 1 h. After digestion is complete, homogenize the tissue in 1640 culture medium with the plunger tip of a 1mL syringe, then pass through a cell strainer to yield lung single-cell suspension.

Flow cytometry

The following antibodies were utilized: Peridinin-chlorophyll-protein (PerCP)/Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse CD4 (clone: RM4-5), Alexa Fluor (AF) 700 anti-mouse CD45 (clone: 30-F11), Brilliant Violet (BV) 421 anti-mouse ST2 (clone: DIH9), Allophycocyanin (APC) anti-mouse CD25 (clone: PC61), Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) anti-human CD4 (clone: RPA-T4), BV421 anti-human CD25, Phycoerythrin (PE) / Dazzle 594 anti-human ST2 (clone: BC96) (all from BioLegend), and PE anti-human Foxp3 (clone: PCH101) (Thermo Fisher), APC anti-human Amphiregulin (clone: AREG559) (Thermo Fisher), PE anti-mouse Foxp3 (clone: FJK-16s) (Thermo Fisher). Cell counting was performed using Precision Count Beads (BioLegend). Surface staining was conducted in the dark for 20 min with the respective surface antibodies, followed by red blood cell lysis. Subsequently, intracellular Foxp3 was stained using Foxp3 antibodies and Foxp3 staining buffer (Thermo Fisher). The dyed specimens were re-suspended and examined on a BD FACS Celesta flow cytometer, with data handled using FlowJo 10.6.2 software. To ensure comparability of the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) across different samples, sample handling and staining strictly adhered to standardized protocols. Consistent voltage and threshold parameters, as well as uniform gating strategies, were systematically applied throughout the experiment. For MFI comparisons between different patient groups and tissue sources, MFI values were normalized by subtracting the MFI of unstained samples from the experimental samples.

Clinicians diagnosed RA-ILD patients based on both clinical evaluations and HRCT findings, in addition to their meeting the RA diagnostic requirements [27].

Quantitative evaluation of HRCT in patients

Our research employed a commercially available software for artificial intelligence-assisted analysis, AVIEW Lung Texture (version 1.1.43.7; Coreline Soft, Hangzhou), which is based on deep learning techniques to conduct quantitative assessments of patients’ chest HRCT images. This software offers fully automated segmentation of lung parenchyma in the CT images provided, categorizing them into six classifications according to both normal and interstitial lung disease (ILD)-associated features: normal lung tissue, emphysema, consolidation, ground-glass opacity (GGO), honeycombing, and reticular pattern. The quantification of each type is expressed as a percentage of the total lung volume. Reticular patterns combined with honeycombing are classified as fibrotic changes (ILD-fibrotic), while the aggregation of GGO, reticular patterns, and honeycombing is referred to as total ILD manifestations (ILD-total). The reliability of the quantitative outcomes generated by this software has been confirmed through prior relevant research [28–30].

Immunofluorescence staining

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded lung tissue sections. For single biomarker detection, the primary antibody targeting the designated protein was incubated with tissue slides following standard experimental protocols. Corresponding fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibody was applied subsequently. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescent signals were captured and analyzed using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Capturing of images was performed using an Olympus BX51 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan), followed by analysis and processing with Inform software.

Cytokine measurement

For the quantitative assessment of cytokine levels, the samples were aliquoted and preserved at -80 °C for later analysis. The concentrations of IL-33 and AREG in the culture supernatant were measured employing enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer (Neobioscience). For graphical visualization, raw undetectable values recorded as zero were assigned a minimal value of 0.001 to avoid overlapping invisible points at the baseline; all statistical analyses were performed using the original raw data. The y-axis was plotted on a log₁₀ scale to resolve visual compression and clearly display the skewed distribution of low-level concentrations.

Magnetic-activated Cell Sorting (MACS)

In line with the guidelines provided by the manufacturer, pulmonary Treg cells were isolated utilizing the Mouse CD4 T Cell Negative Selection Kit (Stem cell) alongside the Mouse CD25 Treg Positive Selection Kit (Stem cell).

Cell culture

The 12-well plates were coated with an anti-mouse CD3 antibody (clone 145-2C11, 3 µg/mL). Following this, they were incubated at either 37 °C for 2 h or at 4 °C overnight. In the complete medium, incorporate 3 µg/mL of the anti-mouse CD28 antibody (clone 37.51), along with mouse IL-2 recombinant protein at a concentration of 5ng/mL, and TGF-β recombinant protein also at 5ng/mL. Modify the cell concentration and inoculate the cells at a density of 1.0 × 10⁶ cells per mL per well, then maintain them for 5 days at 37 °C in an environment with 5% CO₂. On the third day, introduce 2mL of fresh medium to each well. On day 5, recombinant mouse IL-33 (20 ng/mL) (BioLegend) was added where indicated. Finally, harvest the cells and conduct a phenotypic analysis using flow cytometry on day 7.

Fluorescence-activated Cell Sorting (FACS)

Collect in vitro cultured Treg cells, wash the cell suspension with PBS buffer, centrifuge to remove the supernatant, and resuspend the cells in an appropriate amount of PBS buffer. Add ST2 antibody and incubate at room temperature for 20 min. Wash the cells with PBS buffer and centrifuge to remove unbound antibodies. In the Aria III sorting flow cytometer, set appropriate parameters for cell sorting. Based on the flow cytometry results, identify and sort the ST2+ Treg and ST2− Treg subpopulations, and collect the sorted cells for subsequent experiments.

RNA Isolation and RT-PCR

Total RNA extraction was carried out using the Rapid Extraction Kit for Total RNA from Cells/Tissues (Xinsaimei Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), adhering to the protocols provided by the manufacturer. The quantitative RT-PCR analysis was conducted utilizing the StepOnePlus RT-PCR System along with SYBR Green Master Mix (Beijing Juhemei Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The target mRNA expression levels were calculated through the delta-delta Ct method. The sequences for the primers are listed below: Areg forward, 5’- GGTCTTAGGCTCAGGCCATTA-3’; Areg reverse, 5’- CGCTTATGGTGGAAACCTCTC-3’; Gapdh forward, 5’- GGTGAAGGTCGGTGTGAACG-3’; Gapdh reverse, 5’- CTCGCTCCTGGAAGATGGTG-3’. The expression levels of the target genes were adjusted relative to Gapdh levels.

Statistical analysis

The statistical description and analysis of the data were conducted using the software SPSS 23.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0.0. Initially, the Shapiro-Wilk test was utilized to assess the distribution of the data. For datasets that followed a normal distribution across multiple groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted; conversely, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was employed for datasets that did not conform to normality. When performing pairwise comparisons among several groups, Bonferroni post hoc analysis was carried out under conditions of homogeneous variances, while Tamhane’s T2 analysis was applied in cases of heterogeneous variances.

Notably, considering the significant age difference observed between the RA-ILD group and the RA-nonILD group, age was adjusted as a covariate in all intergroup comparisons (including comparisons of Treg frequency, absolute Treg count, and ST2⁺ Tregs levels) using multiple linear regression, which is equivalent to analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). This adjustment was performed to exclude the potential confounding effect of age on the study outcomes.

In correlation assessments between variables, Pearson correlation was utilized for pairs of continuous data with a normal distribution, whereas Spearman correlation was used for data that were non-normally distributed or ranked. The data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), with statistical significance determined at p < 0.05.

Results

Pathological analysis of CIA + BLM mouse model

Collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) serves as a well-established animal model for RA [31]. Similarly, the bleomycin (BLM)-induced murine model for pulmonary fibrosis is considered the gold standard in preclinical studies due to its histological characteristics closely mirroring those found in human fibrosis [32]. The combination of the CIA and BLM models (CIA + BLM) accurately replicates the pathological features and progression of both joint inflammation and pulmonary fibrosis in RA-ILD [33]. However, previous studies typically monitored the model for only 28 days, and found that the pathological features of the CIA + BLM model were similar to those of the BLM group, particularly in terms of pulmonary fibrosis [34]. In contrast, comparisons of disease severity and temporal progression revealed that the BLM group exhibited an earlier onset of pulmonary inflammation. Inflammatory reactions and collagen deposition peaked on day 14 and then subsided progressively, with substantial recovery by day 28, reflecting a typical disease progression. In contrast, the CIA + BLM group showed a delayed onset of inflammation; by day 14, there was increased infiltration of inflammatory cells, significant destruction of lung tissue, and continued collagen deposition. By day 28, inflammation and collagen deposition had further worsened, indicating a progressive disease state.

To further clarify the pathological changes at different disease stages, we extended the observation period of the CIA + BLM group from the initial timeframe to 6 months, selecting day 14 and month 6 as representative time points for the acute phase and chronic phase, respectively (Fig. 1A). Regarding arthritis progression over time, no significant difference was observed in arthritis scores between the CIA + BLM and CIA cohort. Specifically, clinical signs of arthritis appeared on day 21 following the initial immunization, peaked around day 42, and then gradually subsided (Fig. 1B). In the acute phase (day 14 post-BLM treatment), the CIA + BLM group displayed considerable joint swelling, erythema of the foot pads, and restricted activity. In the chronic phase (month 6 after BLM administration), joint swelling gradually subsided, but left behind joint deformity. (Fig. 1C). These observations demonstrate that CIA induction can replicate pathological features mirroring those seen in patients with RA: early-stage lesions manifest as joint swelling and pain. Without treatment, persistent synovial inflammation leads to structural joint damage, culminating in joint deformity.

Concurrently, the scores for lung pathology indicated considerable pulmonary inflammation in both the BLM group and the CIA + BLM group on day 14. This condition was characterized by a disrupted alveolar structure accompanied by widespread infiltration of inflammatory cells. By the sixth month, inflammation in the BLM group had subsided, and fibrotic lesions were stabilizing, whereas the CIA + BLM group still exhibited persistent inflammatory cell infiltration and progressive collagen deposition with more pronounced destruction of the alveolar structure, demonstrating features characteristic of progressive fibrosis (Fig. 1 D-G). These findings indicate that the persistent autoimmune state and joint inflammation in our established combinatorial model may exacerbate and sustain pulmonary inflammation, ultimately leading to irreversible pulmonary fibrosis. The CIA + BLM model effectively recapitulates the progressive clinical course of RA-ILD, particularly in patients with active joint inflammation, providing a valuable tool for investigating the interplay between RA autoimmune activation and pulmonary fibrosis and for evaluating potential therapeutic strategies.

Lung ST2+ Tregs were significantly increased in the CIA + BLM model

To elucidate the variations in frequency and functionality of ST2+ Tregs in the context of autoimmune inflammation and pulmonary fibrosis, we investigated the total Treg populations as well as ST2+ Tregs within the lung and peripheral blood using the CIA + BLM model during both the acute and chronic stages. To eliminate interference from circulating cells in the detection of lung Tregs, 0.2 mL of fluorescently labeled anti-CD45 antibody (3 µg/mL, diluted in PBS) was injected via the tail vein shortly before euthanizing the mice. This procedure labeled the circulating (Circ) cells (CD45+), allowing us to distinguish them from the tissue-resident (TR) cells (CD45−) (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, single-cell suspensions from the lung were prepared for flow cytometric analysis. The results indicated that ST2+ Tregs were enriched in the tissue-resident population, suggesting their potential role in regulating local immune responses within the lung tissue (Fig. 2B). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the proportion of ST2+ Tregs in the lungs of the Control and CIA groups was approximately 10%, similar to that in peripheral blood. However, in the CIA + BLM group, the proportion of the ST2+ subset among TR Tregs in the lungs significantly increased to approximately 20%–40%, indicating that the fibrotic microenvironment may drive local enrichment of this subset.

At various points throughout the progression of the disease, particularly during the acute phase (day 14), the presence of ST2+ Tregs in lung tissues was notably elevated in both the BLM and CIA + BLM groups compared to the Control and CIA groups (Fig. 2C). This observation implies that early-stage inflammatory responses could facilitate the recruitment and expansion of ST2+ Tregs. By the chronic fibrotic phase (month 6), the proportion and number of ST2+ Tregs in the CIA + BLM group persisted at elevated levels, significantly surpassing those observed in the other experimental groups; conversely, the BLM group exhibited a significant reduction. This suggests that the progressive accumulation of ST2+ Tregs within the CIA + BLM model is closely linked to the progression of fibrosis and may be essential for sustaining immune homeostasis during the later stages of fibrotic development (Fig. 2C). These dynamic changes suggest that the CIA background may reshape the pulmonary immune microenvironment, promoting the persistent residence and functional maintenance of ST2+ Tregs, thereby contributing to the regulation of the balance between fibrosis progression and repair. Moreover, in the chronic phase, the percentage of ST2+ Tregs in peripheral blood was markedly higher in the CIA + BLM group than in the control group (Fig. 2C). This suggests that the enrichment of this subset during the acute phase exhibits tissue specificity.

Lung ST2+ Tregs are associated with the degree of pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis, and with blood ST2+ Tregs

Based on the pulmonary pathology results, we further analyzed the correlation between changes in ST2+ Tregs levels and lung injury. The results showed that the proportion of ST2+ Tregs in lung tissue was significantly positively correlated with the extent of inflammatory cell infiltration and collagen deposition in the lung, especially in the acute phase of lung injury (Fig. 3A). This suggests that the accumulation of ST2+ Tregs may be closely associated with pulmonary inflammation and the progression of fibrosis.

Under normal circumstances, TR Tregs exhibit stability and can persist long-term in specific microenvironments, whereas Circ Tregs primarily migrate throughout the body via the bloodstream [35]. Studies have shown that Tregs can also dynamically migrate between tissues and peripheral circulation. TR Tregs home to specific tissues either through T cell receptor (TCR)-specific recognition of local tissue antigens or via chemokine-receptor axis-driven tissue-directed migration [36, 37]. For instance, lung Tregs respond to pulmonary inflammatory signals through two distinct axes: the CCR4-CCL17/CCL20 and the CCR6-CCL17/CCL20 axes, thereby recruiting Tregs to suppress allergic asthma, while migrating to fibrotic lung tissue via the CXCR3-CXCL9/10 axis [38]. In mouse models, peripheral blood Tregs rapidly migrate to damaged sites during tissue injury or inflammatory responses, exerting immunosuppressive effects that help limit excessive inflammation and facilitate tissue repair [39]. Conversely, TR Tregs also exhibit recirculation; approximately 20–30% re-enter circulation during the resolution phase of inflammation by downregulating CD69, displaying a CD62L⁺CCR7⁺ phenotype, and migrating via lymphatic vessels to draining lymph nodes to participate in systemic immune regulation [40].

Therefore, we aimed to determine whether variations in peripheral blood ST2+ Tregs levels correlate with those in lung ST2+ Tregs. Correlation analysis shows that ST2+ Tregs levels in peripheral blood are positively correlated with those in lung tissue, especially during the acute phase (Fig. 3B). This indicates that peripheral blood ST2+ Tregs levels may partially reflect lung ST2+ Tregs alterations. In the chronic phase, the correlation between the proportions of ST2+ Tregs in peripheral blood and in the lung has weakened, although similar proportional trends in both compartments persist (Fig. 3B). Therefore, under specific pathological conditions, peripheral blood ST2+ Tregs may serve as potential indicators of alterations in the lung immune microenvironment.

Peripheral blood ST2+ Tregs were increased and positively correlated with pulmonary involvement severity in RA-ILD patients

To assess the association between ST2+ Tregs levels in peripheral blood and pulmonary complications in patients, we utilized flow cytometry to evaluate ST2+ Tregs in the peripheral blood of individuals with RA-ILD (Fig. 4A). A significant age difference was observed between the RA-ILD group and the RA-nonILD group (64.2 ± 8.9 years vs. 53.9 ± 12.4 years, p = 0.024). Therefore, we adjusted for age as a covariate in all intergroup comparisons using multiple linear regression, which is statistically equivalent to ANCOVA, to mitigate potential confounding effects of age.

IL-33 and AREG are increased in the CIA + BLM model and RA-ILD patients

ST2 serves as the dedicated receptor for IL-33. IL-33 is recognized as an “alarmin” that is released upon epithelial cell damage [41]. AREG is an essential factor for tissue repair, with recent research indicating its expression in Tregs [42]. To explore the precise role and mechanism of ST2+ Tregs in fibrosis, we utilized multicolor immunofluorescence to assess the levels of IL-33 and AREG in the lung tissues of mouse models after six months. Our findings revealed that the expression levels of IL-33 and AREG were significantly higher in the CIA + BLM and BLM groups in comparison to the controls (Fig. 5A-B). Additionally, there was a notable positive correlation between the expression of IL-33 and AREG and the percentage of ST2+ Tregs in the lung tissues (Fig. 5C). Moreover, the expression of these factors positively correlated with pulmonary inflammation (assessed by HE Score) and fibrosis severity (quantified as relative collagen content, %) (Fig. 5D). To further contextualize the role of the IL-33/ST2 axis in our model, we report serum IL-33 levels across experimental groups at the acute (day 14) and chronic (month 6) stages (Fig. 5E). At day 14, IL-33 was significantly upregulated in all disease groups (CIA, BLM, and CIA + BLM) relative to controls, with the combined CIA + BLM group exhibiting the most pronounced elevation, suggestive of an additive effect of the two models. By the chronic stage (month 6), this pattern persisted: IL-33 remained significantly elevated in all disease groups, with the CIA + BLM group again displaying the highest concentrations, surpassing those of the CIA-only and BLM-only groups. Together, these data demonstrate that serum IL-33 levels are dynamically upregulated throughout disease progression in our RA-ILD model, with the combined inflammatory and fibrotic insult driving the most pronounced IL-33 elevation, supporting a key role for this cytokine axis in shaping the immunoregulatory landscape during fibrosis.

Discussion

ILD is a prevalent yet severe extra-articular manifestation of RA and substantially contributes to patient morbidity and mortality [43]. The pathogenesis of RA-ILD is complex, involving an interplay among autoimmune dysregulation, chronic inflammation, and aberrant tissue repair processes that culminate in progressive pulmonary fibrosis [6]. Despite its significant clinical implications, the relationship between systemic joint inflammation and targeted pulmonary fibrosis, as well as the precise cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying this process, remain poorly understood. This highlights a critical knowledge gap that impedes the development of effective targeted therapies. To bridge this knowledge gap, this study systematically investigated the potential functions of ST2+ Tregs and their associated IL-33/ST2 signaling axis in the context of RA-ILD. We leveraged a translational research strategy, integrating an innovative composite mouse model of collagen-induced arthritis with bleomycin-induced lung injury, and executed a comprehensive clinical cohort analysis of RA patients with or without ILD. Our findings indicate an association between ST2+ Tregs and pulmonary fibrosis in RA-ILD, alongside elevated expression levels of IL-33 and AREG. Furthermore, in vitro experiments demonstrate that IL-33 promotes AREG expression in ST2+ Tregs. These findings suggest that ST2+ Tregs may serve as a critical bridge linking systemic inflammatory signals in RA-ILD to pulmonary fibrotic responses, potentially facilitating the progression of pulmonary fibrosis in RA-ILD through IL-33-mediated induction of the fibrotic molecule AREG under inflammatory conditions.

In recent years, the composite model combining collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) and bleomycin (BLM)-induced pulmonary fibrosis (CIA + BLM) has become a pivotal tool for investigating rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD) [44]. The CIA + BLM composite model successfully reproduces the coexistence of arthritis and pulmonary fibrosis, providing a critical tool for elucidating the pathophysiological synergy between systemic autoimmune processes and organ-specific injury. Compared to the single injury control group, the exacerbation of pulmonary fibrosis in this combined model (manifested as elevated histological scores and increased collagen deposition) supports the hypothesis that a pre-existing systemic inflammatory microenvironment induces a more severe and persistent fibrotic response in lung tissue to secondary injury. This interaction may be driven by circulating inflammatory mediators released from arthritic joints, which alter the pulmonary microenvironment, thereby enhancing fibroblast activation and impairing repair processes. It is important to note that the pathogenesis of RA-ILD is highly complex, and joint inflammation is not the sole or direct driving factor behind its development. In fact, many RA patients exhibit joint disease in an active phase while their ILD condition progresses. It is also not uncommon for pulmonary lesions, rather than joint inflammation, to present as the primary clinical manifestation of RA-ILD [45]. Therefore, the CIA + BLM model can only replicate the clinical characteristics of a subset of RA-ILD patients exhibiting both active joint inflammation and progressive pulmonary lesions. Overall, the CIA + BLM model effectively simulates the progressive clinical course of RA-ILD (particularly in patients with active joint inflammation), providing a more clinically relevant research platform for investigating the interaction mechanisms between RA autoimmune activation and pulmonary fibrosis, as well as potential therapeutic strategies.

In this study, we also employed AI-driven quantitative analysis of HRCT scans to assess pulmonary involvement in the patient cohort. Our findings demonstrated a significant increase in pulmonary fibrosis volume among patients with RA-ILD. This result not only validates the clinical relevance of our model but also highlights the potential of such computational tools in facilitating objective and sensitive disease phenotype analysis in future clinical practice. By integrating animal models with quantitative human imaging techniques, we further bolstered the evidence supporting the mechanistic link between rheumatoid arthritis and progressive pulmonary fibrosis.

Our flow cytometric analyses reveal alterations in the ST2⁺ Treg compartment within the lungs of CIA + BLM mice and the peripheral blood of RA-ILD patients, demonstrating a dynamic correlative link between the IL-33/ST2 axis and immunoregulatory Treg homeostasis during fibrotic progression. The specific phenotypic and functional characterization of this Treg subset is crucial, as ST2 expression may demarcate a population with enhanced tissue-homing capabilities and a distinct functional profile, potentially primed to respond to tissue-derived alarmins like IL-33. While previous research in cancer and fibrosis has established that IL-33 can expand and modulate IL1RL1 (ST2)+ Tregs [46–48], the functional consequence of this expansion in RA-ILD remain ambiguous. The pivotal question is whether an increase in ST2+ Tregs represents a compensatory and protective mechanism to restrain inflammation-driven tissue damage or, conversely, signifies a dysfunctional or even pathogenic conversion of Tregs that inadvertently promotes fibrosis. This dichotomy is central to understanding the dual role of immune regulation in fibrotic diseases. Therefore, to elucidate the precise role of these cells in RA-ILD fibrosis, future investigations should prioritize the direct comparison of the functional profiles of ST2+ versus ST2− Treg subsets isolated from pathological tissues, such as by monitoring their dynamic changes over the disease progression leveraging high-resolution approaches such as single-cell RNA sequencing.

The molecular link between IL-33 stimulation and the upregulated expression of AREG in Tregs, particularly within the ST2+ subset, establishes a mechanistic link between immune sensing and pro-fibrotic effector functions. AREG, as an epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) ligand, is a potent mediator of epithelial repair and regeneration, but its dysregulated secretion is implicated in pathological processes such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and fibroblast proliferation [49–51]. The induction of AREG in Tregs via the IL-33/ST2 axis suggests that tissue injury-derived IL-33 instructs regulatory cells to adopt a tissue-remodeling phenotype [52, 53]. This aligns with recent findings in oncology, where an AREG/EGFR-mediated coupling between IL1RL1+ Tregs and cancer-associated fibroblasts establishes a profibrotic and immunosuppressive niche [54]. In the context of RA-ILD, ST2+ Tregs-derived AREG could similarly act on lung structural cells, such as alveolar epithelial cells, to drive EMT or on resident fibroblasts to enhance their activation and extracellular matrix production. Future studies should determine whether this AREG expression is a hallmark of a specialized, tissue-protective Treg subset or a marker of functional deviation under chronic inflammatory pressure.

The functional characterization of isolated ST2+ Tregs via in vitro assays is essential to define their unique properties, which may underlie their role in RA-ILD pathogenesis. Beyond changes in cell frequency alone, demonstrating altered proliferative capacity, unique cytokine profiles, or target-specific suppressive activity within this subset would confirm its identity as a functionally specialized entity. A pivotal consideration concerns the plasticity and stability of these cells within the inflammatory lung milieu; persistent exposure to IL-33 and other inflammatory signals could induce the loss of Foxp3 expression in ST2+ Tregs or their conversion into pro-inflammatory, ex-Treg state, thereby exacerbating rather than mitigating disease [55]. Conversely, they may retain their canonical regulatory function but gain supplementary pro-reparative capabilities through AREG secretion [56]. This nuanced view of Treg function extends beyond the traditional dichotomy of suppression versus dysfunction and positions ST2+ Tregs as pivotal mediators at the immunology-fibrosis interface, whose therapeutic manipulation—either by enhancing their regulatory capacity or depleting a pathogenic fraction—necessitate rigorous investigation contingent upon their specific functional phenotype.

The biochemical quantification of elevated IL-33 and AREG protein levels in the sera and lung tissues of our composite model and RA-ILD patients not only corroborates the cellular and molecular findings but also paves the way for biomarker development. The correlation of these soluble factors with clinical metrics of fibrosis and arthritis severity strengthens their potential utility as non-invasive indicators of disease activity or progression. However, the cellular source of these mediators in RA-ILD warrant detailed elucidation; while damaged alveolar epithelial cells are a major source of IL-33, our data implicate ST2+ Tregs as a significant contributor to AREG. This heterogeneity in cellular composition must be accounted when interpreting serum levels. The therapeutic implications are significant, given that targeting the IL-33/ST2/AREG axis with neutralizing antibodies or receptor antagonists represents a promising strategy, consistent with findings in other fibrotic contexts. Future work should evaluate whether such interventions can interrupt the pathogenic dialogue between immune cells and fibroblasts, moving beyond broad immunosuppression towards a more precise disruption of the fibrogenic immune cascade.

Despite its integrative approach, this study presents certain limitations. First, the translational relevance of the CIA + BLM composite model warrants careful interpretation. Although this model recapitulates coexisting arthritis and pulmonary fibrosis, the acute, chemically induced lung injury may not fully mimic the typically insidious, chronic fibrotic progression observed in human RA-ILD. Second, although the mechanistic insights are suggestive, they lack definitive causal validation in vivo. The established correlation among IL-33, ST2+ Tregs, and AREG expression could be further substantiated by employing Treg-specific ST2-knockout models or performing in vivo neutralization of AREG to demonstrate their functional necessity in disease pathogenesis. Third, we acknowledge the significant age difference between the RA-ILD and RA-nonILD groups. Although we adjusted for age as a covariate in all intergroup comparisons using multiple linear regression (equivalent to analysis of covariance, ANCOVA) and confirmed that the differences in absolute Tregs counts and ST2⁺ Tregs levels were driven by disease phenotype rather than age-related immune changes, the potential impact of age-related immune alterations on study outcomes cannot be completely ruled out and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Fourth, accumulating clinical and experimental evidence has confirmed that conventional synthetic DMARDs, targeted synthetic DMARDs, and biological agents exert substantial modulatory effects on Tregs frequency and functional status, as well as the expression and secretion of IL-33 and AREG in RA and other diseases [57–60]. Although these effects have not been fully elucidated in RA-ILD, notably, variations in treatment regimens, duration of therapy, and drug responses may lead to heterogeneous changes in Tregs, IL-33, and AREG levels across the patient cohort. Notably, such DMARD-induced molecular and cellular dysregulations may contribute to the phenotypic and immunological differences between RA-ILD and RA-nonILD subgroups. Therefore, when interpreting subgroup differences in Tregs, ST2+ Tregs, IL-33 and AREG levels, baseline disease status and DMARD exposure disparities should be regarded as key confounding factors. Further prospective studies employing standardized stratification methods based on drug type and treatment duration are warranted to elucidate the independent role of Tregs, IL-33 and AREG in the pathogenesis of RA-ILD and to mitigate confounding effects from DMARD interventions. Fifth, in addition to the heterogeneity in age and treatment regimens, our clinical cohort also exhibited inconsistencies between positive anti-CCP and anti-MCV results. This may be attributed to these two antibodies targeting distinct carboxylylated antigen epitopes, resulting in inherent variations in serological concordance. Beyond the inherent variations in diagnostic efficacy, multiple clinical factors further contributed to this inconsistency. For instance, anti-MCV levels are closely associated with the disease course and severity of morning stiffness in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), whereas the correlation between anti-CCP levels and extra-articular manifestations differs [61]. The unique pulmonary inflammatory microenvironment in RA-ILD patients alters the pattern of humoral immune responses, which may subsequently contribute to discordant positivity of carboxylylated protein-related autoantibodies [62]. The distinct clinical presentations of RA-ILD, specifically regarding the temporal onset of arthritis versus ILD, result in variations in autoantibody expression profiles and serological characteristics [62]. This also suggests that combined detection of anti-CCP and anti-MCV antibodies can compensate for each other’s diagnostic limitations, thereby enhancing the diagnostic accuracy for RA with concomitant interstitial lung disease. Future studies should further validate these findings in larger cohorts to mitigate the potential impact of such heterogeneity on the results. Finally, although our observational data demonstrate a significant correlation, the exact role of ST2+ Tregs in the pathogenesis of RA-ILD—whether they serve as disease drivers, arise from the inflammatory microenvironment, or exert protective/regulatory effects—remains to be elucidated through in vitro and in vivo functional studies. The current findings represent only preliminary discoveries, underscoring the necessity for more in-depth research.

In summary, this study elucidates a novel immunofibrotic axis in RA-ILD, centered on IL-33/ST2-mediated upregulation of AREG in Tregs. The composite model of mouse and complementary clinical analysis provide robust evidence linking systemic autoimmunity to target-organ fibrosis, establishing the ST2+ Tregs subset as a pivotal cellular nexus. The identification of IL-33 and AREG as key biomarkers underscores their translational potential (Fig. 9). Future research should prioritize large-scale validation of these biomarkers and leverage advanced genetic tools to establish causality, paving the way for targeted therapies modulating this pathway.
