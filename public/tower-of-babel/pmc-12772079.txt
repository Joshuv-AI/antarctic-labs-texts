Association of socioeconomic and lifestyle factors with the prevalence of chronic noncommunicable disease multimorbidity in rural Southwest China: a structural equation modeling approach
Authors: Jing-jing Huang, Guo-hui Li, Lan Liu, Chun-yi Ruan, Xi-min Li, Qing-rou Bao, Rui-min Shi, Fan Gu, Le Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeading global causes of death are noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), which affect mostly low- and middle-income countries. Over 90% of all NCD deaths occur in China, where multimorbidity, which is associated with adverse social circumstances, is an emerging health priority. Rates in China are significantly higher than the rest of the world. The purpose of this study was to investigate, using structural equation modeling (SEM), the relationships and pathways between lifestyle and socioeconomic factors and the prevalence of chronic noncommunicable disease (NCD) multimorbidity in rural Southwest China, an area with limited resources, a high degree of ethnic diversity, and an increasing prevalence of multimorbidity.MethodsData were collected from a cross-sectional health interview and examination survey among individuals aged ≥ 35 years in rural Southwest China. A standard questionnaire was used to gather data on the demographic traits and lifestyle choices of the participants. Each participant’s height, weight, waist circumference (WC), blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, and spirometry before and after bronchodilators were assessed. Principal component analysis was used to create a single socioeconomic position (SEP) index.ResultsThe overall prevalence rates of six common chronic NCDs—raised blood pressure, raised blood glucose, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)—and multiple comorbidities were 56.3%, 10.8%, 7.7%, 6.0%, 4.2%, 3.8%, and 18.9%, respectively. Males had a greater prevalence of COPD than females did, whereas females had greater prevalences of raised blood pressure and hyperlipidemia than males did (p < 0.01). The SEM results indicated that SEP, age, ethnicity, physical inactivity, and an unbalanced diet had both significant direct and indirect effects on NCD multimorbidity, with total effect sizes quantified at -0.065, 0.321, -0.057, 0.110, and 0.090, respectively (p < 0.05). the condition of obesity ( 0.401, p < 0.01) and a familial predisposition to NCDs (0.134, p < 0.01) were directly associated with multiple morbidities.ConclusionsBoth lifestyle and socioeconomic factors are associated with the prevalence of NCD multimorbidity in rural Southwest China. Future interventions for the prevention and control of NCD multimorbidity should focus more on individuals with a low SEP and unhealthy lifestyles.

Background

Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) are now the leading cause of ill health and death in every region of the world [1]. Therefore, NCDs have been responsible for 43 million deaths in 2021 worldwide, of which 18 million deaths are premature or under the age of 70; nearly three-quarters (73%) of these deaths were concentrated in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [2]. In China, the top five NCDs that cause the highest number of deaths were similar to those globally, including cardiovascular diseases (stroke, 22.2%; ischemic heart disease, 16.7%), neoplasms, chronic respiratory disease (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), 11.0%), neurologic disorders, and diabetes mellitus with related chronic kidney diseases (DM&CKD, 3.3%) [3]. Notably, the proportion of deaths related to NCDs in China has reached as high as 90.08% [4]. This figure significantly exceeds the global average of 73% [2], highlighting the dual difficulties in preventing and controlling NCDs in China and areas with inadequate resources.

The number of people living with two or more long-term conditions, also known as multimorbidity, is on the rise alongside the growth in NCDs [5]. Patients with NCD multimorbidity experience poorer health outcomes, increased mortality and more frailty which complicates the management of diseases and increases healthcare utilisation and costs [6]. NCD and multiple morbidities pose a major challenge for patients, carers and society and significantly affect the quantity and quality of life of affected people [1].

Multimorbidity has traditionally been seen as a hallmark of older populations, with a prevalence of 4.8% to 93.1% in older persons [7]. However, multimorbidity resulting from NCDs also occurs among younger people. Socially deprived areas are particularly affected, with onset occurring at a younger age [8]. The prevalence of NCD multimorbidity between the ages of 0 and 99 years ranged from 15.3% to 93.5% in a recent systematic review which indicated an increasing trend [9]. Furthermore, more than half (56.7%) of the middle-aged and elderly population in China have two or more NCDs, which is almost double the pooled prevalence of 33.1% of 18 high-income countries (HICs) and 31 LMICs [10, 11], and calls for more attention to be directed toward NCD multimorbidity in China.

Conventional regression models suggest that socioeconomic factors, lifestyle behaviors (such as alcohol consumption, smoking and physical inactivity) and demographic characteristics are all associated with NCD and multimorbidity development [12]. In HICs, low socioeconomic status is usually associated with higher multimorbidity prevalence. Low education, poor neighborhood and low income have been identified as key risk factors for multimorbidity [13, 14]. Reported findings were similar in studies conducted in China [4].

Traditional regression models have shown that lifestyle and socioeconomic determinants have independent effects on NCD multimorbidity, but it is still unknown how these factors interact. The capacity of structural equation modeling (SEM) to concurrently analyze the direct and indirect impact pathways across many factors has made it a crucial tool in tackling this problem [15]. In China, the application of SEM for analyzing the factors influencing NCD prevalence and multimorbidity has gradually gained attention [16, 17]. These findings offer methodological insights for constructing comprehensive intervention frameworks for NCDs; however, SEM research on the interactive mechanisms of multimorbidity determinants in China have focused on urban areas, the literature is still sparse especially that covering rural areas.

Chinese research shows rising prevalence of NCDs specifically from age 35 onwards [18]. Moreover, the World Health Organization (WHO)’s emphasis on reducing premature mortality from NCDs in individuals aged 30–70 years [19] in addition to China’s national basic public health care strategy, which focuses on individuals aged over 35 years for early detection and treatment of major NCDs including diabetes and hypertension [20]. To align with our research objective of identifying and analyzing determinants of multimorbidity in its early contexts. Thus, this study focused on the adult population aged ≥ 35 years, aimed to characterize the multimorbidity of six common chronic NCDs—raised blood pressure, raised blood glucose, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease (CHD), COPD and stroke—in rural Southwest China; to quantify the direct/indirect effects of demographic, socioeconomic and lifestyle factors on multimorbidity via SEM; and to explore the interactions of these determinants.

Methods

Study design and population

This research was carried out in three rural locations of Yunnan Province, China, using a community-based cross-sectional health survey that included physical exams and interviews. One of China’s most economically undeveloped areas is Yunnan, which is in the southwest of the nation. There were 129 counties in the province’s administrative system as of 2023, and its population was 46.73 million. Yunnan is the province with the most ethnic variety in China, home to 25 different ethnic minority groups, 15 of which are exclusive to the area. It is distinguished by its varied geography of plateaus, mountains, and valleys [21]. The majority of these ethnic minorities live in mountainous or remote areas and suffer from socioeconomic disadvantages as compared to the Han majority. In recent years, the prevalence of NCD multimorbidity in Yunnan has been rising, from 2.3% in 2011 to 9.7% in 2021 [22], with CHD, stroke, and COPD being the top causes of mortality [23]. Significant variations exist amongst ethnic groups in terms of genetic heritage, lifestyles, socioeconomic circumstances, and attitudes toward illness. The smoking and drinking habits seen in these cultures further increase the dangers of multimorbidity of NCDs in this ethnically diverse province [24].

Multistage stratified random sampling was used to select the study sample (Fig. 1). First, all of Yunnan’s 129 counties were classified into three categories according to the per capita gross domestic product (GDP): economically advantaged (>$11,150, n = 24), moderately developed ($6,660 - $11,150, n = 59), and economically disadvantaged (< $6,660, n = 46). Three counties were then obtained by randomly selecting one county from each stratum. Using the median figure as the cutoff, all townships in the chosen counties were further separated into two subgroups (high and low) according to per capita income in the second step. One township was then chosen at random from each grouping, for a total of six townships. The third step included determining the population size from each of these townships’ administrative village committees. Three villages were then chosen from each of the six townships using probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling, for a total of 18 villages. In the last phase, basic random selection was used to choose qualified citizens from each community. According to the inclusion criteria, participants had to be at least 35 years old, free of cognitive impairment or communication difficulties, have lived in the chosen hamlet for at least five years, and have given their approval to take part in the research.

Data collection and measurement

Trained interviewers used a pretested and structured questionnaire to conduct in-person interviews with the participants who gave their informed permission. Information on demographic and socioeconomic characteristics (including gender, age, ethnicity, educational level, annual household income, and walking time to the nearest village clinic), lifestyle factors (including the type, duration, and frequency of exercise; the frequency and amount of fruits, vegetables, legumes, fats, sugar, and salt consumed in the past week), and the diagnosis and self-reported family history of the six studied chronic NCDs were collected. Blood pressure, fasting blood glucose, and anthropometric measures (height, weight, and waist circumference (WC)), and pre- and postbronchodilator spirometry were assessed for each participant by a licensed physician.

According to the World Health Organization’s STEPS guideline, weight, height, and WC were measured [25]. BP evaluation was conducted following American Heart Association guidelines [26], whereby participants, after a 5-minute seated rest, underwent three consecutive readings using a mercury sphygmomanometer. The average of these triplicate measurements served as the final BP value. FBG was measured using a glucometer (FREESTYLE OPTIM) after the participants had an overnight fast of 8–10 h (water permitted; no food, beverages, smoking, gum, or strenuous activity), with their compliance verbally confirmed prior to sampling. Non-fasting participants were invited to undergo random blood glucose (RBG) level testing, which was recorded. A spirometer (CHESTHI-101, Japan) was used to conduct pulmonary function testing before and after bronchodilators, including forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC).

Definitions

Raised blood pressure was defined as a mean systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg and/or the use of antihypertensive medications to treat hypertension in the past two weeks [25]. An FBG level >7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dl), self-reported use of antidiabetic drugs within the previous two weeks, or a previous diagnosis of diabetes verified by a medical expert were all considered indicators of raised blood glucose [25]. When a postbronchodilator FEV1/FVC < 0.70 is present, it indicates that there is a chronic restriction in airflow, which is known as COPD [27]. Hyperlipidemia, CHD, and stroke were defined on the basis of a patient’s self-reported diagnostic records by a physician and their history of medications taken in the last two weeks.

Multimorbidity, is having two or more chronic diseases at the same time among the six chronic diseases included in this study [5].

Using the conventional formula (kg/m2), body weight and height were used to calculate body mass index (BMI). The WHO’s criteria for Asians served as the basis for the operational definitions of obesity: BMI >28 kg/m2 was considered general obesity, and a waist circumference of ≥ 90 cm for males and ≥ 85 cm for women was considered central obesity [24]. An unbalanced diet was defined as a dietary pattern that deviates from the WHO guidelines of a healthy diet and was characterized in this study as follows: (1) insufficient daily intake of fruits, vegetables, and legumes (< 400 g or 5 portions) and (2) excessive daily consumption of total fats (>30% of total energy), sugars (>10% of total energy or 50 g), and salt (>5 g) [28]. Physical inactivity was defined based on the WHO’s physical activity recommendations for adults. An individual was classified as physically inactive if they engaged in moderate-intensity exercise (e.g., walking, running, cycling, jogging) < 5 days per week, with each session lasting ≤ 30 min, during the week preceding the survey [29].

Ethnicity was classified based on participants’ self-identification, with “Han” referring to the ethnic majority and “Non-Han” referring to other ethnic groups, including those with distinct languages, cultures, or religious. The term “socioeconomic position” (SEP) describes the social and economic elements that affect the places that people or groups occupy within a society’s structure; it is commonly operationalised by four indicators—education, income, housing conditions, and occupation [30]. Three characteristics that were often utilized in Chinese studies—education level, yearly family income, and access to medical services—were used to create a composite index of SEP in this research [15]. Education level was classified as illiterate, which is operationalized as the incapacity to understand what is being read or to form a basic phrase about everyday life [24]) and elementary school (grades one through six) or above. Based on the median income of the sample, annual household income was separated into low and high groups. Medical service accessibility was divided into two groups: good when the participant’s walking time from home to the nearest village clinic was < 30 min and poor when the participant’s walking time from home to the nearest village clinic was ≥ 30 min [15].

Statistical analysis

SPSS 22.0 software was used for the data analysis. Continuous variables were reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. To compare continuous variables between two groups, t tests were used. While the Cochran-Armitage trend tests were used to examine linear trends for ordinal categorical data, chi-squared tests were utilized to compare categorical variables. Three variables—annual family income, education, and access to healthcare—were combined to create a composite socioeconomic position (SEP) index using principal component analysis (PCA). Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) statistics (≥ 0.7) and substantial Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.001) validated the data appropriateness, and the PCA component extraction followed the simultaneous examination of the scree plot and Kaiser’s criteria eigenvalues (≥ 1).

Mplus 7.4 was used for structural equation modeling (SEM) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and the data were fitted using weighted least squares with the mean and variance adjusted (WLSMV). SEM was performed in four steps: (1) An initial theoretical model was constructed on the basis of the literature review [23], which included six observed variables (age, gender, ethnicity, family history of chronic diseases, unbalanced diet, and physical inactivity) and two latent variables (obesity status and SEP) that were represented by rectangles and ovals and direct/indirect paths that were represented by arrows through one or more mediators. The assumption for all statistically significant choices was a two-tailed p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 7,600 individuals aged ≥ 35 years were recruited for this study. Of these, 7,508 agreed to participate, corresponding to a response rate of 98.8%.

Table 1 summarizes the general characteristics of the study population. The study population included 3,696 males (49.2%) and 3,812 females (50.8%). Males demonstrated significantly higher levels of educational attainment, annual household income, and SEP compared to females. The prevalence of physical inactivity, an imbalanced diet, obesity, central obesity, a family history of non-communicable diseases, and a higher mean systolic blood pressure was higher in females (p < 0.05).

Discussion

Our results imply that socioeconomic and behavioural factors were correlated with the comorbidity of the six chronic NCDs in the Chinese rural area via direct and indirect pathways. The lines of the pathways are as follows: obesity and family history of NCDs contributed directly, while age, ethnicity, SEP, physical inactivity and diet worked through both pathways.

Raised blood pressure was the most common NCD in this study, followed by raised blood glucose. A nationally representative study in China reported a hypertension prevalence of 24.7% in adults [31], which is much lower than our 56.3%. Similarly, prevalence of raised blood glucose in this study (10.8%) was much higher than the 5.9% reported in a study of over half a million Chinese people by Fan et al. [32]. Prevalence rates of raised blood pressure and raised blood glucose were also higher than those observed in HICs [33, 3435] and LMICs [36, 37]. This presents an immense challenge to public health efforts suggesting that government initiatives to reduce raised blood pressure and raised blood glucose in rural Southwest China may need to adopt more dedicated and multidimensional approaches.

The research found that there are gender differences in the prevalence of NCDs, for example, more males compared with females had COPD, while more females compared with males had raised blood pressure and hyperlipidemia. This was in agreement with other studies in China and other low and middle income countries (LMICs) [38, 39]. This can be explained since in our study population, obesity and central obesity were more common in females than in men, but smoking was far much common in males as compared to girls. As several prior studies have shown, being obese or centrally obese is a known significant risk factor for developing elevated blood pressure and hyperlipidemia, and tobacco use continues to be the primary aetiologic driver of COPD [4, 35]. These pieces of evidence highlight the necessity of implementing comprehensive lifestyle interventions for NCD prevention and management, while also calling for the development of gender-tailored strategies in future public health initiatives.

Our study revealed that the overall prevalence of chronic NCD multimorbidity was 18.9%, which is lower than that reported in northern (27.0%), southern (25.7%), eastern (29.1%), and central (28.0%) China [40] and lower than that reported in HICs such as Singapore (36.9%) [41] and southern Europe (43.9%) [34]. Compared with the rates of LMICs, this rate is significantly higher than that of Bangladesh (8.4%) [42] but lower than that of Egypt (19.6%) [43] and Serbia (30.2%) [44]. The quantity and kinds of chronic illnesses chosen for inclusion and analysis may have a significant impact on these results; thus, comparing NCD multimorbidity studies is difficult.

Numerous studies conducted both inside and outside China have demonstrated that females are at greater risk of experiencing NCD multimorbidity than males are [35, 40]. Nevertheless, our survey did not corroborate this observation in the studied communities. Male and female rates of NCD multimorbidity did not vary significantly in the populations we studied. Why this discrepancy exists is not clear, and more research is needed to ascertain the nature of the relationship between sex and NCD multimorbidity in rural Southwest China.

The prevalence of multimorbidity was found to increase with age. This result is supported by other studies [10, 11]. Among the participants older than 65 years, the proportion with multimorbidity was 27.5%, which is similar to the overall pooled estimate of 29.7% based on data from a meta-analysis conducted in community settings across 31 LMIC [11]. Our results suggest that aging was an important factor leading to the development of multimorbidity as suggested by the pathways derived from SEM analysis. Furthermore, indirect associations were confirmed in this study: the increasing age of individuals contributed to a greater prevalence of multimorbidity in older adults, which was driven by the negative mediation of SEP and the positive mediation of an unbalanced diet and physical inactivity. The findings suggest that while age cannot be changed, an alternative approach could be to strengthen interventions targeting older people with low SEP and unhealthy lifestyles to reduce multimorbidity.

In line with the results of a prior Chinese investigation [45], Han respondents in our study had a greater burden of chronic NCD multimorbidity than their Non-Han counterparts. Furthermore, ethnicity had a negative effect on multimorbidity mediated by SEP according to the SEM analysis. These findings thus suggest that multiple morbidity prevention strategies for NCDs should focus on the Han population with low SEP.

Previous research have indicated that socioeconomically disadvantaged groups carry a higher burden of chronic diseases and morbidity [4, 13]. A recent analysis of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) also highlighted that the strong association of socioeconomic factors (such as education level and income) and lifestyle elements (including physical exercise and activities of daily living) in the formation of multimorbidity among population aged ≥ 60 years [46]. Our SEM results in rural southwest China corroborate and quantify this pathway among rural residents aged 35 and above: The prevalence of certain NCD morbidities was directly and negatively impacted by SEP (−0.050), as well as a substantial indirect effect mediated by its negative association with dietary imbalance and physical inactivity (−0.015). A higher SEP could help someone make better decisions about their food and exercise routine. So this would suggests that other lifestyles changes may be necessary to prevent or manage NCDs multimorbidity in people of lower SEP.

In agreement with previous findings [47], we further found that clustering of NCDs was strikingly more common among participants with unhealthy lifestyles—physical inactivity, dietary imbalance, obesity, and central obesity—each of which exerted a positive statistically significant effect on multimorbidity. Obesity had the strongest total effect (0.401) on NCD multimorbidity, while physical inactivity and dietary imbalance had indirect effects via their positive associations with obesity. These related lifestyle pathways underline the pivotal role that obesity plays in multimorbidity prevention. Prioritizing the management of individuals with high BMI or WC could markedly prevent the development of multiple NCDs, especially in a rural area population with physical inactivity and unbalanced diet.

The following limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to determine causal relationships. Second, chronic disease definitions were based on physical examinations and self-reported data; in particular, adherence to the 8-hour fasting requirement prior to FBG testing was verbally confirmed by participants, while this pragmatic approach is conventional in large-scale field research; it may introduce reporting bias and affect the accuracy of chronic disease prevalence estimates. Third, the restricted set of six chronic non-communicable diseases used to define multimorbidity may have yielded a conservative prevalence estimate. Finally, although the three surveyed counties were selected at random, the modest geographic coverage constrains extrapolation of the findings to the provincial population as a whole.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the prevalence of many NCDs in rural Southwest China is correlated with both socioeconomic and lifestyle variables. More attention should be paid to those with low SEP and unhealthy lifestyles in future treatments for the prevention and management of various comorbidities linked to NCDs.
