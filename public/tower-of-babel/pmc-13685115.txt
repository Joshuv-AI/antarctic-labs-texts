Association of insomnia with anxiety might be greater than that of depression among patients with gastrointestinal cancer: a network analysis
Authors: Gaorong Lv, Yuanyuan Li, Xudong He, Guopeng Li, Xiaona Shen, Ping Li, Xiuzhi Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with gastrointestinal cancer frequently encounter a myriad of sleep disorders, and these disturbances can profoundly exacerbate their psychological distress, particularly anxiety and depression. Elucidating the intricate relationship between sleep disorders and psychological disturbances holds paramount significance in enhancing the therapeutic efficacy and improving the survival outcomes for these patients.MethodsA cross-sectional study including 352 participants from two hospitals was conducted. Insomnia symptoms and psychological distress were assessed. Pearson correlation analysis and network analysis were used to determine the association between insomnia and psychological distress components.ResultsIn this study, the prevalence of psychological distress was recorded at 45.2%, while the prevalence of difficulty initiating sleep (DIS), difficulty maintaining sleep (DMS), and early morning awakening (EMA) stood at 37.5%, 34.9%, and 46.9%, respectively. The partial correlation showed the strongest correlation was observed between DIS and anxiety, with a coefficient of 0.15 (0.00, 0.23), and no direct conditional association was observed between depression and any of the insomnia symptoms within the network analyzed.ConclusionsEmphasizing the crucial role of difficulty initiating sleep (DIS) in relation to anxiety deepens our understanding of the intricate interplay between physical and mental symptoms experienced by gastrointestinal cancer patients. By recognizing and comprehending these interconnected factors, healthcare professionals can devise more tailored therapeutic strategies, ultimately contributing to enhanced overall patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05244-4.

Background

Gastrointestinal (GI) cancers, such as colorectal cancer and stomach cancer, are among the most prevalent cancers globally [1], and disconcertingly, the majority are discovered at advanced stages, resulting in substantial physical and psychological burdens for patients [2–5]. One-third of GI cancer patients have reported experiencing anxiety, and one-fourth of patients have experienced depression [6]. Furthermore, sleep disturbances, notably insomnia, which encompasses difficulty falling asleep (DIS), difficulty maintaining sleep (DMS), and early morning awakening (EMA), are prevalent grievances voiced by these patients [7]. Notably, insomnia symptoms and psychological distress frequently co-occur and are interrelated [8, 9]. A study conducted on elderly cancer patients revealed that approximately one-third of them reported a co-occurrence of insomnia and mood disorders [10]. A meta-analysis has pointed out that a cluster of insomnia, depression, and anxiety symptoms has been widely observed in both population-based and clinical samples [11]. Furthermore, another meta-analysis specifically in cancer patients has provided evidence to support fatigue, depression, and insomnia as a symptomatic cluster [7, 12].

Despite the increased attention paid to the association between insomnia and psychological distress [13, 14], only a limited number of studies have differentiated among the various components of insomnia and established which of these components are strongly related to anxiety and/or depression. Moreover, the conclusions drawn from these studies have been inconsistent [8, 15, 16]. Hartz et al. [17] and La et al. [18] have explored the relationships between distinct insomnia symptoms and psychological distress in rural and general populations, respectively. Their findings indicated that depression was strongly associated with DIS, while anxiety exhibited the strongest association with DMS. Another study focusing on elderly adults reported that depression was associated with all three symptoms of insomnia, but anxiety was only significantly associated with DIS, with this particular association being the strongest [19]. Although these findings have enabled us to gain insights into the diverse relationships among the various components of insomnia, anxiety, and depression, they are somewhat contradictory across different individuals and have yet to be validated in patients with gastrointestinal cancer. Notably, although anxiety and depression are often highly comorbid in both general and oncological populations, they are nevertheless considered distinct constructs with unique features [20]. Given the significance of insomnia and psychological distress in the prognosis of GI cancer patients, it is imperative to develop a comprehensive yet nuanced understanding of these associations to guide the management and treatment of these co-occurring symptoms.

Network analysis (NA), as a methodology that offers a more intricate examination of the relationships between variables, allows for the estimation of partial correlations – the assessment of the associations between key variables while accounting for the influence of covariables [21]. A study conducted on individuals with depression utilized this method to identify DIS as the primary target for preventing depression [22]. This method has also been applied to patients with gastric cancer, enabling the analysis of the relationships between cancer-related physical and psychological symptoms, as well as quality of life [23]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has hitherto employed this methodological approach to delve into the intricate interconnections between insomnia symptoms and the various dimensions of psychological distress among patients with gastrointestinal cancer.

Hence, the primary objective of this study was to investigate the intricate associations between insomnia and psychological distress, focusing on specific symptoms and domains, among patients with GI cancer. Additionally, this study aimed to identify which symptom or domain serves as a pivotal point in these complex relationships, utilizing NA as a tool for deeper insights.

Methods

Participants and procedure

A cross-sectional study was conducted and received approval from the research ethics committee. This study adopted the convenience sampling method. Participants were recruited from two hospitals between January 2020 and September 2020. Prior to the assessment, all participants provided informed consent. The eligibility criteria encompassed: (i) being 18 years of age or older; (ii) having a diagnosis of primary gastric or colorectal cancer; (iii) being on a planned or ongoing anti-cancer therapy; and (iv) expressing willingness to participate. Participants were excluded if they: (i) had cognitive impairment; (ii) had a history of major psychiatric illnesses (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder); (iii) had severe complications affecting one or more organs (e.g., heart failure, other malignant tumors initially occurring in other locations); or (iv) were unable to receive and complete the questionnaires independently or with assistance. A total of 352 participants were included in the final analysis. The researchers approached patients with GI cancer on the day of their hospitalization to invite them to participate. Interested participants were provided with brief information about the study, participant criteria, and incentives. Subsequently, they were invited to complete questionnaires. All questionnaires were collected on the day of distribution.

Measures

Sociodemographic and clinical information

Sociodemographic characteristics consisted of age, gender, residence, marital status, educational level, and financial status, while clinical characteristics included factors such as BMI, comorbidity history, diagnosis, stage, time since diagnosis, and treatments received for gastrointestinal cancer.

Insomnia symptoms

Insomnia symptoms, comprising DIS, DMS, and EMA, were evaluated using three questions derived from the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), a self-report instrument that assesses insomnia symptoms over the past two weeks [24]. The three questions were as follows: (i) “Did it take you more than 30 minutes to fall asleep at night?“; (ii) “On nights when you woke up before your desired wake-up time, did you have difficulty falling back asleep?“; (iii) “Did you wake up too early in the morning?“. Participants could select their answer from five options: 0 = no; 1 = mild; 2 = moderate; 3 = severe; 4 = very severe. A higher score indicates a more severe insomnia symptom. The alpha values were 0.857 (for the 7-item version) and 0.805 (for the 3-item version).

Psychological distress

The Chinese version of Kessler 10 Scale (K10) [25] was utilized to evaluate psychological distress over the most recent 4-week period. This 10-item scale comprises two dimensions: anxiety symptoms (4 items) and depression symptoms (6 items). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (never felt) to 5 (felt all the time). Scores for the two dimensions were calculated by summing the scores of the respective items, and a total score was obtained by adding the scores of the two dimensions together. A higher score indicates a higher level of psychological distress. According to previous studies, scores above 22 are indicative of psychological distress [26–28]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the total scale and the two sub-dimensions were 0.92, 0.82, and 0.88, respectively.

Data analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R 4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) software. All tests were two-sided, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant. In this study, missing values were handled using mean imputation. Descriptive statistics were used to report sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, as well as the scores of insomnia symptoms (including DIS, DMS, and EMA), and psychological distress (including total distress, anxiety symptoms, and depression symptoms) among GI cancer patients. Student’s t-tests, one-way ANOVAs, Rank Sum Tests, and Cochran-Armitage trend tests were conducted to establish variations in insomnia symptoms and psychological distress based on sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. A heat map visualized the correlation coefficients (Pearson) between insomnia symptoms and psychological distress. The criteria for interpreting correlation strength according to Cohen’s framework are: small effect, |r| = 0.10; medium effect, |r| = 0.30; large effect, |r| = 0.50.

Mixed graphical model (MGM) [29], as a type of network analysis, was used to examine the direct and indirect correlations between insomnia symptoms and psychological distress, taking into account sociodemographic and clinical covariates. Compared with other network models, this method has the advantage of being able to include mixed variables such as binary, categorical, ordinal, count, continuous, and/or skewed continuous, among others [29]. The R package mgm() was used to estimate the pairwise network, and the algorithm with “cross-validation with 10 folds” was chosen [29, 30]. To evaluate the robustness of the network structure, sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative model selection methods, including EBICglasso (λ = 0.25), bootnet (λ = 0.001), and cross-validation with 5 folds as well as with 20 folds. The predict() function was called to compute predictions and nodewise errors from the estimated model. Additionally, the qgraph package was chosen to visualize the network, and the resample() function was used to obtain empirical sampling distributions using the nonparametric bootstrap [29]. In the graphical network, each node represents a variable, and edges represent regularized partial correlations between two nodes [31]. The connection strength (cs) was used to quantify the correlations between any two nodes. The thicker the line, the stronger the association. To minimize the impact of different scales of continuous variables, the mean scores of entries were utilized.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Of the 352 GI cancer patients, the ages ranged from 26 to 87 years (mean age = 58.32 years, standard deviation [SD] = 12.05). Two hundred and thirty-seven (67.33%) were male, 161 (45.74%) lived in urban areas, and 320 (90.91%) were married. One hundred and twenty-three (34.94%) patients were diagnosed with gastric cancer, and 229 (65.06%) with colorectal cancer. The majority of them (61.08%) were in the non-metastasis stage, and 142 (40.34%) received a combination of surgery and adjunctive therapies. Univariate analyses revealed significant differences in insomnia, depression, and anxiety across socio-demographic variables such as gender, financial status, stage, and treatments received. Status of psychological distress and insomnia symptoms

According to the cut-off score of the K10, a total of 159 (45.2%) GI cancer patients experienced psychological distress. The mean score for psychological distress was 21.34 (SD = 7.06), for anxiety symptoms was 8.54 (SD = 3.00), and for depression symptoms was 12.79 (SD = 4.41). Regarding the status of insomnia symptoms, based on the cut-off used in this study, 37.5% reported DIS, 34.9% reported DMS, and 46.9% reported EMA. The scores of insomnia symptoms were as follows: DIS (mean = 1.28, SD = 0.99), DMS (mean = 1.21, SD = 0.99), and EMA (mean = 1.50, SD = 1.00).

The relationship between insomnia symptoms and psychological distress, anxiety and depression symptoms

Pearson correlation analysis

The results showed that anxiety had its strongest association with DIS (r = 0.51), followed by DMS (r = 0.49), and its weakest association with EMA (r = 0.41); similarly, depression had a stronger association with DIS (r = 0.43) and DMS (r = 0.43) than with EMA (r = 0.35). In total, insomnia symptoms had a positive, moderate-to-high correlation with psychological distress subtypes, with the correlation coefficients ranging from 0.35 to 0.51 (all p < 0.001), which is depicted in Table 2.

Network analysis

The adequacy of this sample size was verified using a post-hoc parametric bootstrap (200 replicates) based on the estimated network. We found that the overall edge-detection sensitivity was 0.557 (95% CI: 0.318–0.773), specificity was 0.753, and sensitivity for substantively important edges (|weight| > 0.1) reached 0.648.

As shown in Supplemental Table 2, the predictability of nodes in this network revealed that all nodes except for gender (node 1) and financial status (node 3) could be predicted to some extent by their neighboring nodes, indicating that the credibility of the network was acceptable. The strongest edges were between stage (node 2) and time since diagnosis (node 4), and between anxiety symptom (node 9) and depression symptom (node 10). Insomnia symptoms clustered together, and the regularized partial correlation coefficients ranged from 0.17 to 0.45. Among the edges between psychological distress (referring to anxiety symptom and depression symptom) and insomnia symptoms, the strongest were from DIS (node 6) to anxiety symptom (node 9) and from EMA (node 8) to anxiety symptom (node 9). There were no direct correlations between any insomnia symptom and depression symptom. To further enhance the stability of the estimates, a bootstrapped sampling distribution for parameter estimates was run. As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 1, the arithmetic means of the sampling distributions for the edges from DIS (node 6) to anxiety symptom (node 9) and from EMA (node 8) to anxiety symptom (node 9) were located further from zero than other edges. These edges, in contrast, the other edges included those from DMS (node 7) to anxiety symptom (node 9), from DMS (node 7) to depression symptom (node 10), from DIS (node 6) to depression symptom (node 10), and from EMA (node 8) to depression symptom (node 10). This result indicated that the proportion of estimated nonzero effects for the two prominent edges was much higher than that for the other edges. Sensitivity analyses using alternative model selection methods, including EBICglasso (λ = 0.25), bootnet (λ = 0.001), and cross-validation with 5 and 20 folds, produced highly consistent network structures, and all key edges remained stable, confirming the robustness of the findings. The networks are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Furthermore, we assessed potential collinearity between anxiety and depression by computing variance inflation factors (VIFs) with each insomnia symptom as the dependent variable. The VIFs for depression and anxiety ranged from 1.773 to 1.862, confirming that the high bivariate correlation between anxiety and depression did not introduce problematic collinearity. To further examine this, we compared networks with and without the anxiety variable and found that a significant depression–insomnia edge emerged when anxiety was excluded (Fig. 2), indicating that the depression–insomnia relationship may be indirect and mediated through anxiety.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the intricate interrelationships between various facets of insomnia and psychological distress through a comprehensive network analysis. Our findings revealed that these associations varied significantly across distinct insomnia symptoms and subtypes of psychological distress. Specifically, anxiety exhibited the strongest correlation with DIS, followed by EMA, but showed no association with DMS. Conversely, there was no discernible correlation between depression and any insomnia symptom. This finding was similar to that of a previous study focusing on women with invasive ovarian cancer, which found a relationship between insomnia and anxiety [32].

Univariate analysis revealed significant differences in anxiety, depression, and difficulty falling asleep across disease stages: participants in the metastatic stage had higher scores for anxiety, depression, and difficulty falling asleep than those in the non-metastatic stage. Patients with advanced cancer face shorter expected survival, more severe physical symptoms (e.g., pain), and fear of disease progression, all of which collectively exacerbate psychological distress and difficulty falling asleep. Furthermore, network analysis showed that disease stage was associated with disease-related factors such as time since diagnosis and treatment received, which is consistent with clinical reality: patients with advanced-stage disease typically have a longer disease course and receive more comprehensive treatment regimens.

In the current research, nearly half of GI cancer patients reported experiencing psychological distress, while approximately one-third reported insomnia. These findings largely mirror those of previous studies in GI cancer, which reported an overall prevalence of 47.2% for anxiety and 57% for depression, and 38.6% of patients reporting insomnia [4, 5, 33]. Furthermore, the Pearson correlation analysis underscored anxiety’s strongest association with DIS, followed by DMS, and a weaker link with EMA. Likewise, depression demonstrated a stronger correlation with DIS and DMS compared to EMA. These observations are consonant with findings from diverse populations, including cancer patients, further validating their robustness [9, 34, 35]. However, NA solely validated the direct relationships between DIS, EMA, anxiety and depression. The findings of this study are partially consistent with a study of 184 advanced cancer patients (multiple cancer types) [36], which reported that anxiety was associated with DIS and DMS but not with EMA, while depression was not significantly associated with any of the three insomnia symptoms. This lends some support to the generalizability of our findings to the cancer population. Discrepancies between the two studies regarding anxiety’s associations with DMS and EMA may be attributable to differences in statistical approaches, assessment tools, and sample characteristics (e.g., cancer stage, treatment protocols).

Specifically, this research underscores the strongest relationship between DIS and anxiety, mirroring the findings of Geng et al. [8] who also noted that sleep initiation problems were more severe than other insomnia symptoms in individuals with anxiety disorders. A possible explanation lies in two aspects: Firstly, the cognitive model of insomnia posits that DIS, characterized by worry, aligns with the central feature of anxiety disorder [37, 38]. Secondly, anxiety frequently coincides with physiological arousal, such as elevated blood pressure, heart rate, cortisol levels, and serotonin, which disrupts normal sleep-wake regulation and contributes to difficulties in sleep initiation [39]. Regarding the interrelationship between EMA and anxiety in this study, this finding concurs with prior literature [34, 40], which posits that EMA-induced sleep insufficiency can diminish amygdala-prefrontal connectivity, indicative of reduced cognitive control over emotional brain regions, thereby contributing to anxiety [41]. A study involving 1083 subjects revealed that anxiety reduces both the number and duration of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, as well as the duration of the third and fourth stages of non-REM sleep [42]. This phenomenon accelerates circadian rhythms, ultimately contributing to EMA. However, the lack of association between DMS and anxiety observed in this study contrasts with prior research [43], which suggested that anxiety was linked to increased nocturnal awakenings. A plausible explanation for this discrepancy could be that the nocturnal sleep disruptions in our sample were more intimately tied to suboptimal sleep environments in the ward than to emotional states. Moreover, given the relatively low prevalence of DMS (34.9%) in this study, the relationship between sleep maintenance and anxiety may have been underestimated.

Interestingly, our network analysis did not reveal any significant association between depression and insomnia symptoms, despite the existence of significant Pearson correlations. The Pearson correlation reflects the simple linear association between two variables without controlling for others. In contrast, network edges represent partial correlations, capturing the unique association after controlling for all other variables. Thus, if a zero‑order correlation is mediated or confounded by a third variable, the corresponding partial correlation may become negligible. Moreover, the R2 value for anxiety in the network was approximately 0.71, which is attributable to the combined effects of gender, time since diagnosis, DIS, EMA, and depression. Female patients and those with a longer time since diagnosis are more likely to experience anxiety. The relationships of DIS and EMA with anxiety have been described previously, and the interaction between depression and anxiety is widely recognized. Therefore, individuals with these characteristics may be more prone to anxiety.

While this finding is supported by some literature [9, 44, 45], it contrasts with the observations made by Yokoyama et al. [15], who reported a correlation between depression and both early morning awakening and difficulties in sleep initiation. Given the robust correlations observed between anxiety and both insomnia symptoms and depression, we speculate that the relationship between insomnia symptoms and depression may be an indirect one, mediated through anxiety, which has been found in previous studies to exhibit a stronger relationship with insomnia than depression [46]. Another plausible explanation is that a substantial portion (around 38%) of the clinical sample in this study reported experiencing “mild” insomnia issues. Consequently, the inclusion of minor, transient insomnia cases may have diluted the insomnia patient population, potentially weakening the observed association between insomnia and depression.

The findings of this study hold significant implications for both future research endeavors and clinical practices. Notably, the innovative NA approach highlights the potential importance of anxiety, DIS, and EMA within the network, suggesting that they should be carefully considered as potential indicators of comorbid insomnia and psychological distress. This understanding can aid in pinpointing intervention targets for managing comorbid anxiety/depression and insomnia. Furthermore, this discovery is instrumental in elucidating the emotional complexities faced by GI cancer patients experiencing various types of insomnia. Specifically, those manifesting insomnia symptoms, particularly DIS and EMA, are more susceptible to anxiety. Consequently, when devising clinical interventions aimed at alleviating insomnia in GI cancer patients, it is imperative to also consider incorporating strategies or components that specifically target anxiety.

Nonetheless, it is crucial to acknowledge several limitations in our study. Firstly, because this is a cross-sectional investigation, we are unable to establish causal links between insomnia and psychological distress. Longitudinal studies are imperative to unravel these relationships. Secondly, all data are self-reported, and no objective method was used to measure sleep or clinically assess mental disorders. This increases the risk of inflated correlations due to common-method bias. Future research should further incorporate more objective indicators. Thirdly, the insomnia measurement tool employed may not comprehensively capture the participants’ sleep disturbances and may introduce misclassification bias. Furthermore, the recall periods of the instruments used in this study were inconsistent. This mismatch in time frames may introduce bias. Thus, the results should be interpreted with caution and consistent measurement tools should be used in the future. Forth, the relationship between insomnia and psychological distress may be influenced by various factors (e.g., the severity of cancer-related symptoms, medications, comorbid sleep disorders and insomnia treatments) not fully accounted for in our study’s covariate analysis. Lastly, it is difficult to determine whether the observed associations are attributable to the oncological diagnosis itself or reflect patterns commonly observed in the general population without a comparison population. Future studies should include a control group to further clarify whether these relationships are specific to cancer patients.

Conclusions

This research offers a thorough examination of the interplay between insomnia and psychological distress that is mediated by negative affectivity in patients with gastrointestinal cancer, revealing anxiety, DIS, and EMA as crucial junctures linking these conditions. These identified variables hold promise as high-yield intervention points for enhancing the management of patients’ psychosomatic comorbidities. Additionally, DIS and EMA show significant associations with anxiety, and these associations present a promising avenue for anxiety risk detection and preventive strategies.
