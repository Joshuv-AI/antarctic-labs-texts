Spherical designs for finite quaternionic unit groups and their applications to modular forms
Masatake Hirao, Hiroshi Nozaki, Koji Tasaka

Abstract
. For a finite subset X X of the d d -dimensional unit sphere, the harmonic strength T ⁡ ( X ) T(X) of X X is the set of ℓ ∈ ℕ \ell\in{\mathbb{N}} such that ∑ x ∈ X P ⁡ ( x ) = 0 \sum_{x\in X}P(x)=0 for all harmonic polynomials P P of homogeneous degree ℓ \ell . For these three groups, we determine the harmonic strength and show the minimality and the uniqueness as spherical designs. 1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

A finite non-empty subset
X
of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
of the
d
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
spherical
t
-design
if it holds that

\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in X}P(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}P(\boldsymbol{x})d\sigma(\boldsymbol{x})\quad\mbox{for all $P\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]$ of degree $\leq t$},

where
\sigma
is the surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|=\int_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}d\sigma(\boldsymbol{x})
.
This concept was first introduced by Delsarte–Goethals–Seidel in
[
17
]
.
Since then, the problem of determining the maximum value of
t
for a given finite subset
X
of the unit sphere, referred to as the
strength
of
X
, and the characterization of such subsets as spherical designs have been studied in connection with various algebraic structures
[
3
,
5
]
.

We wish to study these problems in the context of spherical designs of harmonic index, which is a slight generalization of spherical
t
-designs.
For
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the
harmonic strength

T(X)
of
X
by

(1.1)

T(X):=\left\{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}\ \middle|\ \sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in X}P(\boldsymbol{x})=0,\ \forall P\in{\rm Harm}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right\},

where
{\rm Harm}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the
\mathbb{R}
-vector space of harmonic polynomials of homogeneous degree
\ell
.
Note that
X
is a spherical
t
-design if and only if
1,2,\ldots,t\in T(X)
.
A finite subset
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
such that
T\subset T(X)
is called a
spherical
T
-design
.
This concept was introduced by Delsarte–Seidel
[
18
]
as a spherical analogue of the design in association schemes
[
16
, Section 3.4]
,
and has been actively studied in a decade (cf.
[
6
,
22
,
24
,
25
,
28
,
30
,
38
]
).

Our fundamental problems are as follows.

Problem 1.1
.

(i)

Given
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, determine
T(X)
.

(ii)

Given
T\subset{\mathbb{N}}
, characterize finite subsets
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
T\subset T(X)
.

As an illustrative example of addressing Problem
1.1
, consider a finite subset
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
that is

, meaning that
-\boldsymbol{x}\in X
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in X
.
In this case, the harmonic index
T(X)
contains the set
\mathbb{O}^{+}
of all positive odd integers, providing a partial solution to (i).
Regarding (ii), a finite subset
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
must necessarily be antipodal if
\mathbb{O}^{+}\subset T(X)
(cf.
[
25
]
).

Remark that Problem
1.1
(i) is closely connected to the theory of modular forms through spherical theta functions, which was first pointed out by Venkov
[
36
]
.
Regarding Problem
1.1
(ii), we note that for given

, the existence of spherical

-designs was shown in
[

]
.
Bondarenko–Radchenko–Viazovska

In this paper, we mainly consider a set of certain quaternions as

Denote by

Taking

The subgroup

We will start from the following finite subgroups of

1.2.
Main results

Let us state our results on Problem
1.1
(i).
For each
G\in\{2T,2O,2I\}
, we will show in Lemma
4.3
that

(1.3)

T(G)\subset T\big(\mathcal{O}_{G,m}\big),\quad\forall m\in{\mathbb{N}}.

This implies that determining the harmonic strength

gives a partial answer to Problem

(i) for the case

.
The first main result of this paper is a complete description of

.

Theorem 1.2
.

The harmonic strength of the finite quaternionic groups
2T,2O
and
2I
are given by

\displaystyle{\rm(i)}\ T(2T)=\{10,4,2\}\cup\mathbb{O}^{+},

\displaystyle{\rm(ii)}\ T(2O)=\{22,14,10,6,4,2\}\cup\mathbb{O}^{+},

\displaystyle{\rm(iii)}\ T(2I)=\{58,46,38,34,28,26,22,18,16,14,10,8,6,4,2\}\cup\mathbb{O}^{+},

where

denotes the set of all positive odd integers.

Let us turn to Problem
1.1
(ii).
We first use the
linear programming method
established in
[
17
]
to obtain a lower bound for the cardinality of finite subsets of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
whose harmonic strength includes (more precisely, contains a subset of)
T(G)
.
Our result shows that each

is the ‘minimal’ subset of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
among those whose harmonic strength contains

.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X
be an antipodal finite subset of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

(i)

If
T(X)\supset\{10,4,2\}
, then
|X|\geq 24
.

(ii)

If
T(X)\supset\{14,10,6,4,2\}
, then
|X|\geq 48
.

(iii)

If

, then

.

In the case of spherical

-designs, the linear programming method provides a so-called Fisher type bound: if

is a spherical

-design, then
|X|\geq b_{d,t}
, where
b_{d,t}=\binom{d+e-1}{e}+\binom{d+e-2}{e-1}
if

and

if

.
However, it is not known whether the lower bound obtained by this method provides the maximum lower bound of spherical
t
-designs for a given

.
Circumstantially, a tight design, meaning that a spherical
t
-design
X
that attains the lower bound
|X|=b_{d,t}
, is known to have some good extremal properties
[
7
,
12
]
, if it exists, and its classification has therefore been studied extensively.

In a similar vein, the study of classification of ‘tight’ spherical
T
-designs has attracted a lot of attention (although no general formulation of tightness is known).
It was started in
[
6
]
for the case
T=\{t\}
.
The case

was also investigated in
[
28
]
.
Zhu et al.
[

]
obtained the classification
of tight spherical

-designs for

,
as well as the asymptotic non-existence of tight spherical

-designs for

.
They also studied the existence problem for tight spherical

.

Our classification problem is based on the fact that, for an antipodal spherical

-design

of

with

its orthogonal transformation

We say

.

(i)

The group

(ii)

1.3.
Spherical theta functions

Let
G\in\{2T,2O,2I\}
.
We will also consider a connection with the
spherical theta function

\theta_{G,P}(z)
defined for
P\in{\rm Harm}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
by

(1.4)

\theta_{G,P}(z):=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{O}_{G}}P(x)q^{\iota_{G}(N(x))}\quad(q=e^{2\pi iz}).

This function is holomorphic on the complex upper half-plane and becomes an elliptic modular form of weight
2+\ell
for
\Gamma_{0}(2)
when
G=2T
, of weight
4+\ell
for
\Gamma_{0}(2)
when
G=2O
, and of weight
4+\ell
for
{\rm SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
when
G=2I
, as shown by Hecke and Schoeneberg (cf.
[
20
, §3.1]
,
[
26
, §4.9]
,
[
27
, §6]
).
The spherical theta function can be written as

\theta_{G,P}(z)=\sum_{m\geq 0}\bigg(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{O}_{G,m}}P(x)\bigg)q^{m}.

From this, we see that, for
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the condition
\ell\in T\big(\mathcal{O}_{G,m}\big)
is equivalent to the statement that the coefficient of
q^{m}
vanishes in each of modular forms
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{v}
, where
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{v}
form a basis of the
{\mathbb{C}}
-vector space

\Theta(G,\ell):=\langle\theta_{G,P}(z)\mid P\in{\rm Harm}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{4})\rangle_{\mathbb{C}},

which is a subspace of the
{\mathbb{C}}
-vector space of modular forms.
In particular, if
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Theta(G,\ell)=0
, then
\ell\in T(\mathcal{O}_{G,m})
holds for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
This idea was employed in
[
29
,
36
]
to determine which degrees are included in the harmonic strength of the shells of a lattice. In fact, by applying the theory of modular forms to estimate the dimension of the space of spherical theta functions of a lattice, they partially determined the harmonic strength of the shells of several important lattices.

In our case, we obtain the following relationship between the harmonic strength
T(G)
and the dimension of the space
\Theta(G,\ell)
.

Theorem 1.5
.

For
G\in\{2T,2O,2I\}
, we have that
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Theta(G,\ell)=0
if and only if
\ell\in T(G)
.

It is worth emphasizing that, combining Theorems
1.2
and
1.5
, we can determine all
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
for which the space
\Theta(G,\ell)
is zero-dimensional.
Our proof does not rely on the theory of modular forms.

We remark that the problem whether the space of modular forms is generated by spherical theta functions is a version of the basis problem as raised by Eichler
[
21
]
(see also
[
23
]
and references therein).
While they use all classes of lattices with the same genus, we focus on the spherical theta functions associated with a single lattice, from the viewpoint of spherical designs.

2.1.
Exceptional quaternionic unit groups

We provide a brief overview of quaternions from the books
[
14
,
15
]
.
Let
\mathbb{H}=\{x_{1}+x_{2}i+x_{3}j+x_{4}k\mid x_{1},\ldots,x_{4}\in\mathbb{R}\}
be the
\mathbb{R}
-algebra of quaternions, where
1,i,j,k
are an
\mathbb{R}
-basis such that
i^{2}=j^{2}=-1
and
ij=k=-ji

Its (non-commutative) multiplication is given for
x=x_{1}+x_{2}i+x_{3}j+x_{4}k
and
y=y_{1}+y_{2}i+y_{3}j+y_{4}k
by

(2.1)

\displaystyle xy

\displaystyle=(x_{1}y_{1}-x_{2}y_{2}-x_{3}y_{3}-x_{4}y_{4})+(x_{2}y_{1}+x_{1}y_{2}-x_{4}y_{3}+x_{3}y_{4})i

\displaystyle+(x_{3}y_{1}+x_{4}y_{2}+x_{1}y_{3}-x_{2}y_{4})j+(x_{4}y_{1}-x_{3}y_{2}+x_{2}y_{3}+x_{1}y_{4})k.

For
x=x_{1}+x_{2}i+x_{3}j+x_{4}k\in\mathbb{H}
, denote by
\bar{x}=x_{1}-x_{2}i-x_{3}j-x_{4}k
the

of

and by

N(x)=x\bar{x}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}

the (reduced)

of

.
The norm map
N:\mathbb{H}^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{\times}
is a surjective group homomorphism.

The set of unit quaternions
\mathbb{H}_{1}=\{\varepsilon\in\mathbb{H}\mid N(\varepsilon)=1\}
forms a subgroup of
\mathbb{H}^{\times}
.
Let
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{H}_{1}
.
We note that
\varepsilon^{-1}=\bar{\varepsilon}
.
For
x\in{\rm Im}\ \mathbb{H}:=\{x_{2}i+x_{3}j+x_{4}k\mid x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in\mathbb{R}\}
, we see that
\varepsilon x\varepsilon^{-1}\in{\rm Im}\,\mathbb{H}
.
The matrix
Z_{x}
defined by the coefficient matrix
(\varepsilon i\varepsilon^{-1},\varepsilon j\varepsilon^{-1},\varepsilon k\varepsilon^{-1})=(i,j,k)Z_{\varepsilon}
is a rotation matrix, i.e.,
Z_{x}\in{\rm SO}(3):=\{A\in{\rm O}(3)\mid\det A=1\}
.
From this, the map

\rho:\mathbb{H}_{1}\rightarrow{\rm SO}(3),\quad\varepsilon\mapsto Z_{\varepsilon}

is a homomorphism.
Moreover, the map
\rho
is surjective.
Its kernel is
\{\pm 1\}
(
[
37
, Corollary 2.4.21]
).
Namely, we obtain

\mathbb{H}_{1}/\{\pm 1\}\cong{\rm SO}(3).

Thus, for any finite subgroup
G
of
\mathbb{H}_{1}
with
-1\in G
, we have the embedding
G/\{\pm 1\}\rightarrow{\rm SO}(3)
.
Here we recall that every finite subgroups of
{\rm SO}(3)
is completely classified as follows (cf.
[
37
, Proposition 11.5.2]
):

(i)

a cyclic group;

(ii)

a dihedral group;

(iii)

the tetrahedral group
A_{4}
of order 12;

(iv)

the octahedral group
S_{4}
of order 24; or

(v)

the icosahedral group
A_{5}
of order 60.

Finite subgroups of
\mathbb{H}_{1}
whose images under the map
\rho
are isomorphic to the cases (iii), (iv) and (v) above are called the binary tetrahedral group
2T
of order 24, the binary octahedral group
2O
of order 48 and the binary icosahedral group
2I
(of order 120), respectively.
Their generators can be found in
[
15
, Chapter 6.5]
.
We follow the definition given in
[
14
, Section 3.5]
.

The representations of the groups
2T
,
2O
, and
2I

Denote by

Lemma 2.1
.

For

-linear map

For

Note that the map

2.2.
Spherical design

The concept of spherical
T
-designs introduced by Delsarte and Seidel
[
18
]
applies to finite subsets of the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=1\}
in the
d
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where for
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, its inner product is denoted by

\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle:=\sum_{a=1}^{d}x_{a}y_{a}.

Under the identification
\mathbb{H}
with
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, for
x\in\mathbb{H}
we have
N(x)=\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle
, and hence,
\mathbb{S}^{3}=\mathbb{H}_{1}
.

For an integer
\ell\geq 0
, denote by
{\rm Hom}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the
\mathbb{R}
-vector subspace of
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
consisting of all homogeneous polynomials of degree
\ell
in
d
variables.
We write

\Delta:=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{d}^{2}}

for the Laplacian operator.
Let

{\rm Harm}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{P\in{\rm Hom}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid\Delta P=0\}

be the
\mathbb{R}
-vector space of harmonic polynomials of homogeneous degree
\ell
.
It is well known (see e.g., Theorem 3.2 in
[
17
]
) that
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm Harm}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\binom{\ell+d-1}{\ell}-\binom{\ell+d-3}{\ell-2}
holds for
\ell,d\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
\ell\geq 2
.

Definition 2.3
.

For a subset
T
of
{\mathbb{N}}
, a non-empty finite subset
X
of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is called a
spherical
T
-design

if for any
\ell\in T
and
P\in{\rm Harm}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have that

\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in X}P(\boldsymbol{x})=0.

Alternatively, a finite subset
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is a spherical
T
-design if
T\subset T(X)
, where
T(X)
is the harmonic strength of
X
defined in (
1.1
).

For a subset
X
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write
-X:=\{-\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{x}\in X\}
.
A set
X
is said to be
antipodal
if we have
-X=X
.
If a finite subset
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is antipodal, then the harmonic strength
T(X)
contains all positive odd integers.
Conversely, for a finite subset
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, if
T(X)
contains all positive odd integers, then
X
is antipodal (cf.
[
25
]
).

For an antipodal finite subset
X
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a subset
X^{\prime}\subset X
is called
a half set of
X
if
X
is a disjoint union of
X^{\prime}
and
-X^{\prime}
;
X^{\prime}\sqcup(-X^{\prime})=X
.
It follows that

(2.4)

|X|=2|X^{\prime}|.

For any antipodal finite subset
X
of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
(note that
\boldsymbol{0}\not\in X
), a half set of
X
always exists, but not unique.
The harmonic strength of the half set satisfies
T(X^{\prime})\cap 2{\mathbb{Z}}=\{2\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid 2\ell\in T(X)\}
(see e.g.
[
22
, Lemma 2.2]
).
It should be noted that
T(X^{\prime})
may contain odd integers (see
[
9
]
).

2.3.
Computing the harmonic strength

We now explain our method to compute the harmonic strength defined in (
1.1
).
We use the Gegenbauer (or ultraspherical) polynomials which form a class of orthogonal polynomials (cf.
[
2
, p.302]
and
[
35
, (4.7.23)]
).
For
\lambda>0
, the Gegenbauer polynomial
C_{\ell}^{\lambda}(s)\in\mathbb{R}[s]
of degree
\ell
is defined by
the coefficient of
u^{\ell}
in the formal power series
\Phi^{\lambda}(s;u):=(1-2su+u^{2})^{-\lambda}
:

\Phi^{\lambda}(s;u)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}C_{\ell}^{\lambda}(s)u^{\ell}.

It is also obtained by the recurrence relation
\ell C_{\ell}^{\lambda}(s)=2(\ell+\lambda-1)sC_{\ell-1}^{\lambda}(s)-(\ell+2\lambda-2)C_{\ell-2}^{\lambda}(s)
for
\ell\geq 2
with
C_{0}^{\lambda}(s)=1,\ C_{1}^{\lambda}(s)=2\lambda s
.
For example, the case
\ell=2
is
C_{2}^{\lambda}(s)=-\lambda+2\lambda(1+\lambda)s^{2}
.
It follows that
C_{\ell}^{\lambda}(1)=\frac{2\lambda(2\lambda+1)(2\lambda+2)\cdots(2\lambda+\ell-1)}{\ell!}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
X
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
For
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have that

\sum_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X}C_{\ell}^{(d-2)/2}(\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle)=0

if and only if
\ell\in T(X)
, where
T(X)
is the harmonic strength of
X
defined in (
1.1
).

Proof.

Write
c_{\ell,d}:=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm Harm}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Let
\{S_{\ell,n}^{(d)}\mid n=1,2,\ldots,c_{\ell,d}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\rm Harm}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with respect to the inner product defined for

F_{1},F_{2}\in{\rm Harm}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

(F_{1},F_{2}):=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}F_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})F_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})d\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}).

From the addition theorem
[
2
, Theorem 9.6.3]
, for
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, one has

\sum_{n=1}^{c_{\ell,d}}S_{\ell,n}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})S_{\ell,n}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{c_{\ell,d}}{|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|}C_{\ell}^{(d-2)/2}(\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle).

Let
Z_{n}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in X}S_{\ell,n}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then we have

\sum_{n=1}^{c_{\ell,d}}Z_{n}^{2}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X}\sum_{n=1}^{c_{\ell,d}}S_{\ell,n}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{x})S_{\ell,n}^{(d)}(\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{c_{\ell,d}}{|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X}C_{\ell}^{(d-2)/2}(\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle).

Thus, the result follows from the definition:
\ell\in T(X)
if and only if
Z_{n}=0
holds for all
n=1,2,\ldots,c_{\ell,d}
.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
d\geq 3
.
For a finite subset
X
of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have that
\ell\in T(X)
if and only if
the coefficient of
u^{\ell}
in
\sum_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X}\Phi^{(d-2)/2}(\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle;u)
is zero.

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X}\Phi^{(d-2)/2}(\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle;u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X}\sum_{\ell\geq 0}C_{\ell}^{(d-2)/2}(\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle)u^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X}C_{\ell}^{(d-2)/2}(\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle)\right)u^{\ell},

the result follows from Lemma
2.4
.
∎

The power series
\sum_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X}\Phi^{(d-2)/2}(\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle;u)\in\mathbb{R}[[u]]
as in Proposition
2.5
can be computed from the
distance distribution

R_{X}:=\{(s,A_{s}(X))\mid s\in A(X)\cup\{1\}\}
of
X
, where for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we write

(2.5)

A_{s}(X):=|\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in X\times X\mid\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle=s\}|

and

(2.6)

A(X):=\{\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle\mid\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X,\ \boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{y}\}

denotes the set of inner products of two distinct elements in
X\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Note that
A_{1}(X)=|X|
.
With this, one has

\sum_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X}\Phi^{(d-2)/2}(\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle;u)=\sum_{s\in A(X)\cup\{1\}}A_{s}(X)\Phi^{(d-2)/2}(s;u).

Our strategy is to express the above right-hand side in terms of simple rational functions in
u
and check the conditions under which the coefficient of
u^{\ell}
vanishes.

Below, we only use the case
d=4
. In this case, we simply write

\Phi(s;u):=\Phi^{1}(s;u)=\frac{1}{1-2su+u^{2}}.

4.
Application to modular forms

4.1.
Orders and modular forms

Let
F
be a totally real number field and
R
its ring of integers.
The
F
-vector space

D(F):=\{x_{1}+x_{2}i+x_{3}j+x_{4}k\mid x_{1},\ldots,x_{4}\in F\}

forms an
F
-algebra, called the
quaternion algebra over
F
.
A subring
O\subset D(F)
is an
R
-order
if there exists a basis
\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4}\}
of
D(F)
such that
O=Rb_{1}\oplus Rb_{2}\oplus Rb_{3}\oplus Rb_{4}
.
Note that the set
N(O)
of all norm values of
O
is a subset of
R
(cf.
[
37
, Chapter 10]
).
An
R
-order of
D
is said to be
maximal
if it is not properly contained in another
R
-order of
D
.

Recall our orders from the introduction.
Let
F_{2T}={\mathbb{Q}},\ F_{2O}={\mathbb{Q}}\big(\sqrt{2}\big)
and
F_{2I}={\mathbb{Q}}\big(\sqrt{5}\big)
.
From (
2.2
), we see that each
G\in\{2T,2O,2I\}
is a subset of
F_{G}^{4}
.
Let
R_{G}
be the ring of integers of
F_{G}
, i.e.,
R_{2T}={\mathbb{Z}},R_{2O}={\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{2}],R_{2I}={\mathbb{Z}}[\tau]
with
\tau=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
, and
\mathcal{O}_{G}=\langle G\rangle_{R_{G}}
the
R_{G}
-subalgebra of the quaternion algebra over
F_{G}
generated by
G
.
Then,
\mathcal{O}_{G}
is a maximal
R_{G}
-order (cf.
[
37
, Chapter 11]
).
Its basis is given in Table
1
.
For
G\in\{2T,2O,2I\}
, we define the map
\iota_{G}:R_{G}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
by

(4.1)

\displaystyle\iota_{2T}(a)=a,\quad(a\in{\mathbb{Z}}=R_{2T}),

\displaystyle\iota_{2O}(a+b\sqrt{2})=a,\quad(a+b\sqrt{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\sqrt{2}=R_{2O}),

\displaystyle\iota_{2I}(a+b\tau)=a,\quad(a+b\tau\in{\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\tau=R_{2I}).

For
G\in\{2T,2O,2I\}
, let

\mathcal{Q}_{G}(x):=\iota_{G}(N(x)).

For
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we study the following finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
:

\mathcal{O}_{G,m}=\{x\in\mathcal{O}_{G}\mid\mathcal{Q}_{G}(x)=m\}.

We give explicit formulas for
|\mathcal{O}_{G,m}|
.
For this, let
\sigma_{k}(m)=\sum_{d\mid m}d^{k}
be the divisor function.
We set
\sigma_{k}(m/2)=0
if
m
is odd.

Proposition 4.1
.

(i)

For
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
|\mathcal{O}_{2T,m}|=24\left(\sigma_{1}(m)-2\sigma_{1}\left(\frac{m}{2}\right)\right)>0
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{O}_{2T,1}=2T
.

(ii)

For
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
|\mathcal{O}_{2O,m}|=240\left(5\sigma_{3}(m)-4\sigma_{3}\left(\frac{m}{2}\right)\right)>0
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{O}_{2O,1}=2O
.

(iii)

For
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
|\mathcal{O}_{2I,m}|=240\sigma_{3}(m)>0
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{O}_{2I,1}=2I\cup\tau\,2I
.

Proof.

For each
G\in\{2T,2O,2I\}
, we first observe that
\mathcal{Q}_{G}(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}
for
x\in\mathcal{O}_{G}
defines a positive definite integral quadratic form (for background on quadratic forms, see
[
27
, Chapter 6]
).
This implies that the spherical theta function
\theta_{G,P}(z)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{O}_{G}}P(x)q^{\mathcal{Q}_{G}(x)}\ (q=e^{2\pi iz})
defined in (
1.4
), is a modular form (cf.
[
26
, §4.9]
,
[
27
, §6]
).
Then, using the theory of modular forms, we express the generating function
\theta_{G,1}(z)=\sum_{m\geq 0}|\mathcal{O}_{G,m}|q^{m}
in terms of the Eisenstein series
E_{2}(z)=1-24\sum_{m\geq 1}\sigma_{1}(m)q^{m}
and
E_{4}(z)=1+240\sum_{m\geq 1}\sigma_{3}(m)q^{m}
.
For the theory of modular forms for the congruence subgroup, see
[
19
]
.
The quadratic form
\mathcal{Q}_{G}(x)
is also used to give a description of
\mathcal{O}_{G,1}
(we solve the Diophantine equation
\mathcal{Q}_{G}(x)=1
with the aid of Mathematica).

For the Hurwitz order
\mathcal{O}_{2T}
, let

which defines a positive definite integral quadratic form of level 2 and discriminant 4.
Hence, the spherical theta function

for

is a modular form of weight
2+\ell
for
\Gamma_{0}(2)
.
The space of modular forms of weight
2
for
\Gamma_{0}(2)
is 1-dimensional spanned by

.
Therefore, we have

\theta_{2T,1}(z)=2E_{2}(2z)-E_{2}(z)=1+24q+24q^{2}+96q^{3}+24q^{4}+\cdots.

By finding integral solutions to the equation

\mathcal{O}_{2T,1}=2T
.
Below is an example of Mathematica code to compute the set

Solve[r1^2+r2^2+r3^2+r4^2-r1*r4-r2*r4-r3*r4==1, {r1,r2,r3,r4}, Integers]

For the case
\mathcal{O}_{2O}
, using the integral basis of
{\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{2}]

\displaystyle=y_{1}^{2}+y_{1}y_{4}+2y_{1}z_{2}+2y_{1}z_{3}+y_{2}^{2}+y_{2}y_{3}+2y_{2}z_{1}+2y_{2}z_{4}+y_{3}^{2}+2y_{3}z_{1}

\displaystyle+2y_{3}z_{4}+y_{4}^{2}+2y_{4}z_{2}+2y_{4}z_{3}+2z_{1}^{2}+2z_{1}z_{4}+2z_{2}^{2}+2z_{2}z_{3}+2z_{3}^{2}+2z_{4}^{2},

which is positive definite.

Hence,
\theta_{2O,P}(z)
for

for

.

.

.

.

For the case

, we also write
x=(y_{1}+z_{1}\tau)+(y_{2}+z_{2}\tau)i+(y_{3}+z_{3}\tau)\zeta+(y_{4}+z_{4}\tau)i\zeta\in\mathcal{O}_{2I}

\displaystyle+y_{3}z_{2}+y_{4}^{2}-y_{4}z_{1}+y_{4}z_{2}+z_{1}^{2}+z_{1}z_{3}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{2}z_{4}+z_{3}^{2}+z_{4}^{2}.

Thus,
\theta_{2I,P}(z)
for

is a modular form of weight

for

.
