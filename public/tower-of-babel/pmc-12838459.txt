Non-suicidal self-injury links with multidimensional cognitive dysfunctions in adolescents with depression
Authors: Shina Li, Xiuling Tian, Yihao Zhang, Ying Yang, Huijing Sun, Jiahui Chen, Defeng Xu, Feiyu Xu, Xingxing Zhu, Kangcheng Wang, Dongdong Qiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) has high prevalence among adolescents with mood disorders and is accompanied by cognitive impairments. Most existing studies have examined their impairments on single cognitive domains. It remains unclear which cognitive deficits are associated with adolescent NSSI and its functions and addictive features, from a multidimensional perspective.MethodsA total of 161 adolescents diagnosed with first-episode major depressive disorder (MDD) and 137 healthy controls were recruited between June 2020 and December 2024. Participants were divided into three groups: adolescents with MDD and NSSI (MDD + NSSI, n = 84, age = 15.47 ± 1.59), those with MDD and without NSSI (MDD–NSSI, n = 77, age = 16.11 ± 2.16), and healthy controls (HCs, n = 137, age = 15.92 ± 3.13). NSSI behavior and its four common functions (internal emotion regulation, external emotion regulation, social influence, and sensation seeking) as well as the addiction feature, were assessed using the Ottawa Self-injury Inventory. Cognitive domains such as emotion recognition, cognitive control, working memory, and reward processing were assessed using an in-house Cognitive Assessment Battery. Data were analyzed via ANOVA, logistic regression, and multiple linear regression.ResultsBoth the MDD + NSSI and MDD–NSSI groups showed significantly greater cognitive dysfunctions compared to the HCs. Logistic regression models indicated that cognitive domains such as memory, cognitive control, reward processing, delay discounting, and time perception were significantly associated with the presence of NSSI among adolescents with MDD. Multiple linear regression analyses further revealed that emotion recognition, working memory, cognitive control, and reward processing were significantly related to NSSI functions and the addictive features.ConclusionsOur results showed that NSSI behaviors, its functions, and addictive features are all associated with multidimensional cognitive dysfunctions. These findings highlight the clinical relevance of implementing thorough, multidomain cognitive evaluations in adolescents with NSSI.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07701-7.

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) manifests in various forms, including scratching, cutting, burning and biting, performed intentionally without suicidal intent [1]. It is highly prevalent among adolescents (11.5% to 33.8%), particularly those with mood and other psychiatric disorders [2]. As a maladaptive behavior with substantial psychosocial and social consequences, NSSI has garnered growing considerable clinical and research attention [3]. Prior research has mainly focused on NSSI functions in adolescents: for some, it acts as a coping strategy to alleviate negative emotions; for others, it may be a means of expressing distress, managing interpersonal conflict, or seeking external attention [4, 5]. Notably, emerging evidence indicates that NSSI is associated with multidomain cognitive impairments [6]. Adolescents engaging in NSSI displayed deficits in emotion recognition, attention, and memory [6, 7]. However, most prior studies have focused on one single or isolated cognitive domains (e.g., attention or emotional processing) [8, 9], resulting in a lack of evidence addressing NSSI-related cognitive impairments from a multidimensional perspective.

Cognitive impairments in adolescents with NSSI involve multiple dimensions, including attention, memory, cognitive control, reward processing, and emotion recognition [6, 7, 10]. Task paradigms could offer objective and accurate assessment for cognitive ability by evaluating indicators such as response time and accuracy. For example, findings from the GO/No-Go paradigm have shown that decreased cognitive control is related to more severe NSSI [11]. Moreover, adolescents with NSSI show impairments in cognitive processes such as reward processing [12], decision making [13] and emotion recognition [14]. Collectively, these studies suggest that cognitive impairments in adolescents with NSSI are not confined to a single dimension. Recent theories further propose that various cognitive functions may interact and collectively contribute to NSSI behavior [15, 16]. Therefore, investigating the multidimensional cognitive dysfunctions in adolescents with NSSI is critical to elucidate underlying mechanisms of their engagement in NSSI behavior and to provide guidance on the development of targeted interventions [17].

Beyond the NSSI behavior per se, the motivations/functions for NSSI have been linked to cognitive impairments to some extent. NSSI primarily serves four functions: internal and external emotion regulation, social influence, and sensation seeking [4, 18]. First, individuals may engage in NSSI to manage internal and external emotional states, such as sadness, numbness, anger, or frustration [19]. The emotion regulation functions are closely associated with impairments in emotion recognition, perception and memory [20, 21]. Second, NSSI can serve interpersonal functions, including eliciting attention or responding to changes in the social environment [22]. This function has been linked to impairments in cognitive control and emotion reaction [23]. Finally, sensation seeking is another key motivation factor, by which individuals engage in NSSI to cope with boredom or emotional numbness [24]. This function is found to be related with deficits in reward processing and cognitive control [25]. While the emotion regulation functions have been considerably associated with cognition [26], limited research has directly examined the multidimensional cognitive underpinnings of other functions.

The addictive feature of NSSI is another core aspect and is closely linked to multidimensional cognitive impairments [27]. Numerous studies have shown that individuals who engage in repetitive NSSI may develop addiction-like features similar to those observed in substance use disorders [18, 28], potentially further exacerbating cognitive impairments. Substance use disorders are known to result in broad impairments across multiple cognitive domains, including memory and reward processing [29, 30]. In parallel, it has been hypothesized that the addictive feature of NSSI may similarly involve multidimensional cognitive dysfunctions [31]. Recent studies have confirmed associations between the addictive feature and deficits in cognitive control [25] and reward processing [32]. However, it remains underexplored whether and which other cognitive impairments are implicated in the addictive aspect of NSSI.

Therefore, the present study aimed to comprehensively examine the associations between NSSI (NSSI behavior, its functions, and addictive feature) and multidimensional cognitive impairments. Given the high prevalence of NSSI among individuals with major depressive disorder (MDD), adolescents with MDD were included as a representative sample in this study. Participants were divided into three groups: adolescents with both MDD and NSSI behavior (MDD + NSSI), adolescents with MDD but without NSSI behavior (MDD-NSSI), and a healthy control group (HC). The associations between NSSI and cognitive impairment were examined by comparing performances across a series of cognitive tasks among three groups. We proposed the following hypotheses: (1) adolescents with MDD would exhibit impairments across multiple cognitive domains compared to healthy controls, with further differences observed between the MDD + NSSI and MDD-NSSI groups; (2) distinct functions for NSSI would be associated with multidomain cognitive deficits, with patterns of impairments varying by function types; and (3) adolescents exhibiting addictive feature of NSSI would demonstrate more pronounced cognitive impairment across various cognitive domains.

Methods

Participants

This study included a total of 298 adolescent participants from the ongoing Shandong Adolescent Neuroimaging of Depression (SAND) project [18, 33]. Among them, 161 adolescents (37 males, age = 15.77 ± 1.91, Table 1) were diagnosed with first-episode MDD by two clinical psychiatrists using the DSM-5 criteria. NSSI behavior was further assessed, and participants were classified based on the frequency of self-injury: those who had engaged in five or more episodes of NSSI on at least five separate days within the past year were assigned to the MDD + NSSI group (n = 84, 15 males, age = 15.47 ± 1.59); those with fewer than five episodes were assigned to the MDD-NSSI group (n = 77, 22 males, age = 16.11 ± 2.16). All these MDD patients were recruited from the inpatient and outpatient departments of Shandong Mental Health Center. These adolescents were taking antidepressant medications, primarily selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, including fluoxetine, sertraline, and escitalopram. Additionally, 137 healthy controls (41males, age = 15.92 ± 3.13, Table 1) were recruited through community advertisements.

Inclusion criteria for the MDD patients were as follows: (a) ability to comprehend the questionnaires and cognitive task instructions and complete them cooperatively; (b) no current comorbidity with other mental disorders; (c) no current or past history of neurological diseases or severe physical illnesses; (d) diagnosis of primary (non-secondary) major depressive disorder was not caused by organic diseases or medication use; (e) no history of substance abuse, dependence or suicide attempt in the past month. Inclusion criteria for the healthy controls included: (a) no current or historical diagnosis of any DSM-5 mental disorder, neurological diseases or severe physical illnesses; (b) ability to understand questionnaires and cognitive task requirements and complete them cooperatively; (c) the total score on Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI) below 12 [34].

The research protocol was approved by the ethics committees of Shandong Mental Health Center and Shandong Normal University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians.

Measurements

Severity of depression

Depressive symptom severity was assessed using the Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI), a widely used and validated instrument in assessing adolescent depressive symptoms and behaviors [34]. The CDI scale consists of 27 items divided into five subscales: low self-esteem, negative emotion, anhedonia, low efficiency, interpersonal problems. Each item is rated on a 3-point Likert scale (0–2), with higher scores indicating greater depressive severity. The CDI demonstrated robust reliability and validity across adolescent populations, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.973 in the current study.

Non-suicidal self-injury assessments

The Chinese version of Ottawa Self-injury Inventory (OSI) was used to assessed NSSI behaviors, functions, and addiction-related feature [35]. This 28-item instrument incorporates both dichotomous (yes/no) and 5-point Likert scale (0–4) response formats. The OSI assesses NSSI functions through a series of sub-items (e.g., “to release unbearable tension” or “to show others how strong or tough I am”), which evaluate the extent to which specific motivations correspond to NSSI engagement. OSI yields four distinct functions: internal emotion regulation, external emotion regulation, social influence, and sensation seeking. In addition, the OSI scale comprises 7 sub-items adapted from the DSM criteria for substance dependence, which were designed to assess the addictive feature of NSSI behavior (e.g., “Despite a desire to cut down or control this behavior, you are unable to do so?“). Each item was rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (always). The OSI has demonstrated good reliability and validity, and has been validated for use in adolescent clinical populations [36].

Cognitive ability assessments

An in-house Cognitive Assessment Battery [33] was developed using PsychoPy3 and incorporated 11 validated cognitive paradigms to evaluate multiple cognitive domains, including emotional recognition, attention bias, memory, cognitive control, reward processing, delay discounting, and time perception. The battery included the following tasks: facial emotional recognition task, dot-probe task, go/no-go task, Stroop color and word task, n-back test, scene recognition task, Flanker task, task switching task, simple guessing task, delay discounting task, and time perception task. Detailed descriptions of each cognitive paradigm are provided in Table S1. Very few participants (2.35%) had missing cognitive data. In addition, 1.68% of data was identified as outliers, defined as values exceeding four standard deviations from the mean for each cognitive measure. Both missing values and outliers were handled using mean imputation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.2. Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted for demographics, clinical characteristics and cognitive task performances. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies(n) and compared across groups using chi-square (χ²) tests. Continuous variables were analyzed using independent samples t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For variables showing significant group differences in ANOVA, post-hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s honestly significant difference test to identify pairwise differences.

To examine the cognitive risk factors associated with NSSI behavior, logistic regression analyses were performed using the glm function with family = binomial. The total sample was randomly divided into training set and test set at a 3:1 ratio. Initial logistic regression models were constructed using HC and MDD + NSSI groups as binary outcomes and cognitive performance variables as predictors, controlling for age and sex. Stepwise regression was performed using the step function to optimize model selection and identify significant predictors of NSSI. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and Nagelkerke pseudo R²; a p-value >0.05 in Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicates acceptable model fit [37]. Model predictive accuracy was tested on the test set using the predict function. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated, and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) was used to assess the classification performance. The same analyses were repeated using the MDD-NSSI and MDD + NSSI groups as the dependent variable, controlling for age, sex, and the total CDI score, to explore predictors of NSSI behavior among depressed adolescents.

To further assess the cognitive impairments associated with NSSI functions and addictive feature, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted within MDD + NSSI group, using lm function. Four NSSI functional dimensions separately served as dependent variables, with cognitive factors as predictors, adjusting for age, sex, and CDI total score. The step function was used to perform variable selection and derive final regression models. A similar regression analysis was performed with NSSI addictive feature regarded as the dependent variable.

Sensitivity analysis

Although the DSM-5 defines NSSI disorder as at least five episodes of NSSI in the past year, we conducted sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our findings using a loosen criterion of ≥ 1 episode within the past year. This approach allowed us to capture a broader range of adolescents with any history of NSSI and to better understand the cognitive risk characteristics associated with even limited self-injurious behavior. Specifically: (1) we reclassified the sample by the presence or absence of any NSSI history into three groups: MDD + NSSI1 (NSSI ≥ 1, n = 117, 19 males, age = 15.68 ± 1.7), MDD – NSSI1 (NSSI = 0, n = 44, 18 males, age = 16.05 ± 2.38) and HC (NSSI = 0, n = 137, 41 males, age = 15.92 ± 3.13). Demographic details were reported in Table S2. All previous statistical procedures were repeated with this grouping. (2) To address potential sex imbalance between groups and control for sex-related confounding, we randomly selected a sex-matched subsample and re-run the analyses (Table S3). These sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness and generalizability of the primary results.

Results

Demographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, and cognitive performances

No significant differences were found among the three groups in terms of sex (χ² = 4.234, p = 0.120) or age (F = 1.406, p = 0.247). Significant group differences were observed in the total score of CDI and subscale scores, NSSI functions, addictive feature, and cognitive abilities (p < 0.05; Table S2). The MDD + NSSI and MDD-NSSI groups showed significant cognitive impairments compared to the HC group. The MDD + NSSI group scored higher on CDI compared to MDD-NSSI group, whereas the two groups did not significantly differ in any single cognitive measure (Table S4).

Cognitive impairments associated with NSSI behavior

The logistic regression model for the MDD + NSSI group and HC group showed good fit (Nagelkerke R² = 0.624). Hosmer-Lemeshow test also indicated a good fit (χ²(8) = 10.633, p > 0.05). Working memory, cognitive control, reward processing, delay discounting, and time perception significantly predicted NSSI behavior in patients with MDD (Table 2). The ROC curve yielded an area of AUC of 0.896 (SE = 0.042, 95% CI: 0.813–0.979, Figure S1).

Sensitivity analyses

When classifying participants based on the presence or absence of any NSSI history, the results remained consistent with the main findings. Cognitive impairments could significantly predict NSSI behavior, its addictive feature and the four NSSI functions in adolescents with depression (Figure S2, Tables S5–8). These findings suggested the robust and stable associations between NSSI behavior and cognitive dysfunctions, independent of NSSI frequency.

In the sex-matched subsample, cognitive predictors also show significant associations with NSSI behavior, addictive feature and NSSI functions. Notably, working memory performance differed significantly between MDD + NSSI and MDD-NSSI groups (Figure S3, Tables S9–12), further highlighting the role cognitive risk factors in the NSSI engagement.

Discussion

In this study, we assessed cognitive functions of MDD adolescents with/without NSSI using a battery of cognitive experimental paradigms that covered multiple cognitive domains. We found that MDD adolescents with NSSI behavior exhibited broad cognitive impairments when compared to HCs. In addition, impairments in emotion recognition, attention, working memory, reward processing, delay discounting, and time perception could differentiate the MDD + NSSI group from the MDD-NSSI group (AUC = 64.2%). Moreover, similar multidimensional cognitive dysfunctions were associated with NSSI functions, as well as the addictive feature of NSSI. These findings highlighted associations between cognitive impairments and NSSI in adolescents with MDD and underscores the potential utility of cognitive markers in identifying and characterizing NSSI behaviors.

Adolescents who engaged in NSSI behaviors showed multidimensional cognitive dysfunctions. Firstly, our findings about deficits in emotion recognition [14], working memory [6], and cognitive control [38] were in line with previous research that focused on individual cognitive domains, supporting the presence of broad cognitive dysfunctions in NSSI adolescents. Secondly, while few studies examined time perception in individuals with NSSI [39], our results provided further evidence of worse time perception in adolescents who engaged in NSSI. Such impairments may cause them to devote more time and disproportionate cognitive resources to emotional processing. Thus, they would struggle with accurately evaluating temporal and social cues, potentially increasing the likelihood of engaging in NSSI behaviors [40, 41]. More importantly, our results were verified in individuals with any history of NSSI, showing patterns consistent with the primary findings. This suggests that adolescents with a history of NSSI also exhibit similar risks of cognitive impairment, highlighting the importance of distinguishing between MDD adolescents with and without NSSI history. From a neurodevelopmental perspective, these multidimensional cognitive impairments may be related to abnormal development of brain networks that are critical for cognitive control, emotion regulation and time perception, including the brain control network and fronto-limbic network [42, 43]. These brain network associations in adolescents with NSSI warrant further investigation in neuroimaging research.

Our analyses on functions of NSSI and the addictive feature also revealed associations with multidimensional cognitive impairments. Notably, emotion recognition ability was significantly related to all functions, suggesting that it may be one of the core cognitive components of NSSI [44]. This finding was consistent with previous studies that conceptualized NSSI as an emotion regulation strategy [26, 45]. Moreover, the most pronounced impairments were observed in social influence. This may be likely because of the heightened sensitivity of adolescents to social interactions during this critical period of social development [23]. Successfully navigating complex social contexts requires the integration of multiple cognitive processes, rendering adolescents more susceptible to cognitive dysfunction in this area [46]. Finally, the addictive feature of NSSI were found to mirror those observed in substance use disorders, with both involving extensive impairments across several cognitive domains. These broad cognitive impairments may reflect heightened reward sensitivity and underdeveloped self-regulation systems during adolescence [32]. Future studies should employ neuroimaging techniques such as functional magnetic resonance imaging to further investigate the neural mechanisms underlying these associations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, NSSI is a transdiagnostic phenomenon observed across a range of psychiatric conditions, such as borderline personality disorder, posttraumatic stress disorder, and oppositional defiant disorder [8, 47]. The inclusion of only adolescents with MDD and NSSI behaviors may limit the generalizability of our findings to other clinical populations. Future studies should include participants from a broader spectrum of mental disorders to enhance the external validity of the findings. Second, NSSI classification was primarily based on the frequency criterion (≥ 5 episodes per year) rather than the assessment of NSSI disorder according to all DSM-5 criteria. Future studies incorporating comprehensive diagnostic evaluations of NSSI disorder are warranted to clarify whether the observed cognitive impairments are specific to NSSI frequency or the full clinical syndrome. Third, while this study employed a comprehensive set of cognitive tasks, the measures primarily captured general cognitive performance and did not assess fine-grained or process-specific cognitive functions (e.g., reward valuation and reward learning). Fourth, several factors associated with NSSI behavior, such as trauma history and sleep disturbances, were not controlled as confounding variables, which may have biased the observed associations between NSSI and cognitive abnormalities. Fifth, all MDD patients were receiving antidepressant treatment, primarily SSRIs, which may have influenced cognitive performance or neural function. Although all NSSI and non-NSSI patients were medicated, potentially minimizing group differences, the confounding effects of medication cannot be fully excluded. Finally, the present study was cross-sectional and precluded any causal inference regarding relationships between NSSI behaviors and cognitive impairments. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal dynamics and directionality [48], and to assess whether targeted cognitive interventions can effectively mitigate NSSI behavior over time.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings identified multidimensional cognitive impairments in adolescents with NSSI. The observed cognitive impairments span multiple domains, including emotion recognition, memory, cognitive control, reward processing, delay discounting, and time perception. These findings not only enhance our understanding of the cognitive mechanisms underlying NSSI, but also provide a novel theoretical foundation for the cognitive intervention in adolescents with NSSI. Our results underscore the importance of conducting multidimensional assessments to fully understand potential cognitive abnormalities in adolescents with NSSI.
