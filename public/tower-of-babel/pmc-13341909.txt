Mid-thoracic predominance and region-specific risk factors for vertebral fractures in rheumatoid arthritis: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Asami Zenitani, Yu Yamashita, Kazuhiro Maeda, Shoutaro Arakawa, Mitsuru Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction/objectivesVertebral fractures are common in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), but whether their risk factors differ by spinal region remains unclear. This study aimed to identify region-specific risk factors for vertebral fractures in RA, particularly in the mid-thoracic spine and thoracolumbar junction, and to describe fracture distribution.MethodsWe retrospectively evaluated 93 patients with RA and 108 knee osteoarthritis controls. Clinical, laboratory, and bone quality markers were collected. Vertebral fractures were identified on spinal radiographs using AI-assisted software with physician confirmation. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify risk factors.ResultsVertebral fractures were more common in RA than in controls (32.3% vs. 12.0%) and clustered in the mid-thoracic region (T4–T10). Overall vertebral fractures were associated with older age, higher cumulative glucocorticoid exposure, and absence of biologic or targeted synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (b/tsDMARD) therapy. In region-specific analyses, mid-thoracic fractures were independently associated with age (odds ratio (OR) 1.10, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.03–1.19) and serum pentosidine levels (OR 17.32, 95% CI 1.49–273.04). Thoracolumbar junction fractures were associated with age (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.03–1.25), b/tsDMARD use (OR 0.11, 95% CI 0.01–0.74), DAS28-CRP (OR 2.30, 95% CI 1.14–5.08), and cumulative glucocorticoid exposure (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.02–1.18).ConclusionsVertebral fractures in RA show distinct region-specific risk profiles. Mid-thoracic fractures were associated mainly with impaired bone quality, whereas thoracolumbar junction fractures were more strongly related to disease severity and glucocorticoid exposure. These findings support region-specific fracture assessment and prevention strategies in RA. Key Points• Vertebral fractures in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) showed a characteristic predominance in the mid-thoracic spine.• Distinct region-specific risk profiles were identified, with mid-thoracic fractures associated with older age and bone quality-related impairment reflected by higher serum pentosidine levels.• Thoracolumbar junction fractures were more strongly associated with RA disease activity and cumulative glucocorticoid exposure.• Region-specific assessment may help identify different mechanisms of vertebral fragility and guide targeted prevention strategies in rheumatoid arthritis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10067-026-08174-z.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is characterized by immune dysregulation that leads to persistent synovial inflammation, osteoclast activation, and subsequent bone erosion as well as periarticular and systemic osteoporosis [1, 2]. Patients with RA exhibit a substantially increased risk of osteoporosis relative to the general population, primarily due to chronic inflammation, reduced physical activity, and glucocorticoid exposure [3, 4].

Beyond reductions in bone mineral density (BMD), long regarded as the central diagnostic criterion for osteoporosis, deterioration of bone quality has emerged as a critical determinant of skeletal fragility [5, 6]. Bone quality encompasses factors such as collagen cross-linking and the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), including pentosidine [5–7]. In patients with RA, ongoing systemic inflammation enhances osteoclast-mediated bone resorption through pro-inflammatory cytokines, accelerates deterioration of bone quality, and contributes to severe skeletal fragility [8, 9]. Therefore, effective management of bone strength requires attention to both BMD and bone quality.

Vertebral fractures are clinically important osteoporotic fractures because they often remain asymptomatic and underdiagnosed but can result in chronic back pain, progressive kyphosis, impaired pulmonary function, and increased mortality [10, 11]. In RA, vertebral fractures may reflect the combined effects of systemic osteoporosis and local inflammatory bone damage and therefore serve as key indicators of skeletal fragility [12]. Recent research has continued to highlight the importance of systemic inflammation and bone quality in skeletal fragility in RA [4].

Although the anatomical distribution and regional characteristics of vertebral fractures are well established in the general population [13, 14], comparable evidence in patients with RA remains limited. In particular, previous studies have suggested that vertebral fractures in RA tend to occur in the mid-thoracic and thoracolumbar regions [15, 16], but it remains unclear whether risk factors differ based on spinal level. Because each spinal region possesses distinct anatomical and biomechanical characteristics influencing fracture susceptibility, clarifying these distribution patterns is clinically relevant. A better understanding of region-specific risk factors may provide insight into the mechanisms underlying vertebral fractures in RA and improve fracture risk assessment.

Previous studies have identified several risk factors for vertebral fractures in RA [3, 4, 12, 15, 16], but most have evaluated vertebral fractures as a single outcome rather than by spinal region. Although regional differences in fracture distribution have been described [15, 16], few studies have integrated anatomical distribution with region-specific clinical, treatment-related, and bone quality-related determinants. This study aimed to characterize vertebral fracture distribution in RA and identify region-specific risk factors, particularly in the mid-thoracic spine and thoracolumbar junction.

Material and methods

Patients

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at the Department of Orthopaedic Surgery, The Jikei University School of Medicine, between April 2024 and September 2025. Informed consent was obtained from all participants via an opt-out procedure. This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Review Committee of Jikei University Hospital (approval No. 37–050 [12687]).

We enrolled 93 patients with RA (76 females and 17 males) aged ≥ 40 years who fulfilled the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism classification criteria [17].

The biologic and targeted synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (b/tsDMARDs) used in this study comprised biologic DMARDs and targeted synthetic DMARDs. Biologic DMARDs included infliximab (n = 1), adalimumab (n = 1), etanercept (n = 7), certolizumab pegol (n = 8), golimumab (n = 2), tocilizumab (n = 10), sarilumab (n = 3), and abatacept (n = 2) and were used in 34 patients. Targeted synthetic DMARDs consisted of Janus kinase inhibitors, including upadacitinib (n = 1), peficitinib (n = 1), filgotinib (n = 1), tofacitinib (n = 1), and baricitinib (n = 2), and were used in 6 patients.

The control group consisted of 108 patients with knee osteoarthritis (OA) (87 females and 21 males) who were treated at the same orthopaedic department during the same study period and underwent the same standardized lateral thoracic and lumbar spine radiographs. They were selected as non-RA musculoskeletal clinical controls rather than healthy controls.

Demographic and disease-related data

Demographic and clinical data for 93 patients with RA followed at the study institution from April 2024 to September 2025 were extracted from medical records. Collected variables included sex, age, disease duration, RA treatment history, osteoporosis treatment status, glucocorticoid use, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c, %), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, mL/min/1.73 m2). We also documented average daily and cumulative glucocorticoid doses. RA-related factors included rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-citrullinated peptide antibodies (ACPAs). Disease activity was assessed using the Disease Activity Score based on 28 joints (DAS28) [18], which incorporates tender and swollen joint counts, the patient’s global disease assessment, and either C-reactive protein (CRP) or erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR).

Medication data included methotrexate (MTX), other conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs), b/tsDMARDs, bisphosphonates, selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs), vitamin D, denosumab, parathyroid hormone analogs, and romosozumab.

Measurement of serum biomarkers

Serum pentosidine, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase 5b (TRACP-5b), and total procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (P1NP) were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (SRL Diagnostics, Tokyo, Japan). These markers are recognized indicators of bone metabolism and quality [5, 19]. Pentosidine reflects AGE accumulation that impairs collagen cross-linking and reduces bone strength [5], whereas TRACP-5b and P1NP serve as reference markers of bone resorption and formation [19].

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry measurements

BMD (g/cm2) was measured using a Discovery dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) system (Hologic, Waltham, MA, USA) from April 2024 to September 2025 at the lumbar spine and femoral neck. Because lumbar BMD may be artificially increased by vertebral deformities or fractures, it was excluded from analysis [20]. One patient with RA who had bilateral total hip arthroplasty was excluded from femoral neck BMD analysis.

Evaluation of vertebral fractures in the thoracic and lumbar spine

All participants underwent standardized lateral radiographs from T4 to L5. Vertebral fracture assessment was conducted using artificial intelligence (AI)-based software. The AI-based Vertebral Body Measurement Software Smart QM™ was developed by the Department of Orthopaedic Surgery, Jikei University School of Medicine, and Shimadzu Corporation (Kyoto, Japan) [21]. The software facilitates Quantitative Morphometry (QM) assessment by placing six measurement points per vertebra, which were reviewed by a physician before diagnosis. When evaluators disagreed, a third orthopaedic surgeon provided adjudication.

To compare efficiency, we randomly selected 10 control cases and compared measurement times between conventional QM and AI-based Smart QM. Two orthopaedic surgeons performed quantitative measurements for each vertebra from T4 to L5. The conventional QM followed the algorithm of the World Health Organization Collaborating Centre for Metabolic Bone Diseases, Sheffield, UK [22]. Anterior (A), central (C), and posterior (P) vertebral body heights were measured. A vertebral fracture was defined as C/A or C/P ratio < 0.80; A/P ratio < 0.75; or > 20% reduction in A, C, and P heights relative to adjacent vertebrae.

Patients with RA and vertebral fractures were categorized by fracture location into mid-thoracic (T4–T10), thoracolumbar junction (T11–L2), and lower lumbar (L3–L5) groups [23] to evaluate anatomical distribution and identify region-specific factors.

Semiquantitative (SQ) grading followed established criteria [24], grading vertebrae from 0 (normal) to 3 (severe): 20–25% height loss for grade 1, 25–40% for grade 2, and > 40% for grade 3. SQ distributions were compared between RA and control fracture groups for each vertebral level (mid-thoracic, thoracolumbar junction, and lower lumbar regions).

Thoracic kyphosis angle was measured using the Cobb method from T4 upper endplate to T12 lower endplate. Measurements by experienced observers were compared between patients with RA and controls without fractures.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or n (%). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using histograms, Q–Q plots, and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared using two-tailed Student’s t-tests when both groups showed no evidence of deviation from normality and the Mann–Whitney U test when normality was not met. Categorical variables were compared using chi-squared tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. The number of vertebral fractures per patient and QM measurement times were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Differences in SQ grade distributions were assessed using ordinal logistic regression, and regional distributions accounting for multiple vertebrae per patient were analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE).

Within the RA cohort, separate multivariable logistic regression models were constructed for overall and region-specific vertebral fractures. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analyses and clinically relevant covariates were entered in these models. Additional multivariable analyses were performed in the combined RA and OA cohort to evaluate whether RA status was associated with vertebral fractures by region. Lower lumbar fractures were excluded from multivariable models because events were few.

Additional analyses related to glycemic status included comparison of HbA1c levels according to vertebral fracture status, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis between HbA1c and serum pentosidine levels, and a multivariable sensitivity model for mid-thoracic vertebral fractures including age, serum pentosidine levels, and HbA1c.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using JMP version 18 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), while GEE analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Except for femoral neck BMD in one patient with bilateral total hip arthroplasty, analyses were performed using complete case data without imputation. Given the exploratory and retrospective nature of this study, a formal sample size calculation was not performed; instead, all eligible patients available during the study period were included.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics of 93 patients with RA and 108 patients with knee OA who served as controls. Significant between-group differences were observed in age and HbA1c, while eGFR, serum pentosidine levels, and total P1NP were comparable. TRACP-5b was not significantly different between groups. Osteoporosis treatments did not differ significantly, although SERMs were more common among patients with RA. Glucocorticoid use was significantly more frequent in patients with RA than in controls. A total of 40 patients with RA (43.0%) used oral glucocorticoids, with a mean daily dose of 1.8 mg. Mean disease duration in the RA group was 18.4 years, and mean DAS28-CRP and DAS28-ESR scores were 1.93 and 2.14, respectively. Femoral neck BMD was significantly lower in patients with RA than in controls (0.765 vs. 0.844 g/cm2; p < 0.001). Prevalence and anatomical distribution of vertebral fractures

AI-assisted quantitative analysis demonstrated a significantly higher prevalence of thoracic and lumbar vertebral fractures in patients with RA than in controls (32.3% vs. 12.0%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 1a). At the mid-thoracic region (T4–T10), fracture prevalence was markedly elevated in the RA group (21.5% vs. 2.8%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 1b). At the thoracolumbar junction (T11–L2), which is more susceptible to osteoporotic compression fractures [25], the prevalence was numerically higher in the RA group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (16.1% vs. 7.4%; p = 0.053) (Fig. 1c). At the lower lumbar spine (L3–L5), prevalence was 7.5% in the RA group and 2.8% among controls (p = 0.192) (Fig. 1d). Because knee OA controls were non-RA musculoskeletal clinical controls rather than healthy controls, additional multivariable analyses adjusted for available fracture-related confounders. After adjustment for age, sex, femoral neck BMD, and osteoporosis treatment, RA status was significantly associated with overall vertebral fracture occurrence (adjusted odds ratio (OR) 4.867, 95% confidence interval (CI) 2.165–11.621; p < 0.001) and mid-thoracic vertebral fracture occurrence (adjusted OR 15.562, 95% CI 4.495–75.484; p < 0.001). The number of fractures per patient did not differ significantly between patients with RA and controls (p = 0.184) (Fig. S1a). In SQ grading, using an ordinal logistic regression model with the maximum vertebral fracture grade as the outcome, patients with RA had a significantly higher fracture severity compared with controls (OR 2.985, 95% CI 1.083–8.735; p = 0.038) (Fig. S1b).

Fracture distribution visually differed between patients with RA and controls: patients with RA showed bimodal peaks at T6 and L1, with involvement across T4–L5, whereas controls showed a single L1 peak, with most fractures below T7. However, GEE showed no significant between-group difference in segmental fracture distribution (p = 0.557) (Fig. S1c).

Clinical characteristics and risk factors for vertebral fractures in RA

In the RA group, the demographic and clinical features of patients with vertebral fractures (n = 30) and those without vertebral fractures (n = 63) are summarized in Table S2. Age and cumulative glucocorticoid dose differed significantly between groups (p < 0.05). Serum pentosidine levels, denosumab use, and b/tsDMARD use showed numerical differences between groups, although these did not reach statistical significance. Femoral neck BMD did not differ significantly (0.742 vs. 0.775 g/cm2; p = 0.350).

Multivariable analysis was then conducted using variables that showed significant differences (p < 0.05) or p values < 0.10 in the univariate comparisons, including age, denosumab use, serum pentosidine levels, non-use of b/tsDMARDs, and cumulative glucocorticoid dose (Table S3). Older age, non-use of b/tsDMARDs, and higher cumulative glucocorticoid exposure were independently associated with vertebral fractures, whereas denosumab use and serum pentosidine levels were not significantly associated with vertebral fractures. To account for the potential influence of glycemic status on bone quality, HbA1c was additionally examined. HbA1c did not differ significantly based on overall, mid-thoracic, or thoracolumbar junction fracture status and was not significantly correlated with serum pentosidine levels. In a sensitivity model including age, serum pentosidine levels, and HbA1c, age and serum pentosidine levels remained significantly associated with mid-thoracic fractures, whereas HbA1c was not (Table S4).

Discussion

This study suggests that vertebral fractures in RA have region-specific risk profiles, with bone quality-related factors predominating in the mid-thoracic spine and disease- and treatment-related factors predominating at the thoracolumbar junction. Fractures occurred most frequently in the mid-thoracic region (T4–T10), followed by the thoracolumbar junction (T11–L2), whereas fractures in the lower lumbar spine (L3–L5) were uncommon. The prevalence in our study (32.3%) was higher than that reported in Japanese RA populations (19.7–27.3%) [26] and slightly lower than that in an all-female European cohort (36%) [15], possibly reflecting differences in cohort composition. RA is characterized by systemic inflammation and altered bone metabolism, contributing to generalized bone loss and deterioration of skeletal microarchitecture [9, 27]. Consistent with this, femoral neck BMD was significantly lower in patients with RA, similar to earlier reports demonstrating widespread bone loss in RA [27].

To explore potential mechanisms underlying this regional vulnerability, we further examined the relative contributions of bone mass and bone quality. Within the RA cohort, femoral neck BMD did not differ significantly between patients with and without fractures, nor between those with mid-thoracic fractures and the non-fracture group. In contrast, patients with thoracolumbar junction fractures had significantly lower femoral neck BMD than those without vertebral fractures in univariable comparisons, although femoral neck BMD was not independently associated with thoracolumbar junction fractures in the multivariable model. Taken together, these findings suggest that reduced BMD alone does not fully account for vertebral fragility in RA. This may be particularly relevant to mid-thoracic fractures, for which femoral neck BMD was not reduced, whereas higher serum pentosidine levels remained independently associated with fracture occurrence, suggesting a greater contribution of bone quality and regional structural vulnerability. In line with this interpretation, serum pentosidine levels, reflecting accumulation of AGEs and impaired collagen cross-linking, were numerically higher in patients with RA who had mid-thoracic fractures and were independently associated with mid-thoracic fracture occurrence. This aligns with evidence linking pentosidine to compromised bone material properties and increased vertebral fragility independent of BMD [5, 28]. Such deterioration of bone quality may contribute to the higher frequency of mid-thoracic vertebral fractures observed in this study.

In addition to bone quality-related factors, disease- and treatment-related factors may also contribute to vertebral fragility in RA. The association between non-use of b/tsDMARDs and vertebral fractures should be interpreted with caution. Although biologic therapies may have favorable effects on bone metabolism and bone loss in RA, direct evidence that b/tsDMARDs reduce osteoporotic fracture risk remains limited [29]. Recent studies have reported lower odds of prevalent vertebral fractures with b/tsDMARD use and faster bone union after biologic therapy in RA [30, 31]. Possible mechanisms include suppression of inflammatory bone resorption, preservation of bone mass, and facilitation of glucocorticoid tapering. Nevertheless, non-use of b/tsDMARDs may reflect greater cumulative inflammatory burden, undertreated disease, or prolonged glucocorticoid exposure rather than a direct fracture-preventive effect.

The concentration of fractures in the mid-thoracic region may appear counterintuitive because, under normal physiological conditions, the rib cage substantially stabilizes the thoracic spine, limits flexion–extension, and reduces excessive compressive loading on mid-thoracic vertebrae [32, 33]. Although mid-thoracic fractures are generally less common than thoracolumbar junction fractures, the mid-thoracic vertebrae possess smaller vertebral body size and a thinner cortical shell, predisposing them to non-traumatic fractures [23]. In patients with RA, this protective mechanism may be impaired. Chronic inflammation and progressive sagittal malalignment, including thoracic kyphosis, alter spinal loading patterns and increase mechanical stress on the thoracic spine [34, 35]. Thus, the increased frequency of mid-thoracic fractures in RA may not be explained by impaired bone quality alone; in our cohort, thoracic kyphosis was already greater in patients with RA without vertebral fractures than in controls without vertebral fractures, suggesting a possible contribution of altered thoracic alignment to mid-thoracic fracture vulnerability (Fig. S2).

Within the RA cohort, determinants of vertebral fractures varied by fracture location. Mid-thoracic fractures were independently associated with older age and higher serum pentosidine levels. Denosumab use and MTX use were not significantly associated with mid-thoracic fractures. The non-significant association with denosumab may at least partly reflect confounding by indication and should be interpreted as a marker of underlying fracture risk, not as evidence that denosumab increases fracture risk. Patients receiving denosumab may have had more severe osteoporosis, higher baseline fracture risk, or prior fracture history. Serum pentosidine levels remained an independent determinant even without reduced BMD, supporting a substantial contribution of bone quality to mid-thoracic fragility. Although MTX use was not independently associated with mid-thoracic fracture risk, effective suppression of systemic inflammation may be relevant to preserving bone mass and bone quality [36]. Collectively, these findings emphasize that deterioration of bone material properties, rather than BMD alone, may play an important role in mid-thoracic vertebral fragility.

In contrast, thoracolumbar junction fractures were independently associated with older age, higher DAS28-CRP, greater cumulative glucocorticoid exposure, and lack of b/tsDMARD use. Although femoral neck BMD was significantly lower in patients with thoracolumbar junction fractures than in those without vertebral fractures in univariable comparisons, it was not an independent factor. These results suggest that, at this mechanically vulnerable transition zone, RA-related inflammatory activity and treatment-related factors may contribute to fracture risk in addition to bone mass, aligning with biomechanical evidence showing concentrated anterior loading at the thoracolumbar junction [37]. This region-specific pattern highlights the need for preventive strategies tailored to anatomical characteristics and patient-specific clinical factors. The observed associations with DAS28-CRP, cumulative glucocorticoid exposure, and b/tsDMARD use further suggest that inflammatory control and treatment strategies may influence region-specific fracture risk in RA [31].

An AI-assisted quantitative morphometry system was used to improve the efficiency of vertebral height assessment, and all final fracture classifications were confirmed by clinicians (Fig. S3). By reducing the burden of manual measurements, this approach may support standardized screening of mid-thoracic vertebral fractures in routine RA care, particularly for non-orthopaedic clinicians.

In the present study, cumulative glucocorticoid exposure and bone quality-related factors, including serum pentosidine levels, were associated with vertebral fracture risk in patients with RA. These findings may support the potential value of incorporating additional clinical modifiers into fracture risk assessment in RA. Unlike conventional FRAX, FRAXplus incorporates additional modifiers, such as glucocorticoid dose and type 2 diabetes-related variables [38]. Such an approach may be useful in patients with RA, in whom the clinical characteristics highlighted in the present study, including cumulative glucocorticoid exposure, glycemic status, and bone quality-related factors, may influence vertebral fracture risk.

However, this study has some limitations. First, this was a single-center study with small fracture subgroups, limiting statistical power and generalizability. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Third, disease activity was assessed using DAS28 (ESR/CRP) because physician global assessment was not consistently available, precluding calculation of the Simplified Disease Activity Index or Clinical Disease Activity Index. Fourth, the control group consisted of patients with knee OA treated in an orthopaedic department rather than healthy or population-based controls. Although this group served as non-RA musculoskeletal clinical controls undergoing the same radiographic assessment, knee OA may be associated with pain-related reductions in activity, altered mobility, body composition, fall risk, and degenerative spinal changes. Therefore, comparisons between patients with RA and controls should be interpreted as comparisons with an orthopaedic OA cohort, and residual confounding, including treatment-related differences such as glucocorticoid exposure, may remain despite adjustment for age, sex, femoral neck BMD, and osteoporosis treatment. Fifth, lumbar spine BMD was not analyzed because DXA-derived lumbar BMD can be falsely elevated by degenerative changes, prevalent vertebral fractures, aortic calcification, metallic implants, and vertebral endplate abnormalities such as Schmorl’s nodes [39, 40]. Femoral neck BMD was therefore used as the main BMD parameter to reduce spinal artifacts; however, femoral neck BMD does not directly reflect vertebral bone strength, local trabecular microarchitecture, bone quality, or region-specific mechanical vulnerability. Finally, frailty status, fall history, sarcopenia-related parameters, muscle strength, physical activity, and daily spinal loading were not systematically available. These factors may be particularly relevant in older patients and may influence vertebral fracture risk through reduced protective muscle function, altered posture and loading, impaired mobility, and increased susceptibility to falls. BMI, menopausal status, prior fracture history, detailed diabetes mellitus status, and detailed inflammatory markers were also unavailable, leaving potential residual confounding. Although HbA1c was not associated with fracture occurrence or serum pentosidine levels, diabetes diagnosis, duration, treatment, complications, and glycation-related bone quality changes beyond HbA1c were not assessed. Given the reported association between reduced psoas muscle mass and osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures [41], future prospective studies should incorporate comprehensive assessments of body composition, muscle mass, frailty, fall history, metabolic factors, and longitudinal fracture outcomes.

Conclusions

Vertebral fractures in RA showed region-specific risk profiles based on spinal level. Mid-thoracic fractures were more strongly associated with impaired bone quality, as reflected by elevated serum pentosidine levels, whereas thoracolumbar junction fractures were more closely related to disease activity, cumulative glucocorticoid exposure, and non-use of b/tsDMARDs. These findings support the importance of region-specific fracture assessment in RA and suggest that fracture prevention strategies may need to be tailored based on spinal region.
