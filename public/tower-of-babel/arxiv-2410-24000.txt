Abstract
. The well-posedness of a class of optimal control problems is analysed, where the state equation couples a nonlinear degenerate Fokker-Planck equation with a system of Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs). Such problems naturally arise as mean-field limits of Stochastic Differential models for multipopulation dynamics, where a large number of agents (followers) is steered through parsimonious intervention on a selected class of leaders. The proposed approach combines stability estimates for measure solutions of nonlinear degenerate Fokker-Planck equations with a general framework of assumptions on the cost functional, ensuring compactness and lower semicontinuity properties. The Lie structure of the state equations allows one for considering non-Lipschitz nonlinearities, provided some suitable dissipativity assumptions are considered in addition to non-Euclidean Hölder and sublinearity conditions.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Presentation of the problem

The aim of this work is to study a class of optimal control problems driven by a dynamic of the type

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-v\cdot\nabla_{x}\mu_{t}+\sigma\Delta_{v}\mu_{t}-\mathrm{div}_{v}(\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}](z)\mu_{t})\quad&(t,x,v)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2d},\\
\mu_{0}=\bar{\mu}\quad&(x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\sigma\in{{\mathbb{R}}}
and the drift field
\mathfrak{v}
is allowed to depend explicitly on a solution
\bm{\mu}
of the problem. Hence,
the above is a nonlinear Fokker-Planck equation driven by a nonlocal drift field
\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}]
depending on the state of the system,
a typical example being the choice

\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}](z)=H(t,z)\ast\mu_{t}\,.

(1.2)

Furthermore, (
1.1
) is the natural mean field counterpart of second order multi-agents systems with additive noise, see (
1.4
) below.
In fact, in this case a drift field of the form
\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}]
naturally arises if one assumes that the natural dynamic is driven by mutual interactions
(see Appendix
D
).
These type of mean field systems are in connection with a great number of applications. Indeed, multiagent systems have been used in several contexts, such as in biology to model cell aggregation
[

,

,

]

[
30

]
, or even to describe human interaction in social sciences
[

,

,

,

]
. Furthermore, controlled leader-follower multiagent systems have been used, for instance, in the context of gene regulation in microbial consortia
[

,

]
, traffic control
[
41
]
, swarms control
[

]
, or even oil cleaning through controlled robots
[

]
. The controllability of this kind of leader-follower systems, with a fixed finite (sufficiently small) number of followers, has been widely studied, see, for instance,
[
28
,
29
,

]
. On the other hand, the mean-field approach for a large number of followers has been considered for instance in

]

Before describing our point of view in detail, it is useful to give a closer look to the notion of solution we need to consider when dealing with
(
1.1
). Indeed, the well-posedness of the class of optimal control problems we consider heavily relies on stability estimates for
solutions in suitable spaces. Thus, this structure immediately suggests that it is natural to look for
solutions to (
1.1
) in Wasserstein spaces (see Section
2
), and hence the definition of solution to (
1.1
) we are interested into is the following.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
p\geq 1
,
T>0
(possibly
T=\infty
) and
\overline{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
. A continuous curve of probability measures
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
is a solution of (
1.1
) if and only if for all
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
and for all
t\in[0,T]
it holds

\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\psi\,d\mu_{t}-\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\psi\,d\overline{\mu}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}\psi+\mathfrak{v}[s,\bm{\mu}](z)\cdot\nabla_{v}\psi+\sigma\Delta_{v}\psi\right)\,d\mu_{s}\,ds.

(1.3)

If
T=\infty
, we say that
\bm{\mu}
is a global solution of (
1.1
), otherwise we call it a local solution.

As a first goal of our work, we will focus on uniqueness and stability issues for (
1.1
). In doing so,
we consider a set of assumptions on the drift term
\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}]
which is as general as possible
and allows for possibly unbounded non-globally Lipschitz drift fields provided some Hölder continuity combined with
a dissipativity condition are satisfied (see Assumptions
1.2
below). Indeed, we refer to
\mathfrak{v}_{3}
as a dissipativity condition since for
p=2
it is implied by the metrical dissipativity condition considered in
[
17
]
, see also
[
16
]
, as shown in Appendix
A
.

Despite the presence of a nonlinearity in the structure of (
1.1
), the results we are going to show strongly rely on well-posedness results for a linear counterpart, that has been analyzed in
[
5
]
.
Furthermore a key point for uniqueness and stability issues consists in showing that (
1.1
) is indeed equivalent to its McKean-Vlasov counterpart,
in the sense that a solution
\mu
is always the law of a stochastic process
of the type

\begin{cases}dX(t)=V(t)dt\\
dV(t)=\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}](X(t),V(t))dt+\sqrt{2\sigma}dB(t)\\
X(0)=X_{0}\qquad V(0)=V_{0}\qquad\bm{\mu}={\rm Law}(X,V),\end{cases}

(1.4)

where
B
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\Sigma,{\sf P})
,
\mathfrak{v}:[0,T]\times C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
is a measurable function,
\sigma>0
and
Z_{0}=(X_{0},V_{0})\in L^{p}(\Omega;{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
.
For this reason, throughout this work we fix a probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,{\sf P})
and we consider the following adapted formalism for McKean-Vlasov SDEs to the case of our interest. For simplicity, by
Z\in L^{p}(\Omega;C({{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
we actually mean that
\{Z(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}\in L^{p}(\Omega;C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
for all
T>0
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
p\geq 1
,
T>0
(possibly
T=\infty
) and consider the McKean-Vlasov SDE (
1.4
), for
t\in[0,T]
.
We say that a stochastic process

is a strong soution of (
1.4

,
\int_{0}^{t}\left|\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}](Z(s))\right|ds<\infty

X(t)=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}V(s)ds\ \mbox{ and }\ V(t)=V_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathfrak{v}[s,\bm{\mu}](Z(s))ds+\sqrt{2\sigma}B(t),

(1.5)

for a.a.
t\in[0,T]
a.s., where
\bm{\mu}={\rm Law}(Z)
. We say that uniqueness in law holds for (
1.4
) if for any two strong solutions
Z_{1},Z_{2}
we have
{\rm Law}(Z_{1}(t))={\rm Law}(Z_{2}(t))
for all
t\in[0,T]
. We say that pathwise uniqueness holds for (

we have

1.2.
Our assumptions

Specifically, in order to state our main results we need to introduce a suitable set of assumptions to consider.
For any
p\geq 1
,
t\geq 0
and
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
we set

\overline{M}_{p}(t,\bm{\mu}):=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}M_{p}(\mu_{s}):=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}}|z|^{p}d\mu_{s},

then the assumptions we will consider throughout this work are the following ones.

(\mathfrak{v}_{0})

\mathfrak{v}:[0,+\infty)\times C({{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+};\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\to\overline{{{\mathbb{R}}}}^{d}
is a Carathéodory map, i.e. it is continuous in the variable
(\bm{\mu},z)\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
and measurable in the variable
t\in[0,T]
.

(\mathfrak{v}_{1})

For all
T>0
there exist two constants
K>0
and
\beta\in[0,1)
such that

|\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}](z)|\leq K\left(1+|x|^{\frac{\beta}{3}}+|v|^{\beta}+\left(\overline{M}_{p}(T,\bm{\mu})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\right)

for every
z\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
,
t\in[0,T]
and for every
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
.

(\mathfrak{v}_{2})

For all
T>0
there exists an exponent
\alpha\in(\beta,1]
with the property that for all
R>0

there exists a constant
L>0
and such that
for all
t\in[0,T]
, for all
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
with
\overline{M}_{p}(T,\bm{\mu})\leq R
and for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
with
|z_{1}|,|z_{2}|\leq R
it holds

\displaystyle\left|\mathfrak{v}[t,\mu](z_{1})-\mathfrak{v}[t,\mu](z_{2})\right|\leq L\left(|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\frac{\alpha}{3}}+|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{\alpha}\right).

(\mathfrak{v}_{3})

For all
T>0
there exists a constant
D\geq 0
such that for any couple of stochastic processes
Z_{j}=(X_{j},V_{j})\in L^{p}(\Omega;C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
with
X_{j}^{\prime}=V_{j}
in
[0,T]
,
Z_{1}(0)=Z_{2}(0)
and
\bm{\mu}^{j}={\rm Law}(X_{j},V_{j})
,
j=1,2
, it holds

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{t}{\sf E}

\displaystyle\left[|V_{1}(s)-V_{2}(s)|^{p-2}(\mathfrak{v}[s,\bm{\mu}^{1}](Z_{1}(s))-\mathfrak{v}[s,\bm{\mu}^{2}](Z_{2}(s)))\cdot(V_{1}(s)-V_{2}(s))\right]\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad\leq D\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{0\leq z\leq s}{\sf E}[|Z_{1}(z)-Z_{2}(z)|^{p}]\,ds\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

(\mathfrak{v}_{4})

For all
T>0
,
t\in[0,T]
and
{\bm{\mu}}^{1},{\bm{\mu}}^{2}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
with
\mu^{1}_{s}=\mu^{2}_{s}
for all
s\in[0,t]
it holds
\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}^{1}]=\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}^{2}]
.

Despite its generality, it is clear that the dissipativity condition
(\mathfrak{v}_{3})
is not easily verified. However, we can still provide some
sufficient conditions for its validity, see Appendix

A
. In the notation for
\mathfrak{v}
, we separate the variables
t
and
\bm{\mu}
and
(X(t),V(t))
for the following reason: throughout the proof of the main theorem, we will freeze the variable
\bm{\mu}
and study (
1.4
) as a simple SDE, and we only need measurability in

.

Eventually, we will turn our attention to optimal control problems connected with nonlinear Fokker-Planck equations.
In the situation we consider, a two population system is given. A discrete leader population

1.4.
Structure of our work

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the notation and we give some preliminaries on the involved spaces of functions and measures. In particular, in Section
2.5
we recall the statement of the well-posedness result in the linear case, as shown in

5
]

Section

. The proof of Theorem

Corollary

represent a stability result in terms of suitable perturbations of the velocity field

. Notice that, since

In Section

Throughout the paper,

2.1.
Wasserstein spaces

Let
(\mathfrak{B},d_{\mathfrak{B}})
be a complete separable metric space and
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{B})
be the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathfrak{B}
. We denote by
\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B})
the space of (Borel) probability measures on
\mathfrak{B}
. For any
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B})
, let
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
be the set of (measure) couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
(see
[
46
, Definition 1.1]
), i.e. Borel probability measures
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B}\times\mathfrak{B})
such that for any
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{B})
we have

\gamma(A\times\mathfrak{B})=\mu(A),\qquad\gamma(\mathfrak{B}\times B)=\nu(B).

For any
p\geq 1
and
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}
we denote by

M_{p}(\mu;x_{0})=\int_{\mathfrak{B}}(d_{\mathfrak{B}}(x,x_{0}))^{p}d\mu(x)

the
p
-th moment (with respect to
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}
) of
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B})
. It is clear that if
M_{p}(\mu;x_{0})<\infty
for some
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}
, then it is finite for all
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}
. The
p
-th Wasserstein space over
\mathfrak{B}
is defined as (see
[
46
, Definition 6.4]
)

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}):=\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B}):\ M_{p}(\mu;x_{0})<\infty,\forall x_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}\},

equipped with the distance (see
[
46
, Definition 6.1]
)

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\left(\int_{\mathfrak{B}\times\mathfrak{B}}(d_{\mathfrak{B}}(x,y))^{p}\,d\gamma(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

It is well-known (see
[
46
, Theorem 6.18]
) that
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B})
is a complete separable metric space. If
\mathfrak{B}
is a Banach space, then we will denote
M_{p}(\mu):=M_{p}(\mu;0)
.

2.2.
Spaces of continuous functions and related Wasserstein spaces

For any
T>0
, we denote by
C([0,T];\mathfrak{B})
the space of continuous functions
f:[0,T]\to\mathfrak{B}
equipped with the uniform distance, i.e.

d_{\infty}(f,g)=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}d_{\mathfrak{B}}(f(t),g(t)).

Clearly,
C([0,T];\mathfrak{B})
is a complete separable metric space. Furthermore, we denote by
C({{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+};\mathfrak{B})
, where
{{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+}:=[0,+\infty)
, the space of continuous functions
f:{{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+}\to\mathfrak{B}
. In general, for reader’s convenience, we will directly refer to
C({{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+};\mathfrak{B})
as
C([0,T];\mathfrak{B})
with
T=\infty
.

Furthermore, for any
t\in[0,T]
, we denote by
{\rm ev}_{t}
the
evaluation map
defined on
f\in C([0,T];\mathfrak{B})
as
{\rm ev}_{t}(f)=f(t)
. For any given measure
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(C([0,T];\mathfrak{B}))
and any
t\in[0,T]
, we denote

\mu_{t}={\rm ev}_{t}\sharp\bm{\mu},

where
\sharp
denotes the pushforward.

In what follows, we will use the notation
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
to denote continuous curves of probability measures, with
\bm{\mu}=\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
. In particular, if
\mathfrak{B}
is a Banach space and
T<\infty
, also
C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
is a Banach space under the norm

\left(\overline{M}_{p}(T,\bm{\mu})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}:=\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\left(M_{p}(\mu_{s})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

We write
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(C([0,T];\mathfrak{B}))
to state that there exists a Borel probability measure (that we still denote
\bm{\mu}
) on
C([0,T];\mathfrak{B})
such that
\mu_{t}={\rm ev}_{t}\sharp\bm{\mu}
. With this identification, we can say that
\mathcal{W}_{p}(C([0,T];\mathfrak{B}))\subset C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
, since, clearly,

\overline{M}_{p}(T,\bm{\mu})\leq M_{p}(\bm{\mu}),

where the second
p
-th order moment is taken in
\mathcal{W}_{p}(C([0,T];\mathfrak{B}))
.

Notice that for any
T_{1}>T_{2}
and any
\bm{\mu}=\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T_{1}]}\in C([0,T_{1}];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
we still denote
\bm{\mu}=\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T_{2}]}
(i.e. truncating the flow at
T_{2}
), so that with such an identification we have
C([0,T_{1}];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))\subset C([0,T_{2}];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
.

For any
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
T>0
(with
T\not=\infty
), we can consider the set
C^{\gamma}([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
of
\gamma
-Hölder continuous curves, i.e.
\bm{\mu}\in C^{\gamma}([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
if and only if

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq t,s\leq T\\
t\not=s\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu_{t},\mu_{s})}{|t-s|^{\gamma}}<\infty.

Thanks to the previous identification, we can also define the set
C^{\gamma}_{\rm loc}({{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+};\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
of locally
\gamma
-Hölder continuous curves, that is to say that
\bm{\mu}\in C^{\gamma}_{\rm loc}({{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+};\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
if and only if
\bm{\mu}\in C^{\gamma}([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B}))
for all
T>0
.

2.3.
Random variables and couplings

Throughout the paper, we will fix a probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,{\sf P})
on which all the random quantities will be defined. In general, we call any measurable function
X:\Omega\to\mathfrak{B}
a
\mathfrak{B}
-valued random variable and we denote the set of all
\mathfrak{B}
-valued random variables as
\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B})
.

The law of a random variable
X\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B})
is defined as
{\rm Law}(X)=X\sharp{\sf P}
, i.e. for any
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{B})

{\rm Law}(X)(A)={\sf P}(X^{-1}(A)).

We denote by
L^{p}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B})
the subspace of
\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B})
of random variables
X

with the property that there exists
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}
such that

{\sf E}[(d_{\mathfrak{B}}(X,x_{0}))^{p}]<\infty,

(2.1)

where
{\sf E}
is the expected value. It is clear that if (
2.1
) holds for a specific choice of
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}
, then it holds for any
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}
.
We define a coupling of two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B})
as any random variable
(X,Y)\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B}\times\mathfrak{B})
such that
{\rm Law}(X)=\mu
and
{\rm Law}(Y)=\nu
(see
[
46
, Definition 1.1]
). It is clear that for any
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B})
and any measure coupling
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, there exists a coupling
(X,Y)
such that
{\rm Law}(X,Y)=\gamma
. Also note that
\mu\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B})
if and only if for any
X\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B})
with
{\rm Law}(X)=\mu
it holds
X\in L^{p}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B})
. In particular, this means that we can rewrite the Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
as

\mathcal{W}^{p}_{p}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(X,Y)\in L^{p}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B}\times\mathfrak{B})\\
{\rm Law}(X)=\mu,\ {\rm Law}(Y)=\nu\end{subarray}}{\sf E}[(d_{\mathfrak{B}}(X,Y))^{p}].

For
T>0
(possibly
T=\infty)
and
X\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;C([0,T];\mathfrak{B}))
, we can define for any
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle X(t):

\displaystyle\,\Omega\mapsto\mathfrak{B}

\displaystyle\,\omega\mapsto X(t)(\omega)={\rm ev}_{t}(X(\omega)).

Hence,
X(t)\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;\mathfrak{B})
for any
t\in[0,T]
, and if
{\rm Law}(X)=\mu
, then
{\rm Law}(X(t))=\mu_{t}
. In particular, we can identify
X
as a
\mathfrak{B}
-valued stochastic process with continuous trajectories.

2.4.
Young functions

Let us recall the definition of Young function: we say that
\Phi
is a Young function if for any
x\geq 0

\Phi(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\varphi(y)dy,

where

(i)

\varphi(0)=0
and
\varphi(y)>0
for any
y>0
;

(ii)

\varphi
is non-decreasing;

(iii)

\varphi
is right-continuous;

(iv)

\lim\limits_{y\to+\infty}\varphi(y)=+\infty
.

We say that a Young function
\Phi
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
condition if there exists a constant
C_{\Phi}>1
such that for any
x\geq 0

\Phi(2x)\leq C_{\Phi}\Phi(x).

It is known (see
[
42
, Remark 4.4.6]
) that any Young function
\Phi
satisfying the
\Delta_{2}
condition is such that there exist two constants
C_{0}>0
and
p>1
such that

\Phi(x)\leq C_{0}x^{p},\ \forall x\geq 1.

The converse is not always true. By definition, Young functions are non-decreasing, convex, and satisfy

\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\Phi(x)}{x}=+\infty

(2.2)

For further details on Young functions, we refer to
[
42
, Chapter 4]
.

For our purposes, it will be useful to consider moments with respect to Young functions. Indeed, let
\Phi:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
be a Young function
and
(\mathfrak{B},d_{\mathfrak{B}})
be a complete separable metric space. Then for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{B})
, we define

M_{\Phi}(\mu;x_{0})=\int_{\mathfrak{B}}\Phi(d_{\mathfrak{B}}(x,x_{0}))\,d\mu(x)

and we say that
\mu\in\mathcal{W}_{\Phi}(\mathfrak{B})
if and only if there exists
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}
such that
M_{\Phi}(\mu;x_{0})<\infty
(or, equivalently,
M_{\Phi}(\mu;x_{0})<\infty
for any
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{B}
). If
\mathfrak{B}
is a Banach space, then we set
M_{\Phi}(\mu):=M_{\Phi}(\mu;0)
. Observe that, for instance, if we set
\Phi_{p}(x)=x^{p}
for
p>1
, then
\mathcal{W}_{\Phi}(\mathfrak{B})=\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B})
. Similarly to what we did for the moments of order
p
, we set

\overline{M}_{\Phi}(t,\bm{\mu})=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}M_{\Phi}(\mu_{s})

for any
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{\Phi}(\mathfrak{B}))
, where
\mathfrak{B}
is a Banach space.

An important result that will be frequently employed is the de la Vallée-Poussin Theorem (see
[
36
, Theorem T22]
).
More precisely, it states that a sequence
X_{n}
of random variables is uniformly integrable if and only if
there exists a Young function
\Phi:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
such that
\Phi(x)\leq x^{2}
and

\sup\limits_{n}{\sf E}\left[\Phi(|X_{n}|)\right]<\infty.

(2.3)

We use this result in combination with the Dunford-Pettis Theorem (see
[
13
, Theorem 4.30]
).
Indeed, if for some
p\geq 1
we have
X\in L^{p}(\Omega)
, then the set
\{|X|^{p}\}
is clearly a weakly compact set in
L^{1}
. Hence, it must be uniformly integrable and thus
by the de la Vallée-Poussin Theorem we know that there exists a Young function such that
{\sf E}[\Phi(|X|^{P})]<\infty
. This proves the following result.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathfrak{B})
. Then there exists a Young function
\Phi
with the property that
\Phi(x)\leq x^{2}
and defining
\Phi_{p}(x)=\Phi(x^{p})
we have

\mu\in\mathcal{W}_{\Phi_{p}}(\mathfrak{B})
.

2.5.
The linear equation

Given a function
F:[0,+\infty)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
we will consider the following set of assumptions:

(A_{0})

F:[0,+\infty)\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
is a Carathéodory map, i.e. it is measurable in the first variable and continuous in the second one;

(A_{1})

For any
T>0
, there exist two constants
C>0
and
\beta\in(0,1)
such that

F(t,x,v)\leq C(1+|x|^{\frac{\beta}{3}}+|v|^{\beta}),

for all
t\in[0,T]
and
(x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
;

(A_{2})

For any
T>0
and any compact set
K\subset{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
there exist two constants
L>0
and
\alpha\in(\beta,1]
such that

|F(t,x_{1},v_{1})-F(t,x_{2},v_{2})|\leq L(|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\frac{\alpha}{3}}+|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{\alpha})

for all
t\in[0,T]
and
(x_{1},v_{1}),(x_{2},v_{2})\in K
.

We also define

[F]_{\beta,T}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[0,T]\\
(x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}\end{subarray}}\frac{F(t,x,v)}{1+|x|^{\frac{\beta}{3}}+|v|^{\beta}}.

We recall the following existence and uniqueness result for an associated linear system
proved in
[
5
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Under Assumptions
(A_{0})
,
(A_{1})
and
(A_{2})
, for any
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
and
T>0
, the equation

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-v\cdot\nabla_{x}\mu_{t}+\sigma\Delta_{v}\mu_{t}-\mathrm{div}_{v}(F(t,z)\mu_{t})\quad&(t,x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{+}_{0}\times\mathbb{R}^{2d},\\
\mu_{0}=\overline{\mu}\quad&(x,v)\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d},\end{cases}

(2.4)

admits exactly one global solution
{\bm{\mu}}:=\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\in{{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+}}\in C({{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+};\mathcal{W}_{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
, which can be characterized as follows. Let
B
be a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion and
(X_{0},V_{0})\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega;{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
be independent of
B
and such that
{\rm Law}(X_{0},V_{0})=\overline{\mu}
, then there exists a stochastic process
(X,V)\in L^{1}(\Omega;C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
for all
T>0
such that
{\bm{\mu}}=Law(X,V)
and
(X,V)
is the pathwise unique strong solution to

\begin{cases}dX(t)=V(t)\,dt&t\in{{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+}\\
dV(t)=F(t,X(t),V(t))\,dt+\sqrt{2\sigma}\,dB(t)&t\in{{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+}\\
X(0)=X_{0},\quad V(0)=V_{0}.\end{cases}

(2.5)

Actually, we can show that the solution
{\bm{\mu}}
is slightly more regular, depending on the regularity of the initial data
\overline{\mu}
. Before doing this, we state an inequality that we will use often throughout the paper, whose interest is not only related to the proof of the next proposition. Precisely, for any
T>0
and
p>1
it holds

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|B(t)|^{p}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{2p\sqrt{T}}{p-1}\right)^{p}\Gamma\left(\frac{p+1}{2}\right),

(2.6)

that follows by Doob’s maximal inequality (see
[
43
, Theorem II.1.7]
) and the formula for the absolute
p
-moments of a Gaussian random variable […..].

We have the following result.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let Assumptions
(A_{0})
,
(A_{1})
and
(A_{2})
hold true. Let
\Phi:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
be either the identity or a Young function satisfying
\Phi(x)\leq x^{2}
and define
\Phi_{p}(x)=\Phi(x^{p})
for any
x\geq 0
and
p\geq 1
. Let
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}))
be the unique solution of (
2.4
). If
\overline{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{\Phi_{p}}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
for some
p\geq 1
, then
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{\Phi_{p}}(C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
and there exist two constants
\overline{C}_{1},\overline{C}_{2}
depending only on
p,\beta,T
and
\sigma
such that

\overline{M}_{\widetilde{\Phi}_{p}}(T,\bm{\mu})\leq M_{\widetilde{\Phi}_{p}}(\bm{\mu})\leq\overline{C}_{2}\left(1+M_{\Phi_{p}}(\overline{\mu})+[F]_{\beta,T}^{2p}\right),

(2.7)

where

\widetilde{\Phi}(r)=\Phi\left(\frac{e^{-\overline{C}_{1}[F]_{\beta,T}^{p}T}}{3\overline{C}_{1}}r\right).

(2.8)

If, furthermore,
\Phi
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
condition, then there exists a constant
\overline{C}_{3}
depending only on
\Phi,p,\beta,T
and
\sigma
such that

\overline{M}_{\Phi_{p}}(T,\bm{\mu})\leq\overline{C}_{3}\left(1+M_{\Phi_{p}}(\overline{\mu})+[F]_{\beta,T}^{2p}\right)e^{\overline{C}_{3}[F]_{\beta,T}^{p}}.

(2.9)

Finally, if we consider the solution
Z=(X,V)
of (
2.5
), it also holds, almost surely,

\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}|Z(s)|\leq C_{4}\left(|Z_{0}|+[F]_{\beta,T}+\sigma\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}|B(s)|\right)e^{C_{4}([F]_{\beta,T}+1)T}.

(2.10)

Proof.

Let us first show that if
\Phi
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
condition, then (
2.7
) implies (
2.9
). Indeed, in such a case, since
\Phi(2x)\leq C_{\Phi}\Phi(x)
for a constant
C_{\Phi}>1
, we have

\Phi(x^{p})\leq C_{\Phi}^{|\log_{2}(2\overline{C}_{1})|}e^{\overline{C}_{1}\log(C_{\Phi})[F]_{\beta,T}^{p}T}\widetilde{\Phi}(x^{p}).

This is enough to prove the desired implication.

Now we prove (
2.7
). Let
Z=(X,V)
be any strong solution of (
2.5
) and recall that
\bm{\mu}={\rm Law}(Z)
. By Assumption
(A_{1})
and the second equation in (

) we know that there exists a constant
C_{1}
depending on

and

such that

|V(s)|^{p}\leq C_{1}\left(|V_{0}|^{p}+[F]_{\beta,T}^{p}+[F]_{\beta,T}^{p}\int_{0}^{t}\left(|X(\tau)|^{\frac{p\beta}{3}}+|V(\tau)|^{p\beta}\right)d\tau+\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\sigma^{p}|B(s)|^{p}\right)

By using Young’s inequality on
|X(\tau)|^{\frac{p\beta}{3}}
and
|V(\tau)|^{p\beta}
with exponents respectively
\frac{3}{\beta}
and
\frac{1}{\beta}
and taking the supremum inside the integral, we get

|V(s)|^{p}\leq C_{2}\left(|V_{0}|^{p}+[F]_{\beta,T}^{p}+[F]_{\beta,T}^{p}\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{0\leq z\leq\tau}\left(|X(z)|^{p}+|V(z)|^{p}\right)d\tau+\sigma^{p}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|B(s)|^{p}\right)

(2.11)

for some constant

depending only on
p,T
and
\beta
. Moreover, we also have, by the first eqution in (

),

\displaystyle|X(s)|^{p}\leq C_{3}\left(|X_{0}|^{p}+\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{0\leq z\leq\tau}|V(z)|^{p}d\tau\right),

(2.12)

where

is a constant depending only on
p
and

. Summing (

) and (

) and taking the supremum, it is clear that

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|Z(s)|^{p}\leq C_{4}\left(|Z_{0}|^{p}+[F]_{\beta,T}^{p}+([F]_{\beta,T}^{p}+1)\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{0\leq z\leq\tau}|Z(z)|^{p}d\tau+\sigma^{p}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|B(s)|^{p}\right),

(2.13)

where

and we can use Grönwall’s inequality to get

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|Z(s)|^{p}\leq C_{4}\left(|Z_{0}|^{p}+[F]_{\beta,T}^{p}+\sigma^{p}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|B(s)|^{p}\right)e^{C_{4}([F]_{\beta,T}^{p}+1)T}.

(2.14)

For

, (

).

Now we apply the function
\Phi
on both sides of (

), obtaining, by convexity and the bound

, setting

,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|Z(s)|^{p}\right)\leq\frac{1}{3}\Phi\left(|Z_{0}|^{p}\right)+[F]_{\beta,T}^{2p}+\sigma^{2p}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|B(s)|^{2p}.

Finally, we take the expectation on both sides of (

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

We proceed in four steps: first we prove the existence of a solution under a stronger assumption; then we weaken such assumption to get the actual existence result; next we prove uniqueness of the local solution; finally, we prove existence and uniqueness of the global solution.

3.1.1.
Existence with
\mathfrak{v}
uniformly sublinear

We first assume that
\mathfrak{v}
satisfies
(\mathfrak{v}_{0})
,
(\mathfrak{v}_{2})
and
(\mathfrak{v}_{3})
, while we substitute
(\mathfrak{v}_{1})
with the following further assumption:

(\overline{\mathfrak{v}}_{1})

For all
T>0
there exist two constants
K>0
and
\beta\in[0,1)
such that

|\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}](z)|\leq K\left(1+|x|^{\frac{\beta}{3}}+|v|^{\beta}\right)

for every
z\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
,
t\in[0,T]
and for every
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
.

Let
T>0
(with
T\not=+\infty
) and
(X_{0},V_{0})
be any random variable independent of
B
with
{\rm Law}(X_{0},V_{0})=\overline{\mu}
. Fix any curve of probability measures
\bm{\nu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
and consider the following SDE.

\begin{cases}dX(t)=V(t)dt&t\in(0,T]\\
dV(t)=F_{\bm{\nu}}(t,X(t),V(t))dt+\sqrt{2\sigma}dB(t)&t\in(0,T]\\
X(0)=X_{0},\qquad V(0)=V_{0},\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
F_{\bm{\nu}}(t,x,v)=\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\nu}](x,v)
.
Notice that, by Assumptions
(\mathfrak{v}_{0})
,
(\overline{\mathfrak{v}}_{1})
and
(\mathfrak{v}_{2})
, we know that the function
F_{\bm{\nu}}
satisfies Assumptions
(A_{0})
,
(A_{1})
and
(A_{2})
, hence, by Theorem
2.2
, there exists a unique strong solution
(X_{\bm{\nu}},V_{\bm{\nu}})\in L^{1}(\Omega;C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
of (
3.1
). Let
\bm{\widetilde{\nu}}={\rm Law}(X_{\bm{\nu}},V_{\bm{\nu}})
. By Proposition
2.1
, we know that there exists a Young functions
\Phi
such that
\Phi(x)\leq x^{2}
and
\overline{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{\Phi_{p}}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
, where
\Phi_{p}(r)=\Phi(r^{p})

2.3
, in particular (

), we know that

M_{p}(\bm{\widetilde{\nu}})\leq C(1+[F_{\bm{\nu}}]_{\beta,T}^{2p})e^{C[F_{\bm{\nu}}]^{p}_{\beta,T}}\leq C(1+K^{2p})e^{CK^{p}},

(3.2)

where we also used Assumption
(\overline{\mathfrak{v}}_{1})
, and

M_{\widetilde{\Phi}^{\bm{\nu}}_{p}}(\bm{\widetilde{\nu}})\leq C(1+[F_{\bm{\nu}}]_{\beta,T}^{2p})\leq C(1+K^{2p}),

where

\widetilde{\Phi}^{\bm{\nu}}(r)=\Phi\left(Ce^{-C[F_{\bm{\nu}}]_{\beta,T}^{p}}r\right).

It is clear, since
\Phi
is increasing, that for all

\overline{\Phi}(r):=\Phi\left(Ce^{-CK^{p}}r\right)\leq\Phi\left(Ce^{-C[F_{\bm{\nu}}]_{\beta,T}^{p}}r\right)=\widetilde{\Phi}^{\bm{\nu}}(r),

hence, by (
3.3
) we have

M_{\overline{\Phi}_{p}}(\bm{\widetilde{\nu}})\leq C(1+K^{2p}).

(3.4)

Notice that both bounds (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) are uniform with respect to
\bm{\nu}
. It is also clear, by (
3.2
), that
(X_{\bm{\nu}},Y_{\bm{\nu}})\in L^{p}(\Omega;C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
. Then we can consider the map

\mathcal{T}:\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))\mapsto\mathcal{T}\bm{\nu}={\rm Law}(X_{\bm{\nu}},Y_{\bm{\nu}})\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})).

We want to show that
\mathcal{T}
admits a fixed point. To do this, we first prove that

{\sf Im}(\mathcal{T})=\{\mathcal{T}{\bm{\nu}}:\ {\bm{\nu}}\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))\}

is relatively (sequentially) compact in
\mathcal{W}_{p}(C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
. First, we claim that the family
\{(X_{\bm{\nu}},V_{\bm{\nu}})\}_{\nu\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))}
is uniformly tight, i.e. for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact set

such that

\sup_{\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))}{\sf P}\left((X_{\bm{\nu}},V_{\bm{\nu}})\not\in K_{\varepsilon}\right)<\varepsilon.

To prove this, we first fix
\gamma<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
and consider the random variable

L_{B,\gamma}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,s\in[0,T]\\
t\not=s\end{subarray}}\frac{|B(t)-B(s)|}{|t-s|^{\gamma}},

that is a.s. finite by the

\displaystyle\quad+CK(|V_{1}(\tau)|^{p-1}+|V_{2}(\tau)|^{p-1}+|V_{1}(\tau)|^{p-1+\beta}+|V_{2}(\tau)|^{p-1+\beta})

\displaystyle\quad+CK(|V_{1}(\tau)|^{p-1}+|V_{2}(\tau)|^{p-1})\left(\left(\overline{M}_{p}(\tau,\bm{\mu}^{1})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}+\left(\overline{M}_{p}(\tau,\bm{\mu}^{2})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq CK\left[1+(|V_{1}(\tau)|^{p}+|V_{2}(\tau)|^{p})+(|X_{1}(\tau)|^{p}+|X_{2}(\tau)|^{p})+\left(\left(\overline{M}_{p}(\tau,\bm{\mu}^{1})\right)+\left(\overline{M}_{p}(\tau,\bm{\mu}^{2})\right)\right)\right],

where in the last inequality we used Young’s inequality. Taking the expectation it is not difficult to check that

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta V(\tau)\right|^{p-1}|\mathfrak{v}[\tau,\bm{\mu}^{1}](Z_{1}(\tau))-\mathfrak{v}[\tau,\bm{\mu}^{2}](Z_{2}(\tau))|\right]\leq CK(1+\overline{M}_{p}(T,\bm{\mu}^{1})+\overline{M}_{p}(T,\bm{\mu}^{2}))

where the right-hand side is independent of
\tau
. Hence, we can use Fubini’s theorem to get

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta V(s)\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=p\int_{0}^{s}{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta V(\tau)\right|^{p-2}(\mathfrak{v}[\tau,\bm{\mu}^{1}](Z_{1}(\tau))-\mathfrak{v}[\tau,\bm{\mu}^{2}](Z_{2}(\tau)))\cdot\delta V(\tau)\right]d\tau

(3.20)

\displaystyle\leq CD\int_{0}^{s}\sup_{0\leq r\leq\tau}{\sf E}[|\delta Z(r)|^{p}]d\tau,

(3.21)

where we used the dissipativity assumption
(\mathfrak{v}_{3})
. We also have, clearly,

{\sf E}\left[|\delta X(s)|^{p}\right]\leq C\int_{0}^{s}{\sf E}\left[|\delta V(\tau)|^{p}\right]d\tau\leq C\int_{0}^{s}\sup_{0\leq r\leq\tau}{\sf E}\left[|\delta Z(r)|^{p}\right]d\tau.

(3.22)

Combining (
3.21
) and (
3.22
) and taking the supremum over
[0,t]
we get

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta Z(s)\right|^{p}\right]\leq C(D+1)\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{0\leq r\leq\tau}{\sf E}[|\delta Z(r)|^{p}]d\tau.

(3.23)

Since this holds for all
t\in[0,T]
, we can use Grönwall’s inequality to achieve
\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta Z(s)\right|^{p}\right]=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
, which in turn implies
Z_{1}=Z_{2}
a.s. Hence
\bm{\mu}^{1}=\bm{\mu}^{2}
. Observe that this also shows that (
1.4
) admits a unique strong solution.

3.1.4.
Constructon of the global solution

Now consider any increasing sequence
T_{n}\uparrow+\infty
and denote by
\bm{\mu}^{n}
the local solution of (
1.1
) on
[0,T_{n}]
. Let
n_{1}<n_{2}
and observe that
\bm{\mu}^{n_{2}}
still solves (
1.1
) on
[0,T_{1}]
. Hence, by uniqueness of the local solution,
\bm{\mu}^{n_{1}}=\bm{\mu}^{n_{2}}
on
[0,T_{1}]
. For such a reason, if we denote
n(t)=\min\{n\geq 0:\ t\geq T_{n}\}
, we can define the continuous curve of probability measures
\bm{\mu}\in C({{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+};\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
by setting

\mu_{t}=\mu_{t}^{n(t)},\ \forall t\in{{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+}.

It is clear that
\bm{\mu}
is the unique global solution we are searching for. Analogously, if we set
(X_{n},V_{n})
to be the respective solutions of (
1.4
), a similar argument shows that the stochastic process
(X,V)\in L^{p}(\Omega;C({{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
defined as

(X(t),V(t))=(X_{n(t)}(t),V_{n(t)}(t))

still solves (
1.4
) and
\bm{\mu}={\rm Law}(X,V)
.

3.1.5.
Hölder continuity of the solution

For any
t\geq 0
, let
\Sigma_{t}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
(X_{0},V_{0})
and
\{B(s);\ s\leq t\}
. Then it is not difficult to check that
(X,V)
is a stochastic process adapted to the filtration
\{\Sigma_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. Now let
\bm{\mu}
be the solution fo (
1.1
) and
Z=(X,V)
the solution of (
1.4
) with
{\bm{\mu}}={\rm Law}(X,V)={\rm Law}(Z)
. Let
T>0
,
t\geq 0
and consider
h>0
such that
0\leq t<t+h\leq T
, without loss of generality. Clearly,
(Z(t+h),Z(t))
constitute a coupling of
\mu_{t+h}
and
\mu_{t}
, hence

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu_{t+h},\mu_{t})\leq\left({\sf E}\left[\left|Z(t+h)-Z(t)|^{p}\right|\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

Set
\widetilde{V}(h)=V(t+h)-V(t)
,
\widetilde{X}(h)=X(t+h)-X(t)
and
\widetilde{Z}(h)=Z(t+h)-Z(t)
. Observe in particular that

\widetilde{X}(h)=\int_{t}^{t+h}V(s)ds

and then

{\sf E}\left[|\widetilde{X}(h)|^{p}\right]\leq h^{p-1}{\sf E}\left[\int_{t}^{t+h}|V(s)|^{p}ds\right]\leq h^{p}\overline{M}_{p}(T;\bm{\mu})\leq C(1+K)e^{KT(1+e^{KT})}h^{p},

(3.24)

where we used (
3.18
). As a consequence, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu_{t+h},\mu_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\left({\sf E}\left[\left|\widetilde{Z}(h)|^{p}\right|\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C\left(\left({\sf E}\left[\left|\widetilde{X}(h)|^{p}\right|\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}+\left({\sf E}\left[\left|\widetilde{V}(h)|^{p}\right|\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\right)

(3.25)

\displaystyle\leq C\left((1+K)^{\frac{1}{p}}e^{\frac{KT(1+e^{KT})}{p}}h+\left({\sf E}\left[\left|\widetilde{V}(h)|^{p}\right|\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\right),

(3.26)

hence we only need to estimate
{\sf E}\left[\left|\widetilde{V}(h)|^{p}\right|\right]
. We have

\widetilde{V}(h)=\int_{0}^{h}\widetilde{\mathfrak{v}}[s,\bm{\mu}](\widetilde{X}(s)+X(t),\widetilde{V}(s)+V(t))\,ds+\sqrt{2\sigma}\widetilde{B}(h).

Where
\widetilde{\mathfrak{v}}[h,\bm{\mu}](z)=\mathfrak{v}[t+h,\bm{\mu}](z)
and
\widetilde{B}(h)=B(t+h)-B(t)
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and set
H_{p,\varepsilon}(x)=(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon)^{\frac{p}{2}}
. Once we recall that
\widetilde{B}
is still a Brownian motion and
H_{p,\varepsilon}\in C^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{d})
, we can use Itô’s formula to write

\displaystyle\begin{split}&H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(h))=\varepsilon^{\frac{p}{2}}\\
&+\int_{0}^{h}\left(\nabla H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\cdot\widetilde{\mathfrak{v}}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](\widetilde{X}(\tau)+X(t),\widetilde{V}(\tau)+V(t))+\sigma\Delta H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\right)d\tau\\
&\quad+\sqrt{2\sigma}\int_{0}^{h}\nabla H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))d\widetilde{B}(\tau).\end{split}

Next, for any
h\geq 0
, let
\widetilde{\Sigma}^{t}_{h}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{(X(s),V(s)),\ s\in[0,t]\}
and
\{\widetilde{B}(\tau);\ \tau\in[0,h]\}
. Then
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{V})
is adapted to the filtration
\{\widetilde{\Sigma}^{t}_{h}\}_{h\geq 0}
and then, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the random variable

\mathcal{T}_{N}(h):=\min\{\inf\{\tau>0:\ |\widetilde{V}(\tau)|\geq N\},h\}

is a
\{\widetilde{\Sigma}^{t}_{h}\}_{h\geq 0}
-stopping time. Observe further that

\displaystyle H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\mathcal{T}_{N}(h)))=\varepsilon^{\frac{p}{2}}

(3.27)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}_{N}(h)}\left(\nabla H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\cdot\widetilde{\mathfrak{v}}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](\widetilde{X}(\tau)+X(t),\widetilde{V}(\tau)+V(t))+\sigma\Delta H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\right)d\tau

(3.28)

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{2\sigma}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}_{N}(h)}\nabla H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\min\{\tau,\mathcal{T}_{N}(h)\}))d\widetilde{B}(\tau).

(3.29)

Before taking the expectation on both sides, observe that

\nabla H_{p,\varepsilon}(x)=p(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}x

(3.30)

and then

\displaystyle|\nabla H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\min\{\tau,\mathcal{T}_{N}(h)\}))|\leq pN(N^{2}+\varepsilon)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}.

Thus the process
\int_{0}^{h}\nabla H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\min\{\tau,\mathcal{T}_{N}(h)\}))d\widetilde{B}(\tau)
is a martingale
[
39
, Corollary 3.2.6]
. Hence, since
\mathcal{T}_{N}(h)
is a bounded stopping time, we can use the optional stopping theorem
[
43
, Theorem II.3.2]
to get

{\sf E}\left[\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}_{N}(h)}\nabla H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\min\{\tau,\mathcal{T}_{N}(h)\}))d\widetilde{B}(\tau)\right]=0.

Taking the expectation in (
3.29
) and then the absolute value we achieve

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\mathcal{T}_{N}(h)))\right]\leq\varepsilon^{\frac{p}{2}}

(3.31)

\displaystyle+{\sf E}\left[\int_{0}^{h}\left(\left|\nabla H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\cdot\widetilde{\mathfrak{v}}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](\widetilde{X}(\tau)+X(t),\widetilde{V}(\tau)+V(t))\right|+\sigma\left|\Delta H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\right|\right)d\tau\right].

(3.32)

Now we use
(\mathfrak{v}_{1})
and (
3.30
) to get

\displaystyle\left|\nabla H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\cdot\widetilde{\mathfrak{v}}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](\widetilde{X}(\tau)+X(t),\widetilde{V}(\tau)+V(t))\right|

\displaystyle\leq pK(|\widetilde{V}(\tau)|^{2}+\varepsilon)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}|\widetilde{V}(\tau)|(1+|\widetilde{X}(\tau)+X(t)|^{\frac{\beta}{3}}+|\widetilde{V}(\tau)+V(t)|^{\beta}+\left(\overline{M}_{p}(t+\tau;\bm{\mu})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}})

\displaystyle\leq CK(|\widetilde{V}(\tau)|^{2}+\varepsilon)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}(1+|\widetilde{X}(\tau)|^{\frac{\beta}{3}}+|X(t)|^{\frac{\beta}{3}}+|\widetilde{V}(\tau)|^{\beta}+|V(t)|^{\beta}+\left(\overline{M}_{p}(t+\tau;\bm{\mu})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}})

\displaystyle\leq CKH_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))+CK(|\widetilde{V}(\tau)|^{2}+\varepsilon)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}(1+|\widetilde{X}(\tau)|^{\frac{\beta}{3}}+|X(t)|^{\frac{\beta}{3}}+|V(t)|^{\beta}+\left(\overline{M}_{p}(t+\tau;\bm{\mu})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}).

To handle the second summand, we use Young’s inequality with exponent
p
, and then we achieve

\displaystyle\left|\nabla H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\cdot\widetilde{\mathfrak{v}}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](\widetilde{X}(\tau)+X(t),\widetilde{V}(\tau)+V(t))\right|

(3.33)

\displaystyle\quad\leq CKH_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))+CK^{p}(1+|\widetilde{X}(\tau)|^{\frac{\beta p}{3}}+|X(t)|^{\frac{\beta p}{3}}+|V(t)|^{\beta p}+\overline{M}_{p}(t+\tau;\bm{\mu}))

(3.34)

\displaystyle\quad\leq CKH_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))+CK(1+|\widetilde{X}(\tau)|^{p}+|Z(t)|^{p}+\overline{M}_{p}(T;\bm{\mu})).

(3.35)

Taking the expectation this leads to

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\left|\nabla H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\cdot\widetilde{\mathfrak{v}}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](\widetilde{X}(\tau)+X(t),\widetilde{V}(\tau)+V(t))\right|\right]

(3.36)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq CK{\sf E}\left[H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\right]+CK(1+(1+K)e^{KT(1+e^{KT})}),

(3.37)

where we also used (
3.18
) and (
3.24
). Plugging (
3.37
) into (
3.32
) we have

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\mathcal{T}_{N}(h)))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{\frac{p}{2}}+CK\int_{0}^{h}{\sf E}\left[H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\right]d\tau

(3.38)

\displaystyle+CK(1+(1+K)e^{KT(1+e^{KT})})h+\sigma\int_{0}^{h}{\sf E}\left[\left|\Delta H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\right|d\tau\right].

(3.39)

Next, observe that since
V
is a global solution of (
1.4
), it cannot blow up in finite time, hence it must hold
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{T}_{N}(h)=h
. Furthermore, for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
,
V
(and then
\widetilde{V}
) is continuous. Hence, by Fatou’s Lemma, taking the limit inferior in (
3.39
) we get

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(h))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{\frac{p}{2}}+CK\int_{0}^{h}{\sf E}\left[H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\right]d\tau

(3.40)

\displaystyle+CK(1+(1+K)e^{KT(1+e^{KT})})h+\sigma\int_{0}^{h}{\sf E}\left[\left|\Delta H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\right|d\tau\right].

(3.41)

Furthermore, notice that

\Delta H_{p,\varepsilon}(x)=p(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon)^{\frac{p-4}{2}}[(p+d-2)|x|^{2}+d\varepsilon],

hence

\left|\Delta H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(h))\right|\leq C(|\widetilde{V}(h)|^{2}+\varepsilon)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}

Now we have to distinguish among two cases. If
p>2
we get, by Young’s inequality with exponent
\frac{p}{p-2}
,

\left|\Delta H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(h))\right|\leq C(1+H_{p,\varepsilon}(|\widetilde{V}(h)|)).

(3.42)

Hence, using (
3.42
) into (
3.41
), we achieve

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(h))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{\frac{p}{2}}+C(1+K)\int_{0}^{h}{\sf E}\left[H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\right]d\tau

(3.43)

\displaystyle\quad+CK(1+(1+K)e^{KT(1+e^{KT})})h.

(3.44)

Since
h\in[0,T-t]
is arbitrary, we can use Grönwall’s inequality to get

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(h))\right]\leq\left(\varepsilon^{\frac{p}{2}}+CK(1+(1+K)e^{KT(1+e^{KT})})h\right)e^{C(1+K)h}.

(3.45)

It remains to take the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
to finally achieve, by the dominated convergence theorem,

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[|\widetilde{V}(h)|^{p}\right]\leq CK(1+(1+K)e^{KT(1+e^{KT})})e^{C(1+K)}h.

(3.46)

Using the latter inequality into (
3.26
), we have the local
1/p
-Hölder continuity of
\bm{\mu}
and the third bound in (
1.6
).

If
p\leq 2
instead we use

\left|\Delta H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(h))\right|\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{p-2}{2}}

(3.47)

and then

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(h))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{\frac{p}{2}}+CK\int_{0}^{h}{\sf E}\left[H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(\tau))\right]d\tau

(3.48)

\displaystyle\quad+CK(1+(1+K)e^{KT(1+e^{KT})})h+C\varepsilon^{\frac{p-2}{2}}h.

(3.49)

Again, by Grönwall’s inequality we have

\displaystyle{\sf E}[|\widetilde{V}(h)|^{p}]\leq{\sf E}\left[H_{p,\varepsilon}(\widetilde{V}(h))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\varepsilon^{\frac{p}{2}}+CK(1+(1+K)e^{KT(1+e^{KT})})h+C\varepsilon^{\frac{p-2}{2}}h\right)e^{CK}.

(3.50)

The latter inequality holds for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
h\in[0,T-t]
, hence, we can take
\varepsilon=h
so that

\displaystyle{\sf E}[|\widetilde{V}(h)|^{p}]\leq\left(1+CK(1+(1+K)e^{KT(1+e^{KT})})\right)h^{\frac{p}{2}}e^{CK}.

(3.51)

Again, we get the local
1/2
-Hölder continuity of
\bm{\mu}
and the third bound in (
1.6
) by (
3.26
).

3.1.6.
Higher moment estimate

It remains to prove the second bound in (
1.6
). Again, consider the Young function
\Phi
such that
\overline{\mu}\in\mathcal{W}_{\Phi_{p}}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
. Observe that, by
(\mathfrak{v}_{1})
and (
3.18
), we have, for all
t\in[0,T]
and
z\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}

\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}](z)\leq C(1+K)e^{KT(1+e^{KT})}(1+|x|^{\frac{\beta}{3}}+|v|^{\beta}).

Hence we can use (
2.7
) to get

\overline{M}_{\widetilde{\Phi}_{p}(\cdot;K)}(T,\bm{\mu})\leq C(1+(1+K)e^{KT(1+e^{KT})}),

where

\widetilde{\Phi}(r;K)=\Phi\left(Ce^{-C(1+K)e^{KT(1+e^{KT})}}r\right).

This ends the proof.

Remark 3.1
.

Notice that, to prove the existence of local solutions, Assumption
(\mathfrak{v}_{3})
is not needed. Furthermore, for uniqueness, one only needs that Assumption

(\mathfrak{v}_{3})
holds on solutions of (
1.4
).

3.2.
Proof of Corollary
1.4

From now on, we fix the time horizon
T>0
.
Let
Z_{j}=(X_{j},V_{j})
and
Z=(X,V)
be the solutions of (
1.4
) associated with
\mathfrak{v}_{j}
and
\mathfrak{v}
respectively. Then, clearly,
\mu_{t}^{j}={\rm Law}(X_{j}(t),V_{j}(t))
and
\mu_{t}={\rm Law}(X(t),V(t))
, hence

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu_{t}^{j},\mu_{t})\right)^{p}\leq C\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left({\sf E}[|Z_{j}(t)-Z(t)|^{p}]\right).

Let
0\leq t\leq T
. Once we notice that
\delta V_{j}(t):=V_{j}(t)-V(t)
is absolutely continuous, we can use the chain rule to get for any
s\in[0,t]

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta V_{j}(s)\right|^{p}\right]=p{\sf E}\left[\int_{0}^{s}\left|\delta V_{j}(\tau)\right|^{p-2}(\mathfrak{v}_{j}[\tau,\bm{\mu}^{j}](Z_{j}(\tau))-\mathfrak{v}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau)))\cdot\delta V_{j}(\tau)d\tau\right].

Now notice that, by Young’s inequality and
(\mathfrak{v}_{1})
we get

\left|\delta V_{j}(\tau)\right|^{p-1}\left|\mathfrak{v}_{j}[\tau,\bm{\mu}^{j}](Z_{j}(\tau))-\mathfrak{v}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau))\right|\\
\leq C(K+1)\left(1+\left|Z_{j}(\tau)\right|^{p}+|Z(\tau)|^{p}+\overline{M}_{p}(T;\bm{\mu}_{j})+\overline{M}_{p}(T;\bm{\mu}_{j})\right),

and then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta V_{j}(\tau)\right|^{p-1}\left|\mathfrak{v}_{j}[\tau,\bm{\mu}^{j}](Z_{j}(\tau))-\mathfrak{v}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau))\right|\right]d\tau

\displaystyle\leq C(K+1)T\left(1+\overline{M}_{p}(T;\bm{\mu}_{j})+\overline{M}_{p}(T,\bm{\mu})\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(K+1)T\left(1+H_{T}(K)\right),

where we also used (
1.6
). Hence we can employ Fubini’s theorem to get

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta V_{j}(s)\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=p\int_{0}^{s}{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta V_{j}(\tau)\right|^{p-2}(\mathfrak{v}_{j}[\tau,\bm{\mu}^{j}](Z_{j}(\tau))-\mathfrak{v}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau)))\cdot\delta V_{j}(\tau)\right]d\tau

(3.52)

\displaystyle=p\int_{0}^{s}{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta V_{j}(\tau)\right|^{p-2}(\mathfrak{v}_{j}[\tau,\bm{\mu}^{j}](Z_{j}(\tau))-\mathfrak{v}_{j}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau)))\cdot\delta V_{j}(\tau)\right]d\tau

(3.53)

\displaystyle\quad+p\int_{0}^{s}{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta V_{j}(\tau)\right|^{p-2}(\mathfrak{v}_{j}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau))-\mathfrak{v}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau)))\cdot\delta V_{j}(\tau)\right]d\tau

(3.54)

\displaystyle=I_{1}+I_{2}.

(3.55)

For the first term, we use
(\mathfrak{v}_{3})
to get

\displaystyle I_{1}\leq D\int_{0}^{s}\sup_{0\leq r\leq\tau}{\sf E}[|\delta Z_{j}(r)|^{p}]d\tau,

(3.56)

where
\delta Z_{j}(r):=Z_{j}(r)-Z(r)
. Next, we move to
I_{2}
. To handle it we use Young’s inequality as follows

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq p\int_{0}^{s}{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta V_{j}(\tau)\right|^{p-1}|\mathfrak{v}_{j}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau))-\mathfrak{v}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau))|\right]d\tau

(3.57)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{0}^{s}\sup_{0\leq r\leq\tau}{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta Z_{j}(r)\right|^{p}\right]d\tau+\int_{0}^{s}{\sf E}\left[|\mathfrak{v}_{j}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau))-\mathfrak{v}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau))|^{p}\right]d\tau\right).

(3.58)

We denote

\mathcal{R}_{j}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}{\sf E}\left[|\mathfrak{v}_{j}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau))-\mathfrak{v}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau))|^{p}\right]d\tau

and we notice that it is a nondecreasing function. Hence we get, combining (
3.55
), (
3.56
) and (
3.58
)

\displaystyle{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta V_{j}(s)\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(1+D)\int_{0}^{s}\sup_{0\leq r\leq\tau}{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta Z_{j}(r)\right|^{p}\right]d\tau+C\mathcal{R}_{j}(s).

(3.59)

Next, we observe that

{\sf E}\left[|\delta X_{j}(s)|^{p}\right]\leq T^{p-1}\int_{0}^{s}{\sf E}\left[|\delta V_{j}(\tau)|^{p}\right]d\tau\leq T^{p-1}\int_{0}^{s}\sup_{0\leq r\leq\tau}{\sf E}\left[|\delta Z_{j}(r)|^{p}\right]d\tau,

(3.60)

where
\delta X_{j}(t):=X_{j}(t)-X(t)
. Combining (
3.59
) and (
3.60
) and taking the supremum on
[0,t]
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta Z_{j}(s)\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(1+D)\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{0\leq r\leq\tau}{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta Z_{j}(r)\right|^{p}\right]d\tau+C\mathcal{R}_{j}(t),

(3.61)

that, by Grönwall’s inequality, implies

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}{\sf E}\left[\left|\delta Z_{j}(s)\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{C(1+D)}\mathcal{R}_{j}(T)

(3.62)

and then

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu_{t}^{j},\mu_{t})\right)^{p}\leq e^{C(1+D)}\mathcal{R}_{j}(T).

It remains to show that the right-hand side converges to
0
. To do this, it is sufficient to observe that, by
(\mathfrak{v}_{1})
and Young’s inequality

\left|\mathfrak{v}_{j}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau))-\mathfrak{v}_{j}[\tau,\bm{\mu}](Z(\tau))\right|\leq CK\left(1+|Z(\tau)|^{p}+\overline{M}_{p}(T,\bm{\mu})\right),

where

\int_{0}^{T}{\sf E}\left[CK\left(1+|Z(\tau)|^{p}+\overline{M}_{p}(T,\bm{\mu})\right)\right]d\tau\leq CK(1+\overline{M}_{p}(T,\bm{\mu}))<\infty.

Hence, by the dominated convergence theorem and the fact that
\bm{\mu}
satisfies (
1.6
),
\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{j}(T)=0
. This concludes the proof.
\square

Now, we want to set a control problem on (

\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}]=\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}(t,\bm{\mu},\bm{Y})\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\Psi(\bm{u}(t,\bm{\mu}))\,dt

.

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

By Theorem
4.2
, for any fixed
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}
the solution
(\bm{\mu},\bm{Y})
of (
4.1
) belongs to

\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
.
First, we show that
\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}]<\infty
for all
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}
. Indeed, by (

) and letting
(\bm{\mu},\bm{Y})
be the solution of
(

) with control

, we have

\left|\bm{u}(t,\bm{\mu})\right|\leq K_{\bm{u}}(1+\mathcal{C}(K_{G})^{\frac{1}{p}}).

Now, let
M_{\Psi}=\sup_{|x|\leq K_{\bm{u}}(1+\mathcal{C}(K_{G})^{\frac{1}{p}})}\Psi(x)
, which exists since
\Psi
is convex and thus continuous. Hence

\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}]\leq\int_{0}^{T}M_{\mathcal{L}}(t)\,dt+M_{\Psi}T<\infty.

Now, we show that for any two controls
\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
such that for all
\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{K}
and
t\in[0,T]
it holds
\bm{u}_{1}(t,\bm{\nu})=\bm{u}_{2}(t,\bm{\nu})
, we have
\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}_{1}]=\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}_{2}]
. To do this, let
(\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{Y}^{1})
and
(\bm{\mu}^{2},\bm{Y}^{2})
be the solutions of (
4.1
) with controls
\bm{u}_{1}
and
\bm{u}_{2}
. Since
\bm{\mu}^{1}\in\mathcal{K}
, then
\bm{u}_{1}(t,\bm{\mu}^{1})=\bm{u}_{2}(t,\bm{\mu}^{1})
, and hence we have

\displaystyle\bm{Y}^{1}(t)

\displaystyle=\overline{\bm{Y}}+\int_{0}^{t}(F[s,\bm{\mu}^{1}](\bm{Y}^{1})+\bm{u}_{1}(s,\bm{\mu}^{1}))\,ds=\overline{\bm{Y}}+\int_{0}^{t}(F[s,\bm{\mu}^{1}](\bm{Y}^{1})+\bm{u}_{2}(s,\bm{\mu}^{1}))\,ds.

Thus,
\bm{Y}^{1}
also solves (
4.3
) with control
\bm{u}_{2}
and measure
\bm{\mu}^{1}
, i.e.

\mathcal{S}[\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{u}_{2}]=\bm{H}^{1}=\mathcal{S}[\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{u}_{2}].

However, this means that

\displaystyle G_{\bm{u}_{1}}[t,\bm{\mu}^{1}](z)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}^{1}](z)+\mathfrak{w}[t,\mathcal{S}[\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{u}_{1}]](z)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{v}[t,\bm{\mu}^{1}](z)+\mathfrak{w}[t,\mathcal{S}[\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{u}_{2}]](z)=G_{\bm{u}_{2}}[t,\bm{\mu}^{1}](z)

and then
\bm{\mu}^{1}
solves (
4.5
) with field
G_{\bm{u}_{2}}
. However, also
\bm{\mu}^{2}
solves (
4.5
) with the field
G_{\bm{u}_{2}}
. Hence, since for a given field

4.5
) admits a unique solution,
\bm{\mu}^{1}=\bm{\mu}^{2}
and also
\bm{H}^{1}=\bm{H}^{2}
. Then, it is clear, by definition of
\mathcal{F}
, that
\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}_{1}]=\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}_{2}]
. Thus we are in a position to consider the equivalence relation
\sim
over
\mathcal{A}
defined as

\bm{u}_{1}\sim\bm{u}_{2}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\bm{u}_{1}(t,\bm{\mu})=\bm{u}_{2}(t,\bm{\mu})\ \forall t\in[0,T],\ \forall\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K},

the quotient set
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{A}/\sim
and the functional
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}:\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
defined as

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}[[\bm{u}]_{\sim}]=\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}],\quad\forall[\bm{u}]_{\sim}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}.

To actually work with
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
, we need to provide a suitable representation of the quotient set
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
. To do this, first notice that for any
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}
we consider a function
\bm{U}:[0,T]\to C(\mathcal{K};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m})
defined as
\bm{U}(t)=\bm{u}(t,\cdot)
for

, so that
\bm{U}(t)(\bm{\mu})=\bm{u}(t,\bm{\mu})
for any
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
. Since

|\bm{u}(t,\bm{\mu})|\leq K_{\bm{u}}(1+(\mathcal{C}(K_{G}))^{\frac{1}{p}}),

and then

\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}}|\bm{u}(t,\bm{\mu})|\,dt\leq K_{\bm{u}}(1+(\mathcal{C}(K_{G}))^{\frac{1}{p}})T,

it is clear that
\bm{U}\in L^{1}([0,T];C(\mathcal{K};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}))
. Let
\Pi:\mathcal{A}\to L^{1}([0,T];C(\mathcal{K};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}))
be the function that maps
\bm{u}
into
\bm{U}
as before. If
\bm{u}_{1}\sim\bm{u}_{2}
, then
\Pi\bm{u}_{1}=\Pi\bm{u}_{2}
, i.e.,
\Pi
is compatible with the equivalence relation
\sim
. In particular, this means that we can define the map
\widetilde{\Pi}:\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\to\Pi\mathcal{A}
such that
\widetilde{\Pi}[\bm{u}]_{\sim}=\Pi\bm{u}
. By definition, this map is surjective. Furthermore, assume that
\widetilde{\Pi}[\bm{u}_{1}]_{\sim}=\widetilde{\Pi}[\bm{u}_{2}]_{\sim}
. Then
\Pi\bm{u}_{1}=\Pi\bm{u}_{2}
hence
\bm{u}_{1}(t,\bm{\mu})=\bm{u}_{2}(t,\bm{\mu})
for all

and
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
, which in turn implies
\bm{u}_{1}\sim\bm{u}_{2}
and
[\bm{u}_{1}]_{\sim}=[\bm{u}_{2}]_{\sim}
. In particular, we have shown that
\widetilde{\Pi}
is a bijection. From now on, without loss of generality, we identify
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
with
\Pi\mathcal{A}
and

\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}]=\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}[\Pi\bm{u}].

We can then rewrite Problem
1.5
as follows:

Find
\bm{U}^{\star}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
such that

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}[\bm{U}^{\star}]=\min_{\bm{U}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}[\bm{U}].

Now let us consider a minimizing sequence
\{\bm{U}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
for
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
. We will prove that this minimizing sequence admits (up to a subsequence) a limit
\bm{U}^{\star}
in some sense that will be specified later. To do this, however, we preliminarily need to prove that
\{\bm{U}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly integrable and uniformly tight, i.e.

\lim_{R\to+\infty}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{\{t\in[0,T]:\ \sup_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}}|\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu})|>R\}}\sup_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}}|\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu})|\,dt=0,

(4.7)

and for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact-valued multifunction
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}:[0,T]\to 2^{C(\mathcal{K};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m})}

with measurable graph such that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|\{t\in[0,T]:\bm{U}^{n}(t)\not\in\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(t)\}<\varepsilon,

respectively.
Let us first show the uniform integrability. Indeed, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\bm{u}^{n}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\bm{U}^{n}=\Pi\bm{u}^{n}
and then, for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
,

|\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu})|=|\bm{u}^{n}(t,\bm{\mu})|\leq K_{\bm{u}}(1+(\mathcal{C}(K_{G}))^{\frac{1}{p}}).

Hence, for
R>K_{\bm{u}}(1+(\mathcal{C}(K_{G}))^{\frac{1}{p}})
, we clearly have

\int_{\{t\in[0,T]:\ \sup_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}}|\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu})|>R\}}\sup_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}}|\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu})|\,dt=0

and then (
4.7
) holds.

Now we show the uniform tightness. To do this, we fix
t\in[0,T]
and notice that, by Assumption
(\mathcal{A}_{2})
it holds

|\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\nu}^{1})-\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\nu}^{2})|\leq L_{\bm{u}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{1}_{t},\mu^{2}_{t}),\,\quad\forall\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{\mu}^{2}\in\mathcal{K}.

Notice that the uniform topology of
C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
is generated by the metric

\mathfrak{d}(\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{\mu}^{2})=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{1}_{t},\mu^{2}_{t}),\ \quad\forall\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{\mu}^{2}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})).

Hence by Lemma
B.3
,
\mathcal{K}
equipped with
\mathfrak{d}
is a compact Hausdorff space. Defining

\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}=\left\{f\in C(\mathcal{K};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}):\ \sup_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}}\left|f(\bm{\mu})\right|+\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{\mu}^{2}\\
\bm{\mu}^{1}\not=\bm{\mu}^{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(\bm{\mu}^{1})-f(\bm{\mu}^{2})|}{\mathfrak{d}(\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{\mu}^{2})}\leq L_{\bm{u}}+K_{\bm{u}}(1+(\mathcal{C}(K_{G}))^{\frac{1}{p}})\right\},

which is a compact subset of
C(\mathcal{K};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m})
by the Arzelá-Ascoli Theorem, we have
\bm{U}^{n}(t)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
for all
t\in[0,T]
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, to get the uniform tightness of
\{\bm{U}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
it is sufficient to set
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(t)=\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}
for all
\varepsilon>0
and
t\in[0,T]
.

Now, we can use a suitable generalization of the Dunford-Pettis theorem, as in
[
10
, Theorem 1.3]
, which tells us that there exists a function
\bm{U}^{\star}\in L^{1}([0,T];C(\mathcal{K},{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}))
such that, up to a non-relabelled subsequence, for all measurable subsets
E\subset[0,T]

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{1}{|E|}\left|\int_{E}(\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu})-\bm{U}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}))\,dt\right|=0.

(4.8)

Then we need to show that
\bm{U}^{\star}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
. To do this, first observe that for any
\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{\mu}^{2}\in\mathcal{K}
it holds, by Assumption
(\mathcal{A}_{2})
,

\frac{1}{|E|}\left|\int_{E}(U^{n}(t)_{j}(\bm{\mu}^{1})-U^{n}_{j}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{2}))\,dt\right|\leq\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}\sup_{0\leq s\leq\sup E}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{1}_{s},\mu^{2}_{s}),

for any
j=1,\dots,m
, where
\bm{U}^{n}=(U^{n}_{1},\cdots,U^{n}_{j})
. Taking the limit as
n\to\infty
and using (
4.8
) we get

\frac{1}{|E|}\left|\int_{E}(U^{\star}_{j}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{1})-U^{\star}_{j}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{2}))\,dt\right|\leq\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}\sup_{0\leq s\leq\sup E}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{1}_{s},\mu^{2}_{s}),

(4.9)

where
\bm{U}^{\star}=(U^{\star}_{1},\cdots,U^{\star}_{m})
. Since
\bm{U}^{\star}\in L^{1}([0,T];C(\mathcal{K};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}))
, by
[
6
, Proposition 1.2.2]
, we know that almost any
t\in[0,T]
is a Lebesgue point for
\bm{U}^{\star}
, i.e. there exists a set
E_{\sf Leb}\subset[0,T]
such that
|[0,T]\setminus E_{\sf Leb}|=0
and for all
t\in E_{\sf Leb}
it holds

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}\sup_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}}|\bm{U}^{\star}(s)(\bm{\mu})-\bm{U}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu})|\,ds=0.

In particular, if we fix
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
for all
t\in E_{\sf Leb}
we have

\lim_{h\to 0}\left|\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}\bm{U}^{\star}(s)(\bm{\mu})\,ds-\bm{U}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu})\right|\leq\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}\sup_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}}|\bm{U}^{\star}(s)(\bm{\mu})-\bm{U}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu})|\,ds=0.

Fix
t\in E_{\sf Leb}
and observe that by (
4.9
) for any
h>0
it holds

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}(U_{j}^{\star}(s)(\bm{\mu}^{1})-U_{j}^{\star}(s)(\bm{\mu}^{2}))\,ds\right|\leq\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t+h}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{1}_{s},\mu^{2}_{s}).

Now we take the limit as
h\to 0
on both sides: on the left-hand side, we use the fact that
t\in E_{\sf Leb}
, while on the right-hand side we use the continuity of
\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{\mu}^{2}\in\mathcal{K}
. Finally, for any
j=1,\ldots,m
we achieve

\displaystyle\left|U_{j}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{1})-U_{j}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{2})\right|\leq\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{1}_{s},\mu^{2}_{s}).

(4.10)

For
t\not\in E_{\sf Leb}
, without loss of generality, we set
\bm{U}^{\star}(t)\equiv 0
, since
|[0,T]\setminus E_{\sf Leb}|=0
and (
4.10
) still holds. Hence, we have
\bm{U}^{\star}
satisfies (
4.10
) for all
t\in[0,T]
. Next, notice that, since
\bm{\delta}_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
and by Assumption
(\mathcal{A}_{1})
, for any measurable subset
E\subset[0,T]
we have

\frac{1}{|E|}\left|\int_{E}\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\delta}_{0})\,dt\right|\leq M_{\bm{u}}.

With the same argument as before, we get, for all
t\in E_{\sf Leb}
,

\left|\bm{U}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\delta}_{0})\right|\leq M_{\bm{u}}.

(4.11)

Combining this with the fact that we set
\bm{U}^{\star}(t)\equiv 0
whenever
t\not\in E_{\sf Leb}
, we infer (
4.11
) holds for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Now, we fix
t\in[0,T]
and introduce another equivalence relation
\sim_{t}
on
\mathcal{K}
, as follows:

\bm{\mu}^{1}\sim_{t}\bm{\mu}^{2}\Leftrightarrow\mu_{s}^{1}=\mu_{s}^{2},\ \forall s\in[0,t].

Notice that, by (
4.10
), if
\bm{\mu}^{1}\sim_{t}\bm{\mu}^{2}
then
U_{j}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{1})=U_{j}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{2})
for all
j=1,\dots,m
. This means that
U_{j}^{\star}(t)
is compatible with the equivalence relation
\sim_{t}
and then we can define the function
\widetilde{U}_{j}^{\star}(t):\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
, where
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}=\mathcal{K}/\sim_{t}
is the quotient of
\mathcal{K}
with respect to the equivalence relation
\sim_{t}
, as

\widetilde{U}_{j}^{\star}(t)([\bm{\mu}]_{\sim_{t}})=U_{j}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}).

Let us also denote by
\pi_{t}:\mathcal{K}\mapsto\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}
the projection, i.e.
\pi_{t}\bm{\mu}=[\bm{\mu}]_{\sim_{t}}
, and endow
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}
with the quotient topology. Notice also that the function
\Pi_{t}:\mathcal{K}\to C([0,t];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
defined as
(\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu})_{s}=\mu_{s}
for all
s\in[0,t]
(i.e.,
\Pi_{t}
is the function that maps
\bm{\mu}
to its restriction on the interval
[0,t]
) is compatible with
\sim_{t}
, i.e., if
\bm{\mu}^{1}\sim_{t}\bm{\mu}^{2}
then
\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu}^{1}=\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu}^{2}
. We endow
\Pi_{t}\mathcal{K}
with the uniform metric
\mathfrak{d}_{t}
on
C([0,t];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
, i.e.,

\mathfrak{d}_{t}(\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{\mu}^{2})=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{1}_{s},\mu^{2}_{s}),\ \bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{\mu}^{2}\in\Pi_{t}\mathcal{K}

It is clear that
\Pi_{t}
is non-expansive, i.e., for all
\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{\mu}^{2}\in\mathcal{K}
it holds

\mathfrak{d}_{t}(\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu}^{1},\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu}^{2})\leq\mathfrak{d}(\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{\mu}^{2}).

Since
\Pi_{t}
is compatible with
\sim_{t}
, we define the map
\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}:\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}\to\Pi_{t}\mathcal{K}
as
\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}[\bm{\mu}]_{\sim_{t}}=\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu}
, which is clearly continuous with respect of the quotient topology on
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}
. Furthermore, notice that if
\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu}^{1}=\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu}^{2}
, then, by definition,
\bm{\mu}^{1}\sim_{t}\bm{\mu}^{2}
. Hence,
\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}
is bijective. Finally, consider an open set
E\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}
. We want to prove that
\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}E
is an open set. To do this, fix
[\bm{\mu}]_{\sim_{t}}\in E
. We want to fix a special representative
\bm{\mu}\in[\bm{\mu}]_{\sim_{t}}
. Precisely, let
\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}=\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}[\bm{\mu}]_{\sim_{t}}
and define
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
by setting
\mu_{s}=\widetilde{\mu}_{\min\{s,t\}}
for
s\in[0,T]
. It is not difficult to check that
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
. In particular
\bm{\mu}\in\pi_{t}^{-1}(U)
, and notice that since
\pi_{t}^{-1}(E)
is an open set in
\mathcal{K}
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mu}):=\{\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{K}:\ \mathfrak{d}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu})<\varepsilon\}\subset\pi_{t}^{-1}(E)
.

Now, we define
B_{\varepsilon}(\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu};t):=\{\bm{\nu}\in\Pi_{t}\mathcal{K}:\ \mathfrak{d}_{t}(\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu})<\varepsilon\}
. Since
\Pi_{t}
is non-expansive, we have
\Pi_{t}B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mu})\subseteq B_{\varepsilon}(\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu};t)
. On the other hand, consider any
\widetilde{\bm{\nu}}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu};t)
and define
\bm{\nu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
by setting
\nu_{s}=\widetilde{\nu}_{\min\{s,t\}}
for
s\in[0,T]
. Again, it is not difficult to check that
\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{K}
and
\mathfrak{d}(\bm{\nu},\bm{\mu})<\varepsilon
. Hence,
\widetilde{\bm{\nu}}=\Pi_{t}\bm{\nu}
with
\bm{\nu}\in B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mu})
and then
\widetilde{\bm{\nu}}\in\Pi_{t}B_{\varepsilon}(\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu};t)
. This shows that
\Pi_{t}B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mu})=B_{\varepsilon}(\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu};t)
. Recalling that
\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu}=\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}[\bm{\mu}]_{\sim_{t}}
, we get

B_{\varepsilon}(\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu};t)=\Pi_{t}B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mu})=\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}\pi_{t}B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mu})\subseteq\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}E,

where we used the fact that since
B_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mu})\subseteq\pi_{t}^{-1}(E)
. Since
[\bm{\mu}]_{\sim_{t}}\in E
is arbitrary,
\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}E
is an open set. In conclusion,
\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}:\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}\to\Pi_{t}\mathcal{K}
is a homeomorphism.

Now we notice that
\widetilde{U}_{j}^{\star}(t)\circ\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}^{-1}
is
\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}
-Lipschitz. Indeed, for any
\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}^{1},\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}^{2}\in\Pi_{t}\mathcal{K}
, letting
\bm{\mu}^{j}\in\Pi_{t}^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}^{j}
for
j=1,2
, it holds

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{U}_{j}^{\star}(t)(\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}^{-1}(\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}^{1}))-\widetilde{U}_{j}^{\star}(t)(\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}^{-1}(\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}^{2}))\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\widetilde{U}_{j}^{\star}(t)([\bm{\mu}^{1}]_{\sim_{t}})-\widetilde{U}_{j}^{\star}(t)([\bm{\mu}^{2}]_{\sim_{t}})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|U_{j}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{1})-U_{j}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{2})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{1}_{s},\mu^{2}_{s})=\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}\mathfrak{d}(\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}^{1},\widetilde{\bm{\mu}}^{2}).

Let us recall that also
\Pi_{t}\mathcal{K}
is a compact subset of
C([0,t];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
. Now we can use McShane’s Extension Theorem (see
[
35
]
) to state that the function
\widetilde{u}_{j}^{\star}(t,\cdot)
defined as

\widetilde{u}_{j}^{\star}(t,\bm{\nu})=\min_{\bm{\mu}\in\Pi_{t}\mathcal{K}}\left\{\widetilde{U}_{j}^{\star}(t)(\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}^{-1}\bm{\mu})+\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}\mathfrak{d}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu})\right\},\quad\bm{\nu}\in C([0,t];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})),

is the maximal
\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}
-Lipschitz extension of
\widetilde{U}_{j}^{\star}(t)\circ\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}^{-1}
on
C([0,t];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
. Finally, we set, for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
,

u_{j}^{\star}(t,\bm{\mu})=\widetilde{u}_{j}^{\star}(t,\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu})

and
\bm{u}^{\star}=(u_{j}^{\star})_{j=1,\dots,m}
. We can clearly rewrite
u_{j}^{\star}
as follows:

u_{j}^{\star}(t,\bm{\nu})=\min_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}}\left\{U_{j}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu})+\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu})\right\},\quad\bm{\nu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})),

so that it is clear that
u_{j}^{\star}
is measurable in the variable
t
, as it is the minimum of a family of measurable functions. Concerning the continuity on
\bm{\mu}
, notice that for all
\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{\mu}^{2}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
,

\left|u_{j}^{\star}(t,\bm{\mu}^{1})-u_{j}^{\star}(t,\bm{\mu}^{2})\right|=\left|\widetilde{u}_{j}^{\star}(t,\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu}^{1})-\widetilde{u}_{j}^{\star}(t,\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu}^{2})\right|\leq\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{1}_{s},\mu^{2}_{s}).

This shows that
\bm{u}^{\star}
is continuous in the
\bm{\mu}
variable, so that
(\mathcal{A}_{0})
holds, and that
(\mathcal{A}_{2})
also holds. Furthermore, notice that

\left|\bm{u}^{\star}(t,\bm{\delta}_{0})\right|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|\widetilde{u}_{j}^{\star}(t,\Pi_{t}\bm{\delta}_{0})\right|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|\widetilde{U}_{j}^{\star}(t)(\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}^{-1}\Pi_{t}\bm{\delta}_{0})\right|^{2}\\
=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|\widetilde{U}_{j}^{\star}(t)([\bm{\delta}_{0}]_{\sim_{t}})\right|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|U_{j}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\delta}_{0})\right|^{2}\leq M_{0}^{2},

by (
4.11
), thus
(\mathcal{A}_{1})
also holds. As a consequence,
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}
. However, if
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
, we have

u_{j}^{\star}(t,\bm{\mu})=\widetilde{u}_{j}^{\star}(t,\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu})=\widetilde{U}_{j}^{\star}(t)(\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}^{-1}\Pi_{t}\bm{\mu})\\
=\widetilde{U}_{j}^{\star}(t)([\bm{\mu}]_{\sim_{t}})=U_{j}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}),

i.e.
\bm{U}^{\star}=\Pi\bm{u}^{\star}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
.

It remains to show that
\bm{U}^{\star}
is solution of Problem
4.3
. Recall that
\{\bm{U}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
, hence there exists a sequence of controls
\{\bm{u}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{A}
such that
\bm{U}^{n}=\Pi\bm{u}^{n}
. Let
(\bm{\mu}^{n},\bm{Y}^{n})
be the respective solutions of (
4.1
). Furthermore, let
(\bm{\mu}^{\star},\bm{Y}^{\star})
be the solution of (
4.1
) with control
\bm{u}^{\star}
. Observe that
\Psi:{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
is a convex function and let
\Psi^{\ast}
be its Legendre transform, i.e.

\Psi^{\ast}(x)=\sup_{y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}}(y\cdot x-\Psi(y)),\quad x\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}.

Recall that
\Psi^{\ast}:{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
is still a convex function and that the Legendre transform is an involution, i.e.,

\Psi(x)=\Psi^{\ast\ast}(x)=\sup_{y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}}(y\cdot x-\Psi^{\ast}(y)),\quad x\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}.

Fix
y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}
and let
\Psi(x;y)=y\cdot x-\Psi^{\ast}(y)
, which is an affine function. Then we have, for any measurable subset
E\subset[0,T]

\displaystyle\int_{E}\Psi(\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n});y)\,dt

\displaystyle=y\cdot\int_{E}\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n})\,dt-\Psi^{\ast}(y)|E|

(4.12)

\displaystyle=y\cdot\int_{E}\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{\star})\,dt-\Psi^{\ast}(y)|E|+y\cdot\mathcal{R}_{n}(E),

(4.13)

where

\mathcal{R}_{n}(E)=\int_{E}\left(\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n})-\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{\star})\right)\,dt.

Let us show that
\mathcal{R}_{n}\to 0
. To do this, first notice that

\left|\mathcal{R}_{n}(E)\right|\leq\int_{E}\left|\bm{u}^{n}(t,\bm{\mu}^{n})-\bm{u}^{n}(t,\bm{\mu}^{\star})\right|\,dt\leq L_{\bm{u}}|E|\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\bm{\mu}^{,}\bm{\mu}^{\star}).

By (
4.8
), we know that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{E}\bm{u}^{n}(t,\bm{\mu})\,dt=\int_{E}\bm{u}^{\star}(t,\bm{\mu})\,dt,\ \forall\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K},

hence we can use Theorem
4.2
to conclude that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left|\mathcal{R}_{n}(E)\right|=0.

Taking the limit as
n\to\infty
in (
4.13
) we get

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{E}\Psi(\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n});y)\,dt=y\cdot\int_{E}\bm{U}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{\star})\,dt-\Psi^{\ast}(y)|E|=\int_{E}\Psi(\bm{U}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{\star});y)\,dt.

In particular, taking the supremum only on the left-hand side, we have

\displaystyle\int_{E}\Psi(\bm{U}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{\star});y)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}}\lim_{n\to+\infty}\int_{E}\Psi(\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n});y)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\sup_{y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}}\int_{E}\Psi(\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n});y)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\int_{E}\Psi(\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n}))\,dt=:\mathcal{G}(E).

Notice that
\mathcal{G}
is superadditive. Indeed, if we consider two measurable sets
E_{1},E_{2}\subset[0,T]
with
E_{1}\cap E_{2}=\emptyset
, then

\int_{E_{1}\cup E_{2}}\Psi(\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n}))\,dt=\int_{E_{1}}\Psi(\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n}))\,dt+\int_{E_{2}}\Psi(\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n}))\,dt

and taking the limit inferior we achieve

\mathcal{G}(E_{1}\cup E_{2})\geq\mathcal{G}(E_{1})+\mathcal{G}(E_{2}).

Hence, by the Localization Lemma (see
[
12
, Proposition 1.16]
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{E}\Psi(\bm{U}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{\star}))\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\sup_{y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}}\Psi(\bm{U}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{\star});y)\,dt

(4.14)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{G}(E)=\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\int_{E}\Psi(\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n})).

(4.15)

Next, recall that by Theorem
4.2
we have

\lim_{j\to\infty}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}(\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu^{j}_{t},\mu^{\star}_{y})+|\bm{H}^{j}(t)-\bm{H}^{\star}(t)|)=0

hence, by
(\mathcal{F}_{1})
,

\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathcal{L}(t,\bm{\mu}^{j},\bm{Y}^{j})=\mathcal{L}(t,\bm{\mu}^{\star},\bm{Y}^{\star}).

\widetilde{\sf P}(Z\in B)={\sf Leb}(\widetilde{Z}\in f^{-1}(B))=\widetilde{\mu}_{t}(f^{-1}(B))=\mu_{t}(B),

i.e.
{\rm Law}(Z)=\mu_{t}
. Let

\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}:=\{Z\in\mathcal{M}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d},\mathcal{B}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}),\widetilde{\sf P}):\ {\rm Law}(Z)\in\mathcal{K}_{t}\}.

Then, by the previous argument,
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}\not=\emptyset
and for all
\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{K}_{t}
there exists
Z\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}
such that
{\rm Law}(Z)=\mu_{t}
. Hence, to prove that
\mathcal{K}_{t}
has uniformly integrable
p
-moments, it is sufficient to show that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}
is uniformly
L^{p}
-integrable, or, equivalently, that
\{|Z|^{p},\ Z\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}\}
is uniformly integrable. However, it is clear that, for any
Z\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}
with
{\rm Law}(Z)=\mu_{t}
for some
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
,

{\sf E}\left[\widetilde{\Phi}_{p}(|Z|;K_{G})\right]\leq\overline{M}_{\widetilde{\Phi}_{p}(\cdot;K_{G})}(T,\bm{\mu})\leq\mathcal{C}(K_{G}),

where the right-hand side of the previous inequality is independent of
Z
. Hence, by the de la Vallée-Poussin Theorem,
\{|Z|^{p},\ Z\in\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{t}\}
is uniformly integrable and then
\mathcal{K}_{t}
has uniformly integrable
p
-moments. By
[
4
, Proposition 7.1.5]
, we know that
\mathcal{K}_{t}
is relatively compact, hence
\mathcal{K}
is equicontinuous, closed and pointwise relatively compact, which in turn implies, by the Arzelá-Ascoli theorem, that
\mathcal{K}
is compact.
∎

Finally, we observe that
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K}_{1}
is a compact subset of
C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))\times C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m})
. Then we are able to describe the behaviour of the field
G_{\bm{u}}
with respect to suitable variations of the control
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
\{\bm{u}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{A}
and
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}
be such that

\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\bm{u}_{j}(s,\bm{\mu})\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}\bm{u}(s,\bm{\mu})\,ds,\quad\forall t\in[0,T],\ \forall\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}.

Then, for any
t\in[0,T]
,
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
and
z\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}

\lim_{j\to\infty}G_{\bm{u}_{j}}[t,\bm{\mu}](z)=G_{\bm{u}}[t,\bm{\mu}](z).

Proof.

Fix
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
,
t\in[0,T]
and
z\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
. Then we have

\displaystyle|G_{\bm{u}_{j}}[t,\bm{\mu}](z)-G_{\bm{u}}[t,\bm{\mu}](z)|

\displaystyle=|\mathfrak{w}[t,\mathcal{S}[\bm{\mu},\bm{u}_{j}]](z)-\mathfrak{w}[t,\mathcal{S}[\bm{\mu},\bm{u}]](z)|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\mathfrak{w}}\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|\mathcal{S}[\bm{\mu},\bm{u}_{j}](s)-\mathcal{S}[\bm{\mu},\bm{u}](s)|.

Taking the limit as
j\to\infty
, we get the statement by Lemma
B.1
.
∎

Appendix C
Other Possible Control Classes

It could be possible, for modelling restrictions, that we can apply controls with some more restrictive assumptions than the ones considered for
\mathcal{A}
. Hence, in this section, we consider three possible subclasses of
\mathcal{A}
. Since the proofs of the existence of the solutions to the related optimal control problems differ from Theorem
1.5
only of some technical details, we will only underline the parts in which the proof are actually different.

C.1.
Controls satisfying the Oscillation Restriction Criterion

First, let us assume that the controls cannot
oscillate too much
. Precisely, we consider the class of admissible controls
\mathcal{A}_{\rm ORC}\subset\mathcal{A}
satisfying the additional condition

(\mathcal{A}_{\rm ORC})

For all compact sets
\mathcal{K}\subset C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
, all measurable sets
A\subseteq[0,T]
and all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a measurable set
B\subset A
such that

\frac{1}{|B|}\int_{B}\sup_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}}\left|\bm{u}(t,\bm{\mu})-\frac{1}{|B|}\int_{B}\bm{u}(s,\bm{\mu})\,ds\right|\,dt<\varepsilon.

Assumption
(\mathcal{A}_{\rm ORC})
can be recognized as a special form of the
Oscillation Reduction Criterion
, introduced in
[
22
]
for real-valued functions and then discussed in
[
24
]
for Bochner integrals. With this assumption, we can actually relax the requirements on the cost functional
\mathcal{F}
. Precisely, we assume that
\mathcal{F}
satisfies
(\mathcal{F}_{0})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{1})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{2})
and

(\mathcal{F}_{\rm ORC})

\Psi:{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
is continuous.

We are interested in the following optimal control problem:

Find
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm ORC}
such that

\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}^{\star}]=\min_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm ORC}}\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}].

Theorem C.1
.

Under
(\mathcal{F}_{0})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{1})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{2})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{\rm ORC})
, Problem
C.1
admits at least a solution.

Proof.

We proceed as in the proof of Theorem
1.5
except that this time
\bm{U}^{n}\to\bm{U}^{\star}
in measure by
[
10
, Theorem 1.4]
. Up to a non-relabelled subsequence, we can assume
\bm{U}^{n}(t)\to\bm{U}^{\star}(t)
in
C(\mathcal{K};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m})
for a.a.
t\in[0,T]
. Moreover, if we denote by
(\bm{\mu}^{n},\bm{Y}^{n})
the solutions of (
4.1
) with controls
\bm{u}^{n}
and
(\bm{\mu}^{\star},\bm{Y}^{\star})
the solution of (
4.1
) with control
\bm{u}^{\star}
, we also know that, by Theorem
4.2
,
\bm{\mu}^{n}\to\bm{\mu}^{\star}
in
C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
. Hence
\lim_{n\to\infty}\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n})=\bm{U}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{\star})
for a.a.
t\in[0,T]
. Furthermore, recall that
\left|\Psi(\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n}))\right|\leq M_{\Psi}
, where
M_{\Psi}
is defined in the proof of Theorem
1.5
. Hence, by dominated convergence, we get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\Psi(\bm{U}^{n}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{n}))\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}\Psi(\bm{U}^{\star}(t)(\bm{\mu}^{\star}))\,dt.

The remainder of the proof is exactly the same sa the one of Theorem
1.5
.
∎

C.2.
Controls with current-state dependence on
\bm{\mu}

It could be the case that the control we want to apply cannot depend on the whole trajectory of
\bm{\mu}
, but only on the current state. To model this case, we consider the class of admissible controls
\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV}\subset\mathcal{A}
satisfying the additional condition:

(\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV})

There exists a function
\bm{u}^{\sharp}:[0,T]\times\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}
such that
\bm{u}(t,\bm{\mu})=\bm{u}^{\sharp}(t,\mu_{t})
for all
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
and

|\bm{u}^{\sharp}(t,\mu)-\bm{u}^{\sharp}(t,\nu)|\leq\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu)

for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
.

We consider then the optimal control problem:

Find
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV}
such that

\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}^{\star}]=\min_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV}}\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}].

Theorem C.2
.

Under
(\mathcal{F}_{0})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{1})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{2})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{3})
, Problem
C.2
admits at least a solution.

Proof.

Recall that, arguing as in Theorem
1.5
, if we consider a minimizing sequence
\{\bm{u}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV}
, then there exists a function
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{1}{|E|}\left|\int_{E}\left(\bm{u}^{n}(t,\bm{\mu})-\bm{u}^{\star}(t,\bm{\mu})\right)\right|\,dt=0.

Hence, to prove the statement, it is sufficient to show that
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV}
. To do this, consider
\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{K}
and observe that, by
(\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV})
, for any
j=1,\dots,m
,
t\in E_{\sf Leb}
and
h>0
small enough,

\frac{1}{h}\left|\int_{t}^{t+h}\left(u_{j}^{n}(s,\bm{\mu})-u_{j}^{\star}(s,\bm{\nu})\right)\,ds\right|\leq\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu_{s},\nu_{s})\,ds.

Taking the limit as
n\to\infty
and then as
h\downarrow 0
, we get

\left|u_{j}^{\star}(t,\bm{\mu})-u_{j}^{\star}(t,\bm{\nu})\right|\leq\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{m}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu_{t},\nu_{t}).

(C.1)

This holds for all
t\in[0,t]
once we assume that
u_{j}^{\star}(t,\cdot)\equiv 0
for
t\not\in E_{\sf Leb}
. Now observe that if
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
, then
\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{K}^{\sharp}
, where

\mathcal{K}^{\sharp}=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}):\ M_{p}(\mu)+M_{\widetilde{\Phi}(\cdot;K_{G})}(\mu)\leq\mathcal{C}(K_{G})\right\}.

On the other hand, if
\mu\in\mathcal{K}^{\sharp}
, then
\bm{\mu}^{\sharp}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
defined as
\mu^{\sharp}_{t}\equiv\mu
for all
t\in[0,t]
belongs to
\mathcal{K}
. Hence, we define
\bm{u}^{\sharp,\star}:\mathcal{K}^{\sharp}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}
as follows

\bm{u}^{\sharp,\star}(t,\mu)=\bm{u}^{\star}(t,\bm{\mu}^{\sharp}).

Then, for all
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
and
t\in[0,T]
, by a simple application of (
C.1
), it must hold

\bm{u}^{\star}(t,\bm{\mu})=\bm{u}^{\sharp,\star}(t,\mu_{t}).

(C.2)

Using McShane’s Extension Theorem as in the proof of Theorem
1.5
, we extend
\bm{u}^{\sharp,\star}(t,\cdot)
to the whole space
\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
and then we define
\bm{u}^{\star}(t,\cdot)
on
C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
by means of (
C.2
). As a consequence,
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV}
, concluding the proof.
∎

C.3.
Controls with separated variables

Among the controls in
\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV}
, one could consider the ones whose dependence on
t
and
\mu_{t}
is
separated
. To do this, let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and fix three positive constants
M_{\bm{h}}
,
M_{\bm{g}}
and
L_{\bm{g}}
. We define the set
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
as the class of couples
(\bm{h},\bm{g})
satisfying the following assumptions:

(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{0})

\bm{h}\in L^{1}([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m\times\ell})
and
\bm{g}\in C(\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d});{{\mathbb{R}}}^{\ell})
.

(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{1})

For all
t\in[0,T]
it holds

|\bm{g}(\delta_{0})|\leq M_{\bm{g}},

where
\bm{\delta}_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
is the Dirac delta measure concentrated on
0\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}
.

(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{2})

It holds

|g_{j}(\mu)-g_{j}(\nu)|\leq\frac{L_{\bm{g}}}{m}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu,\nu),

for all
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d})
,
j=1,\dots,m
and
t\in[0,T]
.

(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{3})

For almost all
t\in[0,T]
it holds

|\bm{h}(t)|\leq M_{\bm{h}},

where
|\bm{h}(t)|
is the Frobenius norm of
\bm{h}(t)
.

Now fix
(\bm{h},\bm{g})\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
and consider the function
\bm{u}:(t,\bm{\mu})\mapsto\bm{h}(t)\bm{g}(\mu_{t})
. Clearly,
\bm{u}
is a Carathéodory map. Furthermore, since the Frobenius norm is sub-multiplicative, for any
t\in[0,T]

|\bm{u}(t,\bm{\delta}_{0})|\leq|\bm{h}(t)||\bm{g}(\delta_{0})|\leq M_{\bm{h}}M_{\bm{g}},

while, denoting by
\bm{h}_{j}
the
j
-th row of
\bm{h}
, for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
,

|u_{j}(t,\bm{\mu})-u_{j}(t,\bm{\nu})|\leq|\bm{h}_{j}(t)||\bm{g}(\mu_{t})-\bm{g}(\nu_{t})|\leq M_{\bm{h}}L_{\bm{g}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mu_{t},\nu_{t}),

hence for
M_{\bm{u}}=M_{\bm{h}}M_{\bm{g}}
and
L_{\bm{u}}=mM_{\bm{h}}L_{\bm{g}}
, we have that
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV}
. Setting
M_{\bm{u}}
and
L_{\bm{u}}
as declared, we can define the call of admissible controls
\mathcal{A}_{\rm SV}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV}
satisfying the additional condition

(\mathcal{A}_{\rm SV})

There exists
(\bm{h},\bm{g})\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
such that
\bm{u}(t,\bm{\mu})=\bm{h}(t)\bm{g}(\mu_{t})
.

We are interested in the following optimal control problem:

Find
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm SV}
such that

\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}^{\star}]=\min_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm SV}}\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}].

Before proceeding with the proof of the existence of a solution for Problem
C.3
, let us notice that, with a similar argument as in the proof of Theorem
C.2
, it is possible to reduce the problem on
\mathcal{A}_{\rm SV}
to an analogous one on
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
. Indeed, consider the function
\Psi^{\prime}:{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m\times\ell}\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{\ell}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
and the functional
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}:\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
defined as follows:

\Psi^{\prime}(\bm{h},\bm{g})=\Psi(\bm{h}\bm{g}),\quad\forall(\bm{h},\bm{g})\in{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m\times\ell}\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{\ell}

(C.3)

and

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}[\bm{h},\bm{g}]=\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}(t,\bm{\mu},\bm{Y})\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\Psi^{\prime}(\bm{h}(t),\bm{g}(\mu_{t}))\,dt,\quad\forall(\bm{h},\bm{g})\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime},

where
(\bm{\mu},\bm{Y})
is the solution of (
4.1
) with control
\bm{u}(t,\bm{\mu})=\bm{h}(t)\bm{g}(\mu_{t})
. Notice that if
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm SV}
, then
\bm{u}(t,\bm{\mu})=\bm{h}(t)\bm{g}(\mu_{t})
for some
(\bm{h},\bm{g})\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}]=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}[\bm{h},\bm{g}]
. On the other hand, if
(\bm{h},\bm{g})\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, then
\bm{u}(t,\bm{\mu})=\bm{h}(t)\bm{g}(\mu_{t})
belongs to
\mathcal{A}_{\rm SV}
and
\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}]=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}[\bm{h},\bm{g}]
. Hence, solving Problem
C.3
is equivalent to solving

Find
(\bm{h}^{\star},\bm{g}^{\star})\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
such that

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}[\bm{h}^{\star},\bm{g}^{\star}]=\min_{(\bm{h},\bm{g})\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}[\bm{u}].

Notice that if we start from Problem
C.3
, then
\Psi^{\prime}
must satisfy (
C.3
) in Problem
C.3
. However, we could directly consider Problem
C.3
and be less restrictive on the dependence of
\Psi^{\prime}
on the separated variables
\bm{h}
and
\bm{g}
. Indeed, we can assume that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
satisfies the following assumptions:

(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{0})

\mathcal{L}:[0,T]\times C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))\times C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{2m})
and
\Psi^{\prime}:{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m\times\ell}\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{\ell}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
are measurable and bounded from below;

(\mathcal{F}_{1})

\mathcal{L}
is continuous in the variables
(\bm{\mu},\bm{Y})
.

(\mathcal{F}_{2})

There exists a function
M_{\mathcal{L}}\in L^{1}(0,T)
such that for a.a.
t\in[0,T]
and all
(\bm{\mu},\bm{Y})\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}

|\mathcal{L}(t,\bm{\mu},\bm{Y})|\leq M_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}(t)

(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{3})

\Psi:{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m\times\ell}\times{{\mathbb{R}}}^{\ell}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
is convex in the first variable and continuous in the second one.

Theorem C.3
.

Problem
C.3
admits at least a solution.

Proof.

Arguing as in Theorem
1.5
, since for all
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
it holds
\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
for any
t\in[0,T]
, solving Problem
C.3
is equivalent to solving

Find
(\bm{h}^{\star},\bm{g}^{\star})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
such that

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}[\bm{h}^{\star},\bm{g}^{\star}]=\min_{(\bm{h},\bm{g})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}[\bm{h},\bm{g}].

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}=\Pi\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
,
\Pi:C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m\times\ell})\times C(\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d});{{\mathbb{R}}}^{\ell})\to C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m\times\ell})\times C(\mathcal{K}^{\prime};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{\ell})
is the
domain restriction map
, i.e. for all
\mu\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
and
t\in[0,T]
it holds
\Pi(\bm{h},\bm{g})(t,\mu)=(\bm{h}(t),\bm{g}(\mu))
, and for all
\Pi(\bm{h},\bm{g})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
it holds

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}[\Pi(\bm{h},\bm{g})]=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}[\bm{h},\bm{g}].

Now consider a minimizing sequence
\{(\bm{h}^{n},\bm{g}^{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
. Since
|\bm{h}^{n}(t)|\leq M_{\bm{h}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and almost all
t\in[0,T]
, then by the Dunford-Pettis theorem (see
[
13
, Theorem 4.30]
) we know that there exists a function
\bm{h}^{\star}\in L^{1}([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m\times\ell})
such that, up to a non-relabelled subsequence,
\bm{h}^{n}\rightharpoonup\bm{h}^{\star}
in
L^{1}([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m\times\ell})
and it is clear, arguing again as in Theorem
1.5
, that
|\bm{h}^{\star}(t)|\leq M_{\bm{h}}
for almost all
t\in[0,T]
. Next, notice that for all
\mu\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}

\left|\bm{g}^{n}(\mu)\right|\leq L_{\bm{g}}(M_{p}(\mu))^{\frac{1}{p}}+M_{\bm{g}}\leq L_{\bm{g}}(\mathcal{C}(K_{G}))^{\frac{1}{p}}+M_{\bm{g}}=:\widetilde{M}_{\bm{g}}.

Hence
\{\bm{g}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C(\mathcal{K}^{\prime};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{\ell})
are equibounded and equiLipschitz (by
(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{2})
). Hence, up to a non-relabelled subsequence, by the Arzelá-Ascoli theorem, there exists
\bm{g}^{\star}\in C(\mathcal{K}^{\prime};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{\ell})
such that
\bm{g}^{n}\to\bm{g}^{\star}
. With the same arguments as in Theorem
1.5
, one can use the McShane extension theorem to extend
\bm{g}^{\star}
to a function in
C(\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d});{{\mathbb{R}}}^{\ell})
whose components are still
\frac{L_{\bm{g}}}{m}
-Lipschitz, hence proving that
(\bm{h}^{\star},\bm{g}^{\star})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
. Furthermore, for all
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
,
t\in[0,T]
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, set
\bm{u}^{n}(t,\bm{\mu})=\bm{h}(t)\bm{g}^{n}(\mu_{t})
and
\bm{u}^{\star}(t,\bm{\mu})=\bm{h}(t)\bm{g}^{\star}(\mu_{t})
. Then, for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\bm{u}^{n}(s,\bm{\mu})\,ds\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\int_{0}^{t}\bm{u}^{\star}(s,\bm{\mu})\,ds\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left|\bm{h}^{n}(s)\right|\left|\bm{g}^{n}(\mu_{s})-\bm{g}^{\star}(\mu_{s})\right|\,ds+\left|\int_{0}^{t}\bm{g}^{\star}(\mu_{s})\left(\bm{h}^{n}(s)-\bm{h}^{\star}(s)\right)\,ds\right|

\displaystyle\leq M_{\bm{h}}T\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{K}}|\bm{g}^{n}(\mu)-\bm{g}^{\star}(\mu)|+\left|\int_{0}^{t}\bm{g}^{\star}(\mu_{s})\left(\bm{h}^{n}(s)-\bm{h}^{\star}(s)\right)\,ds\right|.

Hence, recalling that the function
s\in[0,T]\mapsto\bm{g}^{\star}(\mu_{s})
belongs to
L^{\infty}([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{\ell})
, we can take the limit to get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\bm{u}^{n}(s,\bm{\mu})\,ds-\int_{0}^{t}\bm{u}^{\star}(s,\bm{\mu})\,ds\right|=0.

As a consequence, if we denote by
(\bm{\mu}^{n},\bm{Y}^{n})
the solutions of (
4.1
) with controls
\bm{u}^{n}
and
(\bm{\mu}^{\star},\bm{Y}^{\star})
the solution of (
4.1
) with control
\bm{u}^{\star}
, it holds, by Theorem
4.2
,
(\bm{\mu}^{n},\bm{Y}^{n})\to(\bm{\mu}^{\star},\bm{Y}^{\star})
in
C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))\times C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m})
. Furthermore, notice that

\int_{0}^{T}\Psi^{\prime}(\bm{h}^{n}(t),\bm{g}^{n}(\mu_{t}^{n}))\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\Psi^{\prime}(\bm{h}^{n}(t),\bm{g}^{n}(\mu_{t}^{n}))-\Psi^{\prime}(\bm{h}^{n}(t),\bm{g}^{\star}(\mu_{t}^{\star}))\right)\,dt\\
+\int_{0}^{T}\Psi^{\prime}(\bm{h}^{n}(t),\bm{g}^{\star}(\mu_{t}^{\star}))\,dt.

To handle the first integral, we observe that

\int_{0}^{T}\left|\Psi^{\prime}(\bm{h}^{n}(t),\bm{g}^{n}(\mu_{t}^{n}))-\Psi^{\prime}(\bm{h}^{n}(t),\bm{g}^{\star}(\mu_{t}^{\star}))\right|\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{|\bm{h}|\leq M_{\bm{h}}}\left|\Psi^{\prime}(\bm{h},\bm{g}^{n}(\mu_{t}^{n}))-\Psi^{\prime}(\bm{h},\bm{g}^{\star}(\mu_{t}^{\star}))\right|\,dt

and recall that

t\in[0,T]
and
\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu}\in\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{K}_{n}
,

|u^{\star}_{j}(t,\bm{\mu})-u^{\star}_{j}(t,\bm{\nu})|\leq\frac{L_{\bm{u}}}{md}\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu_{t},\nu_{t}).

(D.17)

The fact that
\bm{u}^{\star}\in(\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV})
follows as in Theorem
C.2
. Furthermore, by (
D.16
), denoting by
(\bm{Z}^{N,j},\bm{Y}^{j}_{N})
and
(\bm{Z}^{N,\star},\bm{Y}^{j}_{N})
the solutions of (
D.8
) with controls
\bm{u}^{j}
and
\bm{u}^{\star}
repsectively and by
\bm{\mu}^{N,j}
and
\bm{\mu}^{N,\star}
the corresponding empirical measures of the followers, we have (
D.9
) and then, up to a subsequence, almost surely

\lim_{j\to\infty}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu^{N,j}_{t},\mu^{N,\star}_{t})+\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\bm{H}_{N}^{j}(t)-\bm{H}_{N}^{\star}(t)|=0.

Hence, by
(\mathcal{F}_{1})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{2})
and the dominated convergence theorem, we have

\lim_{j\to+\infty}{\sf E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}(t,\bm{\mu}^{N,j},\bm{Y}_{N}^{j})\,dt\right]={\sf E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}(t,\bm{\mu}^{N,\star},\bm{Y}_{N}^{\star})\,dt\right].

On the other hand, the same arguments as in Theorem
1.5
and a simple application of Fatou’s Lemma lead to

{\sf E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\Psi(\bm{u}^{N,\star}(t,\bm{\mu}^{N,\star}))\,dt\right]\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}{\sf E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\Psi(\bm{u}^{N,j}(t,\bm{\mu}^{N,j}))\,dt\right].

The remainder of the proof follows as in Theorem
1.5
.
∎

However, it is really difficult to provide (even numerically) a solution of Problem
D
for a big value of
N
. For such a reason, one tries to use a mean field limit approach, justified by Theorem
D.1
, to provide a suitable approximation of the optimal control. Indeed, on (
4.4
), one can consider the optimal control Problem
C.2
, whose solution exists by Theorem
C.2
and is much more affordable, at least from the numerical point of view, since it involves a system of a PDE and
2md
ODEs, as in (
4.1
), in place of a system of
2(N+m)d
SDEs as in (
D.8
). However, it remains to show that a solution of Problem
C.2
actually approximates, in some sense, a solution of Problem
D
. We will do this for a slightly different problem. First we consider the following assumptions, that are more restrictive that the ones we considered in Section
4.2
.

(\mathcal{F}_{0})

\mathcal{L}:[0,T]\times C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))\times C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m})
and
\Psi:{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
are measurable and bounded from below;

(\mathcal{F}_{1}^{N})

There exists a non-negative increasing concave function
\varpi_{\mathcal{L}}:{{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}_{0}^{+}
such that

\left|\mathcal{L}(t,\bm{\mu}^{1},\bm{Y}^{1})-\mathcal{L}(t,\bm{\mu}^{2},\bm{Y}^{2})\right|\leq\varpi_{\mathcal{L}}\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu_{s}^{1},\mu_{s}^{2})+\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|\bm{Y}^{1}(s)-\bm{Y}^{2}(s)|\right)

(\mathcal{F}_{2})

There exists a function
M_{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\in L^{1}(0,T)
such that for a.a.
t\in[0,T]
, for all
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{p}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
and for all
\bm{Y}\in C([0,T];{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m})
,

|\mathcal{L}(t,\bm{\mu},\bm{Y})|\leq M_{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}(t).

(\mathcal{F}_{3})

\Psi:{{\mathbb{R}}}^{m}\to{{\mathbb{R}}}
is convex.

Furthermore, we have to further reduce the set of controls we are working on. Precisely, we define the class of controls
\mathcal{A}_{\rm EVB}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV}
satisfying
(\mathcal{A}_{0})
,
(\mathcal{A}_{2})
,
(\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV})
and

(\mathcal{A}_{B})

For all
t\in[0,T]
and
\bm{\mu}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{W}_{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{2d}))
it holds

|\bm{u}(t,\bm{\mu})|\leq M_{\bm{u}}.

Assumption
(\mathcal{A}_{B})
clearly implies
(\mathcal{A}_{1})
, but it is much more restrictive. We consider then the following optimal control problems:

Find
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EVB}
such that

\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}^{\star}]=\min_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EVB}}\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}].

Find
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EVB}
such that

\mathcal{F}^{N}[\bm{u}^{\star}]=\min_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EVB}}\mathcal{F}^{N}[\bm{u}].

Notice that in both cases the optimal solution
\bm{u}^{\star}
is obtained by considering a minimizing sequence
\{\bm{u}^{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\rm EVB}
such that for any measurable
E\subset[0,T]
and
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{K}
in case of Problem
D
or
\bm{\mu}\in\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{K}_{n}
in case of Problem
D

\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}\bm{u}^{j}(t,\bm{\mu})\,dt=\frac{1}{|E|}\int_{E}\bm{u}^{\star}(t,\bm{\mu})\,dt.

Theorem
C.2
already guarantees that
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EV}
. Furthermore, for all
t\in E_{\sf Leb}
and

small enough

\frac{1}{h}\left|\int_{t}^{t+h}\bm{u}^{j}(t,\bm{\mu})\,dt\right|\leq M_{\bm{u}}.

Hence, taking the limit as
j\to\infty
and then
h\downarrow 0
, one show that
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{EVB}
. As a consequence, we have the following result:

Theorem D.4
.

There exists at least a solution for both Problems
D
and
D
.

Now, we can prove the following result:

Theorem D.5
.

Let
\{\bm{u}^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\rm EVB}
and
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EVB}
be such that for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\bm{\mu}\in\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{K}_{n}

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\bm{u}^{N}(s,\bm{\mu})\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}\bm{u}^{\star}(s,\bm{\mu})\,ds.

Then

\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}^{\star}]\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{F}^{N}[\bm{u}^{N}].

Furthermore for all
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EVB}
it holds

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{F}^{N}[\bm{u}]=\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}].

Finally, it holds

\lim_{N\to\infty}\min_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EVB}}\mathcal{F}^{N}[\bm{u}]=\min_{\bm{u}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EVB}}\mathcal{F}[\bm{u}].

Proof.

Let
\{\bm{u}^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\rm EVB}
and
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{\rm EVB}
as in the statement. Denote by
(\bm{Z}^{N},\bm{H}_{N})
the solutions of (
D.8
) with controls
\bm{u}^{N}
, by
\bm{\mu}^{N}
the empirical measures of the followers
\bm{Z}^{N}
and by
(\overline{\bm{\mu}}^{N},\overline{\bm{Y}}^{N})
the solutions of (
4.1
) with controls
\bm{u}^{N}
. Arguing as in the proof of
[
8
, Theorem 4.6]
and using Theorem
D.1
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left|{\sf E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}\left(t,\bm{\mu}^{N},\bm{Y}_{N}\right)\,dt\right]-\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}\left(t,\overline{\bm{\mu}}^{N},\overline{\bm{Y}}_{N}\right)\,dt\right|=0.

(D.18)

On the other hand, by Theorem
4.2
and Assumptions
(\mathcal{F}_{1}^{N})
(that implies
(\mathcal{F}_{1})
) and
(\mathcal{F}_{2})
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left|\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}\left(t,\overline{\bm{\mu}}^{N},\overline{\bm{Y}}_{N}\right)\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}\left(t,\overline{\bm{\mu}}^{\star},\overline{\bm{Y}}_{\star}\right)\right|=0,

where

Now consider a control
