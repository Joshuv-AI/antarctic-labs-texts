Thyroid functional state-dependent dysbiosis of gut microbiota in Hashimoto’s thyroiditis: a cross-sectional metagenomic profiling study
Authors: Jie Li, Suhang Guo, Hua Yu, Xiaobo Hong, Jing Nie, Hua Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) is a prevalent autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD) closely linked to genetic predisposition and environmental factors. Gut microbiota dysbiosis has recently been implicated as a critical contributor to AITDs’ pathogenesis. Our study aims to systematically investigate the dynamic alterations in gut microbial communities under varying thyroid functional statuses and elucidate their underlying mechanisms.Methods67 HT patients with varying thyroid functional statuses and 23 healthy controls were enrolled. Fecal 16 S rDNA sequencing and analyses (alpha diversity, LEfSe, correlation, functional pathways) assessed microbiota-thyroid function links.ResultsHT patients with hypo/hyperthyroidism had lower gut microbiota richness than euthyroid patients (more reduced in hyperthyroidism). The hyperthyroid group exhibited enrichment of Fusobacterium, the hypothyroid group was dominated by Clostridium sensu stricto_1, and the euthyroid group showed a predominance of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria (e.g., Lactobacillus). Clostridium sensu stricto_1 positively correlated with TPO-Ab levels but negatively correlated with FT3.Pro-inflammatory genera(e.g., Escherichia-Shigella, Streptococcus) demonstrated negative correlations with FT3.Functional prediction analysis revealed potential associations with L-tyrosine degradation in the hyperthyroid group, reduced proportions of bile acid metabolism pathways in the hypothyroid group, and enriched proportions of fatty acid metabolism pathways in the euthyroid group.ConclusionsThis study revealed that gut microbiota dysbiosis is closely associated with thyroid functional statuses in HT. Specific bacterial genera, such as Clostridium sensu stricto_1 and Fusobacterium, may contribute to immune regulation and disease progression. The dynamic alterations in gut microbial profiles provide potential biomarkers for precision diagnosis and treatment of HT.

Introduction

Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT), the most prevalent autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD) leading to hypothyroidism, is characterized by cell-mediated immune responses that drive inflammation and apoptosis, closely associated with elevated autoantibody levels [1]. The pathogenesis of HT involves complex interactions among genetic susceptibility, epigenetic modifications, and environmental triggers. However, the precise mechanisms underlying these processes remain incompletely elucidated. HT demonstrates a striking female predominance, with incidence rates 4–10 times higher in women compared with men [2]. Recent epidemiological trends reveal a rapid escalation in HT prevalence globally. Most HT patients remain clinically asymptomatic, 20–30% develop subclinical or overt hypothyroidism. Intriguingly, a distinct subgroup with characteristic biochemical and ultrasonographic features of HT exhibits persistent hyperthyroidism, occasionally progressing to Graves’ disease(GD) [3].

The gut microbiota constitutes a sophisticated ecosystem within the gastrointestinal tract. Through its interaction with gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), intestinal microbes play a pivotal role in the maturation and regulation of both innate and adaptive immune systems [4]. Notably, compromised immune functionality precipitates diminished intestinal barrier integrity, thereby disrupting microbial homeostasis and fostering dysbiosis [5]. In genetically predisposed individuals, aberrant cross-talk between the gut microbiome and immune system may drive the multifactorial pathogenesis of autoimmune disorders. While dysbiosis has been implicated in various AITDs [6], investigations into HT-specific microbiota interactions remain limited, particularly regarding functional variations across thyroid hormone states [7]. Systematic characterization of these microbial divergences could yield critical insights into the microbiota’s role in HT pathogenesis and progression [8].

This study aims to compare gut microbiota profiles among HT patients with distinct thyroid functional statuses, including euthyroidism, hyperthyroidism, and hypothyroidism. By analyzing microbial composition and diversity, we seek to identify potential microbial biomarkers associated with specific thyroid functional states in HT. These findings may inform novel therapeutic strategies targeting the gut microbiota to improve the clinical management of HT progression.

Inclusion criteria for HT patients: (1) age 18–80 years; (2) elevated serum thyroid peroxidase antibody(TPO-Ab) and/or Thyroglobulin Antibody(Tg-Ab); (3) thyroid ultrasound confirming HT morphology. Participants were categorized by thyroid function parameters into three groups: HTE Group: thyroid stimulating hormone(TSH) below 0.35 mIU/L accompanied by elevated free triiodothyronine(FT3) and free thyroxine (FT4) levels; HTO Group: TSH equal to or exceeding 4.94 mIU/L with reduced FT3 and FT4 levels; HTN Group: TSH, FT3, and FT4 levels within normal reference ranges. The healthy control(HC) group comprised 23 volunteers with normal thyroid function, no personal/family history of thyroid diseases, and negative thyroid autoantibodies. All HT patients underwent standardized medical history inquiry and physical examination. Detailed demographic and clinical parameters, including age, disease duration, smoking history, and other relevant variables, were systematically documented.

Exclusion criteria included: (1)the use of antibiotics, probiotics, or medications affecting gut microbiota(including proton pump inhibitors and antacids) within 1 month prior to sampling; (2)history of gastrointestinal disorders, autoimmune/rheumatic diseases, severe cardiovascular/cerebrovascular diseases, or hepatic/renal/hematopoietic dysfunction; (3) pregnancy or lactation.

Sample collection

Participants fasted overnight for 12 h prior to fasting cubital venous blood collection for routine biochemical parameter analysis. Fresh fecal samples were collected into sterile cryovials and immediately stored at -80 °C pending further microbiota profiling.

Thyroid function and autoantibody testing

Serum FT3, FT4, TSH, TgAb, TPOAb, and Thyrotropin receptor antibody (TRAb) were measured using an iMagic-M7 automated biochemical analyzer (Cubbio Biotechnology, China). Reference ranges: FT3 2.43–6.01 pmol/L; FT4 9.01–19.05 pmol/L; TSH 0.35–4.94 mIU/L; TRAb ≤ 1.22 IU/L; TPOAb ≤ 5.61 IU/mL; TgAb ≤ 4.11 IU/mL.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Fecal genomic DNA was extracted using a BioTeke kit (AU46111-96) following manufacturer protocols, quantified via Qubit (Invitrogen). The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of bacterial 16 S rDNA genes were amplified with primers 341 F/805R under PCR conditions: 98 °C for 30 s(initial denaturation); 32 cycles of 98 °C (10 s), 54 °C (30 s), 72 °C (45 s); final extension at 72 °C (10 min). Purified PCR products (AMPure XT Beads) were quantified, assessed for quality (Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer/Kapa Biosciences kit), and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (PE250) by LC Bio Technology (Hangzhou, China).

Data processing and 16S rDNA analysis

Raw sequences were demultiplexed (cutadapt v1.9), merged (FLASH v1.2.8), and filtered (fqtrim v0.94) to remove low-quality reads (< Q20, < 100 bp, or > 5% N bases). Chimeric sequences were eliminated using Vsearch (v2.3.4). DADA2 [9](Callahan et al., 2016) generated amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), annotated against SILVA/NT-16 S databases via QIIME2 [10]. Alpha diversity (Shannon, Ace, Simpson, Chao) and beta diversity (PCoA) were calculated. Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size(LEfSe) [11] identified differentially abundant genera (LDA > 3.0, P < 0.05). Spearman correlations linked microbial taxa to clinical parameters. False discovery rate (FDR) correction via the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method was applied to the resulting P-values, with a Q-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. In order to predict the potential function of the flora, we used PICRUSt2 [12] software to infer the MetaCyc/KEGG pathways based on the 16 S rDNA gene data.

Results

α-diversity significantly decreases in HTO and HTE groups

HTN group exhibited the most unique ASVs, with 3026 taxa accounting for 34.47%. In comparison, HTE group had 1610 unique ASVs (18.34%), and HTO group had 1428 unique ASVs(16.27%)(Fig. 1A). α-Diversity metrics indicated elevated richness and diversity in HTN group versus healthy controls. HC, HTO and HTE groups all exhibited reduced richness relative to HTN group (Chao1 P < 0.05,Fig. 1B). HTE group showed significantly lower diversity than HTN group (Shannon P < 0.05,Fig. 1C).

Functional thyroid impairment associates with distinct gut dysbiosis signatures

We observed some subtle but significant differences in the relative abundance of gut microbiota. At the phylum level, compared with the HC group, the abundance of Firmicutes was significantly increased in the HTN group but markedly decreased in the HTO group; the abundance of Actinobacteriota was significantly elevated in the HTO group. Proteobacteria showed an increasing trend from healthy controls, Hashimoto’s patients with normal thyroid function, to all patients with thyroid dysfunction. Compared to the HC group, the HTN group showed changes in gut microbiota structure, while HT patients with thyroid dysfunction exhibited a more pronounced pattern of dysbiosis.(Fig. 2A).

Taxonomic characterization of gut microbiota across different thyroid function states. (A) Phylum-level gut microbiota composition profiles across different groups, visualized by stacked bar charts.(B) Taxonomic bubble plot showing the relative abundance distribution at the genus level in HC, HTN, HTO, and HTE groups. Different colors represent distinct phyla, and bubble size is proportional to relative abundance

At the genus level, compared to the HTN and HC groups, the abundance of Bifidobacterium in the HTE and HTO groups was significantly increased, while the abundance of Lactobacillus and Blautia was decreased. In addition, the gut microbiota of HT patients with hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism exhibited different trends of change. In the HTE group, the abundance of Streptococcus, Clostridium sensu stricto_1, and Agathobacter decreased, while the abundance of pro-inflammatory Escherichia−Shigella [13] and antibiotic-resistant Stenotrophomonas [14] increased. Compared to the HTN group, the abundance of Ligilactobacillus in the HTO group decreased, while the abundance of Streptococcus and Klebsiella increased.(Fig. 2B).

Each thyroid functional state harbors unique signature microbial genera

LEfSe analysis revealed significant microbial characteristic differences between the HTN, HTE and HTO groups. Clostridium sensu stricto_1 was identified as the key biomarker to distinguish HTO group from other groups, and this genus has been suggested to potentially participate in bile acid metabolism [15]. The HTE group was characterized by the significant enrichment of Fusicatenibacter, which is associated with short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production and oxidative stress responses [16]. In contrast, the HTN group exhibited a more diverse microbial composition, with Firmicutes, Lactobacillus,Ligilactobacillus, Escherichia−Shigella,HT002 and Agathobacter as the dominant groups, suggesting a potential link between microbial diversity and thyroid homeostasis(Fig. 3).

Autoimmune antibodies and thyroid hormones correlate with specific gut taxa

As shown in Fig. 4A, Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant associations between autoimmune antibodies, thyroid hormones, and specific gut microbial taxa in HT patients. Clostridium sensu stricto_1 exhibited dual clinical correlations: a positive correlation with TPO-Ab levels(r = 0.21, P < 0.05) and a significant negative correlation with FT3 concentrations(r = − 0.29, P < 0.01). This bidirectional relationship suggests potential mechanistic links between this microbial taxon and autoimmune activation alongside thyroid hormone metabolism.

Gut microbiota metabolic pathways diverge by thyroid function

PICRUSt2-based functional prediction analyses identified marked differences in the predicted microbial metabolic pathways among HT patients with distinct thyroid functional statuses.Proportions of the predicted “fatty acid salvage"and “Stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid and gingerol biosynthesis” pathways were significantly higher in the HTN group than in the other groups, suggesting that the intestinal flora of the patients in this group may compensatorily increase the functional expression of fatty acid acquisition and utilization, bile acid production, and inhibition of inflammation and oxidation (P < 0.05). In addition, the HTE group had a higher proportion of the predicted “L-tyrosine degradation I” and “Renin-angiotensin system” pathways, which may be related to tyrosine utilization or oxidative stress during thyroid hormone synthesis. The gut flora has greater genetic potential in regulating metabolic pathways related to cardiovascular function, which may be related to the increased risk of cardiovascular complications in patients with hyperthyroidism [17]. (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B-C).

Discussion

Our study analyzed gut microbiota composition in HT patients with hypothyroidism, euthyroidism, and hyperthyroidism. Compared to healthy controls, euthyroid HT patients had higher microbial richness and alpha diversity. Notably, thyroid dysfunction (hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism) was linked to reduced richness and diversity. In our prior research, it was also concluded that the richness and diversity of the intestinal microbiota increased in HT patients with euthyroidism. Some studies have reported findings consistent with this experiment [18]. However, the majority of current studies indicate that hypothyroidism is associated with excessive bacterial growth in the intestine, as gastrointestinal motility disorders and increased permeability create a more favorable environment for bacterial colonization [19]. These discrepancies might be attributed to differences in sample size and geographic location. Previous studies have shown that gut microbiota composition varies significantly across geographic regions, with regional differences explaining up to 17.9% of the total gut microbial variation [20]. Additionally, most studies reporting reduced microbial diversity in HT patients have relatively small sample sizes and lack detailed stratification of thyroid function status [21], which may have confounded the observed associations.

In HT patients with either hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism, an elevated relative abundance of Bifidobacterium was observed, alongside reduced abundances of Lactobacillus and Blautia.Liu et al. reported higher Bifidobacterium levels in HT patients compared to healthy controls, and this difference increased as the disease progressed [19]. Furthermore, a separate study on GD identified a positive correlation between Bifidobacterium abundance and serum levels of thyroid-related autoantibodies, including TRAb, TG-Ab, and TPO-Ab [22]. Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus are widely recognized as probiotic genera that promote gut health [23]. These bacteria may contribute to preserving intestinal barrier integrity through mechanisms such as stimulating mucus secretion and strengthening tight junctions. Additionally, they may modulate immune responses by inducing the differentiation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and fostering a balanced gut microbiota via selective promotion of beneficial species while suppressing pathogenic overgrowth [24]. HT patients exhibiting functional impairments display a significant reduction in the relative abundance of these probiotic taxa within their gut microbiomes [25]. Blautia has emerged as a novel probiotic genus identified in recent years for its ability to stimulate intestinal mucus production. Studies have demonstrated significant associations between Blautia abundance and various metabolic and inflammatory disorders [26]. Mechanistically, Blautia may potentially enhance gut barrier function by activating the free fatty acid receptor 2 through metabolites such as propionate and butyrate, thereby promoting mucus secretion. This process not only strengthens intestinal integrity but also mitigates pathogenic bacterial invasion [27]. Prior studies have proposed Blautia as a potential biomarker to differentiate healthy individuals from patients with GD and HT [28]. Building on our experimental findings, we hypothesize that Blautia may serve as a critical indicator of thyroid dysfunction during the progression of HT. In the early stages of HT, pro-inflammatory bacterial taxa may dominate the gut microbiota [29]. However, as the disease progresses, the host may modulate gut microbiota composition by secreting cytokines or hormones, and increase the abundance of beneficial taxa to restore intestinal microbial equilibrium [30]. Consequently, fluctuations in these bacterial populations—whether increases or decreases—could reflect adaptive mechanisms [31]. Nonetheless, the precise patterns and implications of such microbial shifts require further investigation to elucidate their role in HT pathogenesis [32].

In euthyroid patients with HT, the gut microbiota is predominantly composed of beneficial bacteria that produce SCFAs, such as Lactobacillus, Ligilactobacillus, Agathobacter and HT002. SCFAs not only protect intestinal barrier integrity by affecting the expression of Mucin 2 mRNA levels but also play a key role in the immune response through their interactions with the G-protein-coupled receptor and histone deacetylase on immune cells, regulating the functions of various immune cells including Treg, which may further contribute to chronic thyroid inflammation and autoantibody production [33]. Some are conditionally pathogenic, such as Escherichia-Shigella and Coprococcus, which suggest a tendency to evoke inflammatory responses when the host immune system is compromised or when the intestinal microecology is imbalanced [29, 30], while a very small number of them are deleterious, such as Clostridium spp., which are associated with a wide range of inflammatory diseases [32], and may serve as markers to differentiate HT from healthy populations [34].

In hypothyroid patients with HT, the gut microbiota was significantly enriched with Clostridium sensu stricto_1. Correlation analyses revealed a significant positive association between TPO-Ab levels and the abundance of Clostridium sensu stricto_1, while FT3 levels exhibited negative correlations with both Clostridium sensu stricto_1 and Escherichia-Shigella. These findings suggest that shifts in the abundance of these taxa may serve as indicators of disease progression in HT. Studies suggest it exerts detrimental regulatory effects on human health in certain disease contexts [35]. Zhao et al. [28] proposed its potential role as a biomarker for distinguishing patients with AITDs from healthy populations. Furthermore, Clostridium sensu stricto_1 has been identified as a signature microbiota in immune-related disorders such as psoriatic arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis [36]. A prior study investigating gut microbiota dysbiosis in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) via the oral-gut axis identified Clostridium sensu stricto_1 and Escherichia-Shigella as key contributors to IBD pathogenesis [37].Recent studies indicate that the abundance of Clostridium sensu stricto_1 is positively correlated with serum pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, 5-HT, TNF-α, IFN-γ) and negatively correlated with anti-inflammatory factors (IL-10, IL-22) [38]. In HT, elevated IL-4 levels may enhance Th2-mediated immune responses by activating eosinophils and promoting mast cell proliferation, hinting at a possible involvement in chronic thyroid inflammation and autoantibody production [39]. Notably, IFN-γ and TNF-α levels have been mechanistically linked to increased TPO-Ab titers in HT [40].Our study indicated that the abundance of Clostridium sensu stricto_1 in HT patients at the euthyroid stage was higher than that in the hypothyroid group. Combined with its pro-inflammatory potential demonstrated in other diseases, this genus is speculated to be one of the initial dysbiotic factors triggering the breakdown of immune tolerance. At the early euthyroid stage, the robust autoimmune response and relatively normal intestinal peristalsis jointly promoted the massive proliferation of this genus; after progression to the hypothyroid stage, the decline in host metabolic activity and intestinal peristaltic function altered the intestinal microenvironment, which in turn restricted the growth of this genus [41]. Furthermore, functional prediction analysis revealed a decreased proportion of bile acid metabolic pathways in the HTO group, and this metabolic shift may represent a key factor driving the reduced abundance of this genus. A study by Hernandez JB et al. confirmed that the abundance of Clostridium sensu stricto_1 was significantly correlated with the downregulation of taurine and bile acid levels, and clarified that this genus can regulate the gut microbiota by modifying bile acid metabolism and is highly sensitive to changes in the intestinal bile acid pool [42]. It should be noted that the above speculations are based on published findings from studies on other autoimmune diseases. However, the specific associative mechanisms and potential causal relationships among Clostridium sensu stricto_1, cytokines, metabolites and thyroid function in HT patients remain unclear, and these aspects still require systematic verification via research strategies including animal experiments, integrated multi-omics and mediation analyses.

In HT patients with hyperthyroidism, the gut microbiota is predominantly colonized by Fusicatenibacter. Fusobacterium is characterized as Gram-positive, obligately anaerobic, non-motile, and non-spore-forming, with metabolic byproducts including lactate, formate, acetate, and succinate. Cao et al. [43] identified a positive correlation between Fusobacterium abundance and the risk of developing Sjögren’s syndrome. Additionally, elevated levels of this genus have been linked to the pathogenesis of multiple malignancies, including thyroid cancer (TC) [44], hepatocellular carcinoma [45], and lung adenocarcinoma [46]. Notably, HT, a known risk factor for TC, frequently coexists with thyroid cancer [47]. Proposed mechanisms for this association include thyroid lymphocytic infiltration, elevated levels of TPO-Ab and TG-Ab, or increased TSH levels—even within the normal reference range [2]. Rodis et al. reported that the association between HT and TC risk was observed only in euthyroid or mildly hypothyroid HT patients, whereas overt hypothyroidism appeared to correlate with a reduced risk [48].Hyperthyroidism induced by HT is a type of destructive hyperthyroidism, which is relatively less severe than that caused by GD and is self-limiting. Changes in intestinal flora in such patients have not been reported.

Our research has several limitations that warrant improvement in future investigations. The most significant issue is the relatively small sample size, which may amplify the influence of random variability on the results and limit the explanatory power of the constructed models. To address this, we plan to extend the data collection period and recruit a larger cohort in subsequent studies to enhance the reliability and generalizability of our findings. HT’s hyperthyroidism has a relatively short disease duration, and the time window for sampling may thus affect the results. In future studies, efforts should be made to collect samples as early as possible during the disease onset.As a cross-sectional study, the research has an inherent limitation in its inability to establish a definitive causal relationship between thyroid function and the gut microbiota. Although the interfering effects of medications including levothyroxine and antibiotics on gut microbiota analysis have been eliminated, uncontrolled potential confounding factors such as dietary patterns and defecation frequency may exert a certain impact on the study outcomes. Large-scale longitudinal cohort studies are warranted in future to systematically elucidate the independent regulatory effects of these confounding factors on the gut microbiota. In addition, there are differences in the bacterial flora colonizing different parts of the gastrointestinal tract, and in addition to fecal samples, gastrointestinal mucosa and saliva samples can also be collected to analyze the intestinal flora, and it is necessary to further explore which method can draw more valuable conclusions. For more in-depth studies, we should use more advanced techniques, such as whole genome sequencing and metabolomics, to explore all aspects of evolution at the molecular level.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates thyroid function-dependent gut microbiota dysbiosis in HT patients. Euthyroid HT patients exhibit higher microbial richness with enrichment of SCFA-producing probiotics, while hypothyroid and hyperthyroid patients show reduced diversity and distinct pathogenic genera (Clostridium sensu stricto_1, Fusicatenibacter). Additionally, functional prediction analysis revealed marked divergences in the predicted microbial metabolic pathways among HT patients with different thyroid functional statuses. These findings highlight the gut-thyroid axis’ role in HT progression, providing potential microbial biomarkers for precision diagnosis and targeted therapies.
