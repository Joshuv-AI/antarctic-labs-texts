Direct Distributional Optimization for Provable Alignment of Diffusion Models
Ryotaro Kawata, Kazusato Oko, Atsushi Nitanda, Taiji Suzuki

Abstract
We introduce a novel alignment method for diffusion models from distribution optimization perspectives while providing rigorous convergence guarantees. We first formulate the problem as a generic regularized loss minimization over probability distributions and directly optimize the distribution using the Dual Averaging method. Next, we enable sampling from the learned distribution by approximating its score function via Doob’s h h -transform technique. The proposed framework is supported by rigorous convergence guarantees and an end-to-end bound on the sampling error, which imply that when the original distribution’s score is known accurately, the complexity of sampling from shifted distributions is independent of isoperimetric conditions. This framework is broadly applicable to general distribution optimization problems, including alignment tasks in Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and Kahneman-Tversky Optimization (KTO). We empirically validate its performance on synthetic and image datasets using the DPO objective.

1
Introduction

Diffusion models
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015
;
Ho et al., 2020
;
Song et al., 2021
)
have recently emerged as powerful tools for learning complex distributions and performing efficient sampling. Within the framework of foundation models, a common approach involves pre-training on large-scale datasets, followed by adapting the model to downstream tasks or aligning it with human preferences
(
Ouyang et al., 2022
)
. This alignment is typically formalized as nonlinear distribution optimization, with a regularization term that encourages proximity to the pre-trained distribution. Examples of such alignment methods include Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF)
(
Ziegler et al., 2020
)
, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
;
Wallace et al., 2024
)
, and Kahneman-Tversky Optimization (KTO)
(
Ethayarajh et al., 2024
;
Li et al., 2024a
)
.

Specifically, there methods solve the minimization problems of a regularized functional
F(q)+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})
over
q
in the probability space
\mathcal{P}
, where
p_{\rm{ref}}
is the probability density corresponding to the pretrained diffusion model.
However, this type of distributional optimization problem over the density
q
is challenging because the output density governed by the reference model cannot be evaluated and neither is the aligned model
q
.
They are accessible only through samples generated from their corresponding generative models.
Existing distributional optimization methods such as
mean-field Langevin dynamics

(
Mei et al., 2018
)
and
particle dual averaging

(
Nitanda et al., 2021
)
resolved this problem by adapting a Langevin type sampling procedure to calculate a functional derivative of the objective without explicitly evaluating the densities.
However, the distributions
q
and
p_{\rm{ref}}
are highly complex and multimodal from which it is extremely hard to generate data by a standard MCMC type methods including the Langevin dynamics.
This difficulty can be mathematically characterized by isoperimetric conditions, such as logarithmic Sobolev inequality (LSI)
(
Bakry et al., 2014
)
, that has usually exponential dependency on the data dimension
d
for multimodal data yielding the curse of dimensionality.
Unfortunately, the existing distribution optimization methods mentioned above are sensitive to the LSI constant, so they suffer from severely slow convergence, failing to align diffusion models.

That is to say, alignment of diffusion models has two challenges: (i) inaccessibility of the output densities and (ii) muitimodality of the densities. This naturally leads to a fundamental question:

Can we develop an alignment algorithm for diffusion models from distribution optimization perspectives, while ensuring rigorous convergence guarantees without isoperimetric conditions?

We address this question by
developing a diffusion-model based distribution optimization method and providing rigorous convergence and sampling error guarantees, and demonstrate its applicability to several tasks involved with diffusion model alignment.
Our method represents the aligned model by a diffusion model that can be described by merely adding a correction term to the score function of the original reference model. During optimizing the model, we don’t rely on any MCMC sampler but only use samples generated by the original reference model (and the aligned diffusion model). This characteristics is helpful to resolve the issue of isoperimetric condition.

1.1
Our Contributions

To tackle the two challenges mentioned above: absence of (i) sampling guarantee and (ii) isoperimetry, we propose a general framework that integrates dual averaging (DA) method
(
Nesterov, 2009
)
and diffusion model
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015
;
Ho et al., 2020
;
Song et al., 2021
)
.
The DA method is an iterative algorithm that constructs the Gibbs distribution converging to the optimal distribution (i.e., alignment).
A key advantage of this DA scheme is its ability to bypass isoperimetric conditions with the help of high sampling efficiency of the reference diffusion model, enabling isoperimetry-free sampling from an aligned distribution. Specifically, an aligned diffusion process that approximately generates the Gibbs distribution obtained by DA method can be constructed through the Doob’s
h
-transform
(
Rogers & Williams, 2000
)
and density ratio estimation with respect to a reference distribution using neural networks (see Figure
1
for illustration).

We summarize our contribution below.

•

We establish a model alignment method to align diffusion models, with convergence guarantees for both convex and nonconvex objectives, based on distribution optimization theory and Doob’s
h
-transform.
Notable distinctions from other mean-field optimization methods are that our method works without isoperimetry conditions such as LSI
and allows for sampling from multimodal distributions.

•

We also analyze the sampling error due to the approximation of the drift estimators with neural networks and the discretization of the process, and evaluate how these errors affect the final sampling accuracy.

•

Our general framework encompasses several major alignment problems such as RLHF, DPO, and KTO, establishing a provable alignment method for these scenarios. We demonstrate the applicability of our framework to these settings and empirically validate its performance on both synthetic and image datasets, aiming at data augmentation for a specific mode of distribution, using DPO objective.

We also emphasize that our method has the potential to be applied to general distribution optimization problems beyond alignment tasks such as density ratio estimation under the covariate shift setting
(
Sugiyama et al., 2008
;
Tsuboi et al., 2009
)
and climate change tracking
(
Ling et al., 2024
)
.

Related work.

Mean-field optimization.
PDA method
(
Nitanda et al., 2021
;
Nishikawa et al., 2022
)
, an extension of DA method
(
Nesterov, 2009
)
to the distribution optimization setting, was the first method that proves the quantitative convergence for minimizing entropy regularized convex functional. Subsequently, P-SDCA method
(
Oko et al., 2022
)
, inspired by the SDCA method
(
Shalev-Shwartz & Zhang, 2013
)
, achieved the linear convergence rate. Mean-field Langevin dynamics
(
Mei et al., 2018
)
is the most standard particle-based distribution optimization method, derived as the mean-field limit of the noisy gradient descent, and its convergence rate has been well studied by
Mei et al. (2018)
;
Hu et al. (2021)
;
Nitanda et al. (2022)
;
Chizat (2022)
;
Suzuki et al. (2023)
;
Nitanda (2024)
. Additionally, several mean-field optimization methods such mean-field Fisher-Rao gradient flow
(
Liu et al., 2023b
)
and entropic fictitious play
(
Chen et al., 2023a
;
Nitanda et al., 2023
)
have been proposed with provable convergence guarantees. We note that the convergence rates of these methods were established under isoperimetric conditions such as log-Sobolev and Poincaré inequalities, which ensure concentration of the probability mass.
IKLPD method
(
Yao et al., 2024
)
shares similarities with our method, as it employs the normalizing flow to solve intermediate subproblems in the distributional optimization procedure, and its convergence does not depend on isoperimetric conditions. However, the applicability of IKLPD to alignment tasks remains uncertain since handling the proximity to the reference distribution is non-trivial.

Fine-tuning of diffusion models.
Recently, alignment of diffusion models have been investigated, inspired by LLM fine-tune methods, such as RLHF
(
Ziegler et al., 2020
)
, DPO
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
, and KTO
(
Ethayarajh et al., 2024
)
. Applying them to diffusion models entails additional difficulty since the output density
p_{\rm{ref}}
of the diffusion model is not available, and hence several techniques have been developed to circumvent the explicit calculation of
p_{\rm{ref}}
.
For instance,
Fan et al. (2023)
;
Black et al. (2024)
;
Clark et al. (2024)
invented the maximization algorithm of the reward in each diffusion time step.
Uehara et al. (2024b)
used Doob’s
h
-transform to compute the correction term that can be automatically derived from the density ratio between the generated and the reference distributions. Instead of optimizing original DPO and KTO objectives,
Wallace et al. (2024)
considered the evidence lower bound (ELBO) and
Li et al. (2024a)
defined a new objective function to replicate KTO.

Marion et al. (2024)
also studied fine-tuning of diffusion models as distributional optimization within the RLHF framework and conducted convergence analysis for the one-dimensional Gaussian distribution.

2
Problem Setting

Distributional Optimization.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be the space of probability density functions with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
Let
F:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}
be a functional and
p_{\rm{ref}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
be the reference density.
In this work, we consider the regularized loss minimization problem over
\mathcal{P}
:

\underset{q\in\mathcal{P}}{\min}\left\{L(q)\coloneq F(q)+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})\right\},

(2)

where
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log\frac{q}{p_{\rm{ref}}}]
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence, and
\beta>0
is a regularization coefficient.
We assume that
F
is differentiable. That is, the functional
F
has
first order variation

\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}:\mathcal{P}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni(q,x)\to\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q,x)\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
,

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}F(q+\epsilon(q^{\prime}-q))}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}=\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q,x)(q^{\prime}-q)(x)\mathrm{d}x.

(3)

In the following we assume that there exists a unique minimizer
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}:=\argmin_{q\in{\mathcal{P}}}L(q)
.

Diffusion Models.

p_{\rm{ref}}
is the output density of a pre-trained diffusion model
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015
;
Song & Ermon, 2019
;
Ho et al., 2020
;
Song et al., 2021
;
Vahdat et al., 2021
)
, while

p_{*}\in\mathcal{P}
is the target distribution of pre-training.
A “noising” process
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
denotes the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process from
p_{*}(x)
.
The law of
\bar{X}_{t}
can be written as
p_{t}(x)=\int\mathcal{N}(m_{t}\bar{X}_{0},\sigma_{t})\mathrm{d}p_{*}(\bar{X}_{0})
with
m_{t}=e^{-t},\sigma_{t}^{2}=1-e^{-2t}
.
Then, the reverse process
\{\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
(
T\geq 0
) can be defined as

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}\sim p_{t},\ \mathrm{d}\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=\{\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}+2\nabla\log p_{T-t}(\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow})\}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t}.

(4)

Then it holds that
\mathrm{Law}(\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow})=\mathrm{Law}(\bar{X}_{T-t})
, which enables us to sample from
p_{*}
.
In practice, we approximate the score
\nabla\log p_{T-t}(\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow})
function by a score network

s:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}:(x,t)\mapsto s(x,t)
.
In addition, we initialize
X^{\leftarrow}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})\simeq p_{T}
and the process is time-discretized.
The random variable generated by the following dynamics with step size
h
is denoted by
\{X^{\leftarrow}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
.

X^{\leftarrow}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),\ \mathrm{d}X^{\leftarrow}_{t}=\{X^{\leftarrow}_{lh}+2s(X^{\leftarrow}_{lh},lh)\}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},\ t\in[lh,(l+1)h],\;l=1,...,L=T/h.

(5)

In the same way, we define
q_{t}
as the density of the diffusion process corresponding to
q_{*}
, and
\{{\bar{X}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{{\bar{X}^{\leftarrow}}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
as the corresponding noising and the backward process.

Now we have two challenges in this problem (
2
):

(A).

Multimodality of
p_{\rm{ref}}
and
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}
.
Our goal is to obtain samples from
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}
.
Now we tackle with the case that
p_{\rm{ref}}
and
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}
have high
multimodality
:
p_{\rm{ref}}
and
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}
have multiple modes or maxima, in other words, the potentials
-\log p_{\rm{ref}}
and
-\log\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}
are extremely far from concave. Probability distributions of real-world data like images often have such complex structures, which implies that LSI is significantly weak.

(B).

Inaccesibility to the density
p_{\rm{ref}}
.
We cannot directly calculate the density
p_{\rm{ref}}
because diffusion models only have the score network. We only have information of
p_{\rm{ref}}
as samples from
p_{\rm{ref}}
.

Inapplicability of Mean-Field Langevin Dynamics.
Mean-field Langevin dynamics (MFLD)
(
Mei et al., 2018
)
is the most standard particle-based optimization method tailored to solve the special case of the problem (
2
) where
F
is convex functional and
p_{\rm{ref}}
is a strongly log-concave distribution such as Gaussian distribution. The convergence rate of MFLD
(
Nitanda et al., 2022
;
Chizat, 2022
)
has been established under the condition where the proximal distribution:
p_{q}\propto p_{\rm{ref}}\exp{\left(-\beta^{-1}\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q,\cdot)\right)}
associated with MFLD iteration
q
satisfies logarithmic Sobolev inequality (LSI), which says sufficient concentration of
p_{q}
. Typically, LSI for
p_{q}
should rely on the isoperimetry of the reference distribution
p_{\rm{ref}}
with Holley-Strook argument
(
Holley & Stroock, 1987
)
since
\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q,\cdot)
is not expected to encourage it in general. Therefore, the replacement of
p_{\rm{ref}}
to a pre-trained distribution, which is highly complex and has multi-modality, leads to failure or weak satisfaction of LSI. As a result, the convergence of MFLD is significantly slowed down.
Additionally, MFLD is practically implemented so that finite particles approximately follow the ideal dynamics of MFLD, and it is not intended for resampling from the final distribution represented by these particles. In short, the lack of (i) a sampling guarantee and (ii) a isoperimetric condition limits the applicability of MFLD in our problem setting.

To address these challenges, we employ the following strategy, which will be detailed in Section
4
.

(A).

Modifications of diffusion models for sampling from a multimodal distribution.
First, we obtain samples from
p_{\rm{ref}}
using a diffusion model which works for a broad range of probability distributions that have complex structures such as multimodality.
Then, we reconstruct the diffusion model to sample from

\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}
by simply adding a correction term.

(B).

A distributional optimization algorithm that does not require the density
p_{\rm{ref}}
.
Second, following the dual averaging (DA) method in the distribution optimization setting, we constract a sequence of distributions
\hat{q}^{(k)}
converging to the optimum, which guides an aligned diffusion process in combination with the density ratio estimation using neural networks with Doob’s
h
-transform.
We only have to calculate the density ratio between
\hat{q}^{(k)}
and
p_{\rm{ref}}
and the samples from
p_{\rm{ref}}
to run our algorithm.

Our framework of the distributional optimization for the pre-trained diffusion models
includes important fine-tuning methods for diffusion models.
The first term
F(q)
in (

(Reinforcement Learning)

(6)

where

is a reward function. In this case, the optimal distribution is obtained as

\textstyle q_{*}(x)\propto\exp\left(\frac{r(x)}{\beta}\right)p_{\rm{ref}}(x)
.
This type of the problems has been studied as the Reinforcement Learning

Example 2

(DPO)
.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
is an effective approach for learning from human preference for not only language models but also diffusion models.
Our algorithm can directly minimize the DPO objective,
while
Wallace et al. (2024)
tried applying DPO to diffusion models via minimization of an upper bound of the original objective.
In DPO, humans decide which sample is more preferred given two samples from
p_{\rm{ref}}
. Let
x_{w}
and
x_{l}
be “winning” and “losing” samples from
p_{\rm{ref}}
.
x_{w}\succ x_{l}
denote the event that
x_{w}
is preferred to
x_{l}
.
The DPO objective can be written as

\textstyle L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(q)\coloneq-\mathbb{E}_{x_{w}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{l}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\gamma\log\frac{q(x_{w})}{p_{\rm{ref}}(x_{w})}-\gamma\log\frac{q(x_{l})}{p_{\rm{ref}}(x_{l})}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{x_{w}\succ x_{l}}(x_{w},x_{l})\right],

(7)

where
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}
denotes expectation with respect to
x
whose probability density is
p\in\mathcal{P}
,
\sigma
is a sigmoid function,
\mathbbm{1}_{x\succ y}(x,y)
is one if
x\succ y
and is zero otherwise.
Precisely, the functional derivative of
L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(q)
is calculated as

\displaystyle\textstyle\frac{\delta L_{\mathrm{DPO}}}{\delta q}(q,x)=

\displaystyle\textstyle-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{x_{l}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\left(1-\sigma\left(-\gamma f(x)+\gamma f(x_{l})\right)\right)\frac{\int e^{-f}dp_{\mathrm{ref}}}{e^{-f(x)}}\mathbbm{1}_{x\succ x_{l}}(x,x_{l})\right]

(8)

\displaystyle\textstyle+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{x_{w}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\left(1-\sigma\left(-\gamma f(x_{w})+\gamma f(x)\right)\right)\frac{\int e^{-f}dp_{\mathrm{ref}}}{e^{-f(x)}}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{w}\succ x}(x_{w},x)\right],

(9)

where
q=e^{-f}p_{\rm{ref}}/\int e^{-f}\mathrm{d}p_{\rm{ref}}
. See Appendix
B
for the derivation.
Therefore, we only need samples from
p_{\rm{ref}}
and the log-density ratio or the potential
f
to calculate the functional derivatives.

Example 3

(KTO)
.

Our algorithm can also minimize
L_{\mathrm{KTO}}
directly.
Assume that the whole data space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is split into a desirable domain
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{D}}
and an undesirable domain
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{U}}
.
The objective of the original KTO

)
is formulated as

\displaystyle\textstyle L_{\mathrm{KTO}}(q)=

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\rm{ref}}}\left[\gamma_{D}\left(1-\sigma\left(\kappa\log\frac{q}{p_{\rm{ref}}}-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})\right)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{D}}\}}\right.

(10)

\displaystyle\textstyle\quad\left.+\gamma_{U}\left(1-\sigma\left(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})-\kappa\log\frac{q}{p_{\rm{ref}}}\right)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{U}}\}}\right],

(11)

where
\gamma_{D},\;\gamma_{U},\;\kappa
are hyper parameters, and
\sigma
is a sigmoid function.

Li et al. (2024a)
defined objectives compatible with diffusion models based on KTO, but our algorithm can directly minimize
L_{\mathrm{KTO}}
.
Like the DPO objective, we only have to calculate samples from
p_{\rm{ref}}
and the potential
f
of the density ratio (
f
is defined as
q=e^{-f}{p_{\rm{ref}}}/\int e^{-f}d{p_{\rm{ref}}}
) to calculate the functional derivatives. Please refer to Appendix
B
for the concrete formulation of
\frac{\delta L_{\mathrm{KTO}}}{\delta q}
.

The Nonlinear Distributional Optimization Algorithm

Now we make a concrete introduction of our proposed approach.
Our goal of the distribution optimization (
2
) is to train a neural network that approximates
\hat{f}_{\mathrm{opt}}=\log\frac{\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}}{p_{\rm ref}}+(\text{const.})
.
To achieve this, we utilize the
Dual Averaging
(DA) algorithm
(
Nesterov, 2009
;
Nitanda et al., 2021
;
Nishikawa et al., 2022
)
, and we iteratively construct a tentative local potential
f_{k}
by approximating the update the DA algorithm.
After we obtain
f_{K}

q_{*}

Doob’s h-transform technique
.

Phase 1: Dual Averaging.
Let
f_{1}
be a randomly initialized potential. First, we initialize

f_{1}

Then, the distribution

is updated recursively by pulling back the weighted sum of gradients from the dual space to the primal space.

\displaystyle=:\exp{(-\bar{g}^{(k)})}p_{\rm{ref}},

to approximate

Chopin et al., 2023
;
Uehara et al., 2024b
;
Heng et al., 2024
)
.
By introducing the correction term
u_{*}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by

u_{*}(y,t)=\nabla\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(-f_{*}(\bar{X}_{T}^{\leftarrow}))\mid\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=y],

(12)

the score function of
q_{*}
at
(x,t)
is written as
\nabla\log q_{t}(y)=\nabla\log p_{t}(X^{\leftarrow}_{t},T-t)+u_{*}(y,t)
, where
q_{t}
is the law of the backward process at time
t
whose output distribution is the optimal solution
q_{*}
.
We provide the derivation in Lemma
12
and refer readers to
Rogers & Williams (2000)
;
Chopin et al. (2023)
for more details and a formal
treatment of Doob’s h-transform.
By approximating
\log p_{t}(X^{\leftarrow}_{t},T-t)
by the score network
s(x,t)
and the correction term
u_{*}(x,t)
by
u(x,t)
and discretizing the dynamics,
we obtain the following update

Y^{\leftarrow}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),\ \mathrm{d}Y^{\leftarrow}_{t}=\{Y^{\leftarrow}_{t}+2(s(Y^{\leftarrow}_{lh},lh)+u(Y^{\leftarrow}_{lh},lh))\}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},\ t\in[lh,(l+1)h],

(13)

where
u(x,t)
can be computed as
u(x,t)=\nabla_{x}\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(-f_{K}(X_{T}^{\leftarrow}))|X_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x]
,
which can be estimated by running the reference diffusion model
(X_{t}^{\leftarrow})
.
The practical treatment for this is discussed in Appendix
C
.
For experimental information, please have a look at Section
6
and Algorithm
2
in Appendix
D.2
.

5
Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we give theoretical justification of our proposed algorithm.
More concretely, we show the rate of convergence of the (inexact) DA method and give an approximation error bound on the diffusion model based on the h-transform.

5.1
Convergence rate of the DA method

We give the convergence rate of the DA algorithm in the two settings: when
F
is (I)
convex and (II) non-convex, respectively.

(I): Convex objective
F
.

First, we show the rate when
F
is convex.
We basically follow the proof technique of
Nitanda et al. (2021)
;
Nishikawa et al. (2022)
.
In the analysis, we put the following assumption on
F
.

Assumption 1
.

The loss function
F
satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}
is bounded: There exists
B_{F}>0
such that
\|\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q)\|_{\infty}\leq B_{F}
for any
q\in\mathcal{P}
,

(ii)

\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}
is Lipshitz continuous with respect to the TV distance: There exists
L_{\mathrm{TV}}>0
such that
\|\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q)-\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\leq L_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathrm{TV}(q,q^{\prime})
for any
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
.

(iii)

F
is convex:
F(q)\geq F(q^{\prime})+\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(q-q^{\prime})
for any
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
,

Then, we can show that the (inexact) DA algorithm achieves the following convergence rate that yields
\mathcal{O}(K^{-1})
convergence of the objective.

Theorem 1

(Convergence of the objective in Option 1 )
.

Suppose that
\beta^{\prime}\geq\beta
and we train the potential
f_{k+1}
so that it is sufficiently close to
\bar{g}^{(k)}
as

\mathrm{TV}(\hat{q}^{(k)},q^{(k)})\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{TV}}
for all
k
.
Then, under Assumption
1
, Option 1 satisfies the following convergence guarantee:

\frac{2}{K(K+1)}\sum_{k=1}^{K}k\left[L(\hat{q}^{(k)})-L(\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}})\right]\leq 2L_{\mathrm{TV}}\epsilon_{\mathrm{TV}}+\left(\frac{2B_{F}}{K(K+1)}+\frac{2\beta^{\prime}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{*}\|p_{\rm{ref}})+2B_{F}^{2}\beta^{-1}}{K}\right).

(14)

See Appendix
A.1
for the proof.
From this theorem, we see that the DA algorithm with Option 1 achieves
{\mathcal{O}}(1/K)
convergence. The assumption (ii) in Assumption
1
is required to bound an expectation of
\bar{g}^{(k)}
in the bound.
The assumption (iii) is required to bound the difference between the exact update
\hat{q}^{(k)}
and the inexact update
q^{(k)}
. If the update is exact, we don’t need this assumption.

(II): Non-convex objective
F
.

We also give a convergence for a non-convex loss
F
.
Here, we put the following assumption.

Assumption 2
.

The loss function
F
satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}
is bounded: There exists
B_{F}>0
such that
\|\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q)\|_{\infty}\leq B_{F}
for any
q\in{\mathcal{P}}
,

(ii)

\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}
is Lipshitz continuous with respect to the TV distance:
\|\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q)-\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\leq L_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathrm{TV}(q,q^{\prime})
for any
q,q^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{P}}
,

(iii)

F
is lower bounded.

Assumptions (i) and (ii) are the same as the convex case (Assumption
1
), and lower boundedness (iii) is weaker than convexity in Assumption
1
.
Assumption (ii) induces the following type of smoothness commonly observed in standard optimization: (ii)’ There exists
S_{F}\geq 0
such that
F(q)\leq F(q^{\prime})+\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(q-q^{\prime})+\frac{S_{F}}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|q^{\prime})
.
When the inner-loop error is ignored, it is possible to prove convergence using only the smoothness assumption (ii)’ instead of assumption (ii). For details, please refer to Appendix
A.1.1
.

Under this assumption, we can show the following convergence guarantee with respect to the sequence
(\hat{q}^{(k)})_{k=1}^{K}
even in a non-convex setting.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that
\beta^{\prime}>\beta+L_{\mathrm{TV}}
and
\mathrm{TV}(\hat{q}^{(k)},q^{(k)})\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{TV}}
for all
k
, then under Assumption
2
, both Option 1 and 2 yield the following convergence:

(i)

\lim_{k\to\infty}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{q}^{(k+1)}\|\hat{q}^{(k)})=0
.

(ii)

For all
K
, it holds that

\underset{k=1,...,K}{\min}\left\{c_{k}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{q}^{(k+1)}\|\hat{q}^{(k)}\right)\}\leq\frac{(\tilde{L}_{1}(\hat{q}^{(1)}-L(\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}))+(L_{\mathrm{TV}}+B_{F})K\epsilon_{\mathrm{TV}}}{K\beta^{\prime}}=:\Psi_{K},

(15)

where
c_{k}=\frac{\beta k+2\beta^{\prime}}{2}
for Option 1 and
c_{k}=1
for Option 2.

See Appendix
A.1.1
for the proof.
The proof utilizes an analogous argument with
Liu et al. (2023a)
that analyzed convergence of a DA method in a finite dimensional non-convex optimization problem. However, we need to reconstruct a proof because we should work on the probability measure space, which is not a vector space, and carefully make use of the property of the KL-divergence.
We see that
\Psi_{K}={\mathcal{O}}(1/K)
if
\epsilon_{\mathrm{TV}}
is sufficiently small as
\epsilon_{\mathrm{TV}}={\mathcal{O}}(1/K)
, and thus the discrepancy between
\hat{q}^{(k+1)}
and
\hat{q}^{(k)}
converges.
Especially, the convergence of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{q}^{(k+1)}\|\hat{q}^{(k)})
yields the convergence of the “dual variable” for Option 2 as in the following corollary.
For that purpose, we define

\tilde{L}_{k}(q)\coloneq L(q)+\frac{\beta^{\prime}}{k}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})

(see its similarity to the inner objective of the DA update (
) and (
)),
and define
\psi_{q}\left(g\right)=\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{q}[\exp(-g+\mathbb{E}_{q}[g])]\right)
which is a “moment generating function” of a dual variable
g
.

Corollary 1

(Convergence in Option 2 )
.

Under the same condition as Theorem
2
, we have that

\textstyle\min\limits_{1\leq k\leq K}\psi_{\hat{q}^{(k)}}\left(\frac{k}{\beta^{\prime}(k+1)}\frac{\delta\tilde{L}_{k}}{\delta q}(\hat{q}^{(k)})\right)\leq\Psi_{K}.

Roughly speaking, this corollary indicates that
the variance of the gradient
\frac{\delta\tilde{L}_{k}}{\delta q}(\hat{q}^{(k)})
converges as
\mathrm{Var}_{\hat{q}^{(k)}}\left(\frac{\delta\tilde{L}_{k}}{\delta q}(\hat{q}^{(k)})\right)={\mathcal{O}}(1/K)
because we may approximate
\psi_{q}(g)\simeq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}_{q}(g)
when
|g-\mathbb{E}_{q}[g]|
is sufficiently small.
Therefore, we have a convergence guarantee of the dual variable (gradient)
\frac{\delta\tilde{L}_{k}}{\delta q}(\hat{q}^{(k)},x)\to 0\;\text{(up to a constant w.r.t. $x$)}
even in a non-convex setting, which justifies usage of our method for general objective functions.

5.2
Discretization error of Doob’s h-transform

We now provide the sampling error analysis after obtaining the approximate solution
f_{K}
.
In the analysis of Dual Averaging,
p_{\rm{ref}}
was assumed to be known, and the goal was to obtain the optimal solution of (
2
) that corresponds to
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}=\exp(-\hat{f}_{\mathrm{opt}})p_{\rm{ref}}
. From here, considering that
p_{\rm{ref}}
estimates
p_{*}
, we shift our focus to sample from the optimal alignment
q_{*}\propto\rho_{*}p_{*}
, while the dynamics we implement involves several approximations:
(i) time discretization approximation, (ii) approximation of the score
\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}
by
s(x,T-t)
, (iii) approximation of
\rho^{*}
by
\rho=\exp(-f_{K})
, (iv) approximation of
u_{*}
by
u
.

To evaluate how such approximation affects the final quality of our generative model,
we will give a bound of the TV-distance between
q_{*}
and
\hat{q}=\rho p_{\rm{ref}}
by putting all those errors together.
To do so, we put the following assumption.

Assumption 3
.

1.

\nabla\log p_{t}
is
L_{p}
-smooth at every time
t
and it has finite second moment
\mathbb{E}[\|\bar{X}_{t}\|^{2}_{2}]\leq\mathsf{m}<\infty
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.

\nabla\log\rho_{*}
is
L_{\rho}
-smooth and bounded as
C_{\rho}^{-1}\leq\rho_{*}\leq C_{\rho}
for a constant
C_{\rho}
.

3.

The score estimation error is bounded by

\mathbb{E}_{\bar{X}_{\cdot}^{\leftarrow}}[\|s(\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow},t)\!\!-\!\!\nabla\log p_{T-t}(\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{2}]\!\leq\!\varepsilon

at each time
t
.

4.

\mathbb{E}_{\bar{X}_{\cdot}^{\leftarrow}}[\|u_{*}(\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow},t)-u(\bar{X}_{lh}^{\leftarrow},lh)\|^{2}]\leq\varepsilon_{\rho,l}^{2}
for any
1\leq l\leq T/h

and
t\in[lh,(l+1)h)
.

This assumption is rather standard, for example,
Chen et al. (2023c)
employed these conditions except the last condition on
u
and
u_{*}
.
The fourth assumption in Assumption
3
is imposed to mathematically formulate the situation:
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}/p_{\rm{ref}}\simeq q_{*}/p_{*}
.
Then, we obtain the following error bound:

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that Assumption
3
is satisfied. Then, we have the following bound on the distribution
\hat{q}
of
Y^{\leftarrow}_{T}
:

\textstyle\mathrm{TV}(q_{*},\hat{q})^{2}\lesssim T\varepsilon^{2}+\sum_{l=1}^{T/h}h\varepsilon_{\rho,l}^{2}+T(L_{p}C_{\rho}^{2}+L_{\rho})^{2}(dh+\mathsf{m}h^{2})+\exp(-2T)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{*}\|N(0,I)).

(16)

The proof is given in Appendix
C.1
.
It basically follows
Chen et al. (2023c)
;
Chen et al. (2023b)
, but we have derived the smoothness of
\nabla\log(q_{*,t})
from that of
\nabla\log(p_{t})
.
In this bound, we did not give any evaluation on
\varepsilon_{\rho,l}^{2}
, however, this error term can be bounded as follows with additional conditions.

Assumption 4
.

(i)
\nabla_{x}s(\cdot,\cdot)
is
H_{s}
-Lipschitz continuous in a sense that
\|\nabla_{x}s(x,t)-\nabla_{y}s(y,t)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq H_{s}\|x-y\|

for any
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
0\leq t\leq T
and
\mathbb{E}[\|s(\tilde{X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh)\|^{2}]\leq Q^{2}
for any
k
,
(ii) There exists
R>0
such that
\sup_{t,x}\{\|\nabla_{x}^{2}\log p_{t}(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|\nabla_{x}^{2}\log s(x,t)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\}\leq R
.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that Assumptions
3
and
4
hold
and
\|\rho_{*}-\rho\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
and
\|\rho\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\rho}
.
We also assume
\nabla\rho^{*}
is bounded and Lipschitz continuous.
Then, for any choice of
0\leq h\leq\delta\leq 1/(1+2R)
, we have that

\displaystyle\textstyle\varepsilon_{\rho,l}^{2}\lesssim

\displaystyle\textstyle C_{\rho}^{3}\left\{\Xi_{\delta,\varepsilon}+R_{\varphi}^{2}\left(\varepsilon^{2}+L_{p}^{2}d(\delta+\mathsf{m}\delta^{2})\right)+[L_{\varphi}^{2}(\mathsf{m}+Q^{2}+dh)+R_{\varphi}^{2}(1+2R)^{2}]h^{2}\right\}

(17)

\displaystyle\textstyle+\min\{T-lh,1/(2+2R)\}^{-1}\varepsilon^{\prime 2},

(18)

where

\Xi_{\delta,\varepsilon}:=C_{\rho}^{2}(1+2R)^{2}\delta+C_{\rho}^{2}\frac{1+\delta R_{\varphi}^{2}}{\delta}[\varepsilon^{2}+L_{p}^{2}d(h+\mathsf{m}h^{2})],
and

R_{\varphi}
and
L_{\varphi}
are constants introduced in Lemma
10
.

The proof is given in Appendix
C.3
.
We applied the so-called
Bismut-Elworthy-Li
integration-by-parts formula
(
Bismut, 1984
;
Elworthy & Li, 1994
)
to obtain the discretization error.
By substituting
\delta\leftarrow\sqrt{h}
to balance the terms related to
h
and
\delta
, we obtain a simplified upper bound of
\sum_{l=1}^{T/h}h\varepsilon_{\rho,l}^{2}
as

\sum_{l=1}^{T/h}h\varepsilon_{\rho,l}^{2}\lesssim\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\right)\varepsilon^{2}T+T\sqrt{h}+(T+\log(1/h))\varepsilon^{\prime 2}.

These results can be seen as
h
-Transform extension of the approximation error analysis given in
Chen et al. (2023c)
.
However, the approximation error corresponding to
u
has worse dependency on
h
. This is because the computation of
u
uses the discretized process
p_{\rm{ref}}
and is affected by its error while the ordinary diffusion model does not require sampling to obtain the score.

7
Conclusion

We proved that the distributional optimization can be solved even if
p_{\rm{ref}}
and
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}
are mutilmodal and we only have access to the score, not the density.
This setting includes important fine-tuning methodologies for diffusion models: Reinforcement Learning, DPO, and KTO.
Our algorithms are based on the DA algorithm and Doob’s h-transform technique and it can solve them more directly than previous works.
They are guaranteed to be useful for general objective functions, the dual variable converges even in a non-convex setting.
While our framework has potential applications in general distributional optimization problems, such as density ratio estimation under covariate shift and climate change tracking, further exploration of these applications is left for future work.

Appendix A
Convergence Analysis of Dual Averaging

Overview for convex loss

In Section
A.1
, we are interested in the convergence of Option 1 when the objective functional
F
is convex in a distributional sense:

F(q)\geq F(q^{\prime})+\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(q-q^{\prime}),\quad\text{for any $q,q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}$}.

(19)

Then, the regularized objective
L(q)=F(q)+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})
becomes strongly convex with
\beta>0
.
Formally, we assume that

(i)

\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}
is bounded: There exists
B_{F}>0
such that
\|\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q)\|_{\infty}\leq B_{F}
for any
q\in\mathcal{P}
,

(ii)

\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}
is Lipshitz continuous with respect to the TV distance: There exists
L_{\mathrm{TV}}>0
such that
\|\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q)-\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\leq L_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathrm{TV}(q,q^{\prime})
for any
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
.

(iii)

F
is convex:
F(q)\geq F(q^{\prime})+\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(q-q^{\prime})
for any
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
,

In the update of Option 1, we iteratively use the distributional proximal operator. We minimize

\mathrm{E}_{q}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{2j}{k(k+1)}g^{(j)}\right]+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})+\beta^{\prime}\frac{2}{k}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})

(20)

by the minimizer
\hat{q}^{(k)}
, where
{g}^{(k)}
denotes
\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{(k)})
with
q^{(k)}\simeq\hat{q}^{(k)}
(
q^{(k)}
is constructed by neural networks and samples from
p_{\rm{ref}}
, while we do not directly compute
q^{(k)}
.). Intuitively, with
k\to\infty
, we hope that
q
is almost converged around the minimizer
\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}
, i.e.
q\simeq\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}
. Then, from the differentiability of
F
, each
\mathbb{E}_{q}[g^{(j)}]
in the first term
would be a linear functional that well approximates
F(q^{(j)})
, with suficiently large
j
. In addition,
\beta^{\prime}\frac{2}{k}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})
vanishes, so the equation (
20
) is roughly written as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{2j}{k(k+1)}\left[\mathrm{E}_{q}\left[g^{(j)}\right]+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})\right]+\beta^{\prime}\frac{2}{k}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})

(21)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{2j}{k(k+1)}(F(q^{(j)})+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}}))+\beta^{\prime}\frac{2}{k}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})

(22)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{2j}{k(k+1)}(F(\hat{q}^{(j)})+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{q}^{(j)}\|p_{\rm{ref}})).

(23)

This is the weighted average of the regularized losses
L(q)=F(q)+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})
.
From this concept, we will show that the convergence represented as

\left[\text{Weighted average of }L(\hat{q}^{(k)})\right]=\left[\text{Weighted average of }L(\hat{q}_{\mathrm{opt}})\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).

(24)

Overview for nonconvex loss

In Section
A.2
, we are interested in the case that
F
is not necessarily convex (mainly) in Option 2.
Alternatively, we use the assumption that
F
is smooth in terms of KL-divergence:

(ii)’

(The weaker smoothness derived from (ii)). There exists
S_{F}\geq 0
such that

F(q)\leq F(q^{\prime})+\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(q-q^{\prime})+\frac{S_{F}}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|q^{\prime})\quad\text{for any}\quad q,q^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{P}},

(25)

This is induced by Lipschitz continuity of
\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}
((ii) of Assumption
1
and
2
) and Pinsker’s inequality. When the inner-loop error is ignored, it is possible to prove convergence using only the smoothness (
25
) instead of Lipschitz continuity of
\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}
.
Please note that KL-divergence would be the “quadratic term” in the ordinary definition of smoothness.
Following the theoretical analysis of standard nonconvex optimization, our goal is to show that the “derivative” of the (regularized) objective
L(q)
goes to zero in the form of functional derivative:

\frac{\delta L}{\delta q}(\hat{q}^{(k)},x)\to 0\quad\text{(up to constant w.r.t. $x$.)}

(26)

Please note that we can ignore
\frac{\delta L}{\delta q}(\hat{q}^{(k)},x)
if it is a constant because of the definition of the functional derivatives.

At the first step, both in Option 1 and 2, the regularized objective
L(q)
(roughly) monotonically decrease during the Dual Averaging. Ignoring some terms, we want to show that

L(\hat{q}^{(k+1)})-L(\hat{q}^{(k)})\lesssim-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{q}^{(k)}\|\hat{q}^{(k+1)})<0.

(27)

Next, by taking a telescoping sum, we obtain that

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\text{weight})_{k}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{q}^{(k)}\|\hat{q}^{(k+1)})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{K}\right).

(28)

From the above equation, it is immediately shown that

\min_{k=1,...,K}(\text{weight})_{k}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{q}^{(k)}\|\hat{q}^{(k+1)})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{K}\right),

(29)

where
(\text{weight})_{k}\simeq k
in the Option 1, while
(\text{weight})_{k}\simeq 1
in the Option 2.
Therefore, when we choose the Option 2, it holds that

\min_{k=1,...,K}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{q}^{(k)}\|\hat{q}^{(k+1)})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{K}\right)

(30)

Finally, we get the conclusion
\frac{\delta L(\hat{q}^{(k)})}{\delta q}\to 0\;\text{(up to constant w.r.t. $x$)}
because
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{q}^{(k)}\|\hat{q}^{(k+1)})
is the approximate “moment generation function” of
\frac{\delta L(\hat{q}^{(k)})}{\delta q}
; when
k\to\infty
,

\min_{k=1,..,K}\left(\text{``Variance" of }\frac{\delta L(\hat{q}^{(k)})}{\delta q}\right)\simeq\min_{k=1,..,K}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{q}^{(k)}\|\hat{q}^{(k+1)})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{K}\right).

(31)

This result aligns with the result for standard, non-distributinal Dual Averaging
(
Liu et al., 2023a
)
,
\min_{k=1,...,K}\|\text{(gradient of the objective)}_{k}\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(1/K)
because the variance is the second moment.

where we used the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality in the last inequality.

A.2
Convergence proof for non-convex loss
F

Here, we give the proof of Theorem
2
and Corollary
1
. In this section, we always assume Assumption
2
holds. We are mainly interested in the property that
F
is smooth with respect to KL-divergence instead of convexity:

(ii)’

(A weaker version of (ii)). There exists
S_{F}\geq 0
such that

F(q)\leq F(q^{\prime})+\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(q-q^{\prime})+\frac{S_{F}}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|q^{\prime})\quad\text{for any}\quad q,q^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{P}}.

(75)

This property can be derived from (ii) in Assumption
2
that is Lipschitz continuity of
\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}
:

Lemma 3
.

Assume that
\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}
is Lipshitz continuous with respect to the TV distance: There exists
L_{\mathrm{TV}}>0
such that
\|\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q)-\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\leq L_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathrm{TV}(q,q^{\prime})
for any
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
.
Then,

F(q)\leq F(q^{\prime})+\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(q-q^{\prime})+\frac{L_{\mathrm{TV}}}{2}\mathrm{TV}(q,q^{\prime})^{2}\quad\text{for any}\quad q,q^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{P}}.

(76)

From Pinsker’s inequality,

F(q)\leq F(q^{\prime})+\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(q-q^{\prime})+L_{\mathrm{TV}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|q^{\prime})\quad\text{for any}\quad q,q^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{P}}.

(77)

Proof.

The proof is almost identical to the proof of
L_{\mathrm{TV}}
-smoothness commonly discussed in the context of standard optimization.
For
q,q^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{P}}
, we define
q_{t}=q^{\prime}+t(q-q^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{P}}
. Then,

\displaystyle F(q)-F(q^{\prime})-\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(q-q^{\prime})

(78)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t=0}^{t=1}\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q_{t})\mathrm{d}(q-q^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t-\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(q-q^{\prime})

(79)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t=0}^{t=1}\int\left[\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q_{t})-\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}(q^{\prime})\right]\mathrm{d}(q-q^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t

(80)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{t=0}^{t=1}tL_{\mathrm{TV}}\mathrm{TV}(q,q^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}t\quad(\text{$L_{\mathrm{TV}}$-Lipshitz continuity of $\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}$})

(81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L_{\mathrm{TV}}}{2}\mathrm{TV}(q,q^{\prime})^{2},

(82)

while we used the fundamental theorem of calculus in the first equation and the assumption in the inequality.

We emphasize that when the inner-loop error is ignored, it is possible to prove convergence using only the smoothness (

Under the assumptions, our goal is to show that the functional derivative of

We prepare the following Lemma about the convexity of

Lemma 4
.

The following equations hold:

(i)

(ii)

For

\tilde{F}(q)=\tilde{F}(q^{\prime})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|q^{\prime}).

\tilde{F}(q)

We omit its proof because it is straight forward.

implies that

also plays a important role as a “quadratic” penalty term of the proximal operator whose output is

\displaystyle r_{k+1}(\hat{q}^{(k+1)})+\left(\frac{\beta k(k+1)}{2}+\beta^{\prime}(k+1)\right)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{q}^{(k)}\|\hat{q}^{(k+1)})

(142)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{k+1}(\hat{q}^{(k)})

(143)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r_{k}(\hat{q}^{(k)})+(\beta k+\beta^{\prime})D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat{q}^{(k)}\|p_{\rm{ref}}).

(144)

Substituting this relation to Eq. (
139
) and noticing
|\frac{\delta F}{\delta q}|\leq B_{F}
, we obtain the assertion.
∎

Appendix B
Derivations of functional derivatives

B.1
Direct Preference Optimization

We can apply the proposed algorithm to the (populational) objective of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
.
DPO is an effective approach for learning from human preference for not only language models but also diffusion models.

Original DPO objective

Let
x_{w},x_{l}
be “winning” and “losing” outputs independently sampled from the reference model
p_{\rm{ref}}
. The event
\{x_{w}\succ x_{l}\}
is determined by the human preference. The original DPO objective is formulated as

L_{\mathrm{DPO,original}}(q)=-\mathbb{E}_{x_{w},x_{l}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\gamma\log\frac{q(x_{w})}{p_{\rm{ref}}(x_{w})}-\gamma\log\frac{q(x_{l})}{p_{\rm{ref}}(x_{l})}\right)\right],

(145)

where
\gamma>0
is a hyperparameter. The expectation is taken by
x_{w},x_{l}
, that are “winning” and “losing” samples from
p_{\rm{ref}}
.

Wallace et al. (2024)
derived a new objective that is the upper bound of
L_{\mathrm{DPO,original}}(q)
, but it is a specialized derivation of the optimization method for DPO.

Reformulating of the DPO Objective

The goal is to directly minimize
L_{\mathrm{DPO,original}}(q)
, however, in the above expression, we cannot apply DPO to diffusion models directly because the expectaion with tuple
(x_{w},x_{l})
is not formulated well. We start with another expression of the objective of DPO:

L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(q)\coloneq-\mathbb{E}_{x_{w}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{l}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\gamma\log\frac{q(x_{w})}{p_{\rm{ref}}(x_{w})}-\gamma\log\frac{q(x_{l})}{p_{\rm{ref}}(x_{l})}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{x_{w}\succ x_{l}}(x_{w},x_{l})\right],

(146)

\mathbbm{1}_{x\succ y}(x,y)
is 1 if
x\succ y
, 0 otherwise.
This
L_{\mathrm{DPO}}
is in the regime of our algorithm and the functional derivative can be derived:

Proposition 2
.

The functional derivative of
L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(q)
is calculated as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta L_{\mathrm{DPO}}}{\delta q}(q,x)

(147)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{x_{l}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\left(1-\sigma\left(-\gamma f(x)+\gamma f(x_{l})\right)\right)\frac{\int e^{-f}dp_{\mathrm{ref}}}{e^{-f(x)}}\mathbbm{1}_{x\succ x_{l}}(x,x_{l})\right]

(148)

\displaystyle+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{x_{w}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\left(1-\sigma\left(-\gamma f(x_{w})+\gamma f(x)\right)\right)\frac{\int e^{-f}dp_{\mathrm{ref}}}{e^{-f(x)}}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{w}\succ x}(x_{w},x)\right],

(149)

where
q=e^{-f}p_{\rm{ref}}/\int e^{-f}\mathrm{d}p_{\rm{ref}}
. This functional derivative is tractable in our settings.

Proof.

In this proof, we use the following notations:

•

p_{\mathrm{ref}}:
the output distribution of a pre-trained model,

•

q\coloneq e^{-f}p_{\mathrm{ref}}/\int e^{-f}dp_{\mathrm{ref}}
: the output distribution of an aligned model,

•

\mathrm{LSL}(q_{1},q_{2})\coloneq\log\sigma(\gamma\log q_{1}/p_{\mathrm{ref}}-\gamma\log q_{2}/p_{\mathrm{ref}})
,

•

\partial_{1}\mathrm{LSL}(q_{1},q_{2})=\gamma(1-\sigma(\gamma\log q_{1}/p_{\mathrm{ref}}-\gamma\log q_{2}/p_{\mathrm{ref}}))\frac{1}{q_{1}}
,

•

\partial_{2}\mathrm{LSL}(q_{1},q_{2})=-(1-\sigma(\gamma\log q_{1}/p_{\mathrm{ref}}-\gamma\log q_{2}/p_{\mathrm{ref}}))\frac{1}{q_{2}}
,

•

\psi(r_{1},r_{2})\coloneq\gamma(\log r_{1}-\log r_{2})
,

•

\mathrm{Inv}(f,x)=\frac{\int e^{-f}dp_{\mathrm{ref}}}{e^{-f(x)}}
.

The objective of DPO is written as

L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(q)\coloneq-\mathrm{E}_{x_{w}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\mathrm{E}_{x_{l}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\mathrm{LSL}(q(x_{w}),qp(x_{l}))\mathbbm{1}_{x_{w}\succ x_{l}}(x_{w},x_{l})\right].

(150)

We obtain the first variation of the objective as follows:

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(q+\epsilon(\tilde{q}-q))

(151)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(q)-\epsilon\mathrm{E}_{x_{w}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\mathrm{E}_{x_{l}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\left(\partial_{1}\mathrm{LSL}(q(x_{w}),q(x_{l}))(\tilde{q}-q)(x_{w})\right.\right.

(152)

\displaystyle\left.\left.\quad\quad\quad+\partial_{2}\mathrm{LSL}(q(x_{w}),q(x_{l}))(\tilde{q}-q)(x_{l})\right)\mathbbm{1}_{x_{w}\succ x_{l}}(x_{w},x_{l})\right]+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})

(153)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(p)

(154)

\displaystyle-\epsilon\left[\mathrm{E}_{x_{l}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\int\gamma(1-\sigma(\psi(\frac{q(x_{w})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{w})},\frac{q(x_{l})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{l})})))(\tilde{q}-q)(x_{w})\mathrm{Inv}(f,x_{w})dx_{w}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{w}\succ x_{l}}\right]\right.

(155)

\displaystyle\left.+\mathrm{E}_{x_{w}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\int\gamma(1-\sigma(\psi(\frac{q(x_{w})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{w})},\frac{q(x_{l})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(x_{l})})))(\tilde{q}-q)(x_{l})\mathrm{Inv}(f,x_{l})dx_{l}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{w}\succ x_{l}}\right]\right]

(156)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

(157)

Then, the first derivative of
F
is

\displaystyle\frac{\delta L_{\mathrm{DPO}}}{\delta p}(p,x)=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{x_{l}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\gamma\left(1-\sigma\left(\psi(\frac{q(x)}{p_{\rm{ref}}(x)},\frac{q(x_{l})}{p_{\rm{ref}}(x_{l})})\right)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{x\succ x_{l}}(x,x_{l})\right]\mathrm{Inv}(f,x)

(158)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{x_{w}\sim p_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\gamma\left(1-\sigma\left(\psi(\frac{q(x_{w})}{p_{\rm{ref}}(x_{w})},\frac{q(x)}{p_{\rm{ref}}(x)})\right)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{x_{w}\succ x}(x_{w},x)\right]\mathrm{Inv}(f,x).

(159)

∎

B.2
Kahneman-Tversky optimization

Assume that the whole data space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is split into a desirable domain
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{D}}
and an undesirable domain
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{U}}
.
The objective of original KTO
(
Ethayarajh et al., 2024
)
is formulated as

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{KTO}}(q)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\rm{ref}}}\left[\gamma_{D}\left(1-\sigma\left(\kappa\log\frac{q}{p_{\rm{ref}}}-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})\right)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{D}}\}}\right.

(160)

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\gamma_{U}\left(1-\sigma\left(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})-\kappa\log\frac{q}{p_{\rm{ref}}}\right)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{U}}\}}\right],

(161)

where
\gamma_{D},\;\gamma_{U},\;\kappa
are hyper parameters, and
\sigma
is a sigmoid function.

Proposition 3
.

The functional derivative of
L_{\mathrm{KTO}}
is calculated as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta L_{\mathrm{KTO}}}{\delta q}(q,x)

(162)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\kappa\gamma_{D}\sigma_{\mathrm{deriv}}(\phi(x))\frac{\int e^{-f}\mathrm{d}p_{\rm{ref}}}{e^{-f(x)}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{D}}\}}

(163)

\displaystyle+(-f(x)-\log\int e^{-f(x)}dp_{\rm{ref}})\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_{\rm{ref}}}\left[\gamma_{D}\sigma_{\mathrm{deriv}}\left(\phi(y)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{y\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{D}}\}}\right]

(164)

\displaystyle+\kappa\gamma_{U}\sigma_{\mathrm{deriv}}(-\phi(x))\frac{\int e^{-f}\mathrm{d}p_{\rm{ref}}}{e^{-f(x)}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{U}}\}}

(165)

\displaystyle-(-f(x)-\log\int e^{-f(x)}dp_{\rm{ref}})\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_{\rm{ref}}}\left[\gamma_{U}\sigma_{\mathrm{deriv}}\left(-\phi(y)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{y\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{U}}\}}\right]

(166)

where
\sigma_{\mathrm{deriv}}(\cdot)\coloneq\sigma(\cdot)(1-\sigma(\cdot))
,

\phi(x)\coloneq\kappa\log\frac{q(x)}{p_{\rm{ref}}(x)}-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_{\rm{ref}})
,
q=\frac{e^{-f}p_{\rm{ref}}}{\int e^{-f}dp_{\rm{ref}}}
.

The functional derivative of KTO can be calculated if you have
f(x)
and the samples from
p_{\rm{ref}}
. Note that
\log q(x)/p_{\rm{ref}}(x)=-f(x)-\log\int e^{-f(x)}dp_{\rm{ref}}
.

Proof.

The first variation of
L_{\mathrm{KTO}}
is

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{KTO}}(q+\epsilon(\tilde{q}-q))-L_{\mathrm{KTO}}(q)

(167)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\epsilon\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\rm{ref}}}\left[-\kappa\gamma_{D}\sigma_{\mathrm{deriv}}(\phi(x))\left(\frac{\tilde{q}(x)-q(x)}{q(x)}+\int\log\frac{q(y)}{p_{\rm{ref}}(y)}\mathrm{d}(\tilde{q}-q)(y)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{D}}\}}\right]

(168)

\displaystyle+\epsilon\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\rm{ref}}}\left[\kappa\gamma_{U}\sigma_{\mathrm{deriv}}(-\phi(x))\left(\frac{\tilde{q}(x)-q(x)}{q(x)}-\int\log\frac{q(y)}{p_{\rm{ref}}(y)}\mathrm{d}(\tilde{q}-q)(y)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{U}}\}}\right]

(169)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon\int\left\{-\kappa\gamma_{D}\sigma_{\mathrm{deriv}}(\phi(x))\mathrm{Inv}(f,x)\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{D}}\}}\right.

(170)

\displaystyle\left.+\log\frac{q(x)}{p_{\rm{ref}}(x)}\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa\gamma_{D}\sigma_{\mathrm{deriv}}(\phi(y))\mathbbm{1}_{\{y\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{D}}\}}\right]\right\}\mathrm{d}(\tilde{q}-q)(y)

(171)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\epsilon\int\left\{\kappa\gamma_{U}\sigma_{\mathrm{deriv}}(-\phi(x))\mathrm{Inv}(f,x)\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{D}}\}}\right.

(172)

\displaystyle\left.-\log\frac{q(x)}{p_{\rm{ref}}(x)}\mathbb{E}\left[\kappa\gamma_{D}\sigma_{\mathrm{deriv}}(-\phi(y))\mathbbm{1}_{\{y\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{U}}\}}\right]\right\}\mathrm{d}(\tilde{q}-q)(y),

(173)

where
\mathrm{Inv}(f,x)=\frac{\int e^{-f}dp_{\mathrm{ref}}}{e^{-f(x)}}
.
Now the desired result immediately follows.
∎

Appendix C
Error Analysis of Diffusion Model

C.1
Overview

Here, we analyze the sampling error caused by the diffusion model. Let us organize the settings and notations used in this section.

Target distribution.
The target distribution is
q_{*}=q_{0}
, which is decomposed as
q_{*}(x)=\rho_{*}(x)p_{*}(x)
with
p_{*}=p_{0}
and
\rho_{*}
.
Here
p_{*}
and
\rho_{*}
represent the distribution of the original model and the density ratio obtained by fine-tuning, respectively.

Sampling with score-based diffusion model.
In the score-based diffusion model, we start with the forward process, which is written as a stochastic differential equation (SDE).
Choosing the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process,
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
follows the following SDE:

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{0}\sim p_{*},\ \mathrm{d}\bar{X}_{t}=-\bar{X}_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},

(174)

where
\{B_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is the standard Brownian motion.
At each time
t
, the law of
X_{t}
is written as

\displaystyle p_{t}(x)=\int p_{*}(y)\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\|m_{t}y-x\|^{2}\bigg)\mathrm{d}x,

(175)

where
m_{t}=e^{-t}
and
\sigma_{t}^{2}=1-e^{-2t}
.
In the same way, define
\{\bar{Y}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
by replacing
p_{*}
by
q_{*}
in (
174
) and let
q_{t}
be the law of
\bar{Y}_{t}
.

Then, for some
T\geq 0
, we can define the reverse process for
\{\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
. (Let
B_{t}
below be distinct from the one in (
174
).)

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{0}^{\leftarrow}\sim p_{T},\ \mathrm{d}\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=\{\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}+2\nabla\log p_{T-t}(\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow})\}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t}.

(176)

Then, the law of
\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}
equals
p_{T-t}
, which is why we call (
176
) as the reverse process.
In the same way, we define
\{\bar{Y}_{t}^{\leftarrow}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
as the reverse process of
\{\bar{Y}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

Doob’s h-transform.

By applying Doob’s h-transform to
\nabla\log q_{T-t}
, it can be decomposed into the original score
\nabla\log p_{T-t}
and a correction term.

\displaystyle\nabla\log q_{T-t}(\bar{Y}_{t}^{\leftarrow})=\nabla\log p_{T-t}(\bar{Y}_{t}^{\leftarrow})+\nabla_{x}\log(\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{0})|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x])|_{x=\bar{Y}_{t}^{\leftarrow}}.

(177)

See Lemma
12
for derivation.

Approximation of the score and correction term.

Because we do not have access to the exact value of
p_{t}
and
\rho_{*}
and therefore cannot implement (
177
) exactly, we consider approximating them by, e.g., neural networks.
We approximate
\nabla\log p_{T-t}(x)
by
s(x,t)\colon\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Also, we approximate

u_{*}(x,t)=\nabla_{x}\log(\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{0})|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x])
by
u(x,t)\colon\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Discretization.

Also, we need to discretize the stochastic differential equation.
We finally obtain the approximation of (
176
), denoted by
\{Y^{\leftarrow}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
, as

\displaystyle Y^{\leftarrow}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),\ \mathrm{d}Y^{\leftarrow}_{t}=\{Y^{\leftarrow}_{t}+2s(Y^{\leftarrow}_{kh},kh)+2u(Y^{\leftarrow}_{kh},kh)\}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},\ t\in[kh,(k+1)h].

(178)

Obtaining the correction term (approximately).

Given the score network
s(x,t)
approximating
\nabla\log p_{t}(x)
and the function
\bar{h}
that approximates
h_{*}
, we can approximate the correction term
u(x,t)
.
Remind that, for fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ t=kh,s=k(h+1)\in\mathbb{R}
, the correction term is calculated as

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\log(\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{0})|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x]|)=\frac{\int\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{0})|\bar{X}_{T-s}=x^{\prime}]\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\mathbb{P}[\bar{X}_{T-s}=x^{\prime}|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x]\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}}{\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{0})|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x]}.

(179)

One way to approximate (
179
) starts from approximating
\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{0})|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x]
.
If we run the reverse diffusion process

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x,\ \mathrm{d}\tilde{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}=\{\tilde{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}+2\nabla\log p_{T-\tau}(\tilde{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})\}\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{\tau},

(180)

we obtain that the law of
\tilde{X}_{T}^{\leftarrow}
is equal to that of
\bar{X}_{0}|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x
.
Therefore, by running the approximated reverse process (with a slight abuse of notation)

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{t}=x,\ \mathrm{d}\tilde{X}_{\tau}=\{\tilde{X}_{\tau}+2s(\tilde{X}_{lh},lh)\}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{\tau},\ \tau\in[lh,(l+1)h],

(181)

multiple times, the sample of
\tilde{X}_{T}
, denoted by
\{\tilde{x}_{T,i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, can approximate
\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{0})|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x]
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{0})|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x]\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho^{\prime}(\tilde{x}_{T,i}),

(182)

where
\rho^{\prime}
is the approximation of
\rho_{*}
.

On the other hand, we approximate
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\mathbb{P}[\bar{X}_{T-s}=x^{\prime}|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x]
by approximating
\mathbb{P}[\bar{X}_{T-s}=x^{\prime}|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x]
with a Gaussian distribution.
Specifically, because
\bar{X}_{T-s}=x^{\prime}|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x
is obtained by the following reverse diffusion process

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x,\ \mathrm{d}\tilde{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}=\{\tilde{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}+2\nabla\log p_{T-\tau}(\tilde{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})\}\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{\tau},

(183)

we approximate
\nabla\log p_{T-\tau}(\tilde{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})
by
s(x_{kh},kh)
to obtain

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x_{kh},\ \mathrm{d}\dot{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}=\{\dot{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}+2s(x_{kh},kh)\}\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{\tau}.

(184)

The distribution of
\tilde{X}_{s}^{\leftarrow}
is denoted by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\big(e^{h}x_{kh}+2(e^{h}-1)s(x_{kh},kh),e^{2h}-1\big).

(185)

Using this, our approximation is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\mathbb{P}[\bar{X}_{T-s}=x^{\prime}|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x]

(186)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{1}{(2\pi(e^{2h}-1))^{\frac{d}{2}}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{(x^{\prime}-(e^{h}x+2(e^{h}-1)s(x_{kh},kh)))^{2}}{2(e^{2h}-1)}\bigg)

(187)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{h}(x^{\prime}-(e^{h}x+2(e^{h}-1)s(x_{kh},kh)))}{(e^{2h}-1)(2\pi(e^{2h}-1))^{\frac{d}{2}}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{(x^{\prime}-(e^{h}x+2(e^{h}-1)s(x_{kh},kh)))^{2}}{2(e^{2h}-1)}\bigg).

(188)

This implies that, if we sample
\{x^{\prime}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
from (
185
),

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{0})|\bar{X}_{T-s}=x^{\prime}]\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\mathbb{P}[\bar{X}_{T-s}=x^{\prime}|\bar{X}_{T-t}=x]\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

(189)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{e^{h}}{(e^{2h}-1)}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{0})|\bar{X}_{T-s}=x^{\prime}_{j}](x^{\prime}_{j}-(e^{h}x+2(e^{h}-1)s(x_{t},t))),

(190)

and approximate each
\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{0})|\bar{X}_{T-s}=x^{\prime}_{j}]
in the same way as (
182
), we can approximate the correction term (
179
).

Now, our goal is to bound the error of the whole pipeline under the following assumptions of
p_{*}
and
\rho_{*}
and the score approximation error.

\displaystyle+\min\{T-lh,1/(2+2R)\}^{-1}\varepsilon^{\prime 2},

(195)

Lemma 9
.

When
C_{\rho}^{-1}\leq h_{*}(x)\leq C_{\rho}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{q_{0|t}(x|x^{\prime})}{p_{0|t}(x|x^{\prime})}\leq C_{\rho}^{2}

(226)

and

.

Proof.

We can write

\displaystyle q_{0|t}(x|x^{\prime})=\frac{q_{0,t}(x,x^{\prime})}{\int q_{0}(x^{\prime\prime})q_{t|0}(x|x^{\prime\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime\prime}}=\frac{p_{*}(x)\rho_{*}(x)q_{t|0}(x^{\prime}|x)}{\int p_{*}(x^{\prime\prime})\rho_{*}(x^{\prime\prime})q_{t|0}(x|x^{\prime\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime\prime}}.

(227)

Because

\displaystyle q_{0|t}(x|x^{\prime})\leq C_{\rho}^{2}\frac{p_{*}(x)p_{t|0}(x^{\prime}|x)}{\int p_{*}(x^{\prime\prime})p_{t|0}(x|x^{\prime\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime\prime}}=C_{\rho}^{2}p_{0|t}(x|x^{\prime}),

C.3
Estimation Error of the Correction Term

Since we have shown the time discretization error in the previous section, what we remain to show is just an upper bound of
\varepsilon_{\rho,l}^{2}
. For that purpose, we put an additional assumption which is almost same as Assumption
4
except the condition (iii).
A bound of
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}
in the third condition (iii) will be given as

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}={\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{2}+h)
by
Chen et al. (2023c)
.

Assumption 7
.

(i)

\nabla_{x}s(\cdot,\cdot)
is
H_{s}
-Lipschitz continuous in a sense that
\|\nabla_{x}s(x,t)-\nabla_{y}s(y,t)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq H_{s}\|x-y\|

for any
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
0\leq t\leq T
and
\mathbb{E}[\|s(\bar{X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh)\|^{2}]\leq Q^{2}
for any
k
.

(ii)

There exists
R>0
such that
\sup_{t,x}\{\|\nabla_{x}^{2}\log p_{t}(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|\nabla_{x}^{2}\log s(x,t)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\}\leq R
.

(iii)

\mathbb{E}_{\bar{X}_{t}}[\mathrm{TV}(\bar{X}_{T}^{\leftarrow},{X}_{T}^{\leftarrow}|\bar{X}_{T-t}^{\leftarrow}={X}_{T-t}^{\leftarrow}=\bar{X}_{t})^{2}]\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}
for any
t\in[0,T]
.

Theorem 7
.

Suppose that
0\leq h\leq\delta\leq 1/(1+2R)

and Assumptions
7
and
5
hold.
Let
L_{\varphi}
and
R_{\varphi}
be as given in Lemma
10
.
Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{X}_{\cdot}^{\leftarrow}}[\|\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})|\bar{X}_{t}]-\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}({X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})|{X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow}]\|^{2}]

(229)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle R_{\varphi}^{2}\left(\varepsilon^{2}+L_{p}^{2}d(\delta+\mathsf{m}\delta^{2})\right)+\Xi_{\delta,\varepsilon}+[L_{\varphi}^{2}(\mathsf{m}+4Q^{2}+dh)+R_{\varphi}^{2}(1+2R)^{2}]h^{2}

(230)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\left(\varepsilon^{2}+\delta+\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}}{\delta}\right),

(231)

where

\displaystyle\Xi_{\delta,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle:=\frac{4c_{\eta}^{2}C_{\rho}^{2}(1+2R)^{2}}{3}\delta+2\exp(2)\left\{C_{\rho}^{2}\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}}{\delta}+CR_{\varphi}^{2}[\varepsilon^{2}+L_{p}^{2}d(\delta+\mathsf{m}\delta^{2})]\right\},

(232)

and

is a universal constant.

By
Chen et al. (2023c)
,
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}={\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{2}+h)
, and thus by substituting
\delta\leftarrow\sqrt{h}
, we finally obtain an error estimate as

\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\right)\varepsilon^{2}+\sqrt{h}.

Proof.

Let
t\in[kh,(k+1)h)
and
t^{*}=kh+\delta
where
\delta
is larger than or equal to
h
:
\delta\geq h
.
We only consider a situation where
T-t\geq\delta
.
The situation where
\delta<T-t
can be treated in the same manner by noticing
a trivial relation
\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})|\bar{X}_{T}^{\leftarrow}=x]=\nabla\rho_{*}(x)
.
Then, for a given initial state
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we define the stochastic processes as

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x,\ \mathrm{d}\bar{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}=\{\bar{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}+2\nabla\log p_{T-\tau}(\bar{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})\}\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{\tau},~~~(t\leq\tau\leq T),

(233)

and its numerical approximation as

\displaystyle{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x,\ \mathrm{d}{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}=\{{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}+2s({X}^{\leftarrow}_{k_{\tau}h},k_{\tau}h)\}\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{\tau}.~~~(t\leq\tau\leq T),

(234)

where

Note that

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})|\bar{X}_{t}]-\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}({X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})|{X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{(\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})|\bar{X}_{t}]-\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}({X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})|{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}])}_{(a)}+\underbrace{(\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}({X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})|{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}]-\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}({X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})|{X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow}])}_{(b)}.

\displaystyle v^{\top}\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{0}^{\leftarrow})|\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\langle\eta_{\bar{X},\tau},\mathrm{d}B_{\tau}\rangle\varphi_{X}(\bar{X}_{t^{*}}^{\leftarrow})~|~\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x\right],

(237)

where

\displaystyle{\textnormal{d}}\eta_{\bar{X},\tau}=(I+2\nabla^{2}\log p_{T-t-\tau}(\bar{X}_{t+\tau}^{\leftarrow}))\eta_{\bar{X},\tau}{\textnormal{d}}\tau,

(238)

\displaystyle\eta_{\bar{X},0}=v.

(239)

Similarly, we define

for the process

\displaystyle{\textnormal{d}}\eta_{{X},\tau}=(\eta_{{X},\tau}+2\nabla_{x}^{\top}s({X}_{k_{\tau}h}^{\leftarrow},k_{\tau}h)\eta_{{X},k_{\tau}h-t}){\textnormal{d}}\tau,

(240)

\displaystyle\eta_{{X},0}=v.

(241)

Then,

\displaystyle v^{\top}\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}(\bar{X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})|\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x]-v^{\top}\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}({X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})|{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x]

(242)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\langle\eta_{\bar{X},\tau}-\eta_{{X},\tau},\mathrm{d}B_{\tau}\rangle\varphi_{Y}({X}_{t^{*}}^{\leftarrow})~|~\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}={X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x\right]

(243)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\langle\eta_{\bar{X},\tau},\mathrm{d}B_{\tau}\rangle(\varphi_{X}(\bar{X}_{t^{*}}^{\leftarrow})-\varphi_{Y}({X}_{t^{*}}^{\leftarrow}))~|~\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}={X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x\right].

(244)

By the Ito isometry, the first term of the right hand side can be bounded as

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\langle\eta_{\bar{X},\tau}-\eta_{{X},\tau},\mathrm{d}B_{\tau}\rangle\varphi_{Y}({X}_{t^{*}})~|~\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}={X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle C_{\rho}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}\langle\eta_{\bar{X},\tau}-\eta_{{X},\tau},\mathrm{d}B_{\tau}\rangle\right)^{2}~|~\bar{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}={X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x\right]

\displaystyle\frac{{\textnormal{d}}\|\eta_{\bar{X},\tau}-v\|^{2}}{{\textnormal{d}}\tau}

Lemma 10
.

Suppose that

\sup_{x}\|\nabla\rho_{*}(x)\|\leq R_{\rho}
,
\|\nabla\rho_{*}(x)-\nabla\rho_{*}(y)\|\leq L_{\rho}\|x-y\|~(\forall x,y)
, and

\nabla_{x}s(\cdot,\cdot)
is
H_{s}
-Lipschitz continuous with respect to
x
.
Let
\varphi_{Y,t}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}({X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})|{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x]
.
Then,
\nabla_{x}\varphi_{Y,t}(x)
is bounded by
R_{\varphi}
and
L_{\varphi}
-Lipschitz continuous for any
0\leq t\leq T
, where

\displaystyle R_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\max\{C_{\rho}2\sqrt{(1+2R)e},e^{1/2}R_{\rho}\},

(305)

\displaystyle L_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\left(\frac{2C_{\eta}^{2}H_{s}^{2}C_{\rho}^{2}}{1+2R}+2(1+2R)\exp(6)R_{\varphi}^{2}\right)^{1/2},\left(2C_{\eta}^{2}H_{s}^{2}R^{2}+e^{2}L_{\rho}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\},

(306)

for a universal constant
C_{\eta}>0
.

Proof.

We show it only when
t=kh
for a positive integer
k
just for simplicity. The proof for a general
t
can be obtained in the same manner.

(i) First, we assume that
T-t\geq 1/4(1+2R)
.
In the following, we let
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be an arbitrary vector with
\|v\|=1
.
We again utilize the Bismut-Elworthy-Li formula:

\displaystyle v^{\top}\nabla\varphi_{Y,t}(x)

(307)

\displaystyle=v^{\top}\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{*}({X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})\mid{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x]

(308)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{S}\int_{0}^{S}\langle\eta_{\tau},{\textnormal{d}}B_{\tau}\rangle\varphi_{Y,S}({X}_{S}^{\leftarrow})\mid{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x\right].

(309)

Hence,

\displaystyle(v^{\top}\nabla\varphi_{Y,t}(x))^{2}

(310)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\rho}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{S^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{S}\langle\eta_{\tau},{\textnormal{d}}B_{\tau}\rangle\right)^{2}\mid{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x\right]

(311)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\rho}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{S^{2}}\int_{0}^{S}\|\eta_{\tau}\|^{2}{\textnormal{d}}\tau\mid{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x\right].

(312)

Here, we know that
\|\eta_{\tau}\|^{2}\leq\exp(4(1+2R)\tau)\|v\|^{2}
, and thus

\displaystyle(v^{\top}\nabla\varphi_{Y,t}(x))^{2}\leq C_{\rho}^{2}\frac{1}{S}\exp(4(1+2R)S)\|v\|^{2}.

(313)

Hence, by taking
S=\frac{1}{4(1+2R)}
, we have that

(v^{\top}\nabla\varphi_{Y,t}(x))^{2}\leq C_{\rho}^{2}4(1+2R)e\|v\|^{2}.

This shows that
\|\nabla\varphi_{Y,t}(x)\|
is bounded by
R_{\varphi}=C_{\rho}2\sqrt{(1+2R)e}
.

Next, we show its Lipschitz continuity.
For that purpose, we define two stochastic processes

\displaystyle{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x,\ \mathrm{d}{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}=\{{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}+2s({X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh)\}\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{\tau}~~(\tau\in[kh,k(h+1)]),

(314)

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=y,\ \mathrm{d}\tilde{Z}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}=\{\tilde{Z}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}+2s(\tilde{Z}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh)\}\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{\tau}~~(\tau\in[kh,k(h+1)]),

(315)

where
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
\|x-y\|\leq\varepsilon
.
Accordingly, we also define

\displaystyle\eta_{{X},0}=v,~~\frac{{\textnormal{d}}\eta_{{X},\tau}}{{\textnormal{d}}\tau}=(I+2\nabla_{x}^{\top}s({X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh))\eta_{{X},\tau},

(316)

\displaystyle\eta_{Z,0}=v,~~\frac{{\textnormal{d}}\eta_{Z,\tau}}{{\textnormal{d}}\tau}=(I+2\nabla_{x}^{\top}s(\tilde{Z}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh))\eta_{Z,\tau}.

(317)

Thus,

\displaystyle{X}_{(k+1)h}^{\leftarrow}-\tilde{Z}_{(k+1)h}^{\leftarrow}={X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow}-\tilde{Z}_{kh}^{\leftarrow}+h[{X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow}-\tilde{Z}_{kh}^{\leftarrow}+2(s({X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh)-s(\tilde{Z}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh))],

(318)

which yields

\displaystyle\|{X}_{(k+1)h}^{\leftarrow}-\tilde{Z}_{(k+1)h}^{\leftarrow}\|\leq

\displaystyle(1+h(1+R))\|{X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow}-\tilde{Z}_{kh}^{\leftarrow}\|

(319)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+h(1+R))^{k+1}\|x-y\|.

(320)

Now, we assume
k\leq S/h
so that we have

\|{X}_{(k+1)h}^{\leftarrow}-\tilde{Z}_{(k+1)h}^{\leftarrow}\|\leq\exp(S(1+R))\|x-y\|
for
k=1,\dots,S/h
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{{\textnormal{d}}(\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau})}{{\textnormal{d}}\tau}

(321)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau})+2\nabla_{x}^{\top}s({X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh))\eta_{{X},\tau}-2\nabla_{x}^{\top}s(\tilde{Z}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh))\eta_{Z,\tau}

(322)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau})+2(\nabla_{x}^{\top}s({X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh)-\nabla_{x}^{\top}s(\tilde{Z}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh))\eta_{{X},\tau}-2\nabla_{x}^{\top}s(\tilde{Z}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh)(\eta_{Z,\tau}-\eta_{{X},\tau}),

(323)

which also yields that

\displaystyle\frac{{\textnormal{d}}\|\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau}\|^{2}}{{\textnormal{d}}\tau}

(324)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\|\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau}\|^{2}+4(\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau})^{\top}(\nabla_{x}^{\top}s({X}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh)-\nabla_{x}^{\top}s(\tilde{Z}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh))\eta_{{X},\tau}

(325)

\displaystyle-4(\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau})^{\top}\nabla_{x}^{\top}s(\tilde{Z}_{kh}^{\leftarrow},kh)(\eta_{Z,\tau}-\eta_{{X},\tau})

(326)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau}\|^{2}+4\|\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau}\|H_{s}\exp(S(1+R))\varepsilon\exp(2(1+2R)S)

(327)

\displaystyle+4R\|\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau}\|^{2}.

(328)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{{\textnormal{d}}\|\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau}\|}{{\textnormal{d}}\tau}

(329)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+2R)\left[\|\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau}\|+2H_{s}\exp(S(1+R))\exp(2(1+2R)S)\varepsilon/(1+2R)\right],

(330)

and thus by noticing
\|\eta_{{X},0}-\eta_{Z,0}\|=0
, we have

\displaystyle\|\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau}\|\leq[\exp(S(1+2R))-1]\frac{2H_{s}\exp(S(1+R))\exp(2(1+2R)S)}{1+2R}\varepsilon,

(331)

for any
\tau\leq S
. Then, by setting
S=1/(1+2R)
, the right hand side can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\|\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau}\|\leq C_{\eta}\frac{H_{s}}{1+2R}\varepsilon,

(332)

for an absolute constant

.
Therefore, we arrive at

\displaystyle(v^{\top}(\nabla\varphi_{Y,t}(x)-\nabla\varphi_{Y,t}(y)))^{2}

(333)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{S}\int_{0}^{S}\langle\eta_{{X},\tau},{\textnormal{d}}B_{\tau}\rangle\varphi_{Y,S}({X}_{S}^{\leftarrow})-\frac{1}{S}\int_{0}^{S}\langle\eta_{Z,\tau},{\textnormal{d}}B_{\tau}\rangle\varphi_{Y,S}(\tilde{Z}_{S}^{\leftarrow})\right]^{2}

(334)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{S^{2}}\int_{0}^{S}(\eta_{{X},\tau}-\eta_{Z,\tau})^{2}{\textnormal{d}}\tau\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{Y,S}({X}_{S}^{\leftarrow})^{2}\right]

(335)

\displaystyle+2\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{S^{2}}\int_{0}^{S}\eta_{Z,\tau}^{2}{\textnormal{d}}\tau\right]\mathbb{E}\left[(\varphi_{Y,S}({X}_{S}^{\leftarrow})-\varphi_{Y,S}(\tilde{Z}_{S}^{\leftarrow}))^{2}\right]

(336)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{S}C_{\eta}^{2}\frac{H_{s}^{2}}{(1+2R)^{2}}\varepsilon^{2}\cdot C_{\rho}^{2}+\frac{2}{S}\exp(4(1+2R)S)\|v\|^{2}R_{\varphi}^{2}\exp(2S(1+R))\varepsilon^{2}.

(337)

Then, for the choice of
S=1/(1+2R)
, the right hand side can be bounded by

\left(\frac{2C_{\eta}^{2}H_{s}^{2}C_{\rho}^{2}}{1+2R}+2(1+2R)\exp(6)R_{\varphi}^{2}\right)\varepsilon^{2}.

This implies that
\nabla\varphi_{Y,t}(\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous with a constant
L_{\varphi}=\left(\frac{2C_{\eta}^{2}H_{s}^{2}C_{\rho}^{2}}{1+2R}+2(1+2R)\exp(6)R_{\varphi}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
.

(ii) Next, we assume that
T-t\leq S=1/4(1+2R)
.
In this situation, we may use the following relation:

\displaystyle v^{\top}\nabla\varphi_{Y,t}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\tau}^{T-t}\nabla\rho_{*}({X}_{T}^{\leftarrow})\mid{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}=x].

(338)

And, tracing an analogous argument by replacing

with

, we obtain the assertion with
