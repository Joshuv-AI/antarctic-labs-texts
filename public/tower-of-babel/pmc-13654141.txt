Fibrous tissue ingrowth through a pectus bar fixation hole resulting in difficult bar removal after a single-incision Nuss procedure: a case description
Authors: Meng Chen, Yimin Xie, Qiang Chen
Published: 2026

Introduction

Pectus excavatum is the most common congenital chest wall deformity (1). It is estimated to affect approximately 1 in 300 to 400 individuals and is more prevalent in males than females (1). Analyses of population-level search behavior indicate that the condition also attracts considerable public interest, with distinct seasonal patterns (2).

Pectus excavatum is typically managed conservatively or by surgical correction (3,4). The Nuss procedure has become the standard minimally invasive approach for surgical correction of pectus excavatum, and various single-incision modifications have been developed to further reduce surgical invasiveness (5-11). Previous clinical observations suggest that peri-bar fibrous tissue may form within 4–6 months of bar implantation and may contribute to bar stabilization (8). Based on this observation, our center (Department of Pediatric Surgery, Chongqing University Three Gorges Hospital, Chongqing, China) employs a single-incision technique that applies polydioxanone sutures (PDS) for fixation on the incision side and relies on fibrous tissue formation for stabilization on the non-incision side (Figure 1). This modification reduces contralateral soft-tissue dissection and removes the need for a second fixation site on the non-incision side.

Schematic diagram of fibrous tissue encapsulation of the pectus bar after a single-incision Nuss procedure. Red shading represents the fibrous tissue surrounding the Nuss system. (A) Incision side. (B) Non-incision side. The figure shows the overall pattern of fibrous tissue encapsulation.

Peri-bar fibrous encapsulation is an expected response to long-term bar implantation. We report a case in which fibrous tissue had grown through an unused fixation hole in the bar, resulting in transverse mechanical anchoring that impeded removal. In this article, we describe the configuration-specific entrapment, the associated intraoperative warning sign, and the principles for safe bar removal.

Case presentation

The patient, an 11-year-old boy, presented with anterior chest discomfort and moderate restrictive ventilatory dysfunction. Physical examination revealed symmetric sternal depression. Chest computed tomography (CT) is not routinely performed as a general screening examination at our institution. However, low-dose chest CT is selectively performed in children for operative planning, when quantification of the Haller index and assessment of chest-wall morphology are considered necessary; radiation-minimizing protocols are used whenever CT is performed in children. CT confirmed Park type I pectus excavatum with a Haller index of 4.0.

The preoperative laboratory investigations were unremarkable. The patient met the indications outlined in the National Consensus on Surgery for Pectus Excavatum in China (12). Given the Haller index of 4.0, symptomatic chest discomfort, and the restrictive ventilatory dysfunction, surgery was not undertaken for cosmetic reasons alone. After comprehensive discussion, the family elected to proceed with the single-incision Nuss procedure and informed consent was obtained.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia. A left lateral thoracic incision was made between the anterior and mid-axillary lines. After thoracoscopic confirmation of normal intrathoracic anatomy, a pectus bar and stabilizer were inserted. The bar was secured to the stabilizer with stainless steel wire (#5, ETHICON®, XZW945), and the stabilizer was fixed to the adjacent ribs with PDS sutures (#2, ETHICON®, XCW9236). The postoperative course was uneventful during the subsequent three-year follow-up period.

At 14 years and 3 months of age, the patient was scheduled for bar removal. The modified acupotomy needle-blade used for percutaneous release was fashioned from a lumbar drainage needle (Figure 2). Through the original incision, the stainless-steel wire securing the bar to the stabilizer was removed and the ingrown fibrous tissue was released; the PDS sutures placed at implantation had already been completely absorbed. The stabilizer was then removed (13). During bar extraction, focal depression of the contralateral chest wall developed near the bar terminus on the non-incision side, and abnormal resistance to extraction was encountered (Figure 3A). As these findings suggested fibrous tethering, forceful traction was discontinued.

Instrument used for percutaneous release: the lumbar drainage needle from which the instrument was fashioned, shown alongside the resulting modified acupotomy needle-blade.

Intraoperative photographs. (A) Focal depression of the contralateral chest wall during bar extraction, indicating fibrous tethering. (B) Percutaneous release of the tethering fibrous tissue with the modified acupotomy needle-blade, performed without an additional contralateral incision.

The serrated edge of the bar terminus was identified by palpation through the skin. Guided by continuous palpation of the bar surface, the needle-blade was introduced percutaneously at the point of maximal depression and advanced in short, controlled movements immediately adjacent to the palpable bar edge, dividing the tethering tissue and then the fibrous tissue passing through the fixation hole (Figure 3B; Video 1). No intraoperative imaging or thoracoscopic guidance was used during this maneuver. After controlled release, the bar was extracted smoothly without an additional contralateral incision, and no clinically apparent bleeding or soft-tissue injury occurred.

Intraoperative video of bar removal. The sequence shows the focal depression of the contralateral chest wall during traction, palpation of the serrated bar terminus through the skin, and percutaneous division of the tethering fibrous tissue with the modified acupotomy needle-blade, followed by smooth extraction of the bar without an additional contralateral incision.

Postoperative chest radiography showed no pneumothorax. The released tissue had the gross appearance and mechanical consistency of mature scar-like fibrous tissue; no specimen was submitted for histopathological examination.

The patient recovered uneventfully and was discharged on postoperative day four. At 2 years after bar removal, clinical follow-up showed a well-maintained chest-wall contour. Formal reassessment of pulmonary function and thoracic volume was not performed, limiting broader conclusions regarding thoracic growth.

Discussion

Peri-bar fibrous encapsulation is common after long-term implantation and may contribute to bar stabilization. In many cases, this tissue can be separated or traversed during routine extraction; however, dense adhesions, osseous reactions, and rib-related changes may complicate bar removal. In the present case, the tissue traversed an unused fixation hole, creating a transverse tether and resulting in a distinct mechanical obstacle to axial bar withdrawal. This represents a specific and rarely documented configuration of fibrous tissue entrapment rather than a novel form of fibrosis. The proposed mechanism and percutaneous release technique are illustrated schematically in Figure 4.

Schematic illustration of the mechanism and its percutaneous release. (A) Bar in situ: fibrous tissue surrounds the serrated bar edge, and a transverse band passes through the unused fixation hole. (B) During extraction, this band resists axial withdrawal and transmits traction to the chest wall, producing focal contralateral depression (blue arrow, direction of extraction force). (C) Percutaneous release: the acupotomy needle-blade is introduced by puncture at the point of maximal depression and advanced along the palpable bar surface (yellow arrows) to divide the tether; no contralateral incision is required. The trajectory shown is schematic and does not represent a verified distance from the pleura, lung, or intercostal vessels. (D) With the tether divided, the fixation hole and serrated edge are free, allowing smooth bar removal.

The focal contralateral chest-wall depression observed during extraction reflects traction transmitted through the fibrous band to the chest wall and represents an intraoperative warning sign. Continued forceful extraction may increase the risk of soft-tissue, vascular, or intrathoracic injury (14-16); thus, traction should be discontinued once abnormal resistance and focal chest-wall depression are observed.

In the present case, the tethering tissue was released percutaneously with a modified acupotomy needle-blade, thereby avoiding an additional incision. Percutaneous release is not mandatory; however, an open contralateral exposure represents a reliable alternative when percutaneous localization is uncertain, osseous adhesion is suspected, or controlled release cannot be achieved. Some surgeons further advocate routinely opening the contralateral site at the time of bar removal to permit release under direct visualization; this may represent the safest default approach, particularly for surgeons who are not familiar with percutaneous release.

A single case cannot establish the superiority of percutaneous release over conventional surgical release. The distinction in the present case is that the needle-blade was used percutaneously, without a contralateral incision, while open release requires direct exposure. Acupotomy instruments have been used for percutaneous soft-tissue release in other anatomical settings, most commonly under ultrasound guidance (17); however, their application to pectus bar removal has not been established.

This case should not be interpreted as an endorsement of single-incision fixation. Bar migration remains a challenge even with conventional bilateral fixation, and more robust stabilization strategies, including bridge fixation and multiple-bar techniques, have subsequently been developed (18,19). The omission of contralateral mechanical fixation may reduce structural stability, while the bilateral-incision technique may offer practical advantages, including bilateral placement of cryoanalgesia probes for intercostal nerve cryoablation, secure bilateral stabilizer fixation, and full thoracoscopic access to both pleural cavities. It may also provide greater operative control when a second bar is required. Direct comparative evidence between single-incision and conventional bilateral-incision approaches remains limited; contemporary high-volume studies have more commonly evaluated other technical variables, including the number of corrective bars used (19).

Operative timing also remains individualized. Some centers prefer to operate after 13 years of age. However, in this case, the Haller index of 4.0, symptoms, and physiological impairment supported intervention despite the patient’s relatively young age. As our center does not have a contemporaneous bilateral-incision cohort, we could not directly compare bar displacement risk between the two techniques. The tissue reactions, rib-related changes, and dense adhesions observed at explantation are potential challenges inherent to pectus bar removal rather than complications unique to this modification; the specific finding in this case was a transfixing fibrous configuration through an unused fixation hole. Where feasible, the use of a bar without an open fixation hole at the non-incision end might reduce the likelihood of this specific configuration, although this possibility requires further evaluation.

In conclusion, fibrous tissue growing through an unused fixation hole may anchor a pectus bar and complicate its removal after a single-incision Nuss procedure. Focal contralateral chest-wall depression accompanied by abnormal resistance during extraction represents a warning sign; forceful traction should be discontinued, and the tethering tissue should be released under controlled conditions. Further evaluation is warranted to clarify the relationship between fixation-hole configuration and patterns of fibrous ingrowth.
