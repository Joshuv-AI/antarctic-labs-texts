Frank-Wolfe Algorithms for ( L 0 , L 1 ) (L_{0},L_{1}) -smooth functions
A.A. Vyguzov, F.S. Stonyakin

Abstract
We propose a new version of the Frank–Wolfe method, called the ( L 0 , L 1 ) (L_{0},L_{1}) -Frank–Wolfe algorithm, developed for optimization problems with ( L 0 , L 1 ) (L_{0},L_{1}) -smooth objectives. We establish that this algorithm achieves superior theoretical convergence rates compared to the classical Frank–Wolfe method. In addition, we introduce a novel adaptive procedure, termed the Adaptive ( L 0 , L 1 ) (L_{0},L_{1}) -Frank–Wolfe algorithm, which dynamically adjusts the smoothness parameters to further improve performance and stability. Comprehensive numerical experiments 1 1 1 The code can be downloaded here confirm the theoretical results and demonstrate the clear practical advantages of both proposed algorithms over existing Frank–Wolfe variants.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in Q}\;f(x),

(1)

where
Q
is a convex compact set and
f
is a convex and
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth function.

A function
f
is said to be
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth
if, for all
x
, it satisfies

\|\nabla^{2}f(x)\|\leq L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x)\|,

(2)

for some constants
L_{0},L_{1}>0
(throughout this paper, unless specified otherwise, we use the standard Euclidean norm
\|.\|
for vectors, and the standard spectral norm
\|.\|
for matrices). This notion was introduced in
[
21
]
, where it was used to explain the superior convergence behavior of the clipped gradient descent method on deep learning problems compared to standard gradient descent (GD).

Later, in
[
20
]
(see Remark 2.3), this definition was extended from twice differentiable to once differentiable functions. Specifically, a differentiable function
f
is said to be
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth if, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\|x-y\|\leq\frac{1}{L_{1}}
, we have

\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\leq(L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(y)\|)\|x-y\|.

(3)

This condition generalizes the standard
L
-smoothness assumption. Indeed, when
L_{1}=0
, the inequalities above reduce to the classical definition of
L
-smoothness. Hence, the
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness condition defines a broader class of functions than the conventional
L
-smooth case.

Motivated by this generalization, we modify the standard shortest-step rule in the Frank–Wolfe method. Let us briefly recall the classical Frank–Wolfe algorithm
[
6
]
with the shortest-step rule
[
10
]
. The update rule is given by

x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}d_{k},

(4)

where
k
is the iteration index,
\alpha_{k}\in[0,1]
,
d_{k}=s_{k}-x_{k}
, and

s_{k}\in\mathrm{LMO}_{Q}(\nabla f(x_{k}))=\arg\min_{z\in Q}\,(\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}z)

(5)

is the output of the linear minimization oracle (LMO) over
Q
. In the case of the shortest-step rule, the step size is chosen as

\alpha_{k}:=\min\left\{1,\frac{-\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{L\|d_{k}\|^{2}}\right\}.

This observation motivates us to extend the Frank–Wolfe method (
4
) to
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth objectives by introducing the following step-size rule:

\alpha_{k}:=\min\left\{1,\frac{-\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{(L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|)\,\|d_{k}\|^{2}_{2}\,e}\right\}.

(6)

It is well known (see, for example,
[
3
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
10
]
) that the Frank–Wolfe method achieves a linear convergence rate under additional assumptions.

Our theoretical analysis shows that the proposed Frank–Wolfe variant, with the step size defined in (
6
), exhibits a superior convergence rate. The acceleration arises from the fact that
L_{1}<L
and
L_{0}<L
, where
L
denotes the standard smoothness parameter. When the solution lies in the interior of a function satisfying the PL condition, our algorithm is significantly faster than the standard method due to the absence of the PL-condition parameter
\mu
in the convergence rate when
L_{0}>L_{1}|\nabla f(x_{k})|
. A summary of the corresponding convergence rate estimates is provided in Table
1
.

Moreover, as mentioned earlier, not all
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth functions are
L
-smooth, meaning that our algorithm applies to a broader class of functions.
To illustrate this, we list several examples (see
[

Next, we introduce a novel adaptive procedure that updates each of the parameters

This procedure differs from standard adaptive techniques—originally proposed in

1.1
Contributions and Outline

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose a new variant of the Frank–Wolfe algorithm designed for
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth objectives and provide a theoretical analysis showing that it achieves superior convergence rates compared to the classical Frank–Wolfe method.

•

We support our theoretical findings by numerical experiments demonstrating that the proposed algorithm significantly outperforms the standard variant.

•

We introduce a novel adaptive procedure for dynamically updating the
(L_{0},L_{1})
parameters. Experimental results show that this adaptation mechanism consistently yields substantial performance improvements across all tested settings.

•

We prove that, in the general convex case, the proposed algorithm is never worse than the classical Frank–Wolfe method and maintains the optimal convergence rate of
O(1/k)
.

Although the

The Frank-Wolfe method is a fundamental tool in constrained optimization. Its projection-free nature and reliance on a linear minimization oracle make it computationally efficient, especially in high-dimensional settings and for structured domains such as simplices, trace-norm balls, and polytopes. In the work

Lemma 1

f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x)\|}{2}\exp\left(L_{1}\|x-y\|\right)\|x-y\|^{2}.

We will derive our algorithm from that upper bound. That inequality can be seen as a generalization of the standard quadratic upper bound

1.4
Frank-Wolfe gap.

Important property of Frank-Wolfe algorithm is FW gap (or in some literature "dual gap", see
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
). That property is the upper bound of a closeness to the optimal solution which implies from the convexity of objective function. So that quantity can be used as stopping criteria.

Definition 1

The Frank-Wolfe gap of a function
f:Q\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

G(x)=\max_{s\in Q}-\nabla f(x)^{\top}(s-x).

Then we have important inequality which we will use often in our analyzes:

G(x)\geq\nabla f(x)^{\top}(x-x^{*})\geq f(x)-f^{*}>0.

(7)

Before doing so, we first establish a descent lemma, which is a standard preliminary result in the analysis of first-order methods.
In our case, we formulate it for the class of
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth objective functions.

Lemma 2
(Descent lemma)

Suppose that
f
is a convex function satisfying the
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness condition (Definition
3
).
Then, for Algorithm
1
and for every iteration
k\geq 1
, the following inequality holds:

f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\leq\frac{\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{2}\cdot\min\left\{1,\;\frac{-\,\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\right)\|d_{k}\|^{2}e}\right\},

where
D=\max_{x,y\in Q}\|x-y\|
and
d_{k}=s_{k}-x_{k}
with
s_{k}\in\mathrm{LMO}(\nabla f(x_{k}))
.

Proof

Let us denote
a_{k}=L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
.

Since
f
satisfies the
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness condition, we can apply Lemma
1
to bound the function and substitute the Frank-Wolfe step (
4
):

f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\leq\alpha_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}+\frac{a_{k}}{2}e^{\alpha_{k}L_{1}\|d_{k}\|}\alpha_{k}^{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}.

(8)

If we choose
\alpha_{k}
such that

\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|=\alpha_{k}\|d_{k}\|\leq\frac{1}{L_{1}},

(9)

then the exponential term can be bounded as
e^{\alpha_{k}L_{1}\|d_{k}\|}\leq e
, and inequality (
8
) simplifies to

f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\leq\alpha_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}+\frac{a_{k}e}{2}\alpha_{k}^{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}.

(10)

Minimizing the right-hand side of (
10
) with respect to
\alpha_{k}
under the constraint
0<\alpha_{k}\leq 1
yields

\alpha_{k}^{*}=\min\left\{1,\frac{-\,\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{a_{k}\|d_{k}\|^{2}e}\right\}.

(11)

We now verify that
\alpha_{k}^{*}
satisfies condition (
9
), i.e.,
\alpha_{k}^{*}\leq 1/(L_{1}\|d_{k}\|)
.
Indeed, when
\alpha_{k}^{*}<1
, we have

(-\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k})L_{1}\|d_{k}\|\leq\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\|d_{k}\|L_{1}\|d_{k}\|e\leq a_{k}\|d_{k}\|^{2}e,

which directly implies that

\frac{-\,\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{a_{k}\|d_{k}\|^{2}e}\leq\frac{1}{L_{1}\|d_{k}\|}.

In the case
\alpha_{k}^{*}=1
, the same inequality follows from the step definition, namely

1\leq\frac{-\,\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{a_{k}\|d_{k}\|^{2}e}\leq\frac{1}{L_{1}\|d_{k}\|}.

Since the step size
\alpha_{k}^{*}
satisfies condition (
9
), inequality (
10
) holds with
\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{k}^{*}
.
Substituting
\alpha_{k}
into the second term of (
10
), we obtain

\begin{split}f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})&\leq\alpha_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}+\frac{e}{2}a_{k}\|d_{k}\|^{2}\alpha_{k}\left(\frac{-\,\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{a_{k}\|d_{k}\|^{2}e}\right)\\
&=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}\,\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}.\end{split}

(12)

Finally, substituting
\alpha_{k}
from (
11
) into (
12
) gives the desired result.
This completes the proof.

2.1
Linear convergence under strongly convex sets

We now establish the linear convergence rate of Algorithm
1
.
For this result, the objective function
f
is not required to be strongly convex; instead, we assume that the feasible set
Q
is strongly convex.

Definition 2
(Strongly convex sets
[
10
]
)

A set
Q
is called
strongly convex
if there exists
\lambda>0
such that for any
x,y\in Q
, every point
z
satisfying

\|z-(x+y)/2\|\leq\lambda\|x-y\|^{2}

also belongs to
Q
.

Examples of strongly convex sets include
\ell_{2}
-balls and ellipsoids.
Such sets possess several important properties, one of which is the so-called
scaling condition
.

Proposition 1
(Scaling condition for strongly convex sets)

Let
Q
be a strongly convex set with constant
\lambda
(Definition
2
), and let
\psi
be any nonzero vector.
Define
s=\operatorname*{argmin}_{y\in Q}\psi^{\top}y
.
Then, for all
x\in Q
, the following inequality holds:

-\psi^{\top}(s-x)\geq 2\lambda\|\psi\|\|s-x\|^{2}.

The original statement of this proposition appears in the seminal work of Levitin and Polyak
[
10
]
(Theorem 6.1, p. 5).
An alternative proof can be found in
[
3
]
(Proposition 2.19), where the result is established for a more general class of
(\alpha,q)
-uniformly convex sets.

Theorem 2.1

Let
f
be a convex
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth function such that
\|\nabla f(x)\|>c>0
, and let
Q
be a strongly convex set with constant
\lambda
.
Then Algorithm
1
satisfies

f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)\min\left\{\frac{1}{2},\,1-\frac{\lambda}{2eL_{1}}\right\}^{T}\min\left\{\frac{1}{2},\,1-\frac{\lambda c}{2eL_{0}}\right\}^{K},

where
T
denotes the number of iterations such that
L_{0}\leq L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
, and
K
denotes the number of iterations such that
L_{0}>L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
.

Proof

We distinguish two cases.

Case 1:

\alpha_{k}<1
.
Substituting the corresponding step size (
6
) into the descent inequality of Lemma
2
, we obtain

\begin{split}f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})&\geq\frac{1}{2e}\cdot\frac{(\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k})^{2}}{a_{k}\|d_{k}\|^{2}}\overset{\eqref{def:scaling_condition}}{\geq}\frac{\lambda}{e}\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}{a_{k}}(-\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k})\overset{\eqref{fw_gap}}{\geq}\\
&\frac{\lambda}{e}\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}{a_{k}}(f(x_{k})-f^{*})=\frac{\lambda}{e}\cdot\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}(f(x_{k})-f^{*}).\end{split}

After straightforward algebraic manipulation, we get

f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq(f(x_{k})-f^{*})\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{e}\cdot\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}{L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}\right).

We now consider two subcases.
If
L_{0}\leq L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
, then

f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq(f(x_{k})-f^{*})\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2eL_{1}}\right).

Otherwise, when
L_{0}>L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
, we have

f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq(f(x_{k})-f^{*})\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{e}\cdot\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}{2L_{0}}\right)\leq(f(x_{k})-f^{*})\left(1-\frac{\lambda c}{2eL_{0}}\right).

Case 2:

\alpha_{k}=1
.
In this case, applying Lemma
2
with
\alpha_{k}=1
yields

f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\leq\frac{\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{2}\overset{\eqref{fw_gap}}{\leq}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(f(x_{k})-f^{*}\bigr),

which implies

f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq\frac{1}{2}\bigl(f(x_{k})-f^{*}\bigr).

Combining these inequalities gives the claimed linear convergence rate.

This completes the proof.

As previously noted, the estimate in Theorem
2.1
improves upon the classical Frank-Wolfe method with the shortest step size, whose rate satisfies

f(x_{k})-f^{\ast}\leq(f(x_{0})-f^{\ast})\max\!\left\{\frac{1}{2},\,1-\frac{\lambda c}{2L}\right\}^{k-1},

(see, e.g.,
[
3
]
), particularly in the regime where
L\gg L_{1}
and
L\gg L_{0}
.

2.2
Linear convergence when
x^{*}\in\mathrm{Int}(Q)
and the Gradient Dominance Condition

We now establish the linear convergence rate of Algorithm
1
under a different set of assumptions.
In this setting, we consider objective functions that satisfy the Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) inequality.

Definition 3
(Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition)

A differentiable function
f
is said to satisfy the
PL condition
if there exists a constant
\mu>0
such that

\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}\geq\mu\,(f(x)-f^{*}),\quad\forall x.

It is important to note that, in this case, the small parameter
\mu
is absent when
L_{0}>L_{1}|\nabla f(x_{k})|
, which results in a faster convergence of our algorithm compared to the standard one.

The PL condition was introduced by Polyak
[
17
]
.
This class of functions includes all strongly convex functions.
Later, Karimi et al.
[
8
]
showed that the PL condition also encompasses several broader families, such as weakly strongly convex functions, functions satisfying the restricted secant inequality, and essentially strongly convex functions.
Typical examples include logistic regression loss and least-squares objectives.

In this setting, we relax the assumptions on the feasible set.
Specifically, we require only that
Q
is convex and that the solution
x^{*}
lies in its interior, i.e.,
x^{*}\in\mathrm{Int}(Q)
.
We now recall a well-known scaling condition that holds under this assumption.

Proposition 2
(Scaling condition when
x^{*}\in\mathrm{Int}(Q)
)

Let
Q
be a convex compact set, and let
f
be a smooth convex function.
If there exists
r>0
such that
B(x^{*},r)\subseteq Q
, then for all
x\in Q
we have

-\nabla f(x)^{\top}(s-x)\geq r\,\|\nabla f(x)\|.

The proof of this result can be found in
[
3
]
, Proposition 2.16.

We are now ready to prove a linear convergence rate for convex objectives satisfying the PL condition.
Notably, this result does not require a lower bound on
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
, in contrast to Theorem
2.1
.

Theorem 2.2

Suppose that
f
is a convex
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth function satisfying the PL condition
3
with constant
\mu>0
.
If there exists
r>0
such that
B(x^{*},r)\subseteq Q
for the solution
x^{*}
, then Algorithm
1
satisfies

f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq(f(x_{0})-f^{*})\min\left\{\frac{1}{2},\,1-\frac{r}{4eL_{1}D^{2}}\right\}^{T}\min\left\{\frac{1}{2},\,1-\frac{r^{2}\mu}{2eL_{0}D^{2}}\right\}^{K},

where
T
denotes the number of iterations for which
L_{0}\leq L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
,
and
K
denotes the number of iterations for which
L_{0}>L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|

Proof

As in Theorem
2.1

Case 1:
Algorithm

\alpha_{k}<1

Starting from the descent inequality of Lemma

\displaystyle f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2e}\cdot\frac{(\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k})^{2}}{a_{k}\|d_{k}\|^{2}}\overset{\eqref{prop:scaling_condition_for_convex_set}}{\geq}\frac{r^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}}{2ea_{k}\|d_{k}\|^{2}}.

Recalling that
a_{k}=L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
, we distinguish two subcases.

When
L_{0}\leq L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{r^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}}{4eL_{0}\|d_{k}\|^{2}}\overset{\eqref{def:pl_cond}}{\geq}\frac{r^{2}\mu(f(x_{k})-f^{*})}{2eL_{0}\|d_{k}\|^{2}}\geq\frac{r^{2}\mu(f(x_{k})-f^{*})}{2eL_{0}D^{2}}.

After straightforward algebra, we obtain

f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq(f(x_{k})-f^{*})\left(1-\frac{r^{2}\mu}{2eL_{0}D^{2}}\right).

(13)

In the remaining case, when
L_{0}>L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
, we have

\begin{split}f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})&\overset{\eqref{prop:scaling_condition_for_convex_set}}{\geq}\frac{r\,(-\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k})}{4eL_{1}\|d_{k}\|^{2}}\overset{\eqref{fw_gap}}{\geq}\frac{r(f(x_{k})-f^{*})}{4eL_{1}\|d_{k}\|^{2}}\geq\frac{r(f(x_{k})-f^{*})}{4eL_{1}D^{2}}.\end{split}

Consequently,

f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq(f(x_{k})-f^{*})\left(1-\frac{r}{4eL_{1}D^{2}}\right).

(14)

Case 2:

\alpha_{k}=1
.
Following the same reasoning as in Theorem
2.1
, we use the descent inequality of Lemma
2
with the appropriate step size:

f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\leq\frac{\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{2}\overset{\eqref{fw_gap}}{\leq}-\frac{1}{2}\left(f(x_{k})-f^{*}\right),

which implies

f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq\frac{1}{2}(f(x_{k})-f^{*}).

Combining these inequalities yields the claimed linear convergence rate.
This completes the proof.

Finally, we compare this result with the classical Frank–Wolfe convergence estimate for
L
-smooth functions under the same setting:

f(x_{k})-f^{\ast}\leq(f(x_{0})-f^{\ast})\max\!\left\{\frac{1}{2},\,1-\frac{r^{2}\mu^{2}}{2LD^{2}}\right\}^{\,k-1}.

Once again, we observe an acceleration when
L\gg L_{1}
and
L\gg L_{0}
.
However, we emphasize an additional distinction from the classical formulation: in our case, acceleration occurs not only due to the inequalities
L\gg L_{1}
and
L\gg L_{0}
, but also due to the absence of the
\mu
parameter from the PL condition
3
in the case
L_{0}>L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
.
Since
\mu
is typically small, the classical convergence estimate is often slower than the rate established for our proposed algorithm.

Lemma 3

Suppose that

(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth function
3
with constants

and
L_{1}
, and let

denote the number of inequality checks in line

Then, for an adaptation factor

, the following bound holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{N}n_{i}

\displaystyle\leq N\!\left(1+\frac{\log(\rho+1)}{\log(\rho-1)}\right)+\frac{1}{\log(\rho-1)}\log\!\frac{\min\{\rho L_{0},L_{0}^{\max}\}+\min\{\rho L_{1},L_{1}^{\max}\}}{L_{0}^{(0)}+L_{1}^{(0)}}

\displaystyle=O(N).

(15)

Proof

Let
a_{k}=L_{0}^{(k)}+L_{1}^{(k)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
,
p_{0}=L_{0}^{(k)}/a_{k}
, and
p_{1}=(L_{1}^{(k)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|)/a_{k}
.
From Algorithm
2
, during iteration

L_{0}^{(i)}+L_{1}^{(i)}=\frac{\prod_{j=0}^{n_{i}-1}(\rho-p_{0}^{(j)})}{\rho+p_{0}}\,L_{0}^{(i-1)}+\frac{\prod_{j=0}^{n_{i}-1}(\rho-p_{1}^{(j)})}{\rho+p_{1}}\,L_{1}^{(i-1)}.

Since
p_{0}+p_{1}=1
, we can bound

L_{0}^{(i)}+L_{1}^{(i)}\geq\frac{(\rho-1)^{n_{i}-1}}{\rho+1}\bigl(L_{0}^{(i-1)}+L_{1}^{(i-1)}\bigr).

Taking logarithms yields

n_{i}\leq 1+\frac{\log(\rho+1)}{\log(\rho-1)}+\frac{1}{\log(\rho-1)}\log\frac{L_{0}^{(i)}+L_{1}^{(i)}}{L_{0}^{(i-1)}+L_{1}^{(i-1)}}.

By Definition
3
and the fact that our function is
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth with some parameters
L_{0},L_{1}
, we have

L_{0}^{(i)}\leq\max\{\rho L_{0},\,L_{0}^{\max}\}
and

L_{1}^{(i)}\leq\max\{\rho L_{1},\,L_{1}^{\max}\}
,
which bounds the numerator in the logarithm.
Summing over

yields the claimed

\sum_{i=0}^{N}n_{i}\leq N\!\left(1+\frac{\log(\rho+1)}{\log(\rho-1)}\right)+\frac{1}{\log(\rho-1)}\log\!\frac{\min\{\rho L_{0},L_{0}^{\max}\}+\min\{\rho L_{1},L_{1}^{\max}\}}{L_{0}^{(0)}+L_{1}^{(0)}}.

This concludes the proof.

4
Convergence rate for the general convex case

In that section we will proof the suboptimal convergence rate of Algorithm
1
for the general convex case.

Lemma 5

Suppose that for a sequence
h_{0},\ldots,h_{N}
and some
K_{\text{max}}>0
we have
h_{k}>0
and

h_{k+1}\leq h_{k}\left(1-\frac{h_{k}}{K_{\text{max}}}\right).

Then it holds that

h_{k+1}\leq\frac{K_{\text{max}}}{k+3}.

Proof

We prove the statement by induction. For
k=0
we have

h_{1}\leq h_{0}\left(1-\frac{h_{0}}{K_{\text{max}}}\right)\overset{\textcircled{1}}{\leq}\frac{K_{\text{max}}}{4}\leq\frac{K_{\text{max}}}{3}=\frac{K_{\text{max}}}{0+3},

where ① follows from the upper bound of the univariate function
f(x)=x-\frac{x^{2}}{K_{\text{max}}}
for
x\geq 0
.

Now assume the statement holds for some
k
, and let us prove

h_{k+2}\leq\frac{K_{\text{max}}}{k+4}.

(16)

We consider two cases.
If
h_{k+1}\leq\frac{K_{\text{max}}}{k+4}
, then (
16
) follows directly since
\left(1-\frac{h_{k+1}}{K_{\text{max}}}\right)\leq 1
in the assumption.

Otherwise, if
h_{k+1}>\frac{K_{\text{max}}}{k+4}
, we have

h_{k+2}\leq h_{k+1}\left(1-\frac{1}{k+4}\right)=h_{k+1}\frac{k+3}{k+4}\overset{\textcircled{1}}{\leq}\frac{K_{\text{max}}}{k+3}\cdot\frac{k+3}{k+4}=\frac{K_{\text{max}}}{k+4},

where ① follows from the induction hypothesis.

This completes the proof.

Theorem 4.1

Suppose that
f
is a convex function satisfying
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smoothness
3
. Then for Algorithm
1
, for each iteration
k\geq 1
, we have

f(x^{k})-f(x^{*})\leq\frac{2eD^{2}\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\sup_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\right)}{k+3},

where
D=\max_{x,y\in Q}\|x-y\|
.

Proof

We use the descent inequality from Lemma
2
:

f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\leq\frac{\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{2}\cdot\min\left\{1,\;\frac{-\,\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\right)\|d_{k}\|^{2}e}\right\}.

(17)

We again consider two cases.

Case 1:
\boldsymbol{\alpha(k)=1.}

From (
17
) we have

f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\leq\frac{1}{2}\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}\overset{\eqref{fw_gap}}{\leq}\frac{1}{2}(f^{*}-f(x_{k})),

and hence

f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq\frac{1}{2}(f(x_{k})-f^{*}).

Furthermore, subtracting
f^{*}
from both sides of
(
10
) with
\alpha(k)=1
gives

\begin{split}f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}&\leq-f^{*}+f(x_{k})+\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}+\frac{a_{k}}{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}e\\
&\overset{\eqref{fw_step_size}}{\leq}\underbrace{-f^{*}+f(x_{k})+\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}(x^{*}-x_{k})}_{\leq 0\text{ (by convexity)}}+\frac{a_{k}}{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}e\leq\frac{a_{k}}{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}e.\end{split}

Thus, for
\alpha(k)=1
, we obtain the inequality

f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq\min\left\{\frac{a_{k}}{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}e,\;\frac{1}{2}(f(x_{k})-f^{*})\right\}.

(18)

Case 2:
\boldsymbol{\alpha(k)<1.}

Substituting the step size (
11
) into
(
17
) yields

f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k})^{2}}{a_{k}\|d_{k}\|^{2}e}.

(19)

We now establish the convergence rate by induction.
For
k=0
and
\alpha(0)=1
, from (
18
) we have

f(x_{1})-f^{*}\leq\frac{a_{0}}{2}\|d_{0}\|^{2}e\leq\frac{2\sup_{k\in[0,1]}a_{k}D^{2}e}{4}.

For arbitrary
k+1
, the desired inequality follows directly from the induction hypothesis and the right-hand side of (
18
).

If
\alpha(k)<1
, we slightly reformulate (
12
):

f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq(f(x_{k})-f^{*})-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k})^{2}}{a_{k}\|d_{k}\|^{2}e}\overset{\eqref{fw_gap}}{\leq}(f(x_{k})-f^{*})\left(1-\frac{\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d_{k}}{2a_{k}\|d_{k}\|^{2}e}\right).

Denoting
h_{k}=f(x_{k})-f^{*}
and
K=2a_{k}\|d_{k}\|^{2}e
(note that
h_{k}\leq K
by (
17
) and the convexity of
f
) and applying Lemma
5
, we obtain

f(x_{k+1})-f^{*}\leq\frac{2eD^{2}\left(L_{0}+L_{1}\sup_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\right)}{k+3}.

This concludes the proof.

It is a well-known fact that the
O(1/k)
rate is optimal for the Frank–Wolfe method (see
[
16
]
).
In the asymptotic regime, our convergence estimate is therefore not better than the standard Frank–Wolfe rate, but it is not worse either.

5.1
Objective Function

We consider the optimization of the logistic regression objective:

5.2

The matrix

A\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Then each column was multiplied on scalar

We considered three geometric settings for the data distribution:

1.

Random points uniformly distributed on the

2.

Random points uniformly distributed on the 1-simplex, with increasing dimension.

3.

Random points uniformly distributed on the

We compared the following methods:

Classic FW

is the Lipschitz constant of the gradient and
s_{k}
is the solution of the linear minimization oracle (LMO).

•

This adaptive strategy was analyzed in
[

•

(L_{0},L_{1})
-FW
:proposed algorithm (Algorithm

•

(L_{0},L_{1})

5.4
Results and Discussion

The experimental results clearly demonstrate that the additional adaptive correction introduced in the proposed Adapt
(L_{0},L_{1})
-FW algorithm provides consistent acceleration in all test cases.
This algorithm achieved the best performance across all experiments. Exactly the same thing we can say about
(L_{0},L_{1})
-FW algorithm which hugely outperform the standard algorithm.

The reason for this adaptive procedure improvement above standard procedure lies in the finer tuning of the step size: the adaptive update in Adapt
(L_{0},L_{1})
-FW increases the parameter more smoothly and conservatively, avoiding abrupt changes that can hinder convergence.
Moreover, alternating the parameter updates further improves convergence speed, since the aggregate value

a_{k}=L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|

grows more gradually and stabilizes earlier than in more aggressive adaptive schemes.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced two new versions of the Frank–Wolfe method designed for
(L_{0},L_{1})
-smooth objective functions: the
(L_{0},L_{1})
-FW
algorithm and its adaptive counterpart, the
Adaptive
(L_{0},L_{1})
-FW
algorithm.
For both methods, we established convergence guarantees under several settings, including general convex objectives, convex objectives over strongly convex feasible sets, and convex objectives satisfying the Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) condition when the solution lies in the interior of the feasible set.

The numerical experiments confirm the theoretical findings and demonstrate that the proposed algorithms outperform both the classical Frank–Wolfe method and existing adaptive variants.
In particular, the adaptive version shows a clear advantage due to its ability to dynamically adjust the parameters
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
based on the local geometry of the problem.

Our adaptation strategy differs from previous approaches that adjust only a single Lipschitz-like constant.
Instead, we adapt both parameters simultaneously, accounting for their relative contributions in the composite smoothness model, which makes the procedure more flexible and responsive.

Despite these promising results, several open questions remain. How large can adaptable parameters become compared to the true sum involving the actual
(L_{0},L_{1})
parameters? And what are the best initial choices of
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
when these parameters are not known in advance?
