The hidden subgroup problem for infinite groups
Greg Kuperberg

Abstract
Dedicated to Eleanor Rieffel, my friend and colleague for 42 years and counting. Following the example of Shor’s algorithm for period-finding in the integers, we explore the hidden subgroup problem (HSP) for discrete infinite groups. On the hardness side, we show that HSP is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard for the additive group of rational numbers, and for normal subgroups of non-abelian free groups. We also indirectly reduce a version of the short vector problem to HSP in ℤ k \mathbb{Z}^{k} with pseudo-polynomial query cost. On the algorithm side, we generalize the Shor–Kitaev algorithm for HSP in ℤ k \mathbb{Z}^{k} (with standard polynomial query cost) to the case where the hidden subgroup has deficient rank or equivalently infinite index. Finally, we outline a stretched exponential time algorithm for the abelian hidden shift problem (AHShP), extending prior work of the author as well as Regev and Peikert. It follows that HSP in any finitely generated, virtually abelian group also has a stretched exponential time algorithm.

Introduction

Some of the most important quantum algorithms are those that solve
rigorously stated computational problems, and that are superpolynomially
faster than classical alternatives. This includes Shor’s famous quantum
algorithm for period finding
Sho 97
, with the equally famous
applications of integer factorization and discrete logarithm problems.
The hidden subgroup problem has been a popular framework for many such
algorithms
ME 99
. We recall its formulation: If
G
is a
discrete group and
X
is an unstructured set, a function
f:G\to X

hides
a subgroup
H\subseteq G
means that
f(x)=f(y)
if and
only if
y=xh
for some
h\in H
. In other words,
f
hides
H
when
it is right
H
-periodic and otherwise injective, as summarized in this
commutative diagram:

G
X
G/H
f

(1.1)

Such an
f
is also called a
hiding function
, while
H
is called a

hidden subgroup
. In an HSP algorithm,
f
is typically functional
input: It is given by either a subroutine or an oracle that returns
individual values
f(x)
. Given a group
G
and given a hiding function

f
, the
hidden subgroup problem
(HSP) is then the question of
calculating the hidden subgroup
H
.

The computational complexity of HSP depends greatly on the ambient group

G
. We may also consider variations in which we promise that
H
is a
normal subgroup, or that
H
is drawn from a specific conjugacy class of
subgroups, etc. The most interesting cases of HSP are those that have
an efficient quantum algorithm, but no efficient classical algorithm.
When
f
is given by an oracle, there is often an unconditional proof that
HSP is classically intractable.

The most widely discussed case of Shor’s algorithm solves HSP when
G=\mathbb{Z}
, and thus
H=p\mathbb{Z}
for some period
p
, in quantum polynomial time.
Even though the group
\mathbb{Z}
is infinite and Shor’s algorithm is one motivation
for HSP, many of the results since then have been about HSP for finite
groups
(
CvD 10
, Ch. VII)
. In this article, we will examine HSP
in several cases when the ambient group
G
is infinite (and discrete).
We establish quantum algorithms in some key cases, but our more surprising
results are hardness results instead, and we lead with these.

We first consider the additive group
G=\mathbb{Q}
, which is abelian but is
not finitely generated. To state our result, we simplify the hidden
subgroup problem to a decision problem. We define the hidden subgroup

existence
problem (HSEP) to be the yes-no question: Is the hidden
subgroup
H
non-trivial? (In Section
2.4
and Proposition
2.3
, we
will discuss why a natural version of HSEP lies in
\mathsf{NP}
.)

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}
be the set of rational numbers viewed as a
discrete group under addition. Then HSEP in the quotient
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
is

\mathsf{NP}
-complete, using either an integer factoring oracle, or assuming a
conjecture in number theory (Conjecture
3.5
). Equivalently, HSP in

\mathbb{Q}
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete if the question is to determine whether the hidden
subgroup strictly contains
\mathbb{Z}
.

Note that Theorem
1.1
yields unconditional
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness with

\mathsf{BQP}
reduction, since Shor’s algorithm can factor integers in quantum
polynomial time.

Our second result is about the normal hidden subgroup problem (NHSP) and
its existence version (NHSEP) in a non-abelian group, meaning HSP with
the extra restriction that the hidden subgroup
H
is normal.

Theorem 1.2
.

NHSEP in a finitely generated, non-abelian free group
F_{k}

is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

We contrast Theorem
1.2
with several results for finite groups. NHSP in
a finite group
G
has a polynomial-time quantum algorithm whenever
the quantum Fourier transform on
G
can be computed in polynomial time

HRTS 00
. For this reason and others, NHSP is considered easier
than the general HSP, although general HSP does have polynomial quantum
query complexity for all finite
G

EHK 04
. By contrast,
the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
both relativize
(see Section
2.2
). As a corollary, both of these infinite HSP problems
have high query complexity.

Corollary 1.3
.

HSEP in
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
and NHSEP in a non-abelian free group

F_{k}
both have quantum query complexity
2^{\Theta(n)}
, where
n
is
the minimum bit complexity to describe a hidden subgroup.

The abelianization of the free group
F_{k}
is the free abelian group

\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, but the abelian analogue of Theorem
1.2
is not the same HSP
that is solved by any version of Shor’s algorithm. The reason is that the
length of a query
w\in F_{k}
in Theorem
1.2
is its word length, which
is analogous to the 1-norm
\lVert\vec{v}\rVert_{1}
of
\vec{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
rather than
the bit complexity
\lVert\vec{v}\rVert_{\text{bit}}
of
\vec{v}
. In other words, the query
complexity is pseudo-polynomial (polynomial in
\lVert\vec{v}\rVert_{1}
) rather
than polynomial (in
\lVert\vec{v}\rVert_{\text{bit}}
). We can also obtain a hardness
result for this alternate HSP for
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. The same pseudo-polynomial
query cost model arises in a quantum algorithm due to Childs, Jao, and
Soukharev
CJS 14
that identifies isogenies between ordinary
elliptic curves. (However, the hidden subgroups that they consider are
different from the ones in the hardness reduction in Theorem
1.4
below.)

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
f:\mathbb{Z}^{k}\to X
be a hiding function with
pseudo-polynomial query cost, and suppose that
f
hides a subgroup
H=\langle\vec{v}\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
which is generated by a single vector
\vec{v}
.
Suppose that there is a (quantum) algorithm to find
H
in polynomial
time, uniformly for all
k
and
f
. Then the unique short vector problem

\mathsf{uSVP}_{a,\operatorname{Poly}(a,k)}
for an integer lattice
L\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
has a
(quantum) algorithm that runs in time
\operatorname{poly}(a,k,\lVert L\rVert_{\text{bit}})
.

In Section
6.1
, we outline another reduction from HSP on
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
with
pseudo-polynomial query cost to identify a lattice from samples with
error. The two reductions together are similar to Regev’s quantum reduction
from short vector problems to learning with errors (LWE)
Reg 09
.
However, Regev’s reduction is much deeper than ours.

Turning to positive results, it has sometimes been said that HSP is solved
for finitely generated abelian groups
G

CvD 10
;
ME 99
.
However, the well-known algorithm due to Shor and Kitaev for this problem

Kit 95
assumes that the hidden subgroup
H
has finite index
in
G
. We give a solution in the trickier case when
H
may have infinite
index or equivalently lower rank.

Theorem 1.5
.

HSP in
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
(with polynomial query cost for the hiding
function) can be solved for arbitrary hidden subgroups uniformly in quantum
polynomial time
\operatorname{poly}(k,n)
, where
k
is the dimension and
n
is the
bit complexity of the answer.

Our proof of Theorem
1.5
begins with the same steps as other Shor-type
algorithms. We apply the hiding function
f
to a superposition of a finite
truncation of
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. Ignoring the output register, we then apply a QFT to
the input register and measure a Fourier mode. In Shor’s algorithm and in
Kitaev’s generalization, the Fourier mode is deciphered using continued
fractions to recognize rational values from their digit expansions.
Deciphering the Fourier mode in Theorem
1.5
is more difficult than
in the Shor–Kitaev algorithm. Our solution uses the LLL lattice reduction
algorithm
LLL 82
, facts about generalized Fourier transforms
(or Pontryagin duality), and various error estimates.

The time complexity in Theorem
1.5
is uniformly polynomial in the
dimension
k
along with other parameters. Implicitly,
k
is given
in unary, which is consistent with the standard notation that requires
one or more bits for each coordinate of a vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, including for
vanishing coordinates. We can also consider compressed notation for sparse
vectors in an abelian group with exponential rank
k
. As discussed in
Section
3.4
, HSP even in the simplest case
(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{k}
becomes
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
if
k
is exponential and vectors are given in sparse notation.

Section
3.4
also defines sparse and dense encodings of elements of

\mathbb{Z}^{\infty}
(the group of infinite sequences of integers with finite
support). We can restate Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
,
together with the proof of Theorem
3.6
, as results about
\mathbb{Z}^{\infty}

using different possible encodings of its elements.

Corollary 1.6
.

Consider HSP in the group
\mathbb{Z}^{\infty}
with a finitely
generated hidden subgroup
H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{\infty}
whose generator matrix has
bit complexity
n
. If
\mathbb{Z}^{\infty}
is given with dense encoding of vectors
and binary encoding of coefficients, then HSP can be solved in quantum
time
\operatorname{poly}(n)
. If
\mathbb{Z}^{\infty}
is given with dense encoding of vectors and
unary encoding of coefficients, then HSP is
\mathsf{uSVP}_{a,\operatorname{Poly}(a,k)}
-hard. If

\mathbb{Z}^{\infty}
is given with sparse encoding of vectors and either unary or
binary encoding of coefficients, then HSP is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

The positive case of Corollary
1.6
is equivalent to Theorem
1.5
,
given the observation that
H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{\operatorname{poly}(n)}
. The negative
case is similarly equivalent to Theorem
1.4
.

We also state a corollary of Theorem
1.5
which applies to any fixed
finitely generated abelian group
G
. In other words, it is a solution
to the abelian hidden subgroup problem (AHSP) in the finitely-generated case.

Corollary 1.7
.

If
G
is a finitely generated abelian group, then
HSP in
G
for an arbitrary hidden subgroup
H
can be solved in quantum
polynomial time in the bit complexity of the answer.

Proposition
2.2
in Section
2.3
describes the relevant encoding of
elements of
G
for Corollary
1.7
, in the greater generality of finitely
generated, virtually abelian groups. Briefly, if a finitely generated
group
G
has an efficient algorithm to compute canonical compressed words,
then those words work as a standard encoding of elements of
G
. If
G

is abelian, then this amounts to describing every
g\in G
as an ordered
product of generators of
G
with the exponents in binary. See also
Section
2.4
for a brief proof of Corollary
1.7
given Theorem
1.5
.

We conclude with a sketch of a result about the abelian hidden shift problem
(AHShP). See Section
2.4
for a definition of the hidden shift problem
(HShP) and a discussion of its relation to HSP.

Theorem 1.8

(Outlined)
.

Let
H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
be a visible subgroup
of any rank with a generator matrix
M
, and let
h=\lVert M\rVert_{\text{bit}}
.
Consider AHShP in the quotient group
\mathbb{Z}^{k}/H
with a hidden shift
\vec{s}
,

n\geq\lVert\vec{s}\rVert_{\text{bit}}
, and assume that
n=\Omega((k+\log(h))^{2})
.
Then there is a quantum algorithm for this problem with quantum time
complexity
2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
, quantum space complexity
\operatorname{poly}(n)
, and
classical space complexity
2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
. The hiding function query
cost may be polynomial or may be as high as
2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
.

Here,
H
is a
visible
subgroup means that its generator matrix
M

is directly given as part of the input.

In Section
8
, we will give an algorithm for AHShP and an informal
analysis that together amount to an outline of a proof of Theorem
1.8
.
We omit some of the statistical estimates that would be needed for a rigorous
proof. Our algorithm is a variation of prior algorithms for dihedral
HSP due to the author
Kup 05
;
Kup 13
, Regev
Reg04b
,
and Peikert
Pei 20
.

As discussed in Section
2.4
, Theorem
1.8
yields the following
corollary for HSP for virtually abelian groups (VAHSP). Recall that a
group
G
is
virtually abelian
when it has a finite-index abelian
subgroup
K\subseteq G
. The corollary generalizes the remark that HSP
for a dihedral group is equivalent to HShP for a cyclic group.

Corollary 1.9
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated, virtually abelian group.
Then HSP in
G
(with compressed word polynomial query cost) can be solved
in time
2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
, where
n
is the bit complexity of the answer.

Background

2.1
Basic notation

We will use the asymptotic notation
\operatorname{poly}(n)
to mean
O(n^{\alpha})
and

\operatorname{Poly}(n)
to mean
\Omega(n^{\alpha})
. We will also extend this notation to
several variables; for instance,
\operatorname{poly}(x,y)
means
O((x+y)^{\alpha})
, etc.
We also follow the convention that if
f(x)=\Omega(g(x))
, then
f
and
g

are both eventually positive and
f(x)/g(x)
is eventually bounded below.
Finally, we will often use asymptotic notation with or without uniformity
assumptions. If
f(x)=O(g(x))
, meaning that
|f(x,y)|\leq Cg(x)

eventually, the constant
C
may or may not be independent of
y
; and
the estimate might only be uniform when
y
lies in a restricted range.

We will often use vector notation
\vec{x}
to refer to points in
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
,

\mathbb{R}^{k}
, etc., mainly to suggest the geometric interpretation of vectors.
This notation is optional and we do not use it for quantum states (we use
Dirac notation
\lvert\psi\rangle
instead), nor for bit strings except when they
are used for linear algebra in
(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{k}
.

2.2
Computational complexity

We refer to Arora–Barak
AB 09
for basics of computational
complexity theory, but we also clarify several points.

As explained in Section
1
, Theorems
1.1
and
1.2

are stated for the hidden subgroup existence problem (HSEP) rather than
the hidden subgroup problem (HSP). Both problems are
\mathsf{NP}
-hard given
the hypotheses of the theorems. However, it does not make sense to
ask whether HSP is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete because it is a function problem and
therefore cannot be in
\mathsf{NP}
. By contrast, HSEP is a decision problem;
as stated in Proposition
2.3
below, a natural interpretation of HSEP is
in
\mathsf{NP}
for any explicit group
G
.

Since HSEP is a decision problem, we can also ask whether it is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
with Karp–Post reduction rather than Cook–Turing reduction. This is
what Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
both achieve. The only
caveat is that the reduction in Theorem
1.1
requires either integer
factorization or Conjecture
3.5
. As explained in Section
1
, since
Shor’s algorithm can factor integers in
\mathsf{BQP}
, we obtain a reduction in

\mathsf{BQP}
unconditionally.

Second, it may seem confusing that HSP can be
\mathsf{NP}
-hard, given
that in full generality it is an oracle problem. To address this,
given a decision problem
d\in\mathsf{NP}
, we construct a hiding function

f
that has a polynomial-time reduction to
d
, thus replacing the
oracle by an
instance
. In fact, the construction works just
as well if
d\in\mathsf{NP}^{g}
, where
g
is another oracle. In other words,
Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
are
\mathsf{NP}
-completeness results
that relativize. Relativizing
\mathsf{NP}
-completeness is an unusual concept
that is possible in this case because HSP is an oracle problem.

Third, the computational complexity of any oracle problem can be modified by
declaring a time cost function for oracle calls, rather than the more usual
convention of unit cost. If an oracle query has length
n
, then the oracle
call has a tacit time cost of
\Omega(n)
, given just the time to write the
query to the oracle tape or register. However, we can also declare a time
fee
t(n)
for such an oracle call that depends on the input length
n
,
or even a fee
t(x)
that depends on the specific input
x
to the oracle.
In the statement of Theorem
1.4
, we assume that the hiding function
f

is an oracle with
pseudo-polynomial
cost. This means that the cost
to evaluate
f(\vec{v})
with
\vec{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
is a polynomial in the 1-norm

\lVert\vec{v}\rVert_{1}
and the dimension
k
, rather than polynomial in the bit
complexity of a description of
\vec{v}
.

An oracle fee can also often be emulated by demanding verbose input. This is
another way to implement a suitable special case of the oracle assumption in
Theorem
1.4
. If the components of a vector
\vec{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
are written
in unary, then its total symbol length is
\Theta(\lVert\vec{v}\rVert_{1}+k)
, counting
also the negation symbol and the commas to separate vector components.

2.3
Explicit groups and canonical words

To interpret the hidden subgroup problem as a computational problem, we need
a computational model for both the group
G
and the hiding function
f
.
In this subsection, we consider a computational model of a discrete,
countably infinite group
G
. We call such a
G
an
explicit group

if it is given as a subset of the set of strings
A^{*}
over some alphabet

A
with a membership function, a group law, and an inverse function:

\begin{array}[]{c@{\qquad}c}d:A^{*}\to\{\text{yes},\text{no}\}&d(x)=x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\in}}G\\
m:G\times G\to G&m(x,y)=xy\\
i:G\to G&i(x)=x^{-1}\end{array}

We assume that all three of these functions are computable in
\mathsf{P}

or functional
\mathsf{P}
. Aaronson and the author previously gave a related
definition of an explicit sequence of finite groups
(
AK 07
, Def. 5.7)
,
except with a trivial membership test.

To interpret our theorems, we need to describe the additive group of
rationals
\mathbb{Q}
, the free groups
F_{k}
, and the free abelian groups
\mathbb{Z}^{k}

as explicit groups. We assume familiar notation for each of these three
examples, in which case the algorithms for
d
,
m
, and
i
are all
straightforward:

1.

We put each rational
t\in\mathbb{Q}
in lowest terms
t=a/b
using the
Euclidean algorithm, which runs in polynomial time. The numerator and
denominator are written in binary.

2.

We express each element
w\in F_{k}
as the unique reduced word
in the
k
generators of the free group
F_{k}
, and their inverses.

3.

In the version of HSP that we solve in Theorem
1.5
, we express
each element
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
as a list of integers in binary. Or we can
match the oracle cost hypothesis of Theorem
1.4
if we write each
coefficient
v_{j}
of
v
in unary. (We also consider sparse
encoding of vectors in Section
3.4
.)

If
G
and
H
are two explicit groups, we define an
efficient
homomorphism
to be a homomorphism
f:G\to H
that can be computed in
deterministic polynomial time. Likewise if
f
is an isomorphism,
then it is
efficient
when
f
and
f^{-1}
are both efficient.
Note that two explicit groups can be isomorphic as groups
without any efficient isomorphism between them. In other words, that
the same group can have more than one explicit encoding.

Examples
.

The group
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
is isomorphic to a free product

(\mathbb{Z}/2)*(\mathbb{Z}/3)

Alp 93
. This yields two explicit encodings,
the former using integer matrices and the latter using words in the
presentation

(\mathbb{Z}/2)*(\mathbb{Z}/3)=\langle a,b\mid a^{2}=b^{3}=1\rangle.

More precisely, we can canonically name elements of
(\mathbb{Z}/2)*(\mathbb{Z}/3)

as words in the two letters
a
and
b
that do not contain either
aa

or
bbb
. These two explicit encodings cannot be equivalent under any
isomorphism due to the equation

\begin{bmatrix}1&N\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}^{N}.

This matrix is an
N
th power and its word must have length
\Theta(N)
,
but its matrix form has bit complexity
\Theta(\log(N))
.

Another example is the infinitely generated abelian group
(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{\infty}

in Section
3.4
. We can encode an element
x\in(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{\infty}

with a variable-length, finite bit string. Alternatively, we can
list the terms of a basis expansion of
x
with a sparse encoding as in
equation (
3.8
). The two encodings are not equivalent under any
automorphism of
(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{\infty}
. In the sparse encoding, exponentially
many linearly independent vectors have polynomial-length encodings, which
is not possible in the standard dense encoding.

Our definition of an explicit group
G
only stipulates a polynomial-time
algorithm to multiply two group elements and group inverses, equivalently a
polynomial-time algorithm to evaluate words in
G
of any bounded length.
We are interested in two refinements of this concept. We call an explicit
group
G

word efficient
if there is an algorithm to evaluate words
in
G
which is uniformly polynomial time for all words. This concept
can be taken one step further if we consider algebraic circuits
C
in
the group law of
G
. In some of the literature, such circuits are also
called
straight-line programs

BS 84
;
Loh 14
. In a
straight-line program, we build a word
w
in
G
by naming intermediate
words that can then be reused. Such a word
w
can also be called a

compressed word
, since similar data structures are used in data
compression methods that name and reuse subwords. We call an explicit
group
G

compressed-word efficient
if it has a polynomial time
algorithm to evaluate algebraic circuits in elements of
G
.

Suppose that
G
is a group generated by the finite set
A
. Then every
element
g\in G
can be expressed, non-uniquely, by words in the set
W_{A}=(A\cup A^{-1})^{*}
. In some cases, there is an efficient algorithm to
convert any word
w\in W_{A}
to a
canonical word
with the same
value
g\in G
, and that represents each
g\in G
uniquely. We can
extend the same concept to compressed words. It may happen that
G

has a polynomial-time algorithm to convert every compressed word in the
alphabet
A
that represents an element
g\in G
to a
canonical
compressed word
for the same
g
. It is easy to see that a canonical
word algorithm or a canonical compressed word algorithm for
G
yields
an explicit structure for
G
. The following proposition is also not
difficult to prove, but it is more subtle.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a group generated by a finite set
A
.

1.

If
G
has an explicit structure given by a canonical word algorithm
for words in
W_{A}
, then
G
is word efficient. If
f:G\to H
is a
homomorphism to another word-efficient group
H
, then
f
is efficient.
In particular, any two canonical word algorithms (even in different finite
generating sets) for
G
yield isomorphic explicit structures.

2.

If
G
has an explicit structure given by a canonical compressed-word
algorithm for compressed words in
W_{A}
, then
G
is compressed-word
efficient. If
f:G\to H
is a homomorphism to another word-efficient
group
H
, then
f
is efficient. In particular, any two canonical
compressed-word algorithms for
G
yield isomorphic explicit structures.

We now examine evaluation of words and compressed words for the three types
of groups defined above.

1.

The additive group of rationals
\mathbb{Q}
is not finitely generated,
so we cannot compare their notation to canonical words or canonical
compressed words. However, the standard notation for rational numbers is
efficient for algebraic circuits that only use the group law of
\mathbb{Q}
.

2.

The standard notation for elements of a free group
F_{k}

using reduced words is a canonical (uncompressed) word structure.

3.

If each vector
v\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
is expressed in binary, then the notation
is equivalent to canonical compressed words in
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. If each
v
is
expressed in unary, then this is equivalent to canonical (uncompressed)
words in
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
.

Remark
.

Remarkably, there is an efficient algorithm to calculate
canonical compressed words in
F_{k}\cong F_{A}
, where
A
is an alphabet with

k
letters. This is established by Lohrey
Loh 14
, whose work
is the culmination of older results by various authors. In Section 3.4
of his book, Lohrey describes a recompression technique due to Jéz that
converts any algebraic circuit

Besides an explicit group, the other ingredient in the hidden subgroup

3.1
Partial fractions

Our idea to calculate canonical representatives of cosets of a subgroup of

\mathbb{Q}
is based on the concept of partial fractions from calculus. Partial
fractions have a generalized form in any principal ideal domain, where they
are related to the Chinese remainder theorem and the Bézout equation.
Recall that every rational function
a(x)/b(x)
over
\mathbb{R}
is uniquely a
sum of special terms called
partial fractions
. For example,

\frac{x^{6}+x^{4}-x+2}{x^{5}-x^{2}}=x-\frac{x-1}{x^{2}+x+1}+\frac{1}{x-1}+\frac{1}{x}-\frac{2}{x^{2}}.

In this decomposition, the first term is a polynomial, while each other
term is of the form
r(x)/p(x)^{k}
, where
p(x)
is irreducible over
\mathbb{R}
and

\deg r(x)<\deg p(x)
. We will need the following analogous lemma in
\mathbb{Q}
.

Lemma 3.1

(Partial fractions in
\mathbb{Q}
)
.

Every rational number
a/b\in\mathbb{Q}

has a unique representation

\frac{a}{b}=n+\sum_{(p,k,r)}\frac{r}{p^{k}},

(3.1)

where
n
is any integer, each
p
is a prime number, each exponent
k

is positive, each numerator
r
satisfies
1\leq r<p
, and each pair

(p,k)
appears at most once. Moreover, given the prime factorization of
the denominator
b
, the partial fraction decomposition can be computed
in
\mathsf{P}
.

For example,

\frac{1}{360}=-2+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{8}+\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{9}+\frac{3}{5}.

(3.2)

Lemma
3.1
is stated and proved by Bourbaki

(
Bou 90
, Sec. VII.2.3)
in the greater generality of
an arbitrary principal ideal domain
A
and its fraction field
F(A)
.
Partial fractions as they arise in calculus are then the special case
A=\mathbb{R}[x]
. However, since Bourbaki does not discuss algorithms, we provide a
(similar) proof that explains why it also provides an efficient algorithm.

Before proving Lemma
3.1
, we restate it with a slightly different
canonical form. We can sum all of the terms for each prime
p
to retain
equation (
3.1
), but now with the rule that each prime
p
(rather than each
pair
(p,k)
) appears exactly once in the summation, and each numerator
r

satisfies
1\leq r<p^{k}
and
p\nmid r
. For example, the abbreviated
form of equation (
3.2
) is

\frac{1}{360}=-2+\frac{5}{8}+\frac{7}{9}+\frac{3}{5}.

Proof.

If
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{\ell}
are coprime
integers and

is their product, then the standard Chinese remainder
theorem

\mathbb{Z}/b\cong\mathbb{Z}/b_{1}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/b_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}/b_{\ell}

can be rescaled by

and thus expressed as the group isomorphism

(\frac{1}{b}\mathbb{Z})/\mathbb{Z}=(\frac{1}{b_{1}}\mathbb{Z})/\mathbb{Z}\oplus(\frac{1}{b_{2}}\mathbb{Z})/\mathbb{Z}\oplus\cdots\oplus(\frac{1}{b_{\ell}}\mathbb{Z})/\mathbb{Z}.

(3.4)

We write (
3.4
) as an equality, which is valid because

(\frac{1}{b_{j}}\mathbb{Z})/\mathbb{Z}\subseteq(\frac{1}{b}\mathbb{Z})/\mathbb{Z}
for each

, and the right
side of (
3.4
) is then an internal direct sum. Note that either
version of the Chinese remainder theorem has an algorithm in
\mathsf{P}
using
modular reciprocals, and thus the Bézout equation and the Euclidean
algorithm.

We can also lift equation (
3.4
) to a set arithmetic summation of intervals:

\frac{1}{b}\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{b_{1}}[0,b_{1})_{\mathbb{Z}}+\frac{1}{b_{2}}[0,b_{2})_{\mathbb{Z}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{b_{\ell}}[0,b_{\ell})_{\mathbb{Z}}.

(3.5)

Since (
3.4
) is a bijection, (
3.5
) is also
a set arithmetic bijection.

Finally, since (
3.3
) is a ring isomorphism, it yields the
multiplicative group isomorphism

(\mathbb{Z}/b)^{\times}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/b_{1})^{\times}\oplus(\mathbb{Z}/b_{2})^{\times}\oplus\cdots\oplus(\mathbb{Z}/b_{\ell})^{\times}.

(3.6)

We can combine equation (
3.6
) with equation (
3.5
) to obtain the set arithmetic
bijection

(\frac{1}{b}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}=\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{b_{1}}[0,b_{1})_{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}+\frac{1}{b_{2}}[0,b_{2})_{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}+\cdots+\frac{1}{b_{\ell}}[0,b_{\ell})_{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}.

(3.7)

Here
(\frac{1}{b}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
is the set of rational numbers
a/b
with

\gcd(a,b)=1
, while
[0,b)_{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
is the set of integers
0\leq a<b
with
\gcd(a,b)=1
. Finally, equation (
3.7
) is the bijection we want
if we take
b_{j}=p_{j}^{k_{j}}
to be the prime power factors of
b
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
: NHSP in free groups

As in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we can prove Theorem
1.2
with
a hiding function
f
on the free group
F_{k}
whose values are canonical
representatives of cosets of a hidden subgroup
N\unlhd F_{k}
. Since
N

is normal, it can be
normally
generated by certificates
w\in F_{k}

that satisfy an
\mathsf{NP}
predicate, meaning that
N
is generated by these
elements and their conjugates. Also since
N
is normal, the coset space

F_{k}/N
is a quotient group
G\cong F_{k}/N
, which is thus a finitely
presented group. Canonical representatives in
F_{k}
of cosets of a normal
subgroup
N
are then exactly canonical words for group elements
g\in G
.

An efficient algorithm to compute canonical words in
G\cong F_{k}/N

yields an efficient algorithm for the word problem in
G
. By definition,
the
word problem
in
G
is the algorithmic question of whether two
words
v
and
w
represent the same element of
G
. Equivalently, the word
problem asks whether an arbitrary word
u=v^{-1}w
represents
1\in G
,
or equivalently whether
u\in N
. An algorithm for the word problem also
implies an algorithm for canonical words: Given a word
w
, we can let its
canonical name be its shortlex equivalent as defined in Section
4.1

below, and we can find
v
by searching through all words up to the length
of
w
. However, an efficient algorithm for the word problem does not
necessarily yield an efficient algorithm to compute canonical words.

Remark
.

The word problem in finitely presented, non-commutative groups
is a major topic in its own right. It has a polynomial-time algorithm in
many finitely presented groups
ECH+ 92
, it can be equivalent
to the halting problem in others
Boo 59
;
Nov 55
, and it is
computable but arbitrarily intractable in yet others
KMS 17
.
Determining whether a finitely presented group is trivial is a related
problem that is also as hard as the halting problem. In our proof of
Theorem
1.2
, we will choose
G\cong F_{k}/N
from a special class of groups
that not only have a polynomial-time algorithm to compute canonical words,
but even an algorithm that is uniformly efficient given only query access
to the relators.

4.1
Group presentations

In this subsection and the next two, we will review some material from
combinatorial group theory and small cancellation theory. We will largely
follow an exposition by Strebel
Str 90
, although some of
the terminology in Section
4.3
is from Peifer
Pei 96

Let

be an alphabet, which in parts of this subsection and the next two
can be either finite or infinite. Recall from Section

that
W_{A}=(A\cup A^{-1})^{*}
is the semigroup of words in letters in

and their
formal inverses. The free group

can be defined as the quotient of

by the equivalence
aa^{-1}\sim 1\sim a^{-1}a
for every

.
A word

is

if it does not have an adjacent pair
of inverse letters. Every element

is represented by a unique reduced
word, which is thus a canonical word. By abuse of notation, we will also
view
F_{A}
as the subset of

consisting of reduced words.

Let

be the set of conjugacy classes in
F_{A}
. Since every word

is conjugate to its cyclic permutations, we can specify a
conjugacy class in
C_{A}
by a
cyclic word

, meaning an orbit
of words under cyclic permutation. The conjugacy class described by
t

does not change if we remove an adjacent, inverse pair of letters anywhere
along the circle of letters of

does not have such a pair of
letters, then it is

, or reduced as a cyclic word.
Every conjugacy class in
C_{A}
is represented by a unique reduced cyclic
word, and we will identify
C_{A}
with the set of such words. Also, by abuse
of notation, we will assume that we can concatenate two linear words
v

and

at both ends rather than at one end to form a cyclic word
t=vw
.

A

G=\langle A\mid R\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}F_{A}/N_{R},

where

is a set of words called

normally generated by

as the quotient of

generated by the equivalences

for all

, and we will write

when

the same element in

is finite, then

is

is

.

If

is a presented group, then we call

in the equivalence class of words

[w]=g\in G

If the alphabet

of the group

is finite and we assume some ordering
of
A\cup A^{-1}

of all of the words in

given by first ordering words by length, and
then within each fixed length ordering them alphabetically. The shortlex
representative

Since

is normally generated by the relators

rather than a set of words in

.
Then since

and that

As remarked above, if

is a finitely presented group, then this by
itself yields few restrictions on the computational complexity of the word
problem or the canonical word problem in

we will consider specific presented groups that have efficient solutions
to their word problems, because their relators do not share long subwords.
This type of behavior for relators is called

More formally, if

4.3
Small cancellation

The goal of this subsection is the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
G
is a finitely presented,
C^{\prime}(1/6)
small cancellation
group, then there is an algorithm to find the shortlex canonical form for

In Section

The author did not find Theorem

If

4.5
Variations and open problems

We briefly consider some variations of the hypotheses of Theorem
1.2
.

Without going into details, there is a simplified version of Theorem
1.2

and its proof when the hidden subgroup
H\subseteq F_{k}
is non-normal
and finitely generated rather than finitely normally generated. If
H\subseteq F_{k}
is any finitely generated subgroup with a given list of
generators, then there is a polynomial-time algorithm to compute canonical
representatives of
F_{k}/H
which is based on building the Schreier graph
of
F_{k}/H

(
GT 87
, Sec. 2.3.1)
. Following equation (
4.1
), the
Schreier graph can still be navigated efficiently in
F_{6}
given only
oracle access to the generators of
H
, if those generators all have the form

h_{y}=y(a_{1},b_{1})y(a_{2},b_{2})y(a_{3},b_{3})

for certificates
y
of an
\mathsf{NP}
predicate
z(x,y)
. The construction
can also be modified for any non-abelian
F_{k}
using the substitution
(
4.2
).

Another variation is to promise that the hidden subgroup
H\subseteq F_{k}

has finite index
[F_{k}:H]<\infty
. In this case,
H
necessarily has

[F_{k}:H](k-1)+1
generators. If the output is a full description of
H

and has length
\operatorname{poly}(n)
for some parameter
n
, then the index is also

\operatorname{poly}(n)
. However, in this case, HSP is in classical
\mathsf{P}
using the
Schreier–Sims algorithm
Sim 70
. On the other hand, if the
question is whether
H
is a non-trivial element of length
\operatorname{poly}(n)

and
[F_{k}:H]
can have exponential size, then we conjecture that HSEP is
again
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

Suppose that the hidden subgroup
H\unlhd F_{k}
is normal and has finite
index, and thus that
G=F_{k}/H
is a finite group of order
[F_{k}:H]
.
Assuming a bound on
\lvert G\rvert
which is exponential in some parameter
n
,
Pyber
Pyb 93
showed that there are only
\exp(\operatorname{poly}(n))
choices
for
G
, and thus a polynomial-length description of
H
of some kind.
Unfortunately, for instance if
G
is cyclic, the generators of
H
might
still have exponential length; worse, it might only be feasible to reach

\operatorname{poly}(n)
elements of
G
and thus only
\operatorname{poly}(n)
values of the hiding
function
f
. However, if we can efficiently evaluate the hiding function

f(w)
for a compressed word given by an algebraic circuit, then it is
feasible to reach every element of
G
as well as a full set of generators
of
H
. We optimistically conjecture that there is an efficient algorithm
for NHSEP in this case, or at least an algorithm with polynomial query
complexity. If it exists, it would generalize the algorithm of Hallgren,
Russell, and Ta-Shma for NHSP in finite groups
HRTS 00
.

Finally, we can go back to NHSP in
F_{k}
when
H
can have infinite index,
but where elements of
F_{k}
are encoded as canonical compressed words.

In this section, we briefly argue Corollary

6.1
Reduction to a lattice learning problem

We have set up Theorem
1.4
as a problem about a lattice
L\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
whose intersection with an allowed ball
B_{r,1}(\vec{0})
is
a discrete line
H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. We conjecture that there are
similar but more complicated reductions even when
L\cap B_{r,1}(\vec{0})

generates all of
L
. If so, then our specific proof of Theorem
1.4

cannot work as described, because the two hiding functions
f
and
g

must agree. However, if we encrypt the values of the hiding function
f

(or even provably obfuscate them with an oracle), then we would expect
an algorithm for HSP to learn something about repeated values of
f
on

B_{r,1}(\vec{0})
. We would therefore expect such an algorithm to detect
vectors in
L
of length at most
2r
.

In contrast with Theorem
1.4
, which is a hardness reduction from the
lattice problem
\mathsf{uSVP}
to HSP on
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
with pseudo-polynomial oracle cost,
we outline a partial algorithm in
\mathsf{BQP}
that reduces this case of HSP to
a certain lattice learning problem. Let
f:\mathbb{Z}^{k}\to X
be a function that
hides a full-rank lattice
H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. We can imitate the Shor–Kitaev
algorithm as described in Section
7
. In our version of this algorithm,
we calculate the hiding function
f
on an approximation of a Gaussian state

\mathbb{R}^{k}

If

\operatorname{Vol}H^{\#}=\operatorname{Vol}H_{\mathbb{R}}/H=\sqrt{\Delta},\qquad H^{\circ}\subseteq\frac{1}{\Delta}\mathbb{Z}^{k},\qquad r_{0}\geq\frac{1}{\Delta}.

Proof.

Let

be a matrix whose columns generate

. The volume

is known as the lattice volume or lattice
determinant of

, and one standard formula for it is

.
Using the fact that
M^{T}M
is the matrix of dot products of the basis

one can check that

is a generating matrix for

.
This implies the standard fact that the lattice volume of

is

i.e.
, that reciprocal lattices have reciprocal lattice volumes.

For the rest of the first equation, we can use the orthogonal direct sum

H^{\bullet}=H^{\circ}\oplus H_{\mathbb{R}}^{\perp}.

Since lattice volume is the reciprocal of lattice density,
H^{\bullet}
thus
has
(k-\ell)
-dimensional volume density
\operatorname{Vol}H_{\mathbb{R}}/H
per unit volume
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. In particular, since the quotient
(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{k}
has unit volume,

H^{\#}
has volume

.

For the second equation, observe that

since vectors
in

have integer dot products with each other. It follows that

\lvert H^{\circ}/H\rvert=V^{2}=\Delta
. Applying Lagrange’s theorem to the
finite group

, we obtain that
\Delta H^{\circ}\subseteq H
,
equivalently that

H^{\circ}\subseteq\frac{1}{\Delta}H\subseteq\frac{1}{\Delta}\mathbb{Z}^{k}.

For an alternate proof of this step,
\Delta(M^{T}M)^{-1}
is an integer
matrix, namely the adjugate matrix of

, which again shows that

\Delta H^{\circ}\subseteq H
.

For the last equation, since the lattice distance of

is

of

is at least

.
∎

7.5
Tempered distributions and Fourier transforms

It will be convenient to use generalized states on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
that are not in
the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
, such as Dirac delta functions and periodic
functions. In physics, such entities are known as “non-normalizable wave
functions”. In rigorous mathematics, a widely useful type of generalized
state is known as a tempered distribution, introduced by Laurent Schwartz.
We will extend all four of the Fourier transforms listed above to tempered
distributions on their domains. We will also interpret all four as special
cases of the Fourier transform operator on tempered distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

A function
\psi(\vec{x})
on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is
Schwartz
when it has all
derivatives, or
\psi(\vec{x})\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
, and every derivative
decays superpolynomially as
\lVert\vec{x}\rVert\to\infty
. For example, Gaussian
functions on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
are Schwartz. The vector space of Schwartz functions
on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is denoted
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
. It has a natural topology which arises
as the limit (or mutual refinement) of an infinite sequence of Hilbert
space topologies.

The concept of a Schwartz function extends to functions
\psi:A\to\mathbb{C}
for other choices of
A
as follows:

1.

If
A=(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{k}
, then
\psi(\vec{x})
is Schwartz when
it is
C^{\infty}
.

2.

If
A=\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, then
\psi(\vec{x})
is Schwartz when it decays
superpolynomially as
\lVert\vec{x}\rVert\to\infty
.

3.

If
A=(\mathbb{Z}/Q)^{k}
, then every function is Schwartz.

A key property of any Schwartz function is that its Fourier transform is
also Schwartz. In other words, there is a Fourier operator

F:\mathcal{S}(A)\to\mathcal{S}(\widehat{A}).

If
A
is one of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
,
(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{k}
, or
(\mathbb{Z}/Q)^{k}
, then a

tempered distribution
(or TD) is a linear functional

\xi:\mathcal{S}(A)\to\mathbb{C}

which is continuous with respect to the topology on
\mathcal{S}(A)
. At a rigorous
level, the input to
\xi
is a Schwartz function
\psi
. For example, if

\xi=\delta_{\vec{x}}
is the unit atomic measure (or Dirac delta function)
at
\vec{x}
, then it is by definition the evaluation map
\delta_{\vec{x}}(\psi)=\psi(\vec{x})
. In Dirac notation,
\xi
is an abstract state with a
well-defined inner product
\langle\psi|\xi\rangle
with every sufficiently tame
state
\lvert\psi\rangle
. Tempered distributions are also written as functions
interpreted through their sums or integrals. For instance, in the case

A=\mathbb{R}^{k}
,

\langle\psi|\xi\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\overline{\psi(\vec{x})}\xi(\vec{x})\;d\vec{x}.

The space of TDs is denoted
\mathcal{S}(A)^{*}
, since it is the topological dual
of
\mathcal{S}(A)
.

Remark
.

Our case-by-case definition of Schwartz functions
and TDs amounts to special cases of a general definition for
arbitrary locally compact, abelian groups which is due to Bruhat

Osb 75
;
Bru 61
. Our treatment describes the
Schwartz–Bruhat theory when
A
(and therefore
\widehat{A}
and every closed
B\subseteq A
) is a finite-dimensional abelian Lie group or discrete group
whose group of components is finitely generated. Every group of this type
is isomorphic to a direct product of copies of the four basic cases
\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{Z}
,
S^{1}\cong\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, and
\mathbb{Z}/Q
.

An important intermediate class between Schwartz functions and TDs is the
space
\mathcal{P}(A)
of
polynomially growing
functions. A smooth function

\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
is polynomially growing if each derivative of

\psi(\vec{x})
is
\operatorname{poly}(\vec{x})
, and we let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
be the space of these.
In the other three cases of interest to us:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z}^{k})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{Z}^{k})^{*}

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}((\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{k})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{S}((\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{k})=C^{\infty}((\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{k})

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}((\mathbb{Z}/Q)^{k})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{S}((\mathbb{Z}/Q)^{k})=\mathbb{C}[(\mathbb{Z}/Q)^{k}].

We state the following standard facts without proof.

Proposition 7.3

If

\displaystyle\lVert F_{(\mathbb{Z}/Q)^{k}}\rVert_{1\to\infty}

\displaystyle=Q^{-k/2}

\displaystyle\lVert P\rVert_{1\to 1}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\lVert\widehat{P}\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}

\displaystyle=1.

We approximate steps 1–3 in Algorithm

The rest of step 2 discards the output register, but this action is optional,
since what is meant is that the algorithm never uses the output register.
For the time being, we keep the output register to help prove estimates.

Lemma 7.9
.

Let

Lemma 7.15
.

If
\vec{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
is a random variable
with density proportional to
\exp(-\pi S^{2}\lVert\vec{u}\rVert_{2}^{2})
, then

\operatorname{Pr}[\lVert\vec{u}\rVert_{2}\geq s_{0}]\leq\frac{\ell}{2\pi S^{2}s_{0}^{2}}

Proof.

Each coordinate

is an independent random variable with
density proportional to
\exp(-\pi S^{2}u_{j}^{2})
. Thus,

\operatorname{Ex}[u_{j}^{2}]=\frac{1}{2\pi S^{2}}\qquad\operatorname{Ex}[\lVert\vec{u}\rVert_{2}^{2}]=\frac{\ell}{2\pi S^{2}}.

Since
\lVert\vec{u}\rVert_{2}^{2}
is a non-negative random variable, the lemma
follows from Markov’s inequality.
∎

\vec{s}

with a Fourier measurement.

As in previous algorithms for DHSP or AHShP, if
\lVert\vec{s}\rVert_{\text{bit}}=n
, the
sieve progressively collimates
\vec{y}
in
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
stages to reach
a desired total precision of
n
bits. In the current
k
-dimensional
context, this means that each component of
\vec{y}
is collimated to
n/k

bits of precision. The phase vectors in the middle of the sieve have
length
2^{\Theta(\sqrt{n})}
, and the total work of the algorithm is also

2^{\Theta(\sqrt{n})}
. Moreover, as Regev realized
Reg04b
,
a collimation sieve can be depth first rather than breadth first.

The current algorithm is a collimation sieve that can look roughly the
same for any periodicity
H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. As stated in Theorem
1.8
,
the time complexity is then nearly uniform in the choice of
H
. The main
new technique is sieve steps that combine single-spot and double-spot phase
vectors to produce a double-spot phase vector at the end. As explained in
Section
8.3
, a collimation step in the sieve combines a double-spot phase
vector and a single-spot phase vector to produce a double-spot phase vector,
or it combines two single-spot phase vectors to produce a single-spot phase
vector. As indicated in Figure
8.4
, the resulting sieve is asymmetrical.
The sieve is organized into a tree with a single path of double-spot phase
vectors; the other nodes are all single-spot phase vectors.

Here is the algorithm in three parts.

Algorithm
SH

(Phase qubit from hiding function)
.

The input is a
dimension
k>0
, a generator matrix
M
for a lattice
H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
,
a joint hiding function
f
as in (
8.1
), and positive integer
parameters
Q=1/q
and
G=1/g
. The output is an approximate qubit
phase vector
\lvert\phi_{\vec{y}}\rangle
, where
\vec{y}\in H^{\#}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{k}

is approximately uniformly random.

1.

Make the state
\lvert\psi_{GC}\rangle
as in step 1 of Algorithm
A
,
with
G=1/g
replacing
S=1/s
there. Tensor it with
\lvert+\rangle
.

2.

Let
U_{f}
be the unitary dilation of the hiding function
f

restricted to the discrete cube
\lVert\vec{x}\rVert_{\infty}<Q/2
. Then evaluate:

U_{f}(\lvert\psi_{GC}\rangle\otimes\lvert+\rangle)\propto\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k},\ b\in\{0,1\}\\
\lVert\vec{x}\rVert_{\infty}<Q/2\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\exp(-\pi g^{2}\lVert\vec{x}\rVert_{2}^{2})\lvert\vec{x},b,f_{b}(\vec{x})\rangle.

Discard the output register to obtain a mixed state
\rho
on the
(\vec{x},b)

input register.

3.

Using the embedding of the discrete interval
\lvert x\rvert<Q/2
into

\mathbb{Z}/Q
, apply the Fourier operator
F_{(\mathbb{Z}/Q)^{k}}
to the
\vec{x}
register
of the state
\rho
. Then measure the Fourier mode
\vec{y}_{0}\in(\mathbb{Z}/Q)^{k}
,
and let

\vec{y}=q\vec{y}_{0}\in q(\mathbb{Z}/Q)^{k}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{k}.

4.

Return the posterior state, which approximates
\lvert\phi_{\vec{y}}\rangle
.

Algorithm
SC

(Collimation sieve)
.

The input is three integers
m>0
,
j\geq 0
,
p\in\{1,2\}
, and a target vector
\vec{y}\in H^{\#}
,
in addition to the input to Algorithm
SH
. If
p=1
, the output is
an
R
-collimated phase vector
\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle
of length
4^{km}\leq\ell<4^{km+1}
, where

R=[-r,r]^{k}\qquad r=2^{-jm-1}.

If
p=2
and
j<km
, the output is an
(R,S)
-collimated phase vector

\lvert\phi_{Y,Z}\rangle
, where also

S=[-r,r]^{k}+\vec{y},

and where the length of each summand is
4^{km}\leq\ell<4^{km+1}
.
If
p=2
and
j=km
, the output is a phase qubit that approximates

\lvert\phi_{\vec{y}}\rangle
.

1.

If
j=0
and
p=1
, form
\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle
as the tensor product of
2km

phase qubits produced by Algorithm
SH
, and return the result.

2.

If
j=0
and
p=2
, form

\lvert\phi_{Y,Z}\rangle=\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle\oplus\lvert\phi_{Z}\rangle

as the tensor product of
2km+1
phase qubits produced by Algorithm
SH
,
where the
2\cdot 4^{km}
phase terms are split equally and randomly between
the two summands. Return the result.

3.

If
j>0
and
p=1
, do the following:

(a)

Recursively call Algorithm
SC
twice with
(j^{\prime},p^{\prime})=(j-1,1)

(and the same values of
m
and
\vec{y}
), to obtain two single-spot phase
vectors
\lvert\phi_{U}\rangle
and
\lvert\phi_{V}\rangle
, both collimated at the scale
r^{\prime}=2^{-(j-1)m-1}
.

(b)

Perform a collimation measurement as in Figure
8.3
(a) to obtain
a single-spot phase vector
\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle
collimated at the scale
r=2^{-jm-1}
.

(c)

If
|Y|<4^{km}
, reject
\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle
and go back to step 3(a).

(d)

If
|Y|\geq 4^{km+1}
(as is usually
the case), partition
\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle
into summands of nearly equal lengths

\ell
with
4^{km}\leq\ell<4^{km+1}
, and measure
\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle

according to this partition to shorten it, and reassign
Y

to call the shortened version
\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle
.

(e)

Return
\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle
.

4.

If
j>0
and
p=2
, do the following:

(a)

Recursively call Algorithm
SC
with
(j^{\prime},p^{\prime})=(j-1,2)
to obtain
a double-spot phase vector
\lvert\phi_{U,V}\rangle
, and a second time with

(j^{\prime},p^{\prime})=(j-1,1)
to obtain a single-spot phase vector
\lvert\phi_{W}\rangle
,
both collimated at the scale
r^{\prime}=2^{-(j-1)m-1}
.

(b)

Perform a collimation measurement as in Figure
8.3
(b) to obtain
a double-spot phase vector
\lvert\phi_{Y,Z}\rangle
, collimated at the scale
r=2^{-jm-1}
.

(c)

If either
|Y|<4^{km}
or
|Z|<4^{km}
, reject
\lvert\phi_{Y,Z}\rangle

and go back to step 4(a).

(d)

If either
|Y|\geq 4^{km+1}
or
|Z|\geq 4^{km+1}
, apply the
shortening procedure in Step 3(d) to each summand
\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle
and

\lvert\phi_{Z}\rangle
separately, and reassign
Y
and
Z
to call the shortened
version
\lvert\phi_{Y,Z}\rangle
.

(e)

If
j=km
, partition
\lvert\phi_{Y,Z}\rangle
into pairs of basis
states, one each in
\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle
and
\lvert\phi_{Z}\rangle
, plus unpaired
states in either
\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle
or
\lvert\phi_{Z}\rangle
, whichever is longer.
Measure
\lvert\phi_{Y,Z}\rangle
according to this partition. If the posterior
state is a qubit, return it; it approximates
\lvert\phi_{\vec{y}}\rangle
. If the
posterior state is unpaired, go back to Step 4(a).

(f)

If
j<km
, return
\lvert\phi_{Y,Z}\rangle
.

Algorithm
SF

(Final measurement)
.

The input is an integer
t>0

such that the hidden shift
\vec{s}
satisfies
\lVert\vec{s}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2^{t-1}
,
in addition to the input to Algorithm
SH
. The output is a value
\vec{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}/H
which is the hidden shift with probability at least
1/2
.

1.

Let
m\geq 2
be the first integer such that

2^{km^{2}}>k(n+2h)2^{t}.

(8.8)

2.

As in Section
8.3
, construct the group

A=A_{1}+A_{2}\subseteq H^{\#}

where
A_{1}
is a complement of
H_{1}^{\#}
in
H^{\#}
and
A_{2}
is the group
of
2^{t}
-torsion of points in
H_{1}^{\#}
.

3.

Decompose
A
into a product of cyclic factors.
For each cyclic factor
D\cong\mathbb{Z}/d
of

A
, do the following, as in Section
8.3
:

(a)

Pick a cyclic generator
\vec{y}\in D\subseteq(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{n}
. Let
e=\lceil\log_{2}(d)\rceil
be the first power of 2 that is at least
d
.

(b)

For each
\ell
with
0\leq\ell<e
, call Algorithm
SC

with
\vec{y}^{\prime}=2^{\ell}\vec{y}
, the given value of
m
,
j=km
,
and
p=2
, to produce an approximate version of
\lvert\phi_{\vec{y}^{\prime}}\rangle
.

(c)

Perform a boolean measure the tensor product of the states

\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle=\lvert\phi_{\vec{y}}\rangle\otimes\lvert\phi_{2\vec{y}}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes\lvert\phi_{2^{e-1}\vec{y}}\rangle

from step 3(b) in the computational basis with the posterior state

\lvert\phi_{D}\rangle
if the measurement succeeds. If the measurement fails,
go back to step 3(b).

4.

Once
\lvert\phi_{D}\rangle
is created for every cyclic factor
D=D_{j}

of
A
, form

\lvert\phi_{A}\rangle=\lvert\phi_{D_{1}}\rangle\otimes\lvert\phi_{D_{2}}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes\lvert\phi_{D_{\ell}}\rangle.

Perform a Fourier measurement on
\lvert\phi_{A}\rangle
to obtain
\vec{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}/H
,
and return this answer.

Here are some points that partly explain why the overall algorithm works.

Note that Algorithm
SH
produces an approximation
\rho
(in general
a mixed state if the hiding function value is left unmeasured) to

\lvert\phi_{\vec{y}}\rangle
with
\vec{y}
random. Then Algorithm
SC
produces

\lvert\phi_{\vec{y}^{\prime}}\rangle
which only approximates
\lvert\phi_{\vec{y}}\rangle
, because

\vec{y}^{\prime}
approximates
\vec{y}
. Each algorithm produces approximations which
are then used in the next algorithm as if they were exact. This is valid if
the total trace distance between every output
\rho
and each corresponding

\lvert\phi_{y}\rangle\langle\phi_{y}\rvert
is low enough to leave a good chance that the algorithms
will not notice the discrepancies.

If we know that Algorithm
SH
is only called
2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
times,
then equation (
8.5
) states that
G
and
Q
can be both
2^{O(n)}

and large enough for accurate output. Since Algorithm
SH
runs in
quantum polynomial time in
\lVert G\rVert_{\text{bit}}
and
\lVert Q\rVert_{\text{bit}}
, this
contributes an acceptable factor to the total running time, one bounded by

2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
if the query cost of the hiding function is also bounded
by
2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
.

Algorithm
SC
produces a sufficiently accurate approximation to each target

\lvert\phi_{\vec{y}}\rangle
when (
8.7
) holds, which is then satisfied
by (
8.8
). Meanwhile, the running time of Algorithm
SC

is
2^{O(km)}
, provided that the chance of rejection in steps 3(c) and
4(c) is bounded away from 1. To bound the running time in terms of
n
,
we can combine (
8.8
) with the relation
n=kt
and the theorem
hypothesis
n=\Omega((k+\log(h))^{2})
to obtain:

k^{2}m^{2}<k\bigl[(t+2)+\log(k)+\log(n+2h)\bigr]=O(n).

Thus
km=O(\sqrt{n})
and the running time of Algorithm
SC
is

2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
. Meanwhile, Algorithm
SF
runs in quantum polynomial
time, outside of its function calls to the other algorithms.

The remaining question in the algorithm analysis is whether Algorithm
SC

usually passes steps 3(c) and 4(c). It does on average, because the
phase vectors in the sieve are generously long in relation to the amount
of collimation.
I.e.
, given that
|U|,|V|,|W|\geq 4^{km}
, the typical
values of
|Y|
and
|Z|
are at least

\frac{4^{2km}}{2^{k(m+1)}}=2^{3km-k}\geq 4^{k(m+1)}.

However, as indicated in Figure
8.2
combined with Figure
8.3
,
the phase multipliers in
\lvert\phi_{U}\rangle\otimes\lvert\phi_{V}\rangle
(for example)
cannot be uniformly distributed in the partition of the cube
[-2r^{\prime},2r^{\prime}]^{k}
,
because they are restricted to
H^{\#}
. Even if
H^{\#}=(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{k}
, we
cannot expect the phase multipliers in
\lvert\phi_{U}\rangle\otimes\lvert\phi_{V}\rangle

to be uniformly distributed. If the multipliers in
\lvert\phi_{U}\rangle

and
\lvert\phi_{V}\rangle
are both uniformly distributed in
[-r^{\prime},r^{\prime}]^{k}
,
the convolved multipliers
U*V
in
\lvert\phi_{U}\rangle\otimes\lvert\phi_{V}\rangle

are not uniformly distributed in
[-2r^{\prime},2r^{\prime}]^{k}
, because the convolution
of the uniform distribution on an interval with itself is not uniform;
the density is a tent function.

However, step 3(c) in Algorithm
SC
is robust regardless of the
distribution on
\lvert\phi_{U}\rangle\otimes\lvert\phi_{V}\rangle
, while step 4(c) is
robust under a heuristic assumption. If we think of each tile
[-r,r]^{k}+\vec{v}

as a “household” in which the phase multipliers “reside”, then we can
apply the “average household” principle: Given
N
people distributed
arbitrarily among
M
households, then a randomly chosen household has at
least
N/2M
residents with probability at least
1/2
.

To show that double-spot collimation in step 4(c) usually succeeds,
we must show that the two summands
\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle
and
\lvert\phi_{Z}\rangle
of

\lvert\phi_{Y,Z}\rangle
are often long at the same time, while the average
household principle only shows that
\lvert\phi_{Y,Z}\rangle
is often long.
To address this, note that the intersection
H^{\#}\cap([-2r^{\prime},2r^{\prime}]^{k}+\vec{y})

is a translate of
H^{\#}\cap[-2r^{\prime},2r^{\prime}]^{k}
, because
\vec{y}H^{\#}
and
H^{\#}

is a subgroup of
(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{k}
. If we assume that the multipliers of long
phase vectors in Algorithm
SC
resemble independent samples in the
infinite-length limit, then the non-uniformity from the convolution in the
multiplier list
U*V
of
\lvert\phi_{U}\rangle\otimes\lvert\phi_{W}\rangle
likewise has
the same form in
\lvert\phi_{V}\rangle\otimes\lvert\phi_{W}\rangle
. In other words,
both of these sources of non-uniformity in the measurement in step 4(c)
and Figure
8.3
(b) are the same in tandem in the two spots. Given the
statistical assumption, the summands
\lvert\phi_{Y}\rangle
and
\lvert\phi_{Z}\rangle

will usually be close in length.

Finally, we point out that Algorithm
SC
(which is the core of the
algorithms taken together) is not fully optimized. We have presented it
in a simplified form to establish a

2^{O(\sqrt{n})}=O(C^{\sqrt{n}})

time complexity bound. It should be possible to optimize the constant
C
,
and after that look for smaller accelerations, with methods similar to those
explored by the author
Kup 13
and Peikert
Pei 20
.
Among other things, the phase vectors in Algorithm
SC
are longer than
necessary. It is also probably not optimal to use the same value of
m

at every stage in the sieve. It might also be better to collimate the
phase vectors in one or just a few directions at each stage, rather than
all
k
directions in parallel.

That said, full optimization of Algorithm
SC
(or any style of collimation
sieve) depends on the specific query cost of the hiding function, and can
depend on specific properties of the periodicity lattice
H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}
. Moreover, if there is an algorithm that is much faster than

2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
, then finding it would be a different type of breakthrough,
because of known reductions from lattice problems (with no hiding function)
to the abelian hidden shift problem
Reg04a
.
