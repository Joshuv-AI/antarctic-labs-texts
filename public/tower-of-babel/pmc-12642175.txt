Association between vascular overload index and arterial stiffness: a secondary analysis of a nationwide cross-sectional study in Japan
Authors: Yibei Liu, Nan Ouyang, Chenchen Zhang, Jiangchuan Jin, Qiong Huang, Xingjie Huang, Chao Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent evidence regarding the association between vascular overload index (VOI) and arterial stiffness in Japanese adults remains limited. Given that brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) has been established as a validated biomarker for assessing atherosclerosis, this study aims to comprehensively investigate the association between VOI and arterial stiffness through baPWV measurements.MethodsThis secondary analysis utilized health examination data from 912 participants (2004–2012) at Gifu Murakami Memorial Hospital, Japan. The baseline data of participants were divided into four groups according to the quartiles of VOI for comparative analysis. Univariate linear regression and multivariable linear regression were used to investigate the association between VOI and baPWV. Linear scatter plots initially assessed the VOI-baPWV relationship, and Subgroup analyses were stratified by age, sex, BMI, smoking status, regular exercise, and fatty liver. VOI was calculated using the standardized formula: VOI = 1.33 × SBP − 0.33 × DBP − 133.3.ResultsWith the increase of VOI level, baPWV increased gradually (1,277.85 ± 162.57 vs. 1,361.45 ± 194.85 vs. 1,415.45 ± 173.10 vs. 1,608.25 ± 299.53, p < 0.05). Multivariable linear regression showed that after adjusting for all covariates, each 0.1-unit increase in VOI was associated with a 0.69-unit increase in baPWV(m/s). Linear correlation scatter revealed a strong positive correlation between VOI and baPWV (r = 0.51, 95% CI 0.461–0.557, p < 0.001). The association between VOI and baPWV was more pronounced in the subgroups of age ≥ 55 years (Beta = 0.90, 95% CI 0.76–1.04, p < 0.001), males (Beta = 0.76, 95% CI 0.66–0.85, p < 0.001), smokers (Beta = 0.81, 95% CI 0.69–0.92, p < 0.001), and those with fatty liver (Beta = 0.85, 95% CI 0.69–1.00, p < 0.001).ConclusionVOI is significantly and independently associated with baPWV and may serve as a potential biomarker for early risk assessment of arterial stiffness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03393-7.Keywords: Vascular overload index, Brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity, Atherosclerosis, Arterial stiffness, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Arteriosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is a chronic inflammatory condition driven by lipid deposition and involving multiple cell types in large and medium arteries. It is a primary cause of coronary artery disease and peripheral artery disease [1]. According to the 2019 Global Burden of Disease study, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) affected 523 million individuals globally in 2019, with 18.6 million deaths, representing a major contributor to global disease burden [2, 3]. Studies have shown that brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) not only predicts future cardiovascular events but also serves as a significant predictor of all-cause mortality [4]. baPWV measures the pulse wave propagation speed between the brachial and ankle arteries [5], providing a quantitative assessment of arteriosclerosis. Its non-invasive nature, simplicity, and high reproducibility make it suitable for large-scale screening [6]. However, the accuracy of baPWV may be compromised by peripheral vascular disease, limb edema [7], severe arterial calcification, or vascular occlusion [5, 7]. Additionally, the lack of standardized diagnostic thresholds limits its comparability. Moreover, the detection of baPWV requires specialized equipment and complex preparatory work, which can increase the burden on medical insurance [8]. Therefore, it is necessary to identify clinically accessible biomarkers that are strongly correlated with baPWV.

Traditional blood pressure parameters reflect static pressure but fail to assess dynamic vascular load (e.g., resistance, elasticity) [9]. The Vascular Overload Index (VOI) was proposed to integrate parameters such as small artery resistance, large artery compliance, and pulse wave reflection, quantifying the vascular remodeling effects of hemodynamic load [10]. Epidemiological evidence indicates that VOI is significantly associated with CVD risk. For example, a 2015–2018 follow-up study in rural China demonstrated VOI's independent predictive value for cardiovascular events [11]. Other studies confirmed its relationship with new-onset ischemic stroke in elderly hypertensive populations and left ventricular hypertrophy in hypertensive patients [12, 13]. However, existing research primarily focuses on hypertensive cohorts, and the relationship between VOI and arteriosclerosis in general populations remains unclear.

Recent studies have introduced the Cardiovascular-Renal-Hepatic-Metabolic Syndrome (CRHMS) concept [14], which underscores the shared pathological underpinnings of cardiac, renal, hepatic, and metabolic diseases, such as inflammation, oxidative stress, insulin resistance, and endothelial dysfunction. This highlights the connection of abnormalities in glucose and lipid metabolism, renal disease, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and ASCVD. Chen et al. [15] also found through analysis of a large prospective cohort study that cumulative exposure to risk factors such as blood pressure, glucose, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and hsCRP significantly increases the risk of CVDs. Building on these findings, the present study utilizes data from a Japanese health examination population and, after controlling for confounding factors such as lipid levels, blood glucose, liver function, fatty liver, and renal function, further explores the relationship between the VOI and baPWV. This study represents the first cross-sectional analysis to investigate the independent correlation between VOI and baPWV in a Japanese general population, offering novel insights for cardiovascular risk stratification.

Methods

Data source

The original data were sourced from the open-access repository Dryad. The specific data package is from Fukuda, Takuya, et al. [16] (10.5061/dryad.m484p). The dataset includes a comprehensive array of variables. Demographic characteristics encompass age and sex. Blood pressure metrics cover systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Vascular function indices include baPWV and ankle-brachial index (ABI). Hepatic function markers consist of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT). Metabolic parameters involve fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and LDL-C. Renal function indicators feature uric acid (UA) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Lifestyle factors include smoking status (current and former smoking are defined as smoking), exercise habits, fatty liver status, and menopausal status. All data were meticulously collected in accordance with stringent quality control protocols to ensure the highest level of accuracy.

Study population

Participants were adults who underwent health examinations at Gifu Murakami Memorial Hospital between March 2004 and December 2012. The study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration and obtained written informed consent from all participants. Exclusion criteria included ongoing hormonal therapy, hepatitis B/C infection, pregnancy, and ABI < 0.95. After screening, 912 participants were included in the final analysis.

Measurements

After the participants had been thoroughly prepared in accordance with the examination protocol, baPWV was measured by professional technicians using an automatic waveform analyzer (Colin Medical Technology, Komaki, Japan). The measurement parameters included the path length from the suprasternal notch to the brachial artery (Lb) and the path length from the suprasternal notch to the ankle (La). The delay time (DTba) from the upstroke of the brachial waveform to the upstroke of each ankle waveform was automatically determined, with the brachial-ankle arterial waveform time difference (DTba) being recorded synchronously. baPWV was ultimately calculated as (Lb–La)/DTba, with the unit expressed in cm/s. The intraobserver and interobserver coefficients of variation and their corresponding correlation coefficients and p-values are detailed in the original text [16].

VOI Calculation: Blood pressure was measured using validated electronic devices in accordance with the 2020 International Society of Hypertension (ISH) guidelines. After participants rested for 5 min in a seated position, three consecutive measurements were taken and averaged. The VOI was then calculated using the formula: VOI = 1.33 × SBP − 0.33 × DBP − 133.3 [10].

Professionally trained technicians conduct hepatic ultrasonography using the SSD-650CL ultrasound device manufactured by Aloka Co, Ltd.in Tokyo, Japan, to determine the presence of fatty liver disease. The resulting ultrasound images are preserved in the form of photocopies for subsequent analysis by a gastroenterology specialist. The specialist evaluates the images based on four criteria: hepatorenal echo contrast, liver brightness, deep attenuation, and vascular blurring. A diagnosis of fatty liver disease is made only when the participant exhibits enhanced hepatorenal echo contrast and increased liver brightness [16].

Fasting blood samples (The participants fasted for more than 8 h) were analyzed using a Hitachi MODULAR ANALYTICS system (Hitachi High-Technologies Corp. Ltd, Tokyo, Japan), which measured hepatic function markers, metabolic parameters, and renal function indicators [16].

Statistical analysis

First, in this study, the baseline data of participants were divided into four groups according to the quartiles of VOI for comparative analysis. For categorical variables, they were presented in the form of frequency (n) and percentage (%), and chi-square tests were used for intergroup comparisons. In the treatment of continuous variables, if they conform to a normal distribution, they were described by mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using one-way ANOVA, whereas for continuous variables that do not conform to a normal distribution, they were displayed in the form of median (quartiles) [M (Q1, Q3)] and compared between groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test. In addition, linear regression analysis was used to assess the relationship between VOI and baPWV, with the results presented in the form of Beta values and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Next, in the linear regression model, to control the impact of collinearity on the model, this study excluded SBP and DBP from the model based on the fact that VOI is a clear linear combination of SBP and DBP. Subsequently, we constructed a fully adjusted model, tested and excluded covariates with variance inflation factors (VIF) greater than 10 (TC was excluded). Then, this study constructed multiple models adjusted for different covariates to assess the robustness of the association between VOI and baPWV: Model 1: unadjusted. Model 2: adjusted for age and sex. Model 3: using the directed acyclic graph (DAG) method to assess the role of each variable in the causal pathway (confounders, mediators, and colliders), the following confounders were ultimately selected: age, sex, BMI, FBS, UA, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, smoking, drinking, fatty liver, eGFR, and regular exercise. Model 4: covariates were selected based on the following principles [17]: (1) Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis. (2) Variables that cause a change in beta of more than 10% when included in the basic model or excluded from the full model. (3) Variables adjusted according to clinical practice and literature reports. Ultimately, age, sex, BMI, smoking, regular exercise, fatty liver, AST, ALT, GGT, FPG, UA, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, and eGFR were included in this model. In the linear regression model, the dependent variable was baPWV (m/s). Since the inclusion of the original value of VOI in the model resulted in an extremely large beta value, which is of little clinical reference value, we included VOI / 10 in the model to obtain a more clinically relevant beta value. Trend tests were used to assess whether there was a linear trend of VOI categorical variables in the model. Linear correlation scatter plots were used to assess the linear trend between VOI and baPWV.

Finally, this study used subgroup analysis to investigate the relationship between VOI and baPWV in specific populations stratified by age (< 55 years vs. ≥ 55 years), sex (male vs. female), smoking (yes vs. no), fatty liver (yes vs. no), BMI (< 24 vs. ≥ 24), and regular exercise (yes vs. no). Additionally, participants were divided into two groups based on whether baPWV was ≥ 14 m/s to conduct sensitivity analyses on the relationship between VOI and baPWV. Interaction tests were performed to assess whether there were interactions between VOI and each stratification variable. All statistical analyses were completed using R 4.2.2 (The R Foundation). In this study, a two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Among 1,445 individuals initially enrolled in this study, 912 participants meeting the inclusion criteria were included in the final analysis, with a mean age of 51.13 ± 9.57 years. Participants were stratified into quartiles (Q1-Q4) based on VOI levels. Intergroup comparisons revealed that in the Q4 group, age, male ratio, BMI, SBP, DBP, AST, ALT, GGT, FPG, UA, TC, TG, ABI, alcohol consumption, postmenopausal state ratio, and fatty liver ratio were significantly increased, while eGFR and HDL-C levels were significantly decreased (p < 0.05). LDL-C, smoking ratio, and regular exercise ratio showed no significant differences among the four groups (p > 0.05). With the increase of VOI level, baPWV increased gradually (1,277.85 ± 162.57 vs. 1,361.45 ± 194.85 vs. 1,415.45 ± 173.10 vs. 1,608.25 ± 299.53, p < 0.05). (Table 1).

Results of the linear regression analysis between VOI and baPWV

In the unadjusted model (Model 1), VOI exhibited a significant positive association with baPWV (Beta = 0.74, 95% CI 0.66–0.82, p < 0.001). This association remained significant after partial adjustment for demographic covariates, including age and sex (Model 2: Beta = 0.66, 95% CI 0.59–0.73, p < 0.001). In Model 3, after further adjustment for age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, regular exercise, fatty liver, FPG, UA,TG,HDL-C,LDL-C, and eGFR, the association between VOI and baPWV remained stable (Beta = 0.69, 95% CI 0.62–0.77, p < 0.001). Notably, even in Model 4, which included additional adjustment for AST, ALT, and GGT on top of Model 3, the positive association between VOI and baPWV persisted and remained statistically significant (Beta = 0.69, 95% CI 0.62–0.77, p < 0.001). Next, we categorized VOI into quartiles and included it in linear regression models for analysis using four models, with covariates adjusted as mentioned above. The results of comparing the fourth quartile (Q4) with the first quartile (Q1) across the four models are as follows: Model 1 (Q4 vs. Q1, Beta = 3.30, 95% CI 2.91–3.70, p < 0.001); Model 2 (Q4 vs. Q1, Beta = 2.85, 95% CI 2.50–3.19, p < 0.001); Model 3 (Q4 vs. Q1, Beta = 2.93, 95% CI 2.56–3.30, p < 0.001); Model 4 (Q4 vs. Q1, Beta = 2.93, 95% CI 2.56–3.30, p < 0.001). Trend analysis across all four models was significant (p < 0.001), indicating that baPWV levels significantly increased with increasing VOI (Table 2).

Results of the linear relationship between VOI and baPWV

The linear correlation scatter revealed a strong positive correlation between VOI and baPWV (r = 0.51, 95% CI 0.461–0.557, p < 0.001). The upward trend in the scatter plot indicates that baPWV increases proportionately with higher VOI values. (Fig. 1).

Subgroup analysis

Significant interactions were observed in the subgroups stratified by age (< 55 years vs. ≥ 55 years), sex (male vs. female), smoking (yes vs. no), and fatty liver (yes vs. no) (all interaction tests p < 0.05). The association between VOI and baPWV was more pronounced in the subgroups of age ≥ 55 years (Beta = 0.90, 95% CI 0.76–1.04, p < 0.001), males (Beta = 0.76, 95% CI 0.66–0.85, p < 0.001), smokers (Beta = 0.81, 95% CI 0.69–0.92, p < 0.001), and those with fatty liver (Beta = 0.85, 95% CI 0.69–1.00, p < 0.001). However, no interactions were observed in the subgroups stratified by regular exercise and BMI (interaction tests p > 0.05). Interaction plots more intuitively display the above relationships (Figs. 2 and 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Participants were divided into two groups based on whether baPWV was ≥ 14 m/s, namely the high baPWV group and the low baPWV group, and the relationship between VOI and baPWV was analyzed separately for each group. In the high and low baPWV groups, the Beta values for the continuous variable VOI in Model 4 were 0.57(95% CI 0.45–0.69, p < 0.001) and 0.26(95% CI 0.19–0.32, p < 0.001), respectively. For the categorical variable VOI in Model 4, the results were as follows: in the high baPWV group, Q4 vs Q1, Beta = 2.08,95% CI 1.41–2.75, p < 0.001; in the low baPWV group, Q4 vs Q1, Beta = 1.02,95% CI 0.71–1.32, p < 0.001.The trend tests were significant in both groups(p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

The primary findings of this study are as follows: 1) baPWV levels increase progressively with rising VOI values; 2) there is a linear positive correlation between VOI and baPWV; 3) after controlling for covariates, the association between VOI and baPWV remains robust, with baPWV increasing by 0.69 m/s for every 1 mmHg increase in VOI; 4) the association between VOI and baPWV is more pronounced in elderly individuals, males, smokers, and those with fatty liver disease.

The VOI dynamically integrates systolic and diastolic blood pressure fluctuations to comprehensively reflect vascular resistance, elasticity, and hemodynamic load. This study, conducted in a Japanese general population (N = 912), is the first to demonstrate a significant independent positive relationship between VOI and baPWV (Beta = 0.69, p < 0.001). Consistent with earlier findings, a cross-sectional study that included 429 ostensibly healthy middle-aged women found a robust positive correlation between common femoral artery pulse wave velocity (PWV) and both SBP and DBP [18]. Zhang et al. [19] reported a similar phenomenon, noting that carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (CA-PWV) was positively correlated with SBP but negatively with pulse pressure (PP) in their analysis of data from 729 healthy subjects. They suggested that SBP, DBP, and PP may have differential effects on CA-PWV. Epidemiological evidence indicates a significant association between the VOI and the risk of CVD. For instance, a follow-up study conducted in rural China from 2015 to 2018 demonstrated VOI's independent predictive value for cardiovascular events [11]. Yu et al. [12] found a close relationship between VOI and new-onset ischemic stroke in elderly hypertensive populations. Additionally, VOI levels are positively correlated with left ventricular hypertrophy and carotid intima-media thickness in hypertensive patients [13]. Atherosclerosis, the common pathological basis for CVD and stroke, was found in this study to have an independent association with VOI, providing a theoretical basis for elucidating VOI's involvement in the pathogenesis of ASCVD. SBP and DBP are closely related to the adverse prognosis of stroke patients. However, current blood pressure management guidelines primarily focus on controlling SBP levels, with limited research on DBP. Some scholars have reported that low DBP (< 60 mmHg) is significantly associated with an increased risk of composite events in patients with ischemic stroke (IS) or transient ischemic attack (TIA) , especially among the elderly [20]. This may be related to changes in blood pressure dynamics following antihypertensive treatment, particularly in patients with symptomatic intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis, where a decrease in SBP is associated with an increased risk of cortex-involved infarcts, while a reduction in poststenotic mean arterial pressure (MAP) is linked to an increased risk of borderzone-involved infarcts. Studies suggest that maintaining adequate poststenotic MAP is more important than simply focusing on SBP reduction, which can help reduce stroke risk [21]. The VOI, an integrated metric of SBP and DBP, reflects vascular pressure load. In stroke patients, especially those with intracranial arterial stenosis, dynamically monitoring VOI levels during vascular management may be beneficial in reducing post-stroke adverse events. However, further comparative studies are needed to validate this.

Notably, the association between VOI and baPWV was more pronounced in several subgroups, including individuals aged ≥ 55 years (Beta = 0.90, 95% CI 0.76–1.04, p < 0.001), males (Beta = 0.76, 95% CI 0.66–0.85, p < 0.001), smokers (Beta = 0.81, 95% CI 0.69–0.92, p < 0.001), and those with fatty liver (Beta = 0.85, 95% CI 0.69–1.00, p < 0.001). As is well known, age, males, and smoking are risk factors for hypertension, conclusions that are reflected in the present study. A meta-analysis has found that non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is an independent risk factor for hypertension; with NAFLD patients having approximately 1.6 times the risk of developing hypertension [22]. The association between the two has been fully elucidated genetically. Yuan et al. [23] found through a two-sample Mendelian randomization study that there is a causal relationship between NAFLD and hypertension.

The potential mechanism underlying VOI-induced arterial stiffness is as follows: VOI serves as an indicator of vascular pressure overload. Elevated vascular pressure overload leads to increased vascular shear stress, which subsequently results in endothelial cell injury and activates pathways such as NF-κB, thereby triggering inflammation [24, 25]. In the presence of insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, arterial stiffness is further exacerbated. Current research has demonstrated that statin therapy can effectively exert anti-inflammatory effects, stabilize atherosclerotic plaques, and protect the vascular endothelium, thereby exerting a vasodilatory effect [26].

The major strengths of this study are as follows: (1) Based on the available covariates, this study rigorously selected the variables to be included in the model through multiple methods. Moreover, by employing multiple models in conjunction with subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses, the robust relationship between the VOI and baPWV outcomes was thoroughly explored. Additionally, interaction plots were utilized to intuitively display the interaction results, thereby enhancing the persuasiveness and interpretability of the study conclusions. (2) Although previous studies have suggested the predictive potential of the VOI index for cardiovascular events, the outcomes of these studies were mostly clinical events that had already occurred. This study, for the first time, established a direct link between VOI and subclinical atherosclerosis (as indicated by baPWV) in a screening population, providing an important reference for the early identification of high-risk groups for CVD.

Several limitations warrant consideration: (1) The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. (2) The sample was restricted to Japanese individuals with high-salt dietary patterns, limiting generalizability. Future multi-center prospective cohorts should validate VOI's cross-ethnic applicability and elucidate genetic (e.g., PNPLA3 polymorphisms) and environmental (e.g., sodium intake) modulators of its regulatory mechanisms. (3) This study is based on secondary data analysis. Some covariates, such as antihypertensives, statins, antidiabetics, and heart rate, were not available for inclusion in the adjustment models. This limitation may introduce some bias into the results.

Conclusion

The VOI is independently and positively related to baPWV in Japanese adults, particularly in the subgroups of elderly individuals, smokers, males, and those with fatty liver. VOI holds promise as a novel, noninvasive, cost-effective indicator for the early identification of arterial stiffness.
