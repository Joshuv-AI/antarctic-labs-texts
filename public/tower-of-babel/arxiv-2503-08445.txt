LLM-Pack: Intuitive Grocery Handling for Logistics Applications
Yannik Blei, Michael Krawez, Tobias Jülg, Pierre Krack, Florian Walter, Wolfram Burgard

Abstract
Robotics and automation are increasingly influential in logistics but remain largely confined to traditional warehouses. In grocery retail, advancements such as cashier-less supermarkets exist, yet customers still manually pick and pack groceries. While there has been a substantial focus in robotics on the bin picking problem, the task of packing objects and groceries has remained largely untouched. However, packing grocery items in the right order is crucial for preventing product damage, e.g., heavy objects should not be placed on top of fragile ones. However, the exact criteria for the right packing order are hard to define, in particular given the huge variety of objects typically found in stores. In this paper, we introduce LLM-Pack, a novel approach for grocery packing. LLM-Pack leverages language and vision foundation models for identifying groceries and generating a packing sequence that mimics human packing strategy. LLM-Pack does not require dedicated training to handle new grocery items and its modularity allows easy upgrades of the underlying foundation models. We extensively evaluate our approach to demonstrate its performance. We will make the source code of LLM-Pack publicly available upon the publication of this manuscript.



I
Introduction

Automation in the logistics industry has become more and more important over the past decades.
In warehouses, autonomous mobile robots and robot manipulators, powered by Artificial Intelligence (AI), already play a key role in fulfilling orders.
Mobile platforms allow seamless transportation of material between processing stations, while robot manipulators enable precise material handling, increasing the efficiency of steps like palletizing and packing
[
1
]
. Due to the economic importance and relatively controlled setting, warehouse operations have been a major focus in robotics and automation research.

More recently, several automation solutions have been introduced to supermarkets. While cashier-free grocery stores are already present in some countries, customers mostly still pick and pack groceries manually.
Although these tasks are simple for most people, they can be challenging for the elderly or individuals with disabilities. In the future, service robots should provide aid by taking a grocery list, autonomously finding and picking the items, and placing them into the customer’s basket. Several such prototype systems have been recently proposed
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
. However, their main focus lies on mobile navigation and object detection.
The aspect of packing groceries has largely remained unexplored.

Even though this step is intuitive to most huwhmans, placing objects in the correct order is crucial for a successful shopping spree.
Placing heavy objects on top of fragile ones, such as eggs, could cause damage.
Similarly, frozen items could leak and affect moisture-sensitive products. This indicates that the manual development of such a packing expert would be challenging, in particular given the huge diversity of objects found in warehouses and grocery stores.
In this paper, we address this challenge by introducing LLM-Pack, a novel approach that optimizes the packing sequence with state-of-the-art foundation models. LLM-Pack operates in an open vocabulary manner and does not require re-training when facing novel grocery classes.
We achieve this by combining Vision Language Models (VLMs) for semantic knowledge, Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) for accurate object localization and task-specific Neural Networks for grasp planning and execution. Together, these components form a flexible pipeline for intelligent object placement.
Its modular design allows seamless integration of improved foundation models as they become available. Finally, the integrity constraint posed by our method on the packaging order can be easily integrated into other logistics applications.

In summary, we make the following contributions:

1.

We propose a novel approach for open vocabulary grocery packing that prevents transport damage by optimizing the packing sequence.

2.

We propose the Packing Consistency Score (C), a novel data-driven metric for human-like grocery packing.

3.

We release a dataset on relative grocery placement, including human-annotated ground truth.

4.

We release the code, permitting easy integration of our method in custom scenarios.

II
Related Work

II-A

Grocery Store Automation

Several approaches were developed to automate or simplify the process of grocery shopping.
Cheng
et al.

[
2
]
present a shopping assistance robot, capable of navigating through a supermarket. It autonomously picks items on the customers shopping list from the shelves, and places them into a basket. Contrary to our approach, it chooses the picking order to minimize traveling distance instead of focusing on product integrity.
Thompson
et al.

[
3
]
introduce a service robot for guiding a customer to product locations in a grocery store. The robot also enables automatic billing and functions as an autonomous trolley, following a given shopping list.
Priya
et al.

[
4
]
follow a similar approach while adding an option for automatic payment with connection to the customers bank account.
Dworakowski
et al.

[
5
]
present ContextSLAM, which enables autonomous navigation in dynamic grocery store environments and aids service robots at locating requested products. The aforementioned works rather focus on navigation, object detection, and object picking and do not consider packing of the groceries.

II-B

Bin Packing Methods

The vast majority of bin packing methods target industrial or warehouse domains. There, individual items are often pre-packed for transportation and their shape can be approximated by simple geometric priors. Thus, research attention has been mainly put on efficient bin space utilization or object stability within the bin.

Jampacker
[
6
]
is a warehouse packing algorithm for cuboid-shaped objects aiming at free space minimization in the bin. The authors also propose a learning-based module for correcting imprecise placement actions by the robot arm.
Similarly, Xiong
et al.

[
7
]
approach the 3D packing problem for rectangular items with a transformer-based architecture trained with reinforcement learning. In contrast to other learning-driven methods, they are able to generalize to different packing bin sizes.
SFD-Pack
[
8
]
optimizes the packing compactness of objects with arbitrary shape which are represented by a Signed Distance Function.
Wang and Hauser
[
9
]
also propose a packing method for complex object shapes that further incorporates stability constraints and feasibility of placement execution.

Only a few works address grocery packing, where the packing sequence is crucial for keeping the goods intact.
Chen
et al.

[
10
]
propose a grocery packing system consisting of a proprioceptive soft gripper, tactile sensors, and
RGB-D
cameras.
They leverage multimodal data to estimate the weight, size, and fragility of groceries and subsequently compute a packing sequence score for each item.
Similar to our work, Santos
et al.

[
11
]
propose to explicitly imitate human grocery packing preferences.
To collect training data, human operators put a set of typical groceries into a box using a VR setup.
A Markov chain model is then trained on the human-defined sequences.
In contrast, our approach is zero-shot and open-vocabulary, i.e., it does not require training and can handle arbitrary objects.

The LLM-Pack approach

Fig. 2

III-A

Overview

Our goal is to pack a diverse set of grocery items into a box while preserving product integrity.
We achieve this by dividing the process into three major steps.
In the perception step, we employ a VLM for general object detection and classification that generates a list of the items in the scene and in front of the robot.
Next, in the planning step, we query an LLM to estimate a proper packing sequence for the detected items.
Lastly, we execute the suggested packing order using the GroundedSAM approach
[
12
]
.
The overall method is summarized in Figure
2
.

III-B

Perception Step

In the perception step we employ a VLM whose output is post-processed using an aggregated parsing mechanism. In our prompt, we ask the model to enumerate the grocery items in the scene.
Since our approach is modular and supports several VLMs, we empirically tune the prompt for each model to increase its performance. Below, we show an example for GPT-4o:

"role": "system", "content": "You are an intelligent AI, assisting a robot in packing a bag of groceries. As a first step, you need to identify the items in the image. Answer in a comma separated string. For example, if the image contains apples and bananas, you should answer ‘‘apples, bananas’’."

"role": "user", "content": "Which grocery items are on the image?"

The response is then parsed into a list of object labels by splitting the output at commas.
Sometimes, VLMs add unnecessary explanations to the output.
Experiments showed that this reduces the performance of the following planning step, especially by increasing the probability for hallucinated predictions.
It is thus important to detect and remove such outliers.
We implement a detection based on the number of chars in each label.
Therefore, we gather a list of
120
common grocery items and calculate the standard deviation of the length in terms of chars.
If the length of an object label exceeds six times the standard deviation, we consider the prediction to be an outlier and remove the respective result.
In our experiments the subsequent planning step has demonstrated to benefit from this parsing process.

III-C

Planning Step

In the planning step we determine a suitable packing sequence for the objects in the previously generated list. To this end, we query an LLM for a packing sequence of the items in the list.
We evaluated several LLMs as detailed in the experimental results section below. We tune the
prompts for each model separately, the prompt for GPT-4o is given below.

"role": "system", "content": "You are an intelligent AI, assisting a robot in packing a bag of groceries. You are provided with a list of grocery items. The bag should be packed so that no item is damaged. Answer in a comma separated string. The first item on the list is loaded first and is thus the lowest in the bag. For example, if the list contains bricks and eggs, you should answer ’bricks, eggs’."

"role": "user", "content": "How should the following items be loaded? <list of items>"

Notably, we refer to a “shopping bag” instead of a “shopping box” in the prompt above.
Extensive tests during development showed, that mentioning a “box” leads the VLM towards thinking of the packing process in terms of layers (e.g. First Layer: Caned Food, Bottle. Second Layer: Strawberries, Eggs). This is beneficial when dealing with known boxes. In this work, however, we do not restrict the size of the box.
Mentioning a bag, helps the VLM to think of the process in terms of a sequence, ultimately boosting results.

We expect the LLM to return a formatted object list in the correct placing order.
While OpenAI models (GPT-4.5, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini and O3-mini) adhere well to the above output format, DeepSeek-R1 generally requires more prompt engineering to obtain an output that can be fed to the subsequent execution step.
Even with a tuned system prompt, however, it tends to hallucinate.
Such hallucinated answers lead to unpredictable results in the down-stream GroundedSAM
[
12
]
object detector.
To prevent this, we check with a string matching technique, if more then
30
% of the previously detected groceries appear in the predicted sequence.
If this test fails, we re-execute the planning step.

III-D

Execution Step

We finally plan and execute the manipulation task based on the obtained object sequence.
First, we retrieve a segmentation mask for the object of interest using GroundedSAM.
We then project the mask onto the scene point cloud to retrieve the points for the respective object.
Based on the point cloud and the RGB frame, we approach the grasp pose.

LLM-Pack relies on Contact-GraspNet (CGN)
[
13
]
, a Neural Network that generates grasping poses from a point cloud representing the scene. We use linear interpolation in the Cartesian space of the end-effector to generate a path connecting the gripper’s initial pose, the predicted grasp pose, and a predefined drop-off position.

Please note that we also tested the grasping performance of two freely available state-of-the-art Vision-Language-Action Models (VLAs), namely Octo
[
14
]
and OpenVLA
[
15
]
. In our experiments, we unfortunately did not obtain a performance that is comparable to that of CGN. In future work, we will further investigate this.

IV-B

Hardware Setup

For real-world experiments, we employ a Franka Research 3 robot manipulator with custom 3D-printed fingers for the gripper which allow us to handle large grocery items with a higher success rate.
We further use an Orbbec Femto Bolt Camera to capture
RGB-D

In all experiments, we execute our neural network models on a workstation equipped with two NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs.
The robot control code is executed by a separate computer running Debian Linux with a real-time kernel.
We further require access to the OpenAI API.

IV-C

Object Recognition Evaluation

We first evaluate the performance of the perception step.
Specifically, we test how well different VLMs recognize objects in a frame. We base our evaluation on the F1 Score which is defined as:

F_{1}=2\times\frac{\text{Precision}\times\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}

(1)

We calculate the F1 Score individually for every grocery class and finally average the results to obtain the Average F1 Score (AF1).
This value is our primary evaluation metric for the detection evaluation.
We also report Average Precision (AP) and Average Recall (AR) as a base for further interpretation (see Tab.
I
).
Overall, GPT-4.5 delivers the best performance with an average F1 score of
86.88
%.
GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, and LLaVA fall behind in this evaluation.

We refer to this number as the “scene size”.
Therefore, we conduct an ablation study to discover any potential scaling problems.

Grocery Packing Dataset

C=\{c_{i}\,\dots,c_{n}\}.

(2)

\leq

IV-E

Packing Consistency Score (C)

We denote this set of human-annotated sequences as

S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{k}\},

(4)

where every sequence
s_{i}
consists of
m
grocery classes

s_{i}=\{c_{i,1},\dots,c_{i,m}\}.

(5)

Given these sequences, we calculate for every pair of grocery classes the frequency or probability

P(c_{i}\leq c_{k})\,\forall i,k

(6)

that the object class
c_{i}
is placed below
c_{j}
by humans. Table
II
contains a fraction of these probabilities

P(c_{i}\leq c_{k})
for a subset of the data we collected.
During evaluation, we employ an LLM to predict a packing sequence based on a given set of grocery classes.
We call this sequence

s^{*}=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{l}\}

(7)

for packing
l
groceries.
The conditional probability of
s^{*}
given the human sequences
S
is

P(s^{*}\mid S)=\prod_{i\leq j}P(c_{i}\leq c_{j}).

(8)

To evaluate packing sequences we employ the so-called Packing Consistency Score (C), which is the log-likelyhood of
P(s^{*}\mid S)
:

\text{C}(s^{*})=\log(P(s^{*}\mid S))

(9)

It serves as a primary evaluation metric in Sections
IV-F
and
IV-H
.

IV-G

Grasp Module Assessemnt

We test both, the VLA- and the CGN-based variant of the Grasp Module on the task of grasping grocery items.
A grasp is considered successful if the respective object is moved from the table and placed into a box.
In total, we conduct 25 tries on 5 objects and compare both variants based on success rate.
Our experiments show that both evaluated VLAs struggle to generalize to previously unseen grocery objects, with neither model successfully grasping them.
This performance gap may stem from insufficient training data.
Future investigations should explore the underlying causes.
The CGN-based variant performs better, achieving a grasp success rate of
72\text{\,}\%
on grocery items.
However, its effectiveness highly depends on the properties of the manipulated object. In particular, translucent items, such as glass bottles, pose a challenge due to the reduced accuracy of point cloud data captured by RGB-D cameras. Consequently, grasps on such objects are often less precise.

IV-H

Full System Evaluation

We evaluate two aspects of our LLM-Pack approach.
First, we assess its performance in proposing the right packing sequence. To that end, we apply the first two steps of our approach to a variety of item sets of different sizes ranging from 6 to 20 items.

As mentioned above, our method uses GPT-4.5 as VLM in the perception step and as LLM in the planning step.
The results are depicted in Fig.
6
.
The performance shows a downward sloping trend with increasing scene size.
The figure also illustrates the constraint satisfaction rate, which remains roughly constant regarding the scene size with an average of
79.83
%.
We achieve an
aC
Score of
-24.22
at a success rate of
100
%.

Second, we conduct experiments with our Franka Research 3 robot to validate the practical applicability of our approach in real-world settings. These experiments evaluate the full system in an end-to-end manner.
In total, we sort five grocery items into a box and repeat the process three times.
We then report the performance in terms of C.
We achieve a packing consistency score of
-0.681
on the practical evaluations.
It should be noted that this score is better than the ones obtained in the first two steps. The reason for this is that the previous two steps do not consider the free space in the box. If there is sufficient space in the box, the third step might place an object into the free space or on top of an object so that it does not violate any packing constraints. The proper inclusion of free space will be subject for future research.

V
Conclusions

In this paper, we introduced LLM-Pack, an approach for open-vocabulary grocery packing that ensures product integrity. To achieve its task, LLM-Pack utilizes VLMs and state-of-the-art grasping techniques.
We validated our approach on a custom dataset containing a variety of grocery classes.
The dataset will be publicly available upon publication of this manuscript.
We quantified the performance of LLM-Pack based on a custom-developed data-driven metric C.
The experiments demonstrate that LLM-Pack shows great performance in sorting of objects according to human-preferred packing order. One opportunity for future work is to include VLAs in executing this task. Additionally, one could use more advanced strategies to reason about the free space in the box for the placing task.
