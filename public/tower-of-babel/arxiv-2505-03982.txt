A residual-iteration framework for alternating projections between affine subspaces
Nguyen T. Thao

Abstract
We reformulate the problem of alternating projections between two affine subspaces of a Hilbert space as the minimization of a least-squares functional associated with a bounded linear operator. This viewpoint reveals that classical alternating projections coincide with the unit-step Landweber iteration and enables the introduction of a general residual-state iteration framework that encompasses Landweber, its steepest-descent variant, and the conjugate-gradient method. Within this framework, we establish abstract convergence principles based on residual extinction and translation equivariance, allowing convergence analyses to be carried out once at the level of least-squares optimization and then transferred directly to alternating projection algorithms. As applications, we obtain new variants of alternating projections accelerated by steepest descent and conjugate gradients, together with convergence guarantees in both the consistent and inconsistent settings. We also establish linear convergence results under closed-range assumptions and express the convergence rates explicitly in terms of the Friedrichs angle and the largest principal angle between the underlying subspaces.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the least-squares formulation of alternating projections and shows that the classical alternating projection method is exactly the unit-step Landweber iteration. Section 3 introduces a general framework of admissible residual-state iterations, within which residual extinction and translation equivariance provide abstract tools for deriving strong convergence results independently of any particular algorithm. Sections 4, 6 and 7 then show that the Landweber, steepest-descent and conjugate-gradient methods all fit this framework, leading to corresponding variants of alternating projections together with convergence guarantees in both the consistent and inconsistent settings. Section 5 specializes these results to alternating projections and relates the convergence behavior to the principal angles between the underlying subspaces.

In addition to strong convergence, we investigate linear convergence under suitable closed-range assumptions. Besides providing quantitative convergence guarantees, these estimates make it possible to compare analytically the convergence speeds of the different methods in the infinite-dimensional setting, where numerical experimentation alone cannot capture the general behavior of the algorithms. In particular, the resulting convergence rates provide a theoretical comparison between classical alternating projections and their steepest-descent and conjugate-gradient accelerations.

2.4
General properties of least-squares solutions

Theorem
2.2
has helped explain a major part of Theorem
1.1
from the broader perspective of minimizing a least-squares functional
\Phi_{Q,w}
. In preparation for algorithmic generalizations, we review several important properties on the set of minimizers
M_{Q,w}
in infinite dimension. We assume again
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}(U,\hat{U})

{\cal D}(Q)

: domain of

{\cal R}(Q)

: range of

Proposition 2.3
.

Let

. Then,

if and only if any of the following properties is satisfied:

(i)

(ii)

(iii)

(iv)

Proof.

(i)
: We have

Qu-w=(Qu-\bar{w})+(w-\bar{w})~~\text{where}~~Qu-\bar{w}\in\overline{{\cal R}(Q)}~~\text{and}~~w-\bar{w}\in{\cal R}(Q)^{\perp}.\vskip-2.84526pt

\|Qu-w\|^{2}=\|Qu-\bar{w}\|^{2}+\|w-\bar{w}\|^{2}

(ii)
: It follows from (

.

(iii)
: This is the well-known characterization of least-squares solutions, see for example

(iv)
: This follows using

∎

Whenever

Closed range condition

2.5.1

2.5.2
Application to alternating projections

Returning to the problem of alternating projections between

Under the definition of (

\displaystyle U_{0}:=U\cap(U\!\cap\!V)^{\perp}\qquad\text{and}\qquad V_{0}:=V\cap(U\!\cap\!V)^{\perp}.

Proposition 2.7
.

\gamma(Q)=\sin\theta_{\mathrm{f}}(U,V).

We first need to establish the following equivalent characterization of

\cos\theta_{\mathrm{f}}=\sup_{u\in U_{0},\|u\|=1}\|P_{V}u\|.

Since

\cos\theta_{\mathrm{f}}=\sup_{u\in U_{0},\|u\|=1}\|P_{V_{0}}u\|

, so that

. Hence,

Next, as

A residual-state framework for least-squares iterations

For a nonexpansive operator
Q
, we showed that the Landweber iteration in (
14
) with
\alpha=1
achieves Property
1.2
at least partially. Our goal is not only to complete proof of Property
1.2
for this iteration, but to explore a larger family of iterations that achieve this property. Throughout this section, we consider a given operator
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}(U,\hat{U})
.

3.1
Family of admissible iterations

As shown in Proposition
2.3
, a vector
u\in U
is a minimizer of
\Phi_{Q,w}
if and only if it is a solution of the normal equation
Q^{*}Qu=Q^{*}w
. For the minimization of
\Phi_{Q,w}
, it is natural to focus on algorithms that generate iterates
(u^{(n)})_{n\geq 0}
whose residuals

r^{(n)}:=Q^{*}Qu^{(n)}-Q^{*}w=Q^{*}(Qu^{(n)}\!-w),\qquad n\geq 0

converge to zero. We propose to achieve this objective using iterations of the form

\displaystyle r^{(n)}

\displaystyle=Q^{*}(Qu^{(n)}\!-w),

(29a)

\displaystyle(d^{(n)},s^{(n+1)})

\displaystyle=J_{n}(r^{(n)},s^{(n)}),

(29b)

\displaystyle u^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=u^{(n)}+d^{(n)},\qquad n\geq 0

(29c)

where
J_{n}
is a mapping of the form

J_{n}:~{\cal R}(Q^{*})\backslash\{0\}\times{\mathscr{S}}_{n}~\longrightarrow~{\cal R}(Q^{*})\times{\mathscr{S}}_{n+1}

and
({\mathscr{S}}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a growing family of state spaces with
{\mathscr{S}}_{0}=\{0\}
. The role of the state variable
s^{(n)}\in{\mathscr{S}}_{n}
is to store certain quantities derived from previous values of
r^{(n)}
. As
{\mathscr{S}}_{0}=\{0\}
, it follows that
s^{(0)}
is 0 by default. We also imply that the iteration is terminated whenever
r^{(n)}=0
, to remain consistent with the residual domain of
J_{n}
.
We call any iteration of the form (
29
) an
admissible iteration
. Such an iteration is uniquely defined by the sequence of mappings
(J_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. Once such a sequence has been fixed, note that
u^{(n)}
and
r^{(n)}
are uniquely determined by
w\in\hat{U}
and
u^{(0)}\in U
. We write that

\displaystyle u^{(n)}

\displaystyle=U_{n}(w,u^{(0)}),

(30a)

\displaystyle r^{(n)}

\displaystyle=R_{n}(w,u^{(0)}),\qquad n\geq 0,~(w,u^{(0)})\in\hat{U}{\times}U.

(30b)

We call
U_{n}
and
R_{n}
the
n
th iterate map and residual map of the iteration.
Note that

R_{n}(w,u^{(0)})=Q^{*}(Q\hskip 1.0ptU_{n}(w,u^{(0)})-w).

(31)

In the case where
M_{Q,w}\neq\emptyset
, we also define

E_{n}(w,u^{(0)}):=U_{n}(w,u^{(0)})-P_{M_{Q,w}}u^{(0)},\qquad n\geq 0.

(32)

We call
E_{n}
the
n
th error map of the iteration, or the error map associated with
U_{n}
.

3.2
Standard methods as admissible iterations

We now describe the three methods of least-squares quadratic minimization that will be studied in this paper and that fit this framework.

3.2.1
Landweber iteration with varying step size

We now study in detail the following general version of Landweber iteration

u^{(n+1)}=u^{(n)}-\alpha_{n}\hskip 1.0ptQ^{*}(Qu^{(n)}\!-w),\qquad n\geq 0

(33)

where
(\alpha_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a given varying sequence of coefficients. This can be written as the two-term recurrence

\displaystyle r^{(n)}

\displaystyle=Q^{*}(Qu^{(n)}\!-w),

(34a)

\displaystyle u^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=u^{(n)}-\alpha_{n}\hskip 1.0ptr^{(n)},\qquad n\geq 0.

(34b)

This fits the structure of (
29

{\mathscr{S}}_{n}=\{0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad J_{n}(r,0)=(-\alpha_{n}\hskip 1.0ptr,0),\quad r\in{\cal R}(Q^{*}),\qquad n\geq 0.

From a computational perspective, note in this case that
J_{n}
does not depend on
Q

3.2.2
Steepest-descent variant of Landweber iteration

This is a variant of the Landweber iteration where
\alpha_{n}
is chosen adaptively at each iteration. Specifically,
\alpha_{n}
is chosen so as to minimize
\Phi_{Q,w}(u^{(n+1)})
. Given that
r^{(n)}=\nabla\Phi_{Q,w}(u^{(n)})
from (

[
20

\alpha_{n}:=\argmin_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}}\Phi_{Q,w}\big(u^{(n)}\!-\alpha r^{(n)}\big)=\dfrac{\|r^{(n)}\|^{2}}{\|Qr^{(n)}\|^{2}}

(35)

This fits the framework of (
29

{\mathscr{S}}_{n}=\{0\}\quad\text{and}\quad J_{n}(r,0):={\big({-}(\|r\|^{2}/\|Qr\|^{2})\hskip 1.0ptr,0\big)},\quad r\in{\cal R}(Q^{*})\backslash\{0\},\qquad n\geq 0.

In this case,
J_{n}
does not depend on

3.3
Consequences of residual extinction

At the level of generality level of admissible iterations, requiring residual extinction is not sufficient to guarantee Property
1.2
. The following proposition shows which part of this property is guaranteed.

Proposition 3.2
.

For an admissible iteration and a given pair
(w,u^{(0)})\in\hat{U}{\times}U

(i)

If

.

(ii)

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\|u^{(n)}\|=\infty

Proof.

(i)
If

converges with limit

, we obtain

converges to zero. Thus,

by Proposition

. Now, by assumption on

for all

. This implies that

(iv)
,

is bounded. Then, there exists a subsequence

What remains to be established is the mere guarantee of iteration convergence in the case

3.5
Closed range and linear convergence

As shown in Section
2.5
, the closedness of
{\cal R}(Q)
ensures that

\Phi_{Q,w}
admits minimizers for every
w\in\hat{U}
. The same
assumption naturally arises when seeking linear convergence. Indeed, in the
standard algorithms considered in this paper, closed range is precisely the
stability condition that allows residual estimates to be converted into
error estimates. We now explain this point in the framework of admissible
residual-state iterations.

Fix an admissible iteration, and let
(w,u_{0})\in{\cal D}(Q^{\dagger}){\times}U
and set

u^{(n)}:=U_{n}(w,u^{(0)})
,
r^{(n)}:=R_{n}(w,u^{(0)})
and
e^{(n)}:=E_{n}(w,u^{(0)})
. By Corollary
3.6
, there exists
\tilde{u}^{(0)}\in{\cal N}(Q)^{\perp}
such that
r^{(n)}=R_{n}(0,\tilde{u}^{(0)})
and
e^{(n)}=U_{n}(0,\tilde{u}^{(0)})\in{\cal N}(Q)^{\perp}
for all
n\geq 0
. Applying (
31
) with 0 in place of
w
, we obtain
r_{n}=Q^{*}Qe^{(n)}
for all
n\geq 0
. As
\gamma(Q^{*}Q)=\gamma(Q)^{2}

[
18
]
and
e^{(n)}\in{\cal N}(Q)^{\perp}={\cal N}(Q^{*}Q)^{\perp}
, then

\|r^{(n)}\|\geq\gamma(Q)^{2}\hskip 1.0pt\|e^{(n)}\|,\qquad n\geq 0.

Recall from Theorem
2.6
that
\gamma(Q)>0
if and only if
{\cal R}(Q)
is closed.
When
\gamma(Q)>0
, any geometric decay estimate for the residuals immediately yields a
geometric decay bound for the effective errors. Otherwise, residual
extinction alone no longer provides uniform geometric control of
the iterates. This does not prove that closed range is necessary for every
possible algorithm, but it explains qualitatively why it is the natural structural
assumption under which standard residual-state iterations can reasonably be expected
to admit linear convergence estimates.

There is an interesting similarity of the steepest-descent variant of alternating projections and the extrapolated alternating projection method (EAPM)

\displaystyle u^{(n+1)}=u^{(n)}\!+\alpha_{n}\big(P_{U}P_{W}u^{(n)}\!-u^{(n)}\big),

(106a)

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}:=\dfrac{\|P_{W}u^{(n)}\!-u^{(n)}\|^{2}}{\|P_{U}P_{W}u^{(n)}\!-u^{(n)}\|^{2}}.

\displaystyle q^{(n)}

\displaystyle=P_{W}u^{(n)}\!-u^{(n)}

(108a)

\displaystyle u^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=u^{(n)}+\alpha_{n}\hskip 1.0ptP_{U}q^{(n)}

(108b)

where the coefficients are given by

\alpha_{n}=\frac{\|q^{(n)}\|^{2}}{\|P_{U}\hskip 1.0ptq^{(n)}\|^{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha_{n}=\frac{\|P_{U}\hskip 1.0ptq^{(n)}\|^{2}}{\|P_{V^{\perp}\!}(P_{U}\hskip 1.0ptq^{(n)})\|^{2}}.

(109)

[

Conclusion

In this paper, we reformulated alternating projections between two affine subspaces of a Hilbert space as the minimization of an associated least-squares functional. This optimization viewpoint identifies the classical alternating projection method as the unit-step Landweber iteration and provides a common framework in which optimization algorithms can be systematically translated into alternating projection algorithms.

To formalize this connection, we introduced the class of admissible residual-state iterations, which encompasses the Landweber, steepest-descent, and conjugate-gradient methods. The notions of residual extinction and translation equivariance were shown to provide a unified mechanism for transferring convergence results from least-squares optimization to alternating projections. This framework yields strong convergence results in both the consistent and inconsistent settings, while separating the analysis of optimization algorithms from their geometric interpretation in terms of projections.

As applications, we derived accelerated variants of alternating projections based on steepest descent and conjugate gradients. Under closed-range assumptions, linear convergence was established and the convergence rates were expressed in terms of the Friedrichs angle and the largest principal angle between the underlying subspaces. The resulting comparison highlights the expected acceleration provided by the conjugate-gradient method over classical alternating projections.

Beyond the algorithms studied here, the proposed optimization framework suggests a general methodology for constructing and analyzing alternating projection methods through their least-squares counterparts. Future work could investigate additional optimization techniques within this framework, including accelerated first-order methods, quasi-Newton methods, and regularization strategies for ill-posed problems.
