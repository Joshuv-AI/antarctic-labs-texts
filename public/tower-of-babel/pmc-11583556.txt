Predictors of amputation in patients with acute compartment syndrome after tibial fracture: a nomogram prediction model
Authors: Tao Wang, Qi Zhang, Zhiyong Hou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposesAmputation is a serious complication in patients with acute compartment syndrome (ACS). However, it risk factors are still poorly understood. Our study aims to investigate the risk factors of amputation in patients with ACS.MethodsWe reviewed the data on ACS patients with tibial fractures from January 2010 to November 2022. Patients with amputation was grouped into the amputation group (AG) and those without amputation was grouped into non-amputation group (NG). We used univariate analysis, logistic regression analysis and nomogram prediction model to analyze the predictors of amputation.ResultsThe rate of amputation was 8.8% (12 of 136) in our study. Crush injury (p = 0.047), heavy object injury (p = 0.045), the presence of blisters (p<0.0001), the number of debridements (p = 0.022), muscle necrosis (p = 0.004), deep venous thrombosis (DVT, p<0.0001), the level of osmotic pressure (p = 0.003) after fasciotomy were found to be associated with amputation in ACS patients by univariate analysis. Logistic regression analysis showed that crush injury [p = 0.036, OR = 16.403, 95% CI (1.198, 224.609)], heavy object injury [p = 0.010, OR = 50.751, 95% CI (2.577, 999.490)], muscle necrosis [p = 0.017, OR = 17.272, 95% CI (1.666, 179.102)], and DVT [p = 0.009, OR = 22.344, 95% CI (2.146, 232.589)] were risk factors of amputation. Then, we constructed a nomogram prediction model with 0.9066 in AUC of the prediction model with good consistency in the correction curve and good clinical practicality by decision curve analysis.ConclusionsWe identified crush injury, heavy object injury, muscle necrosis, and DVT as independent risk factors for amputation in ACS patients. Our nomogram prediction model can availably predict amputation in ACS patients. Additionally, we found that the timing of fasciotomy is not associated with amputation in ACS patients.Level of evidenceLevel III.Keywords: Acute compartment syndrome, Amputation, Crush injury, Fracture, Nomogram prediction model

Introduction

Acute compartment syndrome (ACS), affecting 30.4% of tibial fracture patients, is a severe complication following comminuted fractures or tibial plateau fractures [1–3]. It commonly causes increased intra-compartmental pressure, resulting in decrease of tissue perfusion [4, 5]. Our previous meta-analysis showed that younger age, male patients, hypertension, smoking, and high-energy related fractures were associated with the development of ACS [6]. Currently, ACS is frequently diagnosed and treated primarily on the clinician’s expertise rather than the gold standard testing, it has been challenging for physicians.

Delayed diagnosis and treatment can cause serious consequences, such as sensory deficits, paralysis, infection, muscle necrosis, or even amputation [7], which can lead to disability [6, 8] and place great burdens on individuals and societies. It is urgent to identify the predictors of amputation in ACS patients so that actions can be taken to lower the rate of amputation. To our knowledge, only one study focusing on the predictors of amputation in ACS patients [9], however, Wei [9] considered ACS caused by vascular damage and fractures. Additionally, there is a relative paucity of data assessing the relationship between related factors of fasciotomy, such as timing of fasciotomy and variations of laboratory indicators after fasciotomy, and predictors of amputation. Therefore, we aim to explore the risk factors for amputation in ACS patients according to overall data before and after fasciotomy by a nomogram prediction model.

Methods

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University (S2020-024-1) in compliance with the Helsinki and an exemption from the informed consent was obtained. All data were anonymized before the analysis to safeguard patient privacy.

Patients

We collected data, including demographics, anamnesis, and laboratory examinations before and after fasciotomy, of 136 patients with ACS following tibial fractures, between Jan 2010 to Sep 2023 in our hospital.

The demographics data included age, gender, body mass index, injury mechanism, time from injury to admission, time from injury to fasciotomy, number of debridements, injury type, dehydrant, American society of anesthesiologists, blisters, multiple fractures, muscle necrosis, infection, deep venous thrombosis (based on doppler), as well as patients with a history of smoking, and alcohol. Anamnesis consist of arrhythmia, coronary heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and cerebral infarction. Laboratory indicators covered lymphocyte (LYM), LYM(%), monocyte (MON), MON(%), neutrophil (NEU), NEU(%), neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet (PLT), white blood cell (WBC), red blood cell (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLOB), ALB/GLOB, creatine kinase (CK), creatine kinase myocardial band (CKMB), K+, Na+, Mg2+, P3+, lactic dehydrogenase (LDH), osmotic pressure (OSM), prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), fibrinogen (FIB), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), thrombin time (TT).

We defined ACS patients with amputation as the amputation group (AG) and ACS patients without amputation as the non-amputation group (NG). Inclusion criteria: 1) diagnosed with ACS after tibial fractures based on clinical symptoms and the ∆P < 30mmHg (∆P = diastolic arterial pressureintra-compartmental pressure); 2) ≥ 18 years old; and 3) received fasciotomy; and 4) no comorbidity was present at the time of admission, such as head injury or multiple fractures. The exclusion criteria were: (1) death; (2) vascular injuries; (3) pathological fractures; and (4) incomplete data.

Statistics

We used by SPSS (version 27.0 SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and regarded p < 0.05 as statistical significance. Because the continuous variables not met normality criteria in our study, so we used Mann-Whitney U test. Regarding count data, we used the chi-square test. Furthermore, we used binary logistic regression analysis and receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to identify the independent predictors of DVT and their cut-off values. Finally, R language software was used to establish the nomogram prediction model for amputation, then we assessed the discriminative ability of the prediction model by AUC of ROC, and we evaluated the predicted and actual probabilities of this prediction model by calibration curve. The decision curve analysis is to evaluate the clinical application value of the prediction model.

Results

We collected 136 ACS patients after tibial fractures, including 12 ACS patients with amputation due to severe infection or muscle necrosis and 124 ACS patients without amputation. The rate of amputation in ACS patients was 8.8%. As shown in Table 1, no marked difference was found in age, gender, BMI, time from injury to admission, time from injury to fasciotomy, injury type, dehydrant, ASA, multiple fractures, as well as patients with a history of smoking, alcohol, arrhythmia, coronary heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and cerebral infarction by univariate analysis. However, we found that mechanism of injury (p = 0.009), crush injury (p = 0.047), heavy object injury (p = 0.045), the presence of blisters (p<0.0001), the number of debridements (p = 0.022), muscle necrosis (p = 0.004), deep venous thrombosis (p<0.0001) after fasciotomy were found to be associated with the risk of amputation.

Discussion

ACS is a condition where an increase in pressure within a closed compartment results in a decline in tissue perfusion [10, 11]. If the ischemia of tissue within the confined compartments persists, ACS can lead to complications, such as infection, muscle necrosis, or amputation, ranging from 30 to 40% according to various studies [12, 13]. In terms of these tough complications, Hake [14] found that the age of the patient and negative-pressure wound therapy were predictors of surgical site infection after emergent fasciotomy, while Mortensen [15] only found that open fractures were associated with muscle necrosis following ACS. Wei [9] investigated the predictors of amputation ACS caused by vascular damage and fractures. Our previous study has reported the predictors of muscle necrosis in ACS patients and found crush injury and the level of NEU, CKMB, and PT related to muscle necrosis [16]. However, few study has focused on the potential predictors of amputation in ACS patients and the relationship between timing of fasciotomy and laboratory indicators and amputation. Notably, we conducted a nomogram prediction model to predict amputation.

Therefore, we tried to investigate the predictors of amputation in ACS patients according to overall data before and after fasciotomy. In our study, the rate of amputation was 8.8% (12 of 136). We found no significant association between the timing of the fasciotomy and the increased risk of amputation. Our findings showed that crush injury, heavy object injury, muscle necrosis, and DVT were associated with amputation in patients with ACS in a nomogram prediction model .

According to general assumptions, tissue ischemia can cause reversible damage within three hours, whereas ischemia lasting more than 4–6 h results in irreversible muscle necrosis [17, 18]. The issue of the timing of fasciotomy is still controversial. Velmahos [19] pointed out that the time between the injury and fasciotomy of more than 8 h can significantly increase the risk for complications in ACS patients, which was analogous with the finding by Duckworth [12] that delayed fasciotomy was a predictor of the long-term complications. However, the conclusions of recent studies were inconsistent with previous literature. Mortensen [15] did not find a close relationship between the timing of fasciotomy and muscle necrosis, and Hake [14] discovered that delay in the timing of fasciotomy was not a risk factor for infection after ACS, which complied with our findings that the time from injury to admission or the timing of fasciotomy was not related to amputation in ACS patients. Amputations were also found in 8.1% (7 of 86) of patients who had fasciotomies within 12 h, 8.3% (3 of 36) of those who had fasciotomies between 12 and 24 h, and 14.3% (2 of 14) of those who had fasciotomies more than 24 h. Although the timing of fasciotomy with more than 24 h had the highest rate, there was no significantly different rate among the three groups. In our opinion, the timing of fasciotomy does not represent when the ACS and the ischemia started for each individual, which is actually impossible to confirm. The level of damage energy to the patient at the time of injury determines amputation.

Crush injuries and heavy object injuries were two leading contributors of ACS patients [20, 21]. Our finding showed 30.9% of patients suffering from crush injuries and 12.5% of patients with heavy object injuries, and we found these patients with higher risk of amputation, which was consistent with previous studies [13, 24]. Previous study has confirmed that the trauma can cause death of the muscle cells immediately [22]. Furthermore, high-energy injury can cause less acute pain initially due to neurapraxias, which can conceal the occurrence of ACS and result in a delay or missed diagnosis [23].

Mortensen [15] found 14% of patients suffering from muscle necrosis and open fractures relevant to necrotic muscle, whereas Hope [14] found that patients without fractures were more likely to occur muscle necrosis. In this study, we found 41.2% of patients suffering muscle necrosis after fasciotomy and 17.9% of patients with muscle necrosis experiencing amputation, indicating that muscle necrosis was an independent risk factor for amputation after ACS. Notably, 53.6% of patients experienced muscle necrosis following a crush injury or heavy object injury. Similarly, our findings showed 52.9% of patients experienced DVT after a crush injury or heavy object injury, and 35.3% of patients with DVT received amputation. These findings indicated that crush injury or heavy object injury was the primary cause of amputation.

As we know, an ever-rising pressure in a closed compartment can result in a hypoxic and ischemic microenvironment [25, 26, 27], which can cause variations of immune cells. We first focused on the role of immune cells and their variation after fasciotomy in amputation, but we failed to sort out the significant association. It may be that, as we mentioned above, the prognosis is determined by the condition of the patient at the time of injury. It is well known that high-energy injuries are more likely to cause serious complications, but this is also related to the abilities of patients’ responses to injury.

According to the results of univariate and multivariate analyses, we established a prediction model to evaluate the amputation in ACS patients. The ROC curve of the nomogram suggested good discrimination ability, and the calibration curve of the nomogram with Hosmer Lemeshow goodness of fit test showed well calibrated. Decision curve analysis showed that the nomogram prediction model has good clinical benefits.

Although this was the first study to examine the predictors for amputation in ACS patients and it provided some novel findings, there are several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the precision of the results will inevitably be impacted by a single-center study. There was a need for a large sample size, multicenter, and randomized controlled trial. Second, because it was a retrospective study, the data collection had inherent limitations, particularly when it came to self-reported comorbidities. Additionally, some relevant parameters, including the quantity of debridements and levels of amputation, might not be taken into account in this study. Third, we failed to conduct the subgroup analysis, such as location of fractures, since there were limited number of ACS patients.

Conclusions

We found that crush injury, heavy object injury, muscle necrosis, and DVT were independent risk factors for amputation after ACS. Our nomogram prediction model that can effectively predict amputation in ACS patients. Additionally, we found that the timing of fasciotomy is not associated with amputation in ACS patients.
