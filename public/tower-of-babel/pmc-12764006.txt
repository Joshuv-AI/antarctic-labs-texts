Developing an integrated evaluation indicator system for non-communicable disease management in China’s primary care: a modified Delphi-analytic hierarchy process study based on the structure-process-outcome framework
Authors: Jiajia Zhang, Yuhan Wu, Rui Tian, Dahai Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aging population and increasing prevalence of chronic conditions have led to higher healthcare costs and greater resource utilization globally, exerting considerable pressure on healthcare system sustainability. Integrating services in primary healthcare can improve access and effectiveness for chronic disease management. However, China’s primary healthcare institutions have not achieved full integration of essential clinical services and public health services, hindering the continuity of care and chronic disease management. Background

Chronic noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) are the leading cause of disease burden worldwide [1, 2]. According to the latest data from the World Health Organization (WHO), NCDs accounted for at least 43 million deaths in 2021, equivalent to 75% of non-pandemic-related deaths globally [3]. Beyond their health impact, NCDs also cause negative economic consequences and further influence social development [4, 5]. From 2011 to 2030, the projected global economic loss due to NCDs is USD 47 trillion, approximately 55% of the global GDP in 2020 (USD 85 trillion) [6]. Preventable new NCD cases are predicted to cost an additional USD 301.8 billion between 2020 and 2030 [7], with 73% of new cases expected to occur in low- and middle-income countries, whereas high-income countries shoulder 63% of the economic burden.

In light of the challenges posed by the disease burden and economic pressures, health systems worldwide are transitioning from reactive NCD treatment to proactive prevention-oriented strategies. Within this paradigm, the primary healthcare (PHC) system plays an important role, as it emphasizes early detection, preventive services, and continuous care [8–10]. However, despite international initiatives aimed at strengthening the PHC system—such as the Primary Care Assessment Tool (which emphasizes first contact, comprehensiveness, coordination, and continuity) [11] and the Primary Health Care Performance Initiative (focusing on system-level determinants, service delivery, and outcomes) [12]—service delivery often remains oriented toward treating illness rather than preventing it, and coordination between public health and clinical services remains weak [13]. In response, integrated care has been introduced as a comprehensive approach, defined as a “coordinated and person-centred approach that brings together different services and levels of care to meet people’s needs across the life course” [14]. This model of care is advocated to address service fragmentation and under-resourced primary care through the provision of continuous and coordinated care [13–15].

Several global models also provide multidimensional understandings of integrated care. The WHO Framework on Integrated People-Centred Health Services (IPCHS) proposes strategies such as community engagement, inter-sectoral coordination, and supportive governance to align services around people’ s needs [16]. Valentijn’ s Rainbow Model conceptualizes integration across multiple dimensions (e.g., clinical, professional, organizational, functional, and normative), which links micro- and macro-level health system interactions [17]. Practical assessment tools, such as the SCIROCCO Maturity Assessment Tool, have been developed to evaluate the maturity of integrated care implementation [18]. Within the PHC system, integrated care facilitates the coordination of preventive and clinical services and is crucial in effectively preventing and managing NCDs [19].

While these international models and tools are widely used, the WHO emphasizes the need for context-specific indicators to manage NCDs based on local social, economic, and political conditions [20]. In China, the urgency for such a system is highlighted by projections indicating that NCD-related deaths could reach 93% of all deaths by 2050 [21], imposing a massive economic burden [22]. This situation is further compounded by China’s large, rapidly aging population [23, 24].

However, developing evaluation indicators in China presents unique challenges. PHC institutions in China are responsible for delivering two distinct types of services: essential public health services (primarily preventive care provided by public health professionals), and essential clinical services (focused on medical treatment and delivered by clinicians) [25, 26]. Unlike countries with a single payer system—whether tax-based (e.g., the UK, Australia, Canada) or insurance-based (e.g., the US, Germany, Japan)—that funds both preventive and clinical services through a unified mechanism [27, 28], China adopts a dual financing model. Although a hybrid payment approach may help advance primary care delivery [29], it also presents challenges in delineating institutional responsibilities and ensuring coherent performance accountability. Specifically, essential public health services are funded by government budgets on a per capita basis, whereas clinical services are primarily reimbursed by health insurance as actual expenses. This has led to an institutional bias toward curative care to generate income, weakening the role of prevention in NCD control. Moreover, this dual-financing mechanism complicates institutional coordination and increases structural fragmentation between clinical and public health services, each with its own set of performance indicators. This leads to PHC providers prioritizing meeting the specific requirements of each service independently rather than delivering integrated care [30].

This study aimed to develop a set of core indicators to evaluate the capacity of integrated NCD services in China’s PHC system. Using Donabedian’s structure–process–outcome (SPO) framework [31, 32], healthcare quality was assessed by examining how structural factors (e.g., organizational structure, policies, facilities) influence processes (e.g., service delivery and service utilization) and outcomes, including disease control and costs [33, 34]. Compared with other conceptual models, such as the IPCHS and Valentijn’s Rainbow Model, which offer valuable theoretical insights into how integrated care should function at multiple levels of the health system but are primarily designed for theoretical or policy analysis, the SPO framework offers greater operational applicability for developing and applying evaluation indicators in healthcare practice. Building on this perspective, our indicator system aims to reform China’s fragmented PHC evaluation model and support a more integrated, prevention-oriented PHC service delivery system, serving as a valuable reference for other countries with similar PHC systems.

Methods

Study design

Grounded in the SPO framework, this study employed a modified Delphi method combined with the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) to develop an integrated evaluation indicator system for NCD management in China’s PHC institutions. The selection of the SPO framework was grounded in its applicability to evaluating healthcare quality at the institutional level.

This study was conducted in two phases: the Delphi process and the AHP phase. The Delphi method is a structured approach for collecting expert opinions and achieving consensus [35]. The modified Delphi approach incorporated preparation, expert selection, questionnaire distribution, and feedback collection over two rounds. The subsequent AHP phase was used to determine the weights of the selected indicators identified during the Delphi phase (Fig. 1). Notably, although Chinese PHC institutions manage communicable and non-communicable diseases, this study focused on integrating services for NCDs due to their distinct long-term care challenges compared to infectious diseases.

Development of the primary indicator pool

A literature review and internal discussion were conducted to establish the initial indicator pool. Relevant literature from four databases (Web of Science, PubMed, CNKI, and WANFANG) between December 2019 and December 2024 was reviewed (see Supplemental Appendix 1). Studies were selected based on the following criteria: (1) integration of clinical and preventive services, (2) PHC institutions, (3) not focused on infectious diseases, (4) Chinese or English, (5) accessible full texts, and (6) original research articles. Guided by the SPO framework, the primary indicator pool for the Delphi questionnaire was developed, comprising three first-level indicators, 18 s-level indicators, and 72 third-level indicators. Specifically, the first-level indicators were Structure, Process, and Outcome, with the second- and third-level indicators serving as their respective sub-categories.

Selection of the expert panel

The authors recruited a panel of experts through their network, comprising three types of participants: (1) full-time healthcare personnel from PHC institutions (e.g., family doctors, public health physicians, or heads of medical departments); (2) experts with doctoral degrees from universities or research institutions specializing in PHC quality, health policy, or health services research; and (3) experts with experience in PHC policy-making or involvement in policy implementation, including staff from China’s National/Regional Health Commissions or the Centers for Disease Control (CDC). All experts had at least a bachelor’s degree and over five years of relevant professional experience. To ensure data source diversity [36], 22 experts were recruited from three provinces/municipalities in China (i.e., Shanghai, Shandong, and Zhejiang), with no more than two experts from the same institution. Given the purposes and scope of this study, patients and members of the public were not directly involved.

Data collection

A two-round Delphi survey was conducted online and offline from January to May 2025 (see the questionnaires in Supplemental Appendices 2–3). The questionnaire content was identical across the two modes of administration. For online participation, the survey was distributed via Wenjuanxing (an online survey platform). For offline participation, printed questionnaires were provided during in-person expert meetings and subsequently collected on site. This mixed approach facilitated participation among experts with different work settings and ensured a higher response rate.

In each round, experts scored the importance and feasibility of each third- level indicator on a five-point Likert scale (1 = very unimportant to 5 = very important; 1 = very unfeasible to 5 = very feasible). More specifically, experts were asked “How important is this indicator for evaluating the integration and quality of NCD management in primary care?” and “How feasible would it be for primary care practices to implement or measure this indicator in real-world settings?” in each round. They also provided self-assessments of their decision-making basis and content familiarity. At the end of each round, experts could suggest modifications to the indicators. Follow-up clarifications and group discussions were held to modify the indicators as required. Indicators were considered to have reached consensus if they met the inclusion criteria, defined as a mean score ≥ 4.0, a coefficient of variation (CV) < 0.25, and a full-score percentage ≥ 20% [37]. The primary basis for inclusion or exclusion was the importance score, while feasibility scores served as supplementary references to understand the implementation challenges for important yet less feasible indicators.

Although screening activities were commonly classified as process measure in the SPO framework (Donabedian’s model), we treated screening and check-up participation as coverage outcomes (e.g., blood pressure/glucose/lipid screening rate, and health check-up rate of residents) to show population-level coverage results.

Data analysis

The expert consultations were evaluated based on participation, authority, and coordination. Expert participation was assessed through the questionnaire response rate, with 70% indicating adequate engagement [37]. Expert authority was calculated using the authority coefficient (Cr), derived as the mean of the familiarity coefficient (Cs) and judgment coefficient (Ca) (Cr = (Cs + Ca)/2). A Cr value of ≥ 0.7 was considered acceptable, with higher values indicating greater expert authority (see Supplemental Appendix 4). Expert coordination was measured using Kendall’s W coefficient, ranging from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater consensus reliability. Kendall’ s W and expert coordination were applied only during the Delphi phase to assess consensus on the importance and feasibility ratings before proceeding to the weighting process.

Following the Delphi rounds, the AHP was employed using Yaahp 12.12 software to calculate the weights of the first- and second-level indicators, while the third-level indicator weights were determined using the percentage method [35, 38]. A pairwise comparison judgment matrix was developed based on the outcomes of the final Delphi rounds. Saaty’s nine-point scale [39] was utilized to evaluate the relative importance of each indicator at the first and second levels. Subsequently, a consistency test was performed, with a consistency ratio (CR) of < 0.1 indicating satisfactory consistency within the judgment matrix.

In the second round of the Delphi process, no first- or second-level indicators were deleted or added. However, one third-level indicator was further modified (see Supplemental Appendix 6). Ultimately, an evaluation indicator system for integrating clinical care and preventive services in PHC institutions was established and reached consensus after two Delphi rounds, encompassing 3 first-level, 17 s-level, and 78 third-level indicators. These consensus indicators were subsequently used in the AHP analysis to determine their relative weights.

Based on the SPO framework, the final indicator system contained 34 structural, 15 process, and 29 outcome indicators with 0.2807, 0.2708, and 0.4485 weights, respectively. Each matrix had a CR < 0.1, indicating satisfactory consistency (Tables 4, 5 and 6).

Discussion

To address resource misallocation and inefficiencies associated with separating clinical care and public health services, this study developed an indicator system to assess their integration in China’s PHC institutions to improve NCD prevention and control. Within the first-level indicator categories, the outcome indicators were assigned the most weight, followed by the structure and process indicators. This prioritization differs from current evaluation practices in China, which focus primarily on the quantity of clinical and public health services [5]. Experts emphasized that outcome indicators (e.g., health improvements and economic benefits) should be the core objectives, reflecting a broader global shift toward outcome-oriented healthcare systems, consistent with the development of integrated healthcare system in other developed countries [40].

Among the structure indicators, the highest weights were assigned to the performance and compensation management models, underscoring their pivotal roles in facilitating service integration within China’s PHC institutions. The prevailing compensation mechanism in China (i.e., fiscal subsidies and institutions’ clinical service income) has led to preventive services being neglected, contributing to an increased chronic disease burden and healthcare costs [41]. Internationally, there are various models for paying integrated care costs that can provide insights for China to address this issue. For example, capitation-based payment models in the US, such as the Kaiser Permanente system, or the shared savings approach under Gesundes Kinzigtal in Germany, offer financial incentives for prevention-oriented service delivery [42, 43]. In the Netherlands, the introduction of bundled payments for chronic diseases in 2010 sought to foster proactive, coordinated care tailored to specific disease types while controlling healthcare expenditure growth [44]. Although evidence of the long-term effectiveness of these measures is still emerging, particularly given the delayed benefits of preventive interventions, they nonetheless provide valuable insights for reforming compensation strategies in China’s PHC system.

Performance evaluation was another crucial structure indicator for promoting service integration in PHC institutions and motivating providers [45]. For example, a study pointed out that the basic salary of PHC personnel positively correlates with worsened hypertension control outcomes, whereas performance-based pay can significantly enhance hypertension control effects [46]. Nevertheless, there are two main challenges within the performance system: the proportion of preventive services within the salary assessment framework for PHC staff remains relatively low, and the evaluation of public health services tends to prioritize quantitative measures over health outcomes [5, 47]. Therefore, a recent Chinese study suggested integrating financing streams for preventive and clinical services, strengthening local accountability for health outcomes, and shifting from activity-based to outcome- and value-based performance frameworks to promote service integration and quality improvement within China’s PHC system [48].

Although informatization received relatively low weight in this study, existing research indicates that information systems are essential for monitoring and evaluating the implementation of integration initiatives [49, 50]. The low weight in this indicator may be attributed to the fact that China is currently promoting the establishment of the innovative information platforms, such as the “Integrated Collaborative Management System for Non-communicable Diseases” in Ningbo City [51]. This system provides a chance to connect public health authorities, hospitals, and primary care, which enables real-time monitoring of noncommunicable disease trends, integrates patient management, and ensures surveillance aligns with policy planning [51]. With such centralized systems, informatization may be viewed as a less urgent concern at the institutional level. By contrast, some countries still used disease registries and isolated hospital information systems, which may hinder data interoperability across different levels of healthcare [52]. Although global frameworks such as the WHO Global Monitoring Framework for NCDs provide standardized indicators, implementing these often faces challenges in data availability, linkage, and quality at the country level [53]. China’ s experience provides a suggestion that building a unified digital infrastructure can strengthen horizontal integration between clinical and public health services and improve the responsiveness of NCD policy and management.

Among the process indicators, service awareness and behavior were assigned the highest weights, underscoring their importance in NCD management in PHC. However, many studies point out that family doctor teams in China have not fully fulfilled their role as “gatekeepers” within the healthcare system, partly due to limitations in service capacity and insufficient professional training [54, 55]. Therefore, enhancing training for PHC personnel is important, including improving their clinical and communication skills, health cost management skills, and strategies to promote proactive health behaviors [56, 57]. Meanwhile, the current study detected a relatively low relational continuity score among the process indicators. This contradicted previous findings that long-term patient relationships with healthcare providers or family doctor teams significantly improve trust in primary care services [58–60]. One possible reason is the severe shortage of family doctors in China, which limits their ability to build continuous care relationships with their patients. Specifically, as of 2023, there were only 3.99 family doctors per 10,000 residents, far behind developed countries such as the US and the UK [61]. Improving this situation can create a more stable and sustainable NCD management model that reduces dependence on higher-level hospitals and emergency services, ultimately decreasing healthcare costs [62, 63].

Among the outcome indicators, those related to health (i.e., indicators of NCD intervention, NCD intervention outcomes, and NCD burden) received the highest weight. This underscores the importance of health-related outcomes in evaluating the PHC quality. Similarly, one previous study classified primary care outputs into healthcare outputs, health outcomes, and cost outcomes [64]. Moreover, another study suggested that primary care improves health by increasing access to services, enhancing the quality of care, focusing on prevention and early management of health issues, and reducing reliance on specialized care [65].

Indicators related to economic effects, such as reductions in direct patient out-of-pocket expenses and measures to mitigate the upward trajectory of regional health expenditures, are also considered significant. Improvements in health outcomes are closely related to long-term cost savings. Preventive care and early interventions can reduce the demand for avoidable hospitalizations, emergency services, and expensive specialist care, reducing patient expenditures and health system costs [47, 66–68]. For example, preventable hospital treatments due to inadequate primary care for conditions like diabetes, hypertension, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease account for 12% of emergency visits in the US, 15% in the UK, and 25% in Canada [69]. These findings highlight the importance of strengthening integrated prevention-oriented primary care to improve outcomes and manage rising healthcare costs.

In this study, the feasibility scores of the indicators were generally lower than their importance scores. This discrepancy primarily arises from the lack of widespread implementation of horizontally integrated healthcare (i.e., coordination and integration of clinical care and public health services at the primary care level) within China’s PHC system. Although China has advanced vertical integration by establishing medical alliances (medical consortia in urban areas and county-level medical communities) to integrate PHC institutions with secondary and tertiary hospitals and optimize resource allocation, these reforms have primarily targeted vertical rather than horizontal integration. Thus, although policy-level efforts have been made to promote horizontal integration, progress remains limited [70]. For example, as early as 2018, the National Health Commission formally articulated in policy documents that family doctors should provide residents with integrated clinical and public health services. However, implementation has been slow due to the separation of monitoring, performance evaluation, and management systems [5]. The lack of unified economic incentives and coordination mechanisms between clinical and preventive services at the primary care level is also a significant barrier. This may contribute to the relatively low feasibility scores and less optimistic attitudes toward integration prospects.

Strengths and limitations

This study makes theoretical and practical contributions to the field of health services research. Theoretically, it extends Donabedian’s SPO model to evaluate the integration in PHC institutions for the prevention and management of NCDs. While many healthcare evaluation models emphasize patient perceptions (e.g., SERVQUAL) or consider service provider and patient perspectives (e.g., JCI) [71, 72], this study focuses primarily on the service provider’s perspective utilizing the SPO model. Additionally, this study offers a novel conceptual framework for integrating clinical care and public health services, particularly within Chinese PHC systems that comprise diverse fund sources. Moreover, the developed indicator system aligns conceptually with two global frameworks of integrated care: Valentijn’s Rainbow Model and the WHO IPCHS framework. Structural, process, and outcome indicators correspond to macro-, meso-, and micro-level dimensions of integration in the Rainbow Model and to governance, coordination, and people-centred outcomes in the IPCHS framework (see Appendices 7–8). This alignment demonstrates that the indicator system not only measures institutional efficiency but also supports multi-level integration across the PHC continuum.

Practically, this study emphasizes assessing the capacity of primary healthcare systems for delivering integrated NCD services, rather than individual care pathways or specialty-specific outcomes. Therefore, our developed indicators provide policymakers and administrators with a tool for evaluating the effectiveness of integrated services and implementing targeted improvements. Among these three-level indicator system in our developed system, the outcome indicators received the highest importance. This result is consistent with findings from previous Delphi-analytic hierarchy process studies that emphasized outcome-oriented evaluation in PHC and integrated care settings [73, 74]. By aligning with similar findings from previous research, our indicator system supports the international shift toward results-focused/outcomes-oriented and evidence-based evaluation frameworks in integrated primary care. Furthermore, incorporating experts from academic, managerial, and clinical backgrounds ensured diverse perspectives, enhancing the comprehensiveness and applicability of the evaluation indicator system.

This study also has several limitations. First, most invited experts were from eastern China, although their varied affiliations helped mitigate potential selection bias. Second, using importance as an inclusion criterion led to the incorporation of indicators with relatively low feasibility, which may only be partially adopted or optimized in practice across different regions. Third, the generalizability of the indicator system might be restricted due to varying social contexts and health systems. Nevertheless, this study provides a foundational reference for similar research in other countries. Fourth, since this study mainly focused on institutional- and system-level integration, indicators related to gender, equity or social determinants of health were not examined. Future research may consider developing sex-disaggregated and social determinant-sensitive indicators to enhance inclusiveness and better reflect population diversity. Additionally, although some indicators reflected people-centred aspects in this study, patients’ experiences were not explicitly addressed, which was key element in integrated care. Future research may incorporate patient-reported outcome measures to strengthen the patient perspective within the evaluation framework. Finally, the basis for measuring composite indicators requires further clarification. Empirical studies are needed to refine and validate the system, improving its overall effectiveness and applicability.

Conclusions

By conducting a modified Delphi-AHP study, we established an indicator system for evaluating clinical–preventive integration for NCD management in Chinese PHC institutions, comprising 34 structural, 15 process, and 29 outcome indicators. Structural indicators emphasize the institutional, organizational, and policy foundations, such as resource availability, leadership mechanisms, financing and performance models, and informatization construction. Process indicators reflect the behavioural and operational dimensions of service integration, and also highlight the relationship continuity and staff awareness and motivation to actively provide preventive care and manage healthcare costs. The outcome indicators evaluate the population- and system-level results of integration, including service coverage, health improvement, cost efficiency, and satisfaction. Among them, AHP weighting prioritized the outcome indicators over the structure and process indicators.

This system offers guidance for healthcare policymakers and administrators to assess and improve the quality of PHC services. It also provides insights for other countries, particularly low- and middle-income ones where primary care systems remain underdeveloped and NCD preventive services are still at an early stage. Future studies should refine the indicator set by incorporating gender-sensitive, equity-promoting, and patient-engaged measures to enhance inclusiveness and people-centeredness, and evaluate its feasibility and applicability in real-world PHC contexts to bridge the gap between conceptual design and practical implementation.
