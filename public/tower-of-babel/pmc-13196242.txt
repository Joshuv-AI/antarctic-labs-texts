Associated factors with malnutrition among children under-5 years old in Somaliland: evidence from Somaliland health and demographic survey of 2020
Authors: Sakariye Mohamud Ibrahim, Mirriam Ndanu Mutuku, Asma Abdirahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalnutrition in children under five years is a major public health issue in developing countries, mostly in Africa and Asia regions, contributing substantially to child mortality. This study aimed to assess the associated factors of malnutrition among under five year’s old children, utilizing data from the Somaliland Demographic and health survey.MethodsThe data from this study is sourced secondary data from the Somaliland Demographic and Health Survey, (SDHS) 2020. The dependent variable is malnutrition (stunting. Wasting, and underweight). Descriptive statistics were generated followed by multivariable logistic regression for each malnutrition outcome.ResultsThe prevalence of stunting, wasting, and underweight was reported as 24.99% for stunting, 21.08% for wasting, and 14.52% for underweight. Regional disparities were observed, with children in Sool (AOR = 6.447, p = 0.017), and Sanaag (AOR = 4.560, p = 0.050) having higher odds of stunting compared to Awdal. Rural residence was significantly associated with increased odds of stunting (AOR = 1.656, p = 0.001). Lack of water availability at the handwashing point (AOR = 0.585, P = 0.022) and use of ash or mud instead of soap (AOR = 2.841, p = 0.005) were significantly linked to wasting. Households were the head was aged above 74 showed increased odds of stunting (AOR = 2.711, p = 0.014).ConclusionMalnutrition among under-five children in Somaliland remains a significant public health issue. Regional disparities, residence type, and WASH-related factors were associated with malnutrition outcomes. Interventions should focus on high-burden regions and improving WASH conditions.

Background

Malnutrition in children under five years is a major public health issue in developing countries, mostly in Africa and Asia regions and it is estimated that malnutrition has taken a part to 54% of mortality among children under five [1]. Malnutrition is a condition results from eating food that contains either inadequate or too excess, carbohydrates, proteins, minerals and vitamins [2]. Children’s increased dietary demands throughout their formative years make them more susceptible to macronutrient and micronutrient deficiency [3]. Malnourished children have weakened immunity and face prolonged developmental disruptions and possess mortality risk that is 5 to 20 times higher than that of well-nourished children [4]. Malnutrition results from a scarcity of good nutrition, triggered by not having sufficient food to consume, or not eating enough of the right things [5].

Childhood malnutrition of every form, encompassing undernutrition; wasting, stunting and underweight [6]. According to UNICEF Stunting refers to a child who is too short for his or her age [7]. Wasting, defined as low weight-for-height, is the most visible and lethal type of malnutrition [8]. According to [9], wasting, and particularly severe acute malnutrition, in young children is a medical emergency with high risk of death and negative consequences for human development, child wasting remains a problem in low- and middle-income countries, affecting almost 50 million (7·3%) children under five at any given time.

Children with low weight-for-age are known as underweight, a child who is underweight may be stunted, wasted or both [10]. Underweight is a key indicator of malnutrition in children and results in long term effects such as abnormalities in physical and mental health, behavioral difficulties, and poor educational achievement [11]. In developing nations, underweight is more prevalent than overweight and obesity, although it is anticipated that they will soon surpass the obesity rate in rich nations [12].

Globally the prevalence of stunting among under 5 years old children is 17.9% [13]. The average prevalence of stunting across ten selected sub Saharan African countries analyzed was 11.6%, their stunting rates were 24.8% in Burundi, 13.7%, 12.2% in Ethiopia, 11.6% in Zambia, 10.5% in Malawi, 10.3% in Sierra Leone, 10% in Mali, 9.9% in South Africa, 8.1% in Zimbabwe, and 4.8% in Senegal [14].

Malnutrition under five children in Somaliland remains one of the public health concerns [15]. Based on analysis of SDHS 2020 data, the prevalence of stunting, wasting, and underweight was 25.0%, 21.1%, and 14.5%, respectively. This study was focused the associated factors of malnutrition among under five years old children.

Somaliland is characterized by diverse livelihoods, including urban, rural, and a substantial nomadic and semi-nomadic population, which can influence access to health and nutrition services. These population groups often experience varying levels of food security, healthcare access, and environmental conditions. In particular, nomadic populations may face challenges related to mobility and limited access to health facilities. Additionally, regional disparities exist in infrastructure, water and sanitation services, and economic opportunities, which may contribute to differences in nutritional outcomes among children under five years of age.

Socioeconomic status states to the absolute or relative levels of economic resources, power, and prestige closely associated with wealth of an individual, community, or country, socioeconomic status is a multidimensional construct covering numerous factors, such as income, education, employment status, and other factors [16]. A study conducted from Nepal demonstrated a clear relationship between socioeconomic status and malnutrition particularly stunting, and the prevalence of stunting was inversely related to socioeconomic status, with 21% of individuals from low socioeconomic status, 17% from middle and 13% from lower socioeconomic status experiencing stunting [17]. Another study from Cameroon states that the prevalence of underweight (45.50%) as well wasting (69.20%) were higher in children from mothers with lower education level compared to those from mothers with higher education level [18]. Other study from Pakistan also reported that the maternal education indicators remain among the strongest predictors. Children of mothers with no education had 1.8 times greater odds of stunting (OR = 1.81, 95% CI: 1.66–1.98); for those with below-secondary education, the risk was 1.4 times greater (OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.31–1.55) [19]. After the screening, 29 research papers were included in the review in Africa, it is found that nutrition-sensitive livestock interventions have a significant positive impact on the eating of animal-source foods for children < 5 years (OR = 5.39; 95% CI: 4.43–6.56) and on the likelihood of meeting minimum dietary diversity (OR = 1.89; 95% CI: 1.51–2.37), additionally, the impact of livestock interventions on stunting, wasting, and being underweight varied depending on the type of intervention and duration of the program/intervention implementation [20].

According to study conducted from Ethiopia, there are a notable age related disparities in malnutrition, with older children more likely stunting and underweight compared to young ones, children aged 13–39 months have notably higher probability of being stunted or underweight than those aged 0–12 months [21]. Another study conducted from Ethiopia showed that region, place of residence, and age of the child were significantly associated with stunting and wasting [4]. It has been found that children living in urban areas are taller than other children their age and there is usually a better facility of healthcare in cities and towns than in the countryside, in addition the study found that along with other socio-economic factors, the effect of spatial difference, especially the rural–urban difference, was significant regarding health status [22]. In Rwanda, it is has been reported that the children from households where the head was above 60 years old were more likely to exhibit stunting AOR = 4.809; 95% CI (1.513, 15.283) [23]. In terms of sex of household head, in Nigeria, it is found that the probability of children in male-headed households falling into the stunted and underweight category is reduced by 3.86%, this is an indication that stunted and underweight children are less likely to be in male-headed households relative to the households headed by females [24]. In the other hand, there is still a gender gap in under-nutrition, according to a 2024 cross-sectional study, the prevalence of stunting was 38% among boys and 31% among girls (p < 0.05), suggesting that male children are at a significantly higher risk. The authors speculate that this discrepancy may be caused by biological factors (such as slower growth velocity and weakened immunity) as well as potential cultural feeding preferences [25].

Access to water is sufficient, affordable and physically accessible coverage with safe water of acceptable quality, access to sanitation refers to the availability and use of facilities or services to dispose of urine and feces and hygiene refers to conditions and practices at the individual or community level to prevent infection through contamination [26]. Analysis of survey from 640 Indian districts indicates that districts having the higher share of undernourished children is coming from the particular regions like central, east and west part of the country, on an average about only 35% of household in a district having the access of safe drinking water and 42% of household in a district exposed to open defecation, thus the study found the inverse relationship between district’s water, sanitation, and hygiene facilities development with childhood stunting and underweight [27]. In Myanmar it is reported that the likelihood of stunting was considerably higher for children whose families had to take 1 to 15 min to gather water (AOR = 2.07, 95% CI: 1.46 to 2.94; p-value < 0.01) than for children whose families did not have to take time for water collection. The odds of stunting children were also 55% higher for children whose families had to wait more than 15 to 60 min to obtain water than for reference children (AOR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.08 to 2.23; p-value 0.02) [28]. This community-based study in Karachi, Pakistan, including more than 1,000 children under five, found that the use of pour-flush latrines was associated with 4.41 times greater chances of malnutrition (including stunting, wasting, or underweight) than those with superior sanitation (AOR = 4.41, 95% CI: 2.67–7.30) [29]. A 2020 study that used data from the Nepal Demographic and Health Survey found that children in households with both soap and water available at the hand-washing station had significantly higher weight-for-height (WHZ) (β = 0.13; 95% CI: 0.01–0.24; p < 0.05) and weight-for-age (WAZ) z-scores (β = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.04–0.25; p < 0.01), indicating a meaningfully positive impact on the nutritional status of children [30].

Methodology

Research area

The study was conducted in Somaliland, located in East Africa. The geographical location of Somaliland is bordered by Djibouti at the northwest, Ethiopia at the southwest, Somalia at the east and the Gulf of Aden to the north. The country is not officially recognized by the United Nations (UN).

Data source

The data from this study is sourced secondary data from the Somaliland Demographic and Health Survey, (SDHS) 2020, a nationally representative data collected by the Somali government in collaboration with development partners. The SDHS collected extensive information on health and demographic characteristics across all regions in Somaliland, ensuring comprehensive geographic coverage. The survey included urban, rural, and nomadic residence areas, capturing resident variations that are crucial for understanding the disparities in child malnutrition. This study particularly utilized key variables such as anthropometric measurements, socioeconomic status and socio-demographic characteristics. Data were collected between August 2018 and December 2019 as part of the SDHS [15].

Conceptual framework

Study variables

The dependent variable of this study was the malnutrition under among under five children. The SDHS collected this through anthropometric measurements, stunting, wasting, and underweight was the indicators.

Region of residence, the type of residence, age of household head, sex of child, sex of household head, wealth quintile, attended school (Maternal education), highest level of education, livestock ownership, source of drinking water, source of non-drinking water, time taken to fetch water by foot from drinking water source, toilet facility, presence of soap, detergent or any other cleansing agent at the place for hand wash.

Operational definition of outcome variables

Children’s nutritional status was determined using the WHO 2006 Child Growth Standards. Stunting was defined as height-for-age z-score (HAZ) < − 2 standard deviations (SD), wasting as weight-for-height z-score (WHZ) < − 2 SD, and underweight as weight-for-age z-score (WAZ) < − 2 SD. For logistic regression analysis, each outcome variable was recoded as a binary variable (0 = not malnourished, 1 = malnourished).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Children aged 0–59 months with valid anthropometric measurements were included in the analysis. Cases with missing or implausible anthropometric values were excluded.

Sample and sampling

Sample size

The sample was derived from SDHS 2020 dataset, which contained a nationally representative sample households. A subset of children under five years old, consisting of 2,637 formed the basis of analysis.

Sample techniques

This study used secondary data from SDHS of 2020. The survey is a nationally representative household survey designed to collect information on population health and demographic characteristics. The survey employed a stratified cluster sampling design with sampling weights to ensure national representativeness. Children under five years with complete anthropometric measurements and relevant explanatory variables were included in the analysis.

Data analysis

This study utilized secondary data from the Somaliland Demographic and Health Survey of 2020, which were cleaned to extract socio-economic, socio-demographic, WASH (Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene), and nutrition-related variables which their data in complete in the dataset. Data analysis was conducted using Stata, and the results are presented in tabular form. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify significant predictors of malnutrition among children under five, while controlling for confounders. All analyses were conductedusing Stata’s svy suite to account for stratification, clustering, and sampling weights. Observations with missing values were excluded using complete-case analysis.

Socio-demographic characteristics

The table above (Table 1) indicates the socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents. The sample for this analysis consists of a total of 2,637 children under five years of age, derived from SDHS 2020 data set. A regional breakdown indicates that the highest proportion of children resides in Sanaag, accounting 27.61% (n = 728), followed by Sool with 26.24% (n = 692). Socioeconomic status

Water, sanitation, and hygiene WASH status

The Table 3 gives valuable insights in water, sanitation, and hygiene status of the respondents. In terms of source of drinking water, it was reported that 66.02% (n = 1,741) relied on unimproved sources of drinking water, while 33% (n = 896) accessed improved sources. For toilet facility, 75.39% (n = 1,988) of the households used unimproved toilet facilities, while only 24.61% (n = 649) had access to improved toilets. With regard to time taken to fetch water by foot from the drinking water source, 23.09% (n = 609) of the households had water on premises, 39.70% (n = 1,047) took less than 30 min, 33.07% (n = 872) took 30 min or longer, and 4.13% (n = 109) were don’t know or missing responses.

In terms of the type of water washing point, 88.70% (n = 2,339) used unimproved washing points, and only 11.30% (n = 298) used improved points. Concerning water availability at the handwashing place, 65.83% (n = 1,736) reported no water available, while 34.17% (n = 901) reported that water was available. Lastly, in terms of cleansing agents used at handwashing places, 72.39% (n = 1,909) reported having none, 15.24% (n = 402) used soap or detergent, and 12.36% (n = 326) used ash, mud, or sand.

Nutritional status among under-5 children

The Table 4 shows clear differences in nutritional status among under-5 children across urban, rural, and nomadic residence areas in Somaliland. Stunting affects 29.6% (n = 161) urban, 33.9% (n = 214) rural, and 19.4% (n = 284) nomadic children. This suggests that chronic malnutrition is more common in rural areas.

Wasting was highest among nomadic children at 23.3% (n = 340) compared to 19.5% (n = 123) in rural and 17.1% (n = 93) in urban areas. This reflects acute malnutrition is common in nomadic people. For underweight, 18 (n = 119) rural, 15.3% (n = 83), and 12.4% (n = 184) nomadic children were affected. Rural children had the highest burden.

In summary rural children had highest burden of chronic and under-nutrition, while nomadic are more vulnerable to acute form of malnutrition. Urban children slightly better but still face stunting. Targeted interventions are needed for each population group.

Summary of multivariable logistic regression (Socio-demographic factors)

The regression analysis (Table 5) revealed a significant association between region of residence and stunting. Children living in Sool (AOR = 6.447, p = 0.017) and Sanaag (AOR = 4.560, p = 0.050) were significantly more likely to be stunted compared to Awdal. However, no region showed a statistically significant associationwith wasting or underweight. In terms of residential setting, children residing in rural areas had significantly higher odds of stunting compared to their counterparts (AOR = 1.656, p = 0.016), while nomadic did not yield any significant associations. The age of the household head was also associated with malnutrition. Specifically, children from households headed by individuals aged above 74 years had increased odds of stunting (AOR = 2.711, p = 0.014). Child age in months was not significantly associated with stunting, wasting, or underweight in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. Neither sex of household head nor the sex of child demonstrated any significant association with stunting, wasting, or underweight.

Summary of multivariable logistic regression (Socio-economic factors)

Among the socio-economic variables, wealth quintile showed significant association with wasting. Children from households in the lowest wealth quintile had substantially higher odds of being wasted (AOR = 4.457, p = 0.001) compared to those in the highest quintile. Educational status of the household was significantly associated with stunting and wasting; children from households where no mothers had attended school had significantly lower odds of stunting (AOR = 0.827, p = 0.001), while they had significantly higher odds of wasting (AOR = 1.41, p = 0.002). Ownership of livestock did not show a significant association with any form malnutrition.

Summary of multivariable logistic regression (WASH factors)

Analysis of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) factors revealed that the time required to fetch drinking water had a significant with malnutrition; households that required 30 min or longer to fetch water had significantly higher odds of both wasting (AOR = 2.074, p = 0.013) and underweight (AOR = 2.274, p = 0.006), relative to those with water on premises. Interestingly, the availability of water at the hand washing locations was associated reduced odds of wasting (AOR = 0.585, p = 0.022). Regarding the cleansing agents, the use of ash, mud, or sand for hand washing had significantly higher odds of wasting (AOR = 2.841, p = 0.005) when compared to using soap or detergent. Other WASH-related variables, including source of drinking water, toilet facilities, and type of water washing point, were not significantly associated with any form of malnutrition.

Summary of findings

The study analyzed data from 2,637 under-five children in Somaliland to identify factors associated with stunting, wasting, and underweight. Prevalence results showed that stunting affected 33.9% of rural children, 29.6% of urban children, and 19.4% of nomadic children, indicating a higher burden of chronic malnutrition in rural areas. Wasting was most common among nomadic children (23.3%), followed by rural (19.5%) and urban children (17.1%), reflecting acute malnutrition challenges in mobile populations. Underweight prevalence was also highest in rural areas (18.9%), compared to 15.3% in urban and 12.4% in nomadic settings.

Multivariable logistic regression revealed that stunting was significantly associated with region of residence, rural setting, and households headed by individuals aged over 74 years. Wasting was significantly associated with the lowest wealth quintile and households where no member had attended school. Underweight did not show statistically significant associations with most socio-demographic or socioeconomic variables.

Regarding WASH indicators, time taken to fetch water of 30 min or more was significantly associated with increased odds of both wasting and underweight. Additionally, absence of water at hand washing locations and use of ash, mud, or sand as cleansing agents were significantly linked to higher odds of wasting. No significant associations were found between malnutrition and livestock ownership, sex of the child, sex of the household head, source of drinking water, toilet type, or washing point type.

Discussion

The study revealed that the children living Sool and Sanaag were significantly more likely to be stunted compared to those Awdal. Specially, children from Sool were over 6 times more likely to be stunted, while those Sanaag were 4.5 times more likely. The type of residence was found to have a significant correlation with stunting. Stunting was more common in rural children than in urban ones (AOR = 1.656; p = 0.016). This aligns with a study from Ethiopia, which reported rural children to be more vulnerable to malnutrition due to socioeconomic and infrastructural barriers [4]. It also aligns with the research conducted by Bharati et al. (2008), which indicated that children residing in urban environments were typically taller and healthier owing to enhanced access to healthcare services, sanitation, and infrastructure. However, children from nomadic households exhibited no significant differences in stunting or other indicators of malnutrition, potentially attributable to varied livelihood practices or disparities in access to food and health services not reflected in this dataset.

The households headed by individuals aged above 74 years had higher odds of child stunting (AOR = 2.711;, p = 0.014). this aligns with study from Rwanda, which found children from older household heads; above 60 years old were more likely to exhibit stunting AOR = 4.809; 95% CI (1.513, 15.283) [23].

The sex of the child was not significantly associated with stunting, wasting, or underweight in this study. Although female children had slightly lower odds across all three outcomes, none reached statistical significance (AOR = 1.202, 0.919, 0.863), stunting, wasting, and underweight respectively. This finding contrasts with cross-sectional study from Zambia, which found a significantly higher prevalence of stunting among boys 38% comparing to girls 31% [25].

This study found a strong link between socioeconomic factors and child malnutrition, especially wasting. Children in the lowest wealth quintile were more than four times more likely to be wasted than kids in the highest quintile (AOR = 4.457; p = 0.001). This finding corroborates a study conducted in Nepal, which indicated an inverse correlation between socioeconomic status and stunting, revealing prevalence rates of 21% among low-SES children in contrast to merely 13% among those from high socioeconomic status households [17].

Educational status of the household was also key determinant. Children from households where no attended school had significantly higher odds of wasting (AOR = 1.41; p = 0.002). This aligns with study from Cameroon, which reported that the children whose mothers had a primary education were at greater risk of developing underweight (AOR = 3.33, 95% CI: 0.34–4.34) and wasting (AOR = 11.35, 95% CI: 3.20–40.16) than those whose mothers had higher education level [18], and Pakistan where lack of maternal education significantly increased stunting risk (AOR = 1.81) [19].

On the other hand, livestock ownership did not show a significance association with any malnutrition outcome in this study. This contrasts with review from 29 studies from Africa, which found that nutrition-sensitive livestock interventions had significantly positive impact on the eating of animal-source foods for children < 5 years (AOR = 5.39; 95% CI: 4.43–6.56), furthermore, the impact of livestock interventions on stunting, wasting, and being underweight varied depending on the type of intervention and duration of the program/intervention implementation [20]. The lack of association in this study may be due to differences in livestock utilization, market access, or implementation integrated nutrition programs.

This study did not find a statistically significant link between the source of drinking water and any of the malnutrition outcomes, even though better sources of drinking water were thought to help with malnutrition. This finding is contrasts from a big study from India that found a clear opposite relationship between access to safe drinking water and under-nutrition. In the poorest-performing districts, only 35% of households had access to safe water, and these areas had higher rates of stunting and being underweight [27]. The difference could be due to differences in national infrastructure, water treatment policies, and cultural norms about how to handle water.

However, the time taken to fetch water showed a strong and statistically significant relationship. Children from households that spent 30 min or longer fetching water had significantly higher odds of being wasted (AOR = 2.074; p = 0.013) and underweight (AOR = 2.274; p = 0.006) compared to households with water available on premises. This aligns closely with study from Myanmar, which found that the children from households spend 1–15 min collecting water were more than twice as likely to be stunted (AOR = 2.07, 95% CI: 1.46 to 2.94; p < 0.01), and those spending 15–60 min had a 55% increased risk (AOR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.08 to 2.23; p-value 0.02) than for children whose families did not have to take time for water collection [28].

In this study, having improved toilet facility showed no significant association with any form of malnutrition. This contrasts with a study from Pakistan, where pour-flush latrines were linked to over four times higher odds of malnutrition in under-five children (AOR = 4.41; 95% CI: 2.67–7.30) [29]. Some counterintuitive associations observed in this study, such as the relationship between improved sanitation and malnutrition outcomes, may reflect underlying socioeconomic or environmental differences rather than direct causal effects.

Additionally, the likelihood of wasting was considerably decreased when water was available at hand washing stations (AOR = 0.585; p = 0.022). The Nepal Demographic, and Health Survey analysis (Shrestha et al., (2020), supports this finding by showing that children from households with both soap and water at hand washing locations had higher Weight-for-height z scores (WHZ) and Weight-for-age z scores (WAZ) scores. The significance of appropriate hygiene tools for nutritional outcomes was further demonstrated by the fact that children whose households used ash, mud, or sand as cleansing agents had over 2.8 times higher odds of wasting (AOR = 2.841; p = 0.005).

This study has several strengths, including nationally representative data, inclusion of nomadic populations, and the assessment of multiple nutrition outcomes. The differences observed across nomadic, rural, and urban populations should be interpreted with caution. These variations may not solely reflect differences in vulnerability, but could also be influenced by factors such as population mobility, differential access to health services, and potential measurement challenges during data collection.

Conclusion

This study examined the associated factors of malnutrition among children under five in Somaliland using data from the nationally representative Somaliland Health and Demographic Survey 2020. The results revealed that stunting, wasting, and underweight remain critical public health challenges, especially among children from rural and economically disadvantaged households. Key factors significantly associated with malnutrition included region of residence, type of residence, age of the household head, wealth status, educational level, and WASH-related factors such as time taken to fetch water and the presence of water and soap at handwashing locations. These findings highlight the multifactorial nature of child malnutrition and emphasize the need for integrated, multisectoral interventions targeting both immediate and underlying determinants. Findings related to nomadic populations should be interpreted with caution, as data collection in mobile settings may be affected by selection bias and challenges in obtaining accurate anthropometric measurements.

To address these challenges, the findings highlight the importance of strengthening maternal and caregiver education and improving access to water, sanitation, and hygiene services, particularly in rural areas and regions with a higher burden of malnutrition such as Sool and Sanaag. These results may help inform targeted public health and nutrition programs aimed at improving child nutritional outcomes in Somaliland. In addition, further research is recommended to explore factors not captured in the SDHS dataset, including dietary patterns, cultural feeding practices, and seasonal food insecurity.

Limitations

The cross-sectional nature of the SDHS 2020 data limits the ability to infer causal relationships between the examined variables and child malnutrition outcomes. In addition, the study relied on secondary survey data, which restricted the analysis to variables available in the dataset and may have left some relevant factors unmeasured, leading to potential residual confounding, also child age in months had some missing data, which may have affected the results. Although anthropometric data in SDHS surveys are collected using standardized procedures, measurement error cannot be completely excluded in large population-based surveys. Despite these limitations, the use of nationally representative SDHS data provides important evidence on the factors associated with child malnutrition in Somaliland.
