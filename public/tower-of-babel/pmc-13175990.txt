Ivonescimab and other anti-programmed death-1/ligand 1 (PD-(L)1)/vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) bispecific antibodies: progress and prospects in cancer therapy
Authors: Cunnan Dong, Xue Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn cancer therapy, the synergistic interaction between immunotherapy and anti-angiogenic therapy has been validated by both preclinical and clinical studies, and has laid the foundation for the development of anti-PD-(L)1/VEGF bispecific antibodies. In the HARMONi-2 trial, ivonescimab demonstrated superior efficacy over pembrolizumab for the first time. This milestone may open a new era of immunotherapy with bispecific antibodies replacing anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibodies. Subsequently, the HARMONi-6 trial confirmed that ivonescimab combined with chemotherapy also outperformed tislelizumab plus chemotherapy, adding further evidence. Currently, dozens of anti-PD-(L)1/VEGF bispecific antibodies with distinct molecular structures have entered clinical stages; and only ivonescimab has completed Phase 3 clinical trial validation in multiple clinical trials and obtained approval in China. A growing body of clinical evidence indicates that in immunotherapy-sensitive tumors (e.g. lung cancer), bispecific antibodies have demonstrated superior efficacy compared with monoclonal antibodies. Even in tumors traditionally refractory to immunotherapy (such as colorectal cancer and pancreatic cancer), bispecific antibodies exhibit promising therapeutic effects while maintaining a favorable safety profile. Nevertheless, several issues require further investigation, such as predictive biomarkers and resistance mechanisms. Future investigations surrounding this class of agents are highly anticipated, including anti-PD-(L)1/VEGF bispecific antibodies-based combination therapy in a broader range of tumor types, as well as the development of trispecific antibodies and bispecific antibody–drug conjugates.Keywords: anti-PD-(L)1/VEGF, bispecific antibody, ivonescimab, immunotherapy, cancer therapy

Introduction

Over the past decade, immune check point inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed the landscape of cancer treatment. This revolution began with the first-in-class approval of the Cytotoxic T‑lymphocyte‑associated protein 4 (CTLA­4) inhibitor ipilimumab for advanced melanoma, based on the pivotal Phase 3 trial that demonstrated a statistically significant overall-survival benefit in previously treated patients [1]. Subsequent landmark studies rapidly expanded the field: the Phase 3 CheckMate 067 trial established the superiority of nivolumab plus ipilimumab combination therapy over ipilimumab monotherapy in previously untreated advanced melanoma, with durable long-term survival outcomes [2]; the KEYNOTE-024 Phase 3 trial positioned pembrolizumab monotherapy as superior to platinum-based chemotherapy in the first-line setting for PD-L1 tumor proportion score ≥ 50% metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [3]; and the foundational work on mismatch-repair–deficient tumors led to the approval of pembrolizumab for MSI-H/dMMR solid tumors across multiple cancer types by Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [4]. Up to now, ICI-based therapies have revolutionized medical oncology, with applications across more than 25 tumor types and over 60 indications. Despite their potential to induce broad, deep and durable responses, ICI monotherapies are known to have a low objective response rate (ORR) of about 20%, which varies across tumor types and PD-L1 expression levels [5]. This underscores the unmet medical need to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of anti-PD-(L)1-based treatments. To address this challenge, combination therapies involving anti-PD-(L)1 agents with chemotherapy, anti-angiogenic drugs, or other immunotherapeutic agents have been extensively explored in both preclinical and clinical studies.

The development of bispecific antibodies that integrate two distinct therapeutic targets into a single agent has emerged as a promising strategy in cancer immunotherapy. Up to now, more than 10 bispecific antibodies have been approved for cancer treatment worldwide, spanning both hematologic cancers and selected solid tumors, supported by pivotal trials demonstrating improvements in ORR, progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS). Ivonescimab marks a notable milestone as the world’s first approved anti-PD-1/VEGF bispecific antibody. Developed by Akeso Biopharma, ivonescimab (AK112/SMT112) has a unique tetravalent structure and exhibits excellent clinical efficacy and safety profile. With supportive data from the Phase 3 HARMONi-A and HARMONi-2 trials, it has been approved in China.

In recent years, bispecific antibodies simultaneously targeting PD-(L)1 and VEGF have emerged as a rapidly expanding frontier in anticancer drug development. This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the research progress in this dynamic field. Specifically, we will delineate the development history and key clinical milestones of ivonescimab, summarize the advances of other anti-PD-(L)1/VEGF bispecific antibodies currently in clinical development, discuss the unresolved challenges, and offer forward-looking insights into future directions.

Rationale for developing anti-PD-(L)1/VEGF bispecific antibodies

Angiogenesis and immunomodulation are thought to interact closely. Angiogenic factors can inhibit immune effector cells and promote immunosuppressive cells, inhibit the maturation of dendritic cells and boost the formation of regulatory T cells, promote the recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and enhance the pro-tumor effect of macrophages [6]. VEGF induces abnormal tumor vasculature that can exacerbate the hypoxic microenvironment, thus exacerbating immunosuppression [7]. Furthermore, the immunosuppressive cells, in turn, promote tumor angiogenesis, which create a vicious circle of disrupted immune activation. Anti-angiogenic drugs enable more infiltration and greater activation of immune cells by inducing vascular normalization and transforming the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment to an immunosupportive one. Concurrently, activated immune cells in the tumor microenvironment can secrete interferon- to promote vasculature remodeling, thus completing the cycle [8, 9].

Clinical trial data also support the potentially synergistic interaction between immunotherapy and anti-angiogenesis treatment. As early as 2017, the initial results of the IMpower 150 study were reported. This multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled Phase 3 study demonstrated that bevacizumab plus atezolizumab and chemotherapy significantly improved PFS and OS in metastatic non-squamous NSCLC (median PFS 8.3 vs. 6.8 months, hazard ratio [HR] 0.62, 95% CI 0.52–0.74; median OS 19.2 vs. 14.7 months; HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.64–0.96) compared with bevacizumab plus chemotherapy [10]. Based on this study, FDA approved bevacizumab plus atezolizumab and chemotherapy as first-line treatment for metastatic non-squamous NSCLC in December 2018. In addition, combinations of ICIs with small-molecule anti-angiogenic agents have also been successfully validated in first-line renal cell carcinoma and advanced hepatocellular carcinoma [11–13]. These combination regimens have demonstrated significantly prolonged OS, PFS, and improved ORR in relevant patient populations. Despite these successful cases, several studies of ICIs plus anti-angiogenic agents have failed to demonstrate efficacy, such as the LEAP series studies.

However, combining separate anti-PD-(L)1 and anti-angiogenic agents may be associated with increased toxicity. The LEAP-007 study evaluating first-line lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab versus placebo plus pembrolizumab in patients with metastatic NSCLC was discontinued after it revealed that the incidence of treatment-related adverse events was higher with lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab combination, without OS benefit. The authors attributed the lack of OS benefit in part to post-study treatment and a higher incidence of treatment related adverse events [14]. In other studies of pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib to treat multiple malignancies, the incidence of grade 3 or higher adverse events ranged from 67% to 89% [15]. Additionally, bevacizumab is contraindicated for use in patients with squamous NSCLC due to safety concerns based on a randomized Phase II trial in patients with both squamous and non-squamous advanced NSCLC. In this study, six patients experienced a major life-threatening bleeding described as hemoptysis or hematemesis, including 4 of 13 patients with advanced squamous NSCLC who had received bevacizumab with chemotherapy [16]. A regimen or drug combining the therapeutic benefits of anti-PD-1 agents and anti-angiogenic therapies while mitigating relevant adverse events appears to be a reasonable research direction.

Anti-PD-1/VEGF bispecific antibodies differ from combination therapy

Some evidence highlights differences between bispecific antibodies and conventional two-drug combinations.

Mechanistically, two-drug combinations rely on the independent action of each component, as each drug binds to its respective target and exerts its effects separately, with potential additive or synergistic benefits driven by concurrent pathway inhibition. However, this approach is constrained by variable pharmacokinetic (PK) profiles of the two drugs, which may lead to inconsistent target engagement over time and off-target interactions of individual drugs. In contrast, bispecifics enable spatially coordinated dual targeting within the tumor microenvironment where both PD-1 and VEGF are highly enriched, thereby ensuring concurrent PD-1 blockade and VEGF neutralization [17]. For ivonescimab, the two targets exhibit mutual cooperativity in binding activity: in the presence of VEGF, its binding avidity to PD-1 increases ~18-fold, whereas PD-1 binding reciprocally enhances its affinity for VEGF by more than four-fold. This bidirectional enhancement not only strengthens the potency of pathway blockade but also promotes preferential accumulation of the molecule at the tumor site. These distinctive features are closely linked to its tetravalent molecular architecture [17]. A comparable advantage has been observed with cadonilimab (AK104), another tetravalent bispecific antibody developed on the same platform that targets PD-1 and CTLA-4. A preclinical immuno-PET imaging study using 124I-labeled AK104 demonstrated markedly higher standard uptake values at the tumor site and superior tumor-to-nontumor (T/NT) ratios compared with imaging using monospecific PD-1 or CTLA-4 targeting monoclonal antibodies [18]. Similar cooperative binding characteristics have also been reported for other tetravalent anti-PD-1/VEGF bispecific antibodies.

Clinical efficacy with bispecific antibodies are evident in clinical trials. In HARMONi-2 study with ivonescimab monotherapy in previously untreated advanced NSCLC, the median PFS was doubled compared with pembrolizumab monotherapy. Additionally, safety advantages are paramount. In studies with ivonescimab monotherapy and ivonescimab plus chemotherapy, the incidence of grade ≥ 3 hemorrhagic events was very low (2%), and similar in squamous and non-squamous NSCLC [19, 20].

However, no head-to-head studies have directly compared the efficacy and safety of bispecific antibodies versus two-drug combinations. Nevertheless, accumulating evidence underscores that bispecific antibodies are not merely “replacements” for two-drug combinations, but distinct therapeutic modalities. Their integrated dual-targeting mechanism, spatially precise enhanced efficacy, and more favorable safety profile establish them as a promising alternative to conventional combinations.

HARMONi-A was ivonescimab’s first Phase 3 study, marking the initial demonstration that ivonescimab combined with chemotherapy could significantly prolong PFS (median 7.1 vs. 4.8 months; HR 0.46, P < .001) and OS (median 16.8 vs. 14.1 months; HR 0.74, P = .019) compared with standard chemotherapy in second-line treatment for patients with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated NSCLC who received EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) [21, 22]. Similarly, the global HARMONi study confirmed that ivonescimab combined with chemotherapy significantly improved PFS (median 7.1 vs. 5.5 months; HR 0.72, P = .022) in this patient group [23]. The lack of statistical significance in OS benefit for this event-driven analysis may be attributed to the short follow-up time of just 9.2 months in patients outside of Asia, a product of the sequential enrollment pattern and slow initial enrollment in the Western cohort of patients. A post-hoc analysis of the HARMONi study with a longer follow-up period showed that the HR for OS was 0.78 (95% CI 0.62–0.98) [23]. Both HARMONi-A and HARMONi studies indicate that with longer follow-up duration, the OS benefit becomes more profound. It is noted that the observed benefit on PFS in HARMONi-A and HARMONi demonstrates the consistent efficacy of ivonescimab in both Asian patients and patients outside Asia.

The HARMONi-2 study was the first to demonstrate the superiority of an anti-PD-1/VEGF bispecific antibody compared with an anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody in advanced NSCLC, regardless of PD-L1 expression (median PFS 17.1 vs. 13.3 months; HR 0.51, P < .0001) [20]. It represents a pivotal milestone in cancer immunotherapy, prompting the hypothesis that ivonescimab could provide an alternative to PD-(L)1 inhibitors currently used in cancer treatment. Furthermore, in advanced squamous NSCLC, the HARMONi-6 study showed that ivonescimab plus chemotherapy yielded superior PFS compared with tislelizumab plus chemotherapy (median 10.2 vs. 7.1 months; HR 0.65, P = .0002) [20]. This study is the first to demonstrate that anti-VEGF treatment is effective and safe in patients with advanced squamous NSCLC; these results offer a potential to reshape the treatment landscape for this challenging histologic subtype of NSCLC.

Extending beyond lung cancer, ivonescimab’s therapeutic potential is being explored in other immunotherapy-sensitive tumor types (including biliary tract cancer and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma) via Phase 3 trials comparing it with standard-of-care regimens. Additionally, in malignancies characterized by limited treatment options and intrinsic resistance to immunotherapy, including colorectal cancer, triple-negative breast cancer, and pancreatic cancer, several Phase 3 clinical trials have been initiated.

Following ivonescimab, a growing number of molecules targeting both PD-1 and VEGF have been developed. Table 2 lists the agents that have entered Phase 2 clinical studies. These anti-PD-(L)1/VEGF bispecific antibodies in development often combined with chemotherapy and focused heavily on NSCLC as a primary indication, and other indications under investigation are also similar to those of ivonescimab. For instance, PM8002 has shown promising Phase 2 efficacy and is advancing to Phase 3 in NSCLC, ES-SCLC, TNBC and GC, and SSGJ-707 focus on Phase 3 trials in 1 L NSCLC and 1 L mCRC. However, these agents differ in clinical maturity. Ivonescimab is the most advanced agent, with multiple positive Phase 3 results (including OS benefits), regulatory approvals in China, and broad expansion across more than 10 indications, whereas others are predominantly in Phase 1/2 or early Phase 3 stages, lacking mature survival data.

Different bispecific antibodies exhibit distinct characteristics

As illustrated in Fig. 1, anti-PD-(L)1/VEGF bispecific antibodies display considerable diversity in molecular architecture.

The schematic diagram of molecular structure. Blue: VEGF terminus; red: PD-1 terminus; green: PD-L1 terminus.

Ivonescimab exemplifies the symmetric tetravalent PD-1/VEGF-A IgG1-scFv format, in which anti-PD-1 single-chain variable fragments are fused to the C-termini of an anti-VEGF IgG backbone. This design enables VEGF-dimer-mediated “daisy-chaining,” resulting in strong mutual cooperativity (Fig. 2). Additionally, ivonescimab incorporates Fc-silencing mutations (L234A/L235A) that abrogate FcγRI/IIIa binding, leading to significantly reduced effector functions (ADCC/ADCP) in vitro, which aligns with its favorable safety profile observed in non-human primates and humans [17]. SSGJ-707 is also a tetravalent antibody based on the CLF2 (common light chain linear-Fabs-IgG) platform which reported a 10-fold increase in PD-1 binding affinity compared with ivonescimab in the presence of VEGF [24]. RC148 is an additional PD-1/VEGF tetravalent bispecific antibody possessing analogous cooperative binding [25]. LM-299 has a different molecular design, comprising an anti-VEGF antibody linked to two C-terminal single domain anti-PD-1 antibodies. PM8002/BNT327 and CVL006 are PD-L1/VEGF-A bispecifics that fuse anti-PD-L1 variable domain of heavy chain-only (VHH) domains to an anti-VEGF-A IgG1 scaffold [26]. CVL006 is a novel bispecific antibody generated by fusing an anti-PD-L1 VHH domain with a humanized IgG1 anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody. CVL006 retains antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) functionality [27]. VEGF-trap-based molecules such as HB0025 and IMM2510 are anti-PD-L1/VEGF bispecific antibody fusion proteins. Additionally, IMM2510 has the mAb-Trap structure and can also activate NK cells and macrophages through Fc-mediated ADCC/ADCP activities [28, 29]. JS207 is a recombinant humanized anti-PD-1 and VEGF-A bispecific antibody of IgG4 κ-subtype constructed in a tetravalent IgG-VHH format combining full-length anti-PD-1 IgG with VEGF-A-targeting VHH antibody [30]. DR30206 stands apart as a trispecific fusion protein built on a bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) backbone with an N-terminal anti-PD-L1 VHH and C-terminal TGF-β RII extracellular domain, simultaneously addressing three immunosuppressive pathways [31].

Schematic diagram of tetravalent anti-PD-1/VEGF bispecific antibodies’ mechanism of action.

These structural differences may potentially affect therapeutic efficacy and safety profiles, including the risk of off-target toxicities and immune-related adverse events. Therefore, the clinical significance of these structural variations warrants further evaluation in well-designed clinical trials.

Unresolved issues and future research directions

Based on the accumulated promising results from clinical studies, the therapeutic potential of this novel class of agents warrants further exploration across a broader spectrum of malignancies. Beyond lung cancer, the spectrum of other tumor types that may benefit from these bispecific antibodies remains to be defined. In addition to their application in advanced/metastatic settings, investigations into the utility of bispecific antibodies in the perioperative stage of tumors are highly anticipated. Another critical direction worthy of exploration is whether anti-PD-(L)1/VEGF bispecific antibodies retain efficacy in patients with prior exposure to ICIs, a population with unmet medical needs due to limited treatment options after ICI failure.

Owing to the favorable safety profile, these bispecific antibodies can serve as the cornerstone of immunotherapy. A large umber of studies have been initiated to explore novel combination regimens. Several studies have been initiated to explore the efficacy of ivonescimab combined with ligufalimab (AK117, an anti-CD47 monoclonal antibody) and ivonescimab combined with cadonilimab (AK104, anti-PD-1/CTLA-4 bispecific antibody) in multiple types of cancer. Combination therapies pairing PD-(L)1/VEGF bispecific antibodies or immune checkpoint inhibitors with antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are gaining traction. This approach is being evaluated in ongoing clinical trials such as NCT06892548, which investigates the combination of BNT327 (a PD-L1/VEGF bispecific antibody) and BNT324 (a B7-H3 ADC) in lung cancer, with the goal of synergizing immune modulation with direct cytotoxicity to improve response rates in immunotherapy-resistant disease. A study with PM8002 in combination with BNT325 (Trop2ADC) has reported early clinical data, which showed successful dose escalation (NCT05438329). Research on drug resistance mechanisms and biomarkers will also further provide new insights for combination therapy.

For current and future clinical trial designs of these bispecific antibodies, priority should be given to the inclusion of diverse global cohorts to rigorously evaluate cross-ethnic consistency of efficacy and safety, sufficiently prolonged follow-up periods to obtain mature OS data, and the adoption of adaptive Phase 1/2/3 trial frameworks that seamlessly integrate dose optimization, biomarker stratification, and combination-therapy arms.

Another promising direction lies in the development of next-generation anti-PD-(L)1/VEGF molecules, including trispecific antibodies (e.g. CS2009 [CStone Pharmaceuticals]) and VEGFR1/PD-L1 chimeric antigen receptor (investigated by Sichuan University, China). Additionally, the successful development and clinical validation of PD-(L)1/VEGF bispecific antibodies pave the way for innovative bispecific antibody–drug conjugates (bsADCs) by integrating dual immune checkpoint and angiogenesis blockade with targeted cytotoxic payloads. This evolution could be extended to bsADCs like JSKN027, the first PD-L1/VEGFR2-targeted conjugate, potentially offering superior precision and potency over monotherapy or naked bispecific antibodies in overcoming heterogeneous tumor microenvironments.
