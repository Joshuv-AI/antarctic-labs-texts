Pathologically proven cholesterol crystal embolism causing testicular infarction after endovascular aneurysm repair
Authors: Kenjiro Kaneko, Takao Ohki, Eisaku Ito, Kiichiro Ishikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTesticular infarction following endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) is extremely rare, and its pathogenesis remains unclear. We report a 56-year-old man who developed acute right testicular infarction after EVAR with concomitant internal iliac artery coil embolization for an infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm. Pain migrated atypically from the epigastrium to the right testicle. Doppler ultrasound study suggested ischemia, and contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging confirmed global infarction. Orchiectomy revealed cholesterol crystal embolism in the spermatic cord artery. This case highlights the utility of magnetic resonance imaging for diagnosing post-EVAR testicular infarction and emphasizes the importance of early scrotal evaluation in patients presenting with unexplained or migrating postoperative pain.Keywords: Cholesterol embolism, Endovascular procedures, Aortic aneurysm, Abdominal, Testicular infarction, Internal iliac artery, Case report

Endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) for abdominal aortic aneurysm is widely adopted as a minimally invasive treatment.1 However, EVAR carries a risk of organ infarction because of distal embolization of atheroma or thrombus, with thromboembolic complications reported in 3% to 10% of cases.2, 3, 4

Pelvic ischemia resulting from internal iliac artery (IIA) occlusion is a well-established complication, including buttock claudication (27.9%), erectile dysfunction (10.2%), and ischemic colitis (0.63%).5 In contrast, post-EVAR testicular ischemia or infarction is exceptionally rare, with only 11 cases reported worldwide, and few pathologically confirmed as cholesterol crystal embolism (CCE).2,3,6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14

We report a case of acute right testicular infarction after EVAR with concomitant right IIA coil embolization, in which global infarction was diagnosed by contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and pathologically confirmed as CCE following orchiectomy. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Case report

A 56-year-old man was found to have an infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm (maximum diameter, 46 mm) incidentally identified during the evaluation for ureteral stones. Over 6 months, the aneurysm enlarged to 51 mm, meeting the threshold for elective repair. Concurrent right common iliac artery aneurysm (23 mm, saccular) and internal iliac artery aneurysms (25 mm) were also identified. His medical history included hypertension, dyslipidemia, ureteral stones, and asymptomatic myocardial ischemia requiring treatment. The indication for elective repair was supported by the 51-mm diameter and the 5-mm enlargement over 6 months.15 Given the cardiac risk associated with open repair, the presence of treatment-requiring asymptomatic myocardial ischemia, and the patient's preference for early return to work, EVAR was chosen.

Preoperative contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a small mural thrombus at the aortic wall adjacent to the origin of the right testicular artery (Fig 1, A and B). The right testicular artery arose directly from the infrarenal aorta, and the deferential artery was not delineated due to its small caliber. Although IIA preservation using an iliac branch device was considered, it was deemed anatomically unsuitable due to the extension of the internal iliac artery aneurysm to the IIA bifurcation. Accordingly, EVAR was performed using an Aorfix Endovascular Stent Graft (Lombard Medical) with concomitant right IIA coil embolization with coils deployed from immediately proximal to the bifurcation of the superior and inferior gluteal arteries without interrupting the communication between these two branches (Fig 1, C and D), thereby preserving collateral perfusion to the deferential artery via the superior vesical artery.

On postoperative day (POD) 1, the patient reported epigastric and right flank pain. Contrast-enhanced CT showed no aneurysm rupture, intestinal ischemia, retroperitoneal hematoma, or endoleak. The right testicular artery was no longer visualized due to stent graft coverage, whereas the superior vesical artery remained patent. However, there was no significant asymmetry between the bilateral testes suggestive of testicular ischemia (Figs 1, E and 2, A). Given the patient's history of ureteral stones and the nonspecific pain distribution, ureteral pathology was considered, and conservative management was continued.

Sequential imaging findings following endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR). A, Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) on postoperative day 1, delayed phase (same examination as in Fig 1, C): no significant asymmetry between the bilateral testes was observed, with no evidence of testicular ischemia. B, Doppler ultrasound study on postoperative day 3: marked reduction of intratesticular color Doppler signals with only scattered peripheral flow, indicating severely compromised arterial perfusion. C, Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) on postoperative day 3: contrast-enhanced axial T1-weighted images [repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE), 701/11 ms] demonstrate near-complete absence of enhancement throughout the right testicular parenchyma, consistent with global testicular infarction. MRI scanner: SIGNA Victor (GE Healthcare).

On POD 3, the pain progressively migrated to the right testicle, prompting a scrotal examination. Doppler ultrasound study revealed markedly reduced intratesticular flow with only scattered peripheral signals and heterogeneous echoes (Fig 2, B). Subsequent contrast-enhanced MRI demonstrated near-complete loss of parenchymal enhancement throughout the right testis, confirming global testicular infarction (Fig 2, C).

After thorough discussion with the patient and his family, right orchiectomy was performed in collaboration with the urology department. Histopathological examination demonstrated extensive hemorrhagic necrosis of the seminiferous tubules (Fig 3, A) and cholesterol clefts within the spermatic cord arteries (Fig 3, B), confirming global testicular infarction secondary to CCE. The postoperative course was uneventful, with rapid pain resolution. The patient was discharged on POD 3 following orchiectomy (post-EVAR day 6).

Discussion

Testicular infarction following EVAR is extremely rare; to the best of our knowledge, this is the 12th reported case (Table). Testicular blood flow is supplied by three systems: the testicular artery (approximately 80%), the deferential artery (approximately 20%, derived from the IIA), and the cremasteric artery.12,14 CCE associated with EVAR is considered the primary mechanism of testicular artery microvascular occlusion,2,3,8 with pathological confirmation in only two earlier cases.3,8 In the present case, histopathological examination identified cholesterol clefts within the spermatic cord arteries, establishing CCE as the dominant mechanism of testicular infarction. Stent graft coverage of the testicular artery and right IIA embolization, which reduces deferential artery perfusion, are considered contributory factors rather than independently causal, as neither was directly demonstrated to be responsible in the absence of imaging or operative evidence. Of note, 6 of the 11 previously reported cases (54.5%) occurred without IIA occlusion,2,3,9, 10, 11,13 suggesting that CCE alone can cause testicular infarction independent of IIA status.

This case was characterized by atypical pain migration from the epigastric region to the right flank and then to the testicle, whereas most previous cases presented direct inguinal or scrotal pain. CT on POD 1 revealed no evidence of testicular ischemia, possibly because changes had not yet developed; CT utility in this setting remains limited.8,13 Doppler ultrasound study has been the most frequently used modality for diagnosing post-EVAR testicular ischemia. To our knowledge, MRI has not previously been used in this setting, and the present case suggests that contrast-enhanced MRI provides definitive assessment of testicular infarction (Table).

Among reported cases, orchiectomy was performed in six cases,2,3,6,8,11,14 and conservative management was chosen in five cases.7,9,10,12,13 Conservative management has been reported with favorable outcomes in cases of partial infarction or spontaneous pain resolution,13 whereas orchiectomy was performed in all cases with confirmed global infarction and persistent symptoms. In the present case, MRI-confirmed global infarction with near-complete loss of parenchymal perfusion indicated a high risk of necrosis and infection, supporting orchiectomy. Pain resolved promptly, and the patient was discharged 3 days postoperatively.

This case, together with earlier reports, suggests three practical points. First, a small mural thrombus adjacent to the testicular artery origin is a risk factor for CCE-induced testicular infarction, irrespective of IIA management. Second, when IIA embolization is required, testicular blood flow may be critically compromised by combined testicular artery coverage and reduced deferential artery perfusion. Third, unexplained postoperative lumbar, epigastric, or flank pain, even without scrotal symptoms, should prompt early scrotal evaluation with Doppler ultrasound study, with contrast-enhanced MRI reserved for cases in which residual flow precludes a definitive diagnosis. At 56 years, this patient is the youngest reported to date, and EVAR in younger patients warrants careful risk assessment, including IIA preservation via iliac branch device when feasible.16 The Japanese Circulation Society/Japanese Society for Cardiovascular Surgery/Japanese Association for Thoracic Surgery/Japanese Society for Vascular Surgery 2020 guideline considers invasive treatment appropriate for an aneurysm diameter ≥50 mm or an enlargement rate ≥5 mm over 6 months (both class IIa) and recommends open repair as preferable in younger patients (class IIb).15 Despite the present patient’s comorbidities and social considerations, open repair with bilateral IIA preservation should arguably have been considered, given the known association between IIA sacrifice and ischemic complications.5,16 Recognition of this rare but potentially debilitating complication should inform preoperative planning and shared decision-making in similar candidates.

Conclusions

Testicular infarction after EVAR is an under-recognized complication of which CCE is the principal mechanism. To our knowledge, this is the first case diagnosed by contrast-enhanced MRI. The atypical pain migration highlights the need for a high index of suspicion and a low threshold for scrotal evaluation when postoperative pain is unexplained. Further case accumulation is required to clarify the pathogenesis and optimal management.
