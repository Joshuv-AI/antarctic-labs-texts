Does the development of AVN after the modified Dunn procedure for slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE) depend on slip stability? A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mohammad Kamal Abdelnasser, Ahmed Abdelazim Hassan, Mohammed Ibrahim, Abdelkhalek Hafez Ibrahim, Nariman Abol Oyoun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSlipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE) is a unique clinical entity, and many studies in the past few years have attempted to elucidate its natural history and ideal treatment. The modified Dunn procedure is one of its most widely accepted treatment options. However, the development of avascular necrosis (AVN) remains an issue of great concern. The main aim of this study was to identify the relationship between slip stability and the development of AVN after surgical hip dislocation through the modified Dunn procedure.MethodsA systematic review was conducted according to the PRISMA guidelines. A literature search was performed using the PubMed and Scopus databases with MeSH terms and free text forms. The articles were screened against the preselected criteria, and the ultimately included articles were included in the systematic review. Finally, a meta-analysis was conducted to compare the incidence of AVN between stable and unstable SCFE patients.ResultsBased on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 12 articles were ultimately included in the systematic review, with nine articles eligible for quantitative analysis. Overall, the articles included 532 hips. Only 55 hips developed AVN, with an overall incidence of 10.3%. Among the 55 hips, 18 (32.7%) were stable, and 37 (67.3%) were unstable. Overall, the articles showed a low risk of bias (ROB); however, three articles showed a lack of proper reporting of the patients’ demographics, and one article showed a lack of clear reporting of the clinical information of the study population. The meta-analysis showed that the risk of developing AVN had a cumulative odds ratio (OR) of 0.33 (95% confidence interval 0.11–1.04), with an insignificant effect size of 1.9. (p = 0.06)ConclusionThe development of AVN after surgery remains a multifactorial process, and the procedure remains a reliable option for the treatment of both stable and unstable SCFE in the hands of experienced surgeons with an overall low risk of AVN.Trial registrationThe Study is registered on the PROSPERO registry, Study ID: CRD42022374768.Keywords: SCFE, AVN, Modified dunn procedure, Loder classification

Introduction

Avascular necrosis (AVN) is the most serious complication after slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE) [1]. The incidence of AVN associated with SCFE using different surgical procedures varies between 0% and 66% [2–4]. This difference may be attributed to the disease itself. However, iatrogenic factors may play a role in unstable SCFE, whereas AVN in stable SCFE is believed to be purely iatrogenic [5–8]. Many theories have been proposed to explain the mechanism of AVN in unstable SCFE, including direct injury [5], retinacular vessel kinking [9], vascular tamponade [10–12], closed reduction techniques [13, 14] and the timing of surgery [15].

Mild SCFE can be successfully managed with in situ pinning with good results. However, the optimal option for moderate and severe SCFE is still controversial. Aiming at maximizing safety, in situ pinning has also been suggested for moderate and severe SCFE; however, residual deformity can lead to femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) and eventually arthritis [16, 17]. To avoid this, many osteotomies have been described [18–20]. Unfortunately, they were associated with a high rate of AVN. The modified Dunn procedure was described as a method for anatomically reducing the physis while maintaining its vascular supply to decrease the incidence of AVN [21].

The rate of AVN after modified Dunn’s test is variable. Ziebarth et al. [3] and Masse et al. [22] reported 0% AVN. Similarly, Solongo et al. [23] reported a low incidence of AVN (4%). Others reported a relatively high rate of AVN, as reported by Sanker et al. (26%) [5], or even a very high rate, as reported by Alves et al. (66%) [4]. This wide variation in AVN incidence reflects the controversy surrounding the safety of the procedure. However, another issue of debate is whether stable or unstable SCFE is safer for the modified Dunn procedure. Many authors have shown a difference between the incidence of AVN when using modified Dunn for stable and unstable SCFE, with a higher incidence of AVN in stable SCFE in some studies and the opposite in others.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare the incidence of AVN between stable and unstable SCFE patients after the modified Dunn procedure.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Cochrane Back and Neck Group [24] and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [25]. The study protocol was registered on the PROSPERO registry (no. CRD42022374768).

Search strategy

Electronic searches were conducted in two databases, PubMed and Scopus, from inception until May 19, 2025, using the following search terms and Boolean operators.

PubMed search strategy

((((((femur head necrosis[MeSH Terms]) OR (AVN[Text Word])) OR (AVN hip[Text Word])) OR (“avascular necros*“[Text Word])) OR (“aseptic necros*“[Text Word])) AND ((“modified Dunn procedure“[Text Word]) OR (“modified Dunn osteotomy“[Text Word])) OR (“modified Dunn“[Text Word]))) AND (((((slipped capital femoral epiphyses[MeSH Terms]) OR (SUFE[Text Word])) OR (SCFE[Text Word])) OR (“slipped capital femoral epiphys*“[Text Word])) OR (“slipped upper femoral epiphys*“[Text Word]))

Scopus searching strategy

sufe OR scfe OR “slipped capital femoral epiphys*” OR “slipped upper femoral epiphys*” AND “modified Dunn” OR “modified Dunn procedure” OR “modified Dunn osteotomy” AND avn OR avn AND hip OR avn AND of AND the AND hip OR “avascular necros*” OR “aseptic necros*”.

The included articles underwent forward and backwards snowballing to identify additional relevant articles.

Study selection and eligibility

Two independent reviewers screened the studies for eligibility first by title, then by abstract, and finally by reading through the full text. Studies were eligible for review if they included patients with SCFE who were treated by surgical hip dislocation using the modified Dunn procedure, with a minimum follow-up period of one year. Additionally, the eligible studies needed to classify stability based on Loder’s classification and to report AVN incidence in both the stable and unstable groups separately. All study designs were included except for case reports, case series of less than 10 patients, and review articles. Studies that included revision surgeries were excluded. Finally, non-English articles and studies conducted on cadavers or animals were excluded.

Initially, all studies that were retrieved from manual and electronic searches were checked for duplicates using Endnote X21®. Any disagreements were resolved by consulting with a senior reviewer.

Data extraction

A standardized form was used to extract the following data from the studies: type of study, level of evidence, number of hips, follow-up period, affected side, mean age, sex, number of stable or unstable patients who developed AVN and reported complications.

Risk of bias (ROB) assessment

ROB was assessed by two independent reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) ROB assessment tool [26]. The tool includes 10 questions addressing the internal validity and risk of bias of case series designs. The possible answers were ‘yes’, ‘unclear’, ‘no’ or ‘not applicable’, with one mark allocated for each criterion. The total score was summed in percentages to examine the methodological quality of the studies.

Data synthesis and analysis

Meta-analysis was conducted using Review Manager 5.5 (RevMan®) to compare the incidence of AVN between stable and unstable SCFE patients. Odds ratios (OR) were calculated for dichotomous outcomes, and mean differences (MD) were used for continuous data. The calculated effect sizes were visualised in forest plots using the random effect model, and publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot. A P value ≤ 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Group heterogeneity was assessed using chi-square and I2 tests. A P value ≤ 0.05 indicated significant heterogeneity.

Results

Eligible studies

The initial search yielded 124 articles. After duplicate removal, the number of duplicates was reduced to 108. After screening the titles and abstracts, 53 articles were found to be eligible for full-text screening, which eventually yielded 12 articles, of which only 9 were eligible for meta-analysis.

Articles were excluded primarily for not classifying the patient according to the Loder classification or not mentioning whether the patient who developed AVN had stable or unstable SCFE (17 articles). The second most common cause for exclusion was the use of either a different or a modified surgical technique (9 articles). Other reasons for exclusion were an insufficient follow-up period (4 articles), the inclusion of patients with either stable or unstable SCFE (4 articles) or the inclusion of a review article (4 articles). No additional articles were included from the manual search or snowballing. (Fig. 1).

All articles were case series (Level IV evidence), comprising ten retrospective and two prospective studies, published between 2009 and 2025. The total number of included hips was 532. The follow-up period ranged from 1 year to 22 years. Overall, most of the studies included more stable hips than unstable hips (349 vs. 182, respectively), except for the study by Davis et al. [7], which included more unstable hips.

Regarding the affected side, more left hips were affected than right hips (202 and 256 hips, respectively), although two articles [7, 22] did not report the affected side. The average age of the patients was 13.2 years (range 9–19 years), with a male predominance (268 males vs. 186 females). Among the 532 hips, only 55 developed AVN, with an incidence of 10.3%. Of the 55 hips that developed AVN, 18 (32.7%) were stable, and 37 (67.3%) were unstable.

Regarding other postoperative complications, heterotopic ossification was the most common complication (n = 26 hips, 4.9%). Hip instability (dislocation or subluxation) and hardware failure were the second most common complications (n = 11, 2.06%, each). Other complications included peroneal or sciatic nerve palsy (n = 5, 0.9%), wound infection (n = 5, 0.9%), and chondrolysis (n = 3, 0.56%).

All the studies had a low risk of bias based on the JBI tool, as the studies’ scores ranged between 7 and 9 out of 9. The only domain that had a high risk of bias was the 6th question, as three studies [6, 7, 22] did not clearly describe the patients’ demographics. Additionally, only one article did not clearly describe the clinical information of the patients [33]. Five other studies did not report whether the selected patients were consecutively recruited [22, 28, 29, 31, 33]. Regarding the 9th domain, all of the selected studies were scored as “not applicable”, as there was no expected variation within different geographic locations.

Funnel plot visualising the publication bias

Nine articles were eligible for the meta-analysis [6, 7, 27–33] as the remaining three articles [3, 22, 34] had no cases complicated with AVN.

The cumulative odds ratio for the development of AVN was 0.33 (95% confidence interval, 0.11–1.04), with an overall statistically nonsignificant effect size of 1.9 (p value = 0.06). Most studies reported an OR below 1, indicating a consistent trend toward lower AVN risk in stable slips. Although this suggests a potential 67% reduction in the odds of AVN in stable cases, the result was not statistically significant as the confidence interval includes 1. Moderate heterogeneity was observed (I² = 59%), indicating some variation between studies that may be due to differences in study design, patient selection, or surgical technique.

Visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed a generally inverted funnel shape, with a broader scatter among studies with smaller sample sizes and tighter clustering among larger studies, as expected. However, there was noticeable asymmetry, with fewer studies distributed on the left side of the plot, suggesting potential publication bias or small-study effects. Additionally, a few studies appeared outside the pseudo 95% confidence limits, which may indicate heterogeneity or methodological variability across the included studies.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we investigated the association between slip stability and the development of AVN following the modified Dunn procedure for SCFE. Our pooled analysis of 532 hips from 12 studies demonstrated an overall AVN incidence of 10.3%, with a higher proportion of AVN cases occurring in unstable SCFE. The meta-analysis revealed a trend toward lower odds of AVN in stable slips; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance. These findings underscore the complex, multifactorial nature of AVN development and highlight the need for careful patient selection and surgical expertise when performing the modified Dunn procedure.

These findings align with, yet are also distinct from, previous research exploring AVN outcomes following the modified Dunn procedure. A notable example is the meta-analysis conducted by Jauregui et al. [35]. Their analysis of 26 studies involving 287 hips reported a pooled AVN incidence of 14.3%, notably higher than the 10.3% incidence identified in our review. Differences in patient selection criteria may explain this discrepancy; our study stratified cases by slip stability (stable vs. unstable), allowing for a more granular understanding of AVN risk factors. Furthermore, although they did not assess odds ratios or perform subgroup analysis based on the Loder classification, our meta-analysis incorporated comparative data to evaluate whether slip stability independently influences AVN risk.

The risk factors for AVN after SCFE are still unclear [36]. This may be attributed to heterogeneity among enrolled patients in different studies due to the use of different classification systems without defining the exact rate of AVN in each subgroup of the classification used. Moreover, there is a discrepancy between clinical stability, as proposed by the Loder classification, and actual epimetaphyseal instability [13]. Ziebarth et al. [37] compared the clinical estimation of physeal stability according to the Loder classification with intraoperative findings and found that they did not correlate.

Only a few studies have specifically compared the rate of AVN after modified Dunn for stable and unstable SCFE. For example, Souder et al. [38] compared the rate of AVN between stable and unstable SCFE when using either in situ pinning or the modified Dunn procedure. In stable SCFE, the AVN rate was 0% for in situ pinning and 20% for the modified Dunn procedure. In unstable SCFE, the two methods were not significantly different. Consequently, they recommended using in situ pinning for chronically stable SCFE, whereas in unstable SCFE, the two methods could be used alternatively. Trisolino et al. [39] reported better deformity correction and a lower early reoperation rate favouring the modified Dunn procedure; however, the rate of AVN was greater than that of in situ pinning for severe stable SCFE. Similarly, Davis et al. [7] reported that AVN occurred in 6% of patients with unstable SCFE, whereas it occurred in 29.4% of patients with stable SCFE; accordingly, they recommended the cautious use of this procedure for treating stable SCFE. It is worth noting that Sikora-klak [8] reported a greater rate of AVN in stable SCFE patients treated with the modified Dunn procedure than in those treated with the Imhauser osteotomy and recommended against the use of the modified Dunn procedure for stable SCEF. This finding may be attributed to iatrogenic factors associated with surgical technique rather than the disease itself. In stable SCFE, the vascular supply to the epiphysis is presumed to be intact preoperatively; however, surgical dislocation and subcapital realignment inherently involve manipulation of the femoral head and retinacular vessels. In cases where surgical technique is suboptimal—such as excessive traction, aggressive manipulation, or improper capsulotomy—there is a greater risk of compromising the vascular supply, thereby converting a well-perfused epiphysis into an ischemic one.

In contrast, Birke et al. [6] reported that the procedure is safe and is associated with a lower incidence of AVN based on their series in which they used intraoperative femoral head pulsatile monitoring and modification of capsule incision/closure techniques. However, these safety measures did not decrease the rate of AVN in unstable SCFE patients, indicating the presence of AVN risk factors other than surgical technique. Similarly, Novais et al. [40] reported a tendency for better deformity correction and a lower reoperation rate favouring the modified Dunn procedure compared to in situ pinning in severe stable SCFE.

It should be emphasized that although the results of their work showed nonsignificant differences with regard to the rate of AVN occurrence between the two groups, they attributed the lack of difference to variation in the surgeons’ experience between the two surgical groups. Abdelnasser et al. [27] in their case series explored the relationship between surgical expertise and the development of AVN, and despite having more cases of AVN in the first half of their case series, this difference was statistically insignificant. This fact was supported by Novais et al. [41], who acknowledged that the effect of the long learning curve of the procedure and its technical complexity, despite the lack of statistically significant differences in the incidence of AVN development between the two treatment groups.

Other studies reported a greater incidence of AVN in patients with unstable SCFE. For example, Agashe et al. [28] reported AVN in 18.1% of treated unstable SCFEs, whereas none of the stable SCFEs showed AVN. Similarly, Sarassa et al. [32] reported AVN in 11.1% of patients with unstable SCFE, whereas none of the patients with stable SCFE had AVN. Additionally, Masquijo et al. [30] reported that 33.3% of their six patients with stable SCFE had AVN, while 53% of the 18 patients with unstable SCFE had AVN. Accordingly, they recommended against the use of modified Dunn for acute unstable SCFE and to limit its use to a few selected patients with severe stable SCFE.

Although AVN reportedly occurs after prophylactic pinning of the contralateral side [42], Loder reported that there is no inherent risk for AVN in stable SCFE [43], suggesting the presence of iatrogenic risk factors predisposing patients to AVN [6, 43].

Many theories have been postulated to explain the cause of AVN in unstable SCFE. Potential risk factors may include the nature of the surgical intervention and preinjury to the retinaculum [6]. For example, Careny et al. [16] studied the natural history of chronic severe untreated SCFE and reported the possibility of spontaneous AVN occurrence. Consequently, it may be assumed that unstable SCFE is associated with an inherently greater chance of developing AVN. When properly performed, the modified Dunn procedure could decrease the chance of developing AVN compared with in situ pinning for unstable SCFE [44].

Loder reviewed the rate of AVN after different surgical procedures for unstable SCFE and reported an average rate of AVN of approximately 21% after traditional urgent in situ pinning [44]. The incidence of AVN decreased to an average of 8% [44] when urgent reduction, decompression, and fixation [45, 46] or the modified Dunn procedure was used [23, 47].

This was further confirmed by the work of Sucato and his colleagues [48], who reported a greater incidence of AVN after in situ pinning of unstable SCFE, with an average rate of 23.9% compared to 16.7% after modified Dunn. Furthermore, Alshryda et al. [49], in their meta-analysis of unstable SCFE interventions, reported an AVN incidence of 33% associated with in situ pinning compared to 18% associated with the modified Dunn procedure. In contrast, stable SCFE seemingly has no inherent risk for AVN development, indicating the substantial role of surgical intervention as a risk factor [3, 5–8].

We believe that the causes of the statistical heterogeneity of the meta-analysis stemmed from both disease-related and procedure-related causes. For disease-related causes, the disease population is a very heterogeneous group with multiple factors that can contribute to the development of AVN, especially in rare clinical entities such as SCFE. Among the most important disease-related factors, besides slip stability: slip severity, slip chronicity, epiphyseal-metaphyseal discontinuity and the presence of underlying medical co-morbidity e.g. endocrinopathy. In terms of procedure-related causes, the modified Dunn procedure is a relatively new procedure, with the first case series published by the Bernese group in 2006 compared to other well-established and relatively simpler procedures, such as the Imhauser osteotomy or in situ fixation. Despite the growing popularity of the modified Dunn procedure, it remains a technically demanding procedure, which makes ensuring standardization of its steps between surgeons a very difficult point to judge.

The main limitation of our study was the limited number of included articles, with only twelve. Despite the abundance of studies addressing SCFE in the past 15 years, the effect of slip stability on the incidence of AVN has been overlooked. This led to the exclusion of a large number of studies either due to the lack of reporting of the Loder classification or only including either stable or unstable slips. Another limitation is that all of the included studies were case series with no comparative group, which in turn can be explained by the relative rarity of the condition and the complexity of the procedure, which requires a long learning curve. All of the studies included were retrospective with a high risk of selection and information bias. Finally, the lack of subgroup analysis based on possible confounding factors, such as surgical experience, limited our ability further to explore potential sources of heterogeneity in the meta-analysis.

Large cohort studies need to be conducted to fully estimate and determine the extent of the relationship between the stability of the slip and the development of AVN. This finding was supported by another systematic review by Gorgolini et al. [50], which also added another controversial aspect, the relation between the degree of slip and the development of AVN, as they proposed that the risk for AVN development after the procedure is unjustifiable in patients with mild SCFE and that it should be offered to patients with moderate or severe slips only. Additionally, more research effort should be directed towards understanding the natural history of the disease, as it is not yet fully understood.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that the risk of AVN following the modified Dunn procedure is not determined by slip stability, reinforcing the notion that AVN is a multifactorial complication with a complex and unpredictable course. Despite historical concerns, the modified Dunn procedure remains a robust and dependable surgical option for both stable and unstable SCFE when performed by experienced surgeons. And given the lower inherent risk of AVN development in stable SCFE, the modified Dunn procedure should be used with caution in this patient cohort.
