Fully Geometric Multi-Hop Reasoning on Knowledge Graphs With Transitive Relations
Fernando Zhapa-Camacho, Robert Hoehndorf

Abstract
Multi-hop logical reasoning on knowledge graphs requires faithfully mapping the logical semantics to latent space. Current geometric embedding methods show to be useful on this task by mapping entities to geometric regions and logical operations to latent transformations. While a geometric embedding can provide a direct interpretability framework for query answering, current methods have only leveraged the geometric construction of entities, failing to map logical operations to pure geometric transformations and, instead, using neural components to learn these operations. On the other hand, purely neural-based methods outperform geometric methods, but they lack interpretability in the latent space. We introduce GeometrE, a geometric embedding method for multi-hop reasoning, that maps every logical operation to a purely geometric operation in the latent space. Additionally, we introduce a transitive loss function and show that, unlike existing methods, it can preserve the logical rule ∀ a , b , c : r ⁡ ( a , b ) ∧ r ⁡ ( b , c ) → r ⁡ ( a , c ) \forall a,b,c:r(a,b)\land r(b,c)\to r(a,c) . Our experiments show that GeometrE outperforms current state-of-the-art geometric methods and remains competitive with existing neural-based methods on standard benchmark datasets.



1
Introduction

Different forms of information can be encoded in a graph-structure
manner and large knowledge graphs are found in different application
scenarios
[
6
]
. Knowledge graph completion involves
discovering facts that are not in a knowledge graph but may be added
to it
[
14
]
. Multi-hop reasoning is the task of retrieving
answers for complex queries in knowledge graphs. While symbolic
methods can be employed for this task, they are limited when knowledge
graphs contain noise or are incomplete. Therefore, embedding-based
methods were developed to generate vector representations of queries
and their answers, and use them to answer multi-hop
queries.

Complex queries involve first order logical operations such as
intersection (
\land
), union (
\lor
), negation (
\neg
) and
existential quantification (
\exists
). Existing embedding methods map
queries, answers and logical operators into the embedding space, and
rely on geometric and probabilistic spaces to learn the
embeddings. Geometric methods map queries to points
[
4
]
or
closed regions
[
17
,
24
,
5
]
and associate potential
answers via distance or membership functions, respectively. In terms
of logical operations, methods such as Query2Box uses a box
model
[
17
]
, where intersection is a closed operation. To
further incorporate other operators like negation, more recent methods
leveraged other geometries/spaces such as cones
[
24
,
5
]
or
probability distributions
[
16
]
.

Knowledge graphs represent relationships between entities. Modeling
relation properties (e.g., symmetry, invertibility, transitivity) is
another line of research when generating embeddings for knowledge
graphs. In tasks such as link prediction (1-hop queries) there is a
wide range of methods aiming to preserve relational properties

[
8
]
; however, in the case of multi-hop reasoning, only
recent studies have paid attention to relation
properties
[
5
]
.

We focus on two limitations of current work:

Geometric interpretability.

Interpretability refers to the
degree to which a model’s internal mechanisms and features can be
understood
[
3
]
.
Existing methods to model queries rely purely on neural networks to
model both queries and operators
[
11
]
, or
provide a geometric construction of queries but use neural networks to
learn the logical operations
[
17
,
16
,
24
]
. In the
second case, the interpretability degree is higher, since we can
better understand the mechanism to represent queries. But, in both
cases interpretability of the logical operators is limited as such
operators are
learned
by a neural network.

Modeling of transitive relations.

Transitive relations can
be understood in two ways: (a) the rule form

r\circ\ldots\circ r\equiv r
, where
r
composed with itself is
equivalent to
r
and (b) the logical form

\forall a,b,c:r(a,b)\land r(b,c)\to r(a,c)
where entities
a,b,c

exist in chain-like form under the relation
r
. Based on the rule
form, we can model the relation projection as an idempotent
transformation
[
18
]
and, from the perspective of the logical
form, the chain-like structure of entities can be modeled with an
order-preserving loss function such as those used to preserve
hierarchies
[
21
]
.

While some multi-hop reasoning methods can encode relation
composition
[
17
,
5
]
, they cannot model transitivity
(which is a special case of composition) because their encoding does
not preserve the idempotency property of transitive
relations. Furthermore, current methods rely on distance-based
functions to associate queries and answers, and those methods cannot
encode the chain-like form of transitive relations either.

To overcome the aforementioned limitations, we present GeometrE, a
box model for query answering. In GeometrE, we map every logical
operation to a purely geometric transformation, therefore removing the
need of neural networks and making the embedding process fully
geometrically interpretable. Additionally, we construct an idempotent
geometric operation and propose a transitive loss function to preserve
transitivity of relations in the embedding space. Our contributions
are listed below:

•

We propose a geometrically interpretable embedding method based
on box embeddings for query answering on knowledge
graphs.

•

We introduce a
multiplicative-additive projection mechanism (combining additive and
multiplicative components) that significantly enhances the
expressiveness of relation embeddings compared to purely additive
translations.

•

We design a transitive loss function that captures transitivity
in the embedding space.

•

We show empirical results across several standard benchmarks
demonstrating that our method can outperform state-of-the-art
geometric approaches and closing the gap to neural-based methods.

2
Related Work

Geometric embeddings:

Initial work on geometric embeddings
used points to model queries and answers
[
4
]
. Since queries can
contain more than one answer, modeling queries as closed
regions
[
17
,
24
,
5
]
improved the embedding quality.
Boxes
[
17
]
and cones
[
24
,
5
]
have been used to
model queries. Fundamentally, box models cannot represent negation,
since the complement of a box is not a box. Similarly, cone models
cannot exactly represent intersection because intersection of cones
might produce a set of disconnected cones
[
5
]
. We have chosen
a box model to encode queries, and implement negation as a geometric
exclusion constraint, which works in the case ‘A but not B’, which is
the negation form typically found in knowledge graph queries.

Embeddings for transitive relations.

Knowledge graph
embedding methods that encode transitive relations such as
RotPro
[
18
]
models relations as rotations and implement an
idempotent transformation by projection. Hierarchies, which are
transitive, have been modeled by several approaches, such as using
concentric circles
[
23
]
or hyperbolic spaces

[
13
]
. However, these methods are tailored for
link prediction (1-hop queries) and do not support more complex
queries because they do not provide mechanisms to compute
intersection, negation, or union of entities.

Non-geometric embeddings.

Other types of methods have been
proposed to address the multi-hop reasoning problem. Non-geometric
methods use neural approaches to model logical
operations
[
11
]
or use Large Language Models
(LLMs) to enhance query and entity representations
[
15
]
.
We exclude LLM-based methods because they operate on a fundamentally
different paradigm. While LLMs use pre-trained external knowledge
(mostly natural language), our method relies strictly on the graph
topology and logical structure. Therefore, we consider LLM-based
reasoning out of scope, as our objective is to map logical semantics
to a geometric latent space to enable interpretable reasoning.

3
Preliminaries

Knowledge Graphs.

A knowledge graph is a tuple

(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R})
, where
\mathcal{V}
is a
set of nodes,
\mathcal{R}
is a set of relation labels and

\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{V}
is a set of triples. Every element

(s_{i},r_{j},o_{k})\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{V}

can be seen as a logical binary predicate
r_{j}(s_{i},o_{k})
.

Logical queries.

Query embeddings in knowledge graphs are
represented as logical expressions using First-Order logic and involve
intersection (
\land
), union (
\lor
), negation (
\neg
) and
existential quantification (
\exists
). A first-order logic query
q

consists of a given anchor entity set

\mathcal{V}_{a}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, existentially quantified bound
variables
V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}
and a single target variable
V_{?}
which
corresponds to the query answer. We use queries in Disjunctive Normal
Form (DNF) of the form:

q[V_{?}]=V_{?}:\exists V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}:c_{1}\lor\ldots\lor c_{n}

Expressions such as
c_{i}
are conjunctions

c_{i}=e_{i1}\land\ldots\land e_{im}
, where
e_{ij}
represents a
formula or its negation.
e_{ij}=r(v_{a},V)
or
\neg(v_{a},V)
or

r(V^{\prime},V)
or
\neg r(V^{\prime},V)
and
v_{a}\in\mathcal{V}_{a}
,

V\in\{V_{?},V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\}
,
V^{\prime}\in\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}\}
,

V\neq V^{\prime}
,
r\in\mathcal{R}
.

Relational properties

Logical predicates in
\mathcal{R}
can have properties such as:
symmetry, invertibility, composition or transitivity.
A relation
r\in\mathcal{R}
is symmetric if

\forall a,b\in\mathcal{V}:r(a,b)\to r(b,a)
. Given relations

r,s\in\mathcal{R}
,
r
is the inverse of
s
if

\forall a,b\in\mathcal{V},r(a,b)\to s(b,a)
. Given relations

r,s,t
, we say
t
is the composition of
r
and
s
if

\forall a,b,c\in\mathcal{V}:r(a,b)\land s(b,c)\to t(a,c)
. A
special case of composition is transitivity, where

\forall a,b,c\in\mathcal{V}:r(a,b)\land r(b,c)\to r(a,c)
.

4
Methodology

We revisit the notion of box embeddings presented in
Query2Box
[
17
]
and propose GeometrE, a box embedding
model for queries. We acknowledge that box embeddings are not closed
under negation and this limitation was addressed by using other
geometric spaces
[
16
,
24
]
. However, we model negation as a
geometric exclusion constraint. Our proposed constraint works for the
case ‘A but not B’, which is the negation form typically found in
knowledge graph queries.

Box.

An
n
-dimensional axis-aligned box
B
is a pair

(\mbox{low}({B}),\mbox{up}({B}))
, where
\mbox{low}({B}),\mbox{up}({B})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

\mbox{low}({B})[i]\leq\mbox{up}({B})[i],1\leq i\leq n
. We call
\mbox{low}({B}),

and
\mbox{up}({B})
the
lower
and
upper
corners of
B
,
respectively. Alternatively, a box
B
can be defined as the pair

(\mbox{cen}({B}),\mbox{off}({B}))
, denoting the center and offset of
B
:

\mbox{cen}({B})=\frac{\mbox{up}({B})+\mbox{low}({B})}{2},\quad\mbox{off}({B})=\frac{\mbox{up}({B})-\mbox{low}({B})}{2},

(1)

where
\mbox{up}({B}),\mbox{low}({B}),\mbox{cen}({B}),\mbox{off}({B})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A box

E
has zero-volume when
\mbox{off}({E})={\bm{0}}
(Figure
1(a)
).

Geometric projection.

To model a relation

r\in\mathcal{R}
, we represent its embedding as a 4-tuple

\bm{r}=(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},r_{4})
. Given a box embedding
B=(\mbox{cen}({B}),\mbox{off}({B}))
, we denote
as
B^{\prime}=T_{\bm{r}}(B)
the transformation with respect to the 4-tuple

{\bm{r}}
that generates the box
B^{\prime}=(\mbox{cen}({B^{\prime}}),\mbox{off}({B^{\prime}}))
:

\mbox{cen}({B^{\prime}})=r_{1}\otimes\mbox{cen}({B})+r_{2},\quad\mbox{off}({B^{\prime}})=|r_{3}\otimes\mbox{off}({B})+r_{4}|

(2)

where
\otimes
is coordinate-wise product between vectors. While
methods such as Query2Box
[
17
]
use a projection of the
form
{\bm{r}}=({\bm{1}},v_{2},{\bm{1}},v_{4})
(with only additive components),
we implement a more expressive transformation by incorporating
multiplicative components, which improves the quality of embeddings
while maintaining geometric interpretability. The transformation
T_{r}

(i.e.,
T(r)=(T\circ T)(r)
) is idempotent in two cases: when

T_{r}=id
(the identity function) and when

T(r)=({\bm{0}},v_{2},{\bm{0}},v_{4})
(the constant function). For
formulas
r(A,B)
and
\neg r(A,B)
, we use different relation
embeddings for
r
and
\neg r
.

Geometric Intersection.

Given a set of boxes

\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}\}
, we compute the intersection box
I
as:

I=(\max(\mbox{low}({B_{1}}),\ldots,\mbox{low}({B_{n}})),\min(\mbox{up}({B_{1}}),\ldots,\mbox{up}({B_{n}})))

(3)

where
\min
and
\max
operations are performed coordinate-wise
(Figure
1(b)
).

Prior works replaced geometric intersection with neural networks
(DeepSets) to avoid vanishing gradients when boxes are disjoint

[
12
,
2
,
1
]
. In a pure volume-based
overlap, disjoint boxes yield zero signal. However, our method
remains stable because our optimization objective
(Eq.
4
) minimizes the distance between the query
box corners and the answer entity, rather than maximizing box
overlap volume. Even if two parent boxes
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are
disjoint, the resulting intersection box
I
(calculated via
coordinate-wise max and min) produces coordinates that are
effectively ‘inverted’ in the disjoint dimensions. The distance
function then remains differentiable with respect to these
corners. Consequently, the backpropagation signal effectively pulls
the boundaries of the parent boxes
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
towards the
answer
{\bm{a}}
, forcing the boxes to move until they overlap.

In terms of complexity, processing each
n
-dimensional box using,
for instance, an multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with
L
layers, input
size
n
and hidden size
d
requires
O(n\cdot d+L\cdot d^{2})

operations and the complexity of computing the intersection for
k

boxes is
O(k\cdot(n\cdot d+L\cdot d^{2}))
. Cones are represented
as two
n
-dimensional vectors
[
24
,
5
]
, the complexity of
computing intersection has the same bounds as Query2Box. On the
other hand, following the geometric definition of
Equation
3
requires
O(k\cdot n)
operations.
The geometric definition also provides direct geometric
interpretability, which is not the case with neural networks used in
previous works
[
4
,
17
,
16
,
24
,
5
]
.

Geometric negation.

The geometric complement of a box is not a box (it is the union of
open half-spaces), preventing closed-form logical negation. However,
in multi-hop reasoning, negation rarely appears in isolation (e.g.,
‘give me everything that is not a Person’); it typically appears as a
constraint upon a positive query (e.g., ‘European countries that are
not in the EU’).

We model this specific structure not as a set complement, but as a
geometric exclusion constraint. Given a positive query box
B_{p}
and
a negative query box
B_{n}
, we model the query
B_{p}\land\neg B_{n}

by enforcing the answer embeddings to minimize distance to
B_{p}
while
maximizing the distance from the interior of
B_{n}
. This avoids the
need to explicitly represent the non-box shape of
\neg B_{n}
in the
latent space.

While this design does not
support absolute complement, it effectively models the negation form
typical in KG queries (e.g., ‘A but not B’), making it highly
applicable to standard benchmarks.

4.1
Learning GeometrE embeddings

Standard distance function.

The geometric construction of
GeometrE supports queries shown in
Figure
2
. Every query anchor node
q
(blue nodes
in Figure
2
) is associated to a box embedding

{\bm{q}}=(\mbox{cen}({q}),\mbox{off}({q}))
, where
\mbox{cen}({q}),\mbox{off}({q})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
and an answer node
a
(green nodes in Figure
2
)
are associated to a zero-volume box

{\bm{a}}=(\mbox{cen}({a}),{\bm{0}})
. Intermediate nodes (gray nodes in
Figure
2
) are queries constructed using relation
projection, intersection, negation, or union operations.

A node in the graph can appear both as anchor or answer node. We
differentiate between these two cases in the embedding space and
associate a node
a
in the graph to a query embedding

{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}=(\mbox{cen}({a}),\mbox{off}({a}))
and to an answer embedding

{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}=(\mbox{cen}({a})^{\prime},{\bm{0}})
. As part of the optimization, we
enforce
\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box}}({\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}},{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}})=0
.

In the end, to associate a query embedding
{\bm{q}}
to every answer
embedding
{\bm{a}}
in the answer set
\llbracket q\rrbracket
, we use
the function:

\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box}}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})=\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{out}}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})+\alpha\cdot\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{in}}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})

(4)

where
\alpha
is a hyperparameter that helps to enforce
{\bm{a}}
to be
inside
{\bm{q}}
but not necessarily be equal to
\mbox{cen}({q})
, and:

\displaystyle\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{out}}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})

\displaystyle=||\max(\mbox{cen}({a})^{\prime}-\mbox{up}({q}),{\bm{0}})+\max(\mbox{low}({q})-\mbox{cen}({a})^{\prime},{\bm{0}})||_{1}

(5)

\displaystyle\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{in}}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})

\displaystyle=||\mbox{cen}({q})-\min(\mbox{up}({q}),\max(\mbox{low}({q}),\mbox{cen}({a})^{\prime}))||_{1}

(6)

vectors
\mbox{up}({q}),\mbox{low}({q})
are computed from
\mbox{cen}({q})
and
\mbox{off}({q})

following Equation
1
.

Negation distance function

For queries that include negation, we rely on the assumption that the
positive and negative components of the query must be disjoint. Thus,
we use Equation
4
to compute the distance to the
positive component. For the negative component of the query, we
introduce the following exclusion distance function:

\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{exclusion}}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})=-\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{out}}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})+\alpha\cdot\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{in}}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})

(7)

Unlike the standard distance function,
\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{out}}
is negated, which
enforces the answer to be outside the query box. However, we keep

\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{in}}
positive with a regularization weight
\alpha
to prevent
the answer embedding from moving arbitrarily far from the query region.

Order-preserving function

Additionally, we incorporate an order-preserving function to account
for the transitivity property of some relations. A transitive relation

r
is such that
\forall a,b,c:r(a,b)\land r(b,c)\to r(a,c)

(logic form) or
r\circ\ldots\circ r\equiv r
(rule form). In both
cases, the transformation
T_{r}
must be idempotent. Additionally, from
the logic form it follows that if

\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box}}(T_{r}({\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}),{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}})=0
and

\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box}}(T_{r}({\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}),{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}})=0
then

\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box}}(T_{r}({\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}),{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}})=0
, or approximates
to
0
. However, following Equation
4
, the embedding
model might collapse because it cannot meaningfully represent

a,b,c,r
at the same time. For example, w.l.o.g., assume

\mbox{off}({a})=\mbox{off}({b})=\mbox{off}({c})={\bm{0}}
; then, with
\bm{r}=(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},r_{4})
and

r_{4}={\bm{0}}
, we have that Equation
4
is minimized
when
r_{1}\otimes\mbox{cen}({a})+r_{2}=\mbox{cen}({b})
,

r_{1}\otimes\mbox{cen}({b})+r_{2}=\mbox{cen}({c})
,

r_{1}\otimes\mbox{cen}({a})+r_{2}=\mbox{cen}({c})
.

Therefore, to encode transitivity, we use the following
distance function for transitive relations:

\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box\_tr}}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})=\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box}}(\hat{{\bm{q}}}_{i},\hat{{{\bm{a}}}}_{i})+\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{ordering}}({\bm{q}}[i],{\bm{a}}[i])

(8)

where
\hat{{\bm{q}}}_{i},\hat{{\bm{a}}}_{i}
are projected versions of boxes
where both centers and offsets have the value at coordinate
i

removed. Additionally, at dimension
i
, we enforce an order embedding

\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{ordering}}({\bm{q}}[i],{\bm{a}}[i])=||\max(\mbox{cen}({a})^{\prime}[i]-\mbox{low}({q})[i]+\lambda,0)||_{1}

(9)

The idea behind
\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{ordering}}
is to preserve the ordered structure
of transitive chains. Thus, for our previous example,
\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box\_tr}}

might collapse in all dimensions except
i
, where
\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{ordering}}

will be minimized when
\mbox{cen}({a})>\mbox{cen}({b})^{\prime}
,
\mbox{cen}({b})>\mbox{cen}({c})^{\prime}
and

\mbox{cen}({a})>\mbox{cen}({c})^{\prime}
. In the extreme case that query boxes have volume

0
, we have that
\mbox{cen}({a})>\mbox{cen}({b})>\mbox{cen}({c})
. The parameter

\lambda
ensures a minimum distance between entities. Each
transitive relation is assigned a different projection dimension. In
the case that a transitive relation and its inverse (also transitive)
are present in the graph, the same dimension is used for both
relations and the distance function is modified to:

\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{ordering\_inverse}}({\bm{q}}[i],{\bm{a}}[i])=||\max(\mbox{up}({q})[i]-\mbox{cen}({a})^{\prime}[i]+\lambda,0)||_{1}

(10)

Since the function
\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{ordering}}
reserves specific dimensions of

\mathbb{R}^{n}
for ordering, the number of transitive relations
m
is
constrained by
m\leq n
.

Training Objective.

Given a query
q
, our training
objective aims to minimize distance between the GeometrE embedding of

q
and its answers while maximizing distance between
q
and randomly
sampled negative answers
[
19
]
:

L=-\log\sigma(\gamma-\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box\_tr}}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}))-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\sigma(\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box\_tr}}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{i})-\gamma)

(11)

where
\gamma>0
is margin hyperparameter,
k
is the number of
negative samples,
{\bm{a}}^{\prime}_{i}
is the
i
-th negative sample and

\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function.

Transitive Relation Loss.

Given a transitive relation
r
, with
embeddings
{\bm{r}}=(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},r_{4})
and projection dimension
i
,
we optimize its projected embedding
\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
to approximate the
identity function. Thus:

L_{\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}}=||\hat{r_{1}}_{i}-1||_{1}+||\hat{r_{3}}_{i}-1||_{1}+||\hat{r_{2}}_{i}||_{1}+||\hat{r_{4}}_{i}||_{1}

(12)

The modified distance function
\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box\_tr}}
together with the
transitive relation loss function
L_{{\bm{r}}_{i}}
provide guarantees that the
generated embeddings will preserve transitivity. That is, given a
transitive relation
r
, we have

\forall a,b,c\in\mathcal{V}:r(a,b)\land r(b,c)\to r(a,c)

(Theorem
4.1
).

Theorem 4.1
(Transitive Inference Property)

Let
(a,r,b),(b,r,c)\in\mathcal{E}
with
r
being a transitive
relation, but
(a,r,c)\not\in\mathcal{E}
. Let embeddings

{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}},{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}},{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}
and

{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}},{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}},{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}
for
a,b,c
and relation
embedding for
r
be optimized using the loss function

L^{\prime}=L+L_{\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}}
until convergence. Let
i
be the
dimension chosen for
r
in
\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{ordering}}
. If

\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box\_tr}}(T_{r}({\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}),{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}})=0
and

\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box\_tr}}(T_{r}({\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}),{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}})=0
, then

\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box\_tr}}(T_{r}({\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}),{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}})=0
with

{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}[i]<{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}[i]<{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}[i]
.

Proof

The minimization of
L_{\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}}
ensures that the relation
transformation
T_{r}
approaches idempotency in dimension
i
, with

\hat{r_{1}}_{i}\approx 1
,
\hat{r_{3}}_{i}\approx 1
,

\hat{r_{2}}_{i}\approx 0
, and
\hat{r_{4}}_{i}\approx 0
.

For the observed triples
(a,r,b)
and
(b,r,c)
, the distance
function
\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box\_tr}}
approaching zero implies:

1.

For dimensions other than
i
, all answer entities lie
within their corresponding query boxes after relation projection:

{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}\in T_{r}({\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}})
and

{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}\in T_{r}({\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}})

2.

For dimension
i
, the ordering constraints are satisfied:

\mbox{cen}({{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}})[i]\leq\mbox{low}({{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}})[i]-\lambda
and

\mbox{cen}({{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}})[i]\leq\mbox{low}({{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}[i]})-\lambda

From the first constraint, and given that
T_{r}
approaches an
identity transformation in dimension
i
, we have

T_{r}({\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}})[i]\approx{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}[i]
and

T_{r}({\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}})[i]\approx{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}[i]
.

The idempotency property of box transformation in non-
i
dimensions
(Equation
12
) implies that if

{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}\in T_{r}({\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}})
and

{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}\in T_{r}({\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}})
, then

{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}\in T_{r}({\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}})
for all dimensions except

i

For dimension
i
, we have:

\mbox{cen}({{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}[i]})\leq\mbox{low}({{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}[i]})-\lambda
and

\mbox{cen}({{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}[i]})\leq\mbox{low}({{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}})[i]-\lambda
.

Since every answer embedding is optimized to lie inside its query
embedding, we get:

\mbox{cen}({{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}})[i]\leq\mbox{low}({{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}})[i]-\lambda\leq\mbox{cen}({{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}})[i]
and

\mbox{cen}({{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}})[i]\leq\mbox{low}({{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}})[i]-\lambda\leq\mbox{cen}({{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}})[i]
. Thus,

\mbox{cen}({{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}})[i]\leq\mbox{low}({{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}}})[i]-\lambda\leq\mbox{cen}({{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}})[i]
, satisfying the ordering constraint for

(a,r,c)
. Therefore, we obtain

\mbox{dist}_{\scriptsize\mbox{box\_tr}}({\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize query}},{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}})\approx 0
even though

(a,r,c)\not\in\mathcal{E}
, and the embeddings maintain the
ordering

{\bm{c}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}[i]<{\bm{b}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}[i]<{\bm{a}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize answer}}[i]

We introduced GeometrE, a box model method for multi-hop reasoning on
knowledge graphs. We demonstrated that complex queries can be
constructed without learning logical operators. Instead, under
appropriate conditions, learning the query embeddings themselves is
sufficient. In our approach, these “appropriate conditions” involve
using a combination of additive and multiplicative components for
relation projection, and providing a tailored scoring function for
negation queries.

We also developed a framework to effectively model transitive
relations. While we employed a simple idempotent transformation (the
identity function), we enhanced it with a specialized loss function
that preserves the ordering nature of transitive chains. Our empirical
results confirm that this approach successfully captures the
sequential structure inherent in transitive relationships. A
limitation of GeometrE is that it reserves one dimension per
transitive relation, meaning that simply increasing the embedding size
(
n
) might not scale if the number of transitive relations (
m
) is
very large (
m>>n
). However, under some conditions, dimensions can
be reused. If two transitive relations have disjoint domains and
ranges, they can share the same ordering dimension without
interference. While this approach relies on domain/range knowledge
(the disjointness condition), it relaxes the constraint
m\leq n
and
allows the method to scale to knowledge graphs with more transitive
relations than dimensions.

Furthermore, our method can outperform existing
geometric baselines and approach the performance of purely neural-based
methods, while providing geometric interpretability. A promising
direction for future research would be to investigate different
transformation types and loss functions in alternative geometric
spaces to further improve the representation of relational properties.

7
Conclusion

We present GeometrE, a method to perform multi-hop reasoning by
embedding queries to boxes and mapping logical operators to purely
geometric operations. We additionally introduced a transitive loss
function that preserves the ordered structure of transitive
relationships. Our experiments show that GeometrE outperforms baselines
on diverse types of knowledge graph queries.

Appendix 0.A
Complement Intersection

Lemma 1
(Complement Intersection)

Let
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
be two
n
-dimensional boxes such that

B_{1}\cap B_{2}=\emptyset
. Also, we define the complement of
B_{1}
, denoted
as
\bar{B}_{1}
, as:

\bar{B}_{1}=\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:{\bm{x}}\notin B_{1}\}

Then, the intersection of the complement
of
B_{1}
, denoted as
\bar{B}_{1}
, with
B_{2}
is
B_{2}
itself:

\bar{B}_{1}\cap B_{2}=B_{2}

Proof

Let
B_{1}=(\mbox{low}({B_{1}}),\mbox{up}({B_{1}}))
and

B_{2}=(\mbox{low}({B_{2}}),\mbox{up}({B_{2}}))
be two
n
-dimensional boxes. Since

B_{1}\cap B_{2}=\emptyset
, there exists at least one dimension

j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
such that either

\mbox{up}({B_{1}})[j]<\mbox{low}({B_{2}})[j]
or
\mbox{up}({B_{2}})[j]<\mbox{low}({B_{1}})[j]
.

This means that for any point
{\bm{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

{\bm{p}}\in\bar{B}_{1}
if and only if there exists at least one
dimension
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
such that

{\bm{p}}[i]<\mbox{low}({B_{1}})[i]
or
{\bm{p}}[i]>\mbox{up}({B_{1}})[i]
. For any point

{\bm{q}}\in B_{2}
, we have
\mbox{low}({B_{2}})[i]\leq{\bm{q}}[i]\leq\mbox{up}({B_{2}})[i]

for all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Since

B_{1}\cap B_{2}=\emptyset
, for any point
{\bm{q}}\in B_{2}
,

{\bm{q}}\notin B_{1}
, which means
{\bm{q}}\in\bar{B}_{1}
. Therefore, every
point in
B_{2}
is also in
\bar{B}_{1}
, implying that

B_{2}\subseteq\bar{B}_{1}\cap B_{2}
. Conversely, any point in

\bar{B}_{1}\cap B_{2}
must be in
B_{2}
, so

\bar{B}_{1}\cap B_{2}\subseteq B_{2}
.

Combining both directions, we have
\bar{B}_{1}\cap B_{2}=B_{2}
.
