From Traces to Program Incorrectness: A Type-Theoretic Approach Conference: A Boilerplate Conference; January 0000;
Yongwei Yuan, Zhe Zhou, Julia Belyakova, Benjamin Delaware, Suresh Jagannathan

Abstract
. We present a type-theoretic framework for reasoning about incorrectness in functional programs that interact with effectful, opaque library APIs. Our approach centers on traces – temporally-ordered sequences of library API invocations – which naturally characterize both the preconditions of individual APIs and their composite behavior. We represent these traces using symbolic regular expressions (SREs), enabling formal specification of incorrect abstract data type (ADT) behaviors across function boundaries. The core contribution is a novel type inference algorithm that operates modulo specified incorrectness properties and leverages the symbolic finite automata (SFAs) representations of regexes for compositional reasoning of traces. When the algorithm succeeds, the inferred types witness that an ADT implementation can exhibit some subset of the specified incorrect behaviors. This represents the first systematic approach to underapproximate reasoning against trace-based incorrectness specifications, enabling a new form of trace-guided compositional analysis.



1.
Traces as Incorrectness Specifications

Traces of events are a popular and effective formalism for modeling and reasoning about the behaviors of effectful functional programs

[
4
,
5
,
3
,
8
]
.

Global
safety properties of such programs can be captured as desirable sequences of temporally-ordered program events that cross function boundaries. While such specifications are expressive and amenable to a variety of reasoning techniques, formulating safety properties in terms of traces can involve non-trivial effort.

Consider a linked-list implementation that maintains the ordering
relation among nodes in the list using an effectful key-value store
(named
Nxt
). The key-value store provides two APIs,

\texttt{put}:\mathsf{addr}{\shortrightarrow}\mathsf{addr}{\shortrightarrow}\mathsf{unit}
and

\texttt{get}:\mathsf{addr}{\shortrightarrow}\mathsf{addr}
, where type
\mathsf{addr}

denotes the addresses of nodes. A safety property concerning the
protocol between the linked-list and its underlying
Nxt
store
states that each node must have a unique parent at all time. Such a
property can be expressed in terms of temporally-ordered sequences of
API invocations, namely
traces
; any
Nxt.put
event that
links node
a
to node
b
, denoted by
\langle\texttt{Nxt.put}~a~b\rangle
,
can be followed by sequences of events that does not link nodes other
than
a
to
b
, denoted by

({\backsim}\langle\texttt{Nxt.put}~\cancel{a}~b\rangle)^{*}
, or does not link
nodes other than
a
to
b
until
b
is unlinked from
a
, denoted by

\langle\texttt{Nxt.put}~a~\cancel{b}\rangle
.

One may attempt to regard this unique-parent safety property as a
representation invariant that holds at any program point

[
8
]
and represent it as a regular
expression (regex)

(1)

\bullet^{*}\ \langle\texttt{put}~a~b\rangle\ (({\backsim}\langle\texttt{put}~\cancel{a}~b\rangle)^{*}\ \vee\\
\left(({\backsim}\langle\texttt{put}~\cancel{a}~b\rangle)^{*}\ \langle\texttt{put}~a~\cancel{b}\rangle\ \bullet^{*}\right))

stating that for all pairs of nodes
a
and
b
, all traces accepted
by the regex (with the name of key-value stores omitted for
simplicity) is safe. The statement, unfortunately, is not true, as
illustrated by an unsafe trace that
Eq.

1

unintentionally accepts:

(2)

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\langle\texttt{put}~1~2\rangle\ \langle\texttt{put}~3~2\rangle}\ \langle\texttt{put}~3~4\rangle\ \langle\texttt{put}~1~2\rangle

where we represent node addresses using intergers for ease of
discussion. It is obvious that node
1
and
3
can both be the
parent of node
2
at the same time within

Eq.

2
, violating the safety property. Checking
that
Eq.

2
is eliminated from a hand-written
safety specification like
Eq.

1
, on the other hand,
requires us to consider

(1)

all pairs of
a
and
b
and

(2)

all sorts of events that may precede the

\langle\texttt{put}~a~b\rangle
of our interest as the leading
\bullet^{*}

accepts all prefix traces.

Among other choices, only when
a
is
1
,
b
is
2
, and the last

\langle\texttt{put}~1~2\rangle
matches
\langle\texttt{put}~a~b\rangle
, does

Eq.

1
accept
Eq.

2
. Jumping
through such hoops of
universal
reasoning and coming up with a
regex that
precisely
captures the safety property deems
difficult.

One resort to the universal reasoning problem is a manual
compositional approach where user provide a pair of regexes for each
method under analysis, denoting the required context under which the
method can be invoked and the allowed effect the method can produce
respectively
[
6
]
. For a
method that manipulates an existing linked list, say removing a node
with a given payload from the list, it is reasonable to consider any
existing pair of linked nodes
a
and
b
prior to the method
invocation, as specified by

(
\mathcal{A}_{a\curvearrowright b}
)

\bullet^{*}\ \langle\texttt{put}~a~b\rangle\ ({\backsim}\langle\texttt{put}~a~\_\rangle)^{*}

and check that the set of traces admissible by

({\backsim}\langle\texttt{put}~\cancel{a}~b\rangle)^{*}\ \vee\\
\left(({\backsim}\langle\texttt{put}~\cancel{a}~b\rangle)^{*}\ \langle\texttt{put}~a~\cancel{b}\rangle\ \bullet^{*}\right)

includes those that can be produced by the method. The context regex

\mathcal{A}_{a\curvearrowright b}

helps fixate the linked pairs of nodes whose
unique-parent property is to be checked, alleviating the burden of
universal reasoning. However, this decomposition not only requires
user to specialize the safety property to each method but also is
subject to changes stemmed from the safety property or the
implementation, which is cumbersome at best.

To address these challenges, we exploit the dual of the unique-parent
property and formulate its incorrectness specification, also as a
regex:

(
\mathcal{A}_{a\curvearrowright b\curvearrowleft\cancel{a}}
)

\bullet^{*}\ \langle\texttt{put}~a~b\rangle\ ({\backsim}\langle\texttt{put}~a~\_\rangle)^{*}\langle\texttt{put}~\cancel{a}~b\rangle\ \bullet^{*}

This regex serves as an abstraction pattern that captures a family of
traces witnessing violations of the safety property and identifies a
node being linked to at least two parents. In particular, the pattern
abstract over:

(1)

node addresses, where
a
and
b
represent arbitrary nodes
linked via
\langle\texttt{put}~a~b\rangle
,

(2)

temporal context, where
\bullet^{*}
represents arbitrary events before and after the violation, and

(3)

intermediate operations, where
({\backsim}\langle\texttt{put}~a~\_\rangle)^{*}
represents any sequence of operations that do no intervene with the link from
a
to
b
.

Take
Eq.

2
as an example, its first two events
(highlighted in red) match the incorrectness pattern –

\langle\texttt{put}~a~b\rangle
followed by

\langle\texttt{put}~\cancel{a}~b\rangle
, with
1
,
2
, and
3

instantiating
a
,
b
, and
\cancel{a}
respectively. This
exemplifies our
existential
reasoning principle: for any unsafe
behavior, the existence of corresponding traces matching the
abstraction pattern suffices to demonstrate that the behavior is
accounted for. By shifting from universal reasoning over safety
specifications to existential reasoning over our incorrectness
pattern, it becomes easier to obtain a precise characterization of
exactly the unsafe behaviors one wish to detect.

2.
Trace-Augmented Refinement Types as Incorrectness Witnesses

In this work, we propose a type-theoretic approach for searching
witnesses to this novel form of trace-based incorrectness
specifications. Inspired by the recent work on Incorrectness Logic
(IL)
[
2
]
, our type system views types
through the lens of
must
-style underapproximate reasoning,
ensuring that types serve as true witnesses to program behaviors. One
example of
must
-style types is Coverage Type

[
7
]
– an underapproximate refinement type
denoting a set of values the associated term must be able to produce:

\mathrel{{\vdash}}1:{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{int}\mid\nu=1}\right]}\mathrel{{\vdash}\hskip-5.49995pt{\color[rgb]{0.65,0,0}\text{\tt/}}\hskip 0.61115pt}1:{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{int}}\right]}

To track effectful behaviors in programs, we again leverage traces. A
trace of
put
events with their arguments properly qualified,
e.g.,
Eq.

2
, simply underapproximates the effect
of a program that can perform these
put
operations in sequence.
We additionally keeps track of the traces of events those operations
are conditioned upon before their invocations. Consider a
get

operation, as long as the trace leading to the operation correctly
associates argument
k
with some value denoted by a ghost variable

v
(i.e., admissible by
\mathcal{A}_{k\curvearrowright v}

), its effect is exactly
captured by a singleton event
\langle v{\leftarrow}\texttt{get}~k\rangle
:

\texttt{get}:v{:}\mathsf{addr}{\dasharrow}k{:}\mathsf{addr}{\shortrightarrow}{[}\hyperref@@ii[link-a-to-b]{\mathcal{A}_{k\curvearrowright v}}{]}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathsf{addr}\mid\nu=v}\right]}{[}\langle v{\leftarrow}\texttt{get}~k\rangle{]}

The elimination of such function types allows us to instantiate not
only the parameter but also the ghost variable and the context, thus
underapproximating the behavior specified. For example, we may assume
that the last event is a corresponding
put
storing
1
and
then the invocation of
get
on
1
can be typed as:

\mathrel{{\vdash}}\texttt{get}~1:{[}\bullet^{*}\langle\texttt{put}~1~1\rangle{]}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu=1}\right]}{[}\langle 1{\leftarrow}\texttt{get}~1\rangle{]}

Such types, when composed together, may serve as witnesses that the
program is incorrect provided that the traces within the types overlap
with the set of traces satisfying the incorrectness specification.
This is in contrast to trace-based verification techniques

[
4
,
1
,
5
,
3
]

where an overapproximation of effects is inferred and its inclusion in
the safety property is checked. Underapproximate reasoning, unlike
overapproximate reasoning, is inherently non-deterministic; we may
need to blindly enumerate different underapproximate types before
finding the one that indeed witnesses the incorrectness.

3.
Algorithmic Incorrectness Witness Synthesis via Type
Inference

To reduce the inherent non-determinism in underapproximate reasoning,
we develop a type inference algorithm that harnesses the incorrectness
specification and derives only types that concern the incorrectness.
Consider the following program that manipulates a linked list via
operations to the key-value store
Nxt
:

(
e_{\textsf{bad}}
)

{\color[rgb]{0.2461,0.4336,0.6602}\texttt{let}}\ n_{1}=\texttt{get}~n_{0}\ {\color[rgb]{0.2461,0.4336,0.6602}\texttt{in}}\,{\color[rgb]{0.2461,0.4336,0.6602}\texttt{let}}\ n_{2}=\texttt{get}~n_{1}\ {\color[rgb]{0.2461,0.4336,0.6602}\texttt{in}}\,\texttt{put}~n_{0}~n_{2}

The incorrectness specification

A

a
↷
b
↶

a

can be naturally
decomposed into

A

↷
a
b

followed by

A

↶
b

a

,
defined as:

(
\mathcal{A}_{b\curvearrowleft\cancel{a}}
)

({\backsim}\langle\texttt{put}~a~\_\rangle)^{*}\langle\texttt{put}~\cancel{a}~b\rangle\ \bullet^{*}

Here we use regexes for simplicity while our inference algorithm
operates over their SRE representations of regexes whose edges are
labeled by qualified events as in regexes; any state in the SFA

Before type inference, we hypothesize that that some trace admissible
by

A

↷
a
b

allows the execution of

e
bad

to produce some other trace admissible by

↶
b

The hypothesized context enables intelligent underapproximation of
traces that may reach the program. For example, the first
get
’s
behavior is known under the context
\mathcal{A}_{n_{0}\curvearrowright n_{1}}

where we
simply let
n_{1}
be the ghost variable denoting the stored value. As
our hypothesis is only interested in the program’s behavior under the
context

A

↷
a
b

, we underapproximate the

behavior by finding the overlap between

A

↷
a
b

and

algorithm.

a{:}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\top_{\mathsf{addr}}}\right]},b{:}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu\neq a}\right]},n_{0}{:}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\top_{\mathsf{addr}}}\right]},n_{1}{:}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\top_{\mathsf{addr}}}\right]}\mathrel{{\vdash}}\\

(\hyperref@@ii[link-a-to-b]{\mathcal{A}_{a\curvearrowright b}}\wedge\hyperref@@ii[link-a-to-b]{\mathcal{A}_{n_{0}\curvearrowright n_{1}}})\langle n_{1}{\leftarrow}\texttt{get}~n_{0}\rangle

a{:}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\top_{\mathsf{addr}}}\right]},b{:}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu\neq a}\right]},n_{0}{:}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu\neq a}\right]},n_{1}{:}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu=a}\right]},n_{2}{:}{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,1}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu=b}\right]}\mathrel{{\vdash}}\\

Intuitively, our type inference algorithm facilitates a trace-guided
compositional reasoning of both program behaviors and the

4.
Conclusion

We advocate the adoption of incorrectness reasoning principal for
trace-based specification as it enables existential and local
reasoning of patterns in regex-like specification language. We
further propose a type inference algorithm that automatically
decomposes this novel form of trace-based incorrectness specification
and derive composable type-based witnesses to the incorrectness from
subprograms. As a result, the types inferred from programs correctly
witness their incorrect behavior.
