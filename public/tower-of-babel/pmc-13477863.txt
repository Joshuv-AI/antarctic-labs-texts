Identification of Mitochondrial-Related Signature in Allergic Rhinitis: Novel Insights from Bioinformatics and Machine Learning
Authors: Meili Nuerbolati, Jiang Su, Huajie Yuan, Song Wang, Yuping Yang, Lingling Wang, Yan Wang, Qingping Liu, Hua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAllergic rhinitis (AR) is an immune-mediated inflammatory disease of the nasal mucosa. Numerous studies have demonstrated correlations between AR and mitochondrial dysfunction. The present study was designed to explore mitochondrial-related genes associated with AR.MethodsThe differentially expressed mitochondrial-related genes (DEMRGs) were obtained by intersecting differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and mitochondrial-related genes. Further screening of hub DEMRGs was conducted using three machine learning algorithms. The CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to characterize immune cell infiltration and investigate interactions between the hub DEMRGs and immune cells. Ultimately, the expression levels of hub DEMRGs were confirmed in external datasets and clinical data from human nasal mucosa.ResultsIn total, 12 DEMRGs were identified, and using machine-learning algorithms, two hub DEMRGs (SLC25A36 and RNASEH1) were significantly downregulated in AR samples, indicating good diagnostic potential. These hub DEMRGs were closely associated with the infiltration of activated CD4+ memory T cells, resting dendritic cells, M0 macrophages, and resting NK cells. Validation using external datasets and experiments confirmed that the two hub DEMRGs were downregulated in the AR group, consistent with the bioinformatics analysis.ConclusionBy characterizing both mitochondrial-related genes and patterns of immune cell infiltration in AR, this study suggested a potential link to AR pathogenesis and identified candidate targets for future therapeutic exploration.Keywords: allergic rhinitis, mitochondria, machine-learning, immune infiltration, transcriptomics

Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is a chronic, non-contagious inflammatory condition of the nasal mucosa triggered by immunoglobulin E (IgE).1 It has grown into an important issue for global public health as its prevalence keeps rising every year.2 The pathogenesis of AR is multifaceted, with a complex interplay among environmental factors, immunological dysregulation, genetic predisposition, and inflammatory responses.3 Pollen, dust mites, and animal dander are common allergens that can trigger AR, with typical symptoms including paroxysmal sneezing, nasal itching, rhinorrhea, and nasal congestion. Ocular symptoms, including conjunctivitis, are also highly prevalent.1 Furthermore, persistent and severe AR is associated with an elevated risk of developing asthma.4 At present, the main treatment methods for AR include drug therapy, such as intranasal glucocorticoids, antihistamines, and leukotriene receptor antagonists, as well as surgical treatment and allergen-specific immunotherapy.5 Although these approaches can effectively alleviate symptoms, conventional pharmacotherapy is associated with notable adverse effects, such as epistaxis,6 rhinitis medicamentosa,7 sedation, palpitations, and other cardiovascular discomfort.8,9 In addition, traditional treatment regimens are limited by unstable therapeutic effects and a high recurrence rate, the condition of most AR patients still cannot be adequately controlled.1 Therefore, further clarifying the upstream regulatory mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of AR and screening and identifying novel specific biomarkers are of crucial significance for the development of more targeted precision treatment strategies.

Mitochondria, as central regulators of energy metabolism, redox balance, and immune homeostasis, primarily produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP) through oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), supplying energy for normal cellular physiological activities and pathological tissue repair processes.10 Meanwhile, mitochondria produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) and modulate the intracellular redox state.11 In recent years, numerous studies have indicated that mitochondrial dysfunction plays a crucial role in the pathophysiological progression of AR.12,13 As a typical allergic disease, AR can induce systemic oxidative stress.14 At the same time, excessive ROS are the primary drivers of mitochondrial membrane damage, inhibition of respiratory chain function, and abnormal mitophagy.11 In the pathogenesis of AR, allergen exposure can disrupt mitochondrial ROS homeostasis, induce oxidative stress in nasal mucosal epithelial cells, and ultimately lead to mitochondrial DNA damage and subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction. This cascade triggers biomolecular damage, cell death, and chronic inflammation, forming a vicious cycle of “mitochondrial dysfunction-ROS production-inflammation amplification”.15 Meanwhile, mitochondrial dysfunction and elevated ROS will further impair the integrity of the nasal mucosal epithelial barrier, significantly exacerbating allergic inflammatory responses.16 High-throughput genomic studies suggest that nuclear-encoded mitochondrial-related genes and mitochondrial metabolic pathways serve as critical regulatory mechanisms in the pathogenesis of AR, and mitochondrial dysfunction can promote the development of allergic phenotypes through inflammatory responses.17 Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have further confirmed that mitochondrial-related genes are important susceptible loci for AR, providing vital genetic evidence for the involvement of mitochondria in AR pathogenesis.18 Although the role of mitochondria in AR has attracted increasing attention, most existing studies focus on the experimental verification of single genes or signaling pathways. Systematic screening and identification of mitochondrial characteristic molecules associated with AR at the transcriptome level remain lacking. Further in-depth investigations are warranted to elucidate their precise regulatory mechanisms and explore their potential clinical values.

To clarify the underlying molecular causes of AR, this study used transcriptomic analysis to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between patients and healthy controls. Based on the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and MitoCarta3.0 databases, the current work employed machine learning algorithms to systematically screen for and identify hub genes associated with mitochondria. This study explored the functional roles of hub genes in AR and analyzed their correlations with immune cell infiltration. Our findings advance the understanding of AR pathophysiology and provide a critical foundation for identifying translational biomarkers and therapeutic targets for this disease.

Materials and Methods

Data Acquisition

The gene expression data of AR were downloaded from the GEO public database. Using “allergic rhinitis” as the search keyword, the following criteria were applied: clearly divided into the AR group and the healthy control group; each dataset contained at least 10 samples; and raw data or matrix files from transcriptomic microarrays were available in the GEO database. Finally, the following datasets were selected for subsequent analysis. The GSE43523 dataset was annotated based on the GPL6883 platform and contained nasal epithelial tissue samples from 7 patients with AR and 5 healthy controls. It served as the training set to identify hub genes related to AR. The GSE184382 dataset was generated using the GPL21185 platform and comprised 16 AR patients and 8 non-allergic healthy controls, which was used as the validation set. In addition, the GSE297921 dataset was also obtained in this study for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis.

DEGs Analysis

We processed the expression microarray data using the R package “limma” for normalization, correction, and annotation, as well as identification of DEGs between the AR and normal mucosal tissues (|log2 Fold Change (FC)| > 0.5 and P-value < 0.05). The resulting DEGs were visualized using the R packages “ggplot2” and “pheatmap”, producing a volcano plot and a heatmap.

Identification of Differentially Expressed Mitochondrial-Related Genes (DEMRGs) and Enrichment Analysis

A total of 1136 mitochondrial-related genes were obtained from the human MitoCarta3.0 database.19 The DEMRGs were obtained via crossing the DEGs with mitochondrial-related genes, which were visualized using a Venn diagram. Afterward, they were visualized as a Heatmap with the R package “ComplexHeatmap”. Gene Ontology (GO) annotation analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed on DEMRGs with the R package “clusterProfiler”. In addition, the SangerBox (http://www.sangerbox.com/tool) online analytical tool was used to analyze and visualize mitochondrial pathways from MitoCarta3.0.

Identification of Hub DEMRGs

Based on the DEMRGs obtained from the above analysis, three machine learning algorithms were used to identify hub DEMRGs: least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), and random forest (RF). LASSO is a regression-based method that improves model interpretability and predictive ability. Ten-fold cross-validation was used to determine the optimal λ, setting the minimum λ as the optimal value.20 SVM-RFE is a feature selection algorithm that iteratively removes features with the lowest ranking scores.21 RF, built on decision tree theory, is commonly used to enhance model performance.22 The three algorithms were analyzed using the R packages “glmnet”, “e1071”, “caret”, and “randomForest”. Hub DEMRGs obtained by intersecting the outputs of the three algorithms and then visualized in a Venn diagram.

Assessment of Hub DEMRGs’ Diagnostic Ability

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the hub DEMRGs were generated with the “pROC” R package, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) for individual biomarkers was computed to further demonstrate the diagnostic effectiveness of characteristic genes in differentiating the AR group from the control group.

Construction of Transcription Factors (TFs)-Hub DEMRGs-miRNAs-lncRNAs Network

To explore the regulatory mechanisms of hub DEMRGs, the ENCORI (starBase) database (http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/) was used to predict TFs, miRNAs, and lncRNAs that interact with the hub genes. Subsequently, a regulatory network was constructed and visualized using the “ggalluvia” R package.

Immune Infiltration Analysis

To better compare immune cell profiles between the AR and control groups, the CIBERSORT algorithm was used to estimate differential infiltration abundances of 22 immune cell types.23 This algorithm is a common tool that evaluates the composition and abundance of immune cells from a standardized gene expression matrix using a linear deconvolution approach. Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to investigate correlations among immune cell types and associations between hub DEMRGs and immune cell populations.

Validation of Hub DEMRGs in External Datasets

Dataset GSE184382 was used to further validate the expression levels of hub DEMRGs in AR, and ROC curves were generated to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of these genes. Furthermore, the scRNA-seq dataset GSE297921 was used to evaluate the expression levels of hub DEMRGs in differential cell types using the “Seurat” R package.24 After quality control and normalization, only cells with gene counts ranging from 200 to 8000 and a mitochondrial gene proportion below 20% were kept. The remaining high-quality cells were normalized using the “LogNormalize” function.25 The processed data were further analyzed using principal component analysis, Harmony-based integration, and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for dimensionality reduction, batch effect correction, and visualization. Differential expression analysis was performed using the “FindAllMarkers” function (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) with thresholds of |log2FC|≥0.25 and adjusted P-value < 0.05.

Clinical Data

This study involved the use of human tissue samples. All procedures were conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This research was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (K202503-56), and each participant has completed a written informed consent form. The inclusion criteria for AR patients were formulated in accordance with the 2022 Revised Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Allergic Rhinitis. Exclusion criteria for patients in the AR group and the control group: (1) Patients with severe asthma, severe allergic reactions, a history of organ transplantation, pregnancy, malignant tumors, hepatic and renal insufficiency, or hematological diseases. (2) All participants in both groups have resided locally for at least three consecutive years with similar living environments and dietary habits. 10 AR patients and 10 controls were enrolled in this study. There was no marked variation in sex and age between the groups (Supplementary Table 1). All nasal mucosal tissues used in this study were obtained from discarded surgical specimens from patients with nasal septal deviation accompanied by turbinate hypertrophy or concha bullosa.

Reverse Transcription Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‒qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from human nasal mucosa samples using an RNA extraction kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Subsequently, the RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green I dye (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). All procedures were carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The specific primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R (version 4.5.1). For comparisons between samples from two groups, the t-test was applied to data that met the assumption of normality, while one-way ANOVA was used for multiple-group comparisons.

The DEGs were visualized using a Volcano Plot and a Heatmap (Figure 2A and B).

Identification of DEMRGs and Functional Enrichment Analysis

A total of 12 DEMRGs were identified from the intersection of the MitoCarta3.0 database and DEGs (Figure 3A). The DEMRGs were visualized as a heatmap in Figure 3B, which showed a substantial difference between AR patients and controls. Subsequently, the DEMRGs were further processed for GO functional annotation analysis, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, and MitoCarta3.0 pathway enrichment analysis. The GO analysis showed that the DEMRGs were significantly enriched in Biological Process terms related to small molecule and acyl-CoA metabolism. Cellular Component terms included the cell division process and the TCA cycle enzyme complexes. The significantly enriched Molecular Function terms included oxidoreductase activity process (Figure 3C). These results suggested that DEMRGs may play a role in coupling energy metabolism with cell division. Furthermore, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the DEMRGs were predominantly enriched in pathways related to carbon metabolism, glucose metabolism, and glyceride metabolism (Figure 3D). Additionally, MitoCarta3.0 pathway enrichment analysis indicated that the DEMRGs were significantly enriched in mitochondrial pathways related to Nucleotide metabolism, Lysine metabolism, and SLC25A family functions (Figure 3E).

Screening of Hub DEMRGs

To improve the stability and accuracy of feature gene identification, we applied three machine learning methods—LASSO regression (Figure 4A and B), SVM-RFE (Figure 4C and D), and RF algorithm (Figure 4E and F)—to the 12 DEMRGs. By intersecting the results of the three algorithms with a Venn diagram, we obtained two hub DEMRGs: SLC25A36 and RNASEH1 (Figure 4G). The box plot in Figure 4H presented the expression levels of hub DEMRGs in the training set, RNASEH1 and SLC25A36 were significantly downregulated in AR samples. ROC curves were used to assess the diagnostic efficacy of hub DEMRGs (Figure 4I). The AUC values of SLC25A36 and RNASEH1 were 0.943 and 0.914, respectively. All the above results indicate that these biomarkers may play a potential role in AR.

Regulatory Network of TFs-Hub DEMRGs-miRNAs-lncRNAs

To investigate the possible mechanisms underlying the dysregulated expression of these feature genes, the upstream regulation of the hub DEMRGs was explored by predicting associated TFs, miRNAs, and lncRNAs (Figure 5). Within this network, 14 TFs were identified. Among them, two hub DEMRGs could be simultaneously regulated by the TFs such as KLF6, SP1, HDAC2, MGA, MYC, and MAX. Subsequently, 13 miRNAs were found to interact with the feature genes. The network also comprised 5 lncRNAs, among which ZFAS1 and XIST interacted with a greater number of miRNAs.

Immune Infiltration Analysis

The CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to analyze infiltration of 22 immune cell types in AR, aiming to delineate immune cell characteristics, explore immune regulatory mechanisms, and determine correlations between diagnostic biomarkers and immune cell infiltration. The ridgeline map in Figure 6A illustrated the proportions of infiltrating immune cells in each sample. Specifically, the proportion of activated CD4⁺ memory T cells and resting dendritic cells was abundant in the AR group, these cell types are known to be involved in allergic inflammatory responses. In comparison, M0 macrophages and resting NK cells were more abundant in the control group (Figure 6B). Additionally, the correlations among immune cells were further explored (Figure 6C). Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between the hub DEMRGs and immune cells. In particular, SLC25A36 showed positive associations with memory B cells, while being negatively associated with naive CD4⁺ T cells and resting dendritic cells (Figure 6D–F and H). RNASEH1 was positively associated with M2 macrophages (Figure 6G and I).

Validation of Hub DEMRGs

To further validate hub DEMRGs, the expression levels of the SLC25A36 and RNASEH1 were verified in the validation dataset GSE184382. SLC25A36 and RNASEH1 were both significantly downregulated in AR samples, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance (Figure 7A). ROC curve analysis showed that the AUC for SLC25A36 was 0.914, and RNASEH1 had an AUC of 0.797 (Figure 7B). Furthermore, scRNA-seq results for AR showed that cells in AR samples were separated into 12 cell subgroups (Figure 7C). SLC25A36 is mainly distributed in B cells, and RNASEH1 is expressed at low levels in various cell types with relatively stable expression levels (Figure 7D). The expression levels of SLC25A36 and RNASEH1 were both significantly downregulated in the AR group (Figure 7E).

(C) Annotation results of cell subgroups in the scRNA-seq data of GSE297921 by the UMAP map. (D) Bubble plot of hub DEMRGs in cell subgroups. (E) Expression of hub DEMRGs between groups in scRNA-seq analysis. (F) Validation of hub DEMRGs expression by RT-qPCR. (****, P-value<0.0001).

Verification of Hub DEMRGs in Clinical Samples

To verify the differential expression of the hub DEMRGs in clinical samples, human nasal mucosa samples were used to assess gene expression levels by RT-qPCR. The expression trends of SLC25A36 and RNASEH1 were consistent with those observed in public databases, with both genes showing lower expression levels in the AR group (Figure 7F).

Discussion

AR is a highly prevalent allergic disease, with a progressively increasing incidence and economic burden. Nevertheless, current clinical treatments often fail to provide satisfactory outcomes due to the complexity of their pathogenesis. Damage to nasal epithelial cells is a fundamental factor contributing to AR. Epithelial damage impairs the barrier function of the nasal mucosa and may also induce or exacerbate inflammatory responses.26,27 Mitochondrial dysfunction can further exacerbate epithelial barrier damage and inflammatory responses in AR, but its underlying mechanism remains unclear.13,15 Against this background, it is imperative to fully understand the vital role and molecular processes of mitochondrial dysfunction in AR.

Using bioinformatics methods, the present study identified DEGs of AR from the GEO database. To obtain DEMRGs, the MitoCarta3.0 database was intersected with DEGs, yielding 12 DEMRGs. Then, GO, KEGG, and MitoCarta3.0 pathway enrichment analyses indicate that the DEMRGs were primarily involved in small molecule metabolism, amino acid metabolism, the TCA cycle, and the acyl-CoA metabolic process. Subsequently, the hub DEMRGs SLC25A36 and RNASEH1 were screened out through machine learning methods, and a diagnostic model for AR demonstrated good diagnostic performance. Through verification using external datasets and laboratory experiments, the hub DEMRGs exhibited a consistent expression trend similar to that detected in the previous bioinformatics analysis.

Solute Carrier Family 25 Member 36 (SLC25A36), also known as the mitochondrial carrier family, plays a significant role in several metabolic pathways.28 SLC25A36 encodes pyrimidine nucleotide carrier 2, which is responsible for transporting pyrimidine and guanine nucleotides across the inner mitochondrial membrane. This protein participates in the maintenance of the mitochondrial genome, the regulation of mitochondrial membrane potential, and mitochondrial respiration, further promotes cellular energy generation.29,30 Recent findings have proposed that the downregulation of SLC25A36 in mouse embryonic stem cells results in mitochondrial DNA depletion and leads to mitochondrial dysfunction.31 It is important to note that mutations in the SLC25 family are associated with mitochondrial dysfunction in cancer and with various human metabolic diseases, neurodegenerative diseases, and muscle diseases.32–34 SLC25A36 is involved in mitochondrial metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction resulting from its malfunction may amplify local inflammatory signals, suggesting that SLC25A36 may be implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of AR.

Ribonuclease H1 (RNASEH1) is an endonuclease with dual cellular localization, it is primarily localized in the nucleus but can also be detected in mitochondria.35 RNASEH1 is responsible for cutting the unscheduled RNA-DNA hybrids (R-loops), contributes to the stability of cell nuclear genomic DNA and mitochondrial DNA.36 Studies have shown that loss of RNASEH1 in mice leads to mitochondrial DNA depletion, induces apoptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction, and results in embryonic developmental arrest.37 In our study, RNASEH1 expression was downregulated in AR epithelial cells, which may lead to the accumulation of R-loops, inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and ultimately increasing the risk of AR. Previous studies have shown that oxidative stress‑induced 8‑oxoguanine accumulation impairs the recruitment of RNASEH1 to mitochondrial DNA.38 Aeroallergens can increase oxidative stress and ROS production, disrupt the stability of the respiratory chain complex, and contribute to allergic inflammation.15 Therefore, it is plausible that oxidative stress inhibits RNASEH1 recruitment in AR. Recent studies have demonstrated that exposure to polyvinyl chloride (PVC) nanoplastics exacerbates asthma inflammation by downregulating RNASEH1, which promotes R-loop accumulation and activation of the cGAS-STING pathway in macrophages.39 Given that asthma and AR are part of a continuum of the same airway inflammation,40 this finding provides an important direction for us to understand that the downregulation of RNASEH1 in AR may exacerbate airway inflammation through this signaling axis.

Immune cells have occupied a pivotal role in the progression of AR.41,42 In this study, the CIBERSORT algorithm was used to characterize immune cell infiltration in nasal mucosal tissues from AR patients. We have found that, compared with the control group, the AR group showed significant differences in immune cell infiltration, including activated CD4+ memory T cells and resting dendritic cells. Dendritic cells are immunological sentinels of the nasal mucosal epithelial barrier, they are responsible for engulfing and processing antigens and for initiating antigen‑specific immune responses by activating the initial T lymphocytes.43 Resting dendritic cells express surface markers such as CD207, CCR6, and CD1a, and their efficient antigen uptake capacity contributes to immunological tolerance under physiological conditions.44 However, the epithelial barrier function of AR patients is defective. Resting dendritic cells mature after capturing allergens, upregulate co‑stimulatory molecules and Major Histocompatibility Complex molecules, migrate to lymph nodes to activate primed T cells, and gain the capacity to promote Th2 differentiation.45 CD4+ T cells are the main regulatory cells in atopic diseases, including AR, atopic dermatitis, and allergic asthma, and their differentiation can affect disease progression.46 During an allergic reaction, activated dendritic cell-induced Th2 CD4+ T cells release core cytokines (including IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13) and trigger subsequent inflammation.47 After the initial immune response, partially activated Th2 cells differentiate into CD4⁺ memory T cells. Among them, tissue-resident memory T cells reside in the barrier tissue of the previously inflamed site, where they sustain a type 2 immune response under persistent antigen exposure.48 Pawankar et al49 clarified the central role of CD4⁺ memory T cells in the nasal mucosa of AR for the first time, confirming that CD4⁺ memory T cells promote IgE synthesis, eosinophil infiltration, and mast cell maturation by secreting cytokines such as IL‑4 and IL‑5, providing critical evidence for AR pathogenesis. Furthermore, animal investigations conducted by Nagakubo et al demonstrated that allergen stimulation specifically recruits memory CD4⁺ T lymphocytes to the nasal mucosa.50 Aligning with our results, clinical studies have also shown that intranasal allergen stimulation increases the number of CD4⁺ memory T cells in the peripheral blood of patients.51 These findings confirm the critical role of CD4⁺ memory T cells in immunological memory and persistent inflammation in AR. Mitochondria play an important role in immunity.52,53 The correlation analysis in this study indicated that the expression level of SLC25A36 is negatively correlated with the levels of native CD4+ T cells and resting dendritic cells. In contrast, it is positively correlated with memory B cells. The expression of RNASEH1 is negatively associated with Macrophages M2. These results suggest a potential role for mitochondrial dysfunction in disrupting immune regulation. While these observations may deepen our understanding of the relationship between mitochondrial metabolism and immune cells in AR, further studies are needed.

Currently, biological informatics research on AR mainly focuses on genome-wide family-based screening of DEGs, exploration of immune-related markers, machine learning, and transcriptomics analysis.54–57 Several studies have also confirmed that mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress imbalance play a crucial role in the damage to the nasal mucosal epithelial barrier in AR.14,16 However, studies that specifically focus on mitochondrial-related genes in AR and investigate their relationship with the immune microenvironment are still limited. Based on the MitoCarta3.0 mitochondrial gene set, this study integrated three machine learning algorithms (LASSO, SVM‑RFE, and RF) to screen for hub mitochondrial‑related genes in AR and to identify additional potential molecular markers of AR based on mitochondrial metabolism. This work may serve as an omics reference and analytical framework for future mechanism‑oriented studies in the same field.

Notably, the mitochondrial‑related hub genes identified in this study have not been previously reported in AR. This novelty suggests several potential clinical implications, though they require further validation. Firstly, these genes may serve as a new class of molecular biomarkers for diagnosing AR, particularly when conventional markers (such as total IgE and allergen-specific IgE) are insufficient for a definitive diagnosis. Secondly, from a therapeutic perspective, targeting dysregulated mitochondrial metabolic pathways could represent a new intervention strategy for AR. The hub genes and their associated pathways provide a molecular basis for developing such therapies. Finally, the expression profile of these characteristic genes may help predict disease prognosis or response to conventional treatments, enabling more personalized management. Future prospective clinical studies with larger cohorts are essential to validate these potential applications.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. The sample size of the analyzed data is relatively small, and additional datasets are required to validate our findings. Moreover, our results were validated at the RNA level in nasal mucosal epithelial cells, and further validation through well‑designed animal model experiments is required. Consequently, future research is warranted to explore the impact of the candidate biomarkers on AR progression and to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study aimed to elucidate the biological significance of mitochondrial‑related genes in AR and their association with immune cell infiltration. Using machine learning, we identified two hub DEMRGs as mitochondrial biomarkers, which were validated using external GEO datasets and RT‑qPCR. ROC analysis confirmed high predictive accuracy, and immune infiltration analysis revealed correlations between these hub genes and immune cells. These findings offer mitochondrial‑related biomarkers and provide insights into the immune‑metabolic interplay in AR, contributing to the screening of potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets, and opening new avenues for understanding the molecular mechanisms of AR. Further validation is warranted in future studies.
