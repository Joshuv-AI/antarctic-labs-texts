Awareness and Attitudes of University Health Sciences Faculty Towards Medical Ethics Education: A Study from Jordan
Authors: Toojan E Qadoura, Omar F Khabour, Karem H Alzoubi, Wael K Al-Delaimy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTeaching medical ethics is a fundamental component of medical education, shaping the professional identity of future healthcare providers. This study examined the awareness and attitudes of health faculty members towards medical ethics in Jordan.MethodsThe study utilized a “cross-sectional” approach and targeted health sciences faculty at academic institutions. The study was conducted during the second half of 2024. An online survey was administered using “Google Forms” via professional e-mails and social media platforms.ResultsIn this study,420 faculty members were recruited to assess their knowledge and attitudes toward medical ethics. Among participants, 65.2% self-reported having adequate awareness of the importance and principles of medical ethics, while 92.4% exhibited positive attitudes toward medical ethics. Prior training in ethics was the major predictor of adequate awareness (3.49 [1.58–7.67], P<0.01), and positive attitudes (5.33 [2.05–13.86], P<0.01). Employment sector (4.16 [1.56–11.09], P<0.05) and academic rank (0.06 [0.01–0.39], P<0.01) were associated with attitudes toward medical ethics (P<0.05).ConclusionHealth sciences faculty have adequate awareness and positive attitudes towards medical ethics education. Prior training in ethics was positively correlated with awareness levels and attitudes toward medical ethics education. These findings can inform interventions to enhance medical ethics education in universities in Jordan.Keywords: medical ethics, education, training, healthcare, professional identity

Introduction

Ethics training is an integral part of medical education and medical professional development, serving as a conceptual foundation through which morals and standards of conduct are fostered among both faculty and students.1,2 It focuses on the pressing issues and practical challenges faced by medical professionals, including physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and other healthcare providers, in their daily work.1,3 Stemming from fundamental ethical principles, such as autonomy, beneficence, and justice, medical ethics guide decision-making regarding informed consent, confidentiality, end-of-life care, and the equitable allocation of limited medical resources.4 Therefore, the application of medical ethics ensures patients’ rights and balances societal expectations with professional responsibilities. Accordingly, medical ethics education equips educators and students with the conscience necessary to navigate these complexities, safeguard patient welfare, and minimize the risks of malpractice.5–7

Faculty members in medical schools bear a dual responsibility. They must integrate ethical conduct into their professional practices and serve as positive role models for their students. They are also tasked with imparting ethical knowledge and decision-making skills to future healthcare providers. This requires the ability to translate the code of ethics into clinical reasoning, case discussions, and daily teaching practices.8 Healthcare providers with insufficient ethical education may struggle to address sensitive issues in their daily practice, potentially eroding patient trust and damaging the reputation of healthcare institutions.9

Previous ethics training significantly shapes the awareness and attitudes of health faculty members toward medical ethics education. Drawing from Social Cognitive Theory, past training enhances faculty members’ self-efficacy and outcome expectancies, allowing them to better observe, recognize, and internalize ethical issues within their educational environment.10 Furthermore, Faculty Development Theory suggests that such training systematically refines their professional roles, shifting their identity toward becoming proactive ethical educators and institutional role models.11 Finally, through the lens of the Theory of Planned Behavior, this prior exposure directly shapes their underlying behavioral beliefs and subjective norms, ultimately building a positive attitude and a stronger behavioral intention to prioritize and deliver comprehensive medical ethics curricula.12

In the Middle East, several studies have haps in medical ethics education, including shortage of qualified ethics instructors in Saudi Arabia,13 Turkey,14 Pakistan,15 and Iran.16 Despite the importance of ethics education, studies have shown that ethics education in some medical institutions is still inadequate, treated as a secondary subject, and delivered by faculty members who lack ethics training.13–15,17 These reports underscore the urgent need for systematic training in medical ethics that integrates fundamental ethical principles into the clinical and pedagogical contexts of medical education.18 In Jordan, a study that investigated the struggle for clinical ethics in Jordanian hospitals revealed widespread concern over healthcare mismanagement, corruption, and systemic inefficiency, alongside cultural and religious tensions regarding modern bioethical issues like medical consent, privacy, and gender roles.19 To date, no studies have systematically examined the awareness and attitudes of health faculty members toward medical ethics education in Jordan. Therefore, the knowledge and attitudes of health sciences faculty toward ethical training were investigated. Specifically, the study explores faculty members’ familiarity with ethical principles and guidelines, their views on the importance of medical ethics training in improving healthcare services and reducing malpractice, and their application of ethical guidelines in teaching and the clinical supervision of students.

Methods

Study Design and Subjects

This cross-sectional survey-based study was conducted using an electronic questionnaire between June 15, 2024, and December 31, 2024. A convenience sample of health sciences faculty at Jordanian universities was approached to assess their knowledge and attitudes toward ethical training in the medical field. The survey items were presented to the participants using “Google Forms”. An invitation link was generated and sent to potential participants via professional e-mail and social media platforms, “WhatsApp” and “Facebook”, to promote widespread participation. Faculty members from both governmental and private universities across Jordan were invited to participate. To protect participant anonymity and encourage candid responses, information regarding specific university names or their exact geographical distribution was not collected. A precise response rate could not be determined due to the open-ended nature of the recruitment strategy, as the total denominator of potential participants exposed to the social media and WhatsApp invitation links remains unknown. Participants were provided with detailed information about the study, including its objectives, the expected timeframe for completing the questionnaire, and the voluntary and anonymous nature of participation. Health sciences faculty working outside of Jordan were excluded from participation.

Sample Size

The sample size was estimated using G-power software, with an effect size of 0.2 and a sampling error of 5%. The calculated sample size was 260.

The Study Survey

The research team formulated the study survey based on a thorough examination of relevant previous literature.20,21 The survey was based on “closed-ended questions” and included several sections. The first section collected background and demographic information about the participants (such as highest degree, title/rank, publications, job title, career duration, sector, and gender). The second section explored the awareness and attitudes of faculty members toward medical ethics training. The last section inquired about training needs in the ethics area. The survey questions included answer choices such as “yes or no”, as well as a 5-point Likert scale “5=strongly agree, 4=agree, 3=neutral, 2=disagree, 1=strongly disagree”. To ensure content validity, clarity, and relevance, the survey was reviewed by two experienced university professors, including one expert in medical ethics. Their feedback was used to refine the questions. ‎Based on this review, some items were revised to incorporate the experts’ feedback. To validate the instrument, the questionnaire was distributed to 16 participants twice, one week apart. The “Cohen’s kappa coefficient” analysis showed strong agreement between responses across two different time periods, with values ranging from 0.75 to 0.85.

The composite awareness score was calculated by summing six items on a Likert scale, with possible scores ranging from 6 to 30. The total awareness scores were then dichotomized into “adequate” and “inadequate” knowledge groups. A cut-off point of 24 was used, with scores >24 ((> 80% percentile) indicating adequate awareness.

The attitude domain was assessed using 10 items designed to capture participants’ beliefs, perceptions, and dispositions toward ethical training in medical education. These were measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 “strongly disagree” to 5 “strongly agree”, with higher scores reflecting a more positive attitude. The coding of one of the items with negative wording was reversed. The composite attitude score was calculated by summing the scores of the 10 statements, and the resulting scores ranged from 10 to 50. A cut-off point of 40 was used, with scores >40 (> 80% percentile) indicating positive attitude.12

Ethical Aspects

Ethical approval was obtained from the “Institutional Review Board at Jordan University of Science and Technology” (68/170/2024). Electronic informed consent was a prerequisite for enrolment in the study. Only after giving online consent was the questionnaire offered to participants.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) across three stages. First, descriptive statistics summarized categorical variables (faculty demographics, awareness levels, and attitudes) as absolute frequencies and percentages. Second, response distributions for the knowledge and attitude statements regarding ethics in medical education were presented. Third, binary logistic regression using the enter method identified independent predictors of faculty awareness and attitudes. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Statistical significance for all analyses was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Demographic Data and Relevant Information of Participants Regarding Ethics Training in Jordan

A total of 420 participants were initially recruited for the study. Because data collection was conducted via “Google Forms” with all questions set as mandatory, no incomplete questionnaires were received, and no participants were excluded. Consequently, the final sample included in all subsequent analyses was 420 participants. Table 1 provides an overview of the sociodemographic and other key characteristics of the study sample. Female participants comprised 55% of the sample. Participants’ ages varied, with the largest proportion in the 45–54 age group (37.4%) and the smallest proportion in the 25–34 age group (12.4%). A good distribution across academic ranks and professional backgrounds was noticed, with professors (37.4%) and applied medical sciences (40.5%) having the highest representation. The participants were distributed almost equally between the public sector (46.9%) and the private sector (53.1%). The majority of the sample had more than 10 years of experience (52.2%, Table 1). Regarding academic productivity, 41.4% of participants published more than 20 research papers. Notably, 392 participants (92.1%) reported receiving training in medical ethics.

Assessment of Awareness and Attitude of Health Sciences Faculty Toward Medical Ethics

The survey included 6 items to assess participants’ awareness and knowledge of ethical principles, challenges, and resources related to medical ethics education. Responses were measured using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. As shown in Table 2, the majority of participants expressed a thorough understanding of ethical principles in medical practices, with 93.8% agreeing that they are familiar with the ethical principles and guidelines related to medical practice. Similarly, 91.2% reported familiarity with the moral dilemmas and ethical friction that medical students navigate during their training. Regarding teaching medical ethics, 86.4% of participants agreed that they are familiar with the tools and methodologies used to teach ethics in medical education, and 86.2% were aware of the pedagogical resources available for teaching medical ethics. Finally, 90% reported that they are fully equipped to teach ethical principles and practices to medical students (Table 2).

Table 4 presents the distribution of participants’ responses regarding their attitude toward medical ethics education. Overall, the responses reflected strong support for integrating ethics into practice. A vast majority (98.6%) “agreed”, or “strongly agreed”, on the importance of providing ethical training opportunities for students. Similarly, 98.4% supported the statement that ethical training helps develop responsible individuals in society. Most participants believed that ethical instruction should be integrated across all university curricula (89.6%) and that medical education programs must require comprehensive ethics training to prepare students for complex clinical dilemmas (97.8%). Approximately 98.3% of respondents “agreed”, or “strongly agreed” that ethical training enhances medical care quality, with similar support for its role in minimizing the risk of errors and malpractice (97.2%) and improving medical students’ professional practice (97.9%). Confidence and willingness to engage in ethics were also high. The majority of participants expressed willingness to participate in ethics training programs (95.4%), and to recommend an ethical training program to my colleagues (95.7). However, responses to the negative phrase “I am unconcerned with medical ethics” showed appropriate rejection of this statement, with only 9.3% “agreeing” or “strongly agreeing”, while the majority (84.3%) disagreed.

Discussions

The study aimed to investigate awareness and attitudes of university health sciences faculty toward medical ethics education. The majority of the study participants demonstrated adequate awareness and positive attitudes regarding medical ethics education. Furthermore, prior ethics training was found to be a strong predictor of adequate awareness and positive attitude. Teaching medical ethics is a core element of medical education, as it shapes the professional identity of future healthcare providers.22,23 The application of medical ethics is essential for maintaining patients’ rights, balancing societal expectations with professional responsibilities, and minimizing risks of malpractice.5 Therefore, having competent and qualified instructors to teach medical ethics is of paramount importance.

The study findings revealed that the majority of health sciences faculty have adequate awareness and knowledge of ethical principles, representing a good percentage compared to previous studies in the region. For example, a study from Iran indicated a shortage of qualified ethics instructors,16 and another study from the same country indicated a moderate to low level of medical ethics knowledge among surgical residents.24 Inadequate knowledge about ethical principles was also reported among pediatricians in specialized healthcare in Ethiopia, attributed to insufficient ethics training.25 A similar inadequate knowledge was reported in a study conducted among faculty of medical colleges in Pakistan.15 In Turkey, although most educational institutions have university-level ethics curricula, a deficiency of teaching personnel for ethics courses has been reported in about 34% of academic institutions.14 A study from Saudi Arabia found that 25% or fewer of the faculty members teaching ethics held a qualification in medical ethics.13 Having qualified instructors is crucial, as those with inadequate awareness, knowledge and training can be poor role models, sabotaging the learning process.

In the current study, prior ethics training was found to be a strong predictor of adequate awareness and knowledge. This is also supported by a study from Nepal, which found that medical ethics training was associated with higher levels of knowledge, attitude, and practice’26 In studies from the USA, ethics training significantly enhanced the baseline knowledge, attitudes, and decision-making skills of healthcare providers and trainees in medical ethics.27,28 This consistency suggests that structured educational interventions universally help build foundational ethical competencies. Medical students in a study from Austria highlighted the demand for more ethics training.29 Therefore, several studies have highlighted the importance of training educators to improve ethics education for healthcare providers. It is worth mentioning here that the high proportion of participants with prior bioethics training (92.1%) suggests potential self-selection and non-response bias. It is highly probable that faculty members with a pre-existing interest, education, or positive disposition toward medical ethics were more inclined to participate in this study. Consequently, this overrepresentation may have artificially inflated the reported estimates of ethical awareness and favorable attitudes. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect the perspectives of a highly trained sub-segment rather than the true baseline characteristics of the wider population of Jordanian health sciences faculty. Future research utilizing random sampling techniques is needed to mitigate this bias and establish a more representative baseline.

To ensure the effectiveness of medical ethics education, it is recommended that instructors use diverse teaching methods such as interactive, multimodal, interdisciplinary, and design thinking, with the involvement of faculty members trained in medical ethics.5,30–32 In addition, integrating artificial intelligence and virtue-based teaching methods into medical education, including ethics, and curricular reform to catch rapid technological advancements and societal changes, is of paramount importance.33,34 A study conducted in Nepal demonstrated that team-based learning in ethics education is more effective than traditional teaching methods.35 According to another study, teaching ethics in Turkey tends to be largely theoretical, focusing on the foundations of bioethics.14 Similarly, in the United States, the majority of institutions utilize lectures and small-group teaching methods in ethics education.28 In India, faculty awareness of ethics teaching methods was found to be not sufficient.36 A study from Iran highlighted the importance of using diverse methods in medical ethics teaching and training, including role-play, journal clubs, and debates to achieve desired outcomes.24 Finally, gaps have been identified between theoretical knowledge of ethics and its systematic application in teaching and clinical supervision in different countries.21 Therefore, there is a pressing global need for interventions that improve the teaching of medical ethics.

In the current study, the majority of participants reported positive attitudes toward medical ethics education, with strong endorsement of the importance of ethics in improving healthcare services, reducing malpractices, and fostering responsibilities among medical students. In a study conducted in the United States among course directors and deans of academic affairs, most participants expressed positive attitudes toward ethics education and considered ethics courses to be equal or more important than other foundation courses.28 In a study from Saudi Arabia on healthcare professionals in Al Majma’ah, positive attitudes (75%) were reported regarding the importance of discussing ethical issues during clinical rounds alongside the clinical aspects of patient care.37 While most studies have reported positive attitudes toward medical ethics, inadequate knowledge in some countries suggests that simply having a positive attitude is not enough and that targeted, regular training for medical educators is necessary to bridge the gap between positive attitudes and proper knowledge.

In the current study, slight differences between faculty in the public and private sectors demonstrate how institutional environments influence ethical engagement. Therefore, policymakers should provide adequate financial support to all academic institutions and establish standardized national frameworks. This will ensure consistent ethics education across different sectors and academic institutions in Jordan.

The findings suggest that Jordanian universities should integrate compulsory, standardized medical ethics training early into health sciences curricula such as targeted workshops and structured micro-learning modules. Universities should also train educators to teach this material well. These changes will help future healthcare workers in Jordan handle ethical challenges and follow high standards.

The current study has some limitations. First, it employed a cross-sectional design and relied on self-reporting, making it susceptible to selection and social desirability bias. Faculty members already interested in or engaged with ethics may have been more likely to respond, potentially inflating our estimates of overall awareness and positive attitudes. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to all healthcare professionals, institutions, or regions beyond Jordan. Second, the study did not collect information on the qualifications of the instructors teaching medical ethics or on the teaching methods. Third, another limitation of this study is the dichotomization of continuous awareness and attitude scores into binary categories. While this approach facilitates clinical or practical interpretability, converting continuous metrics into discrete groups reduces data granularity and statistical power.38 Consequently, subtle variations in participant perspectives may be masked, potentially increasing the risk of residual confounding or type II errors. Finally, this study relied on a newly developed questionnaire rather than a pre-validated instrument, and it lacked an objective knowledge assessment to verify participant self-reports. Future research should utilize continuous metrics for awareness and attitude scores instead of binary categorization to prevent the loss of statistical power and avoid masking subtle variations in participant perspectives. In addition, future research should employ a pre-validated version of this instrument and include objective data to cross-verify self-reported findings.

In conclusion, health sciences faculty in Jordan demonstrate adequate knowledge and positive attitudes toward medical ethics education. Prior ethics training correlates positively with higher knowledge and attitude levels. These findings offer valuable insights for designing interventions, such as targeted workshops and structured micro-learning ethical modules, to enhance medical ethics education across Jordanian universities.
