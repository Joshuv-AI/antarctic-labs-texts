Failure of the Flat Chain Conjecture and non-regularity of the prescribed Jacobian equation
Jakub Takáč

Abstract
We show that the Flat Chain Conjecture in Lang’s formulation (that is, without requiring finite mass of the underlying currents) fails for metric k k -currents in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} whenever d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 and k ∈ { 1 , … , d } k\in\{1,\dots,d\} . In all other cases, it holds. We first connect the conjecture to a regularity statement concerning the prescribed Jacobian equation near L ∞ L^{\infty} . We then show that the equation does not have the required regularity. For a Lipschitz vector field π \pi , its derivative D ​ π \mathrm{D}\pi exists a.e. and is identified with a matrix. Our non-regularity results for the prescribed Jacobian equation quantify how “small” the set conv ⁡ ( { det D ​ π : Lip ​ ( π ) ≤ L } ) ⊂ L ∞ \operatorname{conv}(\{\det\mathrm{D}\pi:\textnormal{Lip}(\pi)\leq L\})\subset L^{\infty} is for every L > 0 L>0 . The symbol “ conv \operatorname{conv} ” stands for the convex hull. The “smallness” is quantified in topological terms and is used to show that the Flat Chain Conjecture fails.

Introduction

The main purpose of this paper is to provide a complete resolution of the so-called “Flat Chain Conjecture” in the formulation of Lang
[
27

(see also Ambrosio and Kirchheim
[
2

The conjecture concerns the structure of so-called metric currents, which are objects of interest in Geometric Measure Theory.
They are related to a number of geometric problems (in metric spaces) such as the Plateau problem
[
2
]
, isoperimetric inequalities
[

, flows of measures
[

and even geometric flows
[

(here currents are in particular used to define a suitable notion of distance
[

The conjecture states that metric

with compact support are flat

Our main result is that the conjecture for

Remarkably, our method relies on connecting the conjecture to the (non-)regularity of a certain partial differential equation (PDE), which is usually called the

\det\mathrm{D}\pi=\rho\quad\text{on $Q=[0,1]^{d}$.}

(1.1)

If

is a Lipschitz vector field on

is defined a.e. and lies in

Thus one could ask, for any
\rho\in L^{\infty}(Q)
, is there a Lipschitz vector field
\pi
satisfying (
1.1
) a.e.?
The only available result in this direction is due to Burago and Kleiner
[
9
]
and independently McMullen
[
29

, construct a
\rho\in L^{\infty}(Q)
attaining only two positive values such that there is no

solution to the equation.
We give a far stronger result, asserting that the of set of
\rho\in L^{\infty}(Q)
for which there exists a Lipschitz solution (as opposed to biLipschitz) is “small” (Proposition
1.5
).
The “smallness” is quantified in topological terms.

It is this property of the prescribed Jacobian equation that eventually leads to the resolution of the Flat Chain Conjecture.
We give an overview of some previously available results concerning the equation, as well as of some related problems and of our own results, later in Subsection
1.2
, but for now, we turn our attention to metric currents.

1.1
Gentle introduction to metric currents and the Flat Chain Conjecture

The basic idea behind “currents” is that of generalized surfaces.
This follows the standard analytical approach to minimisation problems, where a correct class of objects in which a compactness theorem is available needs to be used.
Consider, for example, the Plateau problem, which asks for a minimal
k
dimensional manifold
M
having the prescribed boundary
\partial M=N
.
Here, “minimal” is to be interpreted as having the least possible
k
dimensional volume.
The standard way to show that such an object even exists is to take a sequence
M_{n}
, whose volumes converge to the infimal volume and then use some kind of compactness theorem to extract a “convergent” subsequence.
The limit of this subsequence can then be expected to be a solution.
The basic problem with this approach is that the space of (smooth) manifolds is not compact with respect to a useful notion of convergence and therefore we need to “enlarge” the space into something, where compactness is available.
This is usually achieved by various different spaces of currents and the first successful implementation of this idea (for the Plateau problem) is due to Federer and Fleming
[
21
]
.

General theory of currents dates back to de Rham
[
18
]

and is based on the observation that every oriented
k
dimensional manifold acts by integration on, say, smooth and compactly supported differential
k
-forms.
Moreover, this action is linear and (in an appropriate sense) continuous.
A
k
-current is, by definition, any such linear and continuous action.
The approach is analogous to the classical theory of distributions.
Since the theory requires differential forms, it implicitly uses the vector space structure of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and therefore it only makes sense in a Euclidean space (or possibly a space with a suitable notion of a tangent field, such as a manifold).
Thus, if one wishes to make sense of a current in a general metric space a different approach needs to be chosen.

A theory of metric currents was first developed by Ambrosio and Kirchheim in their seminal paper
[
2
]
motivated by earlier ideas of De Giorgi.
Their theory imposed a finite mass condition on the currents and was later extended by Lang
[
27
]
, who developed a more general

To understand appropriately the statement of the Flat Chain Conjecture, we need to introduce also the so-called flat chains.

We continue here by considering only the top-dimensional case of

Every metric

\tilde{C}_{d}T(\varphi)=T(\varphi,x^{1},\dots,x^{d})\quad\text{for $\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$.}

It is instructive to verify that if

Using several well-established identifications (see Subsection

In other words, the conjecture states that if the space of metric

We also remark that the converse statement is known to be true

DePhilippis and Rindler

The principal result that we obtain (and from which the complete resolution of the conjecture in all dimensions and codimensions is obtained) is that Conjecture

The proof in fact relies on two non-constructive steps; we use the open mapping theorem as well as a version of the Hahn-Banach separation theorem.

1.2
The prescribed Jacobian equation and Lipschitz regularity

Due to its geometric meaning, the Jacobian appears when dealing with many different types of problems in mathematical analysis.
Here we point out a couple of connections that are the most relevant for the present paper.
The prescribed Jacobian equataion is of interest in the Calculus of Variations and in elasticity, for which a standard reference is the book of Dacorogna
[
12
, Chapter 14]
.
Specifically in elasticity, a lower estimate on the Jacobian is a natural constraint, since zero Jacobian corresponds to compressing the underlying material infinitely, which would require infinite energy
[
5
, (2.7)]
.
In the recent work of Bevan et al
[
7
]
a constrained minimisation problem is studied and the constraint is given by the equation (
1.1
).
Namely, a functional
\mathcal{F}(\pi)
is minimised over vector fields
\pi
which solve the equation (
1.1
) for a fixed right hand side
\rho
.

Then there is the link of the prescribed Jacobian equation to the theory of optimal transport (via the Monge-Ampère equation
[
4
, Lecture 7]
).
Interestingly, optimal transport is also directly connected to metric currents - in particular (normal) metric
1
-currents, which can be seen as transporting the positive part of their boundary to the negative part.
This idea can actually be used to give an elementary proof of the Flat Chain Conjecture for metric
1
-currents of finite mass.
This case of the conjecture was previously resolved by Schioppa
[
36
]
, with a rather involved proof.
The simpler (and more general) proof will become available in an upcoming work of A. Arroyo-Rabasa and G. Bouchitté and, independently, an upcoming work of the author jointly with D. Bate, E. Caputo, P. Valentine and P. Wald. Similar connection is also explored in the recent work of De Pauw
[
33
]
.

Finally, there is the somewhat surprising connection of the prescribed Jacobian equation to large-scale geometry.
This was first observed independently by Burago and Kleiner
[
9
]
and McMullen
[
29
]
and is of particular interest to our work.
Recall that
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
separated net
, if there are two constants
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
such that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there is
z\in Z
with
\lVert x-z\rVert\leq\varepsilon
and whenever
z,w\in Z
,
z\not=w
, then
\lVert z-w\rVert\geq\delta
.
Burago and Kleiner show that existence of
biLipschitz solutions
(i.e. Lipschitz, injective solutions, with Lipschitz inverses) to (
1.1
) given a right hand side
\rho\in L^{\infty}(Q)
, corresponds to the existence of biLipschitz bijections
F\colon Z\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
Z
is a separated net.

They continue to show that there exists
\rho\in L^{\infty}(Q)
, which, moreover, only attains two positive values, such that there is no biLipschitz solution to (
1.1
).
This is used to infer that there exists a separated net
Z
, for which there is no biLipschitz bijection
F\colon Z\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Existence of such bijections was previously an open question of Gromov.

Later, these ideas were refined by Dymond et al
[
19
]
.
They study the pushforward equation

\pi_{\#}\mathcal{L}^{d}_{|Q}=\rho\mathcal{L}^{d}_{|\pi(Q)}.

(1.2)

Note that if
\pi
is injective, then (
1.2
) is the same as (
1.1
) by the change of variables formula.
They show that there exists
\rho\in L^{\infty}(Q)
(once again, it may be assumed that
\rho
only attains two positive values) such that (
1.2
) admits no
Lipschitz regular solutions
(for the relevant definitions, see the paper
[
19
]
).
This is used to assert a statement about Lipschitz maps from
n^{d}
point subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
to the regular
n^{d}
square in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as
n\to\infty
.

Concerning the (lack of) regularity of the equation (
1.1
), there are multiple “scales” of relevant questions.
Since the equation is of first order, one may for example ask, whether given
\rho\in L^{p}
, there is a solution
\pi\in W^{1,p}
.
This was resolved affirmatively in the case
d<p<\infty
by Ye
[
39
]
.
In the less regular regime of
p<d
, the Jacobian loses its geometric meaning and has very pathological behaviour.
This can be seen in the work of Koumatos et al
[
26
, 1.2]
.
A particular special case of their result asserts that a Sobolev map
\pi
can be found which maps
Q=[0,1]^{d}
to
2Q=[0,2]^{d}
satisfying

\det\mathrm{D}\pi=1\quad\text{in $Q$}\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{|\partial Q}=2\operatorname{Id}.

In fact, the map
\pi
may be required to lie in any Sobolev space
W^{1,p}
for
p<d
.
This is a kind of infinitesimal analogue of the Banach-Tarski paradox, allowing one to double the global volume, while on the infinitesimal scale the volume stays preserved.
In a similar fashion, an earlier construction of Hencl
[
23
]
produces, for any
p<d
, a
homeomorphism

\pi\in W^{1,p}
such that
\det\mathrm{D}\pi=0
a.e.

Given the scaling behaviour of determinants, it is also natural to ask whether for a
\rho\in L^{p}
there exists a solution in
W^{1,pd}
.
In fact, the previously mentioned results of Koumatos et al resolve this affirmatively when
p<1
(i.e.
pd<d
).
For
p=1
(i.e.
pd=d
), this is known to fail due to Coifman et al
[
10
]
(in French), who show that whenever
\pi\in W^{1,d}
, then
\det\mathrm{D}\pi
is actually a member of a Hardy space which is known to be strictly smaller than
L^{1}
.
In the case
p=\infty
the question of existence of solutions in
W^{1,pd}
and
W^{1,p}
agree and simply reduce to the question of existence of Lipschitz solutions for
L^{\infty}
right hand sides.

Another natural question is whether given
\rho\in C^{k,\alpha}
(Hölder space), there is a solution
\pi\in C^{k+1,\alpha}
, for which an affirmative answer was found by Dacorogna and Moser
[
13
]
if
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
However, the question of Lipschitz (i.e.
C^{0,1}
) solutions given a
\rho\in L^{\infty}(Q)
or even
\rho\in C(Q)
remained open, although Riviere and Ye
[
35
]
find less regular Hölder solutions.
We remark that it is sometimes also of interest to construct solutions which are invertible and their inverses also have satisfactory regularity (in particular, in some of the reference given above).

We are interested in Lipschitz solutions for
L^{\infty}
right hand sides and, motivated by resolving the Flat Chain Conjecture, we actually consider a vast generalisation of the equation (
1.1
), given by the following “linearisation”:

\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}=\rho\quad\text{a.e.~on $Q=[0,1]^{d}$.}

(1.3)

Here,
\rho\in L^{\infty}(Q)
is a prescribed function and
f_{i},\pi_{i}
are unknown, possibly infinite, sequences of Lipschitz maps.
Naturally,
\pi_{i}
are vector fields and
f_{i}
are scalar fields.

Definition 1.2

(Lipschitz solutions)
.

Given a function
\rho\in L^{\infty}(Q)
,
a sequence of Lipschitz maps
f_{i}\colon Q\to\mathbb{R}
and
\pi_{i}\colon Q\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called a
Lipschitz solution to (
1.3
) (with the right hand side
\rho
)
if

\sum_{i}\max\{\textnormal{Lip}(f_{i}),\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\textnormal{Lip}(\pi_{i}^{j})<\infty,

(1.4)

and (
1.3
) holds with absolute convergence in
L^{\infty}(Q)
.

Note that the estimate (
1.4
) immediately implies that the sum on the left hand side of (
1.3
) converges absolutely in
L^{\infty}
.

We now continue by defining three notions of
non-regularity
which we will study.
It is the case that in order to resolve the Flat Chain Conjecture, we only need to consider one of these notions (strong Lipschitz non-regularity), however, it is very instructive to discuss all three.

Definition 1.3
.

(1)

We say that the prescribed Jacobian equation has
weak Lipschitz non-regularity
if there exists some
\rho\in L^{\infty}(Q)
for which there does not exists a Lipschitz vector field
\pi
solving the equation (
1.1
) in the sense of equality almost everywhere.

(2)

We say that the prescribed Jacobian equation has
linearised Lipschitz non-regularity
if there exists some
\rho\in L^{\infty}(Q)
for which there does not exists a Lipschitz solution
(f_{i},\pi_{i})
to (
1.3
).

(3)

We say that the prescribed Jacobian equation has
strong Lipschitz non-regularity
if a sequence of
L^{1}(Q)
functions
u_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
can be found satisfying the two following inequalities simultaneously:

(i)

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lVert u_{n}\rVert_{L^{1}(Q)}\geq 1

(ii)

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sup\left\{\left|\int_{Q}u_{n}f\det\mathrm{D}\pi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{d}\right|:\max\{\textnormal{Lip}(f),\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}\}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\textnormal{Lip}(\pi^{j})\leq 1\right\}\leq\frac{1}{n}.

It is clear that linearised Lipschitz non-regularity implies weak Lipschitz non-regularity.
To see that strong Lipschitz non-regularity implies linearised Lipschitz non-regularity we essentially “repeat” the proof of the open mapping theorem in a different setting.
Let

K=\{f\det\mathrm{D}\pi:\max\{\textnormal{Lip}(f),\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}\}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\textnormal{Lip}(\pi^{j})\leq 1\}\subset L^{\infty}(Q).

If the prescribed Jacobian equation does
not
have linearised Lipschitz non-regularity, then in particular

\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{n\rho:\rho\in\overline{\operatorname{conv}K}\}=L^{\infty}(Q).

Thus, an application of the Baire category theorem shows that for at least one
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the set

\{n\rho:\rho\in\overline{\operatorname{conv}K}\}

has non-empty interior.
By the homogeneity of the determinant, we may freely rescale the set and see that in fact the set
\overline{\operatorname{conv}K}
contains an open neighbourhood of
0
.
By definition, therefore, some

\varepsilon>0
exists for which

\overline{\operatorname{conv}K}\supset\{\rho\in L^{\infty}(Q):\lVert\rho\rVert_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\}.

It follows that for any
u\in L^{1}(Q)
,

\varepsilon\lVert u\rVert_{1}\leq\sup_{\rho\in\overline{\operatorname{conv}K}}\left|\int u\rho\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{d}\right|=\sup_{\rho\in\operatorname{conv}K}\left|\int u\rho\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{d}\right|=\sup_{\rho\in K}\left|\int u\rho\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{d}\right|,

where in the first equality we use that the functional
\rho\mapsto\int u\rho
is continuous and in the second equality that it is linear.
By definition of
K
, this contradicts strong Lipschitz non-regularity.

It is not known to us whether there exists a simple way of proving strong Lipschitz non-regularity under the assumption of linearised Lipschitz non-regularity, but in light of the following result, this is not needed.

Theorem 1.4
.

The prescribed Jacobian equation has strong Lipschitz non-regularity.
Therefore, it also has linearised Lipschitz non-regularity and weak Lipschitz non-regularity.

We actually also prove linearised Lipschitz non-regularity more “directly” (it follows immediately from Theorem
5.24
together with Baire category theorem, or from Corollary
5.25
together with the fact that strong non-surjectivity implies non-surjectivity; cf. Section
4
).

Before we discuss some aspects of the proof, we discuss related results in literature.
The lack of regularity of PDEs near
L^{\infty}
as well as near
L^{1}
is not something specific to our particular case.
Consider the so-called Ornstein’s non-inequality
[
30
]
(see also the work of Kirchheim and Kristensen
[
25
, Theorem 1.3]
for a more general statement with a different proof).
One possible way to view the inequality is as saying that under determined PDEs lack regularity gain near
L^{1}
.
In more explicit way, if we have an under determined linear differential operator of order
k
, say
\mathscr{A}
, then for the equation

\mathscr{A}u=f,

we may find a sequence
f_{i}
bounded in
L^{1}
for which any sequence of solutions
u_{i}
must satisfy
\lVert\nabla^{k}u_{i}\rVert_{L^{1}}\to\infty
.
In elasticity theory, this is of interest when
\mathscr{A}
is the symmetric part of the gradient (cf. Korn’s inequality
[
24
]
asserting instead that for
p\in(1,\infty)
a regularity gain can be expected for the symmetric part of the gradient).
In the theory of currents, it is useful to consider
\mathscr{A}=\mathrm{d}
, the exterior derivative.
Except for the case when
\mathrm{d}
coincides with the Frechét derivative, the operator is indeed under determined.

The non-inequality of Ornstein can be dualised to obtain an analogous result near
L^{\infty}
.
It appears that the first instance of an
L^{\infty}
result is due to De Leeuw and Mirkil
[
15
]
(in French) and came before Ornstein’s result.
A duality argument in combination with Ornstein’s result is carried out by McMullen
[
29
]
who uses this idea to show lack of Lipschitz solutions for the
divergence equation
:

\operatorname{div}u=f,

for some right sides
f\in L^{\infty}
.
This result was, however, first obtained by Preiss
[
34
]
using a different technique.
Bouragin and Brezis
[
8
]
provide a number of results for the divergence equation in various limiting cases and in particular provide a simpler duality argument, akin to McMullen’s approach, showing the lack of Lipschitz solutions.
It appears that a similar method could be used for any under determined linear differential operator, but this is not available explicitly in literature.

Our Theorem
1.4
may thus be seen as a multilinear (as opposed to linear) version of these types of statements.
It is clear that linearised Lipschitz non-regularity is a statement about lack of regularity near
L^{\infty}
.
On the other hand, it is not clear, whether strong Lipschitz non-regularity is a result “near
L^{\infty}
” or “near
L^{1}
”.
It concerns
L^{1}
maps, but no operator on acting on these maps is really considered.
In fact, to obtain an operator of this kind, theory of metric currents needs to be developed.
Or rather, a development of such an operator leads immediately to the development of metric currents and eventually to the resolution of the Flat Chain Conjecture.

It is also not clear whether our result is strictly stronger than available linear results (say, for operators of order
1
near
L^{\infty}
).
However, a large scale of linear operators can be considered and non-regularity results for them obtained using our multilinear result.
An example of these types of operators is given by the exterior derivative
\mathrm{d}
acting on
k
-forms for
k\geq 1
.
This is further discussed in Section
7
where we also provide the relevant results.
It appears that the results we collect concerning the operator
\mathrm{d}
are “colloquially known” since it should be possible to obtain them by dualizing the result of Ornstein as we discussed above.
But these results are not available explicitly, thus we provide them together with detailed proofs.

We continue by outlining the basic ideas behind Theorem
1.4
.
There are, broadly speaking, two main steps that we need to carry out.
The first is purely functional-analytic and it is here that we rely on the Hahn-Banach separation theorem.
The second step is proving the following proposition.

Proposition 1.5
.

For any
S>0
, there exists a smooth map
\rho_{0}\in L^{\infty}(Q)
with
\lVert\rho_{0}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 2
together with a weak
∗
open set
U
with
\rho_{0}\in U
and such that for every
\rho\in U
, there is no solution
(f_{i},\pi_{i})
to the equation (
1.3
) satisfying

\sum_{i}\max\{\textnormal{Lip}(f_{i}),\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\textnormal{Lip}(\pi_{i}^{j})\leq S.

(1.5)

An important remark is that the reason we require
\rho_{0}
to be smooth is only to be certain that there exists
some
Lipschitz solution to (
1.3
) with right hand side
\rho_{0}
.

Once we obtain a proof of Proposition
1.5
, the Hahn-Banach theorem is used to construct a functional separating the set
U
from the set of
L^{\infty}(Q)
maps for which there exists a solution satisfying (
1.5
).
Due to working with the weak
∗
topology, this separating functional lies in
L^{1}(Q)
and as we increase
S\to\infty
we obtain the sequence required in the definition of strong Lipschitz non-regularity.

The proof of Proposition
1.5
is overall quite similar to the proof, due to Burago and Kleiner
[
9
]
, that the prescribed Jacobian equation lacks
biLipschitz
solutions.
Therefore it is also quite similar to the main result concerning the pushforward equation of Dymond at el
[
19
]
.
However, four problems arise, some more significant than others, requiring new ideas that need to be implemented in the entire procedure.
As these adjustments cannot really be separated from the rest of the argument, we provide in Section
5
a complete detailed proof, even if it has large overlaps with the aforementioned works.
Here we make the best attempt to point out the changes made to the argument, without getting into too many details.
Our goal is to construct some
\rho
having the prescribed properties together with a weak
∗
open neighbourhood
U
.
The changes done to the construction of
\rho
from
[
9
]
and
[
19
]
can broadly be characterised as

(i)

considering Lipschitz maps as opposed to biLipschitz,

(ii)

considering possibly infinite sums of Jacobians,

(iii)

allowing the Jacobians
\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}
to be multiplied with the coefficients
f_{i}
(which are Lipschitz functions),

(iv)

finding an entire weak
∗
open set of right hand sides
\rho
as opposed to a single one.

The first and the second item are the most complicated to deal with.
First we consider item
(ii)
.
An intermediate results of Dymond et al.
[
19
, Lemma 3.1]
essentially deals with the case of finite sums of Jacobians:

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i},

(1.6)

where we need a control over both the number
k
and the biLipschitz constants of
\pi_{i}
’s.
The definition of
g
on page 614 of their paper is where the “sums” implicitly appear and our counterpart to this idea is in Lemma
5.9
.
One of the adjustments we make is that we take their central intermediate results
[
19
, Lemma 3.3]
for maps from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\geq d
and improve it to hold even for maps from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\ell^{2}
, the separable Hilbert space, which allows us to consider infinite sums instead of finite ones.
This is available in Lemma
5.8
.

To deal with item
(i)
we must find a way to make the argument work for Lipschitz maps as opposed to biLipschitz.
This problem appears at two separate instances of the argument.
Firstly, the biLipschitz constant appears in a certain quantitative estimate concerning the sum (
1.6
) (which in our case may be infinite).
This can be resolved by a simple trick, which we illustrate in the following.
Suppose we have a Lipschitz map
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then the map

\tilde{F}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d+n};\quad\tilde{F}(x)=(x,F(x)),

is biLipschitz.
Remarkably, the entire argument which we need to carry out is completely invariant under such modifications of functions (which we carry out in Lemma
5.9
, where the first
d
variables correspond to the identity).
Moreover, the quantitative estimates needed, which in the approach of Dymond et al
[
19
]
depend on the biLipschitz constants, can be made to only depend on the Lipschitz constants.

The second instance where biLipschitzness appears is of qualitative nature.
Let
Q
be a cube inside
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of side-length
r>0
and
\pi,\kappa\colon Q\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

L
-Lipschitz maps.
Then

|\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\pi-\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa|\leq Cr^{d-1}\lVert\pi-\kappa\rVert_{\infty}L^{d-1}.

This is shown already by Burago and Kleiner
[
9
]
when
\pi,\kappa
are also homeomorphisms (equivalently injective) using a kind of topological argument.
The same argument is also used by Dymond et al
[
19
]
.
However, as it turns out, the exact same estimate (with possibly a different constant) may be obtained for general Lipschitz maps by a use of Stokes theorem - see Lemma
5.1
(cf.
[
2
, Proposition 5.1]
).

The item
(iii)
is resolved fairly simply.
The right hand sides
\rho
which are constructed cause a problem by oscillating very fast on small scales.
These oscillations cannot be disrupted (i.e. cancelled out) by the coefficients
f_{i}
, because they are required to be Lipschitz.
This idea can be seen in the estimate in Lemma
5.2
.

Finally, item
(iv)
is implicitly already resolved in the original paper of Burago and Kleiner (although it is likely very difficult to see until the correct perspective arising from weak
∗
topology is taken).
The point is that a map
\rho
is constructed, for which there are no solutions with controlled constants (in our case, this means solutions satisfying the estimate (
1.5
)).
However, the way it is
proven
that
\rho
has this property only relies on asserting that the quantities

\int_{Q_{i}}\rho

for some
finite
collection of cubes
Q_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N
lie in some open intervals
I_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Such a condition can be stated as belonging to the weak
∗
open set

U=\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\{\rho:\int_{Q_{i}}\rho\in I_{i}\}.

Then, the proof that
any
element of
U
has the same property (lack of a solution with good constants) is exactly the same as it is for the original
\rho
which is constructed.
See Lemma
5.21
for the explicit definition of
U
.

1.3
Connecting regularity to the Flat Chain Conjecture and further discussion

The fact that there is
some
connection between metric currents and Jacobians is not deep, as it can be immediately seen from
[
27
, Theorem 5.5]
, nor new.
The first instance where this is explored seems to be due to De Lellis
[
16
]
, where a theory of Jacobians is developed in a metric space using the theory of metric currents.
Our observations, however, link Jacobians to metric currents closely enough to allow us to obtain invalidity of the Flat Chain Conjecture.
The most precise statement we are able to make about this connection is Theorem
3.18
, special case of which follows.

Theorem 1.6
.

Suppose
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
If the prescribed Jacobian equation has strong Lipschitz non-regularity, then the Conjecture
1.1
fails.

The proof of Theorem
1.6
is non-constructive, as it relies on the open mapping theorem.
The basic idea is that if one attempts to “forcefully” dualise the statement of Conjecture
1.1
(which is about metric currents and
L^{1}
maps), one would expect to obtain a statement about
L^{\infty}
maps and Jacobians of Lipschitz maps.
This is exactly what happens and the dual statement is essentially the negation of strong Lipschitz non-regularity.
By combining Theorem
1.6
and Theorem
1.4
, we see that Conjecture
1.1
fails whenever
d\geq 2
.
Using some basic theory of metric currents and flat chains, a complete resolution of the conjecture in all dimensions and codimensions is obtained.
Our main result is therefore the following.

Theorem 1.7
.

Suppose
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,
k\leq d
.
Then every compactly supported metric
k
-current in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a flat
k
-chain if and only if either
k=0
or
k=d=1
.

To conclude this subsection, we discuss different statements sharing the name “Flat Chain Conjecture” and the relationships between them.
We also give a historical overview of the very limited progress on these questions.
Basic knowledge of flat chains and metric currents is assumed (consult Section
2
for the required definitions).

The first instance of a Flat Chain Conjecture is due to Ambrosio and Kirchheim
[
2
, page 68]
.
They conjectured (in our language), that every compactly supported metric
k
-current of
finite mass
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a flat
k
-chain.
We shall abbreviate this conjecture to
\operatorname{FCC}(\textnormal{AK})[k,d]
.
They also proved the converse statement that every flat
k
-chain is a metric current.
Later, the theory of metric currents was extended by Lang to include currents not of finite mass and an analogous conjecture was stated for this type of currents.
To be explicit, Lang conjectures
[
27
, page 716]
that every compactly supported metric
k
-current in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a flat
k
-chain.
We shall abbreviate this conjecture to
\operatorname{FCC}(\textnormal{L})[k,d]
.
It is obvious that
\operatorname{FCC}(\textnormal{L})[k,d]
implies
\operatorname{FCC}(\textnormal{AK})[k,d]
for any
k
and
d
.
This implication, in light of our results, is not very useful.

It appears that no results concerning
\operatorname{FCC}(\textnormal{L})[k,d]
are available in the literature other than the description of the problem itself due to Lang.
On the other hand,
\operatorname{FCC}(\textnormal{AK})[k,d]
has enjoyed much attention and has been resolved affirmatively in some non-trivial cases.
The case of
\operatorname{FCC}(\textnormal{AK})[1,d]
is known to hold due to Schioppa
[
36
, Theorem 1.6]
(with a simpler proof in the Euclidean space appearing later due to Marchese and Merlo
[
28
]
).
As we already stated in Subsection
1.1
,
\operatorname{FCC}(\textnormal{AK})[d,d]
was proven for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
by DePhilippis and Rindler
[
17
]
.
Remarkably, their approach also relies on PDEs (although, at least seemingly, a different type of a PDE problem is resolved).
It also appears that a number of PDE type conditions are known to some researchers, which would be sufficient (but not necessary) for the validity of
\operatorname{FCC}(\textnormal{AK})[k,d]
, although these findings are unpublished.
The main problem being that in most cases the PDE type conditions are actually known to fail (as, for example, in our case of the regularity of the prescribed Jacobian equation).

Finally, it should be stated that in all cases in which we disprove
\operatorname{FCC}(\textnormal{L})[k,d]
we actually produce a metric
k
-current with compact support but
infinite flat norm
.
Thus one might conjecture that any compactly supported metric
k
-current in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite flat norm is a flat chain.
We shall refer to this conjecture as
\operatorname{FCC}_{FF}(\textnormal{L})[k,d]
, the letters
FF
standing for “finite flat”.
It is natural to expect that
\operatorname{FCC}_{FF}(\textnormal{L})[k,d]
would be very closely related to
\operatorname{FCC}(\textnormal{AK})[k,d]
, however, except for the obvious implication, we are not able to assert any general relationship between the two conjectures.
The exception to this is the top dimensional case
k=d
, where the flat norm coincides with the mass norm, and therefore
\operatorname{FCC}_{FF}(\textnormal{L})[d,d]
is obviously equivalent to
\operatorname{FCC}(\textnormal{AK})[d,d]
and they both hold due to DePhilippis and Rindler.

Structure of the paper

The paper is structured as follows.
In Section

we collect some basic background material which will be needed later.
In Section

we develop basic theory of metric currents and potential forms (which arise when we try to linearise the prescribed Jacobian equation).
It is here where we obtain a proof of Theorem

.
Section

is dedicated to proving a version of the Hahn-Banach separation theorem, where it is our goal to be able to separate sets in a dual Banach space with functionals that lie in its predual.

.

2.1
Classical and metric currents

Classical currents and flat chains

We shall adopt the notation and conventions of Lang
[
27
]
with some slight adjustments.
For a more detailed overview of currents, see the book of Federer
[
20
]

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
We denote by
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and by
\mathrm{d}x_{1},\dots,\mathrm{d}x_{d}
the dual basis of
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
.
The exterior product spaces
\textstyle\bigwedge_{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\textstyle\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
are equipped with the
mass
and the
comass
norms respectively.
We recall that if
v_{1},\dots v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
have norm at most one, then the mass norm of
v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k}
is at most one.
Analogous statement holds for the comass norm.
We identify
\textstyle\bigwedge^{d}\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mathbb{R}
via the map

\lambda\mapsto\lambda\mathrm{d}x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{d}.

(2.1)

We recall the following fact concerning the size of pairings.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
v^{*}_{1},\dots,v^{*}_{k}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
and
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then

|\langle v_{1}^{*}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k}^{*},v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k}\rangle|\leq\prod_{j=1}^{k}\lVert v_{j}^{*}\rVert\lVert v_{j}\rVert,

where the norms on the right hand side are the Euclidean norm of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the dual (also Euclidean) norm of
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
.

For a map
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the symbol
\mathrm{D}f(x)\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
stands for the Frechét differential of
f
at
x
.
In particular, if
m=1
, then
\mathrm{D}f(x)\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
.
For a differential
k
-form
\omega
, we denote its exterior derivative by
\mathrm{d}\omega
.
In particular, if
\omega
is a
0
-form, then
\mathrm{d}\omega=\mathrm{D}\omega
.

We denote by
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\textstyle\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of smooth and compactly supported differential
k
-forms on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Recall that a linear functional

T\colon C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\textstyle\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}

continuous in the sense of distributions is called a
k
-current.
The linear space of all
k
-currents in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by
\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
We say that
T\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is of
finite mass
if it is represented as an integration against a
\textstyle\bigwedge_{k}\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued measure.
If
T
is of finite mass, then the induced measure is determined uniquely, its total variation measure,
\mu
, is called the
mass measure
and we denote
\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\lVert T\rVert
.
The quantity
\lVert T\rVert
is called the
mass
or
mass norm
of
T
.

For a
k
-current
T
(
k\geq 1
), we denote by
\partial T
the boundary of
T
which we recall is defined via:

\partial T(\omega)=T(\mathrm{d}\omega)\quad\text{for $C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\textstyle\bigwedge^{k-1}\mathbb{R}^{d})$.}

A
k
-current
T
is called
normal
if both
T
and
\partial T
are of finite mass.
The support of
T\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\operatorname{spt}T
, is the least closed set
F
such that
T(\omega)=0
for all
\omega\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\textstyle\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{R}^{d})
vanishing in
F
.

Flat chains

Given a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the
flat seminorm (with respect to
K
)
of a smooth differential form and the
flat norm (with respect to
K
)
of a current by

\lVert\omega\rVert_{\mathbb{F}^{k}(K)}=\max\{\sup_{x\in K}\lVert\omega(x)\rVert,\sup_{x\in K}\lVert\mathrm{d}\omega\rVert\}

and

\lVert T\rVert_{\mathbb{F}_{k}(K)}=\sup_{\lVert\omega\rVert_{\mathbb{F}^{k}(K)}\leq 1}|T(\omega)|,

respectively.
Note that
\lVert T\rVert_{\mathbb{F}_{k}(K)}<\infty
implies
\operatorname{spt}T\subset K
.

We recall the equivalent way of defining the flat norm via

\lVert T\rVert_{\mathbb{F}_{k}(K)}=\inf\{\lVert S\rVert+\lVert R\rVert:T=S+\partial R;\;\operatorname{spt}R,\operatorname{spt}S\subset K\}.

By
\mathbb{F}_{k}(K)
, we denote the set of
flat
k
-chains in
K
, i.e. the closure with respect to
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbb{F}_{k}(K)}
of normal currents supported inside
K
.
The space
\mathbb{F}_{k}(K)
is equipped with the flat norm, making it into a Banach space.
We call a
k
-current
flat
, or a
flat chain
, if it lies in
\mathbb{F}_{k}(K)
for some compact
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Next, we identify flat chains in the top dimension.
The symbol
\mathcal{L}^{d}
stands for the Lebesgue measure.
Via (
2.1
), given a
d
-form
\omega
, there exists a unique real valued map which we, using abuse of notation, denote again by
\omega
, such that

\omega=\omega\mathrm{d}x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{d}.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact, convex and has non-empty interior.
Then the map

L^{1}(Q)\ni\varphi\mapsto(\omega\mapsto\int\varphi\omega\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{d})\in\mathbb{F}_{d}(Q)

is an isometry of Banach spaces.

In light of the previous lemma, we may simply identify
\mathbb{F}_{d}(Q)=L^{1}(Q)
.

Given
T\in\mathbb{F}_{k}(K)
and a smooth map
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, one can define the pushforward of
T
by
F
, denoted by
F_{\#}T
.
The definition is given by

F_{\#}T(\omega\mathrm{d}x_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{i_{k}})=T(\eta(\omega\circ F)\mathrm{d}F_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}F_{i_{k}})\quad\text{for $\omega\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{m})$,}

where
\eta
is any smooth, compactly supported function, which is equal to
1
on a neighbourhood of
F(\operatorname{spt}T)
.
By the definition of support, the particular choice of
\eta
does not matter.
It can be verified
[
20
, 4.1.14]
that
F_{\#}T\in\mathbb{F}_{k}(F(K))
.
In particular, we have the following invariance of flatness under isometries.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
T
is a
k
-current in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
supported inside a compact set.
Let
I\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be an isometry.
If
I_{\#}T
is a flat chain, then so is
T
.

Proof.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be given by
F=I^{-1}\circ P
, where
P
is the orthonormal projection to
I(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Then one may verify that
F_{\#}I_{\#}T=T
.
Therefore, if
I_{\#}T
is a flat chain, then by the discussion above, also
T=F_{\#}I_{\#}T
is a flat chain.
∎

Metric currents

We already provided the definition of metric currents in Subsection
1.1
.
Given a locally compact metric space
X
,
the vector space of all metric
k
-currents in
X
is denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{k}(X)
.

One may naturally define
mass
and
boundary
of a metric
k
-current
[
27
]
, but we shall not need to work with these notions explicitly.
In case
X
is a Euclidean space, these notions correspond to the mass and boundary of classical currents.

The support of
T\in\mathcal{D}_{k}(X)
is the least closed set
F\subset X
such that
T(f,\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{k})=0
whenever
f=0
on
F
.

If
T
is a compactly supported metric
k
-current on
X
and
Y
is a locally compact metric space,
F\colon X\to Y
is Lipschitz, then we define the
pushforward
of
T
under
F
by

F_{\#}T(f,\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k})=T(\sigma(f\circ F),\pi_{1}\circ F,\dots,\pi_{k}\circ F)\quad\text{for $f\in\textnormal{Lip}_{c}(Y),\pi_{i}\in\textnormal{Lip}(Y)$.}

where
\sigma
is any Lipschitz, compactly supported map on
Y
with
\sigma=1
on a neighbourhood of
F(\operatorname{spt}T)
(we need to assume that
Y
is locally compact to guarantee existence of such
\sigma
).
The pushforward of a metric current is again a metric current by definition.

Relationship of classical and metric currents in the Euclidean space

Metric currents in the Euclidean space can be identified with classical currents using the so-called comparison map.
The definition we use as well as its basic properties are due to Lang
[
27
, Theorem 5.5]
though for finite mass metric currents with compact support, this has previously been done by Ambrosio and Kirchheim
[
2
, Theorem 11.1]
.
For every
k\geq 0
, there exists an injective linear map
C_{k}\colon\mathcal{D}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

C_{k}(T)(f\mathrm{d}g_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}g_{k})=T(f,g_{1},\dots,g_{k}),

for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

Given any metric space

If we identify

The goal of this section is to characterise a particular topological property of the comparison map in top dimension via the prescribed Jacobian equation.

3.1
Metric compactum

For a compact space
K
, we denote by
C(K)
the space of continuous real valued functions on
K
.
The space is equipped with the supremum norm denoted by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{C(K)}
making it into a Banach space.
We recall that the space

is separable if (and only if) the space
K
is metrisable.

We shall identify the space of metric currents on a compact metric space
X
with a subspace of
C(\mathcal{K})
for a suitable compact (metrisable) space

.
This will allow us to define a “natural” norm on the space of currents via inheritance.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a metric space
X
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
metric
k
-compactum
is the topological space

\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}(X)=\textnormal{Lip}_{b,1}(X)\times[\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(X)]^{k},

equipped with the product topology.

Let
X
be compact.
Then
it follows from the considerations in Subsection
2.2
that the space
\mathcal{K}(X)
is compact and metrisable.
Recall that
\mathcal{D}_{k}(X)
stands for the space of metric
k
-currents on
X
.
Since
\textnormal{Lip}_{b}(X)=\textnormal{Lip}_{c}(X)
, any element
T
of
\mathcal{D}_{k}(X)
restricts, by definition, to a map
T\colon\mathcal{K}(X)\to\mathbb{R}
.
We shall identify
T=T_{|\mathcal{K}(X)}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
X
be a compact metric space and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then
\mathcal{D}_{k}(X)\subset C(\mathcal{K}(X))
.

Proof.

This follows from the joint continuity property.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Suppose
X
is compact and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
For any current
T\in\mathcal{D}_{k}(X)
,

|T(f,\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{k})|\leq\lVert T\rVert_{C(\mathcal{K})}\lVert f\rVert_{L}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\textnormal{Lip}(\pi^{j}),

for every
(f,\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{1})\in\textnormal{Lip}_{b}(X)\times[\textnormal{Lip}(X)]^{k}
.

Proof.

By locality, we may subtract constant maps from each of the last
d
variables of

.
Thus, it is sufficient to prove the inequality assuming
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{d}\in\textnormal{Lip}_{0}(X)
.
By homogeneity in each variable (on both sides of the inequality), it is sufficient to prove the statement under the additional assumption that
(f,\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{k})\in\mathcal{K}(X)
.
Then the statement holds by definition of
\lVert T\rVert_{C(\mathcal{K})}
.
∎

3.2
Potential forms and the PDE

In this subsection we develop some basic theory of what we call potential forms, which make up a linear space that is in natural duality with the space of metric currents (cf. the work of Pankka and Soultanis
[
31
]
, where a different notion of duality is explored).

We fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
and denote
Q=[0,1]^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This is the exclusive underlying compact pointed metric space with which we shall be concerned.
The distinguished point is
0
.
We shall also only be discussing
d
-currents and therefore we fix (consistently with the previous subsection) the metric
d
-compactum

\mathcal{K}=\textnormal{Lip}_{b,1}(Q)\times[\textnormal{Lip}_{1}(Q)]^{d}.

When working with spaces over
Q
, such as
L^{1}(Q)
,
L^{\infty}(Q)
various Lipschitz spaces, or spaces of
forms
and

which we define later, we will often omit writing out the symbol
Q
and write only
L^{1}
,
L^{\infty}
etc.

Definition 3.4
.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, any expression of the form

\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i}),

where

(i)

the summation is implicitly assumed to be over
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

(ii)

for each
i
,
f_{i}\in\textnormal{Lip}_{b}(Q)
and
\pi_{i}\in[\textnormal{Lip}(Q)]^{d}
and

(iii)

s(\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i}))=\sum_{i}\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{L}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\textnormal{Lip}(\pi_{i}^{j})<\infty.

is called a
formal Lipschitz sum
.
We denote by
\mathcal{A}_{d}
the
set
of formal Lipschitz sums.

Expressions such as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f_{i},\pi_{i})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
are identified with the formal Lipschitz sum
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})
where
f_{i}=0
and
\pi_{i}=0
for
i>n
.

Next, we define an equivalence relation
\sim
on
\mathcal{A}_{d}
and then a vector space structure on
\mathcal{A}_{d}/\penalty\sim
.
The equivalence relation is given by
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})\sim\sum_{i}(g_{i},\kappa_{i})
if

\sum_{i}T(f_{i},\pi_{i})=\sum_{i}T(g_{i},\kappa_{i})\quad\text{for every $T\in\mathcal{D}_{d}$.}

The definition if valid due to
(iii)
and Corollary
3.3
.
Indeed, the sums appearing in the definition are even guaranteed to be converging absolutely.

Given a countable set
S
, any family
\{(f_{s},\pi_{s})\}_{s\in S}
such that

\sum_{s\in S}\lVert f_{s}\rVert_{L}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\textnormal{Lip}(\pi_{s}^{d})<\infty,

and any two bijections
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\colon\mathbb{N}\to S

\sum_{i}(f_{\rho_{1}(i)},\pi_{\rho_{1}(i)})\sim\sum_{i}(f_{\rho_{2}(i)},\pi_{\rho_{2}(i)}).

Thus, we define the common value to be
[\sum_{s}(f_{s},\pi_{s})]
.

Using this notation, we may define addition on
\mathcal{A}_{d}/\penalty\sim
via

[\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})]+[\sum_{i}(g_{i},\kappa_{i})]=[\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}(f_{i},\pi_{i})+(g_{j},\kappa_{j})],

We also define scalar multiplication

\lambda[\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})]=[\sum_{i}(\lambda f_{i},\pi_{i})].

It is immediately verified that under these operations,
\mathcal{A}_{d}/\penalty\sim
is a vector space.

Observe the inequality (cf. (

\lVert f\det\mathrm{D}\pi\rVert_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\textnormal{Lip}(\pi^{j}),

which guarantees, that whenever
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})
is a formal Lipschitz sum, the sum
\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}
converges absolutely in
L^{\infty}(Q)
.

Definition 3.5
.

Two formal Lipschitz sums
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})
and
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})
are called
weakly equivalent
if

\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}=\sum_{i}g_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa_{i}\quad\text{a.e.~in $Q$,}

where the convergence is absolute in
L^{\infty}(Q)
.

It is very natural to expect that two formal Lipschitz sums are equivalent if and only if they are weakly equivalent.
We are, however, not able to assert the validity of this statement.

Recall the convention that we usually omit writing out the differential
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{d}
when integrating with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

Lemma 3.6
.

If two formal Lipschitz sums are equivalent, then they are also weakly equivalent.

Proof.

For a function

, we denote by
T_{u}
the metric
d

T_{u}(f,\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{d})=\int_{Q}uf\det\mathrm{D}\pi\quad\text{for $(f,\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{1})\in\textnormal{Lip}_{b}\times[\textnormal{Lip}]^{d}$}.

Suppose now
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})
and
\sum_{i}(g_{i},\kappa_{i})
are formal Lipschitz sums.
Then we have

\sum_{i}T_{u}(f_{i},\pi_{i})=\sum_{i}\int_{Q}uf_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}=\int_{Q}u\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i},

by Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem.
Similar identity holds for
\sum_{i}(g_{i},\kappa_{i})
.
Therefore, since every
T_{u}
is a metric

\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})
and
\sum_{i}(g_{i},\kappa_{i})
are equivalent, then in particular

\int_{Q}u\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}=\int_{Q}u\sum_{i}g_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa_{i}\quad\text{for every $u\in L^{1}(Q)$.}

Thus, the sums are weakly equivalent.

Definition 3.11

For a current
T\in\mathcal{D}_{d}(Q)
, we define its
potential norm
by

\lVert T\rVert_{\mathbb{P}_{d}}=\sup_{\lVert\omega\rVert_{\mathbb{P}^{d}}\leq 1}|\langle\omega,T\rangle|.

The space
\mathcal{D}_{d}(Q)
equipped with the potential norm is called the space of potential
d
-currents and denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{d}=\mathbb{P}_{d}(Q)
.

Theorem 3.12
.

For every metric
d
-current
T\in\mathbb{P}_{d}
, we have

\lVert T\rVert_{\mathbb{P}_{d}}=\lVert T\rVert_{C(\mathcal{K})}.

In particular, the former quantity is finite.

Proof.

The inequality

\lVert T\rVert_{\mathbb{P}_{d}}\geq\lVert T\rVert_{C(\mathcal{K})}

is immediate, as the supremum on the left hand side is taken over a larger set.
We show the converse inequality.
Utilizing Corollary
3.3
, we obtain

\begin{split}\lVert T\rVert_{\mathbb{P}_{d}}&=\sup_{\lVert\omega\rVert_{\mathbb{P}^{d}}\leq 1}|T(\omega)|=\sup\{\sum_{i}T(f_{i},\pi_{i}):s(\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i}))\leq 1\}\\
&=\sup\{\sum_{i}T(f_{i},\pi_{i}):\sum_{i}\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{L}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\textnormal{Lip}(\pi^{j}_{i})\leq 1\}\\
&{\leq}\sup\{\sum_{i}\lVert T\rVert_{C(\mathcal{K})}\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{L}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\textnormal{Lip}(\pi^{j}_{i}):\sum_{i}\lVert f_{i}\rVert_{L}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\textnormal{Lip}(\pi^{j}_{i})\leq 1\}\\
&\leq\lVert T\rVert_{C(\mathcal{K})}.\end{split}

∎

Remark 3.13
.

A modified version of this construction can be carried out for a set
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is, say, open.
In this case, the space of potential currents (those for which the potential norm is finite) would consist of those metric currents, that have only a controlled “explosion” at the boundary of
Q
and therefore, in general, the space of potential currents cannot be expected to coincide with all metric currents.
This justifies the naming convention.

Theorem 3.14
.

The space
\mathbb{P}_{d}
is an isometric closed subspace of
C(\mathcal{K})
, therefore it is a separable Banach space.

Proof.

The fact that
\mathbb{P}_{d}
is an isometric subspace of
C(\mathcal{K})
follows from Theorem

.
We show that it is a closed subspace.
To that end, let

be a sequence converging to

with respect to the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{C(\mathcal{K})}
.
Note that since each

is actually a functional on all of
\textnormal{Lip}_{b}\times[\textnormal{Lip}]^{d}
, the map
T
has a natural extension to
\textnormal{Lip}_{b}\times[\textnormal{Lip}]^{d}
given, for example, by

Since pointwise limits of linear functionals are linear, it follows that

is multilinear.
Since
T
lies in the space

, it is jointly continuous.
It remains to show that

is local.
This is immediate since whenever

and

are such that one of

.
Hence also

.
∎

Remark 3.15
.

Several interesting open questions concerning the functional-analytic structure of the introduced spaces arise.
Firstly, by Theorem

we see that we have a canonical embedding
\mathbb{P}_{d}\to(\mathbb{P}^{d})^{*}
, which is isometric.
On the other hand,
we have the canonical embedding
I\colon\mathbb{P}^{d}\to(\mathbb{P}_{d})^{*}
,
given by

(3.4)

Is

onto?
Is

an isomorphism of Banach spaces?
If

is onto, then (since it is injective), it is also a Banach space isomorphism, which equates to an identification of the dual space

(up to constants).
This would be a result akin to Wolffe’s theorem on duality of flat forms

In the following, we provide a simple characterisation of the surjectivity of

via a compactness statement.
This result is not needed or used further in the present paper, but it is of independent interest.

Proposition 3.16
.

The following two statements are equivalent.

(i)

For any bounded sequence
\omega_{j}\in\mathbb{P}^{d}
a subsequence
\omega_{j_{k}}
and a potential form
\omega\in\mathbb{P}^{d}
may be found in such a way that for every

\langle\omega_{j_{k}},T\rangle\to\langle\omega,T\rangle.

(ii)

The operator

is surjective and a Banach space isomorphism.

Proof.

For
\lambda\geq 0
, we denote by
\lambda B_{\mathbb{P}^{d}}
the ball in
\mathbb{P}^{d}
around
0
of radius
\lambda
.
Since the linear space

separates points of
\mathbb{P}_{d}
(by definition), it is weak
∗
dense in

by the bipolar theorem
[

, Chapter 5, 1.8]
.
Thus,

is surjective if and only

is weak

closed.
By the Kreǐn-Shmul’yan theorem
[

, Chapter 5, 12.1]
, this is the case if and only if

is weak

closed for any

.
Since

is separable, the weak

topology restricted to any ball is metrisable and hence by the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, the above is equivalent to requiring that

be sequentially weak

We now show the implication
(i)

\implies

Since

is bounded, the sequence

is bounded in

Using

We have shown that

is sequentially weak

follows by the paragraph above.

The implication

\implies

(i)
is an immediate consequence of the open mapping theorem and Banach-Alaoglu.
∎

4
A Hahn-Banach separation theorem

The point of this section is to develop a tool from functional analysis, which will allow us to pass from a particularly strong result on the operator
\operatorname{Em}
(into
L^{\infty}
) to a result for the operator
\operatorname{Em}_{*}
(on
L^{1}(Q)
), asserting eventually strong Lipschitz non-regularity for the prescribed Jacobian equation.

We use the following notation.
For a normed linear space
X
and
\lambda\geq 0
, we denote by
\lambda B_{X}
the ball in
X
around
0
of radius
\lambda
.
We also use the convention
B_{X}=1B_{X}
.
The weak
∗
topology on a dual Banach space
Y^{*}
is denoted by
w^{*}
and the symbol
(Y^{*},w^{*})
stands for the locally convex topological space
Y^{*}
equipped with the weak
∗
topology induced by the predual
Y
.
The predual
Y
is always assumed to be a Banach space.
We will identify
Y
with
\epsilon(Y)\subset Y^{**}
, where
\epsilon
is the canonical embedding.
That is, any element
y
of
Y
is identified with the functional acting on
Y^{*}
via

y=(Y^{*}\ni\rho\mapsto\rho(y)\in\mathbb{R}).

For the proof of the following version of a Hahn-Banach separation theorem, see the book of Conway
[
11
, Chapter IV, 3.7 Theorem]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
X
is a topological vector space and
A,B\subset X
are disjoint convex sets.
If
A
is open, then there exists
f\in X^{*}
such that

f(x)<\inf_{B}f\quad\text{for each $x\in A$.}

Firstly, we apply the lemma in case
K
is an absolutely convex subset of a dual Banach space.
Recall that
K
is absolutely convex if
K
is convex and whenever
x\in K
, then also
-x\in K
.
Given a Banach space
Y
,
u\in Y
and
\rho\in Y^{*}
, we use the interchangeable notation

\langle\rho,u\rangle=u(\rho)=\rho(u).

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose
Y
is a Banach space and
K\subset Y^{*}
is absolutely convex.
Suppose
A\subset Y^{*}
is weak
∗
open, convex,
\rho\in A
an
A\cap K=\emptyset
.
Then there exists
u\in Y
such that

u(\rho)>\sup_{k\in K}|u(k)|.

Proof.

The space
(Y^{*},w^{*})
is a topological vector space such that
(Y^{*},w^{*})^{*}=Y
.
We find
f\in Y
from Lemma
4.1
achieving

f(\eta)<\inf_{k\in K}f(k)\quad\text{for every $\eta\in A$.}

In particular,

f(\rho)<\inf_{k\in K}f(k).

By multiplying both sides with
-1
, we obtain

-f(\rho)>-\inf_{k\in K}f(k)=\sup_{k\in K}-f(k)=\sup_{k\in K}f(k),

where the last equality follows from the fact that
K
is absolutely convex.
The last equality also implies that

\sup_{k\in K}f(k)=\sup_{k\in K}|f(k)|,

which in combination with the above yields for
u=-f

u(\rho)>\sup_{k\in K}|-u(k)|=\sup_{k\in K}|u(k)|.

∎

Definition 4.3
.

Suppose
X
and
Y
are Banach spaces and
T\colon X\to Y^{*}
is a bounded linear operator.
We say that
T
is
strongly non-surjective
if for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
\rho_{n}\in\frac{1}{n}B_{Y^{*}}\cap T(X)
and a weak
∗
open neighbourhood
U_{n}
of
\rho_{n}
such that

U\cap T(B_{X})=\emptyset.

It follows from the open mapping theorem, that whenever
T
is surjective, it cannot be strongly non-surjective, justifying the naming convention.
In general, the property of strong non-surjectivity is interesting in cases where
T(X)
is weak
∗
dense in
Y^{*}
in which case it is strictly stronger than non-surjectivity.
Indeed, the identity operator

\operatorname{Id}:C([0,1])\to L^{\infty}([0,1]),

(4.1)

is non-surjective, has weak
∗
dense range, but it is not strongly non-surjective, because
B_{C([0,1])}
is weak
∗
dense inside
B_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}
.
One can verify the non-trivial implication between non-surjectivity and strong non-surjectivity if, additionally,
X
is a dual Banach space and
T
is weak
∗
to weak
∗
continuous.
In more precise terms, suppose
X=Z^{*}
for some Banach space
Z
and the operator
T
is weak
∗
to weak
∗
continuous and onto a weak
∗
dense set.
Then it can be shown that if
T
is non-surjective, then it is strongly non-surjective.
The considerations needed for this assertion are standard, although they require some fairly advanced machinery, such as the Kreǐn-Shmul’yan theorem.
Since this is not something we require in the sequel, we leave the details to the reader.

From these considerations, we see that the notion of strong non-surjectivity is useful, whenever we are not able to verify that the space
X
is a dual Banach space, which is the situation to which we wish to apply the developed theory.
Indeed, recall the diagram in Figure
1
.
The desired application for the theory developed in this section is for
X=\mathbb{P}^{d}
,
Y^{*}=L^{\infty}
and
T=\operatorname{Em}
.
However, since we are not able to assert that
\mathbb{P}^{d}
is a dual Banach space (cf. Remark
3.15
and Proposition
3.16
), strong non-surjectivity does not necessarily follow from non-surjectivity.
On the other hand, we can use the following theorem to obtain strong Lipschitz non-regularity from strong non-surjectivity of
\operatorname{Em}
(see Corollary
4.6
for the explicit statement).

Theorem 4.4

(Predual separation theorem)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces and
T\colon X\to Y^{*}
a bounded linear operator, which is additionally strongly non-surjective.
Then there exists a sequence
v_{n}\in Y
such that

(i)

\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{Y}\geq 1
,

(ii)

\sup_{x\in B_{X}}|\langle T(x),v_{n}\rangle|\leq\frac{1}{n}
.

Proof.

Let
K=T(B_{X})
.
Since
B_{X}
is absolutely convex and
T
is linear,
K
is also absolutely convex.
By definition of the weak
∗
topology (the fact that it is locally convex suffices) there exists a convex open set
V_{n}\subset U_{n}
with
\rho\in V_{n}
.
Let us take
A=V_{n}
and apply Proposition
4.2
obtaining thereby
u_{n}\in Y
such that

u_{n}(\rho_{n})>\sup_{x\in B_{X}}|u_{n}(T(x))|.

(4.2)

Since the right hand side is larger than or equal to
0
, we in particular have
u_{n}(\rho_{n})>0
.
Let

v_{n}=\frac{1}{nu_{n}(\rho_{n})}u_{n}\in Y.

Then we have

\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{Y}\geq|v_{n}(n\rho_{n})|=\frac{1}{nu_{n}(\rho_{n})}u_{n}(n\rho_{n})=1,

by definition of
v_{n}
, using also the fact that
\lVert n\rho_{n}\rVert_{Y^{*}}\leq 1
.
This asserts item
(i)
.
For item
(ii)
, we have

\sup_{x\in B_{X}}|\langle T(x),v_{n}\rangle|=\sup_{x\in B_{X}}|\langle T(x),\frac{1}{nu_{n}(\rho_{n})}u_{n}\rangle|<\frac{1}{nu_{n}(\rho_{n})}u_{n}(\rho_{n})=\frac{1}{n},

where in the last inequality we used (
4.2
).
∎

Remark 4.5
.

If
X
is a normed linear space,
Y
is a Banach space and
T\colon X\to Y^{*}
a bounded linear operator which has weak
∗
range, then it can be shown that if a sequence
v_{n}
as in Theorem

exists, then

is strongly non-surjective.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

, one may take

and verify, by
(ii)
, that

and, by
(i)
and weak

density of the range of

that there is an element

.
Since the factor

is irrelevant, strong non-surjectivity is thus proven.

This allows us to provide an example of an isometric operator with weak

dense range which is strongly non-surjective (cf. the previous example (
4.1
)).
Let
Y
be any Banach space with
\dim Y^{**}/\penalty Y=\infty
and let

be the norm closed subspace constructed by Davis and Lindenstrauss
[

, which is

The following corollary justifies the developed theory of potential forms.
The relevant spaces are once again considered over

Corollary 4.6
.

Suppose the operator
\operatorname{Em}\colon\mathbb{P}^{d}\to L^{\infty}

Proof.

Applying the Predual separation Theorem

to

,

5
Strong Lipschitz non-regularity of the prescribed Jacobian equation

In this section, we show that if
d\geq 2
, then
\operatorname{Em}
is strongly non-surjective and that therefore the prescribed Jacobian equation has strong Lipschitz non-regularity.

We recall the convention that the differential
\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{d}
is usually omitted from integration notation and so we write for example

\int_{Q}f\det\mathrm{D}\pi=\int_{Q}f\det\mathrm{D}\pi\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{d}.

Throughout this section, the letter
Q
will generally be used to denote a generic cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and not refer to the unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
.

5.1
Average determinants

Given a set
E
and a map
f\colon E\to\mathbb{R}
, we use the notation

\lVert f\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(E)}=\sup_{x\in E}|f(x)|.

We shall also use
\sigma
to denote the surface measure of the boundary of the unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\geq 2
, there exists a constant
c_{d}\in(0,\infty)
such that the following holds.
For every cube
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of side length
r>0
, and every pair of Lipschitz maps
\pi,\kappa\colon Q\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

|\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\pi-\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa|\leq c_{d}L^{d-1}r^{d-1}\lVert\pi-\kappa\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)},

(5.1)

where
L
is the maximum of the Lipschitz constants of the functions
\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{d},\kappa^{1},\dots,\kappa^{d}
.
The constant
c_{d}
may be taken as the product of the number
d
and the number of faces a
d
dimensional cube has.

Proof.

Firstly, by the joint continuity property of determinants (Lemma
2.7
), it is sufficient to prove the statement for maps
\pi
and
\kappa
which are defined and smooth on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Since determinant is a multilinear map, we have the formula

\begin{split}&\det D(\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{d})-\det\mathrm{D}(\kappa^{1},\dots,\kappa^{d})=\det\mathrm{D}(\pi^{1}-\kappa^{1},\pi^{2},\dots,\pi^{d})\\
&+\det\mathrm{D}(\kappa^{1},\pi^{2}-\kappa^{2},\pi^{3},\dots,\pi^{d})+\dots+\det\mathrm{D}(\kappa^{1},\dots,\kappa^{i-1},\pi^{i}-\kappa^{i},\pi^{i+1},\dots,\pi^{d})\\
&+\dots+\det\mathrm{D}(\kappa^{1},\dots,\kappa^{d-1},\pi^{d}-\kappa^{d}).\end{split}

Since determinant is also anti-symmetric, the above formula implies that also

\begin{split}&\det D(\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{d})-\det\mathrm{D}(\kappa^{1},\dots,\kappa^{d})=\det\mathrm{D}(\pi^{1}-\kappa^{1},\pi^{2},\dots,\pi^{d})\\
&-\det\mathrm{D}(\pi^{2}-\kappa^{2},\kappa^{1},\pi^{3},\dots,\pi^{d})+\dots+(-1)^{i-1}\det\mathrm{D}(\pi^{i}-\kappa^{i},\kappa^{1},\dots,\kappa^{i-1},\pi^{i+1},\dots,\pi^{d})\\
&+\dots+(-1)^{d-1}\det\mathrm{D}(\pi^{d}-\kappa^{d},\kappa^{1},\dots,\kappa^{d-1}).\end{split}

Therefore, using the Stokes theorem (
2.4
) for each of the summands yields

\begin{split}\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\pi\ -\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa\ =\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\partial Q}(\pi^{i}-\kappa^{i})\langle\omega_{i}(\kappa,\pi),\operatorname{Tan}_{\partial Q}\rangle\mathrm{d}\sigma,\end{split}

where

\omega_{i}(\kappa,\pi)=(-1)^{i-1}\mathrm{d}\kappa^{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}\kappa^{i-1}\wedge\mathrm{d}\pi^{i+1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}\pi^{d}.

In particular, by triangle inequality,

|\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\pi-\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa\ |\leq\sum_{i}|\int_{\partial Q}(\pi^{i}-\kappa^{i})\langle\omega_{i}(\kappa,\pi),\operatorname{Tan}_{\partial Q}\rangle\mathrm{d}\sigma|

(5.2)

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, by (
2.3
), we have

|\langle\omega_{i}(\kappa,\pi),\operatorname{Tan}_{\partial Q}\rangle|\leq L^{d-1},

so that (
5.2
) implies

|\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\pi-\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa|\leq\sum_{i}\lVert\pi^{i}-\kappa^{i}\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)}L^{d-1}\sigma(\partial Q).

Let us denote by
\mathcal{F}(d)
the number of faces a
d
-dimensional cube has.
Then
\sigma(Q)=r^{d-1}\mathcal{F}(d)
so that in particular

|\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\pi-\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa|\leq d\mathcal{F}(d)r^{d-1}L^{d-1}\lVert\pi-\kappa\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)},

which is (
5.1
).
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Given a cube
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a function
f\colon Q\to\mathbb{R}
, we let
f_{Q}
be the value of
f
at the center of the cube
Q
.
For any cube
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of side length
r>0
and any Lipschitz maps
f,g\colon Q\to\mathbb{R}
and
\pi,\kappa\colon Q\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds that

\begin{split}\left|\int_{Q}f\det\mathrm{D}\pi-\int_{Q}g\det\mathrm{D}\kappa\right|&\leq r^{d+1}(\textnormal{Lip}(f)+\textnormal{Lip}(g))\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{d}L^{d}\\
&+r^{d}|f_{Q}-g_{Q}|L^{d}+r^{d-1}|f_{Q}|c_{d}L^{d-1}\lVert\pi-\kappa\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)},\end{split}

(5.3)

where
L
is the maximum of the Lipschitz constants of the functions
\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{d},\kappa^{1},\dots,\kappa^{d}
.

Before we continue with the proof of Lemma
5.2
, since the estimate is quite technical, we first point out why it will later be useful.
There are four summands on the right hand side of (
5.3
).
At the end of our argument, we achieve a contradiciton, by showing that each of the summands is much smaller than the quantity on the left hand side.
This will be achieved very easily for each of the summands, except the last one, where most work needs to be carried out.
The first two summands are dealt with easily simply by taking a small enough cube, due to the factor of
r^{d+1}
(while the left hand side only scales as
r^{d}
).
Furthermore, in our setup, it will be easy to achieve
|f_{Q}-g_{Q}|\lesssim r
, so that the third summand will also be very small for small enough
r>0
.
To deal with the final summand, we will need to cleverly find particular cubes
Q
on which the estimate
\lVert\pi-\kappa\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}}\lesssim\varepsilon r
holds for small
\varepsilon
.
This means that the fourth summand, on these cubes, is asymptotically (for small
r>0
) equivalent to
\varepsilon r^{d}
, which leads to a contradiction for small enough
\varepsilon>0
.

Since in reality we are interested in infinite sums of functions of the form
\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}
, the particular powers of the number
L
in (
5.3
) are also very important and it turns out that powers appearing in the estimate (
5.3
) are precisely what is needed to carry out the argument.
How exactly this works out is partially illuminated by Lemmma
5.3
.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

We write

\begin{split}&\left|\int_{Q}f\det\mathrm{D}\pi-\int_{Q}g\det\mathrm{D}\kappa\right|\leq\left|\int_{Q}f\det\mathrm{D}\pi-\int_{Q}f_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\pi\right|\\
&+\left|\int_{Q}f_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\pi-\int_{Q}f_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa\right|+\left|\int_{Q}f_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa-\int_{Q}g_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa\right|\\
&+\left|\int_{Q}g_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa-\int_{Q}g\det\mathrm{D}\kappa\right|.\end{split}

(5.4)

Since
|\det\mathrm{D}\pi|\leq L^{d}
a.e. in
Q
and
|f-f_{Q}|\leq r\textnormal{Lip}(f)\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{d}
, we have

\left|\int_{Q}f\det\mathrm{D}\pi-\int_{Q}f_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\pi\right|\leq r^{d+1}\textnormal{Lip}(f)\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{d}L^{d}.

(5.5)

Analogously, we also obtain

\left|\int_{Q}g_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa-\int_{Q}g\det\mathrm{D}\kappa\right|\leq r^{d+1}\textnormal{Lip}(g)\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{d}L^{d}.

(5.6)

Using Lemma
5.1
,

\begin{split}\left|\int_{Q}f_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\pi-\int_{Q}f_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa\right|&=|f_{Q}|\left|\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\pi-\int_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa\right|\\
&\leq r^{d-1}|f_{Q}|c_{d}L^{d-1}\lVert\pi-\kappa\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)}.\end{split}

(5.7)

Using once again the fact that
|\det\mathrm{D}\kappa|\leq L^{d}
, we obtain

\left|\int_{Q}f_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa-\int_{Q}g_{Q}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa\right|\leq\int_{Q}|f_{Q}-g_{Q}||\det\mathrm{D}\kappa|\leq r^{d}|f_{Q}-g_{Q}|L^{d}.

(5.8)

Combining (
5.4
) with the estimates (
5.5
), (
5.7
), (
5.8
) and (
5.6
) yields the required inequality.
∎

For the following lemma, recall the Definition
3.4
of formal Lipschitz sums.

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose
Q\subset[0,1]^{d}
is a cube of side length
r>0
and
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})
and
\sum_{i}(g_{i},\kappa_{i})
are formal Lipschitz sums.
Suppose further that
L_{i}
stands for the maximum of the Lipschitz constants of the functions
\pi_{i}^{1},\dots,\pi_{i}^{d},\kappa^{1}_{i},\dots,\kappa^{d}_{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then

\begin{split}|\int_{Q}\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}\ &-\int_{Q}\sum_{i}g_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa_{i}|\\
&\leq r^{d+1}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{d}\sum_{i}(\textnormal{Lip}(f_{i})+\textnormal{Lip}(g_{i}))L^{d}_{i}\\
&+r^{d}\sum_{i}|(f_{i})_{Q}-(g_{i})_{Q}|L_{i}^{d}\\
&+r^{d-1}c_{d}\sup_{i}\{|(f_{i})_{Q}|\}\sqrt{\sum_{i}L_{i}^{d}}\sqrt{\sum_{i}L_{i}^{d-2}\lVert\pi_{i}-\kappa_{i}\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)}^{2}}.\end{split}

(5.9)

Proof.

Since integration over
Q
is a continuous linear functional and
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})
is a formal Lipschitz sum, it follows that

\int_{Q}\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}=\sum_{i}\int_{Q}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}.

and similarly for the
g_{i}
’s and
\kappa_{i}
’s.
Therefore, from the triangle inequality and Lemma
5.2
we have

\begin{split}&|\int_{Q}\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}-\int_{Q}\sum_{i}g_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa_{i}|\leq\sum_{i}|\int_{Q}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}-\int_{Q}g_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\kappa_{i}|\\
&\leq r^{d+1}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{d}\sum_{i}(\textnormal{Lip}(f_{i})+\textnormal{Lip}(g_{i}))L^{d}_{i}+r^{d}\sum_{i}|(f_{i})_{Q}-(g_{i})_{Q}|L_{i}^{d}\\
&+r^{d-1}c_{d}\sum_{i}|(f_{i})_{Q}|L_{i}^{d-1}\lVert\pi_{i}-\kappa_{i}\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)}.\end{split}

(5.10)

Writing
L^{d-1}
as
L^{\frac{d}{2}}L^{\frac{d}{2}-1}
and using Holder’s inequality yields

\begin{split}\sum_{i}|(f_{i})_{Q}|L_{i}^{d-1}\lVert\pi_{i}-\kappa_{i}\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)}&\leq\sup_{i}\{|(f_{i})_{Q}|\}\sum_{i}L^{\frac{d}{2}}(L^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\lVert\pi_{i}-\kappa_{i}\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)})\\
&\leq\sup_{i}\{|(f_{i})_{Q}|\}\sqrt{\sum_{i}L_{i}^{d}}\sqrt{\sum_{i}L_{i}^{d-2}\lVert\pi_{i}-\kappa_{i}\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)}^{2}}.\end{split}

The last estimate combined with (
5.10
) yields the desired inequality (
5.9
).
∎

Note that even if it assumed that
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})
and
\sum_{i}(g_{i},\kappa_{i})
are formal Lipschitz sums, it is still possible that the right hand side of the estimate (
5.9
) is infinite.
This may for example happen if
L_{i}
is much larger than

\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}\textnormal{Lip}(\pi_{i}^{j})\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}.

To avoid this issue (which is really only of technical nature) we shall consider sums where
L_{i}
is the correct number to use.

Definition 5.4
.

A formal Lipschitz sum
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\kappa_{i})
is called
regular
if

(i)

for each
i
either
f_{i}=\pi_{i}=0
or

\max\{\textnormal{Lip}(f_{i}),\rVert f_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\}=1

and

\textnormal{Lip}(\pi_{i}^{j})=\textnormal{Lip}(\pi_{i}^{k})\quad\text{for all $j,k\in\{1,\dots,d\}$,}

(ii)

for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
\pi_{i}(0)=0
.

If the regular formal Lipschitz sum with which we are working is clear from context, the symbol
L_{i}
stands for the value

\textnormal{Lip}(\pi_{i}^{j})\quad\text{where $j\in\{1,\dots,d\}$ is arbitrary.}

Proposition 5.5
.

For every formal Lipschitz sum
\sum_{i}(g_{i},\kappa_{i})
, there exists a regular formal Lipschitz sum
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})\sim\sum_{i}(g_{i},\kappa_{i})
.
If
\sum(f_{i},\pi_{i})
is regular, then

s(\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i}))=\sum_{i}L_{i}^{d}.

Proof.

The latter statement follows immediately from the definitions.
For the former statement, suppose
\sum_{i}(g_{i},\kappa_{i})
is a formal Lipschitz sum.
Let
I_{0}\subset\mathbb{N}
stand for the set of those indices, for which

T(g_{i},\kappa_{i})=0\quad\text{for every $T\in\mathbb{P}_{d}([0,1]^{d})$.}

Let
I_{1}=\mathbb{N}\setminus I_{0}
and let
i\in I_{1}
.
Then it is necessary that
\textnormal{Lip}(\kappa_{i}^{j})>0
for every
j=1,\dots,d
and
g_{i}\not=0
.
We let

L_{i}=\left(\max\{\textnormal{Lip}(g_{i}),\lVert g_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\textnormal{Lip}(\kappa_{i}^{j})\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}.

Furthermore, we define

f_{i}=\frac{g_{i}}{\max\{\textnormal{Lip}(g_{i}),\lVert g_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\}};\quad\pi_{i}^{j}=\frac{\kappa^{j}_{i}}{\textnormal{Lip}(\kappa^{j}_{i})}L_{i}-\frac{\kappa^{j}_{i}(0)}{\textnormal{Lip}(\kappa^{j}_{i})}L_{i}.

For
i\in I_{0}
, we let
f_{i}=0
and
\pi_{i}=0
.
It is now easily checked that
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})
is regular and, consistently with the convention in Definition
5.4
,
L_{i}
agrees with
\textnormal{Lip}(\pi_{i}^{j})
.
Using multilinearity and locality of currents, we have for any
i\in I_{1}
and
T\in\mathbb{P}_{d}([0,1]^{d})

T(f_{i},\pi_{i})=\frac{1}{\max\{\textnormal{Lip}(g_{i}),\lVert g_{i}\rVert_{\infty}\}}\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{d}\textnormal{Lip}(\kappa^{j}_{i})}L_{i}^{d}T(g_{i},\kappa_{i})=T(g_{i},\kappa_{i}).

If
i\in I_{0}
, then
T(g_{i},\kappa_{i})=0=T(0,0)=T(f_{i},\pi_{i})
.
Thus, in particular,

\sum_{i}T(f_{i},\pi_{i})=\sum_{i}T(g_{i},\kappa_{i}).

By definition, this means
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})\sim\sum_{i}(g_{i},\kappa_{i})
.
∎

In light of the previous proposition, until we state and prove the penultimate result, we shall restrict our attention to regular sums.

Lemma 5.6
.

Suppose
Q
is a cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of side length
r>0
.
Suppose that
Q^{\prime}
is another cube of the same radius which shares a face with
Q
and assume
Q,Q^{\prime}\subset[0,1]^{d}
.
Let
\tau=c_{Q^{\prime}}-c_{Q}
, where
c_{Q}
and
c_{Q^{\prime}}
are the centres of
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
respectively.
Suppose
W_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
is a sequence of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i})
be a regular formal Lipschitz sum.
For each
i
, let

\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)=\pi_{i}(x-\tau)-W_{i}.

Suppose that for each
i
,
L_{i}=\textnormal{Lip}(\pi_{i}^{j})
,
j=1,\dots,d
and

S=s(\sum_{i}(f_{i},\pi_{i}))=\sum_{i}L^{d}_{i}.

Then

\begin{split}&\left|\int_{Q}\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}-\int_{Q^{\prime}}\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}\right|\\
&\leq r^{d+1}(\sqrt{d}+1)S+r^{d-1}c_{d}\sqrt{S}\sqrt{\sum_{i}L_{i}^{d-2}\lVert\pi_{i}-\tilde{\pi}_{i}\rVert^{2}_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)}}.\end{split}

(5.11)

Proof.

Let us denote
f_{i}^{\tau}(x)=f_{i}(x-\tau)
.
Since translation by
\tau
is an isometry and since
W^{i}
is a constant vector, we have

\int_{Q^{\prime}}\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{d}=\int_{Q}\sum_{i}f_{i}^{\tau}\det\mathrm{D}\tilde{\pi}_{i}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}^{d}.

Therefore, applying (
5.9
), we obtain

\begin{split}&\left|\int_{Q}\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}-\int_{Q^{\prime}}\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}\right|\leq r^{d+1}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{d}\sum_{i}(\textnormal{Lip}(f_{i})+\textnormal{Lip}(f_{i}^{\tau}))L^{d}_{i}\\
&+r^{d}\sum_{i}|(f_{i})_{Q}-(f_{i})_{Q^{\prime}}|L_{i}^{d}\\
&+r^{d-1}c_{d}\sup_{i}\{|(f_{i})_{Q}|\}\sqrt{\sum_{i}L_{i}^{d}}\sqrt{\sum_{i}L_{i}^{d-2}\lVert\pi_{i}-\tilde{\pi}_{i}\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)}^{2}}.\end{split}

Using that the formal Lipschitz sum is regular, we have

\textnormal{Lip}(f_{i})+\textnormal{Lip}(f_{i}^{\tau})\leq 2\textnormal{Lip}(f_{i})\leq 2,\quad|(f_{i})_{Q}-(f_{i})_{Q^{\prime}}|\leq r\textnormal{Lip}(f_{i})\leq r,\quad\sup_{i}|(f_{i})_{Q}|\leq 1.

Thus, the above estimate implies

\begin{split}\left|\int_{Q}\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}-\int_{Q^{\prime}}\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}\right|&\leq r^{d+1}\sqrt{d}S+r^{d}rS\\
&+r^{d-1}c_{d}\sqrt{S}\sqrt{\sum_{i}L_{i}^{d-2}\lVert\pi_{i}-\tilde{\pi}_{i}\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)}^{2}},\end{split}

which is equivalent to (
5.11
).
∎

We are now ready to outline in more detail how the estimate (
5.11
) will be used.
Consider we find a solution to the equation

\sum_{i}f_{i}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}=\rho\quad\text{a.e.~in $[0,1]^{d}$.}

where
\rho\in L^{\infty}
only attains values
1
and
2
.
Suppose further that there is plenty of very small pairs of adjacent cubes
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
, as in Lemma
5.6
such that
\rho=1
on
Q
and
\rho=2
on
Q^{\prime}
.
Then the left-hand side of (
5.11
) is equal to
r^{d}
, where
r
is the side length of the squares.
If
r>0
is small enough, then the term
r^{d+1}(\sqrt{d}+1)
is negligible compared to
r^{d}
.
Therefore, if we are able to find a particular pair of squares
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
where we also have the estimate

\sqrt{\sum_{i}L_{i}^{d-2}\lVert\pi_{i}-\tilde{\pi}_{i}\rVert_{\ell^{\infty}(\partial Q)}}\lesssim r\varepsilon,

then we obtain a contradiction.
Indeed, we would then have that the right hand side of (
5.11
) is estimated by
\approx r^{d+1}+r^{d}r\varepsilon\lesssim\varepsilon r^{d}
.
On the other hand, the left hand side itself is equal to
r^{d}
, which is indeed a contradiction.
This means that if such a pair of “good squares” exists, then for this particular choice of
\rho
, there is no solution to the PDE.
This is exactly what also happens in the arguments of Burago and Kleiner
[
9
]
and Dymond et al
[
19
]
.

5.2
Behaviour of biLipschitz maps along thin rectangles

In this subsection we shall collect a kind of dichotomy result on Lipschitz maps, which will later enable us to find the pairs of “good squares” which we mentioned earlier.
The first instance of this type of result is due to Burago and Kleiner

]

]
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let

,

.

n

(5.13)

that is not a flat chain.
Let
I\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be any isometry.
The current
I_{\#}C_{k}(T)
is not a flat chain by Lemma
2.3
.
By Lemma
2.6
,
I_{\#}C_{k}(T)
is a metric current (i.e. it lies in the range of
C_{k}
).
We produced a compactly supported metric
k
-current in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is not a flat chain, concluding the proof.
∎

We continue by disproving the conjecture for
1
-currents in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\geq 2
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Suppose
T
is a compactly supported metric
2
-current in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
which is not flat.
Then
\partial T
is not flat.

Proof.

Suppose
\partial T
is a flat chain.
Then by Theorem
6.1
we may write
\partial C_{2}T=\partial S
, where
S
is a flat
2
-chain of finite mass and with compact support.
Now
C_{2}T-S
is a compactly supported current without boundary, therefore there is a
(d+1)
-current
X
such that
\partial X=C_{2}T-S
.
The only
(d+1)
-current is
0
, so
C_{2}T=S
.
This contradicts the fact that
T
is non-flat and
S
is flat.
∎

Corollary 6.4
.

If
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\geq 2
, then there exists a metric
1
-current in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
without boundary, which is not a flat chain.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.3
, upon taking any non-flat metric
2
-current
T
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with compact support
\partial T
is a non-flat metric
1
-current. Moreover, such a non-flat metric
2
-current
T
exists by Corollary
6.2
.
If
d=2
, we are therefore finished.
If
d>2
, we may consider an arbitrary isometry
I\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the current
I_{\#}\partial T
will have the required properties.
∎

Now we observe what happens for
1
-currents in
\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 6.5
.

Every compactly supported metric
1
-current in
\mathbb{R}
is a flat chain.

Proof.

It suffices to show that every metric
1
-current
T
supported in
[0,1]
is of finite mass.
The statement then follows by elementary considerations, which the reader can carry out as an exercise.
Alternatively, one can obtain the statement immediately by applying either the results of
DePhilippis and Rindler
[
17
]
or Schioppa
[
36
]
.

Thus, we continue by showing finite mass.
Let us denote
\mathrm{d}x_{1}=\mathrm{d}t
in this case, as the underlying space is
\mathbb{R}
.
By the basic properties of the comparison map
[
27
, Theorem 5.5]
, it suffices to show that
C_{1}(T)
is of finite mass.
By the Riesz representation theorem, it suffices to show that there is some constant
C_{T}\in(0,\infty)
such that

|C_{1}T(\omega\mathrm{d}t)|\leq C_{T}\lVert\omega\rVert_{\infty}\quad\text{for every smooth $\omega\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$.}

We fix such an
\omega
and define

W(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\omega(s)\mathrm{d}s\quad\text{for $t\in\mathbb{R}$.}

Then
W
is a smooth map with
\textnormal{Lip}(W)=\lVert\omega\rVert_{\infty}
.
We observe that
W^{\prime}\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{d}W
and therefore

T(1,W)=C_{1}(T)(1\mathrm{d}W)=C_{1}(T)(\omega\mathrm{d}t).

But then by taking for
C_{T}
the norm of
T
in
\mathbb{P}_{1}
, we have

|C_{1}(T)(\omega\mathrm{d}t)|=|T(1,W)|\leq C_{T}\textnormal{Lip}(W)=C_{T}\lVert\omega\rVert_{\infty}.

∎

We have now proven all of the cases of Theorem
1.7
except for the case of
0
-currents.
This statement is essentially just Corollary
2.10
, but we give a detailed explanation.

Theorem 6.6
.

Every metric
0
-current in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with compact support is a flat chain.

Proof.

Let
K
be a compact set containing the support of a metric
0
-current
T
.
The flat norm of a
0
-form
\omega
(which is merely a real-valued function) in
K
is equal to

\lVert\omega\rVert_{\mathbb{F}^{0}(K)}=\max\{\lVert\omega\rVert_{\infty},\textnormal{Lip}(\omega)\},

where the supremum and the Lipschitz constant are taken on
K
.
By Corollary
2.10
, we find a sequence of measures
\mu_{i}
, such that

\sup_{\pi}|T(\pi)-\int\pi\;\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}|\to 0,

where the supremum is taken over Lipschitz functions
\pi
on
K
with

\max\{\lVert\pi\rVert_{\infty},\textnormal{Lip}(\pi)\}\leq 1.

If we let
N_{i}
be the
0
-current given by for

N_{i}(\omega)=\int\omega\;\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}\quad\text{for}\;\omega\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\textstyle\bigwedge^{0}\mathbb{R}^{d})=C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}),

then
N_{i}
is of finite mass and therefore normal.
It follows that

\sup_{\lVert\omega\rVert_{\mathbb{F}^{0}}\leq 1}|T(\omega)-N_{i}(\omega)|\to 0,

i.e.
N_{i}
converge to
T
in the flat norm.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

The case
k=0
is Theorem
6.6
, the case
k=1
and
d\geq 2
is a special case of Corollary
6.4
, the case
d\geq k\geq 2
is Corollary
6.2
.
Finally, the case
k=d=1
is Theorem
6.5
.
∎

7
The general case of the lack of regularity for the exterior derivative equation

The main result of this section is that the prescribed exterior derivative equation lacks Lipschitz regularity.
This appears to only be explicitly available in the literature in the case when the exterior derivative coincides with divergence
[
34
,
29
,
8
]
.
This is discussed in more depth in the introductory Section
1
, Subsection
1.2
.
Here we only reitarate, that the dualisation methods employed by Bourgain and Brezis
[
8
]
and McMullen
[
29
]
together with the non-inequality of Ornstein
[
30
]
could likely be adjusted to yield similar results for the general exterior derivative operator.
Given that this is not available in the literature and that these results can be immediately obtained from our main non-regularity result, we provide here the full statement with rigorous proof.

We denote by
L_{\textnormal{cl}}^{\infty}([0,1]^{d},\textstyle\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{R}_{d})
the subspace of distributionally closed
L^{\infty}
differential
k
-forms, i.e. those forms
\omega
for which
\mathrm{d}\omega=0
distributionally.
It is easily verified, that this subspace is weak
∗
closed in
L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d},\textstyle\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{R}_{d})
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Consider the equation for differential
k
-forms in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\mathrm{d}\omega=\eta.

(7.1)

If
k\in\{1,2,\dots,d-1\}
, then there exists
\eta\in L_{\textnormal{cl}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\textstyle\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{R}_{d})
, supported inside
Q=[0,1]^{d}
, such that no Lipschitz
\omega
solving (
7.1
) a.e. in
Q
exists.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\eta
is of the form

\eta=\eta_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{k+1}

(7.2)

and observe that since
k+1\leq d
, if
\eta_{1}
is not identically zero, then neither is
\eta
.

Let
\omega
be a Lipschitz map solving (
7.1
) a.e.
For every
I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
where
i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}
,
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, we find a Lipschitz real-valued map
\omega_{I}
such that

\omega=\sum_{I}\omega_{I}\mathrm{d}x^{I}=\sum_{I}\omega_{I}\mathrm{d}x_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{i_{k}}.

For any
j=\{1,\dots,k+1\}
, let

I^{j}=(1,\dots,j-1,j+1,\dots,k+1).

Then, by considering the projection of
\mathrm{d}\omega
to the basis vector
\mathrm{d}x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{k+1}
,
the equality (
7.1
) in particular implies

\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{j-1}\frac{\partial\omega_{I^{j}}}{\partial e_{j}}=\eta_{1}.

(7.3)

Finally, since the equality holds almost everywhere, by Fubini’s theorem, it also holds
\mathcal{L}^{k+1}
-a.e. on at least one (in fact almost every) section of the cube
[0,1]^{d}
by the plane spanned by
e_{1},\dots,e_{k+1}
.
We may assume, without loss of generality, that it holds on
[0,1]^{k+1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k+1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let

\pi_{j}^{j}=(-1)^{j-1}{\omega_{I^{j}}}_{|[0,1]^{k+1}}\quad\text{for $j\in\{1,\dots,k+1\}$}

and

\pi_{i}^{j}=x_{j}\quad\text{whenever $i\not=j$, $i,j\in\{1,\dots,k+1\}$.}

It is now immediately verified that the definition of
\pi_{i}^{j}
together with (
7.3
) imply

\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\det\mathrm{D}\pi_{i}=\eta_{1}\quad\text{a.e.~in $[0,1]^{k+1}$.}

(7.4)

Since
k+1\geq 2
, using Corollary
5.25
, we may find
\eta_{1}\in L^{\infty}([0,1]^{k+1})
with
\lVert\eta_{1}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
such that no sequence of Lipschitz maps
\pi_{i}
satisfies (
7.4
).
We extend
\eta_{1}
to
[0,1]^{d}
via

\eta_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\eta_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1}).

Finally, we let
\eta
by given by (
7.2
).
By the argument above, the proof is finished once we show that
\eta
is distributionally closed.

The argument for this is completely standard, but for the sake of completeness we provide it.
Firstly, via a mollification argument, we may find a sequence of smooth functions satisfying
\eta_{1}^{i}\to\eta_{1}
weak
∗
in
L^{\infty}([0,1]^{k+1})
.
Once again, we extend

\eta_{1}^{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\eta^{i}_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1}).

Finally, we let

\eta^{i}=\eta_{1}^{i}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{k+1}.

We observe that the weak
∗
convergence of
\eta_{1}^{i}
implies also the weak
∗
convergence
\eta^{i}\to\eta
inside

L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d},\textstyle\bigwedge^{k+1}\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Since the space
L^{\infty}_{\operatorname{cl}}([0,1]^{d},\textstyle\bigwedge^{k+1}\mathbb{R}^{d})

is weak
∗
closed, it suffices to show that each
\eta^{i}
is closed.
This follows from the definition of the exterior derivative and the fact that

\frac{\partial}{\partial e_{j}}\eta^{i}=0

for all
j>k+1
, which is a consequence of the definition.
∎
