Progress in reaching zero-dose children: analysis of population-modifiable risk factors and inequity trends in low- and lower-middle-income countries
Authors: Firew Tekle Bobo, Abdi Gari Negasa, Hawi Tekle Bobo, Kiddus Yitbarek, Girmaye Dinsa, Mirkuzie Woldie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundZero-dose (ZD) children remain concentrated in low- and lower-middle-income countries (LLMICs) and are a major focus of the Immunization Agenda 2030 (IA2030), which aims to halve their number by 2030. Despite this commitment, progress has stalled in many high-burden settings. This study aimed to examine progress in reaching ZD children, trends in wealth-related inequity, and key population-modifiable risk factors across LLMICs over the past decade.MethodsWe analysed two rounds of the most recent Demographic and health survey (DHS) datasets from ten LLMICs to assess progress in reducing the number of ZD children. Countries were included based on the availability of post-2020 survey rounds. ZD children were defined as children aged 12–23 months who had not received any dose of BCG, DTP-containing, polio, or measles-containing vaccines. The concentration index was used to assess wealth-related inequities. Multilevel logistic regression models were used to identify modifiable risk factors associated with ZD status. The population attributable fraction (PAF) was calculated using odds ratios and prevalence estimates to quantify the contribution of modifiable risk factors.ResultsZD prevalence increased significantly in five of ten countries – Mozambique (4.8% to 14.2%), Côte d’Ivoire (4.9% to 8.7%), Nepal (0.8% to 4.5%), Tanzania (2.3% to 4.4%), and Madagascar (13.3% to 18.1%) — while no statistically significant change was observed in the remaining five. Wealth-related inequities were significant in most countries, with ZD children disproportionately concentrated in the poorest households. Home delivery had the highest PAF for ZD (17.1%; 95% CI: 14.1–19.6), followed by fewer than four ANC contacts (8.6%; 95% CI: 7.2–10.0) and non-use of contraceptives (6.9%; 95% CI: 5.3–8.5). The combined contribution of reproductive and maternal health services was (PAF = 29.4%; 95% CI: 24.5–33.7). Socioeconomic factors included lack of maternal education (4.4%; 95% CI: 2.6–6.1) and not listening to the radio (2.7%; 95% CI: 0.4–4.6).ConclusionProgress in reaching ZD children in low- and lower-middle-income countries has declined. A renewed commitment and continued effort are needed to reach ZD children in these regions by improving access to health services and addressing the socioeconomic challenges of families.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-026-02955-3.Keywords: Zero-dose children, Vaccination inequity, Immunization programs, Health inequities, Socioeconomic factors, Maternal health services, Developing countries

Introduction

Vaccines play a vital role in preventing and controlling communicable diseases, strengthening global health security [1]. In 2024, about 85% of infants worldwide (109 million) received all three doses of the diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis (DTP3) vaccine [2]. However, vaccination coverage is uneven both between and within countries [3], with 14.2 million children, mainly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), remaining ZD [4]. For operational purposes, IA2030 defines ZD children as those who did not receive the first dose of DTP containing vaccine (DTP1) by their first birthday. This study adopts a broader definition — children aged 12–23 months who had not received any dose of BCG, DTP-containing, polio, or measles-containing vaccines — which provides a more conservative and comprehensive estimate of vaccination program reach [5].

ZD children receive no protection against vaccine-preventable diseases, making them highly susceptible to infections. This vulnerability leads to higher morbidity and mortality and increases the potential for outbreaks, since large numbers of unvaccinated individuals facilitate disease transmission [5]. ZD children also tend to live in communities facing multiple deprivations, including poverty, lack of maternal education, poor access to clean water and sanitation, and limited primary health care services— compounding their vulnerability [6]. High ZD rates therefore reflect not only individual risk but broader weaknesses and inequities in the health systems [7].

Building on the Global Vaccine Action Plan (2011–2020) [8], which contributed to sustained increases in global vaccination coverage, IA2030 seeks to maximise the lifesaving benefits of vaccines over the decade 2021–2030, with a renewed emphasis on equity and reaching those left behind, including ZD children [9]. IA2030 set targets to reduce the number of ZD children by 25% by 2025 and by 50% by 2030 [9]. The 2025 target has not been met, and the world is not on track to meet the 2030 target either [10]. Despite sustained global efforts, progress in reaching ZD children in LMICs has stalled. The ZD burden remains highly concentrated — 87% of the world’s ZD children live in LMICs, with just six high-burden countries accounting for the majority of the global total [11]. Recent data indicate that approximately 10.4% of children in low-income countries and 7% in lower-middle-income countries are ZD [12]. While the COVID-19 pandemic was undoubtedly a setback early in the decade, it is increasingly recognised that the pandemic alone does not account for the limited progress made to date — deep-seated systemic issues, including socioeconomic inequities and health system weaknesses, continue to compound this problem [13].

Risk factors for ZD status are multifaceted, operating at individual and contextual levels. Poverty, lack of access to maternal health care, low maternal education, limited health care access, being unmarried, young maternal age, and residing in underserved or remote areas have all been identified as determinants [14–18].

Despite this growing evidence base, understanding of the progress made in reaching children in high-burden countries remains limited. In particular, the relative contributions of key population modifiable risk factors to ZD status have yet to be established across multiple countries. While a growing number of multi-country studies have characterised the prevalence and distribution of ZD children across LMICs, few have examined trends over time, and none have combined trend analysis with equity assessment and population-modifiable risk factor quantification across LLMICs [19]. Existing research is largely country-specific, limiting generalisability across LMICs [15, 16, 20–22]. This gap is especially urgent given the IA2030 target of halving the number of ZD children by 2030. Focusing on LLMICs — where the burden of ZD children is disproportionately concentrated and health system capacity most constrained — we aimed to study the progress in reaching ZD children, analyse trends in wealth-related inequity, and identify key population-modifiable risk factors across LLMICs over the past decade.

Methods

Study design and participants

We analysed two rounds of DHS data for each of the 10 countries included. Countries were selected based on two criteria: (1) availability of a most recent survey conducted after 2020 with datasets publicly accessible at the time of analysis, and (2) availability of at least one earlier DHS round to allow trend analysis. Although countries such as Angola and the Democratic Republic of Congo had conducted post-2020 DHS surveys, their datasets were not yet publicly released at the time of data extraction and were therefore excluded. The 2020 cutoff was used to reflect the current status of these countries and assess changes over the past decade.

The selected countries were Burkina Faso (2010, 2021), Cambodia (2014, 2022), Côte d’Ivoire (2011, 2021), Ghana (2014, 2022), Kenya (2014, 2022), Madagascar (2008, 2021), Mozambique (2011, 2023), Nepal (2016, 2022), the Philippines (2016, 2022), and Tanzania (2015, 2022). In Burkina Faso, the 2021 DHS did not reach all pre-selected enumeration areas due to insecurity in certain regions, potentially underrepresenting the most vulnerable populations. This limitation should be considered when interpreting findings for this country.

The DHS uses uniform survey design, standardized questionnaires, and consistent sampling methods across time periods and countries [23]. Children aged 12–23 months were identified through a multistage stratified cluster sampling approach, in which enumeration areas were first selected, followed by systematic household sampling within each cluster, and finally individual eligibility screening. This yielded a final sample of 39,972 children – 19,632 mother/caretaker–child pairs from the most recent surveys and 20,340 from earlier surveys.

Measures

The primary outcome was vaccination status, operationalized as a binary variable (ZD vs. vaccinated). Children aged 12–23 months who had not received any doses of BCG, DTP-containing, polio, or measles-containing vaccines were classified as zero-dose, while those who had received one or more doses of any of these vaccines were classified as vaccinated [6]. This definition provides a conservative estimate of ZD prevalence and supports a broader assessment of the vaccination program reach.

Vaccination status was determined primarily from vaccination cards. Where cards were not accessible, caregiver recall was used, accounting for 7.1% of the data.

Modifiable risk factors

The exposures analysed were modifiable risk factors identified through a review of prior evidence on determinants of ZD status in LMICs [6, 17, 24]. Factors were included if they met three criteria: (1) consistent association with ZD or under-vaccination status in the literature, (2) availability and comparability across all DHS rounds included, and (3) theoretical modifiability through health policy or programmatic intervention. These factors were grouped into two main categories: socioeconomic determinants and health service utilisation factors.

Socioeconomic determinants included the maternal or caregiver educational level, household wealth, and access to mass media. Maternal education was classified into three levels: no education, primary, secondary, and higher education. Household wealth was assessed using a wealth index provided in the DHS datasets, which is derived through principal components analysis of household asset ownership to reflect a household’s economic status. This index is constructed consistently across all DHS surveys, considering a range of assets, including durable goods such as televisions and cars, as well as housing characteristics like flooring material, water sources, and sanitation facilities.

Households were ranked by their wealth index and divided into quintiles, each representing 20% of the population. The first quintile corresponds to the poorest 20%, while the fifth quintile represents the wealthiest 20%. Access to mass media was categorized based on the frequency of newspaper reading and radio listening, classified as not at all, less than once a week or more.

The health service factors analysed include reproductive and maternal health services: contraceptive use, antenatal care (ANC) visits, and place of delivery. Contraceptive use was categorized as either never used or previously/currently used. ANC visits were classified into two groups: fewer than four visits and four or more visits. The place of delivery was grouped into home-based deliveries and facility-based deliveries.

Statistical analysis

We began by estimating the prevalence of ZD children in each country. To visually examine changes over time, we plotted the distribution of ZD children across wealth quintiles and maternal education levels for both survey rounds, highlighting any narrowing or widening of prevalence over these periods.

Within-country wealth related inequality was assessed using the Concentration Index (CCI). The CCI measures inequality by comparing the distribution of ZD children to wealth status. We used the user-written Stata command conindex [25], with ZD status as the health variable of interest and the DHS wealth index as the ranking variable. The CCI is calculated as twice the area between the concentration curve and the line of equality. The index ranges from − 1 to + 1, with a value of 0 indicating an equal distribution of ZD children across wealth groups. A positive CCI suggests a higher concentration of ZD children among wealthier groups, while a negative CCI indicates a disproportionate concentration of ZD children among poorer groups.

To examine whether countries with a higher burden of ZD children also tend to exhibit greater wealth-related inequality in ZD distribution, we assessed the country-level association between ZD prevalence and the CCI using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient [26]. This was calculated using each country’s ZD prevalence and corresponding CCI value as paired observations (n = 10 per survey period) and was computed separately for earlier and more recent survey rounds to examine whether this relationship has changed over time.

The analysis of modifiable risk factors associated with ZD children was conducted in stages. First, we examined the population-level modifiable determinants of ZD children using a Generalized Linear Latent Mixed Model (GLLAMM). This method accounts for the hierarchical structure of the data and adjusts for sampling weights. The model provided estimates of odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the modifiable risk factors. To ensure the reliability of our results, we assessed collinearity among the modifiable risk factors using the variance inflation factor, which showed no evidence of collinearity. To evaluate changes in modifiable risk factors over time, we used two pooled datasets. The first dataset included data from the most recent survey rounds conducted between 2020 and 2023, while the second dataset consisted of data from earlier survey rounds conducted between 2008 and 2017.

The PAF was estimated using Miettinen’s formula to quantify the proportion of ZD children that could potentially be prevented if modifiable risk factors were addressed within the population. The PAF represents a hypothetical scenario, illustrating the potential impact of reducing the prevalence of these risk factors on improving vaccination coverage. We acknowledge that PAF traditionally assumes a causal relationship between exposure and outcome. However, PAF has been widely adopted in observational epidemiological research as a scenario-based metric to inform health policy prioritisation, even in the absence of experimental evidence [27, 28].

In this study, PAF estimates represent the hypothetical proportion of ZD cases potentially avertable if the prevalence of each modifiable risk factor were eliminated, under the assumption that the observed associations reflect modifiable pathways. PAF was calculated separately for earlier (2008–2017) and recent (2020–2023) survey rounds to enable comparison of the relative contribution of modifiable risk factors across time periods. This approach does not assume knowledge of specific interventions implemented during either period but rather provides a descriptive baseline against which changes in population-attributable burden can be assessed. Comparing PAF estimates across periods illustrates whether the relative importance of specific modifiable risk factors has increased or decreased over time, offering insight into shifting programmatic priorities.

To calculate the PAF, data on the prevalence of each modifiable risk factor and the corresponding odds ratios associated with ZD status were used. The formula integrates these values to estimate the fraction of ZD cases that could be potentially averted if the identified risk factors were minimized or eliminated.

Results

Over the past decade, the prevalence of ZD children increased significantly in five of the ten countries studied: Mozambique (4.8% to 14.2%; p < 0.001), Nepal (0.8% to 4.5%; p < 0.001), Côte d’Ivoire (4.9% to 8.7%; p = 0.007), Tanzania (2.3% to 4.4%; p = 0.008), and Madagascar (13.3% to 18.1%; p = 0.006). No statistically significant change was observed in Ghana (1.6% to 2.0%; p = 0.525), Philippines (9.5% to 11.2%; p = 0.246), Cambodia (2.4% to 3.3%; p = 0.251), Kenya (1.7% to 2.1%; p = 0.314), or Burkina Faso (1.8% to 1.7%; p = 0.848) (Fig. 1).

In all countries, the prevalence of ZD children was highest among those whose mothers had no education and lowest compared to among those whose mothers had secondary or higher education. This pattern remained consistent across the two survey rounds.

Several countries did not make progress in reducing the prevalence of ZD children among mothers with no education. Madagascar and Mozambique reported the highest increases, with rises of 5% and 15%, respectively. In contrast, the Philippines achieved a significant decline, with the prevalence decreasing by more than 20%.

The prevalence of ZD children increased across all wealth quintiles in the majority of the countries studied (Fig. 3). The poorest groups experienced a higher prevalence of ZD children in recent survey rounds compared to previous rounds, with the most significant increases observed in Madagascar, Mozambique, and the Philippines.

Table 1 presents the CCI for each country across both survey rounds. Wealth-related inequality widened most markedly in Mozambique (ΔCCI = − 0.154), the Philippines (ΔCCI = − 0.044), and Nepal (ΔCCI = − 0.033), indicating increasing concentration of ZD children among poorer households over time. In contrast, inequality narrowed in Burkina Faso (ΔCCI = + 0.026), Côte d’Ivoire (+ 0.050), and Tanzania (+ 0.024). Madagascar maintained persistently high inequality across both rounds (CCI: −0.208 to − 0.200), despite a marginal narrowing. The Spearman rank correlation between ZD prevalence and CCI, calculated at the country level (n = 10 per period), showed a strong significant association in earlier surveys (r = − 0.98, p < 0.001), which weakened and became non-significant in recent surveys (r = − 0.57, p = 0.08), suggesting a shift in the relationship between overall ZD burden and wealth-related inequality over time.

Trends in zero-dose inequity

Our analysis revealed significant socioeconomic inequalities in the distribution of ZD children in the recent survey rounds, with the burden disproportionately concentrated among disadvantaged groups in Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, Cambodia, Ghana, the Philippines, Mozambique, Madagascar, and Tanzania. However, no significant differences were observed in Nepal, Côte d’Ivoire, and Kenya in surveys conducted before 2020.

After 2020, these inequalities persisted in Madagascar, Mozambique, and the Philippines, where ZD children remained more prevalent among poorer households. Countries with a higher prevalence of ZD children also showed the greatest socioeconomic inequalities in ZD distribution. The highest inequalities were recorded in Madagascar (CCI: -0.20), Mozambique (CCI: -0.218), and the Philippines (CCI: -0.121) in surveys conducted after 2020 (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Using two waves of nationally representative surveys, this study assessed progress in reaching ZD children, trends in wealth-related inequity, and population-modifiable risk factors across LLMICs. ZD prevalence increased in the majority of study countries, representing an apparent reversal of earlier positive trends and a significant setback to the IA2030 target of halving ZD children by 2030. While the COVID-19 pandemic clearly set back routine immunization services and access, it does not fully account for the limited progress made to date; [13] deep-rooted socioeconomic inequities and health system weaknesses remain the more persistent drivers [29, 30].

Progress varied substantially across countries. Significant increases in ZD prevalence were observed in Mozambique, Madagascar, Côte d’Ivoire, Tanzania, and Nepal, each reflecting distinct country-level factors. In Mozambique, compounded disruptions – the COVID-19 pandemic, Cyclone Freddy, and concurrent outbreaks of polio, measles, and cholera — substantially set back routine immunisation between 2019 and 2023 [31]. Madagascar’s, persistently low coverage reflects geographic remoteness, weak outreach capacity, and recurring vaccine-derived poliovirus outbreaks [32].

In Côte d’Ivoire, the increase may partly reflect the financial and programmatic pressures accompanying the country’s entry into the accelerated Gavi transition phase in 2020 [33]. Ghana and Kenya, also approaching Gavi transition, showed relatively stable trends, likely reflecting stronger health system resilience and community outreach; Ghana through targeted catch-up campaigns [34] and Kenya’s through its established Community Health Volunteer programme [35]. Cambodia likewise maintained stable prevalence, consistent with its comparatively strong immunisation recovery efforts following the pandemic. Trends in of Burkina Faso should be interpreted with caution, as insecurity prevented the 2021 DHS from accessing some enumeration areas, potentially underrepresenting the most vulnerable populations.

ZD prevalence was highest among children of mothers with no formal education— a finding consistent with a substantial body of evidence demonstrating a strong inverse relationship between maternal educational attainment and the likelihood that a child remains ZD [7, 15, 22, 36]. Maternal education shapes health-seeking behaviours, vaccine acceptance, and healthcare access regardless of country context [37]. With the exception of the Philippines, the gap in ZD prevalence between mothers with no education and those with secondary or higher education did not narrow in the recent survey. Immunization programmes must therefore extend beyond supply-side improvements to incorporate demand-generation strategies that reach mothers with low or no formal education — including health worker counselling at antenatal and postnatal visits, community health worker outreach, and culturally appropriate messaging through local media and community leaders — all of which have been shown to improve health-seeking behaviour and vaccination uptake [38].

The association between poverty and ZD status is well established across LMICs [6, 12], sub-Saharan Africa [17], India [22], Ethiopia [21], and Somalia [15] and our findings reinforce this across survey rounds. Notably, ZD prevalence in the poorest quintile increased in recent survey, suggesting that the setback from the IA2030 target is most pronounced among the most disadvantaged households. Significant socioeconomic inequalities in ZD distribution were observed in the majority of study countries, with the burden concentrated among disadvantaged groups in Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, Cambodia, Ghana, the Philippines, Mozambique, Madagascar, and Tanzania. This pattern is consistent with evidence from sub-Saharan Africa and other LMICs showing that poorer households and regions bear a disproportionate share of ZD burden [39, 40], and with UNICEF indicating that wider socioeconomic gaps tend to accompany higher ZD prevalence, regardless of country income level [41].

After 2020, wealth-related inequality persisted in Madagascar, Mozambique, and the Philippines, where ZD children remain more prevalent among poorer households. The CCI values reported in these countries reflect substantial wealth-related disparities, consistent with previous reports showing large slope indices of inequality in countries where ZD prevalence is high among poorest groups [5, 14, 41].

Socioeconomic status remains a primary driver of vaccination gaps even where overall coverage improved — a pattern that cuts across country income levels and regions. The country-level correlation between ZD prevalence and wealth-related inequality differed markedly between survey periods. Before 2020, the correlation was strong and significant (r = − 0.98, p < 0.001), indicating that countries with a higher ZD burden consistently showed greater concentration of ZD children among the poorest households — a pattern consistent with systematic reviews evidence identifying wealth disparity as the dominant driver of immunisation inequality, compounding other sources of disadvantage including geography, education, and gender [42]. In more recent surveys, this correlation weakened and was no longer statistically significant (r = − 0.57, p = 0.08), possibly reflecting the influence of targeted immunization campaigns or equity-focused interventions implemented during and after 2020 [4, 43]. However, country-specific CCI trends – including widening inequality in Mozambique and the Philippines – caution against interpreting this shift as evidence of broad equity gains. Continued monitoring of both prevalence and inequality metrics remains essential.

Before 2020, the absence of significant wealth-related inequality in Nepal, Côte d’Ivoire, and Kenya is consistent with evidence that stronger health systems and outreach services can support more equitable vaccination coverage [5, 43]. This heterogeneity across countries points to the role of governance, healthcare infrastructure, conflict, and broader social determinants in shaping inequality patterns – and underscores the importance of country-specific approaches rather than uniform programmatic responses.

Home delivery was the largest PAF contributor to ZD status in both survey periods, underscoring the strong association between births outside health facilities and reduced early childhood vaccination opportunities. Children born at home miss birth doses and their mothers lack access to the counselling and vaccination information provided at health facilities, with downstream consequences for subsequent immunisation [44]. The PAF for home delivery more than doubled between survey periods, indicating a deepening and persistent association between place of delivery and ZD status over time — whether driven by rising home delivery rates, a stronger relationship with ZD risk, or both. Strengthening facility-based delivery and extending outreach to those who deliver at home are therefore among the most consequential levers available to immunisation programmes. Facility-based delivery is consistently identified as among the strongest predictors of timely vaccination initiation and follow-up, given the immediate vaccine access and health service engagement it provides [45–47].

Fewer than four ANC contacts and non-use of contraceptives were also substantial PAF contributors, reinforcing the broader pattern that disengagement from maternal health services – across contraception, ANC, and delivery care – compounds the risk of reduce ZD status at each stage of the continuum [14]. The combined PAF for reproductive and maternal health service factors more than doubled between survey periods, a shift that points not to a new phenomenon but to a deepening and increasingly dominant role of services utilisation gaps in driving ZD status.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine trends in ZD prevalence, wealth-related inequity, and population-modifiable risk factors across LLMICs, using nationally representative DHS data from a large, pooled sample. Several limitations warrant consideration. The analysis draws on data from only 10 LLMICs, selected based on the availability of comparable survey rounds, excluding high-burden countries such as Nigeria, Ethiopia, and India where recent DHS data were unavailable. Where vaccination cards were absent, caregiver recall was used (7.1% of the sample), introducing potential recall bias. DHS data limitations prevented assessment of several factors likely relevant to ZD status, including supply-side constraints (vaccine stockouts, cold chain failures, health worker availability), demand-side factors (vaccine hesitancy, parental trust, distance to facility), and contextual determinants (armed conflict, population displacement, community norms around vaccination). National-level estimates may also obscure important subnational heterogeneity — observed increases in prevalence may reflect localised pockets of disadvantage rather than uniform trends across populations, and future analyses incorporating disaggregated subnational data would better capture these patterns. Finally, in high-fertility settings, changes in ZD prevalence may not fully reflect changes in the absolute number of ZD children, and variations in survey years and the timing of national interventions may affect cross-country comparability.

Conclusion

Progress toward reaching ZD children has stalled— driven not by a single disruption but by deep-seated gaps in maternal health service utilisation and persistent socioeconomic disadvantage. The burden falls most heavily on children of mothers with no education and those in the poorest households, and wealth-related inequality has widened in several countries despite aggregate improvements. Home delivery, insufficient ANC contacts, and non-use of contraceptives emerged as the largest modifiable contributors to ZD status, with their combined influence growing substantially between survey periods — pointing to maternal and reproductive health services as the most consequential and actionable entry point for reducing ZD prevalence. Meeting the IA2030 target will require more than incremental supply-side investments; it demands integrated service delivery, sustained outreach to the poorest households, and purposeful strategies to reach mothers with little or no formal education.
