In vivo circRNA-engineered macrophages mediate localized MMP9 neutralization to rejuvenate aged bone
Authors: Daoyu Zhu, Shifeng Ling, Haoyu Fang, Qianhao Yang, Yawei Du, Wenguo Cui, Yixuan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAging causes osteoporosis, delayed fracture repair, and osteoarthritis, but mechanisms and therapies remain unclear. Transcriptomics of serum and bone from older patients showed marked MMP9 upregulation, highlighting systemic MMP9 neutralization as a potential immunotherapy. Here, we developed an in vivo circRNA-based antibody-engineering strategy that specifically targets macrophages, converting them into biofactories for anti-MMP9 antibodies. Central to this therapy is an apoptosis-mimicking lipid nanoparticle incorporating phosphatidylserine with an optimized formulation (aMMP9-LNP), which enhances macrophage-specific recognition and endocytosis. Leveraging inflammation-guided chemotaxis, this approach enables systemic, targeted MMP9 neutralization. In aged mice, systemic aMMP9-LNP reduced stem cell senescence, boosted osteogenesis, accelerated fracture repair, and mitigated cartilage degeneration. Mechanistically, MMP9 blockade dampened senescence-associated secretory phenotype, restored osteoblast-osteoclast balance, and lowered p21/MMP3. Biodistribution confirmed bone-targeted delivery with preserved tissue homeostasis, supporting translational potential. Collectively, our results identify MMP9 as a key effector and advance an in vivo mRNA immunotherapy for skeletal aging.Keywords: circRNA, MMP-9, Macrophages, Antibody biosynthesis, Degenerative bone disease

Introduction

As the global population continues to age, the clinical burden of skeletal-related conditions is expected to rise significantly [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Age-related bone disorders (e.g., osteoporosis, impaired fracture healing, and osteoarthritis) rank among the most common and debilitating complications in elderly populations. However, current treatment strategies are predominantly palliative, focusing on symptom management rather than addressing the underlying causes or halting disease progression. Growing evidence suggests that these disorders share a common pathophysiological foundation, driven by chronic low-grade inflammation, cellular senescence, and dysregulated tissue remodeling [5,6]. Despite this, the molecular mediators that orchestrate these degenerative changes within the aging skeletal microenvironment remain poorly understood, which remains a critical impediment to creating effective targeted, disease-modifying therapies.

Through transcriptomic analysis of serum and bone samples from elderly human individuals, we identified matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9) as a potential central and consistently upregulated effector in age-related bone dysfunction. MMP9 is well-known for its role in extracellular matrix degradation [7,8], but its persistent elevation in aged individuals and osteoporotic bone suggests a broader pathological role in skeletal aging [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Despite this, its mechanistic contribution to age-related bone loss and its potential as a therapeutic target remain unexplored. This result suggests that the effective clearance of MMP9 in the blood and bone microenvironment as a therapeutic target could be a highly efficient strategy for treating degenerative bone diseases. Clearly, the introduction of neutralizing antibodies is the most direct approach. However, neutralizing antibodies have limitations in terms of safety and cost-effectiveness and lack effective bone-targeting capabilities. In recent years, mRNA-based protein replacement strategies have brought revolutionary breakthroughs to the field [13]. While mRNA-based therapeutics have revolutionized vaccinology and oncology [14,15], their clinical application in age-related degenerative diseases, particularly within orthopedic settings remains elusive.

Messenger RNA (mRNA)-based therapies have garnered increasing attention for their versatility, transient expression, and non-integrating nature [14,16,17]. Recent advances in lipid nanoparticle (LNP) technologies have enabled the efficient systemic delivery of mRNA to target tissues [13,18], with remarkable success in vaccines and cancer immunotherapy [19,20]. However, their translation to age-related degenerative diseases remains limited by several challenges: short-lived expression, limited tissue specificity, and the difficulty of sustaining therapeutic effects in chronic conditions [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. Moreover, most existing mRNA platforms focus on encoding intracellular or membrane-bound proteins, rather than secreted therapeutic agents such as antibodies that could remodel pathological microenvironments. In addition, LNPs tend to accumulate in the liver, creating barriers to selective organ targeting (SORT). This phenomenon is also very prominent in traditional nanoparticles, especially after the introduction of PEG components [25].

In the context of skeletal aging, monocyte/macrophage represent a promising target [[26], [27], [28]]. As resident immune cells in bone tissue, monocytes/macrophages mediate the crosstalk between inflammation, osteogenesis, and senescence. And macrophages are somehow an active “transporter” between serum and bone tissue. Therefore, reprogramming monocytes/macrophages to transiently secrete therapeutic antibodies might offer a powerful strategy to modulate the local niche without inducing systemic immunosuppression [29]. Interestingly, traditional nanoparticles are modified with PEG components to achieve long circulation, precisely to avoid capture by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), including monocytes/macrophages [30]. Therefore, targeting monocytes/macrophages with nanoparticles in the blood system has an inherent advantage. By employing further biomimetic strategies, the recognition of nanoparticles by monocytes/macrophages can be enhanced, such as through the “eat me” effect in phosphatidylserine (PtdSer)-mimicking efferocytosis [31]. Finally, by enabling rapid mRNA-LNP capture, macrophage delivery and gene-engineering can be achieved, which also helps to avoid the accumulation of LNPs in the liver.

To achieve this, we developed PtdSer-modified lipid nanoparticles (aMMP9-LNPs) for macrophage-specific delivery of circular mRNA (circRNA) encoding an anti-MMP9 antibody, which is designed to engineer monocytes/macrophages for the secretion of the MMP9 antibody and to modulate the inflammatory bone niche through the bone marrow tropism of monocytes/macrophages. By exposing PtdSer on their surface, LNPs mimic the membrane features of apoptotic cells, thereby inducing efferocytosis by monocytes/macrophages via PtdSer-mediated virus entry enhancing receptors (PVEERs) [32,33]. This strategy enables the selective uptake of LNPs by monocytes/macrophages, facilitating targeted mRNA transfection for in situ anti-MMP9 antibody production. This therapy was evaluated in multiple in vivo and in vitro experiments, which reverses key hallmarks of skeletal aging in preclinical models. Collectively, our findings establish a mechanistically informed, immunologically guided, and clinically scalable approach to mitigate age-related skeletal degeneration. By targeting a core effector MMP9 through transient, macrophage-directed antibody expression, this platform offers a flexible and translational solution for a range of degenerative disorders of skeletal aging.

The GSEA analysis showed that the WNT pathway, stem cell differentiation, mineralization, osteoblast differentiation was involved (Fig. 1c–d). We therefore suggested that the aging of bone tissue was associated with those pathways. It is acknowledged that SASP factors plays critical role in tissue aging, the aging of bone tissue is somehow related with a key factor remains unclear.

We next collected serum samples from both young patients (<40Y) and elder patients (>65Y). RNA-seq analysis was performed in those samples. Fig. 1f showed the heatmap of differential expression genes from serum samples. 207 genes were significantly up-regulated and 67 genes were significantly down-regulated in elder patients. Among these, the expression level of MMP9 was much higher in elders than in younger patients. The STRING (cn.string-db.org) correlation showed the center role of MMP9 in differential genes (The top 200, P < 0.05) (Fig. 1g). Fig. 1h showed the GO looCircos analysis. Fig. 1i showed the showed the KEGG enrichment barplot of the serum samples. MMP9 is acknowledged as an important SASP biomarker [9,10,29,34], and has also been reported to be highly related to biological functions of bone tissue including WNT pathway, stem cell differentiation, mineralization, osteoblast differentiation [9,29,35,36].

Taken together, we supposed that MMP9 might be a therapeutic target and critical marker of bone aging, and a potential actionable SASP target. For further verification, we measured the MMP9 in serum of human individuals at RNA and protein level, which showed the similar trends (Fig. 1j–k). And we also measured the MMP9 in osteoporotic and non-osteoporotic human bone tissue, which is also higher in elders (Fig. 1l–m). Therefore, the high expression of MMP9 is related to bone senescence, and the clearance of MMP9 in bone tissue might rejuvenate aged bone and promote regeneration.

To further investigate the downstream mechanisms associated with MMP9, transcriptomic analysis was performed comparing NC and MMP9-treated groups. As shown in Fig. S3a, hierarchical clustering revealed distinct gene expression patterns between the two groups. Consistently, volcano plot analysis identified a set of significantly upregulated and downregulated genes upon MMP9 treatment (Fig. S3b). Gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses demonstrated that differentially expressed genes were primarily involved in extracellular matrix organization, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, calcium signaling, and Wnt and Ras signaling pathways (Fig. S3c–e). These pathways are closely associated with bone remodeling, cellular migration, and mechanotransduction processes. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis further revealed interconnected regulatory modules centered on growth factor signaling and tissue remodeling (Fig. S3f), suggesting that MMP9 may function as a key upstream modulator affecting multiple biological processes. Collectively, these results indicate that MMP9 regulates a set of conserved pathways relevant to multiple skeletal disorders, supporting its role as a common therapeutic target rather than a disease-specific factor.

Genetically engineered macrophage as a bio-factory for anti-MMP9 secretion and delivery

Macrophage is an active transporter between serum and bone tissue [27]. We suggest that the gene-engineered monocytes/macrophages might act as a bio-factory for clearance of MMP9 in bone tissue. To achieve efficient uptake by monocytes/macrophages, we increased the proportion of PtdSer phospholipids in the formulation to enhance macrophage recognition, while simultaneously reducing the PEG component to minimize the hydrophilic layer shielding effect on monocytes/macrophages.

Briefly, using a microfluidic-mixing strategy, PtdSer was formulated into LNPs to form LNP-circMmp9 with other lipid components (Fig. 2a). Here, we developed three different LNP formulations with different DSPE-PEG2000 and PtdSer components, and utilized enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) mRNA for a for a better visualized evaluation of transfection efficiency. The results showed that the LNP formulation containing 0.5% DSPE-PEG2000 and 1% PtdSer achieved the highest transfection efficiency (Fig. 2b). The agarose gel electrophoresis image of the in vitro constructed circMMP9 RNA (Fig. 2c). We performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 2d) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) on the aMMP9-LNP. The results indicated that the average size of aMMP9-LNP was 104.97 ± 0.25 nm, and the PDI was 0.144 ± 0.016 (Fig. 2e).

The PtdSer phospholipid component, similar to conventional phospholipids (e.g., DSPC), is exposed on the outer layer of LNPs [37]. PtdSer induces recognition by various macrophage PtdSer-mediated virus entry enhancing receptors (PVEERs), such as TIM-4 and MerTK. monocytes/macrophages utilize similar mechanisms to perform efferocytosis and virus uptake, enabling the specific phagocytosis of apoptotic cells. Therefore, this PtdSer-induced specific recognition by macrophages also served as a natural targeting strategy. In addition, the PEG layer on the outer surface of conventional LNPs is used to stabilize the suspension and to prevent rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) [38]. In this study, specific targeting of macrophages by LNPs clearly does not require the shielding effect of PEG against the MPS. However, to avoid compromising the stability of LNPs by completely removing PEG, we achieved this by reducing the PEG content. Fortunately, the introduction of PtdSer, with its negative charge, helps maintain the stability of these PEG-reduced LNPs. Therefore, the specific PtdSer-modified LNPs represent an ideal gene engineering tool for in vivo transfection of macrophages with the MMP9 antibody.

To characterize the aMMP9 antibody encoded by the circRNA, we designed a single open reading frame comprising an IgK signal peptide (IgKsp), the MMP9-specific heavy chain, a self-cleaving 2A peptide, and the corresponding light chain, enabling co-translational expression and proper assembly of a full-length antibody (Fig. S4). The variable regions (VH and VL) were derived from a previously validated anti-MMP9 antibody, and the corresponding sequence information is provided in Table S3. The circRNA construct was further engineered to include an internal ribosome entry site (IRES) for cap-independent translation, along with optimized flanking sequences to enhance circularization efficiency. RNA fragment analysis revealed a dominant peak at the expected size (∼3.2 kb), accounting for the majority of RNA species, indicating high purity and structural integrity of the circRNA preparation. Consistently, protein expression analysis demonstrated robust and sustained production of the encoded antibody following circRNA delivery (Fig. S5). To directly confirm the circular structure, RNase R digestion was performed on RNA extracted from treated macrophages. As shown in Fig. S6, linear RNA was efficiently degraded upon RNase R treatment, whereas the circRNA remained resistant, confirming its circular topology and exonuclease stability.

To directly compare the expression performance of circRNA and linear mRNA, we evaluated antibody production kinetics following delivery of anti-MMP9-encoding linear mRNA-LNP or circRNA-LNP. The concentration of secreted antibody in the culture supernatant was quantified by human IgG ELISA at different time points. As shown in Fig. S7, both groups exhibited comparable antibody expression levels at early time points (day 1–3). However, antibody levels in the linear mRNA group declined rapidly thereafter, approaching baseline by day 5–7. In contrast, the circRNA group maintained significantly higher antibody levels over an extended period, with sustained expression observed up to day 7–9. These results demonstrate that while initial translation efficiency is similar between the two platforms, circRNA enables markedly prolonged protein expression compared to linear mRNA, consistent with its enhanced stability and resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation.

To better elucidate the structure–function relationship of the LNP formulations, we performed a systematic characterization of aMMP9-LNPs with varying DSPE-PEG2000 and PtdSer ratios in addition to the optimized formulation (0.5% DSPE-PEG2000 + 1% PtdSer). The physicochemical properties of these formulations were evaluated, including zeta potential and encapsulation efficiency. As shown in Fig. S8, all tested formulations exhibited similar negative zeta potentials (approximately −4 to −5 mV), indicating that variations in DSPE-PEG2000 and PtdSer composition had minimal impact on nanoparticle surface charge. Moreover, the encapsulation efficiency remained consistently high (>90%) across all formulations, suggesting robust RNA loading that is largely independent of lipid composition within the tested range. These data indicate that the superior performance of the optimized formulation is unlikely to be driven by differences in basic physicochemical properties, but may instead be attributed to other factors such as cellular uptake or intracellular trafficking.

Next, we evaluated the effects of aMMP9-LNP in vitro. CCK-8 was firstly performed to analyze the safety of LNP. Among the different concentrations of aMMP9-LNP, no significant differences were observed at day 1, day 3 and day 7 (Fig. 2f). Thus, the concentration of 200 μM was adopted in follow-up studies. Fig. 2g showed the schematic illustration. Then the concentration of MMP9 in macrophages was detected by ELISA. Briefly, aMMP9-LNP was added into the culture medium of macrophages and cultured for 24 h. Then the cells were washed with PBS for 3 times. After 1, 3 or 7 days, the supernatant was collected. As shown in Fig. 2h, after the treatment of aMMP9-LNP, the concentration of MMP9 in macrophages was decreased significantly detected by ELISA. While the expression of MMP9 was up-regulated after the treatment of aMMP9-LNP, which could contribute to the negative feedback regulation (Fig. 2i).

To further prove that the effect of anti-MMP9 was due to the release of antibodies into the supernatant instead of intracellular, we added aMMP9-LNP into the macrophages for 1 day, 3 days and 7 days and then collected the supernatant. The supernatant was then added into different plates of macrophages and cultured for different times. The concentration of MMP9 was detected by ELISA. The results showed that the supernatant could also decrease the concentration of MMP9 (Fig. 2j). Besides, the concentration of MMP9 in both supernatant and intracellular was detected. As shown in Fig. 2k, the total concentration of MMP9 was decreased by the used of aMMP9-LNP. Taken together, aMMP9-LNP released the antibodies of MMP9 into supernatant and decrease the concentration of MMP9.

To evaluate whether LNP uptake or intracellular antibody production alters macrophage functional states, we performed flow cytometry analysis of macrophage polarization markers. CD86 and CD206 were used to represent M1-like and M2-like phenotypes, respectively. As shown in Fig. S9, macrophages treated with LNP, MMP9, or aMMP9-LNP exhibited overall similar distributions compared with the control group. No significant shift toward either CD86+ (pro-inflammatory) or CD206+ (anti-inflammatory) populations was observed. These results suggest that neither nanoparticle uptake nor circRNA-mediated antibody expression induces substantial macrophage polarization under the current experimental conditions. Instead, macrophages primarily serve as carriers and producers of therapeutic antibodies without undergoing pronounced functional reprogramming.

Macrophage-derived secretion of anti-MMP9 was able to rejuvenate senescent BMSCs

To directly evaluate whether MMP9 is sufficient to induce senescence phenotypes in BMSCs, in vitro experiments were performed by treating young BMSCs with recombinant MMP9 protein under controlled conditions. As shown in Fig. S10, MMP9-treated BMSCs exhibited a significant increase in SA-β-gal staining compared to untreated controls, indicating enhanced cellular senescence. In addition, transwell migration assays demonstrated a marked reduction in migratory capacity, suggesting impaired cellular function. Osteogenic differentiation potential was also notably diminished, as evidenced by decreased Alizarin Red staining. At the molecular level, quantitative analysis revealed significant downregulation of key osteogenic markers, including Runx2, Col1a1, ALP, and OCN, further confirming that MMP9 treatment compromises the osteogenic capacity of BMSCs. These findings collectively support the conclusion that MMP9 is sufficient to induce senescence-like phenotypes and functional decline in BMSCs.

As the aging of BMSCs is accompanied with deterioration of osteogenic differentiation, proliferation capacity and migration capacity [39], we next assessed whether MMP9 acted in producing senescence in BMSCs. Fig. 3a showed the schematic illustration of co-culture. aMMP9-LNP was added into macrophages and cultured for 24 h. Then macrophages were collected and centrifuged to remove remaining aMMP9-LNP. In the transwell assay, BMSCs were seeded in the lower chamber while macrophages were placed in the upper chamber. For other assays, the configuration was reversed, with BMSCs in the upper chamber and macrophages in the lower chamber.

Firstly, we compared the proliferation of BMSCs derived from young and aged mice. Population doubling was significantly decreased in aged-BMSCs and aged-BMSCs with LNP compared with young-BMSCs and aged-BMSCs with aMMP9-LNP (Fig. 3b). Fig. 3c showed that the percentages of SA-β-gal+ cells (stained with aquamarine blue) were significantly increased in aged BMSCs and aged-BMSCs with LNP compared with young-BMSCs and aged-BMSCs with aMMP9-LNP. This demonstrated that aMMP9-LNP could alleviate the senescence of BMSCs. Next, we investigated the migration of BMSCs. The results showed that the migration activity of BMSCs was also enhanced by anti-MMP9 treatment in transwell assay (Fig. 3d). Moreover, aMMP9-LNP promoted osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs as evidenced by Alizarin Red staining (Fig. 3e). Meanwhile, the chondrogenic differentiation and adipogenic differentiation of BMSCs were up-regulated in aged BMSCs, while anti-MMP9 could inhibit the chondrogenic differentiation and adipogenic differentiation (Fig. 3f–g). These findings were confirmed by the expression of osteogenic-related genes, such as Runx2, Col1a1, Alp and OCN (Fig. 3h).

Targeted inhibition of MMP9 restores bone microarchitecture and improves mechanical strength in aged mice

To determine whether MMP9 accelerated aging-dependent bone loss, we compared the vertebral phenotype of 3-month-old and 24-month-old mice. LNP and aMMP9-LNP was injected by the tail vein into 24-month-old mice. No significant differences in body weight were observed during the experimental period (Fig. S11). The level of calcium, phosphorus and parathyroid hormone (PTH) in serum of mice was lower in aged mice than young mice. While C-telopeptide of type 1 collagen (CTX) was higher in aged mice. The injection of aMMP9-LNP could normalize the level of calcium, phosphorus, PTH and CTX in aged mice while LNP had no similar effects (Fig. S12). Meanwhile, the injection of LNP did not affect other organs like lung, liver, kidney, spleen or heart (Fig. S13). The results of Micro-CT showed that aMMP9-LNP markedly enhanced the bone volume and protected the microarchitecture of trabecular bone in aged mice (Fig. 4a). Bone mineral density (BMD), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular bone volume (BV/TV) and trabecular separation (Tp.Sp) were dramatically reduced in aged mice and aged mice with LNP. By contrast, these parameters were not decreased in aged mice with aMMP9-LNP (Fig. 4b–e). The concentration of MMP9 in serum of mice was also detected. The injection of aMMP9-LNP significantly decreased the concentration of MMP9 in aged mice, while LNP did not alter it (Fig. 4f).

To determine whether MMP9-induced aging-related bone loss was linked to changes in bone turnover, both bone formation and resorption were evaluated. As shown in Fig. 4g, aMMP9-LNP increased the bone mass in aged mice. The osteoblast number in aged mice and aged mice with LNP was lower than young mice and aged mice with aMMP9-LNP, indicating a more active bone remodeling status (Fig. 4h). However, TRAP-positive osteoclast numbers were increased significantly in aged mice and aged mice with LNP compared with young mice and aged mice with aMMP9-LNP (Fig. 4i–j). The immunofluorescence staining of MMP9 in vertebral showed that more positive staining appeared in aged mice and aged mice with LNP (Fig. 4k–l).

We then assessed whether aging-dependent bone loss induced by MMP9 was associated with increased osteocyte senescence and senescence-associated secretory phenotype. The results demonstrated that the senescence markers MMP3 and P21 protein were plentifully accumulated around the trabecular bone in the in aged mice and aged mice with LNP compared with young mice and aged mice with aMMP9-LNP (Figs. S14–15). These findings indicated that MMP9 promotes age-related bone loss by suppressing osteoblastic bone formation, enhancing osteoclastic resorption, upregulating p21, and inducing osteocyte senescence and SASP. Conversely, administration of aMMP9-LNP mitigated this bone loss.

LNP-mediated MMP9 neutralization accelerates fracture healing and enhances callus integrity

The healing of bone defect in postponed along with aging [40]. Delayed-union or nonunion of bone fracture often occurs in aged people. Thus, we performed femur fracture model in aged mice and young mice. Fig. 5a showed the schematic of the experimental design. Mid-shaft fracture model was established before the injection of LNP or aMMP9-LNP. The mice were sacrificed 4 weeks post-surgery. Biomechanical testing has been widely adopted in assessing the quality of fracture healing. As shown in Fig. 5b, both the binding rigidity and modulus of elasticity (E-modulus) were decreased in aged mice and aged mice treated with LNP. aMMP9-LNP could alleviate these. The results of Micro-CT showed that young mice and aged mice treated with aMMP9-LNP had almost achieved fracture union at 28 days post-surgery. However, in aged mice and aged mice with LNP, facture healing was not complete at 28 days. The BV/TV of the femurs were decreased in aged mice and aged mice with LNP than young mice and aged mice with aMMP9-LNP (Fig. 5c).

In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed to observe the distribution of LNP by an In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS). To investigate the distribution of LNP in the organs, the fluorescence signal of the main organs separated from the mice were examined. As shown in Fig. 5, Fig. 24 h after the injection of LNP, fluorescence could be observed among femurs, vertebral, liver and kidney. Until 72 h after injection, the fluorescence could still be observed. Besides, relatively stronger fluorescence intensity was observed in femurs compared with other tissues, suggesting preferential accumulation in bone-associated regions. However, fluorescence signals were also detected in the liver and kidney, which is consistent with the known biodistribution and clearance pathways of lipid nanoparticles through hepatobiliary and renal systems. These results indicate that the observed skeletal enrichment is unlikely to result from passive physicochemical targeting alone, but is more consistent with a cell-mediated delivery mechanism, in which monocytes/macrophages serve as carriers to transport the therapeutic payload to inflamed bone microenvironments. The positive staining of P21 in aged mice and aged mice with LNP demonstrated the senescence in the femur (Fig. 5e). As shown in Fig. 5f, the results of Masson staining showed the fracture sites of young mice and aged mice with aMMP9-LNP achieved union. The results of H&E and Safranin-fixed green staining showed the similar trends (Figs. S16–17). TRAP staining of the fracture sites showed that osteoclasts were over-activated in aged mice and aged mice with LNP (Fig. S18).

In addition, the temporal profile of antibody expression following a single administration was evaluated. As shown in Fig. S19, antibody levels increased within the first few days, reached peak levels around day 3–5, and remained detectable for approximately 1–2 weeks before gradually declining. This result indicates that circRNA-mediated expression is transient yet sustained over a therapeutically relevant timeframe.

Inhibition of MMP9 ameliorates osteoarthritic degeneration associated with MMP13/P21 axis

Primary OA develops gradually during the adult lifespan. Thus, we further investigated the effects of aMMP9-LNP on age-associated osteoarthritis. As shown in Fig. 6a, the OARSI cartilage damage scores were significantly increased in aged mice and aged mice with LNP, suggesting widespread cartilage damage. While the OARSI scores in aged mice treated with aMMP9-LNP were decreased. Micro-CT analysis showed that the proximal tibia in aged mice and aged mice with LNP were widely degenerated (Fig. 6b). The staining of H&E and Safranin O-fast green showed signs of cartilage degeneration in aged mice, including full-thickness defects and cartilage erosion. aMMP9-LNP alleviated the degeneration of the knee (Fig. 6c and e). Quantitative analysis showed that the adipocyte density and the percentage of osteoblasts in the area of knee were up-regulated by aMMP9-LNP (Fig. 6d and f). MMP3 and P21 protein were plentifully accumulated around the tibia in the in aged mice and aged mice with LNP compared with young mice and aged mice with aMMP9-LNP (Figs. S20–21). TRAP-positive osteoclast numbers were increased significantly in aged mice and aged mice with LNP compared with young mice and aged mice with aMMP9-LNP (Fig. S22).

To evaluate whether LNP itself exerts non-specific effects on bone homeostasis, we performed additional in vivo experiments in young mice treated with LNP alone. Micro-CT analysis of lumbar vertebrae, femurs, and proximal tibiae revealed no significant changes in bone architecture compared to controls (Fig. S23a, d and g). Consistently, histological assessments, including H&E, TRAP, Masson, and Safranin O-fast green staining, showed no observable alterations in trabecular structure, osteoclast activity, or cartilage composition (Figs. S23b, c, e and h). Furthermore, immunostaining of p21 at fracture sites did not indicate increased cellular senescence following LNP administration (Fig. S23f).

Discussion

Aging-related bone issues like osteoporosis, delayed fracture healing, and osteoarthritis burden global aging populations, yet effective therapies targeting the underlying molecular mechanisms remain scarce. Current approaches, such as systemic anti-MMP9 antibody injections, are limited by poor tissue targeting and short-lived effects. In this study, RNA sequencing of serum and bone samples from elderly and osteoporotic individuals consistently revealed upregulation of MMP9. This clinical observation anchored subsequent mechanistic exploration in animal models, demonstrating that MMP9 overexpression drives skeletal senescence through BMSC dysfunction and elevated SASP signaling, including senescence markers p21 and MMP3. To overcome this, we engineered biomimetic lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) for selective uptake by monocytes/macrophages and used circRNA to transiently reprogram these cells as in vivo “bioreactors” for sustained, localized anti-MMP9 antibody production. This strategy enabled efficient and lasting MMP9 depletion in blood and inflammatory microenvironments. This genetically-engineered, macrophage-mediated delivery of local MMP9-neutralizing antibodies offers MMP9 clearance within the bone niche.

This platform departs from previous mRNA-LNP approaches promoting anabolic regeneration [41,42] (e.g., BMP-2, VEGF), which primarily address downstream consequences rather than the underlying cause of skeletal aging. Instead, by focusing on the upstream catabolic trigger MMP9, we achieved a more comprehensive correction of age-related bone dysfunction, as evidenced by restored trabecular architecture, reduced osteoclast activity, and enhanced biomechanical strength in naturally aged and fracture models. In osteoarthritis models, our therapy also preserved cartilage integrity by limiting osteoclast-driven subchondral degradation. This holistic rescue contrasts with anabolic-only strategies and aligns with emerging concepts in bone repair biology that advocate for balanced modulation of both construction and remodeling of bone tissue. It should be noted that macrophages represent a heterogeneous population with diverse origins and activation states, particularly in aging and inflammatory conditions. In this study, we primarily assessed macrophage responses using canonical polarization markers (CD86 and CD206), which provide a simplified representation of macrophage activation. While our data suggest that LNP uptake and antibody production do not markedly alter polarization at this level, more detailed characterization, such as single-cell transcriptomic profiling or lineage-specific analysis, will be required to fully elucidate macrophage heterogeneity and functional dynamics in future studies.

Our approach bears resemblance to recent advances in selective organ-targeting LNPs, such as SORT LNPs that preferentially deliver mRNA to cell types beyond the liver [43]. Our design, enriched with PteSer, effectively targeted monocytes/macrophages, cells with a central role in the aged bone inflammatory microenvironment, thus providing dual benefits of local MMP9 neutralization and immunomodulation. Importantly, the therapeutic effects observed in this study are not solely attributable to passive systemic antibody diffusion, but are closely associated with macrophage-mediated local production and delivery. The PtdSer-engineered LNPs are preferentially recognized and internalized by monocytes/macrophages via efferocytosis-related pathways, enabling these cells to function as in situ “bioreactors” for sustained antibody secretion. Consistent with this mechanism, conditioned medium from aMMP9-LNP-treated macrophages was sufficient to suppress MMP9 levels in recipient cells, supporting a macrophage-dependent paracrine mode of action. Furthermore, the natural trafficking behavior of monocytes/macrophages between circulation and bone tissue provides a biological basis for localized therapeutic effects. While prior interventions targeting MMP9 (e.g., small-molecule inhibitors, recombinant proteins) have shown efficacy in isolated contexts, which lacks the precision and convenience of a single-dose, self-amplifying delivery platform.

It should be noted that the “bone-targeted” effect described in this study does not represent strict organ-specific accumulation at the nanoparticle level. Instead, it reflects a functional targeting mechanism mediated by monocytes/macrophages. The PtdSer-modified LNPs are preferentially recognized and internalized by these cells via efferocytosis-related pathways, after which macrophages traffic to inflamed or aged bone microenvironments and locally release the encoded therapeutic antibody. This cell-mediated delivery paradigm differs fundamentally from conventional passive targeting strategies based solely on physicochemical properties. Consistent with this interpretation, in vivo imaging revealed detectable signals not only in bone but also in the liver and kidney, which are known to participate in nanoparticle metabolism and clearance. Importantly, no evident histopathological abnormalities or systemic toxicity were observed in these organs within the experimental timeframe, suggesting that such off-target distribution is within a tolerable range.

Another remarkable advantage of our circRNA-based system is its transient yet potent effect. CircRNAs offer enhanced stability and reduced innate immunogenicity compared to linear mRNAs [[44], [45], [46]]. We leveraged this stability to sustain antibody production in situ, which bridges the gap between transient mRNA therapies and long-term gene programming. Our strategy, by enabling local antibody secretion, reduces systemic exposure and potential off-target effects. Comparing our results to the existing circRNA therapeutic candidates, such as those targeting muscle aging or immune-mediated pathologies, underscores how circRNA payloads can be tailored to diverse disease contexts [[47], [48], [49]]. By encoding neutralizing antibodies directly in immune cells, we suggest a new delivery paradigm that extends beyond protein or miRNA cargo to biologics [38]. One of the most compelling features of our design is its breadth-addressing effect including osteoporosis, impaired fracture repair, and osteoarthritis within a single mechanistic framework. By rebalancing osteoblast–osteoclast coupling and counteracting SASP signaling, we restored both bone structure and functional resilience. Furthermore, we observed no systemic toxicity as body weight remained stable and organ histology was normal. These safety data are critical for translational consideration.

Importantly, our findings indicate that MMP9 neutralization suppresses the enhanced adipogenic and chondrogenic differentiation observed in aged BMSCs, pointing to a regulatory role of MMP9 in stem cell lineage commitment. This effect is likely mediated through its broad impact on the bone microenvironment. As a major extracellular matrix - degrading enzyme, excessive MMP9 activity disrupts matrix integrity and alters biomechanical cues that are essential for osteogenic differentiation. Such changes in the niche have been shown to favor adipogenic and chondrogenic lineages over osteogenesis. In parallel, MMP9 is closely associated with the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, which contributes to a chronic inflammatory microenvironment. Persistent inflammation is known to impair osteoblast differentiation while promoting adipogenesis in mesenchymal stem cells. Consistent with this, we observed that MMP9 inhibition reduced the expression of senescence markers such as p21 and MMP3, suggesting a partial restoration of a more permissive regenerative niche. Furthermore, MMP9-mediated proteolysis may influence the bioavailability of matrix-bound growth factors, thereby reshaping downstream signaling pathways that govern cell fate decisions. Collectively, these observations suggest that MMP9 acts as a key modulator of microenvironmental signals that drive lineage bias in aging. Its neutralization restores microenvironmental balance and promotes a shift toward osteogenic differentiation while suppressing aberrant lineage commitment.

An additional consideration is the cellular origin of MMP9 within the aged bone microenvironment. Accumulating evidence suggests that MMP9 is not derived from a single cell type but is produced by multiple populations, including monocytes/macrophages, osteoclasts, neutrophils, and senescent stromal cells. Among these, monocyte/macrophage lineage cells are considered major contributors, particularly under inflammatory and aging-associated conditions, where they actively secrete MMP9 as part of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype. This cellular context further supports the rationale of our strategy. In the present study, macrophages are not only key producers of MMP9 but also serve as engineered therapeutic mediators. By reprogramming these cells to secrete anti-MMP9 antibodies, our approach effectively converts a major source of pathological MMP9 into a localized site of therapeutic intervention. Such a design establishes a functional feedback loop within the diseased microenvironment, enabling both suppression of excessive MMP9 activity and modulation of inflammatory signaling. This dual role of macrophages highlights their central position in coordinating inflammation, matrix remodeling, and tissue regeneration in aging bone, and further underscores the advantage of targeting this cell population for therapeutic intervention.

Recent advances in immune-engineering and regenerative medicine have highlighted macrophages as central regulators of tissue repair and disease progression. In particular, macrophage reprogramming using nanomaterials or biomaterial-based systems has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy to modulate inflammatory microenvironments and promote regeneration. For example, nanomedicine-based immunomodulation has been shown to reshape macrophage function to enhance tissue repair [50], while biomaterial-guided regulation of macrophage polarization plays a critical role in bone healing and regeneration [51]. In addition, integrative therapeutic systems that coordinate immunoregulation with tissue regeneration further underscore the importance of targeting macrophage-mediated pathways [52]. These findings collectively support the rationale of our macrophage-targeted circRNA delivery strategy.

Recent studies further emphasize that mitochondrial dysfunction is a central driver of bone aging, primarily through increased reactive oxygen species production, impaired energy metabolism, and activation of inflammatory signaling pathways, all of which contribute to stem cell dysfunction and lineage imbalance. In parallel, emerging therapeutic strategies have highlighted the importance of modulating the osteo-immune microenvironment, particularly through regulating macrophage polarization and suppressing inflammasome activation, to restore bone regeneration. These findings support the notion that effective interventions in aging bone should not only target structural components such as the extracellular matrix but also address metabolic and immune dysregulation. In this context, our strategy of MMP9 neutralization may exert broader effects by simultaneously modulating matrix remodeling, inflammatory signaling, and the senescence-associated microenvironment [53,54].

Nonetheless, there are challenges and limitations in our study. We observed some uptake in the liver and spleen, and chronic exposure or repeat dosing might elicit immune responses or off-target effects. Moreover, future translating rodent results to humans will demand validation in larger animal models and assessment across skeletal sites. Manufacturing this therapy at clinical grade will require strict control of circRNA purity, LNP encapsulation, and stability. Furthermore, cGMP-compliant production of circRNA LNPs, along with stability and immune tolerability, must be ensured under stringent regulatory standards.

Another limitation of this study is the relatively limited size and clinical heterogeneity of the human cohorts used for transcriptomic analysis. Although detailed patient information, including comorbidities, is provided, factors such as underlying diseases and medications may introduce variability. In addition, only male subjects were included to reduce sex-related confounding, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies with larger, sex-balanced cohorts and more controlled clinical variables will be necessary to further validate the clinical relevance of MMP9 as a biomarker and therapeutic target.

An important limitation of the present study is the lack of direct macrophage depletion or lineage-tracing experiments to definitively establish the necessity of macrophages in mediating the therapeutic effects. While such approaches would provide rigorous causal evidence, their implementation in naturally aged models is technically challenging and may introduce significant confounding factors. Macrophages are indispensable for bone homeostasis, remodeling, and repair; therefore, systemic depletion strategies could inherently impair skeletal regeneration and obscure interpretation of therapeutic outcomes. Similarly, lineage-tracing approaches require complex transgenic systems that are not readily compatible with long-term aging models. Instead, our conclusions are supported by multiple complementary lines of evidence, including macrophage-targeted LNP design, efficient circRNA expression and antibody production in macrophages, and functional effects mediated by macrophage-conditioned medium. Future studies incorporating inducible depletion models or cell-specific tracking strategies will be important to further dissect the precise cellular contributions.

The modular nature of this platform allows for rapid customization. While this study focuses on MMP9, the same circRNA-LNP scaffold can be readily adapted to encode antibodies targeting other SASP factors. Moreover, co-delivery strategies that pair neutralizing antibodies with anabolic agents may yield synergistic therapeutic effects. The application of next-generation LNPs incorporating SORT chemistry further enables targeted delivery to specific skeletal components, including osteoclast precursors, fibrocartilage, and bone vasculature. Clinically, the transient and localized profile of this therapy supports outpatient use in osteoporosis, enhances healing in elderly fracture patients, and may help preserve cartilage in early-stage osteoarthritis. In contrast to chronic systemic biologics, this approach minimizes immunosuppression and allows for personalized, episodic dosing.

It should be noted that sex-dependent differences in bone metabolism and regenerative capacity represent an important biological variable. In the present study, only male aged mice were included due to the prolonged duration required for natural aging models and ethical considerations regarding animal use. Future studies incorporating female and ovariectomized models will be necessary to further evaluate the influence of hormonal regulation on therapeutic efficacy. In addition, our supplementary data demonstrate that LNP alone does not affect bone structure or senescence, supporting the specificity of our therapeutic strategy and excluding potential confounding effects from the delivery system.

While our results demonstrate that MMP9 neutralization reduces the expression of p21 and MMP3, the underlying regulatory mechanism is likely indirect. As a secreted extracellular protease, MMP9 does not function as a transcriptional regulator but instead modulates the cellular microenvironment through matrix degradation and proteolytic activation of signaling molecules. These processes can influence key pathways associated with cellular senescence, including inflammatory signaling and growth factor-mediated responses. In this context, elevated MMP9 activity in aging may promote a pro-senescent microenvironment by enhancing inflammatory cues and disrupting matrix homeostasis, thereby upregulating downstream effectors such as p21 and MMP3. Conversely, inhibition of MMP9 may alleviate these signals and partially restore a more regenerative state. Although this study establishes a functional link between MMP9 activity and senescence-associated markers, further investigation will be required to define the precise upstream signaling pathways involved.

Systemic inhibition of MMP9 raises potential safety concerns due to its physiological roles in extracellular matrix remodeling and immune regulation. In this study, several design features may help mitigate these risks. First, the circRNA platform enables transient yet sustained antibody expression, avoiding long-term systemic suppression associated with permanent gene modification or repeated administration of long-acting biologics. Second, the incorporation of PtdSer promotes preferential uptake by monocytes/macrophages, which are naturally recruited to inflamed or aged bone microenvironments, thereby biasing antibody production toward pathological sites rather than uniform systemic distribution. Consistent with this, although partial accumulation in clearance-associated organs such as the liver and kidney was observed, no evident histopathological abnormalities or systemic toxicity were detected within the experimental timeframe. These findings suggest that the current delivery strategy maintains a favorable safety profile under single-dose conditions. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that comprehensive evaluation of long-term safety, repeated dosing effects, and potential immune responses will be required in future studies to fully assess the translational potential of this approach.

Although our results demonstrate favorable short-term biosafety of aMMP9-LNP following single-dose administration, long-term safety remains an important consideration for clinical translation. As observed in many lipid nanoparticle-based systems, partial accumulation in the liver and spleen reflects physiological clearance via the hepatobiliary and reticuloendothelial systems. While no histopathological abnormalities were detected within the current observation period, repeated administration may introduce additional risks, including immune activation, antibody production, and cumulative organ exposure. Future studies will therefore focus on extended dosing regimens and comprehensive safety evaluation, including serum biomarkers of liver and kidney function, immune profiling, and long-term tissue analysis, to further define the translational potential of this platform.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents a bench-to-bedside therapeutic development pipeline, starting with the identification of a clinical biomarker (MMP9), elucidating its mechanistic contribution, and culminating in the design of a smart nanomedicine for localized neutralization. Our immune-reprogramming strategy promotes long-lasting skeletal rejuvenation across multiple age-related conditions, offering a disease-modifying treatment that addresses aging pathology at its root. This work provides novel insight into mRNA-based immunotherapies with significant translational potential for skeletal aging and beyond.

Experimental section

Ethics and sample collection

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Committee at Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital (2025-0056). Serum samples were harvested from patients with closed fractures of the limbs and undergoing fracture internal fixation. They were divided into young group (age <40 y, n = 9) and elder group (age >65 y, n = 13). Serum samples were collected from discarded blood samples form routine preoperative examination. Bone samples were harvested from patients (aged >65 y) undergoing hip arthroplasty due to femoral neck fractures and divided into osteoporosis group (n = 6) and non-osteoporosis group (n = 6) based on the results of pelvic X-ray and CT. BMD <0.7 g/cm2 was defined as osteoporosis. Bone samples were harvested from discarded femoral heads. Detailed demographic and clinical information of the enrolled subjects, including age, sex, diagnosis, and comorbidities, are provided in Table S1 (serum samples) and Table S2 (bone samples). Briefly, the serum cohort consisted of young (n = 9, age <40 years) and aged individuals (n = 13, age >65 years), while the bone cohort included osteoporotic (n = 6) and non-osteoporotic donors (n = 6). All subjects included in this study were male to minimize potential confounding effects from sex-dependent differences in bone metabolism. Comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, pulmonary infection, and cardiovascular conditions were present in some aged individuals, reflecting the typical clinical characteristics of elderly populations. These variables are unlikely to account for the consistent upregulation of MMP9 observed across both serum and bone transcriptomic datasets.

RNA sequencing

Briefly, total RNA was isolated and purified by Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Poly (A) RNA was purified from total RNA, fragmented into small pieces and reverse-transcribed to create the cDNA (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). After further modification, the ligated products were amplified with PCR. The average insert size for the final cDNA library was 300 ± 50 bp. At last, we performed the 2×150 bp paired-end sequencing (PE150) on an illumina Novaseq™ 6000 (LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) following the vendor's recommended protocol.

Preparation of aMMP9-LNPs

To evaluate the best LNP formulation for MMP9 circRNA loading for macrophage gene-engineering (aMMP9-LNP), three formulation candidates were used in this study. The first is a classic LNP formulation with a molar ratio of components as follows: Dlin-MC3-DMA: DSPE-PEG2000: DSPC: Cholesterol = 50%: 1.5%: 10%: 38.5%. The second formulation includes an additional 1% PtdSer. In the third formulation, the concentration of DSPE-PEG2000 is reduced to 0.5%, while also incorporating 1% PtdSer. The above five lipid components were first dissolved in anhydrous ethanol according to the molar ratio. Then, the corresponding circMmp9 was added to the citrate buffer solution. The microfluidic mixing method (Unigen Biotech Co., Suzhou, China) was used in the present experiments so that the lipid solution and mRNA solution could fully and rapidly form LNPs with uniform particle sizes in the micro-mixer. Since lipids are soluble in ethanol and nucleic acids in an acidic buffer, residual ethanol was removed by ultrafiltration, and the solution system was displaced into PBS.

Cell isolation and culture

Primary mouse BMSCs were isolated from the femurs and tibias of 3-month-old mice or 24-month-old mice and cultured in α-MEM (Gibco BRL, Grand Island, NY) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco BRL, Grand Island, NY) and 100 mg/ml penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco BRL, Grand Island, NY). The RAW 264.7 cell line was purchased from Servicebio Co. (Wuhan, China), and maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS and 100 mg/ml penicillin and streptomycin.

For lifespan analysis, BMSCs were cultured in 100 mm dishes. After 3 days, adherent cells were counted to quantify the initial number. At 90% confluence, the cells were digested, counted and reseeded at 5 × 103 cells/cm2 in 6-well plates, treated with LNP or aMMP9-LNP. This procedure was repeated until BMSCs reached their maximal life span.

The cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Proliferation of RAW 264.7 cell was detected with CCK-8 (Dojindo, Kumamato, Japan). Briefly, cells at the density of 5 × 103/well were plated into a 96-well plate. Different concentrations of LNP were added into the plate and incubated for 1, 3 and 7 days. Then CCK-8 solution was added and incubated for 1.5 h at 37 °C. The absorbance value of each well was measured by a microplate reader at 450 nm (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA).

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining

Senescent cells were detected with β-galactosidase Staining Kit (Solarbio Co., Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, BMSCs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and rinsed with PBS. Then the cells were incubated with staining buffer overnight. Images were captured with a light microscope.

Cell migration assay

For Transwell assay, the inserts with 8 μm membrane were plated in the wells of 24-well plates. BMSCs were placed in the upper chamber cultured with 10% FBS and α-MEM. RAW 264.7 cells were pre-treated with LNP or aMMP9-LNP for 24 h and then seeded in the lower chamber cultured with 10% FBS and DMEM. After co-culture for 12 h, medium in both lower and upper chamber was removed. BMSCs migrated to the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with paraformaldehyde and then stained with 0.05% crystal violet. Images were captured with a light microscope.

Cell differentiation assay

For osteogenic differentiation, cells were cultured in osteogenic medium containing 100 nM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and 10 μM β-sodium glycerophosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). After 14 days, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and washed with PBS. Then cells were stained with ALP staining buffer according to the instructions (Jiancheng Co., Nanjing, China). After 21 days, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and washed with PBS. Then cells were stained with alizarin red working solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) for 5 min. Images were captured with a light microscope.

For adipogenic differentiation, cells were cultured in adipogenic differentiation medium (Cyagen Co., Nanjing, China). After 21 days, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and washed with PBS. Then cells were stained with Oil-red O according to the instruction. Images were captured with a light microscope.

For chondrocyte differentiation, cells were cultured in chondrogenic differentiation medium (Cyagen Co., Nanjing, China). After 14 days, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and washed with PBS. Then cells were stained with Alcian blue according to the instruction. Images were captured with a light microscope.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from BMSCs and RAW 264.7 cells using RNA Purification Kit (EZBioscience, Houston, TX) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using Color Reverse Transcription Kit (EZBioscience, Houston, TX). The real-time RT-PCR was carried out using an Agilent Real-time System.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Concentration of MMP9 in cells, bone and serum were determined with ELISA kit (Proteintech Co., Shanghai, China) following the instructions provided. Briefly, for determination of serum and bone MMP9, serum and bone were collected from patients or mice and centrifuged. Then the supernatant was added into the kit. For determination of cells MMP9, supernatant or cell lysates were collected and centrifuged. Then the supernatant was added into the kit.

Animals and treatments

Care and use of laboratory animals were approved by the Animal Research Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University affiliated Sixth People's Hospital (2025-0134). The mice were free to access food and water with ample sleep at 12 h alternation of day and night. To establish the naturally aged mice model, male C57BL/6 mice were routinely fed for 24 months in a standard feeding condition (n = 12). 3-month-old mice were served as the control group (n = 12).

The fracture model of was established through the surgery on the long bone of mice. In brief, the mice were firstly placed under anesthesia. A small incision was made near the anterior distal femur and the surfaces of the femurs were exposed by blunt dissection. A 25-gauge needle was inserted through the intramedullary canal. A 1.4 mm defect was created with a round bur. The cuts were closed layer-by-layer using 5-0 silk sutures.

Measurements of serum biochemical parameters

Collect blood samples from experimental animals or human subjects into sterile tubes. Allow the blood to clot at room temperature for 30∼60 min. Centrifuge the blood samples at 2000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Serum calcium, phosphorus, parathyroid hormone (PTH) and C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX) levels were analyzed by an autoanalyzer (Beckam Synchron 67; Beckman Instruments, Billerica, MA).

Micro-CT analysis

Bone tissues were harvested and fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde overnight and stored in 70% ethanol at 4 °C. Micro-CT was performed with a micro-CT scanner (Skyscan 1176; Bruker, Belgium) set at a 9-μm voxel size. Thereafter, volumetric reconstructions and analyses were performed by CTVol software (Bruker, Billerica, MA). Bone mineral density (BMD), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular bone volume (BV/TV) and trabecular separation (Tp.Sp) was measured from the reconstructed images.

Mechanical test

Three-point bending test of long bones were performed using a mechanical testing device. The femur callus was positioned at the center and a central load was applied at a rate of 0.05 mm/s. Load deflection curves were analyzed for maximum load.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis

At the time of euthanasia, lumbar vertebrae, femurs and tibias were dissected free of soft tissue and fixed in paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C and washed with PBS for 15 min55. Then bone specimens were decalcified with 10% ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) and embedded in paraffin, after which 5-μm sections were prepared. Bone sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP), Masson and Safranine. Leica DM 4000 microscope was used to capture the images.

For immunohistochemistry staining, sections were firstly incubated with hydrogen peroxide and washed in PBS. The sections were incubated with the primary antibodies to P21 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and MMP3 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) overnight. After washing with PBS, the slides were incubated with secondary antibody (Sigma, St. Louis, MO). Leica DM 4000 microscope was used to capture the images.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation from at least three individuals. Statistical analysis was used with SPSS 20.0 software. Student's t-test, one-way, or two-way ANOVA was adopted to compare differences between groups. Values of p < 0.05 were of statistical significance. Bioinformatic analysis was performed using the OmicStudio tools at https://www.omicstudio.cn. The plots were drawn based on the R version 4.1.3 (2022-03-10) on the OmicStudio platform (https://www.omicstudio.cn).
