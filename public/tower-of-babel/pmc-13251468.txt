Atypical cutaneous candidiasis mimicking tinea corporis in an otherwise healthy young man: A case report
Authors: Sibo Liu, Yinggai Song, Aiping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCutaneous candidiasis typically affects intertriginous areas and may be overlooked when occurring at non-intertriginous sites. We report a 28-year-old otherwise healthy man with a 7-month history of pruritic scaly erythematous lesions on both upper extremities and the upper abdomen. Before presentation, he had used a topical corticosteroid unsupervised for 2 months. The lesions were initially treated as tinea corporis. Direct microscopy showed pseudohyphae and spores, and two consecutive fungal cultures yielded Candida spp., with CHROMagar Candida supporting Candida albicans. The lesions resolved completely within 2 weeks after oral itraconazole and topical ciclopirox olamine. This case highlights the importance of mycological confirmation in chronic scaly eruptions with unusual distribution, especially in the setting of prior topical corticosteroid exposure.Keywords: Candida albicans, Cutaneous candidiasis, Atypical presentation, Immunocompetent patient, Tinea corporis, Corticosteroid

Introduction

Candida species are common components of the normal human microbiota and frequently colonize the skin and mucosal surfaces, especially the oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, and genitourinary tract [1]. Although most colonization is asymptomatic, certain Candida spp. may behave as opportunistic pathogens when host or local cutaneous conditions become favorable for invasion [1]. Candida albicans is the predominant pathogenic species and is responsible for a wide range of clinical manifestations, from superficial mucocutaneous infection to invasive and potentially life-threatening disease [2]. Cutaneous candidiasis is a relatively common superficial fungal infection, but its classic presentation usually involves intertriginous or other moist areas [3]. Typical lesions present as erythematous, macerated, erosive, or scaly plaques, often accompanied by satellite papules or pustules [3,4]. It is more frequently reported in patients with predisposing conditions such as diabetes mellitus, malignancy, HIV infection, prolonged antibiotic exposure, or other causes of impaired host defense [5]. However, atypical presentations may also occur in otherwise healthy individuals, especially when lesion morphology or distribution deviates from the classic pattern. Here, we report a 28-year-old otherwise healthy man with C. albicans cutaneous candidiasis involving the bilateral upper extremities and abdomen, which clinically mimicked tinea corporis.

Case report

A 28-year-old otherwise healthy man presented with a 7-month history of pruritic skin lesions involving both upper extremities and the upper abdomen. The eruption began as small erythematous papules on the right upper extremity. After approximately 2 months of self-application of a topical corticosteroid, the lesions gradually enlarged, extended to both upper extremities and the upper abdomen, and became persistent and recurrent. He denied animal contact and recent travel abroad. His past medical, family, and allergy histories were unremarkable.

Dermatological examination revealed extensive erythematous papules, patches, and scaly plaques on the flexural aspects of both upper extremities (Fig. 1A). Mild lichenification, excoriation, and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation were noted in some areas. On the upper abdomen, multiple scattered round-to-oval brownish erythematous macules and patches with fine scales were observed (Fig. 1B). Potassium hydroxide (KOH) examination of skin scrapings revealed abundant fungal elements, including pseudohyphae and spores, and fungal culture was performed. Given the annular scaly appearance and distribution of the lesions, the patient was initially treated as having generalized tinea corporis with oral terbinafine (250 mg once daily for 2 weeks) plus topical 2.5% amorolfine cream.

Clinical presentation of the skin lesions. (A) Bilateral forearms showing symmetrically distributed erythematous scaly plaques with partial confluence. (B) Abdomen showing multiple scattered round-to-oval brownish erythematous macules and patches with fine scaling.

At follow-up after 2 weeks of treatment, the lesions on the upper extremities showed little improvement, whereas the abdominal lesions had partially improved. Repeat KOH examination remained positive, again showing abundant pseudohyphae and a few spores (Fig. 2). Fungal culture was repeated, and both cultures yielded yeast-like colonies identified as Candida spp. Further identification on CHROMagar Candida medium supported C. albicans. Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed according to the CLSI M27 reference standard, and the results are summarized in Table 1. The diagnosis was therefore revised to generalized cutaneous candidiasis with an atypical clinical presentation. The patient was then treated with oral itraconazole (200 mg twice daily) together with topical ciclopirox olamine cream. The lesions resolved completely within 2 weeks, leaving only post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.

Discussion

Cutaneous candidiasis classically involves intertriginous or other moist areas and is typically characterized by erythematous macerated plaques with scaling and satellite lesions [6]. In contrast, our patient presented with pruritic scaly erythematous papules and plaques on both upper extremities and the upper abdomen, a distinctly non-intertriginous distribution that closely mimicked tinea corporis.

A notable feature of the present case is the 2-month history of unsupervised topical corticosteroid use before presentation. Such exposure may have contributed to the atypical morphology, prolonged course, and delayed recognition of the eruption. Wong et al. noted that topical corticosteroids may increase susceptibility to fungal infection and complicate clinical recognition [7], while experimental data have shown that betamethasone enhances C. albicans adherence to and invasion of epithelial cells and attenuates epithelial immune responses [8]. Although causality cannot be proven in a single case, these findings provide a biologically plausible explanation for the atypical presentation in our patient.

Several published reports likewise support the view that cutaneous candidiasis can present in misleading ways, particularly after corticosteroid exposure. Facial candidiasis in a 13-year-old girl was initially interpreted as eczema or acneiform dermatosis after failure of topical steroid treatment [9]. Plaque-patterned candidiasis on the lower legs in a 67-year-old woman was first misdiagnosed as dermatitis and later psoriasis despite repeated steroid use [10]. Other reports have also described atypical C. albicans infection in an immunocompetent young patient [11]. Additional reports have documented candidal skin infection mimicking acneiform eruption on the face and even acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis-like disease [12,13]. Together, these observations indicate that cutaneous candidiasis may mimic a wide range of inflammatory dermatoses and that topical corticosteroid exposure may further mask its typical features.

In summary, this case expands the recognized spectrum of atypical cutaneous candidiasis by documenting a C. albicans infection on both upper extremities and the upper abdomen. Although reports of cutaneous candidiasis mimicking other dermatoses are increasingly available, cases specifically resembling tinea corporis at these sites appear to be scarce. Our findings reinforce the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis and performing repeated mycological evaluation in chronic, treatment-refractory, scaly eruptions, particularly when there is a history of prior topical corticosteroid use.
