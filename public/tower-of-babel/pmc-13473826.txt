Effectiveness of Aerobic Exercise on Metabolic Profiles in Women with Polycystic Ovary Syndrome: A Systematic Review, Meta-Analysis, and GRADE Assessment of RCTs
Authors: Rohit Gautam, Pratibha Maan, Sasidharan Sivakumar, Amit Arora, Saubhagya Kumar Jena, Taruna Arora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a metabolic and endocrine disorder that affects women of reproductive age. There is no absolute treatment for PCOS, but it is managed symptomatically. Adopting a healthy lifestyle comprising a balanced diet and physical exercise is the major recommendation for management of PCOS.ObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness of aerobic exercise as an intervention for improving metabolic profiles in women suffering from PCOS.MethodsA systematic search was performed in 4 databases; Scopus, MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, and Web of Science, since inception till 28.2.2025. Risk of bias was performed using Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool and meta-analysis was done by using random-effects models.ResultsTwenty-five RCTs were included in the review for qualitative synthesis and 5 RCTs with a total of 192 participants were included for quantitative analysis. A low but statistically significant decrease was observed in waist-to-hip ratio). Lipid profile analysis revealed a significant reduction in low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol. Further, HIIT also improved insulin sensitivity, by decreasing fasting insulin levels and HOMA-IR. A non-significant reduction was observed in body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, total cholesterol, and fasting glucose. Changes in triglycerides, and HDL were also non-significant.ConclusionAerobic exercise, especially HIIT, appears effective in improving regional fat distribution, LDL cholesterol, and insulin resistance in PCOS women, though evidence for BMI and hormonal changes remains inconclusive. Larger, longer-term RCTs are needed to confirm these findings and clarify hormonal outcomes.Prospero Registration: crd420251003425Keywords: polycystic ovary syndrome, aerobic exercise, high-intensity interval training, body composition, lipid profile, insulin resistance

Introduction

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common metabolic and reproductive disorders that affect especially 18–45 years old women.1 The worldwide prevalence of PCOS ranges between 5.5% and 11%, based on the different diagnostic criteria used (Rotterdam, NIH, and AES criteria).2 Major symptoms of PCOS are polycystic ovaries, hyperandrogenism, and oligomenorrhea.3 Other than these, PCOS women are also at a very high risk of other clinical issues such as metabolic disorders, cardiovascular problems, infertility, and co-morbidities like insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and dyslipidemia.4

Traditionally, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) has been managed symptomatically, based on the major problems faced by the patient. Management is largely pharmacological, employing agents such as anti‑androgens, insulin sensitizers (eg, metformin), and oral contraceptives.5 However, these drugs are usually associated with multiple side-effects and do not address the underlying causes of PCOS.6 To achieve sustainable management and long-term relief, there is a growing shift toward non-pharmacological/lifestyle interventions. Latest international evidence-based guidelines 20237 also recommended the lifestyle interventions as primary strategy for the prevention as well as management of PCOS. Lifestyle intervention is composed of two major components: diet and exercise. Dietary interventions include various types of specific diets such as antioxidant rich, low glycemic index, and calorie restricted that can help in controlling insulin resistance and hormonal imbalance.8 Exercise can also help in improving insulin sensitivity, reducing obesity, and enhancing cardiovascular and mental well-being in PCOS women that might lead to balancing hormones and regularizing menstrual cycle.9,10

Exercise refers to planned, structured, and repetitive physical activity that is typically performed with an objective to enhance or maintain health, well-being, and physical performance.11 Exercise is mainly of four types: aerobic exercise, strength training, flexibility, and balance. Aerobic exercise can be defined as repetitive, structured physical activity that boosts the metabolic system to use oxygen and produce energy.12 It involves brisk walking, swimming, running, jogging, cycling, dancing, etc. It increases blood flow to the muscles, relaxes walls of blood vessels, and improves endurance of lung and heart. It helps to manage cardiovascular health, blood pressure, glycemic index, weight, and inflammation and cholesterol levels.13 Performing regular exercise also decreases the risk of other health issues such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and psychological problems (stress and depression).14,15 In type 2 diabetes patient, it leads to improvement in weight reduction, improves glycemic control, and reduces visceral adiposity and quality of life.16,17

Previous studies have explored the effectiveness of various exercise programs for PCOS women. Various kinds of aerobic exercise programs such as high-intensity interval training (HIIT), moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT), continuous aerobic training (CAT), and intermittent aerobic training (IAT) have shown benefits on PCOS-related outcomes.18–20 They can improve menstrual regularity, lipid profiles, and insulin sensitivity and reduce visceral adiposity and inflammation in PCOS women.10,21 However, evidence remains inadequate for the optimal kind, intensity, and duration of exercise for effective PCOS management. This study aims to critically evaluate the existing studies for effectiveness of aerobic exercise in PCOS women. The evidence from the review can direct future research and assist clinical practice in prescribing exercises for PCOS women.

Review Question

Does aerobic exercise improve the metabolic profiles (anthropometric, biochemical, and hormonal parameters) of women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)?

Methodology

PROSPERO Registration

The present study complies with the standard of PRISMA-2020 guidelines (Preferred Reporting Item for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis) (Supplementary file 1). PROSPERO registration ID for the study is CRD420251003425.

PICOS Criteria

The following PICOS terms were used for this systematic review

Population (P): Women diagnosed with PCOS based on any of the recognized diagnostic criteria (eg, Rotterdam, NIH, or Androgen Excess Society criteria).

Intervention (I): The eligible interventions include moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT), high-intensity interval training (HIIT), continuous aerobic training (CAT), intermittent aerobic training (IAT), and continuous aerobic exercise training (CAET).

Comparison (C): Usual care, placebo, sedentary lifestyle, or no structured exercise intervention.

Outcomes (O): Outcome measures include body composition (BMI, weight loss, waist-to-hip ratio, and waist circumference), biochemical markers (lipid profile, triglycerides, LDL, HDL, blood glucose, insulin resistance), or hormonal profiles (testosterone, estrogen, LH, FSH, AMH, SHBG).

Study design (S): Randomized controlled trial (RCT).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria for Studies

The eligibility criteria for the selection of studies for inclusion in this systematic review are formulated as per the PIOS approach.

Inclusion criteria: RCTs that examined the effects of structured aerobic exercise interventions in PCOS women with at least one relevant primary outcome. Further, studies must feature supervised or structured aerobic exercise programs conducted for a minimum duration of 4 weeks, with clear details on frequency, intensity, duration, and type of exercise. Only studies written in the English language will be included.

Exclusion criteria: Non-randomized studies, review articles, case reports, conference abstracts, editorials, and letters to the editor were excluded. Studies without a comparator group or those that failed to report relevant anthropometric or biochemical outcomes were excluded. Trials involving participants with co-morbid conditions such as metabolic syndromes unrelated to PCOS, endocrine disorders, or other systemic illnesses that could confound the results were also excluded. Animal studies and in vitro research will be excluded. Studies with non-aerobic exercise interventions, such as yoga, Pilates, or stretching exercises without aerobic components will also be excluded. Additionally, studies lacking a well-defined exercise protocol; those focusing on pharmacological or dietary interventions without aerobic exercise as the primary focus, and studies conducted less than 4 weeks, will be excluded from the review.

Data Sources and Search Strategy

Articles were searched in four databases including MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science on 28.2.2025. The search strategy was composed of a combination of keywords and their corresponding Mesh terms (Supplementary File 2). An English language filter was applied while conducting the search.

Study Selection and Screening of Articles

The studies retrieved from all the databases were pooled and managed using blinded screening software RAYYAN. Duplicate studies were removed manually by one author based on similarities in title, author, journal, year of publication, etc. Primary screening of title and abstracts was done by two authors independently and any discrepancies were resolved either by mutual discussion or consulting with a third author to ensure consistency and accuracy. Similar process was followed in secondary full‑text screening. The level of agreement between two authors was 0.88 for primary and 0.94 for secondary screening (calculated through Cohen’s kappa).

Data Extraction

Data extraction was performed by two authors; each author did a pilot test of the data extraction tool independently, before its final implementation. The data extraction sheet included study details such as author, publication year, study design, sample size, participant characteristics (age, PCOS diagnostic criteria), intervention details (exercise type, intensity, frequency, duration), comparator details, outcome measures, and main results. A detailed data extraction sheet was prepared for the studies included in meta-analysis that consists of pre‑ and post‑intervention results for each outcome in both the control and experimental group (Supplementary file 3).

Risk of Bias (Quality) Assessment

The risk of bias of the included studies was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias version 2.0 (RoB 2) tool for RCTs.22 This tool analyzed each RCT in five domains: “bias in the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results”. Each domain is comprised of specific set of questions with responses categorized as “yes”, “no”, “probably yes”, “probably no”, and “not applicable”. Based on adherence to standard quality criteria, each RCT was assigned as low, unclear or high risk.

Effect Measure and Statistical Analysis

The meta-analysis was performed for parameter for which sufficient data was available. Mean differences (MD), standard deviation (SD), and odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals will be used for estimating the effect size. For each outcome, the MD was first calculated within groups (post-intervention value – pre‑intervention value) followed by between-group comparisons (intervention vs control). Here, the correlation coefficient (r) was estimated using the mean and standard deviation values of the groups, with calculations performed using the Campbell Collaboration effect size calculator.23

All the calculations used in quantitative synthesis are presented in Supplementary File S2. Considering the methodological heterogeneity of the studies, a random-effects model was applied while performing meta-analysis.

Certainty of Evidence

Certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE framework (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation), using GRADE pro GDT software.24

Results

Study Selection

A total of 895 studies were retrieved from four database searches, out of which 87 were duplicates. Twenty-five studies were included in the review for qualitative analysis. Based on the comparable similar interventions and outcomes, five studies were included in quantitative evidence synthesis. The process of study selection is shown as the PRISMA flow chart (Figure 1).

PRISMA flowchart of study selection.

Characteristics of Studies

Our systematic review included a total of 25 experimental studies conducted between 2010 and 2025, despite no restrictions on publication years. However, 70% studies were conducted since 2020 only. All studies have recruited women diagnosed with PCOS by Rotterdam criteria. We have included only RCTs as study design. Out of these RCTs, maximum were 2 arm studies. However, 5 studies were three-arm trial, 3 were pilot studies, 2 were secondary analysis of RCT and 1 was cross over study. Maximum (n=7) studies were conducted in Brazil followed by Australia (n=5) and Iran (n=4). Rest trials are from Canada, the USA, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Italy, India, and Pakistan with only 1 or 2 trials. Sample size ranges from 17 to 150. However, there was only 1 study which reached the sample size of 150; all other studies have sample size less than 100. Patients were followed up for 2 to 6 months after intervention, where only 3 studies followed up till 6 months, rest have done less than 4 months. Various types of exercise interventions were used in these included RCTs. Major types of exercise interventions examined were HIIT, MICT, CAT, IAT, CAET, and combined resistance and endurance training (COM). However, all the studies were so variable in terms of type and comparator that only five studies were further included in quantitative analysis. Anthropometric Parameters

The pooled analysis was performed for across three key anthropometric measures: body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) to assess the efficacy of HIIT (Figure 2). For BMI, five randomized controlled trials (n = 172) were included. The overall pooled mean difference was −0.36 kg/m2 (95% CI: −1.49 to 0.76; p = 0.527) with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 39.4%), favoring the HIIT group, although the result did not show statistically significant effect. The pooled mean difference of WC from three studies also showed non-significant reduction in the HIIT group. However, WHR showed a modest but statistically significant decrease in the HIIT group (MD = −0.02; 95% CI: −0.05 to 0.00; P = 0.0451), with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) among the studies. This minimum variation and consistent trend in WHR suggest a potential favorable alteration in regional body fat distribution by HIIT.

Forest plot for anthropometric outcomes in PCOS women after HIIT intervention.

Biochemical Lipid Profile Parameters

All the five RCTs have examined the biochemical lipid parameters including total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels (Figure 3). Only LDL level showed a significant reduction in HIIT group (MD = −0.26 mmol/L; 95% CI: −0.35 to −0.16; P < 0.0001), and with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) among the studies. The prediction interval for LDL (−0.39 to −0.12) further supports favorable results for HIIT group. Total Cholesterol and HDL level also showed changes (MD = −0.14 mmol/L; 95% CI: −0.29 to 0.00; and MD = 0.06 mmol/L; 95% CI: 0.00 to 0.12, respectively), but statistically non-significant (p = 0.0547 and p = 0.0638, respectively). Triglyceride levels remain largely unaffected consistently in all the studies as well as in pooled analysis (I2 = 14.0%). All together, these findings suggest HIIT’s potential to reduce atherogenic lipid fractions, especially LDL, while its effect on other lipid parameters remains modest and needs further exploration.

Forest plot for lipid profile parameters in PCOS women after HIIT intervention.

Glucose-Based Metabolic Parameters

Pooled analysis of fasting glycaemia, fasting insulin, and the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) was performed to assess the effect of HIIT on glucose metabolism in PCOS women (Figure 4). Fasting glycaemia did not change significantly between the intervention and control group (MD = 0.12 mmol/L; 95% CI: −0.08 to 0.33; p = 0.2403), with considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 86.9%), majorly driven by one outlier RCT. Sensitivity analysis was done by excluding the outlier study that reduced the heterogeneity to 0%, but still no significant change was observed (MD of 0.01 mmol/L, 95% CI: −0.11 to 0.14; p = 0.8416).

Forest plot for glucose and insulin parameters in PCOS women after HIIT intervention.

Interestingly, HIIT exhibited statistically significant reduction in fasting insulin (MD = −2.44 μIU/mL; 95% CI: −3.43 to −1.45; P < 0.0001) and HOMA-IR (MD = −0.49; 95% CI: −0.67 to −0.30; p< 0.0001), both with zero heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). These results highlight that HIIT may not directly reduce fasting glucose, but it significantly regulates insulin dynamics, highlighting its potential in managing metabolic outcomes.

Hormonal Parameters

Out of five studies using HIIT as exercise-based interventions, only 2 studies have examined the hormonal parameter (SHBG and testosterone); therefore this outcome was not synthesized quantitatively. Almenning et al (2015) reported significant reduction in SHBG but no change in testosterone levels. Kiel et al (2021) surprisingly reported non-significant increase in testosterone and SHBG in the intervention group. As mentioned in Table 1, studies involving other aerobic exercise interventions also reported significant improvement in hormonal parameters such as testosterone, SHBG, LH, FSH, and DHEAS.18,20,25,29–33,36,43,45

The risk of bias was performed using the Cochrane RoB2 tool for RCTs and achieved 0.89 Kappa score indicating high reliability. Risk of bias for all the included studies and overall summary is represented in Figure 5. As evident from summary, 45% of the studies showed low risk of bias and 13% studies exhibited high risk of bias. Grade Analysis

In this study assessing the impact of high‑intensity interval training (HIIT) compared to placebo exercise, a GRADE evidence synthesis was conducted across multiple outcome domains including anthropometric, lipid, and metabolic parameters. The findings, derived from randomized controlled trials, consistently demonstrated high-certainty evidence supporting the beneficial role of HIIT, yet with few explanations under the domain risk of bias in few parameters (Table 3). Beneficial effects were observed in parameters such as waist circumference, waist–hip ratio, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and indicators of insulin sensitivity including fasting insulin and HOMA-IR. While BMI showed a mild increase post-HIIT, waist circumference and waist–hip ratio demonstrated a tendency toward reduction. Lipid profile outcomes showed positive results with HIIT significantly lowering total cholesterol and LDL, although effects on triglycerides and HDL were minimal or unclear. The glycemic response was generally neutral in terms of fasting glucose, but improvements in insulin sensitivity were significant.

The GRADE assessment showed high confidence in all outcomes, this demonstrates consistency, precision, and adequate methodological rigor across studies. Even certain studies contributing to the pooled analysis presented some concerns, such as issues with randomization or selective reporting; these were not sufficient to downgrade the certainty of evidence. HIIT is a powerful non-pharmacologic intervention for improving cardio-metabolic health, as evidenced by the overall direction of effect favoring it across most health markers. The result demonstrated that HIIT may be incorporated into evidence-based health promotions as they can exert meaningful physiological changes even when compared to low-intensity placebo exercise. This synthesis provides robust support for incorporating HIIT protocols in interventions aimed at improving metabolic, cardiovascular, and anthropometric health profiles.

Discussion

The present systematic review and meta-analysis provide comprehensive evidence on the impact of structure aerobic exercise interventions, particularly high-intensity interval training (HIIT), on anthropometric, metabolic, and hormonal parameters on women diagnosed with PCOS. Our pooled findings indicate that HIIT significantly improves WHR, LDL, cholesterol, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR. In contrast, no statistically significant effects were observed for BMI, WC, fasting glucose, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, or most hormonal markers. In line with the increasing focus on lifestyle interventions by recent PCOS guidelines, findings from our study reinforce the therapeutic potential of aerobic exercise against the metabolic derangement that is at the core of PCOS pathogenesis. The regular exercise regime performed by the women mostly included different forms of aerobic exercise of different intensities (moderate to vigorous). Others were some types of resistance training treatment or a combined resistance and aerobic intervention.

Many of the studies included were randomized controlled trials, with few conducted as crossover RCTs or multi-arm trials.26 Most interventions involved supervised or structured exercise programs (eg, aerobic, interval, or resistance training), dietary modifications (eg, food-based approach shown in Table 1) and/or a combined lifestyle approach; controls typically included no intervention, standard care, or placebo supplements. In the studies, most exercising was done 3 times per week at 30–50 minutes per session, and available on a range of 16 weeks to 6 months. Assessment included body composition (BMI, waist–hip ratio), biochemical parameters (HOMA-IR, fasting glucose, lipid profile) and hormonal markers (testosterone, SHBG, LH/FSH ration). In meta-analysis results the high heterogeneity was observed in fasting glycaemia (I2 = 86.9%) despite exclusive inclusion of HIIT protocols due to variations in exercise protocols, including differences in intensity, duration, and total intervention period across studies. Most trials have shown positive changes in anthropometric and metabolic parameters as well as improved hormone levels; however, data regarding cycle regularity and hyper-androgenism is conflicting. There are advantages of doing structured exercise and lifestyle modification in the management of PCOS symptoms and health risks.

In the present study, particularly high-intensity interval training (HIIT) interventions were eligible for meta‑analysis. In these studies, HIIT exercise was done 30 to 60 minutes per day, thrice a week. However, the total duration of intervention varies between 2 and 6 months; only Kiel et al (2021) followed up the patient for 6 months post 4 months intervention.35 Findings have shown that HIIT intervention did not significantly alter BMI or waist circumference across the included studies, it produced a modest yet statistically significant reduction in waist-to-hip ratio. This implies a beneficial redistribution of body fat, which could have positive effects on metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes in PCOS women. Another systematic review suggested that regular exercise might reduce waist circumference and improve cardiorespiratory fitness in PCOS women.47 A network meta-analysis demonstrated that a combination of nutritional supplement and low-calorie diet, continuous aerobic exercise, and Taichi (a Chinese meditation practice) is the most optimal intervention strategy to improve anthropometric parameters (body weight, BMI, WC and WHR) in PCOS women.48 Our findings are consistent with earlier meta-analyses reporting improvements in insulin resistance following structured exercise interventions in women with PCOS. However, unlike some previous reviews that suggested modest reductions in BMI, our pooled analysis did not demonstrate significant BMI change, possibly due to shorter intervention durations and smaller sample sizes.

The meta-analysis results from lipid profiles, demonstrated a significant reduction in LDL cholesterol following HIIT interventions, with a borderline effect on total cholesterol. Further non-significant changes were observed in other lipid measures, such as HDL and triglycerides, indicating the need for additional long-term studies to conclusively demonstrate HIIT’s wider lipid-modulating capacity. The evidence regarding hormonal parameters remains inconclusive. Variability in baseline androgen levels, small sample sizes, differences in assay methods, and short intervention durations may explain these discrepancies. Hormonal outcomes are also affected by the timing of the menstrual cycle and other lifestyle factors that were not always uniform in all studies.

Changes in the follicular microenvironment are linked to reproductive problems in PCOS. A systematic review conducted by Moreira MV et al (2023) showed changes in follicular fluid composition in women with PCOS undergoing IVF. These included increased oxidative stress, inflammation, and altered growth factors which can further negatively affect oocyte quality and ART outcomes. Nonetheless, the majority of RCTs in our review did not evaluate ovulation, menstrual regularity, or ART outcomes.49

The findings from this study provide strong evidence for the beneficial effects of HIIT on insulin resistance markers. Significant and consistent improvements with little variation were seen in both fasting insulin and HOMA-IR, demonstrating HIIT as a strong non-pharmacological alternative for improving insulin dynamics in PCOS women. Physical activities like resistance training and aerobic activity improve glucose absorption during muscular contraction, which increases insulin sensitivity.50 A meta-analysis (19 RCTs, 777 subjects) also revealed that exercise might improve body composition, insulin resistance, and cardiorespiratory parameters. The improvement is more dependent on intensity of exercise as compared to duration.51 In line with our study, another meta-analysis (7 RCTs, 423 subjects) also stated that HIIT intervention may improve BMI and HOMA-IR in PCOS women.52 A recent network meta-analysis revealed that combination of pharmacological interventions with planned diet and exercise is the most effective strategy for overweight and obese PCOS who wish to conceive.53

This systematic review indicates that exercise, particularly HIIT, might improve PCOS‑related metabolic parameters such as lipid profile and insulin resistance. However, significant evidence gaps are present that limit their integration into clinical practice. Therefore, there is a need for high-quality, large-scale, long-term studies to optimize the exercise regime and strategies for the effective management of PCOS.

Limitations of Study

This review has several limitations. First, the smaller number of studies and participants included in the meta-analysis limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, the heterogeneity in exercise protocols (exercise intensity, volume, and type) may have influenced pooled results. Thirdly, the short intervention durations limit insights into long-term outcomes, which are highly relevant for a chronic condition like PCOS. The limited reporting of hormonal outcomes further restricts interpretation of exercise effects on hyperandrogenism. Finally, due to the small number of studies publication bias could not be evaluated, thus pooled results might suggest cautious interpretation.

Future Perspectives

Future randomized controlled trials on exercise in PCOS should be conducted with adequately large sample size, in multi-centric mode to generate more reliable clinical evidence. Trials with longer follow-ups are also necessary to assess the long-term benefits and sustainability of interventions. Further, there is a need to develop and validate a standardized protocol for each kind of exercise, their session duration, frequency, and optimum intervention length. Further, the evidence regarding hormonal outcomes is still limited because only a few RCTs assessing parameters like testosterone and SHBG. Therefore, conclusions about the effect of aerobic exercise on hyperandrogenism remain inconclusive.

Clinical Implications

The present findings support the incorporation of structured HIIT protocols into routine management of women with PCOS. A prescription of thrice‑weekly sessions lasting 30–50 minutes over a minimum of 12 weeks appears sufficient to yield clinically relevant improvements in insulin sensitivity and LDL cholesterol, even in the absence of significant weight reduction. Clinicians should emphasize these metabolic benefits when counseling patients, as they represent meaningful reductions in long‑term cardiometabolic risk. Current PCOS guidelines highlight lifestyle modification as first‑line therapy; our synthesis strengthens this recommendation by providing high‑certainty evidence for HIIT as a practical, non‑pharmacological intervention.7 While hormonal outcomes remain inconclusive, the consistent improvements in insulin dynamics and lipid fractions justify prioritizing HIIT in clinical practice, with the understanding that weight loss may not be the primary marker of success.

Conclusion

These results emphasize the significance of structured aerobic exercise as an adjunctive treatment modality in the management of PCOS. Our meta-analysis revealed that HIIT may improve WHR, LDL level, insulin level, and HOMA-IR in PCOS women. However, for other outcomes, evidence remained inconclusive. To better understand the sustainability of exercise-induced benefits in PCOS, future research should prioritize high-quality, large-scale RCTs with standardized outcome reporting and extended follow-up periods.
