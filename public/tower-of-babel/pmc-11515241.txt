A study on the effect of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) to promote bone fusion in lateral interbody fusion of the lumbar spine using artificial bone
Authors: Hiroshi Noguchi, Toru Funayama, Kosuke Sato, Masao Koda, Hiroshi Takahashi, Kousei Miura, Hiroshi Kumagai, Masashi Yamazaki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF) via a retroperitoneum approach has gained popularity due to minimal invasiveness, which avoids resection of the spinous process and laminae. However, as challenges in grafting autogenous bone persist, artificial bone has been tested in Japan to fill the spinal cage. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) contains growth factors and anti-inflammatory cytokines to promote cellular proliferation and repair damaged tissues. While the effects of PRP on tendon and ligament repair are widely known, any effects on bone healing are scarcely reported. However, PRP-loaded artificial bone carries potential to improve intervertebral bone fusion.ObjectiveThis study assessed whether PRP enhances intervertebral bone fusion in LLIF surgery using β-tricalcium phosphate artificial bone.MethodsThe current study was a prospective, randomized, controlled trial. We evaluated 13 consecutive patients undergoing LLIF surgery in our hospital. Patients received artificial bone impregnated with PRP or without PRP within the same fusion cage. The primary outcome was the intervertebral bone fusion rate at 6 and 12 months postoperatively, evaluated using CT imaging. The intervertebral bone fusion rates with and without PRP loading and with and without contact part between the endplate and the artificial bone were compared. Secondary outcomes included clinical evaluations using visual analog scale scores for low back pain, buttock-leg pain, and leg numbness from the Japanese Orthopaedic Association Back Pain Evaluation Questionnaire (JOA-BPEQ) and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), plus adverse events information.ResultsOf the 13 patients (29 vertebral segments) included, bone fusion was observed in 43.4% of the PRP group and 26.1% of the non-PRP group at 6 months (p = 0.216). At 12 months, fusion rates were 60.9% with PRP and 34.8% without PRP (p = 0.074). The fusion rate was significantly higher in cases with good contact between the vertebral endplate and the artificial bone (p = 0.0004). Clinical scores improved postoperatively. Adverse events were in accordance with expectations from LLIF surgery and no PRP-specific events occurred.ConclusionPRP did not significantly improve intervertebral bone fusion rates in LLIF surgeries, particularly in cases with poor contact between the vertebral endplate and artificial bone. While PRP may have a limited role in enhancing bone fusion, maintaining good contact between the vertebral endplate and artificial bone is crucial for successful outcomes. Further research is needed to explore optimal uses of PRP in spinal fusion surgeries.Keywords: Platelet-rich plasma, Lateral lumbar interbody fusion, Artificial bone

Introduction

Spinal fusion surgery is a commonly performed procedure to treat spinal degenerative disease, spinal deformities, and/or spinal fractures. In the past, autogenous bones (e.g., iliac bone) would often be grafted between vertebrae [1] but, currently, metal or poly ether ether ketone (PEEK) spinal cages are the mainstream. For posterior lumbar interbody fusion, the spinous process and laminae removed during surgical approach are also usually grafted into the window of the cage or the surrounding area [2].

Recently, lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF) by retroperitoneum approach is rapidly increasing in popularity due to its minimal invasiveness [3, 4]. LLIF is characterized by the placement of a spinal cage with a large footprint at the edge of the mechanically solid vertebral endplate without resection of the spinous process or laminae, facilitating adult spinal deformity corrective surgery with reduced complications [5].

The LLIF technique allows access to the intervertebral space without resection of the posterior elements of the spine, the spinous process, and the laminae. However, this advantage presents a new challenge in that it prevents the harvesting of autogenous bone that would have previously been grafted into the hollow space of the spinal cage. Although autogenous bone can be harvested from outside the surgical field (e.g., the iliac bone), persistent concerns about adverse events, such as bone pain at the extraction site, are amplified along with requiring significant extra time and effort [4, 5]. Therefore, in Japan, where the use of allogeneic bone is uncommon, artificial bone has been tasked to fill the spinal cage. According to diverse reports, while artificial bone can achieve fusion comparable to autogenous bone, alone, it is disadvantageous for interbody fusion [4–8].

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is a blood plasma product containing concentrated autologous platelets with several growth factors, such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and insulin-like growth factor (IGF) [9, 10]. Multiple studies and clinical applications have been conducted on the effects of these growth factors in promoting cellular proliferation and repairing damaged tissues and, in the orthopedic field, PRP has been shown effective in repairing tendons and cartilage. The few extant studies on bone healing, both animal studies and clinical reports, have also shown potential utility for PRP in improved bone healing [11, 12]. There is a small amount of literature on the use of PRP for lumbar interbody fusion, and there is currently no literature on the use of PRP for LLIF surgery. In addition, there are no publications examining the efficacy of PRP for lumbar interbody fusion with artificial bone.

Here, we conducted a controlled, exploratory clinical trial featuring autologous PRP as an add-on treatment for the artificial bone in the spinal cage placed between the vertebrae in order to improve intervertebral bone fusion. We thus aimed to verify intervertebral bone fusion rate outcomes for LLIF surgery using β-tricalcium phosphate (TCP) artificial bone treated with PRP.

Methods

The study was designed as a prospective comparative superiority trial with randomly assigned groups. This trial was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Tsukuba Hospital (TRM2019-05), and consent for participation in the trial was obtained from all patients. This study was registered in the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCTb032200199). The details of the research protocol for this study are published separately [13].

Patients

Patients with diseases such as degenerative kyphoscoliosis, lumbar spinal stenosis, or lumbar spondylolisthesis who were scheduled for LLIF at our hospital were included. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Table 1, and a target sample size of 15 patients (40 intervertebral levels) was set as the number of patients to be included. Based on prior studies [4, 14], LLIF with β-TCP artificial bone has a fusion rate of 70.9%, and autologous iliac crest bone has a rate of 94.5%. A sample size of 40 bone graft spaces per group was calculated with 80% power and a 5% alpha (two-tailed). Therefore, patients will undergo LLIF at 1 to 4 intervertebral levels, involving over 40 levels and at least 80 bone graft spaces for evaluating fusion.

Surgery procedures

We performed either one- or two-stage LLIF surgery for each enrolled patient. In both cases, we used a retroperitoneal approach in the lateral decubitus position to insert the cage between the vertebral bodies, followed by posterior fixation (PF) with instrumentation. Decisions on performing the anterior and posterior surgeries in one- or two- stage were made by each surgeon. In cases with bony spinal canal stenosis, posterior decompression procedures were performed as needed, and in cases requiring correction of kyphosis deformity, Ponte osteotomy and posterior shortening procedures were added as needed to obtain lumbar lordosis. No provisions regarding the screws and rods for PF were set or mandated.

We used CoRoent XL® PEEK cages (NuVasive, San Diego, CA, USA) as intervertebral fusion cages for the LLIF. Since this cage has two windows (left and right) for grafting bone fills, one side was filled with Affinos® (Kuraray Co., Tokyo, Japan) [4, 15, 16], impregnated with PRP, and the other with Affinos® only (Fig. 1). Therefore, two artificial bone blocks could be grafted at each intervertebral level. The sides of the bone grafts with PRP were alternately set in order of case enrollment and intraoperative insertion. In this way, interventions (bone graft with PRP) and controls (bone graft without PRP) were assigned randomly.

Bone grafts in two windows of the intervertebral cage. For each cage, one space was filled with Affinos® β-tricalcium phosphate artificial bone with PRP and the other was filled with Affinos® without PRP. Affinos® can be easily impregnated with PRP quickly by capillary action

Affinos® is a β-TCP artificial bone with a porosity of 57 ± 5%, consisting of a unidirectional porous structure, in which intercommunicating holes of 30–250 μm are arranged in one direction [4, 15, 16]. This specific structure maintains strength against compressive stresses along the direction of the unidirectional porous structure and can be easily and rapidly impregnated with liquids, such as PRP, by capillary action.

Autologous PRP was prepared using a GPS® III kit (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA). On the day of surgery, a total of 52 mL of peripheral blood was preoperatively drawn from each patient, then blood and anticoagulant were centrifuged for 15 min at 3200 rpm. After elimination of platelet-poor plasma, PRP was obtained by extracting the buffy coat layer at the clean bench of our university’s cell processing facility. These operations produced approximately 6 ml of PRP per patient. Based on the void volume of the spinal cage and the porosity of the artificial bone, approximately 1 ml of PRP per vertebral segment was calculated. Therefore, for LLIF surgery, the amount of obtained PRP was sufficient for up to 1–4 intervertebral levels. The platelet concentration rate of PRP was defined as PRP platelet count divided by peripheral blood platelet count.

Since this study was exploratory, strict withdrawal of osteoporosis drugs (such as bisphosphonates, parathyroid hormone agents, or steroids) which may affect intervertebral bone fusion was not conducted and preoperative treatment was continued.

Outcome measures

Radiological evaluation

The intervertebral bone fusion rates at 6 and 12 months postoperatively were evaluated by three spine surgeons using CT multi-planar reconstruction coronal and sagittal images [4]. “Intervertebral bone fusion” was defined as continuity between the artificial bone in the cage and the upper and lower vertebral endplates (Fig. 2). We evaluated each artificial bone window (left and right) for intervertebral bone fusion and these rates were compared between the PRP-treated and control windows.

Representative reconstructed CT images (coronal and sagittal sections) of intervertebral fusion that were obtained immediately after surgery, 6 months postoperatively, and 12 months postoperatively. The central CT coronal section image shows the left and right windows of the LLIF cage. The left and right CT sagittal images show slices at the center of the prosthesis within the left and right windows of the LLIF cage. (A) L3/4 intervertebral in Case 2 (80-year-old male with lumbar spondylolisthesis). β-TCP with PRP that were well grounded to the endplate resulted in bony fusion, whereas β-TCP without PRP that were poorly grounded did not fuse. (B) Intervertebral L3/4 in Case 7 (74-year-old male with adult spinal deformity & lumbar spinal canal stenosis). β-TCP with PRP that were poorly grounded to the endplate did not achieve bony fusion

In addition, the intervertebral bone fusion at 12 months due to the contact condition of the vertebral endplate and the artificial bone immediately after surgery was compared. Contact condition between Affinos® and both upper and lower vertebral endplates was evaluated by CT sagittal reconstruction image immediately after surgery; if the cage was in contact with either end plate this was regarded as “good contact”. In contrast, any gaps between the Affinos® and vertebral endplates were defined as “poor contact”.

Statistical analysis of these bone fusion rates was performed using the Pearson χ-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Adverse events & clinical outcomes

Considering the limited number of previous reports on the use of PRP for spine surgery, information was also collected on adverse events that occurred during the clinical study, such as death, neurological disorders, and postoperative infections, etc. Special vigilance was conducted on adverse events that might prolong the hospital stay or precipitate additional surgeries. According to the postoperative course and imaging findings, we also examined the causal relationship with the use of PRP.

Clinical evaluations were also performed using visual analog scale scores (VAS) for low back pain, buttock-leg pain, and leg numbness from of the Japanese Orthopaedic Association Back Pain Evaluation Questionnaire (JOA-BPEQ), plus the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), to determine if the surgery was effective.

Results

Patients from February 2020 to the end of August 2022 were included in the study. In Case 3, a death due to pulmonary embolism occurred after revision of a postoperative implant infection. The study was suspended for approximately 4 months from December 2020 to March 2021 while the Ethics Committee discussed a possible causal relationship between the encountered pulmonary embolism and PRP use. After revising the consent form regarding patient inclusion criteria and the provision of appropriate information regarding surgical adverse events, the study was resumed and the patient enrollment and study duration were extended by 3 months. To avoid excessive overstretching of the study period, enrollment was finally terminated after 13 patients.

Demographic information on all enrolled patients is summarized in Table 2. LLIF was performed in all 13 cases and all 29 intervertebral spaces, with Affinos® alternating loading (with/without PRP) conducted in the order of cage placement. Case 3 died in the middle of the follow-up period, and Case 13 was lost to follow-up due to early postoperative reoperation, which prevented CT imaging at 6 months postoperatively. Therefore, excluding these two cases, a total of 23 intervertebral spaces in 11 cases were included in the radiological analysis of this study. Adverse events were investigated in all 13 cases included in the study.

Radiological evaluation

・Intervertebral bone fusion rate with and without PRP (Fig. 3).

Intervertebral bone fusion rate with and without PRP

In a total of 11 cases with 23 vertebral interbody cages (23 with/23 without PRP), bone fusion was observed in 10 patients (43.4%) in the group with PRP and 6 patients (26.1%) in the group without PRP at 6 months postoperatively (p = 0.216). At 12 months postoperatively, 14 patients (60.9%) in the group with PRP and 8 patients (34.8%) in the group without PRP showed bone fusion (p = 0.074). Although just over the significance threshold, the study surgeons felt that PRP gave a better chance of fusion at 12 months postoperatively than without.

・Intervertebral bone fusion rate according to contact condition immediately after surgery (Fig. 4).

Intervertebral bone fusion rate with/without PRP according to the contact condition immediately after surgery

Interbody fusion at 12 months postoperatively was observed in 16 of 21 graft windows with good contact and 6 of 25 graft windows with poor contact, with a significantly higher fusion rate in the good contact group (p = 0.0004). Furthermore, the independence between contact condition and the application of PRP was maintained (Pearson’s chi-square test P = 0.138).

In addition, the intervertebral bone fusion rate with and without PRP was also investigated under the same contact condition. In the good contact cases, 11/13 graft windows with PRP and 5/8 graft windows without PRP showed interbody fusion while, in the poor contact cases, 3/10 graft windows with PRP and 3/15 graft windows without PRP showed interbody fusion. In the good contact cases, the bone fusion rate was higher with PRP (but not significantly so) and, in the poor contact cases, the bone fusion rate did not increase with PRP.

・Adverse events & clinical outcomes.

Adverse events occurred as shown in Table 3, with 8 of the 13 patients (61.5%) experiencing an adverse event. The most serious adverse event was death (Case 3) due to pulmonary embolism as described above. Other adverse events included nerve root damage due to endplate fracture, anterior longitudinal ligament injury, osteophyte fracture associated with the LLIF cage insertion, iatrogenic intervertebral foramen stenosis associated with corrective manipulation, and rod fracture after long spinal fusion surgery. These adverse events were reported previously as expected from LLIF/long-range corrective fixation surgery and were determined to be unrelated to the PRP carriage.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the enhancement of bone formation after LLIF using artificial bone with PRP. Although the number of intervertebral bone fusions was slightly higher in the group using PRP, the rate at 6 and 12 months postoperatively was not statistically different between the two groups. We observed that the intervertebral bone fusion rate at 12 months postoperatively was highly dependent on immediate postoperative contact between the endplate and the artificial bone. Thus, if a gap was observed between the endplate and the artificial bone immediately after surgery, the bone fusion rate did not improve with PRP loading.

This study confirms that the enhancement of bone formation by PRP in LLIF is limited, i.e., that PRP is not expected to overcome a lack of cross-bridging from the vertebral body endplate to the artificial bone, because unlike bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), PRP has no osteogenic effect that promotes ectopic ossification. There are only a few reports on intervertebral fusion surgery using PRP and artificial bone in animal studies, coupled with a few reports on clinical studies in posterior lumbar fusion (PLF); this is the first report of its kind that we are aware of. We believe that the standardized inclusion criteria and surgical procedures strengthen the validity and applicability of our results, even if the sample size was too small to ensure statistical power. In general, our results were generally consistent with clinical opinions regarding osteogenic enhancement by PRP.

The number of previous studies using PRP in intervertebral fusion surgery was limited to approximately 30 in the last 10 years, and the efficacy of PRP in combination with intervertebral fusion surgery remains controversial and unresolved. One limitation of research on PRP as a treatment for musculoskeletal disorders is that the concentration and activity of growth factors in PRP are known to be altered depending on endogenous influences, including demographic factors (e.g., age), immune status, metabolic diseases, and concomitant medications (e.g., osteoporosis drugs and analgesics), as well as exogenous factors such as differences in the method of PRP preparation. In other words, the efficacy of PRP is biologically specific to each individual, resulting in variability in study results [9, 17–20]. Three recently published systematic reviews/meta-analyses have shown either decreased or unchanged bone fusion rates when PRP (especially PRP with low enrichment) is used in combination with intervertebral fusion [21–23]. It is unclear whether these results are directly applicable, given the differences in the literature, in addition to the fact that almost all studies were performed with autologous or allogeneic bone (not artificial bone) and that porsterior intervertebral body fusion and TLIF procedures (very different from LLIF) were performed.

We experienced a lower intervertebral bone fusion rate in our current results versus our previous study performed with the same artificial bone. However, the mechanism of action by which PRP could be responsible for the lower bone fusion remains unknown. In corrective fusion surgery, the facet joints are resected and corrected to match the lordotic rod with an awareness of lumbar lordosis acquisition. As a result, the intervertebral space opened up and increased poor contact between the vertebral endplate and artificial bone was assumed to have decreased the rate of interbody fusion. In addition, the inclusion of patients with underlying diseases, such as SLE and RA, may be a contributing factor. As mentioned above, it is suspected that in RA and SLE patients, poor bone formation due to oral steroids and decreased platelet activity due to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs may have had a negative effect on the intervertebral bone formation. Further, the relationship between PRP enrichment rate and bone healing was also confirmed, but no correlation was found between enrichment rate and bone healing in this study.

In one of the three previously noted systematic reviews/meta-analyses, the promotion of intervertebral bone fusion by PRP was mentioned [23]. Similarly, an intermediate analysis of Cases 1–7 of this series found that the HU (Hounsfield Unit) value of the artificial bone was lower with PRP, suggesting that PRP may contribute to β-TCP artificial bone resorption and promote intervertebral bone fusion [24]. Although not statistically significant, the possibility remains that PRP may have some positive effect on bone formation, given that bone fusion rate was higher in the group with PRP in the good contact cases. At this time, the evidence is better for an alternative strategy using BMPs if the surgeon wants to reliably improve the intervertebral bone fusion rate [25, 26].

Based on the results of this study, the safety of PRP in intervertebral fusion surgery is generally acceptable, as in previous reports, but its usefulness remains questionable. Taken together, the current results emphasize the importance of maintaining contact between the vertebral endplate and the artificial bone rather than relying solely on PRP to achieve intervertebral bone fusion. In this study, to improve contact with the endplate, we use a technique in which an artificial bone block is placed so that it protrudes slightly from the cage and is inserted into the intervertebral body using a slider [4]. Based on the results, not all contact situations could be made good with the above technique, but the surgeon should be aware of the need to select a cage with an appropriate lordosis angle and to deliver the size of the artificial bone that hits the vertebral endplate into the intervertebral space. If the intervertebral space opens up due to corrective manipulation of the spinal alignment, resulting in poor contact between the vertebral endplate and the artificial bone, it may be possible to resolve this problem by using a special expandable cage with bone graft paste (e.g., decalcified bone matrix) flowing through the inserter and expanded into implant to completely fill the disc space. If a reliable target and method for PRP, which is inexpensive and easy to prepare, can be found, they will be clinically useful, and further studies are needed. For example, a large prospective trial with more rigorous control of various confounding factors that influence the efficacy of PRP, such as oral medications, medical history, and presence of osteoporosis, should be considered. As the next study design to scientifically clarify the effects of PRP, we will plan to conduct a two-group comparison of patients with and without PRP in cases with proper contact between the artificial bone and the vertebral endplates in the spinal cage, which we speculate may provide new insights.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the number of patients included was small. The statistically necessary sample size at planning time was n = 40 per group, which may have prevented finding significant differences in this study. Second, we could not assign patients to PRP groups without considering the contact condition of the vertebral endplate and artificial bone. As a result, while the independence between contact condition and PRP was maintained, the presence of PRP may have affected the results as there were slightly more cases of good contact with PRP. Third, several confounding factors, such as osteoporosis medication, may have affected intervertebral bone fusion. This study was conducted in an exploratory fashion and did not regulate anabolic agent such as recombinant human parathyroid hormone. Fourth, the relationship between intervertebral bone fusion and clinical outcomes remains unclear. In the present study design, each patient had both conditions (PRP, non-PRP) and it is possible that more significant differences in clinical outcomes could be found by assigning each patient to receive either PRP or control and not both in the same cage. In addition, although the PRP preparation methods were standardized, there were unavoidable variations in concentration rates and growth factor concentrations due to the effects of age, underlying conditions, and concomitant medications in each patient, which may have affected the reproducibility of the results.

Conclusion

We performed an exploratory study on the effect of PRP on promoting intervertebral bone fusion by β-TCP artificial bone impregnating with PRP and filling the spinal cage with it for LLIF. The intervertebral bone fusion rate at 12 months postoperatively was 47.8% in all patients (60.9% in the group with PRP and 34.8% in the group without PRP). There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups and the effect of PRP on enhancing bone fusion appeared limited. In addition, PRP was not capable of overcoming poor ground contact between the vertebral endplate and the artificial bone.
