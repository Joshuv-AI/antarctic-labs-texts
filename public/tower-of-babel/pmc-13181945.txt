The role of Spexin and Phoenixin-20 in girls during puberty
Authors: Serkan Bilge Koca, Tarık Kırkgöz, Leyla Kara, Ebru Gök, Derya Koçer, Hüseyin Demirbilek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpexin has an inhibitory effect on the hypothalamus (GnRH expression), pituitary (LH and FSH expression) and ovary (estradiol expression). However, Phoenixin potentiate GnRH-stimulated GnRH receptor expression. Our aim is to assess the role of these two functionally opposite peptides in girls with Central Precocious Puberty (CPP).MethodsThis prospective study was conducted on three groups of patients (CPP: 33 patients, premature thelarche (PT): 57 patients, and healthy controls: 53 patients). The patients’ anthropometric measurements, pubertal stage, bone age, basal FSH, LH, and estradiol, and peak FSH and LH values obtained from the LH-RH stimulation test were evaluated. The primary aim was to compare Spexin and Phoenixin-20 measurements between groups.ResultsMedian (Q1–Q3) Spexin levels were 502 pg/mL (314–1434) in the CPP group, 530 pg/mL (234–820) in the PT group, and 626 pg/mL (379–1298) in the control group, with no statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.177). Median (Q1–Q3) Phoenixin-20 levels were 264 ng/L (206–383) in the CPP group, 252 ng/L (177–383) in the PT group, and 199 ng/L (139–273) in the control group. A statistically significant difference was observed between groups (p = 0.009). Nevertheless, statistical significance between the Phoenixin-20 level of the CPP group and the PT group disappeared after post-hoc analysis. Although Spexin levels tended to be lower and Phoenixin-20 levels higher in the CPP group compared to PT and control groups, the difference was not statistically significant. Correlation analysis demonstrated a weak positive correlation between Phoenixin-20 levels and Tanner stage (r = 0.190, p = 0.023).ConclusionsAlthough patients with CPP had a lower Spexin and higher Phoenixin-20 level compared to PT and controls, their diagnostic performance was found poor in distinguishing the CPP from PT and healthy controls. Therefore, measurement of these peptides did not find as a diagnostic tool for the diagnosis of CPP and its differentiation from PT.Trial registration numberThis study was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07359092). Unique Protocol ID: SPX-PNX-CPP-2024, Last Update: 01/14/2026

Introduction

The observation of secondary sex characteristics (breast development) between the ages of 8 and 13 is considered the appropriate time for puberty, in girls. Precocious puberty (PP) can be grouped as central precocious puberty (CPP), peripheral PP and variants of normal puberty (premature thelarche, premature adrenarche) [1]. Cases in which breast development begins before the age of 8 are evaluated for precocious puberty (PP) or premature thelarche (PT) [2, 3].

Tanner staging of breast development, hormonal investigations (basal or stimulated FSH, LH, and estradiol levels), bone age determination and pelvic ultrasonography (to assess uterine and ovarian size and endometrial thickness) are diagnostic tools to differentiate CPP from PT [2, 3]. Puberty begins with activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis, which is induced primarily by activation of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH)-stimulating neurons resulting in increased GnRH amplitudes and pulses [4].

Phoenixin is an endogenous peptide that is expressed in the ovaries as well as in the central nervous system such as the hypothalamus, pituitary, and spinal cord [5, 6]. Although G-protein coupled receptor 173 (GPR173) has been called the Phoenixin receptor, it is unclear whether there is a single receptor for the biological effects of Phoenixin [7]. Phoenixin has various isoforms, such as Phoenixin-14 and Phoenixin-20. Phoenixin-20 expression is predominant in the hypothalamus, while Phoenixin-14 is predominant in the heart and spinal cord [6]. Phoenixin has been shown to increase GnRH receptor expression in the pituitary gland and to potentiate GnRH-stimulated GnRH receptor expression [8]. In another study Phoenixin has been shown to increase GnRH and GnRH receptor (GnRHR) mRNA expression as well as GnRH secretion [9]. Animal studies suggest that Phoenixin stimulates reproductive functions by targeting neurons within the HPG axis that express both GnRH and Kisspeptin 1 (Kiss1) [9].

Spexin is a peptide from the galanin family, consisting of 14 amino acids, delivered to the bloodstream and acts as an endocrine hormone [10]. Spexin and its receptors, galanin receptor 2 and 3 (GALR2/GALR3), are expressed in various systems, including the central nervous system, endocrine system, digestive system, reproductive system, muscle, epithelium, and adipose tissue [11]. In animal models, Spexin has been shown to have an inhibitory effect on the HPG axis [12].

In the present study, we aimed to evaluate these two peptide hormones (Phoenixin-20 and Spexin) in the CPP and PT groups compared to healthy controls. We hypothesized that Spexin levels would be lower and Phoenixin-20 levels higher in girls with idiopathic CPP who had not yet begun treatment.

Methods

Subjects

This prospective case-control study was conducted among patients admitted to the pediatric endocrinology outpatient clinics at Kayseri City Education and Research Hospital between September 2024 and September 2025. Girls over 4 years of age but younger than 8 years old were included in this study. Among girls with breast development, those with basal LH levels above 1 U/L, or with a peak LH response of 5 U/L or higher during the Luteinizing Hormone-Releasing Hormone (LH-RH) stimulation test, constituted the CPP group (Group 1). Those with peak LH responses stimulated on the LH-RH stimulation test of less than 5 U/L constituted the PT group (Group 2). The control group consisted of age-matched healthy girls who were admitted to the pediatric outpatient clinic for routine health evaluation or minor non-endocrine complaints (Group 3). All the participants from group 3 had a Tanner stage 1 puberty with no signs of puberty, chronic illness, endocrine disorder, or was receiving medications affecting hormonal status. Participants were recruited consecutively, minimizing selection bias. No selective inclusion based on clinical or biochemical characteristics was performed.

Anthropometric measurements (weight, height, and body mass index) were recorded. Age- and sex-specific reference ranges and standard deviation scores (SDS) were calculated.

The PT group was selected from those who had at least 6 months of follow-up with no sign of rapidly progressive puberty. Patients with concomitant endocrinological problems (hypothyroidism, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, etc.), those having medication such as corticosteroids were excluded.

Laboratory measurements and assessments

Blood samples were obtained in the early morning hours after an 8–10 h overnight fasting. Samples collected from the patient and control groups were left in tubes in a vertical position for 30 min for coagulation. They were then centrifuged at + 4 °C for 7 min at 4500 rpm. The sera specimens obtained were aliquoted and placed into a deep freeze at − 80˚C until the day of analysis. Biochemical tests were analysed with Cobas 8000 modular systems (Cobas c702, Cobas®, Roche Diagnostics Mannheim, Germany).

Blood samples for Spexin and Phoenixin-20 were analyzed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using commercial kits. Phoenixin-20 ELISA kit (Sensitivity: 3.62 ng/L, Assay Range: 7-1500 ng/L, BT LAB, Cat. No. E1755Hu, China) and Spexin ELISA kit (Sensitivity: 4.88 pg/mL, Assay Range: 78.13–5000 pg/mL, A.B.T Laboratory Industry, Cat. No. ABT2692Hu, Türkiye) were studied according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentrations of the samples were calculated through calibration curves obtained from study standards with known levels. The regression coefficients of our calibration curve was r = 0.991. Intraassay coefficient of variation (CV) of the assay is < 8%, interassay CV of the assay is < 10% for both parameters.

Serum FSH, LH, and E2 levels were measured by the electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) using automatic analyzers (Cobas 8000 c502-c702, Cobas 6000 c501-e601, and e602, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). The LH-RH stimulation test was performed by collecting venous blood samples for FSH and LH measurement at baseline, 20th, and 40th minutes following intravenous administration of 100 µg/m2 LH-RH. The lowest detectable values ​​for FSH, LH, and E2 were 0.3 IU/L, 0.1 IU/L, and 5 ng/L, respectively.

Bone age was evaluated by two pediatric endocrinologists with at least 10 years of experience in the field. The Greulich-Pyle method was used for bone age assessments.

Ethics

This prospective case-control study was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of Health Sciences University Kayseri City Education and Research Hospital (Approval decision date: 22/10/2024 and number: 225). Written informed consent to participate in the study was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants younger than 16 years of age.

Statistical analysis

A power analysis was performed with G*Power, it was calculated that at least 26 children should be included in each group, considering the effect size as 0.36 (between medium and large according to Cohen’s effect size), critical F: 3.11, alpha as 0.05, and power as 0.80. “As no previous studies exactly matched our population and outcomes at the time of study design, a medium effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.36) was used in G*Power. Using reference [13] Phoenixin-20 values from a 2025 study (group sizes: 58; means ± SD of each groups: 381.11 ± 99.45, 317 ± 91.45, 283.69 ± 82.27 pg/mL, respectively), the calculated effect size is 0.41. A power analysis with three groups, power = 0.95, α = 0.05, and effect size = 0.41 indicated a required total sample size of 96, confirming the adequacy of our initial estimate.”

Data were analyzed using the BM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 24.0. IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistical analyses were calculated as mean, standard deviation (SD), median, first and third quartiles (Q1–Q3), frequency, and percentages (%). Student’s T-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used for pairwise comparisons of groups. The ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis analysis were applied for comparison of three groups. In addition, post-hoc Bonferroni or Tamhane tests were used if indicated. Pearson’s Chi-square analysis was used for comparison of categorical variables. Spearman correlation analyses were applied to evaluate the bivariate relationships between variables. A multivariate linear regression analysis was performed to investigate the independent predictors of Spexin and Phoenixin-20 levels. Body mass index (BMI), age, and Tanner stage were included in the model as independent variables. The p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

No statistically significant correlation was detected between Spexin and Phoenixin-20 (r= -0.108, p = 0.198).

We performed a multivariate linear regression analysis to evaluate the independent association between BMI and Spexin, and between BMI and Phoenixin-20 levels, respectively.

In the first regression model, Spexin (pg/mL) was included as the dependent variable, while BMI, Tanner stage, and age were included as independent variables. The overall model was not statistically significant (R² = 0.047, adjusted R² = 0.023, F = 1.972, p = 0.122). Furthermore, none of the independent variables, including BMI (β = -0.108, p = 0.267), age (β = -0.096, p = 0.291), or Tanner stage (β = -0.101, p = 0.301), showed a statistically significant association with Spexin levels.

In the second regression model, Phoenixin-20 (ng/L) was included as the dependent variable, while BMI, Tanner stage, and age were included as independent variables. The overall model was not statistically significant (R² = 0.020, adjusted R² = 0.005, F = 0.813, p = 0.489). Furthermore, none of the independent variables, including BMI (β = -0.075, p = 0.446), age (β = -0.036, p = 0.700), or Tanner stage (β = 0.152, p = 0.128), showed a statistically significant association with Phoenixin-20 levels.

Discussion

In the present study evaluating Spexin and Phoenixin-20 levels in patients presented with early puberty and healthy controls, both peptides did not exhibit a good diagnostic validity for distinguishing CPP from PT or prepubertal controls. Besides, there was no significant correlation with age, weight, height, BMI and baseline or peak LH levels in these groups.

In our study, a higher Phoenixin-20 level was detected in CPP group but Spexin level did not reach a statistical significance. Phoenixin-20 levels tend to be higher in the CPP group compared to other groups were also recently reported in another study (CPP group 381.11 ± 99.45 pg/mL, PT group 317.97 ± 91.99 pg/mL, controls 283.69 ± 82.27 pg/mL; p < 0.05) [13]. Nevertheless, statistical significance between the Phoenixin-20 level of the CPP group and the PT group disappeared after post-hoc analysis. Additionally, Phoenixin-20 level was not correlated with baseline or peak LH or estradiol levels. However, positive correlations were observed with these parameters in the study of Qin et al. [13].

On the other hand we also observed a weak but positive correlation between the Tanner stage and Phoenixin-20 levels. However, the number of cases with advanced stage of puberty was smaller. Since our study was designed as prospective, we were not able to obtain enough data on how Phoenixin-20 levels change as puberty progresses, yet. We compared the only patients with Tanner 2 stage puberty in the CPP and PT groups by subgroup analysis, but we did not observe a statistically significant difference.

In another study comparing boys with CPP to healthy controls, Phoenixin-20 levels higher in the CPP group [14]. This study was also examined the subgroups of obese and normal weight, and found that in both groups, Phoenixin-20 levels were significantly higher in the obese subgroups [14]. Additionally, a decrease in Phoenixin-20 levels was observed after 6 months of GnRH agonist treatment from baseline (407.41 ± 212.31 pg/mL vs. 293.54 ± 160.77 pg/mL; p = 0.032).

Similar to our results, in another study, Phoenixin-20 levels were compared in CPP, PT and healthy controls and no significant difference was observed between the groups [15].

In vitro studies have shown that Phoenixin-20 promotes osteogenic differentiation and proliferation of MC3T3-E1 cells through activation of the p38/RUNX2 pathway via GPR173 [16]. These results suggest that Phoenixin may play a role in bone formation and maturation during puberty. However, it is also noteworthy that CPP patients, where Phoenixin-20 levels were more significantly elevated, had significantly advanced bone age compared to the other groups, but no significant correlation was found between bone age and Phoenixin-20 [13].

In human studies, Spexin has been shown to inhibit granulosa cell proliferation and estradiol release [12]. Kurowska et al. observed immunolocalization of Spexin and GALR2/3 in human ovaries, including granulosa cells, oocytes, theca, and cumulus cells [17]. In animal studies, Spexin also suppresses food intake by suppressing orexigenic factors [neuropeptide Y (NPY), agouti-related peptide (AgRP), and apelin] in nuclei in the hypothalamus or by increasing anorexigenic factors [proopiomelanocortin (POMC), cocaine and amphetamine-regulated transcript (CART), melanin concentrating hormone (MCH), cholecystokinin (CCK), nucleobindin-2 (NUCB-2), and peptide YY (PYY)] [18–20]. Spexin inhibits the uptake of fatty acids into hepatocytes, resulting in a decrease in hepatic lipid content [21]. Kolodziejski et al. showed that Spexin decreased adipogenesis and lipogenesis but increased lipolysis in mice and humans [22]. In the same study, they also found that it suppresses glucose uptake into visceral adipose tissue [22]. Circulating Spexin levels and Spexin expression in adipocytes were found to be lower in obese patients [23].

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to compare CPP, PT, and healthy control groups in terms of Spexin levels. Although, Spexin was detected at lower levels in the CPP group, statistically significance was not achieved. We did not find any significant correlation between Spexin and other anthropometric measurements (except height, r= -0.180, p = 0.044), biochemical measurements (such as basal LH, peak LH), or markers of pubertal progression such as Tanner stage and bone age (p > 0.05). The association of Spexin with insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome (MetS) is more pronounced. Serum Spexin levels were found to be lower in children with at least one MetS component than in children without MetS [24].

This study investigated the potential role of these emerging neuropeptides (Spexin / Phoenixin-20) in differentiating CPP from PT. Our findings suggest that circulating levels of these neuropeptides differ between groups, indicating a possible association with pubertal activation. However, these results should be interpreted with caution given several important considerations. One of the main concerns is the significant difference in BMI-SDS across the study groups. In our cohort, BMI-SDS was significantly higher in both the PT and CPP groups compared to controls. Given the known anorexigenic properties of these neuropeptides and their reported association with metabolic status, BMI represents a potential confounding factor. However, in our analysis, we did not observe a significant correlation between neuropeptide levels and BMI. This finding suggests that the observed differences between groups may not be solely explained by adiposity. Nevertheless, the absence of correlation does not completely exclude a confounding effect, particularly in a relatively small sample, and this should be taken into account when interpreting the results.

The biological role of these neuropeptides in pubertal regulation remains incompletely understood. While experimental and clinical data suggest involvement in energy homeostasis and neuroendocrine pathways, its direct contribution to HPG axis activation has not been clearly established. Therefore, our findings should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating, rather than indicative of a definitive mechanistic or diagnostic role. Another limitation of our study is the relatively small sample size, which may reduce statistical power and increase the risk of type II error. In addition, although statistically significant differences were observed, the strength of these associations should be interpreted cautiously. We also acknowledge that established biomarkers of pubertal activation, such as Kisspeptin, were not included in our analysis. Comparison with these well-characterized markers would have provided a stronger framework for evaluating the clinical utility of these neuropeptides. Future studies incorporating these biomarkers are warranted to better define its relative and additive diagnostic value. Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths. It was designed prospectively, and participants were recruited consecutively, reducing the risk of selection bias. The inclusion of both PT and CPP groups allowed for a clinically relevant comparison reflecting real-world diagnostic challenges.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that these emerging neuropeptides may be associated with pubertal status; however, its clinical utility as a diagnostic biomarker remains uncertain. Larger, well-designed studies that control for confounding factors such as BMI and include established biomarkers are needed to validate these preliminary observations.

We believe that new studies are needed by further increasing the sample size, measuring the change in Phoenixin-20 levels according to the Tanner stage, and examining the change in Phoenixin-20 levels before and during treatment in those receiving GnRH agonist treatment.

Conclusion

In girls with idiopathic CPP who had not yet started GnRH agonist treatment, although Spexin levels tended to be lower and Phoenixin-20 levels higher in the CPP group compared to PT and control groups, the difference was not statistically significant. Our data did not suggest Spexin and Phoenixin-20 peptides as a diagnostic tool to distinguish cases with CPP from PT or healthy controls.
