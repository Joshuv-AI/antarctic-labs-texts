Autotune: fast, accurate, and automatic tuning parameter selection for Lasso
Tathagata Sadhukhan, Ines Wilms, Stephan Smeekes, Sumanta Basu

Abstract
Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso), a popular method for high-dimensional regression, is now used widely for estimating high-dimensional time series models such as the vector autoregression (VAR). Selecting its tuning parameter efficiently and accurately remains a challenge, despite the abundance of available methods for doing so. We propose autotune , a strategy for Lasso to automatically tune itself by optimizing a penalized Gaussian log-likelihood alternately over regression coefficients and noise standard deviation. Using extensive simulation experiments on regression and VAR models, we show that 𝖺𝗎𝗍𝗈𝗍𝗎𝗇𝖾 \mathsf{autotune} is faster, and provides better generalization and model selection than established alternatives in low signal-to-noise regimes. In the process, 𝖺𝗎𝗍𝗈𝗍𝗎𝗇𝖾 \mathsf{autotune} provides a new estimator of noise standard deviation that can be used for high-dimensional inference, and a new visual diagnostic procedure for checking the sparsity assumption on regression coefficients. Finally, we demonstrate the utility of 𝖺𝗎𝗍𝗈𝗍𝗎𝗇𝖾 \mathsf{autotune} on a real-world financial data set. An R package based on C++ is also made publicly available on Github.

Introduction

In high-dimensional regression with the Lasso, model selection is performed by optimally choosing a single tuning parameter
\lambda
, the penalty level which is expected to strike a balance between false positive and false negatives in model selection. Recent works have underscored a need for fast and accurate methods to select
\lambda

[
50
]
.
This practice of tuning is also in sharp contrast with the classical practice of regression in low-dimensional settings with a handful of predictors, where one first estimates both the regression coefficients (
\beta
) and the noise standard deviation (
\sigma
). Selection of a predictor in the final model is then governed by a tuning parameter
\alpha
, the significance level, which the practitioner can choose based on their perceived cost of false positive in their context. In this work, we adopt an algorithmic view and develop
\mathsf{autotune}
, a strategy for the Lasso to tune itself by relying only on a pre-specified parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
and jointly estimating
\beta
and
\sigma
. The strategy is faster than existing alternatives, and shows better generalization and model selection in our simulation experiments where signal-to-noise ratio is low.

Formally, for a response vector
\text{Y}=(\text{y}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\real^{n}
and
p
predictors in the design matrix
\text{X}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\text{x}_{ij})}}\right)_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq p}
, Lasso solves the following convex problem

\displaystyle\min_{\beta\in\real^{p}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\beta):=\,\frac{1}{2n}\|\text{Y}-\text{X}\beta\|^{2}+\lambda\|\beta\|_{1}.

(2)

The tuning parameter
\lambda
controls the level of regularization. A faster tuning strategy can help build algorithms that repeatedly uses Lasso, e.g. in the analysis of streaming or sequential data in finance
[
22
]
. A more efficient tuning strategy that performs better model selection with fewer samples can aid new discoveries in large-scale omics problems in biology
[
21
]
.

Among the existing tuning strategies, information criteria such as Akaike and Bayesian Information Criterion (AIC
[
1
]
and BIC
[
39
]
) are fast, but known to perform poorly in high-dimension
[
14
,
52
,
50
]
.
This makes cross-validation (CV) the most popular tuning strategy. But even ignoring its ad hoc features (e.g. randomness in data splitting, arbitrary choice of the number of folds), CV is computationally intensive and not preferable in settings that require multiple Lasso fits, e.g. Gaussian graphical models and Vector Autoregressive (VAR) models. CV is also known to overfit
[
47
,
30
]
and experience accuracy loss
[
5
]
in high-dimensional settings. These issues are more prominent for time series data, where a time series cross-validation (TSCV) is used to select folds such that the training folds can only contain the observations that occurred prior to the test data points.

To avoid the challenges of tuning, a number of prior works have proposed changing the loss function of Lasso to make it tuning-free, e.g. Scaled Lasso
[
44
]
, Square Root Lasso
[
7
]
, Rank Lasso
[
48
]
. To date, in high-dimensional problems, none of these methods are as fast as the Lasso implemented in
glmnet

[
20
]
, and their asymptotic theory for time series regressions is less developed compared to that of Lasso. For VAR models,
[
27
]
has recently proposed an automatic tuning strategy with theoretical guarantees. In this work, instead of changing the loss function, we ask the following question:

Is there a
\lambda
on the original penalty grid of Lasso which provides a more accurate solution than CV, and if yes, is there a way to find that
\lambda
quickly?

Essentially,
\mathsf{autotune}
alternately estimates
\beta
and
\sigma
in a full negative Gaussian log-likelihood with an
\ell_{1}
penalty:

\displaystyle\min_{\beta,\sigma^{2}}\quad\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{2n\sigma^{2}}\|\text{Y}-\text{X}\beta\|^{2}+\lambda_{0}\|\beta\|_{1}.

(3)

Since for any given
\sigma^{2}
, solving for
\beta
in (
3
) is essentially solving Lasso with tuning parameter
\lambda:=\lambda_{0}\sigma^{2}
, sequentially guessing different values of
\sigma^{2}
results in fitting Lasso on an automatic, data-driven sequence of
\lambda
values.

Such an alternating estimation is also at the heart of Scaled Lasso, where
\sigma^{2}
is estimated using the current
\widehat{\beta}
as
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}=\|\text{Y}-\text{X}\widehat{\beta}\|^{2}/n
. The automatic tuning of VAR Lasso in
[
27
]
also uses similar updates.

These estimators of
\sigma
are expected to suffer from the shrinkage bias of
\widehat{\beta}
throughout the iterations. Our main technical contribution is to leverage the
partial residuals
(PR) to correct such shrinkage bias of
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
efficiently in the process of solving the Lasso problem.

For a given guess of
\sigma
, and hence a given
\lambda
, the coordinate descent (CD) algorithm for Lasso updates the regression coefficients iteratively using the formulae
\widehat{\beta}_{k}=\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{x}_{ij}r^{(k)}_{i}/n)
, for
k=1,2,\ldots,p
, where
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(a)=\mathrm{sign}(a)(|a|-\lambda)_{+}
is the soft thresholding operator and the coordinates of PR
r^{(k)}
of the
k^{th}
predictor are defined as

\displaystyle r^{(k)}_{i}=\text{y}_{i}-\sum_{j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\dots,p}}\right\}\setminus\{k\}}\text{x}_{i,j}\widehat{\beta}_{j}\text{ for all $i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\dots,n}}\right\}$ }.

(4)

We observe that for sparse
\beta
, the vector of
p
standard deviations of PR, SD
(r^{(k)})
,
k=1,2,\ldots,p
, exhibits an interesting behavior throughout the CD iterations. Specifically, if
\beta
has
s
non-zero entries, about
s
many coordinates in the above vector are
outliers
, i.e. unusually larger than the rest. So, we build a linear model of Y by sequentially introducing these
outlier
predictors one at a time, using a classical F-test at a significance level
\alpha
to determine when to stop. The mean squared error (MSE) of this final linear model is used as the new estimator of
\sigma^{2}
.

Since we don’t need the multiple linear regression coefficients
\beta
themselves and only the
\sigma^{2}
, the computation reduces to one simple Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization, which is much faster than fitting
s
many linear models. Also, in most cases, this estimator
\sigma^{2}
converges way before the full CD for
\beta
converges. So, with this new guess of
\sigma^{2}
,
\mathsf{autotune}
stops its CD early and goes to a new
\lambda
rather than waiting for its CD to converge in the current
\lambda
. Only when the sequence of
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
converges, leading to a final
\lambda
, we compute our final Lasso solution using CD. This offers substantial gain in speed over methods that require first fitting Lasso over a grid of
\lambda
values.

The
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
from
\mathsf{autotune}
shows promising results in high-dimensional inference compared to alternatives such as Scaled Lasso. By tracking the gain in R
2
in the nested linear models above, we can also design a visual diagnostic analogous to the scree plot in PCA. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first diagnostic tool for checking the sparsity assumption in Lasso.

In a wide range of simulation experiments using data generating process from
[
23
]
, we benchmark
\mathsf{autotune}
against CV Lasso and Scaled Lasso. In
section

4.1.1

\mathsf{autotune}

\mathsf{autotune}

\mathsf{autotune}
over benchmarks on the same metrics are even larger, and
\mathsf{autotune}
is even faster over TSCV and
[
27
]
. Lastly, we show that
\mathsf{autotune}
improves
\sigma^{2}
estimation in high-dimensional data as compared to the state-of-the-art methods, leading to better high dimensional inference
[
15
]
in
section

4.3.1
and provides promising diagnostics for checking the sparsity assumption in
section

4.3.2

and used in

Background

In this section, we describe existing tuning strategies for Lasso in regression and VAR modeling, and discuss their limitations. Then we briefly describe PR in CD algorithms for Lasso, and sequential F-tests for model selection in classical statistics - two key technical ingredients used in
\mathsf{autotune}
.

Tuning with cross-validation (CV):

Tuning
\lambda
via K-fold cross-validation
[
24
]
remains the most common tuning strategy among practitioners. Denote
\widehat{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{argmin}_{\beta\in\real^{p}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\beta)
, (see
eq.

2
). Once we have
\widehat{\beta}(\lambda)
on a grid of
\lambda
values, there are two choices in
\mathsf{glmnet}

[
20
]
:

(
\lambda
1)

CV(min):
\lambda
which minimized the K-fold CV error among all the
\lambda
values in the
\lambda
-grid,

(
\lambda
2)

CV(1se): largest
\lambda
such that its K-fold CV error was within 1 SD of the minimum CV error.

Despite its popularity, the number of folds in CV is set to 5 or 10 in an ad hoc manner. The final chosen
\lambda

item

(

λ

1)
is also known to be susceptible to the fold assignment used in CV
[
13
,
38
]
. Recent works have also shown that CV overfits the data
[
47
,
30
]
and loses accuracy
[
5
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, there has been no systematic study to assess the quality of CV(1se)
item

(

λ

2)
solutions.

Information criterion (IC) based tuning:

AIC
[
1
]
and BIC
[
39
]
introduced the idea of penalizing complexity of the model fitted for inducing model selection. The general formula is
n\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\text{Y}-\text{X}\widehat{\beta}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+kp
where
k=2
for AIC and
k=\log(n)
for BIC. For tuning
\lambda
in Lasso, their ease of computation, and avoiding repeated data-splitting calculations made them a simpler alternative to CV. But these information criteria were formulated under the assumption that
p
is fixed and sample size
n\to\infty
, making them unsuitable for high-dimensional regime
n<p

[
14
,
52
]
.
Other information criterion like eBIC
[
14
]
, HBIC
[
52
]
are also proposed for scenarios where
p
grows with
n
. But these are not yet adopted widely and some recent research
[
25
]
suggests that they do not improve upon the performance of BIC in time series analysis.

Linking
\lambda
with error variance:

High-dimensional asymptotics of Lasso for linear model with Gaussian errors suggests choosing
\lambda
in the order of
\sigma\sqrt{\log(p)/n}

[
11
]
.
Since
\sigma
is unknown in practice, a line of work reformulates the loss function
eq.

2
or
eq.

3
to make the tuning of
\lambda
independent of
\sigma

[
7
,
28
,
48
]
. Statistical properties of these methods in high-dimensional time series are still topics ongoing research. In this work, we instead focus on tuning Lasso whose statistical properties are by now well-understood for time series data.

Another line of work, related more to our method, focuses on first estimating
\sigma
from data and then using it for tuning
\lambda
.
For example, Scaled Lasso
[
44
]
introduced a
\sigma
-based tuning procedure for
\lambda
by modifying the penalized Gaussian
log-likelihood objective
eq.

3
.

\displaystyle\min_{\beta,\sigma^{2}}\quad\sigma/2+\frac{1}{2n\sigma}\|\text{Y}-\text{X}\beta\|^{2}+\lambda_{0}\|\beta\|_{1}.

(5)

In fact,
eq.

5
was motivated by the discussions
[
43
,
2
]
on the paper
[
42
]
. Scaled Lasso
[
44
]
proposes an iterative minimization algorithm alternating over
\beta
and
\sigma^{2}
for tuning
\lambda
in Lasso: use the
\widehat{\beta}
values from the Lasso solution path to calculate the Mean Squared Error (MSE), then do a degrees of freedom correction on MSE to estimate the
\sigma
, and then the
\widehat{\sigma}
tunes the
\lambda
for the next iteration in Scaled Lasso. Moreover,
[
44
]
derived asymptotic guarantees of
\widehat{\sigma}
of Scaled Lasso.

Additionally,
[
51
]
proposed Natural and Organic Lasso which optimized the following modified loss functions respectively:

\displaystyle\min_{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}},\frac{\beta}{\sigma^{2}}\in\real_{+}\times\real^{p}}

\displaystyle\,\,-\frac{1}{2}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}}}\right)+\frac{1}{2n\sigma^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\text{Y}-\text{X}\frac{\beta/\sigma^{2}}{1/\sigma^{2}}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\beta/\sigma^{2}\|_{1},\quad\text{and}\quad\min_{\beta\in\real^{p}}\,\,\frac{1}{2n}\|\text{Y}-\text{X}\beta\|^{2}+\lambda\|\beta\|_{1}^{2}

(6)

[
51
]
arrived at Natural Lasso by making the original Lasso objective
eq.

2
scale invariant. Natural Lasso still needs CV to tune its
\lambda
. For Organic Lasso, authors
[
51
, Thm. 12,]
gave a theoretical choice of
\lambda
using the asymptotic theory of Lasso.

But it is known that
\widehat{\beta}
from Lasso has shrinkage bias
[
24
]
, and using its corresponding MSE may introduce that bias in estimating
\widehat{\sigma}
, leading to inaccurate tuning. While such bias correction is usually done in the literature after the final Lasso fit using ordinary least squares
[
32
,
29
]
, an attempt to correct or even reduce such shrinkage bias in
\widehat{\sigma}
during the Lasso fit may offer some advantage in tuning. We attempt to achieve this in
\mathsf{autotune}
.

Tuning
\lambda
in VAR models:

Vector autoregression (VAR) is a workhorse model in multivariate time series data, widely used in macroeconomics
[
3
,
10
]
, portfolio selection in finance
[
17
]
, identifying gene regulatory networks in genomics
[
33
,
12
]
and for understanding functional connectivity between different brain regions in neuroscience
[
41
,
40
]
. Formally, a VAR(
d
) model on a
p
-dimensional stationary time series
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{Z^{t}}}\right\}_{t\geq 1}

with no contemporaneous association takes the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{VAR}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right):Z^{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{i}Z^{t-i}+\varepsilon^{t},\quad\varepsilon^{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}}\right),\quad\Sigma_{\varepsilon}=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diag}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma^{2}_{1},\dots,\sigma^{2}_{p}}}\right),

(7)

where
A_{1},\dots,A_{d}
are
p\times p
transition matrices which need to be estimated and
\sigma^{2}_{1},\dots,\sigma^{2}_{p}
are the noise variances. Given
n+d
stationary observations of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{Z^{t}}}\right\}_{t\geq 1}
, all the
d
VAR transition matrices can be estimated in the autoregressive design
[
26
]

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z^{n+d}}}\right)^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z^{d+1}}}\right)^{\top}\end{array}}}\right]}_{\text{Y}_{n\times p}}=\underbrace{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z^{n+d-1}}}\right)^{\top}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z^{n+d-2}}}\right)^{\top}&\dots&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z^{n}}}\right)^{\top}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z^{d}}}\right)^{\top}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z^{d-1}}}\right)^{\top}&\dots&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Z^{1}}}\right)^{\top}\end{array}}}\right]}_{\text{X}_{n\times dp}}\underbrace{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A_{1}^{\top}\\
A_{2}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
A_{d}^{\top}\end{array}}}\right]}_{\Phi_{dp\times p}}+\underbrace{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon_{n+d}}}\right)^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon^{d+1}}}\right)^{\top}\end{array}}}\right]}_{E_{n\times p}},

where
\Phi
is the
dp\times p
matrix constructed by stacking
A_{1}^{\top},A_{2}^{\top},\dots,A_{d}^{\top}
. The parameter space dimension (
dp^{2}
) grows quadratically with the number of time series
p
, and the OLS estimate of
\Phi
will be consistent only if
n>dp^{2}
, which is uncommon in modern VAR applications. So, it is common to assume
\Phi
is sparse and use
\ell_{1}
-penalized least squares regressions
[
4
]
. We obtain the VAR Lasso estimator of
\Phi
by conducting
p
separate, columnwise Lasso regressions. Mathematically,

\widehat{\Phi}_{\cdot,i}=\underset{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{dp}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\frac{1}{2n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\text{Y}_{\cdot,i}-\text{X}\beta}}\right\|^{2}+\lambda_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\beta}}}\right\|_{1},\quad\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,p.

(21)

Time series cross-validation (TSCV):

A common approach to tune these
p
different values of
\lambda_{i}
is TSCV
[
26
, Sec. 5.10,]
. It is more constrained because the temporal order of the data must be preserved when choosing the different folds. A “rolling window” is adopted, where the dataset is divided into
2K
folds along the temporal dimension. Then the
(K+1)^{th}
fold is assigned the role of a test set, and all the folds corresponding to previous time points are used as the training data to fit the VAR Lasso on the original grid of
\lambda_{i}
separately for each
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\dots,p}}\right\}
. We repeat this process
K
times, where every fold in the latter half plays the role of test fold once, and in each repetition, all the previous folds are used as training data. Lastly for each
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\dots,p}}\right\}
, TSCV averages the
K
mean squared errors (MSE) for every value in the grid of
\lambda_{i}
to get the mean TSCV error, and chooses a
\lambda_{i}
which minimizes this.
As a result, TSCV has some statistical limitations over the usual CV
[
8
,
9
]
. For example, constraints in data-splitting for TSCV reduces the effective sample size available for tuning of
\lambda
.

Moreover, running
p
separate TSCV on each column can be computationally prohibitive for analysis of modern day large time series datasets. Hence,
prior software
[
49
]
have often focused on uniform penalization across all the separate column-wise lasso regressions, which is inadequate for handling heterogeneity across multiple time series.

Automatic tuning for VAR Lasso:

Recently
[
27
]
proposed an automated tuning mechanism for VAR Lasso, building upon the iterative algorithm proposed in
[
6
]
. Denote
\widehat{\Phi}^{(t)}
as the estimator of
\Phi
at the
t^{th}
iteration. Then,
[
27
]
iteratively computes the following columnwise weighted Lasso estimator for
t\geq 1

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi}^{(t)}_{\cdot,i}=\underset{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{dp}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{1}{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\text{Y}_{\cdot,i}-\text{X}\beta}}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\Gamma^{(t)}_{i}\beta}}}\right\|_{1},\quad\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,p

(22)

\displaystyle\text{where }\Gamma^{(t)}_{i}=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diag}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{v}^{(t)}_{i,1},\dots,\widehat{v}^{(t)}_{i,pq}}}\right),\quad\text{ with }\widehat{v}^{(t)}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\sum_{r=1}^{n}\text{X}_{r,j}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{Y}_{r,i}-\text{X}_{r:}\widehat{\Phi}^{(t-1)}_{\cdot,i}}}\right)^{2}}.

(23)

Here the weights
\Gamma_{i}
are adjusted according to the residuals in the previous iteration. This strategy is similar to the iterative algorithm precursor to Scaled Lasso
[
50
]
, and shares its limitation of shrinkage bias in
\widehat{\Phi}

Pseudocode of Coordinate Descent for Lasso

We provide the pseudocode of Lasso at a fixed

In Lasso, the standard practice
[
24
]
is to calculate
\widehat{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)
over a decreasing
\lambda

\lambda_{\max}
to (almost) 0.
\lambda_{\max}=\max_{j\in[p]}\frac{1}{n}\langle{\text{Y}},{\text{X}_{.,j}}\rangle
is the smallest

which ensures
\widehat{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)=0
. The Lasso solution for each successive
\lambda
in the grid is then obtained by initializing the coordinate descent (CD) algorithm with the solution from the previous
\lambda
, a technique known as warm starts
[
24
]
. This process is repeated until the smallest
\lambda
in the grid is reached, yielding
\widehat{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda_{\min}}}\right)
. This entire procedure is often called pathwise coordinate descent, and the set of solutions
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\widehat{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda_{\max}}}\right),\dots,\widehat{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda_{\min}}}\right)}}\right\}
is interchangeably called solution path or regularization path. Warm starts accelerates the solving of pathwise coordinate descent as compared to running CD separately across different values of
\lambda
in the grid.

Sometimes, to further reduce computational costs, a subset of coefficients called “active set” is selected
[
46
,
24
]
at each
\lambda
in the grid. In contrast to pathwise CD, Scaled Lasso internally uses least angle regression (LARS), which is slower than CD
[
19
]
, and K-fold CV Lasso computes the entire Lasso solution path K
+1
times. So, a data-driven choice of
\lambda
values, coupled with running CD in each
\lambda

Sequential F-tests:

Sequential F-tests
[
34
, Chap 4.6,]
is a classic model selection tool which is often employed to calculate ANOVA tables
[
18
]
. Given the response Y and
p
predictors X
j
,
j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\dots,p}}\right\}
, it looks into the following sequence of nested linear models and their corresponding residual sum of squares (RSS):

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{k}:

\displaystyle\text{ Y}=\text{ X}_{1}\beta_{1}+\text{ X}_{2}\beta_{2}+\dots+\text{X}_{k}\beta_{k}+\varepsilon,\qquad k\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\dots,p}}\right\}

(24)

\displaystyle\text{RSS}(\mathcal{M}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{y}_{i}-\widehat{\text{y}}_{i}}}\right)^{2}\quad\text{where the predictions $\widehat{\text{y}}_{i}$ comes from fitting $\mathcal{M}_{k}$ to data.}\quad

(25)

Then, a sequential F-test iteratively performs the hypothesis test
\mathcal{H}_{0,k}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\beta_{k}=0}}\right\}|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k-1}}}\right)
using the statistic

\displaystyle F=\frac{\text{RSS}(\mathcal{M}_{k-1})-\text{RSS}(\mathcal{M}_{k})}{\text{RSS}(\mathcal{M}_{k})/(n-k)}\underset{\mathcal{H}_{0,k}}{\sim}F_{1,n-k}

(26)

until either we arrive at a
k_{0}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\dots,p}}\right\}
where we fail to reject the
\mathcal{H}_{0,k_{0}}
or we reject
\mathcal{H}_{0,j}
for all
j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\dots,p}}\right\}
. If the procedure stops, we conclude that the significant predictors for the regression of Y on X are
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\dots,k_{0}-1}}\right\}
, otherwise all the predictors are included in the model. Note that in
n<p
regime, we will always arrive at a
k_{0}\leq n
where the sequential F-test stops.
The rank order of predictors in which the nested models
\mathcal{M}_{k}
are constructed is important for selecting the final model.

\mathsf{autotune}

Consider the negative penalized Gaussian log likelihood

\displaystyle\min_{\beta,\sigma^{-2}}\quad

\displaystyle f(\beta,\sigma^{-2}):=-\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma^{-2}+\frac{\sigma^{-2}}{2n}\|\text{Y}-\text{X}\beta\|^{2}+\lambda_{0}\|\beta\|_{1}.

\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{0}\,\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}

(M1)

Given
\sigma^{-2}=\widehat{\sigma}^{-2}

by running Algorithm

(M2)

Given
\beta=\widehat{\beta}
, update
\widehat{\sigma}^{-2}
using the outputs of Algorithm

In fact, the original version of Scaled Lasso algorithm proposed in
[

falls in this general framework, with
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
in
item

(M2)
being estimated by the conditional maximum likelihood estimator
\|\text{Y}-\text{X}\widehat{\beta}\|^{2}/n
. The KPS algorithm for VAR Lasso
[
27
]
can also be viewed as the same strategy employed on
p
different noise variance parameters. A potential shortcoming of this approach is that it does not regularize
\sigma^{2}
in any way. Hence in the Scaled Lasso paper
[
44

, the authors proposed a form of degree of freedom correction
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}=\|\text{Y}-\text{X}\widehat{\beta}\|^{2}/(n-a)
, for some

In our empirical analyses, we found that these strategies of iteratively updating
\sigma
suffer from the shrinkage bias in
\widehat{\beta}
. So we develop a more aggressive bias correction strategy based on the PR which are already being computed inside Algorithm
1

A basic
\mathsf{autotune}
strategy:

Assuming the true
\beta
is
s
-sparse and
s\ll p
, we expect that the standard deviations (SD) of the PR corresponding to some
s
predictors will be somewhat larger than the standard deviation of the other
(p-s)
ones. Betting on this general premise, we rank the SD of the
p
PRs at the end of Algorithm
1
from largest to smallest, and use this rank list to sequentially add predictors to build a nested sequence of linear models using F-tests at the significance level
\alpha
(see Background
Section

2
). The MSE of this final linear model is then taken as the new estimator of
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
.

Since for conducting these F-tests, we only require the RSS and not all the multiple linear regression coefficients from these linear models, we can reduce the computation substantially by performing a single Gram-Schmidt (GS) orthogonalization of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\text{X}_{\cdot,1},\text{X}_{\cdot,2},\dots}}\right\}
. Without loss of generality, let the rank list of predictors be
\{1,2,\ldots,p\}
. Then we define
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u_{\cdot,1},\dots,u_{\cdot,k-1}}}\right\}
to be the set of first
k-1
predictors in the rank list orthogonalized via GS, and
\epsilon^{(k-1)}
to be the vector of residuals in the linear model
\text{ Y}\sim u_{\cdot,1}+u_{\cdot,2}+\dots+u_{\cdot,k-1}
. Formally,

\displaystyle u_{\cdot,k}

\displaystyle=\text{X}_{\cdot,k}-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{\langle\text{X}_{\cdot,k},u_{\cdot,i}\rangle}{\langle u_{\cdot,i},u_{\cdot,i}\rangle}u_{\cdot,i},\quad\epsilon^{(k)}=\epsilon^{(k-1)}-\frac{\langle{\epsilon^{(k-1)}},{u_{\cdot,k}}\rangle}{\langle{u_{\cdot,k}},{u_{\cdot,k}}\rangle}u_{\cdot,k}.

(28)

Note that
\epsilon^{(k)}
is essentially the vector of residuals from the linear model
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
of
Y
on
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\text{X}_{\cdot,1},\text{X}_{\cdot,2},\dots}}\right\}
.
Then, we can perform F-test of
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}
against
\mathcal{M}_{k}
(the linear model we get after adding

to
\mathcal{M}_{k-1}

Let

be the number of predictors in the final linear model. This procedure reduces the computational complexity to

in the basic procedure where each model

is fully fitted without using the previous fit

\sigma

is effectively using Algorithms

Warm starts and initialization of
\lambda_{0}

We use a warm start strategy as is common in
glmnet
. This means we initialize the algorithm for
\lambda_{k+1}
at the solution obtained at the previous candidate value of
\lambda_{k}
, for every

Asymptotic theory suggests
\lambda\asymp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\text{X}^{\top}\varepsilon/n}}\right\|_{\infty}\asymp\sigma\sqrt{\log p/n}
for Lasso. Adjusting for the loss function in
\mathsf{autotune}
,
\lambda_{0}\asymp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\text{X}^{\top}\varepsilon/n}}\right\|_{\infty}/\text{Var}(\varepsilon)\asymp\sigma\sqrt{\log p/n}/\sigma^{2}
is a natural choice. Since
\varepsilon
is unknown in practice, a reasonable starting point for our iterative algorithm is to use
Y
instead of
\varepsilon
in the above expression.

Prior literature
[
20
,
24
]
recommends initiating computation of Lasso path at
\lambda=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\text{X}^{\top}\text{Y}/n}}}\right\|_{\infty}
which is just big enough so that
\widehat{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\text{X}^{\top}\text{Y}/n}}}\right\|_{\infty}}}\right)=0
. But in
\mathsf{autotune}
, we found out this approach makes the regression estimates
\widehat{\beta}
stuck at 0. We started seeing the empirical success of
\mathsf{autotune}
after beginning the Lasso path at a
\lambda
slightly smaller than
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\text{X}^{\top}\text{Y}/n}}}\right\|_{\infty}
. Moreover, as
\mathsf{autotune}
starts from
\widehat{\beta}=0
, we set
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{(initial)}}=

\lambda_{0}
ensures that the initial
\lambda=\lambda_{0}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{\mathsf{(initial)}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\text{X}^{\top}\text{Y}/n}}}\right\|_{\infty}
.

Speeding up predictor ranking:

Switching from standard deviation, which is essentially the
\ell_{2}
norm of PR, to an
\ell_{1}
norm of PR helps improve speed without comprising accuracy. In other words, important predictors are still ranked high in terms of both norms.

Active set selection:

We can also utilize active set selection
[
46
]
to reduce the computational load further. We know from the KKT conditions of Lasso
[
24
]
that for all predictors
\text{X}_{\cdot,j}
with nonzero estimates of coefficients,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\langle{\text{X}_{\cdot,j}},{\text{Y}-\text{X}\widehat{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda}}\right)}\rangle/n}}\right|=\lambda
and for the remaining predictors, the previous term will have a value less than
\lambda
. A reasonable expectation is that at the start of CD at a
\lambda
, predictors with a small inner product with the residuals are more likely to have a zero coefficient as compared to the predictors with bigger inner products. We use this intuition to remove the spurious predictors, which can potentially boost the speed of the computations while still arriving at the optimal minimizer (details in
Active-Autotune-Lasso
, and its empirical performance is demonstrated in
section

A.2
). We find that for correlated predictors, this strategy is not very accurate, so in this work we report results without active set selection and leave this for future work.

4.1
High-dimensional regression

We closely follow the simulation setup of
[
23
]
, where the data generating process (DGP) is completely characterized by 6 parameters:
n
,
p
,
s
(level of sparsity),
\rho
(correlation between the predictors),
\mathrm{SNR}
(signal-to-noise ratio), and Beta-type (pattern of sparsity). We use the following linear model:

\displaystyle\text{Y}=\text{X}\beta+\epsilon\quad\text{where}\quad\text{Y}\in\real^{n},\quad\text{X}\in\real^{n\times p},\quad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}}}\right).

(31)

We first define the true coefficients
\beta\in\real^{p}
according to
s
and the Beta-type described below in
item

(B1)
-
item

(B5)
. We then generate the predictors X
{}_{i,\cdot}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\Sigma}}\right)
for all
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\dots,n}}\right\}
, where
\Sigma_{kl}=\rho^{|k-l|}
, and
\epsilon_{i}
with variance

\sigma^{2}
chosen to satisfy
\mathrm{SNR}:=\frac{\beta^{\top}\Sigma\beta}{\sigma^{2}}
, i.e.
\sigma^{2}=\frac{\beta^{\top}\Sigma\beta}{\mathrm{SNR}}
.
Then we run
\mathsf{autotune}
and the benchmarks on the data (Y,X), and record the relevant performance metrics. Finally, we repeat these steps multiple times and report the average and standard error of the metrics.

Following
[
23
]
, the default value of SNR is set to 2. For simulating correlated predictors, we will primarily use
\rho=0.35
and we consider five different schemes for generating
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
:

(B1)

Beta-type 1:
\beta
has
s
entries equal to 1, placed equidistantly between indices 1 and
p
, with all other entries set to 0.

(B2)

Beta-type 2: The first
s
entries of
\beta
are equal to 1, and the remaining entries are 0.

(B3)

Beta-type 3: The first
s
entries of
\beta
decreases linearly from 10 to 0.5, while the rest are set to 0.

(B4)

Beta-type 4: (Approximate Sparsity) The first
s
entries of
\beta
are equal to 1, and the remaining entries decay exponentially, specifically
\beta_{i}=0.5^{i-s}
for
i=s+1,\dots,p
.

(B5)

Beta-type 5:
\beta
has
s
nonzero entries decreasing linearly from 10 down to 0.5 and placed equally spaced across the index range
1
to
p
, with all other entries set to 0.

Performance metrics:

Let
\beta=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p}}}\right)
be the true coefficient,
\widehat{\beta}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\beta}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\beta}_{p}}}\right)
be the estimated coefficient vector of the algorithm, and
\text{Y}_{\mathrm{test}}|\text{X}_{\mathrm{test}}\sim\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{X}_{\mathrm{test}}\beta,\sigma^{2}\mathbb{I}}}\right)
where the rows of
\text{X}_{\mathrm{test}}
are drawn from
\mathcal{N}_{p}(0,\Sigma)
, i.e. rows of
X
and
\text{X}_{\mathrm{test}}
are i.i.d. We quantify estimation and prediction error using the Relative Mean Square Error (RMSE) and Relative Test Error (RTE), respectively. RTE measures the ratio of expected prediction error using estimates of the algorithm
\widehat{\beta}
to that of the oracle, which knows the true
\beta
.

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathrm{RMSE}}=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\widehat{\beta}-\beta}}}\right\|_{2}}{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\beta}}}\right\|_{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\beta}_{i}-\beta_{i}}}\right)^{2}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}^{2}}},\quad\mathrm{RTE}=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\beta}-\beta}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\beta}-\beta}}\right)+\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\text{Y}_{\mathrm{test}}-\text{X}_{\mathrm{test}}\widehat{\beta}}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|{\text{Y}_{\mathrm{test}}-\text{X}_{\mathrm{test}}\beta}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}.

(32)

Note that if
\widehat{\beta}=0
, i.e. the algorithm fits a null model on the data, then RMSE
=1
and RTE
=\mathrm{SNR}+1
. While for perfect fit, i.e.
\widehat{\beta}=\beta
, RMSE
=0
and RTE
=1
. So, a good frame of reference for RMSE is
(0,1)
, while RTE varies in the range
(1,1+\mathrm{SNR})
.
For variable selection, we use Area Under Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC), a measure of how well an algortihm recovers the sparsity pattern of
\beta
.

Interpretation of SNR:

Our definition of SNR is in line with the SNR in
[
23
]
. They gave a natural interpretation of the SNR in terms of a quantity called the Proportion of Variance Explained (PVE).

\displaystyle\mathrm{PVE}(\widehat{\beta})=1-\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\text{Y}_{\mathrm{test}}-\text{X}_{\mathrm{test}}\widehat{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}(\text{Y}_{\mathrm{test}})}=1-\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\beta}-\beta}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\widehat{\beta}-\beta}}\right)+\sigma^{2}}{\beta^{\top}\Sigma\beta+\sigma^{2}}

(33)

Notice that the PVE of the optimal prediction
\widehat{\beta}=\beta
is PVE(
\beta
)
=\frac{\mathrm{SNR}}{\mathrm{SNR}+1}
. So, SNR sets the best possible PVE that can be achieved by a linear model, hence it serves as a good indicator in the simulations of how hard is the regression problem in terms of prediction. For instance, SNR of 1 means that linear model can explain atmost 50% of the total variation in the response variable Y.

4.2
VAR Lasso using
\mathsf{autotune}

We compare VAR Lasso tuned with
\mathsf{autotune}
against VAR Lasso tuned via TSCV, AIC, BIC, and
[
27
]
in terms of estimation, variable selection and runtime. From here on, we refer to VAR Lasso tuned via
[
27
]
as “KPS Lasso”.

Instead of out-of-sample prediction, we focus on estimation and variable selection accuracy since the primary interest in large VAR models is often learning Granger causality (lead-lag) relationships among the component time series. In variable selection,
\mathsf{autotune}
gave AUROC of nearly 1 in all the simulation setups we considered. Hence, we use another variable selection criterion for comparison, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)

\displaystyle\text{MCC}=\frac{\text{TP $\times$ TN}-\text{FP $\times$ FN}}{\sqrt{(\text{TP+FP})(\text{TP+FN})(\text{TN+FN})(\text{TN+FP})}}

(34)

where TP (and FP) is the number of true positives (and false positives) and TN (and FN) is the number of true negatives (and false negatives).
For the binary classification problem of whether
A_{i,j}=0
or not for all
i,j
, then MCC is the correlation between the actual and estimated binary labels. So, MCC is between 1 and -1, with 1 meaning perfect classification, 0 means the classification is no better than random guessing and -1 means perfect disagreement between true and estimated labels.

We conduct our experiments on two DGP: (i)
p
-dimensional diagonal VAR(1) process X
{}_{t}=A\text{X}_{t-1}+\epsilon_{t}
with diagonal
A=0.5\mathbb{I}_{p}
and
\epsilon_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{p}^{2})
with
\sigma_{j}^{2}=\frac{0.5^{2}}{\nu_{j}}
where
\nu_{j}
is the SNR level of the
j^{th}
time series, (ii) same DGP as (i) except
A
has a block diagonal structure. The blocks of
A
are
2\times 2
matrices with all entries equal to 0.3, making the maximum eigenvalue of the companion matrix 0.6 and
\sigma_{j}^{2}=\frac{0.6^{2}}{\nu_{j}}
. DGP(ii) presents relatively harder problem for estimating
A
as compared to DGP(i). We simulate time series initializing at
\text{X}_{0}=0_{1\times p}
and with a burn-in period of 1000 to ensure stationarity. We vary
p\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{5,10,\dots,30}}\right\}
for DGP (i) and
p\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{10,16,24,30}}\right\}
for DGP (ii). Average of performance metrics across 100 replications along with one standard error bars are displayed in figures.

We focus on the cases

4.3
Noise estimation and sparsity diagnostics with
\mathsf{autotune}

While our main objective in this paper is to design a fast and accurate tuning strategy for Lasso, some intermediate products of our algorithm show promising empirical results in statistical tasks relevant for high-dimensional regression. Here we illustrate some preliminary results for two such tasks, estimation of noise variance
\sigma^{2}
, and a visual diagnostic like scree plot to check the sparsity assumption.

4.3.1
Noise scale estimation for high-dimensional inference

We empirically contrast
\sigma
estimation of
\mathsf{autotune}
against Scaled Lasso, Organic Lasso and Natural Lasso
[
51
]
. We also investigate the quality of estimated
\sigma
of different algorithms by using it in inference of high-dimensional regression coefficients via
\mathsf{hdi}
R package
[
15
]
. We report the sensitivity of
\sigma
estimation to chosen
\alpha
in the sequential F-test (see
Sigma-Update
) of
\mathsf{autotune}
in the
section

A.5
.

Performance metric:

We at first compute the difference between estimator of
\sigma^{2}
of algorithm and the best empirical estimator of noise level,
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{empirical}}=\mathrm{Var}(\text{Y}-\text{X}\beta)
i.e. variance of the residuals obtained via regression of Y on X using true coefficients. Then we plot the boxplots of the difference
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{empirical}}
across 100 replications in
fig.

10
.

For inference, we use De-sparsified Lasso
[
15
]
from the
\mathsf{hdi}
R package. It computes the Lasso coefficients, debiases them, and then it needs an estimate of
\sigma
to compute the p-values for two-sided testing of
\mathcal{H}_{0,j}:\beta_{j}=0
for
j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\dots,p}}\right\}
. In that stage,
\widehat{\sigma}
of
\mathsf{autotune}
and other benchmarks are supplied and their inference results are compared. We set significance level to 0.05 and if p-value of
j^{th}
covariate is less than 0.05,
\mathcal{H}_{0,j}
is rejected i.e. it is flagged as a significant predictor and vice versa. Let
\mathcal{S}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\dots,p}}\right\}:\beta_{j}\neq 0}}\right\}
be the true support set, then
s=|\mathcal{S}|
and the false positive rate (FPR) and power becomes

\displaystyle\mathrm{FPR}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}^{C}}\mathbb{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{H}_{0,j}\text{ was rejected }}}\right]/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p-s}}\right),~~\mathrm{Power}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{I}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{H}_{0,j}\text{ was rejected }}}\right]/s.

(35)

We plot the empirical estimates of these metrics across 100 replications in
fig.

11
, and add a horizontal line
y=0.05
in the FPR diagram for connecting the empirical FPR with the significance level used.

Benchmarks:

Along with Scaled Lasso, we add the Organic and Natural Lasso as
[
51
]
focuses on the estimation of error variance in high-dimensional regression and are publicly available in the R package
\mathsf{natural}
. Moreover,
[
51
]
found Organic Lasso to be uniformly better than the CV Lasso based
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}

[
37
]
, hence we skip CV Lasso.

For better understanding of the difficulty in the experiments, we add an oracle method. It has the knowledge of true support set (predictors whose coefficients are nonzero), hence it performs least squares regression of
Y
on the true support set and report its MSE as the
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
. Note that,
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{empirical}}
has the knowledge of true
\beta
whereas
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
of the oracle method only has access to the support set of true
\beta
.

Lastly, in the inference experiment, we drop the results of Organic and Natural Lasso as their
\sigma
estimation was not competitive in the previous experiment of estimating
\sigma
.

We focus on the moderate and high-dimensional setups of

1
, Beta-types 1
item

and 2
item

(B2)

Results:

We observe in
fig.

10
that
\mathsf{autotune}
estimates the
\sigma
better than all the non-oracle benchmarks. In fact, paired t-test comparisons between
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}
of
\mathsf{autotune}
and the benchmarks across 100 replications reveal that the gains in
\sigma

\mathsf{autotune}
are significant at 0.05 level. The only exception is the setup where the significant predictors are correlated with one another; Beta-type 2 with
\rho=0.35
where Scaled Lasso matches the performance of
\mathsf{autotune}
. 95% CI of paired t-test comparison between

and Scaled Lasso had 0 in it. Comparison of

empirically suggests correlation within the significant predictors hampers the

of

6
Conclusion

We develop
\mathsf{autotune}
, an automatic tuning parameter selection algorithm for Lasso, and show its performance in high-dimensional regression and large VAR model estimation using simulated data. In contrast to existing automatic tuning procedures such as the Scaled Lasso,
\mathsf{autotune}
makes use of intermediate partial residuals computed during coordinate descent of lasso to form a rank list of important predictors, and use that list to get a bias-corrected estimator of
\sigma
which guides tuning parameter selection. The algorithm shows promising results in terms of generalization, model selection and runtime. An R package is also made available on Github.

We did not delve into the convergence analysis of this algorithm in this paper. Characterizing the limit point of our iterative algorithm, and understanding its statistical properties will be crucial to gain insight into conditions under which the algorithm fails. We leave these for future work.
