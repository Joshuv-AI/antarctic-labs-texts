Synergistic fungal-enzymatic fermentation of corn straw enhances nutritional value, microbial stability, and bio-feed quality
Authors: Haosen Zhang, Zaheer Abbas, Hongwan Li, Yang Zhu, Xinmiao Hu, Dayong Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Valorizing mature, dry corn straw into nutritional animal feed is constrained by its recalcitrant lignocellulosic matrix, while conventional silage methods face stability and logistical limitations. Existing enzymatic and bacterial approaches often lack synergistic efficacy and fail to mitigate pathogen risk in dry biomass systems. We engineered a two-stage fungal-enzymatic fermentation strategy employing a consortium of Aspergillus niger LFB-AN14, Coriolopsis trogii LFB-F1, Bacillus subtilis LFB-BS7, and Pediococcus acidilactici A62, integrated with cellulase, xylanase, and laccase under optimized conditions (1% inoculation, 5:5:1:1 ratio, 37 °C, 21 days). Our results demonstrated that the bacterial-enzyme co-treatment (Group A3) significantly reduced fiber content, with neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) decreasing by 22.6% and 29.1%, respectively, compared to the control (p < 0.001). Lignin degradation was enhanced, accompanied by a 4.5-fold increase in water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC). The metabolic profile revealed elevated lactic acid production (36.54 g/kg FM) and the suppression of undesirable byproducts such as propionic and butyric acids. Microbial community analysis revealed a dominant shift toward Pediococcus (> 50% abundance) and inhibition of pathogenic Enterobacter spp. Structural analyses (SEM, FTIR) confirmed extensive lignocellulose deconstruction, particularly through carbonyl and hydroxyl functional groups. Metagenomic analysis revealed upregulated Auxiliary Activity (AA) enzymes and cellulosome modules, elucidating the mechanistic basis for enhanced degradation. KEGG enrichment highlighted enhanced aromatic compound metabolism and yeast proliferation, reflecting superior metabolic efficiency. This integrated fungal-enzymatic approach establishes a safe, scalable, and metabolically efficient strategy for transforming agricultural residues into high-quality bio-feed, resolving key challenges in fiber digestibility, pathogen control, and storage stability for sustainable livestock production.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05190-6.

Introduction

Corn straw, as a primary agricultural by-product in China, represents a crucial renewable lignocellulosic biomass resource. Its high-value utilization has emerged as a key research focus in contemporary biomass resource studies [33]. The development of novel feed resources, the exploration of grain feed alternatives, and the diversification of feed sources constitute an inevitable trajectory for the future advancement of animal husbandry [20, 24]. Nevertheless, the application of lignocellulose-degrading bacteria in livestock production remains at a nascent stage, primarily due to two critical challenges: First, strain safety concerns. Certain bacterial strains may pose potential pathogenic risks to animals, necessitating thorough verification of their suitability for feed applications [37, 53]. Current solutions involve genetic engineering approaches for heterologous high-efficiency expression of lignocellulolytic enzyme-encoding genes, or post-fermentation detoxification treatments. However, these methods require careful evaluation regarding cost implications and potential impacts on nutritional composition. Second, the targeting specificity of lignocellulolytic enzymes secreted by individual bacterial strains [17, 18]. When the enzyme system lacks comprehensiveness, single-strain applications prove inadequate for achieving complete degradation of lignocellulosic biomass [27].

In the realm of animal feed production, silage fermentation technology currently represents the predominant method for corn straw processing: Prior to corn maturity, residual straw undergoes mechanical chopping and compaction processes, followed by fermentation through endogenous nutrients and microbial activity [8]. This approach, harvesting during peak plant growth phases, effectively preserves most nutrients while the elevated moisture content enhances feed digestibility and nutritional value [51]. However, the high moisture characteristic of silage introduces substantial storage stability challenges, primarily manifested through susceptibility to mold proliferation and spoilage. Additionally, the considerable weight presents numerous logistical difficulties in transportation and storage [2, 55].

In contrast, post-harvest processing of mature corn straw through drying techniques effectively addresses these limitations. Desiccation treatment not only significantly reduces the populations of harmful bacteria and molds in feed materials, ensuring hygienic safety, but also facilitates an extended storage duration and simplifies transportation, thereby markedly improving both storage stability and logistical convenience [28]. However, as corn straw matures and undergoes drying, its lignocellulose content increases substantially, resulting in significantly greater biodegradation challenges compared to silage corn straw. This enhanced recalcitrance to biological breakdown presents a major obstacle for efficient utilization of the dried material [13, 21].

Yang et al. enhanced the saccharification efficiency of bagasse and corn straw by adding a novel thermotolerant laccase isolated from Bacillus [52]. Wang et al. co-inoculated green Trichoderma and Bacillus subtilis into corn straw-based compost for 42 days of fermentation, achieving partial degradation of lignocellulose [48]. However, these studies often focus on the application of agricultural waste in composting to convert it into organic fertilizer. Research on converting agricultural waste into feed is still limited [3]. Feed conversion requires addressing two concurrent challenges: 1) Effectively degrading components within lignocellulose that are difficult for animals to absorb or that hinder nutrient absorption, thereby disrupting the lignocellulose structure. 2) Both the inoculated microorganisms and the post-fermentation microbial communities must not harbor significant quantities of pathogens harmful to animal production [4, 34]. Enzyme addition alone is insufficient, as fermentation microorganisms are uncontrollable and cannot be directed to shape specific microbial communities [29]. Simply adding bacteria has limited degradation capacity for lignocellulose and restricts microbial strain selection. For instance, as mentioned earlier, Trichoderma viride produces certain mycotoxins and antibiotics under natural fermentation conditions, which are unacceptable in animal feed [25].

Therefore, this study focused on mature and dry corn straw as the research substrate to conduct synergistic fermentation experiments combining microbial strains with enzyme preparations. The four microbial strains used in this study were selected based on their functional properties. Aspergillus niger LFB-AN14 and Coriolopsis trogii LFB-F1 are filamentous fungi known for their ability to produce lignocellulose-degrading enzymes, including cellulases and laccases [10, 32]. Bacillus subtilis LFB-BS7 was selected for its capacity to secrete various hydrolytic enzymes and its probiotic potential, while Pediococcus acidilactici A62 is a lactic acid bacterium that promotes rapid acidification and suppresses undesirable microorganisms during fermentation [26, 49]. To comprehensively evaluate the fermentation quality, we systematically monitored key parameters, including nutritional composition and metabolic byproducts, throughout the fermentation process. Advanced analytical techniques were employed for in-depth characterization: scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed microstructural modifications, while Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) provided molecular-level insights into functional group transformations. Furthermore, metagenomic sequencing was utilized to investigate microbial community dynamics and functional gene expression patterns in differently treated straw samples. This multi-dimensional approach enabled systematic elucidation of the mechanistic basis underlying quality improvement in straw feed through exogenous bacterial-enzyme supplementation, encompassing both microbial population shifts and enzymatic degradation pathways.

Materials and methods

Microorganism and culture media

Aspergillus niger LFB-AN14, Coriolopsis trogii LFB-F1, Bacillus subtilis LFB-BS7, and Pediococcus acidilactici A62 were all screened and obtained by our laboratory. These strains were isolated from decayed wood and distiller's grains, and identified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing (for bacteria) and ITS sequencing (for fungi). Carboxymethylcellulose sodium (CMC-Na) medium was used to culture Aspergillus niger LFB-AN14. The medium was composed of the following (g L−1): CMC-Na, 5 g; NH4NO3, 2 g; KH2PO4, 1 g; NaCl, 5 g. The medium was sterilized at 121 °C for 20 min. Starch medium was used to culture Coriolopsis trogii LFB-F1. This medium was composed of the following in g L−1. Starch, 16 g; peptone 5 g; CuSO4·5H2O 0.2 g [32]. The medium was sterilized at 115 °C for 20 min. Beef Peptone Yeast (BPY) medium was used to culture Bacillus subtilis LFB-BS7. This medium was composed of the following in g L−1. Beef extract 5 g,Peptone 10 g; yeast extract 5 g; NaCl 5 g, and glucose 5 g. The medium was sterilized at 121 °C for 20 min. De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) Medium was used to culture Pediococcus acidilactici A62. This medium was composed of the following in g L−1. Peptone 10 g; Yeast extract 5 g; Beef extract 10 g; ammonium citrate tribasic 2 g; sodium acetate 5 g; glucose 20 g; K2HPO4 2 g; Tween 80 1.0 mL; MgSO4·7H2O 0.575g; MnSO4·H2O, 0.14 g.

Antagonism assay

To exclude inhibitory interactions among the four strains, pairwise antagonism tests were performed using the cross-streaking method. Each strain was cultured to the logarithmic phase. For bacterial-bacterial pairs, cultures of B. subtilis and P. acidilactici were streaked perpendicularly on BPY and MRS agar plates, respectively. For fungal-fungal pairs, mycelial plugs of A. niger and C. trogii were placed on PDA plates at a distance of 3 cm. For bacterial-fungal pairs, bacteria were streaked linearly on PDA plates, and fungal mycelial plugs were placed adjacent to the bacterial streak. All plates were incubated at 30 °C for 48–72 h and observed for inhibition zones or clear demarcation lines at the intersection. No visible inhibition was observed between any pair, confirming compatibility for co-cultivation.

Preparation of inoculum

A. niger and C. trogii were fermented according to the optimized conditions from the previous experiments. A. niger was inoculated on PDA solid medium and cultured at 30 °C until the plate was fully covered with mycelium. Then, 10 mL of physiological saline was added to the solid medium, and the mycelia and spores were scraped to prepare a suspension. This suspension was inoculated at 5% (v/v) into modified CMC fermentation medium and fermented at 30 °C, 180 rpm for 48 h [56]. C. trogii was prepared as a suspension in the same manner and inoculated at 5% (v/v) into the laccase fermentation medium, followed by fermentation at 30 °C and 180 rpm for 8 days [32]. P. acidilactici and B. subtilis were streaked on MRS and BPY solid medium plates, respectively, and cultured at 37 °C for 16–24 h. Single colonies were then picked and inoculated into liquid medium. After activation in a constant-temperature shaker at 37 °C and 180 rpm, the seed cultures were ready (with viable counts exceeding 108 CFU/mL for both).

Optimization of fermentation conditions

An overview of the experimental design is illustrated in Fig. S1. The substrate straw was harvested after crop maturity, shredded, and dried in a laboratory oven to constant weight. No autoclaving was performed prior to fermentation. The previously dried straw was placed into polyethylene vacuum bags, with moisture content maintained at 70% during the fermentation process. In addition to straw and microorganisms, only a nitrogen source was added to the fermentation medium. Subsequent experiments investigated the types of nitrogen sources employed.

The initial fermentation conditions were as follows: inoculation with A. niger LFB-AN14、C. trogii LFB-F1、B. subtilis LFB-BS7、P. acidilactici A62 at 0.5% (v/w) each, with an inoculation ratio of 1:1:1:1, no additional supplements, a fermentation period of 14 days, and a temperature of 37 °C. The following optimization methods all employ single-factor experiments [36, 41]. ADF measured the quality of all fermentation processes, as ADF encompasses both lignin and cellulose, making it one of the most critical factors affecting feed digestion and absorption [7, 21].

Fermentation methods

Two fermentation methods were employed: anaerobic fermentation and two-stage fermentation. In anaerobic fermentation, the inoculated substrate was placed into polyethylene vacuum bags, and the air was completely removed using a vacuum sealer before sealing the bags airtight. In the two-stage fermentation, the procedure was similar, but a controlled amount of air was intentionally retained inside the bag before sealing to facilitate a transition from aerobic to anaerobic conditions [12]. All experimental groups were subjected to parallel fermentation under identical conditions.

Fermentation temperature and duration

Three temperature gradients were tested: 25 °C, 37 °C, and 42 °C. Other conditions remained unchanged, and fermentation was performed using the optimal strain combination, inoculation volume, ratio, and supplement type. The same indicators were measured. Fermentation periods of 3, 7, 14, 21, and 30 days were tested. All other parameters were set according to the optimal conditions, and the same indicators were measured.

Inoculation volume and ratio

The seed cultures were premixed and inoculated at different volumes (0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, 10%) into corn straw for fermentation optimization. Other conditions remained consistent, and fermentation was conducted using the optimal strain combination. The same indicators were measured. Different inoculation ratios were tested: 1:1:1:1, 3:3:1:1, 5:5:1:1, 10:10:1:1. Fermentation was performed on corn straw under otherwise identical conditions, using the optimal strain combination and inoculation volume. The same indicators were measured. The inoculation ratios tested (1:1:1:1, 3:3:1:1, 5:5:1:1, and 10:10:1:1) were selected based on preliminary experiments conducted in our laboratory (unpublished data). These ratios were designed to cover a range from equal proportions to a tenfold higher concentration of fungi relative to bacteria, allowing systematic evaluation of the optimal balance between lignocellulose-degrading fungi and acid-producing bacteria.

Supplement types

Five supplement conditions were evaluated: Molasses (M), Ammonium sulfate (A), Molasses + Ammonium sulfate (M + A), Urea (U), No supplement (C). Other conditions were kept constant, and fermentation was conducted using the optimal strain combination, inoculation volume, and ratio. The same indicators were measured. The amount of supplement added to each group is 1% of the total weight of the fermented feed.

Experimental grouping

The experiment set up four treatment groups in total: Control, A1 (cellulase + xylanase + laccase), A2 (LFB-F1 + LFB-AN14 + A62 + LFB-BS7), and A3 (LFB-F1 + LFB-AN14 + A62 + LFB-BS7 + cellulase + xylanase + laccase). The enzyme preparations used in this study (cellulase, xylanase, and laccase) were obtained from Zhongke Yifeng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). The enzyme activities were determined to be 10,000 U/mg protein for each enzyme, with enzyme units defined as follows:

Cellulase activity unit (U): One unit of cellulase activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that released 1 μmol of glucose equivalents per minute from carboxymethyl cellulose substrate under standard assay conditions (pH 5.0, 37 °C).

Xylanase activity unit (U): One unit of xylanase activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that released 1 μmol of xylose equivalents per minute from xylan substrate under standard assay conditions (pH 5.0, 37 °C).

Laccase activity unit (U): One unit of laccase activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that oxidized 1 μmol of ABTS [2,2'-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)] per minute under standard assay conditions (pH 5.0, 37 °C).

For fermentation treatment, the enzyme preparations were added at a level of 0.1 g per kilogram of corn straw substrate (0.1 g/kg). All enzyme solutions were prepared by dissolving the enzymes in sterile distilled water to the desired concentrations and were uniformly sprayed onto the sterilized substrate prior to inoculation to ensure thorough contact between enzymes and substrate.

Determination of bioactive profile

Appropriate amounts of fermented feed samples were weighed and immediately placed in an oven at 65 °C for drying over 48 h. Moisture content was then determined to obtain the dry matter (DM) content. The dried straw was crushed and passed through a 40-mesh sieve. Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) was analyzed as described by Van Soest et al., acid detergent fiber (ADF) was determined using the methods of Van Soest et al., acid detergent lignin (ADL), and water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC) were measured [46]. A 20 g sample was mixed with 180 mL of distilled water, and placed for extraction at 4 °C for 24 h, and then filtered through 4 layers of gauze and a 0.22 μm microporous water-based membrane. The filtrate was used to determine pH, ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N), and organic acids. The concentration of NH3-N was determined using the colorimetric procedure of Okuda et al. [31]. Organic acids were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Samples were collected at the end of the fermentation period (day 21). Identification of organic acids was achieved by comparing retention times with those of authentic standards (lactic acid, acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid,Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Quantification was performed using external standard calibration curves constructed from standard solutions at five concentration levels. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and results were expressed as g/kg fresh matter (FM). The method showed good linearity (R2 > 0.99), recovery rates ranging from 95 to 103%, and relative standard deviations (RSD) below 5%.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

For SEM, appropriate amounts of fresh fermented feed samples were cut into straw tissues of 3–10 mm2 in size and less than 3 mm in height. Dissection tools must be sharp to avoid mechanical damage during sampling. The cut tissues were quickly placed in pre-cooled fixative (2.5% glutaraldehyde) and fixed at 4 °C for about 10 h. The fixed samples were subjected to critical point drying and gold sputtering, and finally coated with conductive tape for observation under the microscope.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

Prior to preparation, the sample was briefly baked under an infrared lamp for 5 min. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded at room temperature (22 °C) over a range of 400–4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1. A total of 32 scans were accumulated for each measurement to achieve a signal-to-noise ratio of 50,000:1. The sample was prepared as a uniform film by drying it directly on a zinc selenide crystal.

Microbial community analysis

Feed was collected and stored in a sterile centrifuge tube. 10 g of frozen fermented feed was taken, added with 40 mL of sterile water, and oscillated at 120 rpm for 2 h. The mixture was filtered through double-layer sterile medical gauze, and the gauze was repeatedly rinsed with 40 mL of sterile water to recover microorganisms. The filtrates were combined and centrifuged at 4 °C and 10,000–12,000 g for 15 min. The precipitate was sent to Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd. for processing. Analysis method: The E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Extraction Kit was used to extract the total genomic DNA of the microbial community. The DNA concentration and purity were detected, and the integrity was checked by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Covaris M220 was used to fragment the DNA, and fragments of approximately 350 bp were selected. NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq was used for PE library construction (including adapter ligation, magnetic bead screening, PCR amplification enrichment, and magnetic bead recovery). Metagenomic sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq™ X Plus platform. Diamond was used to align the amino acid sequences of the non-redundant gene set with the NR database (BLASTP parameter e-value set to 1e-5). Species annotation was carried out based on the taxonomic information database corresponding to the NR library, and species abundance was calculated according to the sum of the gene abundances corresponding to the species. All microbial analyses were completed on the Majorbio Cloud Platform.

Statistical analysis

Single-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on the experimental data using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and multiple comparisons were conducted using Duncan’s method. Experimental results were plotted with GraphPad 8.0 software. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (95% confidence interval), and P < 0.05 indicates a significant difference.

Results and discussion

Antagonistic interactions among the four strains

After 48–72 h of incubation at 30 °C, no inhibition zones or clear demarcation lines were observed at the intersections of any bacterial–bacterial, fungal–fungal, or bacterial–fungal pairs (Fig. S2). These results indicate that the four strains are compatible and can be co-cultivated without mutual antagonism.

Optimization of fermentation conditions for biological straw feed

The content of acid detergent fiber (ADF) in the two-stage fermentation group was significantly lower than that in the anaerobic fermentation group (Table 1), indicating that its degradation efficiency of feed fiber was better. The presence of oxygen during fermentation significantly impacts microbial metabolic activity. The aerobic phase may promote the growth and metabolism of certain aerobic microorganisms, thereby creating more favorable conditions for subsequent anaerobic fermentation [6, 35]. Therefore, it was selected as the fermentation method for subsequent experiments. Both 1% and 10% inoculation amounts could effectively reduce the ADF content (Fig. 1A). Considering the fermentation efficiency and economy, the 1% inoculation amount not only ensured significant degradation of ADF but also had a lower cost, so it was determined as the optimal inoculation amount. The fiber degradation effects of the molasses treatment group (ADF 42.1%) and the ammonium sulfate treatment group (ADF 42.0%) were equivalent, both significantly better than that of the control group. The ADF of the combined molasses and ammonium sulfate treatment group was reduced to 40.1%, and the degradation effect was better than that of the single additive treatment group (Fig. 1B). The average ADF content of the treatment group with the inoculation ratio of 5:5:1:1 was 43.77%, which was significantly better than that of the control group and the treatment groups with the ratios of 1:1:1:1 and 10:10:1:1, and only slightly better than that of the 3:3:1:1 group (Fig. 1C). Therefore, it was determined as the optimal inoculation ratio. Determination of nutritional components

As shown in Table 2, there was no significant difference in dry matter content among the treatment groups (P > 0.05), indicating that the addition of exogenous bacteria or enzyme preparations had little effect on the dry matter content of the feed. However, significant differences were observed in neutral detergent fiber (NDF) content: Group C had the highest NDF content, while Group A3 had the lowest. The combination of exogenous bacteria and enzyme preparations significantly reduced the NDF content in the feed, which may help improve feed digestibility. The content of acid detergent fiber (ADF) showed a similar trend to NDF: Group C had the highest ADF content, and Group A3 had the lowest. The ADF contents of Groups A1 and A2 were also significantly lower than that of Group C, suggesting that the addition of exogenous bacteria and enzyme preparations effectively reduced the ADF content and further improved the digestive performance of the feed [11, 45]. Groups A2 and A3 significantly reduced the lignin content in the feed, which may contribute to improving feed digestibility and nutritional value. Group A3 had the highest water-soluble carbohydrate (WSC) content, and the WSC contents of Groups A1 and A2 were also significantly higher than that of Group C, indicating that the addition of exogenous bacteria and enzyme preparations significantly increased the WSC content. In conclusion, the addition of exogenous bacteria and enzyme preparations, especially the combined treatment in Group A3, significantly improved the nutritional components of the feed by reducing fiber contents (NDF, ADF, ADL) and increasing the content of water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC). These changes may help improve feed digestibility and nutritional value, thereby enhancing animal production performance [16, 44]. A detailed discussion of the limitations regarding enzyme activity persistence is provided in Limitations and Future Perspectives.

In terms of lactic acid content, all treatment groups were significantly higher than the control group (P < 0.001). The lactic acid content of the control group was 6.32 ± 0.85 g/kg, while those of groups A1, A2, and A3 were increased to 26.89 ± 1.16 g/kg, 35.47 ± 2.69 g/kg, and 36.54 ± 1 g/kg, respectively. The increases in groups A2 and A3 were more significant, indicating that the addition of exogenous bacteria and enzymes can significantly promote lactic acid production. Additionally, the acetic acid content in all treatment groups was significantly lower than that in the control group (P < 0.001). The acetic acid content of the control group was 31.04 ± 1.29 mg/L, while those of groups A1, A2, and A3 were reduced to 7.67 ± 0.15 mg/L, 10.28 ± 0.83 mg/L, and 13.8 ± 1.11 mg/L, respectively. Furthermore, the propionic acid content of the control group was 32.67 ± 4.16 mg/L, which was significantly reduced to 4.93 ± 1.59 mg/L in group A1 (P < 0.001), while no propionic acid was detected (ND) in groups A2 and A3. The butyric acid content of the control group was 22.49 ± 3.12 mg/L, while those of groups A1, A2, and A3 were significantly reduced to 0.41 ± 0.16 mg/L, 0.26 ± 0.05 mg/L, and 0.16 ± 0.05 mg/L, respectively (P < 0.001). These results indicate that the addition of exogenous bacteria and enzymes can significantly inhibit the production of propionic acid and butyric acid.

In conclusion, the addition of exogenous bacteria and enzymes significantly improves the composition of metabolites in the fermentation system, specifically by: significantly reducing ammonium nitrogen content to decrease nitrogen loss; significantly increasing lactic acid content to promote the accumulation of beneficial metabolites; and significantly inhibiting the production of propionic acid and butyric acid to reduce the accumulation of undesirable metabolites [15]. Among them, group A3 showed the most prominent performance in reducing ammonium nitrogen, increasing lactic acid content, and inhibiting undesirable metabolites, indicating that the combined use of exogenous bacteria and enzymes has a synergistic effect, which can effectively optimize the fermentation process.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis

Scanning electron microscopy was used to observe the microstructure of the sclerenchyma on the surface of yellow-stored corn straw under different treatment conditions. As an important component of the outer epidermis of vascular bundles, the cell walls of sclerenchyma form a lignified structure through uniform thickening and lignin deposition, which means it is extremely difficult to degrade [43].

The results of microscopic observation showed that the control group samples maintained complete organizational structure characteristics: the epidermal hair structure was intact, the morphology of the sclerenchyma was normal, and the texture of the lignocellulose structure was clearly visible (Fig. 2A). In contrast, different treatment groups showed varying degrees of organizational structure changes. The samples in group A1 only showed slight fracture of epidermal hairs, and the overall structure remained relatively complete (Fig. 2B). The samples in group A2 showed significant structural changes, characterized by a significant increase in the degree and range of epidermal hair fractures, and local rupture of the sclerenchyma epidermis, indicating that the lignocellulose structure had undergone initial degradation (Fig. 2C). Microbial community analysis

The exogenous addition of microorganisms and active enzymes affects the overall microecological structure of fermented feed. The quality and safety of biomass-based fermented feed are influenced by endogenous microorganisms, inoculants, and interactions between microbial communities. First, this experiment analyzed microbial diversity. At the species level, the Simpson index was the only indicator showing significant differences between groups (Fig. 4 A-C), with groups A2 and A3 being significantly higher than groups C and A1 (P < 0.05). The α-diversity results indicated that bacterial addition enhanced the biodiversity of fermented feed. For β-diversity analysis at the species level, PcoA (Principal Coordinates Analysis) was used to reduce the dimensionality of complex microbial species data. In the four groups, samples within groups C and A1 were closely clustered, with partial overlap between groups, indicating highly similar microbial communities with minimal differences. Samples within groups A2 and A3 were slightly more dispersed but remained within the confidence interval, with close intergroup distances and overlapping confidence intervals, suggesting certain but not large differences in microbial communities (Fig. 4D). The long distance and non-overlapping intervals between groups C/A1 and A2/A3 indicated dissimilar microbial communities with significant differences, consistent with the mountain plot results. The composition analysis of microbial community species indirectly assesses whether fermented feed is normal or contaminated. At the species level, community structures of groups C and A1 were more similar, as were those of A2 and A3. Groups C and A1 were dominated by Pediococcus acidilactici, Pediococcus sp., Kosakonia cowanii, Enterobacter, Lactobacillus sporogenes, Enterobacter asburiae, and Enterobacter cloacae. Groups A2 and A3 were dominated by Pediococcus acidilactici and Pediococcus sp., with their abundances exceeding 50%, while other microorganisms had low abundances (Fig. 4E). However, one sample in group A3 showed high abundance of methylotrophic bacteria, possibly due to methane production during fermentation, promoting the proliferation of these methane-specific carbon source-utilizing microbes. Compared to non-bacterial groups C and A1, the relative abundance of Pediococcus in groups A2 and A3 increased extremely significantly from 5% to over 50%. Notably, differences in community composition were also observed between A2 and A3. Although both groups were dominated by Pediococcus at comparable abundances, group A3 exhibited a distinct reduction in certain minor taxa (Fig. 4E). This can be attributed to the presence of exogenous enzymes in A3. The added cellulase, xylanase, and laccase hydrolyze lignocellulose, releasing soluble sugars and phenolic compounds that favor rapid growth of Pediococcus, creating a competitive environment that suppresses slower-growing or less acid-tolerant species [19, 54]. Enzymatic breakdown of lignin may also enhance substrate accessibility, further refining the microbial community [14]. These findings highlight the synergistic role of enzymes in shaping microbial succession during fermentation. The limitations of inferring microbial succession from end-point data are discussed in Limitations and Future Perspectives.

Based on comparative genomics, this study systematically investigated expression differences of lignocellulose degradation-related functional gene sets across groups to reveal molecular mechanisms underlying substrate degradation efficiency variations. Compared to the control, groups A2 and A3 showed significantly upregulated expression of Auxiliary Activity (AA) enzyme family genes (p < 0.05), correlated with reduced lignin content (Fig. 5C). Acid detergent lignin (ADL) measurements confirmed that ADL content in A2 and A3 was significantly lower than in C and A1, suggesting high AA enzyme expression promoted lignin degradation. Additionally, cellulosome modules were detected in A2 and A3 (relative abundance 0 in C and A1). Cellulosomes, multi-enzyme complexes produced by anaerobic microbes, contain various cellulases and hemicellulases, forming structures via anchoring-adhesion mechanisms attached to bacterial cell walls for efficient cellulose degradation [1], possibly linked to the significant decrease in cellulose content in A2 and A3. These findings suggest that the composite bacterial agents may enhance lignocellulose degradation efficiency through the upregulation of AA enzyme expression and the presence of cellulosome modules, as inferred from metagenomic data. Further discussion of the limitations associated with these inferred mechanistic links can be found in Limitations and Future Perspectives.

Differentially expressed AA family enzymes have important biological significance. The AA6, AA10, and AA1 families exhibit significant enrichment in groups A2 and A3 (Fig. 5D). The AA6 family, as p-benzoquinone reductases, plays a key role in biodegradation of aromatic compounds and participates in fungal cell protection by scavenging cytotoxic reactive quinones. The AA10 family, identified as lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases, disrupts glycosidic bonds in polysaccharide chains via oxidative cleavage to generate more reducing ends, forming an efficient synergistic degradation system with endo- and exoglucanases to enhance lignocellulose degradation. The AA1 family mainly includes multicopper oxidases, which catalyze reactions using phenols and derivatives as electron donors and molecular oxygen as electron acceptors, further divided into laccase, ferroxidase, and laccase-like multicopper oxidase subfamilies, critical for lignin degradation and various redox reactions [9, 39]. These differentially expressed genes are likely closely related to efficient lignocellulose degradation.

KEGG enrichment analysis effectively identifies significantly regulated functional modules under experimental conditions, extending the analysis from single genes to functional gene clusters to reveal underlying biological mechanisms. Compared to the control (group C), the composite bacterial agent group (A2) showed significantly upregulated pathways mainly involved in yeast metabolism(Fig. 5E), indicating that bacterial addition promoted yeast proliferation and metabolic activity by altering microenvironmental conditions, consistent with microbial community analysis showing Candida glabrata as the dominant eukaryotic microbe in A2. Comparing A2 and A3, group A3 exhibited significantly enhanced activities in pathways related to environmental microbial metabolism, carbon metabolism, and aromatic compound degradation, consistent with its reduced acid detergent fiber and lignin contents(Fig. 5F), suggesting that enzyme addition promoted lignocellulose degradation by regulating metabolic activities of specific functional microbes. This provides a theoretical basis for optimizing bacterial-enzyme synergistic complexes. Systematic comparisons of metabolic pathway differences among different bacterial-enzyme combinations revealed that introducing specific functional strains significantly enhances the synergistic effect of the composite system. Compared to the control, significantly upregulated yeast-related metabolic pathways in group A2 suggest that introducing probiotic yeasts into composite systems may have dual effects: inhibiting excessive proliferation of opportunistic pathogenic yeasts (e.g., Candida glabrata) via niche competition and efficiently converting substrate nutrients into single-cell proteins to enhance overall system functionality [42, 47]. These results provide a new optimization strategy for constructing bacterial-enzyme synergistic systems with precise metabolic regulation.

Limitations and future perspectives

Several inferences drawn in this study should be interpreted with caution, as they are based on indirect evidence rather than direct measurements. First, although we attribute the enhanced fiber degradation in the enzyme-treated groups to sustained enzyme activity throughout the fermentation cycle, residual enzyme activities were not directly monitored in our system. This inference is supported by literature data showing that free lignocellulose-degrading enzymes can retain approximately 40% of their initial activity after 30 days under similar temperature conditions [38], and by the significantly greater fiber reduction observed in the enzyme-treated group (A1) compared to the control (Table 2). Nevertheless, direct time-course monitoring of enzyme activities under actual fermentation conditions is needed to confirm enzyme persistence. Second, the proposed microbial succession pattern—whereby the inoculated fungi and Bacillus dominate during the initial aerobic phase by secreting degradative enzymes, and are subsequently outcompeted by Pediococcus as oxygen depletes and sugars accumulate—is inferred from end-point community composition and general principles of solid-state fermentation. Because our metagenomic analysis was performed only on end-point samples, time-course sampling and analysis would be required to validate this dynamic process. Finally, the mechanistic links between upregulated AA enzyme families, cellulosome modules, and enhanced lignocellulose degradation are based on metagenomic data and require direct experimental confirmation. Addressing these limitations in future studies will further strengthen the mechanistic understanding of the fungal-enzymatic synergistic system and enable more precise optimization of fermentation parameters.

Conclusions

The two-stage fermentation system, when integrated with optimized complex microbial consortia, demonstrated significant enhancement in fiber degradation efficiency. The bacterial-enzyme co-treatment (Group A3) exhibited superior performance, as evidenced by: (1) a marked increase in lactic acid concentration (p < 0.05) coupled with significant suppression of undesirable metabolic byproducts including propionic acid and butyric acid (p < 0.01), collectively contributing to improved feed palatability and nutritional quality; (2) substantial microbial community restructuring, characterized by a predominant shift toward Pediococcus dominance (> 50% relative abundance) and concurrent reduction in the relative abundance of potentially pathogenic Enterobacter spp., indicating the inoculant's capacity for directional modulation of fermentation microbiota and suggesting potential biosafety benefits; and (3) structural analyses through SEM and FTIR spectroscopy, which corroborated the synergistic effects of bacterial-enzyme treatment on lignocellulosic matrix deconstruction, particularly through ultrastructural disruption and enhanced degradation of aromatic polymer components.
