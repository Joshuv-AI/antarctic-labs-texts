Analytic First-Order Entanglement Entropy of Schwarzschild Black Holes with Interacting Scalars
Florin Manea

Abstract




Abstract:
We compute the first-order correction to the entanglement entropy of a scalar field with quartic self-interaction across the horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole. The main novelty of this work is the derivation of a fully explicit analytic expression for both the renormalized Newton’s constant,
G_{F}
, and the corresponding entropy correction,
\delta S^{(1)}
.
Using the Euclidean action, the replica trick, and heat kernel techniques, we obtain
\delta S^{(1)}
in closed form, expressed directly in terms of the horizon area, field mass, interaction strength, and curvature coupling. This expression reveals a non-trivial log-enhanced quadratic divergence (
\sim\ln(m^{2}\epsilon^{2})/\epsilon^{2}
).
Divergences are absorbed into
G_{F}
, which then naturally enters the Bekenstein–Hawking area law, yielding a finite corrected black hole entropy.
Our analytic expressions allow a transparent study of parameter dependence where light fields and strong interactions enhance the correction, curvature suppresses it, and conformally coupled fields (
\xi=1/6
) receive no leading-order contribution.
These results precisely show how quantum field interactions govern the renormalization of the Bekenstein–Hawking entropy.

Keywords:
black hole entropy; entanglement entropy; quantum corrections; scalar field self-interactions; renormalized Newton’s constant

1
Introduction

Black hole entropy, first proposed by Bekenstein
[
1
]
and given a quantum foundation by Hawking’s discovery of black hole radiation
[
2
]
, is fundamental for exploring how quantum fields affect gravity. In
(3+1)
dimensions, the semiclassical entropy of a Schwarzschild black hole is given by the Bekenstein–Hawking formula,

S_{\mathrm{BH}}=\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{H}}}{4G},

(1.1)

where
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{H}}
is the horizon area. Unlike ordinary thermodynamic systems whose entropy scales with volume, black hole entropy scales with surface area, reflecting the unique geometric nature of gravitational entropy.

In quantum field theory on curved spacetimes, Hawking radiation arises from vacuum fluctuations near the event horizon. Through these correlations, the black hole acquires a thermal spectrum with temperature

T=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi},

(1.2)

where
\kappa
is the surface gravity and natural units were used
(\hbar=\mathrm{c}=\mathrm{k_{B}=1})
. The resulting entropy can be interpreted as arising from the entanglement between degrees of freedom inside and outside the horizon.

Many previous papers focused on entanglement entropy, especially for free (Gaussian) fields
[
3
,
5
,
4
]
, where correlations are fully determined by two-point functions. However, physical fields generally exhibit interactions, which, combined with spacetime curvature, can modify horizon correlations and generate nontrivial corrections to the entropy.

Although authors such as Fursaev, Solodukhin
[
6
,
8
,
9
,
7
]
, have addressed entanglement entropy for interacting fields on conical manifolds, the existing formulas do not provide the full analytic structure of the first-order correction. The main contribution of this work is the derivation of a fully explicit analytic expression for the correction
\delta S^{(1)}
and the renormalized Newton’s constant
G_{F}
, explicitly showing their dependence on the field mass, interaction strength, and curvature. This result clarifies, at the analytic level, how renormalization and entanglement entropy corrections are directly linked.

It is well-known from papers like
[
6
,
11
]
that the divergences resulting from the entanglement entropy of free fields can be absorbed to renormalize Newton’s constant
G
.

We show explicitly that the interaction induces new divergent terms that are likewise absorbed into a renormalized constant
G_{F}
, which we compute in analytic form.

From the entanglement perspective, black hole entropy arises because an outside observer has access only to degrees of freedom exterior to the horizon, with the exterior state described by a reduced density matrix. The associated von Neumann entropy naturally reproduces the area law in the free-field limit
[
13
]
.

However, entanglement entropy generically exhibits ultraviolet (UV) divergences due to short-distance correlations near the horizon. These divergences can be absorbed into a renormalized Newton’s constant
G_{F}
, arising from quantum fluctuations of matter fields that contribute to the effective action. This procedure renders the total entropy finite and physically meaningful.

This framework motivates our computation, by systematically incorporating a quartic self-interaction and curvature effects, and by deriving an explicit analytic expression for the associated renormalization and entropy correction, we quantify how first-order quantum corrections refine the semiclassical entropy.

2
Entanglement entropy

Entanglement entropy
[
14
,
15
]
has been extensively applied in relativistic quantum field theory, for example in the study of the Unruh effect, where states beyond the Rindler horizon are inaccessible to an observer. The interpretation of black hole entropy based on quantum entanglement was researched by the authors Bombelli, Koul, Lee and Sorkin (BKLS)
[
16
]
. Consider the global vacuum state
\ket{X}
. If spacetime is divided by a boundary into an interior and an exterior region, one can define a complete basis
\{\ket{A}\}
spanning the interior space and a complete basis
\{\ket{B}\}
spanning the exterior space. We can write the vacuum state as

\ket{X}=\sum_{A,B}\eta_{AB}\,\ket{A}\otimes\ket{B},

(2.3)

where the coefficients
\eta_{AB}
are some complex numbers.
The corresponding density matrix is

\rho_{AB}=\ket{X}\bra{X}=\sum_{A,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime}}\eta_{AB}\eta^{*}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\ket{A}\bra{A^{\prime}}\otimes\ket{B}\bra{B^{\prime}}

(2.4)

The density matrix
\rho_{AB}
in Eq. (
2.4
) describes the global state, while the reduced density matrix
\rho_{A}
represents the degrees of freedom accessible to an external observer.

In our setting, the interior region is inaccessible to external observers. To describe only the observable degrees of freedom, we trace over the unobserved subsystem
B
, obtaining the reduced density matrix

\rho_{A}=\mathrm{Tr}_{B}(\rho_{AB})=\sum_{A,A^{\prime},B}\eta_{AB}\,\eta^{*}_{A^{\prime}B}\,\ket{A}\bra{A^{\prime}}.

(2.5)

2.1
Von Neumann Entropy

The entanglement entropy is defined as the von Neumann entropy of the reduced density matrix
[
8
]
,

S=-\,\mathrm{Tr}\big(\rho_{A}\ln\rho_{A}\big),

(2.6)

which measures how much information is hidden when part of a quantum system cannot be observed.
For a state that is completely pure,
S=0
.
If some parts of the system are ignored, for example regions beyond a horizon,
the remaining state becomes mixed, giving a nonzero entropy.

In continuum quantum field theory, the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is formally infinite-dimensional, and explicitly constructing
\rho_{A}=\mathrm{Tr}_{B}(\rho_{AB})
is intractable due to short-distance correlations and UV divergences. Practical computations therefore require a regulator or indirect techniques, such as the replica trick.
The Rényi entropies provide a convenient generalization,

S_{n}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-n}\ln\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^{n}),\quad n>0,\,n\neq 1,

(2.7)

from which the von Neumann entropy follows in the limit
n\to 1
,

S(\rho)=\lim_{n\to 1}S_{n}(\rho)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\ln\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^{n})\Big|_{n=1}.

(2.8)

2.2
Background Geometry

We consider the Euclidean section of the four-dimensional Schwarzschild black hole,

ds^{2}=f(r)d\tau^{2}+f(r)^{-1}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega_{2}^{2},\qquad f(r)=1-\tfrac{2GM}{r},

(2.9)

with Euclidean time
\tau
identified with period
\beta_{H}=8\pi GM
. The horizon is at
r_{h}=2GM
.
Near the horizon the metric factorizes as

ds^{2}\;\simeq\;d\rho^{2}+\rho^{2}d\tilde{\tau}^{2}+r_{h}^{2}d\Omega_{2}^{2},

(2.10)

where
(\rho,\tilde{\tau})
describe a two-dimensional plane and
d\Omega_{2}^{2}
the horizon two-sphere
\Sigma
of radius
r_{h}
. The replica construction replaces the plane by a cone
C_{n}
with opening angle
2\pi n
, so that locally

\mathcal{M}_{n}\;\simeq\;C_{n}\times\Sigma,

with a conical singularity at
\rho=0
.

For the Schwarzschild background the smooth part of the Ricci scalar vanishes,
R=0
, but the conical defect introduces a localized curvature at the tip. To keep track of non-minimal couplings and possible generalizations, we will allow for bulk curvature
R
and intrinsic curvature
R_{\Sigma}
of the horizon surface in intermediate steps. Using this metric, we’ll explicitly compute the entanglement entropy of the quantum fields across the Schwarzschild horizon.

2.3
Replica Trick Review

This reformulation is particularly useful because, in a quantum field theory with infinitely many degrees of freedom, directly computing the logarithm of the density matrix is generally impossible. Instead, one computes
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n})
using a path integral representation. The replica trick
[
4
,
3
]
expresses this as a Euclidean partition function
Z_{n}
on an
n
-sheeted manifold
\mathcal{M}_{n}
, constructed by cyclically gluing
n
copies of the original spacetime along the entangling region
A
.

This gluing introduces a branch point along the entangling surface: as one encircles the boundary of region
A
, one moves continuously from one replica to the next. In the limit
n\to 1
, this produces a conical singularity with deficit angle
2\pi(1-n)
. The singularity encodes the nontrivial correlations across the entangling surface that give rise to entanglement entropy. This method transforms the non-analytic computation of the logarithm into a procedure involving analytical differentiation.
[
17
]
.

Formally, the
n
-th power of the reduced density matrix can be expressed as a ratio of partition functions,

\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A}^{n})=\frac{Z_{n}}{Z_{1}^{n}},

where
Z_{n}
is the partition function on the
n
-sheeted manifold and
Z_{1}
is the partition function on the original single-sheeted manifold. The normalization by
Z_{1}^{n}
ensures that
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{A})=1
.

The von Neumann entropy can be obtained via analytic continuation,

S=-\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\ln\frac{Z_{n}}{Z_{1}^{n}}\Bigg|_{n=1}.

(2.11)

In the path-integral approach, the entanglement entropy of a region
A
is computed via the replica trick, using the partition function
Z_{n}
on an
n
-sheeted manifold.
If correlations across the boundary of
A
are trivial, the path integral factorizes,
Z_{n}=Z_{1}^{n}
, yielding
S=0
.
For nontrivial correlations, the replicas are coupled along the entangling surface, giving
Z_{n}\neq Z_{1}^{n}
and a nonzero entropy.

Thus, entanglement entropy acquires a geometric interpretation as a property of the replicated spacetime manifold with branch points.

2.4
Free Theory

We assume that the scalar field
\phi
satisfies appropriate boundary conditions to ensure a well-defined functional integral. Specifically,
\phi
vanishes sufficiently fast at infinity (Dirichlet conditions) or satisfies periodic/compact boundary conditions if
\mathcal{M}_{n}
is compact. Near the conical singularity at the entangling surface
\Sigma
, we require regularity in polar coordinates
(r,\tau)
around the tip, so that each Fourier mode
\phi_{k}(r)\sim r^{|k|/n}
remains finite as
r\to 0
. These conditions guarantee that the quadratic operator
\mathcal{O}_{n}
is self-adjoint and that the heat-kernel expansion is valid.
For a (Euclidean) scalar field on a manifold
\mathcal{M}_{n}
with metric
g
, the action for the free field is quadratic,

S_{E}[\phi]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\sqrt{g}\;\phi\,\mathcal{O}_{n}\phi.

(2.12)

For a scalar field on the replicated manifold
\mathcal{M}_{n}
the quadratic fluctuation operator appearing in the Euclidean action is

\mathcal{O}_{n}\;=\;-\Delta_{g}+m^{2}+\xi R(x;n),

(2.13)

where
\Delta_{g}
denotes the Laplace–Beltrami operator on
(\mathcal{M}_{n},g)
,
R(x;n)
is the Ricci scalar which depends on the replica index
n
, and
\xi
parametrizes the non-minimal coupling to curvature (with minimal coupling corresponding to
\xi=0
and conformal coupling in
d
dimensions given by
\xi=(d-2)/(4(d-1))
)
[
18
]
. The free Gaussian partition function is formally defined as a functional Gaussian integral and can be expressed in terms of the determinant of
\mathcal{O}_{n}
. A brief derivation of this relationship is provided in Appendix A.

Z_{n}=\int\mathcal{D}\phi e^{-S_{E}[\phi]}\propto\big(\det\mathcal{O}_{n}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(2.14)

3
Quartic Interaction

We deform the Gaussian theory by a weak quartic coupling
\alpha
, treated perturbatively.
The Euclidean action on the replicated manifold
\mathcal{M}_{n}
is

S[\phi]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}d^{d}x\,\sqrt{g}\;\phi\,\mathcal{O}_{n}\phi+\frac{\alpha}{4!}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}d^{d}x\,\sqrt{g}\;\phi^{4},

(3.15)

where
\alpha
denotes the renormalized quartic coupling, assuming
m^{2}>0
so that no IR subtleties appear at the order considered.
The factor
1/4!
is conventional: Wick’s theorem generates
4!
equivalent contractions of the four fields, and this normalization cancels those permutations.

It is convenient to separate the action as

S[\phi]=S^{(0)}[\phi]+\alpha\,\delta S^{(1)}[\phi],

(3.16)

with
S^{(0)}
the Gaussian part and
\delta S^{(1)}
the normalized quartic term.
The corresponding partition function factorizes as

Z_{n}=Z_{n}^{(0)}\,\Big\langle e^{-S_{\mathrm{int}}[\phi]}\Big\rangle_{0,n},\qquad S_{\mathrm{int}}[\phi]\equiv\frac{\alpha}{4!}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}d^{d}x\,\sqrt{g}\,\phi^{4},

(3.17)

where
Z_{n}^{(0)}
is the free partition function and

\langle\cdot\rangle_{0,n}
denotes expectation values in the Gaussian theory.

Expanding for small
\alpha
, the logarithm of the partition function is

\ln Z_{n}=\ln Z_{n}^{(0)}-\langle S_{\mathrm{int}}\rangle_{0,n}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2}),

(3.18)

which is the first-order perturbative correction.

Wick’s theorem applies on
\mathcal{M}_{n}
since the combinatorial structure of field contractions is independent of the underlying manifold
[
19
]
. For four identical fields at coincident points there are three distinct pairings, yielding

\ln Z_{n}=\ln Z_{n}^{(0)}-\frac{\alpha}{8}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\sqrt{g}\,[G_{n}(x,x)]^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2}).

(3.19)

The coincident propagator
G_{n}(x,x)
is ultraviolet divergent, and consequently the quantity
G_{n}(x,x)^{2}
requires regularization. To control this divergence we introduce a proper-time regulator
\epsilon^{2}
, with the limit
\epsilon^{2}\to 0
taken at the end of the calculation. The propagator on the
n
-sheeted manifold can be represented in terms of the heat kernel as
[
12
]

G_{n}(x,y)=\int_{\epsilon^{2}}^{\infty}ds\,K_{{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}(x,y;s)\,e^{-m^{2}s},

(3.20)

Here
s
denotes the diffusion time. Since the manifold
\mathcal{M}_{n}
possesses a conical singularity, the standard heat-kernel expansion valid for smooth manifolds cannot be applied directly, instead, additional singular contributions localized at the tip must be taken into account.

It was shown that a manifold with a conical singularity can be decomposed into a smooth contribution and a singular part
[
9
]
.

K_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}(x,x;s)=K_{\mathrm{reg}}(x,x;s)+\delta K_{\mathrm{sing}}(x;s).

(3.21)

In the vicinity of the horizon
\Sigma
, the geometry of
\mathcal{M}_{n}
factorizes as
\mathcal{M}_{n}\simeq C_{n}\times\Sigma

[
10
]
,
where
C_{n}
is a two-dimensional cone and
\Sigma
is the horizon surface.

The regular part satisfies:

K_{\mathrm{reg}}(x,x;s)=\frac{1}{(4\pi s)^{d/2}}\left[a_{0}^{\mathrm{reg}}(x;n)+a_{1}^{\mathrm{reg}}(x;n)s+\mathcal{O}(s^{2})\right],

(3.22)

with coefficients
[
12
]
:

\displaystyle a_{0}^{\mathrm{reg}}(x;n)

\displaystyle=1,

(3.23)

\displaystyle a_{1}^{\mathrm{reg}}(x;n)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{6}-\xi\right)R_{\mathrm{smooth}}(x;n).

(3.24)

The singular part is supported on the horizon
\Sigma
:

\delta K_{\mathrm{sing}}(x;s)=\frac{1}{(4\pi s)^{d/2}}\left[\delta a_{0}(x;n)+\delta a_{1}(x;n)s+\mathcal{O}(s^{2})\right]\delta_{\Sigma},

(3.25)

Consequently, the leading singular coefficients are
[
8
]
:

\displaystyle\delta a_{0}(x;n)

\displaystyle=0,

(3.26)

\displaystyle\delta a_{1}(x;n)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{6}-\xi\right)\left(\frac{n^{2}-1}{n}\right).

(3.27)

Therefore, Eq. (
3.20
) can be written as

G_{n}(x,y)=\underbrace{\int_{\epsilon^{2}}^{\infty}ds\,K_{\mathrm{reg}}(x,y;s)\,e^{-m^{2}s}}_{G_{\mathrm{reg}}(x,y)}+\underbrace{\int_{\epsilon^{2}}^{\infty}ds\,K_{\mathrm{sing}}(x,y;s)\,e^{-m^{2}s}}_{G_{\mathrm{sing}}(x,y)},

(3.28)

For our geometry, away from the tip, the Ricci scalar vanishes,
R_{\mathrm{smooth}}(x;n)=0
, so that the regular and singular parts of the propagator can be written to first order in
s
as

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{reg}}(x,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\epsilon^{2}}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{e^{-m^{2}s}}{(4\pi s)^{d/2}},

(3.29)

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{sing}}(x,x)

\displaystyle=(\frac{1}{6}-\xi)\left(\frac{n^{2}-1}{n}\right)\delta_{\Sigma}\int_{\epsilon^{2}}^{\infty}ds\,\frac{e^{-m^{2}s}}{(4\pi s)^{d/2}}s.

(3.30)

Splitting the propagator as
G_{n}=G_{\mathrm{reg}}+G_{\mathrm{sing}}
, the coincident square decomposes as

\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}[G_{n}(x,x)]^{2}\;=\;\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}G_{\mathrm{reg}}^{2}\;+\;2\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}G_{\mathrm{reg}}G_{\mathrm{sing}}\;+\;\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}G_{\mathrm{sing}}^{2}.

(3.31)

However, the crucial point is that the singular heat-kernel coefficient is

\delta a_{1}(n)\;=\;\left(\frac{1}{6}-\xi\right)\frac{n^{2}-1}{n}=(\frac{1}{3}-2\xi)(n-1)+\mathcal{O}((n-1)^{2}).

(3.32)

Thus
G_{\mathrm{sing}}\propto(n-1)
and consequently
G_{\mathrm{sing}}^{2}\propto(n-1)^{2}
even though
\delta_{\Sigma}^{2}
is ill-defined, it doesn’t modify the entropy calculation
[
8
]
.
Since the entanglement entropy is obtained from Eq. (
2.11
),
only terms linear in
(n-1)
contribute.
Therefore the contribution from
G_{\mathrm{sing}}^{2}
vanishes upon differentiation at
n=1
.

4
Four-Dimensional Case

We now specialize the general results to
d=4
, corresponding to the physical spacetime. Writing the expressions for
G_{\rm reg}
and
G_{\rm sing}
in terms of the incomplete gamma function we obtain

\displaystyle G_{\rm reg}(x,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{2}}{(4\pi)^{2}}\Gamma\Big(-1,m^{2}\epsilon^{2}\Big),

\displaystyle G_{\rm sing}(x,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{6}-\xi\right)\left(\frac{n^{2}-1}{n}\right)\,\delta_{\Sigma}(x)\,\Gamma\Big(0,m^{2}\epsilon^{2}\Big).

(4.33)

where
\Gamma
denotes the incomplete Gamma function.
The cross-term in Eq. (
3.31
) includes the contribution from the conical singularity. The singular part of the propagator is supported on the entangling surface
\Sigma
, represented by a delta-function
\delta_{\Sigma}(x)
. In the limit
\epsilon\to 0
,
G_{\rm sing}
becomes an integral over
\Sigma
. This contribution has dimension
L^{d-2}
, the same as the area of
\Sigma
. Consequently, when integrated over the manifold, it produces an area-law contribution
[
9
]

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}d^{4}x\,\sqrt{g}\,G_{\rm reg}(x,x)\,G_{\rm sing}(x,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{6}-\xi\right)\left(\frac{n^{2}-1}{n}\right)\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}d^{4}x\,\sqrt{g}\,G_{\rm reg}(x,x)\left[\delta_{\Sigma}(x)\int_{\epsilon^{2}}^{\infty}ds\,s^{-1}e^{-m^{2}s}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{6}-\xi\right)\left(\frac{n^{2}-1}{n}\right)\int_{\epsilon^{2}}^{\infty}ds\,s^{-1}e^{-m^{2}s}\int_{\Sigma}d^{2}y\,\sqrt{g_{\Sigma}}\,G_{\rm reg}(r=0,y)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{6}-\xi\right)\left(\frac{n^{2}-1}{n}\right)\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}\,G_{\rm reg}(\Sigma)\,\Gamma\Big(0,m^{2}\epsilon^{2}\Big).

(4.34)

where
g_{\Sigma}
denotes the induced metric on the surface
\Sigma
,
A_{\Sigma}
represents the area of the entangling surface
\Sigma
and
G_{\mathrm{reg}}(\Sigma)
represents the average value of the regular Green’s function on
\Sigma
. This is a valid leading-order approximation because
G_{reg}
, while still UV divergent, is a smooth function in the vicinity of the horizon
\Sigma
, and free from the conical singularity.

For
\epsilon\ll 1
, Eqs. (
4.33
) and (
4.34
)
can be expanded as

\displaystyle G_{\rm reg}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{m^{2}}{16\pi^{2}}\Biggl(\chi+\frac{1}{m^{2}\epsilon^{2}}-1\Biggr),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}G_{\rm reg}G_{\rm sing}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}}{256\pi^{4}}\left(\frac{1}{6}-\xi\right)\left(\frac{n^{2}-1}{n}\right)\,\Biggl[-\frac{\mathcal{\chi}}{\epsilon^{2}}+m^{2}\chi\Bigl(1-\chi\Bigr)\Biggr].

(4.35)

where
\gamma_{E}
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant and
\chi\equiv\gamma_{E}+\ln(m^{2}\epsilon^{2})
.
Using these expansions, the logarithm of the partition function to first order in
\alpha
becomes

\displaystyle\ln Z_{n}=\ln Z_{n}^{(0)}-\frac{\alpha m^{4}\Delta^{2}}{2048\pi^{4}}\mathcal{V}({\mathcal{M}_{n}})\,-\frac{\alpha\,\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}}{1024\pi^{4}}\left(\frac{1}{6}-\xi\right)\left(\frac{n^{2}-1}{n}\right)\,\Biggl[-\frac{\chi}{\epsilon^{2}}+m^{2}\chi\Bigl(1-\chi\Bigr)\Biggr].

(4.36)

where
\Delta\equiv\chi+\frac{1}{m^{2}\epsilon^{2}}-1
and
\mathcal{V}({\mathcal{M}_{n}})
is the volume of the manifold
\mathcal{M}_{n}
.
The black hole entropy, Eq. (
2.11
), can be cast in terms of the replica index
n
as

S=-\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\Big(\ln Z_{n}-n\ln Z_{1}\Big)\right|_{n=1}.

(4.37)

The leading-order quantum correction to the entropy is therefore

\delta S^{(1)}\;=\;\frac{\alpha\,\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}}{512\pi^{4}}\left(\frac{1}{6}-\xi\right)\,\Biggl[-\frac{\chi}{\epsilon^{2}}+m^{2}\chi\Bigl(1-\chi\Bigr)\Biggr].

(4.38)

For a Schwarzschild black hole with horizon area

\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}=4\pi r_{h}^{2}=16\pi G^{2}M^{2}
, this yields

\boxed{\delta S^{(1)}=\frac{\alpha G^{2}M^{2}}{32\pi^{3}}\left(\frac{1}{6}-\xi\right)\Biggl[-\frac{\chi}{\epsilon^{2}}+m^{2}\chi\Bigl(1-\chi\Bigr)\Biggr].}

(4.39)

The correction exhibits a UV divergence proportional to
1/\epsilon^{2}
, which can be absorbed through renormalization to yield a finite, physically meaningful observable.

5
Renormalization of Newton’s constant

The expression in Eq. (
3.19
) is directly related to the quantum effective action of the matter field
[
12
]
,

W_{n}\equiv-\ln Z_{n}\,,

(5.40)

The result for the logarithm of the partition function was computed in Eq (
4.36
), by substituting, we obtain the quantum effective action

W_{n}=-\ln Z_{n}^{(0)}+\frac{\alpha m^{4}\Delta^{2}}{2048\pi^{4}}\mathcal{V}({\mathcal{M}_{n}})\,+\frac{\alpha\,\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}}{1024\pi^{4}}\left(\frac{1}{6}-\xi\right)\left(\frac{n^{2}-1}{n}\right)\,\Biggl[-\frac{\chi}{\epsilon^{2}}+m^{2}\chi\Bigl(1-\chi\Bigr)\Biggr],

(5.41)

where we can define

B_{0}\equiv\frac{\alpha m^{4}\Delta^{2}}{2048\pi^{4}},\hskip 20.00003ptB_{1}\equiv\frac{\alpha}{1024\pi^{4}}\left(\frac{1}{6}-\xi\right)\,\Biggl[-\frac{\chi}{\epsilon^{2}}+m^{2}\chi\Bigl(1-\chi\Bigr)\Biggr].

(5.42)

The bulk volume term contributes to cosmological constant renormalization, while the surface term contributes to Newton’s constant renormalization because it is the term proportional to curvature localized on the entangling surface.

In four dimensions, the Einstein-Hilbert action with a cosmological constant reads
[
20
]

S_{EH}[g]=\frac{1}{16\pi G_{B}}\int d^{4}x\sqrt{g}\left(-R-2\Lambda_{B}\right),

(5.43)

where
G_{B}
and
\Lambda_{B}
are the bare Newton’s constant and bare cosmological constant, respectively.

Comparing with the quantum effective action in Eq. (
5.41
), one identifies
B_{0}
as a contribution to the cosmological constant and
B_{1}
as a correction to
1/G_{B}
.

The total effective action can thus be written as

\Gamma_{\rm total}[g]=S_{EH}[g]+W[g]=\frac{1}{16\pi G_{B}}\int d^{4}x\sqrt{g}\left(-R-{2\Lambda_{B}}\right)+W_{n}[g].

(5.44)

For the replicated manifold
\mathcal{M}_{n}
which has a conical singularity the Einstein-Hilbert term
\int\sqrt{g}R
is
[
10
]

\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\sqrt{g}R=4\pi(1-n)\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}+\int_{\mathcal{M}\backslash\Sigma}\sqrt{g}R.

(5.45)

Comparing this to the singular part of the Einstein–Hilbert action,
\frac{1}{16\pi G_{B}}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\sqrt{g}\,R\supset\frac{1-n}{4G_{B}}\,\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
, we see that a term in
W_{n}
of the form
C\,(n-1)\,\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
renormalizes
1/G_{B}
. Our calculated term,
B_{1}\,\frac{n^{2}-1}{n}\,\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
, is of this form since
\frac{n^{2}-1}{n}=2(n-1)+\mathcal{O}((n-1)^{2})
.
The divergence proportional to the Ricci scalar
R
can be absorbed into a renormalized Newton’s constant
G_{\rm F}

Conclusions

In this work, we have derived a closed-form, analytic expression for the first-order quantum correction to the entanglement entropy of an interacting scalar field. By implementing the replica trick on a conical manifold and employing heat kernel methods to compute the Green’s function, we have obtained a fully explicit result for the
\delta S^{(1)}
correction in terms of the field mass (
m
), quartic coupling (
\alpha
), and curvature coupling (
\xi
). We have applied this to the Schwarzschild black hole to provide a concrete physical example.

The central result of our analysis is the rigorous demonstration of the mechanism by which entanglement entropy renormalizes Newton’s constant. The leading-order correction
\delta S^{(1)}
arises from the cross-term
G_{\text{reg}}G_{\text{sing}}
, which encodes the interaction between the bulk (regular) and boundary (singular) parts of the Green’s function. We found this contribution contains a non-trivial log-enhanced quadratic divergence (
\sim\ln(m^{2}\epsilon^{2})/\epsilon^{2}
), far more complex than a simple logarithmic term.

One of the most important findings is that, despite the complex structure that the divergence has, it is perfectly absorbed by the renormalization constant. We have shown that by defining a renormalized Newton’s constant
G_{F}
via the relation
\frac{1}{G_{F}}\equiv\frac{1}{G_{B}}+8B_{1}
, the total physical entropy becomes the finite, regulator-independent Bekenstein-Hawking law,
S_{\text{BH}}=\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}}{4G_{F}}

The apparent divergence in the quantum entropy
\delta S^{(1)}=2B_{1}\mathcal{A}_{\Sigma}
is thus perfectly absorbed into the redefinition of the gravitational coupling.

This result analytically shows the mathematical bridge between the informational and thermodynamic interpretations of gravity. The renormalization of
G
is the precise mechanism that unifies these two perspectives.

Finally, this work reinforces the paradigm of gravity as an emergent, effective field theory. Our findings highlights that Newton’s constant is not a fundamental parameter, but rather a scale-dependent, effective coupling that absorbs the quantum effects of interacting fields, much like any other coupling in quantum field theory. This provides a clear, analytic framework for understanding how the structure of the quantum vacuum directly governs the observed, macroscopic laws of spacetime.
