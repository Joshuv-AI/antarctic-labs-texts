End-Diastolic Forward Flow Through the Pulmonic Valve in Carcinoid Heart Disease
Authors: Karim Hoyek, Ziad Zalaquett, Patrick Collier, Nelson Burbano-Vera, Anand Mehta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnd-diastolic forward flow (EDFF) through the pulmonic valve is classically described after repaired tetralogy of Fallot and is usually attributed to restrictive right ventricular (RV) physiology. Its significance in carcinoid pulmonic valve disease is less established.Case SummaryWe describe 6 patients with carcinoid heart disease and pulmonic valve involvement referred for operative management. Perioperative echocardiography demonstrated late-diastolic antegrade flow through the pulmonic valve after atrial activation and before ventricular systole, consistent with EDFF during atrial contraction. All patients underwent pulmonic and tricuspid valve replacement; additional left-sided valve surgery, coronary bypass, septal closure, or pulmonary artery/RV outflow tract patch augmentation was performed when indicated.DiscussionEDFF in carcinoid heart disease likely reflects organized atrial contraction, severe tricuspid and pulmonic regurgitation, altered RV diastolic properties, and incomplete pulmonic valve closure. Electrocardiography-Doppler correlation is required to avoid misclassification and underestimation of pulmonic regurgitation severity.

Carcinoid heart disease results from exposure of right-sided valves to vasoactive substances, particularly serotonin, in patients with neuroendocrine tumors. Fibrotic plaque deposition causes leaflet thickening, retraction, and impaired mobility, most often involving the tricuspid and pulmonic valves because these mediators are usually inactivated in the pulmonary circulation before reaching the systemic valves.1, 2, 3, 4 End-diastolic forward flow (EDFF) into the pulmonary artery has been classically described after tetralogy of Fallot repair and is associated with restrictive right ventricular (RV) physiology.5,6 However, diastolic pulmonary artery flow is not unique to repaired congenital heart disease. In adults, prominent antegrade diastolic pulmonary artery flow has also been reported with congenital heart disease, severe pulmonic regurgitation, cardiomyopathy, RV infarction, and pericardial disease.7 Hemodynamically, EDFF occurs when atrial systole raises RV diastolic pressure above pulmonary artery diastolic pressure. Importantly, this finding is not specific for a stiff or restrictive RV: Severe pulmonic regurgitation may lower pulmonary artery diastolic pressure sufficiently to permit EDFF despite normal right atrial pressure.1, 2, 3, 4 EDFF therefore reflects the interaction among RV compliance, effective atrial contraction, downstream pulmonary artery pressure, and pulmonic valve competence. In carcinoid heart disease, retracted pulmonic leaflets may remain partially open during diastole, allowing atrial contraction to drive blood across the RV and into the pulmonary artery. We report 6 perioperative cases of EDFF through the pulmonic valve in carcinoid valve disease.

Case Presentation

Patient 1

A 62-year-old woman with established carcinoid heart disease was referred for severe tricuspid and pulmonic regurgitation. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed moderate RV dilation, mildly reduced RV systolic function, preserved left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction, restricted tricuspid and pulmonic leaflets, and severe right-sided regurgitation. Right heart catheterization showed pulmonary artery pressure of 37/17 mm Hg and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 12 mm Hg, without marked pulmonary hypertension. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) showed thickened, retracted tricuspid and pulmonic leaflets with wide-open tricuspid regurgitation, severe pulmonic regurgitation, and incomplete pulmonic coaptation. Color Doppler, continuous-wave Doppler, and color M-mode demonstrated EDFF after the P wave and before QRS complex onset, distinct from systolic forward flow (Figure 1). Patient 2

A 64-year-old man with metastatic well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor and carcinoid heart disease presented with chronic right-sided heart failure, recurrent volume overload, pleural effusion, and ascites. TTE showed preserved LV systolic function, severe RV and right atrial dilation, moderately to severely reduced RV systolic function, severe tricuspid regurgitation, severe pulmonic regurgitation, and moderate mitral regurgitation. He underwent tricuspid and pulmonic valve replacement, mitral valve repair, and left internal mammary artery bypass to the left anterior descending artery. Postoperatively, a pericardial effusion with rising right-sided filling pressures required subxiphoid exploration and washout, after which hemodynamics improved.

Patient 3

A 74-year-old woman with metastatic well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor and carcinoid valve disease presented with acute decompensated heart failure. TTE showed preserved LV systolic function, RV dilation with preserved systolic function, severe tricuspid regurgitation with restricted carcinoid leaflet morphology, moderate aortic regurgitation, and a small pericardial effusion. Right heart catheterization showed right atrial pressure of 12 mm Hg with V waves to 20 mm Hg and pulmonary artery pressure of 20/8 mm Hg. She underwent aortic, pulmonic, and tricuspid valve replacement, coronary bypass to the left anterior descending artery, and pulmonary artery/RV outflow tract patch augmentation. Patient 4

A 73-year-old woman with metastatic well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor involving the liver and carcinoid syndrome was admitted for preoperative octreotide infusion before right-sided valve surgery. Her history included hypertension, Raynaud disease, depression, remote Guillain-Barré syndrome, and prior liver biopsy/embolization complicated by labile blood pressure, seizure, and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome. She had severe tricuspid regurgitation and clinically significant pulmonic regurgitation/stenosis with shortness of breath, leg swelling, fatigue, nausea, and weight loss. EDFF can be visualized in the pulmonic valve on TEE (Figure 3). She underwent tricuspid valve replacement, pulmonic valve replacement, and pulmonary artery/RV outflow tract patch enlargement. Pathology confirmed carcinoid plaques involving both valves. Patient 5

A 56-year-old woman with stage IV well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor, hepatic metastases, and suspected pancreatic head/bowel primary lesions was admitted for preoperative optimization before multivalvular surgery. TTE showed preserved LV systolic function, normal RV size and function, mild right atrial dilation, severe mitral regurgitation, severe tricuspid regurgitation, severe central aortic regurgitation, and at least mild pulmonic regurgitation with thickened leaflets. Catheterization showed upper-normal biventricular filling pressures, normal pulmonary pressures, normal cardiac output, and nonobstructive coronary artery disease. She underwent aortic, mitral, tricuspid, and pulmonic valve replacement, pulmonary artery/RV outflow tract patch augmentation, and patent foramen ovale closure. Pathology confirmed carcinoid involvement of all 4 valves; postoperative issues included transient hemodynamic support, conduction disease progressing to complete heart block, volume overload, anemia/thrombocytopenia, and pulmonary complications that improved with supportive care.

Patient 6

A 65-year-old man with metastatic carcinoid syndrome and carcinoid heart disease was admitted for octreotide infusion before tricuspid and pulmonic valve replacement. He had progressive right-sided volume overload, severe tricuspid and pulmonic regurgitation, and RV dilation. He reported lower-extremity swelling without dyspnea, orthopnea, chest pain, palpitations, or infectious symptoms. Examination showed jugular venous pressure of approximately 8 cm and a 4/6 holosystolic murmur. He underwent tricuspid valve replacement, pulmonic valve replacement, and anterior RV outflow tract augmentation with bovine pericardium. Postpump echocardiography showed well-seated valves with good hemodynamics. His course was uncomplicated aside from transient accelerated junctional rhythm that converted to sinus rhythm and postoperative volume management; he was discharged home in stable condition. Pulmonic valve pathology showed carcinoid plaques and mild free-edge leaflet retraction.

Discussion

This case series demonstrates a rare and clinically important Doppler pattern in carcinoid heart disease: EDFF through the pulmonic valve. Across the series, the key observation was not simply pulmonic regurgitation, but a discrete antegrade flow signal occurring in late diastole, after atrial electrical activation and before ventricular systole. ECG correlation is therefore essential because this signal can otherwise be mistaken for systolic forward flow.

Carcinoid valve disease creates a distinctive anatomic substrate for this finding. Fibrotic plaque deposition thickens and retracts the valve leaflets (Figure 2), limiting both systolic and diastolic mobility.1, 2, 3, 4 In the tricuspid position, this produces severe regurgitation and, in some patients, stenotic physiology. In the pulmonic position, leaflet restriction and incomplete diastolic closure create the anatomic pathway through which atrial contraction can generate antegrade flow into the pulmonary artery before ventricular systole.

The usual explanation for pulmonary artery EDFF comes from the repaired tetralogy of Fallot literature, where EDFF has been interpreted as a marker of restrictive RV physiology: Atrial contraction encounters a stiff right ventricle, raises RV end-diastolic pressure, and transmits flow into the pulmonary artery.5,6 This mechanism may also contribute in carcinoid heart disease, particularly when chronic right-sided volume overload and endocardial fibrosis impair RV compliance. However, the present series supports a more valve-centered and load-dependent mechanism in which atrial contraction drives blood from the right atrium into the RV and across an incompletely closed carcinoid pulmonic valve. Prior invasive observations by Wolfson et al8 are consistent with this interpretation, attributing diastolic RV-to-pulmonary arterial flow in carcinoid heart disease to elevated right atrial pressure, decreased RV compliance, and normal pulmonary arterial pressure.

A useful way to conceptualize EDFF in carcinoid pulmonic valve disease is as an atrial-contraction-driven right-heart flow phenomenon whose magnitude depends on both the strength of the late-diastolic driving force and the permissive valvular anatomy. Qualitatively, EDFF is expected to increase with stronger right atrial contraction, more severe tricuspid regurgitation, and more severe or functionally important pulmonic regurgitation. Severe tricuspid regurgitation may augment late-diastolic right-sided volume flux during atrial contraction, whereas severe pulmonic regurgitation reflects incomplete diastolic pulmonic valve closure, allowing this pressure wave to pass into the pulmonary artery. Conversely, EDFF is expected to decrease as pulmonary arterial diastolic pressure rises, because higher downstream pressure reduces the RV-to-pulmonary arterial gradient available during atrial contraction. This framework also explains why EDFF requires organized atrial systole: the signal should be most apparent in normal sinus rhythm with effective right atrial contraction and may be attenuated or absent when atrial contraction is weak, poorly timed, or disorganized, as in atrial fibrillation (Figure 4). This concept also supports EDFF as primarily a right-heart phenomenon. During late diastole, right atrial contraction can raise right atrial and RV pressure above pulmonary arterial diastolic pressure, permitting antegrade flow through a noncoapting pulmonic valve. The finding has practical implications for perioperative echocardiography. First, a late-diastolic antegrade signal occurring after the P wave and before the QRS complex should not be labeled as systolic forward flow. Second, EDFF can interfere with pulmonic regurgitation grading. The pulmonic regurgitation index is calculated as the duration of pulmonic regurgitation divided by total diastolic time, with values below 0.77 supporting severe pulmonic regurgitation.9 If EDFF is not recognized as a separate end-diastolic event, the duration and severity of pulmonic regurgitation may be underestimated. Third, EDFF should prompt careful inspection of pulmonic valve morphology because, in carcinoid heart disease, it may indicate severe leaflet retraction and incomplete closure rather than isolated RV restriction.

This series also illustrates how multimodality perioperative imaging clarifies a subtle physiologic finding. Two-dimensional imaging establishes fixed and retracted valve morphology; color Doppler identifies end-diastolic flow across the pulmonic valve; continuous-wave Doppler confirms the reproducible late-diastolic antegrade signal; and color M-mode can help separate the late-diastolic flow component from systolic forward flow. Together, these tools support EDFF through the pulmonic valve as a reproducible Doppler finding in advanced carcinoid valve disease.

Conclusions

EDFF through the pulmonic valve can occur in carcinoid heart disease with clinically significant pulmonic valve involvement. In this setting, EDFF likely reflects the interaction of sinus rhythm, effective right atrial contraction, right-sided regurgitant loading, altered RV diastolic properties, and incomplete pulmonic valve closure. Accurate recognition requires integration of ECG timing, color Doppler, spectral Doppler, color M-mode when available, and valve morphology.
