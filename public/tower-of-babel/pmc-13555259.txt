The expression of stemness markers in organoids according to histological subtypes of gastric cancer
Authors: Yie-Ri Yoo, Kyoungyun Jeong, Jaeun Yoo, Hyun Myong Kim, Ji-Yeon Shin, Min Kyu Kang, Kyoyoung Park, Sa-Hong Kim, Chungyoon Kim, Jeesun Kim, Young Suk Park, Seong-Ho Kong, Hyuk-Joon Lee, Han-Kwang Yang, Do Joong Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeGastric cancer is a heterogeneous disease in which cancer stem cell-associated properties may contribute to tumor heterogeneity and progression. This study aimed to establish paired normal and tumor gastric organoids, characterize stemness-related gene expression, and explore its association with organoid formation.MethodsNormal and tumor tissues were obtained from 12 patients with advanced gastric cancer. A total of 28 independent organoid cultures were established from 24 tissue specimens (12 normal and 12 tumor), with selected serial strip specimens divided into segments and cultured independently. Expression of stemness-associated markers (AQP5, CD44, ALDH1A, STMN1, and SOX2) was evaluated by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction and compared according to tissue origin, histological subtype, and organoid formation status.ResultsNormal organoids tended to exhibit higher CD44 expression, whereas tumor-derived organoids showed relatively higher ALDH1A expression, although these differences were not statistically significant. Margin-derived normal organoids tended to show higher expression of multiple stemness-associated markers than those derived from tissue adjacent to tumors. Diffuse-type organoids tended to show higher CD44 expression, whereas AQP5, ALDH1A, STMN1, and SOX2 tended to be higher in intestinal-type organoids. Organoid-forming cultures consistently showed higher stemness-marker expression than non-forming cultures, particularly among tumor-derived organoids.ConclusionStemness-associated gene expression varied according to tissue origin, histological subtype, and organoid formation status. Higher stemness-marker expression in organoid-forming cultures suggests a potential association between stemness-related programs and organoid-forming capacity.

INTRODUCTION

Gastric cancer is a common malignancy with a high incidence rate in the Republic of Korea and ranks as the fifth most common cancer worldwide. Owing to its clinical and biological diversity, extensive research efforts have been devoted to elucidating the heterogeneity of gastric cancer. Indeed, gastric cancer is a highly heterogeneous disease encompassing diverse histological, molecular, and clinicopathological features. To better characterize this heterogeneity, several classification systems have been developed, including Lauren’s classification (intestinal and diffuse types) and the World Health Organization (WHO) classification [1].

Among various research findings, cancer stem cells (CSCs) have been identified as key contributors to this heterogeneity and are known to be associated with tumor initiation, recurrence, and resistance to chemotherapy [2,3]. Accordingly, extensive efforts have been made to develop therapeutic strategies targeting CSCs, accompanied by intensive research into stemness-associated and CSC-related genes. As a result, several molecular markers associated with cancer stemness, including CD44, ALDH1A, and SOX2, have been widely investigated as putative CSC markers across multiple cancer types [4]. CD44 is a well-established CSC-associated marker in a wide range of solid malignancies and is known to play critical roles in cell-cell interactions, cell adhesion, and migration [5,6]. Aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member A (ALDH1A) is another representative stem cell marker that has been implicated in cancer development, progression, and resistance to anticancer therapies [7,8]. Sex-determining region Y-box 2 (SOX2) is a transcription factor essential for the maintenance of stem cell self-renewal, and its overexpression has been reported in various cancer types [9–11]. Stathmin 1 (STMN1) is a microtubule-regulating protein associated with cellular proliferation. Recent studies have identified STMN1 as a marker of actively cycling gastric isthmus stem cells that contribute to gastric epithelial homeostasis [12]. Aquaporin 5 (AQP5), a member of the membrane water channel protein family, regulates transmembrane water transport. Notably, AQP5-positive stem cells have been shown to possess the capacity to initiate organoid formation in vitro [13,14].

Organoids are three-dimensional culture models generated from stem cells or primary tissues that possess the ability to self-organize and replicate essential structural and functional characteristics of the original organ [15]. Organoids can be maintained long-term and cryopreserved, allowing them to serve as a widely applicable platform for cancer research. Patient-derived organoids can provide the platform for studying cancer biology in a patient-specific manner [16]. However, the connection between the success rate of organoid establishment and stemness of the cells dissociated from the original tissue remains incompletely understood.

In this study, we aimed to establish paired normal and tumor gastric organoids from gastrectomy specimens and to characterize the expression patterns of CSC-related markers in these organoids. We further explored whether marker expression was associated with organoid establishment outcomes.

METHODS

L-WRN conditioned medium

L-WRN cells (ATCC, CRL-3276), L cells stably expressing Wnt3A, R-spondin 3, and Noggin, were used to generate medium for organoid culture. L-WRN cells were seeded in 150-mm cell culture plates (TPP, 93150) and subjected to selection with G418 (Sigma, 4727878001) and hygromycin B (STEMCELL Technologies, 3813). Only surviving cells were subsequently expanded, and the conditioned medium was collected and stored at −80 °C until use.

Basal medium

The basal medium, composed of Advanced DMEM (Gibco, 12491-015) supplemented with HEPES (Gibco, 15630-080) and GlutaMAX (Gibco, 35050-061), was used for the preparation of complete culture medium as well as for multiple washing and centrifugation steps during organoid handling.

Establishment of gastric organoid

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. H-2208-114-1351) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved in the study.

Both normal gastric tissues and gastric cancer tissues used for organoid establishment were obtained from 12 patients who underwent gastrectomy for gastric cancer treatment, with informed consent (Table 1). A total of 28 independent gastric organoid cultures were established from 24 tissue specimens (12 normal and 12 tumor specimens). One tumor-derived culture (ST011) was excluded because fungal contamination precluded reliable assessment of organoid establishment. All tumor specimens were obtained from patients with advanced gastric cancer. Normal tissue-derived organoids were designated using the prefix “SN,” whereas tumor-derived organoids were designated using the prefix “ST.” Matching SN and ST samples originated from the same patient. For Cases 9 and 12 (SN009 and SN012), normal tissues were obtained as serial strip specimens extending from the tumor-adjacent region toward the surgical resection margin. The strip specimens were subsequently divided into consecutive segments of approximately 1 cm in length prior to organoid establishment. Each segment was cultured independently and processed separately for RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis. Segment 1 corresponded to the tissue region closest to the tumor lesion, whereas higher-numbered segments represented progressively more distant regions of the strip specimen (Fig. 1A). For organoid cultures preserved in multiple cryovials, each cryovial was thawed and re-cultured independently. Following post-thaw recovery, cultures were classified according to their ability to re-establish three-dimensional organoid structures. Organoid-forming and non-forming cultures were sampled separately for downstream RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analyses when sufficient material was available. The dissected fresh tissue was stored in MACS tissue storage solution (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-100-008) before cell dissociation. For a subset of cases, a portion of the stored tissue was separated prior to cell dissociation, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution in PBS (GeneAll, SM-P01-100), and processed into formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded blocks for histopathological evaluation. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was subsequently performed, and representative images are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. Cells were dissociated from the tissue using a dissociation medium supplemented with 5 mg/mL collagenase type IV (Gibco, 17104-019) and 10 μg/mL DNase I (STEMCELL Technologies, 07469_C). After dissociation, cells were filtered through a 100-μm cell strainer (SPL, 93100), washed repeatedly, and collected as a pellet. The isolated cells were suspended in Matrigel (Corning, 356231) and seeded into 48-well culture plates (TPP, 92048) as dome-shaped drops for 3D culture. The culture medium was prepared by supplementing the basal medium with L-WRN (ATCC, CRL-3276) conditioned medium and additional supplements, including EGF (PeproTech, AF-100-15), FGF (PeproTech, 100-26), gastrin (Sigma, G9145), SB202190 (Sigma, S7067), Y27632 (Tocris, 1254), and additional supplements as described in Table 2. The medium was added to each well in a volume sufficient to fully cover the Matrigel domes (Fig. 1A). Organoids were passaged every 5–7 days. The initial culture was defined as passage 0 (P0), and cultures were maintained up to passage 5 or 6 (P5–P6). During organoid passaging, the culture medium was aspirated using a pipette, and 500 μL of Organoid Harvesting Solution (R&D Systems, 3700-100-01) was added to each well to detach the Matrigel dome. The suspension was transferred to a 5-mL tube (Chembio, CB9004) and incubated on ice for 1 hour. After incubation, the organoids were washed with basal medium and centrifuged as a pellet. For long-term storage, organoids from each passage were stocked in 2-mL cryogenic vials (SPL, 43022) and stored in liquid nitrogen.

Organoid selection for RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Following organoid establishment and expansion, cryopreserved organoids with sufficient remaining stock were selected for RNA extraction. Organoids with limited cryopreserved material were excluded to preserve samples for future studies.

For RNA extraction, cryopreserved organoids were thawed and re-cultured in Matrigel domes. After a recovery period, organoid morphology was evaluated. In some cases, organoids successfully re-formed three-dimensional structures following thawing, whereas others failed to re-establish organoid morphology and remained as adherent cells or cell aggregates. RNA was extracted after this post-thaw recovery period, and samples were classified according to their organoid formation status at the time of RNA collection.

Only RNA samples with sufficient quantity and quality for cDNA synthesis were included in downstream qRT-PCR analyses. Following RNA quality assessment, 18 RNA samples were ultimately included in the final qRT-PCR analysis. A workflow illustrating patient enrollment, organoid culture establishment, RNA extraction, sample selection and inclusion in the final qRT-PCR analysis is shown in Fig. 1B. The establishment status, expansion outcome, RNA extraction status, exclusion criteria, and number of RNA samples analyzed for each organoid culture are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Extraction of total RNA, RT-PCR, qRT-PCR

Organoids were harvested using Organoid Harvesting Solution (R&D Systems, 3700-100-01) and washed with basal medium. Following centrifugation, organoid pellets were collected for RNA extraction. After removal of the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026), and total RNA was extracted. Extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (Enzynomics, EZ005S). qRT-PCR was subsequently performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, 4309155) on a 96-well reaction plate (Applied Biosystems, 4346906). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. Technical qRT-PCR replicates were averaged before calculation of ΔCt values and subsequent statistical analyses. GAPDH was used as the endogenous housekeeping gene for normalization. Relative gene expression levels presented in the figures were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table 3.

Statistical analysis

For qRT-PCR analyses, mean ΔCt values derived from triplicate measurements were used for statistical comparisons. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparison test when appropriate. A two-sided P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Organoid establishment and sample selection for qRT-PCR

Normal and tumor tissues were obtained from 12 patients with gastric cancer. A total of 28 independent gastric organoid cultures were established from 24 tissue specimens (12 normal and 12 tumor specimens), including independently cultured segments derived from serial strip specimens in selected cases. One tumor-derived organoid culture (ST011) was excluded from downstream analyses because of fungal contamination during culture. For molecular analyses, only cultures with sufficient cryopreserved material were selected for RNA extraction. RNA extraction was performed on 32 independently processed cryopreserved samples. Following RNA quality assessment, 18 RNA samples met the quality and quantity requirements for cDNA synthesis and were included in the final qRT-PCR analysis. Of these, 12 RNA samples were derived from normal organoids and six from tumor-derived gastric organoids.

Among the thawed cryopreserved cultures, some organoid samples successfully re-formed three-dimensional organoid structures, whereas others failed to re-establish organoid morphology. RNA samples used for the organoid establishment outcome analysis were collected after post-thaw recovery and classified according to their observed organoid formation status.

Expression patterns of stemness-associated markers in gastric organoids

The mRNA expression levels of five stem cell-associated markers (AQP5, CD44, ALDH1A, STMN1, and SOX2) were evaluated by qRT-PCR. Comparative analysis between normal and tumor-derived organoids revealed trends in stemness-marker expression patterns (Fig. 2A). CD44 expression tended to be higher in normal gastric organoids than in tumor-derived organoids, whereas ALDH1A expression showed the opposite tendency, with relatively higher expression levels observed in tumor organoids. In contrast, AQP5, STMN1, and SOX2 expression levels did not demonstrate marked differences between the two groups. However, none of these comparisons reached statistical significance.

To further investigate heterogeneity within normal gastric tissues, we compared normal organoids derived from tissue adjacent to the tumor lesion with those established from margin sites obtained after gastrectomy (Fig. 2B). Margin-derived normal organoids tended to exhibit higher relative mRNA expression levels of AQP5, ALDH1A, STMN1, and SOX2 than normal organoids derived from tissue adjacent to the tumor lesion. Although these differences did not reach statistical significance, the observed expression patterns suggest potential spatial heterogeneity of stemness-related gene expression within histologically normal gastric tissues.

Among tumor-derived gastric organoids, expression patterns of stemness-associated markers were further evaluated according to anatomical location and histological subtype. When stratified by anatomical location, organoids derived from the cardia, body, and antrum exhibited variable expression patterns (Fig. 2C). AQP5 expression appeared to be higher in cardia-derived tumor organoids, whereas CD44, ALDH1A, STMN1, and SOX2 expression levels showed no consistent location-specific trends. Owing to the limited number of organoids available in each subgroup, these findings were considered descriptive and were not subjected to formal statistical interpretation.

When stratified by histological subtype, diffuse-type gastric organoids tended to exhibit higher CD44 expression than intestinal-type organoids, whereas AQP5, ALDH1A, STMN1, and SOX2 tended to show higher expression levels in intestinal-type gastric organoids (Fig. 2D). However, none of these differences reached statistical significance.

To further explore spatial heterogeneity within histologically normal gastric tissue, serial strip specimens were obtained from tumor-adjacent normal mucosa and divided into consecutive segments extending toward the surgical resection margin in two cases (SN009 and SN012). Independent organoid cultures were established from each segment, and stemness-associated marker expression was subsequently evaluated (Fig. 2E). Although formal statistical analysis was not performed because only a single organoid culture was available for each segment, segment-specific differences in gene expression were observed within both cases. Consistent with the comparison between normal and tumor organoids, normal gastric organoids established from tissue adjacent to the tumor lesion tended to exhibit relatively higher CD44 expression. In contrast, ALDH1A expression showed variable patterns across sequential segments, and the expression levels of AQP5, STMN1, and SOX2 also differed according to segment location. These findings suggest potential spatial heterogeneity of stemness-associated marker expression within histologically normal gastric tissues adjacent to gastric cancer.

To investigate factors associated with organoid formation, gene expression levels were compared between organoid-forming and non-forming cultures (Fig. 2F). Across all organoid-derived RNA samples, organoid-forming cultures tended to exhibit higher expression levels of AQP5, CD44, ALDH1A, STMN1, and SOX2 than non-forming cultures. A similar pattern was observed when the analysis was restricted to tumor-derived organoids (Fig. 2G). Tumor organoid-forming cultures consistently showed higher expression levels of all five stemness-associated markers compared with non-forming cultures. Although statistical significance was not achieved because of the limited sample size, the observed trends suggest that increased expression of stemness-associated genes may be associated with successful organoid formation and maintenance in vitro.

DISCUSSION

In this study, a total of 28 independent gastric organoid cultures were established from normal and tumor tissues obtained from patients with gastric cancer. We established paired gastric normal and gastric tumor organoids from multiple anatomical locations and histological subtypes of gastric cancer and investigated the expression patterns of stemness-associated markers. Our results suggest that stemness-associated gene expression exhibits heterogeneous patterns according to tissue origin, histological subtype, spatial location within the stomach, and organoid formation outcome, highlighting the biological heterogeneity of gastric epithelial cells.

One notable finding was the tendency toward higher CD44 expression in normal gastric organoids compared with tumor organoids, particularly in organoids derived from tissue adjacent to tumor lesions. CD44 has been widely reported as a CSC marker in gastric cancer [5,6]; however, our results suggest that its expression is not restricted to malignant cells. Increased CD44 expression in histologically normal tissue near tumor sites may reflect a field cancerization effect, in which surrounding epithelial cells acquire stem-like or pre-neoplastic features due to chronic inflammation, molecular alterations, or tumor-microenvironment interactions. This observation underscores the importance of spatial context when interpreting stemness-associated markers in gastric tissues.

In contrast, ALDH1A expression was consistently higher in tumor-derived organoids, aligning with previous reports linking ALDH1A activity to tumorigenicity, chemoresistance, and CSC properties [7,8]. Interestingly, ALDH1A expression showed heterogeneity among normal organoids derived from different regions, suggesting that stemness-related programs may already be variably activated in non-malignant gastric epithelium. Such heterogeneity may contribute to differences in tumor initiation potential and warrants further investigation.

We also observed distinct expression trends between intestinal-type and diffuse-type gastric organoids. CD44 expression was higher in diffuse-type organoids, whereas AQP5, ALDH1A, STMN1, and SOX2 tended to be more highly expressed in intestinal-type organoids. These subtype-specific patterns may reflect the molecular heterogeneity of gastric cancer [1] and are consistent with previous studies suggesting that stemness-associated mechanisms contribute to gastric cancer biology and progression [2–4].

Although formal statistical analysis was not performed because of the limited number of samples available in each anatomical subgroup, organoids derived from different gastric regions exhibited variable expression patterns of stemness-associated markers. In addition, sequentially segmented normal tissues obtained from tumor-adjacent mucosa toward the surgical margin demonstrated heterogeneous marker expression profiles, further supporting the presence of spatial heterogeneity within histologically normal gastric tissues.

Importantly, comparison between organoid-forming and non-forming cultures revealed that all analyzed stemness-associated markers were expressed at higher levels in organoid-forming cultures. Although limited by sample size, this observation suggests a potential association between stemness-related programs and successful organoid formation and maintenance in vitro. While stemness-associated marker expression was not assessed in the corresponding primary tissues before organoid establishment, organoid-forming cultures consistently exhibited higher expression levels of all analyzed markers than non-forming cultures. However, because gene expression was evaluated after post-thaw recovery, the present study cannot determine whether elevated stemness-associated marker expression preceded successful organoid formation or resulted from selective expansion of stem-like cells during culture. Therefore, the observed relationship should be interpreted as an association rather than evidence of a causal effect.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small, particularly for subtype- and location-stratified analyses, which may limit the statistical power to detect subtle differences. Second, stemness-associated marker expression was evaluated only in organoid cultures. Expression analyses were not performed in the corresponding primary gastric tissues used for organoid establishment; therefore, it remains unclear to what extent the observed expression patterns reflect the original tissue characteristics. Comparative analyses between primary tissues and matched organoids would help clarify whether organoid cultures faithfully preserve the stemness-associated gene expression profiles of the source tissues. Third, gene expression was assessed at the mRNA level only, and protein-level validation was not performed in this study. Fourth, molecular confirmation of tumor identity, such as mutation profiling, immunohistochemical validation, or comparison with the corresponding primary tumors, was not performed. Although tumor and normal tissues were collected based on gross anatomical assessment during specimen processing, representative H&E images of the source tissues are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. However, the possibility of normal epithelial overgrowth in tumor-derived organoid cultures cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the observed differences between normal and tumor organoids should be interpreted with caution. Future studies incorporating molecular validation of tumor identity, larger patient cohorts, protein-level analyses, and functional assays will be necessary to validate and extend our findings.

Despite these limitations, our study provides insight into the spatial, histological, and biological heterogeneity of stemness-associated gene expression in gastric organoids. The observed expression trends according to tissue origin, histological subtype, and organoid formation status suggest that stemness-associated programs may be associated with gastric epithelial biology and gastric cancer heterogeneity. These findings may improve our understanding of gastric cancer heterogeneity and support the use of patient-derived organoid models for investigating stemness-related mechanisms in gastric cancer.
