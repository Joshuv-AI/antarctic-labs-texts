Arabic version of the General Sleep Disturbance Scale among healthcare night-shift workers: a cross-sectional validation study
Authors: Wesam Osama, Rehab Magdy, Azza Elashiry, Mona Hussein, Mennat-Allah Tarek, Sohaila Mohamed Mohamed Abdelbar, Samah Ali Ahmed Ismail, Basma Hussein Mourad, Mohamed Tharwat Hegazy, Asmaa Farrag, Hoda M Abdel-Hamid, Sarah Heneidy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesThe General Sleep Disturbance Scale (GSDS) has been translated and amended into various languages with adequate psychometric properties. However, the Arabic version has not been validated before. Therefore, this study aimed to test the validity and reliability of the Arabic GSDS among a sample of healthcare night-shift workers.MethodsThis cross-sectional validation study employed an online survey targeting Egyptian healthcare night-shift workers from April to June 2025. The survey included demographic data and the Arabic versions of GSDS and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).ResultsFour hundred forty-three healthcare night-shift workers with a median age of 29 (25–32) years and a median of 6 (4–8) shifts/month were enrolled. The GSDS had an excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.841). The intraclass correlation coefficient for GSDS was 0.978 (95% CI 0.971 to 0.983), indicating excellent test-retest reliability. The convergent validity was confirmed by the significant positive correlation found between total scores of GSDS and PSQI (r=0.501). Moreover, a fair degree of significant correlations were observed between GSDS and sleep onset latency, sleep duration, sleep disturbances, daytime dysfunction and sleep quality (r=0.324, 0.304, 0.409, 0.441 and 0.353, respectively), while the correlations were of weak degree with each of sleep efficiency and sleep medication use sections (r=0.271 and 0.231, respectively).ConclusionThis study confirmed the excellent reliability as well as the convergent validity of the Arabic GSDS. Therefore, it can be used to assist in the detection of sleep disturbances in Arabic-speaking adults.Keywords: SLEEP, SLEEP DISORDERS

Introduction

Sleep is a crucial physiological process that influences mental and physical health and is vital for the proper functioning of all human body systems, including the immunological, endocrine, cardiovascular and nervous systems.1 The quality of sleep is a critical determinant of overall health and well-being. Additionally, the consequences of sleep disturbance extend beyond the individual, potentially impacting social aspects, including public health and economic burdens.2 So, there is a need for accurate and reliable scales to measure sleep disturbances since it is mandatory to comprehend the connections between these comorbidities and sleep disturbances.

Although there are 300 million individuals who speak Arabic as their first language in the Middle East and North Africa, there is a lack of comprehensive evidence concerning the prevalence of sleep disorders within this demographic. The primary reason for this may be attributed to the absence of sleep disturbance scales that have been specifically designed for Arabic-speaking populations.3 Three sleep disturbance scales have been translated and validated into Arabic: the Epworth Sleepiness Scale,4 the Insomnia Severity Index5 and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).6 The majority of these scales are designed to assess particular sleep issues, such as daytime sleepiness with the Epworth Sleepiness Scale4 and insomnia through the Insomnia Severity Scale,5 while ignoring other facets of sleep. The PSQI scale, while more detailed and comprehensive than other Arabic-validated scales, does not adequately address the rapid changes in sleep status, as it assesses sleep problems over the past month.6

The criteria for determining a measurement of sleep should be aligned with the objectives of the study and the specific variable that the researcher aims to analyse. The authors chose to examine the psychometric properties of the General Sleep Disturbance Scale (GSDS)7 among Egyptian healthcare workers on night shifts, as it is regarded as one of the most effective tools for this purpose due to its easy scoring system. Unlike other tools, GSDS evaluates sleep status over the preceding week, effectively capturing rapid changes in clinical conditions and participant health, thereby making it appropriate for epidemiological studies.8 The GSDS is a tool that has undergone translation and validation in several languages, including Italian,9 Turkish10 and Chinese.11 Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the reliability and validity of the Arabic version of the GSDS among Egyptian healthcare night-shift workers. The findings will assist researchers in the future to examine sleep disturbances easily and comprehensively among both healthy and diseased individuals in Arab countries.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional survey was conducted among Egyptian healthcare workers from April to June 2025.

The study employed an online survey targeting all positions of night-shift healthcare workers, including nurses, house officers, residents and assistant lecturers.

Individuals with chronic diseases known to cause sleep disturbances, such as thyroid dysfunction, psychiatric disorders or autoimmune diseases, were excluded from this study. Additionally, those currently receiving hypnotic medications were also excluded from the study. A flow diagram describing invitations, exclusions and retest assessment was demonstrated in figure 1.

Measures

Through various Egyptian medical Facebook and WhatsApp groups, and after introducing the research purpose and obtaining electronic consent to participate, healthcare night-shift workers were invited to complete a survey designed on a Google form.

The survey included the following:

Demographic data (age, sex, medical position).

Demographic data (age, sex, medical position).

The General Sleep Disturbance Scale (GSDS).7It comprises 21 items, each of which was scored based on a Likert-type scale, ranging from 0 (no days) to 7 (every day). The total score ranges from 0 to 147; higher scores indicate greater sleep disturbances.

The General Sleep Disturbance Scale (GSDS).7

It comprises 21 items, each of which was scored based on a Likert-type scale, ranging from 0 (no days) to 7 (every day). The total score ranges from 0 to 147; higher scores indicate greater sleep disturbances.

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).6It is the most commonly used sleep quality index that evaluates sleep disturbance in the past month. The PSQI consists of 24 questions that cover the following aspects: sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep activity, sleep disorders, use of sleeping pills and daytime sleep dysfunction. Higher scores indicate worse sleep quality. Validity and reliability of the Arabic version of PSQI have been examined previously.12

In the invitation email, each participant was given a numeric code and was re-enrolled when completing the survey for the second time 2 weeks later, for test-retest reliability.

Translation

Permission was obtained from the developers of the GSDS, and the original version of the GSDS was translated by two health professionals who are fluent English speakers. Two additional health professionals who are fluent in English back-translated the Arabic version into English to compare it with the original version, until they had compiled the final Arabic version of the scale (online supplemental appendix).

Sample size calculation

The number of items in GSDS was 21. A priori, a 20:1 patient‐to‐item ratio was considered satisfactory to obtain a sufficient sample size for the study.13 Therefore, the required sample size was determined to be 420 participants.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) V.26 (IBM Corp). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to test the normality of data. Internal consistency of the GSDS was measured using Cronbach’s alpha.

The Spearman’s rho correlation coefficient was explored to assess the convergent validity between the GSDS and PSQI. A correlation coefficient of 0.1–0.2 was considered weak, 0.3–0.5 fair, 0.6–0.7 moderate and 0.8–0.9 very strong and 1 perfect.14

The test-retest reliability of the GSDS was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and both single and average measures will be reported. Values below 0.5 indicate poor reliability, between 0.5 and 0.75 questionable reliability, 0.75 and 0.9 good reliability and any value above 0.9 indicates excellent reliability.15

Results

This cross-sectional study was conducted on 443 participants with a median age of 29 (25–32) years. It included 108 (24.4%) house officers, 162 (36.6%) residents, 97 (21.9%) assistant lecturers, 23 (5.2%) clinical pharmacists and 53 (12%) nurses. The median value of the number of night shifts/month was 6 (4–8) shifts. Regarding sleep scales, the median value for PSQI total score was 9 (6–12) and for GSDS total score was 48 (37–62) (tables1 2).

Reliability of GSDS

The Cronbach’s α coefficient value of the GSDS total score was 0.841, indicating excellent internal consistency (table 3). Regarding the characteristics of the 222 participants who completed the retest, the median age was 30 (26–34) years. Most participants were male (n=139, 62.6%), while female constituted 83 participants (37.4%). As for job categories, residents represented the majority of the participants (n=91, 41.0%), followed by assistant lecturers (n=65, 29.3%), house officers (n=39, 17.6%), nurses (n=22, 9.9%) and clinical pharmacists (n=5, 2.3%). The ICC (average measure) for GSDS total score was 0.978 with a 95% CI (0.971 to 0.983), indicating excellent test-retest reliability (table 4).

Convergent validity of GSDS

There was a statistically significant positive correlation between GSDS total score and the number of shifts/month (r=0.186). There were statistically significant positive correlations of a weak degree between GSDS total score and each of sleep efficiency and sleep medication use (r=0.271 and 0.231, respectively). On the other hand, statistically significant positive correlations of a fair degree between GSDS total score and other PSQI components, including sleep onset latency, sleep duration, sleep disturbances, daytime dysfunction and sleep quality (r=0.324, 0.304, 0.409, 0.441 and 0.353, respectively). However, there was a statistically significant positive correlation of moderate degree between GSDS total score and PSQI total score (r=0.501) (table 5).

Discussion

This study indicates that the Arabic version of the GSDS is a reliable and valid tool for evaluating sleep disturbances among a sample of Egyptian healthcare workers. The tool demonstrates a high level of internal consistency, evidenced by a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.841. This value is comparable to the 0.88 reported for the English version in Lee’s original 1992 study.7 The results of both studies are largely consistent, as they both examined the target population of healthcare workers with shifts. The original study focused on nurses, whereas our research encompassed all healthcare workers. The internal consistency of the Arabic version is comparable to that of the Chinese version (0.81),11 which included parents of hospitalised infants and is similar to the Turkish version,10 which involved stroke patients (0.85). However, the internal consistency of the Arabic version is superior to that of the Italian version (0.77),9 which included participants from various employment sectors across Italy.

The Arabic version of GSDS exhibits excellent test-retest reliability (ICC=0.957), in contrast to the Italian9 and Turkish10 versions, which both had a coefficient of 0.78. The observed disparity can be ascribed to the demographic characteristics of participants in each study. For example, the Turkish study included stroke patients, with assessments conducted on admission and 24 hours later, potentially leading to some degree of improvement among the patients.

For test-retest reliability assessment, a 2-week interval is the standard and most commonly used time interval, as it balances between reducing recall of answers that happens with too short intervals and minimising case changes that may happen with longer intervals. Moreover, it is commonly used for health-related quality of life, psychology and behavioural questionnaires.16

The correlation with the PSQI and all components proved the convergent validity of Arabic GSDS.

Notably, the correlation with the PSQI-total score was of a moderate degree. The differing timeframes of each scale can explain this; the PSQI assesses sleep disturbances over the past month, whereas the GSDS evaluates rapid changes in sleep during the last week.

On the other hand, the varying methodologies employed in the formulation of questions on each scale might elucidate the strength of correlations found between Arabic GSDS and certain components of PSQI. Using a precise formula for calculating habitual sleep efficiency in PSQI (actual sleep hours×100)/hours spent in bed,17 which is not used in GSDS, may account for the significant correlations of a weak degree found between Arabic GSDS and the sleep efficiency component of PSQI (r=0.27).

Conversely, using sleep aids was extensively covered by GSDS across six items, while it was only addressed by one question in the PSQI. This may account for a weak positive correlation between the sleep medications component and the total GSDS score found in this study (r=0.231). Another explanation might be provided, as many healthcare workers use adaptation programmes to adjust to night shifts instead of relying on sleep medications.18 19

In comparison to other studies, the Italian GSDS demonstrated a stronger positive correlation with the use of sleeping medications, as well as the sleep efficiency components of the PSQI, with values of 0.49 and 0.34, respectively.9 The observed differences may be attributed to the age variation among participants in each study. In our study, participants’ ages ranged from 25 to 32 years, whereas in the aforementioned Italian study, 56% of participants were over 47 years. Sleep disturbances are prevalent among older adults due to the association of ageing with a higher incidence of multimorbidity, polypharmacy, psychosocial influences on sleep and specific primary sleep disorders.20

In comparison to the Turkish study, a stronger positive correlation was observed between the sleep efficiency domain, use of sleeping medication domain, sleep quality domain and the total score of the PSQI, compared with our study, with values of 0.463, 0.495, 0.64 and 0.76, respectively. The disparity can be attributed to the demographics of the participants in the two studies. The Turkish study included stroke patients aged 28–85 years. Previous research has identified that sleep disturbances are prevalent in stroke patients, including insomnia, hypersomnia, breathing-related sleep disorders, as well as a need for sleep medication.21 22

Study limitations

This study has limitations; it solely compares the Arabic version of GSDS with PSQI. Other objective measurements, such as actigraphy, were not included for precise estimates of sleep disorders. Additionally, future research is necessary to investigate further the relationships between sleep disturbances and their effects on sleep efficacy, dependency, and physical and emotional health. Second, recruitment via social media leads to significant selection bias, over-representing young people and under-rating those with limited digital literacy, thus limiting the generalisability of the results. Information bias is an additional limitation, as surveys may be completed by individuals who do not meet strict inclusion criteria. Finally, some participants may provide dishonest answers, either exaggerating positive behaviours or downplaying negative ones, to conform to social norms or appear more agreeable to the researcher, which constitutes response bias.23 Third, the specification of the sample population may be seen as a restriction in this study, impacting the external validity of the test. The population validity, a form of external validity, is significantly influenced by the sampling process, which evaluates the extent to which findings from a sample can be extended to a broader target population. Subsequent studies will include non-healthcare groups.

Conclusion

The Arabic GSDS demonstrated validity and reliability as a measurement scale. The assessment is straightforward and can be completed in less than 5 min using a non-invasive method to evaluate sleep disturbance. The inquiry focuses on sleep experiences over a week that effectively captures rapid changes in clinical conditions and participant health, making it appropriate for epidemiological studies.
