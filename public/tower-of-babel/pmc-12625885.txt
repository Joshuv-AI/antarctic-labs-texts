Why health services should use generic PROMs and PREMs
Authors: Tim Benson
Published: 2025

Introduction

Understanding the effectiveness of treatment and experience is key to improving health outcomes and service delivery. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) provide one way of doing this. These questionnaires allow healthcare providers to collect information about patients’ views of their outcomes and the experience they have received.1 With feedback from these measures, healthcare organisations can create care that is more patient-centred, improve service delivery and promote informed decision-making. However, despite their benefits, few healthcare services have succeeded in implementing them effectively.2

This paper explores the benefits of generic PROMs and PREMs and challenges to their implementation. The focus of PROMs is on the patient. PROMs help healthcare providers track each patient’s progress over time, compare pretreatment with post-treatment outcomes and support shared decision-making.3 For example, a patient receiving physiotherapy for knee pain may fill out a PROM before and after treatment. If reported pain levels do not improve, the provider and patient can work together to change the treatment plan.

The focus of PREMs is on the provider organisation. PREMs measure patients’ experience of the healthcare services received.4 They can cover communication between the patients and their doctors and nurses, the time spent waiting for appointments or treatments, safety concerns, hygiene and the level of respect and dignity shown by staff to patients. For example, if patients in a hospital consistently report long wait times before being seen, managers can use this feedback to make improvements.5

PROMs measure health outcomes, such as changes in well-being or health status, while PREMs focus on the institution and on how well the patient is treated.6 PROMs show the success or otherwise of treatment, while PREMs highlight areas where the provider’s service might be improved. Taken together, they provide a picture of healthcare quality as it affects patients.

Generic measures

Generic PROMs and PREMs apply across all adult patient populations, medical conditions and treatments. Generic measures assess each patient’s health and healthcare experience in the round, while condition-specific measures focus on a single condition (such as diabetes or heart disease).7 Condition-specific measures have been used successfully in clinical trials to compare one type of treatment against another for a given disease. However, this disease must be diagnosed accurately. Condition-specific measures cannot work if the diagnosis is unknown or when the patient has multiple diagnoses.

Generic PROMs and PREMs help in healthcare settings where patients may have either multiple conditions or undiagnosed symptoms, such as in primary healthcare or emergency medicine. They are not tied to a specific condition, so the results allow comparison across different diseases, treatments and healthcare settings. Policy makers need generic measures to assess healthcare performance at the population level. Generic PROMs and PREMs help them identify which services provide the best value for money and where improvements are needed.8

Applications

PROMs help clinicians to track patients’ progress and make informed treatment decisions. For example, if a patient having mental health treatment reports worsening symptoms through a PROM, his or her medication dosage can be adjusted accordingly.9 PROMs encourage two-way communication between patients and clinicians about needs, preferences and treatment responses. When patients participate actively in healthcare decisions, they are more likely to be satisfied with treatment.10

Shared decision-making involves patients and healthcare professionals collaborating to select the best treatment options.11 PROMs empower patients by providing them with insights into their health, being more comfortable with medical terms and being more informed participants in their own care. For instance, if cancer patients use PROMs to track symptoms and side effects, they and their clinicians can use this information to decide whether to continue a particular chemotherapy regimen or switch to a different approach.12

Most clinicians are competitive and are keen to help their patients. Treatments and technologies need to be evaluated both for their clinical effectiveness and their impact on patients’ quality of life.13 For instance, if minimally invasive surgery for knee replacement shows faster recovery times in clinical trials, PROMs can reveal whether the pain relief and mobility improvements meet patients’ expectations as well.14

PROMs help healthcare systems assess whether treatments deliver good value for money. Payers need to decide which treatments should be funded or reimbursed, and it helps them if the benefits are reported by the patients themselves.

PROMs can track population health trends over time. For instance, PROMs data may indicate rising levels of chronic pain in a population, prompting policy makers to support new pain management programmes.15

PROMs and PREMs can be used together to evaluate service quality and identify areas for improvement.16 Providers can compare their performance against national targets and monitor the impact of targeted quality improvement initiatives. For example, a unit with a low PREM score for patient communication could introduce a training programme designed to improve doctor–patient interactions.

If the benefits of issuing PROMs and PREMs are clear, then what is preventing their widespread use? There are several obstacles and barriers which need to be overcome first.

Barriers

The barriers to use are grouped under the headings: patient-level issues, organisational-level issues and the measures themselves.

Patient-level issues

Perhaps the most important, the PROM and PREM questionnaires should be written in a language that patients clearly understand.17 One solution is to use short, easy-to-read PROMs and PREMs with a Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level of 5 or below (a reading age of 10 or less).18 If questionnaires are long and use arcane technical language, patients will not complete them accurately.

Even when the PROMs and PREMs are short and readable, patients will not complete them unless they believe that the information will be used to improve their care or that of others. Healthcare providers should always ensure that the purpose of the questionnaires is clearly explained and that feedback is provided promptly.19

In general, older patients prefer paper questionnaires, while younger patients prefer mobiles. We should always provide multiple ways to complete PROMs and PREMs (eg, paper forms, online surveys and mobile apps), with reminders if no response has been received.20 Questionnaires must be designed for all modalities. An alternative is to ask them face to face.

Organisational-level issues

Staff vary in their enthusiasm for PROMs and PREMs at all levels.8 The diffusion of any innovation depends on the human property of innovativeness. Rogers outlines five categories, ranging from innovator (2.5%), early adopter (13.5%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%) and laggard (16%).21 Most people (84%—early majority through laggards) do not welcome change. This helps explain why, in the absence of strong top-down directives, PROMs and PREMs have not spread more quickly. Clinicians will need training to ensure that they collect, use and understand patient-reported data.

PROMs need to be fully integrated into electronic health records, so that clinicians can see the results when they see patients or review their records. The primary uses of PROMs and PREMs are clinical, but managers need to understand and use the results as secondary users.22 Senior managers need to compare across departments, so they usually prefer generic measures.

PROMs are usually the responsibility of the medical director, and PREMs are the responsibility of the nursing director. However, both PROMs and PREMs should report to a board quality committee, chaired by the institution’s chief executive, which includes both the medical and nursing directors.

Measures

It helps if all measures work in the same way. For example, good practice might be that a high score is always good, a common scale (eg, 0–100) is used for population means, and common response options are used throughout.2 An example of poor practice is the English National Health Service (NHS) PROMs programme in England, which has run since 2009. Scores are reported using three different scales, which are scored from worst to best: EuroQol Five Dimenson Three Level (EQ-5D-3L) from −0.57 to 1.0, the EuroQol Viisual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS) from 0 to 100 and the Oxford hip and knee scores from 48 to 0.23 Making sense of this is difficult.24

PROMs and PREMs track individual patient data over time, so privacy concerns must always be managed. Healthcare organisations need robust data security policies to protect patient information while ensuring that PROMs and PREMs are used effectively.25

Response rates continue to fall for many of the reasons outlined above. It hinders response rates if questionnaires are long, the reading age exceeds that of many people and the results are not released soon enough. For example, one of the largest PREM surveys (the General Practice (GP) Patient Survey) was sent to 2.72 million people in 2025, but only 25.8% responded after several reminders. This compares with 44.1% in 2006.26

All measures have a noise component x. For example, if the noise is x, the Before score is (B±x), and the After score is (A±x), then the change after treatment is (B-A±2 x). If the change expected is less than twice the noise, the measure should not usually be used.27

Unfortunately, the main clinical coding systems, such as Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms (SNOMED) CT and Loigical Object Identifiers Names and Codes (LOINC), do not support the full range of concepts found in PROMs and PREMs and do not give sufficient attention to how the data were collected and by whom.28

Some of these barriers are easier than others to resolve. We cannot expect to see PROMs and PREMs widely used until this is done.

Quality of care

The WHO defines quality of care as the degree to which health services for individuals and populations increase the likelihood of desired health outcomes and are consistent with evidence-based professional knowledge.29 This implies that outcomes should be measured routinely and continuously improved.

The WHO says that health services should be effective, providing evidence-based healthcare services to those that need them; safe, avoiding harm to the people for whom care is intended; people-centred, providing care that responds to individual preferences, needs and values, within services that are organised around the needs of people; timely, reducing waiting times and sometimes harmful delays for those who seek and for those who give care; equitable, providing the same quality of care regardless of age, sex, gender, race, ethnicity, geographic location, religion, socioeconomic status, linguistic or political affiliation; integrated, providing care that is coordinated across levels and providers and makes available the full range of health services throughout the life course and efficient, maximising the benefit of available resources and avoiding waste.

Currently, most healthcare systems measure only what is done to patients rather than their health outcomes or how they experience it. PROMs and PREMs shift the focus towards outcomes and experience as measured by patients. This helps staff ensure that healthcare services benefit those they are designed to serve.

Conclusion

By systematically collecting PROMs and PREMs, healthcare providers can make more informed clinical decisions, improve quality, enhance patient-centred care, drive service improvements, support data-driven decision-making and ensure that resources are allocated effectively.

Challenges and success strategies for PROMs are similar in all countries. Success factors include the need for (a) patient focus, (b) clinical champions, (c) standards, (d) IT infrastructure, (e) incentives, backed by (f) political will.30

All the barriers to use must be addressed, including low patient engagement (needing improvements to the length and readability), the noise of PROMs and PREMs, privacy, understandability and security/privacy.

However, with the right strategies in place, generic PROMs and PREMs can enhance healthcare services in a measurable way, making them more responsive to the needs of patients. Unfortunately, many existing measures do not meet these criteria; they should be replaced with those that do.
