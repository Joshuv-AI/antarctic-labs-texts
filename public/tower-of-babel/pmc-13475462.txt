The SMART-Youth study: protocol for a longitudinal prospective cohort study to identify disease-associated and lifestyle-associated cardiovascular risk factors for preclinical atherosclerosis in children with a chronic condition
Authors: Isabelle A van der Linden, Danya Muilwijk, Julia J D Raijmakers, Marlies Destoop, Johanna W Hoefnagels, Fieke Terstappen, Michiel L Bots, Ilonca C H Vaartjes, Charlotte Koopal, Frank L J Visseren, Martijn G Slieker, Johannes M P J Breur, Joost Frenkel, Marc R Lilien, Joris van Montfrans, Annet van Royen-Kerkhof, Joost F Swart, Anemone van den Berg, Corine Koopman-Esseboom, Millie E Langemeijer, Cornelis K van der Ent, Sanne L Nijhof, Henk S Schipper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionCardiovascular risk assessment and management in the paediatric population is a relatively uncharted territory. However, atherogenesis starts during childhood, making childhood and adolescence an important window of opportunity to prevent atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) later in life. An emerging group at risk for early ASCVD are children with chronic conditions. This paper describes the rationale, design and methods for the Secondary Manifestations of ARTerial diseases in the Young with a chronic condition (SMART-Youth) study. This study aims to identify disease-associated and lifestyle-associated cardiovascular risk factors for preclinical atherosclerosis in children with a chronic condition. The results of this study may fuel development of tailored cardiovascular risk assessment and management strategies in children at-risk.Methods and analysisThis is a prospective longitudinal cohort study including children aged 8–18 years with various chronic conditions (cystic fibrosis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, systemic autoimmune disease, chronic kidney disease, primary immunodeficiency, autoinflammatory conditions, inflammatory bowel disease, congenital heart disease, premature birth, fetal growth restriction and children with persistent somatic symptoms) at the Wilhelmina Children’s Hospital of the University Medical Center Utrecht in The Netherlands. Assessment of cardiovascular risk factors includes blood pressure, body mass index, visceral and subcutaneous abdominal adipose tissue, nutrition, physical activity, stress, circulating lipids, HbA1c and C-reactive protein measurements. Preclinical atherosclerosis is measured by carotid intima-media thickness, carotid distension and carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity. These assessments are performed at baseline, 2-year follow-up and 17–18 years of age. Enrollment runs from August 2024 onwards, with a minimal study duration of 15 years and an estimated final sample of more than 2000 children. State-of-the-art regression-based methods will be used to examine the association of cardiovascular risk factors and preclinical atherosclerosis. Longitudinal data analysis methods will be used to model these associations over time.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was granted by the Medical Ethics Review Board of the University Medical Center Utrecht (NL84874.041.23). Written informed consent by participants and their parents is required for participation. Collected data will be made available to researchers upon reasonable request to the Steering Committee. Study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, presentations at scientific meetings and meetings with patient organisations.Keywords: Paediatric cardiology, Child, Lipid disorders, Chronic Disease, Cardiovascular Disease, Coronary heart disease

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases (ASCVD) are a leading cause of death worldwide, with ischaemic heart disease and stroke causing over 13 million global deaths in 2021.1 Clinical manifestations of atherosclerosis mainly manifest in middle-aged and elderly people, yet atherogenesis starts already in childhood.2–4

Atherosclerosis is considered a systemic, lipid-driven, inflammatory disorder.5 Multisite and multimodal assessment of preclinical atherosclerosis has emerged as the best way to capture the complexity of atherosclerosis as a systemic disease.6 7 Currently employed methods include carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT), carotid distensibility and carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) measurements. However, the optimal combination of methods to assess preclinical atherosclerosis in the paediatric population remains unchartered territory.6

Atherogenesis in the young is accelerated by lifestyle-associated risk factors such as hyperglycaemia, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and obesity, all with a surging prevalence over the last decades in both paediatric and adult populations.8 Approximately 25% of children are nowadays affected by a chronic condition.9 A paediatric chronic condition can impact physical, social-emotional and cognitive development, potentially leading to an adverse lifestyle over time.10–13 Additionally, disease-associated risk factors can fuel atherogenesis, including inflammation, stress, metabolic, vascular and endothelial factors. Consequently, many children with chronic conditions are at risk for early ASCVD.4 However, the specific contribution of lifestyle-associated and disease-associated risk factors to ASCVD in various paediatric chronic conditions requires further study to refine cardiovascular risk management strategies in paediatric practice.

Cardiovascular risk assessment aims to identify children with a high (lifetime) ASCVD risk and provides unique opportunities to intervene at a young age, thereby improving long-term outcomes in growing numbers of children and adolescents at risk.6 The primary objective of the Secondary Manifestations of ARTerial diseases in the Young with a chronic condition (SMART-Youth) study is to identify disease- and lifestyle-associated cardiovascular risk factors for preclinical atherosclerosis in children with a chronic condition. Secondary objectives are (1) to evaluate the prevalence of disease- and lifestyle-associated cardiovascular risk factors in children with a chronic condition, and (2) to unravel developmental trajectories of preclinical atherosclerosis in children with a chronic condition in the long term. This manuscript describes the SMART-Youth study protocol. Inspired by and building on the established PROactive cohort, which prospectively studies psychosocial well-being across paediatric chronic conditions, SMART-Youth investigates cardiovascular risk factors across paediatric chronic conditions.14 Furthermore, the SMART-Youth study adds a prospective paediatric cohort to the established adult cohort of the Utrecht Cardiovascular Cohort (UCC) SMART study series in Utrecht, The Netherlands, to develop a life course perspective on cardiovascular risk assessment and management over time.15

Methods and analysis

Study population and design

SMART-Youth is a prospective longitudinal cohort study involving children aged 8–18 years with a chronic condition (International Clinical Trials Registry number NL-OMON56581). An overview of the study logistics is presented in figure 1. SMART-Youth builds on the existing infrastructure, logistics and protocol of the ongoing PROactive cohort, which was developed to assess and support psychosocial well-being of children with chronic conditions, and includes an annual follow-up (International Clinical Trials Registry number NL-OMON58567). SMART-Youth builds on the PROactive cohort and embeds cardiovascular risk assessment in the existing annual psychosocial and clinical assessments, creating an integrated framework for holistic paediatric care.

Study design. Secondary Manifestations of ARTerial diseases in the Young with a chronic condition (SMART-Youth): a step-by-step overview of the protocol and goals of the SMART-Youth study. (1) The SMART-Youth study is embedded within the PROactive cohort. The PROactive cohort focuses on psychosocial well-being through annual questionnaires in standard clinical care of children with a chronic condition. (2) Patients are eligible for the SMART-Youth study at the age of 8 years or older. (3) The child and parent(s) will be informed about the SMART-Youth study in the e-mail they receive from PROactive 3 weeks before the outpatient visit at the hospital. In the upcoming week, parents are contacted by the SMART-Youth research staff through telephone to provide them with additional information and answer their questions. (4) The SMART-Youth visit is combined with the annual outpatient visit in the hospital. (5) The SMART-Youth study will take around 1 hour, including cardiometabolic assessment. (6) The child and parent(s) receive feedback about the nutritional assessment of the child, including advice and practical tips to improve nutritional intake. (7) A cardiovascular risk report in the electronic patient dossier with an accompanying interpretation and advice with regard to body mass index, blood pressure and lab values is made. (8) Statistical analyses of the data are performed to answer the primary and secondary research questions. (9) Apply Open Science via the FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) principles to the data generated in the study. (10) By implementing cardiovascular risk assessment and management in paediatric chronic conditions, this study aims to reduce cardiovascular risk in the short term and prevent cardiovascular diseases in the long term.

PROactive currently includes over 3500 paediatric patients with a chronic condition aged 2–18 years receiving outpatient care in the Wilhelmina Children’s Hospital in Utrecht (The Netherlands) from December 2016 onwards and continues to enrol patients.14 16 PROactive is part of standard clinical care. All SMART-Youth participants are recruited from the PROactive cohort from August 2024 onwards and represent a broad range of paediatric chronic conditions, including cystic fibrosis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, systemic autoimmune disease, chronic kidney disease, primary immunodeficiency, autoinflammatory conditions, inflammatory bowel disease, congenital heart disease, children with persistent somatic symptoms and children born premature (<28 weeks of gestational age) or after fetal growth restriction. Additional chronic conditions are likely to be incorporated into the PROactive cohort in the near future, and consequently into SMART-Youth as well. Each condition has a designated medical liaison for study-specific coordination and clinical questions.

Inclusion criteria for the SMART-Youth study include an age of 8–18 years old and the presence of an outpatient visit. All children aged 8–18 years old and their parent(s) are informed about the SMART-Youth study through a notification and information flyer included in the PROactive email invitation, which is sent 3 weeks before their annual outpatient visit. In the weeks prior to their visit, parents are contacted by SMART-Youth research staff via telephone to provide them with additional information and answer their questions. The starting age of 8 years is chosen for two reasons. First, aortic fatty streaks and cIMT progression are generally detectable in a valid manner from this age onwards.17–19 Second, puberty commonly starts between 8 and 13 years of age in girls and 9 and 14 years of age in boys, which affects cardiovascular risk factors and particularly lipid profiles.20 21 Measuring risk factors before puberty, around age 8, is therefore preferred. When the child and parents consent to participate, the SMART-Youth visit is combined with the annual check-up at the hospital outpatient clinic, and it takes approximately 1 hour. After this baseline assessment, a 2-year follow-up assessment will be performed and another follow-up assessment at 17–18 years of age. Participants who discontinue participation or relocate will be documented, and reasons for withdrawal will be recorded when available. Currently, the study duration is set to a minimum of 15 years, with the intention of study extension. To assess the selection bias that occurs in longitudinal studies such as SMART-Youth, routinely collected care data of non-participants will be compared with those of participating children to estimate representativeness and generalisability of the data.

Anthropometric measurements

Body weight is measured in kg using a standard electronic scale and body height in cm is measured using a paediatric stadiometer. Weight and height are used to calculate body mass index (BMI) in kg/m2. BMI SD score (SDS) corrected for sex, age and height will be calculated using Dutch growth references.22 The presence of overweight or obesity is based on international age-specific and sex-specific BMI cut-off values.23 Waist and hip circumference are each assessed twice in cm using a tape measure to the nearest mm. Standing with the feet slightly apart, waist circumference is measured at the level midway between the lowest rib border and the iliac crest, and hip circumference at the widest level over the major trochanter.

Abdominal adipose tissue measurements

Ultrasound has proven to be a reliable tool to assess abdominal visceral adipose tissue (VAT) and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT).24 VAT and SAT depth is measured in millimetres using the MyLabOne Ultrasound 8100 with a SC-3421 convex transducer (Esaote, Genova, Italy) as previously described.25 In short, for abdominal VAT, the distances between the posterior edge of the abdominal muscles and the lumbar spine are measured using electronic callipers. Distances are measured from three different angles: medial and left and right lateral, with the transducer placed longitudinally on a straight line drawn between the left and right mid-point of the lower rib and iliac crest. Measurements are performed at the end of an expiration. The mean distance from the three angles is calculated. Placing the probe transversely at the level of the umbilicus, abdominal SAT is measured with electronic callipers, from the external face of the rectus abdominis muscle (Linea alba) to just below the skin. The measurement is repeated three times, of which the mean is calculated. For all measurements, minimal pressure is applied to eliminate the distortion of tissue.

Blood pressure

Blood pressure (BP) is measured three times with an appropriate cuff size on the right arm while seated, using an automatic oscillometric device after at least 5 min of resting. A systolic and diastolic BP percentile is calculated from the lowest BP value, based on sex, height and age, using the 2017 AAP Guidelines for Screening and Management of High Blood Pressure.26 Ambulatory BP monitoring for 24 hours is advised as a secondary evaluation when BP measurements are enhanced or variable, in accordance with international guidelines.26

Hair cortisol concentration

Hair cortisol is used as a measure of long-term stress exposure.27 A sample of approximately 100–150 hairs is acquired from the posterior vertex. The hair is cut closely to the scalp and stored in a dark closet at room temperature. The samples are processed in the department of Clinical Chemistry at UMC Utrecht (University Medical Center Utrecht, The Netherlands). Hair cortisol concentration (HCC) is performed using an established liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry method.28 Potential influencing factors for HCCs will be registered, including hair washing frequency, time since last wash and hair product use. As stress could also arise from sources unrelated to the child’s chronic condition, other sources of stress experienced by the child are inquired within the PROactive psychosocial questionnaires (eg, bullying, experienced school pressure, home environment).

Laboratory testing

A non-fasting venous blood sample is used to measure total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides, C-reactive protein (CRP) and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c). LDL-c was calculated using the Friedewald equation, but was measured quantitatively when triglyceride levels exceed 8 mmol/L or when the calculated LDL-c is below 0.8 mmol/L. HbA1c was measured on the automated HA8180 HPLC analysers (Arkray Europe, Brussels, Belgium). ApoB was measured using nephelometry (Atellica Neph 630, Siemens, The Hague, The Netherlands). Triglycerides, total cholesterol, HDL-c and CRP were measured using the Atellica CH Analyzer (Siemens, The Hague, The Netherlands). In many patients, lipid blood sampling is routinely performed during outpatient follow-up. For some patient groups, lipid measurements were new and started in the course of the SMART-Youth study after consultation of the disease-group liaisons.

Questionnaires

The SMART-Youth study uses the annual questionnaires administered by the PROactive cohort study in children with a chronic condition.14 These questionnaires are filled out by the child and one parent in the 3-week period leading up to the SMART-Youth visit and focus on psychosocial well-being and cardiovascular risk factors. Psychosocial determinants are well described in the PROactive protocol paper.14 Cardiovascular risk factors include family history and specifically whether parents or grandparents of the patient suffered from ASCVD before the age of 65 years in women and 55 years in men. Furthermore, a physical activity questionnaire for children aged 8–14 years (PAQ-C) or for adolescents aged 14–18 years (PAQ-A) is conducted.29 Finally, a separate questionnaire inquiring about country of origin, level of education, nutrition (Dutch Healthy Diet index 2015 for children: Eetscore), type of chronic condition, medication use and smoking status is taken.30

Carotid intima-media thickness and stiffness

Vascular properties of the right common carotid artery are measured using the ART.LAB program (ESAOTE, Maastricht, The Netherlands), as done previously by our group.31 32 The ART.LAB program is coupled with an ESAOTE MylabOne sonographer equipped with a SL3323 linear probe (4.0–13.0 MHz). Intima-media thickness is measured on 128 radiofrequency (RF) lines (B-Mode, up to a 4 cm zone) in real time. Measurements are performed with subjects in a supine position, with their heads turned 45° to the contralateral side of the artery, after at least 10 min of resting. The measurements are conducted longitudinal and perpendicular to the ultrasound beam, in lateral view, at least 5 mm proximal to the carotid bifurcation of the common carotid artery, and in an area with a clearly defined lumen-intima. Subsequently, cIMT is automatically calculated by the program. Carotid distension reflects the change in diameter of the carotid artery between systole and diastole, with a lower distension reflecting vascular stiffness. Measurement of cIMT and carotid distension is performed three times.

Carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity

Carotid-femoral PWV (cfPWV) measurements are performed by applanation tonometry using the Sphygmocor Xcel device (AtCor Medical, Sydney, New South Wales, Australia).33 cfPWV is measured three times on the right body side in a supine position following the latest European guideline recommendations.34 The subtraction method instead of the 80% method is used for the surface distance measurement as this is the recommended method in children.35 Additionally, central aortic systolic BP, diastolic BP, pulse pressure and mean arterial pressure are recorded once on the right body side using the Sphygmocor Xcel device (AtCor Medical, Sydney, New South Wales, Australia).

Feedback to attending physician

Since we recruit children with a chronic condition, their attending physicians are informed about the SMART-Youth study findings in a cardiovascular risk report in the electronic patient file. This cardiovascular risk report contains the following parameters, including interpretation and advice: BMI (including normal/overweight/obesity), BP (including normotensive/stage 1 hypertension or higher), nutritional score, lipid profiles (total cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol, HDL-cholesterol, triglycerides), HbA1c and CRP.30 A flowchart including reference values and a management plan for abnormal lab values is shown in figure 2A. Next to this, a weekly evaluation of aberrant lipid profiles by the study team is performed to enable early identification of clinically relevant dyslipidaemias, taking their medical history and medication use into account. Cardiovascular risk management with regard to abnormal BMI and BP is presented in figure 2B and C. The attending physician will inform the child and parents about abnormal findings and the proposed management plan. Patient feedback concerning the nutritional assessment (Dutch Healthy Diet index: Eetscore), including advice and practical tips based on the ‘Dutch dietary guidelines 2015’ are automatically shared with the child and parent(s) by e-mail via the ‘Dutch Healthy Diet index assessment tool’ of the Wageningen University.30 Children who are already being treated by a dietitian are instructed to follow their dietitian’s advice. Clinical implications of the other study parameters, including cIMT, carotid distension, cfPWV, ApoB, hair cortisol, body circumferences and abdominal adipose tissue measurements, are still under investigation. Therefore, these parameters are not shared with the attending physician, child or parent(s).

Sample size and power calculation

Since the initiation of the PROactive cohort in December 2016, approximately 3500 children aged 2–18 years have been enrolled. Each year, around 450 children aged 8–18 years complete the PROactive questionnaires. Of these 450 respondents, approximately 250 are newly enrolled participants who undergo baseline measurements, while the remaining 200 are follow-up respondents. Both newly enrolled and follow-up respondents aged 8–18 years with an outpatient visit are eligible to take part in SMART-Youth. Over a 15-year period, this would yield approximately 6750 (450 × 15) eligible participants for the SMART-Youth study. With an estimated response rate of 30–50%, we expect to enrol more than 2000 children with a chronic condition in the SMART-Youth study over a 15-year period.

We also performed a priori sample size calculation based on the primary objective of this study, aiming to identify disease-associated and lifestyle-associated cardiovascular risk factors for preclinical atherosclerosis in children with a chronic condition. A sample size of 168 participants is required to detect an individual effect of partial R² = 0.03 in a multivariable regression model with 15 determinants, at 80% power and α=0.05. The 15 determinants under study consist of age, sex, systolic BP percentile, BMI SDS, LDL-C, non-HDL, HbA1c, CRP, nutritional score, physical activity score, family history of ASCVD, disease activity, hair cortisol, abdominal visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue. Given the estimated inclusion number of more than 2000 children over the total study period, we expect to reach the minimal required sample size of 168 to answer our primary research question. By including more than 2000 participants we aim to realise the secondary objectives as effectively as possible. The power calculation was performed in R statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the ‘pwr’ package, based on Cohen’s framework for power analysis.36 37

Data analysis

Regression-based methods will be used to identify disease-associated and lifestyle-associated cardiovascular risk factors for preclinical atherosclerosis in children with a chronic condition, controlling for potential confounders such as child age and sex (primary objective). Secondarily, stratified analyses per disease group will be performed to capture the possible heterogeneity of results within different disease groups, provided the sample size allows for adequately powered subgroup analyses. Additionally, differences in preclinical atherosclerosis measures cIMT/carotid distension/cfPWV between the disease groups will be explored by regression-based methods. Descriptive statistics such as mean with SD, median with IQR or number with percentage will be used to study the prevalence of disease-associated (eg, inflammation, stress, haemodynamic parameters) and lifestyle-associated (eg, nutrition, physical activity, obesity) cardiovascular risk factors in paediatric chronic conditions (secondary objective 1). Additionally, logistic regression analysis will be employed to investigate differences in the prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors between chronic disease groups, allowing for adjustment for potential confounders (eg, age, sex). Appropriate longitudinal data analysis methods will be used to explore the impact of cardiovascular risk factors on atherosclerosis development over time (secondary objective 2). Finally, we aspire to compare the SMART-Youth study findings with national data on vascular ageing in healthy children from the WHISTLER birth cohort study.38 The WHISTLER birth cohort study provides healthy control data for the abdominal adipose tissue measurements (8–18 years, n>250), cIMT (8–18 years, n>500), cfPWV (12–18 years, n>250) and hair cortisol measurements (12–18 years, n>50), as detailed on the health data space with yearly updates (https://catalogue.hdsu.nl/HDSU/collections/WHISTLER).

Patterns of missing data will be examined, and multiple imputation methods will be used if appropriate. The threshold for statistical significance is two-tailed p value<0.05. Statistical analyses will be performed using R statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval was granted by the Medical Ethics Review Board of the University Medical Center Utrecht (NL84874.041.23). The study is conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and in compliance with the relevant European regulations for research involving human subjects. If the child is between 8 and 15 years old, both parents need to sign the informed consent form. From the age of 12 years onwards, children are asked to sign a separate informed consent form as well. If the child is 16 years or older, no informed consent from the parents is needed. Different versions of the informed consent forms are provided to caregivers, children aged 8–12 years, children aged 12–16 years and adolescents aged 16–18 years, in age-appropriate language. Participants will be able to withdraw from the study at any time.

The SMART-Youth cohort study has a data management plan (DMP) and applies FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) principles to the data generated in the study.39 Given that the data are sensitive, the data themselves cannot be published openly. However, the metadata are published with a DOI on DataverseNL and will therefore be findable for other researchers (https://doi.org/10.34894/FCBXSI). This metadata includes a DMP in addition to a measurement level codebook. Collected data will be made available to researchers upon reasonable request to the PROactive and SMART-Youth Steering Committee. The Steering Committee consists of representatives from both studies and the institutional Child Health Disease Cohort, with epidemiological and ethical expertise, and is tasked to align the study logistics, coordinate scientific projects and collaborations, process data requests and develop and safeguard long-term vision on cohort development and objectives. Data requests are also discussed with clinicians representing the involved disease groups. Study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, presentations at scientific meetings, and meetings with patient organisations. This protocol publication follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.40

Patient and public involvement

Insights from a patient association conference (congenital heart defects), the patients’ physicians and parents, and healthy peers were implemented in the design of the SMART-Youth study. With the patients’ physicians, we discussed patient burden, clinical care and research perspectives, and the importance of combining the SMART-Youth and outpatient visit.

Discussion

Atherogenesis starts in childhood, and its development is fueled by disease-associated and lifestyle-associated risk factors.4 In adults with chronic diseases, the combined burden of disease-associated and lifestyle-associated risk factors was acknowledged years ago, and has already been translated in disease-specific cardiovascular risk management protocols, for example for inflammatory bowel disease and rheumatic disorders.41 42 In children with chronic conditions, this is largely uncharted territory. To our knowledge, the SMART-Youth study is the first to investigate the combined effects of disease-associated and lifestyle-associated cardiovascular risk factors on preclinical atherosclerosis in children with a chronic condition. The SMART-Youth study thereby addresses emerging clinical and scientific questions in the field, as discussed below.

First, the identification of relevant disease- and lifestyle-associated risk factors for preclinical atherosclerosis development in the young with a chronic condition. Disease-associated risk factors that fuel atherogenesis are diverse, and can include inflammation, vascular and endothelial factors.4 43 Lifestyle-associated risk factors include poor nutrition, physical inactivity, obesity, smoking and other substance abuse. Dyslipidaemia and hypertension may arise from either disease-related processes or lifestyle factors.26 44 Some studies suggest that children with a chronic condition engage more frequently in unhealthy and risky lifestyles.11–13 44 Paediatric studies into lifestyle factors have been performed in some disease groups, but a more comprehensive assessment of lifestyle factors, next to its relation with preclinical atherosclerosis is still lacking for most groups.45–51 SMART-Youth provides a unique combination of disease-specific data next to comprehensive cardiovascular data. The embedment of SMART-Youth in the established PROactive cohort additionally allows for the integration of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and lifestyle behaviours, enabling multidimensional risk profiling. Together, these datasets offer a powerful platform for disentangling the respective contributions of disease-associated and lifestyle-associated drivers of early atherosclerosis, and for identifying targets for preventive interventions in paediatric practice. Results of this study will thereby fuel refinement of cardiovascular risk management in paediatric practice. Notably, PROMs, lifestyle factors and preclinical measures of atherosclerosis are similarly assessed in healthy children participating in the WHISTLER birth cohort study, enabling to study differences in lifestyle and cardiovascular risk between healthy children and children with a chronic condition.38

Second, current data suggest that dyslipidaemias are highly prevalent in some childhood chronic disorders, but their prevalence and cardiovascular impact has not been studied across paediatric disease groups yet.52–58 The SMART-Youth study implements a comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment and management protocol in paediatric chronic conditions, including non-fasting lipid and HbA1c screening. This has previously been recommended for youth at risk in international guidelines, but adherence to these guidelines seems suboptimal.59–62 As previous studies revealed a dyslipidaemia prevalence from 20% up to 80%, depending on the chronic condition examined, we expect a high burden of abnormal lipid levels.52–58 Next to disease activity and lifestyle factors, medication use can impact lipid levels and will therefore be taken into account when reviewing dyslipidaemias.63 Considering the prevalence of familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH) is 1 in 230, and no general population screening programme exists in the Netherlands, we expect to occasionally discover a participant with FH.64 In conclusion, the SMART-Youth study adds relevant knowledge regarding the prevalence of dyslipidaemia and its different subtypes across different paediatric disease groups. Moreover, it supports the development of appropriate cut-off values (as outlined in figure 2A) for further evaluation of specific dyslipidemias, while also enabling investigation into associations with medications affecting lipid levels, progression over time and the development of preclinical atherosclerosis.

Third, the SMART-Youth study provides novel insights in the assessment of preclinical atherosclerosis in children. Sonographic evaluation of the cIMT has emerged as the most common non-invasive measure of preclinical atherosclerosis in paediatrics.6 Multiple paediatric chronic disease groups have shown an enhanced cIMT when compared with healthy controls.65 66 Nonetheless, caution to the use of cIMT as the main preclinical measure of atherosclerosis is warranted, as changes in cIMT at a young age could also reflect physiological adaptations of the carotid media, rather than atherogenesis in the carotid intima layer.67 Because atherogenesis starts in the iliac arteries and abdominal aorta during childhood, preclinical measures of atherosclerosis in the aorta, such as the cfPWV, could advance atherosclerosis assessment in paediatrics. Moreover, aortic measures of preclinical atherosclerosis showed superior to cIMT in predicting cardiovascular events in adults.68 Taken together, multimodal and multisite assessment of arterial changes may be needed to capture the complexity of atherosclerosis as a systemic disease.6 7 The SMART-Youth study enables comparison of these different preclinical atherosclerosis measures in childhood. Furthermore, this study aids in understanding the underlying mechanisms behind the potentially varying effects of cardiovascular risk factors on vascular thickness (cIMT) and vascular stiffness (carotid distension, cfPWV) parameters. Thereby, results of the SMART-Youth study could refine risk stratification for different paediatric chronic conditions in the future.

The SMART-Youth study has several limitations that should be taken into account. First, due to the heterogeneity of the study population, rare population subgroups might be underpowered. Risk stratification for these smaller subgroups may therefore be limited. Second, this study involves a non-fasting lipid assessment. Non-fasting lipid screening has emerged as the novel standard, as circulating lipids and lipoproteins are only marginally affected by habitual food intake.69 70 A limitation of the non-fasting measurements is that only HbA1c is assessed as a measure of glucose dysregulation, without evaluating fasting glucose and insulin levels. Lastly, lipid profiles are included in the ‘cardiovascular risk report’. While the SMART-Youth study is at the forefront of developing cardiovascular risk management strategies for paediatric chronic conditions, a current limitation is the difficulty in defining exact cut-off values for additional diagnostics of specific dyslipidemias.

Taken together, the SMART-Youth study offers emerging insights in cardiovascular risk factors and preclinical atherosclerosis in paediatric chronic conditions. Specifically, the study aims to (1) identify both disease-associated and lifestyle-associated cardiovascular risk factors for preclinical atherosclerosis in children with a chronic condition, (2) unravel the prevalence of dyslipidaemia and its different subtypes across different paediatric disease groups and (3) capture the complexity of atherosclerosis development in children with a chronic condition through longitudinal, multimodal and multisite assessment. Therefore, the SMART-Youth study will aid refinement of cardiovascular risk assessment and management in youth at risk. This is increasingly relevant as the life expectancy of many children with chronic disorders has improved over the last few decades, and long-term cardiovascular outcomes emerge as a novel threat in many patient groups.4 71 At the same time, childhood emerges as a unique window of opportunity to modify the course of ASCVD development later in life. Longitudinal studies in children and adolescents at risk, such as the SMART-Youth study, are pivotal to develop lifetime ASCVD risk stratification strategies and ultimately improve outcomes in this growing population.
