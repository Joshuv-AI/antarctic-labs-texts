Goldbach Representations with several primes
Thi Thu Nguyen

Abstract
. We study an asymptotic formula for average orders of Goldbach representations of an integer as the sum of k k primes. We extend the existing result for k = 2 k=2 to a general k k , for which we obtain a better error term. Moreover, we prove an equivalence between the Riemann Hypothesis and a good average order in this case.

11P32, 11M26

1.
Introduction

Let
k\geq 2
be an integer, we define the weighted Goldbach function in the general case

G_{k}(n)=\sum_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}=n}\Lambda(n_{1})\dots\Lambda(n_{k}),

where
\Lambda(n)
is the von Mangoldt function. For
k\geq 2
, the expected asymptotic formula for
G_{k}(n)
is of the form

\displaystyle G_{k}(n)=\dfrac{n^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\mathfrak{S}_{k}(n)+\text{error term},

where

\mathfrak{S}_{k}(n):=\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\left(\dfrac{-1}{p-1}\right)^{k-1}\right)\prod_{p\nmid n}\left(1-\left(\dfrac{-1}{p-1}\right)^{k}\right),

(see
[
7
]
).
The first result of this type was studied by Hardy and Littlewood
[
11
]
which inspired Vinagradov
[
15
]
for
k=3
. Friedlander and Goldston
[
7
]
established this bound for each
k\geq 5
and got a slightly weaker estimate for
k=3
and
4
.
Moreover, Friedlander and Goldston (Corollary,
[
7
]
) proved that for
k\geq 5
the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis is equivalent to the estimate

\displaystyle G_{k}(n)=\dfrac{n^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\mathfrak{S}_{k}(n)+\mathcal{O}(n^{k-3/2}).

In this paper, we will consider its average order, denoted by

S_{k}(X):=\sum_{n\leq X}G_{k}(n).

Studying such average orders is a standard practice in analytic number theory. Here a special motivation is that according to Granville
[
9
,
10
]
, the average order of
G_{k}(n)
can be related to the Riemann Hypothesis (RH). Languasco and Zaccagnini
[
12
]
proved the following asymptotic result for
S_{2}(X)
, under the RH.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assuming the RH and let
X\geq 2
. Then

S_{2}(X)=\dfrac{X^{2}}{2}-2\sum_{\rho}\dfrac{X^{\rho+1}}{\rho(\rho+1)}+\mathcal{O}(X\log^{3}X).

We expect a similar formula in the general case
k\geq 2
. Languasco and Zaccagnini
[
12
]
stated an asymptotic formula of
S_{k}(X)
for
k\geq 3
, with the error term
\mathcal{O}_{k}(X^{k-1}\log^{k}X)
but did not prove it in detail. In fact, we can prove this result by using the original Hardy and Littlewood circle method with the infinite exponential sum.
Note that Theorem
1.1
was later proved by another method
[
8
]
, which uses the finite exponential sum as studied by Bhowmik and Schlage-Puchta
[
4
]
. Here we improve the asymptotic formula of
S_{k}(X)
in
[
12
]
with the method of Goldston and Yang
[
8
]
, who showed a good estimate of the expected value function (see Lemma
2.4
). While Languasco and Zaccagnini used Lemma
[
13
]
for their bound, we use a trivial bound (see Lemma
2.5
). Surprisingly we get a much better estimate, the
\log
-power is always
3
for all
k\geq 2
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
k\geq 2
,
X\geq k
and assume the RH holds. Then we have

S_{k}(X)=\dfrac{X^{k}}{k!}+H_{k}(X)+\mathcal{O}_{k}(X^{k-1}\log^{3}X),

with

H_{k}(X)=-k\sum_{\rho}\dfrac{X^{\rho+k-1}}{\rho(\rho+1)...(\rho+k-1)},

where
\rho
are the non-trivial zeros of Riemann zeta function with
Re(\rho)=1/2
.

We note that the error term in Theorem
1.1
is essentially the best possible because we know the error term is
\Omega(X\log\log X)
[
4
]
.
Similar to the case
k=2
, we expect an omega-result of the average order in the general case as was studied by Bhowmik, Schlage-Puchta
[
5
]
, who proved the error term is
\Omega(X^{k-1})
, while
[
3
]
shows a similar result for the error term to be
\Omega_{\pm}(X^{k-1})
. In this paper, we prove the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
k\geq 2
, we have

S_{k}(X)=\dfrac{X^{k}}{k!}+H_{k}(X)+\Omega(X^{k-1}\log\log X).

To do that, we use the idea in
[
4
]
for
k=2
. We show that for
n
sufficiently large,
G_{k}(n)=\Omega(n^{k-1}\log\log n
). Then Theorem
1.3
will be proved because if Theorem
1.3
is false, that means

\displaystyle S_{k}(n)=\dfrac{n^{k}}{k!}+H_{k}(n)+o(n^{k-1}\log\log n).

This implies

\displaystyle G_{k}(n)=S_{k}(n)-S_{k}(n-1)=o(n^{k-1}\log\log n).

For
k=2
, we know that there is a good relation between the Riemann Hypothesis and the average order, the RH is equivalent to the estimation

S_{2}(X)=\dfrac{X^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{O}(X^{3/2+\varepsilon}),

for any
\varepsilon>0
, as mentioned in
[
2
]
and
[
1
]
. The method of
[
1
]
was generalized in
[
6
]
to obtain a zero-free region for the Riemann zeta-function.

In this paper, we prove that for
k\geq 2
, a good estimation of
S_{k}(X)
is equivalent to the Riemann Hypothesis.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
X\geq k
, the RH is equivalent to

S_{k}(X)=\dfrac{X^{k}}{k!}+\mathcal{O}_{k}(X^{k-1/2+\varepsilon}),

for any
\varepsilon>0
.

In fact, we show that Theorem
1.4
is a consequence of the following theorem.

Theorem 1.5

(Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis)
.

Let
k\geq 2
. We assume that there exists
0<\delta<1
such that

S_{k}(X)=\dfrac{X^{k}}{k!}+\mathcal{O}_{k}(X^{k-\delta}),

then for any non-trivial zero
\rho
of Riemann zeta function, we have
\Re(\rho)<1
.

(Theorem
1.1
) and suppose that it holds up to
k-1

k

[

\displaystyle S_{0}(\alpha,x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda_{0}(n)e(n\alpha),\hskip 28.45274pte(\alpha)=e^{2\pi i\alpha},

Then for

\displaystyle S_{0}(\alpha,x)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}\leq x}\Lambda_{0}(n_{1})\dots\Lambda_{0}(n_{k})e((n_{1}+\dots+n_{k})\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq kx}B_{k}(n,x)e(n\alpha),

When

Let

We then have the estimate
I(X,\alpha)\ll\min\left(X,\dfrac{1}{||\alpha||}\right).
For

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}S_{0}(\alpha,x)^{k}I(X,-\alpha)d\alpha=\sum_{n\leq X}B_{k}(n,x).

Substituting (

(2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{k}(X)&=\int_{0}^{1}S_{0}(\alpha,x)^{k}I(X,-\alpha)d\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{k-2}(-1)^{i+1}\binom{k}{i}\sum_{n\leq X}\sum_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}=n}\Lambda(n_{i+1})\dots\Lambda(n_{k})\\

&=:I_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{k-2}(-1)^{i+1}\binom{k}{i}I_{i}+(-1)^{k}kI_{k-1}+(-1)^{k+1}I_{k}.\end{split}

We estimate
I_{i}

2.1.
Main term of
I_{k}
.

We have

(3)

\displaystyle I_{k}=\sum_{n\leq X}\sum_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}=n}1=\sum_{k\leq n\leq X}\binom{n-1}{k-1}=\dfrac{X^{k}}{k!}+\mathcal{O}(X^{k-1}).

2.2.
Main term of
I_{k-1}
.

\displaystyle I_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq X}\sum_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}=n}\Lambda(n_{k})=\sum_{n\leq X}\sum_{n_{k}=1}^{X-(k-1)}\sum_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{k-1}=n-n_{k}}\Lambda(n_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq X}\sum_{n_{k}=1}^{X-(k-1)}\binom{n-n_{k}-1}{k-2}\Lambda(n_{k})=\sum_{n_{k}=1}^{X-(k-1)}\Lambda(n_{k})\left(\dfrac{(X-n_{k})^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}+\mathcal{O}(X^{k-2})\right)

\displaystyle=\psi_{k-1}(X-k+1)+\mathcal{O}(X^{k-1}),

where for a non-negative integer

\psi_{j}(x):=\dfrac{1}{j!}\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)(x-n)^{j}.

When

\psi_{0}(x)=\psi(x)

We have some properties of this function.

Firstly,

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(x+1)=\psi_{j}(x)+\mathcal{O}(x^{j}).

We note that,

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\psi_{j-1}(t)dt.

Moreover, for

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{x^{2}}{2}-\sum_{\rho}\dfrac{x^{\rho+1}}{\rho(\rho+1)}-\dfrac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(0)x+\dfrac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(-1)+\mathcal{O}(x^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{x^{2}}{2}-\sum_{\rho}\dfrac{x^{\rho+1}}{\rho(\rho+1)}+\mathcal{O}(x).

Then by induction, we obtain

(5)

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(x)=\dfrac{x^{j+1}}{(j+1)!}-\sum_{\rho}\dfrac{x^{\rho+j}}{\rho(\rho+1)...(\rho+j)}+\mathcal{O}(x^{j}).

From (

\displaystyle I_{k-1}=\dfrac{X^{k}}{k!}-\sum_{\rho}\dfrac{X^{\rho+k-1}}{\rho(\rho+1)...(\rho+k-1)}+\mathcal{O}(X^{k-1}).

Consider

\displaystyle I_{0}\ll X^{k-1}\log^{3}X.

\displaystyle\binom{i-1}{i-1}+\binom{i}{i-1}+\dots+\binom{i+m}{i-1}=\binom{i+m+1}{i},

3.
Proof of the Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis

In this part, we consider the power series for
|z|<1
,

(12)

\displaystyle F(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\Lambda(n)z^{n}

We take the
k^{th}
power of
F(z)
and obtain

\displaystyle F(z)^{k}=\sum_{n\geq 1}G_{k}(n)z^{n}=(1-z)\sum_{n\geq 1}S_{k}(n)z^{n}

By the assumption in Theorem
1.5
, we have

(13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{n\geq 1}S_{k}(n)z^{n}&=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\dfrac{n^{k}}{k!}+\mathcal{O}_{k}(n^{k-\delta})\right)z^{n}\\
&=\dfrac{1}{k!}\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{k}z^{n}+\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{k-\delta}|z|^{n}\right).\end{split}

We then evaluate the main term in (
13
) as follows.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any integer
k\geq 2,
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{k}z^{n}=\dfrac{k!}{(1-z)^{k+1}}+\mathcal{O}(|1-z|^{-k}).

Proof.

We note that there exists unique real numbers
a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
such that

(14)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{n+j}{j}a_{j}=n^{k}

holds for integers
n=0,1,\dots,k
. Then (
14
) holds for all positive integers
n
.
From (
14
), we can rewrite

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{k}z^{n}=\dfrac{a_{k}}{(1-z)^{k+1}}+\dfrac{a_{k-1}}{(1-z)^{k}}+\dots+\dfrac{a_{0}}{1-z}.

We calculate the value of
a_{k}

(15)

\displaystyle a_{k}=k^{k}-\binom{k}{1}(k-1)^{k}+\binom{k}{2}(k-2)^{k}-\dots+(-1)^{k-1}\binom{k}{k-1}.

We just need to prove

\displaystyle a_{k}=k!.

Let
k\geq 2
, we define the function

(16)

\displaystyle f_{k,i}=k^{i}-\binom{k}{1}(k-1)^{i}+\binom{k}{2}(k-2)^{i}-\dots+(-1)^{k-1}\binom{k}{k-1}.

We note that

(17)

\displaystyle f_{k,i}=k(f_{k,i-1}+f_{k-1,i-1})

and

(18)

\displaystyle f_{k,i}=0,\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq k-1.

In fact, we prove (
18
) by induction on
i
.
When
i=1
,

\displaystyle f_{k,1}

\displaystyle=k-\binom{k}{1}(k-1)+\binom{k}{2}(k-2)-\dots+(-1)^{k-1}\binom{k}{k-1}

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{1}-2\binom{k}{2}+3\binom{k}{3}-\dots+(-1)^{k-1}k\binom{k}{k},

since

(k-n)\binom{k}{n}=(n+1)\binom{k}{n+1},\text{ for all }0\leq n\leq k.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle(1-x)^{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\binom{k}{j}x^{j},

and its derivative

(19)

\displaystyle k(1-x)^{k-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}j\binom{k}{j}x^{j-1}

Replace
x
in (
19
) by
1
, we obtain
f_{k,1}=0
.

Next, assuming that (
18
) is true for
1\leq i\leq k-2
, we now prove that it is true for
i+1
. By (
17
), we have

\displaystyle f_{k,i+1}=k(f_{k,i}+f_{k-1,i})=0.

From (
17
) and (
18
), we obtain

\displaystyle f_{k,k}=kf_{k-1,k-1}=\dots=k!f_{1,1}=k!.

Thus

a_{k}=f_{k,k}=k!.

∎

Using Lemma
3.1
, we rewrite (
13
) as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}S_{k}(n)z^{n}=\dfrac{1}{(1-z)^{k+1}}+\mathcal{O}_{k}\left((1-|z|)^{\delta-k-1}\right).

Hence, we obtain

(20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}F(z)^{k}&=(1-z)\sum_{n\geq 1}S_{k}(n)z^{n}\\
&=\dfrac{1}{(1-z)^{k}}+\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(|1-z|(1-|z|)^{\delta-k-1}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{(1-z)^{k}}+\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(|1-z|N^{k+1-\delta}\right)\end{split}

on the circle
|z|=R=1-\frac{1}{N}
, for a large positive integer
N
.
We remark that the error term is less than the absolute value of the main tern if
|1-z|<N^{\frac{\delta}{k+1}-1}.
We call this is a major arc on
|z|=R
, denoted by
\mathfrak{M}
. The rest of the circle is called a minor arc, denoted by
\mathcal{M}
.

We consider the major arc. Taking the complex
k^{th}
root of (
20
), then
F(z)
can be written as

\displaystyle F(z)=\dfrac{\omega}{1-z}+\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(|1-z|^{k}N^{k+1-\delta}\right),

where
\omega
is a
k^{th}
root of unity. Then we prove
\omega=1
. In fact, on the circle
|z|=R
, we choose
z
a real number which tends to
1
, for example
z=e^{-1/N}
. Putting
c:=\frac{1}{1-z}
a real number, then
c
tends to infinity and
|1-z|^{k}N^{k+1-\delta}
tends to
0
. Assuming

\omega=a+bi,\text{ for }a,b\in\mathbb{R},b\neq 0,

then,

F(z)=ac+bci.

This is impossible because from the definition of
F(z)
,
F(z)
is real if
z
is a real number. Hence
\omega
is also a real number. We consider two cases for the integer
k
.

Case 1: if
k
is odd, this implies
\omega=1
.

Case 2: if
k
is even,
\omega=\pm 1.
Since
F(z)
is continuous and its coefficients are non-negative, the sign of
\omega
is
+
, i.e,
\omega=1
.

We conclude

(21)

\displaystyle F(z)=\dfrac{1}{1-z}+\mathcal{O}_{k}\left(|1-z|^{k}N^{k+1-\delta}\right)\text{ on }\mathfrak{M}.

Now we introduce the kernel function,

\displaystyle K(z)=z^{-N-1}\dfrac{1-z^{N}}{1-z},

then
K(z)\ll|1-z|^{-1}
. By Cauchy’s integral formula, the Chebyshev function can be written as

(22)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi(N)&=\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=R}F(z)K(z)dz\\
&=N+\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=R}\left(F(z)-\dfrac{1}{1-z}\right)K(z)dz.\end{split}

We split the circle
|z|=R
into the major arc
\mathfrak{M}
and the minor arc
\mathcal{M}
.

On
\mathfrak{M}
, from (
21
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathfrak{M}}\left(F(z)-\dfrac{1}{1-z}\right)K(z)dz

\displaystyle\ll_{k}\int_{\mathfrak{M}}\left(|1-z|^{k}N^{k+1-\delta}\right)K(z)dz

\displaystyle\ll_{k}\int_{\mathfrak{M}}\left(|1-z|^{k-1}N^{k+1-\delta}\right)dz

\displaystyle\ll_{k}\left(N^{\frac{\delta}{k+1}-1}\right)^{k-1}N^{k+1-\delta}N^{\frac{\delta}{k+1}-1}

\displaystyle=N^{1-\frac{\delta}{k+1}}.

On
\mathcal{M}
, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left(F(z)-\dfrac{1}{1-z}\right)K(z)dz

\displaystyle\ll\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}|K(z)|^{2}dz\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left|F(z)-\dfrac{1}{1-z}\right|^{2}dz\right)^{1/2}

Moreover, similar to
[
1
]
for
k=2
, we have the estimations

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left|F(z)-\dfrac{1}{1-z}\right|^{2}dz\leq\int_{|z|=R}\left|F(z)-\dfrac{1}{1-z}\right|^{2}dz\ll N\log N

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}|K(z)|^{2}dz\ll\int_{\mathcal{M}}\dfrac{1}{|1-z|^{2}}dz\ll N^{1-\frac{\delta}{k+1}}.

Hence, we have an estimate on the minor arc

(23)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left(F(z)-\dfrac{1}{1-z}\right)K(z)dz\ll N^{1-\frac{\delta}{2(k+1)}}(\log N)^{1/2}.

Combining the major arc, minor arc and from (
22
), we obtain

\displaystyle\psi(N)-N\ll_{k}N^{1-\frac{\delta}{2(k+1)}}(\log N)^{1/2}.

Therefore, for any non-trivial zero
\rho
of Riemann zeta function, we have

\Re(\rho)<1-\dfrac{\delta}{2(k+1)}<1.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Assuming the Riemann Hypothesis, we can easily deduce the asymptotic formula of
S_{k}(X)
in Theorem
1.4
. Now we prove the reverse in the following steps.

Step 1:
Granville showed a formula of
S_{k}(X)
without using the RH ((1.3),
[
9
,
10
]
), that is

(24)

\displaystyle S_{k}(X)=\dfrac{X^{k}}{k!}+\sum_{\rho}r_{k}(\rho)\dfrac{X^{\rho+k-1}}{\rho+k-1}+\mathcal{O}_{k}(X^{k-2+\frac{4B+2}{3}+o(1)}),

where
B=\sup\{\Re(\rho):\zeta(\rho)=0\}
and

r_{k}(\rho):=-\dfrac{k}{\rho\dots(\rho+k-2)}.

Remark 4.1
.

We know that
1/2\leq B\leq 1
.

Step 2:
We define the corresponding Dirichlet series of
G_{k}(n)

\displaystyle f_{k}(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\dfrac{G_{k}(n)}{n^{s}}.

We note that this series converges absolutely in
\{\Re(s)>k\}
.
From (
24
) in step 1, we have

\displaystyle f_{k}(s)

\displaystyle=s\int_{1}^{\infty}S_{k}(x)x^{-s-1}dx

\displaystyle=s\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\dfrac{x^{k}}{k!}+\sum_{\rho}r_{k}(\rho)\dfrac{x^{\rho+k-1}}{\rho+k-1}+\mathcal{O}_{k}(x^{k-2+\frac{4B+2}{3}+o(1)})\right)x^{-s-1}dx

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{(s-k)(k-1)!}+\sum_{\rho}\dfrac{r_{k}(\rho)}{s-\rho-k+1}+s\int_{1}^{\infty}\mathcal{O}_{k}(x^{k-2+\frac{4B+2}{3}+o(1)})x^{-s-1}dx

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{k!}+\sum_{\rho}\dfrac{r_{k}(\rho)}{\rho+k-1}.

From the above, the series
f_{k}(s)
is analytic on
\{\Re(s)>k\}
and can be continued meromorphically to the haft plane
\{\Re(s)>k-2+\frac{4B+2}{3}\}
.

Step 3:
We assume that
B<1
. Then we obtain

(25)

\displaystyle k-1+B=\inf\{\sigma_{0}\geq k-\dfrac{1}{2}:f_{k}(s)-\dfrac{1}{(s-k)(k-1)!}\text{ is analytic on }\Re(s)>\sigma_{0}\}.

We then prove (
25
). From step 2, the right-hand side of (
25
) is at most
k-2+\frac{4B+2}{3}\leq k-1+B
, since
B\leq 1
.

For the reverse inequality, the right-hand side of (
25
) is at least
k-\dfrac{1}{2}
, then (
25
) is true if
B=\dfrac{1}{2}
. So we can assume
\dfrac{1}{2}<B<1
. Hence

\displaystyle\max\{k-2+\frac{4B+2}{3},k-\dfrac{1}{2}\}<k-1+B.

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle\max\{k-2+\frac{4B+2}{3},k-\dfrac{1}{2}\}<k-1+B-\varepsilon.

By the definition of
B
, there exists a non-trivial zero
\rho
such that

\displaystyle B-\varepsilon<\Re(\rho).

Thus we obtain

\displaystyle k-\dfrac{1}{2}<k-1+B-\varepsilon<\Re(\rho+k-1).

We consider in the haft plane
\{\Re(s)>k-1+B-\varepsilon\}
,
f_{k}(s)
has a pole at
\rho+k-1
. So the right-hand side of (
25
)
\geq k-1+B-\varepsilon
. Let
\varepsilon
tend to
0
, the proof of
(
25
)
is completed.

Step 4:
Let

E_{k}(X)=S_{k}(X)-\dfrac{X^{k}}{k!}.

Then by the assumption of this part,
E_{k}(X)\ll_{k}X^{k-\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}
.

Moreover,

\displaystyle s\int_{1}^{\infty}E_{k}(x)x^{-s-1}dx=f_{k}(s)+\dfrac{s}{(s-k)k!}.

Then

(26)

\displaystyle f_{k}(s)-\dfrac{1}{(s-k)(k-1)!}=s\int_{1}^{\infty}E_{k}(x)x^{-s-1}dx+\dfrac{1}{k!}.

We note that the right-hand side of (
26
) is analytic on

\{\Re(s)>k-\dfrac{1}{2}\}
. Then from Step 3, we obtain

k-1+B\leq k-\dfrac{1}{2},

B\leq\dfrac{1}{2}.

We conclude
B=\dfrac{1}{2}
, that means the Riemann Hypothesis is true.

Step 5:
We prove that
B=1
is impossible.

In fact, by assumption,

S_{k}(X)=\dfrac{X^{k}}{k!}+\mathcal{O}_{k}(X^{k-\delta}),

with
\delta=\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon
. Applying Theorem
1.5
, we obtain
B<1
.

5.
Proof of the omega-result Theorem
1.3

To prove Theorem
1.3
, we use the idea in
[
4
]
that if an integer
n
is divisible by many small primes, then
G_{k}(n)
should be large. We just need to prove

G_{k}(n)=\Omega(n^{k-1}\log\log n).

Let
q=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<x\\
p\nmid q_{1}\end{subarray}}p
be the product of primes which are less than
x
and not divisible by
q_{1}
, where
q_{1}
is the exceptional modulus up to
q
if there exists a Siegel’s zero. From Lemma 4
[
4
]
, for
(a,q)=1
we have

\displaystyle\psi(2x,q,a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq 2x\\
n=a(q)\end{subarray}}\Lambda(n)\geq\dfrac{x}{2\varphi(q)}.

Let
b
be an integer coprime to
q
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq 4x\\
n=b(q)\end{subarray}}G_{2}(n)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{(a,q)=1}\psi(2x,q,a)\psi(2x,q,b-a)\geq\dfrac{x^{2}}{4\varphi(q)},

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq 6x\\
n=b(q)\end{subarray}}G_{3}(n)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{(a,q)=1}\psi(2x,q,a)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq 4x\\
n=b-a(q)\end{subarray}}G_{2}(n)\geq\dfrac{x^{3}}{8\varphi(q)},\dots,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq 2(k-1)x\\
n=b(q)\end{subarray}}G_{k-1}(n)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{(a,q)=1}\psi(2x,q,a)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq 2(k-2)x\\
n=b-a(q)\end{subarray}}G_{k-2}(n)\geq\dfrac{x^{k-1}}{2^{k-1}\varphi(q)}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq 2kx\\
q|n\end{subarray}}G_{k}(n)\geq\sum_{(a,q)=1}\psi(2x,q,a)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq 2(k-1)x\\
n=q-a(q)\end{subarray}}G_{k-1}(n)\geq\dfrac{x^{k}}{2^{k}\varphi(q)}.

So that

\displaystyle\dfrac{2kx}{q}\max_{n\leq 2kx}G_{k}(n)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{x^{k}}{2^{k}\varphi(q)}.

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{n\leq 2kx}G_{k}(n)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{x^{k-1}}{2^{k+1}}\dfrac{q}{\varphi(q)}=\dfrac{x^{k-1}}{2^{k+1}}\prod_{p<x}\left(1-p^{-1}\right)^{-1}\prod_{p_{1}|q_{1}}\left(1-p_{1}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\gg x^{k-1}\log\log x.
