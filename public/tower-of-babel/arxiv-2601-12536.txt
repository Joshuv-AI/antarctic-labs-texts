Examples and counterexamples of injective types
Tom de Jong, Martín Hötzel Escardó

Abstract
. It is known that, in univalent mathematics, type universes, the type of n n -types in a universe, reflective subuniverses, and the underlying type of any algebra of the lifting monad are all (algebraically) injective. Here, we further show that the type of ordinals, the type of iterative (multi)sets, the underlying type of any pointed directed complete poset, as well as the types of (small) ∞ \infty -magmas, monoids, and groups are all injective, among other examples. Not all types of mathematical structures are injective in general. For example, the type of inhabited types is injective if and only if all propositions are projective . In contrast, the type of pointed types and the type of non-empty types are always injective. The injectivity of the type of two-element types implies Fourman and Ščedrov’s world’s simplest axiom of choice . We also show that there are no nontrivial small injective types unless a weak propositional resizing principle holds. Other counterexamples include the type of booleans, the simple types, the type of Dedekind reals, and the type of conatural numbers, whose injectivity implies weak excluded middle. More generally, any type with an apartness relation and two points apart cannot be injective unless weak excluded middle holds. Finally, we show that injective types have no non-trivial decidable properties, unless weak excluded middle holds, which amounts to a Rice-like theorem for injective types.



1.
Introduction

In the context of univalent mathematics
[
Uni13
]
, injective types were
discussed by the second author in
[
Esc21
]
. The interest in
injectivity originated in its use to construct infinite searchable
types
[
Esc19
]
, but the topic turned out to have a rather rich theory
on its own.
With classical logic, i.e. in the presence of excluded middle, the
(algebraically) injective types are precisely the pointed types.
In fact, this characterization is equivalent to excluded middle.
In constructive univalent mathematics, the situation is more interesting, as shown in
[
Esc21
]
and
further emphasized in this paper.

In
[
Esc21
]
, type universes, the type of propositions in a given
universe and the underlying type of any algebra of the lifting monad were all shown to be injective.
Here, we further show that the type of ordinals
[
Uni13
, §10.3]
, the
type of iterative (multi) sets
[
Gyl18
,
Gyl19
]
, the underlying
type of any pointed directed complete poset, as well as the types of (small)

\infty
-magmas, monoids, and groups are all injective.
The injectivity of magmas, monoids, and groups, follows from a general result
(
Theorem

4.15
) that gives a sufficient condition for the injectivity of a

\Sigma
-type.
More precisely, we use a specialization of this general condition to
study mathematical structures as given by
\Sigma
-types over type
universes.
For
subtypes
of injective types, we present a necessary and sufficient
condition (
Theorem

4.26
).

Complementary to the above positive results, we present several counterexamples
of injective types, although we should be careful in what we mean by
counterexample. The only type that is
provably
not injective is the
empty type, because classically any pointed type is injective.
But we present several types that cannot be shown to be injective in
constructive mathematics, because their injectivity would imply a constructive
taboo, which is a statement that it is not constructively provable and false in
some (topos) models.
Often, the relevant taboo for us is
weak excluded middle
which says that
the negation of any proposition is decidable, and which is equivalent to De
Morgan’s Law
[
Joh02
, Proposition D4.6.2]
. Indeed, weak excluded middle
holds as soon as any of the following types are injective: the type of booleans

\mathbf{2}\coloneqq\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{1}
(as already shown in
loc. cit.
), the
simple types (obtained from
\mathbb{N}
by iterating function types), the type of
Dedekind reals, and the type of conatural numbers
[
Esc13
]
.
More generally, any type with an apartness relation and two points apart cannot
be injective unless weak excluded middle holds
(
Theorem

7.6
).
Moreover, we show that injective types have no non-trivial decidable
properties (they are
indecomposable
), unless weak excluded
middle holds (
Theorem

6.10
), which
amounts to a Rice-like theorem for injective types
(cf.
[
Ric53
]
).

Also, not all types of mathematical structures are injective in
general. For example, the type of (small) inhabited types
is injective if and only if all propositions are

projective

[
KECA17
]
(a weak choice principle that
fails in some toposes
[
FŠ82
]
).
In contrast, the type of pointed types and the type of non-empty types are
injective, highlighting the constructive difference between the double negation
and the propositional truncation.
Similar to the non-injectivity of the type of inhabited types is the
observation that the world’s simplest axiom of choice
[
FŠ82
]

holds if the type of two-element types, also known as infinite dimensional
real projective space
[
BR17
]
is injective.

The examples of injective types given above are all large types and this is no
coincidence.
In fact, we also have a class of counterexamples by reduction to a

predicative
taboo with
Theorem

7.12
expressing that there
are no nontrivial small injective types unless a weak propositional resizing
principle holds.
This is also a good opportunity to point out that, in the absence of propositional
resizing, the notion of injectivity is universe dependent, as already observed
in
[
Esc21
]
, and hence we need to carefully track universes.

Related work

Injective sets have already been studied in the context of the constructive and

Outline

2.
Preliminaries

The foundations for this paper are as
in
[
Esc21
, Section 2.1]
and can be summarized as: intensional
Martin-Löf Type Theory with univalent universes (and hence function
extensionality and propositional extensionality), propositional truncations, and
in a few places which are marked explicitly, set quotients.
We mostly adopt the notation and conventions of
[
Uni13
]
and write

\equiv
for the definitional (or judgemental) equality, while we reserve

=
for identity types.
We sometimes find it convenient to abbreviate
\Pi(x:X),Y\,x
as
\Pi\,Y
,
and similarly for
\Sigma
-types.
We also write
(x:X)\to Y\,x
for
\Pi(x:X),Y\,x
to improve
readability in certain cases.

2.1.
Type universes

Type universes are rather important in this paper, so we pause to recall the
assumptions of
[
Esc21
]
.
We postulate that there is a universe
\mathcal{U}_{0}
, and two operations

(-)^{+}
, called successor, and
(-)\sqcup(-)
satisfying the following
conditions. The operation
{\sqcup}
is definitionally idempotent, commutative
and associative, and the successor distributes over
{\sqcup}

definitionally. Finally, we ask that
\mathcal{U}_{0}\sqcup\mathcal{U}\equiv\mathcal{U}
and that

\mathcal{U}\sqcup\mathcal{U}^{+}\equiv\mathcal{U}^{+}
.
Unlike
[
Uni13
]
, we do not require the universes to be
cumulative. However, we stipulate to have an empty type and a unit type in each
universe, which allows one to define lifting functions

\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}\sqcup\mathcal{V}
for any two universes
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
, as
in
[
Esc21
, Section 2.1]
.
In this paper, we let
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W},\mathcal{T}
range over universes.

2.2.
Fibers, equivalences and embeddings

We recall that the
fiber
of a map
f:A\to B
at

b:B
is defined as

\fiber_{f}(b)\coloneqq\Sigma(a:A),f\,a=b.

Besides equivalences, which may be characterized as those maps whose fibers are
singletons, this paper is concerned with
embeddings
, which may be
characterized as those maps whose fibers are subsingletons. Equivalently,

{f:A\to B}
is an embedding precisely when its action on paths

(a=a^{\prime})\to(f\,a=f\,a^{\prime})

is an equivalence for all
a,a^{\prime}:A
.

We also record two consequences of univalence here. First of all, for any two
types
X
and
Y
, we have a map

\operatorname{eqtoid}_{X,Y}:(X\simeq Y)\to(X=Y)
that is an inverse to the canonical
map
\idtoeq:(X=Y)\to(X\simeq Y)
.
Thanks to this equivalence and the induction principle of identity types, we can
derive
equivalence induction
: given a type family
P
indexed over
types
X
,
Y
and an equivalence
e:{X\simeq Y}
, we get an element of

P(X,Y,e)
at every
X
,
Y
and
e
, as soon as we can construct an
element of
P(X,X,\id_{X})
.

2.3.
Small types and resizing

In this paper we don’t assume propositional resizing axioms, but we do discuss mathematical statements that imply forms of resizing that are unprovable of our foundations, and are therefore themselves unprovable.

For a type universe
\mathcal{U}
, we say that a type
X
in any universe is

\mathcal{U}
-small
if it has an equivalent copy in
\mathcal{U}
, i.e. we have a
type
Y:\mathcal{U}
and an equivalence
Y\simeq X
.
We extend this notion to maps by saying that a map is
\mathcal{U}
-small
if all its
fibers are
\mathcal{U}
-small types.

Definition 2.1

(
\Omega_{\neg\neg}\text{-resizing}_{\mathcal{U}}
)
.

We write
\Omega_{\neg\neg}\text{-resizing}_{\mathcal{U}}
for the assertion that the type

\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}^{\neg\neg}\coloneqq\Sigma(P:\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}),(\neg\neg P\to P)
of

\neg\neg
-stable propositions in
\mathcal{U}
, whose native universe is

\mathcal{U}^{+}
, is
\mathcal{U}
-small.

We note that
\Omega_{\neg\neg}\text{-resizing}_{\mathcal{U}}
is unprovable, as
observed by Andrew Swan (personal communication) with the following proof. Given a small copy of
\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}^{\neg\neg}
, we
can interpret classical second order arithmetic via
\neg\neg
-stable
propositions and subsets, but the consistency strength of univalent mathematics
is below that of classical second order arithmetic by
Rathjen’s
[
Rat17
, Corollary 6.7]
.

3.2.
Algebras of the lifting monad

We first briefly recall the lifting monad
[
EK17
, Section 2]

X:\mathcal{U}
and any universe
\mathcal{T}
, we define

\lifting_{\mathcal{T}}X\coloneqq\Sigma(P:\Omega_{\mathcal{T}}),(P\to X).

This lives in the universe
\mathcal{T}^{+}\sqcup\mathcal{U}
. The unit of the monad is given by

\displaystyle\eta_{X}:X

\displaystyle\to\lifting_{\mathcal{T}}X

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto(\mathbf{1},(\star\mapsto x)).

For further details of the monad structure and laws, see
loc. cit.

Theorem 51 of
[

]
assumes propositional resizing to show that the algebraically injective types are precisely the retracts of algebras of the lifting monad. We now remove the resizing assumption by an application of
Lemma

Lemma 3.8
.

For any
X:\mathcal{U}
, the unit of the lifting monad
\eta:X\to\lifting_{\mathcal{T}}X
is a
\mathcal{T}
-small map.

Proof.

We claim that the fiber of
\eta
at
(P:\mathcal{T},i:\isprop P,{\varphi:P\to X}):\lifting_{\mathcal{T}}X
is equivalent to the proposition
P:\mathcal{T}
. By
[
Esc21
, Lemma 45]
, the unit is an embedding so its fibers are propositions, hence it suffices to construct maps in both directions.
Given
p:P
, we have
(P,i,\varphi)=\eta(\varphi\,p)
, and if
(P,i,\varphi)=\eta\,x
for
x:X
, then
P
holds as the domain of definition of
\eta\,x
is the unit type.
∎

As a corollary, we can improve the universe levels of

Example 4.4
.

Later we will see that the type

of Dedekind reals is not in general
injective (

However, the

Dedekind reals are: these generalize
Dedekind reals by dropping the inhabitedness and locatedness condition. Recall
that a Dedekind cut

is located if
p<x
or
x<q
for every two
rationals
p<q
. The inhabitedness (or boundedness) condition says that there
exist rationals
p
and
q
with
p<x
and
x<q
.
Thus, an extended partial Dedekind real
x
is given by a pair of subsets

satisfying the following conditions, where we
(suggestively) write
p<x
for

and
x<q
for

:

(i)

transitivity:
p<x
and
x<q
together imply
p<q
;

(ii)

roundness:

p<x\iff\exists(r:\mathbb{Q}),\,(p<r)\times(r<x)
and
similarly,
x<q\iff\exists(s:\mathbb{Q}),\,(x<s)\times(s<q)
.

The type of extended partial Dedekind reals may be equipped with the structure
of a directed complete poset, by defining

as

(L_{x}\subseteq L_{y})\times(U_{x}\subseteq U_{y})
.
Indeed, this order is easily checked to be antisymmetric, reflexive and
transitive, and the directed join of a family
(x_{i})_{i:I}
of extended

where

and

.
The transitivity condition for

is verified using that the family

is directed.
Finally, this poset has a least element given by a pair of empty sets.
Thus, the injectivity of the extended partial Dedekind reals follows from

Notice that the extended partial reals are the points of the formal
space constructed by Sara Negri
[

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\im\mathcal{A}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}

\hookleftarrow

4.3.
Types of mathematical structures

We now show that several types of mathematical structures, including
pointed types, monoids,
\infty
-magmas and groups, are flabby, or,
equivalently, injective.
We work with an arbitrary
S:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
and want to show that the
type
\Sigma(X:\mathcal{U}),S\,X
, abbreviated as
\Sigma S
, is
flabby. E.g. for
\infty
-magmas in a universe
\mathcal{U}
, we will take

\mathcal{V}
the same as
\mathcal{U}
and
S\,X=X\to X\to X
, specifying that we
have a binary operation on the type
X
, subject to no axioms, and the
type of
\infty
-magmas will be
\Sigma S
. Similarly, the type of
groups will again be of the form
\Sigma S
for a different choice
of
S
.

In outline, to show that the type
\Sigma S
is flabby, it is enough
to show that
S
is closed under proposition-indexed products. Let

P
be a proposition and
f:P\to\Sigma S
be a partial element of

\Sigma S
. In order to attempt to extend
f
to a total element of

\Sigma S
, first observe that
f
is of the form

\displaystyle f\,p=(A\,p,g\,p)

with

\displaystyle A:P\to\mathcal{U},\qquad g:\Pi(p:P),S(A\,p).

So we need to construct
(X,s):\Sigma S
such that

\displaystyle(X,s)=(A\,p,g\,p)

and hence

\displaystyle X=A\,p

for all
p:P
. Because
P
is a proposition, we have, for
p:P
, an
equivalence

\displaystyle\operatorname{pr}_{p}:\Pi\,A\simeq A\,p,

given by projection out of the product, which, in turn, by univalence,
induces an identification

\displaystyle\phi_{p}:\Pi\,A=A\,p,

as shown in
Lemma

2.3
. Hence we can take

\displaystyle X\coloneqq\Pi\,A.

To construct the required
s:S\,X
, we need an assumption on

S
. As discussed above, our assumption will be that
S
is closed under
proposition-indexed products. Roughly, this means that from an element
of the type
\Pi(p:P),S(A\,p)
we can get an element of the type

S(\Pi\,A)
. To make this precise, we consider the
transport-along-equivalences map
\operatorname{treq}_{X,Y}
, for
X,Y:\mathcal{U}
,
defined by

\displaystyle\operatorname{treq}_{X,Y}:X\simeq Y

\displaystyle\to S\,X\to S\,Y

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle\mapsto\transport^{S}(\operatorname{eqtoid}_{{X},{Y}}{h}).

We will omit the subscripts when they are clear from the context.
Because transports are always equivalences, the map

\operatorname{treq}h:SX\to SY
is an equivalence. We define

\displaystyle\rho_{P,A}:S(\Pi\,A)

\displaystyle\to\Pi(p:P),S(A\,p)

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\mapsto\lambda p.\operatorname{treq}\,\operatorname{pr}_{p}\,s,

where we omit the subscripts when they can be inferred from the context.
We refer to
\rho
as the
canonical map associated to
S
.

Definition 4.8
.

We say that
S
is
closed under proposition-indexed products

if the map
\rho_{P,A}
is an equivalence for all propositions
P

and families
A:P\to\mathcal{U}
.

Lemma 4.9

If

is closed under proposition-indexed products, then
\Sigma S
is
\mathcal{U}
-flabby.

Proof.

Continuing from the above, it remains to construct

such that

, where we have taken

. We take

\displaystyle s\coloneqq\rho_{P,A}^{-1}(g):S(\Pi\,A).

Then, omitting the subscripts

\displaystyle\transport^{S}\,(\phi\,p)\,s

\displaystyle\equiv\rho\,s\,p

\displaystyle\equiv\rho\,(\rho^{-1}g)\,p

Therefore, using the characterization of equality in

\displaystyle(X,s)

\displaystyle\equiv(\Pi\,A,\rho^{-1}g)

\displaystyle=(A\,p,g\,p)

\displaystyle\equiv f\,p.

\displaystyle:X\simeq Y\to(SX\to SY)

5.
Weak excluded middle and De Morgan’s Law

A number of statements regarding injectivity, discussed in the following
sections, imply or are logically equivalent to the principle of weak excluded middle.
It is well known that weak excluded middle is equivalent to De
Morgan’s Law in topos logic
[
Joh02
]
. There are various possibilities to formulate the latter, some of which
are propositions, and some of which are not propositions in general,
but in this section we show that they are all logically equivalent
(they imply each other) although not all of them are equivalent as
types.

Regarding foundational issues, we emphasize that although virtually
all results regarding injectivity rely on univalence, none of the
results in this section do, although some implications rely on
function extensionality (but not on propositional extensionality) and
on the existence of propositional truncations.

We start with a technical lemma that says that a type of the form

is a proposition if and only if both
A
and
B
are propositions and
A
and
B
are together impossible.

Lemma 5.1

For any two types

and

\isprop(A+B)\iff\isprop A\times\isprop B\times\neg(A\times B).

It follows that if

is any proposition then the types

and
\neg P+\neg\neg P
are both propositions, equivalent to
P\vee\neg P
and
\neg P\vee\neg\neg P
respectively.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
. We have that
A
is a proposition because if
a,a^{\prime}:A
then
\inl a=\inl a^{\prime}
as
A+B
is a proposition, and hence
a=a^{\prime}
as
\inl
is always left-cancellable, and similarly
B
is a proposition. Now let

and
b:B
, then
\inl a=\inr b
because
A+B
is a proposition, and using the fact that we always have
\inl a\neq\inr b
, we get a contradiction.

(\Leftarrow)
. Given

, we consider four cases. If both are left elements, then they are equal because
A
is a proposition. If both are right elements, then they are equal because
B
is a proposition. If one is a left element and the other is a right element, we get a contradiction from the assumption that
\neg(A\times B)
, and we are done.

For the consequence of the implication
(\Rightarrow)
, notice that
\neg P
is a proposition by function extensionality, and so we get that
P+\neg P
is a proposition, as
P
and
\neg P
are together impossible, and hence
\|P+\neg P\|\implies P+\neg P
by the elimination principle of propositional truncation, and similarly
\|\neg P+\neg\neg P\|\implies\neg P+\neg\neg P
.
∎

6.
A Rice-like theorem for injective types

In this section we establish a Rice-like theorem
[
Ric53
]
for
injective types, which says that they have no nontrivial decidable
properties unless weak excluded middle holds.
Using decompositions as defined below, we may equivalently say that injective types are
indecomposable unless weak excluded middle holds.

The type of ordinals is a particular example of an injective type and
hence cannot have any nontrivial decidable property unless weak
excluded middle holds. Our result should be compared to

[
KNX23
, Theorem 63]
, which shows that the decidability of
certain properties of ordinals imply excluded middle.

Definition 6.1

(Decomposition)
.

For any type
X
, we define the type of decompositions of
X
by

\decomp X=\Sigma(f:X\to\mathbf{2}),\fiber_{f}(0)\times\fiber_{f}(1).

We say that
X

has a decomposition
if its type of decompositions is
pointed.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any
X:\mathcal{U}
, we have an equivalence

(\Sigma(Y:\mathbf{2}\to\mathcal{U}),(Y_{0}+Y_{1}\simeq X))\,\simeq\,(X\to\mathbf{2}).

Proof.

We have a chain of equivalences

\displaystyle\Sigma(Y:\mathbf{2}\to\mathcal{U}),(Y_{0}+Y_{1}\simeq X)

\displaystyle\simeq\Sigma(Y:\mathbf{2}\to\mathcal{U}),((\Sigma(n:\mathbf{2}),Y_{n})\simeq X)

\displaystyle\simeq\Sigma(Z:\mathcal{U}),(Z\to\mathbf{2})\times(Z\simeq X)

\displaystyle\simeq(X\to\mathbf{2}),

where the middle step follows from the fact that
\mathcal{U}
is a type classifier,
i.e. the type
Z
is obtained as the total space of
Y
with the
projection map
Z\to\mathbf{2}
, and conversely,
Y
is obtained from
Z
by
taking fibers.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

For any type
X:\mathcal{U}
,

\decomp X\,\simeq\,(\Sigma(Y:\mathbf{2}\to\mathcal{U}),(Y_{0}+Y_{1}\simeq X)\times Y_{0}\times Y_{1}).

Moreover, the type of decompositions of
X
is equivalent to the type of
retracts of
X
into
\mathbf{2}
.

Proof.

Under the equivalence that sends a map
f:X\to\mathbf{2}
to its fibers

Y:\mathbf{2}\to\mathcal{U}
, the types
Y_{0}
and
Y_{1}
correspond to

\fiber_{f}(0)
and
\fiber_{f}(1)
, respectively. The first claim now follows
from
Lemma

6.2
.
For the second claim, given
f:X\to\mathbf{2}
, we have equivalences

\displaystyle\fiber_{f}(0)\times\fiber_{f}(1)

\displaystyle\simeq\Pi(n:\mathbf{2}),\Sigma(x:X),f\,x=n

\displaystyle\simeq\Sigma(s:\mathbf{2}\to X),\Pi(n:\mathbf{2}),f(s\,n)=n,

so that the type of decompositions of
X
is equivalent to the type of retracts of
X
into

\mathbf{2}
.
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

If weak excluded middle holds and the type

Proof.

Suppose weak excluded middle holds and that

Using weak excluded middle, define

and

7.
Counterexamples

We discuss examples of types that are not injective in general. In
fact, the assumption that some types are injective implies that all
propositions are projective, that a weak form of the axiom of choice
holds, or that a weak form of excluded middle holds. While in order
to establish examples of injective types we invariably rely on
univalence, in order to establish the counterexamples of this section,
functional and propositional extensionality suffice.

7.1.
The booleans

The following result is also

7.4.
Apartness spaces

The above three counterexamples are instances of a general result
concerning types with nontrivial apartness relations.
There are various notions of apartness relation, which are to
be thought of as constructive strengthenings of the negation of equality, with
varying terminology, see e.g.
[
BR87
, p. 7]
,

[
MRR88
, p. 8]
,
[
BV11
, p. 8]
,

[
BB85
, Definition 2.1]
and

[
TvD88
, Section 8.1.2]
.
Here we adopt the following definition: an
apartness relation

\mathrel{\#}

on a type
X
is a binary proposition-valued relation on
X
that is
symmetric, irreflexive and
cotransitive
:

x\mathrel{\#}y
implies
x\mathrel{\#}z
or
z\mathrel{\#}y
.

Definition 7.5

(Nontrivial apartness)
.

We say that an apartness relation
\mathrel{\#}
on a type
X
is

nontrivial
if we have
x,y:X
with
x\mathrel{\#}y
.

Theorem 7.6
.

If an injective type has a nontrivial apartness, then weak excluded middle
holds.
More precisely, if a type
X:\mathcal{U}
is

\mathcal{T},\mathcal{W}
-injective and has a nontrivial apartness valued in
\mathcal{V}
, then
weak excluded middle holds for the universe
\mathcal{T}
.

Note that the parameters
\mathcal{U}
,
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
can vary freely in the above
theorem.

Proof.

Suppose
X:\mathcal{U}
is a
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{W}
-injective type with a nontrivial

\mathcal{V}
-valued apartness relation, as witnessed by points
x\mathrel{\#}y
, and
let
P:\mathcal{T}
be an arbitrary proposition.
We consider the partial element
f:P+\lnot P\to X
given by

f(\inl p)\coloneqq x
and
f(\inr\nu)\coloneqq y
.
By the injectivity of
X
, we can extend
f
to an element
z:X
such
that
P\to z=x
and
\lnot P\to z=y
.
Taking contrapositives, we obtain
z\neq x\to\lnot P
and

z\neq y\to\lnot\lnot P
.
By assumption,
x\mathrel{\#}y
, and therefore
x\mathrel{\#}z
or
z\mathrel{\#}y

by cotransitivity. But these respectively imply
z\neq x
and
z\neq y
,
so that we get
\lnot P
or
\lnot\lnot P
.
∎

The converse holds in the following form and applies to non-injective types as
well, where we recall that a type in
\mathcal{U}^{+}
is
locally
small
(
[
Rij17
, Definition 4.1]
) if all of its identity types are
equivalent to types in
\mathcal{U}
.

Theorem 7.7
.

(1)

If weak excluded middle holds, then any type with two distinct points has a
nontrivial apartness.
More precisely, if weak excluded middle holds for the universe
\mathcal{U}
, then
any type in
\mathcal{U}
with two distinct points has a nontrivial
\mathcal{U}
-valued
apartness.

(2)

More generally, if weak excluded middle holds for the universe
\mathcal{U}
, then
any locally small type in
\mathcal{U}^{+}
with two distinct points has a nontrivial

\mathcal{U}
-valued apartness.

Proof.

We only spell out the proof of the more general statement. Suppose that weak
excluded middle holds for
\mathcal{U}
and that we have a locally small type

X:\mathcal{U}^{+}
with distinct points
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
.
Since
X
is locally small, we have a
\mathcal{U}
-valued identity type
=_{s}

of
X
. We now define
x\mathrel{\#}y\coloneqq\lnot(x=_{s}y)
.
This relation is clearly irreflexive and symmetric, so it remains to prove
cotransitivity.
Suppose we have
x\mathrel{\#}y
and let
z:X
. We apply weak excluded middle to

x\mathrel{\#}z
:

•

If
\lnot\lnot(x\mathrel{\#}z)
holds, then we have
\lnot(x=_{s}z)
,
i.e.
x\mathrel{\#}z
, since three consecutive negations can be reduced to a
single negation.

•

If
\lnot(x\mathrel{\#}z)
holds, then we claim that
y\mathrel{\#}z
holds.
Indeed, unfolding the definition of
y\mathrel{\#}z
, we see that it suffices
to prove that
y=_{s}z
implies
x\mathrel{\#}z
, but since
x\mathrel{\#}y
,
this follows by transport. ∎

In particular, type universes cannot have nontrivial apartness relations in
constructive type theory:

Corollary 7.8
.

A universe
\mathcal{U}
has a
\mathcal{U}
-valued nontrivial apartness if and
only if weak excluded middle holds for
\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

A universe
\mathcal{U}
is
\mathcal{U}
-injective, so that if we had a

\mathcal{U}
-valued nontrivial apartness, then weak excluded middle holds for

\mathcal{U}
via
Theorem

7.6
.
Conversely, we use

Theorem

7.7

together with the fact that universes are locally small: the
identity type
X=Y
is equivalent (by univalence) to the small type of
equivalences
X\simeq Y
.
∎

This may
be seen as an internal version of
[
Koc24
]
’s metatheoretic result
that Martin-Löf Type Theory does not define
any non-trivial apartness relation on a universe
[
Koc24
, Corollary 5.7]
. In
loc. cit.
this fact
is obtained by a parametricity argument.

7.5.
The simple types

Recall that the
simple types
are the smallest collection of
types closed under
\mathbf{2}
,
\mathbb{N}
and function types.

Corollary 7.9
.

If any simple type is injective, then weak excluded middle holds.

Proof.

We inductively define nontrivial apartness relations on the simple types.
Clearly, the negation of equality on the types
\mathbf{2}
and
\mathbb{N}
is a
nontrivial apartness relation on each of them.
Now suppose that we have a nontrivial apartness relation
\mathrel{\#}^{Y}
on a
(simple) type
Y
and consider the function type
X\to Y
.
Then the relation
f\mathrel{\#}^{X\to Y}g
on
X\to Y
given by

\exists(x:X).\,f\,x\mathrel{\#}^{Y}g\,x
is easily seen to be an apartness.
Moreover, it is nontrivial for we assumed to have
y_{0}\mathrel{\#}^{Y}y_{1}
so that

\lambda x.y_{0}
and
\lambda x.y_{1}
are apart in
X\to Y
.
∎

7.6.
Tight apartness spaces

Another class of counterexamples to injectivity is given by types that have a
tight, but not necessarily nontrivial, apartness relation.
We recall that an apartness
\mathrel{\#}
is
tight
if two elements are equal
as soon as they are not apart, i.e.
\lnot(x\mathrel{\#}y)
implies
x=y
.

Theorem 7.10
.

Suppose
X:\mathcal{U}
is a type with a tight apartness and not a subsingleton.
If
X
is
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{W}
-injective, then the double negation of the principle of weak excluded
middle holds for
\mathcal{T}
.

Note that the universe parameter
\mathcal{W}
can vary freely. Moreover, the tight
apartness can be
\mathcal{V}
-valued for any universe
\mathcal{V}
.

Proof.

Let
X
be as in the statement of the theorem and assume for the
sake of contradiction that weak excluded middle does not hold. We
are going to show that
X
must be a subsingleton, contradicting
one of our assumptions, so let
x,y:X
be arbitrary.
By tightness, it suffices to show that
x
and
y
are not apart.
But if
x
and
y
were apart, then
X
has a nontrivial
apartness, so that we can derive weak excluded middle via

Theorem

7.6
,
contradicting our assumption.
∎

A particular instance of the above theorem is given by the class of

totally separated
types
[
Esc19
]
. These types may be
characterized as precisely those types for which the
boolean apartness
is
tight. For a type
X
, this apartness, denoted by
\mathrel{\#}_{\mathbf{2}}
, is given by

x\mathrel{\#}_{\mathbf{2}}y\coloneqq\exists(p:X\to\mathbf{2}).\,p\,x\neq p\,y.

For comparison, we also give a more direct proof that nontrivial totally separated
types cannot be injective which exploits this particular apartness relation.

Proposition 7.11
.

Suppose
X:\mathcal{U}
is totally separated and not a subsingleton.
If
X
is
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{W}
-injective, then the double negation of weak excluded
middle holds for
\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

Suppose
X
is totally separated, injective and not a subsingleton. Using

Theorem

6.10
we reduce the claim to showing
that it is false that
X
has no decomposition.
Indeed, if
X
has no decomposition, then any map
X\to\mathbf{2}
must be
constant (in the sense that any of its values must agree).
In particular, any two elements of
X
must be equal by tightness of the
boolean apartness, contradicting our assumption that
X
is not a
subsingleton.
∎

7.7.
Small types

All the examples of injective types that we have presented so far, namely type universes, the type of small ordinals, the type of pointed types,
etc., are all large collections of types.
This is no coincidence: any nontrivial injective type is large unless
the weak form of propositional resizing introduced
in
Section

2.3
holds, as we now show.

Theorem 7.12
.

If we have a
(\mathcal{U}\sqcup\mathcal{V}),\mathcal{W}
-injective type
D:\mathcal{U}
that has two distinct points
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
, then
\Omega_{\neg\neg}\text{-resizing}_{\mathcal{U}}
holds.

In the above theorem, the universe parameters
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
vary freely
and, for instance, may be taken to be the lowest universe
\mathcal{U}_{0}
.
A particular instance of the theorem is that we cannot have a
\mathcal{U}
-injective
type that itself lives in
\mathcal{U}
unless
\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}^{\neg\neg}
is

\mathcal{U}
-small.

Proof.

Given such a type
D
and points
x_{0},x_{1}:D
, we define

f:\mathbf{2}\to D
as
f\coloneqq[x_{0},x_{1}]
.
Further consider the embedding
j:\mathbf{2}\to\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}^{\neg\neg}
that sends

0
to
\bot
and
1
to
\top
.
This is a
\mathcal{U}
-small map as its fiber at a
\lnot\lnot
-stable
proposition
P:\mathcal{U}
is the type
{P+\neg P}:\mathcal{U}
.
Hence, by
Lemma

3.6
, we get an extension

s:\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}^{\neg\neg}\to D
of
f
along
j
, i.e.
s\circ j=f

holds.
We claim that the map
s
has a retraction which completes the proof, as retracts
of small types are small
[
dJE23
, Theorem 2.13]
.
We define

\displaystyle r:D

\displaystyle\to\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}^{\neg\neg}

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle\mapsto d\neq x_{0}

which is well defined as negated propositions are
\neg\neg
-stable.
To see that
r\circ s=\id
, we claim that
s\,P\neq x_{0}\iff P
.
Indeed, if
P
holds, then
P=\top
, so

s\,P=s(j\,1)=f\,1=x_{1}\neq x_{0}
.
For the converse we use that
P
is
\neg\neg
-stable, so that it suffices
to prove
\lnot P\Rightarrow{s\,P=x_{0}}
. But if
\lnot P
holds, then

P=\bot
, so
s\,P=s(j\,0)=f\,0=x_{0}
, completing the proof.
∎

This internal fact is comparable to the metatheoretic result
[
AvdBGS12
, Corollary 10]
, which states that CZF is consistent with the statement that the only injective
sets
(as opposed to classes) are singletons, where the general uniformity principle is used for that purpose.

7.8.
The type of inhabited types

We proved in
Proposition

4.1
and Example
4.13
(
1
) that the type

\Sigma(X:\mathcal{U}),\neg\neg X
of non-empty types and the type
\Sigma(X:\mathcal{U}),X
of pointed types are both injective.
We now show that the type

\operatorname{Inh}\coloneqq\Sigma(X:\mathcal{U}),\|X\|

of inhabited types is not injective in general.
Bearing in mind the chain of implications
X\to\|X\|\to\neg\neg X
, this illustrates the constructive differences between propositional
truncation and double negation.

Theorem 7.13
.

The following are equivalent:

(1)

the type
\operatorname{Inh}
of inhabited types is injective;

(2)

the type
\operatorname{Inh}
is a retract of
\mathcal{U}
;

(3)

all propositions are projective: for every
proposition
P:\mathcal{U}
and type family
Y:P\to\mathcal{U}
, we have

\left(\Pi(p:P),\|Y\,p\|\right)\to\left\|\Pi(p:P),Y\,p\right\|;

(4)

all types have unspecified split support, in the sense that
for all
X:\mathcal{U}
, we have

\left\|\,\|X\|\to X\,\right\|.

The equivalence of (
3
) and (
4
) was shown
in
[
KECA17
, Theorem 7.7]
.
As also noted there, if
Y\,p
in (
3
) is a two-element
set, then this is known as the
world’s simplest axiom of choice
, which fails
in some toposes
[
FŠ82
]
.
Also notice that (
3
), and thus all the others, follow from
excluded middle.

Proof.

We prove

\eqref{split-support}\Rightarrow\eqref{Inh-retract}\Rightarrow\eqref{Inh-injective}\Rightarrow\eqref{projective-props}\Rightarrow\eqref{split-support}
.

\eqref{split-support}\Rightarrow\eqref{Inh-retract}
: By unspecified
split support we have a map
r:\mathcal{U}\to\operatorname{Inh}
that sends a type

X
to the inhabited type
\|X\|\to X
.
When
X
is inhabited, we have

(\|X\|\to X)\simeq(\mathbf{1}\to X)\simeq X
, so by univalence, the
first projection
\operatorname{Inh}\to\mathcal{U}
is a section of
r
.

\eqref{Inh-retract}\Rightarrow\eqref{Inh-injective}
: This holds because
injective types are closed under retracts.

\eqref{Inh-injective}\Rightarrow\eqref{projective-props}
: Suppose that

\operatorname{Inh}
is injective and let
P:\mathcal{U}
be a proposition with a type family

Y:P\to\mathcal{U}
such that each
Y\,p
is inhabited.
The map
f:P\to\operatorname{Inh}
takes
p:P
to the inhabited type
Y\,p
.
By flabbiness of
\operatorname{Inh}
, we get an inhabited type
X
such that if we have

p:P
then
X=Y\,p
.
In particular, we have a map
X\to\Pi(p:P),Y\,p
, which proves the
desired
\|\Pi(p:P),Y\,p\|
as
X
is inhabited.

\eqref{projective-props}\Rightarrow\eqref{split-support}
: Given

X:\mathcal{U}
, simply take
P\coloneqq\|X\|
and

Y\,p\coloneqq X
in (
3
).
∎

7.9.
Infinite real projective space

The implication
\eqref{Inh-injective}\Rightarrow\eqref{split-support}

from
Theorem

7.13
may be
generalized to the following result, which can then be instantiated to get a
non-injectivity result about the type of two-element types.

Lemma 7.14
.

Let
D:\mathcal{U}
be a
\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}
-injective type with a type family

T:D\to\mathcal{T}
.
If the type
\Sigma(d:D),\left\|T\,d\right\|
is

(\mathcal{T}\sqcup\mathcal{V}^{\prime}),\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
-injective, then each
T\,d
has unspecified split
support, i.e.
\left\|\left\|T\,d\right\|\to T\,d\right\|
for all
d:D
.

Note that the parameters
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
can vary freely; in particular,
we can take them to be
\mathcal{U}_{0}
.
The above indeed generalizes the implication

\eqref{Inh-injective}\Rightarrow\eqref{split-support}
from

Theorem

7.13
by taking

D\coloneqq\mathcal{U}
and
T:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
to be the identity.

Proof.

From the assumed injectivity of the type
\Sigma(d:D),\left\|T\,d\right\|
,

Theorem

4.26
yields a function
f:D\to D
such that
for all
x:D
we have

(2)

\left\|T(f\,x)\right\|\text{ and }\left\|T\,x\right\|\to{f\,x}=x.

To prove
\left\|\left\|T\,d\right\|\to T\,d\right\|
for arbitrary

d:D
, we first make use of the functoriality of propositional truncation to prove

\|T\,d\|\to T\,d
from
T(f\,d)
.
But given
t:T(f\,d)
and
\tau:\left\|T\,d\right\|
, we get

f\,d=d
via (

), so that we can transport
t
along
this equality to get an element of

, as desired.
∎

Recall that the type of two-element types is given by

\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{\infty}\coloneqq\sum_{X:\mathcal{U}_{0}}\left\|X\simeq\mathbf{2}\right\|.

The notation
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{\infty}
comes from
[

]
, where
it is shown that this type has the universal property
of the infinite dimensional real projective space, defined as the sequential
colimit of the finite dimensional real projective spaces. In particular,

\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{\infty}
, which a priori lives in
\mathcal{U}_{1}
, is actually
\mathcal{U}_{0}
-small in
the presence of such colimits.

We have already mentioned the world’s simplest axiom of choice,
just after

Theorem

7.13
, which
is
Item

3
in that theorem but restricted to
families

where

has precisely two elements.
For our purposes, an equivalent formulation will be convenient.

Lemma 7.15
.

The world’s simplest axiom of choice is equivalent to the
assertion that for every
X:\mathcal{U}
, the type

has
unspecified split support.

Proof.

Suppose the world’s simplest axiom of choice holds and let

X:\mathcal{U}
be arbitrary.
We set
P\coloneqq\left\|X\simeq\mathbf{2}\right\|
and let
Y:P\to\mathcal{U}
be
constantly
X\simeq\mathbf{2}
.
Then
X\simeq\mathbf{2}
has unspecified split support by the world’s simplest
axiom of choice, provided we show that each
Y\,p
has exactly two elements.
So suppose we have
p:P\equiv\left\|X\simeq\mathbf{2}\right\|
. Since we are
proving a proposition, namely
\|Y\,p\simeq\mathbf{2}\|
, we may assume that
X\simeq\mathbf{2}
in which case

X\simeq\mathbf{2}
is equivalent to
\mathbf{2}\simeq\mathbf{2}
which indeed has
exactly two elements.

Conversely, suppose that for each
X:\mathcal{U}
, the type
X\simeq\mathbf{2}
has
unspecified split support.
Let
P:\mathcal{U}
be an arbitrary proposition and let
Y:P\to\mathcal{U}

8.
Discussion

After the non-injectivity of
\mathbb{R}\mathsf{P}^{\infty}
, it is natural to wonder about the
injectivity of higher homotopy types, other than the universe, such as the
circle.
Ulrik Buchholtz and David Wärn have sketched models that invalidate the
injectivity of the circle
[
BW23
]
. We leave it as an open
question whether one can derive a constructive or homotopical taboo under the
assumption that the circle is injective.

An anonymous reviewer of our TYPES abstract
[
dJE25
]
suggested that
some of our results could be generalized to weak factorization
systems
[
Rie14
]
.
A full investigation, in particular when it comes to the connections to

algebraic
weak factorization systems, is beyond the scope of this
paper. However, in our Agda development we have already constructed
factorizations and liftings where the left class is given by embeddings and the
right class is given by fiberwise injective maps, i.e. those maps whose fibers
are all injective
types
[
dJon25
]
.
