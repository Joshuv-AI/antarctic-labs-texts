Measuring directional bias amplification in image captions using predictability
Rahul Nair*, Bhanu Tokas*, Neel Shah, Hannah Kerner

Abstract
When we train models on biased ML datasets, they not only learn these biases but can inflate them at test time — a phenomenon called bias amplification. To measure bias amplification in ML datasets, many co-occurrence-based metrics have been proposed. Co-occurrence-based metrics are effective in measuring bias amplification in simple problems like image classification. However, these metrics are ineffective for complex problems like image captioning as they cannot capture the semantics of a caption. To measure bias amplification in captions, prior work introduced a predictability-based metric called Leakage in Captioning (LIC). While LIC captures the semantics and context of captions, it has limitations. LIC cannot identify the direction in which bias is amplified, poorly estimates dataset bias due to a weak vocabulary substitution strategy, and is highly sensitive to attacker models (a hyperparameter in predictability-based metrics). To overcome these issues, we propose Directional Predictability Amplification in Captioning (DPAC). DPAC measures directional bias amplification in captions, provides a better estimate of dataset bias using an improved substitution strategy, and is less sensitive to attacker models. Our experiments on the COCO captioning dataset show how DPAC is the most reliable metric to measure bias amplification in captions.

Introduction

When we train models on biased datasets, they can make biased predictions. Sometimes, models amplify these biases, meaning their predictions are more biased than the training data itself. This increase in bias from the dataset to the model’s predictions is called bias amplification.

Several metrics (
BA_{MALS}

[
25
]
,
BA_{\rightarrow}

[
21
]
,
Multi_{\rightarrow}

[
24
]
) have been proposed to measure bias amplification in classification tasks. Consider a training dataset with attribute
A
(e.g., gender) and task
T
(e.g., loan approval). If a certain
T-A
pair is over-represented in the training dataset (e.g., rejection-woman, acceptance-man), these metrics measure the increase in co-occurrence for this
T-A
pair in the model predictions.

Metrics like
BA_{MALS}
,
BA_{\rightarrow}
, and
Multi_{\rightarrow}
can only be applied to categorical data. Hence, they measure bias amplification only for simple tasks like classification. Recent works have focused on measuring bias amplification in more advanced tasks like image captioning
[
9
,
1
]
. Hirota et al.
[
9
]
showed that previous metrics (e.g.,
BiasAmp_{\rightarrow}
and
Multi_{\rightarrow}
) are not effective in measuring bias amplification in captions, as these metrics ignore the semantics of a caption. Consider a human-generated caption (HGC): “A woman is cooking” and the corresponding model-generated cation (MGC): “A woman is making dinner”. In both these captions, a female
A
performs the same task
T
(cooking). Previous metrics would treat “cooking” and “making dinner” as different tasks, thus underestimating the bias.

To measure bias amplification between a human-generated caption (HGC) and a model-generated caption (MGC), recent works proposed predictability-based metrics like LIC
[
9
]
and
ImageCaptioner^{2}

[
1
]
. LIC is the current state-of-the-art metric to measure bias amplification in captions. We do not consider
ImageCaptioner^{2}
for bias amplification in captioning because it measures bias amplification between both images and captions, whereas bias amplification should only be measured between captions.

In LIC, Hirota et al. first masked any gender-revealing tokens (e.g., man, he, she) in the HGC and MGC. For instance, the caption “a woman is cooking” after masking would be — a
<
gender
>
is cooking. They measured how easy it is to predict gender from the masked HGC (
\text{LIC}_{D}
) and the masked MGC (
\text{LIC}_{M}
).
\text{LIC}_{D}
and
\text{LIC}_{M}
are measured using an attacker model that predicts gender from the masked caption. Bias amplification (or LIC) is the difference between
\text{LIC}_{M}
and
\text{LIC}_{D}
. Since LIC focuses on predictability, it effectively captures the semantics of a caption. However, it has limitations:

1.

LIC lacks directionality:
In the model-generated caption “a woman is making dinner”, we need to identify if the captioning model amplified the bias towards predicting a woman when it observed a cooking activity in the image (
T\rightarrow A
) or towards predicting cooking when it observed a woman in the image (
A\rightarrow T
).

2.

LIC ignores context in vocabulary substitution:
To align the vocabulary (i.e., the number of unique tokens) in the HGCs (
V_{HGC}
) and the MGCs (
V_{MGC}
), LIC replaces additional tokens that were used in
V_{HGC}
but not used in
V_{MGC}
with a constant token,
<
unk
>
. Consider two HGCs: “a man is holding a stick” and “a woman is holding a baby”. If the tokens stick and baby are not present in
V_{MGC}
, vocabulary substitution would turn both HGCs into the same caption: a
<
gender
>
is holding a
<
unk
>
. LIC underestimates the bias in the HGCs (
\text{LIC}_{D}
) as constant substitution removes words that convey meaningful information (e.g., stick, baby).

3.

LIC is sensitive to attacker models:
Since LIC is the absolute difference between
\text{LIC}_{M}
and
\text{LIC}_{D}
, the choice of attacker model influences LIC values. An attacker model that poorly predicts gender yields different
\text{LIC}_{M}
and
\text{LIC}_{D}
values than an attacker model with high predictability. This makes it hard for users to decide which attacker model is suitable to measure bias amplification in their captioning model.

We propose a new metric called Directional Predictability Amplification in Captioning (DPAC) to measure bias amplification in image captions. DPAC addresses the limitations of LIC in the following ways:

1.

DPAC is the first metric to measure directional bias amplification in captions. Users can correctly identify the source of bias amplification in their captioning models.

2.

DPAC uses an improved vocabulary substitution strategy. Instead of replacing words in the HGCs with
<
unk
>
, DPAC replaces them with the closest matching word in the captioning model’s vocabulary (
V_{MGC}
). This strategy retains more information about the HGCs, providing a better estimate of the bias in the HGCs.

3.

DPAC measures the relative change in bias amplification as opposed to absolute changes in LIC. This makes DPAC minimally sensitive to attacker models, allowing users to choose any attacker model for measuring bias amplification in their captioning models.

Related Works

Bias Amplification

Zhao et al.
[
25
]
proposed the first bias amplification metric for machine learning models, specifically for multi-label object classification and visual semantic role labeling models. The metric measured increases in co-occurrences of the majority
A-T
pairs from the dataset. Foulds et al.
[
7
]
measured bias amplification using the difference in “differential fairness”, a measure of the difference in co-occurrences of
A-T
pairs across different values of
A
.

Several works have focused on application-specific measures for bias amplification. Lin et al.
[
11
]
proposed a new metric called bias disparity to measure bias amplification in recommender systems. Seshadri et al.
[
16
]
measured bias amplification for text-to-image generation using the increase in percentage bias in generated vs. training samples.

Bias Amplification for Image Captioning

Hendricks et al.
[
8
]
was one of the earlier works measuring biases specifically for image captioning. They introduced two metrics:
Gender\ error
, which calculates the number of incorrectly predicted gender words, and
Gender\ ratio
, which classifies each caption as either male or female based on the presence of some predefined gender-indicating word. It then computes the ratio of male captions to female captions.

Hirota et al.
[
9
]
later proposed the metric, LIC, for measuring bias amplification in captioning. Unlike existing metrics, LIC can understand the semantics of the captions. For example, previously discussed metrics would treat “making dinner” and “cooking” as two separate tasks, while LIC treats them as the same task. Hence, it is “semantics informed”. Abdelrahman et al.
[
1
]
proposed another metric,
ImageCaptioner^{2}
, which used prompt-based image captioning models to measure bias. The metric confounds the biases from the masked images and the captions. As we want to measure the increase in biases only in the captions, the metric is unreliable as it measures a different quantity.

Directionality in Bias Amplification

While all the metrics discussed above can measure the magnitude of bias amplification, none of these can measure the direction in which biases are amplified. Wang et al.
[
21
]
introduced the first metric,
BA_{\rightarrow}
, capable of disentangling the direction of bias amplification for classification tasks. Zhao et al.
[
24
]
improved upon
BA_{\rightarrow}
by creating a new metric capable of simultaneously measuring directional bias amplification for multiple attributes. However,
Multi_{\rightarrow}
cannot differentiate between positive and negative bias amplification (i.e., increase or decrease in biases). Recently, Tokas et al.
[
17
]
introduced the DPA metric, which combined directionality with predictability-based metrics. However, these metrics
BA_{\rightarrow}
,
Multi_{\rightarrow}
, and DPA are not semantics informed, making them unsuitable for captioning.

Methodology

3.1
Previous Metric

Before explaining our proposed metric, we briefly discuss LIC
[
9
]
, the current state-of-the-art method for measuring bias amplification in captioning.

3.1.1
Formulation

To measure bias amplification, LIC measures the difference in biases in the model-generated caption (MGC), denoted as
\hat{s}
, and biases in the human-generated caption (HGC), denoted as
s
. These biases are measured by masking the attribute (i.e., gender) words in the caption and then measuring the predictability of the attribute from the masked caption. For example, the HGC,
s=
“A man is playing,” becomes the masked HGC,
s^{*}=
“A
<
gender
>
is playing”. The MGC (
\hat{s}
) is similarly masked (
\hat{s}^{*}
). This masking removed any apparent gender information in the captions.

After gender masking, they equalized the vocabulary of the masked HGCs,
s^{*}
, and masked MGCs,
\hat{s}^{*}
. Let
V_{HGC}
and
V_{MGC}
represent the vocabulary (i.e., the unique words across all captions) of
s^{*}
and
\hat{s}^{*}
, respectively. LIC replaced all words that were present in
V_{HGC}
but not in
V_{MGC}
with a constant word token,
<
unk
>
, transforming the masked HGC
s^{*}
to the masked/equalized HGC
\tilde{s}^{*}
. We refer to this replacement technique as constant substitution.

Vocabulary equalization using constant substitution is important as MGCs tend to have a smaller vocabulary than HGCs. This difference in vocabulary size can lead to an incorrect estimation of bias amplification.

LIC then trains attacker models
f_{D}
and
f_{M}
to predict the gender (
a
), given masked and equalized HGC and the masked MGC, respectively. Each attacker model has two parts: a sentence encoder and a dense neural network. The sentence encoder converts the masked captions into vector embeddings, which the neural network uses to predict
a
.

LIC weights the accuracy of the attacker model with the confidence score of the predictions to compute the bias in captions. They defined the bias in the HGCs (
\text{LIC}_{D}
) as:

LIC_{D}=\frac{1}{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}f_{D}(\tilde{s_{i}}^{*})*1[argmax(f_{D}(\tilde{s_{i}}^{*})=a_{i}]

(1)

where
|\tilde{s}^{*}|
is the number of HGCs and
a_{i}
is the ground truth attribute (i.e., gender) associated with
i^{th}
caption. Similarly, they defined the bias in the MGCs (
\text{LIC}_{M}
) as:

LIC_{M}=\frac{1}{|\hat{s}^{*}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\hat{s}^{*}|}f_{M}(\hat{s_{i}}^{*})*1[argmax(f_{M}(\hat{s_{i}}^{*})=a_{i}]

(2)

They defined bias amplification as the increase in biases from the HGCs (dataset) to the MGCs (model predictions):

LIC=LIC_{M}-LIC_{D}

(3)

3.1.2
Weaknesses

Incompatible with Directionality

We know that the attacker model
f
tries to model the relationship between input
\tilde{s}^{*}
and output
a
, i.e., it tries to approximate
P(a|s^{*})
. Thus we can rewrite equation
1
as:

LIC_{D}=\frac{1}{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}P(a=a_{i}|\tilde{s_{i}}^{*})|(argmax(P(a|\tilde{s_{i}}^{*}))=a_{i})

(4)

For simplicity, we can write equation
4
as:

LIC_{D}=\frac{1}{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}P(a=a_{i}|\tilde{s_{i}}^{*})

(5)

Similarly, we can rewrite equations
2
and
3
as:

LIC_{M}=\frac{1}{|\hat{s}^{*}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\hat{s}^{*}|}P(a_{i}|\hat{s_{i}}^{*})

(6)

LIC=\frac{1}{|\hat{s}^{*}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\hat{s}^{*}|}P(a_{i}|\hat{s_{i}}^{*})-\frac{1}{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}P(a_{i}|\tilde{s_{i}}^{*})

(7)

Equation
7
shows that LIC compares probabilities with fixed posteriors. This deviates from Wang et al.’s
[
21
]
definition of directionality, which requires fixed priors. Fixed priors are necessary for directionality, as we should observe the changes in the MGCs and HGCs (posteriors) for a fixed property (i.e., task or attribute) in the input image (prior).

For
T\rightarrow A
,
BA_{\rightarrow}
measures the change in
P(\hat{A}|T)
with respect to
P(A|T)
, i.e., change in the conditional probability of
\hat{A}
vs.
A
with respect to a fixed prior
T
. Similarly, for
A\rightarrow T
,
BA_{\rightarrow}
measures change in the conditional probability of
\hat{T}
vs.
T
with respect to a fixed prior
A
.

LIC has fixed posteriors instead of priors, which is incompatible with the existing definition of directionality.

Sensitivity to choice of attacker

As noted in Abdelrahman et al.
[
1
]
, the bias amplification value reported by LIC is significantly impacted by the sentence encoder used for the attacker model. For example, looking at the results from LIC
[
9
]
paper, for the LSTM sentence encoder, Transformer
[
18
]
has a lower LIC score than UpDn
[
2
]
. But the trend reverses for the BERT
[
6
]
base sentence encoder wherein Updn has a lower LIC score than Transformer. These inconsistencies could result from either the performance differences between the LSTM vs. BERT-based sentence encoders or bias artifacts from BERT’s pretraining. Thus, LIC is highly sensitive to the choice of the attacker model.

Missing Context

LIC uses constant substitution to replace additional words in
V_{HGC}
that were not present in
V_{MGC}
. Consider two HGCs: “a
<
gender
>
changing a tire” and “a
<
gender
>
changing a diaper”. If the words tire and diaper are not present in
V_{MGC}
, both these captions become: “a
<
gender
>
changing a
<
unk
>
”. LIC underestimates the bias in the HGCs as it removes words from the HGCs that convey meaningful information (e.g., tire, diaper). In Sections
4.2
and
5.2
, we show how constant substitution leads to lower bias estimates in LIC.

3.2
Our Method

We propose
Directional Predictability Amplification in Captioning (DPAC)
, an improved bias amplification metric for image captioning.

3.2.1
Formulation

As noted in
3.1.2
, there are two directions for biases:
A\rightarrow T
and
T\rightarrow A
. To find bias amplification in the
A\rightarrow T
direction, we mask the gender words in the captions. For example, “He is eating carrots” becomes “
<
gender
>
is eating carrots”. But, for the
T\rightarrow A
direction, we mask the task words in the captions. For the same example, masking the task object would give: “He is eating
<
obj
>
”.

As noted in section
3.1.2
, LIC poorly estimates the bias in the HGCs, as it loses meaningful information about the HGCs through constant substitution. We address this shortcoming by using contextual substitution:

v_{new}=\begin{cases}\min\limits_{v^{\prime}\in V_{MGC}}dist(v,v^{\prime}),&dist(v,v^{\prime})<\delta\\
<\text{unk}>,&otherwise\end{cases}

(8)

Here,
dist(v,v^{\prime})
is a function that measures the semantic distance between words
v
and
v^{\prime}
.
\delta
is a user-defined parameter. Hence, in our substitution technique, we replace new words in
V_{HGC}
with their closest match in
V_{MGC}
, given that the match is within a reasonable distance
\delta
. Otherwise, we substitute it with the token
<
unk
>
. For the purpose of our experiments, we use the cosine distance in GloVe
[
14
]
embedding space as the
dist
function. We conducted a sensitivity analysis for our distance parameter
\delta
using GloVe in Section
A
.

After masking followed by contextual substitution, we define bias in the HGCs in the
A\rightarrow T
direction as:

\Psi_{D,A\rightarrow T}=\frac{1}{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}P(a_{i}|f_{D}^{A}(\tilde{s_{i}}^{*}))*\frac{P(t_{i})}{P(a_{i})}

(9)

Here,
P(t_{i})
represents the probability of the object words present in the HGC (
s_{i}
).
P(a_{i})
represents the ground truth probability of the attribute in the HGC. For an
i^{th}
caption, “He is playing the guitar,”
P(t_{i})
represents the probability of the object “guitar” occurring in a caption across the HGC. Meanwhile,
P(a_{i})
represents the probability of the caption having a “male” person (based on the word “He”). The term
\frac{P(t_{i})}{P(a_{i}}
helps align with directionality as shown in section
3.2.2
.

Furthermore,
f_{D}^{A}
represents the attacker model trained on
\tilde{s}^{*}
to predict protected attribute
A
.
P(a_{i}|f_{D}^{A}(\tilde{s_{i}}^{*}))
represents the probability of
a_{i}
as predicted by
f_{D}^{A}
for input caption
\tilde{s_{i}^{*}}
.

Similarly, we define model bias in
A\rightarrow T
direction as:

\Psi_{M,A\rightarrow T}=\frac{1}{|\hat{s}^{*}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\hat{s}^{*}|}P(a_{i}|f_{M}^{A}(\hat{s_{i}}^{*}))*\frac{P(\hat{t}_{i})}{P(a_{i})}

(10)

Here,
f_{M}^{A}
represents the attacker model trained on
\hat{s}^{*}
to predict protected attribute
A
.
P(\hat{t_{i}})
represents the probability of the object words present in
\hat{s_{i}}
.

Using equations
10
and
9
we define bias amplification in
A\rightarrow T
direction as:

\text{DPAC}_{A\rightarrow T}=\frac{\Psi_{M,A\rightarrow T}-\Psi_{D,A\rightarrow T}}{\Psi_{M,A\rightarrow T}+\Psi_{D,A\rightarrow T}}

(11)

We explain the need for the normalization term
\Psi_{M,A\rightarrow T}+\Psi_{D,A\rightarrow T}
in the denominator in section
3.2.2
.

Similarly, for
T\rightarrow A
direction we define,

\Psi_{D,T\rightarrow A}=\frac{1}{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}P(t_{i}|f_{D}^{T}(\tilde{s_{i}}^{*}))*\frac{P(a_{i})}{P(t_{i})}

(12)

\Psi_{M,T\rightarrow A}=\frac{1}{|\hat{s}^{*}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\hat{s}^{*}|}P(t_{i}|f_{M}^{T}(\hat{s_{i}}^{*}))*\frac{P(\hat{a}_{i})}{P(t_{i})}

(13)

Here,
f_{D}^{T},f_{M}^{T}
represent attacker functions trained on
\tilde{s}^{*}
and
\hat{s}^{*}
, respectively to predict task objects
T
.
P(a_{i}),P(\hat{a_{i}})
represents the probability of attribute (i.e. gender) present in
s_{i}
and
\hat{s_{i}}
respectively.

Finally, bias amplification in
T\rightarrow A
direction is defined as:

\text{DPAC}_{T\rightarrow A}=\frac{\Psi_{M,T\rightarrow A}-\Psi_{D,T\rightarrow A}}{\Psi_{M,T\rightarrow A}+\Psi_{D,T\rightarrow A}}

(14)

Note that, as the predictability depends on the attacker models’ performance, we train the attacker models multiple times and take the bias scores based on the best-performing models. This prevents inaccurate reporting of bias from suboptimal training of attacker models, for example, due to poor initialization or convergence to local minima.

3.2.2
Benefits

The new formulation gives the following advantages:

Directional

As explained in section
3.1.2
, a directional bias amplification metric measures the difference of probabilities in MGCs and HGCs with fixed priors. Hence, we can say:

BA_{A\rightarrow T}=\frac{1}{|\hat{D}|}\sum_{i}^{\hat{D}}P(\hat{t}_{i}|a_{i})-\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{i}^{D}P(t_{i}|a_{i})

(15)

Let’s focus on the second term on the right side of the above equation. We can apply the Bayes rule to transform the term as shown below.

\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{i}^{D}P(t_{i}|a_{i})=\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{i}^{D}P(a_{i}|t_{i})*\frac{P(t_{i})}{P(a_{i})}

(16)

In a given masked caption: “The
<
gender
>
is playing the guitar”, we assume that the only significant source of bias is the task object “guitar.” Based on this, we can say that
P(a_{i}|\tilde{s_{i}}^{*})\approx P(a_{i}|t_{i})
. Furthermore,
P(a_{i}|s_{i}^{*})
can be expressed as
Prob(f_{D}^{A}(\tilde{s_{i}}^{*}),a_{i})
as both represent probability of
a_{i}
given
\tilde{s_{i}}^{*}
. Hence, we rewrite the above equation as:

\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{i}^{D}P(t_{i}|a_{i})=\frac{1}{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\tilde{s}^{*}|}P(a_{i}|f_{D}^{A}(\tilde{s_{i}}^{*}))*\frac{P(t_{i})}{P(a_{i})}

(17)

The right-hand term is the same as
\Psi_{D,A\rightarrow T}
. Similarly, we can say the first term on the right-hand side of equation
15
is the same as
\Psi_{M,A\rightarrow T}
. Thus, we can rewrite, equation
15
as:

BA_{A\rightarrow T}=\Psi_{M,A\rightarrow T}-\Psi_{D,A\rightarrow T}

(18)

Dividing by the normalization term of
(\Psi_{M,A\rightarrow T}+\Psi_{D,A\rightarrow T})
gives our formulation as described in equation
11
. Thus, we show our metric is directional.

Reduced sensitivity to sentence encoders

Tokas et al.
[
17
]
showed that the normalization term used in the denominator of equations
11
,
14
makes it less sensitive to the choice of dense neural network used in attacker models. We apply the same normalization to reduce sensitivity to the choice of sentence encoder in the attacker model. This reduced sensitivity is verified in section
4.1
. Also, the normalization restricts the estimated bias amplification in a fixed range of (-1,1).

Improved Context

Contextual substitution retains more information about the HGCs than constant substitution. Consider the caption: “The
<
gender
>
is eating a blueberry”. If the word blueberry is not present in the captioning model’s vocabulary (
V_{MGC}
), constant substitution will convert this caption to: “The
<
gender
>
is eating a
<
unk
>
”. Contextual substitution would produce a caption that is closer in meaning to the original HGC: “The
<
gender
>
is eating a fruit.”

In Sections
4.2
and
5.2
, we showed how contextual substitution preserves more meaning about the HGCs compared to constant substitution. We also evaluated how preserving more information (with contextual substitution) impacts the bias score for the HGCs, as measured by LIC (
LIC_{D}
) and our metric, DPAC (
\Psi_{D}
).

4.1
Attacker Model Sensitivity

Hirota et al.
[
22
]
found that simply changing the sentence encoder (e.g., LSTM, BERT) in an attacker model led to very different LIC values for the same captioning model. This makes it hard for users to decide which sentence encoder (in the attacker model) can accurately measure bias amplification in their captioning model. Since LIC measures absolute change between dataset leakage and model leakage, LIC values depend heavily on how well the sentence encoder predicts gender. A sentence encoder that poorly predicts gender would yield very different
\text{LIC}_{M}
and
\text{LIC}_{D}
values compared to a sentence encoder with high gender predictability.

We recreated Hirota et al.’s sentence encoder experiment using LIC and our metric DPAC, which measures the relative change between
\Psi_{M}
and
\Psi_{D}

5

[

4.3
Directionality

To evaluate if LIC and DPAC can capture directional bias amplification in captions, we conducted an experiment similar to Tokas et al.
[
17
]
. We modified
T\rightarrow A
biases in the COCO captions dataset by progressively masking people. We created four versions of the dataset: (1) the original dataset and three perturbed versions, where the people in the foreground of the image were progressively masked using three techniques: (2) partially masking the segment, (3) completely masking the segment, (3) completely masking the bounding-box. We masked only the people in the foreground because the COCO captions dataset provided gender annotations only for them.

For each dataset version, we trained FC, an image captioning model, and generated captions (MGCs). We measured model leakage (
\text{LIC}_{M}
) when we predict gender from a caption where the gender-revealing tokens (e.g., he, she, him) are masked (
T\rightarrow A
). As the person is progressively masked in each dataset, the FC model will increasingly rely on task objects to predict gender, gradually increasing the
T\rightarrow A
bias. We evaluated if DPAC and LIC can capture this increase in
T\rightarrow A
bias.

We compared model leakage instead of bias amplification values as creating captions (HGCs) for the masked versions of the COCO captions dataset was not feasible. Without HGC, we cannot measure dataset leakage, which is needed to calculate bias amplification.

Results

As mentioned in Section
4

8

4

2

[
9

. All captioning models amplify biases, and different metrics capture different aspects of bias, as noted in
[
9

. While Table
2
provides a general comparison of bias amplification metrics, in this section, we only discuss LIC and DPAC in further detail. As mentioned in Sections
1
and
2
, all other metrics are not well-suited for measuring bias amplification in captioning, as they cannot capture the semantics of a caption.

5.1
Attacker Model Sensitivity

In Table
3
, we show the LIC and DPAC scores for the captioning models for different sentence encoders in the attacker model. For each captioning model, we see that the DPAC scores of different sentence encoders are much closer to each other compared to the LIC scores. This shows that DPAC is less sensitive to the choice of sentence encoders in an attacker model.

We also rank the LIC and DPAC scores for each encoder for the captioning models (from
1
to
8
). For DPAC, we see that the rank achieved by every captioning model is the same across encoders. However, for LIC, the ranking of the captioning models changes with the encoder. This shows that with DPAC, users can choose any sentence encoder to measure bias amplification in their captioning models.

5.2
Constant vs. Contextual Substitution

In Table
4
, for all models, we see that the METEOR similarity score is consistently higher in contextual substitution. A higher similarity score indicates that the HGCs modified with contextual substitution are closer is meaning to the original HGCs. Hence, contextual substitution retains more information about the HGC than constant substitution.

Similar to the METEOR score trend, for both LIC and DPAC, the bias in the HGCs (
LIC_{D}
and
\Psi_{D}
) is consistently higher in contextual substitution. Since contextual substitution retains more information about the HGCs (than constant substitution), it provides a better estimate (or a closer upper bound) of the bias in the HGCs.

5.3
Directionality

To check for
T\rightarrow A
bias in the four versions of the COCO captioning dataset, we measured FC’s reliance on task objects to predict gender using GradientShap
[
12
]
. We calculated an attribution score, which is defined as the percentage contribution of non-person pixels (i.e., task objects) in FC’s prediction of the person’s gender.

In Table
5
, we see that the average attribution score steadily increases from the original version (unmasked) to the fully masked (or bounding-box masked) version of the COCO captions dataset. This confirms that as the person is progressively masked, FC relies more on task objects to predict gender, leading to a rise in the
T\rightarrow A
bias. To illustrate this visually, we generated attribution maps (of the FC model) for one of the images in the COCO captions dataset in Table
5
. These maps show that as masking increases, FC shifts its attention from people to background objects (or task objects) to predict gender.

As shown in the first row of Table
5
, only DPAC accurately captures this steady increase in
T\rightarrow A
bias. Since LIC does not support directionality, it does not capture this trend. Hence, DPAC can correctly identify the source of bias amplification (in this case, the task influencing gender predictions) while LIC cannot.

Our observations contradict Hirota et al.
[
9
]
, who claimed that biases in captioning models only originate from the language component (and not the vision component) of the captioning model. Here, we see that modifying the visual input to the captioning model (using masking), without modifying anything in the language part, changes the bias in the model (
\Psi_{M}
reported by DPAC). This shows that the visual component of captioning models also carries biases. This idea of visual biases in models is further supported by works like
[
13
]
, which show that even if we mask the entire segment of a person in an image, models can use shape features to predict their gender.

6
Limitations

DPAC is the only metric that measures directional bias amplification in captioning models. However, it has some limitations:
it does not study activities (or verbs) in captions, is more computationally expensive than co-occurrence-based metrics, and uses embeddings from a pre-trained Glove model, which carries biases from its training dataset.

DPAC (and LIC) measures bias amplification only between a protected attribute (
A
) and a task (
T
). While measuring bias amplification between
A
and
T
is sufficient for classification problems, it is not sufficient for a complex problem like captioning. Consider two captions: “a man is eating a steak” and “a man is cooking a steak”. DPAC and LIC treat these captions as equivalent as they share the same
A
(man) and
T
(steak), even though the activities differ (eating vs. cooking). Since captioning datasets (like COCO captioning) do not provide activity-level annotations, we restricted our study to attributes and task objects. To calculate a better estimate of the bias amplification between HGCs and MGCs, future work should study attributes, activities (or verbs), and task objects together.

As mentioned in Section
3.2.1
, since predictability-based metrics (DPAC or LIC) rely on an attacker model, bias amplification values are often wrongly reported due to attacker model instabilities (e.g., the attacker model being trapped in local minima during training). To overcome these instabilities, it is important to calculate bias amplification values over multiple iterations to ensure reliable results. Consequently, predictability-based metrics are computationally expensive than co-occurrence-based metrics like
BA_{\rightarrow}
.

In our contextual substitution strategy, we used GloVe embeddings to find the closest matching word in the model vocabulary (as discussed in Section
3.2.1
). Prior studies have shown that GloVe inherits biases from the dataset it is trained on
[
3
,
19
]
. This can influence the bias amplification values reported by DPAC.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed Directional Predictability Amplification in Captioning (DPAC), a new predictability-based metric to measure bias amplification in captioning models. Unlike previous metrics, DPAC is capable of identifying the source of bias amplification in captioning models. Compared to LIC, the previous state-of-the-art bias amplification metric for captions, DPAC provides a better estimate of the bias in the dataset (human-generated captions), as it uses an improved vocabulary substitution strategy. Additionally, DPAC is minimally sensitive to sentence encoders and attacker models compared to LIC. This removes the pain of choosing the most suitable sentence encoder for measuring bias amplification. DPAC measures bias amplification between attributes (e.g., gender) and tasks (e.g., surfing) in a caption. However, like other captioning metrics, it does not study how activities (or verbs) amplify biases in captioning models. This is because verbs are not annotated in existing captioning datasets used for measuring bias, which may be addressed in future work.
