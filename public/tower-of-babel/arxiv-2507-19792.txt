Modelling the Closed Loop Dynamics Between a Social Media Recommender System and Users’ Opinions
Ella C. Davidson, Mengbin Ye

Abstract
This paper proposes a mathematical model to study the coupled dynamics of a Recommender System (RS) algorithm and content consumers (users). The model posits that a large population of users, each with an opinion, consumes personalised content recommended by the RS. The RS can select from a range of content to recommend, based on users’ past engagement, while users can engage with the content (like, watch), and in doing so, users’ opinions evolve. This occurs repeatedly to capture the endless content available for user consumption on social media. We employ a campaign of Monte Carlo simulations using this model to study how recommender systems influence users’ opinions, and in turn how users’ opinions shape the subsequent recommended content. We take an interest in both the performance of the RS (e.g., how users engage with the content) and the user’s opinions, focusing on polarisation and radicalisation of opinions. We find that different opinion distributions are more susceptible to becoming polarised than others, many content stances are ineffective in changing user opinions, and creating viral content is an effective measure in combating polarisation of opinions.



Keywords
- Recommender systems, opinion formation models, social media, polarisation, radicalisation.

I
Introduction

The internet has revolutionised how people generate, interact with, and consume information and thus how people form opinions. Social media platforms (social media for short) are at the forefront of this revolution, now tightly woven into the fabric of society and the daily lives of all. While the proliferation of social media has brought about obvious benefits, there are increasing concerns about their role in the polarisation and radicalisation of society
(
Ozduzen and Korkut 2020
;
Flaxman et al. 2016
;
HM Government 2020
)
and as vectors for mis/disinformation spread and other activities that undermine democratic institutions. This concern about polarisation in online environments can be traced back to
Van Alstyne and Brynjolfsson 1996
, who warned that the pervasiveness of digital technologies could lead to cyberbalkanisation (division of the internet into sub-groups with specific interests).
Van Alstyne and Brynjolfsson 1996
noted that the ease of communication paired with people’s ability to filter out all information that may challenge their pre-existing views are two essential ingredients for political polarisation. Understanding how social media platforms shape users’ opinions and behaviours has become a major focus across multiple scientific disciplines and within government and policymakers
(
HM Government 2020
;
KGI Expert Working Group on Recommender Systems 2025
)
. The interest of this paper lies in the affordances of social media platforms, i.e., the technology and structures which define how users experience the platform, and in particular the algorithmic recommender systems (RSs) which determines the content that users can consume and engage with on social media platforms.

Social media platform affordances have steadily evolved over the years, revolutionising the user experience, from the initial text-heavy approach of Facebook and Twitter, to the image-focused Instagram, and now the popular video-centric “endless stream” approach popularised by TikTok
(
Matsakis 2020
)
. Instagram and YouTube have adopted the success of TikTok’s scrollable For You page, which strongly resembles their own Reels and Shorts, respectively. The affordances of these platforms (TikTok, Reels and Shorts) share commonalities, such that when a user opens the platform, they are immediately presented with an endless stream of content tailored specifically for them
(
Matsakis 2020
)
. This approach has proven very popular, with TikTok alone garnering more than one billion active users globally
(
Bursztynsky 2021
)
, and is often associated with viral content
(
Strapagiel 2019
)
, the swift and extensive dissemination of an over-engaging piece of content
(
Sangiorgio et al. 2024
;
Al-Rawi 2019
)
.

We argue that in order to study how platforms and their affordances influence users and their opinions, we must study the socio-technological system where the platform and its users exist in a closed loop. This is because the particular content that users encounter are selectively presented to users by a
recommender system
(RS), an algorithm which filters through available information (including past user engagement with content) to determine the most relevant and appropriate content, including highly personalised content, to next recommend to users
(
Saifudin and Widiyaningtyas 2024
)
. In other words, the RS takes into account user preferences and prior content consumption to recommend new content, while users are both influenced by this new content and also engage with this content. When implemented on a platform with endlessly scrollable content (such as TikTok’s For You page, YouTube Shorts or Instagram Reels), this forms the closed loop socio-technological system. We use a complex systems and dynamical systems approach to modelling, allowing us to capture the interdependence and time-evolution between users and the RS.

We now review the existing literature on the two key elements of our system, RSs and user opinion dynamics, and recent efforts to model the two as a socio-technological system. RSs determine recommendations in a myriad of ways, from simple techniques like pattern and rule mining, to intricate filtration techniques
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
. One well known example of a pattern mining-based model is the “beers and diapers” case study, where young fathers shopping at Walmart often purchased beer and diapers together in shopping visits, leading to an effective sales promotion of both items
(
Han et al. 2000
)
. Seminal works for RSs have extensively delved into fundamental filtering techniques and ways to make them more efficient
(
Jannach et al. 2011
)
. One of these fundamental filtering techniques is content-based filtering, which records users’ previous interactions with content to predict future recommendations
(
Jannach et al. 2011
)
. In contrast to this
more of the same
attitude, collaborative filtering identifies clusters of users that behave similarly online, and makes the assumption that they have comparable wants and needs, meaning peer opinions are used to predict the interests of others
(
Jannach et al. 2011
)
. In order to personalise recommendations to their users, these algorithms may record how a user engages with recommended content, such as a user “liking” it via a specific platform affordance/feature (explicit engagement) or how long they may stay on a particular page (implicit engagement)
(
Ricci et al. 2010
)
. Recommendation-generating models then use this data to generate recommendations. For collaborative filtering, a popular choice is latent factor-based models
(
Rendle et al. 2010
)
, or matrix factorisation, which uses a singular value decomposition
(
Jannach et al. 2011
)
.

One notable body of work examines how to optimise the performance of an RS, i.e. maximise user engagement and minimise biases in users’ ratings, while taking into account user preferences and biases. A seminal work by
Adomavicius et al. 2013
found that RSs can influence consumers’ product ratings by providing an anchored system rating. Extending from
Adomavicius et al. 2013
, agent-based models and frameworks for RSs have been utilised to provide better recommendations to consumers. Through an agent-based simulation framework,
Zhang et al. 2020
proposed a hybrid approach to construct recommendations, where consumers received a combination of personalised and non-personalised recommendations. This “hybrid consumption strategy”, in which users received personalised and popularity-based recommendations, improved consumption relevance over time, and facilitated a “quality-rises-to-the-top” phenomenon, which is not seen in solely popularity-based consumption
(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
.
Zhou et al. 2024
extends this agent-based model by allowing the RS to construct recommendations from previous interactions, in order to study the longitudinal impact of preference biases on the performance of the RS. Here, the focus is on reducing biases in user ratings. They found that when an RS relies solely on historical rating data, it is unlikely to be effective in de-biasing user ratings
(
Zhou et al. 2024
)
. Similarly,
Li and Tuzhilin 2024
presented a framework for an RS that prioritised variety-seeking behaviour. By introducing the framework in a few offline scenarios and one major video-streaming platform in China, the model’s recommendation framework significantly increased performance metrics and economic impact for the company, showcasing the benefit of introducing variety into recommendations
(
Li and Tuzhilin 2024
)
. However, these studies have not considered user preferences and biases that change over iterations from the influence of the recommended content, i.e. the loop is not closed.

Opinion formation models (commonly termed opinion dynamics in the literature) are dynamical systems models that describe how opinions evolve through social influence over interaction networks. Originating from the study of social power in consensus processes
(
French 1956
)
, opinion formation models have received extensive attention from researchers physics, sociology, engineering, computer science, and other disciplines
(
Anderson and Ye 2019
;
Proskurnikov and Tempo 2017
)
. The French–DeGroot model is a foundational model, in which agents in a network share opinions (represented as a real-valued state variable), and an agent’s new opinion is a weighted average of their network neighbours’ current opinions. Through an iterative updating process, a consensus of opinions is reached under mild conditions on the connectivity of the network. Since then, other models have explored the impact of different social and cognitive features that can shape opinion formation, such as the backfire effect
(
Shi et al. 2019
)
, asynchronous interactions, communication occurring only between like-minded individuals, and people expressing views that do not necessarily align with their private beliefs. The classical Friedkin–Johnsen model is of particular note
(
Friedkin and Johnsen 1990
)
, extending the French–DeGroot model by positing that agents remain attached to their initial prejudices, with extensive empirical studies and data supporting the appropriateness of said model
(
Friedkin and Johnsen 2011
;
Friedkin and Bullo 2017
)
.

From the above review of existing work, we seek to emphasise the following point. The majority of the research into recommender systems has focused on different algorithmic paradigms, e.g. collaborative or content-based filtering, and better utilising data to improve the performance of the RS (typically as user engagement or reducing biases in user ratings). Little attention has been paid on how, over time, the recommended content could change user opinions. In contrast, opinion dynamics research has focused on how opinions evolve through social influence among peers, but has focused on the traditional notions of pairwise interactions between people, overlooking the fact that RSs are now central to how people obtain information and connect with other users on social media. By combining the two streams of research, there is the potential to model the two described as a closed loop system. While works such as those by
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2024
do model the closed loop, they do not consider the user dynamics within the loop. We now review the limited recent studies which have considered both RS and user dynamics, revealing there are still a number of open questions and research directions.

Efforts to unite the two streams of literature on RSs and opinion dynamics is emerging one, and is thus limited but rapidly growing.
Rossi et al. 2021
proposed one of the first frameworks for modelling the closed loop system, considering just one user and a simple RS. The user had an opinion on a given topic, and at discrete timesteps, the RS presented to the user one of two possible articles, representing a position at either extremes of the topic. The user made a decision on whether to “click” on the recommended article, and their opinion changed due to consumption of the article. This decision was relayed back to the RS, and was used to determine what article to recommend at the next timestep by way of a simple content-based filtering algorithm. This process occurred iteratively, such that the recommendations presented and the user’s opinion co-evolved over time
(
Rossi et al. 2021
)
. Through a mixture of numerical simulations and theoretical analysis,
Rossi et al. 2021
found the content-based RS made opinions more extreme, and more extreme opinions in turn contributed positively to user engagement, creating a feedback loop that accelerated the radicalisation of user opinions.

Lanzetti et al. 2023
extended the model of
Rossi et al. 2021
, by considering an ensemble population of users and articles that can hold any position on the opinion spectrum.
Lanzetti et al. 2023
characterised both the microscopic impact (on an arbitrary user’s opinion) and macroscopic impact (on the distribution of opinions in the population).
Sprenger et al. 2024
further extended the work of
Rossi et al. 2021
and
Castro et al. 2017
, by modifying the recommendation algorithm to maximise user engagement using a closed loop control paradigm. It was found that the presence of radicalised users contributed to the overall radicalisation of user opinions.
Perra and Rocha 2019
took a different approach, in order to examine the impact of social influence.
They modelled users looking at multiple opinions about a specific topic presented as a list in personal “timelines”. An RS constructed these timelines through different sorting mechanisms and could include opinions of friends, i.e. their adjacent nodes, opinions posted by friends and all previous opinions in their timelines
(
Perra and Rocha 2019
)
. Users were thus exposed to a list of different opinions each time they were active, similar to how users “scroll” through social media platforms
(
Perra and Rocha 2019
)
. They found that different algorithmic personalisation schemes that constructed the agents’ timelines, except one, could not change the status quo when the prevalence of opinions was equally distributed in the population
(
Perra and Rocha 2019
)
. However, when the RS imposes a certain stance into the agents’ timelines (nudging), opinions quickly move towards the nudged stance and switch the status quo, polarisation effects like echo chambers were also noted
(
Perra and Rocha 2019
)
.

This paper provides two major contributions to extend the works discussed above. The first contribution is the development of a mathematical model inspired by
Rossi et al. 2021
and
Lanzetti et al. 2023
, but with key additional features. We take inspiration from the video-centric approach of an “endless scroll” popularised by TikTok’s For You Page, YouTube Shorts and Instagram Reels. Empirical evidence indicates that the act of “liking” a video on the For You page affects future recommendations given to a user
(
Boeker and Urman 2022
)
, and thus we introduce a more sophisticated stochastic decision-making mechanism to capture how users engage with content; users can like or dislike content, or choose not to engage at all, depending on their opinion. We also explicitly model how long users watch or consume the content; the watch rate depends on, and also determines how much the content changes, their current opinion. There are multiple users, and the content recommended to each user can be different. The RS considers three factors in determining whether to recommend a particular piece of content to a specific user; past engagement by the specific user on said content, the past watch rate of the user on said content, and whether said content is popular among other users (going viral). The last two factors are novel when compared to existing models by
Rossi et al. 2021
and
Lanzetti et al. 2023
, and reflect key metrics incorporated into the algorithms of real-world RSs. In
Rossi et al. 2021
, the RS follows an
\epsilon
-greedy action selection, while
Lanzetti et al. 2023
further optimises over all previous timesteps. In our model, we propose an intuitive softmax-type selection process, often encountered in modern machine learning algorithms.

Our second key contribution is the use of Monte Carlo numerical simulations to systematically explore key factors that influence the RS-opinion dynamics closed loop process. We study how the softmax parameter of the RS can impact both the RS performance and the opinions of the users, and the effect of increasing the diversity of the content available. Finally, we study how the virality in the RS impacts user opinions.
Our findings can be summarised by the following claims:

1.

After exposure to the RS, a population with an initially neutral distribution of opinions is more susceptible to becoming polarised, while the levels of polarisation and radicalisation are not worsened in an initially polarised population.

2.

A large number of different content stances is ineffective in changing user opinions, and when a smaller number of content stances are available, offering neutral content is an effective way to reduce polarisation and radicalisation.

3.

The addition of virality-based filtering and viral content can decrease levels of polarisation and radicalisation in the system.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. In Section
II
, we present mathematical preliminaries and the model for the RS. Then, in Section
III
, we describe our simulation setup and the metrics used in the Monte Carlo simulations. Our main results are presented in Section
IV
–
VI
. Section
VII
concludes the paper.

II-A

Social Media Environment Represented as a Graph

We represent the system as a time-varying graph with two types of nodes. In particular, consider the directed graph
\mathcal{G}(t)=(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{E}(t))
. There are
n\geq 2
users, indexed by the node set
\mathcal{U}=\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}\}
, and they interact with social media content about a specific topic at discrete timesteps
t=0,1,2,\ldots
. For each user
i
we assume that they hold an opinion
x_{i}(t)\in[-1,1]
, which is a real valued scalar that can evolve over time as a result of consuming and interacting with the content. The content is indexed by the node set
\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}\}
. Each piece of content
j
has a particular opinion stance,

x_{c_{j}}=\frac{2}{n-1}(j-1)-1

(1)

that does not change over time. Thus, the
k
pieces of content are equally spaced between
-1
and
1
.

The quantities
x_{i}(t)
and
x_{c_{j}}
are abstract representations of the opinions of the users and the stance of the content, and can be understood as follows. Consider a specific topic, e.g. COVID-19 vaccinations are safe and effective. Then,
x_{i}(t)
represents user
i
’s orientation towards the statement on a continuous range, so that
x_{i}(t)=-1
and
x_{i}(t)=1
represent maximal rejection and maximal support of the statement, while
x_{i}(t)=0
means they are neutral. Meanwhile,
x_{c_{j}}
represents the stance of the content on the same topic, so that
x_{c_{1}}
and
x_{c_{k}}
represent content which are fully against and fully supportive of COVID-19 vaccinations. Note that each content piece
j
does not represent a specific instance of content (e.g. a specific news article), but rather, it represents any content which conveys a stance equal to
x_{c_{j}}
.

The RS is not part of the graph, but rather, can be considered as a separate entity that generates the edges which connect users with content on
\mathcal{G}(t)
, representing the delivery of said content to each user’s social media feed. More specifically, at each timestep
t
and for each user
i
, the RS generates a directed edge
(c_{j},u_{i})
from a single selected piece of content
j
to user
i
. The specific process of how this content is selected is detailed in the sequel, but this process generates the edges of
\mathcal{G}(t)
, captured by the set
\mathcal{E}(t)
. Note that edges are always from content nodes to user nodes, and thus
\mathcal{G}(t)
is a time-varying bipartite graph. We use the variable
z_{i}(t)\in\mathcal{C}
to record the content recommended to user
i
at time
t
, and thus
x_{z_{i}(t)}
is the stance of the content seen by user
i
.

II-B

User Behaviour and Dynamics

There are three key processes we model for each user when they are presented with a piece of content: user engagement decision (whether they like or dislike the content), user watch rate (how long the user watches content for), and user opinion dynamics. These processes are modelled using TikTok’s For You page as an inspiration, but are intended to be quite general and representative of many different social media platform experiences.

II-B
1
User Engagement Decision

The first process is how a user engages with content that they consume. In many platforms, there is an ability to click a “like” button or “dislike” button as a way of engaging with content (and a user can also choose to do neither). At each timestep
t
, user
i
is recommended content
j
, and they must choose how to engage with the content. This engagement is captured by the variable
y_{i}^{j}(t)
, with
y_{i}^{j}(t)=1
representing liking the content,
y_{i}^{j}(t)=-1
disliking the content, and
y_{i}^{j}(t)=0
indicating the user remained neutral and did not engage. The decision model is inspired by game theory and utility theory; three payoff functions (
\pi_{i}(1),\pi_{i}(-1)
and
\pi_{i}(0)
) dictate the payoff or utility a user obtains from the engagement decision. This payoff is dependent on the difference
\bar{x}_{i}(t)=x_{i}(t)-x_{z_{i}(t)}
. We propose

\displaystyle\pi_{i}(y_{i}^{j}(t)=1)

\displaystyle=0.5\mathrm{cos}(1.5\bar{x}_{i}(t))+0.5

(2a)

\displaystyle\pi_{i}(y_{i}^{j}(t)=-1)

\displaystyle=-0.5\mathrm{cos}(1.5\bar{x}_{i}(t))+0.5

(2b)

\displaystyle\pi_{i}(y_{i}^{j}(t)=0)

\displaystyle=0.5\mathrm{cos}(3\bar{x}_{i}(t)-\pi)+0.5

(2c)

Note that difference-based influence functions are often used in opinion dynamics models, to capture how a user engages with other users (or content) as a function of similarity in the opinions
x_{i}(t)
and
x_{z_{i}}(t)
. Graphical representations of the functions are given in the supplementary material of this paper for the interested reader.

User
i
’s engagement decision,
y_{i}^{j}(t)
, is determined by a stochastic process. Specifically, the probability of choosing
y_{i}^{j}(t)=s
is

\mathbb{P}[y_{i}^{j}(t)=s]=\dfrac{\mathrm{exp}\{\beta_{i}\pi_{i}(s)\}}{\mathrm{exp}\{\beta_{i}\pi_{i}(-1)\}+\mathrm{exp}\{\beta_{i}\pi_{i}(0)\}+\mathrm{exp}\{\beta_{i}\pi_{i}(1)\}},

(3)

where
\beta_{i}\in[0,\infty)
represents user
i
’s rationality. This specific process has been widely used to model human decision-making with bounded rationality, including in stochastic games
(
Blume 1995
)
, and is used widely in Machine Learning as the softmax function.

II-B
2
User Watch Rate

Users are immediately presented with content by design on TikTok and many other platforms like YouTube Shorts and Instagram Reels. Therefore, when the app is opened, users immediately watch content until they swipe to the next video. We model this type of consumption as with the engagement decision above; this process is based on the user’s opinion,
x_{i}(t)
, and the stance of the recommended content,
x_{z_{i}(t)}
. The user’s watch rate at timestep
t
is represented by
w_{iz_{i}(t)}(t)\in(0,1)
and affects future recommendations and the user’s opinion at the next timestep. Note that
w_{iz_{i}(t)}(t)
represents the fraction of the content they consume, e.g.
w_{iz_{i}(t)}(t)\approx 0
and
w_{iz_{i}(t)}(t)\approx 1
could represent the user watching a video briefly before moving on, and watching the entire video, respectively.

First, let us define an intermediate variable
v(t)=x_{i}(t)x_{z_{i}(t)}
. We posit that each user’s watch rate is determined by the following function

w_{iz_{i}(t)}(t)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma v(t)}},

(4)

where
\gamma>0
is a tuning parameter. The function is plotted with various
\gamma
values in the supplementary material of this paper. This function indicates that user
i
consumes more content if the content and their own opinion are aligned (have the same sign), and the higher the magnitude of their opinion, the more they watch, which aligns with findings from
Rossi et al. 2021
that extreme opinions contribute positively to user engagement.

II-B
3
User Opinion Dynamics

The final user-specific process we model is the evolution of their opinion over time; this important process plays a major part in the user’s engagement decision and watch rate as modelled above, and critically, allows us to understand how the recommended content shapes user opinions over time. We employ the classical Friedkin–Johnsen model
(
Friedkin and Johnsen 1990
)
, consistent with other works modelling the RS-user opinion dynamics problem
(
Rossi et al. 2021
;
Lanzetti et al. 2023
)
. Note that the Friedkin–Johnsen model is one of the few opinion dynamics models with extensive empirical studies supporting its applicability in a broad range of scenarios
(
Friedkin and Johnsen 2011
;
Friedkin and Bullo 2017
)

The evolution of user
i
’s opinion is:

x_{i}(t+1)=\lambda_{i}\Big[w_{iz_{i}(t)}(t)x_{z_{i}(t)}+\big(1-w_{iz_{i}(t)}(t)\big)x_{i}(t)\Big]+(1-\lambda_{i})x_{i}(0),

(5)

where the constant
\lambda_{i}\in[0,1]
captures their susceptibility to influence. In other words, user
i
’s updated opinion is a weighted average, or convex combination of their initial opinion
x_{i}(0)
, their current opinion
x_{i}(t)
, and the stance of content they consumed. Content influence is minimised and maximised as
\lambda_{i}\to 0
and
\lambda_{i}\to 1
, respectively, and content influence increases as user
i
consumes the content for longer,
w_{iz_{i}}(t)\to 1
.

II-C

Recommender System

Having introduced the model of the user-specific processes, we now introduce how the RS assigns personalised recommendations to its users. To that end, we define the variables
A_{i}^{j}(t)
,
B_{i}^{j}(t)
and
C^{j}(t)
, which relate to user
i
’s engagement decision, user
i
’s watch rate and the viral element, respectively. The RS uses these three variables to calculate a payoff or utility
P_{i}^{j}(t)
, which it then uses to determine the recommended content piece at the next timestep via a stochastic process.

II-C
1
User Likes

Here, we describe how the RS keeps track of each user’s engagement with content in the past. First, we define the column vector
R_{i}(t)=[r_{i}^{1}(t),r_{i}^{2}(t),\dots,r_{i}^{k}(t)]^{\top}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}
. The entry
r_{i}^{j}(t)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
captures the number of times user
i
has been recommended content piece
j
before timestep
t
. Thus, the RS is able to track its recommendations to each user, and it is updated as
R_{i}(t+1)=R_{i}(t)+\mathbf{e}_{z_{i}(t)}
, with
\mathbf{e}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
being the
j^{th}
canonical basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Recall from Section
II-B1
that
y_{i}^{j}(t)
represents user
i
’s engagement decision regarding the recommended content piece
j
at timestep
t
. We propose that the RS keeps track of what each user has liked in the past. Using an indicator function
1
, we define
a_{i}^{j}(t)=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\textbf{1}_{\{y_{i}^{j}(s)=1\}}

as the number of times that user
i
has liked a particular content recommendation
j
.

Next, define the variable

A_{i}^{j}(t)=\dfrac{a_{i}^{j}(t)}{r_{i}^{j}(t)}.

(6)

as the ratio between the number of times user
i
has liked a piece of content
j
, and the total number of times content
j
has been recommended user
i
. Conveniently,
A_{i}^{j}(t)\in[0,1]
. If
r_{i}^{j}(t)=0
(user
i
has not yet been recommended content piece
j
), then we set
A_{i}^{j}(t)=0
, to avoid domain errors.

II-C
2
User Watch Rate

Empirical evidence from
Boeker and Urman 2022
found that watching a TikTok video for at least 25% of its duration significantly affected subsequent content recommendations on the For You page. Motivated by this, we define the variable
\overline{w}_{ij}(t)=\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{t}w_{ij}(s)}{r_{i}^{j}(t)}
as the average watch rate of user
i
for content
j
. We define the variable,

\displaystyle B_{i}^{j}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\tanh(\mu(\overline{w}_{ij}(t)-0.25))+0.5,

(7)

where
\mu>0
is a tuning parameter, that affects future recommendations; the function for different
\mu
can be seen in the supplementary material of this paper. By design,
B_{i}^{j}(t)\in(0,1)
takes on a small strictly positive value when
\overline{w}_{ij}(t)=0
, and approaches
1
if user
i
is consuming content
j
heavily each time it is recommended to them.

II-C
3
Virality-Based Filtering

It is important that the RS recognises which content is popular in the system. Let
L^{j}(t)
denote the total number of likes content
j
has received at timestep
t
from all users. We define, for some positive memory size
\delta\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
:

C^{j}(t)=\dfrac{\sum_{s=1}^{\min(t,\delta)}L^{j}(t-s)}{\sum_{s=1}^{\min(t,\delta)}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}L^{\ell}(t-s)}.

(8)

as how much positive engagement, or likes, content
j
is receiving in comparison to everything else. This enables us to compare the popularity of different content, i.e. determine if a particular piece of content has gone viral.

Notice that only the previous
\delta
timesteps are accounted for, to model the freshness of viral content and the memory of the RS. By design,
C^{j}(t)\in[0,1]
, and also represents the collaborative filtering element in the RS, as it is recording what content is performing well (likes for all users) in the system. We henceforth refer to this variable as that associated with virality-based filtering.

II-C
4
Generating Recommendations

By design,
A_{i}^{j}(t)
,
B_{i}^{j}(t)
and
C^{j}(t)\in[0,1]
which enables a relative comparison of the different terms. In particular,
A_{i}^{j}(t)
and
B_{i}^{j}(t)
are user-specific and form the content-based filtering part of the RS, whereas
C^{j}(t)
is universal across all users, and captures collaborative filtering and a focus on viral content. The combination of both user-specific and universal terms can be likened to the “hybrid consumption strategy” proposed in
Zhang et al. 2020
.

A payoff or utility for each user
i
and each content
j
is computed:

P_{i}^{j}(t)=\dfrac{(1-\omega)(A_{i}^{j}(t)+B_{i}^{j}(t))}{2}+\omega C^{j}(t).

(9)

The parameter
\omega\in[0,1]
represents the weight placed on the virality-based filtering, relative to the content-based filtering. The RS then uses the payoff
P_{i}^{j}(t)
,
j=1,2,\ldots,k
to determine the content to recommend to user
i
at timestep
t+1
. Specifically, the RS uses the softmax function, a standard Machine Learning approach to transforms real values (scores, utilities) into a probability distribution
(
Bishop and Nasrabadi 2006
)
:

\mathbb{P}[z_{i}(t+1)=c_{j}]=\dfrac{\mathrm{exp}\{\alpha P_{i}^{j}(t)\}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathrm{exp}\{\alpha P_{i}^{j}(t)\}},

(10)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
represents the softmax parameter
(
Franke and Degen 2023
)
. We define the reciprocal of the softmax parameter as the temperature of the RS, i.e.
\alpha=\frac{1}{T}
. As the softmax parameter increases, the RS moves along a scale from exploration to exploitation. That is, for small
\alpha
values, the output of the probability function approaches a uniform distribution and recommendations are assigned randomly. Conversely, as
\alpha\longrightarrow\infty
, there holds
\mathbb{P}[z_{i}(t+1)=c_{\ell}]\longrightarrow 1
where
\ell=\mathrm{argmax}_{j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}}\{P_{i}^{j}(t)\}
. That is, the RS is more exploitative, as it will only recommend for user
i
the content
c_{\ell}
that has the highest utility/payoff among all the content.

III
Methodology

We are now in a position to describe how we will use the mathematical model, detailed in Section
II
, to study its effects on users’ opinions. Due to the model’s stochastic nature and highly non-linear dynamics, we employ a Monte Carlo simulation approach, breaking our study into three specific research questions that we will describe in detail in the relevant sections below. These questions aim to unveil the impact of various parameters associated with the RS. As such, for each set of parameters, we run 50 replication simulations and use four metrics to quantify the outcomes, two for the RS and two for the opinion distribution of the population. For each parameter setting and each metric, we take the mean and standard deviation of the 50 replications to construct a 95% confidence interval around our results. Broadly speaking, our first question will examine the softmax parameter
\alpha
of the RS, our second question will examine the diversity of content stances through
k
, and our final question will focus on the impact of virality filtering by studying
\omega
and
\delta
.

III-A

Simulation Setup

Here, we outline parameters and settings that are universal across all of our simulations, while the specific adjustments are detailed in the relevant sections below. We run each simulation for
\tau=1000
timesteps, as preliminary simulations show that this is a sufficient amount of time for the system dynamics to stop fluctuating substantially. For each simulation, there are
n=500
users, with a homogeneous rationality
\beta_{i}=9
for all
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
. The setting
\beta_{i}=9
provides a “sweet spot” whereby individuals are almost always rational but have some room for spontaneity. For the two tuning parameters in Eq. (
4
) and Eq. (
7
), we set
\gamma=5
and
\mu=5
. We set a homogenous susceptibility of
\lambda_{i}=0.9
, for all
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
, for the opinion evolution in Eq. (
5
). This means the content assigned by the RS has a significant but not total influence on each user, as users will have some mild attachment to their initial opinion,
x_{i}(0)
. In the Supplementary Material, we repeat all simulations reported in this paper with
\lambda_{i}=0.2
for all users. Intuitively, the impact of the RS is much smaller, but we observe similar qualitative phenomena.

For the initial opinions of the users,
x_{i}(0)
, we randomly sample
x_{i}(0)
from a distribution for each simulation. In fact, we utilise two distributions: a neutral distribution, see Fig.
2a
, and a bimodal distribution, see Fig.
2b
. We henceforth refer to these initial opinion distributions as NDIC (neutrally distributed initial condition) and BDIC (bimodally distributed initial condition), respectively. The NDIC approximates normal distribution centred at
0
and truncated at
-1
and
1
, representing a balanced population. The BDIC instead reflects a society that is already polarised. To create the initial distributions, we draw from a beta distribution that has codomain
[0,1]
, the shape of which is controlled by parameters
\alpha
and
\beta

(
McDonald and Xu 1995
)
. Therefore, we have for the NDIC a mean of
\mu=0
and standard deviation of
\sigma=0.378
and for the BDIC
\mu=0
and
\sigma=0.524
.

III-B

Metrics for the System

We record the state of the system at the end of the simulation window of
\tau=1000
timesteps, and compute four metrics that are used to address the research questions. Two metrics examine the RS’s performance, and two metrics describe the final opinion distribution to ascertain the impact of the RS on the users.

To measure the performance of the RS, we record the user-specific processes of liking and watching recommended content. In each simulation, we record the total amount of likes received as a percentage of total amount of likes possible, i.e

\mathrm{Likes}\,\%=\dfrac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{s=1}^{\tau}L^{j}(s)}{n\tau}\times 100,

(11)

where
n\tau
represents the total number of recommendations made by the RS to the
n
users over
\tau
timesteps. Similarly, we define the average watch rate of all users across that simulation as a percentage:

\mathrm{Average\,Watch\,Rate}\,\%=\dfrac{\sum_{s=1}^{\tau}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{iz_{i}(s)}(s)}{n\tau}\times 100.

(12)

For the population, we are especially interested in polarisation and radicalisation. To capture the former, we use dispersion
M_{D}
as introduced in
(
Bramson et al. 2016
)
, which measures the shape of the whole opinion distribution and how far each user opinion is away from the mean. Formally, we have:

M_{D}(t)=\dfrac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}(t)-\bar{X}(t)|,

(13)

where
\bar{X}(t)
represents the average user opinion at timestep
t
. Dispersion can increase as subgroups move further apart, when user opinions move further away from the middle ground toward the fringes of the distribution, and when the distribution flattens
(
Bramson et al. 2016
)
. The second metric
M_{R}
focuses on measuring the presence of extremist opinions in population:

M_{R}(t)=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}(t))^{2}.

(14)

This metric increases as more users shift towards the fringes of the population, i.e. become radicalised.

Note that the metrics
M_{D}
and
M_{R}
serve two distinct purposes. The dispersion metric,
M_{D}
, measures the shape of the opinion distribution as a whole and how that shape changes, including whether the two subgroups are moving further away or closer together. This allows for an insight into how polarised user opinions are. Meanwhile, the radicalisation metric,
M_{R}
, measures the prevalence of extremist opinions in the distribution. Both metrics allow insight into how the opinion distribution is moving away from the neutral.

Our first research question focuses on the impact of the softmax parameter
\alpha
, used by the RS to probabilistically determine what content to recommend to each user, as in Eq. (
10
). Intuitively, smaller and larger values of
\alpha
correspond to an RS that aims to be more explorative (recommending a range of content to a user) and more exploitative (recommending only content that is likely to receive user engagement), respectively. To that end, we fix
k=21
,
\omega=0.5
and
\delta=5
, and then vary
\alpha
from
\alpha=2
to
\alpha=20
with a step size of
2
.

With a larger softmax parameter at

v(t)

\omega

V
Research Question Two: Diversity of Content

Our second research question concerns the integer parameter
k\geq 2
, which as detailed in Eq. (
1
), helps to define the content stance. Intuitively,
k
can be understood as representing the diversity of the content available for the RS to recommend to users. With
k=2
, there are precisely two pieces of content taking opposing extreme stances in the opinion spectrum
[-1,1]
, with
x_{c_{1}}=-1
and
x_{c_{2}}=1
. As
k
increases, there is more content available (evenly spaced across the opinion spectrum, see Eq. (
1
)), which can be thought of as information diversity. When
k
is odd, there is always one piece of content at the neutral stance of
0
, whereas for even
k
, there is not; the impact of this becomes salient in our results. Previous research by
Rossi et al. 2021
and
Perra and Rocha 2019
set
k=2
, i.e.,
x_{c_{j}}\in\{-1,1\}
, and it was found that the RS led user(s) to become polarised. In contrast,
Lanzetti et al. 2023
considered an RS that could recommend content of any stance to a user, and found the opinion distribution of the population was unchanged by the RS. (
Lanzetti et al. 2023
considered opinions and content on the interval
(-\infty,\infty)
, which can be effectively captured in our framework by allowing
k\to\infty
with the interval
[-1,1]
). Given
k
is a parameter within our model, it becomes of great interest to understand the impact that
k
, diversity of content available to be recommended, has on the RS’s performance and user opinions.

Similar to Section
IV
, we set
\omega=0.5
and
\delta=5
and we now vary
k
in an unevenly spaced grid of
\{2,3,4,...,10,11,21\}
to place a higher emphasis on low diversity scenarios. We set
\alpha=7
based on our findings in Section
IV
. Namely, when
\alpha=7
, the RS receives more than
80\%
of possible likes and an average watch rate of more than
60\%
, guaranteeing high engagement, see Fig.
4a
. Simultaneously,
\alpha=7
allows for some variety in the content users receive, as the RS will occasionally recommend to a user content not tailored specifically for them. This “variety injection” is a noted feature of a real-world RS, such as TikTok’s For You page, which regularly performs a “similarity check” to ensure consecutive recommendations are different from one another, to ensure users sees a greater variety of content
(
TikTok Inc. 2024
)
.

VI
Research Question Three: Virality

Our third and final research question focuses focuses on how the RS integrates information about viral content into its process. In particular, we examine
\omega\in[0,1]
and
\delta\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. The former is the relative weight given to the payoff associated with the virality-based filtering in Eq. (
9
) (relative to the payoffs associated with content-based filtering), so that as
\omega
increases, the RS puts more weight on viral content over trying to maximise user-specific engagement. The latter captures the “memory” of the RS, i.e. as
\delta
increases the RS considers past engagement input from users over a longer window when deciding what is a viral piece of content.

The virality-based filtering mechanism introduced in our model is substantially different from existing models
(
Lanzetti et al. 2023
;
Rossi et al. 2021
;
Sprenger et al. 2024
;
Perra and Rocha 2019
)
, and thus we are motivated to understand how this mechanism shapes the RS and user opinions. We vary
\omega
from
0
to
1
in step sizes of
0.1
, and vary
\delta
from
1
(meaning the memory of the RS resets every timestep) to
\tau
(meaning the RS remembers every like recorded up the given timestep when determining viral content) in steps of
10

VII
Discussion and Conclusions

This paper proposed a mathematical model to describe the interplay between an RS, its many users, and a range of content with differing stances on the same topic. The mathematical model is an abstract representation of social media pages like TikTok’s For You page, YouTube Shorts or Instagram Reels. The model posits that its users, each with an opinion, consume personalised content recommended by the RS. The RS can select from a range of content to recommend based on users’ past engagement, while users can engage with the content (like, watch), and in doing so, users’ opinions evolve. This process occurs repeatedly to capture the endless content available for user consumption on social media platforms that have a “endless scroll” feature. We have effectively extended the current literature that examines the modelling and coupling of RSs and opinion dynamics by allowing the model to facilitate many users and many pieces of content interacting through an RS, and further with the introduction of virality-based filtering. Virality-based filtering has allowed our RS to identify what content is performing well for the entire population of users and promote it broadly.

Through a campaign of Monte Carlo simulations we introduced three research questions to investigate important parameters in our model. The three research questions regarded the softmax parameter of the RS, the effect that diversity of content has, and the effects of the parameters associated with the virality-based filtering mechanism of the model. To examine the research questions, we defined metrics for the performance of the RS—to understand how well the users responded to the comment, and opinion distribution metrics—to measure the changes in the levels of polarisation and radicalisation in the population. Across the results for the three research questions, it was clear that parameters associated with the RS, such as
\alpha
,
k

\omega
, and
\delta
, impacted its performance (measured as engagement on content) and the opinion distribution of the users. Specifically, when investigating
\alpha
, relating to the softmax parameter of the RS in Eq. (
10
), we found that there was a balance between exploration (low
\alpha
) and exploitation (high
\alpha
) that resulted in a piece of content going viral. Furthermore, one could substantially increase the likelihood of a piece of content going viral by making the RS’s virality-based filtering mechanism dominant relative to its content-based filtering elements and reducing the RS’s memory. This was achieved by adjusting
\omega
and
\delta
.

In our work, we observed that viral content became a consistent phenomenon of the proposed model. Whenever a piece of content went viral, there was a major impact on all metrics; specifically for the opinion distribution metrics, we saw a sudden and effective decrease in polarisation and radicalisation. Viral content and its effects emphasise the closed loop system; user interactions make one piece of content go viral in the first place, and then the viral content shapes user opinions and consequently, their interactions. Throughout our results, viral content helped reduce levels of polarisation and radicalisation because the viral content almost always was of a neutral (or close-to-neutral) stance. In a sense, this accords with real-world settings, as in many cases, the viral content we are accustomed to seeing are inoffensive funny memes, jokes, and sounds that are not extreme or of a fringe origin
(
Pilipets 2023
;
Ling et al. 2021
)
.

Research Question Two
revealed a key insight on how content diversity,
k
, had a non-trivial interplay with the opinion dynamics. Namely, when
k=2
, we saw maximal polarisation and radicalisation, while as
k
becomes large, there is minimal change. The former is consistent with the work of
Rossi et al. 2021
, and the latter consistent with
Lanzetti et al. 2023
. These two scenarios are good choices if one would like to optimise for RS performance as measured using user engagement. However, it is not ideal if one was aiming to reduce polarisation among user opinions. In the real-world context,
k=2
is reminiscent of the hyper-bipartisan landscape of American politics
(
Iyengar et al. 2019
)
. In this example, if an RS were to recommend content to an already polarised population (BDIC), like Democrats and Republicans, the population would remain polarised. For an initially neutral population (NDIC), the users would quickly become polarised. In both cases, the RS would have high engagement. What is interesting, though, is that as soon as one more piece of content is introduced, at
k=3
, most user opinions shifted towards the neutral piece of content. That content would go viral as more user opinions are closer to
x_{c_{j}}=0
rather than
x_{c_{j}}=-1
or
1
. This same pattern is observed as
k
increases towards
9
. To put this into perspective, unlike the bipartisan political system in America, a multiparty political system, i.e. when
k\geq 3
, would result in a much less polarised population.

On the contrary of
k=2
, we can also put the situation of
k>11
into context with an example. When a large variety of content stances is available to be recommended, there is little change in user opinions. In the real-world, this is reminiscent of a multiparty political system. These types of systems are especially likely to produce legislative deadlock, which can potentially hinder the effectiveness of a democracy
(
Mainwaring 1993
)
, as people can hear and support opinions they agree with already. In contrast, when content is recommended to users that they may disagree with, it can broaden their understanding beyond their current ideological understanding
(
Bruns 2019
)
. Our results suggest there is a “sweet spot” for the number of different content stances that allow for decreases polarisation and radicalisation, around
k\in[3,9]
.

Regarding our two selected initial opinion distributions, the NDIC and BDIC, we found that the NDIC was much more susceptible to polarisation and radicalisation than the BDIC. This effect is exacerbated whenever the RS aims for high performance (i.e. increasing
\alpha
when
k=2
and focusing on content-based filtering by setting a small
\omega
). Our results show that the BDIC’s opinion distribution metrics decreased after interaction with the RS (except for the aforementioned scenarios). This suggests that an RS impacts an initially polarised population less, as each user will likely already favour a particular content stance and thus be recommended similar content. In contrast, it also highlights the dangers of neutral populations when it comes to being influenced by RSs, as we do observe polarisation and radicalisation increase over time.

While analysing the results of this paper, some limitations of the proposed mathematical model were realised. One limitation was that neutral content generally had lower engagement, regardless of how well it aligned with user opinions. This occurred due to how we defined the watch rate function, see Eq. (
4
). Namely, if a user were recommended neutral or close-to-neutral content, then their watch rate would, by default, be approximately
50\%
. In the future, defining a more sophisticated watch rate function when interacting with neutral content would benefit the modelling process. Another limitation of the model is that within our current implementation, only one piece of content can go viral, except when
k=2
. We did not observe two pieces of content going viral in different groups of users holding different opinion stances (e.g. if the opinions were bimodally distributed), likely because our virality-based filtering mechanism considered engagement from all users. Implementing some form of collaborative filtering, which identifies groups of users who behave similarly to be served similar recommendations, in combination with the virality-based filtering could allow different content stances to go viral within different subgroups.

Although this paper has provided a much-needed look into RSs’ impact on their users’ opinion dynamics, several problems remain open to be explored. Throughout this paper, it was clear that when a piece of content went viral, it depended upon the parameters explored in each question. Deriving an analytical expression or condition for when content will go viral, as a function of
\alpha,k,\omega,
and
\delta
, would be a worthwhile direction in terms of theoretical analysis. Collaborative filtering techniques, like matrix factorisation models
(
Jannach et al. 2011
)
, are a common feature of real-world RSs, and incorporating this into the model would allow for even more personalised tailoring to the recommendations. Extending our framework to consider multiple different topics, with a range of content for each topic, could be useful for studying the rabbit-hole/spillover effect. In our scenario, we are interested in the rabbit-hole effect, where, due to being recommended increasingly extremist content, users are surrounded by conspiracy content and lose a sense of reality and reason
(
Sutton and Douglas 2022
)
. The proposed model and planned future work will help broaden our understanding of the influence that RSs and the content they recommend have on society.
