ToolMind Technical Report: A Large-Scale, Reasoning-Enhanced Tool-Use Dataset
Chen Yang, Ran Le, Yun Xing, Zhenwei An, Zongchao Chen, Wayne Xin Zhao, Yang Song, Tao Zhang

Abstract
Large Language Model (LLM) agents have developed rapidly in recent years to solve complex real-world problems using external tools. However, the scarcity of high-quality trajectories still hinders the development of stronger LLM agents. Most existing works on multi-turn dialogue synthesis validate correctness only at the trajectory level, which may overlook turn-level errors that can propagate during training and degrade model performance. To address these limitations, we introduce ToolMind, a large-scale, high-quality tool-agentic dataset with 160k synthetic data instances generated using over 20k tools and 200k augmented open-source data instances. Our data synthesis pipeline first constructs a function graph based on parameter correlations and then uses a multi-agent framework to simulate realistic user–assistant–tool interactions. Beyond trajectory-level validation, we employ fine-grained turn-level filtering to remove erroneous or suboptimal steps, ensuring that only high-quality reasoning traces are retained. This approach mitigates error amplification during training while preserving self-corrective reasoning signals essential for robust tool-use learning. Models fine-tuned on ToolMind show significant improvements over baselines on τ \tau -bench, τ 2 \tau^{2} -bench, and BFCL-v4. The dataset is available at https://huggingface.co/datasets/Nanbeige/ToolMind .

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have adopted Function Calling (FC) paradigm to interact with external environments, significantly expanding their capacity to solve complex real-world tasks
23
;
14
.
This capability hinges on the model’s proficiency in tool use, which requires reliable interpretation of user instructions, appropriate function selection, and precise parameter generation.
A data-centric approach has proven effective in enhancing such capabilities, typically requiring large-scale, high-quality datasets with diverse and accurate function-call demonstrations.

Recent studies have actively synthesized data for various function calling scenarios
8
;
17
, spanning diverse application domains and interaction modalities such as selection from large toolsets, parallel function calls, and rejection of unspecified requests
10
;
12
;
18
.
However, existing datasets have several critical limitations: limited scale, the absence of explicit reasoning traces, a lack of multi-turn interactions, and insufficient query diversity. These issues highlights the importance of high-quality, large-scale datasets to develop robust and generalizable tool-use models and agents.

A key challenge in constructing high-quality datasets lies in capturing the dynamic nature of real-world user–assistant interactions.
First, user requests are often under-specified, lacking sufficient parameters for task completion (e.g., a query like “What’s the weather in Beijing?” does not specify a time frame).
Second, effective interaction requires an ongoing clarification process, in which the assistant can proactively request missing information from the user.
Current open-source synthetic datasets rarely capture these dynamics, leading to models struggling with such scenarios.

In this work, we introduce ToolMind, a reasoning-enhanced tool-use dataset comprising 360k samples. It integrates 160k assistant response turns synthesized through multi-agent simulations and 200k augmented turns adapted from open-source data.
To ensure diversity, we collect 20k functions from open-source datasets and construct a function graph that captures inter-parameter correlations.
Function chains are then sampled via random walks on this graph to synthesize user intents.
Then, we employ a multi-agent framework for dialogue generation, orchestrating three dedicated roles—user, agent, and tool—each instantiated by a language model to capture the dynamics and complexities of real-world tool-use interactions.
Finally, we apply a rigorous two-stage filtering process, comprising both trajectory-level and turn-level quality checks, to ensure that all training samples contain only valid conversation turns.

Our experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the ToolMind dataset in enhancing the tool-use capabilities of LLMs. With supervised fine-tuning (SFT), models of various scales show significant improvements over baselines across multiple benchmarks, including BFCL-v4
11
,
\tau
-bench
20
, and
\tau^{2}
-bench
1
. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

High-Quality Tool-Use Dataset.
We introduce ToolMind, a large-scale, high-quality tool-use dataset comprising 360k samples that effectively addresses the key limitations of existing datasets—namely in scale, reasoning traces, multi-turn interactions, and query diversity.

•

Data Synthesis and Quality Filtering Pipeline.
We develop a novel data synthesis pipeline that constructs a function graph based on parameter correlations and employs multi-agent simulations with three dedicated roles (a user, an assistant, and a tool), which better capture the dynamic nature of real-world tool-use interactions. A rigorous two-stage filtering process is further applied to ensure high data quality.

•

Effectiveness of Large-Scale Simulation.
We show that ToolMind delivers significant and consistent performance improvements across models of various scales. These results empirically validate that large-scale synthetic data generated from simulated environments can serve as an effective alternative to real-world data for achieving substantial gains in model performance.

Background

Function Calling.

The integration of external tools and functions into language model workflows marks a major step toward extending model capabilities beyond text generation.
Function calling refers to the structured mechanism through which language models invoke predefined functions or APIs to access real-time data or interact with external systems
14
.
An early paradigm for using external tools is React
21
, where models generate both reasoning traces and corresponding function calls in an interleaved manner.
This paradigm was later refined and integrated into thinking models as a native capability (
e.g.,
supporting function calling in chat templates) for training and inference
19
;
22
, also known as the model’s function call pattern.
Our synthetic data aims to enhance the model’s native function calling capabilities.

Benchmarks.

Several benchmarks have been proposed to evaluate the tool-use ability of language models,
i.e.,
, their capacity to call appropriate functions for task completion
9
;
13
;
5
;
11
;
20
;
1
;
24
.
These benchmarks have evolved from basic tool-use evaluations to assessing agent behavior in more realistic, interactive scenarios.
BFCL-v4
11
comprises four distinct versions that systematically progress from single-turn interactions to multi-turn dialogues, and from simple, static tool-use cases to complex tasks involving search and memory capabilities. Concurrently,
\tau
-bench
20
focuses on sustained dialogue and function-calling tasks and
\tau^{2}
-bench
1
further enriches this scenario by extending function access to the user side. Together, these advances demonstrate a clear shift toward more dynamic and complex evaluation frameworks that better reflect real-world conditions. In this work, we evaluate models across the three benchmarks mentioned above to systematically assess the effectiveness of our synthesized data.

3.1
Graph Construction and Function Chain Sampling.

We construct a directed graph over the collected functions to model their input–output compatibility, and then sample function chains via random walks for trajectory synthesis.

3.1.1
Functions Collection and Preprocessing

Existing open-source works have already achieved substantial tool diversity. Building on these resources, we collect functions from public datasets—including
xlam-function-calling-60k
1

1

https://huggingface.co/datasets/Salesforce/xlam-function-calling-60k

,
glaive-function-calling-v2
2

, and
ToolACE
3
3

3

https://huggingface.co/datasets/Team-ACE/ToolACE
. The resulting function set covers a broad range of function types, complexity levels, and application domains, spanning everyday utilities to domain-specific APIs.
We denote the collected function set by
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where each function
f_{i}
has an input space
\mathcal{X}_{i}
and an output space
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
, and may take multiple arguments, written as:

f_{i}:\;\mathcal{X}_{i}\to\mathcal{Y}_{i},\qquad\mathcal{X}_{i}=\prod_{k=1}^{m_{i}}{x}_{i,k},\;\;\mathcal{Y}_{i}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{n_{i}}{y}_{i,\ell}.

To make these function sets compatible for downstream processing, we first standardize incomplete or ambiguous functions (
e.g.,
those missing explicit definitions in certain input or output parameters).
We use a language model to infer the missing descriptions and types for each parameter
r\in\mathcal{X}_{i}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{i}
, denoted as
\mathrm{desc}(r)
and
\mathrm{type}(r)
.
Once the specifications are complete, we embed each parameter using a unified scheme. For each
r\in\mathcal{X}_{i}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{i}
, we concatenate its description and type with literal tags and encode them with an embedding model:

\mathbf{v}(r)=\phi\big(\text{{DESC}}\ \|\ \mathrm{desc}(r)\ \|\ \text{{TYPE}}\ \|\ \mathrm{type}(r)\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

3.1.2
Random Walk with Function Graph

With the parameter embeddings in place, we proceed to build a function graph that captures relationships between functions. In this graph, each function is a node, and edges are established based on the semantic fit between outputs and inputs. Concretely, let
G=(V,E)
be a directed graph with
V=\mathcal{F}
. For two distinct functions
f_{i}
and
f_{j}
, we measure how well an output of
f_{i}
can serve as an input to
f_{j}
by the maximum cosine similarity over their parameter embeddings:

s_{ij}\;=\;\max_{\,y\in\mathcal{Y}_{i},\;x\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\operatorname{sim}\!\big(\mathbf{v}(y),\,\mathbf{v}(x)\big),

where
\mathrm{sim}
denotes cosine similarity. An edge
i\!\to\!j
is created when this similarity exceeds a fixed threshold
\tau>0
, indicating that an output parameter of
f_{i}
is semantically similar to an input parameter of
f_{j}
. To ensure the reliability of these connections, we further employ a language model as a validator and keep only the accepted links.

We also introduce randomness into edge construction. This yields a more diverse connectivity pattern that better reflects the real-world function composition. With the constructed graph, we sample function chains via random walks that traverse existing edges:

\mathcal{W}=(f_{0},f_{1},\dots,f_{L}),L\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\{5,\dots,20\},

where
\mathcal{W}
is a function chain,
f_{t}
denotes the function visited at step
t
, and
L
is the walking length. To avoid oversampling specific nodes, we impose a per-node visit limit during the random walk process. This encourages balanced graph coverage and mitigates bias toward high-degree hubs.

3.2
Multi-Agent Multi-Turn Trajectory Synthesis

Based on the sampled function chains, we synthesize user intents to represent realistic user goals. A user intent represents the underlying goal that a user aims to achieve, rather than the exact query they pose. This distinction provides greater flexibility in modeling user behavior, as users often express the same intent in diverse ways.
Since the tools are not executable in our setup, we employ a simulation framework to generate responses. The trajectories are created through a multi-agent simulation that involves three distinct agents:

•

User Agent
: Initiates the interaction by asking questions based on synthesized intent and drives the conversation forward.

•

Assistant Agent
: Responds to the user’s queries, provides guidance, asks for clarification, or calls external functions when needed.

•

Tool Agent
: Simulates the execution of a function invoked by the assistant, returning tool responses based on simulated outputs.

These three agents operate in an iterative loop: the user agent poses a query, the assistant agent responds, and the tool agent simulates feedback when required. The process continues until a predefined turn limit or stopping condition is reached. This simulation produces a diverse set of interaction trajectories that serve as training data for modeling rich user–assistant–tool dynamics.

Empirical results demonstrate that even without real-world interaction, the large-scale synthetic data generated from these simulations significantly improves model performance. The flexibility and diversity of the simulated conversations provide valuable insights for training models in complex, multi-agent systems.
For a detailed example of the generated data, see Appendix
B
.

3.3
Quality Filtering

To ensure that the synthesized interactions provide reliable learning signals, we apply a two-stage quality filtering process: trajectory-level filtering that maintains goal alignment and coherence, followed by turn-level filtering that removes erroneous or misaligned steps.

3.3.1
Trajectory-Level Filtering

Given the predefined user intents (defined in the previous section), the user agent first judges whether the task is completed or not during the process of multi-turn interaction. Then, a second LLM evaluates goal coverage, coherence, and cross-turn relevance. Only trajectories that both align with the intent and exhibit a coherent flow are retained, while the rest are discarded. This stage produces a high-quality pool of trajectories for finer-grained filtering.

3.3.2
Turn-Level Filtering

Since trajectory-level filtering operates at a coarse granularity, it cannot effectively isolate erroneous turns within otherwise coherent trajectories. Trajectories may still contain erroneous or misaligned turns—such as failed intermediate attempts, tool calls based on incorrect assumptions, or role drift in user/tool messages. To further improve data quality, we address these remaining issues by applying a separate LLM-based judge to each turn.
The model then masks invalid or role-inconsistent steps and retains only correct procedural actions, ensuring the final training data targets effective problem solving and precise tool use.

Ablation Study.
To evaluate the effectiveness of each component in ToolMind, we conduct an ablation study on Qwen3-8B. The study comprises the following configurations: (a) using only synthesized data, (b) using synthesized data without turn-level quality filtering, and (c) using only data augmented from open-source datasets.

Moreover, the model trained on the combined dataset achieves the best results across nearly all evaluation metrics, demonstrating that each component of ToolMind is integral and effective.

5
Conclusion

This paper presents ToolMind, a large-scale dataset designed to address data scarcity in tool-use for Large Language Models. Built through graph-based function sampling and multi-agent trajectory synthesis, ToolMind offers diverse, realistic function-calling trajectories with explicit reasoning traces. Experimental results demonstrate that models fine-tuned on ToolMind achieve consistent and significant improvements across multiple benchmarks. Our work highlights the value of high-quality synthetic data for advancing tool-use capabilities and provides a valuable resource for future research.
