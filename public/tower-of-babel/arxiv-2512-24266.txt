On the word problem for just infinite groups
Alexey Talambutsa

Abstract
. In this note we establish that the word problem is algorithmically decidable for finitely generated just infinite groups given by a recursively enumerable set of relations. The proof does not use the Wilson–Grigorchuk theorem on the classification of just infinite groups, and the argument proceeds directly from the definition, using ideas from classical results on decidability of the word problem: Kuznetsov’s theorem on simple groups and the Dyson–Mostowski theorem on residually finite groups.



1.
Introduction

A group
G
is called
just infinite
if it is infinite, and for every proper subgroup
H\subset G
the quotient group
G/H
is finite.

The class of just infinite groups was proposed by J. Wilson in the early 1970’s (see
[
11
]
) as a generalization of the class of simple groups. It was mentioned that this class also contains the infinite cyclic and dihedral group, and according to a result of J. Mennicke, the projective matrix group
PSL(n,\mathbb{Z})
for
n\geq 3
. Later Grigorchuk and Gupta–Sidki constructed new examples of just infinite groups, which were groups acting on rooted trees.

In Grigorchuk’s paper
[
6
]
, the class of so-called branch groups acting on rooted trees was defined, and based on the results of
[
11
]
, the following result was proved, often called the trichotomy theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated just infinite group. Then either
G
is a branch group, or
G
contains a normal subgroup of finite index isomorphic to a direct product of finitely many copies of a group
L
, where
L
is either simple or hereditarily just infinite (i.e., a residually finite group in which every subgroup of finite index is just infinite).

Since the decidability of the word problem for a finitely generated group and for its subgroup of finite index are equivalent, it follows from Theorem
1
that to solve the word problem in just infinite groups it suffices to solve it for three classes: branch, simple, and residually finite groups that are hereditarily just infinite.

The algorithmic decidability of the word problem for finitely generated simple groups with a recursively enumerable set of relations was established by A.V. Kuznetsov (see
[
2
]
and
[
7
, Theorem 3.6]
). For the class of residually finite groups, V. Dyson and A. Mostowski independently obtained a positive solution to the word problem in the case when the group is given by a finite number of relations (see
[
4
]
and
[
9
]
), but at the same time, as shown by Dyson and S. Meskin (see
[
5
]
and
[
8
]
), there exist examples of groups from this class with recursively enumerable sets of relations for which this problem is algorithmically undecidable.

For spinal groups, which include the Grigorchuk and Gupta–Sidki groups, as well as many other examples of branch groups, an algorithm was constructed in
[
3
]
that solves the word problem. However, the algorithm takes as input not a presentation of the group in terms of generators and relations, but a special oracle that is related to the action of generating automorphisms of the tree. Note also that for the series of Grigorchuk groups
G_{\omega}
it was established that the word problem in
G_{\omega}
is decidable if and only if the sequence
\omega
is computable. Since it is not known whether every branch group is spinal (in particular, this is unknown for the group in
[
10
]
), a complete answer to the question of decidability of the word problem for the class of branch groups has not been established.

As input to the algorithm we will use recursively enumerable presentations of groups. For a finitely generated group
G
, a
recursively enumerable presentation
is a pair
(S,\mathcal{R})
consisting of some finite alphabet of generators
S=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}\}
, and an algorithm
\mathcal{R}
that enumerates the defining relations of the group
G
in the generators
S
. We will also use the notation
\langle S\mid\mathcal{R}\rangle
for the presentation of the group
G
, assuming that in this case
\mathcal{R}
describes a set of relations.

The main result of this work is the following statement:

Theorem 2
.

There exists an algorithm that, given a recursively enumerable presentation
\langle S\mid\mathcal{R}\rangle
of a just infinite, finitely generated group
G
and a word
X
in the generators
S
, checks whether the equality
X=1
holds in the group
G
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
2

We will use the same scheme that is used both in Kuznetsov’s theorem and in the Dyson–Mostowski theorem. Two algorithms work in parallel, one of which tries to prove the equality
X=1
in the group
G
, and the other tries to refute this equality. Since one of the two algorithms must necessarily complete its work, the answer will necessarily be obtained in finite time.

Algorithm 1
(to prove
X=1
). In this case all theorems use the same reasoning. Let the algorithm
\mathcal{R}
enumerate up to the present moment the defining relations
R_{1},R_{2},\ldots,R_{l}
. Then we have
l
parallel processes
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{l}
, and the process
P_{s}
will be enumerating all freely irreducible words that are equal to
1
in the group
\langle S\mid R_{1}=1,R_{2}=1,\ldots,R_{s}=1\rangle
. These will be Dyck words that have form

(1)

\prod_{j=1}^{s}t_{j}R^{\varepsilon_{j}}_{i_{j}}t_{j}^{-1}=1,\quad\text{where }t_{j}\in F_{k}\text{ and }\varepsilon_{i_{j}}\in\{-1,0,1\}.

Let us somehow order the complete enumeration of exponents
\varepsilon_{i_{j}}
and the reduced words
t_{j}
from the free group
F_{k}
of rank
k
. It is known that every word that is equal to
1
in the group
G=\langle S\mid\mathcal{R}\rangle
, after performing free group cancellations, coincides graphically with some Dyck word (see
[
1
, Lemma 5.1]
). If
X=1
then let
q
be the maximal number amongst indices
i_{j}
that were used for
X
in the product (
1
). Then then the word
X
will be found in any enlisting process
P_{s}
with
s\geq q
, and after
X
will be found the whole algorithm will complete its work. If
X\neq 1
, then the Algorithm 1 will never complete its work.

Algorithm 2
(to prove
X\neq 1
). Here we apply reasoning that combines two different ideas that are used in Kuznetsov’s theorem with the Dyson–Mostowski theorem.

First, as in Kuznetsov’s theorem, we extend the presentation of the group
G
with the relation
X=1
and obtain a group presented as
\langle S\mid X\cup\mathcal{R}\rangle
. Note immediately that in the case when the equality
X=1
held in the group
G
, we obtain an infinite group
G_{1}=G
, and in the case
X\neq 1
, from the minimal infinity of the group
G
we obtain that the quotient group
G_{1}=G/\text{Ncl}(x)
by the normal closure of the element
x
is finite.

We now proceed, as in the Dyson–Mostowski theorem, to enumerate all possible multiplication tables for finite groups
T_{1},T_{2},\ldots
and try to find an epimorphism
\psi:T_{i}\to G_{1}
. Obviously, such an epimorphism will exist if and only if
G_{1}
is a finite group. To check the existence of an epimorphism, we will consider each table
T_{q}
as a presentation of a group, taking all its elements
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{r}
as generators, and all equalities of the form
u_{i}u_{j}=u_{k}
from the multiplication table as relations. In order to find the elements that map to the generators
S
under the epimorphism, we will enumerate all possible surjections
\tau:\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{r}\}\to\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}\}
. For each surjection
\tau
we will try to derive from the relations
X\cup\mathcal{R}
of the group
G_{1}
the relations

(2)

\tau(u_{i})\tau(u_{j})=\tau(u_{k}),\,\text{where }u_{i}u_{j}=u_{k}\text{ is an equality from the multiplication table }T_{q}.

Since, as mentioned above, the set of all relations of
G_{1}
is enumerable, if the relations (
2
) hold in
G_{1}
, then all of them will be derived at some point
