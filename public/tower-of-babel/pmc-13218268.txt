A comprehensive review of mitochondrial fluorescent probes: Design principles, fluorophore engineering, and functional parameter sensing
Authors: Fei Peng, Bo Wang, Ran Chai, Mingming Xue, Aibing Wu, Yan Lv, Huijuan Tian, Jianyuan Yu, Guigui Pang, Bin Hao, Baoxiang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondria are essential organelles that play pivotal roles in cellular energy metabolism, signaling, and homeostasis, and their dysfunction is closely associated with various human diseases. In recent years, the rapid development of mitochondria-targeted fluorescent probes has greatly expanded our ability to visualize mitochondrial structure and monitor diverse physicochemical and biochemical parameters in situ. While existing reviews have primarily categorized these probes by specific physiological readouts, a unified perspective that integrates probe design strategies, fluorophore engineering, and target physiological parameters is stilling lacking. To address this gap, we provide a systematic overview of mitochondrial fluorescent probes, focusing on molecular design principles, fluorophore selection, targeting strategies, and the sensing of key mitochondrial physiological indices. In addition, we critically discuss current limitations and emerging challenges, and outline future directions in this field. This review aims to offer theoretical insights and practical guidance for the rational design of mitochondrial fluorescent probes, promoting their broader applications in life science and biomedical research.Keywords: Mitochondria, Fluorescent probes, Physiological parameters, Design strategies

Introduction

Mitochondria are essential organelles that play a central role in a wide range of cellular processes, including energy production, apoptosis, and intracellular signaling [[1], [2], [3]]. The diverse physiological activities of mitochondria are intimately linked to their highly complex architecture and unique microenvironment. For instance, the characteristic double membrane structure is closely associated with the maintenance of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), which is critical for ATP synthesis [[4], [5], [6]]. Likewise, the viscosity of the mitochondrial inner membrane affects protein mobility and enzymatic efficiency [[7], [8], [9]]; the pH and temperature within mitochondria modulate metabolic reaction rates [[10], [11], [12]]; and the local concentrations of ATP, reactive species, and various ions (such as Ca2+ and H+) are tightly regulated to sustain bioenergetic and signaling functions [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. Therefore, precise monitoring of mitochondrial ultrastructure and a broad spectrum of physiological parameters including membrane potential, membrane viscosity, pH, temperature, ATP levels, reactive molecular species, and ionic gradients, is of great significance for understanding mitochondrial function and dysfunction in health and disease.

Fluorescent probes have gained widespread application in mitochondrial studies owing to their ease of structural design, high sensitivity and specificity, as well as their ability to enable non-invasive, real-time, and in situ monitoring of diverse physiological parameters [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. These advantages have significantly advanced our understanding of mitochondrial biology. Importantly, mitochondrial fluorescent probes provide an essential chemical biology toolkit to visualize dynamic physiological processes that are otherwise inaccessible, offering valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying mitochondrial function and dysfunction at the molecular level [[23], [24], [25]]. For example, Wei et al. designed and synthesized a pair of fluorescent probes based on the Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) mechanism for the visualization and detection of MMP [26]. Chen et al. developed the probe PKMO FX using a “fixation-driven chemical crosslinking” strategy, which enables super-resolution imaging of mitochondrial structures in fixed cells [27]. Moreover, Jones et al. expanded the field by employing fluorescence imaging to uncover the formation of cytosolic vesicles derived from the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM), termed VDIMs [23]. Collectively, these examples underscore the vital role of mitochondrial fluorescent probes as powerful tools for elucidating mitochondrial structure and function.

Mitochondrial fluorescent probes typically achieve specific targeting by exploiting the highly electronegative mitochondrial membrane potential (−150 to −180 mV), which drives probe accumulation into mitochondria via Nernstian electrostatic interactions [[28], [29], [30]]. The design of these probes is based on diverse photophysical mechanisms, including photo-induced electron transfer (PET) [[31], [32], [33]], intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) [[34], [35], [36]], fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) [[37], [38], [39]], etc., each of which contributes distinct advantages for monitoring different physiological parameters of mitochondria. In terms of fluorophore selection, these probes often incorporate positively charged moieties—such as triphenylphosphonium cations [[40], [41], [42]], pyridinium cations [[43], [44], [45]], cyanine cations [[46], [47], [48]], etc., to facilitate mitochondrial localization. In addition, widely used commercial mitochondrial dyes, including rhodamine 123 (Rh123) [[49], [50], [51]], tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM) [[52], [53], [54]], mito tracker green (MTG) [[55], [56], [57]], various cyanine dye series, etc., have been employed for specific labeling of mitochondrial structures and functions. Therefore, systematically summarizing the design principles of mitochondrial probes from the perspective of targeting mechanisms, fluorophore selection, and adaptation to specific physiological indicators is of paramount importance. Such comprehensive analysis will greatly advance the rational development of new mitochondrial probes and promote deeper exploration of mitochondrial biology.

In this review, we systematically summarize recent advances in mitochondrial fluorescent probes from three key perspectives: probe design mechanisms, the selection of fluorophores, and the development of probes targeting different mitochondrial physiological indicators. It aims to provide readers with a deeper understanding of the significance of this frontier field and to analyze the strengths and limitations of mitochondrial probes, thereby offering valuable insights for researchers engaged in mitochondrial probe design. Specifically, as illustrated in Scheme 1, this review discusses the design principles underlying probes with different photophysical mechanisms and their applicability under distinct physiological conditions, highlights the photophysical properties and suitability of various fluorophores for mitochondrial imaging, and summarizes representative probes that have been developed for monitoring diverse physiological parameters such as membrane potential, viscosity, pH, reactive species, etc. Furthermore, this review includes the authors’ perspectives on the critical roles of fluorescent probes in mitochondrial sensing and their broader impact on biological research. Finally, key challenges and future directions in the development and application of mitochondrial fluorescent probes are envisioned and outlined, with the aim of inspiring further innovation in this rapidly evolving field.

Overview of mitochondrial fluorescent probe design, mechanisms, and imaging targets.

Probe design strategies

The design of mitochondrial fluorescent probes requires a delicate balance between molecular recognition specificity, photophysical performance, and biological applicability. Since mitochondria possess a unique physicochemical microenvironment, including strong membrane potential, high lipid content, and dynamic structural complexity, probe molecules must be engineered to selectively accumulate in mitochondria while maintaining robust and reliable fluorescence output. An effective design strategy not only ensures accurate localization but also allows the probe to respond sensitively to diverse mitochondrial parameters such as pH, membrane potential, viscosity, reactive oxygen species (ROS), reactive sulfur species (RSS), ATP, and metal ions. This dual requirement of specificity and functionality has driven the development of diverse molecular frameworks that combine well established fluorophores with targeted chemical modifications.

To address the diverse requirements of mitochondrial imaging, a broad spectrum of photophysical mechanisms has been incorporated into probe design, with each mechanism offering unique response behaviors suited to specific biological contexts rather than representing intrinsic advantages or disadvantages. Classical processes such as PET and ICT enable sensitive detection of subtle microenvironmental variations, particularly polarity and pH [[58], [59], [60]]. Meanwhile, mechanisms including FRET, AIE, and ESIPT extend probe functionality to ratiometric quantification, high-contrast imaging [[61], [62], [63]]. These mechanisms collectively enrich the probe design toolbox, allowing researchers to tailor imaging performance according to the required spatial resolution, signal stability, target specificity, and biological parameter of interest. Photoinduced electron transfer (PET)

PET is one of the most widely used strategies in the design of fluorescent probes for biological sensing and imaging [[64], [65], [66]]. Typically, a PET-based probe consists of a fluorophore donor and an electron acceptor moiety. Upon light excitation, the fluorophore reaches an excited state and can transfer an electron to the acceptor, resulting in fluorescence quenching. In specific environments, when the acceptor interacts or reacts chemically with a target analyte, its electron-accepting ability is reduced or eliminated. This change suppresses the PET process and restores the fluorescence emission of the donor, thereby generating a fluorescence signal. The central concept of PET is to achieve an “off–on” fluorescence response by modulating the efficiency of electron transfer.

The design of PET probes primarily revolves around the judicious selection of donor and acceptor units. The donor is usually a fluorophore with favorable photophysical properties, such as naphthalimide, rhodamine derivatives, and other well-established dyes. The acceptor moieties are functional groups with suitable electronegativity, including quaternary ammonium salts, amines, or thioethers. By selecting different acceptors, PET probes can be tailored to respond selectively to a variety of biological and chemical stimuli, such as metal ions, changes in pH, and biomolecules. For instance, in the case of pH-sensitive PET probes, an increase in pH leads to the deprotonation of the acceptor, which reduces its electron-accepting capability and suppresses the PET process, resulting in enhanced fluorescence. Conversely, at lower pH, protonation of the acceptor strengthens electron transfer, leading to fluorescence quenching. An important parameter in PET probe design is the “electron transfer barrier,” determined by the potential difference between the donor and acceptor. By finely tuning this potential gap, researchers can adjust the sensitivity and dynamic range of the fluorescence switching behavior, thereby optimizing the probe's performance for specific applications.

Building upon the fundamental PET mechanism described above, its applicability in mitochondrial systems is particularly evident in scenarios where rapid and reversible regulation of key physicochemical parameters is required, such as MMP fluctuations and local pH variations. These parameters are intrinsically dynamic and often exhibit spatial heterogeneity within individual mitochondria, thereby necessitating probe designs that are not only highly sensitive but also capable of minimizing artifacts arising from probe redistribution or concentration dependence. In this context, PET provides a unique advantage, as its signal output is governed by intramolecular electron transfer rather than probe accumulation, enabling more direct coupling between fluorescence response and mitochondrial functional states. A representative example is the PET-based probe SPIRIT RhoVR (Fig. 1a) [67], in which PET modulation is directly linked to MMP dependent electronic perturbations within the inner mitochondrial membrane. Through enzymatic activation, the probe is converted into a membrane-anchored form, effectively decoupling its localization from MMP-driven accumulation. In contrast, the probe BG-NDM (Fig. 1b) exemplifies a different PET design paradigm [68], where fluorescence modulation arises from protonation–deprotonation of the electron acceptor, thereby enabling sensitive detection of mitochondrial pH changes. By incorporating a SNAP-tag targeting strategy, the probe achieves site-specific localization within mitochondria, allowing PET regulation to report local microenvironmental variations with improved specificity. Together, these examples illustrate that the versatility of PET-based probes lies not only in their electron transfer mechanism but also in their compatibility with diverse targeting and anchoring strategies. Future developments in this area may therefore focus on integrating PET with multi-level regulatory elements, such as membrane affinity, protein-specific binding, or microenvironment-sensitive groups, to enable more accurate and spatially resolved sensing. Such approaches are expected to advance PET-based probes from conventional intensity-based indicators toward more robust and quantitative tools for investigating mitochondrial heterogeneity and dynamics.

Intramolecular charge transfer (ICT)

ICT probes achieve environmental responsiveness by incorporating electron donor and acceptor groups within the same molecular scaffold. Upon excitation, these probes undergo internal charge redistribution, resulting in shifts of their fluorescence emission spectra typically red or blue shifts depending on the surrounding conditions [58]. The design of ICT probes centers on tuning the electronic properties of the donor and acceptor moieties to target specific physiological or chemical parameters. Typically, the donor group is an electron rich unit such as an amino or hydroxyl group, whereas the acceptor is an electron deficient group like a nitro or cyano moiety. Upon photoexcitation, electrons migrate from the donor to the acceptor, producing an intramolecular polarization. Changes in environmental factors, including solvent polarity, pH, or metal ion concentrations, can modulate the electronic characteristics of either the donor or the acceptor, thus altering the ICT process and leading to measurable spectral shifts.

ICT probes have found broad applications in bioimaging and sensing of mitochondrial microenvironments [[69], [70], [71]]. A representative case is pH sensitive ICT probes, which incorporate pH responsive groups such as hydroxyl or carboxyl groups. Under varying pH conditions, these groups modulate the electron donating or withdrawing strength of the donor or acceptor, resulting in shifts in fluorescence emission. This enables dynamic monitoring of mitochondrial pH fluctuations, providing valuable insights into mitochondrial physiology and pathology.

Building on the ICT mechanism described above, this strategy is particularly well suited for probing mitochondrial microenvironments where gradual and continuous physicochemical variations dominate, such as pH, polarity, and redox-related changes. Unlike PET processes that typically produce binary “off–on” responses, ICT enables more nuanced spectral shifts through intramolecular charge redistribution, making it especially advantageous for monitoring subtle and spatially heterogeneous variations within mitochondria. Given that mitochondrial function is closely associated with dynamic changes in local pH and oxidative stress levels, ICT-based probes provide an effective platform for capturing these variations with high sensitivity and spectral tunability. A representative example is the NIR-II probe PN910 (Fig. 2a) [72], in which the ICT process is strategically masked and subsequently restored through a dual-activation mechanism involving reactive oxygen and nitrogen species under alkaline conditions. In this design, the suppression of ICT in the native state ensures low background fluorescence, while ROS/RNS-triggered deprotection restores the donor–acceptor conjugation, leading to strong fluorescence activation. In contrast, the probe SNRF (Fig. 2b) exemplifies a classical ICT modulation strategy based on reversible protonation–deprotonation processes [18]. By incorporating electron-donating and electron-accepting groups within a single molecular scaffold, the probe exhibits pH-dependent shifts in emission, allowing ratiometric monitoring of mitochondrial pH dynamics. Together, these examples highlight that the versatility of ICT-based probes arises from their ability to integrate environmental sensitivity with tunable electronic structures. Future developments may therefore focus on combining ICT with multi-responsive elements, such as coupling pH sensitivity with redox or enzymatic triggers, to achieve more precise and multiplexed sensing. In addition, integrating ICT with advanced imaging modalities or targeting strategies may further enhance its potential for quantitatively resolving mitochondrial heterogeneity in complex biological systems.

Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET)

FRET is a non-radiative process in which energy is transferred from an excited donor fluorophore to an acceptor fluorophore through long range dipole–dipole interactions [[73], [74], [75]]. This transfer occurs when the donor and acceptor are within an optimal distance, typically around 1–10 nm, and when there is significant spectral overlap between the donor's emission and the acceptor's absorption. Upon photoexcitation, the donor molecule transmits energy to the acceptor, leading to fluorescence emission from the acceptor. FRET probe design strategically harnesses this energy transfer mechanism to detect biochemical events or molecular interactions by monitoring changes in the fluorescence intensity ratio of donor and acceptor. The effectiveness of FRET probes critically depends on the selection of donor–acceptor pairs with complementary photophysical properties and sufficient spectral overlap, as well as precise control of their spatial arrangement to remain within the FRET effective range. In mitochondrial research [19], FRET based probes have been widely employed to sense key physiological parameters, including MMP, pH, ions, etc.

In the context of mitochondrial imaging, FRET offers a distinct advantage for monitoring dynamic biological processes that require quantitative and spatially resolved readouts, such as membrane potential fluctuations and structural remodeling. Unlike single-fluorophore systems, FRET relies on distance-dependent energy transfer between donor–acceptor pairs, enabling ratiometric signal output that is inherently less sensitive to probe concentration, excitation intensity, and photobleaching. This feature is particularly valuable in mitochondria, where rapid functional changes are often accompanied by morphological transformations and heterogeneous distributions at the nanoscale. A representative example is the dual-probe system composed of FixD and LA (Fig. 3a) [26], in which FRET efficiency is modulated by membrane potential–dependent redistribution of the acceptor. In this design, the donor remains anchored within the inner mitochondrial membrane, while the acceptor accumulates under polarized conditions and dissipates upon depolarization. As a result, changes in MMP are directly translated into variations in donor–acceptor proximity and FRET efficiency, producing a reliable ratiometric signal. Extending this concept, our recently developed FRET-based system (Fig. 3b) integrates donor–acceptor pairs with precise inner membrane targeting and combines FRET readout with FLIM at super-resolution levels [76]. In this approach, variations in MMP not only modulate FRET efficiency but are also correlated with structural changes of mitochondrial cristae, enabling simultaneous visualization of functional and ultrastructural dynamics. Collectively, these studies underscore that the strength of FRET-based probes lies in their ability to translate nanoscale distance variations into quantitative optical signals. Future efforts may therefore focus on simplifying donor–acceptor architectures, improving targeting precision, and integrating FRET with lifetime or super-resolution imaging techniques. Such developments are expected to further advance FRET from a ratiometric sensing strategy toward a powerful platform for dissecting mitochondrial heterogeneity and dynamic structure–function relationships.

Aggregation-induced emission (AIE)

AIE is a unique photophysical phenomenon in which fluorophores emit strong fluorescence in the aggregated or solid state, while remaining weakly emissive or non-fluorescent in molecularly dispersed solutions. AIE-based probes harness this behavior to achieve significantly enhanced fluorescence signals under conditions that promote molecular aggregation, making them particularly suitable for imaging and sensing in complex biological environments. The design of AIE probes typically relies on incorporating molecular “rotor” structures, which can freely rotate in dilute solutions, leading to non-radiative decay and fluorescence quenching. Upon aggregation or when intramolecular motions are restricted, these rotors become immobilized, reducing non-radiative losses and resulting in a pronounced fluorescence increase. Representative AIE-active scaffolds include tetraphenylethylene (TPE) and distyrylbenzene (DSB), which have been widely used in cell imaging and disease diagnostics due to their rigid structures and excellent photostability [62,77,78].

A major advantage of AIE probes is their ability to provide high-contrast, low-background imaging [79,80]. In biological systems, AIE fluorophores are less susceptible to solvent polarity and environmental effects, ensuring stable fluorescence signals suitable for live-cell imaging. For mitochondrial applications, AIE probes can be functionalized with targeting moieties and AIE-active cores, enabling precise mitochondrial labeling [44,81]. Upon accumulation within the mitochondrial matrix or membrane, these probes aggregate and emit strong fluorescence, facilitating dynamic monitoring of mitochondrial morphology and metabolic activity in living cells.

Distinct from the previously discussed mechanisms, AIE provides a fundamentally different strategy for mitochondrial imaging by converting restricted intramolecular motion into fluorescence enhancement. This feature makes AIE suitable for probing mitochondrial environments characterized by high viscosity, complex lipid organization, and confined spaces, where conventional fluorophores often suffer from aggregation-caused quenching. Moreover, the strong resistance of AIE luminogens to photobleaching and environmental interference enables high-contrast and long-term imaging, which is essential for monitoring mitochondrial dynamics under physiological and pathological conditions. An illustrative example is the AIE-active probe TPA-QLZ (Fig. 4a) [82], which integrates a donor–acceptor framework with a twisted molecular configuration to enable fluorescence activation upon aggregation within mitochondria. In this system, the quinolinium unit facilitates mitochondrial targeting, while the restriction of intramolecular motion under hypoxic conditions leads to pronounced fluorescence enhancement. In another representative case, the probe TPB (Fig. 4b) exploits the sensitivity of AIE to microenvironmental viscosity and polarity [44]. By incorporating a molecular rotor structure, TPB exhibits weak emission in low-viscosity environments but undergoes significant fluorescence enhancement when intramolecular motion is restricted in the mitochondrial matrix or membrane. Overall, these examples demonstrate that the unique strength of AIE-based probes lies in their ability to translate physical constraints within mitochondria into robust fluorescence signals. Future design strategies may focus on integrating AIE with multifunctional sensing elements, such as combining viscosity responsiveness with polarity or reactive species detection, to achieve more comprehensive characterization of mitochondrial microenvironments. In addition, coupling AIE with advanced imaging techniques may further expand its potential for long-term and high-resolution studies of mitochondrial dynamics.

Excited-state intramolecular proton transfer (ESIPT)

ESIPT is a photophysical process in which a proton relocates from a donor group (typically hydroxyl or amino) to an acceptor group (such as carbonyl or carboxyl) within the same molecule upon photoexcitation [[83], [84], [85]]. This intramolecular proton transfer generates a tautomeric excited state, which often emits fluorescence at a significantly red-shifted wavelength compared to the locally excited (LE) state. The resulting dual-emission or large Stokes shift makes ESIPT-based fluorescent probes highly attractive for biological imaging and ratiometric sensing. The core principle of ESIPT probe design lies in the careful modulation of the proton transfer pathway and efficiency, which can be influenced by environmental parameters such as polarity, viscosity, pH, hydrogen bonding interactions, and local dielectric properties. Due to this sensitivity, ESIPT probes are powerful tools for reporting subtle microenvironmental changes within organelles such as mitochondria. In particular, the confined and protein rich mitochondrial matrix, characterized by a negative membrane potential, mildly alkaline pH, and unique viscosity, provides an ideal environment to exploit the ESIPT process [[86], [87], [88]].

In contrast to electron transfer or aggregation driven mechanisms, ESIPT offers a unique photophysical pathway that enables dual-emission behavior and large Stokes shifts, making it advantageous for ratiometric and low-background mitochondrial imaging. Given that mitochondrial environments are characterized by confined spaces, heterogeneous polarity, and dynamic viscosity changes, ESIPT-based probes are well suited to capture subtle microenvironmental variations with high sensitivity while minimizing spectral overlap and self-absorption effects. A representative example is the probe XZTU-Mito-Vis (Fig. 5a) [89], which utilizes an ESIPT-active hydroxyphenyl benzothiazole framework to monitor mitochondrial viscosity changes under oxidative stress conditions. In this system, the ESIPT process is highly sensitive to intramolecular motion: under low-viscosity conditions, non-radiative decay dominates due to free rotation, whereas increased viscosity restricts molecular motion, promoting ESIPT emission and resulting in enhanced fluorescence. In another representative case, the probe TYC (Fig. 5b) demonstrates the applicability of ESIPT in monitoring organelle interactions and dynamic cellular processes [90]. By employing a hydroxyl benzothiazole core, TYC exhibits dual-emission fluorescence that is responsive to local polarity and viscosity changes. When applied to visualize mitochondria–lysosome interactions during autophagy, the ESIPT process enables clear differentiation of microenvironmental states through distinct emission channels. Altogether, these studies indicate that ESIPT-based probes possess significant potential for advanced mitochondrial imaging owing to their intrinsic dual-emission capability and sensitivity to microenvironmental factors. Future developments may focus on integrating ESIPT with targeting strategies or combining it with other photophysical mechanisms to construct multi-modal probes. Such approaches could further improve quantitative accuracy and enable comprehensive mapping of mitochondrial heterogeneity and organelle interactions in complex biological systems.

Common fluorophores for mitochondrial imaging

The development of mitochondrial fluorescent probes relies critically on the careful selection of fluorophore scaffolds, which endow the probes with distinct spectral properties, environmental sensitivities, and biological compatibility [[91], [92], [93]]. Given the highly negative MMP, cationic fluorophores tend to accumulate within mitochondria through electrostatic attraction, providing a fundamental basis for selective mitochondrial imaging. Over the past decades, numerous fluorophores have been employed to visualize mitochondrial morphology, monitor MMP fluctuations, detect ROS, track ion dynamics, and assess changes in the local microenvironment. These diverse applications underscore the essential role of fluorophores not only as signal reporters but also as functional elements that dictate probe performance, imaging depth, and photostability.

Among the available fluorophore families, several classes have become particularly prominent in mitochondrial research. Rhodamine derivatives and silicon–rhodamine (SiR) dyes remain the gold standards for MMP detection and long-term mitochondrial tracking due to their strong cationic nature, bright fluorescence, and excellent photostability [[94], [95], [96]]. Cyanine dyes, with tunable emission from visible to NIR regions, enable both long-term mitochondrial labeling and deep tissue imaging [47,97]. BODIPY derivatives, with their high quantum yields and photostability, are well-suited for detecting viscosity changes, lipid peroxidation, and other mitochondrial microenvironmental parameters [98,99]. Naphthalimides, owing to their strong ICT characteristics, serve as sensitive reporters for pH, polarity, and viscosity changes, while coumarins provide compact scaffolds with high biocompatibility and tunable optical properties [100,101]. Rhodamine derivatives (xanthene dyes)

Rhodamine-based dyes, structurally classified as xanthene derivatives, are classical fluorescent molecules widely employed in mitochondrial imaging due to their outstanding photophysical properties, including strong absorption, high fluorescence quantum yields, and excellent photostability [102]. Rhodamine dyes typically emit bright fluorescence ranging from red to the NIR region, which facilitates their use in live cell and deep tissue imaging applications. Structurally, rhodamine dyes contain amino, amide, or imino functional groups, making them highly versatile for chemical modifications aimed at improving mitochondrial specificity and biological compatibility. Due to their intrinsic positive charge, rhodamine derivatives readily accumulate within mitochondria, driven by the negative MMP. Consequently, these dyes serve as valuable indicators for the detection and monitoring of dynamic changes in MMP, a critical marker of mitochondrial health and cellular viability. Specifically, the fluorescence intensity of rhodamine dyes correlates positively with the magnitude of MMP; thus, decreases in membrane potential result in reduced fluorescence signals, allowing real-time visualization and quantification of mitochondrial depolarization events [23,103,104].

Recent advancements have significantly expanded the applications of rhodamine-based probes in mitochondrial research. Novel rhodamine derivatives have been synthesized with tailored spectral and targeting properties, enabling precise visualization of mitochondrial morphology, function, and related metabolic activities in live cells. Moreover, these dyes have been extensively employed in apoptosis studies, helping elucidate the mechanisms underlying mitochondrial dysfunction and programmed cell death.

Despite their widespread use, the practical performance of rhodamine-based mitochondrial probes is often constrained by intrinsic limitations, particularly their reliance on MMP driven accumulation and the resulting susceptibility to concentration-dependent artifacts. Such features can obscure subtle mitochondrial heterogeneity and compromise quantitative accuracy, especially under conditions involving dynamic depolarization or structural remodeling. Therefore, recent design strategies have increasingly shifted from simple utilization of rhodamine scaffolds toward functional re-engineering that decouples fluorescence output from probe distribution while introducing additional dimensions of signal regulation. A notable example is the development of bis(trifluoromethyl)-carborhodamine (BF) dyes (Fig. 6a) [105], in which electronic structure modulation is employed to control the equilibrium between non-fluorescent and fluorescent states. By introducing strong electron-withdrawing substituents, the fluorogenic response of the rhodamine core is significantly enhanced, enabling low-background imaging when combined with protein-specific targeting strategies such as HaloTag labeling. In a related but conceptually distinct approach, the probe A9-Halo (Fig. 6b) incorporates rhodamine into a dual-modality readout system based on both fluorescence intensity and lifetime [24]. By coupling rhodamine emission with protein-targeting capability, this system enables direct correlation between mitochondrial functional states and protein aggregation processes. These examples collectively indicate that the evolution of rhodamine-based probes is no longer centered solely on improving photophysical properties, but rather on redefining how fluorescence signals are generated and interpreted in mitochondrial systems. Future developments may therefore emphasize the integration of distribution-independent signaling strategies, lifetime-resolved detection, and precise biomolecular targeting, thereby enabling rhodamine scaffolds to more reliably capture mitochondrial heterogeneity and dynamic functional changes.

Silicon-rhodamine dyes

Silicon-rhodamine (SiR) dyes represent a structurally optimized class of rhodamine derivatives, in which the oxygen atom in the xanthene core is replaced by a silicon atom. This substitution significantly extends the π-conjugation system, leading to a pronounced bathochromic shift of both absorption and emission into the far-red to NIR region. The introduction of silicon not only modifies the electronic structure but also increases molecular rigidity, thereby enhancing fluorescence quantum yield and photostability. In addition, the presence of delocalized positive charges allows SiR dyes to effectively accumulate in mitochondria under the influence of MMP, making them highly suitable for mitochondrial imaging. Compared with conventional rhodamines, SiR dyes display reduced photobleaching, superior chemical stability, and compatibility with live cell and deep tissue imaging.

Beyond their structural advantages, SiR dyes have shown remarkable versatility in mitochondrial studies. Their long wavelength emission minimizes autofluorescence and light scattering, enabling high contrast imaging of mitochondria in complex cellular and tissue environments. SiR-based probes have been successfully employed for long-term monitoring of mitochondrial morphology, dynamics, and membrane potential fluctuations in live cells [8,76,106]. Furthermore, their favorable photophysical properties make them excellent candidates for multimodal imaging approaches, such as super-resolution microscopy and fluorescence lifetime imaging.

Compared with conventional rhodamine derivatives, SiR dyes provide an expanded functional platform by introducing a silicon atom into the xanthene framework, which substantially extends π-conjugation and shifts emission into the far-red to near-infrared region. While these features have been well recognized, their more critical advantage in mitochondrial imaging lies in enabling improved photostability, reduced phototoxicity, and enhanced compatibility with advanced imaging modalities. These properties make SiR-based probes particularly suitable for long-term, high-resolution, and multidimensional analysis of mitochondrial dynamics, where conventional fluorophores often fail to maintain signal fidelity. A representative example is the SiR-based probe system combining SiR-C8 (Fig. 7a) [8], in which the SiR scaffold is further functionalized to respond to mitochondrial microviscosity through restriction of intramolecular motion. This design allows real-time visualization of viscosity changes associated with mitochondrial perturbations and mitophagy, providing insights into the physical state of the inner mitochondrial membrane. In another representative case, the probe HBmito Crimson (Fig. 7b) utilizes a “membrane light-up” mechanism, in which fluorescence is selectively activated upon insertion into the mitochondrial inner membrane [106]. Unlike traditional accumulation-dependent probes, this strategy minimizes background signals and enables high-contrast visualization of inner membrane morphology. These studies collectively demonstrate that SiR dyes have evolved from simple mitochondrial trackers into versatile platforms capable of integrating environmental responsiveness, structural targeting, and advanced imaging compatibility. Future developments may further exploit the unique photophysical properties of SiR scaffolds to construct multifunctional probes that combine lifetime imaging, super-resolution techniques, and multi-parameter sensing, thereby enabling more comprehensive and quantitative investigation of mitochondrial structure–function relationships.

Cyanine dyes

Cyanine dyes are a classical class of organic fluorophores whose molecular skeleton is typically composed of a polymethine conjugated chain bridging two aromatic end groups (such as indole, benzothiazole, or quinoline), forming a highly delocalized π–π conjugation system. This unique structural feature endows cyanines with extremely high molar extinction coefficients and strong NIR fluorescence emission, making them highly sensitive probes for optical imaging. By tuning the length of the polymethine chain and the electronic properties of the terminal groups, the emission wavelength can be precisely regulated: extension of the conjugation or incorporation of electron-donating/withdrawing substituents often results in a red-shifted emission, extending into the 650–900 nm region or even further into the NIR-II window. Light in this spectral range exhibits superior tissue penetration and reduced background autofluorescence and scattering, thereby improving imaging contrast. Moreover, cyanine dyes generally exhibit high fluorescence quantum yields and favorable photostability, and structural modifications such as ring-locking or rigidification have further enhanced their stability and photophysical performance. Importantly, cyanine molecules often carry positive charges, enabling electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged inner mitochondrial membrane, which allows for efficient mitochondrial targeting. This intrinsic property makes them ideal tools for probing MMP, respiration, and related pathological processes [[107], [108], [109]].

In contrast to rhodamine-based systems primarily optimized for cellular imaging, cyanine dyes offer distinct advantages for mitochondrial studies that require extended spectral coverage and deeper biological penetration. More importantly, their highly delocalized polymethine framework provides a flexible platform for integrating multiple sensing functionalities within a single molecular scaffold. This structural adaptability enables cyanine dyes to move beyond simple mitochondrial labeling toward simultaneous monitoring of multiple physiological parameters, which is particularly valuable for capturing the complex interplay between mitochondrial metabolism and microenvironmental changes. A representative example is the dual-functional probe AR-Cy-3 (Fig. 8a) [110], which incorporates a cyanine backbone with a molecular rotor and reactive site to simultaneously report mitochondrial cysteine levels and viscosity changes during ferroptosis. In this design, the conjugated polymethine chain not only ensures efficient mitochondrial targeting but also facilitates the generation of spectrally distinct signals corresponding to biochemical and physical parameters. In another representative case, the asymmetric cyanine probe Cy5.5-H-CyN (Fig. 8b) demonstrates the application of cyanine dyes in quantitative ratiometric imaging of mitochondrial pH during mitophagy [47]. By introducing a proton-responsive heterocyclic unit into the polymethine system, the probe achieves pH-dependent modulation of its electronic structure while maintaining mitochondrial localization. Taken together, these examples underscore that cyanine dyes serve as highly versatile platforms for integrating structural tunability with functional complexity. Future developments may further exploit their extended conjugation and modularity to construct probes capable of simultaneously monitoring multiple mitochondrial parameters, particularly in vivo or deep-tissue contexts. Such efforts are expected to advance cyanine-based systems toward comprehensive and multiplexed imaging tools for studying mitochondrial function in complex biological environments.

Boron–dipyrromethene (BODIPY) dyes

BODIPY dyes, short for boron–dipyrromethene, are a well-established class of fluorophores characterized by a rigid and highly symmetrical core structure in which a boron atom is coordinated with two fluorine atoms and a dipyrromethene ligand. This unique coordination motif provides a stable electronic configuration, endowing BODIPY dyes with sharp absorption bands, narrow emission peaks, and high fluorescence quantum yields. Unlike many other fluorophores, BODIPY dyes exhibit minimal sensitivity to solvent polarity and pH, ensuring robust performance across a wide range of biological environments. The absorption and emission maxima of classical BODIPYs typically fall within the green spectral window (500–600 nm), but extensive structural modifications, such as extending the π-conjugation, introducing electron-donating or withdrawing groups, or attaching long alkyl chains, have successfully shifted their emission into the far-red and NIR regions. These modifications not only broaden their optical versatility but also enhance their applicability in deep-tissue imaging where reduced scattering and autofluorescence are essential.

Structurally, BODIPY dyes are compact, chemically stable, and highly tunable, which makes them ideal scaffolds for multifunctional probe design. Their small molecular weight and strong photostability allow for long-term imaging with minimal photobleaching, while their modularity enables facile conjugation with targeting ligands, reactive moieties, or therapeutic components. In the context of mitochondrial studies, BODIPY derivatives have been engineered with lipophilic cations or specific recognition units to achieve selective accumulation within the organelle. These probes have been widely applied to monitor mitochondrial viscosity, membrane polarity, reactive oxygen species, and ion fluctuations with high spatiotemporal precision. Beyond imaging, BODIPY's photophysical versatility has also been harnessed for theranostic applications, where the same dye scaffold can act as both a diagnostic sensor and a photosensitizer in photodynamic or photothermal therapy.

Unlike the preceding fluorophore systems that are often emphasized for spectral expansion or multimodal readout, the value of BODIPY dyes in mitochondrial research lies more prominently in their exceptional structural programmability. For mitochondrial applications, these features are particularly important because probe performance is not only determined by fluorescence brightness, but also by how precisely the scaffold can be engineered to balance organelle targeting, environmental responsiveness, cytocompatibility, and functional output. In this sense, BODIPY offers a highly adaptable platform for translating molecular-level structural variation into controllable mitochondrial imaging behavior. A representative example is the BODIPY-Mito-n series (Fig. 9a) [111], in which the BODIPY core was conjugated with triphenylphosphonium cations to achieve MMP-driven mitochondrial targeting. Importantly, rather than relying on a single targeting motif, this study systematically varied the substituent environment around the phosphonium unit, including cyclohexyl, aryl, and PEG-containing groups, in order to optimize the balance between mitochondrial accumulation, membrane potential responsiveness, and biocompatibility. A conceptually different example is provided by the cation-modified BODIPY nanoparticles (Fig. 9b) [112], where the BODIPY fluorophore was functionalized with triphenylphosphine, trimethylamine, or 1-methylimidazole groups to generate amphiphilic derivatives capable of self-assembly into positively charged nanoparticles. After cellular internalization, these nanoparticles dissociated in lysosomes and subsequently accumulated at mitochondria under the driving force of MMP, enabling localized photodynamic action upon green-light irradiation. Taken together, these two studies suggest that the distinctive advantage of BODIPY dyes is not simply their photostability, but their capacity to support both finely tunable molecular probes and functionally integrated nanoassemblies. Future work may therefore benefit from further leveraging BODIPY chemistry to bridge mitochondrial sensing and intervention, for example by combining MMP-responsive targeting with controllable therapeutic output or by extending these systems toward longer-wavelength excitation and more quantitative readout modes.

Naphthalimide dyes

Naphthalimide based fluorophores represent a versatile class of dyes characterized by a rigid aromatic imide scaffold, which imparts high fluorescence quantum yields, strong photostability, and remarkable chemical robustness. Their core structure consists of a naphthalene ring fused with an imide moiety, creating a highly conjugated system that supports efficient absorption and emission processes. Typically, naphthalimides emit in the blue to green region, but structural modifications such as the extension of π-conjugation, substitution with electron-donating or electron-withdrawing groups, or incorporation of heteroatoms can significantly red-shift the emission, extending into the red and NIR regions. This spectral tunability makes them attractive for bioimaging applications requiring deep tissue penetration and reduced background autofluorescence. Furthermore, the rigid naphthalimide skeleton restricts intramolecular rotation and vibration, minimizing non-radiative decay and thereby enhancing fluorescence brightness. The chemical flexibility of naphthalimide dyes allows facile functionalization at the imide nitrogen or aromatic positions, enabling the introduction of solubilizing groups, targeting ligands, or responsive moieties. These modifications have been widely applied to create probes that selectively accumulate in specific organelles, including mitochondria, by conjugating lipophilic cations or peptide sequences. In mitochondrial studies, naphthalimide derivatives have been engineered as sensitive reporters of microenvironmental parameters such as polarity, viscosity, and pH, taking advantage of their ICT characteristics [59,113,114].

In mitochondrial systems, dynamic variations in pH and redox homeostasis are tightly coupled with essential biological processes such as mitophagy, metabolic reprogramming, and oxidative stress response. These changes often occur at a sub-organelle level and evolve over time, placing stringent demands on fluorescent probes in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and spatial stability. Naphthalimide dyes, owing to their rigid scaffold and versatile functionalization capability, provide a robust platform for integrating targeting groups with regulatory units that control fluorescence through PET modulation or reaction-triggered activation. Such features make them particularly suitable for monitoring mitochondrial microenvironmental changes with high reliability. A representative example is the mitochondrion-immobilized pH probe MTpH (Fig. 10a) [115], which integrates a naphthalimide fluorophore with a morpholine-based pH-responsive unit and a long-chain pyridinium group for mitochondrial targeting and membrane anchoring. In this system, protonation of the morpholine group suppresses PET quenching, resulting in enhanced fluorescence under acidic conditions, while membrane immobilization enables stable mitochondrial localization. Another representative system is TPP-Naph-SS-Fc (Fig. 10b) [116], a mitochondria-targeted probe for GSH detection, which incorporates a disulfide linker and a ferrocene quencher. Upon exposure to mitochondrial GSH, reduction of the disulfide bond releases the ferrocene unit, eliminating fluorescence quenching and restoring strong emission. These examples demonstrate that the strength of naphthalimide dyes lies in their ability to serve as modular platforms for coupling fluorescence regulation mechanisms with mitochondrial targeting. By integrating PET-based control and reaction-triggered activation within a single scaffold, these probes enable precise and reliable detection of dynamic mitochondrial processes. Future developments may further exploit this design strategy to construct multifunctional probes capable of simultaneously monitoring multiple parameters, thereby providing deeper insights into mitochondrial heterogeneity and functional dynamics.

Coumarin dyes

Coumarin-based dyes are a class of fluorescent molecules derived from the naturally occurring coumarin scaffold, which consists of a fused benzene and α-pyrone ring system. This rigid and highly conjugated structure endows coumarins with strong absorption and emission in the UV region, typically between 400 and 500 nm. They generally exhibit high fluorescence quantum yields, excellent photostability, and facile structural tunability, making them attractive platforms for probe design. The presence of electron-donating or electron-withdrawing substituents on the coumarin core can significantly modulate their photophysical properties, particularly by influencing the ICT process. Extension of the conjugated system or incorporation of appropriate substituents can red-shift their emission into the red or even NIR region, thereby improving tissue penetration depth and reducing background autofluorescence during imaging. These features allow the development of mitochondria-targeted coumarin probes that exploit electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged mitochondrial inner membrane, or that respond to mitochondrial microenvironmental changes such as pH, viscosity, or ROS levels. Furthermore, coumarin derivatives can be engineered into ratiometric or multi-channel probes owing to their ICT sensitivity, allowing precise monitoring of mitochondrial physiological and pathological states [[117], [118], [119]].

In mitochondrial systems, rapid fluctuations in reactive species levels and physicochemical properties such as viscosity often occur simultaneously and are closely associated with oxidative stress, metabolic regulation, and disease progression. Capturing these dynamic and interconnected processes requires probes capable of fast response, multi-parameter sensing, and reliable mitochondrial localization. Coumarin dyes, owing to their compact structure, high fluorescence efficiency, and facile functionalization, provide a versatile platform for integrating multiple sensing modalities within a single molecular framework, thereby enabling more comprehensive monitoring of mitochondrial microenvironments. A representative example is the multifunctional probe COU-PAE (Fig. 11a) [118], which is capable of simultaneously detecting HS−, SO32−, and microviscosity in mitochondria. Through rational structural design, this probe exhibits three distinct response modes, including fluorescence quenching toward HS−, a ratiometric signal change for SO32−, and viscosity-dependent fluorescence enhancement. Another representative system is the mitochondria-targeted probe HDCl (Fig. 11b) [120], which is designed for highly selective detection of HClO. In this probe, a specific reaction with HClO triggers a fluorescence “turn-on” response, enabling sensitive detection of this reactive species under physiological conditions. These examples demonstrate that the strength of coumarin dyes lies in their ability to integrate multiple sensing mechanisms within a structurally simple and highly adaptable framework. By combining reaction-based detection with environment-sensitive fluorescence modulation, coumarin-based probes enable simultaneous monitoring of chemical and physical changes in mitochondria. Future developments may further exploit this versatility to construct multiplexed sensing systems with improved spectral properties and quantitative capabilities, thereby advancing the study of mitochondrial dynamics and dysfunction in complex biological systems.

Advances in fluorescent probes for mitochondrial parameters

Mitochondria, as multifunctional organelles, play central roles not only in ATP production and oxidative phosphorylation but also in maintaining cellular redox balance, regulating calcium homeostasis, generating ROS, and orchestrating intrinsic apoptotic pathways. Their highly specialized architecture, comprising the outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae, and matrix, supports these diverse processes. The heterogeneity and compartmentalization of mitochondrial functions necessitate precise spatiotemporal monitoring tools. In this context, fluorescence probes have emerged as indispensable tools, enabling visualization of mitochondrial morphology, dynamics, and microenvironmental changes with high resolution and sensitivity. Compared with traditional biochemical assays, fluorescent probes offer non-invasive, real-time, and subcellular-level readouts, making them highly suitable for live-cell imaging and disease mechanism exploration [[121], [122], [123]].

In recent years, a wide array of mitochondrial fluorescent probes have been developed to target specific physiological and biochemical parameters. MMP sensitive probes remain a cornerstone, as MMP is a vital indicator of respiratory activity and apoptosis. Viscosity responsive probes provide insight into alterations of the inner membrane and cristae structures, which directly influence oxidative phosphorylation efficiency. pH sensitive probes allow monitoring of proton gradients that drive ATP synthesis, while thermosensitive probes reflect mitochondrial thermogenesis during oxidative metabolism. Probes for ATP, ROS, and RSS have advanced understanding of energy metabolism, oxidative stress, and redox regulation under both physiological and pathological conditions. In addition, ion sensitive probes (e.g., for Ca2+, K+, or Zn2+) have revealed the critical roles of ionic homeostasis in mitochondrial signaling and stress response. Collectively, these probes have not only enabled detailed characterization of mitochondrial function but also provided powerful platforms for studying mitochondrial dysfunction in diseases such as neurodegeneration, cancer, and cardiovascular disorders. Mitochondrial cristae super-resolution imaging probes

Mitochondrial cristae, the intricate folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane, represent one of the most critical ultrastructural features of mitochondria. By dramatically increasing the surface area of the inner membrane, cristae provide abundant sites for the electron transport chain and ATP synthase complexes, thereby maximizing oxidative phosphorylation efficiency. The precise architecture and dynamics of cristae, including their density, curvature, and spatial organization, are tightly coupled to cellular processes such as differentiation, senescence, and apoptosis. Alterations in cristae morphology are often early indicators of mitochondrial dysfunction and disease progression, highlighting the importance of developing advanced imaging strategies to resolve these nanoscale structures with high spatial and temporal resolution [[124], [125], [126]].

Super-resolution microscopy has revolutionized the visualization of mitochondrial cristae, overcoming the diffraction limit of conventional light microscopy. Techniques such as STED, STORM, PALM, and SIM have been applied to probe cristae morphology and dynamics in live cells, enabling real-time observation of processes such as cristae remodeling, fusion–fission events, and stress induced structural reorganization [[127], [128], [129]]. To achieve optimal performance under these imaging modalities, researchers have designed mitochondria targeted fluorescent probes with enhanced photostability, high photon output, and resistance to photobleaching. Rhodamine based fluorophores have proven effective in STED and STORM applications, while silicon–rhodamine derivatives with NIR emission offer deeper penetration and reduced background interference. These specialized probes not only permit nanoscale visualization of cristae spacing and structural transitions but also provide insights into the coupling between mitochondrial ultrastructure and bioenergetic function, thereby establishing a crucial link between mitochondrial morphology and physiology.

Resolving mitochondrial cristae in living cells imposes constraints that go beyond conventional probe design, as the relevant structural features exist at the nanoscale and are highly dynamic. In this context, probe performance is determined not only by photophysical properties such as brightness and photostability, but more fundamentally by the ability to achieve persistent and well-defined localization within the IMM. In particular, reliance on membrane potential–driven accumulation is intrinsically incompatible with accurate cristae imaging, since local fluctuations in MMP can induce probe redistribution and obscure structural information. Therefore, current efforts have increasingly focused on developing probes that decouple mitochondrial targeting from electrochemical gradients, while maintaining sufficient photon output and resistance to photobleaching under super-resolution conditions. A representative example is the silicon–rhodamine probe SiRPFA (Fig. 12a) [130], in which a perfluoroalkyl chain is introduced to strengthen hydrophobic interactions with the IMM, enabling stable and MMP-independent membrane anchoring. This design provides sustained localization during dynamic cellular processes and, combined with favorable photophysical properties in the far-red region, allows high-resolution STED imaging of cristae structures in live cells. Another approach is exemplified by the covalent probes HZ Mito Red and HZ Mito Deep Red (Fig. 12b) [129], which incorporate a chloroacetyl moiety to form irreversible bonds with mitochondrial proteins, thereby eliminating probe diffusion and signal fluctuation. This covalent immobilization enables long-term super-resolution imaging, capturing continuous mitochondrial remodeling processes and maintaining structural fidelity over extended imaging periods. Taken together, these studies suggest that the central challenge in cristae imaging is not merely achieving high spatial resolution, but ensuring that the probe faithfully reports membrane architecture without being perturbed by mitochondrial dynamics themselves. Strategies based on hydrophobic anchoring and covalent immobilization represent two effective solutions to this problem, both aimed at stabilizing probe–membrane interactions at the nanoscale. Future developments may benefit from integrating these localization strategies with quantitative imaging approaches, such as fluorescence lifetime or ratiometric readouts, to further disentangle structural and functional heterogeneity within mitochondria and to enable more precise correlation between cristae organization and bioenergetic states.

Mitochondrial membrane potential probes

MMP is a vital electrochemical gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane, typically maintained at approximately −150 to −180 mV [28,131]. It serves as a key indicator of mitochondrial functionality, tightly coupled to ATP synthesis, ROS generation, calcium homeostasis, and overall cellular health. The establishment of MMP originates from proton pumping by respiratory chain complexes during oxidative phosphorylation, and this proton gradient subsequently drives ATP synthase activity. A stable MMP reflects efficient mitochondrial function, whereas its dissipation is often associated with apoptosis, necrosis, or mitochondrial dysfunction. Therefore, real time monitoring of MMP is indispensable for evaluating mitochondrial physiology and pathophysiology, as well as elucidating disease related mechanisms.

To meet this demand, a variety of fluorescent probes have been developed for sensitive and accurate MMP detection. Classical intensity based probes, such as TMRM, TMRE, and rhodamine 123, accumulate in the negatively charged mitochondrial matrix in proportion to MMP, with fluorescence intensity positively correlated to membrane polarization. Despite their wide use, these probes suffer from limitations including dependence on probe concentration, susceptibility to photobleaching, and variations due to excitation power, which compromise quantitative accuracy. To overcome these drawbacks, ratiometric probes have been designed, incorporating principles such as FRET, ICT et al. These advanced systems provide more robust and reliable detection of MMP dynamics under physiological and pathological conditions, enabling long term monitoring with high spatial and temporal resolution.

While the preceding section emphasizes the importance of spatially stable localization for resolving cristae architecture, probing MMP introduces a fundamentally different constraint: the signal must reflect an electrochemical parameter without being dominated by the distribution of the probe itself. This distinction is nontrivial, as most classical MMP probes rely on Nernstian accumulation, where fluorescence intensity is intrinsically coupled to membrane potential. As a consequence, variations in signal can arise not only from genuine changes in MMP, but also from probe redistribution, local concentration differences, or photophysical effects. In this sense, accurate MMP sensing is less a problem of sensitivity than of signal interpretability, requiring strategies that decouple readout from accumulation or introduce internal calibration. A representative approach is the OR-C8/SiR-BA system (Fig. 13a) [132], which converts MMP-dependent probe redistribution into FRET efficiency changes, enabling ratiometric and fluorescence lifetime readouts that are less sensitive to concentration variations. In contrast, the SiR-PDI-OH probe (Fig. 13b) adopts a single-molecule design capable of reversibly tracking MMP fluctuations [20], as demonstrated by its response to CCCP-induced depolarization and subsequent recovery, as well as sequential oligomycin/CCCP perturbations. These examples suggest that the key advance in MMP probe design is not merely improving brightness or targeting efficiency, but redefining how membrane potential is transduced into optical signals. FRET-based systems introduce internally referenced outputs that mitigate concentration dependence, whereas reversible single-probe designs emphasize dynamic fidelity over endpoint contrast. When viewed together with cristae imaging strategies, an emerging direction becomes evident: integrating structurally resolved imaging with quantitatively reliable MMP readouts. Such convergence may ultimately enable mapping of membrane potential heterogeneity at the level of individual cristae, providing a more direct link between mitochondrial ultrastructure and bioenergetic function.

mitochondrial inner membrane viscosity probes

Mitochondrial inner membrane (IMM) viscosity reflects the microfluidity and viscoelasticity of lipids and proteins within the bilayer, serving as a critical physical parameter for maintaining mitochondrial function. IMM viscosity is strongly influenced by factors such as cardiolipin content, fatty acid saturation, cholesterol or sterol like molecules, protein density, and protein aggregation states. In addition, environmental and biochemical cues including temperature fluctuations, oxidative stress-induced lipid peroxidation, and remodeling of respiratory supercomplexes or cristae structures can dynamically regulate IMM viscosity across different time scales. Viscosity directly determines lateral diffusion coefficients, conformational flexibility of membrane proteins, and the efficiency of proton/electron transport, thereby impacting proton gradient maintenance, ATP synthase coupling efficiency, and ROS generation. Excessively high viscosity can hinder conformational dynamics and obstruct electron transport, whereas abnormally low viscosity may compromise cristae stability and impair energy coupling. Thus, dynamic monitoring of IMM viscosity provides valuable insights into metabolic adaptation, cellular aging, ischemia–reperfusion injury, neurodegeneration, and tumor metabolic reprogramming.

Among fluorescence based strategies, TICT molecular rotors remain the most established and widely applied mechanism for IMM viscosity detection. TICT probes consist of donor–acceptor motifs linked by a rotatable bond; upon excitation, free intramolecular rotation favors the formation of a low-emissive or non-radiative TICT state, whereas high-viscosity environments suppress this torsional motion, stabilizing emissive local excited (LE) states and resulting in enhanced fluorescence intensity or lifetime [7,133,134]. Typically, TICT probes follow the Förster–Hoffmann relationship, with log(I) or log(τ) displaying linear dependence on log(η), allowing quantitative calibration using glycerol-based viscosity standards. Probe optimization strategies include tuning donor/acceptor strength and conjugation length to adjust emission wavelength and energy levels; employing steric hindrance or ring-locking to modulate torsional barriers for improved sensitivity and dynamic range; and incorporating mitochondrial targeting groups (e.g., triphenylphosphonium cations, hydrophobic alkyl chains, or peptide anchors) to ensure precise IMM localization. Beyond TICT, alternative mechanisms such as AIE, which produces strong emission upon viscosity or confinement induced aggregation, enable long term imaging but require careful control to avoid artifacts from probe clustering. Meanwhile, ICT, PET, ESIPT, and solvatochromic mechanisms have been integrated into hybrid molecular designs to enable ratiometric or multi-channel readouts, as well as interference correction against environmental factors such as ROS or thiols [33,88,135].

If cristae imaging focuses on membrane architecture and MMP sensing reflects electrochemical status, IMM viscosity provides a complementary physical dimension for understanding how mitochondrial structure and function are mechanically coupled. Viscosity is not simply a passive microenvironmental parameter; it determines lateral diffusion, protein conformational freedom, respiratory complex mobility, and the efficiency of proton/electron transport. Therefore, abnormal viscosity may either reflect structural remodeling of the IMM or actively contribute to impaired oxidative phosphorylation. This makes viscosity probes particularly valuable for moving mitochondrial imaging from “where the membrane is” and “how polarized it is” toward “how dynamically the membrane operates”. A representative example is DHX-V-C12 (Fig. 14a) [136], a TICT-based mitochondrial viscosity probe. The probe exhibits enhanced fluorescence in high-viscosity environments with a linear viscosity-dependent response in PBS/glycerol systems, and it reports mitochondrial viscosity changes induced by nystatin, monensin, rotenone, and starvation stress in live cells. Another example is NS-V (Fig. 14b) [137], which integrates an electron-donating group, fluorophore platform, and electron-withdrawing group to generate a viscosity-sensitive TICT response. NS-V shows pronounced viscosity dependent emission enhancement and visualizes viscosity alterations induced by monensin and nystatin in HeLa cells; importantly, it further extends viscosity imaging to gastritis mice, demonstrating the potential of mitochondrial viscosity probes in disease-related contexts. These examples suggest that IMM viscosity probes should not be viewed merely as reporters of “thickened” or “fluidized” membranes, but as tools for interrogating the physical basis of mitochondrial dysfunction. Compared with cristae probes and MMP probes, viscosity probes occupy an intermediate conceptual position: they can reflect lipid packing, protein crowding, and membrane remodeling, while also influencing proton coupling and respiratory efficiency. Future designs may therefore benefit from integrating viscosity readouts with MMP or cristae imaging, allowing researchers to determine whether changes in mitochondrial bioenergetics originate from electrochemical failure, structural disruption, or altered membrane mechanics.

Mitochondrial pH probes

Mitochondrial pH is a critical parameter in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and energy metabolism, typically stabilized within a weakly alkaline range (approximately 7.8–8.0). This feature not only ensures the establishment of the proton motive force across the inner membrane but also plays essential roles in regulating oxidative phosphorylation efficiency, metabolite transport, and MMP generation. Fluctuations in pH can directly alter the activity of key enzymes, thereby influencing metabolic pathway selection and energy conversion efficiency. More importantly, aberrant pH levels are closely associated with pathological processes: acidic shifts often indicate mitochondrial dysfunction or the initiation of apoptosis, whereas alkalization may reflect abnormal metabolic activity or stress responses. Thus, precise monitoring of mitochondrial pH is indispensable for elucidating the functional regulation of mitochondria in both physiological and pathological contexts. To address the growing demand for accurate monitoring of mitochondrial pH dynamics, various fluorescent probes have been developed to overcome the limitations of traditional single emission probes, which suffer from concentration dependence and photobleaching. Ratiometric probes represent a major advance, as they exhibit dual-wavelength responses under varying pH conditions, allowing quantification through emission ratios while minimizing environmental interference. Among design strategies, the ICT mechanism has been most widely employed [69]. These probes typically incorporate pH-sensitive electron donor or acceptor groups, where protonation or deprotonation significantly modulates the ICT process, leading to distinct changes in emission intensity or wavelength.

If membrane potential reflects the electrical component of the proton motive force and viscosity captures the physical state of the inner membrane, mitochondrial pH provides a more direct representation of the chemical gradient that ultimately drives ATP synthesis. Unlike MMP, which is often inferred indirectly through probe accumulation, pH is intrinsically linked to proton distribution and therefore offers a more proximal readout of respiratory activity. However, this advantage also introduces a key challenge: mitochondrial pH variations are typically subtle, spatially heterogeneous, and tightly coupled to organelle interactions and metabolic transitions. As a result, pH probes must balance sensitivity within a narrow physiological range with the ability to maintain reliable localization and avoid signal distortion, particularly under dynamic conditions such as mitophagy or ferroptosis discussed above. A representative example is the dual-color probe INSA (Fig. 15a) [21], which utilizes an ICT-based design to couple protonation-dependent fluorescence changes with simultaneous visualization of mitochondria and lysosomes. By integrating a rational donor–acceptor scaffold, INSA enables sensitive detection of pH fluctuations while tracking mitochondrial–lysosomal interactions during ferroptosis, revealing coordinated acidification processes associated with mitochondrial dysfunction. Another example is the series of donor–acceptor probes (Fig. 15b) [138], in which pH-dependent modulation of the ICT process generates ratiometric fluorescence responses. Structural optimization leads to probe 8, which exhibits an expanded detection range and improved quantitative capability, while two-photon imaging further enables accurate visualization of mitochondrial pH distribution in living cells. Taken together, these examples highlight that mitochondrial pH probes are evolving from simple indicators of acid–base changes toward tools capable of resolving dynamic proton redistribution within complex cellular contexts. In contrast to MMP probes that are often influenced by probe accumulation, ICT-based pH probes provide internally encoded signal changes that are more directly linked to local chemical environments. When integrated with structural imaging and physical-state measurements, pH sensing offers a critical dimension for understanding how proton gradients, membrane organization, and mitochondrial dynamics are coordinated. Future advances may therefore focus on combining ratiometric or lifetime-based pH readouts with high-resolution imaging, enabling direct mapping of proton heterogeneity at the level of cristae and providing deeper insight into the coupling between mitochondrial ultrastructure and bioenergetic function.

Mitochondrial temperature probes

Mitochondrial temperature serves as a critical physical parameter that reflects the metabolic activity and functional state of cells. As the primary site of oxidative phosphorylation and electron transport chain reactions, mitochondria not only synthesize ATP but also release substantial amounts of heat, resulting in a local temperature that is often several degrees higher than that of other cellular compartments. This temperature gradient is closely associated with mitochondrial energy conversion efficiency and overall cellular metabolic balance. Dynamic changes in mitochondrial temperature are tightly linked to fluctuations in respiratory chain complex activity, ATP synthesis rate, and ROS generation. Abnormal temperature elevation or reduction may indicate mitochondrial stress, dysfunction, or pathological states, including neurodegenerative diseases, cardiovascular disorders, metabolic syndromes, and cancer [139]. Therefore, accurate and real-time monitoring of mitochondrial temperature variations is not only fundamental to understanding mitochondrial function but also crucial for elucidating disease mechanisms and developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

In recent years, the development of molecular imaging techniques has greatly facilitated the design of fluorescent probes for highly sensitive mitochondrial temperature detection. These probes generally rely on temperature responsive fluorescence mechanisms, such as intensity attenuation, lifetime variation, or temperature dependent changes. Representative strategies include the incorporation of molecular rotor structures, where the intramolecular rotation rate is regulated by temperature, leading to predictable fluorescence responses. In addition, AIE systems and FLIM based probes have further improved detection precision in complex biological environments. By coupling with mitochondrial-targeting moieties, such as TPP, these probes selectively accumulate in mitochondria, enabling spatially resolved monitoring of temperature dynamics.

Following the discussion of pH probes, which report the chemical component of proton gradients, mitochondrial temperature sensing offers another dimension for understanding bioenergetic activity: the thermal consequence of oxidative metabolism. Temperature is not an isolated physical parameter, but an integrated output of electron transport efficiency, proton leakage, ATP synthesis, and stress-related metabolic reprogramming. Compared with MMP or pH, however, mitochondrial temperature is more difficult to interpret because thermal changes are small, rapidly dissipated, and easily affected by probe concentration, excitation-induced heating, and local microenvironmental effects. Therefore, effective mitochondrial thermometers must combine high thermal sensitivity with stable mitochondrial localization and internally reliable signal readout. A representative example is the NIR mitochondrial thermometer MTN (Fig. 16a) [140], through the incorporation of diethylamino groups as temperature-sensitive motifs and a mitochondria-targeting unit. MTN showed high temperature sensitivity, broad linearity, and good photostability, enabling dynamic visualization of FCCP-induced mitochondrial temperature elevation in Miha and Huh7 cells, as well as in vivo discrimination of liver fibrosis tissues through elevated mitochondrial thermogenesis. Another example is Mito-TEM 2.0 (Fig. 16b) [141], a mitochondria-anchored ratiometric thermometer. This probe combines a BODIPY donor and rhodamine B acceptor for FRET-based single excitation dual emission readout, while a formaldehyde reactive anchoring group improves long-term mitochondrial retention. It enables real-time imaging of local laser-induced mitochondrial heating and reveals distinct mitochondrial temperature elevation patterns in inflammation models stimulated by Staphylococcus aureus or LPS. Together, these examples show that mitochondrial temperature probes are moving from simple thermosensitive indicators toward more interpretable thermometry platforms. When considered together with MMP, pH, and viscosity probes, temperature sensing provides a complementary readout of mitochondrial energy conversion: MMP and pH describe the stored electrochemical gradient, viscosity reflects the physical state of the membrane, and temperature reports the dissipative outcome of metabolic activity. Future designs may therefore benefit from combining thermometry with ratiometric or lifetime imaging and improved anchoring strategies, allowing mitochondrial heat production to be correlated more directly with local respiratory activity, membrane organization, and pathological remodeling.

Mitochondrial ATP probes

ATP is the central energy currency of the cell and plays an indispensable role in sustaining life activities. As the major site of ATP production, mitochondria generate ATP primarily through oxidative phosphorylation, whereby nutrient derived electrons are transferred along the respiratory chain to drive proton pumping across the inner membrane and subsequently fuel ATP synthase. The maintenance of ATP concentration is essential for normal cellular physiology, as ATP not only provides the energy required for biochemical reactions but also acts as a signaling molecule that regulates apoptosis, oxidative stress, calcium homeostasis, and other key cellular processes. Fluctuations in mitochondrial ATP levels are closely associated with changes in mitochondrial function, while ATP depletion is a hallmark of various pathological states, including neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer [142]. Thus, precise and dynamic monitoring of mitochondrial ATP concentrations is of great scientific importance for elucidating the role of mitochondria in energy metabolism, cellular homeostasis, and disease pathogenesis. Traditional ATP detection relied mainly on bioluminescence based probes, such as luciferase systems, which provided high sensitivity but were susceptible to photobleaching, substrate availability, and environmental perturbations, thereby limiting their use in live cell imaging. Recent advances in fluorescence probe design have introduced multiple strategies to overcome these limitations. In particular, probes based on FRET and ICT mechanisms have emerged as powerful tools, offering ratiometric responses that enhance sensitivity, reduce artifacts from probe concentration or excitation intensity, and enable reliable long-term monitoring of mitochondrial ATP dynamics.

If mitochondrial temperature reflects the dissipative outcome of metabolism, ATP represents the ultimate chemical output of mitochondrial energy conversion. In contrast to parameters such as MMP, pH, or viscosity, which describe intermediate states of the bioenergetic process, ATP directly reports whether the proton motive force is successfully converted into useable cellular energy. This distinction makes ATP probes particularly informative, yet also imposes stricter requirements on probe design: ATP sensing must be selective within a highly competitive nucleotide environment and sufficiently sensitive to resolve dynamic fluctuations without being obscured by probe redistribution or environmental interference. In this context, structure-responsive probes that translate ATP recognition into well-defined photophysical transitions offer a more reliable strategy than simple intensity-based indicators. A representative example is Rh6G−ACFPN (Fig. 17a) [143], which employs a rhodamine-based spirolactam structure that undergoes ATP-induced ring opening. In the absence of ATP, the probe remains in a nonfluorescent closed form, while ATP binding triggers ring opening of the rhodamine moiety, restoring conjugation and generating strong fluorescence emission. A similar structural principle is observed in the NIR probe M838 (Fig. 17b) [144], which also relies on ATP-triggered opening of a rhodamine-like ring system to activate fluorescence. By integrating donor–acceptor groups into the scaffold, M838 further enables a ratiometric response in the near-infrared region, reducing spectral interference and improving quantitative reliability. Taken together, these examples highlight that ATP-responsive ring-opening structures provide a robust and generalizable strategy for mitochondrial ATP sensing, as they convert molecular recognition into a large and well-defined optical signal change. Compared with upstream probes discussed above, ATP probes operate at the endpoint of mitochondrial energy conversion and therefore serve as a critical benchmark for evaluating mitochondrial function. When combined with measurements of MMP, pH, viscosity, and temperature, ATP imaging enables a more complete assessment of whether bioenergetic processes are properly coupled or disrupted. Future developments may focus on integrating such ring-opening mechanisms with ratiometric or lifetime-based readouts and sub-mitochondrial localization strategies, allowing ATP production to be mapped with higher spatial precision and correlated directly with mitochondrial ultrastructure and local metabolic activity.

Mitochondrial ROS probes

Mitochondrial ROS are among the most critical metabolites generated during cellular respiration, primarily originating from electron leakage in the ETC. The major species include O2−, H2O2, and·OH. At physiological levels, ROS act as important signaling mediators, regulating diverse cellular processes such as transcriptional control, cell cycle progression, differentiation, and apoptosis. However, excessive accumulation of ROS or an imbalance in antioxidant defense systems results in oxidative modifications of proteins, lipid peroxidation, and mitochondrial DNA damage, ultimately leading to mitochondrial dysfunction [145]. Such dysregulation is closely associated with pathological processes including cellular senescence, metabolic disorders, neurodegeneration, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer. Thus, mitochondrial ROS serve not only as essential regulators of cellular homeostasis and energy metabolism but also as key driving forces of disease progression. Accurate and dynamic monitoring of ROS fluctuations at the subcellular level is therefore of great scientific significance for elucidating oxidative stress related pathophysiology.

To meet this demand, a wide range of fluorescent probes for mitochondrial ROS detection have been developed, with significant improvements in specificity, sensitivity, and imaging performance. Conventional ROS probes typically rely on specific chemical reactions with ROS to trigger fluorescence changes. For instance, probes containing aryl amines or enol-based groups can selectively detect H2O2 or O2− by undergoing irreversible oxidative transformations [146]. However, such irreversible mechanisms hinder the ability to monitor dynamic ROS fluctuations in real time. To overcome these limitations, probes incorporate reversible recognition units together with advanced photophysical signaling mechanisms such as ICT, PET, and FRET. These strategies enhance both sensitivity and spatiotemporal resolution of ROS imaging. Moreover, the introduction of mitochondrial targeting moieties, such as TPP, ensures selective accumulation within mitochondria, thereby providing accurate subcellular localization of ROS signals.

If ATP represents the biochemical output of mitochondrial energy conversion, mitochondrial ROS provide a redox-sensitive readout of how efficiently this energy conversion process is controlled. ROS are generated mainly from electron leakage in the electron transport chain, meaning that they are not merely by-products of respiration but also indicators of the coupling state between electron transfer, proton gradients, ATP synthesis, and antioxidant defense. This makes ROS probes conceptually different from ATP probes: ATP reports productive energy output, whereas ROS reveal the cost, imbalance, or pathological deviation of that output. The difficulty lies in the transient, reactive, and heterogeneous nature of ROS, which requires probes that combine chemical specificity, mitochondrial localization, and sufficient temporal responsiveness. Moreover, because mitochondrial ROS are closely intertwined with RSS-mediated redox buffering, this section also provides a natural transition to the following discussion of mitochondrial RSS probes. A representative example is QHCl (Fig. 18a) [147], developed by integrating a quinoline fluorophore with a mitochondrial-targeting moiety for selective H2O2 detection. Its sensing mechanism relies on an H2O2-induced ICT change, leading to fluorescence enhancement at 513 nm with a clear linear response over 0–20 μM H2O2. Another example is MND-IR@RESV micelles (Fig. 18b) [148], which extend mitochondrial ROS sensing from molecular detection toward imaging guided intervention. This self-assembled micellar system co-delivers imaging and therapeutic components, responds to mitochondrial ROS, and enables both visualization and treatment enhancement. These examples indicate that mitochondrial ROS probe design is moving along two complementary directions. Small-molecule probes such as QHCl emphasize selective and quantitative detection of specific ROS species, which is essential for understanding oxidative stress dynamics. In contrast, ROS-responsive nanoassemblies such as MND-IR@RESV micelles use ROS not only as a signal to be detected, but also as a pathological trigger that can guide therapeutic action. Future mitochondrial ROS probes may therefore benefit from combining species-level selectivity with reversible or ratiometric readouts, while also integrating redox-responsive delivery or intervention strategies. Such designs would allow ROS imaging to move beyond “damage reporting” toward mechanistic mapping of redox imbalance and its relationship with ATP production, membrane dysfunction, and RSS-regulated antioxidant networks.

Mitochondrial RSS probes

Mitochondrial RSS—including H2S, polysulfides (RSSn) and protein persulfides—constitute a distinct arm of cellular redox chemistry that exerts both signaling and cytoprotective functions within the organelle. Enzymatic sources (e.g. CSE, CBS, 3-MST) and mitochondrial sulfide-oxidation pathways (notably SQR and downstream carriers) control RSS flux, and mitochondrial RSS in turn modulates respiratory complex activity, thiol-based post-translational modifications (persulfidation), and the balance between ROS production and antioxidant defenses [149]. At physiological concentrations RSS participate in redox signaling and cytoprotection; however, dysregulated RSS generation or clearance precipitates oxidative damage to proteins, lipids and mtDNA, perturbs oxidative phosphorylation and membrane integrity, and contributes to pathologies ranging from inflammation and ischemia reperfusion injury to neurodegeneration and cancer.

Fluorescent sensor development for mitochondrial RSS has therefore pursued two complementary goals: high chemical selectivity for specific sulfur species, and spatiotemporally resolved, quantitative readouts in living systems. Chemical recognition strategies fall into several classes: electrophile-based triggers that undergo nucleophilic attack by H2S or polysulfides and reductive triggers that produce irreversible fluorescence activation; metal mediated displacement or precipitation approaches that transduce sulfide binding; and, increasingly, reversible coordination chemistries that permit dynamic monitoring [150]. These recognition motifs are combined with photophysical transduction elements (ICT, PET, FRET, TICT) and mitochondrial-targeting modules (TPP, lipophilic alkyl chains, targeting peptides) to yield probes with improved sensitivity, ratiometric or lifetime readouts and subcellular specificity. Future advances will likely combine rapid, reversible recognition chemistries with NIR-emissive and lifetime-based transduction, orthogonal multi-analyte architectures (simultaneous RSS/ROS/pH readouts), and integration with genetically encoded sensors and super-resolution/FLIM modalities to deliver quantitative, species resolved maps of mitochondrial sulfur dynamics in health and disease.

If mitochondrial ROS probes reveal oxidative pressure generated during dysregulated respiration, RSS probes address the other side of mitochondrial redox regulation: sulfur-based buffering and signaling. Reactive sulfur species are not simply antioxidants that neutralize ROS, but participate in thiol modification, enzyme regulation, and mitochondrial respiratory control through processes such as persulfidation and sulfide oxidation. Therefore, RSS imaging provides a complementary view of mitochondrial redox homeostasis, helping distinguish whether oxidative stress reflects uncontrolled ROS accumulation or a failure of sulfur-mediated protective networks. The analytical challenge is also different from ROS sensing: RSS probes must discriminate among closely related sulfur species, such as SO2 derivatives, H2S, polysulfides, and biothiols, in a high-thiol intracellular background. This requires not only mitochondrial targeting, but also chemically orthogonal recognition and internally calibrated signal readout. A representative example is the indolium hemicyanine probe pair MC-1/MC-2 (Fig. 19a) [151], designed for ratiometric detection of mitochondrial SO2 derivatives. By optimizing the hemicyanine framework through hydroxyl substitution and hydrophobicity regulation, MC-2 achieved faster response and stronger mitochondrial accumulation, whereas MC-1 provided a sensitive ratiometric response with a low detection limit. Both probes rely on ICT-mediated recognition of SO32−, and MC-1 successfully visualized both endogenous and exogenous mitochondrial SO32− through a red-to-green emission shift in HeLa cells. Another example is Mito-AE (Fig. 19b) [152], a ratiometric probe capable of discriminating mitochondrial SO2 derivatives from cysteine through two distinct reaction pathways. HSO3− undergoes 1,4-addition to the activated double bond, disrupting π-conjugation and inducing a blue-shifted emission, whereas Cys cleaves the 2,4-dinitrophenyl protecting group, suppressing PET and producing strong red fluorescence. Together, these examples show that mitochondrial RSS probes are moving beyond simple sulfur-responsive turn-on systems toward species-discriminating redox tools. MC-1/MC-2 emphasize structural tuning of a hemicyanine scaffold to improve mitochondrial targeting and ratiometric SO2-derivative detection, whereas Mito-AE demonstrates how two independent recognition pathways can be integrated into one probe to distinguish chemically related sulfur analytes. In the broader logic of mitochondrial functional imaging, RSS probes complement ROS probes by revealing the sulfur-dependent regulatory capacity that shapes oxidative stress outcomes. Future designs may therefore benefit from combining reversible sulfur recognition, NIR or lifetime-based readouts, and dual ROS/RSS imaging, enabling a more complete assessment of mitochondrial redox balance rather than isolated detection of a single reactive species.

Mitochondrial ion probes

Mitochondrial ions, including Ca2+, K+, Mg2+, Fe3+, Zn2+, play indispensable roles in sustaining mitochondrial and cellular functions [153]. These ions are critically involved in maintaining MMP, regulating energy metabolism, modulating enzyme activity, and coordinating intracellular signaling pathways. Among them, Ca2+ is of particular importance, as its rapid uptake and release directly regulate ATP synthesis, apoptotic signaling, and stress responses. Under physiological conditions, transient Ca2+ influx into mitochondria supports bioenergetics by stimulating oxidative phosphorylation; however, excessive Ca2+ accumulation can trigger mitochondrial permeability transition, leading to apoptosis or necrosis. Similarly, Fe3+, K+ and Mg2+ are essential for preserving ionic homeostasis and supporting metabolic activity, while Zn2+ participates in enzymatic regulation and redox balance. Given these essential functions, real-time monitoring of mitochondrial ion dynamics is vital for elucidating their roles in physiology and pathology.

To achieve accurate ion sensing within mitochondria, considerable efforts have been devoted to the design of small molecule fluorescent probes. These probes typically rely on classical fluorophores such as BODIPY, rhodamine, cyanine, or coumarin, which are functionalized with ion selective recognition groups to ensure specificity. For instance, BAPTA-derived ligands provide high affinity toward Ca2+, crown ethers selectively bind K+, dipicolylamine derivatives target Zn2+, and carboxylate or phosphonate groups enable Mg2+ recognition. To confer mitochondrial specificity, these probes are often coupled with lipophilic cationic moieties such as TPP, quaternary ammonium salts, or hydrophobic alkyl chains, which drive accumulation in mitochondria in a MMP-dependent manner.

If ROS and RSS probes reveal the redox dimension of mitochondrial regulation, ion probes address another layer of mitochondrial signaling: electrochemical communication mediated by metal ions and inorganic cations. Unlike ATP, ROS, or RSS, which often represent biochemical outputs or redox states, mitochondrial ions act as dynamic regulators that directly tune membrane potential, enzyme activity, permeability transition, and organelle–cell signaling. In particular, Ca2+ functions as a rapid messenger linking cytosolic stimulation to mitochondrial metabolism, whereas metal ions such as Fe3+ participate in redox chemistry and metabolic regulation. The central difficulty in mitochondrial ion imaging is therefore not only subcellular targeting, but also discriminating closely related ions under conditions of competing species, variable ionic strength, and rapid temporal flux. This requires probes that combine ion-selective recognition with calibrated optical responses and minimal interference from the mitochondrial microenvironment. A representative example is the KLCA probe series (Fig. 20a) [154], including KLCA-301 and KLCA-501, developed for selective mitochondrial Ca2+ detection. KLCA-301 shows a pronounced Ca2+-induced absorbance shift and fluorescence enhancement around 576 nm, whereas Mg2+ produces negligible changes; KLCA-501 extends the conjugation and gives a red-shifted Ca2+-responsive emission around 668 nm. Another example is the TP-Gal probe system (Fig. 20b) [155], which integrates galactose ligands, TPP-based mitochondrial targeting, and an Fe3+-responsive fluorophore. This design allows selective and concentration-dependent fluorescence response toward Fe3+, while the targeting module supports mitochondrial localization. Together, these examples illustrate two complementary directions in mitochondrial ion probe design. KLCA-301/KLCA-501 emphasize spectral tuning and selectivity to resolve fast Ca2+ signaling in the presence of competing Mg2+, thereby connecting ion dynamics with ATP production and MMP regulation. TP-Gal probes, in contrast, highlight the integration of ion recognition with mitochondrial targeting and cell-interaction motifs for metal-ion sensing. In the broader framework of mitochondrial functional imaging, ion probes provide a bridge between electrochemical gradients, metabolic activity, and stress signaling. Future designs may benefit from ratiometric or lifetime-based readouts, faster kinetics, and multiplex compatibility with MMP, ATP, or ROS probes, enabling ion fluxes to be interpreted not as isolated events but as coordinated components of mitochondrial physiology.

Conclusion

Mitochondria play a central role in cellular metabolism, energy transduction, and signaling pathways, and their dysfunction is implicated in a wide range of diseases including neurodegeneration, cancer, and cardiovascular disorders. Fluorescent probes have emerged as indispensable tools for visualizing mitochondrial structure and function, providing real-time, noninvasive insights into biochemical processes such as membrane potential dynamics, pH regulation, ROS generation, and ion homeostasis. Through rational molecular design and advances in imaging technology, these probes have evolved from simple localization agents to multifunctional platforms capable of quantitative sensing and super-resolution imaging, enabling a deeper understanding of mitochondrial physiology at the sub-organelle level.

Despite remarkable progress, several challenges remain. First, achieving high specificity and targeting precision continues to be a major obstacle. Many mitochondrial probes rely on the electrostatic attraction between cationic dyes and the negatively charged mitochondrial membrane, which may lead to non-specific accumulation or perturbation of membrane potential. Designing targeting strategies independent of MMP, such as ligand receptor recognition, peptide modification, or hydrophobic domain insertion will be critical for maintaining accurate localization under variable physiological and pathological conditions. Additionally, improving photostability, quantum yield, and biocompatibility remains essential for long-term imaging in live cells and tissues.

Another key challenge lies in quantitative and dynamic measurement. While ratiometric and lifetime based probes have improved accuracy by mitigating concentration and environmental effects, the correlation between probe response and absolute mitochondrial parameters often remains semi-quantitative. The integration of FLIM, super-resolution techniques, and machine learning assisted image analysis may enable precise quantification and real time tracking of mitochondrial biochemical states. Moreover, multiplexed imaging that simultaneously visualizes multiple parameters such as potential, pH, and ROS will provide a more holistic picture of mitochondrial function and dysfunction.

Looking forward, the next generation of mitochondrial probes is expected to embrace interdisciplinary design concepts, combining synthetic chemistry, molecular biology, and computational simulation. Probes with activatable or self-calibrating features, multi-modality imaging capability, and therapeutic potential will become increasingly valuable for both fundamental research and clinical applications. Furthermore, the development of probes with deep tissue penetration and NIR emission will facilitate in vivo mitochondrial imaging with minimal photodamage. Ultimately, the convergence of advanced molecular design, intelligent imaging systems, and biological insight will usher in a new era of mitochondrial research, enabling precise diagnosis, real time monitoring, and targeted intervention of mitochondrial related diseases.
