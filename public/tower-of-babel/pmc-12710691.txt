Single-nucleus multiomics reveals the disrupted regulatory programs in three brain regions of sporadic early-onset Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Andi Liu, Citu Citu, Nitesh Enduru, Xian Chen, Chia-Hao Tung, Tirthankar Sinha, Sofia E. Sepulveda, Astrid M. Manuel, Damian Gorski, Brisa S. Fernandes, Meifang Yu, Paul E. Schulz, Lukas M. Simon, Claudio Soto, Zhongming Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sporadic early-onset Alzheimer’s disease (sEOAD) represents a substantial but less-studied subtype of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Here, we generated a single-nucleus multiome atlas derived from the postmortem prefrontal cortex, entorhinal cortex, and hippocampus of nine individuals with or without sEOAD. Comprehensive analyses were conducted to delineate cell type–specific transcriptomic changes and linked candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) across brain regions. We prioritized eight conservative transcription factors in glial cells in multiple brain regions, including RFX4 in astrocytes and IKZF1 in microglia, which are implicated in regulating sEOAD-associated genes. Moreover, we identified the top 25 altered intercellular signaling between glial cells and neurons, highlighting their regulatory potential on gene expression in receiver cells. We reported 33 cCREs linked to sEOAD-associated genes overlapped with late-onset AD risk loci, and found that, in addition, sEOAD cCREs are enriched for neuropsychiatric disorder risk variants. This atlas helps dissect transcriptional and chromatin dynamics in sEOAD, providing a key resource for AD research.

INTRODUCTION

Sporadic early-onset Alzheimer’s disease (sEOAD), characterized by symptom onset before the age of 65 years (1), represents a substantial but understudied type of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). While sEOAD shares neuropathological characteristics with late-onset AD (LOAD, symptom onset after the age of 65 years), including the extracellular accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ) peptides and intracellular aggregation of tau proteins (2), it often exhibits atypical presentations and accelerated disease progression (3). Despite the phenotypic similarities, a substantial gap remains in the understanding of how alterations in gene expression and regulatory circuits in different brain regions contribute to the pathogenesis of sEOAD.

Recent advancements in single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) and single-nucleus assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (snATAC-seq) have facilitated the examination of cell type–specific transcriptomic and epigenomic profiles in the human brain. These advancements have been paramount in elucidating the regulatory elements that govern cell type–specific gene expression patterns (4–6). Furthermore, integrating snRNA-seq with snATAC-seq has proven invaluable in identifying candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) and regulatory networks associated with LOAD (7–9). However, comprehensive analyses of dysregulated regulatory circuits in the context of sEOAD remain limited. Previous research has primarily focused on regulatory mechanisms in the prefrontal cortex (PFC), but two other regions critically implicated in early stages of AD pathogenesis, the entorhinal cortex (EC) and hippocampus (HIP), have not been extensively studied (10, 11).

Mutations in the APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2 genes are known causes of Mendelian EOAD, but they account for only approximately 10% of EOAD cases (12, 13). The remaining 90% of cases are classified as sEOAD, with their genetic underpinnings largely unidentified. Genetic studies in sEOAD have unveiled both common and rare variants in genes associated with LOAD, alongside previously unidentified variants, suggesting a partially overlapping genetic framework between sEOAD and LOAD (14–16). Several genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified more than 75 common variants linked to LOAD, with the APOE ε4 allele being the strongest genetic risk factor (17–22). Moreover, studies have shown that most identified variants associated with LOAD reside in noncoding regions and are suggested to modulate gene expression through nondirect regulatory mechanisms in a cell type–specific manner (4, 7–9, 23). Therefore, a systematic assessment of variants and their potential functions is required for understanding the genetic similarities and differences between sEOAD and LOAD.

To bridge these research gaps and delineate the molecular landscape of sEOAD, we generated single-nucleus multiome data of a small sEOAD cohort. The multiome profiling, which combines snRNA-seq and snATAC-seq in a single experiment, can effectively reduce batch effects among experiments. Our dataset includes gene expression and chromatin accessibility profiles from more than 76,000 nuclei obtained from postmortem brain samples of the PFC, EC, and HIP regions of individuals with sEOAD and age-matched control donors. Here, we conducted systematic bioinformatics analyses to detect cellular compositional changes, transcriptome alterations associated with sEOAD, and cell type–specific cCREs influencing gene expression across different brain regions. We examined transcription factors (TFs) that may exert their functions through distal enhancer-gene connections and identified their targeted genes in the brain of individuals with sEOAD. Furthermore, we examined the altered intercellular communications between nonneuronal and neuronal cell types and identified potential target genes in receiver cells in sEOAD. Last, to explore shared and unique genetic components, we fine-mapped the genetic variants associated with LOAD and other traits to the cell type–specific chromatin accessible peaks in sEOAD brains. Altogether, our study provides a comprehensive overview of gene regulatory mechanisms in sEOAD brains, shedding light on the complex interplay between diverse brain cell types in its pathophysiology.

RESULTS

Joint snRNA-seq and snATAC-seq profiling of sEOAD in three brain regions

To investigate the dysregulated gene regulatory mechanisms in sEOAD brains, we generated joint snRNA-seq and snATAC-seq (snMultiome) profiles from each sample. We conducted 10x Chromium single-nucleus multiome assays on the nuclei isolated from postmortem PFC (n = 9), EC (n = 6), and HIP (n = 6) tissues of individuals with sEOAD and matched controls. The four individuals with sEOAD (age at death: 59 to 64 years) did not have any established causal variants in APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2 associated with EOAD, as documented on the Alzheimer’s Forum website (https://alzforum.org/mutations). The five age-matched controls (age at death: 61 to 65 years) had no neuropsychiatry diagnoses (Fig. 1A and data S1). Sample-level quality control was performed using the CellBender tool (24) to remove systematic background noise in the snRNA-seq assay, followed by filtration of low-quality nuclei in both snRNA-seq assay and snATAC-seq assay and putative doublets in both assays (fig. S1, A to D, and Materials and Methods). Notably, neuronal nuclei, particularly excitatory and inhibitory neurons, tended to show lower transcription start site (TSS) enrichment scores in the snATAC-seq assay, resulting in higher exclusion rates during quality control (QC) (fig. S1B). This trend is consistent with prior reports and likely reflects intrinsic neuronal chromatin features and nucleus fragility (8). In total, we generated high-quality transcriptomic and epigenomic profiles of 76,173 nuclei. The normalization, unsupervised dimension reduction, and integration were performed based on either gene expression, open chromatin accessibility profiles, or both modalities combined. The results revealed consistent cell clustering structures across three brain regions, as visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) (Fig. 1B and fig. S2, A to D).

We annotated the 76,173 nuclei by mapping the snRNA-seq profile to reference snRNA-seq datasets of the human brain from previous studies (25, 26). These 76,173 nuclei were categorized into eight major cell types (with confidence score > 0.95 from references) and high-resolution subcell types (Fig. 1B and fig. S2A). Consistent with previously published snRNA-seq studies of the human brain (7, 9, 25–27), we observed cell type–specific marker gene expression within each annotated cluster, including AQP4 in astrocytes, SLC17A7 in excitatory neurons, GAD2 in inhibitory neurons, CSF1R and CD74 in microglia, MOBP in oligodendrocyte, PDGFRA in oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), FLT1 in endothelial cells, and PDE5A in vascular and leptomeningeal cells (VLMCs)/pericytes (Fig. 1C). Chromatin accessibility information around cell-type marker genes also revealed unambiguous patterns distinguishing major cell types in the brain (Fig. 1D and fig. S2, B to D). Moreover, to further investigate the nuclei with overlapping distributions in the snATAC-seq UMAP (Fig. 1B), we computed chromatin erosion scores using ChromHMM based on a previously described method (8). We found that nuclei within overlapping clusters exhibited significantly elevated erosion scores, suggesting diminished chromatin compartmentalization (fig. S2E). Notably, excitatory and inhibitory neurons showed higher erosion scores compared to nonneuronal cells, indicating greater epigenetic vulnerability. These findings supported the hypothesis that such overlaps may reflect biological states of chromatin erosion. Together, these results indicated that the snRNA-seq and snATAC-seq data were generally of high quality and that cell-type annotations were reliable. Because VLMCs/pericytes and endothelial cells accounted for fewer than 200 nuclei, they were excluded from further analyses.

Our cell-type composition analysis using the single-cell compositional data analysis (scCODA) tool (28) revealed the alterations in cell-type abundance among different brain regions and conditions (Fig. 1E, fig. S3, and data S2). Excitatory and inhibitory neurons were significantly less abundant in the EC and HIP compared to the PFC (posterior probability of inclusion >0.95), while the other six major cell types remained stable across regions (fig. S3A). These regional differences may reflect tissue sampling closer to white matter in the EC and HIP, leading to a higher, though not statistically significant, proportion of oligodendrocytes relative to the PFC, whereas astrocytes and microglia presented comparable levels across the regions. Although a reduced abundance of excitatory neurons was observed in the sEOAD PFC than that in controls, no significant differences in the proportion of other cell types were found between sEOAD and controls across the three brain regions (fig. S3, B to D). These findings aligned with the notion that AD brains are pathologically characterized by neuronal loss, at least in the PFC (29). Although not statistically significant, a higher abundance of oligodendrocytes was observed in sEOAD than in controls, particularly in the EC (fig. S3, B to D).

Cell type–specific dysregulation of gene expression in sEOAD across brain regions

To systematically characterize transcriptomic changes, we performed differential gene expression analysis in each major cell type of different brain regions between the sEOAD and control groups. We identified 1269 sEOAD-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the PFC, 3348 in the EC, and 2735 in the HIP (data S3). These sEOAD DEGs were identified based on consensus signals from both Model-based Analysis of Single-cell Transcriptomics (MAST) and mixed-effect models (adjusted P value <0.05, absolute log2 fold change [|log2FC|] >0.25), accounting for sex, age at death, and batch (Fig. 2A and Materials and Methods). Despite similar numbers of nuclei captured in each brain region (fig. S2, B to D), a higher number of sEOAD DEGs were found in the EC and HIP compared to the PFC. This finding from the snMultiome assay supports the previous reports that the EC and HIP are primarily affected in the early stages of AD pathogenesis (30). Furthermore, sEOAD DEGs exhibited larger effect sizes (|log2FC| > 1) in the HIP and EC compared to the PFC (Fig. 2B). To further validate the robustness of these findings, we performed an additional pseudo-bulk differential expression analysis using aggregated cell-type counts across individuals. While this approach identified fewer DEGs due to reduced statistical power, a substantial portion of the top-ranked DEGs overlapped with those from single-nucleus models in the same cell type and dysregulation direction (fig. S4A and data S3).

Among the DEGs in the sEOAD PFC, 833 were consistent with those identified in previous snRNA-seq studies of LOAD PFC (fig. S4B). Notably, 188 DEGs were up-regulated or down-regulated in the same major cell types reported in a recent snMultiome study (9). Three DEGs were consistent across all studies: up-regulation of MRAS in astrocytes and up-regulation of CHORDC1 and down-regulation of TP53TG5 in oligodendrocytes.

We identified 274 cell type–specific sEOAD DEGs consistently up-regulated and down-regulated across all three brain regions (Fig. 2C and fig. S4C), including 205 genes previously reported in snRNA-seq studies of LOAD PFC (7, 9, 25, 31). For example, ZBTB16 was consistently up-regulated in microglia across the PFC, EC, and HIP (Fig. 2, D and E, and fig. S5A). ZBTB16 encodes a zinc finger and BTB domain-containing TF involved in cell cycle progression and transcriptional repression. The alteration of this gene in early ages in mice brain was reported to be associated with cognitive deficits (32). We also identified other consistently dysregulated genes, such as down-regulated CIRBP and up-regulated ITGB8 in the astrocytes (fig. S4D). Those genes have been reported to be relevant to AD mechanisms (33, 34). Among the consistent DEGs across regions, we reported 69 genes that were not previously reported in snRNA-seq analysis of LOAD in the PFC. For example, MAML2, which we found down-regulated in oligodendrocytes (fig. S4D), is part of the Notch signaling pathway, linked to neurovascular dysfunction in AD (35).

Most cell type–specific sEOAD DEGs showed varying dysregulation patterns across the considered regions (fig. S5A). For example, GRM3, which encodes the glutamate metabotropic receptor 3 and is associated with synaptic function, was down-regulated in astrocytes in the PFC and EC but not in HIP (Fig. 2F). This gene was found altered in AD and other neurological disorders (36, 37). To further dissect such region-dependent expression changes, we performed a diagnosis-by-region interaction analysis. Genes exhibiting significant interaction effects highlighted divergent expression patterns across brain regions in sEOAD versus controls. For example, VIPR1 and NEUROD2 in excitatory neurons, and SLC26A4 and MPPED1 in inhibitory neurons, showed distinct region-dependent dysregulation patterns between diagnostic groups (38). In addition, astrocytic and OPC genes such as FREM2, INPP5D, AKR1C3, and HAPLN1 also exhibited varying expression changes across brain regions (fig. S5B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these interaction genes further revealed that those identified in excitatory and inhibitory neurons were significantly enriched for trans-synaptic signaling and synaptic organization pathways (fig. S5, C and D), supporting the hypothesis that regionally variable synaptic dysregulation contributes to sEOAD pathogenesis.

In our gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of sEOAD DEGs for each cell type, we observed diverse enriched GO BP terms across the three regions [Benjamini-Hochberg (BH)–adjusted P value <0.05] (Fig. 2G). Up-regulated DEGs in inhibitory neurons were consistently enriched in synaptic functions, such as “modulation of chemical synaptic transmission” and “regulation of trans-synaptic signaling,” across all brain regions. However, only up-regulated DEGs in excitatory neurons in the HIP were enriched in synaptic functions, indicating region-specific dysregulation. Up-regulated genes in the microglia were enriched in “ peptidyl-serine phosphorylation” and “peptidyl-serine modification” functions in the EC and HIP but not in the PFC. Down-regulated DEGs in inhibitory neurons across brain regions were enriched in “vesicle-mediated transport in synapse,” “axonogenesis,” and “ATP metabolic process,” suggesting potential synaptic transmission dysfunction and reduced energy metabolism in sEOAD brains (39, 40).

Given the modest sample size, we performed a simulation-based power analysis to better contextualize our differential expression results. The analysis accounted for gene dropout, intra- and interindividual variability, and unequal cell counts. We observed that the power to detect DEGs varied substantially across cell types and brain regions. Specifically, statistical power was limited in less abundant cell type such as inhibitory neurons in the EC, particularly for genes with moderate effect sizes (data S4). In contrast, higher power was observed in more abundant cell types and for DEGs with large effect sizes. This evaluation suggested that the absence of significant DEGs in some cell types or regions might reflect limited power rather than an actual lack of biological signal. Accordingly, larger-scale single-nucleus studies will be essential to resolve more subtle and region-specific transcriptomic changes underlying sEOAD pathophysiology.

Cell type–specific candidate cCREs for sEOAD DEGs

To assess potential cell type–specific open chromatin accessible signals associated with sEOAD, we performed peak calling on snATAC-seq assay using Model-based Analysis of ChIP-Seq 2 (MACS2) (41). We identified 317,963 peaks across all major cell types. Of these, 74.62% overlapped with cCREs from the adult human brain atlas by Li et al. (4), 61.29% overlapped with ENCODE cCREs, 91.66% overlapped with the SCREEN cCREs database, and 48.06% overlapped with the psychSCREEN cCREs database (Fig. 3A) (42, 43). In our further comparison with psychSCREEN’s neuron- and glia-specific cCREs, we observed relatively low overall overlaps (11.26 and 10.92%, respectively), suggesting that many peaks extended beyond currently annotated cell type–specific elements and, thus, might include previously uncharacterized regulatory regions. Stratifying our peaks by cell type revealed strong concordance with expected brain cell type–specific patterns: The peaks from excitatory neurons had a stronger overlap with neuron-specific cCREs, whereas the peaks from astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and OPCs aligned more with glia-specific cCREs (Fig. 3A). These results supported the biological validity and cell-type specificity of our peak calling, and they also suggested the presence of previously unidentified regulatory elements that may be relevant to sEOAD pathogenesis. Genomic annotation revealed that 22.55% of peaks were within 3 kb of the nearest transcription start site (TSS), 46.00% in intronic regions, and 24.18% in distal intergenic regions (Fig. 3B).

To decode the dysregulated transcriptional regulatory circuitry in sEOAD brains, we conducted peak-to-gene linkage analyses in each brain region. We leveraged shared barcodes in snMultiome profiling to directly link epigenomic profiles with the respective transcriptomic profiles. We prioritized open chromatin peaks located within 500 kilo–base pairs of the TSS of each sEOAD DEG, focusing on significant positively correlated peaks (Spearman’s correlation >0.05, BH-adjusted P value <0.05), while accounting for the target gene, considering peak size, GC content, and fragment count. In total, we identified 3802 cCRE-DEG associations in the PFC, involving 727 unique DEGs. In the EC, we found 21,467 of these associations, affecting 2475 unique DEGs, while in the HIP, we reported 15,268 associations affecting 1821 unique DEGs (data S5). Each sEOAD DEG was linked to a median of three cCRE in the PFC, six in the EC, and five in the HIP (Fig. 3C). Despite meeting the statistical thresholds, the peak-gene correlations were modest in magnitude, with median Spearman coefficients of 0.07 (PFC), 0.09 (EC), and 0.08 (HIP) (fig. S6A). As expected, only a small portion of sEOAD-DEG–linked cCREs overlapped with differentially accessible regions (fig. S6B). Such modest correlations were consistent with previous multiome studies (6, 9, 44, 45) and likely reflected the subtle regulatory effects of distal enhancers in the human brain. To further interpret regulatory functions, we annotated sEOAD-DEG–linked cCREs using both the ENCODE and brain-specific psychSCREEN databases. A large proportion of cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs were annotated as distal enhancer-like elements by both the ENCODE (52.33% in PFC, 51.92% in EC, and 55.04% in HIP) and psychSCREEN databases (Fig. 3D, fig. S6C, and data S5). Notably, psychSCREEN also identified a higher proportion of promoter-like cCREs and provided broader coverage of brain-specific regulatory elements (fig. S6C). Annotation patterns were consistent across cell types in each region based on both cCRE databases (fig. S6, D and E). These findings highlighted the widespread, context-dependent roles of both promoters and enhancers in regulating sEOAD transcriptomic changes and underscored the complexity of regulatory element classification in the human brain.

TFs are the primary regulators of gene expression, and their dysregulation has been associated with AD pathogenesis (46). Using snATAC-seq data, we conducted motif enrichment analysis to nominate TFs that may contribute to altered gene expression in sEOAD in each cell type of each brain region. To prioritize bona fide signals, we focused on TFs expressed in more than 25% of the corresponding cell types. In total, 207 TF motifs were enriched in the cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs (BH-adjusted P value <0.05, Fig. 3E and data S6). Among them, 140 motifs were significantly enriched in the cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs across multiple cell types. For instance, motifs of ZNF148 had significant enrichment in cCREs associated with sEOAD DEGs in all major cell types (BH-adjusted P value <0.05, Fig. 3E). Conversely, several TFs exhibited cell type– and region-specific enrichment patterns. For example, EGR1/3 motifs were enriched in excitatory neurons, SPI1 and IKZF1 were enriched in microglia, and ETV6 motifs were enriched specifically in astrocytic cCREs linked to up-regulated DEGs in the EC. Similarly, KLF3 motifs were uniquely enriched in cCREs linked to up-regulated DEGs in inhibitory neurons in the PFC (Fig. 3E and fig. S7A). These findings indicated that, in addition to the shared regulatory programs, region-specific transcriptional regulation might contribute to the molecular heterogeneity of sEOAD. DNA footprinting analysis further supported the motif activity of several TFs in a cell type– and region-dependent manner (Fig. 3, F and G). Notably, many identified TF motifs (189 out of 207, 91.3%) were enriched in the cCREs positively correlated with the up-regulated DEGs, and in separate, nonoverlapping cCREs linked to down-regulated DEGs within the same cell type. This suggested that TFs might function as both activators and repressors in different chromatin regions, thereby targeting different genes. These findings were consistent with prior evidence that TFs can exhibit context-dependent regulatory effects through recruitment of distinct cofactors and chromatin remodeling complexes (47, 48).

Cell type–specific regulatory networks in the sEOAD brain

To further explore the alterations in sEOAD brains, we identified cell type–specific TFs potentially modulating gene expression through enhancer binding in each brain region (Fig. 4A). Using the single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering+ (SCENIC+) algorithm, we inferred enhancer-driven regulons by linking TFs to accessible chromatin regions enriched for their motifs and to coregulated genes via chromatin accessibility profiles (49). To prioritize biologically relevant regulons, we calculated the correlation between the expression of each TF and the regulon activity, quantified by the enrichment scores of corresponding open chromatin regions. Regulons within the top 5% of strongest positive correlations were considered putative activating regulators, where TF expression and chromatin accessibility are positively correlated. On the other hand, those with the bottom 5% negative correlations were considered potential repressors, indicating an inverse relationship between TF expression and chromatin accessibility. This correlation-based filtering enabled the identification of TFs whose expression is tightly coupled with activation or repression of regulatory programs in specific cell types and brain regions.

Across regions, we identified 19 key regulators in PFC, 31 in EC, and 16 in HIP (Fig. 4B; fig. S7, B and C; and data S6). Each regulon comprised, on average, 877 open chromatin regions and 279 target genes. Notably, strong cooperativity was observed among TFs, with extensive overlap in regulatory targets (fig. S7, D to F). For example, IKZF1 and FLI1 exhibited highly overlapping regulatory targets across all three brain regions (Jaccard coefficient > 0.5), suggesting coordinated regulatory roles. These findings support the presence of complex, cooperative TF networks underlying cell type–specific transcriptional regulation in the human brain (50, 51). Eight regulators were found to be conserved in specific cell types across all three brain regions, including RFX4 in astrocytes; FLI1, IKZF1, MEF2C, RUNX1, FOXN3, and POU2F2 in microglia; and SOX6 in OPC (fig. S7G). Despite conservation, shared TFs displayed region-specific differences in target gene repertoires. For example, RFX4 eRegulons showed high cross-regional consistency, with 331 target genes shared across PFC, EC, and HIP. In contrast, IKZF1 targets were more region specific, with 377 shared genes between EC and HIP, but only 51 between PFC and HIP (fig. S7, H and I). These results indicated that, although some TFs are conserved across regions, their regulatory outputs could diverge, likely reflecting region-specific chromatin landscapes and transcriptional contexts. To further support the functional relevance of these TFs, we validated their expression using an independent bulk RNA-seq dataset. Differential expression analysis confirmed up-regulation of IKZF1 and down-regulation of SOX6 in sEOAD brains compared to controls (adjusted P < 0.05; fig. S7, J and K), consistent with their predicted activity in microglia and OPCs, respectively.

We further explored the gene regulatory networks within the EC region because it had the largest number of significant regulons among the three regions. The SCENIC+ analysis revealed 31 regulons in EC, suggesting enhanced regulatory activity in this region when compared to PFC and HIP (Fig. 4B and fig. S7, A and B). Among the 31 EC regulons, we prioritized those whose motifs were significantly enriched in cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs (Fig. 3E and data S5). This identified five astrocytic activators (RFX4, PAX6, RFX2, EMX2, and TCF7L2) and two microglial activators (IKZF1 and FLI1). The five astrocytic TFs collectively regulated 101 up-regulated and 112 down-regulated DEGs (Fig. 4C and fig. S7E). RFX4 in particular showed high specificity in astrocytes, with elevated chromVAR motif activity in sEOAD samples (BH-adjusted P = 8.59 × 10−32, Fig. 4D), and its footprinting further supported strong binding activity (52). The RFX4 regulon included 661 target genes, comprising 82 up-regulated and 96 down-regulated DEGs in sEOAD astrocytes. Enrichment analysis of these targets revealed pathways related to “l-aspartate transmembrane transport,” “regulation of membrane potential,” and “Wnt signaling” (BH-adjusted P < 0.05, Fig. 4, E and F), implicating RFX4 in synaptic signaling and homeostatic maintenance. In addition, we observed the up-regulation of GFAP, which encodes for glial fibrillary acidic protein, which is associated with astrocyte activation during stress and injury (53).

In microglia, both IKZF1 and FLI1 were identified as specific regulators with motifs enriched in cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs. Together, they targeted 71 up-regulated and 19 down-regulated DEGs (Fig. 4G). Footprinting analysis and chromVAR scores indicated sustained IKZF1 activity in microglia across both sEOAD and control samples (Fig. 4H). The IKZF1 regulon involved 46 up-regulated and 19 down-regulated DEGs in the microglia of sEOAD. The dysregulated genes targeted by IKZF1 were predominantly involved in immune-related functions such as “negative regulation of immune response,” “leukocyte-mediated immunity,” and “immune response-activating signaling pathways” (BH-adjusted P value <0.05, Fig. 4I). This underscores the critical role of IKZF1 in mediating the immune response and pathophysiology of sEOAD.

Collectively, these findings indicate that sEOAD-associated gene expression changes are orchestrated by cooperative, cell type– and region-specific TF regulatory networks. RFX4 in astrocytes and IKZF1 in microglia emerge as key regulators in EC, modulating genes involved in transport, synaptic signaling, and innate immune responses, thereby contributing to the molecular pathology of sEOAD.

Dysregulated intercellular signaling between nonneuronal and neuronal cell types

The altered regulatory relationships in nonneuronal cell types and their impact on neuronal circuit homeostasis and proinflammatory responses might play an important role in LOAD brains (54). To estimate such association in sEOAD brains, we conducted comparative cell-cell communication analyses using the MultiNicheNet tool (55). This analysis facilitated the identification of dysregulated intercellular communication mediated by ligand-receptor pairs and their potential regulatory influence on the receiver cells. We prioritized the top 25 differential signals sent and received by specific cell types as condition-specific intercellular signaling.

Here, we highlighted the intercellular communication analysis of astrocytes and microglia in the EC. Specifically, the results showed that all top 25 differential signals sent by astrocytes were down-regulated in sEOAD (Fig. 5A). The communication signals between EFNA5 in astrocytes to EPHA3 and EPHA6 in excitatory neurons were down-regulated (Fig. 5A). The Ephrin-Eph signaling plays an important role in regulating cell migration in neurons. It is also associated with cytoskeletal rearrangements, as it regulates small GTPase activation and inactivation (56). Furthermore, we observed multiple down-regulated ligand-receptor pairs involved in neuroinflammation regulation, such as PTN-PTPRS/PTPRB signaling from astrocytes to excitatory neurons and A2M-LRP1 signaling from microglia to astrocytes (Fig. 5, A and B). Although more up-regulated intercellular signalings were observed in the sEOAD PFC, down-regulated PSAP-LRP1 signaling from microglia to astrocytes was noted in all three brain regions (fig. S8, A to D). The reduced levels of PSAP protein have been associated with increased neurofibrillary tangle development and neuroinflammation (57). Furthermore, we identified up-regulated TGFB1-ITGB8 signaling from microglia to astrocytes, suggesting a potential increasing microgliogenesis in the sEOAD EC and PFC (58).

We further explored the regulatory influence of the ligands in sender cell types on the sEOAD DEGs in receiver cell types. Among the top differential communication pathways from astrocytes to excitatory neurons, ligands encoded by SLITRK2, EFNB2, OLFM2, and EFNA5 in astrocytes exhibited increased ligand activity (Fig. 5C). For example, SLITRK2 (Slit and Trk-like Family Member 2) has an important role in synaptic development and neuronal signaling (59). In excitatory neurons, several TF genes, including EGR1, EGR3, and JUN, were predicted targets of astrocyte-derived ligands (Fig. 5C). The predicted down-regulated DEGs in excitatory were enriched in GO Biological Process related to neurotrophin signaling peptidyl-serine phosphorylation, and cell-matrix adhesion, indicating impaired intracellular signaling and synaptic maintenance in sEOAD brains (Fig. 5D). Conversely, ligands encoded by PSAP and A2M in microglia exhibited high ligand activity, potentially modulating the expression of targeted genes in astrocytes (Fig. 5E and fig. S8, E and F). Enrichment analysis of these up-regulated astrocyte DEGs revealed down-regulation of pathways related to peptidyl-tyrosine phosphorylation, astrocyte differentiation, and axon guidance, whereas up-regulated genes were enriched in estradiol response and immune modulation (Fig. 5, F and G). To further validate the biological relevance of these predicted targets, we examined their overlap with astrocytic regulons inferred from SCENIC+ analysis. Notably, a subset of ligand-targeted genes, including ADGRG1, AGT, AHNAK, ALDOC, BCL6, EGFR, ENO1, F3, FGFR3, HES1, SERPINE2, SLC1A3, SLC1A4, TIMP3, and TUBB2B, were identified as transcriptional targets of astrocyte-specific TFs such as RFX4 and PAX6, among others (Fig. 4C). This convergence highlights the coordinated regulation between extrinsic ligand-receptor signaling and intrinsic transcriptional programs in astrocytes, supporting their role in sEOAD pathogenesis.

Genetic variation at candidate CREs linked to sEOAD DEGs

To better understand the cellular impact of genetic variants identified by AD GWAS, we prioritized risk loci that overlapped with cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs. We curated 148 index single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), reported as genome-wide significant from four LOAD GWAS (Fig. 6A) (17, 18, 20, 21). We expanded these SNPs using linkage disequilibrium (LD) to include nearby variants with high coinheritance probability (LD R2 > 0.8 based on phase 3 of the 1000 Genomes Project data). We identified 3180 unique SNPs associated with LOAD, of which 98.2% were in noncoding regions. A total of 124 LOAD SNPs mapped in cCRE sequences were linked to 23 sEOAD DEGs across brain regions (Fig. 6B). Notably, cCREs linked to the down-regulated HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DRA genes in microglia intersected with the largest number of SNPs associated with LOAD (Fig. 6B). The index SNP rs9271058 [reported in Kunkle et al. (18)] was mapped to a cCRE (chr6:32607514 to 32607728) linked to HLA-DRB1 in microglia in the EC (Fig. 6C). There were 43 SNPs mapped to a cCRE (chr6:32603880 to 32604798) that was linked to HLA-DRB1. This distal-like enhancer cCRE contains TF motifs specific to microglia regulators, such as IKZF1 and FLI1. Besides, we found that the index SNP (rs74685827) intersected with the microglia- and astrocyte-specific cCREs linked to dysregulated SORL1 gene expression in the HIP (fig. S9A) (21). We extended this approach to include 172 unique SNPs (LD R2 > 0.8) from five index SNPs associated with EOAD (P value <5 × 10−8) (60). We next assessed the regulatory relevance of sEOAD-DEG-linked cCREs by integrating the single-cell expression quantitative trait locus (sc-eQTL) dataset (61). We included all significant eSNPs across seven major cell types. Our analysis revealed that cCREs linked to 703 sEOAD-DEGs intersected with at least one significant eSNP in the corresponding cell type. Among these, 94 DEGs showed extensive overlap, each linked to cCREs harboring more than 10 distinct eSNPs (fig. S9B). For example, cCREs linked to down-regulated CLU in astrocytes of the EC overlapped with four astrocyte-specific eSNPs for CLU (fig. S9C). These results suggested that a subset of dysregulated genes in sEOAD might be modulated by cell type–specific genetic regulatory variants. Of note, future work is warranted to confirm the functional consequences of these regulatory interactions in the context of sEOAD.

To validate our finding of decreased HLA-DRB1 expression in microglia from individuals with sEOAD, we performed immunofluorescence staining on brain tissue samples from patients with sEOAD and healthy controls (data S8). Tissue sections were costained for the nuclear marker 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; blue), the microglial marker IBA1 (red) (62), and our protein of interest, HLA-DRB1 (green). Qualitative assessment of the stained tissues revealed a clear difference between the groups. While IBA1-positive microglia in healthy control tissue displayed prominent HLA-DRB1 immunoreactivity, this signal was visibly diminished in microglia from patients with sEOAD (Fig. 6D). To precisely quantify this observation, we measured the fluorescence intensity in a large cohort of individual microglia from both healthy controls (n = 772) and patients with sEOAD (n = 737) (data S8). Crucially, the analysis confirmed our primary observation: The median fluorescence intensity of HLA-DRB1 was significantly lower in microglia from the sEOAD group (median = 0.14) compared to the healthy controls (median = 0.18, P = 2.5 × 10−100) (Fig. 6E). This primary finding was accompanied by a slight but statistically significant reduction in the intensity of the IBA1 marker itself (Fig. 6F, P = 1.7 × 10−17). While the results supported reduced microglial activity in sEOAD, it may also reflect a late-stage disease effect in which chronically activated microglia become functionally impaired with diminished immunoreactivity. Collectively, these quantitative immunofluorescence data helped validate HLA-DRB1 protein expression being down-regulated in microglia within the pathological context of sEOAD.

We next performed stratified linkage disequilibrium score regression (sLDSC) (63) to assess the heritability enrichment of genetic risk loci within cCREs linked to sEOAD-associated DEGs in each cell type. This analysis incorporated GWAS summary statistics from LOAD, immune-mediated disorders, and major psychiatric conditions (64–81) (data S9).

Notably, we did not observe significant heritability enrichment for LOAD-associated SNPs in cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs (BH-adjusted P value >0.05, Fig. 6G and data S10A). In contrast, we identified significant enrichment for neuropsychiatric disorder risk variants—including schizophrenia and bipolar disorder—within cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs in excitatory and inhibitory neurons (BH-adjusted P value <0.05, Fig. 6G).

To further explore genetic architecture at the broader epigenomic level, we performed a complementary sLDSC analysis using cell type–specific cCREs, independent of DEG linkage (Materials and Methods). In line with previous reports (7, 8), microglia-specific cCREs showed significant heritability enrichment for LOAD, as well as for immune-related disorders such as Crohn’s disease, multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, ulcerative colitis, and vitiligo (adjusted P value <0.05, fig. S9D and data S10B). A recent study revealed shared and unique genetic components among these disorders (82). Consistent with the DEG-linked analysis above, the cCREs specific to excitatory neurons were enriched for the heritability of psychiatric traits.

Together, our results indicated that while microglial cCREs globally captured LOAD genetic risk, the cCREs specifically linked to transcriptional changes in sEOAD were more enriched for neuropsychiatric traits and are predominantly located in neuronal regulatory elements. These findings highlighted a partially distinct genetic and regulatory landscape in sEOAD and underscored the need for future studies to identify causal variants and mechanisms specific to early-onset AD.

DISCUSSION

We generated single-nucleus multiome profiles of sEOAD, establishing a comprehensive atlas of gene expression and chromatin regulation in the PFC, EC, and HIP regions. By profiling 76,173 nuclei from four sEOAD cases and five matched controls, we simultaneously assessed chromatin accessibility and gene expression. Our analyses identified cell type–specific transcriptome alterations in sEOAD and linked cCREs across brain regions. We characterized conserved regulons in nonneuronal cells and found that multiple TFs may cooperatively modulate transcriptomic changes, leading to dysregulated synaptic plasticity and neuroinflammation in astrocytes and microglia. In addition, we prioritized altered intercellular signaling pathways and their regulatory impacts on sEOAD DEGs. Our genetic fine-mapping analysis revealed that cCREs linked to sEOAD-associated genes intersected with LOAD risk loci and may share genetic risks with neuropsychiatric disorders.

The inclusion of multiple brain regions in our study has enabled us to examine conserved regulators and their target genes disrupted in sEOAD across the brain regions. The results might imply an early onset of brain aging in patients with sEOAD, characterized by decreased synaptic function and increased neuroinflammation—processes typically observed in later life stages, usually after the ages of 60 (83). Notably, recent work has proposed that the neurodevelopmental pathways that are active during early brain maturation may become dysregulated and contribute to accelerated neurodegenerative processes later in life (84). Consistent with this, several dysregulated TFs identified in our study, including RFX4 and SOX6, have been previously reported with their roles in brain development, neuronal differentiation, and oligodendrocyte maturation (85). Thus, our findings raise the possibility that disruption of neurodevelopmental regulatory programs may predispose to early vulnerability in sEOAD brains. For example, RFX4 in astrocytes and IKZF1 in microglia were among the most conserved regulators across regions. The RFX4 motif was enriched in cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs in all regions, and its target genes were enriched in functions related to transmembrane transport and trans-synaptic signaling. In microglia, genes involved in immune processes, targeted by IKZF1 and FLI1, were consistently up-regulated. We also found that cCREs contain TF motifs specific to microglial regulators such as IKZF1 and FLI1, suggesting that genetic variants within these cCREs may modulate gene expression and contribute to sEOAD risk. These findings indicate that gene expression changes in sEOAD are driven by complex, cell type–specific transcriptional programs acting through distal enhancers and are potentially shaped by both early neurodevelopmental pathways and mechanisms of accelerated brain aging.

However, because of the limited data, we could not dissect the effect of normal aging on these genes and regulations. In addition, we observed that a subset of nuclei formed overlapping clusters in the snATAC-seq UMAP projection (fig. S2E). To explore whether this pattern was due to technical artifacts or biological variation, we computed erosion scores using ChromHMM (8). Overlapping nuclei exhibited significantly higher erosion scores compared to well-separated clusters, suggesting a state of chromatin disorganization consistent with epigenome erosion. Excitatory and inhibitory neurons had the highest erosion scores among all major cell types, indicating that neuronal populations may be particularly susceptible to epigenomic instability in sEOAD. This analysis supported the presence of biologically meaningful erosion patterns in sEOAD and highlighted the need to account for chromatin integrity in interpreting single-nucleus data.

We further prioritized cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs in each brain region through integrated epigenomic and transcriptomic analysis. We found that cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs in microglia intersected with genetic risk loci identified in LOAD GWAS (17, 18, 20, 21). However, we did not find significant heritability enrichment in the sLDSC analysis (Fig. 6D). This lack of LOAD GWAS enrichment in sEOAD-linked cCREs is intriguing, suggesting that sEOAD may involve disease-specific regulatory mechanisms that are distinctive from LOAD. One possible explanation is that the regulatory changes driving sEOAD are not predominantly controlled by the common genetic risk variants identified in older AD cohorts. Many LOAD risk loci map to microglia-expressed genes and immune pathways (86), implying that LOAD is strongly influenced by chronic inflammation and immune dysfunction over decades. In contrast, sEOAD might be triggered by different pathways—for instance, neuronal or synaptic alterations—that do not heavily depend on those late-onset risk variants. In addition, because sEOAD occurs earlier, certain genetic risk factors may require aging-related processes to exert their effects; thus, variants conferring LOAD risk (e.g., TREM2) may play a lesser role in the faster-progressing sEOAD pathology.

Genetic variants associated with psychiatric disorders and traits, such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and neuroticism, were significantly enriched in both the cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs and cell type–specific cCREs in excitatory neurons. This suggested potential shared genetic risks between sEOAD and psychiatric conditions, particularly in excitatory and inhibitory neurons (4, 7). Epidemiological studies report that individuals with major psychiatric disorders have a higher likelihood of developing AD or other dementias later in life (87), and recent integrative analyses have revealed shared genetic risk factors and convergent pathways across these diseases (88). Our findings align with these observations—the enrichment of schizophrenia and bipolar disorder risk variants in excitatory/inhibitory neuron cCREs suggests that early-onset AD and some psychiatric disorders may perturb common neuronal regulatory circuits. Our integrated epigenomic and transcriptomic analysis successfully explored the shared and unique genetic components, and their potential functions associated with sEOAD.

Our study offers insights into cell type– and brain region–specific transcriptome dysregulation and potential regulatory disturbances in sEOAD. However, our study has limitations. First, our analysis was constrained by a small sample size due to the limited availability of postmortem brain samples in sEOAD. In a simulation-based power analysis, we found that statistical power was estimated low for identifying DEGs in low abundant cell types (data S4). This limitation was further compounded by cell-type recovery biases during sample processing and stringent quality control. Excitatory and inhibitory neurons exhibited lower TSS enrichment scores in the snATAC-seq modality and were disproportionately filtered out, leading to higher apparent proportions of glial cells in certain samples (Fig. 1E). Four samples displayed elevated oligodendrocyte content, likely due to reduced neuronal retention after QC. While these samples passed all quality thresholds and were retained to preserve statistical validity, future studies with larger cohorts will be critical to improve resolution of region- and cell type–specific regulatory programs in sEOAD. In addition, given the higher prevalence of AD in women and emerging evidence of sex-specific molecular mechanisms, future studies with sex-balanced sampling will be necessary to enable robust stratified analyses. Although sex was included as a covariate in our models to control for confounding, our current dataset is underpowered to assess sex-dependent regulatory effects. Second, to validate the candidate TFs and cCREs identified in specific cell types, future laboratory experiments are essential. Third, although all sEOAD cases showed confirmed AD pathology, region-specific quantification of amyloid-β and tau burden was unavailable, limiting direct correlation with molecular changes across regions. However, available data suggest widespread pathology at the late stages invested here. Fourth, our study did not comprehensively assess the regulatory mechanisms involving the complex interactions of multiple regulators on target genes. Last, there lacked the access to sEOAD GWAS datasets for combined association with our scMultiome features, because they are not publicly available yet. We will integrate such analysis when sEOAD GWAS datasets are released to the public.

In conclusion, our study provides insights into transcriptome alterations and identifies key gene regulators, such as RFX4 in astrocytes and IKZF1 in microglia, that are crucial in the pathophysiology of sEOAD across three brain regions commonly affected in sEOAD. The intercellular communication analysis revealed the altered roles of nonneuronal cell types in synaptic plasticity, neuroinflammation, and microgliogenesis in sEOAD. We delineated distinct genetic architectures within cell type–specific cCREs. These discoveries set the stage for future investigations into the unique mechanisms of sEOAD pathophysiology and for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Human postmortem brain sample collection

Postmortem brain samples of nine individuals were obtained from the NIH NeuroBioBank from the University of Miami and McGovern Medical School at UTHealth (data S1) in compliance with UTHealth Houston’s Institutional Review Board. sEOAD cases from the McGovern Medical School were defined as patients diagnosed with sEOAD before the age of 65 years and confirmed neuropathological diagnosis postmortem. Brain samples of sEOAD from the NIH NeuroBioBank were selected based on the neuropathological diagnosis of sEOAD with onset before age 65 years. The genotypes of the samples from the NIH NeuroBioBank were examined based on paired whole-genome sequencing data publicly available on the National Institute of Mental Health Data Archive (https://nda.nih.gov/edit_collection.html?id=3917). Patients with known autosomal dominant mutations in APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2 were excluded from the analyses. Control samples were selected from individuals with no documented neuropsychiatric or neurological diagnoses; however, additional clinical data, including information on other comorbidities, were not available from the NIH NeuroBioBank. The sEOAD cases from McGovern Medical School were the patients diagnosed with sEOAD before the age of 65 years and confirmed neuropathological diagnosis postmortem. All tissues were deidentified under the privacy rules of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996. Human brain samples from UTHealth were deidentified and collected with permission from the families. Samples were obtained through the NIH NeuroBioBank at the University of Miami under the University of Miami Institutional Review Board (IRB protocol number: 19920358; CR0001775, Title: Brain Endowment Bank).

Tissue preparation and nuclei isolation

The tissue preparation, dissociation, and nuclei extraction were performed at the UTHealth Cancer Genomic Core. Samples were stored at −80°C. The nuclei isolation protocol was modified based on a published protocol (89) and 10x Genomics protocol (CG000375). Briefly, 30 mg of frozen tissue was finely minced on dry ice and then homogenized in buffer on ice within 20 min. The homogenized tissue was filtered through a 35-μm cell strainer. Small cell debris was removed by centrifuging at 300g for 3 min at 4°C. The large cell debris was removed by gradient centrifugation using OptiPrep density gradient medium (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. D1556-250ML) at 7200g for 15 min at 4°C. The nuclei pellet was resuspended in a 0.1× lysis buffer. After washing the isolated nuclei in the wash buffer, the nuclei were resuspended in 1× nuclei buffer before single-nucleus multiome library construction.

Single-nucleus multiome sequencing

Single-nucleus libraries were constructed following the 10x Chromium Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression protocol (CG000338). Briefly, nuclei suspensions were incubated with a transposase, which fragmented the DNA in open regions of the chromatin and added the adapter sequences to the ends of the DNA fragments. The transposed nuclei were loaded onto Chromium Next GEM Chip J (PN-1000234, 10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA) with portioning oil and barcoded single-cell gel beads, followed by PCR amplification. The ATAC and the gene expression libraries were then prepared separately. Library qualities were assessed using the Agilent High Sensitive DNA Kit (no. 5067-4626) on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). Qualified libraries underwent paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq System (Illumina Inc., USA).

Immunofluorescence on FFPE tissue sections

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through graded ethanol to phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed in tris-EDTA buffer (10 mM tris, 1 mM EDTA, pH 9.0; Abcam, no. ab93684) at 95°C for 20 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. Sections were then blocked with 2.5% donkey serum in PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 for 1 hour at room temperature to reduce nonspecific binding. Primary antibodies for both anti Iba1 (Synaptic Systems, no. 234009) and anti HLA II DRB1 (Abcam, no. ab215835) were diluted in the same blocking buffer and incubated with the sections overnight at room temperature in a humidified chamber. After washing in PBS, sections were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with the following secondary antibodies: goat anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 594 (Abcam, no. ab150176) and donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A 21202), both diluted in 2.5% donkey serum in PBS with 0.2% Triton X-100. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, no. D9542) for 5 min at room temperature. Autofluorescence Eliminator Reagent (EMD Millipore 2160) was used to decrease the tissue autofluorescence, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Last, after rinsing, sections were mounted with an anti-fade medium and imaged using a Leica DMi8 epifluorescence microscope. Fluorophore signals were captured using appropriate filter sets for Alexa Fluor 488, Alexa Fluor 594, and DAPI, and representative images were collected for analysis.

Image analysis and fluorescence intensity quantification

Quantitative image analysis was performed using the open-source software CellProfiler (v4.2.8) to measure the expression of IBA1 and HLA-DRB1 in microglia. The analysis pipeline consisted of the following sequential steps: nucleus segmentation, microglia identification, object filtering, and intensity measurement. First, individual nuclei were identified from the DAPI-stained channel to serve as the primary objects for cell segmentation. The “IdentifyPrimaryObjects” module was used for this task. To account for potential variations in background illumination, an “Adaptive” thresholding strategy was applied, using the “Otsu” method to automatically separate nuclei (foreground) from the background. Each successfully segmented nucleus was defined as an individual object.

Using the identified nuclei as seeds, the corresponding cell bodies of microglia were delineated in the IBA1 channel. This was achieved with the “IdentifySecondaryObjects” module, which expanded from the border of each primary object (nucleus) outward to the edge of the IBA1 signal. The thresholding strategy was set to “Adaptive” with the “Sauvola” method. This step defined the complete area for each potential IBA1+ microglia cell. To ensure that only IBA1+ microglia were included in the final analysis, a filtering step was implemented. The “FilterObjects” module was used to select objects based on their mean IBA1 intensity. A threshold was set to retain only the objects with a minimum mean IBA1 intensity of 0.045, calculated within the cell area defined by the Sauvola method. This critical step effectively removed IBA1-negative cells and background artifacts from further analysis. In the final step, the mean fluorescence intensity of both IBA1 and HLA-DRB1 was quantified within the population of filtered, IBA1-positive (IBA1+) microglia. The “MeasureObjectIntensity” module was used to calculate the mean pixel intensity for both channels within each individual cell object. This yielded the relative expression levels of IBA1 and HLA-DRB1 on a per-cell basis for subsequent statistical analysis. Last, all statistical comparisons were performed using a two-tailed Mann-Whitney U test.

Joint single-nucleus multiome data processing and cell-type annotation

We used the Cell Ranger ARC (v2.0.2) pipeline for aligning the snRNA-seq and snATAC-seq reads from each sample to the GRCh38 genome and quantifying them with “cellranger-arc count.” The raw snRNA count matrix and snATAC fragment counts were further processed using a rigorous pipeline. First, we removed the systematic background noise in the raw snRNA count matrix using CellBender, which applies a deep learning model to accurately distinguish cell-containing droplets from cell-free droplets (24). Second, we applied initial filtration based on RNA-assay matrices (200 < nFeature_RNA < 7500) and ATAC-assay matrices (Nucleosome_signal <2.5, TSS.enrichment >2) using Seurat (v5.0.0) and Signac (v1.12.0) packages, respectively. The major cell-type annotations were predicted for each cell based on SCTransform normalized snRNA-seq reference mapping embedded in Seurat. Gene expression matrix obtained from RNA assays was aligned with existing SCTransform-normalized snRNA-seq datasets from the PFC and middle temporal gyrus. The resulting data were then labeled using cell-type classifications from the reference map. Potential doublets were removed using the DoubletFinder R package using default parameters (90). Then, the RNA counts were normalized using SCTransform with mitochondrial percent per cell regressed out. Principal components analysis (PCA) and UMAP visualization based on the first 30 principal components were performed on normalized RNA counts. In addition, we performed pseudo-bulk analysis on RNA assay to identify and remove outlier samples through PCA.

Predicted major cell-type annotations were determined for each cell based on SCTransform normalized snRNA-seq reference mapping embedded in Seurat. Gene expression (RNA assay) was mapped to SCTransformed normalized snRNA-seq datasets of the PFC and middle temporal gyrus (25, 26) and annotated with the cell types of the reference map. Cells with consistent cell-type predictions and predicted cell-type scores greater than 0.95 in both datasets were kept for downstream analysis. In addition, we leveraged scANVI from scvi-tools (v1.0.3) for high-resolution cellular subclasses annotation based on the reference snRNA-seq dataset (26, 91).

After predicting the major cell types based on RNA assay, we performed open chromatin peak calling on ATAC assay at pseudo-bulk level with MACS2 (41) using the CallPeaks function in Signac. Peaks that overlapped with genomic blacklist regions for the GRCh38 genome were removed, and peak counts were normalized using latent semantic indexing (LSI), including term-frequency inverse-document-frequency. Dimension reductions were performed with singular value decomposition of the normalized open chromatin accessibility matrix. The open chromatin accessibility UMAP visualization was created using the 2nd through the 50th LSI components.

To mitigate batch effects, we applied the Harmony (v1.0.0) (92) algorithm on both snRNA-seq and snATAC-seq data. Then, we created a weighted nearest neighbor (WNN) graph with the FindMultiModalNeighbors function of Seurat to represent a weighted combination of both modalities.

Cell-type proportional analysis by scCODA

We applied the single-cell compositional data analysis using scCODA (v0.1.9) (28), a Bayesian model, to detect the cell-type proportional shift across brain regions and conditions. The statistical test and the posterior inclusion probabilities (Pinc) for credible effects were calculated by including either region or sEOAD diagnosis labels as covariates at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Differential expression analysis

The DEGs between sEOAD and control samples (sEOAD DEGs) were determined for each major cell type across brain regions. VLMCs/pericytes and endothelial cells were excluded from the DEG analysis due to low cell counts. First, we conducted DEG analysis using the MAST framework for the genes presented in at least 25% nuclei in each of the two condition groups. This threshold balances sensitivity and reliability and has been commonly used in single-cell studies of AD (9, 25, 93). To evaluate the robustness of this filtering criterion, we also performed comparative DEG analyses using alternative thresholds: (i) genes expressed in ≥25% of nuclei in both groups, and (ii) genes expressed in ≥50% of all nuclei (regardless of condition). While more stringent filters resulted in fewer genes tested, the number and identity of significant DEGs remained largely consistent, indicating the stability of our findings. Gene expression data underwent log-normalization with a scale factor of 1 × 105. Age, sex, and batch were included in the MAST model as covariates. Second, we implemented a mixed-effect model, using the Libra R package (94), to account for the random effect of patient origin, which accommodates a zero-inflated negative binomial distribution. The proposed formula of the mixed-effect model isGene expression ∼ Diagnosis + Sex + Age at death+ Batch + Offsetlog(total counts)+(1∣donor)

Genes were considered sEOAD DEGs if they met a Bonferroni-adjusted P value threshold of <0.05 in both the MAST and the mixed-effect model, along with an absolute log2FC greater than 0.25 between sEOAD and control in specific cell types. Last, to validate the robustness of DEG findings across modeling strategies, we conducted a pseudo-bulk differential expression analysis using DESeq2. For each cell type, raw counts were aggregated by donor and diagnosis group, and DESeq2 was applied via wrapper function in Seurat package.

GO enrichment analysis

ClusterProfiler (v4.10.0) was used for gene set enrichment analysis to find potential GO BP terms for up- or down-regulated sEOAD DEGs in each cell type within each brain region (95). The background gene set for this analysis included all genes listed in the GO database. In addition, we used the simplify function to remove redundant enriched GO BP terms based on calculated similarity (similarity score > 0.8). The multiple testing correction was performed, and FDR <0.05 was applied.

Open chromatin peaks annotations

The open chromatin peaks identified in each major cell type were annotated using ChIPseeker (96) and TxDb.Hsapiens.UCSC.hg38.knownGene using default 3 kb upstream and 3 kb downstream of the TSS. To assess regulatory relevance, we compared our peaks against multiple external datasets. Specifically, we evaluated overlap with (i) cCREs identified in single nuclei from the adult human brain (4); (ii) the ENCODE Registry of cCREs across tissues (42); (iii) the SCREEN database aggregating ENCODE cCREs across diverse tissues and cell types (42); and (iv) the recently released psychSCREEN database, which provides brain cell type–specific cCREs from PsychENCODE, including distinct neuron- and glia-specific annotations (43). In addition, we intersected peak regions with brain cell type–specific cis-eQTLs (61) to identify potential regulatory variants linked to gene expression.

Peak-gene linkage analysis

We conducted peak-gene linkage analysis on sEOAD DEGs resulting from the previous step in each cell type using the LinkPeaks function in Signac (97), based on the approach initially described by SHARE-seq (45). The Spearman correlation coefficient between gene expression and peak accessibility was calculated. Open chromatin peaks within 5 × 105 bp from the gene TSS were included in the model. The GC content, overall accessibility, and peak size were considered in the model as covariates for correcting bias. BH-adjusted P values were calculated in each region. Only high-confidence positive peak-gene links with BH-adjusted P value <0.05 and correlation coefficients >0.05 were retained for downstream analyses.

Single-nucleus TF binding motif analysis

We retrieved a comprehensive list of TF binding motif position weight matrices covering 755 TFs from the JASPAR 2020 database (collection = “CORE”; species = “Homo sapiens”; access date: 9 March 2024). The DNA sequence motif information was added to the snATAC-seq assay using the AddMotifs function, and the motif activity matrix was calculated using chromVAR implemented in the Signac package (97).

To prioritize the TFs associated with sEOAD, we identified overrepresented motifs for each set of cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs in different cell types and brain regions. This involves using the FindMotifs function to conduct a hypergeometric test, evaluating the likelihood of the observed motif frequency occurring by chance relative to a GC content-matched background peak set. The background set was uniquely determined for each set of peaks linked to DEGs in each cell type. Enriched TFs were kept if the TF gene was expressed in more than 25% of the corresponding cell type.

In addition, we identified TFs with differential motif activity by performing differential testing on the chromVAR z-score between sEOAD and controls in each major cell type using the FindMarker function at Bonferroni-adjusted P value <0.05. Footprinting analysis of selected TFs was conducted, accounting for the Tn5 sequencing insertion bias.

Gene regulatory network inference

We applied the SCENIC+ workflow to construct key TF regulatory networks in specific cell types and conditions (49). Topic modeling of the snATAC-seq data was performed using pycisTopic to identify coaccessible and candidate enhancer regions. Motif enrichment analysis on these regions was conducted using cisTarget and DEM algorithms on cell type– and condition-specific differentially accessible regions. TF regulatory networks were then inferred by modeling region-to-gene and TF-to-gene relationships using a gradient-boosting regression approach. In total, 161,751, 443,615, and 144,891 region-to-gene links were identified in PFC, EC, and HIP, respectively. Of these, 73.51% (PFC), 68.46% (EC), and 72.00% (HIP) had positive correlations and retained. The networks were further filtered to exclude the extended eRegulons if a corresponding direct version was present. Ultimately, 57,719, 147,712, and 51,896 regulon entries were retained in PFC, EC, and HIP, respectively, for downstream analysis. TF-cistrome relationships (Rho) were calculated on the filtered networks, and only the top 5% most positively and bottom 5% most negatively correlated regulons were prioritized.

Differential cell-cell communication analysis

We used MultiNicheNet (v1.0.1, last updated on 20 August 2024) (55), a universal tool to infer the putative context-specific cell-cell communication signals mediated by ligand-receptor pairs across cell type and brain regions in sEOAD and control samples. MultiNicheNet ranks the importance of ligand-receptor pairs based on the “pseudo-bulk” transcriptome profile of each sample. Candidate ligands were extracted if they expressed in at least 25% of the sender cells within their respective clusters, and the genes were significantly differentially expressed between sEOAD and control samples with unadjusted P value threshold of 0.05, as recommended for pseudo-bulk single-cell datasets to mitigate loss of power. To prioritize highly variable intercellular signals in sEOAD, we highlighted the top 25 differential signals sent and received by selected cell types per experimental group. Furthermore, we applied the make_ligand_activity_target_plot function and calculated the regulatory potential scores between prioritized ligands and potential targeted genes within the receiver cell using default parameters.

Genetic variant selection and LD expansion

Index SNPs were collected from four large-scale LOAD GWAS (17, 18, 20, 21). LD expansion was performed using LDlinkR (98) to identify SNPs with LD R2 > 0.8 based on phase 3 of the 1000 Genomes European ancestry (99). We performed Fisher’s exact test to assess the enrichment of genetic variants associated with LOAD in cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs in each cell type across brain regions with a Holm-Bonferroni–adjusted P value <0.05 as the cutoff. The background cCREs set was defined as open chromatin peaks identified in each cell type but not linked to sEOAD DEGs.

GWAS heritability enrichment

The GWAS heritability enrichment analysis was conducted using stratified LD score regression (sLDSC v1.0.1) (63). sLDSC calculated the proportion of GWAS heritability attributable to SNPs within a specific genomic feature through a regression model. We obtained summary statistics from four large-scale LOAD GWAS (17, 18, 20, 21). Additional GWAS summary statistics data were downloaded for 12 immune-mediated and 9 psychiatric disorders (64–81) (data S7).

We performed sLDSC analysis in two scenarios. First, the sLDSC analysis was conducted on cCREs linked to sEOAD DEGs in each cell type in each region (Spearman correlation >0.05; BH-adjusted P values <0.05). Second, we applied sLDSC on cell type–specific cCREs. The cell type–specific cCREs were identified using the FindAllMarkers function from the Seurat package. This function used a logistic regression model and adjusted for the library size. We prioritized the peaks exhibiting a Bonferroni-adjusted P value <0.01 and absolute log2FC ≥1 in each cell type as the cell type–specific cCREs.

Power analysis of DEGs identification

To estimate the power of our dataset in detecting alterations in gene expression, we performed a simulation-based power analysis using the R package Hierarchicell (100). This algorithm takes multiple factors into consideration, including gene dropout rates, intraindividual dispersion, interindividual variation, variable or fixed number of cells per individual, and the correlation between cells within an individual. We used compute_data_summaries and power_hierarchicell functions to simulate and estimate the power with the following parameters: the number of samples in each condition (n = 5, PFC; n = 3, EC; n = 3, HIP), the minimal fold change between conditions (rho = 1.5 or 2, two parameters), the P value cutoff (P = 0.05), the mean of the number of cells in each sample of each condition, and the number of genes (n = 3000).
