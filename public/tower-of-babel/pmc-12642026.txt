The brain washing system in sepsis-associated encephalopathy
Authors: Tao Chen, Jiyun Hu, Ya Liao, Shucai Xie, Lina Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE) represents a prevalent and serious complication in patients with sepsis, contributing to substantial cognitive dysfunction and persistent neurological deficits. The cerebral clearance mechanism, consisting of the glymphatic system and meningeal lymphatic vessels (MLVs), serves a pivotal function in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage and the elimination of metabolic byproducts. The study demonstrated that intraperitoneal administration of LPS (3 mg/kg) resulted in a reduction in Aβ clearance 28 h post-injection. Moreover, acute exposure to a lower dose of LPS (1 mg/kg) led to a decreased distribution of CSF around the middle cerebral artery as early as 3 h. These concurrent observations imply that compromised functionality of this clearance mechanism may contribute to cognitive impairments induced by sepsis. This review comprehensively investigates the involvement of the cerebral clearance mechanism in the pathophysiological processes of SAE, with the objective of uncovering innovative therapeutic approaches.

Introduction

Sepsis, characterized as a life-threatening condition resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection that leads to organ dysfunction, continues to be a predominant cause of mortality in intensive care settings [1]. Recent epidemiological investigations into the global impact of sepsis revealed approximately 489 million cases worldwide, with sepsis-related deaths reaching 110 million, representing 19.7% of total global mortality [2]. In this clinical framework, sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE), manifested by acute consciousness disturbances, cognitive deficits, and behavioral alterations, affects 20–50% of septic patients and significantly worsens clinical outcomes [3, 4].

The precise pathophysiology of SAE remains incompletely elucidated. Emerging evidence indicates that sustained neuroinflammatory activation and cerebral accumulation of neurotoxic metabolites may constitute pivotal mechanisms underlying long-term cognitive impairment following sepsis. The inflammatory cascade in SAE is well-documented. During the progression of sepsis, endothelial cell activation and increased blood–brain barrier permeability collectively induce pathological microglia activation, which secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [5]. These inflammatory mediators subsequently drive the activation of A1-type astrocytes, ultimately contributing to neuronal death through neurotoxic effects. Notably, these neuroinflammatory responses persist beyond the acute phase of sepsis, resulting in long-term cognitive dysfunction [5, 6].

Parallel evidence highlights the critical role of the brain's waste clearance system, the glymphatic system, in neurological health. First proposed in 2012, the glymphatic system, constitutes a critical pathway for the clearance of neurotoxic metabolites, such as Aβ and tau protein aggregates, as well as cellular debris from the brain [7]. Its function relies on astrocytic aquaporin-4 (AQP4) and perivascular space (PVS) structure, with physiological parameters modulating CSF drainage efficiency [7–12]. In animal models, impairment of the glymphatic system through genetic editing or pharmacological interventions leads to reduced clearance rates of Aβ [13, 14]. In human studies, glymphatic function has also been shown to correlate with Aβ burden in Alzheimer's disease (AD) patients. Furthermore, research suggests that glymphatic failure may precede the appearance of amyloid pathology in the course of AD and could serve as a predictor for amyloid deposition, neurodegeneration, and clinical progression [15, 16]. Collectively, these findings indicate that glymphatic dysfunction may represent a significant risk factor in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disorders.

Recent work bridges these two pathways, suggesting that glymphatic impairment may be a critical link between sepsis and long-term neurodegeneration risk. Systemic inflammation directly compromises glymphatic function. For instance, repeated systemic exposure to LPS reduces bulk flow of CSF and impairs the clearance of Aβ, with these effects emerging as early as 28 h post-exposure [17]. This provides a potential explanation for the observed increase in Aβ deposits and phosphorylated tau in sepsis survivors and SAE patients [18–20].Moreover, the glymphatic system is now recognized as immunologically active, regulating neuroinflammation by mediating the exchange of inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β and TNF-α between CSF and ISF [21–23]. Dysfunctional glymphatic activity may trigger pathological activation of microglia and astrocytes, thereby disrupting central nervous system immune homeostasis [24–29]. Notably, recent studies suggest that in SAE, hyperactivated neuroinflammation and the accumulation of cerebral metabolic waste may act synergistically to exacerbate neurological dysfunction—a process potentially mediated by glymphatic system impairment [30].

Although meningeal lymphatic vessels (MLVs) are not anatomically integrated into the glymphatic system, emerging evidence highlights their collaborative role in regulating cerebrospinal fluid-interstitial fluid (CSF-ISF) exchange and central immune homeostasis, thereby maintaining cerebral microenvironmental stability [31–33]. In SAE, MLVs dysfunction not only compromises metabolic waste clearance but also exacerbates neuroinflammation through aberrant immune cell activation, ultimately driving cognitive impairment [34, 35]. This functional interplay between MLVs and the glymphatic system underscores the potential of targeting their interface as a promising therapeutic strategy for SAE.

This review systematically delineates the dynamic remodeling characteristics and pathological consequences of the glymphatic system and MLVs in SAE. It further clarifies their respective roles in neuroinflammatory regulation and metabolic clearance under septic conditions, proposing the novel concept that brain washing system dysfunction may constitute a previously unrecognized pathomechanistic framework in SAE.

Clearance pathways in the brain

The concept and evolution of the brain washing system

The lymphatic system plays a pivotal role in maintaining organismal homeostasis. While CSF has been traditionally regarded as the metabolic waste clearance pathway, its slow diffusion rate and inability to penetrate brain parenchyma fundamentally conflict with the nervous system’s high metabolic demands. Additionally, the mechanisms by which CSF and its metabolic waste cargo are effectively drained from the brain parenchyma remained a persistent knowledge gap [36]. Notably, the anatomical features of MLVs within the human dura mater were first systematically documented in the eighteenth century by Giovanni Paolo Mascagni, an Italian anatomist at the University of Siena [37]. However, this landmark discovery remained underappreciated by the scientific community for over two subsequent centuries. Against this historical backdrop, the prevailing scientific paradigm steadfastly adhered to the doctrine that the brain functioned as an immune-privileged organ devoid of conventional lymphatic drainage, a conceptual framework that has only recently been revolutionized by groundbreaking research [38].

In 2012, lliff et al. utilized in vivo two-photon microscopy and ex vivo fluorescence tracing to delineate a specialized CSF transport network in murine brains. This system leverages PVS, which are formed between astrocytic endfeet and vascular basement membranes, as conduits for fluid movement through the brain parenchyma. The polarized localization of AQP4 on astrocytes facilitates CSF—ISF exchange, thereby enabling the efficient removal of metabolic byproducts such as Aβ [7]. Concurrently, Maiken’s team employed non-invasive dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI to provide critical in vivo evidence for the glymphatic system in murine models [39].

In 2015, Louveau et al. discovered functional lymphatic vasculature within murine meninges, which specifically expressed LYVE-1-positive lymphatic endothelial markers. These vessels were found to establish direct anatomical connections with deep cervical lymph nodes, demonstrating their ability to facilitate the directional drainage of CSF and immune cells toward peripheral lymphoid organs [38]. Subsequently, Absinta et al. further validated these findings using gadolinium-contrasted MRI, confirming the presence of functional MLVs in both human and non-human primates. Their research revealed anatomical features closely resembling those of rodent MLVs and provided critical neuroimaging evidence supporting the existence of anatomically intact lymphatic drainage pathways within the CNS [40].

Since then, the brain washing system has been established, which is fundamentally composed of glymphatic system and MLVs. Subsequent studies have identified glymphatic and MLVs dysfunction in both neurodegenerative disorders (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease) and acute neurological injuries (e.g., traumatic brain injury, stroke, epilepsy), highlighting impaired metabolic clearance as a shared pathological mechanism [23, 41–45].

However, several key aspects remain poorly understood and subject to debate. For instance, in 2017, Verkman's team questioned the importance of AQP4 in the glymphatic system. Nevertheless, subsequent multiple studies have consistently demonstrated that AQP4 deficiency leads to impaired glymphatic function [46]. The negative results reported by Verkman's group may be attributed to the use of avertin anesthesia, which has been reported to suppress CSF flow, thereby potentially diminishing phenotypic differences between wild-type and AQP4-deficient groups and introducing bias into their findings [47]. Furthermore, the precise mechanism by which CSF mixes with ISF remains unknown. What’s more, interspecies divergence warrants attention: while murine models demonstrate well-structured meningeal lymphatic networks, humans exhibit markedly lower lymphatic vessel density, suggesting potential biological limitations in human CSF drainage efficiency [40].

The structure of brain washing system

The structure of the glymphatic system

The glymphatic system represents a sophisticated CSF transport network organized through three principal pathways: para-arterial influx routes, paravenous clearance conduits, and astrocytic AQP4-mediated transparenchymal exchange [7, 24, 48, 49].

The PVS represents a distinctive anatomical feature within the brain, characterized by the potential cavities located between the end-feet of astrocytes and the outer membranes of cerebral blood vessels [50]. These spaces are systematically arranged along the vascular architecture of cerebral arteries and veins, extending down to the capillary level, thereby forming a sophisticated network system [51]. Under physiological conditions, the PVS are filled with fluid, acting as channels for fluid transport, facilitating the exchange between CSF and ISF, and playing an essential role in the clearance of waste products from the brain [52]. Concurrently, as the part of Brain-border immune niches, the PVS plays a crucial role in modulating the migration of immune cells and the inflammatory responses occurring in the brain [51, 53].

AQP4, the predominant water channel in the CNS, exhibits polarized expression on astrocyte endfeet membranes [54]. Its bidirectional water permeability is essential for maintaining cerebral water homeostasis. Structural studies reveal that AQP4 splice variants M1 and M23 assemble into heterotetrameric orthogonal array particles (OAPs) through stoichiometrically regulated oligomerization. Low M23/M1 ratio creates small OAPs (~ 50 nm) enhancing membrane flexibility for cell migration. High M23/M1 ratio forms large OAPs (> 100 nm) stabilizing AQP4 at endfeet membranes [55]. This dynamic process requires coordination with anchoring proteins, such as α-syntrophin [56, 57].

CSF, primarily produced by the choroid plexus, enters the subarachnoid space and subsequently flows into the deep brain parenchyma along the PVS of penetrating arteries. Driven by arterial pulsations, CSF passes through AQP4 channels on astrocytic endfeet to enter the parenchyma and advance toward perivenous spaces. During this process, CSF mixes with ISF and carries a variety of solutes, including metabolic waste products [7].

Initially, the Nedergaard’s team proposed that CSF-ISF mixture primarily reached the perivenous spaces via directional convective flow [12]. However, recent magnetic resonance imaging by Jiang et al. demonstrated that waste clearance in the parenchyma relies on both convective and diffusive mechanisms [58]. Mathematical modeling has further confirmed the contribution of diffusion to solute clearance [59]. Thus, it is now well accepted that both convection and diffusion collectively facilitate the transport of the CSF–ISF mixture to the perivenous spaces. Moreover, Drieu and colleagues revealed that macromolecules can translocate from para-arterial to para-venous spaces through overlapping regions between arterial and venous perivascular spaces along the surface of leptomeninges, providing new insights into the efficient clearance of macromolecules [60]. The structure of the MLVs

The murine calvaria harbors a meticulously organized network of MLVs that exhibit pronounced dorsoventral differentiation in both anatomical architecture and functional properties [38, 61]. Dorsal lymphatic vessels are characterized by narrower luminal diameters, an immature phenotype marked by continuous tight junctions, the absence of functional valves, and a relatively sparse distribution pattern. In contrast, basal lymphatic vessels possess significantly larger luminal diameters, express mature molecular markers, exhibit functional valve structures, and display a higher distribution density [64]. These lymphatic vessels maintain close anatomical proximity to the dural vasculature, collectively forming a highly integrated neurovascular-lymphatic unit. Functional lymphatic clusters observed at key anatomical sites, including the occipital foramen magnum region, cribriform plate, and nasopharyngeal lymphatic plexus, play essential roles in maintaining CNS homeostasis by regulating CSF-ISF equilibrium, mediating immune cell trafficking, clearing metabolic waste, and modulating neuroinflammatory responses [65, 66].

Essential functions of brain washing system

The brain washing system is predominantly composed of the glymphatic system and MLVs, both of which fulfill vital physiological functions within the CNS. Growing evidence underscores their functional interdependence, which has led to the adoption of the unified term “glymphatic-meningeal lymphatic complex system.” These two systems are structurally and functionally interconnected, working synergistically through CSF-ISF exchange and lymphatic drainage to sustain brain homeostasis.

Anatomically, CSF circulates through a continuous pathway: mixing with ISF, traversing perivenous spaces to the dura mater, and ultimately draining into cervical lymph nodes via MLVs [48, 67]. Functionally, their coordinated operation regulates metabolic waste clearance efficiency. Animal studies demonstrate that structural integrity of MLVs directly determines glymphatic performance—lymphatic dysfunction not only reduces CSF drainage efficacy but also exacerbates Aβ deposition [68]. Clinically, synchronized dysfunction of this complex system manifests in aging and neurodegenerative disease models. Thus, elucidating their interactive relationship under pathological conditions represents a crucial direction for future research [31, 32, 69].

Waste removal

The glymphatic system eliminates neurotoxic substances such as Aβ through CSF-ISF exchange. This exchange process is primarily driven by the polarized expression of AQP4 water channels located on astrocytic endfeet, which ensures efficient CSF-ISF circulation [41, 70–72]. Importantly, aging, well as pharmacological or genetic manipulations targeting AQP4 expression or polarization, profoundly affect clearance efficiency. Dysfunction of this system aggravates the pathological buildup of Aβ, underscoring its vital role in preserving brain health [27, 57, 73–75].

Serving as essential drainage conduits for glymphatic system, MLVs contribute to solute clearance via anatomically unique endothelial channels that actively transport neurotoxic metabolites. Research by Mesquita et al. revealed a connection between MLVs dysfunction and AD progression. Notably, aged AD mice showed shrunken lymphatic vessels accompanied by reduced network density, correlating with increased amyloid plaques in the brain [68]. Meanwhile, Patel et al. specifically knocked out vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 3 (VEGFR3) to impair the development of MLVs. In this model, recombinant tau protein injected into the mouse hippocampus exhibited significantly higher brain retention levels at 48 and 72 h, alongside a markedly delayed clearance to the periphery [76].

Immune surveillance and regulation

The glymphatic system is structurally located at the neurovascular interface, not only forming structural coupling with the BBB but also functionally integrating with MLVs. Importantly, meningeal lymphatics are mainly found in the dura mater, a region abundantly populated with diverse immune cells. This unique anatomical distribution challenges the traditional concept of the CNS as an “immune-privileged” organ. Instead, recent findings suggests that this network serves as a critical immunoregulatory hub, maintaining neuroinflammatory homeostasis through resident immune cells and CNS-derived antigen presentation [77–79].

Recent findings suggest the existence of CD45 + positive cells, comprising macrophages, microglia, and antigen-present cells displaying macrophage-like features, within the PVS [78–80]. Importantly, CSF flow dynamically facilitates lymphocyte trafficking into the PVS [81]. Louveau et al. demonstrated meningeal lymphatic-mediated T cell trafficking to deep cervical lymph nodes (dCLNs) through adoptive transfer of memory T cells into CSF, with this process being strictly CCR7-dependent [21, 82]. Subsequent experiments further confirmed that activated dendritic cells (DCs) injected into the CSF first collect in lymphatic structures near the transverse sinus before traveling in a directional manner to dCLNs [21, 38]. Additionally, B cells and neutrophils undergo directional drainage through MLVs, highlighting a crucial pathway for CNS immune surveillance [44, 83].

Immune cells travel via the lymphatic drainage system to cervical lymph nodes, where they communicate with the peripheral immune system to modulate neuroinflammation. Surgical blockade of meningeal lymph-cervical node connections or pharmacological MLVs ablation can regulate T-cell retention in brain parenchyma and influence cognitive function [82]. Intriguingly, the CCR7-CCL21 signaling axis facilitated Th17 cell-mediated clearance through MLVs, mitigating brain injury in subarachnoid hemorrhage [84]. Conversely, functional suppression of MLVs in multiple sclerosis (MS) models reduced pathogenic T-cell CNS infiltration and mitigated neuroinflammation [21]. These findings suggest dual roles of the lymphatic drainage system in diseases, with context-dependent effects varying by pathology type and disease phase.

In recent years, the skull has emerged as a key player in CNS immune regulation. A growing body of evidence highlights a bidirectional crosstalk between the dural MLVs and the skull bone marrow, which collectively maintain brain immune homeostasis [85–87]. The structural basis for this communication involves specialized ossified channels connecting the skull bone marrow and the dura mater. This was identified in mice using electron microscopy and cell tracking techniques by Nahrendorf and colleagues, who observed neutrophils migrating through these channels into the dural space—a process enhanced in ischemic stroke. Importantly, similar channels have been confirmed in human skull specimens [87]. Meanwhile, it has been demonstrated that antigens from the brain can also access the skull bone marrow through PVS of dural vessels. Pulous et al. showed that fluorescent tracers from the cisterna magna can travel along PVS of dural vessels and enter the skull marrow. Crucially, during bacterial meningitis, pathogens were found to co-opt this route to invade the skull bone marrow, thereby initiating the migration of skull-derived leukocytes into the meninges and modulating cerebral inflammation [88].

Beyond its role in adaptive immunity, lymphatic drainage plays a pivotal role in modulating glial activation and innate immune responses within the brain. Experimental findings reveal that targeted modulation of AQP4 to enhance glymphatic function effectively suppresses microglial activation and astrocytic hyperplasia [89, 90]. Furthermore, Mesquita et al. uncovered that the ablation of MLVs in AD mouse models triggers microglial activation (characterized by upregulated cytokine and leukocyte chemotaxis genes) and amplifies endothelial interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and chemokine expression, while concurrently downregulating antigen presentation genes [91].

Additional functions

Firstly, researches have demonstrated that sleep-associated synchronized neuronal activity generates rhythmic ionic fluctuations which enhance CSF perfusion. This potentiated glymphatic clearance correlates with dynamic lactate reduction during wake-sleep transition [92, 93]. Then, cerebral homeostasis depends not only on efficient waste clearance but also on dynamic nutrient distribution and precise ionic balance regulation. The glymphatic system plays a central role through CSF-ISF exchange, where pulsatile flow drives directional transport of metabolic substrates. Drug delivery strategies leveraging this system have demonstrated therapeutic potential [94, 95]. Additionally, the controlled turnover of glial cells is essential for maintaining brain homeostasis. For instance, senescent astrocytes employ membrane-bound VEGF-C to activate VEGFR3 signaling, enabling their CCL21 gradient-guided migration via MLVs to cervical lymph nodes for clearance [96].

From mice to humans: the translational gap in brain washing system research

While the brain's clearance systems have been well-characterized in animal models, technological limitations continue to impede a comprehensive understanding of their human counterparts and clinical translation.

Given its greater volume and complexity compared to the mouse brain, the human brain requires longer metabolic waste clearance pathways, imposing a greater burden on its clearance systems and resulting in more complex glymphatic architecture and drainage patterns. Studies using magnetic resonance contrast tracer techniques have revealed that tracers in the human glymphatic system distribute in a centripetal pattern, progressing from the cortical surface toward subcortical areas [97]. However, due to limitations in spatial resolution, whether these tracers precisely enter the deep brain regions along the periarterial PVS remains uncertain. To date, only one neuropathological report has definitively confirmed that CSF enters the brain parenchyma via the PVS [98]. Driven by diffusion and convection, the cerebrospinal-interstitial fluid mixture travels along the periarterial spaces, through the interstitium, to the perivenous spaces. In humans, tracer studies demonstrate that whole-brain distribution can occur within hours. The efficient dispersal across such a large volume suggests that additional driving forces may contribute to glymphatic drainage in the human brain [99].

Significant differences exist between the meningeal lymphatic systems of humans and mice. Quesada et al. demonstrated that while murine lymphatic vessels are predominantly distributed near the dural venous sinuses, humans possess an extensive network throughout the dura mater, including frontal and temporal regions remote from major sinuses [100]. Furthermore, cribriform plate drainage to the nasal mucosa is crucial in rodents, its functional significance appears limited in humans [101].

Collectively, these distinctions highlight the critical need for direct investigation into the human brain's clearance mechanisms.

The glymphatic system plays a role in the pathogenesis and advancement of SAE

Glymphatic dysfunction as a potential underlying mechanism in SAE

Although glymphatic dysfunction is associated with various neurodegenerative diseases, its role in acute inflammation, particularly sepsis, remains unclear. Nonetheless, indirect evidence suggests a link between glymphatic impairment and sepsis-associated cognitive alterations.

A study conducted by Banks et al. demonstrated that 28 h after exposure to LPS (3 mg/kg), the efflux of intracisternally administered inulin was slowed, indicating impaired CSF outflow. This was accompanied by a reduction in Aβ clearance [17]. Further research by Manouchehrian et al. revealed that exposure to LPS (1 mg/kg) led to a reduction in CSF distribution around the middle cerebral artery within 3 h. Notably, this effect occurred alongside changes in microglial morphology and heart rate, but without affecting respiration, cerebral blood flow, astrocytes, AQP4 expression and polarization, or blood–brain barrier integrity [102]. Notably, neither study established a causal link between the impaired CSF dynamics and the alteration of cognitive function in the context of acute systemic inflammation.

A recent study elucidates the dynamic process by which systemic inflammation affects the glymphatic system. Following LPS administration (5 mg/kg), CSF influx was initially suppressed at 1 h, reversed to an increase by 3 h, and normalized by 72 h. Concurrently, glymphatic clearance was suppressed at 24 h. It is noteworthy that BBB disruption was not observed alongside these changes. Furthermore, the study linked LPS-induced cognitive dysfunction to this glymphatic impairment and demonstrated that levosimendan improved cognitive performance in mice [103].

However, certain limitations exist in these studies. They predominantly utilized low-dose LPS to establish models and consistently reported no disruption of the BBB, which appears to contradict the prevailing notion that BBB dysfunction is a key mechanism in SAE [102–104]. As the CLP model may more closely reflect clinical sepsis, it could provide more direct insights. Interestingly, our unpublished data demonstrating reduced CSF influx at 24 h in the CLP model contrast with findings from LPS models, highlighting that different models may yield divergent findings and calling for future work to resolve these discrepancies. Overall, these initial studies lay a crucial foundation for investigating the relationship between sepsis-associated encephalopathy and the glymphatic system.

Owing to technical and ethical constraints, the function of the human glymphatic system is primarily assessed using neuroimaging. The DTI-ALPS index is a widely used, non-invasive method for evaluating glymphatic function. Its principle involves measuring the directionality of water diffusion within the PVS to indirectly reflect the efficiency of CSF-ISF exchange [105, 106]. However, the specificity and accuracy of the ALPS index remain debated. For instance, postmortem studies have shown only a weak correlation between the ALPS index and Aβ deposition in AD, suggesting that it may not directly represent glymphatic clearance [107]. Future studies should integrate parameters such as perivascular dynamics and microstructural integrity to calibrate the DTI-ALPS index, thereby enhancing its representativeness. Despite these controversies, the DTI-ALPS index remains a valuable tool for assessing human glymphatic function in vivo, owing to its non-invasive nature and operational simplicity.

In a longitudinal follow-up study of patients recovering from COVID-19, He et al. demonstrated that the DTI-ALPS index progressively increased within 1 to 3 months post-recovery, peaking at the third month. Correlation analysis revealed that the DTI-ALPS index in both cerebral hemispheres was significantly negatively correlated with MoCA scores. This suggests that systemic inflammation may exert a prolonged impact on the glymphatic system, potentially contributing to underlying cognitive impairment [108]. However, current researches face constraints including limited sample representativeness, population heterogeneity (particularly in elderly cohorts with hypertension comorbidity).

Association between perivascular space remodeling and glymphatic dysfunction in SAE

PVS constitutes an essential anatomical component of the glymphatic system, and its morphological status serves as a key indicator of the system's functional integrity. On magnetic resonance imaging, PVS enlargement is regarded as a radiological marker of impaired perivascular pathway function; preclinical studies suggest that this phenomenon may stem from obstructed CSF circulation and diminished waste clearance efficiency [50].

In cerebral small vessel disease, amyloid deposition induces vascular wall pathology, resulting in enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS), which have been integrated into neuroimaging evaluation criteria [109, 110]. For neurodegenerative disorders such as AD, glymphatic system dysfunction impairs abnormal protein clearance capacity, while PVS dilatation may serve as an early imaging biomarker of disease progression [15, 111].

However, it is important to emphasize that PVS primarily offers morphological insights and thus only partially reflects glymphatic function. The efficient operation of this system depends not only on the structural integrity of the PVS but also on the dynamic regulation of functional elements such as AQP4 polarity, arterial pulsation, and neuronal activity [93, 112]. In summary, while PVS morphology provides a valuable window into the glymphatic system, it does not represent the complete picture. Future research should focus on developing novel imaging biomarkers capable of directly quantifying intracranial fluid dynamics and clearance efficiency.

Morphological alterations of PVS in SAE

Dilation of PVS serves as a critical biomarker of glymphatic dysfunction, a phenomenon previously validated in cerebral small vessel disease [113, 114]. However, clinical evidence linking PVS alterations to septic encephalopathy remains limited, with current support restricted to isolated autopsy and neuroimaging findings. For instance, postmortem analysis of a COVID-19-associated septic shock case revealed basal ganglia lacunar infarcts accompanied by prominent PVS dilation and perivascular lymphocytic infiltration [115].

Investigations employing animal models of SAE have demonstrated a notable paucity of research specifically focusing on PVS. LPS-induced neuroinflammation triggers EPVS through ultrastructural BBB disruption mediated by perivascular microglial aggregation and astrocytic detachment [116]. A recent study has shown that 24 h after intraperitoneal injection of 5 mg/kg LPS, the influx of CSF into the PVS was significantly increased, while its efflux to the dCLNs was markedly delayed and slowed. This kinetic imbalance between CSF inflow and outflow may lead to the expansion of PVS [103].

Nevertheless, these findings are primarily derived from preclinical models, underscoring the critical need for further clinical and translational studies.

Dynamic remodeling of PVS in SAE

Acute phase of SAE

CSF circulation is jointly regulated by the respiratory and cardiac cycles [12, 117]. Using real-time magnetic resonance imaging, researchers have confirmed that a single forced respiration significantly enhances CSF flow within the cerebral ventricles, while breath-holding suppresses it. The underlying mechanism may involve a cascade of reactions that ultimately alter the perivascular spaces around cerebral veins, thereby regulating CSF outflow [118]. Interestingly, Goodman et al. found that hypercapnia and acidemic may suppress the efflux function of the glymphatic system, and this effect can be reversed by mechanical ventilation [119]. This suggests that chronic respiratory disturbances may persistently alter glymphatic system function.

Arterial pulsations generated by cardiac contractions have also been demonstrated to be a key factor driving bulk fluid flow within the PVS of the mouse brain [120, 121]. Studies have shown that the frequency of cerebral vascular pulsations significantly influences the flow velocity of CSF [120]. Furthermore, adequate cardiac output is crucial for maintaining cerebral perfusion. In an LPS-model mouse study, 24 h after LPS injection, cerebral blood perfusion significantly decreased, accompanied by impaired glymphatic clearance function. When levosimendan was administered to improve cerebral perfusion, the clearance function was subsequently restored [103].

In patients with sepsis, persistent sympathetic overactivation triggers systemic physiological disturbances, characterized by a synergistic deterioration of respiratory rhythm abnormalities and cardiovascular dysfunction [122–124]. Respiratory failure not only induces abnormal oscillations in intrathoracic pressure but also leads to hypercapnia and acidosis [125]. Furthermore, concomitant hypoxia and hyperkalemia can disrupt cerebrovascular autoregulation. Meanwhile, concurrent cardiac arrhythmias diminish cerebral perfusion by reducing cardiac output. A growing body of evidence underscores blood–brain barrier dysfunction as a critical pathological feature of sepsis-associated encephalopathy. Postmortem studies have revealed that reduced expression of tight junction proteins in patients who died from sepsis correlates with more severe disease manifestations and heightened inflammatory responses [126]. Mechanistically, peripheral inflammatory mediators crossing the BBB activate glial cells, triggering a dysregulated release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This cascade stimulates local synthesis of complement proteins, cytokines, and chemokines within PVS, ultimately damaging cerebral microvascular endothelial cells and pathologically increasing BBB permeability [104, 127].

The role of the BBB in the clearance of metabolic waste from the brain cannot be overlooked. Aβ enters and exits the brain with the help of transport proteins located at the BBB [128, 129]. After intraperitoneal injection of LPS, the protein level of low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP-1) on the BBB in mice is downregulated. In vitro, when LPS is applied to cultured brain vascular endothelial cells, LRP-1 exhibits improper localization [77].

Within this established framework of BBB impairment, we hypothesize that pathological enlargement of PVS may represent a potential consequence. We propose that excessive accumulation of protein-rich fluid resulting from increased BBB permeability could drive PVS dilation. This proposed mechanism for PVS enlargement represents a speculative pathway that requires future experimental validation in the context of sepsis(Fig. 2).

Sepsis-induced immune dysregulation entails hyperactivation of platelets and endothelial cells, disrupting the equilibrium between procoagulant and anticoagulant pathways, which progressively progresses to disseminated intravascular coagulation [130, 131]. The resulting microthrombi can obstruct cerebral small vessels, leading to significantly reduced cerebral blood flow perfusion and concomitant attenuation of arterial pulsations due to vascular occlusion. Recent research has demonstrated that arterial patency is a prerequisite for effective CSF drainage. Intravenous administration of tissue-type plasminogen activator(tPA) has been shown to restore glymphatic function after stroke, offering a thrombolytic strategy for rehabilitating CSF circulation [132]. Notably, the study also revealed that intraventricular administration of tPA ameliorated glymphatic dysfunction following subarachnoid hemorrhage, suggesting that thrombolytic therapy may possess the potential to restore CSF circulation by clearing obstructive materials, such as fibrin deposits, from the PVS [132] (Fig. 2).

Serving as a cerebral immune niche [51, 114, 133, 134], PVS harbor resident CD45 + immune populations including macrophages, microglia, and antigen-presenting cells with macrophage-like phenotypes [78–80]. Lymphocytes migrating across a disrupted BBB or through CSF pathways generate immune complexes with locally processed antigens [135, 136]. Pathological accumulation of these complexes obstructs glymphatic clearance, driving compensatory dilatation of upstream spaces and establishing a vicious cycle of "obstruction-dilation" pathophysiology (Fig. 2).

In summary, it is hypothesized that sepsis-induced pathophysiological alterations may collectively contribute to EPVS through multiple potential mechanisms. Specifically, hemodynamic dysfunction is proposed to lead to CSF stagnation and disturbed circulation, while dysregulated BBB permeability may facilitate abnormal fluid extravasation into the PVS—their potential synergy might substantially increase PVS content volume. Furthermore, intravascular microthrombosis-induced reduction in cerebral perfusion could impede metabolic waste clearance, and aberrant immune cell activation within PVS might cause structural obstruction. These mechanisms likely interact, potentially converging to drive significant PVS enlargement.

Chronic phase of SAE

Chronic neuroinflammation is commonly observed in the brains of sepsis patients [137, 138]. This pathological process induces cerebral damage through dual mechanisms: glymphatic system dysfunction leading to metabolic waste accumulation, and neuronal/glial cell death contributing to parenchymal atrophy.

Chronic neuroinflammation impairs the metabolic clearance function of the glymphatic system by disrupting the polarized localization of AQP4 in astrocytes. Concurrently, the diminished efferocytic ability of microglia collectively exacerbates the reduced clearance of neurotoxic metabolites. Current evidence suggests that the accumulation of Aβ and tau proteins is associated with long-term cognitive impairment in sepsis [18, 20, 139]. Following repeated intraperitoneal LPS administration in mice, the clearance of Aβ administered into the brain ventricles was reduced, while the peripheral Aβ concentration also decreased [140]. Moreover, in a murine model of sepsis comorbid with AD, investigators documented an augmented Aβ burden and cognitive impairment [141]. We posit that the retention of these neurotoxic metabolites within the PVS not only reflects glymphatic impairment but also directly contributes to a "downstream clogging" effect. This clogging physically impedes CSF-ISF exchange, thereby transforming the PVS from a passive conduit into an active participant in pathology by perpetuating a vicious cycle of impaired clearance and toxin accumulation.

Persistent neuroinflammation induced by sepsis drives the cascading release of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α) via aberrant activation of microglia and astrocytes. This pathological cascade not only triggers neuronal degeneration and glial cell apoptosis, but also leads to progressively aggravated cerebral parenchymal volume loss. Neuroimaging analyses reveal heterogeneous cerebral atrophy phenotypes in post-sepsis survivors [142]. Importantly, biomechanical tension effects and microstructural remodeling in perivascular tissues secondary to cerebral atrophy may constitute critical pathological mechanisms underlying the development of EPVS.

Importantly, studies in other neurological fields have established EPVS as independent pathological drivers: EPVS in the basal ganglia have been shown to independently predict cognitive impairment at 1 year post-stroke [143]. Moreover, larger PVS diameter is associated with higher dementia risk and accelerated brain atrophy [144]. However, the underlying mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. These observations across disease spectra suggest that once established, severely dilated EPVS may compromise brain function through mechanisms independent of their initial triggers, such as by chronically disrupting fluid dynamics, impairing waste clearance, and potentially isolating blood vessels from normal metabolic support. Therefore, in the context of SAE, we propose that EPVS dilation is not merely a secondary marker but a consequential pathology that actively exacerbates neurodegeneration and cognitive decline.

A hypothetical model of dynamic PVS remodeling In SAE

In the pathological progression of SAE, PVS undergo dynamic remodeling that is closely linked to neuroinflammatory cascades and glymphatic dysfunction. Initially, BBB disruption combined with excessive CSF secretion from the choroid plexus leads to a transient, compensatory PVS dilation, which facilitates the clearance of solutes but also paradoxically promotes the paravascular dissemination of inflammatory mediators. As neuroinflammation escalates, this initial compensation is overwhelmed. The PVS then transitions to a sustained, pathological expansion during chronic stages. This late-stage dilation is driven by the sustained accumulation of neurotoxic metabolites, persistent immune cell infiltration, extracellular matrix remodeling, and the decoupling of the neurovascular unit.

Ultimately, the PVS in SAE transcends its role as a passive CSF conduit, emerging as a tripartite nexus that integrates neuroinflammation, lymphatic dysfunction, and structural damage.

AQP4 and SAE: current knowledge and future challenges

AQP4 performs multiple physiological functions in the cns, with its core mechanisms involving mediating CSF-ISF circulation to maintain cerebral parenchymal homeostasis, facilitating metabolic clearance of neurotoxic substances such as Aβ proteins, and exerting bidirectional regulatory effects on neuroinflammatory responses through modulating the activation of glial cells [55, 90, 145, 146]. A study revealed that AQP4 deficiency significantly improved survival rates and cognitive function in septic mice, potentially by activating astrocytic autophagy and thereby suppressing their pathological overactivation [147]. On the other hand, in AD animal models, AQP4 deficiency has been shown to promote abnormal Aβ protein deposition [55]. These studies suggests that AQP4 may play a dual role in cognitive function.

Recent research findings indicate that AQP4 expression is elevated in cases of SAE; nevertheless, the precise pathophysiological mechanisms underlying this phenomenon remain to be fully elucidated. In murine models of sepsis, enhanced amino acid metabolism leads to non-hepatic hyperammonemia. Ammonia subsequently activated astrocytes via the gut-brain axis, resulting in the upregulation of AQP4 expression in these cells, which ultimately contributed to astrocytic damage [149]. Mechanistically, during sepsis progression, proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β released in excessive amounts can traverse the compromised BBB. These mediators directly upregulated AQP4 expression in astrocytes through activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway [156]. Additionally, in response to pro-inflammatory factors secreted by activated microglia, astrocytes undergo a phenotypic shift from the A2 to the A1 phenotype. This transition is characterized by the aberrant expression of the AQP4 water channel protein, which exacerbates cerebral edema and metabolic dysregulation.

However, some studies have demonstrated that the expression of AQP4 in the cerebral cortex or hippocampus of mice with SAE remains unchanged or exhibits a downward trend [103, 148, 150, 152]. The observed discrepancies may be attributed to variations in LPS dose and different observation time points across these experimental studies. For instance, Liu BT's research team and Xing E's group both employed a dual intraperitoneal LPS injection protocol with 24-h intervals for animal model establishment. This experimental paradigm may induce endotoxin tolerance in mice, with the underlying mechanism potentially involving suppressed IL-1β synthesis capability in subfornical organ (SFO)-associated perivascular macrophages of LPS-tolerant subjects. The deficiency in this pivotal cytokine expression could mediate significant attenuation of neuroinflammatory responses through downstream signaling transduction pathways [157, 158]. In contrast, Ruoyu Zhao et al. utilized a lower LPS dose (5 mg/kg) and reported no disruption of the blood–brain barrier in mice. It is thus plausible that a low LPS dose fails to upregulate cerebral AQP4 expression and instead triggers a compensatory downregulation [103]. Notably, we also observed a declining trend in AQP4 polarity, suggesting that alterations in AQP4 polarization may precede changes in its expression level [103, 150].

Given the lack of existing studies investigating AQP4 alterations during the chronic phase of SAE, it is imperative to conduct systematic investigations to elucidate the intracerebral spatiotemporal dynamics of AQP4 and delineate its pathophysiological regulatory mechanisms across the full disease continuum.

The dual face of AQP4 in SAE: balancing neuroinflammation and lymphatic clearance

AQP4 drives neuroinflammatory storm in SAE

Emerging research has established a critical role for AQP4 in the pathogenesis of SAE, primarily through its function as a pro-inflammatory mediator [89, 145, 147, 159]. Studies conducted by Li's team demonstrates that AQP4 modulates neuroinflammation in SAE. AQP4 regulates sodium influx via voltage-gated sodium channel 1.6 (Na_v1.6). This process disrupting astrocyte calcium homeostasis and causing pathological calcium overload. This overload exacerbates brain injury by suppressing PPAR-γ/mTOR-dependent autophagy and activating astrocyte-specific inflammation, leading to neuronal damage and cognitive decline [147]. robust experimental evidence suggests that astrocyte-specific knockout of AQP4 significantly attenuates LPS-induced astrocyte hyperactivation and subsequent neuroinflammatory cascades [90, 159]. Both dexamethasone administration and hypertonic saline therapy have been shown to effectively suppress LPS-induced aberrant AQP4 protein expression. This suppression subsequently modulates downstream pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, including IL-1β and TNF-α, ultimately leading to improved neurological outcomes in SAE [155, 156].

In addition to its direct role in mediating neuroinflammation, AQP4 may also ameliorate neuroinflammation indirectly by improving its polarization and thereby enhancing glymphatic drainage function. Although this has not been reported in sepsis, studies in Parkinson's disease and diffuse brain injury models have demonstrated that restoring AQP4 polarization through pharmacological or genetic approaches enhances glymphatic function and significantly reduces the expression levels of inflammatory factors in the brain [160, 161]. The underlying mechanism may involve improved glymphatic clearance of metabolic waste, preventing the additional release of inflammatory factors. In conclusion, the role of this pathway in sepsis remains to be elucidated in future studies.

The evolving role of AQP4-mediated waste clearance in SAE

AQP4 plays a multifaceted role in the brain, not only regulating neuroinflammation but also maintaining brain homeostasis by driving CSF flow and clearing metabolic waste. In the pathological progression of SAE, AQP4 exhibits dual regulatory roles with opposing effects: On one hand, it contributes to the development of cerebral edema by modulating water permeability through astrocytic endfeet. On the other hand, it facilitates the clearance of metabolic waste products, such as Aβ, by maintaining glymphatic system functionality.

In murine models of sepsis, researches have demonstrated that genetic knockout of AQP4 in astrocytes or pharmacological inhibition of AQP4 expression significantly alleviates cerebral edema and effectively improves clinical outcomes [90, 153, 156, 162]. Notably, a study conducted by Liu et al. revealed that specific knockout of AQP4 on astrocytes exerted significant protective effects on long-term neurological outcomes in a mouse model of traumatic brain injury. More importantly, the research also found that AQP4 knockout enhances the clearance of Aβ in the ipsilateral cortex of TBI mouse models [163]. This protective mechanism may be achieved through a dual-channel effect: on one hand, the knockout of the AQP4 gene can inhibit the activation of astrocyte-mediated neuroinflammatory pathways; on the other hand, it reduces the migration of antigens from the brain to the MLVs, thereby blocking the abnormal recruitment of peripheral immune cells, ultimately improving the pathological process of brain edema mediated by neuroinflammation. However, these studies carry significant limitations, as they primarily focus on acute-phase manifestations in murine models and fail to elucidate the potential long-term consequences of AQP4 dysfunction on disease progression.

Recent studies have emphasized that the polarized distribution of AQP4 on astrocytes serves as a crucial determinant in regulating glymphatic system function. This polarized arrangement directly governs the circulatory dynamics and efficiency of the CSF-ISF exchange within the cerebral microenvironment [56, 164–167]. Disruption of AQP4 polarization, known as depolarization, exacerbates the accumulation of Aβ and pathological tau aggregates in the brain parenchyma by impairing glymphatic transport function.

While the precise mechanisms underlying AQP4 polarity alteration in SAE remain to be fully elucidated, experimental findings from various disease models indicate that its depolarization is intimately associated with neuroinflammatory processes. Based on this, it is hypothesized that during the pathological progression of SAE, AQP4 may undergo depolarization due to aberrant microglial activation or BBB disruption, thereby aggravating the impairment in the clearance of neurotoxic substances.

During the pathogenesis of SAE, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) trigger microglial activation that drives astrocyte polarization toward the neurotoxic A1 phenotype [168]. This phenotypic transformation exhibits a remarkable spatial correspondence with the remodeling of AQP4 distribution. The seminal study by Shu Feng et al. established a bidirectional correlation pattern: AQP4 depolarization levels showed strong positive correlation with A1-type astrocyte marker C3 complement expression, while exhibiting negative correlation with neuroprotective A2-type marker S100A10 [165]. This differential association implies that A2-type astrocytes with trophic functions may preserve perivascular endfeet AQP4 polarity, thereby exerting protective effects on cerebral microenvironment homeostasis. It should be noted, however, that astrocytes are highly heterogeneous, and their phenotypic dynamics and functional roles in the septic context remain incompletely understood. While the A1/A2 dichotomy provides a useful conceptual framework, current evidence indicates that astrocytes under pathological conditions exhibit considerable heterogeneity and plasticity, with their functional states more appropriately viewed as a continuous phenotypic spectrum [169, 170]. Therefore, further research is warranted to explore the potential relationship between astrocytic phenotypic transitions and AQP4 polarization, as well as their possible regulatory roles in the progression of sepsis.

Matrix metalloproteinase (MMPs) selectively disrupt the AQP4-β-dystroglycan molecular interface through proteolytic cleavage, leading to AQP4 membrane depolarization and glymphatic dysfunction [56, 57, 171, 172]. Pathological hyperactivation of Matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) not only dissociates the AQP4-β-DG complex but also accelerates AQP4 endocytosis and lysosomal degradation [56]. Pharmacological inhibition of MMP-9 has been shown to effectively preserve AQP4 polarity and enhance glymphatic drainage, underscoring its therapeutic potential [171, 172]. However, emerging evidence indicates that excessive MMPs inhibition causes abnormal extracellular matrix accumulation, leading to structural constriction of CSF drainage pathways and subsequent glymphatic impairment [173]. These findings reveal MMPs-mediated biphasic regulation of the glymphatic system: physiological enzyme activity maintains the plasticity of drainage channels, whereas both hyperactivity and hypoactivity under pathological conditions disrupt metabolic clearance through distinct—protein complex dissociation and extracellular matrix deposition, respectively.

In conclusion, AQP4 exhibits a multifaceted and essential role in the pathological progression of SAE, displaying a notable functional duality. It participates in both the modulation of neuroinflammation and the development of cerebral edema, concurrently facilitating the clearance of metabolic waste and upholding cerebral fluid homeostasis. The specific impacts of AQP4 are dictated by dynamic alterations in its expression levels, spatial distribution, and functional states. Further in-depth investigation into the spatiotemporal expression patterns and mechanistic roles of AQP4 in SAE will provide critical theoretical insights into the pathophysiological processes of SAE and offer novel avenues for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

The neuroinflammation-lymphatic failure-waste clearance axis: a dual pathological mechanism in SAE

Glymphatic system is the epicenter and propagation pathway of neuroinflammation

Recent researches suggest a strong correlation between EPVS and neuroinflammation [174–176]. This paper provides a review of the potential associations between neuroinflammation and the impaired drainage of the glymphatic system during SAE.

The PVS, a critical component of the glymphatic system, function as both a drainage pathway and a key site for the initiation of neuroinflammatory processes [7, 48, 173]. Studies have identified the presence of T cells and macrophages within the PVS of the brain. Additionally, the movement of CSF has been shown to facilitate the transport of lymphocytes to these perivascular regions [81, 177]. The ISF containing antigens is conveyed through the PVS to the meninges, where it is processed by antigen-presenting cells and detected by lymphocytes, thereby facilitating immune surveillance within the CNS [38, 178].

During the pathogenesis of sepsis, proinflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-17, and IFN-γ play a pivotal role in driving neuroinflammation via bifaceted regulatory pathways. These cytokines not only upregulate the expression of chemokines, adhesion molecules (including P-selectin and integrin ligands), and matrix metalloproteinases, but also enhance endothelial permeability to facilitate leukocyte transmigration into PVS and brain parenchyma [179–183].

This pathological progression coincides with elevated concentrations of circulating PAMPs (e.g., endotoxins, viral nucleic acids) and DAMPs (e.g., mitochondrial DNA, HMGB1), which penetrate PVS through compromised BBB, creating a persistent immunostimulatory microenvironment. Perivascular macrophages, T cells, and other immune cells infiltrating the brain PVS from the periphery, when stimulated by DAMPs and PAMPs, produce substantial amounts of inflammatory mediators, including IL-23, IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, thereby driving the progression of neuroinflammation [184, 185]. Cerebrovascular endothelial cells are activated by circulating inflammatory factors, leading to the secretion of cytokines into the PVS [186]. Astrocytic endfeet constitute an essential structural component of the PVS, with the highly expressed AQP4 on their membranes playing a pivotal role in mediating pro-inflammatory responses. Accumulating evidence indicates that in murine sepsis models, AQP4 facilitates the activation of A1-type reactive astrocytes and subsequently elevates the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and TNF-α [90, 159]. Notably, both pharmacological blockade and genetic suppression of AQP4 expression not only attenuate pathological astrocyte activation but also significantly diminish the of crucial inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β [155, 156] (Fig. 3).

Systemic inflammation may prime perivascular immune niches even without BBB disruption, potentially exacerbating neuroinflammatory cascades in diseases like SAE. Recent findings suggest that during the pathogenesis of SAE, PVS not only serve as a conduit for peripheral immune cell infiltration into the brain parenchyma, but may also constitute primary initiation sites for neuroinflammatory cascades. In a murine model, Wanaka et al. found that the initial intraperitoneal administration of LPS triggered a rapid IL-1β response in perivascular macrophages, which progressively attenuated upon repeated LPS challenges [187]. Despite the absence of evidence for impairment of BBB integrity, a significant upregulation of Cyclooxygenase-2(COX-2) expression was observed in the perivascular regions of the brain in septic mice [188].

Intriguingly, experimental studies indicate that glymphatic system function may be augmented during the early phase of acute inflammation. In experimental models utilizing intracerebroventricular autologous blood injection, Professor Kahle’s research team elucidated activation of the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR-4) nuclear factor kappa (NF-κB) signaling pathway in choroid plexus epithelial cells. This molecular cascade was found to upregulate the Na+-K+-Cl− cotransporter NKCC1, ultimately driving hypersecretion of CSF [189]. This observation was further supported by Liu et al. using murine hydrocephalus models, validating the conserved nature of this pathological mechanism diverse experimental systems [190]. Within minutes of ischemic stroke onset, diffusion-restricted ischemia triggered pathological vasoconstriction in cerebral vasculature, which drove rapid CSF influx into EPVS. Critically, AQP4 deficiency was observed to significantly attenuate this CSF influx [191] (Fig. 3).

This phenomenon could reflect a compensatory neuroimmune mechanism, wherein enhanced clearance of cellular waste, inflammatory mediators, and antigens through activated drainage pathways could paradoxically promote the dissemination of pro-inflammatory factors to distant brain regions. Upon reaching the brain parenchyma, cytokines activate microglia and astrocytes, initiating an inflammatory chain reaction that propagates throughout neural tissues.

Neuroinflammation disrupting glymphatic system

Neuroinflammation disrupts glymphatic system function through multiple interconnected mechanisms. While microglia are not directly involved in the formation of the glymphatic system, emerging evidence indicates that they may exert a modulatory influence on glymphatic system function. Morphological alterations in microglia could play a regulatory role in CSF drainage. Yao et al. revealed that both microglial morphology and glymphatic system function were governed by circadian rhythms. During wakefulness, microglial branching was diminished, and glymphatic function was correspondingly reduced. In contrast, during sleep, microglial branching increased, and the coverage area expanded. Notably, the peak of glymphatic function coincided with the maximum coverage of microglia [192].

The regulatory influence of microglia on astrocytic activation is critically important in governing glymphatic system functionality. Crucially, the microglia-astrocyte crosstalk not only orchestrates the cytokine gradient distribution at molecular level, but also profoundly modulates the polarization status of AQP4 water channels in astrocytic endfeet, thereby directly determining the functional efficacy of the glymphatic clearance system [193, 194]. The polarized distribution of AQP4 serves as the molecular cornerstone underlying the solute clearance function of the glymphatic system [195].

Nevertheless, during septic encephalopathy, the exacerbated neuroinflammatory response could potentially alter the characteristic distribution pattern of AQP4 in astrocytes. In patients with sepsis, the expression of MMPs, especially MMP-9, is significantly elevated [196, 197]. Recent studies has underscored the pivotal role of dysregulated MMP-9 activation in glymphatic system dysfunction. Mechanistic investigations have revealed that MMP-9 specifically cleaves β-dystroglycan (β-DG) on the astrocytic endfeet, thereby disrupting the polarized distribution of AQP4 and ultimately impairing glymphatic function [56, 57] (Fig. 3).

In addition to the disruption of AQP4 polarization, glymphatic system dysfunction encompasses other pathological mechanisms. During neuroinflammatory responses, activated immune cells accumulate abnormally in the PVS, potentially obstructing CSF circulation through physical blockage [135, 136]. Impaired CSF circulation or compromised glymphatic system function significantly impairs the brain's capacity to clear inflammatory mediators, resulting in the abnormal accumulation of pro-inflammatory factors within the brain parenchyma, thereby triggering sustained neuroinflammatory responses and neuronal damage [198] (Fig. 3).

Meningeal lymphatics: gatekeeping neuroinflammation and toxic waste clearance in SAE

In a septic mouse model, Huang et al. demonstrated that intraperitoneal LPS injection in septic mice induced marked dorsal meningeal lymphangiogenesis (day 4 post-injection), indicative of compensatory functional enhancement. Concurrently, dural macrophages showed morphological and positional alterations with downregulated MHC-II expression, synergistically improving MLVs function to facilitate neuroinflammation resolution [35].

However, recent studies have produced divergent findings. The Dong research team demonstrated that the septic mouse model exhibited significant MLVs dysfunction, marked by impaired lymphatic drainage efficiency and compromised immune cell clearance capacity [34]. Specifically, Dong et al. substantiated that aged mice (16–18 weeks old) subjected to a single LPS challenge (5 mg/kg) developed multifaceted MLVs dysfunction, manifested by reduced spatial tracer distribution density in MLVs, diminished tracer accumulation in deep cervical lymph nodes, and coordinated downregulation of lymphatic endothelial marker LYVE-1 along with valvulogenic regulators Prox1 and Foxc2. [34]. Emerging research has further highlighted that impaired MLVs drainage on post-infection day 3 in Listeria-infected models correlates with coordinated downregulation of pivotal lymphatic valve morphogenesis regulators (e.g., Egfl7, Nrp1, and Foxc2) [199]. Notably, the study revealed significant expansion of MLVs in mice on the third day post-infection, manifested by increased luminal diameter and expanded coverage area of LYVE-1/PDPN-positive endothelial cells. Mechanistic analyses demonstrated that Listeria monocytogenes infection induces VEGF-C production in microglia, astrocytes, endothelial cells, and macrophages [199]. As a critical lymphangiogenic factor, VEGF-C has been shown to substantially enhance MLVs drainage function [45, 200].

This acute-phase lymphatic remodeling phenomenon implies that pathogenic infections may trigger adaptive responses through VEGF-C-dependent molecular mechanisms. Collectively, these findings underscore that MLVs exhibit temporally distinct regulation between lymphangiogenesis and drainage functional improvement, with context-dependent regulatory patterns across disease states. Crucially, the improvement in drainage functionality appears to depend primarily on the structural plasticity of lymphatic architecture rather than on neovascularization alone.

Emerging evidence suggests that pre-existing MLVs dysfunction exacerbates SAE pathogenesis. Dong et al. demonstrated that surgical ligation of bilateral deep cervical lymph nodes exacerbated cognitive deficits in septic mice and heightened neuroinflammation, characterized by microglial hyperactivation and elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-1β. Intracisternal delivery of AAV1-VEGF-C enhanced MLVs drainage, effectively suppressing microglial activation and downregulating sepsis-associated genes (e.g., MMP3, CCL5, Macro), ultimately ameliorating cognitive deficits [34].

MLVs have been identified as a critical modulator of SAE pathogenesis through biphasic regulatory mechanisms. During the initial compensatory phase, MLVs actively restrict the trafficking of PAMPs to dCLNs, thereby limiting peripheral immune cell infiltration into the CNS. However, disease progression triggers a decompensated phase characterized by BBB disruption-induced neuroinflammatory cascades, which degrade lymphatic endothelial junctional complexes and impair antigen-presenting cell (APC) egress. Trapped APCs synergize with accumulated proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α) and neurotoxic metabolites (e.g., Aβ) to establish self-perpetuating inflammatory circuits, ultimately exacerbating SAE-related cognitive and motor deficits. These findings suggest that spatiotemporal-specific modulation of MLVs function—enhancing drainage during early inflammation versus targeting inflammatory mediators in advanced stages—may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for SAE.

Modulation of AQP4

As a pivotal functional component of the glymphatic system, AQP4 channel presents a potential therapeutic target through modulation of its expression patterns. In acute brain injury, upregulated AQP4 expression exacerbates cerebral edema, while its depolarization disrupts CSF-ISF exchange, impairing metabolic waste clearance [191, 214, 217]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that targeted modulation of AQP4 through genetic engineering or pharmacological inhibition can effectively ameliorate cerebral edema severity in acute brain injury models and promote neurofunctional recovery [27, 202, 218].

Contrary to conventional paradigms positing AQP4 hyperactivation exacerbates cerebral edema. Studies employing mifepristone intervention revealed a significant upregulation of AQP4 expression, particularly enhancing its polarized perivascular localization, which augmented glymphatic transport efficiency [214]. Notably, the generalizability of this regulatory mechanism has been pharmacologically validated using TGN-073, a second-generation AQP4 agonist [219].

Although both AQP4 agonists and inhibitors exerted similar regulatory effects on the glymphatic system, indicating multifunctional modulation mechanisms, experimental evidence revealed that both compound types enhanced AQP4 polarization, thereby improving system functionality. These findings indicate that targeting the subcellular localization of AQP4 in astrocytes may serve as a viable strategy for glymphatic system modulation, warranting further researches to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic applications.

Regulation of CSF hydrodynamics

Vascular pulsations are essential for maintaining glymphatic clearance function, which relies on stable cerebral blood perfusion and intact vasomotor function [12, 112]. Levosimendan is a calcium sensitizer that combines inotropic effects with vasodilatory properties, enhancing cardiac output without increasing myocardial oxygen consumption. Recent studies demonstrate that in a LPS-induced sepsis mouse model, levosimendan treatment significantly improved cerebral blood flow perfusion and enhanced cerebrospinal fluid outflow function [103]. Furthermore, in patients with ischemic stroke, recanalization of occluded vessels can effectively restore glymphatic clearance function [132]. The Kipnis research team demonstrated through systemic administration experiments that dobutamine effectively mitigates vasodilation dysfunction induced by perivascular macrophage depletion, while significantly enhancing parenchymal distribution efficiency of tracers within the CNS [173].

Acute brain injury is frequently characterized by cerebral hypoperfusion and impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation, which can occur independently of intracranial hypertension [220–222]. This dysregulation of vascular function directly undermines the hemodynamic forces that drive glymphatic fluid exchange, leading to CSF-lymphatic circulation dysfunction and impaired waste clearance. Therefore, therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring cerebral perfusion pressure and normalizing vasomotor function may represent a promising frontier for preserving glymphatic function and improving neurological outcomes in acute brain injury.

Targeting the adrenergic system to modulate the glymphatic system

Under acute brain injury conditions, adrenergic hyperactivation typically occurs as part of the systemic stress response. Emerging evidence has elucidated that the adrenergic nervous system exerts substantial regulatory effects on glymphatic metabolic clearance.

The pan-adrenergic receptor antagonist PPA (targeting α1/α2/β receptor subtypes) has demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in ameliorating neurocognitive impairments secondary to ischemic stroke, traumatic brain injury, and status epilepticus in preclinical models [209, 223, 224]. Although the precise molecular mechanism underlying PPA-enhanced glymphatic function remains incompletely elucidated, the observed AQP4 polarity reorganization following pharmacological administration may provide critical pathophysiological insights.

While adrenergic receptor antagonists were traditionally considered enhancers of glymphatic function, recent findings suggests that adrenergic agonists may modulate this system through non-canonical pathways, potentially involving neurovascular coupling remodeling or CSF dynamics optimization. The Kipnis group demonstrated that noradrenergic signaling from the pontine locus coeruleus during NREM sleep induces slow-wave rhythmic contractions (0.1–0.3 Hz) of cerebral vasculature. This phasic vascular activity generates pulsatile pressure gradients that drive directional CSF flux through paravascular spaces [225]. Crucially, this neurovascular-fluidic coupling was abolished by pan-adrenergic receptor antagonists, pharmacologically validating the central role of noradrenergic signaling in orchestrating rhythmic glymphatic clearance [225].

Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective α2-adrenergic receptor agonist clinically used for sedation, uniquely enhances glymphatic function by prolonging NREM sleep-like states. Mechanistically, it amplifies CSF flow in PVS, boosting Aβ clearance efficiency [226–228]. As a selective β1-adrenergic receptor agonist, dobutamine modulates cerebrovascular tone to amplify CSF flux within PVS [173]. These studies indicate that adrenergic receptor agonists may exert regulatory effects through non-canonical pathways, including mimicking physiological sleep architecture and modulating CSF dynamics.

Conclusion

Emerging evidence indicates that the glymphatic system and MLVs form a unique lymphatic-like network in the central nervous system, mediating metabolic waste clearance through CSF-ISF exchange and participating in neuroimmune regulation. During the progression of SAE, glymphatic system and MLVs may exert dual regulatory effects, promoting the elimination of neurotoxic metabolites while modulating neuroinflammatory cascades. Building upon this, we propose the concept of “neuroinflammation-lymphatic failure-waste clearance axis” in which PVS serves as the initial site of neuroinflammation, allowing inflammatory mediators to spread throughout the brain via PVS and trigger robust neuroinflammation. In turn, neuroinflammatory responses disrupt glymphatic function, impairing the clearance of inflammatory factors and metabolic waste, thereby establishing a vicious cycle. These pathophysiological characteristics suggest that targeted modulation of this axis could serve as a novel therapeutic strategy to improve neurological outcomes in SAE. Our unpublished data demonstrate that glymphatic clearance dysfunction occurs as early as 24 h post-cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) in a murine sepsis model. Current evidence indicates that modulating AQP4 water channel function, improving cerebrospinal fluid hydrodynamics, and regulating sympathetic nervous system activity can effectively intervene in the brain cleansing system. Clinically, dexmedetomidine and β-adrenergic blockers have been observed to improve cognitive function in septic patients, yet whether this effect is mediated through glymphatic system regulation remains unclear. To better understand the pathogenesis of SAE, future studies should systematically elucidate the dynamic changes of the brain washing system during different disease stages, which may facilitate the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.
