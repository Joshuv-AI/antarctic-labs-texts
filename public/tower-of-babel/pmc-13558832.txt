Family Care Management of Patients With Chronic Diseases: A Concept Analysis
Authors: Reza Nematollahi Maleki, Akram Ghahramanian, Mojgan Lotfi, Azad Rahmani, Reza Negarandeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic diseases are major contributors to global morbidity and mortality, and family members often assume primary caregiving roles. However, the concept of family care management remains poorly defined, limiting consistency in research and practice. This study analysed the concept of family care management of patients with chronic diseases.MethodsWalker and Avant's eight‐step concept analysis method was used. A literature review was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, CINAHL, and Embase using terms related to family, care management, and chronic disease. English and Persian studies published up to August 12, 2026, were included. Study quality was assessed using established appraisal tools.ResultsFour defining attributes were identified: active family participation, holistic support, collaboration with healthcare providers, and adaptability and flexibility. Antecedents included family capacity and readiness and socioeconomic and cultural status. Consequences were introduced as patients's outcomes and caregivers' outcomes.ConclusionClarifying this concept provides a framework to guide nursing practice, education, and future intervention research.Implications for Nursing PracticeThe four defining attributes provide nurses with a structured framework for assessing family involvement, identifying gaps in family support, and designing individualised, family‐centred care plans, particularly in home, community, and chronic disease care settings.Patient and Public ContributionAs this study was a concept analysis based on existing literature, no patient or public involvement was required.

Background and Introduction

Chronic diseases, including diabetes, heart disease, cancer, Alzheimer's Disease (AD), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), constitute a major global health challenge due to their persistent nature and need for ongoing management (Hajat and Stein 2018). According to the World Health Organization, noncommunicable diseases are responsible for approximately 74% of deaths worldwide, underscoring their significant impact on public health systems and societies (Organization 2012). In the United States alone, 60% of adults are living with at least one chronic condition, and 12% are managing five or more, resulting in substantial healthcare costs and personal burdens (Rudin et al. 2021). These conditions not only affect individuals but also place considerable demands on families and healthcare infrastructures, necessitating comprehensive care strategies that extend beyond clinical settings.

Families are central to the management of chronic diseases, frequently serving as informal caregivers who provide a broad spectrum of support. Their roles include offering emotional encouragement, assisting with daily activities, monitoring symptoms, facilitating self‐management, supporting treatment adherence, and managing medication (Advances in Patient Safety 2008; Luo et al. 2024; Rosland et al. 2010). Approximately one in five adults in the U.S. acts as a caregiver for a family member or friend with a chronic health condition or disability (Kilmer et al. 2024), collectively contributing unpaid care valued at an estimated $470 billion annually (Caregiver Statistics: Demographics 2016).

Despite their essential contributions, family caregivers often encounter significant challenges that can adversely affect their own well‐being (Hailu et al. 2024). The responsibilities of caregiving are commonly associated with emotional stress, financial strain, and demanding time commitments, all of which can lead to negative health outcomes such as depression and reduced quality of life. These challenges highlight the need for targeted support and resources to sustain the health and effectiveness of family caregivers (Chu et al. 2025).

Although the critical role of families in chronic disease management is widely recognised, the concept of ‘family care management of patients with chronic diseases’ remains ambiguously defined in the literature. The only concept analysis to directly address this construct was published by Sun et al. (2022) (Sun et al. 2022), but it only examined family care management from the perspective of health professionals and identified attributes centred on nurse behaviours, including supervising the family's care situation, providing professional guidance, and creating partnerships with family caregivers. Notably, as Sun et al. acknowledged that their analysis excluded patients and family members, they explicitly called for future work to examine family care management from the family's own perspective, with particular attention to the role of the patient. To date, this gap has not been addressed. When examined only through a professional lens, family care management risks overlooking how families themselves experience, enact, and adapt their caregiving roles, which can result in inconsistencies in how family involvement is understood, supported, and integrated into clinical practice and research, and can limit the development of interventions and assessment tools that authentically reflect the family's lived experience. Establishing a clear, family‐centred definition is essential for healthcare professionals, particularly nurses to accurately assess family needs, design targeted interventions, and incorporate family support into comprehensive care plans.

To address this gap, the present study applies Walker and Avant's eight‐step concept analysis method, a well‐established approach in nursing scholarship (Walker and Avant 2005). This method systematically explores the defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents of ‘family care management of patients with chronic diseases,’ providing a robust framework to inform future nursing practice, policy, and research.

Methods

Design

This study employed Walker and Avant's concept analysis method (Walker and Avant 2005) to clarify the concept of ‘family care management of patients with chronic diseases.’ The method was selected for its systematic, stepwise framework, which is effective in identifying a concept's defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents. The eight‐step process as outlined by Walker and Avant was followed, comprising: concept selection, determining the analysis aims, identifying all uses of the concept, determining defining attributes, constructing a model case, identifying other cases, identifying antecedents and consequences, and defining empirical referents (Table 1). To ensure methodological rigour and transparency in the reporting of the literature review process, this study also adhered to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines.

Literature Search

A comprehensive literature review was conducted by the first author across multiple health databases, including PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, CINAHL, and Embase. As this is a concept analysis aimed at clarifying the theoretical and defining structure of a specific term (Walker and Avant 2005), rather than a systematic review seeking to comprehensively represent the clinical or epidemiological literature on chronic disease management, the search strategy combined the terms ‘family,’ ‘family caregiver,’ ‘care management,’ ‘chronic disease,’ and ‘chronic illness’ using the Boolean operators ‘AND’ and ‘OR’ in the title field of each database. This decision was informed by a preliminary scoping comparison: searching the same terms across title and abstract fields yielded 1162 records, compared with 246 for a title‐restricted search across the same databases and date range, with the additional records predominantly addressing chronic disease management from clinical, pharmacological, or epidemiological perspectives unrelated to the family‐level care processes that are the object of this analysis. Restricting the search to titles allowed the research team to focus screening resources on records with the highest a priori conceptual relevance to the concept under analysis. To offset the reduced sensitivity inherent to a title‐restricted strategy, the database search was supplemented by manual searching, comprising hand‐searching of the reference lists of all included full‐text articles and forward/backward citation tracking of key sources on family caregiving and care management; this process identified 36 additional records, reported as records identified through other methods in the PRISMA flow diagram. No publication date restrictions were applied, and articles published in either English or Persian up to August 12, 2026, were considered for inclusion. Studies were included if they were relevant to the study's aim and contributed to identifying the antecedents, attributes, or consequences of the concept ‘family care management of patients with chronic diseases.’ Publications that did not address the concept directly or failed to provide pertinent information for the analysis were excluded.

The appraisal tools utilized (by R.N.M. and A.G.) were as follows: the CASP Qualitative Checklist for qualitative studies; the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Systematic Reviews and Research Synthesis for systematic review and meta‐analysis studies; the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) for mixed‐methods studies; the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Analytical Cross‐Sectional Studies for cross‐sectional designs; the SANRA Checklist for narrative reviews; the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Cohort Studies for cohort studies; and the COSMIN Risk of Bias Checklist for studies evaluating measurement properties (Table S1).

Results

The database search identified 246 records (PubMed, n = 36; ScienceDirect, n = 11; Scopus, n = 110; Embase, n = 43; CINAHL, n = 46). An additional 36 records were identified through other methods, comprising hand‐searching of reference lists and citation tracking, yielding 282 records in total. After removing 86 duplicate records, 196 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 110 were excluded as not relevant to the concept. The full texts of the remaining 86 records were sought for retrieval, and all 86 were successfully retrieved and assessed for eligibility. Of these, 38 were excluded for not directly addressing the study's concept, and 2 were excluded due to low methodological quality based on the relevant appraisal checklists, leaving 46 reports included in the concept development (Figure 1). These two excluded studies were appraised using the respective JBI Critical Appraisal Checklists for Quasi‐Experimental and Analytical Cross‐Sectional Studies. Exclusion decisions were made by consensus among the research team based on the presence of substantial deficiencies in key methodological criteria rather than solely on a numerical quality score or predefined threshold. All records were managed using EndNote 2025.3.1.

Flow diagram.

Data analysis was conducted using qualitative content analysis (Elo and Kyngäs 2008). Two researchers independently extracted and coded relevant text segments pertaining to the concept. The resulting codes were then grouped into categories and subsequently mapped onto the Walker and Avant framework of antecedents, attributes, and consequences. Any discrepancies in coding were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached within the research team.

It should be noted that this analysis was a literature‐derived, family‐centred conceptualization rather than a direct empirical study of families' perspectives.

Identifying the Use of the Concept

To explore the concept of ‘family care management of patients with chronic disease’, it is essential to understand the distinct meanings of the key terms from various sources:

Family

Merriam‐Webster Dictionary for Kids defines ‘family’ as ‘a group of individuals living under one roof and usually under one head’; and medically defines it as ‘the basic unit in society traditionally consisting of two parents rearing their children’ (Merriam‐Webster 2026b). Family is also defined in MeSH as ‘A social group consisting of parents or parent substitutes and children.’ (Family 1999).

Care Management

The ‘Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’ (AHRQ) describes care management as follows: Care management is a set of activities to improve patient care, reduce unnecessary need for additional medical care, avoid duplication of care, and help patients and their caregivers more effectively manage their health conditions (Care Management, n.d.).

Chronic Disease

Merriam‐Webster Dictionary defines ‘chronic diseases’ as ‘a disease (such as asthma, coronary heart disease, or diabetes) that continues or occurs again and again for a long time, a medical condition of prolonged duration.’ (Merriam‐Webster 2026a).

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) defines chronic diseases as ‘conditions that last 1 year or more and require ongoing medical attention or limit activities of daily living or both.’ Examples include diabetes, hypertension, and Alzheimer's disease, which impose persistent challenges on patients and their support systems (Chronic Disease, n.d.).

Family Care Management of Patients With Chronic Disease in Nursing Literature

While no single, universally accepted definition was found in the literature, the literature suggests this concept can be defined as the active involvement of family members in the collaborative process of assessing, planning, facilitating, coordinating, evaluating, and advocating for care services to meet the comprehensive health needs of individuals with long‐term conditions (Advances in Patient Safety 2008; Faronbi et al. 2019; Hailu et al. 2024; Moonpanane et al. 2021; Reinhard et al. 2012; Rosland et al. 2010; Rudin et al. 2021; Russell et al. 2016; Wolff et al. 2016).

Defining Attributes

Active Family Participation

Family members are actively involved in providing direct care and making decisions regarding the patient's health management. Hailu et al. emphasize family members as key caregivers in chronic disease management (Hailu et al. 2024). Wolff et al. highlight the critical role of family caregivers in assisting with healthcare activities for chronic conditions (Wolff et al. 2016). Additionally, Russell et al. in their grounded theory study on divorced parents caring for children with chronic illnesses, such as diabetes or asthma, highlight the active participation of parents in managing treatment regimens and coordinating care across households (Russell et al. 2016). Similarly, a phenomenological study revealed that caregivers of older adults with chronic illnesses are actively involved in daily care tasks such as mobility, bathing, grooming, and feeding (Faronbi et al. 2019). In another study by Panagioti et al., it is stated that family caregivers are responsible for providing most caregiving tasks, ranging from personal care to medical care, for older adults with chronic diseases (Panagioti et al. 2015). In the case of children with chronic diseases, the international literature indicates that the main family caregivers are the parents, who experience adversity, risk, and vulnerability associated with the consequences of care during chronic illness (Toledano‐Toledano and Domínguez‐Guedea 2019). A grounded theory study on families caring for children with special healthcare needs in rural Thailand further emphasizes active participation through families' consistent engagement in care routines (Moonpanane et al. 2021).

Holistic Support

The care provided encompasses physical, emotional, and social dimensions to address the comprehensive needs of the patient and family. Hailu et al. describe family care as addressing multiple dimensions of patient needs (Hailu et al. 2024). The role of holistic approaches in improving patient safety and care quality has been underscored (Advances in Patient Safety 2008). Reinhard et al. detail how family caregivers provide complex, multifaceted support for chronic conditions (Reinhard et al. 2012).

Collaboration With Healthcare Providers

Effective family care management involves working collaboratively with healthcare professionals to ensure coordinated and optimal care. Rosland et al. note that family collaboration with healthcare providers enhances self‐management support (Rosland et al. 2010). Rudin et al. discuss the importance of integrating family caregivers into healthcare delivery systems (Rudin et al. 2021). Wolff et al. emphasize the partnership between family caregivers and healthcare teams in chronic care management (Wolff et al. 2016).

Adaptability and Flexibility

A defining attribute of ‘family care management of patients with chronic diseases’ is its dynamic and evolving nature, requiring caregivers to continually adapt to new challenges. This adaptability is crucial as care needs fluctuate with disease progression. For instance, Chu et al. emphasize that caregiving strategies must evolve in parallel with the advancing stages of the illness (Chu et al. 2025). Gitlin & Wolff explore how family caregivers systematically adjust their approaches and routines in response to shifting care demands over time (Gitlin and Wolff 2011).

The very roles within the family unit are also dynamic. Hajat & Stein describe the dynamic reconfiguration of family roles and responsibilities that occurs in response to the demands of managing a chronic condition (Hajat and Stein 2018).

Ultimately, this capacity for adaptation is not merely procedural but is a critical mechanism for mitigating the inherent burden of care. The significant relationship between caregiving burden and the possession of adaptive behaviours underscores this point, as demonstrated in a study focusing on mothers of children with chronic diseases (Chirani et al. 2025).

Model Case

The Johnson family has been managing their mother, Mrs. Johnson's, chronic heart failure for three years. Mrs. Johnson, a 72‐year‐old widow, was diagnosed after experiencing shortness of breath and fatigue. Her adult children, Sarah and Michael, and their spouses have created a comprehensive care plan to ensure her well‐being.

Active Family Participation: Sarah, who lives nearby, visits daily to prepare low‐sodium meals, adhering to dietary guidelines from the American Heart Association. She monitors Mrs. Johnson's weight daily to detect fluid retention, a key indicator in heart failure management. Michael, a software engineer, sets up a digital health app to track symptoms, medication adherence, and exercise. He also assists with household chores, such as vacuuming, to reduce his mother's physical strain. Their spouses help by running errands and ensuring Mrs. Johnson has transportation to appointments, creating a robust support system.

Holistic Support: The family addresses Mrs. Johnson's emotional and social needs alongside her physical health. Sarah organizes weekly family movie nights to keep her mother socially engaged, reducing feelings of isolation common in chronic illness. Michael encourages her to join a local senior center's low‐impact exercise class, which provides both physical activity and social interaction. Recognising the emotional toll of heart failure, they enrol Mrs. Johnson in a support group for heart patients, where she shares experiences and learns coping strategies. The family also attends counselling to manage their own stress, ensuring they can sustain their caregiving roles.

Collaboration with Healthcare Providers: The Johnsons work closely with Mrs. Johnson's cardiologist, Dr. Patel, and her primary care nurse practitioner. They attend all appointments, bringing a notebook to record treatment plans and asking questions about medication side effects. Sarah uses a shared health platform to upload daily weight, blood pressure, and symptom logs, which Dr. Patel reviews remotely. When Mrs. Johnson's symptoms worsened last year, Michael contacted the cardiologist immediately, leading to a timely medication adjustment. The family participates in quarterly care planning meetings to discuss long‐term goals and potential interventions.

Adaptability and Flexibility: The family adapts to changes in Mrs. Johnson's condition. After a hospitalization for an acute exacerbation, Sarah and Michael rearranged their work schedules to provide round‐the‐clock support during her recovery. They learned to use a portable oxygen concentrator and installed home modifications, such as a stairlift, to improve accessibility. When Mrs. Johnson's cardiologist recommended a new diuretic, the family attended a training session to understand its administration and side effects, ensuring they could manage her evolving needs effectively.

This case fully embodies ‘family care management’ by demonstrating all four attributes. The Johnson family's active, holistic, collaborative, and adaptable approach ensures Mrs. Johnson's heart failure is well‐managed, enhancing her quality of life and reducing complications.

Borderline Case

The Patel family is caring for their father, Mr. Patel, a 65‐year‐old retired engineer diagnosed with Type 2 diabetes 8 years ago. Mr. Patel struggles with blood sugar control due to inconsistent adherence to his diet and medication. His wife, Mrs. Patel, and their son, Raj, live with him and provide some support, but their efforts are limited.

Active Family Participation: Mrs. Patel prepares family meals, attempting to incorporate diabetic‐friendly options, such as whole grains and vegetables, as recommended by the American Diabetes Association. However, she often reverts to traditional high‐carb recipes due to time constraints. Raj reminds his father to check his blood sugar occasionally and ensures insulin is stored properly, but he does not consistently monitor or record readings. They assist with some daily tasks, like helping Mr. Patel with grocery shopping, but their involvement lacks a structured routine.

Collaboration with Healthcare Providers: The family attends Mr. Patel's biannual endocrinologist appointments, listening to recommendations about insulin adjustments and lifestyle changes. Mrs. Patel keeps a basic log of his symptoms, which she shares during visits, but they rarely ask questions or seek clarification. Raj considered using a diabetes management app but has not implemented it, limiting their ability to share real‐time data with the healthcare team. Their collaboration is minimal and lacks proactive engagement.

Adaptability and Flexibility: When Mr. Patel experienced a hypoglycemic episode last month, Mrs. Patel called an ambulance and stayed with him at the hospital. Post‐discharge, they adjusted his meal schedule to include more frequent snacks to prevent future episodes. However, they have not sought further education on hypoglycemia management or made long‐term changes to their caregiving approach, such as attending diabetes education classes.

Missing Attribute—Holistic Support: The family focuses primarily on Mr. Patel's physical needs, neglecting his emotional and social well‐being. Mr. Patel often feels anxious about his condition, but Mrs. Patel and Raj avoid discussing his feelings, assuming he prefers privacy. They do not encourage him to join support groups or engage in social activities, leaving him feeling isolated. The family also does not address their own stress, which impacts their ability to provide consistent care.

This case includes active family participation, collaboration with healthcare providers, and some adaptability but lacks holistic support. The Patel family's focus on physical care without addressing emotional or social needs makes this a partial representation of ‘family care management.’

Contrary Case

The Smith family has a member, Mr. Smith, a 58‐year‐old construction worker diagnosed with chronic kidney disease (CKD) four years ago. Despite knowing about his condition, the family has not taken any steps to support his care, leaving him to manage his condition alone.

Lack of Active Family Participation: Mr. Smith's wife, Jane, works long hours and believes his CKD is his responsibility. She does not help with preparing low‐potassium meals or monitoring his fluid intake, which are critical for CKD management. Their adult children, living in different cities, rarely visit and do not assist with tasks like transportation to dialysis or medication management. Mr. Smith struggles to adhere to his treatment plan, often missing dialysis sessions due to lack of support.

Lack of Holistic Support: The family offers no emotional or social support. When Mr. Smith expresses frustration about his fatigue and dietary restrictions, Jane dismisses it, saying, ‘You just need to follow the doctor's orders.’ The children do not call or visit regularly, leaving Mr. Smith feeling isolated and unsupported, which exacerbates his depression.

Lack of Collaboration with Healthcare Providers: The family does not attend Mr. Smith's nephrologist appointments or communicate with his healthcare team. They are unaware of his treatment plan, including medication changes or dialysis schedules. Mr. Smith attends appointments alone, often forgetting key details, which leads to poor adherence and worsening health outcomes.

Lack of Adaptability and Flexibility: When Mr. Smith's kidney function declined, requiring thrice‐weekly dialysis, the family did not adjust their routines or offer support. Jane refused to take time off work, and the children did not visit to help during this critical period. Mr. Smith's condition deteriorated due to missed appointments and lack of home support, highlighting the absence of family involvement.

This case lacks all attributes of ‘family care management.’ The Smith family's complete disengagement from Mr. Smith's care illustrates what the concept is not, emphasizing the negative impact on his health outcomes.

Illegitimate Case

In a small rural community, Ms. Garcia, a neighbour of Mr. Hernandez, a 70‐year‐old man with Alzheimer's disease, claims to be ‘managing’ his care. Mr. Hernandez lives alone, and his family is not actively involved due to living far away.

Misuse of the Concept: Ms. Garcia visits Mr. Hernandez once or twice a week, bringing him homemade meals or checking if he's taken his medications. She occasionally reminds him to attend his doctor's appointments but does not coordinate with his healthcare team or monitor his condition regularly. Ms. Garcia tells community members she is ‘managing Mr. Hernandez's care,’ implying a significant role, but her involvement is sporadic and lacks structure. She is not a family member and does not provide comprehensive support, such as helping with daily activities or addressing his emotional needs.

This case misrepresents ‘family care management’ because Ms. Garcia is not a family member, and her actions do not meet any defining attributes. Her occasional visits lack active participation, holistic support, collaboration, or adaptability, making her use of the term illegitimate.

Antecedents

Family Capacity and Readiness

Before active family participation occurs, family members must be willing and have sufficient capacity to assume caregiving responsibilities. This willingness is often driven by familial bonds or a sense of duty (Rosland et al. 2010; Wolff et al. 2016). There are multiple barriers that impede the family's involvement in caring for patients with chronic illnesses, such as patients' attitudes, families insight into the disease, and the stigmatisation of patients with particular diseases (Dehbozorgi et al. 2022). Dijkman et al. categorized the factors influencing family involvement as follows: (1) patient characteristics, (2) family member characteristics, (3) family system characteristics, (4) physician's role, and (5) cultural influences (Dijkman et al. 2022). Limited access to formal healthcare services, such as professional caregiving or long‐term care facilities, often necessitates family involvement, particularly in resource‐constrained settings (WHO 2010; Reinhard 2019). Another barrier to family involvement in care is structural factors, including inadequate management. Management barriers encompassed poor follow‐up and patient support, bureaucratic health system regulations, and a goal‐oriented rather than holistic approach. Resource‐related barriers involved insufficient hospital facilities, staff shortages, high patient loads, time constraints, and inadequate mental health funding (Dehbozorgi et al. 2022).

Socioeconomic and Cultural Status

Socioeconomic conditions (e.g., financial resources, education) and cultural norms (e.g., expectations of family caregiving) influence a family's ability and motivation to engage in care management (Gitlin and Wolff 2011; Hailu et al. 2024). In a qualitative study, one of the emerging themes was a lack of knowledge among family members, as it was associated with inadequate family involvement (Mabunda 2025). Amanda & Knight reviewed literature on ethnic minority caregivers, emphasizing the key role of ethnicity and culture in Latino caregivers' stress and coping; they suggested that ethnicity and culture, shape stress appraisal, family support perception/use, and coping strategies (Aranda and Knight 1997). Falzarano et al. highlights how cultural values (especially familism) and social support networks influence caregiver outcomes (stress, burden, self‐efficacy) (Falzarano et al. 2021).

Consequences

Consequences are the outcomes resulting from family care management, which can be positive or negative for patients, caregivers, and healthcare systems. The following consequences are identified:

Patients' Outcomes

Family care management enhances patient adherence to treatment, self‐management behaviours, and overall quality of life, leading to better health outcomes (Rosland et al. 2010; Wang et al. 2024). In a study by Rosland et al., most respondents, regardless of their age or health status, reported that family members regularly sought to help them with their chronic disease self‐management; but on the other side, approximately a quarter of respondents perceived significant family barriers to their self‐management, especially those who were female, younger, and more educated (Rosland et al. 2010). In another study, which was a systematic review conducted with 30 studies, Rosland et al. reported that positive family behaviours (e.g., attention to symptoms, cohesion) were associated with better self‐management and clinical outcomes; negative family behaviours (overprotection, criticism, controlling) were associated with poorer outcomes (Rosland et al. 2012). In a study by Wang et al., a positive correlation between family care score and self‐management behaviour was found (Wang et al. 2025). In a study by Shi et al., it was found that family function may improve social networks among patients with chronic diseases in the community (Shi et al. 2024). Psihogios et al., in their meta‐analysis, reported that better family functioning (lower conflict, higher cohesion, better communication/problem‐solving) was significantly positively correlated with improved medical adherence in paediatric chronic conditions (Psihogios et al. 2018).

Caregivers' Outcomes

Caregiving can lead to emotional, physical, and mental health challenges for family members, including stress, depression, and reduced quality of life (Hailu et al. 2024; Lee and Kim 2023; Schulz and Beach 1999). Recent empirical findings have confirmed the complexity and consequences of care in families of children with chronic diseases, mainly in quality of life (Jones 2012; Padmaja et al. 2017; Toledano‐Toledano 2015); well‐being (Glajchen 2012; Koch and Jones 2018; Otis‐Green and Juarez 2012; Salomone et al. 2018); depression in family caregivers (Choi et al. 2016; Toledano‐Toledano and Contreras‐Valdez 2018; Zhou et al. 2018); caregiver burden in the families of children with chronic conditions (Fitzgerald et al. 2018; Olwit et al. 2018; Toledano‐Toledano and Domínguez‐Guedea 2019); and anxiety (Nagano et al. 2010; Sola‐Carmona et al. 2016; Toledano‐Toledano and Moral de la Rubia 2018); and resilience to disease as a process of positive adaptation despite the loss of health, which involves the development of vitality and skills to overcome the negative effects of adversity, risks, and vulnerability caused by disease (Toledano‐Toledano et al. 2019). Family care management contributes significant economic value through unpaid care but also imposes financial burdens on families due to out‐of‐pocket expenses and lost income (Caregiver Statistics: Demographics; Reinhard et al. 2012). Final Conceptual Definition

Synthesizing the defining attributes, antecedents, and consequences identified above, family care management of patients with chronic diseases can be conceptually defined as follows:Family care management of patients with chronic diseases is a dynamic, family‐driven process in which family members actively participate in providing holistic (physical, emotional, and social) support, collaborate systematically with healthcare providers, and adapt their roles and strategies over time to meet the evolving health needs of a relative living with a chronic condition.

Family care management of patients with chronic diseases is a dynamic, family‐driven process in which family members actively participate in providing holistic (physical, emotional, and social) support, collaborate systematically with healthcare providers, and adapt their roles and strategies over time to meet the evolving health needs of a relative living with a chronic condition.

While it generally leads to improved patient adherence, self‐management, and quality of life, it can also take a considerable emotional, physical, and financial toll on the family members.

Empirical Referents

The instruments below are proposed as potential empirical referents. Each measures a construct related to one of the identified attributes, but none was developed to operationalise the concept Family Care Management of Patients with Chronic Diseases as developed in this analysis.

Active Family Participation

Family Involvement in Care Questionnaire (FICQ): A 16‐item questionnaire with a 4‐point Likert scale, designed to measure family involvement in inpatient care, including tasks like emotional support and care coordination. It was developed and validated in the context of open‐heart surgery but is applicable to chronic disease settings due to its generic design (Drakenberg et al. 2023).

Holistic Support

Family Assessment Device (FAD): A 60‐item self‐report questionnaire based on the McMaster Model of Family Functioning, measuring six dimensions (problem‐solving, communication, roles, affective responsiveness, affective involvement, and behaviour control). It assesses the family's ability to provide holistic support, particularly the emotional and social aspects of care, in chronic disease management (Cong et al. 2022).

Collaboration With Healthcare Providers

Family Collaboration Scale (FCS): The Family Collaboration Scale (FCS) assesses collaboration between family members and nurses (Lindhardt et al. 2018). The original Danish version of the FCS consisted of 77 items (Lindhardt et al. 2008), which were later reduced to 42 (Lindhardt et al. 2018). The revised 42‐item FCS covers five dimensions of collaboration: (1) Influence on decisions (10 items); (2) Quality of contact with nurses (9 items); (3) Trust and its prerequisites (11 items); (4) Achieved information level (5 items); and (5) Influence on discharge (7 items) (Ahlström et al. 2022).

Adaptability and Flexibility

The Family Adaptability and Cohesion Evaluation Scales (FACES IV): This 62‐item instrument measures the family's flexibility and cohesion in response to chronic disease challenges. A family profile showing balanced adaptability and cohesion is a strong empirical indicator of the family's capacity to manage the evolving demands of care over time (Sarour and El Keshky 2022).

Discussion

The concept analysis of ‘family care management of patients with chronic diseases’ has provided a comprehensive framework for understanding this critical aspect of chronic illness care. The study identified four defining attributes, active family participation, holistic support, collaboration with healthcare providers, and adaptability and flexibility, which highlight the multifaceted role families play in managing chronic conditions. Additionally, the antecedents (family involvement and socioeconomic and cultural status), consequences (patients' outcomes, and caregivers' outcomes), and empirical referents (FICQ, FAD, FCS, FACES IV) were clarified to operationalise the concept.

This study's findings align with and expand upon existing literature on family caregiving in chronic diseases. For instance, Mestre et al. (2024) emphasize the evolving nature of family self‐care in chronic disease management, highlighting that active family participation and holistic support are essential for improving patient well‐being and reducing healthcare costs (Mestre et al. 2024). Similarly, Knafl and Deatrick (1986) describe the concept of ‘normalization,’ where families adapt to living with chronic illness, which resonates with the attribute of adaptability and flexibility identified in this analysis (Knafl and Deatrick 1986). These studies emphasize the dynamic and integral role of families in chronic care, supporting the defining attributes elucidated here. The attributes identified in this study can also be compared with those of (Sun et al. 2022), who described supervising the family's care situation, providing professional guidance, creating partnerships with family caregivers, and a broader philosophical foundation and management style. As expected, given the difference in perspective between the two studies, the attribute sets do not align closely overall. One notable exception is the correspondence between Sun et al.'s ‘creating partnerships with family caregivers’ and the present study's ‘collaboration with healthcare providers.’ These are best understood as mirror‐image attributes rather than identical ones: Sun et al. capture the provider‐initiated side of this relationship, while the present study captures the family‐enacted side. That this relational dimension emerged independently in both analyses, despite their opposite vantage points, suggests that provider–family collaboration is a comparatively stable feature of family care management, less sensitive to whose perspective is taken than the other attributes identified.

The consequences of family care management, particularly caregiver burden, are well‐documented in recent research. Kilmer et al. (2024) report that while caregiving can enhance patient outcomes, it often leads to increased stress and health decline among caregivers, corroborating this study's identified consequence of caregiver burden (Kilmer et al. 2024).

Regarding antecedents, socioeconomic and cultural factors significantly influence family caregiving. Hailu et al. (2024) and Park et al. (2018) note that while families want to take care of their patients, they face multiple challenges such as financial burden, and lack of formal support, aligning with this study's identified antecedents (Hailu et al. 2024; Park et al. 2018). This consistency suggests that healthcare system demands and cultural contexts are critical preconditions for family care management.

Empirical referents, such as FICQ, FAD, FCS, and FACES IV, may provide practical tools for assessing family dynamics. The inclusion of such referents enhances the applicability of this concept in clinical settings.

This concept analysis offers actionable insights across nursing practice, education, research, and policy.

Implications for Nursing Practice

In clinical practice, the proposed definition and its four attributes provide nurses with a structured framework to assess family involvement, identify gaps in care (such as the absence of holistic support illustrated in the borderline case above), and design individualised care plans that leverage family strengths. This framework is particularly relevant to settings in which sustained family involvement is central to care outcomes, including home healthcare and community/district nursing, primary care chronic disease management clinics, geriatric and memory clinics serving patients with dementia or Alzheimer's disease, oncology and palliative care services, and chronic disease programs in rural or resource‐constrained settings, where formal caregiving services are limited and families often assume a disproportionate share of care responsibility.

Implications for Nursing Education

In nursing education, integrating this concept into curricula can prepare students to systematically assess and support family caregivers rather than treating family involvement as incidental to patient care. Case‐based learning, such as the model, borderline, contrary, and illegitimate cases developed in this study, can illustrate how the presence or absence of specific attributes shapes patient and caregiver outcomes.

Implications for Research

For research, this analysis provides an operational definition together with a set of empirical referents (FICQ, FAD, FCS, FACES IV) that can be used to validate the concept across diverse populations and chronic conditions, and to guide the development or refinement of instruments specific to family care management rather than relying on proxies borrowed from general caregiving research. Future studies could examine the effectiveness of family‐centered interventions built around these four attributes in specific chronic diseases, such as Alzheimer's disease or COPD, and test whether the proposed definition holds across different cultural and healthcare system contexts.

Implications for Policy

At the policy level, a clarified definition of family care management can inform the design of caregiver‐support policies, such as structured discharge‐planning protocols that require assessment of family caregiving capacity, reimbursement mechanisms for family caregiver training, and eligibility criteria for respite care programs. Because socioeconomic and cultural status were identified as key antecedents in this analysis, policies aimed at reducing disparities in family care management, for example, targeted support for low‐resource or rural families, may be particularly consequential for equitable chronic disease outcomes.

Limitations

This concept analysis has certain limitations. Its findings are derived exclusively from a review of the literature, which may not fully capture the lived experiences and nuanced realities of family care management across highly diverse cultural and clinical settings. Furthermore, the strategic decision to restrict the search to English and Persian publications within selected databases (PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, Embase, and CINAHL), while necessary to manage the scope, may have resulted in the omission of relevant studies published in other languages or in non‐indexed sources. Consequently, while the identified attributes, antecedents, and consequences are comprehensive, they may not be universally exhaustive.

Conclusion

By developing this concept, this study establishes a vital framework for nursing practice, emphasizing the need for systematic family assessment and support. It offers a robust foundation for enhancing family‐centred care models, informs the education of nurses to better engage with and empower families, and paves the way for future research. Such research should focus on developing and validating specific assessment tools for this concept and designing targeted interventions to strengthen family care management capabilities, ultimately aiming to optimise health outcomes for both patients living with chronic diseases and their caregiver families.
