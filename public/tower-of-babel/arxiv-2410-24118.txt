Exact calculation of the large deviation function for k k -nary coalescence
R. Rajesh, V. Subashri, Oleg Zaboronski

Abstract
We study probabilities of rare events in the general coalescence process, k ​ A → ℓ ​ A kA\rightarrow\ell A , where k > ℓ k>\ell . For arbitrary k , ℓ k,\ell , by rewriting these probabilities in terms of an effective action, we derive the large deviation function describing the probability of finding N N particles at time t t , when starting with M M particles initially. Additionally, the most probable trajectory corresponding to a fixed rare event is derived.

CCA has been studied using different approaches. Historically, it has been studied using the Smoluchowski equation, a deterministic mean-field, integro-differential equation for the rate of change of number of clusters of a particular size or mass (see Refs.
[
13

15

The Smoluchowski equation ignores fluctuations, both spatial and stochastic. In lower dimensions, when spatial density fluctuations become dominant, the mass distribution in CCA has been studied using both analytical and numerical techniques
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21

. However, these approaches are limited to analyzing the mean or typical mass distribution and its statistical low moments. They do not provide information about the probabilities of rare or atypical events, nor do they explain the pathways that lead to such events.

Recently, we developed a formalism to calculate the large deviation function (LDF), which describes the probabilities for rare events, in CCA
[
22

27

28

An exact expression of the LDF of CCA was obtained for three standard collision kernels, the constant (rate is independent of mass), sum (rate is sum of masses) and product (rate is product of masses) kernels. The LDF has a singularity for the product kernel which is indicative of the sol-gel transition, wherein the Smoluchowski equation no longer conserves mass beyond a certain time. We could also determine the optimal evolution trajectories for a given rare event as solutions to the Euler-Lagrange equations that minimize the effective action
[
22
]

[
29

30

\ell

In this paper, we are interested in rare events in
k-
nary coalescence,
i.e.,
those events that occur at the tails of a probability distribution. The study of rare events is important because they could have significant impact despite their low likelihood of occurrence. Some common examples of rare events are natural disasters such as earthquakes and floods
[

[

[

[
47
]

The large deviation function, or rate function, captures information about large fluctuations (deviations) from the most probable or typical states of a system. The rate function can also be interpreted as a non-equilibrium generalization of entropy or free energy.

In this paper, we compute the LDF for
k-

2
Model

Consider a system of particles which evolves in time through the generalized coalescence process,

kA\xrightarrow{\lambda}\ell A,~~~~\ell<k,

(1)

where
A
denotes a particle. Equation (
1
) describes the aggregation of
k
particles into
\ell
particles at constant rate
\lambda
. In other words, we model the
k
-nary coalescence by a continuous time Markov chain on the state space
\mathbb{N}_{0}
consisting of non-negative integers, defined by the transition
N\rightarrow N-(k-\ell)
with the exponential rate
\lambda{N\choose k}
.

Each collision reduces the number of particles,
N(t)
, by
(k-\ell)
. The final absorbing state of this process contains
\ell,\ell+1,\dots k-1
particles, depending on the value of the initial number of particles,
M
.

In this paper, we study
P(M,N,t)
, the probability that exactly
N
particles remain at time
t
, given that there are
M
particles initially.
The number of collisions that have occurred,
C
, is related to
N
as

C=\frac{M-N}{k-\ell}.

(2)

3
Exact solution

It is possible to obtain the exact expression for
P(M,N,t)
as a sum of exponentials for arbitrary
k,\ell

[
30
]
. Such an exact answer is possible because the collision rate after
C
collisions is known exactly, unlike the situation when masses are assigned to particles and the collision rates depend on the mass distribution.
For a given
k,\ell
, after
i
collisions,
M-(k-\ell)i
particles remain, and the total rate of collision for the
i
-th collision,
\mathcal{R}_{i}
, is therefore given by

\mathcal{R}_{i}=\lambda{M-(k-\ell)(i-1)\choose k}.

(3)

Using the exponential time distribution for waiting times,
P(\Delta t_{i})=\mathcal{R}_{i}e^{-\mathcal{R}_{i}\Delta t_{i}}
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &P(M,N,t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\Delta t_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\Delta t_{2}...\int_{0}^{\infty}d\Delta t_{C+1}~~\mathcal{R}_{1}e^{-\mathcal{R}_{1}\Delta t_{1}}\\
&\mathcal{R}_{2}e^{-\mathcal{R}_{2}\Delta t_{2}}\dots\mathcal{R}_{C}e^{-\mathcal{R}_{C}\Delta t_{C}}e^{-\mathcal{R}_{C+1}\Delta t_{C+1}}\delta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{C+1}\Delta t_{i}-t\right),\end{aligned}

(4)

where
C
is as in Eq. (
2
).
The final waiting time
\Delta t_{C+1}
denotes the waiting time during which no collision occurs. The
\delta
-function constrains the sum of waiting times to the total time
t
. The Laplace transform of
\widetilde{P}(M,N,s)
, defined as

\widetilde{P}(M,N,s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dte^{-st}P(M,N,t),

(5)

is then

\widetilde{P}(M,N,s)=\prod_{i=1}^{C}\frac{\mathcal{R}_{i}}{\mathcal{R}_{i}+s}\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}_{C+1}+s}.

(6)

Doing the inverse Laplace transform, we obtain

P(M,N,t)=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{C}\mathcal{R}_{k}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{C+1}e^{-\mathcal{R}_{i}t}\prod_{j\neq i,\\
{j=1}}^{C+1}\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}_{j}-\mathcal{R}_{i}}.

(7)

Though the exact expression for
P(M,N,t)
can be obtained, it is not straightforward, either to derive the scaling for
N
and
t
with
M
in the large deviation limit, nor to derive the large deviation function directly from Eq. (
7
). Also, it is not possible to obtain the optimal trajectory for a given rare event. Instead, we will derive the large deviation function using the action formalism in Sec.
4
, and use the numerical evaluation of Eq. (
7
) as a check for our results. In the process, we will also derive the optimal paths for rare events.

4
Results

4.1
Master equation and effective action

The time evolution of
P(M,N,t)
is described by the master equation

\frac{dP(M,N,t)}{dt}=\lambda\left[\binom{N+k-\ell}{k}P(M,N+k-\ell,t)-\binom{N}{k}P(M,N,t)\right].

(8)

The first term on the right hand side of Eq. (
8
) is a gain term, which describes the creation of a state with
N
particles, due to the aggregation of
k
particles from a state with
N+k-\ell
particles. The second term is a loss term, which describes the aggregation of
k
particles from a state with
N
particles. We now rewrite the calculation of
P(M,N,t)
in terms of an effective action using the DPZO procedure
[
22
,
48
,
25
]
. Let

\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{N^{\prime}=0}^{M}P(M,N^{\prime},t)\ket{N^{\prime}},

(9)

where
\ket{N}
is the state with
N
particles which is acted upon by creation and annihilation operators
a
and
a^{\dagger}
,
and the number-of-particles operator
\hat{N}:=a^{\dagger}a
as follows:

\displaystyle a\ket{N}=N\ket{N-1},

(10)

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}\ket{N}=\ket{N+1},

(11)

\displaystyle\hat{N}\ket{N}=N\ket{N},

(12)

\displaystyle[a,a^{\dagger}]=1.

(13)

It is also useful to notice that
\braket{N^{\prime}|N}=N!\delta_{N^{\prime},N}
.
In terms of
\ket{\psi(t)}
, the master equation Eq. (
8
) can be rewritten as

\frac{d\ket{\psi(t)}}{dt}=-\widehat{H}(a,a^{\dagger})\ket{\psi(t)},

(14)

where

\widehat{H}(a,a^{\dagger})=-\frac{\lambda}{k!}(a^{\dagger\ell}-a^{\dagger k})a^{k}.

(15)

Equation (
14
) has the solution
\ket{\psi(t_{f})}=e^{-\widehat{H}t_{f}}\ket{\psi(0)}
, where
\ket{\psi(0)}=\ket{M}
and
t_{f}
is the final time. Multiplying Eq. (
9
) on the left with
\bra{N}
, it is easy to see that

P(M,N,t)=\frac{\braket{N|\psi(t)}}{N!}.

(16)

Briefly, to find a path integral representation of Eq. (
16
), the evolution operator is first represented as an infinite
product using Trotter’s formula,

e^{-\widehat{H}(a^{\dagger},a)t}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(1-\widehat{H}(a^{\dagger},a)\frac{t}{n}\right)^{n}.

Next the partition of the identity operator
I
in terms of the complete set of eigenfunctions of the annihilation operator
a
is inserted
between every pair of factors of
\left(1-\widehat{H}(a^{\dagger},a)\frac{t}{n}\right)
entering the Trotter formula,

\displaystyle e^{-\widehat{H}(a^{\dagger},a)t}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(1-\widehat{H}(a^{\dagger},a)\frac{t}{n}\right)\textbf{I}\ldots\textbf{I}\left(1-\widehat{H}(a^{\dagger},a)\frac{t}{n}\right),

\displaystyle\mbox{where }\textbf{I}=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{dzd\tilde{z}}{\pi}e^{-|z|^{2}}\ket{z}\bra{z},~\ket{z}=e^{za^{\dagger}}\ket{0}.

Finally, the matrix elements
\bra{z^{\prime}}\left(1-\widehat{H}(a^{\dagger},a)\frac{t}{n}\right)\ket{z}
are calculated
using the representation of the operators
a,a^{\dagger}
in the basis of
(\ket{z})_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}
(the so-called holomophic representation):

\ket{z}=e^{zw},~a^{\dagger}=w\cdot,~a=\frac{\partial}{\partial w},\braket{z|z^{\prime}}=e^{\overline{z}z^{\prime}},~w,z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}

where the inner product is defined for a pair of holomorphic functions
f,g
as
\braket{f|g}=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{dwd\bar{w}}{\pi}e^{-w\overline{w}}\overline{f(w)}g(w)
.
The final answer is

P(M,N,t)=\int\mathcal{D}\tilde{z}(t)\mathcal{D}z(t)e^{-S(z,\tilde{z},{t})},

(17)

where the action is given by

S(z,\tilde{z},{t})=\int_{0}^{t}dt\left[\tilde{z}\dot{z}+\lambda H(z,\tilde{z})-N\ln z(t_{f})\delta(t_{f}-t)-M\ln\tilde{z}(0)\delta(t)\right]+M+\ln N!,

(18)

and the effective Hamiltonian
H
is

H(z,\tilde{z})=-\frac{\big(\tilde{z}^{\ell}-\tilde{z}^{k}\big)z^{k}}{k!}.

(19)

4.2
Existence of a large deviation principle

Defining
z\to zM^{\alpha}
,
\tilde{z}\to\tilde{z}M^{\beta}
and
\tau=\lambda tM^{\gamma}
, and substituting in the action, we find that the choice
\alpha=1
,
\beta=0
and
\gamma=k-1
keeps the form of the action unchanged. The scaled action is then

\displaystyle S(z,\tilde{z},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\tau})=M\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}\Big[\tilde{z}\dot{z}+E(z,\tilde{z})\Big]-\phi\ln z(\tau)-\ln\tilde{z}(0)+z(0)\tilde{z}(0)+\phi\ln\frac{\phi}{e}\right],

(20)

where
\phi=N/M
.
In the limit
M,N\to\infty
, keeping
\phi=N/M
and
~\tau=\lambda M^{k-1}t
fixed, since the action in Eq. (
20
) is proportional to
M
,
the functional integral Eq. (
17
) is dominated by the minimum of the action. Thus, there exists a large deviation principle

\lim_{M\to\infty}-\frac{\ln P(M,\phi M,\tau[\lambda M^{k-1}]^{-1})}{M}=f(\phi,\tau),

(21)

where
f(\phi,\tau):=\min_{z,\tilde{z}}S(z,\tilde{z},\tau)
is the rate function. We note that the number of particles formed after each collision,
\ell
, does not appear in the scaling of time.

The Euler-Lagrange equations corresponding to the minimimum of the action, Eq. (
20
) are given by

\displaystyle\dot{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}\Big(\ell\tilde{z}^{\ell-1}-k\tilde{z}^{k-1}\Big)z^{k}+\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{z}(0)}-z(0)\right)\delta(\tau),

(22)

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{z}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{k}{k!}\Big(\tilde{z}^{\ell}-\tilde{z}^{k}\Big)z^{k-1}-\frac{\phi\delta(\tau-\tau_{f})}{z(\tau_{f})}=\frac{kE}{z}-\frac{\phi\delta(\tau-\tau_{f})}{z(\tau_{f})}.

(23)

By integrating Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
) about
\tau=0
and
\tau=\tau_{f}
, we obtain the boundary conditions to be

\displaystyle z(0)\tilde{z}(0)=n(0)=1,

(24)

\displaystyle z(\tau_{f})\tilde{z}(\tau_{f})=n(\tau_{f})=\phi,

(25)

where
n(\tau)=z(\tau)\tilde{z}(\tau)
is the fraction of particles at time
\tau
.
Note that the Euler-Lagrange equations conserve energy
E:=H(z(\tau),\tilde{z}(\tau))
,
i.e.,

dE/d\tau=0
. Using
Eq. (
19
), one can rewrite the energy in terms of
n(\tau)
to be

E=\frac{n(\tau)^{k}-n(\tau)^{\ell}z(\tau)^{k-\ell}}{k!}.

(26)

Knowing the values of the boundary conditions
n(0)
and
n(\tau_{f})
, Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
) allows us to write
z(0)
and
z(\tau_{f})
, and consequently
\tilde{z}(0)
and
\tilde{z}(\tau_{f})
in terms of
E
and
\phi
.
Additionally, integrating the first term in Eq. (
20
) by parts, and using Eq. (
23
), the rate function for general
k,\ell
is

\displaystyle f(\phi,\tau)=-(k-1)E\tau-\!\!\frac{\phi}{k-\ell}\left[\ln\big(\phi^{k}-k!E\big)-\ell\ln\phi\right]+\frac{1}{k-\ell}\ln\big(1-k!E\big)+\phi\ln\phi,

(27)

where
E
is a function of
\phi
and
\tau
. It is determined by the equation for the fraction of surviving particles
n

which follows from the Euler-Lagrange equations (
22

We will refer to the this equation

4.3
The instanton equation

The instanton trajectory for
n(\tau)=z(\tau)\tilde{z}(\tau)
that minimizes the action is is derived from Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
) to be

\frac{dn}{d\tau}=(k-\ell)\Big[E-\frac{n^{k}}{k!}\Big],

(28)

which needs to be solved subject to the initial conditon
n(0)=1
and the final condition
n(\tau_{f})=\phi
. As the equation is of the first order, the final condition yields an equation for the ’instanton energy’
E
.
Equation (
28
) implies that if
\tau\to(k-\ell)\tau
, then the instanton trajectory for fixed
k
and different
\ell
should be identical.

When
E=0
, Eq. (
28
) for

n(\tau)=\Big[1+\frac{(k-\ell)(k-1)\tau}{k!}\Big]^{1/1-k},

(29)

It is seen that the typical trajectory achieves the fraction
\phi
of the surviving particles at the time

\tau_{typ}=\frac{k!}{(k-\ell)(k-1)}\left(\phi^{1-k}-1\right).

It follows from (
28
) that
E>0
corresponds to rare events such that the time of reaching
\phi
is smaller than
\tau_{typ}
,

E<0
- rare events reaching
\phi

\tau_{typ}

The atypical trajectories corresponding to

\frac{dx}{d\tau}=-\frac{(k-\ell)e_{0}^{k-1}}{k!}\Big[x^{k}-1\Big],

where

and
e_{0}^{k}=k!E

\frac{1}{x^{k}(\tau)-1}=\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}(x(\tau)-\omega_{j})}=\sum_{m=1}^{k}\frac{A_{m}}{x(\tau)-\omega_{m}},

(31)

where
\omega_{j}=e^{\frac{2\pi ij}{k}}
and the coefficients
A_{m}
are complex.
Solving for
A_{m}

A_{m}=\oint_{\Gamma_{m}}\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\frac{1}{z^{k}-1}=\frac{\omega_{m}}{k}.

(32)

Here

Substituting Eq. (

\sum_{m=1}^{k}\omega_{m}\ln(x(\tau)-\omega_{m})=-\frac{(k-\ell)(k!E)^{k-1}\tau}{(k-1)!}+c,

where

\Omega_{i}=\omega_{i}(k!E)^{1/k}.

(35)

The instanton energy

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Omega_{i}\ln\frac{\phi-\Omega_{i}}{1-\Omega_{i}}=-k(k-\ell)E\tau_{f},

(36)

6
Asymptotic analysis

It is possible to calculate exact asymptotics for the large deviation in the limits
\tau_{f}>>\tau_{typ}
(anomalously slow evolution) and

\tau_{f}<<\tau_{typ}
(anomalously fast evolution). Let us first consider the case
\tau_{f}>>\tau_{typ}
.
In this limit, let us seek the solution to (
36
) in the form

E=\frac{\phi^{k}}{k!}(1-\epsilon(\tau_{f})),

(37)

where
\epsilon(\tau_{f})<<1
. Substituting this Ansatz into (
36
) one finds

\epsilon(\tau_{f})=\exp\left(-\frac{k(k-\ell)\phi^{k-1}}{k!}\tau_{f}+O(1)\right).

(38)

Substituting (

7
Summary and discussion

To summarize, we derived the large deviation function for the general coalescence process,
kA\to\ell A
, for arbitrary
k>\ell
using the path integral approach. The solution minimizing the action allowed us to determine the optimal trajectory for each rare event.

For the reaction
kA\to\ell A
, it is possible to write an exact expression for
P(M,N,t)
as a sum of exponentials. However, it is not straightforward to derive the large deviation function from this expression, neither are the scaling variables obvious. Using the Doi-Peliti-Zeldovich method helps us to circumvent these issues. First the scaling variables become obvious, second we are able to determine the exact expression for the large deviation function and third, the optimal trajectories for rare and typical events can be obtained.

The formalism used in this paper is generalizable to reaction diffusion systems in higher dimensions, where a term related to transport of the clusters, such as a diffusion term would appear in the Euler Lagrange equations. Solvability remains as issue and a promising area for future research. The formalism can also be generalized to coalescence with input
