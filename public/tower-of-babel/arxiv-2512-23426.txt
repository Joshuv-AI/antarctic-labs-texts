Direct Diffusion Score Preference Optimization via Stepwise Contrastive Policy-Pair Supervision
Dohyun Kim, Seungwoo Lyu, Seung Wook Kim, Paul Hongsuck Seo

Abstract
Diffusion models have achieved impressive results in generative tasks such as text-to-image synthesis, yet they often struggle to fully align outputs with nuanced user intent and maintain consistent aesthetic quality. Existing preference-based training methods like Diffusion Direct Preference Optimization help address these issues but rely on costly and potentially noisy human-labeled datasets. In this work, we introduce Direct Diffusion Score Preference Optimization (DDSPO), which—when winning/losing policies are accessible—directly derives per-timestep supervision from these policies. Unlike prior methods that operate solely on final samples, DDSPO provides dense, transition-level signals across the denoising trajectory. In practice, we avoid reliance on labeled data by automatically generating preference signals using a pretrained reference model: we contrast its outputs when conditioned on original prompts versus semantically degraded variants. This practical strategy enables effective score-space preference supervision without explicit reward modeling or manual annotations. Empirical results demonstrate that DDSPO improves text-image alignment and visual quality, outperforming or matching existing preference-based methods while requiring significantly less supervision.

As an alternative, human preference annotations provide a lightweight yet expressive means of encoding such qualitative information in the form of ranked comparisons.

To overcome these limitations, we propose

Unlike prior approaches that define preferences over final samples
(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})

This is implemented by extending preference labels to tuples

derived from the contrastive policy pair,
i.e
.
, preferred and dispreferred denoising policies.

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{w}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{l}

By deriving per-timestep targets from the contrastive policy pair,
DDSPO enables richer supervision across denoising steps and reduces reliance on final outputs
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
.
For practical use of DDSPO, we further introduce an instantiation of the contrastive policy pair using a pretrained reference model conditioned on original and perturbed prompts, which define the preferred and dispreferred denoising behaviors, respectively.
Given an input prompt
\boldsymbol{c}
and its semantically degraded variant
\boldsymbol{c}^{-}
, we construct pseudo preference signals as follows: the reference model’s score prediction
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})
is treated as the target for preferred denoising behavior, while
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c}^{-})
represents a dispreferred direction.
The model is then trained to match the preferred score and to actively avoid the dispreferred score.
This stepwise contrastive signal allows DDSPO to steer the model toward desirable generation behavior without any explicit preference annotations or reward modeling, making the method directly applicable even when human-labeled preference data is unavailable.

As a result, DDSPO consistently improves both text–image alignment and aesthetic quality—achieving performance competitive with or superior to existing preference-based approaches—demonstrating its practicality and broad applicability.
Our main contributions are threefold:

•

We propose DDSPO, a preference optimization framework that enables direct supervision over intermediate denoising steps in score space through a contrastive policy pair.

•

We introduce a practical approach for constructing stepwise preference signals without any labeled datasets or reward models by leveraging prompt perturbation and a pretrained reference model.

•

Extensive experiments show that DDSPO performs on par with or surpasses existing approaches that rely on external preference datasets or reward models, despite requiring neither.

3.1
Preliminary

Direct Preference Optimization

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
41
)
is a learning framework that aligns model outputs with human preferences by directly optimizing on pairwise preference data.
Given a conditioning input
\boldsymbol{c}
, we assume access only to preference-labeled pairs
(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
,
where
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}
denotes the preferred sample and
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}
the dispreferred one.
The interpretation of
\boldsymbol{c}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
depends on the task, where
\boldsymbol{c}
is a text caption and
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
is a synthesized image in text-to-image generation. Such preferences may reflect various notions of quality—such as semantic alignment, aesthetic appeal, or factual correctness—depending on the task context.

These preferences can be formalized using the Bradley–Terry model
2
, which defines the following distribution:

\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}\succ\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{c})=\sigma\left(r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w})-r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})\right),

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}\succ\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}
denotes that
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}
is preferred over
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}
,
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function and
r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
is a latent reward function that is difficult to access directly.
DPO reparameterizes this setup to directly optimize the model distribution
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
.
It begins from the RLHF-style KL-regularized objective, which maximizes reward while constraining the learned distribution to stay close to a fixed reference distribution
p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
:

\max_{\theta}\,\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\sim p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{c})}\left[r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\right]\\
-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{c})\,\|\,p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{c})\right)

(2)

where
\beta
is a parameter that controls regularization strength.
This objective admits a closed-form solution where the optimal distribution
p^{*}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
is proportional to the reference distribution
p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
scaled by an exponential of the reward:

p^{*}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{c})\propto p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{c})\cdot\exp(r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})/\beta)
.
This reformulation reveals that the reward function can be captured by the ratio between the optimized policy and the reference distribution, eliminating the need to model
r(\boldsymbol{c},x_{0})
explicitly.
This leads to the DPO training objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}}\log\sigma\bigg(\\
\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{c})}-\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{c})}\bigg).

(3)

DPO for Diffusion Models

Applying DPO to diffusion models introduces a unique challenge: directly computing the log-likelihood ratio
\log\tfrac{p_{\theta}(x_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(x_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{c})}
is intractable due to the need to marginalize over all possible diffusion trajectories
x_{1:T}
that generate
x_{0}
. To circumvent this,
(
52
)
reformulates the objective over entire denoising paths and approximate the reverse process
p_{\theta}(x_{1:T}\mid x_{0})
using the forward noising process
q(x_{1:T}\mid x_{0})
.
This reparameterization enables training in the
score space
, where models learn to predict the noise (or equivalently, the score function
(
15
;
51
)
) that guides the denoising process:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})\sim\mathcal{D},t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\\
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}),\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})\end{subarray}}\log\sigma\bigg(-\beta\cdot\big[

\displaystyle\quad\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{w}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{w}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle-(\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{l}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{l}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2})\big]\bigg)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\ast}=\alpha_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{\ast}+\sigma_{t}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\ast}

is drawn from
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\ast}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{\ast})
,
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\ast}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
.
The term

\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{w}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{w}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}

penalizes the student whenever its predicted score at
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}
is farther
from the target
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{w}
than the reference model, thereby pushing it
closer to
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{w}
.
The analogous term for
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}
pushes the student away from
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{l}

relative to the reference, producing a pairwise preference signal.
However, both
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{w}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{l}
originate from samples

\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\ast}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\ast}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{\ast})
, so supervision at timestep
t

ultimately stems only from the final samples
(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})
rather than providing supervision over the intermediate denoising steps.

3.2
Direct Diffusion Score Preference Optimization

We now consider a setting in which preference is defined not over final generated samples,
but over a transition between intermediate denoising steps.
Crucially, rather than assuming access only to preference-labeled final samples
(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
, we also assume access to preference-labeled denoising transitions at intermediate steps.
Let
p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
and
p_{\star}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
denote the winning and losing denoising policies, forming a contrastive policy pair.
Sampling from these policies yields tuples
((\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}),(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l})\mid\boldsymbol{c})
.
This enables direct preference of denoising behavior at each timestep of the diffusion process.

Following the Bradley–Terry formulation in
Eq.

1
, we extend the preference supervision from final outputs
(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
to denoising transitions at intermediate steps, using tuples of the form
((\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}),(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l})\mid\boldsymbol{c})
.
The reward function is accordingly redefined as
r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})
, and preferences are modeled over entire denoising transitions.
By applying the same derivation steps as in
Eqs.

1
,
2
and
3
, and extending the supervision to denoising transitions at arbitrary diffusion timesteps
t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)
the loss can be analogously formulated as:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{c}\sim\mathcal{D}(c),\,t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}),\,(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l})}\\
\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\log\!\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{c})}-\beta\log\!\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{c})}\right),

where
(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w})\sim p^{w}_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{c}),\,(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l})\sim p^{l}_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
.
Since the
joint transition distribution
p^{w}_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
is generally intractable, we approximate it as

p^{w}_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{c})\approx q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w})\,p^{w}_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{c})
,
where
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}\sim\mathcal{D}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w})
, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\sim p^{w}_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{c})
.
This provides a practical sampling scheme in which the preferred transition is constructed by forward noising followed by preference-guided denoising.
An analogous approximation is applied to the dispreferred transition
p^{l}_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
.
Under this approximation, we derive the following score-space objective
(
51
)
; see Supp. Mat. for the full derivation:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDSPO}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})\sim\mathcal{D},\,\boldsymbol{c}\sim\mathcal{D}(c),\,t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,\\
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}),\,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})\end{subarray}}\\
\log\sigma\bigg(-\beta\cdot\big[\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{w}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{w}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}\\
-\big(\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{l}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{l}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}\big)\big]\bigg).

(4)

Here,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{w}_{\star}
,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{l}_{\star}
denote denoising score targets obtained from the contrastive policy pair—
p^{w}_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{c})
and
p^{l}_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{c})
—thereby enabling direct preference optimization in diffusion score space at each timestep.

3.3
DDSPO in Practical Use

contrastive policy pair
In practice, direct supervision over intermediate transition preferences, in the form of denoising score pairs
(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{w},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{l})
, is rarely available.
To address this, we introduce a practical and effective strategy for generating such signals based on prompt perturbation.
Specifically, we treat the denoising score predicted from the original prompt
\boldsymbol{c}
as the preferred signal, and construct a dispreferred counterpart by generating a corrupted version of the prompt
\boldsymbol{c}^{-}
and computing the corresponding denoising score.
Note that the type of perturbation applied to the prompt may vary depending on the specific application of preference optimization (
e.g
.
, text-to-image alignment or aesthetic quality).
These signals can be obtained using a pretrained reference model.
In this setup, the noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})
guided by
\boldsymbol{c}
is considered positively aligned with the intended semantics, while the noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c}^{-})
from
\boldsymbol{c}^{-}
is dispreferred, as it reflects guidance from an incomplete or misleading prompt.
By plugging these noises as supervision targets into the DDSPO objective in
Eq.

4
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})\sim\mathcal{D},\,(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{c}^{-})\sim\mathcal{D}(c),t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,\\
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}),\,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})\end{subarray}}\log\sigma\Bigr(-\beta\cdot[

\displaystyle\quad\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle-\big(\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c}^{-})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c}^{-})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}\big)]\Bigr)

(5)

Here, the term
\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}
simplifies to a standard distillation loss, as the second term vanishes.
This encourages the student model to closely follow the reference without degrading its original capabilities.
In contrast, the term
\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c}^{-})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c}^{-})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}
penalizes the model when its prediction moves closer to the degraded direction
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c}^{-})
than the reference model’s own output does.
Together, these two terms encourage the model to retain useful denoising capabilities while avoiding alignment with poor or corrupted guidance signals.
Note that while prompt perturbation has been used in other domains to construct synthetic preference data, DDSPO is the first to extend this idea to step-wise score pairs, enabling supervision at every denoising step.

Training Pipeline
We focus on a setup without preference-labeled image pairs, as

Thus,
we construct an image preference dataset

\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l},\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{c}^{-})\}

by generating
(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})
from the reference model for each prompt pair
(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{c}^{-})
.

, draw

\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\!\sim\!q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w})
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\!\sim\!q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})
via the forward process.
We then compute the denoising score targets

and

from the frozen reference model, and optimize

Efficient DDSPO without Image Preference Pair Dataset
Motivated by recent findings
(
21
)
that effective score distillation does not require strict pairing between the noised image
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
and the conditioning prompt
\boldsymbol{c}
(
e.g
.
, at large
t
the distribution of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
is approximately standard Gaussian regardless of
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
, and reverse generation is driven by the conditioning prompt, so strict prompt–image pairing is unnecessary to obtain meaningful signals), we introduce an efficient variant of DDSPO that avoids
explicitly collecting dispreferred images.
In practical settings where no ground-truth dispreferred images exist, a naïve
approach would be to generate a dispreferred sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}
for every perturbed
prompt
(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{c}^{-})
, but this is computationally expensive. Instead,
we can reuse a randomly sampled positive image
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{+}
(from another prompt) as a surrogate for
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}
, obtain
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{+})
via the forward process, and estimate the losing direction using the perturbed prompt
\boldsymbol{c}^{-}
(
e.g
.
, compute
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c}^{-})
). Although this introduces a prompt–image mismatch on the losing branch, the conditioning still provides a meaningful negative direction—especially at larger timesteps—and aggregation across timesteps and prompts yields informative supervision. In practice, this design cuts the cost of collecting additional dispreferred samples, showing the flexibility of DDSPO in positive-only settings.

3.4
Relationship to Previous Work

Diffusion DPO as a Special Case of DDSPO
While DDSPO uses

p_{\star}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{\ast}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{c})

as the contrastive policy pair,
Diffusion DPO is a special case of DDSPO
that defines
p_{\star}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{\ast}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{c})\;\triangleq\;q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{\ast}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{\ast})
.
This shifts the notion of preference toward the final outputs, making the model heavily reliant on
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}
.
Consequently, supervision is broadcast from these final samples to all timesteps and, especially at large
t
, can be broad and unspecific. In contrast, DDSPO supervises each local transition
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\!\to\!\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}
under the winning or losing denoising policies
p_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})
, yielding more informative, dense, and localized timestep-specific signals and reducing sensitivity to noise in the final samples.

Relationship to DSPO
DSPO
(
68
)
reinterprets preference alignment through a
score-matching formulation by constructing a preference-adjusted target score—
formed by combining the data score with a preference-derived score term—and
fine-tuning the pretrained diffusion model to match this target.
Concretely, DSPO defines a score-matching style objective of the form

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}),\,\boldsymbol{c},\,t}A(t)\Big\|(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{w})\\
\quad-\beta\Big(1-\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})-r_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\big)\Big)\\
\quad\quad\cdot(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c}))\Big\|_{2}^{2}.

(6)

where
(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})\sim\mathcal{D},\,\boldsymbol{c}\sim\mathcal{D}(c),\,t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}),\,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})
.
Here,
A(t)
is a timestep-dependent weighting function determined by the diffusion noise schedule,
and
r_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})
is defined as

r_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})=\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2},

where denoising score target
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
is derived by approximating

p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\!\mid\!\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})

with the forward process
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
,
so that the preference signal at timestep
t
is still induced from preference-labeled final samples via a forward-process approximation.

In contrast, DDSPO removes reliance on the forward process by deriving preference supervision directly
from the contrastive denoising policies—
p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\!\mid\!\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{c})
and

p_{\star}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l}\!\mid\!\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{c}^{-})
—which provide per-step, policy-grounded preference signals.
We can incorporate our per-step preference supervision into the DSPO by redefining

r_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})=\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{w}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{l}
denote denoising score targets derived from the contrastive policy pair—the winning and losing denoising policies
p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{c})
and
p_{\star}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{c})
, respectively, as defined in DDSPO.
In practice, these targets can be obtained from the reference model as

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c}^{-})
.
This reformulation extends DSPO to operate in a stepwise manner, providing dense, per-step supervision analogous to DDSPO, with the detailed derivation and empirical results are provided in Supp. Mat. and
Section

4.2
, respectively.

Relationship to SPO
Similar to DDSPO, Step-by-step Preference Optimization (SPO)
(
29
)
also introduces stepwise supervision along the denoising trajectory.
At each timestep, SPO samples multiple candidates from a shared noisy latent
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
and selects a win–lose pair
(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l})
based on a step-aware reward model pretrained to assess visual quality.
Unlike prior DPO methods that propagate preference labels from final sample pairs across entire trajectories, SPO determines preference locally within a single denoising step using this pretrained reward model, enabling the model to identify preferences focusing on subtle, fine-grained visual details—such as texture, lighting, or color tone—rather than high-level semantic or layout features.
This design effectively enhances image aesthetics by emphasizing nuanced human aesthetic preferences driven by these subtle visual cues;
however, as reported in the original paper
(
29
)
, SPO offers limited improvement for text–image alignment and cannot be directly applied to deterministic flow-matching models
(
31
;
56
)
due to its reliance on stochastic candidate sampling.
In contrast, DDSPO achieves stepwise supervision without such restrictions by directly deriving preference signals from contrastive policy pairs generated via prompt perturbations.
This formulation is effective for both aesthetic enhancement and text–to-image alignment, and can be applied to both diffusion and flow-based generative models. Moreover, unlike SPO, DDSPO requires no auxiliary reward model or labeled preference data, as prompt perturbations intrinsically yield dense, stepwise training signals that enable free and scalable preference alignment. We empirically validate these properties across diverse architectures and tasks in
Section

4
.

5
Conclusion

We introduce DDSPO, a novel preference optimization framework that extends Diffusion DPO by supervising each denoising step with preferred/dispreferred targets derived from winning and losing policies. In practice, we construct these targets label-free using prompt perturbations and a pretrained reference model, eliminating manual annotations and reward models.
Our experiments show that DDSPO consistently improves text-image consistency and visual fidelity, offering a scalable and practical approach within the broader landscape of preference-based training methods.

Derivation

Diffusion Model

Denoising diffusion probabilistic models
15
;
49
;
51
define a generative process that reverses a fixed forward noising process applied to data drawn from
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
. This reverse process is modeled as a discrete-time Markov chain over latent variables
\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}
, with the joint distribution defined as
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T})=p(\boldsymbol{x}_{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
.
Each transition
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
is modeled as a Gaussian distribution:

p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1};\mu_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}),\sigma_{t|t-1}^{2}\frac{\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\boldsymbol{I}).

(7)

During training, the model is optimized to approximate the true reverse process by minimizing a variational bound on the negative log-likelihood. In practice, this reduces to predicting the noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
added at each timestep, leading to a simplified objective of the form:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}=\mathbb{E}_{t,\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{I})}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is the noised sample at timestep
t
, and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}
is the model’s prediction of the noise.

Direct Preference Optimization

In

1
, reward model
r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0})
can be parameterized by a nerual net work
\phi
and estimated via maximum likelihood training for binary classification:

L_{\text{BT}}(\phi)=-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0}}\left[\log\sigma\left(r_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0})-r_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})\right)\right]

(9)

And in RLHF objective
Eq.

2
, the uique global optimal solution
p^{*}_{\theta}
takes the form:

p^{*}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})=p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})\exp\left(r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})/\beta\right)/Z(\boldsymbol{c})

(10)

where
Z(\boldsymbol{c})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})\exp\left(r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})/\beta\right)
is the partition function.
Hence, the reward fucntion is rewritten as

r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\beta\log\frac{p^{*}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})}+\beta\log Z(\boldsymbol{c})

(11)

Using
Eq.

9
the reward objective becomes:

L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)\!=\!-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})}-\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0}|\boldsymbol{c})}\right)\right]

(12)

Diffusion DPO

Diffusion DPO extend the DPO formulation to diffusion models by defining the reward function over full denoising trajectories. Specifically, they assume that the reward is given by the expected trajectory-level score:

r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{c})}\left[R(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T})\right].

(13)

Under this definition, the RLHF objective from
Eq.

2
can be reformulated as:

\displaystyle\max_{p_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{c}\sim\mathcal{D}_{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}\sim p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{c})}\left[r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\right]-\beta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{c})\|p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{c})\right].

(14)

Similar with
Eq.

10

p_{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{c})
has a unique closed-form solution:

\displaystyle p_{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{c})=p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{c})\exp(R(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T})/\beta)/Z(\boldsymbol{c}),

(15)

where
Z(\boldsymbol{c})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{c})\exp\left(r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})/\beta\right)
is the partition function.
The reparameterized form of the trajectory-level reward is given by

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T})

\displaystyle=\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{c})}+\beta\log Z(\boldsymbol{c}).

(16)

Inserting this expression into the definition of

Eq.

13
leads to

r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\beta\mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{c})}\left[\log\frac{p^{*}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{c})}\right]+\beta\log Z(\boldsymbol{c}).

Substituting the resulting reward into the Bradley–Terry objective
Eq.

9
yields a maximum likelihood formulation for diffusion models. Since the partition function cancels in the pairwise setting, the per-example loss becomes:

L_{\text{Diffusion-DPO}}(\theta)=-\log\sigma\left(\beta{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{1:T}\sim p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{c}),\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{1:T}\sim p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0},\boldsymbol{c})}}\left[\log\!\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0:T}\mid\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0:T}\mid\boldsymbol{c})}-\log\!\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0:T}\mid\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0:T}\mid\boldsymbol{c})}\right]\right)

(17)

Here,
\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0}
are drwan from a static dataset, and the conditioning input
\boldsymbol{c}
is omitted for clarity.

To address the intractability of directly sampling from the true reverse process
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1:T}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
, the Diffusion DPO formulation approximates it using the forward posterior
q(\boldsymbol{x}_{1:T}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
. Under this approximation, the trajectory-level preference objective can be rewritten as an expectation over individual timesteps. Specifically, comparisons between full trajectories are expressed as averages over comparisons between corresponding reverse transitions at each timestep, yielding the following expression:

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{\text{Diffusion-DPO}}(\theta)&=-\log\sigma\Biggl(\beta T\mathbb{E}_{t}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1,t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}),\;\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1,t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})}}\Bigl[\\
&\quad\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}-\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}\Bigr]\Biggr)\end{split}

(18)

To derive a tractable approximation, Jensen’s inequality is applied to move the logarithm outside the expectation, yielding an upper bound on the original loss:

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{\text{Diffusion-DPO}}(\theta)&\leq-\mathbb{E}_{t,\,\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}),\,\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})}\log\sigma\Biggl(\beta T\cdot\\
&\quad\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}),\,\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})}\Bigl[\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}-\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}\Bigr]\Biggr)\\
&=-\mathbb{E}_{t,\,\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}),\,\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})}\log\sigma\Biggl(\beta T\cdot\\
&\quad\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}),\,\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})}\Bigl[\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{q(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0,t})}-\log\frac{q(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0,t})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}\\
&-\left(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{q(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0,t})}-\log\frac{q(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0,t})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}\right)\Bigr]\Biggr)\\
&=-\mathbb{E}_{t,\,\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}),\,\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})}\log\sigma\Biggl(-\beta T\cdot\Bigl(\\
&\quad\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(q(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0,t})\,\|\,p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\right)-\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(q(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0,t})\,\|\,p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\right)\\
&\quad-\left(\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(q(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0,t})\,\|\,p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\right)-\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(q(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0,t})\,\|\,p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\right)\right)\Bigr)\Biggr)\end{split}

(19)

Using the Gaussian parameterization of the reverse process (
Eq.

7
), the above loss simplifies to:

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{\text{Diffusion-DPO}}(\theta)&\leq-\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})\sim\mathcal{D},t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}),\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})}\log\sigma\bigg(-\beta\cdot\big[\\
&\quad\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{w}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{w}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-(\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{l}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{l}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2})\big]\bigg)\end{split}

where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{w},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{l}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
thus
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\alpha_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+\sigma_{t}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
.
\lambda_{t}=\alpha_{t}^{2}/\sigma_{t}^{2}
is a signal-to-noise ratio term
22
, in practice, the reweighting assigns each term the same weight
15
.

DDSPO

The DDSPO objective reformulates preference supervision in the diffusion framework by focusing on intermediate denoising transitions rather than final samples. Unlike standard DPO, which defines preferences over terminal outputs
(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
, the DDSPO formulation assumes supervision over denoising transitions
((\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}),(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l})\mid\boldsymbol{c})
at arbitrary timesteps
t
.

Let
p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
and
p_{\star}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
denote preferred and dispreferred denoising distributions respectively, conditioned on prompt
\boldsymbol{c}
. Following the Bradley–Terry approach in
Eq.

9
, the reward is redefined over denoising transitions as a function
r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})
, with preference assigned to transitions sampled from
p_{\star}^{w}
over those from
p_{\star}^{l}
.

In this formulation, preference over transitions can be modeled analogously to
Eq.

1
, by replacing the reward function over final samples
r(c,x_{0})
with a reward function over transitions
r(c,x_{t},x_{t-1})
:

\mathbb{P}((\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w})\succ(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l})\mid\boldsymbol{c})=\sigma\left(r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w})-r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l})\right),

(20)

Similar to
Eq.

9
, the reward model
r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})
can be parameterized by a neural network
\phi
, and trained using maximum likelihood estimation for binary classification:

L_{\text{BT}}(\phi)=-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t}}\left[\log\sigma\left(r_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1})-r_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1})\right)\right]

(21)

This contrasts with standard DPO or Diffusion DPO, where preference pairs
(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
are drawn from a dataset
\mathcal{D}
of full samples. In DDSPO, supervision instead arises from tuples
((\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}),(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l})\mid\boldsymbol{c})
sampled from preferred and dispreferred denoising policies
p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
and
p_{\star}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}\mid\boldsymbol{c})
. This allows DDSPO to directly optimize the model’s denoising behavior at each timestep
t
, rather than the distribution over final outputs.
This formulation enables a direct comparison between denoising transitions at the same timestep, providing fine-grained supervision over the reverse process.

Accordingly, the DDSPO objective adopts a DPO-style structure defined over transitions, analogous to
Eq.

2
:

\max_{p_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{c},\,\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}\sim p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}\mid\boldsymbol{c})}\left[r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})\right]-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left[p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\,\|\,p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\right]

(22)

The optimal reverse transition distribution under this formulation has the form:

p^{*}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})=p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\cdot\frac{\exp(r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})/\beta)}{Z(\boldsymbol{c})},

(23)

where the partition function
Z(\boldsymbol{c})
is defined as

Z(\boldsymbol{c})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}}p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\exp(r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})/\beta).

The reward can thus be reparameterized as

r(\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})=\beta\log\frac{p^{*}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}+\beta\log Z(\boldsymbol{c}).

(24)

Substituting the reward expression from
Eq.

24
into the Bradley–Terry objective
Eq.

21
yields a per-example loss defined over denoising transitions:

\ell_{\text{DDSPO}}^{(t)}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{c}\sim\mathcal{D}(c),\,(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w})\sim p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1,t}\mid\boldsymbol{c}),\\
(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l})\sim p_{\star}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1,t}\mid\boldsymbol{c})\end{subarray}}\left[-\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},c)}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},c)}-\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},c)}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},c)}\right)\right]

(25)

The full DDSPO objective is then obtained by taking the expectation over uniformly sampled timesteps
t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)
.
Equivalently, this corresponds to uniformly averaging per-timestep losses across all diffusion steps:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)}\left[\ell_{\text{DDSPO}}^{(t)}(\theta)\right],

(26)

Concretely, we instantiate it as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDSPO}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{c}\sim\mathcal{D}(c),\,t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w})\sim p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1,t}\mid\boldsymbol{c}),(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l})\sim p_{\star}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1,t}\mid\boldsymbol{c})}\\
\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\log\!\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{c})}-\beta\log\!\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{c})}\right)

As in Diffusion DPO, we approximate the intractable joint transition distribution

\displaystyle p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1,t}^{w}\mid c)\approx q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w})\cdot p_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},c),

(27)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}\sim\mathcal{D}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w})
, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\sim p_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},c)
.
The same applies to the dispreferred trajectory
(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l})
.

Substituting this into the DDSPO objective yields:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDSPO}}(\theta)&=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{c}\sim\mathcal{D}(c),\,t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w})\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w})\cdot p^{w}_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},c),\,(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l})\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})\cdot p^{l}_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},c)}\\
&\left[-\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},c)}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},c)}-\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},c)}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},c)}\right)\right]\\
&\leq-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{c}\sim\mathcal{D}(c),\,t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}),\,\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})}\log\sigma\Biggl(\beta T\cdot\\
&\quad\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}\sim p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c}),\,\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}\sim p_{\star}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}\Bigl[\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}-\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}\Bigr]\Biggr)\\
&=-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{c}\sim\mathcal{D}(c),\,t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}),\,\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})}\log\sigma\Biggl(\beta T\cdot\\
&\quad\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}\sim p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c}),\,\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}\sim p_{\star}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}\Bigl[\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}-\log\frac{p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}\\
&-\left(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\star}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}-\log\frac{p_{\star}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}\right)\Bigr]\Biggr)\\
&=-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{c}\sim\mathcal{D}(c),\,t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{0}),\,\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{0})}\log\sigma\Biggl(-\beta T\cdot\Bigl(\\
&\quad\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\,\|\,p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\right)-\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\,\|\,p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{w}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\right)\\
&\quad-\left(\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(p_{\star}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\,\|\,p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\right)-\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(p_{\star}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\,\|\,p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t-1}|\boldsymbol{x}^{l}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})\right)\right)\Bigr)\Biggr)\end{split}

(28)

Using the Gaussian parameterization of the reverse process
Eq.

7
, the above loss simplifies to:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDSPO}}

\displaystyle(\theta)\leq-\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})\sim\mathcal{D},\,\boldsymbol{c}\sim\mathcal{D}(c),\,t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}),\,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})}\log\sigma\bigg(-\beta\cdot\big[

\displaystyle\quad\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{w}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{w}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\big(\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{l}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}^{l}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\|_{2}^{2}\big)\big]\bigg).

(29)

DSPO

DSPO
(
68
)
follows the standard score decomposition used in conditional diffusion models and expresses the conditional score as

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}}\log p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{c},y)=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}}\log p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{c})+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}}\log p(y\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{c}),

(30)

where
y
represents a human-preference condition.
DSPO proposes to fine-tune the pretrained score model
to match a preference-adjusted target score, leading to the objective

\min_{\theta}\;\omega(t)\left\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}}\log p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{c})-\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{c})+\gamma\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}}\log p(y\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{c})\right)\right\|_{2}^{2},

(31)

where
\omega(t)
is the diffusion timetable weighting function
and
\gamma
scales the preference constraint.
The preference likelihood is modeled as

p(y\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{c})=p(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\succ\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{c})=\sigma\left(r(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})-r(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\right).

(32)

By substituting this likelihood into
Eq.

31
and
deriving the resulting score-matching objective,
the loss can be extended over all timesteps to yield:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})\sim\mathcal{D},\,\boldsymbol{c}\sim\mathcal{D}(c),\,t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T),\,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{w}),\,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\sim q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{l})}A(t)\Big\|(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{w})\\
\quad-\beta\Big(1-\sigma\big(r_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})-r_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})\big)\Big)\cdot(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c}))\Big\|_{2}^{2}.

(33)

Here,
A(t)
denotes a timestep-dependent weighting function; following DSPO, we disregard
A(t)
and the associated parameters involving
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
at the beginning of the objective.
In DSPO, to avoid training an additional probability model,

r(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})
is computed using the implicit reward formulation of
Diffusion-DPO
(
52
)

r(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})=\lambda\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})}{p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})},

(34)

DSPO approximates
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
using its posterior mean under the forward process as

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t},x_{0}]=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{t-1}}{\alpha_{t}}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right).

Then, using this approximation, the estimated
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t},c)
becomes

\lambda\left(\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)^{-d}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta_{t}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\frac{\alpha_{t-1}}{\alpha_{t}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Therefore, the reward function

r(\cdot)=\lambda(\log p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c})-\log p_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{c}))

is represented in DSPO as

r(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})=-\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{\beta_{t}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\frac{\alpha_{t-1}}{\alpha_{t}}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right).

In contrast, our formulation avoids this forward-process approximation and
derives the denoising target directly from the contrastive denoising policies.
Specifically, instead of using

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
obtained from the forward posterior mean approximation, we incorporate
a policy-grounded target
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}
extracted from the winning and losing
denoising policies
p_{\star}^{w}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{w}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},\boldsymbol{c})
and

p_{\star}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}^{l}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},\boldsymbol{c})
, respectively. This leads to a modified approximation
of the form

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t},x_{0}]=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{t-1}}{\alpha_{t}}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}\right),

and accordingly, the reward expression becomes

r(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})=-\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{\beta_{t}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\frac{\alpha_{t-1}}{\alpha_{t}}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\star}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t},t,\boldsymbol{c})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Through this replacement, the reward is directly derived from the contrastive
denoising policies rather than from a forward-process approximation, enabling
policy-grounded preference supervision at each denoising step.

Appendix F
Limitation

As with Diffusion-DPO, DDSPO requires careful selection of the balancing coefficient
\beta
, which controls the trade-off between reward maximization and divergence minimization. This reliance on hyperparameter tuning can add practical complexity, often necessitating task-specific calibration.

DDSPO requires access to score-level preferences during training, which are derived from denoising scores of preferred and dispreferred policies. In this work, we construct such preferences by perturbing prompts and assuming that degraded prompts yield less desirable outputs. While this strategy enables automated supervision, it is applicable only when preferences can be effectively modeled through prompt manipulation. In tasks where such modeling is infeasible—
e.g
.
, when only preferences over final outputs
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
are available—DDSPO cannot be directly applied. Extending DDSPO to such settings may require alternative methods for obtaining score preferences, such as auxiliary reward models, preference classifiers, or contrastive distillation techniques. Exploring these directions remains a promising avenue for future research.

Compared to Diffusion DPO, which requires four score computations per preference pair—namely,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})
,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{w},t,\boldsymbol{c})
,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})
, and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c})
—DDSPO introduces one additional computation:
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{ref}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{l},t,\boldsymbol{c}^{-})
. This results in approximately 25% more computation per training step. In practice, DDSPO achieves strong performance with just 200K pseudo-preference pairs and 100 training steps—significantly less data and computation than prior work, which often uses 800K pairs and over 1,000 steps. On SDXL, generating the 200K pairs took approximately 5 H100-days, and training for 100 steps required an additional 1 H100-day.

Appendix G
Prompt Perturbation

To construct supervision signals for DDSPO, we perturb prompts to produce semantically or aesthetically degraded variants. For each original prompt
\boldsymbol{c}
, we generate a corresponding negative prompt
\boldsymbol{c}^{-}
that is intended to yield lower-quality or misaligned images. We adopt two perturbation strategies depending on the supervision objective.

Alignment Degradation (Random Removal)
For alignment supervision, we generate negative prompts by randomly removing 40–70% of the tokens from the original prompt. This lightweight corruption introduces ambiguity and reduces semantic specificity, resulting in weaker text-to-image alignment.

Alignment Degradation via LLM
We also leverage LLaMA3-8B
10
to generate semantically degraded prompts that obscure key visual concepts. As shown in
Fig.

5(a)
, the instruction guides the model to remove or generalize important elements, while preserving the overall structure of the original prompt. This allows us to obtain more compositional and realistic misalignment cases compared to random token deletion.

Aesthetic Degradation via LLM
To supervise visual quality, we again use LLaMA3-8B to rewrite prompts in a way that intentionally reduces aesthetic appeal. The instruction template, shown in
Fig.

5(b)
, directs the model to degrade aspects like color, lighting, texture, and clarity while preserving the subject. This produces visually inferior prompts in a controlled and targeted way.

These perturbation techniques allow us to generate rich and scalable preference data without requiring manual annotations. Example prompt pairs are provided in
Fig.

D
.
