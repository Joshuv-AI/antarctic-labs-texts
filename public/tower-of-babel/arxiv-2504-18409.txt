Analysis of Multiple-try Metropolis via Poincaré inequalities
Rocco Caprio, Sam Power, Andi Q. Wang

Abstract
We study the Multiple-try Metropolis algorithm using the framework of Poincaré inequalities. We describe the Multiple-try Metropolis as an auxiliary variable implementation of a resampling approximation to an ideal Metropolis–Hastings algorithm. Under suitable moment conditions on the importance weights, we derive explicit Poincaré comparison results between the Multiple-try algorithm and the ideal algorithm. We characterize the spectral gap of the latter, and finally in the Gaussian case prove explicit non-asymptotic convergence bounds for Multiple-try Metropolis by comparison. Keywords: Markov chain Monte Carlo, Multiple-try Metropolis, parallel computation, Poincaré inequalities, spectral gap.

Introduction

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods are one of the most fundamental tools for Bayesian computation and beyond, as a tool to sample from a posterior distribution of interest, known up to a normalizing constant. These methods are based on the construction of a Markov chain having the prescribed target distribution as invariant. Denote by
(\mathsf{E},\mathcal{F})
an underlying Polish space with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra, and let
\pi
denote the target distribution of interest. The
Metropolis–Hastings
algorithm
[
19
,
14
]
constructs such a Markov chain as follows: Given a current position
x\in\mathsf{E}
, one draws
Y\sim q(x,\cdot)
, where
q
is a ‘proposal’ Markov kernel from
\mathsf{E}
to itself, which we use to explore the space. Assuming the existence of appropriate density functions, with probability

\alpha(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min\{1,r(x,y)\},\quad r(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\pi(y)\cdot q(y,x)}{\pi(x)\cdot q(x,y)}

one moves to
Y
, and otherwise remains at
x
. This mechanism produces a
\pi
-invariant Markov chain that can be shown to be ergodic under general conditions.

The Multiple-try Metropolis (MTM) algorithm
[
15
,
11
]
is a sampling method where one introduces
n
different proposal samples, and a selection mechanism based on a weight function
w\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathsf{E}\times\mathsf{E}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The motivations behind this method are twofold. From one side, one would expect that different proposal distributions lead to a better exploration of the space, and thus to faster mixing properties of the chain. On the other hand, modern computing architectures easily allow for sampling and evaluating multiple proposals in parallel. This type of parallelization is to be contrasted with the one immediately available from vanilla Metropolis algorithms, whereby one can simply run
n
chains in parallel. Running multiple independent chains in parallel does not, however, reduce the burn-in required for each chain to equilibrate. As a result, the Multiple-try method has the potential to result in significant gains in terms of
non-asymptotic
computational efficiency; see
[
12
, Section 1.2]
.
In the next section we introduce in more detail the Multiple-try Metropolis algorithm, by viewing it an
auxiliary-variable implementation
of an
importance resampling approximation
to an ‘
ideal
’ MCMC algorithm, which motivates our analysis; we then present our main results.

1.1
Multiple-try Metropolis and limiting kernels

The mixing properties of the Metropolis–Hastings scheme are inherently related to the proposal distribution
q

\displaystyle q^{w}(x,\dif y)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{q(x,\dif y)\cdot w(x,y)}{(qw)(x)},\quad\text{where}\quad(qw)(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int q(x,\dif y)\cdot w(x,y).

(1)

Practical implementation of this chain requires the ability to sample from

Given the empirical success of multiple-try approaches in various practical settings
[
7
,
11
,
18
,
15
,
20
]
, it is natural to seek a theoretical justification for its good performance. In light of our derivation of Multiple-try, it would be natural to argue this point by way of comparison: ideally,
P_{\infty}
is a Markov kernel with excellent mixing properties,
\widetilde{P}_{n}
is a good approximation of it, and
P_{n}
is a good approximation of the latter. The goal of this work is to rigorously enact this heuristic argument to study the convergence behaviour of
P_{n}
.

Our first main result is a general
\mathrm{L}^{2}
comparison result between
P_{\infty}
and
P_{n}
in terms of their Dirichlet forms. This result depends on the moments of the associated
importance weights

\varpi(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\dif q^{w}(x,\cdot)}{\dif q(x,\cdot)}(y)=\frac{w(x,y)}{(qw)(x)},

(3)

which can be interpreted as the weight normalized by the average value with respect to the proposal. Note that multiplying the weights
w
by a
y
-independent factor yields an identical Markov chain; in this sense,
\varpi
serves as a convenient standardisation of the weights. Let

M_{\varpi}(p)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\pi\otimes q)\left(\varpi(X,Y)^{p}\right)

denote their
p
-th moments.

Theorem 1
.

For any
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)
and
s>0
, there holds the inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(P_{\infty},f)

\displaystyle\leq s\cdot\mathcal{E}(P_{n},f)+\beta_{n}(s)\cdot\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\textup{osc}}^{2},\quad\forall s>0.

where
\beta_{n}(s)\to\mathbf{1}\{s\leq 1\}
as
n\to\infty
. Furthermore, if
M_{\varpi}(2p)
and
M_{\varpi}(-2p)
are finite for some
p\in[1,\infty)
.

\displaystyle\beta_{n}(s)\leq s^{-\frac{p^{2}}{1+2p}}\cdot\bigg\{c_{2,n,p}\cdot\bigg(\frac{c_{1,n,p}p}{c_{2,n,p}(1+p)}\bigg)^{1+p}+c_{1,n,p}\cdot\bigg(\frac{c_{1,n,p}p}{c_{2,n,p}(1+p)}\bigg)^{-p}\bigg\},

\displaystyle c_{1,n,p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=M_{\varpi}(p)+K_{1}\cdot n^{-1}\quad

\displaystyle c_{2,n,p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2^{p+1}\cdot\left\{K_{2}\cdot n^{-1}+K_{3}\cdot n^{-2}+M_{\varpi}(2p)+M_{\varpi}(2p)\right\}.

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
14
and the bounds given in Propositions
11
and
13
. The constants
K_{1},K_{2},K_{3}
are explicit in the corresponding proofs.
∎

This comparison result is valid for any weight function, any

and target measure

In particular, the comparison is tighter when the importance weights admit moments of larger order. We provide background on Dirichlet forms and Poincaré inequalities in Section
1.3
below. In particular, Theorem
1
allows us to deduce convergence results for Multiple-try by studying the spectral gap of

, which is a Metropolis–Hastings algorithm with proposal

. As we show, a common choice of weights

actually yields a Multiple-try algorithm whose spectral gap

, and we conclude that one should not use it in practice, strengthening a result of
[
12
]

7

For the following results, we specialise to the Gaussian setting, as has been done previously in theoretical analyses of Multiple-try

Theorem 2

(Spectral gap bounds for
P_{\infty}
)
.

Assume that
\pi(\dif x)=\mathcal{N}(\dif x;0,I_{d})

\displaystyle 2^{-10}\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma^{4}\cdot d}{4}\right)\sigma^{2}(2+\sigma^{2})c_{\gamma}^{2}\leq\gamma(P_{\infty})\leq\frac{3}{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2+\sigma^{2}}\wedge\bigg(1+\frac{\sigma^{4}}{(2+\sigma^{2})^{2}}\bigg)^{-d/2}

Proof.

The lower and upper bound follow from Propositions

∎

If we take

Remark 1
.

In particular, the spectral gap of

In the Random Walk Metropolis algorithm, its optimal diffusion scaling of

1.2
Related work

Despite Multiple-try being a well-known sampling technique, there are not many theoretical results available in the literature. An asymptotic scaling analysis of the algorithm is conducted in
[
4
]
, in the case of high-dimensional product-form targets.
[
23
]
recently obtained the first convergence rate results we are aware of in the case of the independence sampler
q(x,\dif y)=q(\dif y)
, where one does not need to introduce balancing trials.
[
12
,
7
]
introduce a novel weight function we consider in this work, and perform a scaling analysis and mixing time study, the latter in the case of discrete spaces. The analyses in
[
15
,
12
]
are also based on the idea of using a limiting Multiple-try chain to draw conclusions about the Multiple-try algorithm, and, in a sense, we make those observations precise with the Poincaré inequalities introduced.

We now comment on the independent and concurrent work of
[
21
]
. Our two works are related, but are conducted with different emphases and using different tools. In
[
21
]
, the authors study a slightly more general class of algorithms (termed ‘multiproposal Markov chain Monte Carlo’), of which Multiple-try is the primary example, and they focus on delivering negative results, highlighting the fundamental computational limitations of such methods. As such, their results are couched in terms of
upper bounds
on the spectral gaps of these chains. By contrast, while we do also derive some negative results (such as Proposition
9
), the focus of our work is to provide
positive
results and convergence guarantees via Markov chain comparisons. Taken together, these two papers can be seen as a complementary pair of works, with
[
21
]
supplying fundamental limitations on the best-case performance of Multiple-try type chains, and our present work supplying guarantees for the worst-case situation. We will comment in more depth on the similarities and differences of our works throughout the paper.

1.3
Comparison of Markov chains

We introduce some key notation, and then some results and definitions from the framework of weak Poincaré inequalities from
[
1
,
2
]
we will use throughout. For a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathsf{E}\to\mathbb{R}
and a measure
\pi
on
(\mathsf{E},\mathcal{F})
, we denote
\pi(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int\pi(\dif x)f(x)
. We let
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)
denote the set of square integrable real-valued functions
\pi(f^{2})<\infty
, endowed with the inner product
\left\langle{f},{g}\right\rangle_{2,\pi}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\pi(f\cdot g)
and induced norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}^{2}_{2,\pi}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\langle{f},{f}\right\rangle_{2,\pi}
. We further introduce the space
\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\pi)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\pi(f)=0\}
of the mean zero elements of
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)
and the oscillation seminorm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\textup{osc}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\ess_{\pi}\sup f-\ess_{\pi}\inf f
. For two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
(\mathsf{E},\mathcal{F})
, we denote
(\mu\otimes\nu)(A\times B)=\mu(A)\nu(B)
for
A,B\in\mathcal{F}
.
\mathsf{Id}
denotes the
identity operator
on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)
. Given a
\pi
-invariant Markov kernel
P
, the Dirichlet form associated to the pair
(P,\pi)
is defined for any
f\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\pi)
by
\mathcal{E}(P,f)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\langle{(\mathsf{Id}-P)f},{f}\right\rangle_{2,\pi}
. We will also often use the alternative representation

\mathcal{E}(P,f)=\frac{1}{2}\int\pi(\dif x)P(x,\dif y)(f(x)-f(y))^{2}.

P
is said to be
positive
if
P
is reversible and
\left\langle{Pf},{f}\right\rangle_{2,\pi}\geq 0
for all
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)
.

Definition 1

(Standard Poincaré inequality; SPI)
.

We say that a
\pi
-reversible positive Markov kernel

satisfies a Standard Poincaré inequality (SPI) with constant
c_{P}>0
if for all
f\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\pi)
,

c_{P}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{2,\pi}^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}(P,f).

(5)

By iterating (
5
), we can immediately characterize the
\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\pi)
-exponential convergence of
P
in the following sense.

Lemma 4

(SPI
\Rightarrow
geometric convergence)
.

Let
P
be a
\pi
-reversible positive Markov kernel satisfying a SPI. Then, for all
f\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\pi)
we have for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert P^{k}f\right\rVert}_{2,\pi}^{2}\leq(1-c_{P})^{k}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{2,\pi}^{2}.

Proof.

Since
P
is positive, we have that
\mathcal{E}(P,f)\leq\mathcal{E}(P^{*}P,f)
by
[
1
, Lemma 18]
.
It then follows that

c_{P}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{2,\pi}^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}(P,f)\leq\mathcal{E}(P^{*}P,f)=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{2,\pi}^{2}-\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Pf\right\rVert}_{2,\pi}^{2}\hskip 1.00006pt\Rightarrow\hskip 1.00006pt\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Pf\right\rVert}_{2,\pi}^{2}\leq(1-c_{P})\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{2,\pi}^{2}

for all
f\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\pi)
, and the claim follows upon iterating the last inequality
n
times.
∎

In various cases of practical interest, one encounters Markov kernels which are not exponentially ergodic, and so cannot satisfy any SPI. Nevertheless, for these kernels which converge only at slower-than-exponential (‘subgeometric’) rates, we can still obtain fine control on their convergence behaviour in various ways. The following class of functional inequalities is helpful in characterizing these.

Definition 2

(Weak Poincaré inequality; WPI)
.

We say that a
\pi
-reversible positive Markov kernel
P
satisfies a weak Poincaré inequality (WPI) if for all
f\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\pi)
,

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{2,\pi}^{2}\leq s\cdot\mathcal{E}(P,f)+\beta(s)\cdot\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\textup{osc}}^{2},\quad\forall s>0,

(6)

where
\beta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a decreasing function such that
\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\beta(s)=0
.

Lemma 5

(WPI
\Rightarrow
sub-geometric convergence)
.

Let
P
be a
\pi
-reversible positive Markov kernel satisfying a WPI. Then, for all
f\in\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\pi)
such that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\textup{osc}}<\infty
we have for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
that

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert P^{k}f\right\rVert}^{2}_{2,\pi}\leq F^{-1}(k)\cdot\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\textup{osc}}^{2}

(7)

where
F
is a convex decreasing invertible function defined by

\displaystyle F(k)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{k}^{1}\frac{1}{K^{*}(v)}\dif v,\quad\text{where}

K^{*}(v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{u\geq 0}\{uv-K(u)\}\quad\text{and}\quad K(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}u\cdot\beta(1/u),&\hskip 10.22217pt\text{if}\hskip 10.22217ptu\textgreater 0\\
0,&\hskip 10.22217pt\text{if} \hskip 10.22217ptu=0.\end{cases}

Example 1
.

If (
6
) holds with
\beta(s)=cs^{-\alpha}
, then
F^{-1}(n)\leq c(1+\alpha)^{1+\alpha}n^{-\alpha}
.

Lemma 6

(Linear rescaling)
.

Under the same conditions of Lemma
5
, if, instead we have the rescaled WPI

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{2,\pi}^{2}\leq s\cdot\mathcal{E}(P,f)+\beta(cs)\cdot\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\textup{osc}}^{2},\quad\forall s>0,

the bound (
7
) holds with
c\cdot n
in place of
n
.

SPIs and WPIs can also be used to deduce convergence properties of a given Markov kernel
relative to
another one; see
[
1
, Theorem 33]
.

Lemma 7

(Chaining)
.

Let
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
and
P_{3}
three
\pi
-invariant Markov kernels. Assume that for all
s>0
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{0}(\pi)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(P_{1},f)

\displaystyle\leq s\cdot\mathcal{E}(P_{2},f)+\beta_{1}(s)\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\textup{osc}}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(P_{2},f)

\displaystyle\leq s\cdot\mathcal{E}(P_{3},f)+\beta_{2}(s)\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\textup{osc}}^{2}.

Then,

\mathcal{E}(P_{1},f)\leq s\cdot\mathcal{E}(P_{3},f)+\beta(s)\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\textup{osc}}^{2},\quad\forall s>0,f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{0}(\pi),

where
\beta(s)=\inf\{\beta_{1}(s_{1})+s_{1}\beta_{2}(s_{2})\mid s_{1}>0,s_{2}>0,s_{1}s_{2}=s\}
. In particular, if
\beta_{i}(s)=c_{i}s^{-p}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, then
\beta(s)=cs^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{p^{2}}{1+2p}
and
c\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=c_{2}\cdot(\frac{c_{1}p}{c_{2}(1+p)})^{1+p}+c_{1}\cdot(\frac{c_{1}p}{c_{2}(1+p)})^{-p}
.

If
P_{1}(x,\dif y)
is the ‘perfect’ kernel
\pi(\dif y)
, the above result also illustrates how we can deduce convergence bounds for
P_{3}
given a WPI between
P_{2}
and
P_{3}
.

Remark 2
.

If
\beta_{1}(s)=0
for all

for some

, then a WPI implies a SPI between

and

with constant

The following is a practical criterion to establish WPIs and SPIs we will often use; see

Lemma 8

(Off-diagonal ordering)
.

Let

2
Comparison of Multiple-try chains

In this section we derive various comparison results for Multiple-try chains. For the reader’s convenience, we have listed the various kernels and notations in Appendix
A.1
.

2.1
Optimal number of proposals

Using an appropriate SPI, we first establish a
negative result
for Multiple-try Metropolis: increasing the number of proposals can only achieve a limited improvement.

Proposition 9
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)
, it holds

(n-1)\cdot\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{P}_{n},f)\leq n\cdot\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{P}_{n-1},f),\quad\textup{and}\quad(n-1)\cdot\mathcal{E}(P_{n},f)\leq n\cdot\mathcal{E}(P_{n-1},f).

Proof.

See Appendix
A.2
.
∎

Proposition
9
establishes that incrementing by 1 the number of proposals in Multiple-try Metropolis can only improve performance by
at most

n/(n-1)
in the sense of the Dirichlet forms. Thus, the maximal improvement of the spectral gap is bounded as

(n-1)\cdot\gamma(\widetilde{P}_{n})\leq n\cdot\gamma(\widetilde{P}_{n-1}),\quad\textup{and}\quad(n-1)\cdot\gamma(P_{n})\leq n\cdot\gamma(P_{n-1}).

Notably, for example, if the Multiple-try chain is
sub-geometric
(so
\gamma(P_{n})=0
), no increase in the number of proposals can ever make the chain geometric. Interestingly, one can have chains which have a positive spectral gap at any finite
n
, but which vanishes in the large
n
limit.
In light of this result, we can argue that for Multiple-try with conditionally i.i.d. proposals, with a
serial
implementation, in terms of the efficiency per proposal,
n=1
, i.e. the simple Metropolis–Hastings scheme, is optimal. Hence, to obtain better practical performance from Multiple-try schemes, one needs either non-i.i.d. proposals, or a non-serial implementation, motivating approaches such as
[
6
,
9
]
. We notice that a similar optimality of a single pseudo-simple has also been observed in
[
5
]
, but in a different context involving ABC algorithms; as far as we can tell, neither result implies the other.

We show in Proposition
17
that for a common implementation of Multiple-try, it is even true that the spectral gap
vanishes
with
n
. This can be interpreted as follows: the importance resampling approximation is effectively pushing the effective proposal close to the ‘ideal’
q^{w}
, which happens to be a very poor proposal, for which the corresponding ideal Metropolis–Hastings algorithm possess no spectral gap. We discuss this more in detail later.

Remark 3
.

[
23
]
arrives at an analogous conclusions in the restricted case of Multiple-try with state-independent proposals. The concurrent work
[
21
]
presents a very similar result by comparing the spectral gap of
P_{n}
to a single-proposal chain and an analogous conclusion on the optimality of
n=1
in the serial context. Proposition
9
also isolates that this behaviour is caused by the importance resampling approximation step, and it is slightly more general in that provides comparison between each pair
(P_{n},P_{m})
via induction.

2.2
Comparison to ideal algorithms

In this section we derive comparison results between Multiple-try
P_{n}
and ideal chains
P_{\infty}
. When the importance weights admit finite moments of larger order, the comparison is tighter. These results also allow us to deduce convergence bounds for
P_{n}
by analysing
P_{\infty}
, which is done in the next section.

Comparison between ideal and semi-ideal kernels.

This comparison quantifies the loss in performance caused by the importance resampling approximation to
q^{w}
. This loss can be characterized by studying the fluctuations of the importance weights (
3
).
Whenever these can be uniformly bounded above, it is possible to establish a SPI between
\widetilde{P}_{n}
and
P_{\infty}
.

Lemma 10
.

Assume that
|\varpi|_{\infty}<\infty
. Then,

\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{P}_{n},f)\geq|\varpi|_{\infty}^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{E}(P_{\infty},f)\quad\forall f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi).

Proof.

See Appendix
A.3.1
.
∎

Such uniform bounds are expected to be difficult to ensure outside of compact state spaces. When the importance weights are not uniformly bounded, but their moments are at least finite, we can still relate their convergence properties via a WPI. To this end, recall that

M_{\varpi}(p)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\pi\otimes q)(\varpi(X,Y)^{p}).

denotes the
p
-th moment of the importance weights.

Proposition 11
.

For any
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)
and
s>0
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(P_{\infty},f)\leq s\cdot\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{P}_{n},f)+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\textup{osc}}^{2}\cdot\beta_{1,n}(s),

\displaystyle\beta_{1,n}(s)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\pi\otimes q^{w})\bigg(\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varpi(X,Y_{i})}\mid Y_{1}=Y\bigg]<\frac{1}{s}\bigg).

Furthermore, if in addition
M_{\varpi}(p+1)
is finite for some
p\in[1,\infty)
, then, for all
s>0
,

\displaystyle\beta_{1,n}(s)\leq s^{-p}\cdot\left\{K_{1}\cdot n^{-1}+M_{\varpi}(p)\right\}.

Proof.

See Appendix
A.3.2
. The constant
K_{1}
can be traced in the proof.
∎

Remark 4
.

We see from the dominated convergence theorem and the strong law of large numbers that as
n\to\infty
, for each
s>0
,
\beta_{1,n}(s)\to\mathbf{1}\{s\leq 1\}
, corresponding to the best possible comparison between
P_{\infty}
and
\widetilde{P}_{n}
, see Remark
2
.

Comparison between the semi-ideal and the Multiple-try Metropolis kernels.

This comparison captures the loss in performance due to the introduction of the shadow proposals to make the acceptance probability of
\widetilde{P}_{n}
computable. Because these also appear in the denominator of the acceptance function, we need some control on their distance to zero. In particular, if these are also uniformly lower bounded, we immediately obtain a strong comparison result.

Lemma 12
.

Assume that
|\varpi|_{\infty}<\infty
and
|\varpi^{-1}|_{\infty}<\infty
. Then,

\mathcal{E}(P_{n},f)\leq|\varpi^{-1}|_{\infty}^{2}\cdot|\varpi|_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{P}_{n},f)

Proof.

See Appendix
A.3.3
.
∎

These uniform lower boundedness conditions can be relaxed via a WPI approach via an appropriate control on the negative moments of the importance weights.

Proposition 13
.

For any
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)
and
s>0
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{P}_{n},f)\leq s\cdot\mathcal{E}(P_{n},f)+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\textup{osc}}^{2}\cdot\beta_{2,n}(s),

\displaystyle\beta_{2,n}(s)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\pi\otimes\tilde{P}_{n})(\zeta_{n}(X,Y)<1/s),

\displaystyle\zeta_{n}(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left(\frac{(\widetilde{qw})_{n}(x,y)}{(\widehat{qw}_{n})(x,Y^{[n]})},\frac{(\widetilde{qw})_{n}(y,x)}{(\widehat{qw}_{n})(y,Z^{[n]})}\right)\mid Y_{1}=y,Z_{1}=x\right]

Furthermore, if in addition
M_{\varpi}(2p)
and
M_{\varpi}(-2p)
are finite for some
p\in[1,\infty)
, then, for all
s>0
,

\displaystyle\beta_{2,n}(s)\leq s^{-p}\cdot 2^{p+1}\cdot\left\{K_{2}\cdot n^{-1}+K_{3}\cdot n^{-2}+M_{\varpi}(2p)+M_{\varpi}(-2p)\right\}.

Proof.

See Appendix
A.3.4
. The constants
K_{2},K_{3}
can be traced in the proof.
∎

Remark 5
.

Similar to Remark
4
, we have that
\beta_{2,n}(s)\to\mathbf{1}\{s\leq 1\}
as

, which is again the best possible comparison result.

Comparison between Multiple-try and the ideal kernel.

Chaining Proposition
11
and
13
via Lemma
7
returns the following comparison result between Multiple-try and the ideal Metropolis kernel.

Proposition 14
.

For any
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)
and any

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(P_{\infty},f)\leq s\cdot\mathcal{E}(P_{n},f)+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\textup{osc}}^{2}\cdot\beta_{n}(s),

\displaystyle\beta_{n}(s)=\inf\{\beta_{1,n}(s_{1})+s_{1}\beta_{2,n}(s_{2})|s_{1}>0,s_{2}>0,s_{1}s_{2}=s\}

In particular, as

, we have
\beta_{n}(s)\to\mathbf{1}\{s\leq 1\}
. Therefore, in this sense, in the limit of multiple trials, the convergence rate of Multiple-try is ‘close’ to that of the ideal kernel. In fact, with an argument somewhat similar to that in

, pp 25-26]
, if

, again Lemma

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{2,\pi}^{2}\leq s\cdot\mathcal{E}(P_{n},f)+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f\right\rVert}_{\textup{osc}}^{2}\cdot\bigg\{\frac{s}{1+\epsilon}\beta_{n}(1+\epsilon)+\mathbf{1}\{s\leq(1+\epsilon)c_{P_{\infty}}^{-1}\}\bigg\}

so that for large

(see Remark

\pi
and any choice of proposal

. When the importance weights possess suitable moments, one can bound
\beta_{n}(s)
via the corresponding bounds for
\beta_{1,n}(s)
and
\beta_{2,n}(s)
in Propositions

and prove Theorems
1
and

.

Remark 6
.

Theorem 1 in
[
12
]
assumes fourth moment conditions on the weights

3
Convergence analysis of the ideal Markov chain

In this section we analyse the convergence properties of
P_{\infty}
(and thus of
P_{n}
via Theorem
1
). We consider the case when
q(x,\dif y)=\mathcal{N}(\dif y;x,I_{d}\cdot\sigma^{2})
is a Gaussian Random Walk, which is a common choice in the implementation of Multiple-try schemes. We consider two choices of weight function.

(i) The
globally balanced
weights
w(x,y)=\pi(y)/\pi(x)
. This choice is commonly used in Multiple-try algorithms used in practice
[
17
,
18
,
12
]
. The rationale behind this choice is that one should give more weight to points that are in high-probability areas of
\pi
. However, as we illustrate in Section
3.1
, strengthening some results in
[
12
]
, this choice the algorithm is excessively biased towards high-probability regions of
\pi
, resulting in a Markov chain whose spectral gap that vanishes as the number of proposals grow (Proposition
17
).

(ii) The
locally balanced
weights
w(x,y)=\sqrt{\pi(y)/\pi(x)}
were recently introduced in
[
12
,
7
]
.
The rationale behind this choice, inspired by the work of
[
24
]
, is that the resulting proposal kernel
q^{w}
becomes
\pi
-invariant as
\sigma\rightarrow 0
, resulting in a Markov chain with higher expected acceptance probability in the low
\sigma
scenario. In higher dimensions, where one usually has to take near-zero scale parameter
\sigma
, this choice is thus expected to return a better mixing Markov chain. With globally balanced weights,
q^{w}
leaves
\pi
invariant in the case
\sigma\rightarrow\infty
, while in the small
\sigma
regime it is only invariant for
\pi^{2}
, which might look quite different from
\pi
in high-dimensions
[
24
,
12
]
. The better scaling of the locally balanced choice is shown in
[
12
]
via an optimal scaling analysis, and we provide further support to this claim by analyzing the scaling of the spectral gap of
P_{\infty}
with the dimension.
We develop our analysis for Gaussian targets, similarly to
[
12
]
. While this is certainly restrictive, we note that if
\pi
is a Bayesian posterior distribution, then under mild regularity conditions, the Bernstein-von Mises theorem implies that in a suitably data-rich limit,
\pi
can be expected to admit suitably Gaussian-like behaviour, providing some heuristic support for beginning our exploration in this rather idealised setting.

Assumption 1
.

\pi
has a standard Gaussian density:
\pi(\dif x)=\mathcal{N}(\dif x;0,I_{d})
.

Under the Gaussian assumption on the target, for a generic weight function of the form
w(x,y)=(\pi(y)/\pi(x))^{\theta}
for
\theta\geq 0
(which includes the cases above), the proposal kernel
q^{w}
defines Gaussian transitions:

q^{w}(x,\dif y)=\mathcal{N}\bigg(\dif y;x\cdot\frac{1}{1+\theta\sigma^{2}};I_{d}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1+\theta\sigma^{2}}\bigg).

(8)

The resulting ideal Metropolis kernel
P_{\infty}
is always positive:

Lemma 15
.

P_{\infty}
is a positive Markov kernel: it is reversible and
\left\langle{P_{\infty}f},{f}\right\rangle_{2,\pi}\geq 0
for all
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\pi)
.

Proof.

Reversibility is clear since it is a Metropolis–Hastings chain. Let
\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1/(1+\theta\sigma^{2})
,
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sigma^{2}/(1+\theta\sigma^{2})
. We can check that
q^{w}
is reversible with respect to
\nu(\dif x)=\mathcal{N}(\dif x;0,I_{d}\cdot\gamma/(1-\rho^{2}))
, and the result follows by
[
10
, Proposition 3]
upon identifying, in their notation,
\chi=\nu
and
r(u,v)=\mathcal{N}(u;\rho^{1/2}v,\gamma(1-\rho))
.
∎

3.1
Globally balanced weights

Here, we use a conductance approach to show that the spectral gap of
P_{n}
with globally balanced weights vanishes as the number of proposals
n
increases.

Definition 3

(Conductance)
.

The conductance of a
\pi
-invariant Markov kernel
P
is

\Phi(P)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf\bigg\{\frac{(\pi\otimes P)(A\times A^{c})}{\pi(A)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}A\in\mathcal{F},\pi(A)\leq 1/2\bigg\}.

(9)

Lemma 16

(Cheeger’s inequalities)
.

For a
\pi
-reversible positive Markov kernel
P
, it holds that

2^{-1}\Phi(P)^{2}\leq\gamma(P)\leq\Phi(P)

where
\gamma(P)
denotes the spectral gap of
P
.

Proposition 17
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Then, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
\Phi(P_{n})\rightarrow 0
.

Proof.

See Appendix
B.1
.
∎

By Cheeger’s inequality, this also implies that the spectral gap of
P_{n}
vanishes. In light of this result, it is hard to recommend using Multiple-try schemes with weight function
w(x,y)=\pi(y)/\pi(x)
with a random walk proposal. This phenomena is inherently related to the fact that the globally balanced Multiple-try is excessively biased towards high probability regions of
\pi
, and can be understood by considering the example presented in
[
17
]
, illustrated in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Degenerate behaviour of globally balanced Multiple-try Metropolis: the shadow samples are more likely to be drawn in higher probability areas, resulting in slow convergence.

Figure
1
can be interpreted as follows. Suppose the current sample
x
is the green circle, which lies in a lower probability region. Among the proposals in blue, some will also lie in low probability regions, and others in higher regions. This is illustrated in the left plot. With globally balanced weights, one of the high probability proposals is most likely to be chosen as effective proposal, which we marked as
Y_{I}
. However, this leads to all the shadow samples, marked in red, to be also located in higher probability regions. Consequently, the acceptance probability,
\min\{1,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(Y_{i})/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(Z_{i})\}
, is likely to be low. Notice that this phenomena can only worsen with
n
.
On the other hand, this bad behavior cannot occur if we consider an independence sampler
q(x,\dif y)=q(\dif y)
, as each proposal sample is independent of the current position.
[
23
]
shows that the independence globally balanced Multiple-try Metropolis can in fact be uniformly geometrically ergodic, and that the spectral gap does not vanish with
n
.

3.2
Locally balanced weights

We turn the analysis to the locally balanced
P_{\infty}
. We show that with this choice of weight function

is geometrically convergent, has better scaling properties with the dimension than Random Walk Metropolis, and we derive lower and upper bounds on its spectral gap that reflects this – see the discussion in Section

Firstly, we notice that under the Gaussian assumption on the target, the proposal
q^{w}
is Gaussian, given by (

\theta=1/2
, and we can write,

\displaystyle\alpha_{\infty}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{N}\bigg(\dif y;x\cdot\frac{2}{2+\sigma^{2}};I_{d}\cdot\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{2+\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\alpha_{\infty}(x,y),

(10)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\infty}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{1,\frac{\pi(\dif y)q^{w}(y,\dif x)}{\pi(\dif x)q^{w}(x,\dif y)}\right\}

(11)

and we can calculate explicitly
\alpha_{\infty}(x,y)
as

\alpha_{\infty}(x,y)=\min\left\{1,\exp(-\psi(y)+\psi(x))\right\},\quad\text{with}\quad\psi(x)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4(2+\sigma^{2})}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}+\mathrm{const}.

This facilitates the analysis, by allowing us to extend the techniques introduced in
[
3

. In this section we prove Theorem

by proving upper and lower bounds to the spectral gap of

.

3.2.1
Spectral gap lower bound

The main result of this section is the following.

Proposition 18
.

Assume Assumption
1
. Let
\sigma=\zeta\cdot d^{-1/4}
. For any
\zeta>0

4
Conclusions

In this paper, we analysed the Multiple-try Metropolis algorithm using Poincaré inequalities, interpreting it as an auxiliary-variable approach to a resampling-based approximation of an idealized Metropolis-–Hastings algorithm.

We investigated a limitation of Multiple-try approaches: given a Multiple-try Metropolis algorithm with
n-1
proposals, introducing one additional proposal can enhance performance by at most a factor of
n/(n-1)
with respect to the spectral gap. This reinforces the argument that Multiple-try is appropriate (only) in contexts involving parallel computation. For the commonly used ‘globally-balanced’ implementation, we showed that when paired with random walk proposals, the spectral gap diminishes as
n
grows, underscoring its unsuitability even in parallel computing settings. These findings suggest that globally balanced Multiple-try algorithms are best avoided in general.

We derived comparison results between Multiple-try kernels and the idealized Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, framed in terms of the moments of the importance weights. Tighter bounds arise when these importance weights admit finite moments of high order. For Gaussian target distributions, we derived explicit spectral gap estimates for the idealized Metropolis–Hastings algorithm using locally-balanced weights
[
12
,
7
]
. Notably, the spectral gap of the algorithm scales as
d^{-1/2}
, which is better than the
d^{-1}
scaling of plain Random Walk Metropolis, and suggests that in the large
n
limit, the Multiple-try strategy employing locally-balanced weights might achieve good performance in higher dimensional problems compared to more standard algorithms. Combining the comparison results and the spectral estimates, we obtained non-asymptotic convergence bounds for Multiple-try Metropolis under Gaussian targets, expanding on prior work limited to independence samplers
[
23
]
. As reflected in the paucity of available results in the literature, the Multiple-try algorithm is particularly complicated to analyse due its complex structural properties, and the comparison approach described in this paper is one approach to tackle such difficulties. On the other hand, the Gaussian assumptions is clearly restrictive. We expect a similar results to hold, with suitable adaptations, for more general strongly log-concave and gradient Lipschitz target measures. However, in this work, we make use in several places of the Gaussian structure of
\pi
, so the generalisation does not appear immediate regardless. Even in the Gaussian case, the rates of convergence which we obtain are slower-than-exponential; given what is available for the basic Random Walk Metropolis in this case, one expects that there should be room for further refinements of these results.

\displaystyle\leq s^{-p}\cdot 2^{p-1}\cdot(\pi\otimes\widetilde{P}_{n})\bigg(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(\widehat{qw}_{n})(X,Y^{[n]})}{(\widetilde{qw})_{n}(X,Y)}\middle|Y_{1}=Y\right]^{p}+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(\widehat{qw}_{n})(Y,Z^{[n]})}{(\widetilde{qw})_{n}(Y,X)}\middle|Z_{1}=X\right]^{p}\bigg)

where in the last line we used the fact
(a+b)^{p}\leq 2^{p-1}(a^{p}+b^{p})
. Because, under
\pi\otimes\widetilde{P}_{n}
,
Y
is marginally distributed as
\pi
, the two summands above have identical expectations under
\pi\otimes\widetilde{P}_{n}
, and we thus have

\displaystyle(\pi\otimes\widetilde{P}_{n})(B_{n}(s)^{\complement}\cap\{X\neq Y\})

\displaystyle\leq s^{-p}\cdot 2^{p}\cdot(\pi\otimes\widetilde{P}_{n})\bigg(\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{(\widehat{qw}_{n})(X,Y^{[n]})}{(\widetilde{qw})_{n}(X,Y)}\mid Y_{1}=Y\bigg]^{p}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq s^{-p}\cdot 2^{p}\cdot(\pi\otimes\widetilde{P}_{n})\bigg(\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{(\widehat{qw}_{n})(X,Y^{[n]})}{(\widetilde{qw})_{n}(X,Y)}\bigg)^{p}\mid Y_{1}=Y\bigg]\bigg).

(17)

Now we aim to control the expectation inside the right hand side above. We start with the denominator. Since, by Jensen’s inequality, we have
\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(x,Y_{i})}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(x,Y_{i})^{-1}
, we can estimate

\displaystyle(\widetilde{qw}_{n})(x,y)^{-p}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(x,Y_{i})}\middle|Y_{1}=y\right]^{p}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(x,Y_{i})^{-1}\middle|Y_{1}=y\right]^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w(x,Y_{i})^{-p}\middle|Y_{1}=y\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\cdot w(x,y)^{-p}+\frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\int w(x,y^{\prime})^{-p}q(x,\dif y^{\prime}),

For the numerator, instead, just note that one application of Jensen’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{qw}_{n})(x,Y^{[n]})^{p}]\leq\frac{1}{n}\cdot w(x,y)^{p}+\frac{n-1}{n}\int w(x,y^{\prime})^{p}q(x,\dif y^{\prime}).

Recalling that
\varpi(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=w(x,y)/(qw)(x)
, using the above bounds and multiplying and dividing by
(qw)(x)
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\frac{(\widehat{qw}_{n})(x,Y^{[n]})}{(\widetilde{qw})_{n}(x,y)}\bigg)^{p}\middle|Y_{1}=y\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\cdot\varpi(x,y)^{p}+\frac{n-1}{n}\int\varpi(x,y^{\prime})^{p}q(x,\dif y^{\prime})\right)\cdot

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}\cdot\varpi(x,y)^{-p}+\frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\int\varpi(x,y^{\prime})^{-p}q(x,\dif y^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\cdot\varpi(x,y)^{p}+\frac{n-1}{n}\int\varpi(x,y)^{p}q(x,\dif y^{\prime})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\cdot\varpi(x,y)^{-p}+\frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\int\varpi(x,y^{\prime})^{-p}q(x,\dif y^{\prime})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\cdot\left(\varpi(x,y)^{2p}+\varpi(x,y)^{-2p}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{n^{2}}\left(\int\varpi(x,y^{\prime})^{2p}q(x,\dif y^{\prime})+\int\varpi(x,y^{\prime})^{-2p}q(x,\dif y^{\prime})\right)

where the penultimate inequality follows by Young’s inequality
ab\leq a^{2}/2+b^{2}/2
and the final inequality by
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
and Jensen’s inequality.
Upon integration in
\pi\otimes\widetilde{P}_{n}
we thus get

\displaystyle(\pi\otimes\widetilde{P}_{n})\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(\widehat{qw}_{n})(X,Y^{[n]})}{(\widetilde{qw}_{n})(Y,X)}\middle|Z_{1}=X\right]^{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}}\cdot\left(\pi\otimes\widetilde{P}_{n}(\varpi(X,Y)^{2p})+\pi\otimes\widetilde{P}_{n}(\varpi(X,Y)^{-2p})\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2(n-1)^{2}}{n^{2}}\left((\pi\otimes q)(\varpi(X,Y)^{2p})+(\pi\otimes q)(\varpi(X,Y)^{-2p})\right).

(18)

The first two summands multiplied by
n^{-2}
are negligible. In fact,
with the very crude bound
(\widetilde{q\varpi}_{n})(x,y)^{-1}\leq n\cdot\varpi(x,y)^{-1}
, and by observing that
\varpi(x,x)=(qw)(x)^{-1}
,

\displaystyle(\pi\otimes\widetilde{P}_{n})(\varpi(X,Y)^{2p})

\displaystyle\leq\int\frac{\varpi(x,y)^{2p}w(x,y)}{(\widetilde{qw}_{n})(x,y)}\pi(\dif x)q(x,\dif y)+\int(qw)(x)^{-1}\pi(\dif x)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\varpi(x,y)^{2p+1}}{(\widetilde{q\varpi}_{n})(x,y)}\pi(\dif x)q(x,\dif y)+\int(qw)(x)^{-1}\pi(\dif x)

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot(\pi\otimes q)(\varpi(X,Y)^{2p})+\int(qw)(x)^{-1}\pi(\dif x)

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot(\pi\otimes q)(\varpi(X,Y)^{2p})+(\pi\otimes q)(w(X,Y)^{-1})=K_{2}\cdot n^{-1}

where the last bound on the rightmost term follows from Jensen’s inequality
(qw)(x)^{-1}\leq\int w(x,y)^{-1}\cdot q(\dif y)
. One can do analogous computation for
(\pi\otimes\widetilde{P}_{n})(\varpi(X,Y)^{-2p})
. Combining these estimates with (
18
) shows

\displaystyle(\pi\otimes\widetilde{P}_{n})\bigg(\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(\widehat{qw}_{n})(X,Y^{[n]})}{(\widetilde{qw})_{n}(X,Y)}\middle|Y_{1}=Y\right]^{p}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq K_{2}\cdot n^{-1}+\frac{2(n-1)^{2}}{n^{2}}\left((\pi\otimes q)(\varpi(X,Y)^{2p})+(\pi\otimes q)(\varpi(X,Y)^{-2p})\right)

\displaystyle\leq K_{2}\cdot n^{-1}+K_{3}\cdot n^{-2}+2\cdot(M_{\varpi}(2p)+M_{\varpi}(2p))

for a rolling constants
K_{2},K_{3}
, and the final bound follows upon combining this with (
17
).

∎

B.1

Fix an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
. By the definition of conductance, for any arbitrary set

, we can write

\Phi(P_{n})\leq\frac{(\pi\otimes P_{n})(A\times A^{\complement})}{\pi(A)}=\int P_{n}(x,A^{\complement})\pi\rvert_{A}(\dif x)\leq\int\alpha_{n}(x)\pi\rvert_{A}(\dif x),

where
\pi\rvert_{A}
denotes the restriction of
\pi
to
A
. Write
\alpha_{n}(x)\leq\alpha_{\infty}(x)+|\alpha_{\infty}(x)-\alpha_{n}(x)|
.
By
[
12

,
\alpha_{\infty}(x)\leq\exp\bigg(-|x|^{2}\cdot c_{1,\sigma}\bigg)\cdot c_{2,\sigma}
with

Let
R_{\varepsilon}>0

\exp(-|x|^{2}\cdot c_{1,\sigma})\cdot c_{2,\sigma}<\varepsilon

|x|>R_{\varepsilon}

A_{R_{\varepsilon}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{x\in\mathsf{E}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}|x|>R_{\varepsilon}\}
from the previous estimates we have

\Phi(P_{n})\leq\varepsilon+\int|\alpha_{\infty}(x)-\alpha_{n}(x)|\pi\rvert_{A_{R_{\varepsilon}}}(\dif x).

and because, as we show below,
|\alpha_{\infty}(x)-\alpha_{n}(x)|\rightarrow 0
for all
x\in\mathsf{E}
, the bounded convergence theorem shows that the rightmost integral can be made lower than
\varepsilon
for
n
sufficiently large. It follows that
\Phi(P_{n})\leq 2\cdot\varepsilon
for
n

(\widehat{qw}_{n})(x,Y^{[n]})\rightarrow(qw)(x)

[
12
]

\widetilde{q}^{w}_{n}(x,\cdot)\rightarrow q^{w}(x,\cdot)

and the claim follows by dominated convergence.
∎

B.2
Proof of Lemma

Consider (
10
) and let

.
