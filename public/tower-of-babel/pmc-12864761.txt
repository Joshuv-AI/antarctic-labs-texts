Systematic review and meta-analysis of regulator-approved deep learning systems for fundus diabetic retinopathy detections
Authors: Ting-Wei Wang, Wei-Ting Luo, Yu-Kang Tu, Yu-Bai Chou, Yu-Te Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To clarify the real-world performance of regulator-approved deep-learning (DL) systems for autonomous diabetic retinopathy (DR) screening, we systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and ClinicalTrials.gov to 3 April 2025, identifying 82 studies (887,244 examinations) covering 25 devices in 28 countries. Hierarchical bivariate meta-analysis yielded pooled sensitivity/specificity of 0.93/0.90 on a per-patient basis and 0.92/0.93 per eye, closely paralleling expert grading. Meta-regression showed that DR severity threshold, national-income level, image gradability, pupil dilation, reference standard, and diagnostic criteria collectively explained most between-study heterogeneity; any-DR screening, low-income settings, or ungradable images increased false-positive rates, whereas dilated pupils, portable cameras, and adjudicated references improved specificity. Publication bias was minimal. Overall, regulator-approved DL algorithms provide accurate, scalable DR detection, but programs must tailor deployment and reimbursement to disease threshold, image quality, and local resources, and post-market audits with standardized gradability metrics are needed to ensure safe, equitable global adoption.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is one of the most common microvascular complications of diabetes mellitus and a leading cause of preventable blindness among working-age adults worldwide1,2. A landmark meta-analysis estimated that 93 million individuals had DR in 2010, including 28 million with vision-threatening stages1. With global diabetes prevalence projected to reach 783 million by 20453, the number living with DR will rise commensurately. Timely identification and treatment can avert up to 90% of severe vision loss4,5.

Regular retinal screening is, therefore, essential. Current guidelines recommend annual or biennial dilated fundus examinations for people with diabetes6. Yet real-world coverage remains sub-optimal: a recent meta-analysis found that only about 66% of patients in high-income countries and 39% in low- and middle-income countries receive guideline-recommended eye examinations7. Barriers include limited awareness, travel costs, and persistent shortages of trained graders and ophthalmologists, particularly in resource-constrained settings8. In the United Kingdom, the national diabetic eye screening program has contributed to substantial reductions in DR-related blindness; while DR is no longer the leading cause of certifiable blindness in working-age adults, it remains a major contributor9.

Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning offer a scalable solution. Gulshan et al. first reported expert-level performance, achieving sensitivity > 90% and specificity ≈ 98% for referable DR on >100 000 images10. Subsequent systems validated in multi-ethnic cohorts11 and prospective trials across Africa and Asia12 confirm that deep learning can detect DR lesions reliably. A 2025 meta-analysis pooling 21 studies estimated pooled sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 91% for deep-learning models versus human grading13, suggesting that autonomous AI could markedly expand screening capacity and accessibility11,12.

Real-world deployment, however, demands rigorous external validation and regulatory oversight. In 2018 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) granted de-novo clearance to the first autonomous DR-screening device (IDx-DR) after a pivotal prospective trial demonstrated 87% sensitivity and 91% specificity for more-than-mild DR in primary-care settings14. Other systems—including EyeArt and SELENA+—have since obtained FDA 510(k) clearance or Conformité Européenne (CE), marking the strength of similarly robust trials15,16. Early implementation studies show that these tools integrate smoothly into routine workflows and maintain high diagnostic accuracy at scale16,17. Nevertheless, concerns persist about generalizability across populations, algorithmic bias, and post-market performance drift18,19. Regulator-approval is thus a critical milestone—but not the final step—in ensuring safe and equitable adoption.

Previous reviews have largely evaluated experimental AI algorithms, many of which are not yet available for clinical use13. Clinicians, payers, and policymakers, therefore, lack consolidated evidence focused exclusively on regulator-cleared, commercially available AI devices. A recent review centered on a single cleared system (IDx-DR) confirmed high diagnostic performance in multiple studies20, but no synthesis has yet examined the full suite of approved tools. This systematic review and meta-analysis, therefore, aim to (i) catalog every FDA-, CE-, or other authority-cleared AI system for diabetic-retinopathy detection that is currently marketed; (ii) quantify their pooled diagnostic accuracy across independent validation and real-world implementation studies; and (iii) probe study- and device-level factors—such as imaging protocol, disease prevalence, camera type, patient ethnicity, and management of ungradable images—that may influence diagnostic accuracy.

Results

Study identification and selection

Our systematic literature review, depicted in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1), began with a search across PubMed, EMBASE, and ClinicalTrials.gov, yielding 8404 studies. After removing 1723 duplicates, we screened 6681 articles using EndNote, excluding 5518 for insufficient relevance. Further scrutiny of 1163 full-text articles led to additional exclusions for various reasons. Ultimately, this process resulted in selecting 82 studies10,11,14–16,21–97 for our meta-analysis.Fig. 1PRISMA flowchart of included studies.

Overview of study characteristics

Table 1 aggregates 82 evaluation datasets of regulator-approved deep-learning systems for diabetic-retinopathy screening conducted between 2016 and 2025 in 30 countries. Roughly two-thirds of studies arose from high-income economies—most often the United States14–17,41,49,55,56,66,70,92,96, Poland27,28,42,67, Italy32,34,35,45,82, the United Kingdom46,68,81,95, Germany57,62,76 and Australia38,91—while one-third came from middle-income settings such as India21,33,59,60,69,83,84,86,92,93, China24,31,39,43,51–54,58,65,71,73,78,80,85, Thailand22,61,89, Mexico25, Chile29,50, Turkey26 and the Philippines48, with only a single dataset from a low-income environment in Vietnam33. Just over half of the evaluations were prospective real-world or clinical-trial designs, the remainder retrospective or mixed. Primary-care and community screening clinics provided the largest share of data (≈58%), followed by tertiary (31%) and secondary (9%) centers, with a handful of mixed-site or nationwide implementations. Adult participants with type 1 or type 2 diabetes were typically in their sixth decade of life, female representation ranged from 31% to 100%, and sample sizes spanned four orders of magnitude—from a 33-patient pilot30 to a national registry of 207,228 examinations73. All clinically relevant endpoints were covered (any DR, referable DR and vision-threatening DR), with 26 datasets reporting on all three thresholds21,24,27,28,31,33,49,50,55,59,63,67,71,73,74,76–78,81–83,85–87,90,91, enabling comprehensive stratified performance analyses. For external validation, 36/82 studies (44%) used at least one fully external test set collected in a different country and/or ethnic population from the training data. These external cohorts spanned 22 countries across all World Bank income strata, including four lower-middle-income settings (India, Thailand, Philippines, Dominica) and one low-income cohort (Vietnam). Comparative overview of deep learning algorithms in diagnostic studies

Table S4 synthesizes the methodological foundations of the 82 diagnostic-accuracy studies of regulator-approved deep-learning screeners for diabetic retinopathy. Human-graded color fundus photography remains the gold standard: approximately 75 datasets (91%) used double-masked or consensus reads by retina specialists, with a third expert arbitrating any discordance. Clinically anchored reference standards are rarer: only about one study in eight verified ground truth through in-person dilated biomicroscopy or optical coherence tomography (OCT)-aided examination25,29,41,45,58,59,70. Image acquisition is remarkably homogeneous—most studies relied exclusively on 45° desktop fundus cameras—yet 12 incorporated wide-field, ultra-widefield or OCT inputs to probe peripheral or macular disease missed by single fields14,25,28,32,36,38,57,59,62,63,69,70,76,83,93. Pupil-management strategies varied: 15 datasets were fully mydriatic25,29,33,44,48,59,68–70,75,81–83,87,93, 39 strictly non-mydriatic24,27,28,30,31,33,34,37,40–43,45,46,49,51–54,56,57,60,62–64,66,67,71–74,77–80,84,86,91,94, and 26 adopted “dilate-if-needed” contingency protocols10,11,14,15,17,21,22,26,32,35,36,38,39,47,50,55,58,61,65,76,88–90,92,95,96. Single-field imaging predominated (≈60%), while two-field schemes were routine in hospital-based or smartphone studies and ≥3-field/montage approaches appeared in 12 investigations16,25,28,57,59,62,69,70,76,83,87,93. Vendor participation was declared in 30 datasets (37%), chiefly for IDx-DR, EyeArt, ARDA and RetCAD10,14,16,21,22,30,33,37,40,43,50,52,54,59,61,62,64,66,71,73,75,85,86,88–90,92,94–96, whereas independent validations—particularly of Medios, VeriSee, CARE and smartphone implementations—reported no developer involvement25,42,44,49,69,72,79,83,84,87. Collectively, the evidence demonstrates that cleared DL algorithms have been benchmarked across a spectrum of camera platforms, dilation protocols and adjudication workflows, yet still anchor their performance against rigorous expert interpretation.

Technically, the ecosystem remains dominated by Inception-family backbones (v3, v4, Inception-ResNet-v2) deployed either as single models (EyeCheckup, CARE, VeriSee DR, VISUHEALTH-AI DR) or as multi-CNN ensembles (IDx-DR with 5-CNN, EyeArt multi-CNN, ARDA® DL with 10× Inception-v3, RetCAD™ with 5-CNN, AIDRScreening with 3-CNN). Newer products increasingly adopt EfficientNet (Aireen®, LuxIA, EyeWisdom) or EfficientNet-V2 hybrids for improved parameter-efficiency, whereas Medios AI™ employs a MobileNet-lite branch to support on-device smartphone inference. Alternative strategies include DenseNet-121 + bilinear pooling (AI-100), VGG-19 ensemble (SELENA + ™ with 8× VGG-19), ResNet-101 dual networks (Autograder), feature-pyramid ensembles (VoxelCloud Retina with 6× Inception-ResNet-v2 + FPN), and proprietary CNN architectures with heat-map lesion localization (OphtAI). Retmarker employs a deep-learning classifier with MA-turnover biomarker analysis for progression and DME risk assessment. In short, although cleared screeners now span a broad geography and a widening range of regulators, most still converge on convolutional backbones from the Inception/EfficientNet families, often boosted by ensemble voting to maximize robustness across diverse imaging hardware and care settings.

Quality assessment

According to QUADAS-2 criteria, 56.1% of studies were rated low risk of bias for patient selection, 57.3% for the index test, 52.4% for the reference standard, and 41.5% for flow and timing. Flow and timing remained a frequent issue, with 17.1% of studies showing high risk—often due to substantial image exclusion or delays between clinical evaluation and AI testing. Unclear risk of bias was also common, particularly for the index test (42.7%) and patient selection (41.5%), with the reference standard showing 37.8% unclear ratings. Applicability concerns were generally minor: 76.8% of studies had low concern for patient spectrum, 92.7% for the index test, and 52.4% for the reference standard. However, 45.1% had unclear applicability for the reference standard, reflecting inconsistency with ETDRS or Wisconsin Reading Center protocols. In summary, most evaluations were methodologically sound and clinically representative, though approximately 1 in 6 exhibited high risk in either flow/timing (17.1%) or reference standard (9.8%) domains. These results underscore the need for prospectively registered, fully masked diagnostic-accuracy studies (Supplementary Fig. 1; Table 5).

Overall meta-analysis of patient-wise regulator-approved deep learning system

The hierarchical random-effects bivariate model revealed high between-study heterogeneity, particularly for specificity (τ² = 1.02; I² = 86.81%) and, to a lesser extent, for sensitivity (τ² = 0.80; I² = 31.77%). The negative covariance (–0.31) and correlation (ρ = –0.34) indicate the expected trade-off: studies reporting higher sensitivity tended to show lower specificity and vice versa. A highly significant likelihood-ratio test (χ² = 29,231, df = 3, p < 0.001) confirms that the random-effects (RE) specification fits the data far better than a fixed-effects (FE) approach, validating the choice to model heterogeneity explicitly.

After multivariable adjustment in a bivariate random-effects meta-regression, the pooled summary absolute accuracy of 115 tables of regulator-approved AI screeners remained high: summary sensitivity 0.93 (95% CI 0.91–0.95) and summary specificity 0.90 (95% CI 0.87–0.92) (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 2). These figures suggest that, on average, cleared deep-learning systems correctly identify more than nine out of ten true cases of diabetic retinopathy while correctly classifying roughly nine out of ten non-cases—performance levels broadly comparable to expert human graders. Overall meta-analysis of eye-wise regulator-approved deep learning system

The hierarchical random-effects bivariate model showed considerable between-study heterogeneity—greater for specificity (τ² = 0.62; I² = 85.53%) and somewhat lower for sensitivity (τ² = 0.79; I² = 37.63%). A small covariance (0.04) and weak correlation (ρ = 0.05) illustrate the familiar trade-off: studies achieving higher sensitivity tended to report lower specificity, and vice versa. A likelihood-ratio test strongly favored the random-effects specification over a fixed-effects alternative (χ² = 127,870, df = 3, p < 0.001), confirming the need to model heterogeneity explicitly. After multivariable adjustment in a bivariate random-effects meta-regression, the pooled summary of 88 tables remained high. The pooled sensitivity was 0.92 (95% CI 0.89–0.94) and the pooled specificity was 0.93 (95% CI 0.91–0.94) (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 4), indicating that cleared DL algorithms correctly detect more than nine out of ten true cases of referable diabetic retinopathy while correctly excluding roughly nine out of ten non-cases—performance comparable to expert human graders. Deeks’ funnel-plot test suggested no evidence of publication bias for these regulator-approved systems (p = 0.23; Supplementary Fig. 5).

Meta-regression of eye-wise regulator-approved deep learning system

Most moderators resulted in only modest shifts in diagnostic accuracy, but several effects reached statistical significance. Sensitivity was significantly higher in studies using mixed pupil-dilation protocols—where pharmacologic dilation was applied selectively—showing an 8% increase (p = 0.01), and when the reference standard included advanced imaging modalities such as ultra-wide-field photography or OCT, with a 5% gain (p = 0.04). In contrast, specificity was lower in studies conducted in high-income countries (−4%), in those targeting any DR rather than referable or vision-threatening DR (−2%; p < 0.01), and in datasets with high image gradability (−6%; p < 0.01), consistent with the sensitivity–specificity trade-offs observed in patient-level evaluations. Other moderators—including healthcare setting, camera form factor, study design, diagnostic criteria, index-test grader, field strategy, and vendor involvement—did not significantly contribute to heterogeneity (all p > 0.05; Supplementary Table 8).

Multivariate meta-regression incorporating five moderators (Fig. 3b, Table 4)—economic status, DR category, pupil dilation, reference-image modality, and image gradability—substantially improved model fit over the base model (AIC 78570 vs 6550; Supplementary Table 9). Based on likelihood ratio tests, economic status (specificity, p < 0.01) and reference-image modality (both sensitivity and specificity, p < 0.01) showed overall significant effects, while DR category, pupil dilation, and gradability did not reach significance in the omnibus tests. However, individual group comparisons revealed important patterns: Compared to systems targeting vision-threatening DR, algorithms detecting any DR had 4% higher specificity (relative specificity 1.04; p = 0.01) with a corresponding 17% lower sensitivity (relative sensitivity 0.83; p < 0.01), suggesting threshold dispersion across severity definitions. Referable DR systems showed performance similar to vision-threatening DR systems (relative sensitivity 0.99, relative specificity 1.00; both p < 0.01). Mixed dilation protocols showed a trend toward 3-6% higher sensitivity compared to non-mydriatic or mydriatic imaging alone, though these differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.15). Advanced imaging methods (including OCT) showed a 4% sensitivity advantage (relative sensitivity 1.04; p = 0.18), though this was not statistically significant after adjustment. High-gradability datasets maintained significantly higher specificity than low-gradability datasets (6% difference; p < 0.01), with no difference in sensitivity (p = 0.98). Discussion

Our review screened 8404 records and ultimately meta-analyzed 82 eligible studies evaluating regulator-approved deep-learning systems for diabetic retinopathy screening. Pooled accuracy was high—patient-wise sensitivity 0.93 (95% CI 0.91–0.95) and specificity 0.90 (95% CI 0.87–0.92); eye-wise 0.92 (95% CI 0.89–0.94) and specificity was 0.93 (95% CI 0.91–0.94), respectively—yet substantial between-study heterogeneity (τ² ≈ 0.5–0.9) justified random-effects modeling. Multivariate meta-regressions revealed six consistent performance modifiers: DR severity tier, national income level, image gradability, pupil dilation status, diagnostic criteria, and reference image. In particular, “any-DR” tasks, low-income settings, and high-gradability datasets reduce sensitivity, while low-income settings, “vtDR” task, standard color fundus as a reference image, other diagnostic criteria than ICDR, ETDRS, NHS, and mixed dilated pupil protocol, boosted specificity. Eye-level analyses echoed these patterns but with smaller effect sizes; once confounding was controlled, only DR tier and gradability retained independent influence. Multivariate models halved unexplained variance (AIC 5439 → 3909; τ² 0.077 → 0.041). Deeks’ tests indicated marginal publication bias for patient-level results (p = 0.04) and none for eye-level data (p = 0.23). Together, these findings confirm that cleared DL algorithms perform comparably to expert graders while highlighting context-specific trade-offs that should guide real-world deployment.

Despite the uniformly high accuracy, we observed substantial heterogeneity in specificity across studies. Added evidence on co-pathology performance. A further source of real-world uncertainty is the presence of retinal co-morbidities—most commonly age-related macular degeneration (AMD)—whose lesions can mimic or obscure diabetic lesions. Four prospective or retrospective evaluations38,43,62,77 deliberately enrolled mixed DR + AMD cohorts and reported AUCs of 0.88–0.98 with sensitivities ≥84%. However, specificities fell to 66–93%, highlighting a consistent false-positive penalty when exudates or hemorrhages overlap with drusen or geographic atrophy. He et al.44 further showed that cross-camera domain shifts degrade agreement more for AMD than for DR, underscoring the need for multi-disease, multi-device validation before deployment. These findings confirm that current DL algorithms remain feasible in heterogeneous case-mix but that additional multi-center trials—including other look-alike pathologies such as vein occlusion or myopic choroidal neovascularization—are required to quantify workflow impact and cost-effectiveness. In practice, the reported sensitivities and specificities varied, reflecting differences in populations, settings, and implementation. A classic threshold trade-off was evident: studies that reported extremely high sensitivity tended to have lower specificity, and vice versa98. This negative correlation suggests that some implementations tuned the algorithm to prioritize catching all possible DR (yielding very few false negatives at the expense of more false positives), whereas others set a stricter threshold to reduce false positives98. Such threshold effects are common in diagnostic tests—if you “cast a wider net” to catch every case, you inevitably pull in a few healthy eyes as positive. In the UK, the UK National Screening Committee (UK NSC) emphasizes that service load is highly sensitive to specificity and supports risk-stratified recall intervals within the English Diabetic Eye Screening Program (DESP).

Meta-regression analyses revealed substantial heterogeneity in the diagnostic performance of regulator-approved deep learning (DL) systems for diabetic retinopathy (DR) detection across clinical, technical, and contextual moderators. Among these, disease severity tier emerged as a consistent and statistically significant source of variation. Systems evaluated for any DR demonstrated lower sensitivity (by 2 percentage points [pp]) and lower specificity (by 2 pp) compared to those targeting referable or vision-threatening DR (p < 0.01), with an additional 4 pp specificity gain at the eye level with 17% corresponding sensitivity loss. These findings reflect an inherent trade-off between early detection and false-positive burden. While broader screening thresholds may increase case-finding for mild DR, they also heighten the risk of false positives, potentially overwhelming follow-up services. Programs aiming to detect mild disease must therefore implement second-reader workflows, risk-based triage, or structured recall systems to prevent unnecessary escalation of low-risk findings. In the UK, UK NSC guidance recommends risk-stratified recall intervals (e.g., 12–24 months vs 6 months) within DESP to absorb the additional false-positive burden when “any-DR” thresholds are used99.

The analysis also identified clear performance discrepancies across geographic and health system contexts. Studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) exhibited modestly higher sensitivity (+1 pp) but substantially reduced specificity (–6 pp; p ≤ 0.01). This likely reflects higher background disease prevalence, a wider spectrum of disease presentation, and variable image quality, all of which challenge systems primarily trained on high-fidelity datasets from high-income countries. In such contexts, the increased false-positive burden can pose a serious threat to already constrained healthcare infrastructures. This underscores the need for context-aware deployment strategies—including tiered referral systems, integration with teleophthalmology, and infrastructure reinforcement to accommodate the downstream impact of automated detection systems.

Image gradability moderated DL performance differently depending on the unit of analysis. At the patient level, low-gradability datasets (N = 42) showed +3-percentage-point higher sensitivity (0.95 vs 0.92; p = 0.01) but—9-percentage-point lower specificity (0.84 vs 0.93; p < 0.01), suggesting systems err toward caution with poor-quality images—a “safety-positive” behavior that may inflate referrals. At the eye level (Table 4), high-gradability datasets (N = 38) paradoxically showed—6-percentage-point lower specificity (0.90 vs 0.96; p < 0.01) with no significant sensitivity difference, likely reflecting that datasets excluding more ungradable images retain harder borderline cases. These findings support real-time image quality assessment and recapture prompts at point of care to ensure optimal DL performance and minimize unnecessary escalations. The UK NSC calls for standardized gradability reporting to enable cross-site benchmarking and automated audit pipelines99.

Vendor involvement emerged as a significant moderator at the patient level: non-industry studies (N = 79) reported +7-percentage-point higher sensitivity (0.95 vs 0.88; p < 0.01) but—10-percentage-point lower specificity (0.86 vs 0.96; p < 0.01) compared to vendor-involved evaluations (N = 36). However, at the eye level (Table 4), vendor involvement showed no significant effect: non-vendor studies (N = 46) had only—3-percentage-point lower sensitivity (0.90 vs 0.93; p = 0.21) and identical specificity (0.93 vs 0.93; p = 0.61) compared to vendor-involved studies (N = 42). This discrepancy between patient-level and eye-level analyses suggests the patient-level differences may reflect study design factors rather than vendor influence per se. These findings underscore the need for preregistered, independently conducted validations to complement regulatory submissions. The UK National Screening Committee (UK NSC) lists ‘independent, head-to-head evaluations on identical datasets’ as a core evidence domain for adoption99.

On the technical front, portable fundus cameras delivered significantly higher specificity (+5 pp; p ≈ 0.01) without a loss in sensitivity, affirming their value in mobile or community-based screening programs, particularly in resource-limited environments. Smartphone-based systems, in contrast, showed no significant deviation in performance relative to desktop platforms (p > 0.50), suggesting functional equivalence across capture modalities in adequately trained models. Reference imaging modality was significantly associated with between-study heterogeneity, although within-group differences were not statistically significant, suggesting that certain advanced imaging techniques (e.g., ultra-widefield [UWF], OCT) may offer marginal sensitivity benefits that dissipate after adjustment for confounders. Similarly, single-field imaging protocols demonstrated higher specificity in univariate analysis (+7 pp; p < 0.01), although this benefit did not persist in multivariate models, indicating that field strategy alone is not a dominant performance driver.

Other factors—including study design, healthcare tier, pupil dilation, diagnostic criteria, index test grader, field strategy, camera brand, and article type—were not significantly associated with performance in adjusted models (all p > 0.05). This suggests a reassuring robustness of pooled estimates across a wide range of operational conditions and affirms the validity of aggregated performance metrics for regulatory and policy decision-making. However, interaction modeling provided further nuance: performance gains associated with non-vendor studies were concentrated in high-gradability datasets, while specificity losses were most pronounced in low-gradability scenarios. Portable camera advantages persisted across interaction strata, highlighting their resilience to variable imaging conditions.

Taken together, these findings emphasize that the diagnostic performance of DL systems is not static, but rather deeply context-sensitive—shaped by disease target, image quality, geographic setting, technical infrastructure, and transparency of evaluation. As such, regulatory frameworks should move beyond one-size-fits-all benchmarks and adopt dynamic, context-aware approval and surveillance models that reflect real-world deployment conditions. Clinically, implementation should be accompanied by workflow adaptations to address false-positive burden and optimize human-AI collaboration. For health systems—especially those in LMICs—investment in triage capacity, image quality assurance, and portable imaging technology will be essential to realizing the full benefits of AI-assisted DR screening while maintaining efficiency and equity.

Our findings are broadly consistent with and extend previous meta-analyses of deep learning algorithms for diabetic retinopathy (DR) detection. Earlier reviews focusing on experimental, non-regulatory systems reported pooled sensitivities between 87–90% and specificities around 88–92%100. In contrast, our analysis—restricted to regulator-approved systems—demonstrates slightly higher sensitivity (93%) with comparable specificity (90%), suggesting that the regulatory pathway may favor top-performing models. For instance, our pooled sensitivity surpasses the 87.4% reported in the pivotal trial of the first FDA-cleared system, IDx-DR14, and aligns closely with EyeArt’s U.S. FDA trial (96% sensitivity, 88% specificity)15 and UK real-world deployments showing 95–97% sensitivity68.

Autonomous AI screening offers a scalable solution for expanding DR detection beyond ophthalmology clinics. It enables point-of-care use in primary care, endocrinology, and community settings—reaching patients who might otherwise miss annual exams. In a randomized trial, 100% of pediatric diabetes patients completed screening with autonomous AI compared to 22% under standard referral pathways101. In the UK, AI deployment halved the grading workload while maintaining >95% sensitivity68. These findings support a safe shift toward upstream screening with manageable trade-offs. However, regulatory clearance is only the first step. The UK NSC sets out seven evidence domains—pathway-level accuracy, prospective clinical impact, cost-effectiveness, equity analysis, vendor-independent validation, interoperability testing, and post-market surveillance—for the UK context99. These domains map onto our moderator findings and research gaps. Early engagement between developers and screening agencies, plus staged pilots using stepped-wedge or test-treat RCT designs, are highlighted as preferred evaluation routes99. Policy shifts, such as U.S. Medicare reimbursement for Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) 92229, signal growing recognition of AI’s clinical value102. Scaling these tools globally will require national screening integration and reimbursement models that support sustainability. Importantly, our review highlights a persistent evidence gap in low-resource settings. Only one included study was from a low-income country, underscoring the need for greater geographic representation. Smartphone-based fundus imaging combined with AI shows promise for underserved regions26,45,87, but robust validation, public-sector procurement, and international pilot programs will be essential. To avoid widening health inequities, global AI deployment must be accompanied by investments in image quality assurance, usability training, and infrastructure.

This is the first systematic review and meta-analysis focused exclusively on regulator-approved AI systems for diabetic retinopathy (DR) screening. Key strengths include the largest synthesis to date of diagnostic accuracy data for these systems and a uniquely detailed moderator analysis. Notably, we examined previously underreported variables such as vendor involvement and image gradability, which the latter contributed significantly to between-study heterogeneity. Our emphasis on these dimensions directly addresses four of the NSC’s seven evidence domains (accuracy, equity, independence, and image quality), underscoring the translational value of our findings. We also separated patient-wise and eye-wise outcomes to avoid conflating correlated units of analysis—a limitation seen in prior studies. Importantly, we applied a hierarchical bivariate random-effects model with extensions to account for within-study correlation between sensitivity and specificity, inter-moderator dependencies, and study-level clustering, representing one of the most comprehensive modeling frameworks applied to this field.

A key limitation is the inconsistent reporting of image gradability; future studies should adopt a standardized gradability metric to facilitate cross-study comparisons. In addition, patient-level factors that plausibly affect gradability—such as age, cataract, or neurocognitive disorders—could not be examined because most publications lacked individual-patient data; incorporating these covariates in upcoming datasets would enable a more granular exploration of accuracy modifiers. Software versioning was not uniformly documented across studies. Because different versions (e.g., v1 vs v2.2) may materially affect performance and thresholds, incomplete reporting prevented version-specific subgroup or sensitivity analyses. Also, Our publication-based search strategy may have missed some regulator-approved algorithms that lack peer-reviewed validation studies. While publication bias could not be ruled out, we observed no significant asymmetry in funnel plots. Additionally, although the inclusion of a broad range of algorithms enhances generalizability, it also introduces heterogeneity. Fewer than half of the included systems have been stress-tested outside the setting in which they were developed, underscoring the need for prospective, multi-regional validation, particularly in low-resource and ethnically diverse environments. Despite these limitations, our findings are robust and have strong implications for real-world deployment. Future work should include head-to-head trials of multiple AI systems, implementation research assessing clinical outcomes and workflow impact, and the development of automated audit pipelines. Standardized Automated Retinal Image Analysis Systems (ARIAS) application programming interfaces (APIs) and open audit dashboards, as advocated by the UK NSC in the UK program, would also streamline ongoing performance monitoring. Further studies should evaluate cost-effectiveness, prioritize low-resource settings, and explore multi-disease screening with fundus photography.

In conclusion, regulator-approved deep-learning systems have reached—and, in many contexts, surpassed—expert-grader accuracy for diabetic-retinopathy screening, correctly identifying ≈93% of referable cases while excluding ≈87–92% of non-cases. Yet their performance is not monolithic: disease-severity threshold, economic context, image gradability, vendor sponsorship, camera portability, and grader expertise each shift the sensitivity–specificity balance, explaining roughly half of the observed heterogeneity. Programs that target any DR, operate in low-/middle-income settings, or work with low-quality images must plan for a heavier false-positive load, whereas those using portable cameras and retina-specialist adjudication can expect higher specificity. Independent, preregistered evaluations and post-market surveillance are essential to mitigate sponsor bias and dataset drift, and future studies should adopt a standardized gradability metric and report patient-level covariates (e.g., age, cataract) to enable finer-grained moderator analyses. With robust quality-assurance loops, harmonized regulatory oversight, and equitable reimbursement models, autonomous AI can extend high-quality DR screening to the millions who currently lack access, helping to avert preventable vision loss on a global scale.

Methods

General guideline

This systematic review was prospectively registered with INPLASY (registration number: INPLASY202550099)103. We followed the relevant items of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Diagnostic Test Accuracy Studies (PRISMA-DTA) checklist (see Supplementary Table 1,2 for item-by-item compliance)104,105 to quantify the diagnostic accuracy of all regulator-approved deep-learning systems for fundus-based diabetic retinopathy detection and identify key factors influencing their performance across diverse global settings. Title and abstract screening, full-text eligibility assessment, data extraction, and risk-of-bias appraisal were each conducted independently and in duplicate by two reviewers (T-WW, L-WT); disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. Because the review relied solely on previously published, de-identified data, no institutional ethical approval or informed consent was required.

Database searches and identification of eligible manuscripts

Two reviewers (T-W.W. and L-W.T.) systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and ClinicalTrials.gov from database inception to 3 April 2025 for studies that validated a regulator-approved deep-learning system for detecting diabetic retinopathy on color fundus photographs. For each database, we used a structured search strategy combining three concept blocks—condition (diabetic retinopathy and related retinal diseases), technology (artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, automated detection), and system/regulatory context (named commercial algorithms and regulator-approval terms)—with database-specific subject headings (e.g., MeSH, Emtree) and title/abstract fields, without language restrictions. The comprehensive search strategy is detailed in Supplementary Table 3. Records were imported into the Nested Knowledge™ platform for automatic de-duplication and audit-trailed screening. Titles/abstracts and full texts were assessed in duplicate against prespecified criteria; disagreements were resolved by consensus.

We included prospective or retrospective diagnostic-accuracy studies of deep-learning algorithms for DR screening using color fundus photographs in adults (≥18 years); mixed-age cohorts were eligible if ≥85% were adults or if adult-only data allowed construction of a 2 × 2 table. Any clinical screening/diagnostic setting was eligible; country/region refers to the validation dataset location. Index test: DL autonomous or DL-assisted classifier on fundus photographs (excluded: non-DL methods, non-fundus modalities such as OCT/OCTA, and non-image risk models). Reference standard: human graders using recognized DR schemes (excluded: algorithm-only references). Target conditions: any DR grade; analyses prioritized prespecified thresholds for referable DR (≥ moderate non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) ± diabetic macular edema (DME)) and vision-threatening DR, using author-defined cut-points. Study design: must report, or permit calculation of, TP/FP/FN/TN; excluded: development-only studies without an external or distinct hold-out validation. Primary pooling used independent validation only, defined a priori as evaluations on datasets not used for model development. Report characteristics: searches from inception to 3 April 2025, no language restrictions; we meta-analyzed peer-reviewed full texts only, using preprints/abstracts for identification and citation chasing unless superseded before the cutoff. We also performed hand searching of references and backward/forward citation chasing. Where studies reported distinct strata with unique 2 × 2 tables (e.g., by camera, threshold, mydriasis, model version, or grader/reference image), each stratum was treated as an independent sub-study.

Data extraction and management

Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers, followed by a full-text eligibility assessment. Data were captured with a prespecified electronic extraction form, and every exclusion—together with its rationale—was documented in the PRISMA flow diagram. For each eligible study, we recorded (i) bibliographic details (first author, publication year, country (geographic location of the validation dataset)); (ii) study design and context (prospective/retrospective, recruitment period, clinical setting, regulatory-clearance pathway, external validation in different country from training set); (iii) participant characteristics (sample size, mean age, proportion female, economic status (World-Bank income category106)); (iv) index-test specifications (device name, training data country and ethics, fundus-camera model, mydriatic vs non-mydriatic protocol, number and field of images, algorithm name, vendor involvement (participation in coauthor ship or provision of funding support by the algorithm manufacturer), regulatory certification, model architecture, image gradability); (v) reference standard (grading protocol and grader expertise); and (vi) diagnostic-accuracy data (2 × 2 confusion matrix, sensitivity, specificity, 95% CIs). We confirmed status in primary regulator databases (FDA De Novo/510(k), EU CE-MDR/MDD, UKCA, TGA-ARTG, HSA-SMDR, Philippines FDA, NMPA; MDALL/PMDA as needed). If no primary record was available, we corroborated manufacturer pages and press releases. When results were stratified by data source, camera type, AI model, DR severity, mydriasis status, and index test grader or reference image, we treated each stratum as a separate sub-study and entered it as an independent table in the meta-analysis.

Quality assessment

We appraised methodological quality using the QUADAS-2 framework107. Two reviewers (T-W.W. and L-W.T.) first piloted the QUADAS-2 signaling questions on a random set of five studies to calibrate interpretation, then independently applied the tool to each included record. After independent scoring, disagreements were resolved through discussion; if consensus was not reached, a third author adjudicated. Each study was rated for risk of bias and applicability concerns in four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow & timing, classified as “low,” “high,” or “unclear.” Where studies included multiple algorithm versions or imaging protocols, we evaluated the analysis with the highest detail level available to capture AI-specific bias, such as data leakage or threshold optimization.
