Mechanisms of Traditional Chinese Medicine-Derived Alkaloids in Colorectal Cancer Therapy: Molecular Targets, Preclinical Evidence, and Translational Prospects
Authors: Lin Guo, Wei Zhao, Haoyang Wang, Shuxuan Qiu, Ruiying Wang, Jingya Zhang, Shuo Tian, Mingsan Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlkaloids are nitrogen-containing basic compounds derived from plants, characterized by complex cyclic structures. They serve as key active components in most traditional Chinese medicines (TCM) and demonstrate significant clinical value in colorectal cancer therapy. In recent years, TCM-derived alkaloids represented by berberine, matrine, and camptothecin have attracted widespread attention due to their remarkable anti-tumor activity. Research indicates that these compounds exert synergistic effects across multiple pathways and targets by modulating key signaling pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K/Akt, etc. They play important roles in regulating tumor cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and reversal of drug resistance. However, despite the significant multi-pathway and multi-target effects demonstrated by alkaloid compounds, current research has yet to provide a comprehensive and systematic review of their intervention effects on colorectal cancer. To address this gap, this review systematically examines the mechanisms of action of TCM-derived alkaloids and their nano-formulations in colorectal cancer treatment. It summarizes the molecular pathway networks regulated by these representative alkaloids and discusses their potential clinical benefits and current limitations.Building on this foundation, this paper further outlines future research directions, including structure optimization based on molecular structure considerations, targeted drug design strategies, and the prospects of alkaloid nanodelivery systems in clinical translation. The aim is to establish a systematic research framework for alkaloid-based colorectal cancer therapy and provide a theoretical basis for developing novel high-efficiency, low-toxicity treatment strategies.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most common cancer worldwide and is the second leading cause of cancer-related death.1 With global population aging and lifestyle changes, the number of new cases and deaths is projected to rise to 3.2 million and 1.6 million, respectively, by 2040.2 Although current treatment strategies for CRC are diverse, they still face many challenges, such as significant gastrointestinal side effects associated with existing therapies, the high cost of first- and second-line drugs,3 and a low rate of curative surgical removal of metastatic lesions.4 These issues severely limit the effectiveness and accessibility of treatment.Consequently, exploring novel therapeutic approaches and developing efficient targeted drugs have become critical issues that urgently need to be addressed in this field.

The development of CRC is a multistage process driven by genetic mutations and involves multiple pathways (see Figure 1). Abnormal activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is a key driver of CRC development.5 APC gene loss, the most common early mutation in CRC, leads to β-catenin accumulation and continuous activation of Wnt target genes, driving the adenoma-to-carcinoma transition.6 Simultaneously, KRAS mutations activate the Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase/Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase (MAPK/ERK) pathway, sustaining cancer cell proliferation and producing a synergistic effect with the Wnt pathway. In the adenoma-to-carcinoma sequence of CRC, the Wnt pathway (eg., APC mutation) typically acts as an “ignition switch”, while the Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/Protein Kinase B/Mechanistic Target Of Rapamycin Kinase (PI3K/Akt/mTOR) pathway (eg., PIK3CA mutation) functions as an “accelerator.” Dysregulation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway (such as PIK3CA mutation or PTEN loss) leads to persistent Akt phosphorylation, which further promotes cell proliferation, inhibits apoptosis, and accelerates metabolic reprogramming,7,8 approximately 30% of CRC patients harbor PTEN mutations or silencing,9 and this “accelerator” effect often cooperates with APC mutations, markedly speeding up clonal expansion.The role of the TGF-β/SMAD pathway in CRC shifts from a “gatekeeper” to an “assistant”. While the TGF-β/SMAD pathway may suppress tumors early on, it frequently loses this function in advanced tumors and may even promote invasion and metastasis.10 Moreover, CRC development is closely linked to abnormalities in multiple genes, with the functions and regulation of mismatch repair (MMR) proteins and p53 being particularly important.11 MMR proteins help prevent tumor initiation by efficiently correcting DNA errors.12 Mutant p53 protein, induced by DNA damage, facilitates the accumulation of various genetic alterations and can acquire oncogenic properties by promoting cell migration, invasion, and metabolic reprogramming,13,14 thereby worsening CRC malignancy and contributing to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) resistance.15 Thus, CRC initiation and progression are driven by mutations in genes such as APC, KRAS, BRAF, PIK3CA, PTEN, and p53, and are orchestrated by aberrant coordination of signaling pathways including Wnt, MAPK/ERK, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, and TGF-β/SMAD. This complex network not only determines tumor growth characteristics but also influences drug sensitivity and resistance mechanisms. Given the high cost and strong resistance associated with current drugs, there is an urgent need to develop novel, low-toxicity, and targeted natural therapeutics. Natural products are recognized as a rich source of small-molecule drugs. Over 60% of FDA-approved anticancer drugs, such as paclitaxel, topotecan, and vincristine, originate from natural substances,16 underscoring the significant value of natural products in anticancer drug development. Alkaloids, a class of nitrogen-containing secondary metabolites widely distributed in nature, are considered important sources of lead compounds in drug development due to their distinct basic properties and diverse chemical structures. Studies indicate that various alkaloids exert anticancer effects through multiple mechanisms, including interference with DNA replication, inhibition of microtubule assembly, modulation of epigenetic modifications, and alteration of tumor metabolism, demonstrating significant anticancer potential.17 Among natural drug resources, traditional Chinese medicinal plants serve as a key source, rich in structurally diverse alkaloids and supported by extensive clinical experience. For example, vincristine and vinblastine derived from Catharanthus roseus, as well as camptothecin and its derivatives from Camptotheca acuminata, have played crucial roles in the development of chemotherapy agents, offering valuable insights for the development of anticancer drugs from natural sources.

Accordingly, we performed a systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and CNKI for relevant literature published between January 2022 and May 2025 (detailed search strategy in the Appendix and Figure S1). This review summarizes the mechanisms of action, preclinical evidence, and potential translational applications of TCM-derived alkaloids in CRC treatment, aiming to provide a reference for the development of related natural drugs.

Structural Classification of Alkaloids

Isoquinoline Alkaloids

Isoquinoline alkaloids are a large and complex group characterized by an isoquinoline or tetrahydroisoquinoline core. Depending on the attached side chains, they can be classified into benzyl isoquinolines, dibenzyl isoquinolines, phenanthridines, morphinans, etc.18 These compounds are widely distributed in the Papaveraceae, Berberidaceae, Ranunculaceae, and Menispermaceae families, with representative alkaloids including berberine (BBR), sinomenine, sanguinarine, and fangchinoline. Isoquinoline alkaloids exhibit potent biological activity; their unique structures enable them to inhibit tumor cell growth and spread, induce differentiation and cell death, block angiogenesis, and overcome drug resistance.19 The benzyl isoquinoline representative, BBR can trigger autophagy and cell death, suppress tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, and modulate the tumor microenvironment and gut microbiota.20 It also improves chemotherapy sensitivity in refractory cancers by reducing drug resistance and protects the heart, liver, lungs, and kidneys from treatment-related damage.21 Phenanthridine alkaloid sanguinarine primarily modulates apoptosis-related signaling pathways, including Bcl-2, MAPKs, Akt, and NF-κB.22 The dibenzyl isoquinoline fangchinoline shows broad-spectrum anticancer activity. Its mechanisms involve reducing Akt phosphorylation, downregulating key proteins in the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, inhibiting NF-κB activity, and decreasing CAF-secreted IL-8, thereby suppressing tumor cell growth, migration, and invasion while promoting cell death.23

Indole Alkaloids

Indole is an aromatic heterocyclic compound formed by the fusion of a benzene ring and a pyrrole ring, with tryptamine serving as the primary precursor for this class of alkaloids. They are primarily categorized into four groups: simple indoles, tryptamine-derived indoles, monomeric indoles, and dimeric indoles.24 These compounds are commonly found in Brassicaceae and Apocynaceae plants, with representative alkaloids including vinblastine, vincristine, evodiamine(EVO), indigo, indirubin, ergocristine, and hirsutine.Owing to their diverse origins, structural variety, and broad pharmacological activities, indole alkaloids have emerged as a research focus for anticancer drug development.25 Their anticancer mechanisms encompass apoptosis induction, invasion inhibition, and regulation of the cell cycle and miRNAs, highlighting their significant potential in tumor therapy.26 These alkaloids have multiple applications, for instance, EVO not only exerts its effects through traditional apoptosis and cell cycle arrest but also potentially inhibits tumor growth by modulating metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells.27,28 Vinblastine and its derivatives are known for their anti-angiogenic, pro-apoptotic, and immunomodulatory properties, establishing them as internationally recognized precursors for natural anticancer drugs.29 Vinorelbine is the most classic semi-synthetic derivative of vinblastine, offering improved tissue penetration and reduced neurotoxicity compared to the parent compound. Vincristine is a well-known anticancer drug; however, its clinical application is limited due to its cytotoxicity and the development of resistance. Combination therapy with other agents can significantly enhance vincristine’s anticancer activity, achieving stronger therapeutic effects while reducing side effects.

Quinoline Alkaloids

Quinoline alkaloids are characterized by a quinoline core,30 and are primarily found in plants from the Rutaceae and Rubiaceae families, with representative compounds including camptothecin and quinine. Over 300 quinoline derivatives have been marketed or entered clinical trials, with 93 demonstrating anticancer activity. Compounds containing the quinoline core, such as lenvatinib maleate, bosutinib, anlotinib hydrochloride, and brigatinib hydrochloride, have been approved for cancer treatment.7 These alkaloids play a crucial role in cancer development by inducing apoptosis, regulating the cell cycle, and interfering with signaling pathways that promote tumor growth.31

Steroidal Alkaloids

Steroidal alkaloids are nitrogen-containing derivatives of natural steroids, characterized by a steroidal ring system conjugated with nitrogenous groups (often heterocyclic). Based on their core structures, cholestane alkaloids can be classified into cholestane-type and isocholestane-type alkaloids.32 Cholestane alkaloids are typically isolated as glycoside forms,33 commonly found in Solanaceae (eg., Solanum species) and Liliaceae plants, while isocholestane alkaloids are primarily present in the Liliaceae genera Lilium and Fritillaria.34 Representative compounds include solanine, solamargine, lycorine, solasodine, and cyclobalanine. Steroidal alkaloids exhibit a range of bioactivities, including potential anticancer, anticholinergic, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, anti-ischemic, and anti-proliferative effects.35 Solamargine, a typical steroidal alkaloid,36 can reduce phosphorylation of protein kinase Akt, attenuate the expression of transcription factors SP1 and p65, decrease DNA methyltransferase DNMT1 and oncogene c-Jun levels, and promote c-Jun’s feedback regulation of ERK1/2, thereby inhibiting the growth of human lung cancer cells.37 It can also downregulate the expression and activity of matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2), inhibit the oncogenic factor LIF and its downstream pathways, induce apoptosis and autophagy in liver cancer cells, and suppress liver cancer cell proliferation both in vitro and in vivo.38,39 Additionally, it exerts its effects by inhibiting Erk1/2-MAPK phosphorylation, increasing the expression of lncPINT and lncNEAT1-2, which further supports its antitumor activity in vivo. This effect is achieved by inhibiting Erk1/2-MAPK phosphorylation and upregulating the expression of lncPINT and lncNEAT1-2. The inhibition of primary gastric cancer cells further substantiates antitumor efficacy in vivo.40 Solamargine can inhibit Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) activation, activate the Bcl-2/Bax/Caspase-3 apoptosis pathway, suppress continuous proliferation of lung adenocarcinoma H1299 cells, and promote apoptosis. It also reduces Snail/Slug protein expression, affects epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and inhibits migration and invasion.41

Pyridine Alkaloids

Pyridine alkaloids, derived from pyridine and piperidine structures, can be categorized into simple pyridine alkaloids and bis-piperidine alkaloids. These compounds are found in leguminous plants, solanaceous plants, and pepper family plants,42 with key medicinal plants including Sophora flavescens and Piper species. Representative alkaloids include piperine, huangtine, and matrine (MAT). Among them, matrine and its oxidized derivative have been extensively studied for their anti-tumor, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, anti-fibrotic, antiviral, anti-arrhythmic, and immunomodulatory properties.28 MAT leveraging its anti-tumor mechanisms and nano-delivery applications, demonstrates significant potential in intervening major diseases, offering new insights for both basic and clinical drug development. MAT inhibits cancer proliferation and metastasis by modulating key signaling pathways, including the miR-10b/PTEN axis, ERK1/2 MAPK pathway, and JAK/STAT3 pathway. It also promotes apoptosis and suppresses EMT by downregulating the Erlin1-HN1 axis, LINC01116/miR-592 axis, and the Bcl-2-caspase-3 pathway.Additionally, it further inhibits tumor progression and invasion by regulating the PI3K/Akt pathway, Wnt/β-catenin pathway, MMP2/9 enzymes, circ-104075/Bcl-9/miR-182-5P/miR-299-3P/PGAM axis, and the GPX4/SIRT1/YY1 axis.43

Terpene Alkaloids

Terpene alkaloids, a class of compounds based on isoprene units, can be categorized into monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and diterpenes, primarily found in plants from the Orchidaceae, Ranunculaceae, and Gentianaceae families. Research on this class of alkaloids mainly focuses on natural products and synthetic derivatives with core structures such as aconitine-type, atisine-type, and hetisine-type, derived from plants like the Aconitum genus. These alkaloids exhibit a range of bioactivities, including anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, antibacterial, analgesic, and cardioprotective effects.44 Aconitine possesses anti-cancer, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, immunomodulatory, analgesic, insecticidal, and androgen synthesis inhibitory properties.45

Others

Quinolizidine alkaloids are compounds built upon a quinolizidine core,46 primarily found in plants of the Fabaceae, Polypodiaceae, and related families. Notable medicinal plants include Sophora flavescens, Glycyrrhiza, and Astragalus, with representative alkaloids such as MAT and sophocarpine. Sophocarpine is a natural quinolizidine alkaloid.47 Numerous studies have shown that it modulates key cellular pathways, providing anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antiviral, antiparasitic, anticancer, endocrine-regulating, and organ-protective benefits.48

Organic amine alkaloids are relatively simple, featuring nitrogen atoms outside the ring or in open-chain forms. Examples include ephedrine and capsaicin. Capsaicin exhibits diverse pharmacological activities, such as anti-inflammatory, analgesic, anticancer, antioxidant, and metabolic regulation. It is efficiently absorbed in the mammalian gut (up to 90%).49 Advanced delivery systems like micelles, liposomes, nanoemulsions, and nanoparticles have been created to enhance its stability and bioavailability while minimizing irritation and toxicity.50 Nonetheless, some research indicates that low concentrations might promote cancer, highlighting the importance of dosage in its biological effects on tumors.51

Mechanisms of Alkaloids Against CRC

Inhibition of CRC Proliferation, Migration, and Invasion

Sinapine thiocyanate, a widely occurring alkaloid in cruciferous plant seeds, can suppress cell proliferation by downregulating Keratin 6A and its target gene S100 calcium binding protein A2 (S100A2).52 Capsaicin, a vanilloid-type alkaloid, and the research indicates that capsaicin inhibits CRC progression by upregulating Metastasis suppressor-1 (MTSS1) and Transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) proteins, thereby reducing cell invasion, migration, and the levels of Matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) and MMP-9.53 Chelerythrine chloride, a benzophenanthridine alkaloid derived from natural herbs, induces G1 and S phase cell cycle arrest and promotes apoptosis through Reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation. It also modulates the WNT/β-catenin and TGFβ2/Smad2/3 signaling pathways, reducing α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression and inhibiting the metastatic activity of CRC-associated fibroblasts.54

MAT can suppress activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in CRC cells by modulating the expression of agrin proteoglycan (AGRN).55 Cepharanthine, an alkaloid derived from the plant Stephania cephalantha Hayata, reduces the levels of β-catenin,Cyclin D1, and Axin2, as well as β-catenin mRNA, thereby disrupting Wnt/β-catenin signaling and effectively inhibiting the proliferation of CRC cell lines bearing APC mutations.56 Sinomenine hydrochloride, the water-soluble salt form of sinomenine extracted from Sinomenium species, increases the expression of caspase-3 and Bax while decreasing Bcl-2 levels and the p-PI3K/PI3K and p-Akt/Akt ratios, thus suppressing cell growth and metastasis through regulation of the PI3K/Akt pathway.57 BBR down-regulates the expression of aquaporins AQP1, AQP3, and AQP5, up-regulates PTEN, and reduces PI3K, Akt, p-Akt, mTOR, and p-mTOR levels, thereby modulating the PI3K/Akt pathway and inhibiting the growth, migration, and invasion of colon cancer cell lines.58 Gramine impairs the viability and proliferation of LoVo cells, increases intracellular ROS production, and lowers telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) protein levels, thereby controlling CRC cell proliferation.59

6-ethoxysanguinarine, a benzophenanthridine alkaloid screened from Macleaya cordata, targets maternal embryonic leucine zipper kinase (MELK) and downregulates poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) and caspase-3 expression in CRCcells. It also upregulates cleaved PARP and cleaved caspase-3, thereby inhibiting CRC cell proliferation and providing experimental evidence for developing new MELK small-molecule inhibitors for CRC treatment.60 Ethoxydihydrosanguinarine (ESG) can induce mitochondrial apoptosis in CRC cells and suppress their invasive and migratory abilities. Additionally, ESG targets and inhibits the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), thereby affecting downstream Akt/ERK signaling pathways.61

EVO, an indole alkaloid, can inhibit Wnt pathway activation by downregulating proteins such as forkhead box M1 (FOXM1) and β-catenin.62 EVO also activates the PI3K/Akt/p53 signaling pathway by upregulating tumor protein p53 (p53) and downregulating phosphorylated Akt (p-Akt).63 Additionally, EVO can induce CRC cell apoptosis by suppressing the mRNA and protein expression of key Hedgehog pathway factors such as Sonic Hedgehog (Shh) and Glioma-associated oncogene homolog 1 (Gli1).64

BBR can induce CRC cell apoptosis and suppress the malignant phenotype of CRC by inhibiting Hedgehog signaling cascades.65 BBR and zinc synergistically exert anticancer effects by upregulating caspase-3, caspase-8, and G protein-coupled receptor 3 (Gpr3), while downregulating anti-apoptotic genes such as proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA).66 Tetramethylpyrazine (TMP), a naturally occurring active alkaloid in the TCM Chuanxiong, can increase the expression levels of PTEN, cleaved caspase-3, Bax, and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1B (P27), as well as the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, while reducing p-Akt and Cyclin D1 expression. This suggests that TMP may induce CRC cell apoptosis by upregulating PTEN to inhibit the PI3K/Akt pathway.67 Alkaloid extracts from Peganum harmala cause cell cycle arrest at the G2 phase, downregulate glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β) and Bcl-2 protein, and induce HCT116 cell apoptosis by modulating Bax and p53 protein expression.68 Solasonin, a steroid alkaloid extracted from Solanum nigrum L., downregulates histone deacetylase (HDAC) and upregulates the acetylation level of p53 and p21 expression. The results suggest that this alkaloid exerts anti-CRC effects through the HDAC/p53 signaling pathway.69 8-methoxyflindersine (8-MF), a quinoline alkaloid isolated from Dictamnus Root Bark can induce CRC cell apoptosis and cell cycle disorder, involving the activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling. This indicates that 8-MF is a novel lead compound for developing anti-CRC anticancer drugs.70

Inhibition of EMT and Angiogenesis in CRC

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a dynamic process whereby epithelial cells partially or fully acquire mesenchymal characteristics, resulting in the loss of cell adhesion and enabling cancer cell migration and dissemination. Simultaneously, EMT drives angiogenesis through the secretion of factors and remodeling of the microenvironment, while the newly formed vasculature reinforces EMT via hypoxia and paracrine signaling. MAT disrupts actin filament integrity and the EMT process, significantly inhibiting the levels of phosphorylated c-Jun N-terminal kinase (p-JNK) and p-ERK. Additionally, MAT targets Claudin9 to suppress CRC cell progression.71 Rutaecarpine, a β-carboline alkaloid extracted from Evodia rutaecarpa, induces G0/G1 cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. It downregulates the expression of CyclinE, CyclinD1, CDK2, CDK4, CDK6, MMP-7, Snail, and N-cadherin, thereby suppressing EMT biomarkers and inhibiting cell migration and invasive potential through modulation of Wnt signaling.72

Anti-Inflammatory Effects

Pro-inflammatory cytokines in the tumor microenvironment, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-1β, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), promote cell proliferation and inhibit apoptosis by activating NF-κB and STAT3 pathways. Simultaneously, immunosuppressive cells like M2 macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) infiltrate the tissue, secreting TGF-β and IL-10 to suppress anti-tumor immunity, while also promoting angiogenesis and EMT. The Akt/mTOR signaling pathway governs cellular autophagy. Alkaloids can modulate autophagy by targeting upstream and downstream components of this pathway, thereby exerting anti-CRC effects. (−)-N-Hydroxyapiosporamide induces apoptosis and autophagic cell death in CRC cells and suppresses the NF-κB signaling pathway by disrupting the interaction between the TAK1-TRAF6 complex.84 Oxymatrine treatment triggers mitophagy in CRC cells, suppresses leucine-rich repeat sequences, promotes the translocation of Parkin to mitochondria, and reduces the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, which is involved in mitochondrial autophagy.85

It also reduces CRC cell migration and invasion by modulating EMT markers. In vivo models, oral administration of DHE suppresses lung metastasis of CT26 cells.87

BBR increases the abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Bacteroidetes, Lactobacillus, and Firmicutes, significantly downregulates IL-10 levels, and reduces c-Myc, DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1), and DNMT3B levels.100 It also increases Akkermansia abundance and suppresses the IL-6/STAT3 pathway.101 At the genus level, BBR reduces the abundance of pathogenic Escherichia/Shigella.102 BBR may inhibit Veillonella (V). parvula and attenuate V. parvula-induced immunomodulation of B cells, thereby blocking colorectal tumor progression.103 BBR enriches beneficial genera including Lactobacillus, Allobaculum, and Muribaculum, while reducing harmful genera such as Shigella, Dorea, Akkermansia, and Paraprevotella.104 BBR increases the relative abundance of beneficial bacteria while decreasing the abundance of detrimental species.105 Fecal Microbiota Transplantation(FMT) experiments showed that BBR-fed mice exhibited lower adenoma abundance and reduced expression of β-catenin and PCNA in intestinal tissues. BBR reduced IL-1β, TNF-α, and NF-κB expression. BBR pretreatment increased the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and potential beneficial bacteria abundance, while decreasing carcinogen-related microbial groups. Administration of Lactobacillus rhamnosus enhanced BBR’s anti-tumor effects. Overall, BBR pretreatment shows preventive effects against colon carcinogenesis, closely linked to inflammation suppression, tumor proliferation inhibition, and gut homeostasis maintenance.106

BBR increased fecal levels of butyrate, acetate, and propionate, but did not alter isobutyrate, isovalerate, valerate, and hexanoate content. BBR significantly inhibited TLR4, p-NF-κB P65, IL-6, and p-STAT3 expression, indicating that the TLR4/p-NF-κB P65/IL-6/p-STAT3 inflammatory-cancer transition pathway is suppressed. Fecal microbiota transplantation results further confirmed that BBR-treated gut microbiota is sufficient to alleviate colon cancer development in colitis-associated mice.108

The combination of MAT and BBR at a 1:1 ratio demonstrated the best anti-CRC effect. This combination increased Proteobacteria relative abundance while reducing Bacteroidota, Desulfobacterota, Patescibacteria, Actinobacteriota, Deferribacterota, and Spirochaetota relative abundance, along with RAS and p-ERK protein expression. Meanwhile, it increased Sirt3 protein expression. Results indicate that combined MAT and BBR therapy more effectively inhibits CRC than monotherapy. This beneficial effect may depend on improved gut microbiota structure and regulation of the RAS/MEK/ERK-c-Myc-Sirt3 signaling axis.107

Other Mechanisms

Alkaloids can induce tumor cell death through multiple death pathways, inhibit immune escape, and suppress the survival and metastatic capacity of tumor cells. Sanguinarine promotes intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, increases malondialdehyde levels, reduces glutathione levels, and downregulates glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) protein expression. It also upregulates STUB1 expression, thereby inducing ferroptosis in CRC cells via the STUB1/GPX4 axis.109 Solanidine treatment enhances ROS production, lipid peroxidation, and cell membrane damage. It reduces glutathione levels and upregulates ALOX12B, inducing ferroptosis.110 Quinocetone decreases the expression of PCNA, IL-6, p-JAK1, and p-STAT3 proteins while increasing immune killing rates. It may inhibit CRC cell proliferation, immune escape, and promote apoptosis by suppressing the IL-6/JAK1/STAT3 signaling pathway.111 Oxidative apoptosis is a recently discovered ROS-sensitive cell death pathway that is independent of caspase activation and does not involve inflammatory regulation. Activation of the KEAP1-PGAM5-AIFM1 pathway is a key signaling event that triggers oxidative apoptosis. Sanguinarine activates the KEAP1-PGAM5-AIFM1 axis via H2O2, rather than through traditional apoptosis or necroptosis, thereby inhibiting CRC cell growth.112 Hirsutine suppresses key Shh signaling pathway proteins including Gli1, Gli2, and Shh. It enhances CD4+ and CD8+ T cell levels in both xenograft tumors and peripheral blood, exerting significant anti-tumor effects.113 Extracellular vesicles from BBR-treated tumor cells dose-dependently reduce cell viability, migration, and invasion. BBR can reverse the oncogenic function of colon cancer exosomes, thereby significantly inhibiting the survival and metastatic capacity of colon cancer cells.114

Collectively, the aforementioned mechanisms constitute a comprehensive regulatory network model involving multiple pathways and targets, which accounts for the multi-tiered anti-tumor activities of alkaloids in CRC (Figure 2). Drug Delivery System Optimization for Alkaloids

Although alkaloid compounds have demonstrated promising therapeutic effects in CRC research, they face several challenges including poor targeting specificity, low water solubility, broad tissue distribution, and rapid elimination. Developing novel drug delivery systems based on nanomaterials offers a promising strategy to improve water solubility and enhance the anti-tumor activity of alkaloid compounds. This approach improves drug bioavailability, creating more opportunities for tumor therapy applications (see Figure 3). Recent advancements in CPT nanoformulations can be summarized in the following aspects.115 Additionally, CPT conjugated with tumor-homing cyclic peptide iRGD forms a covalent conjugate (iRGD-CPT). This conjugate exhibits significantly higher cytotoxicity in cancer cells than in normal cells, with improved cellular uptake efficiency. It demonstrates strong tumor-targeting capability in xenograft models and effectively inhibits tumor growth.116 Camptothesome, an innovative liposomal nanotherapy for CPT, is primarily internalized by CRC cells through clathrin-mediated endocytosis. It shows potential to trigger potent immunogenic cell death by upregulating three key markers: calreticulin, high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and ATP. PLA/PEI-CPT-SUR-DEX-APT enhances cellular uptake through receptor-mediated endocytosis, leading to increased CPT accumulation, survivin downregulation, and elevated apoptosis rates. It effectively inhibits tumor growth. As a multifunctional co-delivery platform, this system shows promising potential.118 Additionally, CPT has been incorporated into amphiphilic chitosan modified with PEG and oleic acid. This micelle formulation exhibits anti-CRC activity in vitro and in vivo, improves drug solubility, and demonstrates safety toward normal tissues.119

In recent years, based on the promising performance of BBR in CRC, various nanoparticle formulations (including polymeric nanoparticles, liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles, nanoemulsions, and metal nanoparticles) have been shown to significantly enhance BBR solubility, improve cellular uptake, enable sustained release, and achieve targeted delivery to tumor tissues.120 Self-healing hydrogels have significant applications as drug release carriers in biomedicine. Injectable self-healing hydrogels were prepared by reacting a conjugate of doxorubicin and oxidized carboxymethyl cellulose with polyaspartic acid hydrazide. When loaded with CPT, these hydrogels demonstrated enhanced anti-tumor properties and reduced drug toxicity, indicating their great potential for biomedical applications.121 Liposomes represent another effective approach to improve BBR therapeutic efficacy. BBR encapsulated in liposomal carriers exhibits high drug loading efficiency, high stability during long-term storage, low release in human plasma, and specific cytotoxicity toward colon cancer cells. The mechanism is likely associated with the induction of immunogenic cell death. This study provides new insights into the role of BBR in maintaining immune responses against CRC.122 Additionally, zinc nanoparticles biosynthesized with BBR (ZnNPs-BER) can upregulate pro-apoptotic gene mRNA levels (Bax, caspase-3, and p53) while downregulating the anti-apoptotic gene (Bcl-2), thereby promoting tumor cell apoptosis more effectively.123

Clinical Trials of Alkaloids for CRC Treatment

The robust preclinical research on alkaloid compounds has laid a solid foundation for their potential clinical application in CRC treatment. A prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multi-center clinical trial (NCT03333265) investigated the primary chemopreventive effects of BBR hydrochloride in patients with familial adenomatous polyposis. Additionally, the same research team conducted another randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (NCT03281096) to determine whether daily administration of 300 mg BBR hydrochloride could reduce the incidence of new colorectal adenomas in patients with a history of CRC. However, the results of these clinical trials have not yet been publicly disclosed. Another research team conducted a randomized, placebo-controlled, multi-center, prospective clinical study (NCT02226185) to evaluate the preventive effects of BBR hydrochloride against recurrence and carcinogenesis after endoscopic resection of colorectal adenomas. The study findings suggest that BBR may inhibit the activity of Veillonella parvula and further impair the immunomodulatory effects induced by this bacterium on B cells, thereby blocking the development of colorectal tumors.103 Six years after completion of the clinical trial, the research team conducted a retrospective cohort study. The results revealed that the protective effects of BBR persisted for at least six years after treatment cessation, with lower adenoma recurrence rates (34.7% vs. 52.1%) and lower tumor incidence rates (63.4% vs. 71.0%), suggesting that BBR may serve as a potential long-term preventive agent for adenoma recurrence following polypectomy.124

Limitations

Although TCM-derived alkaloids have demonstrated unique anti-tumor potential in CRC and other solid tumors, their clinical translation faces core challenges that extend far beyond simple efficacy verification. Beyond the well-known gastrointestinal toxicity and myelosuppression, the limitations of alkaloid-based drugs manifest primarily in the following critical dimensions:

Limitations in Structure and Chemical Properties

The unique structure of alkaloids often represents two sides of the same coin—pharmacological activity and toxicity. Taking CPT and its derivatives as examples, although these compounds as DNA topoisomerase I inhibitors possess potent anti-cancer activity, their core lactone ring structure is extremely unstable. Under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), the active lactone ring readily hydrolyzes into an inactive carboxylate form, causing rapid loss of efficacy and increased systemic toxicity, thereby limiting clinical dosing regimens.125

Low Bioavailability and Pharmacokinetic Barriers

Most alkaloids (such as BBR and aconitine) face severe absorption and metabolic barriers following oral administration. For instance, BBR has an oral bioavailability of less than 1%, primarily due to poor solubility from self-aggregation in acidic conditions, low permeability, P-glycoprotein (P-gp)-mediated efflux, and hepatic metabolism.126 Although aconitine possesses extremely potent biological activity, it distributes widely throughout the body, easily crossing the blood-brain barrier, resulting in neurotoxicity and cardiotoxicity. This low bioavailability necessitates high doses, which further amplifies toxicity risks.45

Dose-Dependent and Organ-Specific Toxicity

The toxicity of alkaloids typically demonstrates strong dose-dependency. They may exhibit pharmacological activity at low doses, but toxicity increases significantly as the dose rises. For example, BBR shows significant cytotoxicity against cancer cells at high concentrations.127 Alkaloid toxicity exhibits high organ specificity; the primary adverse effect of aconitine is cardiotoxicity. Studies indicate that aconitine can induce cardiomyocyte apoptosis and cardiac arrhythmia through ion channel disruption, mitochondrial damage, and activation of the p38-MAPK signaling pathway.128 Similarly, alkaloids such as arecoline may cause hepatotoxicity and neurotoxicity.129 MAT has high potential therapeutic value, but it exhibits hepatotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and reproductive toxicity.130 This non-specific systemic toxicity limits the safe dosage range for clinical application.

Drug-Drug Interactions and Metabolic Risks

Alkaloids are often multi-target molecules and potential inhibitors of metabolic enzymes. For example, although BBR has been extensively studied clinically, it may inhibit cytochrome P450 enzymes, leading to potential interactions with other medications (such as statins and anticoagulants) and increased risk of adverse reactions.131 This creates additional safety barriers for its clinical application as a single-agent therapy. In summary, the limitations of alkaloids have prompted researchers to develop semi-synthetic derivatives such as irinotecan and topotecan to improve therapeutic indices and pharmacokinetic profiles. Future research directions should focus on structural optimization, intelligent drug delivery systems, and precise metabolomic studies to overcome their inherent toxicity and pharmacokinetic barriers.

Discussion

The formation and progression of CRC is a multi-stage process, providing a critical window period for clinical prevention and intervention. Through literature review and analysis, it was found that current studies primarily utilize mouse models and human cell lines to investigate the mechanisms by which alkaloid compounds treat CRC. TCM-derived alkaloids can inhibit CRC progression through multi-layered synergistic mechanisms encompassing inflammation modulation, signal pathway regulation, microenvironment control, and induction of cell death. Alkaloids can reshape the inflammatory microenvironment by suppressing the NF-κB pathway, block oncogenic signals by targeting the PI3K/Akt and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, regulate the gut microbiota environment by inhibiting carcinogen-promoting microbial proliferation, and ultimately promote cell death through apoptosis or autophagy (Figure 4). The remarkable anti-inflammatory capacity of alkaloids can exert effects throughout the entire spectrum of CRC progression from inflammation to malignant lesions, offering significant possibilities for clinical application. Additionally, current research on gut microbiota primarily focuses on BBR or its combination with other alkaloid compounds. An analysis reveals that formulation innovations are underway based on the characteristics of alkaloid compounds, with extensive research focusing on nanocarriers for CPT and BBR. Regarding clinical trials, only BBR-related studies have been identified to date; however, this finding suggests that alkaloids possess long-term effectiveness in preventing CRC recurrence. Future research should further explore methods to improve the pharmacokinetics of BBR, developing it into an efficient targeted therapy, and potentially position BBR as a secondary prevention drug through “high-risk population screening” for precision prevention in high-risk recurrence populations. Summarizing current research, it is evident that existing experimental and clinical data enable the translation of these mechanisms into specific clinical applications. On one hand, alkaloid compounds can be used for long-term postoperative management and secondary prevention to block the transformation of adenomas into cancer. On the other hand, nanodelivery technology can address drug distribution issues, transforming alkaloids into efficient targeted therapeutics, while precise screening strategies enable early intervention in inflammation-driven CRC to reduce recurrence risk. These translational advances not only validate the potential of alkaloids in tumor suppression but also provide new perspectives for personalized and precision medicine. A systematic review reveals that TCM-derived alkaloids face several key challenges in research and application for anti-CRC: First, physicochemical properties limit their application potential. Most alkaloid compounds exhibit poor water solubility and limited stability, resulting in low oral absorption efficiency and inadequate bioavailability, which restricts their effective in vivo exposure.Additionally, systematic research on solubility modification and structural optimization remains insufficient, constituting a critical factor constraining further development. Second, in vivo behavioral studies and experimental models demonstrate significant limitations. Currently, pharmacokinetic studies on alkaloid compounds are limited, and commonly used experimental animals (such as mice) exhibit significant differences from humans in metabolic enzyme systems (such as CYP450), making it difficult to accurately extrapolate drug half-life and metabolic characteristics to humans.132 Furthermore, current research primarily relies on in vitro cell models or xenograft models, which lack completeness and clinical relevance, while humanized models that better reflect human physiological conditions remain limited in application. Third, safety evaluation systems remain incomplete. Systemic toxicity studies on alkaloid compounds are currently insufficient, particularly in the context of nano-formulations, where a unified and standardized evaluation system is lacking. This has constrained their clinical translation to a certain extent. Fourth, clinical translation progress has been slow. Most TCM-derived alkaloids remain in preclinical stages, with only a few (such as BBR) advancing to clinical application, and large-scale, high-quality clinical trials are lacking. Most ongoing studies are continuations or updates of existing trials, with insufficient new studies initiated.

Under the constraints of the aforementioned challenges, research on TCM-derived alkaloids for anti-CRC remains at a critical transitional stage from “activity discovery” to “clinical translation.” Overcoming physicochemical limitations, enhancing model extrapolation capabilities, and establishing systematic evaluation frameworks have emerged as core issues requiring urgent resolution in this field. Therefore, conducting multi-dimensional integrative research on cutting-edge directions such as nanodelivery, TCM self-assembly, and gut microbiota modulation may provide new breakthroughs for further development of alkaloid-based therapeutics.

First, structural optimization and delivery system innovation centered on physicochemical properties. To address the issues of poor alkaloid solubility and low bioavailability, physicochemical properties can be improved through chemical modifications (such as prodrug strategies and salt formation) and inclusion technologies (such as cyclodextrin inclusion). Meanwhile, nanodelivery systems (such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and nanocrystals) provide important means to enhance the stability and targeting of alkaloids, enabling passive tumor accumulation through the EPR effect while reducing systemic toxicity. Of note, self-assembled nano-systems based on multi-component interactions of TCM have gained increasing attention in recent years,133 where alkaloids can form “carrier-free” nanostructures with flavonoids, polysaccharides, and other components, simultaneously improving solubility and stability while reducing potential toxicity from exogenous carriers.

Second, constructing high-fidelity in vivo research systems to enhance the clinical relevance of mechanistic studies. To address the limitations in in vivo behavioral studies and existing experimental models, future research should prioritize the development of humanized models and novel in vitro simulation systems. For example, by establishing humanized metabolic and immune models along with organoid systems, researchers can more accurately reflect human tumor microenvironments and drug response characteristics. Furthermore, organoids retain the mutational profiles and microenvironmental characteristics of original patient tumors and can simulate authentic human CRC biological processes in vitro, the integration of organoids with microfluidic chip technology (Organ-on-a-Chip) holds promise for achieving dynamic simulation of the gut microbiota, immune system, and tumor microenvironment, thereby more authentically elucidating the mechanisms by which alkaloids regulate the gut microbiota and inflammatory responses.

Third, establishing a standardized, multi-level safety evaluation system. To address the insufficiency in safety evaluation, a systematic toxicity assessment framework covering both in vitro and in vivo approaches should be established. In the context of nano-formulation applications, specific toxicity endpoints such as complement activation-related pseudoallergy, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction should be prioritized, while long-term toxicity and biocompatibility studies are also required. Meanwhile, integrating multi-omics approaches with multi-modal data to construct specialized large models can enable AI-powered drug design and virtual assessment frameworks,134–136 which serve as effective simulation tools for alkaloid research and enhance drug discovery efficiency. Virtual clinical trial frameworks not only reduce development timelines and costs while minimizing reliance on animal testing but also have the potential to transform traditional drug discovery from a “trial-and-error” approach to a “prediction and optimization” paradigm.137

Fourth, promoting multi-disciplinary integration to accelerate clinical translation. At the clinical translation level, efforts should be strengthened in multi-center, large-sample clinical trial designs, combined with real-world evidence to improve data quality. Simultaneously, integrating multi-omics data (including gut microbiota omics) helps systematically elucidate alkaloid mechanisms and individual variability. Recent studies indicate that gut microbiota plays a key regulatory role in CRC development and treatment response, providing important targets for drug intervention.138 The combination of alkaloids with probiotics or nano-carriers holds promise for significantly enhancing anti-tumor effects through modulation of the gut microenvironment.139 In the future, combining AI-driven drug design with virtual clinical trial technologies will further enhance the development efficiency of alkaloid-based therapeutics and accelerate their clinical translation.

Conclusion

In summary, current studies suggest that the role of alkaloid compounds in CRC should not be confined to mechanistic descriptions of multi-target activity, but should be integrated into the broader framework of anti-cancer drug development for evaluation. Limited clinical studies, exemplified by BBR, have preliminarily demonstrated long-term intervention potential in reducing postoperative recurrence risk, suggesting that alkaloids are more suitable as candidates for long-term therapy or secondary prevention rather than as conventional cytotoxic therapeutic agents. Consequently, future development should follow a research pathway centered on “drug development optimization, clinical validation, and precise application”. Specifically: (1) At the clinical development level, multi-center, stratified clinical trials should be advanced with recurrence prevention as the primary objective, with emphasis on evaluating long-term efficacy and safety, while exploring combination strategies with standard treatment protocols. (2) At the drug design level, key challenges such as poor solubility and low bioavailability should be addressed through structural modifications, prodrug strategies, and nanodelivery systems to optimize pharmacokinetic properties. (3) At the evaluation system level, more human-relevant model systems (such as organoids and organ-on-a-chip) should be established to enhance the translational capability of efficacy and safety assessments. (4) At the new drug development strategy level, multi-omics data and gut microbiome regulatory mechanisms should be incorporated to promote the development of alkaloids toward targeted and precision therapeutic approaches. In summary, alkaloid compounds are at a critical transition stage from “active molecules” to “drug candidates”, and the integration of nanotechnology with artificial intelligence-assisted drug design is expected to systematically improve their developability and accelerate their clinical translation in CRC treatment.140
