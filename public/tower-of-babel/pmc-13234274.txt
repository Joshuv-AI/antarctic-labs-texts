Mitochondrial genome characterization and phylogenetic relationships of the subfamily Dipsadinae (Reptilia: Colubridae)
Authors: Carlos F. Prada-Quiroga, Paula Montaña-Lozano, Sergio A. Balaguera-Reina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The double-banded false coral snake (Erythrolamprus bizona) and the false coral snake (E. pseudocorallus) belong to the subfamily Dipsadinae, a highly diverse group of snakes (~ 800 recognized species) with representatives in the Neotropics (Americas) and Asia (Thermophis sp.) whose phylogenetic relationships and molecular evolution remain incompletely understood. Herein, we sequenced and characterized the complete mitochondrial genomes of these two false coral snakes and assembled de-novo 26 more mitochondrial genomes from dipsadid species from the sequence read archive (transcroptome) data to understand the molecular evolution and the phylogenetic relationships of this group. We complemented our analysis with 12 publicly available mitochondrial genomes yielding a comprehensive dataset of 40 mitochondrial genomes across the Dipsadinae subfamily. Each de-novo mitochondrial genome underwent rigorous annotation and verification prior to comparative analyses, including assessments of nucleotide composition, codon usage bias, selective pressure, and phylogenetic relationships. Mitochondrial genomes of dipsadid snake exhibited a conserved gene order; however, false corals (genus Erythrolamprus) showed distinct patterns of nucleotide composition and codon bias relative to other genera. Selective pressure analyses indicated that all mitochondrial genes across the subfamily are subject to measurable evolutionary constraints. Phylogenetic reconstruction consistently supported the monophyly of Erythrolamprus within the Xenodontini clade. By providing the first integrated comparative mitogenomic assessment focused on this genus, our study expands the mitochondrial genomic resources available for dipsadid snakes and offers new insights into evolutionary diversification, phylogenetic structure, and adaptive patterns within the family. These findings underscore the evolutionary significance of Erythrolamprus, a genus notable for its exceptional diversity and widespread distribution in Central and South America and provide a framework for future research on snake systematics and molecular evolution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48462-x.

Introduction

Snakes are ideal evolutionary models due to their remarkable adaptations through their adaptive radiation from a fossorial lifestyle (i.e., loss of limbs and one lung, elongation of torso and lung)1,2 to inhabiting terrestrial habitats consuming prey much larger than their own (i.e., increases in body size and musculature, highly kinetic skulls, and the evolution of highly toxic venom proteins)3. It has been suggested that snakes’ oxidative metabolism is functionally unique among vertebrates and thus an excellent system for exploring alternative metabolic functions. An evolutionary redesign of central metabolic proteins, together with modifications of the mitochondrial genome, may have contributed to the exceptional metabolic and physiological adaptations that shaped snake evolution and radiation3.

Within snakes, the Dipsadinae subfamily, with representatives in the Neotropics (Americas) and Asia (Thermophis sp.), is currently the most diverse in the Western Hemisphere comprising ~ 800 recognized species distributed in approximately 90 genera and representing one of the largest radiations of colubroid snakes (family Colubridae) worldwide4,5. This subfamily includes a broad ecological spectrum from fossorial to arboreal to aquatic forms that distributes in the Neotropics from southern North America through Central and South America, with many taxa endemic to restricted regions, and a specific group (Thermophis sp.) distributed in Asia (China)6. This diversity provides a valuable framework for investigating how genomic changes relate to the metabolic and ecological diversification of snakes. Phylogenetic studies based on multilocus molecular data have resolved several major evolutionary lineages within Dipsadinae, including tribes such as Xenodontini, Alsophiini, Philodryadini, Tachymenini, Hydropsini, and Dipsadini4,5,7,8. These tribes represent important evolutionary subdivisions within the subfamily and provide a useful taxonomic framework for investigating patterns of diversification, genomic evolution, and biogeographic history in Neotropical snakes.

Among Dipsadids, the genus Erythrolamprus Boie, 1826 (traditionally placed within Xenodontini) stands out as one of the most speciose, with approximately 50 described species inhabiting a wide range of environments across Central and South America9. Its members range from Honduras to Argentina, including both flanks of the Andes (Cis and Trans Andes), the Amazon basin, and the Lesser Antilles, from sea level up to ~ 3,500 m. Erythrolamprus spp. are medium-sized (50–150 cm total length), diurnal snakes with fossorial, terrestrial, or semi-aquatic habits; several species (e.g., double-banded false coral snake -E. bizona Jan, 1863; false coral snake -E. pseudocorallus Roze, 1959) are well-known coral snake mimics10,11.

The double-banded false coral snake is one of the most widespread and morphologically variable species of this genus, occurring from Central America to northern South America (including Trinidad and Tobago) in lowland forests, savannas, and agricultural mosaics10. This species is a terrestrial, largely diurnal predator, feeding mainly on other snakes and lizards, and exhibits considerable geographic variation in color pattern and venom composition10. In contrast, the false coral snake has a narrower and more fragmented distribution, historically centered in north-western Venezuela and Colombia, mainly in montane or semideciduous forests between ~ 900 and 1,300 m above sea level. Ecological information for these taxa is scarce, but its apparent habitat specificity and disjunct populations suggest a higher susceptibility to habitat loss and local extinction12.

Mitochondrial genomes have become one of the most widely used molecular resources for phylogenetic and evolutionary studies in vertebrates. The typical vertebrate mitochondrial genome is a small, circular molecule of approximately 16–18 kb that contains genes for 13 proteins (atp6, atp8, cob, cox1–3, nd1–6, nd4L), 2 ribosomal RNA (rRNAs; rrnS, rrnL), and 22 transfer RNA (tRNAs; trnX, where X is the one-letter code for the corresponding amino acid), along with a non-coding control region involved in replication and transcription regulation13. Several features make vertebrate mitochondrial genomes particularly suitable for phylogenetic inference, including their compact and highly conserved gene content, predominantly maternal inheritance, and the near absence of recombination14. Moreover, mitochondrial DNA generally evolves faster than most nuclear genes, providing sufficient phylogenetic signal to resolve evolutionary relationships among closely related species as well as deeper divergences. The relatively high copy number of mitochondrial genomes within cells also facilitates DNA recovery and sequencing, even from limited or degraded biological samples15. With the increasing availability of complete vertebrate mitochondrial genomes, comparative analyses of gene order, nucleotide composition biases, and evolutionary rate variation have become powerful tools for investigating phylogenetic relationships and genome evolution across vertebrate lineages13–15.

Despite their ecological relevance and contrasting ranges, no mitochondrial or nuclear genomes are available for either E. bizona or E. pseudocorallus, hampering robust assessments of their genetic diversity, phylogeographic history, adaptive evolution, and conservation genetics. Thus, generating complete mitochondrial genomes for these two species fills a critical knowledge gap, enabling researchers to test the effect of selective versus mutational pressures in mitochondrial genes, clarifying species limits and evolutionary trajectories, assessing genetic diversity and population structure, and providing an essential foundation for conservation and management strategies for these and related Erythrolamprus lineages.

Several mitochondrial genomes have been described within the subfamily Dipsadinae, yielding valuable insights into their systematics and molecular evolution. Yet, the absence of mitochondrial genome sequences for Erythrolamprus—particularly E. bizona and E. pseudocorallus—represents a major gap that constrains understanding of their diversification, adaptation, and conservation status. In this study, we characterized for the first time 28 Dipsadids mitochondrial genomes (tribes Xenodontini, Alsophiini, Philodryadini, Tachymenini, Hydropsini, Dipsadini, and Thermophis), two of them (E. bizona and E. pseudocorallus) sequenced, assembled, and annotated and 26 assembled de-novo from transcriptome data available at GenBank. We also analyzed phylogenetic relationships within the subfamily including 12 mitochondrial genomes readily available (a total of 40 species). We focused our analysis on understanding nucleotide composition, compositional skew, and codon usage, and evaluated selective versus mutational pressures acting on mitochondrial genes. By filling a critical genomic gap, our work provides an essential foundation for future studies on the molecular evolution, systematics, and conservation of Erythrolamprus and related dipsadid snakes.

Materials and methods

E. bizona and E. pseudocorallus sampling and mitochondrial genome sequencing and assembly

Tissue samples were obtained from one E. bizona and one E. pseudocorallus collected in the Colombian municipalities of Gigante (2°23′12″N 75°32′46″W) department of Huila, and Ibague (4°26’7.33"N 75°15’51.12"W), department of Tolima, respectively. Species identification was initially conducted based on external morphological characters following the diagnostic criteria and taxonomic keys available for the genus Erythrolamprus12,16,17, including coloration pattern, scale counts, and head scalation. Animals were collected under animal research permit (RESOLUCIÓN No 000009 from ANLA), approved by the University of Tolima, in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines18, and performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Individuals collected are referenced in the zoological collection of the University of Ibague, Tolima, (E. bizona; voucher 07) and the biological collection of the National University of Colombia (E. pseudocorallus; voucher ICN-R 14326). The caudal region was extracted and stored in 96% ethanol and kept at − 20° C until analysis. Each single sample was dried at 60° C for at least 8 h then homogenized in a FastPrep96 machine (MP Biomedicals) using sterile steal beads to generate a homogeneous mixture. A subsample of each homogenate was transferred to sample vials before DNA extraction and processed using the Dneasy 96 Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. 100ng/µL DNA extracts were processed using the Illumina DNA prep (Illumina) kit to digest, size select and index DNA fragments within range (100–300 bp) for mapping against reference genomes. Samples were pooled to 100ng/µL each. High-throughput sequencing was performed on Illumina MiSeq using v2 (2*250 bp, 500 cycles, maximum of 20mio reads) chemistry (Illumina) aiming for 250k raw reads for each sample.

FASTQ files were processed using VSEARCH v2.9.119 and Cutadapt v1.1820. Forward and reverse reads of each sample were merged using the VSEARCH “fastq_mergepairs” program with a minimum overlap of 10 bp, yielding sequences of approximately 313 bp in length. Raw reads were assembled de novo using SPAdes v3.15.421 with standard parameters. Contigs were aligned using both the checkered keelback snake (Fowlea piscator; NCBI access number: NC_061951.1) and the Bailey’s snake (Thermophis baileyi; access number: NC_035713) mitochondrial genome as reference to improve assemblage quality. The circular mitochondrial genomes map was generated using the online tool Organellar Genome DRAW v1.2 (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html, accessed on 20 Sep 2025).

Transcriptome mitochondrial genome assembly

Transcriptome reads files of 26 species of the subfamily Dipsadinae were obtained from sequence read archive data – NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra; Table 1). Files were downloaded to a local server and quality of the reads were checked using Fastqc22. Mitochondrial sequences were assembled using the MITGARD pipeline23, which uses Bowtie2 v2.3.5.124 to map all reads against the Bailey’s snake mitochondrial genome (reference mitochondrial genome). This process was performed with the ‘very sensitive’ option, which increases the accuracy of mitochondrial genomes mapping. Subsequently, de novo and reference-based assemblies were performed with Trinity v2.6.625 and rnaSPAdes v3.13.126. Finally, we aligned all contigs generated by the two software against the reference mitochondrial genome using Minimap2 v2.1727 (Table 1). We used E. bizona assembled mitochondrial genome (access number: PP736110) as reference for all Xenodontini sequences (genus Erythrolamprus, Xenodon, and Lygophis) and the Bailey’s snake as reference for all other species.

In a simultaneous process, Geneious software28 was used to map and assemble mitochondrial genomes of transcriptome datasets using a low-medium sensitivity setting. Sequences obtained with each approach (MITGARD and Geneious) were aligned with ClustalO29 to resolve ambiguous nucleotide positions, which allowed us to generate a consensus mitochondrial genome as complete as possible. The location and orientation of protein-coding genes (PCG), tRNAs, and rRNAs in all sequences obtained were predicted and annotated using MITOS web server30. Annotations of the PCG, including start and stop codon corrections, were manually curated. Because mitochondrial genomes occur in high copy numbers in cells, sufficient mitochondrial reads were recovered even from transcriptome datasets with variable sequencing depth. Assemblies were validated using both reference-guided and de novo approaches, and ambiguous regions were resolved through consensus alignment of independent assemblies (MITGARD and Geneious). These procedures ensured that variation in sequencing depth among species did not compromise mitochondrial genome assembly or annotation quality.

Mitochondrial genome characterization

We used the 28 mitochondrial genomes previously generated (two coming from ilumina sequencing and 26 from transcriptome data) and combine them with 12 mitochondrial genomes (final n = 40 species) available in the Organelle – Refseq database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome/organelle/) for analysis. We extracted and concatenated sequences of the 13 PCG of each mitochondrial genome (gene order in plus/plus orientation is as follows: atp6, atp8, cox1, cox2, cox3, cob, nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, and nad6) to obtain the nucleotide composition using MEGA X31. We identified the strand asymmetry in the nucleotide composition of mitochondrial genomes (GC and AT skew) according to Perna and Kocher32.

Bivariate scatter plots were generated to evaluate the separation of tribes based on AT-skew values of the PCG, D-loop, and rRNA regions (PCG– D-loop, PCG–rRNA, and D-loop –rRNA). 95% confidence ellipses were added using the stat_ellipse function of the ggplot233 in Rstudio (v2022.07.2 + 576) with R base version 4.2.134 to visualize the multivariate distribution of each tribe. Data were organized in a data frame, and pairwise correlations were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation tests (cor.test()). Scatterplots were generated with the ggplot2 package33, incorporating fitted regression lines and statistical significance (p-values). Differences among tribes were statistically tested with multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), using each variable pair as the response variable and tribe as explanatory factor.

Concatenated coding genes were used to calculate codon usage bias (CUB), relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU; excluding stop codons), and codon adaptation indices (CAI) via CAIcal software35, using as reference the codon usage table for the banded krait snake (Bungarus fasciatus, Elapidae) available in the codon usage database (http://www.kazusa.or.jp/codon/). We represented in a heat map the obtained values for codon bias using the pheatmap. The RSCU scores were processed in a principal component analysis (PCA) using the package FactoMineR36 and visualized using the package factoextra37. Sliding window analyses were carried out using a Perl script (https://github.com/CaioFreire/CUB) as implemented in the CAIcal software to calculate CAI values for each window with 100 codons (300 bp). We performed this analysis to numerically estimate the synonymous codon usage bias for the mitochondrial genomes and quantify the codon usage similarities between a gene and the reference set, as well as to identify which mitochondrial genes are highly expressed.

The effective number of codons (ENC) of the coding genes was calculated using COUSIN software38. In parallel, the G + C in synonyms (GC3s) was obtained using CAIcal. The observed ENC expected ENC and GC3s values were plotted using ggplot2 package33. The resulting plot provides a way to identify whether mutational pressure or natural selection is influencing the PCG of snake mitochondrial genomes analyzed39. We generated a neutrality plot in which we compared the mean value of GC content at the first and second codon positions (Y-axis) and GC content at the third codon position (X-axis). This plot is complementary to the ENC analysis since it takes into account the role of mutation-selection balance in the disparity of codon usage, which allows us to identify the effect of mutation pressure or natural selection40.

We determined the rates of non-synonymous substitution (Ka), synonymous substitution (Ks), and the ratio of both (Ka/Ks) to provide insight into the direction of evolution and its selective force on gene coding sequences. We aligned each PCG from the 40 species using mitochondrial genes of the Chinese cobra (Naja atra; NC_011389) as a reference, to identify possible differences in the results among tribes. We calculated the values of Ka, Ls, and Ka/Ks using DnaSP41 and plotted them using ggplot package33.

Phylomitogenomics

Phylogenetic relationships within the subfamily Dipsadinae were inferred from the coding genes of 40 mitochondrial genomes, with the Chinese cobra and the Eastern coral snake (Micrurus fulvius; NC_013481) from the RefSeq database used as outgroups. Protein-coding gene sequences were extracted, concatenated for each mitochondrial genome, and aligned with Clustal Omega29. A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic analysis was conducted using IQ-TREE42, and branch support was assessed with 1,000 bootstrap pseudo-replicates. The ML tree was constructed under the GTR + F+R4 substitution model. Bayesian inference was conducted in MrBayes v3.2.743 using two independent runs of four Markov chains for 10 million generations, sampling every 1,000 generations. Convergence was assessed by examining the average standard deviation of split frequencies (< 0.01), and the first 25% of sampled trees were discarded as burn-in. A majority-rule consensus tree was then generated to estimate posterior probabilities for each node. In the resulting phylogenetic tree, node support values are reported as ML bootstrap values followed by BI posterior probabilities. The final tree was visualized and annotated using Figtree 1.4.4 (www.tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/).

Results

Assembled mitochondrial genomes and genome organization

We assembled and circularized the mitochondrial genome of E. bizona from the sequencing reads (access number: PP736110) and E. pseudocorallus (access number: PP736111) with 50x and 62x coverage, respectively. Both genomes had a very similar genome length (E. bizona = 16,448 bp; E. pseudocorallus = 16,440 bp). Complete mitochondrial genome contains 13 PCGs, 22 tRNA genes, 2 rRNA genes, and two copies of control region (CR1 and CR2; Table 2). Gene annotation of E. bizona and E. pseudocorallus mitochondrial genomes confirms the presence of a duplicated control region (CR2) and a translocation of the trnL gene (Fig. 1).

Transcriptome mitochondrial genome assembly

Species assemblies of E. almadensis (access number: BK062896), Gomesophis brasiliensis (access number: BK063431), Helicops leopardinus (access number: BK063432), and Xenodon guentheri (access number: BK063441) have a high average read depth, especially those made by the Geneious software28, which had the strongest support with values over 18,000 (Table 1). In contrast, mitochondrial genomes of E. ocellatus (access number: BK062898), E. poecilogyrus (access number: BK062899), and E. reginae (access number: BK062900) showed low average read depth with values below 10. All access numbers for the mitochondrial genomes assembled from transcriptome data can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Mitochondrial genomes obtained were all compact, including the same altered synteny (gene order nad1, trnI, trnL,-trnQ, trnM), with few or no intergenic spaces, and some overlaps between gene junctions in PCG. All mitochondrial genomes had 13 PCG, two rRNA genes (12 S rRNA and 16 S rRNA), and 22 tRNA genes. The two relatively long intergenic spaces were assumed to represent the control regions with an average length of 787.4 ± 68.1 bp and 573.8 ± 138.1 bp for the CR2 and CR1 (between trnI and trnL), respectively. Out of the 26 mitochondrial genomes assembled, 16 of them had ambiguous bases (Ns), present exclusively in either of the two putative control regions. In all cases, these unassembled regions represented less than 4% of the mitochondrial genome, including Tomodon dorsatus (access number: BK063439; n = 551 bp, which represents 3.4% of the entire mitochondrial genome length), Philodryas nattereri (access number: BK063436; n = 420 bp, 2.6%), and Thamnodynastes hypoconia (access number: BK063428; n = 347 bp, 2.2%) as the species with the highest percentage of Ns (Table 1).

Nucleotide composition

A comparative analysis revealed that the sequences of the 40 mitochondrial genomes belonging to the Dipsadinae subfamily showed relevant variation in all components of the mitochondrial genome. The nucleotide composition of complete mitochondrial genomes exhibited obvious AT bias, with A + T content ranging from 55.5% (Borikenophis portoricensis, access number: BK063424) to 64.5% (Leptodeira polysticta, access number: NC_013990); all displayed a positive AT skew (0.0574– 0.1701) and a negative GC skew (− 0.4140 to − 0.3403), with variation among tribes. For example, species of tribe Xenodontini have a higher AT content (61.03% ± 0.62) than Dipsadini (59.15% ± 1.44) and Tachymenini (58.24% ± 0.58); the nucleotide composition ratio of A > C > T > G in all 40 sequences is shown in Supplementary Table S1.

The relationship between AT skew and GC skew in all complete mitochondrial genomes revealed a significant negative correlation (R = − 0.63, p-value = < 0.001; Fig. 2A). In general, mitochondrial genomes exhibited a positive AT skew (A > T) coupled with a negative GC skew (C > G). At the tribe level, Xenodontini, Dipsadini, Philodryadini, and Alsophiini clustered within the region of moderately positive AT skew and strongly negative GC skew, indicating a consistent enrichment of A and C nucleotides. Hydropsini and Tachymenini displayed comparatively lower AT skew values, reflecting a reduced bias in A over T, although they retained the characteristic negative GC skew. In contrast, Thermophis showed a more balanced nucleotide composition, with lower skews in both axes relative to other groups, positioning it as an outlier in the overall pattern.

The A + T content of PCG ranged from 56.01% (Thermophis baileyi, access number: NC_035713) to 64.05% (Pseudoleptodeira latifasciata, access number: NC_013981), with an AT skew from − 0.0064 to 0.1255 (Supplementary Table S1). The relationship between AT skew and GC skew in PCG revealed a significant negative correlation (R = − 0.632, P = 1.22 × 10−5; Fig. 2B). Across all tribes, PCG generally showed positive AT skew (A > T) and negative GC skew (C > G). Xenodontini stood out as the tribe with the strongest nucleotide asymmetry, clustering toward higher AT skew and more negative GC skew values compared to the remaining groups. Dipsadini, Philodryadini, and Alsophiini followed a similar pattern, although with less extreme values. By contrast, Hydropsini and Tachymenini exhibited weaker AT enrichment, while Thermophis was clearly separated from others due to its more balanced composition, showing reduced strand asymmetry.

Mitochondrial genomes of Dipsadinae contain two copies of non-coding region (Control Region), CR1 located between trnP and trnF with a length from 1,007 to 2,790 bp; while CR2 located between trnI and trnL1 with a length from 1007 to 1581 bp. In CR1 and CR2 the nucleotide composition across Dipsadinae tribes revealed a global AT bias and GC bias of this region with broad values for AT skew (− 0.2215 to 0.3254) and GC skew (− 0.4315 to − 0.2015), indicating an excess of A over T, and negative GC skew values, reflecting a higher frequency of C compared to G all across (Supplementary Table S2). The relationship between AT skew and GC skew was not statistically significant (P = 0.119), suggesting no consistent linear association between the two indices across the dataset. Notably, Xenodontini displayed a differentiated compositional pattern in the AT/GC skew space, highlighting its distinct nucleotide bias compared with the remaining dipsadid tribes (Supplementary Fig. S1A).

Ribosomal RNAs showed a sequence length of 12 S rRNA and 16 S rRNA of 927–933 bp and 1,467-1,485 bp, respectively. They are arranged in the genome between the trnF and nad1 genes, separated by the trnV gene (see Fig. 1). Nucleotide composition in 12 S and 16 S across tribes was positive AT skew (0.2356 to 0.3279) and GC skew (− 0.2672 to − 0.1533), indicating a relative enrichment of A over T, whereas GC skew values were predominantly negative, reflecting an excess of C compared to G. A significant negative correlation was detected between AT skew and GC skew (R = − 0.433, P = 0.0053), suggesting that tribes with higher A bias tend to exhibit a stronger C enrichment. Within this framework, Xenodontini occupied a distinct position in the AT/GC skew space, reinforcing its compositional divergence from the remaining Dipsadinae lineages (Supplementary Figure S1B).

The ternary analysis of AT/GC ratios across mitochondrial regions (Fig. 2C) revealed marked tribe-specific differences. Xenodontini exhibited the strongest asymmetry in coding sequences, clearly differentiating it from all other groups. Dipsadini, Philodryadini, and Alsophiini showed intermediate patterns, while Hydropsini and Tachymenini displayed reduced coding sequences bias with stronger influence of rRNA composition. Thermophis remained distinct with a more balanced distribution, highlighting its unique evolutionary trajectory.

The 95% confidence ellipse analyses revealed highly significant differences in AT skew distributions between PCG, D-loop, and rRNA (p = 2.06 × 10−9; Fig. 2D), confirming that nucleotide asymmetry is region-dependent. Coding sequences showed the strongest skew, particularly in Xenodontini, while D-loop and rRNA displayed distinct but less extreme profiles. Thermophis consistently exhibited a balanced composition, underscoring its unique evolutionary trajectory.

Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU)

Heatmap analysis evidenced that the most frequently used codons in the PCG of Dipsadinae snakes were AT-rich (Fig. 3B), with a strong bias towards CTA, ATA, ACA, and ATT coding for the amino acids Leucine (L), Methionine (M), Threonine (T), and Isoleucine (I), respectively. The most used codon in almost all mitochondrial genomes was CTA (35 of 40 species), followed by ATA as the most used codon in E. pseudocorallus, E. bizona, L. flavifrenatus (access number: BK063435), X. guentheri, and P. latifasciata (access number: NC_013981). As an exception, B. portoricensis was the only species whose most frequently used codon was ATT. The less frequently used codons were GC-rich. CGG, GCG, TCG, and CCG were the least used codons coding for Arginine (R), Alanine (A), Serine (S), and Proline (P) amino acids, respectively. The least used codon in most species was CGG (19 species out of 40), followed by GCG in 6 species, and TCG was the least used in the case of T. variabilis (access number: BK063440) and H. unaocularus (access number: NC_024164). The remaining 12 species shared CGG, GCG, and TCG as the least used codons (Supplementary Table S2).

To explore this in more detail, we used CAI analysis to infer expression levels for each mitochondrial gene (Supplementary Table S4). Similar to the distribution patterns observed in the ENC plot (Fig. 4A), the Hydropsini and Dipsadini clades exhibited higher CAI values than the rest of the family, with averages above 0.73. Notably, in these groups, expressivity increased progressively across the genome, reaching its highest levels in the genes of complex I (nad1–nad5), which showed values above 0.76. However, CAI values decrease markedly in the nad6 gene (between 0.37 and 0.5) in all tribes. A comparable ascending trend was also observed in Philodryadini and Thermophis, although their CAI ranges differed: 0.69–0.73 in Philodryadini and 0.65–0.73 in Thermophis (Fig. 4B).

Xenodontini and Tachymenini exhibited more variable CAI profiles across mitochondrial genes, with marked peaks of expressivity in specific loci. In both clades nad2 showed a pronounced increase in CAI values, while in Tachymenini additional peaks were also observed in nad4 and nad5 (Fig. 4B). Importantly, Xenodontini consistently displayed the lowest overall CAI values across the mitochondrial genome, indicating comparatively reduced codon adaptation and suggesting more constrained patterns of gene expressivity relative to the other tribes.

Protein-coding gene substitution rates

The rate of nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitutions per site varied markedly among PCG and tribes. Overall, Ka values were generally an order of magnitude lower than Ks, ranging from 0.03 to 0.33 and 0.29–2.42, respectively (Supplementary Table S5). Analysis of selective pressure across mitochondrial genes revealed both lineage- and gene-specific patterns within Dipsadinae (Fig. 3C). With the exception of atp6 and nad2, which showed the highest Ka/Ks ratios across tribes, all other mitochondrial genes displayed values below 0.2, consistent with strong purifying selection. In Xenodontini, atp6 values ranged from 0.76 to 1.26, with most species clustering near neutrality (1.0–1.15), whereas nad2 remained more constrained (0.80–0.95), reflecting predominantly purifying selection. By contrast, Alsophiini displayed greater heterogeneity in both genes (0.66–1.28 for atp6; 0.87–1.33 for nad2), with some lineages exceeding neutrality, while Tachymenini showed intermediate values (0.71–0.96 for atp6; 0.73–1.09 for nad2), generally under purifying selection but with occasional elevated ratios (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Table S5).

Phylomitogenomic analysis

Phylogenetic analyses based on the concatenated set of 13 PCG from the mitochondrial genomes recovered a well-resolved topology that largely reflected the recognized tribes’ relationships within Dipsadidae snakes. The phylogenetic analyses recovered a well-resolved topology with high support across most nodes (posterior probabilities and bootstrap values > 95%; Fig. 3A). Within the tree, species were grouped into two major and well-supported clades. The first comprised Xenodontini, Alsophiini, Philodryadini, and Tachymenini, which together formed a monophyletic group, and a second major clade included members of Dipsadini. In addition, the Chinese endemic genus Thermophis was consistently recovered as the sister lineage to these two main clades.

Relationships among tribes within the first clade were also strongly supported with Thamnodynastes (Tachymenini) forming a distinct lineage sister to the Philodryas group and Xenodon clustering with Erythrolamprus species, corroborating their close evolutionary affinity. Amino acid composition across mitochondrial genomes (Fig. 3B) was relatively conserved, dominated by L, S, and I. However, the Ka and Ks substitutions per site varied considerably among protein-coding genes (Fig. 3C).

Discussion

Molecular characterization

This study presents the structural characteristics of the mitochondrial genome of E. bizona and E. pseudocorallus, which is a double-stranded circular DNA molecule encompassing 37 genes and two non-coding control regions. We also obtained another 26 mitochondrial genomes from dipsadid snakes assembled de novo from transcriptome data available at GenBank, with the same genetic order of both sequenced snakes. All mitochondrial genomes had 13 PCG, two rRNA genes (12 S rRNA and 16 S rRNA), and 22 tRNA genes. Such a gene order has been commonly documented in the mitochondrial genomes of members of the Colubridae family45. The two relatively long intergenic spaces were assumed to represent the control regions (CR1 and CR2) with an average length of 787.4 ± 68.1 bp and 573.8 ± 138.1 bp, respectively. CR1 is located between the trnP and trnF genes, whereas CR2 occurs between the trnI and trnL1 genes (see Table 2). The length of these regions is shorter than the 1,000 bp previously reported in other species of the related family Colubridae46–48.

In total, 40 complete mitochondrial genomes representing the subfamily Dipsadinae were analyzed, of which 28 (70%) were newly sequenced in this study. This markedly increases the mitogenomic representation of Neotropical dipsadids, particularly within the tribe Xenodontini, for which all genomes included here are reported for the first time. Notably, these data include the first complete mitochondrial genomes ever described for the genus Erythrolamprus, significantly expanding the available molecular resources for this speciose and ecologically diverse lineage. The inclusion of these new mitochondrial genomes provides a robust framework for assessing phylogenetic relationships, genome organization, and nucleotide composition patterns across Dipsadinae.

The base composition of dipsadids mitochondrial genomes shows a preference for A > C > T > G, with a higher total content of A and T compared to G and C, indicating a high AT content in these genomes. The comparative analysis of the 40 Dipsadinae mitochondrial genomes revealed substantial variation in nucleotide composition and strand asymmetry, consistent with previously observed patterns across colubrid snakes. All mitochondrial genomes exhibited a pronounced AT bias and typical strand asymmetry, characterized by positive AT skew and negative GC skew values, a compositional hallmark of vertebrate mitochondrial genomes49,50; and similar to that found in reptiles51. The range of A + T content observed here (55.5–64.5%) is comparable to that reported for other dipsadid species, such as Oxyrhopus petolarius and Hydrodynastes gigas, which show A + T proportions of 62%52. Among tribes, Xenodontini exhibited the highest A + T content and the strongest asymmetry, suggesting lineage-specific variation in replication or transcriptional mechanisms influencing nucleotide bias53. Conversely, Hydropsini and Tachymenini displayed lower AT skew values, indicating a relatively balanced base composition. The strong negative correlation between AT and GC skews observed in both whole mitochondrial genomes and coding sequences supports the hypothesis that mutational and selective pressures act in concert to maintain strand asymmetry32.

The pronounced heterogeneity in nucleotide composition among Dipsadinae tribes—particularly within the Control Region (CR1 + CR2) and ribosomal RNA genes—reveals lineage-specific mutational and selective pressures shaping mitochondrial genome evolution. Xenodontini consistently exhibited the most divergent AT/GC skew patterns across regions, suggesting shifts in replication dynamics, strand-specific mutation rates, or metabolic rate–associated oxidative biases that differ from other dipsadid lineages. Similar patterns of clade-specific compositional bias have been documented in other squamates, where asymmetrical replication exposes the heavy and light strands to different mutational environments, producing lineage-dependent skews3,53.

The weak or absent correlations between AT and GC skew in the control region contrast with the stronger negative relationships observed in rRNAs, indicating that selective constraints on structural RNA genes impose more consistent compositional pressures13,54. The distinct positioning of Xenodontini in both skew scatterplots and ternary AT/GC analyses mirrors mitochondrial idiosyncrasies previously noted for rapidly diversifying snake groups, where ecological specialization and metabolic shifts may drive accelerated or biased mitochondrial substitution patterns3,53. Moreover, the balanced composition of Thermophis suggests a different evolutionary regime, consistent with its unique phylogenetic placement and previously reported stability in mitochondrial features among high-altitude taxa55. The significant region-dependent differences detected here—particularly the extreme skews in PCG compared with the more constrained rRNAs—support the notion that mitochondrial functional partitions evolve under distinct mutational pressures, reinforcing the value of nucleotide composition as an indicator of lineage-specific evolutionary trajectories in Dipsadinae.

Despite the structural conservation of mitochondrial gene order across Dipsadinae, the pronounced compositional divergence observed in Xenodontini indicates that important evolutionary changes can occur at the level of nucleotide composition rather than genome architecture. Increased AT bias and stronger strand asymmetry may arise from lineage-specific differences in mitochondrial replication dynamics, which expose the heavy and light strands to unequal mutational environments during replication. Such processes can generate persistent compositional skews and contribute to lineage-specific mutational biases that shape mitochondrial sequence evolution without requiring structural rearrangements of the genome. In this context, the strong nucleotide asymmetry observed in Xenodontini suggests that replication-associated mutational processes have played a significant role in shaping mitochondrial genome evolution within this lineage.

Codon usage patterns in Dipsadinae mitochondrial genomes reveal clear lineage-specific signatures that reflect the combined effects of mutational pressures and selective constraints on translational efficiency. The predominance of AT-rich codons (particularly CTA, ATA, ACA, and ATT) across most species aligns with the strong AT bias characteristic of snake mitochondrial genomes3,53. These codon frequency patterns as expected are in accordance with the results presented for other reptiles and birds39,50,56,57. The recurrent underrepresentation of GC-rich codons such as CGG, GCG, TCG, and CCG further supports the influence of asymmetric mutational pressure acting on the mitochondrial heavy strand, a trend broadly observed in squamate mitochondrial evolution58. However, the significant departure from Wright’s theoretical ENC curve indicates that codon bias cannot be explained solely by mutation; selective forces also shape codon preferences in Dipsadinae, particularly within protein-coding genes subject to high energetic demand.

Our effective number of codons analysis (ENC) shows high values ranging from 39 to around 43 in Dipsadinae mitochondrial PCG. Values under 35 indicate a high codon preference, (i.e.,a high bias), while values above 50 suggest a random codon usage59. However, none of the Dipsadinae species show values above or below these limits, suggesting no significant codon usage bias in the analyzed mitochondrial genomes. The ranges of values obtained are completely in accordance with studies of individual mitochondrial genes from other vertebrates60–62 and, as expected, are the same ranges of values as those reported in reptiles51. Relative expression can be estimated from the ENC analysis, a lower ENC value suggests a higher bias and consequently a higher expression levels63. Our comparative analyses show that, the pronounced clustering of Xenodontini in the ENC–GC3s space, characterized by lower ENC values and wider GC3s variation, suggests stronger codon usage bias compared with other tribes. This pattern is consistent with reports in other mitochondrial lineages where accelerated evolution or specialized metabolic strategies intensify selective constraints on synonymous sites51,64. In contrast, tribes such as Hydropsini and Dipsadini displayed higher CAI values across most mitochondrial genes—especially in complex I (nad1–nad5)—indicating enhanced codon adaptation and potentially higher mitochondrial transcriptional or translational efficiency. Similar elevation of CAI in oxidative phosphorylation genes has been documented in mammals, birds, and several reptile clades, reflecting strong purifying selection driven by metabolic optimization51,65.

The steep decline in CAI observed in nad6 across all tribes aligns with previous findings showing that nad6, encoded on the light strand, often experiences different mutational regimes and weaker selective constraints than other NADH dehydrogenase subunits32,66. Meanwhile, the highly variable CAI profiles of Xenodontini and Tachymenini—featuring localized peaks in nad2 (and in nad4/nad5 for Tachymenini)—suggest nonuniform evolutionary pressures across mitochondrial loci, potentially reflecting ecological diversification, differences in life-history strategies, or lineage-specific physiological demands. The codon usage patterns observed here further suggest that mitochondrial compositional bias interacts with selective constraints acting on protein-coding genes. Although mutational pressure clearly contributes to the predominance of AT-rich codons, the deviation from Wright’s expected ENC curve indicates that synonymous sites are also influenced by selective forces associated with translational efficiency and mitochondrial function. The consistently lower CAI values detected for nad6 may be interpreted in this context, as this gene is encoded on the mitochondrial light strand and, therefore, experiences a distinct mutational regime compared with most other mitochondrial genes located on the heavy strand. Differences in strand exposure during replication and transcription may consequently generate gene-specific evolutionary dynamics within the oxidative phosphorylation pathway.

Collectively, these results illustrate that Dipsadinae tribes exhibit both shared and divergent patterns of codon usage and codon adaptation, shaped by the interplay of mutational bias, selection, and strand-specific replication dynamics. The strong codon adaptation observed in certain lineages and mitochondrial complexes underscores the evolutionary importance of optimizing mitochondrial function, while the distinct patterns in Xenodontini highlight its unique trajectory within the family.

Phylogenetic relationships

The phylogenetic relationships inferred from the concatenated mitochondrial protein-coding genes provides strong resolution of relationships within Dipsadinae and reinforces previously recognized tribal boundaries. The recovery of two major well-supported clades—one comprising Xenodontini, Alsophiini, Philodryadini, Tachymenini and Hydropsini,, and another consisting of Dipsadini—mirrors earlier multi-locus and mitogenomic studies that have consistently resolved these lineages as distinct but closely allied components of the Dipsadinae radiation5,8. The placement of Thermophis as the sister lineage to both main clades is also congruent with genomic studies indicating its deep divergence and long independent evolutionary history within Xenodermatinae and Dipsadinae relatives67,68. Within the primary clade, the sister relationship between Thamnodynastes (Tachymenini) and Philodryadini, as well as the clustering of Xenodon with Erythrolamprus, further corroborates patterns recovered in recent phylogenomic assessments using larger nuclear datasets17,69. Nevertheless, the affinity between Thamnodynastes hypoconia and Philodryadini is somewhat unexpected given its current tribal assignment. This pattern could reflect limitations associated with mitochondrial datasets, potential issues of species identification in public databases, or the possibility that Tachymenini may not be strictly monophyletic. Alternatively, it may highlight remaining uncertainties regarding the taxonomic boundaries of Philodryadini, which have been subject to revision in recent systematic studies.

The relative conservation of amino acid composition across tribes—dominated by L, S, and I—is characteristic of snake mitochondrial genomes and reflects strong functional constraints associated with oxidative phosphorylation3. However, the pronounced heterogeneity in substitution rates highlights important gene- and lineage-specific signatures of selective pressure. The markedly low Ka and Ka/Ks ratios in cox1 and cox2 are consistent with their essential catalytic roles and the strong purifying selection observed across vertebrate mitochondrial genomes70,71. Conversely, elevated Ka/Ks ratios in atp8 and nad6—genes typically exhibiting higher evolutionary lability due to weaker structural constraints—suggest pockets of relaxed selection or lineage-specific adaptive pressures, particularly within Xenodontini and Tachymenini. Such patterns have been similarly reported in colubrid snakes, where ecological diversification and variation in metabolic demands can increase selective heterogeneity among mitochondrial genes3,58.

Differences in selective pressures among mitochondrial genes have also been documented in reptiles, including snakes. Comparative mitogenomic studies have shown that snake mitochondrial genomes exhibit heterogeneous evolutionary rates among protein-coding genes, reflecting differences in functional constraints within the oxidative phosphorylation pathway. For example, genes such as Cox1 and Cob often show relatively conserved evolutionary patterns, whereas several Nad genes (e.g., Nad1, Nad4, and Nad6) may exhibit lineage-specific rate accelerations in different snake clades. In addition, structural features such as gene rearrangements and duplication of the control region—frequently observed in snake mitogenomes—may further influence patterns of molecular evolution across mitochondrial genes. These findings suggest that mitochondrial genome evolution in snakes is shaped by a combination of functional constraints, lineage-specific evolutionary dynamics, and structural variation within the genome45,53,72.

Conclusion

Together, these findings highlight that while mitochondrial genome evolution across Dipsadinae is dominated by strong purifying selection, lineage-specific shifts in selective pressure and substitution dynamics contribute to the phylogenetic structure observed. The congruence between tribal relationships and mitogenomic patterns underscores the utility of mitochondrial datasets for resolving mid-level snake phylogeny, while the observed variation in Ka/Ks among genes and tribes point to differential functional and ecological constraints shaping the evolutionary trajectories of dipsadid lineages.
