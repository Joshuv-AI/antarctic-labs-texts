Patient engagement, shared decision-making and equity, diversity and inclusion considerations in research ethics board application forms and templates in Canada: a mixed methods study
Authors: Tamara L Morgan, Kelly Carroll, Meena Mosa, Natasha Hudek, Dawn P Richards, Susan Marlin, Kim Meeking, Cole Etherington, Justin Presseau, Jamie C Brehaut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEquity, diversity and inclusion (EDI), patient engagement and shared decision-making are important considerations throughout clinical trials, including the research ethics review stage. Meaningfully integrating these considerations can enhance the relevance and generalisability of trial results and reduce participation barriers among equity-deserving populations. Presently, it is unclear to what extent such guidance is provided at the ethics application stage for clinical trials. This study aimed to report the degree of guidance on EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making in clinical trial research ethics documents.DesignThis was an embedded mixed methods study conducted in collaboration with Clinical Trials Ontario.SettingThis study analysed research ethics board (REB) forms and templates from 17 institutions across seven provinces in Canada.Participants and methods15 REB application forms, 9 protocol templates and 17 informed consent document (ICD) templates were assessed for guidance related to EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making. The Place of residence, Race, ethnicity, culture and language, Occupation, Gender and sex, Religion, Education, Socio-economic status, Social capital (PROGRESS)-Plus framework, International Association for Public Participation Spectrum of Public Participation, Patient-Oriented Research Level of Engagement Tool, Indigenous Research Level of Engagement Tool and shared decision-making standards guided our coding. We engaged with patients and persons with lived experience to inform interpretation, reporting and dissemination.ResultsEDI guidance from 15 ethics application forms and 9 protocol templates predominantly covered the ‘Race, ethnicity, culture, language’ (n=14; 93.3%), ‘Age’ (n=13; 86.7%) and ‘Gender and sex’ (n=12; 80%) categories of PROGRESS-Plus but lacked nuance on diverse gender identities (n=1; 6.7%). Patient engagement guidance mostly covered the ‘inform’ level (n=7; 46.7%) and applying ‘knowledge in practice’ with non-Indigenous (n=7; 46.7%) or Indigenous communities (n=13; 86.7%). All 17 (100%) ICD templates included guidance on information about options, disclosures, key elements, ethical issues and study design. No guidance was available on time-dependent relationships, empowering patients and communities in co-leading trials or providing structured guidance in making trial participation decisions (all n=0; 0%).ConclusionsWe provided a comprehensive view of EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making guidance in trial ethics applications in Canada. REB guidance may be strengthened in several areas to support the inclusion of equity-deserving populations in trials, meaningful engagement with patients and Indigenous communities and evidence-informed, values-aligned decisions about trial participation.

Introduction

Participation in clinical trials, encompassing participant identification, recruitment and retention, is vital to inform scientific advancements in care.1 However, trial participation is a constant challenge, particularly among equity-deserving populations, such as sexual and gender diverse persons, persons with disabilities and racialised groups, who face barriers to participating in trials.24 Barriers may apply to multiple groups, such as time or financial constraints (eg, requirements to commute or participate during daytime work hours)5 or specific groups (eg, mistrust of clinical research, exclusion of non-English-speaking persons).6 7 Resultantly, equity-deserving populations often remain under-represented, limiting the generalisability of trial findings, reducing access to effective medical interventions and exacerbating health inequities.4 8 9 Optimising trial equity can advance participant diversity and health equity, while accelerating overall recruitment, trial efficiency and improvements to clinical care.10 11

Equity, diversity and inclusion (EDI) and patient engagement—also sometimes referred to as patient and public involvement—are two approaches increasingly recognised by trialists, trial sponsors, health charities and patient organisations as critical for enhancing the accessibility, relevance and utility of clinical trials for patients.12 In clinical trials, EDI can be defined as reducing unjust barriers to participation that relate to a person’s membership in one or more populations (ie, equity), achieving representation across a range of populations and perspectives (ie, diversity), and valuing the lived experiences of these members of diverse populations (ie, inclusivity).13 14 Interventions to improve trial EDI have encompassed cultural competency training for staff, collecting a broader range of sociodemographic characteristics among participants and participant-centred approaches.14

Complementing EDI, patient engagement in clinical trials involves meaningful collaboration with patients and persons with lived experience, including their caregivers, family and community partners, as research partners rather than as participants to inform aspects of trial design and conduct.12 Examples of patient engagement interventions have included patients and the public reviewing informed consent documents (ICDs) to ensure they are relevant and understandable, co-selecting data collection measures and procedures to ensure they are feasible and reflect patient and public experiences, and guiding plans to disseminate trial findings to communities.15

A third, related area that focuses on improving patient experiences is shared decision-making, which was initially developed as a person-centred, theory-informed approach for helping people make deliberative, evidence-informed judgements about healthcare decisions.1618 Using a shared decision-making approach can inform collaborative patient-provider discussions about the evidence supporting different treatment options (eg, imaging, surgeries, medications), the likelihood of experiencing associated risks and benefits of each option, and patients’ preferences and values to arrive at a shared decision.19 Shared decision-making can guide trial participation decisions in a similar way by ensuring potential participants fully understand all relevant information about participation and make a decision that matches their values and preferences, which aligns with the goals of informed consent.18 Nevertheless, in a trial participation context, shared decision-making differs as the decision on whether or not to participate in research is solely the individual’s choice and should not be influenced by the clinician or trial recruiter.20 Among equity-deserving populations, shared decision-making may be even more important to identify participation barriers and clarify which values matter to them when deciding whether or not to participate in a trial, given these populations may face greater barriers to participation.21

It is increasingly argued that investigators should consider how EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making can be integrated across all trial stages; the research ethics application stage is one that remains underexplored. Research ethics boards (REBs) are mandated to ensure that research involving human participants adheres to the highest ethical standards, including non-discrimination, the protection of humans and social responsibility and justice.22 However, there is currently no clear standard for EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making considerations in clinical trial research ethics applications, such as reporting on a broader range of participant characteristics, designing inclusive trials or engaging in patient engagement processes.

REB documents, such as ethics application forms, protocol templates and ICD templates can provide guidance to trialists on how to consider EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making in trial design and conduct. One study in South Africa found that while REB guidance documents included prompts on the rationale and processes for patient engagement, many failed to provide recommendations beyond seeking gatekeeper permission or guidance on sustained engagement.23 In Canada, an analysis of ethics application forms from 12 REBs found variation in formatting but relative consistency in overall content (eg, project registration and description); however, no evaluation of EDI, patient engagement or shared decision-making guidance was done.24 The extent of guidance on EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making considerations in trial ethics forms and templates in Canada is presently unknown. Examining the quality and amount of guidance in research ethics documents from different REB contexts could offer a comprehensive view of the variation in guidance related to EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making available to investigators submitting trials ethics applications in Canada. Further, research supports that providing recommendations on EDI considerations in trial protocol guidelines has led to greater EDI outcomes, for instance, by expanding eligibility criteria to increase participant diversity.25 Thus, optimising REB-driven guidance could advance the standard of ethical and equitable conduct, efficiency and efficacy in clinical trials.21 24 26 27

Study objectives

The purpose of this study was to describe the quality and quantity of guidance in Canada on EDI and patient engagement considerations in trial ethics application forms and protocol templates, and on shared decision-making in trial ICD templates. We also highlight areas where REBs could potentially strengthen the guidance they provide to trialists on EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making considerations.

Methods

Study design and context

Reporting is in line with the Mixed Methods Article Reporting Standards checklist (online supplemental file 1).28 We led an embedded mixed methods study by first extracting qualitative data from REB forms and templates and then quantitatively coding it across several frameworks.29 We used a quantitative descriptive approach within a qualitative phenomenological approach and interpretivist paradigm, which allowed us to identify nuances in EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making concepts while quantifying their frequency and depth of use in REB documents.30

Our study was conducted in collaboration with Clinical Trials Ontario (CTO), which is an independent, not-for-profit organisation based in Ontario, Canada that aims to increase the efficiency and cost-effectiveness of clinical trials and engage patients and the public to improve the trials landscape. Considering our positionality, we can critically reflect on the relevance of our knowledge and experiences to this work and how these informed our data extraction and analyses. The first author is female, cis-gendered, lives with an invisible disability, has an educational background in kinesiology and health promotion, and was postdoctoral fellow at the time of this study. The remaining authors include researchers and executives with graduate-level education, diverse gender identities, lived experiences of chronic disease and backgrounds in psychology, sociology, epidemiology, patient and public engagement, health equity and clinical trials. With the authors’ varied experiences, we were able to reflect on multiple perspectives in extracting, coding and interpreting the data.

Sampling and procedure

19 REBs and institutions that support research ethics review in Canada were identified through discussions with the CEO and the Director of the Research Ethics Review Programme at CTO and networks of the study team. We purposively selected REBs that facilitate ethics review for multisite and single-site trials, with the largest proportion residing in Ontario as more than half of trial activity occurs within this province. We were as inclusive as possible when selecting REBs and omitted only those whose system was currently in a state of change, that largely delegated to another REB or that declined to provide forms and/or templates. The first author manually searched the websites of the 19 REBs and downloaded all available forms and templates beginning in January 2024. When forms and templates were unavailable, the first author emailed the manager or director of the REB with a description of the study purpose and asked if their forms and/or templates could be shared. Follow-up emails were sent approximately two later if no response was received. All included forms/templates were received by May 2024.

Research ethics review was not required for this study as it involves a review of documents in the public domain. Nonetheless, we followed procedures to ensure confidentiality, privacy and autonomy by anonymising study data, reporting findings in aggregate, storing study data in a secure web-based application housed on a secure server provided by the first author’s institution and clarifying to REBs that providing copies of their forms and/or templates was entirely voluntary.

Data extraction

The data extraction form was built in Excel and independently piloted by three reviewers (TLM, KC, MM). Reviewers individually searched ethics application forms and protocol templates for guidance related to EDI and patient engagement and ICD templates for guidance related to shared decision-making. Reviewers independently extracted guidance verbatim from each form/template as open-ended text into cells categorised per the frameworks described below. Data not captured by these frameworks was inductively coded into an ‘other’ category. We iteratively revised the form during our initial extractions to improve clarity and usability. Extraction occurred from June to August 2024.

Equity, diversity and inclusion considerations

We extracted text related to EDI guidance and categorised it into one or more PROGRESS-Plus framework category. PROGRESS-Plus describes a range of equity-relevant characteristics and stands for Place of residence, Race, ethnicity, culture and language, Occupation, Gender and sex, Religion, Education, Socio-economic status, Social capital, ‘plus’ contextual factors including (1) personal characteristics linked to discrimination, like age or disability, (2) relationship features, like presence of a caregiver and (3) time-dependent considerations, like length of travel to a study site.31 Text could relate to trial participant characteristics (eg, checkboxes asking which sociodemographic characteristics of potential participants are being collected) or considerations for trial design or conduct (eg, questions about translating materials to a participant’s primary language).

Patient engagement considerations

We extracted text related to patient engagement using three frameworks (eg, questions about plans to collaborate with patients or communities on study design). First, we used the International Association for Public Participation Spectrum of Public Participation (IAP2 Spectrum) to categorise guidance across five levels: inform, consult, involve, collaborate and empower.32 The IAP2 Spectrum was initially designed for engaging communities in public decision-making33 but is also used in patient engagement research.34 35

Second, we used the Patient-Oriented Research Level of Engagement Tool (PORLET 2.0)32 to categorise guidance on engagement with non-Indigenous persons and communities (eg, questions asking whether patients or members of the public were involved on the research team). The PORLET 2.0 was developed out of the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research (SPOR) and outlines five domains: engaging with patients as partners, patient-identified priorities, outcomes important to patients, translating knowledge into practice and forming a multidisciplinary team.32 Finally, we used the Indigenous Research Level of Engagement Tool (IRLET)32 to categorise guidance on engagement with Indigenous communities (eg, questions about plans to develop a formal research agreement with the relevant Indigenous community). The IRLET encompasses four domains that are significant to Indigenous peoples: partnerships with Indigenous communities, translating knowledge into practice, applying a strengths-based approach and Indigenous ways of knowing.32

Shared decision-making considerations

To extract shared decision-making guidance, we applied 32 Decision Quality standards and 27 Informed Consent standards from previous research36 37 to categorise which key shared decision-making elements ICD templates included. The Decision Quality standards were operationalised from the International Patient Decision Aids Standards instrument to apply to ICDs and the Informed Consent standards were based on various regulatory guidelines and ethics literature and have been deemed to support good-quality participation decisions.36 37 The Decision Quality standards encompass six domains: information about options, probabilities, clarifying values, structured guidance, using evidence and disclosure and transparency. The Informed Consent standards encompass five domains: key elements, study design, ethical issues, formatting and style.36 37 Items in each domain were operationalised to apply to ICD templates rather than ICDs by modifying the wording. For instance, the item ‘The informed consent document describes the health condition or problem’ was changed to ‘The informed consent document template asks investigators to describe the health condition or problem’.

Coding and analysis

For each framework category/domain, two researchers independently coded the level of guidance provided on an ordinal scale from 0 to 3, where:

0 (no guidance) = concept is not mentioned.

0 (no guidance) = concept is not mentioned.

1 (limited guidance) = concept is only mentioned, with no specificity (eg, a single question or statement with checkbox, radio button or open-text response options and no further detail).

1 (limited guidance) = concept is only mentioned, with no specificity (eg, a single question or statement with checkbox, radio button or open-text response options and no further detail).

2 (some guidance) = concept is mentioned with some specificity (ie, a question/statement with one of: EITHER a clear definition or rationale OR follow-up questions/statements OR resources to support its implementation in a trial (eg, a specific Tri-Council Policy Statement (TCPS2) chapter, CIHR guidelines)).

2 (some guidance) = concept is mentioned with some specificity (ie, a question/statement with one of: EITHER a clear definition or rationale OR follow-up questions/statements OR resources to support its implementation in a trial (eg, a specific Tri-Council Policy Statement (TCPS2) chapter, CIHR guidelines)).

3 (detailed guidance) = concept is mentioned with greater specificity (ie, a question/statement with two to three of: a clear definition or rationale AND/OR follow-up questions/statements AND/OR resources to support its implementation).

3 (detailed guidance) = concept is mentioned with greater specificity (ie, a question/statement with two to three of: a clear definition or rationale AND/OR follow-up questions/statements AND/OR resources to support its implementation).

TLM and MM independently extracted and coded EDI and patient engagement guidance. TLM and KC independently extracted and coded shared decision-making guidance. Discrepancies were discussed until a consensus was reached. A third reviewer was consulted for unresolved discrepancies through triangulation.

Data visualisation

Tables and bubble charts were used to illustrate how many forms and templates provided guidance, on which concepts and at what level of detail. As the shared decision-making domains include between one to 12 items (whereas PROGRESS-Plus, the IAP2 Spectrum, PORLET 2.0 and IRLET domains only include one item), the average level of guidance across all Decision Quality and Informed Consent standards for each domain was calculated and rounded up to the nearest whole number to create one overall score per domain.

Patient and public involvement

Engagement in data visualisation and interpretation occurred with 10 members of CTO’s College of Lived Experience, a group of patient and public members who advise CTO and external groups on various projects.38 Three virtual meetings were held (4 hours total) to discuss and learn whether or not findings resonated with lived experiences of clinical trial participation and to guide the discussion of this manuscript. Discussions highlighted the social impact of our findings on trial inclusivity and accessibility. The Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public Short Form was used to report our patient engagement activities (online supplemental file 1).39 To uphold principles of confidentiality, privacy and voluntariness among college members, we allowed members to choose whether they wished to be named in the acknowledgements section or remain anonymous and encouraged members to contribute as little or as much as they wished (ie, attendance was not mandatory).

Results

17 of the 19 REBs were included in the final sample as the forms and templates could not be extracted from one REB’s system and the forms and templates at another REB were undergoing significant revisions regarding EDI and patient engagement concepts at the time of the study (table 1). Included sites represented Western, Central, Eastern and Atlantic Canada, with approximately half coming from Ontario. From these 17 sites, 15 application forms, 9 protocol templates and 17 ICD templates were retrieved. There were fewer protocol templates as some REBs refer investigators to external resources (eg, the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines40 instead of providing their own template.

Equity, diversity and inclusion considerations

All 15 ethics application forms included guidance to support the consideration of EDI in trials. Figure 2 shows how detailed guidance was per PROGRESS-Plus category. Forms described the ‘Race, ethnicity, culture, language’ (n=14; 93.3%) and ‘Age’ (n=13; 86.7%) categories in the most detail. Exemplary guidance on ‘Race, ethnicity, culture, language’ covered: (1) considerations for including Indigenous peoples and diverse ethnic, racial or cultural backgrounds (AF06), (2) plans to translate study materials (AF07), (3) ensuring the research team has the appropriate training to engage with the abovementioned groups and (4) reference to the TCPS2 Chapter 9 and training module 9 on Research Involving the First Nations, Inuit and Métis Peoples of Canada (AF14). Exemplary guidance on ‘Age’ (n=13; 86.7%) included: (1) asking investigators to state whether children, neonates and elderly persons will be recruited and whether there are age-based inclusion or exclusion criteria, (2) guidance on obtaining consent from children and (3) reference to the TCPS2 Article 3.10 on voluntary consent. No forms (0%) discussed factors that may be impacted by ‘Time-dependent relationships’.

Gender and/or sex considerations were prevalent (n=12; 80%), but only one form provided ‘detailed guidance’. This one form (1) discussed at length that people should not be excluded based on gender or sexual orientation and that valid reasoning should be provided for any such exclusion, (2) asked whether investigators planned to recruit and/or engage with 2SLGBTQIA+ persons and (3) gave example descriptions of the reproductive risks of participating using inclusive language (ie, stating ‘being or becoming pregnant’ rather than ‘pregnant woman’ and ‘partner’ rather than ‘father’) (AF11). This was the only form that used inclusive language when describing reproductive risks; the remainder used heteronormative language that could be exclusionary to members of the 2SLGBTQIA+ community, and few forms enforced that exclusion based on gender or sexual orientation must be justifiable. Sex and gender were explicitly conflated in only one form, which listed ‘sex/gender’ in a question about collecting personal health information. In other forms, it was less clear whether sex and gender were being conflated as some listed ‘sex’ but not ‘gender’ or vice versa.

Additional guidance was captured under ‘other’ EDI considerations (n=14; 93.3%). Most forms included guidance on participants who may lack the capacity to consent, while almost half of the forms mentioned participant reimbursement and compensation and equity-deserving populations who did not clearly align with one of the existing categories (eg, we were unable to determine whether ‘people who are unable to read or write’ or ‘communication difficulties’ were distinctly related to disability, language or education level). Variation in EDI guidance

Guidance varied, from one form providing ‘limited guidance’ on ‘Relationship features’ (AF16) to three forms providing guidance on nearly all PROGRESS-Plus categories (AF07, AF11, AF12). Some categories were covered by nearly all forms, ranging from 11 to 14 (73.3–93.3%), while others received guidance from between four and eight forms (26.7–53.3%). For instance, four forms (26.7%) provided ‘limited guidance’ on ‘Education’ considerations, all involving a question asking investigators whether the research aims to recruit ‘educationally disadvantaged people’, and if so, to justify why. Seven forms (46.7%) mentioned ‘Socioeconomic status’ considerations, commonly as a question asking whether the research aims to recruit ‘people in poverty’ or ‘economically disadvantaged people’.

Patient engagement considerations

Figure 3 displays the level of detail of guidance per IAP2 Spectrum level and PORLET 2.0 or IRLET domain. Nine application forms (60%) provided any patient engagement guidance at all, covering every IAP2 Spectrum level except ‘empower’. The ‘inform’ level was discussed among seven (46.7%) application forms, mainly involving one question asking if and how the study results will be shared with the community. While mentioned in fewer forms, the levels ‘involve’ (n=4; 26.7%) and ‘collaborate’ (n=3; 20%) were discussed in the most detail. The strongest guidance on ‘involving’ patients was a question asking investigators which research stage patients will be engaged in, with response options including to ‘advise on recruitment methods’ and ‘give input’ on study materials, and a reference to the SPOR guidance (AF07, AF11). Exemplary guidance on ‘collaborating’ with patients included the same question as above, but with response options including to ‘help form the research question’ and ‘provide ongoing feedback’ and a reference to the TCPS 2 Article 9.10 on the Requirement of Community Engagement in Indigenous Research and the SPOR guidance (AF06, AF07, AF11, AF12).

Nine application forms (60%) gave guidance in line with the PORLET 2.0. The ‘patients are partners’ (n=3; 20%) and ‘knowledge into practice’ (n=7; 46.7%) domains were discussed most comprehensively. Two forms (AF07, AF11) offered guidance on these two domains by asking if investigators have involved or plan to involve patients as research partners or engage with the relevant community(ies), and if so, whether there will be engagement in implementing study results. This question was accompanied by help text with detailed definitions of who is considered a patient partner and what patient-oriented research entails, and a reference to the SPOR guidance for further support. Only two forms (13.3%) provided guidance related to patient-identified priorities (AF07, AF11) and forming a multidisciplinary team (AF14, AF15), and only one (6.7%) provided guidance related to patient-identified outcomes (AF07).

13 (86.7%) application forms gave guidance in line with the IRLET. The ‘knowledge into practice’ domain was discussed most comprehensively (n=13; 86.7%). In 10 forms, guidance related to developing and attaching a formal research agreement with the relevant Indigenous community, including requirements for valid justification where no formal research agreement is being sought and reference to the TCPS2 Article 9.2 on the Nature and Extent of Community Engagement and Article 9.10 on the Requirement of Community Engagement in Indigenous Research. Other guidance within the ‘knowledge into practice’ domain involved questions asking if Indigenous communities will be involved in disseminating or implementing the research findings or if a data governance plan will be in place. Substantially less guidance was given related to Indigenous ways of knowing (n=4; 26.7%), strengths-based approaches (n=2; 13.3%) and partnerships with Indigenous communities (n=2; 13.3%).

Four application forms (26.7%) offered guidance on ‘other patient engagement’ considerations. In three forms, guidance pertained to generic considerations for engaging under-represented groups or communities. In the fourth form, guidance spoke to providing honoraria for ‘other parties’ involved in the study. Variation in patient engagement guidance

The degree of patient engagement considerations varied, from no guidance at all to guidance on nearly all engagement levels and domains. Guidance related to the ‘multi-disciplinary team’ domain of the PORLET 2.0 was particularly lacking, with only two forms (13.3%) including a question that asked if the research team includes members from other disciplines (eg, different divisions, services or patients). Similarly, only two forms (13.3%) provided guidance on fostering partnerships with and offering lasting benefits to Indigenous communities, which asked how the research plan considers mutual benefit to the community and whether there are plans to continue the research relationship after study completion (AF04, AF08).

Decision quality standards

The most detailed guidance pertained to ‘Disclosure and transparency’ as all ICD templates included some guidance related to three of the four standards in this domain. For example, all 17 (100%) ICD templates asked investigators to state the funding sources for the trial and include a section with the name and contact information of the primary investigator and a person to whom potential participants can ask questions about the trial. All ICD templates (100%) also provided guidance for most standards in the domain ‘Providing information about options’. However, three of these 12 standards did not appear in any ICD template: (1) describing the disadvantages of not participating in a consistent order (eg, from least to most severe), (2) structuring the ICD so that the advantages and disadvantages of participation can be easily compared head-to-head, and (3) describing the likely time of onset of possible side effects of participation (eg, immediate, delayed).

Conversely, 13 (76.5%) ICD templates included guidance on ‘Presenting probabilities’, but only four of the eight standards in this domain were met and guidance varied in detail. Two of the standards that were met received guidance from 14 (82.4%) ICD templates: (1) providing information on the probabilities of experiencing all advantages and disadvantages of participating (limited guidance n=1, some guidance n=12, detailed guidance n=1) and (2) providing multiple ways of viewing probabilities (eg, by using words, numbers and diagrams; some guidance n=10, detailed guidance n=4). The other two standards that were met each received some guidance from one ICD template and detailed guidance from one other ICD template: (1) specifying probabilities in natural frequencies and (2) comparing probabilities using the same denominator.

No ICD template (0%) included any guidance related to ‘Structured guidance in deliberation’, which would involve providing potential participants a step-by-step way to decide whether they want to participate and including a worksheet to help potential participants discuss the decision with others (eg, study nurse, family). Guidance related to ‘Clarifying and expressing values’ was relatively low (n=9; 52.9%), including limited guidance on one standard in this domain, which asks investigators to describe participation advantages and disadvantages at a level of detail that helps potential participants imagine the impact on their life. Within ‘Using evidence’ (n=16; 94.1%), only one standard received any guidance, which involved asking investigators to insert a section with the production date of the ICD.

Informed consent standards

All 17 (100%) ICD templates included some guidance on Informed Consent standards. The most comprehensive guidance pertained to ‘Key elements’ (n=17; 100%), encompassing at least moderate guidance on all standards in this domain. The best guidance was given for four standards: (1) stating that the study involves research, (2) including a section outlining all procedures that will be carried out, (3) stating that participation is voluntary and (4) specifying that the potential participant can withdraw from the study at any time.

Guidance was relatively consistent on the ‘Ethical issues’ (n=17; 100%) and ‘Study design’ (n=17; 100%). ‘Ethical issues’ guidance asked investigators to (1) list the potential risks to an embryo, fetus or nursing infant, (2) describe how participating in the trial may benefit society, (3) include a section to indicate how personal health information will be protected and (4) indicate which organisations and individuals will have access to personal health information. Most of the ‘Study design’ standards were captured in questions asking investigators to (1) state the expected duration of participation, (2) state the expected number of participants in the trial and (3) explain the term ‘randomization’. Nine templates (52.9%) provided guidance for most standards related to ‘Style’, and all (100%) templates included guidance on ‘Formatting’, mainly by reminding investigators to ensure their ICD is free of technical language and jargon.

Guidance was relatively equivalent across all ICD templates. Informed Consent standards were discussed more frequently than Decision Quality standards (70–96% of standards discussed compared with 28–56%, respectively). Discussion

Principal findings

In this study, we examined guidance on EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making in trial ethics application forms, protocol templates and ICD templates. Guidance existed for nearly all PROGRESS-Plus categories but mostly lacked detail. When guidance was detailed, it covered commonly reported characteristics like race, ethnicity, age and sex. Most forms provided guidance on patient engagement and Indigenous community engagement; however, it covered more known topics such as informing participants of trial results and developing formal research agreements with Indigenous communities involved in the research. We identified more prevalent and detailed guidance on Informed Consent standards than Decision Quality standards, with most guidance pertaining to describing the rationale, procedures and rights of potential participants in the trial and being transparent when explaining the trial, respectively.

Gaps in guidance

Guidance generally lacked a broader discussion of EDI considerations beyond avoiding unnecessary exclusion criteria or translating trial materials, such as the impact of time-dependent factors, education or disability on trial participation. Guidance on educational considerations was rather narrow as there were no definitions on who qualifies as being ‘educationally disadvantaged’ or what accommodations may be needed. Educational disadvantage could indicate limited knowledge about clinical trials and require additional discussions during informed consent. It could also derive from lower educational attainment, which is associated with a decreased willingness and chance of being asked to participate and may require educating trial recruiters to better provide trial opportunities to eligible individuals in these groups.41 An audit of REB documents from 30 REB-approved cancer clinical trials in the UK showed that while data on age, ethnicity, gender and sex, and health status were collected in most trials, sex and gender data collection was binary, socio-economic factors like employment status and income level were not routinely measured, and patient information sheets had poor readability.42 Taken together, these data indicate a need for greater accommodation of diverse equity-deserving populations in trial design and conduct.

Just over half of forms included questions about adaptations to consent procedures, accommodations (eg, assistive devices) and justification for the inclusion and exclusion of persons with disabilities (predominantly cognitive impairment or communication difficulties). Canadian law mandates accessibility standards for organisations43 44; however, we did not observe these standards in the ethics guidance we reviewed. Comparatively, the TCPS2 principles were heavily reflected across forms and templates.20 Given over one-quarter of people in Canada identify as having one or more disabilities, and almost half report communication-related and information-related barriers, increasing trial accessibility may help more people in Canada participate in trials.45 Trial accessibility could also be improved to better support non-English speaking persons. In an analysis of 698 REB-approved prospective study submissions in Canada, 32.7% outright excluded persons with limited English proficiency, while only 32.4% provided interpretation services and 3.3% provided translated study materials to participants.46 Further, justifications for exclusion were rarely given.46 This evidence suggests that prompting how and when to tailor study materials and procedures, and adding requirements for justifying exclusion criteria for equity-deserving populations, could be useful at the ethics application stage to enhance trial EDI.

Among shared decision-making elements, ICD templates lacked guidance around ways to assist potential participants in making a decision on whether to participate, such as by using a decision aid.18 37 Similarly, little guidance was given on helping potential participants visualise how intervention benefits or side-effects could impact their day-to-day lives and supporting potential participants in understanding their options and deliberating their values when making participation decisions.37 Guidance was also inconsistent on formatting and style elements, such as reminders to define acronyms and abbreviations, use them sparingly, ensure graphical elements are clear and labelled and remove repetitive statements.

Explanations and connections to existing literature

The high number of forms that mentioned sex and/or gender is likely a product of CIHR’s efforts related to sex and gender in research.47 However, sex and gender guidance lacked clear distinction and definition, which leaves room for superficial application and conflation of terms. There was surprisingly no guidance on ‘time-dependent relationships’ despite many populations facing challenges taking time off work or commuting long hours to participate in trials.6 48 Persons living with conditions that are energy-limiting (eg, fibromyalgia, multiple sclerosis, cancer), affect sleep cycles (eg, circadian rhythm sleep disorder, narcolepsy, sleep apnoea) or depend on the time and assistance of a caregiver may also find it extremely challenging to manage their condition while devoting additional time and energy to participate in a trial.

Most patient engagement guidance pertained to the ‘inform’ level, which we would not deem as engagement due to its focus on unidirectional information sharing rather than reciprocal relationships between researchers and patients or communities. Other conceptualisations have depicted engagement levels in a circle rather than on a spectrum, including the Healthcare Excellence Canada Patient Engagement Framework, where different levels can be used concurrently depending on the intended purpose.49 While the ‘inform’ level can be useful, it should not replace more collaborative engagement to ensure patients and persons with lived experience, particularly those from equity-deserving groups, can influence trial decision-making.34 Conversely, the lack of guidance on ‘empower’ was expected as this is the most challenging, resource-intensive level of patient engagement to attain and relatively uncommon in clinical trial design. Only 2 of 48 studies in a systematic review of strategies to promote trial participation among rural communities empowered community members to colead the research.50

In the PORLET 2.0 and IRLET, the ‘knowledge into practice’ domain relates to the extent that patients, the public or Indigenous communities, are engaged as creators and implementers of the knowledge mobilisation plan.51 52 However, in the IRLET, this domain also speaks to creating a formal research agreement on the principles of ownership, control, access and possession.52 53 Indeed, most guidance coded within this IRLET domain pertained to creating a formal research agreement with the engaged community(ies), a direct reflection of the TCPS2 Chapter 9.52 53 More than half of forms discussed ‘knowledge into practice’ at the ‘inform’ level, demonstrating a stronger focus on informing communities of trial findings than on their involvement in interpreting or implementing those findings. While there is currently no requirement for ethical oversight of patient engagement,235456 it is important for trial teams to plan for meaningful and mutually beneficial engagement with patients and the public.

Finally, guidance on Informed Consent standards was likely more detailed as these are elements that every ICD must include for ethical clearance, such as stating that the research is voluntary, any reproductive risks, who will have access to personal health information, and features of all intervention arms.18 37 Conversely, Decision Quality standards are about reducing uncertainty, reflecting on values, setting realistic expectations and avoiding biases in participation decisions.18 37 Examples include providing information about all options, advantages and disadvantages for both participation and non-participation, presenting the probabilities of each advantage and disadvantage, and offering guidance on weighing options against one’s values and preferences.18 37 Perhaps, implementing certain Decision Quality standards is too burdensome for trial teams, too extensive on top of already lengthy consent forms or beyond the scope of REB guidance. Recruiters may also be hesitant to help someone deliberate their decision step-by-step as it could be perceived as undue influence and have associated liability issues if the person experiences later harm.20 Also, few ICD templates asked investigators to remove repetition despite research supporting that lengthy ICDs can hamper the informed consent process.37 Given equity-deserving groups may have communication and accessibility considerations, guidance on proper formatting and style should be a core feature of ICD templates.

Implications and recommendations

Broadening existing guidance on EDI considerations (eg, ethnicity, language) could be achieved with questions about culturally adapting trial materials or processes to better support people who are frequently excluded.5759 Furthermore, providing considerations related to sexual orientation, sexual identity or gender identity could help avoid inadvertent exclusion of 2SLGBTQIA+ and promote cultural safety. For instance, consent forms that advise ‘women of childbearing potential’ to use measures to prevent pregnancy lack language that is inclusive to trans men, cis women in same-sex relationships and non-binary people. For time-dependent relationships, guidance could include questions about accommodations for time and energy demands or recommend adaptations to address barriers among the 10 distinct types of disabilities, including vision, hearing, mobility, flexibility, dexterity, pain-related, learning, developmental, mental health-related and memory.60 Adaptations could involve simplifying text, flexible data collection, making forms accessible for use with a screen reader, providing accessible transportation and decentralised trials to reduce the number of in-person visits.61 For education considerations, clearly defining who fits this category seems a feasible addition, with further expansion on unique considerations of different educational disadvantages also being helpful. Removing participation barriers benefits everyone, supporting both moral and practical reasons to enhance disability guidance in trial ethics.43

Trial teams should aim for the highest level of engagement that is feasible within available resources for supporting meaningful engagement. Guidance should also be explicit on why engagement is sought to help challenge unequitable and colonial practices, such as the use of formal research agreements to reinforce power imbalances.34 62 Indeed, discussions around outcomes, strengths and knowledges that are important to Indigenous communities were largely missing from forms and templates, which may indicate the embeddedness of these colonial practices. Thus, REB guidance could perhaps include questions on co-identifying priorities, outcomes and methodologies important to underserved patients and communities (eg, Indigenous peoples, sexual and gender diverse persons, rural residents). Guidance on areas of engagement beyond what is stated in the TCPS2 (ie, informing participants and communities of the research findings) could also promote accountability and transparency to patients and communities. For example, help text on how patients or communities can be engaged in the protocol or funding stages, or in selection of research questions or study outcomes, could prompt investigators to consider engagement in these areas. While patient engagement has been considered outside of REB’s scope, international ethics guidance including the WHO standards and operational guidance for ethics review of health-related research with human participants63 and the Council for International Organisations of Medical Sciences international ethical guidelines for health-related research involving humans,64 states that REBs should consider plans for patient engagement but do not specify whether or in which documents guidance should be provided, allowing REBs to decide.54

In lieu of support on ‘Structured guidance in deliberation’ and ‘Clarifying and expressing values’, ICD templates could suggest using decision aids to help patients navigate all options and outcomes to make a quality, informed decision.18 65 Decision aids exist for guiding clinical trial participation decisions65 and a framework has been proposed by Gillies and Campbell for developing trial participation decision aids.66 Further, to improve understandability and usability of ICDs among individuals with varying ability levels, ICD templates could recommend the use of digital accessibility tools, such as screen readers, in the formatting section of ICD templates. Decision aids could also be better designed to have inclusive language void of cisnormative and heteronormative assumptions.

Finally, we noted a variation in the length of guidance text, with both limited and detailed guidance coming from shorter and longer passages. For example, shorter guidance could be very to the point and helpful, while longer guidance might have the recommendations buried in the text or lack directions on how to implement guidance. REBs and communities may have conflicting priorities in what information is necessary to include in an ICD. Thus, it is key to ensure patient and public input is heard and incorporated by REBs in developing their ICD templates, or that there is a discussion around why some input may not be feasible to incorporate.

Strengths and weaknesses

A primary strength of this study was our engagement with members of CTO’s College of Lived Experience to guide our interpretation and discussion. Applying the PROGRESS-Plus framework highlighted which equity-relevant characteristics may need further consideration in REB guidance. Considering all PROGRESS-Plus categories can help investigators acknowledge a larger range of participant characteristics that relate to equity and social (dis)advantage in healthcare interventions and include these in trial design. Nevertheless, PROGRESS-Plus has drawbacks, including the stratification of diverse groups into distinct categories, lack of emphasis on other equity-deserving populations within ‘PROGRESS’ categories (eg, people experiencing homelessness, sexual orientation), lower emphasis on groups within ‘Plus’ categories and inclusion of sex and gender in one category, which may imply conflation.67 68 Our examination of application forms, protocol templates and ICD templates provided a comprehensive look at REB-driven guidance. Nevertheless, this study lacked representation from Québec and Manitoba; Prince Edward Island and the Territories of Canada were also not represented, but they do not have institutional REBs that conduct clinical trial ethics review. Guidance on levels or areas of patient engagement beyond our chosen frameworks may have also been missed. However, we inductively coded guidance that did not fit within these frameworks into an ‘other’ category to mitigate this drawback. Finally, we did not include other ethics guidance documents (eg, SPIRIT guidelines), which could have provided additional details relevant to our study aims.

Future directions

Our findings may help optimise ethical guidance on EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making in clinical trials by underscoring where guidance could be improved or supplemented. The present author team is engaging with CTO’s College of Lived Experience to suggest possible revisions to guidance in CTO’s forms and templates, which may also be useful to other REBs in advancing their own guidance around EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making. Similarly, our findings may inform enhancements to REB guidance documents in other countries following similar research ethics frameworks to the TCPS2; however, transferability may be limited where differences in national regulations and local context exist. Future opportunities exist for the developers of the frameworks used in this study to strengthen considerations of underexplored concepts in their frameworks, including sexual orientation, sexual identity and gender identity. Further, given shared decision-making was developed for use in clinical care contexts, our application of shared decision-making elements to trial ethics documents may need further operationalisation to ensure nuances in shared decision-making for research participation decisions are sufficiently captured. In future qualitative or mixed methods studies, researchers could investigate the feasibility of improving REB guidance on EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making and what barriers and enablers exist to their uptake among REB members, trial investigators and trial sites. In future trials, investigators could explore the impact of different study designs or methods (eg, hybrid or decentralised trials, community-based participatory research) on equity-deserving populations’ participation to address participation barriers.

Conclusion

Guidance on EDI, patient engagement and shared decision-making in trial ethics forms and templates varied, with more widespread and detailed guidance found for EDI relative to patient engagement. Within patient engagement guidance, engaging with Indigenous communities was discussed more often and comprehensively than guidance on engaging with patients and the public. Many shared decision-making elements were reflected in ICD templates; however, guidance more commonly related to Informed Consent standards than Decision Quality standards. We identified opportunities to bolster existing guidance in several areas, including more nuanced consideration of sex and gender, age and race, ethnicity, culture and language and to incorporate brief guidance on other characteristics, including time-dependent considerations, educational barriers and disabilities. In lieu of more holistic guidance on shared decision-making in ICD templates, trial recruiters could offer decision aids to support potential participants in navigating participation decisions. Collectively, these improvements to ethics guidance can help trial teams, sponsors and institutions improve the quantity and quality of trial participation among a range of equity-deserving populations.
