Multi-modal Traffic Scenario Generation for Autonomous Driving System Testing DOI: 10.1145/3729348 Journal: PACMSE Volume: 2 FSE FSE078 7 CCS: Software and its engineering Software testing and debugging
Zhi Tu, Liangkun Niu, Wei Fan, Tianyi Zhang

Abstract
. Autonomous driving systems (ADS) require extensive testing and validation before deployment. However, it is tedious and time-consuming to construct traffic scenarios for ADS testing. In this paper, we propose TrafficComposer , a multi-modal traffic scenario construction approach for ADS testing. TrafficComposer takes as input a natural language (NL) description of a desired traffic scenario and a complementary traffic scene image. Then, it generates the corresponding traffic scenario in a simulator, such as CARLA and LGSVL. Specifically, TrafficComposer integrates high-level dynamic information about the traffic scenario from the NL description and intricate details about the surrounding vehicles, pedestrians, and the road network from the image. The information from the two modalities is complementary to each other and helps generate high-quality traffic scenarios for ADS testing. On a benchmark of 120 120 traffic scenarios, TrafficComposer achieves 97.0 % 97.0\% accuracy, outperforming the best-performing baseline by 7.3 % 7.3\% . Both direct testing and fuzz testing experiments on six ADSs prove the bug detection capabilities of the traffic scenarios generated by TrafficComposer . These scenarios can directly discover 37 37 bugs and help two fuzzing methods find 33 % 33\% – 124 % 124\% more bugs serving as initial seeds.

1.
Introduction

Despite significant advancements in autonomous driving, traffic accidents caused by autonomous vehicles continue to occur
(
Associated Press News 2020
;
BBC News 2019
;
BBC News 2016a
;
BBC News 2016b
;
The Mercury News 2018
)
.
This highlights the critical need for effective testing methods for autonomous driving systems (ADSs).
A common practice in ADS testing is asking ADS developers to manually craft test scenarios, in which the developers specify the maneuvers of surrounding vehicles and the ego vehicle
(
Carla 2024
;
Deng et al. 2020
)
.
However, these manual approaches are often tedious and time-consuming.
Recently, researchers have proposed search-based testing
(
Abdessalem et al. 2018
;
Gambi et al. 2019b
;
Gambi et al. 2019c
;
Luo et al. 2021
)
and fuzzing
(
Sun et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Kim et al. 2022
;
Zhong et al. 2023
;
Cheng et al. 2023
)
approaches, which employ heuristic functions or machine learning models to generate test scenarios. In particular, LawBreaker
(
Sun et al. 2022
)
shows that ADS fuzzing can be significantly enhanced with high-quality traffic scenarios and oracles.
However, LawBreaker still requires developers to manually specify the scenarios and oracles in their DSL.

To reduce the manual effort, we propose
TrafficComposer
, a multi-modal approach to automate the generation of executable traffic scenarios in simulation.

TrafficComposer
uses both textual descriptions and reference images as inputs.
The two modalities provide complementary information to describe the designated traffic scenario.
Textual descriptions provide a high-level overview of the desired scenario, but can become cumbersome to convey details, e.g., the specific location and color of each vehicle.
In contrast, reference images can provide the specific details lacking in textual descriptions.
However, due to their static nature, images fail to convey dynamic information, such as the speed and behaviors of vehicles and pedestrians within the scenario, which textual descriptions can help with.

A key feature of
TrafficComposer
is its ability to extract and align the information from multi-modal inputs.

TrafficComposer
leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) to efficiently extract information from textual description inputs.
It also uses pre-trained computer vision models to analyze the reference image for the desired traffic scene.
To encapsulate the information extracted from both textual and visual inputs,
TrafficComposer
employs an intermediate representation (IR) based on a domain-specific language.

TrafficComposer
then aligns and merges the two IRs to generate detailed and accurate traffic scenarios, which will then be converted to an executable traffic scenario in a simulator.

To evaluate
TrafficComposer
, we first measure how closely the generated traffic scenarios align with the text and image descriptions (Section
4.2
). We constructed a benchmark of
120
scenarios (Section
4.1
) and compared
TrafficComposer
with a state-of-the-art approach, TARGET
(
Deng et al. 2023
)
and six multi-modal LLMs.

TrafficComposer
achieved
97.0\%
accuracy, outperforming TARGET by an absolute margin of
22.7\%
and the best-performing multi-modal LLM, GPT-4o, by
7.3\%
.
We then measure the bug detection capabilities of
TrafficComposer
-generated scenarios by using them as direct test cases and as initial seeds for two ADS fuzzing methods
(
Kim et al. 2022
;
Sun et al. 2022
)
(Section
4.3
).
The generated scenarios exposed
37
ADS failures in direct testing and helped the fuzzers discover
33\%
–
124\%
more ADS failures compared to using the same number of original seeds.
We further examine the correlation between scenario accuracy and bug-exposing effectiveness by a comparative study (Section
4.4
) and demonstrate the complementary contributions of textual and visual modalities via an ablation study (Section
4.5
).
We also analyze
TrafficComposer
’s sensitivity to eight LLMs and ten computer vision models, along with the potential errors they introduce (Section
4.6
and
4.7
).

Overall, this work makes the following contributions:

•

We propose a novel multi-modal approach that automatically constructs traffic scenarios in simulation.

•

We develop the first multi-modal benchmark for traffic scenario generation and ADS testing.

•

We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of
TrafficComposer
with
120

TrafficComposer
via both direct testing and fuzz testing. We demonstrated the contribution of each modality via an ablation study and the generalizability of
TrafficComposer
across eight LLMs and ten computer vision models.

2.
Motivating Example

To illustrate the challenges in traffic scenario design for ADS testing, we show a motivating example in Figure
1
.
Suppose that Alice is an ADS developer who wants to test an ADS in a particular scenario of interest.
She can describe the scenario with text as in Figure
1(a)
.
In her textual description, she can efficiently convey information such as weather and time of day about the scenario.
However, she finds it cumbersome to specify the positions of multiple vehicles and pedestrians due to the inherent ambiguity of the natural language description.
For instance, Alice may write, “
there are other cars in front of the ego vehicle in both left and right lanes.
” Yet, this description is too vague to specify the number of vehicles in each lane and their exact locations, which are essential for rendering the traffic scenario in simulators.

Alternatively, Alice can use a reference image to enhance the description of the traffic scenario, as in Figure
1(b)
.
This image clearly depicts the lane divisions, the precise positions of surrounding vehicles and pedestrians, and the status of the traffic lights.
With this information, the desired traffic scenario becomes more detailed.
However, the reference image does not capture the dynamic aspects of the traffic scenario, such as vehicle speeds and maneuvers.
Therefore, Alice should employ both the textual description and the reference image as complementary inputs, combining dynamic and detailed spatial information for a comprehensive description.

Existing end-to-end traffic scenario generation methods, such as AC3R
(
Gambi et al. 2019a
)
and RMT
(
Deng et al. 2020
)
face challenges in accommodating free-form traffic scenario descriptions.
AC3R is designed to reconstruct car crash scenarios from police reports.
It requires input descriptions in a detailed and non-ambiguous narrative.
For instance, all vehicles in the traffic scenario must be explicitly described with their make, model, color, etc.
Similarly, RMT requires input descriptions to strictly follow the “
if–then
” grammar.
TARGET
(
Deng et al. 2023
)
supports a more flexible input format and can process traffic law descriptions in driving handbooks.
However, it exclusively relies on textual input and thus suffers from the inherent ambiguity of natural language descriptions. Consequently, the traffic scenarios generated by TARGET are relatively simple, typically involving only one vehicle or pedestrian.
Domain-specific languages like Scenic
(
Fremont et al. 2019
)
can be used to generate complex traffic scenarios. However, using such DSLs demands extensive manual effort and proficiency from developers.
For example, specifying our motivating example scenario in Scenic requires
114
lines of manual coding, as shown in our source code repository
(
Tu et al. 2025e
)
.

Our
approach,
TrafficComposer
, overcomes these challenges by automatically extracting and aligning information from two complementary modalities, enhancing scenario design precision and comprehensiveness.
Additionally,
TrafficComposer
automates the generation of executable traffic scenarios in simulation for ADS testing, enabling efficient traffic scenario design and generation.

3.
Approach

In this work, we introduce
TrafficComposer
, a multi-modal approach to mitigate the manual effort of constructing traffic scenarios for ADS testing. Figure
2
shows the workflow of
TrafficComposer
.

TrafficComposer
leverages an LLM-based textual description parser (Section
3.2
) and a novel visual information extractor (Section
3.3
) to distill information from the two input modalities.
Then,
TrafficComposer
aligns the information from both modalities and encapsulates it into a comprehensive traffic IR (Section
3.4
).
Subsequently,
TrafficComposer
automatically converts this integrated IR into an executable traffic scenario in a driving simulator (Section
3.5
).

3.1.
Traffic Scenario Representation

\begin{array}[]{lll}\textbf{\emph{Scenario}}&::=&\emph{Environment};\emph{Road\_network};\emph{Actors}\\
\textbf{\emph{Environment}}&::=&\emph{weather};\ \emph{time}\\
\emph{weather}&::=&\texttt{rainy}\ |\ \texttt{foggy}\ |\ \texttt{snowy}\ |\ \texttt{wet}\ |\ \texttt{...}\\
\emph{time}&::=&\texttt{daytime}\ |\ \texttt{nighttime}\\
\textbf{\emph{Road\_network}}&::=&\emph{road\_type};\ \emph{traffic\_signals};\ \emph{lane\_number}\\
\emph{road\_type}&::=&\texttt{intersection}\ |\ \texttt{roundabout}\ |\ \texttt{...}\\
\emph{traffic\_signals}&::=&\emph{traffic\_signs},\emph{traffic\_light}\\
\emph{traffic\_signs}&::=&\epsilon\ |\ \emph{traffic\_sign};\ \emph{traffic\_signs}\\
\emph{traffic\_sign}&::=&\texttt{stop\_sign}\ |\ \texttt{speed\_limit\_sign}\ |\ \texttt{...}\\
\emph{traffic\_light}&::=&\epsilon\ |\texttt{red\_light}\ |\ \texttt{green\_light}\\
\emph{lane\_number}&::=&\texttt{0}\ |\ \texttt{1}\ |\ \texttt{2}\ |\ \texttt{3}\ |\ \texttt{...}\\
\textbf{\emph{Actors}}&::=&\emph{ego\_vehicle};\ \emph{npc\_actors}\\
\emph{ego\_vehicle}&::=&\emph{behavior};\ \emph{position};\ \emph{lane\_idx}\\
\emph{npc\_actors}&::=&\epsilon\ |\ \emph{npc\_actor};\ \emph{npc\_actors}\\
\emph{npc\_actor}&::=&\emph{actor\_type};\ \emph{behavior};\ \emph{position}\\
\emph{actor\_type}&::=&\texttt{car}\ |\ \texttt{truck}\ |\ \texttt{train}\ |\ \texttt{pedestrian}\ |\ \texttt{...}\\
\emph{behavior}&::=&\texttt{go\_forward}\ |\ \texttt{turn\_left}\ |\ \texttt{static}\ |\ \texttt{...}\\
\emph{position}&::=&\emph{reference\_point};\ \emph{relative\_position}\\
\emph{reference\_point}&::=&\texttt{ego\_vehicle}\ |\ \emph{road\_type}\ |\ \emph{traffic\_sign}\\
\emph{relative\_position}&::=&\texttt{front}\ |\ \texttt{behind}\ |\ \texttt{left}\ |\ \texttt{on}\ |\ \texttt{...}\\
\emph{lane\_idx}&::=&\texttt{0}\ |\ \texttt{1}\ |\ \texttt{2}\ |\ \texttt{3}\ |\ \texttt{...}\\
\end{array}

.
The grammar of the traffic scenario DSL.

To effectively represent information extracted from two
modalities, we adopt and extend a Domain Specific Language (DSL) proposed by Deng et al.
(
Deng et al. 2023
)
.
We chose this DSL over other DSLs such as OpenSCENARIO
(
ASAM 2021
)
and Scenic
(
Fremont et al. 2019
;
Queiroz et al. 2019
)
because this DSL has a more concise syntax grammar and requires less information to specify a traffic scenario.
This concise grammar enables more accurate and efficient content generation using LLMs, improving the accuracy of the traffic scenarios generated by our framework.

The DSL from Deng et al. includes three main components: Environment, Road Network, and Actors, as shown in Figure
3
.
Each of these components is further divided into subcomponents to capture the specific semantic elements of traffic scenarios.

Environment
characterizes the time of day and the weather conditions.

Road network
specifies road type, lane configurations, and traffic signals.

Actor
describes each participant in a traffic scenario, including vehicles and pedestrians. For each actor, the DSL specifies the type, position, lane occupancy, and behavior (e.g., going forward, turning left, etc.).

We extend this DSL by incorporating a lane index element for each actor.
This allows for the description of an actor’s position by both rough relative position to the ego vehicle and the precise lane occupancies.
For example, to describe a car “on the left of the ego vehicle”, the DSL supports a relative position representation, such as “
reference_point
:
ego_vehicle
,
relative_position
:
left
”.
Simultaneously, it allows for precise lane occupancy specification, like “
lane_idx
:
1
” for the described vehicle and “
lane_idx
:
2
” for the ego vehicle.
This feature is fundamental to the DSL’s role in aligning multi-modal inputs of
TrafficComposer
, as detailed in Section
3.4
.

3.2.
Textual Description Parser

From the input textual description,
TrafficComposer
identifies three primary categories of attributes: (1) environment aspects, including weather conditions and the layout of road networks; (2) dynamic information about the vehicles and pedestrians, such as the speed, forthcoming maneuvers, driving directions; and (3) rough positions of the surrounding vehicles.

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate their remarkable abilities in interpreting and reasoning text across various domains.
Thus,
TrafficComposer
leverages LLMs to automatically extract traffic scenario information from the textual description using an DSL.
We use GPT-4o
(
OpenAI 2024a
)
(gpt-4o-2024-05-13) as the default LLM for
TrafficComposer
.
We also experimented with other LLMs, including Llama-3.1
(
Dubey and et al. 2024
)
, Claude
(
Anthropic PBC 2024
)
, Mistral
(
Mistral AI 2024
)
, and Titan
(
Amazon 2024
)
, as detailed in Section
4.6
.
We use the default hyperparameter settings for each LLM in experiments.

Since LLMs are sensitive to prompts, we meticulously design our prompts by integrating several effective prompting strategies, including role-playing
(
Shanahan et al. 2023
)
,
step-by-step instructions,
chain-of-thought
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
, and in-context few-shot learning
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
.
Specifically, our prompt is structured into four segments, as illustrated in Table
1
.
First, the LLM is assigned the role of a domain expert in autonomous driving.
Second, the task is introduced through step-by-step reasoning using the chain-of-thought strategy.
Third, an example traffic scenario represented in the DSL (Section
3.1
) is included to introduce the model with the grammar of the DSL.
The last segment of the prompt presents two examples, each consisting of a text description as input and the corresponding desired traffic scenario represented in the DSL format as output.
This is intended to familiarize the LLM with both the input structure and the output DSL representation.
Details on the prompt designs are available in our source code repository
(
Tu et al. 2025d
)
.

However, textual descriptions suffer from inherent ambiguity and lack of precision.
Manually specifying intricate details in textual descriptions, such as vehicle color, model, and precise positioning, is cumbersome
for ADS developers, often requiring 10 to 20 sentences.
These details are important for ADS testing, as evidenced by a well-known Tesla autopilot crash
(
BBC News 2016c
)
. On May 7, 2016, a Tesla Model S operating on Autopilot failed to detect a white trailer against a bright sky, resulting in a fatal collision in Florida.
Hence, to efficiently convey these essential details, we need an additional source, a reference image of the traffic scene, as detailed in the next section.

3.3.
Visual Information Extractor

From the input reference image,
TrafficComposer
extracts three categories of attributes: (1) actors in the image; (2) spatial positioning of the actors; and (3) environmental details, such as traffic light status and the presence of traffic signs.

To accomplish this,
TrafficComposer
uses two advanced pre-trained computer vision models for object and lane detection.
For object detection,
TrafficComposer
uses YOLOv10
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, a state-of-the-art model pre-trained on the COCO dataset
(
Lin et al. 2015
)
.
This model can detect
11
types of objects relevant to traffic scenario descriptions, including pedestrians, six types of vehicles (e.g., car, truck, bus, etc.), traffic lights, traffic signs, parking meters, and fire hydrants.
It achieves an average precision of
54.4\%
on the COCO
(
Lin et al. 2015
)
validation set. In our application for detecting traffic-related objects, it achieves
98.7\%
precision, representing state-of-the-art performance in object detection.
For lane detection,
TrafficComposer
uses CLRNet
(
Zheng et al. 2022
)
, a leading model in monocular lane detection
(
Huang et al. 2023
;
Kirchmeyer and Deng 2023
;
Guo et al. 2023
)
.
Given a traffic scene image, CLRNet generates fine-grained pixel-level annotations for detected lane dividing lines.
It achieves F1 scores of
80.47
and
96.12
on two large-scale lane detection benchmarks, CULane
(
Pan et al. 2018
)
and LLAMAS
(
Behrendt and Soussan 2019
)
, demonstrating strong performance in lane detection.

By integrating the detected positions of vehicles or pedestrians with lane markings,
TrafficComposer
can determine the specific lane occupied by each actor.
Similar to the textual description parser, the visual information extractor represents the extracted information in the DSL.

3.4.
Aligning IR From the Two Modalities

TrafficComposer
integrates information from two modalities by aligning the textual IR with the visual IR to generate the final IR for traffic scenarios.
For each component in the DSL, if conflicts arise between the two IRs,
TrafficComposer
adopts information from the IR with higher predefined priority.
The priority is determined based on the expressiveness of the corresponding modality for that component, as detailed below.
If there are elements not specified by either modality,
TrafficComposer
marks them as unspecified.
These elements are assigned predefined values when generating executable traffic simulations, as detailed in Section
3.5
.

Environment.

Textual descriptions are effective in conveying abstract environmental configurations, such as time of day, weather, and traffic signal information.
Therefore,
TrafficComposer
prioritizes the textual IR for the
Environment
components.
Although it is possible to infer this information from images, a single word in the textual description, such as "rainy" or "daytime," provides a more direct and reliable specification.

Road Network.

TrafficComposer
uses the
road types
,
traffic signs
and
traffic light
from textual IR, while it adpots the detailed
lane number
from visual IR.
Although traffic signal information can be extracted from reference images, we prioritize the textual description as it provides a direct and reliable specification.

Actors.

For the
Actors
element,
TrafficComposer
uses both textual and visual IRs.
The textual descriptions specify the type of actors and their dynamic properties, such as speed and behavior, while the visual input provides accurate positional details, such as lane occupation.
Unlike other elements, where one modality is prioritized,
TrafficComposer
aligns and merges actor information from both IRs.
Actors are categorized into two groups—those mentioned in both modalities and those mentioned in only one.
For actors present in both,
TrafficComposer
aligns them based on location, considering actors from both modalities at the same position to be the same entity.
It then merges information from both modalities. In case of conflicts,
TrafficComposer
prioritizes the textual IR for dynamic properties and the visual IR for positional details.
For actors mentioned in only one modality,
TrafficComposer
checks for potential conflicts, such as overlapping positions, before incorporating them into the final IR.
If any actor features are not specified by either modality,
TrafficComposer
marks these features as unspecified in the IR.
These features are assigned predefined values when converting the IR to executable traffic simulations, as detailed in Section
3.5
.

3.5.
Converting IR to Executable Traffic Scenario in Simulation

The aligned traffic IR provides a comprehensive description of the desired traffic scenario.
However, there is still a gap between this abstract traffic IR and executable traffic simulations.
Specifically, the traffic scenario generated by
TrafficComposer
is represented using an abstract DSL, as shown in Figure
3
, which cannot be directly executed by a simulator.
To bridge the gap,
TrafficComposer
introduces an IR converter, which automates the generation of executable traffic simulations for each simulator.
In our experiments, we implement the IR converter for the two commonly used traffic simulators in recent ADS testing research, CARLA
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2017
)
and LGSVL
(
Rong et al. 2020
)
.
Figure
4
illustrates a segment of the aligned IR and the corresponding generated script for the LGSVL simulator.

We abstract the process of generating traffic simulation scripts into three fundamental procedures:

set_roadnetwork
,
set_actor
, and
set_environment
, reflecting the three core attributes of a traffic simulation.

TrafficComposer
first searches for a map section that meets the required road network configurations, including
road type
,
lane number
, and
traffic signals
.

TrafficComposer
then determines the initial position of the ego vehicle in the selected map section, using its
lane_idx
and relative position in the road network.
For instance, in the example shown in Figure
4
, the ego vehicle is placed at
“lane_221”->5
.
The target position of the ego vehicle is then generated based on the specified
behavior
in IR.
The positions of other actors are calculated similarly, based on their
lane_idx
and their relative positions to the ego vehicle.
The environment configurations, including weather and time, are specified using the corresponding simulator APIs in the simulation script.
Since these operations are foundational for traffic simulations, traffic simulators such as CARLA and LGSVL offer corresponding APIs to configure these elements
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2017
;
Rong et al. 2020
)
.
For example, to add an actor to the traffic scenario, CARLA provides a function
carla.world.spawn_actor()
, and LGSVL has the corresponding function
Simulator.add()
.

TrafficComposer
also handles unspecified information in the aligned traffic IR.
For unspecified attributes in the
Environment
component, such as weather conditions or time of day,
TrafficComposer
assigns default values from predefined candidate sets.
Information for the
Road Network
component is reliably extracted from the reference image, ensuring no unspecified values.
For actors with unspecified positions,
TrafficComposer
assigns available locations while ensuring no overlap with existing actors in the scenario.
For actors with unspecified dynamic attributes, default values are applied.

TrafficComposer
randomly samples a speed between 0 and 30 miles per hour and assigns straightforward driving behavior, maintaining realism in the simulation.

With the IR Converter,
TrafficComposer
detaches the generation of the traffic simulations from their design.
This separation grants
TrafficComposer
the flexibility to swiftly and effortlessly adapt to new traffic simulation platforms.
More examples are provided in the
benchmark
folder of our research artifact
(
Tu et al. 2025a
)
.

RQ2

RQ3

4.1.
Traffic Scenario Benchmark

To evaluate the effectiveness of
TrafficComposer
, we construct a multi-model benchmark comprising
120
traffic scenarios.
Existing datasets such as nuScenes
(
Caesar et al. 2020
)
provide multi-modal sensor data, including camera images, radar signals, and Lidar signals from real-world vehicles. However, they do not include text descriptions or the counterpart tests in the simulation.
In contrast, our benchmark provides textual descriptions, reference images, ground truth IRs, and corresponding simulations, enabling the evaluation of ADS test generation methods in simulation.
These traffic scenarios are sourced from first-person perspective videos, including dashboard camera footages from widely used datasets such as CULane
(
Pan et al. 2018
)
, and
11
in-the-wild scenic drive videos on YouTube
(
J Utah 2018
;
Planet Drive 2023
;
Seoul Walker 2022
;
Walk East 2023
)
.
To ensure diversity, we cluster the videos based on road network types and geographic locations and select representative frames as reference images of the scenarios.
The benchmark is designed to capture the diversity of and complexity of real-world traffic conditions, including low-light scenarios, day-night variations, significant occlusions, and both highway and urban roads across
9
cities in
7
countries.

For each scenario, a textual description is created following a structured guideline
(
Tu et al. 2025b
)
.
The textual description should include three core components: (1) environment, defined by time and weather conditions; (2) road network, including lane configurations and traffic control signals; and (3) actors, focusing on the two to three most relevant actors to the ego vehicle, with details including position, speed, and behavior.
The descriptions should be concise, as the reference images provide additional details.
Ground truth traffic scenario IRs are annotated in the specialized DSL format.
With the textual description and the reference image combined, the ground truth IR of a traffic scenario is unique without ambiguity.
Two authors independently annotated the ground truth IRs based on textual descriptions and reference images.
The initial annotations yield an average Tree Edit Distance (TED) of
2.5
and an average IE accuracy of
97.7\%
, as measured by the metrics detailed in Section
4.2
.
A third author joins the reconciliation to resolve all disagreements.
These ground truth traffic IRs serve as the basis for measuring the effectiveness of
TrafficComposer
in information extraction, as detailed in Section
5.1
, Section
5.4
, and Section
5.5
.

TARGET’s scenarios are simple, each involving only one or two actors in addition to the ego vehicle, while LawBreaker includes only
10
scenarios, with an average of
7
actors per scenario.
In contrast, our proposed benchmark includes
120
scenarios, each containing
5
to
21
actors, with an average of
7.8
actors per scenario.
To quantify dataset diversity, we compute the Vendi Score
(
Friedman and Dieng 2023
)
, a state-of-the-art metric for dataset diversity, following SCTrans
(
Dai et al. 2024
)
.
Our benchmark achieves a Vendi Score of
22.1
, significantly higher than TARGET’s
17.0
and LawBreaker’s
3.1
.
These measurements underscore the benchmark’s advantages in capturing diverse and complex traffic scenarios, making it suitable for evaluating advanced test generation methods.

4.2.
Experiment Setup for RQ1

To answer RQ1, we compare the generated traffic scenarios to the ground truth, using the benchmark described in Section
4.1
.
We evaluate
TrafficComposer
against one single-modal LLM-based, six multi-modal LLM-based, and two traditional NLP method-based approaches, as detailed below.

The first baseline is TARGET
(
Deng et al. 2023
)
, which uses the single-modal LLM GPT-4
(
OpenAI et al. 2024
)
to extract information from driving handbooks.
While TARGET is a strong baseline due to its use of LLM, it cannot process visual inputs.
Therefore, we provide only textual descriptions to TARGET in experiments.
We contacted TARGET’s authors for the original implementation, and adopt their prompt design.
Since the DSL used in
TrafficComposer
is extended on TARGET’s DSL, to ensure a fair comparison, we exclude the extended components when calculating the accuracy for TARGET.

Furthermore, we consider six multi-modal LLMs, including GPT-4o
(
OpenAI 2024a
)
(
gpt-4o-2024-05-13
), GPT-4o-mini
(
OpenAI 2024b
)
(
gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18
), Claude-3.5 Sonnet v2
(
Anthopic PBC 2024
)
(
claude-3-5-sonnet-20241022-v2
), Claude-3.5 Sonnet v1
(
Anthropic PBC 2024
)
(
claude-3-5-sonnet-20240620-v1
), Llama 3.2 90B Vision Instruct
(
Meta 2024
)
(
Llama-3.2-90B-Vision-Instruct
), and LLAVA
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Liu 2023
)
(
llava-v1.6-34b
).
Unlike TARGET, these models can process both the textual description and the reference image as inputs.
For these LLMs, we adapt the prompt design in
TrafficComposer
to handle textual inputs and further modify it to incorporate visual inputs. The adapted prompt integrates all the prompting strategies used in
TrafficComposer
, including role-playing, step-by-step instructions, chain-of-thought reasoning, and in-context few-shot learning.
Details on prompt designs are available in our source code repository
(
Tu et al. 2025c
)
.

Finally, we also consider two traffic scenario generation methods using traditional NLP techniques, AC3R
(
Gambi et al. 2019a
)
and RMT
(
Deng et al. 2020
)
.
AC3R
(
Gambi et al. 2019a
)
uses the Stanford Core NLP library
(
Manning et al. 2014
)
and a domain-specific ontology to extract information about traffic crashes from police reports.
However, AC3R imposes strict requirements on input formatting, such as the need for detailed vehicle information (make, model, color, etc.), making it unable to process the free-form natural language descriptions of our traffic scenarios.
RMT
(
Deng et al. 2020
)
uses dependency parsing
(
Kübler et al. 2009
)
to extract information from natural language descriptions of traffic rules.
RMT requires inputs to follow a strict “if–then” format, making it incapable of processing our free-form textual descriptions.

Evaluation metric.

We compare a generated traffic scenario with the ground truth by measuring the tree edit distance (TED)
(
Pawlik and Augsten 2016
;
Pawlik and Augsten 2015
)
. Tree edit distance is a commonly used metric to measure the similarity of programs in software engineering literature
(
Jiang et al. 2007
;
Yang et al. 2005
)
.
In our experiments, TED quantifies both structure similarity and node similarity. When computing the distance between two IRs, missing or extra tree structures contribute to the distance based on the number of nodes involved. For tree structures that align, the algorithm further examines the values of individual nodes.
We calculate the information extraction (IE) accuracy based on the TED as follows:

(1)

\textit{IE Accuracy}(S;G)=1-\frac{TED(S,G)}{\textit{Total number of AST nodes (G)}}

where
S
is the generated traffic scenario in DSL format, and
G
is the ground truth IR.
This accuracy metric measures how closely the generated scenario aligns with the ground truth.
To mitigate the impact of randomness, we repeat each experiment three times.

4.3.
Experiment Setup for RQ2

To answer RQ2, we evaluate the bug detection capabilities of traffic scenarios generated by
TrafficComposer
in two ways.
First, we use these traffic scenarios as test cases to run ADS testing directly.
Second, we use these scenarios as initial seeds for ADS fuzzing methods. As shown by LawBreaker
(
Sun et al. 2022
)
, high-quality seeds can significantly boost the effectiveness of ADS fuzzing, which forms our hypothesis.

Using generated traffic scenarios as direct test cases.

We directly test six ADSs in the
120
traffic scenarios generated by
TrafficComposer
(detailed in Section
4.1
).
These six ADSs are of different performance levels, ranging from industry-level ADSs to academic prototypes.
We include two Level 4
(
SAE On-Road Automated Vehicle Standards Committee 2014
)
ADSs, Apollo
(
team 2020
)
and Autoware
(
Kato et al. 2018
)
, both of which are recognized for their robust performance and are frequently employed as benchmarks in ADS testing research
(
Li et al. 2020
;
Sun et al. 2022
;
Zhong et al. 2023
;
Kim et al. 2022
;
Feng et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2019
)
. Additionally, we test on MMFN
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
, Roach
(
Zhang et al. 2021
)
, and TransFuser
(
Chitta et al. 2023
)
—three cutting-edge ADSs proposed in top-tier conferences or journals, including IROS, CVPR, and PAMI.
In the end, we also included CARLA Behavior Agent
(
CARLA team 2022
)
, a naive driving system in the CARLA simulator.
This ensures a comprehensive assessment covering a broad range of ADS capabilities, from advanced to basic ADSs.

Using generated traffic scenarios as initial seeds in fuzzing methods.

We test the six ADSs in fuzz testing with two state-of-the-art ADS fuzzing methods, DriveFuzz
(
Kim et al. 2022
)
and LawBreaker
(
Sun et al. 2022
)
.

Details on the two fuzzing methods.

DriveFuzz
(
Kim et al. 2022
)
tests whether the ADS encounters collisions, infractions, or immobility by mutating various factors of traffic scenarios.
These mutation operations include adding a new participant, altering the starting and ending points of a participant, modifying the location, size, and friction of puddles, and adjusting weather conditions.
LawBreaker
(
Sun et al. 2022
)
introduces a signal temporal logic-based DSL for specifying real-world traffic laws and a specification-coverage guided fuzzing algorithm for ADS testing.
It tests ADSs against traffic accidents and traffic law violations, covering 24 Chinese traffic laws.
LawBreaker supports mutations, including changes in position, speed, time, weather, and vehicle type.

Baselines.

The effectiveness of the traffic scenarios generated by
TrafficComposer
is compared to two baseline seed settings: (1) using the original initial seeds of the fuzzing methods, and (2) using the traffic scenario generated by TARGET
(
Deng et al. 2023
)
.
We elaborate on each setting below.

(1)

The initial seeds of DriveFuzz are randomly generated using predefined road maps in the CARLA simulator.
Each scenario includes only one actor, with its position randomly assigned, and the weather condition is fixed to sunny.
DriveFuzz generates
40
initial seed traffic scenarios.
Before the fuzzing process, all seed scenarios are verified for legitimacy by dry-running the ADS under test and ensuring that it operates normally without encountering errors.
LawBreaker’s fuzzing process begins with ten high-quality traffic scenarios handcrafted by LawBreaker’s authors. These traffic scenarios are more complex than those in DriveFuzz, including, on average, seven participants other than the ego vehicle.

(2)

Deng et al.
(
Deng et al. 2023
)
construct
98
traffic scenarios using TARGET based on traffic laws in the Texas Driver Handbook
(
of Public Safety 2022
)
. Each scenario consists of the ego vehicle and one other actor.

DriveFuzz is officially implemented on the CARLA simulator, while LawBreaker is on the LGSVL simulator.
Due to the discontinuation of the LGSVL simulator, the majority of our evaluated ADSs do not support LGSVL.
Thus, we can only run LawBreaker fuzzing experiments on LGSVL and Apollo.
LawBreaker relies on an outdated API, necessitating the use of Apollo v6.0 for compatibility.

To ensure fair comparison, we randomly sample the same number of seeds as each baseline setting.
Specifically, for the comparison with DriveFuzz, we randomly sample
40
scenarios from the
120
scenarios generated by
TrafficComposer
in the benchmark.
For comparison with LawBreaker, we sample ten scenarios.
Before fuzzing, following the DriveFuzz
(
Kim et al. 2022
)
procedure, we pre-validate the sampled scenarios by dry-running the ADS under test to confirm they do not immediately cause crashes or traffic rule violations.
If a scenario fails this validation, we resample and validate another from the benchmark.
For each experiment, we run the fuzzer for six hours, following the time budget setting from DriveFuzz
(
Kim et al. 2022
)
.

6.
Limitations and Future Work

Limitations.

The visual information extractor in
TrafficComposer
leverages two pre-trained Computer Vision models to detect lane divisions and objects such as vehicles, pedestrians, and traffic signals.
The object detection model, YOLOv10
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, achieves an average precision of
98.7\%
in our traffic-specific application.
The lane detection model, CLRNet
(
Zheng et al. 2022
)
, achieves F1 scores of
80.47
and
96.12
on two large-scale lane detection benchmarks, CULane
(
Pan et al. 2018
)
and LLAMAS
(
Behrendt and Soussan 2019
)
.
Based on the information extracted by the two models,
TrafficComposer
determines the position of each vehicle or pedestrian using lane occupancy.
This straightforward and transparent algorithm extracts information from the reference image and improves the overall accuracy of the generated traffic scenarios, as shown in Section
5.4
.
However, the effectiveness of
TrafficComposer
is inherently tied to the performance of the two computer vision models.
Despite these models achieving state-of-the-art performance in their respective domains, the accuracy of
TrafficComposer
’s visual information extraction may be affected by their detection capabilities.

Moreover, the textual description parser in
TrafficComposer
employs an LLM to extract information from textual descriptions, which introduces the potential for hallucination issues inherent in LLMs.
However,
TrafficComposer
mitigates this vulnerability with the visual information extractor, which is resistant to such problems.
The visual information extractor utilizes a deterministic algorithm to analyze bounding boxes predicted by pre-trained computer vision models, ensuring more reliable and interpretable outputs.
By aligning the information from visual and textual input,
TrafficComposer
effectively reduces the impact of hallucinations on its overall performance.

Future Directions.

Based on the insights from our evaluation and analysis of the limitations, we propose the following three future directions for traffic scenario generation for ADS testing.
First,
TrafficComposer
currently accepts only reference images as visual input for scenario generation.
Expanding the capabilities of visual information extractor to process additional types of visual input, such as videos or point clouds, could significantly enhance the richness and accuracy of scenario generation.
Second, there is a need for a larger and more diverse set of high-quality traffic scenarios for comprehensive ADS testing.
We plan to extend our benchmark to include more diverse scenarios, which will better serve the needs of the ADS testing community.
Third, we plan to extend
TrafficComposer
to support other DSLs, such as Scenic and OpenSCENARIO, to expand its potential applications.

7.
Threats to Validity

External validity

concerns the generalizability of
TrafficComposer
.
One potential threat to validity is the applicability of
TrafficComposer
to different ADSs.
While we have made significant efforts to evaluate the effectiveness of
TrafficComposer
in ADS testing, it is not feasible to test all available ADSs.
To mitigate this threat, we conduct experiments with a diverse set of six ADSs, ranging from industrial-level ADSs to simpler ADSs, to assess the bug detection capabilities of
TrafficComposer
-generated scenarios.

Another threat to validity is the generalizability of
TrafficComposer
to various LLMs.
We mitigate this by evaluating
TrafficComposer
with eight LLMs from five model families, including both open-source and closed-source models.
The size of the LLMs ranges from 8B to 405B parameters.
The results demonstrate that
TrafficComposer
maintains robust scenario generation capabilities across a wide range of LLMs used in the textual description parser.

Internal validity

concerns the randomness inherent in
TrafficComposer
and in the evaluation.
One threat to internal validity is the randomness brought by simulation-based testing.
To mitigate this threat, we repeat all fuzzing experiments ten times to reduce the impact of randomness, and further conduct statistical analysis to validate the significance of the findings.
Another potential threat to internal validity is the randomness brought by LLM in textual description parser.
To mitigate this threat, we repeat experiments involving LLMs three times, and report the average results in evaluations.

8.
Related Work

8.1.
Traffic Scenarios Generation

The research most related to our proposal is traffic scenario generation approaches
(
Gambi et al. 2019a
;
Bashetty et al. 2020
;
Fremont et al. 2019
;
Zhou et al. 2023
;
Deng et al. 2020
;
Huai et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2024
;
Zhang and Cai 2023
;
Tang et al. 2024
;
Tian et al. 2024
)
.
These approaches fall into two categories: (i) reconstructing real-world traffic scenarios from data collected from real-world events,
and (ii) generating traffic scenarios from manually created specifications.

AC3R
(
Gambi et al. 2019a
)
and RMT
(
Deng et al. 2020
)
use traditional NLP methods
to extract information from accident reports and traffic rules.
More recently, LLM-based approaches
(
Deng et al. 2023
;
Tang et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2024
)
have emerged.
LeGEND
(
Tang et al. 2024
)
introduces a two-stage approach that transforms accident reports into formal logical scenarios using two LLMs
to generate critical and diverse scenarios.
SoVAR
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
leverages tailored linguistic patterns in LLM prompts to extract information from accident reports.
In parallel, video-based methods, DeepCrashTest
(
Bashetty et al. 2020
)
and M-CPS
(
Zhang and Cai 2023
)
, reconstruct crash scenarios using vehicle dashboard camera and surveillance camera footage, respectively.

Compared to existing work,
TrafficComposer
introduces a novel multi-modal framework that extracts and aligns complementary information from free-form textual descriptions and visual inputs.
Moreover, while existing methods
(
Tang et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2024
;
Deng et al. 2023
)
typically focus on scenarios with one to two actors in addition to the ego vehicle,
TrafficComposer
can generate complex scenarios involving up to
21
actors.
Additionally, unlike M-CPS
(
Zhang and Cai 2023
)
, which processes surveillance camera footage using panoptic segmentation,
TrafficComposer
leverages images readily available from vehicle dashboard cameras and combines object detection with lane detection to produce a comprehensive description of the traffic scenario.

Both Scenic
(
Fremont et al. 2019
)
and AVUnit
(
Zhou et al. 2023
)
introduce Domain Specific Languages (DSLs) for specifying traffic scenarios.
However, the complexity of these DSLs presents challenges for ADS developers. Creating large-scale traffic scenarios with them is effortful and time-consuming.
RMT
(
Deng et al. 2020
)
adopts natural language inputs, offering greater ease of use, but struggles with the inherent ambiguity of natural language that limits detailed scenario description.
In contrast,
TrafficComposer
stands out by leveraging multi-modal inputs for the automated generation of traffic scenarios.
This strategy addresses the challenges posed by natural language ambiguity and DSL complexity and enhances both the precision and efficiency of automated traffic scenario generation.

8.2.
Simulation-based ADS Testing

In addition to directly generating traffic scenarios, there is also a large body of research focusing on searching for traffic scenarios from initial seeds to test ADSs.
These methods typically start with a set of seed scenarios and adopt various search algorithms to explore the scenario search space.
For example, methods like DeepRoad
(
Zhang et al. 2018
)
and DeepTest
(
Tian et al. 2018
)
generate mutations based on the original image to test deep neural network-based ADSs.
However, we will not get into details on this topic and focus on simulation-based methods.
AsFault
(
Gambi et al. 2019b
;
Gambi et al. 2019c
)
searches for complex road network layouts and generates them in BeamNG simulator to test ADS lane-keeping capabilities.
AutoFuzz
(
Zhong et al. 2023
)
mutates the starting and destination locations, directions, and velocities of vehicles or pedestrians near the ego vehicle.
Moreover, AV-Fuzzer
(
Li et al. 2020
)
, EMOOD
(
Luo et al. 2021
)
and BehAVExplor
(
Cheng et al. 2023
)
perform perturbations in driving maneuvers of vehicles or pedestrians around the ego vehicle.
FITEST
(
Abdessalem et al. 2018
)
, LawBreaker
(
Sun et al. 2022
)
, and DriveFuzz
(
Kim et al. 2022
)
further expand the scope by mutating more factors of traffic scenarios, such as weather and traffic signals.
ConfVE
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
focuses on searching the ADS configuration space to detect unique system failures.
LEADE
(
Tian et al. 2024
)
proposes to model evolutionary search as a question-answering task and to use LLMs to search for diverse scenarios.
Our proposed method is complementary to these methods since the traffic scenarios generated by our method can serve as high-quality initial seeds, as demonstrated in our comprehensive experiments.

9.
Conclusion

This paper presents
TrafficComposer
, a novel multi-modal traffic scenario generator specifically designed for ADS testing.
The primary innovation of
TrafficComposer
lies in its unique integration of textual and visual inputs from ADS developers, leveraging the clarity and abstraction of textual descriptions and the detailed specificity of visual inputs.
Evaluated on a benchmark of
120
traffic scenarios,
TrafficComposer
achieves
97.0\%
accuracy, outperforming the best-performing baseline by
7.3\%
.
Testing ADSs directly using these scenarios successfully identifies
37
bugs.
As initial seeds in fuzzing, these scenarios significantly improve bug detection effectiveness by
33\%
–
124\%
and time efficiency by
28\%
–
58\%
.
An ablation study demonstrates the contribution of each input modality, while further experiments confirm the generalizability of
TrafficComposer
across different large language models and computer vision models.
Its ability to seamlessly adapt to different traffic scenarios, ADS configurations, and fuzzing algorithms highlights its potential as a transformative framework in the field of ADS testing.
