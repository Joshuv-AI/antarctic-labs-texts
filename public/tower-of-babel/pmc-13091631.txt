Innovative Applications of Nanomaterials in the Diagnosis and Treatment of Ocular Diseases: Targeted Delivery, Intelligent Therapy, Diagnostic Breakthroughs, and Synergistic Strategies with Stem Cells and Biomaterials
Authors: Guanyu Su, Weixing Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe complex physiological structures and multiple biological barriers of the eye pose significant challenges to drug delivery, resulting in poor absorption and limited therapeutic efficacy of conventional treatments. In this review, nanomaterials with their distinctive physicochemical properties and biocompatibility have emerged as essential tools in the diagnosis and treatment of ocular diseases. We summarized the latest advancements in the application of nanomaterials for targeted drug delivery, smart therapeutic strategies, and early diagnostic technologies specific to ocular diseases. This work provides a comprehensive analysis of the design concepts, biocompatibility, and controlled release mechanisms of various nanocarriers. It also examines their therapeutic potential in inflammatory eye diseases and retinal degenerative conditions. Additionally, the advantages of naturally derived nanomaterials and their potential applications in ocular drug delivery systems are explored. Furthermore, the role of stem cell therapies in enhancing tissue regeneration and supporting treatment efficacy is discussed. In the future, this review discusses the integration of nanotechnology with artificial intelligence to advance the development of precision medicine in ophthalmology by elucidating their synergistic roles. The aim of this article is to provide researchers and clinicians with a systematic theoretical foundation and practical guidelines to promote the clinical translation and application of nanomaterials in the field of ophthalmology. Distinct from existing reviews, this work integrates nanomaterial design advances with stem cell therapy, artificial intelligence and smart responsive systems, comprehensively addresses clinical translation challenges, and provides a forward-looking perspective on personalized ophthalmic medicine, filling the gap of lacking a holistic cross-disciplinary summary in current literature.

Introduction

Ocular diseases are a significant global health concern, affecting millions of individuals and leading to severe visual impairment or blindness.1 The complexity of the eye’s anatomy and physiology presents substantial challenges in effectively diagnosing and treating eye diseases. Furthermore, diseases such as diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma, and age-related macular degeneration not only threaten vision but also they impact the overall quality of life for affected individuals. The intricate interplay of various ocular structures—including the cornea, retina, and optic nerve—creates barriers that hinder the delivery of therapeutic agents and thereby complicate treatment strategies. Thus, there is an urgent need for innovative approaches that can enhance drug delivery, improve therapeutic efficacy, and ultimately provide better outcomes for patients suffering from ocular diseases.2

Traditional ophthalmic drug delivery methods, primarily involving eye drops, face significant challenges, including low bioavailability and rapid clearance from the ocular surface.3 Conventional topical drug delivery methods often require frequent administration, leading to poor patient adherence and suboptimal therapeutic effects.4 The limited penetration of drugs through the corneal epithelium and the presence of protective mechanisms, such as tear film turnover and the nasolacrimal drainage system, further exacerbate the difficulties in achieving effective drug concentrations at the target sites within the eye.5 Consequently, there is a pressing need to explore alternative drug delivery systems that can overcome these limitations and enhance the therapeutic effects of ophthalmic medications.6 Nanotechnology has emerged as a promising solution to address the challenges associated with ocular drug delivery. The unique properties of nanomaterials, including their small size, large surface area, and ability to modify surface properties such as charge and hydrophobicity, enable them to penetrate biological barriers more effectively than conventional formulations.7 Furthermore, recent advancements in nanotechnology have paved the way for the development of novel ophthalmic drug delivery systems, which hold great promise for revolutionizing the treatment of ocular diseases.8

Furthermore, the integration of nanotechnology with other therapeutic modalities, such as stem cell therapy and gene delivery, offers exciting possibilities for advancing personalized medicine in ophthalmology.9 For instance, the combination of nanomaterials with stem cells could facilitate targeted delivery of regenerative therapies to damaged ocular tissues, promoting healing and functional recovery.10 Additionally, the use of nanocarriers for gene therapy has the potential to address genetic disorders affecting the eye, this approach enables correction of underlying pathophysiological mechanisms through gene editing or modulation of gene expression.11 By integrating these innovative strategies, treatment can become more precise and individualized, directly supporting the growing emphasis on personalized medicine in healthcare.12

The objective of this review is to summarize the innovative applications of nanomaterials in overcoming the barriers associated with ocular drug delivery. We aim to analyze the current research on the use of nanotechnology in diagnosing, treating, and regenerating ocular tissues. Additionally, we will discuss the clinical translation challenges that arise when implementing these advanced technologies in practice and explore future directions, such as novel nanomaterial designs and targeted delivery systems, that could shape ophthalmic therapeutics. By providing a comprehensive overview of nanotechnology’s potential in ophthalmology, we hope to inspire further research. We also aim to foster collaboration among scientists, clinicians, and industry stakeholders to improve patient outcomes in ocular health.13

Introduction and Classification of Nanomaterials

Types of Nanocarriers and Their Characteristics

Nanocarriers have revolutionized drug delivery systems, particularly in ophthalmology, where they address the challenges posed by the eye’s complex barriers, such as the corneal epithelium and blood-retinal barrier.14 Common types of nanocarriers include liposomes, dendrimers, nanoparticles, and nanogels.15–18 Each of these carriers exhibits unique physicochemical properties that significantly influence their performance in drug delivery applications. For example, liposomes, which are spherical vesicles composed of lipid bilayers, are particularly advantageous due to their biocompatibility and ability to encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs.19 Their size can be finely tuned, typically ranging from 50 nm to several micrometers. This size control allows for controlled and sustained drug release profiles, leading to improved bioavailability in ocular tissues.20 Dendrimers, on the other hand, are branched macromolecules that can be engineered to possess specific surface functionalities, enhancing their interaction with target cells and improving drug solubility.21 Their uniform size and shape facilitate predictable pharmacokinetics, which is particularly beneficial in achieving targeted delivery.22

Nanoparticles, including polymeric and inorganic types, offer a versatile platform for drug delivery due to their high surface area-to-volume ratio, which enhances drug loading capacity and stability.23 For instance, polymeric nanoparticles can be designed to respond to specific stimuli, such as pH or temperature, allowing for controlled drug release in response to the local environment of the eye.24 In contrast, inorganic nanoparticles, such as gold or silica nanoparticles, can provide additional functionalities, such as imaging capabilities or photothermal effects, which can be leveraged for therapeutic purposes.22 Nanogels, which are cross-linked polymer networks, exhibit remarkable swelling properties and can encapsulate a wide range of therapeutic agents and their ability to respond to environmental changes makes them suitable for sustained release applications, particularly in the treatment of chronic ocular conditions.25

The size, surface modification, and release mechanisms of these nanocarriers play a critical role in determining their efficacy in ocular drug delivery.26 For example, smaller nanoparticles (typically in the range of 100–200 nm) are often preferred for ocular applications as they can penetrate deeper into ocular tissues and achieve better therapeutic outcomes.27 Surface modifications, such as PEGylation or the incorporation of targeting ligands, can enhance the biocompatibility and specificity of nanocarriers, reducing off-target effects and improving drug retention in the eye.28 Furthermore, the release mechanisms of nanocarriers can be engineered to facilitate controlled release profiles, which is crucial for maintaining therapeutic drug levels over extended periods and minimizing the frequency of administration.29 Figure 1 illustrates the various mechanisms by which nanoparticles enhance drug delivery across the ocular barriers, including active targeting, improved bioavailability, increased precorneal retention, enhanced barrier penetration, and controlled release. Summary of Common Nanocarriers Used in Ophthalmic Drug Delivery

Organic Nanocarriers

Organic nanocarriers have emerged as a transformative approach in the field of ophthalmology, particularly for the targeted delivery of therapeutic agents to treat ocular diseases such as keratitis and other inflammatory conditions. As previously noted, the biocompatibility of liposomes makes them ideal for encapsulating various drugs. For instance, liposomes, which are spherical vesicles composed of phospholipid bilayers, can encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs, thereby enhancing their solubility and stability. This property is particularly beneficial for ocular applications where drug bioavailability is often limited due to the eye’s unique anatomical and physiological barriers.30 Furthermore, polymeric nanoparticles, such as those made from biodegradable polymers like poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), can be engineered to release their payload in a controlled manner, thus prolonging the therapeutic effect of the encapsulated drug while minimizing systemic side effects.31

Recent studies have highlighted the efficacy of these organic nanocarriers in delivering anti-inflammatory drugs such as bevacizumab, a monoclonal antibody against vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which plays a crucial role in angiogenesis associated with various ocular diseases.32 The encapsulation of bevacizumab in organic nanoparticles has demonstrated enhanced bioavailability and reduced toxicity compared to conventional delivery methods. Moreover, the physicochemical properties of these nanocarriers can be tailored to optimize their interaction with ocular tissues, facilitating better penetration through the corneal barriers.33

In addition to liposomes and polymeric nanoparticles, dendrimers represent another promising class of organic nanocarriers. Their branched, tree-like structure allows for high drug loading capacity and the possibility of surface modification with targeting ligands, which can enhance the specificity of drug delivery to inflamed ocular tissues.34 This targeted approach is essential in treating conditions such as keratitis, where localized drug delivery is necessary to achieve therapeutic concentrations at the site of inflammation while minimizing systemic exposure.

Despite the promising advantages of organic nanocarriers, challenges remain in their clinical translation. The stability of these nanocarriers in physiological conditions and the reproducibility of their production processes are critical factors that need to be addressed. Additionally, understanding the mechanisms by which these carriers interact with ocular tissues, including adhesion and penetration, is essential for optimizing their design and improving therapeutic outcomes.35 Future research should focus on overcoming these barriers, potentially through the development of hybrid systems that combine the strengths of different nanocarrier types, thereby enhancing the efficacy of ocular drug delivery systems. Overall, organic nanocarriers hold significant potential in revolutionizing the treatment of ocular diseases through targeted and sustained drug delivery, ultimately improving patient outcomes in ophthalmology.

Inorganic Nanomaterials

Inorganic nanomaterials, particularly metal nanoparticles such as gold (AuNPs) and silver nanoparticles, have emerged as pivotal tools in the diagnosis and treatment of ocular diseases. Gold nanoparticles, in particular, have garnered attention for their unique optical properties, which facilitate their use in molecular imaging and pathogen detection. A notable application is their integration into the CRISPR-Cas12a system, where AuNPs enable the visualization of DNA, thereby enhancing the sensitivity and specificity of genetic assays. This innovative approach not only allows for the rapid identification of ocular pathogens but also holds promise for the early detection of genetic markers associated with various ocular conditions. The versatility of gold nanoparticles is further demonstrated by their ability to be functionalized with targeting ligands, which can improve the delivery of therapeutic agents directly to affected ocular tissues, minimizing systemic exposure and potential side effects.36 On the other hand, silver nanoparticles, while also effective in antimicrobial applications, raise concerns regarding their cytotoxicity, particularly in ocular tissues. The release of silver ions can lead to corneal damage, necessitating a careful evaluation of their therapeutic window in clinical settings. The dual nature of these metal nanoparticles—offering both diagnostic and therapeutic benefits while posing potential risks—highlights the need for ongoing research to optimize their use in ophthalmology.

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSiNPs) represent another class of inorganic nanomaterials that have shown promise in ocular drug delivery. Their high drug-loading capacity allows for the encapsulation of a variety of therapeutic agents, making them suitable for sustained release formulations. This characteristic is particularly advantageous for treating chronic ocular diseases, where prolonged drug exposure is essential for therapeutic efficacy. However, the use of MSiNPs is not without its challenges. Recent studies have indicated that the release of silver ions from silver-doped MSiNPs can lead to synergistic toxicity, exacerbating corneal injuries and raising concerns about their safety profile in ocular applications. This highlights the critical need for a thorough understanding of the interactions between nanoparticles and biological systems, particularly in the sensitive ocular environment. The balance between maximizing therapeutic benefits while minimizing potential toxic effects is crucial for the successful integration of MSiNPs into clinical practice. As research progresses, it is essential to refine the design and functionalization of these inorganic nanomaterials to enhance their safety and efficacy in treating ocular diseases.36 Overall, the innovative applications of inorganic nanomaterials in ophthalmology underscore their potential to revolutionize the diagnosis and treatment of ocular diseases, paving the way for more effective and targeted therapies.

Carbon-Based Materials

Carbon-based materials, particularly carbon nanotubes (CNTs), have emerged as a revolutionary tool in the management of diabetic retinopathy through their application in implantable glucose monitoring sensors. Diabetic retinopathy is a significant complication of diabetes, characterized by damage to the retinal blood vessels, which can lead to vision loss if not managed effectively. The integration of CNTs into glucose sensors offers a promising solution due to their unique electrical, mechanical, and thermal properties. These nanomaterials are highly conductive, allowing for rapid and sensitive detection of glucose levels in the bloodstream. The ability to monitor blood glucose levels in real-time is crucial for individuals with diabetes, as it enables timely interventions to prevent the progression of diabetic retinopathy. CNT-based sensors can be designed to be minimally invasive, enhancing patient compliance and comfort while providing continuous monitoring capabilities.

Moreover, the biocompatibility of CNTs is a significant advantage, as it reduces the risk of adverse reactions when implanted in the body. The use of CNTs in sensor technology not only improves the sensitivity and specificity of glucose detection but also facilitates the development of smart devices that can communicate with mobile applications. These applications can provide patients and healthcare providers with valuable insights into glucose trends, enabling more personalized and effective management of diabetes. Additionally, the incorporation of CNTs in these sensors allows for the possibility of integrating other functionalities, such as drug delivery systems that can release therapeutic agents in response to elevated glucose levels, thereby addressing both monitoring and treatment in a single platform.

Recent advancements in the synthesis of CNTs have also focused on enhancing their properties to improve their performance in biomedical applications. Techniques such as functionalization and surface modification have been employed to increase the selectivity of glucose sensors, allowing for the differentiation between glucose and other interfering substances in the bloodstream. This specificity is crucial for the accuracy of readings, as false positives or negatives can lead to inappropriate treatment decisions. Furthermore, the miniaturization of CNT-based sensors has paved the way for the development of wearable technologies, which can provide continuous glucose monitoring without the need for frequent blood draws, a common pain point for diabetic patients.

In summary, the application of carbon nanotubes in implantable glucose monitoring sensors represents a significant advancement in the management of diabetic retinopathy. By providing real-time data on glucose levels, these sensors empower patients to take control of their condition and reduce the risk of complications associated with diabetes. The ongoing research in the field of CNTs and their integration into smart healthcare solutions holds great promise for improving the quality of life for individuals suffering from diabetes and its related ocular complications. As the technology continues to evolve, it is essential to address the clinical translation challenges and ensure that these innovative solutions are accessible and effective in real-world settings.37,38

Design Concepts of Responsive Nanomaterials

The rational design of stimuli-responsive nanomaterials represents a cornerstone of advanced ophthalmic drug delivery and therapeutic innovation, engineered to trigger site-specific, spatiotemporally controlled drug release in response to endogenous or exogenous ocular microenvironmental cues. These smart nanosystems overcome the limitations of passive nanocarriers by precisely regulating therapeutic payload release, maximizing local drug bioavailability at pathological ocular sites (eg, inflamed tissues, ischemic retinas) while minimizing off-target systemic exposure and frequent administration—critical advantages for treating chronic or posterior segment ocular diseases where sustained, targeted therapy is essential. Similarly, temperature-sensitive nanocarriers can be designed to release their payloads in response to localized hyperthermia, a technique often used in conjunction with photothermal therapy. This dual approach not only improves drug localization but also enhances the overall therapeutic effect by ensuring that drugs are released precisely when and where they are needed.39 Light-responsive nanomaterials, on the other hand, utilize photochemical reactions to trigger drug release upon exposure to specific wavelengths of light, providing an additional layer of control over the timing and location of drug delivery.40 The integration of these responsive mechanisms into nanomaterials represents a significant advancement in the field of targeted therapy, particularly in the treatment of ocular diseases where precision is crucial.

In addition to the stimuli-responsive characteristics, the intelligent release mechanisms of these nanomaterials are noteworthy. Smart nanocarriers can be designed to utilize both endogenous and exogenous stimuli for controlled drug release. For example, the incorporation of enzymes that are overexpressed in certain pathological conditions can trigger the release of therapeutic agents in a site-specific manner, enhancing the effectiveness of the treatment while minimizing off-target effects.41 This strategy is particularly relevant in the context of ocular drug delivery, where the unique anatomical and physiological barriers of the eye necessitate innovative approaches to ensure drug bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, the design of multi-stimuli responsive systems, which can react to various environmental cues simultaneously, is gaining traction. Such systems can provide a more sophisticated response to the dynamic changes in the biological environment, allowing for tailored therapeutic interventions that adapt to the specific needs of the patient.42 The ongoing research in this area highlights the potential for developing highly specialized nanocarriers that not only deliver drugs more effectively but also provide real-time feedback on the therapeutic process, thereby paving the way for personalized medicine in ophthalmology.

In ophthalmic practice, these design concepts have been translated into promising preclinical formulations: pH-responsive lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticles for dry eye disease, enzyme-responsive hydrogels for glaucoma filtration surgery anti-scarring therapy, and NIR light-responsive nanocarriers for targeted photothermal-chemotherapy of ocular neovascularization. Mechanisms by Which Nanomaterials Enhance Drug Bioavailability

The enhancement of drug bioavailability through nanomaterials is primarily attributed to their unique properties, that facilitate controlled release, targeted delivery, and improved drug stability.44 Targeted delivery is achieved through surface modifications of nanocarriers that recognize and bind to specific cell receptors, ensuring drugs are released at the desired site.45 Controlled release mechanisms allow for the gradual release of drugs over time; this gradual release not only maintains therapeutic levels in the bloodstream but also minimizes potential side effects associated with peak drug concentrations. For instance, nanocarriers can be engineered to respond to specific stimuli, such as pH changes or temperature variations.46 This design ensures that drug release is optimized for the target site. This is particularly important in ocular applications where maintaining therapeutic concentrations within the eye is crucial for effective treatment. Additionally, the use of nanomaterials as drug carriers significantly enhances drug stability, protecting sensitive compounds from chemical degradation through hydrolysis or oxidation caused by environmental factors such as light, oxygen, and moisture.47 This drug stability is vital for maintaining the efficacy of drugs, especially those that are prone to chemical degradation through hydrolysis or oxidation.48 Furthermore, the small size of nanocarriers allows for improved penetration through biological barriers, such as the corneal epithelium in the eye, thereby increasing the bioavailability of drugs that would otherwise have limited absorption due to their relatively large molecular size or high hydrophilicity.49,50

Moreover, nanocarriers can modulate the pharmacokinetics of drugs, allowing for better absorption and distribution within the target tissues.51 For example, polysaccharide-based nanocarriers have been shown to enhance the solubility and stability of poorly water-soluble drugs, which is a common challenge in ocular drug delivery.52 By encapsulating these drugs within nanocarriers, researchers have been able to achieve higher drug concentrations at the target site of action, thus improving therapeutic outcomes.53 This modulation of drug metabolism and pharmacokinetics benefits not only ocular applications but also various therapeutic areas. These include cancer treatment and chronic inflammatory diseases. Furthermore, the ability of nanocarriers to evade the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), enhances their efficacy by prolonging circulation time and increasing the likelihood of reaching the target site.54,55

In summary, nanomaterials enhance drug bioavailability through multiple mechanisms, including controlled release, targeted delivery, and improved stability. These advancements are crucial for overcoming the limitations of conventional drug delivery systems, particularly in challenging environments like the eye, where barriers to drug penetration exist. As research continues to evolve, the integration of nanotechnology into drug delivery systems promises to revolutionize therapeutic strategies, leading to improved patient outcomes and more effective treatments for a variety of diseases.

Preparation Techniques of Nanomaterials

The synthesis of nanomaterials for ophthalmic applications employs various techniques tailored to achieve desired size, shape, and surface properties. Liposomes are typically prepared via thin film hydration, where lipids are dissolved in organic solvent, dried to form a thin film, and then hydrated with aqueous buffer, followed by extrusion to obtain uniform size. Polymeric nanoparticles can be synthesized using methods such as emulsification-solvent evaporation, which involves emulsifying a polymer solution in an aqueous phase followed by solvent removal, or nanoprecipitation, which relies on rapid diffusion of a polymer solution into a non-solvent. Dendrimers are synthesized through iterative divergent or convergent steps, allowing precise control over molecular weight and surface functionality. Gold nanoparticles are often produced by chemical reduction of gold salts (eg, citrate reduction of HAuCl4), yielding particles with tunable size. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles are synthesized via sol-gel processes using templates like surfactants to create porous structures. Carbon nanotubes are fabricated by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) or arc discharge, with subsequent functionalization for biomedical use. Each method influences the final properties of nanocarriers, such as drug loading capacity, release kinetics, and biocompatibility.

Characterization Techniques for Nanomaterials

To ensure the quality and performance of nanomaterials, comprehensive characterization is essential. Particle size and distribution are commonly measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS), which provides hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index. Surface charge is determined by zeta potential analysis, influencing stability and cellular interaction. Morphology is visualized through transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM), offering direct insight into size, shape, and structure. Drug loading and encapsulation efficiency are typically assessed by UV-Vis spectroscopy or high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) after separating unloaded drug. In vitro release profiles are monitored using dialysis or diffusion methods under physiological conditions. Stability is evaluated by monitoring changes in size, charge, and drug content over time under storage or physiological conditions. Biocompatibility is assessed via cytotoxicity assays (eg, MTT) on ocular cell lines. These characterization methods ensure reproducibility and help correlate nanomaterial properties with biological performance.

Delivery System Breakthrough Strategy

Ocular Biological Barriers to Drug Delivery

Effective drug delivery to ocular tissues is hindered by multiple anatomical and physiological barriers. The corneal barrier, consisting of epithelium, stroma, and endothelium, restricts drug penetration due to the lipophilic corneal epithelium with tight junctions, which limits hydrophilic drug passage, while the stroma impedes lipophilic drugs, resulting in topical bioavailability of less than 5%. The conjunctival barrier covers the sclera and contains blood and lymphatic vessels, leading to drug drainage into systemic circulation. The blood-aqueous barrier, formed by tight junctions in the non-pigmented ciliary epithelium and iris vessels, regulates entry into the anterior chamber. The blood-retinal barrier (BRB), comprising inner (retinal capillary endothelial cells) and outer (retinal pigment epithelium) components, strictly controls molecular exchange between blood and retina, significantly impeding drug delivery to the posterior segment. Additionally, rapid tear turnover and nasolacrimal drainage remove instilled drugs from the ocular surface. These barriers necessitate advanced delivery strategies, such as nanocarriers, to enhance drug penetration and retention.

Dynamic/Static Barrier Penetration Mechanism

Nanocarriers have emerged as a pivotal innovation in enhancing ocular drug delivery, particularly through mechanisms that increase the retention time of drugs on the cornea and facilitate transcellular transport. This is particularly significant given the anatomical and physiological barriers presented by the eye, such as the corneal epithelium and the blood-retinal barrier, which traditionally limit the bioavailability of therapeutic agents to less than 5% with conventional delivery methods.36 Various nanomaterials, including nanocrystals and nanofibers, have been developed to improve drug retention on the corneal surface, thereby prolonging the therapeutic effect. For instance, nanocrystals can enhance the solubility and stability of hydrophobic drugs, allowing for sustained release and increased contact time with the ocular surface.14 Similarly, nanofibers can be engineered to create a matrix that holds the drug in place, allowing for a gradual release into the ocular tissues.

Moreover, the transcellular transport facilitated by these nanocarriers is crucial for overcoming the corneal barrier. Molecular dynamics simulations have provided insights into how nanomaterials interact with phospholipid membranes, revealing that certain nanostructures can penetrate lipid bilayers more effectively than others.56 For example, graphene nanosheets have shown a capacity to penetrate phospholipid membranes, primarily through edge-oriented interactions, which highlights the importance of nanomaterial design in optimizing drug delivery systems.57 This understanding of membrane dynamics is essential for developing nanocarriers that can effectively traverse cellular barriers and deliver therapeutic agents directly to targeted sites within the eye.

The incorporation of nucleic acid-functionalized metallic nanoparticles (N-MNPs) represents another innovative approach to enhancing drug delivery in ocular applications. These nanoparticles exhibit unique properties such as resistance to enzymatic degradation and the ability to penetrate physiological barriers, which are critical for effective ocular therapy.56 By leveraging the programmable self-assembly and dynamic structure-function transformation capabilities of N-MNPs, researchers can create tailored delivery systems that respond to specific biological environments, thereby improving drug bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy.

In addition to enhancing drug retention and transcellular transport, the use of mucoadhesive and mucus-penetrating nanoparticles has been explored for ocular drug delivery. These nanoparticles are designed to interact favorably with the mucus layer, either by adhering to it to prolong drug contact time or by penetrating through it to reach deeper tissues.58 The balance between mucoadhesion and mucus penetration is critical; while strong mucoadhesion may enhance retention, excessive adhesion could hinder the movement of the drug through the mucus layer. This dual functionality is essential for optimizing drug delivery systems in the eye, where achieving the right balance can significantly enhance therapeutic outcomes.

Despite the promising advancements in nanocarrier technology, challenges remain in the clinical translation of these systems. Issues such as manufacturing costs, potential toxicity, and the complexity of biological interactions must be addressed to fully realize the potential of nanomedicine in ophthalmology.36 Future research should focus on refining nanocarrier designs to enhance their biocompatibility and targeting specificity, ultimately leading to more effective and safer ocular therapies. As the field of nanomedicine continues to evolve, the integration of innovative nanocarriers into clinical practice holds the potential to revolutionize the management of various ocular diseases, offering more effective and personalized treatment options for patients. Intelligent Delivery Systems

Stimuli-responsive hydrogels have emerged as a transformative technology in the field of ophthalmology, particularly for the targeted and controlled release of therapeutic agents. These hydrogels are designed to respond dynamically to specific physiological stimuli such as pH and temperature, which are critical in the context of ocular drug delivery. The ability of these hydrogels to swell or contract in response to environmental changes allows for the precise modulation of drug release profiles, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic side effects. For instance, a hydrogel that swells in response to an increase in temperature can release its drug payload more rapidly in the warmer environment of the eye, ensuring that medications are delivered effectively when they are most needed.38 Additionally, pH-sensitive hydrogels can be tailored to release drugs at specific sites within the ocular environment, such as the acidic environment of inflamed tissues, thereby providing a localized therapeutic effect. This targeted approach not only improves drug bioavailability but also reduces the frequency of administration, which is particularly beneficial for patients suffering from chronic ocular conditions. Furthermore, the incorporation of nanocarriers into these hydrogels can further enhance their performance by improving drug solubility and stability, as well as facilitating deeper penetration into ocular tissues.59 As research continues to advance in this area, the potential for stimuli-responsive hydrogels to revolutionize the management of ocular diseases becomes increasingly evident, paving the way for more effective and patient-friendly treatment options.

Nanowafers represent a novel and promising approach to drug delivery in the treatment of glaucoma, a condition characterized by elevated intraocular pressure that can lead to irreversible vision loss. These thin, disc-shaped devices can be easily placed on the conjunctiva, where they adhere securely and provide a sustained release of therapeutic agents over an extended period. The design of nanowafers allows for the incorporation of various drugs, including those that lower intraocular pressure, such as prostaglandin analogs and beta-blockers, which are critical in glaucoma management.60 The sustained release mechanism of nanowafers not only enhances patient compliance by reducing the frequency of dosing but also ensures that therapeutic concentrations are maintained within the ocular tissues for prolonged periods. This is particularly important in glaucoma treatment, where consistent intraocular pressure control is essential to prevent optic nerve damage. Moreover, the biocompatibility of the materials used in nanowafers minimizes the risk of adverse reactions, making them a safe option for chronic use.61 Recent studies have demonstrated that nanowafers can significantly improve the bioavailability of drugs compared to traditional eye drops, which often suffer from rapid drainage and limited ocular retention.62 As the field of nanotechnology continues to evolve, the integration of nanowafers into clinical practice could represent a significant advancement in the management of glaucoma, providing patients with a more effective and convenient treatment modality.

Overcoming Biological Barriers and Nanodelivery Systems

The human eye is protected by several biological barriers, notably the corneal barrier and the blood-retinal barrier, which play crucial roles in maintaining ocular health, but also pose significant challenges for drug delivery in ophthalmology.26 The corneal barrier, primarily composed of stratified epithelial cells, acts as the first line of defense against exogenous substances such as therapeutic agents. This barrier is characterized by its hydrophobic outer layer, which restricts the penetration of hydrophilic drugs, thereby limiting their bioavailability within the eye.49 Similarly, the blood-retinal barrier (BRB) is formed by tight junctions among retinal endothelial cells, preventing the passage of large molecules and pathogens while allowing the selective transport of essential nutrients.63 This selective permeability is essential for maintaining the retinal microenvironment, but it complicates the delivery of therapeutic agents for conditions such as glaucoma and diabetic retinopathy.64 Consequently, overcoming the barriers to drug delivery in the eye is critical for enhancing the efficacy of ophthalmic treatments, and innovative strategies are required to facilitate drug penetration and targeting.

Nanomaterials have emerged as promising solutions to enhance drug delivery across these biological barriers due to their unique physicochemical properties and engineerability for specific applications.65 Chemical or physical surface modifications of nanocarriers can significantly improve their interaction with biological tissues, promoting cellular uptake and targeted delivery. For instance, incorporating targeting ligands such as antibodies or peptides onto nanoparticle surfaces can facilitate receptor-mediated endocytosis, thereby enhancing the precise and efficient delivery of therapeutic agents to the desired ocular tissues.66 Furthermore, the size and shape of nanomaterials can be tailored to optimize their permeability through the corneal and retinal barriers, which means that smaller nanoparticles, typically in the range of 1–100 nm, can diffuse more easily through these barriers compared to larger particles.67 Additionally, the use of stimuli-responsive nanodelivery systems, which can release their therapeutic payloads in response to specific environmental triggers such as pH changes or temperature variations, represents a significant advancement in achieving controlled and targeted drug delivery.64

Moreover, the biocompatibility of nanomaterials is a critical factor in their design, as it influences their safety and effectiveness in clinical applications. This biocompatibility is exemplified by biodegradable nanocarriers, such as liposomes and polymeric nanoparticles, which can minimize the risk of toxicity and adverse reactions, while providing sustained release of drugs at the target site.49 This is particularly important in the context of chronic ocular diseases that require long-term treatment regimens, such as glaucoma. Maintaining therapeutic drug concentrations over extended periods can improve patient adherence and treatment outcomes.68 Furthermore, the integration of nanotechnology into ophthalmic drug formulations not only enhances the bioavailability of therapeutic agents but also, through advanced imaging techniques and biosensors, enables improved diagnosis and monitoring of ocular diseases.66

In conclusion, innovative nanodelivery systems show great promise in overcoming the eye’s biological barriers and improving treatment efficacy for various ocular diseases. By leveraging the unique properties of nanomaterials—such as liposomes, dendrimers, and polymeric nanoparticles—researchers can create targeted, efficient, and safe drug delivery systems that address the limitations of conventional ophthalmic therapies, including poor drug bioavailability and systemic side effects. As the field of nanomedicine continues to evolve, ongoing research will be crucial in optimizing these systems for clinical application by addressing challenges like stability, controlled release, and biocompatibility, ultimately improving specific patient outcomes such as enhanced drug absorption and reduced adverse effects in ophthalmology.

Design and Advantages of Nano Anti-Inflammatory Drug Delivery Systems

The design of nano anti-inflammatory drug delivery systems is fundamentally influenced by the biocompatibility and targeting capabilities of nanomaterials, which are crucial for effective inflammation control. Biocompatibility ensures that the nanomaterials do not elicit adverse immune responses, allowing for prolonged circulation and enhanced therapeutic efficacy.69 For instance, recent studies have highlighted the use of metal-based nanoparticles, such as ceria and zinc oxide, which possess intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties and can be engineered to target specific inflammatory sites while minimizing systemic toxicity.70 These nanoparticles can be functionalized with targeting ligands that selectively bind to inflammatory markers, thereby enhancing their accumulation at sites of inflammation, such as joints in rheumatoid arthritis or inflamed intestinal tissues in inflammatory bowel disease.71 This targeted approach not only increases the local concentration of the anti-inflammatory agents but also reduces off-target effects, which are often a significant concern with conventional systemic therapies. Moreover, the ability to modulate the surface properties of these nanoparticles further enhances their interactions with immune cells, such as macrophages, facilitating a more effective therapeutic response by promoting the polarization of macrophages towards the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype.72

In addition to biocompatibility and targeting, the development of controlled release systems is a pivotal aspect of nano drug delivery systems that achieves sustained anti-inflammatory effects while minimizing systemic side effects. Controlled release mechanisms can be designed to respond to specific physiological triggers, such as pH changes in inflamed tissues, ensuring that the anti-inflammatory agents are released precisely when and where they are needed.73 For example, pH-sensitive nanoparticles have been developed that release their drug payload in response to the acidic microenvironment characteristic of inflamed tissues, thus providing a localized therapeutic effect while reducing the risk of systemic exposure.74 Furthermore, the encapsulation of anti-inflammatory drugs within biodegradable polymeric nanoparticles allows for a gradual release profile that can maintain therapeutic drug levels over extended periods, enhancing patient compliance and treatment outcomes.75 Such systems not only improve the pharmacokinetics of the drugs but also help in achieving a more stable therapeutic effect, which is particularly beneficial in chronic inflammatory conditions where long-term management is often required.

In summary, the integration of biocompatible materials with advanced targeting strategies and controlled release mechanisms in nano anti-inflammatory drug delivery systems presents a promising avenue for enhancing the treatment of inflammatory diseases. By leveraging the unique properties of nanomaterials, these systems can significantly improve the efficacy and safety of anti-inflammatory therapies, addressing the limitations of traditional drug delivery methods and paving the way for more effective management of chronic inflammatory conditions.70,76

Innovations in Nanosenors and Imaging Technologies

The integration of nanomaterials into ophthalmic imaging and sensing technologies has significantly enhanced diagnostic capabilities, particularly through advancements in fluorescence imaging and optical coherence tomography (OCT).85 Nanomaterials, such as quantum dots and carbon nanotubes, exhibit unique optical properties that allow for improved resolution and sensitivity in imaging applications.86 For instance, quantum dots can provide brighter and more stable fluorescence compared to traditional organic dyes, enabling clearer visualization of ocular structures and pathologies at the cellular level.87 The application of these nanomaterials in OCT has also shown promise, as they can enhance the contrast of images, facilitating the early detection of diseases such as glaucoma and diabetic retinopathy.88 By improving the signal-to-noise ratio, nanomaterials enable the visualization of subtle changes in retinal layers that would otherwise go undetected, thereby allowing for timely intervention and better patient outcomes.38 Furthermore, the ability to functionalize nanomaterials with specific ligands can lead to targeted imaging, where the nanomaterials bind to particular biomarkers associated with ocular diseases, thus providing not only structural but also functional information about the eye.89 This innovative approach to imaging, combining the high specificity of nanomaterials with advanced imaging techniques, represents a significant leap forward in the field of ophthalmology.

In addition to enhancing imaging capabilities, nanoparticles are increasingly being utilized as contrast agents to improve diagnostic sensitivity.90 Traditional contrast agents often lack specificity and can lead to adverse reactions; however, the use of nanoparticles offers a more targeted approach.91 For example, gold nanoparticles have been employed as contrast agents in various imaging modalities, including ultrasound and computed tomography (CT), due to their high X-ray attenuation properties.92 Their unique size and surface properties allow for the modification with targeting moieties that can bind to specific cells or tissues, enhancing the contrast in imaging studies.93 This targeted approach not only improves the accuracy of diagnoses but also reduces the risk of side effects associated with conventional contrast agents. Applications of Nanotechnology in Biomarker Detection

Nanotechnology has emerged as a transformative force in the field of biomarker detection, significantly enhancing the specificity and accuracy of diagnostic methods. For instance, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) and quantum dots are being utilized to create highly sensitive biosensors that can detect biomolecules at very low concentrations, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy.97,98 These nanomaterials can be engineered to bind specifically to target biomarkers, enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio and allowing for the detection of disease-related molecules even in the presence of complex biological fluids.99 The integration of nanotechnology into biomarker detection not only increases sensitivity but also allows for multiplexing capabilities, enabling the simultaneous detection of multiple biomarkers, which is crucial for comprehensive disease profiling.100 This advancement is particularly relevant in oncology, where the identification of multiple tumor markers can provide insights into tumor behavior and guide therapeutic decisions.101,102

Furthermore, the development of multimodal diagnostic platforms that leverage nanotechnology is revolutionizing the landscape of biomarker detection. These platforms combine various detection modalities, such as optical, electrochemical, and magnetic sensing, into a single device, enhancing diagnostic capabilities and providing a more holistic view of patient health. For example, a recent study highlighted the use of nanotechnology-based biosensors that integrate both electrochemical and optical detection methods for the real-time monitoring of cancer biomarkers.103 Such multimodal approaches not only improve the accuracy of biomarker detection but also facilitate rapid diagnosis, which is essential in acute clinical settings. The ability to detect biomarkers in real-time using these advanced platforms can significantly influence treatment decisions and patient outcomes, particularly in diseases where early intervention is critical, such as cancer and cardiovascular diseases.104,105

Moreover, the application of nanotechnology in biomarker detection extends beyond traditional assays to include innovative approaches such as liquid biopsies, which analyze circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and extracellular vesicles (EVs) for non-invasive disease monitoring. Nanomaterials are instrumental in isolating and detecting these biomarkers, providing insights into tumor dynamics and treatment responses without the need for invasive procedures.98,106 This non-invasive approach is particularly advantageous for patients who may be reluctant to undergo more invasive diagnostic procedures, thereby improving patient compliance and facilitating regular monitoring of disease progression or remission.107

In conclusion, the application of nanotechnology in biomarker detection is a rapidly evolving field that promises to enhance diagnostic specificity and accuracy significantly. By integrating advanced nanomaterials into diagnostic platforms, researchers are paving the way for more sensitive, rapid, and multiplexed detection methods that can transform patient care. The ongoing research and development in this domain hold great potential for improving early disease detection, monitoring therapeutic responses, and ultimately enhancing patient outcomes across various medical fields. Applications of Natural Nanomaterials in Ocular Drug Delivery

The application of natural nanomaterials in ocular drug delivery has garnered significant attention due to their unique properties and multifunctional capabilities. Among these, metal nanomaterials, carbon quantum dots, and polymer-based nanomaterials stand out as promising candidates. Metal nanomaterials, such as silver and gold nanoparticles, are known for their biocompatibility and ability to enhance the solubility and stability of therapeutic agents, making them ideal for ocular applications.130 For instance, silver nanoparticles have been utilized as antimicrobial agents to combat infections in ocular surgeries, while gold nanoparticles have shown potential in enhancing the bioavailability of drugs administered to the eye.38 Carbon quantum dots, on the other hand, are recognized for their excellent optical properties and low toxicity, which enable their use as imaging agents in ocular diagnostics. These quantum dots can be conjugated with targeting ligands to facilitate specific delivery to diseased tissues, providing a dual function of imaging and therapy.37 Furthermore, polymer-based nanomaterials, particularly those derived from natural sources, offer a versatile platform for drug delivery. These polymers can be engineered to respond to physiological triggers, such as pH or temperature, allowing for controlled and targeted release of therapeutic agents in the ocular environment. Natural deep eutectic systems (NADES), for example, have been explored for their ability to enhance drug solubility and permeability, thereby improving the efficacy of ocular therapeutics.131

The multifunctional nature of these natural nanomaterials extends beyond drug delivery to include their roles as imaging agents and targeting ligands. In the realm of ocular imaging, metal nanomaterials have been utilized to enhance contrast in imaging techniques, allowing for better visualization of ocular structures and pathologies. For instance, gold nanoparticles can be employed in optical coherence tomography (OCT) to improve the imaging of retinal layers, aiding in the diagnosis of conditions such as diabetic retinopathy.132 Similarly, carbon quantum dots can be used in fluorescence imaging, providing real-time feedback on the distribution and efficacy of ocular drugs.133 Additionally, the incorporation of targeting ligands into these nanomaterials enhances their specificity for diseased tissues, thereby minimizing systemic side effects and improving therapeutic outcomes. For example, conjugating nanoparticles with antibodies or peptides that target specific receptors on ocular cells can facilitate the selective delivery of drugs to areas affected by conditions like glaucoma or age-related macular degeneration.134

In conclusion, the integration of natural nanomaterials into ocular drug delivery systems represents a significant advancement in the field of ophthalmology. The unique properties of metal nanomaterials, carbon quantum dots, and polymer-based systems not only enhance drug delivery efficiency but also provide additional functionalities such as imaging and targeted therapy. As research continues to evolve, the potential for these materials to transform ocular therapeutics is immense, paving the way for innovative approaches to treat various ocular diseases effectively. Future studies should focus on optimizing the synthesis and functionalization of these nanomaterials to maximize their therapeutic efficacy while ensuring safety in clinical applications.

Current Applications of Stem Cell Therapy in Ophthalmology

The application of stem cell therapy in ophthalmology has made significant strides, particularly in treating retinal degenerative diseases and corneal injuries. Retinal degenerative diseases, such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and retinitis pigmentosa (RP), are characterized by the progressive loss of photoreceptors and retinal pigment epithelium, leading to irreversible vision loss.135 Recent advancements in stem cell therapy have shown promise in regenerating these damaged retinal cells. For instance, studies have demonstrated that stem cells derived from various sources, including induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), can differentiate into retinal cells and secrete neuroprotective factors that promote retinal cell survival and regeneration.136,137 Clinical trials have indicated improvements in visual acuity and retinal structure in patients receiving stem cell-based interventions, underscoring the potential of these therapies to restore vision in individuals suffering from retinal degenerative diseases.138,139

In addition to retinal diseases, stem cell therapy has also been explored for corneal injuries, particularly those resulting from chemical burns or limbal stem cell deficiency.140 The cornea has a unique regenerative capacity, primarily due to the presence of limbal epithelial stem cells (LESCs) located in the limbus.141 However, injuries can lead to the depletion of these stem cells, resulting in corneal opacity and vision impairment. Recent studies have focused on transplanting LESCs or using adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) to promote corneal healing and regeneration.94,142 These approaches have demonstrated efficacy in restoring corneal transparency and improving visual outcomes in patients with severe corneal damage. Furthermore, the immunomodulatory properties of stem cells play a crucial role in reducing inflammation and promoting tissue repair, which is particularly beneficial in managing ocular surface disorders.143,144

The immunomodulatory and regenerative mechanisms of stem cells are pivotal in their therapeutic applications. Stem cells can modulate the immune response through the secretion of various cytokines and growth factors, which can help mitigate inflammation and promote healing in the ocular environment.145 For example, MSCs have been shown to secrete exosomes containing bioactive molecules that facilitate tissue repair and reduce inflammation in ocular diseases.146,147 This immunomodulatory effect is particularly relevant in the context of ocular graft-versus-host disease (oGVHD), where stem cell therapy can help alleviate symptoms and improve ocular surface health in patients undergoing hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.83,148

Despite the promising outcomes associated with stem cell therapies, several challenges remain, including the standardization of protocols, long-term safety concerns, and the need for efficient delivery methods to target ocular tissues effectively. Ongoing research is focused on optimizing these therapies to enhance their efficacy and safety profiles, paving the way for their integration into routine clinical practice for the management of various ocular diseases.149,150 The future of stem cell therapy in ophthalmology looks promising, with the potential to revolutionize the treatment landscape for patients suffering from debilitating eye conditions.

This dynamic approach ensures that stem cell therapies are more effective and tailored to the unique needs of each patient, ultimately leading to better clinical outcomes in the management of ocular diseases and beyond.157

In addition to optimizing delivery pathways, machine learning plays a crucial role in predicting the efficacy of stem cell therapies and managing associated risks. By analyzing historical data from previous clinical trials and patient outcomes, AI can identify patterns that may indicate the likelihood of treatment success or failure. This predictive capability is particularly valuable in the context of stem cell therapy, where patient responses can vary significantly based on numerous factors, including the type of stem cells used, the method of delivery, and the underlying pathology. Machine learning algorithms can also assess potential risks associated with stem cell treatments, such as immune rejection or tumorigenesis, by analyzing genetic and molecular data from patients.158 By identifying high-risk patients early, clinicians can modify treatment strategies to mitigate these risks, enhancing patient safety. Additionally, AI can facilitate the development of risk stratification models that guide clinicians in selecting the most appropriate candidates for stem cell therapy, ensuring that resources are allocated effectively and that patients receive the most suitable interventions for their conditions. Overall, the incorporation of AI into stem cell therapy not only enhances the precision of treatment delivery but also significantly improves the ability to predict outcomes and manage risks, paving the way for more effective and safer therapeutic strategies in regenerative medicine.159

Pathological Mechanisms and Treatment Challenges of Retinal Diseases

Retinal diseases, particularly age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and diabetic retinopathy (DR), represent significant challenges in ophthalmology due to their complex pathological mechanisms and the difficulties associated with effective treatment. AMD is characterized by progressive degeneration of the retinal pigment epithelium and photoreceptors, leading to central vision loss, primarily in the elderly population.160 The pathogenesis of AMD involves a combination of genetic predisposition, oxidative stress, and inflammation, which contribute to the formation of drusen and the eventual atrophy of retinal cells.161 On the other hand, diabetic retinopathy arises from chronic hyperglycemia, which induces retinal vascular damage, resulting in microaneurysms, retinal hemorrhages, and neovascularization. This condition is a leading cause of blindness among working-age adults and is closely linked to the duration and control of diabetes.162 The intricate interplay of these factors complicates the development of effective therapeutic strategies, as current treatments primarily focus on managing symptoms rather than addressing the underlying causes of these diseases.

One of the major hurdles in the treatment of retinal diseases is the challenge of drug delivery across the blood-retinal barrier (BRB).63 The BRB is a specialized structure that regulates the passage of substances between the bloodstream and the retina, thereby protecting the neural tissue from harmful agents while maintaining the necessary environment for retinal function. However, this barrier also poses a significant obstacle for therapeutic agents, limiting their efficacy in treating retinal diseases.163 Traditional systemic therapies often fail to achieve therapeutic concentrations in the retina, necessitating the exploration of alternative delivery methods. Innovative approaches, such as intravitreal injections and the use of nanomaterials for targeted drug delivery, are being investigated to enhance the bioavailability of therapeutic agents directly at the site of action.164 Additionally, the development of retinal organoids from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) has emerged as a promising strategy for modeling retinal diseases and screening potential therapeutics, offering a platform for personalized medicine in the treatment of inherited retinal diseases.165

Despite these advancements, the complexity of retinal diseases, coupled with the limitations of current treatment modalities, underscores the urgent need for continued research and innovation. Understanding the pathological mechanisms underlying AMD and DR is critical for the development of effective therapies. Furthermore, addressing the challenges associated with drug delivery across the BRB will be essential in translating laboratory findings into clinical practice. As research progresses, it is hoped that novel therapeutic strategies will emerge that not only mitigate the symptoms of retinal diseases but also target their underlying causes, ultimately improving outcomes for patients suffering from these debilitating conditions.

Targeting Retinal Cells with Nanocarriers

The application of nanocarriers in targeting retinal cells has emerged as a revolutionary approach in ophthalmic drug delivery, particularly in addressing the challenges posed by the blood-retinal barrier (BRB) and the complex anatomy of the eye.61 One of the primary strategies involves surface modification of nanocarriers to achieve specific recognition and binding to retinal cells. This is critical because the BRB acts as a formidable barrier, restricting the passage of therapeutic agents to the retina. Recent studies have highlighted the efficacy of various surface-functionalized nanocarriers, such as dendritic nanocarriers and liposomes, which have been engineered to enhance their interaction with retinal cells.166 For instance, the incorporation of specific ligands or antibodies that can selectively bind to receptors expressed on retinal cells has shown to significantly improve the targeting efficiency of these nanocarriers.167 This specificity not only enhances the bioavailability of the drug at the target site but also minimizes off-target effects, which are common with systemic drug administration. Furthermore, the use of biodegradable materials in the construction of these nanocarriers ensures that they can safely degrade within the ocular environment, reducing the risk of toxicity and adverse reactions.168 By employing such targeted delivery systems, researchers have demonstrated improved therapeutic outcomes in conditions like diabetic retinopathy and age-related macular degeneration, where precise drug delivery is crucial for effective treatment.169–171

In addition to surface modification, the development of controlled release systems has been pivotal in extending the duration of drug action within the retina. Traditional drug delivery methods often require frequent dosing due to rapid clearance from the ocular surface, which can lead to suboptimal therapeutic outcomes.36 Controlled release nanocarriers, such as cubic liquid crystalline nanoparticles and polymeric micelles, have been designed to provide sustained release of therapeutic agents over extended periods.172 This is particularly beneficial for chronic retinal diseases where prolonged therapeutic action is necessary. For example, studies have shown that triamcinolone acetonide-loaded cubic liquid crystalline nanoparticles can significantly reduce inflammatory markers and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) levels in diabetic retinopathy models, demonstrating their potential to not only deliver drugs effectively but also to modulate the underlying disease pathology.61,173 The sustained release profile of these nanocarriers allows for a more stable therapeutic effect, reducing the need for frequent administration and improving patient compliance. Moreover, the incorporation of stimuli-responsive mechanisms, such as pH or temperature sensitivity, into these nanocarriers can further enhance their therapeutic efficacy by allowing for the release of the drug in response to specific pathological conditions within the retina. This innovative approach holds promise for the future of retinal therapies, as it combines targeted delivery with controlled release, addressing both the challenges of drug bioavailability and the need for sustained therapeutic action.174,175

In summary, the strategic targeting of retinal cells using surface-modified nanocarriers, coupled with controlled release systems, represents a significant advancement in the field of ocular drug delivery. These innovative approaches not only enhance the specificity and efficacy of therapeutic agents but also pave the way for improved management of retinal diseases, ultimately contributing to better patient outcomes and quality of life. As research continues to evolve in this area, the potential for nanocarriers to transform the treatment landscape for retinal disorders becomes increasingly evident, highlighting the importance of ongoing exploration and development in this promising field.

Applications of Smart Nanomaterials in Complex Ocular Diseases

The integration of smart nanomaterials into the treatment of complex ocular diseases represents a significant advancement in ophthalmology, particularly through the development of multifunctional nanosystems that enable combined therapeutic approaches. One notable example is the use of nanozyme-based eye drops designed to alleviate dry eye disease (DED). These eye drops utilize n-FeZIF-8 nanozymes, which exhibit dual reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging abilities and prolonged corneal retention, effectively addressing the oxidative stress that contributes to DED.176 The unique properties of these nanomaterials, including their ability to physically adhere to the ocular surface and interact dynamically with mucins, enhance their effectiveness in delivering therapeutic agents directly to the target site.176 This multifunctionality not only improves the bioavailability of the active ingredients but also minimizes the need for frequent administration, thus improving patient compliance. Furthermore, the incorporation of natural deep eutectic systems (NADES) in drug formulations has shown promise in enhancing drug delivery to both anterior and posterior segments of the eye, overcoming barriers that typically hinder effective treatment.131 The versatility of these smart nanomaterials paves the way for innovative therapeutic strategies that can be tailored to the specific needs of patients suffering from various ocular conditions, thereby improving clinical outcomes.

In addition to combined therapeutic systems, the construction of intelligent diagnostic and therapeutic platforms is another critical application of smart nanomaterials in ophthalmology. For instance, the development of liquid metal-based microfluidic nanoplasmonic platforms has revolutionized point-of-care (POC) diagnostics, enabling rapid and sensitive detection of biomarkers associated with ocular diseases.177 These platforms utilize the unique properties of liquid metals to create nanostructures that respond to biological interactions, providing real-time feedback on the presence of disease markers. Such advancements not only enhance the accuracy of diagnostics but also facilitate timely interventions, which are crucial in managing conditions like diabetic retinopathy and age-related macular degeneration. Moreover, the incorporation of carbon quantum dots (CQDs) into diagnostic frameworks has been explored for their fluorescent properties, allowing for improved imaging and therapeutic applications in the eye.178 These smart nanomaterials can be engineered to target specific ocular tissues, providing a dual function of diagnosis and treatment, thereby exemplifying the potential of nanotechnology to transform traditional approaches in ophthalmology. Overall, the ongoing research and development in smart nanomaterials hold great promise for enhancing both the therapeutic and diagnostic capabilities in the management of complex ocular diseases, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes and quality of life.

Safety Assessment, Clinical Translation Challenges and Innovation Strategies

Toxic Mechanisms

The interaction between mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSiNPs) and silver ions (Ag⁺) has been shown to induce significant toxicity, particularly in ocular tissues, leading to DNA damage and conditions such as dry eye syndrome. MSiNPs possess unique properties, including a large surface area and high porosity, which enhance their reactivity and potential for toxicity when combined with other hazardous substances like Ag⁺. In a recent study, exposure to MSiNPs alone was found to cause some cytotoxic effects; however, the combination of MSiNPs with Ag⁺ resulted in a marked increase in toxicity, as evidenced by a decrease in cell viability, elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and significant DNA damage.179 The study utilized RNA sequencing to identify that the mRNA surveillance signaling pathway plays a crucial role in regulating the cytotoxic effects induced by the MSiNPs-Ag⁺ combination. This pathway is integral in monitoring and responding to cellular stress and damage, suggesting that the presence of Ag⁺ exacerbates the toxic effects of MSiNPs. Furthermore, in vivo experiments demonstrated that rats exposed to MSiNPs-Ag⁺ exhibited severe corneal damage and symptoms of dry eye compared to those exposed to MSiNPs alone, highlighting the synergistic toxicity of these materials.180 The study underscores the importance of understanding the toxicological profiles of engineered nanomaterials, particularly in the context of ocular applications where exposure is frequent due to the use of cosmetics, sunscreens, and therapeutic agents containing metallic nanoparticles. The findings indicate that while MSiNPs and Ag⁺ may have beneficial applications in drug delivery and therapy, their combined exposure poses significant risks to ocular health. This necessitates a careful evaluation of their safety profiles and the development of strategies to mitigate their toxic effects. One potential approach discussed in the study is the use of a protein corona-based therapy, which involves the application of fetal bovine serum (FBS) to counteract the corneal damage induced by MSiNPs. This innovative method suggests a therapeutic avenue to protect ocular tissues from the adverse effects of nanomaterial exposure, emphasizing the need for further research into biocompatible solutions that can enhance the safety of nanomaterials in ophthalmic applications. Overall, the study highlights the dual nature of nanomaterials, where their innovative applications in ocular disease treatment must be balanced with a thorough understanding of their potential toxic mechanisms, particularly when they interact with other compounds such as Ag⁺. This balance is essential for the development of safe and effective nanomedicine strategies in ophthalmology, ensuring that the benefits of these advanced materials do not come at the cost of ocular health.

Clinical Translation Challenges and Innovative Strategies

The integration of nanomaterials into ophthalmic therapies presents significant clinical translation challenges, particularly concerning safety and biocompatibility. One of the primary safety concerns is the long-term ocular toxicity associated with certain nanomaterials, particularly metal nanoparticles, which can accumulate in ocular tissues and potentially lead to adverse effects such as inflammation or cellular damage. For instance, studies have indicated that the accumulation of metal nanoparticles can provoke oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in the eye, raising concerns about their long-term safety profile.180 Additionally, the immunogenicity of some nanomaterials, such as liposomes, can activate the complement system, leading to unwanted immune responses that may complicate treatment outcomes.181 These safety concerns necessitate the development of innovative strategies to enhance the biocompatibility of nanomaterials used in ocular applications.

To address these safety concerns, researchers are exploring the use of biomimetic nanoparticles that mimic the properties of extracellular vesicles, such as exosomes. These exosome-mimicking nanoparticles can effectively reduce immunogenicity by utilizing materials that are naturally recognized by the body, such as red blood cell membranes. This approach not only minimizes immune responses but also enhances the targeted delivery of therapeutic agents to ocular tissues.182 Furthermore, the development of biodegradable nanomaterials, such as hyaluronic acid and polylactic acid-based carriers, offers a promising solution to the toxicity associated with long-term accumulation. These materials can degrade naturally within the body, releasing their therapeutic payloads in a controlled manner while minimizing the risk of toxicity.49 Such advancements are crucial for ensuring the safety and efficacy of nanomaterial-based therapies in clinical settings.

In addition to addressing safety concerns, innovative drug delivery methods are being developed to improve the administration of nanomaterials in ocular therapies. For example, microneedle arrays represent a revolutionary approach for pain-free delivery of nanoparticles across the corneal barrier, thus replacing traditional injection methods that may be uncomfortable for patients.183 This technique allows for precise and controlled delivery of therapeutic agents directly to the target tissues, enhancing drug bioavailability and therapeutic outcomes. Moreover, drug-eluting contact lenses have emerged as a novel platform for sustained drug release, particularly for the treatment of conditions such as glaucoma. These lenses can continuously release medications, such as timolol-loaded nanomicelles, providing a steady therapeutic effect while improving patient compliance.89 Such innovations in drug delivery not only enhance the effectiveness of treatments but also reduce the frequency of administration, ultimately improving patient adherence to prescribed regimens. Future Development Direction and Conclusion

Clinical Applications and Future Development Trends

The clinical application of nanodrug delivery systems has shown promising results in recent trials, particularly in the field of ophthalmology. These systems leverage the unique properties of nanomaterials, such as their small size and ability to enhance drug solubility, to improve therapeutic outcomes in various ocular diseases. For instance, studies have demonstrated that nanoparticles can significantly enhance the bioavailability of ocular drugs, which is often limited by the eye’s unique anatomical and physiological barriers.184 Clinical trials involving copper-based nanozymes have indicated their effectiveness in scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reducing inflammation in ocular surface diseases, showcasing their potential as a novel therapeutic option.123 Additionally, the use of tetrahedral DNA nanostructures has been explored in clinical settings for their ability to deliver therapeutic agents directly to targeted retinal cells, thus addressing conditions like retinal ischemia and promoting neuroprotection.185 The integration of these advanced nanomaterials into clinical practice not only holds promise for improving treatment efficacy but also for reducing the frequency of administration, thereby enhancing patient compliance. However, the transition from laboratory research to clinical application is fraught with challenges, including regulatory hurdles, the need for extensive safety evaluations, and the necessity for interdisciplinary collaboration among material scientists, pharmacologists, and clinicians to ensure successful implementation of these innovative therapies.180

The potential of combining gene therapy with nanotechnology represents a frontier in ocular treatment strategies. The integration of gene therapy with nanomaterials offers a synergistic approach that could revolutionize the management of ocular diseases. For example, the use of nanoparticles as carriers for gene therapy agents allows for targeted delivery to specific cells in the eye, thereby enhancing the therapeutic effect while minimizing systemic side effects.186 This is particularly relevant for conditions such as age-related macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy, where targeted gene delivery could potentially halt disease progression and restore vision.187 Recent advancements in the engineering of nanomaterials have enabled the development of stimuli-responsive systems that can release therapeutic agents in response to specific biological signals, further enhancing the precision of treatment.181 Moreover, the combination of gene therapy with stem cell interventions, facilitated by nanotechnology, could provide novel pathways for vision restoration, addressing the underlying causes of retinal degeneration rather than merely alleviating symptoms.187 As research continues to evolve, the future of ocular therapeutics will likely see an increased focus on these innovative approaches, emphasizing the importance of continued investment in nanotechnology and gene therapy research to unlock their full potential in clinical applications.
