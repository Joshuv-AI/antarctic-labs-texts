Developing free swell index of soil test as smart experiment in virtual geotechnical engineering laboratory
Authors: Siva Kumar Prasad Chebiyyam, Mallikarjuna Rao Kattamuri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The present work introduces a novel approach to integrating graphical and animation components with a predictive analytics model for developing a web-based virtual laboratory application in Geotechnical Engineering, one of the core domains of Civil Engineering. Most virtual laboratories developed in this field so far are primarily simulation-based. In contrast, the current study utilises historical experimental data to train a machine learning model that predicts experimental outcomes. These predicted results dynamically influence the virtual experimentation process, thereby rendering the experimental experience and outcomes more realistic and enriching for students. As a proof of concept, an experiment titled Free Swell Index of Soils was developed as a smart virtual experiment. Experimental data were compiled from reputable research publications, the authors’ own data, academic projects and theses, and several machine learning regression models—namely Linear Regression, Support Vector Regression, Decision Tree Regression, and Random Forest Regression—were evaluated. Among these, the Random Forest Regression model demonstrated superior predictive performance and was therefore adopted for the final implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31497-x.

Introduction

Education as a process of knowledge dissemination encompasses two fundamental components - theoretical understanding and practical application. While conceptual instruction enables students to understand, hands-on practice empowers them to apply that knowledge effectively. Practical orientation, therefore, plays a pivotal role in teaching and learning, particularly within STEM education. The laboratory serves as a central hub for experiential learning, where theoretical concepts are reinforced through experimentation and observation. Conventional STEM laboratories are typically characterized by dedicated physical spaces, specialized equipment, consumable materials, and qualified personnel for operation and maintenance. Consequently, the establishment and upkeep of such physical laboratories demand substantial financial and logistical investment. As a nation’s economic development is closely linked to industrial and agricultural advancement, a growing need for a skilled technical workforce in large numbers arises. To meet the additional demand for producing more skilled diploma holders and graduates in STEM disciplines, thereby necessitating the expansion of basic laboratory infrastructure, an endeavor is often constrained by practical and financial limitations.

It is essential to acknowledge that a virtual lab cannot replace a physical laboratory—nor should it be. The primary role of a virtual laboratory is to complement physical experimentation by encouraging students to perform a greater number and variety of experiments, many of which may not be feasible due to limitations in physical resources. Virtual platforms thus serve as valuable tools for promoting experiential learning and fostering inquiry-driven exploration. Moreover, they provide a pathway for students to engage in advanced or research-oriented experiments that extend beyond the conventional curriculum—an essential aspect for cultivating advanced learners and future researchers.

Virtual labs, as was named initially, are those environments that mimic physical labs to conduct experiments on computers, possibly cater the increased demand for workforce due to economic development1. Two decades ago, when virtual labs started making their presence, most labs were web-based, and the student intended to work on these labs “anytime, anyplace, online”2. Compared with physical labs, virtual labs have advantages, especially when offered to diploma and UG students, viz. reduction in cost, time, maintenance, and workload of an instructor, apart from round-the-clock availability and sharing of expensive equipment being specific advantages3.

Remote labs typically provide an online interface to physical labs, allowing students to experiment with real-world equipment anywhere and anytime4. Researchers in Germany are carving engineering education to suit the requirement of Industry 4.0 under the project ELLI. In this connection, it is worth mentioning a remote lab facility to conduct experiments for an international Master’s Program in Manufacturing Technology offered by TU Dortmund University, where the students can remotely perform experiments before they even arrive in Germany5.

Virtual Labs is an initiative of the Ministry of Education of the Government of India under the National Mission on Education through ICT; it is an open online platform for more than 100 virtual labs under nine broad areas of Science and Engineering. All these labs are essentially simulation-based, and 11 prestigious institutes in the country are participating in developing these labs6.

Geotechnical engineering, also known as geotechnology, is an essential specialization of civil engineering that deals with aspects related to the strength and deformation of soils to support the structures that eventually rest on the ground and a stable foundation system for the structure to rest. Geotechnology has two significant divisions, namely Soil Mechanics and Foundation Engineering. The mechanical behavior and properties of soils are studied in Soil Mechanics, whereas its application in designing foundations, earth embankments, earth dams, etc. comes under Foundation Engineering. Soil properties are divided into two groups, viz., index properties and engineering properties. Index properties such as in-situ density, specific gravity, relative density, etc., are easy to experiment with and determine within a short duration; however, the engineering properties such as consolidation, shear strength, etc., need a considerable amount of time, costly equipment, and trained lab technicians but these properties are very vital in the design of foundation systems. Soil Mechanics and Soil Mechanics Lab are essential courses in any UG Civil Engineering and Agricultural Engineering programs. Since experiments related to the determination of engineering properties of soils typically require more expensive equipment and skilled lab technicians to train, the introduction of virtual labs in geotechnical engineering is a good idea and is very much justified. Almost all virtual labs in geotechnical engineering/ soil mechanics, including “Virtual Labs” offered by the Ministry of Higher Education, Government of India, are simulation-based virtual labs.

In this work, the authors are proposing a kind of “Smart Virtual Labs” that is an improvement over simulation-based virtual labs to enhance the student’s learning experience. Smart virtual labs utilize actual experimental data and use machine learning/ deep learning algorithms to predict experimental outcomes while the students conduct experiments in these labs.

Some of the most significant virtual lab environments developed in Geotechnical Engineering are discussed in the Literature Review section. The Free Swell Index (FSI) of Soils experiment is selected to develop as a smart experiment in the Geotechnical Engineering Virtual Laboratory, and the details are presented under Experimental Data Modelling. The high-level architecture of a Virtual Laboratory is explained in Virtual Lab Architecture. The functional flow of the application is narrated in Functional Workflow of Virtual Lab. Literature review

Researchers have developed experiments related to soil mechanics, most of which are simulation-based; a summary of them is presented in this section.

The National Science Foundation of USA has created the National Digital Library (NSDL) under which Geotechnical, Rock and Water Resources (GROW) is a civil engineering digital library for which virtual simulation-based soil mechanics experiments such as Triaxial Shear Test, Direct Shear Test, Consolidation Test, etc. was developed by Muniram Budhu and his colleagues at Arizona State University, Tucson7–9.

The University of Alberta has developed a software application titled “The Virtual Geotechnical Laboratory” for its undergraduate students; it is a simulation-based virtual lab and has three modules to test three engineering properties of soils, namely permeability, consolidation, and triaxial test, where the consolidation test takes one to two weeks to test a single specimen in a physical lab10.

Dayakar Penumadu and Amit Prashant have developed “Geo-Sim,” a multimedia software supported by the Windows platform using Visual Basic. The authors developed the software with four modules using vast experimental data to conduct triaxial compression, one-dimensional consolidation, direct shear, and true triaxial on the cubical specimen through this software in the virtual environment11.

IIIT Hyderabad has developed a virtual lab for the “Soil Mechanics Lab” under the initiative of the Ministry of Education, Government of India, through the National Mission on Education through ICT. The lab offers ten experiments; five are to find index properties, and the remaining are to determine the engineering properties of soil; all these experiments are essentially simulation-based.

Methodology

Development of Free Swell Index test as a smart experiment on virtual environment involve two stages viz. (1) Finding appropriate machine learning algorithm for predicting the experimental outcomes, (2) Integration of machine learning (ML) model with virtual experiment to build a smart virtual lab experiment.

Modeling of free swell index test

This section outlines a brief experimental procedure for the Free Swell Index test, including its outcomes, the collection of test data, and the methods applied to the test data to obtain the optimal model for predicting soil swell parameters.

The majority of clays have Kaolinite, Illite, and Montmorillonite as minerals in them. In the presence and absence of water, clay exhibits swelling and shrinkage, and the magnitude of swelling and shrinkage depends on the kind of clay mineral present; out of the three clay minerals mentioned, Montmorillonite clays exhibit high swelling and shrinkage, followed by Illite and Kaolinite12. As the clays exhibiting extreme swelling and shrinkage characteristics are detrimental to the structure, their presence has to be determined prior to the construction of the building during the soil investigation stage. IS: 2720 ( Part XL ) – 1977 prescribed an experiment titled “Determination of Free Swell Index of Soils” to assess the expansive behaviour of soils.

Free Swell Index of soils is a simple test in two 100 ml measuring jars with 10 g of oven-dry soil passing 425 microns IS Sieve in each jar. One jar is filled with distilled water, and the other is with Kerosene. After a detention period of 24 h, the value of soil is measured in each jar. Collection of test data

Researchers in the field of Soil Mechanics have established that the swelling behavior of soils can be estimated with the help of Atterberg Limits, particularly the Liquid Limit (LL) and Plastic Limit (PL), as well as the derived parameter, namely the Plasticity Index (PI), which is defined as LL minus PL. Prakash & Sridharan have worked extensively on clays and published their experimental data on the FSI of soil tests in various research articles14,15. Additionally, the data is collected from the authors’ own work, academic projects, dissertations, and research articles. Initially, 147 data records were collected and examined for outliers using interquartile range and Z-score techniques applied to both input and output data. From these studies, 13 records are identified as outliers; after discarding them, 134 records are finalized as a test database for the modelling. Six datasets, each comprising 134 records, are prepared from the database. In each dataset, FSI, MFSI, and FSR are dependent variables. At the same time, LL and PL are independent variables in three datasets, and LL and PI are independent variables in the remaining three datasets, as shown in Table 1.

Regression analysis

Predicting values of FSI/FSR & MFSI from LL & PL/PI using regression techniques is a crucial step in developing the virtual experiment to enhance the student’s experience while experimenting in a Virtual Lab. Four regression techniques, namely (1) Multi Linear Regression (MLR) (2) Support Vector Regression (SVR) (3) Decision Tree Regression (DTR), and (4) Random Forest Regression (RFR) are considered for the analysis as detailed below:

Multiple linear regression (MLR)

Simple linear regression is a statistical technique used to establish a linear relationship between a dependent variable and a single independent variable. However, when more than one independent variable is involved, the model is extended to Multiple Linear Regression (MLR), provided that the relationship between the dependent variable and each independent variable is linear (i.e., the degree of each independent variable is 1).

If y is a dependent variable and x1, x2,…,xn are the independent variables, the general form of a multiple linear regression equation is:

y = a1 × 1 + a2 × 2 + ……+ an xn + b

a1, a2,…, an are the regression coefficients (weights) assigned to the independent variables, and b is the intercept term.

Support vector regression (SVR)

In the case of a simple linear regression, the dependent variable is plotted along the Y-axis and the independent variable along the X-axis. Support Vector Regression introduces the concept of an ε-insensitive margin, within which deviations of the predicted values from the actual data points are not penalized. This margin forms a “tube” around the regression function. Data points lying within the tube are considered adequately predicted, while those falling outside the tube incur a penalty and influence the model’s optimization process. The algorithm can model both linear and non-linear relationships by employing kernel functions. The data points that lie on or outside the margin and contribute to defining the regression function are referred to as support vectors.

Decision tree regression (DTR)

Decision tree regression works by repeatedly splitting the dataset into smaller and more homogeneous groups based on the values of the input variables. At each step, the algorithm selects a split that minimises the variability of the target variable within the resulting groups. This process continues until the data is divided into terminal nodes, called leaves. The prediction for any new input is obtained by assigning it to the appropriate leaf, and the output value is calculated as the average of the target values of the data points contained in that leaf.

Random forest regression (RFR)

Random Forest Regression is an ensemble learning method that constructs multiple decision trees using different subsets of the data and, often, subsets of the input features. Each tree provides a prediction for the target variable, and the final predicted value is obtained by averaging the predictions from all the trees in the ensemble. This approach reduces overfitting and improves the generalisation performance compared to a single decision tree.

In order to evaluate the model performance four metrices are considered in this study viz. R2, Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE).

The RepeatedKFold class available in the scikit-learn library facilitates systematic data partitioning and repeated cross-validation to achieve a more robust and unbiased evaluation of model performance metrics. In the present study, a five-fold cross-validation was repeated ten times, with a fixed random state of 42 to ensure reproducibility. This procedure evaluates the model across 50 (5 × 10) distinct train–test partitions, wherein 80% of the data is used for training and the remaining 20% for testing in each iteration. The resulting performance metrics are reported as the mean (µ) ± standard deviation (σ) computed over the 50 evaluations. The GridSearchCV class of scikit-learn library is implemented for hyper parameter tuning.

ML model – VL environment integration

The technology stack for the Virtual Geotechnical Laboratory web application is depicted in Fig. 1. The core application is developed on .Net Framework as ASP.NET CORE MVC, where the graphical and animation effects are done with the help of CSS & JavaScript.

The core application connects to a RESTful API for the user data to be processed to get the ML-based predictions. Python Flask-based RESTful API facilitates communication between the user interface and machine learning model that uses scikit-learn libraries.

When the student inputs data from UI sent to the API, the ML model, along with the data file that is stored in the pickle file, will be invoked. This pickle file will evaluate and communicate back the machine-based predictions.

Finally, thus, predicted results will control the student’s experience while conducting the experiments in a Virtual Lab; therefore, the experimental outcome is not merely simulation-based but driven by ML output to be more realistic.

Functional workflow of virtual lab

The student will navigate to the data entry page of the Free Swell Index of Soil experiment when selecting the experiment from the Welcome page, as shown in Fig. 8a. The student has to enter the Liquid Limit and Plastic Limit values for the soil for which Free Swelling Index Test is to be conducted on virtual environment. After clicking the “Conduct Experiment” button, the student will be navigated to the Virtual Lab page, as depicted in Fig. 8b.

The virtual lab environment is divided into four zones viz. (1) Instruction Pannel, (2) Action Pannel, (3) Accessory Store, and (4) Experiment Area. In the Action Pannel the action steps are listed strictly in order; when the student selects an action step, relevant instruction will be displayed on Instruction Pannel. Student can pick up the equipment/tools/accessories from the Accessory Store and drag them onto the Experiment Area to experiment. After completing a part of the experiment, the student has to click “Next” to proceed until the total experiment is conducted. Figure 8c is the image of the virtual environment during the conduct of the experiment. After completing the experiment, the final results, which are predicted by machine learning algorithms, will be displayed on the result page shown in Fig. 8d.

Summary & outlook

Correlation between the index properties of soils and their engineering properties, such as shear strength and consolidation behavior, has long been a subject of continued research interest. Numerous researchers have proposed mathematical and empirical relationships to establish these correlations. However, with the advent of machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques, such studies can now be significantly strengthened by implementing advanced algorithms capable of capturing complex, nonlinear relationships among soil parameters.

Furthermore, site investigation and subsoil exploration are among the most demanding and resource-intensive tasks in geotechnical engineering. Experiments such as the Standard Penetration Test (SPT), Cone Penetration Test (CPT), and Plate Load Test (PLT) require sophisticated equipment and significant expenditure. Establishing correlations between the results of these field tests and simpler index properties can offer valuable insights for developing predictive models and designing virtual experiments that more accurately represent real-world soil behavior.

In geotechnical engineering, particularly in soil mechanics, specific experiments such as the triaxial shear test and consolidation test require extended durations—often ranging from 15 to 30 days—to obtain reliable results. Consequently, conducting such experiments within undergraduate laboratory courses is often impractical due to time constraints, equipment limitations, and cost factors. Developing predictive analytics models that emulate these experiments can substantially enhance the quality and efficiency of geotechnical education by allowing students to explore realistic outcomes in a virtual setting.

In the present work, the authors have developed an integrated virtual environment that combines graphical and animated interfaces, created using CSS and JavaScript, with predictive analytics models built using the scikit-learn library in Python. This integration led to the development of a web-based virtual laboratory in which experimental outcomes are not merely simulation-based but are generated through trained ML models. This approach enhances the experiential quality of virtual experimentation by providing realistic, data-driven responses.
