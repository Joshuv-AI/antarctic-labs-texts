Grounding LTL Tasks in Sub-Symbolic RL Environments for Zero-Shot Generalization
Matteo Pannacci, Andrea Fanti, Elena Umili, Roberto Capobianco

Abstract
In this work we address the problem of training a Reinforcement Learning agent to follow multiple temporally-extended instructions expressed in Linear Temporal Logic in sub-symbolic environments. Previous multi-task work has mostly relied on knowledge of the mapping between raw observations and symbols appearing in the formulae. We drop this unrealistic assumption by jointly training a multi-task policy and a symbol grounder with the same experience. The symbol grounder is trained only from raw observations and sparse rewards via Neural Reward Machines in a semi-supervised fashion. Experiments on vision-based environments show that our method achieves performance comparable to using the true symbol grounding and significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods for sub-symbolic environments.



1
Introduction

Creating agents that can follow a vast set of instructions rather than only solving a specific task has been a central goal in Reinforcement Learning (RL). Formal languages such as Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)
30
are widely adopted for task specification
12
for their unambiguous semantics and compositional syntax. LTL instructions combine atomic propositions that represent relevant features of the environment, or
symbols
, to express temporally-extended tasks involving these features. For example, symbols in an office environment might represent being at the coffee machine, a mail pickup site or a specific desk — a basic temporally-extended task might then be (informally) “pick up the mail and coffee in this order and bring them to my desk”.
If the agent is only equipped with a video camera or other raw sensory equipment, it will then need to recognize office locations from camera frames before even trying to understand the LTL language itself. In general, understanding a task expressed by an LTL formula requires knowledge of both the LTL language itself as well as the mapping between observations and interpretations over symbols, known as the
symbol grounding
.

We can expect many real-world domains like the example above to
not
provide access to the true symbol grounding. These are known as
sub-symbolic
environments. Learning a multi-task policy in sub-symbolic environments requires the agent to
simultaneously
learn: (i) a policy that understands the semantics of LTL operators; (ii) the symbol grounder. While learning multi-task policies using the true symbol grounder has been explored in previous works, the more realistic setting is rarely treated due to the compounding complexity of the two learning problems.

Here we address both of these challenges together and propose a method to learn multi-task policies that generalize to unseen instructions in sub-symbolic environments. Our method builds on Neural Reward Machines (NRMs)
36
to jointly learn a policy and a symbol grounder with the same experience. NRMs frame the problem as a Semi-Supervised Symbol Grounding (SSSG) task to provide indirect supervision to the symbol grounder based on the structure of the LTL formulae. The resulting agent can then follow many different LTL instructions by keeping track of task progression directly from raw observations.
Interestingly, while NRMs are known to struggle with sparse rewards in single-task settings, attempting multiple tasks provides a sufficient learning signal for NRMs to correctly infer the symbol grounding.
We use the state-of-the-art method LTL2Action
38
to condition the policy on task progression information. Our approach is however not tied to the specifics of LTL2Action and can be combined with any transferable task progression encoding.

We study the convergence and zero-shot generalization power of our method on a Minecraft-like discrete environment and a continuous navigation environment. Our method shows little to no loss in performance with respect to using the true labeling function. Our method also improves significantly over
20
, the state-of-the-art method for multi-task RL in sub-symbolic environments.

The main contributions of our work are then:

•

a method to jointly learn a multi-task policy and a symbol grounder by exploiting the indirect supervision provided by the environment;

•

an extension of LTL2Action which lifts the assumption of knowing the labeling function;

•

an empirical evaluation of the proposed method against the state-of-the-art baseline and the upper bound of knowing the true labeling function.

LTL

Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)
30
extends propositional logic with temporal operators to specify properties over time. Given a set of propositions

\varphi
is

\varphi:=\top\mid\bot\mid p\mid\lnot\varphi\mid\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}\mid\mathsf{X}\varphi\mid\varphi_{1}\mathsf{U}\varphi_{2},

(1)

where

\mathsf{F}\varphi\equiv\top\mathsf{U}\varphi
(eventually) and
\mathsf{G}\varphi\equiv\lnot\mathsf{F}(\lnot\varphi)
(globally). We refer to

An LTL formula is interpreted over an infinite trace
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma^{(0)},\sigma^{(1)},\ldots)
, where
\sigma^{(t)}\in 2^{P}
denotes the set of propositions true at time

\sigma^{(t)}\in P

21

Moore Machines

A Moore machine
M=(P,Q,O,q_{0},\delta,\lambda)
consists of a finite input alphabet
P
, states
Q
, output symbols
O
, an initial state
q_{0}\in Q
, a transition function
\delta:Q\times P\to Q
, and an output function
\lambda:Q\to O
.
For a finite input trace
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma^{(0)},\dots,\sigma^{(n)})

\delta^{*}:Q\times P^{*}\to Q

\delta^{*}(q,\epsilon)=q
and
\delta^{*}(q,\sigma_{1}\dots\sigma_{n})=\delta(\delta^{*}(q,\sigma_{1}\dots\sigma_{n-1}),\sigma_{n})

\lambda(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=(\lambda(q_{0}),\lambda(q_{1}),\dots,\lambda(q_{n})),

q_{t}=\delta^{*}(q_{0},\sigma^{(0)}\dots\sigma^{(t-1)}),\ t\geq 1.

Non-Markovian Reinforcement Learning

In reinforcement learning (RL), agent-environment interaction is commonly modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP)
33
, defined by the tuple
(S,A,t,r,\gamma)
, where
S
is the set of states,
A
the set of actions,
t:S\times A\times S\rightarrow[0,1]
the transition function,
r:S\times A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the reward function, and
\gamma\in[0,1]
the discount factor. A
policy

\pi:S\rightarrow A
maps states to actions, while the
value function

V^{\pi}(s)
denotes the expected discounted return obtained by following
\pi
from state
s
. The goal of the RL agent is to learn the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
providing maximum value.
Standard MDPs assume Markovian transitions and rewards, i.e., dependence only on the current state. However, many real-world problems violate this assumption
25
. In a non-Markovian decision process, the reward function
r:(S\times A)^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the transition function
t:(S\times A)^{*}\times S\rightarrow[0,1]
, or both may depend on the interaction history. In this work, we focus on Non-Markovian Reward Decision Processes (NMRDPs)
4
, where non-Markovianity arises solely from the reward function.
Learning optimal policies in NMRDPs is challenging, as rewards may depend on the entire sequence of past state–action pairs, making standard RL methods inapplicable. To address this issue, many approaches construct an augmented Markovian state representation by monitoring the task through a labeling function
L:S\rightarrow P
, which maps environment states to propositional symbols. The resulting labeled traces are used to track task progress, typically via LTL formula progression
34
;
38
or equivalent automata-based representations such as Moore machines
10
;
12
.

Related Work

Reinforcement Learning with Temporal Specifications

Temporal logic formalisms are often used in Reinforcement Learning with non-Markovian rewards to define temporally-extended goals and constraints. Popular choices are Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)
30
and its variant evaluated on finite traces, LTL
f

14
. These allow to recover the Markovian property by conditioning the policy on the state of an automaton constructed from the formula. Examples include Restraining Bolts
12
;
13
and Reward Machines
35
;
10
;
18
. These methods can only learn a single task or a fixed set of tasks and assume perfect knowledge of the true labeling function.

Reinforcement Learning without Perfect Labeling

Several methods have considered a noisy or imperfect labeling function
24
;
9
;
39
without attempting to learn the symbol grounder itself.

36
introduced Neural Reward Machines (NRMs), which allow to learn the symbol grounder from sequences of observations and rewards without any access to the true labeling function. If the task dynamics is unknown, NRMs can also learn the reward machine’s transition and reward functions
15
. These can still only learn single tasks or fixed sets of tasks.

Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning

A popular approach to learning multi-task policies is decomposing specifications into subtasks solved individually, both with LTL formulae
22
;
23
;
3
;
26
and with other specifications
1
;
41
;
29
;
32
. Despite enabling generalization to new tasks, decomposition can result in suboptimal solutions due to uncoordinated subtask policies.

34
applied LTL progression
6
to the problem, to dynamically update the LTL formula as the agent progresses in the task. Their algorithm provably converges to the optimal multi-task policy but does not generalize to unseen tasks.
LTL2Action
38
combines LTL progression and Graph Neural Networks to encode the progressed formula, enabling generalization to new tasks. They also employ environment-agnostic pre-training of the formula encoder, boosting performance significantly.

17
explored a non-learned transferable encoding of reward machine states based on semantic similarity, obtaining mixed results.

19
introduced a representation for LTL formulae based on reach-avoid sequences of truth assignments, allowing to learn multi-task policies on infinite-horizon tasks.

20
is the only existing work on learning multi-task policies that can generalize to new tasks without knowledge of the true symbol grounding. This method combines learned sub-networks into task-specific networks by following the syntactic structure of the LTL formula. We compare to this method in our experiments since it is the only one tackling our same problem setting.

Method

4.1
Problem Setting and Objectives

We study whether it is possible to generalize across temporal tasks expressed in LTL in sub-symbolic environments where the labeling function is unknown. Formally, consider an MDP without rewards
M=(S,A,t,\gamma)
, a co-safe LTL goal
\varphi

M_{\varphi}=(S,A,t,r_{\varphi},\gamma)

r_{\varphi}(s_{0},a_{0},\cdots,s_{t},a_{t})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\langle L(s_{0}),\cdots,L(s_{t})\rangle\text{ is}\\
&\text{a {good prefix} for }\varphi\\
-1&\text{if }\langle L(s_{0}),\cdots,L(s_{t})\rangle\text{ is}\\
&\text{a {bad prefix} for }\varphi\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(2)

A reward of
1

0

semantics

;

, but are omitted here to test a general scenario.
Let

4.3
System Overview

Building on these theoretical insights, we structure our system as shown in Figure
2
(b), as the combination of four main modules: grounder, environment features, LTL features, and RL. Each implements a function learned via a neural architecture:

•

The
grounder module
maps raw observations to symbols,
L_{\theta}:S\rightarrow\Delta(P)
.

•

The
environment feature module
extracts features from environment observations,
f\textsuperscript{$img$}_{\theta}:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n
is the feature dimension.

•

The
LTL module
extracts a feature vector for the original formula and all progression steps
\varphi\textsuperscript{($t$)}
,
t\geq 0
, denoted
f^{LTL}_{\theta}:\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LTL}}(P)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
, with
m
the dimension of the LTL feature space.

•

The
RL module
implements the chosen RL algorithm, taking as input the concatenation of the two feature vectors
f^{img}_{\theta}(s^{(t)})
and
f^{LTL}_{\theta}(\varphi^{(t)})
, and learns a policy function
\pi_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\rightarrow\Delta(A)
and a value function
V_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

At each step,
s^{(t)}
feeds the grounder and environment modules. The grounder predicts a symbolic event
\tilde{p}\in\Delta(P)
, and the environment module produces
f^{img}_{\theta}(s^{(t)})
. The most likely symbol
p=\arg\max L_{\theta}(s^{(t)})
progresses
\varphi^{(t-1)}
to
\varphi^{(t)}
, which is mapped into a vector by the LTL module. The concatenated features are passed to the RL module to select the next action.
This pipeline closely resembles that of
LTL2Action

38
, with one key difference: in our system,
all
modules are trainable, whereas in
LTL2Action
the grounder module is known a priori. In our system, the grounding function is trained alongside the other modules and may not always be correct, especially early in training. This uncertainty affects the progressed formula
\varphi\textsuperscript{($t$)}
, which is used by the agent to reason about the next action, introducing a nontrivial challenge.

The RL and environment modules are trained end-to-end with the RL loss, while the grounder and LTL modules have separate objectives described in the next sections.

4.4
Grounder Training

The
Grounder Module
is a neural network classifier, but it cannot be trained via standard supervised learning, as explicit labels for each observation are unavailable, nor fully end-to-end with Deep RL. Instead, the agent learns the grounding indirectly from the environment through the sequence of rewards, observed states, and the LTL task formula. This falls under
Semi-Supervised Symbol Grounding
(SSSG)
27
;
7
;
37
, where the goal is to map raw observations to symbolic representations using limited or indirect supervision. In this work, we leverage Neural Reward Machines (NRMs)
36
to perform semi-supervised knowledge injection for learning the labeling function.

Neural Reward Machines

NRMs are a probabilistic extension of Reward Machines (RMs)

that model non-Markovian rewards via an automata-based structure, while incorporating uncertainty in transitions, rewards, and symbol grounding. Formally, an NRM is a Probabilistic Moore Machine with a probabilistic symbol grounding function mapping environment states to symbols and interpreting output symbols as rewards.

4.4.1
From LTL formulae to Moore Machines

So far, we expressed tasks as LTL formulae interpreted using
LTL_{3}
semantics
8
, not as Moore Machines. However, the two formalisms are connected, and any co-safe LTL formula can be transformed into a Moore Machine that outputs
-1
,
0
, or
+1
, depending on whether the trace has permanently violated (-1), can still evolve (0), or has permanently satisfied (+1) the specification.

Co-safe LTL formulae can be translated into DFAs recognizing their
good prefixes

21
. To compute the reward function, we also need the
bad prefixes
: sequences that cannot be extended into a satisfying trace. These correspond to non-final states from which no final state is reachable, often called
dead states
. They can be computed as the complement of
live states
, i.e., states from which a final state is reachable.

Given the DFA
\langle Q,P,\delta,q_{0},F\rangle
recognizing the good prefixes of
\varphi
, with
F
and
D
denoting its final and dead states respectively, the corresponding Moore Machine is
\langle P,Q,O,q_{0},\delta,\lambda\rangle
with
O=\{1,0,-1\}
and

\displaystyle\lambda(q)=\begin{cases}1&q\in F\\
-1&q\in D\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

4.4.2
Learning with NRMs

NRMs provide a differentiable implementation of automata, enabling backpropagation to the
Grounder Module
. Unlike
36
, we employ multiple NRMs-one per training task—that share the same grounder but differ in their Moore Machine.
Grounder training consists of two phases: data collection and update. During collection, RL interactions are stored as state and reward sequences
\boldsymbol{s}=\langle s^{(0)},\dots,s^{(t)}\rangle
and
\boldsymbol{r}=\langle r^{(0)},\dots,r^{(t)}\rangle
, together with the task formulae
\varphi
, allowing data collection without additional simulations.

As noted in
36
, episodes with only zero rewards provide little supervision and exacerbate the
reasoning shortcuts
problem
28
, which is common in NeSy predictors for SSSG: when symbolic knowledge is underspecified, multiple symbol mappings may satisfy the same structure, leading to unintended solutions. While
36
addresses this issue using dense, potential-based rewards, in our training setup we successfully eliminate the reliance on dense rewards by exploiting the structure induced by multiple training tasks. To further improve data efficiency, we store only episodes with at least one non-zero reward, or zero-reward episodes in which LTL progression reaches
\top
or
\bot
, indicating incorrect symbol predictions.

In the update phase, sampled episodes are converted into Moore Machines. For each task, an NRM
NRM_{\varphi}
is initialized accordingly, and only the shared grounder is trained by minimizing the cross-entropy between predicted rewards
\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}=NRM_{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{s})
and ground-truth rewards
\boldsymbol{r}
:

L(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{r})=\text{cross-entropy}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}},\boldsymbol{r}).

Thanks to the differentiability of NRMs, gradients are propagated through time to update the grounder.

4.5
LTL Module

The final module of our system is the LTL module, which learns an embedding of task formulae and their progressions. In
38
, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) proved most effective, with two training schemes: 1) end-to-end training with RL and environment modules, and 2) pretraining via LTL-Bootcamp followed by end-to-end training.

In LTL-Bootcamp, the LTL and RL modules are trained on a simple sequence-generation task where actions correspond to formula symbols (
A=P
) and rewards (+1/-1) indicate satisfaction or violation. Pretraining imbues the module with task semantics before environment interaction.
We tested both schemes from
38
and a third variant using only the bootcamp. The latter was most effective in our sub-symbolic setup, because a fixed LTL module stabilizes learning in the presence of the uncertain grounder. More details can be found in appendix
A.4
.

For architecture, we used a Relational Graph Convolutional Network (R-GCN) built from the formula’s Abstract Syntax Tree (AST), with nodes for propositional symbols, logical connectives, or temporal operators, as in
38
(Figure
2
(c)).

. We evaluate the zero-shot transfer both to unseen formulae from the training distributions and to longer and more complex formulae.

Environments

The
Minecraft-like
environment is a simple discrete grid-world with 4-way movement actions that contains a variety of items. Each primitive symbol in this environment corresponds to visiting the cell in which a specific item resides. Although similar to the Minecraft Gridworld environment from
36
, we use a bigger grid, and the layout of items in the grid-world is randomly sampled at every episode instead of being fixed. In fact, a fixed layout would make learning the symbol grounding as trivial as recognizing one image for each symbol. We note that this also makes the policy itself harder to learn, as the agent cannot rely on a symbol always being associated with a fixed location. The
FlatWorld
environment
40
is a continuous 2D plane with continuous movement actions, where primitive symbols correspond to colored circular zones. The placement of the zones is randomly sampled at every episode. Differently to other works, we make sure the zones do not overlap, as to maintain the Declare assumption. More details are provided in Appendix
A.2
.

Tasks

Similar to
38
, we train on two kinds of formulae: Partially-Ordered Tasks, which are conjunction of sequences that can be executed in parallel, and Global Avoidance Tasks, which additionally require avoiding a certain atom for the entire task duration. The automata are required only for training and since translating formulae into automata is too computationally expensive to be done during training, we use a fixed training set of 10.000 formulae whose automata are precomputed. The generalization tasks employ either more sequences
(+conj.)
or longer sequences
(+dep.)
. More details are provided in Appendix
A.1
.

6
Conclusions

We have presented a method to jointly learn: (i) a policy that can satisfy many different LTL formulae; (ii) the mapping from raw observations to the atomic propositions that appear in such formulae. Our method shows little to no performance loss with respect to the upper bound of having access to the true symbol grounder, both on training formulae and on more complex unseen formulae. Moreover, it significantly improves over the previous state-of-the-art. This allows to drop the unrealistic assumption that the environment can provide the true symbol grounding, making multi-task RL applicable to sub-symbolic environments. We believe that this is an important step towards generally-capable agents that can follow many different and possibly unseen instructions directly from raw observations.

While the presented results are promising, several directions remain for future work. First, the robustness of the symbol grounder to raw observations, for instance by providing noisy observations or having symbols’ semantics beyond just being in a certain location.
Second, learning could benefit from reward shaping informed by task progression, particularly during early training when neither the policy nor the grounder produces meaningful behavior. For the same reason, curriculum learning, where easier formulae are trained first, could also be beneficial.
Finally, building on recent work on fuzzy logic conformance checking
16
;
2
could allow to train the symbol grounder without explicitly constructing the automata for the LTL formulae.

Appendix A
Experimental Details

A.1
Tasks

The set of LTL goals used for our experiments are generated following the syntactically co-safe LTL fragment, ensuring they are co-safe LTL formulae.

A.1.1
Partially-Ordered Tasks

Partially-Ordered Tasks consist of multiple sequences of propositions that can be satisfied in parallel: while the propositions within each sequence must be satisfied in order, the different sequences can be interleaved or solved simultaneously. They can be described with production rules of a context-free grammar:

formula

\displaystyle\;\;\rightarrow\;\;\text{sequence}\;|\;(\text{formula}\wedge\text{sequence})

sequence

\displaystyle\;\;\rightarrow\;\;\lozenge\text{term}\;|\;\lozenge(\text{term}\wedge\text{sequence})

term

\displaystyle\;\;\rightarrow\;\;\text{prop}\;|\;\text{prop}\vee\text{prop}

Where
prop
is a propositional atom from the environment’s set of propositions
\mathcal{P}
. The probability of a term being replaced with a disjunction of two atoms is of 25%.

The Minecraft-like environment is trained on tasks whose number of sequences is sampled between 1 and 4, while the length of each sequence is sampled between 1 and 5. The upward generalization tasks are: (i) tasks with increased depth from a maximum of 5 to a fixed 15 and (ii) tasks with increased number of conjunctions from a maximum of 4 to a fixed 12. The FlatWorld environment instead employs a single sequence of length between 1 and 3. The upward generalization tasks are: (i) tasks with increased depth from a maximum of 3 to a fixed 4 and (ii) tasks with increased number of conjunctions from a maximum of 1 to a fixed 2.

A.1.2
Global Avoidance Tasks

True global avoidance cannot be expressed as a co-safe property. However the construct
\neg a\mathbin{\mathcal{U}}b
can be concatenated to create sequential tasks where between two steps a certain proposition has to be avoided. The context-free grammar describing the conjunction of such sequences is:

\displaystyle\text{formula}\;\;\rightarrow\;\;

\displaystyle\text{sequence}\;|\;(\text{formula}\wedge\text{sequence})

\displaystyle\text{sequence}\;\;\rightarrow\;\;

\displaystyle(\text{avoidance}\;\mathbin{\mathcal{U}}\;\text{prop})\;|

\displaystyle(\text{avoidance}\;\mathbin{\mathcal{U}}\;(\text{prop}\wedge\text{sequence}))

\displaystyle\text{avoidance}\;\;\rightarrow\;\;

\displaystyle\neg\text{prop}

With the additional constraint of having the
avoidance
rule always using the same proposition, which instead cannot be used in the rest of the rules, we create a co-safe task where a certain proposition must be avoided for the duration of the task, but as soon as the sequential task is completed the avoidance is lifted and the formula is satisfied.

The Minecraft-like environment is trained on tasks whose number of sequences is sampled between 1 and 2, while the length of each sequence is sampled between 1 and 3. The upward generalization tasks are: (i) tasks with increased depth from a maximum of 3 to a fixed 5 and (ii) tasks with increased number of conjunctions from a maximum of 2 to a fixed 3.
The FlatWorld environment instead employs a single sequence of length between 1 and 2. The upward generalization tasks are: (i) tasks with increased depth from a maximum of 2 to a fixed 3 and (ii) tasks with increased number of conjunctions from a maximum of 1 to a fixed 2.

A.4
Effects of Pretraining

We investigate the effects of the LTL module pretraining on the downstream task learning process. The pretrainings considered are performed on the LTLBootcamp environment for
10
million frames using the pretraining hyperparameters from Table
4
and for both Partially-Ordered and Global Avoidance tasks the
base
configurations as in the standard trainings. The pretrained LTL Module is then transferred into a new agent trained on the symbolic Minecraft-like environment (with known labeling function and randomized maps) for 20 million frames following the hyperparameters from Table
4
. We compare 3 settings: (1) non-pretrained LTL module, (2) pretrained and fine-tunable LTL module and (3) pretrained and frozen LTL module. As shown in Figure
6
, using the pretrained LTL module, both frozen and fine-tunable, results in significantly faster convergence and therefore a higher sampling efficiency. This is particularly evident in the Global Avoidance tasks, where the non-pretrained agents show higher variability during training and not all runs manage to reach convergence within the
20
million frames, while all the pretrained agents are close to convergence within the first
5
million frames. This could denote that, despite being significantly shorter, the Global Avoidance tasks are inherently harder to solve, because of the possibility of failure. Focusing on the pretraining strategies, using a pretrained and frozen LTL module performs better in both settings. An intuition motivating this is that during the first training steps of the fine-tunable agent, when the other modules have only been initialized, the backpropagation partially disrupts the pretraining.
