Emerging topical nonsteroidal therapies in pediatric dermatology: atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, alopecia areata, vitiligo, and seborrheic dermatitis
Authors: Jordan Gillespie, Tracy Funk, Julie Dhossche, Liza Kenny, Alison Small
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of reviewTo review the current literature for updates on pediatric nonsteroidal topical therapies (NSTT) for the treatment of atopic dermatitis (AD), psoriasis, alopecia areata (AA), vitiligo, and seborrheic dermatitis (SD).Recent findingsThe FDA approved difamilast and expanded the approved age ranges for roflumilast, tapinarof, and ruxolitinib to treat AD. Recent studies also show positive results with delgocitinib. In the field of psoriasis, current research shows promise for tapinarof and roflumilast in an expanded age range. Among new AA treatments, relevant literature supports the use of both ruxolitinib and tofacitinib. Research in pediatric vitiligo is currently focused on roflumilast and latanoprost. The FDA approved age ranges for roflumilast in SD treatment was expanded, and recent data shows promising efficacy and safety with long term use.SummaryCurrent pediatric topical therapies are limited for the treatment of AD, psoriasis, AA, vitiligo, and SD. Historically, first line treatment options have included topical corticosteroids (TCS), though these have an increased risk of side effects in a pediatric population. Future clinical practices will likely rely less on TCS and more on NSTT for treatments of chronic pediatric dermatologic conditions.Keywords: nonsteroidal treatments, pediatric dermatology, topical treatments

INTRODUCTION

Topical corticosteroids remain a cornerstone of pediatric dermatology practice and are first line treatments for most chronic conditions. Potential adverse effects such as skin atrophy, striae, and telangiectasias necessitate careful prescribing practices, especially in pediatric populations who are at higher risk of side effects [1]. Additionally, parental concern regarding side effects has been shown to interfere with medication adherence, which negatively impacts disease control [2]. In recent years, there has been a focus on developing topical treatments with better tolerability and efficacy compared to their topical corticosteroids (TCS) counterparts. In this review, we aim to highlight updates in the literature regarding nonsteroidal topical therapies (NSTT) for the treatment of atopic dermatitis (AD), psoriasis, alopecia areata (AA), vitiligo, and seborrheic dermatitis (SD) to expand pediatric dermatology practices.

ATOPIC DERMATITIS

Atopic dermatitis is a chronic, inflammatory skin condition that classically develops at an early age [3]. There are a myriad of topical treatments available, with common Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved NSTT options including tacrolimus, pimecrolimus, and crisaborole [3]. Multiple new topical medications are currently being studied for AD treatment in pediatric populations (summarized in Table 1).

Summary of emerging NSTT for AD

Difamilast

Topical difamilast is a phosphodiesterase-4 (PDE4) inhibitor, with higher selectivity to PDE4B subtype, that was FDA approved for AD treatment in February of 2026 for ages 2 and older [4]. A recent phase 3 trial aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of difamilast ointment in infants aged 3–24 months [10▪▪]. In total, they enrolled 41 patients who completed a primary evaluation period of 4 weeks, with 36 infants continuing on to a long-term extension period lasting 48 weeks. During the primary evaluation, patients received 0.3% difamilast ointment twice daily, and after transitioning to the long-term extension, patients with Validated Investigator Global Assessment for AD (vIGA-AD) scores of two or more had the option to increase to 1% difamilast ointment. Results showed a 56.1% vIGA-AD response rate at week 4 and 75.6% at week 52 (defined as the percentage of patients who achieved a vIGA-AD score of 0 or 1 with at least a 2-point improvement from baseline). Additionally, 82.9% of patients achieved ≥75% reduction in Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI-75) at week 4 and 80.5% of patients achieved EASI-75 at week 52. There was one drug related adverse event (AE; folliculitis) in one patient, but otherwise most AEs were mild to moderate. No relevant abnormalities in laboratory results or vital signs were observed.

Roflumilast

Topical 0.05% roflumilast, a PDE4 inhibitor, was most recently approved in October of 2025 for AD treatment in patients as young as 2 years of age [5]. The INTEGUMENT-PED trial evaluated 0.05% roflumilast cream in patients aged 2–5 years [11▪]. Their cohort consisted of 602 patients, 410 who received 0.05% roflumilast cream once daily, and 192 who received a control cream. Endpoints included vIGA-AD Success (0 [Clear] or 1 [Almost Clear] plus ≥2-grade improvement), EASI-75, and WI-NRS Success (≥4-point improvement in Worst Itch-Numeric Rating Scale with baseline score ≥4). Results showed that a statistically significant number of participants achieved vIGA-AD success (P < 0.0001), EASI-75 (P < 0.0001), and WI-NRS success (P = 0.0002) at week 4 compared to control. Low rates of AEs were reported, with only five subjects discontinuing the trial due to an AE (application site discomfort, dermatitis, impetigo, neurodermatitis, and varicella). One serious AE was reported (cellulitis), though it was ultimately determined not to be related to the study drug.

Ruxolitinib

Ruxolitinib 1.5% cream, a topical Janus kinase (JAK) 1/2 inhibitor, was approved in patients 2 years and older by the FDA in September of 2025 [9]. The TRuE-AD3 trial, a phase 3 randomized controlled trial (RCT), evaluated the efficacy and safety of ruxolitinib cream in children aged 2–11 years old with AD [12▪]. 330 patients were enrolled, 134 of whom received 0.75% ruxolitinib cream, 131 who received 1.5% ruxolitinib cream, and 65 who received a control. Results at week 8 showed that a statistically significant number of patients achieved Investigator Global Assessment Treatment Success (IGA-TS; score of 0 [clear] or 1 [almost clear] with ≥ 2 grade improvement) with both dosages of ruxolitinib compared to control (P = 0.0001). Most AEs were classified as mild to moderate and resolved within 1–4 days, though two patients discontinued the medication due to these events (application site reaction, AD worsening). No serious AEs were reported, and the overall safety profile was found to mirror what had already been observed in prior adult/adolescent trials.

Delgocitinib

Delgocitinib is a topical JAK 1/2/3 and TYK2 inhibitor approved in Japan for AD in ages 6 years and older (FDA approved in adults in the United States) [13,7]. A posthoc analysis from two separate topical delgocitinib trials examined the safety and efficacy of the 0.25% and 0.5% dosages [8]. This analysis showed that the following proportions of patients with mild AD achieved a vIGA-AD score of 0 or 1: 46.2% (0.25% ointment), 71.4% (0.5% ointment), and 7.7% (control). In subjects with moderate to severe disease, the following percentages were observed: 19% (0.25% ointment), 20% (0.5% ointment), and 0% (control). There were no differences observed in the safety profiles of either dose when used in conjunction with other topical treatments.

Tapinarof

Tapinarof, a topical aryl hydrocarbon receptor agonist, was approved in December of 2024 for the treatment of AD in patients 2 years of age and older [6]. Recently, one study aimed to further evaluate the safety and pharmacokinetic properties of 1% tapinarof cream in pediatric patients [14]. A total of 36 patients enrolled and 32 completed the trial, with ages ranging from 2–17 years. They were instructed to apply 1% tapinarof cream every day for 4 weeks. Eight patients reported mild to moderate AEs, and one patient discontinued the trial due to their reaction (headache and vomiting). No serious AEs were observed, and no clinically relevant laboratory test results or vital sign changes were observed. Regarding pharmacokinetics, none to minimal systemic drug exposure was observed. The mean maximum plasma concentration was 2.44 ng/ml, and the median time to achieve this concentration was 2.9 h. Therefore, the study concluded that there were minimal concerns with systemic drug absorption in pediatric populations.

PSORIASIS

Psoriasis is an immune mediated disease that can affect the skin, nails, and joints that often develops in childhood [15]. Current FDA approved NSTT include vitamin D analogues (calcipotriene, calcitriol, and calcipotriol), calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus), and anthralin [16]. Though numerous NSTT are available, current research is evaluating novel treatments in order to expand available options (summarized in Table 2).

Summary of emerging NSTT for psoriasis

Roflumilast

Roflumilast cream is currently approved by the FDA for treatment of plaque psoriasis in individuals 6 years and older [5]. Recently, a 4 week, phase 2, open-label trial investigated daily roflumilast 0.3% cream for a treatment duration of 28 days in patients 2–5 years of age with plaque psoriasis [17▪]. Among the 10 patient cohort, 90% achieved Investigator Global Assessment (IGA) success (clear or almost clear plus ≥2 grade improvement), 90% achieved a 75% or greater reduction in their Psoriasis Area and Severity Index score (PASI-75), and 90% achieved WI-NRS success (≥4 point reduction with baseline score of ≥4) by week 4. Mean reduction in body surface area (BSA) involvement was 79.1%. Systemic exposure to roflumilast and its metabolite roflumilast N-oxide was observed, and when normalized by dose, plasma concentrations were similar to prior adult studies. Specifically, plasma concentrations at week 2 were 2.15 ng/ml (roflumilast) and 22.4 (roflumilast N-oxide) for ages 2–5 years and 3.15 ng/ml and 28.9 for ages 6–11 years. At week four, plasma concentrations were 2.04 ng/ml and 15.8 for ages 2–5 years and 1.68 ng/ml and 15.7 for ages 6–11 years. These results suggest that no dosage change is indicated in pediatric patients. One patient did note an AE (headache), but this did not cause drug discontinuation.

Once daily roflumilast topical foam was recently approved by the FDA in May of 2025 for the treatment of plaque psoriasis in individuals 12 years of age and older [18]. The phase III ARRECTOR trial supported this by investigating daily 0.3% roflumilast foam for 8 weeks compared to vehicle for scalp and body psoriasis in individuals 12 years and older [19]. They enrolled 432 total patients (281 in the experimental arm, 151 in the control arm). By week 8, 66.4% of the experimental group achieved Scalp-Investigator Global Assessment success (clear [0] or almost clear [1] plus ≥2-grade improvement) while only 27.8% of the vehicle group achieved it (P < 0.001). Conversely, 45.5% of the roflumilast group achieved Body-Investigator Global Assessment Success (same definition as above) versus 20.1% of the control group (P < 0.001). Treatment related AEs were reported by 5.7% of the experimental group, and treatment-emergent serious AEs were reported by two patients (gastritis, possibly roflumilast related).

Tapinarof

Tapinarof has yet to be FDA approved for use in pediatric psoriasis, but is approved in AD [6]. An open-label, multicenter, phase 3 study is currently evaluating daily 1% tapinarof cream in individuals aged 2–17 years of age with plaque psoriasis (NCT05172726). Primary outcomes include drug safety profile (AEs, significant laboratory test or vital sign abnormalities), investigator-assessed local tolerability scale, and subject (or caregiver)-assessed local tolerability scale. The study is estimated to be completed in October of 2026.

ALOPECIA AREATA

AA is a nonscarring autoimmune disorder that results in partial to complete hair loss.[20] Current off label NSTT include minoxidil and topical immunotherapy [diphenylcyclopropenone (DPCP) or squaric acid dibutyl ester (SADBE)] [20]. Summary of emerging NSTT for AA

Ruxolitinib

Ruxolitinib cream is used off label for AA treatment [9]. One case reports on a 5 year old female with AA that was recalcitrant to clobetasol [23]. She was started on ruxolitinib 1.5% topical cream twice daily, fluocinonide 0.05% topical solution once daily, and oral fexofenadine 30 mg twice daily. At her 3-month follow-up, all areas of alopecia besides two showed new growth. At her 6-month follow-up, she discontinued fluocinonide and fexofenadine but continued ruxolitinib. At her 12-month follow-up, all alopecic lesions showed diffuse growth, and she did not endorse any medication side effects.

Tofacitinib

Tofacitinib is a JAK inhibitor that is used off label for AA treatment [22]. One recent study enrolled 30 patients with AA ranging from 4 to 43 years of age [22]. They were treated with 2% tofacitinib citrate ointment applied twice daily to alopecic patches. Results showed a significant reduction in the Severity of Alopecia Tool (SALT) score at the 6-month follow-up. On further examination, 40% of patients showed an excellent treatment response (>50% improvement in SALT score at 24 weeks) while 30% of patients had no treatment response. Among the nine pediatric patients enrolled, five had an excellent treatment response while one had an intermediate response (25–50% improvement in SALT at 24 weeks). There were no AEs reported in either the pediatric or adult cohorts.

VITILIGO

Vitiligo is an autoimmune disorder resulting in skin depigmentation that can develop in childhood [24,25]. Current NSTT include tacrolimus, pimecrolimus (both used off label), and ruxolitinib (FDA approved), though multiple novel agents are currently being evaluated (summarized in Table 4) [25].

Summary of emerging NSTT for vitiligo

Roflumilast

Roflumilast cream is not currently FDA approved for vitiligo treatment, but is approved for AD [5]. One pediatric case series notes successful treatment of facial vitiligo with 0.3% roflumilast [26]. In all, there were four patients with vitiligo that was recalcitrant to a plethora of treatments including triamcinolone cream, laser (Xtrac; Strata Skin Sciences), 0.05% clobetasol ointment, imiquimod cream, 1.5% ruxolitinib cream, 0.05% desonide cream, calcipotriene and betamethasone cream, and 2.5% hydrocortisone. After using 0.3% roflumilast cream daily on the face, all patients had moderate improvement to near complete resolution of their facial vitiligo. No AEs were noted.

Latanoprost

Latanoprost, a prostaglandin F2α analog, is not currently FDA approved for vitiligo treatment [27]. A recent case study noted two children with eyelid vitiligo who were treated with 0.005% latanoprost ophthalmic solution applied twice daily to areas of depigmentation using a cotton swab [27]. By week 16, both patients showed at least 75% repigmentation. Additionally, one phase 4 study is currently evaluating the effect of Narrowband Ultraviolet B (NB-UVB), 0.005% latanoprost, and dual treatment with each agent on the tumor necrosis factor weak inducer of apoptosis (TWEAK) serum level in adult and pediatric patients with nonsegmental vitiligo (NCT04811326). The primary outcome is TWEAK serum levels after 3 months of treatment while the secondary outcome is a change in vitiligo. The study was estimated to have been completed in March of 2024, but results have yet to be published.

SEBORRHEIC DERMATITIS

SD is a chronic, inflammatory skin condition characterized by erythematous, greasy, yellow to white plaques that occurs in regions rich in sebaceous glands [28]. Current NSTT treatments include topical antifungal agents (multiple FDA approved options), calcineurin-inhibitors (used off label), sulfacetamide (FDA approved), and pyrithione (FDA approved) [29]. Summary of emerging NSTT for SD

Roflumilast

Roflumilast foam, 0.3% used once daily, was first FDA approved to treat seborrheic dermatitis in individuals 9 years of age and older in December of 2023 [18]. Subsequently, a phase II trial evaluated the safety and efficacy of roflumilast at FDA approved dosing for the treatment of SD [30]. They enrolled 457 patients, of which 32 were children aged 9–17 years. Results showed statistically significant changes in IGA scores within the roflumilast group compared to the vehicle group at weeks 2 and 4. Eight patients in the roflumilast group (2.6% of the total study population) reported an AE that was considered treatment related (application-site pruritus, diarrhea, application-site pain, acne, nausea, abdominal pain).

Additionally, a recent phase II trial also evaluated the safety and efficacy of roflumilast foam in patients 12 years of age and older [31]. Four hundred patients (13 aged 12–17 years) were instructed to apply roflumilast 0.3% foam daily for 24 weeks, and 62 continued the treatment for a total of 52 weeks. There were low rates of treatment related AEs (5.5%; GI upset, headache). Regarding efficacy, 56.4% of the total patients achieved IGA0/1 at week 4, and 76% achieved this at week 24. For the long-term treatment cohort, 80.5% achieved IGA0/1 by week 52. Additionally, the proportion of patients who achieved this endpoint at week 24 was consistent throughout pediatric and adult cohorts.

CONCLUSION

There have been great advancements regarding NSTT in pediatric dermatology. For AD treatment, difamilast was recently FDA approved and roflumilast, tapinarof, and ruxolitinib were recently FDA approved for an expanded age range. New NSTT currently being studied include delgocitinib, and results are promising. For the treatment of psoriasis, current studies are investigating tapinarof and roflumilast cream in a wider age range. For AA, topical ruxolitinib has been shown to be effective in case reports, and trials evaluating tofacitinib had promising results. For vitiligo, current research is focused on roflumilast and latanoprost. Lastly, for SD treatment, the FDA expanded the age ranges on roflumilast, and studies show promising efficacy/safety for long term use.
