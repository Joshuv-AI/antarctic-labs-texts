MIR31HG Modulates IL‐4 Secretion by Keratinocytes and M2‐Like Macrophage Polarization to Promote Local Immunosuppression in Condyloma Acuminata
Authors: Xiaohang Xie, Lian Liu, Huayu Huang, Yuchun Cao, Zhichao Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCondyloma acuminata (CA) is clinically associated with localized immunosuppression, yet the mechanisms underlying HPV‐mediated immune evasion remain poorly understood. Although the long non‐coding RNA MIR31HG has been implicated in inflammatory regulation, its role in the immunosuppressive microenvironment of CA has not been explored.MethodsWe analyzed MIR31HG expression in CA and normal skin tissues. HaCaT cells infected with CA‐derived HPV6/11 (HPV6/11‐HaCaT) and controls treated with skin‐derived supernatant were established to explore the association between HPV6/11 and MIR31HG. The HPV6/11‐HaCaT model was further used to investigate the effects of MIR31HG on cytokine secretion and macrophage polarization. Bioinformatic prediction, dual‐luciferase reporter assays and rescue experiments targeting PI3K/AKT and IL‐4 signaling were performed to investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms.ResultsMIR31HG was significantly upregulated in CA keratinocytes compared with normal skin. In vitro, HPV6/11 induced the expression of MIR31HG. Functional studies demonstrated that MIR31HG induced a cytokine shift in HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells, characterized by elevated anti‐inflammatory IL‐4 and reduced pro‐inflammatory mediators, including TNF‐α, IFN‐β, IL‐6 and IL‐8. This IL‐4 surge promoted M2‐like macrophage polarization, a hallmark of immunosuppression, thereby contributing to an immunosuppressive microenvironment in CA. Mechanistically, MIR31HG functioned as a ceRNA for miR‐214‐3p in HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells, leading to enhanced PTEN expression and subsequent suppression of the PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby contributing to an immunosuppressive cytokine profile and M2‐like macrophage polarization.ConclusionOur findings highlight the immunomodulatory role of keratinocytes in CA and highlight the MIR31HG/IL‐4 axis as a novel therapeutic target for CA, bridging mechanistic insight with translational potential.

Introduction

Condyloma acuminata (CA), a prevalent sexually transmitted disease, is primarily driven by infection with low‐risk human papillomavirus (HPV) types 6 and 11 [1]. Contemporary therapeutic modalities fail to prevent disease recurrence, and the condition carries a latent risk of malignant transformation [2, 3]. The recurrence and progression of CA are closely linked to localized immunosuppression, which involves impaired immune cell functions and altered cytokine production [4, 5, 6]. In particular, patients with CA exhibit reduced levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐2, IL‐12 and IFN‐γ, alongside elevated levels of anti‐inflammatory cytokines like IL‐4, contributing to a shift towards an immune‐tolerant environment [7, 8]. This imbalance, together with a predominance of M2‐like macrophages, creates conditions that favor viral persistence and disease progression [9, 10]. Despite advances in understanding HPV‐induced immune evasion, the molecular mechanisms linking viral infection to these immunosuppressive features remain poorly understood.

Long non‐coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as critical regulators of diverse cellular processes, including immune responses [11, 12]. Microarray data reanalysis (GEO accession number: GSE140662) revealed significant upregulation of lncRNA MIR31HG in keratinocytes from CA lesions compared with normal skin tissues. MIR31HG has previously been implicated in cancer and inflammatory disorders and has been reported to regulate cell proliferation, apoptosis and macrophage polarization [13, 14, 15]. Notably, in rheumatoid arthritis, MIR31HG has been shown to suppress the secretion of TNF‐α, IL‐6 and IL‐8 while attenuating macrophage‐driven inflammation, suggesting a broader role in immune regulation [16]. These findings are consistent with the cytokine imbalance and M2‐like macrophage predominance observed in CA, leading us to hypothesize that MIR31HG may contribute to local immunosuppression in HPV‐infected keratinocytes.

MIR31HG has been linked to the PI3K/AKT pathway, a central regulator of cell proliferation, apoptosis, metabolism, transcription, protein synthesis and immune signaling [17]. In rheumatoid arthritis, MIR31HG inhibits PI3K/AKT activation to curb inflammation, whereas in cancers, it either suppresses or enhances the PI3K/AKT pathway depending on the context [16]. In addition, MIR31HG can inhibit nasopharyngeal carcinoma cell proliferation and promote apoptosis through the PI3K/AKT pathway [18]. In non‐small cell lung cancer, increased MIR31HG expression results in gefitinib resistance through the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway [19]. However, the relationship between MIR31HG and the PI3K/AKT pathway in shaping the immunosuppressive milieu in CA remains unexplored.

In this study, we investigated MIR31HG expression in CA tissues and explored its functional role using an HPV6/11‐infected HaCaT cell model. We interrogated whether MIR31HG drove cytokine skewing (e.g., IL‐4 upregulation) and M2 macrophage polarization through the PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby contributing to local immunosuppression in CA. Our findings aim to bridge the gap between HPV infection, lncRNA‐mediated immune dysregulation, and therapeutic strategies targeting the immunosuppressive niche in CA.

Materials and Methods

Data Processing and Bioinformatic Analysis

The data of GSE140662 was downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and then was analyzed by R software. Non‐coding RNAs were deemed differentially expressed according to fold change > 2 and adjusted p < 0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using Molecular Signatures Database. Differences were considered as statistically significant at p < 0.05 and adjusted p < 0.25. DEGs correlated with MIR31HG were identified based on fold change > 2, p < 0.05 and spearman correlation coefficient > 0.75 and were subsequently subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. Biological Process terms were used to characterize the biological functions associated with MIR31HG‐related DEGs. Potential miRNAs binding to MIR31HG were predicted using ENCORI, LncBase, miRanda, RNAhybrid and PITA, and overlapping results were selected as candidate miRNAs. The main parameters were set as follows: LncBase score ≥ 0.8, miRanda score ≥ 140 and free energy ≤ −25 kcal/mol with strict seed matching, RNAhybrid free energy ≤ −25 kcal/mol, and PITA binding score ≤ −10 kcal/mol.

Human Tissues Collection

The diagnosis of CA was confirmed according to physical examination and positive detection of HPV6/11 DNA by PCR. CA tissues (n = 77) were obtained from patients at the Department of Dermatology, Tongji Hospital of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Wuhan, China). Normal skin tissues (n = 44) were collected from healthy individuals undergoing circumcision at the Department of Urology of Tongji Hospital. Due to the relatively small size of CA wart tissues, each tissue sample was generally sufficient for only one experimental analysis. This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Tongji Hospital (TJ‐IRB202409010).

Fluorescence in Situ Hybridization (FISH)

MIR31HG FISH probe was synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Probe sequences: 5‐DIG‐ACCUUGUGAAUCAUCACUGCUGAGGAAAUAAC‐3. Briefly, paraffin embedded tissue slides were prehybridized and incubated with FISH probe solution for 12 h. FITC‐Tyramide was used for visualization. Nuclei were labeled with DAPI. Slides of Control‐HaCaT and HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and then treated in a similar way according to the instructions.

Reverse Transcription and Real‑Time Quantitative PCR Analysis (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA from tissues and cells was extracted using TRIzol solution (Takara, Japan). qRT‐PCR was performed using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan) and TB Green Fast qPCR Mix (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Data were collected with CFX96 C1000 Thermal Cycler (Bio‐Rad, USA) and analyzed with the comparative Ct (2‑ΔΔCt) method. PCR primers were listed in Table S1.

Methylation Specific PCR (MSP)

The promoter region of MIR31HG was predicted from gene sequence obtained from NCBI (accession number NC_000009.12). MethPrimer was used to predict promoter‐associated CpG islands and design MSP primers (https://www.methprimer.com/). Genomic DNA was extracted using universal genomic DNA purification kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and then subjected to bisulfite conversion (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Bisulfite‐modified DNA samples were PCR‐amplified using either methylated or unmethylated primers (Takara, Japan). The PCR products were subsequently separated and visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis. MSP primers were listed in Table S2.

Cell Culture

HaCaT, THP‐1 and 293T cells were purchased from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC, Wuhan, China). Cells were checked for bacteria, fungus, mycoplasma and short tandem repeat profiling in CCTCC and were cultured according to their instructions. HaCaT, THP‐1 and 293T were cultured for a maximum of 20 passages.

Construction of HPV6/11‐HaCaT Cells

Viral suspensions were handled in a certified biosafety level‐2 facility. To simulate HPV6/11 infected keratinocytes, CA tissues were ground with 1 mL sterile PBS and then were centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was concentrated by Amicon Ultra (Merck, Germany) and the > 3 kDa fraction was further filtrated with 0.22 µm syringe filters (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). HaCaT cells were cultured with 20 µL supernatant for 48 h. HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells were selected according to the positive detection by HPV6/11 nucleic acid assay kit (Liferiver, Shanghai, China). HaCaT cells with Ct values ≤ 38 and a typical S‐shaped amplification curve were considered positive for HPV6/11 infection. As a control, HaCaT cells were treated in parallel with supernatants prepared from normal skin tissues and processed under identical conditions to exclude the potential effects of soluble tissue‐derived factors.

Preparation of Macrophages

THP‐1 cells were seeded into 12‐well plates at a density of 6.5 × 105 cells/well and treated with PMA (200 ng/mL, Abcam, UK) for 48 h, which were regarded as M0 macrophages. Co‐culture experiments were performed after resting for 24 h.

Co‐culture Assay

Co‐culture assay was performed using transwell 12‐well plates (Corning, USA). M0 macrophages were cultured in the lower compartment and HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells were cultured in the upper compartment. Changes of macrophages were analyzed after co‐culture for 48 h.

Overexpression of MIR31HG and miR‐214‐3p

MIR31HG sequences were inserted into the pcDNA3.1 vector, and the plasmids were constructed by General Biotech (Anhui, China). MIR31HG plasmids and the empty pcDNA3.1 vector were transfected into HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells using lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo, USA). After 24 h, the expression of MIR31HG was detected by qRT‐PCR. miR‐214‐3p mimics and the corresponding negative control mimics were purchased from Ribo Biotech (Guangzhou, China) and transfected into HPV6/11‐HaCaT or 293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the same protocol.

Downregulation of MIR31HG and IL‐4

IL‐4 siRNA and MIR31HG smart silencer containing three siRNAs and three ASOs were synthesized by General Biotech (Anhui, China). HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells were transfected with siRNA using lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After 24 h, the efficiency of knockdown was detected by qRT‐PCR. Sequences of siRNAs were listed in Table S3.

Western Blot Analysis

Total protein was extracted using RIPA solution (Thermo, USA) supplemented with cocktail and phosphorylated protease inhibitor (MedChemExpress, USA). Briefly, protein was separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) using a Bio‐Rad wet transfer system. Enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Abbkine, Wuhan, China) was used for visualization. Antibodies were listed in Table S4.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC was performed as described previously [20]. Paraffin‐embedded tissues were sectioned in 3‐µm‐thick slices and mounted on glass slides. Antibodies were listed in Table S4. DAB kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was used for coloration reaction. IHC score was assessed by 2 different pathologists. The total score for each image was derived from the staining intensity score multiplied by the staining area percentage score. A total score of > 8 was considered as high IL‐4 expression, otherwise as low IL‐4 expression.

ELISA

Supernatants were collected 48 h after treatment or transfection and used for subsequent IL‐4 ELISA assay (Dakewe Biotech, Shenzhen, China). All steps were performed according to the instructions.

Treatment With Dupilumab and IL‐4 for Rescue Experiment

20 µg/mL dupilumab (Selleck, USA) was added into cell media of macrophages at M0 stage and co‐culture stage for rescue experiment. HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells were transfected with MIR31HG smart silencer and then co‐cultured with M0 macrophages with 20 ng/mL IL‐4 (R&D Systems, USA) in the cell media of co‐culture system.

Dual‐luciferase Reporter Assay

The pmirGLO reporter plasmids containing the full‐length MIR31HG sequence or the mutant binding sequence were synthesized by General Biotech (Anhui, China). The reporter plasmids and miRNA mimics were co‐transfected into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo, USA). After 48 h of transfection, luciferase activity was measured using a Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Yeasen, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Treatment With LY294002 for Rescue Experiment

To deactivate PI3K, 20 µM LY294002 (MedChemExpress, USA) was added into the media of HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells undergoing MIR31HG smart silencer transfection. Control group was treated with carrier solvent (0.2% DMSO).

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v9.0. Data were presented as mean ± SEM. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and variance homogeneity tests, respectively. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired Student's t‐test, Welch's t‐test, or Mann–Whitney U test was applied as appropriate. For comparisons among three groups, one‐way ANOVA, Welch's ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test followed by appropriate post hoc tests was performed as appropriate. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

MIR31HG Is Upregulated in CA Keratinocytes and Linked to Inflammatory Responses

Noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) exhibit diverse biological functions in human diseases [21]. To investigate ncRNA expression patterns in CA, we performed a reanalysis of our public microarray data and revealed 11 significantly upregulated ncRNAs in CA, including lncRNA MIR31HG (Figure 1a). GO enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) correlated with MIR31HG were significantly enriched in pathways related to innate immune response, cytokine‐mediated signaling pathway and response to virus, among others (Figure 1b). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) showed that MIR31HG was associated with inflammatory responses that play a critical role in HPV‐associated diseases. Therefore, MIR31HG was selected as a candidate lncRNA potentially involved in immune regulation in CA (Figure 1c,d). Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis revealed that MIR31HG was predominantly localized in the cytoplasm of CA keratinocytes, with minimal expression observed in control tissues (Figure 1e). Subsequently, MIR31HG expression was quantified by qRT‐PCR, revealing a significant increase in CA compared to normal skin tissues (Figure 1f). Collectively, these results demonstrated that MIR31HG was aberrantly overexpressed in CA keratinocytes and may play a potential role in shaping the local immunosuppressive microenvironment. In addition, previous studies have identified CpG islands within the MIR31HG promoter and linked MIR31HG regulation to promoter hypermethylation in triple‐negative breast cancer [22]. However, methylation specific PCR (MSP) revealed comparable promoter methylation status of MIR31HG between CA and normal skin tissues, suggesting that MIR31HG upregulation in CA may occur independently of promoter DNA methylation (Figure S1a,b).

MIR31HG Expression Is Induced in HaCaT Cells Infected With CA‐Derived HPV6/11

The elevated expression of MIR31HG in CA prompted us to investigate the potential relationship between MIR31HG and HPV infection. To this end, we developed an in vitro model by exposing HaCaT cells to supernatant derived from CA tissues. Fresh CA warts were homogenized in PBS, followed by centrifugation and filtration to obtain a virus‐enriched supernatant. The control group was established in a similar manner using supernatant derived from normal skin tissues (Figure 2a). HaCaT cells treated with CA‐derived supernatant (HPV6/11‐HaCaT) tested positive for HPV6/11 DNA, whereas the control cells (Control‐HaCaT) remained negative, indicating successful establishment of the HPV6/11‐HaCaT model (Figure 2b). Moreover, HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells remained positive for HPV6/11 after five days of in vitro culture (Figure 2c). FISH analysis revealed that MIR31HG was predominantly localized in the cytoplasm of both HPV6/11‐HaCaT and Control‐HaCaT cells, consistent with the findings observed in CA and normal skin tissues (Figure 2d). Additionally, MIR31HG expression was significantly higher in HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells than in Control‐HaCaT cells, suggesting that HPV6/11 exposure induced MIR31HG expression in vitro (Figure 2e).

Effects of MIR31HG on Cytokine Secretion and Macrophage Polarization

To investigate the role of MIR31HG in CA, HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells were transfected with MIR31HG smart silencer (SS‐MIR31HG) or negative control (SS‐NC), and the knockdown efficiency was validated by qRT‐PCR (Figure 3a). Additionally, HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells overexpressing MIR31HG (OE‐MIR31HG) were generated by transfection with a MIR31HG overexpression plasmid, while the control group (OE‐Vector) was transfected with the empty vector plasmid (Figure 3b). We first examined pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) involved in antiviral immune responses, including TLR3, TLR9, RIG‐I and cGAS‐STING, to investigate whether different MIR31HG expression levels affected PRR expression in HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells (Figure S2a,b). Subsequently, we assessed the cytokine profiles secreted by HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells with varying MIR31HG expression levels. The results revealed that MIR31HG suppressed the expression of TNF‐α, IFN‐β, IL‐6 and IL‐8, while enhancing IL‐4 expression (Figure 3c,d). Considering that macrophage polarization is influenced by specific cytokines, we further investigated whether MIR31HG‐mediated cytokine alterations affected macrophage polarization. HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells with different MIR31HG expression levels were co‐cultured with THP‐1‐induced M0 macrophages for 48 h (Figure S3). The expression of M1‐like and M2‐like macrophage markers was analyzed by qRT‐PCR. The results demonstrated that MIR31HG upregulated the expression of M2‐like markers, such as CD206 and CCL17, without affecting M1‐like markers, including CD86 and CXCL10 (Figure 3e,f). Consistently, western blot analysis further confirmed increased expression of M2‐like markers in macrophages co‐cultured with HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells exhibiting high MIR31HG expression (Figure 3 g,h).

IL‐4 Is Upregulated in CA Keratinocytes and HPV6/11‐HaCaT Cells

Macrophages are known to exhibit different phenotypes depending on the local microenvironment. Pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF‐α, IFN‐γ and IL‐6, steer macrophages toward an M1‐like phenotype, whereas anti‐inflammatory cytokines, like IL‐4 and IL‐13, favor M2‐like polarization [23]. Our study demonstrated that MIR31HG promoted IL‐4 secretion from keratinocytes and drove M2‐like macrophage polarization. To elucidate the mechanism by which MIR31HG promoted M2‐like macrophage polarization, we examined the expression of IL‐4 in CA and normal skin tissues. As shown in Figure 4a, CA tissues exhibited significantly elevated IL‐4 expression compared to normal skin tissues. Moreover, the increased IL‐4 expression was positively associated with a longer diameter of CA warts (Figure 4b). Both qRT‐PCR and western blot analyses confirmed that IL‐4 mRNA and protein levels were markedly increased in CA tissues (Figure 4c,d). Similarly, compared with Control‐HaCaT cells, HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells exhibited significantly increased IL‐4 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figure 4e–g).

MIR31HG Promotes M2‐Like Macrophage Polarization by Inducing IL‐4 Secretion From HPV6/11‐HaCaT Cells

Next, we assessed the effect of MIR31HG on IL‐4 secretion in HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells using ELISA and western blot assays, which showed that MIR31HG significantly enhanced IL‐4 secretion (Figure 5a–d). To further investigate whether MIR31HG‐induced IL‐4 upregulation was responsible for promoting M2‐like macrophage polarization, we first transfected HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells with a MIR31HG overexpression plasmid. Subsequently, we downregulated IL‐4 expression in these MIR31HG‐overexpressing cells by transfection with IL‐4 siRNA (Figure 5e). These treated HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells were then co‐cultured with THP‐1‐derived M0 macrophages. Analysis of M2‐like macrophage markers using qRT‐PCR and western blot assays revealed that IL‐4 knockdown substantially attenuated MIR31HG‐induced M2‐like macrophage polarization (Figure 5f,g). We further employed dupilumab, an anti‐IL‐4‐receptor‐α monoclonal antibody, to inhibit IL‐4 signaling. As shown in Figure 5h and i, dupilumab markedly inhibited IL‐4‐induced M2‐like macrophage polarization. Moreover, dupilumab treatment attenuated MIR31HG‐induced M2‐like macrophage polarization (Figure 5j,k). Consistently, IL‐4 treatment reversed the inhibition of M2‐like macrophage polarization caused by MIR31HG knockdown (Figure 5l,m). Overall, these findings suggested that MIR31HG promoted M2‐like macrophage polarization by inducing IL‐4 secretion from HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells.

MIR31HG Regulates Cytokine Secretion and M2‐Like Macrophage Polarization via miR‐214‐3p/PTEN/PI3K/AKT Axis

Cytoplasmic lncRNAs can function as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) by acting as “molecular sponges” that sequester miRNAs, thereby relieving miRNA‐mediated suppression of target mRNAs and regulating downstream gene expression [24]. We predicted potential miRNAs that could bind to MIR31HG and integrated the results from multiple databases to identify overlapping candidates. Among them, miR‐214‐3p was selected as the most promising candidate for further investigation (Figure 6a). Binding site prediction using miRanda software revealed multiple potential binding sites between MIR31HG and miR‐214‐3p (Figure 6b). Co‐transfection of MIR31HG wild‐type (WT) reporter plasmids and miR‐214‐3p mimics significantly reduced luciferase activity, whereas mutation (MT) of the predicted binding sites abolished this effect, confirming the direct interaction between MIR31HG and miR‐214‐3p (Figure 6c). Furthermore, we explored the function of miR‐214‐3p in HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells. The results showed that miR‐214‐3p exerted effects opposite to those of MIR31HG in HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells, further supporting the ceRNA role of MIR31HG in regulating miR‐214‐3p (Figure S4a‐d).

Phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) is a well‐established downstream target of miR‐214‐3p [25, 26]. In this study, miRanda software was used to predict the binding sites between miR‐214‐3p and the 3’ untranslated region (3’ UTR) of PTEN (Figure 6d). Subsequently, HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells were transfected with miR‐214‐3p mimics or mimics NC, and PTEN expression was analyzed by western blot. The results demonstrated that PTEN expression was significantly reduced in the miR‐214‐3p mimics group compared with the mimics NC group (Figure 6e). We also examined the effects of MIR31HG knockdown or overexpression on PTEN expression in HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells. MIR31HG knockdown reduced PTEN expression, whereas MIR31HG overexpression enhanced PTEN expression (Figure 6f,g). These findings suggest that MIR31HG indirectly promotes PTEN expression by competitively binding to miR‐214‐3p.

PTEN possesses phosphatase activity and can dephosphorylate phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5‐trisphosphate (PIP3) into phosphatidylinositol 4,5‐bisphosphate (PIP2), thereby negatively regulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [27]. In addition, previous studies have established that MIR31HG exerts its effects via the PI3K/AKT pathway [18, 19]. In this study, we investigated the activation status of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells with different levels of miR‐214‐3p and MIR31HG expression. The results showed that miR‐214‐3p enhanced PI3K and AKT phosphorylation, whereas MIR31HG inhibited PI3K and AKT phosphorylation (Figure 6h–j). Interestingly, despite inhibiting PI3K/AKT activation, MIR31HG simultaneously enhanced IL‐4 production, suggesting that suppression of this pathway may trigger compensatory activation of transcriptional regulators in HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells. To further clarify the involvement of the PI3K/AKT pathway, we treated HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells with LY294002, a PI3K inhibitor, to block the PI3K/AKT pathway. As shown in Figure 6j, LY294002 treatment effectively reversed the increased phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT induced by MIR31HG knockdown. Additionally, HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells transfected with MIR31HG smart silencer exhibited altered cytokine secretion following LY294002 treatment (Figure 6k). We then assessed IL‐4 expression using ELISA and western blot assays, which revealed that LY294002 significantly rescued IL‐4 secretion suppressed by MIR31HG knockdown in HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells (Figure 6l,m). In addition, LY294002 also reversed the inhibition of M2‐like macrophage polarization caused by MIR31HG knockdown (Figure 6n,o). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that MIR31HG regulates cytokine secretion and promotes M2‐like macrophage polarization through the miR‐214‐3p/PTEN/PI3K/AKT axis.

Discussion

CA warts are primarily characterized by pathological hyperproliferation of keratinocytes. These keratinocytes not only maintain the structure of warts, but also modulate the immune microenvironment through the secretion of multifunctional cytokines, such as chemokines, antimicrobial peptides and inflammatory mediators [28, 29, 30]. The recurrence and progression of CA are closely associated with the establishment of a local immunosuppressive microenvironment, in which keratinocytes, as key immunocompetent cells in the skin, are likely to play a critical regulatory role. Our study revealed marked upregulation of lncRNA MIR31HG in CA keratinocytes, which promoted the production of the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐4 while suppressing pro‐inflammatory mediators such as TNF‐α, IFN‐β, IL‐6 and IL‐8. These findings are consistent with previous clinical observations reporting elevated IL‐4 levels in both peripheral circulation and warts of CA patients [7, 31].

IL‐4 is a classical Th2‐type cytokine that is primarily produced by activated CD4+ T cells. Under specific conditions, macrophages, eosinophils, basophils and mast cells can also secrete IL‐4 to varying degrees [32]. Traditionally, keratinocytes have not been regarded as a major source of IL‐4, and thus the potential contribution of keratinocyte‐derived IL‐4 to local cutaneous immune regulation has largely been overlooked. In the present study, we found that IL‐4 expression was significantly upregulated in both CA keratinocytes and HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells, suggesting that HPV6/11 infection may induce keratinocytes to produce IL‐4. This finding highlights a previously underappreciated immunomodulatory role of keratinocytes in the CA microenvironment and suggests that keratinocytes may actively participate in the establishment of a local immunosuppressive milieu, rather than merely serving as structural target cells for HPV infection. Given the critical role of IL‐4 in promoting Th2‐skewed immune responses and immune tolerance, keratinocyte‐derived IL‐4 may contribute to viral immune evasion and the persistent progression or recurrence of CA.

Macrophage polarization is critically regulated by the local cytokine milieu, and IL‐4 is well established as an inducer of M2‐like macrophage polarization [33]. M2‐like macrophages have been reported to predominate in several virus‐associated diseases, where they facilitate immune evasion and persistent infection. For instance, M2‐like macrophages have been shown to promote viral replication and the establishment of latent infection in herpes simplex virus type 1 infection models [34]. Moreover, the infiltration of M2‐like macrophages has been positively associated with HPV16 E6/E7 mRNA expression and the severity of cervical lesions, suggesting a potential role for these cells in persistent HPV infection [35]. We hypothesized that MIR31HG promotes paracrine IL‐4 secretion by HPV6/11‐positive keratinocytes, thereby contributing to local M2‐like macrophage polarization. Consistent with this hypothesis, co‐culture experiments demonstrated that HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells with elevated MIR31HG expression promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2‐like phenotype. Furthermore, macrophages co‐cultured with MIR31HG‐silenced HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells exhibited a shift toward the M2‐like phenotype upon IL‐4 stimulation. Pharmacological and genetic inhibition of IL‐4 signaling (via dupilumab and IL‐4 siRNA) abrogated M2‐like macrophage polarization induced by MIR31HG, confirming a mechanistic dependency on IL‐4. Notably, this mechanism is corroborated by the observed decrease in the M1/M2 macrophage ratio in CA warts, underscoring the pivotal role of M2‐like macrophages in fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment [10].

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the effects of MIR31HG, we assessed the activation status of the PI3K/AKT pathway and its upstream ceRNA regulatory network. Our findings revealed that MIR31HG functioned as a ceRNA for miR‐214‐3p, thereby regulating PTEN expression and inhibiting the phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT in HPV6/11‐HaCaT cells. Pharmacological inhibition of PI3K signaling by LY294002 further confirmed the critical involvement of the PI3K/AKT pathway in MIR31HG‐mediated immune regulation in CA. Notably, accumulating evidence has linked the pathogenesis and oncogenic progression of CA to dysregulated activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. Prior investigation has identified DEGs coexpressed with DElncRNAs as being enriched in the PI3K/AKT pathway in CA [36]. In addition, downregulation of miR‐99b along with upregulation of its target gene IGF‐1R may promote activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, resulting in deregulated cell proliferation in CA [37]. Furthermore, CA tissues exhibiting telomerase activity demonstrate elevated p‐AKT expression compared to telomerase‐negative samples, suggesting the involvement of the AKT pathway in malignant progression of CA [38]. Interestingly, our findings reveal a context‐dependent role of MIR31HG, where suppression of PI3K/AKT unexpectedly coincides with increased IL‐4 expression. This may reflect feedback activation of alternative signaling cascades governing anti‐inflammatory cytokine expression. Collectively, our study advances the understanding of the PI3K/AKT pathway in CA, particularly by unraveling its contribution to the immunosuppressive microenvironment.

A schematic model was constructed to illustrate the ceRNA network by which MIR31HG regulates the PI3K/AKT pathway and IL‐4‐mediated macrophage polarization in CA, as well as its potential therapeutic relevance.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that MIR31HG is upregulated in HPV6/11‐positive keratinocytes and promotes IL‐4 secretion through the miR‐214‐3p/PTEN/PI3K/AKT axis, thereby promoting M2‐like macrophage polarization and contributing to the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment in CA. These findings highlight a previously underappreciated role of keratinocyte‐derived IL‐4 in local immune regulation during HPV infection and further underscore the immunomodulatory functions of keratinocytes in CA pathogenesis. Given that IL‐4‐targeted therapies, such as dupilumab, have already been applied clinically, the MIR31HG/IL‐4 axis may represent a promising therapeutic target for the treatment and prevention of CA recurrence in the future.
