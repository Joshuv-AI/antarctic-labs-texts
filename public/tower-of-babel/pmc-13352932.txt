The formation and function of tertiary lymphoid structures
Authors: Chengzhang Zhang, Peng Lv, Yaxin Hou, Zhipeng Yao, Shuping Jiang, Ke Chen, Zheng Liu, Lilong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) are immune cell aggregates that emerge in nonlymphoid tissues during various disease states, including chronic inflammation, autoimmunity, and cancer. TLSs are structurally and functionally analogous to secondary lymphoid organs, and exhibit a maturation continuum (progressing from initial aggregation to mature structures with germinal centers). TLS formation is synergistically regulated by local chemokine networks (e.g. CXCL13, CCL19, and CCL21), lymphotoxin signaling axes, stromal cells, metabolic reprogramming, and the microbiome. This review comprehensively elucidates the biological foundations of TLSs, including their cellular composition, spatial architecture, and developmental dynamics of maturation. We explore the crucial roles of TLSs as favorable prognostic factors and predictors of the immunotherapy response in various solid tumors, including melanoma, breast cancer, lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, and colorectal cancer. Additionally, we analyze their “pathogenic” role in causing tissue damage and disease progression in autoimmune disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis and Sjögren’s syndrome, as well as chronic inflammatory diseases such as COPD, IgA nephropathy, and atherosclerosis. In addition, we thoroughly examine TLS research methodologies, covering a wide range of approaches from conventional hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemical staining to advanced multiplex fluorescence staining, imaging mass cytometry, and spatial transcriptomic techniques. We summarize multiple gene expression signatures (e.g. the 12-chemokine signature and TLS score) for TLS identification and quantification. Finally, we highlight multiple strategies for artificially inducing TLS formation, including cytokine delivery, immunotherapy, engineered scaffolds, microbiome modulation, and organoid technologies, designed to enhance antitumor immunity or reverse immunopathology. This review provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the complex functions of TLSs in human disease and explores their clinical translation potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Introduction

Researchers have repeatedly observed the emergence of a specific type of newly formed immune structure in nonlymphoid tissues in the contexts of chronic inflammation, autoimmune diseases, viral infections, transplant rejection, and various tumor microenvironments. This structure is referred to as a tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) [1]. TLSs share morphological and functional similarities with SLOs. In particular, mature TLSs often display a compartmentalized structure featuring distinct T/B-cell zones, follicular dendritic cell (FDC) networks, and high-endothelial venules (HEVs), which are crucial for supporting coordinated immune responses. In select instances, germinal centers (GCs) may also be formed [1]. In contrast to SLOs, which are programmed during embryonic development, TLSs are predominantly induced by persistent tissue injury or inflammation. The process under investigation relies on the synergistic actions of local proinflammatory and lymphoid organogenesis-associated signals (e.g., the lymphotoxin axis, IL-6, and IL-17) and the involvement of stromal cells [2, 3]. In particular, structural cells such as fibroblast-like stromal cells provide a spatial scaffold and participate in lymphocyte recruitment and localization through the secretion of chemokines such as CCL19/CCL21 and CXCL13. This process, in turn, promotes microstructural organization [2]. Concurrently, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released during cellular stress, apoptosis, or necrosis serve as the starting point for inflammatory amplification, triggering and sustaining local immune remodeling to create conditions conducive to the formation of TLSs [3].

Importantly, TLSs are not exclusive to tumors; rather, they are frequently observed in various peripheral nonlymphoid organs (e.g., the lungs, intestines, kidneys, and skin). In addition to reflecting the spatial clustering of immune cells, TLSs may influence local immune homeostasis and disease progression by regulating processes such as antigen presentation, immune tolerance, and immune memory [4]. In the domain of nephrology, TLSs have been documented in the pathological tissues of patients with various diseases, including lupus nephritis, IgA nephropathy, and transplant-related pathologies. Moreover, TLSs are associated with both clinical and pathological severity [5]. In the context of organ transplantation, TLSs have been shown to contribute to pathogen surveillance and clearance. However, they are also associated with amplified immune rejection responses, with their functional effects being context dependent [6]. The presence of structural elements such as GCs/HEVs in TLSs is frequently associated with increased immune activity. However, in certain microenvironments, the presence of regulatory T cells or immunosuppressive B-cell subsets may result in the suppression of CD8+ T-cell function, thereby inducing local negative immune regulation. These findings imply that TLSs do not invariably result in beneficial outcomes [7]. For instance, in the specific context of acute kidney injury progressing to chronic kidney disease, the production of TLS-associated autoantibodies is considered potentially detrimental, further highlighting their double-edged sword nature and disease specificity [8].

Recent applications of single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and tissue clearing imaging have further revealed the heterogeneity of TLSs in terms of cellular composition and maturation hierarchy. Based on the current knowledge, this review presents a comprehensive examination of the mechanisms underlying TLS occurrence and organization, spatial distribution, and functional associations within the context of tumors. Furthermore, it addresses the advantages and limitations of detection and grading methodologies, along with the opportunities and challenges inherent in the artificial induction and clinical translation of TLSs. TLS composition and maturity

TLS architecture

Unlike secondary lymphoid organs, TLSs lack a fibrous capsule. This unique architecture is hypothesized to facilitate direct exposure to antigens from the surrounding tissue, potentially enabling faster and more effective immune responses [9]. However, this finding should not lead to the assumption that the TLS lacks distinct cellular compartments and structures. In fact, the TLS consists of a T-cell zone adjacent to the B-cell zone, with follicular CD20+ B cells residing within the inner B-cell zone, which is surrounded by the outer T-cell zone [10–12]. Notably, TLSs contain many diverse immune cells, with CD3+ T cells and follicular CD20+ B cells constituting the greatest proportions. However, depending on the context, NK cells are often sparse or unevenly distributed in TLSs [13]. Additionally, HEVs and fibroblastic reticular cells (FRCs) are integral components of the TLS architecture. HEVs typically colocalize with TLSs and express peripheral node vascular addressin (PNAd), establishing a specialized vascular framework for lymphocyte recruitment that increases antitumor immune responses. From extravasation to peripheral entry into tumors, they participate in lymphocyte recruitment through chemokine secretion [14, 15]. FRCs form a network to support T-cell and B-cell zones [2]. In addition to providing structural support for the normal physiological activities of immune cells and anchoring TLSs in areas of chronic inflammatory tissue, FRCs simultaneously secrete chemokines such as CCL19/CCL21 and CXCL13, which participate in the recruitment and localization of lymphocytes [2, 16–24] (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Different types of cells present in TLSs. Maturity of TLSs

In the early literature, TLSs were often described using broad labels such as “primitive” versus “mature.” However, at that time, the operational definition of TLS “maturity” was not standardized across studies; the classification frequently relied on whether key architectural hallmarks, such as GCs, FDC networks, and HEVs, were detectable by histology and immunostaining [16, 25]. As technological methods have matured and further research has been conducted, a widely used framework has been defined that subdivides TLSs into three organizational states. Early-stage TLSs (E-TLSs) lack an FDC network and GC, show limited segregation of T- and B-cell areas, and are often annotated as CD21− CD23−. Primary follicle-like TLSs (PFL-TLSs) exhibit an emerging FDC network but lack a mature GC reaction and are commonly annotated as CD21+ CD23−. The secondary follicle-like TLS (SFL-TLS) represents the most organized state in this framework, featuring a well-formed FDC network together with a GC reaction and often HEV-like vessels, and is frequently annotated as CD21+ CD23+ [23, 26, 27] (Fig. 3). In this review, CD21 corresponds to complement receptor 2 (CR2, encoding CD21), which is commonly used as a marker of FDC networks and follicular organization, whereas CD23 is a follicle-associated marker often assessed in parallel to capture an increasing follicular structure. This three-stage organizational framework provides a practical, semiquantitative reference for comparing TLS states across studies. Importantly, increasing degrees of organization, such as follicle formation, established FDC networks, and GC-like features, may facilitate more coordinated local immune interactions, offering a rationale for stratifying TLSs by developmental/organizational state in mechanistic and translational studies [28]. Accordingly, identifying and classifying TLSs at different stages is often a necessary prerequisite for investigating their cellular programs and clinical associations. For example, in breast cancer, Wang et al. used CD23+ staining as a follicle-associated criterion to screen for mature TLSs (mTLSs) and subsequently linked TLS-associated B-cell programs to the prognosis and efficacy of neoadjuvant therapy [29].

However, this framework has largely been grounded in its morphological resemblance to secondary lymphoid organs (SLOs), which, while practical, may underappreciate the molecular and functional heterogeneity of TLSs across tissues and disease contexts. In particular, the biological roles of structures conventionally labeled “immature” TLSs remain ambiguous because an “immature” phenotype may reflect not only an early organizational stage but also distinct microenvironment-driven molecular states with divergent immunological functions. Through a spatial transcriptomic analysis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tissues, Tang et al. proposed that immature TLSs can be categorized into two types: “conforming” and “deviating” subtypes. They established a benchmark differentiation trajectory from naive B cells to germinal center B cells to assess TLS developmental trajectories. Transcriptomes from naive B cells and GC-B cells within each TLS were compared against this benchmark using linear regression analysis. Some TLSs exhibited developmental trajectories consistent with the established maturation pathway but displayed lower CR2 transcript levels; these TLSs were defined as conforming TLSs. Similarly, TLSs whose developmental trajectory persistently deviated from that of naive B cells to the GC–B-cell differentiation pathway were classified as deviating TLSs [30]. The introduction of these new concepts provides us with a more nuanced understanding of TLS functionality, revealing that TLSs may exhibit distinct immune characteristics in specific microenvironments. These characteristics can significantly influence the response to immunotherapy.

Mechanisms regulating TLS development

As mentioned above, the function of TLS depends on its maturity and structural characteristics, which are formed and maintained through the regulation of complex molecular mechanisms.

Initiation of inflammation

The TLS formation is typically initiated against a backdrop of chronic inflammation or tissue injury. Local inflammatory responses within the tissue microenvironment serve as the driving force for TLS formation. Antigens exposed upon tissue damage (e.g., autoantigens or tumor antigens), in addition to DAMPs derived from necrotic cells, stimulate surrounding matrix fibroblasts and resident immune cells to secrete multiple cytokines and chemokines. These signaling molecules, in turn, activate local immune cells and promote their migration toward TLS formation sites [3, 31, 32]. Common initiating cytokines include IL-6, IL-17, IL-22, LTα, and TNF [33]. Through direct or indirect mechanisms, they initiate the preliminary formation of TLSs. The LTβR–NF-κB signaling axis is among the key molecular pathways driving the transformation of stromal cells. Notably, the sequential expression of IL-7 and LTα1β2 exerts synergistic effects on lymphatic vessel expansion and FDC network formation. Nayar et al. investigated inflammatory models and observed that the expression of IL-7 is initially upregulated to drive immune cell recruitment, followed by LT signaling, which activates epithelial-like transdifferentiation and chemokine expression in stromal cells, thereby establishing the spatial architecture of TLSs [34]. Research by Deteix et al. on a transplant model indicated that dendritic cells (DCs) and T cells, particularly Th17 cells, regulate the response of stromal cells by releasing IL-17. These cells also recruit other immune cells to the tumor site through chemokines such as CXCL13 and CCL19 [35]. DCs are typically located at the periphery of the T-cell zone, where they play a role in recruiting T cells and activating the innate immune system during the initial formation of TLSs [10, 36]. Marinkovic T et al. reported that T cells and CD11c+ dendritic cells are recruited via the CCL21–CCR7 axis to the thyroid, where they induce TLS formation in an LTβ signaling-dependent manner [37]. Additionally, the CD153/CD30 pathway exerts a synergistic effect on TLS expansion in elderly individuals. Sato et al. reported that this signaling drives TLS-associated renal tissue damage and promotes inflammatory amplification, suggesting that this pathway may participate in TLS maintenance and chronic expansion [38].

Cell recruitment

Before discussing the recruitment of cells involved in tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) formation, understanding the canonical model derived from secondary lymphoid organ development, in which lymphoid tissue inducer (LTi) cells (expressing LTα1β2) activate lymphoid tissue organizer (LTo) stromal cells (via LTβR) to initiate the chemokine and adhesion molecule cascades necessary for lymphocyte recruitment and architectural organization, is critical.

In TLSs, stimulated stromal cells and lymphocytes secrete CXCL13, IL-7, and VEGF-A, inducing the recruitment of LTi-like cells [33]. According to reports, Th17 cells, type 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s), effector CD8+ T cells, natural killer cells, B cells, and M1-polarized macrophages can all serve as potential alternative LTi cells in mice and humans with allogeneic transplant rejection, autoimmunity, chronic inflammation, or cancer [20, 39]. Previous studies suggest that cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), and adipocytes—all of which are types of stromal cells—can substitute for LTo cells. These cells interact with LTi cells via the LTα1β2 receptor and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) to initiate TLS formation [34, 35]. In addition, recent studies have confirmed that CCL19-expressing perivascular cells (PvCs) may function as LTo cells in the recruitment of lymphocytes during the formation of TLSs [40]. This interaction promotes the expression of a series of chemokines, adhesion molecules, and inflammatory factors in LTo cells, which play crucial roles in immune cell recruitment and the formation of other TLS structures. For example, VEGF-C and IL-7 facilitate the transition of the endothelial vascular system from a flat endothelium to a highly positioned endothelium [34].

Following HEV formation in TLSs, adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 are continuously released, promoting the homing of various immune cells [10]. The spatial organization of TLSs relies on the expression and gradient formation of key chemokines, particularly the upregulated expression of CXCL13, CCL19, and CCL21, which determines the stratification and functionalization of B-cell and T-cell zones. In mature TLSs, CXCL13 is produced primarily by FDCs and certain stromal cells to attract CXCR5+ B cells and establish B-cell follicles. Denton et al. reported that in inflammatory models, type I interferon significantly induces CXCL13 expression, thereby promoting germinal center formation, demonstrating the close association between viral responses and TLS functional maturation [41]. CCL19/CCL21 mediate T-cell and DC entry and localization via CCR7. These factors not only provide signals for cell migration but also maintain the capacity of TLSs to activate the immune response [33] (Fig. 4). Furthermore, Engelhard et al. reported that certain vascular endothelial cells within tumors can undergo a transformation toward an HEV-like phenotype in inflammatory environments, serving as one indicator of TLS maturation. This result suggests that the role of nonimmune cells in TLS formation has been significantly underestimated [42]. Immune activation

TLS maturation is typically accompanied by the activation and maturation of immune cells, with FDCs, GCs, and follicular helper T cells (Tfhs) playing crucial roles. Studies indicate that TLSs are enriched with functionally active DCs, which mediate antigen presentation via MHC class II molecules, driving the initial activation of CD4+ helper T cells [44]. TLSs comprise distinct DC populations, namely, conventional DC1 (cDC1) and cDC2 cells, along with plasmacytoid DCs. Conventional DC1s possess a high intrinsic capacity to cross-present antigens via MHC class I, thereby activating CD8+ T cells and promoting type 1 Th1 and natural killer responses. Conversely, cDC2s express CXCR5 and polarize naive CD4+ T cells into Tfhs through MHC class II and ICOS signaling [45]. Plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs) predominantly reside in the T-cell zone (TCZ). These pDCs may stimulate CD8+ T cells within the tumor TCZ and serve as predictive markers for favorable clinical outcomes [46]. In CRC, pDCs express IRF7 and reside near CD8+ T cells, suggesting that they may increase antitumor immunity by secreting type I interferons and stimulating CD8+ T cells [47]. During the formation of TLSs, IRF7+ pDCs preferentially reside near CD4+ T cells, whereas pDCs suppress the proliferation of FOXP3+ Tregs via IFN-α [48].

Following antigen stimulation, CD4+ T cells differentiate into distinct subsets and secrete multiple cytokines. Th1 cells produce IFN-γ for cell-mediated responses, whereas Th2 cells stimulate B-cell proliferation and differentiation through cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-5. Additionally, Tfh cells produce interleukin-21 (IL-21), which indirectly increases CD8+ T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity [49, 50].

Moreover, CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) can also receive activation signals within TLSs, resulting in the formation of a CD103+ CD8+ cell population with tissue-resident memory (Trm) CD103+ CD8+ cell populations and the establishment of sustained local immune surveillance [51]. Simultaneously, the TLS serves as the site for B-cell differentiation and antibody production. In the context of cancer, the efficacy of the GC response is jointly regulated by B-cell activity and structural integrity [17]. In functional TLSs with a germinal center function, B cells undergo somatic hypermutation (SHM), affinity maturation, and class-switch recombination (CSR), ultimately differentiating into plasma cells that secrete high-affinity antibodies. This process relies on the assistance of Tfh cells. Costimulatory signaling axes such as CD40L–CD40 and ICOS–ICOSL are continuously active within TLSs, providing the microenvironment for the B-cell germinal center response [10, 16, 17]. Research has shown a potential interaction between Tfh cells and GC B cells via the IL-21–IL-21 R signaling pathway, with IL-21 being implicated in class switching, plasma cell differentiation, and GC function [52]. In addition, plasma cells may migrate to the tumor along the TLS network structure formed by fibroblasts, producing high-affinity IgG and IgA antibodies [53]. In addition to conferring immunity through antibody secretion, memory B cells, which are derived from mature B cells, can rapidly recognize low concentrations of antigens in the environment and present them to nearby T cells, enabling swift immune responses.

Upon activation by antigen stimulation, sparsely distributed CD8+ T cells surrounding TLSs can directly kill tumor cells by releasing granzymes, perforin, and cytokines [54]. Notably, cytotoxic Tfh cells express GZMK within TLSs in individuals with IgG4-related disease, indicating their direct involvement in antitumor activity [55]. Third, TLSs provide an “organizational field” for memory cell generation. Mature TLSs can form a niche that supports the maintenance and reactivation of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and B memory cells. The matrix network composed of FDCs and FRCs supports immune cell survival and function through the secretion of factors such as CXCL13 and IL-7 [56]. CD8+ T cells differentiate into cytotoxic effectors, eliminating tumor cells via PFN and GzmB. TLSs in various solid tumors

In the tumor microenvironment, TLSs not only function as “local immune hubs” for immune cell aggregation but also play pivotal roles in tumor immune evasion, the resolution of immune evasion, and immunotherapy efficacy. Consequently, the presence, distribution, and immune functions of TLSs across different tumor types have become popular research topics. This section focuses on discussing TLS manifestations in various cancers and analyzing their potential value in cancer immunotherapy. TLSs in different tumor types exhibit distinct tissue distributions and significant variations in immunological function. These differences may influence tumor immune evasion mechanisms and treatment efficacy.

Melanoma

Multiple studies have indicated that TLSs primarily localize to the peritumoral and stromal regions in melanoma, with differences in frequency and histological morphology observed between primary and metastatic lesions. Karapetyan et al. analyzed 278 melanoma tissue samples and reported that TLSs were more prevalent in metastatic tumors and were predominantly distributed around the tumor periphery and within connective tissue regions [58]. Cabrita et al. performed a multicenter cohort study of patients with melanoma and showed that TLS-dense regions predominantly occur at tumor margins and are closely associated with T-cell activity [59]. In terms of the cellular composition, TLSs in melanoma often exhibit distinct anatomical segregation, characterized by B-cell follicles surrounded by T-cell zones, resembling the architecture of secondary lymphoid organs (SLOs) [58–60]. Additionally, studies have indicated that certain immunosuppressive subsets can accumulate within TLSs. For instance, Eschweiler et al. reported that follicular regulatory T cells (Tfrs) are widely present within TLS follicles across multiple tumor types, including melanoma. These cells exhibit significant immunosuppressive potential and emerge as a resistance-associated cell population during anti-PD-1 therapy [60].

The presence of TLSs has been demonstrated to correlate with an improved prognosis of the disease. A large retrospective study by Cabrita et al. revealed that melanoma patients with a high TLS density experienced significantly prolonged overall survival, with the TLS density emerging as an independent prognostic factor [59]. Karapetyan et al. reported that compared with tumors without TLS formation, tumors with TLSs were associated with the noninvolvement of regional lymph nodes and longer overall survival (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.52, p = 0.01) [58]. In addition to its prognostic value, the TLS can predict the treatment response. With respect to anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody therapy, Cabrita et al. reported that TLS-dense tumors significantly increased the response rate to immune checkpoint inhibitors [59]. Liu et al. performed a study of neoadjuvant therapy for acral melanoma and reported that patients with a favorable pathological response generally exhibited an increased TLS density in their lesions [61]. With respect to neoadjuvant therapy mechanisms, Helmink et al. analyzed high-risk patients with melanoma and reported that B-cell-enriched TLSs were significantly more abundant in responders than in nonresponders. Their findings revealed that the clonal expansion of B cells within these structures acts synergistically with T cells to promote immunotherapy responses [62]. Animal studies also provide mechanistic support. Chelvanambi et al. analyzed a mouse melanoma model and showed that low-dose STING agonists promote TLS formation, increase the activity of tumor-infiltrating T cells, and ultimately inhibit tumor progression [63].

Breast cancer

Research on TLSs in breast cancer is ongoing, with findings that continue to reveal their critical roles in spatial organization, cellular composition, and clinical relevance. Many studies have confirmed that TLSs are frequently localized at the periphery, stroma, or tumor–host tissue interface of breast tumors. A minority of TLSs are also distributed within the tumor parenchyma, a phenomenon that is particularly prevalent in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [64–66].

With respect to the significance of TLSs for the prognosis, the presence and development of TLSs have been shown to be positively correlated with two key metrics in breast cancer patients: overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). In the TNBC and HER2-positive subtypes, the presence of TLSs has been shown to be a predictor of a favorable treatment response [67, 68]. Wang et al. reported that TLS-positive TNBC patients achieved substantial responses following six cycles of camrelizumab neoadjuvant immunotherapy. The efficacy of neoadjuvant immunotherapy was significantly lower in TLS-negative patients [29]. Previous analyses of CD4+ T-cell subsets in breast cancer TLSs by Grégory Noël et al. indicated that elevated CXCL13 expression correlates with the expression of Tfh markers and tumor infiltration levels. Subsequent studies further revealed that CD4+ Tfh TILs, CD8+ TILs, and TIL-B cells colocalized within TLSs and all expressed the CXCL13 receptor CXCR5 [66, 69]. Additionally, research on breast cancer has revealed that the aggregation of antigen-presenting mast cells (apMCs) in TLSs may play a crucial role in the efficacy of PD-1 blockade [70]. LGALS2, a marker of DC activation within TLSs in breast cancer, is closely associated with the intensity of immune responses and has potential as a therapeutic target [71]. CCL19+ dendritic cells are correlated with an increased response to PD-1 blockade therapy [72]. In addition to the cellular composition, specific structural components serve as practical quantitative surrogates. In TNBC, the abundance of HEVs is a practical surrogate mainly for mature TLSs and should be interpreted with complementary markers (B/T zones, FDCs, etc.) [73]. However, the immune context is critical; in some breast tumors, the presence of Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) within the tumor parenchyma is associated with poor clinical outcomes, suggesting that Tregs may hijack these structures to suppress local immunity [74].

Non-small cell lung cancer

In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), TLSs are distributed both at the tumor periphery (pTLSs) and deep within the tumor (iTLSs). Rather than simply differing in activity, this positioning is strictly regulated by tumor–stromal interactions; for instance, hMENA isoforms expressed in tumor epithelial cells promote TLS formation by upregulating the expression of LTβR, whereas hMENAΔv6 expressed in cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) inhibits this pathway, preventing TLSs from penetrating the tumor parenchyma [75]. In terms of the cellular composition, these structures typically exhibit a germinal center-like organization populated by effector immune cell populations [76, 77].

With respect to the prognosis, multiple large-scale clinical and transcriptomic studies have demonstrated that the TLS density and maturity are significantly positively correlated with OS and PFS [78, 79]. The internal composition offers finer resolution; for instance, the abundance of follicular B cells is a strong predictor of long-term survival, whereas low densities identify high-risk patients [80].

Crucially, the TLS status serves as a distinct biomarker of therapeutic efficacy. While the TLS status is closely associated with the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [77, 81] and predicts a pathological complete response (pCR) in patients receiving neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy [82], the evidence suggests that mature TLSs are ‘immunotherapy-specific’ indicators. Specifically, while they strongly predict responses to ICIs, they do not necessarily correlate with the outcomes of chemotherapy alone [83]. Furthermore, high TLS maturity is associated with a major pathological response (MPR), validating its role as a predictor of therapeutic success [18]. Additionally, metabolic mechanisms such as ACAT1 expression have been shown to suppress TLS formation, suggesting that targeting metabolic checkpoints could further increase anti-PD-1 efficacy [78, 84].

Hepatocellular carcinoma

In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), TLSs can occur within the tumor (iTLSs) or in the peritumoral region (pTLSs), with the distribution of TLSs closely correlated with the extent of immune cell infiltration. Studies indicate that the presence of intratumoral TLSs is associated with a favorable prognosis, predicting longer recurrence-free intervals and overall survival [85, 86]. However, pTLSs indicate greater potential for a response to immunotherapy [87]. In terms of the cellular composition, TLSs in HCC also exhibit typical structures and cell populations [30, 88, 89].

In the latest HCC omics analysis, TLSs were identified as “communication niches” for multiple immune cell interactions. Notably, CD8+ Tex-int cells (GZMK+ PD-1+) that emerged after HAIC therapy preferentially localized near TLSs and were distributed throughout the entire tumor, indicating potential antitumor activity [90]. In terms of the prognosis, the presence of TLSs is typically associated with favorable tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) characteristics and improved clinical outcomes [85, 88]. However, the biological effect of TLSs is strictly site dependent. Preliminary insights indicate that while intratumoral TLSs generally exert antitumor effects, TLSs located in nontumor liver tissue or the distant periphery may act as “microhabitats” for tumor progenitor cells, potentially supporting their survival and indicating an increased risk of recurrence [91, 92]. This result necessitates site-specific stratification, as exclusively peripheral-type TLSs are often linked to a poor prognosis [93]. For example, studies by Kurebayashi et al. indicated that Tfh cells and CXCL13+ CD4+ T cells promote the establishment of TLSs and lymphoplasmacytic microenvironments, respectively. These findings can be utilized to construct immune subtypes, thereby predicting patient responses to surgery and immunotherapy [89]. Other studies have indicated that the absence of TLSs is associated with excessive tumor cell cycle activity and the activation of mTOR signaling [30]. From the perspective of predicting therapeutic efficacy, the TLS density and maturity can serve as predictive biomarkers for the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy. Shu et al. reported that a high TLS density in tumor regions following neoadjuvant immunotherapy is closely associated with the pathological response and reduced recurrence rates [88]. Mechanistically, the spatial distribution of TLSs is also constrained by metabolic barriers. For instance, the accumulation of immunosuppressive metabolic byproducts, such as those driven by aberrant tryptophan metabolism (e.g., IDO1 activity), creates a hostile milieu within the tumor nest. This metabolic restriction likely prevents intratumoral lymphocyte aggregation, thereby confining TLS formation to the peritumoral region [30]. Detailed discussions on metabolic regulation are provided in Sect. “Effects of metabolism on TLSs”. Multiple studies have further revealed key pathways and cell populations that regulate TLS formation. For instance, RORc+ immune cells suppress TLS formation and promote their pro-oncogenic function, whereas their absence facilitates the establishment of antitumor TLSs [94]. Chemokines such as CCL3, CCL19, and VEGF-C, along with CAF subpopulations, have also been shown to promote TLS formation and increase the efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors [95–97]. Additionally, noninvasive TLS prediction tools such as MRI radiomics models have promising performance in accurately distinguishing between iTLSs and pTLSs, providing novel strategies for the risk stratification of and treatment decision-making in HCC patients [87, 98].

Colorectal cancer

In colorectal cancer (CRC), TLSs typically aggregate in the peritumoral region (pTLSs), although their intratumoral infiltration (iTLSs) is observed in immunologically active tumors. Mechanistically, specific signaling axes and microbial interactions regulate TLS integrity. Mi et al. [99] demonstrated that the LIGHT–HVEM signaling axis is critical for maintaining type 3 innate lymphoid cell (ILC3) abundance and TLS structural stability; they further showed that bacteria engineered to induce LIGHT expression could promote mature TLS formation and sensitize tumors to immune checkpoint blockade. In parallel, Niu et al. [100] reported that in SMAD4-deficient microenvironments, targeted therapy could induce the production of CXCL13+ CD4+ T cells and CXCR5+ CD20+ B cells, resulting in the generation of mature TLSs with increased IL-21 and IFN-γ secretion.

Guo et al. [101] conducted a comprehensive multicohort clinical analysis and revealed that patients with a high TLS signature typically harbor mature TLSs in both intratumoral and peritumoral regions, characterized by dense clustering of CD8+ T cells and B cells. In terms of the prognosis, validation in TCGA cohort and multiple independent cohorts confirmed that this high TLS signature is significantly associated with prolonged OS and PFS [101]. Similarly, Lei et al. [102] stratified patients using a TLS score and revealed that the high-TLS score cohort exhibited significantly better prognostic outcomes and enriched immune activation pathways. With respect to the prediction of therapeutic efficacy, Guo et al. [101] further reported that in multiple microsatellite-stable (MSS) CRC cohorts, patients with a high TLS signature were more likely to benefit from PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, highlighting TLSs as crucial biomarkers for the immunotherapy response.

Renal cell carcinoma

The immune landscape in clear cell renal carcinoma (ccRCC) is complex. While CXCL13+ CD8+ T-cell infiltration is paradoxically linked to a poor prognosis because of an exhausted phenotype and high checkpoint expression (e.g., PD-1 and Tim-3) [103], the organization of immune cells into mature TLSs reverses this trend. Mature TLSs, which typically reside in the tumor periphery, are enriched in functional B cells and DCs. High densities of these mature structures (e.g., >0.6/mm2) significantly correlate with prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) [27, 104], whereas immature TLSs containing Tregs and TAMs facilitate immune evasion [103, 105]. Maturity is a decisive factor in treatment outcomes; Masuda et al. reported that in RCC patients, low TLS maturation is correlated not only with a poor prognosis but also with resistance to antiangiogenic therapy following disease recurrence, highlighting the clinical necessity of distinguishing mature follicles from lymphoid aggregates [106].

At the molecular level, VHL mutations—a hallmark of ccRCC—are associated with an increased TLS density and increased immune cell infiltration [36, 107]. Therapeutic interventions can also remodel this landscape; for instance, tremelimumab (an anti-CTLA-4 antibody) combined with cryoablation has been shown to induce TLS formation and increase immune cell infiltration in posttreatment tissues [108]. Furthermore, the functional quality of TLSs is critical: the presence of IgG- and IgA-producing plasma cells within TLSs is correlated with favorable immunotherapy responses, underscoring the role of local antibody production [53].

TLS-related markers have emerged as robust predictors of immunotherapy efficacy. Increased CXCL13 expression alone is associated with improved responses (HR = 0.8, p < 0.0001) [109]. Expanding on these findings, Helmink et al. demonstrated that B-cell-rich TLS signatures in RCC serve as superior predictive biomarkers for the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade, validating the critical role of B cells in the antitumor response [62]. This B-cell/TLS-dependent predictive paradigm appears to be a shared characteristic across urological cancers. In support of this hypothesis, Gao et al. reported that the pretreatment TLS density was significantly higher in patients with urothelial carcinoma who responded to anti-PD-L1/CTLA-4 combination therapy than in those who did not respond. Notably, this TLS-based prediction was independent of PD-L1 expression, suggesting that TLSs reflect a distinct and universal dimension of the antitumor immune microenvironment in the genitourinary tract [110, 111].

Universal dimensions of TLSs as biomarkers: evidence from other solid tumors

In addition to the findings from the major cancer types discussed above, the evidence from other solid tumors (such as soft tissue sarcomas and laryngeal cancer) reveals that the prognostic value of TLSs is governed by three universal dimensions: the cellular composition, maturation status, and standardization.

The specific cellular “neighborhood” within TLSs dictates the outcome. In soft tissue sarcomas, landmark studies have shown that IgG-producing plasma cells are not only bystanders but also active drivers of the antitumor response and are significantly associated with an improved prognosis [112]. Similarly, research by Petitprez et al. revealed that the density and spatial localization of Tfh cells within TLSs predict the level of the tumor immune response [113]. In lung adenocarcinoma and gastric cancer, the proportion of CD103+ tissue-resident memory (Trm) T cells significantly increases with TLS maturation, indicating a more favorable prognosis [114, 115]. Mechanistically, mature TLSs serve as sites for the infiltration of neoantigen-reactive CD8+ T cells, which is positively correlated with patient survival [116–118]. Furthermore, CD4+ Th1 cells within these structures activate macrophages and cytotoxic T lymphocytes to induce effector functions [119]. However, TLSs can also be sites of immunosuppression. FOXP3+ Tregs are generally associated with shorter survival of patients [120]. In mouse models, Treg depletion triggers functional TLS formation and increases T-cell activation [121, 122]. This process also occurs in prostate cancer, in which the presence of Foxp3+ cells within the tumor parenchyma is associated with poor clinical outcomes, in contrast to the protective effects observed in other contexts [123].

The “presence” of TLSs alone is insufficient; their “maturity” determines their function. In laryngeal cancer, Liang et al. reported that immature TLSs were paradoxically enriched in pathways related to tumor invasion and metastasis, with the accumulation of neutrophils rather than functional lymphocytes [124]. This finding underscores a critical principle: immature or dysregulated lymphoid aggregates may fail to execute antitumor responses. The interaction between T cells and stromal components is pivotal for this maturation. Studies have indicated that the chronic activation of CD8+ T cells in the presence of fibroblast-derived TGF-β can promote the B-cell spatial distribution and TLS maturation via CXCL13 secretion [125, 126].

Collectively, these findings support the integration of TLSs into a composite biomarker system [59, 113]. In multiple cancer types, a high TLS abundance is consistently correlated with high immune cell infiltration rates and better immunotherapy efficacy [127, 128]. We recommend that future pathological assessments move beyond a binary “presence/absence” score to reduce confounding effects. Instead, a multidimensional reporting framework that explicitly documents the spatial location (iTLS vs. pTLS) and maturation status (follicles with GCs vs. aggregates) should be adopted to accurately stratify patients’ risk.

Collectively, current evidence from these diverse malignancies—ranging from major cancer types to soft-tissue sarcomas and laryngeal carcinomas—validates the ‘universal’ potential of TLSs as biomarkers. Nonneoplastic diseases and TLSs

TLSs in autoimmune diseases

TLSs are closely associated with not only solid tumors but also numerous autoimmune diseases. Reports indicate that TLSs are present in approximately 40% of patients with untreated rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and can develop only within lymphoid–myeloid clusters [129]. Among patients with focal scores > 1 in salivary gland biopsy specimens, TLSs, which are closely associated with immune fibroblast activation, were detected in approximately 30–40% of patients with Sjögren’s syndrome (SS) [130, 131]. Although TLSs are not commonly observed in patients with lupus nephritis, T-cell and B-cell aggregates and the formation of FDC networks have been documented in 6% of patients [132]. Moreover, TLSs are present in 50% of human type 1 diabetes cases in both preclinical and clinical settings, where HEVs, reticular cells, and abundant immune cell subtypes can be detected [133]. In approximately 40% of postmortem brain tissues from patients with progressive multiple sclerosis (MS), ectopic lymphoid follicles were observed in the meninges and correlated with the severity of cortical degeneration and clinical disease progression [134].

The occurrence of TLSs is typically associated with an improved prognosis for patients with tumors, but in patients with autoimmune diseases, TLSs are commonly linked to tissue damage and fibrosis [130]. Among the many lines of evidence supporting TLSs as a component promoting the progression of autoimmune diseases is their role in sustaining novel antibody specificity. Research on the transplantation of TLS-containing tissue from rheumatoid arthritis patients into immunodeficient mice revealed that the grafts persistently expressed AID and were surrounded by plasma cells producing anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (CCP) antibodies. These findings provide compelling evidence that TLSs within the RA synovium are functional and support the continuous production of anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (CCP) antibodies [135]. Research has also demonstrated that transplanting TLSs from the salivary glands of pSS patients infected with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) into SCID mice supports the production of anti-Ro 52/anti-La 48 and anti-EBV antibodies, as well as the survival of autoreactive B-cell clones [136].

Given the aforementioned evidence of the promotion of autoimmune diseases by TLSs, several studies have investigated the role of TLSs in disease progression. Animal experiments on RA have indicated that modulating cytokines such as IL-27 can negatively regulate TLS formation, whereas signaling pathways such as the IL-6/pSTAT3 pathway can affect the Tfh-to-Tfr ratio, thereby inhibiting the germinal center response [137, 138]. However, the results of clinical trials have been disappointing. The inhibition of LT-β, IL-17, or IL-12/23 did not result in significant therapeutic efficacy [139, 140]. In patients with SS, TLSs are associated with higher antibody levels and more systemic symptoms [131]. Furthermore, they are associated with a 16-fold increased risk of lymphoma in SS patients [131, 141]. Given the importance of TLSs in SS and the efficacy of LTβR blockade in animal models, corresponding clinical trials have been conducted [142, 143]. Although the efficacy of blocking LT-βR remains unproven, this treatment does not completely inhibit lymphocyte recruitment [143].

In summary, the role of TLSs in autoimmune diseases is complex and multifaceted. While their presence in tumors is typically associated with a favorable prognosis, in autoimmune diseases, they are often closely linked to tissue damage, fibrosis, and disease progression. Furthermore, clinical interventions have yet to yield significant therapeutic efficacy.

TLSs in common chronic diseases

Lung diseases

The formation of TLSs in the lungs of COPD patients is significantly increased, indicating the presence of induced bronchial-associated lymphoid tissue (iBALT) within the respiratory system. The development of pulmonary TLSs is correlated with the degree of airflow limitation and hypoxia and serves as a key indicator of disease severity [144]. In patients with severe or end-stage COPD (GOLD stages III and IV), TLSs can be detected in nearly 50% of small airways [145, 146].

With respect to the occurrence of TLSs in COPD patients, studies have identified type 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s) expressing neuropilin-1 in the lung tissues of smokers and patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. These cells possess LTi activity and initiate TLS aggregation through the production of IL-17A and IL-22 [147]. In addition to primary-site ILC3s, cDC2s and CD4+ T cells are attracted to the site of TLS formation by the production of CXCL12, CXCL13, and cholesterol metabolites at the primary site during chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Upon their recruitment, cDC2s drive the polarization of IL-21+ Tfh-like cells and the secretion of cytokines (including IL-6, IL-1β, and TGF-β) via the OX40L (OX40 ligand)–OX40 axis, ultimately promoting TLS formation [45].

In addition to these mechanistic studies, studies in animal models have shown that inhibiting LTβR signaling disrupts smoking-related TLSs in the lungs and induces lung tissue regeneration [148]. Some scholars have proposed that memory Tpath2 cells and Thy1+ LECs constitute the pathogenic components of TLSs in the inflamed lungs, potentially serving as effective therapeutic targets for refractory inflammatory diseases [149]. Moreover, the application of BAFF-neutralizing antibodies (BAFFR-Fc) results in a marked reduction in the pulmonary TLS density, accompanied by diminished alveolar destruction [150]. Whether these therapeutic approaches can be translated into successful clinical trials and whether they can be extrapolated to other pulmonary diseases requires further research.

Kidney diseases

TLSs are observed in various kidney diseases, including IgA nephropathy (IgAN) [151, 152], lupus nephritis [153–155], ANCA-associated glomerulonephritis [154, 155], and interstitial nephritis with tubular involvement [152], and typically exhibit subcapsular, perivascular, and periglomerular locations [156, 157]. In a prospective IgAN cohort, TLSs were identified in 37.5% of patients. High TLS grades (reflecting aggregate density) are strongly associated with adverse pathological features and significantly predict disease progression, indicating that TLSs are closely associated with adverse clinical and pathological features of IgAN [151].

TLSs typically develop in damaged, aging kidneys, forming the basis for persistent inflammation and impaired renal regeneration [158]. Mechanistically, studies have indicated that cellular interactions between CD153+PD-1+CD4+ age-associated T cells and CD30+T-bet+ age-associated B cells drive TLS expansion via the CD153–CD30 signaling pathway [38]. TNF and IFNγ, which are produced within tubulocytes, activate adjacent VCAM1-expressing damaged proximal tubules, leading to the upregulation of ICAM1/VCAM1, CCL2, and CXCL10 expression. This process results in the formation of an inflammatory amplification loop. In addition, TLS-derived STAT1-expressing proinflammatory fibroblasts secrete CCL9/10 and BAFF, thereby sustaining T/B-cell infiltration. Furthermore, TLS-independent profibrotic fibroblasts drive ECM deposition and myofibroblast differentiation [159]. Research has indicated that anti-TNFα drugs and IFNγ/STAT1 signaling pathway inhibitors may prevent progressive injury and TLS expansion in aged injured kidneys by reducing the effects of these cytokines produced within TLSs on renal parenchymal cells. However, further studies are needed to confirm the efficacy of these drugs [159]. Additional studies suggest that circulating sCD30 levels are correlated with TLS formation in IgAN patients, indicating the potential use of sCD30 levels as a noninvasive biomarker [160]. However, the sample size of patients is limited and requires validation in larger cohorts.

Cardiovascular diseases

Arterial tertiary lymphoid organs (ATLOs) are tertiary lymphoid structures associated with atherosclerosis that typically appear in the connective tissue surrounding diseased arteries, specifically the adventitia [161]. In atherosclerosis, adventitial lymphoid-like structures (such as ATLOs) may have both immune homeostasis/protective functions and pro-pathogenic roles in later disease stages. Early, structurally intact ATLOs rely on the VSMC–LTβR axis and CXCL13/CCL21 for maintenance and to organize and stratify T/B-cell responses; the disruption of this axis promotes the degradation of ATLOs and accelerates lesion progression [162, 163]. When the degree of lymphatic drainage of the outer membrane/plaque and cholesterol reverse transport increase, the lipid and inflammatory burden in the arterial wall decreases, suggesting that drainage pathways may represent one mechanism through which ATLOs exert their protective effects [164, 165]. When lesions progress to advanced stages and exhibit high-grade immunohistochemical staining, ATLOs are more readily correlated with plaque fragility. Studies of human coronary arteries have shown that higher grades of epicardial TLOs are associated with an increased plaque volume and greater susceptibility to instability/rupture [166]. Single-cell and multicohort validation further revealed TLO-like structures (PTLOs) within carotid plaques, whose presence was significantly associated with cerebrovascular events and whose immunophenotype was more inclined toward pro-rupture characteristics [167]. Mechanistically, these “high-tier” structures are often associated with active Tfh–germinal center (GC) axes [168]. B2/plasma cells and IgG effector enhancement [169] in combination with macrophages expressing high levels of FcγR [167] form an “antibody–effector cell” closed loop, which suggests an immune ecosystem that destabilizes plaques. Similarly, animal studies and comprehensive reviews suggest that decreasing GC/Tfh signaling can simultaneously reduce lesions and limit the formation of TLOs in arterial walls [168]. In summary, the net effect of ATLOs may be stage dependent: they can either provide immune homeostasis/tolerance or participate in the immune-driven progression of unstable plaques during high-grade states.

Effects of metabolism on TLSs

Metabolic states within the tumor microenvironment significantly modulate TLS assembly and functional specialization. Preclinical studies in murine models have demonstrated that specific metabolic alterations, such as hypoxia-induced lactate accumulation, can impair the recruitment of high endothelial venules (HEVs) and inhibit TLS maturation. In clinical cohorts, transcriptomic and spatial metabolomic analyses of human tumor specimens have further correlated metabolic reprogramming—specifically glucose and lipid metabolism—with the density and prognostic value of mature TLSs. Thus, deciphering these metabolic regulatory circuits provides critical insights into TLS-mediated antitumor immunity and identifies potential metabolic targets to increase the efficacy of immunotherapy.

Glucose metabolism plays a dual role in TLS induction. On the one hand, T-cell activation relies on glycolysis to provide energy and metabolic intermediates, which increase the capacity to secrete CXCL13 to recruit B cells and promote TLS formation. Hu et al. reported that activated B cells can activate mTOR signaling in CD103+CD8+ Trm cells via the LTα–TNFR2 signaling axis, triggering glycolysis and promoting the secretion of CXCL13 and GZMB, thereby facilitating TLS maturation [170]. Some studies have reported that during the early stages of TLS development, tumors with high glycolysis levels produce acid that accumulates in the tumor microenvironment (TME), which decreases the tumor pH. This decreased pH, in turn, inhibits T-cell secretion of IL-2 and IFN-γ, potentially suppressing T-cell LTo-like function and decreasing the efficiency of TLS formation [7]. Similarly, this metabolic inhibition is further evidenced by the clinical correlation between lactate transporters (such as SLC16A1 and SLC16A3) and immune evasion phenotypes in which the active transport of glycolytic byproducts creates a suppressive niche hostile to lymphocyte organization [171]. Therefore, regulating the pH and lactate concentration in the tumor microenvironment represents a potential strategy for maintaining TLS homeostasis.

Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated that ACAT1 (acyl-CoA/cholesterol acyltransferase 1) plays a crucial regulatory role in TLS formation and immune responses within the tumor immune microenvironment. Jiao et al. (2025) performed immunohistochemical staining and flow cytometry and showed that elevated ACAT1 levels correlated with reduced TLS abundance in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and this alteration affected the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy [78]. Moreover, ACAT1 inhibition can ameliorate high-fat diet-induced renal fibrosis and promote TLS maturation in the kidney following ischemia‒reperfusion injury [172].

Amino acid metabolism, particularly the tryptophan–kynurenine–AHR signaling axis, has been established in recent years as a negative regulator of TLS maturation. Tumor cells express enzymes such as IDO1 to degrade tryptophan into kynurenine, activating AHR to inhibit STING-mediated type I interferon signaling and block TLS maturation [30]. Spatial transcriptomic studies revealed that this metabolic axis is a decisive factor promoting TLS stagnation at an immature stage in hepatocellular carcinoma. Interventions targeting this pathway can transform TLSs into mature follicular structures and increase the efficacy of PD-1 blockade therapy [30].

Lipid metabolism similarly affects multiple stages of TLS development. Germinal center (GC) B cells primarily rely on fatty acid oxidation (FAO) for aerobic metabolism to adapt to the low-glucose, high-fat tumor microenvironment; upon differentiation into plasma cells, they require glycolysis to support antibody synthesis, resulting in a phased switch of their metabolic program. This metabolic shift was first observed by Weisel et al. in immunological models, revealing that GC B cells exhibit low glycolytic activity and are highly dependent on fatty acid oxidation [173]. Plasma cells exhibit increased glucose uptake and pentose phosphate pathway activity to cope with the demands for the biosynthesis of large amounts of IgG [173]. If the glucose supply to the tumor is locally restricted, plasma cell production and IgG secretion are significantly inhibited, thereby weakening the effector function of TLSs [173, 174]. Furthermore, lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) is essential for GC B-cell affinity maturation, and its absence leads to a disruption of the germinal center response [175]. These studies provide an empirical foundation for understanding the mechanisms underlying metabolic reprogramming in B cells within TLSs.

Effects of microorganisms on TLSs

In addition to metabolism, microorganisms, particularly alterations in the gut microbiota, influence the tumor microenvironment. Microorganisms may significantly influence TLS formation and immune function by directly activating the immune system or modulating the local immune environment. For instance, the role of the gut microbiota in cancer immune responses is being increasingly elucidated, with specific microbial communities capable of increasing TLS formation and function, thereby increasing the efficacy of immunotherapy. This section explores the roles of microorganisms in TLS formation and analyzes how microbial modulation strategies can increase the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy.

In an infectious context, Koscsó et al. analyzed a Salmonella enteritis-infected model and observed that mucosa-resident CX3CR1hi Mφs (macrophages) recruit T cells and B cells to sites of Salmonella invasion via a CXCL13-dependent mechanism, inducing in situ Salmonella-specific CD4+ T-cell and germinal center B-cell responses, as well as B-cell class switching via translocation to lymphoid structures, thereby driving the local IgA response to infection [176]. Given that mucosal-resident immune cells generally possess dual proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory properties, TLSs may serve as crossroads for mucosal cellular immunity by guiding immune cells toward effector or regulatory phenotypes.

In the context of tumors, the gut microbiota is considered one of the key exogenous factors regulating the antitumor effects of TLSs. On the one hand, the gut microbiota can remotely influence TLS formation in distant organs such as the liver. Overacre-Delgoffe et al. found that colonizing the gut of colon cancer model mice with the bacterium Helicobacter hepaticus induces gut-specific Tfh cell production. These cells migrate to the tumor site, secrete CXCL13, and promote TLS maturation, thereby increasing antitumor effects [177]. Transfer of this Tfh population can restore the TLS architecture and immune responses in a germ-free environment, confirming that microbiota-specific T cells mediate a bridging effect from bacterial stimulation to tumor immunity [177]. Clinical studies have also indicated that the abundance of the Lachnospiraceae family in the intestines of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma is positively correlated with the TLS density in the tumor, suggesting that the microbiota influences TLS formation tendencies through remote immune remodeling [178, 179]. On the other hand, the tumor-associated microbiome (tumor-associated microbes) is closely spatially and functionally associated with TLSs. In HCC and breast cancer, higher tumor-associated bacterial loads are correlated with more numerous and more mature TLSs, and immune cells such as CD20+ B cells significantly accumulate in bacterial enrichment zones [180, 181]. However, this association is not essential. For instance, in aging bladders, TLS formation can be driven by age-related TNFα in the absence of a microbiome, highlighting the importance of tissue-specific factors [182].

In terms of the mechanisms by which microorganisms regulate tumor-associated lymphoid tissues (TLSs), microorganisms may not only serve as antigen sources but also trigger TLS induction programs through pattern recognition receptors. Bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) activates dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages via Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and Nod-like receptors (NLRs), triggering signaling pathways, including the NF-κB and STING pathways. These pathways promote the secretion of chemokines and proinflammatory cytokines, creating “fertile ground” for the initial formation of TLSs [183, 184]. Furthermore, the interaction between microbe-specific T cells and B cells mimics the germinal center response within lymph nodes, driving B-cell activation, class switching, and antibody production within TLSs [177, 185].

These findings provide a theoretical foundation for the therapeutic regulation of TLSs. On the one hand, vaccine strategies can increase the efficacy of the defensive barrier against intestinal pathogens by inducing the localized formation of TLSs to strengthen mucosal IgA responses [176]. On the other hand, activating the microbiota–T-cell signaling pathway in tumors through probiotics, STING agonists, or fecal microbiota transplantation can achieve immune amplification from the microbiota to tumors, thereby increasing the efficacy of immune checkpoint therapy [178, 186]. Furthermore, in autoimmune diseases such as IBD, abnormal TLS hyperreactivity is associated with dysbiosis. Inhibiting CX3CR1hi Mφs or eliminating specific bacterial genera may reverse TLS-related pathological inflammation [176]. Additionally, studies have shown that methylglyoxal (MG) derived from the gut microbiota can increase the efficacy of radioimmunotherapy in patients with colorectal cancer by triggering endoplasmic reticulum stress and activating cGAS–STING signaling. This process not only reduces Treg infiltration in tumors but also increases the presence of functional CD8+ T cells in the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [186]. This approach may serve as a potential avenue for regulating the function of tertiary lymphoid structures.

In summary, as a key immunostimulant for TLS formation, microorganisms can participate in protective immune construction while also triggering persistent inflammation and pathological responses. Their mechanisms of action span multiple levels, including continuous antigen stimulation, the activation of PRR signaling, the regulation of immune cell differentiation, and the modulation of metabolic products. This understanding opens new avenues for disease treatment. Given the dual nature of B cells and TLS function, broad-spectrum B-cell depletion therapies have shown limited efficacy in patients with conditions such as IBD. Future strategies should focus on precise modulation [187]. Dietary interventions, the use of probiotics/prebiotics or specific microbiota transplantation to “shape” beneficial microbial communities, thereby inducing the formation of protective TLSs, have emerged as promising new directions for increasing antitumor immunity or restoring tolerance in inflamed tissues [178, 188, 189]. In summary, an in-depth analysis of the regulatory axis linking the microbiome, TLSs, and immune cells will provide a critical theoretical foundation and practical strategies for developing next-generation precision immunotherapies targeting cancer and chronic inflammatory diseases.

TLS detection

Having reviewed how metabolism and microbiota influence TLS formation and function, we now turn to the challenge of accurately detecting TLSs. Whether in basic research or clinical practice, the ability to effectively detect and evaluate the presence, density, and functional maturity of TLSs is crucial for making informed immunotherapy decisions. Currently, numerous methods exist for TLS detection, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining serves as the standard method for the preliminary screening of TLSs. A widely cited criterion proposes a minimum threshold of 50 immunocellular nuclei within a distinct lymphoid aggregate to differentiate organized TLSs from scattered infiltrating immune cells [190]. While this criterion facilitates the quantification of TLS density, H&E staining alone lacks the lineage-specific contrast to distinguish B-cell zones from T-cell zones or to visualize follicular dendritic cell (FDC) networks. Consequently, accurate grading of TLS maturity—specifically the presence of germinal centers—without the use of confirmatory immunohistochemical (IHC) markers such as CD20 and CD23 is challenging [191]. Despite these limitations, advanced digital pathology offers new possibilities; for instance, Wang et al. developed an automated computational workflow to quantify the TLS density in whole-slide H&E images and showed that computationally derived TLS metrics can serve as independent prognostic biomarkers for lung adenocarcinoma [192]. Additionally, Barmpoutis et al. proposed an automated workflow based on digital images of H&E-stained sections to identify TLSs in lung cancer tissue and quantified the TLS density using this method. This approach was validated as an effective and efficient quantitative analytical tool, aiding in standardizing TLS assessments and serving as a prognostic biomarker for lung cancer [193].

In contrast, IHC and multiplexed imaging technologies play crucial roles in the precise identification of TLSs. IHC staining uses specific antibodies to label cellular populations within TLSs, such as B cells (CD20), T cells (CD3), and FDCs (CD21) [190, 194], to clearly delineate immune cell composition and distribution. By analyzing the distribution patterns of FDCs, B cells, and T cells, Sautès-Fridman et al. reported that IHC staining not only accurately reveals the presence of TLSs but also assesses their maturity and immune response status [10]. Additionally, multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF)/multiplex IHC staining enables the simultaneous labeling of a single section with multiple cellular markers. For instance, Quigley et al. established an Opal-TSA-based mIHC workflow that combines multiplex labeling with TLS grading criteria, providing a standardized approach for TLS detection in human and mouse samples [195]. As a high-throughput evolution of multiplex labeling, imaging mass cytometry (IMC) enables the simultaneous detection of even more protein markers. Le Rochais et al. used IMC to analyze TLSs from different organs and diseases and proposed a three-stage maturity grading method based on the CD20, CD21, and CD23 markers [196]. The advantage of IMC lies in its ability to provide high-resolution spatial data, which reveal the spatial distribution of different immune cells and help researchers understand the role of TLSs in tumor immunity, although this method requires expensive equipment and complex data processing [197].

For an in-depth molecular-level analysis, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics provide additional insights. Fan et al. analyzed TLS regions in breast cancer using scRNA-seq and revealed that interactions between B cells and T cells play a crucial role in immune responses [198]. However, the limitation of scRNA-seq technology lies in its inability to provide spatial localization information about cells; thus, it is typically used in combination with spatial transcriptomics. For example, Le Rochais et al. used spatial transcriptomics to investigate TLSs in gastrointestinal diseases, proposed a maturation grading standard, and described the advantages of this technology in deciphering the TLS microenvironment [196]. Cui et al. also emphasized that integrating scRNA-seq with spatial transcriptomics can help researchers comprehensively reveal the functional roles of cells within the tumor parenchyma and their contributions to tumor immunity [199].

Nevertheless, while current platforms (including spatial transcriptomics and IMC) provide unprecedented molecular resolution, they primarily operate on a two-dimensional (2D) plane. This inherent limitation, as noted in the context of IHC staining and advanced omics, can obscure the full spatial complexity of TLSs and their volumetric integration within the surrounding tissue.

In summary, each of the various TLS detection methods has distinct advantages and limitations. Selecting appropriate technologies is crucial for investigating the biological functions and clinical applications of TLSs. Current research trends involve the integration of multiple detection techniques, such as combining IHC staining with spatial transcriptomics, scRNA-seq, or IMC, to comprehensively analyze the immune microenvironment of TLSs and provide novel biomarkers for tumor immunotherapy.

The 12-chemokine gene expression signature (12-CK GES) is currently among the most widely used TLS gene signatures. It is composed of 12 chemokines (including CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CCL19, CCL21, CXCL9, and CXCL10) and was initially developed using samples from a cohort of patients with colorectal cancer [200]. A strong correlation was subsequently observed between the 12-CK GES and tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) in melanoma [201]. This gene set is primarily associated with the recruitment of T cells and B cells and their distribution within TLSs. For instance, CXCL13 and CCL19/21 play crucial roles in regulating B-cell and T-cell migration, and thus this signature has been widely used for identifying TLSs and assessing the immune response. These chemokines have predictive value for the immunotherapy response, prolonged patient survival, and TLS presence, particularly in hepatocellular carcinoma [91, 92], breast cancer [209], bladder cancer [210, 211], and renal clear cell carcinoma [212]. These findings have been validated across various cancers. Li et al. performed measurements and found that the 12-CK GES is also positively correlated with an increased IGS (immunogram score) and may serve as an effective biomarker for identifying potential responders to immunotherapy across diverse cancers [213]. The 12-CK GES has been validated through extensive research and across various cancers, and thus it represents the most thoroughly studied transcriptomic signature to date. Furthermore, the current understanding of chemokine-mediated cell transport strongly supports the use of the 12-CK GES as a widely used biomarker for TLS formation.

Cabrita et al. constructed a 9-gene signature by performing a differential expression analysis of melanoma samples with and without TLSs [59]. Studies have shown that these TLS features are prognostically significant in patients with metastatic melanoma in TCGA cohort and can also predict prolonged survival following CTLA-4 blockade therapy [59]. Similarly, through RNA sequencing, Helmink et al. identified B-cell-associated genes that were significantly more highly expressed in T cells than other immune markers were [62]. Both groups were able to distinguish responders from nonresponders in their respective ICB trials, but these features still need to be tested in ICB trials for patients with other tumor types. Similar to Helmink et al., who focused on single-cell features, Gu-Trantien et al. reported that the presence of Tfh cells in the TME was closely associated with robust immune cell infiltration and TLS formation in the tumor [66]. An 8-gene Tfh signature was designed to reflect the presence of TLSs. Hennequin et al. reported that Tbet+ T cells and CD20+ B-cell follicles in gastric cancer were correlated with prolonged recurrence-free survival and constructed 19 TLS gene signatures related to Th1 and B-cell niches through further research [202].

Additionally, other gene signatures have been described, including the “TLS-DSP” signature, which is composed of 20 genes [203], and the characteristics of five chemokine genes in TLS-positive groups of patients with Merkel cell carcinoma, as reported by Nakamura et al. [204]. Shang et al. established a four-gene TLS feature in CCA [205], hallmark genes [206], and plasma cell marker genes [22]. Notably, the expression of CXCL13 is strongly correlated with the expression of the 12-chemokine signature associated with TLSs, thus indicating that it is an independent gene signature [113, 207].

Although the aforementioned genetic signatures show good predictive power for TLSs, they generally fail to consider the dynamic progression and spatial localization of TLSs. Meylan’s spatial transcriptomic analysis revealed a TLS signature comprising 29 genes, which were enriched in immunoglobulin genes, plasma cell genes, and T-cell markers that characterize B-cell maturation into plasma cells in mature TLSs [53]. Wu et al. defined a novel TLS-50 signature for high-confidence TLS identification. In batch sequencing data from 239 HCC samples, the performance of the TLS-50 signature was comparable to that of the 12-chemokine signature in assessing TLS abundance [208]. Notably, Wu et al. also examined the correlation between gene expression levels and the distance between TLSs and tumors. Unlike most approaches that establish signature genes solely based on the presence of TLSs, Wang et al. developed 30 TLS signature genes by comparing gene expression data from TLSs with lymphoid compartments and from TLSs with other nonimmune-related compartments. These signatures displayed prognostic and predictive value for TNBC immunotherapy and included 11 B-cell-specific genes, 6 TLS-initiating genes, 4 immunoglobulin genes, 3 T-cell-specific genes, 4 immune response-associated genes, and the TCL1A and ATP2A3 genes [64]. As mentioned earlier, Tang et al. proposed the concepts of “conforming TLSs” and “deviating TLSs” while also analyzing the fundamental characteristics of these two developmental trajectories [30].

Currently, many studies select the most stable subset of these classic genes within a specific tumor cohort to construct a weighted TLS score for predictive modeling. For instance, Ma et al. analyzed pancreatic cancer samples and established a risk score model based on TLS scores derived from the 12-gene and 9-gene signatures. This model categorizes patients into high-risk and low-risk groups, with the low-risk group exhibiting increased immune cell infiltration and an improved prognosis [214]. Xu Z. et al. constructed a TLS-associated prognostic risk model comprising 14 genes and validated it in TCGA and GEO datasets. They identified TLS-associated subclusters and pinpointed key genes, including PRRX1—a potential immunoregulatory factor and therapeutic target—whose expression was increased in TLS-positive CRC cohorts [215].

These analyses collectively suggest that the 12-CK signature serves as a widely used surrogate. Although existing studies have revealed multiple TLS-associated gene signatures and provided theoretical foundations for tumor immunotherapy, numerous challenges and unresolved questions remain. Future research should focus on monitoring the dynamic changes in gene signatures and TLSs during immunotherapy and elucidating their relationships with the immunotherapy response.

Artificial induction of TLSs

Finally, in addition to detecting TLSs, we must also explore how to artificially induce TLS formation to increase the efficacy of immunotherapy. The artificial induction of TLSs not only represents a new strategy for cancer immunotherapy but may also become crucial for reversing tumor immune escape. By manipulating biomaterials, immune cells, and specific metabolic or microbial pathways, we can precisely control TLS formation and function, thereby amplifying immune responses (Fig. 7). Direct induction

This class of strategies focuses on exogenously supplementing or activating key signaling molecules, such as CCL19, CCL21, CXCL13, IL-7, and LTβR/LIGHT (TNFSF14) [216–220], and reprogramming and supplementing organizer-like matrix cells, including FRC-like cells and CCL19+ CAFs [221, 222], to establish T/B compartments and PNAd+ high endothelial venules (HEVs) locally at the lesion site and promote FDC/GC maturation. Early studies induced TLS formation in rats through the transgenic overexpression of IL-7 or CCL21 [219, 223]. Recent studies have demonstrated that LTβR agonists can promote tumor-localized TLS induction in rats, revealing the potential for combining cytokines or chemokines with immunotherapy to increase induction rates. We discuss this possibility further in the immunotherapy section [224–226]. Moreover, the codelivery of CXCL13 and CCL21 successfully induced TLS formation in animal models of melanoma [218]. These strategies have well-defined mechanisms, but most evidence remains confined to animal studies with exogenous induction or early/immunology-focused human research; mature TLSs (including FDCs/GCs) have yet to be systematically established as the primary clinical histological endpoint. Future development should focus on optimizing the safety window for local/sustained-release delivery, such as microrobots [227], increasing maturation through programmed combinations (e.g., CXCL13+CCL21 or STING+LTβR), and prospectively incorporating histological and functional readouts in human studies. The 12-chemokine gene signature mentioned earlier may serve as a starting point. With respect to matrix cell supplementation, as early as 2004, the formation of TLSs containing GCs was successfully induced by transplanting spongy bovine collagen scaffolds containing matrix cells into the subcapsular space of mouse kidneys [228]. Examples of TLSs induced by the subcutaneous injection of a stromal cell line derived from lymph nodes have also been reported [229]. Recently, researchers have proposed the potential of CCL19 PvCs (perivascular cells) to induce TLS formation. These findings indicate that more readily obtainable induction cells, an increased induction efficiency, and integration with genome editing should become complementary approaches to the use of stromal cells.

Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy can induce or amplify TLSs in vivo, often through the indirect activation of signaling pathways or cytokines involved in TLS formation. In mouse tumor models, an oncolytic herpesvirus (oHSV) can recruit TCF1+ CD8+ T cells and promote TLS formation via the CXCL10–CXCR3 axis [230]. In patients with high-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN2/3), the formation of TLSs and the clonal expansion of TLSs can be observed in regressing lesions following vaccination with human papillomavirus oncoprotein vaccines [231]. Similarly, therapeutic vaccination using an irradiated allogeneic granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor-secreting pancreatic tumor vaccine (GVAX) in combination with cyclophosphamide induces TLS formation in the vast majority of patients with pancreatic cancer [232]. Additionally, low-dose STING agonists administered intratumorally promote vascular normalization, increase the number of dendritic cells/effector T cells, and upregulate the expression of factors associated with TLS development, including Ccl19, Ccl21, Lta, Ltb, and Light, accompanied by the emergence of in situ TLSs [63]. Moreover, these two immunotherapies can be combined: oncolytic adenoviruses promote vascular normalization and the formation of nonclassical tertiary lymphoid structures through STING-mediated dendritic cell activation [233]. The combination of anti-VEGFR2 and anti-PD-L1 antibodies induces the formation of multimodal PNAd+ HEVs (a critical structure for TLSs) [234, 235]. Although no evidence exists that this method alone induces the formation of mature TLSs, it may be combined with other maturation induction regimens to increase induction efficiency. As mentioned earlier, in addition to its inherent induction potential, immunotherapy can also be paired with cytokines or chemokines. Recent studies have confirmed that the simultaneous activation of STING and lymphotoxin β receptors can induce B-cell responses in tertiary lymphoid structures and increase antitumor immunity [225]. The delivery of Ad-CCL21-DC (cell-based immunotherapy), which expresses CCL21 for targeting tumors, was safe and feasible in phase I trials while inducing systemic immune responses, although TLS histology was not the primary endpoint. Notably, 7 × 19 CAR-T cells (CAR-T cells expressing IL-7 and CCL19) secrete IL-7+CCL19 within tumors, increasing immune cell infiltration and CAR-T-cell persistence, and highlighting the potential of immunotherapy as a delivery platform [236, 237]. Although these two technologies have not yet been used to induce the formation of TLSs, based on the preceding discussion regarding the roles of IL-7, CCL19, and CCL21 in TLS formation, they may serve as potential research avenues for TLS induction. The vascular targeting peptide VTP effectively delivers LIGHT to tumors upon binding. Researchers have observed the formation of high endothelial venules (HEVs) within tumors in response to LIGHT, as well as the formation of spontaneous TLSs resembling those found in GISTs in patients [238]. These findings indicate the delivery effect of immunotherapy. Notably, while immunotherapy can sometimes induce TLS formation, the overall effect is not necessarily positive. Stimulating B cells with αCD40 can induce TLS, but because the systemic induction of CD11b+ regulatory B cells (Bregs) suppresses T-cell responses, αCD40 does not confer a survival benefit [239].

Extracellular vesicles (EVs)

In studies on induced TLSs, extracellular vesicles (EVs), such as exosomes, have been extensively utilized to deliver immune cytokines. In mouse models, exosome-like vesicles (ApoExos) derived from injured vascular tissues promote the recruitment and activation of γδ T cells, thereby inducing TLS formation [240]. Another study demonstrated that exosome-derived stem cells treated with radiotherapy can activate endogenous immune pathways within the glioblastoma microenvironment, reverse immunosuppression, and promote the formation of TLSs [241]. Moreover, the emergence of more exosome-like complexes and engineered exosomes [242, 243] has allowed the development of EV-based platforms with increased capacities to load immune cytokines and improved targeting capabilities and stability.

Scaffold platform

As an emerging strategy, scaffold platforms can provide structural support, deliver immune factors, and promote immune cell organization to mimic and increase TLS formation. Researchers have developed an injectable hydrogel drug delivery system capable of effectively delivering CXCL13 and LIGHT, promoting TLS maturation, inhibiting tumor growth, and extending mouse survival. Furthermore, combining this hydrogel with anti-PD-1 immune checkpoint inhibitors resulted in increased antitumor efficacy [244]. Another study proposed a hierarchical protein hydrogel platform designed to promote the formation of artificial tertiary lymphoid structures (aTLSs). This platform provides structural support and functional adaptability through a combination of self-assembled protein nanocages, recombinant elastin, and the CP05 peptide. It effectively encapsulates extracellular vesicles, facilitating the formation of aTLSs with germinal center-like structures and thereby increasing the antitumor efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [245]. Moreover, compared with traditional hydrogels, porous 3D scaffolds recruit many immune cells, endowing them with functions similar to those of real lymphoid organs and thereby forming an “artificial tertiary lymphoid structure” [246]. Additionally, the use of covalent organic frameworks (COFs) plays an important role in TLS formation. The COF platform developed by Zhang et al. promotes TLS formation by facilitating the maturation, proliferation, and migration of T cells and B cells through the excessive secretion of cytokines [247]. This strategy exerts potent synergistic antitumor effects with phototherapy combined with a COF and immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as CTLA-4 inhibitors), providing new insights into cancer immunotherapy in the future.

Viral vectors and microbiota

Frontier research on the induction of TLSs by microorganisms focuses primarily on activating the LTβR/LIGHT axis through gene delivery or microbial release, promoting high-endothelial venule (HEV) formation, increasing immune cell infiltration, and improving antitumor immune responses. Ramachandran et al. reported that systemic AAV-LIGHT therapy induced the formation of tumor-associated HEVs and T-cell-rich TLSs in a mouse glioblastoma model [248]. Given that AAV can promote sustained gene expression, the long-term induction of TLSs has significant clinical importance, particularly for treating tumors with immune evasion and drug resistance. Certain natural microorganisms (such as gut probiotics) can be engineered for the targeted delivery of immune-activating factors or cytokines. Early studies revealed that Salmonella typhimurium expressing CCL21 can inhibit tumor growth [249]. Recently, Mi et al. engineered an attenuated Salmonella strain that naturally colonizes tumors to overexpress, lyse, and release LIGHT—a member of the TNF superfamily—thereby inducing the formation of mature TLSs [99]. Additionally, the oncolytic adenoviruses we discussed earlier show great promise in inducing TLS formation. Compared with traditional immunotherapies, microorganisms can provide broader immune activation, are less susceptible to immune escape or immune resistance, and possess the capacity for sustained immune activation [250]. In particular, through the engineered design of tumor-specific microorganisms, these microbes can achieve precise immune modulation, further increasing the antitumor immune response.

Engineering and organoids

In the preceding section, we briefly discussed several engineering applications (such as engineered exosomes and microorganisms). One study designed a recombinant human IL-33 protein capable of activating intratumoral ILC2s to induce TLS formation [251]. Research has also revealed that coculture with 3D lymphocyte spheroids increases the expression of additional FRC markers, particularly CCL21, on MSCs. After implantation into adipose tissue, the lymphocyte organoids derived from mesenchymal stem cells maintained structural integrity and exhibited extensive T-cell infiltration and partial vascularization in vivo after 15 days. Although organized B-cell follicles and FDC markers remained absent, the results of this study indicate the potential of MSCs for inducing TLS formation. Additionally, human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) carrying a modifiable ETV2 allele were used to induce the differentiation of three-dimensional organoids (HEVOs) into HEV-like structures. Following transplantation, these organoids formed mouse lymphoid structures that exhibited a TLS-like anatomy [252]. These bioengineering strategies represent a paradigm shift in organoid technology—moving beyond the ‘epithelium-only’ focus toward the holistic reconstruction of the tumor microenvironment (TME). As highlighted in recent reviews of organoid coculture models [253], the integration of MSCs and stem cell-derived vascular structures is not only a feat of tissue engineering but also a necessary step toward achieving biological fidelity for precision medicine.

However, from a clinical translation perspective, the persistent absence of organized B-cell follicles and germinal centers in these models underscores a critical gap: while we can achieve ‘structural mimicry,’ ‘functional maturation’ remains elusive. The value of these engineered TLS-like structures lies in their potential as dynamic drug-testing platforms. By incorporating these complex immune hubs, organoid models can transcend simple cytotoxicity assays, allowing clinicians to predict how a patient’s unique TME might respond to TLS-inducing therapies or immune checkpoint inhibitors. Ultimately, the evolution of these coculture systems is pivotal for bridging the gap between laboratory observations and personalized clinical outcomes.

Limitations and controversies

While the prognostic value of TLSs is widely recognized, several limitations and controversies remain. First, the heterogeneity of TLSs poses a challenge for clinical interpretation. Not all TLSs are beneficial; structures enriched with regulatory T cells (Tregs) or immunosuppressive B cells may dampen antitumor immunity and correlate with poor outcomes. Second, standardization is lacking. Current studies use varying definitions of TLS maturity and inconsistent scoring systems. The prognostic effect of TLSs can also be confounded by their spatial distribution, as intratumoral TLSs may function differently from peritumoral TLSs. Finally, the therapeutic risks must be considered. Artificial induction of TLSs has the potential risk of triggering off-target autoimmune responses or exacerbating chronic inflammation, which could paradoxically promote tumorigenesis in certain contexts. Therefore, a more granular characterization of the TLS composition and function is essential before their clinical implementation as biomarkers or therapeutic targets.

Conclusions

Research on TLSs is transitioning from descriptive studies to more in-depth mechanistic analyses and clinical interventions. The core conclusion of this review is that TLS function is far from static; it is highly dependent on the maturity, anatomical location, and disease microenvironment in which the TLS resides. This interplay determines its dual identity as both a benefit and a detriment. In the field of tumor immunology, substantial clinical evidence has established the positive value of mature TLSs. For instance, in melanoma and NSCLC, the presence and maturity of TLSs serve as potent biomarkers for predicting patients’ overall survival and response to immune checkpoint inhibitors, with their value independent of PD-L1 expression levels. Their beneficial mechanism lies in concentrating the complete immune circuit, which includes antigen presentation, T-cell activation, B-cell affinity maturation, and antibody production. Recent studies, such as a spatial transcriptomic analysis of hepatocellular carcinoma [30], have revealed how specific metabolic pathways, such as tryptophan metabolism, regulate TLS maturation, providing new targets for combined interventions. However, in the context of autoimmunity and chronic inflammation, TLSs exhibit distinct pathological properties. In individuals with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and Sjögren’s syndrome (SS), TLSs within synovial membranes and salivary glands function as germinal centers, continuously producing pathogenic autoantibodies (e.g., anti-CCP and anti-Ro/La) and correlating with an increased lymphoma risk. In the lungs and small airways of COPD patients, the renal interstitium of patients with IgA nephropathy, and the adventitia of atherosclerotic vessels (forming arterial tertiary lymphoid organs (ATLOs)), the presence of TLSs is closely linked to more severe tissue damage, functional decline, and a poor prognosis of the disease. For example, in a prospective cohort of IgA nephropathy patients, renal interstitial TLS occurrence was associated with higher serum creatinine levels and an increased disease progression risk [152]. Advancements in research techniques serve as the key driving force propelling progress in this field. Detection methods have evolved from a basic screen using H&E staining to mIHC/mIF staining, imaging mass cytometry (IMC), and spatial transcriptomics for in-depth characterization. These approaches enable the precise quantification of the cellular composition, spatial conformation, and molecular features of TLSs. Concurrently, multiple validated TLS-associated gene signatures have been developed. Examples include the universal 12-chemokine (12-CK) signature, the 9-gene signature established by Cabrita et al. in melanoma, and the Tfh signature proposed by Gu-Trantien et al. for breast cancer. These signatures provide powerful tools for objectively evaluating TLSs within transcriptomic data. Based on the understanding of the aforementioned mechanisms, artificially inducing TLSs has emerged as an active therapeutic exploration direction. Strategies exhibit diversity, ranging from the direct supplementation of core chemokines such as CXCL13 and CCL21 to the activation of developmental signaling pathways using LTβR agonists and from the expression of LIGHT within colorectal cancer tumors via attenuated Salmonella to induce the formation of mature TLSs to the development of injectable hydrogels as biomaterial scaffolds for sustained cytokine release. These approaches have the ability to promote TLS formation and increase antitumor immunity in preclinical models. In the future, this field faces several core challenges and opportunities. First, standardizing TLS detection and grading is essential for its adoption as a routine biomarker. Second, we must improve our understanding of the molecular switches regulating TLS functional states to enable precise contextual interventions, such as inducing the protective function of TLSs in tumors while suppressing TLS pathological activity in autoimmune diseases. Finally, while clinical trials targeting pathways such as LTβR in autoimmune diseases have yielded limited results, combining such approaches with tumor immunotherapies (e.g., immune checkpoint inhibitors) holds significant promise. Ultimately, deepening our understanding and achieving the precise regulation of TLSs may revolutionize therapeutic strategies for diseases spanning from cancer to chronic inflammatory diseases.
