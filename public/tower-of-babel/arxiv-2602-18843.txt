ABD : Default–Exception Abduction in Finite First-Order Worlds
Serafim Batzoglou

Abstract
Abduction in knowledge representation is often framed as “explaining away” inconsistencies between a background theory and observations by hypothesizing missing facts or exceptions. Despite decades of KR work on abduction, there are few modern benchmarks that (i) require genuine first-order relational reasoning, (ii) admit unambiguous, solver-checkable verification, and (iii) produce informative error analyses rather than binary right/wrong judgments. We introduce ABD , a family of default–exception abduction tasks over small finite relational worlds. Each instance provides (a) a set of finite structures with observed facts, and (b) a fixed default-like first-order theory that may be violated by those observations. A model must output a first-order abnormality rule α ⁡ ( x ) \alpha(x) that defines an exception predicate 𝖠𝖻 ⁡ ( x ) ↔ α ⁡ ( x ) \mathsf{Ab}(x)\leftrightarrow\alpha(x) , restoring satisfiability while keeping exceptions sparse. We formalize three observation regimes with distinct completion semantics. ABD-Full assumes closed-world observation. ABD-Partial allows unknown atoms under existential completion: α \alpha is valid if some completion makes the repaired theory satisfiable, with cost optimized in the best case. ABD-Skeptical uses universal completion: α \alpha is valid only if the repaired theory is satisfiable under every completion, with cost measured in the worst case. Because domains are finite, validity and costs are computed via SMT (Z3), enabling exact verification and controlled difficulty. We evaluate ten frontier LLMs on 600 instances spanning all three scenarios and seven default theories. The best models achieve 90-99% training validity, but parsimony gaps of ∼ 1 {\sim}1 – 1.5 1.5 extra exceptions per world remain. Holdout evaluation reveals distinct generalization profiles: in ABD-Full and ABD-Partial, the dominant failure is parsimony inflation ; in ABD-Skeptical, it is validity brittleness —rules that work on training often break on holdouts—while survivors show smaller gap inflation.

Introduction

Default reasoning is a cornerstone of knowledge representation: we often model domains with
rules that hold “normally,” while allowing rare exceptions.
In classical KR formalisms—default logic
[
42
]
, circumscription
[
29
]
, and stable-model semantics
[
20
]
—exceptions are
not bugs but a feature: they encode domain irregularities without abandoning general rules.
From the perspective of
abduction

[
39
,
18
]
, exceptions are precisely what we infer when observations conflict
with what a default theory predicts.

Modern language and reasoning models can emit syntactically valid logical expressions,
including quantified first-order formulas.

Can such models propose correct, compact exception rules that repair default theories across multiple
relational worlds?

Answering this requires an evaluation setting that removes natural-language ambiguity, supports quantifiers,
and remains mechanically checkable.

We propose
ABD
, a benchmark suite for default–exception abduction on finite first-order worlds.
Each instance consists of several small relational structures (“worlds”) and a fixed first-order background
theory
\Theta
that uses an abnormality predicate
\mathsf{Ab}
to block defaults.
Rather than providing labeled targets to be matched, we provide only
the theory and the observed facts; the model must output a definition of abnormality: a first-order
formula
\alpha(x)
, such that
\mathsf{Ab}(x)\leftrightarrow\alpha(x)
makes all worlds satisfiable.
To make the task genuinely abductive, we score
parsimony
: among valid hypotheses, we prefer those
that mark as few abnormal domain elements as possible.

The output
\alpha(x)
is a unary concept, but its role is not to predict labels.
Instead it is a
repair policy
that negotiates the tension between (i) a theory that expresses
default regularities and (ii) concrete relational observations that may violate them.
The objective is therefore not to match a provided target extension, but to restore consistency while
introducing minimal exceptional structure.

Three Observation Regimes.

We study three scenarios that share the same interface but differ in how missing information is handled:

ABD-Full

(closed-world),
ABD-Partial
(existential completion: valid if
some
completion satisfies the repaired theory), and
ABD-Skeptical
(universal completion: valid only if
every
completion satisfies the repaired theory).
Parsimony mirrors these quantifiers: best-case cost in ABD-Partial, worst-case in ABD-Skeptical.
These regimes are formalized in Section
4
.

Evaluation Beyond Validity.

Binary validity quickly saturates for stronger models. However, validity is in principle trivial in
this task, by labeling many (or all) elements as abnormal. We therefore emphasize
cost-based
metrics—how
close a model comes to a solver-computed lower bound on abnormality count—and
size-conditioned
variants
that group formulas by syntactic complexity (AST size) to discourage degenerate case-splitting.

Main Empirical Findings.

We evaluate ten frontier models across all three regimes. Holdout evaluation reveals two distinct failure
modes:
parsimony inflation
in ABD-Full/Partial (gaps roughly double on fresh worlds)
and
validity brittleness
in ABD-Skeptical (rules that work on training break on holdouts, though
survivors show smaller gap inflation). Models differ qualitatively in their performance profiles,
pointing to different reasoning strategies. Details are in Section
6
.

worst-case, robust exception costs).

•

and size-conditioned analyses by formula complexity.

•

Related Work

Abduction, Defaults, and Non-Monotonic Reasoning.

Abduction is often formalized as selecting hypotheses that, when added to background knowledge, entail observations while satisfying integrity constraints, typically with a preference for parsimonious explanations
[
39
,
27
,
18
]
.
This connects directly to non-monotonic KR frameworks for defeasible rules and exceptions, including default logic and circumscription
[
42
,
29
]
, and stable-model/answer-set semantics that support explicit minimization principles (including “minimize abnormalities” encodings)
[
20
,
5
,
7
,
19
]
.
Our benchmark adopts a solver-friendly abnormality-predicate encoding with finite grounding, but evaluates more than satisfiability: we quantify
parsimony gaps
between a model’s proposed exception rule and a solver-computed minimum-exception lower bound, and we test whether the learned exception policy remains valid under held-out worlds.

Abductive Logic Programming and Diagnosis.

Abductive logic programming (ALP) provides a general framework for explanation under integrity constraints and has been developed in both logic-programming and KR traditions
[
27
,
15
]
.
Model-based diagnosis similarly treats faults/abnormalities as latent causes chosen to restore consistency, often with minimality criteria and hitting-set structure
[
43
,
12
,
23
]
.
Our setting differs in interface and emphasis: the hypothesis is an explicit
first-order definition
of an abnormality predicate (not a set of abduced ground facts), and it must satisfy a
multi-world
specification, which makes it possible to separate repair-by-case from compact exception structure that generalizes.

Inductive Logic Programming and Relational Learning.

ILP learns relational rules from examples and background knowledge, with classic systems and
formalisms for inducing Horn clauses and relational definitions
[
33
,
32
,
40
,
47
,
10
,
41
]
.
While our hypotheses are symbolic and relational, our tasks are not supervised
concept learning: each instance provides observations plus a default theory, and success depends
on proposing an exception rule that restores satisfiability while minimizing abnormalities.
This shifts the challenge from matching labels to balancing consistency and parsimony under an
explicit non-monotonic objective, with exact verification.
Synthesizing
\alpha(x)
from multi-world specifications is also amenable to symbolic ILP and ASP-based learners such as Progol
[
34
]
and ILASP
[
28
]
, which can search a hypothesis space with completeness guarantees relative to a language bias.
Our goal is not to position LLMs as replacements for such solvers but to use the task as a diagnostic: because ABD instances have mechanically checkable solutions, the benchmark quantifies how closely neural models approximate the output of an exact combinatorial search, and where they systematically fall short (validity failures, parsimony inflation, holdout brittleness).

Program Synthesis and Solver-Aided Search.

Our tasks can be viewed as constrained synthesis: produce a symbolic artifact that satisfies a solver-checkable specification.
This aligns with solver-aided languages and synthesis paradigms such as Sketch, CEGIS, and syntax-guided synthesis
[
45
,
46
,
1
,
49
]
.
A key difference is that our specifications are
multi-world
and explicitly
costed
: hypotheses must simultaneously repair multiple structures, and evaluation tracks both validity and the gap between a model’s abnormality cost and a solver-computed lower bound.

Logic and Relational Reasoning Benchmarks.

A broad set of benchmarks probe reasoning with rules, quantifiers, and structured knowledge, often through natural-language interfaces
[
48
,
24
,
8
]
.
Such settings are valuable but can conflate logical competence with linguistic priors and make intermediate steps hard to verify.
Our benchmark instead presents extensional finite structures and uses solver-backed grounding to make correctness and costs exact, enabling diagnostics about exception sparsity, gap above a solver lower bound, and overfitting across held-out worlds.

Mathematical Reasoning and Formal Proof Benchmarks.

Natural-language math benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH test multi-step reasoning but remain text-mediated
[
9
,
25
]
.
Formal proof benchmarks (e.g., CoqGym, HOList, miniF2F, ProofNet) evaluate mechanically verified outputs in proof assistants
[
53
,
4
,
54
,
3
]
, and large-scale theorem-proving efforts use learning to guide deductive search
[
38
]
.
Our benchmark is complementary: rather than proving theorems, models must
synthesize compact exception definitions
that reconcile defaults with observations under a minimum-abnormality objective.

Neuro-Symbolic Learning and Large Language Models.

Neuro-symbolic work explores combining learning with symbolic representations and constraint-based verification
[
17
]
, including approaches that use abduction as a learning signal
[
11
]
.
Recent LLM analyses highlight that models can produce syntactically plausible symbolic outputs while relying on shortcuts or overly long solutions when unconstrained
[
50
,
16
]
.
Our evaluation emphasizes solver-verifiable semantics, parsimony-sensitive metrics (cost and gap), and held-out-world testing designed to expose hypotheses that "repair" observed worlds without capturing a stable general exception rule.

Abductive Reasoning in Natural Language.

Abduction has also been studied in NLP as a model of commonsense explanation and narrative understanding, including classic logical accounts of interpretation-as-abduction
[
26
]
and benchmark-style tasks for causal/abductive inference
[
44
,
31
,
6
]
.
These tasks are valuable for modeling everyday explanation but are mediated by language and typically lack a fully explicit world model with exact cost verification.
By contrast, our benchmark isolates abductive structure in a formal finite-model setting where validity and costs are mechanically checkable and have a direct interpretation as exception cardinality.

3
Problem Setup

Finite Relational Worlds.

We fix a relational signature

\Sigma=\{P,Q,R,S,=\}
,
with unary predicates
P(\cdot),Q(\cdot)
, binary predicates
R(\cdot,\cdot),S(\cdot,\cdot)
, and equality.
A
world

W
has a finite domain
D_{W}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
(
|D_{W}|\in\{9{-}12\}
; see Table
2
) and (possibly partial) interpretations for
the predicates in
\Sigma
.
In the fully observed setting (ABD-Full),
W
specifies complete interpretations

P^{W},Q^{W}\subseteq D_{W}
and
R^{W},S^{W}\subseteq D_{W}\times D_{W}
.
In the partial-observation settings (ABD-Partial and ABD-Skeptical),
W
additionally designates a set of

unknown
ground atoms
\Omega_{W}
whose truth values are not observed.
In our benchmark,
\Omega_{W}
ranges over ground atoms of
R
and
S
;
P
and
Q
are always fully observed.
In ABD, the world does
not
include labels for a target concept to be predicted.

Background Theories with Abnormality.

Each instance specifies a fixed first-order theory
\Theta
over an extended signature

\Sigma_{\mathsf{Ab}}=\Sigma\cup\{\mathsf{Ab}\}
, where
\mathsf{Ab}(\cdot)
is a unary abnormality predicate.
We use
\Theta
to express default-like constraints that may be blocked by abnormality.
A common pattern is an implication schema of the form

\forall x\;\Big(\mathrm{Ante}(x)\;\wedge\;\neg\mathsf{Ab}(x)\;\rightarrow\;\mathrm{Cons}(x)\Big),

(1)

where
\mathrm{Ante}
and
\mathrm{Cons}
are first-order formulas over
\Sigma
(often involving
existentials over
R,S
).
Intuitively, elements satisfying the antecedent are expected to satisfy the consequent
unless

they are declared abnormal.

Hypotheses as Definitions of Abnormality.

A model outputs a single first-order formula
\alpha(x)
(one free variable) over a restricted
set of allowed predicates (specified per instance).
We interpret the prediction as a
definition
:

\forall x\;\big(\mathsf{Ab}(x)\leftrightarrow\alpha(x)\big).

(2)

This definitional view avoids ambiguity about whether
\mathsf{Ab}
is an independent choice: it is entirely
determined by
\alpha
.

Symbolic Output Language.

To support exact evaluation,
\alpha(x)
is produced in a simple S-expression syntax (atoms, connectives,
and first-order quantifiers) that is parsed deterministically.
The grammar admits boolean connectives (
and
,
or
,
not
,
implies
),
first-order quantifiers (
forall
,
exists
), and atomic predicates from the allowed signature.

Unknown Atoms and Completions.

In partial worlds, each ground atom of
R
and
S
is either
known true
,
known false
, or

unknown
.
Let
\Omega_{W}
be the set of unknown atoms in
W
.
A
completion

\mathcal{C}
assigns a truth value to every atom in
\Omega_{W}
, yielding a fully
specified world
W^{\mathcal{C}}
.
We write
\mathrm{KnownFacts}(W)
for the conjunction of all observed positive facts and observed negative facts in
W

(treating atoms not listed as true or unknown as observed false).
In fully observed worlds,
\mathrm{KnownFacts}(W)
coincides with
\mathrm{Facts}(W)
.
For a completion
\mathcal{C}
, we take
\mathrm{Facts}(W^{\mathcal{C}})
to be
\mathrm{KnownFacts}(W)
together with the literals
that fix each atom in
\Omega_{W}
according to
\mathcal{C}
.

Finite Grounding and Solver Checks.

All domains are finite, so satisfiability of
\Theta
under a predicted
\alpha
can be checked by grounding
quantifiers over
D_{W}
and translating the result to SMT.
In partial worlds, unknown atoms in
\Omega_{W}
are represented as free Boolean variables in the SMT encoding,
so we can quantify over completions by changing which constraints are asserted.
Existential-completion validity (ABD-Partial) reduces to a satisfiability check with unknowns left free,
whereas skeptical validity (ABD-Skeptical) reduces to the absence of a counterexample completion (checked as a single
UNSAT query that asks whether there exists a completion consistent with
\mathrm{KnownFacts}(W)
under which some grounded axiom fails
given
\mathsf{Ab}\leftrightarrow\alpha
).
Because costs are cardinalities over a finite domain, we can compute parsimony objectives exactly (or with
thresholded SAT checks) using Z3
[
13
]
.

We formalize the existential and universal completion semantics as synthesis problems in Section
4
.

4
Default–Exception Abduction Tasks

We now define the three ABD task variants as formal synthesis problems.
Fix an instance
\mathcal{I}
with background theory
\Theta
and training worlds

\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tr}}=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{k}\}
.
The learner outputs
\alpha(x)
and is evaluated by (i) validity and (ii) parsimony.

4.1
ABD-Full: Abduction Under Full Observation

In ABD-Full, each world
W
provides a complete interpretation of
P,Q,R,S
under a closed-world assumption
for the given domain (any unlisted atom is false).
A hypothesis
\alpha
is
valid
on a world
W
if the repaired theory is satisfiable:

W\models_{\mathrm{ABD}}\alpha\;\Longleftrightarrow\\
\mathrm{SAT}\big(\Theta\wedge\mathrm{Facts}(W)\wedge\forall x(\mathsf{Ab}(x)\leftrightarrow\alpha(x))\big).

(3)

An
\alpha
solves an instance iff it is valid on
all
training worlds.

Cost.

For a valid hypothesis, we define the per-world abnormality count

\mathrm{cost}_{W}(\alpha)=|\{a\in D_{W}:W\models\alpha[a]\}|.

The instance-level cost is the sum across worlds:

\mathrm{Cost}(\alpha;\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tr}})=\sum_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tr}}}\mathrm{cost}_{W}(\alpha).

(4)

Lower-Bound Baseline and Gap.

To measure parsimony, we compare a model’s cost to a solver-computed lower bound obtained by
allowing
\mathsf{Ab}
to be assigned freely (not necessarily definable by a single
\alpha
).
Concretely, for each world we compute

\mathrm{OptCost}(W)=\min_{\mathsf{Ab}\subseteq D_{W}}\;|\mathsf{Ab}|\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;\mathrm{SAT}\big(\Theta\wedge\mathrm{Facts}(W)\big),

(5)

and define
\mathrm{OptCost}(\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tr}})=\sum_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tr}}}\mathrm{OptCost}(W)
.
We report the
gap

\mathrm{Gap}(\alpha)=\mathrm{Cost}(\alpha;\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tr}})-\mathrm{OptCost}(\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tr}}).

Because
\mathrm{OptCost}
relaxes the single-formula constraint and allows
\mathsf{Ab}

to vary freely by world, it lower-bounds the best
definable
cost; thus

\mathrm{Gap}(\alpha)
conservatively overestimates suboptimality. On small domains
the bound can often be matched by large extensional case-splits,
motivating our AST- and holdout-based diagnostics.

This baseline is nevertheless solver-verifiable, inexpensive compared to full joint optimization,
and empirically discriminative across models.

4.2
ABD-Partial: Existential Completion

ABD-Partial introduces missing information about the observed predicates (Section
3
).
Recall that
\Omega_{W}
is the set of unknown atoms in
W
and
W^{\mathcal{C}}
denotes the completed world under completion
\mathcal{C}
.

Validity Under Existential Completion.

A hypothesis
\alpha
is valid on
W
if there exists a completion that makes the repaired theory satisfiable:

W\models_{\exists\mathrm{comp}}\alpha\;\Longleftrightarrow\\
\exists\mathcal{C}:\mathrm{SAT}\big(\Theta\wedge\mathrm{Facts}(W^{\mathcal{C}})\wedge\forall x(\mathsf{Ab}(x)\leftrightarrow\alpha(x))\big).

(6)

Cost Optimized Over Completions.

Because
\alpha
may mention unknown relations, its extension can depend on the completion.
We define cost as the minimum abnormality count over completions that satisfy the repaired theory:

\mathrm{cost}_{W}^{\exists}(\alpha)=\min_{\mathcal{C}}\;|\{a\in D_{W}:W^{\mathcal{C}}\models\alpha[a]\}|\\
\text{s.t.}\;\mathrm{SAT}\!\big(\Theta\wedge\mathrm{Facts}(W^{\mathcal{C}})\wedge\forall x(\mathsf{Ab}(x)\leftrightarrow\alpha(x))\big).

(7)

Instance cost sums these per-world values, and gaps are defined relative to a completion-aware lower bound
that also leaves
\mathsf{Ab}
unconstrained:

\mathrm{OptCost}^{\exists}(W)=\min_{\mathcal{C},\,\mathsf{Ab}\subseteq D_{W}}\;|\mathsf{Ab}|\\
\text{s.t.}\;\mathrm{SAT}\big(\Theta\wedge\mathrm{Facts}(W^{\mathcal{C}})\big).

(8)

As in ABD-Full, the free-
\mathsf{Ab}
lower bound is conservative (Section
4.1
).

4.3
ABD-Skeptical: Universal Completion

ABD-Skeptical retains partial observation but treats unknown atoms conservatively.
Intuitively, a hypothesis should repair the default theory not only for
some
completion of missing facts,
but for
all
completions consistent with what is observed.

Validity Under Universal Completion.

A hypothesis
\alpha
is
skeptically valid
on
W
if the repaired theory is satisfiable for every completion:

W\models_{\forall\mathrm{comp}}\alpha\;\Longleftrightarrow\\
\forall\mathcal{C}:\;\mathrm{SAT}\big(\Theta\wedge\mathrm{Facts}(W^{\mathcal{C}})\wedge\forall x(\mathsf{Ab}(x)\leftrightarrow\alpha(x))\big).

(9)

An
\alpha
solves an instance iff it is valid on
all
training worlds under this universal-completion semantics.

Worst-Case Cost Over Completions.

For a skeptically valid hypothesis, the per-world cost is the
worst-case
abnormality count
over completions:

\mathrm{cost}_{W}^{\forall}(\alpha)=\max_{\mathcal{C}}\;|\{a\in D_{W}:W^{\mathcal{C}}\models\alpha[a]\}|.

(10)

The instance-level cost is the sum across training worlds:

\mathrm{Cost}^{\forall}(\alpha;\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tr}})=\sum_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tr}}}\mathrm{cost}_{W}^{\forall}(\alpha).

(11)

Lower-Bound Baseline and Gap.

We lift the free-
\mathsf{Ab}
lower bound (Section
4.1
) to the skeptical setting by taking the worst case over completions:

\mathrm{OptCost}^{\forall}(W)=\max_{\mathcal{C}}\;\mathrm{OptCost}(W^{\mathcal{C}}),

(12)

and define
\mathrm{OptCost}^{\forall}(\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tr}})=\sum_{W\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tr}}}\mathrm{OptCost}^{\forall}(W)
.
We report the skeptical gap

\mathrm{Gap}^{\forall}(\alpha)=\mathrm{Cost}^{\forall}(\alpha;\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tr}})-\mathrm{OptCost}^{\forall}(\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{tr}}).

(13)

This baseline is again conservative in the sense of Section
4.1
.

4.4
Gold Rules and Holdout Evaluation

Planted Gold Rules.

Many instances are generated by selecting a “gold” abnormality rule
\alpha^{\star}
from a controlled
template family and constructing worlds for which
\alpha^{\star}
is valid and nontrivial.
We treat
\alpha^{\star}
as a
generator artifact
, not as the unique correct answer:
models may find different valid rules with lower or higher cost.

Gold-Relative Cost and “Beating Gold.”

To aid analysis and to detect shortcut hypotheses, we report the gap to the planted rule:

\mathrm{Gap}_{\mathrm{gold}}(\alpha)=\mathrm{Cost}(\alpha)-\mathrm{Cost}(\alpha^{\star}).

Negative values indicate that the model found a rule with fewer exceptions than the planted one.
We log such cases and can optionally refine gold rules by searching in a richer hypothesis pool
before finalizing a benchmark release.

Size-Conditioned Metrics.

A model can drive cost down by producing large disjunctive case splits tailored to the finite worlds.
To separate such behavior from compact reasoning, we report size-conditioned metrics that group formulas by AST size into bins (Table
9
) and compare formulas shorter vs. longer than the planted gold rule (Table
10
).

Held-Out Worlds.

To probe generalization, we generate
k{=}5

holdout worlds
per instance from the same world sampler and theory parameters as training, subject to the same per-world acceptance checks (domain size, non-trivial cost floor), but
without
the adversarial competitor-elimination filter.
Holdout is therefore an
out-of-filter distribution refresh
: matched in sampling distribution but not adversarially hardened.
Holdout worlds are pre-generated and cached for reproducibility.
We report holdout validity and cost gaps, as well as
\Delta\text{Gap}=\text{H-Gap}-\text{T-Gap}
(the gap degradation from training to holdout).
For ABD-Skeptical, holdouts additionally require that the planted gold rule satisfies universal-completion validity.

confirms that train and holdout distributions closely match in world counts and per-world gold costs.

Dataset Generation

This paper emphasizes
controllable difficulty
: we want instances where simple exception rules fail,
multiple worlds jointly constrain the solution, and models cannot rely on a single brittle shortcut.
We therefore generate instances using three ingredients:

(i) Controlled Hypothesis Families.

We draw gold rules from a template library stratified by quantifier depth and syntactic size.
Templates range from simple conjunctions (AST
\approx 5
–
10
) to nested quantified formulas (AST
\approx 15
–
30
).
A diversity gate caps each template at
\leq 15\%
of instances to avoid dataset monocultures.

(ii) Multi-World Filtering and Parsimony Floors.

Worlds are accepted only if the induced optimization problem is nontrivial (e.g., minimum abnormality counts
exceed a floor) and if the planted rule is near-optimal under the chosen baseline.
We also filter on aggregate difficulty (e.g., summed opt cost across worlds).

(iii) CEGIS-Like Elimination of Shortcut Competitors.

To prevent trivial competitors (e.g., very small formulas) from matching the gold performance, we maintain a
pool of “survivor” formulas and add additional worlds until surviving shortcuts are eliminated.
This produces training sets where the version space is smaller and the intended relational structure is
more identifiable.
The competitor pool includes formulas up to AST size 15, and we iterate until either all competitors are eliminated or a maximum world budget (capped at 12–15 worlds; averages are 6–9 in this release) is reached.

6.1
Prompting and Inference Setup

We employ a
zero-shot
prompting strategy: no benchmark instances or gold formulas appear as in-context examples.
Each prompt consists of four components:
(1) a
system instruction
establishing the role (“expert in first-order logic and abductive reasoning”) and requiring JSON output;
(2) a
scenario-specific task description
that defines the observation semantics (closed-world, existential, or universal completion), the S-expression grammar for
\alpha(x)
, the validity/parsimony/simplicity objectives, and illustrative grammar examples;
(3) the
instance specification
, comprising the allowed/forbidden predicates, the theory axioms, and the training worlds with their predicate interpretations (and, for ABD-Partial/Skeptical, unknown atoms);
(4) an
output format block
requesting a single JSON line with the S-expression formula and a natural-language description.
The prompt includes structured reasoning guidelines (“identify which objects must be abnormal; look for a pattern; verify against all worlds”) but instructs models to reason internally and emit only the final JSON answer.

All models are queried at temperature
T=0.1
for near-deterministic outputs.
For models offering a thinking mode, we used the provider-default reasoning configuration with 64K token budget; other models were queried in standard generation mode.
Each of the 600 benchmark instances is presented independently (single-turn, no multi-round refinement).
All results correspond to API snapshots from January–February 2026; model behavior may differ in future versions.

Each model outputs a candidate
\hat{\alpha}(x)
in S-expression syntax.
We compute training validity and exception cost as defined in Section
4
, with cost semantics varying by scenario.
All gap values reported are
normalized
by the number of worlds to keep scores comparable across instances.

6.2
Main Results

Table
3
summarizes training performance across models along two axes:
(i)
validity
(does the repaired theory have any model?), and
(ii)
parsimony
(how close is the exception count to a solver lower bound?).
The table reports both Gap (vs. the relaxed free-
\mathsf{Ab}
lower bound; see Section
4.1
) and GapG (vs. planted gold rule).
Two patterns stand out.

Several frontier models achieve high training validity (above 90%, with the best reaching nearly 99%), while the older baseline GPT-4o remains far behind.
This indicates that ABD is not a “syntax-only” benchmark: producing a well-formed formula is easy, but producing a
globally valid
repair policy across multiple worlds is not.

Parsimony Separates Strong Models.

Among valid predictions, the dominant source of separation is cost.
Using normalized gaps (extra exceptions per world), the strongest models cluster around roughly 1.0–1.6 additional abnormalities beyond the lower bound, whereas weaker baselines incur substantially larger gaps.
Interpreting the normalized scale: with
\approx 6
–
10
training worlds per instance (Table
2
), a gap of
\approx 1.1
corresponds to roughly
7
–
11
extra abnormal elements per instance.

Several models “beat gold” (negative gold-relative gap) on a nontrivial fraction of instances (Table
11
).
Gold rules are
generator anchors
, not ground truth; beating gold often correlates with larger AST size.
This signal is useful for refining the competitor pool and identifying gold rules that are overly complex, but it does not indicate “winning” the task—parsimony relative to the solver lower bound remains the primary metric.

6.3
Scenario and Theory Breakdown

Table
3
stratifies results by observation regime (FULL, PARTIAL, SKEPTICAL), while Table
4
breaks down performance by background theory.
Note that T1–T5 appear in all three scenarios, so their rows aggregate across regimes; T6–T7 are ABD-Skeptical only.

Partial Observation Trades Feasibility for Stability.

ABD-Partial often increases feasibility (higher validity) for some models by allowing existential completions of unknown atoms, but it does not uniformly improve parsimony.
In fact, models can remain valid while drifting farther from the lower bound, reflecting that “having more ways to make the world consistent” does not automatically translate into
finding
parsimonious repairs.

Skeptical Completion Changes the Failure Mode.

ABD-Skeptical requires hypotheses to remain valid under
all
completions of unknown atoms, not just some favorable one.
Training validity under skeptical semantics is not uniformly lower—some models even achieve higher training validity on Skeptical than on Full (Table
3
)—but the cost metric differs: models must minimize the worst-case exception count, which penalizes formulas that “get lucky” under particular completions.
The main consequence of robust semantics emerges on holdout (Section
6
): the primary failure mode shifts from parsimony inflation (in ABD-Full/ABD-Partial) to
validity brittleness
, where rules that satisfy universal-completion on training worlds break on holdouts.

Model Families Respond Differently to Missingness.

A notable qualitative contrast in Table
3
is that some models benefit from ABD-Partial (higher validity), while others lose validity under partial observation.
This suggests that the partial setting genuinely probes reasoning about
what could be true
rather than merely making the task easier.

Theory Choice Changes Which Shortcuts Exist.

Across theories (Table
4
), T5 is consistently easier, while T3/T4 exhibit larger spread across models.
A plausible explanation is that theories with stronger relational structure (e.g., nested quantification or additional dependencies in consequents) constrain the exception rule more tightly, reducing the effectiveness of compact heuristics.
This theory-conditioned variation is valuable: it yields a natural set of controlled “difficulty knobs” without changing the output language or evaluation protocol.
Additionally, T6 exhibits negative GapG values for most frontier models (Table
4
), indicating that model predictions frequently beat the planted gold rule; this suggests T6’s gold rules may be overly complex and flags these instances for generator refinement.

6.4
Holdout Generalization

Training worlds in ABD are adversarially filtered to eliminate shortcut competitors; holdout worlds are drawn from the same sampler with the same per-world acceptance checks but
without
competitor elimination (Section
4.4
).
Holdout is therefore an out-of-filter distribution refresh, not an i.i.d. split.
Because training worlds are harder by construction, holdout validity can exceed training validity for some models; the primary signal is the change in parsimony among rules that survive on both sets.

Table
5
shows the overall generalization signature: across models, holdout gaps are substantially larger than training gaps, with
\Delta
Gap typically around
+1
extra exception per world.
We also report conditional holdout validity in Table
6
.

Table
5
aggregates across all three scenarios and mixes two effects: (i) whether a rule stays satisfiable on holdouts (
validity brittleness
), and (ii) how parsimonious it remains when satisfiable (
parsimony inflation
).
Table
6
disentangles these by reporting holdout validity conditioned on training validity.
These two failure modes manifest differently across scenarios, as we show next.

Gap), while very large formulas reintroduce brittleness via case-splitting.
Moderate-size formulas generally degrade least.

Paired Comparison: Shorter vs. Longer.

Table

compares formulas on the same problems, categorized as shorter (AST
\leq
gold) or longer (AST
>
gold), with exact gold matches excluded and metrics macro-averaged across problems.
Shorter formulas achieve higher holdout validity but larger

Gold-Beating Instances.

Table

shows that some models beat the planted gold rule on a nontrivial fraction of instances, confirming that gold rules are generator anchors (Section
4.4
).
This diagnostic identifies instances where the planted rule may be suboptimal and feeds back into generator refinement.

6.6
Discussion

Models differ qualitatively in their performance profiles. Some models (e.g., Gemini 3.1 Pro, DeepSeek Reasoner and Grok 4.1 Fast)
excel in validity, while others (e.g., Gemini 3 Pro, Grok 4) achieve lower gaps among valid predictions. GPT-5.2 achieves strong
overall performance on training worlds but relies on large formulas that often represent case splits.
Taken together, the results support three conclusions.

(i) ABD Is Not Solved.

Parsimony remains challenging: the best systems still incur about one extra abnormality per world beyond a solver lower bound, and this gap roughly doubles on holdouts in ABD-Full/Partial.

(ii) Holdout Evaluation Across All Three Regimes Is Essential.

Training metrics alone are misleading because training sets are adversarially filtered.
Holdouts reveal that the answer differs by scenario: parsimony inflation dominates in ABD-Full/Partial, while
validity brittleness dominates in ABD-Skeptical, suggesting that robust semantics regularizes cost but makes validity harder to preserve.

(iii) Cost- and Size-Aware Reporting Is Necessary.

Binary validity saturates; cost gaps reveal separation; but gap can be driven down by hypothesis complexity.
Improved abductive reasoning involves improvement in all three metrics: validity, gap cost, and formula size.

7
Conclusion

We introduced
ABD
, a solver-checkable benchmark suite for default–exception abduction over finite first-order worlds. In
ABD
, models must synthesize a first-order abnormality definition
\alpha(x)
such that
Ab(x)\leftrightarrow\alpha(x)
repairs a fixed default theory across multiple worlds, while keeping exceptions sparse. We formalized three observation regimes—
ABD-Full
(closed-world),
ABD-Partial
(existential completion with best-case cost), and
ABD-Skeptical
(universal completion with worst-case cost)—and evaluated ten LLMs under exact SMT-based verification.

Across tasks, frontier models often produce
valid
repairs on training worlds, but
ABD
remains unsaturated
because
parsimony
does not saturate. Among strong models, the normalized gap to the solver lower bound
remains roughly
1.0
–
1.6
extra abnormalities per world; the gap to the planted gold rule is roughly
0.5
–
1.0
,
confirming systematic over-use of exceptions. The benchmark was calibrated to be
moderately challenging for frontier models; harder instances can readily be generated in the future.

Holdout evaluation reveals that generalization is the key remaining challenge, and that its prevailing failure mode depends on the completion semantics. In
ABD-Full
and
ABD-Partial
, the primary generalization failure is
parsimony inflation
: when a rule remains valid on held-out worlds, its cost gap typically increases substantially (often by about one additional exception per world), suggesting that models learn repairs that are tailored to the filtered training worlds and do not transfer as parsimoniously to fresh samples.

In contrast,
ABD-Skeptical
induces a different generalization profile. For rules that remain skeptically valid,
gap inflation is often smaller than in
ABD-Full/Partial
, consistent with the worst-case objective acting as an
implicit regularizer on exception marking. However, the main failure becomes
validity brittleness
: rules that
satisfy the universal-completion criterion on training worlds frequently fail outright on holdouts. Taken together, these
outcomes suggest that robust completion semantics shifts difficulty from “how few exceptions can I mark?” to “can I
preserve a universally valid repair policy under distribution refresh?”

Finally, the complexity analyses emphasize that performance cannot be summarized by validity and unbudgeted cost.
Large formulas can reduce cost on training worlds but become brittle on holdouts,
consistent with case-splitting. Moderate-size formulas
often provide the best tradeoff. This motivates reporting
ABD
performance as a multi-objective
profile over validity, parsimony, and syntactic budget.

Limitations.

Domains are small to permit exact SMT verification, which also makes extensional
case-splitting feasible; we therefore report size-conditioned and held-out-world
metrics to diagnose memorization. Our free-
Ab
baseline relaxes the single-formula
constraint, so gap-to-opt is a conservative overestimate. Finally,
planted gold rules are generator anchors rather than uniquely optimal targets;
models often find valid solutions of lower AST than gold.

Future work.

A natural next step is to use
ABD
as a training and diagnosis environment: e.g., solver-in-the-loop refinement that supplies counterexample worlds or counterexample completions, and optimization objectives that explicitly trade off parsimony and robustness. Methodologically, it would be valuable to tighten the lower bound (e.g., by approximating the minimum cost under the single-formula constraint), and to scale beyond small worlds
via approximate verification or sampling-based evaluation while preserving exact verification.

ABD
provides a controlled testbed for studying how learned systems acquire (or fail to acquire) portable
default–exception first-order rules.

After competitor elimination, a second filter guards against

Appendix E
Completion Semantics and Solver Encoding

All validity checks and cost computations are implemented via
reduction to SMT using the Z3 solver.
This section formalises the encoding.

E.1
World Representation

A world
W
over domain
D_{W}=\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1}\}
is specified by:

•

\mathrm{True}_{P}(W)
: set of atoms known true for each predicate
P
;

•

\mathrm{Unk}_{P}(W)
: set of atoms whose truth value is unknown;

•

all remaining atoms are known false (closed-world assumption
on the “rest”).

A
completion

\mathcal{C}
assigns each unknown atom a truth
value. The completed world
W^{\mathcal{C}}
has:

\mathrm{Facts}(W^{\mathcal{C}})\;=\;\mathrm{True}_{P}(W)\;\cup\;\{\,a\in\mathrm{Unk}_{P}(W):\mathcal{C}(a)=\top\,\}.

Z3 encoding.

Each unknown atom
u\in\mathrm{Unk}_{P}(W)
is represented by a free
Z3 boolean variable. Known-true atoms are replaced by

True
, known-false atoms by
False
, and unknown
atoms by their variable. The abnormality predicate
\mathsf{Ab}(a)

is replaced
inline
by the Z3 grounding of
\alpha(a)
, making
the biconditional
\mathsf{Ab}(a)\leftrightarrow\alpha(a)
implicit.

E.2
Validity Queries

Let
\Theta
denote the theory axioms and

\Phi_{W}(\alpha,\mathcal{C})
abbreviate the conjunction of grounded
axioms after substituting
\mathsf{Ab}\mapsto\alpha
and
applying completion
\mathcal{C}
.

ABD-Full.

No unknown atoms exist. Validity reduces to direct evaluation:

\mathrm{Valid}_{\mathrm{full}}(\alpha,W)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\;W\models\Theta[\mathsf{Ab}\mapsto\alpha].

ABD-Partial (existential completion).

\mathrm{Valid}_{\exists}(\alpha,W)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\;\exists\,\mathcal{C}.\;W^{\mathcal{C}}\models\Theta[\mathsf{Ab}\mapsto\alpha].

Encoding.

Assert all grounded axioms with unknown atoms as free variables.
The query is
Sat
; a satisfying assignment witnesses a valid
completion.

ABD-Skeptical (universal completion).

\mathrm{Valid}_{\forall}(\alpha,W)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\;\forall\,\mathcal{C}.\;W^{\mathcal{C}}\models\Theta[\mathsf{Ab}\mapsto\alpha].

Encoding.

Assert the
negation
of the grounded axiom conjunction with
unknown atoms as free variables. The query checks for a

counterexample
completion:

•

Unsat

\Rightarrow
no counterexample exists

\Rightarrow

\alpha
is valid.

•

Sat

\Rightarrow
counterexample found

\Rightarrow

\alpha
is invalid.

E.3
Cost Computation

The abnormality cost in world
W
under completion
\mathcal{C}
is

\mathrm{cost}(\alpha,W^{\mathcal{C}})\;=\;\bigl|\{\,a\in D_{W}:W^{\mathcal{C}}\models\alpha(a)\,\}\bigr|.

ABD-Full.

Direct evaluation; no optimisation needed.

ABD-Partial (best-case cost).

\mathrm{cost}_{\exists}(\alpha,W)\;=\;\min_{\mathcal{C}:\;\mathrm{Valid}_{\exists}(\alpha,W^{\mathcal{C}})}\;\mathrm{cost}(\alpha,W^{\mathcal{C}}).

Encoding.

Use Z3’s
Optimize
solver: assert the grounded axioms
(with unknown atoms as free variables) and minimise

\sum_{a\in D_{W}}\mathbf{1}[\alpha(a)]
.

ABD-Skeptical (worst-case cost).

\mathrm{cost}_{\forall}(\alpha,W)\;=\;\max_{\mathcal{C}:\;\mathrm{Valid}_{\forall}(\alpha,W^{\mathcal{C}})}\;\mathrm{cost}(\alpha,W^{\mathcal{C}}).

Encoding.

Use Z3’s
Optimize
solver: assert the grounded axioms and

maximise

\sum_{a\in D_{W}}\mathbf{1}[\alpha(a)]
.
This finds the least favourable valid completion.

E.4
Baselines: Free-
\mathsf{Ab}
Lower
Bound

The conservative baseline
\mathrm{OptCost}
relaxes the
single-formula constraint:

ABD-Full.

\mathrm{OptCost}(W)\;=\;\min_{\mathsf{Ab}:D_{W}\to\{0,1\}}\;\bigl|\{a:\mathsf{Ab}(a)=1\}\bigr|\quad\text{s.t.}\quad W\models\Theta.

Here
\mathsf{Ab}
is a free function (one independent boolean per
domain element), not constrained to be definable by a single formula.
The solution is computed via Z3
Optimize
.

ABD-Partial.

\mathrm{OptCost}_{\exists}(W)\;=\;\min_{\mathsf{Ab},\;\mathcal{C}}\;\bigl|\{a:\mathsf{Ab}(a)=1\}\bigr|\quad\text{s.t.}\quad W^{\mathcal{C}}\models\Theta.

Both
\mathsf{Ab}
and the completion
\mathcal{C}
are jointly
optimised.

ABD-Skeptical.

\mathrm{OptCost}_{\forall}(W)\;=\;\min_{\mathsf{Ab}}\;\max_{\mathcal{C}}\;\bigl|\{a:\mathsf{Ab}(a)=1\}\bigr|\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\forall\,\mathcal{C}.\;W^{\mathcal{C}}\models\Theta.

Conservativeness.

Because the single-formula definability constraint
(
\mathsf{Ab}(a)\leftrightarrow\alpha(a)
for a
single

\alpha
) is dropped,
\mathrm{OptCost}
is a
lower bound
on
the achievable cost of any formula. The gap

\mathrm{Gap}=\mathrm{cost}(\alpha)-\mathrm{OptCost}
therefore
over-estimates actual sub-optimality but provides a consistent,
formula-independent baseline across all models and instances.

Appendix F
Holdout Construction and Distribution Match

F.1
Holdout Generation Algorithm

Each benchmark instance includes
k{=}5

holdout
worlds used
exclusively for post-hoc generalisation evaluation; no model ever sees
holdout worlds during inference. The holdout sampler reuses the

identical
Z3-based world-generation procedure described in
Appendix
D.1
:

1.

Extract parameters
from the training worlds of each
instance: domain-size range, predicate-density intervals
(Table
D.1
), unknown-atom rates
(Table
D.2
), cost bounds, and gap
constraints.

2.

Sample a world
using the same Z3 density-constrained
generator. For ABD-Partial and ABD-Skeptical, unknown atoms
are masked using the same per-theory rates.

3.

Filter.
Accept the world only if it (i) is not
equivalent to any training world (identical domain size and
predicate extensions), (ii) satisfies the validity semantics
of the scenario, and (iii) has per-world cost and gap within
the range observed across training worlds.

4.

Repeat
until
k
distinct holdout worlds are
accepted (up to 150 attempts per world, 30 s per instance).

This filtering ensures that the planted gold formula
\alpha^{*}
remains
valid on every holdout world and that per-world cost and gap fall within
the training regime, so holdout evaluation measures generalisation under
distributional match rather than distribution shift.

Deterministic seeding.

Each holdout world is seeded via

\texttt{SHA-256}(\mathit{dataset\_path}\mathbin{\|}\mathit{instance\_id}\mathbin{\|}\mathit{holdout\_idx}\mathbin{\|}\mathit{global\_seed})\bmod 2^{31}
,
ensuring full reproducibility.

F.2
What Is Not Done on Holdout

The following training-time hardening procedures
(Appendix
D.3
–
D.4
) are

deliberately omitted
from holdout generation:

•

No competitor elimination.
Training worlds are
iteratively added to defeat shortcut formulas
(§
D.3
); holdout worlds are drawn independently
with no adversarial loop.

•

No cheater filtering.
Training rejects instances
where a trivial pattern matches the gold cost
(§
D.4
); holdout applies no such filter.

•

No gold refinement.
The planted gold formula is
fixed at benchmark-creation time and not re-optimised for
holdout.

These omissions are intentional: holdout worlds provide a pure
i.i.d. distributional test, measuring whether a model’s formula
captures the true pattern rather than artifacts of adversarial
training-set construction.

(and (exists y (and (R x y) (P y))) (not (P x)))

Train cost = 17; holdout: 0/5 worlds valid (catastrophic failure).

Failure

happens to hold for
all abnormal elements in the 8 training worlds, but is not a
consequence of the theory. All 5 holdout worlds contain

P
-positive abnormals, breaking the formula.

R
-atoms.

(exists y (and (R x y) (P y) (forall z (or (not (R x z)) (= z y)))))
