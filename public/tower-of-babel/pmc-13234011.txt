The effect of social media information on college major choices in China: a difference-in-differences analysis
Authors: Lijing Ma, Bo Liu, Yilong Wu, Qingsong Li, Chuanhua Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The choice of a college major in China is a high-stakes decision often plagued by information asymmetry. This study provides a large-scale causal analysis of how social media influencers impact these choices. Leveraging the “Zhang Xuefeng Phenomeno”, an event where a prominent influencer vehemently discouraged Journalism and Communication majors, as a natural experiment, we employ a difference-in-differences (DID) design on a comprehensive dataset of university admission ranks from 2017 to 2023, covering over 3.3 million observations. Our findings reveal that the influencer’s negative signal caused the admission rank for Journalism and Communication to worsen by a substantial 13.1% across all institutions and by 24.7% at elite Double First-Class Initiative universities. A significant asymmetric response is identified, as the negative impact is nearly twice the magnitude of the positive effect observed for the endorsed Computer Science major, suggesting that student decisions are driven more by loss aversion than by the pursuit of opportunity. The robustness of these estimates is confirmed using event-study approaches and placebo tests in untargeted fields such as Philosophy and History. Furthermore, an exploratory analysis offers suggestive evidence of positive spillover effects on related practical fields, such as Transportation. A triple-difference (DDD) analysis reveals this impact is significantly weaker in provinces with higher per capita GDP. This research provides robust evidence that social media influencers have become powerful new players in the educational landscape, capable of significantly influencing students’ intentions to choose a major. It also highlights novel challenges for policymakers in mitigating information asymmetry and optimizing human capital allocation to support the development of new quality productive forces.

Introduction

The choice of a university major is a pivotal decision that shapes the career trajectories, income potential, and social mobility of young adults1–3. In China, this decision is situated within the high-stakes context of the National College Entrance Examination, the primary gateway to university for millions of students each year. The sheer scale of this system creates a formidable challenge: in 2024 alone, the student cohort included 4.90 million enrolled in regular bachelor’s programs, 109,600 in vocational bachelor’s programs, and 5.68 million in higher vocational programs4. This massive group of students confronts a vast landscape of 816 distinct undergraduate majors across 93 fields of study5. The university application and admissions process serves as the core mechanism for matching students to these programs and majors. However, navigating this complex matching problem under conditions of uncertainty and information asymmetry is a significant hurdle for millions of families. This urgent need has given rise to a massive college application consulting market, reaching 1.09 billion yuan in 20256.

The consulting market demand highlights how the decision-making process, while influenced by foundational theories of cultural capital and social habitus7,8, is fundamentally information-dependent9–13. Students and their families actively seek information on program characteristics, career prospects, and labor market outcomes from various sources. In recent years, there has been a dramatic transformation in the information landscape, which traditionally included official university materials and national discipline assessments, but has increasingly expanded to include third-party rankings and social media platforms. The provision of guidance on social media can be academically defined as a form of information intervention that aims to encourage students to make better choices14.

The literature investigating these informational effects can be broadly divided into two streams. One well-established stream confirms the powerful influence of structured information on students’ major choices. This includes information from official sources, such as national discipline rankings15 and universities’ promotional materials16, as well as findings from controlled experiments involving data on career earnings17. In contrast, a more nascent stream of research is beginning to explore the impact of unstructured, subjective information from social media, acknowledging that advice from online influencers now constitutes a significant public signal in the decision-making process14,18–20. While the influence of online personalities on students’ major choices is recognized, the large-scale causal effect of such an information shock remains empirically understudied.

Leveraging a natural experiment, specifically the “Zhang Xuefeng Phenomenon”, this study conducts the first large-scale causal analysis of a social media information shock on college major choices in China, utilizing a comprehensive dataset of university admission rankings from 2017 to 2023. Our contribution is threefold. First, we shift the research focus from traditional, official information sources to the increasingly dominant yet understudied realm of social media. By analyzing the impact of a prominent influencer, we quantify the causal effect of this new form of public signal on high-stakes educational outcomes. Second, we explicitly test for and identify a significant asymmetry in how students respond to information. By separately analyzing the effects on majors that were discouraged and those that were endorsed by the influencer, we find that negative signals trigger a much stronger response. We frame this asymmetry through the lens of loss aversion from behavioral economics. Our results confirm that loss aversion is a primary driver in the high-stakes college major selection process, providing large-scale causal evidence for this behavioral tendency. Third, through a triple-difference analysis, we investigate the moderating role of provincial economic context, offering a more nuanced understanding of how local conditions mediate the influence of large-scale information shocks. Our findings provide insights for students, universities, and policymakers navigating the complexities of the digital age.

Literature review

The choice of an academic major is a pivotal decision made under significant uncertainty, and a substantial body of literature has examined how informational interventions can shape these high-stakes choices. This research can be broadly organized into two paradigms. The first, rooted in economic models of choice, focuses on how students respond to structured, often centralized, information. The second, drawing from communication and sociology, grapples with the recent emergence of a decentralized, peer-driven information ecosystem powered by social media.

Traditional information channels and rational choice

The first paradigm traditionally models students as rational agents who update their beliefs in response to new information about labor market returns, program quality, and personal aptitude. Within this framework, research has focused on two types of interventions. The first is institutional signaling, which includes direct university marketing, career counseling, and government-led policy reforms, such as changes to admissions systems. These studies confirm that official, top-down information can enhance students’ decision-making efficacy and effectively coordinate choices on a large scale9,12,21–24. Complementing this institutional perspective, a second line of inquiry uses experimental and econometric methods to explore how students’ subjective beliefs influence their decisions. Pioneering work by Arcidiacono25 demonstrated the importance of anticipated earnings and self-assessed ability. Subsequent studies have refined this view, showing that while providing accurate earnings data can correct misperceptions and lead to better financial outcomes10, students’ non-pecuniary preferences, such as intrinsic interest and unobserved “tastes”, are remarkably resilient and often dominate pecuniary factors26,27. A key takeaway from this literature is that while students are responsive to information, their choices are filtered through a strong prism of pre-existing personal motivations.

The rise of social media: a new information paradigm

This traditional model, however, is increasingly challenged by the rise of social media, which represents not merely a new channel, but a paradigm shift in the information landscape. Unlike official sources, social media information is decentralized, interactive, and highly subjective. Here, influence is wielded not by institutions, but by peers and online personalities whose credibility is socially constructed28. Emerging evidence highlights the profound impact of this new ecosystem. The “Zhang Xuefeng effect,” for example, illustrates how a single influencer’s commentary can trigger nationwide shifts in application patterns, demonstrating a powerful new form of public signal14,19. Broader research confirms that media exposure, both traditional and digital, consistently shapes students’ career aspirations18,20. These phenomena are well-explained by communication theories like the Elaboration Likelihood Model (which emphasizes source credibility) and social comparison theory (which explains peer influence), as well as concepts like group polarization and echo chambers that describe how online environments can reinforce biases29–32. In the high-stakes context of China’s college admissions, these dynamics appear particularly potent, with studies indicating that informational interventions and transparency can reduce major selection mismatches, that online information can help mitigate poor choices, and that perceived social support can foster academic self-efficacy15–17,33.

Research gaps

Despite growing acknowledgment of social media’s role, the literature is marked by several critical gaps. First, while the influence of this new information channel is widely observed, much of the evidence remains correlational or descriptive, leaving a need for rigorous, large-scale analyses capable of isolating the causal effect of a social media information shock from underlying trends. Second, while some descriptive analyses allude to asymmetric responses to information, there is a lack of rigorous quantitative measurement of this phenomenon. The relative magnitudes of how students react to positive information (endorsements) versus negative information (warnings) have not been empirically estimated. Third, although the concept of regional differences is acknowledged, the impact of national-level information shocks is often treated as monolithic. There is a significant gap in quantitatively estimating how these effects are moderated by regional heterogeneity, such as varying economic conditions or local labor market structures across provinces.

The “Zhang Xuefeng Phenomenon” as a natural experiment

The new signaling role of social media

The college admission process is fraught with information asymmetry. While traditional information interventions have been well-studied, the information landscape has been transformed by the rise of social media. Social media platforms have become primary venues for disseminating guidance, giving rise to a new class of “educational influencers”. Unlike traditional media, information from these influencers has two distinct features. First, it is amplified through interaction. Direct engagement between creators and users enhances receptiveness in a way that one-way media cannot. Second, it carries pronounced subjectivity. In pursuit of engagement, influencers often add strong personal interpretations to factual information, which can be rapidly magnified by internet algorithms. Despite this subjectivity, these widely disseminated views are not private advice but function as powerful public signals, similar in their reach to traditional media. Among the most prominent of these influencers is Zhang Xuefeng, a commentator known for his employment-focused and often provocative advice. His role as a powerful information intermediary set the stage for a nationwide natural experiment in 2023.

Defining the natural experiment

The public interest in educational influencers’ commentary is highly seasonal, consistently peaking during the May-June college application window each year. In May and June of 2023, immediately preceding the crucial college application window, Zhang’s remarks received extensive public attention and circulated widely on social media platforms. He strongly discouraged students from choosing Journalism and Communication majors, while simultaneously endorsing majors he perceived as yielding high labor-market returns, such as computer science. This advice became a topic of national conversation precisely when students were finalizing their decisions34.

Zhang Xuefeng is the most followed educational content creators in China. To establish Zhang Xuefeng’s remarks as a credible information shock, we document the reach and timing of his influence across major Chinese social media platforms, as shown in Table 1 and Fig. 1. His livestreams and short videos during this period accumulated tens of millions of views, with key videos receiving hundreds of thousands of likes and shares. Related hashtag topics trended nationally on multiple platforms, generating extensive mainstream media coverage.

Data and methods

Data sources and sample construction

A key distinction of our study is the construction of an unprecedentedly large and comprehensive dataset on college majors admission scores in China. We employed a manual data collection strategy, compiling the data from multiple public sources for the years 2017 to 2023. Major data sources include: (1) universities’ official admissions websites; (2) authorized third-party platforms (e.g., http://gaokao.chsi.com.cn, the official Gaokao information platform managed by the Ministry of Education). All data were cross-validated across multiple sources when available to ensure accuracy. The unit of analysis is a university-discipline-province-year-subject observation. Our primary outcome variable is the minimum admission rank, which we derived by matching the minimum admission score for each program to the official one-score-one-rank tables (yi fen yi duan biao). This rank-based measure is crucial for our analysis as it is standardized and comparable across different years and provinces, which have varying exam difficulties and numbers of test-takers.

To address inconsistencies in how universities report admissions data, a primary processing step was the aggregation of specific majors into broader disciplines. Majors within the same discipline share similar characteristics, and they are likely to be affected similarly by public information shocks. Majors are aggregated into a consistent and comparable unit of analysis based on the official undergraduate major catalog from the Ministry of Education of China5, as shown in Table 2. Furthermore, for a more focused analysis on elite institutions and to ensure comparability with existing literature, we extracted a specific subset from our main data containing 147 universities The sheer scale of both our comprehensive dataset (3,317,803 observations) and the“Double First-Class Initiative” university subset (560,320 observations) provides the robust empirical foundation for this study’s claim as the first large-scale causal analysis of a social media information shock on college major choices in China. This broad coverage allows for a panoramic and more generalizable analysis of admission patterns nationwide.

Research design

To estimate the causal effect of the social media information shock, we employ a difference-in-differences (DID) research design. This quasi-experimental method is well-suited for this context as it allows us to isolate the impact of the event by comparing the change in outcomes for the affected majors (the treatment groups) before and after the event, relative to the change in outcomes for unaffected majors (the control group) over the same period. The treatment period (post-event) is defined as the year 2023, while the pre-treatment period spans from 2017 to 2022. Based on the content of the influencer’s advice, we define several distinct treatment groups for analysis:Discouraged major Journalism and Communication, which received a strong negative signal.Endorsed major Computer Science, which received strong positive signals.Spillover major Transportation, which serves as an exploratory case study to investigate potential spillover effects.It is worth noting that, to examine whether the influencer’s underlying employment-first ideology generated spillover effects beyond the explicitly named majors, we selected Transportation as an exploratory case study. This choice is motivated by two primary considerations. First, analyzing the broader Engineering category is methodologically problematic due to its extreme internal heterogeneity. While Zhang Xuefeng endorses practical engineering fields, he simultaneously and explicitly discourages certain engineering majors with historically poor employment prospects. An aggregated analysis of all engineering fields would therefore wash out these opposing effects. Second, Transportation represents a theoretically relevant test case. It is a practical, employment-oriented field that aligns closely with Zhang’s pragmatic philosophy, emphasizing job prospects, yet it was not explicitly targeted in his viral commentary. We therefore present the Transportation results as suggestive evidence of potential spillover effects.

Model 4: robustness check with COVID-19 controls Our sample period (2017–2023) includes the COVID-19 pandemic (2020-2022), which may have exerted heterogeneous impacts on different majors. For instance, digital economy-related majors (e.g., Computer Science) may have become more popular during the pandemic, while traditional media majors (e.g., Journalism and Communication) may have been adversely affected by restricted in-person activities. To address this potential confounding factor, we augment Model 2 with COVID-19 control variables:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \log ({\mathrm{Rank}})_{{m,u,p,t,s}} = & \beta _{1} ({\mathrm{Treat}}_{m} \times {\mathrm{Post}}_{t} ) + \beta _{2} ({\mathrm{COVID}}_{t} \times {\mathrm{Digital}}_{m} ) + \beta _{3} ({\mathrm{COVID}}_{t} \times {\mathrm{Traditional}}_{m} ) \\ & \quad + \delta _{m} + \delta _{u} + \delta _{{pt}} + \delta _{{ts}} + \delta _{{ps}} + \epsilon_{{m,u,p,t,s}} \\ \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {COVID}_{t}$$\end{document} is a dummy variable equal to 1 for years 2020–2022, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Digital}_{m}$$\end{document} indicates digital economy-related majors (Computer Science, Electronic Information, Automation), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Traditional}_{m}$$\end{document} indicates traditional media majors (Journalism and Communication, Arts). If the treatment effect remains robust after controlling for these pandemic-related heterogeneities, it provides stronger evidence that our main results are not driven by COVID-19 confounding.

Model 5: DID with a threshold variable To explore the potential heterogeneity of the shock’s impact, we test whether the effect is moderated by provincial economic context. We extend our primary DID specification (Model 2) by incorporating a threshold variable based on provincial per capita GDP. This results in a triple-difference (DDD) model:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\begin{matrix} \log (\text {Rank})_{m,u,p,t,s} =&\beta _{1} (\text {Treat}_{m} \times \text {Post}_{t}) + \beta _{2} (\text {Treat}_{m} \times \text {Post}_{t} \times \text {HighGDP}_{p}) + \delta _{m} + \delta _{u} + \delta _{pt} + \delta _{ts} + \delta _{ps} + \epsilon _{m,u,p,t,s} \end{matrix}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {HighGDP}_p$$\end{document} is a dummy variable equal to 1 if the per capita GDP of province p is above the sample median, and 0 otherwise. The fixed effects structure remains identical to that in Model 2 to ensure a robust estimation. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _1$$\end{document} captures the average treatment effect of the information shock in the baseline group–provinces with lower per capita GDP. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _2$$\end{document} is the key coefficient of interest for the threshold analysis. It measures the difference in the treatment effect between high-GDP provinces and low-GDP provinces. A statistically significant \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _2$$\end{document} would indicate that the impact of the influencer’s advice is indeed conditional on regional economic development. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _1 + \beta _2$$\end{document} represents the net treatment effect for provinces with higher per capita GDP.

Empirical results and analysis

This section presents the empirical results of the information shock event. It begins with graphical evidence to visualize admission rank trends and to underscore the suitability of the empirical strategy. The analysis then proceeds to the core quantitative results from the DID model, which reveal a substantial and asymmetric impact of the information shock. Finally, this average effect is further dissected by exploring its heterogeneity across provinces with different economic contexts, employing a triple-difference (DDD) framework.

Parallel trends test and robustness checks

To validate the causal interpretation of our findings, we conduct two critical robustness checks. First, the parallel trends assumption is tested with an event-study analysis. Second, potential confounding from the COVID-19 pandemic is addressed.

Event-study analysis

Figure 5 presents the results of the event-study analysis (Model 3), which estimates year-by-year treatment effects relative to the reference year 2022. The estimated coefficients for the pre-treatment years (2017–2021) are generally small in magnitude and mostly statistically insignificant at the 5% level, except for 2019. For 2017, 2018, 2020, and 2021, the point estimates hover around zero, with confidence intervals that encompass zero, supporting the parallel trends assumption for these years. The 2019 coefficient is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.038$$\end{document} (p = 0.017), which is marginally significant. While this indicates some pre-existing deviation in that specific year, the overall pattern across the five pre-treatment years does not suggest a systematic divergence in trends prior to the event. Importantly, the treatment effect in 2023 is large, positive, and highly significant (0.102, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p< 0.001$$\end{document}), representing a clear break from the pre-event pattern. Discussion

This study provides causal evidence of the powerful influence that social media influencers exert on the high-stakes educational choices of college applicants in China. The findings reveal that a single influencer’s viral commentary significantly altered national admission patterns, with the impact being substantial, asymmetric, and heterogeneous. This discussion interprets these key findings and considers their broader implications.

The substantial magnitude of the effect, a 24.7% worsening in admission ranks for Journalism at elite universities, highlights the profound influence of this new information channel. The observed asymmetry, where the negative impact on the discouraged major was nearly double the positive impact on the endorsed major, strongly confirms the loss aversion framework. By quantifying this behavioral response at a national scale, our study contributes a novel educational context to the loss aversion literature. The 1.836 empirical ratio indicates that the influencer’s advice primarily acted as a powerful coordinating signal that amplified latent anxieties about future career precarity, i.e., a potential loss, which proved far more potent than the opportunity-highlighting function of the endorsement.

The Transportation major spillover analysis should be viewed as exploratory. The choice of Transportation is influenced by both theoretical considerations, i.e., its alignment with the influencer’s pragmatic philosophy, and the research team’s professional background in transportation engineering. While the significant effect in the elite university subsample is suggestive, we cannot rule out selective reporting or chance findings. Future research should pre-specify potential spillover fields based on explicit theoretical criteria to provide more rigorous tests of how far such information shocks diffuse beyond directly mentioned majors.

The heterogeneous impact across provinces raises an important question about the underlying mechanisms. There are two competing explanations for why the information shock had weaker effects in higher-GDP provinces. First, the economic buffer hypothesis suggests that students in wealthier provinces face lower economic pressure due to more diverse local labor markets and greater household financial resources, making them less sensitive to employment-related information shocks. Families in these regions may perceive a wider array of career opportunities and possess greater capacity to absorb uncertainty, reducing their responsiveness to risk-oriented signals about specific majors’ employment prospects. Second, the rational response hypothesis posits that families in wealthier provinces may be equally or even more concerned about economic returns to education, but possess more sophisticated information-processing capabilities and access to diverse information channels beyond social media. This informational advantage could enable them to critically evaluate and appropriately discount sensational online commentary, relying instead on more comprehensive evidence about labor market outcomes.

We acknowledge that our current data do not permit formal testing of these mechanisms through direct measurement of mediating variables. Future research could more rigorously test these mechanisms by incorporating province-level structural data and conducting formal mediation analysis. Additionally, individual-level survey data on parental characteristics, information sources, and decision-making processes would provide more direct evidence on the mechanisms underlying the observed Conclusions

This study investigated the causal impact of a large-scale social media information shock on college major choices in China. Using a comprehensive national dataset of university admission ranks from 2017 to 2023, the analysis employed a difference-in-differences (DID) and a triple-difference (DDD) design to estimate the event’s effects. The findings demonstrate that the influencer’s commentary led to a substantial and statistically significant decline in the popularity of the discouraged major (Journalism and Communication), an effect that was considerably larger than the positive impact on the endorsed major (Computer Science). Furthermore, this impact was significantly weaker in provinces with higher economic development. In conclusion, this research provides strong evidence that social media influencers have emerged as new and powerful, yet unregulated, actors in the educational landscape, with the capacity to profoundly influence college major choice intentions and present novel challenges for students, institutions, and policymakers in the digital age.
