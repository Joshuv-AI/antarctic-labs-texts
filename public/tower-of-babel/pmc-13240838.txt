Unravelling the protective role of soluble soybean polysaccharide in preserving dough gluten structure under acidic conditions
Authors: Tingting Hong, Yedong Feng, Dan Xu, Fengfeng Wu, Xianfeng Zhong, Guidong Huang, Xueming Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of soluble soybean polysaccharide (SSPS) on dough rheology and water mobility under acidic conditions. Results showed that 1.5% SSPS significantly enhanced viscoelasticity and water retention, whereas 3% SSPS disrupted the gluten network, reducing stability time by 56.33%. While acidification typically promotes protein depolymerization, the SSPS1.5–pH 4.5 treatment mitigated these effects, preserving gluten macropolymers and stabilizing disulfide bonds. LF-NMR and MRI confirmed that SSPS promoted water immobilization. Mechanistically, 1.5% SSPS increased α-helix content and thermal stability, creating a dense, continuous gluten matrix through electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions. Conversely, excess SSPS (3%) exacerbated structural weakening. These findings suggest that moderate SSPS supplementation effectively offsets acid-induced damage, providing a strategic approach to enhance the structural integrity and processing performance of acidic wheat-based products.

Introduction

Wheat has long served as a fundamental constituent of the human diet, underpinning the global production of fermented and baked foods. The tradition of creating wheat-based products, ranging from standard breads to specialized sourdoughs, is facilitated by the distinctive rheological properties inherent in the grain (Shewry & Belton, 2024). In food processing, the dominance of wheat as a primary ingredient attests to the singular composition of its storage proteins. Gliadins typically constitute about 30–35% of the total protein content in wheat dough, contributing to viscosity and extensibility, while glutenins make up approximately 40–50%, providing elasticity and strength (Li et al., 2021). The balance between these two protein fractions is crucial for forming a cohesive gluten network, ultimately influencing the gas retention, texture, and volume of the final product (Delcour et al., 2012). In addition, dough quality is significantly influenced by processing, including mixing intensity, temperature, hydration level, pH and other related factors (Zheng et al., 2025). Notably, the stability of this protein network is frequently compromised in acidic food systems, such as sourdough breads (Fraberger et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2021). Under acidic conditions, the elevated concentration of hydrogen ions increases the net positive charge density on gluten proteins, thereby enhancing their solubility. This electrostatic modification promotes intramolecular and intermolecular repulsive forces, which disrupt the formation of stabilizing disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions essential for gluten network development. Moreover, the acidic pH environment may activate endogenous wheat proteases, further facilitating protein depolymerization and structural modification through proteolytic degradation (Katina et al., 2006; Su et al., 2019; Thiele et al., 2004).

During traditional sourdough fermentation, progressive acidification (pH decrease) is often accompanied by the production of microbial exopolysaccharides (EPS) (Galle & Arendt, 2014). Owing to their hydrophilicity and hydrocolloid-like functionality, EPS could improve the functional performance of the gluten network, resulting in enhanced viscoelasticity and structural stability of the dough (Chen et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2024). Accordingly, to mitigate the deterioration in dough and gluten network integrity under acidic conditions, the incorporation of exogenous hydrocolloids has been proposed as a promising strategy for strengthening the dough matrix and regulating water distribution (Xu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2023).

Soluble soybean polysaccharide (SSPS) is a natural anionic polysaccharide derived from the by-products of tofu, soy protein, soybean oil, and soymilk processing (Tavasoli et al., 2022). The highly branched and compact globular architecture of SSPS contributes to its excellent functional properties, including high water solubility, good dispersibility, and remarkable stability under thermal and acidic conditions (Shen et al., 2025). Moreover, it demonstrates superior film-forming and emulsifying capacities, particularly enhancing interfacial stability at low pH environments, which has garnered increasing attention in food and material science research (Li et al., 2025). While previous investigations have demonstrated that SSPS stabilizes simple globular protein dispersions such as milk proteins via electrostatic and steric mechanisms (Nobuhara et al., 2014), its behavior within the complex and multi-component gluten network remains largely uncharacterized. Unlike simple protein suspensions, the gluten network consists of a complex poly-matrix of gliadins and glutenins, where structural stability is underpinned by an equilibrium of covalent and non-covalent interactions that are susceptible to disruption by pH-induced alterations. Although our previous studies demonstrated that SSPS can modulate starch gelatinization and retard bread staling under acidic conditions (Hong et al., 2022; Hong et al., 2024), the influence of SSPS on the gluten framework and its contribution to dough viscoelasticity remain unexplored.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to investigate the effects of SSPS on the rheological behavior and moisture distribution of wheat bread dough under acidic conditions, and to elucidate the underlying stabilization mechanisms by analyzing changes in protein secondary structure and gluten network microstructure. The findings of this research provide a theoretical basis for applying SSPS to improve the processing performance of wheat-based products formulated under acidic conditions.

Materials and methods

Materials

Commercial high-gluten wheat flour (carbohydrate 72%, protein 12.8%, moisture 13.0%, ash 0.6%, and fat 1.6%) was provided by Yihai Kerry (Shanghai, China). Soluble soybean polysaccharide (SSPS) was provided by Fujian Quanzhou Weibo Food Co., Ltd. (Fujian, China), with a purity of 85.7% and an average molecular weight (Mw) of 7.61 × 105 Da. Its monosaccharide composition was 2.69% fucose, 4.48% rhamnose, 23.82% arabinose, 53.55% galactose, 1.14% glucose, 4.69% mannose, and 9.63% galacturonic acid. All chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade.

Preparation of dough

The formulations of the dough are summarized in Table S1. All ingredients, except for the water/citric acid‑sodium citrate buffer, were initially mixed at level 1 (low speed, 18 rpm) for 1 min using a SINMAG kneader (SM-25, Wuxi, China). For the initial dough formation, the water/citric acid‑sodium citrate buffer was added to the kneader. The dough was then mixed at level 2 (high speed, 40 rpm) for approximately 8–10 min until full gluten development was achieved. The control group consisted of dough without SSPS and citric acid‑sodium citrate buffer. The samples were classified as follows: SSPS0.5, SSPS1.5, and SSPS3.0, which contained 0.5%, 1.5%, and 3.0% SSPS, respectively; pH 4, pH 4.5, and pH 5, which were prepared with citric acid‑sodium citrate buffers at pH 4, pH 4.5, and pH 5, respectively; and SSPS1.5-pH 4.5, which contained 1.5% SSPS along with the citric acid‑sodium citrate buffer at pH 4.5. A portion of the dough was used for physicochemical property testing, while the remainder was freeze-dried, ground, and sieved (150 μm) for further analysis.

Rheological properties

Thermo-mechanical properties

The thermo-mechanical behavior of the dough was characterized using a Mixolab analyzer (Chopin Technologies, Paris, France) following the standard Chopin+ protocol. The mixing speed was maintained at 80 rpm, and water was added to a 75 g sample to reach a consistency of 1.10 ± 0.05 Nm. The analysis was conducted in three successive stages: (1) a constant temperature phase at 30 °C for 8 min to allow dough formation; (2) a gelatinization phase where the temperature was raised to 90 °C at 4 °C/min and held for 7 min; and (3) a retrogradation phase where the dough was cooled to 50 °C at 4 °C/min and maintained for 5 min. From the resulting profiles, water absorption and dough stability of the dough were obtained.

Dynamic rheology

Rheological measurements were performed on the dough samples using a rheometer equipped with 20 mm parallel plates set to a gap of 1 mm. The temperature was controlled at 25 °C throughout the analysis. After the sample was loaded onto the stage, silicone oil was applied to the periphery to minimize moisture loss, and a 5 min equilibration period was allowed. The storage modulus (G'), loss modulus (G"), and loss tangent (tan δ) were determined at a constant shear strain of 0.5% (within the linear viscoelastic region) over a frequency sweep range of 0.1 to 10 Hz.

Water distribution and mobility

Transverse relaxation time

The water mobility and distribution within the dough matrix were evaluated using a low-field NMR spectrometer (MesoMR23–060 V–I, Niumag Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) operating at 23 MHz. For sample preparation, 4.0 g of dough was wrapped in polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) tape to prevent moisture loss and inserted into a 25-mm glass NMR tube. The transverse relaxation properties (T2) were characterized via the Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence. The acquisition parameters were configured as follows: an interval time (TW) of 600 ms, an echo time (TE) of 0.25 ms, 2400 echoes (NECH), and 2 accumulated scans (NS). The resulting decay curves were mathematically inverted to obtain the T2 distribution curve, from which the relaxation times and corresponding peak signal areas (A2) were calculated (Hong et al., 2025).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) was employed to visualize the internal water distribution of the dough. For sample preparation, a 4.0 g dough was wrapped in polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) tape to prevent moisture loss and inserted into a 25-mm diameter glass NMR tube. The imaging was performed using a Spin Echo sequence with the following acquisition parameters: echo time (TE) of 20 ms, repetition time (TR) of 500 ms, and 4 accumulated scans. The slice geometry was defined by a slice thickness (scan width) of 1.0 mm and a slice gap of 1.0 mm. Subsequently, the acquired grayscale data were processed using the Niumag NMR Image Processing Software (Niumag Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) to generate color-coded images, mapping the spatial distribution of proton density within the sample.

Gluten protein molecular size distributions

The molecular size distribution of gluten proteins was determined via Size Exclusion HPLC (SE-HPLC) following the method of Lagrain et al. (2005) with some modification. Freeze-dried dough samples (10 mg) were extracted in 1 mL of 0.05 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) containing 2% (w/v) SDS. The extraction was performed at 25 °C for 60 min, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 5 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and 20 μL of the filtrate was injected into a Shodex Protein KW-804 column (Showa, Kyoto, Japan) maintained at 30 °C. The separation was conducted on a Waters 1525 HPLC system using an elution buffer of 0.05 M PBS (pH 6.8) with 0.2% (w/v) SDS at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min, with detection at 214 nm. To determine the total protein area (ATR), a parallel extraction was performed in the presence of 1.0% dithiothreitol (DTT). The proportions of SDS-soluble polymers (SDS-P), monomers (SDS-M), and insoluble proteins (SDS-I) were calculated using Eqs. (1)–(3):(1)SDS−P%=ASPATR×100%(2)SDS−M%=ASMATR×100%(3)SDS−I%=100%−SP+SM

Where ASP and ASM were represented as the peak area of soluble polymeric and monomeric proteins, respectively; and ATR was represented as the total reduced proteins, which were extracted using an SDS buffer containing 1.0% DTT.

Free sulfhydryl (SH) content

The free sulfhydryl (SH) content was evaluated according to the method of Beveridge et al. (1974) using Ellman's reagent. Freeze-dried dough powder (50 mg) was suspended in 4 mL of Tris-glycine buffer (86 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; 92 mM glycine; 4.1 mM EDTA) containing 2.5% (w/v) SDS. The suspension was agitated at 25 °C for 30 min to solubilize the proteins. Subsequently, 40 μL of 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) solution (4 mg/mL) was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark at 25 °C for an additional 30 min. Following centrifugation at 8000 ×g for 20 min, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 412 nm against a reagent blank (buffer and DTNB). The free SH concentration was quantified using a standard curve constructed with reduced glutathione.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The secondary structure of gluten proteins was analyzed using a Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (IS10, Nicolet, USA). Freeze-dried samples were ground with potassium bromide (KBr) powder at a ratio of 1:100 and compressed into translucent pellets using a hydraulic press. Spectra were acquired in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 accumulated scans. The amide I band (1700–1600 cm−1) was deconvoluted and analyzed using OMNIC 8.2 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) and Peak Fit V.4.12 software (Systat Software Inc., USA) following the method of Zhang et al. (2020).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermal stability was investigated via thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) using a TGA/SDTA851E analyzer (Mettler Toledo Corp., Zurich, Switzerland), following the protocol of Nawrocka et al. (2017). For each measurement, approximately 3.0 mg of lyophilized powder sample was loaded into a crucible. The thermal decomposition profile was recorded by heating the sample from 50 °C to 550 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min under a continuous nitrogen flow (20 mL/min). The degradation temperature (Td) and the corresponding weight loss percentages were calculated using the STAR software (V.9.01).

Morphological structure

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The microstructure of dough was analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (SU8100, Hitachi High-Tech Co., Ltd., Japan). To reveal the cross-sectional microstructure, lyophilized samples were fractured and fixed on aluminum holders. Following gold coating to enhance conductivity, the samples were imaged at 600× and 1500 × .

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

The microstructure of the dough was visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica LSM 710, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Samples were cryo-embedded in Leica gel and sectioned at 20 μm with a CM1950 microtome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were subsequently stained with 5 × 10−5 kg/L rhodamine B for 1 min, rinsed, and covered with a glass slip. Imaging was conducted with an excitation wavelength of 568 nm and an emission wavelength of 625 nm, capturing images at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels. Image analysis was performed using LSM 710 ZEN and Angio Tool software, following the protocol established by Lucas et al. (2019).

Statistical analysis

Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on at least three independent replicates. Data processing was carried out using SPSS 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). To determine statistical differences, a one-way ANOVA was applied, and means were separated using Tukey's test at a significance level of 0.05.

Results and discussion

Rheological properties

Thermo-mechanical properties

The thermo-mechanical properties of the dough were evaluated using a Mixolab device, which simulated dough processing by recording torque evolution as a function of mixing time and temperature. The torque-time curves were presented in Fig. 1 (a). The recorded profiles were characterized by five specific torque points (C1–C5), which represented distinct structural changes in the dough: C1 indicated the maximum torque during the initial mixing stage (dough development); C2 reflected the weakening of the protein network under heating; C3 corresponded to the peak viscosity during starch gelatinization; C4 represented the stability of the hot gel; and C5 denoted the final viscosity during cooling, associated with starch retrogradation. Water absorption was significantly increased by the addition of SSPS in a concentration-dependent manner, as shown in Fig. 1 (b). The highest absorption (64.20%) was exhibited by the SSPS3 group, which was a substantial increase compared to the Control (59.25%). It was suggested that SSPS was highly hydrophilic, competing with gluten proteins for water and retaining more moisture within the dough matrix (Shen et al., 2025). In contrast, water absorption was slightly reduced by acidic conditions (e.g., pH 5 at 57.15%), which was likely attributed to the altered charge density of gluten proteins reducing their hydration capacity (Verdonck et al., 2025). The SSPS1.5–pH 4.5 combination restored dough hydration to a moderate level (60.80%), mitigating the adverse effects of acidic conditions on water absorption.

Dough stability, an indicator of gluten strength, was negatively correlated with SSPS concentration, as shown in Fig. 1 (c). The highest stability (9.87 min) was observed in the control dough. A progressive decline in stability was induced by the addition of SSPS, with the lowest value (4.31 min) recorded for SSPS3, representing a 56.33% reduction. This suggests that excessive SSPS interferes with the formation of a continuous gluten network, possibly by acting as a diluent or disrupting protein–protein interactions. Acidic conditions further destabilized the dough; however, the SSPS1.5–pH 4.5 combination maintained a moderate stability (6.78 min). These results suggested that the presence of SSPS partially mitigated the structural degradation typically induced by acidic conditions.

Dynamic rheology

The dynamic rheological frequency sweeping of dough was presented in Fig. 2. The values of G′ and G′′ moduli increased with an increase in frequency for all samples. The tan δ decreased at a low-frequency range while elevated at a high-frequency range, illustrating that the dough tended to have solid-like behavior when deformed slowly but liquid-like behavior when sheared at a high rate. As illustrated in Fig. 2, a marked increase in both G′ and G″ was observed when 0.5% SSPS (w/w) was added, compared to the control sample. This strengthening effect was attributed to synergistic interactions between SSPS and gluten proteins, which were likely mediated through hydrogen bonding or physical entanglement. However, a further increase in SSPS concentration to 3.0% was accompanied by a decline in G′ and G″, with values approaching those of the control. Concurrently, the highest tan δ values at high frequencies were exhibited by the 3.0% SSPS sample (Fig. 2 c). This phenomenon was likely attributed to the dilution effect of non-gluten polymers or to water competition. Excessive SSPS may interfere with the interactions between gluten, thereby disrupting the continuity of the protein network and resulting in a relatively softer dough structure (lower G′ and G″) (Zhu et al., 2017).Fig. 2Changes in (a) elastic modulus (G′), (b) viscous modulus (G′′) and (c) loss tangent (tan δ) of dough with SSPS under acidic conditions.Fig. 2

Moreover, a progressive reduction in pH from neutral to 4.0 led to a significant decrease in both G′ and G″. The weakening of dough structure under acidic conditions may be attributed to the increase in the net positive charge of gluten proteins, which enhanced electrostatic repulsion and thus inhibited the aggregation required to form a dense network. Furthermore, mild acid-induced gluten protein hydrolysis may also be considered a contributing factor to the loss of elasticity. Notably, the addition of SSPS demonstrated a protective effect against acid-induced structural degradation. The sample containing 1.5% SSPS at pH 4.5 exhibited consistently higher G' values compared to the control dough at pH 4.5 without SSPS. This suggested that SSPS effectively mitigated the weakening effect of acidity, potentially by complexing with gluten proteins or stabilizing the water phase, thereby helping to retain a degree of structural integrity even in an unfavorable acidic environment.

Water mobility and distribution of dough

Spin-spin relaxation time (T2) was utilized to assess water molecular mobility, while the interactions between water molecules and non-aqueous components were evaluated based on the observed variations in T2 (Liu et al., 2023). Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) was employed to investigate the water migration and distribution within the dough matrix under different SSPS concentrations and acidic conditions. The distribution of T2 for the dough samples was illustrated in Fig. 3 (a). Three distinct proton populations were identified, corresponding to three states of water mobility. Strongly bound water, which was closely associated with starch granules and gluten proteins via hydrogen bonding, was represented by the first population, T21 (0.1–10 ms). Immobilized water trapped within the three-dimensional gluten-starch network was attributed to the second and dominant population, T22 (10–100 ms). As shown in Fig. 3 (a), the relaxation curves for all samples were dominated by the T22 peak, indicating that the majority of water in the dough existed in an immobilized state within the gluten network. A notable shift in signal amplitude was observed among the treatments, suggesting that the addition of SSPS and alterations in pH significantly modified the hydration dynamics of the dough.

The relative peak areas (A21, A22, and A23) of the bread dough were quantified as shown in Fig. 3 (b), representing the proportion of water in each distinct state. Across all samples, the immobilized water fraction (A22) was the most abundant, ranging from approximately 73.5% to 76.9%. The addition of SSPS resulted in a discernible redistribution of water. Specifically, as SSPS concentration increased (SSPS 0.5 to SSPS 3), the fraction of bound water (A21) decreased while the immobilized water fraction (A22) increased. This phenomenon could be attributed to the high hydrophilicity of SSPS; the abundant hydroxyl groups in the polysaccharide likely compete with gluten proteins for water binding, thereby retaining more water in the immobilized state within the hydrocolloid network rather than in the tightly bound state (Zhou et al., 2021). Furthermore, the sample at pH 4 displayed the lowest A21 (21.92%) and the highest A22 (76.86%). It was hypothesized that the acidic environment increased the net positive charge of the gluten proteins, leading to electrostatic repulsion and the unfolding of the protein structure (Majzoobi & Abedi, 2014). This structural expansion allowed the gluten network to entrap more water (increasing A22) while reducing the fraction of A21. Interestingly, the combined treatment of SSPS 1.5 at pH 4.5 was found to maintain water distribution proportions (A21: 24.87%, A22: 74.02%) comparable to the control, suggesting that this specific combination promoted a stable hydration equilibrium similar to that of native dough.

The spatial distribution of water protons within the dough was visualized using Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) (Fig. 3 c). In the pseudo-color images, high proton density (high moisture content) was indicated by red hues, whereas low proton density was represented by blue hues. A heterogeneous moisture distribution was exhibited by the control sample, characterized by a high-intensity red center transitioning to yellow/green at the periphery. In contrast, higher signal intensity and larger areas of high density were generally observed in the samples treated with SSPS. These results were correlated with the T2 relaxation data (Fig. 3 b), and the enhancement of the dough's water-holding capacity by SSPS was confirmed. It was postulated that moisture migration was restricted by steric barriers formed by the polysaccharide network, leading to effective moisture retention. A uniform moisture distribution was displayed by the SSPS 1.5-pH 4.5 sample. Consequently, it was inferred that water dispersion was optimized by the interaction between SSPS and the gluten network under mildly acidic conditions, which was identified as a critical factor for consistent dough rheology (Section 3.1) and texture (Hong et al., 2022).

Compared to the control group, the contents of SDS-soluble polymers (SDS-P, Mw ≈ 88,000–698,000) and monomers (SDS-M, Mw < 88,000) increased progressively with decreasing pH of the citric acid–sodium citrate buffer. In contrast, the content of insoluble SDS protein (SDS-I) declined significantly (Fig. 4 b). This trend aligned with the findings of (Zhang et al., 2020), who reported depolymerization of SDS-I in dough systems hydrated under acidic conditions. This result was likely caused by two factors. First, endogenous cereal proteases were activated under acidic pH conditions, resulting in the enzymatic depolymerization of gluten. Second, the net charge of the gluten proteins was altered at low pH, which inhibited the formation of strong intermolecular cross-links and led to a more dispersed and depolymerized protein structure (Garg et al., 2019; Thiele et al., 2004). In addition, partial depolymerization of gluten protein molecules was also caused by the addition of SSPS. Notably, the SDS-I content in the SSPS3 treatment was reduced by 15.92% compared with the control group (Fig. 4 b), indicating a disruption of the glutenin macropolymer (GMP) network. SSPS, as an anionic polysaccharide, was adsorbed onto gluten protein surfaces to form protein–polysaccharide complexes, in which its bulky, negatively charged chains were suggested to introduce steric hindrance and electrostatic shielding that might have weakened intermolecular disulfide and noncovalent gluten–gluten associations. In addition, the high water-binding capacity of SSPS was assumed to lead to competitive hydration, through which gluten protein hydration and mobility might have been altered and proper unfolding and cross-linking might have been hindered (Zhou et al., 2021). Compared with the control group, the SDS-I content in the SSPS 1.5–pH 4.5 group exhibited a discernible decrease (Fig. 4b), which aligned with the corresponding reduction in G' values (Fig. 2). This trend suggests that the partial depolymerization of the glutenin macropolymer network potentially accounts for the transition of the dough from an excessively rigid state into a more balanced, softer, and more stretchable conformation under these specific conditions.

Free sulfhydryl (SH) content

The free SH content was employed as a key indicator of changes in disulfide (SS) bonds in gluten proteins (Wang et al., 2019). Compared to the control, a significant increase in SH content was detected in dough samples supplemented with SSPS and in those prepared under acidic conditions (Fig. 5 a). Among all treatments, the dough mixed with citrate–citric acid buffer at pH 4.0 exhibited the highest SH content, indicating a pronounced disruption and depolymerization of the gluten network under strongly acidic conditions. When comparing the effects of SSPS and acidic pH individually, it was found that the acidic pH exerted a more substantial influence on the gluten protein structure than SSPS alone. Moreover, in the sample subjected to the combined treatment of SSPS and acidic pH (pH 4.5), the SH content was lower than that observed in the sample treated with acidic pH alone, which was consistent with the molecular weight distribution profile obtained from SE-HPLC analysis (Section 3.3). Although acidic conditions markedly promoted the cleavage of disulfide bonds, leading to the exposure of SH groups and extensive gluten depolymerization, it was inferred that SSPS may have interacted with gluten proteins via non-covalent interactions, thereby stabilizing protein conformations or facilitating partial reformation of disulfide linkages. Consequently, the extent of SH group exposure was attenuated in the combined treatment, reflecting a partial stabilization of the gluten macropolymer network. This result was likely due to the interaction of SSPS with positively charged glutenin through electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions. This interaction formed a “biopolymer complex” that could physically stabilize the protein structure under acidic conditions (Nakamura, Furuta, Kato, Maeda, & Yoshimoto, 2003). The attenuated free SH exposure in the SSPS1.5–pH 4.5 sample is consistent with the inhibition of acid-induced disulfide bond cleavage, providing a biochemical basis for the preserved gluten macropolymer content (Section 3.3) and the enhanced dough stability noted in Section 3.1.Fig. 5The free sulfhydryl content (a) and protein secondary structure (b) of dough with SSPS under acidic conditions. Secondary structure

The protein secondary structure of the bread dough was analyzed using ATR-FTIR to evaluate the effects of SSPS addition and acidification (pH 4.0, 4.5, and 5.0) on intermolecular interactions reflected in the amide I absorption band (1600–1700 cm−1). In this study, four major secondary structure components (antiparallel β-sheet, α-helix, β-turn, and β-sheet) were identified, and their relative contents were quantified as the percentage of each component's area relative to the total amide I band area.

For all gluten dough samples, β-sheet structures (including both antiparallel and parallel β-sheets) were the predominant secondary structures, accounting for approximately 45–50% of the total area. This observation was consistent with the major gluten conformations reported in previous studies (Zhang et al., 2020), where intermolecular β-sheets were known to contribute to the stable, elastic network of the dough. Specifically, antiparallel β-sheets were the most abundant individual component, maintaining a stable proportion of approximately 39–40% across all treatment groups (Fig. 5 b), suggesting that the core backbone of the gluten network remained relatively resistant to the treatments applied. However, significant variations were observed in the proportions of α-helix and β-turn, which displayed an inverse relationship depending on the SSPS concentration and pH environment.

Compared to the control, the addition of 1.5% SSPS significantly increased α-helix content to 30% while reducing β-turn to 22%. This transition toward a more ordered conformation was likely driven by electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding between the anionic SSPS molecules and gluten residues, which stabilized the polypeptide backbone and facilitate the folding of disordered chains into compact helical structures (Zhang et al., 2023). Conversely, increasing the SSPS concentration to 3.0% reversed this trend, suggesting a concentration-dependent threshold. At higher concentrations, excess polysaccharide chains likely introduced significant steric hindrance and compete with gluten for water (hydration competition); this macromolecular crowding disrupted the ordered folding observed at lower concentrations, thereby favoring thermodynamically less constrained β-turn structures (Nawrocka et al., 2018). The pH environment similarly modulated protein folding, where less acidic conditions appeared to alter the net charge distribution of the proteins. The resulting reduction in intramolecular electrostatic repulsion likely minimized the folding energy barrier, allowing polypeptide chains to adopt ordered α-helix conformations rather than remaining in extended states (Yan et al., 2021). However, the SSPS1.5-pH 4.5 treatment yielded a profile indistinguishable from the control, suggesting that at pH 4.5, the specific surface charge density of gluten may weaken its affinity for SSPS or induce charge neutralization. Consequently, the distinct stabilizing forces exerted by SSPS and pH appear to counteract one another in this composite system, effectively preserving the native structural equilibrium of the gluten network. The transition toward a more ordered α-helix conformation at moderate SSPS levels aligns with the increased G' values (Section 3.1.2), suggesting that a stabilized protein backbone contributes to the maintenance of dough elasticity under acidic conditions.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Typical parameters from TGA curves were summarized in Fig. 6. The thermogravimetric profiles (Fig. 6 b) showed two main steps: an initial weight loss from 50 °C to 150 °C associated with the evaporation of free or bound water, and a further loss in the temperature range of 150 °C–600 °C involving the cleavage of covalent linkages and the thermal decomposition of the protein-starch network (Zhang et al., 2020). The thermal degradation behavior of the dough was significantly influenced by both pH conditions and the concentration of SSPS. The Control sample exhibited a weight loss of 81.49% and a decomposition temperature (310.18 °C). The addition of SSPS at lower concentrations (0.5% and 1.5%) did not result in a statistically significant difference in weight loss compared to the Control. However, increasing the polysaccharide concentration to 3% (SSPS3) led to a significant increase in weight loss (83.17%), suggesting that an excessive amount of SSPS may disrupt the continuity of the gluten network, thereby reducing its resistance to thermal degradation.Fig. 6The TGA curves (a), first derivative curve (b), weight loss (c) and degradation temperature (d) of dough with SSPS under acidic conditions; Different lowercase letters in panel (c) and panel (d) indicated significant differences among samples (p < 0.05).Fig. 6

In contrast, acidic environments had a marked destabilizing effect on the gluten matrix. As the pH decreased from 5 to 4, the weight loss significantly increased, peaking at 84.82% for the pH 4 sample. Correspondingly, the Td values for the acidic samples (pH 4, 4.5, and 5) were significantly lower than that of the Control, dropping to as low as 306.14 °C. This reduction in thermal stability was likely attributed to the intense electrostatic repulsion between positively charged amino acid residues under acidic conditions, which induced the unfolding of gluten proteins and created a looser, less cohesive network structure that degraded more rapidly upon heating (Zhang et al., 2020). Notably, the thermal stability of the dough system was restored by the simultaneous addition of SSPS and acidification (SSPS1.5–pH 4.5), suggesting that a protective effect was exerted by SSPS on the gluten network under acidic conditions. This effect was likely attributed to electrostatic interactions between anionic soluble soybean polysaccharides and cationic gluten proteins at pH 4.5, which may have reinforced the network and enhanced the thermal resistance of the composite dough (Nakamura et al., 2003). The restored thermal stability in composite doughs further supports the structural integrity of the gluten network, consistent with the findings from chemical bonding (Section 3.4) and secondary structure analyses (Section 3.5).

Morphological structure

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The cross-sections of the dough samples were investigated using SEM at magnifications of 600× (Fig. 7 a) and 1500× (Fig. 7 b). In the Control group, the dough exhibited a standard microstructure where starch granules were embedded within a gluten protein matrix. However, the microstructure exhibited a heterogeneous morphology characterized by structural discontinuities and distinct void spaces. As the concentration of SSPS increased from 0.5% to 1.5%, the protein matrix became progressively smoother and more continuous. Notably, in the SSPS 1.5% sample, the starch granules were almost entirely encapsulated by a dense, sheet-like protein layer, suggesting that SSPS facilitates the formation of a more cohesive gluten network. However, at SSPS 3%, while the coating effect persisted, slight irregularities in the texture were observed, possibly due to the self-aggregation of polysaccharides at higher concentrations. Conversely, the acidification of the dough induced severe structural deterioration. As shown in the samples ranging from pH 5.0 to pH 4.0, the gluten network appeared increasingly porous and fragmented. In the pH 4.5 and pH 4.0 samples specifically, the protein sheet was disrupted, leaving large numbers of starch granules exposed and loosely associated with the matrix. This phenomenon might be attributed to the strong electrostatic repulsion between gluten proteins at lower pH values, which prevented the formation of a compact network and weakens the dough structure. This observation was consistent with the results obtained by SE-HPLC (Section 3.3).

A synergistic effect was observed in the SSPS 1.5 - pH 4.5 sample. Despite the acidic environment, the addition of 1.5% SSPS completely mitigated the acid-induced damage. The microstructure appeared remarkably dense, uniform, and continuous. The starch granules were deeply buried within a thickened protein-polysaccharide matrix. This suggested that anionic SSPS molecules interacted electrostatically with the positively charged gluten proteins in the acidic medium, acting as cross-linking agents that bridged protein chains and stabilized the network against acid hydrolysis and charge repulsion.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM) was utilized to characterize the spatial distribution and continuity of the gluten network, as illustrated in Fig. 7 (c). The Control sample displayed a distinct protein network; however, the distribution was heterogeneous, and noticeable gaps (black areas) representing starch granules or air voids. The SSPS 1.5% sample exhibited a more interconnected and homogenous protein network compared to the control. The red fluorescence was brighter and more uniform, indicating a denser aggregation of gluten proteins promoted by the SSPS addition, which was in agreement with the SEM findings.

The destructive effect of acidity was clearly visualized in the pH 4.5 sample. The protein network appeared highly fragmented and discontinuous, with large voids disrupting the structural integrity. The network discontinuity was corroborated by the Angio Tool analysis (bottom row), characterized by isolated protein clusters instead of a continuous matrix. Notably, a highly compact and continuous network structure was exhibited by the SSPS 1.5-pH 4.5 sample. A protein matrix with minimal fragmentation was observed, which effectively occupied the interstitial spaces between starch granules. This confirmed that the complexation between SSPS and gluten under acidic conditions rearranged the protein microstructure, transforming the fragmented network seen at pH 4.5 into a reinforced, homogeneous matrix. This structural reinforcement explained the improved rheological properties observed in SSPS-fortified acidic doughs (Section 3.1).

Conclusion

The addition of SSPS improved the rheological properties of dough under acidic conditions and restricted the mobility of water molecules. This effect was likely due to the ability of SSPS to prevent the depolymerization of gluten protein polymers through electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding. These interactions stabilized the secondary structure and thermal stability of gluten proteins, resulting in a more compact and cohesive dough microstructure. These findings provided valuable insights into the regulation of protein–polysaccharide interactions for enhancing the processing stability and product quality of acidic wheat-based foods. Nevertheless, further research is needed to characterize the structural-functional relationships and to validate the consistent performance of SSPS across diverse concentration–pH environments and practical food systems.
