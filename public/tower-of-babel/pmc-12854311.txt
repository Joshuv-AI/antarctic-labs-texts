The effect of text message-based health guidelines reinforcement on clinical outcomes following non-surgical periodontal therapy: A randomized clinical trial
Authors: Farzane Vaziri, Milad Eidi, Fahimeh Rashidi Maybodi, Nazanin Roqani Dehkordi, Nahid Khoddami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Periodontitis and gingivitis are two prevalent periodontal diseases caused by oral dysbiosis, which is mostly caused by the presence of tooth plaque. In periodontology, motivational interviewing (MI) is a patient-centered communication strategy that has been shown to improve patient motivation. The Short Message Service (SMS), in particular, has had enormous impacts on healthcare. The purpose of this study is to investigate how text message-based health guidelines reinforcement affects clinical results after non-surgical periodontal treatment in individuals with periodontal disease.METHODS:In this double-blind, randomized study, 36 patients with stage I and stage II periodontitis received non-surgical treatment and were randomly assigned to two experimental and control groups. The experimental group received a motivational-health text message intervention twice weekly for four weeks, whereas the control group did not receive text messages. Before and after the intervention, clinical parameters such as pocket depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), plaque index (PI), and gingival bleeding index (BI) were assessed.RESULTS:Periodontal parameters improved in both the experimental and control groups in this study. All parameters were improved significantly before and after the intervention in both the experimental and control groups (P- value < 0.001). Furthermore, it was revealed that the group who got scaling and root planning in addition to motivating text messages during the maintenance period had a significant reduction in BI (P value < 0.001) and PI (P value < 0.05).CONCLUSION:Text messaging used in conjunction with a traditional approach (SRP + MI) may help patients achieve treatment objectives and aid in the management of periodontal disease.

Introduction

Periodontitis and gingivitis are the two main periodontal diseases.[1] Insufficient oral hygiene can result in oral dysbiosis, which can cause a majority of periodontal diseases, mainly due to the presence of bacterial biofilm known as dental plaque.[2] Experimental models of gingivitis demonstrate that reinstating oral hygiene can reduce inflammation and promote tissue healing.[3] If left without any medical attention, it has the potential to advance to periodontitis in individuals who are susceptible to it.[45] Periodontitis not only causes gum inflammation, but it can also lead to a severe destructive process that specifically affects the supporting structures of the tooth, such as alveolar bone. This destructive process is irreversible and can ultimately result in tooth loss, which can significantly impact an individual’s oral health and overall quality of life.[6] An essential component of periodontal therapy is educating and motivating patients to achieve an adequate level of oral hygiene. The establishment of a proficient oral hygiene routine is an essential factor in the success of periodontal therapy, and several clinical frameworks, such as motivational interviewing (MI), have been created to enable the alteration of behavior. However, two obstacles may impede the success of these approaches. Firstly, patients’ recall of the advice given during consultations may be limited. Secondly, maintaining long-term motivation can be challenging, potentially leading to relapse. These challenges underscore the importance of developing innovative strategies to support patients’ long-term adherence to oral hygiene practices.[7]

Providing patients with guidance and encouragement to attain and sustain an adequate standard of oral hygiene is a crucial component of periodontal treatment. Practitioners can use behavior change techniques to improve a patient’s motivation for maintaining long-term oral hygiene and healthy habits.[8] Motivational interviewing (MI) has been proven to be an effective patient-centered communication technique that enhances patient motivation in the field of periodontology.[910111213]

Alternative modes of communication, such as self-educational manuals and audio-visual aids, have been studied as a substitute for personal instruction. Periodic prompts can motivate individuals to adopt healthy behaviors.[14] Reinforcement is a crucial principle of health education that promotes the adoption of healthy behaviors and lifestyles.[15] In addition to introducing new behaviors, the health education program reinforces and maintains good behavior. This can lead to improved individual, group, and community health results.[1617]

The impact of technological advancements on individuals and society has been substantial and far-reaching, with mobile phones having a ubiquitous influence across all sectors and strata of society. The utilization of mobile devices has experienced a surge on a global scale, and novel advancements in mobile technology have been swiftly revolutionizing the healthcare domain, especially in regions that are geographically isolated and have a high degree of mobile phone adoption.[18] The mobile short messaging service, commonly referred to as SMS, has emerged as a remarkable and extensively employed means of real-time communication, demonstrating remarkable levels of penetration.[19] Providing health information on disease prevention and treatment can be effectively and affordably communicated to people through short messaging services.

Text messaging has demonstrated its efficacy in promoting healthier behaviors including compliance with treatment guidelines, smoking cessation, dietary advice, and exercise regimes, thus contributing to the prevention of behavior-related diseases.[151720]

Considering that this study was conducted during the coronavirus pandemic It is highly recommended that every healthcare professional wear personal protective equipment, such as masks, protective goggles, gowns, helmets, gloves, hats, face shields, and shoe coverings, as the transfer of airborne droplets is thought to be one of the primary ways that infections spread.[21] Chlorhexidine, which is frequently used in dental offices for preprocedural mouthwashing, has not yet been shown to be effective in eliminating 2019-nCoV. It is advised, therefore, to prescribe oxidative agents that include mouthwashes, such as 0.2% povidone or 1% hydrogen peroxide.[21]

Thus far, limited research has explored the potential benefits of using SMS messaging to reinforce health instructions and motivate patients with periodontal disease. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effect of text message-based health guidelines reinforcement on clinical outcomes following non-surgical periodontal therapy among patients with periodontal disease.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

In this double-blind, randomized clinical trial, a total of 36 patients who were diagnosed with periodontitis were selected from the Periodontics Department located at the Dental School of Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences in Yazd, Iran between January 2022 and May 2022. The study was carried out based on the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. Once the patients satisfied all the required selection criteria, they were given detailed information about the study and then requested to provide written consent before participating.

Study participants and sampling

Patients with periodontitis who had stage I and II, based on clinical attachment loss of less than or equal to 4 mm around the teeth and disease severity according to the 2017 international periodontology workshop,[22] were chosen for the study. This study follows the consort checklist.

Inclusion criteria were ages between 18 and 40 years, they have a minimum of 20 teeth, which must include at least four permanent molars and four permanent premolars systemically healthy, not having undergone periodontal treatment in the last 6 months, not having taken antibiotics, having access to a mobile phone and being proficient in reading and writing in the Persian language and negative PCR test (COVID-19 status).

The study excluded individuals who had difficulty maintaining a twice-daily tooth-brushing routine and neglected daily interdental care. Those who were pregnant, smokers, or using removable and fixed orthodontic appliances were also excluded from participation.

Data collection tool and technique

The first assessor measured and recorded the periodontal parameters, comprising probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), plaque index (PI), and gingival bleeding index (BI), for each patient before any procedures being performed. After this assessment, the patients underwent scaling and root planning as well as motivational interviewing. The second assessor proceeded to randomly allocate patients into either the experimental or the control group, where the experimental group was provided with a health-boosting text message intervention twice weekly for four weeks, while the control group did not receive text messages.

After four weeks, the first examiner, who was blinded to the study group assignment, re-measured and recorded all periodontal parameters, including PD, CAL, PI, and BI, for both the experimental and control groups.

The design of this investigation ensured that the primary assessor, who measured the periodontal parameters, was unaware of the patients’ group assignments. This approach was employed to minimize any bias in the evaluation of the periodontal parameters. The secondary assessor, who randomized the patients into their respective groups, played no role in the initial assessment of the periodontal parameters and was also oblivious to the study hypothesis. This was done to guarantee that the randomization process was not influenced in any way. The use of this strategy helped to ensure that the results of the investigation were not biased by any preconceptions the assessors may have had about the patients or their conditions.

Periodontal examinations were performed by utilizing the Ramfjord index,[23] which is a comprehensive method of evaluating the depth of the periodontal pockets and the degree of attachment loss in six specific teeth within the oral cavity. These teeth include the maxillary left first molar, maxillary right first molar, mandibular left first molar, mandibular right first molar, mandibular right central incisor, and maxillary left central incisor.

The Williams probe, a specialized and calibrated instrument, was employed for the assessment of probing depth (PD) and clinical attachment loss (CAL). The PD was quantified by measuring the distance between the gingival margin and the point of deepest probe penetration in the respective areas. On the other hand, the CAL was recorded by measuring the distance between the cementoenamel junction and the point of deepest probe penetration.

The assessment of dental plaque, a crucial indicator of oral health, can be accomplished through a variety of methods. In this study, the plaque index was measured utilizing the Sillness and Loe index. This method involves assigning a score of 0, 1, 2, or 3 based on the amount of visible plaque present on the tooth surfaces, where a score of 0 signifies the absence of plaque, and a score of 3 indicates a significant buildup of plaque that is observable without any magnification.[24]

The gingival bleeding index was evaluated by probing the sulcus of the teeth and calculating the index 30 seconds after the probing procedure, with all clinical measurements performed by a single assessor to ensure uniformity and consistency.[25]

All patients underwent scaling and root planning using an ultrasonic scaler (ultrasonic piezo scaler Uds-K, Woodpecker, China) in the subsequent stage, followed by motivational interviewing. The experimental group received weekly text messages for four weeks. After this time, periodontal examinations were remeasured for both groups, and the periodontal parameters were recorded [Figure 1].

The motivational-health message reads:

“Smile bright with good oral hygiene! Brush and floss twice a day to keep your teeth and gums healthy and beautiful and reduce snacking between meals. Wash your mouth after eating to prevent decay and infection. Let’s share our smiles with confidence!”

Sample size

To determine the appropriate sample size for the current study, a significance level of 5% and a test power of 80% were taken into consideration. The standard deviation of the bleeding size index obtained from prior research[26] was used as the basis for determining the sample size. The value of S was determined to be 0.5. To detect a significant difference of at least 1 unit in the average index, a sample size of 16 individuals per group was required. However, to account for a 10% dropout rate, the sample size was ultimately increased to 18 individuals per group.

Randomization

The individuals who met the criteria for participation were allocated to the experimental and control groups in a randomized manner by employing a random numbers table and the Random Allocation software.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis of the present study was done in two parts: descriptive statistics and inferential statistics. In the descriptive statistics section, central tendency (mean) and dispersion (standard deviation) measures were reported along with tables and graphs. In the inferential statistics section, the normality of the data was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test. To compare the gender and age distribution of the participants between the study groups, Chi-square and Independent sample T-tests were used, respectively. Independent sample T-test was used to compare PD, CAL, PI, and BI variables between the case and control groups before the intervention. Analysis of covariance was used to compare PD, CAL, PI, and BOP variables between the case and control groups after the intervention. Paired sample T-test was used to compare PD, CAL, PI, and BI variables before and after the intervention. For data analysis, use IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0 software. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp. used. The significance level in this study was considered 0.05.

Ethical consideration

The Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee has provided approval for the clinical trial with a designated code of IR SSU.REC.1400.077 and additionally, the trial has been registered with the IRCT and assigned registration code IRCT20171015036782N8 on 22/01/2022.

Results

This clinical trial included 36 patients with periodontitis (stage I and II) who were referred to the periodontology department at Yazd’s Shahid Sadoughi Dental School. The participants were randomized to the experimental and control groups at random. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to determine the normality of the quantitative variables. Of the 36 patients, 18 (50%) were male, while the remaining participants were female. The Chi-Square test showed that the gender distribution of the two groups was not significantly different (P value = 0.999).

The investigation involved a comparison of the average age of the participants in both the experimental and control groups. The independent sample T-test was used to demonstrate that the mean age of the case group was 28.83 ± 7.44, whereas in the control group, it was 27.50 ± 7.59, with an age range of 18-40 years. A thorough statistical analysis was conducted, which revealed that there was no significant variance in the mean age of the patients between the two groups, as indicated by a P value of 0.598. As such, the age factor was not a significant contributor to the differences observed between the two groups.

The investigation evaluated the differences in periodontal clinical parameters observed between the experimental and control groups before and following an intervention. Before the intervention, no statistically significant differences in periodontal markers such as probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), plaque index (PI), and gingival bleeding index (BI) were found using independent sample T-tests. The results obtained from the paired t-test performed on both the experimental and control groups provide substantial evidence for a marked decrease in all variables after the intervention when compared to their pre-intervention values (P value < 0.001).

The paired t-test analysis indicated that after the intervention, the experimental group had a significantly lower BI (16.27 ± 6.37) than the control group (31.70 ± 13.33) (P value < 0.001). Also, the experimental group had a significantly lower PI (16.27 ± 6.37) than the control group (31.70 ± 13.33) after the intervention (P value < 0.05). However, no statistically significant differences in the other variables studied after the intervention were found between the experimental and control groups [Table 1 and Figure 2].

Discussion

Maintaining good oral health is crucial for overall well-being. Although dentists provide patients with optimal treatment and oral care instructions during chairside consultations, patient compliance remains a crucial factor.[27] Many people are not responding favorably to the simple advice of brushing and flossing that is routinely given during their dental hygiene care appointments, as evidenced by various studies.[27] The results of this study have broader implications for practice, particularly due to the cost-effectiveness and ease of use of text messaging (TMs). Text messaging can be effortlessly integrated into the daily routines of oral health practitioners, as well as being integrated into a more comprehensive stepped patient care approach. This underscores the promise of utilizing Text Message interventions as a practical and attainable instrument for encouraging oral health, as well as enhancing patient compliance with oral hygiene practices. Thus, recognizing the potential of text messaging as an alternative means of communication in the field of oral health care is essential to the provision of adequate oral health care services.[28]

Technological advancements have made healthcare facilities more accessible,[26] and dental literature has previously highlighted the benefits of using technology in various aspects of dental sciences.[293031] The use of short messaging services (SMS) through mobile phones is an emerging tool that can promote the adoption of healthier behaviors, including the reinforcement of oral health education messages. Such interventions can be an effective means of improving oral health outcomes.[17]

There was a significant decrease in periodontal markers in both the experimental and control groups in the current investigation. This result may have been caused by the participants’ receiving oral health education before the trial began, which may have led to a boost in oral hygiene practices for as long as one month. Only the experimental group got reinforcement of oral health messages through short SMS after the baseline data was collected. This particular intervention is highly probable to have positively affected the subjects’ consciousness regarding the crucial nature of oral hygiene and encouraged them to practice appropriate oral health habits. In all likelihood, this approach was instrumental in inducing awareness and motivation among the participants to embrace healthy oral practices.

Comparing the experimental group to the control group, a statistically significant reduction in BOP and PI was seen. The individuals in the experimental group could have been more oriented towards health consciousness, alertness, and motivation to implement measures that promote good dental hygiene, such as appropriate brushing and flossing techniques. The results of this study imply that consistent reminders via SMS could foster the adoption of healthy behaviors and ultimately result in better oral health outcomes. Therefore, mobile health technology has the potential to serve as a useful tool in promoting oral health practices and improving overall health outcomes.

Several studies have also reported the effectiveness of text message reminder systems in improving oral hygiene compliance.[283233343536] For instance, Iqbal and colleagues, in their study, discovered that there was a significant reduction in plaque and bleeding scores among the text message group as opposed to the control group.[34] The findings of the study conducted by Araújo and colleagues were consistent with those of our research. Their observations revealed that the use of Novel Floss Holder in conjunction with Text Message resulted in a significant reduction of bleeding on probing (BOP) compared to other groups.[28] Zotti and colleagues found that the use of WhatsApp to engage adolescent patients had a positive impact on their compliance with orthodontic treatment, resulting in better long-term outcomes in terms of orthodontic stability. This suggests that utilizing social media platforms like WhatsApp can be a useful tool for clinicians to improve patient compliance. However, their study also showed that in patients with gingivitis, WhatsApp messages did not provide any additional benefits to oral hygiene practices compared to traditional motivational and educational methods. Therefore, clinicians should carefully consider the patient population and their specific needs before implementing social media platforms like WhatsApp as a part of their treatment approach.[32]

Despite SRP being the gold standard treatment, the main problem is bacterial recolonization.[37] In consideration of this, the implementation of adjunctive therapy is important. It has been demonstrated that using antibiotics improves microbiological and clinical results.

Due to the lack of contraindications and other issues associated with antibiotics, medicines based on para-probiotics may be especially important.[38]

Limitation and recommendation

The short duration of the follow-up period was one of our study’s limitations. Because of the COVID-19 pandemic, we had to end the study early, and we had to depend on the present sample size and follow-up duration, which was deemed sufficient under the circumstances. As a result, large-scale research with larger sample numbers and longer follow-up periods is required to give more strong data. Such studies would be essential in determining the effectiveness of various interventions aimed at improving oral health outcomes and compliance with oral hygiene practices.

Because the patients referred to the university had a low socio-economic status, text messaging was used as a reminder in this study. It is suggested to use educational clips using advanced messengers in the statistical population that have a good socio-economic status.

Given that the research was done during the Corona disease pandemic, some studies have indicated that people with periodontitis have more COVID complications.[394041] As a result, highlighting the necessity of maintaining optimum dental hygiene at this time can be a highly successful strategy, and this is one of our study’s strengths. It is crucial to recognize the significance of maintaining good oral health to mitigate the potential risks associated with COVID-19, and our research underscores this critical message.

Conclusion

This study has shown a decline in periodontal parameters in both the experimental and control groups. Additionally, it has been discovered that the group that received motivational SMS messages during the maintenance phase experienced a substantial decrease in BI. As periodontists, it is our responsibility to encourage policymakers to advocate for telecommunications companies to distribute free oral health educational SMS messages at the community level as a social obligation, to improve the general health and well-being of society.
