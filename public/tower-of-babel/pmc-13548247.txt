Spatial habitat radiomics predicts tertiary lymphoid structure status and identifies an IDO1+ migratory dendritic cell axis in breast cancer
Authors: Yushuai Yu, Qing Wang, Yidan Lin, Kaiyan Huang, Ruijuan Wang, Junxiao Wang, Xiewei Huang, Jie Zhang, Weiwei Chen, Ruiliang Chen, Xuejun Chen, Fan Meng, Hengyu Zhang, Junhui Yuan, Jianqing Lin, Chuangui Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) are spatially organized immune niches associated with therapeutic response and favorable outcomes in breast cancer (BC). However, TLS assessment currently relies on invasive tissue-based analyses, and the biological mechanisms underlying imaging-based TLS prediction remain poorly understood.MethodsWe developed and validated a spatial heterogeneity-based radiomic TLS signature (shTLS) using dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI to non-invasively predict TLS status across multicenter BC cohorts. Spatial habitat radiomics were used to capture intratumoral and peritumoral immune-related heterogeneity. Integrated multi-omics analyses, including transcriptomics, pathomics, genomics, single-cell RNA sequencing, immunohistochemistry, and multiplex immunofluorescence, were performed to biologically interpret shTLS-defined subgroups. Functional drug-sensitivity assays were conducted to assess therapeutic implications.ResultsThe shTLS model achieved robust predictive performance across independent cohorts and molecular subtypes. High shTLS scores were associated with immune-inflamed tumors characterized by spatially clustered activated T cells and dendritic cells (DCs). In contrast, shTLS-low tumors exhibited an immunosuppressive spatial niche with peripheral accumulation of CD4+ PD-1+ T cells and plasma cells, increased immune-tumor separation, and enhanced inflammatory and immunoregulatory signaling. An indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1)-associated immunoregulatory program was observed in the shTLS-low tumors, which appeared to be preferentially expressed by LAMP3+CCR7+ migratory DCs. Pharmacologic inhibition of IDO1 enhanced chemotherapy and CDK4/6 inhibitor sensitivity in vitro.ConclusionThis study establishes spatial radiomics as a non-invasive approach to decode TLS-associated immune ecosystems and supports the presence of an IDO1-associated immunosuppressive phenotype, providing biological insight and translational rationale for patient stratification and future combination strategies.Keywords: Breast Cancer, Macrophage, Neoadjuvant, Pathologic complete response - pCR, Tumor microenvironment - TME

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is characterized by substantial biological and clinical heterogeneity, which contributes to variable responses to neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) and divergent prognostic outcomes.1–4 Among immune-related biomarkers, tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS), ectopic lymphoid aggregates formed within or around tumors, have emerged as clinically relevant markers of antitumor immunity.5 TLS are associated with enhanced immune activation, improved therapeutic response, and favorable prognosis in BC; however, their clinical application remains limited by the reliance on tissue-based assessment.6–9

Histological assessment remains the reference standard for TLS evaluation. Therefore, the purpose of an imaging-based TLS model is not to replace pathology, but to complement it. Although core needle biopsy is routinely required for BC diagnosis, it samples only a limited tumor region and may not fully capture spatially heterogeneous TLS distribution, peritumoral immune organization, or TLS maturation.10–15 In contrast, pretreatment dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) is commonly acquired for local staging and treatment planning in patients considered for NAT. A non-invasive imaging signature may therefore provide whole-lesion and perilesional information before treatment and support early patient stratification.

Previous MRI-based radiomics studies have attempted to infer the BC immune microenvironment, including tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte levels, immune scores, and the spatial distribution of CD8+ T cells.16–18 These studies support the feasibility of using routinely acquired MRI to capture immune-related tumor phenotypes. However, most existing models have focused on global immune infiltration or selected immune cell populations rather than TLS, which are spatially organized lymphoid aggregates with distinct cellular composition, maturation status, and functional relevance. In addition, conventional radiomics approaches often rely on whole-tumor features and may overlook spatial heterogeneity between intratumoral and peritumoral regions. Recent developments in spatially resolved habitat radiomics, which subdivide tumors into imaging-defined subregions that may reflect underlying heterogeneity in perfusion, cellularity, hypoxia, and immune contexture, offer an opportunity to improve predictive performance and interpretability.19 20

Although our previous work suggested the feasibility of imaging-based TLS prediction in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), whether such an approach could be generalized across BC subtypes and linked to deeper biological mechanisms remained unclear.21 To address these gaps, we developed and validated a DCE-MRI-based spatial habitat radiomics machine-learning model, termed shTLS, to predict histologically defined TLS status in BC across diverse subtypes and clinical stages. The model incorporated both intratumoral and peritumoral imaging habitats to capture spatial heterogeneity beyond conventional whole-tumor radiomics.

Finally, we investigated the biological underpinnings of our predictive model through multi-omics and experimental analyses, including pathomics, transcriptomics, immune microenvironment analyses, genomics, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), and in vitro drug-sensitivity assays to interpret the biological differences between shTLS-high and shTLS-low tumors and to explore therapeutically relevant mechanisms. The shTLS model was developed solely from DCE-MRI features, while multi-omics and experimental analyses were performed to provide biological interpretation and validation. Through this integrated framework, this study aimed to link MRI-derived spatial heterogeneity with TLS-associated immune ecosystems and to explore candidate immunoregulatory pathways that may contribute to TLS-deficient tumor states.

Methods

Study design and population

The overall design is illustrated in figure 1. Patients from eight Chinese institutions with histologically confirmed invasive BC, AJCC 8th edition stage T1-4N0-3M0, who received NAT followed by surgery were included (figure 2A,B).22 Exclusion criteria were stage IV disease, inadequate/missing DCE-MRI, history of BC, or incomplete NAT. To expand generalizability, four public datasets were incorporated: TCGA-BRCA (n=137 with DCE-MRI and pathology), DUKE (January 2000 to March 2014) and UCSF cohorts (January 2000 to March 2014), and the I-SPY2 trial (May 2010 to November 2016) (figure 2A,B). Patients received anthracycline/taxane-based NAT; HER2+ tumors also received trastuzumab or dual blockade. In I-SPY2, paclitaxel was combined with investigational agents. Sample size, training, and validation cohorts

Detailed information on sample size estimation is provided in online supplemental methods 1. Based on the sample size estimation, at least 30 patients were required for the final analysis. Ultimately, the training cohort comprised 181 patients from Yunnan Cancer Hospital (YNCH), Fujian Provincial Hospital (FJPH), and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (SHFJ). TCGA-BRCA (n=137) served as external validation. Additional validation was performed in a multicenter Chinese NAT cohort (n=739) and three international cohorts (DUKE n=303, I-SPY2 n=984, UCSF n=60), representing diverse racial and clinical backgrounds.

Outcome evaluation

Treatment response was defined by Miller-Payne (MP) grades 4–5 or Residual Cancer Burden (RCB) 0–I as responders, and MP grades 1–3 or RCB II–III as non-responders. In the I-SPY2 trial, response was defined as pathological complete response (pCR) (responders) versus non-pCR (non-responders), since RCB and MP scores were not reported. Disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) were measured from surgery to event or last follow-up. Detailed outcome evaluation is provided in online supplemental methods 1.

Histopathological evaluation of TLS on H&E slides

H&E-stained whole-slide images (WSIs) were obtained from TCGA-BRCA and three Chinese centers (YNCH, SHFJ, FJPH). TLS presence was assessed by two breast pathologists (>5 years’ experience) blinded to imaging, defined according to consensus criteria, and annotated as present (1) or absent (0) (figure 2H). Discrepancies were resolved by review. TLS was considered positive when at least one discrete, well-demarcated lymphoid aggregate with nodular or lymphoid tissue-like organization was identified within the tumor-associated stroma or peritumoral invasive margin. Diffuse lymphocytic infiltration, scattered inflammatory cells, small non-organized perivascular lymphocytic clusters, necrosis-associated inflammation, and lymphoid tissue unrelated to the tumor were not counted as TLS. Detailed pathological assessment criteria are provided in online supplemental methods 2.

DCE-MRI acquisition and preprocessing procedures

Preoperative DCE-MRI scans were retrieved from institutional PACS and The Cancer Imaging Archive. Standardized preprocessing included motion correction via non-linear symmetric normalization registration, N4 bias correction, resampling to 1 mm3 isotropic voxels, and histogram standardization.23 24 Super-resolution reconstruction was performed on the Onekey AI platform. Detailed DCE-MRI acquisition protocols, image quality assessment, and standardized image preprocessing are provided in online supplemental methods 3.

Traditional and spatial habitat radiomics workflow

Peak-phase DCE-MRI sequences were used for analysis. Two breast radiologists with 5 and 10 years of experience, blinded to clinicopathological data, manually delineated intratumoral regions of interest (ROIs) (L1) using ITK-SNAP (V.4.0.1; http://www.itksnap.org) (figure 2I). Based on L1, the 0–5 mm peritumoral region was automatically segmented using the Onekey AI platform (L2). An image-fusion strategy was then applied to integrate intratumoral and peritumoral features, generating a composite ROI (L) that captured both tumor-intrinsic heterogeneity and microenvironmental context (figure 2I). To characterize spatial heterogeneity, habitat radiomics was performed on L1, L2, and L. Each ROI was subdivided into superpixels using a SLIC-based algorithm, and voxel-wise DCE-MRI perfusion time-series were clustered using K-means. The resulting clusters were mapped back to MRI space to generate interpretable habitat maps for intratumor, peritumor, and fused-tumor regions.25 Detailed segmentation procedures are described in online supplemental methods 4. Radiomic features were extracted with PyRadiomics (https://github.com/Radiomics/pyradiomics). Habitat-based features were computed for each subregion within L1, L2, and L, while conventional radiomic features were extracted from the whole ROIs. Detailed descriptions of radiomic feature extraction, normalization, harmonization, and feature selection are provided in online supplemental methods 5. The reproducibility of the final selected radiomic features was assessed by intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis. All training cases and a random 25% sample from the NAT response validation cohorts were included. ROI segmentation was repeated for these cases, and interobserver and intraobserver ICCs were calculated. ICC values >0.75 were considered acceptable.

This workflow represents a methodological innovation by integrating intratumoral and peritumoral spatial habitat radiomics with traditional radiomics, enabling a more comprehensive characterization of tumor heterogeneity and its microenvironment than previously reported.

Model development and validation

Predictive modeling was performed using scikit-learn (V.0.19.2) in Python V.3.7. Details of the training and independent external cohorts are provided in figure 2B and described in the Sample size, training, and validation cohorts section. Nine feature sets were benchmarked across five machine learning algorithms (XGBoost, ExtraTrees, LightGBM, MLP, random forest): HL1, HL2, HL, HL1+HL2, TL1, TL2, TL, TL1+TL2, and HL1+HL2+TL1+TL2. This design compared traditional intratumoral/peritumoral radiomics with spatial habitat features to capture both global descriptors and localized heterogeneity. Model performance was evaluated through repeated cross-validation in the training set and independent testing in validation cohorts. Model evaluation and statistical analysis are detailed in online supplemental methods 6. The final model was determined by comprehensively comparing performance across all feature sets and algorithms and was termed the spatial-heterogeneity-based TLS model (shTLS model).

Model interpretation

Model interpretability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). SHAP values quantified each feature’s contribution to TLS prediction, allowing ranking of influential radiomic descriptors.26–28

Multi-omics analysis of shTLS-high and shTLS-low groups

The shTLS score was defined as the model-derived probability of histological TLS positivity output by the final trained shTLS model, ranging from 0 to 1. The cut-off for defining shTLS-high and shTLS-low tumors was determined in the training cohort by maximizing the Youden index on ROC analysis and was applied unchanged to all validation cohorts and downstream analyses whenever dichotomization was required. No cohort-specific recalibration or threshold optimization was performed. Multiple-testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method where applicable. Detailed descriptions of multi-omics analysis are provided in online supplemental methods 7.

Bulk transcriptomics and differential analysis

RNA-seq data from the I-SPY2 cohort (GSE194040) were analyzed to compare gene expression profiles between shTLS-high and shTLS-low groups.29 Differential expression analysis was performed using a linear-model framework suitable for processed normalized mRNA expression data. Genes with an adjusted p value of <0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change >0.5 were defined as differentially expressed genes. Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, and gene set enrichment analysis, were performed to characterize biological pathways associated with shTLS stratification. Tumor microenvironment (TME) differences were further evaluated using ESTIMATE scores and CIBERSORTx immune deconvolution.

Genomic variant analysis

Somatic mutation data from TCGA-BRCA were analyzed to assess differences in mutation burden, recurrent gene alterations, mutational spectra, and oncogenic pathway alterations between shTLS-high and shTLS-low groups. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was calculated, and mutation frequencies were compared between groups with FDR correction where applicable.

Pathomics feature analysis

Pathomic features were extracted from available pathological images in the TCGA-BRCA, YNCH, FJPH, and SHFJ cohorts. H&E-stained pathological images were processed into representative patches, from which quantitative features were extracted using CellProfiler (V.4.2.7).30 31 Pathomics feature extraction and selection were provided in online supplemental methods 8. Correlations between radiomic and pathomic features were assessed, and differences in pathomic signatures between shTLS-high and shTLS-low groups were identified. These analyses provided a morphological link between imaging-derived heterogeneity and tissue-level characteristics.

Multiplex immunofluorescence analysis

To further validate TLS-related biology, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections. Tissue sections from 27 patients with TLS confirmed by H&E staining were stained with antibodies against CD20, CD21, and CD23 to classify TLS by maturation stage: early TLS (eTLS, CD20+ CD21− CD23−), primary follicle-like TLS (pTLS, CD20+ CD21+ CD23−), and secondary follicle-like TLS (sTLS, CD20+ CD21+ CD23+) with germinal centers. In parallel, to examine TME differences between shTLS-high and shTLS-low groups, a separate mIF panel (CD4, PD-1, CD138, CD11c, pan-CK, and DAPI) was applied. The detailed experimental procedures are provided in online supplemental methods 9.

Therapeutic target screening

To identify therapeutically relevant genes underlying the imaging-derived score, we analyzed differential expression in the I-SPY2 cohort (GSE194040).29 Genes upregulated in the shTLS-low group, tumors characterized by poor response and reduced immune infiltration, were further contrasted with adjacent normal tissues to refine candidates. Integrating immune context and druggability evidence, indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) emerged as the most compelling target, given its robust overexpression, established role in immunosuppression within the TME, and availability of clinical inhibitors. IDO1 expression was examined in four independent GEO datasets (GSE115577, GSE1456, GSE21653, and GSE25055), where its association with survival was consistently observed.32–35 In addition, pan-cancer expression profiling of IDO1 was performed, comparing BC and other tumor types with corresponding normal tissues. IDO1 expression was further evaluated by immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining comparing TLS-positive versus TLS-negative and shTLS-high versus shTLS-low groups (online supplemental methods 9). To characterize genomic correlates of IDO1, we used TCGA-BRCA data to assess somatic mutations and copy number variations (CNV). Immune correlates were investigated by integrating IDO1 expression levels with immune cell fractions estimated by CIBERSORTx and further validated using the TIMER2.0 platform.

The scRNA-seq dataset (GSE246613) was obtained from the GEO dataset.36 Processed matrices were analyzed in Seurat (V.4.4): normalization (LogNormalize), identification of variable features (vst), principal component analysis, and uniform manifold approximation and projection.37 38 Cell clusters were annotated using the SingleR package, followed by manual refinement based on the top differentially expressed marker genes. Detailed subgroup analyses were subsequently conducted for each cluster. Co-localization analysis of IDO1 and migratory DC markers (LAMP3; CCR7) was performed on FFPE tumor tissues using mIF. The detailed experimental procedures are provided in online supplemental methods 9.

Cell viability assays were performed in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 BC cell lines to evaluate the effect of IDO1 inhibition on chemotherapy and CDK4/6 inhibitor sensitivity. Cells were treated with paclitaxel or dalpiciclib, with or without an IDO1 inhibitor, across a series of concentrations. Cell viability was quantified using CCK-8 assays, and IC₅₀ values were calculated from dose–response curves. All experiments were conducted in biological triplicate with multiple technical replicates. Details regarding cell culture conditions, drug preparation, staining procedures, and data acquisition are provided in online supplemental methods 10.

Results

Association of TLS with treatment response and prognosis

We first evaluated TLS as a biomarker of prognosis and therapeutic response. In the METABRIC cohort, TLS scores, calculated using the 12-chemokine (12-CK) signature, were significantly associated with OS in multiple subtypes (figure 2C–E).39 Patients with ER−/HER2− (p=0.0054) and ER+/HER2− (p=0.0021) tumors in the TLS-high group achieved superior survival compared with TLS-low cases, while HER2+ patients showed a similar but non-significant trend (p=0.055) (figure 2C–E). These findings were independently validated in Chinese cohorts (YNCH, SHFJ), where TLS-positive patients showed prolonged DFS (p=0.037; figure 2F).

Regarding therapeutic response, TLS-positive patients across YNCH, SHFJ, and FJPH cohorts demonstrated significantly higher response rates to NAT compared with TLS-negative cases (p<0.001; figure 2G). Collectively, these results establish TLS as a clinically meaningful biomarker, conferring both survival and therapeutic benefits across populations.

Patient characteristics

Building on these observations, we assembled a multicenter training cohort and four independent external cohorts (see the Sample size, training, and validation cohorts section and online supplemental table 1) to develop and validate a non-invasive TLS prediction model. Baseline characteristics, including age, Ki-67 index, histological grade, molecular subtype, and clinical T stage, were generally well balanced across the cohorts, though minor differences in menopausal status, subtype composition, and nodal stage were noted between centers (online supplemental table 1). These variables were addressed in subsequent stratified and subgroup analyses to minimize potential confounding. A detailed summary of the baseline characteristics for both training and validation cohorts is provided in online supplemental table 2.

Development and validation of the TLS prediction model

We benchmarked nine feature sets across five machine-learning algorithms (see the Model development and validation section). Overall, the combined HL1+HL2+TL1+TL2 feature set showed the most robust performance across cohorts compared with models based on either habitat-only or traditional radiomic features, supporting the added value of integrating intratumoral and peritumoral spatial information. Interobserver and intraobserver ICCs for all radiomic features ranged from 0.79 to 0.85 and from 0.88 to 0.89, respectively, with all values exceeding 0.75. These results indicated good feature reproducibility and are provided in online supplemental data 1. Using LASSO logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation, dimensionality reduction was performed, and the λ tuning parameter was optimized to eliminate non-informative variables (online supplemental figure 1A,B). Ultimately, 20 radiomic features with non-zero coefficients were selected, none of which showed significant collinearity, thereby confirming their independent contribution to the model (figure 3A; online supplemental figure 1C).

In the training cohort, the final shTLS model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.864, with a sensitivity of 0.788, specificity of 0.876, and accuracy of 0.851. Its performance remained robust in the independent TLS validation cohort, with an AUC of 0.825, supporting the generalizability of the DCE-MRI-based TLS prediction model (figure 3B; table 1).

The shTLS model was further applied to independent NAT cohorts to assess its association with treatment response, showing consistent performance in the multicenter Chinese cohort (AUC=0.810), DUKE (AUC=0.859), I-SPY2 (AUC=0.893), and UCSF (AUC=0.905) (figure 3C). These results indicate strong reproducibility across both Asian and Western populations. The model retained stable predictive ability across molecular subtypes, with AUC values of 0.878 in HR+/HER2−, 0.913 in HER2+, and 0.870 in TNBC (figure 3D). Additional subgroup analyses based on age, menopausal status, Ki-67 expression, MammaPrint gene status, cT/cN stage, molecular subtypes, and treatment regimens demonstrated consistently high AUC values across strata (online supplemental figure 2A–I), underscoring the robustness of the model across diverse clinical contexts. Across training and all external cohorts, decision-curve analysis showed consistent net benefit over treat-all/none strategies, and calibration closely followed the ideal line (figure 3E–J and O,P; online supplemental figure 2J–M).

Clinical implications of the shTLS score in survival and treatment response

A strong correlation was observed between the shTLS score and TLS presence. Patients in the shTLS-high group had a significantly higher proportion of TLS-positive pathology compared with those in the shTLS-low group (p<0.001, online supplemental Figure 3S). Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analysis confirmed that the shTLS score was an independent predictor of TLS status (table 2). Other important clinicopathological variables, such as age, tumor subtype, and clinical T/N stage, did not retain consistent predictive value in multivariate analysis, underscoring that the shTLS score is the major determinant of TLS presence in this study population.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that a high shTLS score was significantly associated with improved OS and DFS across the entire BC cohort. Patients in the shTLS-high group had markedly longer OS and DFS compared with the shTLS-low group (HR, 0.39 (95% CI 0.19 to 0.83), p=0.014; HR, 0.57 (95% CI 0.35 to 0.93), p=0.026 (figure 3K–N)). Subgroup analyses further supported the robustness of this association. The prognostic benefit of a high shTLS score was consistently observed across different clinical subsets, including younger patients (≤65 years), premenopausal women, early-stage tumors (cT1–2), and advanced nodal status (cN2–3), where significant improvements in OS and DFS were noted (online supplemental figure 3). In contrast, the association was less pronounced in older or postmenopausal patients, possibly due to smaller sample sizes and competing mortality risks. Univariate and multivariable Cox regression identified the shTLS score as a significant predictor of both OS and DFS (figure 3Q,R). After adjusting for age, menopausal status, clinical stage, and molecular subtype in multivariate models, the shTLS score remained an independent prognostic factor, with high-score patients exhibiting reduced risk of recurrence and death (online supplemental tables 4–7).

The shTLS score was strongly associated with NAT response across subtypes and clinical subgroups (figure 4). In HR+/HER2− tumors, response rates were markedly higher in high-score patients (paclitaxel+pembrolizumab: 75.0% vs 0%; anthracycline/taxane: 57.9% vs 8.3%) (figure 4K). In HER2+ disease, the most striking difference was observed with T-DM1+ pertuzumab (92.9% vs 17.0%), while in TNBC, high scores predicted superior benefit with paclitaxel+pembrolizumab (87.5% vs 38.5%) and carboplatin-containing regimens (85.7% vs 17.7%) (figure 4L,M). This predictive association was consistent across tumor and nodal stages as well as MammaPrint risk categories, indicating independence from baseline clinicopathological or genomic factors. Logistic regression confirmed the shTLS score as an independent predictor of NAT response (p<0.001), whereas conventional variables showed limited significance (table 3). Baseline clinicopathological characteristics were otherwise broadly comparable between shTLS-high and shTLS-low groups, except for molecular subtype distribution (table 4).

Multi-omics differences between shTLS-high and shTLS-low groups

Transcriptomic differences

Differential expression analysis revealed distinct transcriptomic programs between shTLS-high and shTLS-low tumors (figure 5D). shTLS-low tumors showed higher expression of selected chemokine, inflammatory, and immunoregulatory genes, including CXCL13, GBP5, GRB7, IDO1, SOX11, and members of the S100 family (S100A7, S100A8, and S100A9). By contrast, genes related to epithelial differentiation and hormone signaling, such as SCUBE2, RERG, FOXA1, AGR2, and MSMB, were relatively downregulated in shTLS-low tumors and showed higher expression in shTLS-high tumors. Functional enrichment analyses further highlighted cytokine/chemokine signaling, IL-17-related pathways, interferon and inflammatory responses, and epithelial/hormone-related programs as major transcriptomic differences between the two groups (figure 5E–G; online supplemental figure 4A,B). Together, these findings suggest that shTLS-low tumors are characterized by a TLS-deficient transcriptomic state with enhanced inflammatory and immunoregulatory signaling, including IDO1 upregulation, while epithelial and hormone-related genes were relatively reduced in this group.

Genomic single nucleotide variants landscape and subgroup differences

In the TCGA-BRCA cohort, the overall single nucleotide variant (SNV) landscape was dominated by single-nucleotide substitutions, with missense variants as the major mutation class and PIK3CA and TP53 as the most frequently altered genes (figure 5L; online supplemental figure 4C,D). When stratified by the shTLS score, shTLS-high and shTLS-low tumors shared common BC driver alterations, including TP53 and PIK3CA, but showed modest differences in several recurrently altered genes, such as CDH1, MAP2K4/MAP3K1, and NCOR1 in the shTLS-high group, and GATA3, KMT2C, MUC16, HMCN1, and FLG in the shTLS-low group (figure 5M,N). At the pathway level, alterations in RTK-RAS, PI3K, TP53, NOTCH, Hippo, and WNT signaling were broadly comparable between groups (figure 5O). These findings suggest that shTLS-defined immune phenotypes are not primarily explained by distinct driver pathway burdens, but may instead reflect differences in how shared oncogenic backgrounds interact with the tumor immune microenvironment.

Pathomic feature differences

To further explore the tissue-level correlates of the shTLS model, we compared pathomic features between shTLS-high and shTLS-low tumors. Quantitative features related to nuclear, cytoplasmic, and hematoxylin-based texture and morphology differed between the two groups, suggesting that the shTLS score captured microscopic morphological heterogeneity in addition to MRI-derived spatial heterogeneity (figure 5P). Correlation analysis further showed that these pathomic features were largely non-redundant, supporting their complementary contribution to the histopathological characterization of shTLS-defined tumors (figure 5Q).

TME differences

To further elucidate the biological underpinnings of the imaging-derived score, we systematically assessed the TME. shTLS-high tumors displayed significantly elevated Immune Scores and ESTIMATE scores compared with shTLS-low tumors (both p<0.001), indicative of enhanced immune infiltration within the TME (figure 5H,I). In line with this, tumor purity was markedly reduced in the shTLS-high group (p<0.001; figure 5J), reflecting increased immune-stromal content. Stromal Scores were modestly higher but did not reach statistical significance (figure 5K), suggesting that the primary difference between groups was immune-driven rather than stromal. shTLS-high tumors harbored greater proportions of M1 macrophages, activated CD4+ memory T cells, follicular helper T cells, and activated dendritic cells (DCs), whereas shTLS-low tumors were enriched in resting CD4+ memory T cells, plasma cells, and resting mast cells (all p<0.05; online supplemental figure 5A). Correlation analysis further demonstrated that effector T-cell subsets positively correlated with each other but negatively correlated with immunosuppressive cell types, underscoring the immune-active phenotype of shTLS-high tumors (online supplemental figure 5B).

To assess the association between the model score and TLS maturation, we examined TLS-positive specimens using mIF (figure 6A–D). The shTLS-score increased progressively from eTLS to pTLS and reached the highest levels in sTLS with germinal centers (figure 6E), indicating a positive correlation between the score and TLS maturation. These findings suggest that the shTLS-score not only identifies TLS presence but also captures their immunological maturity.

We further used mIF to investigate differences between the shTLS-high and shTLS-low groups in intratumoral and peritumoral immune cell density, spatial distribution, and intercellular distances. Within the intratumoral areas (figure 6G), shTLS-high tumors had significantly higher densities of CD11c+ DCs and CD138+ plasma cells than shTLS-low tumors, with CD4+ T cells showing a similar tendency toward higher abundance. In contrast, the fraction of CD4+ PD-1+ cells among total CD4+ T cells was markedly lower in the shTLS-high group. The overall density of PD-1+ cells did not differ substantially between groups, suggesting that the key distinction lies in the spatial distribution and relative representation of PD-1-expressing CD4+ T cells rather than in the absolute abundance of PD-1+ cells (figure 6G, online supplemental figure 5C).

In the peritumoral areas (figure 6H), CD4+, CD11c+, and CD138+ cell densities remained higher in shTLS-high than in shTLS-low tumors, although absolute levels were lower than in intratumoral areas, yielding a “center-high, periphery-intermediate” pattern. Mirroring the intratumoral findings, the proportion of CD4+ PD-1+ cells among CD4+ T cells was consistently lower in the shTLS-high group (online supplemental figure 5D).

Radial density analyses (figure 6I) further resolved these patterns. In shTLS-high tumors, CD4+ T cells, CD11c+ DCs, and CD138+ plasma cells peaked within the tumor core and gradually declined across the tumor-stroma interface, indicating genuine penetration into tumor nests followed by a decaying gradient toward the surrounding stroma. In shTLS-low tumors, CD11c+ and CD4+ cells were globally reduced and dropped to near-zero levels shortly after crossing the tumor boundary, consistent with a sharp exclusion barrier. Notably, CD138+ plasma cells and CD4+ PD-1+ cells in shTLS-low tumors showed a distinct peritumoral peak: their densities were low inside the tumor but increased in a 50–100 µm stromal rim before declining again, whereas in shTLS-high tumors these populations displayed a monotonic decrease from tumor interior to stroma. This indicates that key effector and exhausted populations are trapped at the periphery in shTLS-low tumors rather than engaging the tumor parenchyma.

Neighborhood analyses (figure 6J) corroborated these observations at the single-cell level. In shTLS-high tumors, the average distance between CD4+ T cells and panCK+ tumor cells, as well as between CD138+ plasma cells and tumor cells, was significantly shorter than in shTLS-low tumors, supporting a more intimate immune-tumor interface. By contrast, the distances between CD4+ T cells and CD11c+ DCs or CD138+ plasma cells were comparable across groups, implying that antigen-presenting and helper niches exist in both settings, but only in shTLS-high tumors do these niches form within the tumor nests.

Identification of IDO1 as a potential therapeutic target

Building on the transcriptomic evidence that immune-modulatory genes were differentially expressed between score groups, we next sought to identify tractable therapeutic targets. We first compared gene expression between BC tissues and adjacent normal controls. Both unpaired and paired analyses identified a panel of consistently upregulated genes in tumor samples, including CXCL13, GBP5, GRB7, SOX11, MMP1, and IDO1 (online supplemental figure 5E,F). Among these, IDO1 drew particular attention given its well-documented role in tumor immune evasion through tryptophan metabolism and kynurenine pathway activation.40–43 Importantly, stratification by the shTLS score revealed that IDO1 expression was significantly enriched in the shTLS-low group compared with the shTLS-high group (p<0.001; online supplemental figure 5G). IDO1 upregulation may contribute to the TLS-deficient, inflammatory, and immunosuppressive phenotype associated with poorer therapeutic responsiveness in shTLS-low tumors. Taken together with prior evidence that pharmacological inhibition of IDO1 can restore antitumor T-cell activity, these results nominate IDO1 as a potential therapeutic target in BC, particularly for patients in the shTLS-low subgroup.

Multi-omics and experimental validation of IDO1

Prognostic impact and pan-cancer expression of IDO1

Across four GEO cohorts (GSE115577, GSE1456, GSE21653, and GSE25055), high IDO1 expression was consistently associated with worse survival outcomes. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated significantly reduced DFS/RFS in the IDO1-high groups (all p<0.05; online supplemental figure 5H-K). In addition, pan-cancer evaluation revealed widespread IDO1 overexpression in multiple tumor types, including breast, gastric, and renal cancers (all p<0.001; online supplemental figure 6A). These findings extend the clinical relevance of IDO1 beyond BC, underscoring its potential as a broadly applicable therapeutic target.

Genomic alterations associated with IDO1 expression

Genomic profiling further showed that IDO1 expression was associated with distinct genomic and epigenomic features in TCGA-BRCA. Although common BC driver alterations, including TP53, PIK3CA, CDH1, GATA3, and MAP3K1, were observed across both IDO1-high and IDO1-low tumors, IDO1-high tumors showed a higher frequency of TP53 alterations and a higher overall mutational burden (figure 7A,B; online supplemental figure 6G). Consistently, IDO1-high tumors exhibited increased TMB, APOBEC enrichment scores, and TCW motif burden, suggesting a potential association with mutational processes and genomic instability (online supplemental figure 6D–F). Additional pathway-level alterations, CNV patterns, and methylation differences are summarized in online supplemental figure 6H–M. Overall, these findings suggest that IDO1-high tumors are associated with broader genomic and epigenomic remodeling rather than a single dominant driver alteration.

TME correlates of IDO1 expression

We next examined the immune contexture associated with IDO1 expression. Immune deconvolution showed that IDO1-high tumors were characterized by increased infiltration of several immune populations, including activated CD4+ memory T cells, follicular helper T cells, Tregs, γδ T cells, and activated DCs, together with remodeling of the macrophage compartment (online supplemental figure 6B,C). These findings indicate that IDO1-high tumors are not simply immune-cold, but instead display a mixed immune state characterized by concurrent immune activation and immunosuppressive elements. This pattern is consistent with the shTLS-low/IDO1-high phenotype, in which TLS deficiency coexists with dysregulated inflammatory and immunoregulatory signaling and poorer therapeutic response. Together, these data support IDO1 as a marker of immune remodeling and a candidate pathway for future therapeutic exploration.

scRNA-seq and mIF-based validation

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling revealed that IDO1 expression was highly enriched within the myeloid compartment, with negligible expression in lymphoid (B, T) or mast cell populations (figure 7C–E; online supplemental figure 6N,O; online supplemental data 2; online supplemental data 3). Among myeloid subsets, migratory DCs exhibited the highest IDO1 expression (figure 7F–J). Importantly, correlation analysis from the TIMER2.0 platform confirmed a strong positive association between IDO1 expression and activated myeloid DC infiltration, supporting the robustness of these findings across analytic frameworks (online supplemental figure 6P). Clinically, non-responders had higher levels of IDO1 in migratory DCs than responders, suggesting that IDO1 enrichment in this cell subset is associated with inferior therapeutic outcomes (figure 7K). This observation is consistent with the imaging-based low-score group, which also showed poor treatment response, thereby providing a potential biological link for the prognostic value of IDO1.

To further validate whether IDO1 expression on migratory DCs is associated with the response to NAT, we performed clinical validation using mIF. mIF further demonstrated strong colocalization of IDO1 with LAMP3+CCR7+ migratory DCs (mregDCs), and IDO1-high mregDCs were more abundant in non-responders than in responders (figure 8A–C). These findings suggest a cellular correlate for the reduced therapeutic response associated with the shTLS-low group.

Functional evaluation of IDO1 inhibition

Consistent with the model-derived predictions, IHC analysis demonstrated a clear inverse relationship between TLS presence and IDO1 expression. Tumors with low shTLS scores were predominantly TLS-negative and exhibited significantly higher IDO1 staining intensity, whereas TLS-positive and shTLS-high tumors showed markedly reduced IDO1 expression (p<0.05; figure 8D,E). This concordance establishes a biologically coherent link between the imaging-based model output and underlying immune phenotypes, indicating that lower shTLS scores correspond to TLS-deficient tumors characterized by IDO1 enrichment and immunosuppressive inflammatory signaling.

To further evaluate the translational relevance of IDO1 as a therapeutic target, we performed in vitro drug sensitivity assays in two BC cell lines. In MDA-MB-231 cells, co-treatment with the IDO1 inhibitor enhanced the cytotoxic effect of paclitaxel (figure 8F). The IC50 of paclitaxel alone was 17.8 nM, whereas the addition of the IDO1 inhibitor reduced the IC50 to 12.92 nM, indicating increased chemosensitivity. A similar potentiation effect was observed in MCF-7 cells: the IC50 of dalpiciclib monotherapy was 21.6 µM, which decreased to 15.36 µM on combination with the IDO1 inhibitor (figure 8G). These results suggest that pharmacological IDO1 inhibition may enhance the efficacy of both chemotherapy (paclitaxel) and CDK4/6 inhibition across distinct BC subtypes, supporting the potential involvement of IDO1 in therapeutic resistance.

Representative feature visualization across score groups

Representative visualizations were used to illustrate imaging-pathology correspondence across shTLS score groups. The shTLS-high/TLS-positive responder showed more heterogeneous intratumoral and peritumoral MRI activation patterns and immune-enriched stromal-tumor interfaces on WSI patches, whereas the shTLS-low/TLS-negative non-responder showed more spatially uniform MRI activation and fewer immune-enriched pathological regions (figure 8H). Longitudinal examples further illustrated early radiological regression in a shTLS-high patient who achieved pCR, in contrast to persistent residual disease in a shTLS-low patient after NAT (figure 8I).

Discussion

This study developed and validated a DCE-MRI-based spatial habitat radiomics score, termed shTLS, for the non-invasive assessment of TLS-related immune phenotypes in BC. Clinically, shTLS is envisioned primarily as a pretreatment stratification tool rather than a replacement for histopathological TLS assessment. Because DCE-MRI is routinely acquired before NAT, shTLS may complement biopsy-based evaluation by capturing whole-lesion and perilesional heterogeneity, thereby helping distinguish immune-inflamed, TLS-enriched tumors from TLS-deficient, immunosuppressive tumors. This stratification may support prediction of NAT response and survival outcomes, while identifying patients who may require closer monitoring, additional molecular evaluation, or consideration for future combination-treatment strategies. Beyond prediction, multi-omics, spatial immune profiling, scRNA-seq, and functional assays linked shTLS-low tumors to a program involving IDO1-associated migratory DCs, providing biological insight into the imaging-derived score and a rationale for therapeutic exploration.

After the development and validation of shTLS as an imaging-derived score for histologically defined TLS status, we further evaluated whether the fixed shTLS score was associated with NAT response and survival outcomes. In these subsequent clinical relevance analyses, the shTLS score demonstrated consistent associations across different clinical stages, molecular subtypes, and therapeutic contexts, supporting its potential robustness in real-world settings. Unlike conventional markers that are often restricted to specific subtypes or regimens, the shTLS score retained stratification performance in patients receiving diverse neoadjuvant therapies, highlighting its potential as a broadly applicable, non-invasive tool for pretreatment patient stratification.44–46 Notably, shTLS-high patients with HR+/HER2- BC and TNBC showed higher observed response rates within immunotherapy-containing regimens. This observation aligns with recent pivotal trials such as KEYNOTE-756 and KEYNOTE-522, which demonstrated that pembrolizumab significantly increased pCR rates in selected patient populations.47 48 In KEYNOTE-756, the addition of pembrolizumab to chemotherapy achieved a pCR rate of 24.3% in HR+/HER2− BC, and further stratification showed that patients with programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) CPS ≥1 and ER positivity <10% achieved the highest pCR rate of 57.6%. In our cohort, shTLS-high patients achieved a response rate of 75%. A similar pattern was observed in TNBC: in the PD-L1 positive subgroup of KEYNOTE-522, the reported pCR rate was 68.9%, whereas the shTLS-high subgroup in our cohort reached a response rate of 87.5%. Although PD-L1 has been widely adopted as a biomarker for immunotherapy, multiple studies have shown that it does not consistently distinguish responders from non-responders, and its assessment by IHC is technically challenging and subject to variability.46 49 50 In this context, shTLS may provide complementary, non-invasive, and biologically interpretable information by capturing whole-lesion and perilesional TLS-related immune organization. Therefore, rather than replacing established biomarkers, the shTLS score may serve as an additional stratification tool for patient selection and biomarker-driven trial design, pending further prospective validation.

Mechanistically, our data suggest that the shTLS score does not merely capture the bulk presence of TLS, but encodes a whole immune ecological state that spans spatial architecture, cellular composition, and metabolic regulation. TLS-high tumors defined by a high shTLS-score showed dense intratumoral aggregation of CD4+ T cells, CD11c+ DCs, and CD138+ plasma cells, with radial density curves peaking within the tumor nests and rapidly declining toward the stroma, and with CD4+ PD-1+ cells representing only a minority of the CD4 compartment. This configuration is highly reminiscent of “mature, productive TLS,” where B cells, T follicular helper cells, and activated DCs co-localize in germinal center-like niches and support effective cytotoxic responses.5 6 51 BC specific studies have similarly shown that intratumoral or peritumoral TLS with abundant helper T cells and plasma cells associate with improved survival and chemosensitivity.52 53 The shTLS-high pattern most closely resembles an “inflamed-permissive” state, in which immune cells can physically enter the tumor nests, show a lower relative representation of CD4+ PD-1+ and remain in close proximity to panCK+ cancer cells, as reflected by shorter CD4+ cells to tumor and CD138+ cells to tumor distances. These spatial data complement the transcriptomic findings by showing that the shTLS score reflects qualitative differences in TLS-associated immune organization rather than simply measuring overall lymphocyte abundance. Specifically, shTLS-high tumors exhibited more organized immune infiltration and closer immune-tumor interactions.

By contrast, shTLS-low tumors appear to represent a TLS-deficient, IDO1-associated immunoregulatory niche, in which immune-related signals are present but fail to form organized TLS-associated antitumor immune architecture. In these tumors, immune infiltrates are quantitatively reduced, skewed toward the tumor margin and peritumoral stroma, and disproportionately enriched for CD4+ PD-1+ cells, suggestive of altered CD4+ T-cell states or dysfunctional immune organization. At the same time, we observed a marked upregulation of IDO1 and its concentration within mregDCs, a population increasingly recognized as a key immunoregulatory hub capable of licensing T-cell exhaustion and Treg expansion in multiple cancers.54 55 This pattern matches a broader body of work showing that tryptophan catabolism through IDO1 and related enzymes depletes local tryptophan, generates kynurenine metabolites, engages the AhR pathway, and drives T-cell anergy, Treg differentiation, and myeloid reprogramming.56–58 Taken together, these findings suggest a possible immuno-ecological pattern in shTLS-low tumors, characterized by TLS paucity, accumulation of mregDCs at the tumor-stroma interface, IDO1-associated metabolic immunosuppression, enrichment of CD4+ PD-1+ T cells, and reduced immune engagement with tumor nests.

High IDO1 expression in mregDC-rich regions was associated with poorer response to NAT, and pharmacological IDO1 inhibition further sensitized BC cells to taxanes and CDK4/6 inhibition in vitro. These findings suggest that the mregDC-IDO1 axis may be linked to therapeutic resistance. Together with the negative results of unselected IDO1 pathway inhibitor trials in BC or melanoma, which have been attributed in part to the lack of robust stratification biomarkers,59 60 our findings suggest that an imaging-defined shTLS-low or IDO1-high phenotype may represent a rational target population for revisiting IDO1-directed combinations. In summary, the shTLS-score connects radiomic spatial heterogeneity to a TLS-centered immune architecture and an IDO1-centered metabolic immunoregulatory program, outlining an integrated immune ecological pattern that may help explain why some “hot-looking” tumors mount productive antitumor immunity while others remain clinically refractory despite immune infiltration.

In summary, our study demonstrates that the shTLS model shows robust predictive performance and provides biological insight into TLS-related immune organization in BC. By integrating spatial habitat radiomics with multi-omics profiling and functional validation, the findings support the potential value of shTLS as a non-invasive imaging-derived marker for pretreatment stratification. The biological analyses further suggest an IDO1-associated immunosuppressive phenotype in TLS-deficient tumors, providing a rationale for future biomarker-guided therapeutic exploration. However, several limitations remain. The bulk transcriptomic analysis was mainly based on the I-SPY2 cohort, and genomic variant analysis was limited to TCGA-BRCA because comparable paired imaging-omic data were not available in the other validation cohorts. Although the biological findings were supported by spatial immune profiling, IHC, mIF, and functional assays, additional paired imaging-transcriptomic/genomic cohorts are needed to confirm these signatures. Moreover, in vivo studies and biomarker-stratified clinical cohorts will be required to clarify the causal and therapeutic relevance of the TLS-mregDC-IDO1 axis.

Conclusion

Our study identifies a radiomic-derived shTLS signature as a robust predictor of therapeutic response and a spatially grounded surrogate for TLS in BC. By integrating spatial multi-omics and spatial mIF, we reveal that shTLS-high tumors are characterized by intratumoral clustering of CD4+ T cells, DCs, and plasma cells, forming a TLS-like immune niche with a lower relative representation of PD-1-expressing CD4+ T cells. In contrast, shTLS-low tumors display peripheral immune exclusion and enrichment of IDO1-expressing migratory DCs (mregDCs), suggesting an IDO1-associated immunoregulatory axis-related T-cell dysfunction and immune escape. These findings provide biological validation of the shTLS score and support its use as a spatially and functionally informed biomarker for predicting treatment response and guiding therapeutic strategies, including IDO1-targeted intervention.
