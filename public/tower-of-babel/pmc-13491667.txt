Background

Aucklandia lappa Decne, also known as Saussurea lappa Clarke or Aucklandia costus Falc., is a perennial herbaceous plant belonging to the Asteraceae family [1]. It is native to India and was introduced to Lijiang in Yunnan Province, China, after the 1940s. Its primary production areas are Lijiang, Shangri-La, and Weixi in Yunnan, with cultivation now also occurring in Sichuan, Hubei, Shanxi, and so on. The roots of A. lappa are used medicinally, primarily containing volatile oils, sesquiterpene compounds and other active constituents. Research indicates that costunolide and dehydrocostus lactone are potential anti-cancer agents [2, 3]. According to the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 Edition, Volume I), A. lappa as a precious medicinal plant, it not only possesses traditional therapeutic effects such as promoting digestion, relieving bloating, regulating qi, alleviating depression, and alleviating pain while stabilising pregnancy, but also demonstrates broad application prospects in fields including anti-inflammatory, anti-ulcer, hepatoprotective, cholagogue, and antitumour activities [4–8].

Mitochondria are essential organelles that play a central role in organismal growth, development, and reproduction by serving as the primary hub of cellular energy metabolism [9–11]. Their evolutionary origin is widely attributed to an ancient endosymbiotic event involving an alpha-proteobacterium [12]. As semi-autonomous organelles, mitochondria harbor their own genome (mtDNA), which, although derived from an endosymbiotic ancestor, has undergone independent evolutionary trajectories relative to the nuclear genome [10, 13, 14]. The mitogenome participates in critical biological processes, including energy production, gene expression regulation, and responses to environmental stresses [9, 11, 14]. The mitogenomes in animal are relatively small (14–20 kb), typically adopting a conserved circular double-stranded structure with highly compact gene organization and minimal intergenic regions (5%-10%) [15]. In contrast, The mitogenomes in plant are substantially larger, ranging from 66 kb to 12 Mb, primarily due to the expansion of introns and intergenic spacers, which collectively account for approximately 72% of the genome [11, 15]. Consequently, mitogenomes in plant can be 100 to 10,000 times larger than mitogenomes in animal and displays markedly greater structural complexity [16, 17]. The molecular architecture of land plant mtDNA is dynamic, with circular, linear, and branched forms coexisting as a result of recombination events mediated by repetitive sequences in intergenic regions. Moreover, shifts in mtDNA conformation have been observed across different developmental stages in plants [18], yet the coding regions of plant mitochondrial genes exhibit remarkably low mutation rates, which significantly lower than those observed in both nuclear genomes and animal mitogenomes [19].

In addition, compared to the highly conserved chloroplast genome, the mitogenome displays greater variability in size, structure, and gene content [20]. The mitogenome exhibits significant size variation across different species [21], primarily due to frequent recombination mediated by abundant dispersed repetitive elements (REs), intron expansion, and gene transfer events [22–24]. For instance, the mitogenome of Stemona tuberosa lour.spans 605,873 bp in length and encodes 66 genes [25], that of Lagenaria siceraria is 357,496 bp with 64 genes [26], and Agropyron cristatum is 381,065 bp containing 52 genes [27].

The findings reveal that plant mitogenomes hold great potential for DNA-based molecular identification of medicinal plants. Consequently, mitogenome sequencing of medicinal species has increasingly become a focus of researchers, and the number of medicinal plants with completed mitogenome sequences continues to grow. For example, the mitogenome of Astragalus membranaceus (Fisch.) Bunge is 398,048 bp in size and contains 54 genes [28], Angelica biserrata is 228,775 bp and 54 genes [29], Panax notoginseng is 661,779 bp and 56 genes [30], Angelica dahurica is 228,315 bp and it encodes 56 genes [31], Polygonatum kingianum is 647,110 bp and 61 genes [32], Fritillaria ussuriensis Maxim. is 737,569 bp and 55 genes [33]. The mitogenome of Arctium lappa, which is closely related to A. lappa, is 312,598 bp and 131 genes, Arctium tomentosum is 312,609 bp and 130 genes [34].

Although the mitogenome of A. lappa has been assembled previously, only a preliminary phylogenetic placement within the Asteraceae has been carried out; a comprehensive characterization of its mitogenome features is still lacking [35]. In this study, we combined second and third generation sequencing technologies to assemble and annotate the complete mitogenome of A. lappa. We then performed an integrated suite of analyses covering structural annotation, codon-usage bias of the 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs), genome-wide identification of RNA-editing sites, MTPT (Mitochondria to Chloroplasts) homology, inter-species synteny across the Asteraceae, and phylogenetic inference. These results not only provide a more comprehensive description of the unique characteristics of the mitogenome of A. lappa, but also provide a theoretical basis for further research on the differences, evolutionary relationships and mitochondrial genetic patterns of the organellar genomes of the Asteraceae.

Materials and methods

Plant material and mitogenomic sequencing

The sample material of A. lappa was collected from Lameirong Village, Ludian Township, Yulong Naxi Autonomous County, Lijiang City, Yunnan Province, China (27°19′N, 99°45′E) (Fig. S1). The plant sample was identified by Professor Tao Aien. The specimen of A. lappa has been deposited in the Herbarium of Traditional Chinese Medicine at Lijiang Culture and Tourism College, with the voucher number: 2401-LJCTC-YXY-0001. The samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and genomic DNA was extracted using the Cetyltrimethylammonium Bromide (CTAB) method [36]. Libraries were prepared using the SQK-LSK109 kit and sequenced on the Nanopore PromethION platform (Nanopore Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA). Data processing was carried out using NanoFilt and NanoPlot from NanoPack25. Concurrently, libraries were prepared from high-quality DNA using the Nextera XT DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), with an average fragment length of 350 bp. These libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system, generating 19.85 GB of Raw base. Following quality control processing with the NGS QC Toolkit v2.3.3, 19.66 GB of Clean base, which exhibited a GC content of 38.17%, with Q20 and Q30 scores reaching 98.86% and 96.97%, respectively.

Assembly and annotation of mitogenome

The third-generation sequencing data were aligned to the reference sequence (A. lappa, MW465116.1) using minimap2 (v2.15-r905). The resulting alignments were subsequently assembled into preliminary contigs using miniasm (v0.3-r179). This initial assembly was further polished with nextPolish (v1.3.1) to improve accuracy. Thereafter, second-generation sequencing reads were mapped to the refined assembly using Bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1). The Illumina short reads were then integrated into the draft contigs via Unicycler (v0.4.8), and the assembly graph was visualized using Bandage [37]. To finalize the assembly, Nanopore long reads were realigned to the contig sequences using minimap2, allowing incorporation of connectivity information from Bandage, validation of inter-contig linkages, and manual curation to resolve ambiguities and complete the mitogenome structure. This iterative process enabled the successful reconstruction of the complete mitogenome of A. lappa. Annotation of the mitogenome was performed using MFANNOT and MITOFY [22, 38]. The circular map of the A. lappa mitogenome was generated with OGDRAW [39]. The assembled mitogenome sequence of A. lappa is publicly accessible from the NCBI GenBank under accession PX590301.

Relative synonymous codon usage and RNA editing analysis

The protein coding sequences (PCGs) were extracted using the default settings of Phylosuit software [40]. The relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) for all mitochondrial coding regions was calculated using MEGA (v7.0 https://www.megasoftware.net/) software. The RSCU profiles produced were visualized interactively using the RSCU-Plot Shiny application (https://pcg-lab.shinyapps.io/RSCU-Plot/) [41–44]. Deepred-Mt software, embedded within the MGA workflow, was employed to predict C-to-U RNA editing sites within protein-coding genes of the Asteraceae mitogenome [45]. All protein-coding gene sequences served as input data, with predictions retaining a score > 0.9 retained for subsequent analysis.

Repetitive sequences analysis

Simple repetitive sequences (SSRs) within the A. lappa mitogenome were identified using the MISA (https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/) software system. The minimum repeat lengths for mononucleotides, dinucleotides, trinucleotides, tetranucleotides, and pentanucleotides were set at 10,5,4,3,and 3, respectively.Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF) v4.09 (https://tandem.bu.edu/trf/trf.advanced.submit.html) software was employed for tandem repeat analysis. Parameters were set as follows: match = 2, mismatch = 7, insertion/deletion (indel) = 7, minimum alignment score = 50, and maximum cycle length = 500. The REPuter web server was employed for the analysis of random element (RE) sequences.

Ka/Ks analysis

To assess the selective pressure acting on mitochondrial genes, the ratio of non-synonymous substitution rate (Ka) to synonymous substitution rate (Ks) of 13 PCGs shared by 20 species of Asteraceae family was calculated using the Ka/Ks_Calculator (v2.0) software [46]. The calculation results were plotted as box plots using Origin 2021 software for presentation.

Homologous fragments and interspecies synteny analysis

The chloroplast genome of A. lappa was assessed using the BLASTN program [38] under default parameters. Additionally, the amino acid sequences derived from the plant’s mitogenome were compared with those of the chloroplast genome to identify potential sequence similarities. The resulting homologous regions were visualized using Circos software [47]. To further investigate evolutionary relationships, Mauve (version 2.3.1) was employed to align sequencing reads against reference mitogenomes. Collinearity block lengths were determined based on BLAST-derived alignments, enabling a more detailed analysis of genomic conservation and divergence across species [48]. Additionally, visualisation analysis of gene transfer events from chloroplasts to mitochondria was performed using Tbtools (v2.056) [49].

Collinear and phylogenetic analysis

To explore the structural conservation of mitogenomes among different species, pairwise comparisons of the genomes were conducted using BLASTN [50]. MCscanX software was employed to identify syntenic blocks between Saussurea lappa and five other species of the Asteraceae family, and a multiple syntenic map was drawn [51]. The conserved syntenic regions were visualized using the Circos package in R v4.2 software. The amino acid sequences of core genes were aligned using MAFFT v7.505 software [52], and the alignment results were trimmed with TrimAl (v1.2rev57) to remove low-quality alignment regions [53]. The maximum likelihood (ML) tree was constructed using IQ-TREE v2.2.0 software [54]. The phylogenetic tree was visualized and beautified through the online website ITOL (https://itol.embl.de/).

Results

Characteristics of mitogenome of A. lappa

Based on HiFi sequencing reads, we successfully assembled the mitogenome of A. lappa. The complete mitogenome is a single circular molecule of 436,648 bp in length with a GC content of 45.06%, as shown in Fig. 1 and 2. Annotation revealed a total of 54 genes, comprising 31 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 19 tRNA genes, and 4 rRNA genes, as listed in Table 1. Among these, 22 are core respiratory genes: five ATP synthase genes (atp1, atp4, atp6, atp8, atp9), four cytochrome c biogenesis genes (ccmB, ccmC, ccmFC, ccmFN), one cytochrome b gene (cob), three cytochrome c oxidase genes (cox1, cox2, cox3), one maturase gene (matR), one protein transport subunit gene (mttB), and seven NADH dehydrogenase genes (nad1, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad6, nad7, nad9). In addition, non-core genes include one succinate dehydrogenase gene (sdh4), two ribosomal large subunit genes (rpl5, rpl10), and five ribosomal small subunit genes (rps3, rps4, rps8, rps12, rps13). Notably, the nad9 gene is present in three copies, while atp8, cox3, sdh4, nad3, ccmB, rpl10, rps12, rps13, rrn18, and rrn5 each occur as two copies. Furthermore, three tRNA genes located within repetitive regions, including trn-P-UGG, trnfM-CAG, and trnS-GCU,which are present in either two or three copies.

Analysis of codon usage bias

The codon usage preference of 31 protein coding genes (PCGs) in mitochondrial DNA of A. lappa was analyzed. The results showed that 13,128 codons were used (Table S1). Except for nad7 and rps3, which start with CTC and TTA, respectively, all other protein-coding genes use ATG as the start codon ; five stop codons were detected: TGA, TAG, CAT, TAA, and GAG. RNA editing from C to U was detected in the stop codons of atp9 and ccmFC, as show in Table 1. The mitogenome contains 64 standard codons, 61 of which encode 20 standard amino acids and the other 3 are stop codons. The most commonly used amino acids are Leucine (leu), Serine (ser) and Arginine (arg), while Methionine (met) and Tryptophan (trp) are the least frequently used. Among the four synonymous codons of Alanine (ala), GCT exhibits the highest RSCU value of 1.494, whereas GCG shows an RSCU of only 0.498, indicating a markedly lower usage frequency. In addition, 32 frequently used codons (RSCU > 1) were identified. Except AGG (Arginine, Arg), ACC (Threonine, Thr), TAG (stop codon) and TAC (tyrosine, Tyr), most of the preferred codons end in A or U, showing obvious AT preference, as depicted in Fig. 3 and Table S1. This feature is consistent with the codon usage pattern prevalent in land plants mitogenome.

RNA editing sites prediction

In this study, a total of 431 potential RNA editing sites were predicted in the 31 protein-coding genes (PCGs) of A. lappa mitogenome (Table 2; Fig. 4). Among them, the atp6 and rps8 genes had no RNA editing sites, while atp9 and rps1 had only one RNA editing site each. In contrast, the mttB gene showed the most RNA editing sites among all mitochondrial genes, with a total of 37; both the ccmB and nad7 genes had 35 RNA editing sites. Following RNA editing, 41.3% were hydrophobic amino acids which remained unchanged. However, 9% of hydrophobic amino acids were converted to hydrophilic, while 32.3% of hydrophilic amino acids became hydrophobic. All RNA editing events in A. lappa mitogenome involved C-to-U conversions, and editing occurred at the first, second, and third positions of the codon, as presented in Table 2.

Analysis of repeat sequence in A. lappa

We analyzed the repetitive sequences of the mitogenome of A. lappa, according to the sequence characteristics, these repetitive sequences were divided into three types: simple sequence repeat (SSRs), tandem repeat and dispersed repeats. Among them, 27 simple sequence repeat (SSRs) were identified (Table 3 and Table S2), with monomer nucleotide repeats (23, 85.2%) and dimer nucleotide repeats (4, 14.8%) as the main ones. In addition, as shown in Table 4, 30 tandem repeats were identified in the mitogenome of A. lappa, ranging in length from 12 to 57 bp, and the percent matches was between 80% and 100%. In addition, a total of 780 dispersed repeats with a length of 30 bp or longer were identified, of which 422 were forward repeats (D) and 358 were palindromes (P). The longest forward repetitive sequence is 2,762 bp, while the longest palindrome repetitive sequence is 116,788 bp. Among them, forward repetition (D) and palindrome repetition (P) are the most abundant in the range of 30–39 bp, followed by 40–49 bp (Fig. 5 and Table S3).

Ka/Ks analysis

In genetics, the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitutions (Ka/Ks) is vital for understanding gene evolutionary dynamics. It helps judge if a protein-coding gene (PCG) undergoes selective pressure during evolution. Under neutral selection, Ka equals Ks(Ka/Ks = 1). Whereas if Ka > Ks (Ka/Ks > 1), it signifies positive selection; if Ks > Ka (Ka/Ks < 1), it indicates negative (purifying) selection. To investigate the influence of selective pressure on mitogenome evolution in A. lappa, we analyzed the ratio of non-synonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitution rates across 13 common protein-coding genes (PCGs) in several closely related species within the Asteraceae family, including A. lappa, A.tomentosum, Saussurea inversa, and Carthamus tinctorius.etc (Fig. 6). The results showed that the Ka/Ks ratio values lower than 1 for the majority of genes in all species, implying widespread purifying selection. However, the genes ccmb, rps13, and rpl5 exhibited Ka/Ks ratios above 1, which may indicate positive selection potentially linked to environmental adaptation. Additionally, three genes (atp9, cox2, and rpl10) had Ka/Ks ratios close to 1.

Analysis of homologous fragments between mitochondria and chloroplasts

During the evolution of higher plants, the movement of genetic material within cells is a frequently observed event in mitogenomes. It is important to note, however, that sequence segments transferred from chloroplasts tend to display limited conservation. To further explore this phenomenon, we carried out an analysis focusing on the transfer of chloroplast-derived sequences into mitogenomes in A. lappa. We identified 26 homologous fragments between the mitochondrial (mt) and chloroplast (cp.) genomes, with a total length of 8539 bp, ranging from 28 to 913 bp, accounting for approximately 1.96% of the A. lappa mitogenome (Fig. 7). The number of mismatches in these fragments varied from 0 to 179. We refer to these fragments as MTPTs, which represent the transfer of sequences from the chloroplast to the mitochondrial organelle. Among these 24 homologous fragments, the largest was MTPT 1, which measured 913 bp in length. Annotation results of MTPTs showed that these fragments were derived from the protein-coding gene, tRNA gene and intergenic regions of the plastid genome. We proceeded to annotate these homologous sequences and identified four complete tRNA genes (trnW-CCA , trnP-UGG,trnD-GUC,trnN-GUU), which might remain functional in the mitogenome (Table S4).

Analysis of collinearity among A. lappa and other five species

To explore the conservation of mitogenome evolution between A. lappa and five other Asteraceae species (Arctium tomentosum, Saussurea inversa, Chamaemelum nobile, Carthamus tinctorius, Matricaria chamomilla), multiple syntenic maps were constructed via MCscanX based on sequence similarity (Fig. 8). Syntenic analysis of mitogenomes showed a certain synteny among A. lappa and the other five species, implying a close evolutionary relationship or retention of common mitogenome structural features. Moreover, differences in syntenic block arrangement indicated extensive gene rearrangements during mitogenome evolution, which might underlie unique mitochondrial sequences in A. lappa.

Phylogenetic analysis

In this study, in order to determine the evolutionary position of the A. lappa, phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the mitogenome of 28 species, including 20 Asteraceae, 7 other genera (Apiaceae, Lamiaceae, Solanaceae) and Ginkgo biloba as the outgroup (Fig. 9). S.inversa, Dolomiaea costus, and A. lappa were grouped within the same clade, showing a closer phylogenetic relationship, indicating a high degree of evolutionary relatedness. Meanwhile, A. lappa was placed in a well-supported clade together with Arctium lappa and A. tomentosum, with a bootstrap support value of 81%. Other closely related species also occupied similar evolutionary positions. Two species each from Nicotiana (Nicotiana tabacum and Nicotiana sylvestris) and Solanum (Solanum lycopersicum and Solanum pennellii)were clustered into a single major branch, whereas Daucus carota, a member of the Apiaceae family, did not cluster with its expected relatives. Ajuga reptans and Salvia miltiorrhiza, both belonging to the Lamiaceae family, formed a distinct clade with a bootstrap value of 95, while D. carota instead clustered with members of the Asteraceae family. These findings provide valuable insights for future botanical classification efforts. The placement of A. lappa supports its current taxonomic classification and underscores the conservation of mitogenomes within this species. The results offer a solid foundation for analyzing the phylogenetic position of A. lappa.

Discussion

Through further sequencing, assembly, and annotation of A. lappa mitogenome, we determined its length to be 436,648 bp, differing from the 320,439 bp reported in prior studies [35]. This discrepancy may arise from differing annotation standards post-sequencing or the presence of variable numbers of tandem repeat sequences within its mitochondrial control region. The A. lappa mitogenome is longer than those of Asteraceae plants D.costus (320,439 bp), S. inversa (335,372 bp), Arctium lappa (312,598 bp), A. tomentosum (312,609 bp) [34], those of the Apiaceae plants A. dahurica (228,315 bp) [31] and A. biserrata (228,775 bp) [29]. However, it is shorter than medicinal plants such as the Lamiaceae S.miltiorrhiza (499,236 bp) [55] and the Araliaceae P. notoginseng (662,479 bp) [30]. These findings indicate variations in mitogenome length across different plant families and genera, potentially attributable to the expansion and contraction of non-coding DNA. The non-coding DNA encompasses introns, repetitive sequences, and DNA fragments integrated from other genomes, such as chloroplast, mitochondrial, and nuclear genomes.

Although plant mitogenomes are large, they usually contain only 50–60 coding genes, because the coding regions only account for 7–17% of the total mitogenome, and the rest are composed of intergenic regions [26]. In A. lappa, 31 protein-coding genes were identified, accounting for 7.51% of the mitogenome, consistent with the number of PCGs in Taraxacum mongolicum [56]. In comparison, Mikania micrantha possesses 36 PCGs and Mikania cordata 39 PCGs [57], exhibiting similar PCG numbers. However, Arctium lappa (98) and A. Tomentosum (97) PCGs, respectively [34], indicating considerable variation in PCGs number among plants within the same family. GC content is another important indicator for species evaluation [58]. The GC content of A. lappa, T. mongolicum, M. micrantha, M. cordata, Arctium lappa and A. tomentosum were 45.06%, 45.5%, 42,25%, 42.41%, 45.43% and 45.44%, respectively, so the GC content of Asteraceae plants was about 42.41%-45.5%, which highlighted the evolutionary diversity within this group. The number of tRNA genes in different species varies greatly, including 19 in A. lappa, 25 in Arctium lappa, 18 in T. mongolicum, and 22 in M. micrantha and M. cordata, which indicates that the tRNA genes have undergone great changes in the evolution of Asteraceae plants.

Deciphering these codon-usage signatures sharpens our view of how mitochondrial genes evolve and what constraints shape them, revealing the subtle drivers that steer mtDNA divergence among species. Codon usage analysis shows that, like most other plants, Leu, Ser and Arg are the most common amino acids in A. lappa, while Met and Trp are the least frequently used [59]. Consistent with the codon usage pattern of most dicotyledonous plants, A. lappa mitochondrial genes strongly favor codons that terminate with A or T [60].

As a key mechanism in plant mitogenome, frequent RNA editing leads to amino acid changes through insertion, deletion and substitution, which not only greatly promotes genetic diversity, but also plays an important role in plant evolution [61, 62]. RNA editing can take many forms, including C to U, U to C and A to I conversion [63]. However, we found that the RNA editing of A. lappa is mainly from cytosine to uridine (C to U), which is consistent with the pattern observed in T. mongolicum [56]. In addition, 430 RNA editing sites were identified in A. lappa, which were similar to those found in M. micrantha (473) and M. cordata (492) [57], but different from those found in T. mongolicum (278) [56]. The underlying reason may be that different Asteraceae species have evolved distinct energy metabolism demands to adapt to varied habitats (e.g., aridity, high elevation, or C3/C4 photosynthesis). This variation in metabolic demand may alter the selective pressure acting on mitochondrial protein functions, thereby influencing the evolutionary retention or loss of specific RNA editing sites. This insight not only reveals a potential molecular mechanism for plant environmental adaptation but also provides a theoretical basis for future efforts to enhance plant stress tolerance through synthetic biology approaches, such as the rational design of editing sites. In addition, RNA editing sites occur at the first, second, and third base positions of codons, with the first and second positions undergoing more frequent changes. This result is consistent with previous research. High-frequency RNA editing is a key strategy for mitochondria to cope with genome contraction, environmental stress and complex regulatory requirements, and plays an important role in its post-transcriptional regulation.

Repetitive sequences in plant mitogenomes are key factors influencing genome size, architecture, and recombination events [64]. In the mitogenome of A. lappa, various types of repeats were detected, including dispersed repeats, simple sequence repeats (SSRs), and tandem repeats. SSRs are mainly of the monomer nucleotide repeat sequence (A/T) type, which is consistent with the situation in Arctium lappa and other Asteraceae plants, indicating that SSR types within the same family and genus are more similar. 30 tandem repeats were identified in the mitogenome of A. lappa, The number is different from M. micrantha (49) and M. cordata (51), which may be the result of the combined effects of genetic mechanisms, evolutionary history, and environmental adaptation. Dispersed repeats in A. lappa are restricted to direct (D) and palindromic (P) types, mirroring the repeat landscapes reported for M. micrantha and M. cordata [57]. This suggests that they may share a common ancestor with a mechanism for the formation of direct repeats (D) and palindromic repeats (P). The specific functions repeated sequences perform in the mitochondrial context remain to be further investigated.

As the core metric of nucleotide substitution rate, the Ka/Ks ratio measures selective pressure on protein-coding genes (PCGs), revealing the relationship between biological functions and interspecific evolutionary dynamics [65, 66]. It provides a perspective and method for in-depth understanding of biological adaptability. Generally speaking, owing to the high degree of conservation in protein coding genes (PCGs), most undergo neutral or negative selection, resulting in a Ka/Ks ratio that typically does not exceed 1 [67, 68]. Comparing the A. lappa mitogenome with 19 other Asteraceae species revealed that the overwhelming majority of PCGs have evolved under purifying selection, underscoring their high conservation across the family. However, three genes(ccmb, rps13, and rpl5 ) tend to be under positive selection. The ccmb may be involved in the maturation process of mitochondrial cytochrome c by regulating the export and metabolism of heme [69]. Additionally, rps13 and rpl5 play crucial roles in the growth, development, and environmental adaptation of A. lappa.

Plant DNA trafficking occurs in three flavors: organelle-to-organelle, organelle-to-nucleus, and lateral jumps between species [70]. However, the degree of this transfer varies significantly among species [10]. In our study, we conducted a homologous sequence analysis that revealed 26 homologous fragments (MTPTs), totaling 8539 bp and constituting 1.96% of the A. lappa mitogenome, between the chloroplast and mitochondria. This ratio is higher than that of T. mongolicum (1.87%) but lower than that of Arctium lappa (2.63%) [34, 56]. The proportion of these transfer fragments in A. lappa (1.96%) was in agreement, being 1-10.3% of mtDNA in most angiosperm mitogenomes for comparison [71]. These findings back up the inference that plastid derived sequences are unlikely to become the primary driver of mitogenome expansion [72].

Collinearity and rearrangement patterns between A. lappa and other Asteraceae mitogenomes capture both the family’s deep structural conservatism and the genomic reshuffling that accompanies speciation, offering a high-resolution window into the evolutionary dynamics of Asteraceae. Mitogenome can effectively reveal the phylogenetic relationships among higher plants and serves as a valuable source of genetic information for phylogenetic research. as evidenced in Asteraceae [73], Malpi ghiales [74], and Apiales [29]. To further insight into the phylogenetic position of A. lappa in Asteraceae, comprehensive phylogenetic analysis was performed from 28 species. Phylogenetic tree analysis howed that S. inversa, D. costus, Arctium lappa, A. tomentosum, and A. lappa all clustered into Asteraceae. However, it is anomalous that D. carota (Apiaceae) appeared within the cluster of Lamiaceae species (S. miltiorrhiza and A. reptans), with a sequence homology as high as 95%. This result may suggest that horizontal transfer of the mitochondrial genome has occurred between D. carota and species of the Lamiaceae family. Overall, The topology of the phylogenetic tree constructed based on mitochondrial DNA data is consistent with the classification framework of the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG). Importantly, the way these 28 species cluster on the tree is in line with their traditional taxonomic affiliations, effectively demonstrating the congruence between traditional and molecular taxonomic findings. Furthermore, our findings affirm the effectiveness of mitogenome data for revealing taxonomic relationships across familial, ordinal, and higher systematic ranks in higher plants.

Conclusions

This research systematically characterized the mitogenome structure of A. lappa for the first time and conducted a comparative analysis with the published mitochondrial genomes of Asteraceae plants. By integrating long-read and short-read sequencing data, we successfully assembled the mitochondrial genome of A. lappa. The assembled genome was found to be a single circular molecule, with a total length of 436,648 bp. It encodes a total of 54 genes, including 31 protein-coding genes, 19 tRNA genes, and 4 rRNA genes. Subsequently, a comprehensive analysis was carried out on multiple aspects, including codon usage bias, prediction of RNA editing sites, types of repetitive sequences, the ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous mutations, the pattern of gene transfer between organelles, genome collinearity, and phylogenetic relationships. The results support the independent phylogenetic status of A. lappa within the Asteraceae family and uncover the evolutionary features of its mitogenome. This study not only fills the void in the research of the A. lappa mitogenome but also provides a theoretical foundation and data support for the genetic background analysis and molecular breeding of this species.
