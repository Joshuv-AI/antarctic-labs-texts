A stochastic optimization algorithm for revenue maximization in a service system with balking customers
S. A. Bodas, H. Honnappa, M. Mandjes, L. Ravner

Abstract
This paper analyzes a service system modeled as a single-server queue, in which the service provider aims to dynamically maximize the expected revenue per unit of time. This is achieved by constructing a stochastic gradient descent algorithm that dynamically adjusts the price. A key feature of our modeling framework is that customers may choose to balk — that is, decide not to join — when facing high congestion. A notable strength of our approach is that the revenue-maximizing algorithm relies solely on information about effective arrivals, meaning that only the behavior of customers who choose not to balk is observable and used in decision-making. This results in an elaborate interplay between the pricing policy and the effective arrival process, yielding a non-standard state dependent queueing process. An important contribution of our work concerns a novel Infinitesimal Perturbation Analysis (IPA) procedure that is able to consistently estimate the stationary effective arrival rate. This is further leveraged to construct an iterative algorithm that converges, under mild regularity conditions, to the optimal price with provable asymptotic guarantees. Introduction

In this paper, we examine a service facility represented by a single-server queue, where customers arrive in response to the price set by the provider. The service provider’s objective is to determine the optimal price, i.e., the price that maximizes the expected revenue per unit of time. Achieving this goal is particularly challenging, as it involves a nuanced understanding of the intricate relationship between pricing, customer demand, system congestion, and the resulting impact on revenue performance.

Offline and offline revenue maximization —
To put our work into perspective, we first provide a high-level categorization of the existing literature on revenue optimization. Broadly speaking, two dominant approaches can be identified, that one could refer to as the
offline
and
online
paradigms.

\circ

Offline revenue optimization is typically studied using a demand curve
d(p)
that depends on the price
p
. The standard static optimization problem takes the form
\max_{p\geq 0}p\,d(p)
.
However, in the context of service systems, an additional layer of complexity arises: congestion can also reduce demand.
Specifically, users may decide to balk if the (expected) delay exceeds their individual utility thresholds.
In stylized Markovian models, one can explicitly derive the revenue-maximizing price. A canonical example is Naor’s model
[
21
]
, which features an observable queue and linear waiting costs.
In such settings, the
effective demand
, captured by the
effective arrival rate

\lambda(p)
, depends on both the price and the stationary queue-length distribution.
The problem of monopolistic pricing in congested systems has been studied extensively under various modeling assumptions; we refer the reader to
[
7
,
12
]
for comprehensive reviews.
In studies where congestion is modeled through a queueing mechanism, the objective function often includes a deduction for queueing-related costs — see, for example,
[
17
,
18
]
, in which a term proportional to the mean stationary queue length is subtracted. These analyses typically assume that the primitives of the underlying queueing model (in particular the service-time distributions) are known.

\circ

In the dynamic pricing literature, the optimal price is iteratively adjusted until, with some level of confidence, it can be said that the revenue-maximizing price has been found.
This line of work typically assumes uncertainty in the demand curve, which must be learned, and in addition that the queueing primitives are not, or partly, known.
Such a setting naturally calls for online algorithms that learn and optimize simultaneously; we refer to, e.g.,
[
23
]
for a survey of this strand of the literature.
An added layer of complexity arises when customers are delay-sensitive — for instance, when they decide not to enter the system upon observing excessive congestion.
In the queueing literature this is typically handled by considering stylized models. For example, in
[
1
]
it is shown that a Bayesian algorithm converges to the optimal price for a small-buffer Markovian system with two customer types.

The settings considered come in various flavors. First, they may differ in the components included in the revenue function.
While the core component reflects income from service, many formulations also include a penalty term for performance degradation — commonly modeled as a function of mean queueing delay.
Second, there are differences in the definition of the
decision space
. In some studies, the objective is solely to determine the optimal price, while others also optimize over additional parameters such as the service rate or the number of servers.
Third, and specific to the model-free approach, there are several variants based on the type of information available to the service provider when making pricing decisions (e.g., the customers’ arrival times, queueing delays, etc.).

Both offline and online approaches have distinct strengths and limitations. We begin by outlining key considerations related to the offline setting.
(1) In this setting, it is typically assumed that the revenue can be expressed as a function of price. While evaluating this objective is tractable in simple queueing models, often relying on exponential inter-arrival and service times, it becomes considerably more challenging when incorporating more realistic features, such as general service-time distributions or realistic customer behaviors like balking upon encountering excessive workloads at arrival.
(2) The offline approach also requires knowledge of the queueing model’s primitives and the demand curve. While certain customer parameters can often be estimated from historical data (e.g., the service-time distribution), the demand curve is more difficult to infer, as it demands observations across a broad range of pricing scenarios. An important but often overlooked issue is
model uncertainty
: the effects of estimation errors are typically ignored.
(3) When revenue cannot be expressed explicitly, the offline approach frequently resorts to
heavy-traffic approximations
, which involve scaling up arrival rates and service capacity. This can make the optimal price sensitive to system size, entailing that in contexts where such scaling is inappropriate, the accuracy of these approximations degrades.

We now turn to the advantages and challenges of online pricing approaches. A key strength of these methods is that they do not require an explicit revenue function or prior parameter estimation. Instead, prices are adjusted dynamically based on observed system behavior.
The central challenge lies in devising a price update mechanism. An natural strategy is to define
cycles
within which the price remains fixed, and based on the observed behavior adapt the price.
This setting can be framed as a multi-armed bandit problem, where each arm corresponds to a different price, and the objective is to identify the price that maximizes expected revenue.
However, unlike classical bandits with independent rewards, queueing systems exhibit
memory effects
: customers carried over from previous cycles influence the current system state and, consequently, the observed revenue. Therefore, any effective algorithm must account for these transient dynamics when evaluating performance at different price levels.
Another challenge concerns how prices are updated. A common
gradient-based
method adjusts the price iteratively using an estimate of the objective’s gradient. The main difficulty is estimating this gradient accurately from data while ensuring fast convergence.
The ultimate goal is to develop algorithms with low
regret
, meaning minimal cumulative revenue loss relative to the optimal fixed price over time.

Our setup —
As mentioned, the class of revenue maximization problems we consider requires a sound modeling of the interaction between price, demand, and congestion. While other approaches typically separate these effects — combining a demand-based revenue term with a congestion penalty (often proportional to the mean queueing delay) — our framework
integrates
congestion effects directly into the demand-related revenue term.

Our base model is a single-server system with Poisson arrivals and a general service-time distribution.
Upon arrival, customers are informed of the current price
p
set by the administrator and the current workload level
v
, and decide whether to join or balk. Let
H_{\theta}(p,v)
denote the probability that a customer joins when observing workload
v
and price
p
, where
\theta
is a known (possible multidimensional) parameter characterizing the customer population.
This setup is flexible in the sense that it can represent a heterogeneous population of strategic customers with some parametric utility distribution, or it can simply reflect a behavioral (e.g., empirical) joining rule. The objective of the administrator is to maximize long-term revenue, i.e., they aim to find
p^{*}
that maximizes

p\,\mathbb{E}[H_{\theta}(p,V)],

(1.1)

where
V\equiv V(p)
is the stationary workload distribution at price
p
. Thus, the
effective arrival rate
in this dynamic pricing problem is

\lambda(p):=\mathbb{E}[H_{\theta}(p,V)].

It is noted that for known queueing primitives, this quantity is generally intractable; even in the fully Markovian case with exponentially distributed service times, one has to resort to numerical evaluation.

This type of optimization problem naturally lends itself to a stochastic gradient descent (SGD) approach. However, implementing such an approach presents significant challenges: (1) the gradient of
\lambda(p)
must first be estimated, and (2) the bias and variance structure of the resulting estimator must be understood to establish meaningful asymptotic guarantees for the algorithm’s performance. Throughout this work, we assume that
\theta
is known, as it can be efficiently estimated in general settings with balking customers, as discussed in
[
4
,
13
]
.

Online learning for control of queueing systems —

Stochastic approximation (SA) algorithms, the class of iterative methods for finding the roots or extrema of functions when only noisy observations are available, constitute a flexible and robust approach for solving stochastic optimization problems
[
6
]
); a specific subclass is SGD, which relies on noisy gradient estimates.
In the context of queueing systems there is a difficulty that stationary expectations and their gradients are often intractable, which makes direct implementation of standard SA algorithms unfeasible.
A useful method to overcome this difficulty is infinitesimal perturbation analysis (IPA); see
[
11
]
for an extensive introduction to this method and
[
16
]
for an example of its use in a queueing context.
The regenerative properties of such systems can often be used in order to obtain unbiased estimators of the objective function and to verify convergence to the true solution
[
10
]
.
In
[
22
]
an SA algorithm based on busy periods is used to evaluate the Nash equilibrium for queues with balking customers, and more general strategic customer behavior.
Reinforcement learning is another related approach for steady-state optimization of queueing networks
[
9
]
, particularly well-suited to settings with a complex or high-dimensional policy space.

Our work follows in the tradition of
[
8
]
, in that it also develops an SGA-based online learning-based dynamic pricing method aimed at identifying the revenue-maximizing price in a setting that includes congestion effects. In
[
8
]
, these congestion effects are incorporated into the objective function through a term proportional to the mean stationary queue length, while the arrival rate depends solely on the current price level. It is assumed that the function
\lambda(p)
is known, while the customers’ service-time characteristics are not. The price adaptation scheme involves observations of customer delays, where in the proofs it plays a central role that the queue process is geometrically ergodic
[
8
, Lemma 2]
. This approach necessitates assuming that the moment-generating and cumulant-generating functions of service and inter-arrival times satisfy certain Cramér-type conditions.
The analysis relies on a specific construction for the inter-arrival and service time distributions to enable the use of conventional IPA.
Specifically, their setup involves a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables
(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where the
n
-th inter-arrival time, for a given price
\lambda(p)
, is modeled as
U_{n}/\lambda(p)
; a similar construction is applied to the service times.
Notably, this definition implies that the effective arrival process is not congestion-dependent. In contrast, our approach avoids imposing such a structure, in that we assume that the inter-arrival distribution depends on both the price and the workload.

Contributions —

We now outline the main contributions of our work, distinguishing between three aspects: (1) the problem formulation; (2) the results; and (3) the underlying techniques.

(1)

Regarding the problem formulation, we explicitly model the behavior of clients who choose not to join the system when faced with high congestion, resulting in lost revenue. Incorporating such balking customers is particularly challenging, as in our framework we observe only the
effective
arrivals — that is, data related to the customers who do not balk and enter the system. The dependence of each customer’s joining decision on the congestion level introduces several evident technical hurdles. A useful insight is that the arrival rate at a given price
p
can be written as the reciprocal of the mean effective inter-arrival time at the same price.

Our objective function (
1.1
) consists of a single term that naturally captures the effect of congestion. This formulation is conceptually more transparent than the approach in
[
8
]
, which combines a revenue term with a separate congestion penalty and thus requires specifying the relative weight of each component. Moreover, while the congestion penalty in
[
8
]
reflects the performance degradation experienced by customers, it does not account for the reduced willingness of customers to join the system under high congestion.

(2)

We develop an algorithm that provably converges to the optimal price
p^{*}
. The adaptive queue control schemes in previous works, such as
[
8
,
11
,
16
]
, typically rely on observations of customer delays, where in the proofs it plays a central role that the queue process is geometrically ergodic
[
8
, Lemma 2]
. This approach necessitates assuming that the moment-generating and cumulant-generating functions of service and inter-arrival times satisfy certain Cramér-type conditions. Importantly, our price update scheme avoids such assumptions. The process at the center of our analysis is the sequence of inter-arrival times, which is well-behaved (light tails and quick mixing) regardless of the workload process. We assume Poisson arrivals, which is a mild assumption in the context of a large pool of potential customers independently requesting service.

As the algorithm adapts to the distribution of the underlying process, the resulting observations become non-stationary, introducing bias into the gradient estimator. Nevertheless, this estimator remains consistent, with its variance uniformly bounded with respect to the price. Furthermore, the bias decays at a rate of
\mathcal{O}\big(\eta_{k-1}({T^{*}_{k-1}})^{-2}\big)
, where
\eta_{k}
denotes the learning rate of the SGD algorithm and
T^{*}_{k}
represents an increasing sampling window size at iteration
k\geq 1
. By setting
\eta_{k}=\eta k^{-\alpha}
, the algorithm achieves a regret bound of
\mathcal{O}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{L}T^{*}_{k}k^{-\alpha/2}\big)
, where
L
is the total number of iterations.

(3)

To establish convergence to the optimal price
p^{*}
, it is necessary at various stages to control the transient behavior of the underlying queueing system.
During each cycle of the price-adjustment algorithm, the price is held constant — say at value
p
— so the system temporarily behaves like an M/G/1 queue with balking customers, where the per-customer joining probability is
H_{\theta}(p,v)
when the observed congestion level is
v
.
Analyzing such queues is notoriously difficult, even in terms of their stationary behavior. We succeed in developing sufficiently sharp bounds, based on a natural coupling idea, that enable proving the convergence to the revenue maximizing price.
These enable us to identify explicit bounds on the bias and ‘variability’ of the gradient of the revenue (i.e., the derivative of the revenue with respect to the price, in each step of the algorithm).

The IPA estimator required the development of a new recursive formulation of the sample-path gradients pertaining to the effective arrival process. It is noted that the standard analysis in this area covers processes that are considerably more elementary, typically of random-walk type. Providing performance guarantees for the specific form of the resulting gradient estimator required novel coupling arguments. As mentioned, the arrival rate is the reciprocal of the mean effective inter-arrival time, which is a non-standard object in the context of IPA analysis.

(4)

Our approach paves the way for the development of more robust online control algorithms for queueing systems with congestion-dependent joining behavior. Unlike in
[
8
]
, we do not optimize over service capacity. However, a byproduct of our analysis is the derivation of workload gradients, which makes our method extensible to settings that include holding costs.
Notably, these workload gradients differ structurally from those obtained through classical IPA schemes, due to the dependency between inter-arrival times and the workload. Furthermore, our approach naturally extends to multi-server systems, enabling optimization over the number of servers as well. We empirically validate the effectiveness of the resulting price update scheme in the multi-server setting.

We have organized our paper as follows: Section
2
introduces the model, formulates the problem that is being studied, and in short highlights the stochastic gradient descent algorithm designed to solve it.
Section
3
goes into intricate mathematical details and establishes crucial results about the model that are key to the goal of this paper.
Section
4
is devoted to the construction of the gradient estimator used in the stochastic gradient descent algorithm.
By this point, the reader is exposed to the complete algorithm with all the details.
Section
5
then focuses on the analysis of the proposed gradient estimator.
Upper bounds on the bias and the variability of the gradient estimator are established.
To evaluate the performance of the proposed algorithm, Section
6
analyzes a widely used metric known as
regret
.
Section
7
discusses a series of simulation experiments, confirming our estimation procedure’s efficacy.
Appendix
A
contains the proofs to some results in this paper, and Appendix
B
consists of additional formulae for the interested reader, which were used to obtain some of the plots in Section
7
.

Model and objectives

In this section we discuss the model under study, introduce our objective function, and specify our learning algorithm.

2.1
System dynamics

We model the service system via a FCFS (first-come-first-served) single-server queue.
Potential customers arrive according to a Poisson process with
known
rate
\Lambda
.
Let the customers’ service requirements form a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables, distributed as the generic random variable
S
with cumulative distribution function (CDF)
G(\cdot)

To join the system, each customer must pay the admission price
p\in\mathcal{P}\equiv\big[\underline{p},\overline{p}\big]
quoted by the service operator (where
0\leq\underline{p}\leq\overline{p}
).
The system operator is free to adapt this price over time.
After noting the admission price
p
and their prospective waiting time (before service) in the system
V

H(p,V)
, where
H:\mathcal{P}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto[0,1]
, and otherwise they balk.
These join/balk decisions are sampled independently across customers, and independently of both the arrival process and the service requirements.
Customers that join the system remain until service completion. Over a period during which the price
p
is fixed, the model described above can be denoted — using standard Kendall notation — as an M/G/1 +
H(p,V)
queue, to emphasize that it is an M/G/1 queue with customers who are sensitive to both price and congestion.

Objective function

We are now ready to formally define the

The primary goal of this paper is to find an efficient algorithm to identify the

\displaystyle p^{*}=\argmax_{p\in\mathcal{P}}{\Psi(p)}.

The following proposition characterizes

For any

\displaystyle\Psi(p)

\displaystyle=\Lambda\exptn\big[H(p,W_{\infty}(p))\big]\,p

(2.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{\exptn\left[A_{\infty}(p)\right]}.

Crucially, it allows for the estimation of both the objective function and its gradient — quantities that form the foundation of the learning algorithm presented in Subsection

2.3
Learning algorithm

In this subsection we define our stochastic gradient descent algorithm to learn the optimal price

Lemma 3.7
.

If
p\in\mathcal{P}
,
\zeta\sim\text{Uniform}[0,1]
, and
\overline{W}
is such that
\exptn\big[\overline{W}\big]<\infty
, then
\exptn\big[F^{-1}_{p}\big(\zeta;\overline{W}\big)\big]<\infty
.

Proof.

Observe the following two facts:

(i)

Because the inverse of a probability distribution function is monotonically increasing, we have
\big\{\zeta\leq F_{p}\big(\overline{W};\overline{W}\big)\big\}=\big\{F^{-1}_{p}\big(\zeta;\overline{W}\big)\leq\overline{W}\big\}
. These are scenarios where the effective interarrival time is not larger than the initial workload
\overline{W}
.

(ii)

Similarly,
\big\{\zeta>F_{p}\big(\overline{W};\overline{W}\big)\big\}=\big\{F^{-1}_{p}\big(\zeta;\overline{W}\big)>\overline{W}\big\}
. These are scenarios where the effective interarrival time is larger than the initial workload. Therefore, the workload in the system hits 0 after time
\overline{W}
. By the memoryless property associated with the Poisson process, the remaining time
\big(F^{-1}_{p}\big(\overline{W},\overline{W}\big)-\overline{W}\big)
follows an
\text{Exp}\big(\Lambda H(p,0)\big)
distribution (i.e., is exponentially distributed with mean
1/\Lambda H(p,0)
).

Let
\widetilde{\zeta}\sim\text{Uniform}[0,1]
be independent of
\big(\zeta,\overline{W}\big)
. Then (i) and (ii) can mathematically be written as

\displaystyle F^{-1}_{p}\big(\zeta;\overline{W}\big)\mathbbm{1}\big\{\zeta\leq F_{p}\big(\overline{W};\overline{W}\big)\big\}\leq_{\text{a.s.}}\overline{W},

\displaystyle\overline{W}\leq_{\text{a.s.}}\

\displaystyle F^{-1}_{p}\big(\zeta;\overline{W}\big)\mathbbm{1}\big\{\zeta>F_{p}\big(\overline{W};\overline{W}\big)\big\}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\overline{W}+F^{-1}_{p}\big(\widetilde{\zeta};0\big).

Upon combining the above, we conclude that

\displaystyle\exptn\big[F^{-1}_{p}\big(\zeta,\overline{W}\big)\big]\leq\exptn\big[\overline{W}\big]+\exptn\big[F^{-1}_{p}\big(\tilde{\zeta};0\big)\big]=\exptn\big[\overline{W}\big]+\frac{1}{\Lambda H(p,0)}<\infty.

This proves the claim.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

For any
p\in\mathcal{P}
we have

\nabla\exptn\left[A_{\infty}(p)\right]=\exptn\left[\nabla A_{\infty}(p)\right]
.

Proof.

The interchange of derivative and expectation is justified by using the dominated convergence theorem (see e.g.
[
3
]
). With
\zeta\sim\text{Uniform}[0,1]
, recall that
A_{\infty}(p)=F^{-1}_{p}\big(\zeta;\overline{W}_{\infty}(p)\big)
, where by Proposition
3.1
we have
\exptn\big[\overline{W}_{\infty}(p)\big]<\infty
. Now,

1.

By Lemma
3.7
,
\exptn\left[F^{-1}_{p}\left(\zeta;\overline{W}_{\infty}(p)\right)\right]<\infty
.

2.

By Assumption
2.3
,
F^{-1}_{p}\left(\zeta;\overline{W}_{\infty}(p)\right)
is continuously differentiable in
p
.

3.

By Assumption
3.4
,
\nabla_{p}F^{-1}_{p}\left(\zeta;\overline{W}_{\infty}(p)\right)\leq_{\text{a.s.}}G_{\max}
.

Observe that this means that the hypotheses of the dominated convergence theorem are met, and the result therefore follows.
∎

We emphasize that Proposition
3.8
is crucial for the construction of an estimator for the gradient
\nabla\Psi(p)
, as needed in the recursive algorithm (
2.6
). Suppose one aims so to obtain an estimator for the gradient of the expectation of a random variable with respect to a given parameter — something that is often highly non-trivial to compute. Infinitesimal Perturbation Analysis (IPA)
[
11
]
provides a framework that justifies the interchange of the derivative and expectation operators. This allows us to build the estimator by essentially ‘differentiating the sample path’, thus yielding a pathwise gradient estimate under appropriate regularity conditions.

(3.5)

As a result, and by applying the conclusion of part (a),

\displaystyle\exptn\left|A^{1}_{j}-A^{2}_{j}\right|<\exptn\left|\overline{W}^{1}_{j}-\overline{W}^{2}_{j}\right|\leq\gamma_{1}^{j}\exptn\left|\overline{W}^{1}_{0}-\overline{W}^{2}_{0}\right|.

The
n
-th effective arrival time in system
i=1,2
is given by
\widetilde{A}^{i}_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A^{i}_{j}
. By Equation (
(b)
),

\displaystyle 0\leq\widetilde{A}^{1}_{n}-\widetilde{A}^{2}_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(A^{1}_{j}-A^{2}_{j}\right)<\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\overline{W}^{1}_{j}-\overline{W}^{2}_{j}\right).

Again using the result from part (a),

\displaystyle\exptn\big|\widetilde{A}^{1}_{n}-\widetilde{A}^{2}_{n}\big|

\displaystyle=\int_{\big\{\overline{W}^{1}_{0}>\overline{W}^{2}_{0}\big\}}\left(\widetilde{A}^{1}_{n}-\widetilde{A}^{2}_{n}\right)\ \mathrm{d}\prob+\int_{\big\{\overline{W}^{2}_{0}\geq\overline{W}^{1}_{0}\big\}}\left(\widetilde{A}^{2}_{n}-\widetilde{A}^{1}_{n}\right)\ \mathrm{d}\prob

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\big\{\overline{W}^{1}_{0}>\overline{W}^{2}_{0}\big\}}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\overline{W}^{1}_{j}-\overline{W}^{2}_{j}\right)\ \mathrm{d}\prob+\int_{\big\{\overline{W}^{2}_{0}\geq\overline{W}^{1}_{0}\big\}}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\overline{W}^{2}_{j}-\overline{W}^{1}_{j}\right)\ \mathrm{d}\prob

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\left|\overline{W}^{1}_{j}-\overline{W}^{2}_{j}\right|\ \mathrm{d}\prob=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\exptn\left|\overline{W}^{1}_{j}-\overline{W}^{2}_{j}\right|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\gamma_{1}^{j}\exptn\left|\overline{W}^{1}_{0}-\overline{W}^{2}_{0}\right|

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{1-\gamma_{1}^{n}}{1-\gamma_{1}}\bigg)\exptn\left|\overline{W}^{1}_{0}-\overline{W}^{2}_{0}\right|.

(c)

We again restrict our analysis to the event
\big\{\overline{W}^{1}_{0}\geq\overline{W}^{2}_{0}\big\}
.
Define

\displaystyle K:=\inf\left\{n\geq 1:\underline{W}^{1}_{n}=\underline{W}^{2}_{n}\right\},

i.e.,
K
is the effective arrival number starting when the two queues are
coupled with a time-lag
, where this time-lag is given by
\Delta:=\big(\widetilde{A}^{1}_{K}-\widetilde{A}^{2}_{K}\big)
. Note that
N_{1}\left(0,t\right]-N_{2}\left(0,t\right]
is increasing for
t\leq\widetilde{A}^{1}_{K}
, and is constant for
t>\widetilde{A}^{1}_{K}
. Then, for
t>\widetilde{A}^{1}_{K}
,

\displaystyle N_{1}\big(0,t\big]

\displaystyle=N_{1}\big(0,\widetilde{A}^{1}_{K}\big]+N_{1}\big(\widetilde{A}^{1}_{K},t\big]=K+N_{1}\big(\widetilde{A}^{1}_{K},t\big],

\displaystyle N_{2}\big(0,t\big]

\displaystyle=N_{2}\big(0,\widetilde{A}^{2}_{K}\big]+N_{2}\big(\widetilde{A}^{2}_{K},t\big]=K+N_{2}\big(\widetilde{A}^{2}_{K},t\big]=K+N_{2}\big(\widetilde{A}^{2}_{K},t-\Delta\big]+N_{2}\big(t-\Delta,t\big].

Observe that, since the two queues are coupled with a time-lag,

\displaystyle N_{1}\big(\widetilde{A}^{1}_{K},t\big]=N_{2}\big(\widetilde{A}^{2}_{K},t-\Delta\big].

We then have that, for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle N_{2}\left(0,t\right]-N_{1}\left(0,t\right]=N_{2}\left(t-\Delta,t\right]\leq_{\text{st}}\overline{N}\left(\Delta\right),

(3.6)

where the random variable
\overline{N}(\Delta)
records the number of arrivals in an interval of length
\Delta
, under a Poisson stream of arrivals with rate
\Lambda H\big(\underline{p},0\big)
. The stochastic dominance follows from an evident coupling argument: each interarrival time in the second queue stochastically dominates an
\text{Exp}\big(\Lambda\,H\big(\underline{p},0\big)\big)
random variable.
Similarly, under the complementary event
\big\{\overline{W}^{1}_{0}<\overline{W}^{2}_{0}\big\}
we have
(
3.6
) as well.
In conclusion,

\big|N_{1}\left(0,T^{*}\right]-N_{2}\left(0,T^{*}\right]\big|\leq_{\text{st}}\overline{N}\left(\Delta\right).

We have that
\overline{N}(\Delta)
conditional on
\Delta=\delta
has a Poisson distribution with parameter
\Lambda\,H\big(\underline{p},0\big)\,\delta
.
Thus, by appealing to part (b) of the present proposition,

\displaystyle\exptn\left|N_{1}\left(0,T^{*}\right]-N_{2}\left(0,T^{*}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\exptn\Big[\overline{N}\big(\Delta\big)\Big]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exptn\Big[\overline{N}\big(\Delta\big)\,\big|\,\Delta=\delta\Big]\,\prob\big(\Delta\in\mathrm{d}\delta\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Lambda H\big(\underline{p},0\big)\,\delta\,\prob\big(\Delta\in\mathrm{d}\delta\big)

\displaystyle=\Lambda\,H\big(\underline{p},0\big)\,\exptn\big[\Delta\big]\leq\frac{\Lambda\,H\big(\underline{p},0\big)}{1-\gamma_{1}}\exptn\left|\overline{W}^{1}_{0}-\overline{W}^{2}_{0}\right|.

(d)

Suppose there are
\sigma_{T}-2
regeneration cycles excluding the partial regeneration cycles at the start and end of the interval
(0,T]
. Let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{\sigma_{T}}
be the corresponding number of arrivals in each of these time intervals, with
C_{2},\ldots,C_{\sigma_{T}}-1
being i.i.d. Let
C
,
\tau
be steady-state number of effective arrivals and steady-state regeneration cycle length respectively.
Then,

\displaystyle\frac{N_{1}\left(0,T\right]}{T}=\frac{C_{1}+\cdots+C_{\sigma_{T}}}{\sigma_{T}}\times\frac{\sigma_{T}}{T}\>\to_{\rm a.s.}\>\frac{\exptn\left[C\right]}{\exptn[\tau]}=\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(p)]}.

As a consequence, recalling that
g(\cdot)=x^{m}+a_{1}x^{m-1}+\cdots+a_{m}
is a degree-
m
polynomial that
T
has support
[T^{*},\infty)
,

\displaystyle\exptn\left[\frac{1}{g\left(N_{1}(0,T]\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{\prob}\left(\exptn\left[\frac{1}{N_{1}\left(0,T\right]^{m}}\right]\right)=\mathcal{O}_{\prob}\left(\exptn\left[\frac{1}{T^{m}}\frac{1}{\left(N_{1}\left(0,T\right]/T\right)^{m}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}_{\prob}\left(\sqrt{\exptn\left[\frac{1}{T^{2m}}\right]\exptn\left[\frac{1}{\left(N_{1}\left(0,T\right]/T\right)^{2m}}\right]}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}_{\prob}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{{T^{*}}^{2m}}\exptn\left[\frac{1}{\left(N_{1}\left(0,T\right]/T\right)^{2m}}\right]}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{{T^{*}}^{m}}\right).

This completes the proofs of the four parts.
∎

4
Gradient estimation

Recalling the gradient estimator (
2.8
), the main objectives of this section are (i) to show how its components are computed and (ii) to establish almost convergence of the estimator when the sampling window increases.

For
k\geq 1
, let
N_{k}
be the random variable which denotes the number of effective arrivals during the
k
-th iteration, i.e., during the time interval
\big[\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k}\big)
, and let
\widetilde{N}_{k}=N_{1}+\cdots+N_{k}
.
Let
\left\{T^{*}_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a strictly increasing deterministic sequence, chosen by the service operator.
Since
T_{k}
is a random variable, the service operator cannot choose its value.
Therefore, a quantity
T^{*}_{k}
is chosen such that

\circ

T_{k}\geq_{\text{a.s.}}T^{*}_{k}
, and

\circ

there is an effective arrival at time
\overline{T}_{k}
, i.e.,
\overline{T}_{k}=A_{\widetilde{N}_{k}}
.

We detail later how one could pick values
\left\{T^{*}_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 1}
so that in the process of learning the optimal price
p^{*}
, the regret of enforcing suboptimal prices remains well controlled.

A natural estimator for
\exptn\left[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\right]
is the sample average of effective interarrival times observed in the
k
-th iteration, i.e.,

\displaystyle\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1}):=\frac{T_{k}}{N_{k}}=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{i=\widetilde{N}_{k-1}+1}^{\widetilde{N}_{k}}A_{i}.

(4.1)

It is inherently challenging to define an estimator for
\nabla_{p}\exptn\left[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\right]
. On the other hand, a natural estimator for
\exptn\left[\nabla_{p}A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\right]
is the sample average of price-derivatives of observed effective interarrival times. We can therefore leverage Proposition
3.8
to justify using the latter as a proxy for estimating the former. Recall that for
i\in\big\{\widetilde{N}_{k-1}+1,\cdots,\widetilde{N}_{k}\big\},\ A_{i}=F^{-1}_{p}\big(\zeta_{i};\overline{W}_{i-1}\big)
. We therefore define the IPA-type estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1}):=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{i=\widetilde{N}_{k-1}+1}^{\widetilde{N}_{k}}\nabla_{p}F^{-1}_{p}\left(\zeta_{i};\overline{W}_{i-1}\right).

(4.2)

Evaluating
\nabla_{p}F^{-1}_{p}\big(\zeta_{i};\overline{W}_{i-1}\big)
through Equation (
3.3
) requires knowledge of
\nabla_{p}\overline{W}_{i-1}
, the computation of which we now explain.
First, we set
\nabla_{p}\overline{W}_{\widetilde{N}_{k-1}}:=0
.
Then for any
i\in\big\{\widetilde{N}_{k-1}+1,\cdots,\widetilde{N}_{k}\big\}
,
\nabla_{p}\overline{W}_{i}
can be computed through the recursion

\displaystyle\nabla_{p}\overline{W}_{i}=\begin{cases}\nabla_{p}\overline{W}_{i-1}-\nabla_{p}F^{-1}_{p}\left(\zeta_{i};\overline{W}_{i-1}\right)\hskip 28.45274pt&\text{if}\ \underline{W}_{i}>0,\\
0&\text{if }\underline{W}_{i}=0.\end{cases}

Combining these statistics yields the estimator
\widehat{\nabla\Psi}(p)
as defined in (
2.8
). Under the assumptions made above we have the following result regarding consistency of the gradient estimator.

Proposition 4.1
.

Under Assumption
2.1
,
the estimator
\widehat{\nabla\Psi}(p)
converges almost surely to
\nabla\Psi(p)
as the interval size
T
on which price
p
is enforced goes to
\infty
.

The proof relies on applying a law of large numbers for regenerative processes and is deferred to Appendix
A.4
.

5
Convergence analysis of the learning algorithm

In this section we focus on the analysis of the proposed gradient estimator.
Specifically, upper bounds on the bias and the variability of the gradient estimator are established.

Recall that, for
k\geq 1
, our price update equation is given by (
2.6
).
Define a filtration
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 0}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{0}=\emptyset,\quad\mathcal{F}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sigma\Big(\widehat{\nabla\Psi}(p_{0}),\cdots,\widehat{\nabla\Psi}(p_{n-1})\Big)\,\ \text{for }n\geq 1.

Let
\beta_{k}
denote the bias of the gradient estimator in the
k
-th iteration of the algorithm, and let
\nu_{k}
denote its variability, i.e.,

\displaystyle\beta_{k}

\displaystyle:=\exptn\left[\widehat{\nabla\Psi}(p_{k-1})|\ \mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]-\nabla\Psi(p_{k-1}),

(5.1)

\displaystyle\nu_{k}

\displaystyle:=\exptn\left[\widehat{\nabla\Psi}(p_{k-1})^{2}\right].

(5.2)

The following two subsections study how this bias and variability behave as
k
goes to
\infty
. In particular, we establish the following proposition. The proofs of the two upper bounds are detailed in the following two subsections.

Proposition 5.1
.

There exists constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that, for all
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\nu_{k}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1},

(5.3)

\displaystyle|\beta_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\frac{\eta_{k-1}}{{T^{*}_{k-1}}^{2}}.

(5.4)

Corollary 5.2
.

If the window sizes
\left\{T^{*}_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
are chosen so that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{\eta_{k-1}}/({{T^{*}_{k-1}})^{2}}<\infty
, then
p_{k}\>\to_{\rm a.s.}\>p^{*}
as
k\to\infty
.

5.1
Variability analysis

The goal of this subsection is to prove the uniform upper bound (
5.3
).
From Equation (
2.8
), in combination with Corollary
3.6
, it follows that

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla\Psi}(p_{k-1})^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}+\overline{p}^{2}\frac{\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{4}}\leq\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}+\overline{p}^{2}G_{\max}^{2}\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{4}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\exptn\left[\widehat{\nabla\Psi}(p_{k-1})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\exptn\left[\exptn\left[\widehat{\nabla\Psi}(p_{k-1})^{2}\ \big|N_{k}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exptn\left[\exptn\left[\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}\ \Big|N_{k}\right]\right]+\overline{p}^{2}G_{\max}^{2}\exptn\left[\exptn\left[\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{4}}\ \Big|N_{k}\right]\right].

(5.5)

By definition,

\displaystyle\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})=\frac{T_{k}}{N_{k}}\>\to_{\rm a.s.}\>\exptn\left[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\right].

Hence, by the continuous mapping theorem, for any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{m}}\>\to_{\rm a.s.}\>\frac{1}{\exptn\left[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\right]^{m}}\leq\frac{1}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(\underline{p})\big]^{m}}.

It follows that
\nu_{k}=\mathcal{O}(1)
as
k\to\infty
, thus verifying (
5.3
).

5.2
Bias analysis

In the analysis of the bias, the starting point is

\displaystyle\big|\beta_{k}\big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\exptn\left[\widehat{\nabla\Psi}(p_{k-1})|\ \mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]-\nabla\Psi(p_{k-1})\Big|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\exptn\left[\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}-p_{k-1}\frac{\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}\ \Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]-\left(\frac{1}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]}-p_{k-1}\frac{\nabla\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]^{2}}\right)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\omega^{\rm(I)}_{k}+\overline{p}\,\omega^{\rm(II)}_{k},

(5.6)

where

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(I)}

\displaystyle:={\Bigg|\exptn\left[\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}\ \Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]-\frac{1}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]}\Bigg|},

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(II)}

\displaystyle:={\Bigg|\exptn\left[\frac{\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}\ \Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]-\frac{\nabla\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]^{2}}\Bigg|}.

We aim to determine an upper bound on the rate at which
\beta_{k}
vanishes as
k\to\infty
, which we do by analyzing the decay behavior of both
\omega_{k}^{\text{(I)}}
and
\omega_{k}^{\text{(II)}}
.

5.2.1
Analysis of
\omega_{k}^{\rm(III)}

In our analysis a key role is played by the following result.

Lemma 5.3
.

As
k\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(III)}:=\left|\exptn\left[\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})\ \big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]-\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\eta_{k-1}\frac{1}{{T^{*}_{k-1}}^{2}}\right).

(5.7)

Proof.

As a first step, observe that
\omega_{1}^{\rm(III)}
can be majorized via

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(III)}=\

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{j=\widetilde{N}_{k-1}+1}^{\widetilde{N}_{k}}A_{j}\,\bigg|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{j=\widetilde{N}_{k-1}+1}^{\widetilde{N}_{k}}\left(A_{j}-A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\right)\,\bigg|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{j=\widetilde{N}_{k-1}+1}^{\widetilde{N}_{k}}\left(A_{j}-A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\right)\,\bigg|\,N_{k},\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\,\bigg|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N_{k}}\left|\sum_{j=\widetilde{N}_{k-1}+1}^{\widetilde{N}_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[A_{j}-A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\,\big|\,N_{k},\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\right|\,\bigg|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{j=\widetilde{N}_{k-1}+1}^{\widetilde{N}_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|A_{j}-A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\right|\,\big|\,N_{k},\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\,\bigg|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right],

(5.8)

where in the first inequality we have used Jensen’s inequality, and in the second inequality we have used the triangle inequality.
In order to further dominate the expectation in (
5.2.1
), we construct a probability space
\chi
, and on this space we couple two M/G/1 +

\circ

\circ

We now have a setup where Proposition
3.9

\circ

. Therefore, for

\circ

Then, for any

(c),

\displaystyle\exptn\big[\big|A_{j}-A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big|\ \big|N_{k},\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\big]\leq\gamma_{1}^{j-\widetilde{N}_{k-1}}\exptn\left[\left|W\left(\overline{T}_{k-1}\right)-\overline{W}_{\infty}\left(p_{k-1}\right)\right|\ \big|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right].

Substituting this into inequality (

\gamma_{1}<1
,

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(III)}

\displaystyle\leq\exptn\left[\frac{1}{N_{k}}\exptn\left[\left|W\left(\overline{T}_{k-1}\right)-\overline{W}_{\infty}\left(p_{k-1}\right)\right|\ \big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\sum_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\gamma_{1}^{j}\ \Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle<_{\text{a.s.}}\exptn\left[\left|W\left(\overline{T}_{k-1}\right)-\overline{W}_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\right|\ \big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\ \exptn\big[\left|W\left(\overline{T}_{k-1}\right)-\overline{W}_{\infty}(p_{k-2})\right|\ \big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\big]+\exptn\big[\left|\overline{W}_{\infty}(p_{k-2})-\overline{W}_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\right|\ \big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\big],

\displaystyle\exptn\left[\left|W\left(\overline{T}_{k-1}\right)-\overline{W}_{\infty}(p_{k-2})\right|\ \big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\leq\gamma_{1}^{N_{k-1}}\exptn\left[\left|W\left(\overline{T}_{k-2}\right)-\overline{W}_{\infty}(p_{k-2})\right|\ \big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right],

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(III)}\leq\ \delta_{k-1}\leq\gamma_{1}^{N_{k-1}}\delta_{k-2}+\rho_{k-2},

with, for any

1
,

\displaystyle\delta_{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}^{N_{1}+\cdots+N_{k-1}}\delta_{0}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-2}\rho_{\ell}\left(\prod_{m=\ell+2}^{k-1}N_{m}\right)=\mathcal{O}_{\prob}\left(\rho_{k-2}\right);

\gamma_{1}<1

Applying Taylor’s theorem, and invoking Corollary

\displaystyle\rho_{k-2}

\displaystyle=\exptn\left[\left|W_{\infty}(p_{k-2})-W_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\right|\ \big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq G_{\max}\exptn\left[\left|p_{k-2}-p_{k-1}\right|\big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\leq G_{\max}\exptn\left[\left|\eta_{k-1}\widehat{\nabla\Psi}(p_{k-2})\right|\ \big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

where the equality marked

\displaystyle\rho_{k-2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\eta_{k-1}\frac{1}{{T^{*}_{k-1}}^{2}}\right).

(5.12)

Upon combining the above, we conclude that the stated holds.

5.2.2
Analysis of
\omega_{k}^{\rm(I)}

Our next goal is to use Lemma
5.3
to obtain an upper bound on the rate of convergence of
\omega_{k}^{\rm(I)}
as
k\to\infty
.

Lemma 5.4
.

As
k\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(I)}=\mathcal{O}\left(\eta_{k-1}\frac{1}{{T^{*}_{k-1}}^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Let
\epsilon
be such that
0<\epsilon<\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(\underline{p})\big]<\infty
. Then, by Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\omega_{k}^{\rm(I)}\leq\exptn\left[\left|\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}-\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]}\right|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\leq\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.a)}+\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.b)},

where, with
{\bm{1}}_{k}=\mathbbm{1}\big\{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})\geq\epsilon\big\}
,

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.a)}

\displaystyle:={\exptn\left[\left|\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}-\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]}\right|{\bm{1}}_{k}\,\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]},

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.b)}

\displaystyle:={\exptn\left[\left|\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}-\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]}\right|(1-{{\bm{1}}}_{k})\,\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]},

We now proceed to analyze both terms separately.

We start by exploring the decay of
\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.a)}
. By Taylor’s theorem, there exists a random variable
\xi
between
\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})

and
\exptn\left[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\right]

such that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}-\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]}\right|=\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\left|\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right|.

Then,

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.a)}

\displaystyle=\exptn\left[\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\left|\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right|\,{{\bm{1}}}_{k}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq_{\text{a.s.}}\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\exptn\left[\left|\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right|{{\bm{1}}}_{k}\ \big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq_{\text{a.s.}}\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\exptn\Big[\left|\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right|{{\bm{1}}}_{k}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\left|\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right|(1-{{\bm{1}}}_{k})\ \big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\exptn\left[\Big|\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\Big|\ \big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\eta_{k-1}\frac{1}{{T^{*}_{k-1}}^{2}}\right),

where we have used
\xi>\epsilon
in the first inequality, and Lemma
5.3
in the final step.

We continue by showing that
\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.b)}
, as
k\rightarrow\infty
, decays faster than
\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.a)}
, so that
\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.b)}
becomes negligible. By ‘splitting the event of interest into slices’, we can bound the expectations corresponding to the resulting subevents. As a first step, with

\displaystyle{\bm{i}}_{k,j}:=\mathbbm{1}\big\{\epsilon/2^{j+1}<\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})\leq\epsilon/2^{j}\big\},

we write

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.b)}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\ \exptn\Bigg[\left|\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}-\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]}\right|\,{\bm{i}}_{k,j}\,\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Bigg].

Now note that in case
{{\bm{i}}}_{k,j}
equals
1
for a given
j\geq 0
, we have, recalling that
0<\epsilon<\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(\underline{p})\big]
,

\displaystyle\frac{2^{j+1}}{\epsilon}>\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}-\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]}\geq\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}-\frac{1}{\exptn\left[A_{\infty}(\underline{p})\right]}>\frac{2^{j}}{\epsilon}-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\geq 0,

thus leading to the inequality

\displaystyle\exptn\Bigg[\left|\frac{1}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}-\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]}\right|\,{\bm{i}}_{k,j}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Bigg]

\displaystyle<\frac{2^{j+1}}{\epsilon}\,\prob\{{\bm{i}}_{k,j}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\},

so that

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.b)}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\ \frac{2^{j+1}}{\epsilon}\ \prob\{{\bm{i}}_{k,j}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\}.

We continue by further bounding
\prob\{{\bm{i}}_{k,j}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\}
.

\displaystyle\prob\{{\bm{i}}_{k,j}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\}

\displaystyle\leq\ \prob\left\{\frac{\epsilon}{2^{j+1}}<\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2^{j}}\ \Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right\}=\prob\left\{\frac{\epsilon}{2^{j+1}}<\frac{T_{k}}{N_{k}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2^{j}}\ \Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\prob\left\{\frac{2^{j}}{\epsilon}T_{k}\leq N_{k}\ \Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\prob\left\{\frac{2^{j}}{\epsilon}T^{*}_{k}\leq N\left(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k-1}+T^{*}_{k}\right]+1\ \Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right\}.

Let
\overline{N}(\cdot)
be the counting process of arrivals in a Poisson process with arrival rate
\Lambda^{\circ}=\Lambda H\big(\underline{p},0\big)
.
From an easy coupling argument, it follows that
\overline{N}\big(T^{*}_{k}\big)\equiv\overline{N}\big(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k-1}+T^{*}_{k}\big]\geq_{\text{st}}N_{k}
, so that

\displaystyle\prob\{{\bm{i}}_{k,j}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\}\leq\prob\left\{\frac{2^{j}}{\epsilon}T^{*}_{k}-1\leq\overline{N}(T^{*}_{k})\right\}.

Now pick
a_{j}={2^{j}T^{*}_{k}}/{\epsilon}
,
\mu=\Lambda^{\circ}T^{*}_{k}
, and
f_{j}=a_{j}/\mu
. Then, using the Chernoff bound for
\overline{N}\left(T^{*}_{k}\right)
, we find

\displaystyle\prob\left\{\overline{N}\left(T^{*}_{k}\right)\geq a_{j}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\mu\left(f_{j}\log(f_{j})-f_{j}+1\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\Lambda^{\circ}T^{*}_{k}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{2^{j}T^{*}_{k}}{\epsilon}\big(j\log 2-\log\left(\Lambda^{\circ}\epsilon\right)-1\big)\right).

Now put
C_{2}:=\log 2/\epsilon
and
C_{3}:=-\log\left(\Lambda^{\circ}\epsilon\right)-1
. Then, for any
\epsilon<\Lambda^{\circ}/e
,
C_{2},C_{3}>0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\prob\left\{\overline{N}\left(T^{*}_{k}\right)\geq a_{j}\right\}\leq\exp\left(\Lambda^{\circ}T^{*}_{k}\right)\exp\left(-C_{2}T^{*}_{k}\ j2^{j}-C_{3}T^{*}_{k}\ 2^{j}\right)\leq\exp\left(\Lambda^{\circ}T^{*}_{k}\right)\exp\left(-C_{2}T^{*}_{k}\ j2^{j}\right).

Upon combining the above, we have arrived at the upper bound

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.b)}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\Lambda^{\circ}T^{*}_{k}\right)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{2^{j+1}}{\epsilon}\exp\left(-C_{2}T^{*}_{k}\ j2^{j}\right)=\exp\left(-\Lambda^{\circ}T^{*}_{k}\right)\frac{2}{\epsilon}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\exp\left(j\log 2-C_{2}T^{*}_{k}\ j2^{j}\right).

We are left with verifying that the expression on the right-hand side is negligible compared to
\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.a)}
as
k\to\infty
.
Since
j\log 2\ll j2^{j}
, there exists
C_{2}^{\prime}<C_{2}
such that
j\log 2-C_{2}T^{*}_{k}\ j2^{j}<-C_{2}^{\prime}T^{*}_{k}\ j2^{j}
.
As a consequence,
\exp\left(j\log 2-C_{2}T^{*}_{k}\ j2^{j}\right)<\exp\left(-C_{2}^{\prime}T^{*}_{k}\ j2^{j}\right)
, which becomes exponentially small as
j\rightarrow\infty
. As we can find a constant
C_{2}^{\prime\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-C_{2}^{\prime}T^{*}_{k}\ j2^{j}\right)\leq C_{2}^{\prime\prime},

we thus conclude that

\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.b)}\leq\mathcal{O}\big(e^{-\Lambda^{\circ}T^{*}_{k}}\big),

which is asymptotically negligible relative to the contribution of
\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.a)}
.
Combining our findings regarding the decay of both terms, we obtain an upper bound to the the decay rate of
\omega_{k}^{\rm(I)}
.
∎

5.2.3
Analysis of
\omega_{k}^{\rm(II)}

We proceed by proving the following lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

As
k\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(II)}=\mathcal{O}\left(\eta_{k-1}\frac{1}{{T^{*}_{k-1}}^{2}}\right).

Proof.

In the analysis of the decay of
\omega_{k}^{\rm(II)}
we use a reasoning that is similar to the one we used for analyzing
\omega_{k}^{\rm(I)}
in Lemma
5.4
, in that we split the quantity of interest into two contributions, as follows:

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(II)}

\displaystyle\leq\exptn\Bigg[\left|\frac{\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}-\frac{\nabla\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]^{2}}\right|\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Bigg]=\omega_{k}^{\rm(II.a)}+\omega_{k}^{\rm(II.b)},

where, with
{{\bm{1}}}_{k}
as defined in the proof of Lemma
5.4
,

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(II.a)}:={\exptn\Bigg[\left|\frac{\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}-\frac{\nabla\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]^{2}}\right|\,{{\bm{1}}}_{k}\,\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Bigg]},

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(II.b)}:={\exptn\Bigg[\left|\frac{\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}-\frac{\nabla\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]^{2}}\right|(1-{{\bm{1}}}_{k})\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Bigg]}.

We start by analyzing the decay of
\omega_{k}^{\rm(II.a)}
. Let
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
lie between
\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})
and
\nabla\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]
. By the multivariate version of Taylor’s theorem, applied to the function
f(x,y)={x}/{y^{2}}
at the point
\left(\nabla\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})],\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}-\frac{\nabla\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\nabla\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right)\cdot\frac{1}{\xi_{2}^{2}}\>-

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left(\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right)\cdot 2\,\frac{\xi_{1}}{\xi_{2}^{3}}

so that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}-\frac{\nabla\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]^{2}}\right|\leq\left|\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\nabla\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right|\left(\frac{1}{\xi_{2}^{2}}+2\,\frac{\xi_{1}}{\xi_{2}^{3}}\right).

Recalling that
\xi_{2}>\epsilon
, and
\xi_{1}\leq G_{\max}
, we find

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(II.a)}

\displaystyle\leq_{\text{a.s.}}\exptn\Bigg[\left|\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\nabla\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right|\left(\frac{1}{\xi_{2}^{2}}+2\frac{\xi_{1}}{\xi_{2}^{3}}\right)\,{{\bm{1}}}_{k}\,\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq_{\text{a.s.}}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}+G_{\max}\frac{2}{\epsilon^{3}}\right)\exptn\left[\left|\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\nabla\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right|\,{{\bm{1}}}_{k}\,\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq_{\text{a.s.}}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}+G_{\max}\frac{2}{\epsilon^{3}}\right)\exptn\Big[\left|\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right|\,{{\bm{1}}}_{k}

\displaystyle\hskip 91.04872pt+\left|\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right|(1-{{\bm{1}}}_{k})\,\big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Big]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}+G_{\max}\frac{2}{\epsilon^{3}}\right)\exptn\left[\left|\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})-\nabla\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]\right|\big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\eta_{k-1}\frac{1}{{T^{*}_{k-1}}^{2}}\right).

We finally aim to show that
\omega_{k}^{\rm(II.b)}
is asymptotically negligible relative to
\omega_{k}^{\rm(II.a)}
. To this end, observe, with
{{\bm{i}}}_{k,j}
as defined in the proof of Lemma
5.4
,

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(II.b)}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\exptn\Bigg[\left|\frac{\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}-\frac{\nabla\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]^{2}}\right|{{\bm{i}}}_{k,j}\,\Big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\Bigg].

On the event
\{{\epsilon}/{2^{j+1}}<\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})\leq{\epsilon}/{2^{j}}\}
, again applying Taylor’s theorem, and with reference to Corollary
3.6
,
\left|\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})\right|\leq G_{\max}
and
\nabla\exptn[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})]<G_{\max}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}-\frac{\nabla\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{\widehat{\nabla A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})}{\widehat{A_{\infty}}(p_{k-1})^{2}}\right|+\left|\frac{\nabla\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p_{k-1})\big]^{2}}\right|\leq G_{\max}\frac{2^{2j+3}}{\epsilon^{2}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\omega_{k}^{\rm(II.b)}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}G_{\max}\frac{2^{2j+3}}{\epsilon^{2}}\,\prob\{{\bm{i}}_{k,j}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\}.

From this point on, we essentially follow the argumentation developed in the analysis of
\omega_{k}^{\rm(I.b)}
. In particular, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}G_{\max}\frac{2^{2j+3}}{\epsilon^{2}}\prob\left\{\frac{2^{j}T^{*}_{k}}{\epsilon}-1\leq\overline{N}(T^{*}_{k})\right\}=\mathcal{O}\big(e^{-\Lambda^{\circ}T^{*}_{k}}\big).

Combining the results we found for
\omega_{k}^{\rm(II.a)}
and
\omega_{k}^{\rm(II.b)}
, we have proven the stated.
∎

6
Regret Analysis

This section is the culmination of the results obtained in the previous sections, in that we present our two main theorems. The first theorem quantifies the convergence of the price iterates
p_{k}
to the optimal price
p^{*}
. The second theorem assesses the
regret
associated with the algorithm given in Subsection
2.3
(relying on the estimates proposed in Section
4
).
While learning the optimal admission price
p^{*}
by following this algorithm, the operator sets sub-optimal admission prices and thereby does not earn optimal revenue. This deficit between the theoretical optimal revenue and the actual revenue generated is called regret. We denote the cumulative regret after
L
iterations of the algorithm by
R(L)
.

Let
N(\cdot)
be the counting process corresponding to effective arrivals and let
W(\cdot)
be the workload process in a M/G/1  +
H(p,V)
queue.
Let
\mathscr{F}=\left(\sigmaf_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
be a filtration such that
\sigmaf_{0}=\emptyset
, and
\sigmaf_{k}
consists of all queueing data from the first
k
iterations of the algorithm, i.e., from time
0
till time
\overline{T}_{k}
.
Then the regret can be written as

\displaystyle R(L)=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\Psi(p^{*})\exptn\left[T_{k}\right]-\exptn\Big[p_{k-1}\exptn\big[N\big(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k}\big]\big|\sigmaf_{k-1}\big]\Big].

We can decompose the above as

\displaystyle R(L)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\Psi(p^{*})T^{*}_{k}-\exptn\Big[p_{k-1}\exptn\big[N\big(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k-1}+T^{*}_{k}\big]\big|\sigmaf_{k-1}\big]\Big]+\sum_{k=1}^{L}\Psi(p^{*})\exptn\left[T_{k}-T^{*}_{k}\right]-\exptn\left[p_{k-1}\right],

where
\exptn\left[T_{k}-T^{*}_{k}\right]=o\left(T^{*}_{k}\right)
and
\exptn\left[p_{k-1}\right]=o\left(\exptn\Big[p_{k-1}\exptn\big[N\big(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k-1}+T^{*}_{k}\big]\big|\sigmaf_{k-1}\big]\Big]\right)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle R(L)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{L}\Psi(p^{*})T^{*}_{k}-\exptn\Big[p_{k-1}\exptn\big[N\big(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k-1}+T^{*}_{k}\big]\big|\sigmaf_{k-1}\big]\Big]\right).

Let
R_{1}(L)
correspond to the regret due to picking a suboptimal price, and
R_{2}(L)
correspond to the regret due to non-stationarity:

R(L)=R_{1}1(L)+R_{2}(L)\ =\sum_{k=1}^{L}R_{1,k}+\sum_{k=1}^{L}R_{2,k},

where

\displaystyle R_{1,k}

\displaystyle:={\Psi(p^{*})T^{*}_{k}-\exptn\Big[\Psi(p_{k-1})\Big]T^{*}_{k}},

\displaystyle R_{2,k}

\displaystyle:={\exptn\Big[\Psi(p_{k-1})\Big]T^{*}_{k}-\exptn\Big[p_{k-1}\exptn\big[N\big(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k-1}+T^{*}_{k}\big]\big|\sigmaf_{k-1}\big]\Big]}.

In order to derive an upper bound on
R(L)
, we analyze
R_{1}(L)
and
R_{2}(L)
in the following two subsections.

6.1
Analysis for
R_{1}(L)

The following lemma establishes an upper bound on
R_{1}(L)
.
Define

\displaystyle\sigma:=\max\left\{\frac{1}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(\underline{p})\big]},\left|\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(\overline{p})]}-\overline{p}\ G_{\max}\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(\underline{p})]^{2}}\right|\right\}.

Lemma 6.1
.

For any
L
,

\displaystyle R_{1}(L)\leq\sigma\sum_{k=1}^{L}T^{*}_{k}\exptn\big|p^{*}-p_{k-1}\big|.

Proof.

By the mean-value theorem for random variables
[
26
]
, for a random variable
\xi_{k-1}
taking values in
(0,1)
, and for
\widetilde{p}_{k-1}=\xi_{k-1}p^{*}+\big(1-\xi_{k-1}\big)p_{k-1}
,

\displaystyle R_{1,k}

\displaystyle=T^{*}_{k}\exptn\big[\Psi(p^{*})-\Psi(p_{k-1})\big]=T^{*}_{k}\exptn\Big[(p^{*}-p_{k-1})\exptn\big[\nabla\Psi\big(\widetilde{p}_{k-1}\big)\big]\Big]\leq T^{*}_{k}\exptn\Big[\big|p^{*}-p_{k-1}\big|\exptn\big|\nabla\Psi\big(\widetilde{p}_{k-1}\big)\big|\Big].

With reference to Corollary
3.6
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(\overline{p})]}-\overline{p}\ G_{\max}\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(\underline{p})]^{2}}\leq\nabla\Psi(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(p)]}-p\frac{\nabla\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(p)\big]}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(p)]^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(\underline{p})\big]}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle R_{1,k}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{\exptn\big[A_{\infty}(\underline{p})\big]},\left|\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(\overline{p})]}-\overline{p}\ G_{\max}\frac{1}{\exptn[A_{\infty}(\underline{p})]^{2}}\right|\right\}T^{*}_{k}\exptn\big|p^{*}-p_{k-1}\big|

\displaystyle=\sigma\ T^{*}_{k}\exptn\big|p^{*}-p_{k-1}\big|.

From this, the result follows.
∎

6.2
Analysis for
R_{2}(L)

In this subsection we state and prove an upper bound on
R_{2}(L)
.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any
L
,

\displaystyle R_{2}(L)\leq\overline{p}\ \frac{\Lambda H\big(\underline{p},0\big)}{1-\gamma_{1}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\exptn\big|W_{\infty}(p_{k-1})-W\big(\overline{T}_{k-1}\big)\big|.

Proof.

In order to dominate the expectation above, we construct a probability space
\big(\Omega,\sigmag_{k-1},\prob\big)
and on it, we couple two M/G/1  +
H(p,V)
queues with the same admission price
p_{k-1}
, but with different initial workloads.

1.

The first queue with initial workload
W_{\infty}(p_{k-1})
and counting process
N_{1}(\cdot)
of effective arrivals.

2.

The second queue with initial workload
W\left(\overline{T}_{k-1}\right)
and counting process
N_{2}(\cdot)
of effective arrivals.

Then,

\displaystyle R_{2,k}

\displaystyle=\exptn\Big[\Psi(p_{k-1})\Big]T^{*}_{k}-\exptn\Big[p_{k-1}\exptn\big[N\big(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k-1}+T^{*}_{k}\big]\big|\sigmaf_{k-1}\big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\exptn\Big[p_{k-1}\exptn\big[N_{1}\big(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k-1}+T^{*}_{k}\big]-N_{2}\big(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k-1}+T^{*}_{k}\big]\big|\sigmag_{k-1}\big]\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\overline{p}\ \exptn\Big[\exptn\Big[\left|N_{1}\big(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k-1}+T^{*}_{k}\big]-N_{2}\big(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k-1}+T^{*}_{k}\big]\right|\ \Big|\sigmag_{k-1}\Big]\Big].

From Proposition
3.9
(d), we know that

\displaystyle\exptn\Big[\big|N_{1}\big(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k-1}+T^{*}_{k}\big]-N_{2}\big(\overline{T}_{k-1},\overline{T}_{k-1}+T^{*}_{k}\big]\big|\ \Big|\sigmag_{k-1}\Big]\leq\frac{\Lambda H\big(\underline{p},0\big)}{1-\gamma_{1}}\exptn\left|W_{\infty}\left(p_{k-1}\right)-W\left(\overline{T}_{k-1}\right)\right|,

from which we can conclude that

8
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we analyzed a dynamic pricing problem in a single-server queueing system, where the objective was to maximize the expected revenue per unit time. Customers were allowed to balk in response to high congestion, which introduced a non-trivial interaction between the pricing policy and the effective arrival process. Importantly, the service provider had access only to data from effective arrivals — those customers who chose to join the queue — making the system partially observable and inherently state-dependent.

To address this challenge, we developed a stochastic gradient descent algorithm that dynamically adjusted the price based solely on observable information. A key contribution of our work was the design of a novel IPA-based procedure, which consistently estimated the stationary effective arrival rate. This estimator was then used to construct an iterative learning algorithm that, under mild regularity conditions, was shown to converge asymptotically to the optimal price. Numerical experiments further confirmed the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed algorithm.

While our work makes several significant contributions, there remain multiple avenues for future research. We highlight a few key directions here:

\circ

A natural next step is to extend the analysis to multi-server queueing systems. This poses significant challenges, as the coupling arguments developed in Section
3.2
for queues with different initial workloads do not straightforwardly generalize to multi-server settings (cf. the observations in
[
5
]
). Substantial adaptations of the theoretical framework would therefore be required.

\circ

Another important direction is to consider scenarios where both the arrival rate and the customers’ disutility threshold distribution are unknown. If the disutility threshold distribution is parametric with unknown parameters, existing estimation procedures, such as those developed in
[
4
,
13
]
, could be incorporated. However, this would introduce an additional layer of complexity, as the algorithm would need to update not only the price but also estimates of the arrival rate and patience distribution at each iteration to compute the gradient estimator accurately. In cases where the disutility threshold distribution is non-parametric, this problem naturally invites approaches from reinforcement learning.

\circ

Assumption
2.3
requires that
\Psi(\cdot)
is a strongly concave function. An open problem of theoretical interest is whether Theorem
6.4
and thereby Theorem
6.5
, or some weaker versions of them, can also be established when
\Psi(\cdot)
is not strongly concave.
