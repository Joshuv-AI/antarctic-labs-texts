Activity-based planning of primary care utilization in a model of prospective demand for workforce
Authors: Ilja Stepanov, Igor Sheiman
Published: 2025

Introduction

A shortage of primary health care (PHC) workers has become an increasingly significant health issue in many countries [1], including Russia [2]. Despite holding a leading position globally in terms of the number of physicians per capita, the country is currently experiencing an acute deficit of healthcare professionals. Studies indicate that this is a structural problem: a shortage of physicians in outpatient settings coexists with a surplus in hospitals; some specialties face shortages, while others exceed demand; the limited functions of medical nurses create additional demand for doctors; and there is substantial regional inequality [2, 3].

These imbalances are largely attributed to shortcomings in the planning system. While Russia has established a system for current health workforce planning, it lacks strategic planning for the medium and long term [3]. The government has set a goal to create such a system, and an expert group has been established to develop a methodology for strategic planning.

In the literature, four types of strategic planning models are distinguished: health workforce-to-population ratio planning, utilization-based planning, needs-based planning, and target-based planning. Needs-based models are promising as they account for patient-oriented medical care expectations, but these expectations are challenging to evaluate and predict [4]. The Russian system of current workforce planning is based on indicators of care volumes, while a long-term planning system is still being developed. Therefore, it is unrealistic to begin with complex, multi-factor needs-based models. Instead, a utilization-based model of strategic planning has been proposed for Russia by the expert group.

This model is based on the triad of: morbidity, utilization of health care, and labor per unit of care utilization (staffing norms). This paper focuses on forecasting future needs in PHC utilization, specifically the number of physician visits per capita. In addition to the traditional approach of determining care utilization based on general factors (mostly demographic), an original approach is suggested that assesses the impact of expected policy activities aimed at strengthening primary care. Key activities are identified, including those that potentially reduce the need for physicians.

The objective of this paper is to present a new model for strategic planning of the need for primary care workers in Russia, as well as its most critical element—a methodology for anticipating the number of physician visits, and therefore the number of primary care physicians needed to serve these visits.

Within the framework of strategic planning, a model for the supply of health workers, accounting for their influx and exit, has also been developed. A demonstration evaluation has been conducted; however, due to the limited scope of the case study, only the activity-based approach to evaluating PHC utilization for determining demand for primary care physicians is presented.

Basic elements of the model for prospective demand for PHC workforce

The demand model is estimated for the entire country and its regions based on a new integrated forecasting model. The model is focused on estimating the number of primary care physicians that includes generalists of a so called “district service” (district therapists, district pediatricians, general practitioners) and outpatient specialists. Contrary to post-communist Central and Eastern European countries, Russian primary care is based on highly consolidated service provision in big multispecialty facilities and the dominance of public ownership. General practice is still in its infancy, while an attempt has been made to compensate for this with a growing number of specialists in primary-care settings. The overspecialization is a specific characteristic of primary care organization in Russia [5, 6]. This characteristic was taken into account in long-term planning.

Demand forecast consists of the following elements (successive steps).

Determining the projected population size and morbidity

In the model, official government demographic forecasts are used, and morbidity is estimated by disease class and extrapolated to the forecast period. Assumptions are made regarding deviations in morbidity dynamics for individual classes, either upward or downward. Aggregated population and morbidity data are used without breakdowns by age and sex groups, which may reduce the accuracy of projected parameters but facilitates the assessment of the macro impact of individual policy activities on PHC utilization and workforce demand. Further development and validation of the approach will determine the necessary level of data disaggregation to be used in subsequent modeling initiative.

Assessing aggregate PHC utilization

A major parameter—the prospective number of total physician visits per capita per year—is determined by assessing the impact of general factors such as population and morbidity dynamics, the emergence of new types of medical organizations, major technological changes, shifts in the ratio of outpatient and inpatient care, and health expenditure trends. The utilization vector is determined for each factor on a scenario basis.

The future aggregate utilization under the most realistic scenario serves as a control value for subsequent calculations of the impact of individual policy activities. If the sum of their impacts significantly deviates from the aggregate value, an analysis is conducted to identify the reasons for the discrepancy, and adjustments are made to the disaggregated estimates.

A preliminary assessment suggests that factors increasing the volume of primary health care in the medium term will dominate over those reducing it. Under the preferred scenario, the need for physician visits per capita increases from 7.9 in 2023 to 9.0 in 2030. In the subsequent period, up to 2036, this need will gradually decrease to 8.8 visits.

Evaluating the activity-based number of physician visits

A set of policy activities is identified based on priorities reflecting the most significant problems in primary care, as presented in previous studies [5, 6]. Two groups of activities are identified: Group A, which increases demand for physician visits, and Group B, which decreases demand. These activities are adjusted for 2036 (Table 1).

Evaluating physician visits across specialties

The total number of visits is detailed for individual physician specialties using coefficients for each type of specialist. These coefficients indicate deviations in the number of visits per capita relative to the average for all specialties. Values are determined using a regression model that measures the dependency between the number of visits per capita for a specific specialty (dependent variable) and morbidity by disease classes (predictors) over 2012–2023.

Specialists' involvement in treating other diseases is also considered. For example, the number of visits to obstetricians and gynecologists is estimated using a regression model that accounts for diseases in multiple classes, such as “pregnancy, childbirth, and postpartum period,” “neoplasms,” “skin diseases,” and “diseases of the genitourinary system.” This approach results in higher coefficients for certain specialties.

A special approach is used for district service physicians, who have a catchment population (patient list). Targets for this indicator are set, aiming to reduce the number of people served by one district physician by 10% in 2030 and by 20% in 2036 compared to 2023 (1877 people per district physician). The total number of primary care physicians is the sum of outpatient specialists and district physicians.

Regional estimates account for unique territorial characteristics, such as population density, which affects physician workload. National standards are adjusted using coefficients determined by the Federal Ministry of Health.

Determining the number of medical nurses

The predicted ratio between physicians and nurses is used to estimate the number of nurses. Changes in this ratio result from: (a) Delegating some physician functions to nurses [8], (b) New factors driving demand for nurses [9].

In 2023, the ratio of nurses to physicians in PHC in Russia was 1.58:1 [10]. The average ratio for 37 OECD countries is 2.5:1, while post-Soviet countries average 2.2:1 [11]. The preferred scenario aims to reach a ratio of 2.1:1 by 2036 and 1.8:1 by 2030.

Discussion

A proposed methodology is an adaptation of the traditional utilization-based model of health workforce demand. It seeks to partially overcome a limitation of the traditional model—its reliance on past and current utilization patterns, because it overlooks unmet needs or instances of overutilization of care.

The proposed new model uses time series analysis of past trends to estimate prospective population and morbidity as an initial basis for prospective planning. The model seeks to address underutilization and overutilization for the base period, and the impact of current and planned policy activities to address these issues is estimated for future demand. This assessment is based on prior research on primary care developments in Russia and internationally [3, 5].

The model allows for approximating workforce demand estimates to align with the actual processes of current and future PHC development. Unlike routine econometric analysis, this approach determines potential causal links between policy activities and care utilization.

In the Russian context, the implementation of the proposed model is facilitated by the government’s standardization of the number of visits per capita for certain types of care delivery. This standardization applies to policy activities in Group A (A1–A4). For these activities, the selected planning method is preferable, as it does not require statistical methods involving probabilistic assessments of impact factors. However, some activities are not standardized (e.g., developing general practice, delegating physician functions to nurses, increasing the availability of medicines in PHC) and therefore require the use of statistical tools.

The assessment of demand for specific physician specialties is based on time series and regression analysis. Trends in morbidity for each class of diseases are used to predict service utilization, including for “related” classes of diseases. Even in this case, total and activity-based estimates of utilization are used as benchmarks. The sum of estimates for specific physician specialties is adjusted to align with the total utilization estimate.

The potential of the proposed approach depends on the correct selection of priority activities that have the greatest impact on PHC volume and physician demand. These activities are determined by specific problems and features of primary care organization in the country, as well as current and expected priorities for its development. We selected activities planned by the government, along with additional activities dictated by long-term needs.

The advantage of this approach lies in its relative simplicity for strategic workforce planning. It uses measurable indicators of physician visits per capita, which clearly explain the expected changes in PHC organization.

The issue of uncertainty in long-term planning in this model [15] is addressed by developing expert assumptions about the parameters of policy activity impacts under various scenarios. These assumptions are quantified in terms of the impact of each activity on workforce needs. A preferred scenario is selected for each activity individually. The approach of simultaneously selecting scenarios for all activities was rejected due to its high complexity.

Validation of the model is recommended with the involvement of external experts. The goal is to reach a consensus on the set of policy activities and the assumptions regarding the impact of each activity. Additionally, the compatibility of aggregate estimates with estimates of the impact of specific activities should be validated. A substantial gap between these estimates may indicate the need to adjust the assumptions.

The major limitation of the model is its reliance on prior, arbitrary assumptions about the demand for medical nurses and other health workers. Using the ratio of nurses to physicians is a simplified approach that should eventually be replaced by direct evaluations of demand for each group of personnel using specific models. However, this limitation reflects the complexity of the roles and interdependence of various service provider groups, which are influenced by numerous factors and are difficult to predict. Simultaneously planning the demand for all workforce groups would greatly complicate the process. Therefore, we decided to start with evaluating physician demand as a foundation for building models for other health workforce groups with more detailed and empirically based assumptions.

Additionally, some assumptions about the impact of certain activities were made without in-depth analysis. This limitation was partially addressed during the practical evaluation stage of the model. Some original assumptions were adjusted based on additional empirical data and input from new experts. It is worth noting that modeling and evaluation are separated in time, and gaps in empirical data are common at various planning stages.

Similarly, the lack of age- and sex-specific morbidity data during the modeling stage can be compensated for by assessing the impact of individual policy activities on PHC utilization and workforce demand. Further development and validation of the approach will determine the degree of necessary data disaggregation.

The major health policy recommendation is to link the strategic planning of health workforce to the selected policy activities to strengthen primary care.

Conclusion

The proposed model for strategic planning for the primary care workforce is based on care utilization models. In addition to the traditional approach of statistical analysis, this model focuses on assessing judgements from experts about the qualitative impact of current and expected policy activities. In the context of Russia, enhancing the capacity of PHC settings and expanding chronic disease management are the major drivers of increased demand for physician visits per capita and the number of primary care physicians. However, this model can also be useful for other countries implementing or planning medium- and long-term policy activities with substantial potential to increase or reduce workforce demand.

The major strength of the model is its relative simplicity, while its primary weakness lies in its dependency on the correct selection of priority activities and assumptions about forecast parameters.
