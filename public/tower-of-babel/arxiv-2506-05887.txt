Explainability in Context: A Multilevel Framework Aligning AI Explanations with Stakeholder with LLMs
Marilyn Bello, Rafael Bello, Maria-Matilde García, Ann Nowé, Iván Sevillano-García, Francisco Herrera

Abstract
The growing application of artificial intelligence in sensitive domains has intensified the demand for systems that are not only accurate but also explainable and trustworthy. Although explainable AI (XAI) methods have proliferated, many do not consider the diverse audiences that interact with AI systems: from developers and domain experts to end-users and society. This paper addresses how trust in AI is influenced by the design and delivery of explanations and proposes a multilevel framework that aligns explanations with the epistemic, contextual, and ethical expectations of different stakeholders. The framework consists of three layers: algorithmic and domain-based, human-centered, and social explainability. We highlight the emerging role of Large Language Models (LLMs) in enhancing the social layer by generating accessible, natural language explanations. Through illustrative case studies, we demonstrate how this approach facilitates technical fidelity, user engagement, and societal accountability, reframing XAI as a dynamic, trust-building process.

Introduction

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) has become a foundational area of research in the pursuit of trustworthy AI systems. As machine learning models, particularly black-box systems, are increasingly deployed in sensitive and high-impact domains such as healthcare, finance, and justice, the ability to interpret and understand their behavior is no longer optional, but essential. XAI addresses the critical need for transparency, allowing stakeholders to assess model decisions, detect biases, ensure compliance, and build user trust
[
1
]
. According to Ali et al.
[
2
]
, while significant progress has been made in developing explainability methods, key challenges remain in aligning these techniques with real-world trust requirements. Their work underscores that achieving trustworthy AI requires explainability approaches that are not only technically sound, but also contextually meaningful and user-centered.

Recent work highlights that XAI must go beyond mere model interpretability and embrace a broader, human-centered vision that incorporates the goals, expectations, and values of diverse stakeholders across the AI lifecycle
[
1
]
. This evolving perspective reframes XAI as a dynamic socio-technical process, not merely a technical add-on. The definition of XAI given in
[
3
]
:
“Given an audience, an Explainable AI is one that produces details or reasons to make its functioning clear or easy to understand”
highlights the importance of considering the target audience when generating explanations.

According to Bodria et al.
[
4
]
, explanations in the context of black-box machine learning models are defined as
“human-interpretable descriptions of the internal logic of a model or the reasons for its predictions.”
These explanations aim to make opaque decision-making processes understandable to stakeholders by highlighting relevant input features, representative examples, or simplified decision rules. Their work emphasizes the importance of tailoring explanation strategies to specific tasks and audiences, considering the trade-offs between fidelity, interpretability, and computational cost.
In
[
5
]
, a series of misconceptions and fallacies are identified that, according to their criteria, affect the development and application of XAI. Among the key issues highlighted, it is pointed out that there is no universally suitable explanation method for all cases. Furthermore, according to
[
6
]
, identifying the most effective explanation can be challenging. The variety and diversity of XAI methods makes it difficult to select the most appropriate one to explain an intelligent system. Hence, it is beneficial to know about the existence of different XAI methods and to have a guide on how to generate such explanations.

There have been several categorization proposals for XAI methods from several perspectives, each of them complementary and providing a different view of the field.
In
[
7
]
, three categories are proposed to classify the different approaches within XAI based on the type of explanation they generate. The first category encompasses methods that generate explanations by identifying the parts of the input that are relevant to the solution produced by the intelligent system, called attribution methods. The second category includes methods that provide declarative explanations, which explicitly and formally exposes knowledge. The third category comprises methods that produce explanations in natural language, using representations that are more accessible and understandable to humans.

Therefore, a fundamental challenge persists: most existing approaches prioritize technical transparency without adequately considering the diverse cognitive, contextual, and ethical needs of different users. As AI systems permeate high-stakes and socially sensitive domains, there is an urgent need to move beyond one-size-fits-all explanations. Generic or overly technical explanations risk alienating non-expert users, undermining trust, and failing to meet regulatory or societal expectations. What remains lacking is a structured methodology for tailoring explanations to distinct audiences, ensuring that they are not only technically faithful, but also contextually meaningful, ethically grounded, and socially relevant. This paper addresses this gap by proposing a multilevel audience-aware framework for explainability that aligns explanation strategies with the varying goals, knowledge levels, and trust requirements of stakeholders. To this end, we propose a solution to these issues through a multilevel framework, which can be summarized as:

1.

algorithmic and domain-based level, responsible for the fidelity between the model and the explanation,

2.

human-centered level, responsible for integrating human and machine expertise,
and

3.

social explainability level, responsible of making the explanation understandable to non-expert users.

Figure
1
shows how these levels are integrated and interact. Our proposal focuses on explaining the predictions of a black-box model for individual data instances. We begin by generating explanations based on the inference process and domain knowledge, where the first level of the framework is applied. Then, we extend this with interactive explanations, where users actively contribute to shaping or refining the interpretation with the second level. Finally, we add a natural language explanation module to enhance the system’s transparency and foster its social acceptance. This natural language module is particularly relevant in the context of Large Language Models (LLMs), which can generate human-readable explanations that are more accessible to non-expert users.

This multilevel approach reflects how AI systems are actually adopted in real world contexts, where explanations evolve alongside users’ familiarity, system sophistication, and broader social demands. Our proposal combines existing XAI techniques with a timeline-oriented framework that helps practitioners align explanation strategies with the gradual development of trust, understanding, and accountability. To our knowledge, this is the first XAI approach to explicitly treat explanation generation as a dynamic, audience-aware process that increases in semantic depth and social relevance over time.

Background on Explainable AI and Audience-Centered Explanations

To lay the foundation for our proposed multilevel framework, this section provides essential background on the landscape of XAI and the importance of tailoring explanations to diverse user needs. First, in Section

2.1
Explainable AI: Models and Motivations

As AI systems become more complex and pervasive, the need for transparency and interpretability has become increasingly important. The field of XAI has emerged to address this need, focusing on developing methods and techniques that make the functioning and decisions of complex models more transparent and understandable to humans. Depending on the knowledge representation, we can distinguish two categories:
white-box
models and
black-box
models
[
8
,
9
]
.

•

White-box models are those that can be easily interpreted by humans. Examples of these models are linear regression models, decision trees, association rules, and instance-based models, since the reasoning behind their predictions can be easily understood by humans. Each of these models has a clear and interpretable structure, such as coefficients in linear regression or paths in decision trees, which allows users to trace how input features contribute to the final prediction.

•

In contrast, black-box models are those that are difficult to interpret, such as deep neural networks, random forests, or support vector machines. These models are often more complex and involve intricate relationships between input features and predictions, making it challenging for humans to understand the reasoning behind their decisions. Even though we have the mathematical formulation of the black-box model, the relationships between input features and the predictions can make it difficult to understand how the model arrived at a specific decision.

Although the distinction between white-box and black-box models may seem clear, recent studies have shown that the boundary between these categories is often blurred. For instance, even models traditionally considered interpretable, such as linear regression or decision trees, can become difficult for humans to understand when they involve a large number of features or highly complex structures. Also, the construction of mixed interpretable and non-interpretable designs, such as neuro-symbolic systems, can lead to models that are partially interpretable.

The development of methods to extract rules from the knowledge collected in artificial neural networks, as described in
[
10
]
, represents an important precedent in the direction of explainable artificial intelligence. Later, the term XAI was first introduced in
[
11
]
as part of academic research that required a comprehensive understanding of the underlying AI system. It was not until
[
3
]
that the field of XAI was formally defined as a dedicated research area focused on developing methods and techniques to make the functioning and decisions of complex models more transparent and understandable to humans and focused on the audience.

Focused on explanations, in
[
12
]
, four reasons for the development of XAI are defined:

1.

explanations to justify a decision,

2.

explanations for control situations,

3.

explanations to improve a process, and

4.

explanations to help discover knowledge in an application domain.

Each reason has a different goal, a different target audience, and different expectations. XAI must be developed considering these differences, offering a suitable explanation for each case. According to
[
13
]
, an explanation process should be developed by offering multiple explanations, considering the different addressees, and building the explanations cooperatively through a human-machine dialogue. They stated that XAI has the following motivations:

•

To evaluate intelligent system
to ensure that the system behaves as expected and to detect possible biases.

•

To improve the system’s work
to gain security against possible attacks.

•

To learn from the system
to extract the knowledge discovered by the system.

•

To comply with current legislation
to respond to legal issues.

•

To help the user accept the technology
to make them understand how the system works, to avoid rejection due to distrust of the system.

This led us to focus the attention on the audience that must use and manage the explanations. Biecek and Samek
[
14
]
present a position paper with a very interesting division of the XAI area into two complementary cultures that focus attention in audience.

•

Human/value-oriented explanations called BLUE XAI (responsiBle models, Legal issues, trUst in predictions, Ethical issues).

•

Model/validation-oriented explanations approach, known as RED XAI (Research on data, Explore models, Debug models).

This categorization focuses on the purpose and audience of the explanations, rather than the technical details of the methods themselves. RED XAI emphasizes the need for researchers and developers to understand and improve AI systems, while BLUE XAI focuses on the ethical and legal implications of AI, aiming to build trust and accountability for all potential end-users of AI systems.

As noted by the authors, RED XAI emerges as a compelling but relatively untapped dimension within the broader XAI landscape. This perspective prioritizes in-depth analysis of data, thorough examination of model behavior, and effective debugging to support system dependability. In Figure
2
, this is visually represented by the red hue, which corresponds to technical stakeholders such as researchers, developers, and system designers. The figure also includes two shades of blue, symbolizing varying degrees of expertise among audiences focused on human-centric values.

The RED XAI paradigm holds substantial promise for enhancing the robustness and safety of AI technologies, offering a rich area for further investigation. By equipping model developers with targeted feedback, especially during model development and training, RED XAI provides practical insights that can improve system performance and transparency. Its commitment to aligning explanations with both data integrity and the internal mechanics of the model positions it as a valuable instrument for cultivating trust and promoting accountability across AI-driven applications.

[

-

-

-

-

What minimum changes must be made to an input to obtain another prediction?

-

How far can an input be modified to keep the exact prediction?

Each of these questions requires a different approach, even when some of them can be answered with the same method.

Explanations can be presented in several ways. The most common ones, according to several authors
[
3
,
6
]
, are:

1.

Numerical explanations, which measures the contribution of the input variables to the model output. This contribution quantifies the sensitivity of a feature to the model output by ranking the variables that most influenced the solution.

2.

Textual explanations, which uses natural language to ensure that humans can easily understand the explanation.

3.

Visual explanations, which uses visual artifacts that highlight specific areas of an image or words in a text.

4.

Explanations by example, which extracts prototypical samples on data previously validated that relate to the output generated by a given model.

5.

Counterfactual examples, which presents cases that are similar to the original input but lead to a different output. This type of explanation is useful for understanding the boundaries of the model’s decision-making process.

6.

Explanations are simplified, which builds an interpretable model on top of the black-box model. This category of methods aims to replace the decision function with an approximate self-explanatory local model.

7.

Rules, which illustrates the decision-making process of a model step by step. They are schematic and logical, more structured than visual or textual explanations.

The analysis presented in
[
29

Regardless of the form of explanation representation, we can distinguish two ways of presenting explanations: statically, where explanations do not change in response to any user demand, and interactively, where different types of explanation are iteratively considered with the aim of satisfying different user demands
[
60
]
. Those explanation presentations are not mutually exclusive, and they can be combined to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the model’s behavior.

3
A Multilevel Framework for Audience-Aware Explainability

This section presents the multilevel explainability framework that generates explanations that are relevant to the target audience and the type of explanation to be generated. This framework is composed of three explainability components: (1) algorithmic and domain-based, which is responsible for generating explanations based on the learned model and domain knowledge, (2) human-centered, which can be seen as an interactive process that integrates human and machine expertise, and (3) societal centered, designed to generate explanations that are tailored to the needs and expectations of different stakeholders. These components structure the explanation process for an intelligent system, from model behavior to user understanding. The central argument of this approach is that explainability is not merely a technical feature, but a relational and ethical requirement for building trust in AI. A layered approach to explanation, rather than a single method, is necessary for the adoption of trustworthy AI.
Ilustrative examples are introduced to show the potential usefulness of the multilevel framework.

3.1
General description of the Multilevel Framework for Explainability

In this section, we show how the three-level framework to generate explanations works and how they interact. Each level is designed to address different aspects of explainability. Those levels are defined as follows:

•

The first level,
Algorithmic and domain-informed explainability
, is responsible for ensuring that the explanation is built using information from the learned model and including domain knowledge.

This level focuses on generating explanations that are faithful to the model’s behavior and aligned with domain-specific knowledge. It uses techniques such as feature importance, rule-based explanations, and example-based explanations to provide a clear understanding of how the model makes decisions. This perspective aligns closely with the goals of RED XAI, which emphasizes the importance of deeply understanding model internals, validating outputs through data exploration, and enabling systematic debugging. RED XAI supports this level by guiding developers and researchers in generating technically sound and diagnostically useful explanations, especially during the development and training phases. Its emphasis on interpretability grounded in algorithmic and data-level evidence enhances the reliability of the explanations produced, making this level not only the foundation of the framework but also a key contributor to AI robustness and trustworthiness.

•

In the second level,
Human-centered and interactive explainability
, the explanation is built through the interaction between the intelligent system and stakeholders. This level emphasizes the importance of user-centered design, where explanations are tailored to the specific needs and expectations of different stakeholders. This level has the goal of integrating human and machine expertise, allowing users to refine explanations based on their knowledge and experience. This level is intended to also allow expert users to refine the behavior of the system, providing feedback to the system and allowing it.

This human-centered approach resonates with the principles of BLUE XAI, which focuses on aligning explanations with human values, ethical considerations, and the need to build trust among stakeholders by making AI systems understandable, fair, and accountable.

•

The third level,
Societal transparency and trust
, aims to reach a level of transparency that allows evaluation of the ethical and legal aspects at the level of end-users and society. This level has the objective of ensuring that the explanations are understandable to non-expert users and making sure that the system’s behavior is aligned with societal values and norms. It uses natural language explanations to communicate the model’s behavior based on the explanations generated in the first two levels. Moreover, it also fully embodies the BLUE XAI philosophy by prioritizing transparency, ethical alignment, and trust for non-expert users and society at large, ensuring that AI systems are not only explainable but also socially accountable.

The relation between the three levels is shown in Figure
1
. The first level provides the foundation for the explanations, which are then refined and adapted at the second level through stakeholder interaction. This interaction may involve feedback loops, where stakeholders can ask questions or request clarifications, leading to more tailored and relevant explanations. The third level builds on the explanations generated in the first two levels, ensuring that they are socially acceptable and aligned with ethical standards. This level emphasizes the importance of the societal context and the need for explanations that resonate with a broader audience, including end users and society.

This multilevel approach reflects how intelligent systems are actually adopted in real world contexts, where explanations evolve alongside users’ familiarity, system sophistication, and broader social demands. Our proposal combines existing XAI techniques with a timeline-oriented framework that helps practitioners align explanation strategies with the gradual development of trust, understanding, and accountability. To our knowledge, this is the first XAI approach to explicitly treat explanation generation as a dynamic, audience-aware process that increases in semantic depth and social relevance over time. The following subsections discuss the three explainability levels of the proposed framework.

3.2
Level 1: Algorithmic and Domain-Informed Explainability

The first level of our framework focuses on generating explanations based directly on the trained model and incorporating knowledge of the application domain. This level is essential for ensuring that the explanations are faithful to the model’s behavior and aligned with domain-specific knowledge. We show on Figure
3
an illustration of this level, which highlights the relationship between the model, the domain knowledge, and the generated explanations.

Figure 3:
Algorithmic and Domain-Based Explainability level.

Three main families of methods are used to generate explanations based directly on the trained model:

•

Inherently interpretable models:
These include decision trees, linear regression, rule-based systems, and case-based reasoning. They offer direct transparency into how decisions are made, although complexity can still reduce interpretability.

•

Post-hoc explainability:
These methods approximate the reasoning of black-box models. They can be further divided into:

–

Feature attribution methods:
Examples include SHAP
[
31
]
, LIME
[
30
]
, LRP
[
38
]
, and Grad-CAM
[
39
]
, which attribute influence to input features on model output. These approaches can reveal feature importance, generate heatmaps, or highlight relevant text.

–

Example-based explanations:
These methods provide explanations by presenting specific instances or examples that illustrate the model’s behavior, such as prototypes or counterfactuals. They help users understand the decision-making process by showing similar cases or hypothetical changes that lead to different outcomes.

One way to improve these algorithmic explanations is to supplement them with domain knowledge
[
61
]
. Domain knowledge enriches the explanations with semantic information by introducing vocabulary and terminology from the application domain and verifying the consistency of the explanations with the expectations and experiences of the domain experts.

This line of work structures the learning process by introducing an explicit layer of human-interpretable concepts between the input and the prediction

[

All of these alternatives can provide explanations that align with the behavior of the learned model and can be complemented by each other to generate more comprehensive explanations. The choice of the approach depends on the specific context and requirements of the explanation task.

Although these explanations confer algorithmic explanations and are based on domain knowledge, there are still some limitations that need to be addressed, such as the need for explanations that are tailored to the specific needs and expectations of different stakeholders. This is where the second level of our framework comes into play, focusing on human-centered explainability.

3.3
Level 2: Human-Centered and Interactive Explainability

On this section, we describe the second level of our framework. This level focuses on generating explanations through interaction with stakeholders. It recognizes that explanations are dynamic processes that evolve through dialogue between the intelligent system and its users. This dialogue allows at some point to reach a human-feedback loop, where the system can adapt its explanations based on user feedback and preferences. In Figure

, we illustrate the interaction between the intelligent system and stakeholders, highlighting the importance of user feedback in shaping the explanations.

The generation of explanations in this level could be developed as an interactive process structured in domain analysis, requirements analysis, and interaction design, where it is possible to establish a bidirectional relationship with the first level to reconstruct the explanation interactively according to the user’s point of view and interest. However, according to
[
72
]
, designing an interactive explanation system that satisfies the interests of different target audiences is a complex task, but it can be gradually and partially developed in such a way that it meets the expectations of stakeholders.

This could be done following two approaches, one in which patterns of explanations are conformed from use cases, and another in which the explanation of each case is built interactively. In the first case, it is possible to get a preliminary insight into the kind of explanation that users expect to receive from the system by asking users to provide explanations themselves based on the use cases. In the other case, interactive interfaces allow users to freely explore the algorithms by changing the values of the model parameters and showing the impact of these changes on the inferences made by the intelligent system. In addition, users can obtain information by analyzing different combinations of inputs. The interaction in this stage is focused on experts or, at least, on users with some knowledge of the system, as managing the interaction requires a certain level of understanding. These approaches allow expert users to refine the behavior of the system, providing feedback to it. Since these XAI systems are designed to help end users decide certain actions, these explanations should at least be understandable to users with domain knowledge, but not necessarily with technical knowledge. In this context, the explanations generated at this level are more user-centered and tailored to the specific needs and expectations of certain stakeholders. These cases may be one of the critical societal domains such as healthcare, finance, education, and public administration. Each domain expert may use the XAI system to decide on tasks in which far-reaching consequences may affect individuals and communities.

However, even if the expert understands the model, or the explanation provided by the system, it is not guaranteed that the social impact of the decision is understood by non-expert audiences. In this context, explanations must go beyond technical transparency or user-level understanding. They must address broader societal concerns such as fairness, accountability, trust, and ethical alignment. For this purpose, the third level of the framework is designed to extend the explanations to nonexpert users and society, ensuring that the explanations are accessible and meaningful for all users.

3.4
Level 3: Societal Transparency and Trust

In this section, we present the final level of our XAI framework. This level aims to bridge the gap between AI outputs and collective human values by fostering public oversight, enabling informed debate, and supporting democratic legitimacy. They are essential for cultivating trust among diverse end users, ensuring that AI systems operate in socially acceptable, legally compliant, and culturally sensitive ways.

In this light, explanations must evolve from merely technical or interactive narratives to those that address societal-level impacts. This third level acknowledges that the widespread adoption of AI technologies demands a layer of explanation that speaks to social legitimacy, ethical acceptability, and cultural resonance. For example, for users, the distinction is made in
“understand the advice, verify fair decisions, and gain scientific knowledge”

“trust in the model itself, rely on the decision”

Societal explanations for users and society are fundamental. We can consider the following aspects:

•

Societal explanation as collective accountability:
Although individual explanations may suffice for personal decision making, societal explanations function as tools for public oversight. Mechanisms such as citizen juries, regulatory sandboxes, and participatory audits are emerging as essential tools in this space. They embed the explanation in institutional practices, allowing affected communities to contest, reinterpret, or reshape the role of AI in their lives. This is related to
understanding the advice, verifying fair decisions, and gaining scientific knowledge
.

•

Reconceptualizing explanation as social dialogue:
Social explanations must inform and engage. They need to support civic trust and encourage critical discourse around AI. This is related to
trusting the model itself and relying on the decision

In essence, societal explanations represent the final and broadest purpose of XAI, as we show in Figure

They serve not just as an epistemic function, but as a moral and civic one: supporting inclusive, reflective, and ethically grounded AI adoption.

Case Studies: Applying the Multilevel Framework with LLMs

To illustrate the application of our multilevel explainability framework in practice, we now present a set of concrete case studies. Each example is drawn from a common scenario—loan approval—to demonstrate how explanations can be constructed across different levels and enriched using Large Language Models (LLMs). These cases show how LLMs can support the generation of explanations by translating technical output into user-friendly narratives, facilitating interactive dialogue, and contextualizing decisions for greater societal understanding.

The first case study demonstrates how LLMs can generate explanations grounded in model outputs and domain knowledge, helping users interpret black-box predictions through structured, informative narratives. The second highlights the role of LLMs in enabling interactive, dialogue-based explanations, even when the underlying model is inherently interpretable, by adapting responses to user questions in natural language. The third illustrates how LLMs can enhance prototype- and counterfactual-based reasoning, providing intuitive and actionable insights into model behavior.

Together, these examples demonstrate how LLMs can effectively support explanation generation at the algorithmic, interaction, and societal levels of our framework. The first case leverages SHAP values and LLMs for user-centered personalized explanations of a black-box model. The second integrates interpretable models, such as decision trees, with LLMs to provide clear, conversational justifications. The third uses prototypical and counterfactual reasoning, enriched with natural language explanations, to help users understand and engage with the boundaries of model decisions. Collectively, these scenarios showcase the potential of high-quality, LLM-enhanced explanations to foster transparency, trust, and accountability in AI systems.

The following examples are generated using OpenAI’s GPT-4 model
[
79
]
. The model was prompted to generate explanations based on the provided context and user queries.

The first case study exemplifies how LLMs can enhance explanations at the algorithmic and domain-based level by translating complex model outputs—such as SHAP values—into coherent, user-friendly narratives. In this scenario, a machine learning model is used to assess loan applications and its predictions are interpreted using feature attribution techniques. LLMs play a central role in converting these numerical explanations into natural language justifications that incorporate relevant domain knowledge, such as financial thresholds and applicant profiles. This allows users, including those without technical expertise, to understand which factors influenced the decision and how adjustments to their input data might change the outcome. By focusing the explanation on both model logic and contextual knowledge, this case highlights the ability of LLMs to act as effective mediators between technical transparency and meaningful communication.

Case study 1: LLM-Based Explanation in a Loan Approval Scenario.

Context:
A financial institution uses a machine learning model to evaluate loan applications. An applicant has been denied a loan and requests an explanation. The system internally uses SHAP values to interpret the model outputs. Credit score 580 (SHAP value:
-0.35
); Annual Income $28,000 (SHAP value:
-0.25
); Existing Debt: $10,000 (SHAP value:
-0.15
); Employment Length (SHAP value:
+0.10

User:
Why was my loan denied?

System:

Your loan was denied because key factors negatively impacted your application. The most influential was your credit score of 580, which contributed -0.35 to the decision. Your annual income of $28,000 and existing debt of $10,000 also reduced your approval likelihood, contributing -0.25 and -0.15 respectively. Your employment length had a small positive impact (+0.10), but it was not enough to offset the negatives. These values reflect how each factor influenced the model’s output based on similar past applications.

User:
What if I had a higher income?

System:

If your income were higher, the SHAP value for that feature would likely be less negative or even positive, which could improve your overall evaluation. For example, if your income increased to around $50,000, the contribution might shift closer to 0 or above, reducing the model’s confidence in denying the loan. However, the final outcome would still depend on how other factors, like credit score and debt, interact with the higher income. Improving multiple factors generally has a greater effect than changing one alone. We can run a simulation if you’d like to see the impact of a specific income level.

The second case study focuses on an inherently interpretable model—a decision tree—which naturally offers rule-based explanations for its predictions. Unlike black-box models that require post-hoc interpretation techniques, decision trees provide transparent logic by design, outlining decision paths through a sequence of conditions based on input features. This case complements the first by showing how interpretable models can still benefit from integration with LLMs. Here, the decision logic is not only readily accessible but can be translated into human-readable, conversational formats, making it more digestible for a wider range of users. Through LLMs, even straightforward rule sets can be personalized, contextualized, and clarified in response to user queries. This supports the delivery of explanations at both the algorithmic and the interaction levels, reinforcing understanding, promoting user trust, and allowing clarification without requiring in-depth technical knowledge. Case study 2 integrates well with the previous SHAP and LLM examples, showing a rule-based alternative that is easy to explain.

The third case study explores how prototype-based and counterfactual explanations can be used to provide intuitive insights into model behavior, particularly when the underlying model operates in a latent feature space. In this scenario, a machine learning model trained as an autoencoder evaluates loan applications by comparing new instances to previously learned patterns. Instead of explaining individual feature contributions, the model identifies a typical (prototype) case similar to the applicant and a contrasting (counterfactual) example that would have resulted in a different decision. This dual perspective helps users understand why a decision was made and what changes could lead to an alternative outcome. LLMs play a critical role in translating these technical comparisons into accessible, human-centered narratives. This supports users in understanding both the model’s rationale and actionable pathways, bridging levels 1 and 2 of the framework. Furthermore, by articulating these scenarios in natural language, LLMs help frame the explanation in terms relevant to fairness, opportunity, and future planning, core aspects of societal transparency at level 3.

This structured analysis allows us to assess the role and contribution of each level, which is algorithmic, human-centered, and societal, in generating trustworthy and audience-adapted explanations. By aligning specific elements of the case studies with the corresponding levels, we show how the framework supports layered, progressive understanding, and how LLMs play a unifying role across the spectrum of explanation needs.

Conclusions

In this work, we introduce a multilevel framework for XAI that addresses the needs of developers, domain experts, and broader societal audiences. The first level focuses on model-based explanations, combining inherently interpretable models and post hoc analyses to reveal how input drives outputs and how domain knowledge is incorporated. The second level supports human-centered explanations, allowing stakeholders to interact with, query, and adapt the explanation content through dialogue. The third level delivers socially grounded narratives that foster public trust, regulatory compliance, and collective accountability. By structuring explanations in this way, our framework ensures that each successive level builds on the fidelity and technical rigor of the previous level while extending accessibility to non-technical users.

A central contribution of our approach is the integration of LLMs as mediators between technical outputs and user-oriented explanations. In our case study of a loan-denial scenario, we show how SHAP values, tree-based models, or example-based explanations can be transformed by an LLM into a conversational narrative that highlights the most influential features, contextualizes them within legal and ethical considerations, and addresses user-specific concerns. This integration preserves the true behavior of the underlying mode while generating clear, personalized explanations that empower end-users and domain experts alike.

Beyond individual comprehension, fostering societal trust in AI requires that systems explain why decisions are made and how these decisions align with shared values and legal expectations. Our framework treats XAI as a democratic instrument: interpretable models offer auditable logic, post-hoc techniques expose complex relationships, and LLM-based interfaces bridge the gap to public understanding. Achieving this vision allows interdisciplinary collaboration to identify and incorporate diverse ethical norms and to design participatory mechanisms that capture community perspectives and ensure that explanations remain meaningful to citizens.

While the proposed multilevel framework offers a structured approach to audience-centered explainability, it remains a conceptual foundation that requires further empirical validation. The illustrative case studies provide plausibility but are not exhaustive across domains or user types. Additionally, the use of LLMs introduces challenges related to factual consistency, bias amplification, and explainability of the LLMs themselves.

Looking ahead, several research directions remain open. First, there is a critical need for standardized qualitative and quantitative metrics to evaluate the effectiveness, usability, and ethical adequacy of explanations at all three levels. Second, adaptive systems could dynamically tailor explanation types to individual user profiles, learning contexts, or decision criticality. Third, operationalizing social explanations on a scale will require integrating participatory audit systems, regulatory dashboards, and community feedback loops. Finally, since LLMs play an increasingly central role in these explanation pipelines, ensuring their fairness, robustness, and alignment with societal values is essential. Addressing these challenges will help transform our three-level framework from a conceptual roadmap into a practical, trustworthy solution for real-world AI systems.

An important avenue for future research lies in deepening the methodological rigor of Level 1 within the framework, particularly in connection with RED XAI principles. While current case studies demonstrate how algorithmic transparency can clarify model behavior using LLMs, future work could formalize how such explanations can be used for understanding and also for iterative model improvement. This would involve creating structured feedback loops between the explanation process and model design, enabling researchers and developers to diagnose, refine, and validate model behavior during training and deployment. Using explainability as a tool for debugging and enhancement, rather than merely post hoc justification, this approach can further advance the reliability and robustness of AI systems.

As AI systems become increasingly embedded in critical decision-making domains, the future of explainability must be grounded not only in technical advancements but also in real-world deployment constraints and stakeholder diversity. Future research should explore how multilevel explanations can be integrated into operational AI pipelines in domains such as healthcare, finance, and public services, where transparency and accountability are paramount. This includes designing user studies across different stakeholder groups to assess how explanations influence understanding, trust, and behavior. In addition, interdisciplinary collaboration will be essential, with legal scholars, social scientists, and domain experts, to ensure that the explanations are not only comprehensible, but also normatively sound. Ultimately, developing frameworks that link technical fidelity with legal robustness and civic legitimacy will be critical for embedding AI systems within sustainable and trustworthy governance structures.
