Effects of five-minutes music intervention on college students’ memory
Authors: Laila Yakoub-Agha, Jameel Soqia, Lujain Nahas, Mohammad Basheer Alameer, Lama Mohamad, Marah Yakoub-Agha, Caroline Almohsen, Samer Mohsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the impact of five-minutes of music on types of memory in college students. Sixty-nine students successfully completed the study, they were randomly assigned to either a control group or an intervention group. This study was two days long, and the intervention (listening to five minutes of Oriental Jazz music) was applied in the second day. We assessed short-term visual memory (immediate recall and five-minute delayed recall) with Rey–Osterrieth Complex Figure Test, short-term verbal-auditory memory (immediate recall and ten-minute delayed recall) with Rey Audio–Verbal Learning test, Working memory with forward and backward Digit Span test, and false memory with Deese–Roediger–McDermott test. Results showed that the intervention did not cause any significant effect on the recall of verbal information, on working memory performance, or on the incidence of false memories. However, music had a positive impact on visual memory. This study suggests the possible positive impact of music if used in educational settings aligned with visual teaching methods.

Introduction

In today’s fast-paced world, the pressure to meet ever-increasing requirements has taken a toll on people’s cognitive abilities. College students, in particular, bear the brunt of these challenges, often experiencing stress and fatigue1. Such issues can significantly impact their attention and memory, especially working memory1,2. Previous research has explored the overall cognitive functions such as memory, sustained attention3,4, concentration, and analyzing speed5,6, However, memory with its complexity stands out as a unique aspect of cognitive function of a college student that requires special attention due to its crucial role in academic performance and learning capabilities1,7. Therefore, we believe that it is an essential target for interventions aimed at supporting college students’ cognitive abilities.

Many papers have studied the possible intervention effects on different aspects of memory, especially music which is considered a pivotal element in the life of a college student. Previous studies have reported conflicting results regarding the effect of listening to music on memory.

Some studies argued for its positive effects; they demonstrated that music could influence cognitive performance positively by improving emotional state8 and by enhancing concentration and attention9. background music was also found to improve short-term memory, especially when it is self-selected10. Instrumental music were found to be superior to irrelevant speech and vocal music for working memory11. Similarly, one study highlighted that classical music enhanced working memory12 . Others argued that activating music can yield beneficial effects on cognitive performance13,14.

Conversely, the “Mozart effect”, initially believed to enhance cognitive performance, has been argued to be an artifact of arousal and mood15. Additionally, another study reported the negative impact of music on memory16, and music with lyrics was shown to hinder verbal memory, visual memory, and reading comprehension17. Classical music has also been reported in other studies to negatively affect memory18, with Mozart’s music specifically causing a significant distraction in individuals, consequently, a decline in their ability to process memories17.

Some researchers did not link the effect of music on memory to its genre or type, but rather to its relationship with the individual’s personal preferences and characteristics19,20. This line of evidence points to a more nuanced understanding of music’s effects on cognition, with personal and cultural familiarity playing a critical role.

Various genres of music were used to explore cognitive enhancement after listening to them namely: the classic music18,21, Jazz22, rock23, and lo-fi22, in addition to various types such as relaxing music13 and activating music14. Critically, previous studies19,20 argued that personal characteristics supersede genre effect. This presents a challenge for genre-focused interventions. However, we propose that culturally congruent music might modulate cognitive effects by aligning intrinsic auditory patterns and emotional associations. Therefore, we selected Oriental Jazz as intervention music in this study for its complex acoustic properties (combining both uplifting and relaxing music and avoiding lyrics), which can distinctly engage attentional and memory networks, and its cultural familiarity among Syrian participants. Previous research has shown that cultural familiarity with music enhances memory performance24, reduces perceptual uncertainty and shapes emotional perception25, and elicits stronger emotional engagement compared to unfamiliar music26. This can provide a unique perspective to test the interaction between musical features, listener characteristic and cultural familiarity that this genre provides and was not previously explored.

The current randomized controlled trial (RCT) aimed to examine the impact of brief, culturally relevant music on specific memory types among college students, and explicitly test how Oriental Jazz with its hybrid structure compares to the findings of Western genres. This could address the gap in how cultural context modulates music cognitive effects. Considering the time constraints faced by individuals nowadays, it is reasonable to design an intervention that would only require five minutes. Previous studies proved that even brief music exposure can have a measurable effect on cognitive performance and verbal memory27,28. If the results showed a promising effect, this concise intervention could be conveniently incorporated into students’ break periods, aligning with the popular pomodoro technique, or utilized in between lectures as an effective intervention for memory enhancement in educational settings.

On one hand, this study expected an enhancement in verbal auditory memory through emphasizing the auditory processing, and in working memory due to auditory network engagement and sustained attention. On the other hand, we expected visual and false memory to show minimal improvement. However, we aimed to navigate music effects on the last two subtypes of memory to assess modality specificity (solo effect on auditory memory), and accuracy (enhancement is not associated with error increase).

Methods

Study design

This study is an open-label randomized controlled trial conducted in the Faculty of Health Sciences in Damascus, Syria. Our study focused on college students to investigate the potential effects of five-minute intervention of music on the memory of participants specifically: Visual memory, Verbal-Auditory memory, Working memory, and False memory. We used a standardized piece of Oriental Jazz played for only five minutes.

Participants and randomization

The sample consisted of college students aged from 18 to 24, who are from the same faculty (Health Sciences) to reduce daily life differences, and to ensure a relatively uniform cognitive baseline, as they shared similar academic experiences and training. Additionally, only students who have not consumed any alcoholic beverages in the last 24 h, have not taken any medications that affect cognitive performances (such as antidepressants), have not previously suffered from a head injury which caused a concussion, and have not scored high on the self-perceived stress survey and the sleep disturbances survey, were included in the study, otherwise excluded. All faculty students were invited to join the study voluntarily by filling out the online survey which consists of clear statement of consent to participate and a request for (name, age, contact info, alcohol consumption in 24 h, previous head injuries, specific medication intake). This survey was administered on Google Docs and sent to the official group of the faculty by the dean.

Sample Size was calculated using GPower version (3.1.9.4). Given the challenges in recruiting a large number of students for an in-person experiment, we chose an effect size of Cohen’s dz = 0.30 (paired T-test for two dependent means), which presents small to medium by conventional benchmarks. While we acknowledge that this effect size is conservative compared to other music based cognitive enhancement studies, we considered it a meaningful target for detecting potential benefits of culturally relevant music on memory in a student population. We used the effect size from a previous research29 as a reference. Even though this study included a different population, we used it as a general guide not a precise estimate of our participants. One hundred and two responses were initially recorded. However, 12 responses were excluded for not meeting the sample criteria. There were 90 students who were given numbers based on their rank in filling out the online joining form. Twenty-one students expressed inability to show up for the experiment for consecutive two days, therefore were excluded. The final number of participants was 69 students. We inserted students’ numbers in a random number generating app to randomly assign them to either group A (music intervention) or group B (control) with a ratio of 34:35, No blocking or other restrictions were applied. Additionally, only the first author knew which group was each participant assigned to, which minimized bias when performing memory tests. All participants completed both days of the trial, and their complete data were included in the analysis (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1CONSORT-style flow diagram illustrating participant recruitment, randomization into control and intervention groups, music intervention, and subsequent cognitive testing on Day 1 and Day 2.

To minimize any potential personal bias, students were intentionally kept unaware of the study’s purpose or any specific details until the beginning of the study. They were told that these tests are to evaluate their memory and they will receive their scores after the tests. This approach was taken to ensure blinding; we provided a brief comprehensive explanation of the entire study to the participants once it was done. All lists of words, shapes, and numbers were changed on the second day to limit the effect of memorizing the test.

Measurements

The perceived stress scale (PSS)

This self-administered questionnaire contains ten items designed to measure the stress levels over the past month. Scores range from 0 to 4030,31, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress. A validated Arabic version of the scale was used in this study32. This test was used to exclude participants with high stress level with scores higher or equal to 27/40.

The Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI)

This nineteen-item self-administered questionnaire assesses sleep quality and disturbances over the past month33. It comprises seven items:

(1) Sleep duration (2) sleep latency (3) sleep disturbance (4) daytime dysfunction due to sleepiness (5) sleep efficiency (6) overall sleep quality (7) sleep medication use.

Each item is scored from 0–3, resulting in a global score where higher values indicate poorer sleep quality. A validated Arabic version of the questionnaire was used in the study34. This test was used as a screening tool to exclude participants with the lowest sleep quality with scores greater than five.

Both PSS and PSQI tests were used for two reasons: first to ensure the sample is similar on these bases (anxiety and sleep quality), and to exclude high levels of anxiety and lowest scores of sleep quality, which will confound memory performance.

The Rey auditory-verbal learning test (RAVLT):

Participants listened to a list of 15 words read at a rate of one word per second in a normal-pitched voice (list A). They were asked to recall as many words as they can possible. The test involves five repetitions of list A, followed by an interfering list (list B) read once. After that participants were asked to repeat list A immediately, and again after 10 min. Four different Lists of words were used for Day1 and Day 2 sourced from35 (two lists for each day). Each trial score was recorded, representing the number of words recalled. Only the first trial score was treated as immediate auditory memory; the mean of the first five trials’ scores (list A) was used as immediate auditory learning, the seventh trial score represented the after-interference score, and the eighth trial represented short-term auditory memory (after ten minutes).

The Rey-Osterrieth complex figure test (ROCF)

This is a vastly used assessment tool for visuospatial recall and it first asks the examinee to copy an intricate shape and then draw it from memory immediately after copying it (thirty seconds) and after a delay (five minutes). Two different figures were chosen from35, as the first shape was used on Day one, and the second on Day two. Each figure is a shape created from eighteen components, including lines and symbols. Scoring evaluated both accuracy and process, as Successful recall of a component gave two points score, partial recall gave one point, and no recall gave no points35. Additionally, the drawing process was evaluated based on the sequence and organization of components, which means recalling the element in the right position gave two points, recalling it in the wrong position gave one point, no recall gave no points35. Although the first author (who was not blind to the conditions) conducted the scoring, group assignments were recorded separately, and the author did not recall the group numbers during scoring. Therefore, all participants were scored according to the standardized instructions without knowledge of group, minimizing potential bias.

The Deese-Roediger-McDermott (DRM) test

This tool was developed to test for false memory, which represents the possibility of recalling something that did not actually happen or recall it differently from the way is happened. This test presents takers with a list of semantically related words (encoding section), after a short delay of ten seconds, participants are then presented with a collection of words and must determine whether the presented word was originally shown to them. Both “luring” and non-present words were included in the recall task to test for false retrieval and results included false and true memories. The test consisted of two parts; the short test in which a random list of fifteen words is shown on the screen, and the long test in which two lists of words were presented to participant, each are fifteen words. The test in both parts was fifteen words. Six lists of words were chosen from36.

The digit-span test

This is a test for forward and backward recall of numbers to test working memory and short-term memory37. Forward testing begins with a sequence of three numbers and upon each correct answer, a new number is added to the sequence while a number is deducted if the participant fails to memorize and recall two consequent sequences. Backward testing begins with two digits and flows similarly to forward testing. The sequence of numbers in both modes of testing is played on the screen then after a delay period of ten seconds the examinee is asked to retype the numbers as presented or in reverse order starting. The test is concluded when the participant has reached the maximum number of the sequence (nine for forward testing and eight for backward) or when they fail to report a sequence. The test generated different numbers each day.

The time of these tests (DRM, Digit Span) depended highly on the student’s response speed which was recorded in PsychoPy and analyzed in the results.

All tests administrated in the second day were parallel version of the original assessments, designed in previous studies to match the first-day tests in difficulty, structure, and length.

Ethical considerations

Terms and conditions of the two-day trial were explained in the participation survey and the completion and submission of the online response meant that informed consent was obtained from participants. The study was approved by the ethical committee at Damascus University (ID number: FHS-140724 -274, session 14 on 14/7/2024) and has been conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration (as revised in 2013. about informed consent, voluntariness, and anonymity). Questionnaires did not include names or personal information that could compromise participants’ safety. Participants were also informed that they were free to withdraw at any time if they did not wish to continue the experiment.

Data analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. The results included demographic data, pre-tests, and post-tests scores of all tests conducted, PSS, and PSQI scores. Independent samples T test and Mann–Whitney U test were used—with 95% confidence intervals—to assess the changes in test scores between baseline and Day 2 between study arms after ensuring that the data did not follow normal distribution using Shapiro–Wilk test. Paired Samples T-test and Wilcoxon test were used to compare the changes to the entire sample results. All Bayesian analyses were two-tailed, with the alternative hypothesis defined as a difference between groups. The prior for the effect size was a zero-centered Cauchy distribution with width 0.707 (the default), reflecting that moderate effect sizes were considered plausible a priori. A p value of < .050 was considered statistically significant. Cohen’s d was calculated for normally distributed data, while the effect size was calculated manually (Rank Biserial correlation and r) for abnormally distributed data. Missing data were excluded.

The normality of the difference scores (post-test minus pre-test) for each group was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Results indicated that the difference scores for the immediate recall task were not normally distributed in either Group A (W = 0.79, p = 0.001) or Group B (W = 0.92, p = 0.014). For immediate recall on the ROCF test, the mean difference score for the intervention group was 2.51 (SD = 3.83), compared to 0.27 (SD = 6.72) in the control group. The Mann–Whitney U test showed a significant group difference (U = 353, z = − 2.92, p = 0.004, r = 0.29), reflecting a modest effect size. Music effect on digit span test

For the Digit Span forward test, Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated non-normal distributions in both group A (W = 0.81, p < 0.001) and group B (W = 0.86, p < 0.001). Bayesian analysis yielded a posterior mean of 0.418 (95% HDI [0.29, 0.55]) and BF₁₀ = 0.13 (BF01 = 7.69), indicating moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis.

For the Digit Span backward test, Shapiro–Wilk tests again indicated non-normal distributions in both group A (W = 0.71, p < 0.001) and group B (W = 0.85, p = 0.0002). Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated non-normal distribution for group A (W = 0.89, p = 0.002) and normal distribution for group B (W = 0.95, p = 0.132). Discussion

Starting from our belief in the importance of music in the everyday life of college students, we aimed to investigate the potential effects of music on cognitive functions, more specifically, memory. While our results did not fully align with initial expectations, they did show a fascinating outcome: a notable improvement in the recall of visual memory tests after brief exposure to Oriental Jazz. This result contradicts the findings of previous studies suggesting the absence of impact of music on visual memory38,39, or even arguing for its negative effect on visual information recall40. Our findings strongly support a positive effect on visual memory, as Bayesian analysis provided decisive evidence for a robust effect of music on visual memory, reinforcing the observed improvement. There is moderate Bayesian evidence for a real (but very small) effect in delayed verbal recall, backward digit span, and task completion times, even though frequentist analyses did not reach significance. While the practical impact may be limited, larger studies could further explore these subtle effects.

The age and mood factors

Since our study was conducted among young adults, it is possible that age contributed to the response to music intervention, as suggested by previous papers demonstrating that the efficacy and processing speed of visual information were significantly better in younger adults41, specifically early in the twenties42. This could possibly be explained by the readiness of specific parts of the cortex to be more influenced by stimuli than others. A previous study determined the areas of the brain that were activated due to listening to music, involving the superior temporal gyrus, the intraparietal sulcus (IPS), the precentral sulcus, the inferior frontal sulcus and gyrus, and the frontal operculum (OFC)43. Although these areas’ association with verbal and working memory was previously discussed43, the IPS, in particular, has been highlighted for its role in visual memory through storing multiple features of an object together and, therefore, helps in the recognition of objects over short periods44. Additionally, the OFC is a part of the reward system that contributes to the creation of an emotional attitude towards music45, which can potentially change someone’s mood and thereby affect cognitive functions. This idea is supported by the findings of previous studies which explained the positive influence of music on cognitive function with the mood-arousal hypothesis46,47. Although pre- and post-intervention mood assessments were not included in this study, incorporating them would be a plausible approach to further explore the role of mood in cognitive effects. We recommend that future studies include such assessments. Therefore, we suggest that these and other areas of the brain, which are related to visual information processing and recall, might have been triggered at this age, potentially resulting in a better recall of visual information. Although the current data did not detect statistically significant effects in delayed verbal recall or working memory, Bayesian analysis revealed a possible existence of subtle effects that larger studies might be able to fully explore.

The length of music factor

It is possible that the short period of music, which we aimed for in this study, was able to reveal the initial response of these individuals to music, and also to explain the fact that we were not able to measure an effect of music on verbal-auditory memory and working memory. This claim is consistent with other studies showing that music training., significantly longer periods of exposure, can enhance both verbal memory48,49 and working memory50,51. Thus, it is possible that producing a positive response to five minutes of music on verbal and working memory would require longer periods of previous exposure. This effect of music training on reshaping the brain is supported by its contribution to the modulation of the cortex synchronization of neural networks involved in verbal memory formation49. However, in our study, participants were exposed to an Oriental Jazz piece. While this genre may be culturally familiar to Syrian participants in general, we did not directly measure individual familiarity. Therefore, the specific effect of prior music training or familiarity could not be confirmed and may represent a personal factor. Moderate Bayesian findings imply that even short music exposure may have initiated small-to-moderate effects in delayed verbal recall and task performance times, which were not statistically significant, likely due to limited power. Future studies with larger samples are recommended to further explore these potential effects.

The genre of music factor

Previous studies’ results showed that younger adults were more influenced by the genre of music19,52. In our study, participants listened to an Oriental Jazz piece. Given the cultural background of our sample, the Oriental elements of the music were likely familiar and may have facilitated engagement. At the same time, the fusion with jazz introduced novel auditory features. This combination of familiarity and novelty might have encouraged imaginative processing, which could in turn have activated visual memory-related areas53,54. In contrast, verbal-auditory recall and working memory may be more strongly influenced by familiarity with specific sound patterns55. These possibilities remain speculative and require further investigation. Moderate Bayes factors observed in delayed verbal recall, backward digit span, and completion times suggest that music genre or familiarity might have contributed to subtle cognitive effects beyond visual memory.

Timing of listening to music factor

It might have played an important role. On one hand, in this study, participants were exposed to music before performing the cognitive tasks, and the results showed no effect of music on verbal memory recall and working memory tasks, which is consistent with a previous study18. Thus, it is possible that the effect of music had declined as the tests were being performed. On the other hand, participants in some studies were exposed to music during the performance of cognitive tasks and argued for its negative impact, causing deterioration of participants’ scores18,56. Therefore, we recommend further studies to explore the effect of listening to music before test performance with longer periods of music that, for example, match the length of tests. Bayesian evidence points to small potential effects in delayed verbal recall and task completion times, which were not detected by conventional p-values but may warrant further exploration with larger samples.

The practice effect

It is worth mentioning that the effect that music can have on cognitive functions is quite complex and can’t be fully understood by one experiment. The improvement in the means scores of some of these tests, especially working memory, false memory, and visual memory tests, does highlight the effect that practice can have on cognitive abilities21,57, but still does not deny the effect that music had on visual memory was beyond practice. In the current study, we attempted to mitigate this by modifying the tests on Day 2 to reduce practice effect. However, we acknowledge that this does not fully eliminate this effect. That said, the selective improvement observed in visual memory, despite all four tests being repeated on day two, raises the question of why practice effects would manifest only in this domain and not in others. Furthermore, if practice effects were the main explanation, similar improvements would be expected in both groups; instead, only the intervention group showed an increase in visual memory performance, whereas the control group’s mean score slightly declined. This pattern suggests that while practice effects cannot be fully excluded, they are unlikely to solely account for the observed findings. Therefore, we highly recommend that this effect be further investigated using more developed methods such as fMRI in order to better understand its complexity.

Conclusion

This study aimed to investigate the potential effects of music on memory. Results showed that listening to five minutes of Oriental Jazz did not significantly enhance auditory-verbal memory, working memory, or reduce false memories. However, a significant improvement was observed in immediate visual memory (modest effect) and delayed visual memory. While current frequentist analyses did not detect effects in delayed verbal recall, backward digit span, and task completion times, Bayesian analysis revealed the possible existence of small effects that larger studies could further explore. We conclude that a brief music intervention may selectively enhance visual memory in college students, suggesting potential applications in educational settings that incorporate visual teaching methods. Further research is needed to explore the generalizability and underlying mechanisms of this effect.

Limitations and recommendations

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the results of this study. Firstly, this study was conducted among college students; thus, its results may not be generalizable to other populations and age groups. Secondly, this study relies on the accuracy of students’ answers in PSS and PSQI tests. Thirdly, the control group did not receive an alternative auditory stimulus (e.g., white noise or another music genre), which means the results may not precisely isolate the effect of Oriental Jazz from other auditory influences. However, the primary aim of this study was not to compare music with other auditory conditions, but rather to examine whether introducing music as a short break in a setting that mimics an actual educational environment could influence students’ memory performance. The findings still demonstrate a measurable effect of music on memory, though future studies should compare Oriental Jazz with other auditory stimuli to further clarify this effect. Despite all participants belonging to the same faculty, personal differences and background were not controlled in this study, and familiarity with the genre was assumed based on its cultural relevance, but was not formally tested. Moderate Bayesian evidence indicates that some subtle effects may exist that were not detected by conventional significance testing; future research with larger samples could confirm these findings.
