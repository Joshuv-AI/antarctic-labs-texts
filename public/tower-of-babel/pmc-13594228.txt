A somatic checkpoint through NHR-49/HNF4α governs reproductive investment and longevity in C. elegans
Authors: Sharada Gopal, Amaresh Chaturbedi, Tej Ramachandrula, John OuYang, Rebecca Rodell, Siu Sylvia Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The nuclear hormone receptor NHR-49, a homolog of mammalian PPARα and HNF4α, is a key transcriptional regulator of nutrition sensing and fatty acid metabolism in Caenorhabditis elegans. Here, we examine the role of NHR-49 in reproduction, including oocyte activation and ovulation. Loss of NHR-49 causes inappropriate oocyte activation and laying of unfertilized oocytes in the absence of sperm, resulting in rapid loss of yolk and stored fat, and drastic shortening of lifespan. Conversely, prevention of yolk transfer into the oocytes largely restores fat storage and partially rescues lifespan in the nhr-49 mutants. Additionally, NHR-49 couples germline proliferation to nutritional status, as evidenced by its requirement for pausing germline proliferation upon starvation. Interestingly, NHR-49 primarily acts in somatic cells, rather than the germline itself, to regulate oocyte activation and ovulation. Combined genomic analyses identify GSA-1, a G protein alpha s subunit (Gαs), as a direct transcriptional target of NHR-49 highlighting a mechanistic link between metabolic sensing, fertility and longevity maintenance.

Introduction

Reproduction is one of the most energetically demanding biological processes1, requiring organisms to tightly coordinate reproductive activity with metabolic status2,3. In nutrient-poor conditions, animals often suppress germline proliferation, delay ovulation, or halt reproduction entirely to conserve resources4. These adjustments are especially crucial for optimizing reproductive success and ensuring the survival of both parent and offspring5. However, the molecular mechanisms that link nutritional cues to reproductive timing remain incompletely understood.

C. elegans are primarily hermaphrodites and possess both oocytes and sperm, making them capable of self-fertilization6. During the L4 larval stage (L4), the germline stem cells in the distal region actively proliferate, and the cells further away from the distal tip cell undergo meiosis, where they take up the sperm fate, and the developed sperm are stored in the spermatheca7. From the young adult stage onwards, germ cells switch fate to an oogenic fate, and the developing oocytes are arrested in meiosis I and stored in the oviduct8. A highly regulated process enables the oocytes most proximal to the spermatheca to sense the presence of sperm, become activated, i.e., begin to progress past meiosis I, and simultaneously be ovulated into the spermatheca where they become fertilized; the fertilized embryos continue to develop in the uterus until they are laid outside of the parent at around the 300-cell stage9. The FEM (feminization) and FOG (feminization of germline) genes are crucial regulators of sex determination and spermatogenesis in C. elegans1. Loss-of-function mutations of fem-3, fog-2 or fog-3 result in defective spermatogenesis and feminization of worms that are incapable of self-fertilization10 (Fig. 1a). Similar to hermaphrodites, the germline in these mutants contains mitotic and meiotic cells; however, the germ cells do not take up the spermatogenic fate during development. This results in an organism that has no sperm in their spermatheca. In the absence of males, these mutants accumulate ~20 oocytes in their oviduct, and the process of germline stem cell proliferation and differentiation appears paused. Upon mating with males, or when sperm are provided11, the feminized mutants undergo normal stem cell proliferation and differentiation into oocytes, which are fertilized by the sperm. Mated feminized mutants produce a similar number of healthy progenies as mated hermaphrodites. Thus, feminized mutants in C. elegans resemble females of non-hermaphroditic species11. Feminized C. elegans mutants have been crucial in revealing the molecular pathways that regulate how oocytes sense sperm and become activated and ovulated12–14. In C. elegans, the nuclear hormone receptor NHR-49—a putative functional homolog of mammalian PPARα and structural homolog of HNF4α—is a central regulator of lipid metabolism and energy homeostasis16. Like many nuclear hormone receptors, NHR-49 is a DNA-binding transcription factor that is likely regulated through ligand binding17,18. NHR-49 is known to regulate the expression of both lipid synthesis and lipid hydrolysis genes19. For example, NHR-49 transcriptionally regulates stearoyl-CoA desaturation genes fat-6 and fat-7, which maintain membrane fluidity and polyunsaturated fatty acid synthesis20. It also activates acs-2, a gene involved in mitochondrial β-oxidation16,20. In addition to fat metabolism, NHR-49 is also known to regulate oxidative stress response21, hypoxia adaptation22, peroxisome proliferation23, pathogen avoidance24, innate immunity25, and longevity26. Interestingly, NHR-49 has been suggested to participate in regulating adult reproductive diapause, a state in which severely starved L4 worms undergo extensive germline attrition and the animal can survive for a period of several weeks27–29, and the germline can regenerate when food becomes available again.

Using feminized mutant C. elegans as a model, we uncover a previously unrecognized role for NHR-49 in regulating oocyte activation and ovulation in a cell-non-autonomous manner. We observe that loss of nhr-49 results in inappropriate oocyte activation and ovulation, even in the absence of sperm signal, leading to laying of unfertilized oocytes. This inappropriate laying of unfertilized oocytes triggers excessive yolk and fat loss and a drastic shortening of lifespan. Notably, when yolk transport to oocytes is blocked, thereby retaining yolk within the parent, fat storage is largely restored in nhr-49 mutant, and their shortened lifespan is also partially rescued. Furthermore, nhr-49 mutant fails to halt germline proliferation under nutrient-restricted conditions, suggesting a disruption in the organism’s ability to couple reproductive output to nutritional status. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that NHR-49 likely regulates sperm sensing and oocyte activation by binding to the promoter of gsa-1 and regulating its expression in the gonadal sheath cells. Together, our findings suggest that NHR-49 plays a broader role in coordinating energy metabolism and reproductive restraint, and in doing so, orchestrates the balance between parental resource management and reproductive success, with a major impact on longevity.

Results

NHR-49 is required for the longer lifespan and elevated fat storage of feminized C. elegans

Our lab recently reported that when not mated, feminized mutants exhibit significant lifespan extension and elevated fat storage compared to wild-type N2 (wild-type) hermaphrodites at 25 °C (Fig. 1b, Source data)2. While investigating the molecular factors required for the lifespan extension and fat metabolism of feminized worms, we identified NHR-49, a key metabolic regulator25,26,30,31, as essential for the enhanced lifespan of feminized worms (Fig. 1b, Source data). Loss of nhr-49 drastically reduced the lifespan of feminized worms, where the double mutant nhr-49(gk405);fem-3(e1996) had a lifespan similar to the short lifespan of the nhr-49(gk405) single mutant30.

While characterizing the nhr-49(-);fem-3(-) double mutant, we noticed a surprising and interesting phenotype, where these feminized mutant worms laid unfertilized oocytes during early adulthood (Fig. 1c). In C. elegans, oocyte maturation and ovulation are strictly dependent on the presence of sperm, and consequently, feminized mutants lacking sperm retain their oocytes within the oviduct12. Accordingly, we observed no oocytes laid on the plate for fem-3(-) mutant (Fig. 1c, d, Source data)3. When examining the nhr-49(-);fem-3(-) double mutant, we observed that these worms laid their unfertilized oocytes even though they lack sperm (Fig. 1c, d, Source data). A similar observation was made with a different feminized mutant strain11 fog-2(-), and nhr-49(-);fog-2(-) (Fig. 1d, Source data). NHR-49 has not been implicated in regulating oocyte ovulation. To test whether NHR-49 uniquely regulates oocyte retention only in feminized mutants or whether it represents a more general role of NHR-49, we monitored nhr-49(-) mutant hermaphrodites. C. elegans hermaphrodites reach the end of their reproductive period around day 4 of adulthood at 20 °C, when their sperm supply is depleted, and the remaining oocytes are retained in the oviduct, mirroring the reproductive state of feminized mutants32. We first examined nhr-49 single mutant hermaphrodites at this post-reproductive stage and observed that they also laid unfertilized oocytes when assessed on day 6 of adulthood (Supplementary Fig. 1a, Source data), a phenotype similar to that observed for the nhr-49(-);fem-3(-) double mutant (Fig. 1c, d). We additionally monitored egg laying of nhr-49(-) single mutant compared to wild-type hermaphrodites and observed that nhr-49(-) laid a reduced number of fertilized embryos, but a significantly higher number of unfertilized oocytes, throughout the reproductive period, as well as during the post-reproductive period (i.e., >120 h after reaching adulthood) (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c, Source data). This unexpected finding likely explains the previously reported lower brood size phenotype associated with the nhr-49(-) mutant23 (Supplementary Fig. 1d, Source data). Together, our results demonstrate a fundamental role of NHR-49 in regulating the retention of unfertilized oocytes, with this function becoming particularly evident in feminized mutants where sperm are absent throughout development.

Different types of self-sterile C. elegans mutants, including the feminized fem-3(-) mutant, accumulate excess fat15. Here, we found that the increased fat accumulation in fem-3(-) worms depends on functional NHR-49. We used Oil Red O (ORO) staining15,33 to visualize storage lipids, and observed that both hermaphrodites and feminized mutant worms lacking nhr-49 showed significantly lower ORO staining on day 6 of adulthood (Fig. 2a, b, Source data). Interestingly, when comparing the ORO levels between day 1 and day 6 of adulthood in the different strains, wild-type showed a significant increase in stored fat as they progressed towards the post-reproductive period, as previously published34,35. fem-3(-) mutant showed no significant change during this timeframe. Hermaphrodites and feminized worms lacking nhr-49 showed a drastic reduction in ORO levels from day 1 to day 6 (Fig. 2a, b, Source data), suggesting a depletion of stored fat in worms lacking nhr-49. This temporal loss of lipid appears coincidental with the time-period where mutants lacking nhr-49 are expelling their unfertilized oocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1). Adult C. elegans hermaphrodites are known to transfer lipid-bound yolk to their oocytes to support embryonic development36. Since both hermaphrodites30 and feminized worms lacking nhr-49(-) are short-lived (Fig. 1b) and we found that they rapidly lose yolk by day 2 of adulthood (Supplementary Fig. 2), we hypothesized that attenuating yolk loss would help to preserve stored fat and lifespan. In C. elegans, yolk produced in the intestine is transported to oocytes via the endocytic receptor RME-238. As expected, RNAi knockdown of rme-2 resulted in yolk retention in the nhr-49(-);fem-3(-) double mutant, observed as increased VIT-2::GFP reporter signal in the soma (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b, Source data). Yolk retention also largely restored fat storage, as indicated by ORO staining (Fig. 2c, d, Source data). Interestingly, knockdown of rme-2 partially restored the lifespan of both the nhr-49(-);fem-3(-) double and nhr-49(-) single mutants (Fig. 2e, Source data). Taken together, these findings indicated that laying of unfertilized oocytes is accompanied by shunting of yolk away from the soma, which largely accounts for the rapid fat loss and partially accounts for the shortened lifespan of both hermaphrodites and feminized worms lacking nhr-49(-).

We next tested whether yolk transfer into oocytes can also modulate aberrant oocyte laying observed in the nhr-49(-);fem-3(-) mutant. In worms where yolk uptake was inhibited due to rme-2 RNAi, despite effective yolk retention (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b), we observed no significant change in the number of oocytes laid compared to control RNAi (Supplementary Fig. 2d). These results indicated that inappropriate oocyte laying in the absence of NHR-49 occurs independently of somatic lipid transfer to the germline. Thus far, our results support a role for NHR-49 in preventing the inappropriate laying of unfertilized oocytes, which is linked to lipid retention and lifespan maintenance.

NHR-49 prevents oocyte activation and ovulation in the absence of sperm

To understand the molecular basis of inappropriate oocyte laying upon loss of nhr-49, we first characterized the activation status of the oocytes. In C. elegans, oocytes are naturally arrested in meiotic prophase I and require sperm-derived signals to resume meiotic progression, as part of oocyte maturation, which leads to ovulation and subsequent fertilization. Doubly phosphorylated MPK-1 (dpMPK-1), a homolog of the ERK MAP kinase, is a well-established marker of oocyte activation13 (Fig. 3a). We performed dpMPK-1 immunostaining of day-1 adults to examine the status of oocyte activation in hermaphrodites and feminized worms with or without nhr-49. While fertile wild-type hermaphrodites showed the expected dpMPK-1 signal in the oocytes proximal to the spermatheca, feminized strains, both fem-3(-) and fog-3(-)14, did not (Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b, Source data), indicative of a loss of oocyte activation in the absence of sperm. Loss of nhr-49 did not substantially impact dpMPK-1 signal in reproductive hermaphrodites on day 1 of adulthood (Fig. 3b, c, Source data). Interestingly, loss of nhr-49 resulted in prominent dpMPK-1 signal in the proximal oocytes of feminized worms (Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b), which was comparable to that in hermaphrodites, indicating inappropriate activation of the oocyte maturation pathway despite the absence of sperm. Oocytes arrested in meiotic prophase I maintain minimal protein quality control activity, resulting in the accumulation of protein aggregates throughout the cytoplasm39–41. During oocyte maturation, proximal oocytes eliminate these protein aggregates to prepare for fertilization42 (Fig. 3a). To further assess the activation status of the oocytes in mutants lacking nhr-49, we examined protein aggregate clearance in feminized strains with or without nhr-49, using the HCP-1::GFP reporter that has been previously used to detect protein aggregates in C. elegans oocytes42. In day-1 adults, as expected, fem-3(-) single mutant showed significantly higher HCP-1::GFP signal (Fig. 3d, e, Source data) in their proximal oocytes compared to reproductive wild-type hermaphrodites, consistent with the oocytes not being activated and accumulating protein aggregates. RNAi knockdown of nhr-49 in feminized worms resulted in an aberrant decrease of HCP-1::GFP signal (Fig. 3d, e) in the proximal oocytes, further supporting inappropriate activation of the oocytes when nhr-49 is lost. In summary, in both post-reproductive hermaphrodites and feminized strains, loss of nhr-49 leads to mis-activation of oocytes, despite the absence of sperm, and inappropriate ovulation and laying of unfertilized oocytes.

In C. elegans, successful reproduction requires continuous oocyte production to replace those that are fertilized to become embryos. Continuous oocyte production depends on two key processes: active proliferation of the mitotic stem cells in the distal region of the germline, and subsequent oogenesis in the meiotic region. Since unfertilized oocytes were inappropriately activated and laid in feminized worms lacking nhr-49, we wondered whether their germline stem cell proliferation and oogenesis are enhanced. We assessed mitotic proliferation by EdU labeling15,43, which marks nuclei undergoing DNA synthesis, and oogenesis by immunostaining for dpMPK-1. Feminized worms normally produce only a fixed number of oocytes due to them being retained in the oviduct, and as expected, they contained a reduced number of EdU+ nuclei in the mitotic region (Fig. 3f, g, Source data) and showed lower dpMPK-1 signal intensity in the oogenic region (Supplementary Fig. 4a, c), compared to wild-type hermaphrodites. Examination of the nhr-49(-);fem-3(-) double mutant worms revealed upregulated proliferation of the distal germline stem cells, as evident by higher levels of EdU+ nuclei in the mitotic region and enhanced oogenesis, as indicated by increased dpMPK-1 staining in the meiotic region (Fig. 3f, g and Supplementary Fig. 4a, c). Consistently, post-reproductive nhr-49 mutant hermaphrodites also showed a significant elevation of dpMPK-1 signal in the meiotic region compared to wild-type worms at the same age, suggestive of enhanced oogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f, Source data). Collectively, these data revealed that, when NHR-49 is inactivated, despite the absence of sperm, both proliferation of the mitotic germline stem cells and oogenesis are upregulated, correlating with aberrant activation and laying of oocytes. However, it is not clear whether aberrant activation of oocytes and subsequent laying of unfertilized oocytes trigger distal germline proliferation and oogenesis or vice versa in nhr-49(-) mutants.

NHR-49 is a well-established metabolic regulator, with particular importance in maintaining lipid homeostasis upon starvation20,44. NHR-49 has also been suggested to regulate starvation-induced germline attrition, a process known as adult reproductive diapause45. We therefore tested whether the role of NHR-49 in the germline, as detailed above, is also sensitive to nutritional status. We monitored germline proliferation in reproductive hermaphrodites using EdU43 labeling and, as expected, observed that germline proliferation was significantly reduced in response to starvation (24 h) in wild-type worms. We found that nhr-49(-) single mutant showed reduced germline proliferation, as evident by lower EdU+ nuclei in the distal region. NHR-49 likely regulates oocyte activation through the somatic gonadal sheath cells

To dissect the pathway through which NHR-49 regulates oocyte activation and ovulation, we examined its genetic interactions with known regulators of this process. MSPs (Major Sperm Proteins) are sperm-derived cytoskeletal proteins that are also secreted as signaling molecules to promote oocyte maturation and sheath cell contraction and ovulation46. vab-1, encoding an ephrin receptor expressed on oocytes, and ceh-18, a homeodomain transcription factor essential for sheath cell function, act to prevent oocyte activation in the absence of MSPs, and are critical regulators of the MSP-dependent oocyte activation and ovulation pathway47. In feminized worms, inactivation of vab-1 or ceh-18 induced oocyte laying (Fig. 5a, b, Source data), although the effect of vab-1(-);fog-2(-) was modest, consistent with previous findings14. RNAi knockdown of nhr-49 led to a high number of oocytes being laid in feminized worms, ~10-fold higher than in vab-1(-), and similar to that of ceh-18(-) (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. 5a, b, Source data). Combined loss of nhr-49 and vab-1 in fog-2(-) feminized worms resulted in an oocyte-laying phenotype that was similar to nhr-49 RNAi alone (Fig. 5a, Source data), suggesting that nhr-49 and vab-1 act in a shared or converging genetic pathway; however, given the mild effect of vab-1 loss, it is also possible that an additive effect would be difficult to detect. Given that NHR-49 is a transcription factor, we next asked whether its control of oocyte activation might operate through the transcriptional regulation of vab-1. If NHR-49 normally promotes vab-1 expression, then loss of nhr-49 could lower VAB-1 levels, and restoring VAB-1 independently of NHR-49 should be sufficient to correct the downstream dpMPK-1 mis-activation observed in nhr-49(−);fem-3(−) double mutants. Despite robust, nhr-49-independent expression of VAB-1::GFP, its overexpression was not sufficient to suppress the pMPK-1 mis-activation phenotype characteristic of feminized nhr-49 knockdown animals (Supplementary Fig. 5c–e, Source data). The persistence of aberrant dpMPK-1 activation even when VAB-1 was provided exogenously indicates that reduced vab-1 transcription is not the basis of the nhr-49 loss-of-function phenotype. Together, these results demonstrate that NHR-49 regulates oocyte activation through a mechanism independent of vab-1 transcription.

We additionally investigated whether inactivation of vab-1 and ceh-18 in feminized worms produces fat loss and upregulated germline stem cell proliferation phenotypes, similar to those observed in nhr-49(-) mutants. We detected a significantly higher number of EdU+ nuclei in the fog-2(-);ceh-18(-) double mutant compared to the feminized mutant alone (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b, Source data), to a degree comparable to that of nhr-49(-);fem-3(-) mutant (Fig. 3g). In contrast, the number of EdU+ nuclei in vab-1(-);fog-2(-) was not significantly different than that in the fog-2(-) mutant alone (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b, Source data). We found similar differences in ORO staining between vab-1(-);fog-2(-) and fog-2(-);ceh-18(-) mutants compared to fog-2(-). We found that inactivation of either vab-1 or ceh-18 also led to reduced fat levels by day 6 of adulthood. However, fat depletion was markedly more severe in the fog-2(-);ceh-18(-) mutant (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d, Source data), similar to that of nhr-49 depletion, whereas vab-1(-);fog-2(-) mutant exhibited a mild phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). These results indicated that the severity of the oocyte laying phenotype correlates with the degree of fat loss and germline proliferation, corroborating our earlier findings in nhr-49(-) mutant.

To further test whether NHR-49 acts cell-autonomously in oocytes or from somatic tissues to regulate oocyte activation and ovulation, we used a C. elegans strain that enables germline-specific RNAi, where the rde-1 null mutant is complemented with functional RDE-1 only in the germline (rde-1(-); Psun-1::RDE-1)48. Germline-specific knockdown of nhr-49 in feminized worms failed to recapitulate the oocyte-laying and dpMPK-1 upregulation phenotypes observed with whole-body nhr-49 RNAi (Fig. 5c–e, Source data). Taken together, the genetic interaction and tissue-specific RNAi results, we speculate that NHR-49 is largely dispensable in the germline and instead acts through somatic tissues—likely the gonadal sheath cells—to regulate oocyte activation and ovulation.

Genome-wide analyses identify metabolic and stress-related transcriptional targets of NHR-49

To determine the transcriptional outputs of NHR-49, we performed CUT&RUN, a chromatin profiling method that maps protein-DNA interactions, in day 1 adult wild-type animals using the CRISPR-engineered nhr-49(hj293) strain, which harbors a GFP::TEV::3xHA tag at the C-terminus of the endogenous nhr-49 locus23. This profiling revealed 2,227 high-confidence NHR-49 binding sites, with the majority of NHR-49-bound peaks localized to gene promoters and transcription start sites (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b, Supplementary Data 1), consistent with a role of NHR-49 in transcriptional regulation. Gene set enrichment analysis49 of NHR-49-bound genes highlighted key categories related to lipid and amino acid metabolism, detoxification and pathogen response, and NHR (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d).

We additionally performed RNA-seq on nhr-49(-) mutant and wild-type control worms at day 1 of adulthood. Differential expression analysis revealed significant up- and downregulation of genes associated with lipid metabolism, stress response, and extracellular matrix remodeling (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b, Supplementary Data 2). As an additional quality control, we compared our nhr-49(-) RNA-seq data to previously published transcriptomic profiles of nhr-49 mutants22,30. We found that our data showed significant overlap with the other two datasets22,30 in both upregulated and downregulated genes, confirming a shared core set of NHR-49–dependent gene expression changes across studies (Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). However, each dataset also displays distinct subsets of regulated genes, suggesting that constitutive loss of nhr-49 function can lead to gene expression changes that are likely to be highly experiment-specific.

Integrating CUT&RUN and RNA-seq data revealed 27 genes that were both bound by NHR-49 and differentially expressed in nhr-49 mutants (Supplementary Fig. 8e, Supplementary Data 3), suggesting they represent direct transcriptional targets of NHR-49. Gene set enrichment analysis indicated that these putative NHR-49 direct targets that become upregulated in nhr-49(-) mutants are enriched for lipid metabolism and pathogen stress response.

NHR-49 represses gsa-1 expression to prevent inappropriate oocyte activation

Among the NHR-49-bound targets, we identified gsa-1, which encodes a Gαs subunit known to act in the gonadal sheath cells to mediate MSP signaling and regulate oocyte activation50. NHR-49 binding peaks were detected at the promoter and second intron of gsa-1 (Fig. 6a). Interestingly, RNA-seq indicated that gsa-1 RNA expression was modestly elevated in nhr-49 mutant (Fig. 6b, Source data), albeit not reaching significance. Importantly, functional analysis showed that RNAi knockdown of gsa-1 suppressed the excessive oocyte laying phenotype of the nhr-49(-);fem-3(-) animals (Fig. 6c, Source data), indicating that elevated gsa-1 expression likely contributes to the aberrant laying of unfertilized oocytes. Since our earlier data indicated that aberrant laying of oocytes led to yolk loss, we next asked whether gsa-1 RNAi would affect yolk levels. We performed gsa-1 RNAi in a nhr-49(-);fem-3(-);VIT-2::GFP background, where VIT-2::GFP serves as a reporter for yolk accumulation. Consistent with the observation that gsa-1 RNAi fully rescued the oocyte-laying phenotype, we found that gsa-1 knockdown also restored yolk retention in these animals (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b, Source data). Together, these results suggested that GSA-1 activity is required for aberrant oocyte activation and the consequent depletion of yolk due to excessive egg laying. Discussion

Our findings uncover a previously unrecognized role for the nuclear hormone receptor NHR-49 in regulating oocyte activation and ovulation in C. elegans. This reproductive role appears to be mediated through the transcriptional repression of gsa-1, a Gαs protein that promotes oocyte activation, and likely occurs via the gonadal sheath cells rather than the germline itself. We find that inappropriate oocyte activation and laying of unfertilized oocytes lead to premature yolk loss and depletion of parental resources. Suppressing yolk loss can partially restore lifespan, linking the reproductive phenotype to diminished somatic maintenance, and offers a partial explanation for the previously described lifespan shortening phenotype of the nhr-49 mutant. While NHR-49 has been extensively characterized as a regulator of lipid metabolism and lifespan, our data further support the idea that metabolic regulators such as NHR-49 influence longevity not only by altering fat metabolism, but by controlling the timing and scale of reproductive investment. Our data suggest that NHR-49 is required to suppress germline proliferation in adult worms subjected to acute nutrient deprivation. We found that nhr-49 mutants fail to reduce mitotic activity4 in the distal germline following short-term starvation, suggesting that NHR-49 mediates nutrient-responsive germline restraint. NHR-49 is also known to be required for adult reproductive diapause (ARD)27, a distinct starvation-induced state in L3/L4 animals characterized by drastic germline shrinkage. While the mechanisms underlying these two responses may differ, the shared requirement for NHR-49 raises the possibility that it acts as a conserved metabolic regulator coordinating reproductive quiescence in response to energetic stress. Given that specific dietary and microbiota-derived metabolites can activate NHR-4936, these findings support a broader model in which NHR-49 functions as a metabolic sensor linking environmental inputs to germline decisions. Our CUT&RUN profiling reveals that NHR-49 binds near the loci of many other nuclear hormone receptors, suggesting that it may serve as a broader transcriptional regulator of the NHR network itself. This opens the possibility that NHR-49 tunes hormonal signaling pathways across tissues in response to metabolic state. Future CUT&RUN studies in fed versus starved animals could determine whether NHR-49 differentially engages or recruits other NHRs depending on metabolic conditions. Additionally, future studies employing acute depletion systems will likely reveal the immediate, direct transcriptional responses to NHR-49 loss from secondary effects that accumulate in constitutive mutants. Genomic profiling coupled with functional analysis, particularly sheath-cell-specific restoration of NHR-49, supports the notion that NHR-49 constrains oocyte activation and ovulation in the absence of sperm by repressing gsa-1 transcription in the sheath cells. Because activation of Gαs-ACY-4 signaling in sheath cells is both necessary and sufficient to drive oocyte maturation independently of sperm50, transcriptional control of gsa-1 abundance by NHR-49 provides an upstream mechanism by which nutritional state can set the threshold for oocyte activation. The gonadal sheath cells, which harbor many functional similarities with mammalian granulosa cells52, thus emerge as a metabolic checkpoint relay: somatic metabolic state, as read out by NHR-49, is translated into regulation of gsa-1 and, consequently, oocyte maturation and ovulation52.

It is tempting to draw a parallel here to the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis of vertebrates, where somatic neurons integrate energy status and gate reproduction accordingly. In mammals, negative energy balance suppresses hormonal signaling that normally stimulates follicular maturation and ovulation, through peripheral factors including leptin, ghrelin, and insulin53, and PPAR family members have been shown to influence this axis during fasting54–56. While the C. elegans gonad is far simpler, the underlying principle appears similar: somatic cells monitor metabolic state and use that information to calibrate germline output by regulating the signaling axis that responds to MSPs, secreted factors that are functionally analogous to follicular hormones. What is striking about our findings is that NHR-49, likely a functional homolog of PPARs, appears to accomplish this through direct transcriptional control of GSA-1, a core signaling component in the gonadal soma. Whether this reflects an ancestral mechanism that was later elaborated into a full neuroendocrine axis in vertebrates remains an open question. Recent identification of specific dietary and microbiota-derived lipid metabolites as upstream activators of NHR-4936 raises the possibility that dietary components can directly influence the threshold for oocyte activation, suggesting that the reproductive gating function we describe here is sensitive to dietary input. Related to this, dietary supplementation with the omega-3 fatty acid alpha-linolenic acid extends lifespan in a manner that requires NHR-49, likely through activation of β-oxidation gene targets57. If dietary lipids can modulate NHR-49 activity in the sheath cells, they would be predicted to affect gsa-1 expression and thereby influence the propensity for oocyte activation proceeds. This notion is consistent with our finding that NHR-49 is necessary for the germline to pause proliferation when animals are starved. Together, the findings reported here position NHR-49 as a metabolic gatekeeper that couples environmental and internal nutritional status to reproductive investment. By limiting oocyte activation and germline proliferation when resources or sperm are lacking, NHR-49 helps align reproductive effort with metabolic capacity, promoting long-term survival and maternal resource preservation.

Methods

C. elegans culture methods and strains

C. elegans strains were maintained using standard protocols. Unless otherwise stated, all strains were grown on nematode growth medium (NGM) plates seeded with E. coli OP50-1 as a food source. The following strains were obtained from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (CGC): N2, DG2189 [fog-3(q443) I/hT2 [bli-4(e937) let-?(q782) qIs48] (I;III); tnIs13 [pie-1p::vab-1::GFP + unc-119(+)] ltIs44 [pie-1p::mCherry::PH(PLC1delta1) + unc-119(+)]; DG2190 [fog-3(q443) I/hT2 [bli-4(e937) let-?(q782) qIs48] (I;III); tnIs13 [pie-1p::vab-1::GFP + unc-119(+)]V]; VC470 [nhr-49 (gk405)]; DCL569 [mkcSi13[sun-1p::rde-1::sun-1 3′UTR + unc-119(+)] II; rde-1(mkc36)V]. These strains were previously generated in our laboratory, combining strains obtained from the CGC: IU613 [fem-3(e1996)/nT1 [qIs51] (IV;V)]; IU615 fem-3(e1996)/nT1 [qIs51] (IV;V); nhr-49(gk405) I; pwIs23 (YP170::GFP).

Lifespan analysis

Lifespan assays were performed as previously described15. 10–15 gravid worms were placed on plates containing the desired bacterial food to obtain approximately 150–200 embryos. Upon reaching day one of adulthood, 30 worms were transferred to each of three RNAi plates and monitored every other day. Worms were gently transferred to fresh plates every other day to prevent starvation and mixing of generations. Care was taken to avoid transferring larvae. Lack of movement in response to gentle touch at the nose was used to confirm death. Worms that died due to rupturing, bursting, or leaving the plate were considered censored. The lifespan data were analyzed using the OASIS online survival analysis tool1 available at https://sbi.postech.ac.kr/oasis2/, and log-rank tests were used to determine statistical significance between groups. All lifespan assays were performed at least twice.

RNAi treatment

The RNAi procedure was carried out as previously described15. RNAi was conducted by feeding the worms with HT115 bacteria that expressed double-stranded RNA of the target gene. The RNAi clones were obtained from RNAi feeding libraries created by Julie Ahringer and Marc Vidal’s laboratories2,3. RNAi bacterial strains were first recovered by streaking a single colony onto LB agar (with 100 μg/mL ampicillin) and incubating the plates overnight at 37 °C. Individual colonies were then inoculated into 5 mL LB medium containing ampicillin (100 μg/mL) and tetracycline (15 μg/mL) and cultured overnight at 37 °C with shaking. To confirm the clones, plasmid DNA was extracted, and the insert was verified by sequencing using the universal T7 promoter primers. Following sequence confirmation, a fresh overnight culture was prepared from a single colony in 5 mL LB containing ampicillin (100 μg/mL) and tetracycline (12.5 μg/mL). In parallel, bacteria carrying the empty L4440 vector were grown as controls in all RNAi experiments. For preparation of RNAi feeding cultures, overnight bacterial cultures were diluted to 5% of the final culture volume into LB medium supplemented with ampicillin (100 μg/mL) and grown for an additional 3–4 h at 37 °C with shaking. When cultures reached an OD600 of approximately 0.6–0.8, IPTG (Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside) was added to a final concentration of 1 mM to induce dsRNA production, and cultures were incubated for a further 2–3 h. Bacteria were subsequently collected by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min at room temperature and concentrated in the appropriate volume of the original supernatant to yield 200 μL culture per plate. For each target gene, two to three RNAi feeding plates were prepared by spreading 200 μL bacterial suspension onto NGM agar plates lacking streptomycin but supplemented with ampicillin (100 μg/mL), tetracycline (15 μg/mL), and IPTG (1 mM). After drying under a laminar flow hood, 10 adult worms were transferred onto each plate and allowed to lay eggs overnight. Adults were then moved to a fresh set of RNAi plates for an additional 2–3 h egg lay to obtain synchronized progeny. The resulting embryos were maintained on the corresponding RNAi treatment until the appropriate developmental stage, at which point phenotypic analyses were performed.

pMPK-1 immunostaining

Approximately 100 worms were grown on OP50-1-seeded NGM plates, then washed off using PBST (PBS + 0.1% Tween-20) and collected in a 1.5 ml Eppendorf tube. The worms were spun for 15 s at 2000 rpm, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was washed with 1X PBS twice, using 1 ml each time. Approximately 50 worms in 100 µl of PBS buffer were then transferred using a cut 200 µl micropipette tip onto a border-painted glass slide. To anesthetize the worms, 5 µl of 5 mM levamisole was added. Using 25- or 26-gauge needles, a cut was made near the pharyngeal bulb of each worm to release gonads, and all animals were dissected within 5–6 min. Dissected gonads were collected into a 1.5 ml tube using PBS-Tween-coated, cut-end 200 µl tips. Excess liquid was removed, and 200 µl of 4% formaldehyde fixative (prepared by mixing 100 µl 10X phosphate buffer, 100 µl 37% formaldehyde, and 800 µl ddH₂O to make a total volume of 1 ml fixative) was added. Samples were incubated at room temperature for 20–30 min. Fixative was then removed, and worms were incubated in 1 ml of chilled methanol (−20 °C) for 5 min. After removing methanol, worms were washed three times with PBST (5 min each). Excess liquid was removed, and worms were resuspended in 200 µl blocking buffer (0.1% Triton X-100, 0.1% Tween-20, and 2% BSA (Bovine Serum Albumin) in 1X PBS) and incubated for 1–2 h. Primary antibody diluted 1:100 in blocking buffer (50 µl per sample) was added and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rocking. To detect the double-phosphorylated form of MPK-1, a mouse monoclonal anti-MAP Kinase, Activated (Diphosphorylated ERK-1&2) antibody (M8159, Millipore Sigma) was used. Samples were then washed three times with blocking buffer for 10 min each. Secondary antibody (1:200 dilution in blocking buffer, 50 µl per sample), consisting of sheep anti-mouse IgG (whole molecule) F(ab′)₂ fragment–Cy3 antibody (C2181, Millipore Sigma), was added together with DAPI (final concentration 5 µg/ml) was added, and samples were incubated for 2 h at room temperature in the dark with rocking. Following three washes with blocking buffer, worms were resuspended in 10 µl of Vectashield mounting medium. Worms were mounted on a 2% agarose pad prepared on a glass slide and covered with a coverslip for microscopy.

EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine) staining

EdU staining was performed as previously described15 using the Click-iT® EdU Imaging Kit (ThermoFisher Cat. No. C10337). For EdU incorporation, 50–100 worms were washed and collected from plates using M9 buffer containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (M9T) and washed twice with 1 mL M9T. 50 µL of M9T containing the worms was transferred into a fresh 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube using a cut tip, followed by the addition of 50 µL of 10 mM EdU. The worms were gently mixed and incubated on a shaker for 40 min at room temperature (22–25 °C). Worms were washed twice with 1 mL M9T. Next, 30 µL of EBT (prepared by mixing 110 µL 10X egg buffer, 10 µL 10% Tween-20, 6.5 µL 10% tetramisole, and 850 µL distilled water to a final volume of 976.5 µL) was pipetted onto a slide, and approximately 50 worms were transferred into the EBT drop. The worms were anesthetized for 10–15 s, and their heads were cut to extrude the gonads. The gonads were collected into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube, fixed with 4% formaldehyde in egg buffer for 20–30 min, and washed twice with 1 mL of 3% BSA in PBS. For permeabilization, the gonads were incubated in 1 mL of 1% Triton X-100 in PBS at room temperature for 20 min. After permeabilization, the buffer was removed, and the samples were washed twice with 1 mL of 3% BSA in PBS. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of the Click-iT® reaction cocktail (as per the kit instructions) was added, and the worms were incubated at room temperature for 30 min on a shaker and covered to protect from light. The reaction cocktail was removed, and the samples were washed twice with 1 mL of 3% BSA in PBS. For the EdU chase experiment, after the 10 mM EdU incorporation step, worms were transferred to the feeding plates for 24 h, and the subsequent steps were performed as described above. Finally, 10 µL of Vectashield was added, and the worms were mounted on a 2% agarose pad prepared on a glass slide for imaging. Images were acquired using a Keyence BZ-X810 microscope.

Fluorescence quantification and imaging

For imaging, 10–20 worms were immobilized on a 2% agarose pad prepared on a glass slide in 10 µL of 5 mM levamisole in PBS. Images were acquired using the appropriate fluorescence channel on a Leica DM5000B compound microscope or a Zeiss LSM 710 or LSM 880 inverted confocal microscope. Images were acquired as Z-stacks, and image tiling was used to scan at 63X magnification under oil immersion. For data processing, ZEN software was used for image stitching, and FIJI was used for image analysis and processing. To generate representative images, Adobe Photoshop was used.

RNA isolation and library preparation for RNA-seq

RNA isolation and RNA-seq library preparation were performed as previously described15. For the collection of whole worm samples, approximately 200 worms were collected in TRI reagent (MRC, Cat. No. TR 118). For tissue-specific analysis, germlines were surgically isolated from 100-150 animals using fine-gauge needle dissection, with 80-100 intact germlines collected per sample in TRI reagent. Two to three biological replicates were collected for each genotype. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the QuantSeq 3′ mRNA-Seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (FWD) (Lexogen), and sequencing was performed by the Biotechnology Resource Center (BRC) of the Cornell Institute of Biotechnology.

RNA-seq data analysis

RNA-seq data analysis was performed as previously described15 with minor modifications. To obtain the total read count of RNA-seq data were processed using Linux on the BioHPC server at Cornell University. Raw sequencing data underwent quality control and preprocessing using Trim Galore (v0.6.5), which incorporates Cutadapt58 (v3.4) and FastQC (v0.11.8). Parameters were set to retain reads with quality scores ≥20 (-q 20) while performing FastQC quality assessment. Preprocessed reads were mapped to the C. elegans reference genome (ce11/WBcel235) using the STAR aligner (v2.7.9a)59, with parameters configured for strict alignment: two computing threads (--runThreadN 2), gene count quantification (--quantMode GeneCounts), unique mapping only (--outFilterMultiMapNmax 1), a maximum of two mismatches (--outFilterMismatchNmax 2), and coordinate-sorted BAM files (--outSAMtype BAM SortedByCoordinate). For 3′ RNA-seq data analysis, the middle column of counts from the STAR-generated tab-delimited files was used to construct expression matrices for comparative analysis. Matrices were organized either by genotype (comparing different mutant strains under identical conditions) or by treatment (comparing identical genotypes under different RNAi conditions). Differential expression analysis was performed in R (RStudio) using DESeq2 (v1.34.0)60. To improve statistical power, genes with fewer than 10 reads across fewer than 2 samples were excluded. Principal component analysis was conducted on variance-stabilizing transformed data (using the vst function), visualized using the plotPCA function with default parameters, and further refined using ggplot2 (v3.3.6). Statistically significant differentially expressed genes were identified using the standard DESeq2 workflow with an adjusted p value threshold of 0.05. For genes of interest, normalized counts were visualized using DESeq2’s plotCounts function. Gene set comparisons between genotypes or between RNA-seq and CUT&RUN datasets were performed using DeepVenn (https://www.deepvenn.com/)61 online software, with statistical significance determined by Fisher’s exact test. Gene set enrichment analysis was conducted using WormCat 2.0 (www.wormcat.com)49, which also employs Fisher’s test with FDR correction. For heatmap generation, log2 fold-change values were extracted from DESeq2 results tables without filtering out low-expression genes. Heatmaps were created using the ComplexHeatmap package (v2.10.0) with row clustering enabled (cluster_rows = TRUE), column clustering disabled (cluster_columns = FALSE), and row names hidden (show_row_names = FALSE). For clustered analyses, heatmaps were partitioned (split = 3), and genes from each cluster were extracted using the row_order function.

Sheath cell-specific NHR-49 strain generation

To restore NHR-49 specifically in the gonadal sheath cells, the Plim-7::nhr-49c::gfp construct was assembled by Gibson assembly. The lim-7 enhancer and the nhr-49 isoform c coding sequence were synthesized as two gBlocks (Twist Bioscience), with the nhr-49c gBlock additionally encoding a 36 bp linker (AGTACTAGCGGTGGCAGTGGAGGTACCGGCGGAAGC) at its 3′ end to fuse nhr-49c in frame to the vector-encoded GFP. The gBlocks were assembled by three-fragment Gibson assembly into the pEB163 (Phyp-7::mlt-4::GFP) backbone, which had been linearized by KpnI digestion to excise the Phyp-7::mlt-4 cassette, placing lim-7 and nhr-49c::linker in frame upstream of GFP. Colonies were screened for correct insert size by PCR using vector-internal primers, and clones of the expected size were verified by whole-plasmid sequencing (Plasmidsaurus). A transgenic strain carrying an extrachromosomal array was then generated by injecting the verified plasmid, along with the co-injection marker Pmyo-2::mCherry, into wild-type animals. Array-positive offspring were identified by pharyngeal mCherry fluorescence and maintained as a stable line, which was subsequently crossed into the nhr-49(−);fem-3(−) background. Sheath-cell expression and nuclear localization of the NHR-49::GFP fusion were confirmed by fluorescence microscopy, and array-negative siblings were scored in parallel as non-transgenic controls.

CUT&RUN for NHR-49 binding sites in the genome

CUT&RUN experiments were performed using our previously published protocol62,63. A sufficient number of embryos were collected from gravid adult worms grown on 10-cm NGM plates seeded with OP50-1. For each CUT&RUN reaction, 3000 embryos were plated onto 15-cm NGM plates supplemented with 1 mL of 25× concentrated OP50-1 culture (grown overnight). Sample preparation was scaled to ensure sufficient worm numbers (3000 animals per reaction) for each experimental condition. Plates were incubated at 25 °C for approximately 48–52 h until populations reached the predominantly young adult stage. At this developmental stage, worms were harvested by washing plates with PBS buffer, and CUT&RUN experiments were performed according to a detailed protocol previously published62, which follows standard CUT&RUN methodology with minor modifications specific to C. elegans samples. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, catalog numbers E7645S and E7335S). Library amplification was performed with 14 PCR cycles according to the manufacturer’s guidelines, with slight procedural adjustments as described in the published protocol64. The resulting libraries underwent paired-end sequencing (2 × 32 bp) on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform, performed by the Biotechnology Resource Center (BRC) of the Cornell Institute of Biotechnology. All CUT&RUN experiments were conducted with at least two independent biological replicates.

CUT&RUN analysis

CUT&RUN analysis was performed as previously described62,64–67. Paired-end reads were trimmed to remove adapters and low-quality bases and aligned to the C. elegans reference genome (ce11/WBcel235) with Bowtie2; PCR duplicates and mitochondrial reads were removed, and reads were concurrently mapped to the spike-in genome to derive per-sample normalization factors. Normalized coverage tracks were generated with deepTools and visualized in IGV. NHR-49 binding sites were identified with MACS2 using the untagged control as background, and peaks reproducible across biological replicates were retained. Peaks were annotated to nearby genes relative to the transcription start site, and functional enrichment of target genes was determined with WormCat.
