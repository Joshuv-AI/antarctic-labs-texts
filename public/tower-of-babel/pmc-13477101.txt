Twenty-five–year single-center experience with aortic root replacement with a composite valve graft
Authors: Rodolfo V. Rocha, Tirone E. David, Anna Chu, Amine Mazine, Syed M.A. Hassan, Dominique Vervoort, Douglas S. Lee, Terrence M. Yau, Maral Ouzounian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe study objective was to evaluate the in-hospital and long-term outcomes of aortic root replacement (Bentall procedure) from a single institution.MethodsAll patients who underwent the Bentall procedure from 1990 to 2014 were included. Aortic valve–sparing root replacement or Ross procedures were excluded. The primary end point was all-cause mortality. The secondary end point was major adverse valve-related events, a composite of death, aortic valve reoperation (surgical or transcatheter), aortic valve endocarditis, major bleeding, and the need for a permanent pacemaker or defibrillator.ResultsA total of 1332 adults underwent a Bentall procedure during the study period, with 525 (39.4%) undergoing concomitant procedures and 807 (60.6%) undergoing the isolated Bentall. Mean age was 57.6 ± 15.6 years, with 130 patients (9.8%) having endocarditis and 400 patients (30.0%) having a previous cardiac surgery. Isolated aortic insufficiency was present in 600 patients (45%), and a mechanical valve was used in 516 of 1332 patients (38.7%). In-hospital or 30-day death occurred in 65 of 1332 patients (4.9%), which was worse among those undergoing concomitant valve or coronary bypass, 45 of 525 (8.6%) versus 20 of 807 (2.5%) in patients undergoing the isolated Bentall (P < .01). Patients undergoing an elective, first-time isolated Bentall (471 cases) had less than 1% mortality and stroke rates. Overall, 25-year survival was 33.8% (95% CI, 29.9-37.8), worse among those undergoing concomitant procedures (hazard ratio, 2.14; 95% CI, 1.83-2.50). Late survival after an isolated Bentall was worse than in the age- and sex-matched general population (hazard ratio, 1.49; 95% CI, 1.33-1.68).ConclusionsBentall procedures are associated with excellent short-term outcomes in a complex population with a large number of previous operations, endocarditis, and concomitant procedures. Long-term survival is worse than in age- and sex-matched controls, particularly among younger patients.

Central Message

The Bentall procedure is associated with excellent short-term outcomes even in a complex population. Long-term survival is worse compared with the general population, particularly among younger patients.

Perspective

Bentall procedures are associated with excellent short-term outcomes, even in a complex population, but long-term excess mortality and adverse events remain a challenge.

Surgical aortic root replacement with a composite valved graft (also known as the “modified Bentall procedure”) is the standard of care for patients with unrepairable aortic valves and aortic root pathologies. In the most recent Society of Thoracic Surgeons report of outcomes after aortic root replacement procedures in North America, elective root surgery had a 2.2% operative mortality.1 In the same study, a Bentall procedure was associated with an increased risk of mortality (odds ratio, 2.08; 95% CI, 1.13-3.81) compared with valve-sparing root replacement.

Late adverse events after a Bentall procedure include valve-related complications such as thromboembolism, anticoagulation-related bleeding, prosthetic valve endocarditis, prosthetic valve degeneration, or progression of distal aortic disease.2 These complications are influenced by several factors, including patient factors (age, sex, presence and severity of comorbidities, socioeconomic status), cardiac function (ejection fraction and severity of coronary disease), and type of valve implanted (mechanical, stented bioprosthesis, stentless bioprosthesis).

Patients with aortic root pathologies often present other associated cardiac valvulopathies or severe coronary artery disease.3,4 Mitral valve, tricuspid valve, and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) during Bentall procedures are common.5 Historically, most reports of patients undergoing Bentall procedures with these concomitant procedures have observed worse perioperative and long-term survival compared with patients undergoing isolated Bentall procedures.

Another limitation of studies evaluating the outcomes of patients who undergo a Bentall procedure is that follow-up duration is limited, with few reports describing survival and adverse events past 10 to 15 years.5,6 Thus, we sought to evaluate in-hospital and long-term outcomes of modified Bentall procedures from a single institution.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

We used the Toronto General Hospital cardiac surgery database (TGH-db) to identify all Bentall procedures performed from 1990 to 2014. The study period was truncated at 2014 to include homogeneous perioperative data from our original surgical database and to have the longest possible follow-up data in this patient cohort. The TGH-db is a prospectively collected database that was established in 1990 and collects detailed preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative data on all patients undergoing cardiac surgery at the Toronto General Hospital. The TGH-db was linked to multiple population-based health databases from the province of Ontario using patient-level encrypted health card numbers at ICES (formerly the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences) to determine long-term outcomes. ICES is an independent, nonprofit research institute whose legal status under Ontario's health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyze health care and demographic data, without consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. The use of data in this project was authorized under section 45 of Ontario's Personal Health Information Protection Act, which does not require review by a Research Ethics Board.

Data Sources and Definitions

All patients undergoing a Bentall procedure with or without concomitant procedures (mitral or tricuspid valve repair/replacement or CABG), emergency cases, and reoperations were included. If a patient underwent multiple aortic root procedures at our institution, only the index operation was included. We excluded 219 patients undergoing valve-sparing root replacement and 57 Ross procedures that occurred during our study period (1990-2014). Finally, 169 international and out-of-province patients were not included because they did not have a provincial health card number to enable tracking of their long-term outcomes. There were 3 index hospitalization variables with missing data in the TGH-db: preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin, and whether the patient required blood transfusion. We therefore did not include these data. Our outcomes of interest were obtained through linkage of multiple databases of routinely collected health and vital statistics data from our single-payer universal health coverage system. Therefore, we had no missing data for late events, and the follow-up was 100% complete.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest of the study was all-cause mortality. Our secondary outcome of interest was major adverse valve-related events (MAVRE), a composite of death, aortic valve reoperation (surgical or transcatheter), aortic valve endocarditis, major bleeding, and the need for a permanent pacemaker (PPM) or defibrillator. Tertiary outcomes included the individual components of MAVRE. Deaths were determined using the Registered Persons Database, which contains vital status information for all residents of Ontario. Rates of the individual components of MAVRE were identified using previously validated algorithms through the Canadian Institute for Health Information Discharge Abstract Database (aortic valve endocarditis, major bleeding, and the need for a PPM or defibrillator), which contains information from all acute care hospitalizations in Ontario.7, 8, 9 Finally, the CorHealth registry, which captures procedural details on all Ontario residents who undergo cardiac surgery, and the Ontario Hospital Insurance Plan Schedule of Billings database, which tracks physician payments for procedures, were used to identify any aortic valve reoperation (surgical or transcatheter).

All baseline demographics were obtained from the TGH-db, except for neighborhood income quintile and rurality of residence. Statistics Canada's census data were used to determine the neighborhood income of individuals' residences at the time of surgery to serve as a proxy for socioeconomic status. These datasets were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analyzed at ICES.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as the mean ± SD or median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. As per ICES privacy regulations, all cells with fewer than 6 events were presented as “≤ 5” to avoid potential patient identification.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis identified preoperative risk factors for long-term mortality. Kaplan–Meier and cumulative incidence functions were estimated for long-term survival, MAVRE, and the individual components of the composite. Cause-specific hazard models were generated for patients undergoing isolated Bentall versus Bentall procedures with a concomitant valve or CABG, accounting for death as a competing risk where appropriate.10

Given the distinct perioperative and late risk of patients undergoing Bentall procedures with versus without concomitant procedures in the literature, we examined both groups separately and evaluated whether outcomes were different between the 2 groups. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4.5 (SAS Institute, Inc).

Survival After Isolated Bentall Procedure Versus General Population

Each patient who underwent an isolated Bentall procedure was matched to 3 random individuals from Ontario based on age and sex to compare survival after an isolated Bentall to a control population without a history of the procedure. Kaplan–Meier curves of long-term survival and Cox proportional hazards models were generated for patients undergoing an isolated Bentall compared with the general population. A predetermined subgroup analysis was performed to compare survival after an isolated Bentall to the general population stratified by age (<50, 50-59, 60-69, or ≥70 years).

Results

Population Characteristics

From January 1, 1990, to August 31, 2014, 1549 Bentall procedures were performed at the Toronto General Hospital. Among them, 1332 of 1549 (86.0%) occurred in Ontarians with a valid health card, comprising our study population. There were 400 of 1332 cases (30.0%) with a previous sternotomy, of which 315 of 1332 (23.6%) were patients with a previous aortic valve/root operation. Isolated Bentall procedures were performed in 807 of 1332 cases (60.6%), with the remaining 525 of 1332 (39.4%) undergoing concomitant valve procedures or CABG (Table 1). The mean overall age was 57.6 ± 15.6 years, 354 of 1332 (26.6%) were women, and 1006 of 1332 (75.5%) were elective cases. Aortic valve endocarditis and acute aortic dissection were the indications for surgery in 130 of 1332 cases (9.8%) and 78 of 1332 cases (5.9%), respectively. With regard to valve pathology, 674 of 1332 patients (50.6%) had aortic valve stenosis and 600 of 1332 patients (45.0%) had isolated insufficiency. Among patients undergoing isolated Bentall procedures with aortic insufficiency, 266 of 383 (69.5%) had a bicuspid aortic valve or were operated for aortic dissection or endocarditis, or in a redo setting. Risk Factors for Late Death

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis identified previous cerebrovascular accident (HR, 1.81 [95% CI, 1.15-2.87], P = .03), a Charlson score greater than 2 (HR, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.20-2.10], P < .01), and having an active aortic valve abscess (HR, 2.74 [95% CI, 1.25-5.99], P = .01) as independent risk factors for long-term mortality after isolated Bentall procedures.

Of note, the rates of aortic valve and aortic reoperation at 25 years were 16.8% (95% CI, 14.2-19.7) and 13.0% (95% CI, 10.5-15.7), respectively (Figures E1 and E2), and the rate of overall cardiac reoperation (aortic valve, mitral valve, tricuspid valve, or CABG) was 33.6% (95% CI, 30.2-37.0) (Figure E3). The rate of PPM requirement was 24.0 (95% CI, 21.4-26.7) (Figure E4) and endocarditis was 5.0% (95% CI, 3.7-6.7) (Figure E5). Among the 153 patients who required a PPM postdischarge from the index operation, 70 initially underwent a Bentall with a concomitant procedure as their index operation and 83 had an isolated Bentall. Among these, 119 of 153 (77.8%) had their PPM implanted within 30 days after a cardiac reintervention after discharge from the index operation.

Bioprosthetic Versus Mechanical Bentall Procedures

When stratifying our population by type of valve used (biological vs mechanical), we observed that there was no significant difference in in-hospital or 30-day mortality (43/816 [5.3%] vs 22/516 [4.3%], P = .41) or in-hospital stroke (25/816 [3.1%] vs 18/516 [3.5%], P = .88) for patients having biological or mechanical valves, respectively.

The cumulative incidence of reoperation for aortic valve replacement at 25 years was 4.5% (95% CI, 2.5-7.3) for mechanical valves and 25.1% (95% CI, 21.6-30.4) for biological valves (P ≤ .01). The 25-year survival probability of patients with mechanical conduits was better compared with biological valves (43.6% [95% CI, 34.0-50.0] vs 27.6% [95% CI, 22.8-32.7], P ≤ .01).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest single-center report of 25-year outcomes of patients undergoing a modified Bentall procedure with 100% completeness of follow-up. The main findings of our study are the following: (1) Despite a complex population including reoperations, endocarditis, type A dissections, and concomitant procedures, patients undergoing Bentall procedures had excellent in-hospital outcomes; (2) despite encouraging short-term outcomes, long-term survival remains a challenge, particularly for young patients, with a worse life expectancy compared with the general population observed in every age category until age 70 years; (3) the need for postoperative pacemaker implantation is significant and continues to occur after discharge; (4) the need for any cardiac reoperation (not related to the aortic root) is high, even among isolated Bentall procedures; and (5) aortic valve morphology (bicuspid vs tricuspid), mode of valvular dysfunction (stenosis vs insufficiency vs mixed), and aortic prosthesis type (mechanical vs stented vs stentless biological) did not influence long-term survival.

The volume/outcome relationship in aortic root procedures has been evaluated by Hughes and colleagues,11 with North American institutions performing more than 30 to 40 aortic root procedures yearly having improved outcomes. Our favorable early results in this complex population may be due to the high volume of aortic root procedures performed. We observed a 2.5% mortality in isolated Bentall procedures spanning 24 years, in a population in whom 7% of patients had aortic valve endocarditis, 20% of patients had a previous aortic valve or root intervention, 25% of patients had a previous sternotomy, and 21% were nonscheduled cases. Mortality and stroke rate of first-time, isolated elective Bentall procedures were less than 1% each. Wallen and colleagues1 reported North America's short-term results after scheduled aortic root procedures, using the Society of Thoracic Surgeons database from 2011 to 2016 after excluding endocarditis, reoperations, and concomitant arch procedures. In-hospital or 30-day mortality was 2.2%, with a Bentall procedure increasing the odds of death by 33%.

We observed a continuous decline in survival after a Bentall procedure, with only 45.5% of patients being alive 20 years postprocedure. This steady decline in survival is similar to what has been observed in other large series.5 After adjusting for age and sex, we observed a significant survival penalty in patients who had root replacements with prosthetic valves when compared with the general population. This survival penalty was most pronounced in the youngest patients but persisted in older patients until age 70 years. After 70 years of age, there was no difference in life expectancy of patients undergoing Bentall procedures compared with age- and sex-matched controls. We believe that the younger a patient is at the time of a Bentall procedure, the more time they are exposed to the lifelong inherited risks of having an artificial valve (structural and nonstructural valve deterioration, infective endocarditis, bleeding, thromboembolic events), which may reduce their life expectancy compared with the general population. Therefore, if valve replacement can be avoided, reconstructive options such as valve-preserving operations or Ross procedure would be preferred in young patients.

A large proportion of our population undergoing isolated Bentall procedures presented with isolated aortic insufficiency (47.5%). Among those, the majority (70%) had a bicuspid aortic valve or were operated for aortic dissection or endocarditis, or in a redo setting. In our current practice, our preference is to perform a valve-sparing root replacement in patients with isolated aortic insufficiency and aortic root pathology given the excellent long-term outcomes survival similar to the general population.12 Nonetheless, we were more conservative in sparing valves, particularly bicuspid valves, in the earlier years of our practice. Furthermore, the complex nature of this population, with a large proportion of them having previous aortic valve/root surgery or aortic valve endocarditis precluded valve preservation.

Patients requiring concomitant valve or CABG experienced worse outcomes compared with those requiring an isolated Bentall. Concomitant procedures were associated with inferior short-term outcomes (mortality, dialysis, need for PPM, myocardial infarction) and long-term outcomes (survival, freedom from MAVRE, stroke, need for PPM). Their worse baseline status, in addition to a more extensive procedure, reflected in longer aortic crossclamp and cardiopulmonary bypass time, may have led to higher rates of perioperative complications. In addition, their worse long-term-outcomes, including survival, are likely related mostly to their baseline comorbidities that led them to have aortic valve/root disease and an associated valve or CABG. Careful consideration of surgical eligibility for patients requiring Bentall and multiple concomitant procedures needs to be undertaken given their higher risk of morbidity and mortality.

The need for aortic valve reinterventions was higher in patients undergoing an isolated Bentall compared with concomitant Bentall procedures. We believe patients with concomitant procedures have a higher perioperative risk presenting for a reoperation and therefore deemed not eligible for aortic valve reinterventions as patients who underwent an isolated Bentall.

In our unadjusted Kaplan–Meier survival probability analysis comparing biological versus mechanical Bentall procedures, 25-year survival was greater in the mechanical subgroup. Nonetheless, in our multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis, long-term survival was not significantly different between mechanical or biological valve conduits, suggesting that baseline demographics (accounted for in the Cox analysis) likely influence the long-term outcomes more rather than the valve type, per se.

Postoperative PPM implantation rates were high in our study, with 9.6% of our overall population and 7.2% of isolated Bentall procedures requiring one before hospital discharge. The relatively large proportion of patients with previous aortic valve/root procedures or endocarditis who may have required extensive annular debridement or reconstruction may explain our rates of conduction problems. Nevertheless, the need for PPMs continued to increase during follow-up, with a cumulative incidence of more than 20% at 25 years. Most studies do not report long-term rates of PPM implantation after Bentall procedures.13, 14, 15, 16 Our results should encourage other centers to report their long-term PPM rates allowing proper evaluation of this adverse event that has been poorly reported to date.

Structural valve deterioration leading to reoperation of the aortic valve is expected in the long-term, particularly in cases of a bioprosthetic valve. Nonetheless, we also observed that more than 25% of patients required various cardiac reoperations, mostly due to cardiac pathologies other than the aortic valve/root. The risk of other valvular or coronary disease requiring surgery after correction of aortic root pathology in this cohort needs to be better understood.

Limitations

Our results must be interpreted in the context of some limitations. Our findings may not be generalizable to other institutions, because this is a single-center study where the long-term results reflect the clinical care and follow-up within the Canadian single-payer health system. In addition, the large institutional volume may have influenced the short-term outcomes given the well-established volume-outcome relationships in complex cardiac surgical operations. Despite 100% completeness of follow-up, we could not evaluate the cause of death or reason for reoperation during the follow-up period. Furthermore, a significant limitation is that we do not have echocardiographic data to determine prosthetic valve function in follow-up. In addition, our historical database does not specify if the bypass was performed as a salvage maneuver during a Bentall procedure. Nonetheless, we believe this is a small percentage of the concomitant CABGs performed during the Bentall procedures. We unfortunately do not have information about heritable thoracic aortic disease or genetic aortopathy. Finally, we were not able to account for comorbidities when comparing patients undergoing an isolated Bentall with age- and sex-matched controls.

Conclusions

Bentall procedures are associated with excellent short-term outcomes, even in a complex population. Long-term survival and adverse events remain a challenge, particularly the need for PPM implantation and cardiac or valvular reoperation.
