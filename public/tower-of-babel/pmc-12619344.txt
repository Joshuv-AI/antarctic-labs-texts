Education, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behaviors and female infertility: mendelian randomization study
Authors: Yiyuan Qu, Xujia Huang, Jinqiao Mi, Xuzhi Chen, Bingbing Wu, Jian Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have indicated that education, socioeconomic status, and leisure sedentary behavior may be associated with female infertility. However, it remains unclear whether these associations imply causal relationships.MethodsGenetic variants from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of education, socioeconomic status, and leisure sedentary behaviors were obtained from the UK Biobank and MRC-IEU database (Medical Research Council Integrative Epidemiology Unit), female infertility data was acquired from the FinnGen Biobank. Univariable and multivariable MR analyses were performed to explore the relationships between these traits and female infertility.ResultsThe results of the univariate MR analysis indicated that age of full-time education had a protective effect on female infertility (odds ratio [OR] 0.471; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.24 to 0.93; p = 0.03). Multivariable MR and reverse MR studies support the existence of a relationship between them. However, no causal correlation was found between other traits and female infertility. No significant heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy was detected, and the stability of the results was confirmed through sensitivity analysis and the leave-one-out test.ConclusionsA later age of completion of full-time education may be causally related to a reduced risk of female infertility, but no causality is established between other educational levels factors, socioeconomic status, or sedentary behaviors and infertility risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-03849-2.

Introduction

Infertility, a medical condition affecting approximately 186 million individuals worldwide, ranks as the third most prevalent health issue after cancer and cardiovascular diseases [1, 2]. It profoundly impacts individuals and families, and also imposes a significant financial burden on society [3]. Ignoring this public health challenge can lead to societal pressures and exclusions, triggering anxiety, depression, and other mental health issues in patients [4, 5]. This situation underscores the critical need for preventive measures. Therefore, early identification of infertility risk factors, comprehensive assessment, and effective diagnosis and treatment are urgent and crucial tasks facing reproductive medicine today.

Past epidemiological studies have indicated that female infertility may be associated with socio-economic factors and unhealthy lifestyle habits [6–8]. As research progresses, it has been discerned that a woman's education level, socioeconomic status, and leisure sedentary behaviors may be correlated with the incidence of infertility. For instance, Brenna E Swift et al. revealed that fertility awareness among reproductive age Canadian women correlates with education level [9]. Iba Arisa et al. discovered that among Japanese women who experienced fertility problems, higher household income was associated with a higher infertility clinic attendance probability [10]. This tendency may arise from the informational advantage conferred by their higher levels of education [11].Additionally, another study found increased leisure-time sedentary behaviors can have adverse effects on female fertility, which may be attributable to an unhealthy lifestyle [12]. However, these studies offer only a partial retrospective understanding of the relationship between education, socioeconomic status, sedentary leisure behavior, and female infertility, which complicates the establishment of effective preventive measures for female infertility.

The causality between educational level, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behaviors, and female infertility remains elusive due to the limitations inherent in traditional observational studies and the confounding effects of various factors. Mendelian Randomization (MR), a statistical technique that leverages single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) to emulate the randomization present in controlled trials, is increasingly utilized to dissect causal relationships among diverse health outcomes [13].

Previous MR studies suggested that education attainment correlate with a reduced risk of female infertility [14, 15]. Nonetheless, they have largely overlooked distinctions such as the age at which educational attainment was achieved, college graduation status, and detailed educational years. Furthermore, no comprehensive study has examined these factors in an integrated manner. Furthermore, there has been no comprehensive study examined the association between. This research addresses this gap, exploring the causal links among socioeconomic status, leisure-time sedentary behaviors, and female infertility.

Method

Study design

The design and implementation of this MR study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE-MR) checklist (Supplemental Table S1) [16]. The schematic workflow of the MR study is depicted in Fig. 1. In this investigation, we performed a two-sample MR analysis to explore the causal relationships between education, socioeconomic status, as well as leisure sedentary behaviors and female infertility using summary statistics from genome-wide association studies (GWAS). To ensure unbiased causal effects, the MR analysis must satisfy three assumptions: 1) All chosen instrumental variables (IVs) should have a strong correlation with the exposure; 2) All selected IVs must be independent of any confounding factors; 3) All chosen IVs should influence the outcome solely through the exposure [17].Fig. 1Diagram for assumptions of mendelian randomization analyses. Data sources

In our study, the GWAS datasets derived from two esteemed sources: the FinnGen consortium and UK Biobank OpenGWAS project from the Medical Research Council Integrative Epidemiology Unit (MRC-IEU). The FinnGen consortium (https://r8.finngen.fi/) is a large-scale genetic research initiative, compiling GWAS and PheWAS results from 500,000 Finnish biobank participants to explore the relationship between genomic information and health characteristics within the Finnish population. Concurrently, the MRC-IEU GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk) stands as a large-scale open-source genomic project [18]. It encompasses 346,793,265,925 genetic associations, derived from a comprehensive collection of 50,044 GWAS summary datasets. GWAS of female infertility

The genetic data for female infertility were obtained from FinnGen database, comprising 75,450 individuals ((6,481 cases, 68,969 controls) and 16,377,038 SNPs. The summary-level data covered women of European descent, with participants in the case group self-reporting or being diagnosed with infertility as classified by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes N14.

GWAS of education, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behaviors

In prior research, Neale et al. identified 226,899 participants and 10,894,596 SNPs associated with the age at completion of full-time education (ukb-a-505). Loh PR et al. conducted an analysis that extracted 470,941 samples of individuals who completed college (ebi-a-GCST90029012) and 461,457 individuals with different years of education (ebi-a-GCST90029013) to explore the genomics of varying levels of education attainment. These two datasets provided relevant data from both the age of completion of education and the level of education achieved by participants, facilitating a comprehensive acquisition of information from multiple perspectives. Consequently, our study integrated these datasets from the MRC-IEU GWAS database to leverage this multidimensional information.

In addition, genetic associations with socioeconomic status and leisure sedentary behaviors were also obtained from MRC-IEU. For socioeconomic status, Ben Elsworth et al. identified 263,615 individuals engaged in heavy manual or physical labor (ukb-b-2002), 397,751 with data on average total household income before tax (ukb-b-7408), 462,464 with Townsend Deprivation Index scores at recruitment (ukb-b-10011), and 461,369 involved in leisure and social activities such as pubs or social clubs (ukb-b-4171). Our research specifically examines particular forms of leisure sedentary behaviors, including driving, computer use, watching television, and frequenting bars. These activities collectively encompass a significant proportion of individuals'leisure time in contemporary daily life. Regarding sedentary behaviors, the study encompassed three distinct traits: 310,555 individuals reported time spent driving (ukb-b-3793), 360,895 on computer use (ukb-b-4522), and 437,887 on time spent watching television (ukb-b-5192).

Socioeconomic status is a multifaceted construct, with manual labor indicating lower status and average household income reflecting economic strength. The Townsend deprivation index reveals community poverty levels, while engagement in leisure activities such as attending pubs or social clubs suggests a higher socioeconomic status and active social participation. Additionally, sedentary behavior is often associated with unhealthy lifestyle habits. These aspects provide a comprehensive view of an individual's economic and social standing.

All participants in this study were selected from the European ancestry in order to reduce pleiotropic deviation from cross-lineage cases.

Genetic instrument selection

In this study, education, socioeconomic status, and leisure sedentary behaviors served as exposure factors, while female infertility was employed as the outcome indicator. To satisfy the three core assumptions, we employed the following set of IVs: 1) SNPs were initially identified at a genome-wide significance threshold of p < 5 × 10−8. 2) Linkage disequilibrium tests were conducted across SNPs using the European 1000 Genomes Project reference panel (R2 < 0.01 and clump distance > 10,000 kb) [17]. SNPs in linkage disequilibrium were assessed, and the SNP with a higher p value was removed. 3) LDlinK in the LDproxy platform (https://ldlink.nci.nih.gov/) was utilized to search for and identify overlapping proxies with high LD (r2 > 0.80), and overlapping SNPs were discarded. 4) The PhenoScanner GWAS database (v2; http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) was consulted to identify potential associations between instrument SNPs and confounding phenotypes related to the outcome [19]. If a clear association was present, the SNP was removed. 5) In this MR study, participants were primarily of European descent, which avoided the issue of population stratification and prevent a violation of the independence hypothesis. Finally, the strength of genetic instrumental variables is assessed using the F-value, calculated by the equation F = R2 (n-2)/(1-R2), where R2 represents the proportion of sedentary behavior variance explained by genetic variance or SNPs, and n is the sample size. An F-value greater than 10 suggests a potentially low risk of weak instrument bias in MR analysis [20].

Mendelian randomization analysis

This study employed the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method as the primary approach, supplemented by MR Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode as validation methods, to explore the genetic associations between education, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behaviors and female infertility. Consistency in the direction of estimates across all MR methods enhanced confidence in the causal relationships [17]. We employed sensitivity analysis to testify potential biases that may affect the results of the MR analysis and to assess the impact of these biases on the outcomes, thereby enhancing the credibility of causal inferences, such as genetic pleiotropy and data heterogeneity. To ensure results reliability, Cochran's Q statistics and funnel plots were utilized for heterogeneity analysis and visualization. The cutoff p-value for the Cochran Q statistic was set at 0.05, where P > 0.05 indicates no heterogeneity among instrumental variables [21]. The MR Egger intercept test and MR pleiotropy residual sum and outliers (MR-PRESSO) global test were applied to assess horizontal pleiotropy [21, 22]. For the Egger intercept test to indicate horizontal pleiotropy, the MR Egger regression intercept must be greater than 0 with a statistically significant result (P < 0.05) [23]. Additionally, the MR-PRESSO global test, as a supplementary method, mainly employs the MR-PRESSO distortion test to identify horizontal pleiotropy, with P < 0.05 indicating its presence [24, 25]. Finally, as a critical sensitivity tool, leave-one-out analysis was conducted to test result sensitivity, it entails sequentially excluding one SNP at a time to assess potential biases or outliers. All statistical analysis and visualization were performed using R software (version 4.2.0), utilizing the TwoSampleMR, Mendelian Randomization, and MR-PRESSO packages.

Results

Univariable MR analyses

After removing SNPs with LD, palindromic SNPs and duplicated SNPs, we have extracted 6 ~ 207 valid SNPs as genetic instruments for education, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behaviors traits. Among these, 19 independent and significant SNPs were associated with age at completion of full-time education, 200 associated with college completion, 207 associated with years of education, 22 associated with job involves heavy manual or physical work, 45 associated with average total household income before tax, 18 associated with townsend deprivation index at recruitment, 16 associated with leisure social activities pub or social club, 6 associated with time spent driving, 78 associated with time spent using computer and 110 associated with time spent watching television, respectively. The F statistics were all higher than 10, ranging from 28.91 to 170.28 indicating a low risk of weak instrument bias in the MR analyses. The lists of instrumental SNPs of education, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behaviors phenotypes finally for female infertility, which were obtained from MR analyses, are provided in online Supplemental Table S2. As the data presented in Figs. 2, 3, 4, and 5 and Table 2, the results showed that an older age at completion of full-time education is associated with a protective effect against female infertility, since an OR estimated by the IVW method was 0.471(95%CI: 0.238–0.93, P = 0.03) and weighted median method was 0.40 (95%CI: 0.17–0.95, P = 0.04), respectively. However, this causal relationship has not been confirmed by other models, such as OR estimated by the MR Egger method was 0.15(95%CI: 0.01–3.33, P = 0.25), simple mode method was 0.34 (95%CI: 0.07–1.58, P = 0.18), and weighted mode was 0.35(95%CI: 0.07–1.64, P = 0.20). In addition, no significant genetic association was detected between college completion and years of education and female infertility risk. Regarding socioeconomic status, no significantly genetic association between four socioeconomic status traits and female infertility risk was detected, the OR of job involves heavy manual or physical work was 0.15(95%CI: 0.86–2.66, P = 0.15),average total household income before tax was 0.77(95%CI: 0.52–1.15, P = 0.20), townsend deprivation index at recruitment was 0.73(95%CI: 0.35–1.54, P = 0.41), and leisuresocial activities Pub or social club was 0.79(95%CI: 0.12–5.38, P = 0.81) respectively by using a IVW method. In addition, no significant genetic association was detected between time spent driving (OR 1.61; 95%CI 0.37–0.70, P = 0.53), time spent using computer (OR 0.84; 95%CI 0.58–1.22, P = 0.36), as well as time spent watching television (OR 1.29; 95%CI 0.88–1.89, P = 0.19) and female infertility risk. The Cochran’s Q test indicated no heterogeneity between education, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behaviors traits and female infertility. Additionally, the MR-Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO global tests revealed that there was no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy between education, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behaviors and female infertility (Table 3). Multivariable MR analyses

To adjust for other traits and explore potential mediators, we also conducted a multivariate MR on the association between educations, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behaviors traits with female infertility. As shown in Table 4, we harvested a combination of 319 SNPs associated with education attainment, 43 SNPs associated with socioeconomic status and 102 SNPs associated with leisure sedentary behaviors to conduct multivariable MR analyses. Three factors related to educational attainment, including age completed full-time education (OR 1.54; 95%CI 0.68 to 3.50; p = 0.30), college completion (OR 4.49; 95%CI 0.46 to 43.70; p = 0.20), and years of education (OR 0.86; 95%CI 0.69 to 1.06; p = 0.16), did not have a significant independent impact on the genetic causal effect on female infertility risk. Four socioeconomic status traits, including of job involves heavy manual or physical work (OR 1.91; 95%CI 0.86 to 4.20; p = 0.11), average total household income before tax (OR 1.55; 95%CI 0.81 to 2.97; p = 0.19), townsend deprivation index at recruitment (OR 1.55; 95%CI 0.68 to 3.51; p = 0.30), and leisuresocial activities Pub or social club (OR 0.58; 95%CI 0.13 to 2.64; p = 0.48) were not found to be the main factors contributing to the genetic causal effect on female infertility risk. In addition, leisure sedentary behaviors, such as time spent driving(OR 1.16; 95%CI 0.44 to 3.03; p = 0.76), time spent using computer(OR 0.95; 95%CI 0.49 to 1.84; p = 0.89) and time spent watching TV(OR 1.61; 95%CI 0.85 to 3.06; p = 0.14) were not independently and predominately responsible for the genetic causal effect on female infertility risk. Reverse mendelian analysis

To eliminate the bias of reverse causation, an evaluation was conducted on the causal impact of female infertility on education, socioeconomic status and leisure sedentary behaviors. We extracted 10 SNPs strongly and independently predicting female infertility (Supplemental Table S2). As shown in Tables 5 and 6, bidirectional MR analyses did not find a significant genetic association between female infertility and factors such as age completed full-time education, college completion, years of education, job involving heavy manual or physical work, average total household income before tax, Townsend deprivation index at recruitment,participation in leisure social activities like going to a pub or social club, time spent driving, time spent using a computer, and time spent TV. The Cochran’s Q test indicated no heterogeneity between female infertility and education, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behaviors traits. Additionally, the MR-Egger intercept and MR-PRESSO global tests revealed that there was no evidence of unbalanced horizontal pleiotropy in the MR-IVW analyses (Table 5). Finally, the results of the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that detected estimation effects were not dependent on specific SNPs. Discussion

Infertility is becoming a global challenge in both medical and social aspects [24, 25]. Epidemiological studies found potential relationships between educational level, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behavior, and female infertility [9, 10, 26]. However, it is still unclear whether there is a causal relationship between these factors and female infertility. MR is a randomized controlled type of study that uses genetic variants as tools to investigate the causal relationship between specific exposures and specific outcomes [27]. Compared to traditional clinical research, MR can overcome limitations such as study design, sample size, natural medical history, and confounding factors, and has therefore been widely applied [13]. In our recent study, we conducted a comprehensive MR analysis to examine the potential causal relationship between education, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behaviors and female infertility. The findings revealed that the age at which individuals completed their full-time education had a protective effect on female infertility. However, no significant genetic associations were found between the other two educational levels, four socioeconomic statuses, three leisure sedentary behaviors and female infertility. The robustness of these genetic relationships was confirmed through sensitivity analysis and pleiotropy analysis. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first of its kind to investigate the causal link between education, socioeconomic status, sedentary behavior and female infertility using a large-scale dataset. The findings have the potential to enhance our understanding of this area and inform preventive measures based on previous associations.

Currently, it is believed that there may be various factors influencing the relationship between education level and female infertility. These factors can include age, economic conditions and family planning [9, 28–30]. There are significant differences in total fertility rates among women with different education levels, and this descriptive evidence and theoretically plausible mechanisms suggest a possible causal relationship between female education and lifetime fertility rates [30, 31]. In addition, some studies found a clear link between education level and fertility rates as well as infant health [31, 32]. Education is also closely associated with factors such as economic income, religious beliefs, contraceptive prevalence, family planning, family adaptability and the stress related to infertility [33–35]. In this study, we found that age at completion of full-time education may confer protective effects against female infertility. It may offer protection by increasing health awareness, promoting healthy behaviors, improving access to healthcare services, facilitating fertility planning, and providing enhanced socioeconomic resources and social support; however, further investigation is required to fully elucidate these associations. The age at completion of full-time education may affect fertility awareness and fertility capability, thereby influencing the occurrence of female infertility [36]. However, college education itself does not directly cause female infertility. Interestingly, both multivariable MR and reverse MR analyses show no significant causal relationship between educational attainment and female infertility. The observed correlation may be attributed to the multifaceted influence of education on fertility, which encompasses physiological, psychological, and socio-economic factors. Exploring the mediating variables and horizontal pleiotropy of the impact of educational attainment on female infertility is an important direction for future research.

The relationship between socioeconomic status and female infertility is a complex issue. It is believed that there is a certain association between socioeconomic status and female infertility, but the specific degree of impact and mechanisms may vary depending on individuals, regions, and countries [37, 38]. In terms of fertility treatment, research in high-income countries suggests that higher socioeconomic status is related to the possibility of assisted reproductive technology (ART) treatment [39]. This may be attributed to the ability to afford the cost of fertility treatment and access high-quality healthcare services, especially when ART requires more economic support and technical assurance [40]. Furthermore, socioeconomic factors such as average income, employment status, age at first marriage, life expectancy and infant mortality rate are related to fertility rates [11, 41, 41, 42]. These factors may indirectly affect women’s fertility and the risk of infertility. Additionally, socioeconomic factors, lifestyle and environmental conditions may interact with each other and potentially affect the reproductive system function of both men and women [42]. Our MR study does not support the presence of a relationship between female infertility and socioeconomic status indicators, including occupations involving heavy manual or physical labor, average total household income before tax, the townsend deprivation index at recruitment, and participation in leisure social activities at pubs or social clubs. It is possible that the associations observed in epidemiological studies can be partially attributed to common biological or environmental confounding factors. Therefore, further validation of our results is needed with larger sample sizes and data from individuals of different racial backgrounds.

In recent years, the relationship between sedentary leisure activities and infertility has received widespread attention. However, this study found no significant causal relationship between three sedentary leisure activities including driving, computer use and television viewing time and female infertility. This conclusion is inconsistent with the findings of epidemiological studies. Epidemiological studies have shown that lifestyle factors in adults, including smoking, diet and sedentary habits in women, are important factors that affect fertility in men and women [43, 44]. These factors may also affect the fertility of their offspring, with sedentary behavior being significantly associated with female infertility [26]. Additionally, research has confirmed that sedentary behavior and insufficient physical activity are two independent risk factors associated with female infertility [45]. Furthermore, certain lifestyle factors related to sedentary behavior, such as high-fat diet, delayed childbearing, age at marriage, smoking, alcohol consumption, sexual behavior, anxiety, depression and perception, are significantly associated with female infertility by affecting hormone secretion and menstrual cycles [2, 8]. The discrepancies in past research findings may be due to environmental confounding factors, which have not been adequately accounted for in the relevant epidemiological studies. Therefore, the specific impact and mechanisms of sedentary leisure activities on female infertility may vary depending on the specific types of activities, individual educational backgrounds and socioeconomic conditions. Further research is needed to explore these factors in more depth.

The present study has several notable strengths. Firstly, our study analyses information from various angles, including education, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behaviors, to explore the association between female infertility and these factors. Secondly, in comparison to clinical studies, this study utilized two-sample MR, Multivariable MR and reverse MR analysis methods to minimize confounding factors and establish causal relationships. By employing these rigorous analytical approaches, the study aimed to provide more robust evidence regarding the relationship between education, socioeconomic status, leisure sedentary behaviors and female infertility. Lastly, the current MR analysis incorporated multiple large-scale GWAS datasets, which significantly increased the sample size of both the experimental and control groups. This larger sample size enhances the credibility and accuracy.

Despite these advantages, it is important to acknowledge and address certain limitations. Firstly, the subjects in this study were exclusively from the European population. The predominance of European ancestry in GWAS datasets may limit the generalizability of the findings; thus, it is indeed a factor that could affect the external validity of the results. To alleviate this situation, we will continue our efforts to incorporate more diverse populations into future studies, thereby enhancing the representativeness and applicability of our research findings. Additionally, while the current study's results may be more reflective of European populations, the use of robust statistical methods like MR still provides valuable insights into the biological mechanisms, which can lay the groundwork for further research in other ancestral groups.

Secondly, although this study employed MR intercept and MR PRESSO global tests to minimize horizontal pleiotropy as much as possible, there are still existing confounding factors that exist, particularly BMI, dietary habits, family status and social support, which are closely associated with female infertility. Thirdly, due to the limitations of GWAS summary data, we were unable to perform effective stratified or subgroup analysis, including factors such as tubal obstruction, endometritis, intrauterine adhesions, endometrial polyps, premature ovarian failure, ovulation disorders and cervical lesions.

Conclusion

In summary, this study indicates that in the European population, a relatively later age of completion of full-time education may be associated with a lower risk of female infertility. However, there is currently no evidence supporting a causal relationship between other two educational levels, four socioeconomic statuses, three leisure sedentary behaviors and the risk of female infertility. Furthermore, given the limitations of this study, it is necessary to further validate our findings through high-quality, rigorously designed basic and clinical trials.
