XGBoost-based urinary microbial signatures enable non-invasive diagnosis and prognosis for urothelial carcinoma
Authors: Hao Xie, Changming Dong, Yue Li, Jiahao Guo, Yufan Yang, Jinshan Yang, Xinxin Li, Jiazi Cha, Shixian Hu, Chunhua Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrothelial carcinoma (UC) is the most common malignant tumor of the urinary system, characterized by high incidence and recurrence rates, posing a serious threat to human health. While previous studies have linked urinary microbiota alterations to bladder cancer, little is known about the broader spectrum of UC subtypes or their clinical implications. To address this gap, we analyzed microbiota profiles across multiple UC subtypes, incorporating microbial network analyses and machine learning to establish diagnostic models and identify prognostic biomarkers.MethodA total of 112 subjects were enrolled for 16 S rDNA sequencing of clean-catch midstream urine samples, including 63 patients with bladder cancer (BCA), 29 with Upper Tract Urothelial Carcinoma (UTUC), 9 with renal pelvis cancer (RPC), and 40 healthy controls (HC). Microbial diversity, community networks, and clinical associations were analyzed. An XGBoost-based diagnostic model was developed with a 70/30 train–test split, cross-validation, and external validation. Model interpretability was assessed with the SHAP algorithm.ResultsUC groups showed elevated α-diversity versus HC, with consistent enrichment of Streptococcus and Clostridium. Microbial structure networks significantly differed in tumors. A urinary microbiota-based diagnostic model achieved high accuracy for BCA detection (AUC = 0.927), and Lachnospiraceae family members showed potential prognostic value.ConclusionOur study illuminates the microbial profiles in the UC and suggests that urinary microbiota signatures represent promising non-invasive independent biomarker for the diagnosis and prognosis of UC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04455-w.Keywords: Urothelial carcinoma, Microbe, Intratumoural microbiome, Prognosis, XGBoost

Introduction

Urothelial carcinoma (UC) is one of the most prevalent malignant tumors of the urinary system, ranking as the leading genitourinary system malignancies. It is characterized by high recurrence rates and poses a substantial threat to human health [1]. Although there have been many improvements in the therapeutic strategies of UC, early diagnosis remains critical for optimal patient outcomes. Therefore, the identification of novel tumor biomarkers is a current priority in clinical research.

The Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine persists as the standard first-line therapy for non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer and effectively reduces postoperative recurrence. This live attenuated strain of Mycobacterium bovids inhibits bladder cancer recurrence by activating the host immune system–an effect partially attributed to BCG-associated shifts in microbial composition and diversity. Historically, urine was considered sterile, with the presence of a certain number of bacteria indicating infection. However, advances in microbial culture sequencing technology have revealed specific microbial communities in the urinary tract of healthy individuals, leading to the proposal of the urinary microbiome concept [2, 3]. Studies have demonstrated that the endogenous bladder flora confers protective effect, and its disruption correlates closely with t system pathologies, particularly bladder disorders [4, 5]. For example, reduced diversity of Lactobacillus species characterizes the urinary microbiome in interstitial cystitis, while female urge incontinence patients exhibit increased relative abundance of Gardnerella alongside decreased Lactobacillus [6]. Further studies associate increased diversity of urinary flora with urinary urgency incontinence symptoms, hormone levels, and body mass index, but not with stress incontinence alone [7]. In addition to microbial factors, systemic inflammation also plays an important role in bladder cancer progression. Recent clinical research in early-stage bladder cancer has shown that inflammation-related blood markers are associated with recurrence and aggressive disease features, suggesting that simple, non-invasive tests could help identify patients at higher risk and guide personalized monitoring and treatment [8]. Although the role of bladder microbiota in urinary diseases is becoming increasingly evident, systematic investigations into its relationship with UC remain scarce.

Accumulating research underscores the role of microbiota alterations in the onset and progression of cancer. Taking the investigations into Clostridium difficile and colorectal cancer as an example, the molecular mechanism of the influence of tumor flora on oncogenesis is gradually becoming a research focus. Microbiota composition has also been linked to prognosis in other malignancies, such as pancreatic cancer, where tumor microbiota diversity correlates with patient survival and gut microbial profiles [9]. Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data, including bladder cancer tissues, has identified bacterial sequences, indicating that microbial signatures in tissues and blood may serve as novel diagnostic strategies [10]. In addition, studies have shown that the tumor microbiome is composed of cancer type–specific intracellular bacteria [11] Together, these findings support the potential of urinary microbial signatures as non-invasive biomarkers for diagnosis and prognosis, which we further explore using a machine learning approach.

Although tumor-associated microbiota has become a prominent research focus, its distribution and functional roles in UC remain insufficiently characterized, largely due to low microbial biomass and methodological limitations in identification. Systematic profiling of UC-associated microbiota is helpful to elucidate the mechanism of UC pathogenesis and progression and may offer novel, reliable biomarkers for diagnosis and prognosis.

In recent years, machine learning approaches have emerged as powerful tools for analyzing complex microbiome datasets. Algorithms such as XGBoost, random forest, and support vector machines can integrate high-dimensional microbial abundance data with clinical variables to build predictive models for disease diagnosis and prognosis [12–14]. In the context of UC, machine learning enables the identification of key microbial features that distinguish patients from healthy individuals, while also providing interpretability of their contribution to model predictions. These methods not only enhance diagnostic accuracy but also facilitate the discovery of potential prognostic biomarkers, offering a translational bridge from microbiome profiling to clinical application.

This study employed 16SrDNA high-throughput sequencing to analyze the urinary tract microbiota in patients with UC, aiming to identify specific bacterial genera associated with UC and assess the potential of urinary microbiota as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers.

Method

Sample collection

Following written informed consent, clinical and demographic data were recorded for each participant, including (1) demographic characteristics namely date of birth, long-term residence, occupation, gender, and initials; (2) medical history and physical examination findings, including height, weight, vital signs, systematic physical examination results, smoking and alcohol consumption history, family history, and personal history of malignant tumors and metabolic diseases; and (3) relevant laboratory test results, such as urinalysis. All patients included in this study were enrolled consecutively according to the eligibility criteria. Under the guidance of experienced nursing staff, clean-catch midstream morning urine (30–50 mL) from participants was collected. Samples were immediately stored at 4 °C and centrifuged within 2 h at 16,000×g for 10 min. Pelleted sediment was stored at −80 °C.

DNA extraction and 16 S rDNA sequencing

Microbial DNA was isolated from the samples using the Tiangen DNA Microbiome Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) and quantified via Nanodrop™. The quality of the extracted DNA was evaluated using 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) that targets the V3–V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was performed using the following primers: forward primer, 5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′; and reverse primer, 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′. PCR products were purified with magnetic beads and quantified by fluorescence spectroscopy. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the Illumina TruSeq® Nano DNA LT Library Prep Kit (Illumina, USA). Enriched library fragments underwent size selection and purification via 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. Each qualified onboard sequencing library was gradient diluted and mixed according to the required ratio, and onboard sequencing was performed. Paired-end amplicon sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Sequencing data processing and decontamination

Raw sequencing data in FASTQ format were first subjected to primer and barcode removal, followed by quality control and filtering. Data analysis was conducted using the Easy Amplicon pipeline, incorporating USEARCH and VSEARCH software (USA). Sequences < 200 bp and with low-frequency reads (< 8 occurrences) were truncated and removed to reduce sequence redundancy. Chimeras were identified and eliminated using the Ribosomal Database Project (RDP) database (http://rdp.cme.msu.edu).

Data were further denoised using the VSEARCH Denoise algorithm, and high-quality sequences were assigned to amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) at a 97% sequence similarity threshold. Taxonomic annotation was performed using the silva database (https://www.arb-silva.de/), and plastids were removed. To minimize environmental and reagents-derived contamination, the following decontamination measures were applied: (1) contaminant ASVs were identified based on the decontam algorithm; (2) ASVs with relative abundance exceeding 0.5% in control samples were removed; and (3) ASVs that appeared in less than 10% of the tissue sample were removed to avoid contingency. To remove potential contaminants, 20 negative controls were incorporated during sample processing (mean reads per sample: 500 vs. 30,000 in tissue samples). To monitor and control potential contamination, negative controls were included at all stages of sample processing and sequencing. Negative controls yielded substantially fewer reads (approximately 500) compared with tissue or urine samples (approximately 30,000 reads), indicating minimal contamination. During downstream analyses, reads detected in negative controls were used to identify and filter out potential contaminant taxa from the dataset. Only microbial features absent or negligible in negative controls were retained for α- and β-diversity analyses, network construction, and machine learning modeling, ensuring the robustness and reliability of the results.

Microbiome analysis

The α-diversity was calculated using the R package vegan and displayed as Shannon indices. The β-diversity was analyzed via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distance. Microbial differences between groups were analyzed using the linear regression models. Microbial network analysis and visualization were performed using the R package ggClusterNet. Xgboost algorithm was used to build a diagnostic model. Briefly, the whole cohort was used as internal model establishment. The dataset was split into 70% for model training and 30% for testing. Another 10 bladder cancer (BCA) patients and controls were used for external validation. Performance evaluation metrics including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, recall, precision, and area under the curve (AUC) with 95% CI are used to assess the models’ effectiveness. Cross-validation was performed to assess the model’s robustness and generalizability. The SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) algorithm was applied to interpret and dissect the underlying predictors used in the developed models. Two-tailed P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Population characteristics

A total of 667 potential participants were initially identified between January 2023 and May 2024 including 437 bladder cancer (BCA) cases (lower-tract UC), 105 ureteral carcinoma (UTUC) cases (upper-tract UC), 52 inflammation cases, and 73 renal pelvis cancer (RPC) case, with an additional 30 BCA cases enrolled between April and May 2024 (Fig. 1). Following stringent eligibility criteria, 112 patients qualified for inclusion, whereas 575 were excluded due to predefined exclusion factors: absence of urinary tract infection history (n = 256), lack of other urinary system diseases (n = 112), no history of major urological surgery (n = 30), no antibiotic treatment within the preceding 4-week period (n = 28), and no history of urinary catheterization in the last 4 weeks (n = 149). The final cohort enrolled 112 patients comprising BCA (n = 63), UTUC (n = 29), inflammation (n = 11) and RPC (n = 9), and 40 healthy controls (HC). These participants were stratified into training and validation sets to develop a diagnostic model and evaluate microbiota characteristics, clinical features, and other contributing factors. All exclusion and inclusion criteria were rigorously applied to ensure cohort homogeneity and data reliability.

Urinary Microbiome characteristics

Comparative analysis revealed significantly higher microbial diversity in BCA compared to HC (Fig. 2A). While UC and RPC groups exhibited elevated diversity trends, these differences were not statistically significant (P = 0.12 for RPC and P = 0.23 for UC). PCoA demonstrated clear separation of microbial communities among disease groups, with statistically distinct clustering between HC and disease cohorts (P = 0.022, Fig. 2B). These findings suggested disease-specific alterations in gut microbiota composition.

(A) Alpha-Diversity Analysis of Microbial Communities in Disease Diagnosis. (B) PCoA Analysis of Microbial Communities in Disease Diagnosis. *P < 0.05

Significant correlations emerged between urine microbiome α-diversity and clinical parameters, with reduced creatinine (Cr) and white blood cell (WBC) levels correlating with lower Shannon and Simpson indices (Fig. 3A). PERMANOVA further demonstrated significant urine microbiome variations linked to host characteristics, including gender, BMI, and alcohol consumption (P < 0.05, Fig. 3B), suggesting potential interactions between host physiology and urinary microbiota structure.

At the individual bacterial level, linear models revealed a consistent enrichment of Enterococcus in BCA, UTUC, and RPC compared to HC (Fig. 4). Furthermore, Atopobium, Dialister, and Peptostreptococcus were significantly enriched in at least two UC subtypes, suggesting a potential expansion of opportunistic pathogens in UC. Conversely, commensal bacteria—including Faecalibacterium, Alistipes, Lachnospiraceae UCG-002, and Coprococcus—were reduced in UC patients. These taxa are known to colonize the human gut, skin, and solid organs and are associated with health-promoting effects.

Microbial co-occurrence networks

To explore the bacteria-bacteria interaction among BCA, UTUC, and HC, co-occurrence networks were constructed (Supplementary Figure A-C). Analysis results revealed that Lachnoclostridium was a keystone taxon across BCA and UTUC, ranking among the top abundant taxa and displaying the highest number of node connections. Both BCA and UTUC samples exhibited strengthened microbial interactions versus HC, reflected by a greater number of correlation modules (three in diseases vs. two in controls) which potentially contribute to pathogenesis. In BCA, Acetoanaerobium and Agathobacter also functioned as core hub bacteria, indicating their potential value as non-invasive diagnostic markers. In UTUC samples, Lachnoclostridium and its related species, including Escherichia.Shigella, Desulfovibrio and Parabacteroides also exhibited consistently high relative abundance. Therefore, this widespread presence of Lachnoclostridium in the urine microbiota of BCA and UTUC patients underscores its potential role as a key microbial component in disease-associated microbiome. Moreover, the UTUC-specific microbial network highlighted additional core taxa, inclduing Acetoanaerobium, Vagococcus and Marvinbryantia, suggesting disease-specific bacterial signatures (Supplementary Figure B-C). Due to the limited number of RPC cases, differential co-occurrence networks could not be reliably constructed.

Diagnostic performance of urinary microbiota

To assess the diagnostic potential of urinary microbiota, XGboost algorithm was employed to distinguish BCA from HC with high accuracy in the primary cohort (AUC = 0.927, Fig. 6A). Additionally, External validation using 10 BCA patients and age, sex-matched controls demonstrated robust model discrimination (AUC = 0.811, Fig. 6B).

Subsequently, SHAP analysis identified CHKCI001 (contribution score = 0.119), Lachnospiraceae UCG-004 (contribution score = 0.093), and Rosenbergiella (contribution score = 0.065) as top discriminative features (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

Urothelial carcinoma (UC) is clinically challenging due to its complex etiology, diagnostic difficulty, and frequent recurrence, with mechanisms still unclear. Emerging evidence links the urinary microbiota to UC pathogenesis via inflammatory, immune, and metabolic pathways [15–18]. This study systematically validated the diagnostic and prognostic potential of urine microbiota in UC using a multi-cancer cohort. Key findings include: significantly elevated urinary microbial α-diversity in UC patients compared to HC (P < 0.05); subtype-specific microbiota profiles between bladder UC (BCA) and upper tract UC (UTUC); identification of Lachnospiraceae as a core hub genus with extensive co-occurrence network interactions; and superior performance of a microbiota-based machine learning model (AUC = 0.927 in training set, AUC = 0.811 in validation set), underscoring its clinical translational potential.

A major strength of this study lies in the simultaneous inclusion of BCA, UTUC, and healthy cohorts, revealing both shared and subtype-specific microbiota features. Consistent with previous reports of Escherichia and Staphylococcus enrichment in BCA [19–21], we observed elevated overall bacterial abundance in UC. However, UTUC exhibited distinct Helicobacter enrichment compared to Corynebacterium predominance in BCA tissues reported by Knorr et al. [22], highlighting tumor niche-specific microbiota reshaping. Notably, conflicting findings of reduced microbiota diversity in BCA may reflect methodological variations (e.g., catheterized vs. midstream urine collection) or cohort heterogeneity [23–25]. Furthermore, recent advances in liquid biopsy for urological cancers, particularly through urine-based ctDNA and methylation analyses, have similarly highlighted the impact of pre-analytical protocols and cohort selection on biomarker reproducibility [26, 27]. Sex-specific microbiota differences were also evident (Fig. 3B), aligning with established patterns of Veillonella and Streptococcus dominance in healthy male urine, suggesting potential sex-dependent UC risk modulation [28, 29].

The core genus Lachnospiraceae (e.g., Lachnoclostridium) likely contributes to UC progression through multiple mechanisms: (1) Metabolite-mediated immunomodulation—butyrate production initially suppresses NF-κB signaling via GPR109A activation, but tumor microenvironment acidification may reverse this effect, promoting epithelial barrier dysfunction [30]; (2) Genotoxic stress—PKS island-expressing strains could induce DNA double-strand breaks and tumor suppressor gene mutations, analogous to colibactin-producing Escherichia coli [31]; (3) Niche competition—anaerobic proliferation in hypoxic UC microenvironments may disrupt barrier integrity through mucin depletion, facilitating opportunistic pathogen colonization [32, 33]. Urinary microbiota sources likely include tumor-intrinsic microbial translocation, gut/genitourinary migration, or endogenous urinary tract colonization.

This study introduces the first non-invasive diagnostic model integrating multi-subtype urinary microbiota. Compared to conventional markers (urine cytology sensitivity ≈ 40%, NMP22 ≈ 55%), our model demonstrated robust generalization in the validation cohorts (AUC = 0.811, 95%CI: 0.752–0.875), with SHAP analysis confirming Lachnospiraceae_UCG004 as the top predictor (contribution score = 0.993), consistent with microbiota-host interaction theories. This approach aligns with the growing emphasis on liquid biopsy technologies, such as urine-based ctDNA and exosomal RNA profiling, which have shown high sensitivity and specificity for detecting and monitoring urological malignancies [26, 27]. This model offers new opportunities for early UC detection, particularly in cases with equivocal hematuria. Notably, BCA-associated genera like Acinetobacter and Haemophilus promote carcinogenesis through biofilm formation and DNA damage [10, 31–35], while Lachnoclostridium identified herein may similarly impact UC via analogous pathways, though functional strain-level resolution requires further investigation.

Limitations of this study include: limitation in genus-level resolution of 16 S rDNA sequencing, which precludes distinguishing pathogenic/protective Lachnospiraceae strains; single-center cohort size constraints (particularly for UTUC); and lack of direct microbiota-host metabolite interaction validation. Further validation in multi-institutional cohorts, as emphasized in recent liquid biopsy studies, will be essential to confirm the diagnostic and prognostic utility of urinary microbiota signatures in UC. Future multi-omic studies combining metagenomics/metabolomics with human organoid-microbiota co-cultures are warranted to elucidate Lachnospiraceae functional pathways and therapeutic potential. Another limitation of our study is the unbalanced distribution of UC subtypes, particularly the small number of RPC cases (n = 9). This limited sample size may reduce statistical power for detecting subtle microbial differences specific to this subgroup and may affect the generalizability of findings. Consequently, the results related to RPC should be considered preliminary. Future studies with larger and multicenter cohorts are warranted to validate these findings and further explore subtype-specific urinary microbiota patterns. Also, differences in α-diversity between our study and previous reports may be due to methodological variations. These include sample collection methods (clean-catch midstream urine versus catheterized urine or tissue), sequencing depth and quality control, exclusion of participants with recent antibiotic use, and differences in bioinformatic and statistical pipelines. Collectively, these factors highlight the need for standardized protocols and careful consideration of study design when comparing urinary microbiota diversity across studies. Additionally, while rigorous negative controls confirmed microbiota presence in healthy urine, their physiological roles necessitate larger cohorts and mechanistic exploration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides an integrated analysis of urinary microbiota across multiple UC subtypes, revealing distinct microbial patterns associated with disease susceptibility and progression. By combining microbial diversity profiling, network analysis, and machine learning–based diagnostic modeling with external validation, we identified urinary microbiota signatures that serve as potential independent biomarkers for both early detection and prognostic prediction. These findings suggest that the urinary microbiota may serve as a potential independent biomarker for early detection and prognostic prediction of UC. Leveraging urinary microbiome signatures may contribute to the development of non-invasive diagnostic tools and personalized therapeutic strategies for UC patients.

The methodology used in our study, including 16 S rDNA sequencing, library preparation, and XGBoost-based data analysis, involves specialized kits, reagents, sequencing instruments, and bioinformatic pipelines, which entail a relatively high cost per sample. While this approach provides high-resolution microbial profiling and robust diagnostic modeling, we acknowledge that the associated expenses may limit immediate widespread implementation, especially in low-resource settings.

Nevertheless, the workflow can be adapted and optimized for broader clinical application. For example, smaller targeted panels or alternative sequencing platforms could reduce costs while maintaining reasonable predictive performance. Furthermore, our study demonstrates the potential of urinary microbiota as a non-invasive biomarker for bladder cancer, which could ultimately improve patient stratification, reduce invasive procedures, and optimize surveillance strategies globally.
