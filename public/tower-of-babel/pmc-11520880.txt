Risk factors associated with stunting incidence in under five children in Southeast Asia: a scoping review
Authors: Devi Azriani, Masita, Nabila Salma Qinthara, Intan Nurma Yulita, Dwi Agustian, Yenni Zuhairini, Meita Dhamayanti
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStunting or growth failure is a health problem faced by the world today, especially in developing countries, including Southeast Asia. The Southeast Asian region accounts for more than a quarter of the number of children experiencing stunting worldwide. In addition to impacting children’s growth and development, mortality and morbidity rates will also affect the economies and productivity of countries in the future.Research objectivesThis scoping review aimed to systematically map the research that has been conducted in this area and identify existing gaps in knowledge related to risk factors for stunting in the Southeast Asian region.MethodeA scoping review was conducted using three databases: Scopus, PubMed, Embase, and one academic publisher, Taylor & Francis. The keywords used were “Children,” “Risk Factors,” “Stunting.”. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies with a population of children under five years of age, (2) studies addressing risk factors for stunting, (3) studies conducted in countries in the Southeast Asian region, (4) studies of all research types, and (5) articles published in English. Of the 2252 articles, 54 met the inclusion criteria.ResultIn this review, the risk factors associated with stunting in children under five years of age in Southeast Asia were categorized into direct, underlying, and basic factors. Direct factors include parental and child characteristics, food intake, and health. Underlying factors include the practice of providing food and access to health services and care. The basic factors causing stunting are environmental, household health, and socioeconomic factors.ConclusionsThis scoping review determined the risk factors for childhood stunting in Southeast Asia, such as attributes of the child, traits of the parent, the amount of food the child consumes, the health of the child, feeding habits, access to health services, household and environmental health, and socioeconomic status. These factors are interrelated and affect each other. Therefore, it is necessary to pay attention to these factors to reduce the risk of stunting among underfives in Southeast Asia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-024-00656-7.Keywords: Risk factors, Stunting, Children under 5 years old, Southeast Asia

Introduction

Stunting is a condition in which there is impaired linear growth caused by chronic malnutrition from early life, and is one of the most significant obstacles to human development globally. Stunting is represented by a height-for-age z-score of less than two standard deviations based on World Health Organization (WHO) growth standards, characterized by a decrease in growth acceleration [1]. Stunting is a global problem because it is one of the most significant barriers to human development. The Global Nutrition Report reports that by 2020, 22% or 149.2 million children worldwide will be stunted, with 21.8% in Asia. The largest region in Asia is South Asia at 30.7%, followed by Southeast Asia at 27.4% [2]. Although the estimated number of stunted children under five has decreased, it is relatively slow, at approximately 1.5% per year [3].

Stunting in childhood has long-term consequences for human resource capabilities, including decreased physical growth and lower attainment of education, cognition, labor productivity, and wages. This has an impact on a decrease in income of up to 20% and increases vulnerability to poverty by up to 33% [3, 4]. Stunting can hinder economic growth and labor productivity, affecting 11% of GNP (Gross National Product) annually in Asia and Africa [4] Stunting also has an impact on increasing health service costs where children who suffer from stunting are at high risk of suffering from non-communicable diseases in the elderly [3] Global Nutrition Report reports that in 2020 there were 22% or 149.2 million children in the world experiencing stunting, of which 21.8% were in the Asian region. The largest region in Asia was South Asia (30.7%), followed by Southeast Asia (27.4% [2].

Stunting is caused by multiple factors both directly and indirectly. UNICEF explains that the factors that cause stunting include social and community factors, including political and economic factors, health services, sociocultural factors, access to education, food security, and environmental sanitation. Other factors included household and family factors, inadequate complementary feeding practices, breastfeeding, and clinical and subclinical infections [5, 6].

Southeast Asia is a region located in the southeast of the Asian continent. This region has diverse economic, cultural, social, geographical, and demographic fields. In addition to diversity, countries in this region also differ in accessibility to education, health services, and sanitation, including access to good nutrition. Despite their many diversities and differences, most Southeast Asian countries are developing, with the exception of Singapore. This has caused persistent poverty and economic inequality in many countries in the region. Limited access to nutritious food and adequate healthcare results in a high risk of stunting among children under five in the region. Southeast Asia consists of 11 countries, seven of which have a stunting prevalence above the global average. The highest prevalence of stunting was in Timor-Leste (48.8%), followed by Indonesia (31.8%), Laos (30.2%), Cambodia (29.9%), Philippines (28.7%), Myanmar (25.2%), and Vietnam (22.3%) [2]. Although the prevalence of stunting in Southeast Asia has tended to decrease compared to that in 2000 (38%), the decline has been slow. According to the stunting prevalence data released by the WHO, Southeast Asia accounts for more than a quarter of the world’s stunted children. This not only affects the growth and development of children and the rates of mortality and morbidity but also has future economic and productivity implications for the countries involved. Therefore, this scoping review aims to systematically map the research conducted in this area and identify gaps in knowledge related to risk factors for stunting in Southeast Asia. The research question is, what are the risk factors associated with stunting in children under five years of age in Southeast Asia?

Materials and methods

Review design

This was a scoping review. This form of research is a scoping review adopted through the methodological framework developed by Arksey and O’Malley (2005) [6]. The scoping review consists of five stages: (1) identifying research questions; (2) Searching for relevant literature; (3) choosing a study; (4) mapping data; and (5) compiling, summarizing, and reporting the results [7] The review is carried out methodically and transparently. A scoping review evaluates the type and amount of research available on a topic and potential knowledge gaps. Through this article, the author addresses the research question “What are the risk factors related to the incidence of stunting in children under five years of age in the Southeast Asian region?. The population in this study is children under the age of five, the concept of this review is risk factors for stunting, while the context of the review is countries in Southeast Asia This literature review was prepared using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [7] as listed in appendix 1, but this protocol has not yet been registered.

Search strategy

This evidence-based study involved a systematic review that used various methods, including reference tracking through databases. Only original articles and papers were included in this review. The article search process was carried out using three databases, namely Scopus, PubMed, and Embase, and one academic publisher (Taylor & Francis). Data were collected from 2012 to 2022 using predetermined keywords. The keywords used for each database were “children”, “stunting”, “risk factors”, and “Southeast Asia”. These data were then entered into Mendeley and checked for duplicates. Of a total of 2,252 articles in the literature search results, 160 were duplicate articles.

Article selection criteria

The selection of articles for scoping reviews followed the scoping review framework described by Danielle and Andrea [8] and The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [9]. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies with a population of children under five years of age; (2) studies addressing risk factors for stunting; (3) studies conducted in countries in the Southeast Asian region; (4) studies of all research types; 4) articles published from 2012 to 2022 in four predefined databases; 6) articles published in English; and 7) articles in the form of academic journals and document availability (free) and published in the fields of medicine and public health.

This review did not include studies conducted on pregnant women or those that discussed the impact of stunting. Review articles, books, letters to editors, and paid were also not included in this review.

Study design

The research design adopted for this study was an original article that included randomized controlled trials, cross-sectional studies, and case-control studies. This review did not involve articles, books, letters, or editors.

Study process

This study was conducted in January 2023. All the authors contributed according to their respective roles in compiling this scoping review. The article selection process was conducted in several stages. The first step was to check for duplicates in the available articles by using Mendeley. The second stage was to conduct an abstract review and study title independently by two assessors, based on the agreed inclusion criteria. The criteria assessment at this stage was performed using the https://sysrev.com/. The third stage assessed the quality of articles. This stage was carried out by two assessors by reading the complete article and conducting an assessment using the JBI Critical Appraisal Tools Checklist downloaded from https://jbi.global/critical-appraisal-tools. The article quality was assessed using a checklist adjusted for the research method of each study. The fourth stage extracts data based on the objectives of the scoping review that has been set. The study process can be seen in Fig. 1 from the search results in the database resulting in 2252 articles. After selecting titles, abstracts, complete texts, and duplicates, 54 articles met the inclusion criteria.

Flow diagram of included and excluded studies

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted independently by two researchers to reduce bias and ensure accuracy in the extraction process. The extraction included all relevant data related to risk factors for stunting in children under five years old in Southeast Asia. Based on article quality assessment, the extracted articles scored > 50. Data items included author name, study title, year of publication, country of origin, study population, sample size, study methodology, stunting prevalence, and the main findings of the study (risk factors for stunting). We categorized stunting risk factors as child characteristics, parental characteristics, dietary intake, child health, child feeding, access to health services, environmental health, and socioeconomic conditions. The team then mapped the data based on each characteristic using a mutually agreed table to answer the research question.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The authors do not require ethical approval for this study. This study was a review of previously published papers and clinical trials with ethical approval.

Direct risk factors of stunting

Characteristics of children and parents

The results of the review indicated that the characteristics of children and parents are direct risk factors of stunting.

Table 2 shows the various risk factors related to the characteristics of children and parents that which are stunting risk factor. Twenty-six (26) articles explained that sex was a risk factor for stunting. The other factors included age, birth weight, and birth length. Table 2 also shows that maternal height (17 articles) was the most discussed parental characteristic factor. Other parental characteristics associated with stunting included maternal BMI, maternal age, paternal height, parity, gestational age, maternal upper arm circumference, paternal age, paternal BMI, parental stress, and maternal infection.

Discussion

This scoping review focuses on the risk factors for stunting in children under five years of age in Southeast Asia. This review shows that the risk factors for stunting in Southeast Asia are grouped into three main categories: direct, underlying, and basic risk factors of stunting. This study explains that the direct risk factors for stunting consist of child and parent characteristics, dietary intake, and child health. The underlying risk factors of stunting are feeding practices, access to health services, and environmental and household health issues. The basic risk factors for stunting are socioeconomic factors, including parental education, occupation, income, household wealth index, and household size. Based on epidemiological theory, a disease can be caused by more than one causative mechanism and one causative mechanism will involve various interactions of other causative components [63]. This can also describe the condition of stunting in Southeast Asia. Stunting can be caused by more than one risk factor for stunting and involves the interaction of multiple risk factors.

Child and parental characteristics

Direct risk factors for stunting, which include child characteristics, parent characteristics, food intake, and child health, are influenced by the underlying risk factors for stunting. Child characteristics describe the growth and development of children during the womb including the fulfillment of maternal nutrition during pregnancy. This condition is associated with the underlying risk factor for stunting, namely access to health services during pregnancy. Regular antenatal care for pregnant women is essential for maternal health, nutrition, and fetal development. Proper care reduces the risk of stunting. Access to healthcare services and skilled birth attendance also improve children’s growth [64, 65].

Child characteristics are also influenced by maternal characteristics including maternal age, pregnancy < 18 years, pregnancy > 35 years, maternal BMI, maternal height, anemia/malaria during pregnancy, parity, and pregnancy spacing/interval. Maternal health during pregnancy will affect the growth and development of the fetus in the womb. Mothers who experience infection during pregnancy are at risk for preterm labor [64].

In addition to maternal characteristics, paternal characteristics and parental stress also influence stunting among under-fives in Southeast Asia. Paternal height, age, and body mass index are factors that play an important role in the incidence of stunting, especially in developing countries, including Southeast Asia [66]. Children with short mothers (height < 145.0 cm) and/or short fathers (height < 161.9 cm) have a higher risk of being stunted at birth, which is related to the genetic mechanisms that transfer talent from parents to their children [67–69]. In addition, short parents tend to work for relatively lower rewards and, therefore, have a lower ability to invest in their children’s health [66]. Parental stress, including distress and stress experienced by fathers or mothers, leads to poor parenting behaviors and increases psychological distress in children, thus affecting growth through multiple pathways [50]. Stress in pregnant women can affect nutrient absorption, immune function, and fetal growth. Maternal stress and depression during pregnancy increase the risk of preterm birth, low birth weight, and small birth size [50, 70–72].

The review also explained that the age of the child affected stunting. The risk of stunting increases with age. This is due to the inability to meet children’s nutritional needs, which increase with age. Inappropriate exclusive breastfeeding, including the quality, quantity, and frequency of breastfeeding, affects a child’s growth and immune function. Stunting in children aged 24–35 months tends to reflect the cumulative effects of poor growth during the first 1000 days of life [13–16, 22–24, 26–31, 34–42].

Gender is associated with stunting risk. Boys are more prone to stunting than girls. This is due to higher levels of physical activity, which consume the energy needed for growth and development [10–34, 73, 74]. In addition, girls had a stronger adaptive immune response than boys did. This is influenced by environmental conditions and household health. In environments with high pathogen loads, biological disparities in immune function based on sex can contribute to increased morbidity owing to infection.

Children’s health

Child health issues that can cause stunting include diarrhea, fever, infections, chronic diseases, respiratory infections, dental caries, and digestive disorders. Children’s health conditions are influenced by environmental and household factors [13, 20, 44, 55, 57]. These factors include living conditions, access to clean water, sanitation, exposure to cigarette smoke, and hygiene habits. An unhealthy environment makes children vulnerable to infections, especially gastrointestinal infections. Children living in rural areas have a higher risk of stunting due to food insecurity, low parental knowledge, and limited parental decision-making power regarding health [16, 25]. Child health is also associated with access to health facilities including iodized salt use, immunization status, child upper arm circumference, vitamin A consumption, and multivitamins [64, 65].

An interesting finding of this study was the effect of exposure to cigarette smoke and lead on the incidence of stunting in Southeast Asia. According to WHO data from the Global Health Observatory, 40% of the world’s children are affected by diseases caused by exposure to tobacco smoke, with almost one-third dying when the child is under the age of five, and two-thirds of these incidences in Sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia [75]. Exposure to cigarette smoke can increase the risk of respiratory diseases in children. Additionally, exposure to cigarette smoke during maternal pregnancy can result in low birth weight, premature birth, and fetal growth disorders. This is because nicotine contained in cigarette smoke inhaled by the mother during pregnancy causes embryonic and bone growth disorders and enters the baby’s body during breastfeeding [75, 76]. Prolonged exposure to cigarette smoke increases nicotine levels in children, leading to a reduced oxygen supply and hindering the absorption of vital nutrients, such as calcium and vitamin C, which are necessary for growth. Smoking also compels low-income families to allocate funds to cigarettes instead of necessities, increasing the risk of malnutrition, particularly in developing countries [76].

Food intake

Food intake is related to the quality, quantity, and diversity of foods consumed by children. Children’s food intake and health play important roles in their growth and development. Dietary diversity reflects the presence of essential micronutrients and macronutrients necessary for optimal growth and development [64]. Macronutrients include the daily intake of protein, energy, and calories, while micronutrients include essential amino acids.

Food diversity, minimum feeding frequency, children’s appetite, and eating problems are other risk factors related to food intake. Eating difficulties result in an unbalanced diet or insufficient nutritional intake, which can affect the growth and development of children. Food intake is influenced by child feeding practices, namely breastfeeding practices include exclusive breastfeeding, age-appropriate feeding, weaning age, food safety, complementary feeding age, and pre-lacteal practices.

The growth and development of children depend on the intake of nutrients. During the first few months, breast milk is the most suitable food for the digestive system of a mature baby. Exclusive breastfeeding can boost the immune system of infants and protect them from infections that can stunt their growth. The duration of exclusive and overall breastfeeding is related to a child’s growth rate [77].

Underlying risk factors for stunting

The factors underlying stunting are divided into three groups: feeding practices, access to health facilities, and environmental and household health. These three factors are interrelated and affect children’s nutrition and health conditions. Poor feeding practices often occur due to lack of access to adequate health information, which should be obtained through health facilities. In addition, when a child is exposed to an unhealthy environment, the risk of infection increases, and at the same time, the body’s absorption of nutrients from food decreases. For example, a child living in an environment with poor sanitation is more prone to diarrhea, which will worsen his or her nutritional condition even if the food provided is adequate. On the other hand, limited access to health facilities makes matters worse, as children do not receive proper treatment or nutritional supplements to aid recovery and growth.

In addition, environmental health also affects access to nutritious food. In poor and unhealthy neighborhoods, limited economic resources often force families to buy cheaper, but less nutritious food. As a result, children in these environments are more prone to malnutrition, which eventually leads to stunting. Improper feeding is also often triggered by a lack of proper information and education from health facilities, or a lack of attention to the importance of a clean environment in supporting child development.

Basic risk factors for stunting

Socioeconomic factors are the underlying causes of stunting in Southeast Asia. A scoping review found that the risk factors associated with stunting in Southeast Asia are maternal education, the household wealth index, household size, maternal occupation, parental income, paternal education, and paternal occupation [78]. Economic status is related to feeding behavior and family nutrient intake as well as the family’s ability to access health services and meet nutritional needs before, during, and after childbirth, as well as access to clean water and sanitation [64].

Socioeconomic status was reflected in the household wealth index, parental education level, and parental employment. Parents’ education levels influence job security and the household wealth index. Parental education is a risk factor for stunting. Maternal education is more closely related to paternal education because mothers are more involved in childcare [79]. Low parental education increases the risk of underweight children as parents may not fully understand proper nutrition. Education helps parents better understand the relationship between food and health. Education is also related to parents’ parenting of children [80].

Household income is related to the ability to meet basic needs such as nutrition, water, sanitation, and medical care [64]. Low family income affects the quality and quantity of food consumed by the family, especially that related to child growth, such as protein sources, amino acids, and minerals [19, 38, 40, 48, 58].

The results of the scoping review explain that various components of risk factors impact child stunting in Southeast Asia. factors can be differentiated based on the degree of difficulty to intervene or change. Easier-to-modify factors tend to be related to behaviors or conditions that can be changed through health programs, education, or policy interventions. Whereas factors that are difficult to change tend to be more structural, genetic, or require greater systemic change. Factors that tend to be easier to change are food intake, feeding practices, access to health facilities, environmental and household health, parental stress, and parenting. Factors that are more difficult to change relate to socioeconomics, formal parental education, and parental characteristics.

Summary of selected studies

The studies selected for this review were based on the risk factors for stunting in children in Southeast Asia. In this review, we identified 54 articles on the risk factors for stunting in Southeast Asia published between 2012 and 2022, and all articles were observational studies. Our findings showed that the risk factors for stunting in Southeast Asian children were similar to those in the UNICEF undernutrition framework. However, other risk factors associated with stunting include paternal characteristics, parental stress, and cigarette smoke exposure.

Strengths and limitations of this article

Strength

This review paper attempts to uncover risk factors related to stunting in Southeast Asia. This review reinforces the results of other studies discussing the causes of stunting in Southeast Asian countries. All studies selected in this review were generally of good quality. The included studies discussed the stunting risk factors.

Limitations

This scoping review had some limitations. The scoping review used only three databases and one academic publisher to find the articles. Most articles were from Indonesia. In addition, the article search used only three keywords and did not add grey literature.

Clinical implications and future research recommendations

Clinical implications

The results of this scoping review reveal various factors associated with stunting. Risk factors for stunting in children in Southeast Asia consist of direct factors, underlying factors, and interrelated basic factors. This can be a source of reference for handling stunting through a holistic and integrated approach involving various parties beyond the health sector. Support in the form of poverty alleviation programs, access to good sanitation, and a healthy household environment can help to reduce the prevalence of stunting.

Future research recommendations

Further studies on the relationship between parental stress and stunting are needed to better understand the role of these psychological mechanisms in stunting. Future research could examine psychosocial-based interventions to help reduce parental stress, especially in families with low economic risks. In addition, more in-depth longitudinal studies are needed to understand how stunting risk factors influence each other over longer periods. Such studies can provide insights into the cumulative effects of the stunting risk factors.

Suggestions

This scoping review shows the risk factors for childhood stunting in Southeast Asia, including children, parents, environmental health, and socioeconomic conditions. These factors contribute directly or indirectly to stunting and influence each other. Therefore, to prevent stunting, it is necessary to involve various sectors, starting from the maternal and child health, environmental health, education, and socioeconomic sectors, so that the stunting prevention program becomes more optimal. In addition, there needs to be strong coordination and collaboration between sectors, especially when running various programs that can synergize with each other. Furthermore, monitoring and evaluation by policyholders are expected to assess success and formulate policies based on available evidence.

Conclusions

The scoping review determined the risk factors for childhood stunting in Southeast Asia, such as attributes of the child, traits of the parent, amount of food the child consumes, health of the child, feeding habits, access to health services, household and environmental health, and socioeconomic status. These factors are interrelated and affect each other. Therefore, it is necessary to pay attention to these factors to reduce the risk of stunting among underfives in Southeast Asia.
