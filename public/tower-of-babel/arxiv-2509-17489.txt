MapCoder-Lite : Squeezing Multi-Agent Coding into a Single Small LLM
Woongkyu Lee, Junhee Cho, Jungwook Choi

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have advanced code generation from single-function tasks to competitive-programming problems, but existing multi-agent solutions either rely on costly large-scale ( > 30 ​ B >30B ) models or collapse when downsized to small open-source models. We present MapCoder-Lite , which upgrades a single 7B model into four role-specialised agents—retriever, planner, coder, and debugger—using only rank-32, role-specific LoRA adapters ( < 3 % <3\% extra parameters). Three lightweight techniques make this possible: (i) trajectory distillation from strong LLMs fixes format fragility in retrieval and debugging, (ii) supervisor-guided correction strengthens planning and coding agents, and (iii) agent-wise LoRA fine-tuning delivers memory-efficient specialisation. Comprehensive evaluation on xCodeEval, APPS, and CodeContests shows that MapCoder-Lite more than doubles xCodeEval accuracy (13.2% → 28.3%), eliminates all format failures, and closes to within six points of a 32B baseline while cutting GPU memory and token-generation time by 4 × 4\times . These results demonstrate that careful agent-wise fine-tuning unleashes high-quality multi-agent coding on a small language model.

Introduction

LLMs have revolutionized code synthesis, achieving near-perfect accuracy on function-level tasks like HumanEval
Chen et al. 2021

competitive programming
, which demands efficient algorithms, robust implementation, and resilience against hidden test cases. Benchmarks like CodeElo

and the 671 B-parameter DeepSeek-V3

, which dominate leaderboards despite their computational costs and proprietary nature.

To meet the cognitive breadth of competitive programming, recent research has shifted from monolithic prompting to
multi-agent code-generation
frameworks that allocate distinct roles to specialised language-model agents
Islam et al. 2024
. These systems separate problem solving into discrete stages—algorithm selection, planning, coding, and debugging—enabling focused expertise and structured information exchange between agents
Huang et al. 2024
;
Hong et al. 2024
;
Islam et al. 2024
;
Islam et al. 2025
. MapCoder
Islam et al. 2024
(Fig.

However, multi-agent systems present two significant challenges. First, they are computationally intensive when using large models. Our MapCoder implementation with Qwen-2.5-32B on the CodeContests benchmark required 3,000 forward passes, each involving 70 GB of parameter loading, resulting in 27.5 hours of runtime on a single A100 GPU—far exceeding the cost of single-prompt approaches. Second, downsizing the agents to 7B models leads to a sharp decline in reliability (see Fig.

Achieving a balance between competitive performance and computational efficiency remains a key open problem.

Background

2.1
LLMs for Competitive Programming

Large language models have already revolutionised
function-level
code generation: specialised variants such as Codex, Qwen-2.5-Coder, DeepSeek-Coder, and CodeLlama routinely achieve near-perfect scores on HumanEval, MBPP, and LiveCodeBench
Chen et al. 2021
;
Hui et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2024
;
Rozière et al. 2024
;
Jain et al. 2024
.
But recent community attention has shifted toward the far more demanding arena of
competitive programming
, where a model must translate a prose problem statement into a complete, time- and memory-efficient program that solves all hidden test cases. Datasets such as APPS
Hendrycks et al. 2021
, CodeContests
Li et al. 2022
, and xCodeEval
Khan et al. 2024
embody this shift: they assess full-program synthesis, algorithm selection, and constraint reasoning under the stringent standards of contest judging. Progress on these benchmarks now defines the “state of the art” for code-capable LLMs
OpenAI et al. 2025
;
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025a
, but their difficulty also exposes the limits of single-agent prompting, motivating the multi-agent systems discussed next.

2.2
Multi-Agent Code Generation

As competitive programming problems grow exceptionally in linguistic length and algorithmic subtlety, it is not uncommon for a single LLM prompt to miss hidden edge cases or violate the constraints of the competition. Recent work, therefore, turns to
multi-agent pipelines
, in which a set of specialised LLM agents collaborate under an explicit workflow
Huang et al. 2024
;
Pan et al. 2025
.
Among these systems,
MapCoder

Islam et al. 2024
stands out with its attractive design that performs well in APPS and CodeContests.
Islam et al. 2024
.

Fig.
1
illustrates MapCoder’s four-stage coding pipeline.
A
retrieval
agent first queries a private corpus of textbook algorithms and returns a short, schema-constrained XML snippet that explains a candidate approach.
Next, a
planning
agent expands this snippet into several step-wise execution plans, and a confidence score is generated for each plan using XML format.
Plans are sorted by confidence, and the top one is passed to a
coding
agent that writes full source code in the required language.
Finally, a
debugging
agent compiles, executes, and patches the program until it clears all unit tests; if it fails, the loop backtracks to the next plan.

Role specialisation makes the pipeline
modular
: each agent faces a simpler, well-scoped sub-task, so errors can be isolated and corrected step-by-step, yielding higher overall accuracy than a monolithic prompt can achieve. The price of this modularity, however, is that the underlying LLM must possess enough
general
capability to perform four very different roles—retrieving algorithms, reasoning about plans, writing code, and fixing bugs—without losing coherence across stages.

2.3
Task-Specific Fine-Tuning

MapCoder relies on large-scale (
>30B
), general-purpose LLMs at every stage, trading speed and memory for accuracy.
Because each agent tackles a well-defined sub-task—retrieving algorithms, planning steps, writing code, or fixing bugs—a natural question is whether a single 7 B backbone can match MapCoder if it is fine-tuned
per role
.
Fine-tuning smaller models has proved effective in other multi-agent settings
Zhao et al. 2025
;
Liang et al. 2025
;
Shen et al. 2024
, and several studies have enhanced code-related abilities through supervised updates or parameter-efficient adapters

;
Tsai et al. 2024
;
Yu et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2024a
.
To our knowledge, however, no work has comprehensively studied how far role-aligned fine-tuning can push a small LLM inside a multi-agent pipeline for competitive programming.

2.4
Challenges

We identify key challenges in multi-agent code generation based on our implementation of MapCoder.

High Cost with Large Models.

We evaluated MapCoder using the Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct model on the CodeContests benchmark, which comprises 165 competitive programming problems. As shown in Table
1
, the system required 27.53 hours of runtime, processed approximately 5.08 million input tokens and 1.60 million output tokens, and made 3,095 API calls. This substantial resource usage stems from MapCoder’s multi-agent design involving four distinct agents and multiple iterations for planning and debugging. These results highlight the heavy runtime and memory burden of using large-scale language models (LMs) throughout the pipeline. We hypothesize that replacing each agent with a fine-tuned small LM can significantly reduce token-generation time and GPU memory usage, even with similar token and API call counts.

Failure Cases with Small Models.

Fig.
2
illustrates typical failures when downsizing MapCoder’s model to a 7B scale.

•

Format Following Failures
: Multi-agent systems often rely on structured outputs in predefined formats. For instance, in MapCoder, the retrieval and planning agents must return XML-formatted responses with tags like <root>, <algorithm> and <confidence>. In Fig.
2
(a), the 7B model inserts an extraneous <description> tag and omits the closing </root> tag, violating the expected format. Such structural errors disrupt downstream processing, as subsequent agents or parsers depend on well-formed inputs. These issues are particularly critical in multi-agent pipelines, where format consistency ensures reliable coordination.

•

Low Role Performance
: Small models often struggle with role-specific tasks due to limited capacity. As shown in Fig.
2
(b), the 32B model’s planning agent enumerates all relevant cases, demonstrating robust and comprehensive reasoning. In contrast, the 7B model omits a key sub-case involving the combination of multiple ticket types. Incomplete planning like this can yield incorrect solutions, even if individual steps seem plausible. Similar shortcomings appear in other roles: the coding agent may generate incorrect logic or indexing, while the debugging agent frequently fails to identify and correct bugs, leading to repeated failures across iterations.

To mitigate these issues, we propose task-specific fine-tuning to improve both format compliance and role execution, thereby enhancing overall code generation accuracy.

3
Methodology

To replace MapCoder’s 32B backbone with a memory-efficient 7B model while maintaining accuracy, we introduce a
role-aligned supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
pipeline that tailors one shared 7B backbone to four agents—retrieval, planning, coding, and debugging—via lightweight LoRA adapters.
These changes present three specific challenges:

•

Role-specific data scarcity
: Public code-generation datasets provide ground-truth programs but lack datasets for retrieval queries, step-wise plans, and debug traces, hindering agent-wise supervision.

•

Cross-agent consistency
: Naïvely fine-tuning each agent in isolation overlooks inter-agent dependencies; a fix applied to one step can still fail downstream.

•

Parameter budget inflation
: Even if fine-tuning recovers agent-specific capability, storing four independent 7B checkpoints nullifies memory savings, approaching the original 32B footprint.

We address (i) by distilling high-quality
trajectories
from a strong LLM for retrieval & debugging, tackle (ii) with a
supervisor
model that diagnoses end-to-end failures and regenerates only the faulty segment for planning & coding, and solve (iii) by sharing a single 7B backbone augmented with rank-32
LoRAs
—adding
<3\%
parameters while preserving MapCoder’s modularity.

(a) When a strong LLM generates a complete solution that passes unit tests, the retrieval agent’s input-output pair is extracted as a training example. (b) To collect debugging data, a 7B model is used for planning and coding, and the strong LLM is used for debugging when the initial code fails. If the revised output passes, the debugging trajectory is added to the dataset.

3.1
Strong LLM for Retrieval and Debugging

Observations.

Two weaknesses repeatedly surface when the 32B backbone is replaced with a 7B model.
First, the retrieval agent often violates the mandated XML schema or returns partial algorithm descriptions, so downstream agents cannot even parse its output.
Second, the debugging agent fails to patch seemingly simple boundary-case bugs, leaving many programs one step short of success.
Experiments with stronger LLMs such as Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct and DeepSeek-V3 show that these larger LLMs seldom make such mistakes, indicating that their trajectories can serve as high-quality supervision for smaller LLMs.

Challenges.

Effective role-specific fine-tuning is hampered by two data gaps.
(i) Public code-generation benchmarks provide only problem–solution pairs and contain no retrieval queries, step-wise plans, or debug traces; thus, no off-the-shelf corpus targets the intermediate process of the multi-agent coding pipeline.
(ii) Even when synthetic trajectories are generated, many are noisy and fail the unit tests, so naïvely training on them would propagate errors rather than correct them.

Methods.

Fig.
3
shows the proposed trajectory construction method. Our data pipeline begins by asking
strong LLMs
—Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct or DeepSeek-V3—to solve each coding task while explicitly printing the intermediate artefacts (i.e.
trajectories
) obtained from each MapCoder role. In one shot, the model emits an algorithm description for the retrieval agent, a step-wise plan for the planning agent, source code for the coding agent, and a patched version for the debugging agent. Because the same LLM generates the entire trajectory, the four outputs are mutually consistent and naturally aligned with their respective prompts.

To convert these raw trajectories into reliable supervision, we exploit the fact that MapCoder’s final objective—passing all the unit tests—is objectively verifiable. After executing the LLM-produced code, we keep the complete trajectory only if the program passes. This
pass-based filtering
eliminates noisy or partially correct traces without any manual inspection: a failure at test time almost always implies that at least one upstream artefact (faulty retrieval cue, flawed plan, or ineffective patch) is wrong, so discarding the whole sequence prevents error propagation during fine-tuning. The surviving set—about 2,200 retrieval examples and 4,200 debugging examples from APPS, xCodeEval, and CodeContests—forms a high-quality, role-aligned corpus that directly trains rank-32 LoRA on the shared 7B backbone. Empirically, models tuned on this filtered dataset regain XML-schema fidelity and bug-repair accuracy comparable to the original 32 B system while retaining the memory footprint advantages of a 7B model (see Table
2
for resulting accuracy enhancement).

3.2
Supervisor-Guided Planning and Coding

Observations.

Even after the role-specific fine-tuning on strong LLM trajectories, the 7B planning agent still emits fragmentary execution flows, omitting edge cases, misordering steps, or drifting from the prescribed outline. Also, the coding agent continues to generate programs that compile yet collapse under the full unit-test suite. These
local errors propagate
: an incomplete plan starves the coder of crucial hints, and a superficially correct program deceives the debugger until final evaluation. Table
2
confirms that this trajectory-only adaptation closes part of the gap but suffers from a noticeable accuracy loss. In contrast, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct and DeepSeek-V3 produce structurally sound plans and largely correct first-pass implementations, indicating that stronger models possess global cues that the smaller model has not yet internalised.

Challenges.

Despite the availability of agent-wise strong-model trajectories, two obstacles remain for high-quality fine-tuning. First, a locally tuned agent lacks
global awareness
: improving its own output does not guarantee end-to-end success unless that output is judged in context. Second, the
capacity gap
hinders direct imitation: a 7B model exposed to large-scale models’ plans and coding results may underfit or copy surface patterns without learning the deeper reasoning (phenomenon called capacity gap
Bansal et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2025
).

Methods.

We propose a
supervisor-guided refinement pipeline
that supplies global feedback without enlarging the runtime model. As shown in Fig.
4
, a 7B MapCoder first produces a complete retrieval→planning→coding trajectory. If the resulting program fails its unit tests, the entire trajectory is forwarded to a high-capacity
supervisor
LLM (DeepSeek-V3). The supervisor analyses the trace, pinpoints the agent primarily responsible for the failure, and issues concise, role-specific feedback, and only that agent regenerates its output. The revised trajectory is re-tested; once all tests pass, the final plan–code pair is added to the fine-tuning corpus.

Supervisor-guided refinement is applied selectively to trajectories where the strong LLM succeeds but the 7B model fails, minimizing generation overhead. The supervisor operates
only
during data generation, keeping inference lightweight. We store just the corrected agent input–output pairs, omitting the feedback itself, so the 7B model learns solely from information available at runtime. Crucially, every example in this corpus is produced and execution-validated by the 7B model, eliminating concerns about a capacity gap. Iterating over thousands of problems yields a dataset that aligns planners and coders with end-to-end success. Table
2
confirms that this strategy delivers a substantial accuracy boost.

3.3
Multi-Agent LoRA Fine-Tuning

To enable role-specific specialization in our multi-agent framework, we adopt an
agent-wise LoRA
approach. All four agents—retrieval, planning, coding, and debugging—share a frozen 7B base model, each paired with its own lightweight LoRA. These adapters are fine-tuned independently using pass-based-filtered and supervisor-guided trajectory distillation. This setup significantly reduces GPU memory usage during fine-tuning and supports modular training and deployment, allowing each agent to be updated without duplicating the full model. As shown in Table
3
, LoRA significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters and memory footprint, while achieving even higher accuracy than full fine-tuning. We attribute this accuracy gain in part to LoRA’s implicit regularization, which steers the model toward role-specific behaviors without overwriting core pretrained knowledge—a finding consistent with prior work
Hu et al. 2021
;
Houlsby et al. 2019
.

Models.

All agent-wise fine-tuning experiments use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as the base model. For comparison, we include larger models—Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, serving as upper bounds to assess the effectiveness of our lightweight strategy.

Benchmarks.

We evaluate models on three competitive programming benchmarks: xCodeEval
Khan et al. 2024
, APPS
Hendrycks et al. 2021
, and CodeContests
Li et al. 2022
. For xCodeEval, we use the 106 problems from the compact program synthesis split. For CodeContests, we evaluate all 165 official test problems. We randomly select 150 test-set problems for APPS, following MapCoder
Islam et al. 2024
. Each benchmark includes natural language problem descriptions with sample I/O and is evaluated using hidden unit tests. We also optionally report results on HumanEval and MBPP to assess generalization to function-level tasks.

Training Configuration.

Each agent is fine-tuned using LoRA adapters with rank 32 applied to the linear modules for query, key, value, and output projection. Training is implemented with Hugging Face Transformers and PEFT libraries
HuggingFace
, using a learning rate of 2e-5, gradient accumulation of 16, and 3 training epochs. Hyperparameters were tuned individually per agent to ensure stable and consistent performance. All training experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU.

Evaluation Setting.

We report pass@1 accuracy as the primary metric, reflecting whether the model’s top-1 output passes all test cases. All outputs are generated using greedy decoding for reproducibility and consistency with baseline evaluation.

Experimental Results

5.1
Effectiveness of Agent-wise Fine-Tuning

Table
4
compares the performance of a small LM (Qwen2.5-7B) across various single-agent prompt optimization methods—direct prompting, chain-of-thought (CoT)
Wei et al. 2023
, self-planning
Jiang et al. 2024b
, and analogical prompting
Yasunaga et al. 2024
—as well as multi-agent approaches: MapCoder and our proposed MapCoder-Lite. Without fine-tuning, MapCoder shows only marginal gains over single-agent methods and even underperforms Direct prompting on xCodeEval (13.21% vs. 18.87%), despite using four specialized agents. This highlights the limitations of small LMs in multi-agent setups, where poor coordination and frequent format violations (as indicated by numerous format failures) prevent reliable end-to-end performance.

In contrast, MapCoder-Lite, with agent-wise fine-tuning, achieves substantial improvements across all benchmarks. On xCodeEval, accuracy more than doubles to 28.30%, outperforming all prompting baselines. Similar gains are seen on APPS (6.00% → 8.00%) and CodeContests (6.06% → 13.33%). Notably, fine-tuning eliminates all format failures, indicating that even small LMs can produce structurally consistent outputs when role-specialized. These results emphasize the critical role of agent-wise fine-tuning in realizing the full potential of small LMs in multi-agent frameworks.

5.3
Ablation Study

Contribution of Individual Agent Fine-Tuning.

Table
6
shows an ablation study evaluating the impact of fine-tuning each agent in MapCoder on xCodeEval. Fine-tuning the retrieval agent alone improves accuracy from 13.21% to 18.87% and reduces format failures from 29 to 12, indicating better structural consistency. Adding the planning agent further boosts accuracy to 26.42% and eliminates format errors entirely, increasing both non-debug passes (17 → 18) and debug-assisted completions (3 → 10). Fine-tuning the coding agent (without debugging) raises non-debug passes to 24 but reduces debug-assisted passes to 2, since MapCoder only invokes the debugger when initial code fails sample I/O tests. Stronger code may bypass debugging entirely, leaving some errors uncaught. Conversely, fine-tuning the debugging agent (without coding) increases accuracy to 27.36%, with 12 debug-assisted completions—showing that an effective debugger can recover from weaker initial code. When all four agents are fine-tuned, overall accuracy peaks at 28.30%, with a strong balance between direct (24) and debug-assisted (6) completions. These results demonstrate that each agent addresses distinct failure modes and that coordinated fine-tuning is essential for maximizing end-to-end performance.

Evaluation on Simpler Coding Tasks.

To evaluate generalization beyond competition-level programming, we compare MapCoder and MapCoder-Lite on two function-level benchmarks: HumanEval and MBPP. As shown in Table
7
, MapCoder-Lite outperforms the baseline by significantly reducing format-related failures and achieving higher end-to-end accuracy. It improves both coding (increased passes without debugging) and debugging (even greater gains in passes with debugging), indicating enhanced reliability. These results suggest that MapCoder-Lite not only preserves generalization capability but also improves robustness on structurally simpler tasks.

Case Study

To qualitatively assess the impact of agent-wise fine-tuning, we examine representative before-and-after examples for each agent. As illustrated in Appendix

6
Conclusion

We introduced
MapCoder-Lite
, a multi-agent coding framework built on a 7B model with lightweight LoRA adapters. Through trajectory distillation, supervisor-guided refinement, and agent-wise specialization, it achieves competitive performance with lower cost. Our results show that small models, when carefully fine-tuned, can match the reliability and accuracy of larger systems.

Limitations

Our framework relies on distilled trajectories from strong models like DeepSeek-V3 and Qwen2.5-32B, yet the fine-tuned 7B model does not fully replicate their performance. Rather than indicating a ceiling, this gap highlights opportunities to further enhance small models—potentially through architectural extensions, better training objectives, or reinforcement-based learning from distillation feedback.

In addition, the multi-agent structure introduces a wide design space for fine-tuning, as each agent has a distinct role and optimization goal. While we adopted a uniform tuning recipe across agents, this modularity also enables targeted specialization and co-training strategies in future work, suggesting that more adaptive tuning could unlock even greater performance.

Details of Supervisor

Fig.
5
and Fig.

illustrate the operation of the supervisor used in our data curation pipeline. The supervisor receives the full execution trajectory of a problem, including the problem description, intermediate agent outputs (e.g., algorithm explanation, plan, code), and the result of test case execution.

As shown in Fig.
5
, the input prompt explicitly instructs the supervisor to analyze the trajectory, determine which agent is responsible for the failure, and provide natural language feedback targeting that agent. The goal is to isolate the error at the correct stage (retrieval, planning, or coding), and produce actionable guidance that can be used to re-generate only the faulty component.

Fig.
6
shows a typical response from the supervisor. It correctly attributes the failure to the planning agent, explains why the plan is insufficient, and suggests how it should be modified. This output is then used to re-invoke the corresponding agent with additional guidance, creating high-quality training data without requiring human annotation.
