Exploring leadership skills development in nursing: a qualitative investigation into the excellence in care program in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia
Authors: Monirah Albloushi, Abdulaziz M Alodhailah, Thurayya Eid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeadership at the point of care is pivotal to safety, flow, and patient experience in ambulatory settings. In Saudi Arabia, the Excellence in Care (EiC) program is a multi-component initiative that uses workshops, simulation, quality-improvement micro-projects, and mentoring to strengthen nurses’ leadership behaviours, yet the mechanisms by which this learning transfers to everyday practice remain underexplored.AimTo explore how the EiC program shapes nurses’ leadership development in Saudi ambulatory clinics and to identify contextual factors that enable or hinder the transfer and sustainability of these behaviours.MethodsA qualitative phenomenological study was conducted with 24 nurses and nursing leaders working in King Saud University–affiliated polyclinics. Using purposive, maximum-variation sampling, participants who had engaged with EiC within the previous 24 months took part in semi-structured interviews (35–60 min). Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed in Arabic and/or English, and analysed using Colaizzi’s phenomenological method, supported by Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis and NVivo 14.ResultsFour interwoven themes described leadership-in-practice after EiC: (1) Leading in the flow of ambulatory care—micro-leadership moves such as brief huddles, explicit role calls, SBAR, and closed-loop confirmation that stabilised throughput and escalations; (2) From course to clinic: conditions for transfer—proximal coaching, micro-simulation, and brief debriefs that enabled enactment despite workload pressures; (3) Culture, hierarchy, and voice—shared scripts that legitimised speaking up across professional and seniority gradients, modulated by gendered and multicultural dynamics; and (4) Systems and signals: making leadership visible—alignment with KPIs, local dashboards, recognition, and communities of practice that sustained behaviours beyond individual champions.ConclusionsEiC provided nurses with shared language, tools, and confidence for point-of-care leadership. Durability of these behaviours depended on coaching, micro-time protection, strategies for navigating hierarchy, and organisational visibility. Embedding standardised artefacts, proximal coaching, balanced dashboards, and peer learning can help convert personal initiative into shared clinic routines.Keywords: Nursing leadership, Ambulatory care, Training transfer, Qualitative research, Saudi Arabia

Background

Leadership at the point of care is a decisive lever for quality, safety, and workforce sustainability in nursing. Over two decades of research have linked relational leadership approaches with improved patient outcomes, including higher satisfaction, lower mortality rates, fewer medication errors, and reduced hospital-acquired complications [1]. More recent syntheses suggest that transformational leadership can strengthen the practice environment through empowerment, commitment, and job satisfaction, factors that are themselves associated with safer care and lower turnover [2]. At the same time, the evidence base for “evidence-informed” leadership remains uneven, with reviews calling for more rigorous designs and context-sensitive evaluations to understand when and how leadership development translates into measurable outcomes [3]. Together, these strands underscore a persistent implementation challenge: cultivating leadership capabilities is necessary but insufficient unless learning reliably transfers into everyday behaviors at the bedside and within unit teams.

Global policy frameworks reinforce this agenda. The World Health Organization’s Global Strategic Directions for Nursing and Midwifery 2021–2025 positions leadership, alongside education, jobs, and service delivery, as a core pillar for achieving universal health coverage and resilient health systems [4]. Monitoring updates emphasize investment in leadership pathways and the creation of enabling organizational conditions for nurses to lead improvement at micro- (patient), meso- (unit/organization), and macro-levels (policy) [5]. Within this policy arc, leadership development programs are expected not only to enhance individual competencies but also to generate practice-level changes aligned with patient safety and experience priorities [6].

In the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), the Vision 2030 Health Sector Transformation Program (HSTP) is transforming the health system through a new Model of Care, expanded access, digitalization, and a stronger emphasis on performance and accountability [7, 8]. These reforms create both demand and opportunity for nurse leadership: demand, because care redesign, quality improvement, and interprofessional coordination depend on frontline leadership; and opportunity, because transformation initiatives often include capability-building platforms, protected time, and recognition mechanisms that can anchor leadership behaviors in practice [9, 10]. As the HSTP advances, Saudi nursing must navigate the dual task of scaling workforce numbers and elevating leadership capacity to deliver on access, quality, and value-based care ambitions [11].

The national workforce context heightens the salience of leadership development. Saudi Arabia has expanded the nursing workforce and local training pipelines, but continues to contend with distribution, skill-mix, and retention challenges across regions and sectors [12]. Continuing professional development (CPD), regulated by the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS), has become a central mechanism for quality and career advancement, with CPD credit requirements tied to licensure renewal and progression [13, 14]. Yet studies in Saudi settings report barriers to effective CPD, including workload, limited time, and uneven managerial support, that can blunt the impact of training unless implementation is deliberately designed for transfer to practice [15–20].

Internationally and locally, a consistent lesson is that training transfer, the degree to which learning is applied and sustained on the job, is contingent on multilevel conditions: learner motivation and self-efficacy; alignment with role expectations; supervisory coaching; peer norms; workload and staffing; and signals from the organization [21]. In nursing, qualitative studies document that post-course application is strongest when participants receive goal-oriented feedback, opportunities to lead small-scale improvements, and reinforcement through huddles, debriefs, or communities of practice [22, 23]. Emerging measurement work is also developing integrated instruments to capture transfer determinants and track change over time, indicating a maturing field that can support program evaluation [24]. These insights imply that leadership programs should be studied not only for content and satisfaction but also for the mechanisms that convert learning into visible leadership behaviors in clinical microsystems [25–29].

Against this backdrop, the Excellence in Care (EiC) program represents a timely case for inquiry in the Saudi context. While Saudi hospitals increasingly offer structured development initiatives, there is limited qualitative evidence detailing how nurses experience leadership capability building within transformation settings, and how contextual features, organizational culture, hierarchy, family involvement in care, digital tools, and accreditation pressures shape the enactment and sustainability of leadership behaviors after training [30]. Additionally, global discourse highlights gendered leadership gaps in health systems, with women comprising the majority of the nursing workforce but a minority of senior leadership roles; addressing these gaps requires targeted support and enabling cultures within organizations [31]. A granular understanding of EiC’s mechanisms, what components are perceived as most powerful, which conditions enable transfer, and what outcomes are noticed at nurse, patient, and service levels, can inform iterative improvement of the program and offer practice-ready guidance for hospitals implementing leadership initiatives under Vision 2030.

Therefore, this qualitative investigation will explore how EiC shapes leadership skill development among nurses across multiple Saudi hospitals, with attention to the multilevel determinants of training transfer and the organizational signals that sustain (or erode) leadership behaviors over time. By situating findings within WHO strategic directions and the HSTP, the study aims to generate actionable insights, toolkits, supervisory practices, and measurement approaches, that align frontline leadership with national reform goals and the everyday realities of clinical work in Saudi Arabia.

Aim of the study

To explore how the “Excellence in Care” (EiC) program shapes nurses’ leadership skills development in Saudi hospitals, and to identify the contextual factors that enable or hinder the transfer and sustainability of these leadership behaviors in everyday clinical practice.

Research question

How do nurses and nursing leaders perceive the EiC program’s contribution to their leadership development, and what organizational, cultural, and workflow conditions influence the application and sustainability of these leadership behaviors at the point of care?

Method

Design

This study adopted a qualitative phenomenological design to explore nurses’ lived experiences of leadership development and transfer to practice following participation in the “Excellence in Care” (EiC) program. Phenomenology was selected for its strength in illuminating the subjective meanings, situational judgments, and embodied enactments of leadership within everyday clinical work [32]. A phenomenological design is particularly well-suited to examining how clinicians make sense of complex, value-laden, and identity-shaping experiences, such as leading care, influencing peers, and negotiating hierarchies in fast-paced ambulatory settings. Leadership is not merely a set of competencies; it involves shifts in self-efficacy, professional identity, and relational influence that unfold in context. Phenomenology privileges first-person narratives and lived meanings, allowing participants to articulate experiences that may be culturally constrained, organizationally sensitive, or difficult to capture through standardized instruments. Consistent with the study’s aim, to understand how nurses experience, apply, and sustain leadership behaviors after EiC, the design prioritized rich, first-person accounts to access psychological, social, and organizational layers of this practice. The research process adhered to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) to ensure transparency, reflexivity, and interpretive rigor [33].

Study setting and recruitment

The study was conducted with participants across outpatient specialty clinics in Riyadh, characterized by high patient throughput and interprofessional collaboration. Participants were drawn from nursing staff and leaders across these clinics, encompassing diverse roles such as staff nurse, charge nurse, clinic coordinator, and nurse educator, with varying years of experience. The organizational culture was marked by structured clinical governance, integration of digital health tools, and performance monitoring systems that shaped expectations around leadership, coordination, and patient experience. Recruitment was facilitated through collaboration with nursing administration: site liaisons distributed an invitational email and information sheet to eligible nurses, and interested staff contacted the research team directly to ensure voluntariness and minimize perceived coercion. Maximum-variation sampling was used to ensure representation by clinic type, role seniority, gender, and tenure in EiC.

The Excellence in Care (EiC) program is a multi-component leadership and quality-improvement initiative established at King Saud University Medical City to strengthen nurses’ point-of-care leadership in ambulatory services. EiC combines classroom workshops on communication, teamwork, and patient-flow redesign with simulation and debrief sessions focused on escalation, structured handovers, and managing clinic surges. Participants complete small, clinic-based quality-improvement micro-projects and receive mentoring or peer coaching from more experienced nurse leaders over several weeks. The program is underpinned by experiential and relational learning principles, emphasizing practice-based rehearsal, structured reflection, and the use of scripts and artefacts (e.g., SBAR, role-call prompts, escalation pathways) to embed leadership behaviours into routine clinic workflows.

Recruitment was facilitated through collaboration with nursing administration and internal professional networks. Site liaisons circulated an invitational email and study information sheet to eligible nurses in the polyclinics, and interested staff contacted the research team directly to preserve voluntariness and minimize perceived coercion. In parallel, a brief study invitation was shared via internal social media channels (e.g., WhatsApp groups) and professional word-of-mouth, which supported maximum-variation sampling across clinics, roles, and shifts. Although the research team did not systematically record the exact number of staff approached, informal counts suggested that a minority declined or did not respond, most commonly citing workload or schedule constraints. No line managers directly nominated or required staff to participate, and interviews were arranged outside formal appraisal processes to reduce power-related pressures.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews designed to elicit thick narratives about leadership capability development, transfer to practice, and contextual enablers/constraints. This method is appropriate for exploring sensitive organizational processes and relational dynamics [34]. Interviews were conducted in private meeting rooms within the polyclinics or via secure video conferencing for off-site participants. The lead researcher, a nurse academic trained in qualitative methods and independent of clinic line management, conducted all interviews to minimize social desirability bias and power differentials. Brief demographic/context forms captured role, years of experience, clinic type, and EiC exposure (modules completed, mentoring received). Field notes were written immediately after each interview to document contextual details, interactional cues, and analytic hunches.

Reflective probes explored transfer conditions (workload, staffing, digital workflows), cultural scripts (hierarchy, communication norms), and organizational signals (recognition, KPIs). Interviews lasted 30–45 min. The guide remained flexible to follow emergent storylines, negative cases, and concrete examples of practice.

Recording and transcription

All interviews were digitally audio-recorded with written consent. Files were stored on encrypted, password-protected university devices. Recordings were transcribed verbatim by a professional transcriptionist fluent in Arabic and English. For Arabic interviews, bilingual transcripts were produced; a second bilingual researcher independently checked a 20% sample for accuracy to preserve linguistic nuance and idiomatic meaning. Transcripts were cross-checked against audio to ensure fidelity and were de-identified by replacing names and units with role-based descriptors.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using a phenomenological and reflexive thematic approach, combining Colaizzi’s method with Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework. First, the lead researcher immersed in the data through repeated reading of each transcript while listening to the audio files, documenting initial impressions and analytic memos. Following Colaizzi’s steps, significant statements related to leadership development, transfer of learning, and contextual enablers/constraints were extracted and clustered, and preliminary meanings were formulated. These clusters were then synthesized into rich, descriptive accounts of participants’ experiences and refined into a provisional structural description of leadership-in-practice after EiC. In parallel, and to support a flexible, reflexive engagement with the data, we applied Braun and Clarke’s six phases of thematic analysis: generating inductive codes across the dataset, collating codes into candidate themes, reviewing and refining these themes against the full corpus (including negative cases), defining and naming themes, and producing an analytic narrative. NVivo 14 was used to organise transcripts, codes, and memos, and constant comparison across roles and clinics helped identify both shared and divergent patterns. Team discussions focused on checking the coherence of themes with Colaizzi’s phenomenological descriptions and on ensuring that theme labels captured both the experiential and practice-oriented aspects of leadership described by participants.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of King Saud University, Saudi Arabia (KSU-HE-24-782) on 17 September 2024. Written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. Participation was voluntary, with the right to withdraw at any time without consequences for employment. Confidentiality was safeguarded through pseudonyms (e.g., N03-Charge) and removal or alteration of indirectly identifying details (e.g., specific clinic schedules). Audio files and transcripts were stored on encrypted drives accessible only to the research team, in line with institutional data protection policies. No clinical performance data were collected.

Thematic findings

Reflexive thematic analysis generated four interwoven themes describing how nurses in King Saud University–affiliated polyclinics experienced leadership development through the Excellence in Care (EiC) program and translated it into everyday practice. Together, the themes trace a pathway from acquiring micro-leadership moves, through contextual enablers and constraints of transfer, to the organizational “signals” that shape enactment and sustainability. While many participants described greater confidence and clearer communication after EiC, the durability of these behaviors depended on workload, hierarchical norms, and the visibility of leadership work in performance systems. Themes, Sub-Themes, and descriptions

Leading in the flow of ambulatory care

This theme captures how participants enacted leadership in fast-moving polyclinic environments where time, space, and attention are constantly negotiated. Nurses described leadership not as a grand role but as a sequence of small, well-timed interventions, setting the tone in huddles, clarifying tasks during surges, and aligning the team when priorities collided. Rather than separating “leadership” from “care,” they framed it as embedded in the flow of triage, assessment, and patient movement between rooms and services. The tempo of ambulatory work, short appointments, overlapping schedules, walk-ins, and rapid escalations, made leadership highly situational: the same nurse might lead decisively during an overcrowded morning clinic and step back to coach a junior during quieter hours. Participants repeatedly emphasized that EiC gave them a shared lexicon (SBAR, closed-loop confirmation, explicit role calls) and a repertoire of micro-moves that made coordination visible and credible. When these moves were consistently reinforced by charge nurses or educators, teams “snapped into alignment” faster and recovered more smoothly from disruptions like staff absences or sudden high-acuity cases.

Micro-leadership at the point of care

Nurses described a set of actionable, repeatable behaviors they could “drop into” the clinical stream without pausing the clinic. The most common were: initiating two-minute huddles at the start of a surge, announcing role assignments aloud (“I’m triage A; you’re runner; coordinator monitors vitals queue”), calling time-outs before high-risk procedures, and using SBAR to compress decision-making with physicians. These micro-moves were small enough to fit between patient calls yet strong enough to reorient the team.

“When the waiting room starts filling, I don’t need a meeting. I say, ‘Quick huddle, today I’m triage lead, Fatimah is runner, Ahmad checks referrals.’ That 30 seconds changes the whole hour.” (N04, clinic coordinator).

“If I see back-to-back unstable sugars, I switch the handover to SBAR for the next two patients. It’s like shifting gears; everyone hears the pattern and follows.” (N11, staff nurse).

Participants stressed that micro-leadership was often preventative rather than corrective, smoothing handovers before they frayed, clarifying who would phone radiology before bottlenecks formed, and pre-flagging likely escalations. Several contrasted pre-EiC habits (“trying to do more by myself”) with post-EiC behaviors that redistributed cognitive load across the team. Quick visual cues (a huddle board, a laminated escalation card, a whiteboard count of waiting patients) extended their influence beyond voice commands and reduced the need to repeat instructions.

“Before, I would just work faster. Now, I slow down for one minute to assign roles, and we go faster together.” (N06, staff nurse).

Micro-leadership also included “tidying the process” mid-stream: standardizing phrasing for phone consults, asking for closed-loop confirms (“Say back the plan, please”), and nudging the team to debrief a near-miss in 90 s at the end of a session. Nurses framed these moves as low-cost, high-yield: small enough to avoid resistance, visible enough to spread by imitation.

Relational influence and calm authority

While tools and scripts mattered, participants emphasized that how they spoke determined whether leadership landed under pressure. “Calm authority” was the phrase several used to describe a tone that was steady, specific, and respectful, even when triage queues were swelling or a physician was delayed. Calm authority blended clarity (“Here’s the next right step”), containment (“I’ve got triage; you take vitals overflow”), and courtesy (“Thank you, closing the loop now”). Nurses reported that this relational stance reduced defensiveness and pulled attention toward shared goals, especially in mixed-seniority or interprofessional moments.

“When I keep my voice even and name the task, people move. If I sound rushed, they mirror the rush and details get lost.” (N15, clinic coordinator).

“I learned to ask for confirmation without sounding like I’m checking up on them, ‘Can you echo the plan?’ It keeps dignity and we still get safety.” (N20, charge nurse).

Participants also described who they influenced first. Several intentionally targeted “informal hubs”, the receptionist who shaped patient expectations, the healthcare assistant who controlled room turnover, the pharmacist who could prioritize urgent scripts. Winning these allies early created a cascade effect that exceeded the nurse’s formal authority. Conversely, when team relationships were frayed or a dominant personality resisted coordination, calm authority served as a boundary: nurses stayed specific (“Patient X is symptomatic; we need ECG within 10 minutes”) and returned to shared standards (protocols, KPIs) rather than personal appeals.

“I go to the connector in the room, the person everyone listens to. If they buy in, the room changes.” (N08, nurse educator).

Finally, calm authority was described as contagious. When charge nurses modeled steady, respectful direction, juniors mirrored the cadence with patients and peers. Participants linked this emotional regulation to smoother escalations: fewer raised voices, faster decisions, clearer handovers. In clinics where recognition was given for these behaviors (e.g., shout-outs in huddles, dashboard improvements), calm authority became a team norm rather than an individual trait.

From course to clinic: conditions for transfer

This theme captures the translation work required to move EiC learning from classrooms and simulation suites into the thrum of ambulatory practice. Participants consistently framed transfer as a social and structural achievement rather than a purely individual decision. Skills introduced in workshops, SBAR phrasing, huddle choreography, escalation scripts, became durable only when embedded in relationships (coaching, mentoring, peer cues) and scaffolded by small design choices (protected minutes, visible prompts, quick debriefs). Where those supports were present, leadership behaviors surfaced under pressure with less prompting; where they were thin or inconsistent, application faded behind throughput demands and “habit gravity.” The sub-themes below trace the conditions that either caught new practices and held them in place or let them slip back into pre-EiC routines.

Coaching, Mentoring, and safe practice spaces

Participants described coaching as the hinge between knowing and doing. Charge nurses and educators who offered brief, targeted feedback at the point of care, “one thing you did well, one next move”, created a safe runway for trying leadership behaviors in real cases. Mentoring relationships extended that effect over weeks, normalizing small risks (calling a time-out, redirecting roles) and reframing mistakes as shared learning.

“After EiC I understood SBAR, but using it in front of everyone was different. My mentor stood next to me the first few times and nodded. That nod made it possible.” (N12, charge nurse).

“We run a two-minute debrief at the end of busy sessions, what helped, what we’ll do tomorrow. It’s not blame; it’s rehearsal for the next day.” (N08, nurse educator).

Several clinics instituted “micro-sim” drills, a single scenario before the first patients arrived or a five-minute huddle with one escalation vignette. Participants said these created muscle memory without disrupting flow, and the low-stakes setting allowed juniors to practice calm voice, closed-loop confirmation, and delegating without fear of being judged.

“We practice one escalation script before doors open. When the real thing happens, the words are ready in my mouth.” (N17, charge nurse).

Psychological safety was repeatedly named as the enabling substrate. Coaching that honored dignity (e.g., private correction, public thanks) increased the odds that nurses would attempt leadership moves the next time. Conversely, corrective feedback delivered in a rushed or critical tone pushed some back toward silence.

“If feedback comes like a warning, I shut down. If it comes like partnership, ‘Let’s try this line next time’, I try again.” (N06, staff nurse).

Importantly, participants did not treat safety as softness. Safe spaces were structured: clear expectations, role cards for huddles, and visible checklists. This combination of warmth and structure, “support with standards”, was described as the sweet spot for transfer.

Friction points: time, staffing, and digital workflows

When clinics entered sustained “surge mode,” transfer faltered. Participants identified predictable friction points: compressed appointment templates that left no slack for huddles, last-minute staffing changes that scrambled role clarity, and digital workflows that multiplied clicks at exactly the wrong moments.

“On double-book days I can feel EiC slipping out of my hands, no time to huddle, just chasing rooms.” (N01, staff nurse).

“If the float nurse is unfamiliar with our flow, I spend the first hour fixing assignments instead of leading.” (N15, clinic coordinator).

Electronic systems were a frequent locus of drag. Nurses described toggling between modules to place referrals, check labs, and document handovers; each switch diluted attention and lengthened the window for miscommunication. Alert fatigue compounded the problem, important safety cues were buried among routine pop-ups.

“The system makes me click through four screens just to book an urgent ECG. By the time I’m done, I’m narrating less and people guess the plan.” (N11, staff nurse).

“We get so many alerts that the real ones don’t stand out. It trains you to ignore.” (N20, charge nurse).

Staffing variability intensified these digital frictions. On days with fewer healthcare assistants or an absent coordinator, nurses took on “invisible glue” tasks, room turnover, chasing results, that displaced leadership behaviors. Participants emphasized that transfer was not about personal willpower alone; it depended on minimal conditions: a two-minute start-of-day huddle, a known escalation route, and a functioning back-up when a key role was vacant.

“Give me 120 seconds at the start and one reliable runner, I can lead the rest. Without those, it’s firefighting.” (N04, clinic coordinator).

Some clinics mitigated friction by redesigning micro-processes: pre-loading common orders, pinning escalation numbers on phones, creating a one-tap template for SBAR notes, and aligning reception scripts with clinical flow (e.g., pre-identifying likely escalations). Where these “process prosthetics” were implemented, participants reported more consistent leadership enactment despite pressure.

“We built a quick button for ‘urgent referral.’ Now I can keep speaking while I click. The words stay, the team stays with me.” (N22, staff nurse).

In short, transfer thrived when coaching made practice safe and visible, and when the clinic removed routine friction from time, staffing, and systems. Without these conditions, EiC behaviors competed with throughput and often lost; with them, micro-leadership became the default rather than the exception.

Culture, Hierarchy, and voice

This theme captures how leadership is negotiated within the layered social order of university-affiliated polyclinics, where medical hierarchy, institutional norms, and diverse team cultures intersect at speed. Participants consistently framed leadership as a communication act that must “fit” context: the same instruction could mobilize action in one room and stall in another depending on who spoke, who listened, and how status was signaled. EiC provided shared language (SBAR, closed-loop, escalation scripts) that lowered interpersonal friction; yet the perceived legitimacy of a nurse’s voice still hinged on unwritten rules, deference to senior physicians, the politics of specialty prestige, and the implicit ranking of clinic roles. Nurses described learning to read the room: deciding when to lead openly and when to “seed” a suggestion through a respected intermediary; when to quote protocol and when to invoke patient risk; when to ask, and when to assert. The result was a practiced agility, leadership as calibrated entry, not constant broadcast.

Negotiating professional boundaries

Participants navigated boundaries between coordination and “stepping on toes.” Many recounted moments where assertive clarity was welcomed during time-critical decisions, contrasted with others where the same clarity was read as overreach. The art, they said, was to anchor requests in shared standards and patient safety rather than personal preference.

“If I say, ‘Doctor, protocol requires ECG within 10 minutes,’ it lands differently than ‘we should do ECG now.’ The first is system voice, not my voice alone.” (N20, charge nurse).

“I don’t argue scope; I clarify roles. ‘I’ll coordinate flow. You decide the plan. Here’s what we need to keep the line safe.’ That keeps respect intact.” (N04, clinic coordinator).

Nurses leveraged tools to legitimize leadership without provoking defensiveness: printed escalation pathways, time-stamped triage notes, and KPI dashboards. Several described “boundary bridges”, brief pre-clinic huddles where expectations were set explicitly, reducing later contestation about who calls radiology, who manages walk-in surges, and who communicates delays to patients.

“If the boundary is negotiated at 7:30 a.m., we don’t have to fight it at 11:30 a.m.” (N08, nurse educator).

Still, hidden rules persisted. Junior nurses sometimes held back when a senior physician’s preferences contradicted clinic flow, fearing reputational risk. Others learned to route safety-critical messages through a charge nurse or to pair the request with data (“three high-risk patients waiting,” “two abnormal vitals unreviewed”) to convert personal voice into institutional signal.

Gendered and multicultural dynamics

Teams were gender-mixed and culturally diverse, which enriched care but complicated voice. Several women, especially newer staff, reported needing to demonstrate consistency before their directives were reflexively accepted. Internationally trained nurses described tonal adjustments to avoid being read as abrupt or submissive, depending on interlocutor expectations.

“If I’m firm too fast, it’s ‘why is she bossy?’ If I’m soft, the task drifts. I learned a steady tone: short, specific, respectful.” (N15, clinic coordinator).

“In my culture we don’t interrupt seniors. Here, if you wait, the moment passes. I practiced one sentence that opens the door: ‘For patient safety, may I add…’” (N11, staff nurse).

Participants also noted specialty prestige shaping who was heard first: voices from procedural or acute-care rooms sometimes carried more weight than from chronic-disease clinics, regardless of content. EiC’s common lexicon helped flatten these gradients, when everyone recognized SBAR or the escalation card, attention shifted from who spoke to what was said. Peer alliances mattered too. Nurses intentionally cultivated “bridges” across language groups and shifts so that requests traveled with social backing, not just formal rationale.

“I share the plan with the healthcare assistant who everyone trusts. Once she repeats it, the room moves, even if I said it first.” (N06, staff nurse).

Gendered expectations also surfaced around emotional labor. Female nurses described being defaulted to patient apology and de-escalation roles, which consumed time and reduced capacity to lead flow; naming and redistributing that work in huddles was a quiet but powerful leadership act. Conversely, some male nurses reported that their directives were quickly accepted but scrutinized if tone slipped toward urgency; practicing “calm authority” protected credibility.

In sum, culture and hierarchy did not simply constrain leadership; they provided terrain that skilled nurses learned to navigate. EiC supplied shared scripts, but effective enactment depended on boundary-sensitive communication, strategic alliances, and an awareness of how gender and multicultural norms inflect whose voice mobilizes action. When nurses aligned message, medium, and moment, and when systems reinforced standards over status, their leadership traveled farther, faster.

Systems and signals: making leadership visible

This theme captures how organizational “signals”, what is counted, displayed, and celebrated, shape whether leadership behaviors seeded by EiC remain optional or become part of the clinic’s operating system. Participants repeatedly described a tipping point: once huddles, escalation times, and handover quality appeared on shared dashboards (or in brief reports) and drew recognition from supervisors, the same behaviors that initially felt like “extra” solidified into routine. Visibility altered attention. Nurses said they started planning for measurables, time-stamping escalations, noting near-miss learnings, because the system asked for evidence, not anecdotes. Where metrics were absent or buried, leadership practices faded with schedule pressure or staff turnover, reverting to individual style rather than a team standard.

KPIs, dashboards, and recognition

Participants linked durability to the alignment between leadership behaviors and key performance indicators (KPIs). When daily huddles mapped to concrete targets, on-time starts, first-contact resolution, referral turnaround, teams perceived a direct payoff. Dashboards provided quick feedback loops: a line chart that dipped after a week of missed huddles; a run chart that improved after adding an escalation prompt to the handover sheet. Seeing their influence “on the wall” converted soft skills into trackable contributions.

“When our huddle compliance went onto the dashboard, it stopped being ‘nice to do.’ We could point at the line and say, ‘This is us showing up.’” (N15, clinic coordinator).

“We time-stamped ‘call-to-ECG.’ Once it was a number, physicians cared, managers cared, everyone cared. My reminder wasn’t nagging anymore, it was protecting a metric.” (N20, charge nurse).

Recognition amplified the effect. Small, immediate acknowledgments, shout-outs in morning huddles, a weekly note from the nurse manager, a badge on the KPI board for “zero escalation delays”, were described as disproportionately motivating. Importantly, participants preferred recognition that named the practice (“clear SBAR, closed loop, role call”) rather than the person alone, because it taught the behavior and reduced perceptions of favoritism.

“When my manager said, ‘Great closed-loop today; we avoided duplication,’ it taught the team what to repeat. Not just ‘Well done, Fatimah,’ but why.” (N06, staff nurse).

Where KPIs were misaligned (e.g., volume-only targets without quality signals), nurses felt pulled back to throughput at the expense of coordination. Several emphasized the need for “balanced boards”, pairing access metrics with safety/communication indicators, so leadership work wasn’t invisible when volumes spiked.

Sustainability loops: communities of practice

Beyond formal metrics, participants described informal routines that acted as maintenance loops for leadership, short peer exchanges that kept scripts alive and spread artifacts across clinics. The most common were end-of-day micro-debriefs (“what helped today, what to carry forward”), five-minute peer huddles mid-week to refine an escalation script, and message-group prompts sharing a one-page checklist or a screenshot of a successful flow tweak. These communities of practice (CoPs) were lightweight, voluntary, and pragmatic, designed to fit the ambulatory tempo.

“Our Thursday five-minute ‘what worked’ is gold. Someone brings a tiny fix, like a phrasing for phone referrals, and on Sunday three clinics are using it.” (N08, nurse educator).

“We keep a ‘script shelf’, laminated cards for SBAR lines, escalation triggers. New staff grab them; old staff update them. It’s our common language.” (N12, charge nurse).

CoPs also served as onboarding accelerators. New nurses learned the unit’s leadership “accent” by shadowing a huddle lead, practicing SBAR with a buddy, and debriefing near-misses in psychologically safe spaces. Participants noted that where CoPs were missing, adoption depended on a few champions; when those individuals rotated, practices decayed. Conversely, in clinics with simple artifacts (prompt cards, whiteboard cues) and recurring peer touchpoints, leadership behaviors persisted despite staffing changes.

“Champions move on. The card stays. The two-minute debrief stays. That’s how the behavior outlives the person.” (N23, staff nurse).

Finally, CoPs bridged metrics and meaning. While dashboards signaled what mattered, peer loops preserved why, linking a faster escalation to a patient story or a smoother handover to reduced staff stress. Participants said this narrative layer protected against “checkbox fatigue,” re-energizing practices when numbers alone lost their pull.

Discussion

This qualitative study examined how nurses in university-affiliated polyclinics enacted and sustained leadership behaviours after participating in the Excellence in Care (EiC) program. The analysis extends prior work that links nursing leadership to care quality and staff outcomes by detailing the mechanisms of transfer in high-throughput ambulatory settings, what nurses actually do in the moment, what helps these behaviours travel from training to practice, and which organizational signals make them stick over time [35]. Four integrative insights arise: (1) leadership in ambulatory care is enacted through micro-behaviours that stabilize clinical flow; (2) transfer depends on proximal coaching and protected “practice spaces”; (3) culture and hierarchy contour whose leadership is heard; and (4) visibility through metrics and peer routines converts personal initiative into shared practice.

First, participants framed leadership as micro-leadership in flow: initiating brief huddles, declaring roles, standardizing phrasing (SBAR, closed-loop), and choreographing escalations under time pressure. These moves align with evidence that structured communication and team routines reduce omissions and error propagation in ambulatory and acute settings [36, 37]. Rather than emphasizing trait-based leadership, nurses described situated influence, precise, teachable behaviours that compress decision time and distribute cognitive load across the team. This resonates with literature on “tool-supported leadership,” where scripts and checklists enable clinicians without formal authority to produce coordination effects comparable to those achieved by formal leaders [38]. Importantly, participants characterized these moves as both clinically useful and low friction, a combination associated with higher adoption in complex systems [39].

Second, transfer from classroom to clinic hinged on coaching, mentoring, and psychologically safe practice spaces. Where charge nurses or educators reinforced EiC content through short station-side coaching, micro-simulations, and huddle debriefs, participants reported easier recall and greater fidelity. These features mirror well-established determinants of training transfer: supervisor support, opportunity to perform, and feedback loops proximal to the task [40]. In nursing contexts, ward-level coaching has been shown to amplify the impact of formal leadership training on safety climate and teamwork behaviours [41]. Our data adds nuance specific to ambulatory care: minutes matter. Participants repeatedly contrasted “one extra minute for a huddle” with “ten extra minutes of downstream delays,” reframing protected micro-time as a throughput enabler rather than a drag on productivity. This reframing may be essential in clinics where volume metrics dominate attention.

Third, cultural scripts and hierarchical norms shaped leadership audibility. Consistent with scholarship on psychological safety and voice, junior and internationally trained nurses described calibrating when and how to speak up, often relying on shared scripts to legitimize escalation [42]. Gendered expectations and specialty prestige further modulated whose prompts triggered action, findings that echo broader evidence on gender and professional hierarchies in health systems [43]. While EiC’s common language reduced interpersonal friction, it did not erase status dynamics; rather, it offered a neutral bridge (“echo the plan,” “SBAR now”) that participants used to cross status boundaries with less interpersonal cost. Practically, this suggests that leadership curricula in similar settings should pair technical communication tools with explicit strategies for negotiating hierarchy (e.g., graded assertiveness, advocacy-inquiry) and build these into scenario design and feedback.

Fourth, systems and signals determined sustainability. When leadership behaviours were linked to key performance indicators (KPIs), for example, huddle adherence, call-to-ECG times, or referral turnaround, and displayed on local dashboards with timely recognition, behaviours persisted and spread. This aligns with implementation science showing that data transparency, coupled with small, immediate reinforcement, promotes maintenance beyond initial champions [44]. Conversely, misaligned metrics (volume-only dashboards) pulled attention away from coordination work, confirming the truism that “what gets measured gets done.” Participants also relied on lightweight communities of practice (CoPs), five-minute “what worked” exchanges, prompt-card libraries, and message-group tips, to keep practices alive across staff changes. CoPs are known to accelerate sense-making and diffusion of tacit know-how in clinical microsystems [45]; our findings illustrate how they convert leadership from a person-held attribute to a team-held routine. Notably, CoPs bridged numbers and meaning: while dashboards signalled what mattered, stories in debriefs preserved why, buffering against checkbox fatigue.

Taken together, these insights position EiC as a catalyst that provides language, artefacts, and confidence; the enduring effect depends on relational coaching, manageable workload, hierarchical negotiation, and organizational visibility. This helps to reconcile uneven effects reported in leadership-training meta-analyses, where improvements in knowledge and attitudes do not always translate into behaviour change or clinical outcomes [46]. Our data suggest that “last-mile” design, ensuring proximal coaching, micro-time protection, metric alignment, and peer routines, is the difference between transient and sticky impact.

Limitations of the study

This single-system, qualitative study was conducted within King Saud University–affiliated polyclinics, which may limit transferability to inpatient or non-academic ambulatory settings. Self-selection and self-report introduce potential social desirability and recall biases regarding leadership behaviours after EiC participation. Although bilingual transcription and independent checks were used, translation may have softened culturally specific meanings. We did not triangulate interview accounts with audited operational or safety outcomes (e.g., call-to-ECG times, referral turnaround), so inferences about impact remain perceptual. Variability in EiC implementation across clinics (exposure to simulation, mentoring, or QI modules) may have shaped experiences, but fidelity was not formally measured. Finally, cross-sectional interviewing within a 24-month window cannot capture temporal dynamics (initiation, drift, renewal) of leadership practices. In addition, variability in EiC implementation across clinics (e.g., differential access to simulation, mentoring, or QI modules) may have shaped participants’ experiences, although this fidelity was not formally measured.

Implications for nursing education and practice

Education and service leaders can convert training into durable practice by (1) standardizing micro-leadership artefacts, two-minute huddle scripts, role-call prompts, escalation triggers, and SBAR/closed-loop phrasing, embedded in orientation and refreshers; (2) resourcing proximal coaching, brief, station-side coaching and micro-simulation with rapid debriefs led by charge nurses/educators; (3) aligning balanced dashboards that pair access/throughput indicators with coordination/safety signals, and giving timely recognition that names the behaviour (e.g., “clear SBAR, closed loop”) rather than the individual only; (4) cultivating communities of practice, five-minute end-of-day “what worked” exchanges and shared “script shelves” (laminated prompt cards) to spread tacit know-how across rotations; (5) integrating graded assertiveness and advocacy-inquiry into leadership curricula to help juniors navigate hierarchy and mixed professional teams; and (6) protecting micro-time (1–2 min) for huddles during surges, reframing it as a throughput enabler rather than an added task.

Conclusion

Leadership in ambulatory nursing emerged as a set of small, teachable behaviours, micro-huddles, explicit role calls, SBAR, and calm authority, performed under time pressure to stabilize patient flow and safety. The EiC program supplied language and tools; their persistence depended on proximal coaching, manageable workflow, and organizational signals that made leadership visible and valued. Embedding standardized artefacts, coaching, balanced metrics, recognition, and peer learning can shift leadership from personal initiative to shared clinic routine, strengthening day-to-day reliability and aligning frontline practice with system goals for access, safety, and patient experience.
