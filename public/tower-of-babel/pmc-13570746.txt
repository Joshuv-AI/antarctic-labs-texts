Size Does Matter! Prioritizing Rare Diseases for Luck Egalitarian Reasons
Authors: Didde Boisen Andersen, Andreas Brøgger Albertsen, Sarah Wadmann, Mickael Bech
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRarity provides a challenging case for contemporary priority setting. On the one hand, many philosophers and economists argue that rarity has no inherent moral value, and thus that rare diseases merit no special treatment in priority setting decisions simply because they are rare. On the other hand, existing priority‐setting practices demonstrate a higher willingness to pay for rare disease treatments. We argue that special priority for rare diseases might be justified on egalitarian grounds. Specifically, we develop and defend what we call the “bad numbers luck” argument for prioritizing rare diseases. This is a variant of the luck egalitarian idea of bad price luck. We conclude by discussing how higher willingness to pay, adjusted priority‐setting processes, and a broader societal focus on rare diseases could address such injustices. Recognising bad numbers luck clarifies the relationship between fairness and efficiency in health care priority setting.

Introduction

When resources are scarce, priority setting becomes unavoidable [1, 2]. To ensure prudent use of strained budgets, cost‐effectiveness analysis (CEA) has become a mainstay in contemporary priority setting in healthcare. The ethics of employing CEA in this context are widely debated [1, 2, 3, 4]. Central points of contention are the alleged tendency of CEA based on quality‐adjusted life years (QALY) to give lower priority to senior citizens [3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11], and people with disabilities [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Partly as a response to such concerns, scholars and policymakers have modified CEA or supplemented it with other values to better reflect fairness concerns [1, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Recent debates indicate a similar concern with fairness when prioritising treatment for rare diseases [22, 23, 24, 25, 26].

By definition, rare diseases affect small patient groups [27]. But despite this, questions of funding for their treatment are of immense importance for contemporary priority‐setting. Recent advances in precision medicine subdividing even prevalent diseases into categories with small numbers of patients in each subcategory and the growing number of therapies obtaining orphan designation mean that treatments for rare diseases are becoming more common [27, 28]. The orphan drug market is experiencing significant growth. In 2019, worldwide sales of orphan drugs accounted for 16% of the global pharmaceutical prescription market [29]. In 2024, just over 50% of all new drug approvals by the FDA obtained orphan designation [30]. While EU numbers are lower, their growth remains significant [29]. The development of orphan therapies offers hope to patients, but also poses substantial challenges, as these treatments often come with high prices.

Treatments for rare diseases are often disadvantaged in standard CEA frameworks for various reasons [22, p. 86; 31, p. 1017; 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37], Small patient populations and/or lack of treatment alternatives can make it challenging to undertake randomized, controlled trials [38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. As development, testing, and marketing are costly [23, p. 22; 40], high prices per patient or unit of health benefit are typical [22, p. 86; 23, p. 22; 31, p. 1016]. Consequently, if usual thresholds for willingness to pay are applied, treatments for rare diseases may not be prioritized [22, p. 86; 23, p. 22; 31, p. 1017; 32, 36, pp. 335–336; 37, 43, 44, 45], This raises the central question: Should we accept that rare diseases are systematically deprioritized, or should special priority be given to rare diseases to avoid this? In practice, however, orphan drug legislation has been adopted in the US, the EU, and beyond to provide incentives for manufacturers of orphan drugs, and several priority setting institutions give special priority to rare diseases. How might such priorities be justified? After all, they have opportunity costs. CEA weighs the interests of all patients—both those who will benefit from a new treatment and those who will be adversely affected by the lack of resources allocated to treatments that benefit them.

This paper contributes to the ongoing philosophical debate about healthcare prioritization and rare diseases. While we do not challenge the widely held belief that rarity is not morally important in itself, we point to the secondary effects of belonging to a small patient group and argue that this may justify prioritizing the treatment of rare diseases. These secondary effects give rise to distinctive egalitarian concerns. We argue that these features, which are necessarily connected to the rarity of the disease, give rise to egalitarian concerns when using CEA to set treatment priorities for rare diseases. We call the identified injustice bad numbers luck: it is bad luck to be part of a very small patient group, and people should not be disadvantaged because of it. We conclude with a discussion of how society might prioritize rare disease treatment to address these injustices.

The Problem of Small Patient Groups and Equality

The dominant view among ethicists and health economists is that rarity is not morally important in itself [21, 22, 25, 31, 43, 46, 47, 48], and the general public exhibits no higher willingness to pay for treatments for rare diseases [32, 46, 47, 49, 50, 51, 52]. Thus, if asked to choose between two patients, one with a rare disease and one with a common disease, who are equal in prognosis, severity, and capacity to benefit, we have no morally relevant reason to favour either patient. Size does not matter in making such a choice. As disease prevalence is not inherently morally relevant, we might prefer to draw lots. However, lottery systems are not practically feasible for broader priority‐setting decisions, which involve trade‐offs between different types of treatments and patient groups. Rather, CEA is a mainstay, as many societies aim to maximize health from available resources. 1 Still, if CEA systematically disadvantages those with rare diseases, using it would contradict the view that neither rarity nor commonness of disease should lead to preferential treatment. That would implicitly amount to unequal treatment based on a morally irrelevant factor, namely, the size of the patient population. Is this fair?

Recently, an argument grounded in the principle of formal equality has been put forward that posits that CEA's systematic disadvantaging of rare diseases can be considered unfair [23, 24, 26]. Formal equality asserts that similar cases should be treated alike unless a morally relevant distinction can be identified [23, 24, 53, pp. 227–229; 54], Assuming that all else is equal (degree of severity, capacity to benefit, etc.), the only distinguishing factor between rare and common diseases is patient population size, which is not a morally relevant factor [23, 24, pp. 309–310]. Thus, when rare diseases fare worse than common diseases in CEA merely because of the size of the patient population, this conflicts with formal equality [23, 24, p. 309]. According to Juth et al. (2021), we might, therefore, have reasons of formal equality to prioritize rare diseases, but not any substantial egalitarian reasons [24]. While we might have reasons to treat rare and common diseases similarly, we do not have additional reasons to give rare diseases special priority.

Contributing to this ongoing debate about the moral status of rarity, this paper develops a substantive egalitarian argument for giving priority to small patient groups. This bad numbers luck argument adds to the existing debate by proposing that giving higher priority to rare diseases need not be justified solely by pursuing formal equality between rare and common diseases. Instead, this prioritization can also be justified by egalitarian concerns related to disease rarity itself. The argument demonstrates that although patient population size is not inherently morally relevant, having a rare disease may entail morally relevant disadvantages. While this paper adopts a luck egalitarian approach, we do not deny that other egalitarian theories may reach similar conclusions. We do think, however, that luck egalitarianism picks up a distinct unfairness, which we call bad numbers luck.

The Bad Numbers Luck Argument

We argue that from the perspective of a luck egalitarian theory of distributive justice [55, 56, 57, 58, 59], those with rare diseases might suffer from what we call bad numbers luck. If people with rare diseases are deprioritized in priority setting decisions due to the consequences of bad numbers luck, they are treated unjustly.

Luck egalitarianism is a prominent responsibility‐sensitive theory of distributive justice. The core sentiment of the theory is that it matters morally whether a given distribution reflects differential bad luck or choices that people are themselves responsible for [55, 56, 57, 58, 60, 61]. According to this theory, only distributions that reflect differential bad luck are considered unjust. This theory has often been applied to healthcare [59, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66, 67, 68]. Critics have challenged luck egalitarianism for its alleged harsh consequences for those who are responsible for their health disadvantage [69, 70, 71, 72], the difficulty of distinguishing between those who are responsible for such disadvantages and those who are not [73, 74, 75, 76], and the potentially demeaning practices required for making such distinctions [77]. However, the theory remains worth considering in the case of rare diseases because its most controversial aspect—giving lower priority to people responsible for their disadvantage—does not apply to rare diseases.

Particularly relevant here is Cohen's idea of bad price luck [78]. Bad price luck captures the distributive injustice occurring when a person, through no fault of their own, finds that what they need to be as well off as others is expensive [78, 79, p. 85]. Those with needs or preferences that are more expensive to satisfy, for reasons they are not responsible for, suffer from bad price luck [78]. Cohen stresses that this view challenges many market‐generated inequalities [55, p. 923; 78, p. 17; 79, p. 86; 80], Cohen's argument is highly relevant to health‐related disadvantages and unmet health needs. People are typically not responsible for their need for treatment or its cost, as they have little control over the price of their medical care.

We suggest that if a treatment for a rare disease is not approved because it is not deemed cost‐effective, the affected patient group experiences a form of bad luck that is very similar to bad price luck. 2 Under CEA, treatments for rare diseases are disadvantaged precisely because the small number of patients may drive up prices and/or limit the available evidence. The resulting inequality arises from the composition of the patient group, not from personal responsibility, making it a clear case of brute luck. They are unlucky if the rarity of their disease implies a treatment cost that exceeds accepted thresholds and/or has an evidence base that is too uncertain to be acceptable for healthcare payers. As uncertain evidence may not directly affect the price, it seems imprecise to call this a case of bad price luck. Rather, we propose the term bad numbers luck to capture the form of luck egalitarian unfairness faced by people with rare diseases. While the term differs, the underlying luck egalitarian concern corresponds to that of bad price luck. Bad numbers luck differs from bad price luck in that the disadvantage stems from population size rather than cost fluctuations or market contingencies.

Let us consider some objections to this account of the unfairness involved. Recently, critics have argued that luck egalitarian concerns regarding disease rarity are over‐inclusive, as they would justify higher priority not only for rare diseases but also for common diseases that are particularly expensive to treat or for which possibilities for generating strong evidence of treatment effects are severely inhibited, for reasons unrelated to population size [25, p. 626]. Thus, variants of bad price luck may also affect common diseases. For instance, when a common disease is especially expensive to treat due to a morally irrelevant factor, one may argue that patients should not face unequal access to treatment simply because they suffer from a disease that is especially expensive to treat through no fault of their own. Patients with common diseases can therefore also experience bad price luck and other luck egalitarian distributive injustices. However, this observation does not undermine our argument. This is because bad numbers luck represents a unique disadvantage in rare diseases. While other forms of bad brute luck are associated with both rare and common diseases, bad numbers luck, all else being equal, provides a distinct egalitarian reason to consider giving priority to the treatment of rare diseases. This reason is absent in the case of common diseases.

Another concern is that, given the well‐known criticisms of luck egalitarianism and the fact that most of contemporary healthcare is distributed in ways that are not luck egalitarian, why is it important that there is a luck egalitarian argument against these inequalities? If we accept luck egalitarianism here, how does bad numbers luck trade off against other types of bad brute luck and against other concerns? In response, we submit that our purpose here is primarily conceptual and our claims are conditional. Our aim is not to defend luck egalitarianism as a general framework for health justice, but to explore what it reveals about the specific disadvantages faced by patients with rare diseases. We do not take a stance on whether this finding supports or undermines the broader plausibility of luck egalitarianism in health. Nor do we assess whether luck egalitarianism's implications for health in other contexts make it an all‐things‐considered unattractive theory. Such questions are beyond our current scope.

In addition, it is important to recall that most luck egalitarians are pluralists [5, p. 906; 56, p. 81; 58, 59, 81, pp. 22; 31]. They readily acknowledge that other values are important, and suggestions for how to implement luck egalitarianism in the context of health reflect this [59, 62, 63, 64, 65]. In practice, the concern to eliminate unchosen disadvantage may legitimately be outweighed by other moral considerations. These considerations suggest that bad numbers luck identifies a genuine distributive concern. Cost‐effectiveness analysis concerns aggregate welfare, while luck egalitarianism identifies when inequalities in that distribution are morally arbitrary. These two perspectives can coexist: CEA tells us how to maximise health, and luck egalitarianism tells us when such a maximisation perpetuates injustice.

Conclusion and Implications

While rarity lacks inherent moral significance, some features specific to small patient groups may unfairly disadvantage people with rare diseases. Our argument provides at least a pro tanto reason for giving some additional priority to people with rare diseases, grounded in the distinct form of unfairness we call bad numbers luck. The conclusion is pro tanto because a full luck egalitarian assessment of individuals' positions would require a more comprehensive analysis of how their levels of advantage came about. While having a rare disease might be considered bad luck, this does not rule out the theoretical possibility that other factors point in the opposite direction for specific individuals. However, as health care priority setting is about principles, factors, and overall assessments, as opposed to specific individuals this level of analysis seems appropriate for current purposes. Recognising bad numbers luck in priority‐setting does not, by itself, tell us how to respond in practice. It does, however, help identify where unmitigated CEA risks creating unfairness. The following examples illustrate different ways policymakers have explicitly or implicitly addressed such concerns.

A significant difficulty is that giving higher priority to orphan drugs entails an inevitable trade‐off: when societies fund orphan medicines that do not meet standard CEA criteria, the opportunity cost entails a loss of health for other patient groups [22, 24, p. 313; 31, 43, 82, 83]. A recent study examining the impact of decisions made by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) over the past 20 years found an adverse population health impact of adopting new treatments with high costs per unit of effect [84]. Irrespective of whether we are debating how to allocate funds within a health budget or how to spend additional funding, the trade‐off remains. It is not how much more political decision‐makers in a given society are willing to pay to achieve justice, but how much net health will be sacrificed to that end [26, p. 58].

Several countries have recently increased willingness‐to‐pay thresholds for treatments addressing very rare and very severe diseases [85]. A notable example is NICE's Highly Specialised Technology (HST) programme, which evaluates interventions for very rare conditions (affecting < 1:50,000 people). In comparison with NICE's normal reference values, the HST programme operates with a 10‐fold higher willingness‐to‐pay if the treatment demonstrates substantial QALY gains for conditions that shorten life significantly or severely impact quality of life [86]. Likewise, in Sweden, a higher willingness‐to‐pay threshold has recently been set for rare, highly severe conditions [87]. Such programs can be interpreted as a structured attempt to offset bad numbers luck for select groups.

In contrast to such explicit and systematic approaches, the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme in Australia applies a more discretionary approach. This includes a Rule of Rescue that can be applied when novel treatments for rare, life‐threatening conditions are ruled out by CEA [88]. Resting on largely qualitative criteria, 3 the Australian Rule of Rescue does not give a direct economic incentive for manufacturers to price up to a given threshold and allows for substantial discretion; that is, context‐sensitive judgment. However, this discretion also makes it vulnerable to critique for ad hoc decision‐making [89].

Alternative approaches exist beyond increasing willingness to pay. To address the scientific challenges of bad numbers luck (e.g., difficulties of conducting sufficiently large randomized trials), one can accept greater uncertainty about the clinical effectiveness of orphan medicines while staying within conventional willingness‐to‐pay thresholds. This option is pursued—more or less formalized—by health technology assessment (HTA) agencies in various countries, including Denmark and England [42, 90]. This approach provides for (some) expenditure control and does not incentivize high pricing of orphan medicines, but also has its drawbacks. Indirectly, acceptance of greater uncertainty about cost‐effectiveness will also lead to greater uncertainty about the opportunity costs of this prioritization.

Finally, alternative payment models are gaining renewed attention from the pharmaceutical industry and policymakers as a means of addressing uncertainties about the cost‐effectiveness and budgetary impact of (particularly expensive) orphan medicines [91], including conditional reimbursement, pay‐for‐performance, payment by instalments, risk pools, expenditure caps, and subscription models—each with its own practical and ethical challenges [85, 91, 92]. Such models aim to share the financial risk between healthcare payers and manufacturers. By linking payment to treatment outcomes, some of these models aim to address the challenges of uncertain cost‐effectiveness—but they do not directly address the development costs and difficulties related to rarity.

Further options could include scrutinizing orphan drug pricing mechanisms [82, 93]. Some scholars argue that certain rare disease treatments might have relatively low development costs (e.g., by harnessing possibilities for drug repurposing, direct targeting of disease‐causing mechanisms, drawing on basic research funded by patient organizations or charities) [93, 94, 95]. Moreover, orphan designation is already rewarded by regulators, including the EMA and the FDA, with additional years of market exclusivity. Sandman notes that this raises questions about whether the factors driving inequality can be addressed adequately within national healthcare systems or more efficiently at the transnational level or other policy arenas [26, 29]. 4

The practical implications outlined above illustrate how fairness‐based considerations can be integrated into existing efficiency‐oriented frameworks without abandoning their core rationale. If societies want to address the injustices faced by people with rare diseases, a strategy with several elements seems preferable. Increasing willingness to pay for or creating various exemptions for drugs targeting rare diseases may be insufficient and costly. Broader strategies, including risk‐sharing mechanisms, therefore merit consideration. Although we have focused on the distributive implications of bad numbers luck within cost‐effectiveness analysis, similar mechanisms may disadvantage people with rare diseases beyond the realm of reimbursement decisions. Rarity can entail social isolation [96, 97], and people with rare diseases may experience stigmatization in social settings, such as in education, workplaces, and healthcare provision, due to lacking public awareness of their condition [97, 98, 99, 100]. These forms of disadvantage arguably share the same moral structure: they arise from statistical rarity rather than personal fault, exemplifying the broader significance of bad numbers luck.

Ultimately, recognising bad numbers luck invites a more reflective balance between efficiency and fairness in health policy. While no system can eliminate all brute luck, acknowledging when institutional design amplifies morally arbitrary disadvantages is a first step toward greater justice in healthcare priority‐setting.
