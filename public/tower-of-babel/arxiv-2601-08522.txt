Degree bounds for linear differential equations and recurrences
Louis Gaillard

Abstract
Linear differential equations and recurrences reveal many properties about their solutions. Therefore, these equations are well-suited for representing solutions and computing with special functions. We identify a large class of existing algorithms that compute such representations as a linear relation between the iterates of an elementary operator known as a pseudo-linear map . Algorithms of this form have been designed and used for solving various computational problems, in different contexts, including effective closure properties for linear differential or recurrence equations, the computation of a differential equation satisfied by an algebraic function, and many others. We propose a unified approach for establishing precise degree bounds on the solutions of all these problems. This approach relies on a common structure shared by all the specific instances of the class. For each problem, the obtained bound is tight. It either improves or recovers the previous best known bound that was derived by ad hoc methods.



1
Introduction

Numerous elementary and special functions (resp. sequences) may be
defined as a solution of a linear differential (resp. recurrence)
equation with polynomial coefficients. Equivalently, one can view
such an equation as a linear differential (resp. recurrence) operator
that acts on functions and annihilates solutions. The solutions to
these equations define the class of D-finite functions
(resp. P-recursive sequences)
[
37
,
27
]
. Many
mathematical properties satisfied by D-finite functions can be
directly computed from their defining equation.
This motivates a fruitful line of research in Computer Algebra, that
consists in considering operators as an efficient data-structure for
representing their solutions
[
35
,
36
]
.
This paradigm requires efficient routines for computing with this
data-structure and implementing the
arithmetic of operators
.

Let
k
be a field of characteristic 0 and
x
an indeterminate. We
denote by
\partial_{x}
the usual derivation
d/dx
on the rational function
field
k(x)
and by
\sigma_{x}
the shift endomorphism
x\mapsto x+1
,
that maps any rational function
f(x)
to
f(x+1)
. By a slight abuse
of notation, we denote by
k(x)\left<\partial\right>
where
\partial
is a
derivation on
k(x)
, the skew polynomial ring of linear ordinary
differential operators in
\partial
, and
k(x)\left<\sigma_{x}\right>
for
the skew polynomial ring of linear ordinary recurrence
operators. Those are two examples of Ore
algebras
[
34
,
12
]
. Up to multiplying by the
denominator, one can always manipulate operators with polynomial
coefficients in
k[x]
. In this work, we study three families of
computational problems for operators.

Closure operations

D-finite functions are closed under sums and
products
[
37
, Thm. 2.3]
. For example, if two
functions
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are respectively represented by an
annihilating operator
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, one wants to compute a
representation for
\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
, that is, a nonzero
operator
L
such that
L(\alpha)=0
. Thus, the effective closure by
sum is the following problem: given

L_{1},L_{2}\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
, compute

L\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>\setminus\{0\}
that annihilates all
the sums
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
, where

L_{1}(\alpha_{1})=L_{2}(\alpha_{2})=0
. If one also requires
L
to have
minimal order, then it is a
least common left multiple (LCLM)

of
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
. Several algorithms have been designed and analysed
for computing LCLMs
[
11
,
40
]
. For the closure
by product, the analogous notion is called a
symmetric product
.
More generally, one can handle closure of D-finite functions under any
polynomial operation of the following type: given operators
L_{1},\dots,L_{s}\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
, compute a nonzero linear
differential operator
L
such that for arbitrary solutions
\alpha_{i}

of
L_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq s
,
L
annihilates

\alpha=J(x,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{1}^{(i_{1})},\dots,\alpha_{s},\dots,\alpha_{s}^{(i_{s})})
where
J
is a fixed multivariate polynomial.
Algorithmic approaches also exist at this level of
generality
[
26
]
. All these problems directly translate
in the shift case for recurrence operators.

Algebraic power series

A power series
\alpha(x)
over the field
k
is said to be

algebraic
if it is a root of a nonzero bivariate
polynomial
P(x,y)\in k[x,y]
.
It is known that such an algebraic function is
also D-finite
[
1
, p. 287]
, and we call

differential resolvent
an operator annihilating all the roots
of
P
. Cockle provided an algorithm
[
16
]
for computing
differential resolvents, that is the basis of a more recent efficient
algorithm for this problem
[
10
]
. The method extends
to the problem of the computation of an annihilating operator for the
composition
h=f\circ g
of a D-finite power series
f
and an
algebraic power series
g

[
28
]
, that is also known to be
D-finite
[
37
, Thm. 2.7]
.

Creative telescoping for bivariate rational functions

Creative telescoping refers to a family of methods that have proved
successful for symbolic integration and summation. The general
concept was first introduced by Zeilberger
[
41
]
and
it applies to a large class of functions. Here we focus on the simple
integration of a bivariate rational function

f(x,y)=p/q\in k(x,y)\setminus\{0\}
with
p
and
q
coprime.
For any integration contour
\omega
that avoids the poles of
f
, the
definite integral
F(x)=\int_{\omega}f(x,y)\,dy
is known to be
D-finite and the problem consists in computing a linear differential
equation satisfied by
F
. The heart of the method relies on finding
a
telescoper
for
f
, namely a linear differential operator

L\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>\setminus\{0\}
such that

L(f(x,y))=\partial_{y}(h(x,y))
for some rational
function
h
. Under reasonable assumptions on
h
and
\omega
, the
operator
L
is then proved to annihilate
F
. We specifically study
an algorithm
[
8
]
based on successive Hermite
reductions to address the problem of computing a telescoper for

f(x,y)
.

Despite the diversity of these problems, they all admit algorithmic
solutions where the coefficients of the computed operator are obtained
as an element in the nullspace of a matrix of rational functions, that
has a certain
structure
. The common principle in these
algorithms, that explains the structure in the matrix, is the
following: consider an arbitrary solution
\alpha(x)
of the computed
operator
L
; this solution
\alpha
is defined implicitly by a set of
relations it satisfies. These relations are given by the definition of

L
and allow to see
\alpha
as an element
a_{0}
of a finite
dimensional vector space
\mathcal{A}
over
k(x)
. Then, using the
relations satisfied by
\alpha
, each successive derivative

\alpha^{(i)}
is also identified to an element
a_{i}
of
\mathcal{A}
,
and finally, the operator
L
is obtained as a linear relation between
the vectors
(a_{i})_{i\geq 0}
. Such a relation exists by the finite
dimension of
\mathcal{A}
.

For example, for computing an LCLM of

L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, consider an arbitrary solution
\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
defined by the relations

L_{1}(\alpha_{1})=L_{2}(\alpha_{2})=0
. Then, express the successive
derivatives of
\alpha
using
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
to replace high order
derivatives of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
, until a linear relation is
found. Cockle’s algorithm
[
10
, Sec. 2.1]
computes a
differential resolvent of
P\in k[x,y]
using an analogous
strategy:
\alpha
is defined by
P(x,\alpha)=0
, then, the successive
derivatives of
\alpha
can be seen as elements of the quotient ring

k(x)[y]/(P)
, that is a finite dimensional vector space over
k(x)
.

This general principle can be formalised by the following algebraic
setup. We are given a sequence
(a_{i})_{i\geq 0}
of
k(x)^{n}
defined
by the simple inductive formula:

\displaystyle a_{0}=a\in k[x]^{n},\quad a_{i+1}=\theta(a_{i}),~i\geq 0,

(1)

where
\theta:k(x)^{n}\rightarrow k(x)^{n}
is a
pseudo-linear
map

[
23
,
12
]
, that is, there exists a linear
map
T
, seen as a matrix in
k(x)^{n\times n}
, such that

\theta=\partial+T
in the differential case, or

\theta=T\cdot\sigma_{x}
in the shift case. One may interpret the
matrix
T
as carrying out the action of the derivation (resp. the
shift) modulo some relations. The problem is to compute a minimal
linear relation with polynomial coefficients between the first
elements of the sequence
(a_{i})_{i\geq 0}
.

Problem 1
.

Given
\theta
a pseudo-linear map on
k(x)^{n}
,
a\in k[x]^{n}
and
letting
\rho=\dim\spn_{k(x)}(\theta^{i}a,i\geq 0)\leq n
, find

\eta=(\eta_{0},\dots,\eta_{\rho})\in k[x]^{\rho+1}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\eta_{0}\cdot a+\eta_{1}\cdot\theta a+\cdots+\eta_{\rho}\cdot\theta^{\rho}a=0.

(2)

In the case
\rho=n
, the vector
a
is called a cyclic vector of

\theta

[
2
,
5
,
9
,
15
]
and
the above relation gives a characteristic polynomial of

\theta

[
4
]
. By analogy with the classical Krylov
method for computing the characteristic polynomial of a matrix

T

[
29
,
32
]
, the linear
system (
2
) is referred to as a

pseudo-Krylov
system.
Each of the problems mentioned above reduces to solving the pseudo-Krylov
system (
2
) for a specific choice of

\theta
(and thus of the linear map
T
) and
a
.
Moreover, each reduction
to Problem
1
corresponds to an existing
algorithm in the literature
[
37
,
26
,
10
,
28
,
8
]
. The corresponding algorithm
constructs and solves the associated pseudo-Krylov system. Each
algorithm has usually the best known complexity for solving the
problem (except for LCLMs, where there exist faster algorithms using
Sylvester-type matrices adapted to the skew
setting
[
11
]
, or an evaluation-interpolation approach
based on truncated fundamental basis of
solutions
[
40
]
). However, for all specific instances,
the degree bound on the solution
\eta
that is derived from the
linear system (
2
) is an overestimation
of the actual degree. Tighter bounds are sometimes established and
obtained by
ad hoc

constructions
[
11
,
10
,
8
]
, or only
conjectured
[
7
,
28
]
.
This degree gap suggests the existence of an unrevealed structure
in the pseudo-Krylov system relative to each instance.
We aim at understanding this structure to accelerate the resolution
of the system (
2
) for all specific instances.

Our main contribution is to provide a refined technique for proving
degree bounds, and thus to explain the degree gap.
In each instance, a tight bound is obtained by viewing the matrix
of rational functions
T

as performing reduction modulo some relations. Indeed, we show that
this enables to find a certain
realisation
of
T

[
17
]
,
namely, writing

\displaystyle T=XM^{-1}Y,

where
X,M,Y
are polynomial matrices of
small
degree of sizes

n\times h,h\times h,h\times n
respectively. Since
the matrices
X,M,Y
have small degrees compared to the matrix
T
,
such a realisation yields a much more compact representation of
T
, providing
tight degree bounds and explaining the gap.
This is an
extension
to more general pseudo-Krylov systems
of the recent approach
[
20
]

where only the differential
case
\theta=\partial_{x}+T
is handled.
Also, the previous approach requires the matrix
T
to be

strictly proper
— namely, each entry of
T
has
numerator with degree strictly less than the denominator — as a technical assumption for the proof. Due to this assumption, the
degree bound obtained in each problem from this approach is only
valid under a genericity or a regularity assumption on the input. The
extension we now present is freed from the strict properness
assumption, leading to a unified approach for proving
bounds that hold unconditionally in each
instance.

1.1
Overview of the method

Our approach for establishing bounds mostly relies on tools
originating from control theory for linear systems over
k(x)
. For a
matrix of rational functions
R\in k(x)^{n\times p}
, one can define
an appropriate notion of degree, the
McMillan degree

of
R

[
30
,
31
]
, denoted as

\degMM(R)
. The McMillan degree extends the definition of degree of a
rational function as its number of poles counted with multiplicity,
including those at infinity. We relate
\degMM(R)
with the minimal
degree of an element of the (right) nullspace of
R
. Actually, if

\rho
denotes the rank of
R
and
\rho<p
, we show that there
exists a non-trivial polynomial solution to the linear system

R\eta=0
with

\displaystyle\deg(\eta)\leq\frac{\degMM(R)}{p-\rho}.

The precise definitions and results can be found in
Section
2
(see Theorem
2
).
This bound follows from classical results on minimal bases of

k[x]
-modules
[
19
]
. It can also be seen as an extension
to matrices of rational functions of the degree transfer between a
polynomial matrix and a minimal nullspace
basis
[
38
, Thm. 3.3]
(see
also
[
39
,
6
]
for a proof).

Next, our degree bounds for solutions of
Problem
1
follow from this result
applied to the matrices defining the pseudo-Krylov
systems (
2
); the difficulty is then to bound
their McMillan degree. Those matrices are called

pseudo-Krylov matrices
and are of the form
K=\begin{bmatrix}a&\theta a&\cdots&\theta^{m}a\end{bmatrix}\in k(x)^{n\times(m+1)}
, where
a\in k[x]^{n}
is a vector of polynomials
of degree at most
d_{a}
and
\theta
a pseudo-linear map
of the following shapes. There exists a matrix
T\in k(x)^{n\times n}
such that

\theta=p(x)\partial_{x}+T
where
p
is a polynomial of degree at most
1
in the differential case,
or
\theta=T\sigma_{x}
in the shift case. These two cases cover a class of Ore
operators containing differential operators in the usual derivation
\partial_{x}
or in
the Euler derivation
x\partial_{x}
and classical recurrence operators in
\sigma_{x}
.
In the differential case, the assumption on the degree of
p
ensures that
the derivation
\partial=p\partial_{x}
does not add any pole (including infinity)
when differentiating a rational function.
In Section
3
, we study and bound the McMillan
degree of this class of pseudo-Krylov matrices.
We show in Theorem
3
that

\displaystyle\degMM(K)\leq\rho d_{a}+m\degMM(T).

As a consequence, we establish our main result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\theta
be a pseudo-linear map of the shape defined above with
a matrix
T\in k(x)^{n\times n}
(
\theta=p(x)\partial_{x}+T
with

p\in k[x]
of degree
\leq 1
, or
\theta=T\cdot\sigma_{x}
).
Let
a\in k[x]^{n}
of degree
d_{a}
and

\rho=\dim\spn_{k(x)}(\theta^{i}a,i\geq 0)
. Then, for all

m\geq\rho
there exists a linear relation

\eta_{0}\cdot a+\cdots+\eta_{m}\cdot\theta^{m}a=0,
with

\eta=(\eta_{0},\dots,\eta_{m})\in k[x]^{m+1}\setminus\{0\}
such
that

\displaystyle\deg(\eta)\leq\left(\rho d_{a}+m\degMM(T)\right)/(m+1-\rho).

In particular, there exists a solution

\eta\in k[x]^{\rho+1}\setminus\{0\}

of Problem
1
for
(\theta,a)

satisfying

\displaystyle\deg(\eta)\leq\rho d_{a}+\rho\degMM(T).

The coefficients of a solution of
Problem
1
are thus bounded by

O(nd_{a}+n\degMM(T))
. Besides, if one relaxes the minimality on the
order of the operator in output in
Problem
1
and allows for relations of
length
m+1\geq\rho+1
, we give a degree bound on a solution of
minimal degree of the over-determined pseudo-Krylov system that is
parameterised by
m
. When applied to a specific instance, this gives
degree bound for operators that is parameterised by the order. The
obtained parameterisations are called
order-degree curves
and
explain the observation that higher order operators generally have
lower degree
[
13
,
14
]
.

By Theorem
1
, we are led to
estimate the McMillan degree of the matrix
T
of the problem in order
to establish tight degree bounds. In
Sections
4
-
9
, we bound
\degMM(T)

for each problem mentioned before,
using a realisation
XM^{-1}Y
for
T
.
Indeed, a tight bound on
\degMM(T)
can be derived from the degrees
of the polynomial matrices
X,M,Y
.

Matrices of rational functions

We recall the needed results and definitions from the theory of
matrices of rational functions. In particular, we underline how the
structure of the minors of a matrix of rational functions, that is
captured by its so-called
determinantal
denominators

[
17
]
,
is connected to the size of a
minimal basis of the nullspace
[
19
]
. Most material we
need here originates from control theory (see
e.g.,
[
24
]
).

For a rational matrix
R\in k(x)^{n\times p}
of rank
r
, the
(right) nullspace of
R
is the
k(x)
-vector space of dimension
p-r

containing all vectors
\eta\in k(x)^{p}
such that
R\eta=0
. One
can restrict to polynomial vectors
\eta\in k[x]^{p}
and see the
nullspace as a
k[x]
-module
[
19
]
. For a polynomial
matrix
M
with
c
columns, the column degree of
M
designates the
non-decreasing sequence
v_{1}\leq\cdots\leq v_{c}
of the degrees of the
columns of
M
. A basis
N\in k[x]^{p\times(p-r)}
of the
nullspace is called a
minimal basis
if the columns of
N
have
non-decreasing degrees and the column degree

v_{1}\leq\cdots\leq v_{p-r}
of
N
is minimal among all nullspace
bases
N^{\prime}
, that is, the column degree
v_{1}^{\prime}\leq\cdots\leq v_{p-r}^{\prime}
of
N^{\prime}

satisfies
v_{i}^{\prime}\geq v_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq p-r
. The
degrees
v_{1},\dots,v_{p-r}
are structural invariants of the matrix

R
and are called the
(right) Kronecker indices
of

R

[
24
, Sec. 6.5.4, p. 455]
. Our main goal is to bound
the minimal degree of a solution of pseudo-Krylov
systems (
2
). In other words, we want to
bound the first Kronecker index
v_{1}
of the associated pseudo-Krylov matrix.

The main result of this section is to relate the Kronecker
indices of a matrix of rational functions with its
McMillan degree. The definition of McMillan degree can be found in
Section
2.3
.
The following
result extends to rational matrices the degree transfer between a polynomial
matrix and a minimal nullspace basis
[
38
, Thm. 3.3]
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
R\in k(x)^{n\times p}
of rank
r<p
and Kronecker indices

v_{1},\dots,v_{p-r}
. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p-r}v_{i}\leq\degMM(R),\text{ and }v_{1}\leq\frac{\degMM(R)}{p-r}.

(3)

It is sufficient to estimate the McMillan degree of a matrix
R
in
order to bound the smallest degree of a polynomial solution to

R\eta=0
. Therefore, a bound on the degree of a solution of
Problem
1
can be derived from the
McMillan degree of the associated pseudo-Krylov matrix.
Theorem
2
is proved in
Section
2.4
after recalling some useful
facts and definitions for this work.

2.1
Determinantal denominators and Smith-McMillan normal form

For
R\in k(x)^{n\times p}
and a positive integer
\ell
, we denote
by
\varphi_{\ell}(R)
the monic least common denominator of all minors
of
R
of size at most
\ell
. We also set
\varphi_{0}(R)=1
. The
polynomials
\varphi_{\ell}(R)
are called the
determinantal
denominators
of
R

[
17
]
. Note that
\varphi_{1}(R)
is
the monic least common denominator of the entries of
R
. So one can
write
R=\left(1/\varphi_{1}(R)\right)\cdot N
with
N
a polynomial
matrix.
By definition, for
\ell\geq 0
,
\varphi_{\ell}(R)
divides

\varphi_{\ell+1}(R)
and
\varphi_{\ell}(R)=\varphi_{\ell+1}(R)

for
\ell\geq\rk(R)
. Moreover by a direct expansion of the
determinant,
\varphi_{\ell}(R)
divides
\varphi_{1}(R)^{\ell}
. We recall
how determinantal denominators behave with respect to sums, products
and inverse of matrices.

Proposition 1
.

[
17
, Thm. 1-2]

If a rational matrix
R
is the sum
R_{1}+R_{2}
of two rational
matrices
R_{1},R_{2}
, then for all
\ell
,
\varphi_{\ell}(R)
divides

\varphi_{\ell}(R_{1})\varphi_{\ell}(R_{2})
. Moreover, if
\varphi_{1}(R_{1})

and
\varphi_{1}(R_{2})
are coprime then equality holds. Similarly if

R
is the product
R_{1}R_{2}
of two rational matrices
R_{1},R_{2}

2.2
Matrix fraction description

We consider representations of a rational matrix

R\in k(x)^{n\times p}
of the form

\displaystyle R=XM^{-1}Y,

(6)

where
X,M,Y
are polynomial matrices of sizes

n\times m,m\times m,m\times p
respectively and
M
is
non-singular. Such a representation for

is called a

[
17
]
.

Proposition 3
.

[
17
, Thm. 10]

Let
R=XM^{-1}Y
be a realisation of
R
. For all
\ell\geq 0
,

\varphi_{\ell}(R)
divides
\det M
.

As special cases of realisations, we say that

with

N\in k[x]^{n\times p},D\in k[x]^{p\times p}
non-singular is a

right matrix fraction description
(MFD), and similarly,

with

non-singular
is a
left
MFD. In both cases, we say that

or

is the
denominator of the description. Realisations and MFDs are not unique.
For example, for any non-singular
p\times p
polynomial matrix

,
one has
R=NV(DV)^{-1}
.

Recall that a matrix
U\in k[x]^{n\times n}
is unimodular if its
determinant is a nonzero element in
k
. Let
A,B
be
n\times m

and
h\times m
polynomial matrices. A matrix

G\in k[x]^{m\times m}
is a
common right divisor
of
A
and

B
if there exist polynomial matrices
A_{1},B_{1}
such that
A=A_{1}G

and
B=B_{1}G
. The matrices

and

are said to be
right
relatively prime
if their only common right divisors are
unimodular. Similarly, one can define
common left divisors
and

relatively left prime
matrices by taking transposes in the
above definitions. Now a right (resp. left) MFD
R=ND^{-1}
is said
to be
irreducible
if
N,D
are right (resp. left) relatively
prime.

Proposition 4
.

[
17
, Thm. 10]

A matrix fraction description of

R\in k(x)^{n\times p}
with denominator
D
(either right or
left) is irreducible if and only if

\varphi_{\ell}(R)=\varphi_{\ell}(D^{-1})
for all
\ell\geq 0
.

2.3
McMillan degree

A matrix
R(x)\in k(x)^{n\times p}
is called
proper
if

R(x)
admits a finite limit when
x
tends to infinity. In other
words, for every entry of
R
, its numerator has degree less or equal
than its denominator. A strictly proper matrix tends to
0
at infinity.

When
R
is proper, the
McMillan degree of
R
, denoted

\degMM(R)
, is defined as the sum of the degrees of the denominator
polynomials in its Smith-McMillan form
[
24
, p. 444]
.
By (
5
),

\displaystyle\degMM(R)=\deg(\varphi_{r}(R)),

(7)

where
r
is the rank of
R
. For arbitrary matrix

R\in k(x)^{n\times p}
, one can always decompose
R=R_{o}+W

where
R_{o}
is strictly proper and
W
is a polynomial matrix. Then,

W(1/x)
is proper and one can define
[
24
, p. 466]

\displaystyle\degMM(R)=\degMM(R_{o})+\degMM(W(1/x)).

(8)

Also,
\degMM(R)=\sum_{\alpha}g_{\alpha}

[
18
, Cor. 2]

where
\alpha
ranges over all the poles of
R
, infinity included,
and
g_{\alpha}
is the maximum order to which the pole
\alpha
occurs
in a minor of
R
of arbitrary size. In particular, for polynomial
matrices
M
of rank
r
,
\degMM(M)
is the maximum of the degrees of
the
i\times i
minors of
M
for all
i\leq r
. We also define the

minor degree
of a polynomial matrix
M
to be the maximum of
the degrees of minors of
M
of size
exactly

r
and denote it

\Minordeg(M)
. By a slight abuse of notation, the
McMillan
degree
sometimes refers the
minor degree
in the literature
as in
[
39
]
. Yet in this work, we distinguish these
two notions of degrees.

By a result of Kalman, one can always reduce to proper rational
matrices thanks to a suitable change of variables.

Proposition 5
.

[
25
, Prop. 11]
For any rational matrix

R\in k(x)^{n\times p}
, there exists a
transformation
\mu(x)=(\alpha x+\beta)/(\gamma x+\delta)

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in k
,

c=\alpha\delta-\beta\gamma\neq 0
,
\gamma\neq 0
,
such that
R(\mu(x))
is proper and

\degMM(R(x))=\degMM(R(\mu(x)))
. The result still holds for a
finite family of matrices
(R_{1},\dots,R_{\ell})
, there exists
\mu

such that for all
1\leq i\leq\ell
,
R_{i}(\mu)
is proper and

\degMM(R_{i})=\degMM(R_{i}(\mu))
.

In Proposition
5
, the
transformation
\mu
is an invertible change of variables that sends
all the poles of the matrix, infinity included to finite distinct new
poles. In particular,
\mu
moves the point at infinity to the finite
point
-\delta/\gamma
. For a rational matrix (or vector)
R
, let

R_{\mu}
denote the transformed matrix
R(\mu(x))
. By (
7
), we have

\degMM(R)=\deg(\varphi_{r}(R_{\mu}))
with
r
the rank of
R
. We
further rely on the following properties of McMillan degree.

Proposition 6
.

[
31
,
18
]

Let
R
be a rational matrix. If
R
is a product
R_{1}R_{2}
or a sum

R_{1}+R_{2}
, then
\degMM(R)\leq\degMM(R_{1})+\degMM(R_{2})
. If
R

is square and non-singular, then
\degMM(R^{-1})=\degMM(R)
.

When the matrices involved in
Proposition
6
are proper,
the results directly follow from
Propositions
1

and
2
on determinantal denominators. In the
general case, one reduces to the proper case by a change of variable
of the shape of Proposition
5
.
Therefore, up to a change of variables, the McMillan degree of a matrix

R
is given by the
degree of the
largest determinantal denominator of the matrix, which, by Propositions
3

and
4
, can be estimated
via a realisation or a description of
R
.

3
Degree bounds for pseudo-Krylov systems

In this section, our main result is established: we bound the degrees
of the polynomial coefficients in the solutions of
Problem
1
for pseudo-linear maps of the
form
\theta=p(x)\partial_{x}+T
with
p
a polynomial of degree at most

1
or
\theta=T\cdot\sigma_{x}
. We also derive an order-degree
curve by relaxing the minimality on the order in
Problem
1
and allowing over-determined
pseudo-Krylov systems.

The degree bound is obtained from
Theorem
2
by bounding the McMillan
degree of the matrix of the pseudo-Krylov
system (
2
).

Theorem 3
.

Let
T\in k(x)^{n\times n}
,
\theta=p(x)\partial_{x}+T
with

p\in k[x]
of degree at most
1
or
\theta=T\cdot\sigma_{x}
,
and
a\in k[x]^{n}
of degree
d_{a}
. Let

K\in k(x)^{n\times(m+1)}
be the pseudo-Krylov matrix
K=\begin{bmatrix}a&\theta a&\cdots&\theta^{m}a\end{bmatrix},

and let
\rho
denote its rank.
Then,

\displaystyle\degMM(K)\leq\rho d_{a}+m\degMM(T).

The proof of Theorem
3
is
developed in the rest of the section. As a consequence, we obtain
Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
K=\begin{bmatrix}a&\theta a&\cdots&\theta^{m}a\end{bmatrix}\in k(x)^{n\times(m+1)}
. Note that
\rho
is the rank of
K
.
By Theorem
2
,
the right Kronecker index
v_{1}
of
K

satisfies

v_{1}\leq\degMM(K)/{(m+1-\rho)}
,
and by Theorem
3
,
there exists an element in the nullspace of
K
of degree at most

1/(m+1-\rho)\cdot(\rho d_{a}+m\degMM(T))
.
The last claim follows by setting
m=\rho
.
∎

The general sketch for proving
Theorem
3
is the following: if both

T
and
K
are proper matrices, then
by (
7
), their McMillan degree is
completely determined by their determinantal denominators. In
Section
3.1
, we study the determinantal
denominators of pseudo-Krylov matrices. Finally in
Section
3.2
, we prove
Theorem
3
by reducing the general
case to the proper case using a change of variable and

5

applying the change of variable

in

, the obtained matrix

is no longer a pseudo-Krylov matrix satisfying the assumptions
of Theorem

(see Propositions
7

and
8
). In particular, in the

\theta=p(x)\partial_{x}+T

.

3.1
Determinantal denominators of pseudo-Krylov matrices

For
T\in k(x)^{n\times n}
, we study the determinantal denominators
of pseudo-Krylov matrices of the following shape:

\displaystyle K=\begin{bmatrix}\theta^{s_{1}}u_{1}&\cdots&\theta^{s_{m}}u_{m}\end{bmatrix}\in k(x)^{n\times m},

(9)

with
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\in k[x]^{n}
,
s_{1},\dots,s_{m}
non-negative
integers, and
\theta
the pseudo-linear map

T\sigma_{x}
or
p(x)\partial_{x}+T
, where in this section,

is a
polynomial of

.

3.1.1
Differential case

We start by the differential case with
\theta=\partial+T
, where
\partial

is any derivation on
k(x)
of the form
p(x)\partial_{x}
where

p(x)\in k[x]
. An elementary reasoning by induction shows the
following.

Lemma 1
.

Let
T=D^{-1}N\in k(x)^{n\times n}
be a description,

\theta=\partial+T
and
\theta_{1}=\partial+(N-\partial(D))D^{-1}
. For all

s\geq 0
,
\theta^{s}=\partial^{s}+D^{-1}\Omega_{s}
with

\Omega_{s}=\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}\theta_{1}^{i}N\partial^{s-1-i}
.

Proof.

The results holds for
s=0
. Suppose it holds for
s-1\geq 0
, then
by induction

\displaystyle\theta^{s}

\displaystyle=\theta\theta^{s-1}=\left(\partial+D^{-1}N\right)\left(\partial^{s-1}+D^{-1}\Omega_{s-1}\right),

Then, since
\partial(D^{-1})=-D^{-1}\partial(D)D^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\theta^{s}

\displaystyle=\partial^{s}+D^{-1}N\partial^{s-1}+D^{-1}ND^{-1}\Omega_{s-1}-D^{-1}\partial(D)D^{-1}\Omega_{s-1}+D^{-1}\partial\Omega_{s-1}

\displaystyle=\partial^{s}+D^{-1}\left(N\partial^{s-1}+\left((N-\partial(D))D^{-1}+\partial\right)\Omega_{s-1}\right)=\partial^{s}+D^{-1}\left(N\partial^{s-1}+\theta_{1}\Omega_{s-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\partial^{s}+D^{-1}\Omega_{s}.\qed

Proposition 7
.

Let
s_{1}\leq\cdots\leq s_{m}
be non-negative integers,

T\in k(x)^{n\times n}
and
\theta=\partial+T
. For any vectors of
polynomials
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\in k[x]^{n}
, the pseudo-Krylov matrix

\displaystyle K=\begin{bmatrix}\theta^{s_{1}}u_{1}&\cdots&\theta^{s_{m}}u_{m}\end{bmatrix}\in k(x)^{n\times m},

satisfies
\varphi_{\ell}(K)
divides
\varphi_{\ell}(T)^{s_{m}}
for all

\ell\geq 0
.

Proof.

We prove that any minor of size
\ell
of
K
can be written with
denominator
\varphi_{\ell}(T)^{s_{m}}
. It suffices to prove the result
for
\ell=m
since one can delete in
K
the
m-\ell
columns that
are not selected in the minor.
We proceed by induction on
m+s_{m}\geq 1
with
m\geq 1
. For

m=1
, the result holds since
\theta^{s_{m}}u_{m}
can be written with
denominator
\varphi_{1}(T)^{s_{m}}
. For
s_{m}=0
,

K\in k[x]^{n\times m}
, thus
\varphi_{m}(K)=1
. Now suppose
that
m\geq 2
and
s_{m}\geq 1
. First, if
s_{1}=0
, the first
column of
K
has polynomial entries. Let
\kappa
be a minor of
K
of
size
m

\kappa
along the first column of
K

is a linear combination of minors of size
m-1

m-1

K
with polynomial coefficients. By induction, it can
be written with denominator
\varphi_{m-1}(T)^{s_{m}}
which divides

\varphi_{m}(T)^{s_{m}}
. Finally if
s_{1}\geq 1
, let
T=D^{-1}N
be
an irreducible left MFD of
T
. Then, by
Lemma
1
for
1\leq j\leq m
,

\displaystyle\theta^{s_{j}}u_{j}=u_{j}^{(s_{j})}+D^{-1}\Omega_{s_{j}}(u_{j}),

where
\Omega_{s}=\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}\theta_{1}^{i}N\partial^{s-1-i}
for all

s\geq 0
,
\theta_{1}
is the pseudo-linear map
\partial+T_{1}
with

T_{1}=(N-\partial(D))D^{-1}
. So, we get

\displaystyle K=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{(s_{1})}&\cdots&u_{m}^{(s_{m})}\end{bmatrix}+D^{-1}\cdot K_{1},

with

has column

\Omega_{s_{j}}(u_{j})

and

\varphi_{m}(D^{-1})\varphi_{m}(K_{1})=\varphi_{m}(T)\varphi_{m}(K_{1})

Now, by multi-linearity of the determinant, any minor of size

of

is the sum of the same minors of the matrices

\displaystyle\overline{K}=\begin{bmatrix}\theta_{1}^{i_{1}}(Nu_{1}^{(s_{1}-1-i_{1})})&\cdots&\theta_{1}^{i_{m}}(Nu_{r}^{(s_{m}-1-i_{m})})\end{bmatrix},

3.2.1
Differential case

Lemma 2
.

Let
T\in k(x)^{n\times n}
and consider the pseudo-linear map

\theta=p(x)\partial_{x}+T
with

a polynomial of degree at most 1.
Let

, and
j\geq 0
. Then, for all transformations

\mu(x)
of the shape of
Proposition
5
,

\displaystyle(\theta^{j}a)(\mu(x))=\overline{\theta}^{j}(a_{\mu}),

with
a_{\mu}=a(\mu(x))
and
\overline{\theta}=\Delta+T_{\mu}

where
\Delta
is the derivation
(p_{\mu}/\mu^{\prime})\cdot\partial_{x}
over

k(x)
with
T_{\mu}=T(\mu(x))
and
p_{\mu}=p(\mu(x))
. Moreover, letting
u
the
denominator of
\mu

a\in k[x]^{n}
and has degree
d_{a}

there exists

\displaystyle\overline{\theta}^{j}a_{\mu}=1/u^{d_{a}}\sum_{i=0}^{j}{p_{i,j}}\overline{\theta}^{i}a^{*},

is a
polynomial
.

Proof.

The first claim holds for
j=0
. Suppose it holds for
j\geq 0
.
Because,
\theta^{j+1}a=T\cdot\theta^{j}a+p(\theta^{j}a)^{\prime}
,

\left(\theta^{j+1}a\right)(\mu)=T_{\mu}\left((\theta^{j}a)(\mu)\right)+p_{\mu}(\theta^{j}a)^{\prime}(\mu)
. However, by the chain rule

(\theta^{j}a)^{\prime}(\mu)=1/\mu^{\prime}\left((\theta^{j}a)(\mu)\right)^{\prime}
.
Thus,

\left(\theta^{j+1}a\right)(\mu)=\overline{\theta}\left((\theta^{j}a)(\mu)\right)=\overline{\theta}^{j+1}(a_{\mu})
,
by induction.

Next, let
\mu=(\alpha x+\beta)/(\gamma x+\delta)

with
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in k
,

,

\gamma\neq 0
and

. Then,
\mu^{\prime}=c/u^{2}
and

is of degree at most 1,

is a polynomial
and is divisible by

. Therefore, by induction on

,

\Delta^{\ell}(1/u^{d_{a}})
can be written with denominator

and thus,

is a polynomial for all

. Besides, we
have
a_{\mu}=a^{*}/u^{d_{a}}
with

a vector of polynomials of
degree at most
d_{a}
, then (
10
) holds
for
j=0
. Finally, suppose it holds for
j\geq 0
, then by induction,

\displaystyle\overline{\theta}^{j+1}(a_{\mu})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\left(\binom{j}{i}\Delta^{j-i}(1/u^{d_{a}})\overline{\theta}^{i+1}a^{*}+\binom{j}{i}\Delta^{j-i+1}(1/u^{d_{a}})\overline{\theta}^{i}a^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j+1}\left[\binom{j}{i-1}+\binom{j}{i}\right]\Delta^{j-i+1}(1/u^{d_{a}})\overline{\theta}^{i}a^{*}=\sum_{i=0}^{j+1}\binom{j+1}{i}\Delta^{j+1-i}(1/u^{d_{a}})\overline{\theta}^{i}a^{*},

whence the result.

3.2.2
Shift case

Lemma 3

Let

be an arbitrary matrix of rational

\degMM(\sigma_{x}(T))=\degMM(T)

Proof.

Write

is

\sigma_{x}(A)/\sigma_{x}(B)

\sigma_{x}(A)
and
\sigma_{x}(B)

\varphi_{\ell}(\sigma_{x}(T_{o}))=\sigma_{x}(\varphi_{\ell}(T_{o}))
.
Thus, by (

),

\degMM(T_{o})=\degMM(\sigma_{x}(T_{o}))
. Also,

\degMM(W)=\degMM(W(1/x))
is the maximum of the degrees of any

W
. Since
\deg(\sigma_{x}(p))=\deg(p)
for any polynomial

, then
\degMM(W)=\degMM(\sigma_{x}(W))
, and

),
\degMM(T)=\degMM(\sigma_{x}(T))
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

for

4
Least common left multiple

We start with the closure by sums for
D
-finite
functions. Let
L_{1},\dots,L_{s}\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
, we want

L=\LCLM(L_{1},\dots,L_{s})
a least common left
multiple of the
L_{i}
’s, that is, a minimal order operator that annihilates
all the sums of solutions of the
L_{i}
’s.

Theorem 4
.

Let
L_{1},\dots,L_{s}\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
be of respective orders

r_{1},\dots,r_{s}
, and degrees at most
d
in
x
. Then, there exists
an
LCLM

L
of
L_{1},\dots,L_{s}
of order
\rho\leq\sum_{i=1}^{s}r_{i}
and
degree at most
s\rho d
. For all
m\geq\rho
, there exists a
nonzero common left multiple of order
m
and degree at most

smd/(m-\rho+1)
.

For least common left multiples of
s
operators of order at most
r
and
degree at most
d
, our bound behaves like
ds^{2}r
. We retrieve the leading term
of the best known bound for LCLMs
[
11
, Thm. 6]
.
Also, for all
m\geq\rho
, there exists common left
multiples of
L_{1},\dots,L_{s}
of order
m
and degree at most

msd/(m-\rho+1)
. Also from Theorem
4
,
the arithmetic size of a common left multiple is minimized
for
m=2(\rho-1)
, so there exists a common left multiple of
arithmetic size at most
4(\rho-1)sd\leq 4s^{2}dr
which is a small
improvement over the bound

O(s^{2}(d+r)^{2})

[
11
, Thm. 10]
.

Reduction to Problem

For simplicity, we first assume

We follow the standard approach for computing an LCLM

Let

L_{2}

5
Symmetric Product

After sums come the closure by products. In
terms of operators, it corresponds to symmetric product. For

L_{1},\dots,L_{s}\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
, we want to compute a
minimal order operator
L=L_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes L_{s}
that
annihilates all the products of solutions of the
L_{i}
’s. We again
first consider the case
s=2
and then extend the approach for
arbitrary
s
. For
s=2
, we prove the following result.

Theorem 5
.

Let
L_{1},L_{2}\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
be of respective orders

r_{1}
and
r_{2}
and degrees
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
. Then, there exists a
symmetric product
L=L_{1}\otimes L_{2}
of order
\rho\leq r_{1}r_{2}

and degree at most
\rho(d_{1}r_{2}+d_{2}r_{1})
. For all

m\geq\rho
, there exists a left multiple of
L
of order
m
and
degree at most
m(d_{1}r_{2}+d_{2}r_{1})/(m-\rho+1)
.

Our new bound is an improvement of the previous best known bound

[
26
, Thm. 8]

by one order of magnitude. Experiments suggest that the actual degree
of
L
is bounded by

(r_{1}r_{2}-r_{1}-r_{2}+2)(d_{1}r_{2}+d_{2}r_{1})

[
7
, Chap. 10]
,
and this experimental bound shares the same leading term as our bound.

,

\displaystyle\degMM(T)\leq d_{1}r_{2}+d_{2}r_{1}.

(13)

Finally, Theorem
5
follows from
Theorem
1
with
d_{a}
and
\degMM(T)\leq d_{1}r_{2}+d_{2}r_{1}
.

5.3
Symmetric product of several operators

This can be generalised to the computation of a symmetric product of

s\geq 2
operators. Let
L=L_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes L_{s}
with

L_{i}
of order
r_{i}
and degree at most
d
for all
i
. Let

R=r_{1}\cdots r_{s}
and
r=\max_{i}(r_{i})
. Let

\alpha=\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{s}
where
L_{i}(\alpha_{i})=0
for all

i
. By Leibniz rule, one can express the successive derivatives
of
\alpha
as linear combinations of

(\alpha_{1}^{(h_{1})}\cdots\alpha_{s}^{(h_{s})})
for
0\leq h_{i}<r_{i}
,

1\leq i\leq s
. Therefore
L
can still be derived from a solution
of Problem
1
with

T\in k(x)^{R\times R}
. And one can show that

\degMM(T)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{s}(r_{1}\cdots r_{i-1}\cdot d\cdot r_{i+1}\cdots r_{s})
, generalising the proof
of (
13
). Then,
\degMM(T)\leq sdr^{s-1}
so
L
is
of order at most
R
and degree bounded by

sRdr^{s-1}\leq sdr^{2s-1}
. By comparing with the previous
best known bound

sR^{2}d\leq sdr^{2s}

[
26
, Thm. 8]
, we observe that
we save the same factor
r
as in the case
s=2
.

6
Polynomials

In Sections
4
and
5
, we gave bounds for
the effective closure properties of D-finite functions by sums and
products. More generally, any function
\alpha
that can be expressed
as a polynomial in some D-finite functions
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s}

and their derivatives is also D-finite
[
37
]
. In
this section, we
bound the arithmetic size of an annihilator of

\alpha
in terms of the size of the annihilators of the
\alpha_{i}
’s
and improve the previous bound
[
26
]
.

Suppose that
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s}
are generic solutions of the
operators
L_{1},\dots,L_{s}
with orders
r_{1},\dots,r_{s}
and degree

d_{1},\dots,d_{s}
respectively, we want an annihilator
L
of

\displaystyle\alpha=J(x,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{1}^{(r_{1}-1)},\dots,\alpha_{s},\dots,\alpha_{s}^{(r_{s}-1)}),

(14)

where
J(x,y_{1,0},\dots,y_{1,r_{1}-1},\dots,y_{s,0},\dots,y_{s,r_{s}-1})

is a multivariate polynomial. To derive degree bounds for
L
, one
could decompose
J
as sums and products and obtain bounds from
Theorem
4
and
5
.
But this direct method
fails in establishing tight degree bounds
[
26
]
as it
does not exploit the structure of the polynomial
J
.
Instead, we adopt the settings of
[
26
]
:
the operator
L
is computed
directly from the polynomial
J
by expressing the successive
derivatives of
\alpha
until a linear relation is found. We denote

\deg_{x}(J)
for the degree of
J
with respect to
x
and

\Deg(J)=(k_{1},\dots,k_{s})
where
k_{i}
is the total degree of
J
in
the variables
(y_{i,j})_{0\leq j<r_{i}}
. We further assume that
J

is homogeneous with respect to each group of variables

(y_{i,j})_{0\leq j<r_{i}}
and of degree
k_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq s
.
We further refer to this assumption by saying that
J
is
homogeneous
.
If
J
is not
homogeneous, then one can decompose
J=J_{1}+\cdots+J_{\ell}
where
every
J_{i}
is homogeneous, get a bound for each
J_{i}
and combine
them using Theorem
4
. It turns out that the
previous overestimation does not occur when every homogeneous part is
handled as a whole
[
26
]
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
L_{1},\dots,L_{s}\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
be of respective
orders
r_{i}
and degrees
d_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq s
. Let
J
be a
homogeneous multivariate polynomial as
in (
14
) with
\Deg(J)=(k_{1},\dots,k_{s})
. Then, there exists an operator
L\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>

such that for all
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s}
such that

L_{1}(\alpha_{1})=\cdots=L_{s}(\alpha_{s})=0
and

\alpha=J(x,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha^{(r_{1}-1)},\dots,\alpha_{s},\dots,\alpha_{s}^{(s-1)})
, we have
L(\alpha)=0
and
L
has order

\rho\leq R=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\binom{k_{i}+r_{i}-1}{k_{i}}

and degree at most

\rho\deg_{x}(J)+\rho R\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{k_{i}d_{i}}{k_{i}+r_{i}-1}
. For all
m\geq\rho
, there exists a nonzero operator
of order
m
, which also annihilates these functions
\alpha
, of
degree at most

1/(m-\rho+1)\cdot(\rho\deg_{x}(J)+m\cdot R\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{k_{i}d_{i}}{k_{i}+r_{i}-1})
.

This result improves the previous degree bound

R\deg_{x}(J)+R^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{s}k_{i}d_{i}

[
26
, Thm. 8]

for an operator of minimal order. Similarly it improves the
associated order-degree
curves
[
26
, Thm. 13]
. Note that if
one applies this result in the case of the symmetric product (i.e.
, with

J=y_{1,0}y_{2,0}
), one retrieves
Theorem
5
as a corollary.
Other special cases of this general
theorem are presented in Section
6.3
.

6.1
Reduction to Problem
1

Using Leibniz’s rule, the successive derivatives of
\alpha
all lie
in the
k(x)
-vector space
\mathcal{V}
spanned by the elements

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{x}^{h_{1,1}}\alpha_{1}\right)\cdots\left(\partial_{x}^{h_{1,k_{1}}}\alpha_{1}\right)\cdots\left(\partial_{x}^{h_{s,1}}\alpha_{s}\right)\cdots\left(\partial_{x}^{h_{s,k_{s}}}\alpha_{s}\right),

(15)

with
0\leq h_{i,1}\leq\cdots\leq h_{i,k_{i}}<r_{i}
for all

1\leq i\leq s
. The dimension of
\mathcal{V}
is at most

R=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\binom{k_{i}+r_{i}-1}{k_{i}}
and so is the
order of
L
. Now, if
V_{\ell}
is the vector of coefficients of

\alpha^{(\ell)}
in the above decomposition, we have

V_{\ell+1}=\theta V_{\ell}
where
\theta
is the pseudo-linear map

\partial_{x}+T
with
T\in k(x)^{R\times R}
mapping
any above element to its derivative where we rewrite high order
derivatives using
L_{1},\dots,L_{s}
. Moreover, one can read
V_{0}
from

\alpha
given by (
14
). Hence, one can compute
the coefficients of
L
from a solution to
Problem
1
for
(T,V_{0})
.

6.2
McMillan degree

The columns of
T
consist of the derivatives of the elements of the
generating set as in (
15
) according
to this same generating set. To each column of
T
corresponds a
tuple

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}=(h_{1,1},\dots,h_{1,k_{1}},\dots,h_{s,1},\dots,h_{s,k_{s}}),

with
0\leq h_{i,1}\leq\cdots\leq h_{i,k_{i}}<r_{i}
for all

1\leq i\leq s
. We denote by
\mathbf{H}
the set of columns of
T
.
Generalising the approach of Section
4.1
, we write

T=XM^{-1}
where
M\in k[x]^{R\times R}
is a diagonal matrix
whose diagonal entries are the denominators of the columns of
T
, and

X=TM\in k[x]^{R\times R}
is a polynomial
matrix. Let
q_{i}(x)
be the leading coefficient of
L_{i}
(i.e.
, the
coefficient of
\partial^{r_{i}}
).
Differentiating (
15
) and rewriting
high order derivatives using
L_{1},\dots,L_{s}
show that the
derivative of (
15
) can be written
with denominator

\displaystyle q_{\mathbf{h}}=\prod_{i=1}^{s}q_{i}^{\delta({h_{i,k_{i}},r_{i}-1})},

where

\delta({a,b})=1
if
a=b
,
0
otherwise. These products give the
diagonal entries of
M
. It has degree bounded by

d_{\mathbf{h}}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\delta({h_{i,k_{i}},r_{i}-1})\cdot d_{i}
and the
corresponding column of
X
satisfies the same degree bound. Now, for

\mu
a transformation such that
T_{\mu}
is proper with denominator

u
, we define
D_{u}=\diag(u^{d_{\mathbf{h}}})_{\mathbf{h}\in\mathbf{H}}
.
Then,
X^{*}=X_{\mu}D_{u}
and
M^{*}=M_{\mu}D_{u}
are both polynomial
matrices whose column
\mathbf{h}
has degree bounded by
d_{\mathbf{h}}

for all
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbf{H}
. So,
T_{\mu}=X^{*}(M^{*})^{-1}
and is
proper. So,

\degMM(T)=\deg(\det M^{*})\leq\sum_{\mathbf{h}\in\mathbf{H}}d_{\mathbf{h}}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}R_{i}d_{i}
, where
R_{i}
is the number
of elements in
\mathbf{H}
satisfying
h_{i,k_{i}}=r_{i}-1
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle R_{i}=R\frac{\binom{k_{i}+r_{i}-2}{k_{i}-1}}{\binom{k_{i}+r_{i}-1}{k_{i}}}=\frac{Rk_{i}}{k_{i}+r_{i}-1}.

Finally, it follows that

\displaystyle\degMM(T)\leq R\sum_{i=1}^{s}d_{i}\frac{k_{i}}{k_{i}+r_{i}-1}.

(16)

Again, Theorem
6
is obtained as a consequence of
Theorem
1
, with
d_{a}=\deg_{x}(J)

and (
16
).

6.3
Applications

We already mention the symmetric product
as a consequence of Theorem
6
.
We exemplify
Theorem
6
through three other special cases:
symmetric power, associate and Wronskian. For a family of power series

(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
, we define its Wronskian

w(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
as the determinant of

(\alpha_{j}^{(i-1)})_{1\leq i,j\leq r}
.

Corollary 1
.

1.

(Symmetric Power) Let
L\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
of order

r
and degree
d
. Then, there exists an operator

M\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
such that for all solution
\alpha

of
L
, we have
M(\alpha^{\ell})=0
and
M
has order at most

\rho=\binom{\ell+r-1}{\ell}
and degree at most
\rho^{2}\frac{\ell d}{\ell+r-1}
.

2.

(Associate) Let
L\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
of order
r

and degree
d
and another operator
A\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>

of order
<r
and degree
d_{A}
. Then, there exists an operator

M\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
such that for all solution
\alpha

of
L
, we have
M\cdot A(\alpha)=0
and
M
has order at most

r
and degree at most
r(d_{A}+d)
.

3.

(Wronskian) Let
L_{1},\dots,L_{r}\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
all
of order
r
and degree
d
. Then, there exists an operator

M\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
such that for all

\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}
such that

L_{1}(\alpha_{1})=\cdots=L_{r}(\alpha_{r})=0
, we have

M(w(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}))=0
and
M
has order at most

r^{r}
and degree at most
r^{2r}d
.

Proof.

Part 1 is Theorem
6
with
J=y_{1,0}^{\ell}
,
thus
s=1
,
\Deg(J)=\ell
and
\deg_{x}(J)=0
. Similarly, in Part 2,
if
A=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}a_{j}(x)\partial_{x}^{j}
we have

J=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}a_{j}y_{1,j}
, thus
s=1
,
\Deg(J)=1
and

\deg_{x}(J)=d_{A}
and the result follows. Finally, in Part 3, we
have

, thus

,

\Deg(J)=(1,\dots,1)
and
\deg_{x}(J)=0
and the result again

.
∎

Each part of Corollary
1
benefits from the
refined bound in Theorem
6
to
improve the previously best known bound (cf.
[
26
]
).

7
Differential equation for algebraic function

Let
P(x,y)\in k[x,y]
be a nonzero bivariate polynomial.
All the roots
\alpha(x)
of
P
are D-finite
[
1
]
and
we address the problem of
computing a nonzero (minimal order) differential operator annihilating all these roots.
Cockle’s
algorithm
[
16
,
10
]

computes such an operator as a linear relation between the
images of
\alpha^{(i)}
modulo
P
. In fact, this algorithm solves
an instance
of Problem
1

[
10
, Sec. 2.1]
.
Here we explicit this particular instance, and then derive the following
degree bounds.

Theorem 7
.

Let
P\in k[x,y]
be square-free with respect to
y
,

(d,r)=(\deg_{x}(P),\deg_{y}(P))
. Then, there exists a differential
resolvent of
P
of order
\rho\leq r
and degree at most

\rho(2r-1)d
. Moreover, for all
m\geq\rho
, there exists a
nonzero operator of order
m
annihilating all the roots of
P
of
degree at most
m/(m-\rho+1)\cdot(2r-1)d
.

For a differential resolvent, our bound is in
O(r^{2}d)
and governed by

2r^{2}d
. The previous best known bound behaves like

4r^{2}d

[
10
, Thm. 1]
and its proof is
ad hoc
,
namely, not derived from the analysis of the algorithm.
So this is an improvement of the
constant from 4 to 2.

Experimentally, if we assume that
r=d
, we observe that the output
degree is bounded by
d(2d^{2}-3d+3)
. Our bound gives
d(2d^{2}-d)

and the previous bound is

d(4d^{2}-11d/2+7/2)

[
10
]
. This is the
first time that a bound with the first term
2d^{3}
is obtained.

Let
P(x,y)\in k[x,y]\setminus\{0\}
be square-free with respect to

y
and let
d
be its degree in
x
and
r
in
y
. We write

P_{x}=\partial_{x}(P)
and
P_{y}=\partial_{y}(P)
. In this section,
\mathcal{A}

denotes the vector space
k(x)[y]_{<r}
.

7.1
Reduction to Problem
1

Let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}
be the roots of
P
in an algebraic
closure of
k(x)
. For any root
\alpha
of
P
, by differentiating

P(x,\alpha)=0
and since
P
is square-free, we have

\partial_{x}(\alpha)=-P_{x}(x,\alpha)/P_{y}(x,\alpha)
. Hence the
derivation
\partial_{x}
uniquely extends to

k(x)(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
. By induction, for all
i\geq 1
,
there exists a polynomial
C_{i}\in k[x,y]
such that

\alpha^{(i)}=C_{i}(x,\alpha)/P_{y}(x,\alpha)^{2i-1}
, for any root

\alpha
of
P
. Since
P
and
P_{y}
are coprime, one can define

D_{i}(x,y)
to be the polynomial in
\mathcal{A}

such that
D_{i}(x,\alpha)=\alpha^{(i)}
for all roots
\alpha
of

P
. Note that
D_{0}=y
. And thus it suffices to find the minimal
linear dependence between the
D_{i}
’s to compute the differential
resolvent of
P
.

Moreover, by differentiating
\alpha^{(i)}=D_{i}(x,\alpha)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha^{(i+1)}

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}(D_{i}(x,\alpha))+\partial_{x}(\alpha)\cdot\partial_{y}(D_{i}(x,\alpha))

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}(D_{i}(x,\alpha))-\frac{P_{x}(x,\alpha)}{P_{y}(x,\alpha)}\partial_{y}(D_{i}(x,\alpha)).

Therefore
D_{i+1}=(\partial_{x}+T)(D_{i})
, where
T
is the

k(x)
-linear map

T\colon a\mapsto-\partial_{y}(a)P_{x}/P_{y}~\mathrm{mod}~P
on

\mathcal{A}
. Finally, the differential resolvent of
P
is the
solution of Problem
1
for
(T,y)
.

7.2
McMillan degree

Fix the monomial basis
(1,y,\dots,y^{r-1})
of
\mathcal{A}
and view

T
as a matrix in
k(x)^{r\times r}
.

Lemma 4
.

There exist matrices

X\in k^{r\times(2r-1)},M\in k[x]^{(2r-1)\times(2r-1)}
and

Y\in k[x]^{(2r-1)\times r}
, with
M
invertible,
\deg M\leq d

and
\deg Y\leq d-1
, such that
T=XM^{-1}Y
.

Proof.

For
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
Ta
is the solution
V
of the following
Bézout equation

\displaystyle-\partial_{y}(a)P_{x}=U\cdot P+V\cdot P_{y}.

So if

Y\colon a\in\mathcal{A}\mapsto-\partial_{y}(a)P_{x}\in k(x)[y]_{<2r-1}
and
X
is the projection
(U,V)\mapsto V
onto the last

r
coordinates, we have
T=XM^{-1}Y
where
M
is the
Sylvester map associated to
P
and
P_{y}
which is invertible since

P
is square-free, and
X,M,Y
satisfy the required property.
∎

By Proposition
5
, let
\mu
be such
that
T_{\mu}
is proper and
\degMM(T_{\mu})=\degMM(T)
and denote by

u
the denominator of
\mu
. Then,
T_{\mu}=XM_{\mu}^{-1}Y_{\mu}
and

M_{\mu}=1/u^{d}\cdot M^{*}
,
Y_{\mu}=1/u^{d-1}\cdot Y^{*}
, where

M^{*},Y^{*}
are both polynomial matrices with
\deg M^{*}\leq d
and

\deg Y^{*}\leq d-1
. So,
T_{\mu}=X(M^{*})^{-1}\cdot uY^{*}
. By (
7
) and
Proposition
3
, we get

\displaystyle\degMM(T)\leq\deg(\det M^{*})\leq(2r-1)d.

(17)

Finally, Theorem
7

follows from Theorem
1

and (
17
), with
T
and

a=(0,1,0,\dots,0)^{t}\in k[x]^{r}
since
D_{0}=y
.

8
Composition of algebraic and D-finite functions

It is known that for any algebraic function
g
and D-finite function

f
, the composition
h=f\circ g
is again
D-finite
[
37
]
. The case of the differential
resolvent is the particular case where

. We apply the
same approach as in Section
7
by extending
Cockle’s algorithm in order to derive degree bounds. We compare our
results with the previous known
bounds
[
28
]
.

Let
P(x,y)\in k[x,y]
be a nonzero bivariate polynomial which is
square-free with respect to
y
, and let
d_{P}
be its degree in
x

and
r_{P}
in
y
. Again,
P_{x}
(resp.
P_{y}
) denotes the
partial derivative of
P
with respect to
x
(resp.
y
). Let

L\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
be a nonzero differential operator of
degree
d_{L}
and order
r_{L}
. Let
l(x)\in k[x]
be the leading
coefficient of
L
, we require that
\gcd(P(x,y),l(y))=1
. This is
the case if
P
is assumed to
have no divisor in
\overline{k}(y)
where
\overline{k}
is an algebraic closure
of
k

[
28
]
.
We want a nonzero operator
M
annihilating all the functions

h=f\circ g
where
L(f)=0
and
g
is a root of
P
.
Again, the method consists in computing a relation between the
successive derivatives of a generic solution
h=f\circ g
modulo
the relations
P(x,g)=0
and
(L(f))(g(x))=0
.
This enables to view the problem as an instance of
Problem
1
and prove
the following degree bound.

Theorem 8
.

Let
P\in k[x,y]
be square-free with respect to
y
,

(d_{P},r_{P})=(\deg_{x}(P),\deg_{y}(P))
and

L\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>
with leading coefficient
l\in k[x]

such that
l(y)
is coprime with
P(x,y)
, and let

(d_{L},r_{L})=(\deg_{x}(L),\deg_{\partial_{x}}(L))
. Then, there exists

M\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>\setminus\{0\}
annihilating all the
power series
h=f\circ g
where
L(f)=0
and

P(x,g(x))=0
, of order
\rho\leq r_{P}r_{L}
and degree at most

\rho d_{P}(r_{L}(2r_{P}-1)+d_{L})
. Also, for all
m\geq\rho
there
exists an operator of order
m
annihilating all these power series
of degree at most
m/(m-\rho+1)d_{P}(r_{L}(2r_{P}-1)+d_{L})
.

For minimal annihilators, our degree bound is in

O(r_{P}r_{L}d_{P}(r_{L}r_{P}+d_{L}))
which is an improvement of the
previous bound in

O(r_{P}r_{L}d_{P}(r_{L}r_{P}+r_{L}d_{L}))

[
28
, Thm. 8]
. Also the leading
term of our bound (
d_{P}r_{L}r_{P}(2r_{L}r_{P}+d_{L})
) coincides with the one
conjectured from
experiments
[
28
, Conj. 10]
.

8.1
Reduction to Problem
1

As in Section
7.1
, we have

g^{\prime}=-P_{x}(x,g)/P_{y}(x,g)
for any root
g
of
P
. Thus,

\displaystyle h^{\prime}=(f\circ g)=g^{\prime}\cdot(f^{\prime}\circ g)=-\frac{P_{x}(x,g)}{P_{y}(x,g)}f^{\prime}\circ g.

By induction on
\ell\geq 0
, using
g^{\prime}=-P_{x}(x,g)/P_{y}(x,g)
and

(L(f))(g(x))=0
, it follows that

\displaystyle h^{(\ell)}=(f\circ g)^{(\ell)}=\frac{1}{U(x,g)^{\ell}}\sum_{j=0}^{r_{L}-1}E_{i,j}(x,g)(f^{(j)}\circ g),

where

U(x,y)=P_{y}^{2}(x,y)l(y)

[
28
, Lem. 4]
.
Note that
U(x,g)\neq 0
since
P
and
l(y)
are coprime and
P
is square-free. So, one
can define

\displaystyle V_{\ell}(x,y,\partial)=\sum_{i=0}^{r_{L}-1}\sum_{j=0}^{r_{P}-1}e_{i,j}(x)y^{j}\partial^{i},

with
e_{i,j}(x)\in k(x)
such that

h^{(\ell)}(x)=\left(V_{\ell}(x,g(x),\partial)(f)\right)\left(g(x)\right)
. We somehow
reduce the computation to linear algebra in the
k(x)
-vector space

\mathcal{A}=k(x)[y]_{<r_{P}}[\partial]_{<r_{L}}
. According to this
decomposition, we have
V_{0}=1
and differentiating

\displaystyle h^{(\ell)}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{r_{L}-1}\sum_{j=0}^{r_{P}-1}e_{i,j}(x)g(x)^{j}f^{(i)}(g(x)),

we obtain

\displaystyle h^{(\ell+1)}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{r_{L}-1}\sum_{j=0}^{r_{P}-1}\left(e^{\prime}_{i,j}(x)g(x)^{j}f^{(i)}(g(x))+e_{i,j}(x)g^{\prime}\left(jg^{j-1}(x)f^{(i)}(g(x))+g^{j}(x)f^{(i+1)}(g(x))\right)\right).

Thus, it follows that

\displaystyle V_{\ell+1}=\partial_{x}V_{\ell}+T\cdot V_{\ell}=\theta V_{\ell},

with

-linear map on

\displaystyle T\colon a\in\mathcal{A}\mapsto-\frac{P_{x}}{P_{y}}\left(\partial_{y}(a)+a\cdot\partial\right)~\text{ mod }\left<L(y,\partial),P(x,y)\right>.

Finally, one can compute the operator

For all

One can prove the following results for matrices of multiplication.

Lemma 5
.

Let
M_{y}\in k(x)^{r_{P}\times r_{P}}
be the matrix of multiplication
by

modulo

, i.e.

.
For all

\displaystyle M_{y^{k}}=(M_{y})^{k}=V_{k}^{(\ell)}D_{\ell}^{-1}U_{\ell}.

The proof is based on a construction to build a realisation of a product

By induction on

Let

9
Hermite reduction for creative telescoping

Let
f=p/q
be in
k(x,y)\setminus\{0\}
with
p
and
q

coprime in
k[x,y]
. For simplicity, we assume that
q
is
square-free with respect to
y
,
\deg_{y}p<\deg_{y}q=r
and

\deg_{x}p\leq\deg_{x}q=d
. We also denote
q_{y}=\partial_{y}(q)
and

q_{x}=\partial_{x}(q)
. A telescoper

L\in k[x]\left<\partial_{x}\right>\setminus\{0\}
for
f
(i.e.
, such
that
L(f)=\partial_{y}(h)
for
h\in k(x,y)
), satisfies the following
degree bound.

Theorem 9
.

Let
f(x,y)=p/q\in k(x,y)
with
q
square-free with respect to

y
,
(d,r)=(\deg_{x}(q),\deg_{y}(q))
and
\deg_{x}p\leq d
,

\deg_{y}p<r
. Then there exists a minimal telescoper for
f
of
order
\rho\leq r
and degree at most
\rho(d+2rd)
. Also, for
all
m\geq\rho
there exists a non-minimal telescoper for
f
of
order
m
and degree at most
1/(m-\rho+1)\cdot(\rho d+2mrd)
.

We retrieve the best known bound on the degree of a minimal order telescoper
for a bivariate rational
function
[
8
, Thm. 25]
, namely a degree
governed by
2d_{y}^{2}d_{x}
. This bound was derived from the analysis of
the Almkvist-Zeilberger algorithm for rational
functions
[
3
]
.
In the rest of the section, we investigate the
Hermite
reduction-based algorithm
[
8
]
for computing a telescoper
and prove that it can be seen as an instance of Problem
1
.
Finally, Theorem
9

is proved as a consequence of Theorem
1
.
This is the first time a degree
bound in
O(d_{y}^{2}d_{x})
is derived directly from the Hermite
reduction-based algorithm (see
[
8
, Lem. 18]
).

9.1
Reduction to Problem
1

For any rational function
g\in k(x,y)
whose denominator is a power
of
q
, we denote by
\herm(g)
its Hermite
reduction
[
21
]
, namely the unique polynomial

such that
\deg_{y}(r)<\deg_{y}(q)
and

for
h\in k(x,y)
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the
vector space
k(x)[y]_{<r}
. It is known that
\herm
defines a

k(x)
-linear map from
k(x)[y,q^{-1}]
to
\mathcal{A}
and

\herm(g)=0
if and only
g
is a derivative (wrt.
y
). Moreover,
one can show
[
8
]
that

\herm(\partial_{x}(g))=(\partial_{x}+T)(\herm(g))
where
T
is the

k(x)
-linear map:

\displaystyle T\colon a\in\mathcal{A}\mapsto-\herm(q_{x}a/q^{2})\in\mathcal{A}.

A telescoper
L
for
f
is such that
\herm(L(f))=0
and thus
by linearity of
\herm
it can be seen as a linear relation between

\herm(\partial_{x}^{i}(f))
for

. Besides, by induction on

, we obtain
\herm(\partial_{x}^{i}(f))=\theta^{i}(p)
where

is the numerator of

and
\theta
the pseudo-linear
map
\partial_{x}+T
. Therefore, a minimal telescoper for
f
can be
obtained as the solution of Problem
1
on
input
(T,p)
. This is essentially the algorithm
in
[
8
, Sec. 3.1]
.

10
Conclusion

Theorem
1
provides a unified approach
to establish tight
degree bounds in output of several algorithmic problems
for D-finite functions and P-recursive sequences.
As future work, we aim at exploiting the structure we enlightened in this work
for the design of new efficient algorithms. We notably intend to propose algorithms
solving Problem
1
whose runtime is sensitive to the
size of the operator we compute.
We seek for a way of taking advantage of a
small
realisation of the matrix
T

for solving pseudo-Krylov systems while relying on recent breakthroughs of polynomial
linear algebra.
