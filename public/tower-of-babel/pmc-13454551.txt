Sudden Respiratory Impairment due to Simultaneous Airway Obstruction and Tension Pneumothorax in a Patient Who Underwent Tracheostomy
Authors: Yuko Ono, Kazuaki Shinohara, Nobuto Nakanishi, Kazushi Takayama, Yutaka Kondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAn 86‐year‐old woman with a tracheostomy developed airway obstruction caused by viscid sputum. Forceful manual ventilation through her obstructed airway precipitated tension pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, pneumopericardium, pneumoperitoneum, and pneumoretroperitoneum. This case suggests that airway obstruction can induce secondary tension pneumothorax, further worsening respiratory function.

The patient was an 86‐year‐old Japanese woman who had been diagnosed with papillary thyroid cancer approximately 40 years earlier. She underwent left and right thyroid lobectomies 35 and 27 years ago, respectively. She also underwent a tracheostomy approximately 1 year prior to presentation to address recurrent laryngeal nerve paralysis secondary to cervical lymph node metastasis. She had never smoked and had no known pulmonary cysts or emphysema.

Two days after developing a lower respiratory tract infection, the patient experienced sudden respiratory distress. She was brought to our emergency department (ED) by ambulance at midnight. The details of resuscitation team and her initial vital signs were included in the Supporting Information. On arrival, she exhibited chest retraction, labored breathing, and cyanosis.

A previously inserted speech cannula (internal diameter: 8.0 mm; #3404, Koken, Tokyo, Japan) was replaced with an endotracheal tube (ETT; internal diameter: 7.0 mm). Marked resistance was observed during manual bag ventilation. Inspiration was only possible with high‐pressure manual bag ventilation, rendering forceful manual ventilation unavoidable. A suction catheter could not be advanced through the ETT, raising suspicion of obstruction. When the ETT was replaced, the removed tube was occluded with viscid sputum (Figure 1).

Endotracheal tube completely obstructed by viscous sputum (arrowhead).

Manual ventilation remained difficult despite tube replacement. Bag ventilation resistance was extremely high, requiring the attending ED physician to apply forceful ventilation. The patient gradually became hypotensive and bradycardic (heart rate, 60 beats/min; blood pressure, unmeasurable; femoral pulse, faintly palpable; radial pulse, absent; percutaneous oxygen saturation, < 80%). Although the ED physician suspected ETT misplacement, end‐tidal carbon dioxide was detectable. Another ED physician responding to the emergency call noted diminished breath sounds over the left hemithorax, and jugular vein distension.

A left thoracic tube was promptly inserted for suspected tension pneumothorax associated with airway obstruction. Air immediately escaped from the tube. Ventilation of the left lung subsequently improved, and manual bag resistance decreased. Chest radiography confirmed correct tube placement and revealed pneumomediastinum, pneumopericardium, and subcutaneous emphysema (Figure 2). Computed tomography revealed bilateral pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, pneumopericardium, pneumoperitoneum, pneumoretroperitoneum, and massive subcutaneous emphysema (Figure 3 and Video S1). A right thoracic tube was subsequently inserted.

Computed tomography images. (A) Whole‐body coronal section showing marked subcutaneous emphysema, pneumomediastinum, pneumopericardium, and pneumoperitoneum. (B) Axial chest view showing right pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, pneumopericardium, and subcutaneous emphysema. (C) Axial upper abdominal view showing pneumoperitoneum, pneumoretroperitoneum, and subcutaneous emphysema.

Pneumomediastinum, pneumopericardium, pneumoperitoneum, and pneumoretroperitoneum resolved spontaneously with conservative management. The patient was weaned from mechanical ventilation 5 days after onset of pneumothorax and transferred to a rehabilitation hospital without sequelae following removal of both thoracic tubes.

Sudden respiratory impairment in patients with tracheostomy is a medical emergency. The mnemonic “DOPE” (Displacement, Obstruction of the ETT, Pneumothorax, and Equipment failure) is useful for differential diagnosis in intubated patients. The possible pathophysiological mechanisms of pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, pneumopericardium, pneumoperitoneum, pneumoretroperitoneum observed in this case are included in the Supporting Information. This case highlights that the “O” (obstruction) and “P” (pneumothorax) components of the DOPE mnemonic can coexist. From a patient‐safety perspective, early recognition of tube obstruction and pneumothorax is essential to prevent catastrophic outcomes [1, 2].

ETT obstruction may induce a secondary tension pneumothorax. Persistent resistance during manual ventilation, even after resolving the obstruction, should prompt clinicians to consider pneumothorax as a diagnosis.
