Daptomycin is associated with higher treatment failure rates than alternatives for Enterococcus faecium bloodstream infections in critically ill patients: a multicentric retrospective cohort

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnterococcal infections represent 10% of intensive care unit (ICU)-acquired infections and are associated with adverse outcomes, particularly in the case of E. faecium infections. Some studies focusing on non-critically ill patients have cast doubt on the non-inferiority of daptomycin compared to other antibiotics for these infections. We aimed to describe antibiotic treatment practices for monomicrobial E. faecium bloodstream infections (BSI) in patients admitted to the ICU, and to identify factors associated with treatment failure.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter study included adult patients presenting with a monomicrobial E. faecium BSI during their stay in one of 11 ICUs of Paris University hospitals between 2017 and 2022. Patients receiving daptomycin as a definitive antibiotic regimen were compared to those receiving another molecule using competing-risks models and propensity score-based analyses. The primary outcome was a composite criterion of treatment failure including: (1) prolonged bacteremia (≥3days) under treatment and/or (2) relapse within 30 days after the end of treatment and/or (3) the need for salvage therapy within 30 days.ResultsOf the 166 included patients, 26 received daptomycin as a definitive antibiotic regimen, at a median dose of 10 [10-10] mg/kg/day and 140 patients received non-daptomycin-based regimens (vancomycin (69%), linezolid (19%) or a beta-lactam (11%)). Source of BSI was predominantly unknown (38%), digestive (37%) or an intravascular device (16%). All isolates were vancomycin-susceptible. Median daptomycin MIC was 3 [2-3.25] mg/L. Time to adequate antibiotic regimen (1 [0-2] vs 1 [0-2] days, p=0.22) and rates of source control (63% vs 43%, p=0.17) were not different between groups. Regarding primary outcome, daptomycin as a definitive antibiotic regimen was associated with a higher rate of treatment failure in the multivariate adjusted analysis using a Fine and Gray model (aSHR 2.53 [1.14-5.62], p=0.022). Sensitivity analyses using propensity score overlap weighting (HR 2.48 [1.05-5.86], p=0.038) or with only patients treated by vancomycin as comparators (aSHR 2.26 [1.00-5.10], p=0.051) yielded similar results.ConclusionsIn this multicenter retrospective cohort of critically ill patients with monomicrobial E. faecium bloodstream infections, use of daptomycin as the definitive antibiotic regimen was associated with a higher rate of treatment failure. Background

Enterococcus species are an emerging and increasingly important cause of healthcare-associated infections. They have been reported as the second most common pathogens in healthcare-associated infections [1] accounting for 10% of intensive care unit (ICU)-acquired infections and associated with an ICU mortality rate of approximately 30% [2]. Common infection sites include primary bloodstream infections (BSI), intra-abdominal and urinary tract infections [2, 3]. Documentation of Enterococcus species has been associated with poor outcome [4–8], particularly in cases of E. faecium infection, known for its high rate of beta-lactam resistance. Risk factors for E. faecium over E. faecalis include the healthcare associated nature of the infection, illness severity, prior antibiotic exposure and comorbidities such as cancer or cirrhosis [4, 6–12]. Consequently, critically ill ICU patients are at heightened risk of beta-lactam resistant E. faecium infections.

Ease-of-use, safety profile and patent expiration of glycopeptide alternatives for beta-lactam resistant gram positive pathogens, such as linezolid and daptomycin, have led to an increase in their use [13]. Expert guidelines, based on small-scale non-randomized clinical studies, support their use for enterococcal infections when first-line agents are contraindicated or ineffective [14, 15]. But in contrast to staphylococcal infections, evidence is scarce regarding daptomycin use for enterococcal infections, with EUCAST refraining from proposing minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) break-points for daptomycin and Enterococcus species [16]. Three retrospective studies, which included few critically ill patients, suggest daptomycin may be associated with higher rates of clinical failure when compared to glycopeptides in E. faecium BSI [17–19]. Currently, no high-quality data or strong recommendations guide the management of enterococcal infections in critically ill patients. This study aimed to characterize antibiotic treatment practices for monomicrobial E. faecium BSI affecting ICU patients and to identify factors associated with treatment failure.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective multicenter study included all adult patients diagnosed with monomicrobial E. faecium BSI during their stay in one of 11 ICUs of APHP (Greater Paris University Hospitals) between 2017 and 2022. Patients who received either daptomycin or an alternative agent as definitive antibiotic regimen (i.e. after antibiotic susceptibility testing for E. faecium isolate was available) were compared. The primary outcome was a composite criterion of treatment failure defined as one or more of the following: (1) prolonged bacteremia (≥3days) under treatment and/or (2) relapse within 30 days after the end of treatment and/or (3) the need for salvage therapy within 30 days.

Data were extracted from medical charts. Collected variables included: demographics (age, gender, weight), comorbidities (diabetes, immunosuppression (i.e. active cancer or solid organ transplant, or cirrhosis or immunosuppressive drugs), chronic kidney disease, cardio-vascular comorbidities (i.e. obliterating arteritis of the lower limb or ischemic heart disease or former ischemic stroke), reason for ICU admission, E. faecium BSI characteristics (healthcare-associated or postoperative nature, severity as assessed by Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Pitt bacteremia score on the day of BSI as well as organ support within 24 h of BSI onset, source of infection, antibiotic susceptibility testing of E. faecium isolate), management (antibiotic treatments received before BSI onset, time to empiric antibiotic regimen for BSI and its adequacy, source control if applicable, definitive antibiotic regimen choice and duration (for patients treated with daptomycin, daptomycin MIC and dose were also collected) and outcome (rates and time to microbiological eradication defined as first sterile blood culture after BSI onset, E. faecium BSI relapse within 30-days after the end of treatment, need for salvage antibiotics defined as a new antibiotic regimen for E. faecium BSI treatment failure or relapse, in-ICU and in-hospital mortality).

All patients were informed that data extracted from their medical charts could be used for research purposes. The study was approved by an institutional review board (Comité d’Ethique de la Société de Réanimation de Langue Française, (IRB00014135, project n° 23–082).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described as median and interquartile range [IQR] and compared using Wilcoxon’s rank sum test or Kruskall-Wallis’ test. Categorical variables were described as counts (percent) and compared using Chi2 of Fisher’s exact test. A competing risk analysis using a Fine and Gray model was performed to assess the cumulative risk of treatment failure and depict it, with death as a competing event. Patients were administratively censored at 30 days after treatment initiation. To assess the adjusted effect of antibiotic regimen on outcome, a multivariate Fine and Gray model or a Cause specific cause model were used, adjusting on the following predefined confounding factors identified through literature review: SAPSII, immunocompromised status, infection source. Correlation and interaction were carefully checked. The proportional assumption was checked by plotting the Schoenfeld residuals against failure time and by including a time dependent covariate in the regression model. Data were presented as cause-specific hazard ratios (HR; 95%CI), or adjusted sub distribution-hazard ratios (asHR; 95%CI) according to the used model.

To explore potential causal relationships between antibiotic regimen and treatment failure, a propensity score analysis using overlap weighting was performed. The propensity score was derived from a logistic regression model including baseline covariates associated with both treatment allocation and outcome: SAPSII score, Pitt bacteremia score, presence of a foreign material, immunocompromised status, age, chronic kidney disease and infection source. The balance between groups before and after weighting was evaluated using standardized mean differences. Outcome analysis was then performed using a cause-specific Cox model with a robust variance estimator, and cumulative incidence functions were plotted. To address the risk of immortal time bias, post hoc analyses evaluating the effect of daptomycin on outcomes were conducted using landmark and time-varying covariate models.

All tests were two-sided and p-values lower than 5% were considered to indicate significant associations. Analyses were performed using R statistical platform, version 3.0.2 (https://cran.r-project.org/).

Outcome according to definitive antibiotic regimen

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort of critically ill patients with monomicrobial E. faecium BSI, use of daptomycin as a definitive regimen was associated with higher rates of treatment failure.

We focused on daptomycin because prior studies had raised doubts on its non-inferiority compared to glycopeptides [20, 21]. In our cohort, comparators included glycopeptides, linezolid, and beta-lactams but our sensitivity analysis restricted to vancomycin-treated patients only, while not statistically significant, demonstrated a similar trend, reinforcing the primary finding. Moreover, two retrospective studies focusing on vancomycin resistant enterococcal BSI found that daptomycin was associated with an improved survival when compared to regimens mainly based on linezolid or tigecycline [22, 23]. This suggests that including comparators other than glycopeptides should have favored the daptomycin group.

The probability of target attainment and clinical success for vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus infections treated with daptomycin have been reported as low for high MICs (i.e. ≥ 4 mg/L) and/or low dosing regimens (i.e. <12 mg/kg/day) [24]. In a recent study by Rinaldi et al. daptomycin MIC did not appear to influence outcomes, but doses <10 mg/kg/day were associated with worse prognosis in univariate analysis [19]. In our study, sample size limitations precluded definitive analysis of MIC or dosing effects. However, we observed no treatment failures among the four patients with the highest MICs (4 mg/L), and no clear association between dose (< or ≥10 mg/kg/day) and outcome was evident. Further studies with a larger sample and systematical daptomycin MIC assessment are needed to explore issues regarding MIC and dosage influence.

The population examined in this study—critically ill patients with monomicrobial E. faecium BSI—is a rare and particularly severe subgroup, often admitted to the ICU for reasons unrelated to the infection itself. Nonetheless, this setting provides an opportunity to evaluate the impact of antibiotic practices on outcomes. Whether these findings can be extrapolated to a broader population of critically ill patients with enterococcal infections, —including polymicrobial BSIs or infections of different sources— remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

This study has several limitations, primarily related to its retrospective design and relatively limited sample size. First, neither the choice of definitive antibiotic regimen (including drug selection and dosing), source control strategies, nor follow-up blood cultures timing were standardized, leading to substantial heterogeneity in clinical practice. Some patients experienced cross-overs between daptomycin and other molecules during the transition from empirical to definitive regimen. Although this might have been expected to homogenize outcomes, we still found a difference between groups. Time to adequate antibiotic regimen was only collected in days, time to source control or therapeutic drug monitoring were not available and data on treatment duration was largely missing. As a result, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Finally, due to the observational nature of the study, causal inference cannot be established, and findings related to clinical outcomes should be interpreted with caution. However, our results are consistent with recent reports questioning the efficacy of daptomycin in this setting [19, 20] and underscore the need for prospective, adequately powered studies to guide optimal treatment of severe E. faecium infections in ICU populations.

Conclusions

In this multicenter retrospective cohort of critically ill patients with monomicrobial E. faecium BSI, definitive treatment with daptomycin was associated with a higher rate of treatment failure. Due to the risk of residual confounders associated with the retrospective design, further prospective, adequately powered studies are needed to inform treatment of severe E. faecium infections in ICU populations.
