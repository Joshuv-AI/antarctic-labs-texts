Role of BTLA in ovarian cancer and its clinical prognostic significance based on multi-omics analysis
Authors: Yiliminuer Abulajiang, Yumei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOvarian cancer (OV) is a highly lethal gynecological malignancy with limited effective biomarkers for prognosis and precision immunotherapy. B and T lymphocyte attenuator (BTLA) is an immune checkpoint molecule involved in tumor immune regulation, but its role in OV remains unclear.MethodsTranscriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were analyzed to evaluate BTLA expression and its potential clinical relevance in OV. Exploratory multivariable Cox regression analysis was performed to examine the association between BTLA expression and patient prognosis. Functional characteristics were investigated through gene set enrichment and variation analyses, protein–protein interaction analysis, and regulatory network construction. Single-cell RNA sequencing (ScRNA-seq) was applied to explore cellular heterogeneity and BTLA-related cell–cell communication. Drug sensitivity analyses and molecular docking were conducted to assess therapeutic relevance. Experimental assays were performed to validate BTLA expression.ResultsBTLA was significantly upregulated in OV tissues compared with normal controls. Higher BTLA expression was associated with patient prognosis in exploratory Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier (KM) analyses. Functional enrichment revealed associations with immune- and tumor-related pathways including epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT), interleukin-10 signaling, and interferon responses. Single-cell analysis demonstrated BTLA-related transcriptional heterogeneity across cell populations, with epithelial cells showing the highest activity. Drug sensitivity profiling identified differential responses between BTLA expression groups, and molecular docking suggested moderate binding affinity between BTLA and genistein (Vina score = − 6.3 kcal/mol). Experimental validation confirmed increased BTLA expression at both mRNA and protein levels in OV tissues.ConclusionThese findings suggest that BTLA may be involved in the tumor immune microenvironment of OV and may provide preliminary clues as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13048-026-02231-6.

Introduction

OV remains one of the most lethal malignancies within the female reproductive system, characterized by its insidious onset, aggressive progression, and poor clinical outcomes. Despite advances in diagnostic imaging and serum tumor biomarkers, early detection remains challenging, often resulting in diagnosis at advanced stages when therapeutic options become limited and prognosis worsens [1, 2]. The disease burden imposed by OV is considerable, with high morbidity and mortality rates contributing substantially to healthcare costs and adversely impacting patients’ quality of life worldwide [3]. Current standard treatments, primarily consisting of cytoreductive surgery combined with platinum-based chemotherapy, have improved survival to some extent; however, the overall five-year survival rate remains unsatisfactory, especially for high-grade and late-stage tumors [2, 4].

In recent years, molecular targeted therapies and immunotherapies have emerged as promising adjuncts to conventional treatment modalities. Immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting pathways such as programmed death receptor 1(PD-1)/programmed death ligand 1(PD-L1), and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4(CTLA-4) have demonstrated efficacy in various solid tumors but have shown limited success in OV due to its complex tumor microenvironment and immune evasion mechanisms [5, 6]. The heterogeneity of OV, both at the molecular and cellular levels, poses significant challenges for the development of effective, personalized immunotherapeutic strategies. Consequently, there remains an urgent need to identify novel biomarkers and immune regulatory molecules that could serve as therapeutic targets or prognostic indicators to enhance precision medicine approaches in this malignancy [7].

BTLA has recently attracted attention as a critical immune checkpoint receptor modulating lymphocyte activation and tolerance. Belonging to the immunoglobulin superfamily, BTLA interacts with its ligand, the herpesvirus entry mediator, to deliver inhibitory signals that attenuate immune responses [4, 8]. While BTLA’s immunosuppressive role has been well documented in various immune-related diseases and hematologic malignancies, its function within the context of solid tumors, particularly OV, remains inadequately explored. Previous studies have implicated aberrant immune checkpoint expression in the ovarian tumor microenvironment as a key factor facilitating immune escape and tumor progression, yet the specific contribution of BTLA and its molecular regulation in OV pathobiology warrants comprehensive investigation [9, 10].

Addressing this knowledge gap, the present study integrates multi-omics data and advanced bioinformatics approaches to systematically characterize BTLA expression patterns, prognostic relevance, and underlying molecular mechanisms in OV. Large-scale transcriptomic data from TCGA, combined with ScRNA-seq and multi-layered functional analyses, were employed to dissect BTLA-associated cellular heterogeneity, signaling pathways, and regulatory networks through Cox regression modeling, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), and protein interaction network construction. In addition, drug sensitivity profiling and molecular docking analyses were conducted to explore the therapeutic implications of BTLA. To substantiate the bioinformatic findings, quantitative real-time PCR(q-PCR) and Western blotting (WB) were performed to validate BTLA expression in clinical OV specimens relative to normal ovarian tissues. In this study, we innovatively integrated multi-omics data with single-cell sequencing technology to comprehensively dissect the role of BTLA in OV at the transcriptional, proteomic, and cellular levels, while exploring its feasibility as a potential therapeutic target. The findings are expected to provide a theoretical foundation for the stratified management of OV and the development of individualized BTLA-targeted immunotherapy strategies.

Materials and methods

Data download

We downloaded the OV dataset (TCGA-OV) from TCGA using the TCGA biolinks package [11], excluding samples lacking prognostic information. A total of 419 OV samples with clinical data were obtained. Clinical data were acquired from the UCSC Xena database [12]. The raw count sequencing data and corresponding FPKM-formatted data from TCGA-OV dataset were standardized using the limma package [13]. As the TCGA-OV dataset does not include control samples, we downloaded normal ovarian tissue samples from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database as controls [14], obtaining count-formatted sequencing data for 88 control samples. The count data from TCGA OV samples and GTEx control samples were integrated into a combined dataset (GTEx-TCGA-OV) and normalized to FPKM format.

BTLA expression and clinical prognostic analysis

BTLA expression was compared between OV and normal ovarian tissues in the GTEx-TCGA-OV dataset. For prognostic analyses, normal tissues were excluded, and patients in the TCGA-OV cohort were divided into BTLA-high and BTLA-low groups according to the median BTLA expression level. Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to evaluate the associations between BTLA expression, clinical variables, and patient prognosis. Variables with a P value < 0.1 were subsequently included in a multivariate Cox regression model. KM survival analysis was conducted using the R package survival (Version 3.5–7) to assess overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival between the two groups [15]. An exploratory nomogram was constructed based on the multivariate Cox regression model to predict the probability of survival. Calibration curves were generated to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed using the R package ggDCA to assess the potential clinical utility of the nomogram for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival in OV patients [16].

Functional enrichment and pathway analysis

Normal samples were excluded, and TCGA-OV patients were divided into BTLA-high and BTLA-low groups according to the median BTLA expression level. Differential expression analysis was performed using the R package limma. Genes with logFC > 2 and adjusted P < 0.05 were defined as upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs), whereas genes with logFC < − 2 and adjusted P < 0.05 were defined as downregulated DEGs. All genes were ranked by logFC, and the 15 most upregulated and 15 most downregulated genes were selected as BTLA-associated co-expressed genes.GSEA was performed using the R package clusterProfiler [17] and the c2.all.v2024.1.Hs.symbols.gmt gene set from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) [18]. Gene sets with Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05 and FDR q < 0.25 were considered significantly enriched. Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was conducted using the h.all.v7.4.symbols.gmt Hallmark gene set from MSigDB to compare pathway activities between the BTLA-high and BTLA-low groups [19]. Pathways with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.A protein–protein interaction network of BTLA and its co-expressed genes was constructed using the STRING database, with a minimum interaction score of 0.150 [20]. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler [21]. GO analysis included biological process, cellular component, and molecular function (MF) categories. Terms with P < 0.05 and FDR q < 0.25 were considered significantly enriched [22].

Co-expression network, mutation, and regulatory analysis

The mutational landscape of BTLA in the TCGA-OV cohort was analyzed using cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics [23]. MicroRNAs associated with BTLA-related genes were retrieved from the StarBase v3.0 database, and an mRNA–miRNA regulatory network was constructed [24]. Transcription factor (TF)–target relationships were obtained from the ChIPBase database and used to construct a TF–mRNA regulatory network. The TF–mRNA and mRNA–miRNA networks were subsequently integrated to generate a TF–mRNA–miRNA regulatory network, which was visualized using Cytoscape. RNA-binding protein (RBP)–target relationships involving BTLA-related genes were retrieved from the ENCORI database, and an RBP–mRNA regulatory network was constructed.

ScRNA-seq analysis

ScRNA-seq data were imported and processed using the Seurat R package (v5.0) [25]. Genes expressed in fewer than three cells and cells expressing fewer than 200 genes were initially excluded. Low-quality cells were further removed according to the following criteria: nCount_RNA < 1,000 or > 20,000, nFeature_RNA < 500, or mitochondrial gene proportion > 10%.The data were normalized using the LogNormalize method, and the top 2,500 highly variable genes were identified using the variance-stabilizing transformation method. After data scaling and principal component analysis, the first 12 principal components were selected for graph-based clustering at a resolution of 0.3 and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection visualization. Cell types were manually annotated according to established marker genes.Cluster-specific marker genes were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and the top 10 marker genes from each cluster were retained for downstream analyses. AUCell was used to evaluate the activity of BTLA and its associated gene set in individual cells and to identify cell populations with high BTLA-associated activity [26]. GO and KEGG analyses were subsequently performed on marker genes from the cell population with the highest AUCell scores. Terms with Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05 and false discovery rate q < 0.25 were considered significantly enriched.Intercellular communication was inferred using CellChat (v2.1.1) with the CellPhoneDB human ligand–receptor database [27]. The epithelial cell population was subsequently extracted and re-clustered using Seurat (v5.0.1). Epithelial subtypes were manually annotated according to canonical marker genes obtained from CellMarker 2.0.Finally, GSVA was performed using the h.all.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt Hallmark gene set from the MSigDB and the average expression profiles of epithelial subpopulations to compare pathway activities among epithelial cell subtypes.

Drug sensitivity analysis and molecular docking

Drug sensitivity in the TCGA-OV cohort was predicted using the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database [28]. In this study, we utilized the calcPhenotype algorithm in the R package oncoPredict to calculate the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values for TCGA-OV samples [29]. Predicted drug sensitivity was then compared between the BTLA-high and BTLA-low groups. BTLA-associated compounds were retrieved from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database. Genistein was selected as a candidate compound for molecular docking. Its three-dimensional structure was obtained from PubChem (CID: 5,280,961) [30], and the crystal structure of BTLA was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 8F60) [31]. Blind molecular docking between BTLA and Genistein was performed using AutoDock Vina integrated into the CB-Dock2 web server [32, 33]. The docking score was used to estimate the binding affinity between BTLA and Genistein.

Experimental validation

Normal ovarian tissues (N1–N3) and tumor tissues (S1–S3) obtained from surgical resection were compared by WB and q-PCR. BTLA (~ 124 kDa) and VINCULIN (~ 42 kDa, loading control) were detected using primary antibodies from Proteintech (BTLA: Cat# 29,233–1-AP, 1:1000; VINCULIN: Cat# 26,520–1-AP, 1:1000). For protein extraction, freshly resected tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in pre-cooled lysis buffer. Proteins were quantified by BCA assay, subjected to SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% milk or BSA, incubated with primary and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and detected using ECL substrate. For RNA extraction, tissues were homogenized in TRIzol with chloroform extraction and purified using column-based kits. q-PCR was performed with the following primers: BTLA-F: CATCTTAGCAGGAGATCCCTTTG;BTLA-R: GACCCATTGTCATTAGGAAGCA;GAPDH-F: GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG;GAPDH-R: ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA (GAPDH served as the internal reference).

Statistical analysis

All analyses used R software (v4.3.0). Continuous variables are shown as mean ± SD. Group comparisons employed Student's t-test (normal distribution) or Wilcoxon rank-sum test (non-normal), with Kruskal–Wallis test for ≥ 3 groups. Correlations used Spearman's method. Two-sided p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Study workflow

This study systematically investigated the expression, prognostic relevance, and potential biological functions of BTLA in OV. Bulk transcriptomic data were used to assess BTLA expression, its association with patient survival, and BTLA-related molecular pathways and regulatory networks. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis was subsequently performed to characterize BTLA-associated activity, cellular heterogeneity, and intercellular communication within the OV microenvironment. Drug sensitivity prediction and molecular docking were used to explore potential therapeutic associations. Finally, q-PCR and Western blotting were performed to validate BTLA expression in clinical ovarian tissue samples.

BTLA expression and clinical prognostic value

BTLA expression was significantly higher in OV tissues than in normal ovarian tissues in the integrated GTEx–TCGA-OV dataset (P < 0.001; Fig. 1A). In the exploratory KM analysis, patients with high BTLA expression exhibited improved OS compared with those with low BTLA expression (P < 0.0001; Fig. 1B). Comparisons of clinical characteristics further showed differences in survival status, age, stage, and tumor grade between the BTLA-high and BTLA-low groups, while the increased expression of BTLA in OV was consistently observed in an additional visualization of the integrated dataset (Fig. S1A–E). To further assess the prognostic relevance of BTLA, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed for BTLA expression and selected clinical variables, including age, grade, and stage. Variables with P < 0.1 were subsequently included in the multivariate Cox regression model. The univariate and multivariate analyses indicated that BTLA expression remained associated with patient prognosis after adjustment for the selected clinical variables (Fig. 1C–D).An exploratory nomogram incorporating BTLA expression and clinical characteristics was constructed from the multivariate Cox model. BTLA expression contributed substantially to the total prognostic score in this model (Fig. 1E). Calibration analysis showed that the predicted and observed outcomes were most closely aligned for 3-year survival, and DCA demonstrated a greater net benefit for the 3-year prediction than for the 1- and 5-year predictions (Fig. S2A–F). BTLA functional enrichment and pathway analysis

Differential expression analysis between the BTLA-high and BTLA-low groups identified a distinct set of BTLA-associated genes, with the most significantly upregulated and downregulated genes highlighted in the volcano plot (Fig. 2A). GSEA further demonstrated that the BTLA-high group was enriched in several immune- and tumor-related gene sets, including epithelial–mesenchymal transition, proinflammatory and profibrotic signaling, and interleukin-10 signaling (Fig. 2B; Fig. S3A–D). Consistent with the GSEA findings, GSVA revealed significant differences in multiple Hallmark pathways between the BTLA-high and BTLA-low groups. These pathways were mainly related to tumor progression, immune regulation, cellular metabolism, and stress responses, including Notch and TGF-β signaling, angiogenesis, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, hypoxia, interferon responses, oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis, and reactive oxygen species signaling (Fig. 2C; Fig. S3E). Protein–protein interaction analysis showed that 25 of the 30 BTLA-associated genes were interconnected within the network (Fig. 2D). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses further indicated that these genes were involved in diverse biological processes, cellular components, MFs, and signaling pathways relevant to OV (Fig. 2E). Owing to the low frequency of these alterations, their functional relevance remains uncertain. AUCell analysis revealed heterogeneous BTLA-associated gene-set activity across the identified cell populations, with epithelial cells exhibiting the highest activity scores (Fig. 4D–E). Functional enrichment analysis of epithelial cell marker genes further indicated their involvement in biological processes and signaling pathways relevant to OV (Fig. S4A). Cell–cell communication analysis demonstrated extensive interactions among the eight cell types, with endothelial cells showing relatively frequent and strong intercellular communication (Fig. S4B–C). Among the examined immune signaling interactions, the HLA-B–CD8A ligand–receptor pair showed the strongest predicted interaction within the CD8+T-cell autocrine network (Fig. S4D). To further characterize epithelial cell heterogeneity, epithelial cells were re-clustered into 13 subpopulations and subsequently classified into secretory and ciliated epithelial phenotypes according to canonical marker genes (Fig. S5A–D). GSVA further revealed substantial differences in Hallmark pathway activity between the secretory and ciliated epithelial cell populations, indicating functional heterogeneity among epithelial subtypes (Fig. S5J).

Drug sensitivity analysis and molecular docking

Drug sensitivity analysis using the GDSC database revealed that the BTLA-high and BTLA-low groups exhibited significantly different predicted drug responses to multiple targeted agents (Fig. 5A–E). Specifically, the BTLA-high group showed lower predicted IC50 values for Ruxolitinib (JAK inhibitor), GSK343 (EZH2 inhibitor), PLX-4720 (BRAF inhibitor), SB216763 (GSK-3 inhibitor), and AGI-6780 (IDH2 inhibitor), suggesting that tumors with higher BTLA expression may be more sensitive to these agents. To identify a potential compound directly associated with BTLA, BTLA-related drug–gene interactions were further examined. Genistein was selected as a candidate compound for molecular docking with the BTLA protein. The predicted binding pose demonstrated a moderate binding affinity, with a Vina score of − 6.3 kcal/mol (Fig. 5F–G). The interaction involved multiple BTLA residues (including TYR39, SER44, HIS46, and others) primarily through hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and cation–π interactions. These residues contributed to the predicted binding through hydrogen bonding, weak hydrogen bonding, cation–π interactions, and hydrophobic interactions. Collectively, these findings suggest a potential interaction between BTLA and Genistein, although further experimental validation is required.

Experimental validation confirms increased BTLA expression in OV tissues

To validate the bioinformatic findings, BTLA expression was examined in clinical tissue samples by q-PCR and WB. q-PCR analysis showed that BTLA mRNA expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues than in normal ovarian tissues (Fig. 6A). Consistently, WB analysis demonstrated increased BTLA protein expression in tumor samples relative to normal controls (Fig. 6B). Discussion

OV remains one of the most lethal malignancies affecting the female reproductive system, characterized by its insidious onset and high mortality rate. The disease often presents with vague symptoms, leading to late-stage diagnosis and aggressive tumor behavior that complicates effective management. Despite advances in surgical techniques and chemotherapy regimens, the overall five-year survival rate remains disappointingly low, underscoring the critical need for improved diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Current clinical approaches are hindered by limitations such as chemoresistance and the lack of reliable early detection biomarkers, which collectively contribute to poor patient outcomes and substantial healthcare burdens worldwide [34, 35].

Our differential expression analysis revealed a significant upregulation of BTLA in OV tissues compared to normal controls, corroborated by both transcriptional and protein-level validations. This elevation aligns with prior observations that BTLA expression is heightened in multiple malignancies, including OV, where it functions as an immune checkpoint modulating tumor immune escape [36, 37]. Mechanistically, BTLA engagement with its ligand HVEM initiates inhibitory signaling cascades that dampen T cell activation, fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [38]. Notably, our finding that BTLA overexpression associates with younger age, lower stage, but higher grade tumors suggests a complex role in early immunoediting and tumor aggressiveness, potentially reflecting differential immune pressure during tumor evolution. This heterogeneity in BTLA expression and its correlation with distinct clinical features extend previous observations linking BTLA to immune dysfunction in OV and other solid tumors [39, 40]. However, unlike several previous studies reporting an association between increased BTLA expression and poor prognosis, our data suggested that higher BTLA expression was associated with more favorable OS in the TCGA-OV cohort. This seemingly paradoxical observation may reflect the dual role of BTLA in OV: while BTLA can mediate immune evasion through T-cell inhibition, its elevated expression may also serve as a surrogate marker of pre-existing antitumor immune activity, as higher BTLA levels are often accompanied by increased tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. Additionally, differences in cohort composition, such as tumor stage distribution, treatment history, and genetic background, may contribute to the divergent prognostic associations reported across studies. These findings highlight that the prognostic significance of BTLA is likely context-dependent and warrants further investigation in well-defined clinical cohorts. The integration of wet-lab confirmation with multi-omics analyses strengthens the evidence that BTLA dysregulation is not merely a bystander event but may participate in OV pathobiology through modulation of antitumor immunity.

In the exploratory multivariable Cox analysis, BTLA expression showed an association with prognosis after adjustment for selected clinical variables. Consistently, the KM analysis suggested differences in OS between BTLA high- and low-expression groups in the TCGA-OV cohort. These findings are broadly consistent with previous studies in other cancer contexts, in which BTLA expression has been linked to immune regulatory features and clinical outcomes [8, 41]. However, the nomogram generated in this study was used only to visualize the exploratory multivariable Cox results, and the calibration curves and DCA should be interpreted as preliminary descriptive assessments rather than evidence of validated predictive performance. The observed co-expression of BTLA with other immune checkpoints, such as PD-1 and CTLA-4, may suggest potential coordination among inhibitory immune pathways, as also reported in pan-cancer studies [41]. Nevertheless, these findings remain exploratory and require further validation in independent clinical cohorts and functional studies before BTLA can be considered for clinical risk stratification, patient selection, or therapeutic decision-making.

Investigation into BTLA mutational status identified a rare alteration at Q185 within exon 4, which may be associated with alterations in BTLA protein conformation and its ligand-binding affinity. Although the functional implications remain to be experimentally elucidated, structural modeling and molecular docking analyses suggest that these mutations could be related to variations in BTLA-mediated inhibitory signaling [42]. Notably, the observed difference in predicted drug sensitivity to the JAK inhibitor Ruxolitinib was derived from in silico algorithms (e.g., oncoPredict) based on gene expression profiles; thus, this finding reflects a computational prediction rather than a direct measurement of drug sensitivity or clinical outcome, and requires further experimental validation [37, 43]. In addition, molecular docking analysis revealed a favorable binding energy between BTLA and the natural compound Genistein, providing preliminary computational clues for potential BTLA–ligand interactions. However, the biological relevance and pharmacological effects of this interaction remain to be validated through experimental studies. Overall, these findings suggest that the mutational and expression landscape of BTLA may be associated with immune modulation and tumor-related pathways, while their implications for drug response remain exploratory and warrant further investigation.

Our enrichment analyses delineate BTLA’s involvement in multifaceted biological processes, which can be categorized into distinct functional dimensions based on the analytical methods employed. GSEA and GSVA highlight the immunoregulatory and tumor-promoting roles of BTLA. The significant positive enrichment of Interleukin-10 signaling and proinflammatory mediator pathways indicates that BTLA skews the immune milieu toward immunosuppression and chronic inflammation, thereby facilitating tumor progression [44]. Furthermore, the activation of hallmark pathways, including Notch signaling, TGF-β signaling, angiogenesis, and EMT in the BTLA-high group, aligns with evidence that immune checkpoints can orchestrate non-immune oncogenic processes, such as stromal remodeling and metastasis [45, 46]. Conversely, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses underscore BTLA’s association with metabolic reprogramming and structural dynamics. These analyses reveal significant involvement in metabolic processes, membrane organization, and enzymatic activities, suggesting that BTLA may influence tumor cell physiology through metabolic adaptation and alterations in membrane architecture.

Single-cell transcriptomic dissection revealed pronounced heterogeneity among BTLA-expressing cell populations, with epithelial subsets classified into secretory and ciliated phenotypes exhibiting distinct BTLA and signature gene expression profiles. This intratumoral heterogeneity aligns with recent findings that BTLA-mediated signaling varies across immune and non-immune compartments, influencing functional states and intercellular communication [47, 48]. CellChat analysis elucidated complex cellular crosstalk networks, notably robust interactions among endothelial cells and between CD8+T cells via HLA-B/CD8A receptor-ligand pairs, suggested cell-type-related heterogeneity of BTLA-associated signatures [49]. The identification of BTLA-associated functional subtypes underscores the necessity to consider cellular context and spatial heterogeneity when targeting BTLA therapeutically. The above results provide supplementary evidence for the BTLA-related characteristics of the tumor microenvironment.

This study still has certain methodological limitations. Firstly, this study mainly conducted a retrospective analysis based on TCGA, GTEx, and single-cell sequencing data. There may be differences in the sources, sequencing platforms, sample processing procedures, and clinical information completeness of the samples from public databases. Although standardized processing was carried out during the research process, the cross-platform integration between TCGA tumor tissues and GTEx normal ovarian tissues may still introduce potential technical biases. Therefore, the related expression difference results still need to be further verified in the same center or multi-center clinical cohorts. Secondly, the high and low expression groups of BTLA were divided using the median as the cutoff value. This method is suitable for exploratory bioinformatics analysis, but it cannot represent a stable clinical determination threshold. The prognostic evaluation value of its assessment still needs to be further confirmed by combining a larger sample size and complete follow-up data. However, this nomogram lacks independent external validation and there is a possibility of overfitting. Currently, it cannot be directly used as a clinical prediction tool. Its prognostic evaluation value still requires further verification through a larger independent cohort study. Moreover, single-cell analysis provides supplementary information on the heterogeneity and cell communication characteristics of BTLA-related cells, but the number of included samples is limited, and the treatment status and individual differences of different patients may affect the cell composition and gene expression patterns. The drug sensitivity analysis and molecular docking results mainly come from computational predictions, which can provide clues for subsequent drug research, but they cannot be directly equivalent to the real clinical efficacy or clear drug action mechanisms. Finally, this study conducted preliminary experimental verification of BTLA expression by q-PCR and Western blot, but the verification sample size was small, and it was not sufficient to fully clarify the functional role of BTLA in the occurrence and development of OV. In the future, it is still necessary to combine larger-scale clinical samples, independent validation cohorts, and functional experiments to further clarify the biological significance and potential clinical application value of BTLA.

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive evaluation of BTLA expression and its potential relevance in OV, suggesting that BTLA may be associated with OV prognosis and BTLA-related molecular features. The integrated analyses, together with experimental validation, offer preliminary evidence for future biomarker and therapeutic studies. However, these findings remain exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. Further validation in independent clinical cohorts, as well as functional and mechanistic experiments, is required to clarify the biological role of BTLA and its potential clinical applicability. Future studies focusing on BTLA-related molecular characteristics and tumor heterogeneity may help to better understand its role in OV and provide a basis for subsequent translational research.
