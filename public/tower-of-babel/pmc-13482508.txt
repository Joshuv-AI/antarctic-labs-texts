Effect of corn silage treated with homo- and heterofermentative lactic acid bacteria on nutrient digestibility, rumen characteristics and rumen microbiota in goats
Authors: Smerjai Bureenok, Benya Saenmahayak, Jinxin Liu, Yunlong Gao, Jan Thomas Schonewille
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study we aimed to investigate the effects of corn silage treated with Lactiplantibacillus paraplantarum ST1 (LPL) and Limosilactobacillus fermentum N4 (LFN4) on nutrient digestibility, ruminal fermentation, and rumen microbiota in goats. Fifteen cross-bred goats were randomly assigned to one of the 3 corn silages: untreated silage (control), LPL-silage, or LFN4-silage. It was found that LPL versus LFN4 treatment of corn silage, caused a greater (P<0.05) digestibility of dry matter, crude protein, neutral detergent fiber (NDF), and acid detergent fiber (ADF). Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were found to be the two dominant phyla of the rumen microbiota in goats. The feeding of LPL treated corn silage tended to cause enrichment of rumen fluid with Prevotellaceae_UCG_001 and Fretibacterium (P<0.01). In conclusion, the inoculation of L. paraplantarum ST1 (LPL) during ensiling improved nutrient digestibility and changed the bacterial community in rumen. These changes are considered to be beneficial for animal performance.Keywords: Fermentation, Inoculant, Lactiplantibacillus paraplantarum, Limosilactobacillus fermentum

Introduction

World-wide, ensiling is widely used to preserve high-moisture feedstuffs for ruminants. During the ensiling process, epiphytic lactic acid bacteria (LAB) utilize water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC) to produce lactic acid, which serves as the main acid for preserving the nutritional value of the material crop (McDonald et al., 1991).

Lactiplantibacillus plantarum is well known for its efficacy to preserve silage because of the rapid conversion of WSC into lactic acid (Kim et al., 2021). In addition, various authors reported (Ellis et al., 2016; Jin et al. 2024; Keady and Steen, 1994, 1995) that the inoculation of forage with a single strain of L. plantarum increased the digestibility of the silage in question. L. plantarum is classified as a facultatively heterofermentative bacterium (Corsetti et al., 2016), producing lactic acid as its primary fermentation end product. Nevertheless, during the fermentation of hexoses and pentoses, it generates only small amounts of other organic acids, including acetic acid. Lactiplantibacillus paraplantarum (LPL) is a facultatively heterofermentative lactic acid bacterium that primarily produces lactic acid during carbohydrate fermentation and exhibits physiological characteristics similar to those of L. plantarum (Curk et al., 1996). The presence of acetic acid in silage, however, can contribute to its aerobic stability (Da Silva et al., 2017). From this perspective, hetero-fermentative LAB can thus be considered of interest. Fitriani et al. (2024) reported that Limosilactobacillus fermentum improved the aerobic stability of sorghum silage. Previously, Pitiwittayakul et al. (2021) showed that supplementation of the hetero-fermentative bacterium L. fermentum N4 (LFN4) to Stylo legume (S. guianensis) was highly instrumental to improve the silage quality but currently it is not known whether the use LFN4, like L. plantarum, results in a greater digestibility of the silage. Thus, we measured macro-nutrient digestibility in goats fed either untreated corn silage or corn silage inoculated with either LFN4 or L. paraplantarum where the latter silage served as a positive control.

Next to their capacity to preserve high-moisture feedstuffs, LAB are known for their probiotic properties. Indeed, it has been shown by Matthews et al. (2019) and Astuti et al. (2022) that LAB can stimulate the population of fiber degrading bacteria. Furthermore, Pitiwittayakul et al. (2021) also indicated that both LFN4 and L. paraplantarum ST1 (LPL) have probiotic properties. However, it is yet not known whether the use of supplemental LFN4 and LPL influences the rumen microbiome under feeding conditions. In case the composition of the rumen microbiome is affected by supplemental LFN4 and LPL, however, it could be instrumental, at least theoretically, to explain a potential increase in silage digestibility.

Materials and methods

LAB inoculants

L. fermentum N4 (LFN4) and L. paraplantarum ST1 (LPL) strains, which were isolated from Napiergrass (Pennisetum purpureum) and Stylo legume, respectively, were used in this study (Pitiwittayakul et al., 2021). Each LAB strain was kept in glycerol and grown anaerobically in 10 mL MRS (De Man, Rogosa, Sharpe) broth medium (Acumedia Manufactures Inc., Baltimore, MD, USA) at 37 °C for 24 h in an anaerobic incubator. Then, each LAB was transferred into 1,000 mL of MRS broth and incubated at 37 °C for another 48 h in an anaerobic incubator. After incubation, the concentration of bacteria was determined by spectrometry at optical density (OD600). The characteristics of the two LAB strains are shown in Table 1.

Preparation of experimental silages

Corn plants, approximately 90 days old, were harvested at the one-half milk-line stage of maturity with a dry matter content of ∼ 355 g/kg (Bal et al., 2000) and chopped prior to the preparation of the experimental silages. The fresh corn material was chopped to a length of 1.0–2.0 cm. Then, the corn was either or not inoculated with 1 × 105 CFU/g fresh forage of LPL or LFN4. The inocula were diluted with sterilized distilled water to achieve the desired inoculation level and the added volume of LAB suspension was 1 ml /kg of fresh matter (FM). An equal volume of sterilized distilled water was added to corn that was not inoculated with LAB, and this treatment served as a control. After the preparation, the chopped corn plants were tightly packed into a 100-liter plastic drums and stored at room temperature (27–30 °C) for 60 days prior to the start of the digestibility trial. For the assessments on the microbial community of silage, each treatment (n = 3) was prepared and packed into plastic bags (19.7 × 29.9 cm), with 200 g per bag, and sealed using a vacuum sealer and stored at ambient temperature (27–30 °C) for 60 days. After fermentation, the silage samples were collected and kept at -40 °C for DNA extraction.

Silage quality

For assessments on silage quality, ∼ 50 g of silage was mixed thoroughly with 150 ml of distilled water, and stored at 4 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the sample was filtered, and the pH was measured using a pH meter (SI analytics GmbH, Mainz, Germany). Thereafter, water extracts were used to determine the concentrations of ammonia-nitrogen (Cai, 2004), organic acids (lactic acid, acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid), and ethanol using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Shimadzu Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) with a 30GX340EKN Vertex column (300 mm × 8 mm i.d.; column temperature of 60 °C; flow rate of 0.80 mL/min, mobile phase 0.005 M H2SO4) (Knauer, Berlin, Germany).

For microbe evaluation, 10 g of silage sample were added to 90 mL of sterile 0.85 % NaCl solution and serially diluted from 10-1 and 10-10. Pouring plates were prepared using de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe agar (MRS, Acumedia Manufactures Inc., Baltimore, MD, USA) for LAB, and potato dextrose agar (PDA, Acumedia Manufactures Inc., Baltimore, MD, USA) containing 1 % of tartaric acid solution (10 % w/v) for yeasts counts. Aerobic bacteria were counted on nutrient agar. The MRS plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h in anaerobic jars with disposable anaerobic gas pack (Anaerocult® A, Merck KGaA, Germany). The aerobic bacteria and PDA plates were incubated aerobically at 30 °C for 5 days. All colonies were counted on plates with 30–300 colony-forming units (CFU). For microbial community analysis, 5 g of silage was collected and stored at -40 °C for DNA extraction.

Digestibility trial

The animal experiment was carried out according to a protocol approved by the Animal Ethical Committee of Rajamangala University of Technology Isan, Nakhon Ratchasima, Thailand (U1-03319-2566). Fifteen male Anglo Nubian x Thai native crossbred goats, with an initial body weight (BW) of 15 ± 1.2 kg (mean ± SD), were used. Prior to the experiment, the goats were dewormed with Ivomectin (IVOMEC F plus, Bangkok, Thailand) and supplemented with vitamins A, D3, and E (Biotecnocem, Dallas, USA) by means of an intra muscular injection. During the experiment, the goats were housed individually in metabolic cages (148 × 115 × 120 cm) and water was available all times. The animals were fed twice daily at 09.00 and 16.00 h. The experiment consisted of a 14-day adaptation period followed by 7 days of feces collection. The goats were randomly assigned to one of the three experimental silages, i.e. untreated corn silage (control), or corn ensiled with either LFN4 or LPL. During the first 10 days of the adaptation period, the goats were fed a commercially available concentrate (1 % BW, as fed) and the replacement of concentrate by corn silage was carried out gradually over a 5-day period until the animals were fed solely corn silage. During the collection period, however, the experimental corn silage was the sole source of nutrition which was fed at 90 % of the mean daily dry matter intake observed during the week prior to the week of feces collection so as to ensure complete consumption of the offered amount of corn silage (Agbossamey et al., 2000). Body weight was measured before the morning feeding, both at the beginning and the end of study. Samples of the experimental silages were collected and weighed throughout the 7 days of the collection period. The samples were stored at -20 °C pending chemical analysis. Feces were collected quantitatively from each goat in the morning and afternoon. The daily feces production of each goat was mixed thoroughly, and a subsample (10 % of the total wet weight) was stored at -20 °C. At the end of the collection period, the stored feces fractions from each goat were combined, mixed thoroughly, and sampled. The samples were then dried at 60 °C for 96 h in a forced-air oven, ground and stored in air-dry form in sealed jars at 32 to 35 °C pending analysis.

Rumen fluid sampling

On the last day of the collection period, rumen fluid samples were collected 30 min before (0 h) and 4 h after the morning feeding using a stomach tube connected to a vacuum pump (Rocker, New Taipei City, Taiwan (R.O.C.)). Approximately 50 mL of rumen fluid was collected after discarding the initial portion to minimize the contamination of rumen fluid with saliva. Immediately after collection, the rumen fluid collections were filtered through 2 layers of cheesecloth. Then, the pH of the filtrates was measured using a pH meter (SI Analytics GmbH, Mainz, Germany). Thereafter, 9-mL aliquots of the filtrate were acidified with 1 mL of 1 M H2SO4, and subsequently centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was stored at -20 °C pending the analysis of volatile fatty acids (VFA). The rumen VFA concentrations were determined using HPLC (30GX340EKN Vertex, 300 mm×8 mm i.d.; column temperature, 60 °C; flow rate, 0.80 mL/min, mobile phase 0.005 M H2SO4, Knauer, Berlin, Germany). Rumen samples obtained from 0 h prior to the morning feeding were collected and stored at -40 °C for DNA extraction.

Microbial community

Metagenomic DNA for prokaryotes was isolated using QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocols. Briefly, 0.25 g samples from silage and rumen fluid were extracted. The quality of the extracted DNA was determined via DeNovix Fluorometer.

The prokaryotic 16S rRNA gene at V3V4 region was performed using the Qiagen QIAseq 16S/ITS Region panel (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). For the microbial community analysis, 16S rRNA amplicons were labeled with different sequencing adaptors using QIAseq 16S/ITS Region Panel Sample Index PCR Reaction (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The quality and quantity of DNA libraries were evaluated using DeNovix QFX Fluorometer and QIAxcel Advanced (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), respectively. 16S rRNA libraries were sequenced using an illumina Miseq600 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

The raw sequences were categorized into groups based on the 5′ barcode sequences. The sequences were processed following DADA2 v1.16.0 pipeline (https://benjjneb.github.io/dada2/), which identifies microbial diversity and community structures using unique amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) (Callahan et al., 2016). Microbial taxa were classified using the Silva version 138 as a reference database (Quast et al., 2013). Alpha diversity index (Chao1 richness, Shannon, and PD whole tree) was computed using DADA2 software. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) and cladogram plot were performed to identify the bacterial biomarkers.

Chemical analysis

Air-dried samples of feed and feces were analyzed to determine the contents of neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) using the method of Van Soest et al. (1991), with the addition of sodium sulfite and α-amylase to the samples. Both NDF and ADF were expressed on ash-free basis. The nitrogen (N) content was determined using the Kjeldahl method, and the crude protein (CP) content was calculated as N x 6.25 (AOAC, 2000).

Statistical analysis

All data obtained from the animal experiment was analyzed using one-way ANOVA (SAS, 1994). Differences between treatment means were determined using Tukey’s test. For the bacterial community analysis, a pairwise comparison of alpha diversity (Observed ASVs, Chao1, Shannon, and PD whole tree) was calculated using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Moreover, the Kruskal-Wallis sum-rank test was also used in LEfSe analysis to identify bacterial biomarkers that differ significantly in abundant taxons between sample groups. Throughout, statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 and trends at 0.05 ≤P < 0.10.

Numerically, the highest lactic acid concentration was found in LPL treated silage while the highest acetic acid concentration was found in the untreated control. In all experimental silages, ammonia-N and ethanol concentrations were low and butyric acid was not detected thereby indicating that all three silages were well preserved. Compared to fresh corn, LAB- and yeast counts and the number of aerobic bacteria, were numerically lower in the three silages.

Digestibility of nutrients and rumen fermentation

The daily intake of DM (Table 3) was found to be similar between the experimental treatments (P = 0.744) but the apparent digestibility of both DM and ADF, were respectively 5.9 % and 13.9 % greater (P ≤ 0.020) when the LPL treated corn silage instead of the control silage was fed. The apparent digestibility’s of DM and ADF were found to be similar between the LNF4 treated silage and the control. The NDF digestibility was similar between the two silages treated with LAB but LPL treatment increased NDF digestibility by 9.2 % compared to the untreated control silage (P = 0.044). Both, LNF4 and LPL treatment of corn silage caused a greater CP digestibility but the most profound effect on CP digestibility was found when LPL treated silage was fed, i.e. a value 33.9 % greater compared to the control (P = 0.001).

Rumen bacterial community

After processing, 470,054 high quality reads of 16S rRNA were obtained. There were no differences between treatments in terms of bacterial richness and diversity indices of rumen (Fig. 3, P > 0.05). A total of 16 different bacterial phyla were found (Table 5), with only six enriched phyla (with relative abundance > 1 % at least in one group). The most prevalent bacterial phyla were found to be Firmicutes, followed by Bacteroidota, and Proteobacteria and their relative abundances were similar between treatments (P ≥ 0.813). The relative abundance of Synergista was lower in goats fed LFN4-treated silage compared to those in the LPL-treated silage group (P = 0.011). Overall, 136 genera were detected and those with a relative abundance > 1 % are presented in Table 5. The dominant genera were found to be Prevotella, Christensenellaceae R-7 group, and Rikenellaceae RC9 gut group but their abundances were similar between treatments. Goats fed with the control silage tended to have an increased abundance of Lachnospiraceae NK3A20 group (P = 0.054) while the bundance of Prevotellaceae UCG-001 tended to be increased in goat fed LPL-treated silage (P = 0.052). The relative abundance of Fretibacterium was higher in goats fed LPL-treated silage (P = 0.009).

Discussion

Silage quality

The current results on fermentation parameters in silage, such as pH value, BA, and NH3-N content, indicate that, irrespective of treatment, all silages were well preserved (McDonald et al., 1991). It thus appears that supplemental LAB are not needed to achieve a nutritious corn silage. Indeed, epiphytic LAB counts in the control were found to be 7.0 log cfu/g FW and such a high epiphytic LAB count is sufficient to ensure a rapid conversion of WSC into lactic acid and therefore the production of a good quality silage (Oliveira et al., 2017). Firmicutes was the predominant phylum in corn silage, accounting for more than 95 % of the bacterial community, followed by Proteobacteria and Actinobacteriota. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies of corn silage, where Firmicutes became the dominant phylum during ensiling due to the proliferation of lactic acid bacteria under anaerobic and acidic conditions (Hu et al., 2018; Jiang et al., 2020). Proteobacteria and Actinobacteriota generally remained as minor community members after fermentation. The predominance of Firmicutes observed in the present study is therefore consistent with the typical microbial succession reported in well-fermented corn silage and may indicate successful fermentation and preservation of the silage. This supports that the silage fermentation was strongly driven by LAB populations (Jiang et al., 2020). LEfSe analysis identified Pandoraea as a biomarker in the control silage, whereas Propionibacteriales and Nocardioidaceae were enriched in the LPL treatment. Pandoraea belongs to the phylum Proteobacteria and is commonly associated with plant and environmental habitats. The higher abundance of this genus in the control group may reflect the persistence of indigenous epiphytic microorganisms in the absence of inoculant application. In contrast, the lower abundance of Pandoraea in inoculated silages suggests that LAB inoculation promoted a microbial environment that favored fermentative bacteria and suppressed competing microorganisms.

Propionic acid has been reported to inhibit the growth of yeasts and molds and thereby improve the aerobic stability of silage (Kung et al., 2018). Therefore, the increased abundance of Propionibacteriales may indicate a microbial community with a greater potential to contribute to silage preservation and resistance to aerobic spoilage. Unfortunately, the functional role of these taxa was not measured in the present study thus the aforementioned notion need further substantiation. In addition, Nocardioidaceae, belonging to the phylum Actinobacteriota, are recognized for their ability to metabolize complex organic substrates and survive under stressful environmental conditions. Although these taxa are not considered dominant silage fermenters, their enrichment may indicate a shift in microbial community structure that supports nutrient transformation and silage preservation.

Nutrient digestibility and rumen fermentation

The LPL-treated corn silage increased ruminal digestion of DM, CP and fiber. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting that the addition of L. plantarum in the silage increased crude protein and fiber digestibility (Jin et al., 2024).

The observed increase in fiber digestibility was neither mirrored by a greater TVFA concentration nor a shift in the VFA profile. This lack of relationship between fiber digestibility and TVFA concentration can be explained by VFA absorption. Fiber digestion primarily promotes ruminal AA production (Russell and Rychlik, 2001; Van Soest, 1994) but acetate is rapidly absorbed through the rumen wall and thus an increase in its production does not necessarily lead to a higher concentration of AA in rumen fluid. Thus, in case the rate of VFA absorption parallels its production rate, the ruminal VFA concentrations can remain stable (Bergman, 1990; Steele et al., 2016). We therefore speculate that the observed increase in fiber digestion was too low to influence individual VFA concentrations. Unfortunately, the current data on rumen fermentation do not provide clues to explain the observed increase in CP digestibility when LAB treated silages were fed. However, it can be speculated that in the LAB-treated silages large proteins were converted into peptides and amino acids during the process of ensiling (Xia et al., 2025), thereby enhancing the digestibility of CP. On the other hand, it cannot be excluded that the observed improvement of CP digestibility is secondary to the increase in fiber digestibility. Indeed, it is well known that part of the protein in plants is bound to fiber, i.e. NDF bound N (N-NDF, Licitra et al., 1996). Thus, an increase in fiber digestibility may render N-NDF available for rumen fermentation and subsequently improve CP digestibility.

Rumen microbial community

In this study, the dominant phyla in the goat’s rumen were found to be Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, which is in agreement with the findings of Li et al. (2022). However, the phylum Synergistetes, but not Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, differentially responded to LAB treatment of corn silage. This observation is in line with the impact of LPL vs LNF4 on Fretibacterium because the latter genus belongs to the phylum Synergistetes (McSweeney et al., 2025). Moreover, the genus Fretibacterium is known to be involved in amino acid, peptide and protein fermentation (McSweeney et al., 2025). It is therefore suggested that the increased abundance of Fretibacterium is causally related to the LPL induced increase in CP digestibility.

The abundance of the genera Lachnospiraceae NK3A20 group and Prevotellaceae UCG-001, respectively belonging to the phyla Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes (Khairunisa et al., 2022), tended to be affected by LAB treatment of the silages. The results related to the genus Lachnospiraceae NK3A20, however, are not easy to interpret. Indeed, the numerically lowest abundance of Lachnospiraceae NK3A20 was found in LPL treated silage while this silage had the greatest fiber digestibility. In contrast, the numerically greatest abundance of Prevotellaceae UCG-001 was found in the silage with the greatest fiber digestibility, i.e., LPL treated corn silage. Prevotellaceae UCG-001 contain Prevotella strains that play an important role in the degradation of cellulose and xylan (Zhang et al., 2022). We therefore suggest that the LPL induced increase in fiber digestibility is related to the bacteria species belonging to the genus Prevotellaceae UCG-001.

Unfortunately, the relative abundances of the genera Rikenellaceae RC9 gut group, Christensenellaceae R7, Ruminococcus, and Ruminococcaceae NK4A214 were not affected by LAB treatment of the silages. For obvious reasons, it is quite difficult to linearly extrapolate responses at genera level to the level of individual bacteria species level, but we speculate that the cellulolytic bacteria belonging to the aforementioned genera (Huang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2019), were not sensitive to the LAB treatment of the silage.

Conclusions

The use of LPL inoculant for silage making is a conceivable strategy to improve the quality of silage. Our results indicate that both the digestibility of crude protein and fiber is positively affected by LPL treatment. It is suggested that the increase in nutrient digestibility is mediated by the genera Fretibacterium and Prevotellaceae UCG-001.
