Gaussian Invariant Markov Chain Monte Carlo
Michalis K. Titsias

Abstract
We develop sampling methods, which consist of Gaussian invariant versions of random walk Metropolis (RWM), Metropolis adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA) and second order Hessian or Manifold MALA. Unlike standard RWM and MALA we show that Gaussian invariant sampling can lead to ergodic estimators with improved statistical efficiency. This is due to a remarkable property of Gaussian invariance that allows us to obtain exact analytical solutions to the Poisson equation for Gaussian targets. These solutions can be used to construct efficient and easy to use control variates for variance reduction of estimators under any intractable target. We demonstrate the new samplers and estimators in several examples, including high dimensional targets in latent Gaussian models where we compare against several advanced methods and obtain state-of-the-art results. We also provide theoretical results regarding geometric ergodicity, and an optimal scaling analysis that shows the dependence of the optimal acceptance rate on the Gaussianity of the target.



Keywords:
Metropolis-Hastings, variance reduction, control variate, Poisson equation.

1
Introduction

In Bayesian statistics a popular approach to sampling-based inference relies on Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms which are employed to draw a sample from an ergodic Markov chain
\{X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{n-1}\}_{n\geq 0}
with continuous state space
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, transition kernel
P
and invariant measure
\pi(\cdot)
, referred to as
target
, for which we assume there is a density denoted by
\pi(x)
. The expected value
\mathrm{E}_{\pi}[F]:=\pi(F)=\int F(x)\pi(x)dx
of a real-valued function
F
is estimated through the ergodic average

\mu_{n}(F)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}F(X_{i}),\,\,\,X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}

(1)

which satisfies, for any initial distribution of
X_{0}
, a central limit theorem of the form

\mu_{n}(F)\overset{D}{\to}N\Big(\pi(F),\frac{\sigma^{2}_{F}}{n}\Big),

(2)

with the asymptotic variance
\sigma^{2}_{F}
given by

\sigma^{2}_{F}:=\mathrm{Var}_{\pi}[F(X_{0})]+2\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{Cov}_{\pi}\{F(X_{0}),F(X_{i+1})\}.

(3)

Different MCMC algorithms are usually compared with respect to their computational complexity and their statistical efficiency which is measured by the size of the asymptotic variance
\sigma^{2}_{F}
. We focus on the popular Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm that defines a
\pi
-invariant kernel
P

satisfying the detailed balance or reversebility condition.
At each iteration, given the current state
x
, a candidate state
y
is drawn from a proposal distribution
q(y|x)
and it is accepted or rejected with probability

\alpha(x,y)=\min\Big\{1,\frac{\pi(y)q(x|y)}{\pi(x)q(y|x)}\Big\}.

This results in a transition kernel with density

P(y|x)=\alpha(x,y)q(y|x)+\left(1-\int\alpha(x,z)q(z|x)dz\right)\delta_{x}(y),

(4)

where
\delta_{x}
is a point mass centred at
x
.
Two widely-used samplers are the RWM with proposal

q(y|x)=\mathcal{N}(y|x,2\gamma\Sigma),

(5)

and
MALA with proposal

q(y|x)=\mathcal{N}(y|x+\gamma\Sigma\nabla\log\pi(x),2\gamma\Sigma)

(6)

where
\mathcal{N}(y|\mu,\Sigma)
denotes the multivariate Gaussian density with mean vector
\mu
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
. In (
5
) and (
6
) we write the covariance matrix as
2\gamma\Sigma
to
make it comparable with the notation in Section
2
where
\gamma>0
is a global step size
and
\Sigma
is a preconditioning covariance matrix.
\Sigma
can be either known beforehand
or found by adaptive MCMC
(
Haario et al. 2001
)
. There exist also generalizations of MALA
where the constant preconditioner

\Sigma
is replaced by a state-dependent

A_{x}
such as an inverse Fisher matrix or the negative inverse Hessian matrix
(
Girolami & Calderhead 2011
,
Petra et al. 2014
)
.

The main motivation of our work is that ergodic estimation based on RWM and MALA can be sub-optimal due to lack of invariance to Gaussian distributions.
Specifically,
these algorithms do not have the Gaussian invariance property defined as follows:

Definition 1
.

(Gaussian invariance) Given a Gaussian target
\pi(x)=\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)

a MH sampler is Gaussian invariant if the proposal
distribution is reversible to
\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)
, i.e., it holds
\mathcal{N}(y|\mu,\Sigma)q(x|y)=\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)q(x|y)
.
In such case the acceptance probability is always
\alpha(x,y)=1
, so that all proposed samples are accepted.

The absence of Gaussian invariance
of RWM and MALA
can result in reduced statistical efficiency for two reasons:

(a)

When the target
\pi
becomes close to a Gaussian distribution these algorithms cannot recover i.i.d. sampling. For instance, in the limiting case when the target is

\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)
there is no value of
\gamma>0
, in either RWM or MALA, that results in i.i.d. sampling from
\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)
.
MALA comes closer to achieve i.i.d. sampling since by setting
\gamma=1
the proposal
in (
6
) becomes the independent Metropolis proposal

q(y)=\mathcal{N}(y|\mu,2\Sigma)
which has the same mean
\mu
with the target, but still twice the target covariance.

(b)

For a Gaussian target
\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)
a Gaussian invariant kernel can achieve zero variance estimation for many functions
F(x)
for which
the integral
\int F(x)\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)dx
is analytic.
This is achieved by solving the corresponding Poisson equation of the Markov chain and obtain a control variate that results in a zero variance ergodic estimator for
E_{\pi}[F]
.
In contrast, when applying RWM or MALA to sample from
\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)
, it seems infeasible to obtain
zero variance estimation since as shown by
Alexopoulos et al. 2023

we cannot solve analytically the Poisson equation, not even
for the simplest function
F(x)=x
.
In practice, this implies that efficient variance reduction for non-Gaussian
targets when running RWM or MALA can be very hard, since it is already very difficult for the tractable Gaussian target case.

Motivated by the above, we develop Gaussian invariant (GI) samplers that can be viewed as
direct modifications of RWM, MALA and second order Hessian or Manifold MALA
(
Girolami & Calderhead 2011
,
Petra et al. 2014
)
.
We refer to the new schemes
as GI-RWM and GI-MALA.
In the simplest case the Gaussian invariant MCMC proposal we use is the
preconditioned Crank Nicolson (pCN)

(
Cotter et al. 2013
,
Beskos et al. 2008
,
Neal 1999
)
which is a popular way to add invariance to Gaussian prior distributions in latent Gaussian models. In the most advanced cases we introduce new second order Hessian schemes,
and we construct
variants that can overcome state-of-the-art sampling in latent Gaussian models
(
Titsias & Papaspiliopoulos 2018
)
, without increasing the computational cost.

In a second contribution
we follow previous
work
(
Dellaportas & Kontoyiannis 2012
,
Alexopoulos et al. 2023
,
Douc et al. 2024
,
Liu et al. 2024
)
, and construct
control variates through approximate solutions of the Poisson equation
in order to reduce the variance of Monte Carlo estimators. More precisely, for Gaussian invariant samplers we derive exact solutions to the Poisson equation
for several functions
F
and Gaussian targets. Note that such exact solutions are not feasible for RWM and MALA
(
Alexopoulos et al. 2023
)
.
Then, we use these solutions
to increase the sampling efficiency of the ergodic estimators under intractable non-Gaussian targets, by applying variance reduction using control variates.

Furthermore, we establish theoretical results demonstrating the geometric ergodicity of our samplers
by following

Roberts & Tweedie 1996a
,
Roberts & Tweedie 1996b
and
Roy & Zhang 2023
.
When an analytical solution to the Poisson equation is unavailable, we produce an approximate solution and prove its convergence properties. Finally, we study in detail the optimal scaling of GI-MALA. While previous studies on the optimal scaling of MCMC algorithms assume that the dimension of the target tends to infinity
(
Gelman et al. 1997
,
Roberts & Rosenthal 1998
,
Roberts et al. 2001
)
, such an assumption yields a trivial solution in our setting. Therefore, we obtain an approximate optimal scaling under a finite dimension and characterize its dependence on deviations from the Gaussianity of the target.

2
Gaussian invariant MCMC

In this section we develop Gaussian invariant MCMC proposal distributions based on three levels of increasing computational cost: (i) a zero-order RWM-like
proposal, (ii) a first-order or MALA-like proposal
that exploits gradients

\nabla\log\pi(x)
of the
log target, and (iii)
a second-order proposal that
automatically specifies the preconditioning by possibly exploiting some information of the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}\log\pi(x)
.

To start with the simplest case (i), suppose for
\pi(x)

we have a global Gaussian approximation
\hat{\pi}(x)=\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)
. A way to use
\hat{\pi}(x)
in MCMC for targeting
\pi
is to
consider the independent Metropolis algorithm with proposal
q(y)=\hat{\pi}(y)
. However, this can have high rejection rates when
\hat{\pi}
is not a good approximation to
\pi
. Another way is to use a RWM-like scheme with proposal the following
autoregressive density,

q(y|x)=\mathcal{N}(y|(1-\gamma)x+\gamma\mu,(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})\Sigma)

(7)

where the parameter
\gamma\in(0,2)
controls the autocorrelation between
y
and
x

so that when
\gamma=1
the proposal
reduces to independent Metropolis, while when
\gamma
is close to zero,
y
will be close to
x
.
It is easy to verify that the above
q(y|x)

is reversible and therefore invariant to the Gaussian approximation
\hat{\pi}(x)=\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)
.
Also, it has the same form with pCN that has been applied to latent Gaussian models

(
Cotter et al. 2013
,
Neal 1999
,
Beskos et al. 2008
)
. The difference is that here we care about
invariance over a Gaussian approximation
\hat{\pi}
to some generic target
\pi
, where
\pi
is not necessarily a latent Gaussian model.

A limitation of the proposal in (
7
)
is that it requires to choose specific values for both parameters

\mu
and
\Sigma
. We can
avoid this by adding information from the target
\pi
, such as first and second order derivatives. In case (ii) we make use of the gradient vector
\nabla\log\pi(x)
and construct the proposal

q(y|x)=\mathcal{N}(y|x+\gamma\Sigma\nabla\log\pi(x),(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})\Sigma)

(8)

which depends only on
\Sigma
and not on
\mu
. As a result this proposal is reversible not only with respect to a specific Gaussian approximation
\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)

but over the full family of Gaussian approximations having a certain
covariance matrix
\Sigma
, but arbitrary mean
\mu
. To see this note that

x+\gamma\Sigma\nabla\log\hat{\pi}(x)=x+\gamma\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}(\mu-x)=(1-\gamma)x+\gamma\mu
,
which
shows that if we plug in the gradients of a Gaussian target with covariance matrix
\Sigma

in the proposal (
8
), it
reduces to the Gaussian invariant proposal from (
7
).
However, unlike (
7
), (
8
) enjoys the advantage that does not require to specify
\mu
.

In case (iii) we want to additionally avoid having to
specify
\Sigma
, and for that we need more information from the target
\pi
. To formalize this we define the following preconditioning.

Definition 2
.

The matrix
A_{x}:=A(x,\pi)
, that is a function of
x
and
\pi
, is called a Gaussian optimal preconditioning if it is a symmetric positive definite matrix and for Gaussian targets
\pi(x)=\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)
it holds

A(x,\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma))=\Sigma
.

Using
A_{x}
we can consider the
proposal distribution

q(y|x)=\mathcal{N}(y|x+\gamma A_{x}\nabla\log\pi(x),(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})A_{x}),

(9)

which has the same form as (
8
) but it does not require to specify
\Sigma
; instead it uses
A_{x}
which is a function of current state
x
and
\pi
. Observe that, due to the Gaussian optimality of
A_{x}
, this proposal is invariant to any
Gaussian distribution.

One way to specify
A_{x}
is to make it equal to
the negative of the inverse Hessian of
\log\pi
(given that it is positive definite), i.e.,
A_{x}^{-1}=-\nabla^{2}\log\pi(x)
.
The use of the Hessian has been popularised by the stochastic Newton MCMC algorithm of

Petra et al. 2014
,
although this idea appears earlier

(
Qi & Minka 2002
)
. In fact, the MCMC proposal in
Petra et al. 2014
is a special case of (
9
) obtained for
\gamma=1
. The simplified manifold MALA algorithm in
Girolami & Calderhead 2011

also uses a state-dependent preconditioning which is often close or identical to the negative inverse Hessian of the log target. However, this manifold MALA is never a special case of
(
9
) since any MALA is not Gaussian invariant.

When the Hessian is too expensive to compute we
can use as
A_{x}
some approximation to the Hessian, as long as
for Gaussian targets
it returns the exact covariance matrix
\Sigma
. For instance, in Section
5
where we specialize the proposal (
9
) to latent Gaussian models with target density
\pi(x)\propto\exp\{g(x)\}\mathcal{N}(x|0,\Sigma_{0})
, we show how to construct computationally
efficient and easy to implement approximations to the Hessian, that result in state-of-the-art
performance among many advanced previous samplers.
Also, the preconditioning
A_{x}
does not need to be state-dependent
to be compatible with Definition
2
. For instance, the optimal constant preconditioning for MALA in
Titsias 2023
, having the inverse Fisher form

A=\mathcal{I}^{-1}
with
\mathcal{I}=\mathrm{E}_{\pi(x)}[\nabla\log\pi(x)\nabla\log\pi(x)^{\top}]
, is a suitable option since for a Gaussian
target the Fisher matrix
is
\mathcal{I}=\Sigma^{-1}
. Obviously, the covariance matrix of the target
\pi
is also a valid option. This suggests that in practice we could use adaptive MCMC algorithms
(
Haario et al. 2001
,
Titsias 2023
)
to
estimate a constant
A
.

By comparing the proposals (
7
)
and (
8
)-(
9
) with the corresponding expressions for RWM and MALA from (
5
)-(
6
)
we observe that the connection especially with MALA
is really striking. In particular, the MALA proposal
in (
6
)
and the Gaussian invariant
gradient-based proposal
in (
8
)
have exactly the same mean

x+\gamma\Sigma\nabla\log\pi(x)
, and they differ
only in the multiplicative scalar in front of
\Sigma
: MALA uses
2\gamma
while (
8
)
uses smaller scalar equal to

2\gamma-\gamma^{2}
.
Despite these differences,
we shall refer to
(
7
)
as Gaussian invariant RWM (GI-RWM)
and similarly to (
8
)-(
9
)
as GI-MALA.

3
Exact solutions to Poisson equation

We improve the statistical efficiency of the ergodic estimators
E_{\pi}[F]
of GI-RWM and GI-MALA for certain function
F
by taking advantage of the Gaussian invariance property.
We first show that it is possible to
solve analytically the
Poisson equation
for Gaussian targets and several functions
F(x)
. Then, in Section
4
we use these solutions to construct control variates for variance reduction of ergodic estimators in non-Gaussian targets.

For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
a natural choice for a control variate in ergodic estimators of Markov chains is a function of the form

U(x)=\int P(y|x)G(y)dy-G(x)=PG(x)-G(x)

where we denote
PG(x)=\int P(y|x)G(y)dy=\mathrm{E}[G(X_{1})|X_{0}=x]
.
Since the transition density
P(y|x)
is
\pi
-invariant, i.e.,

\pi(y)=\int P(y|x)\pi(x)dx
,
we have
\mathrm{E}_{\pi}[U(x)]=0
. Then for samples

X_{i}
drawn from
\pi
we can modify
the estimator in (
1
)
by adding the control variates
U(X_{i})

and construct the unbiased estimator

\mu_{n,G}(F)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\{F(X_{i})+PG(X_{i})-G(X_{i})\}.

(10)

For the MH kernel in (
4
)
PG
is written as

\displaystyle PG(x)

\displaystyle=\int\alpha(x,y)q(y|x)G(y)dy+\left(1-\int\alpha(x,z)q(z|x)dz\right)G(x)

\displaystyle=G(x)+\int\alpha(x,y)(G(y)-G(x))q(y|x)dy

(11)

and the estimator simplifies
as

\mu_{n,G}(F)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left\{F(X_{i})+\int\alpha(X_{i},y)(G(y)-G(X_{i}))q(y|X_{i})dy\right\}.

(12)

This estimator still depends on an intractable integral over
y
and we discuss in Section
4
how to deal with it by following previous work
(
Alexopoulos et al. 2023
)
. In the
remainder of this section we unlock
a remarkable property of Gaussian invariant proposals that results in closed-form optimal choices for
G
when the target is Gaussian.

To choose a suitable function
G
in the estimator (
10
)
we consider the Poisson equation, that characterizes the optimal choice resulting in zero variance.
This equation says that there is a function

\hat{F}(x)
that satisfies

\int P(y|x)\hat{F}(y)dy-\hat{F}(x)=-F(x)+\pi(F),\ \ \forall x\sim\pi(x)

(13)

or with shorthand notation

P\hat{F}(x)-\hat{F}(x)=-F(x)+\pi(F).

We observe that if
\hat{F}
is a solution then

\hat{F}+c
is also a solution, thus in practice additive constants can be ignored. If we know
\hat{F}

we can use
G=\hat{F}

in (
10
) and obtain

\mu_{n,G}(F)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\{F(X_{i})+P\hat{F}(X_{i})-\hat{F}(X_{i})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\{F(X_{i})-F(X_{i})+\pi(F)\}\}=\pi(F),

(14)

so this estimator is exact, i.e., it has zero variance.
A theoretical way to write the solution is as the following infinite series

\hat{F}(x)=F(x)-\pi(F)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(P^{n}F(x)-\pi(F)\right)

(15)

where
P^{n}F(x)=\mathrm{E}[F(X_{n})|X_{0}=x]\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}\pi(F)
.

For the Gaussian invariant
proposals such as GI-RWM and GI-MALA we have the following
simplification of the Poisson
solution.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose for the target

\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)
we apply MH using
any of the proposals (

),

The

, where
\beta=1-\gamma
and the solution from (
15
)
becomes

\hat{F}(x)=F(x)-\pi(F)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\int\mathcal{N}(x_{n}|\beta^{n}x+(1-\beta^{n})\mu,(1-\beta^{2n})\Sigma)F(x_{n})dx_{n}-\pi(F)\right).

(16)

For several functions
F(x)

we can obtain closed-form
expressions of (

F(x)=x
we have

\hat{F}(x)=x-\mu+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\beta^{n}x+(1-\beta^{n})\mu-\mu\right)=\frac{x-\mu}{1-\beta}=\frac{x-\mu}{\gamma}

4
Variance reduction through the Poisson equation

Recall
the ergodic estimator in (
12
) for any non-Gaussian target
\pi
.
We assume that samples
\{X_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
have been collected by running MCMC using either the Gaussian invariant GI-RWM proposal
from (
7
)
or GI-MALA from (
8
)-(
9
). Since the target is not Gaussian, we cannot
solve the Poisson equation to make the variance zero, but we still hope to use a suitable function
G(x)

that can reduce the variance.
As
G(x)
, and for a given
F(x)
, we will use the exact (or the approximate truncated) solution to the Poisson equation for a Gaussian target, i.e., set
G(x)=\hat{F}(x)
to the corresponding entry from Table
1
.

Having specified
G(x)
, we still need to overcome that
the estimator (
12
)
requires the evaluation of the intractable integral

\int\alpha(X_{i},y)(G(y)-G(X_{i}))q(y|X_{i})dy,

for each sampled point
X_{i}
.
This is not feasible to compute analytically because the probability
\alpha(X_{i},y)
(as a function of
y
) can take values smaller than one and it depends on the intractable target
\pi
.
Since the transition kernel of the MH algorithm required one sample
Y_{i}\sim q(\cdot|X_{i})
every time the chain was at state
X_{i}
, an unbiased estimator of this integral
can be based on the sample size one estimator
\alpha(X_{i},Y_{i})(G(Y_{i})-G(X_{i}))
, where
all acceptance probabilities
\alpha(X_{i},Y_{i})
are available at no additional cost. This suggests that (
12
) can be unbiasedly approximated by

\mu_{n,G}(F)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\{F(X_{i})+\hat{\beta}_{1}\alpha(X_{i},Y_{i})(G(Y_{i})-G(X_{i}))\},

(21)

where we also introduced the scalar parameter
\hat{\beta}_{1}
in front of the control variate that we will specify shortly.
A final trick to improve the estimator is to use a further control variate to reduce the variance of (
21
), by reducing the variance of the sample size one estimate
\alpha(X_{i},Y_{i})(F(Y_{i})-F(X_{i}))
of the integral
\int\alpha(X_{i},y)(F(y)-F(X_{i}))q(y)dy
.
The idea, also used in

Alexopoulos et al. 2023
is rather
simple, if we remove

\alpha(X_{i},Y_{i})
we can tractably
center the function

G(Y_{i})-G(X_{i})
with respect to

Y_{i}\sim q(\cdot|X_{i})

according to
G(Y_{i})-G(X_{i})-\mathrm{E}_{q(y|X_{i})}[G(y)-G(X_{i})]=G(Y_{i})-\mathrm{E}_{q(\cdot|X_{i})}[G]
. Thus,
G(Y_{i})-\mathrm{E}_{q(\cdot|X_{i})}[G]
has zero expectation over
Y_{i}
and it can be used as a control variate, resulting in the estimator

\mu_{n,G}(F)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left\{F(X_{i})+\hat{\beta}_{1}\alpha(X_{i},Y_{i})(G(Y_{i})-G(X_{i}))+\hat{\beta}_{2}(G(Y_{i})-\mathrm{E}_{q(\cdot|X_{i})}[G])\right\}

(22)

where we introduced a second scalar parameter

\hat{\beta}_{2}
in front of the newly added
control variate. We observe that
when the target
\pi
is Gaussian so that
\alpha(X_{i},Y_{i})=1

and by setting
\hat{\beta}_{1}=1,\hat{\beta}_{2}=-1
the overall control variate (after the cancellation of

G(Y_{i})
) reduces to

-(G(X_{i})-\mathrm{E}_{q(\cdot|X_{i})}[G])

and thus (
22
) becomes the zero variance estimator from (
17
).

We specify jointly the parameters
\hat{\beta}_{1},\hat{\beta}_{2}
to minimize the asymptotic variance according to the estimator of
Dellaportas & Kontoyiannis 2012
. We first note that both of the control variates
H_{1}(X_{i},Y_{i})=\alpha(X_{i},Y_{i})(G(Y_{i})-G(X_{i}))
and
H_{2}(X_{i},Y_{i})=G(Y_{i})-\mathrm{E}_{q(\cdot|X_{i})}[G]
in the estimator (
22
) are defined over the Markov chain
(X_{i},Y_{i})
. Therefore, we choose
\hat{\beta}_{1},\hat{\beta}_{2}
that minimize the variance of (
22
) over the distribution with density proportional to
\pi(x)q(y|x)
under which
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
have zero mean. Then, if
\hat{\beta}=(\hat{\beta}_{1},\hat{\beta}_{2})
and we set

\hat{\beta}=\mathrm{K}_{n}^{-1}[\mu_{n}(FH)-\mu_{n}(F)\mu_{n}(H)],

where
H=(H_{1},H_{2})^{\top}
is a vector of functions and
\mu_{n}(H)=(\mu_{n}(H_{1}),\mu_{n}(H_{2}))^{\top}
and
\mu_{n}(FH)=(\mu_{n}(FH_{1}),\mu_{n}(FH_{2}))^{\top}
are vectors of ergodic means with
\mu_{n}(H_{j})
and
\mu_{n}(FH_{j})
defined as in equation (
1
) and
K_{n}
is a
2\times 2
matrix with
(K_{n})_{ij}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}H_{i}(X_{t})H_{j}(X_{t})
we have from
Dellaportas & Kontoyiannis 2012
that the variance in (
22
) is minimized.

4.1
An example on
F(x)=x

To give a full example of the general
estimator from (
22
)
let us consider the case
F(x)=x
, where

x
is the
d
-dimensional state vector.
We want to derive the specific
forms of the estimator for both
GI-RWM and GI-MALA
that use the Gaussian
invariant proposals in (
7
)-(
9
).
From Table
1
, as
G(x)
we will use
the function
\frac{x}{\gamma}
and therefore the
GI-RWM estimator takes the form

\mu_{n,G}(F)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left\{X_{i}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\hat{\beta}_{1}\circ\alpha(X_{i},Y_{i})(Y_{i}-X_{i})+\frac{1}{\gamma}\hat{\beta}_{2}\circ(Y_{i}-(1-\gamma)X_{i}-\gamma\mu)\right\}.

(23)

and GI-MALA with preconditioning

A_{x}
is

\mu_{n,G}(F)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left\{X_{i}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\hat{\beta}_{1}\circ\alpha(X_{i},Y_{i})(Y_{i}-X_{i})+\frac{1}{\gamma}\hat{\beta}_{2}\circ(Y_{i}-X_{i}-\gamma A_{x_{i}}\nabla\log\pi(X_{i}))\right\},

(24)

where
\circ
denotes
element-wise multiplication between vectors and

\hat{\beta}_{1},\hat{\beta}_{2}
are
d
-dimensional vectors of parameters estimated
separately for the
d

dimensions of
x
.

If the target is
\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)
, so that
\alpha(X_{i},Y_{i})=1
and
\hat{\beta}_{1}=1,\hat{\beta}_{2}=-1
, then the above estimators will reduce
to those in (
18
)-(
19
) having zero variance. Also,
the GI-MALA estimator is more back-box than
GI-RWM as it will have zero variance for any possible Gaussian target. In practice,
this means
that this estimator can have reduced variance even when a real non-Gaussian target is multi-modal as long as in different parts of the state space it can
be locally approximated by a Gaussian. An example of this is given in
supplementary material
using a mixture target distribution.

5
Application to latent Gaussian models

As a main application we consider latent Gaussian models which have the form

\pi(x)\propto\exp\{g(x)\}\mathcal{N}(x|0,\Sigma_{0}).

(25)

Latent Gaussian models are typically high-dimensional and applicable to a wide range of statistical problems; ranging from Gaussian process regression and classification
(
Williams & Rasmussen 1995
,
Neal 1999
)
to Bayesian inverse problems
(
Cotter et al. 2013
,
Beskos et al. 2017
)
. In these models, it is typically assumed that the observations given the latent state
x
are independent and thus
g(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}g(x^{(i)})
. To construct a proposal distribution for the target
\pi
we rely on the GI-MALA proposal in (
9
) where we specify a fast
approximation
A_{x}
to the negative inverse Hessian matrix. The exact negative Hessian is

-\nabla^{2}\log\pi(x)=-\nabla^{2}\log\mathcal{N}(x|0,\Sigma_{0})-\nabla^{2}g(x)=\Sigma_{0}^{-1}+L_{x}

where
L_{x}
is a diagonal
matrix with
entries

(L_{x})_{ii}=-\frac{\partial^{2}g(x)}{\partial x^{(i)}\partial x^{(i)}},\,\,\,i=1,\ldots,d
.
One option is to set
A_{x}
to the exact negative inverse Hessian
(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}+L_{x})^{-1}
.
However, this can be very expensive since if the
diagonal matrix

L_{x}
is non-isotropic (i.e., it is not a scalar times identity), then at each MCMC iteration we need to apply an
O(d^{3})
decomposition of
\Sigma_{0}^{-1}+L_{x}
.
In contrast,
current samplers
that preserve Gaussian invariance
to the prior

\mathcal{N}(x|0,\Sigma_{0})
, such as pCN

(
Beskos et al. 2008
,
Cotter et al. 2013
,
Neal 1999
)
and the methods in
Titsias & Papaspiliopoulos 2018
, have
O(d^{2})
cost per MCMC iteration.
Thus, to be efficient as these methods we will
build a fast
O(d^{2})

approximation to
(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}+L_{x})^{-1}
.

Without loss of generality
we will assume that the negative second
derivative matrix of log likelihood

-\nabla^{2}g(x)

does not contain a additive
constant term.
If it was
-\nabla^{2}g(x)=-\nabla^{2}\tilde{g}(x)+C
with

C
a constant diagonal matrix, then we could absorb
C
into
the Gaussian prior
covariance by redefining the Gaussian prior to have
covariance
\Sigma_{0}\leftarrow(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}+C)^{-1}
and using a new likelihood proportional to
\exp\{\tilde{g}(x)\}
. Under this assumption,
at each MCMC iteration
we introduce as a preconditioning the matrix

A_{x}=(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}+\delta_{x}I_{d})^{-1},\ \ \delta_{x}=\text{mean}\{(L_{x})_{11},\ldots,(L_{x})_{dd}\},

(26)

where
\delta_{x}

is the mean of all diagonal elements of
L_{x}
.
Based on this preconditioning the proposal in (
9
) takes the form

q(y|x)=\mathcal{N}(y|x+\gamma A_{x}(\nabla g(x)-\Sigma_{0}^{-1}x),(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})A_{x}),

(27)

where we used that

\nabla\log\pi(x)=\nabla g(x)-\Sigma_{0}^{-1}x
. The following
Proposition
2
shows how the MCMC algorithm with proposal
(
27
)
can be implemented with
O(d^{2})
cost per iteration.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\pi(x)\propto\exp\{g(x)\}\mathcal{N}(x|0,\Sigma_{0})
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
and
\Sigma_{0}=U\Lambda U^{\top}

is a precomputed eigenvalue decomposition of the prior covariance matrix
\Sigma_{0}
with
\lambda_{i}=(\Lambda)_{ii}
denoting an eigenvalue. Each MH iteration with proposal (
27
) can be implemented with
O(d^{2})
cost as

(i)

Sampling from the proposal
q(y|x)
is done as

y=(1-\gamma)x+U\left[\gamma\Lambda(\Lambda+\delta_{x}^{-1}I)^{-1}\zeta_{x}+\sqrt{\delta_{x}^{-1}(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})}\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Lambda+\delta_{x}^{-1}I)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon\right],

where
\zeta_{x}=U^{\top}(x+\delta_{x}^{-1}\nabla g(x))

and
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
.

(ii)

The acceptance probability
a(x,y)
is computed as

a(x,y)=\text{min}\left(1,\exp\{g(y)-g(x)+h(x,y)-h(y,x)\}\right),

where

h(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta_{x}}{(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})}||y-x-\frac{\gamma}{\delta_{x}}\nabla g(x)||^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log(\lambda_{i}\delta_{x}+1)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\gamma}{(2-\gamma)}\zeta_{x}^{\top}\left(\Lambda+\delta_{x}^{-1}I_{d}\right)^{-1}\zeta_{x}.

The proof is given in the supplementary material.
Note that the assumption we made that the log likelihood

g(x)
has no constant additive terms in the second derivatives and if it has then this is absorbed to

\Sigma_{0}
,
guarantees that the
preconditioning
A_{x}
from
(
19
) is Gaussian optimal (see Definition
2
). This means that for conjugate
latent Gaussian models, such
as Gaussian process regression with log-likehood
g(x)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}||y-x||^{2}+const
,
the proposal from (
27
) is reversible to the target and in fact for
\gamma=1
gives i.i.d. sampling. In such case we can also obtain zero variance estimation
by using exact solutions to Poisson equations.

The ergodic estimators for the above GI-MALA sampler follow the general form in
(
22
). For instance, if
F(x)=x
the estimator is
(
24
).

7
Theory

We provide precise conditions under which our algorithm is geometrically ergodic and derive convergence rates for the truncated approximation (
20
) to the solution of the Poisson equation. We also establish that our final estimators follow the law of large numbers (LLN) and the central limit theorem (CLT). Finally, we study the optimal acceptance rate and choice of
\gamma
for a finite dimension
d
. We follow the developments of
Roberts & Tweedie 1996a
,
Roberts & Tweedie 1996b
who provided the uniform geometric ergodicity of MALA under certain restrictive conditions. This theory was later extended by
Roy & Zhang 2023
to address position-dependent versions of MALA. Furthermore, recent work by
Oliviero-Durmus & Moulines 2024
provides a quantitative analysis of convergence rates for MALA. To establish LLN and CLT, we directly follow the theory developed by
Dellaportas & Kontoyiannis 2012
. The works of

Gelman et al. 1997
,
Roberts & Rosenthal 1998
,
Roberts et al. 2001
and
Titsias & Dellaportas 2019
inspired the development of the optimal scaling results.

All proofs can be found in the supplementary material.

7.1
Geometric ergodicity

Let
\|x\|
denote the Euclidean norm of a vector
x\in\Re^{d}
and

\|A\|:=\sup_{x\in\Re^{d},x\neq 0}\{\|Ax\|/\|x\|\}
denote the matrix norm of a matrix
A\in\Re^{d\times d}
.
For a measurable Borel function
f
and some function
V:\Re^{d}\rightarrow[1,\infty)
, the
V
-norm of
f
is given by
\|f\|_{V}:=\sup_{x\in\Re^{d}}|f(x)|/V(x)
. We define the function space

L_{V}^{\infty}:=\{f:\|f\|_{V}<\infty\}

and the
V
-norm distance

\|P-Q\|_{V}:=\sup_{f\in L_{V}^{\infty}}|Pf(x)-Qf(x)|

for some measures
P
and
Q
.
Moreover, denote by
R_{A}(x):=\{y:\pi(x)q(y|x)\leq\pi(y)q(x|y)\}
the acceptance region of the GI-MALA algorithm and by
R_{R}(x):=R_{A}(x)^{c}
the potential rejection region. Finally, define
I(x):=\{y:\|y\|\leq\|x\|\}
,
\mu(x)=x+\gamma A_{x}\nabla\log\pi(x)
and
\Sigma_{x}=(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})A_{x}
.
The following assumptions are convenient for the proof of the geometric ergodicity of our algorithm, and they have been discussed in some detail in
Roberts & Tweedie 1996a
,
Roberts & Tweedie 1996b
and
Roy & Zhang 2023
.

Assumption 1

(A1)
.

For all x, there exists a constant
k>0
, such that

s:=2k\sup_{x}\|\Sigma_{x}\|<1

and

\lim_{\|x\|\rightarrow\infty}\inf\left\{\|x\|^{2}-\frac{\|\mu(x)\|^{2}}{1-s}\right\}>\frac{d}{2k}\log\frac{1}{1-s}.

Moreover,
\mu(x)
is bounded on bounded sets.

Assumption 2

(A2)
.

The acceptance region
R_{A}(x)
converges inward in the proposal
q(y|x)
:

\lim_{\|x\|\rightarrow\infty}\int_{A(x)\Delta I(x)}q(y|x)dy=0

where
R_{A}(x)\Delta I(x)=(R_{A}(x)\cap I(x))/(R_{A}(x)\cup I(x))
is the symmetric difference.

Theorem 1
.

Under (A
1
), (A

) and

7.4
Optimal scaling

The parameter
\gamma
plays the role of the global step size. Following optimal scaling results
(
Roberts et al. 2001
)
, the standard practice for RWM and MALA is to tune
\gamma
to reach acceptance rates
0.234
and
0.574
. However,
these guidelines may not be relevant
for the Gaussian invariant proposals, where optimal acceptance rates could vary with the target. An extreme case is obtained, by Proposition
3
, when the target is Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(x|\mu,\Sigma)
, the acceptance rate is
1.0
(since all samples are accepted)
and the optimal step size is
\gamma=1
,
which results in i.i.d. sampling.
For non-Gaussian targets, we observe in our experiments that by tuning
\gamma
to reach acceptance rates higher than those of RWM and MALA we achieve better performance for GI-RWM and GI-MALA.
For example, in our GI-MALA experiments we found that the optimal
\gamma
is obtained when the acceptance rate is around
0.8
.
Consider another extreme case: Let the preconditioning matrix be the identity matrix

in the GI-MALA proposal (

decays faster with dimension than the term involving

\gamma^{2}
scales as
O(d^{-2/3})
while
2\gamma
scales as
O(d^{-1/3})

8
Discussion

We have presented general-purpose Gaussian
invariant MCMC proposal distributions,
such as the gradient-based GI-MALA methods, and we have studied similarities as well as differences with standard MALA samplers
(
Roberts & Stramer 2002
)
and second order or Hessian-based
methods
(
Girolami & Calderhead 2011
,
Petra et al. 2014
)
.
An interesting property of ergodic estimation using these
samplers is that we can exploit
analytical solutions
to the Poisson equation
to construct control variates
for variance reduction.
The main application we considered in latent Gaussian
models shows that we can easily apply these methods to high-dimensional targets and achieve
large effective sample sizes and low cost variance reduction.

In the theoretical results
the optimal scaling analysis reveals an importance difference
between Gaussian invariant MCMC
and standard RWM and MALA schemes. While for RWM and MALA there exist well-established specific optimal acceptance rates based on diffusion limits

(
Roberts et al. 2001
)
, for Gaussian invariant MCMC the acceptance rates depend on the Gaussianity of the target.
Therefore, an approximate optimal scaling is derived under finite-dimensional assumptions, with a detailed characterization of its dependence on non-Gaussian features of the target density.

For future work it would be useful to extend the automatic selection of the position-dependent preconditioner
A_{x}
in GI-MALA beyond latent Gaussian models, in order to balance computational cost and statistical efficiency for generic targets. Also it would be interesting to consider alternative ways to select the value of
\gamma
by applying, for instance, some gradient-based optimization procedure to optimize the global speed measure.

Proof of Proposition 2

We first prove (i). Recall the form of the proposal

q(y|x)=\mathcal{N}(y|x+\gamma A_{x}(\nabla g(x)-\Sigma_{0}^{-1}x),(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})A_{x}).

The mean can be written as

x+\gamma A_{x}(\nabla g(x)-\Sigma_{0}^{-1}x-\delta_{x}x+\delta_{x}x)=x+\gamma A_{x}(\nabla g(x)-A_{x}^{-1}x+\delta_{x}x)

which further simplifies as

(1-\gamma)x+\delta_{x}\gamma A_{x}(x+\delta_{x}^{-1}\nabla g(x))
. Since
\Sigma_{0}=U\Lambda U^{\top}

we have

A_{x}=(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}+\delta_{x})^{-1}=\frac{1}{\delta_{x}}U\Lambda(\Lambda+\delta_{x}^{-1}I)^{-1}U^{\top}.

Then, if we denote

\zeta_{x}=U^{\top}(x+\delta_{x}^{-1}\nabla g(x))
,
we observe that the mean of the proposal is written as

(1-\gamma)x+U\gamma\Lambda(\Lambda+\delta_{x}^{-1}I)^{-1}\zeta_{x},

while

y=(1-\gamma)x+U\left[\gamma\Lambda(\Lambda+\delta_{x}^{-1}I)^{-1}\zeta_{x}+\sqrt{\delta_{x}^{-1}(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})}\Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Lambda+\delta_{x}^{-1}I)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon\right],\ \ \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})

generates a sample from the proposal. Note that the computation of
y
requires
only a single matrix vector multiplications of cost
O(d^{2})
to obtain
U\times[\ldots]
. The rest operations are
O(d)
since
\Lambda
is diagonal matrix.
Note also that
\zeta_{x}
has been precomputed and stored from the previous step; see next.

To prove (ii) we need to show that the ratio

\frac{N(y|0,\Sigma_{0})q(x|y)}{N(x|0,\Sigma_{0})q(y|x)}=\exp\{h(x,y)-h(y,x)\}
.
We first observe that an alternative way to write the
proposal is as

q(y|x)=\frac{\mathcal{N}\left(y|x+(\gamma/\delta_{x})\nabla g(x),\frac{2\gamma-\gamma^{2}}{\delta_{x}}\right)\mathcal{N}(y|(1-\gamma)x,(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})\Sigma_{0})}{\mathcal{N}\left(0|\gamma\zeta_{x},(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})(\Sigma_{0}+\delta_{x}^{-1})\right)}.

Since
\mathcal{N}(y|(1-\gamma)x,(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})\Sigma_{0})
is invariant to the
Gaussian prior, these terms
together with the Gaussian
priors cancel out in the ratio which simplifies as

\frac{N(y|0,\Sigma_{0})q(x|y)}{N(x|0,\Sigma_{0})q(y|x)}=\frac{\mathcal{N}(x|y+(\gamma/\delta_{y})\nabla g(y),\frac{2\gamma-\gamma^{2}}{\delta_{y}})}{\mathcal{N}(0|\gamma\zeta_{y},(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})(\Sigma_{0}+\delta_{y}^{-1}))}\times\frac{\mathcal{N}(0|\gamma\zeta_{x},(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})(\Sigma_{0}+\delta_{x}^{-1}))}{\mathcal{N}(y|x+(\gamma/\delta_{x})\nabla g(x),\frac{2\gamma-\gamma^{2}}{\delta_{x}})}

from which is straightforward to obtain the final result

\frac{N(y|0,\Sigma_{0})q(x|y)}{N(x|0,\Sigma_{0})q(y|x)}=\exp\{h(x,y)-h(y,x)\}

where

h(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta_{x}}{(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})}||y-x-\frac{\gamma}{\delta_{x}}\nabla g(x)||^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log(\lambda_{i}\delta_{x}+1)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\gamma}{(2-\gamma)}\zeta_{x}^{\top}\left(\Lambda+\delta_{x}^{-1}I_{d}\right)^{-1}\zeta_{x}.

To obtain the ratio we need to perform a single
O(d^{2})
matrix
vector multiplication to obtain

\zeta_{y}
for the newly proposed
sample, while all remaining computations are
O(d)
.
Therefore, in total only two

O(d^{2})
operations are needed per MCMC iteration.

Proof.

Recalling the definition of the speed measure given in

. Therefore, the speed measure becomes

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=C(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})^{\kappa d/2}E_{\pi_{d},q}[\alpha(x,y)]

is derived from the Taylor expansion with respect to both
\gamma

\epsilon

0
via MATHEMATICA 13.3 software. Thus, for any constant
