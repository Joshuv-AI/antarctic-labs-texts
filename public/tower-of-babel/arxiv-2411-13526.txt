The density and distribution of CM elliptic curves over ℚ {\mathbb{Q}}
Adrian Barquero-Sanchez and Jimmy Calvo-Monge

Abstract
. In this paper we study the density and distribution of CM elliptic curves over ℚ {\mathbb{Q}} . In particular, we prove that the natural density of CM elliptic curves over ℚ {\mathbb{Q}} , when ordered by naive height, is zero. Furthermore, we analyze the distribution of these curves among the thirteen possible CM orders of class number one. Our results show that asymptotically, 100 % 100\% of them have complex multiplication by the order ℤ ⁡ [ − 1 + − 3 2 ] {\mathbb{Z}}\left[\frac{-1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}\right] , that is, have j j -invariant 0. We conduct this analysis within two different families of representatives for the ℚ {\mathbb{Q}} -isomorphism classes of CM elliptic curves: one commonly used in the literature and another constructed using the theory of twists. As part of our proofs, we give asymptotic formulas for the number of elliptic curves with a given j j -invariant and bounded naive height.

1.
Introduction

It has long been recognized by experts in the theory of elliptic curves, that the elliptic curves that have complex multiplication are exceedingly rare among all elliptic curves. Nevertheless, these curves possess many properties that make them special and gives them extra structure, which is something that has been exploited many times, for example making the family of CM elliptic curves the first for which more general theorems have been proved; for instance some of the first cases of the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, proved by Coates and Wiles in
[
CW77
]
and the proof of modularity of CM elliptic curves by Shimura in
[
Shi71
]
. This is why over the past century, through the efforts of many mathematicians, the theory of elliptic curves with complex multiplication has flourished into a rich and very satisfying field, whose elegance was underscored by David Hilbert’s famous quote, as recalled by Olga Taussky in Hilbert’s obituary in the journal Nature
[
Tau43
]
:

It is interesting to recall that, in connection with a lecture by Prof. R. Fueter at the 1932 Zurich Congress, Hilbert asserted that the theory of complex multiplication (of elliptic modular functions) which forms a powerful link between number theory and analysis, is not only the most beautiful part of mathematics but also of all science.

In this paper, we aim to investigate the density and distribution of CM elliptic curves over
\mathbb{Q}
in greater detail from an analytic number theory perspective. To make our study precise, we consider the family
\mathcal{E}
consisting of all elliptic curves
E_{A,B}
given by the equation
y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B
, where
A,B\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfy the condition
\Delta_{E_{A,B}}=-16(4A^{3}+27B^{2})\neq 0
, and there exists no prime number
p
such that
p^{4}|A
and
p^{6}|B
. The last condition on the prime divisors of
A
and
B
ensures that each elliptic curve
E/{\mathbb{Q}}
is isomorphic over
{\mathbb{Q}}
to a unique elliptic curve in the family
\mathcal{E}
. In other words, the family
\mathcal{E}
serves as a complete set of representatives for the
{\mathbb{Q}}
-isomorphism classes of elliptic curves over
{\mathbb{Q}}
(see e.g.
[
Was08
, Proposition 2.19]
,
[
Sil09
, p. 45]
and
[
Poo13
, §1.3]
).

In particular, we want to study how large certain subsets of
\mathcal{E}
are. To make this precise, we use the notion of natural density, which we define below. First, since the sets that we want to study are infinite, we need a way to bound their size so that we can count finite subsets. We do this by employing the
naive height

\displaystyle h^{\textrm{naive}}(E_{A,B}):=\max{\{4|A|^{3},27|B|^{2}\}}.

Then, for any
X>0
we let
\mathcal{E}(X):=\{E_{A,B}\in\mathcal{E}\;|\;h^{\textrm{naive}}(E_{A,B})\leq X\}
.
Now, given any subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
, we define its
natural density
by

\displaystyle d(\mathcal{S}):=\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{\#(\mathcal{E}(X)\cap\mathcal{S})}{\#\mathcal{E}(X)},

if the limit exists.

Now, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{cm}}:=\{E_{A,B}\in\mathcal{E}\;|\;\text{$E_{A,B}$ has complex multiplication}\}

and define
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{cm}}(X):=\mathcal{E}(X)\cap\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{cm}}
.

1.2.
The distribution of CM elliptic curves

The basic theory of elliptic curves classifies the possibilities for the endomorphism ring
\operatorname{End}(E)
of an elliptic curve
E/F
over a field
F
. In particular, if the
\operatorname{char}(F)\neq 0
, the endomorphism ring
\operatorname{End}(E)
is either isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}
or to an order
\mathcal{O}
in an imaginary quadratic field (see e.g.
[
Sil09
, Corollary III.9.4]
).

We recall that if
K
is an imaginary quadratic field with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, an order in
K
is a subring
\mathcal{O}\subset K
such that
\mathcal{O}
is a finitely generated
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module and
\mathcal{O}
contains a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-basis of
K
. Moreover, it is known that an order
\mathcal{O}
in
K
is a subring of the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
and has finite index in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. In particular, if
f=[\mathcal{O}_{K}\colon\mathcal{O}]
then

\mathcal{O}={\mathbb{Z}}+f\mathcal{O}_{K}.

The index
f
is also called the conductor of the order
\mathcal{O}
. A good reference for these basic facts is the beautiful book of Cox
[
Cox22
, §7]
. Now, an important result in the theory of complex multiplication of elliptic curves says that if
E/{\mathbb{C}}
is an elliptic curve with complex multiplication, then
j(E)\in\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{{\mathbb{Z}}}
is an algebraic integer and moreover, if
\operatorname{End}(E)\cong\mathcal{O}
for some order
\mathcal{O}
in an imaginary quadratic field
K
, then

(1.3)

\displaystyle[{\mathbb{Q}}(j(E))\colon{\mathbb{Q}}]=[K(j(E))\colon K]=h(\mathcal{O}),

where
h(\mathcal{O})=\#Cl(\mathcal{O})
, i.e., the class number of the order
\mathcal{O}
(see e.g.
[
Hus04
, §12.4]
or
[
Sil94
, Theorem II.4.3]
). Furthermore, if
E/{\mathbb{Q}}
is an elliptic curve with
\operatorname{End}(E)\cong\mathcal{O}
, the degree formula (
1.3
) implies that
h(\mathcal{O})=1
because
j(E)\in{\mathbb{Q}}
. Thus, for elliptic curves
E/{\mathbb{Q}}
with complex multiplication, the endomorphism ring
\operatorname{End}(E)
can only be isomorphic to an order
\mathcal{O}
with class number
h(\mathcal{O})=1
.

Now, it is known that
h(\mathcal{O})
is an integer multiple of
h(\mathcal{O}_{K})
and moreover, that it satisfies the explicit formula

(1.4)

\displaystyle h(\mathcal{O})=\frac{h(\mathcal{O}_{K})f}{\left[\mathcal{O}_{K}^{*}:\mathcal{O}^{*}\right]}\prod_{p\mid f}\left(1-\left(\frac{d_{K}}{p}\right)\frac{1}{p}\right),

where
d_{K}
denotes the discriminant of
K
and
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{*}
and
\mathcal{O}^{*}
denote the corresponding groups of units (see e.g.
[
Cox22
, Theorem 7.24]
). Hence, given that
h(\mathcal{O})
is an integer multiple of
h(\mathcal{O}_{K})
, a necessary condition for
h(\mathcal{O})
to be one is that
h(\mathcal{O}_{K})
= 1, that is, the imaginary quadratic field
K
must have class number 1. In this respect, the famous resolution of Gauss’ class number one problem by Heegner in 1952
[
Hee52
]
and later independently in 1966 by Baker
[
Bak66
]
and Stark
[
Sta67b
,
Sta67a
]
, determined that there are exactly nine imaginary quadratic fields
K={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{d_{K}})
with
h(\mathcal{O}_{K})=1
; namely those of discriminant

d_{K}\in\{-3,-4,-7,-8,-11,-19,-43,-67,-163\}.

Then, from this and the explicit formula (
1.4
), it can be proved that there are exactly thirteen orders
\mathcal{O}
of imaginary quadratic fields with class number
h(\mathcal{O})=1
(see e.g.
[
Cox22
, Theorem 7.30]
). More precisely, the thirteen orders of class number one are determined by the first two columns of Table
3

K
and the conductor
f
of the order
\mathcal{O}={\mathbb{Z}}+f\mathcal{O}_{K}
. This table also includes a representative in short Weierstrass form for each
{\mathbb{C}}
-isomorphism class of CM elliptic curves over
{\mathbb{Q}}
and the corresponding LMFDB label
[

How are the CM elliptic curves over
{\mathbb{Q}}

, let

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{j}:=\{E_{A,B}\in\mathcal{E}\;|\;j(E_{A,B})=j\}.

In other words,
\mathcal{E}_{j}
is the subset of all elliptic curves in
\mathcal{E}
with
j

\mathcal{J}^{\textrm{cm}}
be the set of all the possible thirteen

{\mathbb{Q}}
, as appearing in Table

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\textrm{cm}}=\{

\displaystyle 0,1728,-3375,8000,-32768,54000,287496,-12288000,

\displaystyle 16581375,-884736,-884736000,-147197952000,-262537412640768000\}.

For a given
j\in\mathcal{J}^{\textrm{cm}}

has complex multiplication by the order corresponding to the given CM

j

we have

\mathcal{E}_{j}\subset\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{cm}}.

We will also let
\mathcal{E}_{j}(X):=\mathcal{E}_{j}\cap\mathcal{E}(X)
. Then, the classification of CM elliptic curves over
{\mathbb{Q}}
implies that we can decompose the families
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{cm}}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{cm}}(X)
as disjoint unions given by

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{cm}}=\coprod_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{\textrm{cm}}}\mathcal{E}_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{cm}}(X)=\coprod_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{\textrm{cm}}}\mathcal{E}_{j}(X)

for every
X>0
.

1.4

1.4

\operatorname{End}(E)\cong\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{-1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}\right]

-invariant 0 and bounded height as the bound on the height increases. We confirm what the trend in Table

Theorem 1.5
.

The elliptic curves with
j
-invariant 0 comprise
100\%
of all the CM elliptic curves in
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{cm}}
. More precisely, the natural density of the family
\mathcal{E}_{0}
in

\displaystyle d(\mathcal{E}_{0})=\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{\#\mathcal{E}_{0}(X)}{\#\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{cm}}(X)}=1.

2.
The distribution of CM elliptic curves over
{\mathbb{Q}}
in the family
\mathcal{E}

As stated in the introduction, Brumer in
[
Bru92
, Lemma 4.3]
provided a count of elliptic curves in the family
\mathcal{E}
with height bounded by
X
using a different normalization for the naive height than the one we use. For the reader’s convenience, we adapt Brumer’s argument to align with our height normalization. This adjustment not only ensures consistency within our framework but also we describe in more detail some ideas that lay the groundwork for our subsequent enumeration of CM elliptic curves.

Theorem 2.1
.

The number of elliptic curves in
\mathcal{E}(X)
satisfies the asymptotic formula

(2.1)

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{E}(X)=\frac{2^{4/3}}{3^{3/2}\zeta(10)}X^{5/6}+O(X^{7/12}),

as
X\to\infty
, where
\zeta(s)
denotes the Riemann zeta function.

Proof.

First we define some families of curves. Recall that for any point
(A,B)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
we define the curve
E_{A,B}\colon y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B
. Then, for
X>0
let

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(X)

\displaystyle=\{E_{A,B}\;|\;(A,B)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2},\,h^{\textrm{naive}}(E_{A,B})\leq X\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(X)

\displaystyle=\{E_{A,B}\in\mathcal{D}(X)\;|\;(A,B)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\smallsetminus\{(0,0)\}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(X)

\displaystyle=\{E_{A,B}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(X)\;|\;\text{there's no prime $p\in{\mathbb{Z}}$ such that $p^{4}\mid A$ and $p^{6}\mid B$}\},\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(X)

\displaystyle=\{E_{A,B}\in\mathcal{M}(X)\;|\;\text{$E_{A,B}$ is singular}\}.

Note then that
\mathcal{E}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{M}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{D}(X)
and moreover we have

\mathcal{E}(X)=\{E_{A,B}\in\mathcal{M}(X)\;|\;\Delta_{E_{A,B}}\neq 0\}.

Next, for
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we define
d*E_{A,B}:=E_{d^{4}A,d^{6}B}
. Observe in particular that from the definition of the naive height we have

h^{\textrm{naive}}(d*E_{A,B})=d^{12}\cdot h^{\textrm{naive}}(E_{A,B}).

Therefore
h^{\textrm{naive}}(d*E_{A,B})\leq X
if and only if
h^{\textrm{naive}}(E_{A,B})\leq X/d^{12}
. Using this, it follows that every curve in the family
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(X)
can be written uniquely as a twist
d*E_{A,B}
for a unique curve
E_{A,B}\in\mathcal{M}(d^{-12}X)
. Therefore we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(X)=\coprod_{d=1}^{\lfloor X^{1/12}\rfloor}d*\mathcal{M}(d^{-12}X)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{M}(X)=\mathcal{E}(X)\coprod\mathcal{S}(X).

We note that
h^{\textrm{naive}}(E_{A,B})\leq X
if and only if
|A|\leq\dfrac{X^{1/3}}{2^{2/3}}
and
|B|\leq\dfrac{X^{1/2}}{3^{3/2}}
. Hence, the bound on the naive height
h^{\textrm{naive}}(E_{A,B})\leq X
is equivalent to the lattice point
(A,B)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
lying on a rectangular box centered at the origin.

Now, for the singular curves in
\mathcal{M}(X)
we claim that

(2.3)

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{S}(X)=O(X^{1/6}).

Indeed, a curve
E_{A,B}
is singular if and only if
\Delta_{E_{A,B}}=-16(4A^{3}+27B^{2})=0
. Then, if
\Delta_{E_{A,B}}=0
, the number
A
must be negative and moreover, the real points
(a,b)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that
4a^{3}+27b^{2}=0
can be parametrized by
a=-3w^{2}
and
b=2w^{3}
for
w\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then, using this we can easily see that we must have
|w|\leq\dfrac{X^{1/6}}{2^{1/3}3^{1/2}}
to ensure
h^{\textrm{naive}}(E_{A,B})\leq X
and that
w
must belong to
{\mathbb{Z}}
to have
(a,b)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then, counting the number of integers
w\in{\mathbb{Z}}
satisfying the inequality
|w|\leq\dfrac{X^{1/6}}{2^{1/3}3^{1/2}}
, we conclude that
\#\mathcal{S}(X)\leq 2\dfrac{X^{1/6}}{2^{1/3}3^{1/2}}=\dfrac{2^{2/3}}{3^{1/2}}X^{1/6}
, which proves the claim (
2.3
).

Next, observe that
\#\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(X)
is equal to the number of lattice points
(A,B)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\smallsetminus{\{(0,0)\}}
within the box determined by the previous inequalities on
|A|
and
|B|
. Thus we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(X)=\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{X^{1/3}}{2^{2/3}}\right\rfloor+1\right)\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{X^{1/2}}{3^{3/2}}\right\rfloor+1\right)-1.

Now, a form of the Möbius inversion formula is the following. If
F(x)
and
G(x)
are complex valued functions defined in the interval
[1,\infty)
such that

G(x)=\sum_{1\leq n\leq x}F\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)

for every
x\geq 1
, then

F(x)=\sum_{1\leq n\leq x}\mu(n)G\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)

for every
x\geq 1
. Therefore, from the decomposition (
2.2
) we have

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(X)=\sum_{1\leq d\leq X^{1/12}}\#(d*\mathcal{M}(d^{-12}X))=\sum_{1\leq d\leq X^{1/12}}\#\mathcal{M}(d^{-12}X),

and using the Möbius inversion formula just cited, we get

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{M}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq d\leq X^{1/12}}\mu(d)\#\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(d^{-12}X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq d\leq X^{1/12}}\mu(d)\left\{\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{(d^{-12}X)^{1/3}}{2^{2/3}}\right\rfloor+1\right)\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{(d^{-12}X)^{1/2}}{3^{3/2}}\right\rfloor+1\right)-1\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq d\leq X^{1/12}}\mu(d)\left\{\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{X^{1/3}}{d^{4}2^{2/3}}\right\rfloor+1\right)\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{X^{1/2}}{d^{6}3^{3/2}}\right\rfloor+1\right)-1\right\}.

Now, using the fact that
\lfloor X\rfloor=X+O(1)
for every
X
, we get

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{M}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq d\leq X^{1/12}}\mu(d)\left\{\left(2\frac{X^{1/3}}{d^{4}2^{2/3}}+O(1)\right)\left(2\frac{X^{1/2}}{d^{6}3^{3/2}}+O(1)\right)-1\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq d\leq X^{1/12}}\mu(d)\left(4\frac{X^{5/6}}{d^{10}2^{2/3}3^{3/2}}+O(X^{1/2})\right)

(2.5)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2^{4/3}}{3^{3/2}}\sum_{1\leq d\leq X^{1/12}}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{10}}\right)X^{5/6}+\left(\sum_{1\leq d\leq X^{1/12}}\mu(d)\right)O(X^{1/2}).

We will now give an asymptotic formula for the expression inside the first parentheses in (
2.5
) and a bound for the expression inside the second parentheses. Thus, for the expression inside the first parentheses we have

\displaystyle\frac{2^{4/3}}{3^{3/2}}\sum_{1\leq d\leq X^{1/12}}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{10}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{4/3}}{3^{3/2}}\left(\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{10}}-\sum_{d>X^{1/12}}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{10}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{4/3}}{3^{3/2}\zeta(10)}-\frac{2^{4/3}}{3^{3/2}}\sum_{d>X^{1/12}}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{10}},

where we used the well known formula

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\zeta(s)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{s}}

for the reciprocal of the Riemann zeta function.

Next, using the asymptotic bound (see e.g.
[
Apo76
, Theorem 3.2]
)

\displaystyle\sum_{n>x}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=O(x^{1-s}),\quad\text{for }s>1\text{ and }x\geq 1,

we see that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{d>X^{1/12}}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{10}}\right|\leq\sum_{d>X^{1/12}}\frac{|\mu(d)|}{d^{10}}\leq\sum_{d>X^{1/12}}\frac{1}{d^{10}}=O(X^{-9/12}).

Hence we have

(2.6)

\displaystyle\frac{2^{4/3}}{3^{3/2}}\sum_{1\leq d\leq X^{1/12}}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{10}}=\frac{2^{4/3}}{3^{3/2}\zeta(10)}+O(X^{-9/12}).

Now, for the expression inside the second parentheses in (
2.5
) we have the trivial bound

(2.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq d\leq X^{1/12}}\mu(d)=O(X^{1/12}).

Therefore, substituting (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) in (
2.5
) we obtain

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{M}(X)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2^{4/3}}{3^{3/2}\zeta(10)}+O(X^{-9/12})\right)X^{5/6}+O(X^{1/12})\cdot O(X^{1/2})

(2.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{4/3}}{3^{3/2}\zeta(10)}X^{5/6}+O(X^{7/12})

Finally, from (
2.2
) we have that

(2.9)

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{E}(X)=\#\mathcal{M}(X)-\#\mathcal{S}(X).

Consequently, combining this formula with the asymptotic formulas (
2.8
) and (
2.3
) we obtain

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{E}(X)=\frac{2^{4/3}}{3^{3/2}\zeta(10)}X^{5/6}+O(X^{7/12}),

which proves the theorem.
∎

k
-free. We recall that an integer

is said to be

-free (for

) if there is no prime

such that

, that is, if

is not divisible by a perfect

[

. A more modern reference is the book of Montgomery and Vaughan
[

.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
X\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
and
k\geq 2
an integer, let
Q_{k}(X)
denote the number of positive
k
-free integers

such that

. Then

\displaystyle Q_{k}(X)=\frac{1}{\zeta(k)}X+O(X^{1/k})

as
X\to\infty
, where
\zeta(s)
is the Riemann zeta function.

Moreover, in our arguments we will also need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
a=\frac{p}{q}\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}
with
p,q\in{\mathbb{Z}}
coprime integers and consider the cuspidal cubic
\mathcal{C}_{a}:y^{2}=ax^{3}
. Moreover, let
\mathcal{C}_{a}({\mathbb{Z}})
be the set of integral points on
\mathcal{C}_{a}

(x_{0},y_{0})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
such that

. Then for every

we have

(2.10)

\displaystyle\#\{(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathcal{C}_{a}({\mathbb{Z}})\;|\;|x_{0}|\leq T\}\leq 2\sigma_{0}(q)\sqrt{|pq|}T^{1/2},

where
\sigma_{0}(q):=\sum\limits_{d\mid q}1
is the number of positive divisors of

(2.11)

\displaystyle\#\{(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathcal{C}_{a}({\mathbb{Z}})\;|\;|x_{0}|\leq T\}=O(T^{1/2}).

Proof.

By projecting from the double point at the origin in the curve
\mathcal{C}_{a}
onto the vertical line

as in Figure

\left(x(t),y(t)\right)=\left(\dfrac{t^{2}}{a},\dfrac{t^{3}}{a}\right)
for

.

x
y
\mathcal{C}_{a}\colon y^{2}=ax^{3}
x=1
y=tx
(1,t)
\left(\dfrac{t^{2}}{a},\dfrac{t^{3}}{a}\right)

. The graph shows a typical curve with

Indeed, note that when we substitute

into the equation for

\displaystyle t^{2}x^{2}=ax^{3}\iff x^{2}(ax-t^{2})=0\iff x=0\quad\text{or}\quad x=\dfrac{t^{2}}{a}.

This parametrization gives us a bijection

\displaystyle\varphi\colon

\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{a}

(2.12)

\displaystyle t\longmapsto P_{t}:=\left(\frac{t^{2}}{a},\frac{t^{3}}{a}\right).

Moreover, note that restricting
\varphi
to
{\mathbb{Q}}
also gives us a bijection

, where
\mathcal{C}_{a}({\mathbb{Q}})

Suppose now that

\displaystyle\frac{q}{p}\cdot\frac{r_{0}^{2}}{s_{0}^{2}}=x_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{q}{p}\cdot\frac{r_{0}^{3}}{s_{0}^{3}}=y_{0},

or equivalently, that

(2.13)

\displaystyle qr_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=x_{0}ps_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle qr_{0}^{3}

\displaystyle=y_{0}ps_{0}^{3}.

Then, using the coprimality condition

, the first equation in (

s_{0}^{2}
divides
q
and hence
s_{0}
divides
q
. Therefore we have

\displaystyle\#\{(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathcal{C}_{a}({\mathbb{Z}})\;|\;|x_{0}|\leq T\}

(2.14)

\displaystyle\leq\#\left\{t_{0}=\frac{r_{0}}{s_{0}}\;|\;\text{$r_{0},s_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}$ coprime, with $s_{0}\mid q$ and $|t_{0}|\leq|a|^{1/2}T^{1/2}$}\right\}.

Then, for each integer

Therefore, applying this to every positive divisor of

, we use (

\displaystyle\#\{(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathcal{C}_{a}({\mathbb{Z}})\;|\;|x_{0}|\leq T\}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{0}(q)\cdot\#\left\{r_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}\;|\;|r_{0}|\leq\sqrt{|pq|}T^{1/2}\right\}\leq 2\sigma_{0}(q)\sqrt{|pq|}T^{1/2}.

We then have proved that

\displaystyle\#\{(x_{0},y_{0})\in C_{a}({\mathbb{Z}})\;|\;|x_{0}|\leq T\}=O(T^{1/2}).

Theorem 2.4
.

Let

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{E}_{j}(X)=O(X^{1/m(j)}),

where

\displaystyle m(j)=\begin{cases}6&\text{if }j\neq 0,1728,\\

Moreover, for

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{cm}}(X)=\frac{2}{3^{3/2}\zeta(6)}X^{1/2}+\frac{2^{1/3}}{\zeta(4)}X^{1/3}+O(X^{1/6}),

First, recall that for a curve

if and only if

, and

B=0
. Now, in order to count the number of curves in

\displaystyle y^{2}=-\frac{4}{27}x^{3}.

The

equals
1
, that is

\displaystyle d(\mathcal{E}_{0})=\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{\#\mathcal{E}_{0}(X)}{\#\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{cm}}(X)}=1.

Proof.

Using the asymptotic formulas from Theorem
2.4
we have

\displaystyle d(\mathcal{E}_{0})=\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{\#\mathcal{E}_{0}(X)}{\#\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{cm}}(X)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{\frac{2}{3^{3/2}\zeta(6)}X^{1/2}+O(X^{1/12})}{\frac{2}{3^{3/2}\zeta(6)}X^{1/2}+\frac{2^{1/3}}{\zeta(4)}X^{1/3}+O(X^{1/6})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{\frac{2}{3^{3/2}\zeta(6)}+O(X^{-5/12})}{\frac{2}{3^{3/2}\zeta(6)}+\frac{2^{1/3}}{\zeta(4)}X^{-1/6}+O(X^{-1/3})}=1.

∎

3.
Isomorphism classes of elliptic curves and twisting

In this section, we revisit fundamental definitions and results regarding twists of elliptic curves, as outlined in
[
Sil09
, Chapter X]
. This provides an alternative method of indexing the
K
-isomorphism classes of elliptic curves over a perfect field
K
. Subsequently, we employ this approach to enumerate CM elliptic curves over
{\mathbb{Q}}
in a different way than the one used in Section
2
and we study their distribution in Section
4
.

First, if
C/K
is a smooth projective curve over a perfect field
K
, then the group of all
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{K}
-isomorphisms
C\longrightarrow C
, called the
isomorphism group
of
C
, is denoted by
\operatorname{Isom}(C)
. Then, a
twist
of
C/K
is a smooth curve
C^{\prime}/K
that is isomorphic to
C
over
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{K}
. Moreover, two twists are treated as
equivalent
if they are isomorphic over
K
, and the set of all twists of
C/K
, up to
K
-isomorphism, is denoted by
\operatorname{Twist}(C/K)
.

Now we briefly explain how
\operatorname{Twist}(C/K)
can be identified with a particular pointed cohomology set. Given a twist
C^{\prime}/K
and an isomorphism
\phi\colon C^{\prime}\longrightarrow C
defined over
\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{K}
, let
\xi\colon G_{\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{K}/K}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Isom}(C)
be given by
\xi(\sigma)=\xi_{\sigma}:=\phi^{\sigma}\phi^{-1}
, for every
\sigma\in G_{\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{K}/K}
. It turns out that the map
\xi
satisfies the relations defining a 1-cocycle, that is, we have

\xi_{\sigma\tau}=\left(\xi_{\sigma}\right)^{\tau}\xi_{\tau}\quad\text{ for all }\sigma,\tau\in G_{\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{K}/K}.

In particular, we denote by
\{\xi\}
the associated cohomology class in
H^{1}(G_{\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{K}/K},\operatorname{Isom}(C))
. Moreover, it is known that the cohomology class
\{\xi\}
is completely determined by the
K
-isomorphism class of
C^{\prime}/K
and that it is independent of the choice of isomorphism
\phi
. Therefore, this allows one to define a map

(3.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Twist}(C/K)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow H^{1}(G_{\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{K}/K},\operatorname{Isom}(C))

\displaystyle C^{\prime}/K

\displaystyle\longmapsto\{\xi\}.

Furthermore, this map is a bijection. These facts can be found in
[
Sil09
, Theorem X.2.2]
.

Now, if
E/K
is an elliptic curve then the automorphism group of
E
, denoted by
\operatorname{Aut}(E)
, is the subgroup of
\operatorname{Isom}(E)
consisting of all isomorphisms
E\longrightarrow E
that also map the origin
O\in E
to itself. Then, the inclusion
\operatorname{Aut}(E)\subset\operatorname{Isom}(E)
induces an inclusion

H^{1}(G_{\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{K}/K},\operatorname{Aut}(E))\subset H^{1}(G_{\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{K}/K},\operatorname{Isom}(E)).

By the bijection (
3.1
), the pointed cohomology set
H^{1}(G_{\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{K}/K},\operatorname{Isom}(E))
is identified with
\operatorname{Twist}(E/K)
and hence we denote the cohomology group
H^{1}(G_{\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{K}/K},\operatorname{Aut}(E))
by
\operatorname{Twist}((E,O)/K)
. Also, it is known that if
C/K\in\operatorname{Twist}((E,O)/K)
, then
C/K
can be given the structure of an elliptic curve over
K
and conversely, if
E^{\prime}/K
is an elliptic curve that is isomorphic to
E
over
K
, then
E^{\prime}/K
represents an element of
\operatorname{Twist}((E,O)/K)
. These facts can be found in
[
Sil09
, Proposition X.5.3]
.

Furthermore, if
\operatorname{char}(K)\neq 2,3
, then the elements of the group
\operatorname{Twist}((E,O)/K)
can be explicitly described, as the two following results show. These are stated verbatim from
[
Sil09
]
.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
Sil09
, Proposition X.5.4]
)
.

Assume that
\operatorname{char}(K)\neq 2,3
, and let

n=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if }j(E)\neq 0,1728,\\
4&\text{ if }j(E)=1728,\\
6&\text{ if }j(E)=0.\end{cases}

Then
\operatorname{Twist}((E,O)/K)
is canonically isomorphic to
K^{\times}/\left(K^{\times}\right)^{n}
.
More precisely, choose a Weierstrass equation

E:y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B

for
E/K
, and let
D\in K^{\times}
. Then the elliptic curve
E_{D}\in\operatorname{Twist}((E,O)/K)
corresponding to
D\left(\bmod\left(K^{\times}\right)^{n}\right)
has Weierstrass equation

(i)

E_{D}:y^{2}=x^{3}+D^{2}Ax+D^{3}B\quad
if
j(E)\neq 0,1728
,

(ii)

E_{D}:y^{2}=x^{3}+DAx
if
j(E)=1728
(so
B=0
),

(iii)

E_{D}:y^{2}=x^{3}+DB
if
j(E)=0
(so
A=0
).

Corollary 3.2

(
[
Sil09
, Corollary X.5.4.1]
)
.

Define an equivalence relation on the set
K\times K^{\times}
by

(j,D)\sim\left(j^{\prime},D^{\prime}\right)\quad\text{ if }\quad j=j^{\prime}\quad\text{ and }\quad D/D^{\prime}\in\left(K^{\times}\right)^{n(j)},

where
n(j)=2
(respectively
4
, respectively
6
) if
j\neq 0,1728
(respectively
j=1728
, respectively
j=0
). Then the
K
-isomorphism classes of elliptic curves
E/K
are in one-to-one correspondence with the elements of the quotient

\frac{K\times K^{\times}}{\sim}.

We will apply these two results when
K={\mathbb{Q}}
. In this case we note the following.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 1}
be an even integer and let
r\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}
. Then there exists a unique
n
-th power-free integer
z_{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
such that
r\cdot({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}=z_{r}\cdot({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}
. In other words, every coset in the quotient group
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}/({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}
contains a unique representative that is an
n
-th power-free integer. Thus, there is a bijection

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}/({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}/({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}

\displaystyle r\cdot({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}

\displaystyle\longmapsto z_{r}\cdot({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}.

Here we denote
{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}:={\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

Let
r=a/b\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}
for some
a,b\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
with
a
and
b
both relatively prime and
n
-th power-free, that is, both are not divisible by the
n
-th power of some integer greater than
1
. We can safely assume this, as in the case where either
a
or
b
is not
n
-th power free, the largest perfect
n
-th powers dividing them would be absorbed within the quotient
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}/({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}
.

Then we have

r\cdot({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}=\frac{a}{b}\cdot({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}=\frac{ab^{n-1}}{b^{n}}\cdot({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}=ab^{n-1}\cdot({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}.

Now, we have
ab^{n-1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
but it may not be
n
-th power-free. Thus we remove the largest
n
-th power
c^{n}
dividing it and write
ab^{n-1}=z_{r}\cdot c^{n}
with
z_{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
and
n
-th power-free. Then clearly

r\cdot({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}=z_{r}\cdot({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}.

To prove uniqueness, suppose that
w_{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
is
n
-th power-free and that
z_{r}\cdot({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}=w_{r}\cdot({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}.
Thus there are relatively prime integers
d,e\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
such that

z_{r}=w_{r}\cdot\left(\frac{d}{e}\right)^{n}=w_{r}\cdot\frac{d^{n}}{e^{n}}.

Hence
z_{r}e^{n}=w_{r}d^{n}
. This implies that
e^{n}
divides
w_{r}
and similarly that
d^{n}
divides
z_{r}
because
\gcd(d^{n},e^{n})=1
since
\gcd(d,e)=1
. Given that both
z_{r}
and
w_{r}
are
n
-th power-free and that
n
is even, this implies that
d^{n}=e^{n}=1
and therefore
z_{r}=w_{r}
.
∎

The following result shows how the naive height behaves after twisting.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
E\colon y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B
be an elliptic curve over
{\mathbb{Q}}
with
A,B\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and suppose that
D\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}={\mathbb{Z}}\smallsetminus\{0\}
is a nonzero integer. Also, let
n=n(E)\in\{2,4,6\}
, according to the value of
j(E)
, as defined in Proposition
3.1
. Then, if
E_{D}
denotes the twist of
E/{\mathbb{Q}}
corresponding to the coset
D\pmod{({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n}}
as in Proposition
3.1
, the naive height of
E_{D}
satisfies

h^{\textrm{naive}}(E_{D})=|D|^{m}h^{\textrm{naive}}(E),

where
m
is given by

(3.3)

\displaystyle m=\begin{cases}6&\text{if $j(E)\neq 0,1728$},\\
3&\text{if $j(E)=1728$},\\
2&\text{if $j(E)=0$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The proof is just a short calculation using the definition

h^{\textrm{naive}}(E):=\max{\{4|A|^{3},27|B|^{2}\}}

of the naive height of an elliptic curve
E\colon y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B
and the explicit equations for the twists
E_{D}
, as given in Proposition
3.1
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Observe that the values of
m
appearing in the previous lemma coincide with the values of
m(j)
that appear in Theorem
2.4
.

4.
The distribution of CM elliptic curves over
{\mathbb{Q}}
in the family
\mathcal{ET}

In this section we study again the distribution of CM elliptic curves over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, but this time we use the theory of twists that was summarized in Section
3
in order to produce a different family of representatives for the
{\mathbb{Q}}
-isomorphism classes of CM elliptic curves over
{\mathbb{Q}}
than the family
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{cm}}
that was used in the introduction and in Section
2
.

For each
j\in{\mathbb{Q}}
, let
E^{j}
be any
fixed
elliptic curve in short Weierstrass form
E^{j}\colon y^{2}=x^{3}+A_{j}x+B_{j}
with
A_{j},B_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and having
j
-invariant
j(E^{j})=j
. Then, for each of the curves
E^{j}
we define the corresponding family of twists

(4.1)

\mathcal{ET}_{j}:=\{E^{j}_{D}\;|\;D\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}/({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n(j)}\},

where
n(j)=2,4
or
6
, according to whether
j\neq 0,1728
,
j=1728
, or
j=0
, respectively, as was defined in Corollary
3.2
. Moreover, we only consider representatives
D\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
for the cosets as a consequence of Lemma
3.3
. In particular, when taken in combination with Corollary
3.2
we have that every elliptic curve defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}
and with
j
-invariant equal to
j
is isomorphic over
{\mathbb{Q}}
to a unique elliptic curve in the family
\mathcal{ET}_{j}
.

Next, recall that
\mathcal{J}^{\textrm{cm}}
was defined in (
1.6
) as the set of thirteen CM
j
-invariants of CM elliptic curves over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then, we define the family

(4.2)

\mathcal{ET}^{\mathrm{cm}}:=\coprod_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{\textrm{cm}}}\mathcal{ET}_{j}.

In particular, if
E/{\mathbb{Q}}
is any CM elliptic curve, then
E
is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-isomorphic to a unique elliptic curve in the family
\mathcal{ET}
.

Remark 4.1
.

We have chosen the notations
\mathcal{ET}
,
\mathcal{ET}_{j}
and
\mathcal{ET}^{\mathrm{cm}}
to reflect the fact that the families consist of the twists of certain fixed elliptic curves.

Remark 4.2
.

By Proposition
3.1
every elliptic curve in
\mathcal{ET}_{j}
is in short Weierstrass form so its naive height is well defined.

Remark 4.3
.

The definitions of the families
\mathcal{ET}_{j}
and
\mathcal{ET}^{\mathrm{cm}}
depend on the choice of elliptic curves
E^{j}
for different values of
j
. Therefore, a notation like
\mathcal{ET}(j,E^{j})
might be more appropriate. However, to avoid overcomplicating the notation, we decided to use the former.

Then, as we did with the families studied in the introduction and in Section
2
, for each
X>0
we define the
finite
subsets of
\mathcal{ET}
given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{ET}^{\mathrm{cm}}(X):=\{E\in\mathcal{ET}^{\mathrm{cm}}\;|\;h^{\textrm{naive}}(E)\leq X\},

and for each CM
j
-invariant
j\in\mathcal{J}^{\textrm{cm}}
the subsets

\displaystyle\mathcal{ET}_{j}(X):=\{E\in\mathcal{ET}_{j}\;|\;h^{\textrm{naive}}(E)\leq X\}.

It then follows that we have the disjoint union decomposition

(4.3)

\mathcal{ET}^{\mathrm{cm}}(X)=\coprod_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{\textrm{cm}}}\mathcal{ET}_{j}(X).

Then, using these definitions, we want to study how the CM elliptic curves are distributed inside the family
\mathcal{ET}^{\textrm{cm}}
among the thirteen different CM
j
-invariants. In particular, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
j\in{\mathbb{Q}}
. Let
n(j)=2,4
or
6
and
m(j)=6,3
or
2
according to whether
j\neq 0,1728
,
j=1728
or
j=0
, respectively. Then for
X>0
we have

(4.4)

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{ET}_{j}(X)=C(j)X^{1/m(j)}+O(X^{1/12})

as
X\to\infty
, where

(4.5)

\displaystyle C(j):=\frac{2}{\zeta(n(j))}\left(\frac{1}{h^{\textrm{naive}}(E^{j})}\right)^{1/m(j)}.

Here
\zeta(s)
denotes the Riemann zeta function and
E^{j}
is an elliptic curve in short Weierstrass form
E^{j}\colon y^{2}=x^{3}+A_{j}X+B_{j}
with
A_{j},B_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
having
j
-invariant equal to
j
. Moreover, we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{ET}^{\mathrm{cm}}(X)=C(0)X^{1/2}+C(1728)X^{1/3}+\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{cm}}\\
j\neq 0,1728\end{subarray}}C(j)\right)X^{1/6}+O(X^{1/12}).

Proof.

Let
j\in{\mathbb{Q}}
be fixed and let
X>0
. Then, using the formula from Lemma
3.4
, for a twist
E^{j}_{D}\in\mathcal{ET}^{\mathrm{cm}}_{j}
we have

\displaystyle h^{\textrm{naive}}(E^{j}_{D})\leq X

\displaystyle\iff|D|^{m(j)}h^{\textrm{naive}}(E^{j})\leq X

\displaystyle\iff|D|^{m(j)}\leq\frac{1}{h^{\textrm{naive}}(E^{j})}X

\displaystyle\iff|D|\leq\left(\frac{1}{h^{\textrm{naive}}(E^{j})}\right)^{1/m(j)}X^{1/m(j)}.

Then, since

\displaystyle\mathcal{ET}_{j}(X)=\{E^{j}_{D}\;|\;D\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}/({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{n(j)}\,\text{and $h^{\textrm{naive}}(E_{D}^{j})\leq X$}\},

we conclude that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{ET}_{j}(X)=\left\{D\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}\;|\;\text{$D$ is $n(j)$-free and }|D|\leq\left(\frac{1}{h^{\textrm{naive}}(E^{j})}\right)^{1/m(j)}X^{1/m(j)}\right\}.

Now, recall from Proposition
2.2
that for
X>0
and
k\geq 2
an integer,
Q_{k}(X)
denotes the number of positive
k
-free integers
n
such that
n\leq X
. Then, it follows from (
4.7
) that

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{ET}_{j}(X)=2Q_{n(j)}\left(\left(\frac{1}{h^{\textrm{naive}}(E^{j})}\right)^{1/m(j)}X^{1/m(j)}\right).

Hence, Proposition
2.2
implies that

(4.8)

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{ET}_{j}(X)=C(j)X^{1/m(j)}+O(X^{1/m(j)n(j)})=C(j)X^{1/m(j)}+O(X^{1/12}),

where the constant
C(j)
is given by

(4.9)

\displaystyle C(j)=\frac{2}{\zeta(n(j))}\left(\frac{1}{h^{\textrm{naive}}(E^{j})}\right)^{1/m(j)}

and we used the fact that
m(j)n(j)=12
, which follows from the definitions of
m(j)
and
n(j)
given in the statement of the theorem. This proves (
4.4
). Finally, to prove (
4.6
) recall that we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{ET}^{\mathrm{cm}}(X)=\coprod_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{\textrm{cm}}}\mathcal{ET}_{j}(X).

Hence, combining this decomposition with the asymptotic formulas (
4.4
) for all
j\in\mathcal{J}^{\textrm{cm}}
gives the desired asymptotic. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

5.
Computational methods and code description

The
Python
code used for the computations made in this article can be found in the
GitHub
repository
[
BSCM24
]
. This code computes the distribution of
j
-invariants of CM elliptic curves with naive height less than
X
by counting the appearances of
j
-invariants in each family
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{ET}
.

Here is a detailed explanation of what each function in the code does, along with its role in the overall analysis.

1.
disc(a, b)

Calculates the discriminant of the elliptic curve
E_{a,b}:y^{2}=x^{3}+ax+b
.

2.
naive_height(a, b)

Computes the
naive height
of the elliptic curve
E_{a,b}
.

3.
prime_condition(a, b)

Checks the primality condition on the integers
a
and
b
, ensuring there is no prime
p
such that
p^{4}
divides
a
and
p^{6}
divides
b
. The function analyzes the prime factorizations of
a
and
b
and returns
True
if the condition is satisfied, otherwise
False
.

4.
Get_Distribution_J_invariants_in_family_E(Xmax)

Computes the distribution of
j
-invariants in the family
\mathcal{E}
for elliptic curves with naive height
\leq X_{\text{max}}
.

Steps
:

(1)

Iterates over integers
a
and
b
within the range dictated by
X_{\text{max}}
.

(2)

For each pair
(a,b)
, checks that the discriminant is non-zero and that the curve satisfies the prime condition.

(3)

Computes the
j
-invariant for valid curves and counts how often specific
j
-invariants appear.

This function finds all elliptic curves in the family
\mathcal{E}
up to the given height bound and generates a dictionary of
j
-invariants and their frequency. The pseudocode for this function can be found in Algorithm
1
.

5.
is_k_power_free(n, k)

Checks if the integer
n
is
k-power-free
, meaning no prime factor of
n
is raised to an exponent of
k
or more in its factorization.

6.
get_power_free_integers(X, k)

Generates a list of integers
d
such that
d
is
