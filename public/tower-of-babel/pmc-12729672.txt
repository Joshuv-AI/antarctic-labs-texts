Clinical features, epidemiology, treatment, and prognosis of multicentre pediatric pneumococcal meningitis in China from 2019 to 2021

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePneumococcal meningitis (PM) causes significant illness and death in children worldwide, especially in high-burden countries. This multicenter study aimed to examine the epidemiology, clinical symptoms, prognosis, strain serotyping, antibiotic resistance, and outcome-related factors in children with PM.MethodsA prospective surveillance study on children under 18 years old with laboratory-confirmed invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) was conducted at 19 hospitals, mainly in tertiary pediatric hospitals, from 2019 to 2021. This period was early in the post-PCV13 era in China, when the vaccine had been licensed but coverage was still limited. Given the high morbidity associated with PM, we collected data on children diagnosed with PM between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2021. We thoroughly analyzed their demographics, epidemiology, vaccination status, clinical characteristics, laboratory results, treatment, and prognosis.ResultsA total of 157 children participated in this study, with a median age of 30.4 (9.7, 53.7) months. Only three children had received the vaccination before admission. The most common season of onset was winter (37.6%), with a bimodal peak of incidence occurring in January and April. Overall, 61 children (38.9%) had a poor prognosis at discharge, and 30 children (19.1%) died during the disease course or follow-up. Among the 157 patients, serotyping was successfully performed in 118 (75.2%) cases, identifying 25 distinct serotypes. Among the 118 serotyped isolates, the most dominant serotypes were 19F (26.3%), followed by 23F (12.7%) and 6B (11.9%). All strains were sensitive to vancomycin (100.0%) and levofloxacin (100.0%), followed by ceftriaxone (61.9%). Interestingly, serotype 19F was more likely to be resistant to penicillin, ceftriaxone, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole compared with other serotypes, while serotypes 14 and 15A were more sensitive to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole than other serotypes. In addition, among the 118 serotyped isolates, 112 (94.9%) were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR). Among these MDR isolates, 51.7% exhibited combined resistance to β-lactams, macrolides, clindamycin, tetracyclines, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.ConclusionsChildren with PM had a poor prognosis and a high mortality rate. Most children with PM were not vaccinated before admission. Nearly three-quarters of isolates were included in PCV13. Introduction

Pneumococcal meningitis (PM) is a severe acute infection of the central nervous system (CNS) caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, associated with high morbidity and mortality rates [1, 2]. Even with the widespread use of the Pneumococcal Conjugate Vaccine (PCV) and combination antibiotic treatment for PM patients, the case fatality rate of PM remains high. It accounts for 17.7% of meningitis-related deaths in children under 5 years old, making it the leading cause of meningitis-related death [3]. Approximately 12.2% of deaths occurred in developed countries, and this figure rose as high as 25.8% to 33.2% in developing countries [4–7]. PM is the most severe form of Invasive Pneumococcal Disease (IPD). Our multicenter retrospective study involving 1,138 children with IPD from 2012 to 2017 found that the mortality rate among children with PM was 22.9% (102/446), with meningitis-related deaths making up 65.4% (102/156) of the total mortality in children with IPD [8]. Moreover, about 35.0% of PM survivors experienced neurological sequelae such as secondary epilepsy, intellectual disability, and hearing impairment, placing a significant financial burden on both families and society [9, 10]. The etiology and pathophysiological mechanisms of PM are complex, arising from the host’s immune response and the virulence factors of Streptococcus pneumoniae, especially its capsular polysaccharide [11, 12].

The classification of pneumococcal serotypes mainly relies on the capsular polysaccharide of Streptococcus pneumoniae. PCV can influence the distribution of multiple serotypes across different countries. A nationwide prospective surveillance of IPD revealed that serotypes 3, 14, and 19A remained the main serotypes covered by the PCV13, while serotypes 8 and 12F emerged during the late PCV13 period in Israel [13]. A European multicenter study demonstrated that the introduction of PCV13 rapidly altered the pneumococcal serotype distribution, resulting in replacement [14]. In China, since IPD has not yet been included in the list of statutory infectious diseases and does not need to be reported, there is currently a lack of nationwide research on the incidence and epidemiology of IPD. A population-based study in Suzhou reported that the annual incidence of IPD hospitalizations among children under 5 years old ranged from 8.16 to 17.86 per 100,000 children from 2010 to 2017 (before the use of PCV13), with PM (31.4%) being the most common diagnosis [15]. Long-term, consistent, proactive PM monitoring in children is vital for developing more effective disease prevention and control strategies. However, in countries with a high burden of PM, only a few have included pneumococcal serotypes in routine IPD surveillance [16]. In China, a systematic review study that included 56 studies published from 1980 to 2022 suggested that the annual incidence of PM in children under 5 years old in China was about 2.1 cases per 100,000, with a combined case fatality rate of 24.6%. Among these 56 studies, only two studies had information on the serotype distribution of Streptococcus pneumoniae, of which the serotypes mainly included 6B, 14, 19F, and the coverage rate of PCV13 was 91.1% [1]. Our government approved PCV7 in 2008 and PCV13 in 2016 for population vaccination. Although PCVs have been introduced in some regions, they are still not part of the national free immunization program. The overall vaccination rate among children remains limited [17]. In 2017, the estimated national PCV coverage among children under 5 years old was only 1.3% [18]. Additionally, there were a few multicenter surveillance studies on pediatric PM in China. Therefore, this multicenter study was designed to focus on children with PM in China.

Method

Study design and participants

We conducted a hospital-based surveillance study on children with culture-positive IPD under 18 years old in 19 hospitals (mainly tertiary pediatric hospitals) in China between 2019 and 2021 [19]. All clinical and microbiological data were recorded in the China Pediatric IPD Surveillance Database. Since PM contributes significantly to poor prognosis and mortality in IPD, we collected data on children diagnosed with PM between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2021, from the Database, to comprehensively analyze the epidemiology, clinical characteristics, treatment, prognosis, serotyping, and antibiotic resistance of PM.

PM case definition, inclusion criteria

The PM including criteria for this study was one of the following [20]: (1) Children who underwent lumbar puncture with positive CSF cultures for Streptococcus pneumoniae; (2) Children who underwent lumbar puncture with negative CSF culture for Streptococcus pneumoniae, but Streptococcus pneumoniae was culture positive from other sterile sites, exhibited one or more neurological manifestations or signs, accompanied by elevated CSF white blood cell (WBC) counts (>15 × 106/L), protein level (>1000 mg/L), or decreased glucose (<2.2mmol/L); (3) Children who did not undergo lumbar puncture due to severe illness, or lumbar puncture failed, but Streptococcus pneumoniae was culture positive from other sterile sites, who presented with two or more neurological manifestations or signs, along with elevated hematological inflammatory markers including WBC counts (>12 × 109/L), or C-reactive protein (CRP) (>50 mg/L), or procalcitonin (PCT) (>2ug/L). Those cases diagnosed only with positive pneumococcal antigen were not included in this study.

Data collection

The main information for the enrolled children included: (1) demographics (sex, age, living environment, season, ethnic group, vaccination); (2) symptoms and signs on admission include: acute fever with an axillary temperature over 38 °C or rectal temperature over 38.5 °C, headache (identified by clinician-documented behavioral signs such as head holding or banging in younger children, and by self-report in older children during history taking), meningeal irritation (neck stiffness, Kernig’s sign, or Brudzinski’s sign), mental weakness (poor responsiveness to verbal or tactile stimulation, reduced interaction with caregivers, or failure to achieve age-appropriate behavioral responses), disorders of consciousness, convulsions, irritability, bulging anterior fontanelle, cough, shortness of breath, dyspnea, diarrhea, bellyache; (3) Laboratory test results, including CSF WBC counts, protein concentration, glucose concentration, and blood WBC counts, CRP concentration, and PCT concentration; (4) Antibiotic use was divided into two phases: empiric therapy (given before confirming Streptococcus pneumoniae) and targeted therapy (adjusted based on culture and susceptibility results of Streptococcus pneumoniae). The use of two or more classes of antibiotics was classified as combination therapy. Samples were collected from sterile sites of patients for pathogen culture when there was clinical suspicion of an acute invasive infection [21].

Clinical outcome and follow-up

The primary outcome was the discharge status, assessed using the Glasgow Outcome Score (1 point = death; 2 points = persistent vegetative state; 3 points = severe disability; 4 points = moderate disability; 5 points = good recovery) [22]. A good prognosis was defined as 5 points, indicating a good recovery, while a poor prognosis was defined as 1–4 points, indicating either disability or death. The initial follow-up for all patients occurred between October and December 2022. A second follow-up was conducted between October and December 2024, focusing on children who had neurological sequelae at the initial follow-up to assess their recovery.

Streptococcus pneumoniae culturing, serotyping, and multi-locus sequence typing

Firstly, samples from CSF, blood, or other sterile sites were cultured using standard microbiological techniques. The isolates were then subcultured onto blood agar plates and incubated at 35 °C in 5–10% CO₂ for 18 to 24 h. Colonies suspected to be Streptococcus pneumoniae were confirmed based on their colony morphology (α-hemolysis on blood agar), optochin sensitivity, and bile solubility. Despite repeated attempts at transportation, a subset of Streptococcus pneumoniae isolates could not be revived for further analysis. This loss of viability is consistent with the bacterium’s well-known autolytic nature, which can lead to Streptococcus pneumoniae death under suboptimal transport or storage conditions. All available Streptococcus pneumoniae isolates were subsequently transferred to Beijing Children’s Hospital for serotyping, multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) genotyping, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing [21]. The serotyping utilizes the classical Quellung reaction method, employing antisera sourced from the Statens Serum Institute in Copenhagen, Denmark. The operating procedures and judgments can be found in the literature [23]. Furthermore, genomic DNA was extracted from overnight cultures of Streptococcus pneumoniae isolates grown on 5% tryptic soy agar (Oxoid Ltd) using the Genomic DNA Extraction Kit from SiMax™. The housekeeping genes aroE, gdh, gki, recP, spi, xpt, and ddl were amplified from chromosomal DNA by a polymerase chain reaction, and the amplified products were sent to Huiyuan Corporation for sequencing [24]. The MLST and the relationships between isolates were analyzed by using BURST analysis (https://pubmlst.org/spneumoniae/), and strains were assigned to clonal complexes (CCs) based on strict group definitions of six of the seven shared alleles [25]. The goe BURST software (http://www.phyloviz.net/goeburst/) was used to draw the relationship between the isolates and the CC.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) method of E-test (Liofilchem, Italy) was utilized to test all isolated Streptococcus pneumoniae strains to determine the MIC values. The isolates were classified as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant based on cutoff values for resistance in meningitis isolates as defined by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [26]. Streptococcus pneumoniae ATCC 49,619 was used as the standard reference strain for quality control of culture media and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Multidrug-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae (MDRSP) is resistant to three or more types of antibiotics tested in this study.

Statistical analyses

The SPSS software (Version 22.0) was used for statistical analysis. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while those with a non-normal distribution are presented as median (Q1, Q3). Categorical variables are shown as the number of cases (%). Continuous variables with a normal distribution were analyzed using an independent sample t-test, whereas those that were not normally distributed were analyzed with the Mann-Whitney test. The χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test were used to compare categorical groups. A P-value(P) of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the analysis of prognosis risk factors, Symptoms or signs on admission with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression model, and the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated.

Laboratory result

The results of blood and CSF examinations are shown in Table 2. CRP > 50 mg/L was observed in 123 cases (78.3%), and the PCT > 2ug/L in 120 cases (76.4%). CSF WBC counts ≥ 1000 × 109/L were present in 71 cases (47.7% ), CSF protein ≥ 2000 mg/L in 59 cases (40.1%), and the glucose levels ≤ 1.11mmol/L in 87 cases (59.2%).

Complications and therapy

Nearly half of the patients experienced one or more neurologic complications or sequelae during their hospitalization. The most frequent neurological complication was subdural effusion or empyema, occurring in 33 patients (21.0%), followed by epilepsy in 24 cases (15.3%), and cerebral edema in 21 cases (13.4%). The most common non-neurological complications were pneumonia in 81 children (51.6%) and sepsis in 77 children (49.0%). Severe non-neurological complications mainly included respiratory failure in 38 patients (24.2%), shock in 21 patients (13.4%), and disseminated intravascular coagulation in 13 patients (8.3%). The median length of hospital stay was 20.0 (16.0, 40.0) days and 36 patients (22.9%) were hospitalized for more than 14 days (Table 3).

All children received initial empirical antibiotic therapy before the Streptococcus pneumoniae culture was available. A total of 123 children (78.3%) were treated with two or more antibiotics, including 53 in the poor prognosis group and 70 in the good prognosis group. Regarding different antibiotics, the most frequently used empirical antibiotics were cephalosporins in 109 cases (69.4%), followed by carbapenems in 86 cases (54.8%), and vancomycin in 84 cases (53.5%). After the Streptococcus pneumoniae culture results were reported, 128 children (81.5%) received two or more antibiotics, including 46 in the poor prognosis group and 82 in the good prognosis group. As for various antibiotics, the most frequently administered antibiotics were vancomycin in 86 cases (54.8%), followed by cephalosporins in 79 cases (50.3%), and carbapenems in 77 cases (49.0%). The use of cephalosporins decreased from 69.4% to 50.3% (P = 0.001), and penicillin use dropped from 12.1% to 5.1% (P = 0.027), while linezolid use increased from 19.1% to 39.5% (P < 0.001). A small number of patients received macrolides in the course of the disease, likely due to coexisting respiratory tract symptoms, suspicion of atypical pathogens, or allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics (Table 4). Additionally, adjunctive corticosteroid therapy was given in 81 cases (51.6%), and intravenous immunoglobulin infusion was provided to 23 patients (14.6%).

Clinical outcomes and follow-up

By the time of discharge, 61 children (38.9%) had a poor prognosis (score 1–4), and 96 children (61.1%) had a good prognosis (score 5). During the first follow-up of 1 to 3 years after discharge, only one patient (0.6%) with neurological sequelae at discharge was lost to follow-up, 30 children (19.1%) died, and 126 patients (80.3%) survived. Among the 30 patients who died, 25 died within 30 days of admission, including several after discharge, and only 2 died more than 30 days but within 1 year. The time of death was not recorded for 3 patients. Of these 30 patients, 23 were under 2 years of age at admission. The leading causes of death were brain herniation in 16 cases, multiple organ failure in 7 cases, respiratory failure in 3 cases, intracranial hemorrhage in 2 cases, and one case each of acute cerebral edema and symptomatic epilepsy. Among the 126 surviving patients, 97 children (77.0%) had a good prognosis (score 5), which included 96 cases that had a good prognosis (score 5) at discharge and one child with epilepsy who recovered after regular outpatient treatment and follow-up. In contrast, 29 children (23.0%) still had neurological sequelae.

Long-term follow-up was performed 3–5 years after discharge in 29 children with neurological sequelae identified during the first follow-up, with successful follow-up in 26 cases. Of those reached, 16 children still had one or more neurological sequelae. The main neurological sequelae included intellectual disability in 7 cases, language disorders in 6 cases, symptomatic epilepsy in 4 cases, hearing impairment in 3 cases, strabismus in 2 cases, and 1 case each of facial paralysis, muscle weakness, memory impairment, and developmental delay.

Analysis of the factors affecting discharge outcomes

Univariate analysis revealed that patients aged < 2 years, presenting with disorders of consciousness, convulsions, shortness of breath, and dyspnea on admission, were associated with poor prognosis. In contrast, headache and vomiting on admission were associated with a good prognosis (Table 1). Based on demographics, symptoms, or signs on admission with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis, multivariate logistic regression showed that children under 2 years old and impaired consciousness were independent risk factors at admission for poor prognosis at discharge (Table 5).

Laboratory tests show that blood WBC count ≤12 × 10⁹/L and CSF protein level ≥2000 mg/L were associated with a poor prognosis (Table 2). Among the complications, all serious non-neurological complications, most neurological complications, and pneumonia were associated with a higher risk of poor prognosis (Table 3). In addition, regarding antibiotics and the prognosis of children, we found that the absence of carbapenems during empirical medication was higher in the good prognosis group (Supply Table 4).

Comparison of clinical characteristics between children under 2 years and those 2 years or older

Given that age < 2 years on admission is an independent risk factor for prognosis in children with PM, more attention should be paid to this high-risk group during clinical management. Further stratifying by age group (age < 2 years and aged ≥ 2 years), the presentation of severe symptoms or signs, such as convulsion (65.5% vs. 34.5%, P<0.001), cough (59.1% vs. 40.9%, P = 0.034), and shortness of breath (80.0% vs. 20.0%, P = 0.004), occurs at a higher rate in children aged < 2 years compared to those aged ≥ 2 years. Except for hearing impairment and peripheral nerve damage, other neurologic complications and severe complications were significantly more likely to occur in patients in the < 2-year group than in the ≥ 2-year group (Supply Table 5). The laboratory tests revealed that the <2 year-old group was more likely to have blood WBC count<12 × 109/L, CSF WBC count<1000 × 106/L, CSF glucose>1.11mmol/L and blood CRP >50 mg/L compared with children in the ≥ 2 years age group (Supply Table 6).

Serotypes distribution of isolated strains

The serotypes of Streptococcus pneumoniae were determined for 118 cases (75.2%), revealing 25 distinct serotypes. Among the 118 cases with serotyped Streptococcus pneumoniae, most were covered by PCV13 (79.7%), with serotype 19F being the most prevalent in 31 cases(26.3%), followed by serotype 23F in 15 cases (12.7%), and serotype 6B in 14 cases (11.9%). Among the 118 cases, a total of 44 were in the poor-prognosis group. The most common serotypes were 19F in 14 cases, 19A in 8 cases, and 23F in 5 cases. The poor prognosis rate in 19A was higher than for other serotypes (8/11 vs. 36/107, P = 0.026) (Fig. 5). In addition, of the 44 cases in the poor-prognosis group, 22 resulted in death, including 7 cases of 19F, followed by 6B in 3 cases, 14, 19A, and 23F in 2 cases each, and 9V, 3, 27, 15A, 15C, and 18F in one case each.

Antimicrobial susceptibility analysis

The detailed antibiotic susceptibility results of the 118 isolated strains are shown in Table 6. The antibiotic resistance was higher in erythromycin (97.5%), clindamycin (96.6%), and tetracycline (93.2%). All strains were susceptible to vancomycin (100.0%) and levofloxacin (100.0%). Penicillin resistance was more common in serotypes covered by the PCV13 (89.0% vs. 63.0%, P = 0.004). Serotype 19 F was more likely to be resistant to penicillin, ceftriaxone, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole compared with other serotypes (100.0% vs. 78.0%, P = 0.011; 48% vs. 1%, P<0.001; 84% vs. 53%, P = 0.002), while the serotypes14 and 15 A were each more sensitive to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole compared with other serotypes (77% vs. 34%, P = 0.003; 100% vs. 35%, P = 0.003). It is important to note that 112 strains (94.9%) were MDR, with a multidrug resistance rate of beta-lactamases, macrolides, clindamycin, tetracycline, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole reaching 51.7%, as shown in Fig. 7.

Discussion

This comprehensive multicenter study investigates the clinical characteristics and isolates of Streptococcus pneumoniae in children with PM in China. Our findings indicate that PM cases declined from 87 to 40, and then to 30, before and after the COVID-19 outbreak. A study conducted in Germany across 26 hospitals revealed that the annual PM cases increased from 87 to 120 cases per year between 2016 and 2019, dropping to 64 cases in 2020 [6]. Furthermore, an extensive epidemiological study revealed that the incidence of IPD decreased by 68% just four weeks after the COVID-19 outbreak, and dropped 82% by the eighth week [27]. After the COVID-19 outbreak, public health measures such as widespread mask-wearing and school closures likely contributed to a reduction in the incidence of pediatric PM. However, winter remains a seasonal peak for pediatric PM in China, particularly among children under 2 years of age. During winter, children are more susceptible to upper respiratory tract infections, and younger children, with their immature immune systems, are especially vulnerable to PM.

Children with disorders of consciousness, hypotension, or requiring endotracheal intubation on admission have a higher risk of death. In our study, age <2 years and impaired consciousness are independent risk factors at admission for poor prognosis. Children under 2 years old were more likely to experience convulsions, bulging anterior fontanelles, coughing, shortness of breath, and difficulty breathing. In contrast, individuals older than 2 years were more likely to display typical symptoms, such as headache and vomiting. A study on bacterial meningitis(BM) from a tertiary hospital in Vietnam found that although fever was the most common symptom in children with acute BM, impaired consciousness dominated the deaths of patients [28]. Notably, in our study, the ratio of headache and vomiting in the good prognosis group was higher than in the poor prognosis group. Patients with CSF leakage, immune dysfunction, and chronic inflammation may have a higher risk of IPD and are more likely to have a poor prognosis, which increases the risk of death [9]. In our study, 37.6% of patients had underlying diseases or high-risk factors, which was slightly higher than the 30.8% in 2–18 years PM patients reported in the French national surveillance study [29]. However, there was no significant difference between the poor prognosis group (32%) and the good prognosis group (42%) in our study. Given that the patients enrolled in our study primarily came from the pediatric infection center of a tertiary hospital, the multidisciplinary approach may effectively prevent the further deterioration of related factors and thus mitigate the progression of PM.

Laboratory examination is important for the diagnosis and monitoring of PM. Lower WBC and higher CRP in blood are associated with mortality in children with IPD [30]. Patients with BM may present with normal CSF WBC counts on admission, especially in severe, rapidly progressing BM, but their CSF protein levels are often elevated [31]. In our study, more than two-thirds of children had high laboratory examinations on admission; blood WBC counts ≤12 × 109/L and CSF proteins ≥2000 mg/L were higher in the poor prognosis group. Further analysis showed that the proportions of blood WBC counts ≤12 × 109/L and CSF proteins ≥2000 mg/L were more pronounced in children under 2 years old. Therefore, when children under 2 years with PM present with normal or even low WBC counts, it may indicate rapid progression and a poor prognosis.

Among the 118 isolates with identified serotypes, most of them were covered by PCV13, the top serotypes were 19F (26.3%), 23F (12.7%), and 6B (11.9%). In our study, 19F was the most common serotype in children with PM in 31 cases and also the leading cause of death in children with PM in 7 cases. In addition, the poor prognosis ratio in serotype 19A is higher than in other serotypes. A study in Peru found that 19A increased and accounted for 49.4% (42/85) of all pediatric IPD isolates in 2016-2019, but there was no significant correlation with prognosis [32].

Although PCV13 was introduced in China in November 2016, it has yet to be incorporated into the national immunization program and remains self-paid. Consequently, the overall coverage across the country remains low. Even in relatively developed cities like Shanghai, the primary vaccination rate with PCV13 did not exceed 50% [17]. In other regions, the coverage was even lower. A multicenter study revealed that among infants aged 0–15 months, only 16.1% had completed the full primary series of PCV, and just 12.5% had received a booster dose by 2021 [33]. In addition, the unvaccinated children are more likely to develop IPD requiring hospitalization. Therefore, increasing PCV13 vaccination rate could effectively reduce the incidence of PM. At the same time, strengthening surveillance and reporting of relevant serotypes of PM will further help assess the actual disease burden and guide public health interventions more effectively.

Over the three-year study period, the rates of multidrug-resistant antibiotics were as high as 94.9%. The resistance rate of ceftriaxone was 13.8%, similar to the resistance rate of 14% in a previous multicenter study based on 10 children’s hospitals in 2013–2017 [34]. Research from South Africa found that pneumococcal transmission is so entrenched and slow-spreading that vaccine-related antibiotic resistance declines may be temporary [35]. Notably, serotype 19F showed high resistance compared with other serotypes to cephalosporins and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. A study from Taiwan also found that infection with serotype 19F was associated with an increased risk of ceftriaxone resistance [36]. In contrast, our results indicate that serotypes 14 and 6B are highly susceptible to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.

There are some limitations in our study. First, our study only included cases of PM with positive laboratory cultures, which may introduce bias in the cases selected, especially for patients who received antibiotics at admission and had negative cultures, potentially missing some cases. Second, although some studies have shown that, after the COVID-19 policy was relaxed, there was an increase in PM cases in some regions of countries [37], our study unfortunately did not include relevant cases. Therefore, further monitoring and data collection are required to determine the epidemic’s impact on the disease.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results indicate that the COVID-19 pandemic has reduced the number of hospitalized children with PM, but the rate of poor prognosis remains very high. About 38.9% of children with PM experienced a poor outcome, and 19.1% died. Age under 2 years and impaired consciousness are independent prognostic risk factors at admission. Nearly all children with PM were not vaccinated prior to admission. Most isolated serotypes were covered by PCV13, especially 19 F, which is the dominant serotype. Additionally, 19 F is more likely to resist ceftriaxone and other antimicrobial drugs than other serotypes in PM. A better pneumococcal vaccine coverage is needed to prevent pediatric PM.
