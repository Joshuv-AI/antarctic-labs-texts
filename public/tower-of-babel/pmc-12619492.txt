Neuronal differentiation regulator CEND1 coordinately suppresses tumor growth and energetics via AMPK signaling in brain glioma
Authors: Yuting Shu, Yunbo Yuan, Yuze He, Linzi Ji, Qiuyun Yuan, Jingwen Gong, Siliang Chen, Yanhui Liu, Wanchun Yang, Mina Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aggressive proliferation and metabolic adaptability of glioma contribute to poor clinical prognosis, necessitating novel targets concurrently reprogram glioma cells toward a neuron-like, less proliferative, and metabolically suppressed state. Here, we identified neuronal differentiation factor CEND1 as a candidate and explored its impact on glioma growth and metabolism. We demonstrated that CEND1 was significantly reduced in high-grade gliomas and inversely correlated with patient survival. Elevated CEND1 in glioma cells induced a neuron-like morphology, accompanied with attenuated proliferation and migration. CEND1 overexpression suppressed tumor growth and prolonged the survival of animal models of intracranial orthotopic tumor formation. Metabolomics and biochemical assays revealed that CEND1 inhibited PDH activity and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, ultimately reducing ATP levels. Mechanistically, CEND1 activated AMPK to induce cell proliferation arrest and enhance metformin sensitivity. Altogether, our findings reveal that CEND1 coordinates neuronal differentiation with mitochondrial energetic metabolic suppression to exert anti-proliferative function in glioma, supporting its role as a potential target for glioma therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-025-01500-z.Keywords: Glioma, CEND1, Cell proliferation, Metabolism, AMPK

Introduction

Glioma, particularly glioblastoma (GBM), is a highly lethal primary brain cancer, characterized by aggressive tumor growth and poor clinical outcomes [1]. Despite advances in current therapies, including maximal safe surgical resection, temozolomide chemotherapy, radiotherapy [2], tumor treating fields (TTFields) [3] and immunotherapy [4], the overall survival (OS) of glioma patients remains unsatisfactory. This therapeutic limitation is largely attributable to the rapid tumor cell growth and acquired resistance to glioma treatments. The rapid proliferation of glioma cells imposes high energetic demands, which are supported by the activation of distinct metabolic pathways [5]. Accordingly, strategies that coordinately target both glioma cell proliferation and metabolic dependency may provide a more effective approach to enhance glioma therapy and improve patient outcomes [6].

Unlike tumors in peripheral tissues, gliomas originate within the brain, where they coexist with diverse neural cells, particularly neurons. In the brain, glioma cells exhibit aggressive proliferative behavior compared with neighboring neurons, which are predominantly post-mitotic and terminally differentiated. To support neuronal differentiation and development, neurons are heavily dependent on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation to generate high levels of ATP required for complex functions such as synaptic formation and neurotransmitter trafficking [7]. In contrast, glioma cells are known to utilize aerobic glycolysis to sustain tumorigenic proliferation [8, 9], and they also maintain mitochondria metabolism to meet the energetic demand for rapid tumor growth [10, 11]. The distinct proliferative divergence and metabolic heterogeneity between glioma cells and neurons raise the possibility that reprogramming glioma cells towards a neuron-like state could significantly attenuate their proliferative malignancy.

Recent studies have directed at neuronal reprogramming of glioma cells as a potential strategy to reduce glioma aggressiveness [12]. For instance, forced expression of neuronal transcription factors, including ASCL1 [13], SOX9 [14], Ngn2 [15], and NeuroD1 [15], as well as the introduction of neuronal proteins, such as synapsin III [16], has been shown to drive the transdifferentiation of GBM cell lines and primary glioma stem cells to a neuron-like state, thereby suppressing their proliferative properties. Additionally, pharmacological strategies employing drug cocktails containing protein kinase inhibitors, activate neural transcription factor-associated pathways that similarly promote the reprogramming of GBM cells into a non-proliferative, neuron-like state [17–19]. All these genetic and pharmacological interventions to redirect the fate of glioma cells offer a therapeutic avenue for glioma. However, these studies mainly focused on modulating neuronal transcriptional programs. Identification of novel factors that can concurrently inhibit glioma cell proliferation to a neuron-like status and block its metabolic energy production remains not well understood.

In this study, we conducted an extensive analysis to identify neuronal differentiation factors that exhibit dual functionality—attenuating glioma cell proliferation and reducing their metabolic production. We demonstrated that CEND1, a neuronal differentiation regulator [20], was dramatically decreased in glioma tumors versus normal brain tissues. Elevated CEND1 expression suppresses glioma cell proliferation and concomitantly blocks mitochondrial metabolism. Mechanistically, CEND1 activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling to inhibit glioma growth both in vitro and in vivo, thereby rendering glioma cells less aggressive and more susceptible to metformin intervention. Our work reveals the potential of neuronal differentiation regulator CEND1 as a suppressor of proliferative and metabolic aberrancies of glioma, and provides a potential therapeutic target for glioma intervention.

Materials and methods

Patient samples and public data source

Glioma and matched adjacent brain tissues were procured from patients undergoing surgical resection at West China Hospital, Sichuan University, between January 2019 and January 2020. All procedures were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant. Clinical samples were used for CEND1 expression analysis by Western blots and immunohistochemistry. Clinical and molecular data of low-grade glioma (LGG) and glioblastoma (GBM) cohorts were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) via the Genomic Data Commons portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov) and from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) platform (http://www.cgga.org.cn). An independent validation cohort from West China Hospital (WCH) was incorporated in compared analyses. All the detailed experimental methods are described in Supplementary Materials.

Cell culture, lentivirus transfection and drug treatment

Human glioblastoma cell lines U87-MG and T98G were obtained from the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology (Chinese Academy of Sciences) and authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. Cells were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ExCell, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 U/mL and 100 µg/mL; Boster, China) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Full-length human CEND1 cDNA or empty vector control was cloned into a lentiviral backbone by homologous recombination. Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells and transfected into U87-MG and T98G cells following standard protocols. Transfected cells were selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL) for 7 days to establish stable lines. Overexpression of CEND1 was confirmed by Western blotting and immunofluorescence microscopy. For pharmacological treatment, metformin hydrochloride and doxorubicin (Selleck Chemicals) were reconstituted in sterile PBS to generate a 100 mM and 10 mM stock solutions, aliquoted, and stored at –20 °C. For experiments, cells were treated with metformin (1 mM) or doxorubicin (1 µM). Following a 24-h incubation, cells were harvested, and total protein was extracted for Western blot. For IC50 determination, cells were seeded at 2 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates. Cells were then exposed to a concentration gradient of metformin (0–16 mM) for 48 h. Following treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and cell viability was expressed as the mean optical density (OD). Dose–response curves and IC50 values were calculated using nonlinear regression analysis in GraphPad Prism 10. For cell viability assays, cells were seeded at 5 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates. Following treatment, cells were treated for 24 h with metformin at its IC50 concentration (5.081 mM) and doxorubicin (1 µM).

Western blot

Glioma cells were harvested and lysed in a 2% SDS buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher), and protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of lysate were resolved by SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CEND1 (Cell Signaling Technology, CST #8944), HA-Tag (CST #2367), MAP2 (Abcam #AB11268), Tuj1 (Santa #AA10), NeuN (Selleck #F0103), PSD95 (Millipore #MAB1596) p-AMPKα(Thr172) (CST #2535), AMPKα (CST #2532), p-ACC(Ser79) (CST #3661), ACC (CST #3676), PAK3 (Abclonal #A3363), p-DRP1(Ser616) (CST #3455), DRP1 (CST #8570), p-AKT(Thr308) (CST #2965), p-AKT(Ser473) (CST #4060), AKT (CST #9272), p-S6(Ser240/244) (CST #2215), S6 (CST #2217), p-4EBP1(Thr37/46) (CST #9459), p-PDH-E1α(Ser293) (Abcam #ab92696), HK2 (Abclonal #A20829), PKM2 (CST #4053), Cyclin D1 (Abclonal #A2708), p21 (CST #8831), and β-Actin (Boster #BM0627). After washing in TBS-T, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, developed by imaging instruments (Clinx Science Instruments, China).

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

For hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemical (IHC) stainings, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded glioma patient samples and murine brain tissues were sectioned at 3 µm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and rinsed in PBS. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and nonspecific binding was blocked with 3% normal goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. For human glioma samples, slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-CEND1 (CST; 1:300). For murine brains, brain sections were similarly probed with anti-PCNA (Abcam; 1:400), anti-pAMPKα(Thr172) (CST; 1:200) and anti-Tuj1 (Santa; 1:100). After PBS washes, sections were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibody, followed by avidin–biotin–peroxidase complex and development with diaminobenzidine. Stained sections were digitized and analyzed using an Olympus VS200 microscope.

For immunostaining, glioma cells were seeded onto sterile glass coverslips in 24-well plates and cultured overnight. To label mitochondria and HA-tagged proteins, live cells were incubated with 200 nM MitoTracker (Invitrogen) in complete DMEM for 30 min at 37 °C. Following three washes in PBS, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. After blocking in 10% goat serum for 1 h, coverslips were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer: anti-HA (CST; 1:500) for HA-tag detection, anti-MAP2 (Abcam; 1:100), anti-PSD95 (Millipore; 1:100) and anti-Tuj1 (Santa; 1:200) to visualize neuronal markers. The following day, samples were washed and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen; 1:1,000). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI using ProLong® Gold Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen). High-resolution images were acquired on Olympus SpinSR and Nikon confocal microscopes and processed using image-analysis software.

Tumor cell behavior assays

Cell viability was quantified using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (TargetMol). Glioma cells were seeded at 2 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates. At 24-h intervals over four consecutive days, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and cell viability was expressed as the mean OD relative to control wells. To assess glioma cell proliferation, the Cell-Light™ EdU Apollo®567 In Vitro Kit (RiboBio) was used, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were cultured on glass coverslips in 24-well plates, incubated with EdU labeling solution for 2 h, then fixed, permeabilized, and stained to detect proliferating cells. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and EdU-positive cells were quantified by fluorescence microscopy. Cell migratory capacity was assessed using 8 µm pore Transwell inserts. Cells were suspended in serum-free DMEM and seeded into the upper chamber at 2 × 104 cells per well, while the lower chamber was filled with DMEM containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 8 h at 37 °C, cells not migrated were removed from the upper membrane surface, and migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Migrated cells were counted in five random fields under a light microscope.

Metabolomics and ATP measurement

Metabolomic profiling of glioma cells was performed on a 600MRM LC–MS/MS system (Biotree, Shanghai, China). Cells were extracted in acetonitrile-methanol–water (2:2:1, containing isotope internal standards), subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles, sonicated, incubated at − 40 °C, and centrifuged. Supernatants were dried, reconstituted in 60% acetonitrile, and centrifuged before 1 µL injections onto a Waters Atlantis Premier BEH Z-HILIC column (1.7 µm, 2.1 × 150 mm). Separation was achieved on a Waters H-Class UPLC. Detection was carried out on an AB Sciex QTrap 6500 plus, and data were processed using SCIEX Analyst Work Station Software (Version 1.7.2) and BIOTREE BioBud (Version 2.1.4). To evaluate ATP production levels, glioma cells were plated in 6-well dishes and cultured to 80–100% confluence. Intracellular ATP levels were then measured using the ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Luminescence signals were recorded on a plate luminometer with a 10 s integration time per well, and ATP concentrations were determined by reference to a standard curve.

Mitochondrial respiration assay

Mitochondrial respiration was assessed using the Seahorse XF Pro Analyzer (Agilent Technologies) with the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Alicelligent), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, U87-MG cells were seeded in XF pro 96 Cell Culture Microplates (Agilent Technologies) at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2 to allow adherence. On the day of the assay, culture medium was replaced with Seahorse XF assay medium (Alicelligent) supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine, adjusted to pH 7.4. Cells were then incubated in a non-CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 1 h prior to measurement. The sensor cartridge was hydrated in Seahorse XF Calibrant solution overnight at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator. During the assay, the following inhibitors were sequentially injected to assess mitochondrial function: oligomycin (1 µM) to inhibit ATP synthase, FCCP (1 µM) to uncouple oxidative phosphorylation, and a combination of rotenone (0.5 µM) and antimycin A (0.5 µM) to inhibit complex I and III, respectively. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured in real time, and parameters including basal respiration, ATP production and maximal respiration were calculated using Seahorse Wave software (Agilent).

Animal models

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University. For U87 xenograft model, male athymic BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old; Charles River Laboratories, Chengdu, China) were acclimated under specific pathogen-free conditions for 1 week. Mice were anesthetized and secured in a stereotactic frame (RWD Life Science, China), after which luciferase-expressing U87 cells (5 × 105 cells in 5 µL PBS), either control or stably overexpressing CEND1, were injected into the right striatum (coordinates: AP + 1.5 mm, ML + 1.5 mm, DV − 2.0 mm relative to bregma). Tumor engraftment and progression were monitored weekly by bioluminescence imaging following intraperitoneal administration of D-luciferin (150 mg/kg; Sigma, USA). Photon flux was quantified using Living Image software (AniView). Finally, mice were deeply anesthetized and euthanized by cervical dislocation; brains were harvested, fixed in formalin, and processed for H&E and IHC analysis. For GL261 allograft model, male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old; GemPharmatech, Jiangsu, China) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions for 1 week prior to surgery. Control or CEND1-overexpressing GL261 cells expressing luciferase (1 × 105 cells in 5 µL PBS) were implanted into the right striatum (AP + 2.0 mm, ML + 0.8 mm, DV − 2.9 mm). Mice were monitored at defined time points by cervical dislocation. Harvested brains were fixed, sectioned, and subjected to H&E staining and IHC to evaluate tumor morphology and marker expression.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). CEND1 expression levels in tumor tissues were compared with peritumor tissues by two-tailed paired Student's t-test, and other two-group comparisons were conducted with two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. For analyses involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test was applied. Survival differences between CEND1-high and CEND1-low groups were evaluated by log-rank test. A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

CEND1 is a neuronal differentiation regulator decreased in glioma and inversely correlated with tumor malignancy

To identify candidate genes capable of governing neuronal differentiation and potentially redirecting glioma cells toward a neuronal lineage, we analyzed the intersection of known neuronal differentiation regulators with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of glioma tissues compared with normal brain tissues. Results of the Venn diagram showed 44 overlapping DEGs (Fig. 1A), among which CEND1 as one of the most markedly downregulated genes, and its expression was significantly positively correlated with overall survival (OS) in the TCGA, CGGA325, CGGA693, and WCH cohorts (Figs. 1A, B, S1 and Tables S1, S2). CEND1 was originally characterized as a neuronal marker linked to terminal neuron-generating divisions and exit from the cell cycle [20, 21]. We found that CEND1 exhibited marked reduction in glioma tissues within both TCGA and CGGA datasets (Figs. 1C, D, S2A), a tendency validated in our institutional cohort (West China Hospital, WCH) (Fig. S2B). The decreased mRNA levels of CEND1 were confirmed at the protein level, revealing that CEND1 protein was reduced in the tumor compared with adjacent brain tissues (Fig. 1E, F). Moreover, IHC results showed robust CEND1 signals localized within the soma of neural cells in peritumoral regions, but decreased or even undetectable in tumor cells (Figs. 1G, S2C). Next, comparative expression analysis across glioma grades revealed a consistent and significant decrease of CEND1 in Grade 4 gliomas, implying an inverse correlation between CEND1 levels and glioma malignancy (Fig. S2D–L). Furthermore, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients with high CEND1 expression experienced significantly better OS in the TCGA, CGGA, and WCH cohorts (Figs. 1H–J, S2H). Altogether, these results indicate that CEND1, a neuronal differentiation regulator, was downregulated in gliomas, and inversely associated with increased tumor malignancy and poor patient outcomes.

Elevated CEND1 expression induces neurotransdifferentiation and reduces glioma cell growth

Given the inverse relationship between CEND1 and glioma malignancy, we asked whether elevated CEND1 expression could directly suppress glioma growth. Thus, we employed lentiviral system to overexpress HA-tagged CEND1 into U87-MG and T98G GBM cell lines. Western blot confirmed that CEND1 expression yielded a threefold increase in CEND1 protein relative to Control (Ctrl) (Fig. 2A–D). HA immunofluorescence also confirmed HA-CEND1 localized predominantly to the cytosol (Fig. 2E). Consistently, CEND1-overexpressing (OE) cells resulted up-regulation of neuronal markers Tuj1, NeuN, and MAP2 expression, indicating partial neuron-like fate direction (Fig. 2F, G). Morphologically, CEND1-OE cells adopted an elongated, bipolar or multipolar phenotype with extensive neurite-like projections and higher-order branching. Immunofluorescence analysis also revealed MAP2, PSD95 and Tuj1 up-regulated in CEND1-OE cells, further supporting neuron-like transdifferentiation (Fig. 2H, I).

We next assessed the functional results by CEND1-OE, and found that elevated CEND1 led to a significant reduction in cell viability in both U87 and T98G cells (Fig. 3A, D). EdU assay further demonstrated a decrease of the percentage of EdU + cells, indicating reduced cell proliferative capacity (Fig. 3B, C, E, F). Consistent with the decreased cell proliferation, Western blot analysis showed a pronounced downregulation of the cell-cycle driver Cyclin D1 accompanied by upregulation of the cell-cycle inhibitor p21 (Fig. 3G, H). Moreover, transwell assay revealed that CEND1-OE cells exhibited decreased cell migrative motility (Fig. 3I–L). All these in vitro results support the notion that CEND1 overexpression exerts potent anti-proliferative effects in glioma cells.

To validate the inhibitory effect of CEND1 on cell growth in vivo, we established intracranial xenografts by implanting luciferase-labeled Ctrl or CEND1-OE U87 cells into the brain of BALB/c nude mice. Bioluminescence imaging on day 7 post-injection revealed robust tumor signals in all Ctrl animals, whereas only one CEND1-OE mouse exhibited detectable luminescence (Fig. 4A). Serial imaging at days 14 and 21 demonstrated progressive tumor growth in Ctrl group, while CEND1-OE tumors exhibited significantly decelerated growth kinetics (Fig. 4A, B). Kaplan–Meier analysis showed an extension of median OS from 19 days in the Ctrl group to 28.5 days in the CEND1-OE group (P = 0.0186) (Fig. 4C). Histological examination of brain sections revealed larger tumor volumes in Ctrl compared with CEND1-OE mice by H&E staining (Fig. 4D). IHC of PCNA confirmed a dramatic reduction in proliferative index within CEND1-OE tumors (Fig. 4D, E). To further verify that CEND1 overexpression promotes neuronal differentiation of U87 cells, we also examined the expression of Tuj1 in vivo. Consistent with the in vitro findings, the Tuj1 levels was markedly increased in the CEND1-OE group (Fig. S3A, B). To extend these findings to an immunocompetent context, we generated intracranial implants of luciferase-expressing Ctrl and CEND1-OE GL261 cells in C57BL/6 mice (Fig. S3C). Similarly, CEND1-OE in GL261 mouse glioma cells significantly inhibited tumor growth and prolonged median OS relative to Ctrl (median OS: 37.5 vs. 27.5 days; P = 0.0019) (Fig. S3D, E). Consistent with these survival benefits, H&E and PCNA staining of mouse brain tissues confirmed reduced tumor volume and lower proliferative activity in CEND1-OE allografts (Fig. S3F, G). Collectively, all these data demonstrate that CEND1 robustly inhibits glioma progression and extends survival in animal models, implying its potential as a therapeutic target of glioma treatment.

Elevated CEND1 expression inhibits glioma mitochondrial energetics

Given that malignant glioma cells depend on activated metabolism to fuel rapid proliferation, we hypothesized that CEND1-mediated neuronal-like phenotype and cell proliferation arrest would be accompanied by a concomitant reduction of energetic production. To test this hypothesis, we performed metabolomic profiling on U87 cells of CEND1-OE versus Ctrl. Pathway enrichment analysis of differentially abundant metabolites revealed a marked alteration of glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation pathways (Fig. 5A, B). Particularly, levels of key mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation intermediates such as fumaric acid and L-malic acid were significantly decreased in CEND1-OE group, whereas glycolytic metabolites like glucose, fructose-6-phosphate and lactate were increased (Figs. 5B, S4A–F). As a result, intracellular ATP level was reduced upon CEND1 overexpression, indicating impaired mitochondrial energy production in glioma cells by elevated CEND1 (Fig. 5C).

Our prior work has proved the central role of pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) in mitochondrial ATP generation [8], and thus we examined PDH activity and observed a pronounced increase in the inhibitory phosphorylation of the PDH E1α subunit at Ser293, indicating reduced PDH enzymatic activity (Fig. 5D, E). These results support a model that CEND1 impairs PDH activity and inhibits mitochondrial energetics, thereby reducing ATP production. It’s also noted that expression of glycolytic enzymes hexokinase 2 (HK2) and pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) remained preserved (Fig. S4G, H). Considering previous reports of CEND1 modulating mitochondrial morphology in neurons [22], we further assessed mitochondrial dynamics in glioma cells via MitoTracker staining and dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) analysis. Glioma cells displayed a predominantly fragmented mitochondrial network regardless of CEND1 status, and neither total nor phosphorylated DRP1 levels were altered by CEND1-OE (Fig. S4I–M). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that elevated CEND1 impairs glioma cell metabolism by specifically suppressing mitochondrial energy production.

CEND1 activates AMPK signaling and sensitizes glioma cells to metformin

Finally, we investigated the molecular mechanisms by which CEND1 coordinately inhibited cell growth and mitochondrial metabolism in glioma cells, and thus assessed energy-sensing pathways in CEND1-OE glioma cells. Because AMPK is a major pathway that responds to energy status, and exerts cell cycle suppressor functions [23], we examined AMPK activity in CEND1-OE cells. Results of Western blots showed an increase in phosphorylation of AMPKα at Thr172 and its downstream substrate ACC at Ser79 [24], accompanied by upregulation of PAK3 [25] (Fig. 5F, G). We also examined p-AMPK (Thr172) expression in our orthotopic xenograft mouse model, which revealed elevated p-AMPK (Thr172) levels in the CEND1-OE group (Fig. S3A, B), indicating that AMPK signaling is activated by CEND1-OE. To further clarify the impact of CEND1 reduction on mitochondrial metabolism, we performed a seahorse mitochondrial stress tests to evaluate the effect of CEND1 overexpression on mitochondrial respiratory capacity in U87 cells. We found that the mitochondrial basal respiration, maximal respiration and ATP production were significantly impaired in CEND1-OE group compared with control (Fig. 5H–K). It’s noted that CEND1-OE did not affect the activity of mTORC1 signaling, indicated by the unchanged protein levels of phospho-S6 (Ser240/244) and phospho-4EBP1 (Thr37/46) (Fig. S4N, O). These results support a model that CEND1 impairs PDH activity, reduced mitochondrial respiratory capacity, lowers ATP levels, and thereby activates AMPK signaling.

Given the dual role of AMPK as an energy sensor and cell-cycle suppressor which may be responsible for the inhibitory effect of cell growth of CEND1-OE, we wonder whether CEND1 OE alters cellular sensitivity to metformin, a canonical AMPK activator [26]. Cell viability assay revealed that CEND1 OE potentiates the anti-proliferative effect of metformin on glioma cells (Fig. S5A). Consistently, dose–response assays demonstrated a significant reduction in the IC50 of metformin in CEND1-OE cells (5.081 mM) versus Ctrl (9.921 mM) (Fig. S5B). Moreover, cell viability assays using metformin at the IC₅₀ concentration of CEND1-OE cells (5.081 mM) further confirmed their enhanced susceptibility to AMPK-dependent metabolic perturbation (Fig. S5C). Western blot results further confirmed that upon metformin treatment, CEND1-OE cells exhibited a more pronounced decrease in Cyclin D1 levels and an increase of p21 compared with Ctrl (Fig. S5D, E). Collectively, these results establish that CEND1 activates AMPK signaling and primes glioma cells for enhanced efficacy of AMPK-targeted therapies.

Elevated CEND1 expression induces neurotransdifferentiation in an AMPK activation–dependent manner

To determine whether modulation of the AMPK pathway influences the neuronal-like transformation induced by CEND1 overexpression, we examined the effects of pharmacological manipulation of AMPK signaling in U87 cells. Treatment with 1 µM doxorubicin (DOX), an established AMPK inhibitor [27, 28], did not significantly affect U87 cell viability but partially attenuated the upregulation of neuronal markers, including MAP2 and Tuj1, as confirmed by immunofluorescence staining analysis (Figs. S5F–H, S5K). Furthermore, treatment with DOX led to a clear reduction of p-AMPK (Thr172) protein levels, and the upregulation of neuronal markers NeuN, MAP2, and PSD95 observed in the CEND1-OE group was markedly attenuated compared with the control (Fig. S5I, J). These findings suggest that AMPK inhibition compromises, but does not completely abolish, the neuronal-like phenotype driven by CEND1. Collectively, our results indicate that AMPK activity contributes to the neuronal differentiation–like transformation induced by CEND1, thereby mechanistically linking AMPK-dependent mitochondrial regulation to the neuronal phenotype observed in glioma cells.

Discussion

Aggressive proliferation and metabolic adaptation contribute to poor clinical prognosis of glioma patients [29], necessitating novel targets capable of concurrently reprogram glioma cells toward a neuron-like, less proliferative, and metabolically suppressed state. Here, we identified CEND1 as a neuronal differentiation regulator dramatically reduced in glioma tissues, and indicated less tumor malignancy and better patient survival. Elevated CEND1 expression effectively attenuated glioma cell proliferation, migration, and mitochondrial metabolism. Mechanistically, CEND1 activated AMPK signaling and sensitization of glioma cells to metformin-induced growth inhibition. Our findings establish CEND1 as a novel suppressor of glioma growth by concurrently modulating proliferation and metabolic energetics (Fig. 6).

Diagram illustrating that CEND1 induces neuron-like transdifferentiation and inhibits glioma proliferation. Reduced CEND1 expression correlates with glioma malignancy and poor prognosis. CEND1 suppresses glioma mitochondrial energetics by reducing PDH activity and CEND1-driven AMPK activation inhibits glioma growth and enhances metformin sensitivity

In recent years, transdifferentiation of glioma cells into terminally differentiated neuron-like phenotypes has emerged as a promising approach. Neurons, characterized by their terminal differentiation status and minimal proliferative capacity, present an ideal cellular fate for reprogramming strategies aimed at reducing glioma malignancy. Previous research mainly focused on transcription-factor-driven approaches, employing factors such as ASCL1, NeuroD1, and Ngn2 to redirect glioma cell fate toward neuronal lineages [30]; however, these studies did not address the metabolic dependencies to rapid glioma cell proliferation. Based on these studies, we sought to identify factors capable of coordinately suppressing cell proliferation and altering metabolic pathways. We identified CEND1 as a potential candidate. We found weak CEND1 expression in glioma tissues and cell lines, consistent with its known role as a promoter of neuronal differentiation. Forced overexpression of CEND1 in multiple glioma cell lines resulted in morphological changes indicative of neuron-like transdifferentiation, coupled with inhibition of cell proliferation. These growth inhibitory effects were validated in glioma animal models. Furthermore, CEND1 exerts significant metabolic effects, notably the suppression of ATP production via inhibiting PDH activity. Therefore, the dual-functionality of CEND1 on cell proliferation and energetic production represents a substantial advancement beyond previously described neuronal transdifferentiation strategies, highlighting CEND1 as a promising therapeutic target capable of achieving both neuronal fate conversion and metabolic inhibition.

CEND1 was originally identified as a critical regulator of neuronal differentiation through its role in modulating neural progenitor cell (NPC) proliferation and differentiation in the central nervous system [20]. Previous studies documented its function in orchestrating the timely exit of NPCs from the cell cycle and their differentiation into mature neurons [31]. CEND1 can also direct cortical astrocytes toward neuronal lineages [32], highlighting its robust neurogenic capabilities beyond NPC populations. Moreover, altered CEND1 expression is also implicated in neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease [22] and depression [33]. These findings established the role of CEND1 as a regulator of neuronal identity and brain homeostasis. Extending these insights to oncology, our study investigated the role of CEND1 in glioma, an NPC- or glial progenitor-derived primary brain malignancy. Some pilot studies have reported that reduced CEND1 expression correlates with glioma poor prognosis and resistance to temozolomide chemotherapy [34, 35]. Our study provides more comprehensive clinical, in vitro, and in vivo evidence to demonstrate that elevated CEND1 robustly inhibits glioma cell proliferation and mitochondrial metabolism by activating AMPK signaling.

Mechanistically, the observed neuron-like differentiation of glioma cells induced by CEND1 originates from its natural function as a neuronal differentiation factor, aligning with previously documented mechanisms of neuronal lineage specification [21, 36]. Additionally, the anti-proliferative effect of CEND1 in glioma cells is mediated by the activation of AMPK. AMPK activation is known to inhibit cell cycle progression through the suppression of Cyclin D1 expression. In our study, we used DOX at a concentration of 1 µM, which was confirmed by cell viability assays to exert no significant cytotoxic effect on U87 cells. Under this non-toxic condition, DOX effectively attenuated the CEND1-induced increase of neuronal markers such as Tuj1 and MAP2. These findings indicate that the observed effect was not due to general cytotoxicity but rather to the inhibition of AMPK signaling. Previous reports have also demonstrated that DOX can suppress AMPK activation in different cell types [27, 28, 37], supporting our conclusion that the neuron-like transdifferentiation driven by CEND1 is partially AMPK dependent. Thus, our results highlight a mechanistic role of AMPK in glioma cell plasticity and validate the use of low-dose DOX as a pharmacological tool to dissect AMPK-dependent signaling in glioblastoma models. Intriguingly, this mechanism parallels the action of metformin, which similarly facilitates Cyclin D1 degradation via AMPK [38]. Consistent with these insights, our study revealed that CEND1 overexpression activates AMPK by lowering cellular ATP levels, consequently reducing Cyclin D1 levels and arresting cell proliferation. Furthermore, the metabolic suppressive effect of CEND1 appears predominantly mediated through its inhibition of PDH activity. Our prior work established PDH as a critical regulator of acetyl-CoA synthesis, TCA cycle flux, and ATP generation, modulated by reversible phosphorylation at multiple regulatory sites in neurons [8]. Our current findings extend this finding to glioma, demonstrating that CEND1 inhibits PDH, thereby attenuating acetyl-CoA production and reducing ATP synthesis. Notably, the impact of CEND1 on glioma metabolism specifically targets mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation without affecting mitochondrial morphological dynamics—processes largely governed by mTORC1 signaling [39]. To support this notion, we observed that mTORC1 activity remained unchanged in response to CEND1 overexpression. Altogether, our results support the model that CEND1 inhibits mitochondrial energy production and thereby activate AMPK, ultimately inducing cell proliferation arrest.

Conclusions

In sum, our findings define CEND1 as a multifaceted suppressor of glioma progression. By coordinately driving neuron-like transdifferentiation, blocking mitochondrial energy production and activating AMPK signaling, CEND1 exerts both anti-proliferative and anti-bioenergetic functions in glioma, thereby suppressing tumor growth and enhancing responsiveness to metformin, highlighting its therapeutic potential in glioma treatment.
