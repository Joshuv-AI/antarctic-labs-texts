Crop classification in the middle reaches of the Hei River based on model transfer
Authors: Huazhu Xue, Yongkang Fan, Guotao Dong, Shi He, Yaokang Lian, Wenfei Luan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Crop classification using remote sensing technology is highly important for monitoring agricultural resources and managing water usage, especially in water-scarce regions like the Hei River. Crop classification requires a substantial number of labeled samples, but the collection of labeled samples demands significant resources and sample data may not be available for some years. To classify crops in sample-free years in the middle reaches of the Hei River, we generated multisource spectral data (MSSD) based on a spectral library and sample data. We pre-trained a model using labeled samples, followed by fine-tuning the model with MSSD to complete the crop classification for the years without samples. We conduct experiments using three CNN-based deep learning models and a machine learning model (RF). The experimental results indicate that in the model transfer experiments, using a fine-tuned model yields accurate classification results, with overall accuracy exceeding 90%. When the amount of labeled sample data is limited, fine-tuning the model based on MSSD can enhance the accuracy of crop classification. Overall, fine-tuning models based on MSSD can significantly enhance the accuracy of model transfer and reduce the reliance of deep learning models on large-scale sample datasets. The method to classify crops in the middle reaches of the Hei River can provide data support for local resource utilization and policy formulation.

Introduction

With the increasing population and social economy development, it is necessary to increase crop yields to ensure food security. The middle reaches of the Hei River are a representative inland river basin in China and a significant commodity grain production area in the northwestern region of China1. It plays an important role in maintaining the ecological security and food security stability of the Hexi Corridor region. However, due to the limited precipitation, most crops in the middle reaches of the Hei River rely on irrigation. Surface water from the river serves as the primary source of irrigation in this region. Against the background of the continuous decrease of arable land area in China, the expansion of cultivated land in the arid regions of Northwest China has been notable2. This trend has further intensified the existing tensions between water supply and demand in the region. Against the background of high agricultural water demand, accurately mapping the crop planting structure in the middle reaches of the Hei River is crucial for estimating crop yields and managing water resources effectively.

Crop planting structure refers to the distribution of crop types, reflecting the utilization of agricultural resources in a particular region, which is an important basis for understanding the water resource adjustment and adjusting the agricultural structure3–5. Therefore, fine classification of crops is of strong support for agricultural production, policy making and ecological environment protection, which is important for precise management of present agricultural production and ensuring national food security6,7. However, identifying the multi-year crop planting structure in a region presents significant challenges due to factors such as climate variability and human activities. Remote sensing is a technology for remotely perceiving targets or natural phenomena and is characterized by a broad observation range, strong timeliness, and long-distance perception. It is widely applied in agricultural monitoring tasks, promoting the development of agricultural production toward intelligence, scale, and specialization, and accelerating the effective utilization and rational distribution of agricultural resources8,9.

In recent years, remote sensing image interpretation based on deep learning has become one of the commonly used methods for obtaining crop planting structures. Convolutional neural network (CNN) is a type of deep learning model that were originally applied for semantic segmentation and target detection. Subsequently, they have also demonstrated strong performance in the classification of remote sensing images10–13. For example, Xu et al.5 constructed a CNN model based on pyramid pooling to classify crops in the Erhai Lake Basin and Beian City using GF-6 and Sentinel-2 satellite data. The overall accuracy was 94.17% and 92.28%, respectively. But CNN models can only learn local features, and it is difficult to learn global features. To address this challenge, several studies have proposed enhancing model performance through the integration of CNN and attention mechanisms14–17. Zhang et al.18 developed SANet by integrating 3D CNN with multiple attention structures to classify crops in the WHU-Hi-HongHu, WHU-Hi-HanChuan, and WHU-Hi-LongKou hyperspectral datasets. The model attained overall accuracies of 97.12%, 96.49%, and 99.78% across the three datasets, respectively. The development of deep learning has greatly improved the accuracy of crop classification using remote sensing imagery, but deep learning models require many accurate and comprehensive labeled samples for model training19. Sample collection primarily relies on field surveys and manual interpretation through high-resolution imagery, which is time-consuming and labor-intensive. This makes it difficult to collect data across large study areas or ensure sufficient samples over consecutive years, thereby limiting the application of deep learning in crop classification.

To address these challenges, several studies have employed model transfer by utilizing large sample datasets or generating new samples, enabling crop classification in regions with sparse or missing sample data20–25. Rusnak et al.26 utilized sample data from 2018 to 2021 in the Danubian Lowlands to train machine learning classification models, including Random Forest (RF) and Support Vector Machine (SVM). The trained models were then applied to classify crops in 2022, achieving an overall accuracy of 89%. This method successfully classified crops in years without sample data; however, it requires a substantial amount of labeled samples for model training, which limits its widespread applicability. Nowakowski et al.27 utilized high-resolution imagery collected by drones to fine-tune pre-trained VGG16 and GoogLeNet models for crop mapping in African. The fine-tuned models outperformed those trained from scratch, achieving an accuracy of 90% in Gaza Province, Mozambique. Similarly, Wang et al.28 used unlabeled remote sensing images for model pre-training, followed by fine-tuning the pre-trained model with labeled sample data from the target domain. The research focused on crop classification in five regions: the Hexi Corridor, Ili River Basin, Tianshan Corridor, and Western and Eastern Heilongjiang, achieving overall accuracies between 79% and 87%. These methods significantly reduce the reliance of deep learning models on sample data; however, they do not address the issue of crop classification in regions with no available labeled sample data. Furthermore, these model transfer approaches primarily focus on mitigating spatial domain shifts between geographic areas, while overlooking the temporal domain shifts caused by changes over time.

Spectral libraries contain pure endmember spectral data, which can accurately reflect the spectral characteristics of various land cover classes. For different crops, the endmember spectral characteristics during crop growth season would be different. Some studies have indicated that using endmember spectral data for land cover classification is feasible in the absence of sample data29,30. Roth et al.31 employed spectral mixture analysis techniques to extract endmember spectra from hyperspectral imagery and demonstrated the applicability of endmember data in image classification tasks. Moreover, Priem et al.32 generated sample data through linear mixing of endmember spectra for land classification, comparing the results with those based on sample data, thereby validating that the accuracy of land classification using endmember spectra is not inferior to that of classification based on sample data. Although endmember spectra have been applied in land cover classification, there is a scarcity of research on their application in crop classification. Consequently, investigating the application of endmember spectra in crop classification is deemed necessary.

To resolve these issues, we integrate endmember spectral data of crops with model fine-tuning algorithm to facilitate model transfer, thereby generating a multi-year crop planting structure map for the middle reaches of the Hei River. The main content of this paper includes using 2019 sample data and endmember spectral data from various crops as training data, we trained and migrated the model to perform crop classification in 2017–2018 and analyze the effect of endmember spectral data on the accuracy of model migration.

Materials

Study region

The study area is located in the northern region of Zhangye city, extending from the Qilian Mountains in the south to the Heli Mountains in the north (98°57′E-100°52′E, 38°32′N-39°59′N; Fig. 1), with a total area of 10,736 square kilometers. Ganzhou District, Linze County, and Gaotai County have a temperate continental climate, continental desert grassland climate, and cold temperate continental arid climate, respectively, with the major crops being maize, wheat, and vegetables, and the planting areas of various crops as shown in Table 1. These three regions exhibit differences in their natural environments and cropping structures. Therefore, the model is trained with sample data from Ganzhou District. The sample data from Linze County and Gaotai County, which are selected as validation data for model transfer. By comparing the classification accuracy across these three regions, a more comprehensive assessment of the model’s generalization ability and robustness can be achieved. The study area is an important maize cultivation region in China, making the establishment of a stable and accurate crop planting monitoring system essential.

Study data

Optical images are susceptible to the influence of weather conditions, making it challenging to ensure both the quantity and quality of remote sensing imagery. In contrast, synthetic aperture radar (SAR) imagery remains unaffected by atmospheric conditions. The fusion of optical and SAR imagery is known to enhance the precision of crop classification33. Hence, we employ Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 image data as experimental datasets.

The Sentinel-1 satellite consists of two satellites, Sentinel-1 A and Sentinel-1B, both of which are equipped with C-band SAR. The revisit period for a single satellite is 12 days, and the combined revisit period for both satellites is 6 days. The Sentinel-2 satellite consists of two satellites, Sentinel-2 A and Sentinel-2B, which are equipped with a Multi-Spectral Instrument (MSI). The revisit period for a single satellite is 10 days, and the combined revisit period for both satellites is 5 days.

Field sampling data were collected via GPS in May and July 2019 within the training region, resulting in 2886 samples. For regions lacking samples, visual interpretation was performed based on optical imagery from Google Earth with a 1 m resolution to acquire sample data, as shown in Table 2. The sample data from Ganzhou District in 2019 was split into a training set and a test set in a 7:3 ratio. The remaining sample data was used as a test set to validate the model’s transfer accuracy.

To exclude the influence of buildings, wasteland, etc. on the experimental results, the image data were masked using vector areas, keeping only the vegetated areas in the study area.

Data preprocessing

This study utilizes remote sensing images obtained from the Google Earth Engine (GEE) cloud platform. The Sentinel-1 images are Level-1 GRD data, which have been processed for orbit restitution, terrain correction, and radiometric calibration. The Sentinel-1 images are filtered using the Lee filter34 to diminish the influence of speckle noise on the classification results. Research indicates that obtaining time series information from synthesized SAR imagery can improve the accuracy of crop classification results35,36. Therefore, SAR images for the primary crop growing season, from April to October, are selected for monthly mean synthesis. According to the research, when combining optical and SAR images for crop classification, using VH coefficients can obtain accurate classification results, and using the two coefficients, VH and VV, cannot further improve the classification accuracy37,38. Therefore, we choose the VH coefficients of SAR images as the research data.

The Sentinel-2 images are Level-2 A data, which have been processed for atmospheric correction. By utilizing remote sensing imagery from various crucial periods, vital features of crop growth can be obtained, thereby improving crop classification accuracy39,40. In early May, wheat is in the jointing stage, while other crops have not yet been sown or have not germinated. By late July, wheat has been harvested, maize is in the jointing stage, and some vegetables are being planted. In late September, maize reaches the grain-filling stage, nearing harvest, and exhibits distinctive characteristic information. Consequently, we selected images from these three critical phenological stages to perform crop classification. By filtering remote sensing images during the growing period of crops, cloud-free images were obtained for three periods, as shown in Table 3. Analysis of the imagery confirmed the absence of cloud cover over the farmland, ensuring that cloud would not impact the accuracy of crop classification.

Generation of training data

The training data in this study consisted of two parts: a sample spectral dataset (SSD) generated based on sample data and a mixed spectral dataset (MSD) generated based on a spectral library. The data obtained by fusing the SSD with MSD are referred to as multisource spectral data (MSSD). MSD is generated by mixing endmember spectra of multiple crop types in different proportions. The calculation formula is as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M=\sum\nolimits_{{i=1}}^{j} {{f_i}{r_i}}$$\end{document}

where j denotes the number of endmember spectra in the mixed spectrum, ri denotes the reflectance curve of the endmember spectrum i, and fi represents the weight of the endmember spectrum i.

The study region primarily includes maize, wheat, vegetables, and others. Vegetables include cabbage, broccoli, rapeseed, etc., and others include trees, alfalfa, nurseries, etc. We employed the pixel purity index (PPI)41 and n-dimensional visualization tools to obtain endmember spectra for various crops from remote sensing imagery, which together form a spectral library. This process was conducted within the ENVI software environment.

In the study area, there are distinct boundaries between land parcels, with most mixed pixels occurring at the edges of these parcels; these pixels contain spectral information from two land cover types and are identified as binary mixed spectra. A minority of mixed pixels contain spectral information from three land cover types, identified as ternary mixed spectra. Mixed pixels that contain spectral information from more than three land cover types are rare and thus not considered.

Before conducting spectral mixing, it is essential to determine the weights of the endmember spectra for each land cover class. These weights can be defined using a constant step size or arbitrary percentages. To ensure a more representative mixed spectrum, we employ a constant step size for determining the spectral weights. The mixed spectrum is divided into the target spectrum and the background spectrum, and the weight value of the target spectrum is larger. For binary mixed spectra, the weights of the background spectra are set between 0 and 0.4. For ternary mixed spectra, the sum of the weights of the background spectra is constrained within the range of 0 to 0.5.

In the study area, 2000 binary mixed spectra and 1000 ternary mixed spectra were generated based on actual conditions.

JM distance provides a much reliable criterion because as a function of class separability, it behaves much more like probability of correct classification43,44. A JM distance exceeding 1.9 signifies that the spectra demonstrate excellent separability. When the JM distance falls within the range of 1.9 to 1.8, it suggests a moderate level of separability. Model transfer method

The model transfer method in this paper consists of two steps: the pre-training phase and the fine-tuning phase. Compared to training a new model from scratch, fine-tuning a pre-trained model yields better classification results26. During the model pre-training phase, sample data from the Ganzhou District in 2019 is used to train the model, allowing it to learn the growth patterns of crops and accumulate substantial prior knowledge. Specifically, the sample data is augmented by randomly adding noise points, thereby expanding the sample size and enabling the model to better handle previously unseen crop characteristics. Subsequently, the parameters of the pre-trained model are assigned to the new model, with all parameters set to be learnable, and fine-tuning is performed using MSSD. In the pre-training phase, the epoch, batch size, and learning rate are set to 200, 64, and 1e-3, respectively. In the fine-tuning phase, these parameters are adjusted to 20 epochs, 64 batch size, and a learning rate of 1e-4.

To validate the effectiveness of model fine-tuning based on MSSD, we applied models trained on the sample dataset directly to classify crops in different years or regions. The approach that utilizes MSSD for model fine-tuning is referred to as MSSD-based method (M_MSSD), while the approach that employs only SSD for model training is referred to as SSD-based method (M_SSD).

Modeling classification model

The endmember extraction method can extract spectral information for various crops. We concatenate the obtained optical endmember spectra with the SAR endmember spectra to create one-dimensional data to be used as training data for the classification model38. To better process the spectral feature information of crops, we constructed a multi-scale attention extraction model (MSA_Net) by integrating one-dimensional convolution operations with an attention mechanism, as illustrated in Fig. 3. MSA_Net incorporates a multi-scale attention extraction module based on convolutional neural networks. This module consists of a feature compression (FC) component and a spectral self-attention (SSA) component. Prior to extracting attention information, the model employs a linear connection layer to encode the spectral information at each time point.

FC: By applying a convolution operation to the input data, feature information at different scales can be obtained. Convolutional operations preserve important features while removing redundant information, facilitating subsequent attention extraction. Result

Endmember spectral analysis

Figure 5 presents the endmember spectral data for various types of crops, with representative samples of tomato selected for the vegetable category and forest for the other category. This figure illustrates that different crops exhibit distinct characteristic information. Maize is planted in late May, resulting in lower VH values before June. In contrast, wheat and some vegetables with double cropping are sown in early to mid-April, leading to higher VH values compared to maize before June. Due to the similar planting times of wheat and some vegetables, their backscattering coefficient curves show little distinction. However, as illustrated in Fig. 5(a), there are significant differences in the spectral characteristics of these two crop types in optical imagery. Before July, the spectral values for vegetables are higher than those for wheat, but this trend reverses by September, when the values for vegetables fall below those of wheat. In the period before the planting of crops, the VH values for trees are distinctly higher than those for crops, enabling effective differentiation between them. Due to the significant differences in growth cycles and spectral characteristics among various vegetation types, the VH values from SAR imagery, along with the spectral values from optical imagery, can be utilized to distinguish between different vegetation categories.

To verify whether the endmember spectral have good separability, the JM distances of different crop types under two distinct datasets were calculated, and the results are shown in Fig. 6. When utilizing the SSD, the JM distances between some crop types are less than 1.8, indicating low separability among categories. However, when utilizing the MSD, the separability between crop types significantly improved, especially for the JM distance between wheat, vegetables, and the other category was greater than 1.9. The results of the JM distances demonstrate the good separability of the MSD.

Evaluation of the classification accuracy of different models

In order to better utilize the temporal spectral characteristics of various crops in remote sensing images, this study trains MSA_Net, Self-Attention-1D-CNN (SA_Net)47, Double-Branch Multi-Attention Mechanism Network (DBMA)48, and Random Forest (RF)49four models and performs accuracy evaluation. To verify the effectiveness of the multi-scale attention extraction module in the MSA_Net model, we conducted an ablation study by removing this module (No_MSA) from the experiments. To mitigate the impact of training randomness on the results, each model was trained five times, and the average accuracy was calculated as the result.

The classification accuracy results are presented in Table 4. MSA_Net achieves the best performance, with an overall accuracy and kappa coefficient of 97.93% and 0.9658, respectively. The classification accuracy of the RF model is greater than that of the two deep learning models SA_Net and DBMA, with an overall accuracy and kappa coefficient of 97.17% and 0.9534, respectively, which is in line with the conclusions of Wang28. By comparing the classification results of the MSA_Net and No_MSA, it is evident that MSA_Net achieves higher classification accuracy. This demonstrates that the multi-scale attention extraction module enables the model to learn spectral feature information more effectively. In summary, all four models effectively handle one-dimensional spectral information, providing a wealth of usable classification models for subsequent transfer experiments.

Model transfer accuracy assessment

To investigate the influence of M_MSSD on crop classification and model transfer, the classification models MSA_Net, SA_Net, DBAM, and RF were utilized. Each model was trained five times, and the average value was calculated as the final accuracy. To examine whether the endmember spectra of crops can enhance model transferability, the RF classification model was trained using MSSD and SSD, respectively. The trained models were then applied to classify crops in years or regions without available samples, without any model fine-tuning. The model accuracy was validated using test datasets from the Ganzhou District in 2017, 2018, and 2019, Gaotai County in 2019, and Linze County in 2019, which are called Scenario 1, Scenario 2, Scenario 3, Scenario 4, and Scenario 5, respectively. The accuracy results are shown in Fig. 7.

In Scenario 3, when using M_SSD, the overall accuracy of the four models was 97.93%, 95.40%, 96.82%, and 97.17%, respectively. When using M_MSSD, the overall accuracy of the four models was 97.83%, 97.37%, 97.37%, and 97.67%, respectively. The classification accuracy of the two methods exhibited only minor differences, with MSA_Net achieving higher accuracy when using M_SSD. This is because SSD consists of sample data from the target region, making it highly representative and effectively reflecting the characteristics of various crops. In contrast, the mixed spectral data in MSSD is generated through linear mixing, which may not accurately represent the true spectra of the crops. When sufficient sample data is available, the mixed spectra do not contribute to improving classification accuracy.

In Scenario 1, the transfer accuracy of all four models is notably greater with the use of the M_MSSD than with the M_SSD. When using the M_SSD, the overall accuracies of the four models were 93.79%, 93.32%, 92.94%, and 92.97%, respectively. When using the M_MSSD, these accuracies increased to 95.59%, 94.77%, 94.23%, and 94.41%. In Scenario 2, when using M_SSD, the models exhibited poor transfer performance, with overall accuracy of 93.64%, 93.31%, 92.93%, and 92.97%, respectively. When using M_MSSD, the overall accuracy of the four models increased to 94.98%, 94.14%, 94.21%, and 94.10%, indicating a significant improvement in transfer accuracy. In Scenario 4 and Scenario 5, when using the M_SSD, the overall accuracy of all four models was 93.37%, 92.52%, 93.18%, 92.66%, and 92.98%, 90.99%, 92.98%, 92.22%, respectively. However, with M_MSSD, the transfer accuracy of each model significantly improved, with overall accuracy reaching 95.14%, 93.98%, 94.44%, 93.71%, and 95.96%, 92.98%, 94.80%, 94.73%, respectively. In conclusion, M_MSSD can effectively improve the classification accuracy of crop models after transfer.

Discussion

Uncertainty analysis of model transfer method

During the model transfer process, there may be uncertain factors that affect accuracy. First, we employed a linear mixing approach to construct mixed spectra. However, remote sensing images often contain mixed spectra generated by nonlinear mixing, which can impact the accuracy of model transfer32. Nonlinear mixing arises from interactions among various land cover types. Existing nonlinear mixing models require prior knowledge of endmembers or corresponding feature information, making them challenging to apply broadly50. Second, the generation of mixed endmember spectra did not take into account other factors affecting crop growth, such as crop variety, natural environment, and human influences, resulting in discrepancies between the generated mixed endmember spectra and the mixed pixels. Therefore, when the sample size is sufficient, the feature information within the mixed endmember spectra may not further enhance classification accuracy. Third, this study employs endmember extraction algorithms to obtain the spectral endmember of various crops from remote sensing imagery. However, due to the limitations of these algorithms, there is a discrepancy between the extracted endmember spectra and the true spectral information of the crops. Endmember spectra can be acquired through field measurements using a spectrometer, which provide more accurate spectral data. Zhong et al.51 compiled a spectral library that includes the endmember spectra of various land covers in China, significantly enriching the data available for crop classification. Future research should consider utilizing existing spectral libraries for crop classification, promoting the application of spectral libraries and deep learning models in agricultural resource monitoring.

Limitations analysis

In this paper, the extraction of multi-year crop cropping structure was conducted in the middle reaches of the Hei River, which is a small study area. Future research should apply the model transfer methods used in this study to larger regions, such as Northeast China and the North China Plain, to validate the performance of this approach.

Since endmember spectra do not contain spatial information, we employed 1D CNN as the classification model, which tends to exhibit salt-and-pepper noise in the classification results. Methods such as 3D CNN and Markov Random Field (MRF) have been shown to effectively mitigate this noise in classification outputs49,52,53. The occurrence of same-object-different-spectrum and same-spectrum-different-object phenomena, attributed to crop growth patterns and remote sensing image resolution, renders pixel-based classification methods susceptible to misclassifications. Extracting spatial information of pixels can effectively reduce such misclassifications, thereby establishing a more accurate and robust agricultural monitoring system.

Conclusions

In this study, we employed model fine-tuning techniques along with a crop spectral library for model transfer to achieve multi-year crop classification in the middle reaches of the Hei River. By fine-tuning a pre-trained model with mixed endmember spectra, we achieved an overall classification accuracy exceeding 90% for years without sample data, representing a significant improvement compared to model transfer that relied solely on sample data. When the sample size was reduced, the use of mixed endmember spectra during model fine-tuning effectively mitigated the decline in classification accuracy observed in both crop classification and model transfer experiments. Overall, classifying crops and transferring models based on mixed endmember spectra is an effective approach to reducing the need for large-scale sample data in deep learning models.
