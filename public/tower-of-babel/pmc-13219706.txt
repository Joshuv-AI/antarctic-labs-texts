Development of VP1 based indirect ELISAs for BK and JC polyomaviruses with seroprevalence assessment and cross reactivity evaluation
Authors: Amir Hossein Alipour, Fatemeh Hoda Fallah, Bahman Abedi Kiasari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBK virus (BKV) and JC virus (JCV) are widespread human polyomaviruses with significant clinical relevance, especially in immunocompromised populations. Reliable serological assays play a key role in epidemiological surveillance and clinical diagnostics. In this study, indirect ELISAs based on recombinant VP1 capsid proteins of BKV and JCV—expressed and purified using insect cell-based systems—were developed and optimized. A serological analysis of 67 human serum samples revealed an overall seroprevalence of 77.6% for BKV and 34.3% for JCV, with antibody prevalence increasing progressively with age. Importantly, competitive inhibition ELISAs demonstrated no significant cross-reactivity between antibodies against BKV and JCV VP1 antigens, confirming the high specificity of the assays. Comparative evaluation indicated that baculovirus-expressed VP1 antigens yielded higher quality proteins yielding stronger and more reliable assay signals, endorsing their use for large-scale seroepidemiological studies. These findings provide robust serological tools for accurate assessment of polyomavirus exposure and have potential clinical utility in monitoring populations at risk of polyomavirus-associated diseases, such as transplant recipients.Keywords: BK virus, JC virus, VP1 protein, Baculovirus system, ELISA, SeroprevalenceSubject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Human polyomaviruses are a group of small, non-enveloped viruses possessing circular double-stranded DNA genomes. These viruses establish persistent and typically asymptomatic infections in their hosts, with primary exposure generally occurring during early childhood1. To date, at least 15 distinct human polyomaviruses have been identified, among which BK virus (BKV) and JC virus (JCV) are the most extensively studied due to their significant clinical impact, particularly in immunocompromised populations1,2. In addition, Merkel cell polyomavirus (MCPyV) is an oncogenic human polyomavirus causally linked to Merkel cell carcinoma, underscoring the medical relevance of the Polyomaviridae beyond opportunistic disease3. Reactivation of latent BKV infection is a major cause of polyomavirus-associated nephropathy (PVAN) and hemorrhagic cystitis, conditions that predominantly affect renal and hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients, contributing substantially to graft dysfunction and patient morbidity4. Similarly, JCV reactivation precipitates progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), a demyelinating disease of the central nervous system characterized by severe neurological deficits and high mortality, primarily observed in patients with AIDS or those receiving immunosuppressive therapies5.

The major capsid protein VP1 constitutes the primary antigenic component of both BKV and JCV, mediating viral attachment and entry into host cells and eliciting antibody-mediated immune responses6,7. Given its immunodominance and virus-specific antigenic determinants, VP1 serves as an ideal target for the development of serological assays designed to detect virus-specific antibodies6. Given their clinical relevance in immunocompromised individuals, particularly transplant recipients, serological surveillance for BK and JC viruses is primarily important in high-risk patient populations rather than the general public. However, the high sequence homology between VP1 proteins of different polyomaviruses, notably between BKV and JCV, poses challenges to serological assay specificity and accuracy, necessitating the generation of highly specific antigens and optimized immunoassays8,9.

Recombinant expression systems, particularly insect cell-based platforms, offer scalable and reliable production of conformationally accurate VP1 proteins suitable for serological applications10. Among these, baculovirus-mediated expression systems (such as BacMagic™) and plasmid-based insect cell systems (InsectDirect™) represent promising technologies for producing eukaryotic proteins while preserving their native folding and antigenicity11,12. Despite their potential, comparative analyses of these expression platforms focusing on yield, protein quality, and diagnostic utility for VP1 antigens are scarce. Accordingly, this study aims to: (1) compare the expression efficiency of BKV and JCV VP1 proteins utilizing BacMagic and InsectDirect systems; (2) develop and optimize indirect ELISA assays based on purified VP1 proteins; (3) determine age-specific seroprevalence of BKV and JCV antibodies; and (4) evaluate potential cross-reactivity between BKV and JCV VP1 antigens through competitive binding assays to establish assay specificity.

Materials and methods

Study population and specimens

A total of 67 human serum samples submitted to the Clinical Virology Laboratory between January 2013 and September 2013 were included in this study. The cohort included participants ranging from neonates (< 1 day) to adults aged 62 years (median 31 years, mean 30 years). All specimens had been stored at − 70 °C until further use. For the present investigation, archived samples were reanalyzed under the approval of the Ethics Committee of the University of Tehran, Tehran, Iran. To ensure confidentiality, each specimen was anonymized by renumbering and removal of all patient identifiers prior to analysis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at the time of sample collection.

Biosafety and laboratory containment

All work involving human clinical specimens, recombinant DNA constructs, and recombinant baculoviruses was performed in compliance with institutional biosafety policies and approved laboratory procedures. Handling and processing of archived human serum samples and clinical material (including nucleic acid extraction and PCR amplification) were carried out under Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2) containment in a certified Biosafety Level-2 laboratory, using Class II biological safety cabinets for procedures with potential aerosol generation. Personnel used appropriate personal protective equipment (laboratory coat, gloves, and eye protection) and followed standard precautions. Recombinant baculovirus infections of Sf9 cells and subsequent protein expression/purification procedures were conducted under BSL-2 practices as per the baculovirus supplier’s recommendations and institutional guidelines. All waste materials potentially containing infectious agents were decontaminated by autoclaving or chemical disinfection (0.5% sodium hypochlorite) prior to disposal. Where applicable, samples or reagents were rendered non-infectious (for example by heat inactivation or nuclease treatment) prior to downstream handling outside containment.

VP1 expression

VP1 coding sequences correspond to BK polyomavirus (GenBank M23122.1) and the reference JC polyomavirus genome (GenBank NC_001699.1). The coding sequences of the VP1 capsid proteins from BKV and JCV were obtained either by direct amplification from clinical specimens (BKV) or from a plasmid harboring the complete JCV genome (pJCV[1–4]). Amplified fragments spanning the full-length VP1 open reading frame were cloned into vectors compatible with insect cell expression systems. For all constructs, VP1 was expressed as an N‑terminal 6×His fusion protein to facilitate purification by Ni‑NTA affinity chromatography.​ Two insect-based approaches were initially evaluated—transient plasmid transfection (InsectDirect™) and baculovirus-mediated infection (BacMagic™)12. Both systems successfully expressed VP1 in Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) cells; however, the BacMagic™ baculovirus system yielded markedly higher levels of soluble VP1 and was therefore selected for all large-scale protein productions. High-titer recombinant baculovirus stocks were generated and used for VP1 expression under optimized infection conditions. Expression was confirmed by VP1-specific immunofluorescence and PCR verification of recombinant constructs.

VP1 purification

Recombinant VP1 proteins were extracted from infected Sf9 cell pellets and culture supernatants using CytoBuster® (Merck Millipore), which provided the highest recovery efficiency in preliminary comparisons. Cell lysates were treated with benzonase nuclease to degrade residual nucleic acids, and purification was performed under native conditions using Ni–NTA affinity chromatography with an optimized imidazole gradient10,12. The purified His-tagged VP1 proteins exhibited high purity and expected molecular weight (~ 42 kDa) as confirmed by SDS-PAGE and Western blot analyses. Consistent with expression results, baculovirus-derived VP1 proteins showed superior yield and quality and were used in all subsequent ELISA and competition assays. Protein concentrations were determined by the Bradford assay (Bio‑Rad), using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the calibration standard. Standard curves (0–1.0 mg/mL BSA) were fit in the linear range (R² > 0.99), and sample absorbance was read at 595 nm in 96‑well microplates; concentrations were calculated from the standard curve and reported as the mean of technical duplicates (CV < 10%).

Western blot analysis

Purified VP1 proteins were resolved on 12% SDS‑PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Molecular weight markers were Novex Sharp Protein Standard Ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Membranes were blocked with 5% non‑fat dry milk in PBST (0.05% Tween‑20) and probed with either BKV‑positive human sera (primary; 1:1,000–1:2,000) for detection of BKV VP1 or a mouse monoclonal anti‑JCV VP1 antibody 8E8 (Abcam, ab34756; 1 µg/mL) for detection of JCV VP1. After washing, HRP‑conjugated secondary antibodies appropriate to the primary (anti‑human IgG‑HRP for BKV‑positive sera; anti‑mouse IgG‑HRP for ab34756) were applied. Signals were developed using ECL chemiluminescent substrate and imaged on a digital chemidoc system. Bands corresponding to VP1 monomer (~ 40–50 kDa) and occasional higher‑order species (~ 80 kDa) were observed.

ELISA development

An indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was established employing purified recombinant VP1 proteins expressed in the baculovirus system as the capture antigen. High-binding 96-well microtiter plates (Nunc MaxiSorp™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 44–2404-21) were coated with VP1 protein diluted in various candidate coating buffers, including carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and Tris-buffered saline (TBS, pH 7.4). Coating was performed overnight at 4 °C to ensure optimal antigen adsorption. Buffer optimization was conducted by comparing optical density (OD) readouts obtained from known positive and negative human serum controls, with carbonate-bicarbonate buffer ultimately selected based on superior signal-to-noise ratio.

Post-coating, plates were blocked using either 5% non-fat dry milk or 1–3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 to minimize non-specific protein binding and reduce background noise. After blocking for 1–2 h at room temperature, serum samples were diluted serially (typically 1:100 to 1:400) in blocking buffer and added in duplicate to the antigen-coated wells. Each assay included duplicate wells of known positive controls, negative controls (seronegative sera), and blank wells for background correction.

Following incubation (1 hour at room temperature) and multiple washes with PBS-Tween (0.05%), horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies specific to the species of primary antibodies were applied. These included anti-human IgG-HRP for detection of human serum antibodies and anti-mouse IgG-HRP for monoclonal antibody validation assays. After a 1-hour incubation and thorough washing steps, 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was added and incubated until adequate color development occurred under controlled conditions. The enzymatic reaction was terminated by adding 1 N sulfuric acid, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a calibrated microplate spectrophotometer.

To discriminate positive from negative samples, a cut-off OD value was calculated as the mean OD of negative control samples plus two standard deviations (mean + 2 SD). Presumptive seronegative controls were derived from neonatal specimens (< 1 year), which were used to calculate the cut‑off; potential maternal IgG interference was considered a limitation. As our archived serum panel did not include sufficient samples from children aged 1–3 years—an age group commonly used to establish a true seronegative baseline—we calculated the cut-off using the mean + 2 SD method based on available seronegative control sera. This limitation has now been acknowledged and addressed in the Discussion section. Samples yielding OD values above this threshold were classified as seropositive, while those below were considered seronegative. Samples with OD values near the cut-off (equivocal range) were subjected to repeat testing to confirm serostatus.

Seroprevalence analysis

A total of 67 human serum samples were used. The cohort encompassed a broad age range, from neonates younger than one day to adults aged 62 years, with a median age of 31 years and a mean age of 30 years. Samples were stratified into predefined age groups to enable age-specific seroepidemiological assessment of polyomavirus antibody prevalence.

Each serum specimen was assayed in duplicate using the indirect ELISA developed in this study to detect VP1‑specific IgG antibodies against BKV and JCV. Seropositivity was determined based on established cut-off values derived from control sera. The prevalence of virus-specific antibodies was calculated for each age group as the proportion of positive samples relative to the total number tested within that stratum.

Statistical analyses were performed to evaluate the significance of differences in seropositivity across age groups and overall population prevalence. Ninety‑5% confidence intervals (95% CI) were computed for seroprevalence estimates. We set α = 0.05 for hypothesis testing (exact p‑values reported); where multiple comparisons were performed in seroepidemiological analyses, appropriate adjustments and stricter thresholds were applied.

Cross-reactivity study

To evaluate the specificity of polyomavirus VP1-specific antibody responses and to investigate potential cross-reactivity between BKV and JCV VP1 antigens, a competitive binding ELISA was performed.

In this assay, serum samples previously confirmed as seropositive for either BKV or JCV VP1-specific IgG antibodies were subjected to a pre-adsorption step with purified, heterologous VP1 proteins. Specifically, sera positive for BKV antibodies were pre-incubated with purified JCV VP1 protein to competitively block any cross-reactive antibodies, and vice versa. In addition to BKV-positive sera, a reciprocal competition assay was performed using JCV-positive sera pre-adsorbed with homologous (JCV VP1) or heterologous (BKV VP1) antigen to evaluate bidirectional specificity. A serial titration of purified VP1 protein (ranging from low to high concentrations) was conducted to establish the minimum effective concentration necessary to achieve maximal inhibition of antibody binding. An optimal concentration of 3 µg/mL purified VP1 was identified, effectively saturating cross-reactive antibodies without causing non-specific interference.

Following pre-adsorption (incubation at room temperature for 1 h), sera were transferred onto microtiter plates coated with either homologous (matching virus) or heterologous (non-matching virus) VP1 antigens and incubated under standard ELISA conditions. After washing, bound antibodies were detected using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies specific for human IgG, followed by substrate addition and absorbance measurement at 450 nm.

Competitive inhibition was quantified by comparing OD values of pre-adsorbed sera against non-pre-adsorbed controls. A significant reduction in OD upon pre-adsorption with heterologous VP1 indicated the presence of cross-reactive antibodies recognizing shared epitopes between BKV and JCV VP1 proteins. Conversely, lack of inhibition suggested antibody specificity for unique epitopes.

Each virus-specific positive serum sample thus underwent reciprocal pre-adsorption and ELISA testing, enabling quantitative assessment of cross-reactivity. This approach provided insights into the antigenic overlap between polyomavirus VP1 proteins and the potential impact on serological assay specificity.

Results

VP1 expression and purification

Recombinant VP1 proteins of BKV and JCV were successfully expressed in Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) cells using insect cell–based expression systems. Among the two approaches evaluated—transient plasmid transfection (InsectDirect™) and baculovirus-mediated expression (BacMagic™)—the baculovirus system produced markedly higher levels of soluble VP1 protein. Indirect immunofluorescence assays confirmed robust VP1 expression with intense fluorescence signals in baculovirus-infected cells, increasing up to 72 h post-infection (h.p.i.).

SDS-PAGE and Western blot analyses of baculovirus-expressed VP1 demonstrated strong bands corresponding to the monomeric (~ 42 kDa) and dimeric (~ 80 kDa) forms of the recombinant protein, with minor degradation products. Extraction using CytoBuster® reagent yielded the highest recovery and purity of VP1 under native purification conditions (Ni–NTA affinity chromatography).

Figure 1 shows SDS-PAGE results illustrating higher purity of JCV VP1 purified with CytoBuster®, while Fig. 2 demonstrates the overall superior expression and purification profiles obtained using the BacMagic™ baculovirus system. Western blot assays (Fig. 3A,B) confirmed specific immunoreactivity of VP1 proteins, indicating preserved antigenicity. Quantitative yield assessment showed that BacMagic™ produced 1.29–1.55 mg/ml of VP1 per 10 ml culture, approximately threefold higher than the plasmid-based system (Table 1).

Quantitative assessment revealed that the baculovirus-mediated expression system (BacMagic) produced significantly higher yields of VP1 protein compared to the plasmid-based insect cell platform (InsectDirect). The average protein yields for InsectDirect ranged from 405 to 460 µg/ml, while BacMagic produced between 1.29 and 1.55 mg/ml per 10 ml culture volume. These quantitative results align with qualitative analyses from gel electrophoresis and western blot assays (Table 1).

ELISA performance and seroprevalence

The optimized VP1-based indirect ELISAs were applied to a panel of 67 human serum samples to determine seroprevalence of polyomavirus-specific IgG antibodies. The ELISA assays demonstrated strong specificity and sensitivity for both BKV and JCV antibodies.

Figure 5 A,B present the distribution and cross‑platform comparison of ELISA OD values for BKV and JCV using baculovirus‑ and plasmid‑derived VP1 antigens. Correlation was assessed using Pearson’s coefficient: BKV r = 0.84, two‑tailed p = 0.001; JCV r = 0.79, two‑tailed p = 0.002. The straight line in 5A is a least‑squares fit and the displayed R^2 refers to this visualization only.

Overall seroprevalence analysis revealed that 77.6% (52/67) of individuals were seropositive for BKV VP1 antibodies, whereas JCV VP1 seropositivity was observed in 34.3% (23/67) of samples. Seroprevalence demonstrated clear age-dependent trends (Table 2): the youngest cohort (< 1 year) exhibited minimal seropositivity, whereas older age groups (> 25 years) displayed consistently high BKV seropositivity rates (80–91%). JCV seroprevalence showed a progressive increase with age, peaking at 50% in individuals aged 55–64 years.

Cross-reactivity assessment

A competitive inhibition ELISA was performed to investigate the antigenic specificity and potential cross-reactivity between BKV and JCV VP1-specific antibodies. Positive serum samples were incubated with increasing concentrations of either homologous (BKV VP1) or heterologous (JCV VP1) purified VP1 proteins prior to ELISA testing. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, pre-incubation with homologous BKV VP1 antigen resulted in a dose-dependent reduction of BKV-specific antibody binding, reflected by a marked decrease in OD values. In contrast, addition of JCV VP1 antigen at similar concentrations had little to no effect on BKV seroreactivity. These data indicate that the developed ELISA exhibits high specificity for BKV VP1 antibodies and demonstrates minimal cross-reactivity with JCV VP1, confirming the ability of the assay to reliably differentiate between antibody responses induced by these two closely related polyomaviruses.

To further substantiate assay specificity, we performed the reciprocal competition experiment in which JCV-positive human sera were pre-adsorbed with increasing concentrations of either homologous JCV VP1 or heterologous BKV VP1 antigen. As shown in Fig. 6B, pre-adsorption with homologous JCV VP1 led to a clear dose-dependent reduction in ELISA reactivity, whereas addition of BKV VP1 had minimal impact on JCV-specific antibody binding. These results confirm the reciprocal nature of antigenic specificity and demonstrate minimal cross-reactivity between BKV and JCV VP1 proteins.

Comparative analysis of ELISA performance using VP1 antigens expressed via baculovirus and plasmid systems indicated a strong correlation between the two approaches (BKV: r = 0.84, two‑tailed p = 0.001; JCV: r = 0.79, two‑tailed p = 0.002). However, baculovirus-derived antigens consistently produced higher protein yields and slightly stronger ELISA signals (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we successfully expressed and purified the VP1 capsid proteins of BKV, and JCV utilizing two insect-cell-based expression systems: the baculovirus-mediated BacMagic system and the plasmid-based InsectDirect system. Our findings reveal two primary observations: (i) the baculovirus-mediated BacMagic system produced higher amounts of soluble and immunoreactive VP1 proteins under our experimental conditions, while both insect-based systems successfully supported VP1 expression, and (ii) no measurable cross-reactivity was detected between BKV and JCV VP1 antigens using the developed ELISA assays. These results align with and extend prior studies on polyomavirus VP1 expression and serological analyses11,13,14. While both bacterial and insect expression systems are widely used for recombinant VP1 production, our data indicate that, in this study’s conditions, BEVS provided practical advantages—namely higher soluble yield and preserved conformational reactivity—rather than universal superiority. Such advantages likely stem from the capacity of insect cells to support eukaryotic folding and assembly11,15–17; however, the optimal platform remains application‑specific and depends on target protein properties and assay requirements. Overall, these observations are consistent with context‑dependent strengths of BEVS for generating conformationally intact eukaryotic antigens suitable for serological assay development.

Importantly, the increased expression efficiency directly translated into stronger ELISA signals and higher antigen purity, beneficial for scaling diagnostic assay development. Competitive ELISA inhibition assays validated the high specificity of the assay, demonstrating no detectable cross-reactivity between BKV and JCV VP1 antigens. This resonates with serological mapping studies indicating that, despite approximately 75% amino acid sequence identity in VP1 proteins, conformational epitopes that govern antibody specificity are largely distinct between these two viruses18,19. The reciprocal competition assay (Fig. 6B) further confirmed that JCV-specific antibody responses were not inhibited by BKV VP1, supporting the bidirectional specificity of the developed ELISAs. Such antigenic specificity is critical for the accuracy of epidemiological studies and for preventing misclassification in clinical screening.

The enhanced sensitivity of the BacMagic-derived VP1 ELISA likely results from the BEVS platform’s ability to preserve native conformational epitopes important for antibody recognition, a feature that can be more limited in other expression systems15,20. Our findings indicate that the BacMagic system yields higher quantities of stable VP1 proteins, in agreement with studies supporting the use of baculovirus systems for efficient and scalable antigen production21.

The VP1 proteins expressed via baculovirus exhibited superior immunoreactivity and purity, as evidenced by stronger Western blot bands and more intense immunofluorescence signals compared to those derived from plasmid-based expression. Notably, maximum VP1 concentrations achieved were approximately 1.55 mg/ml for BacMagic and 460 µg/ml for InsectDirect. These qualitative and quantitative differences emphasize the advantages of baculovirus-mediated expression for generating high-quality antigens.

Serological evaluation of 67 human serum samples confirmed overall IgG seroprevalence rates of 77.6% for BKV and 34.3% for JCV, consistent with epidemiological data showing earlier and more frequent BKV infection during life compared to JCV. Our seroprevalence estimates align broadly with large-scale studies conducted in Europe and North America, which report adult BKV seroprevalence typically ranging from 70 to 90% and JCV between 35 and 65%3,13. Regional and temporal variances have been documented, as exemplified by a Finnish cohort exhibiting BK seropositivity up to 99% and comparatively lower JCV seroprevalence (~ 59–72%)22.

Several factors may account for inter-study and geographical differences in seroprevalence, including viral genetic diversity and the circulation of distinct VP1 genotypes/subtypes influencing local viral exposure and serological responses23,24. Differences in transmission ecology, age of primary infection, hygiene, and socio-behavioral practices also shape seroepidemiological profiles. Moreover, methodological variations—such as antigen type employed (VLPs versus monomeric VP1), assay cut-off determination, and population sampling frameworks—and host immune factors further contribute to observed heterogeneity24,25.

Our observation of higher BKV IgG prevalence compared to JCV mirrors multiple seroepidemiological investigations, reflecting BKV’s earlier acquisition during infancy or childhood and its longer co-evolutionary history with humans4,22,26. Proposed transmission routes for JCV, including fecal-oral pathways, may influence its delayed acquisition and lower overall prevalence compared to BKV27,28. Age-stratified data also revealed relatively stable high BKV seropositivity across adult age groups, while JCV seropositivity increased gradually with age, suggesting differences in transmission, latency, or immune responses between these viruses.

Assessing ELISA specificity, our competitive inhibition assays demonstrated no meaningful cross-reactivity between BKV and JCV VP1 proteins, underscoring the assay’s diagnostic precision. However, it should be noted that utilization of VP1 monomers or pentamers instead of native virus-like particles (VLPs) may limit ultimate assay specificity, given that VLPs better preserve conformational epitopes responsible for species-specific antibody recognition29.

While ELISAs based on both baculovirus- and plasmid-derived VP1 proteins correlated strongly (r = 0.84 and r = 0.79 for BKV and JCV, respectively), baculovirus-derived antigens showed somewhat higher signal intensity and purity. These results suggest that both systems are suitable for serological studies, although the baculovirus platform offers practical advantages for large-scale antigen production11,17.

Clinically, the high sensitivity and specificity of our VP1-based ELISA have important implications, particularly in kidney transplant recipients—a population vulnerable to BKV reactivation and polyomavirus-associated nephropathy (BKVAN), which affects up to 10% of transplant patients and is a major cause of allograft loss19,30. Early detection of BK-specific antibodies in donors and assessment of cellular immunity in recipients using such assays can guide risk stratification and prompt immunosuppressive therapy adjustments to prevent BKVAN progression31.

Additionally, the assay’s scalability and specificity render it valuable for surveillance of other high-risk groups, including immunosuppressed individuals such as HIV-positive patients and organ transplant recipients. Integration of VP1-based serological monitoring into routine clinical workflows could offer dynamic tracking of humoral immune responses following treatment or vaccination, thereby optimizing patient management and personalized care32.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the use of recombinant VP1 monomers or capsomers as ELISA antigens may not fully capture the conformational complexity of native VP1 or VLP structures, which could influence assay sensitivity and specificity. Second, our serum panel did not include a sufficient number of samples from children aged 1–3 years—an age group typically used to establish a rigorous seronegative baseline. Consequently, the cut-off value in this study was determined using the standard mean + 2 SD method based on the available negative sera. A limitation is the use of neonatal sera as presumptive seronegatives because maternally derived IgG may persist in early infancy and elevate OD values in some cases. Incorporating early-childhood samples in future work will allow a more refined calibration of seronegativity thresholds. Finally, the serum samples analyzed were used specimens, which may introduce selection or spectrum bias and limit the generalizability of the results to the broader population.

Future studies should consider employing VLP-based ELISAs or multiplex immunoassays such as bead-based Luminex platforms that preserve native conformational epitopes and enable simultaneous quantification of antibodies against multiple polyomaviruses with enhanced specificity and throughput33. Expanding sample acquisition via population-based, randomized recruitment would also improve the representativeness of seroprevalence estimates and reduce selection bias.

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrated the expression, purification, and serological application of recombinant VP1 capsid proteins of BK virus (BKV) and JC virus (JCV) using insect cell-based expression systems. The baculovirus-mediated BacMagic platform yielded higher quantities of correctly folded and immunoreactive VP1 proteins under our experimental conditions, supporting its utility for scalable and reproducible antigen preparation.

The developed indirect ELISAs employing baculovirus-derived VP1 antigens exhibited high sensitivity and specificity for detecting polyomavirus-specific IgG antibodies in human sera. Seroprevalence data confirmed widespread exposure to BKV across all age groups, whereas JCV seropositivity increased progressively with age, aligning with known epidemiological patterns. Critically, competitive inhibition ELISA assays established the absence of cross-reactivity between BKV and JCV VP1 antibodies, validating the diagnostic specificity of the developed methods.

Collectively, these findings highlight the advantages of baculovirus-expressed VP1 proteins as reliable antigen sources for serological diagnostics and epidemiological studies. Furthermore, the purified VP1 antigens may serve as promising candidates for future vaccine research and monoclonal antibody development targeting human polyomaviruses. The standardized and sensitive ELISA format described here provides a robust platform that can be adapted for large-scale serological and clinical investigations.
