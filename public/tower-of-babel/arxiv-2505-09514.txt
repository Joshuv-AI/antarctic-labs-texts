Abstract
Cumulative prospect theory (CPT) is the first theory for decision-making under uncertainty that combines full theoretical soundness and empirically realistic features [ 1 , Page 2] . While CPT was originally considered in one-shot settings for risk-aware decision-making, we consider CPT in sequential decision-making. The most fundamental and well-studied models for sequential decision-making are Markov chains (MCs), and their generalization Markov decision processes (MDPs). The complexity theoretic study of MCs and MDPs with CPT is a fundamental problem that has not been addressed in the literature. Our contributions are as follows: First, we present an alternative viewpoint for the CPT-value of MCs and MDPs. This allows us to establish a connection with multi-objective reachability analysis and conclude the strategy complexity result that memoryless randomized strategies are necessary and sufficient for optimality. Second, based on this connection, we provide an algorithm for computing the CPT-value in MDPs with infinite-horizon objectives. We show that the problem is in EXPTIME and fixed-parameter tractable. Moreover, we provide a polynomial-time algorithm for the special case of MCs.



I
Introduction

Risk-aware Decision Making.

A decision-maker wants to find the optimal choice in a situation with multiple possible decisions, but the outcomes of these decisions are affected by uncertainty.
Such situations arise in countless applications, from strategic business decisions over financial investment to medical treatments, see e.g.
[
1
, Chap. 1.1]
.

Example I.1

(Motivating example)
.

Imagine you find a coupon for a bet.
There are two bets available:
You can use your coupon and choose to play a
safe bet
with a 95% chance of winning 20€.
Alternatively, you can pay an additional 5€ and play a
risky bet
where you can win 55€ with 51%, 5€ with a 5% chance, but you can also lose the bet and get nothing with a 44% chance.
Thus, taking into account the additional 5€ paid, the second bet can result in a loss of 5€, no change, or winning 50€.
Most people tend to choose the safe bet.
This has been claimed to be
“irrational”
because the expectation of the safe bet is only 19€, while the expectation of the risky bet is higher at 23.3€.
We discuss a sequential version of this example (finding two coupons) in
Ex.

II.2
.

\triangle

Cumulative Prospect Theory over Expected Utility.

Expected utility theory cannot explain human behaviour in examples like this.
Thus, there has been an effort to replace it with another decision theory that faithfully represents human decision-making in order to predict or prescribe decisions that align with human preferences.
We refer to
[
2
]
for a history of expected utility theory and the criticism it has received and to
[
1
, P. 2]
for an overview of the developments that led to the inception of
cumulative prospect theory
(CPT), introduced in
[
3
]
.
Unlike expected utility theory, CPT correctly models the decision of preferring the safe bet in the motivating example, as it accounts for factors in human decision-making like loss-aversion (losing money is more hurtful than gaining an equal amount) and re-weighting of probabilities (the small 5% chance for getting nothing is increased in human perception, while the larger 95% chance is decreased).

Desired Properties of CPT.

CPT combines theoretical soundness with empirical realism:
It is sound insofar as it satisfies two important axioms, called gain-loss consistency and sign-comonotonic tradeoff consistency, whereas other theories of decision under risk do not
[
4
]
.
It is empirically realistic, as it has been confirmed by several studies, see
[
1
, Chap. 9.5]
for an overview.
In 2002, Daniel Kahneman received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for his work on prospect theory.
In 2010, CPT was
“still the only theory that can deliver the full spectrum of what is required for decision under uncertainty”

[
1
, P. 2]
.

CPT in Sequential Decision Making.

CPT was originally considered in one-shot settings, but is also relevant in the context of sequential decision-making.
For this, the most fundamental and well-studied models are Markov chains (MCs, e.g.
[
5
]
), and their generalization Markov decision processes (MDPs, e.g.
[
6
]
).
Adapting the motivating example, MDPs can model not only a single betting decision but when we find two coupons for bets and we can bet twice consecutively.
An optimal strategy is a risk-aware plan for both bets.
Usually, the value of an MDP is calculated as the expectation over the rewards of all paths.
Replacing this expectation with the CPT-function allows risk-aware sequential decision-making in problems too large for humans to comprehend.
The computational study of MDPs with CPT is a fundamental algorithmic problem in risk-aware sequential decision making which has not been addressed in the literature.

Our contribution.

We focus on the algorithmic analysis of CPT in MCs and MDPs with infinite-horizon objectives, namely the basic and well-studied weighted reachability and long-run average reward.
We provide the first deterministic algorithms and the first computational and strategy complexity results.

•

First, we present a new, more intuitive definition of the CPT-value in sequential decision-making, which allows us to establish a connection to multi-objective reachability queries.
We utilize this connection to apply results from multi-objective model checking.
This way, we directly obtain the strategy complexity result that memoryless randomized strategies are necessary and sufficient for achieving the optimal CPT-value.

•

Second, we provide algorithms for approximating the CPT-value of an MC or MDP to arbitrary precision
\varepsilon>0
.
(We explain in
Rem.

1
why computing the exact CPT-value is unreasonable and infeasible.)
Based on this, we also establish upper bounds on the computational complexity of the CPT-value approximation problem:
Given an MDP, weighted reachability objective, precision
\varepsilon
, and threshold
v
, decide whether the CPT-value of the MDP is greater than
v+\varepsilon
or smaller than
v-\varepsilon
.
This CPT-value approximation is fixed-parameter tractable.

Technical contributions.

One of our key contributions is a new and intuitive definition of the CPT-value in the sequential setting (
Sec.

III-A
).
The new definition immediately leads to our polynomial-time algorithm for Markov
chains (
Sec.

IV-A
).
For MDPs, the new definition allows us to establish a connection to multi-objective reachability (
Sec.

III-C
).
For the algorithmic results, we exploit this connection in order to reduce the problem of computing the CPT-value to a non-convex optimization problem (
Sec.

IV-B
).
The complexity of non-convex optimization is usually not discussed and only described as exponential in the number of variables, see e.g.
[
7
, Chap. 3]
.
We provide an algorithm with explicit runtime analysis.
Finally, we highlight that our theory is robust in the sense that we can generalize it to variants of CPT or to other objectives like mean payoff (
Sec.

V
).

Related Work.

Risk-aware MDPs.

Recently, there has been a lot of work on risk-aware MDPs.
Often, expectation is not replaced but rather complemented with something by considering a conjunction of objectives. This includes combining expectation with conditional value-at-risk
[
8
,
9
]
, percentiles
[
10
]
, quantiles
[

,

, or variance
[
13
,
14
]
.

In contrast, CPT is a
“monolithic”
theory of decision, i.e. it computes a single value for every choice, such that the largest value is the one that a human would prefer.
A similar approach is used in
[
15
,
16
]
, replacing expectation with entropic risk, or
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
, using variance-penalized expected payoff.

In
[
20
,
21
]
, the CPT-value in MDPs is approximated using model-free reinforcement learning algorithms.
These randomized algorithms only converge in the limit and have no stopping criterion indicating how close the current approximation is to the result; in contrast, our solution is deterministic and terminates with a required precision after finitely many steps.
In
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
, the authors consider a
“nested”
formulation of the CPT-value, i.e. the CPT-function is applied for every transition that a path in the MDP takes.
The convergence of their algorithms is proven only under additional restrictive assumptions, namely that the reward measure is b-bounded
[
22
, Theorem 3.1]
(also used in
[
24
]
) or that the MDP is uniformly transient

Additionally, they differ from our work in that the considered objectives are finite-horizon
[
22
,
24

or discounted and transient
[
23

Comparison of Theories of Risk.

CPT is the dominant theory of risk in psychology, and it generalizes classical expected utility, as well as the value-at-risk
[
1
, Exerc. 6.4.4]
in the sense that for a certain choice of internal parameters, CPT degenerates to these functions.
It is based on empirical studies
[
3
]
and has been mathematically axiomatized, in particular satisfying the properties of gain-loss consistency and sign-comonotonic tradeoff consistency
[
4
]
.
The conditional value-at-risk and entropic risk have also been mathematically axiomatized in
[
25
]
and
[
26
]
, respectively, and are often employed in finance. Entropic risk has been shown to be consistent with human choice
[
27
]
in portfolio selection; however, as it developed from an axiomatization (in contrast to CPT, where the axiomatization was derived from the empirical studies), there are fewer empirical studies and an ongoing debate about which variant of entropic risk is preferable
[
28
,
27
,
29
]
.
The variance-penalized expected payoff is also a very common approach, and Harry Markowitz received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for it
[
30
]
; however, the theoretical foundations have been subject to a lot of discussion, see the summary in
[
31
]
, and we are not aware of an axiomatic characterization.
Overall, there are arguments for investigating MDPs under these theories of risk, and we complement the state-of-the-art by providing results for CPT, for which so far there is no principled analysis of algorithms and complexity.

Algorithms for CPT in other settings.

There exist algorithms converging in the limit to the CPT-value of a discounted deterministic two-player Markov game
[
32
]
, or to approximate Nash-equilibria in multi-player stochastic games with incomplete information
[
33
,
34
]
.
None of these papers provide results on algorithmic or strategy complexity.

II-A

Evaluating Prospects Using Expected Utility Theory

When deciding which prospect to prefer, humans evaluate them intuitively; CPT is one model for this evaluation.
For better accessibility of the CPT-function, we first recall the well-known and simple definition of expected utility.
It is a function
\mathsf{eu}:\mathbb{O}^{k}\times[0,1]^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
that takes a prospect and computes its expected value while also re-weighting every outcome with a utility function
u:\mathbb{O}\to\mathbb{R}
.
This utility function already allows capturing some parts of human behaviour, for example, the fact that the perceived gain between no money and one million Euros is larger than between the first and second million.
Commonly, utility functions have an
“S-shape”
, as we exemplify in
Fig.

1
.
Formally,
\mathsf{eu}
is defined on a prospect
\vec{x}=(\vec{o},\vec{p})
as follows:

\mathsf{eu}(\vec{x})=\mathsf{eu}((\vec{o},\vec{p}))=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{u}(o_{i})\cdot p_{i}

II-B

Semantics: Paths and Strategies

The
semantics
of MDPs are defined as usual: The non-deterministic choices (which actions to choose) are resolved by means of a strategy (also called policy or scheduler).
This induces an MC with the respective probability space over infinite paths; we briefly recall this approach and refer to
[
36
, Chap. 10]
for a more extensive description.
We use the following standard notation: For a set
S
, we denote by
S^{*}
and
S^{\omega}
the set of finite and infinite sequences comprised of elements of
S
, respectively.

In every state
s
, we choose an available action
a\in\mathsf{A}(s)
and advance to a successor state
s^{\prime}
according to the probability distribution given by
\delta(s,a)
.
Starting in a state
s_{0}
and repeating this process indefinitely yields an infinite path
\rho=s_{0}a_{0}s_{1}a_{1}\dots\in(\mathsf{S}\times\mathsf{A})^{\omega}
such that (i) for every
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have
a_{i}\in\mathsf{A}(s_{i})
and (ii)
s_{i+1}\in\{s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{S}\mid\delta(s_{i},a_{i})(s^{\prime})>0\}
.
A finite path is a prefix of an infinite path ending in a state, i.e. an element of
(\mathsf{S}\times\mathsf{A})^{\ast}\times\mathsf{S}
satisfying the conditions (i) and (ii).

\mathsf{FPaths}_{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathsf{IPaths}_{\mathcal{M}}
denote the set of all such finite and infinite paths in a given MDP
\mathcal{M}
.
Further, we write
\rho_{i}
to denote the
i
-th state
s_{i}
in a path.

A
strategy
(also policy or scheduler) describes a way to choose actions.
In full generality, a strategy is a function
\sigma\colon\mathsf{FPaths}_{\mathcal{M}}\to\mathcal{D}(\mathsf{A})
.
It samples an action from a distribution that is chosen depending on the history (the finite path observed so far).
We differentiate
history-dependent
strategies from
memoryless
strategies, whose choice depends only on the last state of the finite path.
We differentiate strategies that are
randomized
from those where the distribution over actions is Dirac; these choose a single action surely and are called
deterministic
.
We denote the set of all strategies by
\Sigma
.

Resolving all nondeterministic choices by fixing a strategy
\sigma
yields a (countably infinite) Markov chain
[
36
, Def. 10.92]
, denoted by
\mathcal{M}^{\sigma}
.
This MC induces a unique probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}
over the set of all infinite paths
\mathsf{IPaths}_{\mathcal{M}}

[
36
, Chap. 10.1]
(where paths starting in
s_{0}
have measure 1).
Referring to the probability measure of an MC
\mathsf{M}
, we omit the superscript and write
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{M}}
.

II-C

Expected Value and CPT-value

The definition of the value of an MDP under CPT is quite complicated.

Remark 1

(Exact computation)

There are two reasons why we are only interested in approximating the CPT-value, namely that an exact computation is unreasonable and infeasible.
It is unreasonable because the parameters of the CPT-function, e.g. the utility function

Technical Notions Required for the Solution

For the analysis of MDPs, the following two graph theoretical concepts are of great importance:
strongly connected components
and

II-D
1
Strongly Connected Components

A non-empty set of states
C\subseteq\mathsf{S}
in an MDP is
strongly connected
if for every pair
s,s^{\prime}\in C
there is a non-empty finite path from
s
to
s^{\prime}
.
Such a set
C
is a
strongly connected component
(SCC) if it is inclusion maximal, i.e. there exists no strongly connected
C^{\prime}
with
C\subsetneq C^{\prime}
.
SCCs are disjoint, so each state belongs to at most one SCC.
An SCC is
bottom
(BSCC) if additionally no path leads out of it, i.e. for all
s\in C
,
a\in\mathsf{A}(s)
,
s^{\prime}\in S\setminus C
we have
\delta(s,a,s^{\prime})=0
.
The set of BSCCs of an MDP
\mathcal{M}
, denoted
\mathsf{BSCC}(\mathcal{M})
, can be determined in linear time
[
40
]
.

A state might not be in a BSCC in an MDP, but in a BSCC in the induced MC under some strategy, see
Ex.

III.3
.

II-D
2
Maximal End Components

A pair
(T,B)
, where
\emptyset\neq T\subseteq\mathsf{S}
and
\emptyset\neq B\subseteq\bigcup_{s\in T}\mathsf{A}(s)
, is an
end component (EC)
of an MDP
\mathcal{M}
if (i) for all
s\in T,a\in B\mathbin{\cap}\mathsf{A}(s)
we have
\{s^{\prime}\mid\delta(s,a,s^{\prime})>0\}\subseteq T
, and (ii) for all
s,s^{\prime}\in T
there is a finite path
\rho=sa_{0}\dots a_{n}s^{\prime}\in(T\times B)^{*}\times T
, i.e. the path stays inside
T
and only uses actions in
B
.
Intuitively, an EC describes a set of states where for some particular strategy all possible paths remain inside these states (i.e. under this strategy, the EC is a BSCC in the induced MC).
An EC
(T,B)
is a
maximal end component (MEC)
if there is no other EC
(T^{\prime},B^{\prime})
such that
T\subseteq T^{\prime}
and
B\subseteq B^{\prime}
.
The set of MECs in a subset
\mathsf{S}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathsf{S}
, denoted by
\mathsf{MEC}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathsf{S}^{\prime})
, can be computed in polynomial time
[
41
]
.

III
Mathematical characterization

Throughout the paper, we often fix a weighted reachability (WR) objective
\Phi
with the target set
\mathsf{F}
and reward function
\mathsf{r}
,
and then talk about the associated vector
k
-vector of outcomes
\vec{o}=(o_{1},\ldots,o_{k})
.
We formalize this intuitive notion:

Firstly, this uses the fact that we overload
\Phi
to denote both the tuple
(\mathsf{F},\mathsf{r})
describing the weighted reachability objective as well as the induced function that maps infinite paths to reals.
Secondly, the set of associated outcomes is formally defined as
\mathsf{outc}(\Phi)\vcentcolon=\{o\mid\exists s\in\mathsf{F}.\mathsf{r}(s)=o\}\cup\{0\}
.
Intuitively, the set of outcomes is the image set of the reward function
\mathsf{r}
, i.e. all numbers that some state maps to.
Finally, we comment on a technicality: When applying a history-dependent strategy on an MDP, the set of states in the induced MC are all paths in the MDP
[
36
, Def. 10.92]
. Thus, the set of target states becomes the set of all paths that contain a target state.
We do not make this transformation explicit in the paper, but overload
\mathsf{F}
to always refer to the correct set of target states. Note that the set of outcomes is unaffected by this transformation.

III-A

Alternative Definition of CPT-value

The hitherto existing definition for CPT-value of an MDP (
Eq.

4
) is quite complicated.
We propose a new, equivalent definition that is more intuitive.
Further, the new definition immediately leads to an algorithm for MCs (
Sec.

IV-A
).
Additionally, it allows us to establish a connection between the CPT-value in MDPs and multi-objective reachability queries (
Sec.

III-C
).
We exploit this connection for the strategy complexity result and the algorithm for MDPs (
Sec.

IV-B
).

The Prospect Induced by a Markov Chain.

The functions for computing both expected utility and CPT are defined on prospects, see
Sec.

II-A
.
Our goal is to interpret an MC (the result of fixing a strategy) as a prospect.
Then, we can evaluate strategies by applying the function given in
Eq.

2
, instead of mixing its internals into the value definition (as in
Eq.

4
).

So what is the prospect induced by a Markov chain
\mathsf{M}
?
Intuitively, we want to compute the probability of reaching each of the absorbing target states; this yields a prospect because it gives us the probability for every outcome.
We need to consider two technicalities:
Firstly, there can be multiple target states with the same reward, so their probabilities need to be summed up.
Secondly, a path in the MC can reach a state from which no target state is reachable anymore. Such a path eventually reaches a non-target
bottom strongly connected component
(BSCC, see
Sec.

II-D1
).
The probability of these kinds of paths has to be summed up for the ourcome 0.
To formalize this, we define the set of states that obtain a certain outcome
o_{i}
, using a case distinction to take care of outcome 0.

\mathsf{obtainset}(\mathsf{M},\Phi,o_{i})\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}\{s\in\mathsf{F}\mid\mathsf{r}(s)=o_{i}\}&\mbox{ if }o_{i}\neq 0\\
\bigcup_{C\in\mathsf{BSCC}(\mathsf{M}).C\cap\mathsf{F}=\emptyset}C&\mbox{ if }o_{i}=0\end{cases}

(5)

Using this and denoting the associated outcome vector of
\Phi

\vec{o}
, we define the induced prospect where the probability of each outcome is the probability to reach its
\mathsf{obtainset}

\displaystyle\mathsf{prospect}(\mathsf{M},\Phi)\vcentcolon=(\vec{o},\vec{p}),

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\text{where for }1\leq i\leq k:p_{i}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{M}}[\lozenge\mathsf{obtainset}(\mathsf{M},\Phi,o_{i})]

(6)

Lemma III.1

(Correctness of Induced Prospect)
.

For every MC
\mathsf{M}
and WR-objective
\Phi

\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{M}}[\Phi(\rho)=o_{i}]=p_{i}

Proof.

This proof works by straightforward application of the definition of

and induced prospect.
By

p_{i}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{M}}[\lozenge\mathsf{obtainset}(\mathsf{M},\Phi,o_{i})]

We now distinguish the cases whether the outcome

Case 1:
For

\mathsf{obtainset}(\mathsf{M},\Phi,o_{i})=\{s\in\mathsf{F}\mid\mathsf{r}(s)=o_{i}\}

Since all non-target states have a reward of 0 and all target states are absorbing,
we can only obtain reward

Thus, we conclude by stating:

\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{M}}[\Phi(\rho)=o_{i}]=\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{M}}[\lozenge\mathsf{obtainset}(\mathsf{M},\Phi,o_{i})].

Case 2:

(7)

Now we conclude as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{M}}[\Phi(\rho)=0]=1-\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{M}}[\lozenge\mathsf{F}]

(By definition of
\Phi

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{M}}[\lozenge\{C\in\mathsf{BSCC}(\mathcal{M}^{\sigma})\mid C\cap T=\emptyset\}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{M}}[\lozenge\mathsf{obtainset}(\mathsf{M},\Phi,0)].

(By

III-C

Connection between CPT-value and Multi-objective Reachability Queries

Intuitively, the new definition of CPT-value in
Eq.

8
leads to the following algorithm:
For every strategy, evaluate the prospect of its induced MC, and take the supremum of these values.
As the set of all possible prospects is infinite, we now describe a way to finitely represent it.
Essentially, we construct a multi-objective reachability query from the given WR-objective
\Phi
.
Consequently, the Pareto frontier for this query contains all possible probability distributions over target states and thus represents all prospects.

To transform the WR-objective to a multi-objective reachability query, we define the set of states that obtain a certain outcome.
We need to lift the definition of
\mathsf{obtainset}
from MCs to MDPs.
All non-zero outcomes
o
are obtained in target states that w.l.o.g. are absorbing (see
Sec.

II-B3
).
For all strategies
\sigma
, we have
\mathsf{obtainset}(\mathcal{M},\Phi,o)=\mathsf{obtainset}(\mathcal{M}^{\sigma},\Phi,o)
.
However, the
\mathsf{obtainset}
of 0 can vary depending on the strategy (see
Ex.

III.3
).
This is why in the previous
Sec.

III-B
, we proved that w.l.o.g. we can restrict to stopping MDPs, where (i) states obtaining the reward 0 have been merged into a single sink state
\mathsf{z}
; and (ii)
\mathsf{F}\cup\{\mathsf{z}\}
is reached almost surely under all strategies.
We have
\mathsf{obtainset}(\mathcal{M},\Phi,0)=\{\mathsf{z}\}
.
We define the multi-objective reachability query for a stopping MDP
\mathcal{M}
and a WR-objective
\Phi
(with outcomes numbered
o_{1}
to
o_{k}
):

\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{M},\Phi)\vcentcolon=(\mathsf{obtainset}(\mathcal{M},\Phi,o_{1}),\ldots,\mathsf{obtainset}(\mathcal{M},\Phi,o_{k}))

(9)

We highlight that every point on the Pareto frontier of this transformed query
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{M},\Phi)}
is a probability distribution.
This is because
\biguplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{obtainset}(\Phi,o_{k})=\mathsf{F}\cup\{\mathsf{z}\}
and the MDP is stopping.
Thus, every point
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
on the Pareto frontier uniquely identifies a possible prospect.
We formalize this:

Lemma III.5
.

Fix a stopping MDP
\mathcal{M}
and WR-objective
\Phi
, with
\vec{o}
being the vector of outcomes.
The set of all prospects
\mathcal{M}
can induce under some strategy
\sigma

\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{M},\Phi)

Formally:

\Bigl\{\mathsf{prospect}(\mathcal{M}^{\sigma},\Phi)\mid\sigma\in\Sigma\Bigr\}=\Bigl\{(\vec{o},\vec{p})\mid\vec{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{M},\Phi)}\Bigr\}

Proof.

We show equality by proving inclusion in both directions.
First, we fix an arbitrary strategy
\sigma\in\Sigma
and show
\mathsf{prospect}(\mathcal{M}^{\sigma},\Phi)\in\Bigl\{(\vec{o},\vec{p})\mid\vec{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{M},\Phi)}\Bigr\}
.

Let
(\vec{o},\vec{q})=\mathsf{prospect}(\mathcal{M}^{\sigma},\Phi)
be the induced prospect.
By definition of induced prospect, for every
i
with
1\leq i\leq k
, it holds that
q_{i}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}[\lozenge\mathsf{obtainset}(\mathcal{M},\Phi,o_{i})]
.
Thus, by definition of
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{M},\Phi)
and the achievable set, we know that
\vec{q}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{M},\Phi)}
, as the target sets of the multi-objective query correspond exactly to the outcomes.

Every point on the Pareto frontier
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{M},\Phi)}
is a probability distribution.
Hence, there is no point that is strictly better than
\vec{q}
.
Thus,
\vec{q}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{M},\Phi)}
, which proves our goal.

The other direction works analogously:
Let
\vec{p}
be a point on the Pareto frontier.
Hence, a strategy
\sigma
exists that achieves exactly this distribution over the target sets, which correspond to the outcomes.
The prospect corresponding to this point is also in the set of all prospects induced by the MDP, or more formally:
(\vec{o},\vec{p})\in\Bigl\{\mathsf{prospect}(\mathcal{M}^{\sigma},\Phi)\mid\sigma\in\Sigma\Bigr\}
.
∎

We lift this connection between all possible prospects and the Pareto frontier to the CPT-value:

Theorem III.6

(Connection between CPT-value and Multi-objective Reachability)
.

Fix an MDP
\mathcal{M}
and WR-objective
\Phi
with
k
outcomes, and let
\vec{o}
be the vector of these outcomes.
We can construct in polynomial time an MDP
{\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}
and a multi-objective reachability query
\mathcal{Q}({\widehat{\mathcal{M}}},\Phi)
of dimension
k
with absorbing targets, such that the CPT-value of
\mathcal{M}
equals the supremum over all prospects corresponding to points in the Pareto frontier
\mathcal{P}_{{\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}}^{\mathcal{Q}({\widehat{\mathcal{M}}},\Phi)}
.
Formally:

\mathsf{CPT}\text{-}\mathsf{V}(\mathcal{M},\Phi)=\sup_{\vec{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{{\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}}^{\mathcal{Q}({\widehat{\mathcal{M}}},\Phi)}}\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p})).

Proof.

Using
Lem.

III.4
, we construct
{\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}
from
\mathcal{M}
in polynomial time, with
\mathsf{CPT}\text{-}\mathsf{V}(\mathcal{M},\Phi)=\mathsf{CPT}\text{-}\mathsf{V}({\widehat{\mathcal{M}}},\Phi)
.
Then:

\displaystyle\mathsf{CPT}\text{-}\mathsf{V}({\widehat{\mathcal{M}}},\Phi)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\mathsf{cpt}(\mathsf{prospect}({\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}^{\sigma},\Phi))

(By
Eq.

8
)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\vec{x}\in\{\mathsf{prospect}({\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}^{\sigma},\Phi)\mid\sigma\in\Sigma\}}\mathsf{cpt}(\vec{x})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\vec{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{{\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}}^{\mathcal{Q}({\widehat{\mathcal{M}}},\Phi)}}\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p}))

(By
Lem.

III.5
)

∎

Key contribution.
The definition of the multi-objective query in
Eq.

9
, together with the technical stopping assumption from
Sec.

III-B
, allows us to reduce the problem of computing the CPT-value to finding the CPT-optimal point on the Pareto frontier.
Solving this optimization problem is the only remaining step to obtain the algorithm for MDPs in
Sec.

IV-B
.

Moreover, this reduction immediately yields an upper bound on strategy complexity:
The optimal strategy achieves a point on the Pareto frontier
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{M},\Phi)}
by
Thm.

III.6
, and every point on the Pareto frontier can be achieved using a memoryless randomized strategy by
Rem.

2
. Together with an example that randomized strategies can achieve a strictly higher CPT-value than deterministic ones (see
Sec.

B-C
), we obtain the following strategy complexity:

Corollary III.7

(Strategy Complexity)
.

Memoryless randomized strategies are necessary and sufficient to achieve the optimal CPT-value in an MDP
\mathcal{M}
with a WR-objective
\Phi
.

Lemma IV.6

(Lipschitz-continuity of CPT-function)
.

The CPT function
\mathsf{cpt}
is Lipschitz-continuous with respect to the probability vector
\vec{p}
, i.e. for every
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
and vector of outcomes
\vec{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have that if two vectors
\vec{p},\vec{q}\in[0,1]^{k}
satisfy
\lvert\vec{p}-\vec{q}\rvert\leq\varepsilon
(using point-wise difference and
L_{2}
-norm), then

\lvert\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p}))-\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{q}))\rvert\leq\varepsilon\cdot\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{cpt}},

where
\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{cpt}}\vcentcolon=\mathsf{u}^{*}\cdot\max(\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{+}},\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{-}})\cdot(2k^{2}+k)
is the Lipschitz-constant of the CPT-function, which depends on the outcome with the largest absolute utility
\mathsf{u}^{*}\vcentcolon=\max_{i\in[1,k]}\lvert\mathsf{u}(o_{i})\rvert
, the Lipschitz-constants
\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{+}},\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{-}}
of the probability weighting functions
\mathsf{w}^{+}
and
\mathsf{w}^{-}
, and the number of outcomes
k
.

Proof Sketch.

This very technical proof (
Sec.

C-C
) proceeds by straightforward unfolding of the CPT-function
\mathsf{cpt}
.
∎

Lemma IV.7

(Solving the Optimization Problem)
.

Given a Pareto frontier
\mathcal{P}
and an outcome vector
\vec{o}
, we can
\varepsilon
-approximate

\sup_{\vec{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p}))
, and the computation takes time

(\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{cpt}}\cdot\sqrt{k}}{\varepsilon})^{k}\cdot\mathcal{O}(n\log(n))
, where
n=\max(\mathsf{EP}(\mathcal{P}),2^{k})
.

Proof.

The Pareto frontier is a set of
k
-dimensional vectors, i.e.
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Further, all components of the vectors are bounded to be in
[0,1]
.
We can discretize this space of vectors into hypercubes with side length
\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{cpt}}\cdot\sqrt{k}}
.
The resulting number of hypercubes is finite, namely
(1/\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{cpt}}\cdot\sqrt{k}})^{k}=(\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{cpt}}\cdot\sqrt{k}}{\varepsilon})^{k}
.

The largest distance between two vectors in a hypercube is less than the length of its diagonal, i.e.
\sqrt{k}
times its side length, resulting in
\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{cpt}}}
.
Consequently, the difference between the CPT-function evaluated on two different vectors in the same hypercube is at most
\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{cpt}}}\cdot\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{cpt}}=\varepsilon
.
Using this, we proceed as follows:
For each hypercube, compute its intersection with
\mathcal{P}
, and if it is non-empty, select one vector from the intersection.
The CPT-value of this vector is
\varepsilon
-close to the CPT-value of all other vectors in the hypercube.
Consequently, taking the maximum over all hypercubes yields a point that is
\varepsilon
-close to the desired quantity
\sup_{\vec{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p}))
.

The runtime of this approach is dominated by the number of hypercubes we consider,
(\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{cpt}}\cdot\sqrt{k}}{\varepsilon})^{k}
.
For each hypercube, computing the intersection takes time in
\mathcal{O}(n\log(n))
, where
n
is the number of vertices of the considered polytope
[
47
, Lem. 3]
.
Thus,
n
is the maximum of
\mathsf{EP}(\mathcal{P})
and
2^{k}
, depending on whether the Pareto frontier or the the hypercube has more vertices.
Evaluating the CPT-function takes constant time because we assumed that we have an oracle for it.
∎

While the proposed algorithm is exponential, its asymptotic complexity is in line with the best known algorithms for non-convex optimization, which are at least exponential in the number of variables
[
7
, Chap. 3]
, i.e. in our case also exponential in
k
.
Still, in practice, we recommend using the Branch-and-Bound algorithm
[
7
, Chap. 3.1]
, which, intuitively, incrementally discretizes the set of possible probability vectors, potentially avoiding to investigate all hypercubes.
Using it requires specifying a
“bound operation”
, which is possible using the Lipschitz-continuity of the CPT-function.

Remark 3

(Lower runtime bound for the algorithm)
.

It is unlikely that with an approach like ours — using non-convex optimization to find the optimal point on the Pareto frontier — the worst-case runtime can be polynomial in
k
.
The CPT-function is necessarily non-convex, as both the utility and the probability weighting functions have a non-convex
“S-shape”
.
One of the largest known classes of optimization functions, where such an optimization problem still is polynomial, is the class of
pseudo-convex
functions
[
48
]
.
However, as shown by the counter-example in
Sec.

C-A
, the CPT-function is not pseudo-convex.
Thus, the existence of a polynomial-time algorithm for the computation is non-trivial and challenging.

The complexity of the CPT-value approximation problem largely depends on factors not related to the MDP, namely number of outcomes, largest utility, and precision.
However, the number of outcomes
k
is typically small compared to the size of the MDP, and similarly, we can often bound the largest appearing utilities in the objective (using knowledge of the objective) and the precision (commonly
10^{-6}
).
Thus, the problem is fixed-parameter tractable
[
49
, Def. 1]

V
Robustness of Our Result

V-A

Variations of the CPT-function
\mathsf{cpt}

We have always used
\mathsf{cpt}
as a black-box. Our results only require that it is Lipschitz-continuous (
Lem.

IV.6
).
In particular, our theory still applies for all reasonable choices of utility function
\mathsf{u}
and probability weighting functions
\mathsf{w}^{+}
,
\mathsf{w}^{-}
(which are used for the decision weight
\pi
).
This is relevant because these functions differ between individuals, as they are affected by, e.g., cultural differences
[
50
]
.
Further, if an improved variant of CPT is developed (much like CPT
[
3
]
replacing the original prospect theory
[
51
]
), our methods are most probably still applicable:
This new function will still map prospects to reals and probably be Lipschitz-continuous, so that all our reasoning applies.

V-B

Variations of the Objective

Minimizing Instead of Maximizing.

So far, our formulations only look at the supremum, as we deem it intuitive to maximize rewards.
Naturally, you could also use infimum to minimize the obtained reward, for example, to find the minimal possible CPT-value in an MDP (aka. worst-case scenario).
All our techniques still apply by replacing the supremum with an infimum.
In particular, computing prospects and the Pareto frontier are independent of the use of maximum/minimum.

Variants of Weighted Reachability.

We can change the outcome that is associated with not reaching any target state.
Throughout the paper, we assumed that this corresponds to no change.
Depending on the modeled situation, it could be reasonable to say that not reaching any target should be penalized by giving some reward that is less than every other outcome.
Our theory can easily be extended to this case: Modify the definition of prospect induced by an MC to assign this penalty outcome to all non-target BSCCs.
With this change, all results follow without any further modification.
In particular, we can compute the Pareto frontier representing all prospects and solve the optimization problem on it.

Mean Payoff.

Given a reward function
r\colon\mathsf{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
, a mean payoff objective (a.k.a. long-run average reward) computes the average reward obtained in the limit:

\text{MP}(\rho)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}r(\rho_{i})\right)

In
App.

D
, we provide a reduction from mean payoff to weighted reachability under CPT, based on a similar reduction under expected utility given in
[
8
]
.
Thus, all our results carry over to mean payoff.

Theorem V.1

(Reduction of Mean Payoff to Weighted Reachability)
.

We can transform an MDP
\mathcal{M}
with a mean payoff objective
\Phi^{MP}
to another MDP
\mathcal{M}^{f}
with a weighted reachability objective
\Phi^{WR}
in polynomial time, such that

\mathsf{CPT}\text{-}\mathsf{V}(\mathcal{M},\Phi^{MP})=\mathsf{CPT}\text{-}\mathsf{V}(\mathcal{M}^{f},\Phi^{WR})

Proof Sketch.

For mean payoff, the decisive parts of an MDP are the infinite tails, which occur within a MEC of the MDP.
For each MEC, it is possible to compute the mean payoff within it.
Using this, one can add an additional state for each MEC in the MDP, that represents the idea of staying in the MEC forever (similar to the
\mathsf{stay}
-action in
Sec.

III-B
) and obtaining its mean payoff.
A strategy can choose whether it wants to leave the MEC, making the MEC transient and irrelevant to the mean payoff; or it can choose to stay within the MEC, obtaining its mean payoff.

Each of the newly added states has a reward equal to the mean payoff of the respective MEC.
We can view it as an MDP with a weighted reachability objective that computes the same prospects as the MDP with the mean payoff objective.
∎

VI
Conclusion

We have thoroughly investigated MDPs with CPT, in particular (i) giving a new intuitive definition of CPT-value in an MDP, (ii) providing an algorithm that correctly approximates the CPT-value for every precision
\varepsilon>0
, and (iii) establishing results on the strategy and computational complexity.
These results are based on connecting the CPT-value with multi-objective reachability queries.

We list several interesting directions for future work:
Implementing the algorithm allows to evaluate both its performance as well as the quality of the decisions that are recommended.
To improve the former, we can employ a branch-and-bound algorithm, utilizing the information that the Pareto frontier only intersects with a fraction of the possible hypercubes. Further, outcomes are ordered, so some prospects are clearly dominated by others.
This reduces the number of points that have to be considered in the optimization problem.
Regarding the quality of decisions recommended, it is also interesting to compare CPT with other theories of risk like mean-variance
[
30
]
, conditional value-at-risk
[
52
]
, or entropic risk
[
26
]
.

Our definition of CPT-value employs a kind of
“static”
semantics, in the sense that we fix a complete strategy for the whole MDP and then view it as a prospect.
A
“dynamic”
semantics is also of interest, where the current state and the history that led up to it affect the decision; such a dynamic prospect theory has recently been proposed
[
53
]
.
This comes with several complications and design choices to be made, as discussed in
[
1
, App. C]
: In particular, under non-expected utility, it makes a difference whether we propagate rewards forward or backward through the graph.
It is open whether the nested definition of CPT-value in
[
22
,
23
]
coincides with one of these views or is something else entirely.

Finally, extending our theory to multi-player environments (cf.
[
33
,
34
]
) would allow to lift the unrealistic assumption that all players behave rationally (i.e. according to expected utility theory).
Similarly, it is unrealistic to assume that all transition probabilities are fully known.
We refer to
[
54
]
for a psychological analysis of how people judge probabilities and to
[
1
, Part III]
for a thorough discussion of this setting and how CPT extends to it.

Definition of CPT-function

We list the major components of CPT. Recall that we fix a prospect
\vec{x}=(\vec{o},\vec{p})
with
k
different outcomes.

•

CPT uses a utility function
\mathsf{u}:\mathbb{O}\to\mathbb{R}
with the same purpose as in expected utility theory.

•

CPT splits the outcomes
\vec{o}
into gains and losses because the relation to the current status quo greatly affects human preference.
In the following, let
o_{j}=0
be the outcome indicating that nothing changes.
As the outcomes are ordered, all outcomes with

o_{i}<0

•

CPT uses probability weighting functions

\mathsf{w}^{-}

•

CPT multiplies each outcome with a

.

of the outcome.
In other words, the decision weight of a positive outcome

o_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=i+1}^{k}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}[\Phi(\rho)=o_{m}]

(By
Lem.

III.1
)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}[\Phi(\rho)>o_{i}]

(By definition of the ordering of
\vec{o}
)

By using the same argument, we can also get the analogue for the probability to get something equal or better:
p_{i}+\mathsf{grank}(\vec{x},i)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}[\Phi(\rho)\geq o_{i}].

Using this, we now show that the decision weights for positive outcomes are defined as in
Eq.

4
:

\displaystyle\pi_{i}(\vec{x})

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathsf{w}^{+}(p_{i}+\mathsf{grank}(\vec{x},i))-\mathsf{w}^{+}(\mathsf{grank}(\vec{x},i))

(Definition of decision weight for positive outcomes)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathsf{w}^{+}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\Phi(\rho)\geq o)\right)-\mathsf{w}^{+}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma}(\Phi(\rho)>o)\right)\right)

(By the above analysis of gain rank)

The proof for negative outcomes follows by an analogous analysis of the loss rank
\mathsf{lrank}
.
Thus, we conclude that
Eq.

4
and
Eq.

coincide, i.e. they describe the same value.
∎

Proof.

Let
{\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}
be the MEC Quotient of
\mathcal{M}

.
The MECs can be computed in polynomial time, and iterating over them once to replace each of them takes linear time.
We now prove that
{\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}
is stopping: There are no MECs in
{\widehat{\mathsf{S}}}\setminus(\mathsf{F}\cup\{\mathsf{z}\}
, i.e. in the part of the state space other than the absorbing targets and sink.
As a MEC is reached almost surely

35

, we have that for every strategy
{\widehat{\sigma}}
in
{\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}
it holds that

\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}}^{{\widehat{\sigma}}}[\lozenge\mathsf{F}\cup\{\mathsf{z}\}]=1
.

It remains to show that
\mathsf{CPT}\text{-}\mathsf{V}(\mathcal{M})=\mathsf{CPT}\text{-}\mathsf{V}({\widehat{\mathcal{M}}})
.
To this end, we show that for every strategy
\sigma
in the original MDP, we can construct a strategy
{\widehat{\sigma}}
in the quotient MDP such that the prospect of the induced MCs is the same, i.e.
\mathsf{prospect}(\mathcal{M}^{\sigma},\Phi)=\mathsf{prospect}({\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}^{\widehat{\sigma}},\Phi)

(Note that, as usual, we have fixed a weighted reachability objective

From this, it follows by the definition of CPT-value in
Eq.

8
that the CPT-value of both MDPs is the same.
Note that, by the same argument and
Eq.

10
, the expected value in both MDPs also coincides.

Let
(T_{i},A_{i})
be an arbitrary non-target MEC of
\mathcal{M}
, and let
\rho s
be the history so far, where the last state in the history
s
is in the EC
T_{i}
.
In the following, we use
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\sigma(\rho)}
to denote the probability measure in the MDP with

We now distinguish two cases:

•

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\sigma(\rho)}[\lozenge\mathsf{S}\setminus T_{i}]=0

{\widehat{\sigma}}(\rho\widehat{s}_{i})
surely picks the action
\mathsf{stay}
.
Then, we have that in the original MDP

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\sigma(\rho)}[\lozenge\mathsf{obtainset}(\mathcal{M}^{\sigma}(\rho,\Phi,0))]=1
and in the quotient MDP

\mathbb{P}_{{\widehat{\mathcal{M}}},s}^{{\widehat{\sigma}}(\rho)}[\lozenge\mathsf{obtainset}({\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}^{\widehat{\sigma}}(\rho,\Phi,0))]=1
.
Intuitively, we have shown that we can mimic the staying behaviour.

•

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\sigma(\rho)}[\lozenge\mathsf{S}\setminus T]_{i}>0

Intuitively, in this case we need to compute the distribution over all leaving actions in the original MDP so that we can mimic the leaving behaviour in the quotient MDP.
Formally, this is cumbersome to write down.

Consider the countable induced MC
\mathcal{M}^{\sigma}
where paths in the MDP
\mathcal{M}
are states (see
[
36
, Def. 10.92]
for details on this standard notion), and let the initial state be
\rho s
.
Modify this MC as follows: For every state
\rho s\rho^{\prime}
, make it absorbing if there exists a state

such that

.
Every of these new absorbing states has the form is labelled
\rho s\rho^{\prime}at
, where
a
is the action that was picked before
t
was reached.
Thus, by computing the limiting distribution in this modified MC, we can infer the probability distribution over the leaving actions.
This distribution is exactly the probability distribution over actions that we use as strategy
{\widehat{\sigma}}
in the quotient MDP.
Thus, for every target state, the probability to reach it in
\mathcal{M}
and
{\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}
is equal.

We comment on a further technicality: Reaching different states in the MEC can lead to different behaviour.
For example, assume that after seeing the history
\rho
there is an equal chance of reaching
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
, both of which are in
T_{i}
.
The strategy might recommend staying in
s_{1}
, but leaving in
s_{2}
.
In the quotient MDP, we cannot distinguish these cases, as the history only contains
\widehat{s}_{i}
, the representative of the whole MEC.

This is not a problem for two reasons.
Firstly, our overall goal is to find the optimum strategy.
Since all states in a MEC can reach each other with probability 1, all states in a MEC have the same value.
Thus, an optimal strategy need not distinguish between states in the MEC.

Secondly, we can modify the definition of quotient MDP slightly to account for this fact; this is necessary to prove our current sub-goal, i.e. that we can mimic the behaviour of every strategy such that
\mathsf{prospect}(\mathcal{M}^{\sigma},\Phi)=\mathsf{prospect}({\widehat{\mathcal{M}}}^{\widehat{\sigma}},\Phi)
; intuitively, we also want to be able to mimic
“stupid”
strategies that differentiate between different states in a MEC.
To this end, we keep every original MEC state in the quotient, but only give it one action that surely transitions to the representative
\widehat{s}_{i}
.
This representative is unchanged.
(The transition function then becomes simpler, because we do not have to distinguish transitions leading into a MEC and sum the probability of all MEC states, but we can just go to the original state.)
In this quotient MDP, the history now contains the original state which allows us to also mimic behaviour that differs depending on the entry state.

Overall, we have shown that we can indeed construct a strategy
{\widehat{\sigma}}
such that for every strategy
\sigma
in the original MDP, we can mimic its behaviour in the quotient MDP using
{\widehat{\sigma}}
.
The proof for the other direction is mostly analogous and even slightly simpler.
The
\mathsf{stay}
action can be replaced with a strategy that chooses an arbitrary subset of actions in
A_{i}
.
A distribution over leaving actions is achieved by picking an appropriate memoryless randomized strategy inside the MEC.
Formally, this can be achieved as follows:
Every leaving action can be considered a target set of a multi-objective reachability query.
Since every state in the MEC can reach every other state almost surely (by definition of MEC), the Pareto frontier of this query contains all probability distributions over leaving actions.
Thus, using
[
42
, Cor. 3.5]
, we can obtain a memoryless randomized strategy that achieves exactly the distribution over leaving actions that is prescribed by
{\widehat{\sigma}}
.

Thus, we get that the set of all induced prospects in both MDPs are equal, and can conclude that the CPT-value is equal.

∎

This lemma allows us to assume that w.l.o.g. the MDP we are given is stopping because given an arbitrary MDP, we can transform it to a stopping MDP with the same CPT-value and then compute the CPT-value in the stopping MDP.

Non Pseudo-Convexity of

Lemma C.1

(Non-Pseudo-Convexity)
.

The CPT function is not pseudo-convex (and thus not convex).

Proof.

We denote by
\nabla f
the gradient of
f=(\frac{\partial f}{x_{1}},\ldots,\frac{\partial f}{x_{n}})
.
A function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is pseudo-convex (see, e.g.,
[
48
]
) if the following holds for all
x,y\in X
:

\nabla f(x)^{T}(y-x)\geq 0\Rightarrow f(y)\geq f(x)

To show that
\mathsf{cpt}
is not generally pseudo-convex, we provide a counterexample.
Assume, we have two prospects
\vec{x}^{1}
and
\vec{x}^{2}
, with the outcomes
\mathsf{outc}=\{0,1,2\}

We have

\displaystyle\mathsf{cpt}(\vec{x}^{1})=

\displaystyle\mathsf{u}(2)\cdot w(p^{1}_{1})

\displaystyle+\mathsf{u}(1)\cdot(w(p^{1}_{1}+p^{1}_{2})-w(p^{1}_{1}))

\displaystyle+\mathsf{u}(0)\cdot(1-w(p^{1}_{1}+p^{1}_{2}))

and equivalently

\displaystyle\mathsf{cpt}(\vec{x}^{2})=

\displaystyle\mathsf{u}(2)\cdot w(p^{2}_{1})

\displaystyle+\mathsf{u}(1)\cdot(w(p^{2}_{1}+p^{2}_{2})-w(p^{2}_{1}))

\displaystyle+\mathsf{u}(0)\cdot(1-w(p^{2}_{1}+p^{2}_{2}))

We have that

\displaystyle\nabla\mathsf{cpt}(\vec{x}^{1})^{T}=[\mathsf{u}(2)\cdot w^{\prime}(p^{1}_{1})+\mathsf{u}(1)\cdot(w^{\prime}(p^{1}_{1}+p^{1}_{2})-w^{\prime}(p^{1}_{1})),

\displaystyle\mathsf{u}(1)\cdot w^{\prime}(p^{1}_{1}+p^{1}_{2})]

where
w^{\prime}(x)
is the derivative of
w
(which we omit to write out for readability).

If we assume the prospects to have the following numbers,
\vec{x}^{1}=[2:0.01,1:0.31,0:0.68]
,
\vec{x}^{2}=[2:0.01,1:0.01,0:0.98]
, and using
\mathsf{u}(x)=x
, and

w(x)=\frac{x^{2.25}}{(x^{2.25}+(1-x)^{2.25})^{1/{2.25}}}

we get that

\nabla\mathsf{cpt}(\vec{x}_{1})^{T}(\vec{x}_{2}-\vec{x}1)\;\approx\;0.245>0

but

\mathsf{cpt}(\vec{x}_{2})-\mathsf{cpt}(\vec{x}_{1})\approx-0.105<0

Therefore, we found a violation of the pseudo-convexity condition.
Since it is known that pseudo-convexity implies convexity
[
48
]
, this also proves (what by inspection of
w
and
\mathsf{u}
we already assumed) that in general
\mathsf{cpt}
is not convex.
∎

C-B

Non-monotonicity of the CPT-function

The CPT-function is not monotonic with respect to the probability vector in the prospect. Intuitively, this is the case because increasing the probability of a negative outcome decreases the CPT-function.
We formalize this intuition in
Lem.

C.2
.
Further, we show that it is in fact a
difference-of-monotonic
function (
Cor.

C.3
); this notion is relevant in non-convex optimization, cf.
[
46
, 11.1.2]
. We do not utilize this fact, but include it for completeness.

Lemma C.2
.

Assume that
\mathsf{u}
,
\mathsf{w}^{+}
, and
\mathsf{w}^{-}
are increasing functions.
The CPT function
\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p}))
is monotonically increasing in
\vec{p}
for only positive outcomes and monotonically decreasing for only negative outcomes. Formally, for two vectors
\vec{p}\geq\vec{p}^{\prime}
, we have

1.

if
\vec{o}>\vec{0}
then
\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p}))\geq\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p}^{\prime}))

2.

if
\vec{o}<\vec{0}
then
\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p}))\leq\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p}^{\prime}))

Proof.

The proof is straightforward by rewriting the CPT-function.

The CPT function satisfies stochastic dominance
[
1
]
, but we want to show monotonicity nevertheless since the definition in
[
1
]
lacks a precise mathematical characterization.
As usual, w.l.o.g. let
\vec{o}
be ordered increasingly.

Property (1)

Recall that for
\vec{o}>\vec{0}
, we have

\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p}))=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{u}(o_{i})\cdot\left(\mathsf{w}^{+}\left(\sum_{j=i}^{k}p_{j}\right)-\mathsf{w}^{+}\left(\sum_{j=i+1}^{k}p_{j}\right)\right)

We regroup this sum, such that each term only looks at one sum of probabilities
\sum_{j=i}^{k}p_{j}
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p}))

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=2}^{k}\mathsf{u}(o_{i})\cdot\mathsf{w}^{+}\left(\sum_{j=i}^{k}p_{j}\right)-\mathsf{u}(o_{i-1})\cdot\mathsf{w}^{+}\left(\sum_{j=i}^{k}p_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathsf{u}(o_{1})\cdot\mathsf{w}^{+}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\right)-\mathsf{u}(o_{k})\cdot\mathsf{w}^{+}(0)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=2}^{k}(\mathsf{u}(o_{i})-\mathsf{u}(o_{i-1}))\cdot\mathsf{w}^{+}\left(\sum_{j=i}^{k}p_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathsf{u}(o_{1})\cdot\mathsf{w}^{+}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\right)

We know from the increasing ordering of
o
and because
\mathsf{u}
is an increasing function that
\mathsf{u}(o_{i})\geq\mathsf{u}(o_{i-1})
.
Since also
\mathsf{w}^{+}
is an increasing function, and since we have the assumption
\vec{p}\geq\vec{p}^{\prime}
, we have for each term

\displaystyle(\mathsf{u}(o_{i})-\mathsf{u}(o_{i-1}))\cdot\mathsf{w}^{+}\left(\sum_{j=i}^{k}p_{j}\right)\geq

\displaystyle(\mathsf{u}(o_{i})-\mathsf{u}(o_{i-1}))\cdot\mathsf{w}^{+}\left(\sum_{j=i}^{k}p_{j}^{\prime}\right)

and clearly

\mathsf{u}(o_{1})\cdot\mathsf{w}^{+}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\right)\geq\mathsf{u}(o_{1})\cdot\mathsf{w}^{+}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{\prime}\right).

Thus, we have
\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p}))\geq\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{p}^{\prime}))
.

Property (2)

The proof works analogously.
We have to consider that
\mathsf{u}
is now negative, and we use the weighting function
\mathsf{w}^{-}
instead of
\mathsf{w}^{+}
.
The regrouping works equivalently, and the arguments of an increasing
\mathsf{u}
and increasing
\mathsf{w}^{-}
still hold.
∎

Corollary C.3
.

The CPT-function
\mathsf{cpt}
is a difference-of-monotonic function.

Proof.

The CPT-function is a sum over all outcomes.
We can also write it as the difference between the following two monotonic functions:
firstly the CPT-function on only the positive outcomes, and secondly
(-1)
times the CPT-function on only the negative outcomes.
By
Lem.

C.2
, both of these functions are monotonically increasing (the latter since the CPT-function on negative outcomes is decreasing, and we multiply it with
(-1)
).
∎

Lipschitz-continuity of CPT-function

See

Proof.

The following chain of equations proves our goal

\displaystyle\lvert\mathsf{cpt}

\displaystyle((\vec{o},\vec{p}))-\mathsf{cpt}((\vec{o},\vec{q}))\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{u}(o_{i})\pi(p_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{u}(o_{i})\pi(q_{i})\rvert

(Unfolding definition of
\mathsf{cpt}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lvert\mathsf{u}(o_{i})(\pi(p_{i})-\pi(q_{i}))\rvert

(Reordering and triangle inequality)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{u}^{*}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lvert(\pi(p_{i})-\pi(q_{i})\rvert

(Definition of
\mathsf{u}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{u}^{*}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\max(\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{+}},\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{-}})\cdot(2k^{2}+k)

(See below)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\cdot\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{cpt}}

(Definition of
\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{cpt}}

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lvert(\pi(p_{i})-\pi(q_{i})\rvert\leq\varepsilon\cdot\max(L_{\mathsf{w}^{+}},L_{\mathsf{w}^{-}})\cdot(2k^{2}+k).

We first analyse the summand, i.e. the difference of decision weights.
The definition of decision weight
\pi
distinguishes whether an outcome is 0, positive, negative.
If it is 0, then its decision weight is also 0 independent of the associated probability being
p_{i}
or
q_{i}

We now focus on the case of a positive outcome, i.e.

\displaystyle\left|\pi(p_{i})-\pi(q_{i})\right|

\displaystyle=|\left(\mathsf{w}^{+}(p_{i}+\mathsf{grank}(\vec{p},i))-\mathsf{w}^{+}(\mathsf{grank}(\vec{p},i))\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\left(\mathsf{w}^{+}(q_{i}+\mathsf{grank}(\vec{q},i))-\mathsf{w}^{+}(\mathsf{grank}(\vec{q},i))\right)|

(Unfolding definition of
\pi

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\left(\mathsf{w}^{+}(\sum_{m=i}^{k}p_{m})-\mathsf{w}^{+}(\sum_{m=i+1}^{k}p_{m})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\left(\mathsf{w}^{+}(\sum_{m=i}^{k}q_{m})-\mathsf{w}^{+}(\sum_{m=i+1}^{k}q_{m})\right)\Bigg|

\mathsf{grank}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathsf{w}^{+}(\sum_{m=i}^{k}p_{m})-\mathsf{w}^{+}(\sum_{m=i}^{k}q_{m})\right|

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\left|\mathsf{w}^{+}(\sum_{m=i+1}^{k}q_{m}))-\mathsf{w}^{+}(\sum_{m=i+1}^{k}p_{m})\right|

(Reordering and triangle inequality)

Now, we use the fact that
\mathsf{w}^{+}

Thus, we need to bound the difference between its arguments.
We have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{m=i}^{k}p_{m}-\sum_{m=i}^{k}q_{m}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{m=i}^{k}p_{m}-q_{m}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=i}^{k}\max_{j\in[i,k]}\left|p_{j}-q_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=i}^{k}\varepsilon

(As
\lvert\vec{p}-\vec{q}\rvert\leq\varepsilon

\displaystyle=(k-i)\cdot\varepsilon

By the analogous argument, we get that

\left|\sum_{m=i+1}^{k}q_{m}-\sum_{m=i+1}^{k}p_{m}\right|\leq(k-i+1)\cdot\varepsilon.

We denote the Lipschitz-constant of
\mathsf{w}^{+}
by
\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{+}}

Utilizing these facts and the Lipschitz-continuity of
\mathsf{w}^{+}

\displaystyle\left|\pi(p_{i})-\pi(q_{i})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{+}}\cdot(k-i)\cdot\varepsilon\right)+\left(\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{+}}\cdot(k-i+1)\cdot\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\cdot\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{+}}\cdot(2k-2i+1)

We repeat the argument for negative outcomes. The only difference is in the borders of the summations, now running from 1 to
i
or from 1 to
i-1
. Thus, the last factor becomes
i-1+i-2
, which is equal to
2i-3
.
Overall, for
o_{i}<0
, we have

\left|\pi(p_{i})-\pi(q_{i})\right|\leq\varepsilon\cdot\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{-}}\cdot(2i-3)

We unify the expressions for positive and negative outcomes.
For this, we take the larger of the two Lipschitz-constants
\max(\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{+}},\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{-}})
, and upper bound the last factor by
2k+1
(which is certainly larger than
2i-3
, as
i\leq k
).
Thus, we have

\left|\pi(p_{i})-\pi(q_{i})\right|\leq\varepsilon\cdot\max(\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{+}},\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{-}})\cdot(2k+1)

Now we have analysed the summand, and in the process eliminated the dependence on
i
.
Applying our knowledge, we conclude with the desired claim:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lvert(\pi(p_{i})-\pi(q_{i})\rvert

\displaystyle\leq k\cdot\left(\varepsilon\cdot\max(\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{+}},\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{-}})\cdot(2k+1)\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\cdot\max(\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{+}},\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{w}^{-}})\cdot(2k^{2}+k)

∎

Reduction of Mean Payoff to Weighted Reachability

The MEC Quotient MDP describes an MDP where all MECs are collapsed into single states.
We modify the MEC quotient MDP as described in
Def.

Intuitively, it assumes that we already know the outcome of a MEC.
We add another action to each collapsed state (

Proof.

We first demonstrate how one can intuitively transform the mean payoff MDP to a weighted reachability MDP, but the resulting MDP might be exponentially bigger.
Then we show that we can restrict ourselves to a much smaller subset, making the transformation polynomial.

We can find a function

\sigma

For a fixed strategy

Therefore, for each MEC

r(C_{i}^{\sigma})&\text{else}\end{cases}

[

Therefore, any strategy induces a function
f^{\sigma}
with which we can transform the MDP to a weighted MEC quotient MDP.

For a fixed strategy
\sigma

\mathcal{M}^{\sigma}

\mathsf{prospect}(\mathcal{M}^{\sigma},\Phi^{MP})=\mathsf{prospect}({\mathcal{M}^{f}}^{\sigma})

In the steady state distribution, any transient state has a probability of 0.
Therefore, we only care about the BSCCs.
In the transformed MDP

In the original MDP, they might be actual BSCCs containing several states.
However, we transformed each such BSCC into one single state in the transformed MDP, with the exact mean payoff value as a reward.

First, prove that if we add a stay action for every BSCC that can result from the MEC, then we have exactly the same set of prospects.
Then, we can easily show that

\displaystyle\{\mathsf{prospect}(

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\sigma},\Phi^{MP})\;|\;\sigma\in\Sigma\}=

\displaystyle\{\mathsf{prospect}(\mathcal{M}^{f_{i}},\Phi)\;|\;\forall o_{i}\;\text{MD strategies}\}

Note that there are only finitely many memoryless deterministic strategies for a finite MDP.

We show equality by proving inclusion in both directions.

(i) Suppose we are given a prospect in the original mean payoff MDP.
This means there must be a strategy
\sigma_{j}
under which we can achieve a set of outcomes with certain probabilities.
Then, each outcome must be achieved in a BSCC, and since we added a specific state with this outcome to the transformed MDP

On the other hand, the probability of such an outcome is only determined by the probability of reaching its respective BSCC.
Since we don’t change transient states in the transformed MDP, the probabilities are still the same.

(ii) If we are given a prospect in the transformed MDP, each outcome is received in a target state.
If this target state was already a state in the original MDP, the outcome must still be the same in the original.
If the state is a collapsed state, it comes from an MEC in the original MDP, for which there must exist a strategy such that this BSCC can achieve the exact outcome as a mean payoff.
Thus, there must be a strategy that will reach this BSCC and returns the outcome as mean payoff.

However, this new MDP is now potentially exponentially bigger than before.
But we know that we are not interested in all additional states
\hat{s_{i}}^{f}

If

Thus, we can ignore all new stay actions with less than maximum reward.

Thus, overall, we add one stay action per MEC in the weighted MEC quotient, and certainly, the sup over strategies in the weighted reach MDP is the same as in the MP MDP.

D-C

Effect of the Number of Outcomes

As an interesting side-observation, we discuss the effect of the number of outcomes on the applicability of decision theories in general, and CPT in particular.

Objectives with Two Outcomes.

Switching from expected utility to CPT only makes a difference for the resulting strategy if we have more than two possible outcomes.
Intuitively, when there are only two outcomes with one being strictly better than the other, an optimal strategy will try to put as much probability on the better outcome as possible.
Thus, both CPT or expected utility will always yield a higher value for more probability mass in the higher outcome.
More generally, every function for evaluating prospects that is monotonic results in the same set of optimal strategies.
Thus, it does not make sense to investigate risk-aware MDPs with these kinds of objectives, including non-weighted reachability or
\omega
-regular objectives.

Objectives with Infinitely Many Outcomes.

We remark that our theory does not apply to objectives with infinitely many outcomes such as
total reward
[
6
, Chap. 7]
, where the reward is obtained along the path.
Since no set of states uniquely corresponds to an outcome, there is no
\mathsf{obtainset}
.
Even if there was, the multi-objective reachability query would have infinitely many target sets.
Already for a Markov chain, the computation of a prospect is non-trivial, as the following example shows:
