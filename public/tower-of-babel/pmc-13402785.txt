Multi-scale structural engineering enables ultra-strong and tough eutectogels
Authors: Ning Tang, Yanlong Yin, Hao Zhang, Jianhua Tang, Ousheng Zhang, Jigang Yang, Jun Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAchieving simultaneous enhancement of strength, stiffness, and toughness in polymer gels remains a fundamental challenge due to the thermodynamic incompatibility between energy storage and energy dissipation. Here, we present a multi-scale regulation approach that synergistically integrates directional annealing with deep eutectic solvent-mediated solvent exchange to precisely modulate the polymer network at molecular, nanoscale, and microscale levels. This coordinated hierarchical design yielded poly(vinyl alcohol) eutectogels exhibiting exceptional tensile strength of 62.2 ± 1.8 MPa, a Young’s modulus of 355.3 ± 32.9 MPa, and a toughness of 179.0 ± 11.1 MJ m−3, representing 311-, 11843-, and 597-fold enhancements over the original hydrogel, respectively. The synergistic effects of enhanced interchain hydrogen bonding, crystalline domain formation, and anisotropic network alignment enabled high fracture resistance (131.5 ± 2.3 kJ m−2), fatigue threshold of 15.9 kJ m−2, and damping efficiency of 95.8%, providing robust protection against impact-induced damage. The multi-scale regulation strategy not only offers a promising solution to overcome the conventional trade-offs in mechanical properties, but also establishes universal principles for the design of next-generation soft materials, with significant potential for applications in flexible electronics, wearable devices, and advanced impact-resistant systems.Subject terms: Mechanical properties, Gels and hydrogels

Introduction

The strength-stiffness-toughness trade-off in polymer gels represents a fundamental constraint governed by polymer physics. An open question in soft materials science is whether this trade-off can be substantially overcome through rational structural design. This challenge arises from the thermodynamic incompatibility between energy storage and energy dissipation1–3. High strength and stiffness necessitate densely crosslinked structures that minimize chain conformational entropy, whereas high toughness requires extensive molecular motion to maximize entropy production during deformation4–6. Classical polymer theory elucidates this inverse correlation: rubber elasticity theory establishes that modulus scales with crosslink density (G ~ vₑkT, where vₑ is the number density of elastically effective chains)7,8, while the Lake-Thomas model demonstrates that fracture energy scales with the number of monomers per network strand (Γ ~ NU, where N is the polymerization degree and U is the bond dissociation energy per monomer)9,10. This creates a fundamental dilemma: increasing crosslink density enhances modulus but reduces the chain length between crosslinks, thereby diminishing fracture energy11. Resolving this paradox is of growing importance for emerging applications in flexible electronics12, soft robotics13, and biomedical implants14, where materials must simultaneously withstand mechanical loads and maintain dimensional stability in diverse deformation conditions.

Substantial efforts have been devoted to enhancing gel mechanics through various strategies, each offering distinct advantages while facing specific limitations. Increasing crosslinking density can effectively enhance stiffness but often at the expense of extensibility15,16. Nanocomposite reinforcements improve strength, although stress concentration at filler-matrix interfaces remains a concern17,18. Double network architectures have achieved notable toughness improvements, yet irreversible sacrificial bond rupture can lead to mechanical fatigue under cyclic loading19,20. Recent advances in highly entangled networks and phase-separated systems represent promising directions, though challenges related to processing complexity, environmental sensitivity, or structural stability persist21–23. These considerations suggest that achieving simultaneous improvements in strength, stiffness, and toughness may require design principles that coordinate multiple reinforcing mechanisms rather than optimizing individual ones.

Inspiration for such coordinated design emerges from natural structural materials24,25. Wood, spider silk, and tendons achieve the combinations of high strength and toughness through hierarchical organization, in which structural elements spanning molecular to macroscopic dimensions work synergistically26–28. Translating these design principles into synthetic gels requires the strategic integration of complementary structural elements at distinct organizational levels. At the molecular scale, reversible non-covalent interactions act as sacrificial bonds for dissipating energy29–31. At the nanoscale, ordered structural domains (such as crystalline regions, phase-separated structures, and polymer aggregates) serve as physical crosslinks, which simultaneously reinforce the network and provide additional energy dissipation mechanisms via interfacial interactions and domain deformation32–35. At the microscale, directional alignment creates preferential stress-transfer pathways that distribute mechanical loads efficiently within the gel material36–39. The key challenge lies in achieving coordinated control over structure formation at all three levels within a unified fabrication process40.

Herein, we addressed this challenge through an integrated multi-scale regulation strategy, which systematically orchestrated polymer network architecture using complementary physical processes. Our approach combined directional annealing with deep eutectic solvent (DES)-mediated solvent exchange to achieve precise structural control at different length scales. Annealing has long been used to tailor the network architecture of poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) gels, where elevated temperature accelerates polymer chain mobility and promotes crystalline domain formation. When extended to anisotropic gels, annealing has been combined with structural alignment in two ways. One route first pre-aligns the polymer network through pre-stretching, freeze-casting, or templated assembly, and then applies a subsequent annealing process to consolidate the oriented architecture41–43. The other applies external loading concurrently with thermal treatment to construct an oriented network in a single step44–46, an approach we refer to as directional annealing. In aqueous environments, however, water solvates PVA hydroxyl groups and competes with interchain hydrogen bonding, which limits the density of physical crosslinks attainable by annealing alone. DES alters this thermodynamic balance by weakening polymer-solvent solvation, which drives further interchain association and supports the growth of crystalline domains within the pre-established oriented network. Coupling directional annealing with DES-mediated solvent exchange therefore offers a route to coordinated regulation across molecular, nanoscale, and microscale levels in a single material system. This coordinated multi-scale regulation enabled fabrication of PVA eutectogels with mechanical synergy that extends beyond current material boundaries: tensile strength of 62.2 ± 1.8 MPa, Young’s modulus of 355.3 ± 32.9 MPa, and toughness of 179.0 ± 11.1 MJ m−3, representing 311-, 11843-, and 597-fold enhancements compared to the precursor hydrogel. These simultaneous improvements in traditionally competing properties demonstrate that the strength-stiffness-toughness trade-off can be substantially mitigated through coordinated multi-scale structural regulation. Beyond mechanical strength and stiffness, the hierarchically structured eutectogels exhibited exceptional fracture resistance (fracture energy of 131.5 ± 2.3 kJ m−2) and fatigue tolerance (threshold of 15.9 kJ m−2). The directionally aligned polymer chains facilitated efficient energy dissipation via coordinated molecular mechanisms including frictional interactions and reversible hydrogen bond disruption, resulting in a damping efficiency of 95.8% and strong impact resistance. Furthermore, systematic investigation of various DES compositions validated the universality of our multi-scale regulation strategy, establishing generalizable principles for the rational construction of next-generation soft materials.

Results

Multi-scale regulation strategy transcends conventional mechanical trade-offs

Figure 1 illustrates our integrated approach for fabricating hierarchically structured eutectogels through coordinated multi-scale regulation. The fabrication process (Fig. 1a) commenced with a single freezing-thawing cycle of 20 wt% aqueous PVA solution, yielding a precursor hydrogel (PVA20). This hydrogel was subsequently subjected to directional annealing using a custom clamping device (Supplementary Fig. 1): the sample was uniaxially stretched to 100% strain, dehydrated at 40 °C for 10 h, and thermally annealed under 90 °C for 1.5 h (Supplementary Fig. 2). The annealing temperature was strategically chosen to exceed the glass transition temperature of PVA (75.2 °C) while remaining well below its melting point (222.9 °C, Supplementary Fig. 3), thereby enabling optimal chain mobility and reorganization without compromising network integrity. The PVA matrix obtained after this step behaved as an oriented polymer film (Supplementary Fig. 4). After rehydration, the resulting anisotropic hydrogel (PVA-DA) underwent solvent exchange with D-sorbitol/choline chloride (D-SL/ChCl) DES (Supplementary Fig. 5) to produce the final eutectogel (PVA-DA-SE).

This two-step process induced structural evolution across three hierarchically connected length scales (Fig. 1b). At the molecular scale (~10−10 m), interchain hydrogen bonding was progressively enhanced through controlled annealing and thermodynamically driven polymer-solvent interactions. At the nanoscale (~10−9 m), crystalline domains nucleated and grew, serving as physical crosslinks that reinforced the network. At the microscale (~10−6 m), polymer chains adopted preferential orientations along the stretching direction, creating an anisotropic fibrillar architecture. The thermodynamic driving forces underlying this hierarchical organization, including entropy reduction during directional stretching, thermal activation of molecular rearrangement, and altered polymer-solvent interactions in DES, are systematically elucidated in subsequent sections.

Mechanical characterization revealed the synergistic effect of this multi-scale regulation (Fig. 1c, d). The pristine PVA20 hydrogel exhibited typical soft gel behavior with a tensile strength of 0.2 ± 0.03 MPa, Young’s modulus of 0.03 ± 0.01 MPa, and toughness of 0.3 ± 0.1 MJ m−3. Directional annealing dramatically transformed these properties. PVA-DA showed a 76-fold increase in tensile strength (15.2 ± 1.6 MPa), accompanied by marked improvements in both modulus and toughness, highlighting the critical contribution of structural alignment. Subsequent solvent exchange further amplified these enhancements, yielding PVA-DA-SE with a tensile strength of 62.2 ± 1.8 MPa, Young’s modulus of 355.3 ± 32.9 MPa, and toughness of 179.0 ± 11.1 MJ m−3, which corresponded to 311-, 11843-, and 597-fold improvements over the original hydrogel, respectively. Correspondingly, the true stress increased from 1.7 MPa for PVA20 to 340.3 MPa for PVA-DA-SE (Supplementary Fig. 6). Systematic comparison with existing high-performance PVA-based gel materials revealed the performance position of our eutectogel in the stiffness and toughness properties. PVA-DA-SE eutectogel occupied a previously unattained region in the property space, exceeding the performance of PVA-based hydrogels, ionogels, and eutectogels (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 1)5,25,32,35,36,42,47–54. Moreover, similar performance advantages were also observed for PVA-DA-SE compared to other reported eutectogels (Fig. 1e, f, and Supplementary Table 2)32,35,42,53–79. In particular, compared with representative isotropic eutectogels enabled by variable temperature solvent exchange32, PVA-DA-SE achieves approximately 1.5-, 3.4-, and 2.1-fold improvements in tensile strength, Young’s modulus, and toughness, respectively. These results validated that the strength-stiffness-toughness trade-off is not an inherent thermodynamic constraint but rather arises from insufficient structural coordination across length scales.

It is noteworthy that no obvious thermal transition peak was observed in the DSC curve over the temperature range from 15 to –80 °C, indicating that the PVA-DA-SE eutectogel demonstrates excellent freeze resistance (Supplementary Fig. 8). Moreover, the resultant PVA-DA-SE demonstrated high temperature resistance, with a 5% mass loss temperature (Td5%) of 158.2 °C (Supplementary Fig. 9). This value is much higher than those of the PVA20 and PVA-DA hydrogels (<100 °C), which can be attributed to the low volatility of the DES component80. Additionally, the PVA-DA-SE eutectogel exhibited favorable long-term ambient stability, with less than 2% mass change and a tensile strength retention of 94.9% after 7 days of room-temperature storage (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Molecular-scale regulation of hydrogen bonding network

The substantial mechanical enhancements observed in PVA-DA-SE originated from coordinated structural modifications across multiple length scales. At the molecular level, the hydrogen bonding governed interchain associations and polymer-solvent thermodynamics, forming the foundation for higher-order structural organization. Attenuated Total Reflection Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to confirm the composition of the eutectogel and analyze hydrogen bonding interactions during the fabrication process (Supplementary Fig. 11). Deconvolution analysis of the hydroxyl absorption band (3000–3750 cm−1) revealed a systematic increase in the proportion of PVA-PVA hydrogen bonds, with their relative area fraction rising from 15.6% (PVA20) to 18.0% (PVA-DA) and further to 26.8% (PVA-DA-SE), as quantified by the characteristic peak at 3210 cm−1 (Fig. 2a)55,81. This progressive enhancement occurred through two distinct mechanisms. The initial directional annealing promoted interchain interactions by removing water molecules from between the pre-aligned polymer chains during controlled dehydration, thereby facilitating the formation of interchain hydrogen bonding. Subsequent solvent exchange replaced water (a relatively good solvent for PVA) with D-SL/ChCl DES (a poor solvent for PVA, which can partially dissolve PVA at 150 °C, Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Fig. 13), which thermodynamically favored polymer-polymer over polymer-solvent interactions and thus further drove interchain association. Notably, the solvent content was reduced after directional annealing and solvent exchange owing to the stepwise densification of the PVA network (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations provided atomic-level insights into the thermodynamic driving forces underlying this transformation (Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Fig. 16). As shown in Fig. 2b, PVA chains adopted relatively extended conformations in water but collapsed into curlier conformations in DES, with the peak radius of gyration (Rg) decreasing from 12.8 to 9.7 Å (Supplementary Fig. 17). Consistently, experimental results revealed a macroscopic volume contraction for PVA-DA-SE eutectogel, where the volume decreased to 40.1 ± 2.0% of the original PVA-DA hydrogel after solvent exchange (Supplementary Fig. 18). This difference directly reflected the contrasting solvent qualities: water solvates PVA hydroxyl groups effectively, promoting chain expansion, whereas DES provides a less favorable solvation environment that drives chain contraction and interchain aggregation. Comparative analysis indicated that PVA chains formed 40% more interchain hydrogen bonds in D-SL/ChCl DES than in water (Fig. 2c), with the corresponding interaction energies becoming more negative (from −0.75 to −1.65 kcal mol−1; Fig. 2d). Concurrently, the polymer-solvent interaction energies weakened from −3.25 kcal mol−1 for PVA-water to −1.92 kcal mol−1 for PVA-DES (Fig. 2e), reflecting reduced competition between solvent molecules for PVA hydroxyl groups. This thermodynamic shift favored the preferential association of PVA chains over polymer-solvent interactions, thereby establishing a molecular-scale driving force for the formation of higher-order structures.

To probe the mechanical relevance of these hydrogen bonding networks, two-dimensional correlation spectroscopy (2DCOS) measurements were performed on PVA20, PVA-DA, and PVA-DA-SE under tensile mode. In the 2DCOS spectra, red regions indicate positive cross-peaks while blue regions indicate negative cross-peaks. The response sequence of two bands (v1, v2) can be measured by Noda’s rule. If the cross-peaks at (v1, v2) show the same sign in both synchronous and asynchronous spectra (both red or both blue), v1 responds before v2; if the signs are opposite, the sequence is reversed. For PVA20 (Fig. 2f), the cross-peak at (3210, 3554) cm−1 appeared red in the synchronous spectrum (top) but blue in the asynchronous spectrum (bottom), indicating that free or weakly associated hydroxyls (3554 cm−1) responded before interchain hydrogen bonds (3210 cm−1). In contrast, both PVA-DA (Fig. 2g) and PVA-DA-SE (Fig. 2h) exhibited the same color (red) at this position in both spectra, indicating that interchain hydrogen bonds (3210 cm−1) responded before free hydroxyls (3554 cm−1). This inversion reflected the fundamentally different network architectures. In the precursor hydrogel, the sparse interchain hydrogen bonds were not the primary load-bearing elements, whereas in the processed materials, the enhanced hydrogen bonding network directly participated in stress transfer and energy dissipation. In summary, directional annealing initiated interchain hydrogen bond formation by activating chain mobility and reducing hydration, while subsequent solvent exchange amplified this effect by shifting the thermodynamic balance to favor polymer-polymer associations. The resulting architecture, characterized by strengthened interchain cohesion and mechanically responsive hydrogen bonding networks, provided the molecular foundation for the higher-order structural organization.

Nanoscale crystalline domain formation and growth

The enhanced interchain hydrogen bonding established at the molecular scale created favorable thermodynamic conditions for the nucleation and growth of crystalline domains. Initial evidence of this progressive structural organization came from optical transmittance measurements. All materials maintained high transparency (>80% over 400–800 nm), ruling out large-scale phase separation. Nevertheless, a systematic decrease in transmittance at 600 nm from 94% for PVA20 to 87% for PVA-DA and 83% for PVA-DA-SE suggested the emergence of nanoscale light-scattering structures (Fig. 3a, b). X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) provided direct evidence for this crystallization (Fig. 3c). The pristine PVA20 hydrogel exhibited an amorphous structure, with two broad halos centered at approximately 2θ ≈ 28° and 41°, characteristic of water-dominated systems. After directional annealing, PVA-DA showed distinct crystalline peaks at 2θ = 19.6° and 40.6°, corresponding to the (101) and (102) reflection planes of PVA crystallites, respectively. Upon solvent exchange, these peaks sharpened obviously in PVA-DA-SE, with an additional peak appearing at 2θ = 22.9° (200 plane of PVA crystallites), demonstrating improved crystalline order41,82. Quantitative analysis based on the Scherrer equation revealed that crystalline domain size increased from 5.7 nm (PVA-DA) to 6.3 nm (PVA-DA-SE) (Supplementary Fig. 19), while crystallinity rose from 6.8 to 19.1% (see “Methods” for the details on calculation)83,84.

Small-Angle X-ray Scattering (SAXS) further clarified the spatial distribution of these nanoscale crystalline domains (Fig. 3d, and Supplementary Fig. 20 and Supplementary Fig. 21)60,85. Despite the increased domain size and crystallinity following solvent exchange, the average interdomain spacing (L) remained constant at 11.2 nm for both PVA-DA and PVA-DA-SE. These findings pointed to a two-stage crystallization mechanism: directional annealing first established a spatial template that defined the distribution of crystalline nuclei, while subsequent solvent exchange promoted the growth and perfection of existing domains rather than generating new nucleation sites (Fig. 3e, f). The resulting nanoscale architecture, characterized by moderate crystallinity (~19%) and well-defined interdomain spacing, served to balance mechanical reinforcement and energy dissipation. The crystalline domains served as physical crosslinks that enhanced network strength and stiffness, while the preserved amorphous regions between domains allowed for chain mobility. To examine whether these crystalline structures participate in mechanical deformation, XRD measurements were performed on PVA-DA-SE stretched to 100% strain (Fig. 3g). The crystallinity decreased from 19.1 to 16.5%, indicating partial disruption of crystalline domains under loading. This result suggested that the crystalline domains underwent partial disruption under loading and contributed to energy dissipation. The detailed strain-dependent evolution is further discussed in the following sections.

Microscale structural alignment and mechanical anisotropy

Building upon the molecular-scale hydrogen bonding networks and nanoscale crystalline domains, microscale structural alignment completed the hierarchical architecture by translating these lower-level organizations into direction-dependent mechanical properties. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Fig. 4a) and optical microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 22) directly visualized this structural transformation. PVA20 exhibited a random, isotropic network, whereas PVA-DA and PVA-DA-SE displayed highly aligned fibrillar architectures along the stretching direction. This alignment was further confirmed by atomic force microscopy (AFM) phase imaging (Supplementary Fig. 23). Notably, both the oriented fibrillar architecture and the confined DES phase were preserved in PVA-DA-SE after stretching to 100% strain, confirming the structural and compositional stability of the eutectogel under large tensile deformation (Supplementary Fig. 24, Supplementary Fig. 25, and Supplementary Fig. 26). Two-dimensional SAXS patterns provided additional evidence at the nanoscale. The circular scattering ring observed in PVA20 transformed into distinctly elliptical patterns in PVA-DA and PVA-DA-SE (Fig. 4b), with azimuthal intensity profiles indicating this preferential orientation (Fig. 4c).

The anisotropic structure resulted in pronounced direction-dependent mechanical properties (Fig. 4d). Along the alignment direction, PVA-DA-SE achieved an ultimate tensile strength of 62.2 ± 1.8 MPa, higher than that in the perpendicular direction (45.5 ± 3.4 MPa). Similarly, Young’s modulus and toughness showed strong directional dependence: 355.3 ± 32.9 MPa and 179.0 ± 11.1 MJ m−3 parallel to alignment, compared to 189.9 ± 20.9 MPa and 128.1 ± 17.0 MJ m−3 in the perpendicular direction (Supplementary Fig. 27). The corresponding anisotropy ratios (defined as the ratio of mechanical properties parallel and perpendicular to the orientation direction) were 1.37 for strength, 1.87 for Young’s modulus, and 1.40 for toughness (Fig. 4e)86. The similar ratios for strength and toughness indicated that both properties were governed by the same structural features, while the higher ratio for Young’s modulus revealed that the elastic stiffness was more sensitive to structural alignment. To validate the critical role of directional processing, an isotropic control sample (PVA-A-SE) was prepared by annealing and solvent exchange without prior stretching. This control exhibited a tensile strength of 57.3 ± 0.8 MPa, a Young’s modulus of 319.8 ± 15.2 MPa, and a toughness of 155.9 ± 14.3 MJ m−3, all intermediate between the parallel and perpendicular values of PVA-DA-SE (Supplementary Fig. 28), demonstrating that pre-stretching-induced structural alignment was important for achieving the observed mechanical enhancement and anisotropy.

The hierarchical architecture also conferred exceptional fracture resistance through the simultaneous activation of multiple toughening mechanisms. Fracture energy measurements revealed pronounced directional anisotropy with values reaching 131.5 ± 2.3 kJ m−2 parallel to the alignment direction, compared to 50.7 ± 0.4 kJ m−2 in the perpendicular direction (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 29). This nearly three-fold difference can be attributed to distinct crack propagation modes. Cracks growing perpendicular to the alignment must rupture aligned polymer chains and traverse oriented crystalline domains, whereas cracks propagating parallel to alignment primarily separate weaker lateral interactions (Supplementary Fig. 30). Finite element analysis (FEA) supported this interpretation, showing that the stress at the crack tip when fracture occurred was higher for PVA-DA-SE// than for PVA-DA-SE⊥ (Fig. 4g). Fatigue threshold measurements demonstrated the damage tolerance under cyclic loading conditions, with values of 15.9 kJ m−2 along the alignment direction (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 31), nearly twice the value in perpendicular direction (8.0 kJ m−2) (Supplementary Fig. 32 and Supplementary Fig. 33). Trouser tearing tests (Fig. 4i) provided further insight into direction-dependent fracture resistance. The steady-state tearing force was higher when the crack propagated perpendicular to the alignment direction (97.5 kN m−1) compared to parallel propagation (91.6 kN m−1). Collectively, these results demonstrated that the microscale alignment, in conjunction with the molecular and nanoscale structural features, created an integrated hierarchical architecture that enabled both high strength and exceptional damage tolerance.

Cross-scale synergy and energy dissipation mechanisms

The preceding sections characterized structural evolution at three distinct length scales. The critical question remains how these hierarchical features function synergistically during mechanical deformation. Single-cycle loading-unloading tests revealed progressive activation of energy dissipation with increasing deformation (Fig. 5a). The damping capacity increased systematically from 89.2% at 0.5 strain to 95.8% at 2.0 strain (Supplementary Fig. 34), indicating the sequential engagement of distinct dissipation pathways at different strain levels. Based on the structural characterization results, we proposed a hierarchical dissipation model (Supplementary Fig. 35). At the molecular scale, energy dissipation originated from the reversible disruption and reformation of interchain hydrogen bonds, where 2DCOS revealed that interchain hydrogen bonds responded prior to free hydroxyls under mechanical loading (Fig. 2h), and FTIR deconvolution further quantified their disruption at low strains and subsequent reformation at 300% strain (Supplementary Fig. 36). At the nanoscale, crystalline domains underwent progressive deformation and partial disruption with increasing strain, accompanied by stress-induced recrystallization at high strains as revealed by XRD (Supplementary Fig. 37). At the microscale, 2D SAXS measurements verified continuous enhancement of chain orientation with increasing strain (Supplementary Fig. 38), which promoted intensified interchain slippage and friction and dissipated mechanical energy efficiently. Collectively, the coordinated evolution of hydrogen bonding, crystalline domains, and chain alignment across multiple length scales accounted for the high damping efficiency and efficient energy dissipation of the PVA-DA-SE eutectogel.

Cyclic tensile testing provided further insight into the structural origins of energy dissipation (Fig. 5b). During the first loading cycle to a strain of 1.0 mm mm−1, PVA-DA-SE dissipated 24.9 MJ m−3 with a residual strain of 0.76 mm mm−1. The second cycle showed a dramatic decrease to 6.0 MJ m−3, representing approximately 76% reduction. The dissipated energy subsequently stabilized at 2.6 MJ m−3 by the twentieth cycle (Supplementary Fig. 39). This behavior can be correlated with our XRD observations. Stretching to 100% strain reduced crystallinity from 19.1 to 16.5% (Fig. 3g), representing partial disruption of crystalline domains. The energy dissipation in the first cycle was thus consistent with the irreversible rupture of weaker crystalline domains and less stable hydrogen bond clusters. The persistent but diminished energy dissipation in subsequent cycles reflected the continued operation of reversible mechanisms, primarily dynamic hydrogen bond exchange within the remaining network. This observation supports the hierarchical design concept, in which sacrificial elements coexist with recoverable interactions. The strain rate dependence of mechanical behavior further illuminated the multi-scale nature of deformation mechanisms (Fig. 5c). As the loading rate increased from 50 to 400 mm min−1, the damping capacity increased from 93.7 to 98.0% at 1.0 strain, accompanied by a corresponding increase in dissipated energy (Supplementary Fig. 40). This rate-sensitive response is characteristic of viscoelastic materials with multiple relaxation processes. At higher loading rates, insufficient time for molecular relaxation restricts chain mobility, which promotes increased bond rupture and consequently higher energy dissipation. Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) corroborated these observations (Fig. 5d), showing frequency-dependent increases in both storage and loss modulus across the range of 0.01 to 100 Hz. This frequency dependence suggests the involvement of relaxation processes operating across different time scales. Rapid hydrogen bond exchange dominates at higher frequencies, while slower crystalline domain reorganization contributes at lower frequencies. Furthermore, variable-temperature DMA measurements demonstrated that the peak tan δ value appeared at 26.4 °C, indicating that the eutectogel exhibited optimal energy dissipation capacity at room temperature (Supplementary Fig. 41).

The combination of high stiffness, high toughness, and efficient damping suggests potential applications in impact protection. To evaluate this potential, we conducted a series of impact resistance tests. In a falling ball test, a steel ball (20 mm diameter, 32.7 g) was dropped from 25 cm onto glass slides with and without eutectogel protection (Supplementary Fig. 42). The unprotected glass shattered directly upon impact (Supplementary Fig. 43). As displayed in Fig. 5e, the glass protected by PVA-DA-SE remained intact, confirming its effective impact energy absorption capacity. In contrast, the glass protected by PVA20 was directly broken; the glass protected by PVA-DA developed cracks upon the first impact and was subsequently shattered by the rebounded steel ball. Puncture testing revealed a maximum resistance force of 50.9 ± 8.2 N (Fig. 5f), indicating resistance to localized penetration. Dynamic impact experiments provided quantitative comparison of protective performance (Supplementary Fig. 44). Plastic marbles fired from a model pistol created craters in floral foam, with crater depth serving as a metric for impact resistance. Without protection, crater depth reached 30.8 ± 1.3 mm (Supplementary Fig. 45). Protection by PVA20 hydrogel reduced this to 12.6 ± 0.5 mm, though the hydrogel was penetrated. PVA-DA prevented penetration entirely, reducing crater depth to 8.6 ± 0.5 mm. PVA-DA-SE achieved the best performance among the PVA-based materials, with a crater depth of only 3.2 ± 0.4 mm. For comparative context, we also tested several commercial materials of equivalent thickness (0.5 mm) under identical conditions (Fig. 5g). Direct comparison between materials of fundamentally different compositions and architectures has inherent limitations, but these results provide useful benchmarks for practical applications. Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) film and ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA) foam were penetrated by the marbles, while polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), polyurethane (PU), polyethylene (PE), and polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) films successfully prevented penetration. Among the non-penetrated materials, PVA-DA-SE produced a shallower crater than PU and PE, though deeper than PTFE. The impact resistance of PVA-DA-SE can be attributed to the synergistic contribution of its mechanical properties. The high Young’s modulus provides initial resistance to elastic deformation, limiting force transmission to the protected substrate. The high toughness prevents brittle fracture under impact loading, enabling controlled energy absorption through plastic deformation and network chain sliding. The high damping capacity originated from the hierarchical hydrogen-bonding network and converted mechanical impact energy into heat, preventing energy accumulation that could lead to catastrophic failure. While the precise quantitative contribution of each mechanism requires further investigation, the collective effect of these properties enables effective impact protection performance.

Universality of multi-scale regulation strategy

Directional annealing serves as the structural starting point of the multi-scale regulation strategy, establishing the anisotropic network architecture on which the subsequent solvent exchange further enhances the mechanical performance. Building on this foundation, we further assessed the generality of the strategy by systematically investigating its compatibility with diverse DES compositions. These DESs were formulated by combining ChCl with various polyols, including ethylene glycol (EG), 1,2-propylene glycol (1,2-PG), 1,3-propylene glycol (1,3-PG), glycerol (Gly), xylitol (Xyl), and D-sorbitol (D-SL), all at a consistent hydroxyl-to-ChCl molar ratio of 6:1 (Fig. 6a)87. These polyols span a range of carbon chain lengths (from 2 to 6 carbons) and hydroxyl group numbers (from 2 to 6), enabling systematic investigation of structure-property relationships. ATR-FTIR analysis of the resulting eutectogels revealed a direct correlation between DES composition and the relative area of the 3210 cm−1 peak, which is assigned to PVA-PVA hydrogen bonds. The relative area followed the order: 1,3-PG/ChCl <EG/ChCl <1,2-PG/ChCl <Gly/ChCl <Xyl/ChCl <D-SL/ChCl (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 46). Based on the thermodynamic framework established in the molecular-scale regulation section, this trend can be interpreted in terms of solvent quality. DESs containing polyols with more hydroxyl groups generally acted as poorer solvents for PVA, thereby thermodynamically favoring polymer-polymer over polymer-solvent interactions. Notably, among polyols with identical hydroxyl numbers, structural isomerism also plays a role. 1,3-PG/ChCl exhibited lower interchain hydrogen bonding than both EG/ChCl and 1,2-PG/ChCl. This deviation can be attributed to the greater spacing between hydroxyl groups in the 1,3-substitution pattern compared to the vicinal arrangement in EG and 1,2-PG, which may weaken the cooperative interactions within the DES and consequently enhance its solvation capability toward PVA chains.

The connection between molecular-level interactions and nanoscale crystallization was further corroborated by XRD analysis. As shown in Fig. 6c, the crystallinity of the eutectogels exhibited a trend consistent with the aforementioned intermolecular hydrogen bonding behavior. This consistency underscored the cascading effect of our hierarchical approach. Molecular-scale regulation established thermodynamic conditions that facilitate chain association and crystallization (Fig. 6d). The higher performance of D-SL/ChCl DES may be attributed to its ability to enhance intermolecular interactions while preserving sufficient chain mobility for crystalline domain growth, thereby providing a favorable environment for controlled crystallization. Accordingly, the mechanical properties of the eutectogels showed a clear correlation with polyol structure (Fig. 6e, f). Tensile strength, Young’s modulus, and toughness all increased with the number of carbon atoms and hydroxyl groups in the polyol (except for 1,3-PG/ChCl), aligning with the trend observed for intermolecular interactions and crystallinity (Supplementary Table 3). The anomalous behavior of 1,3-PG/ChCl is consistent with its distinct hydroxyl positioning those results in lower interchain hydrogen bonding. These correlations indicate that the multi-scale regulation mechanism operates across all tested DES systems, with the extent of mechanical enhancement governed by the polyol molecular structure.

Moreover, we verified the applicability of our multi-scale strategy to other solvent systems. Taking polypropylene glycol (PPG-400) and 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate (BMIBF4) as representative organic solvent and ionic liquid, respectively, the corresponding organogel and ionogel were prepared via directional annealing and solvent exchange. Both gels achieved pronounced mechanical reinforcement, highlighting the effectiveness of our strategy in diverse solvent environments (Supplementary Fig. 47). These results confirm the generality of this multi-scale regulation approach beyond DES systems, supporting its robustness and potential for constructing high-performance soft materials.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a multi-scale regulation strategy integrating directional annealing and DES-mediated solvent exchange for the fabrication of ultra-strong and tough eutectogels. By precisely engineering hydrogen bonding interactions at the molecular scale, crystalline domain dimensions at the nanoscale, and anisotropic network alignment at the microscale, we constructed an aligned PVA network crosslinked by densely packed crystalline domains. The optimized PVA-DA-SE eutectogel exhibited outstanding mechanical properties. Along the alignment axis, it achieved a tensile strength of 62.2 ± 1.8 MPa, a Young’s modulus of 355.3 ± 32.9 MPa, and a toughness of 179.0 ± 11.1 MJ m−3, accompanied by the tear resistance and fatigue tolerance. Furthermore, the material enabled efficient energy dissipation through frictional interactions between PVA chains, reversible rupture and reformation of hydrogen bonds, and dynamic reorganization of crystalline domains under deformation, conferring impressive impact resistance. These results demonstrated that the long-standing strength-stiffness-toughness trade-off in polymer gels could be substantially mitigated through coordinated structural regulation across multiple length scales. Collectively, these synergistic features provide a generalizable design framework for advanced soft materials, with potential applications in vibration damping, soft robotics, and impact protection in next-generation wearable devices.

Methods

Preparation of PVA-DA-SE

PVA20 hydrogel was fabricated via a freeze-thaw cycling process. Briefly, a 20 wt% aqueous PVA solution was poured into a glass mold with internal dimensions of 6.5 cm × 10 cm × 2 mm, frozen at −18 °C for 12 h, and followed by thawing at 25 °C for 2 h. The as-prepared hydrogel was subjected to mechanical conditioning through uniaxial stretching using a clamping system, in which the central 4.5 cm region of the sample was stretched to a predetermined strain while the two 1 cm regions at both ends were fixed by the clamps. Subsequent thermal treatment consisted of sequential dehydration and annealing: initial stabilization at 40 °C for 10 h and then thermal annealing at 90 °C for 1.5 h in a vacuum oven. The dehydrated polymeric matrix was then rehydrated by immersion in deionized water for 24 h to obtain the PVA-DA hydrogel. Finally, eutectogel formation was achieved via solvent exchange: the PVA-DA hydrogel was immersed in 500 mL of DES for 24 h to displace water, and the resulting material was designated as PVA-DA-SE.

Mechanical tests

Mechanical tests were conducted on a testing machine (ETM504C, WANCE) using dumbbell-shaped specimens (25 mm gauge length × 4 mm width) at room temperature. A 500 N load cell was utilized, with the tensile rate set at 50 mm min−1. Toughness was quantified as the area enclosed by the engineering stress-strain curve, while Young’s modulus was derived from the slope of the initial linear segment (0–5%) of the curve. Cyclic tensile tests were conducted under three conditions: (1) 20 cycles at 100% strain without interval; (2) One cycle at each strain level from 50 to 200%; (3) 100% strain at tensile velocities of 50, 100, 200, and 400 mm min−1. Tear tests were performed using trouser-shaped gel samples (50 mm length × 15 mm width, 20 mm crack length) by clamping two arms (one fixed, one stretched at 50 mm min−1). Fracture energy was evaluated via unnotched and single-edge notched samples (10 mm gauge length × 5 mm width × 0.5 mm thickness; single-edge notch of 1 mm), with uniaxial stretching performed at a speed of 3 mm min−1. The fracture energy (Gc) was calculated using the formula: Gc = 6wc/λc, where c denotes the notch length (1 mm), λc represents the elongation at break of the notched specimen, and w corresponds to the strain energy of the unnotched sample integrated up to εc. Fatigue resistance was evaluated using single-notch dumbbell samples (25 mm gauge length × 4 mm width) on a universal testing machine. Digital image correlation tracked 1 mm initial crack propagation during continuous cycles without relaxation. The Nth cyclic strain energy density was calculated as W(N) = ∫0εσdε, and the energy release rate was calculated as G(N) = 2k(N) × c(N) × W(N), where k(N) = 3/ε+1, and c(N) represents the crack length at the Nth cycle.

Other characterizations

ATR-FTIR spectra covering 4000–400 cm−1 were acquired with a Shimadzu IRXross spectrometer, and curve-fitting was performed in the 3000–3750 cm−1 region with PeakFit software. UV-Vis transmittance measurements were conducted on a G-9 spectrometer over the 400–800 nm wavelength range at 1 nm intervals. XRD patterns were collected on a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer (Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.5418 Å), with the 2θ range scanned from 5° to 60° at a continuous rate of 10 ° min−1. All samples were measured in their as-prepared solvent-containing state without any drying or pretreatment. Crystallinity was evaluated as the proportion of the diffraction peak area associated with the (101) crystal plane (2θ = 18−21°) relative to the total integral area of the entire XRD pattern. The crystalline domain size (D) was derived via the Scherrer equation: D = kλ/(βcosθ), where k takes a constant value of 1, β represents the full width at half maximum (FWHM) at 2θ = 19.6°, while θ = 9.8°. SAXS measurements were performed with a Pilatus 1MF detector (Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.5418 Å), with a sample-detector distance of 3260 mm and an exposure time of 600 s. The average interdomain distance (L) was calculated as L = 2π/qmax, where qmax denotes the q-vector corresponding to the intensity peak, and Lorenz-corrected 1D curves were used to illustrate the variation trend of I(q)*q2 with q. The freezing and melting points of D-SL/ChCl DES were determined using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC, TA 25) under a N2 atmosphere, involving cyclic cooling and heating over the range of 15 to −80 °C and back to 15 °C, both conducted at a constant rate of 5 °C min−1. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was executed with a METTLER TGA/DSC3+ under N2 protection, following a linear heating program from 40 to 800 °C at 10 °C min−1. Gel surface morphologies were observed using a JSM-7610F SEM. All samples were dehydrated prior to imaging. PVA20 and PVA-DA hydrogels were directly freeze-dried for 24 h, whereas PVA-DA-SE eutectogel was first immersed in deionized water for 24 h to extract DES and then freeze-dried for 24 h.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

MD simulations were executed to calculate the number of hydrogen bonds between PVA chains in H2O or DES, as well as the interaction energies between PVA chains and between PVA and H2O or DES, for two systems denoted as M1 and M2. M1 consisted of 20 PVA chains and 1720 H2O molecules, while M2 contained 20 PVA chains, 15 ChCl and 15 D-SL molecules, with both models matching the actual molecular composition of the PVA-DA hydrogel and PVA-DA-SE eutectogel, respectively. All constituent elements of each system were randomly distributed within cubic simulation boxes prior to MD simulations, which were implemented through the Forcite module in Materials Studio (MS) 2020, adopting the COMPASS Ⅲ force field88,89. For the simulation system, Van der Waals interactions were managed via the atom-based scheme with a cut-off distance of 12.5 Å, whereas Coulomb interactions were computed using Ewald summation. A time step of 1 fs was adopted for the integration of the equations of motion. After energy minimization and annealing were completed, each system was thoroughly relaxed for 500 ps in the NPT ensemble (1 atm, 298.0 K) under periodic boundary conditions with the Nose thermostat regulating temperature and the Berendsen barostat controlling pressure. The length of this relaxation period was adequate to stabilize the system’s temperature, potential energy, and total energy. Upon achieving equilibrium, a further 500 ps NPT ensemble simulation was performed to extract trajectories and relevant data for hydrogen bond counts and interaction energies. Dynamic trajectories of each system were recorded at 5 ps time intervals. The criteria for hydrogen bond formation are: bond length <2.7 Å and bond angle >120°.
