A retrospective analysis of risk factors for intestinal necrosis in pediatric secondary intussusception
Authors: Jinfang Sun, Haiyan Hu, Sanli Fan, Yan Qin, Xiaoling Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to identify risk factors of intestinal necrosis in pediatric patients with secondary intussusception, in order to inform clinical strategies for early and timely intervention in high-risk patients.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 92 children treated from October 2018 to October 2023. Descriptive comparisons were made for sex, hematochezia, and symptom duration. Firth logistic regression was used to evaluate the main and interaction effects of age and etiology. A prediction model and a simplified bedside scoring system were developed and validated using ROC analysis.ResultsAmong 92 children, intestinal necrosis occurred in 17 (18.5%). In multivariable Firth logistic regression including etiology and age, IgA vasculitis (IgAV) (OR 7.31, p = 0.005) and Meckel’s diverticulum (MD) (OR 4.43, p = 0.045) showed independent associations with necrosis, whereas age (per year) was not independently significant (p = 0.400). Age significantly modified the IgAV–necrosis association (IgAV×Age interaction OR = 0.416, p = 0.001); within IgAV, each additional year of age was associated with ≈ 29% lower odds of necrosis (per-year OR ≈ 0.72). The interaction-based model achieved AUC 0.847 on internal validation. A simplified 0–4 score (AUC 0.735) stratified necrosis risk into three bands: ≤1 (5% necrosis; NPV 95%), 2–3 (22.5% necrosis; intermediate-risk), and = 4 (50% necrosis; PPV 50%, specificity 92%) at the observed prevalence of 18.5%.ConclusionThe risk of necrosis is significantly higher in intussusceptions secondary to IgAV and probably even higher in the younger child. External validation in broader cohorts is needed.Keywords: Secondary intussusception, Pediatrics, Intestinal necrosis, Risk factors, Interaction effects, Firth logistic regression

Introduction

Intestinal necrosis is a severe and relatively frequent complication of secondary intussusception in children. It often necessitates urgent surgical intervention and is closely associated with increased morbidity and poor outcomes [1–3]. Although clinical features such as symptom duration, hematochezia, and patient age have been proposed as potential predictors [4–11], their diagnostic reliability remains controversial, and no consensus has been established [2–4, 12]. These uncertainties hinder timely recognition and preoperative risk stratification, particularly in resource-constrained or emergency settings.

Among the known pathological lead points (PLPs), two etiologies—MD and IgAV—have been increasingy implicated in heightened necrosis risk [3, 12]. MD may promote bowel compromise through mechanical traction and acid-secreting ectopic mucosa, while IgAV-associated vasculitis induces submucosal edema and hemorrhage, both mechanisms contributing to impaired mesenteric perfusion. Age, meanwhile, is an important biological moderator; developmental differences in vascular architecture, immune maturity, and mucosal resilience may influence the the bowel’s susceptibility to ischemic insult [4, 8]. However, the role of age is complex—while younger children are often thought to be more vulnerable due to immature circulation, some studies have paradoxically reported higher necrosis risk in older patients [11].

Despite these observations, most prior research has evaluated age and etiology in isolation. In clinical practice, however, these factors may interact synergistically, with age modulating the ischemic impact of specific PLPs. The potential for etiology–age interactions remains underexplored and may account for inconsistencies across studies. To address these gaps, this analysis focused on determining the independent and interaction effects of MD, IgAV, and age on the risk of intestinal necrosis in pediatric secondary intussusception. We hypothesized that younger age amplifies necrosis risk in children with high-risk etiologies. Furthermore, to enhance clinical applicability, we developed a simplified scoring system to support early bedside risk stratification and guide surgical decision-making.

Methods

Study population

Following ethical approval from the Shanxi Children’s Hospital Ethics Committee (approval No.: IRB-KYYN-2026-G009), we retrospectively reviewed pediatric patients admitted with secondary intussusception between October 2018 and October 2023. Inclusion criteria were: (1) definitive diagnosis confirmed by abdominal ultrasound, air enema, surgical exploration, or endoscopy; (2) clearly identifiable organic etiology verified by pathological or imaging evidence; and (3) complete medical documentation including clinical symptoms, treatment history, and follow-up data. Exclusion criteria were: (1) primary (idiopathic) intussusception; (2) congenital intestinal malformations or significant systemic comorbidities (e.g., Hirschsprung disease, congenital heart disease, immunodeficiency disorders); and (3) incomplete critical data.

Etiological classification and diagnostic procedures

Etiologies of secondary intussusception were grouped into three mutually exclusive categories based on pathological lead points (PLPs): Meckel’s diverticulum, IgAV, and other causes (e.g., intestinal duplication, polyps, lymphoma, enterogenous cysts, gastric volvulus, inflammatory or allergic conditions). In regression analyses, “other causes” served as the reference group; MD and IgAV were modeled as binary predictors.

Etiologic diagnoses were established through a combination of clinical features, imaging results, surgical findings, and histopathological confirmation. Among the 92 patients, 29 (31.5%) were diagnosed preoperatively based on characteristic presentations (e.g., palpable purpura in IgAV) or imaging findings (e.g., gastric volvulus). The majority (n = 56, 60.9%) were diagnosed intraoperatively and confirmed by postoperative histopathology. An additional 7 cases (7.6%) were diagnosed via colonoscopy with tissue biopsy. This multimodal diagnostic strategy ensured a high level of etiologic specificity for subsequent analysis.

Diagnostic criteria for intestinal necrosis

Intraoperative suspicion of intestinal necrosis was based on visual cues such as dusky or cyanotic bowel segments, absent peristalsis, or lack of capillary refill. Histopathological confirmation required full-thickness transmural ischemia, coagulative mucosal necrosis, and extensive hemorrhage in the muscularis propria and subserosa. In patients with IgAV-associated intussusception, differentiation was made between vasculitis-induced submucosal hemorrhage and true ischemic necrosis. Only patients who met both intraoperative and histological criteria were classified as necrotic [13].

Clinical variables

Extracted variables included age, sex, symptom duration (in days), and the presence or absence of hematochezia. Imaging reports (ultrasound or CT) were reviewed for presence of intussusception and bowel wall edema.

Statistical analysis

Statistical computations were performed using SPSS (v26.0) and R (v4.4.2). Normality of continuous variables was checked with the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. When the assumption was violated, between-group comparisons used the Mann–Whitney U test. For categorical data, we applied Pearson’s χ² test, or Fisher’s exact test when any expected cell count was < 5.

To account for small-sample bias, Firth’s logistic regression was applied to determine standalone predictors of bowel tissue death. Predictor variables included age (continuous), IgAV (binary), and Meckel’s diverticulum (binary). Interaction terms (etiology × age) were introduced to assess effect modification. Only statistically significant or clinically relevant predictors were retained in the final regression scheme. Predictive capability was assessed using area under the curve (AUC) from ROC analysis. Bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations was applied to derive robust 95% confidence intervals for odds ratios (ORs) and AUC values to ensure robustness. A bilateral p-value below 0.05 was regarded as indicating statistical significance.

Sensitivity analysis

To examine the clinical relevance of symptom duration under a low-events setting, we performed a sensitivity analysis using Firth logistic regression consistent with the primary model, adding duration as an extra main-effect covariate. Duration was entered as ln(days + 1) to accommodate right-skewness (1 df). In complementary checks, duration was also explored as a three-level categorical variable (≤ 24 h, 24–48 h, > 48 h). Discrimination (AUC) and key coefficients were compared with the primary model.

Model parsimony and clinical utility assessments

A prespecified parsimonious model (predictors: IgAV, Meckel’s diverticulum, age; no interactions) was fit with Firth logistic regression to assess robustness under low EPV. Model performance of the parsimonious and the full interaction models was compared using ROC analysis with DeLong’s test for AUC differences. Calibration was evaluated by plotting observed versus predicted risks across deciles. Decision-curve analysis (DCA) was used to quantify standardized net benefit across probability thresholds relevant to clinical triage (10–40%).

Development of the simplified risk scoring system

A parsimonious bedside risk score was derived from the final interaction model. Using stratum-specific restricted cubic splines (three nodes placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th quantiles of the distribution) and a univariate ROC scan of candidate thresholds (6/7/8 years), we observed a shallow 6–8-year plateau. Although Youden’s J was marginally higher at 8 years in the IgAV stratum (ΔJ = 0.034), the 7-year cut-point provided a more balanced trade-off between sensitivity (0.667) and specificity (0.625) and the smallest distance to the ROC upper-left corner; age alone performed poorly overall (negative Youden’s J), and showed limited value in Meckel’s. We therefore dichotomized age at < 7 vs. ≥ 7 years and retained age as a 1-point modifier in the score. Integer weights were assigned from standardized β-coefficients to preserve the relative contribution of predictors: IgAV = 3 points, MD = 2 points (mutually exclusive), and age < 7 years = 1 point. The total score ranges from 0 to 4 (maximum achievable: IgAV + age < 7 = 4).

Risk stratification and threshold determination

For bedside interpretability, the 0–4 score was mapped to three risk bands a priori: low (≤ 1) to prioritize rule-out, intermediate-risk (2–3), and rule-in (= 4) to prioritize specificity/PPV. These cut-points were informed by the single-threshold ROC/Youden analysis and the observed score–outcome distribution. We summarized discrimination by AUC for the ordinal score and reported operating characteristics for the two operational cut-points (≥ 2 to define ≥intermediate-risk vs. low-risk; and = 4 to define rule-in). Internal stability was examined with 1,000-bootstrap resampling.

Results

Incidence and risk factors for intestinal necrosis

Among 92 pediatric patients with secondary intussusception, 17 (18.5%) developed intestinal necrosis. Univariate analysis (Table 1) revealed a significant association between etiology and necrosis, with IgAV showing the highest incidence (36.0% vs. 11.9% in non-IgAV cases, p = 0.007). MD was associated with a moderately elevated necrosis rate (22.2%), though this was not found to be statistically meaningful (p = 0.148). No notable group differences emerged in relation to sex (p = 0.863), hematochezia (p = 0.305), or symptom duration (p = 0.779). Median age was slightly higher in the necrosis group (6 [IQR 3–9] years) than in the non-necrosis group (5 [IQR 2–7] years), the comparison did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.095). The age-related risk would be examined when modeled as an effect modifier of etiology in the interaction effects next step.

Main and interaction effects of etiology and age on the risk of intestinal necrosis

Firth’s logistic regression analysis demonstrated that both IgAV (OR = 7.31, p = 0.005) and MD (OR = 4.43, p = 0.045) demonstrated an independent correlation with elevated likelihood of intestinal necrosis relative to other etiologies. Age, modeled as a continuous variable, was not independently significant in the main-effects model (p = 0.400), although overall model fit was acceptable (LRT p = 0.016; Wald p < 0.001) (Table 2).

This explains why the necrosis group can appear older in aggregate (case-mix shift) even though within the IgAV stratum younger children have higher odds of necrosis (e.g., median 6.0 [5.6–7.0] vs. 7.0 [5.75–9.25] in non-necrosis). Comparative predictive performance of individual and combined models

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis of the interaction model demonstrated strong discriminatory power, with an AUC of 0.847 (95% CI: 0.784–0.905), sensitivity of 94.1%, and specificity of 62.7% (Fig. 2). In contrast, individual predictors exhibited limited diagnostic value: IgAV alone (AUC = 0.660), age (AUC = 0.630), and MD (AUC = 0.540). Interaction terms alone yielded moderate performance (AUCs 0.701–0.735). However, the full interaction model significantly outperformed all individual predictors, confirming the additive value of age–etiology interactions in refining necrosis risk estimation (Fig. 2; Table 4).

Sensitivity analysis including symptom duration

Using Firth logistic regression, we examined symptom duration as a three-level categorical variable (≤ 24 h, 24–48 h, > 48 h; n = 19/21/52) and as a continuous covariate ln(duration + 1). In the categorical model adjusted for age, sex, IgAV, and Meckel’s diverticulum, neither 24–48 h nor > 48 h differed from ≤ 24 h (OR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.12–5.52, p = 0.91; OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.22–2.79, p = 0.72, respectively); unadjusted analyses were similar. With duration modeled continuously, the estimated effects of IgAV, Meckel’s diverticulum, and their interactions with age remained directionally consistent and of similar magnitude; duration itself was not independently associated with necrosis (β=−0.59, OR ≈ 0.55, p = 0.238). Overall discrimination was essentially unchanged (AUC = 0.857; 95% CI: 0.771–0.942, vs. 0.847 in the primary model). Thus, adding duration—either categorically or continuously—did not materially alter the estimates nor supplant the etiology–age interaction in predicting necrosis.

Comparison with a parsimonious model and clinical utility

The parsimonious model (IgAV + MD + age) achieved an AUC of 0.736 (95% CI: 0.631–0.831), whereas the full interaction model yielded an AUC of 0.847 (95% CI: 0.784–0.905). DeLong’s test indicated a significant improvement (ΔAUC = 0.111, 95% CI: 0.010–0.213, p = 0.031). Calibration plots showed closer agreement between predicted and observed risks for the full interaction model. In DCA, the interaction model provided greater net benefit than the parsimonious model across thresholds of 10–40%, supporting better clinical usefulness for perioperative decision-making.

Development and validation of a simplified risk scoring tool

To facilitate clinical translation, a simplified score was derived from the interaction model using three predictors: IgAV (3 points), MD (2 points), and age (< 7 years = 1 point; ≥ 7 years = 0 points), yielding a 0–4 range. The score showed moderate discrimination (AUC 0.735; 95%CI, 0.592–0.826) (Fig. 3). Using a three-band scheme, necrosis risk was 5% (NPV 95%) for scores ≤ 1, 22.5% (intermediate-risk) for scores 2–3, and 50% (PPV 50%, specificity 92%) for score = 4, at the observed prevalence of 18.5%. Operating characteristics at the two decision boundaries—≥2 (defining ≥intermediate-risk vs. low-risk) and = 4 (rule-in)—are shown in Tables 5 and 6. For preoperative use under etiologic uncertainty, the MD term is set to 0; accordingly, the score is intended as adjunctive triage support at two decision points—rule-out at ≤ 1 and rule-in at = 4—rather than a stand-alone directive. The final scoring formula was: score = 3×IgAV + 2×MD + Age(< 7y).

Discussion

Background and study rationale

Secondary intussusception in children is frequently caused by anatomical abnormalities that serve as pathological lead points (PLPs), compressing mesenteric vessels and potentially leading to intestinal ischemia, necrosis, or perforation if not promptly managed [2, 14]. Although non-operative reduction remains the standard initial approach, cases with severe bowel wall edema or early necrosis tend to respond poorly and are more likely to experience iatrogenic perforation during pneumatic or hydrostatic procedures [15–17]. These challenges highlight the need for accurate preoperative risk assessment. However, effective clinical tools to identify necrosis-prone cases before surgery remain limited [3, 8].

Main findings and modeling strategy

In response to this gap, we retrospectively analyzed 92 pediatric cases of secondary intussusception to identify demographic and etiological predictors of intestinal necrosis and assess their interaction with age. Using Firth logistic regression, we identified IgAV and MD as independent risk factors, with age functioning as a significant effect modifier. In light of the present results, an interaction model (AUC = 0.847) and a simplified risk score were established to support early risk stratification in practice.

Age as a modifier of ischemic susceptibility

Immature mesenteric perfusion and fragile intestinal wall structure in younger children may increase susceptibility to ischemic insult [8]. This vulnerability may relate to underdeveloped vascular and inflammatory responses in early childhood [8]. While age alone was not statistically significant in univariate testing, its interaction with IgAV and MD revealed that younger age exacerbated the risk of necrosis in these specific subgroups. These findings help reconcile inconsistencies in earlier literature, where age or etiology were often examined in isolation [3, 11, 12]. At the whole-cohort level, the necrosis group skews older because it is enriched for etiologies—most notably IgAV—that themselves occur at older ages; this case-mix shift elevates the group-wise medians. In the multivariable model, however, the significant age×etiology terms isolate the within-etiology effect: conditional on IgAV (and to a lesser extent MD), each year of age corresponds to a lower odds of necrosis (IgAV×Age OR 0.416; MD×Age OR 0.588). Hence, the statement “younger age is higher risk” applies to within-etiology risk, not to unstratified cohort summaries.

IgAV as a risk-enhancing factor in younger children

IgAV-associated intussusception is mechanistically linked to immune complex deposition in small-caliber mesenteric vessels, triggering vasculitis, increasing vascular permeability, and leading to submucosal edema and hemorrhage [18, 19]. These changes predispose the affected bowel to telescoping and subsequent ischemia. Once intussusception occurs, venous congestion and arterial compromise further accelerate necrosis. Factor XIII deficiency has also been implicated in impaired hemostasis and delayed mucosal repair [18]. Our study confirmed that IgAV significantly increases necrosis risk, particularly in younger patients whose immune and vascular systems are still underdeveloped. This pattern is consistent with the typical age distribution of IgAV, which peaks between 5 and 10 years of age [12, 15, 19]. These findings emphasize the necessity of early recognition and tailored perioperative management in younger IgAV patients presenting with intussusception.

Distinct ischemic patterns in Meckel’s Diverticulum

Meckel’s Diverticulum (MD), resulting from incomplete closure of the vitelline duct, represents the commonest developmental anomaly of the digestive tract. In 45%–80% of MD cases, ectopic tissue—most commonly gastric mucosa—can produce acid, which may result in ulceration, inflammation, or even intestinal ischemia and intussusception [20–22]. Prior studies suggest that ectopic mucosal activity is age-dependent, becoming more clinically significant in older children [22, 23]. Younger children, by contrast, more often experience mechanical complications such as fibrous bands, which can cause early-onset intussusception without necessarily increasing ischemia risk. This is consistent with the known bimodal age distribution of MD, with congenital-type presentations in infancy and inflammatory-type presentations in later childhood [20–22].

Although our univariate analysis failed to demonstrate a statistically significant association between MD and necrosis (22.2% vs. 16.9%, p = 0.148), we observed a non-negligible trend toward elevated risk. Given the well-established pathological mechanisms and the potential for age-related modulation, we retained MD in our multivariable and interaction models. Notably, our final model confirmed a significant age–MD interaction (OR = 0.588, p = 0.023), indicating that the necrosis risk associated with MD decreases as age increases. However, this age-dependent association should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Because MD is seldom identified preoperatively—and all MD-related necrosis in our series was diagnosed intraoperatively—its current preoperative decision value is limited. Future studies integrating imaging markers (e.g., focused sonographic signs) or biomarker panels may improve preoperative recognition and better define MD-related risk.

Hematochezia as a potentially protective indicator

In our cohort, none of the Children with MD who presented with hematochezia developed intestinal necrosis (0.00%). Although this observation was not statistically significant in the full cohort (p = 0.305) and was not retained in multivariate analysis, it may reflect a mechanistic distinction in the bleeding profile of MD. Specifically, bleeding in MD is generally triggered through acid production secretion by heterotopic gastric tissue, which occurs before the onset of obstruction or ischemia and often prompts early diagnosis [24]. In contrast, gastrointestinal bleeding in IgAV is a secondary event associated with immune complex–mediated small-vessel vasculitis, occurring concurrently with or following mucosal ischemia. These temporal and mechanistic distinctions may partially explain why bleeding in MD could serve as a protective indicator against necrosis, in contrast to IgAV-related bleeding [25].

Recognizing high-risk etiologies preoperatively

While definitive diagnosis of pathological lead points often requires surgical exploration and histopathology, our findings suggest that a subset of high-risk etiologies may be identifiable preoperatively based on clinical and imaging clues. In this cohort, 31.5% (29/92) of patients were diagnosed with a definitive etiology prior to surgery—primarily via characteristic signs such as palpable purpura in IgAV or typical features detected by colonoscopy or imaging (e.g., gastric volvulus) [6, 12, 19, 22]. MD, though typically confirmed intraoperatively, also presented several suggestive preoperative features in retrospective analysis, including fixed ileal loops, localized intussusception in the distal ileum, blind-end signs on ultrasound, and recurrent intussusception episodes. While these features are not diagnostic, they may serve as indirect markers warranting further evaluation. Previous studies have shown that recurrent or atypical intussusception is more likely associated with pathological lead points, including MD [22, 26]. Collectively, these findings highlight that a proportion of high-risk cases can be clinically suspected prior to surgery, especially when integrating disease-specific signs and radiological patterns. Incorporating such indicators into future predictive models may help translate statistical associations into practical tools for early triage and perioperative planning.

Symptom duration: clinical relevance and findings in this cohort

Symptom duration is widely regarded as clinically meaningful in intussusception, being linked to lower success rates of non-operative reduction and a higher likelihood of ischemic injury in prior reports [27]. In our cohort, however, symptom duration did not show an independent association with intestinal necrosis once etiology and age were accounted for. In sensitivity analyses mirroring the primary modeling strategy (Firth regression), duration—evaluated both categorically (≤ 24 h, 24–48 h, > 48 h; n = 19/21/52) and as ln(duration + 1)—showed no independent effect after adjustment (24–48 h vs. ≤ 24 h: OR = 0.81, 95% CI 0.12–5.52, p = 0.91; >48 h vs. ≤ 24 h: OR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.22–2.79, p = 0.72; continuous term β=−0.59, OR ≈ 0.55, p = 0.238). Model discrimination was essentially unchanged (AUC = 0.857 vs. 0.847), indicating that duration did not supplant the etiology–age interaction. These negative findings may reflect (i) confounding/mediation by etiology-specific pathophysiology and age (e.g., IgAV-related microvascular injury in younger children), (ii) measurement error in onset timing (parental recall, intermittent symptoms), and (iii) limited events-per-variable in a single-center dataset. We therefore interpret duration as clinically important but not independently discriminative beyond etiology and age in this sample. Future studies should capture standardized symptom timestamps and apply time-to-event or landmark/dynamic modeling to clarify how duration interacts with evolving clinical, laboratory, and imaging features to influence ischemic risk.

The role of laboratory biomarkers in early identification

In addition to clinical-demographic predictors, increasing evidence suggests that laboratory biomarkers may aid in the early detection of intestinal ischemia and necrosis. Inflammatory indices—such as C-reactive protein (CRP), lymphocyte-to-CRP ratio (LCR), and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR)—have been associated with systemic inflammation and tissue hypoperfusion in necrotic cases [6, 8, 10]. Coagulation-related markers, including D-dimer and fibrin degradation products, may reflect mesenteric microvascular compromise. Moreover, enterocyte-specific indicators such as intestinal fatty acid–binding protein (I-FABP) and D-lactate offer improved specificity for mucosal injury [28–30]. Procalcitonin (PCT), widely used to assess systemic inflammatory severity in critical illness, has been linked to poor outcomes in abdominal sepsis when persistently elevated [31]. Although our retrospective design limited biomarker inclusion, these parameters warrant prospective validation in future predictive models.

Model development and clinical utility

Based on our findings, we developed a regression-based prediction model incorporating etiology, age, and their interaction. This model demonstrated good discriminative performance (AUC = 0.847), supporting its utility in preoperative risk assessment. Future work should aim to integrate dynamic variables—such as symptom trajectory and serial imaging—to improve model robustness and facilitate individualized clinical decision-making in secondary pediatric intussusception. Although some 95% CIs are wide due to the small number of necrosis events, model-level evidence supports practical utility: the interaction model demonstrated higher discrimination than a parsimonious alternative (significant ΔAUC), better calibration, and higher net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities in decision-curve analysis. We therefore present the tool as exploratory yet clinically informative, pending multicenter external validation to further stabilize interval estimates.

Simplified risk score for bedside use

To facilitate clinical translation, we derived a simplified 0–4 score (IgAV = 3, MD = 2, age < 7 = 1). Discrimination was moderate (AUC 0.735; 95% CI 0.592–0.826). We deploy the score in three bands (≤ 1 rule-out; 2–3 intermediate-risk; =4 rule-in). At the lower boundary (≥ 2 vs. ≤ 1) sensitivity was 88.2% with NPV 95.0%; at the upper boundary (= 4 vs. ≤ 3) specificity was 92.0% and PPV 50.0% (Fig. 3; Table 6), at the observed prevalence of 18.5%. By design, its clinical use is limited to these two preoperative decision points and covered 52/92 (56.5%) children in our cohort (≤ 1: 40/92; =4: 12/92); scores 2–3 do not direct management but prompt adjunctive testing/observation. Thus, the score provides adjunctive triage support, not a stand-alone directive, and aligns with prior pediatric necrosis-prediction work, underscoring the need for multicenter validation [6, 7, 10, 28–31].

Conclusion

This study identified the combination of etiology and age—particularly IgAV in younger children—as key driver of intestinal necrosis in pediatric secondary intussusception. We provide a parsimonious interaction-based model and a bedside score with good rule-out performance for low-risk patients; both are exploratory and require multicenter validation before clinical adoption.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The small number of necrosis cases (n = 17) limited the events-per-variable ratio and may affect model stability despite the use of Firth regression and bootstrap validation. The single-center retrospective design also restricts generalizability. Although MD is typically diagnosed intraoperatively, our findings suggest that younger age amplifies its necrosis risk. While this interaction currently lacks direct preoperative applicability, it offers mechanistic insight and highlights the need for future research to incorporate imaging or biomarker-based predictors.

Given these limitations, the models should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Nonetheless, they offer a foundational framework for multicenter validation and future model refinement. Notably, nearly one-third of patients in our cohort presented with preoperative clues suggestive of underlying pathological lead points, reinforcing the clinical value of integrating etiology-specific signs into early diagnostic pathways.

In summary, this study underscores the prognostic value of age–etiology interaction in predicting intestinal necrosis. The proposed scoring system provides a practical and accurate tool for early risk stratification, especially in identifying low-risk patients who may benefit from conservative management or deferred surgery.
