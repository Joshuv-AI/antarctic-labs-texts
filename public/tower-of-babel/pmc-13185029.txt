Efficacy of nebulised dexmedetomidine versus aminophylline for treatment of postdural puncture headache in patients undergoing subarachnoid block posted for caesarean section: a double-blind, randomised controlled study
Authors: R K V Kavya, Neeraj Kumar, Abhyuday Kumar, Monika Anant, Alok Ranjan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Background Postdural puncture headache (PDPH) following spinal anaesthesia significantly affects postpartum recovery. We evaluated the efficacy of nebulised dexmedetomidine and aminophylline in women with moderate to severe PDPH after caesarean delivery.MethodsIn this single-centre, double-blind, randomised controlled trial, 96 postpartum women, with American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status II, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), with PDPH (VAS>4; Lybecker>2) after subarachnoid block were allocated to receive nebulised dexmedetomidine (1 µg/kg), aminophylline (1.5 mg/kg) or saline placebo. All participants received standardised conservative treatment and remained supine for 72 hours. Nebulisation was administered two times per day for ≤72 hours. The primary endpoint was change in VAS pain score at 24 hours. Secondary endpoints included VAS and Lybecker scores at 48 hours and 72 hours, Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC) and adverse events.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable between groups. At 24 hours, median VAS was significantly lower with dexmedetomidine (3 (3–4)) and aminophylline (4 (3–6)) compared with control (6 (5.5–6)) (p<0.001). Pain reduction persisted at 48 hours and 72 hours, with dexmedetomidine producing the fastest resolution. Lybecker scores improved earlier in the treatment groups (p<0.001). PGIC showed ‘very much improved’ in 100% (dexmedetomidine), 53.1% (aminophylline) and 0% (control) (p<0.001). Adverse events were mild and infrequent, with cough and nausea occurring only in the aminophylline group.ConclusionsNebulised dexmedetomidine and aminophylline provided effective, well-tolerated, non-invasive treatment for PDPH. Dexmedetomidine produced the greatest and earliest improvement. These therapies may represent practical alternatives for PDPH management in postpartum patients; larger multicentre trials allowing early mobilisation are warranted.Trial registration numberClinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI/2024/01/061322; date of prospective trial registration: 10 January 2024, date of first patient enrolment: 15 January 2024, https://ctri.nic.in/Clinicaltrials/login.php).Keywords: OBSTETRICS, HEADACHE, PAIN, Self-Care

Introduction

Postdural puncture headache (PDPH) is one of the most common and distressing complications following spinal or epidural anaesthesia, particularly in obstetric patients undergoing caesarean delivery. Despite advances in needle design and the use of smaller-gauge pencil-point spinal needles, PDPH continues to affect 0.5–2% of parturients worldwide following neuraxial anaesthesia.1 However, the incidence of PDPH after spinal anaesthesia performed with a Quincke cutting needle varies from 3–25% with a 25-G needle.2 Globally, PDPH represents a significant burden, affecting nearly 1.5 million patients annually, with higher incidence rates in low- and middle-income countries, where spinal anaesthesia remains the preferred technique for caesarean section.3 4 In addition to its frequency, PDPH has a significant impact on maternal well-being. It can impair breastfeeding, hinder maternal-neonatal bonding, delay ambulation and prolong hospital stay, leading to increased healthcare costs and patient dissatisfaction.4

There is significant variation in how different institutions treat and prevent PDPH, largely due to the lack of standardised guidelines and protocols. This results in diverse management approaches across healthcare centres.5 The International Headache Society has defined PDPH as a headache that develops within 5 days of dural puncture and resolves spontaneously within 1 week or within 48 hours after effective treatment of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak.4 90% of PDPHs occur within 3 days of the procedure, and 66% start in the first 48 hours.6 Headache typically manifests as dull, throbbing or pressure-like pain in the frontal or occipital region that worsens in an upright position and improves when lying down. Associated symptoms, such as neck stiffness, photophobia, nausea, vomiting, tinnitus and visual disturbances, are often present.7 Non-invasive pharmacological alternatives are increasingly being explored to reduce symptom severity and minimise epidural blood patch (EBP) requirements.

Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective α₂-adrenergic agonist, provides sedative and analgesic effects primarily via inhibition of norepinephrine release and induces cerebral vasoconstriction. When delivered by nebulisation, it achieves systemic absorption with favourable patient tolerance and minimal haemodynamic disturbances.8 Aminophylline, a methylxanthine derivative, promotes CSF production and causes cerebral vasoconstriction through adenosine receptor antagonism, mechanisms that are beneficial in alleviating PDPH.9 Although intravenous aminophylline has demonstrated efficacy, nebulised administration remains unexplored, offering a potential non-invasive route with fewer systemic effects.

Based on these pharmacological properties, we hypothesised that both nebulised dexmedetomidine and aminophylline would be more effective than standard conservative therapy in reducing PDPH severity and improving patient-reported outcomes. Therefore, this double-blind, randomised controlled trial aimed to evaluate and compare the efficacy of nebulised dexmedetomidine and nebulised aminophylline in managing PDPH among post-caesarean women who underwent subarachnoid block.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, single-centre, double-blind, randomised controlled trial was conducted at the All-India Institute of Medical Sciences Patna, Bihar, India, between January 2024 and March 2025 (14 months). 96 postpartum female participants aged 21–40 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA-PS) II (normal pregnancy is designated as ASA II according to current ASA guidelines) and body mass index (BMI) <35 kg/m² who developed PDPH following caesarean section under subarachnoid block were included.

The exclusion criteria were hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, cardiac arrhythmia, a history of chronic headache or migraine, convulsions, cerebrovascular disease and refusal to participate. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in their native languages. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Ref. No. AIIMS/Pat/IEC/PGTh/Jan23/63; dated 05 Dec 2023) and registered with the Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI/2024/01/061322; date of prospective trial registration: 10 January 2024, date of first patient enrolment: 15 January 2024, https://ctri.nic.in/Clinicaltrials/login.php). Aminophylline is Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved for intravenous/oral administration in asthma treatment; nebulised use for PDPH represents off-label, novel delivery, analogous to nebulised dexmedetomidine (also off-label). The study was conducted as an investigator-initiated trial with Institutional Ethics Committee approval.10 11 PDPH diagnosis was based on the International Classification of Headache Disorders-III criteria4 12 (online supplemental table S1). Participants were actively screened for PDPH during their inpatient stay by structured follow-up, and only those with moderate-to-severe PDPH with a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) >4 and Lybecker classification13 >2 were enrolled in our study group. Online supplemental table S2 summarises the Lybecker classification.

Randomisation and blinding

Study participants were randomly allocated (1:1:1) into three groups: dexmedetomidine (group D), aminophylline (group A) and control (group C) using computer-generated block randomisation (block size=6) via Sealed Envelope software. Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes opened immediately before the intervention.

The study was double-blinded: participants were blinded to groups D, A and C assignments, and all study drugs were prepared by nursing personnel not otherwise involved in this study. The investigator responsible for outcome assessment and data analysis remained blinded to the allocation throughout the study.

Intervention protocol

All participants in the study were provided with standard-of-care treatment. Standardised supine bed rest for up to 72 hours was employed to reduce postural variability during serial outcome assessments and to enhance case ascertainment in patients with moderate to severe PDPH. This approach was instituted as part of our institutional protocol for patient monitoring rather than as a primary therapeutic measure. Early mobilisation was permitted once treatment response criteria were met (VAS<3 and Lybecker<2), provided it was clinically appropriate.

Oral meals or fluids given ad libitum (as desired) alongside standardised intravenous fluids at 30 mL/kg/day as a balanced approach to fluid therapy and oral paracetamol (500 mg) with caffeine (25 mg) every 8 hours. A 25-G Quincke spinal (cutting) needle was used for the subarachnoid block as per institutional availability and documented anaesthesia records. Owing to the involvement of multiple operators and variability in procedural documentation, more granular needle-related details could not be consistently verified and were therefore not included in the further study analysis. Patients were actively followed for PDPH during their hospital stay and by telephonic screening on postoperative day 2 and day 5 and identified symptomatic patients for inperson assessment/intervention; a standard postdischarge protocol was given after the 48–72-hour stay14 at our institute. Once the diagnosis of PDPH was made, study participants were randomly allocated to receive nebulised treatment immediately; nebulisation was delivered two times per day and continued for up to 72 hours or until both VAS<3 and Lybecker<2 were achieved.

Participants were then assigned to one of three intervention groups (n=32 per group):

Group D: 1 μg/kg dexmedetomidine diluted in 4 mL 0.9% saline was nebulised.

Group A: nebulised 1.5 mg/kg aminophylline diluted to a total volume of 4 mL.

Group C: nebulised 4 mL 0.9% saline.

All drugs were administered using a mesh nebuliser (Model 404, Nureca, Shenzhen Combei Technology, Guangdong, China).

Outcome measures

The main endpoint was to assess any change in VAS-measured pain intensity from baseline to 24 hours after starting nebulisation therapy. The secondary endpoints included serial VAS and Lybecker score measurements at 48 hours and 72 hours, respectively. This classification was assessed through supervised posture challenge: patients attempted bedside sit-to-stand transition (30° head elevation to upright) under direct observation. Scoring reflected maximum tolerated posture and functional capacity at each time point (24 hours, 48 hours and 72 hours), despite standardised rest protocol:

Score 1: patient not bedridden (if study patient tolerates sitting/standing ≥6 hours).

Score 2: bedridden most of day (as ≥6-hour supine was required).

Score 3: bedridden all day.

We also assessed the Patient Global Impression of Change based on a seven-point Likert scale; these seven points were further clinically classified into three meaningful categories (very much improved=1; much improved=2; minimally/no improvement=3–7) for tabular presentation. The haemodynamic parameters (systolic arterial pressure) during and after nebulisation, improvement in neck stiffness and incidence of adverse events were the other secondary outcomes. Prespecified adverse events were bradycardia (<50 beats per minute), hypotension (if ≥20% decrease in systolic blood pressure (SBP) from baseline), sedation, cough and unpleasant taste; unsolicited events (such as nausea) were also documented during follow-up.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated from a previous study by Mowafy et al,1 which reported mean (± SD) 24 hours VAS scores of 5.81±3.48 (control) and 3.52±2.46 (dexmedetomidine). The study was powered to detect differences between active treatment and control; comparisons between the two active interventions were exploratory and hypothesis-generating. With α=0.05 and 80% power, 28 participants in each group were required. To account for a 10% dropout rate, 32 participants were enrolled per group (total n=96). Data were analysed using Jamovi software (V.2.6.26). Continuous variables are expressed as mean±SD or median with IQR, depending on the distribution verified by Q-Q plots. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Normally distributed data were compared using one-way ANOVA, Fisher’s (Post-hoc) test and non-parametric group comparisons were reported using Kruskal-Wallis ε² and pairwise Cliff’s δ; pairwise median differences were estimated using Hodges-Lehmann estimators and 95% CIs. We performed post-hoc pairwise comparisons using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction; similar results were obtained using the Dwass-Steel-Critchlow-Fligner (DSCF) method. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used for adjusting baseline BMI imbalance. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

Out of the 1595 pregnant mothers, 133 were assessed for eligibility. However, 37 participants were excluded (not meeting criteria=28; declined to participate=9). 96 participants were randomly assigned to three equal study groups (D, A and C), each comprising 32 patients (figure 1: Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 flow diagram). Baseline demographic and intraoperative characteristics were comparable across groups, except for a modest difference in BMI (p=0.009). The mean±SD age, BMI, preoperative haemoglobin level, number of spinal puncture attempts, estimated blood loss and interval between lumbar puncture and PDPH onset are summarised in table 1. None of these variables, except for BMI, showed statistically significant intergroup variation, confirming overall baseline comparability. Baseline BMI differed significantly (p=0.009), but all study values were within the normal range. Although the published literature identifies lower BMI as a known risk factor for PDPH, our study did not find any such association. The ANCOVA, adjusted for baseline BMI imbalance, confirmed treatment effects were independent of this covariate (group F=13.6, group p<0.001; BMI p=0.222). The post-hoc assessment between groups A and C (p=0.0003) and D and C (p<0.001) showed significant differences. Online supplemental table S3 shows baseline BMI as covariate based on ANCOVA.

Pain intensity (VAS scores)

Baseline VAS scores were comparable across the groups (p=0.459). At 24 hours, the median (IQR) VAS scores were 3 (3–4) in group D, 4 (3–6) in group A and 6 (5.5–6) in group C (p<0.001). The mean VAS scores showed a significant reduction at 24 hours, 48 hours and 72 hours post intervention in all groups (p<0.001). Groups D and A exhibited a faster decline in pain intensity than group C, as shown in figure 2. The Kruskal-Wallis test demonstrated a large effect (ε² = 0.38), indicating that 38% of the variance in pain scores was explained by the treatment group. Post-hoc DSCF comparisons confirmed significantly lower VAS scores in group D than in groups A (p=0.009) and C (p<0.001) and in group A than in group C (p=0.001). At 48 hours, the median (IQR) VAS scores were 0 (0–0) in group D, 0 (0–3) in group A and 3 (3–4.5) in group C (p<0.001; ε² = 0.47). Pairwise analysis again showed significant differences between groups D and A (p=0.002), A and C (p<0.001) and D and C (p<0.001). By 72 hours, pain had largely resolved in groups A and B (0 (0–0)), whereas residual pain persisted in group C (3 (3–3)), maintaining a significant group difference (p<0.001; ε² = 0.45). Table 2 shows the VAS and Lybecker score distributions among the three study groups. Pairwise DSCF testing confirmed persistent differences between groups D and C and A and C (both p<0.001), whereas groups D and A no longer differed (p=0.577). Online supplemental table S4 shows the DSCF pairwise comparisons of VAS scores between the study groups. These results indicate the statistical and clinically meaningful superiority of nebulised dexmedetomidine for pain relief.

Functional recovery (Lybecker score)

Baseline Lybecker scores were similar among the groups (3 (2–3); p=0.866). At 24 hours, the median (IQR) scores were 1 (1–1) in group D, 1.5 (1–2) in group A, and 2 (2–2) in group C (p<0.001). The mean Lybecker classification scores declined progressively across all groups, with a significantly faster improvement in groups D and A than in group C (p<0.05; figure 3). The overall Kruskal-Wallis effect was large (ε=0.33). Post-hoc DSCF analysis showed significantly better postural tolerance in group D than in groups A (p=0.041) and C (p=0.001) and between groups A and C (p=0.001). At 48 hours, most participants in groups D and A achieved full upright tolerance (1 (1–1)), whereas group C continued to experience postural limitations (1.5 (1–2); p<0.001; ε² = 0.28). Patient Global Impression of Change

All participants in group D rated their improvement as very much improved (100%), compared with 53.1% in group A and none in group C (p<0.001, χ² = value). In group A, 37.5% reported much improvement and 9.4% reported minimal improvement, whereas all participants in group C (100%) rated improvement as minimal (table 3). χ2 analysis demonstrated a highly significant association between treatment group D and perceived recovery (p<0.001). The magnitude of the association was large (Cramer’s V=0.82), indicating a strong treatment effect. The absolute difference in very much improved responses between groups D and A was 46.9% (95% CI 29.2 to 64.6), corresponding to a relative risk of 1.88 (95% CI 1.33 to 2.65). None of the participants required EBP during the 72 hours observation period. Because all participants were kept on standardised bed rest for 72 hours, the study could not determine whether EBP would be required after resumption of activity. Our findings show accelerated symptom resolution and improved postural tolerance, outcomes that are clinically relevant for earlier ambulation and may reduce subsequent EBP demand, which requires confirmation in studies allowing early mobilisation. Adverse events were infrequent and mild across all groups, as shown in table 4. Two participants (2.1%) in group A experienced transient cough during nebulisation, and one (1%) reported mild nausea that resolved spontaneously without treatment. The overall incidence of adverse events did not differ significantly among the groups (p=0.402, Fisher’s exact test). Baseline haemodynamic, SBP parameters were comparable across the three study groups (p=0.56). Although statistically significant differences were observed before and after treatment (p<0.001), the absolute differences between the groups were minimal and remained within normal physiological limits, indicating that nebulised dexmedetomidine and aminophylline did not produce clinically significant haemodynamic alterations. No haemodynamic instability, sedation or respiratory compromise was reported during or after nebulisation. These findings confirm the favourable safety profile and tolerability of nebulised dexmedetomidine and aminophylline in this clinical setting.

Discussion

In this double-blind randomised controlled trial, we evaluated and compared the efficacy of nebulised dexmedetomidine and aminophylline with standard conservative therapy in the management of PDPH following spinal anaesthesia for caesarean sections. The screening pool included 1595 caesarean deliveries; of these, 133 were assessed for PDPH, and 96 met the inclusion criteria (VAS>4 and Lybecker>2). Our study found a PDPH incidence rate of 8.3%, whereas rates with a 25-G Quincke needle can reach 25%. The results should not be considered representative of the overall incidence at our institution. Active surveillance, inclusion of only moderate-to-severe cases, and the use of a cutting spinal needle likely contributed to the higher case identification compared with routine clinical reporting.

Our findings demonstrate that both nebulised dexmedetomidine and aminophylline significantly reduced headache intensity and improved functional recovery, as evidenced by reductions in the VAS and Lybecker scores over 72 hours. Dexmedetomidine produced a faster and more sustained decline in pain scores, with a higher proportion of patients reporting marked improvement on the seven-point Likert scale. Although the EBP requirement was not assessed as an outcome, earlier functional recovery and improved postural tolerance represent clinically meaningful endpoints for postpartum care and mobilisation. This study was not designed to evaluate the avoidance of EBP. However, the absence of EBP during the observation period reflects standardised conservative management and bed rest rather than clinically meaningful prevention of EBP after mobilisation. We used an FDA and CE-approved Mesh Nebulizer (Mesh Nebulizer 404, Nureca Limited, Shenzhen Combei Technology, Guangdong, China) to treat PDPH in all three groups. The observed benefit of nebulised dexmedetomidine in our study aligns with the prior literature, suggesting that α₂-adrenergic agonists reduce intracranial blood flow and cerebral vasodilation, thereby mitigating the compensatory vasodilation associated with CSF hypotension.1 Dexmedetomidine’s central sympatholytic action and its inhibitory effects on nociceptive neurotransmission further contribute to its analgesic properties.3 Nebulisation provides systemic bioavailability while avoiding invasive routes and minimising haemodynamic instability, which is a notable advantage in the postpartum population. This is first study trial of nebulised aminophylline for treatment of PDPH. The dose of 1.5 mg/kg diluted to 4 mL based on previous intravenous studies that showed that this weight-based dosing was effective for preventing and treating PDPH.15 The dosing was adapted for nebulisation to test a non-invasive method while aiming for similar systemic exposure. Since there have been no prior trials using nebulised aminophylline for PDPH, this approach is exploratory; however, pharmacokinetic data indicate that nebulised administration can produce plasma levels comparable to intravenous use in bronchodilation cases.16 This research addresses the unmet need for novel methods of delivering adenosine antagonists. Similarly, aminophylline demonstrated a meaningful reduction in PDPH severity. The efficacy of nebulised agents in PDPH may be explained by their potential for systemic and central nervous system delivery via pulmonary absorption. The pulmonary alveolar-capillary network provides a large surface area and rich vascularity, allowing the rapid systemic uptake of lipophilic drugs such as dexmedetomidine. Once in the systemic circulation, dexmedetomidine crosses the blood-brain barrier owing to its high lipid solubility and exerts central sympatholytic and analgesic effects.9 17 Aminophylline may enhance CSF dynamics through adenosine receptor antagonism and phosphodiesterase inhibition, promoting mild cerebral vasoconstriction and restoring intracranial pressure balance.4 Reza Barati-Boldaji et al8 conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis observed significant reduction in the pain score following aminophylline/theophylline treatment (SMD −1.67; 95% CI −2.28 to −1.05; p<0.001, I2 84.7%; p<0.001).

The non-invasive pulmonary route represents a plausible mechanism for their beneficial effects in PDPH. Although EBP remains the gold standard, it is invasive and carries risks such as infection, back pain and neurological complications.5 No participants required EBP during the 72 hours observation period. As all participants remained on standardised bed rest, the absence of EBP cannot be interpreted as permanent avoidance of EBP after mobilisation. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as evidence of accelerated symptom resolution and improved postural tolerance during the acute conservative management period. Importantly, early pain resolution and Lybecker improvement in the intervention groups indicate readiness for ambulation earlier than the control group, a clinically meaningful endpoint that could reduce length of stay and EBP once posture resumes. In clinical practice, pain relief sufficient to permit safe ambulation is the therapeutic goal and the significantly faster reduction in VAS and Lybecker scores in the intervention groups suggests that both nebulised dexmedetomidine and aminophylline could support earlier mobilisation once routine activity is resumed. Standard PDPH treatments (bed rest, hydration, caffeine) offer transient relief, high EBP need (invasive risks) and limited efficacy for moderate-to-severe cases.14 18

In our study, patients received oral meals/fluids ad libitum alongside standardised intravenous fluids (30 mL/kg/day)14; we found no intergroup differences in total fluids, the doses (paracetamol 1.5 g/day, caffeine 75 mg/day) were adjunctive symptomatic relief per ASAPS guidelines, prioritising breastfeeding safety (caffeine <200–300 mg/day to avoid neonatal irritability)19 20; on using PDPH doses for caffeine 300–500 mg may possess added risk in our control group, and provided doses were ethically sufficient as primary endpoint targets add-on therapy efficacy. Nebulised aminophylline was chosen for non-invasive adenosine antagonism (similar to caffeine but sustained), building on intravenous efficacy and nebulised dexmedetomidine prior success.1 18 It fills the gap in nebulised therapies, reducing EBP reliance.21 Aminophylline independently blocks adenosine at tested doses; while low-dose caffeine adds to baseline care, aminophylline outperforms it based on previous intravenous studies. Full-dose caffeine may have similar effects, but there is a lack of evidence regarding its efficacy in nebulised PDPH evidence.16 Furthermore, haemodynamic parameters remained stable across the groups. The nebulised delivery of dexmedetomidine and aminophylline offers a favourable pharmacokinetic profile, providing gradual systemic absorption and avoiding haemodynamic instability associated with intravenous administration. The strengths of our study include a double-blind randomised design, clearly defined clinical outcome measures (VAS, Lybecker and Likert scales) and adherence to the CONSORT standards. The simplicity and low cost of nebulisation make these interventions particularly attractive for use in low- and middle-income countries, where invasive procedures and advanced monitoring may not be readily available. This study had some limitations. It was a single-centre trial with a modest sample size, absence of plasma pharmacokinetic measurements, short follow-up duration and standardised 72-hour bed rest, which restricts generalisability to settings where early mobilisation is practised. Operator-dependent variability in the spinal technique could not be adjusted for due to incomplete procedural documentation. The original sample size calculation was based on detecting a difference vs placebo (group D vs group C). We acknowledge that the study was not primarily powered for direct (group D vs group A) non-inferiority testing; however, the observed effect sizes support clinically meaningful differences and provide estimates for future clinically meaningful trials. All participants received paracetamol with caffeine as baseline therapy; because caffeine and aminophylline share adenosine antagonism, this may have attenuated the between-group differences. Future multicentric trials with larger cohorts, pharmacokinetic analyses and standardised needle documentation are warranted to validate these findings and optimise dosing strategies.

Conclusion

Nebulised dexmedetomidine and aminophylline were associated with faster reduction in pain intensity and postural limitation compared with conservative therapy alone in postpartum patients with moderate-to-severe PDPH. Dexmedetomidine showed a more rapid and consistent improvement in patient-reported outcomes. Both interventions were well tolerated by the patients. These findings support further evaluation of nebulised therapies as non-invasive adjuncts for PDPH symptom control, particularly in settings where access to EBP is limited.
