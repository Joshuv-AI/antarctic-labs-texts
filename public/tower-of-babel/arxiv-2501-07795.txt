Black-box Optimization with Simultaneous Statistical Inference for Optimal Performance
Teng Lian, Jian-Qiang Hu, Yuhang Wu, Zeyu Zheng

Abstract
Black-box optimization is often encountered for decision-making in complex systems management, where the knowledge of system is limited. Under these circumstances, it is essential to balance the utilization of new information with computational efficiency. In practice, decision-makers often face the dual tasks of optimization and statistical inference for the optimal performance, in order to achieve it with a high reliability. Our goal is to address the dual tasks in an online fashion. Wu et al. (2022b) point out that the sample average of performance estimates generated by the optimization algorithm needs not to admit a central limit theorem (CLT). We propose an algorithm that not only tackles this issue, but also provides an online consistent estimator for the variance of the performance. Furthermore, we characterize the convergence rate of the coverage probabilities of the asymptotic confidence intervals. Keywords: Black-box optimization, Stochastic approximation, Statistical inference, Convergence rate.



1
Introduction

Black-box, according to
Audet and Hare (2017)
, is defined as “
any process that when provided an input, returns an output, but the inner workings of the process are not analytically available
.”

Golovin et al. (2017)
states that “
any sufficiently complex system acts as a black-box when it becomes easier to experiment with than to understand
.” Hence, black-box optimization serves as a highly valuable tool for complex systems management, as an appropriate mathematical model for the behavior of a complex system may be unknown, imprecise, or computationally expensive to evaluate. Besides, quantifying the solution quality (which will be rigorously defined later) is a critical task for reliability assessment, of which a standard (offline) approach is statistical inference based on Monte Carlo simulation. Hereafter, black-box optimization with statistical inference for the optimal performance will be referred to as the
dual tasks of optimization and statistical inference
.

In recent years, streaming data processing has gained significant attention from both industrial practitioners and academic scholars, driven by rapid developments in the Internet of Things (IoT) and 5G communication technologies. Amazon Web Service claims that “
the nature of enterprise data and the underlying data processing systems have changed significantly
” and “
stream processing system is beneficial in most scenarios where new and dynamic data is generated continually
” (
https://aws.amazon.com/what-is/streaming-data/
). Not coincidentally, researchers in both computer science
(
Ahmad et al. 2017
)
and operations research & management science
(
Iquebal and Bukkapatnam 2018
)
have increasingly focused on streaming data change points analysis.
Detecting such change points often requires substantial computational resources, which could otherwise be allocated to optimization tasks.
Effectively addressing the dual tasks of optimization and statistical inference within streaming data constitutes a critical challenge.

To provide more motivation of the dual tasks within streaming data contexts, consider the case where the decision-makers repeatedly solve similar problems over
n
periods, such as portfolio optimization problem on every exchange day and online contents recommendation etc.
In a typical offline setting, given the complexity and uncertainty involved, decision-makers typically formulate various decisions based on their expertise, knowledge, and experience. Subsequently, they evaluate the performance under these decisions to determine which one yields the best results. However, each individual task necessitates a sufficiently large sample to attain a precise and reliable outcome. Consequently, the separation of these dual tasks incurs enormous costs. For example, online platforms (e.g., Airbnb, Amazon, JD, Taobao, etc.) usually design various advertisement recommendation systems and then conduct A/B tests to select the system with the highest Click-Through Rate (CTR), which requires a substantial amount of data. Thereafter, they must repeat these procedures as many times as possible to arrive at an adequately informed decision. Intuitively, the information provided by the preceding tests holds the potential to aid in evaluating the performance of a new system.
In addition, if there exists some change points during the tests, extra costs occur for change points analysis so as to utilize new information.
This raises the question: why not simultaneously address these dual tasks in an online fashion to balance the utilization of new information with computational efficiency?

Consider a black-box that outputs a realization of
Y(\theta)
once the decision variables
\theta
is input, which we will refer to henceforth as the (input) parameter vector. The closed form of the performance
{\bf\sf E}\left[Y(\theta)\right]
(the expected value of
Y(\theta)
) is unknown. Suppose that the parameter vector takes values in a compact set
\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
, where
p
is a positive integer. Assuming that the realizations of
Y(\theta)
are independent, a widely-used model of the random output is given by

Y(\theta)=\mu(\theta)+\sigma(\theta)\cdot\epsilon_{\theta},\quad\theta\in\Theta,

(1)

where
\mu(\theta)={\bf\sf E}\left[Y(\theta)\right]
represents the expected outcome,
\sigma(\theta)
the standard deviation, and
\epsilon_{\theta}
an independent noise with a mean of 0 and unit variance. It is important to note that, for different choices of
\theta
, the probability distribution of
\epsilon_{\theta}
is not necessarily identical.
The primary objective is to simultaneously address two critical tasks. First and foremost, we aim to perform the task of optimization, such as minimizing the performance:

\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu(\theta).

(2)

Additionally, we aspire to accomplish the task of statistical inference for
\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu(\theta)
simultaneously. In other words, we seek not only some minimizer
\theta^{*}\in\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu(\theta)
but also aim to provide the associated confidence interval (CI) of
\mu(\theta^{*})
in an online fashion.

In the stream of simulation optimization literature, various methods have been developed to solve (
2
) when neither performance function nor gradient information are analytically available and only realizations can be observed.
Fundamental techniques include random search
(
Andradóttir 2006
,
Andradóttir 2015
)
, sample average approximation
(
Kim et al. 2015
)
, stochastic approximation
(
Robbins and Monro 1951
,
Chau and Fu 2015
)
, and surrogate-based methods
(
Hong and Zhang 2021
)
as known as metamodel-based methods
(
Barton and Meckesheimer 2006
,
Barton 2009
)
or response surface methodology. Benefiting from the development of computing power, a recent trend is to incorporate some optimization techniques into surrogate-based methods, e.g., STRONG
(
Chang et al. 2007
,
Chang et al. 2013
)
, SPAS
(
Fan and Hu 2018
)
and SMART
(
Zhang and Hu 2022
)
.
However, most of these methods do not adapt to the online computing environment. In addition, as is suggested by
Audet and Hare (2017)
, “if gradient information is available, reliable, and obtainable at reasonable cost, then gradient-based methods should be used.” Therefore, we are interested in the standard gradient-based method: the Kiefer-Wolfowitz (KW) stochastic approximation (SA) algorithm
(
Kiefer and Wolfowitz 1952
,
Kushner and Clark 1978
,
Spall 1992
,
L’Ecuyer and Yin 1998
,
Kushner and Yin 2003
)
. Moreover,
L’Ecuyer and Yin (1998)
investigate the convergence rate for mean square error (MSE), and
Kushner and Yin (2003)
establish the central limit theorem (CLT) for the parameter vector generated by the SA algorithm. As for online optimization,
Hong et al. (2020)
show that the regret of KWSA algorithm for multi-product dynamic pricing with unknown demand is of order
\sqrt{T}
, which is further extended to SPSA by
Yang et al. (2024)
. Surprisingly, few works address the issue of inference for performance, despite its significant relevance in practical scenarios.

In pursuit of valid statistical inference for
\mu(\theta^{*})
, two predominant viewpoints have emerged: Bayesian and frequentist. Bayesian optimization typically employs a Gaussian process as a prior, with the inference component also referred to as stochastic kriging or Gaussian process regression
(
Sun et al. 2014
,
Sun et al. 2018
,
Meng et al. 2022
,
Wang et al. 2023
)
. Within the realm of frequentist inference, a naive approach involves conducting stochastic approximation until a reliable estimate of
\theta^{*}
is obtained, followed by drawing a sufficiently large sample under this estimate to perform inference for
\mu(\theta^{*})
. However, this method is inefficient and lacks adaptability to the online environment. To our knowledge, actor-critic (AC) algorithms
(
Konda and Tsitsiklis 1999
,
Konda and Tsitsiklis 2003
,
Bhatnagar et al. 2009
,
Sutton and Barto 2020
,
Zhang and Hu 2022
)
combined with multi-time-scale SA
(
Borkar 1997
,
Borkar 2008
,
Bhatnagar and Borkar 1997
,
Bhatnagar et al. 2000
)
present potential methods for addressing both optimization and statistical inference tasks concurrently. Nevertheless, in a black-box setting,
Wu et al. (2022b)
have pointed out that the samples used for stochastic approximation may not be sufficiently representative to provide the best estimator for performance, even when choosing the “fastest” time scale for performance estimation. They propose a four-point method to address this issue, but the construction of CIs in practical use is performed offline fashion and does not adapt to the online environment.

In this paper, we propose another approach to address the issue mentioned above. In the field of machine learning, constant step size (denoted by
\gamma
) is favored due to its exponential convergence in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
towards a neighborhood of
\mu(\theta^{*})
bounded by
\mathcal{O}\left({\gamma}\right)
. Remarkably, when applied to inference for
\mu(\theta^{*})
, this technique aligns with exponential smoothing
(
Hyndman et al. 2008
)
, which has its origins in the work of
Brown (1963)
. We amalgamate these concepts to devise an algorithm that employs Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation
(
Spall 1992
, SPSA,)
with standard decreasing step sizes for optimization and exponential smoothing for statistical inference of performance. Under appropriate conditions, our algorithm inherits the convergence rate of SPSA for optimization, and furthermore it provides a normalized (performance) estimator that asymptotically behaves like an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process. Finally, by utilizing the estimation techniques for parameters of OU processes, we construct an online consistent estimator for the variance of the performance within the algorithm for practical use.

The contributions of our work in this paper can be summarized as follows:

•

In the black-box setting, we provide an online algorithm that simultaneously addresses the dual tasks of optimization and statistical inference for optimal performance.

•

We establish a CLT for the performance estimator and propose an online consistent estimator for the variance of the performance within the algorithm.

•

In terms of statistical inference, we characterize the convergence rate of the coverage probabilities of the asymptotic CIs, using it as a measure to validate statistical inference for the optimal performance.

•

To the SA theory, we extend the multi-time-scale technique to a simple combination of decreasing and constant step sizes.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
describes the details of our algorithm, offering insight and heuristic arguments.
Theoretical guarantees, specifically convergence rate analysis for the parameter vector and the CLT for the performance estimator of our algorithm, are provided in Section
3
. Section
4
compares the coverage probabilities of the asymptotic CIs generated by our algorithm with those of other methods in the literature.
In Section
5
, we present numerical experiments to illustrate and evaluate the algorithm’s performance.
Finally, Section
6
offers a conclusion and some potential future research directions.

2
Algorithm

In this section, we propose an online algorithm that performs the dual tasks of optimization and statistical inference in a black-box setting that produces conditional independent random outputs for any given parameter vector. The term “online” means that the algorithm (a) applies the realizations to updating the parameter vector and the estimates for both the performance and its variance, and (b) needs not to store these realizations for statistical inference.

2.1
Algorithm description

Suppose that a sample of
\tau
realizations of
Y(\theta)
is available after the value of the parameter vector
\theta
is set, where
\tau
is a positive integer that will be henceforth referred to as batch size. For optimization, we resort to finite-difference-based gradient estimator to obtain the descent direction, such as SPSA, which needs a predetermined sequence of perturbation sizes
\{c_{k}\}
. Therefore, our algorithm requires two perturbation samples, which will be formally defined later, to produce a new value for the parameter vector and update the estimates for inference in the meanwhile. The iterations are indexed by
k=0,1,\cdots,n-1
, where
n
is the number of total iterations performed, hence we need a sample of size
2n\tau
in total. Conventionally, let
y_{kj}^{\pm},~j=1,2,\cdots,\tau
, denote the perturbation realizations of (
1
) in iteration
k
. Throughout this paper,
\|\cdot\|
means Euclidean norm.

Now we are ready to describe the details of our algorithm. To begin with iteration
k
, we have precedent parameter vector
\theta_{k}
, performance estimate
\mu_{k}
and variance estimate
v_{k}
. Let
u_{k}
be a
{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
-valued random vector uniformly distributed in the unit sphere
S^{p}=\{u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}:\|u\|=1\}
. Then we draw independent realizations
y_{kj}^{\pm},~j=1,2,\cdots,\tau
, of
Y(\theta_{k}\pm c_{k}u_{k})
, respectively. We average each perturbation sample

y^{\pm}_{k}=\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}y_{kj}^{\pm},

and then estimate the gradient and performance under
\theta_{k}
as

\displaystyle g_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{k}^{+}-y_{k}^{-}}{2c_{k}}u_{k},

(3a)

\displaystyle\bar{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{k}^{+}+y_{k}^{-}}{2}.

(3b)

At the end of iteration
k
, we update the parameter vector as well as the estimates for both the performance and its variance as follows

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\Theta}\left[\theta_{k}-a_{k}g_{k}\right],

(4a)

\displaystyle\mu_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mu_{k}+\gamma(\bar{y}_{k}-\mu_{k}),

(4b)

\displaystyle v_{k+1}

\displaystyle=v_{k}+\frac{1}{k+1}\left[(\bar{y}_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}-v_{k}\right],

(4c)

where
\Pi_{\Theta}
is the operator of projection onto
\Theta
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Repeating it
n

\theta_{n}
(the estiamte of the minimizer
\theta^{*}
),
\mu_{n}
(the estimate of the performance
\mu(\theta^{*})
), and
v_{n}
(the estimate of the performance variance), among which the latter two are used to construct the CI. The complete algorithm is presented as follows.

2.2
On the estimators

In this subsection, we provide intuition and heuristic arguments on the estimators of the performance and the variance of the performance. Some discussions on the properties of the random search FD-based gradient estimator are attached in the Appendix
A
.

Why performance estimator takes constant step size.

There exists an implicit trade-off between optimization and statistical inference: the samples used for stochastic approximation may not be sufficient representative to provide the best estimator for the performance, which is crucially needed for statistical inference. In other words, if we use a pathwise average to estimate
\mu(\theta^{*})
, the samples under
\theta_{k}
’s far away from
\theta^{*}
may yield significant bias for inference of
\mu(\theta^{*})
.

From the previous works
(
L’Ecuyer and Yin 1998
,
Hu et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, we know that the variance of
g_{k}
generated by (
3a
) is
\mathcal{O}\left({c_{k}}^{-2}\right)
, and if we further take
a_{k}=A(k+1)^{-\eta}
,
c_{k}=C(k+1)^{-\nu}
for some constants
A\geq 1
,
C>0
,
\eta\in(1/2,1]
and
\nu\in((1-\eta)/2,\eta-1/2)
, then
{\bf\sf E}\left[\left\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left({a_{k}c_{k}^{-2}}\right)
. It follows from Taylor expansion and convexity that
\left|\mu(\theta_{k})-\mu(\theta^{*})\right|=\mathcal{O}\left({\left\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|}^{2}\right)
, then

\sqrt{n}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}{\bf\sf E}\left[\mu(\theta_{k})-\mu(\theta^{*})\right]\right|=\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta+2\nu}\right).

On the other hand, it follows from Taylor expansion and compactness of
\Theta
that
{\bf\sf E}\left[\bar{y}_{k}-\mu(\theta_{k})\right]=\mathcal{O}\left({c_{k}}^{2}\right)
, presuming
\nabla\mu(\cdot)
is continuous, then

\sqrt{n}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}{\bf\sf E}\left[\bar{y}_{k}-\mu(\theta_{k})\right]\right|=\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{\frac{1}{2}-2\nu}\right).

We need both
\frac{1}{2}-\eta+2\nu<0
and
\frac{1}{2}-2\nu<0
to hold, which yields the contradiction between (A) “
\eta\leq 1
” and (B) “
\eta>2\nu+1/2
and
\nu>1/4\implies\eta>1
”. While
Wu et al. (2022b)
propose a four-point method sacrificing more sample resources to address this issue, we come up with sufficient small constant step size, which makes the estimate take heavier weight on the samples under
\theta_{k}
’s closer to
\theta^{*}
, so that we can avoid the condition (B) which yields the contradiction.

Intuitively, we can imagine the performance estimator on an “accelerating” time scale with respect to the parameter vector. In the time scale of performance, the change of the parameter vector is exponentially decreasing so that
\theta_{k}
is almost static with respect to
\mu_{k}
. In other words, the performance estimator would contain less information of those samples that are not sufficient representative but weigh more on the samples with
\theta_{k}
’s sufficient close to
\theta^{*}
as the algorithm progresses.

Variance estimator is expected to be consistent.

Suppose
\theta^{*}
is known, and draw
n
samples
\{y_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
under
\theta^{*}
, then the (biased) sample variance
\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}
is given by

\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(\bar{y}_{k}-\bar{\mu}_{n})^{2},

(5)

where
\bar{\mu}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\bar{y}_{k}
. Compared with (
4c
), which is equivalent to

v_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(\bar{y}_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2},

in the context of statistics,
\bar{\mu}_{n}
is the sample average while
\mu_{n}
is the exponentially weighted moving average (or exponential smoothing). Intuitively, if
\mu_{n}
is somehow as good an estimate as
\bar{\mu}_{n}
, then
v_{n}
may also be as consistent an estimate as
\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}
. We will present rigorous analysis on this in the next section.

3
Convergence Analysis

Let
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\bf\sf P})
be the probability space induced by Algorithm
1
, where
\Omega
is the collection of all sample paths generated by the algorithm,
{\mathcal{F}}
is a
\sigma

\Omega
, and
{\bf\sf P}
is a probability measure on
{\mathcal{F}}
. Define the
\sigma

{\mathcal{F}}_{k}=\sigma(\theta_{0},\mu_{0},v_{0},\cdots,\theta_{k},\mu_{k},v_{k})
for
k=0,1,\cdots
, i.e., the information up to
\left(\theta_{k},\mu_{k},v_{k}\right)
. Assume the feasible region
\Theta
is a convex set characterized by inequality constraints:

\Theta=\left\{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}:h_{j}(\theta)\leq 0,j=1,\cdots,m\right\},

where
h_{j}(\theta)\leq 0,j=1,\cdots,m
are continuously differentiable convex functions with
\nabla h_{j}(\theta)\neq 0
whenever
h_{j}(\theta)=0

(
Kushner and Yin 2003

. Therefore, the projection operation in (
4a
) is equivalent to adding an extra projection term

, resulting in the recursion

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-a_{k}g_{k}+a_{k}Z_{k},

(6)

where
a_{k}Z_{k}=\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}+a_{k}g_{k}
is the real vector with the smallest Euclidean norm needed to take
\theta_{k}-a_{k}g_{k}
back to the feasible region
\Theta
.

For notational convenience, let
{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\cdot\right]={\bf\sf E}\left[\left.\cdot\right|{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\right]
, and
\mu^{*}=\mu(\theta^{*})
. Note that
{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\cdot\right]={\bf\sf E}\left[\left.\cdot\right|\theta_{k}\right]~w.p.1
. Let us introduce the following assumptions, which are very similar to those in the literature
(
Spall 1992
,
L’Ecuyer and Yin 1998
)
.

Assumptions:

A1

The model (
1
) satisfies that for every
\theta\in\Theta
,

(a)

\mu(\cdot)
is third order continuously differentiable.

(b)

\mu(\cdot)
is strongly convex and the unique minimizer

lies in the interior of
\Theta
.

(c)

\sigma(\cdot)
is non-negative, and second order continuously differentiable.

A2

The random directions
u_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}{\textsf{Uniform}}(S^{p})
for all

.

A3

The positive sequences
\{a_{k}\}
and

satisfy that

(a)

\sum_{k}a_{k}=\infty,~\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}<\infty

(b)

.

Remark 1
.

) is solvable as a convex problem, even though no analytical information is available:

Under these assumptions, standard

.

Suppose Assumptions

In addition, if

\gamma\searrow 0
,
\{U^{\gamma}(t+\cdot),W^{\gamma}(t+\cdot)\}
converge weakly to some limit
\left(U(\cdot),W(\cdot)\right)
that satisfies the following stochastic differential equation (SDE)

dU(t)=-U(t)dt+\frac{\sigma(\theta^{*})}{\sqrt{2\tau}}dW(t),

(8)

where
W(t)
is a standard Wiener process.

The proof of Theorem
1
is given in Appendix
C.3
.

Remark 3
.

This result can be translated into the (weak) convergence of
U^{\gamma}_{k}
. Note that the limit SDE (
8
) is an OU process, and it satisfies that

{\bf\sf E}\left[U(t)\right]=e^{-t}U(0),~~~{\bf\sf Cov}\left[U(t),U(s)\right]=\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{4\tau}(e^{-|t-s|}-e^{-(t+s)}),

and hence

{\lim_{\gamma\searrow 0}U^{\gamma}_{m(t)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}~}U(t)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(e^{-t}U(0),\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{4\tau}\left(1-e^{-2t}\right)\right).

Then, the result of Theorem
1
implies that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\lim_{\gamma\searrow 0}\frac{\mu_{m(t)}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{4\tau}\right).

In practice, one should choose the sample size
n
according to
\gamma
. Since
m(t)=\left\lfloor t/\gamma\right\rfloor
, we have

{\bf\sf E}\left[\frac{\mu_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\right]\approx e^{-n\gamma}\frac{\mu_{0}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\gamma}},~~~{\bf\sf Var}\left[\frac{\mu_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\right]\approx\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{4\tau}\left(1-e^{-2n\gamma}\right).

Thus, we need
\lim_{\gamma\searrow 0}\left.e^{-n\gamma}\right/\sqrt{\gamma}=0
, which requires
n>-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\log\gamma
. To this end, one is supposed to choose
n_{\gamma}=\left\lfloor-\frac{c}{2\gamma}\log\gamma\right\rfloor+1
for some
c>1
at least.

Remark 4
.

In a typical multi-time-scale scheme, the constant step size
\gamma
is replaced by a sequence of decreasing step sizes such as
\{\gamma_{k}\}
. For the purpose of estimation, the sequence of step sizes is supposed to satisfy that
a_{k}=o(\gamma_{k})
in addition to the standard conditions of decreasing step size, so that the parameter vector would be almost still in the time-scale of the performance estimator. Using a constant step size can be interpreted as laying the performance estimator on an “accelerating” time-scale so as to avoid the contradiction mentioned in subsection
2.2
.

3.2
Consistent variance estimator

In this subsection, we propose a consistent estimator of the variance for valid statistical inference. Specifically, we indirectly estimate the variance via the deviation term
\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{2\tau}
of the OU process (
8
). A naive estimator of the variance is the sample variance
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\bar{y}_{k}-\bar{\mu}_{n})^{2}
, as discussed in Subsection
2.2
, but it does not adapt to the online environment. If
\mu^{*}
was known, and we obtain some discrete-time observations
\{U_{k}\}
of the OU process (
8
), of which the time interval is denoted by
\gamma
. Then, according to
Wu, Hu, and Yang (2022a)
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(e^{\gamma}U_{k+1}-U_{k})^{2}\longrightarrow\frac{(e^{2\gamma}-1)\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{4\tau}~w.p.1,~\forall\gamma>0.

Theorem
1
implies that as
\gamma
approaches 0, there exists some integer
N_{\gamma}>0
such that
\{U^{\gamma}_{k}\}_{k\geq N_{\gamma}}
behaves statistically similar to discrete-time observations of the OU process (
8
), then we obtain

\lim_{\gamma\searrow 0}\frac{1}{n\gamma}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(e^{\gamma}U^{\gamma}_{k+1}-U^{\gamma}_{k})^{2}\longrightarrow\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{2\tau}~w.p.1,~n\to\infty.

(9)

In practice, we can only use
\{\bar{y}_{k},\mu_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
to estimate the variance. The proposed variance estimator has a recursive form as follows.

v_{k+1}=v_{k}+\frac{1}{k+1}\left[r_{k}(\bar{y}_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}-v_{k}\right].

(10)

Note that (
4c
) is a special case of (
10
) with
r_{k}\equiv 1
. The consistency of our variance estimator is established in the theorem below, of which the proof is given in Appendix
C.4
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
A1
-
A3
hold. Then for
n>-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\log\gamma
, as
\gamma\searrow 0
,

v_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf\sf P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{2\tau}.

It is an immediate corollary that the normalized estimator provided by Algorithm
1
is asymptotically normal distributed.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
A1
-
A3
hold. Then for
n>-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\log\gamma

\frac{\mu_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\gamma v_{n}/2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right),~~~\gamma\searrow 0.

Proof.

Proof
It is straightforward based on the continuous mapping theorem and Slutsky’s theorem, in combination with Theorems
1
and
2
, that

\frac{\mu_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\gamma v_{n}/2}}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{2\tau v_{n}}}\frac{\mu_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\gamma\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})/(4\tau)}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right),~n>-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\log\gamma,\gamma\searrow 0.

∎

Remark 5
.

The above corollary states that for each
\varepsilon>0
and each sufficient small
\gamma>0
, there exists an integer
N_{\gamma}>0
such that
\forall n\geq N_{\gamma}
,
\left|F_{n}(x)-F(x)\right|<\varepsilon,~x\in\mathcal{C}(F_{n})
, where
F_{n}
denotes the distribution function of the normalized estimator, which is defined by

\frac{\mu_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\gamma v_{n}/2}},

and
\mathcal{C}(F_{n})
denotes the continuity set of
F_{n}
.
Then it gives the asymptotic
(1-\alpha)
-CI constructed by

\mu_{n}\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\gamma v_{n}/2},

where
z_{\alpha/2}
is the lower
\alpha/2
-quantile of standard normal distribution.

4
Comparative Analysis

In this section, we compare the coverage probabilities of the asymptotic CIs generated by Algorithm
1
with those generated by other methods. Note that the coverage probabilities of the asymptotic CIs equal to the probabilities that the normalized estimates fall in the associated intervals of standard normal distribution.
Formally, consider a general performance estimator in the following form:

\mu_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})\bar{y}_{k}.

Here,
\gamma_{k}
represents the sequence of performance step sizes without any assumption.

Specifically, let
\left\{\theta_{k}\right\}
be a sequence of parameter vectors generated by some optimization algorithm. Then, by Taylor expansion and
A1
A1(a)
, for each
k
, there exists some
\tilde{\theta}_{k}^{\pm}
in the line segments between
\theta_{k}
and
\theta_{k}\pm c_{k}u_{k}
, respectively, so that

y_{k}^{\pm}=\mu(\theta_{k})\pm c_{k}\nabla\mu(\theta_{k})^{\bf\sf T}u_{k}+\frac{c_{k}^{2}}{2}u_{k}^{\bf\sf T}\nabla^{2}\mu(\tilde{\theta}_{k}^{\pm})u_{k}+\sigma(\theta_{k}\pm c_{k}u_{k})\bar{\epsilon}_{k}^{\pm},

thus we can decompose (
3b
) as

\bar{y}_{k}=\mu(\theta_{k})+\beta_{k}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{k},

(11)

where

\displaystyle\beta_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{k}^{2}}{4}u_{k}^{\bf\sf T}\left[\nabla^{2}\mu(\tilde{\theta}_{k}^{+})+\nabla^{2}\mu(\tilde{\theta}_{k}^{-})\right]u_{k},

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\sigma(\theta_{k}+c_{k}u_{k})\bar{\epsilon}_{k}^{+}+\sigma(\theta_{k}-c_{k}u_{k})\bar{\epsilon}_{k}^{-}\right].

Then, we obtain

\mu_{n}-\mu^{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})\left[\mu(\theta_{k})-\mu^{*}+\beta_{k}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}\right]-\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})\mu^{*}.

(12)

Let
V_{n}
be some non-increasing positive sequence that
the normalized estimator

\frac{\mu_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{V_{n}}\sigma(\theta^{*})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right).

(13)

We are interested in comparing the convergence rates of the probability that it falls in the interval
[-z,z]
under different choice of
\{\gamma_{n}\}
and
\{V_{n}\}
, where
z=z_{1-\alpha/2}
.
Before going further, consider the specific forms of the sequences

a_{n}=An^{-\eta},\quad c_{n}=Cn^{-\nu},\quad\gamma_{n}=\gamma n^{-\delta},\quad V_{n}=Vn^{-\kappa},

where
A,C,\gamma,V
are positive constants.
Then by simple algebra, the second term on the right-hand side of (
12
) is a higher-order infinitesimal term with respect to
\sqrt{V_{n}}
.

By (
12
), the normalized estimator can be separated into endogenous bias and exogenous noise, and hence the error of coverage probabilities can be separated in the same way. Define

F_{n}(z)\triangleq{\bf\sf P}\left\{\frac{\mu_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{V_{n}}\sigma(\theta^{*})}\leq z\right\},

then,

\left|F_{n}\left(z\right)-F_{n}\left(-z\right)-1+\alpha\right|\leq{\bf\sf E}\left[\left|E_{1}\right|\right]+{\bf\sf E}\left[\left|E_{2}\right|\right],

where

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=F_{n}\left(z\right)-F_{n}\left(-z\right)-\Phi\left(-R_{n}+z\right)+\Phi\left(-R_{n}-z\right),

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=\Phi\left(-R_{n}+z\right)-\Phi\left(-R_{n}-z\right)-1+\alpha

with

R_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V_{n}}\sigma(\theta^{*})}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})\left[\mu(\theta_{k})-\mu^{*}+\beta_{k}\right]+o(1),

and
\Phi
denoting the distribution function of standard normal distribution.
Conventionally, denote
\phi
the density function.
It is worth noting that the exogenous error
E_{1}
is independent of
R_{n}
and characterizes the convergence speed of the noise term

S_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\xi_{nk},

(14)

where
\xi_{nk}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V_{n}}}\gamma_{k}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}
. Under proper condition,
E_{1}
is bounded by the Berry-Esseen bound for martingale CLT.
For the endogenous error, by Taylor expansion, it yields

E_{2}=-z\phi(z)R_{n}^{2}+o(R_{n}^{2}),

thus
E_{2}
is bounded by
\mathcal{O}\left({R_{n}}^{2}\right)
. Assumption
A1
and the compactness of
\Theta
imply that for some positive constants
\lambda
and
L
,

\lambda\left\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}\leq\mu(\theta_{k})-\mu^{*}\leq L\left\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}.

Note that since
\nabla\mu(\theta^{*})=0
,
\lambda
coincides with the infimum of the smallest eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}\mu(\theta)
over
\Theta
, and
L
the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla\mu(\cdot)
. Hence, the endogenous error
E_{2}
is mainly contributed by the bias term because

R_{n}={\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{V_{n}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})\left[\left\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}+\beta_{k}\right]\right),

(15)

where
y_{n}={\boldsymbol{\Theta}}(x_{n})
means
\limsup_{n}\left|y_{n}/x_{n}\right|<\infty
and
\liminf_{n}\left|y_{n}/x_{n}\right|>0
.

4.1
Quantification of the exogenous error

In this subsection, we want to quantify the exogenous error in finite sample.
Firstly, we study the sufficient conditions for the weak convergence of the noise term, because the asymptotic CIs are meaningless without asymptotic normality. Here we use the following condition in addition,

(
C
)

There exists some constant
d>0
such that
\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}{\bf\sf E}\left[\left|\epsilon_{\theta}\right|^{2+d}\right]<\infty
.

Remark 6
.

The effect of condition (
C
) is two-fold. First, by choosing the decay rate of
V_{n}
carefully, condition (
C
) implies the so-called “conditional Lyapunov condition”, i.e.,

\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\left|\xi_{nk}\right|^{2+d}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf\sf P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,~~~n\to\infty,

(16)

which further implies “conditional Lindeberg condition”: for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\xi_{nk}^{2}{\mathbbm{1}}\left\{\left|\xi_{nk}\right|>\varepsilon\right\}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf\sf P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,~~~n\to\infty,

where
{\mathbbm{1}}
is the indicator function.
Consequently, if there exists some constant
\sigma^{2}<\infty
such that

\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\xi_{nk}^{2}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf\sf P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sigma^{2},~~~n\to\infty,

(17)

then the CLT holds for (
14
)
(
Shiryaev 2019
, Theorem 4, Section 8, Chapter 7,)
.
Secondly, as far as the authors are aware of, condition (
C
) also implies the sufficient condition for the Berry-Esseen bound of martingale CLT
(
Fan 2019
, Theorem 2.1,)
, namely, there exists some
\varepsilon_{n}\searrow 0
so that for all
k\leq n

{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\left|\xi_{nk}\right|^{2+d}\right]\leq\varepsilon_{n}^{d}{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\xi_{nk}^{2}\right]~w.p.1.

It yields

E_{1}=\mathcal{O}\left({\varepsilon_{n}}^{d}\right).

The remained question is what decay rate
V_{n}
should take so that “conditional Lyapunov condition” is satisfied.
The following proposition formally states the requirement of
V_{n}
for the validity of CLT and the associated Berry-Esseen bound.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
A1
-
A3
and condition
(C)
holds.
If
V_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\gamma_{n})
,
then the CLT for (
14
) holds, i.e.,

S_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{2\tau}\right),~~~n\to\infty.

In the meanwhile, it also supports the associated Berry-Esseen bound for the martingale CLT

\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left|{\bf\sf P}\left\{\sqrt{2\tau}S_{n}\leq z\sigma(\theta^{*})\right\}-\Phi\left(z\right)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left({\gamma_{n}}^{d/2}\right).

The proof of Proposition
2
is given in Appendix
C.5
.

Remark 7
.

Proposition
2
quantifies the exogenous error, and it points out that only
\gamma_{n}
and the constant
d
in condition (
C
) matter. For Algorithm
1
, it also gives a Berry-Esseen bound in terms of
\gamma
. But we can approximate the best finite sample bound by interchanging
n>\frac{1}{2\gamma}\log\frac{1}{\gamma}
to
\gamma>\frac{\log n}{n}
.

In fact, we establish that

\displaystyle\left|E_{1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{z}\left|F_{n}(z)-\Phi(z-R_{n})\right|

\displaystyle=2\sup_{z}\left|{\bf\sf P}\left\{\sqrt{2\tau}S_{n}\leq z\sigma(\theta^{*})\right\}-\Phi(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left({\gamma_{n}}^{d/2}\right).

In next subsection, we can show that
E_{1}
is usually dominated by
E_{2}
.

4.2
Comparison on the endogenous errors

Now we are ready to study the decay rates of step sizes for performance estimator given the other step sizes (optimization and perturbation), namely the convergence rates of (
15
) for the methods given in Appendix
D.1
. Taking expectation on the right-hand side of (
15
), it yields

\displaystyle\sf\bf{\displaystyle E}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})\left(\left\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}+\beta_{k}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})\left({\bf\sf E}\left[\left\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}\right]+{\bf\sf E}\left[\beta_{k}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left({\frac{{\bf\sf E}\left[\left\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}\right]}{\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left({\frac{{\bf\sf E}\left[\beta_{n}\right]}{\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}}}\right),

where the inequality is due to Lemma
2
. First of all, for Algorithm
1
, it is easy to see that fixed
\gamma_{n}=\gamma\in(0,1)
leads to

{\bf\sf E}\left[\left|R_{n}\right|\right]=\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{-\eta+2\nu}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{-2\nu}\right).

As is proved in the Section
4.1
, the bias of the normalized estimator of Algorithm
1
goes to 0 rapidly. In exchange, it sacrifices the accuracy of the estimation, i.e., it does not give a performance estimator with decreasing variance, but it provides one with variance of order
\gamma
.

Four-point method.

Wu et al. (2022b)
shows that
{\bf\sf E}\left[\left\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{-1+2\nu}\right)
with
\nu\in(1/6,1/4)
, and
{\bf\sf E}\left[\beta_{n}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{-3\nu}\right)
in four-point method. With
\gamma_{n}=1/n
, it follows from Lemma
2
that

{\bf\sf E}\left[\left|R_{n}\right|\right]=\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{-1/2+2\nu}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{1/2-3\nu}\right),

which goes to 0 for
\nu\in(1/6,1/4)
, and the fastest rate
\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{-1/10}\right)
is attained when
\nu=1/5
.

Multi-time-scale method.

Recall that for ordinary SPSA algorithm,
{\bf\sf E}\left[\left\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{-\eta+2\nu}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{-4\nu}\right)
and
{\bf\sf E}\left[\beta_{n}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{-2\nu}\right)
. Using
\gamma_{n}=\gamma n^{-\delta}
with
\delta<2\gamma/(2+\gamma)
, then

{\bf\sf E}\left[\left|R_{n}\right|\right]=\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{\frac{\delta}{2}-\eta+2\nu}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{\frac{\delta}{2}-2\nu}\right).

The right-hand side above goes to 0 if both
\frac{1}{2}<\delta<\eta\leq 1
and
\frac{\delta}{2}<(\eta-2\nu)\wedge(2\nu)
hold, which is equivalent to
\nu\in\left(\frac{1}{8},\frac{\eta}{2}-\frac{1}{8}\right)
and
\delta<2(\eta-2\nu)\wedge(2\nu)
. Here,
x\wedge y=\min(x,y)
. In this case, the fastest rate
\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{\frac{\delta-\eta}{2}}\right)
can be attained by taking
\nu=\eta/4
with
\delta\in(1/2,\eta)
.

Forward SPSA method.

Following the same procedures in the proof of Proposition
1
(see Appendix
C
), one can easily show that it turns out
{\bf\sf E}\left[\left\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{-\eta+2\nu}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{-2\nu}\right)
and
{\bf\sf E}\left[\beta_{n}\right]=0
for forward SPSA algorithm. Then, it follows that

{\bf\sf E}\left[\left|R_{n}\right|\right]=\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta+2\nu}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left({n}^{\frac{1}{2}-2\nu}\right)

with
\gamma_{n}=1/n
, and it needs not to converge to 0 because
-(\eta-2\nu)\wedge(2\nu)\geq-\eta/2\geq-1/2
.

Summary

We have derived the convergence rates of
{\bf\sf E}\left[\left|R_{n}\right|\right]
for Algorithm
1
and the benchmarks, respectively.
Notably,
{\bf\sf E}\left[\left|E_{1}\right|\right]
is typically dominated by
{\bf\sf E}\left[\left|E_{2}\right|\right]
. From
E_{2}=-z\phi(z)R_{n}^{2}+o(R_{n}^{2})

{\bf\sf E}\left[\left|E_{2}\right|\right]=z\phi(z){\bf\sf E}\left[R_{n}^{2}\right]+o\left({\bf\sf E}\left[R_{n}^{2}\right]\right)=\boldsymbol{\Omega}\left({\bf\sf E}\left[\left|R_{n}\right|\right]^{2}\right),

which dominates

The only exception occurs when

, which corresponds to the fastest convergence rate of the parameter vector.
To this extent, the convergence rates of

5
Simulation Experiments

In this section, we demonstrate a 2-dimensional simulation experiment on a set of synthetic examples to compare the performance of Algorithm
1
with that of the benchmarks in the literature. Let’s begin with the detailed settings. Consider the following cases in which the output given the input value
\theta
has different distribution but shares the common mean function
\mu(\theta)
:

Case 1

Y(\theta)\sim{\textsf{Bernoulli}}\left(\mu(\theta)\right)
, then
\sigma(\theta)=\sqrt{\mu(\theta)\left[1-\mu(\theta)\right]}
;

Case 2

Y(\theta)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu(\theta),\sigma^{2}(\theta)\right)
with
\sigma(\theta)=1.5\sin\left(2\pi\left|\theta\right|\right)+2.5
;

Case 3

Y(\theta)\sim{\textsf{Gamma}}\left(\alpha,\mu(\theta)/\alpha\right)
with
\alpha=4
, then
\sigma(\theta)=\left.\mu(\theta)\right/\sqrt{\alpha}
;

Case 4

Y(\theta)\sim{\textsf{Pareto}}\left(\alpha,(\alpha-1)\mu(\theta)/\alpha\right)
with
\alpha=3
, then
\sigma(\theta)=\left.\mu(\theta)\right/\sqrt{\alpha\left(\alpha-2\right)}
;

Case 5

Y(\theta)\sim{\textsf{Lognormal}}\left(\log\mu(\theta)-s^{2}/2,s^{2}\right)
with
s=1
, then
\sigma(\theta)=\mu(\theta)\sqrt{e^{s^{2}}-1}
;

We test the algorithms in the cases above with various functions, which can be categorized into one-dimensional functions and multi-dimensional functions. We only demonstrate a 2-dimensional function here, the other experiments can be found in Appendix
E
.
All computations are performed using Python 3.8.11 on a Windows server which has two Intel Xeon Silver 4215R CPUs (with 32 cores in total, each running at 3.20 GHz) and 256 GB of memory. The codes are available on Github:
https://github.com/HenryLean/BBO_Inference
.

Consider a 2-dimensional function

f(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\theta^{\bf\sf T}{\bf A}\theta-{\bf b}^{\bf\sf T}\theta+1,~~~\theta\in[-2,2]^{2},

where

more rapidly

\gamma

\gamma

6
Conclusion

To address the dual tasks of optimization and statistical inference simultaneously in a black-box setting, we propose an algorithm based on SPSA for optimization and SA with constant step sizes for statistical inference. It updates the performance estimator on an “accelerating” time scale to prevent it from the contradiction between the CLTs of
\mu(\theta_{k})-\mu^{*}
and
\mu_{k}-\mu(\theta_{k})
. In addition, we provide an online consistent estimator for the performance variance, which can be used in constructing the asymptotic CIs. This paper extends multi-time-scale SA to the combination of decreasing and constant step sizes, where the variable on the “accelerating” time scale does not influence the variable on the slower time scale in return. Furthermore, we characterize the convergence rate of the coverage probabilities of the asymptotic CIs, and show the reason why our algorithm outperforms the benchmarks.

Here we list some potential extensions. It is worth (i) considering more general stochastic black-box models (e.g., hidden Markov model) in the black-box setting; (ii) investigating other optimization algorithms with simultaneous statistical inference when the objective function is non-convex; and (iii) developing theory of multi-time-scale SA or analyzing AC algorithms using the combination of decreasing and constant step sizes.

Lemma 2

)
.

Proof

that

\displaystyle\text{(finite part)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(1-a_{j})^{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n_{0}-1}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-a_{j})\right]^{-d}a_{k}^{d}b_{k}

\text{(tail part)}\leq C\sum_{k=n_{0}}^{n-1}\left[\prod_{j=k+1}^{n-1}(1-a_{j})^{d}\right]a_{k}^{d}(k+1)^{-\nu}.

(B-1)

.

\left|T_{n}(x)-T_{n}(y)\right|=(1-a_{n})^{d}\left|x-y\right|

a_{n}
by condition, it is easy to see

\left|\left.dx^{-\eta(d-1)-\nu}\right/dx\right|=\left[\eta(d-1)+\nu\right]x^{-\eta(d-1)-\nu-1}

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left[\prod_{j=k+1}^{n-1}(1-a_{j})^{d}\right]a_{k}^{d}b_{k}=\mathcal{O}\left({a_{n}^{d-1}b_{n}}\right).

B-1

\text{(tail part)}\geq C^{\prime}\sum_{k=n_{0}}^{n-1}\left[\prod_{j=k+1}^{n-1}(1-a_{j})^{d}\right]a_{k}^{d}(k+1)^{-\nu}.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n\varepsilon}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}r_{k}{\bf\sf E}\left[\left|(\bar{y}_{k}-\mu^{*})(\mu_{k}-\mu^{*})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n\varepsilon}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}r_{k}\sqrt{{\bf\sf E}\left[(\bar{y}_{k}-\mu^{*})^{2}\right]{\bf\sf E}\left[(\mu_{k}-\mu^{*})^{2}\right]}.

By (
9
), it suffices to show that the right-hand sides of both inequalities go to 0 as
\gamma\searrow 0
.
By Theorem
1
, we have

\lim_{\gamma\searrow 0}U_{m(t)}^{\gamma}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}U(t),~~~\forall t>0.

Note that by Lemma
1
, we have for all
t>0
,

\limsup_{\gamma\searrow 0}\gamma{\bf\sf E}\left[\left(U_{m(t)}^{\gamma}\right)^{2}\right]<\infty,

then

\gamma{\bf\sf E}\left[\left(U_{m(t)}^{\gamma}\right)^{2}\right]=\gamma{\bf\sf E}\left[U(t)^{2}\right]+o(\gamma),

where a sequence (indexed by
\gamma
)
a_{\gamma}=o(\gamma)
means that
\lim_{\gamma\searrow 0}a_{\gamma}/\gamma=0
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}{\bf\sf E}\left[\left(\mu_{k}-\mu^{*}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}{\bf\sf E}\left[U(k\gamma)^{2}\right]+o(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left[e^{-2k\gamma}\left(\mu_{0}-\mu^{*}\right)^{2}+\frac{\gamma\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{4\tau}\left(1-e^{-2k\gamma}\right)\right]+o(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{4\tau}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{-2k\gamma}\left[\left(\mu_{0}-\mu^{*}\right)^{2}-\frac{\gamma\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{4\tau}\right]+o(\gamma).

By Riemann-Stieltjes integration, we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{-2k\gamma}\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(1+\int_{0}^{n-1}e^{-2\gamma x}dx\right)=\frac{1}{2n\gamma}\left(1+2\gamma-e^{-2(n-1)\gamma}\right).

The right-hand side goes to 0 as
\gamma\searrow 0
with
n>-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\log\gamma
.

Combined with (
9
), we obtain

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}r_{k}(\bar{y}_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf\sf P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{2\tau},~~~{n>-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\log\gamma},\gamma\searrow 0.

Finally,
v_{n}
generated by (
10
) is equivalent to

v_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}r_{k}(\bar{y}_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2},

which is a consistent estimator of
\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})/2
.
∎

C.5
Proof of Proposition
2

Following the arguments in Subsection
4.1
, it suffices to show that both (
16
) and (
17
) holds. It follows from Assumption
A1
A1(c)
and condition (
C
) that for some constant
d\leq 1
and
K>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\left|\xi_{nk}\right|^{2+d}\right]

\displaystyle={V_{n}}^{-1-d/2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}^{2+d}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})^{2+d}{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\left|\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}\right|^{2+d}\right]

\displaystyle\leq K{V_{n}}^{-1-d/2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}^{2+d}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})^{2+d}\left(\sigma^{2+d}(\theta_{k})+c_{k}\right).

By Lemma
2
, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}^{2+d}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})^{2+d}\left(\sigma^{2+d}(\theta_{k})+c_{k}\right)=\left(1+\left\|\theta_{n}-\theta^{*}\right\|+c_{n}\right)\mathcal{O}\left({\gamma_{n}}^{d+1}\right),

then it is a sufficient condition for (
16
) that

V_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Theta}(\gamma_{n})
,
which yields

\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\left|\xi_{nk}\right|^{2+d}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left({\gamma_{n}}^{d/2}\right).

Similarly, rewrite (
17
) as

\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\xi_{nk}^{2}\right]={V_{n}}^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}^{2}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})^{2}{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\left|\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}\right|^{2}\right],

where
{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\left|\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}\right|^{2}\right]=\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})/(2\tau)+\mathcal{O}\left({\left\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\right\|}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left({c_{k}}\right)
. Thus, we have to show that
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma_{k}^{2}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})^{2}
is on the same order of
\gamma_{n}
. The upper bound is immediately supported by Lemma
2
, while the asymptotic lower bound on the same order needs in addition that
\delta<2\gamma/(2+\gamma)
. Then, a stronger version of (
17
) holds that

\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\xi_{nk}^{2}\right]\longrightarrow\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{2\tau}~w.p.1.

Then the CLT holds.

Note that

\displaystyle\frac{{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\left|\xi_{nk}\right|^{2+d}\right]}{{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\xi_{nk}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle={V_{n}}^{-d/2}\gamma_{k}^{d}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})^{d}\frac{{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\left|\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}\right|^{2+d}\right]}{{\bf\sf E}_{k}\left[\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq K{V_{n}}^{-d/2}\gamma_{k}^{d}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})^{d}

\displaystyle\leq K{V_{n}}^{-d/2}\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\gamma_{k}^{d}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(1-\gamma_{j})^{d}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left({\gamma_{n}}^{d/2}\right)

Then, by Theorem 2.1 in
Fan (2019)
, the Berry-Esseen bound for the martingale CLT follows, i.e.,

\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left|{\bf\sf P}\left\{\sqrt{2\tau}S_{n}\leq z\sigma(\theta^{*})\right\}-\Phi\left(z\right)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left({\gamma_{n}}^{d/2}\right).

Appendix D
Benchmarks

D.1
Benchmarks in Section
4

This subsection briefly introduces some benchmarks: four-point method
(
Wu et al. 2022b
)
, multi-time-scale method
(
Borkar 2008
)
and forward FD method. For notational simplicity and consistency, we use diacritics to distinguish the estimates generated by different methods.

Four-point method.

Samples
y^{(\pm)}_{ki}
(
i=1,\cdots,\tau_{1}
) and
y^{(h\pm)}_{kj}
(
j=1,\cdots,\tau_{2}
) are drawn as realizations of
Y(\theta_{k}\pm c_{k}u_{k})
and
Y(\theta_{k}\pm hc_{k}u_{k})
, respectively, where
h>1
is a user-specified hyperparameter such that
h=\tau_{1}/\tau_{2}
and
\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}=\tau
. Then the sample averages are given by

y_{k}^{(\pm)}=\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tau_{1}}y^{(\pm)}_{ki},~~~y_{k}^{(h\pm)}=\frac{1}{\tau_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\tau_{2}}y^{(h\pm)}_{kj},

and the gradient and performance can be estimated as

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{-y^{(h+)}_{k}+h^{3}y^{(+)}_{k}-h^{3}y^{(-)}_{k}+y^{(h-)}_{k}}{2h^{2}(h-1)c_{k}}u_{k},

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{-y^{(h+)}_{k}+h^{2}y^{(+)}_{k}+h^{2}y^{(-)}_{k}-y^{(h-)}_{k}}{2(h^{2}-1)}.

The parameter vector is still updated via (
4a
) with
g_{k}
being replaced by
\hat{g}_{k}
. For inference, it uses the offline sample average of
\hat{y}_{k}~k=0,1,\cdots,n
, which is equivalent to an online estimator generated by

\hat{\mu}_{k+1}=\hat{\mu}_{k}+\frac{1}{k+1}(\hat{y}_{k}-\hat{\mu}_{k}).

(D-1)

The variance is estimated by the sample variance of
\{y_{i},~i=I,I+1,\cdots,2\tau n\}
for some
I\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}
, which does not adapt to the online environment. Here,
y_{i}
(
i=2k\tau+1,k\tau+1,\cdots,2(k+1)\tau
) represents a random permutation of
\{y^{(\pm)}_{ki},y^{(h\pm)}_{kj},~i=1,2,\cdots,\tau_{1},~j=1,2,\cdots,\tau_{2}\}
.
Following the arguments in Subsection
3.2
, it is reasonable to use (
10
) to estimate the variance for both methods. Conventionally, denote
\hat{v}_{n}
the variance estimator for the four-point method. According to
Wu et al. (2022b)
, we know that

\frac{h^{2}-1}{h^{2}+1}\cdot\frac{\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sigma(\theta^{*})/\sqrt{2\tau n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right),~~~n\to\infty,

and one can prove that

{\bf\sf E}\left[\left(\hat{y}_{k}-\hat{\mu}_{k}\right)^{2}\right]\longrightarrow\left(\frac{h^{2}+1}{h^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{2\tau},~~~k\to\infty,

which further implies that

\hat{v}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\bf\sf P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\left(\frac{h^{2}+1}{h^{2}-1}\right)^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{2\tau},~~~n\to\infty.

Then the associated normalized estimator is

\frac{(h^{2}-1)}{(h^{2}+1)}\frac{\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_{n}/n}},

Multi-time-scale method.

The optimization procedure of the multi-time-scale method employs SPSA as Algorithm
1
does, while the recursive equation of performance estimation merely replaces
\gamma
by a decreasing positive sequence
\left\{\gamma_{k}\right\}
. Specifically, (
4b
) and (
4c
) are replaced by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mu}_{k}+\gamma_{k}(\bar{y}_{k}-\tilde{\mu}_{k}),

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{v}_{k}+\zeta_{k}\left[\left(\bar{y}_{k}-\tilde{\mu}_{k}\right)^{2}-\tilde{v}_{k}\right],

respectively, where
\left\{\gamma_{k}\right\}
and
\left\{\zeta_{k}\right\}
satisfy the conditions
\min\left(\sum_{k}\gamma_{k},\sum_{k}\zeta_{k}\right)=\infty,~\sum_{k}(\gamma_{k}^{2}+\zeta_{k}^{2})<\infty
and
a_{k}=o(\gamma_{k}),~\gamma_{k}=o(\zeta_{k})

(
Borkar 2008
)
.

Previous result
(
Kushner and Yin 2003
, Theorem 2.1, Section 10.2)
implies that, under proper conditions, for sufficiently large
n
,

\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}}\approx\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta^{*})}{2\tau}\right).

Thus, the associated normalized estimator

\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{n}\tilde{v}_{n}}}

is valid to construct CIs if the variance estimator is consistent.

Forward SPSA method.

Instead of realizations
y^{-}_{kj},j=1,2,\cdots,\tau
of
Y(\theta_{k}-c_{k}u_{k})
in Algorithm
1
, forward SPSA utilizes samples of
Y(\theta_{k})
of which the batch average is denoted by
\check{y}_{k}
in the
k
th iteration. It is naive to avoid the bias induced by perturbation via using only the samples of
Y(\theta_{k})
to estimate the performance, even though it neglects the information provided by perturbed performances.
Specifically, replace (
3a
) by

\check{g}_{k}=\frac{y^{+}_{k}-\check{y}_{k}}{c_{k}}u_{k},

then substitute
g_{k}
in (
4a
) by
\check{g}_{k}
. Instead of (
4b
), the associated performance estimates are generated by

\check{\mu}_{k+1}=\check{\mu}_{k}+\frac{1}{k+1}\left(\check{y}_{k}-\check{\mu}_{k}\right).

It corresponds to the variance estimator

\check{v}_{k+1}=\check{v}_{k}+\frac{1}{k+1}\left[\left(\check{y}_{k}-\check{\mu}_{k}\right)^{2}-\check{v}_{k}\right],

and the associated normalized estimator

\frac{\check{\mu}_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\check{v}_{n}/n}}.

D.2
Benchmarks in Section
5

Apart from the algorithms introduced in Subsection
D.1
, we briefly describe two supplementary methods without theoretical guarantees here.

Ordinary SPSA.

It is a naive algorithm for the dual tasks. It means to use SPSA for optimization, and simply take the sample average
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\bar{y}_{k}
as the performance estimator. It replaces (
4b
) and (
4c
) by

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\bar{\mu}_{k}+\frac{1}{k+1}\left(\bar{y}_{k}-\bar{\mu}_{k}\right),

\displaystyle\bar{v}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\bar{v}_{k}+\frac{1}{k+1}\left[\left(\bar{y}_{k}-\bar{\mu}_{k}\right)^{2}-\bar{v}_{k}\right],

and the associated normalized estimator is

\frac{\bar{\mu}_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\bar{v}_{n}/n}}.

It has been shown in Subsection
2.2
that this method fails in statistical inference for the optimal performance.

Multi-time-scale four-point method.

Here, we brutely combine the multi-time-scale SA with the four-point method. Intuitively, we can use the four-point method for optimization and then replace (
4b
) and (
4c
) by

\displaystyle\acute{\mu}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\acute{\mu}_{k}+\gamma_{k}\left(\hat{y}_{k}-\acute{\mu}_{k}\right),

\displaystyle\acute{v}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\acute{v}_{k}+\zeta_{k}\left[\left(\hat{y}_{k}-\acute{\mu}_{k}\right)^{2}-\acute{v}_{k}\right],

where
\gamma_{k},~\zeta_{k}
satisfy the conditions mentioned above. The associated normalized estimator is

\frac{(h^{2}-1)}{(h^{2}+1)}\frac{\acute{\mu}_{n}-\mu^{*}}{\sqrt{\zeta_{n}\acute{v}_{n}}}.
