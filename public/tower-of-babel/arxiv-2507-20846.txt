Precision spectral estimation at sub-Hz frequencies: closed-form posteriors and Bayesian noise projection PubID: pubid: Preprint July 28, 2025 Contact information: lorenzo.sala@unitn.it , stefano.vitale@unitn.it
Lorenzo Sala 1,2 , Stefano Vitale 1

Abstract
We present a Bayesian method for estimating spectral quantities in multivariate Gaussian time series. The approach, based on periodograms and Wishart statistics, yields closed-form expressions at any given frequency for the marginal posterior distributions of the individual power spectral densities, the pairwise coherence, and the multiple coherence, as well as for the joint posterior distribution of the full cross-spectral density matrix. In the context of noise projection—where one series is modeled as a linear combination of filtered versions of the others, plus a background component—the method also provides closed-form posteriors for both the susceptibilities, i.e., the filter transfer functions, and the power spectral density of the background. Originally developed for the analysis of the data from the European Space Agency’s LISA Pathfinder mission, the method is particularly well-suited to very-low-frequency data, where long observation times preclude averaging over large sets of periodograms, which would otherwise allow these to be treated as approximately normally distributed.



I
Introduction

Spectral analysis using Welch’s method
[
1
,
2
]
is the most common approach to noise characterization. This method estimates power spectral densities (PSD) and cross-spectral densities (CPSD) of multivariate noise time series from their properly normalized discrete Fourier transforms, known as periodograms.

To enhance estimate precision and quantify uncertainty, Welch’s method divides the time series into
M
equal-length possibly overlapping segments, generating
M
periodogram samples. A common frequentist approach estimates the PSD and CPSD by averaging these samples, with uncertainty proportional to the standard deviation of the mean. This relies on the central limit theorem, assuming that averaging rapidly yields Gaussian statistics suitable for confidence level predictions.

However, spectral resolution decreases with
M
, and in many applications, particularly those at very low frequency,
M
must remain small, sometimes even
M=1
. As
M
decreases, the Gaussianity assumption becomes increasingly inaccurate, leading, for instance, to paradoxical outcomes such as non-negligible probabilities for negative PSD values. At
M=1
, the frequentist approach becomes entirely infeasible. In such cases, Bayesian inference, free from these limitations, remains the only viable approach.

We encountered this situation while analyzing data from the LISA Pathfinder mission
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. The mission’s objective was to precisely measure the noise spectrum of force disturbances acting on two nominally freely falling test masses in space, reaching acceleration levels as low as a few
\mathrm{fm}
and frequencies down to approximately
20\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro Hz}
.

Unlike typical spectral estimation applications, where the goal is to extract a signal from a noisy stochastic process, LISA Pathfinder aimed to measure the noise itself with the highest possible accuracy, particularly at the lowest achievable frequencies.

While reviewing the literature for a consistent and practical Bayesian approach suited to our needs, we found that the fundamental principles had long been established. However, we could not find a detailed, practical method applicable to our data processing, leading us to develop one independently.

We applied this method to the data analysis of LISA Pathfinder in Refs.
[
3
,
6
]
, and briefly summarized its main features in the appendices of the second paper. Here, we provide a detailed description of the method, discussing its foundations, deriving key procedures rigorously, and presenting quantitative evidence of its validity through numerical simulations.

The paper is organized as follows: in
Section

II
we define the key experimental quantities of multi-variate time series and derive their likelihood under the Gaussian data hypothesis; in
Section

III
we build the Bayesian posteriors for all the related spectral quantities; in
Section

IV
we discuss the case of noise projection, that is the case where one series is modeled as a linear combination of filtered versions of the others, plus a background component of which one wants to estimate the spectrum; finally in
Section

V

II
Periodograms, key periodogram functions and their likelihood

In this section, we recall a few basic concepts and results that we will use in our Bayesian inference method. We assume that data are Gaussian for the purpose of the our analysis, something we found to be quantitatively true in the case of LISA Pathfinder
[
3
]

II-A

Basic definitions and nomenclature

In our approach, we assume that we have acquired, synchronously and with sampling time
T
, the time series of
p

x_{i}[n]
, with
1\leq i\leq p

The joint statistics of these samples is fully contained in the mean values of their products

\langle x_{i}[n]x_{j}[m]\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Sigma_{i,j}(f)e^{i2\pi(m-n)fT}{d}f,

(1)

Our goal is to infer each element of

independently at each frequency, without assuming any functional dependence on

The main tool for such an inference is the

N

(2)

the coefficients of a suitable tapering window. Since

X_{i}[k]
is Gaussian, complex, and zero-mean. Key for the inference are the complex mean values:

\begin{split}&\langle X_{i}[k]X_{j}^{*}[k^{\prime}]\rangle=\frac{T}{N}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Sigma_{i,j}(f)\times\\
&\times\tilde{w}\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}k-2\pi fT\right)\tilde{w}^{*}\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}k^{\prime}-2\pi fT\right)\,df\end{split}

(3)

with
\tilde{w}(\phi)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w[n]e^{-i\phi n}
the Fourier sequence transform of the tapering window

.

If, ideally, one could choose

such that

\begin{split}\tilde{w}&\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}k-2\pi fT\right)\tilde{w}^{*}\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}k^{\prime}-2\pi fT\right)=\\
&=2\pi N\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\delta\left(\frac{2\pi}{N}k-2\pi fT\right),\end{split}

In reality,

-periodic function with a central lobe at

The width of the central lobe depends on the choice of the window, but is always of the form

3

Thus,

is an estimator of
\Sigma_{i,j}(f)
averaged over the band

.

From now on, with
\bm{\Sigma}(f)
, unless otherwise specified, we indicate this averaged version of the CPSD matrix.

As usual in statistics, the precision of the estimator can be increased by averaging over repeated measurements. To this aim, Welch’s method prescribes to split the available time series into
M
segments
1
1

1

Thus, in summary, the matrices

III
Bayesian inference for spectral quantities

We now use the results from the previous section to perform Bayesian inference of the theoretical distribution underlying a set of observed spectral quantities.

Our starting point is the likelihood in
Equation

11
, which, when multiplied by an appropriate prior distribution
p(\bm{\Sigma})
, yields the Bayesian posterior for the theoretical CPSD matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
. Since
\bm{\Sigma}
is the only free parameter in the sample distribution, this posterior fully captures the statistical information of the stochastic processes under investigation.

The key step in this approach is selecting a suitable prior. Before addressing the general case for
p>1
, we begin with the simpler, yet illuminating case of
p=1
, the inference of the PSD of a single stochastic process, which provides valuable insight for the general case.

III-A

Inference of the PSD for a single stochastic process

When
p=1

Equation

11

Equation

12

S

We have considered three options.

1.

The uniform, non-informative prior
p(S)=\Theta(S)
, with
\Theta(S)
the Heaviside theta function. With this choice, the posterior distribution of

conditional on the observation of

S|\Pi\sim\text{inv}\Gamma(M-1,M\Pi)

(19)

\text{inv}\Gamma

2.

Jeffreys non-informative prior
[
14
]
. Calculating the Fisher information

from Eq. (
12

p(S)\propto\sqrt{\mathcal{I}(S)}\propto 1/S

As

and corresponds then to a complete lack of prior knowledge even on the order of magnitude of

, a rather realistic description of the situation in most cases of noise calibration.

Note that the main property of the Jeffreys prior is the invariance under re-parametrization. Thus, the switch

does not change the prior probability of an event.

With the Jeffreys prior:

S|\Pi\sim\text{inv}\Gamma(M,M\Pi)

(20)

3.

For comparison we have also considered a prior

This distribution has a few problems. First, it carries some bias. Though defining what bias is for a matrix distribution may be difficult, it is worth inspecting the posterior predictive distribution of a future observation
\tilde{\bm{W}}
conditional on the observation of
\bm{W}
.

We find that
\bm{W}^{-1}\cdot\tilde{\bm{W}}
is
\bm{W}^{-1}\cdot\tilde{\bm{W}}\sim\mathbb{C}\text{B}_{p}^{II}(M,M-p)
with
\mathbb{C}\text{B}_{p}^{II}(a,b)
the matrix-variate type-2 complex Beta distribution
[
16
]
.

Ref.
[
16
]
shows that
\langle\bm{W}^{-1}\cdot\tilde{\bm{W}}\rangle=\mathbf{I}_{p}M/(M-2p)
with
\mathbf{I}_{p}
the
p\times p
identity matrix, while for an unbiased estimation one would expect
\langle\bm{W}^{-1}\cdot\tilde{\bm{W}}\rangle=\mathbf{I}_{p}
. Note that this mean value bias depends on the number of series considered together and becomes infinite when
M=2p
.

That such dependence of the bias on
p
is paradoxical is well illustrated by the marginal distribution of the diagonal elements. Indeed, the marginal distribution of
\Sigma_{ii}
, that is the estimate of the PSD of the
i
-th time series
S_{i}
, can be calculated
[
15
]
to be
S_{i}|\Pi_{ii}=\Sigma_{ii}|\Pi_{ii}\sim\text{inv}\Gamma(M-2p+1,M\Pi_{ii})
, a distribution only defined for
M\geq 2p
, and different from that one gets by considering the
i
-th series alone,
S_{i}|\Pi\sim\text{inv}\Gamma(M-1,M\Pi_{ii})
.

Thus, just assuming there are other
p-1
series that may be correlated with the one under study would change the inferred posterior for
S_{i}
, and would induce a bias increasing with
p
.

The situation is slightly better for the Jeffreys prior. This can be calculated to be
[
17
]

p(\bm{\Sigma})=\lvert\bm{\Sigma}\rvert^{-p}
yielding

\bm{\Sigma}|\bm{W}\sim\mathcal{CW}^{-1}(\bm{W},M).

(24)

With this choice,
\langle\bm{W}^{-1}\cdot\tilde{\bm{W}}\rangle=\mathbf{I}_{p}M/(M-p)
. Still
S_{i}|\Pi=\Sigma_{ii}|\Pi\sim\text{inv}\Gamma(M-p+1,M\Pi_{ii})
, instead of the almost unbiased posterior
S_{i}|\Pi\sim\text{inv}\Gamma(M,M\Pi_{ii})
that one gets from the Jeffreys prior in the
p=1
case. Thus, the bias is somewhat reduced but still paradoxically depends on
p
.

The prior that gives the marginal posterior
\Sigma_{ii}|\Pi\sim\text{inv}\Gamma(M,M\Pi_{ii})
, consistent with that estimated from the
i
-th series alone and the Jeffreys prior, is
p(\bm{\Sigma})=\lvert\bm{\Sigma}\rvert^{-2p+1}
. For this:

\bm{\Sigma}|\bm{W}\sim\mathcal{CW}^{-1}(\bm{W},M+p-1)

(25)

Such prior falls within the class on non-informative priors for
\bm{Q}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
discussed in
[
17
]
,
p(\bm{Q})\propto\lvert\bm{Q}\rvert^{-K}\mathrm{etr}\left[-\bm{Q}\mathbf{\Lambda}\right]
. Indeed, remembering that the Jacobian of the transformation
\bm{Q}\to\bm{\Sigma}
is
\lvert\bm{\Sigma}\rvert^{-2p}

[
15
]
, this prior corresponds to
K=-1
and
\mathbf{\Lambda}=0
, that is
p(\bm{Q})\propto\lvert\bm{Q}\rvert^{-1}
.

Note that for the posterior in
Equation

25

\langle\bm{W}^{-1}\cdot\tilde{\bm{W}}\rangle=\mathbf{I}_{p}M/(M-1)
. As the distribution only holds for
M>1
, and actually the entire multidimensional Bayesian inference only holds for
M\geq p
, the bias remains smaller than
p/(p-1)
and independent of
p
.

Based on the discussion above, we recommend the
p(\bm{Q})\propto\lvert\bm{Q}\rvert^{-1}
prior when in need of inferring the whole CPSD at a given frequency.

When only particular functions of the CPSD are needed, as in some of the following sections, the proper priors will be formulated in terms of those functions and not of the whole CPSD.

IV
Noise projection and time series decorrelation

We now consider the case where the
p
-variate stochastic process consists of a “main” process
x(t)
and
r=p-1
“disturbances”
y_{i}
, with
1\leq i\leq r
, modeled as

x(t)=x_{0}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\alpha_{i}(t-t^{\prime})\,y_{i}(t^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

(28)

Here,
x_{0}(t)
—the ‘residual’—is a process independent of the
y_{i}(t)
’s. We refer to the Fourier transforms of the functions
\alpha_{i}(t)
, denoted by
\alpha_{i}(f)
, as the ‘susceptibilities’.

Our goal is to estimate the PSD
S_{x_{0}x_{0}}
of the residual
x_{0}(t)
, and the susceptibilities
\alpha_{i}(f)
. This task, often referred to as
noise projection
or
noise decorrelation
, is common in what is known as
noise hunting
, which involves identifying the source of noise in the main data series of a physical apparatus by examining correlations with other independently measured disturbances that may have coupled into the primary measurement. The noise hunting carried out for LISA Pathfinder was no exception, and it required us to develop the approach described below.

We consider two cases:

1.

When
{\alpha}_{i}(t)
is a general function. This allows estimation of both
\alpha_{i}(f)
and
S_{x_{0}x_{0}}(f)
independently at each frequency.

2.

When
{\alpha}_{i}(t)=\alpha_{i}\delta(t)
, with
\alpha_{i}
constant. In this case,
\alpha_{i}(f)=\alpha_{i}
becomes frequency-independent, and thus a global parameter of the estimate, while
S_{x_{0}x_{0}}(f)
remains dependent on frequency.

It is convenient for the rest of the discussion to make the following block partition of
\bm{\Sigma}
:

\bm{\Sigma}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}S_{xx}&\bm{S}_{xy}\\
\hline\cr\bm{S}_{xy}^{\dagger}&\bm{S}_{yy}\end{array}\right)

(29)

where
S_{xx}
is just the PSD of
x(t)
,
\bm{S}_{yy}
is the
r\times r
CPSD matrix of the disturbances, and
\bm{S}_{xy}
is the
1\times r
vector of the CPSD between
x(t)
and all the disturbances
y_{i}(t)
. Note that, for clarity, we have omitted the explicit dependence of all quantities on frequency. We continue to do so in the rest.

Within the model in
Equation

28
,

\begin{split}S_{xx}&=S_{x_{0}x_{0}}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}^{*}S_{y_{i},y_{j}}=\\
&=S_{x_{0}x_{0}}+\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{S}_{yy}\cdot\bm{\alpha}^{\dagger}\end{split}

(30)

where we have introduced the
r
-long vector
\bm{\alpha}
with components
\alpha_{i}
. Furthermore,

\bm{S}_{xy}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}S_{y_{i},y_{j}}=\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{S}_{yy}

(31)

that is
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{S}_{xy}\cdot\bm{S}_{yy}^{-1}
, a relation that will be useful in the following.
3
3

3

This relation is exactly true only if, at a given frequency
f
,
\bm{S}_{xy}(f)
and
\bm{S}_{yy}(f)
are the exact values at
f
and not those smoothed over the spectral window (see
Equation

3
) that we are using here. This spectral smoothing may bias the estimation of
\bm{\alpha}(f)
should it have a strong dependency on frequency. Such bias can be mitigated by properly reducing the width of the spectral window.

Note that
S_{x_{0}x_{0}}
is the Schur complement
\bm{\Sigma}/\bm{S}_{yy}
of
\bm{S}_{yy}
in the matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
:

S_{x_{0}x_{0}}=S_{xx}-\bm{S}_{xy}\cdot\bm{S}_{yy}\cdot\bm{S}_{xy}^{\dagger}=\bm{\Sigma}/\bm{S}_{yy}=1/(\bm{\Sigma}^{-1})_{11}

(32)

and that the multiple coherence is

R^{2}=1-\frac{S_{x_{0}x_{0}}}{S_{xx}}

(33)

i.e., the fraction of the PSD of
x(t)
contributed by the disturbances, a useful quantity one wants to estimate.

IV-A

Inference of susceptibilities, residuals, and CPSD of disturbances in the general case

Our starting point is a key re-parametrization of the sample distribution in
Equation

11
. For the sake of such re-parametrization, we need to introduce, in analogy with
Equation

29
, the block partition of
\bm{W}
and
\bm{\Pi}

\bm{W}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}W_{xx}&\bm{W}_{xy}\\
\hline\cr\bm{W}_{xy}^{\dagger}&\bm{W}_{yy}\end{array}\right)=M\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\Pi_{xx}&\bm{\Pi}_{xy}\\
\hline\cr\bm{\Pi}_{xy}^{\dagger}&\bm{\Pi}_{yy}\end{array}\right)

(34)

Two functions of
\bm{W}
that we also need in the following are:

1.

the ‘observed’ residual noise PSD
\Pi_{0}
that we define from

\Pi_{0}=\frac{1}{M-r}\times\frac{1}{(\bm{W}^{-1})_{xx}}\equiv\frac{1}{M-r}\times W_{0}

(35)

with
(\bm{W}^{-1})_{xx}
the upper-left
1\times 1
block of
\bm{W}^{-1}
;

2.

the ‘observed’ susceptibility vector

\bm{\alpha}_{0}=\bm{W}_{xy}\cdot\bm{W}_{yy}^{-1}

(36)

In Appendix
A
, where we show that the sample distribution in

11

\begin{split}\hskip-12.80373ptp(\bm{W}|&S_{x_{0}x_{0}},\bm{\alpha},\bm{S}_{yy})\propto\frac{1}{S_{x_{0}x_{0}}^{M}}\exp\left[-\frac{W_{0}}{S_{x_{0}x_{0}}}\right]\times\\
&\times\exp\left[-\frac{(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{0})\cdot\bm{W}_{yy}\cdot(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{0})^{\dagger}}{S_{x_{0}x_{0}}}\right]\times\\
&\times\frac{1}{|\bm{S}_{yy}|^{M}}\mathrm{etr}\left[-\bm{S}_{yy}^{-1}\bm{W}_{yy}\right]\end{split}

(37)

Thus, the distribution splits into two independent parts, one depending on
S_{x_{0}x_{0}}
and
\bm{\alpha}
but not on
\bm{S}_{yy}
, and one that only depends on
\bm{S}_{yy}
. Thus, if one select a prior of the kind
p(S_{x_{0}x_{0}},\bm{\alpha})\times p(\bm{S}_{yy})
, then the posterior also splits into the product of the joint posterior for
S_{x_{0}x_{0}}
and
\bm{\alpha}
, with the posterior for
\bm{S}_{yy}
alone. The estimate of the latter reduce to the estimate of the CPSD that we have already treated. From now on we focus then on the estimate of
S_{x_{0}x_{0}}
and
\bm{\alpha}
only.

The most realistic, least informative prior for
S_{x_{0}x_{0}}
, as for all other PSDs we have met, is again
p(S_{x_{0}x_{0}})\propto 1/S_{x_{0}x_{0}}
independently of the value of
\bm{\alpha}
.

On the other hand, the components of
\bm{\alpha}
are, in the language of statics, location parameters. If they can be assumed independent of each other, then, for each of them, the least informative prior is just
p(\alpha_{i})=1
.

From the above consideration, it follows that a sound non-informative joint prior for
S_{x_{0}x_{0}}
, and
\bm{\alpha}
is
p(S_{x_{0}x_{0}},\bm{\alpha})=1/S_{x_{0}x_{0}}
, with which their properly normalized joint posterior becomes:

\begin{split}p(&S_{x_{0}x_{0}},\bm{\alpha}|\bm{W})=\\
&=\frac{(W_{0})^{M-r}}{\Gamma(M-r)S_{x_{0}x_{0}}^{M-r+1}}\exp\left[-\frac{W_{0}}{S_{x_{0}x_{0}}}\right]\times\\
&\times\frac{\left\lvert\frac{\bm{W}_{y,y}}{S_{x_{0}x_{0}}}\right\rvert}{\pi^{r}}\exp\left[-(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{0})\cdot\frac{\bm{W}_{y,y}}{S_{x_{0}x_{0}}}\cdot(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{0})^{\dagger}\right].\\
\end{split}

(38)

This is equivalent to stating that:

S_{x_{0}x_{0}}\left\lvert\right.\bm{W}\sim\text{inv}\Gamma(M-r,W_{0})

(39)

and

\bm{\alpha}\left\lvert\right.\bm{W},S_{x_{0}x_{0}}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\bm{\alpha}_{0},S_{x_{0},x_{0}}\bm{W}_{yy}^{-1})

(40)

with
\mathcal{CN}(S_{x_{0},x_{0}}\bm{W}_{yy}^{-1},\bm{\alpha}_{0})
the complex, circularly symmetric
r
-variate Gaussian distribution, with covariance matrix
S_{x_{0},x_{0}}\bm{W}_{yy}^{-1}
and mean value
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
.

Note that the marginal distribution of
S_{x_{0}x_{0}}
is already given by
Equation

39

\bm{\alpha}
may be obtained by integrating

38

.

By performing this integration we get that

\bm{\alpha}\sim ct_{r}\left(\bm{\alpha}_{0},W_{0}\bm{W}_{yy}^{-1},M-r\right),

(41)

the latest being the complex multivariate t-distribution for an
r
-long complex vector, with mean value
\bm{\alpha}_{0}

W_{0}\bm{W}_{yy}^{-1}
and
M-r

This means that the real and imaginary parts of
\bm{\alpha}

\bm{\alpha}_{R}\sim t_{2r}\left(\bm{\alpha}_{0,R},\,\bm{\Omega},2(M-r)\right),

with

\bm{\alpha}_{0,R}=\left(\begin{matrix}\operatorname{Re}\bm{\alpha}_{0}\\
\operatorname{Im}\bm{\alpha}_{0}\end{matrix}\right)
, and a scale matrix given by

\bm{\Omega}=\frac{1}{2}\,\Pi_{0}\,\left(\begin{matrix}\operatorname{Re}\bm{W}_{yy}&\operatorname{Im}\bm{W}_{yy}\\
-\operatorname{Im}\bm{W}_{yy}&\operatorname{Re}\bm{W}_{yy}\end{matrix}\right)^{-1}

(43)

From this joint marginal distribution, we also get the marginal distributions of the single components of
\bm{\alpha}_{R}
that are univariate
t

degrees of freedom
[
18
]
, and scale parameter given by the corresponding element in
\bm{\Omega}
.

Note that the covariance of the elements of
\bm{\alpha}_{R}
,
((M-r)/(M-r-1))\bm{\Omega}
, decreases with decreasing
\Pi_{0}
, the PSD of residuals. This is expected as, for a given value of the total PSD, a small
\Pi_{0}
implies a large contribution of the disturbances and then a large signal-to-noise ratio for the components of
\bm{\alpha}_{R}
.

It is also straightforward to calculate that
\bm{\Omega}\propto M^{-1}
, so that this signal-to-noise ratio, as expected, also increases with increasing averaging.

The model discussed so far assumes the disturbances
y_{i}(t)
are measured with negligible readout noise. It is therefore important to consider, before concluding this section, the consequences of applying the method when such noise is in reality not negligible.

Let us consider first the estimate of
S_{x_{0}x_{0}}
. Our method in reality estimates
1/\Sigma_{11}^{-1}
, whatever the detailed form of
\bm{\Sigma}
is. Indeed our starting point is
1/W_{11}^{-1}
, whose sampling distribution is
1/W_{11}^{-1}\sim\Gamma(M-r,1/\Sigma_{11}^{-1})
.

From this, and the distribution in
Equation

20
, one can derive the sampling distribution of our Bayesian estimate for
S_{x_{0},x_{0}}
. We find that this distribution is
\beta^{\prime}\left(M-r,M-r,1/\Sigma_{11,\text{true}}^{-1}\right)
, a distribution whose median is equal to
1/\Sigma_{11}^{-1}
, and a relative uncertainty that only depends on
M-r
. This confirms that the methods gives an unbiased estimate of
1/\Sigma_{11}^{-1}
.

In the presence of readout noise,
1/\Sigma_{11}^{-1}\neq S_{x_{0}x_{0}}
, as the true form of
\bm{\Sigma}
is not that in
Equation

29
. Indeed, in the simplest model of additive noise, the measured disturbance is
y_{i}(t)+n_{i}(t)
, with
n_{i}(t)
a zero mean stationary process independent of all the
y_{i}
’s. In this case, the lower diagonal block of
\bm{\Sigma}
becomes
\bm{S}_{yy}+\bm{S}_{n}
with
\bm{S}_{n}
a diagonal matrix whose generic element
S_{n_{i},n_{i}}
is the PSD of
n_{i}(t)
.

Working out the formula for
1/\Sigma_{11}^{-1}
in the general case is a bit cumbersome. It becomes particularly simple if also
\bm{S}_{yy}
is diagonal, that is, if the disturbances are mutually uncorrelated. One can readily calculate that in this case

1/\Sigma_{11}^{-1}=S_{x_{0}x_{0}}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert^{2}\frac{S_{y_{i},y_{i}}S_{n_{i}n_{i}}}{S_{y_{i},y_{i}}+S_{n_{i}n_{i}}}

(44)

with
S_{y_{i}y_{i}}
the PSD of
y_{i}(t)
. One can recognize that in the limit of dominant readout noise
1/\Sigma_{11}^{-1}\to S_{xx}
. In other words, a dominant readout noise, as expected, completely obscures any correlation between
x(t)
and the
y
’s.

Furthermore, within the same simplification of uncorrelated disturbances, the product
\sum_{j=2}^{p}\Sigma_{1,j}\Sigma_{j,k}^{-1}
, with
k>1
which, in the noiseless limit, is
(\bm{S}_{xy}\cdot\bm{S}_{yy}^{-1})_{k-1}=\alpha_{k-1}
, becomes instead

\sum_{j=2}^{p}\Sigma_{1,j}\Sigma_{j,k}^{-1}=\frac{S_{y_{k-1}y_{k-1}}}{S_{y_{k-1},y_{k-1}}+S_{n_{k-1}n_{k-1}}}\alpha_{k-1}.

(45)

Thus, in the presence of significant readout noise, our method overestimates the PSD of the residuals, underestimates the absolute value of the susceptibility, and should only be used for an upper limit on
S_{x_{0},x_{0}}
.

]
. To further test its validity, in particular with respect to bias, we have also studied a simulated case. This is discussed in the next section.

(46)

with
T=1\text{\,s}
the sampling time,
f_{1}=0.10\text{\,Hz}
and
f_{2}=0.11\text{\,Hz}
the two roll-off frequencies, and
f_{0}=1\text{\,mHz}
the cross-over frequency between the tail and the plateau.

The disturbances are in the form
z_{i}(t)=c_{lf,i}z_{lf,i}(t)+c_{hf,i}z_{hf,i}(t)+c_{i}z_{0}(t)
, where all the time series on the right hand side are Gaussian, zero-mean and mutually independent, and the coefficient
c_{lf,i}
,
c_{hf,i}
and
c_{i}
are real and randomly selected.

The
z_{lf,i}(t)
and
z_{0}(t)
share the same PSD

S_{lf}(f)=\left|\frac{1-e^{-T/\tau_{1}}e^{-i2\pi f_{0}T}}{1-e^{-T/\tau_{1}}e^{-i2\pi fT}}\frac{1-e^{-T/\tau_{2}}e^{-i2\pi f_{0}T}}{1-e^{-T/\tau_{2}}e^{-i2\pi fT}}\right|^{2}

(47)

with
\tau_{1}=1.0\times 10^{5}\text{\,s}
and
\tau_{2}=1.1\times 10^{5}\text{\,s}
. For
f\gg 1/\tau_{1},1/\tau_{2}
this PSD amounts to a
\propto 1/f^{4}
low frequency tail with unit value at
f=f_{0}
.

The
z_{hf,i}(t)
have PSD

S_{hf}(f)=\left|\frac{1-e^{-T/\tau}e^{-i2\pi f_{0}T}}{1-e^{-T/\tau}e^{-i2\pi fT}}\right|^{2}

(48)

again a
\propto 1/f^{2}
tail with unit value at
f=f_{0}
.
The presence of the shared series
z_{0}(t)
induces correlation among the
z
’s with CPSD
S_{z_{i},z_{j}}(f)=c_{i}c_{j}S_{lf}(f)
.

All PSDs above must be intended to be zero for
\lvert f\rvert\geq 1/(2T)
. With this prescription, they can be read as discrete time Fourier transforms of the corresponding discrete time autocorrelation, and their shape allows a straightforward implementation as auto-regressive moving average (ARMA) stochastic processes.

IV-C

The case of real frequency-independent susceptibilities

In many practical circumstances, one can safely assume that
\bm{\alpha}
is a real frequency-independent vector. If this is the case,
\bm{\alpha}
becomes a common parameter in the sampling distribution of the
\bm{W}
’s at all frequencies. Thus, to build up a posterior for
\bm{\alpha}
, one needs to consider the joint likelihood of all the
\bm{W}
’s for a given value of
\bm{\alpha}
. We anticipate that, in this case, we do not get a closed form posterior distribution, but a useful form that can be integrated by using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo approach.

To build this posterior, let us call
\bm{W}_{i}
the sample at frequency
f_{i}
—obtained by averaging over
M_{i}
periodograms—and similarly let’s indicate the corresponding theoretical quantities with
S_{x_{0},x_{0},i}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{yy,i}
.

Let also assume that
f_{i}
and
f_{i+i}
are sufficiently far apart that
\bm{W}_{i}
and
\bm{W}_{i+1}
may be treated as independent, so that
the likelihood of the
\bm{W}_{i}
is just the product of their marginal likelihoods
\prod_{i=1}^{N_{f}}p\left(\bm{W}_{i}|S_{x_{0}x_{0},i},\bm{\alpha},\bm{S}_{yy,i}\right)
, with
N_{f}
the number of considered frequencies.

In addition, we assume that
S_{x_{0},x_{0},i}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{yy,i}
have independent prior distributions, that the prior for
S_{x_{0},x_{0},i}
is, as before,
\propto 1/S_{x_{0},x_{0},i}
, and that the priors for the component of
\bm{\alpha}
are, again as before, independent and uniform.

Using also the fact that, for real
\bm{\alpha}
,
Equation

60
gives
W^{\prime}_{x_{0}x_{0},i}=W_{xx,i}-2\bm{\alpha}\cdot\operatorname{Re}(\bm{W}_{xy,i})+\bm{\alpha}\cdot\operatorname{Re}(\bm{W}_{yy,i})\cdot\bm{\alpha}
, we get for the logarithm
\Lambda
of the joint posterior of all parameters

\begin{split}\Lambda=&-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{f}}(M_{i}+1)\log(S_{x_{0},x_{0},i})-\\
&-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{f}}\frac{W_{xx,i}}{S_{x_{0}x_{0},i}}+2\bm{\alpha}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N_{f}}\frac{\operatorname{Re}(\bm{W}_{xy,i})}{S_{x_{0}x_{0},i}}-\\
&-\bm{\alpha}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{f}}\frac{\operatorname{Re}(\bm{W}_{yy,i})}{S_{x_{0}x_{0},i}}\right)\cdot\bm{\alpha}\end{split}

(50)

plus an independent term that only contains
\bm{S}_{yy,i}
and that is not used here.

The posterior in
Equation

50
can be used for the MCMC estimate of the parameter posterior distribution. We have used this method extensively within the data processing of LISA Pathfinder
[
3
]
. We have also applied it to simulated noise with the same properties as that discussed in
Section

IV-B
, except that here the disturbances have not been filtered, that is,
y_{i}(t)=z_{i}(t)
. With this prescription, the susceptibilities are just the real, frequency-independent numbers
\alpha_{i}=n_{i}
.

An example of the result of such a simulation is presented in
Figures

and
11
.

Before closing this section it is worth noticing that one advantage of this simultaneous fit to the data at all frequencies, is that one can include also data at very low frequency where the condition
M_{i}\geq p
may be violated. This is shown as follows.

First, the distribution of a singular
\bm{W}_{i}
obtained from
M_{i}<p
periodograms, is the singular complex Wishart distribution
[
20
]
:

p\left(\bm{W}_{i}\big|\bm{\Sigma}_{i}\right)=\frac{\pi^{M_{i}(M_{i}-p)}\left|\bm{\Lambda}_{i}\right|^{M_{i}-p}}{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{p}(M_{i})\left|\bm{\Sigma}_{i}\right|^{M_{i}}}\ \mathrm{etr}\left[-\bm{\Sigma}_{i}^{-1}\bm{W}_{i}\right],

(51)

with
|\bm{\Lambda}_{i}|
, the product of the non-zero eigenvalues of
\bm{W}_{i}

The dependence of the likelihood in
Equation

51
on
\bm{\Sigma}
and
M
is the same as that in
Equation

11
. Thus the difference between the two likelihoods makes no difference for the derivation of the posterior.

Furthermore, the stability of the posterior in
Equation

50
, requires that the matrices
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{f}}\frac{\operatorname{Re}(\bm{W}_{xy,i})}{S_{x_{0}x_{0},i}}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{f}}\frac{\operatorname{Re}(\bm{W}_{yy,i})}{S_{x_{0}x_{0},i}}
are full rank, not the individual
\bm{W}_{i}
. As the rank of a sum of positive semi-definite matrices is larger than or equal to the maximum rank of the terms in the sum
[
21
]
, it suffices that just one of the
\bm{W}_{i}
is of full rank to give full rank to both sums.

As, in practice, all the
\bm{W}_{i}
above a certain frequency are full rank, the posterior is well defined even if a few terms at the lowest frequency have
M_{i}<p
.

V
Conclusions

In conclusion, we have presented a set of Bayesian low-bias closed-form posteriors—based on simple and physically meaningful priors—for the most commonly estimated quantities at a given frequency in the spectral analysis of multivariate time series, and in particular in noise projection of physical instruments.

The distributions of some of these priors are available within the main software platforms, which makes the calculation of credible intervals and other statistical quantities particularly simple. For the case of noise projection, we have shown with simulations that the method is capable of retrieving, with negligible bias, a residual whose ASD is orders of magnitude smaller than the part due to the measured disturbances, and we have also investigated the robustness of the method in the presence of readout noise in the disturbance measurement.

Still on noise projection, in addition to the single frequency closed-form prior, we have also presented a simple likelihood to be used in multi-frequency noise-projection in case the susceptibilities may be confidently assumed to be real and frequency independent.

We want to stress again that these results originate from the experience of data processing at very low frequency, from
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro Hz}
to Hz
[
3

, in which, due to length of the required measurement time, only comparatively few periodograms are available, and should be particularly suitable for any situation in which a similar limitation in number of available periodograms may occur.

Finally, as for almost all commonly used results in data processing, also those presented here are based on Gaussian statistics of the time series under processing. The applicability of the result depends then on how much the data may be safely considered Gaussian.

Appendix A
Derivation of noise-projection posterior.

Starting from the complex Wishart distribution
Equation

11
, we derive the joint posterior of the decorrelation parameters in
Equation

38
. This was synthetically discussed in
[
3
]
, and we repeat it here, in more detail, for the reader’s convenience.

We start by defining the block matrix
\bm{U}

\bm{U}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}1&\bm{\alpha}\\
\hline\cr\bm{0}&\mathbb{I}\end{array}\right)

(52)

Where
\mathbb{I}
is the
r\times r
identity matrix.

\bm{U}
performs the linear transformation
x_{0}\rightarrow x
,
y_{i}\rightarrow y_{i}
. Its inverse is obtained from
\bm{U}
, by simply replacing
\bm{\alpha}
with
-\bm{\alpha}
.

A second important block matrix is,

\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}S_{x_{0}x_{0}}&\bm{0}\\
\hline\cr\bm{0}&\bm{S}_{yy}\end{array}\right)

(53)

It is straightforward to show that

\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}\bm{U}^{\dagger}

(54)

where
\bm{U}^{\dagger}
is the conjugate transpose of matrix
\bm{U}
.

As the determinant of
\bm{U}
is
\lvert\bm{U}\rvert=1
, then

|\bm{\Sigma}|=|\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}|=S_{x_{0}x_{0}}\,|\bm{S}_{yy}|.

(55)

The exponent in
Equation

11
can be rewritten in terms of
\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}
as:

\mathrm{etr}\left[-\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{W}\right]=\mathrm{etr}\left[-\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime-1}\bm{W}^{\prime}\right].

(56)

with
\bm{W}^{\prime}
defined as
\bm{W}^{\prime}=\bm{U}^{-1}\bm{W}(\bm{U}^{\dagger})^{-1}
.

As

{\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime}}^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}1/S_{x_{0}x_{0}}&\bm{0}\\
\hline\cr\bm{0}&\bm{S}_{yy}^{-1}\end{array}\right)

(57)

by using the block decomposition

\bm{W}^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}W^{\prime}_{x_{0}x_{0}}&\bm{W}^{\prime}_{x_{0}y}\\
\hline\cr(\bm{W}^{\prime}_{x_{0}y})^{\dagger}&\bm{W}^{\prime}_{yy}\end{array}\right)

(58)

we get

\begin{split}&\mathrm{etr}\left[-\bm{\Sigma}^{\prime-1}\bm{W}^{\prime}\right]=e^{-\frac{W^{\prime}_{x_{0}x_{0}}}{S_{x_{0}x_{0}}}}\mathrm{etr}\left[-\bm{S}_{yy}^{-1}\cdot\bm{W}^{\prime}_{yy}\right]=\\
&=e^{-\frac{W^{\prime}_{x_{0}x_{0}}}{S_{x_{0}x_{0}}}}\mathrm{etr}\left[-\bm{S}_{yy}^{-1}\cdot\bm{W}_{yy}\right]\end{split}

(59)

where, in the last term, we have used the straightforward result
\bm{W}^{\prime}_{yy}=\bm{W}_{yy}
.

W_{x_{0}x_{0}}^{\prime}
in
Equation

59
may be written as:

\begin{split}W^{\prime}_{x_{0}x_{0}}&=W_{xx}-\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{W}_{xy}^{\dagger}-\bm{W}_{xy}\cdot\bm{\alpha}^{\dagger}+\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{W}_{yy}\cdot\bm{\alpha}^{\dagger}\\
\end{split}

(60)

By introducing

\bm{\alpha}_{0}=\bm{W}_{xy}\cdot\bm{W}_{yy}^{-1}.

(61)

we get

\begin{split}&\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{W}_{xy}^{\dagger}=\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{W}_{yy}\cdot\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{\dagger}\\
&\bm{W}_{xy}\cdot\bm{\alpha}^{\dagger}=\bm{\alpha}_{0}\cdot\bm{W}_{yy}\cdot\bm{\alpha}^{\dagger}\end{split}

(62)

so that

\begin{split}W^{\prime}_{x_{0}x_{0}}&=W_{xx}+(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{0})\cdot\bm{W}_{yy}\cdot(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{0})^{\dagger}-\\
&-\bm{W}_{xy}\cdot\bm{W}_{yy}^{-1}\cdot\bm{W}_{x,y}^{\dagger}.\end{split}

(63)

But

=W_{xx}-\bm{W}_{xy}\cdot\bm{W}_{yy}^{-1}\cdot\bm{W}_{x,y}^{\dagger}=\bm{W}/\bm{W}_{yy}=\frac{1}{(\bm{W}^{-1})_{1,1}},

(64)

thus finally

\begin{split}W^{\prime}_{x_{0}x_{0}}=\frac{1}{(\bm{W}^{-1})_{1,1}}+(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{0})\cdot\bm{W}_{yy}\cdot(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{0})^{\dagger}\end{split}

(65)

As
W^{\prime}_{x_{0}x_{0}}
is independent of
\bm{S}_{yy}
, the probability density in
Equation

11
splits in the product of two pieces, one that contains only
\bm{\alpha}
and
S_{x_{0}x_{0}}
, and one that contains only
\bm{S}_{yy}
.

Using
Equation

65
, and the definition in
Equation

35
, we get:

\begin{split}p(\bm{W}|&S_{x_{0}x_{0}},\bm{\alpha},\bm{S}_{yy})=\frac{\left|\bm{W}\right|^{M-p}}{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{p}(M)}\times\\
&\frac{1}{S_{x_{0}x_{0}}^{M}}e^{\frac{(M-r)\Pi_{0}}{S_{x_{0}x_{0}}}}\times e^{-\frac{(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{0})\cdot\bm{W}_{yy}\cdot(\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}_{0})^{\dagger}}{S_{x_{0}x_{0}}}}\times\\
&\times\frac{1}{|\bm{S}_{yy}|^{M}}\mathrm{etr}\left[-\bm{S}_{yy}^{-1}\bm{W}_{yy}\right]\end{split}

(66)

This is used for
Equation

37
.
