New insights into the charge property at active sites boost PMS activation over LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3
Authors: Danhua Jiao, Ying Li, Xiaodong Cai, Tianxue Li, Wenjuan Lu, Weiwei Xu, Qizhao Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Perovskite-based materials have become a new direction for peroxymonosulfate (PMS) activation in pollutants degradation. However, the mechanism towards PMS activation remains insufficient. In this paper, a series of LaMxMn1−xO3 materials with B-site doping were developed by sol-gel self-propagating combustion method. The prepared LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system exhibited the desired catalytic activity, almost 100% removal efficiency of TC can be achieved within 50 min and 98% initial catalytic activity could be maintained after 5 cycles. Quenching experiments and EPR tests together certified the vital roles of •O2− and 1O2 during the TC degradation procedure. Simultaneously, the catalytic performance of the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system was examined in relation to varying pH values, coexisting chemicals, and water conditions. DFT calculations demonstrated an increased electron concentration in the reactive sites after Fe doping. In addition, LaFexMn1−xO3 at the electron-rich state increased the bond strength with O atoms and encouraged the adsorption/desorption of intermediates in the PMS activation process. The aforementioned outcomes showed the possibility for application in intricate, realistic aquatic environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81108-4.

Introduction

Water contamination has grown to be a serious and urgent issue in recent decades1,2. Antibiotic overuse (e.g., ciprofloxacin, amoxicillin, tetracycline hydrochloride, sulfamethoxazole, etc.) not only increases microbiological resistance to antibiotics but can also induce endocrine disruption, chronic toxicity, and other problems in target species. It is now critical to develop a cost-effective and efficient antibiotics treatment3–7.

Advanced oxidation technologies (AOPs) based on peroxomonosulfates (PMS) have received much attention in environmental remediation because of their low cost, high oxidizing capacity over a wide pH range, and operational advantages. By cleaving the peroxide bonds in the PMS molecule, the reactive oxygen species (ROS) that are produced, such as •OH, SO4•−, •O2−, and1O2, are oxidized and destroy a range of organic contaminants that are challenging to degrade8. Peroxymonosulfate (PMS) can be easily oxidized by alkali9, heat10, microwave11, visible light12, electricity13, ultrasound14 and transition metal (Co2+, Fe2+, Mn2+, etc.) activation15,16. The primary issues with this technology at the moment are the catalysts catalytic stability and the comparatively low charge transfer. The development of efficient, stable and reusable catalytic materials is highly desired.

Cost-effective calcite-type oxides (LaMeO3, where Me stands for the transition metals Fe, Co, Mn, Ni and do on) have attracted much attention. This kind of material is regarded as an alternative to platinum group metal catalysts due to the good thermal stability and the excepted catalytic activity17. Nevertheless, employing LaMnO3 to activate persulfate oxidation technique has numerous disadvantages: (1) the loss of active sites owing to the agglomeration of nanoparticle; (2) the tardy charge transfer leads to slow reaction kinetics; (3) the catalytic activity is insufficient during the degradation process. It has been found that these weaknesses can be overcome by forming composites, generating pores and doping with metal cations18. Metal doping, in particular, effectively regulates the band structure and is commonly used in the activity of modified catalyst11,19. Fe2+ has been shown to be the most effective homogeneous catalyst for PMS activation and has attracted a lot of interest20. Because of similar metal radii, Mn in the LaMnO3 chalcogenide matrix can be partially substituted with Fe to increase multiphase catalysis and avoid subsequent contamination. At the same time, Fe doping can control the oxidation state of LaMnO3 and introduce oxygen vacancies without destroying the structure. In addition, the generated LaFexMn1−xO3 chalcogenides can form a redox system that promotes the redox cycles of Fe2+/Fe3+ and Mn3+/Mn2+, further enhancing PMS activation and reactive oxygen species (ROS). The generation of ROSs is significantly influenced by the adsorption and desorption capabilities of intermediates. Currently, there remains insufficient comprehension regarding the influence of metal doping on the production of active free radicals and the identification of intermediates throughout the degradation process.

In this paper, LaMnO3 and LaFexMn1−xO3 samples were facile obtained using sol-gel method. The LaFexMn1−xO3/PMS system demonstrated expected efficiency on TC degradation over a broad pH range of 9.0 to 11.0 and good tolerance to interference. Quenching experiments revealed that •O2− and1O2 play important roles in the TC degrading process. DFT calculations explained the enhanced catalytic properties and provided a convincing reaction mechanism. HPLC-MS identified the intermediates produced during the TC degradation process and provide additional insights into the TC degradation pathways. This work offers a novel approach to the manufacture of catalysts doped with transition metals for the effective treatment of wastewater.

Results and discussion

Crystallographic and morphology

Figure 1(a) displays the XRD spectra of samples (including LaFexMn1−xO3 (x = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5), LaFeO3, and LaMnO3). As shown, the evident diffraction peaks at 2θ = 22. 87°, 32.4o, 40.02o, 46.71o, 58.62o, 68.58o, and 77.91o are attributed to (012), (110), (202), (024), (018), (208) and (128) planes of LaMnO3 (PDF # 86-1228), respectively. The characteristic peaks at 22.61o, 32.19o, 41.39o, 46.14o, 58.67o. 68.40o and 77.73o correspond to (101), (121), (131), (202), (232), (410) and (252) planes of LaFeO3 phase (PDF #37-1493)21. Following Fe doping, the (110), (202), and (024) diffraction peaks of LaMnO3 moved in the direction of the lower diffraction angle, suggesting that Fe element has been doped into the lanthanum manganese oxide lattice of LaMnO3 and produced a lattice deformation. This aligns with the findings documented in the literature22.

The porosity of LaMnO3 and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 was examined using N2 adsorption-desorption test, and type IV curves with typical H3 hysteresis return lines were observed (Fig. 1(b)). The specific data are shown in Table S1. As illustrated, LaMnO3 and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 have typical pore diameters of 7.099 nm and 5.154 nm, respectively, with mesoporosity ranging from 2 to 50 nm. This indicates that the prepared LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 samples have higher dispersal and larger specific surface area. In addition, the pore volumes of LaMnO3 and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 are 0.046 and 0.047 cm³/g, respectively. The specific surface areas (SBET) of LaMnO3 and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 are 27.06 and 30.56 m2/g, respectively.

Morphology is also an important consideration when measuring the performance of catalysts. As shown in Fig. 2(a-b), pure LaMnO3 exhibits serious agglomeration. After Fe element doping, the prepared LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 (Fig. 2(c-d)) exhibits a changed dispersivity, which is in good agreement with its mesoporous structure and the increased specific surface area. The coexistence of La, Fe, Mn, and O elements across the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 surface was certified by EDX (Fig. 2(e)), which validates the XRD findings. The average particle size (206 nm for LaMnO3 and 198 nm for LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3) and the TEM images (Fig. 2(f-g)) indicate the possibility that the active sites in LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 may be fully exposed, which could hasten the pace of charge migration and enhance the catalytic activity.

XPS surface elemental analysis

XPS technique was used to study the valence states and the elemental composition. Figure S1 describes the co-presence of La, Fe, Mn, and O elements in LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 catalyst, which verifies the EDX results. Figure 3(a) exhibits the La 3d spectrum. Two sets of peaks are observed, one for La 3d5/2 (at 834.1 and 837.9 eV) and the other for La 3d3/2 (at 850.5 and 854.6 eV). It is worth noting that both 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 features have two maxima separated by approximately 4 eV, indicating that the La ions in LaMnO3 and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 samples exist as La3+ 23. Fe doping does not alter the electronic structure of La. Figure 3(b) depicts the Mn 2p spectrum, which is subdivided into Mn 2p3/2 and Mn 2p1/2. The peaks located at 641.8 eV and 653.1 eV are attributed to Mn3+, and the signals at 643.1 eV and 656.3 eV are from Mn4+. These indicate that Mn4+ and Mn3+ coexist in the catalyst. When comparing the spectra of LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 with pure LaMnO3, the redshift of Mn4+ peak after Fe doping confirms the changing electronic structure of the Mn atom. This finding implies the enhanced charge exchange of Mn atoms after Fe doping. The Mn3+/Mn4+ peak area ratios in LaMnO3 and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 are 0.99 and 2.07, respectively, based on the fitting results. This resulted from the partial conversion of Mn4+ ions into Mn3+ ions, which was necessary to make up for the electrical neutral in the system18. Figure 3(c) displays the Fe 2p spectra in LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 material. Two sets of signals from Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 are observed at 724.5 eV, 713.0, and 711.1 eV, and a satellite peak at 718.1 eV is obvious. This proves the coexistence of Fe3+ and Fe4+ in LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 catalyst. The O 1s spectrum is shown in Fig. 3(d). Three peaks are involved, including O1 (529.7 eV), O2 (531.3 eV), and O3 (533.1 eV). They are commonly ascribed to the lattice oxygen, defect oxygen, and physically adsorbed oxygen, respectively. The increased O2 peak area in the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 may attributed to the replacement of Mn ions by Fe ions. The larger radii of Fe4+ more than Mn4+ leads to distortions in the cubic perovskite structure and increases the oxygen vacancy concentration24.

Catalytic performances

The catalytic activity in the presence of PMS was investigated on the TC target. As shown in Fig. 4(a), PMS alone achieves a degradation efficiency of just 39.6%. The circumstances are very different when the catalysts and PMS are simultaneously utilized. As shown, the degradation efficiencies of TC by LaMnO3/PM and LaFeO3/PMS are 50.1% and 81.2%, respectively. The amount of Fe element dopant has a close relationship with the catalytic performances of LaFexMn1−xO3 samples. LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 exhibits the anticipated catalytic property (greater than 99.9% TC degrading efficiency). This may be related to the synergistic bimetallic effect of Mn and Fe, which is thermodynamically favorable for the electrons transferring between them so as to achieve the recycling of redox pairs. However, the TC degradation efficiency decreases when Fe is overparticipated. This is due to the decrease of Mn4+ and Mn2+ sites and the increase of Mn3+) ions. As shown in Fig. 4(b), the degradation rates of LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PDS and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/H2O2 systems are only 20% and 15%, respectively. Figure 4(c) displays that the quasi-primary reaction rate constant of LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system is the highest at most 0.06 min− 1. Figure 4 (d) further illustrates that the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system has the highest TOC (about 62.1%), which is about 3 times as much as PMS alone (about 22.1%) and 2.3 times as much as LaMnO3/PMS (about 27.1%). When the Fe content reaches a specific level, the TOC value drops rather than increasing. This is because when Fe is over-doped, the number of atoms entering the crystal lattice decreases, indicating a greater tendency to create the doped element’s own oxide, resulting in a loss in catalytic activity and a decrease in TOC value. It is compared with other perovskite-based oxides that activate PMS for pollutant degradation, as indicated in Table S2 in Supporting Information, where the degradation efficiency of LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS is notably high within a short time.

The effect of PMS dosage was further explored in Fig. 5(a). As displayed, the TC degradation efficiency increases from 90 to 100% as the PMS concentration increases from 0.1 to 0.3 g/L. The reason for this could be the elevated concentration of active species (SO4•− and •OH), which increases the likelihood of interaction between the pollutants and free radicals. As the catalyst dosage increases from 0.05 g/L to 0.3 g/L, the TC elimination efficiency rises from 90 to 100% and the total degradation time decreases to 30 min (Fig. 5(b)). This observation may be attributed to an increase in the number of active sites and reactive oxygen species. Because free radicals have varying oxidizing capacities at different pH values, the impact of starting pH on the efficiency of pollutant breakdown was also studied. As shown in Fig. 5(c), high TC removal efficiency is maintained over the whole pH range. Furthermore, it was possible to ensure a degradation efficiency of over 90% even at a dosage of 30 mg/L for TC, indicating its potential utility in industrial settings, as illustrated in Fig. 5(d).

Coexisting chemicals in wastewater might negatively impact target pollutant removal in real engineering applications. Therefore, a specific evaluation of the impacts of inorganic anions, such as H2PO4−, Cl−, H2CO3−, SO42−, Urea, and HA, on TC removal was conducted. Previous reports indicate that H2PO4− would suppress the generation of SO4•− and •OH. A similar phenomenon is shown in Fig. 6(a). The fact that Cl−, H2CO3−, SO42−, Urea, and HA had almost no significant effect on TC elimination indicates that the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system has an exceptional interference resistance (as shown in Fig. 6(b-f)). This result also suggests that SO4− is merely not responsible for TC removal in this study. In general, Cl− tends to react with SO4•− and •OH to generate Cl•, Cl2−• and ClOH− radicals with weak oxidizing capacity, which somewhat inhibit the decomposition of organic pollutants to a certain extent25. The addition of HCO3− inevitably increases the solution pH, which is favorable for TC removal, which agrees well with Fig. 5(c). Reactive species consumption is responsible for the suppression observed in the presence of urea (Fig. 6(e)). With respect to Fig. 6(f), the presence of HA at elevated concentrations indicates a little reduction in the effectiveness of TC removal. This observation may be attributed to that HA could easily absorbed onto the catalyst surface due to its functional groups and competes with reactive species, limiting catalytic degradation.

Electrocatalytic performance

In order to investigate the reason for the increased catalytic activity of the composites, the electrochemical properties were analyzed in this work. The LSV results in Figure S2(a) demonstrate that the addition of PMS significantly increases the current density. This phenomenon can be explained by the interaction between PMS and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3, which forms metastable reaction complexes26. Another increase in the current density following the addition of TC indicates that the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system vigorously decomposed the TC, producing a significant quantity of charge27. From Tafel Figure S2(b), LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 shows smaller corrosion potentials and higher corrosion currents compared to LaMnO3 and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3, further suggesting that the faster electron transfer rate of LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 activated PMS28.

Catalytic mechanism

Quenching experiments were applied to assess the active ingredients of the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system. In this study, methanol (MeOH, a scavenger of •OH and SO4•−), tert-butanol (TBA, a scavenger of •OH), histidine (L-histidine, a scavenger of •O2− and •OH) and ascorbic acid (VC, a scavenger of1O2) were employed. As shown in Fig. 7(a), the TC degradation efficiency decreases to 90%, 87%, 70% and 40% after the addition of TBA, MeOH, L-histidine, and VC, respectively. According to the preliminary findings, the TC degrading process involves the significant roles that •OH1, O2, SO4•−, and •O2− play. And here’s what they might do in order:1O2 ˃ •O2− ˃ •OH ˃ SO4•−.

In order to further verify the free radicals generated by the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system, EPR tests were carried out using DMPO, TEMP as spin trapping agents. Figure 7(b-c) illustrates the formation of •O2− and1O2 in the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system. At five minutes into the reaction, the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system appeared to produce SO4•− and •OH signals, as seen in Fig. 7(d). The intensity of the •OH signal is greater than that of the SO4•−, which is due to the fact that H2O/OH− and SO4•− produce -OH29. And the intensities of both •O2− and1O2 are also stronger than SO4•−. These findings highlight the crucial roles that •O2−1, O2 and •OH play in the procedure of TC removal, which is consistent with the Quenching experiments.

Firstly, Mn3+ in the reactive sites with the enhanced charge exchange capability could react with PMS to produce additional SO4•− (Eq. (1)). The redox between Mn3+ and Mn4+, as proven by XPS, plays a significant role in the catalytic cycle. It is possible to produce additional radicals (SO5•−) that would enhance the generation of SO4•− (Eqs. (2–3)). In the meantime, the interaction between SO4•− and OH−/H2O might produce •OH radicals (Eqs. (4–5)). Additionally, the O2 produced by Eq. (3) gains an electron and becomes •O2− (Eq. (6)). Equation (7) states that1O2 is produced in the generated HSO5− and SO5•− reaction section. Ultimately, TC would be broken down into intermediate products by the reactive radicals (•OH, •O2−1, O2, and SO4•−) (Eq. (8)).

Intermediates identification and pathway of TC degradation

HPLC-MS was carried out to identify the intermediates produced during the TC degradation process and provide additional insights into the TC degradation pathways via the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system30–34. Figure S3 showed that there were 11 main intermediates in the TC breakdown process. Figure 8 depicted potential TC degradation pathways based on the degradation intermediates. The degradation steps of TC can be separated into two processes. In pathway A, radicals attacked the carbon double bond of the A ring, resulting in P1(m/z = 477). Meanwhile, the methyl group on the dimethylamine group on the A ring was gradually removed to offer P2 (m/z = 439). P3(m/z = 317) and P4 (m/z = 274) were produced by additional ring-opening and oxidation of product P2. The second reaction pathway involves the N-demethylation of TC, which can be initiated by radicals. This process leads to the formation of P5(m/z 431), P6(m/z = 418) and P7(m/z = 339) through the successive removal of methyl and amide groups from the A ring of P5. P7 undergoes additional oxidation to produce the intermediate compound P8(m/z = 278). Ultimately, the aforementioned intermediate products (P4 and P8) undergo oxidation to produce P9(m/z = 120), P10(m/z = 100) and P11(m/z = 85). These compounds are then further oxidized to yield H2O, CO2, NH4+, resulting in the complete mineralization of TC.

The toxicity of TC and degradation products were evaluated in Figure S4 by utilizing toxicological estimation software T.E.S.T. As illustrated, the median lethal concentration values for the fathead minnow and Daphnia magna are 0.9 and 8.73 mg/L, respectively. As degradation advances, the LC50 values of the degradation products exceed that of the original TC solution. As shown in Figure S4(a), only P4 (0.42 mg/L) exhibits a value lower than the TC solution, thereby substantially diminishing the toxicity of the breakdown products. In Figure S4(b), all degradation intermediates, with the exception of P4 (2.09 mg/L) and P7 (5.82 mg/L), exhibit LC50 values that exceed that of TC. Degradation can convert “toxic” TC into “harmful” molecules or organic compounds, hence diminishing TC pollution in aquatic environments. Furthermore, the developmental toxicity and mutagenicity were examined. Figure S4(c) illustrates that, with the exception of P6, the developmental toxicity of the majority is diminished, suggesting that the breakdown of TC can effectively mitigate developmental toxicity and restore water supplies. Concerning the mutagenicity illustrated in Figure S4(d), the TC solution and the majority of intermediates exhibited “mutagenicity positive,” but P4, P9, and P10 demonstrated “mutagenicity negative.” The results demonstrate that Fe doping in LaMnO3 significantly reduces the toxicity of TC. This signifies that the approach can decompose poisonous TC into less deleterious molecules, which is essential for environmental and ecological safety.

Stability and practical application of LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system

To further evaluate the active centers, XPS was carried out on pristine LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 and after reaction. In the Mn 2p spectrum (Figure S5(a)), the Mn3+/Mn4+ peak area ratios in LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 and after reaction are computed to be 2.07 and 1.13, respectively. This resulted from the partial conversion of Mn3+ ions into Mn4+ ions. In the Fe 2p spectrum (Figure S5(b)), the blue shifts and the multiple Fe species generation in the catalytic process demonstrate the primary active center of Fe. The Fe3+/Fe4+ peak area ratios in LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 and after reaction are calculated to be 1.82 and 2.0, respectively. This finding may be ascribed to the hypothesis that the Fe doping changed the electronic nature of LaMnO3, and the electrons moved from Fe to Mn during the catalytic procedure. Furthermore, the co-existence of Fe2+, Fe3+, and Fe4+ would play important role in PMS activation. Fe2+ functions as an electron donor to promote the reduction of PMS to generate •OH and SO4•−, while being oxidized to Fe3+. Fe3+ functions as an electron acceptor, interacting with PMS to oxidize it into SO5•−, resulting in a cyclic conversion between Fe2+ and Fe3+. Fe3+ primarily exists as Fe(OH)2+, which quickly interacts with PMS under visible light to generate SO4•− and •OH. Concurrently, Fe3+ is oxidized to Fe4+ following the loss of electrons. Thereafter, Fe4+ acquires electrons and activates PMS, producing reactive species and establishing a Fe(III)/Fe(IV) cycle, which expedites the rate of electron transfer within the system and markedly improves the activation efficiency of PMS.

The lifetime of the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system was investigated in order to learn more about the long-term applications (Fig. 9(a)). As demonstrated, after five successive testing, the degradation rate of the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system is still able to maintain roughly 98%. Furthermore, there is nearly no alteration in the SEM and XRD images before and after 5 cycles (Fig. 9(b-c)). To confirm the system’s adaptability in real water, three more target contaminants were specifically chosen. As shown in Fig. 9(d), the removal rates of CR (Congo red), CV (crystal violet) and CH (chlortetracycline hydrochloride) are all about 100% under the same conditions, which proves the practical value of LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 catalyst.

Density functional theory calculation

In this study, the electronic structure and PMS activation mechanism of LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 catalysts were further demonstrated using density flooding theory. The theoretical calculation process is shown in the supporting information. Charge density difference of LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 is shown in Fig. 10(a). As shown, the cyan and yellow represent electron depletion and accumulation. The Fe doping changed the electronic nature of LaMnO3, and the electrons moved from Fe to O atoms and accumulated in the O atoms, enhancing the charge exchange of Mn atoms in the reaction site, thus improving the catalytic performance.

The projected densities of states (PDOS) of LaMnO3 and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 were evaluated to provide d-band center information (Fig. 10(b-c)). The Fermi energy level was set to 0 eV. For LaMnO3, the d-band center is 0.95 eV, while for LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3, the d-band center is 0.50 eV. The decrease in d-band center leads to a more empty anti-bonding state and an increase in bond strength with O atoms, encouraging the adsorption/desorption of intermediates in the PMS activation process35. It is well known that the ability to accept or lose electrons around the active site is crucial for catalytic reactions. The d-band center of the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 system is closer to the Fermi energy level, indicating a greater ability to obtain or lose electrons. In addition, the improved conductivity of the LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 system around the Fermi energy level boosts electron transfer, which may simultaneously enhance its catalytic efficiency36. The adsorption structures of PMS on the upper site of LaMnO3 and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 are shown in Figure S6(a-b), which we chose for the subsequent theoretical calculations. The adsorption process is depicted as follows: LaMnO3 and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 firstly adsorbs PMS in the solution and releases energy. Then, the adsorbed PMS is activated and the contaminant TC is adsorbed. Subsequently, the O atoms of LaMnO3 and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 attack the C atoms of TC and leave the LaMnO3 and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 site to form the intermediates. After desorption of the oxidized TC, the catalyst can be reactivated for the next cycle for PMS activation and TC oxidation. The total energies released during the reaction on the LaMnO3 and LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3 sites are shown in Fig. 10(d) are 6.32 and 4.73 eV, respectively. This finding suggests that Fe doping of LaMnO3 lowers the activation energy, accelerates the catalytic reaction rate, and enhances PMS activation.

Conclusion

In summary, LaFexMn1−xO3 (x = 0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1) chalcogenides were readily synthesized via facile sol-gel method. Comprehensive experimental and theoretical investigations revealed that (1) fast electron transport was developed to improve the charge concentration in the reactive sites because of the difference in electronegativity of the two elements; (2) Fe doping adjusted the electronic structure of LaFexMn1−xO3 into an electron-rich state, resulting in more empty anti-bonding state and increased binding strength with O atoms, promoting the intermediates adsorption/desorption. The LaFe0.3Mn0.7O3/PMS system had merely 100% degradation rate of TC and remained steady (98% removal efficiency) after 5 successive tests. It also resisted interference from solution pH and coexisting organic and inorganic ions and demonstrated good catalytic capacity in actual water. Furthermore, •O2− and1O2 reactants were certified to dominate the reaction. HPLC-MS indicated that during the reaction process, TC molecules were converted into organic substances with a lower molecular weight. This study shows that doping metallic elements is an effective technique for modulating the electronic structure of a catalyst, hence modifying its catalytic characteristics.
