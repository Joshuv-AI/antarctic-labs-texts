Bdelloid rotifers harbor a voltage-gated proton channel with distinct mechanistic features
Authors: Liang Yan, Chiara Boschetti, Liang Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bdelloid rotifers are renowned for their extraordinary resilience. They can survive desiccation and long-term freezing and revive once favorable conditions return. Such remarkable survival abilities suggest that the evolution of specialized molecular adaptations sustain pH homeostasis under extreme environmental stress. In the present study, we identify and functionally characterize an isoform of the voltage-gated proton channel (Hv) from the bdelloid rotifer Adineta ricciae, which we name ArHv2. Unlike its paralog ArHv1 and human Hv1 that activate only at positive membrane potentials and contribute modestly to proton extrusion under normal conditions, the ArHv2 activates even at negative potentials, enabling rapid and efficient proton efflux or influx when cells face freezing or metabolic arrest. We discover that this behavior is conferred by a positively charged lysine located in the S4 transmembrane segment. This lysine is absent from other known Hv homologs but is highly conserved in bdelloid rotifer Hv2 channels. We further delineated the molecular mechanism by which this lysine fine-tunes ArHv2 proton channel activation. The discoveries in the present work highlight how the evolution of ion channel architecture at the amino acid level can yield physiological adaptations.

Introduction

The voltage-gated proton channel (Hv) is known to play a key role in the regulation of cellular pH1–5. It mediates the extrusion of protons, preventing intracellular acidification that arises during metabolic activity. Hv channel dysfunction disrupts pH homeostasis and has been linked to diseases5–12. Structurally, Hv channels are organized as dimers, with each subunit comprising a proton-permeable voltage-sensing domain (VSD) formed by four transmembrane segments (S1–S4), and the two VSDs are held through a C-terminal coiled-coil domain13–17. The Hv channel S4 helix contains three positively charged residues, typically arginines, that drive voltage-dependent conformational changes essential for channel gating.

Bdelloid rotifers are microscopic organisms renowned for their ability to survive extreme environmental stress18–21. A study has reported a living bdelloid rotifer from 24,000-year-old Arctic permafrost18, and the rotifer withstood slow cooling and freezing for a long time and revived when favorable conditions returned. Such extraordinary survival ability suggests that rotifers have evolved unique molecular adaptations that maintain cellular pH homeostasis and sustain metabolism under extreme conditions lethal to most organisms22,23. In particular, the ability to preserve intracellular pH balance is essential for protecting protein stability, enzymatic activity, and redox homeostasis during prolonged metabolic arrest. These adaptations involve specialized proton transport mechanisms that enable rapid and energy-efficient adjustment of intracellular proton concentrations in bdelloid rotifers and thereby safeguard cellular integrity in the face of desiccation, freezing, and other environmental challenges.

In this work, we identify two Hv isoforms in the bdelloid rotifer Adineta ricciae24, named ArHv1 and ArHv2. While the ArHv1 channel resembles the properties of human Hv1 (hHv1), the ArHv2 channel displays different gating properties and activates at more negative potentials, enabling more efficient proton transport near-resting membrane potential. Notably, ArHv2 contains a positively charged lysine residue in the S4 segment that modulates channel activation. We investigated how this residue and its interactions with conserved acidic residues within the rotifer Hv2 family contribute to ArHv2 activation and discuss how it regulates this Hv isoform’s function.

Result

Bdelloid rotifers expressed an isoform of the Hv channel

Using the human Hv1 channel (hHv1) sequence as a query in Protein BLAST, we discovered Hv channel paralogs in bdelloid rotifers including A. ricciae, A. steineri, D. carnosus, R. sordida, and R. magnacalcarata. We performed phylogenetic analysis (Fig. 1a; Table S1), and the results revealed two groups of Hv in bdelloid rotifers; we named them the Hv1 and Hv2 groups, respectively. In this study, the term Hv2 refers to the second Hv isoform identified in rotifers. This nomenclature is consistent with previous studies in Hv proton channels, in which multiple Hv isoforms within the same organism are named sequentially to distinguish coexisting paralogs25,26. Hv paralogs from A. ricciae (ArHv1 and ArHv2) have predicted topological features characteristic of other voltage-gated Hv proton channels, including four transmembrane segments forming the voltage-sensing domain (VSD) and a C-terminal coiled-coil domain (CCD) (Fig. 1c; Fig. S1). The results verify that both ArHv1 and ArHv2 are present in A. ricciae and can be specifically amplified using paralog-specific primers (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

The ArHv2 channel exhibited different activities

Whole-cell configuration recording was performed with intra- and extracellular solutions buffered at pH 7.0. We successfully recorded strong voltage-dependent currents from both ArHv1 and ArHv2 under conditions established for other organism Hv channels (Fig. 2). While ArHv1 activates slowly and only at positive membrane potentials (Fig. 2a), ArHv2 activates more rapidly and at both positive and negative membrane potentials (Fig. 2c). Hv channels are known for their almost perfect selectivity for protons. Since the rotifer Hv2 isoform is evolutionarily distinct from its Hv1 isoform and other animal Hv homologs, we wonder whether the rotifer Hv2 isoform ArHv2 keeps this property. We measured its reversal potentials (Vrev) under different transmembrane pH gradients. Vrev values for ArHv2-mediated currents were obtained using a tail current protocol and compared with the theoretical equilibrium potentials for protons (EH) predicted by the Nernst equation. Prolonged cold stress is known to enhance anaerobic metabolism, promoting intracellular acidification that is harmful for cellular function. An efficient mechanism enabling H⁺ extrusion near-resting membrane potential would therefore promote survival. We examined ArHv2 function under defined pH gradients and found that, under intracellular acid load (pHi 6.0 / pHo 7.0), ArHv2 mediates robust outward H⁺ currents at 0 mV (Fig. 4a). Reversing the pH gradient correspondingly reversed the driving force and current direction (Fig. 4b). These results indicate that ArHv2 can effectively mediate proton transport across the transmembrane gradient at 0 mV. The lysine (K243) in the S4 segment of ArHv2 regulates channel voltage-dependent activation

In voltage-gated ion channels, the S4 transmembrane segment of the VSD contains several positively charged residues, mostly arginines, located at every third position33. The S4 positively charged residues play a central role in channel activation34–38. They not only control the voltage sensitivity of the channel but also shape the kinetics of activation. Since ArHv2 exhibited differential activation ranges and kinetic properties (Fig. 2), we analyzed the S4 segment of Hv channels. A lysine (K) in ArHv2 was shown to take the second position of S4 arginine (R) in ArHv1 and other Hv channels (Fig. 5a). To determine whether and how this lysine affects the ArHv2 channel function, we substituted it with an arginine at the second position of S4 in ArHv2. This charge-conserving replacement (ArHv2-K243R) dramatically shifted the G–V curve to the right and generated an approximately 80 mV shift in the V1/2 (Fig. 5b, c). The result suggests that the lysine (K) at the second position of S4 plays a significant role in channel activation and makes the channel much easier to open. Conversely, introducing lysine at the second position of the ArHv1 channel (ArHv1-R211K) produced a left-shifted G–V compared to the wild-type ArHv1 (Fig. 5d, e). The data was consistent with the observation that native ArHv2 (with K at the second position) favors channel opening. Moreover, we investigated the human Hv1 channel (hHv1), and similar results were found in hHv1, in which the lysine substitution at the second position (hHv1-R208K) notably shifted the G–V curve in the negative direction (Fig. 5f, g). Taken together, these results indicated that the lysine at the second position of S4 regulates the voltage-dependent activation of the Hv channels and plays an essential role in rotifer proton channel ArHv2 activation and function.

Effects of mutations at position K243 depend on the negative residues in the S1 and S3 transmembrane segments

Since the conserved lysine in rotifer Hv2 controls the channel activity, we set out to determine how this lysine affects the ArHv2 voltage-dependent activation. There are several negatively charged residues distributed from S1 to S3 segments of the VSD in the voltage-gated ion channels. The function of negatively charged residues was proposed to interact with S4 positively charged residues to mediate the channel activation39–41. These negatively charged residues are highly conserved in the Hv channel family. However, there are two changes that occurred in ArHv2 channel: one aspartate (D129 in ArHv2) takes the position of glutamate (E) in the area near the extracellular side of S1, and conversely, a glutamate (E218 in ArHv2) takes the position of aspartate (D) in the area near middle of S3 (Fig. 6a). Notably, this “D/E swap” in S1 and S3 is preserved across the rotifers Hv2 family (Supplementary Fig. 4). To investigate whether the S1/S3 negatively charged residues are coupled with the S4 lysine, we applied a mutant cycle approach to probe potential interactions between them. A double-mutant cycle analysis has been used to study residues’ interactions between transmembrane segments in the Hv1 channel42. We applied the approaches to investigate the relationship between K243 and D129 or E218 (Fig. 6b–g). The residues near the highly conserved K243 (e.g., W242 and I244) in the S4 segment were investigated as well (Fig. 6b–g, Supplementary Fig. 5).

The G–V curves of the S4 segment mutation (W242, K243, or I244) with or without mutation at D129 or E218 were summarized in Fig. 6h–m. The G–V curve was fitted with the Boltzmann equation, and V1/2 and k values were determined and used to assess the ΔG0, which is the free energy difference between the closed and open states of the activation. The thermodynamic coupling energy |ΔΔG0| between two mutated positions was determined by the thermodynamic coupling analysis43. A significant interaction between two residues was defined as a |ΔΔG0| > 4.2 kJ/mol (1 kcal/mol)40,44. The |ΔΔG0| values obtained were reported in Fig. 6n. It was shown that the combinations K243R-D129E and K243R-E218D produced |ΔΔG0| values of 6.9 and 5.1 kJ/mol, respectively, supporting the thermodynamic coupling interactions between K243 and D129 or E218 (Fig. 6n, Table S2). In contrast, combinations W242A-D129E and W242A-E218D yielded |ΔΔG0| values (1.2 and 0.5 kJ/mol, respectively) well below 4.2 kJ/mol, indicating no thermodynamic couplings between W242 and D129 or E218. We further studied another residue, I244, near K243, and the I244A-D129E and I244A-E218D combinations generated much smaller |ΔΔG0| (1.0 and 0.7 kJ/mol, respectively) when compared with K243R-D129E and K243R-E218D (Fig. 6n, Table S2). Collectively, these data strongly suggest that K243, but not W242 or I244, is coupled to D129 and E218, supporting functional interactions between K243 and D129/E218 during voltage-dependent channel activation.

K243 interacts with E218 to stabilize the closed state of the channel, and K243 interacts with D129 to stabilize the open state of the channel

The activation of VSD involves the outward movement of positively charged amino acids in the S4 segment across the membrane electric field33. Since the double-mutant cycle analysis indicated that K243 interacts with D129 and E218 during channel activation—a process by which the channel transitions between closed and open states—we propose that interactions between K243 and D129 or E218 play a regulatory role in controlling the conformational transitions of the ArHv2 VSD.

A charge-conserving mutation at position 129 (D129E) was introduced into the ArHv2 channel (Fig.7). The D129E mutation shifted the G–V relationship toward more positive direction (Fig. 7g), and exhibited slowed activation (Fig. 7b, h) and accelerated deactivation (Fig. 7e, i), indicating destabilization of the open state of the channel and stabilization of the closed state of the channel. D129E also exhibited less negative ΔG₀ and V10%max (voltage at 10% of maximum conductance) compared with the WT channel (Supplementary Fig. 6, Table S2). Taken together, these results suggest that the native D129 contributes to stabilizing the open conformation. We further assessed the interactions between D129 and K243 by cysteine pair crosslinking and observed that Cd2+ produced sustained tail current and significantly delayed the deactivation process in the double mutant K243C-D129C (Supplementary Fig. 7), indicating that Cd2+-mediated coordination locks the channel in the open conformation and impairs channel closing. This energy-reaction model has been used to describe transitions between functional states of voltage-gated ion channels at a phenomenological level44,45. The model consists of a closed state and an open state, each represented by an energy well. Discussion

In the S4 segment of voltage-sensing domains (VSDs), positively charged residues serve as the main charge carriers that drive the stepwise movement of S4 in voltage-gated ion channels33. Through stable hydrogen bonds and salt bridges with residues in other segments of the VSD, these charges mediate conformational transitions essential for channel activation and serve as the primary molecular mechanism underlying voltage-dependent activation. The positively charged residues, most commonly arginine, play critical roles in channel gating35–38.

In the bdelloid rotifer A. ricciae, we discovered an isoform of voltage-gated proton channel ArHv2. The ArHv2 channel harbors a positively charged lysine (K243) in the second position of the S4 segment. This lysine is highly conserved in the rotifer Hv2 family (Fig. S4) but not present in all other Hv homologs identified so far (Fig. 5a).

Lysine, compared to arginine, has a smaller, more flexible amino group46, which better stabilizes interactions with acidic residues and modulates ArHv2 VSD gating. Substituting lysine at the second position of S4 shifted the G–V curves of ArHv1, ArHv2, and hHv1 toward the negative direction, facilitating channel opening. In rotifers, the native lysine in ArHv2 likely lowers the energetic barrier for activation, fine-tuning charge-charge interactions during S4 movement and stabilizing specific channel states.

Mutational analyses further clarify these interactions. In the resting state, K243 interacts with E218 (Figs. 9a and 10); the E218D mutation shifted the G–V curve in the negative direction, accelerated activation, and delayed closure, consistent with weakened K243-218 coupling and destabilization of the closed state. In the open state, K243 interacts with D129 (Figs. 9b and 10); the D129E mutation slowed activation and shifted the G–V curve in the positive direction, indicating destabilization of the open conformation. These results highlight a dynamic role of K243, stabilizing distinct channel conformations through context-dependent interactions with conserved acidic residues to fine-tune the channel activity. A previous study applied mutagenesis to study the role of a lysine/arginine switch in potassium channels47. Tao et al. showed that lysine at different positions within the Shaker channel S4 segment can exert opposing effects on channel gating: lysine replacing native arginine at the outer position (R1K) stabilizes the “closed” state; however, lysine at an inner position (K5) stabilizes the “open” state47. These results indicate that the functional impact of lysine strongly depends on its position within the S4 segment in the Shaker channel. In the present study, we discovered that the single lysine residue (K243) in the ArHv2 channel interacts with E218 to keep the channel closed; and upon activation, K243 moves upward to interact with D129 to keep the channel open. These findings reveal a distinct role of lysine in regulating voltage-gated proton channel function.

The presence of lysine in ArHv2 enables the channel to open at 0 mV. This property can help rotifers survive in harsh environments. During extended low-temperature exposure, cold stress can increase physiological constraints (e.g., promoting anaerobic metabolism) and accumulation of acidic equivalents (e.g., lactate and related metabolites)48–50. Sustained intracellular acidification is known to disrupt cellular function and induce cell death. ArHv2 channel is able to generate robust outward H⁺ currents at 0 mV under intracellular acid load, and this function enables rapid and efficient H+ extrusion near the resting potential during cold stress, which reduces anaerobic metabolism-induced intracellular acidification and improves survival.

The lysine substitution at the second position of the S4 segment was identified in bdelloid rotifer Hv2 channels. While this appears to represent a molecular adaptation in rotifers, other anhydrobiotic animals may develop diverse or lineage-specific strategies to modify proton channel voltage sensitivity in response to extreme environmental stress.

In contrast to ArHv2, the rotifer ArHv1 channel exhibits a more positively shifted G–V relationship. This difference implies that ArHv2 is the dominant contributor to H+ extrusion at resting or near-resting membrane potentials, whereas ArHv1 is largely inactive under the same conditions. The coexistence of two Hv isoforms in rotifers might allow adaptation to a broader range of voltage or pH conditions over which rotifers can regulate proton flux for fluctuating environments. Future studies are needed to determine how these channels are differentially regulated and whether loss of either proton channel compromises organismal viability.

In summary, our results identify the conserved lysine in the S4 segment of ArHv2 as a molecular determinant that fine-tunes channel activation through its interactions with negatively charged residues in resting and open states. This distinctive feature sets rotifer Hv2 channels apart from other animal Hv homologs and may underline their ability to regulate proton homeostasis under extreme stress. The discoveries in the present work highlight the connection between molecular specialization at the amino acid level and organism function, illustrating how evolution in ion channel architecture can yield physiological adaptations.

Methods

Hv sequence analysis

The human Hv1 channel (hHv1) sequence is used as a query in Protein BLAST to identify rotifer Hv homologs51. For phylogenetic analysis, a multiple Hv sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree were performed using Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (MEGA12) software52. Tree scale 0.2 = 20% difference between sequences.

The following organism Hv protein sequences were used to construct the phylogenetic tree: human (H. sapiens, NP_001035196.1), mouse (M. musculus, NP_001035954.1), zebrafish (D. rerio, NP_001002346.1), coral (A. millepora, XP_029202331.2), snail (H. trivolvis, XP_055871465.1), insect (N. phytophile, AMK01488.1), sea squirt (C. intestinalis, NP_001071937.1), sea slug (A. californica, XP_005100666.1 and XP_005093106.1), coccolithophore (C. braarudii, ADM25825.1 and CAD8615310.1; E. huxleyi, XP_005762299.1 and XP_005794250.1), dinoflagellate (K. veneficum, AEQ59286.1), bdelloid rotifers (A. ricciae, CAF1315436.1 and CAF0979481.1; A. steineri, CAF1145379.1 and CAF1220596.1; D. carnosus, CAF0799904.1 and CAF1276142.1; R. sordida, CAF1201389.1 and CAF1163634.1; R. magnacalcarata, CAF1450926.1 and CAF2039880.1). Detailed information for the protein sequences was summarized in Table S1.

Generation of structural models

Structural models of ArHv2 were generated by the AlphaFold (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/)53. The input sequence comprises the full-length ArHv2 protein (i.e., residues 1–325), and the PDB file of ArHv2 is included in the Supplementary Information. For the model of the closed state, the structure of the isolated VSD was applied (Fig. 9a). For the generation of the model of the open state, the “one-click” model was applied to modify the closed state. In this model, the S4 helix undergoes a single upward step corresponding to one register shift of the charged residues along the helix. Accordingly, the position originally occupied by residue 237 was reassigned to Arg240, Arg240 was reassigned to Lys243, and Lys243 was reassigned to Arg246 (Fig. 9b). Models were visualized, and inter-residue distances were measured using BIOVIA Discovery Studio.

PCR expression analysis

Cultures of Adineta ricciae were maintained in plastic Petri dishes (3–15 cm diameter) with 3–50 ml sterile ddH2O (depending on the vessel) and fed twice a week with a solution of autoclaved yeast extract and peptone (2.5% w/v each)54. Rotifers were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 6 min at 4 °C, the supernatant removed and the pellet used immediately or frozen at −20 °C until DNA extraction. DNA was extracted with a Qiagen Blood and Tissue Kit (cat. 69504) following the “Tissue” protocol, and the DNA eluted in 20−40 µl of buffer AE. Purification and concentration were measured with a Nanodrop. The DNA concentrations ranged from 20 to 100 ng/µl. The resulting DNA was used as a template for PCR analysis under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1.5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 98 °C for 20 s, 63 °C for 30 s, and 73 °C for 5 min. A final extension was performed at 72 °C for 15 min, and reactions were cooled to 4 °C for 30 min. The resulting PCR products were analyzed on a 2% agarose gel.

Mutagenesis

DNA constructs encoding wild-type ArHv1 and ArHv2 were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). The coding sequences were subcloned into the pIRES-GFP vector (for HEK293 cells)55 or pGEMHE vector (for Xenopus laevis oocytes)56 using BamHI/XbaI restriction sites. The construct containing the cDNA sequence from human Hv1 in the pNICE vector was used to express hHv1. Single-point mutations were introduced with standard PCR techniques. PCR primers were purchased from IDT DNA Technologies. Custom-designed primers for mutagenesis of Hv constructs were listed in Table S3. Mutations were generated by amplifying plasmid templates under the following PCR conditions: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1.5 min, followed by 25 cycles of 98 °C for 20 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 5 min. A final extension was performed at 72 °C for 15 min, and reactions were cooled to 4 °C for 30 min. The template plasmid was digested with DpnI (NEB, R0176S), and the resulting PCR products were transformed into Stbl2 competent cells (Invitrogen, 10268019). Plasmids were isolated from bacterial colonies using the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen, 27106). All mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing before use in subsequent transfection experiments.

Cell culture and Hv transfection

HEK293 cells (Sigma-Aldrich) were cultured on coverslips in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Upon reaching ~60% confluence, cells were transiently transfected with 2 μg of rotifer Hv cDNA plasmid (pIRES-GFP) using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s protocol. For human Hv1, cells were transiently transfected with 2 μg of hHv1 cDNA plasmid (pNICE) and 0.25 μg of a plasmid encoding GFP. After transfection, cells were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C before electrophysiological studies. For recordings, coverslips containing HEK293 cells were placed in a bath solution within a recording chamber mounted on an Olympus fluorescence microscope. Transfected cells were identified by GFP fluorescence, and whole-cell patch-clamp experiments were performed 1–3 days post-transfection. Xenopus laevis oocytes (Stages V-VI) were obtained from Ecocyte Bioscience (USA) and maintained at 18 °C in ND96 solution containing 96 mM NaCl, 2 mM KCl, 1.8 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES, 5 mM pyruvate, and 100 mg/mL gentamicin (pH 7.2). pGEMHE plasmids encoding ArHv2 constructs were linearized with NheI (New England Biolabs, USA), and RNA synthesis was carried out with a T7 Transcription Kit (Ambion, USA). Oocytes were injected with 50 nL of cRNA (1.5 mg/mL) and incubated for 3–5 days prior to electrophysiological experiments.

Patch-clamp electrophysiology

Hv proton currents in HEK293 cells were recorded in the whole-cell configuration using an Axopatch 200B amplifier (Molecular Devices) controlled by pClamp 11 software via an Axon Digidata 1550B digitizer. The external bath solution at pH 7.0 contained 75 mM N-methyl-D-glucamine (NMDG), 180 mM N-(2-Hydroxyethyl) piperazine-N′-(2-ethanesulfonic acid) (HEPES), 10 mM glucose, 1 mM MgCl₂, and 1 mM CaCl₂, adjusted to pH 7.0 with methanesulfonic acid. The pipette solution at pH 7.0 consisted of 75 mM NMDG, 180 mM HEPES, 10 mM glucose, 1 mM MgCl₂, and 1 mM EGTA, adjusted to pH 7.0 with methanesulfonic acid. The external bath solution at pH 6.0 contained 75 mM NMDG, 110 mM 2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid (MES), 80 mM glucose, 1 mM MgCl₂, and 1 mM CaCl₂, adjusted to pH 6.0 with methanesulfonic acid. The pipette solution at pH 6.0 consisted of 75 mM NMDG, 110 mM MES, 80 mM glucose, 1 mM MgCl₂, and 1 mM EGTA, adjusted to pH 6.0 with methanesulfonic acid. Hv proton currents in oocytes were recorded using the inside-out patch-clamp configuration. Both bath and pipette solutions contained 100 mM MES, 30 mM tetraethylammonium (TEA) methanesulfonate, 5 mM TEA chloride, and 5 mM EGTA, with the pH adjusted to 6.0 using TEA hydroxide. Recordings were performed under constant perfusion at 22 ± 3 °C using pipettes with access resistance of 5–10 MΩ. Currents were low-pass filtered at 1 kHz and analyzed with Clampfit 11 (Molecular Devices) and Origin 2019 (OriginLab).
