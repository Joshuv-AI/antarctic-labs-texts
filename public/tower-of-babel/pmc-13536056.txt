Effects of music therapy and music–movement interventions on social withdrawal, parent–child relationship and happiness in children with autism spectrum disorder: a protocol for systematic reviews and meta-analysis
Authors: Lirou Wen, Feng Wang, Wen Fen Beh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChildren with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) often experience challenges in social interaction, emotional regulation and parent–child bonding, which can negatively impact overall well-being and happiness. Music therapy, particularly when integrated with movement-based interventions, such as piano training, is increasingly recognised as a promising non-pharmacological approach to enhance social engagement and improve psychological outcomes in this population. Although multiple primary studies and pilot trials have been conducted, the evidence remains fragmented and inconsistent. To date, no comprehensive systematic review has synthesised the effects of music-based interventions on (1) social withdrawal, (2) parent–child relationship quality and (3) subjective happiness in children with ASD.Methods and analysisThis protocol adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols guidelines. A systematic search will be conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, China National Knowledge Infrastructure and Wanfang Data from inception to May 2026. Eligible studies will include randomised controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies evaluating music or music–movement interventions for children with ASD. The primary outcome is social withdrawal, whereas the secondary outcomes are the parent–child relationship and overall happiness assessed using validated measures. Data extraction and risk-of-bias assessments will be conducted independently by two reviewers. Where feasible, quantitative synthesis will be performed using random-effects meta-analysis. Subgroup analyses will explore the influence of age, intervention type and intervention duration. The certainty of evidence will be assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation approach.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required as this review involves published data only. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and community reports targeting clinicians, therapists and parents.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251132116.

Introduction

Social withdrawal in autism spectrum disorder

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterised by deficits in social communication, restricted interests and repetitive behaviours.1 Its prevalence has increased over time, and globally, approximately 1 in 100 children is diagnosed with the disorder.2 Beyond the core symptoms, children with ASD often experience social withdrawal, difficulties in building relationships and reduced subjective well-being.3 These challenges can further strain parent–child interactions and family functioning.4

Social withdrawal is the core behavioural symptom of ASD,5 manifesting as avoidance of peer interaction, reduced participation in group activities and challenges in engaging with caregivers. It has been linked with heightened anxiety, loneliness and developmental delays. Recent studies highlight that children with ASD displaying higher levels of social withdrawal are more vulnerable to secondary mental health issues including depression and reduced resilience.6 Addressing social withdrawal is therefore a critical therapeutic goal.

Parent–child relationship in autism spectrum disorder

The parent–child relationship plays a central role in shaping emotional regulation and adaptive behaviour in children with ASD. High-quality parent–child interactions are associated with better developmental outcomes,7 whereas strained relationships may exacerbate behavioural difficulties and caregiver stress.8 Parent support for emotion regulation has been shown to buffer behavioural difficulties in everyday contexts.9 However, parents often report difficulties in engaging their child meaningfully, underscoring the need for structured interventions that foster bonding.

Happiness and well-being in children with autism spectrum disorder

Happiness is a subjective but essential dimension of quality of life.10 Happiness is conceptually distinct from broader constructs such as quality of life, subjective well-being, emotional well-being, positive affect and life satisfaction.11 In this review, happiness refers to an individual’s subjective experience of positive emotions and enjoyment rather than these broader or related constructs. Children with ASD may face challenges in experiencing or expressing happiness due to sensory sensitivities, restricted activities or social exclusion.12 However, happiness is less frequently assessed in intervention trials, leaving an important gap in evidence synthesis. Interventions that encourage play, creative expression and social engagement show potential to improve well-being,13 14 but rigorous evaluation and consistent measurement remain limited. This gap underscores the need for validated indicators of happiness to better understand and promote subjective well-being in children with ASD.

Music therapy and music–movement interventions

Music therapy is a structured therapeutic practice that uses the emotional and psychological impact of music to support mental health and enhance overall well-being.15 16 Music therapy has emerged as a promising intervention for enhancing cognitive and behavioural development in special education, particularly for students with ASD.17 Music therapy engages multiple sensory modalities and fosters communication without relying solely on verbal expression. Active music-making (singing and instrument playing) encourages participation and social synchrony, whereas receptive approaches provide calming and regulatory effects.18

Although these interventions differ in delivery format and intensity, they share a common therapeutic foundation by using music as the primary medium to facilitate emotional regulation, social interaction and behavioural engagement. Therefore, they are considered related music-based interventions within the scope of this review. Movement-based approaches, such as music–movement integrated piano teaching or rhythmic exercises, address the most natural way to express and represent music itself through the body.19 Evidence shows that embodied musical activities can reduce negative emotions and feelings,20 21 strengthen emotional attunement and promote mutual regulation between children and caregivers.22

Rationale for this review

Evidence suggests that music therapy and music–movement interventions have beneficial effects for individuals with ASD. For instance, research indicates that music therapy can reduce stress, anxiety and depression while promoting emotional expression and social connection.23 However, the findings remain fragmented, and considerable heterogeneity along with other limitations undermines their validity.24 Moreover, music therapy and music–movement interventions vary widely in delivery mode, intensity and duration, which limit their generalisability. To date, no review has comprehensively synthesised outcomes across social withdrawal, parent–child relationship and happiness despite their critical importance for quality of life.

This systematic review and meta-analysis will address these gaps by synthesising evidence from randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental studies (QES). Evaluating both social, behavioural and well-being outcomes, exploring moderators such as intervention type, duration and child characteristics.

By integrating these dimensions, the review aims to provide robust evidence for clinicians, educators and families regarding the role of music and music–movement interventions in supporting children with ASD.

Methods and analysis

Protocol registration and ethics

This study is registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) platform (Registration Number: CRD420251132116). Since it uses data from previously published RCTs, separate ethical approval was not required.

Patient and public involvement

This protocol did not involve patients or the public in its planning, execution, reporting or dissemination. Because the review relies entirely on data from previously published RCTs, there was no requirement to collect new information directly from patients or community members.

Planned start and end dates

The study is planned to commence on 1 December 2025 and is expected to be completed by 30 April 2026.

Rationale for primary and secondary outcomes

Social withdrawal (primary outcome)

Social withdrawal is a core difficulty in ASD, restricting peer engagement, joint attention and participation in everyday social interactions. Music therapy provides an interactive and motivating context for practising social skills through rhythm, turn-taking and emotional communication. Evidence from clinical trials suggests that music therapy can improve social initiation, adaptive functioning and parent–child interactions in children with ASD.25 26 However, findings are heterogeneous, and a comprehensive synthesis is needed to clarify the magnitude and consistency of effects.

Parent–child relationship (secondary outcome)

The parent–child relationship is a critical determinant of child development in ASD. Family-centred music therapy approaches, such as parent–child joint music-making programmes, have been associated with improvements in dyadic engagement, communication and reductions in ASD-related symptoms.27 Broader systematic reviews also indicate that music therapy may strengthen family interactions and relational bonds, although evidence across studies remains variable.25

Happiness (secondary outcome)

Happiness will be assessed using validated self-reported, caregiver-reported or clinician-reported instruments that explicitly measure happiness, such as standardised happiness scales or other validated measures identified by the study authors as assessing happiness. Although research explicitly measuring happiness in children with ASD is limited, music interventions are frequently associated with enhanced emotional well-being, including reductions in anxiety and stress.28 Observational studies of parent–child music–play interactions report increases in shared positive affect and emotional engagement, suggesting potential benefits for happiness and subjective well-being.29

Measurement integrity

All outcomes (social withdrawal, parent–child relationship and happiness) will be assessed using validated instruments as reported in eligible studies. Examples include the Social Responsiveness Scale, the Autism Treatment Evaluation Checklist, the Child–Parent Relationship Scale and comparable standardised measures. Using validated tools will support consistency and reliability across studies.

Definition of randomised controlled trial

RCTs are studies in which participants are randomly allocated to an intervention group or a comparison/control group (which may involve usual care, an alternative intervention or placebo). Both individual-level and cluster-randomised designs will be included. Studies described as ‘randomised’ but lacking sufficient detail on the randomisation or allocation process will undergo further assessment before eligibility is confirmed.30

Definition of quasi-experimental studies

QES are non-randomised designs that evaluate intervention effects where random allocation is not feasible.31 Participants are assigned to groups through methods such as convenience sampling, matching or allocation based on pre-existing characteristics. Common designs include pre–post comparisons, non-equivalent control groups and interrupted time-series analyses. Although QES provide valuable insights into intervention effectiveness in naturalistic settings, they are generally more vulnerable to selection bias and confounding than RCTs.

Handling missing data

If essential data (eg, means and SD) are not reported, corresponding authors will be contacted to request the missing information. When data cannot be retrieved, appropriate imputation strategies may be applied. If imputation is not feasible or would introduce substantial bias, the study will be excluded from quantitative synthesis but retained in the narrative review. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to examine the robustness of findings in relation to missing data.32 33

Each domain will be rated as low, moderate, serious or critical risk of bias, with an overall judgement derived from domain-level assessments. This comprehensive approach ensures a thorough evaluation of potential biases in QES.

Certainty of evidence assessment (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation)

The certainty of evidence for each primary and secondary outcome will be assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.37 38 Two reviewers will independently rate the certainty of evidence as high, moderate, low or very low based on five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias.37 Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. A summary of findings table will be presented to report the overall certainty of evidence for the main outcomes.37 38

Statistical analysis and data synthesis

Data from eligible studies will be synthesised using both qualitative and quantitative approaches. If homogeneity in study design, participants, interventions and outcome measures is sufficient, a meta-analysis will be performed. Effect sizes will be calculated for each PROSPERO-registered outcome, including measures of primary outcome (social withdrawal) and secondary outcomes (parent–child relationship and happiness). For continuous outcomes, mean differences (MD) or standardised mean differences (SMD) with 95% CIs will be used.40 For dichotomous outcomes, risk ratios (RR) or ORs with 95% CI will be calculated.41 When multiple validated instruments are reported for the same outcome within a study, the most appropriate or most frequently reported measure will be selected according to a predefined outcome hierarchy to avoid double counting.

A random-effects model will be applied as the primary approach to account for between-study variability. The DerSimonian–Laird (DL) method will be used as the default estimator, with exploration of alternative estimators if the number of studies is small or if the DL method appears inappropriate.42 The inverse−variance method will be used for study weighting. All pooled estimates will be reported with 95% CIs.

Heterogeneity will be assessed using the χ² test and quantified with the I² statistic, with thresholds of 25%, 50% and 75% indicating low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively.43 In cases of substantial heterogeneity (I²>50%), subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses will be conducted, exploring factors such as type of intervention (music therapy vs music–movement), delivery format (individual vs group), intervention duration (short term vs long term) and participant characteristics, including age group (3–6 vs 7–13 years) and ASD severity, where reported. Where sufficient studies (generally≥10) are available, meta-regression may be conducted to further explore potential sources of heterogeneity.43

If meta-analysis is not feasible because of insufficient data, outcome heterogeneity or methodological differences, a narrative synthesis will be presented. Studies will be summarised by intervention type and outcome domain. Publication bias will be evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test if ≥10 studies are available.44 All analyses will be conducted using Review Manager (RevMan) and/or Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

ASD bring increasing harms to children and their family. This study will incorporate existing literature on the use of music therapy and music–movement intervention in the treatment of children with ASD. The results of this review will provide a comprehensive overview of the reporting and methodological quality of existing studies as well as the effects of music therapy in healthcare for preschool education.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval is not required, as this review involves only the secondary analysis of published data and does not involve direct contact with human participants. The findings will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed journals. Presentations at relevant academic and clinical conferences.

This review aims to provide robust, evidence-based guidance for clinicians, educators and families regarding the effectiveness of music therapy and music–movement interventions in supporting children with ASD. The results will also identify gaps in the current literature and inform future research priorities.
