Sign changes on ∑ f ⁡ ( n ) n \sum\frac{f(n)}{\sqrt{n}} for random completely multiplicative f f
Rodrigo Angelo

Abstract
. If f : ℕ → { ± 1 } f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\} is a sample of the random completely multiplicative function, we show that almost surely ∑ n ≤ x f ⁡ ( n ) n \sum_{n\leq x}\frac{f(n)}{\sqrt{n}} changes signs infinitely many times, answering a question of Aymone.



1.
Introduction

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
be the random completely multiplicative function, defined by
f(p)=\pm 1
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
independently at the primes, and then extended multiplicatively to the natural numbers. Given
\alpha\in[0,1]
, Aymone
[
1
]
considered the question of sign changes in partial sums of

\sum_{n}\frac{f(n)}{n^{\alpha}}.

For
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
the sum is known to converge to a positive number with probability
1
as
x\rightarrow\infty
(namely, it converges to its Euler product), therefore changes signs finitely many times. For
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
he stablished that the sum changes sign infinitely many times as
x\rightarrow\infty
with probability
1
. In this paper we settle the case
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
that was previously left open:

Theorem 1.1
.

If
f
is a sample of the random completely multiplicative function then with probability
1
the sum

S_{x}=\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{f(n)}{\sqrt{n}}

changes signs infinitely many times as
x\rightarrow\infty
.

Our method is akin to
[
2
]
, using oscilations in
\sum\frac{f(n)}{n^{s}}
for real
s\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}^{+}
to produce sign changes, without using ever using complex values of
s
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Let

F(t)=\int_{1}^{\infty}S_{x}\frac{dx}{x^{1+t}}.

By integration by parts for
t>0

F(t)=\int_{1}^{\infty}S_{x}\frac{dx}{x^{1+t}}=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{f(n)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}+t}}.

Where we use the fact that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{f(n)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}+t}}
converges almost surely for
t>0
. If
S_{x}
is positive for
x\geq X_{0}
, then

F(t)=\int_{1}^{\infty}S_{x}\frac{dx}{x^{1+t}}\geq\int_{X_{0}}^{\infty}S_{x}\frac{dx}{x^{1+t}}-C^{\prime}\geq\int_{X_{0}}^{\infty}S_{x}\frac{dx}{x^{1+2t}}-C^{\prime}\geq\int_{1}^{\infty}S_{x}\frac{dx}{x^{1+2t}}-C=F(2t)-C,

so in that case one has
F(t)\geq F(2t)-C
for every
t>0
where
C
is a constant depending only on
X_{0}
(if
S_{n}
is eventually negative we can obtain
F(t)\leq F(2t)+C
and make a similar argument then).

From now on, assume
t
belongs to a sequence
t_{1},t_{2},...
tending to zero quickly, say
t_{i}=2^{-2^{i}}
. By the almost sure Euler product we have

F(t)=\frac{1}{t}\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{f(p)}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+t}}\right)^{-1}=\exp\left(\sum_{p}\frac{f(p)}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+t}}+\frac{3}{2}\log\frac{1}{t}+O(1)\right).

The probability that
\sum_{p}\frac{f(p)}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+t}}<-\log\frac{1}{t}
is
\ll\frac{1}{\log 1/t}
(by a variance computation, namely
\mathbb{E}[(\sum_{p}\frac{f(p)}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+t}})^{2}]=\sum_{p}\frac{1}{p^{1+2t}}\ll\log\frac{1}{t}
), so as long as
\sum\frac{1}{\log 1/t_{i}}
converges this happens for finitely many
t_{i}
a.s. So except in an event of probability
0
one has for small enough
t_{i}
the inequality
F(t_{i})>\frac{1}{t_{i}^{1/2}}>2C
. So for small enough
t
in the sequence,
F(t)\geq F(2t)-C
implies
F(t)>F(2t)/2
(if
S_{x}
is eventually negative, we can make the case
F(t)<2F(2t)
). By our approximation for
F
this implies

R(t)=\sum_{p}f(p)(\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+t}}-\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+2t}})>-K,

for some constant
K
(it is an explicit number that does not even depend on
X_{0}
). But because the maximum over
p
of
(\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+t}}-\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+2t}})
tends to
0
as
t
tends to
0
, we have that
R(t)
satisfies a central limit theorem, and converges in distribution to a gaussian with variance

\sum_{p}(\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+t}}-\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+2t}})^{2}=\log(1/2t)+\log(1/4t)-2\log(1/3t)+O(t)\rightarrow\log(9/8)=0.117...

(here we used the approximation
\sum\frac{1}{p^{1+t}}=\log\frac{1}{t}+c_{0}+O(t)
).

So the probability that
R(t)>-K
converges to
1-\delta
for a fixed constant
\delta
(coming from a tail of a gaussian fo variance
\log(9/8)
after
K
). As long as
t_{i}
decreases quickly enough, the correlation between
R(t_{i})
and
R(t_{i+1})
is negligible, making them close to independent. Indeed for
t_{2}<t_{1}
their correlation is

\sum_{p}(\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+t_{i}}}-\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+2t_{i}}})(\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+t_{i+1}}}-\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+2t_{i+1}}})=\log\frac{t_{i}+2t_{i+1}}{t_{i}+t_{i+1}}+\log\frac{2t_{i}+t_{i+1}}{2t_{i}+2t_{i+1}}+O(t_{i})\rightarrow 0

since
\frac{t_{i+1}}{t_{i}}\rightarrow 0
.

With this we can establish with a multivariate central limit theorem that if
m
is fixed,
(R(t_{i+1}),...,R(t_{i+m}))
tends in distribution to
m
independent Gaussians as
i\rightarrow\infty
. Here we can use
[
3
]
using the matrices
A_{i}=(\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+t_{n+k}}}-\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+2t_{n+k}}})_{p,k}
for
k=1,2,...,m
and
p=2,3,5,...,p_{i}
where
k_{n}
is a further out truncation of the primes, and
\epsilon(i)=(f(2),f(3),...,f(p_{i}))
. The correlation computations we made imply that that the covariance matrix
B_{n}
converges to the diagonal matrix
\operatorname{diag}(\log(9/8),...\log(9/8))
. The fact that the maximum over
p
of
(\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+t}}-\frac{1}{p^{\frac{1}{2}+2t}})
tends to zero means condition I
∗
of the main theorem of
[
3
]
applies, and conditions II and III hold trivially since the random variable
f(p)
is imply
\pm 1
. The conclusion of the Theorem provides that
A_{i}\epsilon(i)=(R(t_{i+1}),...,R(t_{i+m}))
tends in distribution to
N(0,\operatorname{diag}(\log(9/8),...\log(9/8)))
.

So the probability each of these is
>-K
converges to
(1-\delta)^{m}
. This bounds above the probability that
S_{x}
nonnegative for
x\geq X_{0}
by
(1-\delta)^{m}
, where
\delta
is an absolute constant. Because
m
can be made arbitrarily large, we obtain this probability is
0
. This argument can be made for
X_{0}=1,2,...,
and because the union of countably many events of probability
0
is
0
, we obtain the probability that there exists
X_{0}
such that
S_{x}\geq 0
for
x\geq X_{0}
is
0
. Similarly the probability that there exists
X_{0}
such that
S_{x}\leq 0
for
x\geq X_{0}
is
0
(the argument is the same but with the inequality
F(t)\leq F(2t)+C
and then
F(t)<2F(2t)\Rightarrow R(t)<K
instead). As a consequence, the probability that
S_{x}
experiences finitely many sign changes is
0
.
