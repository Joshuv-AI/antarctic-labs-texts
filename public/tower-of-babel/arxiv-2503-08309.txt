1
Introduction

We investigate the asymptotic behavior of a one-dimensional higher-order Ginzburg-Landau type model, which is given by the family of free-energy functionals

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[u]\coloneq\int_{I}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(u)-\lambda\varepsilon^{2n-3}(u^{(n-1)})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2n-1}(u^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x,\quad u\in W^{n,2}(I),

(1.1)

where
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is an open bounded interval,
\lambda>0
is a constant,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 2}
is the order of the highest derivative, and
W\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R})
is a suitable double-well potential. Functionals of this form are used to model the behavior of complex materials undergoing phase separation processes (see e.g.
[
CMM92
]
). Using the framework of
\Gamma
-convergence, we derive an asymptotic effective model for the family of functionals given in (
1.1
) as
\varepsilon\to 0
. More precisely, we show that for every order
n
there exists a critical parameter
\lambda_{n}>0
such that the
\Gamma
-limit of
G^{\lambda,n}_{\varepsilon}
with respect to the
L^{1}
-topology in the subcritical regime
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{n})
is given by a constant multiple of the perimeter functional.

The energy
G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}
is a generalization of several known phase separation models.
For instance, the family of functionals
G_{\varepsilon}^{0,1}
represents a classical first-order phase transition model introduced by Cahn and Hilliard
[
CH58
]
. It describes the free energy of a system where phase separation processes between two coexisting isotropic liquids occur. From the theory of thermodynamics it is known that, for many materials, the interface between these phases becomes thinner with decreasing temperature (see e.g.
[
RS82
]
). This effect motivates the investigation of the functional’s asymptotic behavior as the parameter
\varepsilon
, describing the non-dimensionalized thickness of the transition layer, tends to zero. Based on the work of Modica and Mortola
[
MM77
]
, Modica
[
Mod87
]
and Sternberg
[
Ste88
]
demonstrated a certain compactness property for sequences with equi-bounded energy and
\Gamma
-convergence of
G_{\varepsilon}^{0,1}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
towards a sharp interface functional, provided that the double-well potential
W
satisfies a linear coercivity condition. Corresponding results were later shown for
G_{\varepsilon}^{0,2}
by Fonseca and Mantegazza
[
FM00
]
, and extended to higher orders by Brusca et al.
[
BDS24
]
who presented an examination of
G_{\varepsilon}^{0,n}
for arbitrary
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Specifically, they derived a suitable general compactness property and showed that the sequence
G_{\varepsilon}^{0,n}

\Gamma
-converges to a sharp interface functional.

Coleman et al.
[
CMM92
]
introduced the second-order model
G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,2}
. It describes phase separation processes in nonlinear materials that exhibit periodic layering phenomena, for instance, in concentrated soap solutions or metallic alloys. They argue that if the negative contribution exceeds a certain threshold, the phases become unstable and minimizers of this energy may develop a periodic structure. This was later rigorously confirmed by Mizel et al.
[
MPT98
]
using variational methods. Therein, they proved that for the standard double-well potential
W(t)=(t-1)^{2}(t+1)^{2}
and sufficiently large
\lambda>0
, the minimal energy tends to
-\infty
with increasing oscillation as
\varepsilon\to 0
. However, Cicalese et al.
[
CSZ11
]
and Chermisi et al.
[
CMFL11
]
studied the asymptotic behavior of
G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,2}
through
\Gamma
-convergence for small
\lambda>0
. They found that there exists a critical constant
\bar{\lambda}>0
depending on
W
such that for
\lambda\in(0,\bar{\lambda})
, the
\Gamma
-limit is again given by a sharp interface functional if
W
satisfies a quadratic coercivity condition. While Chermisi et al.
[
CMFL11
]
focused on considering the model in higher dimensions, Cicalese et al.
[
CSZ11
]
dealt with the one-dimensional problem but provided an upper bound for
\bar{\lambda}
such that no oscillations of the minimizers occur and a lower bound such that oscillations certainly arise. In addition, Hilhorst et al.
[
HPS02
]
provided a consistent
\Gamma
-convergence result for
\lambda<0
.

In the case
\lambda>0
, the energy functional
G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,2}
incorporates a strictly concave term, making it difficult to find uniform lower bounds. To overcome this issue, Cicalese et al.
[
CSZ11
]
and Chermisi et al.
[
CMFL11
]
found nonlinear interpolation inequalities, which allowed for a lower bound of the energy in terms of
G_{\varepsilon}^{0,2}
and
G_{\varepsilon}^{0,1}
respectively, provided the loss of convexity is not too strong (meaning
\lambda>0
is sufficiently small). Furthermore, nonlinear interpolation inequalities are also used in the theory of nonlocal phase separation models, where control in terms of positive local phase separation models is achieved (see e.g.
[
FHLZ16
,
GPZ24
]
, see also
[
Sol24
]
).

In this work, we aim to find a matching theory for the higher-order functionals
G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}
given by (
1.1
). The starting point of our analysis are the results from
[
CSZ11
]
, where the related second-order functional was investigated, and impose the same quadratic coercivity condition on the double-well potential (see (
W3
)). The latter is needed in order to derive an adapted nonlinear interpolation inequality (see Section
3.1
) which in turn is used to obtain
L^{1}
-compactness for sequences with equi-bounded energy (see Section
3.2
). Finally, we show
\Gamma
-convergence towards a sharp interface functional as
\varepsilon\to 0
(see Section
4
).

This article is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the mathematical setup, preliminaries, and state our main results. In Section
3
, we focus on the consideration and derivation of suitable interpolation inequalities and prove compactness for our family of energy functionals. In Section
4
, we prove its
\Gamma
-convergence. Some auxiliary interpolation inequalities are moved to the appendix.

2
Preliminaries and Main Results

In the following, we always assume
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 2}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lambda>0
. We restrict ourselves to the one-dimensional case where
I\subset\mathbb{R}
denotes an open bounded interval of length

. Given

and

, we denote as usual the Lebesgue spaces by

and the Sobolev spaces by

\displaystyle\|f\|^{p}_{L^{p}(I)}\coloneqq\int_{I}|f(x)|^{p}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{W^{m,p}(I)}\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{m}\|f^{(j)}\|_{L^{p}(I)},

(2.1)

where

denotes the

-th weak derivative of

. We denote by

the space of functions with bounded variation only taking the values

almost everywhere, i.e.

\displaystyle V[u]\coloneqq\sup\left\{\int_{I}u(x)\,\varphi^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x:\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(I),\ \|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leq 1\right\}<\infty.

(2.2)

It is well known that

with finitely many points of discontinuities. We note that

, where

. For more details, see
[

]
. In the following, we implicitly identify

with

. In this article, we use

We fix the double-well potential

and impose the following assumptions.

(W1)

W
is continuous and
W\geq 0
.

(W2)

W(t)=0
if and only if

.

(W3)

There exists
L>0

These assumptions on the double-well potential match the assumptions already imposed e.g. in

,
CMFL11
]
. Lastly, given

, we define
G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}\colon L^{1}(I)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[u]\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\int_{I}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(u)-\lambda\varepsilon^{2n-3}(u^{(n-1)})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2n-1}(u^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x&\quad\text{for all }u\in W^{n,2}(I),\\[10.0pt]
+\infty&\quad\text{for all }u\in L^{1}(I)\setminus W^{n,2}(I).\end{array}\right.

Our first main theorem establishes a novel nonlinear interpolation inequality of arbitrary order of differentiation, based on the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality
[

,
Nir59
]
and the techniques developed in

, Section 3]

Theorem 2.1

(Nonlinear interpolation for higher orders)
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 2}
and assume
W
satisfies
(

)
–
(

)
. Then there exists

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\int_{I}(u^{(n-1)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{|I|^{2n-2}}\int_{I}W(u)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+|I|^{2}\int_{I}(u^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x

The proof is given at the end of Section

. The main difficulties lie in the incorporation of the nonlinear double-well potential

, and in obtaining suitable scaling factors. Replacing the

-norm by the nonlinear term in the interpolation requires the use of the coercivity condition (

) on intervals where

.

Remark 2.2
.

We note that a subquadratic coercivity condition, e.g. linear coercivity, instead of
(

)
would not suffice to show (

). This is because the double-well potential does not provide sufficient control over fine-scale oscillations in the subquadratic case. A detailed elaboration of this reasoning for the case

can be found in
[

, Section 3.1]

We fix
\lambda_{n}>0
to be the optimal constant established in Theorem

. We call

and supercritical if
\lambda>\lambda_{n}
. Theorem

allows us to control the concave term in

by the remaining non-negative terms in the subcritical regime. In Section

, we use this control to estimate

from below by

up to a constant. Brusca et al.
[

]
already found that sequences with equi-bounded energy

are precompact with respect to convergence in measure. Thus, we obtain compactness in

of the energy functional

by combining the result from
[
BDS24
]
and Vitali’s Convergence Theorem. We note that in the following

means that the statement holds for every sequence

as

.

Theorem 2.3

(Compactness)
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 2}
,
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{n})

(

)
–
(

)
. Let

be an open bounded interval. Let

. Our last main theorem deals with the asymptotic behavior of the family of energies

. For this purpose, we introduce the sharp interface functional

\displaystyle G^{\lambda,n}[u]\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C_{W}^{\lambda,n}\#S(u),&\quad\text{for all }u\in BV(I,\{\pm 1\}),\\

\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(f)-\lambda(f^{(n-1)})^{2}+(f^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x:f\in{\mathcal{A}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})\right\},

While the explicit value of

Theorem 2.4

(
\Gamma
-convergence)
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 2}
,
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{n})
and assume
W
satisfies
(
W1
)
–
(
W3
)
. Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open bounded interval. Then
G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\longrightarrow}}G^{\lambda,n}

-topology, that is

•

for every

and every

such that

, we have

\displaystyle G^{\lambda,n}[u]\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[u_{\varepsilon}].

)

•

for every
u\in L^{1}(I)
there exists
u_{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}(I)
such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[u_{\varepsilon}]\leq G^{\lambda,n}[u].

)

The Theorem follows from the combination of Proposition
4.4
and Proposition
4.5
, which are proved in Section

. It reveals that, in the subcritical regime, the diffuse interface energy

converges to the sharp interface model

.

The Limsup inequality is obtained by a well established approach from the theory of singular perturbation models (see e.g.
[
CSZ11
,
CMFL11
,
BDS24
]
). The primary challenge is the proof of the Liminf inequality. In order to obtain the desired estimate, for every sequence

under consideration, we construct an energetically favored competitor sequence (see Section

). It is necessary to make further considerations due to the higher-order derivatives, in particular we must ensure that the favored competitor belongs to

. This is achieved by a suitable coupling of the two phases, whose energy is controlled using a suitable version of the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality (see Theorem
A.5
).

Remark 2.5
.

As usual in phase transition models, one can impose a mass constraint by considering the energy functional by changing the class of admissible functions from
W^{n,2}(I)
to

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathcal{A}}^{n}}(I)\coloneqq\left\{u\in W^{n,2}(I):\int_{I}u(x)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x=m\right\}

(2.10)

for some fixed
m\in(-|I|,|I|)
. However, the changes in the analysis are minor, and therefore we refrain from imposing it.

Remark 2.6
.

It might be worthwhile to rigorously generalize the theory to higher dimensions by considering open and bounded domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
instead of intervals
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, which is usually done by developing a higher-dimensional variant of the nonlinear interpolation inequality, using Fubini’s Theorem and blow-up arguments (see
[
CMFL11
]
, see also
[
Bra98
]
). In addition, as in the case

, the explicit dependence of the critical parameter
\lambda_{n}
on
W
and
n
is unknown to us.

3
Compactness

In this section, we prove the compactness property given by Theorem
2.3
, which is needed to show our
\Gamma
-convergence result. To this end, in Section
3.1
, we establish a novel Gagliardo-Nirenberg type inequality that contains the nonlinear double-well potential term. In Section
3.2
, using this interpolation inequality, we find a lower bound for
G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}
up to a constant, namely the energy
G_{\varepsilon}^{0,n}
for which a compactness result has already been shown by Brusca et al.
[
BDS24
]
. In the last step, we transfer the compactness statement into the
L^{1}
-topology using Vitali’s Convergence theorem.

3.1
Nonlinear interpolation

In order to obtain a lower bound for
G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}
, we exploit the structure of the energy functional and estimate the second highest-order term by the highest-order term and the potential term. This recalls the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality from Theorem
A.5
. In order to adapt it to the present setting, we first consider another auxiliary interpolation, which is a variant of Lemma
A.2
for the special case
p=q=r=2
and can also be found in
[
CMFL11
, Lemma 3.3]
. The main difference lies in the more general scaling factors, which are relevant to the proof of the nonlinear interpolation in Theorem

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 2}
. There exists

\displaystyle c\int_{I}(u^{(n-1)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2n-2}}\int_{I}u^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+\sigma^{2}\int_{I}(u^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x

for every open bounded interval

and every

Proof.

We prove the inequality by induction on

starting with

. The case

is covered by Lemma

with the choice

. Now, for

, we set

. We subdivide the interval

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{I}(u^{\prime})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x&=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{I_{i}}(u^{\prime})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}\int_{I_{i}}u^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\int_{I_{i}}(u^{\prime\prime})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\right)\\

(3.2)

Now we assume that (

\displaystyle\int_{I}(u^{(n-1)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2(n-1)}}\int_{I}u^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+\sigma^{2}\int_{I}(u^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\right)

. Let

\displaystyle\int_{I}(u^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\int_{I}(u^{(n-1)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+\sigma_{1}^{2}\int_{I}(u^{(n+1)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\right)

(3.4)

\displaystyle\hskip-3.92648pt\overset{\eqref{Nir_IA}}{\leq}C_{3}\left(\frac{C_{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{2}^{2(n-1)}}\int_{I}u^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+\sigma_{2}^{2}\int_{I}(u^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\right)+\sigma_{1}^{2}\int_{I}(u^{(n+1)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\right),

\displaystyle lm^{2}\leq C\left(\frac{1}{l^{2n-2}}\int_{0}^{l}W(u)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+l^{2}\int_{0}^{l}(u^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\right).

(3.11)

Without loss of generality, we assume
m^{2}>4l\int_{0}^{l}(u^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x
, otherwise the claim follows immediately from
W\geq 0
. Using Jensen’s inequality, we then have
2\|u^{(n)}\|_{L^{1}(I)}<|m|
and hence

\displaystyle 2|m|-2|u^{(n-1)}(y)|\leq 2|m-u^{(n-1)}(y)|\overset{\eqref{u-m_Ska_n}}{\leq}2\int_{0}^{l}|u^{(n)}|\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x<|m|.

(3.12)

Thus, we obtain
0\leq\frac{1}{2}|m|<|u^{(n-1)}(y)|
for all
y\in I
. Consequently, applying Rolle’s Theorem inductively yields that
u
has at most
n-1
roots in
I
. We denote by
(\alpha,\beta)\subset I
some subinterval of maximal length without roots of
u
, where
\beta-\alpha\geq\frac{l}{n}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
u>0
on
(\alpha,\beta)
. We now apply Lemma
3.1
with
\sigma=\frac{l}{n}
and the coercivity condition (
W3
) to
u-1
, and obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}c\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}(u^{(n-1)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x&\overset{\eqref{eq:NirIneq_n}}{\leq}\frac{n^{2n-2}}{l^{2n-2}}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}(u-1)^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+\frac{l^{2}}{n^{2}}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}(u^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\\
&\overset{{\rm{(\hyperref@@ii[it-W3]{W3})}}}{\leq}n^{2n-2}\max\left\{\frac{1}{L},\frac{1}{n^{2n}}\right\}\left(\frac{1}{l^{2n-2}}\int_{0}^{l}W(u)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+l^{2}\int_{0}^{l}(u^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\right).\end{split}

(3.13)

Moreover, using (
3.12
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{l}{n}m^{2}\leq\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}m^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\leq 4\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}(u^{(n-1)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x

(3.14)

and hence, combining (
3.13
) and (
3.14
), the claim (
3.11
).
∎

From now on, the constant
\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{n}(L)>0
is fixed to be the optimal constant in Theorem
2.1
. As alluded to in Section
2
, it separates the sub- and supercritical regime for the parameter
\lambda
.

Analogously to
[
CSZ11
, Remark 3.2]
, by subdividing
\mathbb{R}
into intervals of the form
(k,k+1)
with
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and applying (
2.5
) to each of these intervals, we obtain the following version of Theorem
2.1
on the real line.

Corollary 3.2

(Nonlinear interpolation on the real line)
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 2}
and assume
W
satisfies
(
W1
)
–
(
W3
)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u^{(n-1)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(u)+(u^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\text{for all }u\in W^{n,2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}).

(3.15)

3.2
Lower bound and compactness

Now, we use the nonlinear interpolation inequality from Theorem
2.1
to estimate the energy
G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}
from below by the related energy functional
G_{\varepsilon}^{0,n}
without the negative term. The latter has already been examined regarding compactness and
\Gamma
-convergence by Brusca et al.
[
BDS24
]
, where the case
n=2
was previously studied by Fonseca and Mantegazza
[
FM00
]
. The strategy for the estimation originates from
[
CSZ11
, Proposition 3.3]
.

Lemma 3.3

(Lower bound)
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 2}
and
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{n})
and assume
W
satisfies
(
W1
)
–
(
W3
)
. Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open bounded interval and
\delta>0
. Then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle\Big(1-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{n}}-\delta\Big)G_{\varepsilon}^{0,n}[u]\leq G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[u]

\displaystyle\text{for all }u\in W^{n,2}(I),\ \varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}).

(3.16)

Proof.

We use the substitution
x\mapsto\varepsilon x
, with
v(x)\coloneq u(\varepsilon x)
and
\frac{I}{\varepsilon}\coloneq\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\varepsilon x\in I\}
. For every
u\in W^{n,2}(I)
, this results in the representation

\displaystyle G^{\lambda,n}_{\varepsilon}[u]=\int_{\frac{I}{\varepsilon}}W(v(x))-\lambda(v^{(n-1)}(x))^{2}+(v^{(n)}(x))^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x.

(3.17)

Next, we set
m_{\varepsilon}\coloneq\min\{m\in\mathbb{N}:m\geq\frac{|I|}{\varepsilon}\}
and subdivide the interval
\frac{I}{\varepsilon}
into
m_{\varepsilon}
disjoint open intervals
I_{\varepsilon}^{1},\ldots,I_{\varepsilon}^{m_{\varepsilon}}
each of length
\frac{|I|}{\varepsilon m_{\varepsilon}}\eqcolon l_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
. Applying Theorem
2.1
on each subinterval, we get

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[u]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\varepsilon}}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}^{i}}W(v)-\lambda(v^{(n-1)})^{2}+(v^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x

(3.18)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\varepsilon}}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}^{i}}\!W(v)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{n}}\bigg(\frac{1}{l_{\varepsilon}^{2n-2}}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}^{i}}\!W(v)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+l_{\varepsilon}^{2}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}^{i}}(v^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\bigg)+\int_{I_{\varepsilon}^{i}}(v^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x

(3.19)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{n}l_{\varepsilon}^{2n-2}}\right)\int_{\frac{I}{\varepsilon}}W(v)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+\left(1-\frac{\lambda l_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{\lambda_{n}}\right)\int_{\frac{I}{\varepsilon}}(v^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x.

(3.20)

The claim follows from resubstitution and
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}l_{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{|I|}{\varepsilon m_{\varepsilon}}=1
, which is a direct consequence of the definition of
m_{\varepsilon}
.
∎

The compactness property for
G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}
(see Theorem
2.3
) can now be derived from Lemma
3.3
and the corresponding compactness property for
G_{\varepsilon}^{0,n}
proven by Brusca et al. for even more general double-well potentials (see
[
BDS24
, Proposition 5.1]
). However, they show compactness with respect to convergence in measure. In order to improve the result to
L^{1}
-convergence, we use Vitali’s Convergence Theorem by employing the coercivity condition (
W3
). In contrast to the proof of Theorem
2.1
, a linear coercivity condition would suffice here as well.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

According to Vitali’s Convergence Theorem (see
[
Kle20
, Satz 6.25]
), the convergence of
u_{\varepsilon}
in
L^{1}(I)
is equivalent to the convergence in measure if
u_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly integrable, that is, if it satisfies

(i)

\sup_{\varepsilon}\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}(I)}<\infty
and

(ii)

for every
\gamma>0
there exists
\delta(\gamma)>0
such that
\sup_{\varepsilon}\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}(J)}\leq\gamma
for every measurable set
J\subset I
with
|J|<\delta(\gamma)

(see
[
Kle20
, Satz 6.24]
). Moreover, the theorem states that the two limit functions coincide. Therefore, if we show the uniform integrability of
u_{\varepsilon}
, we can deduce the claim from the compactness result
[
BDS24
, Proposition 5.1]
. Without loss of generality, after choosing a subsequence, we may consider
u_{\varepsilon}\in W^{n,2}(I)
with
\sup_{\varepsilon}G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[u_{\varepsilon}]\leq\tilde{M}<\infty
.

To show
(i)
, we use the coercivity condition (
W3
) and obtain

\displaystyle W(t)\geq L(t\mp 1)^{2}\geq L(\pm 2t-3)\quad\text{for all }\pm t\geq 0,

(3.21)

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{W(t)}{2L}+\frac{3}{2}\geq|t|\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.22)

Together with Lemma
3.3
, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, this yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{I}|u_{\varepsilon}|\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x&\leq\frac{1}{2L}\int_{I}W(u_{\varepsilon})\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+\frac{3}{2}|I|\leq\frac{1}{2L}\int_{I}W(u_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon^{2n}(u_{\varepsilon}^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+\frac{3}{2}|I|\\
&=\frac{\varepsilon}{2L}G_{\varepsilon}^{0,n}[u_{\varepsilon}]+\frac{3}{2}|I|\leq\frac{\varepsilon C}{2L}G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[u_{\varepsilon}]+\frac{3}{2}|I|\leq\frac{\varepsilon C}{2L}\tilde{M}+\frac{3}{2}|I|\leq M<\infty,\end{split}

(3.23)

where
M
only depends on
L
,
n
and
\lambda
.

To show
(ii)
, let
\gamma>0
, and choose
\varepsilon_{0}\coloneq\frac{L}{C\tilde{M}}\gamma
. Then, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon C}{2L}\tilde{M}\leq\frac{\gamma}{2}\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}).

(3.24)

Consequently, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, if we choose
\delta\coloneq\frac{\gamma}{3}
and reuse the estimate (
3.23
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{J}|u_{\varepsilon}|\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{\varepsilon C}{2L}\tilde{M}+\frac{3}{2}|J|\leq\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}=\gamma\quad\text{for }|J|\leq\delta,

(3.25)

which concludes the proof.
∎

In Lemma
3.3
, a straightforward application of the nonlinear interpolation inequality from Theorem
2.1
yielded the desired estimate by a known functional of the same order and therefore the compactness property we needed. However, it is also feasible to estimate
G^{\lambda,n}_{\varepsilon}
by lower-order functionals using a technique by Chermisi et al.
[
CMFL11
, Section 4]
. In the following remark, we briefly discuss this alternative approach.

Remark 3.4

(Alternative lower bound)
.

Instead of bounding
G^{\lambda,n}_{\varepsilon}
from below by
G^{0,n}_{\varepsilon}
as in Lemma
3.3
, it can also be estimated by
G^{0,n-1}_{\varepsilon}
of one order lower. To see this, we again use the representation

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[u]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\varepsilon}}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}^{i}}W(v)-\lambda(v^{(n-1)})^{2}+(v^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x

(3.26)

from the first part in the proof of Lemma
3.3
. Then, we apply a technique from
[
CMFL11
, Section 4]
and write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{W(v)}{l_{\varepsilon}^{2n-2}}-\lambda(v^{(n-1)})^{2}+l_{\varepsilon}^{2}(v^{(n)})^{2}=&\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{n}-\lambda}{\lambda_{n}+1}\right)\!\left(\frac{W(v)}{l_{\varepsilon}^{2n-2}}-\lambda_{n}(v^{(n-1)})^{2}+l_{\varepsilon}^{2}(v^{(n)})^{2}\right)\\
&\ +\frac{\lambda_{n}-\lambda}{\lambda_{n}+1}\left(\frac{W(v)}{l_{\varepsilon}^{2n-2}}+(v^{(n-1)})^{2}+l_{\varepsilon}^{2}(v^{(n)})^{2}\right).\end{split}

(3.27)

According to Theorem
2.1
, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,m_{\varepsilon}\}
it holds

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{1}{l_{\varepsilon}^{2n-2}}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}^{i}}W(v)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x-\lambda_{n}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}^{i}}(v^{(n-1)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x+l_{\varepsilon}^{2}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}^{i}}(v^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x.

(3.28)

Hence, by integrating equation (
3.27
) over
I_{\varepsilon}^{i}
, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}^{i}}\frac{W(v)}{l_{\varepsilon}^{2n-2}}-\lambda(v^{(n-1)})^{2}+l_{\varepsilon}^{2}(v^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x&\geq\frac{\lambda_{n}-\lambda}{\lambda_{n}+1}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}^{i}}\frac{W(v)}{l_{\varepsilon}^{2n-2}}+(v^{(n-1)})^{2}+l_{\varepsilon}^{2}(v^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\\
&\geq\frac{\lambda_{n}-\lambda}{\lambda_{n}+1}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}^{i}}\frac{W(v)}{l_{\varepsilon}^{2n-2}}+(v^{(n-1)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(3.29)

From
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}l_{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{|I|}{\varepsilon m_{\varepsilon}}=1
and by summing over all
I_{\varepsilon}^{i}
, we finally obtain that for any
\delta>0
there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for every
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{n})
,
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
and
u\in W^{n,2}(I)
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\lambda_{n}-\lambda}{\lambda_{n}+1}-\delta\right)G_{\varepsilon}^{0,n-1}[u]\leq G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[u].

(3.30)

In particular, it is possible to bound the second-order functional
G^{\lambda,2}_{\varepsilon}
by the classical Cahn-Hilliard functional
G_{\varepsilon}^{0,1}
, as done by Chermisi et al.
[
CMFL11
, Section 4]
. It is also conceivable to estimate the higher-order functional
G^{\lambda,n}_{\varepsilon}
inductively by
G_{\varepsilon}^{0,1}
for
\lambda>0
sufficiently small through an estimation chain of the type

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}^{0,1}\leq C_{1}G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,2}\leq C_{1}G_{\varepsilon}^{0,2}\leq\ldots\leq C_{n-2}G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n-1}\leq C_{n-2}G_{\varepsilon}^{0,n-1}\leq C_{n-1}G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}.

(3.31)

4
Asymptotic Analysis

In this chapter, we prove the main result stated in Theorem
2.4
. To show the Liminf inequality, we define an energetically favored competitor sequence for every sequence
u_{\varepsilon}\in W^{n,2}(I)
converging in
L^{1}(I)
. The exact construction is explained in Section
4.1
. These auxiliary sequences are piecewise defined, and must therefore be carefully examined for regularity. Thus, we present a technical lemma that addresses the coupling of the different segments of the sequence. In Section
4.2
, we finally show the Liminf inequality and the Limsup inequality, using the compactness property from Section
3.2
and the considerations from Section
4.1
.

4.1
Construction of favored competitors

To prove the Liminf inequality, we consider sequences
u_{\varepsilon}\in W^{n,2}(I)
with
u_{\varepsilon}\to u
in
L^{1}(I)
and
u\in BV(I,\{\pm 1\})
. For each of these sequences, we construct a favored competitor sequence
v_{\varepsilon}\in W^{n,2}(I)
such that

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[u]\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[v_{\varepsilon}]\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[u_{\varepsilon}].

(4.1)

The idea of the construction is to modify the sequence
u_{\varepsilon}
so that it equates the limit function
u
away from its points of discontinuity
s_{1},\ldots,s_{N}\in S(u)
. To ensure that the sequence
v_{\varepsilon}
is a sequence of test functions for the optimal profile problem
C_{W}^{\lambda,n}
(see (
4.20
) below), the coupling of
u_{\varepsilon}
to the constant segments must guarantee the competitor’s absolute continuity of all derivatives up to order
n-1
. For certain
y_{0},\ldots,y_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}
, these couplings are given by rescaled versions of functions
\zeta\in C^{n}([0,1])
satisfying

\displaystyle\zeta(0)=y_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\zeta(1)=1,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\zeta^{(k)}(0)=y_{k}\quad

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\zeta^{(k)}(1)=0\text{ for }k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\},

(4.3)

and functions
\eta\in C^{n}([0,1])
satisfying

\displaystyle\eta(0)=-1\quad

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\eta(1)=y_{0},

(4.4)

\displaystyle\eta^{(k)}(0)=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\eta^{(k)}(1)=y_{k}\text{ for }k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\},,

(4.5)

respectively, depending on whether it is a coupling to
1
or
-1
. In the subsequent considerations, we will choose
y_{0},\ldots,y_{n-1}
as the corresponding evaluations of the derivatives of
u_{\varepsilon}
to ensure the regularity of the favored competitor. We define the sets

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}^{n}_{I}(y)

\displaystyle\coloneq\{\zeta\in C^{n}([0,1]):\zeta\text{ satisfies \eqref{bed1_zeta} and \eqref{bed2_zeta}}\},

(4.6)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}^{n}_{J}(y)

\displaystyle\coloneq\{\eta\in C^{n}([0,1]):\eta\text{ satisfies \eqref{bed1_eta} and \eqref{bed2_eta}}\},

(4.7)

and justify that they are not empty by showing the existence of polynomials satisfying the respective conditions.

Lemma 4.1

(Existence of a coupling)
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 2}
and
y\coloneq(y_{0},\ldots,y_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. There exist polynomials
p_{n}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{n}_{I}(y)
and
q_{n}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{n}_{J}(y)
of degree
N\leq 2n-1
.

Proof.

We first establish the case
p_{n}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{n}_{I}(y)
. In general, polynomials of degree
N\leq 2n-1
and their derivatives are of the form

\displaystyle p_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{2n-1}a_{i}\,x^{i},\qquad p_{n}^{(k)}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{2n-1-k}\frac{(k+i)!}{i!}\,a_{k+i}\,x^{i}\quad\text{for }k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\},

(4.8)

where
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i\in\{0,\ldots,2n-1\}
. With this representation, (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) read as

\displaystyle a_{0}=y_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=0}^{2n-1}a_{i}=1,

(4.9)

\displaystyle k!\,a_{k}=y_{k}\quad

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=0}^{2n-1-k}\frac{(k+i)!}{i!}\,a_{k+i}=0\quad\text{for }k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}.

(4.10)

Consequently, finding a polynomial satisfying (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) is equivalent to solving a system of linear equations given by the matrix equation

\displaystyle\Biggl(\begin{array}[]{rr}\mbox{\large$A$}&\mbox{\large$0$}\\
\mbox{\large$B$}&\mbox{\large$C$}\end{array}\Biggr)\big(\ a_{0}\ \cdots\ a_{2n-1}\ \big)^{T}=\big(\ y_{0}\ \cdots\ y_{n-1}\ 1\ 0\ \cdots\ 0\ \big)^{T}

where the coefficient matrix is a lower block triangular. The submatrices
A=(A_{ij}),\,\allowbreak{B=(B_{ij})},\,\allowbreak C=(C_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are defined as

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(i-1)!,&i=j,\\
0,&i\neq j,\end{array}\right.\qquad B_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{(n+j-1)!}{(n+j-i)!}&i\leq j,\\
0,&i>j,\end{array}\right.\qquad C_{ij}=\frac{(n+j-1)!}{(n+j-i)!}

for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The system of equations (
4.1
) is uniquely solvable if and only if the determinant of the coefficient matrix is non-zero. This determinant is given by the product of the determinants of the submatrices
A
and
C
(see
[
Sil00
, Section 2]
). Since
A
is a diagonal matrix, we can easily calculate

\displaystyle\det(A)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(i-1)!>0.

(4.17)

In order to determine the determinant of
C
, we write

\displaystyle C_{ij}=\frac{(n+j-1)!}{(n+j-i)!}=(i-1)!\binom{n+j-1}{i-1}.

(4.18)

For
D=(D_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
given by
D_{ij}=\binom{n+j-1}{i-1}
, we have
\det(D)=1
(see
[
Net01
, Paragraph 167]
), and thus

\displaystyle\det(C)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(i-1)!\det(D)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(i-1)!>0.

(4.19)

Hence, the coefficient matrix is invertible and (
4.1
) is uniquely solvable, so that we can conclude that there exists a polynomial
p_{n}
of degree
N\leq 2n-1
satisfying (
4.2
) and (
4.3
). The polynomial
q_{n}
can be obtained through translation and reflection of
p_{n}
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

We note that the admissible polynomials in Lemma
4.1
are uniquely determined if we require that the degree be no greater than
2n-1
. In addition, their coefficients continuously depend on the data
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

4.2
\Gamma
-convergence

Equipped with the compactness property from Theorem
2.3
and the investigations from Section
4.1
, we now separately prove the Liminf inequality and the Limsup inequality. However, we first address the well-definedness of the
\Gamma
-limit functional (
2
). Specifically, we show that the optimal profile problem

\displaystyle C_{W}^{\lambda,n}\coloneq\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(f)-\lambda(f^{(n-1)})^{2}+(f^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x:f\in{\mathcal{A}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})\right\},

(4.20)

where
{\mathcal{A}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})\coloneq\left\{f\in W^{n,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R})\colon f(x)=1\text{ for }x>T\text{ and }f(x)=-1\text{ for }x<-T\text{ for some }T>0\right\}
, defines a positive constant. To this end, we refer to the study of the optimal profile problems in
[
BDS24
, Section 4]
and the estimate from Lemma
3.3
.

Proposition 4.3

(Optimal Profile on the real line)
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 2}
,
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{n})
and assume
W
satisfies
(
W1
)
–
(
W3
)
. Then
C^{\lambda,n}_{W}>0
is well-defined.

Proof.

We show the claim by comparing it with the optimal profile problem

\displaystyle C_{W}^{n}\coloneq\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(f)+(f^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x:f\in{\mathcal{A}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})\right\},

(4.21)

which has already been shown to be a positive constant (see
[
BDS24
, Section 4]
). By Corollary
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{n}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(f)+(f^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(f)-\lambda(f^{(n-1)})^{2}+(f^{(n)})^{2}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}x,

(4.22)

which yields the estimate

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_{n}}\right)C^{n}_{W}\leq C_{W}^{\lambda,n}\leq C^{n}_{W}

(4.23)

and therefore the positivity and well-definedness of
C_{W}^{\lambda,n}
.
∎

Knowing that (
4.20
) defines a positive constant, we now show that the functional
G^{\lambda,n}
defined by (
2
) is an asymptotic lower bound for the sequence
G^{\lambda,n}_{\varepsilon}
, thus proving the validity of the Liminf inequality. As described in Section
4.1
, the strategy consists of finding a favored competitor sequence
v_{\varepsilon}\in W^{n,2}(I)
for every sequence
u_{\varepsilon}\in W^{n,2}(I)
that converges in
L^{1}(I)
, and proving (
4.1
). Similar methods for the related functionals
G^{\lambda,2}_{\varepsilon}
and
G^{0,2}_{\varepsilon}
are employed in
[
CSZ11
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
FM00
, Proposition 2.7]
, respectively.

Proposition 4.4

(Liminf inequality)
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 2}
and
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{n})
. For every sequence
{u_{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}(I)}
and
u\in L^{1}(I)
such that
u_{\varepsilon}\to u
in
L^{1}(I)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we have

\displaystyle G^{\lambda,n}[u]\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}G^{\lambda,n}_{\varepsilon}[u_{\varepsilon}].

(4.24)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let
u_{\varepsilon}\in W^{n,2}(I)
and

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}G_{\varepsilon}^{\lambda,n}[u_{\varepsilon}]\leq C<\infty.

(4.25)

Exploiting Theorem
2.3
, we conclude
u\in BV(I,\{\pm 1\})
. Now, let
S(u)\coloneq\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{N}\}

\displaystyle\|u^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R})}<h^{-2+\frac{1}{r}}\frac{1}{3k}\leq h^{-2+\frac{1}{r}}\frac{|u(x)|}{3}.

(A.17)

Hence, for such an
h>0
we have

\displaystyle\psi_{x}(h)

\displaystyle\overset{\hphantom{\eqref{hklein1}}}{\leq}h^{1+\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r}}\|u^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R})}\overset{\eqref{hklein2}}{\leq}h^{-1+\frac{1}{p}}\frac{|u(x)|}{3}=h^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}-1}\left(\int_{x-\frac{h}{2}}^{x+\frac{h}{2}}\left(\frac{|u(x)|}{3}\right)^{q}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}

(A.18)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{hklein1}}{\leq}h^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}-1}\left(\int_{x-\frac{h}{2}}^{x+\frac{h}{2}}\left(\frac{|u(t)|(1+\varepsilon)}{3}\right)^{q}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\!\leq h^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}-1}\left(\int_{x-\frac{h}{2}}^{x+\frac{h}{2}}\left(\frac{|u(t)|}{2}\right)^{q}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}\!\leq\varphi_{x}(h).

(A.19)

Accordingly, it follows that the set
{\{h>0:\psi_{x}(h)\leq\varphi_{x}(h)\}}
is non-empty. The set is also bounded, as there exists
h_{x}>0
such that
\psi_{x}(h_{x})=\varphi_{x}(h_{x})
and
\psi_{x}(h)>\varphi_{x}(h)
for
h>h_{x}
. The latter is evident from the examination of the exponents in (
A.14
). More precisely, since
\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}-1<0<1+\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{r}
and
u\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R})
, the expression
\varphi_{x}(h)
is bounded, whereas
\psi_{x}(h)
approaches infinity as
h\to\infty
. Now, consider the open covering
\bigcup_{x\in E_{k}}\left(x-\frac{h_{x}}{2},x+\frac{h_{x}}{2}\right)
of
E_{k}
. Since
E_{k}
is compact, we can choose a finite subcovering

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{\tilde{N}}I_{k,i}\quad\text{where}\quad I_{k,i}\coloneq\left(x_{i}-\frac{h_{i}}{2},x_{i}+\frac{h_{i}}{2}\right).

(A.20)

The previous considerations ensure the validity of
(ii)
. Lastly, we select a subsystem such that
(iii)
is also satisfied. If there exists
x\in\bigcap_{j=1}^{M}I_{k,i_{j}}
with
M\geq 3
and
i_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,\tilde{N}\}
pairwise different, we define

\displaystyle a_{x}\coloneq\min_{1\leq i\leq\tilde{N}}\{\inf(I_{k,i}):x\in I_{k,i}\}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{x}\coloneq\max_{1\leq i\leq\tilde{N}}\{\sup(I_{k,i}):x\in I_{k,i}\}

(A.21)

and omit all intervals
I_{k,i_{j}}
from the covering except for one of the form
(a_{x},s)
with
s\in\mathbb{R}
and one of the form
(t,b_{x})
with
t\in\mathbb{R}
. By repeating this process for all
x
that lie in more than two intervals of the covering, we obtain, after a finite number of steps, the desired covering
I_{k,1},\ldots,I_{k,N}
satisfying
(i)
–
(iii)
.

Step 2: Extension to
\mathbb{R}
.
Now, we are in a position to show (
A.13
) for
j=1
and
m=2
using Lemma
A.2
. In this case, condition (
A.12
) translates to

\displaystyle\frac{2}{p}=\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{q}

(A.22)

for
1\leq p,q,r<\infty
, and we have
\theta=1-\theta=\frac{1}{2}
. Exploiting the properties of the covering from
Step 1
and applying Lemma
A.2
, which is applicable to open bounded intervals, we get

\displaystyle\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(E_{k})}^{p}

\displaystyle\overset{\ \,\textit{(i)}\,\ }{\leq}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(I_{k,i})}^{p}\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(|I_{k,i}|^{p+1-\frac{p}{r}}\|u^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{r}(I_{k,i})}^{p}+|I_{k,i}|^{-p+1-\frac{p}{q}}\|u\|_{L^{q}(I_{k,i})}^{p}\right)

(A.23)

\displaystyle\overset{\ \textit{(ii)}\ }{=}2C\sum_{i=1}^{N}|I_{k,i}|^{p+1-\frac{p}{r}}\|u^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{r}(I_{k,i})}^{\frac{p}{2}}\left(|I_{k,i}|^{-2-\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}}\|u\|_{L^{q}(I_{k,i})}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}

(A.24)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{bed12GN}}{=}2C\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\|u^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{r}(I_{k,i})}^{r}\right)^{\frac{q}{q+r}}\left(\|u\|_{L^{q}(I_{k,i})}^{q}\right)^{\frac{r}{q+r}}.

(A.25)

With that, using Hölder’s inequality for sums, we further estimate

\displaystyle\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(E_{k})}^{p}

\displaystyle\overset{\hphantom{\textit{(iii)}}}{\leq}2C\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|u^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{r}(I_{k,i})}^{r}\right)^{\frac{q}{q+r}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|u\|_{L^{q}(I_{k,i})}^{q}\right)^{\frac{r}{q+r}}

(A.26)

\displaystyle\overset{\textit{(iii)}}{\leq}4C\|u^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R})}^{\frac{qr}{q+r}}\|u\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})}^{\frac{qr}{q+r}}\overset{\eqref{bed12GN}}{=}4C\|u^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R})}^{\frac{p}{2}}\|u\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})}^{\frac{p}{2}}.

(A.27)

Due to the continuity of
u^{\prime}
, the set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}:u^{\prime}(x)\neq 0\}
is open and thus can be represented as a disjoint union of countably many open intervals
\{J_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
(see
[
RF10
, Chapter 1, Proposition 9]
). We note that
u
is strictly monotone on each of these intervals. Consequently, we have that
J_{k}\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}:u(x)=0\}
consists of at most one point for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and conclude that

\displaystyle\{x\in\mathbb{R}:u^{\prime}(x)\neq 0\text{ and }u(x)=0\}=\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}(J_{k}\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}:u(x)=0\})

(A.28)

is countable, i.e. has Lebesgue measure zero. Accordingly,
u^{\prime}=u^{\prime}\chi_{\{u\neq 0\}}
holds almost everywhere in
\mathbb{R}
, and since
|u^{\prime}\chi_{E_{k}}|\nearrow|u^{\prime}\chi_{\{u\neq 0\}}|
as
k\to\infty
, the Monotone Convergence Theorem yields
\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(E_{k})}\nearrow\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}
. In conclusion, we have

\displaystyle\|u^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}=\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|u^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{p}(E_{k})}\leq C\|u^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R})}\|u\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})},

(A.29)

which corresponds to (
A.13
) for
j=1
and
m=2
.

Step 3: Induction on
m
and
j
.

Finally, we prove (
A.13
) for
\theta=\frac{j}{m}
by induction on the orders. Initially, we consider
j=1
and perform an induction on
m
where the case
m=2
is covered by
Step 2
. We assume the statement holds for
j=1
and a fixed
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 2}
. Now, let (
A.12
) be fulfilled for
m+1
, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{(m+1)r}+\frac{m}{(m+1)q}.

(A.30)

Additionally, we define the exponent
\tilde{p}\coloneq\frac{pq}{2q-p}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{2}{p}=\frac{1}{\tilde{p}}+\frac{1}{q}.

(A.31)

Comparing this with (
A.22
), we notice that we can apply (
A.29
) and subsequently use the induction hypothesis for
u^{\prime}
, such that

\displaystyle\|u^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\tilde{p}}(\mathbb{R})}\|u\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C\|u^{(m+1)}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R})}^{\frac{1}{m}}\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}^{1-\frac{1}{m}}\|u\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})}.

(A.32)

Here, the induction hypothesis is applicable since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{p}}\overset{\eqref{bedtilp}}{=}\frac{2}{p}-\frac{1}{q}\overset{\eqref{bedm+1GN}}{=}\frac{m-1}{mp}+\frac{1}{mr}.

(A.33)

By rearranging, we obtain

\displaystyle\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C\|u^{(m+1)}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R})}^{\frac{1}{m+1}}\|u\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})}^{\frac{m}{m+1}},

(A.34)

which corresponds to the statement for
m+1
.

Now, we perform another induction to prove (
A.13
). Initially, we suppose the statement holds for fixed arbitrary
j,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
j<m
and aim to deduce the statement for
j+1
and
m+1
. Accordingly, we assume

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}=\frac{j+1}{(m+1)r}+\frac{m-j}{(m+1)q},

(A.35)

and define the exponent
\tilde{q}\coloneq\frac{pr(m-j)}{mr-jp}
such that
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{j}{mr}+\frac{m-j}{m\tilde{q}}
. Applying the induction hypothesis to
u^{\prime}
yields

\displaystyle\|u^{(j+1)}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C\|u^{(m+1)}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R})}^{\frac{j}{m}}\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{\tilde{q}}(\mathbb{R})}^{1-\frac{j}{m}}.

(A.36)

From the previous induction we also obtain

\displaystyle\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{\tilde{q}}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C\|u^{(j+1)}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}^{\frac{1}{j+1}}\|u\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})}^{1-\frac{1}{j+1}}

(A.37)

which holds due to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m-j}\left(\frac{m}{p}-\frac{j}{r}\right)\overset{\eqref{bedj+1GN}}{=}\frac{1}{m-j}\left(\frac{m}{p}-j\frac{q(m+1)-p(m-j)}{pq(j+1)}\right)=\frac{1}{(j+1)p}+\frac{j}{(j+1)q}.

(A.38)

Together, we conclude

\displaystyle\|u^{(j+1)}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C\|u^{(m+1)}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{R})}^{\frac{j+1}{m+1}}\|u\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})}^{\frac{m-j}{m+1}}.

(A.39)

This completes the induction, and we obtain the validity of (
A.13
) under condition (
A.12
) for arbitrary
j,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
j<m
and
\theta=\frac{j}{m}
.
∎

From Theorem
A.3
, we derive the corresponding statement on bounded intervals using an extension operator introduced by Rogers
[
Rog06
]
. Before that, we establish another auxiliary interpolation inequality, noting that an even stronger result is proved in
[
LZ22
]
.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open bounded interval,
1\leq q,r<\infty
and
j,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
where
j<m
. Then, there exists
C=C(q,r,m,I)>0
such that for all
u\in L^{q}(I)\cap W^{m,r}(I)
, we have

\displaystyle\|u^{(j)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}\leq\|u^{(m)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}+C\|u\|_{L^{q}(I)}.

(A.40)

Proof.

We argue by contradiction and suppose there exists a sequence
u_{n}\in W^{m,r}(I)
fulfilling

\displaystyle\|u_{n}^{(m)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}+n\|u_{n}\|_{L^{q}(I)}\leq\|u_{n}^{(j)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}.

(A.41)

We define the sequence
v_{n}\coloneq u_{n}\left(\|u_{n}\|_{W^{m-1,r}(I)}+\|u_{n}^{(j)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}\right)^{-1}\in W^{m,r}(I)
such that

\displaystyle\|v_{n}\|_{W^{m-1,r}(I)}+\|v_{n}^{(j)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}=1.

(A.42)

Since (
A.41
) also holds for the sequence
v_{n}
, we then obtain

\displaystyle\|v_{n}^{(m)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}+n\|v_{n}\|_{L^{q}(I)}\leq\|v_{n}^{(j)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}\overset{\eqref{constseq}}{\leq}1-\|v_{n}\|_{W^{m-1,r}(I)}\leq 1,

(A.43)

and thereby

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|v_{n}\|_{W^{m,r}(I)}&\overset{\eqref{bounded1}}{\leq}\|v_{n}\|_{W^{m-1,r}(I)}+1-n\|v_{n}\|_{L^{q}(I)}\\
&\overset{\eqref{constseq}}{\leq}2-\|v_{n}^{(j)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}-n\|v_{n}\|_{L^{q}(I)}\leq 2.\end{split}

(A.44)

Hence, there exist
\tilde{v}\in W^{m,r}(I)
and a subsequence of
v_{n}
(not relabeled), such that
v_{n}^{(i)}\rightharpoonup\tilde{v}^{(i)}
in
L^{r}(I)
as
n\to\infty
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
. Since
W^{1,r}(I)
is compactly embedded into
L^{r}(I)
, we obtain strong convergence of a further subsequence for the derivatives up to order
m-1
, that is

\displaystyle\|v_{n}-\tilde{v}\|_{W^{m-1,r}(I)}+\|v_{n}^{(j)}-\tilde{v}^{(j)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\|v_{n}^{(i)}-\tilde{v}^{(i)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}+\|v_{n}^{(j)}-\tilde{v}^{(j)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}\to 0

(A.45)

as
n\to\infty
. At the same time, we have

\displaystyle\|v_{n}-\tilde{v}\|_{W^{m-1,r}(I)}+\|v_{n}^{(j)}-\tilde{v}^{(j)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}\overset{\eqref{constseq}}{\geq}1-\|\tilde{v}\|_{W^{m-1,r}(I)}-\|\tilde{v}^{(j)}\|_{L^{r}(I)},

(A.46)

implying
\|\tilde{v}\|_{W^{m-1,r}(I)}+\|\tilde{v}^{(j)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}\geq 1
.
From (
A.43
), we further deduce

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|v_{n}\|_{L^{q}(I)}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left(1-\|v_{n}^{(m)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}\right)\overset{\eqref{bounded2}}{=}0.

(A.47)

Thus,
v_{n}
has a subsequence that converges to zero almost everywhere. On the other hand, we find a further subsequence that converges almost everywhere to
\tilde{v}
due to the strong convergence in
L^{r}(I)
. Consequently, we have
\tilde{v}=0
almost everywhere, which is a contradiction.
∎

From Theorem
A.3
and Lemma
A.4
we can now derive the Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality on bounded intervals using a suitable extension operator for Sobolev spaces from intervals to the real line (see
[
Rog06
]
). An analogous approach for general bounded Lipschitz domains in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is described in
[
LZ22
, Theorem 1.3]
.

Theorem A.5

(Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality on bounded intervals)
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open bounded interval,
1\leq p,q,r<\infty
,
and
j,m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
where
j<m
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}=j+\theta\left(\frac{1}{r}-m\right)+\frac{1-\theta}{q}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{j}{m}\leq\theta<1.

(A.48)

Then there exists
C=C(q,r,m,j,\theta,I)>0
such that for all
u\in L^{q}(I)\cap W^{m,r}(I)
, we have

\displaystyle\|u^{(j)}\|_{L^{p}(I)}\leq C\left(\|u^{(m)}\|^{\theta}_{L^{r}(I)}\|u\|^{1-\theta}_{L^{q}(I)}+\|u\|_{L^{q}(I)}\right).

(A.49)

Proof.

In
[
Rog06
, Theorem 8]
, it is shown that for any
1\leq s<\infty
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
there exists a bounded linear extension operator
T\colon W^{k,s}(I)\to W^{k,s}(\mathbb{R})
and a constant
c=c(s,k,I)>0
such that for all
u\in W^{k,s}(I)
we have

\displaystyle\|Tu\|_{W^{k,s}(\mathbb{R})}\leq c\|u\|_{W^{k,s}(I)}.

(A.50)

Thus, with Lemma
A.4
and Theorem
A.3
, for
u\in L^{q}(I)\cap W^{m,r}(I)
we obtain

\displaystyle\|u^{(j)}\|_{L^{p}(I)}

\displaystyle\overset{\hphantom{\eqref{extop}}}{\leq}\|(Tu)^{(j)}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\overset{\eqref{eq:GagNirR}}{\leq}C\|Tu\|^{\theta}_{W^{m,r}(\mathbb{R})}\|Tu\|^{1-\theta}_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})}

(A.51)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{extop}}{\leq}C\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\|u^{(i)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}+\|u^{(m)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}\Big)^{\theta}\|u\|^{1-\theta}_{L^{q}(I)}

(A.52)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:IntLemAbstr}}{\leq}C\Big(\|u\|_{L^{q}(I)}+\|u^{(m)}\|_{L^{r}(I)}\Big)^{\theta}\|u\|^{1-\theta}_{L^{q}(I)}

(A.53)

\displaystyle\overset{\hphantom{\eqref{extop}}}{\leq}C\Big(\|u^{(m)}\|^{\theta}_{L^{r}(I)}\|u\|^{1-\theta}_{L^{q}(I)}+\|u\|_{L^{q}(I)}\Big),

(A.54)

where the last step holds due to the subadditivity of
x\mapsto x^{\theta}
for
x\in[0,\infty)
and
\theta\in(0,1)
.
∎
