Therapeutic Effects of Forced Running on Osseointegration in Rats With Diabetes
Authors: Wang Yuxi, Chen Zijun, Yang Liu, Song Shuang, Tian Lei, Ding Mingchao, Liu Xiangdong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPoor osseointegration in diabetes is a significant challenge faced by dental implants; existing strategies are still limited in their effectiveness to fully promote osseointegration. Physical exercise is an important treatment for diabetes and has shown its effect in improving the skeletal condition of patients with diabetes. Yet it remains unclear whether physical exercise has an effect on long-term implant retention of diabetes.ObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the effect of exercise on implant osseointegration in diabetic rats and to provide proof-of-concept evidence that nonpharmacological intervention may improve long-term survival rates of dental implants in diabetic patients.Material and methodsType 2 diabetes was induced in a subset of rats via a high-sucrose and high-fat diet followed by a low-dose streptozotocin injection. Some of these rats were forced running for 40 minutes/d. Diabetes-related parameters were measured in groups of healthy rats (NC), diabetes rats (DM), and running diabetes rats (DM + EX). Titanium implants were implanted in proximal tibia of some rats, and the osseointegration was compared in the 3 groups.ResultsA running-diabetes-rat model was successfully established, where effect of running significantly effect of running significantly reduced the high blood levels of triglyceride and low-density lipoprotein, but did not improve hyperglycaemia or insulin resistance. In addition, osseointegration of the metal implants was substantially improved upon running.ConclusionA simple running device for rats can be used to study effect of physical exercise on diabetes-related deficits. Intensive physical exercise may substantially improve health condition of diabetes patients and reduce the failure rate of implant for diabetes.Key words: Exercise, Bone, Diabetes, Dental implant, Osseointegration

Introduction with objectives

Patients with diabetes exhibit a significantly increased risk of tooth loss due to long-term hyperglycaemia-induced metabolic disturbances, leading to a higher demand for dental restoration.1 However, diabetes adversely affects osseointegration of implants.2,3 Mechanistically, hyperglycaemia has been shown to inhibit osteoblast proliferation, reduce the bone-implant contact rate, and accelerate early marginal bone resorption.4 Clinical data indicate that diabetic patients present significantly greater probing depths and marginal bone loss compared to nondiabetic individuals, along with a higher rate of early implant failure. To address this issue, in addition to pharmacological interventions, nutritional control, weight management, and physical activity are recognized as highly feasible and effective nonpharmacological systemic measures.5, 6, 7

Given the multifaceted benefits of exercise, previous studies have demonstrated that aerobic or resistance exercise significantly reduces fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels and increases the density of glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) on skeletal muscle cell membranes, thereby improving insulin sensitivity.8 Furthermore, regular exercise downregulates systemic inflammatory markers (interleukin-6 [IL‑6], tumor necrosis factor-α [TNF‑α], CRP), mitigating the negative impact of chronic low-grade inflammation on bone metabolism.9 Specifically regarding skeletal health, a cross-sectional survey involving 92 hospitalized diabetic patients revealed that 87.5% of those with regular exercise habits did not develop osteoporosis.10 In an obesity-insulin resistance model, running combined with mild caloric restriction was shown to preserve trabecular bone volume and further enhance cortical bone density. This osteoprotective effect has been corroborated in preclinical settings, as animal studies have shown that various forms of exercise (swimming, running, HIIT) reduce bone loss in diabetic rodent models, suggesting that the positive regulatory effect of exercise on bone metabolism may also apply to peri-implant bone tissue.11

Collectively, existing evidence supports the benefits of exercise for systemic bone health. However, a critical knowledge gap remains: whether exercise directly improves implant osseointegration in diabetic patients has yet to be explored. To address this gap, this study employs a forced running apparatus to simulate human running exercise in rats. By inducing type 2 diabetes via streptozotocin injection combined with a high-sucrose and high-fat diet, we established a ‘running–diabetic–rat’ model to evaluate the restorative effect of exercise on diabetes-related bone impairments and to elucidate the protective role of systemic physical activity in implant osseointegration among diabetic individuals. Specifically, this study aims to quantify the restorative effect of exercise on diabetes-induced impairments in peri-implant bone microstructure and biomechanical stability, and provide proof-of-concept evidence that nonpharmacological systemic intervention may improve dental implant outcomes in diabetic patients.

Materials and methods

Animal groups

The study protocol was proven by the animal ethics review (2021-k9-010). The Guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of China were followed. Animal studies are reported in rats were compliance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. A priori power analysis (GPower; f = 0.50, α = 0.05, power = 0.80) determined a minimum of 10 rats per group; we therefore enrolled 12 per group to allow for attrition. Thirty-six 8-week-old male Sprague–Dawley rats were randomly divided into three groups (n = 12/group): (1) Normal control (NC): Nondiabetic, sedentary; (2) diabetes mellitus (DM): Diabetic, sedentary; (3) diabetes mellitus+ exercise (DM + EX): Diabetic, with running. A computer-generated random number sequence was used for group allocation. Cage locations were randomized weekly to minimize environmental confounders. Only rats with successful diabetes induction (blood glucose ≥16.7 mmol/L) and without infection were included in the final analysis. Three rats were excluded from the final analysis – one for failing to meet the diabetes criterion (fasting glucose <16.7 mmol/L), one for a purulent wound infection, and one for severe cachexia (>20% weekly weight loss) – with no further exclusions. Final sample sizes were NC = 12, DM = 10, and DM + EX = 11 after exclusions. Outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group allocation.

Running-diabetes-rat

Diabetes induction: To induce diabetes, 4-week-old male Sprague–Dawley rats were fed with a high-sucrose and high-fat diet (Chengdu Dossy Experimental Animals Co., Ltd) for 4 weeks, and subsequently injected with 20 mg/kg streptozotocin (Sigma) freshly dissolved in 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH 4.5, Figure 1A). The nondiabetic control group (NC) received an equivalent volume of citrate buffer alone. After 3 days, FBG was measured from the tail vein using a glucometer; rats with FBG > 16.7 mmol/L were defined as diabetic rats. All diabetic animals were maintained on the HSHFD throughout the remainder of the experiment to sustain the diabetic phenotype.

Establishment of the animal model. (A) Procedure of animals modelling; (B) rats in the running treatment; (C) femoral implant surgery. The preparation and disinfection of the surgical area (1), the skin and fascia separation layer by layer (2), exposed of the femoral head (3), location of the implant area using a ball drill (4), expanded the implant hole step by step (5), the implant socket was prepared and inserted the implant (6-8), reset suture (9, 10).

Surgery procedure: Titanium implants with a length of 5 mm and a diameter of 2 mm were surgically inserted into the proximal tibia of all rats. Rats were injected intraperitoneally with 10% chloral hydrate (0.05 ml/kg rat weight). Skin and fascia were separated layer by layer, exposing the tibia head where an implant was inserted 2.0 mm proximal to the growth plate (Figure 1C). Postoperatively, antibiotics were given for 5 days to prevent infection. One week after the implantation, alizarin red S (30 mg/kg) (Sigma-Aldrich) were administered intraperitoneally. Animals with infection at the implantation sites were sacrificed before end of the experiment.

Exercise intervention: The exercise protocol is illustrated in the experimental timeline (Figure 1A). All DM groups rats underwent a 2-week acclimatization period on a motorized treadmill (model EXER-6M; Columbus Instruments) to habituate them to the running environment. NC and DM groups were placed on a stationary treadmill to control for handling and environmental stress. During DM groups rats acclimatizating, the speed was gradually increased from 5 m/min (10 minutes/d) to 10 m/min (40 minutes/d, Figure 1B). Rest for 2 weeks after the surgery for the healing of the implant wound. Postimplantation exercise phase: After a 2-week postsurgical recovery period (during which all animals were sedentary to allow wound healing), the DM + EX group resumed the same running regimen (10 m/min, 40 minutes/d, 5 days/wk) for an additional 8 weeks. Thus, the total exercise intervention comprised 10 weeks of active running (2 weeks preimplantation + 8 weeks postimplantation), with a mandatory 2-week rest period immediately after surgery.

The forced treadmill exercise protocol was designed and conducted in strict accordance with animal welfare considerations. Throughout the intervention, running sessions were continuously monitored by trained personnel; any animal showing signs of severe exhaustion (inability to maintain pace, laboured breathing, or prostration) was immediately removed and allowed to rest, ensuring that no undue suffering occurred. During formal training, aversive stimuli (eg, mild air puffs or gentle mechanical prodding) were only applied as a secondary motivator, with the primary goal being to encourage running without direct physical contact.

Outcome measures

Metabolic monitoring: Body weight and blood glucose levels of all rats were monitored weekly throughout the experimental period. To evaluate systemic glucose metabolism, an intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) and an insulin tolerance test (ITT) were carried out sequentially, with a 1-week interval between the two assays to minimize cross-interference. Their blood was taken from the tail vein within 60 minutes after running. Then, all rats were sacrificed at the end of the experiment. Prior to euthanasia, blood samples were collected from all animals. Serum was separated for the subsequent measurement of triglyceride (TG) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels.

Microcomputed tomography analysis: The tibia bones were cut to appropriate size and fixed in 70% ethanol overnight. The trabecular region 200 μm around the implant was scanned using a microcomputed tomography scan (Siemens Inveon) in a high-resolution mode. The scanned images were reconstructed into a three-dimensional structure, and relevant parameters were measured automatically, including bone volume percentage (BV/TV%), trabecular thickness, and trabecular number.

Scanning electron microscopy, histology and immunohistochemistry: The tibia bones with the implants were fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde, 4% paraformaldehyde or frozen for scanning electron microscope, histology and immunological analysis. Staining with haematoxylin and eosin, Masson, Safranin and fast green.

Counter torque analysis: The mechanical integrity of the bone-implant interface was evaluated through reverse torque testing using a calibrated torque meter (SANLIANG, SNS; range: 0.01 to 0.5 N·m, resolution: 0.0001 N·m). A torque wrench was engaged with the implant’s internal connection and rotated counterclockwise at a constant rate until the onset of implant rotation, indicating failure of the bone-implant interface. The maximum torque value recorded immediately prior to this failure point was designated as the reverse torque value, representing the minimal torque required to disrupt the established osseointegration.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Following micro-CT scanning, peri-implant bone tissues of the proximal tibia samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified in 10% EDTA, and embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections (6 μm) were subjected to antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) at 125°C for 1 minute, followed by boiling at 100°C for 2.5 minutes. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 15 minutes. Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against alkaline phosphatase (ALP), BMP2, RUNX2, TNF-α, and IL-6, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 20 minutes and DAB substrate for 10 minutes. Counterstaining was performed with haematoxylin. For immunofluorescence, sections were incubated with Cy3-conjugated secondary antibodies (ab6939, Abcam) for 2 hours at room temperature after primary antibody incubation. Images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica). Positively stained areas were quantified using ImageJ software by investigators blinded to group allocation.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and gene set enrichment analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the peri-implant bone tissues of the proximal tibia of DM group and DM + EX groups. Then, high-quality RNA samples were converted into cDNA libraries using the VAHTSTM mRNA-seq V2 library Preparation kit from Illumina (NR601, Vazyme). Fragments were purified with VAHTSTM DNA Clean beads (Vazyme#N411) during library construction. The purified product was concentrated by 12 to 15 cycles of polymerase chain reaction to obtain the final library. Finally, libraries on Illumina HiSeq X Ten were sequenced according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Readings were compared with the TOPHAT program (version 2.0.11). In addition, fpkm values of genes were calculated, Pearson correlation analysis was performed. In our study, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as fold change >2 and P < .05. The biological significance of DEGs was further explained by Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed using SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0. Normality was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances by Levene’s test. For comparisons among three groups with unequal sample sizes, Welch’s ANOVA with Games–Howell posthoc tests was applied. For repeated measures data (body weight, blood glucose, IPGTT, and ITT), repeated measures ANOVA was performed with Group as the between-subjects factor and Time as the within-subjects factor, followed by Bonferroni posthoc tests. Partial η² was calculated as a measure of effect size. Data are presented as mean ± SD. A two-tailed P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Exact P values are reported throughout.

Results

Running selectively ameliorates major diabetes-related parameters

To investigate the therapeutic potential of exercise, diabetic rats were subjected to a forced running protocol (40 minutes/d, 12 weeks; Figure 1B). Diabetic rats (DM) exhibited characteristic phenotypes, including lethargy, poor grooming, and significant weight loss, which were visibly less severe in the exercise-intervened (DM + EX) group (Figure 2A).

(F and G) AUC of IPGTT and ITT in serum of different groups (n = 10). (H and I) Triglycerides (TG, F), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL, G) levels in serum of different groups (n = 10).

All diabetic rats experienced progressive body weight loss. However, this loss was significantly more profound in the sedentary DM group (P < .05 vs NC), whereas the DM + EX group maintained a body weight comparable to the nondiabetic NC group (Figure 2B). FBG levels remained markedly and similarly elevated in both the DM and DM + EX groups, indicating that the running regimen did not reverse hyperglycaemia (Figure 2C).

IPGTT and ITT revealed severe systemic insulin resistance in the DM group and DM + EX group. Following insulin injection, the blood glucose levels in the DM group and DM + EX group decreased at a significantly slower rate compared to the NC group (Figure 2C,D). The AUC analysis confirmed this observation (Figure 2E,F), it confirmed that the DM group and DM + EX group exhibited glucose intolerance (IPGTT-AUC: 34,249 ± 869.7 in DM and 30,387 ± 987.0 in DM + EX vs 28,127 ± 789.0 in NC, P < .05) and insulin resistance (ITT-AUC: 2012 ± 76 in DM and 42 1712 ± 62.92 in DM + EX vs 657.4 ± 20.82 in NC, P < .05). Despite the exercise intervention, the ITT indicated that systemic insulin resistance persisted in the DM + EX group. The rate of blood glucose clearance following insulin injection was not significantly improved in the DM + EX group compared to the sedentary DM group (P > .05). The blood glucose curves for both diabetic groups (DM and DM + EX) were largely superimposable and showed a markedly attenuated decline relative to the NC group (Figure 2G). ITT indicating that the running regimen failed to improve systemic insulin resistance in diabetic rats.

In marked contrast to the persistent hyperglycaemia, the drastically elevated serum levels of TG and LDL in DM rats were substantially corrected by exercise. The DM + EX group exhibited TG and LDL levels comparable to those of the NC group (P < .05 vs DM; P = .682 and P = .574 vs NC, respectively; Figure 2E,F), indicating that the running regimen selectively restored lipid homeostasis despite failing to improve glycaemic control.

Running improve osseointegration of implants in diabetic rats

We next evaluated the impact of exercise on implant osseointegration. Micro-CT 3D reconstructions visually demonstrated a substantial increase in new bone formation (yellow) around the implants in the NC and DM + EX groups compared to the DM group (Figure 3A). Quantitative micro-CT analysis confirmed this observation, revealing that diabetes induction led to severe bone microarchitectural impairments in the DM group, with the bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular number (Tb.N) measured at (6.71 ± 0.65)%, (3.21 ± 0.71)μm, and (1.87 ± 0.17)/mm, respectively. Forced running largely rescued these deficits, significantly elevating the values in the DM + EX group to (32.82 ± 0.56)%, (8.62 ± 0.60)μm, and (3.44 ± 0.12)/mm (P < .001 vs DM group; Figure 3B), which were comparable to the levels observed in the NC group ([31.93 ± 1.35]%, [7.90 ± 0.88]μm, [3.35 ± 0.26]/mm).

This pro-osteogenic effect of exercise was further validated by histomorphometric analysis. Both VG-stained and toluidine blue-stained sections showed enhanced new bone formation in the DM + EX group (Figure 3C,D), with the bone-to-implant contact significantly increased to (38.93 ± 4.21)% in the DM + EX group from (19.56 ± 7.34)% in the DM group (P < .05), approaching the (35.4 ± 7.62)% observed in the NC group.

Histological analysis provided further insight into the microarchitectural improvements. Haematoxylin and eosin and Masson staining showed that the DM group exhibited rod-like, degenerated trabeculae, whereas the DM + EX and NC groups displayed healthier, plate-like cancellous bone (Figure 4A,B). Furthermore, running enhanced the formation of fibrous connective tissue around the implant. Safranin O & Fast Green staining revealed more abundant chondroblasts (red) and denser newly formed cancellous bone (blue) in the DM + EX group, suggesting an augmented bone healing process (Figure 4C).

Exploration of the mechanisms underlying exercise-enhanced osseointegration in diabetic rats

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which exercise promotes bone formation and osseointegration under diabetic conditions, we performed high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on peri-implant bone tissue samples obtained from the DM + EX and DM groups.

Differential expression analysis identified a total of 2842 DEGs, of which 1396 were upregulated and 1446 were downregulated in the exercise group compared to sedentary diabetic controls (Figure 5 A).

(D) Immunohistochemical images of immune-related, osteoblast-related and osteoclast-related genes in different groups, scale bar = 250 μm; (E-G) statistical results of the immunohistochemical deposition for osteoblast-related, immune-related and osteoclast-related genes in image (D), *P < .05; **P < .01.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis demonstrated that the DEGs were significantly enriched in biological processes related to bone development, extracellular matrix organization, skeletal system morphogenesis, porphyrin-containing compound metabolic process, and wound healing. Exercise also modulated genes associated with altered cellular components and molecular functions, including collagen trimer, extracellular matrix, rough endoplasmic reticulum membrane, structural constituent of ribosome, tubulin binding, and transforming growth factor beta binding (Figure 5B).

To gain further mechanistic insight, we performed gene set enrichment analysis. This revealed that gene sets associated with immune receptor activity and adaptive immune response were significantly downregulated in the exercise group, suggesting that exercise attenuates the chronic inflammatory state characteristic of diabetes. Notably, key components of the JAK/STAT signalling pathway – including JAK3, STAT6, and STAT3 – were downregulated, along with its upstream receptor IL6R (Figure 5C). Given that IL-6 is a classical inflammatory stimulus in this pathway, these results imply that exercise may reduce cellular responsiveness to the proinflammatory diabetic microenvironment by suppressing IL6R expression, thereby mitigating the deleterious effects of chronic inflammation on bone cells.

Concurrently, we observed upregulation of several osteogenesis-related genes. Within the skeletal system morphogenesis category, RUNX2 and MMP2 were elevated, as were extracellular matrix genes such as COL1A1. During connective tissue formation, PARP1 and RUNX2 were also upregulated (Figure 5D. Given that COL1A1 and RUNX2 are established positive regulators of bone formation, these transcriptional changes suggest that exercise promotes osteogenic differentiation. Notably, MMP2 and PARP1, which have also been implicated in bone resorption, were similarly upregulated, indicating a concurrent activation of both bone formation and remodelling processes, potentially reflecting an overall increase in bone turnover.

To validate these transcriptional findings at the protein level, we performed IHC staining for ALP, COL1, RUNX2, IL-6, and TNF-α (Figure 5F). Positive immunoreactivity for all markers was observed in both groups; however, the DM + EX group exhibited substantially more extensive positive staining regions for ALP, COL1, and RUNX2, accompanied by markedly reduced IL-6 expression around the implant (Figure 5E-G). Quantitative analysis confirmed that RUNX2 expression was significantly elevated in the DM + EX group compared to sedentary diabetic controls, while IL-6 levels were significantly reduced (P < .05). TNF-α immunoreactivity showed a trend towards reduction in the DM + EX group, consistent with the anti-inflammatory effect observed for IL-6.

Collectively, these findings suggest that exercise enhances bone formation in the diabetic milieu through a dual mechanism: (1) suppression of local inflammation, particularly via downregulation of IL-6 signalling and immune receptor activity; and (2) upregulation of key osteogenic transcription factors and extracellular matrix components. This anti-inflammatory and pro-osteogenic transcriptional program may underlie the improved mineralization potential and osseointegration observed in exercised diabetic rats.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that forced running exercise could effectively counteract the adverse effects of type 2 diabetes on implant osseointegration in a rat model. A striking and arguably the most instructive finding of this study is the dissociation between the effects of exercise on glucose vs lipid metabolism. While the running regimen robustly corrected dyslipidaemia – normalizing TG and LDL to levels comparable to healthy controls – it failed to reduce fasting hyperglycaemia or improve systemic insulin resistance. Running also achieved levels of bone integration comparable to healthy controls. The substantial restoration of diabetes-induced bone deterioration – including restored trabecular structure and reduced fibrous tissue formation – strongly suggests that exercise could functionally restore the bone healing capacity compromised by diabetes.

The systemic metabolic improvements observed provide a plausible explanation for these structural recoveries. The normalization of TG and LDL cholesterol creates a more favourable environment for bone regeneration, potentially mitigating the chronic inflammation that typically impedes healing in diabetic conditions. This was in line with existing literature that Lin et al demonstrated that treadmill exercise activated the irisin/AMPK pathway, which significantly improved insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia, and bone metabolism in type 2 diabetic rats.12 It is particularly noteworthy that this biomechanical improvement occurred despite the persistence of hyperglycaemia, as evidenced by our glucose tolerance data. This dissociation indicates that the benefits of exercise on osseointegration are, at least in part, mediated through glucose-independent pathways. This finding is of considerable clinical relevance, as it suggests that physical activity could improve dental and orthopaedic implant outcomes in diabetic patients even before optimal glycaemic control is fully achieved.

Torque played a significant role in the retention of dental implants.13 The counter torque analysis results provide direct biomechanical evidence that exercise significantly restores the impaired osseointegration caused by diabetes. The markedly lower push-out force in the DM group underscores the well-documented detrimental impact of the diabetic milieu on bone healing, characterized by hyperglycaemia-induced suppression of osteoblast function, chronic inflammation, and accumulation of advanced glycation end products that compromise bone quality.14 These may include the downregulation of proinflammatory cytokines, improved local blood flow, and the stimulation of osteogenic signalling pathways, thereby enhancing bone formation and strengthening the bone-implant interface.

However, the quality of osseointegration observed histologically also suggests that local mechanical stimulation through running directly enhanced bone formation at the implant site. Research involving diabetic rabbits has demonstrated that daily low-magnitude vibration (below 1 g, 30 min/d) could improve bone microstructure and enhance implant osseointegration. This indicates that even mild mechanical stimulation is sufficient to locally promote bone formation. Notably, the strain induced by such vibration is substantially lower than that generated during running, suggesting a potentially gentler therapeutic approach.15 The combination of systemic metabolic correction and local mechanical adaptation likely explains the exceptional recovery seen in exercised diabetic animals. Huang et al reported that 20 weeks of voluntary wheel running improved BMD and enhanced Wnt signalling in type 2 diabetic rats, supporting that exercise promotes bone formation, but their voluntary exercise model lacks quantifiable intensity. In contrast, our forced running protocol ensured a consistent and standardized exercise load across all animals.16

Among the cytokines involved in glucose homeostasis and bone metabolism, IL-6 has been most extensively linked to exercise-induced immune regulation. Elevated IL-6 in diabetic patients inversely correlates with bone quality, and its canonical JAK/STAT signalling pathway is implicated in multiple inflammatory bone diseases. Consistent with this, our RNA-seq data revealed exercise-induced downregulation of IL6R, JAK3, STAT3, and STAT6 in diabetic peri-implant bone, which was corroborated by reduced IL-6 protein expression on IHC. This suggests that exercise attenuates IL-6-mediated inflammatory signalling, alleviating the chronic inflammatory burden on bone. Concurrently, we observed upregulation of osteogenic genes including RUNX2 and COL1A1. Previous studies support the functional relevance of JAK/STAT modulation in bone healing: Yuan et al showed that JAK/STAT activation promotes diabetic wound healing, while Liu et al reported that running accelerates tendon-bone healing via JAK/STAT-dependent macrophage polarization. Collectively, these findings suggest a dual mechanism – suppression of IL-6/JAK/STAT inflammation and activation of osteogenic programs – through which exercise improves osseointegration in the diabetic microenvironment.

From a clinical perspective, these findings are particularly relevant for oral and maxillofacial surgery. The potential to improve implant success rates in diabetic patients through nonpharmacological means represents a significant advance.

Consistent with our preclinical data, exercise exerts favourable impacts on implant osseointegration under diabetic conditions, yet several factors warrant cautious clinical extrapolation. Anatomically, rat tibial bone differs from human maxillofacial bone in microstructure, mechanical loading patterns and healing characteristics. Besides, the standardized forced treadmill running protocol used herein involves relatively intense daily activity that may not be easily replicated in diabetic patients’ daily routines, which calls for further optimization of exercise schemes suitable for human application.

Our data also reveal that exercise facilitates peri-implant bone repair without reversing hyperglycaemia, indicating its bone-protective functions are partially decoupled from glycaemic regulation. This work does not, however, include direct comparisons between physical activity and routine hypoglycaemic medications such as metformin or insulin, nor have we explored potential synergistic effects when exercise is combined with antidiabetic drugs. Previous studies have investigated the use of biomaterials or peptide-coated titanium plates to enhance wound healing, but we did not consider this direction in our current work.17,18 Even so, exercise still represents a promising nonpharmacological auxiliary strategy for diabetic implant patients, and further translational investigations based on large-animal jaw models and exercise dose-gradient trials will help advance its clinical application.

In addition, although our RNA-seq and IHC analyses clearly identified local anti-inflammatory and pro-osteogenic alterations around implants in exercised rats, these tissue markers cannot precisely quantify the stress response triggered by forced running. Collectively, the prominent bone-improving phenotypes observed in the DM + EX group imply that exercise’s therapeutic advantages overshadow any minor unfavourable metabolic shifts induced by treadmill-related stress, though dedicated testing of stress biomarkers would be required to fully dissect the interplay between physical activity and psychological stress in bone remodelling.

Notably, the translational significance of our rodent findings is further reinforced by our previously published clinical retrospective study focusing on type 2 diabetic implant patients.19 In that clinical investigation, we observed that diabetic individuals sustaining regular physical activity before and after implant surgery exhibited markedly reduced marginal bone loss around implants, even among patients receiving metformin therapy. This clinical evidence aligns well with the preclinical outcomes reported herein, jointly indicating that physical exercise exerts protective effects against diabetes-induced peri-implant bone resorption in both human patients and diabetic rats. Nevertheless, discrepancies between rat tibial implant models and human maxillofacial bone anatomy, loading patterns, and exercise compliance still warrant cautious interpretation of direct clinical extrapolation. Future studies should establish dose-response relationships by testing shorter durations and lower intensities to determine the minimal effective exercise regimen. Identifying such thresholds will be crucial for designing practical, sustainable exercise programs that diabetic patients could adhere to long-term.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that forced running selectively corrects diabetes-associated dyslipidaemia, but does not improve hyperglycaemia or insulin resistance. Nevertheless, this partial metabolic improvement is sufficient to rescue peri-implant osseointegration in diabetic rats.
