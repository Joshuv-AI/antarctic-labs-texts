Thoracic spinal cord myelopathy from complex arachnoid web formation/exacerbation after palliative radiation therapy for spinal metastasis: illustrative case

Radiation therapy is a common treatment for spinal metastases. Radiation-induced myelopathy is a rare, but potentially devastating complication of spinal cord irradiation resulting from damage to the white matter of the spinal cord due to vascular injury, demyelination, and/or alternations to the spinal cord microenvironment.1–6 The incidence of radiation-induced myelopathy is 0.2% when the radiation dose to the spinal cord remains under the recommended tolerance of 50 Gy in increments of 1.8–2 Gy per fraction.7 The risk of radiation myelopathy at palliative doses of radiation therapy of 8 to 30 Gy is exceedingly low. Radiation myelopathy typically presents in two forms: early delayed and late delayed. The early-delayed form occurs within 6 months after radiation therapy and is often transient and self-limited. The late-delayed form presents months to years after treatment and can lead to progressive and irreversible neurological deficits.4,5,8 MRI findings in radiation myelopathy include T2 hyperintensity, cord expansion, and variable enhancement patterns.2 While the effects of radiation on the spinal cord itself are well documented, the understanding of radiation’s effects to the surrounding structures, such as the arachnoid membrane, is limited.

Arachnoid webs and cysts are rare structural abnormalities that can cause significant neurological symptoms secondary to spinal cord compression. There are several proposed mechanisms for the formation of arachnoid webs, including traumatic, infectious, postoperative, and congenital etiologies.9 In contrast, arachnoid cysts are thought to form from irregular CSF flow during embryonic development, where abnormal CSF pulse pressure may lead to trapped fluid when meninges close improperly.10 To date, there are no published articles directly linking prior spinal radiation therapy to the formation or alteration of arachnoid webs or cysts.

Theoretically, radiation-induced inflammation and fibrosis of the arachnoid membrane could lead to adhesions and trabeculations that alter CSF dynamics, potentially contributing to the formation of arachnoid webs. The vascular changes and altered permeability of the blood–spinal cord barrier associated with radiation exposure could also play a role in modifying the arachnoid environment.2,4–6

In this paper, we present a unique case of a patient who developed a compressive complex multiloculated arachnoid web (a further description is in the Illustrative Case section below) following palliative radiation therapy for spinal metastases. This case highlights the importance of exploring alternative etiologies for myelopathy symptoms in an irradiated distribution, particularly when radiation dosage to the spinal cord is within accepted tolerance.

Illustrative Case

A 64-year-old female was initially diagnosed with right breast invasive ductal carcinoma in January 2022 and subsequently developed spinal osseous metastases in the T6 and T7 vertebrae (Figs. 1A and 2A). Systemic treatment for this patient’s breast cancer included TCHP (docetaxel, carboplatin, trastuzumab, and pertuzumab) initiated in June 2022, followed by maintenance therapy with letrozole, trastuzumab/pertuzumab, and denosumab. She underwent palliative external beam radiation therapy for bony metastases from T5 to T8, which consisted of 3000 cGy given over 10 fractions in January 2023. Approximately 6 months following her first radiation treatment (July 2023), the patient developed progressive myelopathy. Specifically, her symptoms included sensory changes below the T7 dermatome affecting bilateral lower extremities with burning/stinging paresthesias, saddle anesthesia, proprioception deficits, and painful spasms, all of which were refractory to conservative management. Thoracic spine MRI performed in October 2023 revealed no change in known osseous metastatic lesions, but there was new dorsal cord enhancement at the T6 level and significant cord edema spanning from T3 to T7 (Figs. 1B and 2B). The differential diagnosis at that time included delayed radiation myelopathy versus new intramedullary metastatic disease.

An interdisciplinary neurological tumor board review concluded that the likelihood of intramedullary metastatic disease was extremely low. The spinal cord imaging findings on MRI were at the exact levels of the radiation field. However, radiation myelopathy was felt to be also highly unlikely in this patient, given the relatively short length of cord irradiation, no use of concurrent chemotherapy, and that the prescribed radiation dose of 3000 cGy in 10 fractions was well below the recommended tolerance of 4500–5000 cGy. Given that another etiology could not be identified, the patient was treated with a long course of high-dose steroids for presumed radiation-induced myelopathy. Her symptoms failed to improve with steroids; therefore, bevacizumab was then initiated as second-line therapy for radiation-induced myelopathy, which also did not improve symptoms.

A follow-up thoracic spine MRI study on December 11, 2023, revealed an interval increase in cord enhancement but a decrease in cord edema. The improvement of edema was attributed to her recent steroid course. This MRI study also raised suspicion for an intrathecal structural abnormality at the T7 level, specifically, a focal indentation of the dorsal cord with possible ventral CSF effacement (Figs. 1C and 2C). Retrospective imaging review showed possible similar dorsal cord contour abnormality in March 2022 (prior to irradiation), although it was less pronounced at that time (Fig. 1A). The constellation of MRI findings was concerning for ventral cord herniation related to the presence of previously abutting vertebral body metastasis. A compressive dorsal arachnoid web or cyst was also a consideration, although considered less likely due to the absence of preradiation cord signal abnormality.

A CT myelography study was performed on December 15, 2023, to evaluate for ventral cord herniation versus dorsal compression. The CT myelogram examination confirmed dorsal cord indentation at the inferior T7 level and ventral cord deviation, without ventral CSF effacement to suggest cord herniation or adhesion to the ventral thecal sac (Fig. 3). These findings were most consistent with compressive dorsal arachnoid web and associated venous congestion accounting for rostral cord edema and enhancement. Given the progressive neurological decline, concerning imaging, and lack of improvement with multiple medical therapies, the patient underwent a T6–8 laminectomy and intrathecal exploration on August 15, 2024. Intraoperatively, a multiloculated intradural pouch of CSF compressing the cord was immediately observed spanning from T5 through T8 and extending laterally around the cord. Specifically, the abnormal structure appeared to be thickened arachnoid mater with interspersed arachnoid bands that terminated as a blind-ending pouch at the T5 level (Figs. 3C and 4). The arachnoid bands/webs were fenestrated until no inhibition to CSF flow was noted. Complete fenestration was confirmed with ultrasound. Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring showed immediate improvement in somatosensory evoked potentials. Postoperatively, the patient reported immediate improvement in lower extremity motor function and sensory symptoms. She demonstrated consistent improvement in ambulation, urinary incontinence, and lower extremity paresthesias, which continued to improve over the following several months. At her 8-month postoperative visit, the patient reported continued improvement in motor strength and balance with mild persistent weakness and paresthesias in bilateral lower extremities. The most recent thoracic MRI study, performed in March 2025, revealed decreased cord enhancement and resolution of expansion with minimal residual T2 hyperintensity and new volume loss, suggestive of persistent myelomalacia and injury (Figs. 1D and 2D).

Discussion

Observations

While the clinical presentation and imaging findings in our case were initially suggestive of radiation-induced myelopathy, an important consideration was that the patient received a palliative radiation dose of 3000 cGy in 10 fractions, which is significantly lower than the expected threshold for myelopathy. The incidence of radiation-induced myelopathy is 0.2% when the radiation dose to the spinal cord remains under the recommended tolerance of 50 Gy in increments of 1.8–2 Gy per fraction. The risk of myelopathy increases exponentially at doses above this tolerance, with an estimated myelopathy risk of approximately 5% at 5400–6100 cGy and 50% at 6800–7300 cGy.7,11

In the absence of significant improvement with steroids, bevacizumab was attempted. The patient tolerated one cycle of bevacizumab and requested to discontinue due to reported worsening symptoms. While fatigue is a known adverse effect of bevacizumab, there have been no reports of worsening myelopathy as a side effect in the literature.12 It is unclear whether the subjective worsening of myelopathy was a result of extreme fatigue from the bevacizumab treatment or if the myelopathy was aggravated by another factor at that time. The ineffective medical management followed by immediate clinical improvements after surgical intervention confirmed the structural etiology of the patient’s symptoms and deficits. The continued neurological improvement over 8 months postsurgery as well as her most recent MRI findings further support this conclusion.

To our knowledge, the development or exacerbation of arachnoid webs following radiation therapy, as seen in our case, has not been previously reported in the literature. While the exact mechanism remains unclear, we hypothesize that radiation-induced changes to the spinal cord and surrounding tissues likely contributed to the formation or alteration of the complex multiloculated arachnoid web, given that radiation therapy is known to cause fibrosis, vascular damage, and alter the permeability of the blood–spinal cord barrier.2,4–6

Lessons

This case highlights the complexities involved in the diagnosis and management of myelopathy in patients with a history of spinal radiation therapy. It underscores the importance of considering radiation-induced myelopathy, intramedullary metastasis, and intrathecal structural lesions, such as arachnoid webs, as various potential etiologies of progressive myelopathy.

While limited in scope to only one example of an arachnoid anomaly exacerbated by radiation therapy, this case raises important questions about the long-term effects of radiation on spinal canal structures beyond the cord itself. It suggests that clinicians should maintain an index of suspicion for structural abnormalities in patients with a history of spinal radiation therapy who present with progressive myelopathy, even when initial imaging does clearly indicate an underlying etiology and radiation doses are within conventionally accepted safe limits. When there is reasonable cause for suspicion, workup should include myelography for evaluation of intrathecal changes that could result in impediments in CSF flow, which are potentially undetectable on MRI. Further research is needed to elucidate the potential relationship between radiation therapy and the development or exacerbation of abnormalities in the arachnoid environment. Prospective studies examining the long-term effects of radiation on intrathecal structures could provide valuable insights into this possible association and inform future treatment strategies.
