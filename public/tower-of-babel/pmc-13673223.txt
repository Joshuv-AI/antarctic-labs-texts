Genome‐Wide Association Studies of Hospitalized Influenza Identify Four Risk Loci and Reveal Shared and Unique Genetic Factors Compared With COVID‐19
Authors: Xiao Xue, Yaning Feng, Yu‐Ping Lin, Pui‐Ching Chan, Hon‐Cheong So
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundInfluenza remains a significant global health burden, potentially leading to hospitalization and mortality. Although previous studies have explored genetic susceptibility to influenza infection, the genetic basis of influenza hospitalization and its relationship with respiratory diseases like COVID‐19 remain poorly understood.MethodsWe performed a binary GWAS of hospitalized influenza using UK Biobank data (UKBB; 1037 cases/404,108 controls) and then meta‐analyzed results with the FinnGen GWAS, yielding 11,171 cases and 782,400 controls. A time‐to‐event GWAS in UKBB was also performed to identify genetic factors underlying the timing (age) of first influenza hospitalization. p‐values from both analyses were integrated by the harmonic mean p‐value (HMP) method to enhance association detection. Further differentiation and colocalization analyses with COVID‐19 investigated their distinct and shared genetic features. An integrative framework combining functional annotations, multi‐omics analyses, and phenome‐wide scans prioritized key risk and differential genes.ResultsThe binary GWAS meta‐analysis identified three loci (ST6GAL1, AASDHPPT, SPATS2L) associated with hospitalization risk. The time‐to‐event GWAS recognized UGT2B4 as linked to hospitalization timing. HMP analysis, which combines evidence across the binary and time‐to‐event analyses, identified ST6GAL1 (p = 3 × 10−9) and UGT2B4 (p = 3.63 × 10−8) as genome‐wide significant; however, each signal was driven predominantly by one component test, ST6GAL1 by the binary meta‐analysis (meta p = 1.5 × 10−9), and UGT2B4 by the time‐to‐event analysis (SPACox p = 1.82 × 10−8), rather than showing independent support from both analyses. Differentiation analysis identified 29 loci and two tissues distinguishing influenza from COVID‐19, whereas analysis of shared association signals (cofdr) highlighted candidate shared genetic factors, including ST6GAL1 and ICAM5 (gene‐level PPA ≥ 0.9).ConclusionsThis study uncovers the genetic architectures of hospitalized influenza. It also highlights genetic differences and similarities with COVID‐19, providing hypothesis‐generating insights that may inform future functional and therapeutic research.

Introduction

Influenza is an acute respiratory infection that affects millions of people worldwide, causing 3 to 5 million severe cases and 290,000 to 650,000 deaths annually [1]. While often self‐limiting with recovery in 2 to 7 days for healthy individuals, influenza can lead to a range of medium‐ and long‐term complications affecting multiple organ systems, including the respiratory, cardiovascular, digestive, and neurological systems [2]. Certain populations, such as young children, pregnant women, older adults, and people with chronic conditions, are at a higher risk of hospitalization and mortality [3, 4].

Recent studies have increasingly recognized the role of genetic factors in influenza severity and susceptibility. For instance, Everitt et al. sequenced the IFITM3 gene in 53 patients hospitalized with seasonal or pandemic influenza and found an overrepresentation of rs12252‐C compared with matched Europeans from the 1000 Genomes [5]. A key limitation of this study is that it employed a candidate gene approach rather than a genome‐wide scan, potentially missing genetic associations at other locations. In another study in the Chinese population, Zhou et al. performed a small‐scale GWAS in 25 patients with severe H1N1 influenza and 26 controls with mild disease. Further genotyping was performed in 374 participants. The study revealed an association between severe influenza and the rs2564978‐T variant in the CD55 gene [6] (p = 0.0024). This study was however limited by its small sample size (N = 51 for GWAS analysis) with no loci reaching genome‐wide significance, and it was limited to H1N1 influenza in a single year (2009). More recently, Kosmicki et al. performed a cross‐ancestry meta‐analysis of GWAS on self‐reported influenza, comprising 18,334 cases and 276,295 controls [7]. This analysis identified two risk loci, including rs16861415 in ST6GAL1 (p = 1.4 × 10−10) and rs2837112 in B3GALT5 (p = 1.3 × 10−19). This study primarily focused on susceptibility to influenza infection rather than severity; however, genetic factors of severe outcomes may differ from those influencing susceptibility. In fact, very few GWAS (apart from Zhou et al.) have explicitly targeted hospitalized influenza cases, leaving a critical gap in our knowledge of the genetic determinants behind severe disease outcomes. In addition, previous genetic studies have not studied the age of first hospitalization. Notably, hospitalization from influenza at a younger age may suggest higher (genetic) susceptibility to severe disease.

To broaden our understanding, it is also valuable to consider related respiratory infections such as COVID‐19, which shares similar transmission pathways and clinical symptoms with influenza [8]. However, the two diseases are caused by different viruses and have different etiological characteristics. For instance, human influenza viruses utilize α‐2,6‐linked sialic acid receptors for cell entry, whereas SARS‐CoV‐2 relies on ACE2 and TMPRSS2. Although Kosmicki et al. provided important insight into genetic susceptibility to influenza infection [7], several aspects of hospitalized influenza remain unaddressed. First, genetic determinants of progression to severe disease may differ from those governing susceptibility to infection. Second, no prior genome‐wide study has examined the age of first influenza hospitalization. Third, a systematic comparison between the genetic architecture of hospitalized (rather than self‐reported) influenza and hospitalized COVID‐19 has not previously been performed.

The present study addressed each of these knowledge gaps by characterizing the genetic landscape of hospitalized influenza and exploring its genetic similarities and differences with hospitalized COVID‐19. We conducted both binary and time‐to‐event GWAS analyses to identify loci associated with the risk of influenza‐related hospitalization. An integrative gene prioritization framework was then applied to identify potential causal genes underlying these associations. We further performed genetic differentiation and colocalization analyses to determine the shared and distinct genetic features underlying hospitalized influenza and hospitalized COVID‐19. Collectively, our findings provide novel insights into the host genetic factors of hospitalized influenza and its relationship with hospitalized COVID‐19, which may help to clarify their respective pathophysiology and inform targeted therapeutic approaches.

Methods

Genome‐Wide Association Study (GWAS) Analysis of influenza in UKBB

We conducted a GWAS on hospitalized influenza (Flu_UKBB) (as a binary outcome) using individual‐level phenotypic and genotypic data from the UK Biobank (UKBB) [9]. Cases were defined as UKBB participants with hospitalization or mortality due to influenza (ICD‐10 codes: J09, J10, J11; HESIN‐restricted, UKBB Category 2000), whereas all other UKBB participants served as controls.

It should be noted that controls in this analysis were unselected population controls drawn from the remaining UK Biobank participants. This control group may include individuals who were never infected with influenza as well as individuals who experienced mild or undiagnosed influenza without requiring hospitalization. The association signals identified in this study may reflect a combination of genetic susceptibility to infection and genetic factors influencing progression to hospitalization, rather than severity conditional on confirmed infection alone. We therefore use the term hospitalized influenza throughout this manuscript to describe this phenotype accurately.

To minimize population stratification bias, we included only White British individuals of Caucasian ancestry, as defined in UKBB Data‐Field 22006, resulting in 1037 cases and 404,108 controls. Genetic variants with an imputation score > 0.3 were retrieved and subject to quality control using PLINK2 [10], applying a Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium test threshold of p < 1 × 10−6 and a missing call rate < 0.01. An INFO score threshold of > 0.3 is a widely used, relatively lenient quality‐control threshold for large‐scale UK Biobank GWAS, adopted here to maximize genomic coverage for the subsequent meta‐analysis with FinnGen. Reassuringly, all lead and independent significant SNPs reported in Table 1 had considerably higher INFO scores (range 0.80–1.00), well above this minimum threshold, indicating that our key findings do not depend on low‐confidence imputed genotypes. GWAS was conducted using fastGWA‐GLMM [11], which efficiently fits a mixed‐effects logistic regression model for binary traits in large‐scale biobank data and applies Saddle Point Approximation (SPA) to correct for case–control imbalance. Covariates, including age, sex, age2, age × sex, and the first 10 principal components, were adjusted for in the analysis. BETA represents the log‐odds ratio (log (OR)) per copy of the effect allele, estimated from (mixed‐model) logistic regression in fastGWA‐GLMM; we additionally report OR (95% CI), calculated as exp. (BETA ± 1.96 × SE), in Table 1 for ease of interpretation. We standardized GWAS summary statistics using MungeSumstats [12] with default parameters, aligning them to GRCh37. After filtering, 7,074,802 biallelic SNPs with a minor allele count (MAC) > 10 present in the reference file (dbSNP144) were retained for further meta‐analysis.

Time‐to‐Event GWAS

We also performed a time‐to‐event analysis to reveal genetic factors associated with time to the first flu hospitalization, as hospitalization at a younger age may indicate stronger susceptibility to severe infection. To account for family relatedness in survival analysis, we removed 48,034 third‐degree relatives (gcta ‐‐grm‐cutoff 0.125) from a cohort of 405,145 genetically confirmed Caucasians with phenotypic, genotypic, and clinical data updated to 2021 in the UKBB. For each unrelated participant, those with at least one influenza hospitalization record were assigned event = 1, with survival time (years) calculated as the time from birth to first diagnosis. For all others, event = 0, and survival time was determined as the minimum of death date or lost‐to‐follow‐up date, minus the birth date. It should be noted that hospital admission records linked to UK Biobank (Hospital Episode Statistics) are available from 1997 onward for participants in England, 1998 for Wales, and 1981 for Scotland, whereas survival time in this analysis was calculated from birth. This approach does not formally account for left truncation, as only individuals who reached the start of the observable period without a qualifying event could contribute to the risk set at earlier ages. To evaluate the robustness of our findings to this modeling assumption, we performed a sensitivity analysis (see the Results section) comparing standard Cox models against left‐truncated Cox models, in which entry time was approximated as the start of hospital record linkage (1997), for the independent SNPs at the UGT2B4 locus. The same covariates and genotype quality control criteria as in the binary flu GWAS were applied. The genome‐wide time‐to‐event analysis was conducted using SPACox [13], an SPA‐based Cox proportional hazards model, to identify SNPs associated with time‐to‐hospitalization due to influenza (Flu_SPACox).

Meta‐Analysis Across UKBB and FinnGen

We also retrieved GWAS summary results from FinnGen (release 11) and conducted meta‐analysis with UKBB to improve statistical power.

FinnGen summary statistics (Flu_FinnGen) for clinically diagnosed influenza (endpoint: J10_INFLUENZA [https://r11.risteys.finregistry.fi/endpoints/J10_INFLUENZA], representing hospitalized or fatal cases only; in total 10,134 cases and 378,292 controls) were downloaded. After standardizing the GWAS formats for UKBB and FinnGen, overlapping variants with MAC > 10 were meta‐analyzed. Meta‐analysis was performed using METAL [14] under an inverse‐variance weighted fixed‐effects model.

The resulting summary statistics were subsequently used as input for DIST [15] to impute the GWAS results of the unmeasured SNPs. Following quality control (MAC > 10, imputation quality INFO > 0.8), we obtained 8,258,810 biallelic SNPs, comprising 7,241,071 common variants (minor allele frequency [MAF] > 0.01; Flu_meta) and 1,017,739 rare variants (MAF < 0.01). Our subsequent analyses and results focused on the common variants.

Meta‐Analysis With Time‐to‐Event GWAS

To further consider GWAS results from the time‐to‐hospitalization GWAS, we also performed meta‐analysis of p‐values from Flu_SPACox and Flu_meta using the harmonic mean p‐value (HMP) method [16]. Sample overlap exists between Flu_SPACox and the UKBB component of Flu_meta. However, the HMP method is robust to dependency among tests and can benefit from positive correlations, as demonstrated by Wilson [16]. This analysis included 6,212,866 SNPs with MAF > 0.01 (Flu_hmp), which were meta‐analyzed using the R package harmonicmeanp.

Estimation of SNP‐Based Heritability and Genetic Correlation

We first merged GWAS results with HapMap3 SNPs to obtain a set of stable, high‐quality variants. LD score regression (LDSC) [17] was then used to estimate SNP‐based heritability (h 2) on the liability scale and genetic correlation (rg) using precomputed 1000 Genomes European LD scores. The case ratio was assumed to represent both sample and population prevalence.

Functional Mapping and Annotation of GWAS

All GWAS summary statistics were annotated and characterized using the FUMA [18] online platform. Genomic risk loci were identified via the SNP2GENE module with default settings. Additionally, MAGMA [19] was used for gene and gene‐set analyses without extending genic boundaries to detect phenotype‐associated genes and biological pathways. MAGMA gene‐property analysis integrated GWAS with gene expression data from 54 tissue‐specific datasets in GTEx v8 and 12 single‐cell expression datasets, identifying enriched tissues and cell types. Multiple testing correction was applied using a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Transcriptome‐Wide Association Study (TWAS) and Splicing TWAS (spTWAS)

Tissue‐specific TWAS and spTWAS analyses were conducted to identify genes whose genetically predicted mRNA expression or splicing activity is associated with hospitalized influenza. We first integrated GWAS data with GTEx v8 eQTL and sQTL MASHR models for 49 tissues using S‐PrediXcan [20]. To enhance statistical power, we then performed a cross‐tissue TWAS/spTWAS using S‐MultiXcan [21]. Intron coordinates were mapped to genes using leafcutter [22] for improved interpretability.

Proteome‐Wide Association Study (PWAS)

To detect risk genes whose genetically regulated protein abundance is associated with hospitalized influenza, we conducted a PWAS using FUSION [23]. Predictive models of plasma protein levels from European American populations [24] were combined with GWAS data to estimate proteome‐phenotype associations.

Integrative Gene Prioritization Framework

We employed an integrative framework to prioritize risk genes within genome‐wide significant loci by combining multiple GWAS annotation tools and gene‐based association tests. First, Open Targets [25] was used to identify the closest gene and the gene with the highest V2G score for each significant SNP. The V2G pipeline integrates various datasets, including QTLs, chromatin interactions, physical distances, and computational functional predictions, to rank potential causal genes. FUMA [18] was then applied to annotate influenza GWAS results. SNPs were assigned to genes based on significant eQTL mapping (FDR < 0.05), with the gene showing the strongest eQTL signal (minimum FDR) designated as the most relevant. SNPs were further characterized using CADD scores [26] (variant deleteriousness) and RegulomeDB scores [27] (regulatory potential). Functional enrichment was assessed using ANNOVAR [28]. Genes annotated with the highest CADD scores, best RegulomeDB ranks, or nonsynonymous substitutions were prioritized. Additionally, significant genes from MAGMA, TWAS, spTWAS, and PWAS analyses were incorporated. Finally, a phenome‐wide association study (PheWAS) was performed using GWAS Atlas [29] to test gene associations with immunological or respiratory traits.

Identification of SNPs/Genes Distinguishing Hospitalized Influenza From COVID‐19

To identify genetic markers distinguishing hospitalized influenza from hospitalized COVID‐19, we applied the DDx algorithm [30], treating hospitalized influenza as the “pseudo‐case” and hospitalized COVID‐19 as the “pseudo‐control.” GWAS data for hospitalized COVID‐19 were obtained from the COVID‐19 Host Genetics Initiative (Release 7, B2_ALL, primarily European ancestry). Effect sizes of differential associations between the two conditions were calculated using the original GWAS effect sizes. Further analysis of genes, pathways, tissues, and cell types was conducted using MAGMA, TWAS, spTWAS, and PWAS, similar to the approach described earlier.

Identification of Colocalized SNPs and Genes

To identify genetic variants contributing to both hospitalized influenza and COVID‐19 risk, we conducted a colocalization analysis using the cofdr [31] approach with GWAS Z‐scores as input. This analysis yields posterior probabilities (PPA) that categorize each SNP as being associated with (1) influenza only, (2) COVID‐19 only, (3) both diseases (PPA > 0.9), or (4) neither disease.

We note that cofdr identifies SNPs/genes with statistical evidence of association with both traits based on shared Z‐score patterns but, unlike formal colocalization methods (e.g., coloc) that explicitly model a shared‐versus‐distinct causal variant assumption, does not by itself distinguish a single shared causal variant from two distinct, linked causal variants. Given that no SNP reached the conventional colocalization threshold of PPA ≥ 0.9 (the highest observed SNP‐level PPA was 0.564; Table 8), we describe the SNP‐level findings as “shared association signals” rather than conclusive colocalization throughout this manuscript, reserving the term “colocalization” for the gene‐level results that reached PPA ≥ 0.9 (ST6GAL1 and ICAM5), while still interpreting even these with appropriate caution regarding the possibility of distinct, linked causal variants.

Cofdr was also extended to the gene level using Z‐scores from MAGMA, TWAS, spTWAS, and PWAS. For further methodological details, please refer to ref [31] and the supporting information therein.

Flu_meta comprises participants of European ancestry only (UK Biobank White British and FinnGen Finnish populations), whereas the COVID‐19 HGI B2_ALL (Release 7) GWAS is a multi‐ancestry meta‐analysis that is predominantly, but not exclusively, of European ancestry, with smaller contributions from East Asian, South Asian, African, and admixed American populations. Because UK Biobank contributes to both Flu_meta and B2_ALL, partial sample overlap exists between the two GWAS, predominantly through shared population controls. To assess whether this overlap (or residual population stratification) could inflate the apparent genetic correlation or shared association signals between hospitalized influenza and COVID‐19, we performed bivariate LDSC between Flu_meta and B2_ALL using precomputed 1000 Genomes European LD scores and examined the cross‐trait LDSC intercept as a diagnostic for sample overlap/stratification‐driven confounding.

Results

Genome‐Wide Risk Loci Associated With Hospitalized Influenza

In summary, we conducted a GWAS on hospitalized influenza in the UK Biobank (UKBB) and subsequently performed a meta‐analysis incorporating the GWAS of hospitalized influenza from FinnGen. Z‐scores for SNPs that were not directly measured in the meta‐analysis were imputed and filtered using an INFO score threshold of > 0.8.

Applying FUMA's default LD‐clumping parameters, we identified eight independent significant SNPs across four genomic risk loci in Flu_meta, six of which were imputed. However, rs139529053 was the only SNP at its locus, and no surrounding common variants were found to be in linkage disequilibrium (LD) with it. Therefore, it was excluded as a lead SNP (Table 1; Figure 1c).

Manhattan plot of the common variant GWAS for hospitalized influenza. Each data point in the Manhattan plot represents the −log₁₀ p‐value of a single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) with a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.01 from (a) UK Biobank (UKBB), (b) FinnGen, and (c) a meta‐analysis of UKBB and FinnGen data. The red horizontal line indicates the conventional genome‐wide significance threshold (p < 5 × 10−8). The nearest protein‐coding gene to each lead SNP (within ± 500 kb, as defined by Open Targets) is labeled in the plot.

The strongest association was observed for rs13088725, an intronic variant of ST6GAL1, which encodes β‐galactoside α‐2,6‐sialyltransferase I, an enzyme responsible for the synthesis of α‐2,6‐linked sialic acid receptors [32]. A PheWAS in the GWAS Atlas database [29] indicated that the rs13088725‐C allele was associated with the inflammatory marker mean platelet volume (MPV) [33] at an FDR threshold of 0.05 (p = 6.31 × 10−4, FDR = 0.013). Additionally, four other independent significant SNPs were identified within this locus, including rs17374212 and rs3868901, both of which were present in the UKBB and FinnGen cohorts and passed heterogeneity tests. Specifically, rs17374212 was nominally significant in Flu_UKBB (p = 0.0173) and was close to genome‐wide significance in FinnGen (p = 5.78 × 10−8), and rs3868901 showed a similar pattern (p = 0.0481 in UKBB; p = 2.77 × 10−7 in FinnGen). These influenza‐associated SNPs also function as significant expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) for ST6GAL1 in various tissues, as identified using GTEx [34] and eQTLGen [35]. For instance, rs13088725‐C and rs13061952‐C were associated with lower ST6GAL1 expression in blood (p = 5.5 × 10−11) [35] and lung (p = 1.5 × 10−5) [34], respectively.

The second most significant association was observed for rs17093811, located near AASDHPPT, which encodes the phosphopantetheinyl transferase enzyme [36]. PheWAS revealed that this SNP was correlated with a human CD4 T cell subset, CD28+CD127− (p = 1.96 × 10−4, FDR = 0.021) [37]. Additionally, the rs17093811‐C allele was linked to higher AASDHPPT expression in blood (p = 7.9 × 10−23) [35].

Another genome‐wide significant association was identified for rs117391169, located within intronic regions of SPATS2L, a type I interferon (IFN)–stimulated gene implicated in multiple autoimmune diseases [38, 39].

For the two independent significant SNPs directly genotyped/imputed in both UKBB and FinnGen prior to meta‐analysis (rs17374212 and rs3868901, both at the ST6GAL1 locus), direction of effect was fully concordant between cohorts, and Cochran's Q test showed no evidence of heterogeneity (P_het = 0.384 and 0.653, respectively; I 2 = 0% for both; Table S16). For the remaining five independent significant SNPs, cohort‐specific summary statistics were unavailable in the raw UKBB GWAS output, most likely reflecting the considerably smaller UKBB case sample (1037 cases) relative to FinnGen (10,134 cases) and the associated MAC/QC filtering; formal cross‐cohort heterogeneity testing is therefore not applicable to these SNPs. Notably, however, three of these five SNPs (rs13088725, rs73187789, and rs13061952, all at the ST6GAL1 locus) already reached genome‐wide significance based on FinnGen data alone (FinnGen p = 2.99 × 10−8, 1.92 × 10−8, and 5.13 × 10−8, respectively), independent of any contribution from UKBB or DIST imputation, providing strong independent support for this locus.

Regional association plots for all four genomic risk loci (ST6GAL1, SPATS2L, and AASDHPPT from Flu_meta; UGT2B4 from Flu_SPACox), generated using FUMA, are shown in Figure S1. These plots confirm that, at each locus, the genome‐wide significant signal is supported by a cluster of surrounding SNPs in moderate‐to‐high LD with the lead variant, consistent with a genuine, non‐artefactual association signal.

To further evaluate whether these loci are supported independently of DIST imputation, we repeated the search for the best‐associated SNP within each locus region using only SNPs directly present in both UKBB and FinnGen prior to DIST imputation (Table S17). The ST6GAL1 locus remained genome‐wide significant using this restricted, directly measured SNP set alone (rs56254521, p = 1.49 × 10−9), confirming that this association is not an artefact of DIST imputation. In contrast, the best directly measured SNPs at the SPATS2L and AASDHPPT loci reached only nominal significance (p = 1.26 × 10−3 and 2.32 × 10−2, respectively), indicating that the genome‐wide significant status of these two loci in Flu_meta currently relies substantially on DIST‐imputed variants. We highlight this distinction explicitly in the Discussion and Limitations and Future Directions section and recommend that the SPATS2L and AASDHPPT associations be interpreted with additional caution pending replication in datasets with direct genotyping coverage of these regions.

A Novel Locus Identified in the Genome‐Wide Survival Analysis of Hospitalized Influenza

We applied SPACox to the hospitalized influenza cohort from UKBB and identified three genome‐wide significant SNPs, all mapping to a single locus (Flu_SPACox; Table 2; Figure 2a). After integrating p‐values from Flu_SPACox and Flu_meta using the HMP approach, we identified four independent SNPs across two loci (Flu_hmp; Table 3; Figure 2b). Notably, the ST6GAL1 locus was detected in both the conventional binary GWAS (Flu_meta) and the meta‐analyzed time‐to‐event GWAS (Flu_hmp). Examination of the component p‐values (Table 2b) indicated that this signal was driven predominantly by the binary meta‐analysis (meta p = 1.50 × 10−9) rather than the time‐to‐event analysis alone (SPACox p = 0.0150–0.0170). HMP was used here to combine dependent test statistics from the two analyses to enhance overall detection power, and these results should not be interpreted as independent evidence that ST6GAL1 or UGT2B4 influence both hospitalization risk and its timing.

A novel locus, rs6821234, was identified through a genome‐wide survival analysis of hospitalized influenza patients. This SNP is located closest to UGT2B4, with UGT2A1 and UGT2A2 being the second and third nearest genes, respectively. Rs6821234 has been shown to directly influence the expression levels of various isoforms within the UGT2A and UGT2B gene families, including UGT2A1 in the liver [34], UGT2A3P7 in the left ventricle of the heart [34], UGT2B4 in the lung [34], and UGT2B11 in blood [35]. Additionally, a PheWAS revealed a significant association between rs6821234 and TNF‐α levels in the immune system (p = 1.95 × 10−4, FDR = 0.013) [40].

To assess the robustness of these findings to the choice of time origin in the survival model, we performed a sensitivity analysis for the three independent SNPs at the UGT2B4 locus, comparing the original birth‐origin Cox model against a left‐truncated Cox model in which entry time was approximated as the start of hospital record linkage (1997) (Table S15). Thirteen individuals (0.004% of the analysis sample) had an event or censoring time preceding this assumed entry time and were excluded from both models to ensure a matched comparison sample. Effect estimates were highly consistent between the two approaches: rs6821234 showed HR = 0.741 (95% CI: 0.666–0.823, p = 2.61 × 10−8) under the birth‐origin model versus HR = 0.740 (95% CI: 0.666–0.823, p = 2.50 × 10−8) under the left‐truncated model, and similarly, close estimates were observed for rs1583243 and rs13122577 (Table S15). These results indicate that our time‐to‐event findings are not materially affected by left truncation under this approximation.

We also note that rs6821234 did not reach significance in the binary case–control meta‐analysis (Flu_meta p = 0.117; Table 2b), indicating that this locus is associated specifically with the timing of hospitalization rather than with overall hospitalization risk.

Gene Prioritization Results for Influenza

The gene prioritization framework was applied to genes that met at least one of the following criteria: (1) the closest gene to independent significant SNPs in the meta‐analyzed influenza GWAS or time‐to‐event influenza GWAS, (2) the gene with the highest variant‐to‐gene (V2G) score for these SNPs, or (3) genes identified in any of the gene‐based association tests.

Among the prioritized genes, ST6GAL1 received the highest prioritization score, making it the strongest candidate for association with hospitalized influenza. Gene‐based association analysis using MAGMA identified ST6GAL1 as the most significantly associated gene (Table S2). TWAS analysis linked elevated ST6GAL1 mRNA expression in seven tissues to an increased risk of hospitalized influenza (Table S3), including the thyroid, sigmoid colon, testis, left ventricle, atrial appendage, tibial artery, and minor salivary gland. Additionally, spTWAS identified a significant association between splice activity at chr3:186681714‐186756530 within ST6GAL1 and hospitalized influenza in prostate tissue (Table S4). Furthermore, PheWAS analysis demonstrated significant associations between ST6GAL1 and various types of blood cells [33].

SPATS2L ranked second in our prioritization analysis and was associated with pulmonary function [41], pain in throat and chest [42], and red blood cells [33], as indicated by PheWAS. MSANTD4 was prioritized by the V2G pipeline in Open Targets, despite not being the nearest gene to the lead SNP rs17093811 on chromosome 11. TWAS identified MSANTD4 as significantly associated with gene expression in the liver, small intestine terminal ileum, and esophagus mucosa (Table S3).

AASDHPPT was the closest gene to the lead SNP rs17093811 and had the second‐highest V2G score. SpTWAS analysis in whole blood suggested its involvement in hospitalized influenza through splicing regulation (Table S4). UGT2B4 satisfied two prioritization criteria and was the only gene identified in the time‐to‐event GWAS of hospitalized influenza.

ADIPOQ and CADM1 received lower prioritization scores. ADIPOQ had the highest V2G score for the independent SNP rs17374212 on chromosome 3, despite being the second closest gene to the SNP. CADM1 was the only gene identified by PWAS and exhibited protective effects against hospitalized influenza (Table S5).

SNPs/Genes Differentiating Between Hospitalized Influenza and COVID‐19

To identify distinct genetic architectures underlying hospitalized influenza and COVID‐19, we applied the DDx method to compare the GWAS results of these two diseases (Flu_meta vs. B2_ALL). Our analysis identified 3019 SNPs that were differentially associated with the two conditions at a stringent p‐value threshold of 5 × 10−8, aggregating into 29 risk loci (Table 4a; Figure 3). Notably, all identified lead SNPs reached genome‐wide significance in COVID‐19, except for rs2876034 and rs12567716, which achieved significance at p < 5 × 10−7. In contrast, none of these SNPs showed significant associations with influenza, even at the suggestive significance level of p < 1 × 10−5 (Table 4b). As such, the loci listed in Table 4 are differentially associated between the two conditions at current sample sizes; given the considerably larger sample size of the COVID‐19 GWAS, this pattern may reflect a combination of true COVID‐19‐specific effects and insufficient power to detect a shared association in the smaller influenza GWAS. Furthermore, only two SNPs, rs34725611‐G (BETA = −0.084, p = 0.023) and rs10774679‐T (BETA = −0.076, p = 0.011), marginally reached nominal significance (p < 0.05) in the GWAS for hospitalized influenza. Both SNPs conferred protective effects against hospitalized influenza but exhibited opposite effects in hospitalized COVID‐19.

Analysis of Shared Association Signals (cofdr) Between Hospitalized Influenza and COVID‐19, With Gene‐Level Colocalization

Before examining shared association signals in detail, we first assessed the overall genetic relationship between hospitalized influenza and hospitalized COVID‐19 using bivariate LDSC. This analysis revealed a modest, statistically significant positive genetic correlation (rg = 0.221, SE = 0.087, Z = 2.56, p = 0.0106; observed‐scale h 2 = 0.0034 [SE = 0.0007] for Flu_meta and 0.0038 [SE = 0.0003] for B2_ALL; liability‐scale h 2 = 0.037 [SE = 0.008] and 0.033 [SE = 0.003], respectively; Table S1). Importantly, the cross‐trait LDSC intercept, which reflects confounding from sample overlap and/or shared population stratification, was small in magnitude (0.0112, SE = 0.0050), corresponding to only a minor fraction of the total estimated genetic covariance (0.0008, SE = 0.0003, observed scale). This suggests that the observed genetic correlation and the shared association signals identified below are unlikely to be driven predominantly by sample overlap between the two studies, although some residual confounding cannot be fully excluded given partial contribution of UK Biobank to both GWAS.

To identify SNPs with evidence of shared association between hospitalized influenza and COVID‐19, we applied the cofdr algorithm using Z‐scores of overlapping variants from the respective GWAS as input. The top 10 independent SNPs with the highest posterior probability of association (PPA), as determined by cofdr, are listed in Table 8. Notably, rs2278442‐A exhibited the highest PPA, though at a moderate level of 0.564. This SNP was nominally significant in hospitalized influenza (p = 5.69 × 10−4) and suggestively significant in hospitalized COVID‐19 (p = 1.58 × 10−6). The effect allele (A) was associated with an increased risk of hospitalized influenza but had a protective effect against hospitalized COVID‐19. The algorithm also identified rs2278442‐A as a significant differential SNP at a suggestive significance level (p = 1.03 × 10−7).

Discussion

We conducted both binary and time‐to‐event GWAS to investigate the genetic factors influencing hospitalized influenza, utilizing data from the UK Biobank and FinnGen. Our analyses identified four genomic risk loci, with the strongest association observed at the ST6GAL1 gene. Further differentiation analysis comparing hospitalized influenza and COVID‐19 GWAS findings revealed distinct genetic architectures, with 29 differentially associated loci and genes, as well as three gene sets and two tissue types specific to COVID‐19. Additionally, colocalization analysis suggested shared genetic mechanisms between these respiratory infections, highlighting ST6GAL1 and ICAM5 as potential key candidates.

ST6GAL1 as a Key Locus in Influenza Pathogenesis

ST6GAL1 consistently emerged as a significant locus in both the binary and meta‐analyzed time‐to‐event GWAS, making it the most promising candidate associated with hospitalized influenza. It should be noted that this HMP signal was driven predominantly by the binary GWAS component (meta p = 1.50 × 10−9) rather than by the time‐to‐event analysis alone (SPACox p = 0.0150–0.0170; Table 2b), and should not be interpreted as evidence that ST6GAL1 independently influences the timing of hospitalization. The lead SNP within this locus, rs13088725, was particularly notable for its strong association with a reduced risk of hospitalization and the lowest p‐value among all examined SNPs, showing its potential role in genetic susceptibility to influenza. Moreover, rs13088725 is associated with MPV, an inflammatory marker, suggesting its involvement in immune response and inflammation. This variant is also significantly linked to lower ST6GAL1 expression in blood tissue, offering insights into a possible genetic mechanism influencing influenza risk. Functionally, ST6GAL1 regulates sialic acid receptor synthesis, which serves as a binding target for influenza viruses, facilitating viral entry into host cells [43, 44]. Suppressing ST6GAL1 expression or activity has been shown to reduce viral entry and replication in epithelial cells [45, 46, 47] and infant mice [47], suggesting it may warrant further investigation as a potential therapeutic target, pending functional validation.

ST6GAL1 was also highlighted by Kosmicki et al., who examined general susceptibility to self‐reported influenza using a 23andMe cross‐ancestry discovery cohort, with UK Biobank and FinnGen used only for replication of their lead signal (rs16861415) [7]. Because UKBB and FinnGen serve as the primary discovery cohorts in the present study, some samples overlap with the replication cohorts of Kosmicki et al. are unavoidable. However, the phenotypes differ fundamentally between self‐reported susceptibility to infection and hospitalization for a clinically confirmed, more severe outcome.

The top SNP we identified (rs13088725) is in moderate LD with the lead variant reported by Kosmicki et al. (rs16861415; r 2 = 0.370, computed from the UK Biobank in‐sample genotype data used as the LD reference panel below; a concordant estimate, r 2 = 0.386, was independently obtained using the 1000 Genomes European panel via LDpop [48]). To formally evaluate the relationship between these two signals, we performed a GCTA‐COJO conditional analysis using UK Biobank in‐sample genotypes as the LD reference. Conditioning rs13088725 on rs16861415 attenuated its effect size by approximately 55% (unconditional BETA = −0.0122, p = 7.36 × 10−10; conditional BETA = −0.0056, p = 5.14 × 10−3), whereas conditioning rs16861415 on rs13088725 produced a similar attenuation of approximately 61% (unconditional BETA = −0.0163, p = 6.36 × 10−9; conditional BETA = −0.0063, p = 2.48 × 10−2). Both SNPs retained nominal significance after mutual conditioning but fell well below the genome‐wide significance threshold, indicating that the two loci are not fully statistically independent but rather represent partially overlapping association signals at the ST6GAL1 locus, consistent with their moderate pairwise LD. This suggests that rs13088725 and rs16861415 likely tag overlapping, but not identical, genetic effects, reflecting either two correlated proxies for a single underlying causal variant or two distinct but correlated functional variants within this locus (Table S14). In addition, this study provides further insight into the functional role of ST6GAL1 through TWAS, PWAS, and splicing‐TWAS analyses, which were not explored in the previous study.

Roles of SPATS2L and AASDHPPT in Immune and Metabolic Pathways

SPATS2L ranked second in our integrative prioritization framework. We note that, unlike the ST6GAL1 locus, the genome‐wide significant status of the SPATS2L and AASDHPPT loci in Flu_meta depends substantially on DIST‐imputed summary statistics; the best SNP directly measured in both UKBB and FinnGen at each of these loci reached only nominal significance (p = 1.26 × 10−3 and 2.32 × 10−2, respectively; Table S17). These associations should therefore be interpreted with additional caution and would benefit from replication in independent, directly genotyped datasets. SPATS2L is upregulated in response to IFNs and plays a role in autoimmunity [38, 39]. The IFN signaling pathway is crucial for both innate and adaptive immune responses against viral infections, including influenza, by triggering antiviral protein production [49]. Additionally, SPATS2L was identified as a key biomarker with high fold‐change in gene expression during influenza challenge tests [50], suggesting its potential involvement in disease pathophysiology. Its differential expression in the whole blood of pediatric influenza patients [51] further supports its role in antiviral immune responses. Moreover, its identification as a causal gene for lung function [41] and bronchodilator response in asthma [52] highlights its broader impact on respiratory health. Although direct experimental evidence linking SPATS2L to influenza remains limited, its role in IFN signaling and respiratory function suggests intriguing diagnostic and therapeutic possibilities.

Another significant locus identified in the binary GWAS was rs17093811, located near the AASDHPPT gene. This gene encodes a phosphopantetheinyl transferase enzyme involved in the biosynthesis of coenzyme A (CoA) [53], a key factor in amino acid and lipid metabolism, which influences cellular energy production and utilization [54]. AASDHPPT also plays a role in mitochondrial fatty acid synthesis (mtFAS) [55, 56, 57], an essential process for mitochondrial respiration and oxidative metabolism. Given its fundamental role in biosynthetic pathways, disruptions in AASDHPPT function may affect metabolic balance and indirectly influence susceptibility to influenza.

UGT2B4 and Temporal Dynamics of Influenza Hospitalization

In the time‐to‐event GWAS, we identified a novel locus, rs6821234, near the UGT2B4 gene, with UGT2A1 and UGT2A2 as the closest neighboring genes. Notably, this locus reached genome‐wide significance in the time‐to‐event analysis (SPACox p = 1.82 × 10−8) but not in the binary case–control meta‐analysis (Flu_meta p = 0.117; Table 2b), indicating that its association is specific to the timing of hospitalization rather than overall hospitalization risk. This finding should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution and warrants replication in independent time‐to‐event analyses. This locus is responsible for regulating isoform expression in the UGT2A and UGT2B gene families, which encode UDP‐glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) enzymes essential for metabolizing various substances [58, 59]. The involvement of UGT2B4 in xenobiotic metabolism suggests that genetic variation in detoxification pathways may influence the timing of influenza hospitalization. The role of UGT2B4 in metabolizing estrogens and fatty acids [60] raises intriguing questions about sex‐specific or age‐related differences in the timing of hospitalization, requiring further investigation.

Differential Genetic Architectures Between Influenza and COVID‐19

In the differential GWAS, we identified 29 loci with lead SNPs reaching genome‐wide significance in COVID‐19 (excluding rs2876034 and rs12567716, which had p < 5 × 10−7) but not in hospitalized influenza. Given the considerably larger sample size of the COVID‐19 GWAS relative to Flu_meta, this pattern may partly reflect differences in statistical power rather than genetic factors that are exclusively specific to COVID‐19. Notably, RAB2A and FBRSL1 emerged as key differentiating genes, suggesting their distinct roles in COVID‐19 pathophysiology. RAB2A has a proviral role in COVID‐19 by facilitating SARS‐CoV‐2 replication through interactions with viral proteins NSP7 and ORF3a [61]. Although FBRSL1 has not been previously associated with lung traits, PheWAS linked it to red blood cell levels [62], which are vital for oxygen transport, and to comorbidities commonly observed in COVID‐19 patients, such as type 2 diabetes [63] and cardiovascular disease [64, 65]. Aggregating gene‐level differential effects further revealed three gene‐sets and two tissues (lung and spleen) that reached significance for COVID‐19 but not influenza at current sample sizes. Although this may point to genetic factors more strongly influencing immune and pulmonary function in COVID‐19, we cannot exclude that this reflects the greater statistical power of the COVID‐19 GWAS; larger influenza GWAS will be needed to resolve this.

Shared Mechanisms and Therapeutic Implications

Consistent with the SNP‐ and gene‐level shared association signals described below, genome‐wide bivariate LDSC indicated a modest but statistically significant positive genetic correlation between hospitalized influenza and COVID‐19 (rg = 0.22, p = 0.011), with only minor evidence of inflation from sample overlap or shared population stratification (cross‐trait intercept = 0.011, SE = 0.005; Table S1). In the colocalization analysis, the top‐ranking variant rs2278442‐A exhibited opposing effects between hospitalized influenza and COVID‐19 and was identified as a suggestively significant differential SNP by the DDx algorithm. PheWAS linked this variant to lower lymphocyte counts (p = 4 × 10−32) [66], an indicator of weakened immune response. Additionally, rs2278442‐A is an intronic variant of the ICAM3 gene, which encodes intercellular adhesion molecule 3, a protein highly expressed in lymphocytes [34] and essential for immune function. Given that rs2278442‐A is an expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) associated with higher ICAM3 expression [35], this variant may influence immune response modulation and, consequently, differential susceptibility to influenza and COVID‐19.

Gene‐level colocalization analysis identified ST6GAL1 as the gene with the highest posterior probability of shared association (PPA = 0.971) using MAGMA‐based cofdr, aligning with its known role in viral pathogenesis and its upregulation in severe COVID‐19 cases [67].

Additionally, ICAM5 showed evidence of shared association with both hospitalized influenza and COVID‐19 through PWAS‐based cofdr (gene‐level PPA = 0.955), with opposing directions of effect between the two diseases. In COVID‐19, this is broadly consistent with previous Mendelian randomization studies suggesting a protective role of ICAM5 against severe disease [68, 69, 70]. Its role in influenza hospitalization is, by contrast, far less established; we note only that other intercellular adhesion molecules are known to participate in viral attachment and entry [71], and that ICAM1, a structurally related family member [72], is upregulated during influenza infection and contributes to host immune defense [73]. Given this indirect and largely circumstantial evidence, we suggest ICAM5 as a hypothesis‐generating candidate for future functional studies in influenza rather than a validated causal gene and emphasize that PWAS/cofdr evidence alone cannot establish causality or direct therapeutic relevance.

Furthermore, among the 29 differential genes identified in the differential GWAS, four genes, THBS3, ABTB2, OAS3, and ANAPC4, also showed evidence of shared association at a more relaxed PPA threshold. These genes not only exhibit colocalization for both traits but also show differential associations, suggesting that they may modulate hospitalization risk in opposing directions.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study has some limitations. First, the meta‐analyzed GWAS of hospitalized influenza focused only on White British and Finnish populations, limiting its generalizability. The statistical power was constrained by the moderate sample size. Nevertheless, to our knowledge, this study is already the largest GWAS on hospitalized influenza infection to date. Additionally, we relied ICD‐10 codes for case definitions and did not distinguish influenza subtypes. Moreover, our control group consisted of unselected UK Biobank participants rather than individuals with confirmed influenza infection who were not hospitalized. The resulting phenotype therefore reflects a combination of susceptibility to infection and progression to hospitalization, and our findings should not be interpreted strictly as markers of disease severity conditional on infection. Future studies incorporating individuals with confirmed influenza infection who did not require hospitalization as controls would help disentangle genetic effects on susceptibility to infection from those specifically influencing hospitalization risk. Furthermore, our time‐to‐event analysis used age from birth as the time scale, which does not formally account for left truncation arising from the fact that hospital record linkage does not cover each participant's complete lifetime. We approximated entry time using the documented start of hospital record linkage (1997) as a sensitivity check for the UGT2B4 locus, which yielded highly consistent effect estimates (Table S15). This approximation does not account for the earlier linkage start dates in Scotland (1981) and Wales (1998), and future studies with access to individual‐level recruitment dates would allow more precise modeling of left truncation. In addition, six of the eight independent significant SNPs identified in Flu_meta relied on DIST imputation for at least one contributing cohort. Sensitivity analyses restricted to SNPs directly measured in both UKBB and FinnGen (Tables S16 and S17) confirmed that the ST6GAL1 locus is robust to the exclusion of DIST‐imputed variants, whereas the SPATS2L and AASDHPPT loci currently depend more heavily on DIST‐imputed data and should be interpreted with corresponding caution pending future replication. Our analyses did not account for environmental or lifestyle factors, such as temperature, BMI, and smoking, which could influence influenza susceptibility. Furthermore, the DDx differentiation analysis is inherently sensitive to differences in statistical power between the two contributing GWAS. Because the COVID‐19 HGI B2_ALL GWAS has a substantially larger effective sample size than Flu_meta (11,171 cases), many of the 29 loci and associated genes/tissues flagged as “differential” may reflect a lack of power to detect a shared association in the smaller influenza GWAS, rather than genetic effects that are truly exclusive to COVID‐19. This limitation should be borne in mind when interpreting the differentiation results, and we have revised our terminology throughout the manuscript to reflect this uncertainty. In addition, our findings warrant further experimental validations, making it necessary to interpret differential and colocalized results with caution, particularly given the risks of pleiotropy and LD.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our binary and time‐to‐event GWAS analyses identified four genomic risk loci associated with hospitalized influenza. The strongest association with ST6GAL1 highlights its candidate role in influenza pathogenesis and hospitalization risk and suggests it may warrant further investigation as a potential therapeutic target. The prioritization of SPATS2L, AASDHPPT, and UGT2B4 suggests their role in influenza hospitalization risk. Differential analysis with hospitalized COVID‐19 identified 29 loci and genes that were differentially associated between the two conditions at current sample sizes, reflecting a combination of power differences and potentially disease‐specific genetic architecture. Analysis of shared association signals further highlighted ST6GAL1 and ICAM5 as candidate genes with evidence of joint involvement in both diseases. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the genetic basis of hospitalized influenza and its relationship with COVID‐19, providing hypothesis‐generating insights that may inform future functional and therapeutic research.
