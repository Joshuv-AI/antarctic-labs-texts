In-situ Damascus-patterning enables tunable surface electric fields for bioactive titanium implants
Authors: Hanyang Yu, Nan Hou, Subrahmanyam Pattamatta, Sien Lin, Shi-ting Chen, Weixi Wu, Fenghui Duan, Youneng Xie, Xuliang Chen, Yunchen Long, Gan Li, Yuhan Chen, Siyao Chen, Yicheng Han, Xiaojiao You, Zhuoyuan Li, Yu Chai, Gang Li, Jian Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional titanium implants, although biocompatible, are inherently bioinert and typically require complex surface modifications to achieve satisfactory osseointegration. Here, we report a new additive manufacturing strategy that directly imparts bioactivity to high-performance titanium–tantalum implants (elastic modulus: 60–80 GPa; strength: >1100 MPa) through in situ compositional modulation, and this functionality is retained after subsequent subtractive processing. By precisely regulating the extent to which tantalum melts into the titanium matrix during laser powder bed fusion, periodic Damascus-like compositional patterns are generated that, after simple oxidation, produce stable periodic surface potential differences (P-SPD) tunable from 5.59 to 48.01 mV, spanning the range observed in natural tissues. The resulting electric field (∼2.35 V/m) also lies within the biologically active range used in external stimulation studies to promote osteogenesis, directed migration, and neural responses. The resulting endogenous electric field nearly doubles osteogenic performance relative to Ti64ELI controls, as evidenced by both in vitro assays and in vivo cranial defect repair. Mechanistic studies further reveal that P-SPD promotes both cell migration and osteogenic differentiation. This work establishes a strategy for the in situ integration of structural and biological functions through controlled melting behavior, offering a new paradigm for the surface functionalization of bioimplants.

Introduction

Bone regeneration remains a major challenge in the treatment of skeletal disorders and traumatic injuries. Metallic implants are widely used to provide the mechanical strength and structural stability required for skeletal repair, and titanium alloys, particularly Ti-6Al-4V ELI (Ti64ELI), have become the clinical standard for femoral, cranial, and intervertebral reconstruction owing to their high specific strength and excellent corrosion resistance. However, despite their excellent biocompatibility, these alloys are intrinsically bioinert and lack osteoinductive activity, thereby necessitating costly and complex surface modifications to achieve durable osseointegration.

Endogenous electric fields (EFs) regulate bone homeostasis and regeneration; accordingly, EF-functionalized materials can actively enhance osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and related cellular processes to accelerate healing [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. The regulatory effects of EFs are closely linked to ion channel activity, particularly that of membrane Ca2+ channels, which govern intracellular calcium homeostasis and thereby regulate diverse cellular processes [6]. EF-mediated activation of voltage-gated Ca2+ channels induces Ca2+ influx. This process governs the spatiotemporal dynamics of calcium signaling, thereby activating the CaMKII/CREB pathway to accelerate mineralization and collagen synthesis [7], while also regulating endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondrial functions [8]. Furthermore, mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation serves a dual role: it enhances ATP production through the activation of key mitochondrial enzymes to meet cellular energy demands, while also directly promoting osteogenic activity [9]. Surface potential gradients generated on implant surfaces can, upon implantation, induce local electric fields that facilitate Ca2+ influx through membrane potential–driven mechanisms and activate osteogenic pathways to enhance osseointegration [10,11]. In view of the osteogenic benefits of EFs, current therapeutic approaches include the implantation of piezoelectric materials and the surface coating modification of orthopedic implants; however, both require additional processing steps, leading to increased manufacturing complexity and cost [[12], [13], [14]]. Because cells directly interact with implant surfaces during migration, spontaneous polarization arising from heterogeneous surface structures can generate a surface potential difference (SPD), thereby creating a stable and long-lasting electric field without external intervention and facilitating bone defect repair. Motivated by the work-function disparity between titanium and tantalum [15], we identified the possibility of establishing periodic surface potential differences (P-SPD) through the spontaneous polarization of alternating Ti–Ta sheets [11].

Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) has emerged as a transformative platform for alloy design, offering an unprecedented ability not only to tailor raw metallic powders but also to consolidate dissimilar alloys within a single build. This capability fundamentally reshapes solidification pathways and permits controlled microscale immiscibility, thereby generating microstructural motifs unattainable through conventional metallurgical routes [[16], [17], [18]]. By harnessing such non-equilibrium states, additive manufacturing has opened a new conceptual frontier for the creation of architected microstructures in high-performance structural materials. Building on this paradigm, we re-examine the Ti–Ta system, in which the large melting-point disparity between Ti (∼1725 °C) and Ta (∼3020 °C) naturally hinders complete mixing during LPBF [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Importantly, by tuning the laser energy input, the fraction of Ta that melts and incorporates into the Ti matrix can be actively controlled, thereby producing Ti alloy matrices with tunable Ta contents. Rather than regarding the resulting heterogeneity as a defect, we exploit it as a design feature. Through deliberate in situ compositional modulation, periodic Ti–Ta Damascus-like patterns can be generated, which in turn give rise to programmable surface potential differences. This strategy transforms incomplete melting, traditionally regarded as a limitation, into a functional advantage, enabling direct surface bioactivation of additively manufactured implants without complex post-processing. In addition, Ta acts as a β-phase stabilizer in Ti, reducing the elastic modulus to 60–80 GPa and thereby improving mechanical compatibility and expanding the design space for implants [23,24]. When integrated with additive manufacturing, such bioactive Ti–Ta alloys provide a versatile platform for highly customized implant design [21,[25], [26], [27], [28]].

In this study, medical-grade Ti64ELI powder was mixed with 25 wt% Ta. By optimizing the L-PBF laser parameters, we identified two near-dense structures with distinct Ta contents in the matrix, both of which exhibited a low elastic modulus (60–80 GPa) and high strength (>1100 MPa). Alternating these parameter sets produced a Damascus-pattern architecture, and subsequent simple aqueous oxidation generated a P-SPD surface on the resulting hydrophilic oxide layer. Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) revealed a surface potential gradient spatially aligned with the patterned bands, while first-principles calculations further confirmed the tunability of the SPD. In vitro assays demonstrated that P-SPD promotes both osteogenesis and cell migration. When integrated into a metamaterial-inspired design and implanted in a rat defect model, the material exhibited excellent biocompatibility, no detectable cytotoxicity, and superior bone repair performance. This work provides a new strategy for fabricating Ti–Ta implants with intrinsic bioactivity through L-PBF and subsequent simple oxidation, while maintaining excellent mechanical properties.

Materials and methods

Raw materials for L-PBF printing

Throughout the study, Ti-6Al-4V ELI powder (15–53 μm, ASTM Grade 5, Aisa Materials, China) and tantalum powder (5–25 μm, Guangzhou Xinyuan, China) were utilized. A 25 wt% fraction of 5–25 μm tantalum powder was mixed with Ti64ELI. The mixing process was facilitated using agate balls in a rotary mixer for 2 h at a rotation speed of 20 rpm to ensure homogeneous powder distribution. Fig. S1 shows the SEM image and statistical data of particles calculated by ImageJ. To ensure powder-mixing homogeneity, samples were collected from the top, middle, and bottom layers of the storage container, processed by microwave digestion, and then quantitatively analyzed by ICP-OES (AVIO 220 MAX Spectrometer). The results are presented in Table S1, indicating that the powder mixture was reasonably uniform.

L-PBF printing for all testing samples

The L-PBF printing in this study was carried out using an EOS M100 machine (EOS GmbH, Germany). This system is equipped with a 200W Yb-fiber laser featuring a spot size of 40 μm. The printing process was conducted under an argon-protected environment with an oxygen concentration of ∼1000 ppm to prevent oxidation. The laser power varied between 60 and 170 W, with scanning speeds ranging from 400 to 1500 mm/s. Hatching spacing was set at 0.04, 0.06, 0.08, or 0.11 mm, while maintaining a constant layer thickness of 0.02 mm. The scanning direction was rotated by 67° between consecutive layers.

Materials characterization

The porosity and residual Ta particle content are calculated by ImageJ software, the calculation strategy is indicated by Fig. S2. The identification of phases were conducted by X-ray diffraction (D2 phaser XE-T X-ray Diffractometer System, Bruker) with Cu Kα radiation. The diffraction angle 2θ varies between 20° and 90° with a scanning rate of 0.2°/s. The microstructure of the alloy is observed by scanning electron microscope (SEM, Apreo 2, Thermo Fisher). The SEM built-in EDS system (Oxford Instruments, Ultim Max Infinity 100) was used to evaluate the chemical distribution on the tested surface. The transmission electron microscope (TEM) samples were prepared by focused ion beam (FIB, Scios, Thermo Fisher and CIQTEK DB550). TEM observations were conducted by JEOL-2100F with EDS.

Mechanical test

Rectangular-shaped blocks with a dimension of 12 mm × 58 mm × 4.5 mm was printed for the fabrication of tensile specimens. Electrical discharge machining (EDM) was used to separate the block and the substrate and also used to roughly cut into the tensile specimen with a width, gauge length, and thickness of 3 mm, 12.8 mm, and 1.5 mm, respectively. 240 to 2000 meshes grinding paper were used to remove the contaminated layer after EDM. The tensile tests were conducted by the INSTRON 3382 UTS system with a 10 mm extensometer. All tensile tests are conducted at a strain rate of 10−3. To protect the extensometer, it is removed when the strain reaches 2.5%. All experiments ensure that the material reaches the yield point before the extensometer is removed, which does not affect the observation and calculation of yield strengths and the elastic properties. To ensure the accuracy of the experiment, each sample group was tested three times.

Electrochemistry characterization

To study the basic corrosion behavior of the Ti64-Ta alloy, polarization curves were record. The experiment utilized the CH660e equipment (Chenhua, Shanghai). The test adopted a three-electrode system, namely the working electrode, counter electrode, and reference electrode. The working electrode used an in-house-made holder with an exposed area of 1 cm2. The Ti64-Ta specimen used for testing were polished and finely diamond-polished to minimize the influence of surface roughness on the experimental results. A platinum plate of 10 mm × 10 mm x 0.1 mm was used as the counter electrode, while a saturated calomel electrode served as the reference electrode. The test was conducted in a PbS buffer solution at 37 °C. All the tests were conducted 3 times for each type of sample.

Surface oxidation treatment and characterization

The surface oxidation treatment of the material was conducted in an acidic hydrogen peroxide solution. The reaction system had a total volume of 100 mL, consisting of 15% hydrogen peroxide and 1 mL hydrochloric acid. The oxidation process was carried out in an oil bath at 60 °C for 3 h to ensure stable reaction conditions and uniform surface modification. The contact angle before and after oxide film formation was measured using DataPhysics Contact Angle Tester. The droplet volume is 5 μL and the 4 tests were run for each specimen.

The characterization of surface oxides on the samples was performed using XPS (Thermo Fisher ESCALAB XI+). The obtained spectral data were analyzed using Avantage software to determine the chemical valence states and elemental composition ratios. All the tests were conducted 3 times on the surfaces of each type of sample. The corresponding Ti/Ta quantitative analysis is based on the 3 times of measurement. The surface roughness of the samples were measured by laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM, Nikon).

The surface potential differences (SPD) were measured by SKPM (scanning Kelvin probe microscope, Asylum Research, Cypher ES, Oxford Instrument) with Bruker SCM-PIT-v2 probes. The selected region spans across stripe boundaries. To obtain a stable surface potential map, the scanning speed was set at 0.1 Hz with a resolution of 512.

In vitro experiments

Cell culture

Bone marrow MSCs were isolated from the bone marrow fluid of SD rats. Cells were cultured in α-MEM medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin. BMSCs with passage 4 generation cells were cultured at density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 24 well plate.

Cytocompatibility

After culturing BMSCs on different samples, CCK-8 kits (Beyotime, China) were used for the determination of proliferation of BMSCs cultured on different samples for three consecutive days. Briefly, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured on a full-wavelength plate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, USA) after 200 μL of 10% CCK-8 solution was added to each sample Beyotime, China) after 1.5 h of incubation.

Cell adhesion and morphology

The BMSCs cultured on the different samples were also detected by fluorescence staining. In brief, after 72 h of cell culture, the cells were fixed on different fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at 4 °C, and 0.1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, China) was used to permeabilize the cells for 10 min. Then phalloidin-Alexa Fluor 488 (C2201S, Beyotime, China) was added and incubated for another 1 h in the dark. The cells were then counterstained with DAPI (C1006, Beyotime, China), followed by PBS, and observed under a laser confocal microscope (Nikon, JAPAN).

SEM was also performed to examine cell adhesion and morphology. After 5 days in co-culture, the cells were fixed with glutaraldehyde (2.5% v/v) for 2 h and dehydrated in ethanol gradients from 20%(V/V) to 100%(V/V). The samples were immersed in isoamyl acetate and critical-point dried. The prepared samples were imaged using SEM (Thermofisher Apreo 2S).

Matrix mineralization

BMSCs were cultivated on the surface of different samples. After 7 days of osteogenic induction, ALP staining, and Alizarin Red Staining were performed. Cells were washed three times in PBS and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (26 °C, 10 min). For ALP staining, the cells were incubated in a BCIP/NBT working solution (Beyotime, China) for 20 min in the dark. For Alizarin Red Staining, the cells were incubated in 40 mM Alizarin red S (Beyotime, China) was added for staining (26 °C, 30 min). Subsequently, the cells were washed 3 times with deionized water and observed by digital camera (Sony, Japan).

qRT-PCR

After co-culturing with different samples for 7 days, the cell media was removed before the addition of the Trizol reagent. After extraction, RNA samples were treated with RNase-free DNase I (AG, China). They were then reverse transcribed into cDNA using a synthesis kit (AG, China). The gene levels of ALP, Runx2, COL1A1 and OPN were analyzed by qRT-PCR (AG, China) using a mixture of dNTP reagent (AG, China) and RNase-free H2O (AG, China), as well as the forward and reverse primers listed below. The 2ΔΔCT method was used to analyze gene expression data. The primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Western blot

BMSCs co-cultured with different samples for 14 days at a density of 4 × 105 cells per well in the 6-well plates. The proteins were further quantified using the BCA kit. The samples with equal amounts of proteins (20 μg) were then separated by SDS-PAGE gel. Subsequently, 5% skimmed milk was used to block the PVDF membranes with transferred proteins. After incubation with primary antibodies, including COLIA1 (1:1000), ALP (1:1000), RUNX2 (1:1000), OPN (1:1000), Wnt3a(1:10000), p-GSK-3β(1:10000) and GAPDH(1: 1000) overnight at 4 °C, the PVDF films were incubated with anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-conjugated antibody (1:2000) for a further 1.5 h. Chemiluminescence detection was performed to visualize protein bands using ECL Substrate. The source data file contains untrimmed and unprocessed full-scan images of all Western blots.

Whole-cell patch clamp

The BMSCs (3 × 103 cells/cm2, Neurobasal medium) were co-cultured with each group for 3 days before being used for electrophysiological experiments. Data were collected using an EPC-10 amplifier (HEKA, Germany).

Resting membrane potential (RMP): RMP was recorded in current clamp mode (I = 0) after attaining whole-cell mode. A step command from −70 to 0 pA with a duration of 500 ms was applied, allowing the recording of voltage changes in response to the current. Input resistance was calculated using the formula:Resistance=Voltage/Inputcurrent

To detect inward calcium currents, CsCl was added to the internal electrode solution. TTX (1 μM) and 4-AP (1 mM) were sequentially added to the perfusion solution to block Na+ and K+ currents. Cells were voltage clamped at −70 mV, and a step command ranging from −90 to +10 mV with a duration of 500 ms was applied. After stabilizing the whole-cell configuration for 5 min, ion channel currents at different voltage conditions were recorded (Patchmaster 2x92 software).

Cell migration ability – wound healing assay

The Ti64, Ti64Ta-L, Ti64Ta-H, 400 μm strip, 200 μm strip metal plates are placed in 24-well plates. The samples were sterilized under 121 °C 20min.Subsequently, BMSCs with passage 4 generation cells were cultured on the surface of Ti64, Ti64Ta-L, Ti64Ta-H, 400 μm strip, 200 μm strip sample sheet to form a confluent monolayer within 48 h. Once confluency is reached, a uniform scratch is created in the monolayer using a sterile pipette tip dragged across the surface.

Cell migration on the plates was measured with cell tracker green CMFDA (Yeasen, China). Briefly, after removing the medium, 2 μL CMFDA was added to each sample and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. After washing with PBS, the cells were observed by a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Mitochondrial morphology imaging

BMSCs seeded on the ibidi 8-well μ-Dish with different samples for 48 h were used to measure Mitochondrial morphology. Cells were stained with PK Mito Orange (Genvivo Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, after removing the medium, Serum-free medium containing PKMO dye at a concentration of 0.1% was added to each sample and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. To remove unbound dye, cells were washed with alpha-MEM complete medium. Subsequently, they were replenished with fresh, pre-warmed alpha-MEM complete medium and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Finally, the cells were imaged via Stimulated Emission Depletion (STED) microscopy (Abberior STED microscope, Germany).

In vivo experiments

In vivo modeling

Seventy Sprague-Dawley rats (SD, male, 5 weeks old, mean weight = 250 ± 30 g) were randomized into six groups: Ti64ELI, Ti64Ta-L, Ti64Ta-H, 400 μm stripe, 200 μm stripe and Blank control group (6 animals per group).

All experimental rats were then given two implants in cranial bone under aseptic conditions. Briefly, SD rats were intraperitoneally injected with 3% pentobarbital at a dose of 3.5 mg/100g. After the anesthesia took effect, the cranial area was shaved, disinfected, draped, and the rat was placed in a prone position. A 3 cm longitudinal incision was made on the rat's skull, cutting through the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and muscle-periosteal layer, followed by flap reflection. Blunt dissection was performed to expose the skull plate. Using a high-speed drill with a 5 mm outer diameter bone-cutting burr, two circular full-thickness periosteal bone defects, each 5 mm in diameter, were created on both sides of the cranial midline suture.

At 12 weeks post-operation, the animals were sacrificed by intravenous overdose of pentobarbital and cranial bones from the defect sites of all animals were harvested along with the periosteum and preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde. Then, the cranial bone with for the following procedures; First, A cylinder with a diameter of 200 μm and a length of 15 mm, located near the growth femoral growth plate, was defined as the volume of interest for micro-CT (VENUS VNC-102S, China) analysis. 3D image reconstruction was performed and morphometric parameters including bone volume/total volume, trabecular number, and trabecular separation were systematically evaluated.

Histological and bone-implant contact evaluation

Immobilization of implanted femurs in 4% phosphate formalin for 48 h and dehydration in a series of graded ethanol (70–100%, v/v) d. The specimens were embedded in MMA and then sectioned perpendicular to the long axis of the implants at a thickness of 150 μm using a Leica SP1600 microtome (Leica, Germany). They were polished to a thickness of 20 μm. Van Gieson's staining was used to reveal the mineralized bone tissue, which appeared as red. At the same time, histological analysis was performed using a light microscope.

Ethics statement

The protocol for animal experiments was approved by the animal ethics committee of Shenzhen Huateng biomedical Technology Co., Ltd (NO B202412-15).

Statistical analysis

Values were presented as mean ± standard deviation and were calculated using GraphPad Prism9.4 (San Diego, CA). All experiments were repeated at least three times. Tests between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test, and multiple comparisons were performed using nested one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post-hoc analysis. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A difference was considered statistically significant at ∗p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Establishing Damascus-patterns through in-situ concentration modulation

The formation of Damascus patterns typically requires the stacking and processing of two or more steels with distinct chemical compositions, thereby enabling the emergence of intricate patterns after subsequent polishing and acid etching. In conventional fabrication, such an effect can be readily achieved by selecting raw materials with different compositions. However, introducing such compositional variation is generally difficult in L-PBF processes. In this study, we demonstrate that in situ concentration modulation can be achieved by precisely controlling the melting and mixing behavior of Ta within a Ti64ELI matrix. Based on the fundamental printability of the material, we conducted a high-throughput parameter screening (see Supplementary Information, L-PBF parameter optimization) and identified two parameter sets, denoted Ti64Ta-L and Ti64Ta-H, for producing samples with programmable patterns. Fig. 1A illustrates the basic printing process. Within a single fabricating process, the volume was spatially partitioned, and adjacent regions were fabricated by alternately applying these two parameter sets, thereby generating Damascus patterns with different average matrix compositions. After etching, highly controllable Damascus patterns were observed on both polished surfaces and a spinal fusion cage demonstrator (Fig. S3). SEM observations showed that the Ti64Ta-L sample (Fig. 1B–ii) exhibited a lava-like microstructure, resulting from the solidification of mixed powders before the melt pool became fully homogenized during printing. At higher magnification (Fig. 1B–iv), Ta-rich regions were observed adjacent to partially melted Ta particles. These brighter regions contained cellular sub-grains identified as the β phase by SAED analysis (Fig. 1B–a). In contrast, the darker regions exhibited a distinct lath-like α-Ti structure. TEM analysis of the α-lath region (Fig. 1B–vi), further confirmed by SAED, revealed α phase interspersed with α″ martensitic twins, as shown in Fig. 1B–b and c. As summarized in Fig. S4E, EDS mapping of this FIB-lifted TEM sample indicated that Ta was concentrated in the pure β regions, with a mass fraction of approximately 52.8 wt%, whereas the darker, Ta-depleted α regions contained only about 10.8 wt% Ta. In comparison, SEM images of Ti64Ta-H (Fig. 1B, iii, v) showed only slight contrast variations; although unmelted Ta particles were still detectable, no typical α-phase morphology was observed (Fig. 1B–vii). The SAED diffraction pattern Fig. 1B, d indicated that this sample consisted of a single β phase. Fig. 1C presents the XRD patterns of Ti64Ta-L, Ti64Ta-H, and as-printed Ti64ELI. As-printed Ti64ELI primarily exhibits α′-Ti peaks. The Ti64Ta-H sample exhibits a single β phase. A lower-intensity peak appears on the left side of the main β peak, likely corresponding to the BCC diffraction signal of unmelted Ta particles. In contrast, the XRD pattern of Ti64Ta-L indicates that the sample produced using the Ti64Ta-L parameter set possesses a mixed microstructure composed of α′, α″, and β phases. Previous studies have shown that tantalum acts as a weak β-phase stabilizer in titanium alloys, and that increasing Ta addition drives the alloy from the α phase toward a fully β-phase structure [23,29]. The XRD results further confirm that Ta is distributed highly heterogeneously in Ti64Ta-L, with regions of Ta depletion and enrichment, leading to a multiphase α′/α″ + β structure.

The addition of Ta significantly reduces the elastic modulus of the resulting alloy. The mechanical properties of Ti64Ta-L, Ti64Ta-H, and Ti64ELI were compared by tensile testing, as shown in Fig. S5A. The elastic moduli of Ti64Ta-L and Ti64Ta-H decreased to 61.70 GPa and 79.80 GPa, respectively (Table S3). For biomedical titanium alloys, lower elastic moduli are advantageous because they allow more suitable effective stiffness to be achieved through structural design, thereby mitigating stress shielding in bone implants. Moreover, Ti64Ta-H exhibited a high strength of 1199.50 MPa, together with a total elongation at fracture of 8.76%. The strength of Ti64Ta-L was nearly identical to that of Ti64ELI, whereas its elongation at fracture was markedly higher than that of as-printed Ti64ELI, reaching 16.00%. In addition to their promising strength, the key mechanical indicators of the Ti64Ta alloys developed in this study, namely strength and modulus, were compared with those of other titanium alloys used for biomedical implants, as shown in Fig. S5B. Their combination of high strength and low modulus is superior to that of currently reported biomedical titanium alloys. The high strength-to-modulus ratios of both alloys further highlight their mechanical advantage (Fig. S5C), providing a solid foundation for practical application.

Construction of P-SPD based on programmed Damascus-patterns

As schematically illustrated in Fig. 2A, samples for P-SPD characterization and in vitro tests were fabricated with two pattern widths, 200 μm and 400 μm, denoted as 200 μm Stripe and 400 μm Stripe, respectively. To ensure the spatial regularity of the subsequently generated P-SPD distribution, stripe patterns were selected for the following study. The rationale for selecting the stripe widths is provided in the Supplementary Information, Selection of stripe widths. In general, as-polished titanium alloy surfaces exhibit poor wettability, as indicated by a relatively large contact angle, which is unfavorable for cell adhesion and proliferation [30]. Therefore, a simple surface treatment was applied to the as-polished samples to generate an oxide layer. XPS revealed no significant differences in the oxidation states of Ti and Ta on the oxidized surfaces (Fig. 2B and C), although Ti64Ta-L and Ti64Ta-H stripes exhibited a pronounced contrast in secondary-electron SEM images after surface oxidation (Fig. 2D). Ti was present in the +4 oxidation state, whereas Ta was detected in the Ta5+ and Ta0 states, with no intermediate oxidation states (e.g., Ta2+) observed [31]. Notably, no significant V signal and only a weak Al signal were detected in the oxide films formed on the surfaces printed using the two parameter sets (Fig. S6) [32]. Given the potential adverse effects of Al and V in biomedical applications, this result is favorable for the long-term stability of metallic implants. XPS spectral analysis was further used to quantify the compositions of the oxide films on the two surfaces. The Ta content in the oxide film of Ti64Ta-H was lower than that in Ti64Ta-L, being 22% and 33%, respectively (Fig. 2D). To further cross-validate the accuracy of the compositional data obtained by XPS, the surface oxide layer was characterized by TEM-EDS using focused ion beam (FIB) sample preparation (Fig. S7). The resulting data were in excellent agreement with the XPS measurements. Moreover, the intentionally introduced oxide layers significantly reduced the contact angle relative to the bare alloy surfaces. The as-polished surfaces exhibited slight hydrophobicity (Fig. 2E), with contact angles above 90° for all tested samples. In contrast, the contact angles of all three oxidized surfaces decreased markedly, with Ti64Ta-H showing the greatest reduction to 45.0°, indicative of excellent hydrophilicity. The contact angle of oxidized Ti64ELI was 77.0°, which remained higher than that of Ti64Ta-L (65.7°). This difference is consistent with the distinct oxide compositions of Ti64Ta-L and Ti64Ta-H after oxidation. This observation is consistent with previous studies showing that a higher Ta2O5 content in the surface coating leads to a larger contact angle [33].

The polarization curves of Ti64ELI, Ti64Ta-L, and Ti64Ta-H are shown in Fig. 2F. Compared with Ti64ELI, both Ta-containing titanium alloys exhibited more positive corrosion potentials and higher corrosion currents. The corrosion potentials and corrosion currents of all three materials were measured in triplicate, and the detailed results are shown in Fig. S8 and Table S4. The observed differences in corrosion behavior suggest that there may be slight differences in oxide film growth between the different regions. To examine whether this affected surface topography, we measured the surface roughness before and after oxidation, and the results are presented in Fig. S8 and Table S5. The results show that oxidation increased the surface roughness of all samples, including the control Ti64ELI alloy, but the magnitude of this increase remained small in all cases. As shown in Table S5, only slight changes in the arithmetic mean roughness (Sa) were observed after oxidation. However, the LSCM images in Fig. S9 reveal minor topographical differences associated with the different corrosion behavior of the stripes. Specifically, a slight height offset formed between adjacent stripes, and a small protrusion developed at the center of each band; this feature was more pronounced in the 400 μm stripe sample than in the 200 μm stripe sample. Nevertheless, these topographical features were only on the order of ∼1 μm in height and did not significantly affect the overall roughness values. From the curves, it can be seen that among the three materials, Ti64Ta-H entered the passivation regime at the lowest potential and remained stable throughout the tested range, with no evidence of pitting. In contrast, the passivation potential of Ti64Ta-L was comparable to that of Ti64ELI, although its pitting potential was lower. For Ti64ELI, once pitting occurred, the continuously increasing current indicated sustained localized corrosion. By comparison, Ti64Ta-L was able to suppress the continued increase in corrosion current through re-passivation, thereby mitigating further pit propagation. Overall, these results indicate that the oxidized Ti–Ta surfaces designed in this study possess favorable corrosion resistance under moderately corrosive conditions.

Having established the surface chemistry and wettability of the oxidized stripes, we next examined whether a stable SPD could be generated between adjacent regions. The SPD between adjacent Ti64Ta-L and Ti64Ta-H stripes was measured by KPFM, and the measurement location is indicated in the far-right circular schematic of the oxidized surface in Fig. 2A. The corresponding results are shown in Fig. 3A–C. An SPD of approximately 48.01 mV was observed between the two stripes, with a gradual transition rather than an abrupt step-like change. This behavior is attributed to interdiffusion between adjacent stripes during fabrication, as well as to the compositional transition developed during surface oxide formation. To further quantify the spatially distributed electric field generated by the patterned surface, additional KPFM measurements were performed over the maximum accessible lateral scan area (30 μm × 30 μm) across the Ti64Ta-L/Ti64Ta-H interface. The corresponding 30 μm × 30 μm KPFM results are further presented in Fig. S10. By averaging the surface potential values along the Y-axis, a smooth lateral potential profile was obtained, which better reflects the practical gradient across the patterned surface. The measured surface potential ranged from 108.62 mV to 38.13 mV, corresponding to a potential difference of 70.49 mV over 30 μm and an estimated electric field strength of 2.35 × 103 mV/mm (or 2.35 V/m). This field strength exceeds the typical range of endogenous electric fields reported at wound sites and falls within the biologically active ranges used in external stimulation studies to promote osteogenesis, directed migration, and neural responses. A comparison of previously reported cell-stimulatory devices and externally applied electric field strengths is summarized in Table 1. Together, these results demonstrate that the patterned surface generates not only a measurable SPD but also a substantial and spatially distributed electric field with the potential to regulate cellular behavior. Experimental and theoretical study of the controllability of P-SPD

To verify the tunability of the SPD, two additional parameter-set pairs, designated Para 01/02 and Para 03/04, were selected, and the P-SPD experiments were repeated. KPFM was employed to characterize the resulting SPD (Fig. S11). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed for cross-validation (see Supplementary Information, First-Principles Calculation) Using the FIB lift-out technique, cross-sectional samples of the surface oxide layer were extracted and characterized by TEM to determine the structural states of Ti and Ta; these results were then used to construct the initial models for the DFT calculations. In addition, the surface-averaged compositions obtained from XPS, which reflect the relative abundances of the phases present, were incorporated into the modeling. Fig. 3E presents the SPD calculation models based on compositions derived from other candidate parameter combinations. The experimentally measured SPDs are compared with the DFT-calculated values in Fig. 3F.

The relationship between SPD and the Ti–Ta ratio in the oxide film indicates that the SPD decreases as the compositional disparity diminishes. For example, in the Ti64Ta-L/H group, where the Ta contents in the oxide films were 33.03% and 21.89%, respectively, the maximum SPD (∼48 mV) was observed; by contrast, in the Para03/04 group, where the Ta contents were nearly identical (16.64% and 17.30%), only a weak SPD of a few millivolts was detected. These findings suggest that the surface SPD can be tuned by adjusting the alloy matrix composition to modulate the Ti/Ta ratio in the oxide film, thereby exploiting the inherent work-function difference between Ti and Ta oxides.

The microstructure of the oxide layer was characterized in a relatively homogeneous region of the Ti64Ta-H sample to establish the DFT model. STEM combined with EDS analysis of the FIB lift-out sample clearly revealed a Pt protective layer on the surface, an oxide layer approximately 50 nm thick, and the underlying metallic matrix (Fig. 3D–a), with Ti and Ta signals uniformly distributed throughout the oxide layer. HRTEM showed that the oxide layer consisted of crystalline regions together with some amorphous domains (Fig. 3D–b). Given that Ta2O5 can exist in an amorphous state at room temperature [52], the observed mixed amorphous–crystalline microstructure is reasonable. FFT analysis indicated that part of the crystalline phase corresponded to stable rutile TiO2 [53,54], whereas another part was attributable to the thermodynamically stable ε-Ta2O5 phase [55,56], as annotated in the iFFT images in Fig. 3D–c, d. Because the amorphous phase could not be explicitly incorporated into the DFT model, the system was approximated using a randomly arranged ordered structure composed of titanium and tantalum oxides. This simplification does not fully capture the influence of the amorphous phase on the SPD, which likely contributes to the discrepancy between the calculated and measured values. Nonetheless, as shown in Fig. 3F, the calculated SPD exhibits a trend and magnitude consistent with the experimental results, thereby supporting the validity of the experimental findings.

We compared the potential differences generated between adjacent stripes with the magnitudes of artificial electrical cues previously used to promote tissue repair, including bone and skin regeneration. The potential difference achieved in this work (∼48 mV) lies within the same order of magnitude as those reported in earlier studies (0–100 mV) [10,34,35,37,[39], [40], [41]]. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 3G, these values were further compared with endogenous bioelectric signal amplitudes measured in vivo during processes such as cell migration and tissue regeneration [36,38]. Importantly, the tunable potential difference generated between adjacent stripes falls within the range of naturally occurring bioelectric cues, highlighting its potential relevance for promoting tissue repair.

After 24 h of incubation, BMSCs on Ti64ELI and Ti64Ta-H exhibited limited lamellipodia and were predominantly elongated in a spindle-like morphology, indicating retention of their initial morphology without marked differentiation. In contrast, BMSCs on the other Ti64Ta samples adopted a polygonal morphology with abundant lamellipodia extending along the surface. Previous studies have shown that lamellipodia and other large cellular protrusions are driven by dynamic contractile mechanisms and play crucial roles in cell adhesion, polarization, and spreading [57]. Furthermore, cells have been reported to respond to stimuli arising from heterogeneous surface potential regions by actively sensing and adapting to the altered microenvironment. Such continuous morphological adjustment positively influences the functional differentiation of stem cells [58]. Among the different samples, Ti64Ta-L exhibited substantial local compositional variation due to uneven material mixing caused by the lower printing power, resulting in localized potential differences that promoted cell stretching and adhesion. By contrast, Ti64Ta-H, which consisted of a relatively uniform β phase, displayed cell behavior similar to that observed on Ti64ELI surfaces, further supporting the role of surface potential gradients. Higher-magnification SEM images provided additional insight into how the Damascus patterns affected cell morphology. The localized view of cells by Fig. 4C revealed that, although the cells exhibited similar spreading morphologies on metallic surfaces with and without the designed Damascus-patterned structure, their spatial distributions were markedly different. On the stripe-free Ti64Ta-L surface, the cells were randomly distributed, whereas on the Damascus-patterned surface they showed a clear tendency to spread across adjacent stripe interfaces. In our previous analysis, we found that oxidation generated slight protrusions at the stripe boundaries; however, these subtle topographical features did not induce the typical focal-adhesion-guided growth of cells along the groove direction [59,60]. Consistently, SEM images showed that cells spanning neighboring stripes did not display the characteristic alignment parallel to the stripe direction [28,61,62]. Instead, they preferentially oriented perpendicular to the stripes, or along the direction of the surface potential gradient, which is consistent with previous reports and our earlier findings [11,63,64]. These observations suggest that the slight and sparsely distributed topographical variations caused by differential corrosion are insufficient to govern cell adhesion and alignment in our system. Rather, the preferential cell arrangement across the stripes is more likely driven by the interfacial electrical cue established by the P-SPD.

Based on these observations, we hypothesize that both the heterogeneous mixing of Ti64ELI and Ta and the intentional design of Damascus patterns influence cell adhesion and differentiation across multiple length scales. To further estimate the spatial range of the P-SPD effect, we analyzed larger-area SEM images and attempted to quantify its zone of influence (see Supplementary Information, Galvanotaxis Analysis). The analysis suggests a characteristic influence range of approximately 240 μm. In this context, the selected stripe widths of 200 μm and 400 μm, lying below and above this characteristic length scale, respectively, provide a useful framework for distinguishing the biological effects associated with P-SPD. At the microscale, compositional variations within individual stripes may enhance cell adhesion and induce cytoskeletal remodeling, whereas at the submillimeter scale, the periodic potential differences generated by the stripes may facilitate directional cell stretching and spreading.

Cell proliferation assays were conducted to evaluate the fundamental cytocompatibility of the Ti64Ta alloys. Fig. 4D presents the results of CCK-8 assays performed on days 1, 3, and 5 after BMSCs were seeded onto the surfaces of Ti64, Ti64Ta-L, Ti64Ta-H, 200 μm stripe, and 400 μm stripe samples. The proliferation rate of BMSCs on the two non-striped Ti64Ta samples was slightly lower than that on Ti64. In contrast, the proliferation rates of cells cultured on the experimental stripe-patterned surfaces were significantly higher than those in all four control groups. Furthermore, within the experimental groups, the cell proliferation rate on the 200 μm stripe sample was slightly higher than that on the 400 μm stripe sample. These results indicate that although all tested samples exhibited good cytocompatibility, the stripe-patterned samples, particularly the 200 μm stripe, significantly promoted cell proliferation.

Because the metallic substrates used in this experiment are opaque, visible-light imaging was employed instead of fluorescence confocal microscopy. This approach enabled simultaneous observation of the surface characteristics of the metallic substrates and their influence on cellular osteogenic differentiation, thereby facilitating a more comprehensive comparison of the experimental results. The upper row of Fig. 4E shows the early osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs in vitro, as assessed by ALP activity. The stronger ALP staining observed on the 200 μm and 400 μm stripe samples suggests enhanced osteogenic differentiation compared with the weaker staining observed in the control groups (CON, Ti64, Ti64Ta-L, and Ti64Ta-H). The lower row shows the staining results of mineralized calcium nodules in BMSCs cultured on the sample surfaces after 7 days of osteogenic induction. The results indicate more intense staining and evident calcium nodule formation on the two stripe-patterned samples, whereas no significant calcium nodules were observed on the CON, Ti64, Ti64Ta-L, or Ti64Ta-H surfaces. These results suggest that the P-SPD generated by the spaced stripes promotes osteogenic differentiation.

The mRNA expression levels of the osteogenesis-related genes ALP, COL-I, RUNX-2, and OPN were compared, as shown in Fig. 4F. The results indicate that the relative mRNA expression levels of these genes were significantly upregulated on the experimental Damascus-patterned surfaces. Notably, the 200 μm Stripe exhibited the most pronounced enhancement, with the expression levels of these mRNAs reaching approximately twice those of the control group.

The expression of osteogenesis-related proteins was analyzed by Western blot (WB), and the results are presented in Fig. 4G. The quantified data for the WB bands are shown in Fig. S12. Histogram analysis indicates that protein expression levels were markedly upregulated in the two experimental groups with Damascus patterns. However, in contrast to the mRNA analysis, in which the 200 μm stripe group consistently showed higher expression levels than the 400 μm stripe group, the protein expression profiles were more variable. Interestingly, although the Ti64Ta-L group exhibited lower protein expression levels than the experimental groups with periodic stripes, its expression remained markedly elevated relative to the other control groups. In contrast, the osteogenic protein expression levels in the Ti64ELI and Ti64Ta-H samples were comparable to, or even lower than, those in the negative control group. Taken together, these findings suggest that periodic bands formed through in situ compositional regulation significantly enhance the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs at the cellular level.

The metallic implants were designed according to metamaterial principles and incorporated a triply periodic minimal surface (diamond-TPMS) architecture inspired by the diamond-like arrangement observed in starfish. This design not only further reduces the relative density and effective modulus compared with conventional dense alloys, but also provides excellent damage tolerance [65].

In the experimental group, the implanted metallic blocks featured volumetric Damascus patterns arranged radially to form concentric structures with stripe widths of 400 μm and 200 μm. The control groups consisted of three single-parameter samples: Ti64ELI without Ta addition, and Ti64Ta-L and Ti64Ta-H alloys containing 25 wt% Ta. Each rat received two metallic implants at the cranial defect sites, with the experimental and control groups distributed in a balanced manner. This preliminary observation suggests excellent osseointegration of the implanted materials.

We pharmacologically isolated calcium currents by blocking Na+ channels with tetrodotoxin (TTX) and K+ channels with CsCl and 4-aminopyridine (4-AP) [68]. Voltage-clamp recordings showed that, as the test potential was stepped from −90 mV to +10 mV, the amplitude of the Ca2+ currents progressively increased (Fig. 6E). At +10 mV, comparison of the current amplitudes between the control and 200 μm stripe groups revealed significantly enhanced Ca2+ channel activation under P-SPD conditions. This increase in channel activity may elevate intracellular Ca2+ levels, thereby contributing to membrane depolarization and the promotion of cellular differentiation. To investigate the duration over which P-SPD influences cellular depolarization, we stained the cells with the membrane potential-sensitive probe DiBAC4(3) and examined them at different time points after adhesion (24 h, 3 days, 14 days, and 21 days). The corresponding results are provided in Supplementary Information, Time-Dependent Membrane Depolarization Induced by P-SPD. We found that cells influenced by P-SPD gradually recovered from the depolarized state and returned to a near-baseline membrane potential by approximately day 21. We consider this trend to reflect an early-stage depolarizing effect induced by P-SPD, followed by gradual electrophysiological adaptation and partial restoration of membrane potential homeostasis. This interpretation is consistent with the past studies showing that early membrane depolarization can modulate MSC signaling, whereas osteogenic progression is generally associated with a subsequent shift toward a more hyperpolarized state [[69], [70], [71], [72]].

Mitochondrial activity has been shown to be strongly correlated with BMSC proliferation and osteogenic differentiation, and thus plays a pivotal role in bone defect healing [[73], [74], [75]]. Stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy was employed to observe mitochondrial morphology in cells adhering to different sample surfaces. Typically, undifferentiated BMSCs remain in a relatively quiescent state and require neither a large number of mitochondria nor highly developed mitochondrial networks. As shown in Fig. 6F for the negative control group and in Fig. S16 for the Ti64ELI and Ti64Ta-H groups, the mitochondria predominantly exhibited a disrupted state characterized by fragmented and swollen morphology, suggesting low osteogenic differentiation potential and relatively inactive cellular states. This observation is consistent with the relatively low expression of osteogenic mRNA markers, further supporting the limited differentiation potential of these samples. We further evaluated the effect of P-SPD on mitochondrial activity by performing immunofluorescence (IF) staining to examine the distribution and expression level of the ATP synthase β subunit, together with JC-1 staining to assess the mitochondrial membrane potential (Supplementary Information, Mitochondrial Activity Analysis). By combining morphological observations with quantitative fluorescence analysis, we established the following ranking of mitochondrial activity in cells on the different samples: 200 μm Stripe >400 μm Stripe > Ti64Ta-L ≈ Ti64Ta-H > control > Ti64ELI. These results support the promoting effect of P-SPD on cellular mitochondrial activity.

During osteogenic differentiation, intracellular protein synthesis increases markedly, thereby requiring enhanced energy supply and metabolic activity. At this stage, immature, fragmented, and spherical mitochondria, predominantly localized around the nucleus, become insufficient to meet the metabolic demands of differentiating BMSCs. Consequently, mitochondria undergo extensive redistribution throughout the cell, thereby supporting efficient oxidative metabolism and providing sufficient energy for differentiation [76]. In the Ti64Ta-L sample and the samples with 400 μm and 200 μm stripe patterns, mitochondrial activity appeared markedly enhanced, with morphology transitioning from fragmented spherical structures to elongated and interconnected networks. Compared with the pattern-free Ti64Ta-L sample, the Damascus-patterned samples, particularly the 200 μm stripe sample, exhibited more active mitochondrial behavior. This suggests that a more developed mitochondrial network may not only provide the energy required for osteogenic differentiation, but also support the additional energy demand associated with cell migration. Furthermore, thinner Damascus patterns, corresponding to more frequent potential alternation, may be associated with higher energy demands, further supporting their potential role in enhancing cell migration capacity.

To further investigate the effect of P-SPD on cell migration, we performed a scratch wound-healing assay using Ti64ELI, pattern-free Ti64Ta, and samples with 400 μm and 200 μm stripe patterns. As shown in Fig. 6G, after 24 h of incubation, cells on the pattern-free samples exhibited minimal migratory capacity, with no obvious displacement of the wound boundary. In contrast, both Damascus-patterned samples displayed appreciable cell migration. Notably, compared with the 400 μm stripe sample, the 200 μm stripe sample showed a greater number of cells within the scratch area, and the original wound boundary became less distinct, indicating enhanced cell migration. Comparison of the two stripe widths suggests that the potential differences between adjacent stripes may facilitate directional cell stretching and thereby promote migration; moreover, more frequent potential alternation appears to enhance this effect.

Here, we propose that the engineered P-SPD plays a pivotal role in both osteogenic differentiation and cellular energy metabolism. Patch-clamp measurements revealed that the Ti64Ta-L and Ti64Ta-H samples exhibited a modest reduction in RMP compared with Ti64ELI, indicating a limited but positive effect of Ta on Ca2+ channel activation and osteogenesis [[77], [78], [79]]. The presence of P-SPD further reduced the RMP and markedly enhanced Ca2+ channel activation, accompanied by upregulation of the Wnt signaling pathway, as evidenced by increased Wnt3a and p-GSK-3β expression, thereby promoting BMSC differentiation (Fig. 6H). We further supported this conclusion from another direction by introducing the calcium channel inhibitor verapamil. After calcium channel blockade, both the Wnt pathway and p-GSK-3β were suppressed, and the promotive effects of the 400 μm and 200 μm stripe groups were partially attenuated (Fig. S17). Moreover, BMSCs cultured on both long-range P-SPD surfaces displayed pronounced mitochondrial morphological and functional changes, suggesting enhanced mitochondrial ATP production to meet the demands of proliferation and differentiation. Wound-healing assays showed the strongest migration in the 200 μm stripe group, consistent with elevated mitochondrial activity, while shorter pattern periods appeared to produce a greater enhancement of migration. For porous scaffolds, such enhanced migration may facilitate deeper cell infiltration. Fig. 6I schematically illustrates the proposed mechanism of P-SPD in this study. As reported in previous studies, the Wnt–GSK-3β signaling pathway plays a major role in mediating the enhancement of osteogenic activity by endogenous electric fields; however, other potential mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

Conclusion

In this work, we re-examined mixed-powder laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) and, for the first time, reported an in situ compositional modulation strategy for the surface functionalization of metallic materials. Using Ti–6Al–4V–Ta alloys with distinct matrix compositions and microstructures, which exhibit a low elastic modulus and a high strength-to-modulus ratio, we achieved direct surface functionalization of bone implants through in situ compositional modulation. After a simple surface treatment, this approach yields bioactive titanium alloy implants with excellent hydrophilicity, corrosion resistance, and a programmable surface potential difference (P-SPD), while retaining these functionalities after subsequent mechanical processing. Mechanistically, P-SPD appears to enhance osteogenesis through dual effects on Ca2+-channel-mediated Wnt signaling and mitochondrial function, thereby promoting both BMSC differentiation and cell migration into implant pores. Building on this mechanism, we engineered metamaterial implants with tunable mechanical properties and demonstrated significant in vivo bone regeneration in a rat cranial defect model under P-SPD conditions. The rat cranial defect model provides a stable, non-load-bearing orthotopic platform in which new bone formation primarily reflects the intrinsic bioactivity of the implant surface, owing to the minimal mechanical loading and high reproducibility of the model [[80], [81], [82]]. Robust osseointegration was observed even in the absence of mechanical stimulation. Nevertheless, because mechanical loading is a critical determinant of bone healing [83], future studies should extend these findings to load-bearing models, such as critical-sized segmental defects or spinal fusion, to verify mechanical stability and osseointegration under physiological conditions. More broadly, this strategy may be extended to a wider range of material systems, including biodegradable metals for gradient implants with programmable degradation behavior.
