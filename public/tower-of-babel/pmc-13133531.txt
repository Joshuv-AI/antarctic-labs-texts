Oxygen saturation and mortality for acute pulmonary embolism: multinational retrospective cohort study
Authors: David Jiménez, Roger D Yusen, Juan Manuel Velasco, Raquel Morillo, Alfonso Muriel, Luis Jara-Palomares, Laurent Bertoletti, Luis Manuel Hernández-Blasco, Aída Gil-Díaz, Farès Moustafa, Ángeles Fidalgo, Parham Sadeghipour, Khanh Quoc Pham, Behnood Bikdeli, Manuel Monreal, the RIETE Investigators

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAmong patients with acute pulmonary embolism (PE), we aimed to evaluate for an association between oxygen saturation (SatO2) and 30-day post-PE mortality.MethodsBetween January, 2001 and April, 2025, we conducted a retrospective cohort study of 38,739 non-hypotensive patients with acute PE enrolled from 18 countries in the Registro Informatizado de la Enfermedad Tromboembólica (RIETE) registry. Outcomes included 30-day all-cause and PE-specific mortality. We used hierarchical logistic regression to assess for an association between baseline SatO2 and outcomes. We aimed to identify optimal SatO2 cut-off points that maximized sensitivity and specificity in relation to mortality from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. RIETE is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT02832245.FindingsBaseline SatO2 was significantly associated with mortality, where lower SatO2 had worse outcomes. A SatO2 cutoff value of 96% for the highest SatO2 group while breathing room air might help better identify patients with acute PE at low-risk for mortality. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Arterial oxygen saturation (SatO2) by pulse oximetry has an association with prognosis in acute pulmonary embolism (PE) patients. We searched PubMed from database inception to January 01, 2026, for articles using the search terms “hypoxemia” AND “acute pulmonary embolism”, identifying 633 articles. Of these, most of the studies were case reports or physiopathological reviews. The largest prospective study that evaluated oxygen saturation as a risk factor for short-term mortality in stable PE included 946 patients, and the study found that a SatO2 <88% predict the risk of death. As a result of this study, we evaluated in the Registro Informatizado de la Enfermedad Tromboembólica (RIETE) population the presence of hypoxemia by SatO2, and determined the hypoxemia-associated risk of short-term mortality in this population.

Added value of this study

We used the RIETE registry, a multicentre and multinational cohort that included 38,739 non-hypotensive patients with acute PE and oxygen saturation at baseline, to show an inverse association between SatO2 at presentation and odds of all-cause and PE-associated mortality over the subsequent 30 days, independent of age, sex, comorbidities (cancer, immobilization, chronic lung disease, and chronic heart disease), and vital signs (systolic blood pressure and heart rate). We found that mild decreases in SatO2 were associated with increased risk for PE-related death. We also showed that a revised SatO2 cutoff value (96%) might provide better survival prognostication than the standard cutoff (90%) for identifying low-risk PE.

Implications of all the available evidence

Future studies should assess the contribution of SatO2 in addition to established prognosticators to predicting the risk of death. Also, our results have implications for the design of clinical trials, supporting further evaluation of supplemental oxygen therapy in non-hypoxemic patients (e.g., SatO2 90–96%) with intermediate-risk acute symptomatic PE. Since a baseline SatO2 equal to or lower than 96% was associated with higher PE-related mortality, available risk stratification scores could benefit by modifying SatO2 cut-off points that can predict disease course. Although standard dichotomization of SatO2 (i.e., SatO2 <90% vs. ≥90%) may be useful for guideline recommendations, our findings will allow for more accuracy regarding risk classification and clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Hypoxemia commonly occurs in patients with acute symptomatic pulmonary embolism (PE),1,2 and several mechanisms explain its presence.3 Mismatching of ventilation and perfusion commonly causes impaired pulmonary oxygen transfer.4 PE causes redistribution of blood flow so that some lung gas exchange units have high ratios of ventilation to perfusion, whereas other lung units have low ratios. Shunting of venous blood through the lungs, without passing through ventilated gas exchange units of the lung, occurs less frequently and is often clinically inconsequential. Uncommonly, the failure of supplemental oxygen to correct arterial hypoxemia accompanying acute PE occurs, and this typically reflects the existence of right to left shunting of venous blood through the heart,5 the lungs, or both. PE may also directly decrease the oxygen diffusing capacity of the lung. When PE causes right ventricular failure, the associated low left ventricular cardiac output leads to increased extraction of oxygen in the tissues and a subsequent decrease in mixed venous oxygen. A low partial pressure of oxygen in venous blood may contribute to arterial hypoxemia.3

Arterial oxygen saturation (SatO2) by pulse oximetry, which is a simple and readily available vital sign, has an association with prognosis in acute PE patients.6 Studies have shown an association between SatO2 <90%, while breathing ambient air, and worse outcome after acute PE. Some clinical prognostic scores (e.g., Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index [PESI] or simplified PESI [sPESI], National Early Warning Score-2 [NEWS-2] score) include oxygen saturation as a prognostic parameter.7, 8, 9 However, previous studies have not determined if there is an association between SatO2 and outcomes across the continuum of oxygen saturation values. Furthermore, prior studies have not assessed if a SatO2 cutoff different from the typically used SatO2 <90% (representing hypoxemia), could more accurately capture the patients with acute symptomatic PE at low-risk.

We hypothesized that different degrees of subnormal SatO2 have prognostic significance for hemodynamically stable patients who present with acute PE. We sought to assess for an optimal SatO2 cutoff for risk stratification. To address these issues, we analyzed data from the Registro Informatizado de la Enfermedad TromboEmbólica (RIETE), a large ongoing, multicenter, multinational, prospective registry of consecutive patients with objectively confirmed, acute venous thromboembolism (VTE).

Methods

The study methods and results are reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.10

Study design

This retrospective cohort study used data from the international RIETE registry for patients enrolled from January 1, 2001 through April 29, 2025. The RIETE registry prospectively collects information on patients with confirmed acute VTE (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier, NCT02832245). Previous publications have described the design and conduct of the RIETE registry.11 In summary, investigators at each participating RIETE site enrolled eligible consecutive patients diagnosed with acute VTE. To ensure the reliability of coding and data entry, trained monitors periodically visited each participating hospital and compared the information in a random sample of hospital charts with the information entered into the RIETE database. RIETE also uses electronic data monitoring to prevent inconsistent or implausible values.

Ethics

The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital Germans Trias i Pujol (number PR (AG)213/2020). All participating centers obtained local ethics approval, and all patients provided informed consent as required by national regulations.

Patient selection

Patient eligibility for this study required presentation with acute symptomatic PE without hypotension (i.e., systolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg) and documentation of SatO2. Patients breathing room air and those receiving supplemental oxygen were enrolled in the study. Confirmatory testing for PE consisted of high probability ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) scintigraphy,12 contrast-enhanced, PE-protocol chest computerized tomography (CT) angiography [single or multi-detector CT] showing acute PE,13 or lower limb venous compression ultrasonography positive for proximal deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in a patient presenting with symptoms of PE.14

Data collection

Patients enrolled in RIETE had data from around the time of PE diagnosis that included but were not limited to: age; sex; body weight; vital signs including systolic blood pressure, heart rate and SatO2; presence of coexisting conditions such as chronic heart or lung disease; recent (<30 days prior to PE) major bleeding; presence of risk factors for PE including active cancer (defined as newly diagnosed cancer or cancer undergoing treatment [i.e. surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormonal, or support therapy]), recent immobility (defined as non-surgical patients assigned to bed rest with bathroom privileges for ≥4 days in the 2-months prior to PE diagnosis), surgery (defined as those who had undergone major surgery in the 2 months prior to PE); echocardiographic findings (if available); and laboratory results at hospital admission that included serum creatinine, hemoglobin, and troponin.

Follow-up and outcomes

The co-primary outcomes included 30-day all-cause and PE-specific mortality. Secondary outcomes included all-cause and PE-related mortality within 7 days following the diagnosis of PE. The RIETE database collects date and cause of death after PE. For patients who died, medical record review, proxy interviews when necessary, and review of autopsy reports if available, assisted with determination of the date and cause of death. Local investigators adjudicated death due to PE (fatal PE) if it was (1) confirmed by autopsy or (2) clinically suspected soon after the initial PE or shortly after an objectively confirmed recurrent event, in the absence of any alternative diagnosis.

Statistical analyses

SatO2 was determined by pulse oximetry or arterial blood gas analysis. For patients who had both determinations, we used pulse oximetry for analyses. The study defined hypoxemia as a SatO2 <90%. We a priori designed the study to categorize patients into quartiles based on admission SatO2 level: more than 96%, 94–96%, 90–93%, and less than 90%. Quartiles of patients were chosen to ensure a minimum and comparable number of patients in each quartile. We also designed the study to analyse SatO2 as a continuous variable. We used descriptive statistics to describe patient's baseline characteristics. For covariates that had missing data below 5%, we assumed that missing values were in the normal range. For a higher proportion of covariate missing data, the denominator only included those who had data reported.

We used Kaplan–Meier survival analysis to examine the difference in all-cause and PE-related mortality between patients categorized as with or without hypoxemia, and the difference between each group was assessed by the log-rank test. To assess the association between admission SatO2 and outcomes, we used hierarchical logistic regression (with hospital site as a random effect to account for clustering across centers) and adjusted for a set of potential confounders, including demographic variables such as age and sex; a list of pre-existing chronic conditions including cancer, immobilization, chronic lung disease, and chronic heart disease; and vital signs like systolic blood pressure and heart rate. We sequentially generated the following models to determine the successive influence of potential confounders on the relationship between admission SatO2 (categorical reference group, >96%) and mortality: (1) unadjusted; (2); adjusted only for age and sex; and (3) adjusted for age, sex, coexisting conditions (i.e., cancer, immobilization, chronic lung disease, chronic heart disease), and severity of PE (i.e., systolic blood pressure, heart rate) at hospital admission. Our study considered right ventricle (RV) dysfunction and myocardial injury (i.e., elevated troponin) as potential mediators, and were chosen for further examination in subgroup analysis.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed using SatO2 as continuous variable in logistic regression models to obtain accurate estimates of area under the curve (AUC) in relation to mortality. We identified optimal cut-off points that maximized sensitivity (for identification of low-risk patients) and specificity (for identification of intermediate-high risk patients) from ROC curves.

Analyses were repeated in predefined subgroups of sex (male or female), age (older [>80 years] or younger [≤80 years]), RV function (presence or absence of RV dysfunction), myocardial injury (presence or absence), and chronic cardiopulmonary disease (presence or absence). As a post-hoc analysis, we also analyzed observed data (complete case analysis) ignoring the missing data. Finally, we ran analyses in the subgroup of 11,223 patients who had a SatO2 value obtained while documented as breathing room air (i.e., not receiving supplemental oxygen).

Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value of <0.05 for all analyses. Analyses were performed using SPSS, version 29.0 for Mac (SPSS, Inc. Chicago, IL, USA).

Role of the funding source

The funder had no specific role in any of the following study aspects: the design and conduct of the study; collection, management, analysis, and interpretation of the data; preparation, review, or approval of the manuscript; and the decision to submit the manuscript for publication.

Results

Study sample

We identified 65,747 patients with objectively confirmed symptomatic PE enrolled in RIETE during the study period. The study excluded 25,673 patients without documentation of SatO2. In comparison to those included in the study, RV enlargement/dysfunction more frequently occurred among patients excluded from the study because of no documentation of SatO2 data. Otherwise, when comparing patients with (included) and those without (excluded) documentation of SatO2, we did not find a statistically significant difference (i.e., standardized difference [SRD] <10% in absolute value difference [16]) between included and excluded patients regarding demographics, medical history, and clinical presentation variable data (eTable 1). After excluding patients who had hypotension around the time of PE diagnosis, the final analysis included 38,739 patients (Fig. 1). The final cohort was divided into four approximately equally sized patient quartiles, with approximately 9000 patients in each quartile.

Patients in the lower SatO2 categories had higher age and a greater proportion with female sex, medical immobilization, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and congestive heart failure. Patients in the lower SatO2 strata had a more severe presentation with a higher proportion of patients having syncope, dyspnea, tachycardia, hypotension, central thrombus location, RV dysfunction, myocardial injury (elevated troponin), and elevated creatinine. Patients in the lower SatO2 groups had a lower proportion with concomitant DVT.

Regarding treatment, use of any kind of advanced therapy, such as systemic thrombolysis or catheter-directed thrombolysis/mechanical thrombectomy, was more frequent in patients in the lower SatO2 strata compared to patients in the highest SatO2 strata. Reperfusion was administered to 9.9% of patients with a systolic blood pressure <100 mm Hg, and to 3.1% of those with a systolic blood pressure ≥100 mm Hg. Inferior vena cava filter insertion did not significantly differ among the SatO2 groups (Table 1).

Outcomes

The study had complete survival information for all (100%) patients for the 30-day follow-up period; 1860 of the 38,739 (4.8%) died within 30-day of follow-up. Four hundred ninety-six patients (1.3% of the study cohort) died from PE (27% of deaths), and 1364 (3.5% of the study cohort; 73% of deaths) died from other causes (cancer 386, infection 120, hemorrhage 103, heart failure 78, unknown 142, other 535).

Among the group of patients with hypoxemia (i.e., SatO2 <90%), seven hundred and twenty-four (724 of 9142 patients; 7.9%; 95% CI, 7.4%–8.5%) died within 30-days of follow-up, whereas 1136 deaths (1136 of 29,597 patients; 3.8%; 95% CI, 3.6%–4.1%) occurred in the group of patients without hypoxemia (Fig. 2, log-rank P < 0.001; eTable 2). PE-related mortality rates were 2.7% and 0.9% in those with and those without hypoxemia, respectively (Fig. 2; log-rank P < 0.001). The difference in the PE-related mortality mostly occurred within the first 10 days of follow-up.

After adjustment for age, sex, coexisting conditions (i.e., cancer, immobilization, chronic lung disease, chronic heart disease), and vital signs (i.e., systolic blood pressure, heart rate), patients in the two lower SatO2 strata had significantly higher odds of 30-day all-cause death compared to patients with SatO2 >96% (reference category) (adjusted odds ratio [OR] 1.81; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.55–2.11 for SatO2 <90%; OR 1.23; 95% CI, 1.05–1.44 for SatO2 90–93%; and OR 0.96; 95% CI, 0.82–1.13 for SatO2 94–96%) (Table 2; model 3). A similar trend was observed for 30-day PE-related death. As compared with patients in the reference stratum of SatO2 >96%, patients with SatO2 <90%, patients with SatO2 90–93%, and patients with SatO2 94–96% had a 3.95-, 2.04, and 1.68-fold greater risk of 30-day PE-related death, respectively. Subgroup analyses

To explore the sensitivity of our findings, we repeated the analysis among a priori defined patient subgroups (Fig. 4 and eTables 4–9). Subgroup analyses according to sex, age, RV function, myocardial injury and chronic cardiopulmonary disease had findings similar to the main analyses. Subgroup trends were also similar to the overall cohort after categorizing the enrollment calendar period into quartiles (eTable 10).

C) Right ventricle dysfunction (presence or absence). D) Myocardial injury (presence or absence). E) Chronic cardiopulmonary disease (presence or absence).

When we analyzed observed data (complete case analysis) ignoring the missing data, results were similar to the main analyses (eTable 11). In the subgroup of 11,223 patients with SatO2 measured while breathing room air (eFigure 2), the analyses had 30-day all-cause and PE-related mortality analysis trends similar to those of the overall cohort (eTable 12 and eFigure 3). However, this subgroup had lower mortality in comparison to the overall cohort, especially for those at the lower end of the SatO2 distribution. Only the hypoxemic quartile showed statistically significantly worse odds of all-cause and PE-related survival in comparison to the other quartiles.

Discussion

In this large contemporary cohort of non-hypotensive patients diagnosed with acute symptomatic PE, we assessed the prevalence, clinical characteristics, and outcomes of hypoxemic and non-hypoxemic patients, and made the following observations: (1) hypoxemia defined as SatO2 <90% commonly occurs with acute symptomatic PE in normotensive patients, accounting for approximately one quarter of the PE population, and it is characterized by distinct demographic and clinical traits; (2) initial SatO2 level and 30-day all-cause and PE-related mortality have an inverse relationship, after use of various adjustment techniques; (3) mild decreases in SatO2 were associated with increased risk for PE-related death; and (4) a revised SatO2 cutoff value (96%) might provide better survival prognostication than the standard cutoff (90%) for identifying low-risk PE.

Previous studies have identified an association between SatO2 on hospital admission and prognosis of patients with acute PE.6,15, 16, 17 In a retrospective study of 65 patients with acute symptomatic PE, Erol and colleagues found that a SatO2 threshold of 91.5% was the optimal target saturation for excluding respiratory failure.15 Among 303 normotensive patients with PE treated initially with heparin anticoagulation, Kline and colleagues found that a pulse oximetry reading of 95% was the sole criterion to differentiate patients with PE into high-risk (SatO2 ≥95%) and low-risk (SatO2 <95%) groups.17 We found an inverse relationship between admission SatO2 level and 30-day all-cause and PE-related mortality throughout the full spectrum of SatO2. Our results expanded the findings of previous reports by showing that a baseline SatO2 equal to or lower than 96% was associated with higher PE-related mortality, in comparison to those with SatO2 greater than 96%. This study further validated that lower SatO2 (<90%) was associated with increased risk for mortality. While a SatO2 <90% was associated with increased risk for death, the positive predictive value for mortality was still low. We should, however, acknowledge that this pattern has been similarly observed for other individual prognosticators and even the existing conventional risk classification models. Oxygen saturation should be combined (i.e., scores) with other variables to identify this subgroup of patients.18

Our study's large sample size, adjustment for potential confounders (i.e., coexisting conditions, severity of PE, and laboratory results), robustness of the findings, and results of the sensitivity analyses provide evidence that supports consideration of the modification of the traditional SatO2 cutoff for prognostication of PE patients. Furthermore, future trials could evaluate if supplemental oxygen therapy influences mortality in non-hypoxemic patients (e.g., SatO2 90–96%) with intermediate-risk acute symptomatic PE. The Air vs Oxygen for Intermediate-Risk Pulmonary Embolism (AIR) trial provided proof-of-concept for use of non-traditional SatO2 cutoffs for supplemental oxygen use in patients with acute PE. The study evaluated the effect of oxygen therapy among non-hypoxemic patients with intermediate-risk acute symptomatic PE, and it showed improvement in some ancillary efficacy outcomes.19

In our study, patients with lower SatO2 had more signs of PE clinical severity than those with a high SatO2.20,21 Low oxygen saturation reflects an integrated noninvasive measure of the extent of pulmonary arterial thrombosis, consequent anatomic and physiologic derangements, and available cardiopulmonary functional reserve, and thus it seems to accurately capture many important aspects of the clinical severity of the disease. Similar to previous studies, we also found that a higher proportion of hypoxemic patients had central thrombus location and associated RV dysfunction.22 Obstruction of the pulmonary vascular tree is the main factor that increases pulmonary vascular resistance in patients with acute PE, resulting in pulmonary hypertension and causing hypoxemia.23 In patients with chronic cardiopulmonary disease, the association between SatO2 and mortality was less pronounced. A possible explanation is a preconditioning effect through chronic hypoxia, which is characteristic of these patients. Also, in these patients hypoxemia might be unrelated to PE and could be potentially reflecting prior cardiopulmonary disease. Interestingly, roughly 20% of patients with SatO2 <90% did not show echocardiographic RV dysfunction. Since these patients had a less severe presentation than those with RV dysfunction, mechanisms other than mismatching of ventilation and perfusion (e.g., right to left shunting) might dominantly explain this presentation, and they might just benefit from anticoagulation and supplemental oxygen, without the need of advanced therapies for their PE.

This study has several limitations. RIETE did not have a strict time stamp for measurement of SatO2 with respect to time of PE confirmation. Nevertheless, the majority of the measurements occurred close to diagnosis of PE. Furthermore, RIETE did not capture subsequent measurements of SatO2, and we therefore did not assess trends in SatO2 or its effects on outcomes. Though some authors have suggested that models containing SatO2 (e.g., NEWS-2 [9]) might help monitor sick patients with acute PE, studies have not clearly identified or the optimal way to assess an acute response to therapy.24 Importantly, this study's main analyses of the full cohort could not fully account for the use of supplemental oxygen. Therefore, it is possible that some patients with PE receiving this therapy have been misclassified as having a normal SatO2. However, this limitation would tend to bias our results toward the null, where hypoxemic patients placed into better SatO2 categories or levels may have worse outcomes, leading to possible underestimation of the potential risks associated with hypoxemia. However, in support of the study's main findings, our subgroup analysis that excluded patients receiving supplemental oxygen at the time of SatO2 measurement had similar overall trends with the larger cohort. Arterial blood gas PaO2 (partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood) values might be more accurate than pulse oximetry SatO2 values for assessing gas exchange among patients with acute symptomatic PE. However, SatO2 has greater accuracy than PaO2 for assessing systemic oxygen delivery to tissues because SatO2 reflects the proportion of hemoglobin bound with oxygen which is the primary carrier of oxygen to the body's cells. PaO2, however, only measures dissolved oxygen, not the total amount of oxygen available, making it less useful for determining overall oxygen delivery to tissues. In addition, pulse oximetry is a noninvasive test, and it shows good correlation with the arterial blood gas calculated estimate of oxygen saturation, particularly when SatO2 is greater than 90%.25,26 Finally, although this is a large study and the data in this registry allowed us to adjust for a number of key variables (including chronic lung and heart disease), the possibility of residual confounding still remains.

In conclusion, in a large multinational study of non-hypotensive patients diagnosed with acute PE, we found an inverse association between SatO2 at presentation and odds of all-cause and PE-associated mortality over the subsequent 30 days. This study provides evidence that a SatO2 cutoff value of 96% might help better identify low-risk (i.e., SatO2 >96%) patients with acute PE than a cutoff of 90%. Clinical trials should evaluate if supplemental oxygen could be effective and safe for PE patients with SatO2 90–96%.
