Abstract
. We construct examples of variational bivectors that are not Poissonian.

1.
Introduction

It was proved in
[
3
, Theorem 1]
that for
evolutionary equations
u_{t}=f
with the non-degenerate symbol of the
right-hand side all variational multi-vectors of degree
\geq 3
are
trivial. In particular, it follows from this fact that any variational
bivector defines a Hamiltonian operator (a Poisson structure) for such an
equation (all the necessary definitions will be given in
Section
2
).

We show below that for the equations
u_{xy}=0
,
u_{xx}+u_{yy}
, and

u_{xyz}=0
(which can obviously be transformed to the evolutionary form,
but with the degenerate symbol) there do exist variational bivectors whose
Schouten bracket does not vanish. So, they are not Hamiltonian structures and
their brackets are nontrivial
3
-vectors.

2.
Variational bivectors

In this section, we follow
[
4
,
5
]
.

Let
\mathscr{E}\subset J^{\infty}(\pi)
be an infinitely prolonged equation,
where
\pi\colon E\to M
is a vector bundle, see
[
2
]
. For
the sake of simplicity, we assume
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

E=\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the coordinates
x^{1},\dots,x^{n}
and

u^{1},\dots,u^{m}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, respectively. Assume
also that
\mathscr{E}
is defined by the relation
F=0
, where

F=(F^{1},\dots,F^{m})
is a vector-function on
J^{\infty}(\pi)
. Denote by
P

the module of such functions. Denote also by
\varkappa
the module of sections
of the pull-back
\pi_{\infty}^{*}(\pi)
, where
\pi_{\infty}\colon J^{\infty}(\pi)\to M
. Let
\mathscr{F}
be the algebra of smooth functions on
J^{\infty}(\pi)

(or on
\mathscr{E}
). We use the
notation
\hat{\bullet}=\hom_{\mathscr{F}}(\bullet,\Lambda_{h}^{n})
, where

\Lambda_{h}^{n}
is the module of horizontal
n
-forms. We also denote
by
\CDiff(A,B)
the module of all
\mathscr{C}
-differential operators
(i.e., operators in total derivatives)
A\to B
,
A
ans
B
being

\mathscr{F}
-modules. Let
\ell_{F}
denote the linearization of
F\in P
and

\ell_{\mathscr{E}}=\left.\ell_{F}\right|_{\mathscr{E}}\colon\varkappa\to P
.

We impose some regularity conditions on
\mathscr{E}
:

(1)

\mathscr{E}
is a smooth submanifold in
J^{\infty}(\pi)
;

(2)

\mathscr{E}
surjectively projects by
\pi
onto
J^{0}(\pi)
;

(3)

if an “object” (function, differential form,

\mathscr{C}
-differential operator, etc.)
O\in\mathscr{O}
admits a
restriction
\left.O\right|_{\mathscr{E}}
from
J^{\infty}(\pi)
to
\mathscr{E}

and the restriction vanishes, then

O=\Delta(F),\qquad\Delta\in\CDiff(P,\mathscr{O});

(2.1)

(4)

the compatibility complex of
\ell_{\mathscr{E}}
is trivial.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathscr{E}=\{F=0\}\subset J^{\infty}(\pi)
. Then

\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{E}=\{F=0,\ell_{\mathscr{E}}(p)=0\}
is called
the cotangent equation of
\mathscr{E}
, while

\mathrm{t}^{*}\colon\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{E}
is the
cotangent covering. Here
p=(p^{1},\dots,p^{m})
is an odd variable of
parity
1
.

Definition 2
.

A
\mathscr{C}
-differential operator
H\colon\hat{P}\to\varkappa
is a
(variational) bivector on
\mathscr{E}
if

\displaystyle\ell_{\mathscr{E}}(H)=0,

(2.2)

\displaystyle(\ell_{\mathscr{E}}\circ H)^{*}\equiv H^{*}\circ\ell_{\mathscr{E}}^{*}=\ell_{\mathscr{E}}\circ H.

(2.3)

Remark 1
.

To any
H=(\sum_{\sigma}h_{ij}^{\sigma}D_{\sigma})\in\CDiff(\hat{P},\varkappa)
,
where
D_{\sigma}
denotes the composition of total derivatives corresponding
to the multi-index
\sigma
, the vector-function

H_{u}=(\sum_{\sigma,j}h_{ij}^{\sigma}p_{\sigma}^{j})
on
\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{E}

corresponds. The expression
\ell_{\mathscr{E}}(H)
in Equation (
2.2
)
is to be understood as
\ell_{\mathscr{E}}(H_{u})
.

Theorem 1

(cf.
[
4
,
5
]
)
.

Let
H
be a bivector on
\mathscr{E}
. Then there exists an element
H_{p}

of parity
2
such that the evolutionary derivation
\mathbf{E}_{\varphi(H)}
of
parity
1
with the generating section
\varphi(H)=(H_{u},H_{p})
is a symmetry
of
\mathscr{T}^{*}\mathscr{E}
.

Proof.

Using Equations (
2.1
) and (
2.3
), we see that

\ell_{F}(H(p))-H^{*}(\ell_{F}(p))=\nabla(F,p),

(2.4)

on the ambient
J^{\infty}(\hat{\pi})
, where

\Delta\colon\varkappa\times\hat{P}\to P
is a bi-differential operator in
total derivatives. Then

H_{p}=\left.-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{*_{1}}(p,p)\right|_{\mathscr{E}}

is the desired element. Here
\Delta^{*_{1}}
denotes the adjoint to
\Delta

with respect to the first argument.
∎

Remark 2
.

Due to Equation (
2.2
), the operator
H
takes symmetries
of
\mathscr{E}
to its cosymmetries. Then, for two conservation
laws
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
, one can define their
bracket
\{\omega,\omega^{\prime}\}_{H}
in the standard way:

\mathscr{L}_{\delta(\omega)}(\delta(\omega^{\prime}))=\delta(\{\omega,\omega^{\prime}\}_{H}),

where
\mathscr{L}
is the Lie derivative and
\delta\colon E_{1}^{0,n-1}\to E_{1}^{1,n-1}
is the differential in the first term of Vinogradov’s spectral
sequence,
[
6
]
.

Definition 3
.

For the two bivectors
H
and
H^{\prime}
, define their Schouten
bracket
[\![H,H^{\prime}]\!]
by

[\mathbf{E}_{\varphi(H)},\mathbf{E}_{\varphi(H^{\prime})}]=\mathbf{E}_{[\![H,H^{\prime}]\!]},

where
[\cdot\,,\cdot]
is the anti-commutator. A bivector is a Poisson
structure if
[\![H,H]\!]=0
, i.e., if
\mathbf{E}_{\varphi(H)}
is a nilpotent
vector field. Two Poisson structures are compatible if
[\![H,H^{\prime}]\!]=0
.

Remark 3
.

The bracket
[\![\cdot\,,\cdot]\!]_{H}
can be computed by

[\![H,H^{\prime}]\!]=\mathbf{E}_{\varphi(H)}(H^{\prime})+\mathbf{E}_{\varphi(H^{\prime})}(H).

(2.5)

Theorem 2
.

If
H
is a Poisson structure, then
\{\cdot\,,\cdot\}_{H}
is a Poisson
bracket on the space of conservation laws, i.e., the bracket is
skew-symmetric and enjoys the Jacobi identity.

3.
Examples

We present here three examples of PDEs that admit bivectors which are not
Poisson structures. It is assumed everywhere that the jet order of the
operators are
\leq
the order of the equation.

3.1.
The wave equation

Consider the 2D wave equation in the form

u_{xy}=0.

(3.1)

The cotangent covering is

\{p_{xy}=0,\ u_{xy}=0\}\to\{u_{xy}=0\}.

Let

H=H_{0}+H_{1}D_{x}+H_{2}D_{y}+H_{3}D_{x}^{2}+H_{4}D_{y}^{2},

where
H_{i}=H_{i}(x,y,u,u_{x},u_{y},u_{xx},u_{yy})
. Then

\displaystyle\ell_{\mathscr{E}}\circ H=D_{x}\circ D_{y}\circ(H_{0}+H_{1}D_{x}+H_{2}D_{y}+H_{3}D_{x}^{2}+H_{4}D_{y}^{2}),

\displaystyle H^{*}\circ\ell_{\mathscr{E}}^{*}=(H_{0}-D_{x}\circ H_{1}-D_{y}\circ H_{2}+D_{x}^{2}\circ H_{3}+D_{y}^{2}\circ H_{4})\circ D_{x}\circ D_{y}.

Solving the system

\ell_{\mathscr{E}}\circ H=H^{*}\circ\ell_{\mathscr{E}}^{*},\quad D_{x}D_{y}(H_{u})=0,

where
H_{u}=H(p)=H_{0}p+H_{1}p_{x}+H_{2}p_{y}+H_{3}p_{xx}+H_{4}p_{yy}
, we get
the following solutions:

B_{0}=p,\quad B_{1}=\frac{1}{2}D_{x}(h_{1})p_{x}+h_{1}p_{xx},\quad B_{2}=\frac{1}{2}D_{y}(h_{2})p_{y}+h_{2}p_{yy},

where
h_{1}=h_{1}(x,u_{x})
,
h_{2}=h_{2}(y,u_{y})
.

Remark 4
.

The bivector
B_{0}
is the identical Poisson structure, that exists
on
\mathscr{E}
, because
\ell_{\mathscr{E}}
is a self-adjoint
operator. The bivectors
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
transform to each other by the
discrete symmetry
x\leftrightarrow y
of
\mathscr{E}
.

Proposition 1
.

The bivector
B_{1}
is a Poisson structure on Equation (
3.1
) if and
only if
\partial h_{1}/\partial u_{x}=0
. Similarly
,

B_{2}
is a
Poisson structure iff
\partial h_{2}/\partial u_{y}=0
. All these structures
are pair-wise compatible.

Proof.

Let us first find the operator
\nabla
from
Equation (
2.4
). Extending all necessary objects to the
space
J^{\infty}(\hat{\pi})
, where

\hat{\pi}\colon\mathbb{R}^{4}(p,u,x,y)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}(x,y)
, by
tedious but straightforward computations we find that

\ell_{F}\circ H-H^{*}\circ\ell_{F}^{*}=D_{x}(F)A_{1}+FA_{0},

where

A_{1}=\frac{\left(\dfrac{\partial h_{1}^{\prime}}{\partial x}+\dfrac{\partial h_{1}^{\prime}}{\partial u_{x}}u_{xx}\right)p_{x}}{2}+h_{1}^{\prime}p_{xx}\\

and

\displaystyle A_{0}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}h_{1}^{\prime}}{\partial x\partial u_{x}}u_{xx}+\frac{\dfrac{\partial^{2}h_{1}^{\prime}}{\partial x^{2}}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}h_{1}^{\prime}}{\partial^{2}u_{x}^{2}}u_{xx}^{2}+\dfrac{\partial h_{1}^{\prime}}{\partial u_{x}}u_{xxx}}{2}\right)p_{x}

\displaystyle+\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\partial h_{1}^{\prime}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial h_{1}^{\prime}}{\partial u_{x}}u_{xx}\right)p_{xx}+h_{1}^{\prime}p_{xxx}.

Here
h_{1}^{\prime}=\partial h_{1}/\partial u_{x}
. Consequently,

\nabla^{*_{1}}(\cdot,p)=-\frac{1}{2}D_{x}(\cdot)\left(\left(\frac{\partial h_{1}^{\prime}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial h_{1}^{\prime}}{\partial u_{x}}u_{xx}\right)p_{x}+2h_{1}^{\prime}p_{xx}\right)

and

\nabla^{*_{1}}(p,p)=-\frac{1}{2}p_{x}\left(\left(\frac{\partial h_{1}^{\prime}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial h_{1}^{\prime}}{\partial u_{x}}u_{xx}\right)p_{x}+2h_{1}^{\prime}p_{xx}\right)=-h_{1}^{\prime}p_{x}p_{xx},

since
p_{x}\cdot p_{x}=0
. Hence the components of the symmetry
\varphi(H_{1})

are

H_{u}=\frac{1}{2}D_{x}(h_{1})p_{x}+h_{1}p_{xx},\quad H_{p}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial h_{1}}{\partial u_{x}}p_{x}p_{xx}.

Let us now compute
[\![H_{1},H_{1}]\!]
. One has by Equation (
2.5
):

[\![B_{1},B_{1}]\!]=2\mathbf{E}_{\varphi(H_{1})}(H_{u})\\
=2\left(D_{x}(H_{p})\frac{\partial H_{u}}{\partial p_{x}}+D_{x}^{2}(H_{p})\frac{\partial H_{u}}{\partial p_{xx}}+D_{x}(H_{u})\frac{\partial H_{u}}{\partial u_{x}}+D_{x}^{2}(H_{u})\frac{\partial H_{u}}{\partial u_{xx}}\right)\\
=\frac{1}{2}\big(D_{x}(h_{1}^{\prime})p_{x}p_{xx}+h_{1}^{\prime}p_{x}p_{xxx}\big)D_{x}(h_{1})\\
+\big(D_{x}^{2}(h_{1}^{\prime})p_{x}p_{xx}+2D_{x}(h_{1}^{\prime})p_{x}p_{xxx}+h_{1}^{\prime}p_{xx}p_{xxx}+h_{1}^{\prime}p_{x}p_{xxxx}\big)h_{1}\\
+\big(D_{x}^{2}(h_{1})p_{x}+3D_{x}(h_{1})p_{xx}+2h_{1}p_{xxx}\big)\big(\frac{1}{2}D_{x}(h_{1}^{\prime})p_{x}+h_{1}^{\prime}p_{xx}\big)\\
+\frac{1}{2}\big(D_{x}^{3}(h_{1})p_{x}+4D_{x}^{2}(h_{1})p_{xx}+5D_{x}(h_{1})p_{xxx}+2h_{1}p_{xxxx}\big)h_{1}^{\prime}\\
=-h_{1}h_{1}^{\prime}p_{xx}p_{xxx}+(h_{1}D_{x}(h_{1}^{\prime})-2D_{x}(h_{x})h_{1}^{\prime})p_{x}p_{xxx}\\
+\big(h_{1}D_{x}^{2}(h_{1}^{\prime})-D_{x}(h_{1})D_{x}(h_{1}^{\prime})-D_{x}^{2}(h_{1})h_{1}^{\prime}\big)p_{x}p_{xx},

which proves the result for
H_{1}
. The proof for
H_{2}
is the same; to check
compatibility is a quite straightforward computation.
∎

We shall omit technical details in what follows.

3.2.
A third-order equation

Consider the equation

u_{xyz}=0

(3.2)

and find bivectors of the form

H=H_{0}p+H_{1}p_{x}+H_{2}p_{y}+H_{3}p_{z}+H_{4}p_{xx}+H_{5}p_{xy}+H_{6}p_{xz}+H_{7}p_{yy}+H_{8}p_{yz}\\
+H_{9}p_{zz}+H_{10}p_{xxx}+H_{11}p_{xxy}+H_{12}p_{xxz}+H_{13}p_{xyy}+H_{14}p_{xzz}\\
+H_{15}p_{yyy}+H_{16}p_{yyz}+H_{17}p_{yzz}+H_{18}p_{zzz},

where
H_{i}
depend on

x,y,z,u,u_{x},u_{y},u_{z},u_{xx},u_{xy},u_{xz},u_{yy},u_{yz},u_{zz},\\
u_{xxx},u_{xxy},u_{xxz},u_{xyy},u_{xzz},u_{yyy},u_{yyz},u_{yzz},u_{zzz}.

By direct computations, the following solutions are found

\displaystyle B_{1}=hp_{x},

\displaystyle B_{2}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}p_{xx}+hp_{xxx},

\displaystyle B_{3}=\frac{1}{2}D_{x}(g)p_{xy}+gp_{xxy},

\displaystyle B_{4}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}u_{xyy}-\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}u_{xxy}\right)p_{xy}+g(u_{xyy}p_{xxy}-u_{xxy}p_{xyy}),

and another eight obtained from the above presented by the transpositions

x\leftrightarrow y\leftrightarrow z\leftrightarrow x
. Here
h=h(x)
,
g=g(x,y,u_{xy})
.

Proposition 2
.

The bivectors
B_{1}
,
B_{2}
, and
B_{3}
are Poisson structures on
Equation (
3.2
). The bivector
B_{4}
is a Poisson structures if and
only if
g=0
. All such structures are pair-wise compatible.

Proof.

Let us describe the operator
\nabla
for
B_{3}
and
B_{4}
first. One has
for
B_{3}
:

\nabla_{3}(\cdot,p)=(\cdot)A_{0}(p)+D_{x}(\cdot)A_{1}(p)+D_{y}(\cdot)A_{2}(p)+D_{x}D_{y}(\cdot)A_{12}(p),

where

\displaystyle A_{0}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}D_{x}^{2}D_{y}(g^{\prime})p_{xy}+\frac{3}{2}D_{x}D_{y}(g^{\prime})p_{xxy}+\frac{1}{2}D_{x}^{2}(g^{\prime})p_{xyy}

\displaystyle+\frac{3}{2}D_{x}(g^{\prime})p_{xxyy}+D_{y}(g^{\prime})p_{xxxy}+g^{\prime}p_{xxxyy},

\displaystyle A_{1}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(D_{x}D_{y}(g^{\prime})p_{xy}+D_{x}(g^{\prime})p_{xyy}+D_{y}(g^{\prime})p_{xxy})+g^{\prime}p_{xxyy},

\displaystyle A_{2}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}D_{x}^{2}(g^{\prime})p_{xy}+\frac{3}{2}D_{x}(g^{\prime})p_{xxy}+g^{\prime}p_{xxxy},

\displaystyle A_{12}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}D_{x}(g^{\prime})p_{xy}+g^{\prime}p_{xxy}.

Here
g^{\prime}=\partial g/\partial u_{xy}
. Consequently,

\nabla_{3}^{1_{*}}(\cdot,p)=D_{x}D_{y}(\cdot)\left(\frac{1}{2}D_{x}(g^{\prime})p_{xy}+g^{\prime}p_{xxy}\right)

and

\nabla_{3}^{1_{*}}(p,p)=p_{xy}\left(\frac{1}{2}D_{x}(g^{\prime})p_{xy}+g^{\prime}p_{xxy}\right)=g^{\prime}p_{xy}p_{xxy}.

(3.3)

The expression for
\nabla_{4}(\cdot,p)
is to big to present it here, but

\nabla_{4}^{*_{1}}(\cdot,p)=D_{x}D_{y}(\cdot)\left(\frac{1}{2}\Big(u_{xyy}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}-u_{xxy}\frac{\partial g^{\prime}}{\partial y}\Big)p_{xy}+g^{\prime}(u_{xyy}p_{xxy}-u_{xxy}p_{xyy})\right)

and

\nabla_{4}^{*_{1}}(p,p)=g^{\prime}p_{xy}(u_{xyy}p_{xxy}-u_{xxy}p_{xyy})

(3.4)

So,

\displaystyle H_{3,u}=\frac{1}{2}D_{x}(g)p_{xy}+gp_{xxy},\quad H_{3,p}=\frac{1}{2}g^{\prime}p_{xxy}p_{xy},

\displaystyle H_{4,u}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}u_{xyy}-\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}u_{xxy}\right)p_{xy}+g(u_{xyy}p_{xxy}-u_{xxy}p_{xyy}),

\displaystyle H_{4,p}=\frac{1}{2}g^{\prime}(u_{xyy}p_{xxy}-u_{xxy}p_{xyy})p_{xy}.

Consequently,

[\![B_{3},B_{3}]\!]=2\Big(D_{x}D_{y}(H_{3,p})\frac{\partial H_{3,u}}{\partial p_{xy}}+D_{x}^{2}D_{y}(H_{3,p})\frac{\partial H_{3,u}}{\partial p_{xxy}}\\
+D_{x}D_{y}(H_{3,u})\frac{\partial H_{3,u}}{\partial u_{xy}}+D_{x}^{2}D_{y}(H_{3,u})\frac{\partial H_{3,u}}{\partial u_{xxy}}\Big)=2gg^{\prime}p_{xxxxyy}p_{xy}+O(5),

where
O(5)
denotes the terms that depend on
p_{\sigma}
with
\left|\sigma\right|\leq 5
. The result follows from the fact that
[\![B_{3},B_{3}]\!]
is
bilinear in
g
and
g^{\prime}
.

In a similar way,

[\![B_{4},B_{4}]\!]=2\Big(D_{x}D_{y}(H_{4,p})\frac{\partial H_{4,u}}{\partial p_{xy}}+D_{x}^{2}D_{y}(H_{4,p})\frac{\partial H_{4,u}}{\partial p_{xxy}}\\
+D_{x}D_{y}^{2}(H_{4,p})\frac{\partial H_{4,u}}{\partial p_{xyy}}+D_{x}D_{y}(H_{4,u})\frac{\partial H_{4,u}}{\partial u_{xy}}\\
+D_{x}^{2}D_{y}(H_{4,y})\frac{\partial H_{4,u}}{\partial u_{xxy}}+D_{x}D_{y}^{2}(H_{4,u})\frac{\partial H_{4,u}}{\partial u_{xyy}}\Big).

Notice now that in the expression for
[\![B_{4},B_{4}]\!]
there is a unique
term of the form

-2g^{2}(u_{xyy}p_{xxxxyy}p_{xyy}+u_{xxy}p_{xxyyyy}p_{xxy}),

and this completes the proof.
∎

3.3.
The Laplace equation

Our last example is the 2D Laplace equation

u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0.

(3.5)

Let

H=H_{0}p+H_{1}p_{x}+H_{2}p_{y}+H_{3}p_{xy}+H_{4}p_{yy},

where
H_{i}=H_{i}(x,y,u,u_{x},u_{y},u_{xy},u_{yy})
. Then
H
is a bivector
on Equation (
3.5
) if
H_{0}
is a constant and

\displaystyle H_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial u_{x}}u_{yy}-\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial u_{y}}u_{xy}-\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle H_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial u_{x}}u_{xy}+\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial u_{y}}u_{yy}+\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial y}\right),

\displaystyle H_{3}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial y},\quad H_{4}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial x},

where the function
h=h(x,y,u_{x},u_{y})
enjoys the system

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial y^{2}}=0,\quad\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial u_{x}}-\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial y\partial u_{y}}=0,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial y\partial u_{x}}+\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial u_{y}}=0,\quad\frac{\partial^{3}h}{\partial y\partial u_{y}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{3}u}{\partial y\partial u_{x}^{2}}=0.

We failed to find a generic solution and solved it under the assumption
that
h
is quadratic in its variables. Then the solutions are

\displaystyle B_{0}

\displaystyle=p,\quad B_{1}=p_{yy},\quad B_{2}=p_{xy},

\displaystyle B_{3}

\displaystyle=p_{y}+2(xp_{xy}+yp_{yy}),\quad B_{4}=p_{x}+2(yp_{xy}-xp_{yy}),

\displaystyle B_{5}

\displaystyle=u_{yy}p_{y}-u_{xy}p_{x}+2(u_{y}p_{yy}-u_{x}p_{xy}),

\displaystyle B_{6}

\displaystyle=u_{yy}p_{x}+u_{xy}p_{y}+2(u_{y}p_{xy}+u_{x}p_{yy}),

\displaystyle B_{7}

\displaystyle=(u_{y}+xu_{xy}+yu_{yy})p_{x}+(u_{x}+yu_{xy}-xu_{yy})p_{y}

\displaystyle+2(yu_{x}-xu_{y})p_{yy}+2(xu_{x}+yu_{y})p_{xy},

\displaystyle B_{8}

\displaystyle=-(u_{x}+yu_{xy}-xu_{yy})p_{x}+(u_{y}+xu_{xy}+yu_{yy})p_{y}

\displaystyle+2(xu_{x}+yu_{y})p_{yy}-2(yu_{x}-xu_{y})p_{xy}.

Proposition 3
.

The bivectors
B_{0}
–
B_{4}
are Poisson structures on the 2D Laplace
equation, while
B_{5}
–
B_{8}
are not and the brackets
[\![B_{i},B_{j}]\!]
,

5\leq i\leq j\leq 8
are nontrivial
3
-vectors on this equation.

The proof follows the same lines as the previous ones and we skip it.

4.
Concluding remarks

We constructed examples of PDEs that admit non-Poissonian bivectors. As a
consequence, this implies existence of nontrivial
3
-vectors. Thus, the
non-degeneracy of symbol in
[
3
, Theorem 1]
is an
essential condition indeed. On the other hand, the 3D example
(Section
3.2
) seems to us somewhat degenerate; in other
3D examples: the Poincaré equation
(Appendix
C
) and the 3D Laplace equation
(Appendix
B
), all the bivectors are Poissonian. This leads to a
conjecture that in the
n
-dimensional case,
n>2
, generically (whatever this
means) nontrivial
k
-vectors,
k>2
, do not exist.
