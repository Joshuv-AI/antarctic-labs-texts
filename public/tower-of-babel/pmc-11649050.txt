The State of Pediatric Kidney Transplantation in India: A Survey-based Analysis of Practices, Challenges, and Educational Needs
Authors: Arka Banerjee, Adwait S. Bendre, Gayatri Munghate, Minnie Bodhanwala
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Technical and immunological advancements have improved patient and graft survival rates in pediatric kidney transplantation (PKT). However, the landscape of PKT in India remains underexplored. We conducted a questionnaire-based survey among Indian pediatric surgeons to delineate contemporary trends in PKT.Methods:The online survey comprised 10 questions assessing pediatric surgeons’ involvement in PKT, existing challenges, and educational needs.Results:Of 610 surveyed pediatric surgeons, 120 responded (19.67% response rate). Majority of the respondents were affiliated with teaching programs, either in the public sector (61.18%) or in corporate setups (25.21%). While 65.83% of respondents managed pediatric patients with chronic kidney disease, only 10% had PKT programs at their institutes. Most of the respondents (67.5%) believed that pediatric surgeons should perform PKTs. Despite this, only 15.83% were part of the renal transplantation team at their institute and only 2.5% routinely performed PKTs. Most of the respondents (63.33%) advocated for mandatory KT exposure during postdoctoral training.Conclusion:Despite enthusiasm among pediatric surgeons, actual involvement remains low. Dedicated PKT programs are necessary to address these challenges along with improved training by integrating transplantation sciences into the pediatric surgical curriculum. The study underscores the need for pediatric surgeons to specialize in transplantation and advocates for policy changes to address the prevailing challenges.

INTRODUCTION

Since its inception in 1954, kidney transplantation (KT) has evolved as the definitive treatment for patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Initially, pediatric KT (PKT) faced significant challenges, resulting in lower survival rates compared to adults.[1] However, advancements in immunology, immunosuppressants, and surgical techniques have improved outcomes.[2] Currently, PKT boasts impressive 1- and 5-year patient survival rates of approximately 98% and 94%, respectively.[3]

While the procedural aspects of KT in pediatric and adult patients may appear superficially similar, PKT differs fundamentally from adult KT in etiologies, complications, donor selection, growth, comorbid conditions affecting the lower urinary tract, medication adherence, and transitioning to adult care, particularly for children under 10 years of age.[4]

Despite these complexities, the majority of PKTs in India, as is the case globally, are performed by surgeons who primarily practice adult KT. Moreover, the pediatric surgery curriculum provides limited focus on PKT, resulting in scarce exposure for aspiring pediatric surgeons.

Against this backdrop, our questionnaire-based survey aims to explore contemporary trends and practices among Indian pediatric surgeons regarding PKT, delineate their current state of involvement, pinpoint prevailing challenges, and highlight the unmet needs regarding PKT within the pediatric surgical community.

METHODS

A questionnaire-based survey was developed to investigate PKT practices among Indian pediatric surgeons, assessing trends, involvement in PKT programs, and the perceived value of incorporating PKT into the pediatric surgery curriculum.

The anonymized questionnaire, comprising 10 questions, was distributed via digital platforms, primarily WhatsApp groups, and the responses were collected through Google Forms. Data analysis involved descriptive statistics, with frequencies calculated for nominal variables and measures of central tendency and dispersion for ordinal or interval variables.

RESULTS

The survey was sent to around 610 pediatric surgeons, with a participation rate of 19.67% (120 completed responses). Most of the respondents were employed in the public sector (61.18%) or part of corporate entities with teaching programs (25.21%). Ninety-eight out of 120 (81.67%) respondents reported that pediatric urology constitutes at least 50% of their practice, and 79/120 (65.83%) respondents managed patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD).

Respondents varied significantly in terms of their experience [Figure 1].

Pediatric surgical experience

While 63/120 (52.5%) respondents confirmed the presence of a pediatric nephrology department or subdivision at their institutions, only 12 out of 120 (10%) indicated the existence of a PKT program. Within this subset, merely 7 surgeons admitted active participation in their institution’s PKT program. In addition, 10 (8.33%) respondents acknowledged their involvement in PKT programs whereas 6/120 (5%) said that they were previously involved in PKT but no longer participate [Figure 2]. A total of 30 out of 120 (25%) participants highlighted that PKTs at their centers are performed by adult urologists.

Proportion of pediatric surgeons affiliated to a pediatric kidney transplantation program

Even though 81.67% (98/120) of respondents admitted to having never conducted or assisted in a KT, 95% (114/120) believed that PKT should be done by pediatric surgeons. Only 3/120 (2.5%) respondents suggested that they should be involved only in PKT in small kids when adult transplantation surgeons need assistance and an equal number believed that pediatric surgeons need not engage in KTs at all. Only 3 out of 120 respondents (2.5%) regularly performed PKTs, whereas 19/120 (15.83%) were part of the transplantation team but not as lead surgeons [Figure 3].

The survey also explored opinions on inclusion of PKT in the pediatric surgery curriculum. Thirty-two out of 120 (26.67%) respondents recognized its current inclusion but noted its undervalued importance in examinations. Seventeen (14.17%) respondents were unaware of its presence in the syllabus but supported its inclusion. A majority (76/120; 63.33%) of the respondents advocated for mandatory postings in renal transplantation units during training, while 15.83% (19/120) viewed PKT as unnecessary in the curriculum, suggesting postdoctoral fellowships as an alternative. Opinions also emerged suggesting the specialization of transplantation services and the prerequisite of adult KT training before venturing into PKTs. A small fraction, 7/120 (5.83%), doubted the feasibility or value of exposing pediatric surgical trainees to KT.

An analysis based on the respondents’ experience, categorized as “millennials” (up to 20 years of experience) versus veterans (over 20 years of experience), revealed significant differences in engagement with PKTs, with younger surgeons displaying more involvement, indicating a shift toward developing broader skill sets among newer generations of pediatric surgeons (P = 0.002; 95% confidence interval: 11.8%–36.26%), as summarized in Table 1.

DISCUSSION

The absence of a comprehensive registry obscures the true burden of pediatric ESRD and the unmet need for PKT in India. The current estimates suggest an incidence of ESRD in India of approximately 151–160 per million population annually, with children constituting about 1%–2% of these cases.[5]

However, the establishment of specialized KT services for children faces significant hurdles due to a critical shortage of trained professionals, including pediatric transplantation surgeons, compounded by restricted access to tertiary care, insufficient chronic dialysis facilities for children, limited health insurance coverage, and an ineffective deceased donor program.[6] This study represents the inaugural investigation from India into the perspectives, attitudes, and practices of pediatric surgeons regarding PKT.

Renal transplantation is the only curative treatment for children with ESRD, markedly extending life expectancy by 20–40 years compared to ongoing dialysis treatment,[7] while also enhancing the quality of life[8] and offering the most favorable long-term cost-effectiveness.[9]

The distinction between pediatric and adult renal transplantation is underscored by differences in patient size, fluid requirements, immune responsiveness, etiologies of renal failure, pharmacokinetics, and a spectrum of other medical and social considerations.[10] Despite the regulatory mandates in developed countries for renal transplantation physicians to possess specific training in pediatric nephrology and transplantation, no analogous regulations govern the specialization of transplantation surgeons.

Evidence suggests superior outcomes achieved by specialist surgeons compared to their generalist counterparts, with a well-documented volume–outcome relationship existing within KT.[1112] Contento et al.’s findings suggested inferior outcomes for pediatric patients at low-volume centers, attributed in part to the absence of dedicated pediatric surgeons.[13] This is corroborated by further research indicating suboptimal outcomes at low-volume pediatric solid-organ transplantation centers.[1014]

Although prior research has linked specialty training in pediatric surgery with enhanced surgical outcomes in procedures such as inguinal hernia repairs,[15] appendectomies,[16] and pyloromyotomies,[17] no studies have specifically examined outcomes of pediatric renal transplantation conducted by pediatric versus adult surgeons.

Our study employed a simple, anonymized questionnaire to elucidate the scarcity of pediatric surgeons within India’s transplantation workforce. However, it is beyond the scope of this study to directly correlate pediatric specialization in transplantation surgery with improved outcomes. Factors extending beyond surgeon experience, such as operational staff and pediatric-specific hospital facilities, are well known.[18]

Our nationwide survey was aimed to assess the landscape of pediatric ESRD and renal transplantation, exploring the prevalence of PKT in adult centers, the involvement of pediatric surgeons in adult KT teams, and identifying gaps in pediatric transplantation training. A notable limitation of this survey is its low response rate, which may have excluded those surgeons actively performing renal transplantations in children. Nonetheless, responses from major pediatric surgical centers suggest that the findings should closely mirror the contemporary state of pediatric renal transplantation in India.

The survey reveals a glaring paradox: while most pediatric surgeons manage children with CKD, less than a fifth of them engage in renal transplantation [Figure 3]. However, a burgeoning interest among the younger generation of pediatric surgeons in incorporating transplantation surgery into their skill set is evident, suggesting a shift toward embracing broader surgical practices.

We posit that an increase in pediatric surgeons participating in the national transplantation workforce could lead to a demographic shift, potentially increasing pediatric transplantation rates in younger children and boosting pediatric cadaveric organ donations. Whether this will translate into a discernible reduction in surgical complications is speculative at this juncture. It is our hope that this study will catalyze the formation of a dedicated PKT taskforce in India, marking a significant step forward in addressing this critical health-care need.

CONCLUSION

Despite enthusiasm among pediatric surgeons, actual involvement remains low. Dedicated PKT programs are necessary to address these challenges along with improved training by integrating transplantation sciences into the pediatric surgical curriculum. Further research is needed to assess the clinical outcomes of KTs performed by pediatric transplantation surgeons, focusing on graft and patient survival rates and postoperative complications. Integrating transplantation sciences into the pediatric surgical curriculum could broaden access to KT for children in need. This study aims to encourage pediatric surgeons to specialize further in pediatric transplantation surgery, ultimately improving PKT outcomes in the long run.
