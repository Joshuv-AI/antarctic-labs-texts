Clinical Impact and Lesion Characteristics of Suboptimal Optical Coherence Tomography Stent Deployment in Patients With Acute Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Jiawei Zhao, Rui Zhao, Yuzhu Chen, Lina Cui, Xianqin Ma, Tong Lin, Fuhong Dong, Jiawen Chen, Jinfeng Tan, Tianyu Wu, Zhao Sun, Chengmei Jin, Lili Xiu, Wei Wang, Huai Yu, Yanchao Liu, Guo Wei, Lulu Li, Yini Wang, Jingbo Hou, Chao Fang, Jiannan Dai, Bo Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuboptimal stent deployment detected by optical coherence tomography (OCT) correlates with adverse outcomes, yet its long‐term prognosis and associations with lesion characteristics in patients with acute myocardial infarction (MI) receiving drug‐eluting stents remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the impact of OCT‐defined stent parameters on long‐term outcomes and identify lesion features predictive of suboptimal stent deployment in patients with acute MI.MethodsBetween January 2018 and December 2020, patients with acute MI undergoing successful intervention with drug‐eluting stents and postprocedural OCT imaging at the target lesions were included. The end point was device‐oriented cardiovascular events, a composite of cardiac death, target‐vessel related MI, and target‐lesion revascularization. Suboptimal OCT stent deployment was defined as the presence of ≥1 OCT findings significantly associated with device‐oriented cardiovascular events. In a subgroup with preprocedural OCT imaging for target lesions, lesion morphology was assessed.ResultsAmong 881 patients (906 lesions with final OCT imaging), minimum stent area<4.5 mm2, irregular protrusion, and distal reference lumen narrowing were independent predictors of device failure, at a median follow‐up of 4.0 years. Patients with suboptimal OCT stent deployment had a higher incidence of device‐oriented cardiovascular events, which was primarily driven by cardiac death and target lesion revascularization. In the subgroup (including 789 lesions), multivariate analysis identified plaque rupture, cholesterol crystals, and smaller reference lumen area as predictors of suboptimal OCT stent deployment.ConclusionsSuboptimal stent deployment, defined by specific quantitative OCT criteria, was associated with device failure at long‐term follow‐up in patients with acute MI successfully treated with drug‐eluting stents. Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. 1 Although percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the mainstay of treatment, stent‐related events persist over time with all types of stents, including drug‐eluting stents (DES). 2 , 3 Optical coherence tomography (OCT), with its high spatial resolution allowing real‐time visualization of intraluminal and superficial structure of the vessel wall, could provide valuable information on lesion characteristics and identify interventional details of stent failure. 4

Multiple randomized trials 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 and meta‐analyses 9 , 10 have confirmed that OCT‐guided PCI is an effective method for reducing suboptimal stent implantation and adverse cardiovascular events, compared with angiography‐guided PCI, particularly in complex lesions. In addition, OCT‐identified suboptimal stent results have been observed as a potential factor contributing to poorer outcomes in patients with coronary artery disease. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 However, the validity of these results is uncertain due to the heterogeneity in study design and analyses, and the extent to which these parameters apply to patients with AMI, a particularly severe clinical presentation, remains largely uncertain. Of note, comprehensive OCT lesion characteristics for suboptimal stent deployment are still lacking. Therefore, this study aims to identify OCT‐guided stent implantation criteria linked to long‐term outcomes and to assess the association between baseline OCT lesion characteristics and suboptimal stent deployment in patients with AMI treated with DES.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

Between January 2018 and December 2020, 1193 patients diagnosed with AMI underwent postprocedural OCT of the treated lesions after immediate angiographic success (defined as residual stenosis <30% with Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction flow grade 3) 15 at the 2nd Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. A total of 881 patients with AMI with postprocedural OCT imaging of target lesions (covering the entire stent and reference segments within 5 mm of both edges) were analyzed (Figure 1). After excluding 10 patients without preprocedural OCT imaging and 106 with poor image quality, 765 patients were included in a subgroup analysis (Figure 1).

Study flow chart.

The present study included 881 patients with AMI with 906 treated lesions as the full analysis set. Specifically, 62 patients and 63 treated lesions were included in the group with DoCE, and 819 patients and 843 treated lesions were included in the group with without DoCE to identify suboptimal OCT stent deployment in patients with AMI. Subsequently, 471 patients and 479 lesions were included in the suboptimal OCT stent deployment group and 294 patients and 310 lesions in the optimal OCT stent deployment group to identify the predictors of suboptimal OCT stent deployment. AMI indicates acute myocardial infarction; DoCE, device‐oriented cardiovascular events; and OCT, optical coherence tomography.

The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the 2nd Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (Harbin, China). All enrolled patients provided written informed consent. Definitions of AMI and coronary risk factors are provided in the Supplemental Material.

Interventions

PCIs were conducted using standard techniques via femoral or radial access. OCT imaging was performed using a commercially available frequency‐domain system (ILUMIEN OPTIS System, Abbott Vascular). All OCT images of treated lesions were sent to the Intravascular Imaging and Physiology Core Lab. Two experienced investigators (J.Z. and R.Z.) who were blinded to the patients' information used offline review workstation software (Abbott Vascular) to analyze OCT images, with disagreements resolved by a third investigator (C.F.). Quantitative and qualitative analyses followed established criteria and consensus. 4 , 11 , 16 , 17 , 18

For the purposes of this study, only OCT findings obtained at the end of the procedure were considered. In particular, the decision to imaging OCT during procedure was at the discretion of the operator; no formal selection criteria or treatment strategies were prospectively adopted in the enrolled case samples. If the operator performed optimization after the respective OCT, the final OCT images obtained after optimization were adopted. Abnormal postprocedural OCT metrics included minimum stent area (MSA) <4.5 mm2, in‐stent lumen under‐expansion, intrastent plaque/thrombus protrusion, severe acute stent malapposition, stent edge dissection and reference lumen narrowing. Detailed descriptions about abnormal postprocedural OCT parameters were presented in the Table 1. Figure S1 shown the representative images of abnormal postprocedural OCT findings.

In the subgroup of preprocedural OCT imaging, quantitative coronary angiography analyses were performed using the Cardiovascular Angiography Analysis System version 5.10 (Pie Medical Imaging B.V., Maastricht, the Netherlands). Minimum lumen diameter, reference lumen diameter, lesion length, and percentage diameter stenosis were measured for each lesion. Prepocedural OCT of the target vessel was attempted before treatment of the target lesion. Quantitative and qualitative preprocedural OCT analyses were described in Supplemental Material.

Clinical Outcome

All enrolled patients received guideline‐directed optimal medical therapy according to current European Society of Cardiology recommendations, with scheduled follow‐up at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post discharge, then annually thereafter. Patients were followed from the date of admission until the study cutoff date (October 2023) or the date of last known contact, whichever came first. The end point was a composite of device‐oriented cardiovascular events (DoCE), including cardiac death, target vessel‐related MI, and clinically driven target lesion revascularization. The detailed definitions of DoCE were shown in Supplemental Material. Suboptimal OCT stent deployment was defined as the presence of ≥1 of the OCT findings significantly associated with DoCE.

Statistical Analysis

The distribution of continuous data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were presented as mean±SD and compared using the independent sample t test or presented as median (25th–75th percentiles) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as counts (proportions) and compared using the chi‐square or Fisher's exact test. Generalized estimating equations were used to compare per‐lesion data to avoid potential clustering of multiple lesions treated in a single patient. The κ coefficient of agreement for the post‐ and preprocedural OCT characteristics was used to quantify intraobserver and interobserver differences. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis using the Obuchowski method for clustered data was performed to determine the optimal cutoff value of MSA for predicting lesion‐level events; the highest Youden index (J statistic) representing the maximum potential effectiveness was used to determine the optimal cutoff.

DoCE were analyzed on a per‐patient hierarchical basis, compared using the log‐rank test. Associations between OCT findings and clinical outcomes were evaluated using univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression, with robust SEs clustered by patient identifier to account for within‐patient correlation. All variables of clinical, procedural, and postintervention OCT characteristics were tested for bivariate association with DoCE; if statistically significant (P<0.05), they were simultaneously entered into a Cox regression model to identify independent outcome predictors of outcome and calculate adjusted hazard ratios (HR) with associated 95% CIs. The following variables were included in the Cox regression model: gender, left ventricular ejection fraction, diabetes, and abnormal postprocedural OCT metrics. The proportional hazards assumption was met using Schoenfeld residuals and visual assessment of log(−log) plots. Additionally, internal validation of the prognostic model was performed using bootstrap resampling, and the corrected C‐index is reported.

Relationships between lesion characteristics and suboptimal OCT stent deployment were analyzed using a generalized estimating equations log‐binomial regression model with stepwise selection of variables with P<0.10 in univariate analysis. Multicollinearity among the variables in the models was assessed by tolerance and the variance inflation factor, considering a tolerance <0.1 and a variance inflation factor >10 as indicative of problematic collinearity. A 2‐sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with SAS, version 9.4, and R V.4.0.5.

RESULTS

Clinical and procedural characteristics of the study population were summarized in Table 2 and Table S1. There were 881 patients with AMI and 906 treated lesions in the final analysis. Specifically, the mean age of patients was 60.4 years, 30.3% were female, and 71.2% were diagnosed with ST‐segment–elevation MI. The treated lesions were located in the left anterior descending artery (52.8%), left circumflex artery (16.8%), and right coronary artery (30.4%). Most lesions had a complex profile (Ellis class B2/C, 81.3%), and half of the cases had an initial Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction flow grade of ≤1 (49.9%).

Postprocedural OCT Characteristics

Postprocedural OCT characteristics were summarized in Table 3. The cumulative incidence rate of events for each OCT parameter group was summarized in Table S2. Lesions in the group with DoCE had smaller MSA, reference lumen area, and stent diameter compared with their counterparts without DoCE. Among the prespecified abnormal postprocedural OCT parameters, lesions with DoCE had a higher frequency of MSA <4.5 mm2, irregular protrusion, reference lumen narrowing, and distal lumen narrowing than those without DoCE. Other abnormal OCT findings were comparable between the 2 groups. Figure 2 shows the predictive value of each of the individual abnormal postprocedural OCT parameters at the patient level. Notably, MSA <4.5 mm2, irregular protrusion, and distal reference lumen narrowing were associated with DoCE. No significant multicollinearity was found in the models (Table S3). The multivariable Cox model demonstrated a C‐index of 0.826. After optimism correction via bootstrap internal validation, the corrected C‐index was 0.784 (95% CI, 0.723–0.832). Sensitivity analysis on the basis of 108 patients matched for the propensity to incur DoCE confirmed the results stemming from the main analysis in terms of both statistical direction and magnitude (Table S4). As noted, suboptimal OCT stent deployment was defined as either MSA <4.5 mm2, irregular protrusion, or distal reference lumen narrowing.

Clinical Outcome

All patients were grouped based on suboptimal OCT stent deployment, with 540 patients (61.3%) showing suboptimal results. The risk of DoCE rose with the number of suboptimal criteria per lesion (Figure 3B). Additionally, the increased risk of DoCE in suboptimal OCT stent deployment group was consistent across subgroups (Figure 4). OCT Lesion Characteristics in Subgroup

A total of 789 lesions had evaluable preprocedural OCT imaging, with 479 classified into the suboptimal stent deployment group. Baseline lesion characteristics are shown in Table 4. Angiography revealed smaller reference and minimum lumen diameters in the suboptimal group. DISCUSSION

This large observational study of 881 patients with AMI with successful DES implantation undergoing post‐DES OCT imaging and long‐term follow‐up revealed key insights: (1) suboptimal stent deployment, defined by OCT criteria (MSA <4.5 mm2, irregular protrusion, and distal reference lumen narrowing), independently predicted DES failure at long‐term follow‐up; (2) patients with suboptimal stent deployment faced a higher risk of DoCE, driven by cardiac death and target lesion revascularization, with worse outcomes as suboptimal criteria increased; (3) lesion characteristics (plaque rupture, cholesterol crystals, and smaller RLA) predicted suboptimal stent deployment. These findings underscore the value of OCT in optimizing stent deployment and highlight how plaque characteristics influence PCI outcomes in patients with AMI.

Identification of Suboptimal OCT Stent Deployment

Despite PCI often being guided by angiography, previous studies have confirmed that intravascular imaging can optimize stent deployment and improve outcomes. 5 , 19 , 20 , 21 OCT, with its higher resolution compared with intravascular ultrasound, is more accurate in identifying stent structures and suboptimal deployments, such as edge dissection and malapposition. However, not all suboptimal deployment criteria are clinically significant, and most studies have primarily focused on patients with coronary artery disease, with few patients with AMI included. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 22 The current study broadens the previous reports by assessing abnormal post‐DES OCT findings during long‐term outcome in patients with AMI.

As summarized in Table S9, previous studies have shown the association between post‐PCI OCT‐derived parameters and clinical outcome. Most of these studies included patients with coronary artery disease, used various stent types, and not all used an independent core laboratory. Whatever, a smaller MSA was a strong predictor of DoCE, 14 , 23 , 24 which was consistent with our findings that absolute stent under‐expansion (the MSA itself) rather than relative stent underexpansion (% stent narrowing) was associated with worse outcomes. Correspondingly, the cutoff value for MSA in our study was close to 4.5, further supporting the current European consensus document on intravascular imaging guidance of PCI endorses a MSA>4.5 mm2 by OCT as acceptable. 17

More important, special attention should be given to the distal landing zone and the stent expansion area during PCI, in addition to achieving a larger stent area near the target lesion. Previous studies found reference segment disease was associated with DES thrombosis and restenosis in intravascular ultrasound study. 25 , 26 In the present study, we proved the role of OCT‐identified suboptimal reference segment metrics at the distal stent edge was important in promoting DoCE in the long‐term follow‐up. Reference narrowing with significant residual plaque may indicate extensive atherosclerosis. In particular, distal reference lumen narrowing had the greatest long‐term impact post implantation, with an HR of 4.77 in the study. Previous studies indicate that residual stenosis at the stent edge alters local hemodynamics, increasing radial flow impact against the stent struts while reducing wall shear stress. This abnormal flow environment predisposes to intrastent thrombus formation and progression. 27 The lumen area was smaller at the distal, so the likelihood of obstructive thrombosis was higher, which may be one of the reasons for poorer prognosis in these patients. This finding highlights the critical importance of appropriate stent sizing and optimal landing zone selection, which could theoretically be improved through OCT‐angiography coregistration to minimize suboptimal outcomes. Interestingly, unlike previous studies, 11 , 12 , 14 our research found no clinical significance of stent edge dissections, regardless of the width, length, arc, or location of the dissections, possibly due to the lower incidence of dissections in our cohort. Additionally, unlike previous studies, we did not exclusively include high‐risk populations, and all stents used in our study were DES, which may have contributed to the improvement of dissections during follow‐up.

Specifically, we demonstrated that irregular protrusion was also a predictor of DoCE. Previous studies have shown that stent implantation exacerbated arterial inflammation and thrombogenicity by causing lipid core penetration and deep vessel injury, thereby increasing the risk of early stent thrombosis and restenosis. 28 , 29 Irregular protrusion indicated more severe vessel injury, such as medial disruption and lipid core penetration, and was strongly associated with lipid‐rich cores with thinner fibrous caps. 18 Of note, in our study, irregular protrusion was slightly less frequent than in previous studies, 18 , 30 but it remained common after stent implantation. This finding underscored the importance of avoiding unnecessary aggressive dilation during PCI to minimize coronary lumen damage, especially when dealing with lesions that have thinner fibrous caps. These findings in the current study supported the important role of OCT‐identify suboptimal stent criteria in patients with AMI, and intrastent thrombus may be the one important trigger in worse outcomes.

The study found that patients with suboptimal stent deployment faced higher risks of DoCE, primarily driven by cardiac death and target lesion revascularization, and the presence of an increasing number of suboptimal stent deployment criteria significantly elevated the risk of stent failure. Notably, we observed a significant increase in the risk of cardiac death in the suboptimal stent deployment group, whereas there was no significant difference in MI events between the groups. Suboptimal stent deployment may have a more pronounced impact on the occurrence of acute, fatal events, which are recorded as cardiac death. 25 , 29 Additionally, the vast majority of patients in this study received guideline‐directed medical therapy, which may have effectively reduced the overall incidence of ischemic events (including MI), resulting in a relatively low number of MI events and no significant intergroup difference, though it was insufficient to fully counteract the fatal risks driven by suboptimal deployment. Subgroup analysis in the present study showed that even in cases without bifurcation or severe calcification, suboptimal OCT stent deployment still increased the risk of stent failure. Of note, the cumulative rate of target lesion failure in the present study was lower (62/881, 7.5%), compared with CLI‐OPCI (Centro Per La Lotta Contro L'infarto–Optimization of Percutaneous Coronary Intervention) LATE study (11.9%) during 3‐year follow‐up, 12 mainly due to lower risk of target vessel MI (our study: 1.2%; CLI‐OPCI: 7.2%) and target lesion revascularization (our study: 3.1%; CLI‐OPCI: 8.4%). This relatively low event rate may be attributed to the low incidence of irregular protrusion in the study population and the widely implemented statin therapy. 18 , 31 Nevertheless, abnormal post‐PCI OCT parameters remained strongly associated with adverse outcomes in the present study. Furthermore, our event rate was similar to ILUMIEN IV trial (6.2%, 2‐year follow‐up), which was a prospective multicenter study. 14

Furthermore, the findings of this study do not negate the clinical significance of previously established indicators of suboptimal stent implantation, such as edge dissection and malapposition, which may have been identified and addressed intraoperatively. This study highlights that 3 specific metrics identified on OCT performed after operator‐confirmed procedural success, specifically MSA <4.5 mm2, irregular protrusion, and distal reference lumen narrowing, may still independently influence adverse patient outcomes.

Lesion Characteristics of Suboptimal OCT Stent Deployment

Whereas previous studies have linked suboptimal OCT stent deployment to adverse events, the role of OCT plaque characteristics remains poorly understood. Our findings emphasize the importance of preprocedural OCT assessment, revealing that vulnerable features like plaque rupture and cholesterol crystals increase the risk of suboptimal stent deployment. Coronary interventions, particularly stent implantation, can cause vessel wall injury, the extent of which depends on factors such as plaque vulnerability, underlying plaque morphology, and inflation pressure. 32 Plaque rupture has been demonstrated to correlate strongly with larger lipid cores and thinner fibrous caps, frequently accompanied by a substantial thrombus formation. 33 , 34 , 35 Stent implantation on a lipid core with a thin fibrous cap may result in vessel wall disruption manifested as irregular protrusion, which was consistent with the previous study. 30 Additionally, the presence of cholesterol crystals also increased the inflammatory response of macrophages and neutrophils in the plaque and caused mechanical damage to the fibrous cap, 36 which may increase the susceptibility of the vessel wall to stent damage and prevent full stent expansion.

In the current study, lesions with suboptimal stent deployment showed a higher degree of preexisting luminal narrowing versus those with optimal deployment. Of note, smaller RLA was found a predictor of lesions with MSA <4.5 mm2 or distal lumen narrowing. Consistent with previous findings, achieving greater absolute stent area was a primary objective in OCT‐guided PCI optimization 14 , 23 , 37 ; however, smaller baseline RLA tended to restrict poststenting area. These findings suggested that the choice of stent size as well as proper postdilatation was important in segments with smaller RLA at baseline.

Limitations

First, the present study was a single‐center, retrospective study, and selection bias was not avoided. Some patients (ie, with cardiac shock, extremely tortuous vessels, and chronic total occlusion) were not suitable for OCT imaging, so our conclusion might not be generalized to those patients with AMI. Second, our conclusion was not applicable to treated lesions located with left main artery because those lesions were excluded due the proximal stent edge was not observed. Third, all PCI procedural decision was at the discretion of the operator. Therefore, the current data reflect real‐world post‐PCI scenarios under OCT guidance. Fourth, potential interobserver variability in the interpretation of imaging data exists. We have provided a comparative analysis in the Supplemental Material. Although no significant differences were found, this variability reflects real‐world clinical practice and should be considered when interpreting the results. Fifth, most patients with DoCE did not undergo OCT reassessment, complicating the determination of stent failure mechanisms. Sixth, the relatively low number of DoCE events (62 patients, 7.5%) necessitates caution in interpreting the subgroup analyses. Although our study was adequately powered for the primary analysis, post hoc power calculations revealed that certain subgroup analyses were underpowered. The findings in these underpowered subgroups should be considered exploratory and require validation in larger, prospective studies. Finally, we did not have access to physiological ischemia measures. Such additional measures, used in conjunction with OCT‐derived suboptimal stent deployment, could further refine risk stratification during long‐term follow‐up.

CONCLUSIONS

This study highlights the prognostic value of suboptimal stent implantation parameters identified by OCT following successful intervention in patients with AMI, suggesting that OCT‐guided interventions to optimize these parameters may potentially improve long‐term outcomes. Key predictors of device failure included MSA <4.5 mm2, irregular protrusion, and distal reference narrowing. Additionally, vulnerable lesion features such as plaque rupture, cholesterol crystals, and smaller RLA were independent predictors of suboptimal stent deployment. These findings underscore the importance of OCT in guiding stent implantation and identifying high‐risk lesion characteristics. Prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings.
