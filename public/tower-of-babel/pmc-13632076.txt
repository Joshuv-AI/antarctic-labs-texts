Diet patterns are associated with progression of coronary artery calcification and differ across race/ethnicity: The Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis
Authors: Mohetaboer Momin, Ana Baylin, Jennifer Garner, Karol Watson, Kosuke Inoue, Eric J Brandt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInference regarding the longitudinal association between diet and coronary artery calcification (CAC) progression is limited, particularly when using empirically derived dietary patterns rather than hypothesis-driven diet indices. Prior studies have demonstrated discordance between calcification propensity and cardiovascular events across racial and ethnic groups, yet the effect of diet on CAC remains unknown.MethodsWe analyzed 3182 participants from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) with CAC measurements and completed food frequency questionnaires (FFQ47) at Exams 1 (2000–2002) and 5 (2010–2012). Dietary patterns were identified using principal components analysis. CAC progression was defined by incidence, progression and slow/moderate/fast annual change rate as categorical variables. Associations between baseline dietary patterns, dietary changes, and CAC outcomes were assessed using ordinal regression models and logistic regression models, adjusted for baseline age, sex, race/ethnicity, smoke, diabetes, hypertension, baseline CAC score, total energy intake, education level, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and lipid-lowering medications. Multiplicative interaction terms between each dietary pattern PC and race/ethnicity were introduced in multivariable regression models to assess whether the associations differ by race/ethnicity.ResultsFour major dietary patterns were identified: High Fat and Processed Meat; Vegetables and Fish; Whole Grains and Fruit; Beans, Tomatoes, and Refined grains. In multivariable models adjusting for demographic and cardiovascular risk factors, the Whole Grains and Fruit pattern was inversely associated with incident CAC (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.80–0.99; P = 0.04), whereas the High Fat and Processed Meat pattern was not. For CAC progression, the High Fat and Processed Meat pattern was positively associated (OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.01–1.43; P = 0.03), while the Whole Grains and Fruit pattern was inversely associated (OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.76–0.98; P = 0.03). Similar associations were observed for annual CAC change rate. In ordinal logistic regression models that incorporated death within the CAC progression outcome, the High fat and Processed Meat dietary pattern was associated with higher odds of progression (OR = 1.12; 95% CI: 1.00–1.25; borderline significance, p = 0.05), whereas the Whole Grains and Fruit pattern was associated with lower odds (OR = 0.86; 95% CI: 0.79–0.93). No significant associations were found for the Vegetables and Fish or the Beans, Tomatoes, and Refined Grains patterns. Race/ethnicity stratified analyses showed significant modification effect for the High Fat and Processed Meat and Whole Grains and Fruit patterns. Associations between dietary patterns and CAC progression were evident among White participants but were attenuated among Black participants (P-interaction=0.009 and 0.006, respectively).ConclusionsDiets high in fat and processed meats are associated with increased CAC progression, whereas whole grain and fruit/greens-based diets are associated with lower odds of CAC incidence and progression. Compared with White participants, these associations were attenuated among Black participants.

Introduction

Coronary artery calcification (CAC) represents the direct measurement of calcium deposition within the coronary arteries, reflecting the cumulative burden of atherosclerotic plaque. The presence and extent of CAC strongly correlate with future risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality; The Agatston CAC score is widely recognized as one of the most robust tools for refining CVD risk prediction beyond standard clinical risk factors [1,2]. CAC progression has been linked to adverse outcomes independent of baseline CAC, underscoring its importance not only as a diagnostic marker of subclinical disease but also as a dynamic measure of ongoing atherosclerotic activity [3,4]. However, while modifiable risk factors such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and smoking have been linked to CAC progression, they provide only modest improvements in prediction models for CAC progression [5]. Diet is hypothesized to influence the risk of coronary heart disease through pathways that extend beyond its effects on conventional risk factors such as blood pressure, lipids, and glucose metabolism [6,7].

However, the relationship between dietary patterns and CAC progression remains understudied. Three important knowledge gaps remain. First, to date, most epidemiological research on diet and CAC progression has been limited to cross-sectional analyses, limiting inference regarding the longitudinal association between diet and CAC progression. Macronutrients such as trans-fats and sugars have been implicated in increasing calcification risk [8,9], whereas omega-3 fatty acids from oily fish appear protective [10]. Conversely, low-carbohydrate and ketogenic diets have been associated with increased calcification [[11], [12], [13]]. CAC progression represents an active, regulated mineralization process influenced by inflammation, metabolic factors, and diet. Because dietary exposures may affect ongoing plaque biology rather than established calcification. Second, the majority have evaluated hypothesis-driven dietary indices rather than empirically derived dietary patterns that better capture real-world eating behaviors. Dietary patterns and scores, including the Mediterranean diet, Dietary Approach to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, and other high-quality dietary scores, have been inversely associated with calcification risk in prospective studies [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. Conversely, pro-inflammatory dietary patterns may contribute to vascular calcification [19,20]. These scores were designed for use in general populations and emphasize adherence to externally recommended food consumption guidelines, rather than capturing the naturally occurring patterns of food intake. Third, few studies have examined whether these associations differ across racial and ethnic groups or have been conducted in diverse, multiethnic populations, limiting the generalizability of their findings. Prior studies have demonstrated lower calcification propensity in Black individuals despite comparable or higher cardiovascular risk [21], moreover, calcified plaque differs biologically and clinically from lipid-rich plaque [22,23], These observations underscore the need for further investigation into potential racial and ethnic differences in the relationship between dietary patterns and CAC progression and the biological mechanisms underlying these differences.

Based on those, firstly we hypothesized that empirically derived dietary patterns would be more strongly associated with CAC progression than with CAC presence. we anticipated that PCA‑derived patterns would show associations with longitudinal CAC change even if cross‑sectional associations were absent. Second, we further hypothesized that dietary effects on calcification may differ across race and ethnic groups. We anticipated that the strength and direction of associations between dietary patterns and CAC progression would vary across subgroups.

It is worth mentioning that in a prior study using the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) study, no significant relationship was observed between the principal components analysis (PCA)-derived patterns and CAC presence after adjusting for covariates [24]. First, the previous study was cross-sectional and evaluated prevalent CAC, our study prospectively examines the association between baseline dietary patterns and incident CAC, CAC progression, and annual CAC change over approximately 10 years. Second, compared with the earlier study, our analyses include more comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders, including baseline CAC score, kidney function, lipid-lowering medication use, educational attainment, and total energy intake, and evaluate dietary patterns as continuous PCA scores rather than categorical quintiles to maximize statistical power. They investigated the association with much similar models and methods. Third, we investigated effect modification by race and ethnicity, which was not examined in the previous analysis and addresses whether diet-CAC associations differ across population subgroups.

With this in mind, we evaluated the association between data-driven dietary patterns and CAC progression and their differences across race/ethnicity subgroups. Understanding these relationships could provide valuable new insights into dietary strategies for CAC prevention and management.

Methods

Study population

MESA is a prospective cohort study of 6814 men and women aged 45–84 years at baseline, free of clinical cardiovascular disease, recruited from six U.S. field centers between 2000 and 2002 (Exam 1). Participants underwent standardized assessments including cardiovascular risk factors, imaging studies, and lifestyle questionnaires. Follow up for Exam 5 was conducted from 2010 to 2012. Objectives and design of the MESA study have been described previously [25]. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of all participating institutions, and all participants gave written informed consent.

Among the 6814 participants at baseline (Exam 1), all completed CAC measurements and the food frequency questionnaire with 47 food categories (FFQ47). At follow-up (Exam 5), CAC measurements were available for 3305 participants. Among those 3182 participants have both CAC measurements and completed FFQ47 at Exams 1 and 5(Fig. 1).

Flowchart of study design.

Dietary patterns

We used FFQ 47 for generating dietary patterns using the same method as a prior MESA study [24]. We generated dietary patterns using PCA with the principal() function from the psych package in R (version 2.4.3) to explore the underlying structure of the FFQ47 for Exam1 [26]. Prior to analysis, variables were standardized (By standardizing each dietary variable to a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1). A varimax rotation was applied to allow for correlation among components and determine PCA scores.

CAC measurement

CAC measurements were obtained in two heart passes for each participant. Scanning was performed using an electron-beam CT scanner or a multi-detector CT system that utilized helical scanning with reconstruction in 5 mm thick cuts and 350 mm field of view. Two CT scans were acquired for each participant. The methodology for acquisition of the scans has been described previously along with the comparability and reproducibility of image data collected using different scanners [27,28]. These scans were read independently at a centralized reading center (Harbor-UCLA research and Education Institute in Torrance, Calif). Measurements from the two CAC scans were averaged to quantify the amount of calcium present, using the Agatston scoring method. All scans were brightness-adjusted with a standard phantom containing four bars of known calcium density [29].

CAC was scored using the Agatston method (phantom adjusted) and CAC progression was defined as follows: CAC incidence was defined as a CAC score of 0 at Exam 1 and >0 at Exam 5, while controls were defined as participants with a CAC score of 0 at both examinations. CAC annual change rate was calculated as (CAC score at Exam 5 – CAC score at Exam 1) divided by the follow-up time in years, CAC change rate is divided into three groups base on distribution: slow progress (CAC change rate<10/year), moderate progress (≥10/year to <30/year, fast progress (CAC change rate≥30/year) [30]. CAC progression was defined as an increase in CAC score between exams (Exam 5 – Exam 1 > 0), with controls defined as no increase (Exam 5 – Exam 1 ≤ 0).

Covariates

Baseline demographic and clinical variables were assessed at Exam 1 using standardized MESA protocols. Covariates included age (years, continuous), race and ethnicity (White, Asian, Black, Hispanic/Latino), sex (female or male), diabetes status defined according to the 2003 American Diabetes Association criteria (normal vs impaired fasting glucose and diabetes), hypertension defined by JNC VI (1997) criteria (systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medications), and smoking status (never vs ever, including former and current smokers). Follow-up time was calculated as the number of days between Exam 1 and Exam 5 CT scans. Self-reported highest educational attainment, grouped as Low (no schooling through high school/GED), Medium (some college, technical certificate, or associate degree), and High (bachelor’s or graduate/professional degree). Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated from serum creatinine using the CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) 2009 equation. Self-reported or medication inventory–verified use of all lipid-lowering drugs (e.g., statins, fibrates, ezetimibe) at baseline. Daily total energy intake (kcal/day) was estimated from the validated 47-item food frequency questionnaire and divided by 100 to improve numerical stability and interpretability of model coefficients. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²). Physical activity level was assessed using metabolic equivalent task minutes per week (MET-min/week) and categorized into low, moderate, and high activity levels.

Statistical analysis

Logistic regression models were utilized to assess CAC incidence, and CAC progression. Ordinal regression models were used to evaluate the association between dietary patterns and CAC annual change groups. The proportional odds assumption was assessed using the Brant test. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% Cis were reported while adjusting for potential confounders. Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations (SD). Categorical variables were expressed as proportions. Models evaluating CAC incidence and CAC annual change were adjusted for baseline age, sex, race, smoking status, diabetes, hypertension, total energy intake (per 100 kcal), education level, eGFR, and lipid-lowering medication use. CAC progression models additionally included baseline CAC score as a covariate. Each dietary pattern derived from PCA was evaluated separately in individual models to avoid mutual adjustment between dietary patterns.

Assessment of effect modification by race/ethnicity

To evaluate whether the association between dietary patterns and CAC progression differed by race/ethnicity, we included multiplicative interaction terms between dietary pattern scores (modeled as continuous variables) and race/ethnicity in multivariable logistic regression models. Race/ethnicity was modeled as a categorical variable with White participants as the reference group, and statistical significance of interaction terms was assessed using Wald tests.

To facilitate interpretation, we estimated race/ethnicity-specific associations by combining the main effect of the dietary pattern score with the corresponding interaction term, and expressed results as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). This approach was chosen to maximize statistical efficiency, as estimation within a single model leverages the full sample size and provides more stable estimates compared with stratified analyses, which may be limited by reduced sample size and statistical power within subgroups. All models were adjusted for demographic and cardiovascular risk factors.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using R studio (version 2024.04.2 Build 764) packages.

Sensitivity analyses

For all primary analyses, we used a complete-case approach to handle missing data. However, because a substantial portion of loss to follow-up resulted from death and other forms of non-random attrition, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using inverse probability of censoring weighting. This approach accounts for informative dropouts and allows estimation of associations as if follow-up were complete. In addition, to address deaths that occurred before the follow-up CAC assessment, we created combined outcomes by incorporating death into the CAC progression variables, treating death as the worst category for both CAC progression status and CAC annual change rate. Ordinal regression models were then applied to these combined outcomes, reporting odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) while adjusting for potential confounders. For each outcome model, we additionally adjusted for BMI and physical activity. Besides, we conducted Cox proportional hazards regression analyses to evaluate the associations between each dietary PC and all-cause mortality and incident cardiovascular disease (CVD). Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported.

Results

Study population

Of the 6814 participants at baseline, 3182 had CAC measurements and completed the FFQ47 at Exam 1 as well as CAC measurements at Exam 5; these participants were included to investigate baseline dietary patterns and CAC progression. The characteristic description was shown in Table 1. Participants were followed for a mean of 9.6 ± 0.6 years. At baseline, the mean age was 60.2 ± 9.4 years, and 47.6% were male. The prevalence of diabetes was 10.1%, hypertension 41.2%, and ever smokers 48.1%. The racial and ethnic composition was 39.8% White, 12.1% Chinese, 26.1% Black, and 22.0% Hispanic/Latino. For outcomes: among 1850 participants with a CAC score of 0 at baseline, 850 developed incident calcifications. Among participants with baseline CAC, 24.9% experienced CAC progression during follow-up. Of these, 62.9% had slow progression, 17.5% had moderate progression, and 19.6% had fast progression. We also compared baseline characteristics of included versus excluded participants. These results demonstrate that excluded participants were older, had higher cardiometabolic risk factors and baseline CAC score, consistent with selective attrition due to death and dropout.

Association between dietary patterns and CAC outcomes

CAC incidence

In multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for baseline age, sex, race/ethnicity, smoking status, diabetes, hypertension, total energy intake, education, eGFR, and lipid-lowering medication use, the Whole Grains and Fruit dietary pattern was inversely associated with incident CAC when modeled continuously (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.80–0.99, P = 0.04). The High Fat and Processed Meat pattern was not significantly associated with incident CAC (OR 1.13, 95% CI 0.97–1.32, P = 0.12). Similarly, no significant associations were observed for the Vegetables and Fish pattern (OR 1.09, 95% CI 0.97–1.23, P = 0.15) or the Beans, Tomatoes, and Refined Grains pattern (OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.84–1.10, P = 0.55; Table 3.)

Annual CAC change rate

In ordinal logistic regression models examining tertiles of annual CAC change, the High Fat and Processed Meat pattern was positively associated with higher CAC change categories (OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.04–1.34, P = 0.01). The Whole Grains and Fruit pattern was inversely associated with higher CAC change categories (OR 0.91, 95% CI 0.83–0.99, P = 0.03). No significant associations were observed for the Vegetables and Fish pattern (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.89–1.10, P = 0.82) or the Beans, Tomatoes, and Refined Grains pattern (OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.92–1.34, P = 0.67) (Table 5).

Effect modification analysis across racial and ethnic groups

In race/ethnicity-stratified analyses, the High Fat and Processed Meat pattern was positively associated with the CAC progression among White participants (OR 1.38, 95% CI 1.12–1.70, P = 0.003), with a significant modification effect observed in Black participants (P-interaction=0.009), where the association was attenuated (OR 0.99). The Whole Grains and Fruit pattern was inversely associated with the CAC progression among White participants (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.64–0.92, P = 0.005), and this association differed significantly in Black participants (OR 1.16, 95% CI 0.92–1.47, P-interaction=0.006). No significant race/ethnicity modification effects were observed for the Vegetables and Fish pattern or the Beans, Tomatoes, and Refined Grains pattern, and no consistent associations were observed across racial groups for these patterns. Sensitivity analyses

Inverse probability of censoring weighting analyses

To address potential bias from dropout, we conducted inverse probability of censoring weighting analyses. Under IPCW, the associations between the High‑fat and Processed Meat pattern and the Whole Grains and Fruit pattern with incident CAC were no longer statistically significant (Supplementary Tables 1–4). These findings were consistent with the adjusted complete‑case analyses. Importantly, the High‑fat and Processed Meat dietary pattern remained associated with higher odds of annual CAC change and overall CAC progression, while the Whole Grains and Fruit pattern remained associated with lower odds of annual CAC change and CAC progression (Supplementary Tables 5–12).

Combined CAC annual change rate with all-cause death

We additionally created death-combined outcomes by treating death as the worst category. We also incorporated all cause death into the CAC annual change rate, the High-fat and Processed Meat pattern showed higher odd (OR = 1.13; 95% CI: 1.03–1.25) with boarder line P value(p = 0.009), and the Whole Grains and Fruit pattern showed lower odds (OR = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.81–0.93) (Supplementary Table 17–20).

Additional Cox models for all CVD events and all-cause mortality

We also conducted Cox models for all CVD events and all-cause mortality through 2020. Among the 6814 participants, there are1,303 CVD cases and 2113 all-cause death cases. After adjustment for the same covariates, the High-fat and Processed Meat dietary pattern showed a non-significant trend toward higher all-cause death hazard (HR = 1.06; 95% CI: 0.99–1.45; p = 0.06), the association was not significant with CVD risk. (Supplementary Table 21–24). In contrast, the Whole Grains and Fruit dietary pattern was associated with lower HRs of both all-cause mortality (HR = 0.91; 95% CI: 0.84–0.97) and CVD events (HR = 0.95; 95% CI: 0.90–0.99). Interestingly, Vegetables and Fish was associated with lower HR of all-cause death (HR = 0.91, 95%CI: 0.86 −0.98). (Supplementary Table 25–28).

Additional covariates adjustments (Physical activity and BMI)

We conducted sensitivity analyses including both physical activity measures and BMI in adjustments. The inclusion of physical activity did not materially alter the associations between dietary patterns and CAC outcomes. The inclusion of BMI attenuated the associations between dietary patterns and CAC outcomes. The association between the Whole Grains and Fruit dietary pattern and CAC incident is no longer significant. For CAC progression, OR for High-fat and Processed Meat pattern dropped (OR = 1.17; 95% CI: 0.99–1.40, P = 0.057), and OR for the Whole Grains and Fruit pattern stayed the same (OR = 0.879; 95% CI: 0.77–0.99, P = 0.048). Discussion

Central Illustration.

In this large, multiethnic community-based cohort, we identified four main dietary patterns at baseline—High Fat and Processed Meat, Vegetables and Fish, Whole Grains and Fruit, and Beans, Tomatoes, and Refined grains—consistent with prior studies. We examined their associations with CAC outcomes and found important differences across patterns. A High Fat and Processed Meat dietary pattern were positively associated with CAC progression, and annualized change rate in CAC score. In contrast, the Whole Grains and Fruit pattern was inversely associated with CAC incident, CAC progression and annual change rate. Our results highlight those dietary patterns and components, beyond their effects on traditional risk factors, play an important role in CAC prevention.

Importantly, sensitivity analyses using inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) revealed that associations between the High Fat and Processed Meat and Whole Grains and Fruit patterns with CAC incidence were attenuated and no longer statistically significant. These results suggest that incidence findings are more susceptible to dropout bias. In contrast, associations with CAC progression and annualized change remained robust under IPCW, underscoring the relative stability of progression findings. Finally, we note a discordance between IPCW-adjusted CAC incidence results and full-cohort Cox models of CVD events and all-cause mortality, High Fat and Processed Meat is not associated with CVD events and all-cause mortality, while Whole Grains/Fruit patterns and the Vegetables/Fish pattern are associated with lower hazard ratios, highlighting the broader relevance of diet beyond calcification endpoints and suggesting that diet may influence plaque biology and calcification differently than it influences clinical event risk. It is noteworthy that the High Fat and Processed Meat dietary pattern may contribute to cardiovascular disease risk primarily through its adverse effects on traditional risk factors such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes. At the same time, our findings suggest that this pattern also exerts an independent influence on coronary artery calcification, highlighting that diet may affect vascular mineralization beyond its impact on conventional cardiometabolic pathways. These findings emphasize the importance of considering both attrition bias and endpoint specificity when interpreting diet–CAC associations.

Compared with the earlier MESA study, which evaluated cross-sectional associations between dietary patterns and baseline CAC burden, our analysis uses long-term prediction models with more extensive confounder adjustment, indicated that dietary exposures may affect ongoing plaque development overtime rather than established calcification. Our findings align with several prior prospective studies [24]. In the six-year CACTI longitudinal cohort study of adults with and without type 1 diabetes, a dietary pattern rich in total fats, proteins, processed and red meats, and alcohol was significantly associated with a higher risk of both the presence and progression of coronary artery calcification (CAC), with all associations maintained in adjusted models [15]. The Heinz Nixdorf Recall study reported that a “Mediterranean-like” diet was linked to lower CAC burden and slower progression [14]. A retrospective cohort study from Korea further supported that lifestyle factors, including diet(high fat, high carbohydrate, low vegetable and fruit), were associated with CAC changes [16]. Similarly, a recent study demonstrated that dietary and lifestyle components link to higher inflammation scores (red and processed meats, refined grains, sugary foods/drinks, fried foods, high-fat dairy) were linked to increased incidence and progression of CAC [20]. Additional evidence has shown that low-carbohydrate diets, especially those rich in animal-based protein and fat, are associated with increased CAC progression [12]. Conversely, adherence to a DASH diet has been associated with lower risk of CAC development, particularly among South Asian men in the U.S [31]. The same result is reported that the DASH diet may be beneficial for lower odds of CAC progression in those without T1D [17].

The biological mechanisms underlying these associations are well established. Diets high in saturated and trans-fat can activate inflammatory pathways (e.g., cytokine release, NF-κB–related signaling), oxidative stress, and metabolic disturbances (dyslipidemia, insulin resistance), all of which promote osteogenic change in vascular cells and calcification [32]. Additionally, gut microbiota metabolizes l-carnitine from red meat into trimethylamine-N-oxide, which accelerates cholesterol deposition and vascular calcification [33]. In contrast, whole grains and plant-based diets offer multiple protective benefits. Whole grains improve lipid profiles, reduce inflammation, stabilize blood glucose, and provide antioxidants, all of which attenuate CAC development. They also promote favorable gut microbiome through fiber fermentation and short-chain fatty acid production [34]. Importantly, plant-based foods are rich in vitamins K and E, magnesium, and folic acid—nutrients that have been shown to protect against vascular calcification [[35], [36], [37], [38]].

Another novel aspect of our study is that there is a racial heterogeneity in diet–CAC progression associations persisted after adjustment for baseline CAC, suggesting that differences are unlikely to be explained solely by lower initial calcification burden among Black participants. Coronary artery calcification represents an actively regulated osteogenic process within established atherosclerotic plaques, involving vascular smooth muscle cell differentiation, inflammatory signaling, and mineral metabolism pathways. Adjustment for baseline CAC isolates progression or incident calcification beyond pre-existing plaque burden, indicating that dietary patterns may relate differently to calcification dynamics across racial groups. Prior studies have demonstrated lower calcification propensity in Black individuals despite comparable or higher cardiovascular risk [21], supporting the possibility that differences in vascular mineralization biology, rather than overall atherosclerosis, may contribute to attenuated or divergent diet–CAC associations. These findings highlight that CAC reflects a specific calcification phenotype and that dietary influences on plaque mineralization may vary across populations even after accounting for initial calcification status. However, statistically significant interactions may sometimes reflect differences in estimate precision across subgroups rather than true etiologic heterogeneity, that larger studies with greater numbers of progression events and more diverse populations are needed to validate these interactions.

Strengths of this study include its large, multiethnic, community-based design, long follow-up, and comprehensive adjustment for confounders. We derived dietary patterns empirically using PCA and incorporated longitudinal changes in diet, providing a dynamic view of diet–CAC associations.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, as with all observational analyses, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and causal inference is limited despite extensive adjustment for traditional risk factors. Second, attrition and competing risks may have influenced our results. Although inverse probability of censoring weighting was applied to mitigate dropout bias, associations between dietary patterns and CAC incidence were attenuated under IPCW, underscoring the sensitivity of incidence findings to attrition. In contrast, progression associations remained robust, highlighting endpoint-specific differences. Third, we observed a discordance between IPCW-adjusted CAC incidence results and full-cohort Cox models of cardiovascular events, suggesting that diet may influence calcification biology differently than it influences clinical event risk. Fourth, interpretation of race and ethnicity interactions is complex. While heterogeneity persisted after adjustment for baseline CAC, structural and biological factors may both contribute, and subgroup sample sizes limit precision. Fifth, exclusions and selection bias may have affected generalizability, as participants without complete dietary or imaging data differed in baseline characteristics. Finally, dietary intake was self-reported, which introduces measurement error and may not fully capture relevant aspects of diet. Together, these limitations provide important context for interpreting our findings and highlight the need for replication in diverse cohorts and complementary evidence from randomized trials.

Conclusion

A High Fat and Processed Meat dietary pattern is associated with increased risk of CAC progression. In contrast, a whole grain and fruit-based dietary pattern is associated with lower risks of CAC incidence and progression. Moreover, our findings suggest possible racial and ethnic heterogeneity in diet–CAC associations. In conclusion, reducing high-fat and processed meats and increasing whole grains and fruits may help prevent CAC progression, and highlight the potential to personalize preventive strategies according to population context and clinical outcomes.
