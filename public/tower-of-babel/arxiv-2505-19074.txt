Uniqueness and nonuniqueness of p p\mspace{1mu} -harmonic Green functions on weighted 𝐑 n \mathbf{R}^{n} and metric spaces
Anders Björn

Abstract




Abstract.

We study uniqueness
of
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic Green functions in
domains
\Omega
in
a complete metric space equipped with a doubling measure
supporting a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality,
with
1<p<\infty
.
For bounded domains in unweighted
\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
the uniqueness was shown for the
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplace operator
\Delta_{p}
and all
p
by
Kichenassamy–Véron (
Math. Ann.

275
(1986), 599–615),
while for
p=2
it is an easy consequence of the linearity of the Laplace
operator
\Delta
.
Beyond that, uniqueness is only known in some particular cases, such as in
Ahlfors
p\mspace{1mu}
-regular spaces, as shown by
Bonk–Capogna–Zhou (
arXiv:2211.11974
).
When the singularity
x_{0}
has positive
p\mspace{1mu}
-capacity,
the Green function is a particular multiple
of the capacitary potential for
\capp_{p}(\{x_{0}\},\Omega)
and is therefore unique.
Here we give a sufficient condition for uniqueness in metric spaces, and provide
an example showing that the range of
p
for which it holds
(while
x_{0}
has zero
p\mspace{1mu}
-capacity) can be a nondegenerate interval.
In the opposite direction, we give the first example
showing that uniqueness can fail in metric spaces, even for
p=2
.

Key words and phrases
:
doubling measure,
metric space,
nonuniqueness,

p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic Green function,
Poincaré inequality,
weighted Euclidean space,
uniqueness.

(2020):
Primary:
35J08, Secondary:

1
Introduction

In the Euclidean space
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, a function
u
is said to be
the (
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic) Green function for a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
with singularity at
x_{0}\in\Omega

if it is the weak solution of the equation

-\Delta_{p}u=\delta_{x_{0}}\quad\text{in $\Omega$}

(1.1)

with the Dirac measure
\delta_{x_{0}}
in the right-hand side and zero boundary values on
\partial\Omega

in a weak sense.
Here

\Delta_{p}u:=\text{div}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u),\quad 1<p<\infty,

is the
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplace operator.
The Green function is
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic outside of its singularity,
that is, it satisfies the
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplace equation
\Delta_{p}u=0
.

In metric spaces, Holopainen–Shanmugalingam
[
22
]
introduced
a notion of singular functions which extended the notion of Green functions from

\mathbf{R}^{n}
and Riemannian manifolds to more general settings.
More precise definitions and various characterizations of Green functions in metric spaces
were later given in Björn–Björn–Lehrbäck
[
7
]

and Björn–Björn
[
5
]
,
see Definitions
4.1
and
4.4
below.
For domains in complete metric spaces
X=(X,d)
,
equipped with a doubling measure
\mu
supporting a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality
with
1<p<\infty
,
it was shown in
[
5
]
and
[
7
]
that
Green functions exist in a domain
\Omega

if and only if the
p\mspace{1mu}
-capacity
{C_{p}}(X\setminus\Omega)>0

or
X
is
p\mspace{1mu}
-hyperbolic.

Roughly speaking, by
[
7
, Theorem 8.5]
,
u:\Omega\to(0,\infty]
is
a (
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic)
Green function
with singularity at
x_{0}\in\Omega
for a (bounded) domain
\Omega

in a metric space
X
if it is
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic in

\Omega\setminus\{x_{0}\}
, has zero boundary values on
\partial\Omega

in a suitable sense and is properly normalized
so that

\capp_{p}(\{x:u(x)\geq b\},\Omega)=b^{1-p},\quad\text{when }0<b<u(x_{0})=\lim_{x\to x_{0}}u(x).

This normalization captures in a quantitative way the right-hand side
\delta_{x_{0}}
in (
1.1
).

In this paper we study whether such Green functions in metric spaces are unique.
Our first result answers this question in the affirmative
in spaces satisfying a critical volume growth condition.
Here
B_{r}=B(x_{0},r)=\{y\in X:d(y,x_{0})<r\}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X
be a complete metric space and assume that
\mu
is doubling
and supports a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality on
X
, where
1<p<\infty
.
Assume that

\limsup_{r\to 0}\biggl(\frac{\mu(B_{r})}{r^{p}}\biggr)^{1/(p-1)}\int_{r}^{1}\biggl(\frac{\rho^{p}}{\mu(B_{\rho})}\biggr)^{1/(p-1)}\frac{d\rho}{\rho}=\infty.

(1.2)

If
u
and
v
are
(
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic
)
Green functions in a domain
\Omega
with singularity at
x_{0}\in\Omega
,
then
u=v
, i.e. the Green function in
\Omega
with singularity at
x_{0}
is unique.

Remark 1.2
.

When
{C_{p}}(\{x_{0}\})>0
, the Green function is a particular multiple
of the capacitary potential for
\capp_{p}(\{x_{0}\},\Omega)
and is therefore unique, see
Björn–Björn–Lehrbäck
[
7
, Theorem 1.3]

(for bounded
\Omega
) and
Björn–Björn
[
5
, Corollary 10.3]

(for general
\Omega
).

Theorem
1.1

provides a new sufficient condition (
1.2
) for the uniqueness of the Green function in the case
{C_{p}}(\{x_{0}\})=0
. When the space
X
is Ahlfors
Q
-regular and
p=Q
, (
1.2
) is satisfied and our result recovers the uniqueness of Green functions on a bounded regular
domain
\Omega\subset X
in Bonk–Capogna–Zhou
[
10
, Theorem 1.1(ii) and Remark 1.1]
.

More generally, condition (
1.2
) holds if
p>q_{0}
, where

q_{0}:=\sup\biggl\{q>0:\frac{\mu(B_{r})}{\mu(B_{R})}\lesssim\Bigl(\frac{r}{R}\Bigr)^{q}\text{ for all }0<r<R\leq 1\biggr\},

see
Proposition
5.3
1
.
Example
5.4
shows that
the range of new exponents
p

covered by Theorem
1.1

can be a nondegenerate arbitrarily large interval.

At the same time, in Example
6.1
we prove the following nonuniqueness result
with a
p\mspace{1mu}
-admissible weight in
\mathbf{R}^{2}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and either
\Omega=\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{2}:|x|<R\}

or
\Omega=\mathbf{R}^{2}
.

Then there is a metric
d
on
\mathbf{R}^{2}
, biLipschitz equivalent to the Euclidean metric,
and a doubling
measure
d\mu=w\,dx
supporting a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality,
such that for the metric space
X:=(\mathbf{R}^{2},d,\mu)

there are uncountably many
(
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic
)

Green functions in
\Omega
with a singularity at
0
,
that is, the Green functions in
\Omega
are not unique.

The metric
d
is independent of
p
and
\Omega
.
If
1<p<2
then we can choose
w\equiv 1
.

For domains in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, equipped with the Euclidean metric and the Lebesgue measure,
Green functions are known to be unique.
For
p=2
, this is an easy consequence of the linearity of the Laplace operator and the maximum
principle for harmonic functions.
When
p\neq 2
, the
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplace operator is nonlinear and the above argument does not apply.
In this case, uniqueness was proved only in 1986 by
Kichenassamy–Véron
[
24
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Beyond that, uniqueness has been shown in the following cases:
for regular relatively compact domains in
n
-dimensional
Riemannian manifolds (and
p=n
) by Holopainen
[
21
, Theorem 3.22]
,
and more
recently for bounded regular domains in Ahlfors
Q
-regular metric spaces (and
p=Q
)
in Bonk–Capogna–Zhou
[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
.
The uniqueness also holds when
{C_{p}}(\{x_{0}\})>0
, by

[
5
, Corollary 10.3]
and
[
7
, Theorem 1.3]
,
since the Green function is then
just a suitable multiple of the capacitary potential for
\{x_{0}\}
in
\Omega
.

The
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions

in a general metric measure space
(X,d,\mu)
are defined as
continuous local minimizers of the
p\mspace{1mu}
-energy integral

\int_{\Omega}g_{u}^{p}\,d\mu,

(1.3)

where
g_{u}
is the minimal
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient of
u
,
see Sections
2
and
3
.
This is a natural generalization of the fact that the
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplace equation
\Delta_{p}u=0
in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
is the Euler–Lagrange
equation for minimizers of the
p\mspace{1mu}
-energy
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p}\,dx
.

If
X
is equipped with a smooth differentiable structure, such as for
Riemannian manifolds, then one can derive
an Euler–Lagrange equation which characterizes
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions.
In particular, if
\mathbf{R}^{n}
is equipped with a
p\mspace{1mu}
-admissible weight
w
,
as in Heinonen–Kilpeläinen–Martio
[
17
]
,
then
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions are local minimizers of the weighted
p\mspace{1mu}
-energy integral

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u(x)|^{p}w(x)\,dx

and solve the weighted
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplace equation

\dvg(w(x)|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)=0.

In general metric measure spaces, such a description is not always possible –
see however Gigli–Mondino
[
15
]
.

A seminal result of Cheeger
[
11
]
states that every
complete metric measure space
(X,d,\mu)
,
with
\mu
doubling and supporting a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality, can
be equipped with a
measurable differentiable structure
.
In such cases, the
p\mspace{1mu}
-energy can be modified to yield an Euler–Lagrange-type equation.
This is obtained via differential calculus, as developed in
[
11
]
.
Namely, one can define a vector-valued differential
Du
,
as a measurable section of a measurable tangent bundle,
whose pointwise Euclidean norm
|Du|
is comparable to the minimal
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient
g_{u}
of
u
.
For such a choice of a fixed measurable differentiable structure,
functions minimizing the Cheeger
p\mspace{1mu}
-energy

\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{p}\,d\mu

satisfy the equation

\int_{\Omega}|Du|^{p-2}Du\cdot D\varphi=0\quad\text{for all functions $\varphi\in\Lip_{0}(\Omega)$}

(1.4)

and their continuous representatives are called
Cheeger
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions
.

If
X
is infinitesimally Hilbertian, as in e.g.
[
15
]
, the inner product in (
1.4
)
can be chosen in such a way that

|Du|^{2}:=Du\cdot Du=g_{u}^{2}

at almost every point, see also
[
11
, Theorem 6.1 and Corollary 4.41]
.
In this case, Cheeger
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic and (upper gradient)
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions
coincide, see
[
15
, Theorem 4.2]
.
This case occurs for example in all analytically
one-dimensional cases, such as the Laakso spaces
considered in
[
27
]
, see also
Cheeger–Kleiner
[
12
, Theorem 9.1]
.

On the other hand, in Theorem
1.3

we do not have a scalar product, and as a result,
the (upper gradient)
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic Green functions
differ significantly from the Cheeger
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic Green functions.
From the metric perspective, the space
(\mathbf{R}^{2},d)
in Theorem
1.3

is biLipschitz equivalent to the Euclidean space,
but from a PDE point of view their Laplacians are very different.
The metric
d

is defined through an
\ell^{1}
norm in the tangent bundle
and is similar to a Finsler structure studied in Finsler geometry
– see e.g.
[
1
]
,
[
13
]
and
[
28
]
for introductions to this vast field.

The notion of
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions
(based on (
1.3
))
only depends on the metric
and the measure of the underlying space.
In contrast, the definition of Cheeger
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions also depends on the choice
of a measurable differentiable structure.
In particular, when the space fails to be infinitesimally Hilbertian,
the upper gradient
g_{u}
in (
1.3
)
is only comparable to the
|Du|
norm and there is no Euler–Lagrange equation for
the
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions
(based on (
1.3
)), which therefore differ from the
Cheeger
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions.

Note, however, that any “positive”
result proved for the energy minimizing
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions
(such as our uniqueness Theorem
1.1
)
holds also for the Cheeger
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions.
Indeed, it suffices to replace
g_{u}
by
|Du|
in all the proofs.
In particular, Theorem
1.1
provides new uniqueness
results for Green functions in weighted
\mathbf{R}^{n}
as in Heinonen–Kilpeläinen–Martio
[
17
]
,
cf. Example
5.4
.
However, we do not know whether nonuniqueness as in Theorem
1.3

can be obtained for Cheeger
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic Green functions.

The examples in Theorem
1.3

also yield counterexamples to other problems.
The strong comparison principle for the
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplace operator
\Delta_{p}
,

1<p<\infty
, states that
if
u
and
v
are two
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions in a domain
\Omega
satisfying
u\geq v
,
then either
u>v
in
\Omega
or
u\equiv v
in
\Omega
.
When
p=2
, this is a direct consequence of the linearity of the Laplace operator
and the maximum principle for harmonic functions.
For the nonlinear
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplace operator when
p\neq 2
, this argument does not hold.
Using the theory of quasiregular mappings,
Manfredi
[
29
, Theorem 2]
proved the strong comparison principle
for planar domains (in unweighted
\mathbf{R}^{2}
).
In higher dimensional Euclidean spaces
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, with
n\geq 3
,
the strong comparison principle is still an open problem.
In metric spaces, we have the following negative result.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
1<p<\infty
.
Then there is a metric
d
on
\mathbf{R}^{2}
, biLipschitz equivalent to the Euclidean metric,
and a
p\mspace{1mu}
-admissible measure
d\mu=w\,dx
,
such that the following hold for
X=(\mathbf{R}^{2},d,\mu)
:

1.

The strong comparison principle
fails for
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions in the punctured unit disc
\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{2}:0<|x|<1\}
.

2.

The theory for
2
-harmonic functions is nonlinear
:

there are
2
-harmonic functions
u
and
v

in the punctured unit disc
\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{2}:0<|x|<1\}
such
that
u+v
is not
2
-harmonic.

The paper is organized in the following way.
Section
2
contains some definitions and preliminary results
about upper gradients and Newtonian functions.
In Section
3
,
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions and some auxiliary results for them are presented.
Section
4
deals with the definitions of
singular and Green functions and their properties.
The uniqueness Theorem
1.1
is proved in Section
5
,
while Theorem
1.3
and Corollary
1.4
are proved in
Section
6
.

2
Upper gradients and Newtonian spaces

We assume throughout the paper that
1<p<\infty
and that

X=(X,d,\mu)
is a metric space equipped
with a metric
d
and a positive complete Borel measure
\mu

such that
0<\mu(B)<\infty
for all balls
B\subset X
.
We also assume that
\diam X>0
. Note that it follows from these assumptions that
X
is separable.
Additional standing
assumptions are added at the beginning of Section
3
.

In this section, we introduce
the necessary metric space concepts used in this paper.
Proofs of most of the results mentioned in
this section can be found in the monographs
Björn–Björn
[
4
]
and
Heinonen–Koskela–Shanmugalingam–Tyson
[
20
]
.

We denote by
B(x,r):=\{y\in X:d(x,y)<r\}

the open ball of radius
r>0
centred at
x\in X
.
For
B:=B(x,r)
we use the notation
\lambda B=B(x,\lambda r)
.
In some parts, we will also write
B_{r}
, when the centre of the ball is fixed.
In metric spaces
it can happen that balls with different centres or
radii denote the same set.
We will, however,
make the convention that a ball
B
comes with a predetermined
centre and radius.
Unless said otherwise, balls are assumed to be open in this paper.
By
{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}}
we mean the closure of the open ball
B=B(x,r)
,
not the (possibly larger) set
\{y:d(x,y)\leq r\}
.
We write
\diam A
for the diameter of a set
A\subset X
.

The measure
\mu
is
doubling

if there exists a
doubling constant

C_{\mu}\geq 1
such that

\mu(2B)\leq C_{\mu}\mu(B)\quad\text{for every open ball }B.

The measure
\mu
is
Ahlfors
Q
-regular

if there is a constant
C\geq 1
such that

C^{-1}r^{Q}\leq\mu(B(x,r))\leq Cr^{Q}\quad\text{for all $x\in X$ and $0<r<2\diam X$}.

A
curve
is a continuous mapping from an interval,
and a
rectifiable
curve is a curve with finite length.
A rectifiable curve can be parameterized by its arc length
ds
.
A property holds for
p\mspace{1mu}
-almost every curve

if the curve family
\Gamma
for which it fails has zero
p\mspace{1mu}
-modulus,
i.e. there is
\rho\in L^{p}(X)
such that

\int_{\gamma}\rho\,ds=\infty
for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
u:X\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}:=[-\infty,\infty]
be a function.
A Borel function
g:X\to[0,\infty]

is an
upper gradient
of
u

if for all nonconstant rectifiable curves

\gamma:[0,l_{\gamma}]\to X
,

|u(\gamma(0))-u(\gamma(l_{\gamma}))|\leq\int_{\gamma}g\,ds,

(2.1)

where the left-hand side is considered to be
\infty

whenever at least one of the
terms therein is infinite.
A measurable function
g:X\to[0,\infty]

is a
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient
of
u

if it satisfies (
2.1
) for
p\mspace{1mu}
-almost every
nonconstant rectifiable
curve
\gamma
.

The upper gradients were introduced in
Heinonen–Koskela
[
18
]
,
[
19
]

and
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradients in Koskela–MacManus
[
26
]
.
It was also shown in
[
26
]

that if
g\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}(X)
is a
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient of
u
,
then one can find a sequence
\{g_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

of upper gradients of
u
such that
\|g_{j}-g\|_{L^{p}(X)}\to 0
.
If
u
has an upper gradient in
L^{p}_{\rm loc}(X)
, then
it has an a.e. unique
minimal
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient

g_{u}\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}(X)

in the sense that
g_{u}\leq g
a.e. for every

p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradient
g\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}(X)
of
u
,
see Shanmugalingam
[
31
]
.

Following Shanmugalingam
[
30
]
,
we define a version of Sobolev spaces on the metric space
X
.

Definition 2.2
.

For a measurable function
u:X\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
,
let

\|u\|_{N^{1,p}(X)}=\biggl(\int_{X}|u|^{p}\,d\mu+\inf_{g}\int_{X}g^{p}\,d\mu\biggr)^{1/p},

where the infimum is taken over all upper gradients
g
of
u
.
The
Newtonian space
on
X
is

N^{1,p}(X)=\{u:\|u\|_{N^{1,p}(X)}<\infty\}.

The quotient
space
N^{1,p}(X)/{\sim}
, where
u\sim v
if and only if
\|u-v\|_{N^{1,p}(X)}=0
,
is a Banach space and a lattice, see Shanmugalingam
[
30
]
.

In this paper it is convenient to assume that functions in
N^{1,p}(X)

are defined everywhere (with values in
{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
),
not just up to an equivalence class in the corresponding quotient space.
This is important for upper gradients and
p\mspace{1mu}
-weak upper gradients to make sense,
and is also convenient in other places, e.g. when defining the capacities below.

We say that
u\in N^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(X)
if
for every
x\in X
there is a ball

B(x,r_{x})

such that
u\in N^{1,p}(B(x,r_{x}))
.
If
u,v\in N^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(X)
, then
g_{u}=g_{v}
a.e. in
\{x\in X:u(x)=v(x)\}
.
In particular
g_{\min\{u,c\}}=g_{u}\chi_{\{u<c\}}
a.e. in
X

for
c\in\mathbf{R}
, where
\chi
denotes the characteristic function.
For any nonempty open set
\Omega\subset X
,
the spaces
N^{1,p}(\Omega)
and
N^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
are defined by
considering
(\Omega,d|_{\Omega},\mu|_{\Omega})
as a metric space in its own right.

The
Sobolev capacity
of an arbitrary set
E\subset X
is

{C_{p}}(E)=\inf_{u}\|u\|_{N^{1,p}(X)}^{p},

where the infimum is taken over all
u\in N^{1,p}(X)
such that

u\geq 1
on
E
.
The Sobolev capacity is countably subadditive.

A property of
points
x\in X
holds
quasieverywhere
(q.e.) if it is true outside a set of capacity zero.
The Sobolev capacity is the correct gauge
for distinguishing between two Newtonian functions.
If
u\in N^{1,p}(X)
, then
v\sim u
if and only if
v=u
q.e.
Moreover, if
u,v\in N^{1,p}(X)
and
u=v
a.e., then
u=v
q.e.

The Newtonian space with zero boundary values is defined by

N^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega):=\{f|_{\Omega}:f\in N^{1,p}(X)\text{ and }f=0\text{ on }X\setminus\Omega\}.

Definition 2.3
.

Let

Definition 2.4
.

3
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic and superharmonic functions

In addition to the assumptions from the beginning of Section
2
,
we assume from now on that

X
is a complete metric space
equipped with a doubling measure
\mu
that
supports a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality, where
1<p<\infty
.
We also fix a point
x_{0}\in X
, write
B_{r}=B(x_{0},r)
,
and assume that
\Omega\subset X
is a nonempty open set.

It follows from these assumptions that
X
is proper
(i.e. every closed bounded set is compact) and connected.
Moreover,
X
is
quasiconvex
, i.e. there is a constant
C

such that for every pair of points
x,y\in X
there is a rectifiable curve
\gamma

from
x
to
y
with length
\ell_{\gamma}\leq Cd(x,y)
.
In particular
X
is locally connected, and components of open sets
are open.
See e.g.
[
4
, Proposition 3.1 and Theorem 4.32]
or

[
20
, Lemma 4.1.14 and Theorem 8.3.2]
.

A function
u\in N^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
is a

minimizer
in
\Omega

if

\int_{\varphi\neq 0}g^{p}_{u}\,d\mu\leq\int_{\varphi\neq 0}g_{u+\varphi}^{p}\,d\mu\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in N^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega).

(3.1)

Equivalently, one can require (
3.1
)
only for
\varphi\in{\Lip_{c}}(\Omega)
,
see Björn
[
3
, Proposition 3.2]

(or
[
4
, Proposition 7.9]
) for
this
and other characterizations.
A continuous minimizer is called a
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic function
.
(It follows from Kinnunen–Shanmugalingam
[
25
]
that
every minimizer has a continuous representative.)

Definition 3.1
.

A function
u:\Omega\to(-\infty,\infty]
is

superharmonic
in
\Omega
if

(i).

u
is lower semicontinuous;

(ii).

u
is not identically
\infty
in any component of
\Omega
;

(iii).

for every nonempty open set
G\Subset\Omega
with
{C_{p}}(X\setminus G)>0

and every function
v\in C({\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}})
which is
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic in
G
and such
that
v\leq u
on
\partial G
, we have
v\leq u
in
G
.

This definition of superharmonicity is the same as the one
usually used in the Euclidean literature, e.g. in
Heinonen–Kilpeläinen–Martio
[
17
, Section 7]
.
It is equivalent to other definitions of superharmonicity on metric spaces,
by Theorem 6.1 in Björn
[
2
]

(or
[
4
, Theorem 14.10]
).
It is not difficult to see that a function
u
is
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic if and only if
both
u
and
-u
are superharmonic.

The Harnack
inequality for nonnegative
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic function was obtained
in Kinnunen–Shanmugalingam
[
25
, Corollary 7.3]
using De Giorgi’s method
(see however
[
9
, Section 10]
or
[
4
, Section 8.4]
for
some necessary modifications).
A different proof, using Moser iteration, was given by
Björn–Marola
[
9
, Theorem 9.3]
,
and one using a combination of both methods by
Björn–Björn
[
4
, Theorem 8.12]
.
The strong maximum principle is a direct consequence of the Harnack inequality.

Theorem 3.2
.

(Harnack’s inequality)

There exists a constant
A>0
depending only on
p
and
the doubling and Poincaré constants
C_{\mu}
,
C_{\rm PI}
and
\lambda
,
such that for any
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic function
u\geq 0
in
\Omega
, one has

\sup_{B}u\leq A\inf_{B}u,

for every ball
B\subset 50\lambda B\subset\Omega
.

We need the following version of Harnack’s inequality, which is obtained by iteration.

Proposition 3.3
.

There are constants
C_{0},\alpha>0
, depending only on
p
and
the doubling and Poincaré constants
C_{\mu}
,
C_{\rm PI}
and
\lambda
,
such that if
u\geq 0
is
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic in a ball
B
, then for all
0<\delta\leq 1/50\lambda
,

\sup_{\delta B}u\leq(1+C_{0}\delta^{\alpha})\inf_{\delta B}u.

Let
k
be the smallest integer such that
\delta>(50\lambda)^{-k-2}
.
Note that
k\geq 0
.
For
j=0,1,\dots,k+1
, let

B^{j}=(50\lambda)^{j}\delta B,\quad M_{j}=\sup_{B^{j}}u\quad\text{and}\quad m_{j}=\inf_{B^{j}}u.

Then
u-m_{j+1}
is a nonnegative
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic function in

B^{j+1}=50\lambda B^{j}\subset B
.
Let
A
be the constant in Harnack’s inequality (Theorem
3.2
).
Thus

M_{j}-m_{j+1}\leq A(m_{j}-m_{j+1})

and hence

A(M_{j}-m_{j})=(A-1)M_{j}+M_{j}-Am_{j}\leq(A-1)(M_{j}-m_{j+1}).

Dividing by
Am_{j+1}
, we get

\frac{M_{j}}{m_{j}}-1\leq\frac{M_{j}-m_{j}}{m_{j+1}}\leq\frac{(A-1)(M_{j}-m_{j+1})}{Am_{j+1}}\leq\frac{A-1}{A}\biggl(\frac{M_{j+1}}{m_{j+1}}-1\biggr).

Iterating this inequality and another use of the Harnack inequality yields

\frac{M_{0}}{m_{0}}-1\leq\biggl(\frac{A-1}{A}\biggr)^{k}\biggl(\frac{M_{k}}{m_{k}}-1\biggr)\leq\biggl(\frac{A-1}{A}\biggr)^{k+1}A\leq C_{0}\delta^{\alpha},

where
\alpha\log 50\lambda=\log(A/(A-1))>0

and
C_{0}=A(50\lambda)^{\alpha}
.
∎

The following lemma will serve as
a substitute for the Loewner-type estimate
[
10
, Lemmas 2.11 and 2.12]

used in the proof of Lemma 3.4 in
Bonk–Capogna–Zhou
[
10
]
for
Q
-harmonic functions
in Ahlfors

-regular spaces.
Here we do not assume
Ahlfors regularity and consider
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions for any

.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
u
be a superharmonic function in a domain
\Omega
, which
is
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic in
\Omega\setminus{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}}
for some ball
B=B(x_{0},r)
such that

4B\subset\Omega
.
Let

M=\sup_{3B\setminus 2B}u\quad\text{and}\quad m=\inf_{3B\setminus 2B}u

and assume that
u
is normalized so that for all
m\leq a<b\leq M
,

\int_{\{x\in\Omega:a<u(x)<b\}}g_{u}^{p}\,d\mu=b-a.

(3.2)

If
\Omega_{M}:=\{x\in\Omega:u(x)>M\}\Subset\Omega
, then

M-m\lesssim\biggl(\frac{\mu(B)}{r^{p}}\biggr)^{1/(1-p)},

where the comparison constant in
\lesssim
only depends on
p
and
the doubling and
Poincaré constants

C_{\mu}
,
C_{\rm PI}
and
\lambda
.

By the lower semicontinuity and the strong
minimum principle for superharmonic functions

[
4
, Theorem 9.13]
,
we see that

m=\min_{\overline{3B}}u

is attained at some
x_{m}\in\partial(3B)
and that

3B\subset\{x\in\Omega:u(x)\geq m\}
.

Next, let
G
be a component of
\Omega_{M}
.
Since
u\leq M
in
3B\setminus 2B
, we have
either
G\subset 2B
or
G\subset\Omega\setminus 3B
.
However, the latter is impossible because of the strong maximum principle for
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions
and the assumption that
G\Subset\Omega
.
We therefore conclude that
\Omega_{M}\subset 2B
and
by the continuity of
u
in
\Omega\setminus{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}}
, there is
x_{M}\in\partial(2B)
such that
M=u(x_{M})
.

Next, note that
u-m
and
M-u
are nonnegative
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions in

B^{M}:=B(x_{M},r)\subset 3B\setminus B\quad\text{and}\quad B^{m}:=B(x_{m},r)\subset 4B\setminus 2B,

respectively.
Proposition
3.3
then provides us with

0<\delta<1
,
depending only on

p
,
C_{\mu}
,
C_{\rm PI}
and
\lambda
,
so that

\sup_{\delta B^{M}}(u-m)\leq\frac{4}{3}\inf_{\delta B^{M}}(u-m)\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\delta B^{m}}(M-u)\leq\frac{4}{3}\inf_{\delta B^{m}}(M-u).

Then

M^{\prime}:=\inf_{\delta B^{M}}u\geq m+\frac{3}{4}(M-m)\quad\text{and}\quad m^{\prime}:=\sup_{\delta B^{m}}u\leq M-\frac{3}{4}(M-m).

Subtracting the second inequality from the first, we get that

M^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}(M-m).

(3.3)

Next, let
v=\min\{M^{\prime},\max\{m^{\prime},u\}\}
be the truncation of
u
at
the levels
m^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime}
.
Then, by the doubling property of
\mu
,

\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{4B}|v-v_{4B}|\,d\mu\geq\frac{1}{2}(M^{\prime}-m^{\prime})\frac{\min\{\mu(\delta B^{M}),\mu(\delta B^{m})\}}{\mu(4B)}\gtrsim M^{\prime}-m^{\prime}.

Inserting this into the
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality for
v
in
4B
implies that

M^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\lesssim r\biggl(\mathop{\mathchoice{\kern 1.28333pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.78336pt}{\kern 1.16664pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.50002pt}\kern-4.66666pt}{\kern 1.02428pt\vbox{\hrule width=3.07292pt}\kern-4.09721pt}{\kern 0.96526pt\vbox{\hrule width=2.89586pt}\kern-3.86111pt}}\mathopen{}\int_{4\lambda B}g_{v}^{p}\,d\mu\biggr)^{1/p}\lesssim\frac{r}{\mu(B)^{1/p}}\biggl(\int_{\{x\in\Omega:m^{\prime}<u(x)<M^{\prime}\}}g_{u}^{p}\,d\mu\biggr)^{1/p},

where
\lambda
is the dilation constant in the
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality.
Note that
m\leq m^{\prime}\leq M^{\prime}\leq M
.
The normalization (
3.2
)
then yields

M^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\lesssim\frac{r}{\mu(B)^{1/p}}(M^{\prime}-m^{\prime})^{1/p}

and hence

M^{\prime}-m^{\prime}\lesssim\biggl(\frac{r^{p}}{\mu(B)}\biggr)^{1/(p-1)}.

Finally, (
3.3
) concludes the proof.

4
Singular and Green functions

Green functions are properly normalized singular functions, see
Definition
4.5
and also Theorem
4.6
below
for the close relation between singular and Green functions.
We begin by defining singular functions.
The following definition was formulated in
Björn–Björn–Lehrbäck
[
7
]
for bounded
\Omega
.

Definition 4.1
.

(
[
7
, Definition 1.1]
)
Let
\Omega\subset X
be a bounded domain.
A positive
function
u:\Omega\to(0,\infty]
is a
singular function
in
\Omega

with singularity at
x_{0}\in\Omega
if it satisfies the following
properties:

(S1).

u
is
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic in
\Omega\setminus\{x_{0}\}
;

(S2).

u
is superharmonic in
\Omega
;

(S3).

u(x_{0})=\sup_{\Omega}u
;

(S4).

\inf_{\Omega}u=0
;

(S5).

{\tilde{u}}\in N^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(X\setminus\{x_{0}\})
, where

{\tilde{u}}=\begin{cases}u&\text{in }\Omega,\\
0&\text{on }X\setminus\Omega.\end{cases}

The last condition
(S5)
says that
u
has “zero boundary values
in the Sobolev sense”.
This makes it possible to treat arbitrary bounded domains
\Omega
and not only
regular bounded domains, where the zero boundary data are attained
as limits
\lim_{\Omega\ni y\to x}u(y)=0
for all
x\in\partial\Omega
.

However, Definition
4.1

is not suitable for unbounded domains
\Omega
, including
X
itself, since
the condition
{\tilde{u}}\in N^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(X\setminus\{x_{0}\})

does not capture the zero boundary condition at
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
.
This is demonstrated by the following example.
To simplify the notation, we use
\lim_{x\to{\boldsymbol{\infty}}}
as a shorthand
for
\lim_{d(x,x_{0})\to\infty}
.

Example 4.2
.

Let
X=\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
n>2=p
and
\Omega=\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle B(0,1)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(0,1)}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle B(0,1)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(0,1)}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle B(0,1)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(0,1)}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle B(0,1)}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B(0,1)}}}
.
Let
u
be the classical
2
-harmonic Green function
in
\Omega
with singularity at some
x_{0}\in\Omega
.
Then
\lim_{x\to{\boldsymbol{\infty}}}u(x)=0
and
u
satisfies
Definition
4.1
.
Let
c>0
.
Then also the function
{\bar{u}}(x):=u(x)+c(1-|x|^{2-n})
satisfies the conditions in
Definition
4.1
, but

\lim_{x\to{\boldsymbol{\infty}}}{\bar{u}}(x)=c>0
is not desirable
for Green functions, since
\mathbf{R}^{n}
is
2
-hyperbolic
in the sense of Definition
4.7
.

Replacing
{\tilde{u}}\in N^{1,p}_{\rm loc}(X\setminus\{x_{0}\})

in Definition
4.1
by

{\tilde{u}}\in N^{1,p}(X\setminus{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle B}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{B}}}_{r})
for every
r>0

does not help since the desired Green function (such as
u(x)=c|x|^{(p-n)/(p-1)}

in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
) is typically not
L^{p}
-integrable at
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
.

Thus, for unbounded domains, the zero boundary value at
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
needs to be captured
in a different way than by Newtonian spaces.
One way of doing this simultaneously both for finite boundary points
and for
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}

is as in Björn–Björn
[
5
]
by means of Perron solutions
with respect to the extended boundary

\partial^{*}\Omega:=\begin{cases}\partial\Omega\cup\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\},&\text{if $\Omega$ is unbounded,}\\
\partial\Omega,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
is the point added in the
one-point compactification

X^{*}:=X\cup\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\}
of
X
when
X
is unbounded.

Definition 4.3
.

Assume that
\partial^{*}\Omega\neq\varnothing
.
Given
f:\partial^{*}\Omega\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
,
let
\mathcal{U}_{f}(\Omega)
be the collection of all superharmonic functions

u
in
\Omega
that are bounded from below and such that

\liminf_{\Omega\ni y\to x}u(y)\geq f(x)\quad\textup{for all }x\in\partial^{*}\Omega.

The
upper Perron solution
of
f
is defined by

{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}_{\Omega}f(x)=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{f}(\Omega)}u(x),\quad x\in\Omega.

The
lower Perron solution

Lemma 5.1
.

Let

Then for every ball

(5.2)

Since
{C_{p}}(\{x_{0}\})=0
, we have

by Lemma 9.5 in Björn–Björn
[

Fix

and let

be sufficiently small so that

By the continuity of

-harmonic functions

Next, Lemma 9.9 in
[
5

shows that the set

Similar statements hold with

\displaystyle M(r)-m(R)

6
Nonuniqueness of Green functions

In the following example we construct a nonstandard metric on
\mathbf{R}^{2}
so
that Green functions with singularity at the origin
are not unique, both in
\mathbf{R}^{2}
and in discs
\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{2}:|x|<R\}
.
This proves Theorem
1.3
.
The metric is comparable to the Euclidean metric
and possesses a Finsler type structure.

Example 6.1
.

(
Proof of Theorem
1.3
)
Let
X=\mathbf{R}^{2}
be equipped with the weighted Lebesgue measure

d\mu=w\,dx
, where
w(x)=|x|^{\alpha}
with
\alpha>-1
,
and a nonstandard metric
d
that we will now define.
We also let
1<p<2+\alpha
.
Note that the weight
w
is
p\mspace{1mu}
-admissible, see
Heinonen–Kilpeläinen–Martio
[
17
, Corollary 15.35]
.

For
z\in\mathbf{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
we use polar coordinates with
complex notation and
write
z=re^{i\theta}
with
r>0
and

\theta\in\mathbf{R}
.
Thus our functions will be
2\pi
-periodic in
\theta
.
Equivalently, we restrict
\theta
to the unit circle

\mathbf{S}^{1}=[0,2\pi)
(identifying
0
and
2\pi
).
In polar coordinates, the standard orthonormal basis for the tangent space
T_{z}\mathbf{R}^{2}

at
z=re^{i\theta}\neq 0
is given by

\partial_{r}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}:=\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}.

The metric
d
on
\mathbf{R}^{2}
is defined using an
\ell^{1}
-norm on
T_{z}\mathbf{R}^{2}
by

d(x,y)=\inf_{\gamma_{x,y}}\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{\gamma(t)}\ dt,\quad x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{2},

where the infimum is taken over all Lipschitz curves
\gamma_{x,y}

connecting
x
and
y
,
and

\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{z}=|a|+|b|\quad\text{when }z\neq 0\text{ and }\dot{\gamma}(t)=a\partial_{r}+\frac{b}{r}\partial_{\theta},

while
\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{0}=0
.

The metric
d
on
\mathbf{R}^{2}

is comparable to the Euclidean metric by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
,
since the Euclidean norm of
\dot{\gamma}
is given by
\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
.
It follows that
the measure
\mu
is doubling and supports a
p\mspace{1mu}
-Poincaré inequality
also on
(\mathbf{R}^{2},d)
, by e.g.
[
4
, Proposition 4.16]

Let
u\in{\Lip_{\rm loc}}(\mathbf{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\})
.
By Rademacher’s theorem (see e.g.
[
14
, Theorem 3.2]
)
for a.e.

there exists a linear mapping (the differential)
du(z):\mathbf{R}^{2}\to\mathbf{R}^{2}
such that

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{|u(z+h)-u(z)-du(z)(h)|}{\|h\|_{z}}=0.

Expressed in polar coordinates with
h=a\partial_{r}+b\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}
,
the differential can be written as

du(z)(h)=a\partial_{r}u+b\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}u.

Using this
and Theorem 6.1 in Cheeger
[
11
]
(or Theorem 13.5.1
in Heinonen–Koskela–Shanmugalingam–Tyson
[
20
]
),
we obtain that for any
u\in{\Lip_{\rm loc}}(\mathbf{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\})
and
a.e.

,

\displaystyle g_{u}(z)

\displaystyle=\Lip u(z):=\limsup_{\rho\to 0}\sup_{\|h\|_{z}\leq\rho}\frac{|u(z+h)-u(z)|}{\|h\|_{z}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\rho\to 0}\sup_{|a|+|b|\leq\rho}\frac{|a\partial_{r}u(z)+b\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}u(z)|}{|a|+|b|}=\max\biggl\{|\partial_{r}u(z)|,\frac{|\partial_{\theta}u(z)|}{r}\biggr\}.

This calculation expresses the fact that the dual basis of

\{\partial_{r},\tfrac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\}
is given by
\{dr,r\,d\theta\}
,
and the dual of the
\ell^{1}
-norm on the tangent space is the
\ell^{\infty}
-norm
on the cotangent space.

Next, we let
f:\mathbf{S}^{1}\to\mathbf{R}

1
-Lipschitz function and

a_{p}=\frac{2+\alpha-p}{p-1}>0.
