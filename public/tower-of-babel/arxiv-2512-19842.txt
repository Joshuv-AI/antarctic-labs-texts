Abstract
The complexity and scale of Internet attacks call for distributed, cooperative observatories capable of monitoring malicious traffic across diverse networks. Holoscope is a lightweight, cloud-native platform designed to simplify the deployment and management of distributed telescope (passive) and honeypot (active) sensors, used to collect and analyse attack traffic by exposing or simulating vulnerable systems. Built upon K3s and WireGuard, Holoscope offers secure connectivity, automated node onboarding, and resilient operation even in resource-constrained environments. Through modular design and Infrastructure-as-Code principles, it supports dynamic sensor orchestration, automated recovery and processing. We build, deploy and operate Holoscope across multiple institutions and cloud networks in Europe and Brazil, enabling unified visibility into large-scale attack phenomena while maintaining ease of integration and security compliance.



I
Introduction

Understanding cyber threats requires the ability to continuously observe malicious activities across the Internet. Security monitoring infrastructures play a central role in identifying emerging threats and characterising adversarial behaviour. Among these, network telescopes/darknet (i.e., unused IP address space that receives unsolicited traffic) and honeypots (i.e., decoy systems that imitate vulnerable services to study attacker behaviour) enable researchers and operators to capture unsolicited traffic, attack attempts/evolution and scanning campaigns at scale.

While such sensors have proven effective, their deployment is often limited to individual organisations or network domains, constraining the scope of observation and the diversity of captured threats. To obtain a broader view of adversarial behaviour, we advocate as essential the ability to combine observations from multiple, heterogeneous networks
[
10
]
.
However, building and maintaining such distributed platforms remains challenging, as it requires secure data sharing, automation, and scalability across administrative boundaries.

We introduce Holoscope, a novel distributed and scalable cybersecurity observatory developed within the PROTECT-IT/SERICS nationwide project. It is designed to simplify the deployment and management of sensors across multiple geographically distributed networks owned by different organisations. Holoscope aims to create a configurable, scalable network of passive and reactive sensors that continuously collect, process, and share unsolicited and potentially malicious traffic from diverse vantage points, producing high-value data that the research and security communities can leverage to extract and model new attack patterns and to study large-scale adversarial behaviour.

At the time of writing, we have deployed Holoscope across 10 organisations, including university campuses, service providers, and cloud infrastructures, in Europe, US and Brazil. We primarily operate Holoscope as a distributed darknet, with honeypots selectively activated at different times and locations. Holoscope is the first platform that offers an open-source, out-of-the-box, container-based, distributed cybersecurity observatory running on edge Kubernetes clusters. Holoscope will be publicly available upon acceptance. In this paper, we present Holoscope design and implementation, the core modules we foresee deployed in the platform, some initial deployment experience, and look forward to extending it to more collaborators.

II-A

Holoscope design

We design Holoscope as a multi-layer architecture, illustrated in
Figure

1
.
The first layer provides a reliable and secure channel, leveraging a VPN, across the organisations that will join Holoscope, fulfilling REQ
I
.
Furthermore, the use of a VPN normalises networking across diverse partner organisations (coping with NAT, firewalls, dynamic addressing, etc.). We configure the VPN topology as a point-to-point connection between the organisations and the central server(s). This approach prevents sensor visibility and, in the event of a sensor compromise, eliminates the risk of unwanted direct sensor-to-sensor communication. We use WireGuard, a well-recognised lightweight and efficient VPN.

The second layer uses a container orchestration platform (e.g., Kubernetes) that builds a cluster, on top of the VPN, to distribute applications across the sensors.
We motivate the use of a container orchestrator for two reasons: (i) containers allow us to distribute applications effectively by packaging each one with its dependencies into isolated and portable environments, and (ii) the orchestrator manages the deployment, scaling, and lifecycle of these containerized applications (i.e., pods) and enforce a high level of isolation (REQ
I
). We adopt K3s as the container orchestrator of Holoscope.

By deploying a K3s cluster, we satisfy most of the requirements listed in
Table

I
. In particular, K3s operates efficiently in resource-constrained environments (REQ
I
) and in high-latency networks (REQ
I
), such as the geographically one of Holoscope. Additionally, by leveraging Kubernetes features – such as deployment, services, and RBAC – K3s ensures that all applications run as intended and maintain their desired state, i.e., they operate in the correct sensors and in the required number of instances (REQ
I
). Combined with K3s’s built-in support for redundancy
etcd
nodes, this ensures that the entire platform operates consistently (REQ
I
). Finally, K3s supports the execution of any application that can be containerised, enabling the use of existing and new applications by simply providing their images (REQ
I
).

On top of this infrastructure, there are the modules, i.e., the Helm Chart of the applications that K3s will execute on the sensors. Examples of these modules are the applications for installing a darknet and honeypots (REQ
I
, REQ
I

Any node can host the central controller responsible for orchestrating the entire platform. A node may act as a data lake offering a long-term traffic storage (REQ
I
), enabling the collection of the sensors’ generated data, being it raw or locally pre-processed (REQ
I
). Holoscope runs on any Unix-based machine, being it physical or virtual.

II-B

Holoscope sensor management

We now describe how Holoscope manages its sensors, focusing on the processes for onboarding new machines, performing initial configuration, maintaining and updating nodes, while tracking different users, NIC configuration, network addresses and modules.

To handle these processes, we adopt Ansible, an open-source IT automation engine that automates the management through reusable playbooks. This approach ensures consistency across heterogeneous nodes and simplifies large-scale deployments by reducing the need for manual intervention by applying the Infrastructure-as-Code paradigm (REQ
I
).

Holoscope sensor onboarding

Onboarding a new node requires an existing SSH connection. Once connected, an Ansible playbook configures the machine by (i) installing the required software (i.e., Docker, K3s, and WireGuard), (ii) setting up the VPN, and (iii) adding the node to the K3s cluster. Thanks to this Ansible-based onboarding process, we strongly limit the need for manual intervention by the sensor owner for the setup (REQ
I
).

Adding modules to Holoscope

Holoscope offers a private container image registry that we use to deploy modules (see Sec.
III
). We chose to offer a private registry to avoid depending on a third-party registry, while keeping our modules secured. To add a new module image into Holoscope, it is sufficient to either provide a
Dockerfile
, which can be built and uploaded to the private registry.

Deploying modules on sensors

A key aspect of Holoscope is the heterogeneity of its sensors. Each sensor has its own configuration, e.g., NIC names, IP address range as well as specific policies. To handle this heterogeneity, we employ Helm, a Kubernetes package manager that allows creating parametrised, reusable flexible deployments. By using Helm, we define module templates and later customise them with sensor-specific details, thereby applying the Infrastructure-as-Code paradigm to module deployment.

III
Modules offered by Holoscope

III-A

Darknet

The Darknet module activates a passive sensor over an IP address range. The module supports several darknet’s configurations:
(i) The sensor’s NIC has all the darknet IP addresses directly assigned. This is the simplest approach, and the unsolicited traffic reaches the sensor as if it were normal traffic.
(ii) the organisation configures the ingress router to forward darknet traffic to the one IP address of the sensor. This requires changing the routing policy in the organisation’s network;
(iii) The module sends forged ARP replies in response to ARP requests from the border routers, redirecting traffic destined for the darknet to the sensor. According to the darknet’s configurations, the Darknet module interacts with the Toolbox module (described later) to properly configure the sensor’s iptables rules.

III-B

Honeypot

Holoscope supports any honeypots that can be containerised. When dealing with containerised honeypots in a cluster, two major challenges arise: (i) how to expose the pod containing the honeypot over the sensors’ port, and (ii) how to guarantee the pod is not compromised.

Kubernetes can expose a pod directly on a sensor’s port using
hostNetwork
, making it appear like a local service. However, this creates a major security risk, since a compromised pod would have unrestricted access to the sensor’s network. Additionally, we aim at exposing honeypots with control over the
range of ports
and
range of IP addresses
exposed to attackers. For example, we envision scenarios where Holoscope routes traffic from specific attackers to different backends, or steers all packets targeting some addresses to basic L4 responders that complete the TCP three-way handshake to capture initial payloads
[
10
]
. Kubernetes does not support this natively, thus we rely on our Toolbox module described next.

To preserve honeypot integrity and reduce the risk of pod compromise, we leverage Kubernetes’ built-in isolation mechanisms, ensuring each pod operates with the minimum required privileges. Since this is a static approach, we additionally integrate Falco, an active monitoring system that tracks runtime behaviour and detects signs of compromise.

III-C

Toolbox

This module provides traffic steering in Kubernetes, complementing Kubernetes network management. It provides, for example, the blocking of outgoing traffic (e.g., ICMP error or TCP RST packets from darknet IP addresses) and custom traffic steering for specific scenarios (e.g., aggregation of destination ports and addresses in a few backends).

The Toolbox module runs as a privileged DaemonSet, present on every sensor. It generates and updates the sensor’s iptables based on the pods running on the node. We prevent interference with the native iptables chains managed by the K3s Container Network Interface (CNI) by using additional custom chains. Additionally, the Toolbox leverages rate-limiting options to control the amount of outgoing traffic sent by the sensor, a fundamental requirement to avoid being part of possible attacks when hosting active responders.

III-D

Traffic Collector and Log Sync

This module captures traffic exchanged by sensors. It stores traces on the sensor’s disk, creating a file every hour to simplify indexing. The module operates independently of other components, and thanks to CI/CD versioning, we track which modules were active on the host during each capture.

Additionally, the module retrieves traces and other module logs from sensors and stores them in storage nodes. This design serves two purposes: (i) preventing disk saturation on individual sensors and (ii) ensuring collected data are properly stored and readily accessible for subsequent analysis. Naturally, this module requires an ad-hoc setup for any other module to consume its logs. Moreover, organisations can limit/disable this feature, e.g., in cases where organisation-specific data retention policies apply.

III-E

Federated Learning

Finally, Holoscope supports privacy-preserving analytics through a federated learning (FL) module, enabling collaborative model training across distributed sensors without centralising raw data. This design allows partners to jointly detect and classify attack patterns while maintaining compliance with local data policies. Our implementation follows a lightweight, containerised approach using the Flower FL framework. The module currently operated on Holoscope implements FL-Darkvec
[
6
]
to demonstrate the platform’s capability to execute distributed learning tasks over heterogeneous, geographically dispersed networks.

IV
Deployment of Holoscope

We now describe our initial deployment of Holoscope over 3 months of collected traces.

IV-A

Participating Organizations

We deploy Holoscope through collaboration with several Italian and Brazilian organisations, as shown in
Table

II
. All organisations provide an IP range within their address space to install a darknet. Additionally, some support honeypot deployment and workload execution, e.g., data processing, directly within their infrastructure.

Each organisation offers different-sized subnet ranges and sensor capabilities. For simplicity, we consider each /24 subnet a separate sensor, splitting larger ranges into multiple /24 sensors. Sensors joined at different times. For instance, we activated sensor
H
, hosted in Microsoft Azure East US, for one month during August 2025. Within each sensor’s address range, we select a /24 subnet to compare visibility into unsolicited traffic. A single /24 typically observes between 1.3 million and 6.1 million packets per day from approximately 42 thousand unique external senders. Currently, Holoscope operates a distributed network telescope covering 3.8 million IP addresses, with most sensor owners authorising active experiments using honeypots and local workload execution.

IV-B

Resource Overhead

We evaluate memory overhead for the VPN and K3s Holoscope layers using a testbed with three Virtual Machines, each with 2 vCPUs and 2 GB RAM. One runs the K3s master node, the others act as sensors. The Holoscope setup consumes approximately 200 MB RAM in the sensor (negligible resources) while about 1 GB RAM in the master node requires due to additional K3s components.
Activating the toolbox, darknet, and traffic collector modules consumes negligible additional memory, while each honeypot module’s requirements depend on the honeypot application. After activation, memory requirements remain constant.

V
Related Work

V-A

Benefits of distributed vantage points

Network telescopes at a single vantage point suffer from bias, as characteristics of the monitored address space – prefix size, IP allocation, and geographic or topological position – strongly influence both traffic volume and composition
[
10
]
. While some scanners sweep the entire IPv4 space uniformly, most target narrower regions such as top-routed prefixes or DNS-advertised hosts
[
9
]
, causing telescopes in different network environments to capture distinct perspectives of their activities.

Cloud-based deployments (e.g., AWS) exemplify this contrast, recording higher traffic volumes and markedly different distributions of targeted ports and services compared to on-premise darknets
[
8
]
. Similar divergence appears across network types, as sensors in data centers, enterprise networks, or ISP address space attract different scanner classes and distinct probing behaviors
[
11
]
.
A distributed telescope mitigates these limitations by combining observations from multiple, diverse sensors. It reduces bias, improves coverage of senders targeting specific address blocks or network types, and increases detection of rare or localised activities
[
11
]
.

V-B

Distributed telescope and honeypot platform

Parallel research has explored distributed measurement infrastructures leveraging multiple network telescopes or honeypot deployments for broader visibility and collaborative analysis. While several systems were designed for controlled, institution-specific environments
[
3
,
9
]
, our focus is on platforms promoting multi-organisational participation and coordinated monitoring.

Prominent examples include the National Distributed Network Telescope (NDNT)
[
7
]
and EWIS
[
5
]
. NDNT is a U.S. initiative building a nationwide telescope, while EWIS deploys sensors across Greece to create a jointly managed passive monitoring infrastructure, demonstrating the feasibility of harmonising diverse operational environments under a common measurement framework.

The honeypot ecosystem has similarly seen platforms coordinating large numbers of active sensors, including DiPot
[
4
]
, GCA AIDE
[
1
]
, and SweetsPot
[
2
]
. DiPot proposes a tiered architecture with capturing and data-processing nodes. GCA AIDE operates over 200 nodes worldwide running the proprietary ProxyPot framework capable of hosting multiple honeypots, while SweetsPot extends support to various honeypots from standard ones like T-POT to custom implementations.

Despite heterogeneous implementations, these platforms share common traits: most rely on secure VPN and SSH-based channels for communication between sensors and a central aggregation server. However, they generally lack mechanisms for centrally orchestrating sensor behaviour and node onboarding – each node’s role is typically predefined and static, requiring updates or configuration changes to be performed directly on the sensor.

VI
Conclusion

We presented Holoscope, a open, lightweight, cloud-native platform for cybersecurity data collection. Leveraging K3s and WireGuard, it provides secure, automated, and resilient orchestration across heterogeneous networks. Our initial multi-node deployment showed its scalability and potential to reveal traffic insights unattainable from a single vantage point. Future work will enhance federated analytics, privacy-preserving data sharing, and AI-driven modules to consolidate Holoscope as a flexible framework for Internet-scale threat observation.
