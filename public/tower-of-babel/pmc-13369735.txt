Pangenomics of Limosilactobacillus fermentum reveals genomic diversity and bacteriocin activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli
Authors: Muhammed Zahid Kasapoglu, Idil Acar, Ismail Gumustop, Ismail Erol, Ibrahim Cagri Kurt, Fatih Ortakci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLimosilactobacillus fermentum is a versatile lactic acid bacterium with significant probiotic and biotechnological potential, yet the genomic determinants underlying its ecological adaptation and therapeutic applications remain underexplored. This study performed a comparative genomic analysis of 52 L. fermentum strains, with a focus on the novel strain ATT-06 isolated from traditional Turkish shalgam. We uncovered substantial genetic diversity, evidenced by an open pan-genome (18,647 genes) and a small core genome (718 genes). CRISPR-Cas systems were prevalent (46 strains), with Type-IE and Type-IIA being most common and mutually exclusive from Type-IC. Strain ATT-06, which uniquely produced gamma-aminobutyric acid at 17.74 µg/mL, harbored a Type-IIA CRISPR system and a single prophage. In silico molecular docking and dynamics simulations revealed that the bacteriocin Lactococcin, encoded by strain ATA-LTC-Lf170503, exhibited strong binding affinities (ΔG: –8.1 to –13.0 kcal/mol) against Rho proteins of Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, outperforming Acidocin A. These findings highlight the genomic plasticity and adaptive mechanisms of L. fermentum, and position strain ATT-06 as a promising probiotic candidate with potential neuroactive and antimicrobial applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51219-1.Keywords: Limosilactobacillus fermentum, Comparative genomics, CRISPR-cas systems, Bacteriocins, Gamma-aminobutyric acidSubject terms: Biotechnology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are Gram (+), catalase (–), nonspore forming bacteria that play a crucial role in many food fermentation ecosystems1,2. Due to their extensive metabolic diversity, lactobacilli are found and utilized as functional starter cultures in a wide range of fermented foods, such as cheese, plant-based fermented products, fermented meats, wine, beer, and sourdoughs3. Additionally, lactobacilli are significant contributors to the fermentation process of fermented shalgam products4. Along with their roles as starters and probiotics, LAB serve as potential bioprotective cultures due to their ability to produce peptide-based antimicrobials like nisin, enterocin, pediocin, salivaricin, and plantaricin5,6 which can be found in various fermented dairy and vegetables7,8.

Bacteriocins exhibit various attributes, such as being nontoxic or having a low molecular weight; possessing cationic or neutral peptides; or demonstrating hydrophobic or amphiphilic properties. As a result, their spectrum and mechanism of action can differ9,10. Additionally, bacteriocins possess diverse molecular structures, methods of biosynthesis, biochemical characteristics, molecular weights, thermal stabilities, and genetic factors. They are widely acknowledged as safe substances with specific bacteriocins such as nisin and pediocin having obtained formal regulatory approval (e.g., GRAS status) for use in foods. Some bacteriocins may inhibit activity against closely related microorganisms, while others can suppress a wide variety of microorganisms, including pathogens like Listeria monocytogenes, Bacillus cereus, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, Pseudomonas spp., and Clostridium tyrobutyricum10,11.

Diet is a primary modulator of gut microbiota composition and function, directly influencing host health12. Fermented foods, in particular, contribute both live microbes and bioactive metabolites (i.e., short-chain fatty acids, gamma-aminobutyric acid, bacteriocins) that can enhance microbial diversity, strengthen intestinal barrier function, and exert systemic immunomodulatory and neuroactive effects12–16. Consequently, the isolation and characterization of novel LAB from dietary-fermented sources like shalgam represent a strategic approach to identifying strains with combined technological, probiotic, and therapeutic potential.

Shalgam, a traditional lactic acid-fermented beverage native to the Southern Anatolia region of Turkey, is recognized as a rich source of lactobacilli. These include species such as Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Lacticaseibacillus paracasei, Levilactobacillus brevis, Levilactobacillus parabrevis, Lactobacillus acidophilus, Lactobacillus gasseri, Lactobacillus helveticus, and Limosilactobacillus reuteri, with the latter two species being particularly predominant17,18. These autochthonous species are promising candidates for next-generation probiotics due to their historical association with food matrices and presumed resilience to gastrointestinal conditions19. Limosilactobacillus (L.) fermentum is another heterofermentative species of LAB found in various food fermentation ecosystems, including sourdough fermentation20, fermenting cocoa pulp-bean mass21, and other plant-based fermentations9. It is also present in environments such as breast milk22 and is part of human-associated microbiota, including the colon, urogenital tract, and oral cavity23,24. While not always the dominant species in shalgam, its metabolic versatility and probiotic potential make it of significant interest. In this context, our group isolated a novel Limosilactobacillus fermentum ATT-06 from artisanal shalgam. Its ecological versatility and genomic plasticity make it a valuable model for studying adaptation mechanisms and mining for beneficial traits such as antimicrobial production, vitamin synthesis, and neurotransmitter generation25,26. Notably, certain strains exhibit psychobiotic potential through the production of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), a key inhibitory neurotransmitter involved in gut-brain communication27.

In the present study, we employ a comprehensive comparative genomics approach to explore the strain level diversity across L. fermentum species that were isolated from various fermented foods and human gut microbiome and to identify the genetic basis of the traits associated with L. fermentum ATT-06. Our objectives were to: (i) elucidate the genomic diversity, CRISPR-Cas systems, and mobile genetic elements across the species; (ii) characterize the carbohydrate-active enzyme profile and GABA biosynthesis capability of strain ATT-06; and (iii) mine the L. fermentum pangenome for potential bacteriocins and evaluate their therapeutic potential against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli.

Materials and methods

Strain isolation and cultivation

Limosilactobacillus fermentum ATT-06 was isolated from fermented shalgam that was obtained from an artisanal local producer in Adana, Turkey. A 10 mL sample of shalgam was mixed with 90 mL of Maximum Recovery Diluent (Merck, GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany) in a Schott bottle and vortexed at high speed for 1 min (MS-3 Basic, IKA-Werke GmbH, Staufen, Germany). Serial dilutions were prepared, and 100 µL of the diluted sample was spread onto MRS agar (Merck). The plates were incubated anaerobically at 30 °C for 48 h. The isolate, later named ATT-06, was selected and purified through two rounds of colony isolation. Gram staining and catalase tests were performed on the pure isolate. Cryovial stocks of AGA58 were prepared using MRS broth (Merck) supplemented with 25% glycerol and stored at –80 °C.

Genomic DNA extraction

The cryopreserved culture of L. fermentum ATT-06 was sub-cultured twice in MRS broth and incubated anaerobically at 32 °C for 24 h. A 1 mL aliquot of the fresh culture was transferred to a sterile 2 mL microcentrifuge tube and centrifuged at 6000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and genomic DNA was extracted from the cell pellet using the PureLink Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo-Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed using a Nanodrop and 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Whole-genome sequencing and De Novo assembly

Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and sequencing was performed on the Illumina Novaseq platform, generating paired-end (PE) reads of 2 × 150 bases. Adapter sequences and contaminants were removed using the JGI-RQC Filter pipeline (BBTools v38.22), retaining only reads corresponding to the ATT-06 strain. The genome was assembled using the Bacterial and Viral Bioinformatics Resource Center genome assembly service (https://www.bv-brc.org/app/Assembly2) with an auto-assembly strategy28 and deposited to NCBI with the following accession number of JBXTVT000000000.

Strains and data acquisition

The 52 strains were selected to represent the ecological diversity of L. fermentum, encompassing isolates from fermented foods, human-associated environments, and probiotic products. This selection ensured phylogenetic breadth, provided context for the novel strain ATT-06, and included high-quality, publicly available genomes to facilitate a robust comparative analysis. The following strains were evaluated towards comparative genomic analysis 279, 712, 1019, 45,756, A51, ACA-DC, 179, AGA52, ATA-LTC-Lf170503, ATCC 23,271, CECT 9269, CIRM-BIA 1951, CIRM-BIA 2157, CIRM-BIA 2221, CIRM-BIA 697, CRL1446, CYLB55, DSM 20,052, EFEL6800, FAM 25,337, FI11572, FI11600, FI11615, FOSU-YHD19, FTDC8312, FUA3588, GD181, GD752, GR1103, ICIS24, ike38, JX201, KA16MA, KMB_612, LfU21, LMT2-75, NKN-51, QAULFN21, QH221, R17-5, RI-508, SCB0035, SH272, SN4-1, SP331, TCI757, UBLF-31, USM 8633, WX91, XC416, YC74, YGRT89 and ATT-06.

Pangenome and comparative analysis

The completeness of over 350 L. fermentum genomes genome assemblies available to date in NCBI Genbank was assessed using BUSCO analysis with the bacteria_odb10 lineage dataset29,30. Genome assemblies with over 95% completeness were annotated using Prokka (version 1.14.6)31. Core- and pan genome analyses were performed by first annotating the genomes with Prokka32 using the argument --kingdom Bacteria. The resulting Prokka output files were then processed with Roary (version 3.13.0)33 using the arguments -e -n -v -r. Functional annotation of pan- and core genes was conducted using EggNOG-Mapper34, with the COG database35(available at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/COG/). The core and pan COGs were visualized using R and ggplot236. A SNP-based phylogenetic tree was constructed using TYGS and iTOL37,38. The ANI for each pair of genomes was determined through a Perl script that is part of the GET_HOMOLOGUES pipelines, and the resulting matrix was then clustered and visualized with the Pheatmap R package39. Carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme)-related genes were identified using the CAZy database (v10) on the dbCAN server and HMMER (version 3.3.2)40, following the recommended dbCAN protocol. L. fermentum strains were categorized based on the number of CAZymes they contained.

CRISPR and mobile genetic elements

CRISPR elements, including repeats and spacers, were identified, aligned, and visualized using the CRISPRviz tool41. CRISPR types were confirmed using CRISPRCasFinder18. CRISPR loci types were further classified using CRISPRClassify42. Open reading frames (ORFs) within +/- 20 kb of each CRISPR array were predicted using Prodigal (v2.6.3)43. Annotation of Cas proteins in ORFs was performed using hmmsearch with curated HMM profiles43, applying the criteria: HMM E-value <1e-5, HMM alignment coverage > 70%, and bitscore ≥ 40. Partially predicted proteins from Prodigal44 were excluded. Plasmids in L. fermentum genomes were identified using PLSDB (version 2021_06_23_v2) with default settings45,46. Prophages were detected using the Phage Search Tool Enhanced Release (PHASTEST)47,48. Potential bacteriocins and bacteriocin-expressing regions were identified using the BAGEL4 web tool38.

In silico docking and MD simulations

Protein-bacteriocin complex generation

Two rho proteins extracted from S. aureus NCTC 8325 and E. coli K-12 were considered as target proteins. The RHO-bacteriocin complexes were obtained using ColabFold49. The best ranked complex further analyzed with PRODIGY (PROtein binDIng enerGY prediction) server50.

Experimental validation

Gas formation test

To confirm the obligatory heterofermentative lifestyle of L. fermentum ATT-06, Durham tube method46 was utilized. The MRS broth was distributed into two 5 mL sterile test tubes, and the sterile Durham tubes were carefully inverted and placed in each test tube, ensuring they were completely submerged. Then, 100 µL of an overnight culture of the bacterial strain was inoculated into each MRS broth tube. The inoculated tubes were incubated at 32 °C for 48 h. After incubation, the Durham tubes were examined for gas bubbles indicating positive gas production.

Gaba analysis

Limosilactobacillus fermentum ATT-06 strain was grown in MRS broth supplemented with 2.5 g/L dextrose and 10 g/L monosodium glutamate (MSG) at 32 °C for 48 h to measure the GABA production ability. The strain, sub-cultured twice, was inoculated into MRS broth (containing 1% MSG and 0.25% dextrose) and incubated at 37 ℃ for 48 h. The culture medium was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min, and the cells were removed. The cell-free supernatant (CFS) was then freeze-dried using a freeze-dryer. Subsequently, around 20 mg of each CFS, MSG, and GABA were placed into separate cylindrical glass NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance) tubes and dissolved in deuterium oxide. Each prepared sample was analyzed via proton nuclear magnetic resonance (¹H-NMR) using an NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Model 400, MA, USA) at 400 MHz for 5 min. To confirm GABA production, negative control containing sterile MRS medium was utilized. A commercially available pure GABA standard was used as a positive control, and the obtained spectra and quantitative analyses were verified by comparing them with a known GABA standard51.

Core and pan-genome analysis

The genomic conservation of all L. fermentum genomes available in NCBI GenBank (i.e., 348 strains) was analyzed with regard to pan- and core genomes. It was found that only 3.85% of the total genes were conserved at 95% blastp identity cutoff. Among the total 18,647 coding sequences, 718 of them were shared among 348 genomes that represent the core genome (Fig. 2A). The cloud genes comprised 87.6% of the total CDS; however, shell genes represented 8.54% of total coding sequences suggesting the robust phenotypic variations across L. fermentum isolates. To elaborate further, random subsampling was performed to create trend lines of each isolate’s core genome and pan-genome (Fig. 2B). Although the size of the core genome was close to a flat line, the pan-genome size never reached a plateau. Because the number of genomes continues to go up and novel genes are still in the process of discovery, the pan-genome stays open among L. fermentum strains. The trend of an open pan-genome was statistically validated using the Heap’s Law alpha parameter (α < 0.95).

The bar chart produced by eggNOG-mapper illustrates the comparison of the distribution of COG (Clusters of Orthologous Groups) functional categories between the core genome (genes present in all strains) and the pangenome (the overall set of genes found across all strains, comprising both core and accessory genes) (Fig. 2C). The core genome primarily consists of essential “housekeeping” functions necessary for fundamental survival, including translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis, replication, recombination and repair, as well as energy production and conversion. On the other hand, pan-genome is enriched in accessory genes, including: defense mechanisms, secondary metabolites biosynthesis, and carbohydrate transport and metabolism. A remarkable number of coding sequences were assigned to function-unknown in both core and pan-genome categories.

To quantitatively assess the similarity among the 52 L. fermentum genomes including ATT-06, the average nucleotide identity (ANI) was determined (Fig. 3). The findings showed that all strains were classified as L. fermentum, supported by an ANI value exceeding 96%55. According to ANI-based phylogenetic relationships across 52 genomes analyzed, four major clades were identified. The first clade from top down was composed of the single member of RI-508 that was isolated from Cacao bean fermentation. The second major clade was composed of FUA3588, FI11600, FI11572, FI11615, the former was isolated from Mahewu whereas the latter three strains were isolated from pearl millet and its byproducts. The third clade was consisted of five strains (i.e., NKN-51, LMT2-75, YGRT89, 1019, 45756) that were isolated from Yak milk, kimchi, yogurt, Injera fermented dough and Pumiao rice noodle. The remaining forty-two strains including ATT-06 and reference genome EFEL6800 formed the fourth clade that were isolated from diverse ecological niches such as plant based fermented foods, dairy, feces, saliva and fermented meat (i.e., 8633). The closest hit to ATT-06 based on ANI value was achieved with AGA52 that was another plant based fermented food isolate.

Mobile genetic elements

Table S2 shows intact prophages incorporated into bacterial genomes, specifying their genomic positions, and GC content. 38 out of 52 genomes were predicted to encode at least one intact prophage (Table S2). It was demonstrated that PHAGE_Lactob_LF1_NC_019486 (60.53%) and PHAGE_Lactob_LfeSau_NC_029068 (31.58%) are the most prevalent prophages found across 38 L. fermentum genomes screened. PHAGE_Coryne_Lederberg_NC_048790, PHAGE_Staphy_SPbeta_like_NC_029119 and PHAGE_Paenib_Shelly_NC_041909 were only detected in four, three and two L. fermentum strains, respectively. The remaining eight phages were unique to single strains. L. fermentum ATT-06 was predicted to encode a single prophage namely PHAGE_Lactob_LfeSau_NC_029068.

Ten L. fermentum strains were predicted to carry putative plasmids ranging from 1.3 kb to 75.9 kb. ATA-LTC-Lf170503, CYLB55, LMT2-75, and YGRT89 carried multiple plasmids in their genome (Table 1). While the reference genome EFEL6800 was found to harbor a putative plasmid at a size of 34 kb, Malaysian isolates of GR1103, LMT2-75, USM 8633, and YGRT89 were predicted to carry megaplasmid at a size of 75.9 kb. Noteworthy, LMT2-75 carried three plasmids with a lump sum of over 1 Mb.

CRISPR-Cas system

Forty-six out of fifty-two genomes (88.5%) were predicted to carry at least one CRISPR-Cas system (Table S3). Type-IE is the most common (67.39%) CRISPR system, followed by Type-IIA (63.04%). Type-IIA co-occurs with both IC and IE subtypes, explaining its high prevalence. Noteworthy, Type-IIU is unique to JX201 (2.17%). Type-IIIA is rare (10.87%) and only observed in strains with IC or IE systems. No strain has both Type-IC and Type-IE suggesting mutual exclusivity of both types across L. fermentum genomes analyzed. For example, strains with TypeIC (e.g., NKN-51, A51) lacked Type-IE. Likewise, strains with Type-IE (e.g., CECT_9269, CRL1446) lacked TypeIC.

ATT-06 harbored a single Cas-Type-IIA similar to another plant based fermented food isolate AGA52. Five genomes (i.e., NKN-51, A51, ICIS24, 1019, SN4-1) carried putative Type I-C among which NKN-51, A51, and ICIS24 encoded Type II-A. It can also be deduced that Type-IIA frequently co-occurs with both Type-IC and Type-IE. Type-IIIA is present in some strains with Type-IC (e.g., NKN-51) and a few with Type-IE (e.g., CECT_9269).

Crisprviz analysis revealed that only four strains had spacer identity across fifty-two isolates analyzed suggesting the presence of evolutionary heterogeneity56 (Fig. 5). CRISPR loci 4 of CRL1446 and CRISPR loci 3 of CECT 9269 had an identical spacer profile. In a parallel manner, CRISPR loci 1 and loci 2 of GR1103 and USM_8633 had an identical spare profile. Noteworthy, CRL1446 and CECT9269 share the same subclade in SNP based phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1A). In parallel, GR1103 and USM_8633 are the subclade members.

Gaba biosynthesis

The ¹H-NMR spectra show the results obtained for the GABA (Fig. S2), MSG (Fig. S3), and supernatant sample (Fig. S4). In the spectrum of the GABA standard, characteristic peaks were observed at chemical shifts of 2.95 ppm, 2.31 ppm, and 1.78 ppm. In the MSG standard, distinct peaks were detected between 3.65 ppm and 2.20–2.05 ppm. When the ¹H-NMR spectrum of the supernatant was examined, the observation of signals belonging to GABA in the 2.95 ppm and 2.31 ppm regions confirmed the conversion of MSG to GABA by ATT-06. This suggests ATT-06 can produce GABA likely through the glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) enzyme system.

In silico docking and MD simulations

Bacteriocin genes were identified in only one strain, ATA-LTC-Lf170503, encoding Acidocin A and Lactococcin. To evaluate their potential antimicrobial mechanism, we selected Rho transcription termination factor as a docking target due to its essential role in bacterial viability and conservation across Gram-positive and Gram-negative species. Acidocin A was 31 aminoacid long and had a sequence of VIPGTLCRKQSLPIKQDLKILLFFLPRHSEQ while Lactococcin was 46 aminoacid long and had a sequence of MISSHQKTLTDKELALISGGKTHYPTNAWKSLWKGFWESLRYTDGF.

Formation of RHO-bacteriocin complexes

PROtein binDIng enerGY prediction (PRODIGY) server results are shown in Table 2. The table presents the predicted binding energies (ΔG in kcal/mol) for protein-bacteriocin complexes involving two proteins, E. coli RHO and S. aureus RHO, and two bacteriocins, Acidocin A and Lactococcin. All ΔG values are negative, indicating thermodynamically favorable binding interactions. Lactococcin demonstrates stronger binding affinities compared to Acidocin A for both proteins, with ΔG values of –8.1 kcal/mol (E. coli RHO) and − 13.0 kcal/mol (S. aureus RHO), versus − 5.5 kcal/mol and − 7.9 kcal/mol for Acidocin A, respectively. Notably, S. aureus RHO exhibits significantly stronger binding affinities for both bacteriocins compared to E. coli RHO, particularly for Lactococcin, which shows the most stable interaction with a ΔG of -13.0 kcal/mol. These results suggest that Lactococcin may be more effective in targeting both E. coli and S. aureus, with a pronounced affinity for S. aureus RHO. The observed differences in binding affinities may reflect structural or functional variations in the protein binding sites.

Discussion

A comprehensive comparative evaluation of genomic and functional analysis of 52 Limosilactobacillus fermentum strains isolated from diverse ecological niches were explored, with a focus on the novel isolate ATT-06, isolated from lactic acid-fermented beverage, shalgam. We aimed to uncover the genomic diversity, ecological adaptability, GABA production capacity and test encoded bacteriocins’ in silico efficacy against common Gram-negative and Gram-positive human pathogens. The genome sizes achieved were between 1.91 and 2.17 Mbp which is in alignment with the range of LAB. The G + C% of L. fermentum is also in alignment with low G + C% LAB. Whole genome analysis determined either single or multiple plasmid sequences in nine L. fermentum strains which is consistent with a single plasmid found in the reference genome EFEL 6800. It was proposed that LAB are highly specialized for their particular ecological environment, a notion that is bolstered by the presence of plasmids that can be rapidly acquired and exchanged during periods of swift environmental changes57.

Comparative genome analysis of all L. fermentum genomes available in NCBI GenBank (i.e., 348 strains) showed an open pan-genome across L. fermentum species with a lump sum of 18,647 coding sequences and relatively small core genome (i.e., 718). This together with the prophages found in L. fermentum possibly highlights the genomic plasticity proposing the adaptation capacity of the species across various ecologies allowing it to survive in fermented foods and host-associated niches58. Even though ATT-06 was isolated from shalgam which is a plant-based beverage, its clade members in both SNP-based phylogenetic tree and principle coordinate analysis were those isolated from fecal samples. This perhaps suggests potential horizontal gene transfer events or functional convergence which enable adaptation against gut ecosystems. Its close evolutionary relationship with AGA52, another isolate derived from plants-based environment, further supports that environmental selection pressures might form shared genomic traits among strains from related or even different origins59.

CAZymes analysis showed that ATT-06 encodes a moderate number of GTs and GHs, in particular GH73 which might enable the effective utilization of polysaccharides in shalgam. This supports the ecological fitness of ATT-06 in shalgam microenvironment. Moreover, sub-clade members of ATT-06 were AGA52 and RI-508 that were isolated from plant-based ecologies which is an indication of emphasis against degradation of plant-derived substrates. Noteworthy, slight differences in abundances of the CAZy subfamily was seen for example higher levels of GT11, GT14 and GH8 found in ATT-06 as opposed to AGA52 perhaps imply metabolic divergence or niche-specific adaptation59.

Mobile genetic elements of prophages and plasmids were explored to uncover the potential horizontal gene transfer and genomic plasticity across 52 L. fermentum genomes. We found that ATT-06 encodes a single prophage (PHAGE_Lactob_LfeSau_NC_029068) as opposed to other strains possessing large plasmids and megaplasmids. We speculate that lack of a plasmid and existence of a single prophage in ATT-06 might imply a relatively stable genome architecture on one hand but on the other hand it might be an indication of an absence of phage hits or existence of robust phage resistance mechanisms. This was supported by identification of a Type-IIA CRISPR-Cas system in ATT-06 which is one of the two most abundant CRISPR types among 52 analyzed genomes. The lack of Type-IC and Type-IE in ATT-06 along with other isolates underscore unique evolutionary paths in CRISPR immunity60. The co-existence of Type-IIA with both Type-IC and Type-IE in other strains may suggest a complex relationship between phage pressure and the diversification of CRISPR systems61.

The high prevalence of prophage types of PHAGE_Lactob_LF1_NC_019486 (60.53%) and PHAGE_Lactob_LfeSau_NC_029068 (31.58%) likely reflects host-phage co-evolution in lactobacilli species. Strains that were found to carry multiple putative prophages might show enhanced genomic plasticity or niche adaptability. Prophages such as PHAGE_Staphy_SPbeta_like suggest cross-genus interactions or horizontal gene transfer. Strains with TypeIE (e.g., CECT_9269, CRL1446) often carry Lactob phages, perhaps suggesting CRISPR-Cas systems may target these invaders62.

As mentioned above, only ATA-LTC-Lf170503 among 52 L. fermentum genomes screened was predicted to carry bacteriocins namely Acidocin A and Lactococcin.

Recent in silico studies have highlighted the therapeutic potential of bacteriocins against resistant pathogens. Hasnat et al. identified Rho and HupB as conserved targets in clinical mastitis pathogens, demonstrating that peptides BP8 and BP32 form stable complexes that inhibit Rho by disrupting critical RNA and ADP binding sites63. Similarly, Halder and Mandal characterized a 13.1 kDa bacteriocin from L. fermentum LMEM22 (MIC 41–199 µg/ml), utilizing molecular docking to validate that its J32 peptide binds strongly to P. aeruginosa Exotoxin A (-70.35 kcal/mol) and S. aureus Sortase A (-49.90 kcal/mol)64. Khan et al. identified Enterocin B as a leading candidate against MRSA, exhibiting superior binding affinity (-7.5 kcal/mol) and stability with the resistance determinant PBP2a compared to other bacteriocins65. Collectively, these findings underscore the transformative role of computational modeling in accelerating the discovery of bacteriocin-based therapeutics as viable alternatives to conventional antibiotics. Building upon the established potential of Rho as a therapeutic target, the current study extends this research by conducting a comparative evaluation of Lactococcin and Acidocin A against the Rho proteins of human pathogens. The docking scores revealed that Lactococcin showed higher binding affinities compared to Acidocin A, for S. aureus and E. coli, implying potential antimicrobial capacity66,67. These findings could guide future strategies in peptide engineering focused on improving the specificity and efficacy of bacteriocins68,69.

Stable bacteriocin–Rho interactions observed in silico suggest a non-canonical antimicrobial mechanism targeting bacterial transcription termination. Rho is an essential ATP-dependent helicase that mediates transcription termination via RNA binding and translocation, and its inhibition causes lethal transcriptional dysregulation70. Structurally exposed and conserved hydrophobic and charged regions involved in RNA recognition render Rho a plausible interaction partner for peptide-based antimicrobials. Docking and molecular dynamics analyses indicate that bacteriocin binding is stabilized predominantly by hydrophobic contacts, π–π stacking, and complementary electrostatic interactions, which are characteristic of class II bacteriocins71,72. Lactococcin exhibited more persistent interactions and lower inter-run RMSD variability than Acidocin, suggesting stronger anchoring to conserved Rho surface patches. Importantly, such local interaction stability may impair Rho dynamics without inducing major global conformational changes. Although Rho is cytoplasmic, bacteriocin-mediated membrane perturbation may enable intracellular access, allowing interference with transcriptional regulation that complements classical membrane-associated antimicrobial effects70.

We also demonstrated the GABA biosynthesis capability of ATT-06 using ¹H-NMR which implies the conversion of monosodium glutamate to GABA likely through the glutamate decarboxylase pathway73. The confirmed conversion of monosodium glutamate to GABA by ATT-06 implies the activity of the glutamate decarboxylase pathway, a well-characterized acid resistance and metabolic system in lactic acid bacteria74. The GAD enzyme catalyzes the irreversible decarboxylation of L-glutamate, consuming a cytoplasmic proton in the process which contributes to intracellular pH homeostasis, a key survival mechanism in acidic environments75 like lactic acid-fermented shalgam. Beyond its role in bacterial physiology, microbial GABA production which serves as a primary inhibitory neurotransmitter within the human central nervous system, the in situ microbial synthesis of GABA by a probiotic strain may hold significant therapeutic potential, especially in the context of stress-related or mood disorders76,77. The dual function of the GAD pathway—enhancing survival under acidic conditions while producing a neuroactive metabolite—supports the classification of ATT-06 as a potential psychobiotic candidate, although in vivo validation is required to substantiate functional relevance in the host context78.

Overall, ATT-06 stood out with its capability to biosynthesize GABA, carry CRISPR-based defence against phages, and inhabited a phylogenetic clade associated with plant- and gut-related strains, placing it as a robust candidate for probiotic development. However, even though in silico docking experiments put forth promising preliminary information regarding bacteriocin-Rho interactions, these insights are prone to further experimental validations via antimicrobial assays. To confirm psychobiotic potential of ATT-06, the efficacy of GABA biosynthesis should be explored under GI conditions and host model systems. While the current study establishes the genomic and in vitro functional profile of L. fermentum ATT-06, future translational and clinical studies will be required to determine an optimal route of administration. Given its origin from a fermented beverage and intended probiotic application, oral delivery is the most feasible route; subsequent research should focus on formulation, dosage, and survival in the gastrointestinal tract to validate its efficacy in vivo.

We acknowledge that while our genomic survey of 52 strains provides meaningful insights into L. fermentum diversity, it represents a curated subset of the species’ pangenome. Furthermore, the promising probiotic and antimicrobial traits identified here—including GABA production and predicted bacteriocin activity—are derived from preclinical, in silico, and in vitro analyses. Consequently, their clinical relevance remains to be established through future translational studies involving formulation, in vivo models, and human trials. Finally, the molecular mechanisms proposed, such as GABA synthesis via the glutamate decarboxylase pathway and bacteriocin inhibition via Rho protein binding, require direct experimental validation through targeted gene editing, expression studies, and purified antimicrobial assays.

Conclusion

This study establishes Limosilactobacillus fermentum ATT-06 as a novel strain of biotechnological interest through a comprehensive genomic and functional analysis. The primary novel contribution lies in its unique combination of functional traits: its ability to produce gamma-aminobutyric acid in vitro, its possession of a Type-IIA CRISPR-Cas system and specific prophage profile, and its genetic linkage to a potent bacteriocin (Lactococcin) with high predicted binding affinity against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli. These attributes, coupled with its genomic adaptation to both plant-based and gut-associated environments, position ATT-06 as a multifaceted candidate for probiotic development and natural antimicrobial applications. Our findings are not without limitations. The antimicrobial potential of the identified bacteriocins is based on in silico predictions and requires experimental validation through antimicrobial assays. The genetic basis for GABA production and other adaptive traits, while strongly indicated, needs confirmation via targeted molecular studies. Furthermore, as a genomic and in vitro investigation, this study does not address translational parameters essential for probiotic application. Future research should therefore focus on: (i) experimental validation of Lactococcin’s antimicrobial activity and its mechanism of action; (ii) molecular characterization of the GABA biosynthesis pathway in ATT-06; (iii) formulation and dose optimization studies to ensure survival and functionality in the gastrointestinal tract; and (iv) controlled pre-clinical and clinical trials to evaluate its safety, efficacy, and mental health benefits in vivo. Addressing these directions will be crucial to translate the promising genomic potential of L. fermentum ATT-06 into tangible health and biotechnological applications.
