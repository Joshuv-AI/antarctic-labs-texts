Modeling and simulation of ZnO-based TFTs by dielectric engineering and temperature analysis for enhanced performance
Authors: Pranaw Kumar, Syed Sadique Anwer Askari, Mukul Kumar Das
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work presents the modeling and simulation of thin-film transistors (TFTs) based on zinc oxide (ZnO). Strong agreement was found when the developed device model was validated against experimental data. Additionally, the analog performance of ZnO TFTs was examined to find out how the gate dielectric material, dielectric thickness, and operating temperature affect important electrical parameters such as drain current (ID), field-effect mobility (µFE), subthreshold slope (SS), threshold voltage (VTh), on/off current ratio (Ion/Ioff), drain conductance (gd), transconductance (gm), transconductance generation factor (TGF), and early voltage (VEA). With SS = 0.396 V/dec, VTh = 0.49 V, and µFE = 53.2 cm²/V·s, HfO₂ showed the best performance among the dielectrics under investigation. Improved characteristics were obtained by further optimization using a 120 nm HfO₂ layer: µFE = 54.2 cm²/V·s, VTh = 0.27 V, SS = 0.315 V/dec, and Ion/Ioff ≈ 10¹¹. According to temperature-dependent analysis, device performance deteriorates at higher temperatures (> 400 K) because of increased trap effects and Fermi-level shifts, which lower mobility and result in threshold voltage instability. The potential of ZnO-based TFTs for high-performance analog and optoelectronic applications is highlighted by their optimal performance at room temperature. These results, which enable the extraction of analog FoMs pertinent to circuit designers, are based on a device model calibrated to experimental data and a methodical sweep of dielectric material, dielectric thickness, and operating temperature.Keywords: Thin film transistor, Zinc oxide, High-k dielectric material, Gate oxide thickness, Thermal stabilitySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

Since thin film transistors are now the foundation of the thin film electronics industry, they have attracted a lot of attention from researchers in recent years. The TFT market is anticipated to grow at a CAGR of 17.34% between 2021 and 20261. LED or flat panel displays use TFT as a pixel switching element2–4. The production of large-area, high-resolution displays is the main goal of the display industry. As a result, TFT performance needs to be improved immediately. The TFT is a unique kind of transistor that has a supporting substrate on which a semiconductor, dielectric, and contact layer is deposited5. Advantages like uniformity, feasibility of low temperature processing and scalability makes metal oxide TFTs a better performer than TFTs using the hydrogenated amorphous silicon and poly silicon as channel layer6,7. Amorphous metal oxide has drawn remarkable attention of researchers for developing TFTs. Metal oxide-based semiconductor channel of thin film transistors (TFTs) has been studied for the last three decades8. Unique features like high mobility, superior material properties, ease fabrication, low process temperature make TFTs applicable for various useful applications9,10. Also, due to their wide band gap, these can be operated at high voltages11. Zinc oxide, due to its several noteworthy features used widely in applications like gas sensors, pyroelectric devices, transparent electrodes and surface acoustic devices12–14. The features like high exciton energy of 60meV, ferroelectric properties, wide direct band gap of 3.37 eV, n-type conductivity and piezoelectric effect make ZnO, very popular material for researchers as a semiconductor channel material15,16. Due to the advanced techniques gained in deposition methods, possibilities of growing ZnO thin films of excellent quality are very high. Though ZnO based TFTs have huge potential, there are few challenges too. First, the proper understanding of device physics is required for the TFTs based system. Second, extraction of threshold voltage in metal oxide TFTs is difficult due to disorder in the structure of semiconductor channel material. Third, most of the TFTs suffer from instability in threshold voltage. And lastly, in order to reduce the threshold voltage, suitable high-k dielectric material is needed. ZnO is very popular among those oxides, which at relatively low deposition temperature can be grown as a single crystalline material on substrates like plastics and glasses17. The wide band gap of ZnO makes possible for it to withstand visible light. The realization of TFTs with ZnO as an active layer is thus possible employing these key characteristics. Selecting good dielectric material in ZnO based TFTs has been a challenge for the researchers to analyze the performance parameters like threshold voltage, on-off current ratio, sub-threshold slope and carrier mobility. By using the atomic layer deposition (ALD) process, Carcia18 reported a mobility of 17.6 cm2/V-s for ZnO TFTs with varied gate insulators. A novel approach was investigated19, when TiO2 and Al2O3 gate insulators were superlatticed and deposited by ALD. SiOxNy gate insulators were deposited by RF sputtering on ZnO TFTs to lower the gate leakage current. It was noted that the carrier had a mobility of 70 cm2/V-s20. In ZnO-based TFTs, a researcher used Ta2O5 as the gate insulator and attained a high carrier mobility of 60.4 cm2/V-s21.

This work reports a simulation study using Silvaco TCAD’s ATLAS tool22 to examine the suitability of ZnO as a channel layer in TFTs. Various analog parameters have been investigated in order to illustrate the use of ZnO-based TFT in analog applications. We also have reported the effect of different gate dielectric materials, gate dielectric thickness and operating temperature on the TFT device performance in this work. To the best of our knowledge, a unified TCAD analysis that concurrently varies dielectric material, dielectric thickness, and operating temperature for ZnO TFTs while evaluating analog FoMs (TGF, VEA) has not been systematically presented; prior works addressed subsets of these variables. Additionally, it has been observed that high density analog applications encounter difficulties in achieving improved device performance, such as low power operation. The TFTs can be successfully made to operate at low voltages with reduced subthreshold swing using a variety of techniques, such as changing the material, temperature variability, and geometric dimension scaling23–25. Many researchers view scaling the dimension of TFT as a potential approach to meet current device requirements. The dielectric thickness is likewise scaled by scaling. Downscaling has been shown to improve electrical parameters such as current ratio, field effect mobility and subthreshold slope, but at the expense of increasing tunneling gate leakage current, according to Kumar et al.26. In response, Vyas et al.27 proposed that high-k dielectric materials, such as Al2O3, HfO2, and others, could enhance TFTs’ electrical performance. High-k material has the same effect of scaling SiO2 without increasing the leakage current because it is physically thicker without being electrically thicker. The impact of gate dielectrics on oxide-TFT performance has been examined in previous experimental and simulation studies (e.g., S. Vyas et al.27, 2018; M. Labed et al.28, 2019; P. Kaur et al., 202229, taking reliability and high-κ integration into consideration. Nevertheless, there is a dearth of thorough research that concurrently investigates dielectric material, dielectric thickness, and operating temperature for ZnO-based TFTs, validates a calibrated trap/DOS model to experiment, and reports analog figures-of-merit like transconductance generation factor (TGF) and early voltage (VEA) across these conditions. By offering (i) a calibrated TCAD model verified by experiment, (ii) a methodical material/thickness/temperature sweep, (iii) the extraction of analog FoMs pertinent to circuit designers, and (iv) useful dielectric-thickness guidelines for optimal analog performance, this work addresses that gap.

Temperature analysis is also necessary for many TFT applications, such as active-matrix liquid crystal displays (AMLCD), active-matrix organic light emitting diodes (AMOLED), biomedical devices, etc., since it indicates the device’s operating temperature range. The thermal factor kT/q sets a limit on the MOSFET’s switching speed, or SS value. The MOSFET’s minimum SS value is 60 mV/decade in an ideal scenario, but in a real-world scenario, it might be even lower. A thorough investigation is conducted on the TFT device in order to get the impact of thermal analysis. A detailed 2D simulation of a ZnO-based TFT and its analog analysis are carried out by adjusting various physical parameters while taking into account all of the aforementioned factors. Device modeling and simulation

Physical models

Silvaco Atlas, which solves equations relating the material’s microscopic parameters and the device’s geometry to its macroscopic performance using the finite difference method, is used to create the device simulation model. By considering the thickness of 200 μm along the z-direction (perpendicular to the x-y plane) in the equations, the entire device was simulated using a two-dimensional platform (x-y plane) while retaining its basic physical characteristics. Solving governing equations for thin amorphous oxide (ZnO) takes into account fundamental physical relationships for band tail states, carrier distribution, etc.

Band tail state and carrier transport model

When a positive gate bias is applied, accumulation in a bottom gate TFT creates a channel at the interface between the gate-oxide (such as HfO2) and the channel layer (ZnO). By including the drift-diffusion model, which essentially solves the continuity equation and Poisson’s equation consistently, it is now possible to simulate the transport carriers from source to drain through the channel. For carrier distribution, Fermi-Dirac distribution model has been considered. However, it’s crucial to solve these equations using the appropriate mobility model. The effective mobility model, as explained below, is taken into consideration in order to comprehend the carrier transport mechanisms in amorphous oxide channels and then incorporate them into the simulation model30,31. Mobility modeling in bulk material involves the following: (a) characterizing electron and hole mobility in relation to doping and lattice temperature, (b) describing the transition between the low-field mobility and saturated velocity regions, and (c) characterizing saturation velocity in relation to lattice temperature. Charge transport in these disordered systems has been described by mobility models such as the Multiple Trapping and Release (MTR) Model, Percolation Theory (Effective Medium Approaches), and Field-Dependent Mobility models. Carriers travel through extended states in the MTR model, but they are momentarily trapped in the bandgap’s localized tail states. Between trapping events, transport takes place via these extended states after carriers are thermally excited from trap states into the conduction/valence band. Mobility is highly influenced by temperature. According to percolation theory, carrier transport takes place in a disordered energy landscape via a network of “favorable” paths. Carriers can move above a critical energy level, also known as the mobility edge. Conduction is dominated by pathways with greater connectivity and energy continuity. The distribution of the energy barrier can affect non-linear field dependency.

In fact, in a disordered amorphous material, the band gap contains a considerable number of randomly distributed defect states (tail states). In contrast to a-Si, the density of localized traps or band tail states in amorphous oxide semiconductors (ZnO) is substantially lower. The tail state carrier density is substantially less than the free carrier density at room temperature (300 K). Percolation thus outweighs trap limited conduction (TLC). The average height of the potential barrier (Φbarrier) and the average barrier width (Wbarrier) to distance (Dbarrier) ratio are the primary determinants of percolation conduction, as shown in Fig. 1. As a result, in the case of a TFT device, the conduction process depends on both the gate voltage and operating temperature. Interestingly, percolation takes over at high gate voltage32, while trap limited conduction predominates at low gate voltage33.

Measurement-side parasitic in the manufactured device and insufficient modeling of contact and leakage phenomena in the simulation are probably the causes of the roughly two-order magnitude difference between the simulated and experimental off-current. Specifically, compared to an ideal, contact-limited simulation, Schottky barriers or large source/drain series resistance can suppress the measured off-current; on the other hand, unmodeled trap-assisted leakage, gate leakage through the dielectric, or surface leakage paths can increase experimental Ioff current. A large portion of the discrepancy may be resolved by adding series contact resistance and non-ideal contact physics (thermionic emission, thermionic-field emission, and tunnelling) to the device simulation. Therefore, we deduce that the observed two-order mismatch is caused by a combination of contact effects and trap-assisted leakage, with contact/Schottky effects being primarily responsible for suppressing the measured off current in our devices.

TFTs rely on the alteration of a current that travels through the gate electrode between the source and drain. And, to create the field effect, the gate electrode alters the current flowing by capacitive injection of carriers adjacent to the dielectric semiconductor contact39,40. By applying appropriate value of gate voltage, the active layer or channel becomes conductive. The drain current rises linearly with rise in drain voltage. Major performance parameters of TFTs are field effect mobility, threshold voltage, Ion/Ioff current ratio and subthreshold slope. The effective carrier mobility in the TFT channel, as determined by the transfer characteristics of the transistor, is known as field effect mobility. When a gate electric field is applied, it describes how effectively carriers, i.e., electrons and holes move through the channel. Carriers travel through the channel more quickly when there is high field effect mobility, which raises the current at the same gate voltage. It also shows the quality of the channel. Defects, scattering, interface states, crystallinity (which is crucial for oxide TFTs like ZnO and IGZO), and surface roughness all have a significant impact on this mobility. Field Effect Mobility, which is derived from the transistor’s current-voltage characteristics, is the mobility of charge carriers in the TFT channel when the gate electric field is present. The gate’s ability to modulate channel conductivity is directly reflected in this. In addition, the mobility seen in TFTs differs from the typical intrinsic bulk mobility of semiconductors. It is because of charge transfer is made possible by the narrow channel (thin film) in TFTs42. Field effect mobility in TFTs is determined by the following relation at low VDS43.

The lowest gate-to-source voltage at which a conductive channel forms between the source and drain and permits a sizable drain current to flow is known as the threshold voltage () of a TFT44. Very few carriers are present in the channel below , indicating that the transistor is in the OFF state. Again, sufficient carriers are induced above , indicating the formation of a channel and a rapid increase in current. A low threshold voltage can be used for low-power operation and faster switching, while a high can be used for slower switching but better OFF-state leakage control. It is a crucial factor that controls TFT stability, power consumption, and switching behavior. One method for evaluating this parameter is to extrapolate it linearly from the IDS-VGS curve at very low VDS values.

For digital switching applications (such as logic circuits and displays), a high current ratio allows the TFT to distinguish between ON and OFF states with clarity. Poor device performance is indicated by a low current ratio, which also suggests a high leakage current or a weak ON current. A TFT’s ability to transition between conducting (ON) and insulating (OFF) states is gauged by its current ratio (Ion/Ioff). Greater values indicate more energy-efficient devices, reduced leakage, and improved switching performance. Ion/Ioff current ratio is the ratio of ‘on’ current to the minimum measured current (off current). A higher ‘on’ current gives better driving capability. An ‘off’ current corresponds to leakage current. Hence, a higher Ion/Ioff ratio is preferred45,46.

In the subthreshold (weak inversion) region of a TFT, the subthreshold slope (SS) is a parameter that indicates how well the gate voltage regulates the drain current. It is the amount of change in gate voltage needed to alter the drain current by a factor of 10, or one order of magnitude. Faster switching, improved gate control, and a sharper turn-on are all correlated with lower SS. A higher SS denotes poorer electrostatic control, more leakage, and slower switching. A value of SS = 60mV/dec is reported for optimal MOSFETs operating at room temperature (300 K). However, SS is typically much higher (100 mV/dec to several hundred mV/dec) for TFTs (ZnO, IGZO, a-Si, etc.) because of the following: trap states at the semiconductor/dielectric interface, bulk defects in the semiconductor, and poor dielectric quality. A TFT’s subthreshold slope indicates how abruptly it turns on. Better switching speed, less leakage, and more energy-efficient operation are all associated with lower SS. Generally, SS is the inverse of the slope of log (ID) at different gate voltage as per Eq. (11)47.

Furthermore, analog properties like transconductance (gm), drain conductance (gd), transconductance generation factor (TGF) and early voltage (VEA) have also been studied. Transconductance (gm) is the rate at which the drain current (ID) changes in relation to the gate-to-source voltage (VGS) in a thin film transistor or any field-effect transistor when the drain-to-source voltage (VDS) remains constant. It indicates the degree to which the gate voltage regulates the channel current. A higher transconductance indicates better amplification capability because a small change in VGS results in a large change in drain current. The gain capability of TFTs in analog circuits is directly determined by their conductance, which is a measurement of the drain current’s sensitivity to gate voltage. gm is the derivative of drain current with respect to VGS at constant VDS28.

The rate at which the drain current changes in relation to the VDS while maintaining a constant VGS is known as the drain conductance (gd) of a TFT. In the saturation region, gd quantifies the degree to which the drain current is dependent on the VDS. The ideal TFT would have zero drain conductance, or infinite output resistance, since the drain current should ideally be independent of VDS at saturation. However, in real TFTs, the drain current still rises with VDS, resulting in a finite gd, because of trap states, mobility degradation, and channel length modulation. Because it directly affects output resistance, gd, a crucial parameter for analog performance, measures how sensitive drain current is to VDS in a TFT’s saturation region. gd is determined by differentiating the drain current Eq. (8), with respect to VDS, keeping VGS constant48.

The ratio of gm to drain current is known as the Transconductance Generation Factor (TGF) of a TFT. TGF indicates the transconductance generation efficiency of a TFT per unit of current consumption. For low-power analog/RF design, a high TGF indicates that the device can achieve strong gate control (high gm) while using less current. Poor efficiency is indicated by a low TGF, meaning that more current is required to produce the same gain. The gm /ID methodology is frequently used by designers in analog and radio frequency circuits to optimize power vs. performance trade-offs. Since TFTs (such as ZnO, IGZO, and HfO2-based) typically have lower mobility than crystalline MOSFETs, TGF aids in comparing efficiency across various materials and dielectrics. Influence of high- κ dielectric material on the performance of ZnO TFTs

Since the last few decades, SiO2 has been the most widely used gate dielectric material. The size of the transistor can also be diminished by reducing the SiO2 gate dielectric thickness, which in turn raises the gate capacitances per unit area, thus enhanced the performance of the transistor. However, diminishing the SiO2 gate dielectric thickness beyond a certain limit, can lose its dependability due to a spike in tunneling gate leakage current, which may eventually result in higher power consumption and subsequently impair the transistor performance. To tackle this issue, researchers developed the idea of effective oxide thickness (EOT). EOT signifies increased physical gate dielectric thickness without an increase in electrical or effective gate dielectric thickness. This is easily accomplished by raising the gate dielectric’s dielectric constant, or ‘k’ which lowers gate leakage current while maintaining ideal gate capacitance. Considering SiO2 gate dielectric with dielectric constant ‘k’=3.9, and if we consider another gate dielectric material, ‘X’, with dielectric constant ‘k’=39, then the material ‘X’ can be 10 times thicker than SiO2 gate dielectric. As a result, high-k dielectric materials are physically thicker rather than electrically thicker, raising gate capacitance per unit area without commensurate leakage consequences4,27,41,49. In an optimal state, each carrier (or electron) is on the gate’s uphill side. The carriers have an approximate likelihood of landing on the downstream end of the gate despite really splashing over it when the gate dimension is incredibly thin. This is when gate leakage occurs, which is known as quantum mechanical tunneling. As mentioned, thinner gate cause tunneling current and thus results in high power consumption. By raising the gate dielectric’s physical thickness, this tunneling current can be decreased. The performance of the transistors is once again degraded when the electrical oxide thickness of SiO2 increases along with its physical thickness. As one of the strategies opted to enable even further miniaturization of the devices, high-k dielectrics have been utilized in semiconductor-based devices to substitute low k-SiO2 dielectric or any other dielectric50–52. Influence of thickness of dielectric on the analog performance of ZnO TFTs

Hence, continuing our discussion in the last section, analog performance parameters are strongly affected by the gate dielectric materials. Replacing the low-κ dielectric material with high-κ material results with low leakage current, low power consumption and enhanced performance devices. Capacitance is inversely proportional to the thickness of gate dielectric materials. Thus, capacitance increases with the decrease in thickness of dielectric material. This induces large amount of charge carriers at the same output voltage, and thus cause increase in drain current. In order to study the impact of dielectric thickness, TFT with HfO2 as dielectric has been studied for five different thickness, 80, 120, 160, 200 and 240 nm.

Influence of temperature on the analog performance of ZnO TFTs

In this section, effect of temperature on various performance parameter of TFTs, with HfO2 as dielectric and with thickness of 120 nm, is studied. There were two phases to the optimization of the HfO₂ gate dielectric. Initially, the dielectric’s physical characteristics, particularly its thickness, were optimized at a specific operating temperature. This method was chosen because the gate-oxide capacitance (), the electric-field coupling into the channel, and the TFT’s baseline electrical performance (µFE, VTh, SS, and Ion/Ioff) are all directly influenced by the dielectric thickness. Before introducing thermal variations, geometric and electrostatic factors are stabilized by determining the ideal thickness at a fixed temperature. It would be challenging to distinguish geometric effects (thickness-dependent ) from thermally induced material changes if the operating temperature were changed first, as this would concurrently alter microstructure, defect density, and interface chemistry.

The second stage involved temperature-dependent simulations after the ideal thickness (120 nm HfO₂) was determined. This was required because, in the ZnO semiconductor, temperature mainly affects trap occupancy, interface states, and Fermi-level movement rather than the dielectric’s geometric parameters. The impact of thermal effects could be separated and assessed without interference from thickness-dependent changes in or field distribution by maintaining a constant thickness throughout temperature analysis. Consequently, a clean, decoupled assessment of the two effects and a more rigorous interpretation of the device physics result from optimizing the thickness first and then conducting temperature analysis.

The effect of temperature variation (from 300 to 500 K, with a 50 K gap) on TFT’s analog performance is explained in this section. The covalent bond inside the body material’s lattice begins to break when the temperature rises above room temperature, producing a large number of electron and hole pairs (EHPs). The intrinsic carrier concentration of semiconductors () is directly proportional to the EHP generation rate56,57. According to , the increases exponentially with temperature. Due to a shift in the fermi level with respect to the trap distribution within the energy gap, the transistor’s performance degrades with the increment in temperature57. Three factors are frequently held accountable for the degradation: (i) Electron/hole trapping at the junction of two layers of material (ii) the development of ionised oxygen vacancies, and (iii) the emergence of donor-like defects in ZnO. The first factor shifts the threshold voltages to peak values, whereas the second and third factors shifts the threshold voltage to the negative values58. Semiconductor oxide material are widely used in AMOLED, sensors, medical display circuit and many other applications. Operating temperature has a strong impact on the stability of circuit’s applications. For example, for the application based on medical circuits prefer to work in the range of 300–400 K. Thus, for such applications, temperature stability needs to be optimized and studied.

Conclusion

The study examined the effects of temperature, thickness, and gate dielectric material on TFT performance using ATLAS TCAD simulations. The best dielectric was found to be HfO₂; thinner layers improve capacitance and current, but if they are reduced too much, they result in increased leakage and a low threshold voltage. With a thickness of 120 nm, HfO₂ demonstrated exceptional low-temperature performance, exhibiting a sharp SS (~ 0.31 V/decade), high mobility (~ 54 cm²/V-s), and a very high Ion/Ioff current ratio (~ 10¹¹). At the end, in order to examine the thermal stability and reliability, thermal analysis of the device was performed. These TFTs show improved performance for room temperature to moderate temperatures, but they perform less well for digital switching at high temperatures (> 400 K). The Ion/Ioff ratio decreased, mobility deteriorated, and leakage increased as the temperature rose, but SS stayed constant, indicating robust gate control. Future TFTs will need high-k dielectrics with a large bandgap, strong thermal stability, low trap density, stable interfaces, and good morphology. The current model does not fully account for interface state density variation with temperature and bias stress, despite the strong correlation between simulation and experimental data. There was no explicit modeling of effects like contact resistance, self-heating, and bias instability. Furthermore, the study ignores potential grain boundary scattering in polycrystalline ZnO films, which may affect mobility at higher temperatures, and assumes uniform dielectric properties. In order to more accurately forecast long-term device stability, future research may concentrate on: including bias stress and trap dynamics models. Also, investigating various high-k dielectrics or bilayer dielectrics (such as HfO₂/Al₂O₃ or HfO₂/Si₃N₄) in order to minimize interface traps and leakage. Looking into doping effects and bandgap engineering to enhance thermal stability and carrier transport even more. Investigating nanostructured ZnO channels for improved mobility and reduced defect density, such as nanorods or nanowires.
