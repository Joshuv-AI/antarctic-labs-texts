AAD-2004 through clearing H2O2 reduces astrocyte proliferation and promotes neural regeneration after spinal cord injury
Authors: Xiaodan Mi, Keming Shan, Xiangming Ye, Ruidong Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo assess the effect of AAD-2004 on spinal cord injury (SCI) and to explore its mechanism, we employed an in vitro model using OGD/R-challenged astrocytes to investigate the effects of AAD-2004 against cell death (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling, tunel), oxidative stress (H2O2 level), and the expression of the key neuroprotective factor MAP2.AAD-2004[2-hydroxy-5-[2-(4-trifluoromethylphenyl)-ethylaminobenzoic acid] is a hydrogen peroxide(H2O2) scavenger primarily used for the treatment of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and Alzheimer disease that has demonstrated certain neuroprotective properties. In parallel, modified allen’s method was adopted, further exploring the potential molecular mechanism in vivo. Based on these conditions, histological and behavioral analysis were performed by Nissl staining, basso mouse scale and footprint analysis. The level of molecules associated with glial scar formation, nerve regeneration, axonal regeneration and H2O2 level were analyzed using western blot, immunofluorescence staining and H2O2 kit. AAD-2004 significantly improved the movement function after SCI and inhibited the proliferation of astrocytes, thus preventing the formation of glial scar by inhibiting of H2O2. At the same time, AAD-2004 promoted nerve regeneration, and the effect was due to neuronal regeneration and axonal regeneration pathways. The expression levels of GFAP and vimentin were significantly downregulated in AAD-2004-treated, and the expression level of Ki67 and PH3 were downregulated. The mean fluorescence intensity of neuronal regeneration (Neun+and MAP2+) and axonal regeneration-related (NF+ and GAP43+) were significantly upregulated after AAD-2004 treatment. Scavenging H2O2 level is a viable therapeutic strategy, and that AAD-2004 is prospective, and that scavenging H2O2 facilitated nerve regeneration and inhibited glial scar formation for SCI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33322-x.Keywords: Hydrogen peroxide, Glial scar, Spinal cord injury, Neural regeneration, OGD/RSubject terms: Biochemistry, Molecular neuroscience, Regeneration and repair in the nervous system

Introduction

SCI, as a serious and incurable disease, not only leads to permanent disability for the patient, but also creates a heavy financial burden on the family and society1. Due to the condition and reduced mobility, the patient may also develop other complications such as cerebrovascular disease, osteoporosis, which severely reduces the quality of survival and further adds to the financial burden2. A study systematically reviewed the literature that published between January 1990 and November 2020 and analyzed the overall direct medical costs of SCI during the acute phase, inpatient rehabilitation phase, first year after injury and subsequent years3. The results showed that the average cost of care for patients with SCI was a staggering $612,590 during the acute phase of care, after transfer to the inpatient rehabilitation phase, the average cost remained high at $443,040, and the overall medical overhead during the first year post-injury climbed to an average of $1,156,400. The cost of treatment for this patient typically includes, but is not limited to, the cost of surgery, medications, assistive devices, and rehabilitation, with the more severe the condition, the higher the costs incurred4,5. It is important to note that these are only direct medical costs and do not include the patient’s lost income due to the disability. In the face of high treatment costs and limited treatment options, innovative and effective treatment strategies are imperative6,7.

There are two stages of SCI: the first stage (primary injury) is the destruction of spinal cord structures, including nerve parenchyma, axonal connections, etc. Subsequent secondary injury results in mechanical, physiological, and biochemical alterations of tissues8,9. The clinical phenotype of secondary injury is extremely complex and includes many pathophysiological changes such as increased cell permeability, excitotoxicity10,11 and acute secondary injury phenomena are manifested after the primary injury, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), inflammation, edema and necrosis, and other changes will further exacerbate the secondary injury, and ultimately limit neural repair and regeneration after SCI through characteristic manifestations such as neuronal apoptosis and glial scarring9. Based on the complex pathophysiologic changes of SCI, it is important to develop new strategies for the treatment of SCI by exploring approaches11,12.

Astrocytes are the most abundant glial cells, and at first their role was categorized as a kind of glue that binds the brain13. Today, it is becoming an important breakthrough for many neurological and psychiatric disorders, and diversity is increasingly recognised, a feature which, together with its highly complex morphology, allows them to play a variety of roles in different regions of the central nervous system14,15. It is shown that the density of astrocytes in each region of the central nervous system(CNS) is not only proportional to the density of neurons, but also that in terms of numbers, the astrocyte population does not vary much, which may be required for the maintenance of homeostasis in the organism. In addition to this, astrocytes, as part of the functional neurovascular unit (NVU), are also closely related to endothelial cell density16. Ultimately, there are regional differences in astrocytes that are driven by upregulated genes, which further contributes to their functional diversity17. In the AD model, there are genes that are closely related to astrocyte morphology and regional and reactive properties, among which specific astrocyte AST6 and AST1 subpopulations have emerged as potential therapeutic targets for AD because of their association with cholesterol homeostasis, K+ homeostasis, neurotransmitter homeostasis, and synapse function18. Astrocyte morphological diversity due to gene expression may be a common feature of different CNS disorders and should be given more attention19.

The astrocyte ridges show typical proliferation and hypertrophy after SCI, and reactive astrogliosis (RA) is a common pathological manifestation present in SCI20. Most typically the changes are enhanced expression of glial fibrillary acid protein (GFAP), and astrocytes in the acute phase of injury play a major role in limiting primary damage, whereas the chronic phase of SCI is characterised by the activation of astrocytes, reactive hyperplasia and the formation of glial scarring, which are the main pathological features of SCI, and become a major obstacle to the main barrier to nerve regeneration21. Reactive astrocyte scarring has been regarded as an unfavourable factor in the repair process of SCI, mainly due to the fact that scarring form a physical barrier that inhibit axonal extension and regeneration during CNS injury22.

Recently, Heejung Chun et al. found that H₂O₂ is an important switch that regulates astrocyte reactivity, and that severe reactive astrocytes are the result of high levels of H₂O₂23. On the one hand, high levels of H₂O₂ stimulate astrocyte activation and secretion of pro-inflammatory-related cytokines, exacerbating inflammatory responses and cellular damage.on the other hand, appropriate concentrations of H₂O₂ may also be involved in neuroprotective and repair processes, which promote neuronal regeneration and functional recovery by modulating astrocyte responsiveness and signal. Therefore, what role H₂O₂ play in the pathophysiological mechanisms of SCI remains to be investigated.

In mammals, H₂O₂ is an endogenous oxidant as far as redox signal and regulation are concerned. It is the most stable of the reactive oxygen species and is involved in the regulation of protein function24. Due to its physicochemical properties, H₂O₂ is also able to act as a messenger, transmitting redox signal from the site where it is generated to the target site. In various oxygen metabolites, H₂O₂ is involved in redox transduction by controlling single enzyme activities or at the transcriptional level or causing oxidative damage to biomolecules (oxidative stress). Under healthy homeostatic condition, the production and elimination is balanced, but under conditions of oxidative stress, the superoxide anion O₂⁻ and consequently H₂O₂ are produced in excess25. Multiple mechanisms exist to increase ROS in SCI, including H₂O₂. Since cell death due to increased ROS production is characteristic of SCI and contributes to the progression of associated CNS diseases26. ROS further exacerbate secondary SCI, where the change from O₂⁻ to H₂O₂ formation after injury has been verified27. In the report, H₂O₂ level was significantly higher than the temporal changes in normal rat for 12 h after injury, thus suggesting that alleviating H₂O₂ level may be an effective way to reduce secondary injury.Riluzole is a sodium-glutamate antagonist that has shown favorable results in promoting recovery in pre-clinical models of traumatic SCI and in early phase clinical trials.

AAD-2004 [2-hydroxy-5-[2-(4-trifluoromethylphenyl)-ethylaminobenzoic acid] is a derivative of 5-aminosalicylate that exhibiting nanomolar antioxidant activity in primary cortical cell. It was recently demonstrated that AAD-2004 reduced disease-induced neuronal damage in the model of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis(ALS)28, including autophagosome formation, axonal pathology, and motor neuron degeneration, while improving motor function and extending lifespan. Similarly, in an Alzheimer’s disease(AD) model29, AAD-2004 significantly inhibited neuronal loss and neuroinflammation-associated atrophy.With the dual pharmacological actions, AAD-2004 did not cause gastric damage at a dose 400-fold higher than efficacy doses, prevented protein aggregation and axonopathy, and improved neurological function and survival better than riluzole30. Concurrent blockade of free radical and microsomal prostaglandin E synthase-1-mediated PGE2 production improves safety and efficacy in a mouse model of ALS. AAD-2004 is less likely to cause side effects, and it is effective in preventing CNS inflammation, motor neuron degeneration, axonal damage, thereby reducing motor dysfunction and improving survival, which demonstrate its potential to mitigate the damage caused by SCI.

In this study, we established model of SCI and reproduced the increase in hydrogen peroxide after SCI. We then confirmed that AAD-2004 enhanced the recovery of motor function and the repair of damaged tissue morphology after injury. Importantly, by analysing the levels of genes associated with reactive and scar-forming astrocytes, we found that AAD-2004 significantly inhibited glial scar formation and promoted nerve regeneration. In conclusion, our study provides molecular insights into functional recovery after SCI and confirms that modulation of H₂O₂ level can serve as a potential treatment for injury to the spinal cord and possibly other parts of the CNS.

Materials and methods

Primary cell culture

Primary astrocytes were isolated from the spinal cord of postnatal day 1–3 (P1-P3) C57BL/6J mouse pups. Briefly, the dissected spinal cord was digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, triturated, and the resulting cell suspension was plated in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Senbeijia, BC-SE-FBS07) onto poly-D-lysine-coated (sangon, E607014) flasks31. After reaching confluence, microglia were removed by orbital shaking to achieve highly pure astrocyte cultures. Primary neurons were obtained from embryonic C57BL/6J mice (E14–18) by digesting the dissociated cerebral cortices with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA. Cells were triturated in the presence of DNase I and plated in Neurobasal Plus (Gibco, 21103049) Medium containing B-27(Gibco, 17504044) supplement on poly-D-lysine/laminin-coated plates. To inhibit glial growth, cytosine arabinoside (MCE, HY-13605) was added after 2–3 days in vitro. Half of the culture medium was changed every 3–4 days. Neurons were typically used for experiments between 7 and 14 DIV32.

Neuron-astrocyte co-culture

The neuronal-astrocyte co-culture system was adapted from a previously described protocol33. Briefly, neurons were seeded onto poly-d-lysine (PDL)-coated 6-well plates. Astrocytes were seeded onto 0.4 mm transwell filters (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA). The transwell filters were positioned above the neuronal layer, and both sides of the culture medium were replaced with glucose-free DMEM medium(Gibco,11965092).Prior to OGD/R, the inserts were placed above neuronal cultures to allow interaction between the two cell types under identical conditions.

OGD/R model

To establish an OGD/R model, primary astrocytes at 70–80% confluence were washed with pre-warmed phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)and incubated in glucose-free DMEM. Cultures were then transferred into a hypoxic chamber (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, 94% N₂) and maintained for 4 h. After hypoxia, the medium was replaced with normal medium and cells were returned to normoxic conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂)34.

Experimental groups and interventions in vitro

The control group was maintained under standard culture conditions without any intervention. The model group was exposed to 25 µM H₂O₂ to induce oxidative stress. For the AAD-2004 intervention group(model + AAD-2004), cultures were treated with 25 µM H₂O₂ in combination with 1 mM AAD-2004 (BioChemPartner, BCP44811).

Apoptosis assay

Neuronal cell death was quantitatively assessed TUNEL with a detection kit from (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China).Following fixation and permeabilisation, cells were incubated at 37 °C for one hour with a mixture containing fluorescein isothiocyanate-labelled 12-dUTP. After washing, the cells were incubated with a mounting medium containing DAPI(Beijing Saierbo Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Animals

The experimental animals used in this experiment were SPF grade C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old, 19–20 g) purchased from Zhejiang Vital River Experimental Animal Technology Co. LTD (Charles River lab China). Mice were housed in cages in groups (≤ 5 mice per group) and had free access to food and water at a temperature of 23 ± 1 °C and 40% humidity. The daily environment was subjected to a 12-hour day/night cycle. Animal care & use and all protocols in this study were under the guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No: 20241221100145). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Administration and animal group

The animals were randomly divided into three groups (Sham + V, SCI + V and SCI + AAD-2004). AAD-2004 (obtained from BioChemPartner, BCP44811) was dissolved in dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) as vehicleand then administered via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection at a dosage of 3.3 mg/kg daily for a period of 14 days. To investigate the effect of AAD-2004 in SCI, both SCI + V and Sham + V groups received injections of an equivalent volume of the vehicle solution. The concentration and administration route of AAD-2004 were determined based on prior research23.

H2O2 content assay

Animals were anaesthetized using isoflurane, and spinal cord tissue was quickly dissected from vertebral canal of the spine for western blot analysis and H2O2 production analysis. The H2O2 concentration in vivo and in vitro was measured utilizing an H2O2 content assay kit (Solarbio, BC3595). The absorbance was assessed with a UV-visible spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 415 nm, and the H2O2 concentrations were subsequently calculated following the manufacturer instruction.

Behavioral analysis

The effects of AAD-2004 were measured with the BMS at 1, 3, 7, 14, and 21 days post-injury. All animals were evaluated by one or two observers who had confidentiality about the experimental conditions and were scored from 0 to 9 according to a scoring system as described previously36.

Footprint analysis

To evaluate hindlimb motor function, mice were made to traverse a paper-lined runway — a method adapted from previous studies36. Their forelimbs and hindlimbs were coated with non-toxic black and orange ink, respectively, to enable qualitative assessment of stride patterns.

Nissl staining

Mice were anaesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (1%, 0.1 mL/10 g) at days 21 after surgery. Animals were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate buffer for Nissl staining. The number and morphology of neurons in the anterior horn of spinal cord and the area of damage are presented by nissl staining of cross-sectional and long-sectional sections, respectively. The specific operation protocol was as follows. After first perfusing mice with pre-cooled 0.1 mol/L PBS and 4% PFA, the spinal cord was immersed in 4% PFA for 24 h and then transferred to ethanol solution for dehydration. After hyaline and paraffin-embedded tissues, the spinal cord was cut into 10 μm-thick transverse and horizontal sections using a freezing microtome (Thermo, USA). Paraffin sections were dried and residual paraffin was removed with xylene and alcohol. Sections that underwent the above steps were immersed in nissl staining solution for 40 min (about 55℃). Finally dehydrated, transparent, and neutral resin-sealed sections were used for photographic image acquisition (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Consistent with nissl staining, the tissues were fixed, embedded, and sectioned, and the sections were placed at a temperature of 92–96 °C for 10–15 min for antigenic repair. The spinal cord tissue sections were blocked for 1 h in 5% BSA at room temperature, for overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C, and washed three times in PBS and then were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with appropriate secondary antibodies. Secondary antibodies included donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor488 (A21206, Invitrogen, 1:1000), Cy3 conjugated Goat Anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:300, Servicebio, GB21301), donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor546 (A10040, Invitrogen, 1:1000).VS120 was used for fluorescence imaging.

For cultured cells, IF was performed to assess astrocytic activation and neuronal integrity. After OGD/R and corresponding treatments, cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including rabbit anti-MAP2 (8707 S, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:500) and mouse anti-GFAP (MAB360, Millipore, 1:500). After three washes in PBS, cells were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies such as Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG(A21206/A10040, Invitrogen, 1:1000) and Cy3-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (GB21301, Servicebio, 1:300) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI(Beyotime, P0131). Images were captured using a confocal fluorescence microscope(Leica, STELLARIS 5)and all fluorescence signal data were quantified using Image J software.

Western blot (WB)

Animals were anaesthetized using isoflurane, and spinal cord tissue was quickly dissected from vertebral canal of the spine. Ice-cold RIPA buffer containing PMSE (S1020, Coolaber) was incubated on the disease for 30 min, then ground for 60 s in a grinder, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 30 min to extract the supernatant. After detecting the protein concentration, it was adjusted to a uniform concentration with 1×PBS. Next, extraction was performed with 5 × Loading buffer (G2013, Servicebio). Finally, protein samples were boiled at 100 °C for 10 min. Samples were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore).After blocking in 5% skim milk for 2–3 h, the immunoblots were incubated with primary antibodies (Vimentin: ab92547,Abcam,1:1000,GAPDH: Cell Signal Technology, D16H11,1:10000).After three times washing, the blots were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. Western blots were detected using an ECL assay kit (Bio-Rad, USA) and were analyzed using Image Lab.

Statistical analysis

All data presented represent results as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was performed using Student’s t-test or using ANOVA, when the values were not normally distributed, the Kruskal-Wallis H-test (followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test)was used. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Inhibiting of H2O2 weakened reactivity in astrocytes and exerted neuroprotective effect

To investigate whether AAD-2004 exerted directly protective effect in astrocytes, we applied AAD-2004 to astrocytes subjected to oxidative stress (Fig. 1a). Primary astrocytes were treated with AAD-2004 immediately following H2O2 exposure. As depicted in Fig. 1b, AAD-2004 significantly reduced H2O2 production in reactive astrocytes. Furthermore, in co-cultures of primary astrocytes and neurons, we observed that AAD-2004 significantly mitigated neuronal damage post-OGD/R. Cells treated with AAD-2004 exhibited markedly improved outcomes regarding neuronal apoptosis (as assessed by TUNEL staining) (Fig. 1c&f) and microtubule associated protein 2 (MAP2) (Fig. 1d&e) labelled neuronal axonal injury. In summary, these findings indicated that in vitro, AAD-2004 attenuated astrocytic reactivity by reducing H2O2 level and exerted neuroprotective effect.

AAD-2004 promoted the recovery of motor function after SCI

Post-SCI motor function was assessed using nissl staining, behavioral analysis and footprint analysis. When compared to the sham-operated group, nissl staining revealed a more prominent glial scar in the lesion area of the SCI group and the number of motor neurons was significantly decreased. In contrast, the sham group exhibited more intact neuronal cells, with preserved neuronal morphology and clearly visible nucleoli within the spinal cord tissues, indicating no nerve damage. Conversely, the damaged spinal cord tissues displayed a loss of normal structure, disrupted cellular integrity, and lysed and condensed neuronal nucleus, accompanied by diminished and shrunken nissl bodies. The nucleoli were not clearly discernible in these damaged tissue. After pharmacological intervention, the structure be clearer and more complete, the reduction in the number of motor neuron was alleviated, although small cavities in the gray matter on the injured side still existed (Fig. 2e,&2f). Next, the recovery of hindlimb motor function was assessed by BMS and footprint analysis, we found that the AAD-2004 group performed significantly better than the SCI group on stride length and overall stepping ability after post-injury day 21(Fig. 2a, b and d, p < 0.05). In conclusion, these results suggest that AAD-2004 contributed to functional recovery after SCI.

AAD-2004 attenuated SCI-induced motor dysfunction by reducing H2O2 concentration

To quantify changes in H2O2, we established SCI model in wild-type C57BL/6J mice and then measured H2O2 concentration at 14 days after injury. As shown, concentration of H2O2 increased significantly after SCI (Fig. 2c, p < 0.01), and H2O2 was significantly decreased in the AAD-2004 group compared to the SCI group (Fig. 2c, p < 0.05). These results suggested that SCI led to an increasing trend in H2O2, which was reversed by AAD-2004.

AAD-2004 inhibited glial Scar formation in SCI mice

According to previous studies37, glial scarring is an important factor in the SCI treatment, and inhibiting the formation of glial scarring has a neuroprotective effect. To assess glial scarring, we collected spinal cord tissue at day 14 and evaluated the expression of GFAP and vimentin. Administration of AAD-2004 significantly inhibited the upregulation of GFAP and vimentin expression after SCI as revealed by IF staining (Fig. 3b, p < 0.01). Meanwhile, the expression level of vimentin protein was significantly up-regulated after surgery as detected by WB (p < 0.01). However, the expression level was down-regulated in the AAD-2004 group of mice (p < 0.01, Fig. 3).

AAD-2004 reduced astrocyte proliferation after SCI

It has been shown that the closer the area of injury, the more pronounced the proliferation of astrocytes, so we further tested whether AAD-2004 inhibit glial scar formation by suppressing the reduction of astrocyte proliferation in the glial scar area. By double-labeled immunofluorescence of GFAP with Ki67 and PH3, we found an increase in the number of Ki67 and PH3 per astrocyte compared to the sham-operated group. In addition, the mean fluorescence intensity of Ki67+/GFAP+ and PH3+/GFAP+ in the injured region of AAD-2004-treated animals was significantly decreased (Fig. 4,p < 0.01). In summary, AAD-2004 effectively reduced astrocyte proliferation after SCI and further inhibited glial scar formation and expansion of the injured area after SCI.

AAD-2004 facilitated axonal regeneration after SCI

It has previously been reported that neurofilament (NF, neurofilament) and growth-associated protein-43 (GAP43) were used to analyze axon regeneration.NF are intermediate filaments found in the cytoplasm of neurons. Together with microtubules and microfilaments, they form the neuronal cytoskeleton and are involved in axonal transport, whereas GAP43, which promotes axon growth, regeneration, and axon guidance, is considered to be a hallmark protein for axon growth during nerve development and injury repair. Therefore, we utilized NF and GAP43 as markers of axonal regeneration and found that NF+ and GAP43+ positive axons in the injury region were significantly reduced compared to the sham-operated group (Fig. 6, p < 0.01). After treatment with AAD-2004, the mean fluorescence intensity of GAP43+ and NF+ was significantly increased (p < 0.05,p < 0.01). These results suggested that AAD-2004 facilitated axonal regeneration after SCI.

Discussion

SCI is recognized as a major medical challenge, often resulting in high rates of disability. The pathophysiology of SCI can be divided into two phases: primary and secondary injury. The primary injury involves irreversible damage to the spinal cord. Consequently, it is essential to manage secondary injury effectively after SCI, with the goals of promoting the survival of remaining neurons, preserving neural function, and facilitating spinal cord repair. While the exact mechanisms underlying secondary injury are not fully understood, factors such as oxidative stress38 and the formation of glial scars39 have been recognized as significant contributors to this secondary damage.

H₂O₂ is known as a reactive oxygen species in the body. Initially regarded as deleterious, increasing research demonstrates its diverse physiological roles under different conditions. Following SCI, primary injury causes vascular rupture, cellular damage, and disruption of the blood-spinal cord barrier, initiating a cascade of pathological events, including hemorrhage, glutamate release, acute inflammation, and apoptosis40. Moreover, acute injury exacerbates excitotoxicity due to excessive glutamate and overproduction of ROS. During mitochondrial dysfunction induced by impaired energy metabolism, ROS such as superoxide anions (O₂⁻) are produced41. A small portion of these ROS can be neutralized by antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, and glutathione (GSH)24. However, the majority of ROS persist, damaging cellular components such as proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids, ultimately impairing their structural integrity and functions. As early as 1999, Liu et al. used a rat SCI model to confirm increased hydrogen peroxide concentrations in the early stages of SCI. Their study suggested that superoxide anions (O₂⁻) and H₂O₂ may not directly cause cellular damage. Instead, prolonged hydroxyl radical (•OH) production, derived from H₂O₂ through the Fenton reaction, might mediate cellular injury27. The continuous production of ROS and their derived products post-SCI contributes significantly to secondary injury, indicating that scavenging O₂⁻and H₂O₂ could be an effective therapeutic strategy for mitigating such damage42. Chronic SCI outcomes are often hindered by glial scar formation, which is a major obstacle to neuronal regeneration. Following SCI, astrocytes at the injury site transition into reactive astrocytes, which subsequently mature into scar-forming astrocytes43,44. These scar-forming astrocytes fail to provide trophic support or protection to surviving neurons and oligodendrocytes. Instead, they contribute to neuronal and oligodendrocyte apoptosis. Glial scars, along with their secreted extracellular matrix components such as chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs)45 and vimentin46, are well-established inhibitors of axonal regeneration. These molecular inhibitors are also implicated in a range of neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, ALS, and multiple sclerosis. A recent study by Heejung Chun et al. demonstrated that severe reactive astrocytes exacerbate Alzheimer’s disease pathology through excessive H₂O₂ production23. Their findings revealed that H₂O₂, produced in reactive astrocytes, drives glial activation, chronic neuroinflammation, neuronal loss, brain atrophy, cognitive deficits, and eventual mortality. AAD-2004, with more pronounced therapeutic effect than riluzole, also has a demonstrated significant benefits in the adverse effect of AD. These effects were significantly alleviated by the H₂O₂ scavenger AAD-2004.These findings highlight the potential therapeutic significance of reducing H₂O₂ levels after severe SCI to regulate astrocyte reactivity, limit the formation of scar-forming astrocytes, and ultimately promote functional recovery.

In this study, we have demonstrated for the first time that the hydrogen peroxide scavenger AAD-2004 facilitates recovery in a mouse model of SCI. Within 21 days post-injury, mice treated with AAD-2004 exhibited significantly better BMS score and stride length compared to the SCI + V group.Our findings indicate that AAD-2004 effectively reduces H₂O₂ concentration following SCI, improves neuronal health, and promotes greater neuronal survival and axonal regeneration while suppressing glial scar formation. This represents the first investigation of AAD-2004’s role in SCI, providing a new insight and potential therapeutic strategy for SCI recovery.

Previous studies have confirmed that the upregulation of GFAP and vimentin expression is a critical factor in glial scar formation and impairs functional recovery47. In this study, we evaluated the expression of GFAP and vimentin in the spinal cord using IF and western blot analysis. Consistent with prior findings, GFAP and vimentin were significantly upregulated following SCI. Our data further indicate that the downregulation of GFAP and vimentin in the SCI + AAD-2004 group suggests a potential role for H₂O₂ in modulating astrocyte reactivity post-SCI. Reducing H₂O₂ levels appears to inhibit glial scar formation, thereby fostering an environment conducive to neurological recovery. Double-label IF analysis of GFAP with Ki67 and phospho-histone H3 (PH3) revealed a significant reduction in the mean fluorescence intensity of Ki67+/GFAP+ and PH3+/GFAP+ in the injury regions of AAD-2004-treated group. These results collectively indicate that AAD-2004 mitigates astrocyte proliferation following SCI, thereby suppressing glial scar formation and limiting the expansion of the lesion area. Oxidative stress is a critical factor contributing to sustained neuronal damage long after the initial trauma48. During the chronic phase of SCI, mature glial scars further inhibit neuronal and axonal regeneration. To explore this, we examined the expression of neuronal markers, including Neun and MAP2. Our results show a sharp decline in Neun and MAP2 expression following SCI, while AAD-2004 treatment reversed these changes, suggesting that scavenging H₂O₂ may enhance neuronal survival and regeneration. The in vitro findings correlate well with the in vivo observations.Additionally, nissl staining revealed that motor neurons in the ventral horn of the AAD-2004 group exhibited morphology closer to normal tissue, further supporting its neuroprotective effects. This study highlights the therapeutic potential of AAD-2004 in mitigating oxidative stress and astrocyte proliferation, providing a foundation for future interventions targeting SCI recovery.

We also considered another crucial factor associated with the recovery of functional deficits in SCI, namely axonal regeneration and functional reconstruction, which is also linked to glial scar formation39. Axonal degeneration occurs distally over time, leading to a loss of function. The proximal portion of the axon, whether short or long, remains connected to the neuronal soma and receives some degree of nutritional support, allowing for a regenerative response. Promoting axonal regeneration has long been a primary goal in this field of research. IF analysis confirmed that, in the AAD-2004-treated group, the mean fluorescence intensity of NF+ and GAP43+ were significantly increased. This suggests that AAD-2004 promoted axonal regeneration after SCI, offering potential for enhancing recovery. Certainly, there are several limitations. First, the exact mechanism by which AAD-2004 inhibit astrocyte proliferation remains unclear. Second, the potential correlation between the inhibition of glial scarring and the promotion of axonal regeneration has not been fully determined.

In conclusion, AAD-2004, a hydrogen peroxide scavenger, can inhibit glial scar formation by reducing hydrogen peroxide levels, thereby promoting neuronal and axonal regeneration. Through intervention with AAD-2004, we found that it significantly enhances the recovery of neurological function in SCI. SCI mice showed cavity formation in the injury area with significant cell infiltration and scar formation. In contrast, the AAD-2004 group exhibited significantly smaller cavities, scar areas, and neuronal death rates compared to the SCI group. Then, the AAD-2004-treated mice consistently showed better overall hindlimbs ability. Combined, these results suggested that AAD-2004 is a clinically translatable drug for the treatment of SCI. It fills a relative gap relative to riluzole and deserves to be studied. Future research should focus on three dimensions. Firstly, at the mechanistic level, it is imperative to elucidate its specific downstream signalling pathways (such as PI3K/Akt and MAPK) to comprehensively reveal the molecular panorama from H₂O₂ clearance to cellular fate regulation. Secondly, obtaining precise pharmacokinetic parameters (such as bioavailability and spinal tissue distribution) will bridge fundamental research with clinical application. Finally, developing optimised local delivery strategies, such as hydrogel sustained-release technology, holds promise for maximising therapeutic efficacy while mitigating systemic risks. In summary, this study constitutes the foundation for advancing AAD-2004 towards clinical application and provides a clear direction for subsequent work.

Conclusion

Our research utilizing SCI mice and the H2O2 scavenger AAD-2004 suggest that clearing H2O2 is a viable and promising method for enhancing neural regeneration and reducing glial scar formation after SCI. Our findings indicate that H2O2 may function as a regulatory switch, influencing the reactivity of astrocytes in SCI. That means that AAD-2004 may be a therapeutic strategy to mitigate the severe effects associated with SCI (Fig. 7).

AAD-2004 is a promising therapeutic agent that has demonstrated safety and efficacy in the treatment of spinal cord injury. Further investigation into its pharmacological mechanisms following spinal cord injury is warranted.
