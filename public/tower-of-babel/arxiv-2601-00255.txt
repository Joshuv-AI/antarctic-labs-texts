Hydrodynamic flows induced by localized torques (rotlets) in wedge-shaped geometries
Abdallah Daddi-Moussa-Ider, Jakob Mihatsch, Michael J. Mitchell, Elsen Tjhung, Andreas M. Menzel

Abstract
Wedge-shaped geometries in low-Reynolds-number flows are of increasing importance, for instance, in the design of microfluidic devices. The corresponding Green’s functions describing the induced flow in response to a locally applied force were derived some time ago. To achieve a complete characterization of particle motion at low Reynolds numbers, we derive the flow response to locally applied torques. This is accomplished through a direct calculation based on the Fourier–Kontorovich–Lebedev transform. We then illustrate the resulting flow fields, highlighting their structure, key features, and dependence on the geometry and orientation of the applied torque. Based on these solutions, we compute the corresponding hydrodynamic mobility tensor that couples torque and motion. Owing to the broken spatial symmetry imposed by the wedge-shaped confinement, a particle subjected to a torque will experience not only rotational motion but also translational motion. These results provide analytical tools relevant for predicting and controlling particle behavior in confined microfluidic environments.



I
Introduction

Wedge-shaped geometries have been studied in hydrodynamics because of their peculiar effect on intrinsic fluid flows, and because of their practical abundance.
If a net velocity is prescribed on one surface of the wedge in radial direction under low Reynolds number conditions, vortices will be induced near the edge of the wedge, specifically at sufficiently low opening angles of the wedge
Moffatt 1964
. For instance, such net motion along one surface can be introduced by radial motion of the surface under no-slip conditions. Wedge-shaped geometries were further investigated under low-Reynolds-number conditions concerning their induced flows due to chemically active surfaces. It was demonstrated that resulting chemical concentration gradients can induce corresponding vortices near the edge of the wedge
Nowak and Lisicki 2025
.

Generally, any microfluidic channel bounded by straight walls features wedge-like shapes near the edges of confinement, even if simply of the specific angle of ninety degrees. Additionally, wedges of different opening angles were introduced in microfluidic devices, for example, to promote cell sorting, also with medical applications in mind
Yang et al. 2018
.
Moreover, wedge-shaped microchannels were produced to generate sharp-edged microparticles
Zhou et al. 2022
.

In view of the still growing field of microfluidics
Battat, Weitz, and Whitesides 2022
;
Nunes and Stone 2022
, for example, if thinking of lab-on-a-chip devices, an explicit understanding and quantitative description of underlying flows and mechanisms of such geometries is thus highly desirable. If the flow is driven by internal force application, then the corresponding Green’s function provides the fundamental solution from which all cases of flow can be formulated. Specifically, it quantifies the induced flow in response to a force applied locally at one point within the fluid.

Low-Reynolds-number flows of incompressible viscous fluids near three-dimensional corners were first investigated by Sano and Hasimoto
Sano and Hasimoto 1976
;
Sano and Hasimoto 1977
;
Sano and Hasimoto 1978
;
Hasimoto and Sano 1980
;
Sano 1977
. The corresponding Green’s function for free-slip wedge-shaped confinement has also been derived for wedges with commensurate opening angles, employing an image method
Sprenger and Menzel 2023
.
Stokes flow near a corner has been revisited using a complex analysis approach
Dauparas and Lauga 2018
;
Dauparas 2018
.
More recently, we have derived corresponding mathematical expressions in previous investigations for different boundary conditions, free-slip and no-slip
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2025
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider and Menzel 2025
.

Generally, the response to applied higher-order localized force distributions, such as force dipoles or quadrupoles, can be derived from this Green’s function, which is particularly relevant for modeling microswimmers and other active particles
Spagnolie and Lauga 2012
;
Lopez and Lauga 2014
;
Mathijssen et al. 2016
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2019
;
Sprenger et al. 2020
;
Zöttl and Stark 2023
. Application of a torque at one point within the fluid is another mode of action on the system. Here, we derive the resulting fluid flow of such a “rotlet” applied to the fluid explicitly. In practice, example situations of imposed rotations at small length scales comprise induced mixing by rotational components
Lee et al. 2009
;
Owen et al. 2016
or micro-rheological investigations by rotating local probes
Wilhelm et al. 2003
;
Schmiedeberg and Stark 2005
;
Squires and Mason 2010
;
Richter, Deters, and Menzel 2021
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider, Lisicki, and Gekle 2018
;
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2018
. Typically, such rotations are induced using magnetic probe particles in reorienting external magnetic fields
Huang et al. 2016
;
Kreissl, Holm, and Weeber 2021
. In our context of the Green’s function, we would consider them as point-like.

In addition to deriving the flow field generated by a point torque in a wedge, we use this solution to determine, to leading order, the associated hydrodynamic coupling and rotational mobility tensors of a small particle subjected to a torque. In an unbounded fluid, a torque acting on a spherical particle generates only rotational motion, with no coupling to translation. In contrast, the wedge geometry breaks this symmetry, so a torque applied to a particle can induce a small but finite translational motion. This translation–rotation coupling arises solely from hydrodynamic interactions with the confining walls.

We proceed as follows. In Sec.
II
, we overview the mathematical formulation of the problem and introduce the Fourier-Kontorovich-Lebedev transformation, a reliable mathematical tool convenient for solving the low-Reynolds-number flow equations in a wedge-shaped confinement. We use it to solve the flow equations in Sec.
III
. Specifically, we distinguish between two different orientations of the rotlet, namely, applying the torque along or perpendicular to the edge of the wedge.
Resulting flows are illustrated.
In Sec.
IV
we show that taking the limit of an opening angle of
\pi
recovers previously derived solutions for a rotlet near a planar wall. Leading-order expressions for the hydrodynamic mobilities for rotational–translational coupling and purely rotational effects due to the confining boundaries are presented in Sec.
V
.
We conclude in Sec.
VI
.

II.2
Fourier-Kontorovich-Lebedev transform

II.2.1
General overview and definitions

To determine the velocity field generated by a point torque in a wedge-shaped geometry, we employ the Fourier–Kontorovich–Lebedev (FKL) transform. In this approach, the axial
z
, and radial
r
coordinates, are transformed into their corresponding wavenumbers, denoted by
k
and
\nu
, respectively.
The transform was first introduced by the Soviet mathematicians Kontorovich and Lebedev to address some boundary value problems
Kontorovich and Lebedev 1938
;
Kontorovich and Lebedev 1939
;
Lebedev and Kontorovich 1939
. The mathematical framework was later developed by Lebedev
Lebedev 1946
;
Lebedev 1949
. We refer the reader to Erdélyi
et al.

(
Erdélyi et al. 1953
, p. 75)
for additional details on this integral transform.

Laplace’s equation becomes significantly simpler to solve in FKL space because the transform diagonalizes the differential operators involved, reducing the problem to an algebraic form in the transformed variables. The Fourier transform applied along the axial direction converts the associated derivative into an algebraic multiplier. At the same time, the Kontorovich–Lebedev transform, which is naturally suited to Bessel-type operators, diagonalizes the planar part of the Laplacian. As a result, the full partial differential equation, which in physical space involves coupled derivatives and radial dependence, reduces in FKL space to a straightforward ordinary differential equation in the polar angle. The solution can then be obtained directly in FKL space and recovered in physical space by applying the inverse transforms.

We begin by defining the forward Fourier transform of a function
f(r,z)
with respect to
z
as

\hat{f}(r,k):=\mathscr{F}\left\{f\right\}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(r,z)\,e^{ikz}\,\mathrm{d}z\,,

(10)

where we adopt the convention of a plus sign in the exponent. We then define the Kontorovich–Lebedev (KL) transform with respect to
r
of the already Fourier-transformed quantity as

\widetilde{f}(\nu,k):=\mathscr{K}_{i\nu}\left\{\hat{f}\right\}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\hat{f}(r,k)\,K_{i\nu}(|k|r)\,r^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}r\,.

(11)

Here,
K_{i\nu}(|k|r)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind
Abramowitz and Stegun 1972
with purely imaginary order
i\nu
.
For positive arguments,
K_{i\nu}
is real if
\nu
is real.
Throughout, the symbol of a hat
\widehat{}
denotes the Fourier transform of the original function, while a tilde
\widetilde{}\,
indicates the Kontorovich–Lebedev transform of the Fourier-transformed function. We note that the polar angle
\theta
remains unchanged under these transformations. As a result, the partial differential equation in Eq. (
1
), which governs the hydrodynamic velocity field, is reduced to a system of ordinary differential equations in the polar angle
\theta
.

In what follows, the hyperbolic sine, cosine, and tangent functions are denoted by sh, ch, and th, respectively.
In addition, sch and csh are the secant and cosecant hyperbolic functions, respectively.
For convenience, we define the notation for the combined FKL transform as

\widetilde{f}=\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{f\right\}=\mathscr{K}_{i\nu}\left\{\mathscr{F}\left\{f\right\}\right\}.

(12)

The inverse FKL transform is expressed as the double integral

\displaystyle f(r,z)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}^{-1}\left\{\widetilde{f}\right\}=\frac{1}{\pi^{3}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k\,e^{-ikz}\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{f}(\nu,k)\,K_{i\nu}(|k|r)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\,\nu\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,.

(13)

It is worth noting that the forward FKL transform is typically performed by first computing the Fourier transform, as defined in Eq. (
10
), followed by the Kontorovich–Lebedev (KL) transform given in Eq. (
11
). In contrast, the inverse transformation is generally not carried out by applying the inverse KL transform first; instead, the inverse Fourier transform is evaluated initially. This approach reduces the problem to a single infinite integral over the radial wavenumber
\nu
, which is usually computed numerically.

II.2.2
Key Properties of the FKL Transform

Knowing the FKL transform of
1/s

2

While Fourier transforms are generally straightforward to evaluate, determining KL transforms often requires consulting classical references of tabulated integrals and transforms, such as Erdélyi
et al.

Erdélyi et al. 1953

The FKL transform of the derivative with respect to
z

\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right\}=-ik\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\{f\}\,.

(14)

The following identities are proven in the Appendix.
The FKL transform of the derivative with respect to
r

\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right\}=\frac{|k|}{2i\nu}\left((i\nu+1)\mathscr{T}_{i\nu+1}\{f\}+(i\nu-1)\mathscr{T}_{i\nu-1}\{f\}\right).

(15)

Moreover, the FKL transform of a function divided by
r
is given by

\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{\frac{f}{r}\right\}=\frac{|k|}{2i\nu}\left(\mathscr{T}_{i\nu+1}\{f\}-\mathscr{T}_{i\nu-1}\{f\}\right).

(16)

Furthermore, the following FKL transform property holds,

\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{\frac{z}{r}\,f\right\}={}-\frac{\operatorname{sgn}k}{2\nu}\left(\left(i\nu+1+k\,\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\right)\mathscr{T}_{i\nu+1}\left\{f\right\}+\left(i\nu-1-k\,\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\right)\mathscr{T}_{i\nu-1}\left\{f\right\}\right),

(17)

where sgn denotes the sign function.

It is worth noting that when
f
is an even function of
z

1/s

z

15

16

14
) and (

However, for the sake of streamlined subsequent calculations, we choose to present the results in the above forms.

We define the shorthand notations

\lambda_{1}=2\left(\operatorname{sh}^{2}(\alpha\nu)+\sin^{2}\alpha\right),\,\lambda_{2}=2\left(\operatorname{sh}^{2}(\alpha\nu)+\cos^{2}\alpha\right),\,\,\delta_{1}^{\pm}=2\left(\operatorname{ch}^{2}(\alpha\nu)\pm\cos(2\alpha)\right),\,\delta_{2}^{\pm}=2\left(\operatorname{sh}^{2}(\alpha\nu)\pm\cos(2\alpha)\right),

(30)

together with

\Gamma_{\pm}^{-1}=\pm\bigl(\operatorname{ch}(2\alpha\nu)\pm\cos(2\alpha)\bigr)\bigl(\operatorname{sh}(2\alpha\nu)\mp\nu\sin(2\alpha)\bigr)\,.

(31)

Accordingly, we express the solution for

in the form

\displaystyle\Lambda_{j}

(32a)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{j}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{-\sigma}\,\nu\left(a_{j}^{\dagger}\nu\sin(2\alpha)+b_{j}^{\dagger}\right)\,,\quad\quad H_{j}^{\dagger}=\Gamma_{-\sigma}\left(p_{j}^{\dagger}\nu\sin(2\alpha)+w_{j}^{\dagger}\right)\,,

for

\sigma=1

(33a)

We define
\delta
as the angle between the torque vector and the radial direction, such that
\tan\delta=L_{r}/L_{\theta}
. Accordingly,
\delta=0
corresponds to purely radial torque, while
\delta=\pi/2
corresponds to purely azimuthal torque.

The free-space components of the solution for a radial rotlet are given by

\phi_{r}^{\infty}=\frac{z}{s^{3}}\,\sin(\theta-\beta-\delta)\,,\quad\phi_{\theta}^{\infty}=\frac{z}{s^{3}}\,\cos(\theta-\beta-\delta)\,,\quad\phi_{z}^{\infty}=-\frac{1}{s^{3}}\left(\rho\sin\theta+r\sin(\theta-\beta-\delta)\right),\quad\phi_{w}^{\infty}=\frac{z}{s^{3}}\,\sin\delta\,.

(37)

Corresponding expressions in FKL space are obtained as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}_{r}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=-\sin(\theta-\beta-\delta)\,\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\frac{1}{s}\right\},\qquad\widetilde{\phi}_{\theta}^{\infty}=-\cos(\theta-\beta-\delta)\,\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\frac{1}{s}\right\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}_{z}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sin\delta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{\frac{1}{s}\right\}-\cos\delta\operatorname{sgn}(\theta-\beta)\,\frac{1}{\rho}\,\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\frac{1}{s}\right\},\quad\widetilde{\phi}_{w}^{\infty}=-\sin\delta\,\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\frac{1}{s}\right\}.

(38)

In this case, since
r\phi_{r}^{\infty}+z\phi_{z}^{\infty}+\rho\phi_{w}^{\infty}=0
, Eq. (
9
) can be written in FKL space as

\widetilde{\phi}_{r}^{\infty}+\widetilde{\phi}_{r}=0\,,\quad\widetilde{\phi}_{z}^{\infty}+\widetilde{\phi}_{z}=0\,,\quad\widetilde{\phi}_{w}^{\infty}+\widetilde{\phi}_{w}=0\,,\quad\frac{\partial\widetilde{\phi}_{r}}{\partial\theta}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\,\mathscr{T}_{i\nu}\left\{\frac{z}{r}\,\phi_{z}+\frac{\rho}{r}\,\phi_{w}\right\}-2\left(\widetilde{\phi}_{\theta}^{\infty}+\widetilde{\phi}_{\theta}\right)=0\,,\qquad\text{at}\penalty\ \theta=\pm\alpha\,.

(39)

We obtain

\Delta_{w}=-\sin\delta\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{sh}\left((\pi-\alpha)\nu\right)\operatorname{csh}(\alpha\nu)\,,\qquad\Delta_{w}^{\dagger}=-\sin\delta\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{ch}\left((\pi-\alpha)\nu\right)\operatorname{sch}(\alpha\nu)\,,

(40)

and

\Lambda_{z}=\nu\cos\delta\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{sh}\left((\pi-\alpha)\nu\right)\operatorname{csh}(\alpha\nu)\,,\qquad\Lambda_{z}^{\dagger}=\nu\cos\delta\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{ch}\left((\pi-\alpha)\nu\right)\operatorname{sch}(\alpha\nu)\,.

(41)

In addition,
H_{z}=\Delta_{w}
and
H_{z}^{\dagger}=\Delta_{w}^{\dagger}
.

We express the solution for
j\in\{x,y\}
in the form

\Delta_{j}=\Gamma_{\sigma}\left(c_{j}\nu\sin(2\alpha)+d_{j}\right)\,,\qquad\quad\Delta_{j}^{\dagger}=\Gamma_{-\sigma}\left(c_{j}^{\dagger}\nu\sin(2\alpha)+d_{j}^{\dagger}\right)\,,

(42)

where, again,
\sigma=-1
for
j=x
and
\sigma=1
for
j=y
, and the expression of
\Gamma_{\pm}^{-1}
is given by Eq. (
31
).
Defining
C=\cos(\beta+\delta)
and
S=\sin(\beta+\delta)
, we obtain

\displaystyle c_{x}

\displaystyle={S}\bigl(\lambda_{1}\operatorname{ch}(\pi\nu)-\operatorname{sh}(2\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\bigr)\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)+{C}\sin(2\alpha)\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\,,

(43a)

\displaystyle c_{y}

\displaystyle={C}\bigl(\operatorname{sh}(2\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)-\lambda_{2}\operatorname{ch}(\pi\nu)\bigr)\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)-{S}\sin(2\alpha)\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\,,

(43b)

\displaystyle c_{x}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle={S}\bigl(\operatorname{sh}(2\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)-\lambda_{2}\operatorname{ch}(\pi\nu)\bigr)\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)+{C}\sin(2\alpha)\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\,,

(43c)

\displaystyle c_{y}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle={C}\left(\lambda_{1}\operatorname{ch}(\pi\nu)-\operatorname{sh}(2\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\right)\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)-{S}\sin(2\alpha)\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\,,

(43d)

and

\displaystyle d_{x}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}{S}\operatorname{ch}(\pi\nu)\operatorname{sh}(2\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)-2\operatorname{ch}(\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\left(\delta_{1}^{-}{S}\operatorname{ch}(\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)+{C}\sin(2\alpha)\operatorname{sh}(\alpha\nu)\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\right),

(44a)

\displaystyle d_{y}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}{C}\operatorname{ch}(\pi\nu)\operatorname{sh}(2\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)-2\operatorname{ch}(\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\left(\delta_{1}^{+}{C}\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)\operatorname{ch}(\alpha\nu)+{S}\sin(2\alpha)\operatorname{sh}(\alpha\nu)\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\right),

(44b)

\displaystyle d_{x}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}{S}\operatorname{ch}(\pi\nu)\operatorname{sh}(2\alpha\nu)\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)+2\operatorname{sh}(\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\left({C}\sin(2\alpha)\operatorname{ch}(\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)-\delta_{2}^{+}{S}\operatorname{sh}(\alpha\nu)\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\right),

(44c)

\displaystyle d_{y}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}{C}\operatorname{ch}(\pi\nu)\operatorname{sh}(2\alpha\nu)\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)+2\operatorname{sh}(\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\pi\nu)\left({S}\sin(2\alpha)\operatorname{ch}(\alpha\nu)\operatorname{sh}(\beta\nu)-\delta_{2}^{-}{C}\operatorname{sh}(\alpha\nu)\operatorname{ch}(\beta\nu)\right).

(44d)

III.3
Solution in real space

Having derived the solution of the flow problem in FKL space, we now apply the inverse transform in Eq. (
13
) to recover the solution in real space. The integration with respect to the axial wavenumber
k
can be carried out using tabulated integrals, leaving a single remaining integral over the radial wavenumber
\nu
, which is then evaluated numerically using standard routines.
Depending on the specific forms of the coefficients
A_{j}
and
A_{j}^{\dagger}
that define the solution for
\phi_{j}
, the resulting real-space solution will take correspondingly distinct forms.

For an axial rotlet, as well as for
j=z
in the cases of radial and azimuthal rotlets, the coefficients in FKL space are given by Eq. (
24
). In this setting, the solution can be written in the form

\phi_{j}(r,\theta,z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\,\psi_{j}(\theta,\nu)+\xi_{j}(\theta,\nu)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)\mathcal{K}_{i\nu}(r,z)\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,,

(45)

with the kernel defined as

\mathcal{K}_{i\nu}(r,z)=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(kz)K_{i\nu}(kr)K_{i\nu}(k\rho)\,\mathrm{d}k\,,

(46)

and

\psi_{j}(\theta,\nu)=q_{l}\left(\Lambda_{j}\operatorname{sh}(\theta\nu)+\Lambda_{j}^{\dagger}\operatorname{ch}(\theta\nu)\right),\qquad\xi_{j}(\theta,\nu)=q_{l}\left(H_{j}\operatorname{sh}(\theta\nu)+H_{j}^{\dagger}\operatorname{ch}(\theta\nu)\right).

(47)

Here,
l\in\{\parallel,\perp\}
specifies the direction of the point torque.

For transverse torque, the coefficients for
j\in\{x,y,w\}
are given by Eq. (
25
). In this setting, the solution takes the form

\phi_{j}(r,\theta,z)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{j}(\theta,\nu)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\,\mathcal{K}_{i\nu}(r,z)\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,,

(48)

where

\varphi_{j}(\theta,\nu)=q_{\perp}\left(\Delta_{j}\operatorname{sh}(\theta\nu)+\Delta_{j}^{\dagger}\operatorname{ch}(\theta\nu)\right).

(49)

The improper integral in Eq. (
46
) is convergent, and its value is given in classical textbooks as

\mathcal{K}_{i\nu}(r,z)=\left(\rho r\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}P_{i\nu-\frac{1}{2}}(c)\,\operatorname{sch}(\pi\nu)\,,

(50)

see Gradshteyn and Ryzhik
(
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2014
, p. 719)
, Eq. 6.672, or Prudnikov
et al.

(
Prudnikov, Brychkov, and Marichev 1992
, p. 390)
, Eq. 2.16.36(2). Here,
P_{n}
is the Legendre function of the first kind of degree
n
, with argument

c=\frac{\rho^{2}+r^{2}+z^{2}}{2\rho r}\,.

(51)

Depending on the direction of the applied point torque, the solution for the fluid velocity can be expressed as

v_{j}(\bm{r})=v_{j}^{\infty}(\bm{r})+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi_{j}(\bm{r},\nu)\,\mathcal{K}_{i\nu}(r,z)\,\mathrm{d}\nu\,,\qquad j\in\{r,\theta,z\}\,,

(52)

where the free-space velocity
v_{j}^{\infty}
is defined in Eq. (
2
).
For axial point torque, we have
\phi_{z}=0
and
\xi_{w}=0
.
The expressions for the operator
\bm{\Psi}
are obtained by substituting the integral forms given in Eq. (
45
) into the Papkovich–Neuber–Imai representation in Eq. (
6
), yielding

\Psi_{r}=\frac{\partial\Pi_{\parallel}}{\partial r}-2\left(\frac{\psi_{r}}{\rho}+\xi_{r}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right),\qquad\Psi_{\theta}=\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\Pi_{\parallel}}{\partial\theta}-2\left(\frac{\psi_{\theta}}{\rho}+\xi_{\theta}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right),\qquad\Psi_{z}=\frac{\partial\Pi_{\parallel}}{\partial z}\,,

(53)

for axial point torque, and

\Psi_{r}=\frac{\partial\Pi_{\perp}}{\partial r}+2\varphi_{r}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\,,\qquad\Psi_{\theta}=\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\Pi_{\perp}}{\partial\theta}+2\varphi_{\theta}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\,,\qquad\Psi_{z}=\frac{\partial\Pi_{\perp}}{\partial z}-2\left(\frac{\psi_{z}}{\rho}+\xi_{z}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right),

(54)

for transverse point torque.
The expressions for
\Pi_{\parallel}
and
\Pi_{\perp}
are obtained from Eq. (
7
) as

\Pi_{\parallel}=r\left(\frac{\psi_{r}}{\rho}+\xi_{r}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)+\psi_{w}\,,\qquad\Pi_{\perp}=z\left(\frac{\psi_{z}}{\rho}+\xi_{z}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)-\left(r\varphi_{r}+\rho\varphi_{w}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\,.

(55)

Note that
\psi
,
\xi
, and
\varphi
with subscripts
r
and
\theta
denote the projections of the corresponding quantities onto the radial and azimuthal directions in polar coordinates.

Based on these expressions, from Eq. (
52
), we can thus calculate the resulting flow field within the wedge-shaped confinement for any orientation of the applied rotlet.
In Fig.
2
, we show a representative flow field generated by a torque singularity oriented in the axial direction, parallel to the edge of the wedge. In this example, the wedge has a semi-opening angle of
\alpha=\pi/6
, and the rotlet is positioned at
\beta=\alpha/2
.
Near the singularity, the flow resembles that of an unbounded fluid. At greater distances, however, this unbounded solution is distorted by the wedge-shaped confinement. The location of the velocity maximum in planes that do not contain the torque singularity shifts away from the wedge surface as the height above the rotlet increases.
In addition to the primary swirl induced by the rotlet, secondary swirling structures are present, though their velocities are significantly smaller.

For the same geometry as in Fig.
2
, Fig.
3
shows the flow field induced by a rotlet oriented radially away from the wedge edge. Here, we again observe that the location of the maximum velocity shifts away from the wedge surface with increasing height above the torque singularity.

An example of the flow field generated by a torque applied in the azimuthal direction, that is, perpendicular both to the wedge edge and to the radial direction, is shown in Fig.
4
. All other parameters are the same as those used for Fig.
2
.
As before, the velocity maximum is repelled by the boundaries and shifts toward the center of the wedge with increasing height above the torque. In the axial–radial plane, the flow is strongly distorted due to the wedge confinement.

Overall, the wedge-shaped geometry has a pronounced effect on the resulting fluid flows compared to the unbounded geometry. In all cases, we find locally induced vortices, see, for example, Figs.
2
(c),
3
(c), and
4
(c). Partly as a consequence, and together with the incompressibility of the fluid, we find that the flows significantly above the applied rotlet, see Figs.
2
(b),
3
(b), and
4
(b), can show markedly different orientations than closer to the rotlet, see Figs.
2
(a),
3
(a), and
4
(a), respectively.

IV.1
Axial torque

Substituting the corresponding coefficients from Tab.
1
into Eq. (
47
), we directly obtain

\psi_{x}=-3q_{\parallel}\nu\cos\beta\operatorname{sh}\left((\theta+\beta)\nu\right)\,,\quad\psi_{y}=-q_{\parallel}\nu\sin\beta\operatorname{sh}\left((\theta+\beta)\nu\right)\,,\quad\psi_{z}=0\,,\quad\psi_{w}=q_{\parallel}\nu\operatorname{sh}\left((\theta+\beta)\nu\right)\,,

(56)

and

\xi_{x}=-3q_{\parallel}\sin\beta\operatorname{ch}\left((\theta+\beta)\nu\right)\,,\quad\xi_{y}=q_{\parallel}\cos\beta\operatorname{ch}\left((\theta+\beta)\nu\right)\,,\quad\xi_{z}=\xi_{w}=0\,.

(57)

Using the integral presented in Appendix C of Ref.
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2025
,

\mathscr{L}=\left(\rho r\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{ch}(b\nu)\operatorname{sch}(\pi\nu)\,P_{i\nu-\frac{1}{2}}(c)\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\rho^{2}+2\rho r\cos b+z^{2}}}\,,

(58)

for
b\in[-\pi,\pi]
, if follows from Eq. (
45
) that

\phi_{x}=-3q_{\parallel}\left(\frac{\cos\beta}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial b}-\sin\beta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right)\mathscr{L},\qquad\phi_{y}=q_{\parallel}\left(\cos\beta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}-\frac{\sin\beta}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial b}\right)\mathscr{L}\,,\qquad\phi_{w}=\frac{q_{\parallel}}{\rho}\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial b}\,,

(59)

evaluated at
b=\theta+\beta
.
Simplification yields

\phi_{x}=\frac{3q_{\parallel}}{\overline{s}^{3}}\left(\rho\sin\beta-r\sin\theta\right),\qquad\phi_{y}=-\frac{q_{\parallel}}{\overline{s}^{3}}\left(\rho\cos\beta+r\cos\theta\right),\qquad\phi_{w}=\frac{q_{\parallel}}{\overline{s}^{3}}\,r\sin(\theta+\beta)\,,

(60)

where
\overline{s}=\mathscr{L}\left(b=\theta+\beta\right)=\sqrt{r^{2}+\rho^{2}+2\rho r\cos(\theta+\beta)+z^{2}}
, representing the value of
s
with
\theta
substituted by
\pi-\theta

V
Hydrodynamic mobilities

The solution for the flow field derived above can be used to evaluate how the confining wedge influences the motion of a small spherical colloidal particle of radius
a
subjected to an external torque. Due to hydrodynamic interactions with the no-slip boundaries, the particle will in general be displaced, and its rotational velocity will be affected. The leading-order term in the hydrodynamic coupling mobility is obtained by evaluating the image solution for the induced flow field at the position of the particle. This procedure yields an expression for the mobilities in terms of the small parameter
\epsilon=a/d
, where
d=\rho\sin(\alpha-\beta)
and
a
is the particle radius.
The change in rotational mobility when compared to the situation in an unbounded fluid is obtained by evaluating half the vorticity of the flow resulting from the image solution at the position of the rotating particle.

Defining
\mu_{0}^{rr}=1/(8\pi\eta a^{3})
as the bulk hydrodynamic rotational mobility in an unbounded fluid, the translational and rotational velocities resulting from the hydrodynamic interactions with the boundaries are related to the hydrodynamic torque
\bm{L}
acting on the particle through

\bm{V}=\left.\bm{v}(\bm{r})-\bm{v}^{\infty}(\bm{r})\right|_{\bm{r}=\bm{r}_{0}}\,,\qquad\bm{\Omega}=\left.\mu_{0}^{rr}\bm{L}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bm{\nabla}\times\left(\bm{v}(\bm{r})-\bm{v}^{\infty}(\bm{r})\right)\right|_{\bm{r}=\bm{r}_{0}}\,.

(65)

Accordingly,

\bm{V}=-\mu_{0}^{tr}\,\epsilon^{2}\bm{A}\cdot\bm{L}\,,\qquad\bm{\Omega}=\mu_{0}^{rr}\left(\bm{I}-\epsilon^{3}\bm{B}\right)\cdot\bm{L}\,,

(66)

where
\mu_{0}^{tr}=1/(6\pi\eta a^{2})
is used to nondimensionalize the translation–rotation coupling,
\bm{I}
denotes the identity matrix, and

\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&a_{rz}\\
0&0&a_{\theta z}\\
a_{zr}&a_{z\theta}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\bm{B}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{rr}&b_{r\theta}&0\\
b_{\theta r}&b_{\theta\theta}&0\\
0&0&b_{zz}\end{pmatrix}

(67)

are dimensionless matrices, corresponding to the rotational–translational coupling mobilities and purely rotational mobilities, respectively.
It follows that, to leading order, the coupling mobilities scale as
\epsilon^{2}
, while the correction to the rotational mobilities exhibit an even weaker scaling of
\epsilon^{3}
.
By the symmetry of the mobility tensor, it follows that
b_{r\theta}=b_{\theta r}
.
Accordingly, we are left with determining eight components that fully characterize the response to the applied torque.

We mention that for
\bm{A}
, related to rotational–translational coupling in the hydrodynamic mobilities, the prefactor is
6\pi\eta a^{2}
. Conversely, for the resistance it should be
8\pi\eta a^{2}
, as used in Sano and Hasimoto
Sano and Hasimoto 1978
. As a side remark, to compare with the dimensionless matrix
\bm{t}
associated with rotational–translational coupling as defined in Ref.
Sano and Hasimoto 1978
, we can note the relation
\bm{A}=\bm{t}^{\top}
. Generally, the grand mobility tensor and the grand resistance tensor are inverses of each other.
The following results are derived using Eq. (
65
) together with the solution for the flow field as given by Eq. (
52
).

VI
Conclusions

Summarizing, we here derived analytical expressions for low-Reynolds-number fluid flows induced by a localized torque in a wedge-shaped geometry. No-slip surface conditions are applied at the surfaces of the wedge-shaped confinement. To perform the mathematical derivation, we rely on the Fourier-Kontorovich-Lebedev transform. From the illustration of resulting flow fields, we observe the obvious and expected distortion of the resulting fluid flows and vortices by the wedge-shaped geometry.

Wedge-shaped geometries of low-Reynolds-number flows play a significant role, for instance, in microfluidic devices, as outlined above
Yang et al. 2018
. In that example, separation of mixtures of biological cells was identified as an application of wedge-shaped channels. On the opposite side, enhanced mixing between different components or fluids is a frequent topic in microfluidics
Chen and Meiners 2004
;
Lee et al. 2016
;
Li et al. 2022
. Introducing rotational components is one strategy to promote this effect
Janssen et al. 2009
;
Chong et al. 2018
;
Owen et al. 2016
. Our mathematical expressions provide a quick estimate for the resulting induced fluid flows when assuming the rotational probes as localized in space. We recently derived corresponding expressions for the geometry of two parallel, concentric disks
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2021
. Here, we provide them for the case of a rigid wedge.

One step further, we relied on the derived solution for the flow induced by the rotlet to calculate the lowest-order hydrodynamic mobilities in this geometry. They express how objects small in size when compared to the distance from the boundaries are displaced and rotated when they exert a torque on the surrounding fluid. Such motion results from the hydrodynamic interactions with the no-slip boundaries of the wedge-shaped confinement. As a consequence, transport can be induced by imposed rotations, in contrast to the case of an unbounded fluid in the absence of any boundaries.
