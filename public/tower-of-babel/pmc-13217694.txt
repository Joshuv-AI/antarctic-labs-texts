RYR1+ skeletal muscle-derived extracellular vesicles are exercise responsive and associated with insulin action
Authors: Xin Zhang, Christopher G. Vann, David B. Bartlett, Alastair Khodabukus, George A. Truskey, Nenad Bursac, Joseph A. Houmard, Johanna L. Johnson, Kim M. Huffman, Brian J. Andonian, William E. Kraus, Virginia Byers Kraus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSkeletal muscle is a central regulator of insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis. The ryanodine receptor 1 (RYR1) is highly expressed in skeletal muscle and plays a key role in myogenic differentiation. We hypothesize that RYR1+ extracellular vesicles (EVs) represent a skeletal muscle-derived EV subpopulation whose abundance and small RNA (smRNA) cargo are associated with aging, insulin action, and exercise responsiveness.MethodsWe tested this hypothesis through in vitro analyses of smRNAs of skeletal muscle-derived EVs and in vivo evaluation of their exercise responsiveness and association with insulin action in older adults. Using an integrated workflow combining centrifugation, polymer-based precipitation, and single-EV sorting, we isolated RYR1+ and RYR− EVs secreted from myobundles—3D contractile skeletal muscle tissues engineered from primary muscle progenitor cells obtained from healthy donors (n = 6). We also isolated plasma EVs from 48 human participants and performed targeted high-resolution flow cytometry to evaluate EV biomarkers associated with aging, insulin action, and exercise responsiveness in older adults.ResultsBy smRNA sequencing, compared to myobundle RYR− EVs, RYR1+ EVs contained three unique microRNAs (miRNAs) and an additional 21 miRNAs with significantly greater abundance (including canonical myoMiRs miR-206, miR-1-3p, and miR-208a-3p). Of the 24 RYR1+ EV-enriched miRNAs, experimentally-supported mRNA targets (n = 422) are involved in pathways governing cell proliferation, apoptosis, senescence, insulin and glucose signaling. In two independent cohorts, including older adults with prediabetes or unknown prediabetes status, frequencies of RYR1+ EV subsets were significantly upregulated by chronic exercise with greater RYR1+ EV frequencies associated with better insulin action.ConclusionsThese complementary in vitro and in vivo data identify skeletal muscle-derived RYR1+ EVs as upregulated by exercise and as carriers of miRNAs linked to insulin action in older adults, including those with prediabetes. These results highlight skeletal muscle-derived EVs as novel biomarkers and potential mediators of systemic metabolic regulation and healthy aging.Graphical Abstract Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08215-w.

Introduction

Skeletal muscle is a primary regulator of insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis [1]. Aging is accompanied by progressive declines in skeletal muscle mass and function, which contribute to impaired metabolic health and increased risk for insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes [1, 2]. Exercise is one of the most effective interventions to preserve skeletal muscle integrity and metabolic function across the lifespan.

We and others have demonstrated that exercise training, including low-volume/high-intensity interval training (HIIT), improves insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis in older adults with prediabetes [3] and in mouse models [4]. These benefits are accompanied by reductions in fasting insulin, fasting glucose, and the Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), an index of hepatic insulin resistance [5, 6]. However, the mechanisms by which skeletal muscle communicates exercise‑induced metabolic adaptations to other tissues remain incompletely defined.

Emerging evidence suggests that extracellular vesicles (EVs) serve as an important mode of intercellular communication during exercise [7]. EVs are nanosized lipid‑bound particles released by most cell types and transport bioactive cargo, including proteins, mitochondria, messenger RNAs, and small RNAs (smRNAs) such as microRNAs (miRNAs), that reflect the physiological state of their tissue of origin [8–17]. Exercise alters the abundance of circulating EVs, implicating EV-mediated signaling as a candidate mechanism underlying exercise-induced metabolic adaptation [7].

Among EV cargo components, miRNAs are of particular interest due to their established roles in muscle development, aging, insulin signaling, and metabolic regulation [18]. While prior studies have linked circulating EV miRNAs to metabolic phenotypes and exercise responses [4, 19–21], the specific smRNA composition of skeletal muscle-derived EVs, and how this composition relates to aging, metabolic health, and exercise training in humans, remains largely unknown.

A major barrier to studying skeletal muscle-derived EVs in humans is the heterogeneity of circulating EV populations originating from multiple tissues [15]. This challenge necessitates the identification of tissue‑specific EV markers. The ryanodine receptor 1 (RYR1) is a large intracellular calcium release channel that is highly and selectively expressed in mature skeletal muscle, with minimal expression in other tissues [22–25]. RYR1 expression markedly increases during late-stage myogenic differentiation and is essential for excitation-contraction coupling [24]. These properties make RYR1 a biologically plausible marker for identifying EVs of skeletal muscle origin in circulation.

To define the smRNA cargo of skeletal muscle-derived EVs under controlled conditions, we employed an established in vitro human skeletal muscle model in which primary muscle progenitor cells are engineered into three-dimensional (3D) functional skeletal muscle tissues termed “myobundles” [23]. This system provides a well-defined source of skeletal muscle EVs and enables molecular characterization without the confounding influence of non‑muscle tissues. We complemented this approach with in vivo analyses of human plasma EVs to assess relevance to aging, metabolic health, and exercise training.

Based on the central role of skeletal muscle in metabolic regulation, the exercise responsiveness of EV release, and the regulatory potential of smRNAs carried by skeletal muscle-derived EVs, we hypothesize that RYR1+ EVs represent a skeletal muscle-derived EV subpopulation whose abundance and smRNA cargo are associated with aging, insulin action, and exercise responsiveness. This framework allows us to evaluate RYR1+ EVs as correlative biomarkers of metabolic health, while generating testable hypotheses regarding their potential involvement in inter‑tissue metabolic communication.

Rather than focusing on acute post‑exercise responses, which are strongly influenced by transient mechanical and inflammatory stimuli [26, 27], we analyzed resting plasma samples collected 16–24 hours after the final bout of chronic exercise training [3, 28, 29]. This approach preferentially captures stable, training‑associated adaptations in EV phenotypes. Using established EV surface marker panels [7, 15], we characterized RYR1+ EVs in both myobundle-derived EVs and human plasma EVs to enable focused analysis of a putative skeletal muscle-derived EV subpopulation.

Accordingly, we employed complementary in vitro and in vivo approaches to systematically investigate skeletal muscle-derived EVs. Using a human 3D myobundle model, we first defined the smRNA cargo of skeletal muscle-derived EVs under controlled conditions and identified smRNAs enriched within RYR1+ EVs. We then examined whether the abundance and molecular features of circulating RYR1+ EVs are associated with aging and measures of insulin action at rest. Finally, we evaluated whether RYR1+ EVs are modulated by chronic aerobic exercise training of varying intensity and amount.

Materials and methods

Study cohorts

This study analyzed specimens from four cohorts totaling 48 human participants: (1) six human skeletal muscle (vastus lateralis) samples from healthy donors for generating myobundles and collecting supernatants for myobundle EV isolation including three younger (mean age 25.3 ± 8.5 years [range 19–35], 2 female) and three older (mean age 54.3 ± 9.1 years [range 19–35], 3 female) adults; (2) 12 healthy donor plasma specimens purchased from a commercial vendor (Zen-Bio, Durham, NC, USA) including six younger (mean age 24.7 ± 6.0 years [range 18–32], 3 female) and six older (mean age 55.5 ± 11.5 years [range 47–78], 3 female) adults; (3) 20 plasma specimens collected at baseline and 16–24 h after the final exercise session of a 10-week supervised HIIT trial [3] from 10 older adults with prediabetes (mean age 71.4 ± 4.9 years [range 65–80], 6 female); (4) 40 plasma specimens collected at baseline and 16–24 h after the final exercise session of a 6–8 month supervised training intervention (the Studies of a Targeted Risk Reduction Intervention Through Defined Exercise (STRRIDE) protocol [28–30]) from 20 older adults, including 12 (mean age 63.6 ± 1.9 years [range 61–68], 6 female) assigned to the STRRIDE low-amount/vigorous-intensity group and eight (mean age 56.9 ± 3.0 years [range 53–61], 4 female) assigned to the STRRIDE high-amount/vigorous-intensity group.

Zen-Bio provided an assurance that the samples were acquired under donor consent and Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval. The other plasma samples were acquired with written informed consent under Duke University Health System IRB approval. Muscle tissue samples were collected through standard needle biopsy under Duke IRB approval.

HIIT exercise protocol

As we previously reported [3], the inclusion criteria of the HIIT study included being prediabetic (baseline HbA1c of 6.1 ± 0.3%) and sedentary. The exclusion criteria included cancer, cardiovascular disease, or autoimmune disease. All women were post-menopausal, and none were receiving hormone replacement therapy. All specimens collected from participants in this study were used for EV analysis. With the exception of age and sex, investigators were blinded to all clinical variables until completion of the EV analyses.

The exercise protocol of the HIIT intervention consisted of ten weeks (3 × 30-minute sessions/week) of supervised treadmill walking (Clinical Trial Registration: http://www.ClinicalTrials.gov, identifier NCT02441205) [3]. Each session consisted of ten 60-second intervals of low-intensity (50–60% heart rate reserve [HRR]) separated by similar durations of intervals of high-intensity (80–90% HRR). The intervals were designed to elicit a heart rate corresponding to 80–90% of oxygen consumption (VO2) reserve (high-intensity intervals) and 50–60% VO2 reserve (active recovery). Available measures of metabolic health related to insulin action—fasting glucose (mmol/L), fasting insulin (mmol/L) and HOMA-IR—were included in the analysis.

STRRIDE exercise protocol

The STRRIDE study (Clinical Trial Registration: http://www.ClinicalTrials.gov, identifier NCT00200993) [28–30] included sedentary, overweight or mildly obese (body mass index [BMI] 25–35 kg/m2) individuals, with mild to moderate lipid abnormalities (defined as low-density lipoprotein [LDL] cholesterol levels between 130 and 190 mg/dL, or high-density lipoprotein [HDL] cholesterol levels below 40 mg/dL for men or below 45 mg/dL for women) [28, 29]. All female participants were postmenopausal, and half were taking hormone replacement therapy. Exclusion criteria included the use of other medications that might affect carbohydrate metabolism, a diagnosis of diabetes, orthopedic conditions that could limit exercise participation, hypertension, and known heart disease. We selected samples based on availability from the STRRIDE low-amount/vigorous-intensity and high-amount/vigorous-intensity groups from STRRIDE I [30]. In this study, we included only participants aged > 50 years and ensured a balanced age distribution between males and females. With the exception of age and sex, investigators were blinded to all other clinical variables until completion of the EV analyses.

The exercise protocol of the STRRIDE low-amount/vigorous-intensity intervention consisted of 6–8 months of supervised low-amount (~1300 kcal/week), vigorous-intensity (65–80% peak relative oxygen consumption [RVO2]) aerobic exercise, equal to 14 kcal/kg of body mass/week, approximately 19 km/week at 65–80% peak RVO2.

The exercise protocol of the STRRIDE high-amount/vigorous-intensity intervention consisted of 6–8 months of supervised high-amount (~2200 kcal/week), vigorous-intensity (65–80% peak RVO2) aerobic exercise, equal to 23 kcal/kg of body mass/week, approximately 32 km/week at 65–80% peak VO₂.

Exercise equipment in both interventions included cycle ergometers, treadmills, and elliptical trainers. Participants underwent an initial ramp phase of 2–3 months with gradually increased exercise amount and intensity, followed by six months of training at the prescribed exercise doses. The available metabolic health data related to insulin action, including fasting glucose (mg/dL), fasting insulin (µU/mL), and HOMA-IR, were used for analysis.

Human satellite cell isolation and culture

Human skeletal muscle tissue was acquired from vastus lateralis biopsies; satellite cells were isolated as previously described [31, 32]. Briefly, freshly harvested human vastus lateralis muscle was washed in PBS with 1X Gibco™ Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Anti-Anti; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and then finely minced with a scalpel. Tissue fragments were enzymatically digested in 0.05% Trypsin for 30 minutes on a rocker at 37 °C. Following digestion, trypsin was neutralized using Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA), the suspension was spun down and resuspended in DMEM Low Glucose (Sigma, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) supplemented with 1X Anti-Anti, then pre-plated in 1 T-175 for 1 hour at 37 °C. Finally, tissue fragments were resuspended in skeletal muscle 2D growth media (DMEM Low Glucose, 10% FBS, 0.4 ng/mL Dexamethasone [Sigma], 10 ng/mL epidermal growth factor [ProSpecBio, Rehovot, Israel], and 0.064 mg/mL Penicillin) supplemented with 2X Anti-Anti. This suspension was plated on Matrigel coated plates to enable cell outgrowth for approximately 1 week (with media changes every 2 days). Following outgrowth, cells were further cultured on Matrigel (Corning, New York, NY, USA) coated flasks in skeletal muscle growth media and passaged with 0.025% Trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) when they approached 85% confluency. During passaging, cells were frozen for downstream use, and at passage 5 utilized for all described experiments.

Human primary myobundle production

Human primary myobundles were generated as previously described [31–36]. Briefly, myobundles were formed in polydimethylsiloxane PDMS molds with two 5-mm long, 1-mm wide semi-circular channels. Molds were coated with 2% (w/v) pluronic F-127 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 30 minutes at room temperature to prevent myobundle adhesion, as previously described [31–33]. Cerex® (nylon) frames were placed in the molds to serve as anchor points for the myobundles, creating uniaxial passive tension. A cell solution (375K myoblasts in 9.3 µL media and 1 µL of 50 U/mL thrombin (Sigma) in 1% BSA in PBS per myobundle) and a gelling solution (1.75 µL media, 5 µL Matrigel, and 5 µL of 22 mg/mL fibrinogen [Sigma] in PBS) were prepared separately on ice in vials to make 4 myobundles at a time. Cell solution was added to the gelling solution, pipetted into the channels of the PDMS molds, and left to polymerize for 30 minutes at 37 °C prior to the addition of media. Myobundles were then cultured dynamically on a rocker in skeletal muscle 3D growth media (2D growth media supplemented with 1.5 mg/mL 6-aminocaproic acid [Sigma] for 4 days, after which media was switched to custom-designed human plasma like media for differentiation [36]. Media was changed every other day through both the growth and first 6 days of differentiation phases, and 48-hour conditioned media was collected at 8-, 10-, 12- and 14-days post differentiation.

Myobundle EV isolation, characterization, and single-EV sorting

For these experiments we used the 48-hour conditioned media collected from differentiated myobundle cultures on day 12. Following the protocol reported in our previous study [37], culture supernatants were centrifuged at 2,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove cells and debris, then aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until analysis. To minimize co-isolation of contaminants, we employed an EV isolation workflow that integrates centrifugation, precipitation, and single-EV sorting. First, the supernatant aliquots were thawed and pre-cleared by centrifugation at 2,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove residual debris. Second, EVs were isolated from the cleared supernatants using polymer-based precipitation (ExoQuick-TC, System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA, USA), as previously described [37]. Finally, the isolated myobundle EVs were further purified by single-EV sorting using a high-resolution flow-cytometry-based sorter (Supplementary Fig. 1) or characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution flow cytometry, following the Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV 2023) recommendations [38].

PBS was filtered two times through a 100 nm filter (EMD Millipore) to produce double-filtered PBS (df-PBS) with minimal background noise. Myobundle EVs were suspended in df-PBS for immunostaining and subjected to single-EV sorting. Since no high-quality fluorescence-conjugated RYR1 antibody is available for flow cytometry, we used fluorescence-conjugated antibodies against human RYR (Catalog # NB300-543PECY55, which recognizes both RYR1 and RYR2) and RYR2 (#NBP2-80143APCCY7) (Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA). As previously reported [15], the RYR1+ popula­tion was defined as RYR+RYR2−, the RYR2+ population as RYR+RYR2+, and the RYR− population as RYR−RYR2−. RYR1+ and RYR− EVs were sorted from myobundle EVs using a high-resolution Sony MA 900 Multi-Application Sorter (Sony Biotechnology, San Jose, CA, USA) with our newly optimized EV acquisition [39–41] and sorter settings, including an Ultra-Purity mode, 3-nanoliter drop volume, sample pressure of 8 to 10, and acquisition rates below 500 events/second.

Plasma EV isolation and characterization

Following our reported protocols [15, 41], fresh whole blood specimens were collected in EDTA tubes and centrifuged to remove cells, platelets, and debris; samples were aliquoted and frozen at −80 °C until analysis. To minimize co-isolation of contaminants, we also employed an integrated EV isolation workflow combining centrifugation and precipitation. On the day of EV separation, frozen plasma aliquots were fully thawed and centrifuged at 2,000 g for 10 minutes at 4 °C to remove residual debris. EV pellets and EV-depleted supernatants were then isolated from the cleared plasma using polymer-based precipitation (ExoQuick, System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and subsequently characterized by TEM imaging and high-resolution flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 2), following MISEV recommendations [38].

High-resolution multicolor flow cytometry

Following our previously described high-resolution flow cytometry protocols [15], separated plasma EVs and myobundle EVs were characterized for size distribution, number, lipid bilayer structure, surface markers, and cargo mitochondria. Briefly, separated EVs were resuspended by df-PBS and stained with fluorescence-conjugated antibodies against human Cluster of Differentiation (CD)9 (#743048), CD81 (#740079), CD63 (#565426), CD29 (#743785), CD8 (#555634), CD56 (#563041), CD15 (#560827), CD14 (#555398), CD68 (#565594), CD235a (#563810), CD31 (#740777), CD41a (#559768), CD34 (#560710), Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA)-ABC (#555552), HLA-DRDPDQ (#740302), CD90 (#563070), CD73 (#562430), CD105 (#563466) (BD Biosciences), CD4 (#47–0048-42), CD19 (#15–0199-42), HLA-G (#MA1-19643) (ThermoFisher Scientific), Fatty Acid-Binding Protein 4 (FABP4; #sc-271529 PE), Muscle Cadherin (MCAD; #sc-398107 FITC) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), RYR, RYR2 and APOA1 (#MAB3664AF405) (Novus Biologicals‎), as well as PKH26 (#MIDI26-1KT) (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA), MitoTracker™ Green (#M7514) and MitoTracker™ Deep Red (#M22426) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After staining, the EVs were re-pelleted by polymer-based precipitation thereby removing excess unbound dye that remains in the EV-depleted supernatant. Unstained EVs, single antibody or dye-stained EVs, and single antibody-stained UltraComp™ eBeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used to determine the fluorescence background. The percentages (%) of EVs carrying each tested molecule were determined using a high-resolution multicolor BD LSR Fortessa X-20 Flow Cytometer (BD Bioscience). The acquisition volume was below 1 µl/second; side scatter (SSC) 200 was used as the flow cytometer threshold, which excludes small debris particles but detects 100 nm silica beads; with this setting, the acquisition events of df-PBS were below 20 events/second. Silica size reference beads of mean size 100, 1000 (NanoXact silica nanoparticles, VWR, Radnor, PA, USA) and 5000 nm (Uniform Silica Microspheres, Bangs Laboratories, Fishers, IN, USA) were used for size estimation [15]. Flow cytometry data analysis was performed using FCS Express 5 software (De Novo Software, Pasadena, CA, USA).

TEM imaging

The morphology of isolated myobundle EVs and plasma EVs were validated using TEM imaging. Formvar/carbon-coated 400-mesh copper grids (Ted Pella, Redding, CA, USA) were glow-discharged for two minutes prior to sample loading. Subsequently, 20 µL isolated EV samples were floated on the grids for 10 minutes, briefly washed twice with deionized water, stained with 2% aqueous uranyl acetate for one minute, and gently dried. Samples were imaged using a JEOL JEM-1230 transmission electron microscope operated at 80 kV (JEOL USA, Peabody, MA, USA). TEM images were captured with a Gatan Orius SC1000 CCD camera and processed using Gatan Microscopy Suite software version 3.10.1002.0 (Gatan, Pleasanton, CA, USA).

SmRNA sequencing (smRNA-seq) and data analyses

RNA was extracted from the sorted RYR1+ and RYR− myobundle EVs using an ExoQuick® Exosome RNA Column Purification Kit (System Biosciences), as reported in our previous study [37]. The total RNA from each sample was dissolved in 20 µl water, and the RNA amount was determined by Qubit™ RNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions [37]. Library preparation using QIAseq miRNA Library Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, Netherlands) and smRNA-Seq were performed by the Duke Sequencing and Genomic Technologies Shared Resource on the NovaSeq 6000 Sequencing System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) yielding 50bp paired-end reads at a depth of 600 M reads.

Following sequencing, FAST-Q files were uploaded to the GeneGlobe RNA Analysis Portal (QIAGEN) for preprocessing, with resultant raw reads imported to R (v4.3.1) for further analysis. Data normalization was completed using a dilution factor equal to the total RNA/µL after which normalized data were analyzed for differential expression using edgeR, similar to our previous study [37].

MiRNA-to-mRNA target identification was completed using miRTarBase (v9.0) [42], requiring each predicted target to have at least one publication verifying the union using well accepted methods (i.e., reporter assay, western blot, RT-qPCR, microarray, sequencing), and NCBI Blast queries with reverse complement sequences [43].

Identified miRNA were analyzed using STRING network analysis [44] to assess protein-protein interactions and functional enrichment. Enrichment was summarized using the STRING Signal, which combines effect size and statistical significance as a weighted harmonic mean of the observed-to-expected gene count ratio and the negative log of the false discovery rate (−log FDR). Because FDR values tend to favor larger gene sets that are more likely to achieve low p-values, whereas observed-to-expected ratios emphasize smaller gene sets with high foreground-to-background enrichment but limited statistical power, the Signal score balances these complementary metrics to enable a more intuitive ranking of enriched terms.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA), JMP 19 software (SAS, Cary, NC, USA), and R version 4.3.1 were used for statistical analyses. Analyses performed in this study used the following: (1) Shapiro-Wilk test (n < 8) or D’Agostino & Pearson test (n ≥ 8) to assess the data distribution; as specified in each dataset in the figure legend, nonparametric analyses were applied for the datasets with non-normally distributed markers, while parametric analyses were applied for the datasets with all normally distributed markers; (2) the edgeR package to analyze differential expression of dilution-normalized smRNA counts; (3) Mann-Whitney test to compare differential plasma EV profiles between younger vs older healthy donors; (4) Spearman correlation to examine associations among baseline EV biomarkers and clinical variables; (5) Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test to evaluate the profiles of plasma EVs in response to exercise compared to baseline. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 or FDR q < 0.05.

Results

Myobundle EV characterization

TEM confirmed that total myobundle-derived EVs from healthy donors exhibited typical EV morphology and the expected heterogeneity in size and shape (Fig. 1A). The broad size distribution observed by TEM was further confirmed by flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 1B). Consistent with our observations in other biofluid-derived EVs [15, 39, 40], three major EV subpopulations were identified, with their sizes estimated using silica bead references acquired under identical settings: small EVs (SEVs; ~100 nm and smaller), medium EVs (MEVs; ~100–1000 nm), and large EVs (LEVs; ~1000–5000 nm) (Fig. 1B). Phenotypically, myobundle EVs displayed a characteristic bilayer membrane structure (indicated by PKH26 staining) and contained both mitochondria (MitoTracker™ Green-positive, indicating mitochondria regardless of membrane potential) and functionally active mitochondria (MitoTracker™ Deep Red-positive) [14, 45]. Surface marker-based single-EV sorting from myobundle-derived EVs

Previously, we compared miRNA within myobundles and within EVs from those corresponding myobundles and found differential expression of 116 miRNAs (41%) [37], suggesting skeletal muscle miRNA are selectively packaged into EVs. In the present study, we compared miRNA cargo profiles of RYR1+ and RYR− EVs secreted from myobundles. Although single-EV sorting is a major technical challenge, using our newly optimized EV profiling and sorting protocol, we successfully achieved high-purity isolation of RYR1+ myobundle EVs and RYR− EVs (purity > 90%) from the total myobundle EV population (Fig. 1C). This novel approach enables precise isolation of defined EV subpopulations for downstream molecular characterization.

RYR1+ myobundle EVs carry miRNAs with putative health-benefits whose expression level is largely unchanged with healthy aging

Using smRNA-seq, we profiled the miRNA cargo of RYR1+ and RYR− (comparator) myobundle EVs (Fig. 2A). Among the total of 319 miRNAs identified in myobundle EVs from healthy donors, the most abundant miRNAs in both RYR1+ and RYR− myobundle EVs (Fig. 2B) included the following: the muscle-enriched miRNAs (myomiRs) miR-206, miR-1-3p, and miR-133-3p [46]; the regenerative miRNA miR-21-5p [47]; and miR-128-3p, which is highly expressed in skeletal muscle and promotes myoblast differentiation [48]. Compared with RYR− EVs, RYR1+ myobundle EVs contained a total of 24 enriched miRNAs (Graphical Abstract), including three miRNAs (miR-181d-5p, miR-323b-3p and miR-424-3p) exclusively detected in this EV subpopulation (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 1), along with an additional 21 miRNAs with significantly higher counts (p < 0.05; including let-7 g-5p, miR-1-3p, miR-24-3p, miR-26b-5p, miR-30e-3p, miR-34a-5p, miR-128-3p, miR-149-5p, miR-206, miR-208a-3p, miR-214-3p, miR-376a-3p, miR-377-3p, miR-454-3p, miR-455-5p, miR-493-3p, miR-493-5p, miR-660-5p, miR-769-5p, miR-1307-5p and miR-3613-3p) (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 2). Collectively, these 24 RYR1+ EV-enriched miRNAs were predicted to target 422 mRNAs with strong experimental evidence (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). STRING network analysis [44] of these targets revealed enrichment of 2,244 biological processes with an FDR < 0.05 (Fig. 2C). The top ten enriched biological processes, ranked by Signal (see Methods), included response to growth factor, gland development, cellular response to growth factor stimulus, regulation of smooth muscle cell proliferation, negative regulation of programmed cell death, positive regulation of cell population proliferation, negative regulation of apoptotic process, negative regulation of cell death, positive regulation of transferase activity, and cellular senescence (Fig. 2D). In addition, 9, 11, and 92 Biological Processes were associated with the regulation of insulin, glucose, and various metabolic pathways, respectively (Supplementary Table 3).

In addition, compared with RYR− EVs, RYR1+ myobundle EVs exhibited significantly lower counts of two miRNAs: miR-126-5p and miR-3117-3p (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 2). According to MiRTarBase 9.0, miR-126-5p has eight experimentally validated target mRNAs with strong evidence, whereas miR-3117-3p has no confirmed targets with strong evidence (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 2). STRING network analysis [44] of the miR-126-5p targets did not reveal enrichment of any biological processes at an FDR < 0.05 (Fig. 2C).

Age influenced the abundance of several miRNAs in RYR1+ myobundle EVs. Specifically, compared with younger donors, older healthy donors exhibited significantly higher counts in RYR1+ myobundle EVs of two miRNAs (miR-152-3p and miR-675-3p) and lower counts of nine miRNAs (miR-10b-3p, miR-142-3p, miR-142-5p, miR-320b, miR-378a-5p, miR-381-3p, miR-493-3p, miR-1275, miR-4492) (Fig. 2E). Apart from the modest reduction in miR-493-3p in older donors, the abundance of all other RYR1+ myobundle EV-enriched miRNAs (23 of 24) associated with putative health benefits remained stable with age (Fig. 2E). These findings suggest that RYR1+ myobundle EVs from older healthy donors may retain a youthful, health-promoting miRNA signature.

Exercise modulates plasma EV subpopulations related to immune and muscle cells

Since plasma EVs comprise highly heterogeneous subpopulations of diverse cellular and tissue origins, we adapted our established surface marker panels from our recent studies and further profiled plasma EVs for surface markers predominantly associated with hematopoietic cells including major immune cells, hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), MSCs, skeletal muscle, and other cells [7, 15]. Compared to baseline EV profiles, HIIT in older adults with prediabetes significantly increased the frequencies of nine plasma EV subpopulations that are predominantly related to immune cells as defined by their surface markers [15], including HLA-ABC (LEVs, MEVs), CD9 (LEVs, SEVs), CD14 (MEVs, SEVs), CD29 (LEVs), CD81 and HLA-DRDPDQ (SEVs) (Fig. 6D), consistent with enhanced innate immune function [3]. In addition, CD41a+ MEVs, which are associated with platelets and HSCs, were also elevated following HIIT (Fig. 6D).

High-amount/vigorous-intensity mode significantly increased the frequencies of fourteen plasma EV subpopulations compared to baseline, including those carrying surface markers predominantly enriched in the following cell types: skeletal muscle—MCAD and FABP4 (SEVs); adipocytes—FABP4 (SEVs); MSCs—CD105 (LEVs and SEVs); red blood cells (RBCs) and/or pluripotent stem cells (PSCs)—CD235a (MEVs and SEVs); immune cells—CD8 (LEVs, MEVs, SEVs), CD56 and CD19 (MEVs, SEVs), CD68 (MEVs) (Fig. 6E). In contrast, this exercise mode significantly decreased immune cell-related LEVs (HLA-ABC+, CD31+) (Fig. 6E).

Compared to baseline, the low-amount/vigorous-intensity mode significantly increased the frequencies of three plasma EV subpopulations predominantly related to skeletal muscle (MCAD+ LEVs), MSCs (CD105+ SEVs), and immune cells (CD29+ SEVs) (Fig. 6F).

Notably, the frequencies of some EV biomarkers in plasma LEVs or SEVs were induced by two different exercise modes in older adults (Fig. 6G). Specifically, both HIIT and high-amount/vigorous-intensity modes induced EVs related to skeletal muscle (RYR1+) (Fig. 6A, B); both high-amount/vigorous-intensity and low-amount/vigorous-intensity modes induced EVs related to cardiac muscle (RYR2+) (Fig. 6B, C), skeletal muscle (MCAD+), and MSCs (CD105+) (Fig. 6E, F); both HIIT and low-amount/vigorous-intensity modes induced EVs related to immune cells (CD29+) (Fig. 6D, F).

Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively characterized the smRNA cargo of skeletal muscle-derived EVs and explored their potential roles in healthy aging, metabolic regulation, and exercise responsiveness. Our findings provide several important insights supporting RYR1+ skeletal muscle-derived EVs as a correlative biomarker of metabolic health and a robust indicator of exercise responsiveness. Separately, these observations generate the hypothesis that RYR1+ EVs may potentially act as functional mediators of systemic metabolic regulation. However, this proposed role is not directly tested in the present study and will require future investigation using targeted functional approaches, including RYR1+ EV transfer experiments and gain- or loss-of-function models targeting miRNAs enriched within RYR1+ EVs.

The most abundant miRNAs (miR-206, miR-21-5p, miR-1-3p, miR-128-3p, and miR-133-3p) and piRNAs (DQ575660, DQ597916, DQ570956, DQ594453, and DQ575659) were shared between RYR− and RYR1+ myobundle EVs, defining a conserved smRNA signature of skeletal muscle-derived EVs. Notably, among these conserved miRNAs, miR-206, miR-1-3p, miR-133-3p, and miR-128-3p are enriched in skeletal muscle and promote myoblast differentiation [46, 48], while miR-21-5p, identified in our recent study [47], exhibits regenerative capacity. Although the functions of piRNAs remain largely unexplored, two of the most abundant piRNAs, DQ597916 (piR-28004) and DQ594453 (piR-24541), have been reported to be enriched in MSC-derived SEVs [17], suggesting their potential for health benefits that warrant further investigation.

Our previous study demonstrated that myobundle EVs selectively package specific miRNAs from their parent myobundle cells [37]. The present study supports this observation, showing that a range of smRNAs with putative health benefits were significantly enriched in RYR1+ myobundle EVs compared with RYR− EVs. Notably, several RYR1+ myobundle EV-enriched miRNAs, including miR-206, miR-1-3p, miR-208a-3p, miR-24-3p, miR-128-3p and miR-493-3p, play roles in promoting or regulating skeletal muscle proliferation and differentiation [46, 50–52]. The RYR1+ myobundle EV-enriched miRNAs, targeting 422 mRNAs with strong experimental support, revealed enrichment of over 2,200 biological processes, including growth factor responses, regulation of cell proliferation, apoptosis, and cellular senescence. Importantly, many of these processes are directly related to insulin signaling, glucose homeostasis, and broader metabolic regulation, suggesting that RYR1+ EVs may mediate muscle-metabolic crosstalk. By targeting pathways involved in survival, proliferation, and metabolic regulation, these EVs may be associated with tissue homeostasis, cellular resilience, and systemic metabolic health, although these mechanisms require further investigation. Interestingly, the abundance of RYR1+ -enriched smRNAs was largely unchanged with age, indicating that certain skeletal muscle-derived EV subpopulations preserve their molecular cargo with aging and may help maintain muscle-systemic communication in older adults.

In contrast to the age-related stability of RYR1+ EV-enriched smRNA cargo during health aging, plasma EVs demonstrated age-associated shifts in skeletal muscle-related RYR1+ EV subpopulations. Specifically, the proportions of RYR1+ MEVs and SEVs declined with age, whereas RYR1+ LEVs remained stable. Notably, greater plasma levels of RYR1+ MEVs and SEVs in two independent human cohorts of older adults showed consistent associations with better insulin action, as evidenced by their inverse correlations with fasting insulin and HOMA-IR. Together, these findings suggest that distinct skeletal muscle-derived EV subpopulations may serve as biomarkers of systemic metabolic regulation, warranting validation in larger human cohorts.

We previously reported that ten weeks of HIIT in older adults with prediabetes reduced fasting insulin and HOMA-IR, and improved glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity [3]. In the present study, analysis of plasma from this same HIIT cohort revealed that HIIT significantly increased the abundance of RYR1+ plasma LEVs. Additionally, high-amount/vigorous-intensity exercise significantly increased skeletal muscle-related RYR1+ and cardiac muscle-related RYR2+ SEVs, whereas low-amount/vigorous-intensity exercise significantly increased RYR2+ LEVs. Our findings suggest that different modes of exercise selectively promote the release of specific muscle-derived EV subpopulations. In parallel, preclinical studies in mice show that HIIT improves insulin sensitivity and alters the smRNA cargo of plasma SEVs; transfer of plasma EVs from trained to sedentary mice enhances glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity [4]. Additionally, HIIT increased the frequencies of nine immune cell-derived EV subpopulations, supporting the role of EVs in improvements in innate immune function associated with HIIT [3]. Similarly, low- and high-amount/vigorous-intensity exercise significantly increased the frequencies of three and fourteen plasma EV subpopulations, respectively, originating from multiple cell types, including immune cells. Together, these observations underscore the dynamic responsiveness of plasma EV populations to physiological stimuli and reinforce the role of skeletal muscle-derived EVs as key mediators of intercellular and inter-tissue communication [19, 20]. In addition, exercise induces a range of systemic factors, including circulating hormones, inflammatory mediators, and other metabolic intermediates, which may independently or synergistically influence changes in insulin action [53, 54].

A key limitation of the present study is that the link between RYR1+ skeletal muscle-derived EV miRNA cargo and insulin signaling is based on STRING network analysis on enrichments of mRNA targets rather than direct functional validation. Although several miRNAs enriched in RYR1+ EVs are predicted or previously reported to target components of insulin signaling pathways, we did not perform gain- or loss-of-function experiments, nor did we assess the effects of EV transfer on insulin signaling in recipient cells or tissues. Therefore, the observed relationships should be interpreted as correlative. Future studies employing targeted manipulation of specific miRNAs and EV-based functional assays will be necessary to establish causal mechanisms and to define the direct contribution of RYR1+ EV cargo to insulin action. An additional limitation of this study is that we did not directly demonstrate a causal contribution of RYR1+ skeletal muscle-derived EVs to the regulation of insulin action. Although RYR1+ EVs are responsive to exercise and associated with measures of insulin sensitivity, exercise elicits a complex systemic response involving circulating hormones, inflammatory mediators, and metabolic adaptations in multiple tissues that may independently or interactively influence insulin action. Therefore, the relative contribution of RYR1+ EVs and other exercise-modulated EV subpopulations cannot be isolated from broader exercise-induced systemic adaptations. Future studies designed to isolate EV-specific effects, including RYR1+ EV transfer approaches and integration with systemic metabolic profiling, will be necessary to define their roles in insulin action.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that human skeletal muscle-derived EVs, particularly RYR1+ EVs, carry a distinct repertoire of smRNAs with potential health-promoting functions. Notably, miRNAs enriched in RYR1+ EVs are largely maintained with age, and the association of RYR1+ MEVs and SEVs with improved insulin action suggests that these EV subpopulations may be associated with the preservation of muscle and metabolic health during aging. Furthermore, exercise-induced modulation of both skeletal muscle- and immune cell-derived EVs provides mechanistic insight into how physical activity enhances metabolic and immune function. Collectively, these results highlight skeletal muscle-derived EVs as novel biomarkers and potential mediators of systemic metabolic regulation and healthy aging. While our data support an association between exercise-responsive RYR1+ EVs and measures of insulin action, we acknowledge that we did not experimentally demonstrate a causal contribution of these EVs to insulin regulation. Future studies should elucidate the functional roles of these EV subpopulations, including their cargo-mediated effects on target tissues such as pancreatic β-cells, adipose tissue, and other metabolic organs. In addition, defining the mechanisms governing selective EV release and cargo packaging during aging and in response to exercise will be critical for developing EV-based therapeutic strategies to preserve metabolic and musculoskeletal health in older adults.
