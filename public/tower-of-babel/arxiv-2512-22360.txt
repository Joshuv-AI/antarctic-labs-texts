Generalized K K -theoretic invariants and wall-crossing via non-abelian localization
Ivan Karpov, Miguel Moreira

Abstract
. Given an abelian category and a stability condition satisfying appropriate conditions, we define generalized K K -theoretic invariants and prove that they satisfy wall-crossing formulas. For this, we introduce a new associative algebra structure on the K K -homology of the stack of objects of an abelian category, which we call the K K -Hall algebra. We first define δ \delta -invariants directly coming from the stack of semistable objects and use the K K -Hall algebra to take a formal logarithm and construct ε \varepsilon -invariants. We prove that these satisfy appropriate wall-crossing formulas using the non-abelian localization theorem. Based on work of Joyce in the cohomological setting, Liu had previously defined similar invariants assuming the existence of a framing functor; we show that when their definition of invariants makes sense it agrees with ours. Our results extend Joyce–Liu wall-crossing to non-standard hearts of D b ​ ( X ) D^{b}(X) , for which framing functors are not known to exist.



1.
Introduction

A sizable portion of enumerative geometry concerns understanding invariants arising from moduli spaces of objects in certain abelian categories
{\mathcal{A}}
, such as the abelian category of sheaves on a smooth projective variety
X
, of representations of a quiver
Q
, or of objects in non-standard hearts of
D^{b}(X)
. To form good moduli spaces we require a stability condition
\mu
and look at the moduli space (or moduli stack)
M_{\alpha}^{\mu}
of
\mu
-semistable objects on
{\mathcal{A}}
of topological type
\alpha\in C({\mathcal{A}})
– topological type can mean, for example, the Chern character of a sheaf, or the dimension vector of a representation of a quiver. The prototypical example of a stability condition is the slope
\mu(E)=\deg(E)/\mathrm{rk}(E)
of a vector bundle
E
on a curve. There are many different invariants we may attach to the moduli space
M_{\alpha}^{\mu}
, such as:

(1)

Motivic invariants, for example the Euler characteristic
e(M_{\alpha}^{\mu})
or the virtual Poincaré polynomial.

(2)

Cohomological invariants, i.e. (virtual) intersection numbers of tautological classes
D
:

\int_{[M_{\alpha}^{\mu}]^{\mathrm{vir}}}D\in{\mathbb{Q}}\,.

(3)

K
-theoretic invariants, i.e. (virtual) Euler characteristic of tautological
K
-theory classes
V
:

\chi(M_{\alpha}^{\mu},{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathrm{vir}}\otimes V)\in{\mathbb{Q}}\,.

In either case, a fundamental problem is to understand how the invariants depend on the choice of stability condition
\mu
. A closely related question is to define such invariants in cases where there are strictly semistable objects.

In the motivic setting, a very successful theory of wall-crossing has been around for 20 years, developed by Joyce
[
Joy2
,
Joy3
,
Joy4
,
Joy6
]
, Joyce–Song
[
JS
]
and Kontsevich–Soibelman
[
KS
]
. It plays a key role, for example, in the proof of the DT/PT correspondence
[
Tod2
,
Bri1
]
, the PT rationality conjecture
[
Tod3
]
, and in many other applications.

A more recent development is a proposal of a wall-crossing theory for cohomological invariants
[
Joy1
,
Joy7
,
GJT
]
. Liu adapted the ideas from the cohomological setting to the
K
-theory setting in
[
Liu
]
. The cohomological and
K
-theoretic settings share some similarities with the motivic one, most notably the existence of a Lie algebra that is used to write down the wall-crossing formulas; indeed, the wall-crossing formulas in all 3 contexts look exactly the same, but in 3 different Lie algebras.

However, there are also many differences. In the motivic wall-crossing setting, the invariants in the presence of strictly semistable objects can be defined from the stacks of semistable objects, which have well-defined virtual Poincaré polynomials, for example. On the other hand, there is no reasonable way to take intersection numbers on a stack, so Joyce’s approach in the cohomological case is quite different. He instead uses the additional data of a framing functor to construct auxiliary moduli spaces of pairs – which do not have strictly semistable objects –, and uses those to define “generalized cohomological invariants”.

There are natural framing functors for sheaves or for representations of quivers. However, if we are interested in counting (weak) Bridgeland stable objects – which is necessary in some applications, such as the PT rationality
[
Tod3
,
Bri1
]
or the rank
r
from rank
0/1
theorem
[
FT2
,
FT1
]
–, it is unclear how to construct framing functors, and even if one should expect them to exist. For this reason, wall-crossing formulas in such contexts were so far only conjectural.

In this paper, we develop a completely new approach to Joyce–Liu style generalized
K
-theoretic invariants and wall-crossing formulas. Our approach does not require a framing functor, and therefore we are able to prove wall-crossing formulas in greater generality, including for example (weak) Bridgeland stability conditions. What we do is much closer in spirit to the motivic approach, where invariants are extracted directly from Artin stacks of semistable objects.

We expect that this increase in scope will have interesting applications soon. Our proof is also shorter and, arguably, conceptually cleaner – although it requires some machinery, most notably the virtual non-abelian localization theorem of Halpern-Leistner
[
HL1
]
– so we hope this will make the theory more accessible and easier to extend to new contexts.

Summary of results

Throughout,
{\mathcal{A}}

\mu

\alpha

1.1.1.
\delta
-invariants

A feature of
K
-theory which plays an essential role in this paper is that we can take Euler characteristics of perfect complexes on stacks with appropriate assumptions. In particular,

\chi({\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}^{\mu,\mathrm{rig}},V)\coloneqq\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}(-1)^{n}\dim H^{n}({\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}^{\mu,\mathrm{rig}},V)\in{\mathbb{Z}}

is well-defined for any perfect complex
V
, meaning that the cohomology groups are finite dimensional and only finitely many are non-trivial. Note that the stacks considered have derived structures, so this is really a virtual Euler characteristic. Thus, the stack
{\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}^{\mu}
defines a functional

\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu}\colon K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathrm{rig}})\rightarrow K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}^{\mu,\mathrm{rig}})\xrightarrow{\chi}{\mathbb{Z}}\,,

where
K^{\ast}(-)
denotes
K
-theory of perfect complexes and the first map is restriction along the open inclusion
{\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}^{\mu,\mathrm{rig}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathrm{rig}}
. For technical reasons, it is important that this functional can be upgraded to an element of
K
-homology
\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu}\in K_{\ast}({\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathrm{rig}})
, see Definition
3.9
and Theorem
3.12
. The necessary material on
K
-theory and
K
-homology is covered in Section
3
.

1.1.2.
K
-Hall algebra and
\varepsilon
-invariants

While the
\delta
-invariants carry information concerning
K
-theoretic invariants of the stack of semistables, they are not the “correct” ones. For example, they do not agree with the ones defined in
[
Liu
]
. Indeed, it turns out that our
\delta
-invariants do not even live in the version of
K
-homology that Liu uses (cf. Definition
7.8
, Example
4.14
). A similar phenomena happens in the motivic setting, and we use the same solution: define
\varepsilon
-invariants from
\delta
-invariants by taking a “logarithm”. We survey the motivic setting, and the no-pole theorem, in Section
1.2
, which served as inspiration for our approach.

It turns out that we can define a product on
K_{\ast}({\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathrm{rig}})
, which should be regarded as the analog of the multiplication in the motivic Hall algebra.

Theorem A

(=Theorem
4.6
)
.

Definition
4.2
makes

{\mathbb{K}}({\mathcal{A}})\coloneqq K_{\ast}({\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathrm{rig}}_{\mathcal{A}})_{\mathbb{Q}}

an associative algebra.

We use this product structure to take a formal logarithm and define

\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu}\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{k}=\alpha\\
\mu(\alpha_{i})=\mu(\alpha)\end{subarray}}\delta^{\mu}_{\alpha_{1}}\ast\ldots\ast\delta^{\mu}_{\alpha_{k}}\,.

This sum is finite by our assumptions on the stability condition. The relation can be inverted to express
\delta
-invariants in terms of
\varepsilon
-invariants:

\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{k}=\alpha\\
\mu(\alpha_{i})=\mu(\alpha)\end{subarray}}\varepsilon^{\mu}_{\alpha_{1}}\ast\ldots\ast\varepsilon^{\mu}_{\alpha_{k}}\,.

1.1.3.
Wall-crossing formula

The
\delta
and
\varepsilon
invariants satisfy wall-crossing formulas that can be expressed in terms of the associative algebra structure on
{\mathbb{K}}({\mathcal{A}})
. These wall-crossing formulas are obtained as a consequence of a general theorem by Halpern-Leistner, which expresses Euler characteristics of perfect complexes in terms of the centers of a
\Theta
-stratification – a notion that generalizes Harder–Narasimhan (HN) stratifications.

Every object
E
of
{\mathcal{A}}
admits a canonical
\mu
-HN filtration

0=E_{0}\subsetneq E_{1}\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq E_{n}=E

such that the slopes of the successive quotients are increasing. When the stack
{\mathfrak{M}}
, without any stability, is itself finite type and quasi-smooth, the wall-crossing formula can be expressed as the statement that the element

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}\\
\mu(\alpha_{1})>\ldots>\mu(\alpha_{n})\end{subarray}}\delta_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mu}\ast\ldots\ast\delta_{\alpha_{n}}^{\mu}

in
{\mathbb{K}}({\mathcal{A}})
does not depend on the stability condition
\mu
, and indeed it is equal to the
\delta
-invariant defined with the trivial stability condition. This is the case only in very simple examples, such as representations of quivers.

In general, we use the
\mu
-HN stratification of the stack
{\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}^{\mu_{0}}
to prove a wall-crossing formula between
\mu
and
\mu_{0}
, where
\mu_{0}
is a stability condition on a wall and
\mu
is in a chamber adjacent to
\mu
.

Example 1.1
.

Let
\alpha
be a topological type and
\mu_{0}
be on a simple wall determined by a partition
\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
, and
\mu_{-},\mu_{+}
be stability conditions on the two adjacent chambers, so that

\mu_{0}(\alpha_{1})=\mu_{0}(\alpha_{2})\,,\quad\mu_{-}(\alpha_{1})>\mu_{-}(\alpha_{2})\,,\quad\mu_{+}(\alpha_{1})<\mu_{+}(\alpha_{2})\,.

A
\mu_{0}
-semistable object
E
has two possible types of
\mu_{-}
-HN filtrations: either
E
is
\mu_{-}
-semistable itself, or it is an extension of the form

0\to E_{1}\to E\to E_{2}\to 0

where
E_{1},E_{2}
are semistable
1
1

1

It is part of the “simple wall” assumption that
\mu,\mu_{-},\mu_{+}
stabilities are equivalent for objects in class
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
, and moreover there are no strictly semistables in those classes. Hence

\varepsilon_{\alpha_{i}}^{\mu_{0}}=\varepsilon_{\alpha_{i}}^{\mu_{-}}=\varepsilon_{\alpha_{i}}^{\mu_{+}}=\delta_{\alpha_{i}}^{\mu_{0}}=\delta_{\alpha_{i}}^{\mu_{-}}=\delta_{\alpha_{i}}^{\mu_{+}}\,.

and have topological types
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
, respectively. Thus, the stack
{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mu_{0}}_{\alpha}
admits a
\Theta
-stratification of the form

{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mu_{0}}_{\alpha}={\mathfrak{M}}^{\mu_{-}}_{\alpha}\sqcup{\mathfrak{S}}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}

where
{\mathfrak{S}}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}
is the stack of extensions as above. Non-abelian localization then says that

\chi({\mathfrak{M}}^{\mu_{0}}_{\alpha},F)=\chi({\mathfrak{M}}^{\mu_{-}}_{\alpha},F)+\chi({\mathfrak{M}}^{\mu_{-}}_{\alpha_{1}}\times{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mu_{-}}_{\alpha_{2}},F\otimes E_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}})

for any perfect complex
F
on
{\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}
, where for ease of notation we omit all the restrictions of
F
. The complex
E_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}
is, roughly speaking, the inverse Euler class of the normal bundle to the direct sum map
{\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha_{1}}\times{\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha_{2}}\to{\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}
.

The product on
{\mathbb{K}}({\mathcal{A}})
is cooked up so that this formula can be expressed as

\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu_{0}}=\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu_{-}}+\delta_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mu_{-}}\ast\delta_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mu_{-}}\,.

This is an instance of the dominant wall-crossing formula (Theorem
5.14
). Similarly, we have

\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu_{0}}=\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu_{+}}+\delta_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mu_{+}}\ast\delta_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mu_{+}}\,.

If we convert from
\delta
-invariants to
\varepsilon
-invariants using

\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu_{\pm}}=\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu_{\pm}}\quad\textup{ and }\quad\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu_{0}}=\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu_{0}}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mu_{0}}\ast\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mu_{0}}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mu_{0}}\ast\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mu_{0}}

we obtain the identities

\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu_{-}}+\frac{1}{2}\big[\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mu_{-}},\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mu_{-}}\big]=\varepsilon^{\mu_{0}}_{\alpha}=\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu_{+}}-\frac{1}{2}\big[\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mu_{+}},\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mu_{+}}\big]\,,

where
[\phi,\psi]=\phi\ast\psi-\psi\ast\phi
is the commutator in
{\mathbb{K}}({\mathcal{A}})
. It is a general feature that wall-crossing formulas between
\varepsilon
-invariants can be written using exclusively commutators.

Theorem B

(=Theorem
5.15
)
.

If
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
can be connected by a continuous path crossing finitely many walls then we have the wall-crossing formulas

\displaystyle\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{l}=\alpha}S(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};\mu,\mu^{\prime})\cdot\delta_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mu}\ast\ldots\ast\delta_{\alpha_{n}}^{\mu}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{l}=\alpha}\tilde{U}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};\mu,\mu^{\prime})\cdot[[\ldots[\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mu},\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mu}],\ldots,],\varepsilon_{\alpha_{n}}^{\mu}]

in
{\mathbb{K}}({\mathcal{A}})
, where
S(-),\tilde{U}(-)\in{\mathbb{Q}}
are explicit combinatorial coefficients (cf.
[
Joy6
, Section 4]
).

A particular setting where our theorem establishes a wall-crossing formula that was not previously proven is that of tilt stability:

Corollary 1.2
.

If
X
is a surface with nef anticanonical or a Fano 3-fold satisfying the BMT inequality then we have generalized
K
-theoretic invariants of the moduli of tilt semistable objects and these satisfy the wall-crossing formulas in Theorem
B
.

We refer to Sections
2.3
and
5.6
for details on this application.

Comparison with Joyce–Liu invariants

Sections

Theorem C

Let

\Pi_{\ast}\big(\Lambda_{-1}({\mathbb{T}}_{\Pi}^{\vee})\cap[P_{\alpha}^{\textup{JS}}]^{\mathrm{vir}}\big)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}=\alpha\\
\mu(\alpha_{i})=\mu(\alpha)\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n!}\lambda(\alpha_{1})\big[\big[\ldots\big[\varepsilon_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mu},\varepsilon_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mu}\big],\ldots,\varepsilon_{\alpha_{n}}^{\mu}\big]\,.

This identity is taken as the definition of

-theoretic invariants in
[

\widecheck{K}_{\ast}^{\mathrm{reg}}({\mathfrak{M}})_{\mathbb{Q}}

Theorem D

(=Theorem

The natural map

An important point here is that the Lie algebra
\widecheck{K}_{\ast}^{\mathrm{reg}}({\mathfrak{M}})_{\mathbb{Q}}
is not obtained as the commutator of some naturally defined product. Indeed, its image in
{\mathbb{K}}({\mathcal{A}})

\widecheck{K}_{\ast}^{\mathrm{reg}}({\mathfrak{M}})_{\mathbb{Q}}

{\mathbb{M}}_{\mathrm{reg}}({\mathcal{A}})
and
{\mathbb{M}}({\mathcal{A}})

Finally, in Section

\phi\in K_{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})

Theorem E

(=Theorem

)
.

If there exists a framing functor, Joyce’s classes in

are homological lifts of

We expect canonical homological lifts of

1.2.
Analogy with motivic wall-crossing

Part of the motivation for the present article is to make the cohomological or
K
-theoretic wall-crossing
[
Joy7
,
Liu
]
closer to the approach to motivic invariants
[
Joy4
,
Joy2
,
JS
,
KS
,
Bri2
]
. We will now describe some aspects of the motivic story to make this comparison apparent. For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to hereditary categories
{\mathcal{A}}
, such as representations of a quiver or moduli of bundles on a curve, and to standard Euler characteristic (i.e. “naive DT invariants”), rather than Behrend weighted Euler characteristics (“genuine DT invariants”). We also work only in the quantum torus, and not in the motivic Hall algebra.

Let
\mu
be a stability condition on
{\mathcal{A}}
and
\alpha
a topological type. When
\mu
-stability and
\mu
-semistablity coincide, we are interested in motivic invariants of the good moduli space
M_{\alpha}^{\mu}={\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}^{\mu,\mathrm{rig}}
, such as its Euler characteristic
e(M_{\alpha}^{\mu})
or its virtual Poincaré polynomial
P_{q}(M_{\alpha}^{\mu})\in{\mathbb{Q}}[q^{\frac{1}{2}}]
, which is the unique motivic invariant which agrees with the usual Poincaré polynomial

P_{q}(X)=\sum_{i}(-q^{1/2})^{i}\dim H^{i}(X)

when
X
is smooth and proper. Being motivic means that it satisfies

P_{q}(X\times Y)=P_{q}(X)P_{q}(Y)\textup{ and }P_{q}(X)=P_{q}(U)+P_{q}(X\setminus U)

for any
X,Y
and
U\subseteq X
open. The virtual Poincaré polynomial can be constructed for a general variety
X
using the weight filtration on its cohomology
[
DK
]
. The specialization
q^{1/2}=1
recover the Euler characteristic.

To define invariants in the presence of strictly semistable objects, one might try to use directly the stack
{\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}^{\mu}
or its rigidification
{\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}^{\mu,\mathrm{rig}}
. Indeed, it is explained in
[
Joy5
, Section 4.2]
that there is a unique way to extend the virtual Poincaré polynomial to finite type stacks with affine stabilizers by requiring that
P_{q}([X/G])=P_{q}(X)/P_{q}(G)
for special groups
G
(in particular,
G={\rm GL}_{n}
is special); for such stacks
{\mathcal{X}}
,

P_{q}({\mathcal{X}})\in{\mathbb{Z}}[q^{\pm 1/2},(1-q^{i})^{-1}]\,.

Since
P_{q}({\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}^{\mu,\mathrm{rig}})
has a pole at 1 when stable is not equal to semistable, we cannot set
q^{1/2}=1
and obtain the Euler characteristic. One way to obtain something like an Euler characteristic is to first take a “logarithm” on the motivic Hall algebra, or on the quantum torus.

The quantum torus of
{\mathcal{A}}
is defined to be the associative
{\mathbb{Q}}[q^{\pm 1/2},(1-q^{i})^{-1}]
-algebra

{\mathbb{M}}({\mathcal{A}})\coloneqq\bigoplus_{\alpha\in C({\mathcal{A}})}{\mathbb{Q}}[q^{\pm 1/2},(1-q^{i})^{-1}]\cdot e^{\alpha}

with product given by
2
2

2

Usually there is no factor of
1/(q-1)
and
\delta
invariants below are defined using the non rigidified stack, but to make the analogy with the
K
-theory setting more clear we are making this small modification. The usual
\delta,\varepsilon
invariants are
\frac{\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu}}{q-1},\frac{\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu}}{q-1}
in our convention.

e^{\alpha}\ast e^{\beta}=\frac{q^{-\chi(\beta,\alpha)}}{q-1}e^{\alpha+\beta}\,.

The virtual Poincaré polynomial of the (rigidified) stack defines an element

\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu}\coloneqq P_{q}({\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}^{\mu,\mathrm{rig}})\cdot e^{\alpha}\,.

Then, we modify these classes by taking a logarithm in the quantum torus, i.e.

\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}\\
\mu(\alpha_{i})=\mu(\alpha)\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}\delta_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mu}\ast\ldots\ast\delta_{\alpha_{n}}^{\mu}\,.

A remarkable theorem of Joyce
[
Joy4
, Theorem 8.7]
is that the
\varepsilon
invariants no longer have a pole at
q=1
– see also
[
BNK
]
and
[
BR
]
for more recent approaches to the proof of this result. More precisely, they are regular elements, i.e. lie in

{\mathbb{M}}_{\mathrm{reg}}({\mathcal{A}})\coloneqq\bigoplus_{\alpha\in C({\mathcal{A}})}{\mathbb{Q}}[q^{\pm 1/2},(1+q+\ldots+q^{i-1})^{-1}]\cdot e^{\alpha}\subseteq{\mathbb{M}}({\mathcal{A}})\,.

In particular, it now makes sense to specialize
q^{1/2}=1
. This produces the definition of generalized (naive) DT invariants:

\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu}|_{q=1}=\mathrm{DT}_{\alpha}^{\mu}\cdot e^{\alpha}\,.

Example 1.3
.

Consider
{\mathcal{A}}=\mathsf{Vect}
the abelian category of finite dimensional vector spaces with the trivial stability condition. Then

\delta_{n}=P_{q}(B{\rm PGL}_{n})\cdot e^{n}=q^{-n+1}\prod_{n=1}^{n-1}(q^{n}-q^{i})^{-1}\cdot e^{n}\,.

Then we have, for example,

\varepsilon_{2}=\delta_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{1}\ast\varepsilon_{1}=\left(\frac{1}{q(q^{2}-1)}-\frac{1}{2q(q-1)}\right)\cdot e^{2}=-\frac{1}{2q(q+1)}\cdot e^{2}\,.

In particular,
\mathrm{DT}_{2}=-\frac{1}{4}
. More generally,
\mathrm{DT}_{n}=\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n^{2}}

[
JS
, Example 6.2]
.

The regular elements
{\mathbb{M}}_{\mathrm{reg}}({\mathcal{A}})\subseteq{\mathbb{M}}({\mathcal{A}})
do not form a subalgebra of
{\mathbb{M}}({\mathcal{A}})
. However, they form a Lie subalgebra of
{\mathbb{M}}({\mathcal{A}})
with the commutator
[u,v]=u\ast v-v\ast u
. This is clear from the observation that

[e^{\alpha},e^{\beta}]=\frac{q^{-\chi(\beta,\alpha)}-q^{-\chi(\alpha,\beta)}}{q-1}\cdot e^{\alpha+\beta}

is a polynomial in
q
.

Wall-crossing formulas can be efficiently written in
{\mathbb{M}}({\mathcal{A}})
. Indeed, they take exactly the form discussed in Section
1.1.3
, and served as inspiration for our construction of
{\mathbb{K}}({\mathcal{A}})
. For example when
{\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}
itself is finite type

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}\\
\mu(\alpha_{1})>\ldots>\mu(\alpha_{n})\end{subarray}}\delta_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mu}\ast\ldots\ast\delta_{\alpha_{n}}^{\mu}=P_{q}({\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha})\cdot e^{\alpha}

does not depend on
\mu
. Indeed, this is a consequence of the Harder–Narasimhan stratification, as the product
\ast
is built so that the Harder–Narasimhan stratification gives this formula. This provides a way to compare classes
\delta^{\mu}_{\alpha},\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu^{\prime}}
for two stability conditions
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
. Joyce shows
[
Joy6
, Theorem 5.4]
that when we replace
\delta
classes by
\varepsilon
classes, the resulting formula can be written entirely using the commutator on
{\mathbb{M}}({\mathcal{A}})
; in other words, it can be written entirely inside the Lie subalgebra
{\mathbb{M}}_{\mathrm{reg}}({\mathcal{A}})
.

1.3.
Conjectures and future directions

In certain aspects, we have opted to keep a slightly simpler (and hence less general) setup than in
[
Joy7
]
. For example, we do not consider here the possibility for reduced obstruction theories, necessary for wall-crossing on surfaces with
p_{g}>0
. We have also opted to do everything non-equivariantly, unlike
[

]
. It should be possible to incorporate both of these aspects in our approach without too much trouble.

Recently,
[

,
BNK
]
established a generalization of the motivic DT theory to stacks that do not come from linear moduli problems. This is done by replacing the motivic Hall algebra by a structure they call “motivic Hall induction”, intrinsically associated to any stack (under reasonable conditions). A similar extension of the content of this paper using their formalism is possible, and will be pursued elsewhere. This should, in particular, answer questions raised, e.g., by Bu in the setting of wall-crossing for self-dual categories:
[
Bu2
]
.

A more ambitious extension is to the setting of sheaves on Calabi–Yau 4-folds, or more generally
(-2)
-shifted symplectic stacks. A mild modification of our
K
-Hall algebra allows a definition of generalized
K
-theoretic invariants in that context. The missing piece is an appropriate version of the non-abelian localization theorem for
(-2)
-shifted symplectic stacks.

We make Conjectures
7.12
and
8.9
, which are similar in spirit to the no-pole theorem in the motivic setting. The first says that the classes
\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu}
more or less comes from a scheme, and the second says that
\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu}
admits a homological counterpart that lives in algebraic degree equal to the virtual dimension. We show that both of these hold when there are framings, but it would be desirable to have a more general argument. We hope these conjectures might be addressed by relating
\varepsilon
classes to the (derived) Kirwan desingularization of the moduli stack, cf.
[
HRS
]
.

The more straightforward applications are the ones to explicit wall-crossing problems which still remain unsolved at the time of our writing the present paper. First of all, it seems that with little extra care the PT-rationality for Fano threefolds (i.e., the Fano analogue of
[
Tod3
]
) may be obtained. Second, in some cases, we are able to apply the present results to imitate Feyzbakhsh-Thomas programme (cf., e.g.,
[
FT2
]
) for Fano
3

[

, p.14]

Notation and conventions

Every scheme, algebraic space, stack and derived stack in this paper is considered over
{\mathbb{C}}
. By (derived) stack we mean a (derived) 1-Artin stack; all the (derived) stacks in this paper will be locally of finite type, have affine diagonal, and a perfect cotangent complex. Higher (derived) stacks are in the sense of higher Artin stacks of
[
Toë
]
. We will typically denote stacks with calligraphic font (e.g.
{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Z}},{\mathcal{M}}
) and derived stacks with Fraktur font (e.g.

).

By (co)homology of a derived stack

we mean the (co)homology with
{\mathbb{Q}}
-coefficients of the topological realization (cf.
[

, Section 3]
) of the classical truncation
{\mathcal{X}}

H_{\ast}(-)=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}H_{n}(-)\textup{ and }H^{\ast}(-)=\prod_{n\geq 0}H^{n}(-)\,.

Chern classes of perfect complexes are defined by pulling back universal Chern classes along the topological realization of the induced map

2.2.
Stability conditions,
\Theta
-stratifications and good moduli spaces

To make sense of taking integrals (i.e, Euler characteristics) of perfect complexes over some stack, one usually requires the existence of a proper
good moduli space
. This statement is formalized as Theorem
3.12
below.

Definition 2.6

(
[
Alp
]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be an Artin stack. A good moduli space for
{\mathcal{X}}
is an algebraic space
X
together with a morphism
\phi:{\mathcal{X}}\to X
such that

(1)

\phi
is quasi-compact;

(2)

the pushforward functor
\phi_{*}\colon\mathrm{QCoh}({\mathcal{X}})\to\mathrm{QCoh}(X)
is exact on the categories of the quasi-coherent sheaves;

(3)

the natural morphism
\mathcal{O}_{X}\to\phi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}}
is an isomorphism.

A good moduli space of a derived stack
{\mathfrak{X}}
is a good moduli space of its classical truncation
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathfrak{X}}_{\mathrm{cl}}
(alternatively, see
[
AHPS
]
).

Note that if
{\mathcal{X}}
admits a proper good moduli space then
{\mathcal{X}}
is quasi-compact. This is rarely the case for the stacks parametrizing objects in abelian categories considered above: it is not true for
{\mathcal{A}}=\mathrm{Coh}(X)
if
\dim(X)>0
, but it is true for representations of acyclic quivers.

Proposition 2.7

(
[
AHLH
, Theorem 7.23]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a good abelian category and
\alpha\in C({\mathcal{A}})
. If the stack
{\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}
is of finite type, then
{\mathfrak{M}}_{\alpha}
admits a proper good moduli space.

Typically, to get proper good moduli spaces we need to impose some sort of stability. In the rest of the present section, we will work with a good abelian category
\mathcal{A}
as in Section
2.1
, and recall the criterion for the existence of proper good moduli spaces for the moduli stacks of semistable objects on
{\mathfrak{M}}_{\mathcal{A}}
(following the exposition of
[

, Subsection 3.1]

We start with the notion of a weak stability condition on
\mathcal{A}
. The definition we use is due to Joyce
[
Joy4
]
; its roots go back to Rudakov
[
Rud
]

Let
(T,\leq)
be a totally ordered set, and let
\mu
be a map
\mu:C(\mathcal{A})\to T

E
of
{\mathcal{A}}
, we will write
\mu(E)\coloneqq\mu([E])
; in other words,
\mu
is a locally constant function

Definition 2.8
.

We call

a weak stability condition

\mathcal{A}
if for any
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in C({\mathcal{A}})
with
\beta=\alpha+\gamma

\mu(\alpha)\leq\mu(\beta)\leq\mu(\gamma)
, or
\mu(\alpha)\geq\mu(\beta)\geq\mu(\gamma)
.
4
4

4

We recall that for

to be a stability condition (as opposed to a

stabilty condition), the more restrictive condition should hold: either
\mu(\alpha)<\mu(\beta)<\mu(\gamma)
, or
\mu(\alpha)>\mu(\beta)>\mu(\gamma)
, or
\mu(\alpha)=\mu(\beta)=\mu(\gamma)
.

A weak stability condition defines the notion of
\mu

-semistabile objects as follows.

Definition 2.9

An object

is said to be

\mu

-stable we say it is strictly semistable.

If the category

is

-Artinian and

has a canonical Harder-Narasimhan filtration

0=E_{0}\subsetneq E_{1}\subsetneq E_{2}\ldots\subsetneq E_{n}=E\,,

so that all of the successive quotients

\mu

\mu(F_{1})>\mu(F_{2})>\ldots>\mu(F_{n})\,.

We call (

-HN filtration of

-HN factors of

Moreover, any

Definition 2.12

We say that

Let

be a derived Artin stack. We briefly review the algebraic

]

A complex is perfect/coherent if it is perfect/coherent on smooth affine charts (see

When the structure sheaf

is bounded, in the sense that

for all but finitely many

\mathsf{Perf}({\mathfrak{X}})\subseteq D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}({\mathfrak{X}})\,.

Proposition 3.1

(
[

)
.

If
{\mathfrak{X}}
is quasi-smooth then
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathfrak{X}}
is bounded.

The algebraic

are defined as follows:

Definition 3.2
.

Let
{\mathfrak{X}}
be a derived stack. We let

\displaystyle K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})

\displaystyle=K_{0}(\mathsf{Perf}({\mathfrak{X}}))\,,

\displaystyle G({\mathfrak{X}})

\displaystyle=K_{0}(\mathrm{Coh}(X))=K_{0}(D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}(X))\,

where

denotes the Grothendieck group of a category.

The notation

is not standard, but we will use it to indicate that we think of

as a cohomology theory. When

is bounded, there is a canonical morphism

induced by the inclusion of the respective categories; if

is regular this map is an isomorphism. It it shown in
[

, Corollary 3.4]
that if

is Noetherian then

-theory is insensitive to the derived structure, i.e. the inclusion of the classical truncation
\iota\colon{\mathcal{X}}\coloneqq{\mathfrak{X}}_{\mathrm{cl}}\to{\mathfrak{X}}

The analogous statement is not true in general for
K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})

[
Ann
]
.

Remark 3.3
.

Instead of working with algebraic
K
-theory, another option is to use topological

-theory, in the sense of Blanc
[

]

K^{\ast}_{\textup{top}}({\mathfrak{X}})=K^{\textup{top}}_{0}(\mathsf{Perf}({\mathfrak{X}}))\oplus K^{\textup{top}}_{1}(\mathsf{Perf}({\mathfrak{X}}))\,.

We can do the same with

[

also holds for Blanc’s

3.1.1.

Given a morphism of derived stacks

\displaystyle\colon K^{\ast}(\mathfrak{Z})\to K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})

\displaystyle f^{\ast}

\displaystyle\colon G(\mathfrak{Z})\to G({\mathfrak{X}})\quad\textup{($f$ has finite Tor amplitude)}

If

\displaystyle f_{\ast}

\displaystyle\colon K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})\to K^{\ast}(\mathfrak{Z})\quad\textup{($f$ proper, representable,}

finite Tor amplitude, locally finite type)

\displaystyle f_{\ast}

\displaystyle\colon G({\mathfrak{X}})\to G(\mathfrak{Z})\quad\quad\textup{($f$ proper, representable)}

There are some situations where pushforwards exist even without the map being proper, see

(

3.1.2.
Exterior powers

Exterior powers
\Lambda^{n}(-)
endow

with the structure of a

(3)

\Lambda_{-u}(V)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-u)^{n}\Lambda^{n}(V)\in K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})\llbracket u\rrbracket\,.

A common theme in the paper will be how to expand
\Lambda_{-u}(V)
in different directions. We regard (
3

\Lambda_{-u}(V)
around

Lemma 3.5
.

If
V
is a vector bundle, then

(4)

\Lambda_{-u}(V)=(-u)^{\mathrm{rk}(V)}\Lambda_{-u^{-1}}(V^{\vee})\otimes\det(V)\,

in
K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})[u]
.

Proof.

Let
r=\mathrm{rk}(V)
. This follows from the fact that

\Lambda^{n}(V)\otimes\Lambda^{r-n}(V)\to\Lambda^{r}(V)=\det(V)

defines a perfect pairing, and hence an isomorphism

\Lambda^{n}(V)\simeq\Lambda^{r-n}(V)^{\vee}\otimes\det(V)\simeq\Lambda^{r-n}(V^{\vee})\otimes\det(V)\,.\qed

Remark 3.6
.

When
V
is a vector bundle the class

\Lambda_{-1}(V^{\vee})=\sum_{n=0}^{\mathrm{rk}V}(-1)^{n}\Lambda^{n}(V^{\vee})

plays the role of the inverse Euler class of

\displaystyle\Lambda_{-u}(V)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1-uL_{i})^{\epsilon_{i}}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big((1-u^{-1}L_{i}^{\vee})^{\epsilon_{i}}(-uL_{i})^{\epsilon_{i}}\big)

\displaystyle=(-u)^{\mathrm{rk}(V)}\Lambda_{-u^{-1}}(V^{\vee})\otimes\det(V)

as rational functions in

, but the left hand side is the expansion in

The expansion around

3.1.3.
K
-theory of
B{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
and rigidifications

A quasi-coherent sheaf on
B{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
corresponds to a representation of
B{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
on a (possibly infinite dimensional) vector space
V
. Such representation is coherent if and only if it is perfect if and only if
V
is finite. The
K
-theory of
B{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
is isomorphic to

K^{\ast}(B{\mathbb{G}}_{m})\simeq R({\mathbb{G}}_{m})\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}[u^{\pm 1}]

as a ring, where
R(G)
denotes the representation algebra of a group
G
and
u
is the class of the standard representation corresponding to the weight 1 action of
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
on
{\mathbb{C}}
. The cohomology groups of a quasi-coherent sheaf
V
are given by

H^{i}(B{\mathbb{G}}_{m},V)=\begin{cases}V^{G}&\textup{ if }i=0\\
0&\textup{otherwise}\end{cases}

In particular, if
\pi\colon B{\mathbb{G}}_{m}\to{\mathsf{pt}}
then the functor
\pi_{\ast}=R\pi_{\ast}
is exact and preserves perfect complexes. The pushforward

{\mathbb{Z}}[u^{\pm 1}]\simeq K^{\ast}(B{\mathbb{G}}_{m})\xrightarrow{\pi_{\ast}}K^{\ast}({\mathsf{pt}})\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}

is identified with the operator
[u^{0}]
of extracting the constant term of a Laurent polynomial.

Consider now a (derived) stack
{\mathfrak{X}}
equipped with a
B{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
action
\Psi\colon B{\mathbb{G}}_{m}\times{\mathfrak{X}}\to{\mathfrak{X}}
. Given a complex
F
on
{\mathfrak{X}}
, the
B{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
action induces a canonical weight decomposition

F=\bigoplus_{\mu\in{\mathbb{Z}}}F_{\mu}

where

\Psi^{\ast}F=\bigoplus_{\mu\in{\mathbb{Z}}}u^{\mu}\otimes F_{\mu}\,.

A complex is said to be of weight
\mu
if
F=F_{\mu}
. The next proposition relates the
K
-theory of a stack and its rigidification (cf. Definition
2.5
).

Proposition 3.8
.

The pullback

\pi^{\ast}\colon K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}}^{\mathrm{rig}})\to K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})

is injective and its image are the weight 0 classes. Moreover, the pushforward

\pi_{\ast}\colon K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})\to K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}}^{\mathrm{rig}})

is well defined and we have

\pi^{\ast}\pi_{\ast}F=F_{0}\,.

Proof.

This follows essentially by
[
BS
]
. There, it is shown that the weight decomposition induces an equivalence of categories

(5)

D\mathrm{QCoh}({\mathfrak{X}})\simeq\prod_{\mu\in{\mathbb{Z}}}D\mathrm{QCoh}({\mathfrak{X}})_{\mu}

where
D\mathrm{QCoh}({\mathfrak{X}})_{\mu}
denotes the Serre subcategory of weight
\mu
complexes. Moreover,
[
BS
, Proposition 5.7]
shows that
\pi^{\ast}=L\pi^{\ast}
gives an equivalence

D\mathrm{QCoh}({\mathfrak{X}}^{\mathrm{rig}})\xlongrightarrow{\raisebox{-0.90417pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}D\mathrm{QCoh}({\mathfrak{X}})_{0}\,

with inverse given by
\pi_{\ast}=R\pi_{\ast}
. It is clear that the decomposition (
5
) restricts to an isomorphism

\mathsf{Perf}({\mathfrak{X}})\simeq\bigoplus_{\mu\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathsf{Perf}({\mathfrak{X}})_{\mu}\,.

From here all the statements in the proposition are clear.
∎

If
T
is a
n
-dimensional torus then these statements generalize in a straightforward way. The
K
-theory of

where

is the weight lattice of the torus. A

on any sheaf on

induces a basis on

3.2.
K
-homology

Under appropriate conditions, a moduli space
M
with a virtual structure sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}^{\mathrm{vir}}
determines an “integration” functional
K^{\ast}(M)\to{\mathbb{Z}}
by taking the Euler characteristic:

V\mapsto\chi(M,{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathrm{vir}}_{M}\otimes V)\,.

Liu defines in
[
Liu
]
a notion of
K
-homology, which roughly is the space where such functionals live. The most naive thing would be to simply define
K
-homology as the dual to
K
-theory. However, the lack of a Kunneth decomposition creates issues when trying to define some algebraic structures on
K
-homology. This is solved by working with an operational theory that forces the existence of a Kunneth morphism.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
{\mathfrak{X}}
be a derived stack. An element
\phi
in the
K
-homology group
K_{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})
is a collection of
K^{\ast}(S)
-linear maps

\{\phi_{S}\colon K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}}\times S)\to K^{\ast}(S)\}

for every derived stack
S
which satisfies the following compatibility condition: for any morphism
h\colon S\to S^{\prime}
, the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}}\times S^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}}\times S)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K^{\ast}(S^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K^{\ast}(S)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi_{S^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\id\times h)^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi_{S}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h^{\ast}}

commutes.

Remark 3.10
.

There are two more conditions in the definition of
K
-homology in
[
Liu
]
. The “equivariant localization” is irrelevant for us since we are not working equivariantly. The “finiteness condition”, on the other, is a fundamental difference and we really need to exclude it. For example, for us

K_{\ast}(B{\mathbb{G}}_{m})\simeq\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(K^{\ast}(B{\mathbb{G}}_{m}),{\mathbb{Z}})\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathbb{Z}}\,;

with the finiteness condition, the
K
-homology of
B{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}[\phi]
(cf.
[
Liu
, Proposition 2.3.5]
).
We will further discuss this finiteness condition in Section
7.3
.

Note that, by taking
S={\mathsf{pt}}
, a class in
K
-homology determines a functional
\phi_{\mathsf{pt}}\colon K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})\to{\mathbb{Z}}
. If
{\mathfrak{X}}
has the property that the Kunneth map

K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})\otimes K^{\ast}(S)\to K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}}\times S)

is an isomorphism for every
S
, then
K_{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})\simeq K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})^{\vee}
. For most purposes, it is enough to think of a
K
-homology class as just a functional, so we will often – when no subtlety arises – do so for the sake of clarity of exposition.

We have the following structures on
K
-homology:

Cap product.
There is a cap product

\cap\colon K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})\otimes K_{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})\to K_{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})

obtained from the tensor product on
K^{\ast}
. At the level of functionals,

(V\cap\phi)_{\mathsf{pt}}(W)=\phi_{\mathsf{pt}}(V\otimes W)

for
V,W\in K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})
.

Kunneth map.
For any
{\mathfrak{X}},\mathfrak{Z}
there is a Kunneth map

\boxtimes\colon K_{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})\otimes K_{\ast}(\mathfrak{Z})\to K_{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}}\times\mathfrak{Z})\,.

It is defined by setting
(\phi\boxtimes\psi)_{S}
to be the composition

K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}}\times\mathfrak{Z}\times S)\xrightarrow{\phi_{\mathfrak{Z}\times S}}K^{\ast}(\mathfrak{Z}\times S)\xrightarrow{\psi_{S}}K^{\ast}(S)\,.

Note how this definition uses the operational definition in a fundamental way.

Pushforward.
Given an arbitrary map
f\colon{\mathfrak{X}}\to\mathfrak{Z}
there is a pushforward
f_{\ast}\colon K_{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})\to K_{\ast}(\mathfrak{Z})
. At the level of functionals, this is the dual of the pullback on
K^{\ast}
.

Proper pullback.
If
f\colon{\mathfrak{X}}\to\mathfrak{Z}
is a proper, representable, finite Tor amplitude, locally finite type morphism, then there is a pullback
f^{\ast}\colon K_{\ast}(\mathfrak{Z})\to K_{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})
. At the level of functionals, this is the dual of the pushforward on
K^{\ast}
. By Proposition
3.8
we also have pullbacks in
K_{\ast}
along rigidification morphisms.

G
to
K_{\ast}
and universal invariants.
Suppose that
{\mathfrak{X}}
is Noetherian and admits a proper good moduli space. Then there is a canonical map

(6)

G({\mathfrak{X}})\to K_{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})\,.

At the level of functionals, it sends a coherent complex
C\in G({\mathfrak{X}})
to the functional

K^{\ast}({\mathfrak{X}})\ni V\mapsto\chi({\mathfrak{X}},V\cap C)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,.

The operational description requires some work. The coherent complex
C
is sent to
\phi
where

\phi_{S}(V)=(Rp_{1})_{\ast}(V\otimes p_{2}^{\ast}C)

and
p_{1},p_{2}
are the projections of
{\mathfrak{X}}\times S
onto
{\mathfrak{X}}
and
S
respectively. Using the fact that pushforward along the good moduli morphism preserves coherent complexes (cf. Proposition
3.4
), it is not hard to see that the complex above is coherent, but being actually perfect requires a proof.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let as above
{\mathfrak{X}}
be a Noetherian derived stack admitting a proper good moduli space,
V
in
\mathsf{Perf}({\mathfrak{X}}\times S)
and
C
in
D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}({\mathfrak{X}})
. Then
(Rp_{1})_{\ast}(V\otimes p_{2}^{\ast}C)
is in
\mathsf{Perf}(S)
.

Proof.

Let
\iota\colon{\mathcal{X}}={\mathfrak{X}}_{\mathrm{cl}}\to{\mathfrak{X}}
be the classical truncation. Since
D^{b}\mathrm{Coh}({\mathfrak{X}})
has a bounded
t
-structure with heart

4.
The
K
-Hall algebra and generalized

-theoretic invariants

In this section we will introduce an associative algebra structure on

is a good abelian category. This algebra is the analog of the motivic Hall algebra (or of the quantum torus) in

-Hall algebra.

As in the motivic setting, we will use this product to define generalized
K
-theoretic invariants –

\delta

\varepsilon

7

Remark 4.1
.

Let us emphasize that, despite sharing some similarities, the algebra that we introduce is not the
K
-theoretic Hall algebra from
[
Pă
]
. The
K
-theoretic Hall algebra is also an associative algebra, but the underlying vector space is

and the product is defined by a push-pull construction on the stack of extensions. Another difference is that the

-theoretic Hall algebra requires some extra condition, such as

being quasi-smooth or

-shifted symplectic, while ours does not. We hope that the distinction “

-Hall algebra” versus “

-theoretic Hall algebra” will be enough not to confuse the reader. It would also be interesting to understand the relation between the two constructions when
{\mathcal{A}}
has homological dimension

4.2.

In analogy with the generalized motivic Donaldson–Thomas invariants, we use the

Definition 4.8

Let

be a good abelian category, let

the pushforward in

We then define the generalized

-theoretic invariants

\mu(\alpha_{i})=\mu(\alpha)\end{subarray}}\delta^{\mu}_{\alpha_{1}}\ast\ldots\ast\delta^{\mu}_{\alpha_{k}}\,.

are also permissible, so that

is defined. If there is no non-trivial decomposition

with

is stable and hence

is the good moduli space of

-theoretic invariants of the good moduli space.

\alpha
(i.e.,
{\mathcal{A}}
has homological dimension
\leq 2
), then we might take the trivial stability condition in Definition
4.8
and obtain invariants
\delta_{\alpha},\varepsilon_{\alpha}
which are intrinsically associated to the abelian category
{\mathcal{A}}
. If
\mu
is a stability condition and for some
s\in T
all classes with fixed slope
\mu(\alpha)=s
are permissible, then we might think of the invariants
\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu},\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu}
as being intrinsically attached to the exact subcategory
{\mathcal{A}}^{\mu}_{s}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
of
\mu
semistable objects with fixed slope
s
.

We will show later that, in general, the classes
\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu}
are precisely the
K
-theoretic Joyce–Liu classes, denoted by
Z_{\alpha}(\mu)
in
[
Liu
]
, when the latter are defined. Note that the definition of Joyce–Liu classes is only available when there is a framing functor, and it is a hard theorem that they are actually independent of the choice of framing functor (cf.
[
Joy7
, Proposition 9.12]
and
[
Liu
, Theorem 4.2.5]
). On the other hand, in our approach we do not need a framing functor, which extends the definition to new cases – for example, moduli spaces of (weak) Bridgeland stable objects on Fano surfaces or 3-folds – and makes the independence of the framing functor obvious. We see this as one of the strengths of our new approach. For example, with our definition the following fact is basically trivial:

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
B\colon{\mathcal{A}}\xlongrightarrow{\raisebox{-0.90417pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
be an isomorphism of abelian categories and let
\mu
be a stability condition on
{\mathcal{A}}
as in Assumption
2.11
. Let
B^{\ast}\mu
be the stability condition on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
defined by
B^{\ast}\mu(\alpha^{\prime})=\mu(B^{-1}(\alpha^{\prime}))
. Then

B_{\ast}\delta_{\alpha}^{\mu}=\delta_{B(\alpha)}^{B^{\ast}\mu}\quad\textup{ and }\quad B_{\ast}\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{\mu}=\varepsilon_{B(\alpha)}^{B^{\ast}\mu}\,.

Proof.

The equality for the
\delta
classes is trivial once we notice that
B(E)
is
B^{\ast}\mu
semistable if and only if
E
is
\mu
semistable. Then the equality for
\varepsilon
classes follows from the definition and the observation that
B_{\ast}\colon{\mathbb{K}}({\mathcal{A}})\to{\mathbb{K}}({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})
is an algebra homomorphism.
∎

A typical scenario where these type of results might be useful is when
B
is an automorphism of a derived category,
{\mathcal{A}}
is the heart of a
t
-structure and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
the image of
{\mathcal{A}}
by
B
. In the Calabi–Yau setting, automorphisms of derived categories have often been used to constrain enumerative invariants, see for example
[
Tod3
,
OS
,
BM
,
Tho
]
.

In contrast with the trivial proof given above, in Joyce’s setting these results might be quite subtle. Since the definition of invariants depends on a framing functor, equalities as in Proposition
4.10
are only immediate if we choose compatible framing functors on both sides. So a general statement requires the hard result that the choice of framing functor is actually not important; that requires
[
Joy7
, Assumptions 5.1(g), 5.2(h)]
, which could potentially be difficult to verify in examples.

4.3.
Example:
\mathsf{Vect}

We consider now the simplest possible abelian category, the category of vector spaces
{\mathcal{A}}=\mathsf{Vect}
. The stack of vector spaces is

{\mathfrak{M}}_{\mathsf{Vect}}=\bigsqcup_{n\geq 0}B{\rm GL}_{n}

where
n\in C(\mathsf{Vect})={\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
corresponds to the dimension of the vector space. Its rigidification is

{\mathfrak{M}}_{\mathsf{Vect}}^{\mathrm{rig}}=\bigsqcup_{n\geq 0}B{\rm PGL}_{n}\,.

Note that
K^{\ast}(B{\rm PGL}_{n})
is the representation ring of
{\rm PGL}_{n}
, and
K_{\ast}(B{\rm PGL}_{n})
is the dual to the representation ring. Given a representation
V
of
{\rm PGL}_{n}
, the Euler characteristic of the corresponding vector bundle on
B{\rm PGL}_{n}
is

\chi(B{\rm PGL}_{n},V)=\dim(V^{{\rm PGL}_{n}})\,.

This follows from the fact that representations of
{\rm PGL}_{n}
are completely reducible and Whitehead’s lemma, which states that
H^{i}({\rm PGL}_{n},V)=0
for any
i
and any non-trivial irreducible representation
V
. So the functional
\delta_{n}
is the functional that sends a representation to the dimension of its fixed part or, in other words, to the multiplicity of the trivial representation in its decomposition into irreducible representations. For
n=1
the group
{\rm PGL}_{1}
is trivial and
\varepsilon_{1}=\delta_{1}
is the isomorphism
K^{\ast}(B{\rm PGL}_{1})\xlongrightarrow{\raisebox{-0.90417pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mathbb{Z}}
sending
V
to
\dim(V)
.

In the following proposition we show that
\varepsilon_{n}=0
for
n>1
. Note that in Joyce’s
[
Joy7
]
approach to cohomological invariants the analogous statement is trivial since the class he defines has negative homological degree
2(1-n^{2})
. However, from our perspective this is a non-trivial statement. One way to prove it is via the connection to the homological invariants which we will show later in Theorem
8.8
. We present here a direct proof using the Weyl character formula.

Proposition 4.11
.

If
{\mathcal{A}}=\mathsf{Vect}
then
\varepsilon_{n}=0
for
n>1
. In other words, we have

\dim(V^{{\rm PGL}_{n}})=\frac{1}{n!}(\underbrace{\varepsilon_{1}\ast\ldots\ast\varepsilon_{1}}_{n\textup{ times}})(V)

for every representation
V
of
{\rm PGL}_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\rho^{\ast}V
be the induced representation of
{\rm GL}_{n}
for which the diagonal
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
acts trivially. Let
T=(B{\mathbb{G}}_{m})^{\times n}
be the maximal torus of
{\rm GL}_{n}
and let

\Sigma_{[n]}\colon{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}^{\times n}=BT\to B{\rm GL}_{n}={\mathfrak{M}}_{n}

be the induced map. By Proposition
4.7
and the discussion in Section
4.1
we have

(\varepsilon_{1}\ast\ldots\ast\varepsilon_{1})(V)=[u_{1}^{0}\ldots u_{n}^{0}]\big(\Gamma_{[n],-}\otimes\Sigma^{\ast}_{[n]}\rho^{\ast}V\big)

where

\Gamma_{[n],-}=\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}\left(1-\frac{u_{i}}{u_{j}}\right)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[u_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,u_{n}^{\pm 1}]\simeq K^{\ast}(BT)\,;

note that
\textup{Ext}_{<}
is
\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}u_{j}/u_{i}
and similarly for
\textup{Ext}_{>}
, and recall Remark
4.5
. Note also that

\Sigma^{\ast}\rho^{\ast}V=\sum_{\mu\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}\dim(V_{\mu})u^{\mu}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[u_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,u_{n}^{\pm 1}]

is the character of the representation
V
. Recall that the root system of
{\mathfrak{sl}}_{n}
is
\Delta=\{e_{i}-e_{j}\}_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}
, where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
is the standard basis of
{\mathfrak{t}}^{\vee}
. Therefore, we can rewrite
\Gamma_{[n],-}
more conceptually as

\Gamma_{[n],-}=\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}(u^{\alpha/2}-u^{-\alpha/2})\,.

Note that this is almost the Weyl denominator, except that we take the product over all roots and not just the positive ones. The proof is now concluded using the following lemma for general semisimple Lie algebras.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}
be a semisimple Lie algebra and
L
an irreducible representation of
{\mathfrak{g}}
. Then we have

[u^{0}]\left(\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}(u^{\alpha/2}-u^{-\alpha/2})\cdot\mathrm{ch}(L)\right)=\begin{cases}|W|&\textup{ if }L\textup{ is the trivial representation}\\
0&\textup{ otherwise}\end{cases}

where
\Delta\subseteq{\mathfrak{t}}^{\vee}
is the root system of
{\mathfrak{g}}
,
W
the Weyl group, and
\mathrm{ch}(L)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[{\mathfrak{t}}^{\vee}]
is the character of
L
.

Proof.

Choose a set of positive roots
\Delta_{+}
and let
\Delta_{-}=\Delta\setminus\Delta_{+}
be the negative roots. Let
\lambda
be the highest weight vector of
L
, which is integral and dominant. We recall the Weyl character formula:

\mathrm{ch}(L)=\frac{\sum_{\sigma\in W}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)u^{\sigma(\lambda+\rho)}}{\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\big(u^{\alpha/2}-u^{-\alpha/2}\big)}

where
\rho=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta_{+}}\alpha
is the Weyl vector. Using this and the Weyl denominator formula for the negative roots, we have

\displaystyle\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta}(u^{\alpha/2}-u^{-\alpha/2})\cdot\mathrm{ch}(L)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{-}}(u^{\alpha/2}-u^{-\alpha/2})\left(\sum_{\sigma\in W}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)u^{\sigma(\lambda+\rho)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in W}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma^{\prime})u^{-\sigma^{\prime}(\rho)}\right)\left(\sum_{\sigma\in W}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)u^{\sigma(\lambda+\rho)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in W}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma\sigma^{\prime})u^{\sigma(\lambda+\rho)-\sigma^{\prime}(\rho)}\,.

If
\lambda=0
then the terms with
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}
are precisely the ones contributing to the constant coefficient, which shows what we wanted. We now argue that the constant coefficient is equal to 0 for
\lambda\neq 0
by showing that there are no
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
such that
\sigma(\lambda)=\sigma^{\prime}(\rho)-\sigma(\rho)
; without loss of generality we may assume that
\sigma^{\prime}=\id
, so suppose that
\sigma(\lambda)=\rho-\sigma(\rho)
. Since
\lambda
is dominant we have

0\leq\langle\rho,\lambda\rangle=\langle\sigma(\rho),\sigma(\lambda)\rangle=\langle\sigma(\rho),\rho\rangle-\lvert\lvert\sigma(\rho)\rvert\rvert^{2}\leq 0

where the last inequality follows from Cauchy-Schwarz, and we have used twice that
\sigma
is an isometry. Hence
\langle\rho,\lambda\rangle=0
, which implies that
\langle\alpha,\lambda\rangle=0
for every positive root
\alpha
and therefore
\lambda=0
.∎

.

Our
K
-Hall algebra admits a generalization to arbitrary stacks in the spirit of “intrinsic Donaldson–Thomas theory” from
[

BNK

Example 4.14
.

In
[

, the author introduces a slightly different notion of

and the discussion that follows it. Let us check that

5.1.

The non-abelian localization is stated in the language of derived

We have the following canonical maps of derived stacks:

Definition 5.1

Definition 5.2

(

.

Let

(1)

From

-theory to cohomology

So far, our approach to generalized invariants and wall-crossing has been entirely in the realm of
