Gut microbiota-derived riboflavin mediates protective effects of MUC2 in acute pancreatitis via CD40-macrophage pathway
Authors: Yang Fu, Nuoming Yin, Zehua Huang, Wenfei Qin, Binqiang Xu, Huizhen Huang, Jia Hu, Mengyan Cui, Qixiang Mei, Junjie Fan, Yingying Lu, Qiaoli Jiang, Junjie Hu, Chunlan Huang, Yue Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGut barrier dysfunction is critical in the pathogenesis of acute pancreatitis (AP), yet the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Mucin 2 (MUC2), the primary component of the intestinal mucus layer, is essential for gut homeostasis and microbial eubiosis. This study aimed to elucidate the protective mechanism of intestinal MUC2 in AP.ResultsWe reported that intestinal MUC2 expression was decreased in AP patients and correlated with disease severity. Intestinal epithelial-specific Muc2 knockout (Muc2ΔIEC) mice demonstrated exacerbated AP in a gut microbiota-dependent manner. Decreased abundance of Lactobacillus and correlated reduced levels of riboflavin were found in Muc2ΔIEC mice after AP induction. Supplementation with riboflavin effectively ameliorated AP in Muc2ΔIEC mice, whereas administration of an engineered strain deficient in riboflavin synthesis failed to provide a protective effect. MUC2 deficiency aggravated a macrophage-dominant immune imbalance in the pancreas, which was reversed by riboflavin. In vitro experiments revealed that riboflavin suppresses pro-inflammatory macrophage activation by inhibiting the CD40 signaling pathway. This inhibition preserved mitochondrial function and reversed the histone H3 acetylation at pro-inflammatory gene promoters.ConclusionOur study demonstrates that intestinal MUC2 deficiency is associated with reduced levels of microbiota-derived riboflavin, which could partially suppress pro-inflammatory macrophage activation via the CD40 pathway. Targeting the microbiota-derived riboflavin in AP may help to ameliorate the disease course. Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is an acute inflammatory disorder of the pancreas, initiated by premature trypsinogen activation within the acinar cells [1]. The disease is characterized by a local and systemic inflammatory response and has a varying clinical course [2]. Although most patients present with self-limiting mild AP, approximately 20% of AP patients develop moderate or severe AP, which is associated with systemic complications and increased mortality [3]. The gut is one of the target organs in systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) during AP [4]. Nearly 60% of AP patients develop gut barrier dysfunction, leading to bacterial translocation and infected pancreatic necrosis [4, 5].

Gut barrier dysfunction in AP includes epithelial barrier disruption, gut dysbiosis, and intestinal immune dysfunction. Accumulating evidence suggests that gut dysbiosis, characterized by increased opportunistic pathogens (Escherichia shigella, Enterococcus, etc.) and decreased probiotics (Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, etc.) [6–11] and beneficial metabolites (short-chain fatty acids and tryptophan derivatives) [10, 12], contributes to AP progression. Furthermore, neutrophil and macrophage recruitment participate in intestinal immune dysfunction and oxidative stress and NF-κB signaling activated [13, 14]. A recent study implicates that activated intestinal Tregs might also promote bacterial translocation into necrotic areas [15].

MUC2, secreted by goblet cells, is an integral component of the epithelial barrier and exhibits protective functions in intestinal homeostasis [16]. MUC2 builds the mucus layer, serving as a selective niche that supports the colonization of O-glycans-dependent commensals (Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii) and separating pathogens from the intestinal epithelium [16]. Disruption of the mucus barrier integrity facilitates increased colonization of pathogenic bacteria, leading to gut dysbiosis, infection and enhanced inflammation [17, 18]. Reduced MUC2 expression also triggers excessive immune activation, marked by enhanced recruitment of innate immune cells such as macrophages, altered dendritic cell subpopulations, and elevated production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which exacerbate epithelial barrier disruption [19]. Muc2 knockout models showed increased susceptibility to intestinal inflammation, spontaneous colitis, and colon cancer, underscoring its critical protective role [20, 21]. Our previous research demonstrated that pilocarpine-induced MUC2 depletion exacerbated AP severity and gut barrier dysfunction in rats [22]. However, the potential mechanisms remain unclear.

This study aims to investigate mechanisms through which MUC2 exerts its protective effects against AP. Intestinal-specific Muc2 knockout mice and AP experimental models were established. Multi-omics analysis, 16S rRNA sequencing, untargeted metabolomics, and transcriptomics, combined with engineered Lactobacillus plantarum lacking Rib gene, were implemented to identify core regulatory mediators, construct molecular interaction networks, and validate mechanistic determinants in the pathogenesis of AP.

Result

Decreased intestinal MUC2 is involved in exacerbated pancreatitis and related intestinal injury

A cohort including AP patients with different etiologies and healthy controls was selected to detect intestinal MUC2. Characteristic profiles (age, sex, and body mass index (BMI)) showed no statistical differences between the groups (Table S1). Number of goblet cells and expression of MUC2 decreased significantly in AP patients by periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining and immunofluorescence (Fig. 1A, B). Meanwhile, three typical mouse models of AP (cerulein-induced AP (Cae-AP), L-arginine-induced AP (Larg-AP) and sodium taurocholate-induced AP (NaTCA-AP)) also demonstrated decreased MUC2 levels in the colon (Fig. S1A, B). MUC2 expression presented a strong negative correlation with pancreatic injury, serum amylase activity, bacterial translocation, apoptosis in acinar and colonic epithelial cells and pro-inflammatory cytokines (interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα), and IL-6) in serum, pancreas and colon (Fig. S1C). Colonic tight junction proteins claudin-1 and occludin had strong positive correlations with MUC2 expression (Fig. S1C).

To further explore the role of intestinal epithelial-derived MUC2 in AP and eliminate potential effects from its expression in extra-intestinal tissues, intestinal specific Muc2 knockout (Muc2ΔIEC) mice were applied (Fig. S2A-C). Muc2ΔIEC exacerbated pancreatic injury in the AP model induced by cerulein, showing marked acinar cell necrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, and tissue edema (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, serum amylase activity significantly increased (Fig. 1D) accompanied by evident pancreatic apoptosis (Fig. 1E) and elevated inflammatory factors (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6) in the pancreas and serum (Fig. 1F, G).

Evaluation of intestinal barrier showed that Muc2ΔIEC in AP disrupted epithelial integrity, leading to severer inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 2A) and colonic epithelial cell apoptosis (Fig. 2B). Claudin-1 and occludin expression also significantly decreased (Fig. 2C), and intestinal inflammatory factors and intestinal permeability increased (Fig. 2D, E). Via fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) by EUB338 probe, bacteria translocation to pancreas and intestinal epithelium increased while the same tendency was detected in colony-forming units (CFUs) in mesenteric lymph nodes and liver (Fig. 2F, G). The above phenomena were confirmed in Muc2ΔIEC mice with L-arginine-induced AP as well (Fig. S3A-F).

Gut microbiota-derived riboflavin ameliorates AP in Muc2ΔIEC mice

MUC2 is a major component of mucus layer organized by an inner colon mucus layer impenetrable to bacteria and an outer mucus layer that is expanded to allow microbiota colonization [23]. In view of microbial eubiosis being crucial in AP pathogenesis [24], we explored changes of gut microbiota related to MUC2 deficiency. Severer pancreatic and colonic histopathological injuries were presented in Muc2ΔIEC + AP mice when transplanted with autologous feces than those from wild type (WT) donors (Fig. S4A-D), indicating MUC2 deficiency-associated gut microbiota is involved in AP aggravation. Then, we characterized gut microbial communities through 16S rRNA sequencing in Muc2ΔIEC mice. After AP induction, Muc2ΔIEC mice demonstrated reduced α-diversity than WT mice, and β-diversity plots revealed different clustering (Fig. S5A-B). Alterations in microbes were assessed according to the top 20 bacterial communities listed by relative abundance of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Muc2ΔIEC remodeled gut microbiota by increasing Clostridia_UCG-014 NR, Alistipes, Turicibacter Bacteroides, Rhodospirillales NR and Zag_111, and decreasing Lactobacillus (Fig. 3A). After AP induction, the Muc2ΔIEC mice demonstrated similar dysbiosis, showing increase of Clostridia_UCG-014 NR, Alistipes, Turicibacter and Parabacteroides, and decrease of Lactobacillus and Bradyrhizobium (Fig. 3B).

We next leveraged on fecal non-targeted metabolomics to measure microbiota-derived metabolites. Four hundred fifty-six metabolites were upregulated and 909 downregulated in Muc2ΔIEC mice compared to WT mice, 430 metabolites were upregulated and 828 downregulated in Muc2ΔIEC + AP mice compared to AP mice, and 223 metabolites were upregulated and 491 downregulated in AP mice compared to WT mice (Fig. S5D-F). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that fecal differential metabolites in different groups (Muc2ΔIEC mice vs. WT mice, Muc2ΔIEC + AP mice vs. AP mice and AP mice vs. WT mice) were mainly involved in riboflavin metabolism with the highest impact value (Fig. 3C, D and Fig. S5G). We also identified the top 20 differential metabolites based on variable importance in projection (VIP) scores in orthogonal projection to latent structure-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) models (Fig. 3E, F and Fig. S5H). Riboflavin and its derivative flavin mononucleotide (FMN) were significantly decreased in AP mice compared with WT mice, and were further reduced in the feces of Muc2ΔIEC + AP mice (Fig. 3G). Notably, the metabolic changes were not limited to feces. Targeted metabolomics also revealed a consistent decrease in pancreatic riboflavin and its derivative flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) in AP mice compared with WT mice with a further reduction in Muc2ΔIEC + AP mice (Fig. 3H). Given that riboflavin cannot be synthesized endogenously and is only derived from microbial synthesis and dietary intake [25], we explored the specific microbe related to riboflavin reduction due to MUC2 deficiency after AP induction. Based on the Spearman correlation coefficients, a network diagram was constructed to correlate different genera and metabolites. The abundance of Lactobacillus was positively correlated with the levels of metabolites in riboflavin metabolism (Fig. 3I). Intriguingly, Lactobacillus includes a number of microbial riboflavin-producing strains [26]. Remarkably, a stepwise decrease in Lactobacillus abundance was observed, with the highest level in WT mice, followed by AP mice and the lowest level in Muc2ΔIEC + AP mice (Fig. S5I). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis further confirmed significant depletion of Lactobacillus in the Muc2ΔIEC + AP group (Fig. S5J, K). Consistent with our findings above, Muc2ΔIEC mice exhibited a significantly reduced abundance of the riboflavin-producing bacterium Lactobacillus, alongside a significant decrease in fecal and pancreatic riboflavin levels. These data suggest that intestinal MUC2 deficiency leads to a reduction in gut microbiota-derived riboflavin, which might be associated with AP severity.

We further utilized a Lactobacillus-engineered strain deficient in riboflavin synthesis in our study to confirm the source of riboflavin. In consideration of Lactobacillus plantarum abundance being most significantly decreased in Muc2ΔIEC mice (Fig. S6A), we constructed L. plantarum AR113ΔRib, whose riboflavin biosynthesis was deficient due to deleting the gene cluster (ribBA, ribD, ribH, and ribE) (Fig. 4A). Compared to wild type L. plantarum AR113, the culture supernatant of L. plantarum AR113ΔRib had lower riboflavin levels by targeted metabolomic analysis (Fig. S6B). Wild type L. plantarum AR113 or L. plantarum AR113ΔRib increased significantly after 3 weeks of gavage and remained high through AP induction (Fig. S6C). Moreover, L. plantarum AR113ΔRib lost the protective capacity, resulting in severe pancreatic and colonic histopathological damage (Fig. 4B–D), systemic and colonic inflammation (Fig. 4E, F), reduced tight-junction protein expression, and increased bacterial translocation in Muc2ΔIEC + AP mice (Fig. 4G, H) compared to wild type L. plantarum AR113.

Riboflavin supplementation modulates pancreatic and colonic macrophage infiltration in AP

The immune response to pancreatic injuries in AP involves the rapid and substantial infiltration of innate immune cells, primarily monocytes/macrophages and neutrophils, into the pancreas [13]. These activated innate cells amplify local tissue damage by releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines and forming neutrophil extracellular traps, followed by recruitment of adaptive immunity (Th17 and Treg) [13]. In our study, we also found AP induction elicited a significant innate immune response, characterized by the infiltration of pro-inflammatory macrophages and neutrophils, which was further confirmed by flow cytometry showing a significant expansion of F4/80+CD86+ pro-inflammatory macrophages and CD11b+Ly6G+ neutrophils compared to WT mice (Fig. 6A–D). This pro-inflammatory profile was further augmented in Muc2ΔIEC + AP mice, evidenced by a more substantial expansion of pro-inflammatory macrophages and neutrophils compared to AP mice (Fig. 6A–D), along with a notable reduction in F4/80+CD206+ anti-inflammatory macrophages (Fig. 6C). Besides, we also evaluated the adaptive immune responses, including Th17 cells and Tregs in AP (Fig. 6E, F). Among these immune response, pro-inflammatory macrophage activation emerged as the dominant feature in Muc2ΔIEC + AP mice (Fig. 6A–F).

Subsequently, we explored the influence of riboflavin supplementation on macrophage in vivo. Consistent with previous findings on its anti-inflammatory properties [27], intraperitoneal injection of riboflavin significantly reduced F4/80+NOS2+ pro-inflammatory macrophage infiltration in the pancreas of Muc2ΔIEC + AP mice (Fig. 6G). A similar pattern of immune dysregulation was also observed in the colon, where riboflavin supplementation exerted a comparable suppressive effect on pro-inflammatory macrophages, highlighting its systemic anti-inflammatory potential (Fig. S8A-G).

RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to induce the inflammatory microenvironment experienced by macrophages. The riboflavin treatment at different concentrations inhibited LPS-induced Nos2 mRNA upregulation, with 25 μg/mL identified as the most effective concentration (Fig. 6H). This concentration notably decreased the mRNA levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, as well as the protein level of NOS2, confirming the direct suppression of pro-inflammatory macrophage activation in vitro (Fig. 6I, J).

Riboflavin inhibits pro-inflammatory macrophage activation mediated by CD40-related mitochondrial dysfunction in AP

To investigate the mechanism by which riboflavin inhibits the pro-inflammatory phenotype of macrophages, we performed transcriptomic sequencing. LPS stimulation induced broad transcriptional changes in RAW264.7 cells, with 1721 genes upregulated and 1525 genes downregulated (Fig. 7A). Riboflavin intervention counteracted part of these changes, resulting in 106 upregulated and 185 downregulated genes (Fig. 7B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in both comparisons (LPS vs. CON and Riboflavin (RF) + LPS vs. LPS) were primarily enriched in inflammatory pathways, such as TNF signaling, NF-κB signaling, and lipid metabolism pathways (Fig. 7C, D). A Venn diagram showed 187 overlapping DEGs (Fig. S8A). KEGG pathway and combined intersection analyses identified CD40, a member of lipid metabolism pathways, as a potential hub gene. LPS stimulation significantly increased CD40 expression, which was reversed by riboflavin intervention (Fig. 7E and S8B-C). To further confirm that CD40 was a key mediator of riboflavin’s effect, we treated LPS-stimulated macrophages with a CD40 agonist, FGK45. As expected, when CD40 was artificially activated by FGK45, the suppressive effect of riboflavin on pro-inflammatory genes (Nos2, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6) was significantly weakened (Fig. 7F).

Having identified CD40 as a key mediator of riboflavin’s anti-inflammatory effect, we next sought to determine which of riboflavin’s core biochemical functions were responsible for this modulation. In the cytoplasm, riboflavin undergoes a two-step enzymatic conversion into its bioactive coenzyme forms, FMN and FAD, which participate in oxidative stress, mitochondrial energy metabolism, and epigenetics. The FAD-dependent glutathione reductase (GR) converts oxidized glutathione (GSSG) to reduced glutathione (GSH) to neutralize reactive oxygen species [28]. We found that LPS stimulation of macrophages induced significant oxidative stress, evidenced by decreased GR activity and a lower GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. S8D-E). In epigenetics, FAD-dependent lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1) could remove histone H3 methylation marks [29]. Our results showed that LPS suppressed LSD1 expression, leading to an increase in its target activating histone mark H3K4me (Fig. S8F-G). In mitochondrial metabolism, FMN-dependent complex I (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH): ubiquinone oxidoreductase) initiates the electron transport chain (ETC), while FAD-dependent complex II (succinate dehydrogenase, SDH) transfers electrons to ubiquinone [30]. We observed that high glycolytic rates induced by LPS in macrophages resulted in a decreased NAD+/NADH ratio and reduced SDH activity (Fig. S8H-I). Upon riboflavin intervention, we observed no significant restoration of GR activity, GSH/GSSG ratio, or LSD1 and H3K4me expression (Fig. S8D-G). However, riboflavin treatment significantly restored the activities of the key ETC enzymes, mitochondrial complex I and complex II (Fig. S8H-I). These results suggest that the primary anti-inflammatory effect of riboflavin appears to be mediated through the restoration of mitochondrial ETC function.

Mitochondria are critical for regulating the activation, differentiation, and survival of macrophages and other immune cells [31]. It has been reported that mitochondrial dysfunction is involved in the CD40-related pro-inflammatory macrophage activation [32]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the modulation effect of riboflavin on macrophages was mediated by influencing mitochondrial metabolism. Mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) and intracellular calcium concentration were measured to evaluate the macrophage mitochondrial function. A high Δψm is essential for maintaining mitochondrial ATP production, and its loss is a key indicator of cellular stress and damage. JC-1 staining, where a shift from red to green fluorescence, indicated a significant loss of Δψm after LPS stimulation (Fig. 7G). Fluo-4 AM probe was applied to measure intracellular calcium concentration. LPS treatment led to significant calcium overload in macrophages (Fig. 7H). We further confirmed that mitochondrial dysfunction was ameliorated by riboflavin intervention (Fig. 7G, H).

Mitochondrial metabolism is intrinsically linked to the epigenetic landscape, as mitochondrial metabolites such as acetyl-CoA serve as essential substrates and cofactors for histone acetylation [32], thereby influencing gene expression. We then investigated whether riboflavin affects these downstream epigenetic events. Western blot showed that LPS stimulation significantly increased histone H3 acetylation (AcH3), which was reversed by riboflavin treatment (Fig. 7I). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays demonstrated that LPS induced a significant enrichment of AcH3 at the promoters of the pro-inflammatory genes IL-6 and IL-12, which were related to the activation of pro-inflammatory macrophages. This enrichment was significantly reduced by riboflavin intervention (Fig. 7J).

Collectively, these results suggest that gut microbiota-derived riboflavin participates in the protective role of MUC2 during AP by suppressing the activation of pro-inflammatory macrophages. This effect appears to be mediated through the inhibition of the CD40 signaling pathway, which in turn preserves mitochondrial function and modulates the epigenetic landscape to limit pro-inflammatory gene expression.

Reduced intestinal MUC2 expression and serum riboflavin level are negatively correlated with severity in AP patients

To understand the impact of decreased intestinal MUC2 expression and serum riboflavin concentrations on AP severity, we analyzed the data in the initial patient cohort. Spearman correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between intestinal MUC2 expression and serum riboflavin concentration in AP patients (r = 0.91, p < 0.001; Fig. 8A). Consistent with the intestinal MUC2 expression levels, the serum riboflavin concentrations were also significantly lower in patients with severe AP (SAP) compared to those with mild AP (MAP)/moderately severe AP (MSAP) (Fig. 8B, C). Both MUC2 expression and serum riboflavin concentration were negatively correlated with disease severity, as measured by the Ranson score (r = −0.89, p < 0.001 and r = −0.82, p < 0.001, respectively; Fig. 8D, E).

To further confirm the clinical relevance of these findings, a separate validation cohort consisting of 32 AP patients was organized and the serum riboflavin concentrations were detected. Characteristic profiles (age, sex, and BMI) and clinical data are shown in Table S2. We also observed a strong negative correlation between serum riboflavin concentration and the Ranson score (r = −0.94, p < 0.01; Fig. 8F), reinforcing the link between low riboflavin level and increased disease severity.

Discussion

AP is an acute inflammatory disease which could progress to systemic inflammatory response and organ dysfunction [33]. Intestinal barrier dysfunction is considered the core “engine” driving the severity of AP [34], yet the specific pathways connecting intestine and pancreas remain to be elucidated. Our study reveals that intestinal MUC2 acts as a protective factor in AP, at least in part, by attenuating pancreatic pro-inflammatory macrophage activation via gut microbiota-derived riboflavin.

AP triggers systemic inflammatory response and microcirculatory disturbances, including splanchnic hypoperfusion and ischemia/reperfusion injury, which can disrupt the intestinal epithelium [13, 34]. We confirmed a significant reduction of intestinal MUC2, the major component of mucus, in both AP patients and experimental AP mice. Impaired intestinal barrier integrity leads to gut dysbiosis, dysregulated microbial metabolism, and bacterial translocation, thereby acting as a second hit that exacerbates AP severity [6, 7]. Using the Muc2ΔIEC mouse model, we demonstrated that the absence of MUC2 aggravated pancreatic and intestinal injuries and accelerated AP progression. While targeting intestinal MUC2 cannot prevent AP, preserving the mucus barrier may help to prevent progression to severe forms [22]. Collectively, these observations support a bidirectional, self-amplifying loop between AP and gut mucus dysfunction.

AP is often accompanied by intestinal dysbiosis, leading to potential pathogens translocation into pancreatic necrosis, which is a major driver of organ failure and mortality [15, 35]. FMT experiments confirmed that altered gut microbiota was causally linked to the exacerbation of AP in Muc2ΔIEC mice, and 16S rRNA sequencing identified a significant decrease in probiotics such as Lactobacillus. This finding indicates that the function of MUC2 extends far beyond a physical barrier in AP. The O-glycan chains abundant in MUC2 provide unique attachment sites and nutrient sources for specific commensal bacteria, such as Lactobacillus, acting as an intestinal gardener that actively cultivates and maintains a beneficial microbial ecosystem [16, 36]. This may explain why the absence of MUC2 leads to dysbiosis characterized by a reduction in Lactobacillus.

The beneficial role of microbial metabolites in AP has been increasingly recognized, with studies demonstrating the protective effects of metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids, indole derivatives, and bile acids [10, 12, 37, 38]. In this study, non-targeted and targeted metabolomics revealed that in Muc2ΔIEC + AP mice, the levels of riboflavin and its derivatives, FMN and FAD, were consistently decreased in feces and pancreas, and showed a significant positive correlation with the abundance of Lactobacillus. Riboflavin supplementation alleviated the severity of AP, whereas administration of a genetically engineered Lactobacillus strain incapable of synthesizing riboflavin (L. plantarum AR113ΔRib) exacerbated the disease. These results provide a coherent line of evidence supporting that gut microbiota-derived riboflavin is a major contributory metabolite regulated by MUC2. Our untargeted metabolomics also revealed a significant reduction of other metabolites like dihydroorotic acid and ureidosuccinic acid in Muc2ΔIEC + AP mice. Although we did not observe distinct associations between these metabolites and microbial alterations in correlation network analysis, the altered metabolites suggest that the protective effects of MUC2 likely involve multiple factors.

Immune imbalance, particularly proinflammatory macrophage activation, is a central element driving pancreatic damage and systemic inflammation in AP [13]. Our study found a significant increase in F4/80+CD86+ macrophages in the pancreas and intestine of Muc2ΔIEC + AP mice, which was reversed by riboflavin supplementation. Further in vitro experiments and transcriptomic analysis showed that riboflavin could inhibit LPS-induced proinflammatory macrophages, and CD40 was identified as the key regulatory gene. CD40, a member of the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily, is a critical signal for driving inflammatory responses upon its activation on antigen-presenting cells like macrophages [39]. Our data showed that LPS upregulated CD40 expression in RAW264.7 macrophages, which was significantly inhibited by riboflavin. Through experiments with a CD40 agonist, we confirmed that when the CD40 pathway was artificially forced into activation, the anti-inflammatory effect of riboflavin was significantly weakened, providing evidence that CD40 is a key node for riboflavin’s action. A recent study indicated that CD40 signaling can promote proinflammatory macrophages by reprogramming mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation in macrophages, where the resulting acetyl-CoA serves as a key substrate for histone acetylation at the promoter regions of pro-inflammatory genes, thus epigenetically unlocking their expression [32]. We also found that LPS treatment led to mitochondrial dysfunction in macrophages and increased AcH3 levels at the promoters of pro-inflammatory genes (IL-6 and IL-12), and riboflavin intervention could reverse both phenomena simultaneously. Riboflavin is a precursor to the essential mitochondrial electron transport chain coenzymes FMN and FAD. The deficiency of riboflavin can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress [40]. We proposed a model where riboflavin deficiency in AP impaired macrophage mitochondria. When inflammatory signals (DAMPs/PAMPs) subsequently activate the CD40 pathway to drive metabolic reprogramming, mitochondria collapse and inflammation is amplified. The role of riboflavin is to ensure basic mitochondrial function and stability and prevent the production of acetyl-CoA for histone acetylation and thus cut off the pro-inflammatory gene expression. This series of findings links gut metabolites, proinflammatory macrophages, mitochondrial metabolism, and epigenetic regulation, offering new insight into the immunopathology of AP.

In conclusion, our study delineates a novel protective role of intestinal MUC2 in AP, mediated by gut microbiota-derived riboflavin to suppress pro-inflammatory macrophage activation via the CD40 pathway. This discovery provides new molecular insights into AP pathophysiology and identifies several promising therapeutic targets. Randomized controlled trials are warranted to evaluate the safety and efficacy of riboflavin for the treatment of AP. Developing engineered live biotherapeutics with riboflavin-overexpressing bacteria offers a next-generation strategy for precision medicine. CD40 antagonists currently in clinical trials for Crohn’s disease may also hold therapeutic potential for AP [41, 42].

In this study, some limitations warrant consideration. The upstream mechanisms driving MUC2 downregulation in our animal models remain to be elucidated. Besides, the genetic validation for the role of CD40 in this pathway, using macrophage-specific CD40 knockout mice, is still required.

Materials and methods

Subjects and sample collection

To investigate alterations of intestinal MUC2 expression, we established an exploratory cohort comprising AP patients with different etiologies. AP patients were diagnosed by revised Atlanta classification [43] and categorized into three groups, acute biliary pancreatitis (ABP), hypertriglyceridemic AP (HTGAP) and alcoholic AP [44]. Biopsy specimens in descending duodenum were collected from AP patients who had clinical indications for endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) or nasojejunal tube placement for enteral nutrition. Healthy control (HC) subjects were recruited from individuals undergoing routine physical examinations. Exclusion criteria comprised chronic pancreatitis, inflammatory bowel disease, irritable bowel syndrome, gastrointestinal tumors, celiac disease, fatty liver disease, and type 2 diabetes, which may influence intestinal MUC2 expression [45, 46]. Duodenal biopsy specimens were fixed in 4% formaldehyde. A validation cohort was established to confirm the correlation between serum riboflavin levels and AP severity. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for this cohort were consistent with those of the exploratory cohort. MAP has no organ failure, local or systemic complications. MSAP is defined by the presence of transient organ failure (< 48 h), local complications, or exacerbation of co-morbid disease. SAP is defined by persistent organ failure (> 48 h). Serum samples were collected for riboflavin (vitamin B2) measurement.

Animals and experimental design

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade male C57BL/6J mice, aged 6–8 weeks (weighing 20–22 g), were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Company. Conditional knockout mice targeting the Muc2 gene were generated on a C57BL/6J background (Cyagen Biosciences). Exon 2 of Muc2 (ENSMUSG00000025515) was selected as the floxed region, encompassing a 262 bp coding sequence. Two loxP sites were inserted into intron 1 and intron 2 flanking exon 2, which constitutes approximately 1.91% of the coding region. Homology arms and the conditional knockout cassette were amplified from BAC clone RP23-100D6. Following gene targeting, founder mice were genotyped by PCR and verified through sequencing analysis. Successful Cre-mediated recombination results in a frameshift mutation and functional knockout of Muc2(Vil1-cre; Muc2-flox/flox, Muc2ΔIEC).

Mice were housed in the animal experiment center of Shanghai General Hospital under SPF conditions. The environment was maintained with a stable and moderate temperature, sufficient air exchange, appropriate humidity, and minimal noise. A 12 h light/dark cycle was provided using incandescent lighting.

Intestinal MUC2 changes were verified in three classic AP mice models. Mice were randomly assigned into four groups (n = 6 per group): control (CON), Cae-AP, Larg-AP, and NaTCA-AP. Mice in the Cae-AP group were injected intraperitoneally with 100 mg/kg cerulein (MedChemExpress, China) 10 times at hour intervals [11]. L-arginine (4.5 g/kg, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was injected intraperitoneally twice at an hour interval to establish Larg-AP mice [11]. As previously described [47], mice in the NaTCA-AP group were anesthetized with Zoletil 50 (Virbac, France), and 50 µL of 2% sodium taurocholate solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was infused into the biliopancreatic duct at a rate of 5 µL/min. Following AP induction, mice were sacrificed at specific time points to collect samples: 3 h after the last cerulein injection for the Cae-AP group [11], 72 h after the first L-arginine injection for the Larg-AP group [11], and 24 h after the intraductal infusion for the NaTCA-AP group [47]. Pancreas, serum, colon, cecal content, and mesenteric lymph nodes were then stored at −80 °C or in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent analysis.

Muc2ΔIEC mice were further utilized to investigate the impact of intestinal MUC2 deficiency on AP severity. WT mice were randomly assigned into WT and AP groups (n = 6 per group). Muc2ΔIEC mice were randomly assigned into Muc2ΔIEC and Muc2ΔIEC + AP groups (n = 6 per group). AP was induced by cerulein as described above.

We also leveraged FMT to explore gut microbiota alterations in exacerbating AP in Muc2ΔIEC mice. WT or Muc2ΔIEC mice were administered an antibiotic cocktail (ABX) consisting of vancomycin (0.5 mg/mL), neomycin (1 mg/mL), ampicillin (1 mg/mL), and metronidazole (1 mg/mL) (Sangon Biotech, China) dissolved in drinking water for 7 consecutive days to deplete gut microbiota [48]. Feces from donor mice (WT or Muc2ΔIEC) were collected, and approximately 100 mg of feces was suspended in 1 mL of sterile saline, followed by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The pellets were resuspended in sterile saline and used as the FMT suspension. WT mice were randomly assigned into WT → WT and Muc2ΔIEC → WT groups (n = 6 per group). Muc2ΔIEC mice were randomly assigned into WT → Muc2ΔIEC and Muc2ΔIEC → Muc2ΔIEC groups (n = 6 per group). Mice were gavaged with 200 µL of FMT suspension once daily for 7 days. Subsequently, AP was induced by cerulein in all groups following FMT administration.

An engineered Lactobacillus plantarum AR113 lacking the Rib gene was constructed to clarify the relationship between alternations of riboflavin and gut microbiota in Muc2ΔIEC mice. L. plantarum AR113 was cultured in MRS fluid medium (HOPEBIO, China). The Rib knockout strain was constructed as previously described [49]. Briefly, the RecE/T-assisted plasmid pLH01 was first electroporated into L. plantarum AR113 competent cells to generate AR113 (pLH01) cells. These competent cells were then prepared for further transformation. Subsequently, the verified knockout plasmid pLd-Rib was electroporated into AR113 (pLH01) competent cells using the following parameters: 5 kV, 200 Ω, and 25 μF. Immediately after electroporation, 900 μL of recovery medium (MRS broth supplemented with 500 mM sucrose and 20 mM MgCl₂; Sangon Biotech, China) was added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The cultures were then plated onto MRS agar supplemented with antibiotics (erythromycin and chloramphenicol at working concentrations of 10 μg/mL each) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 days. Colonies were screened by PCR using specific primers (AR113-Rib-KO-F: GCATCCAACCACATGATCGT and AR113-Rib-KO-R: ACGCGCAGGGCCACTTCGCT), followed by sequencing to confirm successful gene knockout. Verified strains were cultured in MRS fluid medium (HOPEBIO, China). Mice were randomly assigned into Muc2ΔIEC + AR113 + AP and Muc2ΔIEC + AR113ΔRib + AP groups (n = 6 per group). Muc2ΔIEC mice were pretreated with a broad-spectrum antibiotic cocktail for 7 days to deplete gut microbiota. L. plantarum AR113 and L. plantarum AR113ΔRib (108 CFU dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), once a day) were then administrated by oral gavage for 3 weeks in Muc2ΔIEC + AR113 + AP and Muc2ΔIEC + AR113ΔRib + AP groups, respectively. AP was induced by cerulein afterwards.

To explore the potential effects of riboflavin on AP, WT and Muc2ΔIEC mice were supplemented with riboflavin. Riboflavin (MedChemExpress, China) was dissolved in saline to a concentration of 10 mg/mL. WT mice were randomly assigned into WT and AP + RF groups (n = 6 per group). Muc2ΔIEC mice were randomly assigned into Muc2ΔIEC + AP and Muc2ΔIEC + AP + RF groups (n = 6 per group). Mice received an intraperitoneal injection of riboflavin (50 mg/kg) half an hour prior to AP induction with cerulein. Mice serving as controls received intraperitoneal injections of sterile saline or sterile distilled water at the same volume and frequency.

Histological examination

Pancreatic tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, while colonic tissues were fixed in Carnoy’s fixative at 4 °C for 48 h. Tissues were then processed through sequential washes in double distilled water, a series of rehydration and dehydration steps, and then embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections were cut at 4 μm. Slides were stained with either PAS or hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined by light microscopy (Leica, Germany) at 100× and 200× magnification. Pancreatic histopathological changes were evaluated based on the Schmidt criteria [50]. Colonic pathological damage was assessed according to the methods of Araki and Mehmet [51, 52].

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, diamine oxidase (DAO), and D-lactate were quantified using ELISA kits from MultiSciences Biotech, following the manufacturers’ protocols.

Serum amylase assay

Serum amylase activity was determined using a standardized enzymatic assay on an Advia 2400 automated chemistry analyzer (Siemens, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot

Colonic tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer (Epizyme Biotech, China) containing protease inhibitor (Epizyme Biotech, China) using a high-throughput tissue grinder (Onebio Biotech, China). Lysates were incubated on ice for 1 h and centrifuged at 10,000g for 10 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected, mixed with SDS-loading buffer (Epizyme Biotech, China), and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. Proteins (10 μL) were separated by 7.5% SDS-PAGE using a PAGE gel kit (Epizyme Biotech, China) and transferred to 0.2 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against MUC2 (A14659; Abclonal, China) or CD40 (A22714; Abclonal, China) in primary antibody dilution buffer (Epizyme Biotech, China). After washing three times with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG(H + L) (Epizyme Biotech, China) for 60 min at room temperature and washed again with TBST. Bands were visualized using an Amersham 600 imager (General Electric, USA) with HRP substrate peroxide solution (Millipore, USA).

Immunofluorescence

Sections of colonic tissue were heated at 60 °C for 1 h, then deparaffinized and rehydrated using a Leica Autostainer XL (Leica, USA). Antigen retrieval was performed with a citrate solution (Sangon Biotech, China). After washing in PBS, tissues were circled with a super pap pen (Biosharp, China), blocked with immunostaining blocking buffer (Sangon Biotech, China) for 1 h at room temperature, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against MUC2 (A14659; Abclonal, China), Occludin (A2601; Abclonal, China), and Claudin1 (ab211737; Abcam, USA) in primary antibody dilution buffer (Sangon Biotech, China). Slides were washed with PBS, incubated with Alexa Fluor 488/594 AffiniPure donkey anti-rabbit IgG (Yeason, China) for 1 h at room temperature, washed again with PBS, and stained with DAPI (Yeason, China) for 10 min. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, USA).

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using a tissue RNA purification kit plus (EZBioscience, USA). Prior to reverse transcription, genomic DNA was removed using the 8× gDNA Remover included in the kit (EnzyArtisan, China). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized with HyperScript III RT SuperMix (EnzyArtisan, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Bacterial DNA was extracted from fecal samples using an E.Z.N.A. stool DNA kit (Omega, USA). RNA or DNA concentration was measured by NanoDrop2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA). Real-time PCR was performed with S6 universal SYBR qPCR mix (EnzyArtisan, China) on the QuantStudio 6 Flex real-time PCR system (Thermo Scientific, USA). Gene expression was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

TUNEL and FISH assays

Apoptosis in acinar cells and colonic epithelial cells was evaluated using a TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Red Fluorescence) (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Bacterial translocation was detected by FISH. Pancreatic and colonic sections were dewaxed, subjected to antigen retrieval, and digested with proteinase K at 37 °C for 5 min. Sections were then pre-hybridized at 37 °C for 1 h and hybridized overnight at 37 °C with EUB338 probe (5′-Cy3-GCTGCCTCCCGTAGGAGT-3′) in a humidified chamber. After washing, sections were stained with DAPI, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, USA).

Bacterial cultures and plate counting

Mesenteric lymph nodes and liver tissues were collected in sterile PBS, fully ground using a high-throughput tissue grinder (Onebio Biotech, China), and plated onto brain heart infusion agar plates for culture of anaerobic bacteria. The plates were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C in a Vinyl Anaerobic Chamber (Coy, USA). The plates producing 25 to 250 CFUs were counted.

16S rRNA sequencing

Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from fecal and pancreatic tissues using the FastPure DNA Isolation Kit (MJYH, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified using primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) on a T100 thermal cycler (Bio-Rad, USA) [53]. PCR products were resolved on a 2% agarose gel, purified with a PCR Clean-Up Kit (YuHua, China), and quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equimolar amounts of purified amplicons were pooled and subjected to paired-end sequencing on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, USA) following the protocols provided by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology. After demultiplexing, the resulting sequences were quality filtered with fastp and merged with FLASH. Then the high-quality sequences were de-noised using DADA2 plugin in the Qiime2 pipeline with recommended parameters, which obtains single nucleotide resolution based on error profiles within samples. DADA2 denoised sequences are usually called ASVs. Downstream bioinformatic analyses were performed on the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Alpha diversity indices were calculated using Mothur (v1.30.1), and beta diversity was assessed by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities [54]. Group differences were evaluated using PERMANOVA via the vegan R package (v2.5-3). LEfSe was applied to identify differentially abundant taxa among groups [55].

Untargeted metabolomics

A total of 50 mg of solid sample was placed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube together with a 6-mm diameter grinding bead. Metabolites were extracted using 400 μL extraction solution (methanol:water = 4:1, v/v) containing 0.02 mg/mL of the internal standard (L-2-chlorophenylalanine). Samples were ground using a frozen tissue grinder for 6 min (−10 °C, 50 Hz), followed by ultrasonic extraction for 30 min (5 °C, 40 kHz). After extraction, samples were incubated at −20 °C for 30 min, centrifuged at 13,000g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were transferred into sample vials for untargeted metabolomics analysis using an UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY HSS T3 column. Metabolite identification was performed using several databases, including HMDB, Metlin, and the Majorbio Database. Metabolite concentrations were calculated based on linear regression from standard curves generated using external standards.

Further bioinformatics analysis was carried out on the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). To effectively extract group-discriminating metabolic features from the high-dimensional dataset, we employed OPLS-DA to filter out orthogonal variation and maximize group discrimination. Differential metabolites were subsequently identified based on VIP scores derived from the OPLS-DA, which quantify the contribution of each variable to the separation, in combination with p values derived from Student’s t-test. Metabolites with VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. These differential metabolites were subsequently subjected to metabolic pathway enrichment and analysis to identify their involvement in relevant biochemical pathways.

Targeted metabolomics

Pancreatic tissue samples were thawed at 4 °C, and an aliquot was mixed with methanol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to precipitate proteins. Following vortexing, sonication, and chilling, samples were centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. Targeted detection of these metabolites was performed using a UPLC system coupled with a Qtrap mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Acquity UPLC BEH Amide column using a gradient elution with mobile phases consisting of water containing 0.05% formic acid and 20 mM ammonium formate (Aladdin, China) and acetonitrile (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The column temperature and flow rate were controlled, and a specific gradient program was employed. The mass spectrometer was operated with an ESI ion source in MRM mode, utilizing compound-dependent transitions and parameters for sensitive and selective detection. Standard solutions were prepared in methanol at various concentrations, and standard curves were generated by plotting peak areas against concentrations. The standard curves exhibited good linearity (R2 > 0.99), which was used for quantification of the target substances in the serum samples.

Flow cytometry

Colonic lamina propria mononuclear cells (LPMCs) were isolated according to the method as described previously [56]. Briefly, the colon was cut into 0.5 cm pieces, washed thoroughly with cold PBS to remove all debris and blood. After incubating with 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and 5 mM EDTA in PBS at 37 °C for 20 min twice under gentle shaking to remove intestinal epithelial cells, the tissues were digested in 10 mL 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS)-RPMI with Collagenase D (0.5 mg/mL, Roche), DNase I (0.5 mg/mL, Sigma), and Dispase II (3 mg/mL, Roche) at 37 °C for 30 min. Samples were filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer. Lamina propria cells were then collected and further purified via density gradient centrifugation with 40% and 80% Percoll-RPMI solution. LPMCs were collected from the interphase, then analyzed by flow cytometry.

Pancreatic leukocytes were isolated using a collagenase digestion method described previously [57]. In brief, pancreatic tissues were carefully excised, rinsed in pre-cooled collection buffer (F10 buffer supplemented with trypsin inhibitor), and minced in 10 mL of collection buffer. After settling on ice for 3 min, tissue fragments were pelleted and resuspended in 2 mL of collagenase IV solution (2 mg/mL), followed by enzymatic digestion at 37 °C on a shaking incubator at 240 rpm for 12 min. Digestion was terminated with wash buffer (FACS buffer supplemented with BSA and trypsin inhibitor), and the resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. Red blood cells were lysed using RBC lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended for subsequent staining and flow cytometry.

LPMCs and Pancreatic leukocytes were stained with the following markers: CD45-APC-CY7, CD11b-FITC, F4/80-BV605, CD86-BV421, and Ly6G-BV421 (BD PharMingen, USA). After surface antigens were stained, the cells were fixed and permeabilized by Transcription Factor Buffer Set (BD PharMingen, USA). Then, cells were stained for the intracellular marker CD206-PE (BD PharMingen, USA). Cells were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo software.

Cell culture and intervention

RAW 264.7 murine macrophage cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution. RAW 264.7 cells were used for interventions after being passaged at least twice post-thawing to ensure stable growth. LPS treatment was performed at a concentration of 500 ng/mL for 12 h. Successful LPS stimulation was morphologically confirmed by the differentiation of RAW 264.7 cells from a rounded shape to an irregular, elongated morphology. Riboflavin intervention was conducted either alone or in combination with LPS. For combined treatment, Riboflavin and LPS were added simultaneously to the cell culture medium. Riboflavin was supplemented at a concentration gradient of 0, 6.25, 12.5, 25, and 50 μg/mL, respectively. CON group was treated with an equal volume of sterile PBS.

Transcriptomic sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from tissues using TRNzol Universal Reagent (TIANGEN, China), yielding > 2 μg total RNA per sample. RNA purity and integrity were assessed by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometry. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the APExBIO RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (APExBIO, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, first-strand cDNA was generated by oligo (dT)-primed reverse transcription with template switching, followed by PCR amplification to obtain double-stranded cDNA. cDNA products were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, USA). Libraries were constructed by Tn5 tagmentation and index PCR, and fragments of approximately 350–550 bp were size-selected. Library quantity and size distribution were assessed using Qubit and an Agilent Bioanalyzer/TapeStation system. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw paired-end reads (FASTQ format) were processed using Trim Galore (via Cutadapt) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases. Clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome mm10 using HISAT2, followed by transcript assembly and gene expression quantification using StringTie. DEGs were identified using DESeq2 with |log2(fold change)|≥ 1 and adjusted p < 0.05. Functional enrichment analyses for KEGG terms were performed using the clusterProfiler R package (p < 0.05).

GR activity assay

To reflect the level of cellular oxidative stress, GR activity was measured in tissue lysates using a colorimetric assay kit (S0055, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The assay is based on the GR-catalyzed reduction of GSSG in the presence of NADPH. This reaction is coupled to the reduction of 5,5'-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), leading to an increase in absorbance at 412 nm, which was monitored kinetically. GR activity was calculated and normalized to total protein concentration.

GSH and GSSG assay

To evaluate the cellular redox state, levels of GSH and GSSG glutathione were quantified using a colorimetric assay kit (S0053, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). For total glutathione measurement, deproteinized tissue extracts were incubated with glutathione reductase and DTNB, and the rate of color development at 412 nm was measured. For GSSG measurement, GSH was first chemically scavenged from the extracts before performing the same enzymatic reaction. Concentrations were calculated from a GSSG standard curve, and the GSH level was determined by subtracting the GSSG fraction from the total glutathione.

SDH activity assay

To assess mitochondrial electron transport chain function, SDH activity was assessed in tissue lysates using a colorimetric assay kit (S0530, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The assay measures the rate of succinate-dependent reduction of the electron acceptor 2,6-dichloroindophenol (DCIP). The decrease in absorbance at 600 nm, corresponding to the reduction of DCIP, was monitored kinetically. SDH activity was calculated based on a DCIP standard curve and normalized to total protein concentration.

NAD+/NADH assay

To reflect the function of mitochondrial Complex I, intracellular NAD+ and NADH levels were determined using a colorimetric assay kit based on WST-8 (S0175, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Cell or tissue extracts were prepared using the provided lysis buffer. To measure total NAD+/NADH, the extract was incubated with alcohol dehydrogenase to convert all NAD+ to NADH, and the total NADH was quantified by the reduction of WST-8 to a formazan dye measured at 450 nm. To measure NADH alone, NAD+ was first degraded by heating the extract at 60 °C for 30 min. Concentrations were determined using an NADH standard curve.

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement

Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using the JC-1 fluorescent probe according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Cells were washed once with PBS, and then incubated with JC-1 working solution mixed with complete culture medium at 37 °C for 20 min. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with ice-cold 1 × JC-1 staining buffer. Finally, cells were replenished with complete culture medium and immediately observed using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, USA). JC-1 monomers, indicating low potential, were detected by green fluorescence, while JC-1 aggregates, indicating high potential, were detected by red fluorescence.

Intracellular calcium ion measurement

Intracellular calcium ion levels were measured using the Fluo-4 fluorescent probe according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Fluo-4 staining solution was prepared by diluting 500 × Fluo-4 AM and 500 × Solubility Enhancer in Assay Buffer. Cells were incubated with the Fluo-4 staining solution at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Green fluorescence signals, reflecting intracellular calcium ion concentration, were observed using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, USA).

ChIP

ChIP assays were performed using the BeyoChIP™ Enzymatic ChIP Assay Kit (Protein A/G Magnetic Beads) (P2083, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The method involved crosslinking of cells with formaldehyde, followed by cell collection and lysis. Chromatin was fragmented using Micrococcal nuclease (MNase). The fragmented chromatin was then subjected to immunoprecipitation using an Acetyl-Histone H3-K27 Rabbit mAb (T59439; Abmart, China) and Protein A/G magnetic beads to capture protein-DNA complexes. A control immunoprecipitation with a non-specific IgG antibody was performed in parallel. After extensive washing, the protein-DNA complexes were eluted from the beads, crosslinking was reversed by heating, and proteins were digested with Proteinase K. The enriched DNA fragments were then purified. Finally, the presence and relative enrichment of the IL-6 and IL-12 gene promoter regions in the immunoprecipitated DNA were detected and quantified by qPCR.

Statistical methods

The variables of continuous data with normal distribution are expressed as mean (SD) and the variables with non-normal distribution were expressed as median (IQR). Comparisons between two groups with a normal distribution were performed by t-test. The Mann–Whitney U-test was used for the comparisons between two groups with non-normal distribution. Single-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparison among three or more groups. For categorical variables, data were expressed as numbers and percentages and evaluated by Chi-squared test. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to determine correlations. All the statistical analyses were carried out in IBM SPSS Statistics 25. p < 0.05 indicates that the difference was statistically significant.
