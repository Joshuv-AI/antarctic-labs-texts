Measuring Non-Hermitian Topological Invariants Directly from Quench Dynamics
Xiao-Dong Lin, Long Zhang

Abstract
While non-Hermitian (NH) topological phases and phenomena have been observed across various quantum systems, directly measuring NH topological invariants remains a significant challenge. In this study, we present a generic and unified framework for the direct measurement of various NH topological invariants in odd-dimensional systems through quench dynamics. We demonstrate that in one-dimensional (1D) NH systems with sublattice symmetry, the line-gap winding number and point-gap braiding degree can be extracted from the winding patterns of a dynamically constructed field based on post-quench spin textures. Specifically, line-gap topology is characterized by integer-valued winding, whereas point-gap complex-band braiding is revealed by half-integer or integer winding with abrupt jumps. We also extend our approach to higher-dimensional winding numbers and non-Bloch topological invariants under open-boundary conditions. Additionally, we propose a practical cold-atom setup to realize and detect 1D NH topological phases, showing that our dynamical measurement scheme is feasible in current experimental settings. This work paves the way for the direct measurement of NH topological invariants in quantum systems.

Dynamical measurement scheme.—

We start with 1D NH systems with SLS to illustrate our scheme.
The Hamiltonian is generally written as

\displaystyle~\begin{split}H(k)&=H_{0}(k)+\mathbbm{i}H_{1}(k),\\
H_{0}(k)&=\sum_{i=x,y}h_{i}(k)\sigma_{i},\quad H_{1}(k)=\sum_{i=x,y}h^{\prime}_{i}(k)\sigma_{i},\end{split}

(1)

for which
S=\sigma_{z}
. Here
\sigma_{x,y,z}
are the Pauli matrices and
h^{\prime}_{x,y}
can be constant including zero.
The right eigenvectors of
H(k)
are denoted as
|u_{n=\pm}^{\rm R}(k)\rangle
with eigenvalues
E_{\pm}(k)=\pm{\varepsilon}(k)
.
Here
{\varepsilon}(k)=[\sum_{i=x,y}(h_{i}+\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{i})^{2}]^{-1/2}
for real-line-gapped
H(k)
.
We investigate the time evolution governed by
H(k)
starting from a chosen initial fully-polarized state
\ket{\psi_0(k)}
, which can be realized experimentally by applying an additional Zeeman field and suddenly changing its
strength from a large value to zero.

To measure the NH topology, we introduce an auxiliary Hermitian matrix
Q(k)
:

\displaystyle~Q(k)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{H(k)}{\varepsilon(k)}+\frac{H^{\dagger}(k)}{\varepsilon^{*}(k)}\right],

(2)

and define the
normalized
long-time spin textures (LTSTs)

~\overline{\langle\sigma_{i}(k)\rangle}=\frac{{\langle\sigma_{i}(k)\rangle}_{\infty}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=x,y}\langle\sigma_{j}(k)\rangle_{\infty}^{2}}},\quad i=x,y,

(3)

where

\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle_{\infty}=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\cal N}_{k}^{-1}{\rm Tr}(e^{-\mathbbm{i}Ht}\rho_{0}e^{\mathbbm{i}H^{\dagger}t}\sigma_{i})
with
{\cal N}_{k}\equiv{\rm Tr}(e^{-\mathbbm{i}Ht}\rho_{0}e^{\mathbbm{i}H^{\dagger}t})
and the density matrix of the initial state
\rho_{0}=\ket{\psi_0}\bra{\psi_0}
.
After some derivation, we find that (i)
H(k)
is topologically equivalent to
Q({k})
in terms of a line (real or imaginary) gap
[
92
]
, and (ii) regardless of the initial state
\rho_{0}

[
93
]
, the topology of the Hermitian
Q
matrix is characterized by the winding number of a dynamical field
{\bm{g}}(k)=(g_{x}(k),g_{y}(k))
:

\displaystyle~W_{1}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\rm BZ}d{k}\left[g_{x}(k)\partial_{{k}}g_{y}(k)-g_{y}(k)\partial_{{k}}g_{x}(k)\right],

(4)

where the integral is over the first Brillouin zone (BZ) and
{\bm{g}}(k)
is defined either by
g_{i}(k)=\overline{\langle\sigma_{i}(k)\rangle}
for imaginary line gaps, or by the
deformed
LTSTs as

\displaystyle~g_{i}(k)=\widetilde{\langle\sigma_{i}(k)\rangle}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\overline{\langle\sigma_{i}(k)\rangle}&\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon({k})]>0\\
-\overline{\langle\sigma_{i}(k)\rangle}&\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon({k})]<0\end{array}\right.,\,\,i=x,y,

for real line gaps
[
92
]
. Therefore, one can characterize the NH topology of
H(k)
by measuring
W_{1}
from LTSTs.
Although it is introduced to detect the line-gap topology,
W_{d}
can, remarkably, also characterize the point-gap topology of
H(k)
, which will be detailed below.
We emphasize that all the LTSTs introduced above are defined only in the basis of right eigenvectors
\{|u_{n}^{\rm R}(k)\rangle\}
,
and thus can be directly measured in experiment.

Conclusion.—
We have proposed a generic and unified scheme to directly measure line-gap winding numbers, point-gap braiding degrees, and non-Bloch topological invariants in odd-dimensional systems.
The experimental setup we’ve designed not only demonstrates the practicality of detecting 1D NH topological phases but also highlights the accessibility of these measurements with existing technologies.
Despite only odd-dimensional systems being considered, the proposed scheme can certainly be generalized to NH systems in even dimensions,
which will be our next study.

I. Non-Hermitian systems with sublattice symmetry

For odd-dimensional non-Hermitian (NH) systems exhibiting sublattice symmetry:
SH({\bm{k}})S^{-1}=-H({\bm{k}})

[
1
]
, the Hamiltonian can generally be written as

\displaystyle~\begin{split}H({\bm{k}})&=H_{0}({\bm{k}})+\mathbbm{i}H_{1}({\bm{k}}),\quad H_{0}({\bm{k}})={\sum}_{i}h_{i}({\bm{k}})\gamma_{i},\quad H_{1}(\bm{k})=\sum_{i}h^{\prime}_{i}(\bm{k})\gamma_{i},\end{split}

(S1)

where
h^{\prime}_{i}
can be constant including zero, and the
\gamma
matrices obey the Clifford algebra
\{\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\}=2\delta_{ij}\mathbbm{1}
and are of dimensionality
2^{(d+1)/2}
for odd
d
-dimensional (
d
D) line-gapped insulators. The right eigenvectors of NH Hamiltonian (
S1
) are denoted as
\ket{u_{n^{(\pm)}}^R(\bm{k})}
with eigenvalues
E_{n^{(\pm)}}(\bm{k})=\pm\varepsilon(\bm{k})
. Here
{\varepsilon}(\bm{k})=\sqrt{\sum_{i}[h_{i}(\bm{k})+\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{i}(\bm{k})]^{2}}
for real-line-gapped phases.

I.1
A. Definition of the
Q
matrix and proof of the topological equivalence

For the sake of simplicity, we focus on one-dimensional (1D) systems in this subsection. Nonetheless, both the results and the proof can be easily extended to higher-dimensional systems. For a 1D NH Hamiltonian
H(k)
, the right and left eigenvectors are defined by

\displaystyle H(k)|u_{\pm}^{\rm R}\rangle=\pm\varepsilon(k)|u_{\pm}^{\rm R}\rangle,\quad H^{\dagger}(k)|u_{\pm}^{\rm L}\rangle=\pm\varepsilon^{*}(k)|u_{\pm}^{\rm L}\rangle.

(S2)

When
\varepsilon(k)\neq 0
, we introduce the
Q
matrix as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&Q(k)=\mathbbm{1}-P_{-}^{\rm R}(k)-P_{-}^{\rm L}(k),\\
&P_{\pm}^{\rm R}\equiv|u_{\pm}^{\rm R}\rangle\langle u_{\pm}^{\rm L}|,\quad P_{\pm}^{\rm L}\equiv|u_{\pm}^{\rm L}\rangle\langle u_{\pm}^{\rm R}|.\end{split}

(S3)

Since
P_{+}^{\rm R}+P_{-}^{\rm R}=\mathbbm{1}
and
H/\varepsilon=P_{+}^{\rm R}-P_{-}^{\rm R}
, we have
2P_{-}^{\rm R}=\mathbbm{1}-H/\varepsilon
.
Similarly, one can derive
2P_{-}^{\rm L}=\mathbbm{1}-H^{\dagger}/\varepsilon^{*}
. The
Q
matrix is then rewritten as

\displaystyle~Q(k)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{H(k)}{\varepsilon(k)}+\frac{H^{\dagger}(k)}{\varepsilon^{*}(k)}\right].

(S4)

We then prove the topological equivalence between
H
and
Q
.
We define

\displaystyle{\cal H}(s)\equiv s\frac{H}{\varepsilon}+(1-s)Q=\frac{1+s}{2}\frac{H}{\varepsilon}+\frac{1-s}{2}\frac{H^{\dagger}}{\varepsilon^{*}},

(S5)

where
s\in[0,1]
. One can derive that

\displaystyle{\cal H}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+s)^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{H}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}+\frac{(1-s)^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{H^{\dagger}}{\varepsilon^{*}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1-s^{2}}{4}\frac{HH^{\dagger}+H^{\dagger}H}{|\varepsilon|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+s)^{2}}{2}+\frac{1-s^{2}}{2}\frac{[{\rm Re}(H)]^{2}+[{\rm Im}(H)]^{2}}{|\varepsilon|^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1+s)^{2}}{2}.

(S6)

We then have
{\cal H}^{2}>0
, i.e.,
{\cal H}(s)
is always gapped for
0\leq s\leq 1
, which means
H/\varepsilon
and
Q
are topologically equivalent.
Therefore,
H
is topologically equivalent to
Q
in terms of line gaps.

I.2
B. Dynamical measurement scheme

We define the normalized long-time spin textures (LTSTs) as

~\overline{\langle\gamma_{i}(\bm{k})\rangle}=\frac{{\langle\gamma_{i}(\bm{k})\rangle}_{\infty}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j}\langle\gamma_{j}(\bm{k})\rangle_{\infty}^{2}}},

(S7)

where

\langle\gamma_{i}\rangle_{\infty}=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\cal N}_{\bm{k}}^{-1}{\rm Tr}(e^{-\mathbbm{i}Ht}\rho_{0}e^{\mathbbm{i}H^{\dagger}t}\gamma_{i})
, with the normalization factor
{\cal N}_{\bm{k}}\equiv{\rm Tr}(e^{-\mathbbm{i}Ht}\rho_{0}e^{\mathbbm{i}H^{\dagger}t})
and a chosen initial state
\rho_{0}
. Since
e^{-\mathbbm{i}Ht}=\cos(\varepsilon t)-\mathbbm{i}\sin(\varepsilon t)H/\varepsilon
, one can prove that
after long-term dynamics, the LTSTs are given by

\displaystyle~\langle\gamma_{i}(\bm{k})\rangle_{\infty}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\cal C}_{1}^{-1}{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{0}\gamma_{i}(1+\frac{H}{\varepsilon}-\frac{H^{\dagger}}{\varepsilon^{*}}-\frac{H^{\dagger}H}{\varepsilon^{*}\varepsilon}+2\frac{h_{i}-\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon^{*}}\gamma_{i}+2\frac{h_{i}-\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon^{*}\varepsilon}\gamma_{i}H)\right]&\quad\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon(\bm{k})]>0\\
{\cal C}_{2}^{-1}{\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{0}\gamma_{i}(1-\frac{H}{\varepsilon}+\frac{H^{\dagger}}{\varepsilon^{*}}-\frac{H^{\dagger}H}{\varepsilon^{*}\varepsilon}-2\frac{h_{i}-\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon^{*}}\gamma_{i}+2\frac{h_{i}-\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon^{*}\varepsilon}\gamma_{i}H)\right]&\quad\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon({\bm{k}})]<0\end{array}\right.,

with
{\cal C}_{1}={\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{0}(1+\frac{H}{\varepsilon}+\frac{H^{\dagger}}{\varepsilon^{*}}+\frac{H^{\dagger}H}{\varepsilon^{*}\varepsilon})\right]
and
{\cal C}_{2}={\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{0}(1-\frac{H}{\varepsilon}-\frac{H^{\dagger}}{\varepsilon^{*}}+\frac{H^{\dagger}H}{\varepsilon^{*}\varepsilon})\right]
.
When the initial state is prepared as
\rho_{0}=(\mathbbm{1}-S)/2
at each
{\bm{k}}
, we have

\displaystyle~\langle\gamma_{i}(\bm{k})\rangle_{\infty}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\cal C}_{1}^{-1}\left[\frac{h_{i}+\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{h_{i}-\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon^{*}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\neq i}{\rm Tr}(S\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j})(\frac{h_{j}+\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{j}}{\varepsilon}-\frac{h_{j}-\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{j}}{\varepsilon^{*}})\right]&\quad\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon(\bm{k})]>0\\
-{\cal C}_{2}^{-1}\left[\frac{h_{i}+\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{h_{i}-\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon^{*}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\neq i}{\rm Tr}(S\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j})(\frac{h_{j}+\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{j}}{\varepsilon}-\frac{h_{j}-\mathbbm{i}h^{\prime}_{j}}{\varepsilon^{*}})\right]&\quad\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon({\bm{k}})]<0\end{array}\right.,

which yields the normalized LTSTs

\displaystyle~\overline{\langle\gamma_{i}(\bm{k})\rangle}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\frac{h_{i}+ih^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{h_{i}-ih^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon^{*}}\right)/\left[\sum_{j}\left(\frac{h_{j}+ih^{\prime}_{j}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{h_{j}-ih^{\prime}_{j}}{\varepsilon^{*}}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}&\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon(\bm{k})]>0\\
-\left(\frac{h_{i}+ih^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{h_{i}-ih^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon^{*}}\right)/\left[\sum_{j}\left(\frac{h_{j}+ih^{\prime}_{j}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{h_{j}-ih^{\prime}_{j}}{\varepsilon^{*}}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}&\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon(\bm{k})]<0\end{array}\right..

Note that, while for
d
D NH systems it is necessary to choose
\rho_{0}=(\mathbbm{1}-S)/2
, in the case of 1D NH systems, the results of
\langle\sigma_{i}(k)\rangle_{\infty}
are independent of the initial state
\rho_{0}
. To see it, one can project
\rho_{0}
onto the eigenstates of the NH Hamiltonian
\rho_{0}=\sum_{nm}c_{nm}|u_{n}^{R}(\bm{k})\rangle\langle u_{m}^{R}(\bm{k})|
with
c_{nm}=\langle u_{n}^{L}(\bm{k})|\rho_{0}|u_{m}^{L}(\bm{k})\rangle
, and
\rho(T)=\sum_{nm}c_{nm}e^{-i(E_{n}-E_{m}^{*})T}|u_{n}^{R}(\bm{k})\rangle\langle u_{m}^{R}(\bm{k})|
after the evolution time
T
. Since
E_{n^{(\pm)}}=\pm\varepsilon(\bm{k})
, one has

~\begin{aligned} \rho(T)&=\sum_{n^{(+)},m^{(+)}}c_{nm}e^{2{\rm{Im}}(\varepsilon T)}\ket{u_{n^{(+)}}^R(\bm{k})}\bra{u_{m^{(+)}}^R(\bm{k})}+\sum_{n^{(-)},m^{(-)}}c_{nm}e^{-2{\rm{Im}}(\varepsilon T)}\ket{u_{n^{(-)}}^R(\bm{k})}\bra{u_{m^{(-)}}^R(\bm{k})}\\
&+\sum_{n^{(+)},m^{(-)}}c_{nm}e^{-\mathbbm{i}2{\rm{Re}}(\varepsilon T)}\ket{u_{n^{(+)}}^R(\bm{k})}\bra{u_{m^{(-)}}^R(\bm{k})}+\sum_{n^{(-)},m^{(+)}}c_{nm}e^{\mathbbm{i}2{\rm{Re}}(\varepsilon T)}\ket{u_{n^{(-)}}^R(\bm{k})}\bra{u_{m^{(+)}}^R(\bm{k})}.\end{aligned}

(S14)

In the infinite-time limit, only the terms with positive imaginary energies persist in the time evolution, yielding

\displaystyle~\rho(T\to\infty)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{n^{(+)},m^{(+)}}c_{nm}e^{2{\rm{Im}}(\varepsilon T)}\ket{u_{n^{(+)}}^R(\bm{k})}\bra{u_{m^{(+)}}^R(\bm{k})}&\quad\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon(\bm{k})]>0\\
\sum_{n^{(-)},m^{(-)}}c_{nm}e^{-2{\rm{Im}}(\varepsilon T)}\ket{u_{n^{(-)}}^R(\bm{k})}\bra{u_{m^{(-)}}^R(\bm{k})}&\quad\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon(\bm{k})]<0\end{array}\right.,

where the coefficients
c_{nm}
depend on
\rho_{0}
. However, in one dimension, there are only two complex-energy bands and the result simplifies to

\displaystyle\rho(T\to\infty)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\ket{u_{+}^R(\bm{k})}\bra{u_{+}^R(\bm{k})}&\quad\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon(\bm{k})]>0\\
\ket{u_{-}^R(\bm{k})}\bra{u_{-}^R(\bm{k})}&\quad\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon(\bm{k})]<0\end{array}\right.,

which is independent of the initial state
\rho_{0}
.

As introduced in the above subsection, the
Q
matrix is given by

\displaystyle~Q(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{H(\bm{k})}{\varepsilon(\bm{k})}+\frac{H^{\dagger}(\bm{k})}{\varepsilon^{*}(\bm{k})}\right].

(S19)

One can write

\displaystyle Q({\bm{k}})=\sum_{i}Q_{i}({\bm{k}})\gamma_{i},\,\,{\rm where}\,\,Q_{i}(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{h_{i}+ih^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{h_{i}-ih^{\prime}_{i}}{\varepsilon^{*}}\right),

(S20)

and easily prove that

\displaystyle~\overline{\langle\gamma_{i}(\bm{k})\rangle}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\overline{Q_{i}(\bm{k})}&\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon(\bm{k})]>0\\
-\overline{Q_{i}(\bm{k})}&\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon(\bm{k})]<0\end{array}\right.,

where
\overline{Q_{i}(\bm{k})}={Q_{i}(\bm{k})}/\sqrt{\sum_{j}Q_{j}(\bm{k})^{2}}
.
This result forms the foundation of our dynamical measurement scheme.
We accordingly introduce the dynamical field

to characterize the topology of the

for imaginary line gaps, or by the deformed LTSTs as

\displaystyle~g_{i}(\bm{k})=\widetilde{\langle\gamma_{i}(\bm{k})\rangle}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\overline{\langle\gamma_{i}(\bm{k})\rangle}&\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon(\bm{k})]>0\\
-\overline{\langle\gamma_{i}(\bm{k})\rangle}&\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon(\bm{k})]<0\end{array}\right.

for real line gaps. From Eqs. (
I.2

Due to the topological equivalence between

I.4
D. Open boundary conditions and generalized Brillouin zone

In the presence of skin effects, the energy spectrum of a NH system under open boundary conditions (OBCs) exhibits marked differences compared to the spectrum under periodic boundary conditions (PBCs); notably, the OBC spectrum can even be entirely real, as exemplified by the NH SSH model [Fig. 3(b)] discussed in the main text. The long-term dynamics resulting from complex and real spectra diverge significantly. For complex eigenenergies, only the right eigenvectors with positive imaginary parts (
{\rm{Im}}(E)>0
) persist, while a real spectrum is characterized by the absence of gain or dissipation, resulting in dynamics akin to that of Hermitian systems. In the following, we will consider the 1D non-Bloch Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle H(k_{G})=h_{x}(k_{G})\sigma_{x}+h_{y}(k_{G})\sigma_{y},

(S26)

and explore the quench dynamics associated with both the complex and real spectra in detail.
The non-Bloch Hamiltonian
H(k_{G})
is obtained from
H(k)
by the replacement
e^{\mathbbm{i}k}\to\beta\equiv e^{\mathbbm{i}k_{G}}
, where
k_{G}
is complex
[
2
]
.
The corresponding
Q
matrix
Q(k_{G})
also takes the form of Eq. (
S4
), i.e.,

\displaystyle Q(k_{G})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{H(k_{G})}{\varepsilon(k_{G})}+\frac{H^{\dagger}(k_{G})}{\varepsilon^{*}(k_{G})}\right],

(S27)

with
E_{\pm}(k_{G})=\pm\varepsilon(k_{G})
being the right eigenvalues of
H(k_{G})
. Note that the proof presented in Eq. (
S6
) also applies to OBCs.

Here we show how to derive spin textures in the GBZ from a real-space initial state.
The two complex-momentum bases are
\left\{\langle\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G})|\right\}
and
\left\{|\beta^{\rm R}(k_{G})\rangle\right\}
,
where
|\beta^{\rm R}(k_{G})\rangle=L^{-1/2}\sum_{i}e^{ik_{G}x_{i}}\ket{x_i}
and
|\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G})\rangle=L^{-1/2}\sum_{i}e^{ik_{G}^{*}x_{i}}\ket{x_i}
, with
i
denoting the lattice site index and
L
being the lattice length. We first consider the simple case where the complex-momentum bases are biorthogonal, i.e., they satisfy
\langle\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G})|\beta^{\rm R}(k^{\prime}_{G})\rangle=\delta_{k_{G},k^{\prime}_{G}}
and
\sum_{k_{G}}|\beta^{\rm R}(k_{G})\rangle\langle\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G})|=\mathbbm{1}
.
The projection operator
P_{k_{G}}\equiv|\beta^{\rm R}(k_{G})\rangle\langle\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G})|
can be introduced
to project the time-evolving density matrix
\rho(x,t)=e^{-\mathbbm{i}Ht}\rho_{0}(x)e^{\mathbbm{i}H^{\dagger}t}
, where
\rho_{0}(x)=\ket{\psi_0(x)}\bra{\psi_0(x)}
with
\bra{\psi_0(x)}
being the real-space initial state,
onto the GBZ, leading to
\rho(k_{G},t)=P_{k_{G}}\rho(x,t)P_{k_{G}}^{\dagger}
.
This then gives the normalized LTSTs in the GBZ:

~\overline{\langle\sigma_{i}(k_{G})\rangle}=\frac{{\langle\sigma_{i}(k_{G})\rangle}_{\infty}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=x,y}\langle\sigma_{j}(k_{G})\rangle_{\infty}^{2}}},\quad i=x,y,

(S28)

where
\langle\sigma_{i}(k_{G})\rangle_{\infty}=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\cal N}_{k_{G}}^{-1}{\rm Tr}[\rho(k_{G},t)\sigma_{i}]
with
{\cal N}_{k_{G}}\equiv{\rm Tr}[\rho(k_{G},t)]
.

The projection procedure above can also be extended to more general scenarios where the complex-momentum bases are not biorthogonal.
Assuming that the time-evolving state
\ket{\psi(t)}=e^{-\mathbbm{i}Ht}\ket{\psi_0}
can be written as

\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{k_{G},\sigma=\uparrow\downarrow}C_{k_{G}}^{\sigma}(t)|\beta^{\rm R}(k_{G}),\sigma\rangle
,
we have

\langle\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G}),\sigma\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{k^{\prime}_{G}}C_{k^{\prime}_{G}}^{\sigma}(t)\langle\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G})|\beta^{\rm R}(k^{\prime}_{G})\rangle,\quad\sigma=\uparrow\downarrow,

(S29)

or, in a matrix form,

{\bf D}^{\sigma}={\bf M}\cdot{\bf C}^{\sigma},

(S30)

where the
N\times N
matrix
{\bf M}
is defined as
{\bf M}=\left[\langle\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G,i})|\beta^{\rm R}(k^{\prime}_{G,j})\rangle\right]_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
, and the two vectors are
{\bf C}^{\sigma}=\left(C_{k_{G,1}}^{\sigma},C_{k_{G,2}}^{\sigma},\dots,C_{k_{G,N}}^{\sigma}\right)^{\rm T}
and
{\bf D}^{\sigma}=\left(\langle\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G,1}),\sigma\ket{\psi(t)},\langle\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G,2}),\sigma\ket{\psi(t)},\dots,\langle\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G,N}),\sigma\ket{\psi(t)}\right)^{\rm T}
. Here
N
is the number of lattice sites.
Therefore, for each spin
\sigma=\uparrow\downarrow
, the coefficients
C_{k_{G}}^{\sigma}(t)
can be derived as follows:

{\bf C}^{\sigma}={\bf M}^{-1}{\bf D}^{\sigma}.

(S31)

The density matrix
\rho(t)
is then given by
\rho(t)=\ket{\psi(t)}\bra{\psi(t)}=\sum_{k_{G}k_{G}^{\prime},\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}C_{k_{G}}^{\sigma}(t)C_{k_{G}^{\prime}}^{\sigma^{\prime}}(t)|\beta^{\rm R}(k_{G}),\sigma\rangle\langle\beta^{\rm R}(k^{\prime}_{G}),\sigma^{\prime}|
. With this result, the normalized LTSTs
\overline{\langle\sigma_{x,y}(k_{G})\rangle}
can also be obtained as defined in Eq. (
S28
).

For a complex spectrum, following the procedure described in Eq.(
I.2
) and Eq.(
I.2
), one can prove that the normalized LTSTs satisfy

\displaystyle~\overline{\langle\sigma_{i}({k_{G}})\rangle}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\overline{Q_{i}({k_{G}})}&\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon({k_{G}})]>0\\
-\overline{Q_{i}({k_{G}})}&\mathrm{Im}[\varepsilon({k_{G}})]<0\end{array}\right.,\quad i=x,y.

By defining the dynamical field
{\bm{g}}(k_{G})=(g_{x}(k_{G}),g_{y}(k_{G}))
as Eq. (
I.2
) with the replacement
k\rightarrow k_{G}
, one can then characterize the topology of the Hermitian matrix
Q(k_{G})
(and hence
H(k_{G})
) by the winding number of
{\bm{g}}(k_{G})
:

\displaystyle~W_{1}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\rm GBZ}d{k_{G}}\left[g_{x}(k_{G})\partial_{{k_{G}}}g_{y}(k_{G})-g_{y}(k_{G})\partial_{{k_{G}}}g_{x}(k_{G})\right].

(S34)

For a real spectrum, we rewrite
H(k_{G})
as

\displaystyle{h}_{x}(k_{G})={h}_{xr}(k_{G})+\mathbbm{i}{h}_{xi}(k_{G}),\quad{h}_{y}(k_{G})={h}_{yr}(k_{G})+\mathbbm{i}{h}_{yi}(k_{G}),

(S35)

where
h_{\alpha r}
(
h_{\alpha i}
) represent the real (imaginary) parts of
h_{\alpha=x,y}
. Accordingly, the
Q
matrix can be written as

Q(k_{G})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon(k_{G})}({h}_{xr}\sigma_{x}+{h}_{yr}\sigma_{y}).

(S36)

The spectrum being real requires
h_{yi}/h_{xr}=-h_{xi}/h_{yr}=q
and
q^{2}<1
, which gives

\varepsilon(k_{G})=\sqrt{(h_{xr}^{2}+h_{yr}^{2})(1-q^{2})}
.
Due to the bulk-surface duality
[
5
]
,
the topological characterization of Hermitian Hamiltonians can reduce to topological invariants defined in particular momentum subspaces called band inversion surfaces (BISs).
Here, for
Q(k_{G})
, the BIS refers to the complex momenta where
h_{xr}(k_{G})=0
, and the associated topological invariant is given by

\displaystyle C_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\rm BIS}_{i}}\left[\mathrm{sgn}[h_{yr}(k_{G,iR})]-\mathrm{sgn}[h_{yr}(k_{G,iL})]\right],

(S37)

where
k_{G,iL/R}
are a pair of
k_{G}
that composes the
i
th BIS, i.e.,
h_{xr}(k_{G,iL/R})=0
.

To characterize the topology of
Q(k_{G})
, we introduce the time-averaged spin textures (TASTs)

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{i}(k_{G})\rangle_{T}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{{\cal N}_{k_{G}}}{\rm Tr}\left[\rho(k_{G},t)\sigma_{i}\right]dt,\quad i=x,y,

(S38)

with the normalization factor
{\cal N}_{k_{G}}\equiv{\rm Tr}\left[\rho(k_{G},t)\right]
.
When choosing the initial spin state to be
(\mathbbm{1}-\sigma_{x})/2
, one can derive that

\begin{split}{\langle\sigma_{x}(k_{G})\rangle}_{T}&=-1+C\left(\frac{q^{2}h_{xr}^{2}+h_{yr}^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right),\\
{\langle\sigma_{y}(k_{G})\rangle}_{T}&=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{{\cal N}_{k_{G}}}\left[\sin 2\varepsilon t\frac{qh_{xr}}{\varepsilon}-(1-\cos 2\varepsilon t)\frac{h_{xr}{h}_{yr}}{h_{xr}^{2}+{h}_{y_{r}}^{2}}\right]dt,\end{split}

(S39)

where
C=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{{\cal N}_{k_{G}}}(1-\cos 2\varepsilon t)dt
. Note that near
{h}_{xr}=0
,

{\langle\sigma_{y}(k_{G})\rangle}_{T}=-C\frac{{h}_{xr}{h}_{yr}}{{h}_{xr}^{2}+{h}_{y_{r}}^{2}},

(S40)

which indicate

\mathrm{sgn}[\partial_{k_{G}}{\langle\sigma_{y}(k_{G,j})\rangle}_{T}]=-\mathrm{sgn}[h_{yr}(k_{G,j})]\cdot\mathrm{sgn}[\partial_{k_{G}}h_{xr}(k_{G,j})],

(S41)

where
k_{G,j}
can stand for
k_{G,iL}
or
k_{G,iR}
.
Since
\mathrm{sgn}[\partial_{k_{G}}h_{xr}(k_{G,jL})]=-\mathrm{sgn}[\partial_{k_{G}}h_{xr}(k_{G,jR})]
, we finally arrive at the conclusion

\displaystyle C_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\mathrm{sgn}\left[\partial_{k_{G}}{\langle\sigma_{y}(k_{G,j})\rangle}_{T}\right],

(S42)

where
k_{G,j}
denote the paired points at which
{\langle\sigma_{y}(k_{G,j})\rangle}_{T}=0
and
|{\langle\sigma_{x}(k_{G,j})\rangle}_{T}|
takes local minima.

Although our discussion in this subsection is restricted to 1D cases, the extension to higher-dimensional GBZ is straightforward.
The key idea behind the dynamical characterization of 1D non-Bloch topological bands is to introduce the projection operator
P_{k_{G}}
, which is constructed using the complex-momentum bases
\{\langle\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G})|\}
and
\{|\beta^{\rm R}(k_{G})\rangle\}
introduced in the 1D GBZ theory
[
2
]
.
This operator projects the time-evolving wave function from real space into complex-momentum space [see the discussion above Eq. (
S28
)].
Through this approach, we can extract the spin textures measured in the GBZ and use them to characterize the topology. Similarly, in higher odd dimensions

d\geq 3
, once the complex-momentum bases
\{\langle\beta_{i}^{\rm L}(k_{G})|\}
and
\{|\beta_{i}^{\rm R}(k_{G})\rangle\}
(
i=1,2\dots,d
) of the
d
-dimensional GBZ are determined through an appropriate theoretical framework, such as the recently-established Amoeba formulation
[
3
]
or geometry-adaptive spectral potential theory
[
4
]
,
one can dynamically measure the non-Bloch topological invariants by applying the same projection procedure.

II
II. Non-Hermitian systems with chiral symmetry

II.1
A. Dynamical measurement scheme

Chiral symmetry, which requires
\Gamma H^{\dagger}({\bm{k}})\Gamma^{-1}=-H({\bm{k}})
, is notably distinct from sublattice symmetry in NH systems
[
1
]
. Here, we consider odd
d
D line-gapped NH systems, and write the Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle~\begin{split}H({\bm{k}})&=H_{0}({\bm{k}})+\mathbbm{i}H_{1}({\bm{k}}),\quad H_{0}({\bm{k}})={\sum}_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}({\bm{k}})\gamma_{i},\quad H_{1}(\bm{k})=h^{\prime}_{\Gamma}(\bm{k})\Gamma,\end{split}

(S43)

with
\Gamma=\mathbbm{i}^{(d+1)/2}\prod_{i=0}^{d}\gamma_{i}
. We suppose that the non-Hermitian term
{h^{\prime}}_{\Gamma}
is sufficiently weak, allowing this system to exhibit real spectra
E_{\pm}(\bm{k})=\pm\varepsilon({\bm{k}})
with
\varepsilon(\bm{k})=\sqrt{\sum_{i}^{d}h_{i}^{2}-{{h}^{\prime}_{\Gamma}}^{2}}
.
Consequently, the
Q
matrix, defined in Eq. (
S19
), is now given by

\displaystyle Q({\bm{k}})=\sum_{i}Q_{i}({\bm{k}})\gamma_{i},\,\,{\rm where}\,\,Q_{i}(\bm{k})=\frac{h_{i}(\bm{k})}{\varepsilon(\bm{k})}.

(S44)

To characterize the topology of
Q(\bm{k})
, we adopt the dynamical bulk-surface correspondence
[
5
]
, which states that the bulk topology of a
d
D phase has a one-to-one correspondence to quench-induced dynamical topological patterns emerging on
(d-1)
D BISs.
We introduce the TASTs

\displaystyle\langle\gamma_{i}(\bm{k})\rangle_{T}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{{\cal N}_{\bm{k}}}{\rm Tr}\left[e^{-\mathbbm{i}Ht}\rho_{0}e^{\mathbbm{i}H^{\dagger}t}\gamma_{i}\right]dt,\,\,\,i=0,1,\dots,d

(S45)

with the normalized factor
{\cal N}_{\bm{k}}\equiv{\rm Tr}\left[e^{-\mathbbm{i}Ht}\rho_{0}e^{\mathbbm{i}H^{\dagger}t}\right]
.
The integrand can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[e^{-\mathbbm{i}Ht}\rho_{0}e^{\mathbbm{i}H^{\dagger}t}\gamma_{i}\right]={\rm Tr}\left[\rho_{0}\gamma_{i}\left(\cos^{2}\varepsilon t-\mathbbm{i}\sin 2\varepsilon t\frac{H_{0}}{\varepsilon}-\sin^{2}\varepsilon t\frac{\varepsilon^{2}-2\mathbbm{i}{h^{\prime}}_{\Gamma}\Gamma H_{0}+{h^{\prime}}_{\Gamma}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\mathbbm{i}\sin 2\varepsilon t\frac{h_{i}\gamma_{i}}{\varepsilon}+2\sin^{2}\varepsilon t\frac{h_{i}\gamma_{i}H}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\right].

(S46)

We first consider higher-dimensional systems with
d\geq 3
and set the initial state to be
\rho_{0}=(\mathbbm{1}-\gamma_{i})/2
at each
{\bm{k}}
(
i=0,1,\dots,d\,
). Without loss of generality, we choose
\rho_{0}=(\mathbbm{1}-\gamma_{0})/2
in the following.
The TASTs are simplified to

~{\langle\gamma_{i}(\bm{k})\rangle}_{T}=-C\frac{h_{0}({\bm{k}})h_{i}({\bm{k}})}{\varepsilon^{2}(\bm{k})},\quad i=1,2,\dots,d.

(S47)

Here
C=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{{\cal N}_{\bm{k}}}(1-\cos 2\varepsilon t)
is positive.
We then define

\displaystyle~{\rm BIS}\equiv\{{\bm{k}}|h_{0}({\bm{k}})=0\}.

(S48)

and introduce a dynamical spin-texture field
\bm{\mathcal{G}}(\bm{k})=(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{G}_{d})

[
5
]
, whose components are given by

{\mathcal{G}_{i}(\bm{k})}\equiv-\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{g}}\partial_{k_{\perp}}{\langle\gamma_{i}\rangle}_{T}

(S49)

with
\mathcal{N}_{g}
being the normalization factor and
k_{\perp}
is the momentum perpendicular to BISs and pointing from the region
{h}_{0}<0
to
{h}_{0}>0
. It can be derived from Eq.(
S47
) that on the BIS,

{\mathcal{G}_{i}(\bm{k})}|_{{\bm{k}}\in\mathrm{BIS}}=\overline{Q_{i}(\bm{k})},

(S50)

with
\overline{Q_{i}(\bm{k})}={Q_{i}(\bm{k})}/\sqrt{\sum_{j}Q_{j}(\bm{k})^{2}}
. Therefore, due to the topological equivalence between
H
and
Q
, the line-gap topology of NH Hamiltonian can be characterized by a
(d-1)
D Chern number

C_{d-1}=\sum_{j}\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}}\frac{1}{(d-1)!}\int_{{\rm BIS}_{j}}{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}(\mathrm{d}{\bm{\mathcal{G}}})^{d-1}

(S51)

where
\tilde{\Gamma}(x)
is the Gamma function,
{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}(\mathrm{d}{\bm{\mathcal{G}}})^{d}\equiv\epsilon^{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{d+1}}\mathcal{G}_{i_{1}}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{G}_{i_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}\mathcal{G}_{i_{d+1}}

with
\epsilon^{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{d+1}}
being the fully antisymmetric tensor and
i_{1,2,\dots,d+1}\in\{0,1,2,\dots,d\}
,
and ‘
\mathrm{d}
’ denotes the exterior derivative.

However, for 1D systems, the dynamical characterization diverges significantly.
Rather than being polarized in
\gamma_{0}
, the initial state should be chosen as
\rho_{0}=(\mathbbm{1}-\Gamma)/{2}
. Suppose that
\gamma_{0}=\sigma_{x}
,
\gamma_{1}=\sigma_{y}
and
\Gamma=\mathbbm{i}\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}=-\sigma_{z}
. We have
\rho_{0}=(\mathbbm{1}+\sigma_{z})/{2}
and

\displaystyle~{\langle\sigma_{x}\rangle}_{T}=-C_{1}\frac{h_{y}}{\varepsilon^{2}},\quad{\langle\sigma_{y}\rangle}_{T}=C_{1}\frac{h_{x}}{\varepsilon^{2}},

(S52)

where
C_{1}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt{\cal N}_{k}^{-1}\left[-h^{\prime}_{z}+h^{\prime}_{z}\cos(2\varepsilon t)-\varepsilon\sin(2\varepsilon t)\right]
.
We employ the normalized TASTs

~\overline{\langle\sigma_{i}(k)\rangle}_{T}=\frac{{\langle\sigma_{i}(k)\rangle}_{T}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=x,y}\langle\sigma_{j}(k)\rangle_{T}^{2}}},\quad i=x,y,

(S53)

which define the dynamical field
{\bm{g}}(k)=(g_{x}(k),g_{y}(k))
as

\begin{split}g_{x}=\overline{\langle\sigma_{y}({k})\rangle}_{T},\quad g_{y}=-\overline{\langle\sigma_{x}({k})\rangle}_{T}.\end{split}

(S54)

The topology of the Hermitian
Q
matrix is characterized by the winding number of the dynamical field
{\bm{g}}(k)
:

\displaystyle~W_{1}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\rm BZ}d{k}\left[g_{x}(k)\partial_{{k}}g_{y}(k)-g_{y}(k)\partial_{{k}}g_{x}(k)\right].

(S55)

III
III. Summary of dynamical characterization methods

To summarize, we provide an overview of dynamical characterization methods employed in our measurement scheme for systems under PBCs. As emphasized throughout the discussion, a carefully selected initial state can significantly simplify the measurement process.
These choices are closely tied to the dimensionality and symmetry of the non-Hermitian system under consideration.
A summary of the selected initial states is provided in Table
S1
.

The differentiation in initial-state choices and methods stems from the fact that both the number and dimensionality of the
\gamma
matrices that constitute our model Hamiltonian
[refer to Eqs. (
S1
) and (
S43
)] strongly depend on the system’s dimensionality.
In particular, 1D systems are characterized by only three Pauli matrices and two energy bands.
The minimal degrees of freedom governing the quench dynamics necessitate the use of distinct initial states or even different characterization
methods for 1D systems as compared to higher-dimensional systems.

Furthermore, unlike systems with SLS, chiral-symmetric systems exhibit real energy spectra under conditions of weak dissipation.
This results in quench dynamics that remain unitary, rendering the situation analogous to the characterization of Hermitian systems
[
5
]
.
In such cases, spin oscillations on BISs are resonant and pronounced, while spin dynamics away from the BISs become negligible.
As a result, in higher-dimensional systems with chiral symmetry, dynamical characterizations are primarily based on measurements taken on the BISs
rather than across the entire BZ.

IV.3
C. Mapping from the BZ to the GBZ

In cold-atom experiments, measuring observables in quasi-momentum space, such as spin textures and energy dispersion
\varepsilon(k)
, is relatively straightforward.
Here we demonstrate that by utilizing these observables measured in the BZ, one can construct the dynamical field
{\bm{g}}(k_{G})
defined in the GBZ
to characterize non-Bloch topology, thus circumventing the challenging of measuring the real-space wave function.
In the following, we focus on 1D real-line-gapped systems with SLS, while the analysis can be extended to point-gapped or imaginary-line-gapped systems in a similar manner.

We assume the initial state
\ket{\psi_0(x)}
is prepared at the lattice center with spin
\ket{\uparrow}
, and during the evolution, the wave function keeps far away from the boundary.
Projecting the initial state into quasi-momentum space gives
\rho_{0}=\sum_{kk^{\prime}}p_{kk^{\prime}}\ket{k,\uparrow}\bra{k',\uparrow}
with
p_{kk^{\prime}}=\bra{k}\ket{\psi_0(x)}\bra{\psi_0(x)}\ket{k'}
.
After time
t
, we have

\displaystyle~\rho(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{kk^{\prime}}p_{kk^{\prime}}e^{-\mathbbm{i}H(k)t}|k,\uparrow\rangle\langle k^{\prime},\uparrow\!|e^{\mathbbm{i}H^{\dagger}(k)t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{kk^{\prime}}p_{kk^{\prime}}\left[\mathcal{A}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)|k,\uparrow\rangle\langle k^{\prime},\uparrow\!|+\mathcal{B}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)\theta_{k}|k,\downarrow\rangle\langle k^{\prime},\uparrow\!|+\mathcal{C}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)\theta^{*}_{k^{\prime}}|k,\uparrow\rangle\langle k^{\prime},\downarrow\!|+\mathcal{D}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)\theta_{k}\theta^{*}_{k^{\prime}}|k,\downarrow\rangle\langle k^{\prime},\downarrow\!|\right],

(S66)

where
\theta_{k}=b_{k}/a_{k}
represents the ratio of the spin components of the right eigenvector
|u^{\rm R}(k)\rangle=a_{k}\ket{k,\uparrow}+b_{k}\ket{k,\downarrow}
, and

~\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{A}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{1}{4}\left(e^{-\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)-\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}+e^{\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)+\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}+e^{-\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)+\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}+e^{\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)-\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}\right),\\
&\mathcal{B}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{1}{4}\left(e^{-\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)-\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}-e^{\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)+\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}+e^{-\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)+\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}-e^{\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)-\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}\right),\\
&\mathcal{C}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{1}{4}\left(e^{-\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)+\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}+e^{\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)+\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}-e^{-\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)+\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}-e^{\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)-\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}\right),\\
&\mathcal{D}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{1}{4}\left(e^{-\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)+\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}-e^{\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)+\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}-e^{-\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)+\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}+e^{\mathbbm{i}[\varepsilon(k)-\varepsilon^{*}(k^{\prime})]t}\right).\end{aligned}

(S67)

We first consider the simple case where the two complex-momentum bases are biorthogonal, i.e.,
\langle\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G})|\beta^{\rm R}(k^{\prime}_{G})\rangle=\delta_{k_{G},k^{\prime}_{G}}
. Here
|\beta^{\rm R}(k_{G})\rangle=L^{-1/2}\sum_{i}e^{\mathbbm{i}k_{G}x_{i}}\ket{x_i}
and
|\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G})\rangle=L^{-1/2}\sum_{i}e^{\mathbbm{i}k_{G}^{*}x_{i}}\ket{x_i}
. The time-evolving wavefunction projected onto the complex-momentum bases can be written as

\rho(t)=\sum_{k_{G}k^{\prime}_{G},\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}p_{k_{G}k^{\prime}_{G}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)|\beta^{\rm R}(k_{G}),\sigma\rangle\langle\beta^{\rm R}(k^{\prime}_{G}),\sigma^{\prime}|
.
We define

~T_{k_{G}k}\equiv\langle\beta^{\rm L}(k_{G})|k\rangle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i}e^{-\mathbbm{i}(k_{G}-k)x_{i}},

(S68)

which gives

p_{k_{G}k_{G}}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(t)=\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}T_{k_{G}k}p^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)T^{*}_{k_{G}k^{\prime}},

(S69)

where

~p^{\uparrow\uparrow}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)=p_{kk^{\prime}}{\cal A}_{kk^{\prime}}(t),\quad p^{\downarrow\uparrow}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)=p_{kk^{\prime}}{\cal B}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)\theta_{k},\quad p^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)=p_{kk^{\prime}}{\cal C}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)\theta^{*}_{k^{\prime}},\quad p^{\downarrow\downarrow}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)=p_{kk^{\prime}}{\cal D}_{kk^{\prime}}(t)\theta_{k}\theta^{*}_{k^{\prime}}.

(S70)

One can obtain LTSTs in the GBZ as

~\langle\sigma_{x}(k_{G})\rangle_{\infty}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{p_{k_{G}k_{G}}^{\uparrow\downarrow}(t)+p_{k_{G}k_{G}}^{\downarrow\uparrow}(t)}{p_{k_{G}k_{G}}^{\uparrow\uparrow}(t)+p_{k_{G}k_{G}}^{\downarrow\downarrow}(t)},\quad\langle\sigma_{y}(k_{G})\rangle_{\infty}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbbm{i}\frac{p_{k_{G}k_{G}}^{\uparrow\downarrow}(t)-p_{k_{G}k_{G}}^{\downarrow\uparrow}(t)}{p_{k_{G}k_{G}}^{\uparrow\uparrow}(t)+p_{k_{G}k_{G}}^{\downarrow\downarrow}(t)},\quad\langle\sigma_{z}(k_{G})\rangle_{\infty}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{p_{k_{G}k_{G}}^{\uparrow\uparrow}(t)-p_{k_{G}k_{G}}^{\downarrow\downarrow}(t)}{p_{k_{G}k_{G}}^{\uparrow\uparrow}(t)+p_{k_{G}k_{G}}^{\downarrow\downarrow}(t)}.

(S71)

As discussed in the above subsection, the limit
t\to\infty
can be approximated as
t>\tau^{*}
, where
\tau^{*}
denotes a finite time satisfying
\tau^{*}\gg 1/\eta
.
With the result in Eq. (
S71
),
one can further derive both the normalized and deformed LTSTs as defined in Eq. (
S7
) and Eq. (
I.2
), respectively.
The dynamical field
{\bm{g}}(k_{G})
can then be constructed to characterize the topology.

(S75)
