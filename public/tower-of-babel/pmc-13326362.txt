Social information processing and peer interaction in gifted preschool children
Authors: Fatma Betül Şenol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuring early childhood, the foundation for social information processing skills is laid, enabling children to understand their social environment and interactions with peers and adapt to them. Although social interaction skills have generally been studied in young children, there are limited studies that directly address the relationship between social information processing and social interaction in gifted children. Therefore, the development of early interventions to support the social adaptation of gifted children is of critical importance. This study aims to examine the relationship between social information processing and social interaction skills in gifted children during the early developmental period.MethodsThe study was designed as a cross-sectional analysis. This study relied on convenience sampling, a type of purposive sampling technique. Thirty gifted children were included in the investigation. The study employed the Social Information Processing Interview–Preschool Version and the Interaction Rating Scale between Children.ResultsThe study found a significant and positive connection between the Benign Intent aspect of social information processing skills in gifted children and their cooperation and assertiveness abilities in relationships. A significant and positive correlation was found between the positive response structure of social information processing skills and the self-regulation dimension of interaction skills.ConclusionsGifted children’s advanced ability to interact positively in social situations plays a significant role in fostering unity within their peer groups and strengthening their confident communication abilities. Having strong self-regulation abilities affects one’s capacity to create positive responses.Keywords: Gifted children, Interaction, Social information processing, Preschool children

Introduction

As the consequences of individual variations in development and education become increasingly important in today’s society, a holistic approach to the developmental characteristics of gifted children is imperative. Gifted children exhibit remarkable abilities and potential in one or more areas [1, 2]. Gifted children display various characteristics that distinguish them from their peers who have not been gifted. These characteristics are obvious in cognitive [3, 4], linguistic [5], social-emotional [6, 7], and psychomotor [8, 9] development. Gifted children are unusually inquisitive, extremely curious [10], and highly assertiveness in comparison to their contemporaries [11]. Alongside these abilities, individuals also possess social maturity, empathy, a well-developed sense of humour [2], the ability to learn quickly, a robust memory [12], and advanced mathematical skills [13]. The developmental characteristics of gifted children are multifaceted and complex. Beyond their cognitive abilities, these children demonstrate unique skills in the area of social and emotional development. These skills are evident in the social interactions they establish with their peers [14, 15].

Children who are gifted typically display these traits from an early age. Children with exceptional abilities often exhibit high levels of inquisitiveness from an early age [16, 17]. Participating in a discovery cycle leads to heightened interaction with their environment [18, 19]. At an early stage, students acquire essential academic skills, such as mathematics [20], reading, and writing [21]. Individuals possess highly advanced metacognitive skills [22]. Individuals exhibiting these characteristics possess robust memory, swiftly absorb information, and are capable of sustained concentration [23, 24]. The children of these families develop social skills earlier than their peers [2]. They are typically more cooperative, willing to share information, and compliant [25, 26]. They challenge rules that could negatively impact their social integration [27]. As children develop, their moral awareness grows, and the visibility of behaviors like justice, equality, and compassionate actions that align with societal values becomes more obvious [28, 29].

Gifted children, due to their advanced cognitive development, are typically able to evaluate the social problems they face at a young age, respond constructively, and tend to examine the underlying cause of the issue. These characteristics may also facilitate SIP progression. Social information processing is a model that governs social adaptation and behaviour, comprising a sequence of steps shaped by children’s responses to the situations they encounter [30]. This cyclical model describes a six-step cognitive process for the formation of a behavioural response [31]. People encode and interpret social clues in their first two steps before developing a response to social situations. In the third step, the encoded cues are used to create solutions for the encountered situation. In step four, the participants retrieve a suitable behavioural response from memory; in step five, they choose an appropriate response; and in step six, they assess their responses [32, 33]. The social information processing mechanisms of gifted children may differ due to their cognitive advantages. Thanks to these characteristics, gifted children are able to process and analyse social cues from their environment more quickly [34].

Gifted children’s sensitive interactions with their environment can be negatively impacted by their earlier development of advanced social skills and deeper understanding of their surroundings [35, 36]. The continuity and quality of social interaction are shaped by gifted children’s ability to regulate and guide their behaviour to some degree. Therefore, self-regulation skills need to be assessed in gifted children [37, 38]. Gifted children often exhibit exceptional cognitive abilities in comparison to their peers, but they may struggle with social and emotional challenges, including processing social information and interacting with their peers [39, 40]. According to the social information processing theory, the process involves perceiving social cues, interpreting them, responding to them, and selecting an appropriate behaviour based on information from the child’s surroundings [31]. Deficiencies in social information processing or cognitive distortions can harm the quality of social interactions and a child’s ability to adapt in social situations. Failing to comprehend the connection between social interaction and information processing in gifted young children may result in the underlying causes of this group’s difficulties with social adaptation being overlooked. In gifted children, this situation is referred to as asynchronous development. Asynchronous development in gifted children is characterised by progress at different rates across cognitive, emotional, social and physical domains, and is explained in particular by the fact that cognitive development does not proceed in parallel with social-emotional development [41, 42]. If asynchronous development is overlooked, the full potential of gifted children may not be realised, and they may experience various adjustment difficulties. Indeed, the literature indicates that this mismatch between cognitive and socio-emotional development can lead to difficulties in peer relationships, social isolation and adjustment problems [17, 43, 44]. This situation has been found to be associated with outcomes such as peer rejection, exposure to bullying, and difficulties in social interaction among gifted children [45, 46].

Examining the link between interaction and social information processing skills in gifted youngsters during early childhood is expected to provide significant theoretical and practical insights for developing educational and intervention programs that aid in the social-emotional development of gifted children [47]. This study seeks to investigate the connection between social information processing and interaction skills in gifted children during the early years of childhood.

Hypothesis

There is a correlation between socially information processing and interaction skills in gifted children.

Method

Research design

Conducted as a cross-sectional study, this type of research involves a single time point, capturing a snapshot of findings and associated variables. Cross-sectional studies aim to explore the connections between risk factors and outcomes [48].

Study group

A purposeful sampling method known as convenience sampling was used. The research was conducted in the kindergarten section of a special education institution that offers special education programs exclusively for gifted children in the Central Anatolia region of Turkey. The study group includes all accessible participants suitable for the research. The study group consisted of 30 children who met the eligibility requirements for a gifted assessment and were currently in kindergarten. In this study, the identification of giftedness was not carried out by the researchers; participants were identified as part of the standard admission process at the institution where the research was conducted. In this process, face-to-face interviews are used alongside psychometric tools such as the WISC-R, WISC-IV, Stanford-Binet or the Anadolu-Sak Intelligence Scale (ASIS) to assess the students’ cognitive and developmental characteristics. Furthermore, during the assessment process, information obtained from the family regarding the child’s developmental history, interests, and areas of ability is also taken into account. All participants underwent the same standardised, multi-stage assessment process administered by the institution. This enhances the ecological validity of the study, as the sample comprises individuals identified under real-life conditions. However, the use of different assessment tools, and in particular the normative limitations of the WISC-R, were identified as a limitation of the study and discussed in the relevant section. The Study was conducted using a convenience sampling method and a cross-sectional design from a single institution serving gifted children. This may limit external validity and generalisability. Post hoc power analyses were performed using G*Power 3.1 software. Although Spearman correlation coefficients were used in the analyses, power estimations were conducted using the bivariate normal approximation. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). The absence of a comparison group consisting of typically developing peers limits the interpretability of the findings. Therefore, the results obtained should be considered as preliminary and exploratory findings rather than definitive conclusions. In the study involving 30 children, 53.3% of the participants were boys (n = 16) and 46.7% were girls (n = 14). The participants’ mean age was 68 months, having all attended preschool for three years.

Data collection tools

The data collection tools included a personal information form, a social information processing process test, and a child-to-child interaction scale.

Personal information form

Researchers designed the form. The information includes the children’s gender, age, and the amount of time they have been attending preschool.

Social information processing test - preschool form

The test was created to understand social information processing in preschool children [49]. The Social Information Processing Interview-Preschool Version (SIPI-P) developed by Ziv and Sorongon [49] was used in this study. The Turkish version of the SIPI-P was adapted and validated by Şenol and Metin [50], and this version was employed for all participants. The SIPI-P comprises four narratives and corresponding questions. The first and third stories centre around a child bullied by a peer. The second and fourth scenes depict a child’s efforts to participate in a game already in progress with two other children. The test has different versions for boys and girls, with identical stories and matching illustrations. The subscales of the social information processing test are ‘Benign Intent, Positive Response Construction, And Positive Response Decision’. The social information processing test and its subscales were developed by Ziv & Sorongon [49].

Three sub-dimensions of SIPI-P are used in this study. It is stated that these scores have an important place in predicting the social behavior of children. The three sub-dimensions are: interpretation of cues, response construction, and response decision. Interpreting cues subdimension reveals Benign Intent of children. The high scores the children received, representing the absence of a hostile attribution bias, show that they do not have a hostile intention. The range for this score is between 0 and 4.

In the positive response construction score sub-dimension, children are asked an open-ended question. Answers given by the children are coded as Sufficient (1 point), Inadequate / Introverted, and Aggressive / Aggressive (0 point) and Other-Don’t Know: (-1 point). The range for this score is between 0 and 8. Increasing scores show children’s positive response construction.

The response decision sub-dimension determines the positive response score. Children are presented with types of responses to the social situation in the story, and they are asked to evaluate these responses. The scores that children get from this section are between 0 and 36.

High scores in this sub-dimension indicate that children evaluate social situations positively. SIPI-P’s internal consistency reliability is 0.76 for the interpretation of cues, 0.78 for the response construction and 0.87 for the response decision [49].

Interaction Rating Scale for Children-IRSC

The scale assesses children’s interactions with their peers within classroom environments [51]. It was developed by Anme et al. [51] and adapted into Turkish by Şenol and Metin [52]. The IRSC is a multidimensional assessment tool that examines the peer relationships of children through the lens of their social abilities and interactions in everyday situations. The scale is applied to children aged 3–18. However, the observation period varies depending on the age group in the activity being observed. The scale is applied to children of all age groups during free play time, unstructured group activities, or work hours. Children are observed individually for at least five minutes and up to the completion of the activity via camera recording [51]. The IRSC comprises two sections.

IRSC consists of two sections. The first section is the “Behavior Assessment” section, which consists of 43 items and three sub-dimensions. The Cooperation sub-dimension consists of 20 items and observes children’s cooperative and empathetic behaviors. The Self-Regulation sub-dimension consists of 10 items and observes children’s behaviors in situations of disagreement. The Aggressiveness sub-dimension consists of 13 items and observes children’s entrepreneurial behaviors. During the observation process, if the children exhibited the specified behavior, it was coded as “yes (1 point)”; if they did not exhibit it, it was coded as “no (0 points)”. Children receive a maximum of 43 points and a minimum of 0 points from the first section of the IRSC.

The second section of the scale is the “Impression Assessment” section, which consists of three sub-dimensions and three items. The sub-dimensions are: Adjustment and adaptation (Harmony with friends is observed), Assertiveness (The child’s assertive behaviors are observed), and Group Solidarity (The visibility of dynamics among friends is observed). The impression evaluation section determines the researcher’s impression of the child. The researcher’s impressions in this section are coded using a five-point scale: “Not at all apparent (1), Not apparent (2), Neutral/Undecided (3), Apparent (4), Quite apparent (5)”. Children score between a minimum of 3 and a maximum of 15 points on the impression assessment section of the scale. The internal consistency coefficient of the original scale is 0.87, and the inter-rater reliability is 0.90 [51].

Data collection

Data were collected by the teachers of the children participating in the study. The researcher initially trained the teachers in using the scales. During the training, continuous communication was maintained regarding potential problems and questions that teachers might encounter during the implementation process, and necessary guidance was provided. The researcher provided ongoing consultation and guidance to support the proper execution of the implementation. All teachers were trained in advance on the correct and consistent application and scoring of the scales and maintained constant communication with the researcher during the application process to address questions and issues. Standardization of application and scoring among evaluators was achieved through training and a collaborative problem-solving process. The Social Information Processing Test was individually administered to the children by their teachers in a quiet, distraction-free setting. The administration process took approximately 20 min for each child. Conversely, Interaction Rating Scale between Children was administered by observing the children individually during their free play time. During the free play period, the children were observed individually in succession.

Data analysis

The data collected in the study were examined using IBM SPSS Statistics v25 software programme. Statistics were calculated for the variables, including the mean, standard deviation, median, and quartile values (25th–75th percentiles) for continuous variables, as well as frequency and percentage distributions for categorical variables.

The normality of the data distribution characteristics was evaluated by examining the parametric assumptions (Shapiro-Wilk Test), and the variables did not conform to a normal distribution. Hence, nonparametric methods were employed to investigate the variables’ interrelationships.

Spearman’s rho correlation analysis was employed to establish the relationship between the continuous data variables. The correlation coefficients (r) and their corresponding p-values were determined. Due to zero variance (SD = 0.00), the variable “Positive response decision” was excluded from inferential and correlational analyses and is reported only descriptively. To control the risk of Type I error due to multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Results meeting the adjusted significance criterion were considered statistically significant. The alpha level was set at a value less than 0.05.

Discussion

This study examined the correlation between gifted children’s social information processing and interaction skills. The study found a positive and significant correlation between the benign intent sub-dimension of social information processing skills and the assertiveness (r = 0.406, p = 0.026) sub-dimension of interaction skills. A positive and significant correlation was also found between the positive response construction of social information processing skills and the self-control sub-dimension of interaction skills (r = 0.565, p = 0.001). In addition to these findings, it is observed that the median scores obtained by gifted children on the subscales of the assessment tools are close to the maximum score. The high scores that gifted children received on the subscales of social information processing and social interaction skills suggest that these skills may be strong in these children. However, due to the absence of a typically developing control group in the study, the generalizability of these findings is limited and should be interpreted only within the context of the gifted child sample examined. Previous research indicates that typically developing children may exhibit different subscale scores in social information processing [49, 53] and social interaction [51, 53, 54] skills. In this context, the results obtained in the current study have been evaluated within the framework of the relevant literature and suggest that similar or different patterns may be present in the gifted sample.

Skills such as social information processing and interaction are associated with social skills. Children’s mental, emotional, and academic development heavily rely on the possession of adequate social skills. Children at risk, including gifted children, are significantly influenced by their social skills.

The results of the current study suggest that the responses of gifted children to social situations may be related to social information processing. According to social information processing theory, children’s behaviour in social settings is closely related to how they encode and interpret social cues. It has been noted that children who can correctly perceive and interpret social cues develop more positive intent patterns and exhibit more adaptive response patterns [55]. In this context, the advanced cognitive capacities of gifted children may contribute to their ability to process cues in social situations more quickly and accurately. Indeed, studies in the literature indicate that gifted children demonstrate strong abilities in perceiving and interpreting social signals [56–60]. This suggests that the social information processing abilities of gifted children may influence the quality of their social interactions with their peers.

The results of this study suggest that self-control skills in gifted children may be related to social information processing. Self-control, cooperation, assertiveness, and group solidarity are considered fundamental components of social competence that enable children to interact effectively in social settings [61]. Self-control skills, in particular, are an important factor determining children’s capacity to regulate their emotions and behaviour in social settings. The positive response patterns observed in this study may be related to the advanced self-control skills of gifted children. Indeed, the literature indicates that gifted children may demonstrate stronger skills in managing their emotions and understanding others’ perspectives during social interactions [62]. Furthermore, it has been reported that children with high cognitive capacity can regulate their behaviour and emotions by using problem-solving strategies when faced with negative social situations [63–65]. This suggests that there may be a reciprocal relationship between social information processing and self-control skills. Advanced self-control skills may contribute to children interpreting social cues more accurately and developing more constructive response strategies. Findings in the literature showing that gifted children tend to express positive emotions and exhibit more cooperative behaviour in social interactions also support this interpretation [35]. However, some studies also suggest that gifted children may be more sensitive to difficulties in social settings due to their advanced cognitive and emotional awareness [35, 66]. Studies involving typically developing children have indicated a relationship between self-control and social cognitive processing [53, 67]. However, it was observed that this relationship differs partially in gifted children. In this context, the results obtained in the current study have been evaluated within the framework of the relevant literature, without relying on a direct comparison. This result suggests that self-control skills play a more pronounced and effective role in the social information processing of gifted children, indicating that social interaction and behavior regulation mechanisms in this group may differ from those of their peers. Therefore, self-control skills can be considered an important mechanism supporting social adjustment. The findings of the current study suggest that social information processing and self-control processes may form a mutually reinforcing structure and that both mechanisms may play a role in the social interactions of gifted children. Furthermore, this finding cannot be explained solely by the cognitive capacities of gifted children. It has been noted that the executive functions observed in gifted children are influenced by the prefrontal cortex [68]. This indicates that the neurological underpinnings play an effective role in the self-control and social information processing stages of gifted children. Emotional sensitivity is another factor influencing the relationship between self-control and social information processing skills in gifted children. Children with high emotional intensity may be more sensitive to environmental stimuli in the situations they encounter [69]. As gifted children have high emotional sensitivity, it is thought that this may influence their self-control and social information processing skills. In addition to all this, metacognitive skills enhance self-control by influencing an individual’s ability to manage their own learning process. In this regard, the relationship between the level of metacognitive awareness observed in gifted children and self-control is significant, and it facilitates gifted children’s ability to achieve their goals in social settings [70]. The findings from the current study and those reported in the literature suggest that the relationship between self-control and social information processing skills in gifted children has a multi-layered structure when neurological characteristics such as sensory sensitivity and contextual factors are taken into account.

The results of this study suggest that assertiveness skills in gifted children may be related to social information processing. Assertiveness is associated with the capacity to initiate and maintain social interactions and is considered one of the fundamental components of social skills [71]. The positive social response patterns observed in our study may be related to the cognitive advantages of gifted children in interpreting social situations and developing appropriate responses. The literature indicates that children who respond appropriately and positively to social situations are more accepted by their peers, which contributes to the maintenance of social interactions [55]. Conversely, hostile response patterns have been reported to be associated with antisocial behaviour and to negatively affect social interactions [72]. In this context, interpreting social situations positively and strengthening the tendency towards Benign Intent may contribute to the development of children’s social and assertiveness skills [73]. However, the existing literature shows that there are limited studies directly examining the relationship between assertiveness skills and social information processing in gifted children. Therefore, it is thought that the findings obtained may contribute to the literature on understanding the social interactions of gifted children. The ability to accurately interpret peers’ intentions in social situations is closely related to cognitive capacity. Given the advanced cognitive abilities of gifted children, it seems likely that these children can assess social situations more accurately and, as a result, exhibit more assertive and cooperative behaviour in social interactions. Studies involving typically developing children have demonstrated a significant relationship between social information processing and assertiveness skills [53, 74, 75]. When evaluated in light of the current literature, these results suggest that there may also be a significant relationship between social information processing and assertiveness skills in a sample of gifted children. However, our current findings suggest that this relationship is more pronounced in gifted children; this suggests that advanced cognitive capacities enhance the processes of evaluating social situations and developing appropriate responses. In this context, it is believed that proactive and collaborative behavioral patterns may be prominent in the social interactions of gifted children. In light of all these findings, the relationship between assertiveness and social cognitive processing skills in gifted children must be addressed using a multidimensional approach. In particular, developmental and neurological differences render the interaction between these two skills more complex and multidimensional. Developmental characteristics (e.g. age-related cognitive maturity) [76] and neurological differences (e.g. hypersensitivity) [77] determine the dynamics of this relationship.

On the other hand, group solidarity also stands out as an important social dynamic that shapes peer relationships. Group solidarity is related to the quality of relationships between group members and the level of compliance with group norms [78]. It has been suggested that gifted children’s advanced self-awareness and social sensitivity skills may contribute to their better perception of group norms and stronger bonds with their peers [79, 80]. Indeed, the literature indicates that increased group solidarity is associated with the development of more positive intent patterns among group members [81]. In this context, it is thought that gifted children’s advanced cognitive and social skills may be a factor that supports group solidarity. It is also noted that children’s social situations shape their goodwill skills in relation to social behavior. Previous studies have indicated that Benign Intent are associated with prosocial behaviors such as cooperation in children, while hostile intents are associated with aggressive behaviors [31, 61]. Arioli et al. [62] concluded that understanding others’ intentions, one of the social cognition processes, plays a central role in planning social behaviors. It has been found that the presence of benevolent or hostile behavior increases cooperative behavior [63]. Caproro et al. [64] concluded that benevolent behaviors are associated with cooperative behaviors and that people cooperate more when they are encouraged to think benevolently. Furthermore, a study investigating how motivated cognitive processes affect cooperative behavior found that individuals’ expectations and perceptions of norms in a cooperative environment increase cooperative behavior [65]. Studies conducted with typically developing children indicate that cooperative behaviors are associated with prosocial behaviors [35, 53]. In light of these results, the results obtained in the current study suggest that there may be a relationship between social information processes and tendencies toward cooperative and altruistic behaviors. This suggests that such behavioral patterns in the social interactions of gifted children may be related to cognitive and social information processing.

Although most of the observed associations demonstrated adequate statistical power, some correlations with smaller effect sizes yielded relatively lower power values. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with larger sample sizes are recommended.

The emergence of zero variance (ceiling effect) in the ‘positive response decision’ sub-dimension of the Social Information Processing Test, obtained without any prior training, is a notable finding. This result implies that all participants achieved the highest score and that no variability was observed. When evaluating this result, it is necessary to consider that whilst they typically demonstrate performance far ahead of their peers in cognitive domains, their emotional and social development does not progress at the same pace—that is, they exhibit asynchronous development. This asynchronous development may lead to the formation of distinctive patterns in their information processing during social interactions [82]. This situation may result in the formation of specific patterns in gifted children’s positive response decision -making skills. A child who can rapidly analyse social situations cognitively may exhibit more sensitive or more compliant emotional responses compared to their peers. This may lead to homogenisation in the positive response decision sub-dimension and the emergence of a ceiling effect. Due to this asynchrony, whilst they may exhibit homogenised responses in some areas (e.g., positive response decision), they may demonstrate greater diversity in others (e.g., emotional regulation or peer relationships) [83, 84]. The emergence of a ceiling effect in the ‘positive response decision’ subscale may be linked to the perfectionist tendencies of gifted children. Due to their perfectionist nature, gifted children may prefer to display the ideal response in social settings [85]. This, in turn, may lead to all students achieving the highest score in the ‘positive response decision’ subscale.

Conclusion

This study aimed to examine the relationships between social cognitive processing and social interaction skills in gifted children. The findings suggest that the “Benign Intent” dimension of social information processing may be significantly and positively associated with assertiveness, whilst the “Positive response construction” dimension may be significantly and positively associated with self-control. These findings indicate that there may be a relationship between the ways in which gifted children interpret social situations and their social interaction behaviours. In particular, it is thought that a tendency to evaluate social situations within the “Benign Intent” framework, alongside positive response constructions, may be observed in conjunction with social interaction skills such as assertiveness and self-control. In this regard, it can be argued that social information processing may serve as a relational variable that should be considered when understanding the social adaptation and peer relationships of gifted children. The emergence of zero variance (ceiling effect) in the positive response decision sub-dimension of the Social Information Processing Test obtained from the study can be linked to the asynchronous development, perfectionist tendencies and developmental characteristics of gifted children. In conclusion, this study provides an empirical contribution to the literature regarding the relationships between the social cognitive processes and social interaction skills of gifted children. The findings suggest that considering gifted children not only in terms of their cognitive characteristics but also in terms of their social information-processing processes may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of their social development.

Limitations and recommendations

This study was conducted on gifted children in the preschool period. However, the failure to identify gifted children in the preschool period in Turkey resulted in an insufficient sample size. It is recommended that gifted children in the preschool period be identified and receive the necessary interventions. The study was conducted using a convenience sampling method and a cross-sectional design from a single institution serving gifted children. This may limit external validity and generalisability. The relatively small sample size (n = 30) may have reduced statistical power and increased the risk of sampling bias. The absence of a comparison group consisting of typically developing peers limits the interpretability of the findings. Therefore, the results obtained should be considered as preliminary and exploratory findings rather than definitive conclusions. In future research, the inclusion of larger and more heterogeneous samples from different institutions will increase generalisability. Furthermore, longitudinal and multi-centre studies will help to strengthen causal inferences in this field. a ceiling effect was detected in the Positive Response Decision variable, which may limit the interpretability of correlations involving this measure. This study examined the relationship between gifted children’s social information processing and interaction skills. Different measurement tools and typically developing children were included in the study for comparison. This study was designed using quantitative measurement tools. The obtained data could be diversified by including qualitative measurement tools in the study. In this study, the identification of giftedness was not carried out by the researchers; participants were identified as part of the institution’s standard admission process. The use of various intelligence tests with different norms (WISC-R, WISC-IV and ASIS) is considered a limitation of the study, as this may lead to heterogeneity within the sample. The use of different psychometric tools (WISC-R, WISC-IV, Stanford-Binet, ASIS) in this process, and particularly the normative limitations of the WISC-R, constitute a potential limitation. Furthermore, whilst the inclusion of information obtained from families in the assessment offers a multi-source approach, it may increase inter-instrument variability. Conversely, the fact that all participants were selected from the same institutional process supports sample homogeneity and strengthens the study’s ecological validity. In this study, data were collected by teachers who had been observing the children in their natural environments for a long time. Whilst this strengthens ecological validity, potential observer bias specific to teachers cannot be entirely ruled out. However, the presence of external observers was considered to potentially influence children’s natural behaviour and the flow of the educational setting. Similarly, the administration of the Social Information Processing Test by teachers may encourage children to provide more natural responses; however, the likelihood of providing socially desirable responses was assessed as a limitation. A limitation of the study is that inter-rater reliability analyses could not be conducted because the data were collected by a single administrator (teacher). As the fact that the data was collected by teachers may increase the likelihood of observer bias and the social desirability effect, this should be considered a limitation of the study.
