Viral–bacterial codetection and clinical and laboratory characteristics in children hospitalized with lower respiratory tract infections in a private hospital in Lima, Peru: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ana F. Rojas-Cosi, David R. Soriano-Moreno, Anderson N. Soriano-Moreno, Nelson Díaz, Fernando Bobadilla, Jorge Quinto, Alex Dutra, Gabriele Oliveira, Jorge Alave
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundViral–bacterial codetection is common and may increase clinical severity, but evidence in the Peruvian pediatric population is limited. The objective of the present study was to evaluate the clinical, laboratory, and seasonal characteristics associated with viral–bacterial codetection in children hospitalized for lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs).MethodsCross-sectional secondary database study from a private hospital in Lima, Peru. We included patients < 13 years hospitalized for LRTIs with RT-qPCR results for respiratory viruses and/or bacteria. Viral–bacterial codetection was compared against other detection patterns using Poisson regression for binary outcomes and linear regression for continuous outcomes, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons.ResultsA total of 548 patients were included (median age 2.0 years; 50.5% female). Viral–bacterial codetection was identified in 21.5% of patients (n = 118), with RSV + Haemophilus influenzae being the most frequent combination. Compared with other detection patterns, viral–bacterial codetection was significantly associated with a higher prevalence of crackles (aPR: 1.30; 95% CI: 1.08–1.57), lower oxygen saturation at admission (β: −0.57; 95% CI: −1.04 to − 0.10), higher platelet counts (β: 30,452; 95% CI: 5,113–55,792), higher hemoglobin levels (β: 0.29 g/dL; 95% CI: 0.03–0.56), and longer hospital stay (β: 0.66 days; 95% CI: 0.02–1.29). However, after FDR correction for multiple comparisons, none of these associations reached statistical significance (q-values: 0.056–0.113). No difference was detected according to seasonality.ConclusionsViral–bacterial codetection was common and was associated with crackles, lower oxygen saturation, longer hospital stay, and higher platelet counts; however, after FDR correction, none of these associations remained statistically significant, underscoring the exploratory nature of these findings and the need for larger, confirmatory studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40794-026-00303-5.

Introduction

Lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) are the second leading cause of death among children under five years of age worldwide [1] and are particularly prevalent in developing countries [2, 3]. Globally, an estimated 5,750 episodes of LRTIs and 76.2 deaths occur per 100,000 children under five [4]. In Peru, during 2023, there were 493 cases of acute respiratory infections per 1,000 children under five, as well as 7.6 cases of pneumonia per 1,000, of which 31.5% required hospitalization, approaching pre-pandemic levels [5].

The etiology of LRTIs can be viral or bacterial, and viral–bacterial codetection has been reported to occur in approximately 19% [6]. Commonly detected pathogens include respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza viruses, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Haemophilus influenzae [6–10], although the most frequent combinations vary across settings and study populations. Codetection may arise when a viral infection damages the respiratory epithelium and alters mucociliary clearance, facilitating bacterial colonization and exacerbating pulmonary inflammation [11], leading to greater clinical severity, prolonged hospital stays, and higher mortality [12]. A study in Ohio reported a higher frequency of viral codetections in children (18%) compared to adults (2.8%), with the prevalence of multiple viral infections ranging from 11% to 56% in children and 5.8% to 8% in adults [13]. Codetection is more frequent in spring (32.2%) and summer (29.4%), and increases with age, from 18.5% in infants to 37.7% in children aged 6 to 14 years [8].

Previous studies in adults have linked codetection with factors such as nephropathy, lack of COVID-19 vaccination, SARS-CoV-2 infection, lower blood pressure at admission, or longer hospital stay [14]. However, evidence in the pediatric population remains limited. Most studies in this age group focus solely on describing the frequency of codetection [6, 13, 15], and few analyze in detail the associated factors, including clinical features, laboratory findings, and seasonality. This lack of information limits the development of diagnostic and therapeutic strategies tailored to this population. Differentiating between viral, bacterial, or mixed infections is essential in pediatrics to guide appropriate antibiotic use, hospitalization, and monitoring. In the absence of evidence, the risk of inappropriate treatment and antimicrobial resistance increases. Generating local evidence is crucial, as the epidemiology of respiratory pathogens, seasonal circulation, and clinical characteristics may vary across countries.

In this context, the objective of the present study was to evaluate the association between clinical characteristics, laboratory findings, and seasonality with viral–bacterial codetection in pediatric patients hospitalized for LRTIs in a private hospital in Lima, Peru.

Methodology

Study design

An analytical cross-sectional study was conducted using a secondary database. The primary study had a descriptive objective and aimed to evaluate the clinical and epidemiological characteristics of the study population [16]. The present study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines [17].

Population and sample

This study used clinical and laboratory data from pediatric patients admitted to hospitalization in Clínica Good Hope, located in the district of Miraflores, Lima, Peru, between 2019 and 2022. All records of patients younger than 13 years who were admitted with a clinical diagnosis of LRTI, required hospitalization outside critical care areas, and underwent viral and bacterial detection testing were included. The focus on hospitalized patients allowed the assessment of more severe cases of LRTI with better access to clinical and microbiological data. Patients with incomplete data for variables of interest were excluded. As this was a secondary data–based study, no sample size calculation was performed. However, statistical power was calculated considering a reference codetection proportion of 30% and detectable differences in proportion of symptoms of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%, yielding statistical power values of 0.17, 0.50, 0.82, and 0.97, respectively.

Variables

The dependent variable was viral–bacterial codetection, defined according to the results obtained from reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) assays performed on nasopharyngeal swab samples for respiratory viruses and bacteria using GENOMICA’s Clinical Array Technology, CLART®. Viral and bacterial RT-qPCR results were obtained from tests requested as part of routine clinical care and institutional diagnostic practice and were not performed specifically for this study. For bacterial detection, the PneumoCLART® bacteria kit was used, which identifies Bordetella parapertussis (BPP), Bordetella pertussis (BP), Bordetella spp., Bordetella holmesii, Bordetella bronchiseptica, Haemophilus influenzae, Haemophilus spp., Moraxella catarrhalis (MC), Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP), Streptococcus pneumoniae (SP), and Staphylococcus aureus. Viral detection was performed using the CLART® Fast PneumoVir kit, which identifies adenovirus, bocavirus, coronavirus, enterovirus, H1N1, H3N2, influenza A–C, metapneumovirus A–B, parainfluenza virus types 1–4, rhinovirus, and RSV A–B.

Independent variables included clinical characteristics, laboratory parameters, and seasonality. Clinical variables comprised fever, oxygen saturation, cough, dyspnea, odynophagia, diarrhea, vomiting, rhinorrhea, hyporexia, otalgia, sneezing, seizures, subcostal retractions, rhonchi, and wheezing. Laboratory variables included hemoglobin, leukocyte count, neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets, and C-reactive protein. Seasonality was categorized as summer (December 21 to March 20), autumn (March 20 to June 21), winter (June 21 to September 22), and spring (September 23 to December 20).

Covariates included age (years), sex (female, male), history of previous respiratory illness, pre-pandemic period (before March 16, 2020), post-pandemic period (after April 4, 2022), and the pandemic period defined as the interval between these two dates.

Procedures

As the study was based on a review of medical records, no active participant enrollment was conducted. Patients were identified based on ICD-10 codes corresponding to LRTI. Data were manually collected and entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. For the present study, the database was cleaned by applying inclusion and exclusion criteria and removing missing or implausible values.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). First, a descriptive analysis of clinical, laboratory, and seasonal characteristics of pediatric patients hospitalized for LRTIs was conducted. Patients were classified as: (1) no pathogen detected, (2) viral detection only, bacterial detection only, or viral–viral codetection, and (3) viral–bacterial codetection. Categorical variables were summarized using absolute and relative frequencies, while numerical variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Bivariate comparisons were performed to evaluate differences between patients with viral–bacterial codetection and those with other detection patterns. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for continuous variables, and Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. For the primary analyses, Poisson regression models with robust variance were used to estimate crude (cPR) and adjusted prevalence ratios (aPR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for binary clinical outcomes, comparing patients with viral–bacterial codetection versus those with other detection patterns (reference group: viral detection only, bacterial detection only, or viral–viral codetection; n = 300). Additionally, linear regression models with robust standard errors were used to estimate mean differences (β coefficients with 95% CI) for continuous laboratory outcomes and length of hospital stay. All adjusted models included age, sex, period, season and prior history of respiratory illness as covariates. To avoid unstable estimates, only binary outcomes with more than 10 events were included in the regression analyses. Given the conceptual differences between groups of outcomes (clinical symptoms, laboratory markers, and clinical severity indicators), false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied separately within each group. This approach accounts for the natural grouping of hypotheses and avoids overly conservative adjustments that may arise when applying FDR across heterogeneous sets of tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for conventional comparisons; q-values are reported for contextual interpretation of multiple testing.

Ethical considerations

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of Universidad Peruana Unión (No. 135-M-2025/UPEU-FCS-CF), ensuring the protection of data confidentiality. The study posed no harm and did not compromise participants’ health, upholding respect for dignity and the protection of rights and well-being, in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Of the 612 patients initially recorded in the database, 64 were excluded due to incomplete outcome data. A total of 548 pediatric patients were included in the study (Fig. 1). Included and not included children were similar across most evaluated characteristics, although they differed in the period of care, season, frequency of fever and odynophagia, and length of hospitalization (Supplementary Table 1). Analyses comparing types of detection were restricted to the 418 patients with at least one identified pathogen, excluding 130 patients with no detected pathogen.

General characteristics

The median age was 2.0 years (IQR: 0.0–5.0), with the majority being younger than 5 years (74.6%). Sex distribution was balanced between males (49.5%) and females (50.5%). Most patients were from West Lima (45.1%), were hospitalized during autumn (34.9%) and winter (26.3%), and were admitted during the pre-pandemic period (70.8%). A history of prior respiratory illness was reported in 32.5% of patients. The most frequent clinical symptoms were cough (96.7%), dyspnea (59.1%), and wheezing (54.9%). Crackles were present in 53.6% of patients, and subcostal retractions in 19.2%. The median hospital stay was 6.0 days (IQR: 5.0–7.0) (Table 1).

Among bacterial pathogens, Haemophilus influenzae (21.5%), Mycoplasma pneumoniae (8.4%), and Moraxella catarrhalis (4.6%) were the most common (Table 2).

Signs and symptoms according to detection results

Crackles were significantly more frequent in patients with viral–bacterial codetection (65.3%) compared with those with other detection patterns (47.7%) (aPR: 1.30; 95% CI: 1.08–1.57; p = 0.007). However, after FDR correction, this association did not reach statistical significance (q = 0.102). No statistically significant differences were found in other clinical signs or symptoms after adjustment, including fever, dyspnea, cough, rhinorrhea, rhonchi, wheezing, subcostal retractions, intercostal retractions, hyporexia, vomiting, diarrhea, sneezing, odynophagia, otalgia, or seizures (all adjusted p > 0.05) (Table 4).

Laboratory results according to detection test

Regarding laboratory and clinical measurements, viral–bacterial codetection was associated with higher platelet counts (adjusted β: 30,452; 95% CI: 5,113–55,792; p = 0.019), higher hemoglobin levels (adjusted β: 0.29 g/dL; 95% CI: 0.03–0.56; p = 0.032), lower oxygen saturation at admission (adjusted β: −0.57%; 95% CI: −1.04 to − 0.10; p = 0.019), and longer hospital stay (adjusted β: 0.66 days; 95% CI: 0.02–1.29; p = 0.042) compared with other detection patterns. No significant differences were found in leukocyte count, neutrophil proportion, lymphocyte proportion, or respiratory rate at admission. After applying FDR correction for multiple comparisons, none of these associations reached statistical significance (q-values ranged from 0.056 to 0.326) (Table 5).

Discussion

Main findings

The objective of this study was to analyze the clinical, laboratory, and seasonal characteristics associated with viral–bacterial codetection in children hospitalized for LRTIs. This is one of the few Peruvian studies to use multiplex RT-qPCR techniques to simultaneously identify viral and bacterial pathogens in this population. The findings corroborate the relevance of mixed etiology in moderate-to-severe respiratory infections and provide updated evidence on codetection patterns and their relationship with clinical severity.

In the present study, 21.5% of pediatric patients hospitalized for LRTIs had viral–bacterial codetection, with the most frequent combination being RSV and Haemophilus influenzae. This proportion is within the range reported in the literature. In Peru, Del Valle-Mendoza et al. [6] reported an 18.5% codetection rate, while Gil et al. in Portugal identified a 27.9% frequency of viral–bacterial codetection among infants under two years of age, with a predominance of Haemophilus influenzae [18]. In Asia, Liu et al. reported a 27.9% codetection rate in China [8]; Choo et al., 43.4% in Korea [7]; Lin et al., 32.4% in Taiwan [9]; and Qu et al., 20.7% in China [10]. Among individual viral pathogens, RSV was the most frequently detected, followed by influenza A, influenza B, and metapneumovirus, while Haemophilus influenzae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Moraxella catarrhalis predominated among bacterial pathogens, patterns also described in recent Asian and Latin American studies [6–10].

The variability observed across studies may be explained by epidemiological and methodological factors, including the sensitivity of RT-qPCR panels used, timing and type of specimen collection, age composition of the samples, and seasonal circulation of respiratory viruses [19, 20]. In addition, coverage of vaccination against Haemophilus influenzae type b and Streptococcus pneumoniae, prior antibiotic use, and bacterial carriage status may modulate the likelihood of codetection [21].

A notable finding of the present study was the higher frequency of crackles among patients with viral–bacterial codetection (65.3%) compared with those with other detection patterns (47.7%), with a 30% higher adjusted prevalence (aPR: 1.30; 95% CI: 1.08–1.57; p = 0.007). Although this finding did not survive FDR correction (q = 0.102), the biological plausibility is strong, since crackles in LRTI reflect alveolar or small airway consolidation and accumulation of secretions, processes that may be amplified when both viral mucosal damage and bacterial involvement are simultaneously present. This partially aligns with the finding reported by Gao et al., who described greater respiratory distress (dyspnea) associated with co-detection by Mycoplasma and adenovirus [22]. Conversely, when comparing clinical presentation across infection groups, no significant differences were identified for most symptoms (fever, dyspnea, rhinorrhea, vomiting, among others). This lack of symptom-based differentiation limits the ability of clinical judgment alone to identify codetection cases and underscores the value of molecular testing to improve etiological diagnosis.

Another notable finding was that viral–bacterial codetection was associated with approximately 0.66 additional days of hospitalization on average (adjusted β: 0.66; 95% CI: 0.02–1.29; p = 0.042), a finding that remained consistent with the direction of previous results although it did not reach significance after FDR correction (q = 0.063). Furthermore, viral–bacterial codetection was associated with lower oxygen saturation at admission (adjusted β: −0.57%; p = 0.019), suggesting greater acute respiratory compromise, and with higher platelet counts (adjusted β: 30,452; p = 0.019) and hemoglobin levels (adjusted β: 0.29 g/dL; p = 0.032), which may reflect reactive thrombocytosis as part of the inflammatory response and possible differences in baseline characteristics between groups. While none of these associations survived multiple testing correction, the biological coherence of these findings—crackles, lower O₂ saturation, and longer hospital stay all pointing toward greater lower airway involvement—supports their potential clinical relevance and warrants prospective investigation. These findings are consistent with reports by Qu et al., who documented prolonged hospital stays among codetection children [10]; Lin et al., who found increased risk of intensive care unit admission and mechanical ventilation in RSV pneumonia with bacterial codetection [9]; and Chien et al., who showed that bacterial codetection in influenza was associated with a higher frequency of systemic complications, need for advanced therapies, and longer hospital stays [23]. Similarly, Niu et al. reported in a meta-analysis that codetection significantly increases clinical severity in adenovirus infections [24], while Choo et al. described a more prolonged and severe course in Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia with viral codetection [7].

Together, these results support the hypothesis that mixed etiology may worsen the clinical course of LRTIs. Regarding underlying mechanisms, the simultaneous presence of viruses and bacteria may amplify the local inflammatory response, compromise the integrity of the respiratory epithelium, and reduce the effectiveness of mucociliary clearance mechanisms. This synergistic interaction between pathogens favors persistence of infection and may explain the longer disease course and greater lower airway involvement observed in co-detection cases [25, 26].

Implications and recommendations

These findings have relevant clinical and public health implications, as they suggest that viral–bacterial codetection in children hospitalized with lower respiratory tract infections may identify a subgroup with greater respiratory compromise and potentially higher hospital resource use, as reflected by the higher frequency of crackles, lower oxygen saturation at admission, and longer hospital stays. However, these results should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution, as they are intended to generate hypotheses rather than establish definitive clinical conclusions. In this context, the use of multiplex molecular testing could be strengthened in pediatric patients hospitalized with moderate or severe LRTIs, especially when the clinical presentation does not clearly distinguish a mixed etiology, while continuous epidemiological surveillance of codetection patterns and their seasonality should also be encouraged. In addition, future prospective studies with larger sample sizes and a more detailed assessment of factors such as vaccination status, prior antibiotic use, and bacterial colonization are needed to confirm the clinical relevance of these associations and to guide more precise management strategies, including risk stratification and treatment optimization in this population.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing temporality or causality. Second, reliance on medical records is subject to documentation errors, missing data, and variable data quality. Third, the study population comes from a single private hospital in Lima, predominantly from West Lima and largely from the pre-pandemic period, limiting the generalizability of findings to public, rural, or socioeconomically different settings. Fourth, although multiplex RT-qPCR was performed on nasopharyngeal samples, this technique cannot reliably distinguish active infection from colonization for certain pathogens, such as Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Staphylococcus aureus, and the timing of nasopharyngeal swab collection relative to symptom onset was unknown [27]. Fifth, the large number of comparisons performed across bivariate and regression analyses increased the risk of type I error; after FDR correction, none of the associations remained statistically significant (all q-values > 0.05), underscoring the exploratory nature of all reported findings. Sixth, potential confounders such as nutritional status, vaccination history, comorbidities, or environmental exposures were not assessed and may influence infection severity. Seventh, the study may have lacked sufficient statistical power to detect smaller differences in clinical symptoms across infection groups. Eighth, key severity outcomes such as ICU admission, need for supplemental oxygen, and mechanical ventilation were not available in the secondary database and could not be evaluated. Ninth, the viral panel did not include SARS-CoV-2 detection, preventing identification of COVID-19 cases that may have coexisted with other respiratory pathogens and potentially altered codetection patterns. Tenth, because only children who underwent both viral and bacterial PCR testing were included, diagnostic indication bias cannot be excluded, as clinicians may have been more likely to request both panels in more severe or atypical cases during certain periods, which could have influenced the observed associations, including longer hospital stays. Finally, the study period includes years affected by the COVID-19 pandemic, during which respiratory virus seasonality was disrupted, potentially biasing results toward atypical patterns [28].

On the other hand, the study provides practical information supporting the optimization of diagnostic approaches through molecular techniques. It may also inform antimicrobial treatment selection and seasonal surveillance, and more broadly contribute to the design of preventive strategies such as RSV vaccination.

Conclusions

Among pediatric patients hospitalized for LRTIs in a private hospital in Lima, Peru, viral–bacterial co-detection was identified in 21.5% of patients, with RSV and Haemophilus influenzae being the most common combination. Based on conventional significance thresholds, viral–bacterial codetection was associated with crackles, lower oxygen saturation at admission, longer hospital stay, and higher platelet counts; however, after FDR correction for multiple comparisons, none of these associations remained statistically significant. These findings are exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. Overall, these results suggest a potential clinical impact of viral–bacterial co-detection on lower airway involvement and hospital course, but confirmation in larger, prospective, multicenter studies is necessary.
