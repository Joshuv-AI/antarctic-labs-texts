Development and reliability and validity test of China college students’ online language use scale
Authors: Tiejun Zhu, Jin Yang, Jiaqi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOnline language is widely used among college students and reflects their communication patterns and psychosocial characteristics in digital contexts. However, existing studies mainly focus on specific aspects such as motivation or emoji use, lacking comprehensive and validated measurement tools. This study aimed to develop and validate a multidimensional scale of online language use among Chinese college students.MethodsA multi-stage scale development approach was employed. Initial items were generated through literature review, grounded theory–based interviews, and existing instruments, followed by expert evaluation. Data from 310 students were used for item analysis and exploratory factor analysis (EFA), while 1,171 students participated in confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and reliability and validity testing. A subsample of 180 students completed a one-month retest. Analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0, AMOS 29.0, and JASP. ResultsThe final scale comprised 15 items across three dimensions: usage effectiveness, usage motivation, and usage behavior. EFA supported a three-factor structure, which was confirmed by CFA with good model fit (GFI = 0.992, AGFI = 0.988, SRMR = 0.041, NFI = 0.988). The scale demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.891; McDonald’s ω = 0.877) and excellent test–retest reliability (r = 0.957). Construct and criterion-related validity were also supported. ConclusionsThe scale is a reliable and valid instrument for assessing online language use among Chinese college students, conceptualized as a multidimensional construct integrating effectiveness, motivation, and behavior.

Introduction

Network language is vivid, concise, humorous, innovative, rich in content and flexible in form, which deeply attracts contemporary teenagers. And because of its independent consciousness, full capacity for civil conduct and the needs of study and life, the use of the Internet has been rooted in its daily activities, and network language has become a common object of its daily communication. This is the case with frequent online media communication. Even in real life, the popularity of online language, especially online buzzwords, has not decreased, and even the real-life phenomenon of online language has appeared. “Network language is integrated into people’s real life, and some network terms have solidified and become part of people’s real life communication terms.” [1].

From the background of the emergence of network language, network language originally refers to the protocol language between computers, which is used to execute certain instructions and complete certain tasks. From this perspective, “network language” seems to have become a new machine language. Of course, with the rapid popularization and application of the Internet, “network language” has been endowed with more meanings and attributes, showing two categories in general. The first category is extended from its basic attributes, that is, network professional terms such as network protocol terms and specific industry terms related to the network, such as network links, websites, firewalls, network viruses and so on; The second category refers to the symbols created, used and disseminated by netizens through online media and online platforms (such as Weibo, WeChat, Tik Tok, etc.) [2].

This paper focuses on the second category. In this regard, different disciplines have different definitions and understandings of network language. According to the existing research, from the perspective of communication, it is considered that network language is a tool used by people to communicate on network media, and it is also a new media of human society, and it expresses special significance in the communication of specific network media; From the perspective of sociology, the relationship between cyber language and real society and virtual society is emphasized, and the social essence of cyber language is revealed. It is considered that cyber language is a social dialect, which is produced and used by netizens in a specific social group, and depends on the unique cyberspace and network platform, and its expression form and content have certain variation attributes. From the perspective of linguistics, it is considered that network language includes not only the terms and vocabulary of Internet and computer technology and application, but also the language used by people to convey information through the Internet, including static symbols such as words and images, dynamic symbols such as animation and video, and their various combinations. From the perspective of culturology, we mainly pay attention to the relationship between language and culture, and think that network language is the carrier of network culture that reflects and conveys the cultural life and values of netizens with the emergence of the Internet.

This study conducts interdisciplinary research drawing upon pedagogy, psychology, linguistics, communication studies, sociology, and information science. It proposes that online language constitutes a novel linguistic form and communication medium that reflects human sociocultural psychology, developed through cyberspace platforms and online communication technologies. Beyond possessing the inherent sociocultural attributes of language and its function in advancing human cognitive development, online language also serves as an emerging communication vehicle that extends human educational activities and expands the scope of educational spaces.

By revealing both the positive effects and negative impacts of college students’ online language usage on their physical and mental health, this study contributes to effectively regulating their online behavior. Through establishing guidance strategies and development pathways for their online language conduct, it promotes healthy interpersonal communication and interaction across both digital and real-world spheres, fostering balanced physical and mental well-being. This approach significantly enhances students’ online ethical literacy, providing a scientific, systematic, and operationally precise guide for cultivating positive social attitudes among Chinese college students.

College students are not only among the creators of internet slang but also its primary users. Existing research indicates that enhanced self-efficacy among college students can effectively promote prosocial behaviors such as negative emotion self-regulation, anger control, and self-motivation. Students with higher self-efficacy levels exhibit greater positive emotions, thereby increasing the likelihood of engaging in prosocial actions like actively helping others [3–5]. Furthermore, since individuals’ online states are susceptible to environmental influences, frequent use of online language aids in improving emotional states, thereby making individuals more likely to engage in prosocial behaviors [6]. This study also demonstrates that online language usage is a significant factor influencing college students’ prosocial behaviors and can further stimulate such behaviors through enhanced self-efficacy.

Internet language serves as an effective vehicle for college students to release emotions [7]. Simultaneously, due to the tendency toward stratification in college students’ online group lifestyles, the diverse and stratified expression of internet language becomes a driving mechanism for venting their emotions and mental states. University students actively engage in the entire process of creating, using, and decoding online language, particularly internet slang. Diverse online language provides them with “carnivalesque emotional experiences, alleviating inner anxiety stemming from growth challenges and role realization difficulties” [8]. Moreover, the continuous expansion of college students’ online social circles, the rich diversity of the digital world, the sustained novelty and stimulation experienced during online language use, and the increasing influence of their online discourse have effectively enhanced their sense of happiness and accomplishment. This fosters positive attitudes toward the internet and its usage. “A positive attitude toward the internet is a prerequisite for an individual’s successful use of the internet” [9], and it is also a prerequisite for individuals to gain positive self-experiences through the use of online language.

Based on existing research, while there is a wealth of studies compiling, analyzing, and cataloging netizens’ usage of online language terms, along with annual selections and evaluations of internet buzzwords by relevant social institutions, investigations specifically targeting adolescents—particularly college students—regarding their online language usage are also not lacking. However, overall, these studies tend to focus primarily on the online language itself, reflecting its developmental changes and characteristics through usage patterns among youth groups such as college students. Second, they emphasize statistical surveys of adolescents’ usage periods, frequency, contexts, and attitudes toward online language. Building upon prior research, this study specifically investigates the current status of developing online language usage scales for contemporary Chinese university students. It prioritizes university students as the primary research subjects and emphasizes the impact of online language on their online social mindset and values.

Literature review

In October 1969, Leonard, a pioneer in the field of computer network in the United States, used “LO” to send information in the first internet communication experiment in history, which was regarded as the embryonic form of network language [10–12]. Many foreign scholars focus on the influence of network language on new media and its development [13–16], etc. Since the 1990s, research on online language from a sociological perspective has emerged, with scholars mainly studying the social and psychological motivations behind the spread of online language [17–21], etc. The social issues related to online language include online language violence, online game addiction, and the proliferation of online symbolic language [22–24]; The research on network language from the perspective of linguistics also began at the end of the 20th century, and the research results mainly focused on the evolution of network style, the construction of network words, the evolution of network language style and the semantic transfer of network language [25–27]; The discussion of network language from the perspective of culturology is mainly reflected in the interaction between the study of online cultural ontology and the study of online linguistic texts, as well as in the themes of anti-tradition and the modern development dilemma of network culture and language [28–33].

The earliest expression of “network language” in China can be traced back to 1991 [34]. In 1994, China established the first WEB server and the first set of web pages appeared, which marked China’s official access to the Internet. Since 1994, The development of online language in China has roughly experienced the following stages: the stage of adopting traditional realistic language (1994), network neologism (1995–1996), free development and rapid growth (1997–1999), spontaneous standardization (2000–2005), part-of-speech imitation (2006–2007), sentence imitation stage (2008–2009), style creation and conscious legislation stage (2010–2018), ecological governance start-up stage (2019–2020), realistic virtual universe era and ecological governance deepening stage (2021-present).

China’s research on network language originated in the 1990s, and the first paper on network language appeared in China academic journals in 1996, which was called “new network language” [35]. After that, it quickly became a hot field of academic concern, and a large number of research results have been produced so far, which are summarized as follows:

The first is the proposal, deepening, and expansion of the emerging interdisciplinary field of “network linguistics”. Although research on network language in China began later than in foreign countries, the term network linguistics was first proposed by Professor Zhou Haizhong in the article A brand-new language discipline—network linguistics in 2000, which attracted the attention of international academic circles, thus marking the birth of network linguistics [36]. Subsequently, Chinese scholars applied word-formation theory [37], discourse analysis [38], adaptation theory [39], cognitive theory [40], stylistic theory [41], memetics theory [42, 43], resonance theory [44], and metaphor theory [45] to systematically study the pragmatic issues of the network age, the composition, derivation, and function of network language phenomena, thereby deepening and expanding the scope and content of network linguistics research.

The second is to investigate, track, and systematically study the emergence, development, and evolution of network language in China. Early studies examined the usage patterns and characteristics of online vocabulary through surveys and descriptive analysis, particularly among college students and online communities [46, 47]. Subsequent research further explored the developmental trajectory and dissemination of internet language through literature review and corpus-based analysis, revealing its patterns of innovation, semantic expansion, and continuous evolution in digital communication [34, 48–51].

The third is to study the characteristics and values of network language, such as its economic and innovative function in communication, simplicity and vividness, humor and personalization, compatibility and youth-oriented features, informality and collectivism, convenience and vulgarity, randomness and diversification, and virtuality and defamiliarization [52–54].

The fourth is to study the influence of network language on social aspects, such as its impact on traditional power discourse and communication ethics, teenagers’ speech behavior, social class consciousness, the relationship between online language and adolescents’ social communication, and teenagers’ public participation [55–60].

The fifth is the comparative study of Chinese network language and foreign network language [61–64].

The sixth is the collection, collation, interpretation, and compilation of network language. The first book of this kind was the Internet Fashion Dictionary published in 2000, which focused on network argot, digital language, and network terminology [65]. Then linguist Yu Genyuan published the China Internet Language Dictionary in 2001, which triggered the first wave of publications of similar dictionaries in the same year [66]. From 2012 to 2014, a second wave emerged, including the Xinhua Dictionary of Internet Language [67], Don’t Ask for Help of Internet Language [68], Searching for Internet Language [69], and the Latest Dictionary of Internet Communication Terms (Revised Edition) [70].

There are few research results on college students’ online language, and some studies mainly interpret it from a macro perspective, such as how college students’ online lifestyle affects their social mentality and ideological behavior. For example, some studies discuss youth emotions and social mentality through the spread of online buzzwords and propose online expression channels and emotional release platforms [7]; others analyze adolescents’ socialization development and cognition of online language under the Internet context [71]; some scholars examine group and psychological attributes of adolescents from the dynamic mechanism of online buzzword production and dissemination [72]; other research explores the complex social mentality of adolescents through the rich social and cultural connotations of online language [73]; some studies analyze the overall influence of online language on adolescents’ ideological and behavioral activities from communication and linguistic perspectives [74–77]; another study examines online language as an important symbol of contemporary youth culture affecting socialization [78]; some research discusses moral anomie caused by non-standard use of online language [79]; other scholars argue that carnival and joking tones in youth online buzzwords reflect social situations of young people [80]; finally, some studies focus on adolescents’ self-media behavior, linking online speech rights to moral behavior and online language violence [81].

From the above, it can be seen that network language has been widely studied by scholars from its initial germination to its current vigorous development. However, research directly focused on developing scales for online language is scarce. Most scholars have proposed new perspectives on emoticons, internet slang, and online emoticons. For instance, Zhang CY [82] examined the basic usage of online emoticons based on stereotype model theory and developed a questionnaire on the motivations for using online emoticons. This questionnaire comprises two dimensions—“expressing emotions” and “controlling interactions”—to measure individuals’ motivations for using online emoticons. Other scholars explored university students’ motivations for using online emoticons from perspectives including seeking enjoyment, self-expression, trend-following, conversation facilitation, impression management, and group integration [83]. Researchers developed a questionnaire on the impact of online emoji usage on information exchange, conducting empirical studies on communication effectiveness through dimensions such as textual annotation, visual presentation, perceived comprehensibility, social presence, and perceived enjoyment [84].

Another study examined factors influencing young users’ willingness to use online emoticons from a user experience perspective [85], proposing that factors include visual appeal, content presentation, information interpretation, interaction platform, interaction partner, interaction scenario, flow experience, and social presence. The earliest measurement of college students’ online language usage in China was the “College Student Online Language Usage Motivation Scale” developed by [86]. This scale comprises four dimensions: expressive strategy needs (economy principle), escapist affiliation needs (self-identity), communicative needs, and masking needs, providing preliminary insights into the characteristics of college students’ online language usage and development.

Although the aforementioned studies have conducted in-depth research on the use of online language, systematic research examining the overall landscape remains insufficient. In particular, there is a notable lack of research on developing measurement scales specifically for Chinese university students’ online language usage. Existing measurement tools either focus solely on the motivational aspects of online language use or concentrate on specific components like emoticons. To better measure and analyze the current state of online language use among Chinese university students, this study developed a new online language use scale for Chinese university students. This scale was created based on existing literature, prior research, and preliminary data analysis and qualitative studies.

The second category is about the research on the motivation of using network language and its influence. For example, some scholars point out that college students’ behavior of using online buzzwords for interpersonal communication is not only guided by their own values orientation, but also driven by motivation [87]. Some scholars believe that the emergence and development of online buzzwords are not only the product of online communication by netizens, but also the reflection of netizens’ cognitive motivation to society, psychology and communication [88]. In addition, it analyzes the reasons why college students use online buzzwords, including cultural pursuit, psychological expectation, living conditions and social influence [89]. Some scholars have revealed the development characteristics of college students’ use of online language from four dimensions: expressing strategic needs (economic principles), avoiding belonging needs (self-identity), communicating needs and covering up needs, and investigated the motivation of college students’ use of online language [90]. Some scholars have suggested that in the process of using online buzzwords, the most important use behavior of college students is information acquisition, and identification and motivation are the direct driving forces, while emotion is the weakest driving force in the process of using online buzzwords [91].

Some scholars, in their research papers on the compilation of college students’ online communication motivation scale, divide online communication motivation into instrumental communication motivation, which mainly includes obtaining information, assisting their studies and facilitating their lives, and expressing their feelings, relieving pressure and confirming themselves [92]. Some scholars have suggested that college students’ motivation to use online language includes seven dimensions: expressing demands, communicating needs, venting emotions, conformity, showing personality, modifying language and being convenient and quick [93]. Some scholars have suggested that the use of online buzzwords is mainly for recreation, social and interpersonal needs and the need to vent personal emotions [94].

And many scholars have also put forward the relationship between the use of online language and emotion [95–100]. Some scholars point out that college students achieve the purpose of alternative presence and self-presentation through the use of expression packs [101]. Some scholars have pointed out that network emoji has the functions of transmitting information, visual expression, emotional catharsis, highlighting personality, symbolizing rhetoric, simulating reality and economy and culture [102]. In addition, foreign scholars have concluded through research that emoji and emoji can convey facial expressions and express personal feelings in interpersonal communication [103]. Some scholars have come to the conclusion through empirical experiments that the use of personal emoticons is determined by the psychological and perceived utility levels of self-image consistency [104]. Some scholars have studied the influence of emoticons on interpersonal communication from linguistic and non-linguistic perspectives [105]. Some scholars have found that teenagers are more inclined to establish self-identity and social identity through the use of Internet technologies, such as the use of mobile phones, the use of social software, the creation of games and the use of online buzzwords [106].

The third category is about the study of network language use behavior and its influence. In terms of behavior, some scholars suggest that the positive role of online buzzwords is mainly reflected in interpersonal communication [107]. Some scholars have raised questions about the frequency, reasons, occasions and attitudes towards online buzzwords [108]. Some scholars have put forward the differences in the frequency of using online buzzwords [109]. Another scholar pointed out that teenagers’ online language use: online language acquisition, media contact habits and identity [110]. Some scholars point out that the research should be carried out from four aspects: college students’ use of online buzzwords, ways of use, parts of speech and frequency of use, and the influence of online buzzwords on college students’ position and behavior [109]. Some scholars have mentioned that the use of emoticons includes the object of use, the way of acquisition and the context of use [111].

In summary, previous studies have examined the characteristics, development, and dissemination of online language from linguistic and communication perspectives. However, relatively few studies have developed systematic measurement tools to assess online language use among Chinese college students. Existing research tends to focus on descriptive analyses of vocabulary features or communication patterns, lacking a comprehensive scale to capture the multidimensional nature of online language use. To address this gap, the present study aims to develop and validate a scale for measuring online language use among Chinese college students. Specifically, this study seeks to answer the following research questions: What are the underlying dimensions of online language use among Chinese college students? Does the developed scale demonstrate satisfactory reliability and validity?

Research methods

Study design

This study employed a multi-stage scale development design. In the first stage, initial items were generated through a literature review, theoretical analysis, and interview analysis. Following expert review to establish content validity, items were deleted to form a preliminary scale. Subsequently, 312 college students were selected to participate in item analysis and exploratory factor analysis to test the reliability of the preliminary scale, reveal the underlying factor structure, and validate and refine the scale items to form the final scale. Subsequently, 1,593 college students were recruited to conduct reliability and validity tests, as well as confirmatory factor analysis. Finally, 184 participants were selected for a retest one month later to confirm the factor structure and examine the psychometric properties of the scale.

Item generation and content validation procedures

The development of scale items followed three main approaches. First, a comprehensive review and analysis of the literature and existing research on college students’ use of online language was conducted. Relevant constructs and expressions reported in previous studies were extracted and adapted to generate preliminary items. Examples include statements such as “Using online language allows me to express emotions and information more fully” and “Using online language can foster a more harmonious atmosphere of communication among students.”

Second, qualitative interviews were conducted to further explore university students’ experiences with online language use and to verify the saturation of the theoretical framework. A qualitative research design based on in-depth interviews and grounded theory analysis was adopted. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling from a university in Anhui Province, China. A total of 20 university students participated in the interviews, including 5 males and 15 females, aged between 22 and 27. Each interview lasted between 7 and 15 min (mean = 12 min). All interviews were audio-recorded with the participants’ consent and transcribed verbatim, ultimately yielding approximately 50,000 words of textual data.

Data analysis was conducted using a grounded theory approach, including open coding, axial coding, and selective coding. NVivo 11 software was used to facilitate the coding and organization of the text data. Fifteen interview transcripts were used in the initial coding process, while the remaining five were used to assess theoretical saturation. Through the coding process, several themes related to online language use were identified, such as emotional expression, ease of communication, social interaction atmosphere, and identity expression. These themes provided a crucial basis for the development and refinement of scale items.

Third, the item pool was further optimized by drawing on existing measurement tools, including the “questionnaire on the use behavior of online buzzwords” [92] and the “Scale of Motivation for Internet Language Use Among College Students” developed by Gan Xuehua [91], as well as empirical studies on emojis and online communication [84]. Relevant statements extracted from these studies—such as “Internet language makes communication easier to understand” and “Internet language makes online communication smoother”—were adapted and incorporated into the scale.

Through the integration of literature review, qualitative interviews, and existing measurement tools, we have preliminarily developed scale items for data collection to be used in subsequent exploratory factor analysis and tests of reliability, validity, and confirmatory factor analysis.

In this paper, the effects of college students’ use of online language are mainly summarized into three categories: social value, spiritual pleasure and easy understanding. Motivation includes social support, emotional catharsis and exchange learning. Usage behavior includes surfing habits, media contact and access channels, as shown in the Fig. 1 below.

Based on the above research, we developed a preliminary draft of the scale consisting of 54 items. The scale is divided into three main sections, comprising three dimensions and 54 items, with each item offering 23, 17, and 14 response options, respectively. The first section assesses “University Students’ Level of Understanding of Internet Language”; the second section evaluates “The Extent to Which University Students’ Intentions Align with the Use of Internet Language”; and the third section examines “The Extent to Which University Students’ Behavior in Using Internet Language During Online Activities Aligns with Their Intentions.”

We invited nine experts in education, psychology, linguistics, sociology, and management to evaluate the relevance and validity of the preliminary scale and provide suggestions for revision. Following receipt of their feedback, adjustments were made to the scale items.

Each scale item determines the CVI score. Experts use a four-point Likert scale to assess the “relevance” of each item. The Scale Content Validity Index (S-CVI) is derived by calculating the Item Content Validity Index (I-CVI), and the average I-CVI for all items is based on the experts’ ratings [112]. The Scale Content Validity Index (S-CVI) is calculated by first determining the Item Content Validity Index (I-CVI) for each item in the instrument, then averaging the I-CVI values for all items. Items with an I-CVI below 0.70 are excluded [113]. In addition, education experts evaluated the scale based on its educational applicability, relevance to the college student population, and suitability for teaching contexts; psychology experts assessed it in terms of psychometric validity, the accuracy of construct definitions, and the reasonableness of item-scale relationships; and linguistics experts examined the scientific validity of online language classification, the accuracy of terminology, and the comprehensiveness of linguistic phenomena covered. sociology experts assessed the social functions of online language, group differences, subcultural characteristics, and digital existence; and management experts evaluated the practical value of online language in organizational communication, brand promotion, and public opinion management. They also assessed the readability of the 54 items and made revisions accordingly, 26 entries have been removed.

During the expert review phase, to assess the consistency of the experts’ evaluations of the scale items, this study employed Kendall’s W to analyze inter-rater reliability. Kendall’s W is a nonparametric statistical method used to measure the consistency of rankings assigned by multiple raters to the same items; it is suitable for ordinal data or Likert scale data. According to relevant research standards [114, 115], a W value greater than 0.5 indicates good consistency. The overall Kendall’s W for the scale items was 0.746, suggesting good consistency among experts in their evaluations of the items. Based on expert feedback at this stage, the items were reorganized into 28 items. The average Scale-Level Content Validity Index (S-CVI/Ave) for the 28-item scale was 0.948 (≥ 0.90), indicating good content validity. Following this phase, a final set of 28 items was selected to form the revised version of the initial scale assessing college students’ use of online language.

Sampling and participant characteristics

Sample for Exploratory Factor Analysis: Using convenience sampling, undergraduate students from a university in Anhui Province, China, who voluntarily participated in the survey were selected as the initial sample. Item analysis and exploratory factor analysis were conducted on the preliminary scale. An offline survey was administered using the “Survey on College Students’ Use of Internet Language.” The inclusion criteria were as follows: participants must be currently enrolled undergraduate students; they must have experience using the internet and online communication platforms that utilize common internet slang; voluntarily participate and independently complete the scale. Exclusion criteria included: scale with a large number of missing data; scale with obviously abnormal response patterns (e.g., identical answers to all questions); scale completed in an unrealistically short time; and participants who were not currently enrolled university students. After applying the above criteria, invalid scale were excluded prior to statistical analysis. A total of 312 scale were distributed and returned, of which 310 were valid. Data collection procedure

The scale was distributed via two methods: offline paper copies and the Wenjuanxing online platform. Of the 310 and 184 data samples, respectively, the research team’s affiliated university staff coordinated with faculty members and counselors to organize students for offline completion. The remaining 1,171 samples were collected by the research team through faculty members and counselors at universities within the same province, who organized class meetings and other activities to gather students and have them scan a QR code to complete the scale online. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and all respondents signed an informed consent form before completing the scale. After data screening, invalid responses—such as incomplete scales or those with excessively short response times—were excluded.

Data analysis methods

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0, AMOS 29.0, and JASP. The analysis was conducted in the following order: (1) item analysis, (2) exploratory factor analysis (EFA), (3) confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and (4) reliability and validity testing.

Item analysis

First, SPSS 27.0 was used to screen the sample data for missing values and outliers; cases with missing responses were excluded prior to analysis. Standardized scores were examined for potential outliers, and no significant impact on the dataset was found. The normality of the data was assessed using the K-S test. Item analysis was conducted using the Critical Ratio (CR) method based on upper and lower 27% groups and item-total correlation analysis [116] to examine the item validity and discriminant validity of the 28 items in the preliminary scale. Following the classical test theory approach proposed by Kelley [117], participants were ranked according to their total scale scores, and the top 27% and bottom 27% of the sample were selected as the high-scoring and low-scoring groups, respectively. This method maximizes intergroup differences while maintaining a sufficient sample size to ensure stable estimates. In this study, participants in the top 27% of total scores (n = 84) were defined as the high-scoring group, and participants in the bottom 27% of total scores (n = 84) were defined as the low-scoring group. If the data deviated from a normal distribution, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare differences in all items between the high- and low-scoring groups. If the significance P-value reached the significance level (p < 0.05) and the critical ratio (CR) was greater than or equal to 3, the item was considered to have good discriminative power and was retained; If the p-value did not reach the significance level (p > 0.05), or if it reached the significance level (p < 0.05) but the critical ratio (CR) was less than 3, the item was deleted.

In addition, the Spearman correlation coefficient is used to calculate the correlation between each item and the total score, in order to assess the consistency of each item with the overall scale. A higher correlation between an item and the total score indicates stronger homogeneity between the item and the overall scale, and suggests that the psychological trait or underlying behavior being measured is more closely aligned. A common criterion is that the correlation between an item and the total score must be greater than 0.30, and items with insignificant correlations should be excluded [116].

Exploratory factor analysis

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) is used to assess the construct validity of a scale. Prior to conducting EFA, Bartlett’s sphericity test and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) method were employed. When Bartlett’s test yields a statistically significant result and the KMO adequacy of sampling value is 0.80 or higher, the items are considered suitable for factor analysis [118]. The KMO statistic assesses whether the partial correlation coefficients among variables are small, thereby determining whether the variables share common factors. Bartlett’s test examines whether the correlation matrix deviates significantly from the identity matrix; a significant deviation indicates sufficient inter-variable correlation, making the data suitable for factor analysis. To determine the number of factors to extract, both scree plots and parallel analysis can be conducted simultaneously [119, 120]. Parallel analysis compares the eigenvalues obtained from the sample data with the expected values from fully random data to determine the expected number of factors; only eigenvalues exceeding the 95th percentile of the simulated eigenvalues are retained as substantive factors.

Subsequently, principal axis factor analysis is employed, using the principle of eigenvalues greater than 1 and the direct oblique method for exploratory factor analysis. The criteria for retaining or deleting items are determined based on both statistical indicators and conceptual considerations. Specifically, items are considered for deletion if they meet one or more of the following conditions: (1) Commonality less than 0.4; (2) Factor loading less than 0.5, as in scale development studies, a factor loading of 0.50 or higher is generally considered to indicate a substantial and significant association between an item and a factor [121] ; (3) The factor contains fewer than 3 items; (4) Cross-loadings are present, meaning a particular item has high loadings on multiple factors, and all loadings are greater than 0.3; (5) Ambiguity and obvious misclassification exist, making the factor theme difficult to interpret. These criteria are commonly used in scale development research to ensure appropriate factor structure and interpretability [122]. In addition to statistical criteria, the conceptual relevance and theoretical interpretability of items were also considered during the item deletion process to ensure that the retained items adequately represent the latent structure. Following using the principal axis factoring analysis and Caesar normalization oblique rotation, three factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted; items were then deleted, and exploratory factor analysis was repeated.

Confirmatory factor analysis

Based on exploratory factor analysis (EFA), a three-factor model of college students’ online language use was constructed. To statistically evaluate the factor structure identified by EFA, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using AMOS 29.0 software.

First, the distribution characteristics of the data were examined; if the results indicated a possible deviation from a normal distribution, unweighted least squares (ULS) estimation was selected for the CFA to assess its construct validity. Since this method does not require the assumption of multivariate normality and does not provide chi-square degrees of freedom (χ²/df) or chi-square-based fit indices (RMSEA, CFI, TLI), GFI, AGFI, SRMR, and NFI were selected as criteria for evaluating model fit [123]. To assess construct validity, the following criteria were adopted, including: (1) CFI > 0.90; (2) AGFI > 0.90; (3) SRMR < 0.05; (4) NFI > 0.90 [123, 124]. If the first-order model exhibits high variable correlations and good fit, a second-order CFA is conducted to test structural validity.

Reliability and validity testing

SPSS 27.0 software was used to test reliability and validity. To examine validity evidence based on relationships with other variables, correlation analyses were conducted between scale scores and external variables. Given that the scale items were measured using a Likert-type scale and that some variables did not strictly meet the assumption of normality, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to test the relationships among variables. For ordinal data and non-normally distributed variables, Spearman’s correlation is considered more appropriate.

First, the relationships between the three factors and the total score of the College Students’ Online Language Use Scale were analyzed (Table 8) to test the scale’s construct validity. If the correlation coefficients between each factor and the total score are higher than those between the factors themselves, this indicates that the scale possesses good construct validity; Subsequently, the correlation between the College Students’ Online Language Use Scale and the College Students’ Online Language Use Motivation Scale was analyzed (Table 9) to test the scale’s criterion-related validity. A significant positive correlation would provide supporting evidence for the scale’s criterion-related validity.

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, and the item-total correlation coefficient [113].

Retest reliability analysis

A random sample of 184 participants was retested one month later, and the correlation between the two test results was calculated using Spearman’s correlation analysis in SPSS 27.0.It is used to assess the stability of scores between two administrations of a test over a period of time.

Results

Item analysis and item validity

The results showed that all 28 items reached statistical significance (p < 0.05) and had a cut-off ratio (CR) of 3 or higher, indicating good item discrimination; therefore, all items were retained for further analysis. Overall correlation analysis revealed that the correlation coefficients between each item and the total score ranged from 0.628 to 0.832 (p < 0.01), indicating that all items were significantly correlated with the total scale score. The critical ratios for all items were statistically significant (p < 0.01), suggesting that each item possessed good discriminant validity and could effectively distinguish between respondents with different levels of online language proficiency.

Calibration validity

Scale for the Use of Internet Language Among College Students: This scale consists of 15 items, divided into three dimensions: usage effects, usage motives, and usage behaviors. It employs a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

Scale for the Motives Behind the Use of Internet Language Among College Students: This scale was developed by Gan Xuehua in 1999 and comprises four dimensions: strategic expression needs (economic principles), avoidance of attribution needs (self-identity), communicative needs, and coverage needs, consisting of 22 items in total. It employs a five-point Likert scale; a higher total score indicates stronger motivation among college students to use online language. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale was 0.955.

Discussion

This study developed and validated a scale for measuring online language use among Chinese college students. This study fills a gap in the domestic literature regarding comprehensive measurement tools for college students’ use of online language. Unlike the scale developed by Gan XH (2010), which focuses on motivation, this scale employs exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to construct a three-dimensional model comprising three dimensions: “usage effects,” “usage motivation,” and “usage behavior.” This structure aligns with Uses and Gratifications Theory, which posits that individuals’ psychological needs (motivation) drive specific media consumption behaviors (behavior), ultimately leading to psychological or social gratification (outcome).

First, the dimension of usage effectiveness aligns with prior studies suggesting that online language enhances communication efficiency, emotional expression, and social interaction [13, 24]. The high factor loadings observed in this dimension indicate that college students perceive online language as a meaningful communicative tool rather than merely informal slang. This finding extends earlier descriptive studies by providing quantitative evidence supporting the communicative value of online language.

Second, the usage motivation dimension corresponds closely to motivation-based frameworks proposed in previous studies, such as emotional expression, social integration, and self-presentation [91]. Compared with earlier scales that focused solely on motivation, the current study integrates motivation within a broader structural model, thereby demonstrating that motivation is not an isolated construct but interacts with both perceived effectiveness and actual behavior.

Third, the usage behavior dimension reflects observable engagement patterns, consistent with research on media usage habits and online communication practices [18, 110]. The relatively strong loadings suggest that behavioral engagement is a stable and measurable component of online language use, supporting the inclusion of behavioral indicators in scale development.

Overall, the three-factor model confirms that online language use is not a single construct but a systematic interaction of cognition (effectiveness), intention (motivation), and action (behavior).

Reliability and Validity in the Context of Psychometric Standards

The scale demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.891) and high test–retest reliability (0.957), which exceed commonly accepted psychometric thresholds [116]. These results indicate that the scale is both stable over time and internally coherent.

Moreover, the construct validity results—particularly the higher correlations between each factor and the total score compared to inter-factor correlations—are consistent with theoretical expectations of multidimensional scale design. This pattern supports the distinct yet related nature of the three dimensions, aligning with classical test theory [116].

The criterion-related validity findings, showing significant correlations with an existing online language motivation scale, further confirm that the instrument is grounded in established constructs. Importantly, this also demonstrates that the new scale does not merely replicate existing tools but extends them by incorporating behavioral and effectiveness dimensions, thereby offering a more comprehensive measurement framework.

Limitations

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged.

First, the sample was primarily drawn from universities in Anhui Province, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or cultural contexts. Future studies should include more geographically diverse samples.

Second, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to social desirability bias or response bias. Incorporating behavioral data (e.g., digital trace data) could improve measurement accuracy.

Third, although the three-factor model demonstrated good fit, the second-order factor structure was not fully supported, indicating that the higher-order construct requires further theoretical refinement.

Finally, while the study identifies a multidimensional structure, it does not examine causal relationships among the dimensions. For example, the extent to which motivation influences behavior or perceived effectiveness remains unclear.

Future research directions

Future research can build upon this study in several ways:

Cross-cultural validation: Testing the scale in different cultural contexts to examine its universality and adaptability.

Longitudinal studies: Investigating how online language use evolves over time and its long-term psychological or social effects.

Model extension: Incorporating additional variables such as self-efficacy, digital literacy, or social identity to develop a more comprehensive theoretical model.

Mixed-method approaches: Combining quantitative scales with qualitative or behavioral data to enhance explanatory depth.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated the College Students’ Online Language Use Scale, providing a systematic measurement tool for assessing online language use among Chinese university students. The findings confirm that online language use can be conceptualized as a multidimensional construct comprising usage effectiveness, usage motivation, and usage behavior, thereby offering empirical support for a structured understanding of this phenomenon.

The primary contribution of this study lies in addressing the gap in existing research, where most prior studies focused on isolated aspects such as motivation or specific forms of online expression. By integrating multiple dimensions into a single validated scale, this study advances the measurement and conceptualization of online language use. The scale demonstrates good reliability and validity, suggesting its applicability for future empirical research in related fields. From a practical perspective, the findings provide useful insights for educators and policymakers in understanding college students’ online communication patterns. The scale can serve as a diagnostic tool for evaluating students’ online language use and for designing interventions aimed at promoting healthy and constructive online communication behaviors.
