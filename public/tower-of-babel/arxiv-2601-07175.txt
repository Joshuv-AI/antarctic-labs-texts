Optimal Equivariant Matchings on the 6-Cube With an Application to the King Wen Sequence
Alejandro Radisic

Abstract
We characterize perfect matchings on the Boolean hypercube { 0 , 1 } n \{0,1\}^{n} that are equivariant under the Klein four-group K 4 = ⟨ comp , rev ⟩ K_{4}=\langle\mathrm{comp},\mathrm{rev}\rangle generated by bitwise complement and reversal. For n = 6 n=6 , we prove there exists a unique K 4 K_{4} -equivariant matching minimizing total Hamming cost among matchings using only comp \mathrm{comp} or rev \mathrm{rev} pairings, achieving cost 120 versus 192 for the complement-only matching. The optimal matching is determined by a simple “reverse-priority rule”: pair each element with its reversal unless it is a palindrome, in which case pair with its complement. We verify that the historically significant King Wen sequence of the I Ching is isomorphic to this optimal matching. Notably, allowing comp ∘ rev \mathrm{comp}\circ\mathrm{rev} pairings yields lower cost (96), but the King Wen sequence follows the structurally simpler rule. All results are formally verified in Lean 4 with the Mathlib library.

Introduction

The Boolean hypercube
\{0,1\}^{n}=\{0,1\}^{n}
admits two natural involutions:
bitwise complement

\mathrm{comp}
, which flips each bit (
0\leftrightarrow 1
), and
bit reversal

\mathrm{rev}
, which reverses bit order (
b_{0}b_{1}\cdots b_{n-1}\mapsto b_{n-1}\cdots b_{1}b_{0}
). Together these generate the Klein four-group
K_{4}=\{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{comp},\mathrm{rev},\mathrm{comp}\circ\mathrm{rev}\}
.

A
\mathrm{comp}/\mathrm{rev}
matching
is a perfect matching where each pair
\{h,h^{\prime}\}
satisfies either
h^{\prime}=\mathrm{comp}(h)
or
h^{\prime}=\mathrm{rev}(h)
. More generally, a
K_{4}
-equivariant matching
also permits
h^{\prime}=\mathrm{comp}(\mathrm{rev}(h))
.

This paper addresses the optimization problem:

Among
\mathrm{comp}/\mathrm{rev}
matchings on
\{0,1\}^{n}
, which minimize total Hamming distance (Definition
2.4
)?

For
n=6
, one might expect this to be trivial: just pair each element with the cheaper option. But in most equivariant matching problems, locally optimal choices conflict globally—choosing the cheapest partner for
h
may force a suboptimal choice elsewhere. Our main result is that for
\{0,1\}^{6}
,
no such conflicts arise
: a simple greedy rule yields the unique global optimum. The key is Lemma
2.12
, which shows that
\mathrm{comp}
and
\mathrm{rev}
never genuinely compete—whenever they differ in cost,
\mathrm{rev}
is strictly cheaper.

As an application, we verify that the King Wen sequence—a traditional ordering of the 64 hexagrams of the I Ching into 32 pairs—is isomorphic to this unique optimal matching. We also note that relaxing to full
K_{4}
-equivariant matchings (allowing
\mathrm{comp}\circ\mathrm{rev}
pairings) yields lower cost (96 vs 120), but requires per-orbit case analysis rather than a uniform rule.

Preliminaries

2.1
Hexagrams as Binary Vectors

In the I Ching, a
hexagram
is a figure composed of six stacked horizontal lines, each either solid (representing yang) or broken (representing yin). Reading solid as
1
and broken as
0
, from bottom to top, each hexagram corresponds to a 6-bit binary string. There are
2^{6}=64
hexagrams, corresponding to the vertices of the 6-dimensional hypercube.

Definition 2.1

(Hexagram)
.

A
hexagram
is an element
h\in\{0,1\}^{6}
, with positions indexed
0
(bottom) through
5
(top).

Remark 2.2
.

The correspondence between hexagrams and binary numbers was noted by Leibniz
[
2
]
.

Definition 2.3

(Hamming Distance)
.

For
h_{1},h_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{6}
, the
Hamming distance
is

d_{H}(h_{1},h_{2})=\#\{i:h_{1}(i)\neq h_{2}(i)\}=\sum_{i=0}^{5}|h_{1}(i)-h_{2}(i)|

Definition 2.4

(Total Hamming Cost of a Matching)
.

For a perfect matching
M
on
\{0,1\}^{6}
(a partition into 32 disjoint pairs), the
total Hamming cost
is the sum of Hamming distances over all pairs:

\mathrm{Cost}(M)=\sum_{\{h_{1},h_{2}\}\in M}d_{H}(h_{1},h_{2})

This measures how “different” the paired elements are overall.

2.2
The Klein Four-Group Action

Definition 2.5

(Complement and Reversal)
.

Define operations on hexagrams:

\displaystyle\mathrm{comp}(h)(i)

\displaystyle=1-h(i)\quad\text{(bitwise complement)}

\displaystyle\mathrm{rev}(h)(i)

\displaystyle=h(5-i)\quad\text{(bit reversal)}

Proposition 2.6
.

The operations
\mathrm{comp}
and
\mathrm{rev}
satisfy:

1.

\mathrm{comp}\circ\mathrm{comp}=\mathrm{id}
(complement is an involution)

2.

\mathrm{rev}\circ\mathrm{rev}=\mathrm{id}
(reversal is an involution)

3.

\mathrm{comp}\circ\mathrm{rev}=\mathrm{rev}\circ\mathrm{comp}
(they commute)

Thus
\{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{comp},\mathrm{rev},\mathrm{comp}\circ\mathrm{rev}\}
forms the Klein four-group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

(Orbit)

The
orbit

h
under the Klein four-group is

\mathrm{orbit}(h)=\{h,\mathrm{comp}(h),\mathrm{rev}(h),\mathrm{comp}(\mathrm{rev}(h))\}

2.3
Hamming Distances of Group Actions

Proposition 2.11
.

For any hexagram
h
:

1.

d_{H}(h,\mathrm{comp}(h))=6
(complement flips all bits)

2.

d_{H}(h,\mathrm{rev}(h))\leq 6
, with equality iff
h(i)\neq h(5-i)
for all
i

Lemma 2.12

(No-Conflict Lemma)
.

For any hexagram
h
, exactly one of the following holds:

1.

d_{H}(h,\mathrm{rev}(h))<d_{H}(h,\mathrm{comp}(h))=6
(reversal is strictly cheaper), or

2.

\mathrm{rev}(h)=\mathrm{comp}(h)
and both distances equal
6
(the options coincide), or

3.

\mathrm{rev}(h)=h
(palindrome, so
\mathrm{rev}
yields trivial pairing).

In particular,
\mathrm{rev}
and
\mathrm{comp}
never genuinely compete: when both are valid and distinct,
\mathrm{rev}
is strictly cheaper.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{comp}
flips all bits,
d_{H}(h,\mathrm{comp}(h))=6
always. For
\mathrm{rev}
: if
d_{H}(h,\mathrm{rev}(h))=6
, then every position differs, so
h(5-i)\neq h(i)
for all
i
. This means
h(5-i)=1-h(i)=\mathrm{comp}(h)(i)
, hence
\mathrm{rev}(h)=\mathrm{comp}(h)
. If
\mathrm{rev}(h)=h
, then
h
is a palindrome. Otherwise
d_{H}(h,\mathrm{rev}(h))<6
.
∎

(Why Greedy Works)

In typical equivariant matching problems, choosing the locally optimal partner for

\mathrm{rev}
unless it’s trivial) is
unambiguous
. There is no trade-off to optimize, hence no room for conflict (see Figure

). This is why the simple reverse-priority rule achieves the unique global optimum.

4
Optimality of the King Wen Matching

4.1
The Reverse-Priority Rule

Definition 4.1

(Reverse-Priority Matching)
.

Define the
priority partner
function:

\mathrm{partner}(h)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{comp}(h)&\text{if }h=\mathrm{rev}(h)\text{ (palindrome)}\\
\mathrm{rev}(h)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The intuition: prefer reversal (which has distance
\leq 6
) unless reversal is trivial (palindrome), in which case use complement.

Theorem 4.2

(Involution)
.

The priority partner function is an involution:
\mathrm{partner}(\mathrm{partner}(h))=h
.

Proof.

Two cases:

1.

If
h
is a palindrome, then
\mathrm{comp}(h)
is also a palindrome (complement preserves palindrome structure), so
\mathrm{partner}(\mathrm{comp}(h))=\mathrm{comp}(\mathrm{comp}(h))=h
.

2.

If
h
is not a palindrome, then
\mathrm{rev}(h)
is also not a palindrome, so
\mathrm{partner}(\mathrm{rev}(h))=\mathrm{rev}(\mathrm{rev}(h))=h
.

∎

4.2
Cost Minimization

Definition 4.3

(Total Hamming Cost)
.

For a perfect matching
M
on
\{0,1\}^{6}
, the
total Hamming cost
is

\mathrm{Cost}(M)=\sum_{\{h_{1},h_{2}\}\in M}d_{H}(h_{1},h_{2})

Theorem 4.4

(Optimality among
\mathrm{comp}/\mathrm{rev}
Matchings)
.

Among
K_{4}
-equivariant perfect matchings on
\{0,1\}^{6}
where each pair uses
\mathrm{comp}
or
\mathrm{rev}
, the reverse-priority matching uniquely minimizes total Hamming cost.

Proof.

Restricting to
\mathrm{comp}/\mathrm{rev}
pairings, the options for each hexagram
h
are
\mathrm{comp}(h)
or
\mathrm{rev}(h)
.

•

By Proposition
2.11
,
d_{H}(h,\mathrm{rev}(h))\leq 6=d_{H}(h,\mathrm{comp}(h))
.

•

Equality holds iff
\mathrm{rev}(h)=\mathrm{comp}(h)
(Lemma
2.12
).

•

For palindromes,
\mathrm{rev}(h)=h
, so the only non-trivial option is
\mathrm{comp}(h)
.

Thus the reverse-priority rule chooses the minimum-distance option at each step. Since there is no actual choice when distances are equal (the options coincide), the matching is uniquely determined.
∎

Theorem 4.7

(Uniqueness)
.

Any equivariant matching satisfying the reverse-priority rule equals the priority partner function.

Proof.

The reverse-priority rule uniquely specifies the partner at each hexagram. By Theorem
4.2
, this defines a valid involution, hence a perfect matching.

5
Discussion

5.1
Orbit Structure

The
K_{4}
-action on
\{0,1\}^{6}
partitions the 64 elements into orbits of size 2 or 4:

•

Size-4 orbits
: 12 orbits containing 48 generic elements

•

Size-2 orbits
: 8 orbits containing 16 palindromes (elements fixed by
\mathrm{rev}
)

Within each orbit, the equivariant matching must pair elements. For size-4 orbits, there are three equivariant pairings induced by the non-identity involutions
\mathrm{comp}
,
\mathrm{rev}
, and
\mathrm{comp}\circ\mathrm{rev}
. The reverse-priority rule uses
\mathrm{rev}
(falling back to
\mathrm{comp}
for palindromes), which minimizes cost among
\mathrm{comp}/\mathrm{rev}
matchings. A mixed strategy using
\mathrm{comp}\circ\mathrm{rev}
on orbits where
d_{H}(h,\mathrm{rev}(h))=4
achieves the global minimum cost of 96.

5.2
Uniqueness Mechanism

The key to uniqueness is the No-Conflict Lemma
2.12
:
\mathrm{comp}
and
\mathrm{rev}
never genuinely compete. When they have different costs,
\mathrm{rev}
wins; when they have equal cost, they coincide. This eliminates all apparent degrees of freedom, making greedy optimization globally consistent.

5.4
Subgroup Matchings

One may ask whether the theorem holds for subgroups of
K_{4}
:

•

Complement-only
(
\langle\mathrm{comp}\rangle
): Valid perfect matching with cost 192, trivially unique since each hexagram has exactly one complement.

•

Reversal-only
(
\langle\mathrm{rev}\rangle
):
Not
a valid perfect matching. The 8 palindromes satisfy
\mathrm{rev}(h)=h
, so they cannot pair with their own reversal. A perfect matching requires pairing with a
different
element.

Thus the reverse-priority rule is the minimal hybrid that produces a valid matching: use
\mathrm{rev}
whenever possible, fall back to
\mathrm{comp}
for the 8 palindromes.

5.5
Extensions

The
K_{4}
-action generalizes to
\{0,1\}^{n}
for any
n
. The orbit structure and optimal matching problem remain well-defined; we conjecture the reverse-priority rule remains optimal for all
n
.
