Comparative analysis of insulin resistance indicators in predicting reversion from prediabetes to normoglycemia
Authors: Junlin Yi, Jieli Qin, Xinqun Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndicators for insulin resistance (IR) have been proposed for predicting dysglycemia; however, their comparative effectiveness in predicting reversion from prediabetes to normoglycemia remains unclear. This study aimed to compare the predictive performance of four IR indicators for normoglycemia reversion in Chinese adults with prediabetes.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 15,415 participants with prediabetes—fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels of 5.6–6.9 mmol/L. Normoglycemia reversion was defined as FPG levels < 5.6 mmol/L without a diabetes diagnosis at follow-up. Baseline metabolic scores for IR (MetS-IR), triglyceride glucose (TyG) index, TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), and triglyceride to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio were calculated. Cox proportional hazards models assessed associations between IR indicators and normoglycemia reversion. C-statistics, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated to assess the predictive ability of IR indicators in addition to a base model.ResultsOver 3.2 years, 6,624 (43.0%) participants reverted to normoglycemia. After adjusting for potential confounders, a one-standard-deviation increase in MetS-IR, TyG, TyG-BMI, and TG/HDL-C was significantly associated with a lower likelihood of normoglycemia reversion (hazard ratios [95% confidence interval {CI}]: 0.79 [0.77, 0.82], 0.89 [0.87, 0.91], 0.84 [0.82, 0.86], and 0.88 [0.85, 0.90], respectively). The base model showed modest discrimination (C-statistic: 0.608). Adding MetS-IR improved C-statistics most (+ 0.018), followed by TyG-BMI (+ 0.009), TG/HDL-C (+ 0.008), and TyG (+ 0.006). MetS-IR significantly improved NRI (0.109, 95% CI 0.071–0.172) and IDI (0.016, 95% CI 0.009–0.025).ConclusionsMetS-IR appears to be a more effective predictor of normoglycemia reversion compared to other IR indicators, potentially aiding in the early identification of individuals with prediabetes likely to achieve spontaneous reversion, thereby supporting personalized interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-02004-z.

Introduction

Prediabetes—marked by impaired fasting glucose (IFG) and impaired glucose tolerance (IGT)—elevates the risk of diabetes development [1]. In 2019, the International Diabetes Federation estimated that 7.5% of adults aged 20–79 years had IFG, affecting approximately 374 million individuals globally [2]. Given lifestyle changes [3], high genetic susceptibility to insulin resistance (IR) [4], and the rapidly aging Chinese population [3], the situation is particularly concerning in China, where the prevalence of prediabetes was reported to be 35.7%, approximately 374 million individuals in 2017 [5]. Even when diabetes progression is delayed or prevented, individuals with prediabetes still face an increased risk of cardiovascular complications [6]. However, some individuals with prediabetes can revert to normoglycemia, substantially lowering the future risk of diabetes and its complications [7–9]. Therefore, a key focus in managing prediabetes should be promoting normoglycemia reversion.

IR, a key factor in the development of prediabetes, manifests through multiple tissue-specific metabolic pathways [10]. In skeletal muscle, IR leads to impaired translocation of a specific glucose transporter to the cell membrane, thereby reducing glucose uptake [10]. In adipose tissue, IR disrupts lipid metabolism and adipokine secretion [10], while in the liver, it increases the production of de novo glucose and lipids [10]. The gold standard for assessing IR is both complex and invasive [11]. Consequently, several surrogate indicators of IR have been developed. These indicators capture tissue-specific aspects of IR in different ways. For example, the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio indicates IR in adipose tissue [12]; the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index primarily reflects IR in skeletal muscle [13]; and the metabolic score for IR (MetS-IR) integrates multiple parameters including body mass index (BMI), TG, blood glucose, and HDL-C, which may better capture the whole-body IR [14]. Numerous studies have confirmed the utility of these indicators in predicting adverse outcomes, including prediabetes [15, 16], diabetes [14, 17–19], cardiovascular disease (CVD) [20–23], and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [24]. Recently, a few studies have explored the relationship between IR indicators and reversion from prediabetes to normoglycemia [25–29]. However, comprehensive studies comparing the predictive abilities of different IR indicators for normoglycemia reversion are still lacking. The identification of the optimal IR indicators for predicting normoglycemia reversion could lead to changes in monitoring, referral criteria, and the pursuit of interventions.

This study had two primary objectives: to evaluate the performance of IR indicators in predicting normoglycemia reversion beyond traditional risk factors, and to compare the predictive performance of these indicators. Specifically, we hypothesize that MetS-IR will demonstrate superior predictive ability for normoglycemia reversion compared with other IR indicators.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study used data from Chen et al. [30]. The original dataset consisted of medical records from a database developed by Rich Healthcare Group, which focused on identifying predictors of glucose regulation. This dataset was considered suitable for evaluating IR indicators in the context of prediabetes. Participants underwent health checkups across 32 provinces in Mainland China between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2016.

Study population

Initially, 685,277 participants aged ≥ 20 years who had undergone at least two health examinations, were enrolled. Participants with missing data for weight, sex, height, fasting plasma glucose (FPG); a pre-existing diagnosis of diabetes; visit intervals of less than 2 years; extreme BMI values; and undefined diabetes status were excluded. The final participant count in the source study was 211,833.

Table S1 presents the baseline characteristics of the original cohort. The mean age was 42.10 ± 12.65 years with 54.8% male participants. The cohort exhibited generally healthy baseline characteristics, with an average BMI of 23.24 ± 3.34 kg/m2 and mean FPG levels of 4.92 ± 0.61 mmol/L. Among lifestyle factors, 20% were current smokers and 2.2% were current alcohol drinkers. The lipid profile showed mean TG levels of 1.34 ± 1.03 mmol/L, total cholesterol (TC) of 4.71 ± 0.90 mmol/L, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and HDL-C levels of 2.77 ± 0.68 and 1.37 ± 0.31 mmol/L, respectively. For IR indicators, the mean MetS-IR was 33.70 ± 6.66, median TyG was 8.33 (interquartile range [IQR]: 7.93–8.77), median TyG-BMI was 191.58 (IQR: 166.84–219.63), and median TG/HDL ratio was 0.81 (IQR: 0.52–1.34).

We additionally excluded participants with baseline FPG levels of < 5.6 or > 6.9 mmol/L (n = 185,815), missing TG and HDL-C values at baseline, and missing FPG values during follow-up (n = 10,603), resulting in a final sample size of 15,415 participants for analyses (see flowchart for details [Fig. S1]).

The study procedures were approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Rich Healthcare Group. Informed consent was not required owing to its retrospective design and the use of anonymized data. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to the guidelines of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.

Definition of prediabetes and study outcomes

Prediabetes was defined as FPG levels between 5.6 and 6.9 mmol/L (100–125 mg/dL), based on the American Diabetes Association criteria [31]. The primary outcome was the reversion from prediabetes to normoglycemia, defined as achieving FPG levels < 5.6 mmol/L without a diagnosis of diabetes during follow-up.

Laboratory measurements

Fasting blood samples were collected from all participants and were evaluated using an autoanalyzer (Beckman 5800; Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), to determine the levels of FPG, TG, TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C. FPG levels were measured using the glucose oxidase method, while lipid levels were quantified using enzymatic colorimetric methods.

Statistical analysis

The participants’ baseline characteristics were summarized based on the presence of normoglycemia reversion during follow-up. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]), while categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group differences were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis H test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables.

We assessed the relationship between IR indicators and normoglycemia reversion using Cox proportional hazards models, selected for their capacity to manage time-to-event data. We validated the proportional hazard assumption using Schoenfeld residuals (Table S2). Models were adjusted for established dysglycemia risk factors, including age, sex, smoking status, family history of diabetes, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) [31]. We calculated the Generalized Variance Inflation Factor (GVIF) to assess potential multicollinearity between the variables in our Cox proportional hazards models. The adjusted GVIF values were all below 5, indicating minimal collinearity concerns. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were subsequently calculated.

Missing data were considered missing at random (MAR). We applied multiple imputation by chained equations to address missing values. We conducted 20 imputations with 10 iterations per imputation to create 10 datasets. All variables used in the analysis models were included as predictors in the imputation models to preserve relationships between variables. The results from the imputed datasets were combined using Rubin’s rules to account for both within- and between-imputation variance. Table S3 shows the proportions of missing values for the covariates.

We performed restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses using four knots (5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles) to further explore the potential non-linear relationships between IR indicators and normoglycemia reversion. The likelihood ratio test was used to assess the significance of non-linearity.

The C-statistic index and reclassification analyses, including net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), were used to evaluate improvements in the predictive performance of IR indicators.

Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the discrimination of IR indicators by calculating the area under the curve (AUC) at regular intervals throughout the follow-up period. Additionally, the Brier score was calculated to evaluate the accuracy of probabilistic predictions for IR indicators, with lower scores indicating better possible calibration.

Two subgroup analyses were conducted. One analysis examined the relationship between IR indicators and normoglycemia reversion across various subgroups. The second one was to identify the most predictive IR indicators for normoglycemia regression at the fifth-year across different subgroups. These subgroups were defined by age ( < 50, ≥ 50 years), sex (men, women), BMI ( < 25, ≥ 25 kg/m2), smoking status (never, ever), family history of diabetes (no, yes), SBP ( < 140, ≥ 140 mmHg), and DBP ( < 90, ≥ 90 mmHg). Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, we did not adjust for multiple comparisons.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.0.2; The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) with the “rms,” “survival,” “survminer,” and “pROC” packages. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

The mean age of the participants was 50.9 (SD, 13.4) years, and 10,003 (64.9%) were male (Table 1). In total, 6624 (43.0%) participants reverted from prediabetes to normoglycemia, with a median follow-up period of 3.2 years (45,565 person-years). Compared with those without normoglycemia reversion, individuals who reverted were more likely to be women (2,594 [39.2%] versus 2,818 [32.1%]),younger (mean [SD] age, 47.5 [13.3] versus 53.5 [12.9] years), and less likely to be current smokers (1,315 [19.9%] versus 2,175 [24.7%]) or current drinkers (239 [3.5%] versus 446 [4.9%]) (all P values < 0.001). Prediction of normoglycemia reversion beyond traditional risk factors

Traditional risk factors in the model predicting normoglycemia reversion yielded modest discrimination (C-statistic, 0.608; 95% CI, 0.600–0.616) (Table 3). Adding each IR indicator improved the model discrimination (all P < 0.001). TyG-BMI also improved IDI (0.008, 95% CI 0.003–0.015) and NRI (0.080, 95% CI 0.045–0.133). Discussion

This large cohort study comprising 15,415 participants with prediabetes revealed that all four IR surrogates offered added predictive value for normoglycemia reversion beyond traditional risk factors. Importantly, MetS-IR exhibited the most significant improvement in predictive performance metrics compared with the other surrogates.

Comparison with previous research

To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically compare the predictive abilities of multiple IR indicators for normoglycemia reversion in individuals with prediabetes. Previous research has mainly focused on the magnitude of association between these indicators and normoglycemia reversion. For instance, a recent study using our cohort revealed that MetS-IR had the strongest association with normoglycemia reversion when compared with other IR indicators [29]. Our study also replicated these findings. Additionally, our study extends prior investigations by providing comprehensive evidence for the superior predictive ability of MetS-IR through multiple statistical approaches. The C-statistic index quantifies a model’s ability to distinguish between individuals who experience an event (in this case, normoglycemia reversion) and those who do not. Our finding that MetS-IR provided the greatest improvement in C-statistic when added to the base model align with the seminar work by Bello-Chavolla et al. [14], who demonstrated that MetS-IR exhibited the highest discriminatory ability for IR, followed by TyG-BMI and TG/HDL-C. Another retrospective cohort study of 7,291 Chinese participants aged > 40 years showed that MetS-IR demonstrated superior discriminatory power for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) as compared with other IR indicators [35].

NRI and IDI evaluate how effectively new indicators such as MetS-IR enhance the classification and prediction of outcomes beyond traditional risk factors. Our finding that MetS-IR yielded the greatest improvements in both NRI and IDI aligns with previous studies investigating a variety of cardiometabolic outcomes. For example, in a retrospective cohort study of 12,290 non-obese participants, MetS-IR significantly improved diabetes risk prediction, with NRI and IDI values of 0.176 and 0.015, respectively [36]. In another study involving 14,032 participants with hypertension, MetS-IR showed significant improvements in reclassification metrics when added to the basic model for stroke prediction [37]. Similarly, the Urumqi Research on Sleep Apnea and Hypertension study demonstrated that adding MetS-IR to the prediction model with established risk factors for CVD significantly improved both NRI and IDI [38].

However, inconsistent results were found in a prospective study involving 4,543 Chinese adults [39]. This study found that TyG showed better predictive performance than MetS-IR for incident prediabetes over a 5-year follow-up period, with improvements in both C-statistic and reclassification metrics [39]. A potential explanation for this discrepant finding is that the sequence and relative contribution of IR, beta-cell function and endogenous glucose output may differ significantly when transitioning between different glycemic states [10]. For example, during progression to dysglycemia, IR may play a primary role, while beta-cell dysfunction becomes more prominent in later stages [10]. In contrast, the reversal to normoglycemia might require coordinated improvements in both insulin sensitivity and beta-cell function, along with reduced hepatic glucose output.

TyG-BMI versus TyG index

Our study also confirmed previous reports that TyG-BMI demonstrated better predictive performance compared to TyG. For instance, data from the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey showed that TyG-BMI was the most effective surrogate of IR, outperforming the TyG index [40]. A national cohort study of 3,640 middle-aged and older adults reported that when predicting metabolic syndrome in men, TyG-BMI yielded a greater AUC value compared with the TyG index, suggesting that TyG-BMI offers enhanced discriminatory power [41]. Similarly, in the NAGALA cohort involving 15,462 Japanese participants, the AUC for TyG-BMI was larger than that of the TyG index for predicting incident diabetes [42]. However, when evaluating predictive power of these indicators, most prior research mainly relied on AUC analyses, which did not account for time effects and other traditional risk factors. Our study addresses these limitations through a more comprehensive analytical approach that incorporates time-dependent ROC analyses, reclassification metrics, and consideration of traditional risk factors. This comprehensive evaluation provides stronger evidence that TyG-BMI offers enhanced predictive capability compared to the TyG index.

Clinical implications

While the overall discriminative ability of our models is modest, likely reflecting the complex and multifactorial nature of glycemia regulation [10], our findings still have clinical implications. First, the improvements in predictive performance suggest that IR indicators can meaningfully enhance the detection of individuals likely to achieve normoglycemia. Second, Given that a variety of these indicators can be easily calculated from routine laboratory tests, our study identified the most effective one for predicting normoglycemia reversion. Lastly, at a population level, even small improvements in predictive accuracy could translate to a substantial number of individuals being identified as candidates for early intervention or closer monitoring.

Mechanisms underlying mets-IR’s superior performance

The exact mechanisms underlying the superior predictive performance of MetS-IR have not yet been fully elucidated. MetS-IR incorporates multiple metabolic components, including BMI, FPG, TG, and HDL-C, which collectively provide a more comprehensive assessment of IR compared to other simpler indicators. The inclusion of BMI in the MetS-IR formula is particularly important as it captures overall adiposity, which are key drivers of IR [10]. HDL-C adds another crucial dimension to MetS-IR’s predictive capability, as it is not only closely related to IR but also positively affects beta cell function [43]. Finally, the logarithmic transformation applied to the MetS-IR formula may help capture the nonlinear relationships between these metabolic parameters and IR, potentially enhancing its predictive accuracy.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of our study include a large sample size and comprehensive analyses of various IR surrogates. Additionally, a time-dependent ROC analysis allowed us to evaluate the performance of these surrogates over time, offering valuable insights into their long-term predictive capabilities. However, this study has some limitations. First, the diagnosis of prediabetes was based on FPG levels. This approach may have led to an underestimation of the true prevalence of prediabetes. Caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to populations where prediabetes is defined using different diagnostic criteria. Future studies incorporating HbA1c and OGTT criteria may provide a more comprehensive understanding of normoglycemia reversion. Second, our median follow-up duration of 3.2 years, while sufficient to observe significant changes in glycemic status, may not capture the full spectrum of normoglycemia reversion patterns. Future studies with extended follow-up periods could help elucidate these temporal patterns of glycemic improvement. Third, our findings were based on the Chinese population, which may limit the generalizability of our results to other ethnic groups. Fourth, although we adjusted for several potential confounders, residual confounding factors, such as dietary factors, may have been present. However, the objective of this study was to compare the predictive performance of different IR indicators alongside a consistent set of traditional risk factors rather than to develop a comprehensive prediction model.

Conclusions

MetS-IR emerged as a modestly better indicator for predicting normoglycemia reversion in patients with prediabetes, showing advantages over other IR indicators in both discriminatory power and reclassification metrics. Incorporating MetS-IR into routine health checkups could allow for more accurate identification of individuals with prediabetes who are likely to revert to normoglycemia, thereby enabling targeted lifestyle or pharmacological interventions. Future studies are warranted to establish optimal MetS-IR cut-off values for clinical decision-making.
