Exact risk curves of signSGD in High-Dimensions: quantifying preconditioning and noise-compression effects
Ke Liang Xiao, Noah Marshall, Atish Agarwala, Elliot Paquette

Abstract
In recent years, signSGD has garnered interest as both a practical optimizer as well as a simple model to understand adaptive optimizers like Adam . Though there is a general consensus that signSGD acts to precondition optimization and reshapes noise, quantitatively understanding these effects in theoretically solvable settings remains difficult. We present an analysis of signSGD in a high-dimensional limit, and derive a limiting SDE and ODE to describe the risk. Using this framework we quantify four effects of signSGD : effective learning rate, noise compression, diagonal preconditioning, and gradient noise reshaping. Our analysis is consistent with experimental observations but moves beyond that by quantifying the dependence of these effects on the data and noise distributions. We conclude with a conjecture on how these results might be extended to Adam .

Introduction

The success of deep learning has been driven by the effectiveness of relatively simple stochastic optimization algorithms.
Stochastic gradient descent (
SGD
) with momentum can be used to train models like ResNet50 with minimal hyperparameter tuning.
The workhorse of modern machine learning is
Adam
, which was designed to give an approximation of preconditioning with
a diagonal, online approximation of the Fisher information matrix
(
Kingma, 2014
)
.
Additional hypotheses for the success of
Adam
include its ability to maintain balanced updates to parameters across
layers and its potential noise-mitigating effects
(
Zhang et al., 2020b
;
Zhang et al., 2024
)
. Getting a quantitative, theoretical understanding of Adam and its variants is hindered by their complexity. While the multiple exponential moving averages are easy to implement, they complicate analysis.

The practical desire for simpler, more efficient learning algorithms as well as the theoretical desire for
simpler models to analyze have led to a resurgence in the study of
signSGD
.

signSGD
is
a variant of
SGD
where the stochastic gradient is passed through the
sign function
\sigma
, leading to an update vector of
\pm 1
s.
On average,
signSGD
’s updates at every
step have positive dot product with the average
SGD
step, but it can have dramatically different convergence properties
(
Bernstein et al., 2018a
;
Karimireddy et al., 2019
)
.
Multiple studies point towards sign-based methods as an effective proxy given that the sign component of the gradient has been shown to play an important role in
Adam

(
Kunstner et al., 2023
;
Balles & Hennig, 2018
;
Bernstein et al., 2018b
)
.

signSGD
is also the basis for new practical methods;
the
Lion
algorithm
(
Chen et al., 2023
)
combines
signSGD
with multiple exponential moving averages,
and
signSGD
+ momentum was used to train
LLMs with performance comparable to
Adam

(
Zhao et al., 2024
)
.

Despite the promise of
signSGD
, a detailed quantitative understanding of its dynamics in realistic settings remain elusive—in particular the nature of the preconditioning and the effect of the
\sigma
function on the noise are not well understood. A
crucial first step is to understand these effects on quadratic optimization problems.

Motivated by these questions, we provide the first analysis of the learning dynamics of

signSGD
in a high-dimensional stochastic setting (Section
2
). We make the following contributions:

•

We derive a limiting stochastic differential equation (SDE) for
signSGD
and combine it with a concentration result to derive a deterministic ordinary differential equation (ODE) that describes the dynamics of the risk in our setting (Section
3
).

•

We compare the dynamics of
signSGD
and vanilla
SGD
, isolating
4
effects:

effective learning rate
,

noise-compression
,

diagonal preconditioning
, and

gradient noise reshaping
(Section
4
).

•

We quantitatively analyze these four effects and their contributions to learning, including exact results in specific settings (remainder of Section
4
).

Our work addresses significant technical challenges in analyzing both the preconditioning and noise
transformation effects of
signSGD
.
Our analysis is consistent with more general experimental observations about adaptive methods,
but provides a more quantitative understanding in our setting.
We conclude with a discussion of the implications of our results for future study of adaptive algorithms, including
a conjecture on the limiting form of
Adam
in an equivalent setting.

Problem Setup

Our work considers linear regression using the mean-squared loss
\mathcal{L}
in the one-pass scenario, where data is not reused.
signSGD
, with mini-batching of size
b
, is first initialized by some
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and then follows the update rule:

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{k+1}=\bm{\theta}_{k}-\eta_{k}^{\prime}\sigma\left(\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k},\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{k+1},y^{(i)}_{k+1})\right),

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x},y)=\|\left\langle\mathbf{x},\bm{\theta}\right\rangle-y\|^{2}/2,

(2)

where
\sigma
denotes the sign function applied element-wise and
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1})=(\left\langle\bm{\theta}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k+1}\right\rangle-y_{k+1})\mathbf{x}_{k+1}
. It is typically assumed that the mini-batch
(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{(i)},y_{k+1}^{(i)})
for
1\leq i\leq b
are drawn i.i.d.

We will assume that the samples
\{(\mathbf{x}_{k},y_{k})\}_{k\geq 0}
, consisting of data
\mathbf{x}_{k}
and targets
y_{k}
, satisfy the following:

Assumption 1
.

The data
\mathbf{x}
are mean
0
and Gaussian with positive definite covariance matrix
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. The targets
y
are generated by
y=\left\langle\mathbf{x},{\bm{\theta}_{*}}\right\rangle+\epsilon
, where
{\bm{\theta}_{*}}
is the ground-truth and
\epsilon
the label noise.

Definition 1
.

Define the population risk
\mathcal{P}
and the noiseless risk
\mathcal{R}
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}(\bm{\theta})&=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}\left[(\left\langle\mathbf{x},\bm{\theta}\right\rangle-y)^{2}\right]/2\\
\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta})&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\left\langle\mathbf{x},\bm{\theta}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}}\right\rangle^{2}\right]/2.\end{split}

(3)

Although our theory is framed in the setting of Gaussian data, as we will see,
the results are still a good description for real-world,
a priori
non-Gaussian settings (Figure
1
).
This is an instance of
universality
, wherein the details of the data distribution do not affect the precise high-dimensional limit law (see discussion in
(
Tao, 2023
)
section 2.2). Formalizing this is left to future work.

In contrast, the distribution of the label noise has a nontrivial impact on the behavior of the process. We shall require that the noise is well-behaved in a neighborhood around
0
.

Assumption 2
.

There exists
a_{0}>0
such that the law of the noise
\epsilon
has an
C^{2}
density on
(-a_{0},a_{0})
.

Assumption
2
ensures our SDE (
8
) is Lipschitz (c.f. Lemma
12
) and applies to many distributions; it encompasses heavy-tailed distributions such as
\alpha
-stable laws, and we make no assumptions on any tail properties of the noise. Due to the non-smoothness of the
\sigma
function at
0
, extraordinary behavior of the noise near
0
will lead to degraded performance of
signSGD
as the risk vanishes. At the cost of a less-informative theorem, it is possible to drop Assumption
2
; see Theorem
6
in the Appendix.

An important characterizing feature of
signSGD
is its effect on the covariance of the signed stochastic gradients. We introduce the following transformations on
\mathbf{K}
:

\begin{split}\overline{\mathbf{K}}&\equiv\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{K}\quad\text{and}\\
\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}&\equiv\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{i})\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{j})]\right]_{i,j}=\left[\arcsin\left(\frac{\mathbf{K}_{ij}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{K}_{ii}\mathbf{K}_{jj}}}\right)\ \right]_{i,j},\end{split}

(4)

where
\mathbf{D}=\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{K})}
. We remark that
\overline{\mathbf{K}}
is similar in the matrix-sense to
\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, thus
\overline{\mathbf{K}}
has all real, positive eigenvalues.
\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}
is proportional to
the covariance of
\sigma(\mathbf{x})
. We assume some properties of the matrices
\mathbf{K}
,
\overline{\mathbf{K}}
, and
\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}
.

Assumption 3
.

Suppose:

i).

The spectrum of
\mathbf{K}
is bounded from above and away from
0
independently of
d
.

ii).

The sign-data matrix
\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}
also has operator norm bounded independent of
d
.

iii).

The resolvent of
\overline{\mathbf{K}}
defined by
\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})=(\overline{\mathbf{K}}-z\mathbf{I})^{-1}
satisfies

\max_{i\leq d}\max_{i\neq j}\left\|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{ij}}\right\|=O\left(\frac{d^{\delta_{0}}}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(5)

for all
z\in\partial B_{2\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|}
and
for some
\delta_{0}<1/12
. (Equivalently, one may instead assume the same bounds with
\overline{\mathbf{K}}
replaced by
\mathbf{K}
).

The upper bound on
\mathbf{K}
in Assumption
3

(i)
is standard and can always be achieved by rescaling the risk. But the lower bound is a nontrivial assumption that is necessary for analyzing how
\sigma
affects the stochastic
gradient.
Assumption
3

(ii)
is convenient for the proof. A full understanding of when it holds is highly nontrivial; there exists some theory establishing it for some random
\mathbf{K}

(
Fan & Montanari, 2019
)
. A simple case where
(ii)
is satisfied is when
\left\|{\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}^{-1}}\right\|<1
.
Assumption
3

(iii)

can be interpreted as a condition that the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{K}
contain no low-dimensional structure: for example, it is satisfied with high probability if the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{K}
are taken to be uniformly random. Additionally, it is trivially satisfied for any diagonal
\mathbf{K}
. For a further discussion, including applicability to real data, see
Paquette & Paquette (2022)
, Figure 2.

We assume the learning rates have a high-dimensional limiting profile:

Assumption 4
.

The learning rates follow

\eta_{t}^{\prime}=\eta(t/d)/d,

(6)

where
\eta:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a continuous bounded function. We will write
\eta_{t}
for
\eta(t)
.

This scaling is critical: it ensures that as the problem size grows, both the bias and variance terms in the risk evolution are balanced (see e.g. Equation (
25
)).

Finally, we assume the initialization remains (stochastically) bounded across
d
:

Assumption 5
.

The difference between
{\bm{\theta}_{*}}
and initialization
\bm{\theta}_{0}
satisfies

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}&\left(\left|\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{{\rm T}}(\bm{\theta}_{0}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}})\right|\geq t\right)\leq\\
&C\exp\left(-ct^{2}d/\|\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}\|^{2}\right),\end{split}

(7)

for all
1\leq i\leq d
with absolute, positive constants
c,C
.

For example, this assumption holds for any initialization and target generated by normalized Gaussian. In the case that the initialization and target are deterministic, if
\bm{\theta}_{0}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}}
have entries of order
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}})
then rotating the data
\mathbf{x}
by a Haar-distributed orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{U}
, we obtain a new data covariance matrix
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{U}^{\rm T}
. Moreover,
\overline{\mathbf{A}}\approx\sqrt{d/\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{K})}\mathbf{A}
with high probability. See (
33
). It follows with high probability that
\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{A}})\approx\frac{1}{\xi}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R}\left(z/\xi;\mathbf{K}\right)\mathbf{U}^{\rm T}
where
\xi=\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{K})/d}
. One may then derive a similar probabilistic bound as in (
7
) on non-symmetric rays
(-\infty,-t+O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}})]
and
[t+O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}),\infty)
, where the probability is now taken with respect to
\mathbf{U}
.

3
signHSGD

The analysis of
signSGD
in high-dimensional settings presents a unique set of technical challenges and requires careful mathematical treatment. A core difficulty lies in the transformative effect of the sign operator on the gradient. Unlike traditional
SGD
, where the gradient direction remains consistent with the magnitude of the update,
signSGD
changes the gradient’s direction, via a
non-Lipschitz
compression operation. This compression alters the optimization landscape observed by the optimizer, in ways we will explore in Section
4
.

Nonetheless, we show that under the assumptions above, there is a
continuous
stochastic process Sign-Homogenized SGD (
signHSGD
) which captures the high-dimensional behaviour of
signSGD
; see
(
Thygesen, 2023
)
or
(
Karatzas & Shreve, 1991
)
for background on SDEs.

Definition 2

(
signHSGD
)
.

We define
\bm{\Theta}_{t}
as the solution of the stochastic differential equation:

\mathrm{d}\bm{\Theta}_{t}=-\eta_{t}N_{b}\frac{\varphi^{(b)}(\mathcal{R}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}))}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}(\bm{\Theta}_{t})}}\overline{\mathbf{K}}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}})\mathrm{d}t+\eta_{t}\sqrt{\frac{2\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}}{\pi d}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t},

(8)

with initial condition
\bm{\Theta}_{0}=\bm{\theta}_{0}
. Here,
the constant
N_{b}=\mathbb{E}[\|(Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{b})\|]/\mathbb{E}[|Z_{1}|]
for i.i.d. standard normals
Z_{j}
, and

\begin{split}\varphi^{(b)}(\mathcal{R}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}))&=\frac{2}{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon_{b}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{-\epsilon_{b}^{2}}{4\mathcal{R}(\bm{\Theta}_{t})}\right)\right],\end{split}

(9)

where
\epsilon_{b}=\sum_{i=1}^{b}\epsilon^{(i)}\omega_{i}
for
\{\epsilon^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{b}
and
\{\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{b}
i.i.d samples of
\epsilon
and entries of
b
-dimensional uniform vectors on the sphere, respectively.

Remark 1
.

N_{b}
may be computed explicitly to be

N_{b}=\frac{\Gamma((b+1)/2)}{\Gamma(b/2)\Gamma(1/2)}\sim\sqrt{b}\quad\text{as }b\to\infty.

(10)

This is consistent with the square-root scaling hypothesis, as the bias increases relative to the noise by a factor of square-root of the batch (which leads to the popular square-root learning rate scaling rule).

Remark 2
.

In the case
\epsilon\equiv 0
, we take
\varphi^{(b)}(x)\equiv 2/\pi
. While this
\epsilon
does not satisfy Assumption
2
, we formulate Theorem
6
in the Appendix which covers this case.

It is worth noting that, in practice,

is often easy and inexpensive to compute numerically;
we compute it analytically for some common distributions (Figure
2
).
In general, it is simple to fit a Gaussian mixture model to the noise and use that to compute
\varphi^{(b)}
(Appendix
I
).

For the remainder of this paper, we focus on the single batch setting, i.e.,

b=1
. For notational convenience, we will write
\varphi
in place of
\varphi^{(1)}
in the single batch setting. Numerical validation of our main result, Theorem

We can now state the first part of our main theorem:

Theorem 1

(Main Theorem, part 1)
.

Given
b=1
, Assumptions
1
–
5
and choosing any fixed even moment
2p\in(0,d)
, there exists a constant
C(\overline{\mathbf{K}},\epsilon)>0
such that for any
\delta\in(1/3,1/2)
and all

\begin{split}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}&|\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}_{\lfloor td\rfloor})-\mathcal{R}(\bm{\Theta}_{t})|\leq\\
&\frac{Td^{\delta}\left\|{\mathbf{K}}\right\|}{\sqrt{d}}\exp\left(C(\overline{\mathbf{K}},\epsilon)\left\|{\eta}\right\|_{\infty}T\right),\end{split}

(11)

with probability at least
1-c(2p,\overline{\mathbf{K}})d^{p(1/3-\delta)}
for a constant
c(2p,\overline{\mathbf{K}})
independent to
d
.

In other words, the risk curves of
signSGD
are well approximated by the risk curves of
signHSGD
and this approximation improves as dimension grows.
Numerical simulations suggest that in practice this correspondence is strong even by

(Figure

(a), (b)).

Other statistics like iterate norms or distance to optimality can also be

5

The risk curves of both
signSGD
and
signHSGD
concentrate around the same deterministic path.
Let
R_{t}
be this

of
signSGD
; we will refer

In order to find the deterministic equivalent we introduce a family of scalars

The scalars

\frac{\mathrm{d}r_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-2\eta_{t}\frac{\varphi(R_{t})}{\sqrt{2R_{t}}}\lambda_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{K}})r_{i}+\eta_{t}^{2}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}^{{\rm T}}\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{u}_{i}}{\pi d}

where

,

respectively. A similar argument,

which we will call

(Main Theorem, part 2)

Let

be given by (

–

such that for any

and all

\begin{split}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}&|\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}_{\lfloor td\rfloor})-R_{t}|\leq\\

4
Comparing
signSGD
to vanilla
SGD

To produce an apples-to-apples comparison, we compare the
signHSGD
to the analogous SDE for vanilla
SGD
from
(
Collins-Woodfin & Paquette, 2023
)
:

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{\operatorname{SGD}}&=-\eta^{\operatorname{SGD}}_{t}\mathbf{K}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{\operatorname{SGD}}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}})\mathrm{d}t\\
&+\eta^{\operatorname{SGD}}_{t}\sqrt{\frac{2\mathbf{K}\mathcal{P}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{\operatorname{SGD}})}{d}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}\end{split}

(15)

To control for the adaptive-scheduling inherent in
signSGD
, we run vanilla
SGD
with a risk dependent learning rate schedule
\eta^{\operatorname{SGD}}_{t}
given by

\eta^{\operatorname{SGD}}_{t}={\color[rgb]{0.3047,0.4727,0.6563}\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\eta_{t}}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{P}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{\operatorname{SGD}})}}}=\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\eta_{t}}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x},y)\|^{2}}},

(16)

which is to say that we scale the steps in
SGD
inversely proportional to the norm of the gradients. We note that the
\mathcal{P}
-risk requires the noise
\epsilon
to have finite variance
\dutchcal{v}
; indeed if the variance is infinite, then
signSGD
is overwhelmingly favored, see Remark 1 in
Zhang et al. (2020a)
.
Training
signSGD
with learning rate
\eta_{t}

and
SGD
with learning rate
\eta^{\operatorname{SGD}}_{t}
, we
can use (
8
) to write, with
\psi
as in (
18
)),

\mathrm{d}\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{\operatorname{SGD}}=-\eta^{\operatorname{SGD}}_{t}\mathbf{K}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{\operatorname{SGD}}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}})\mathrm{d}t+\eta_{t}\sqrt{\frac{4\mathbf{K}}{\pi^{2}d}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}

(17a)

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\bm{\Theta}_{t}&=-{\color[rgb]{0.3047,0.4727,0.6563}\eta^{\operatorname{SGD}}_{t}}{\color[rgb]{0.4883,0.2773,0.4297}{\underbrace{\psi(\mathcal{R}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}))}_{\text{$\epsilon$-compress.}}}}{\color[rgb]{0.8008,0.4063,0.0195}\underbrace{\mathbf{D}^{-1}}_{\text{D.Precond.}}}\mathbf{K}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}})\mathrm{d}t\\
&+\eta_{t}{\color[rgb]{0.1992,0.4805,0.1602}\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{2\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}}{\pi d}}}_{\text{Reshape}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}.\end{split}

(17b)

Comparison of the minibatched version can be found in Appendix
D
.

We summarize the precise effects below:

Effective learning rate.

The effective learning rate of
signSGD
can be considered as risk dependent, effectively matching the expected
\ell^{2}
–norm of a gradient.

\epsilon
-compression.

The distribution of the label noise (be it from model-misspecification or otherwise) rescales the bias term. Formally, letting
\dutchcal{v}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^{2}]
,

\psi(x)=\frac{\pi\varphi(x)\sqrt{2x+\dutchcal{v}^{2}}}{2\sqrt{2x}}.

(18)

Diagonal preconditioner.

The matrix
\mathbf{D}^{-1}
gives the diagonal preconditioner (
\mathbf{D}_{ii}=\sqrt{\mathbf{K}_{ii}}
).

Gradient noise reshaping.

Finally, passing the gradient through the
\sigma
function results in a different covariance structure to the gradients, which is accounted for in the differing diffusion term.

Although all the effects appear in concert in
signSGD
, we will now attempt to isolate and address each one separately in the following sections.

4.1
Effective learning rate and convergence

We recall that to match the learning rate of
SGD
to
signSGD
, we had to use the identification (
16
),

\eta^{\operatorname{SGD}}_{t}={\color[rgb]{0.3047,0.4727,0.6563}\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\eta_{t}}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{P}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{\operatorname{SGD}})}}}.

In particular, the effective learning rate gets smaller when the optimizer’s position is far from optimality and gets larger as it gets closer. In the convex setting this is generally undesirable at both extremes. When far from optimality, the algorithm slows far beyond what would tend to be favorable, while at small risks this behavior can impede convergence. On the other hand, it can easily be addressed by using a learning rate schedule.

In a nonconvex setting, identifying
2\mathcal{P}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{\operatorname{SGD}})
with the expected square-norm of the gradients (c.f. (
16
)), one possible benefit of this schedule is that it may be helpful in dynamically adjusting to saddle manifolds in the loss landscape.

Stationary point of
signSGD

If the learning rate is
any
constant
\eta_{t}\equiv\eta
, we have a unique stationary point of the ODE system (

The
\eta

Theorem 3
.

With fixed learning rate
\eta_{t}\equiv\eta\in(0,\infty)
and
\epsilon\sim N(0,\dutchcal{v}^{2})
, the ODEs have a unique stationary point
\left[s_{i}:1\leq i\leq d\right]

given by Equation (

, is given by

R_{\infty}=\frac{\pi\eta}{32d}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{D})\left(\frac{\pi\eta\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{D})}{2d}+\sqrt{\frac{\pi^{2}\eta^{2}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{D})^{2}}{4d^{2}}+16\dutchcal{v}^{2}}\right).

(19)

Notice that the limiting risk’s dependence on
\eta
changes depending on the relationship between
\eta
and
\dutchcal{v}
, for small

\eta
. See Figure

for numerical validation, and see (

4.2
\epsilon
-compression

The influence of the distribution of the noise
\epsilon
on the optimization, in the case of finite variance, can be summarized
by (c.f. (
18
) and (
9
))

\psi(\mathcal{R})=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{-\epsilon^{2}}{4\mathcal{R}}\right)\right]\times\sqrt{{1}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^{2}]}{2\mathcal{R}}}.

(20)

When
\psi<1
, the descent term of (
17b
) is decreased, and hence
signSGD
is slowed with respect to
SGD
with learning rate
\eta^{\operatorname{SGD}}_{t}
. Conversely, when
\psi>1
the descent term is increased, and
signSGD
is favored.
When
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^{2}]=\infty
,
\psi
can be interpreted as
\infty
, corresponding to overwhelming
signSGD
favor, although the quantitative meaning in (
17b
) breaks down.

In the Gaussian case,
\psi=1
. Hence, we can interpret
\psi
as the effect that
deviation from Gaussianity
has on the drift term of the SDE.
We note that all the influence of the label noise
\epsilon
on
signSGD
is entirely through (
20
) which in turn only depends on
\epsilon^{2}
. Hence
signSGD

symmetrizes
the noise distribution.

A full comparison of
SGD
and
signSGD
requires optimizing the learning rates of both algorithms independently. We will show that in the case of isotropic data, this procedure is tractable
and produces
a different threshold
\psi=\frac{\pi}{2}
above which
signSGD
is favored (see Equation (
28
)).

Setups favoring
signSGD
.

In the presence of heavy tails,
\psi(\mathcal{R})
can be large and hence
signSGD
is very favored. Indeed, among some parametric classes, such as the Student’s-t family, this is observed numerically to always be larger than
1
(Figure
3
, left) and increase to
\infty
as the kurtosis increases. More generally, as
\mathcal{R}
tends to
0
, letting
f_{\epsilon}(0)
be the density of the noise at
0
, one has

\lim_{\mathcal{R}\to 0}\psi(\mathcal{R})=\sqrt{2\pi\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^{2}]}f_{\epsilon}(0),

(21)

which can be arbitrarily large, in particular when the noise distribution puts more mass in its tails than at
0
.

Conversely, for
all
distributions, we also observe that when the risk is relatively large,
signSGD
is always modestly favored over
SGD
under the
\eta^{\operatorname{SGD}}
learning rate as we have

1\leq\mathbb{E}\left[1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4\mathcal{R}}\right]\times\sqrt{{1}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^{2}]}{\mathcal{R}}}\leq\psi(\mathcal{R})\leq\sqrt{{1}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^{2}]}{\mathcal{R}}}

(22)

for all
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^{2}]/\mathcal{R}\leq\frac{3}{2}
.

Setups where
signSGD
does not improve.

For light-tailed noises, the factor
\psi
can only mildly favor
SGD
. A density
f
on
\mathbb{R}
is called
log-concave
if it can be written as
e^{g}
for concave
g
(see
(
Saumard & Wellner, 2014
)
for discussion). The exponential, uniform and many other canonical noise distributions are log-concave. Note these decay no slower than exponentially at infinity.
Then as
\varphi(\mathcal{R})/\sqrt{4\pi\mathcal{R}}
is the density at
0
of a log-concave density,
Saumard & Wellner (2014)
, Proposition 5.2 gives us

\psi(\mathcal{R})\leq\sqrt{2\pi}.

(23)

Hence, for these distributions, while there may be limited gains from using
signSGD
, they are bounded by an absolute constant factor.

Setups where
signSGD
is catastrophic.

In the situation that the noise is bounded away from
0
by some
\delta,
it follows that we have the upper bound:

\psi(\mathcal{R})\leq e^{-\tfrac{\delta^{2}}{4\mathcal{R}}}\times\sqrt{{1}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^{2}]}{2\mathcal{R}}}.

(24)

This tends to
0

exponentially
in
1/\mathcal{R}
(e.g. see the Rademacher case of Figure
3
). For such noise distributions,
signSGD
will effectively experience a floor on the risk, which is completely induced by distributional properties of the noise (and unrelated to the underlying optimization problem geometry). In this situation,
SGD
is heavily favored for small risks, which are seen late in training.

Scheduling
signSGD
.

We have discussed adjusting the
SGD
learning rate to match the behaviour of
signSGD
. However, when using
signSGD
there is the
question of how to optimize its learning rate.
This is analytically tractable for
isotropic data
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{I}_{d}
, for which
\overline{\mathbf{K}}=\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{I}_{d}
which allows us to isolate the effects of the label noise. It is easy to check that the
d
-system of ODEs for
signSGD
in (
12
) may be reduced to the following single ODE:

\frac{\mathrm{d}R_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{2\eta_{t}\varphi(R_{t})}{\sqrt{2R_{t}}}R_{t}+\frac{\eta^{2}_{t}}{2},\qquad R_{0}=\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}_{0}).

(25)

If we greedily optimize in
\eta_{t}
we arrive at

\frac{\mathrm{d}R_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\varphi(R_{t})^{2}R_{t},\qquad\text{where}\quad\eta_{t}^{*}=\varphi(R_{t})\sqrt{2R_{t}}.

(26)

So generally for large risks, the optimal stepsize compensates for the effective gradient rescaling in (
16
). This compensation is seen for all risks in the Gaussian
\epsilon
setting.

As a point of comparison, we may repeat the same procedure for the
SGD
risk ODE
R^{\operatorname{S}}
with learning rate
\eta^{\operatorname{S}}
, which can be derived from (
15
):

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}R^{\operatorname{S}}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-2\eta^{\operatorname{S}}_{t}R^{\operatorname{S}}_{t}+\frac{(\eta^{\operatorname{S}}_{t})^{2}}{2}(2R^{\operatorname{S}}_{t}+\dutchcal{v}^{2})\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}R^{\operatorname{S}}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-\frac{2R^{\operatorname{S}}_{t}}{2R^{\operatorname{S}}_{t}+\dutchcal{v}^{2}}R^{\operatorname{S}}_{t}\quad(\text{optimized in }\eta^{\operatorname{S}}).\end{split}

(27)

Hence (
26
) can also be expressed as

\frac{\mathrm{d}R_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\left(\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\psi^{2}(R_{t})\right)\times\frac{2R_{t}}{2R_{t}+\dutchcal{v}^{2}}R_{t}.

(28)

Thus the performance benefits of
signSGD
using the optimal learning rate can again be reduced to a question of the magnitude of
\psi
, albeit with a crossover at
\psi=\pi/2
.

In the non-isotropic setting, locally greedy stepsizes can be very far from optimal, even with two eigenvalues
(
Collins-Woodfin et al., 2024
)
. But we expect the conclusion of (
28
) remains mostly true in well-conditioned settings.

4.3
Diagonal preconditioner

Next, and strikingly, we see that
signSGD
performs a diagonal preconditioning step on the gradients, with the preconditioner given by
\mathbf{D}_{ii}=\sqrt{\mathbf{K}_{ii}}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}]},
where
\mathbf{x}
is a sample. To produce this bias term in
SGD
, we would need to run the algorithm

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\eta_{k}\mathbf{D}^{-1}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},\mathbf{x},y)\right).

(29)

We expect the dynamical preconditioner in
Adam
also simplifies to
\mathbf{D}
in high-dimensions; for details, see Appendix
F
.

As
\overline{\mathbf{K}}
appears naturally in (
8
), its spectrum regulates the rate of convergence of the optimization to stationarity. By utilizing our
d
-systems of ODEs we can establish the following convergence rate:

Theorem 4
.

Assume
\epsilon\sim N(0,\dutchcal{v}^{2})
and let
s_{i}
be the stationary points to (
13a
).
Then there is an absolute constant
c>0
so that if

\eta\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\overline{\mathbf{K}})}{2d}\leq\min\left\{c,\frac{4\dutchcal{v}}{\pi}\right\},\quad\text{and}\quad R_{0}\leq c\dutchcal{v},

(30)

then we have, setting
R_{\infty}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}
to be the limit risk,

|R_{t}-R_{\infty}|\leq 2(R_{0}+R_{\infty})e^{-t\eta\lambda_{\min}(\overline{\mathbf{K}})/(\pi\dutchcal{v})}.

(31)

The proof is given in Appendix
C
.
In contrast to vanilla
SGD
, where the risk converges (in a high-dimensional setting) with rate
\frac{1}{\overline{\kappa}}
, where
{\overline{\kappa}}(\mathbf{K})=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{K})}{d\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K})}
is the average condition number
(
Paquette et al., 2022b
)
. Theorem
4
states that the risk of
signSGD
converges at a rate
\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\overline{\mathbf{K}})}{d\lambda_{\min}(\overline{\mathbf{K}})}
, after selecting the largest allowed
\eta
.

signSGD
is therefore favored over
SGD
when

{\overline{\kappa}}(\overline{\mathbf{K}})<{\overline{\kappa}}(\mathbf{K}).

See Figure
9
for experimental validation.

Settings in which the preconditioned
\overline{\mathbf{K}}
is preferable.

Theorem
4
shows that the rate of convergence is governed entirely by
\overline{\mathbf{K}}
. The clearest setting when this is favourable is if
\mathbf{K}
is diagonal, so that
\overline{\mathbf{K}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{K}}
. In this case, the convergence rate is, up to constants

\frac{1}{d}\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\overline{\mathbf{K}})}{\lambda_{\min}(\overline{\mathbf{K}})}=\frac{1}{d}\frac{\operatorname{Tr}({\overline{\mathbf{K}}})}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K})}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{Tr}({\mathbf{K}})}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K})}}.

(32)

Hence on diagonal problems,
signSGD
attains a speedup over
SGD
commensurate to the speedup of optimal deterministic convex optimization algorithms such as Conjugate gradient over gradient descent
(
Nocedal & Wright, 2006
)
.

Diagonally dominant matrices
should see similar benefits — consistent with prior work showing that
signSGD
is effective when the
the Hessian of the risk
(
\mathbf{K}
in our setting) is sufficiently diagonally concentrated
(
Balles et al., 2020
)
.

A second situation in which one may have substantial speedups are for block-diagonal
\mathbf{K}
, where the blocks are scaled by greatly differing constants; diagonal preconditioning by
\mathbf{D}
partially corrects for this effect. It has been argued that one of the principal advantages of
Adam
is that it correctly adapts learning rates across different layers of Transformers and MLPs
(
Zhang et al., 2024
)
, which can have similar structures in their Jacobians.

Settings in which
\overline{\mathbf{K}}
does not help.

Like preconditioning generally,
\overline{\mathbf{K}}
does not always have a smaller condition number than
\mathbf{K}
. See Appendix
H
for a counter example.

In addition, if the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{K}
are randomized to make a new covariance matrix
\mathbf{A}
, say by performing a uniformly random orthogonal change of basis, the entries of the diagonal of
\mathbf{A}
will concentrate to be

\max_{i}\left|\mathbf{A}_{ii}-\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{K})}{d}\right|=O((\log d)d^{-1/2}),

(33)

and so the preconditioner
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{A})^{-1/2}
does not affect the condition number of
\mathbf{A}
.
Thus, the benefit of diagonal preconditioning is strongly tied to special properties of the basis in which the optimization is performed
(
Nocedal & Wright, 2006
)
.

4.4
Gradient noise reshaping

Finally, there is gradient noise reshaping, wherein the
SGD
gradient noise matrix
\mathbf{K}
is replaced by the matrix
\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}
up to constants. This is a complicated mapping; there is no short answer about the impact of this effect.
In some cases passing from
\mathbf{K}\to\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}
might affect the magnitudes of the eigenvalues but not their structure; see Figure
4
for an example on CIFAR10.

In the case that
\mathbf{K}
itself is a sample covariance matrix, the matrix
\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}
is strongly related to a
kernel inner product matrix
, for which there is a large literature. This includes properties of bulk spectra
(
Karoui, 2010
;
Cheng & Singer, 2013
)
, norms
(
Fan & Montanari, 2019
)
and more.

When
\mathbf{K}
is a diagonal matrix then
\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}=\frac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{I}
and so this can be considered a type of preconditioning of the gradient noise, albeit with a
more
aggressive preconditioner than
\mathbf{D}
.

We
expect
that for
\mathbf{K}
with power-law spectra, often seen in practice
in vision (e.g. in Figure
4
) and language embeddings,
\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}
again has powerlaw spectra of the same exponent. Beyond the spectral distribution, replacing
\mathbf{K}
by
\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}
may also serve to slightly
break the alignment of large directions of gradient variance from large gradient biases (they are perfectly aligned in
SGD
), which should be beneficial both to stability of the algorithm and performance.

(see Remark

found in the drift term of

becomes the expectation of a function of the risk across a distribution that differs from the original noise. This can have significant effects on the noise compared to single-pass.
Indeed, when

\omega_{i}
as defined in (

\epsilon^{(i)}
. Which is to say that
\varphi^{(b)}
may have improve decay rate of no-worse than

when
\mathcal{R}
approaches

.
This due to the addition of a density
\omega_{i}
.

can be bounded from below by

Cf(0)
where

6
Discussion

Our high-dimensional limit sheds a quantitative light on the precise ways in which
signSGD
can be compared to
SGD
, via change of
effective learning rate
,
noise compression
,
preconditioning
, and
reshaping of the gradient noise
.

Theorem
2
, the main technical contribution of this work, required substantial technical efforts. Although similar in formulation to
existing work like
Collins-Woodfin et al. (2024)
, there are technical complexities in working with the nonsmooth
\sigma
function: both in terms of deriving the
relevant concentration of measure estimates (the textbook versions of which require smoothness) and in terms of the additional pathology of the resulting

signHSGD
(especially the
\varphi
). We believe that a version of Theorem
2
is true in much greater generality than we have proven
it, even for the linear setting: two desirable mathematical generalizations are
quantifying dimension
intrinsically
(instead of through the ambient dimension) and generalizing the theory to settings of non-Gaussian data.
We note that our analysis is in a qualitatively different regime than the concurrent work of
Compagnoni et al. (2025)
; see
Appendix
G
for more details.

More detailed analysis of the effects of non-diagonal covariance is a key future direction.
There is an active area of research on the benefits of
non-diagonal

preconditioning methods as an alternative to Adam variants, and studying the non-diagonal case in
signSGD
may
provide more insight into cases where the diagonal preconditioner is insufficient to improve optimization. Our SDEs and ODEs provide
the tools to analyze such settings.

Though our work focuses on the case of MSE loss and linear regression, there is a path towards extending results to more general settings using
recent results in high-dimensional optimization
(
Collins-Woodfin et al., 2023
)
.
In practical settings, models undergo dramatic changes in local geometry during training;
nonetheless, stability analysis of the linearized problem is still useful for understanding
aspects of the non-linear dynamics of these systems
(
Cohen et al., 2022
;
Agarwala & Pennington, 2024
)
.

Finally, our analysis of
signSGD
gives hints towards understanding
Adam
in a similar setting. A heuristic analysis shows that
Adam
has a homogenized process similar to
signHSGD
: it appears to share the
preconditioner
\mathbf{D}
while differing from
signHSGD
in its noise compression and gradient noise effects
\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}
(Appendix
F
).
For well-behaved noises
\epsilon
,
signSGD
should be nearly path-identical to
Adam
; we note that
Lion
has been recently observed to do just that
(
Zhao et al., 2024
)
.
We leave investigation of
Adam
for future work.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine Learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

Appendix A
Proof of Main Theorem

A.1
Approximation of the conditional updates

For simplicity of our proofs, we will assume
\eta
is constant as well as batch-size is
b=1
. The proof remains unchanged if
\eta
is defined as in Assumption
4
and if
b>1
. For the convenience of the reader and to avoid confusion, we provide the typical notions of convergence in high-dimensions.

Definition 3
.

An event
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
holds with high-probability, if there exists some
\delta>0
independent to
d
such that
\mathbb{P}(A)\geq 1-Cd^{-\delta}
for some
C
independent to
d
.

Definition 4
.

An event
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
holds with overwhelming-probability, if for all
\delta>0
there exists
C_{\delta}
such that
\mathbb{P}(A)\geq 1-C_{\delta}d^{-\delta}
.

Denote
\mathcal{F}_{k}
to be the natural filtration generated by the data
\{\mathbf{x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
and the label noise
\{\epsilon_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
. For notational convenience, we define the ”centered”
signSGD
iterate
\bm{\nu}_{k}=\bm{\theta}_{k}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}}
. We also denote
\mathcal{R}_{k}=\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta}_{k})
when it is clear.

Notice now, that the
k+1
th update of
signSGD
is given by

\bm{\nu}_{k+1}-\bm{\nu}_{k}=-\frac{\eta}{d}\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\sigma(\left\langle\mathbf{x}_{k+1},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle-\epsilon_{k+1}).

(34)

A key component of our proof aims to compute the mean of this update (conditioned on
\mathscr{F}_{k}
) and then simplify this mean by introducing errors which vanish in high-dimensions.

Lemma 1
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, the mean of the
i
-th element of (
34
) is given by

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\nu}^{i}_{k+1}-\bm{\nu}^{i}_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=-\frac{\eta}{d}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}2h_{k}^{i}(0)\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{\eta}{d}\mathbb{E}[\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{k+1})R_{k+1}^{i}],

(35)

where, denoting
\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}
as the law of the noise,

h^{i}_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\left(2\mathcal{R}_{k}-\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle^{2}\right)}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(\frac{-(x+y)^{2}}{2\left(2\mathcal{R}_{k}-\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle^{2}\right)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}(y),

(36)

and

R^{i}_{k+1}=O\left(\left(\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{K}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle}{\mathbf{K}_{ii}}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}\right)^{3}\right).

(37)

Proof.

Following the update rule (
1
), we start by computing the conditional update of the
i
-th entry of the iterates

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\nu}^{i}_{k+1}-\bm{\nu}^{i}_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{k+1})\sigma(\left\langle\mathbf{x}_{k+1},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle-\epsilon_{k+1})|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

(38)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{k+1})\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma\left(\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\bm{\nu}^{j}_{k}\mathbf{x}^{j}_{k+1}-\epsilon_{k+1}\right)\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{k},\,\mathbf{x}^{i}_{k+1}\right]\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right].

(39)

Given that the data is Gaussian distributed, upon conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{k}
we see that

\sum_{j\neq i}\bm{\nu}^{j}_{k}\mathbf{x}^{j}_{k+1}\sim N(0,2\mathcal{R}_{k}-2\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\left\langle\mathbf{K}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle+\mathbf{K}_{ii}(\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i})^{2}).

(40)

Additionally, for
c_{i}
is any constant, we can write

\sum_{j\neq i}\bm{\nu}^{j}_{k}\mathbf{x}^{j}_{k+1}=\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\bm{\nu}^{j}_{k}\mathbf{x}^{j}_{k+1}-c_{i}\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}\right)+c_{i}\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}.

(41)

Let
y^{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}\bm{\nu}^{j}_{k}\mathbf{x}^{j}_{k+1}-c_{i}\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}
. Choosing
c_{i}=\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{K}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle-\mathbf{K}_{ii}\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}}{\mathbf{K}_{ii}\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}}
,
makes

y^{i}
uncorrelated to
\mathbf{x}^{i}_{k+1}
and hence independent. Additionally, notice
y^{i}\sim N(0,2\mathcal{R}_{k}-\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle^{2})
. Moreover, since
y^{i}
is independent to
\epsilon_{k+1}
their difference
y_{i}-\epsilon_{k+1}
has density given by

h^{i}_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\left(2\mathcal{R}_{k}-\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle^{2}\right)}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(\frac{-(x+y)^{2}}{2\left(2\mathcal{R}_{k}-\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle^{2}\right)}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}(y).

(42)

Using (
42
), we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma\left(\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\bm{\nu}_{k}^{j}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{j}-\epsilon_{k+1}\right)\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{k},\mathbf{x}^{i}_{k+1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma\left(y^{i}-\epsilon_{k+1}+(1+c_{i})\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}\right)\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{k},\mathbf{x}^{i}_{k+1}}\left(y_{i}-\epsilon_{k+1}>-(1+c_{i})\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}\right)-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{k},\mathbf{x}^{i}_{k+1}}\left(y_{i}-\epsilon_{k+1}\leq-(1+c_{i})\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{-(1+c_{i})\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}}^{\infty}h^{i}_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{-\infty}^{-(1+c_{i})\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}}h^{i}_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(43)

Define

H(s)=\int_{-s}^{\infty}h^{i}_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{-\infty}^{-s}h^{i}_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(44)

where upon differentiating it is easy to see that

H^{\prime}(s)=2h_{k}^{i}(-s).

(45)

Taylor expanding around
0
we obtain,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma\left(\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\bm{\nu}^{j}_{k}\mathbf{x}^{j}_{k+1}-\epsilon_{k+1}\right)\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{k},\,\mathbf{x}^{i}_{k+1}\right]

\displaystyle=H((1+c_{i})\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i})

\displaystyle=H(0)+2h_{k}^{i}(0)\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{K}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle}{\mathbf{K}_{ii}}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}h_{k}^{i}(0)\left(\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{K}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle}{\mathbf{K}_{ii}}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}\right)^{2}+R^{i}_{k+1},

(46)

where

R^{i}_{k+1}=O\left(\left(\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{K}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle}{\mathbf{K}_{ii}}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}\right)^{3}\right).

(47)

Plugging this back into (
39
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\nu}^{i}_{k+1}-\bm{\nu}^{i}_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i})\left(H(0)+2h_{k}^{i}(0)\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{K}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle}{\mathbf{K}_{ii}}\mathbf{x}^{i}_{k+1}\right)\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\eta}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i})\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}h_{k}^{i}(0)\left(\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{K}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle}{\mathbf{K}_{ii}}\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}\right)^{2}+R^{i}_{k+1}\right)\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta}{d}2h_{k}^{i}(0)\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{K}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle}{\mathbf{K}_{ii}}\mathbb{E}|\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i}|-\frac{\eta}{d}\mathbb{E}[\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{k+1})R_{k+1}^{i}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta}{d}2h_{k}^{i}(0)\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}-\frac{\eta}{d}\mathbb{E}[\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{i}_{k+1})R_{k+1}^{i}].

(48)

∎

In the next two lemmas we will show that for all
1\leq i\leq d
, the risk dependent factors
2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}h_{k}^{i}(0)
can be well-approximated by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})
, where

\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{4\mathcal{R}_{k}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}(y).

(49)

To do this, we will show that:
\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle=O(d^{-s})
for some
s>0
. Additionally, this would also imply
the error
R^{i}_{k+1}
vanishes as
d\to\infty
. This simplifies (
35
) by removing the latter error term and reducing each
h^{i}_{k}
into a single constant factor, i.e.

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\nu}_{k+1}^{i}-\bm{\nu}_{k}^{i}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\approx-\frac{\eta}{d}\frac{\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle.

(50)

Our proof makes use of the
resolvent

\mathbf{R}(z;\mathbf{K})=(\mathbf{K}-z\mathbf{I})^{-1}
, a matrix valued function essentially encoding powers of
\mathbf{K}
. The following lemma will allows us to to control
\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle
by a finite net of resolvents.

Lemma 2
.

There exists a net
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
of order
O(d)
and
C(\overline{\mathbf{K}})>0
such that for all
k
and
1\leq i\leq d
,

|\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle|\leq C_{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\bm{\nu}_{k}}\right|.

(51)

Proof.

It is easy to check by the Cauchy’s integral formula that

\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}z\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\bm{\nu}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}z.

(52)

By Assumption
3
, we may bound
\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}}\right|
for all
z\in\Gamma
by a finite collection of
z_{0}\in\Gamma
. Indeed, if
\Gamma_{0}
is a
1/\sqrt{d}
-net on
\Gamma
then
|\Gamma_{0}|=O(d)
. It follows that for all
z\in\Gamma
, there exists some
z_{0}\in\Gamma_{0}
such that
|z-z_{0}|\leq 1/\sqrt{d}
. Then, by resolvent identities we see that for all
1\leq i\leq d
and
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{a}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\mathbf{R}(z_{0};\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{a}+(z-z_{0})[\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})\mathbf{R}(z_{0};\overline{\mathbf{K}})]_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{a}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\mathbf{R}(z_{0};\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{a}}\right|+\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\left\|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}}\right\|\left\|{\mathbf{R}(z_{0};\overline{\mathbf{K}})\mathbf{a}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq(1+M_{R})\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z_{0};\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{a}}\right|.

(53)

In particular,

\max_{z\in\Gamma}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{a}}\right|\leq(1+M_{R})\max_{z_{0}\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z_{0};\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{a}}\right|.

(54)

Plugging this into (
52
),

\displaystyle\left|{\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\Gamma}\left|{z}\right|(1+M_{R})\max_{\mathbf{z}_{0}\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z_{0};\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\bm{\nu}_{k}}\right|\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=4(1+M_{R})\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|^{2}\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\bm{\nu}_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle=C_{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\bm{\nu}_{k}}\right|.

(55)

∎

Note that terms such as
\eta
and
\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|
are bounded by assumption, thus we make the convention moving forward any constants independent to
d
such as
C_{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}
may change from line to line. Therefore, to show that
\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\rangle
shrinks as
d\to\infty
, it suffices to show that
\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}_{k}}\right|
shrinks as
d\to\infty.

Before we do so, it will be convenient to work under the setting that the risk is bounded. As such, let
L>0
and define the following stopping time,

\tau_{0}=\min\left\{k;\,\left\|{\bm{\nu}_{k}}\right\|>L\right\},

(56)

as well as the stopped process
\mathbf{v}_{k}=\bm{\nu}_{k\wedge\tau_{0}}
. We show in Lemma
8
that
L
may be chosen so that
\mathbf{v}_{k}=\bm{\nu}_{k}
with overwhelming probability, effectively removing the bounded constraint.

Lemma 3
.

Given Assumptions
1
-
5
, there exists a net
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
of order
O(d)
, such that for all
t>0
and
1/6+\delta_{0}<\delta<1/4
,

\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\max_{0\leq k\leq\lfloor td\rfloor}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}_{k}}\right|<\frac{d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}

(57)

with high-probability.

Proof.

For clarity of notation, let
\widetilde{h}^{i}_{k}=2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}h^{i}_{k}(0)
and
\widetilde{h}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})
. In addition, define
\mathbf{A}_{k}
be a diagonal matrix with entries given by
\widetilde{h}_{k}^{i}
, as well as the vector
\mathbf{E}_{k+1}=(\mathbb{E}[\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i})R_{k+1}^{i}])_{i=1}^{d}
. By (
35
), for a fixed
z\in\Gamma_{0}
and
1\leq i\leq d
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}(\mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{k})|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta}{d}\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\left(\mathbf{A}_{k}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}+\mathbf{E}_{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta}{d}\widetilde{h}_{k}\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\eta}{d}\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\left(\mathbf{A}_{k}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}-\widetilde{h}_{k}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}+\mathbf{E}_{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta}{d}\widetilde{h}_{k}\left(z\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}_{k}+\mathbf{v}_{k}^{i}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\eta}{d}\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\left(\mathbf{A}_{k}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}-\widetilde{h}_{k}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}+\mathbf{E}_{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta}{d}\widetilde{h}_{k}z\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}_{k}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{\eta}{d}\left(-\widetilde{h}_{k}\mathbf{v}_{k}^{i}+\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\left(\widetilde{h}_{k}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{A}_{k}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}-\mathbf{E}_{k+1}\right)\right)}_{\coloneqq\mathcal{E}^{i}_{k}(z)}.

(58)

By the Doob decomposition we see that,

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}_{k}+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}(\mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{k})|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]+\Delta M^{i}_{k+1}(z)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\eta}{d}\widetilde{h}_{k}z\right)\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}_{k}+\mathcal{E}^{i}_{k}(z)+\Delta M^{i}_{k+1}(z),

(59)

where
\Delta M_{k+1}^{i}(z)
are the martingale increments of
\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\left(\mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{k}\right)
. Let

L_{k}=\prod_{j=0}^{k}\left(1-\frac{\eta}{d}\widetilde{h}_{j}z\right),

then upon iterating (
59
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}_{k+1}=L_{k}\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}_{0}+L_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{1}{L_{j}}\left(\mathcal{E}^{i}_{j}(z)+\Delta M_{j+1}^{i}(z)\right).

(60)

It is easy to check that
\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{1}{L_{j}}\Delta M_{j+1}^{i}(z)
is a martingale so we shall denote it by
{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathcal{M}}_{k+1}^{i}(z)
.
Let

\tau_{1}=\min\left\{k;\quad\left|{\mathbf{R}(z,\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}_{k}}\right|\geq\frac{d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}\text{ for some }1\leq i\leq d\text{ and }z\in\Gamma_{0}\right\}.

(61)

It suffices to show (
57
) holds for the stopped process
\mathbf{v}_{k\wedge\tau_{1}}
given that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq\lfloor td\rfloor}\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}_{k}}\right|\geq\frac{d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}\,\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}_{\lfloor td\rfloor\wedge\tau_{1}}}\right|\geq\frac{d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

(62)

For notational clarity, we will write
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{k}=\mathbf{v}_{k\wedge\tau_{1}}
. Note that (
60
) holds all
k
, so it must also hold for the stopped process
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
. Given that the entries of
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
move at increments of
\frac{\eta}{d}
, we observe the following bound on the stopped process,

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}+\frac{\eta M_{R}}{\sqrt{d}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta M^{\prime}_{R}d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}.

(63)

Moreover, by Lemma
12
we know that

\widetilde{h}_{k}\leq M_{\epsilon}
. This in turn implies
L_{k}
is bounded from above and below. Indeed,

\displaystyle\left|{L_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=0}^{k}\left|{1-\frac{\eta}{d}\widetilde{h}_{k}z}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j=0}^{k}1+\frac{\eta M_{\epsilon}\left|{z}\right|}{d}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{2\eta M_{\epsilon}\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|}{d}\right)^{\lfloor td\rfloor}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left({\eta C_{t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}}\right).

(64)

Similarly for the lower bound,

\displaystyle\left|{L_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{j=0}^{k}1-\frac{\eta}{d}\widetilde{h}_{k}\left|{z}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{2\eta M_{\epsilon}\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|}{d}\right)^{\lfloor td\rfloor}

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(\frac{-\frac{2\eta M_{\epsilon}\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|}{d}\lfloor td\rfloor}{1-\frac{2\eta M_{\epsilon}\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|}{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-\frac{2\eta M_{\epsilon}\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|}{d}\lfloor td\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\eta C_{t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right),

(65)

provided that
\frac{\eta M_{\epsilon}\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|}{d}<\frac{1}{2}
.
Therefore, up to a constant factor

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta,t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\left(\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{0}}\right|+\left|{{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathcal{M}}_{k+1}^{i}(z)}\right|+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left|{\mathcal{E}_{j}^{i}(z)}\right|\right).

(66)

We will now bound the error
\mathcal{E}^{i}_{j}(z)
. By (
51
), we already know that

\displaystyle|\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}\right\rangle|\leq C_{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}}\right|.

(67)

Similarly,

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{i}_{j}|\leq C_{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}}\right|.

(68)

We also observe for all
1\leq i\leq d
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{j+1}^{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{j+1}^{i})R^{i}_{j+1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{K}_{i},\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}\right\rangle\mathbf{x}_{j+1}^{i}}{\mathbf{K}_{ii}}\right|^{3}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(|\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}\right\rangle|^{3}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\left(\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}}\right|\right)^{3}\right).

(69)

In particular, for some constant
C_{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}>0
,

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbf{E}_{j+1}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(E_{j+1}^{i}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d}C_{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\left(\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}}\right|\right)^{3}.

(70)

For our last error term we apply the Lipschitz bound obtained by Lemma
12
. That is the map

s\mapsto\psi(s)=\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt{s}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\epsilon}(y),

(71)

is Lipschitz with constant
L_{\epsilon}
. Moreover,
\psi(2\mathcal{R}_{j}-\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}\right\rangle^{2})=\widetilde{h}_{j}^{i}
and
\psi(2\mathcal{R}_{j})=\widetilde{h}_{j}
. By (
67
), for all
1\leq i\leq d
,

\displaystyle|\widetilde{h}_{j}^{i}-\widetilde{h}_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\epsilon}\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leq L_{\epsilon}\left(C_{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}}\right|\right)^{2}.

(72)

It follows that

\displaystyle\left|{\widetilde{h}_{j}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}-\mathbf{A}_{j}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\widetilde{h}_{j}\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{A}_{j}}\right\|\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\epsilon}\left(C_{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}}\right|\right)^{2}\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\sqrt{d}\left(\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}}\right|\right)^{3}.

(73)

For notational clarity, let us write
\omega_{k}=\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{k}}\right|
.
Putting all this together we have up to constant factor,

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{E}^{i}_{j}(z)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{d}\left(\left|{\widetilde{h}_{j}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{i}}\right|+\left\|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})}\right\|\left(\left\|{\widetilde{h}_{j}\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{A}_{j}}\right\|\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{j}}\right\|+\left\|{\mathbf{E}_{j+1}}\right\|\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta C_{\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}}{d}\left(\omega_{j}+2\sqrt{d}\omega_{j}^{3}\right).

(74)

Returning to (
66
), upon taking the max across
z\in\Gamma_{0}
and
1\leq i\leq d
and up to a constant
C_{\eta,t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}>0
, we obtain for all
k\leq\lfloor td\rfloor

\displaystyle\omega_{k}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta,t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\left(\omega_{0}+\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\max_{1\leq k\leq\lfloor td\rfloor}{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathcal{M}}_{k}^{i}(z)+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{\eta}{d}\left(\omega_{j}+2\sqrt{d}\omega_{j}^{3}\right)\right).

(75)

Define

\beta_{t}=C_{\eta,t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\left(\omega_{0}+\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\max_{1\leq k\leq\lfloor td\rfloor}{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathcal{M}}_{k}^{i}(z)\right),

(76)

as well as the stopping time

\displaystyle\tau_{2}=\min\left\{k\,;\omega_{k}\geq 3\beta_{t}\exp\left(C_{\eta,t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right)\right\}.

(77)

As before, we note that
\omega_{k}
can only move at increments of at-most
\frac{\eta M_{r}}{\sqrt{d}}
. Thus,

\omega_{k\wedge\tau_{2}}\leq 3\beta_{t}\exp(C_{\eta,t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}})+\frac{\eta M_{r}}{\sqrt{d}}\eqqcolon\beta^{\prime}_{t},

(78)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Plugging this into (
75
),

\displaystyle\omega_{\lfloor td\rfloor\wedge\tau_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{t}+C_{\eta,t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{(\lfloor td\rfloor-1)\wedge\tau_{2}}\frac{\eta}{d}\omega_{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor td\rfloor}\frac{\eta}{d}\left(2\sqrt{d}\omega_{j}^{3}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{t}+C_{\eta,t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\left[2\sqrt{d}(\beta_{t}^{\prime})^{3}\right]+\sum_{j=0}^{(\lfloor td\rfloor-1)\wedge\tau_{2}}C_{\eta,t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\frac{\eta}{d}\omega_{j}.

(79)

By Gronwall’s inequality,

\displaystyle\omega_{\lfloor td\rfloor\wedge\tau_{2}}\leq\left(\beta_{t}+C_{\eta,t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\sqrt{d}\left[2(\beta_{t}^{\prime})^{3}\right]\right)\exp\left(C_{\eta,t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right).

(80)

If we can show that
\beta_{t}
can be made sufficiently small so that

\displaystyle C_{\eta,t,\epsilon,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\left[\sqrt{d}(\beta_{t}^{\prime})^{3}\right]\leq\beta_{t},

(81)

then
\omega_{\lfloor td\rfloor\wedge\tau_{2}}=\omega_{\lfloor td\rfloor}
. To see this, recall by Assumption
5
we know that for any constant
\xi>0
, the former term of
\beta_{t}
has the following tail bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}_{0}}\right|\geq\frac{\xi d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\leq Cd^{2}\exp\left(-c^{\prime}\xi^{2}d^{2\delta}\right).

(82)

To bound the martingale term, we first fix
z\in\Gamma_{0}
and
1\leq i\leq d
, then let

\tau_{3}=\min\left\{k\,;\,|{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathcal{M}}_{k}^{i}(z)|\geq\frac{\xi d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}\right\}.

(83)

Let
X_{k}={}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathcal{M}}_{k\wedge\tau_{3}}^{i}(z)
. Notice that
\mathbb{E}\left[{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathcal{M}}_{k}^{i}(z)\right]=0
, so
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{k}\right]=0
. It follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq\lfloor td\rfloor}|{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathcal{M}}_{k}^{i}(z)|\geq\frac{\xi d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|{X_{\lfloor td\rfloor}}\right|\geq\frac{\xi d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

(84)

Notice that

\displaystyle\left|{{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathcal{M}}_{k+1}^{i}(z)-{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathcal{M}}_{k}^{i}(z)}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|{L_{k}}\right|}\left|{\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}(\mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{k})-\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})_{i}^{\rm T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{k}\big|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{t,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\eta M_{R}}{\sqrt{d}}.

(85)

Hence,
\left|{X_{k}-X_{k-1}}\right|\leq\frac{C_{t,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\eta M_{R}}{\sqrt{d}}
almost surely for all
k
. However, we may improve this increment bound by
\frac{d^{s}}{d}
for
\frac{1}{6}+\delta_{0}<s<\delta
.
Indeed, by Corollary
2
for all even moments
2p<d
, there exists a constant
C(2p,\eta,\mathbf{K})
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|{X_{k+1}-X_{k}}\right|\geq\frac{d^{s}}{d}\right)\leq C(2p,\eta,\mathbf{K})d^{p\left(\frac{1}{3}-2s+2\delta_{0}\right)}.

(86)

It follows by Lemma
15
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|{X_{\lfloor td\rfloor}}\right|\geq\frac{\xi d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^{2}d^{2(\delta-s)}}{C_{\eta,t,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}}\right)+\lfloor td\rfloor C(2p,\eta,\mathbf{K})d^{p\left(\frac{1}{3}-2s+2\delta_{0}\right)}

(87)

Thus, taking union bounds across
z\in\Gamma_{0}
and
1\leq i\leq d
gives,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{z\in\Gamma_{0}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\max_{1\leq k\leq\lfloor td\rfloor+1}\left|{{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathcal{M}}^{i}_{k}(z)}\right|\geq\frac{\xi d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq Cd^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^{2}d^{2(\delta-s)}}{C_{\eta,t,\overline{\mathbf{K}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2Cd^{2}\lfloor td\rfloor C(2p,\eta,\mathbf{K})d^{p\left(\frac{1}{3}-2s+2\delta_{0}\right)}

(88)

It is easy to see that for
d
sufficiently large, we may choose
p
large so that
s>\frac{1}{6}+\delta_{0}+\frac{3}{2p}
, implying the latter term converges to
0
as
d\to\infty
. Therefore,
\beta_{t}\leq\frac{\xi d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}
with high-probability. Returning to (
81
), up to a constant factor that is independent to
d
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{d}\beta_{t}^{3}>\beta_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\beta_{t}^{2}>\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{d^{2\delta}}{d}>\beta_{t}^{2}>\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\beta_{t}^{2}>\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}},\,\beta_{t}^{2}\geq\frac{d^{2\delta}}{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\beta_{t}\geq\frac{d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(89)

provided that
\delta<1/4
. Thus, (
81
) is satisfied and
\omega_{\lfloor td\rfloor\wedge\tau_{2}}=\omega_{\lfloor td\rfloor}
with high-probability.
By choosing
\xi
appropriately in accordance to (
77
), we conclude
\omega_{\lfloor td\rfloor}\leq\frac{d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}
with high-probability.
∎

We can now formalize our prior statement of

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{v}_{k+1}^{i}-\mathbf{v}_{k}^{i}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\approx-\frac{\eta}{d}\frac{\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\rangle,

(90)

for all
1\leq i\leq d
.

Lemma 4
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, the mean of (
34
) is

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{k}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=-\frac{\eta\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})}{d\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}+\mathbf{E}_{k+1},

(91)

where

is an error term such that for all

, with high-probability

for all

.

Proof.

By (
35
), the coordinate-wise error can be defined as

\mathbf{E}^{i}_{k+1}=\frac{\eta}{d}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{i})R^{i}_{k+1}\right]+\frac{\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\rangle-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}2h^{i}_{k}(0)\left\langle\overline{\mathbf{K}}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\rangle\right).

(92)

Applying Lemma
3
onto (
69
) and (
72
) yields the result.
∎

A.2
Convergence of
signSGD
to
signHSGD

In this section we will show convergence of the dynamics of
signSGD
to that of
signHSGD
. Recall
signHSGD
is defined as in (
8
). Similarly to
signSGD
we will impose a stopping time onto
signHSGD
,

\tau_{0}^{\prime}=\min_{t>0}\left\{t;\,\left\|{\bm{\Theta}_{t}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}}}\right\|>L\right\}.

(93)

We will also define the stopped process by
\mathbf{V}_{t}=\bm{\Theta}_{t\wedge\tau_{0}^{\prime}}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}}.

We will prove the main result for the stopped
signSGD
process
\mathbf{v}_{k}
and stopped
signHSGD
process
\mathbf{V}_{t}
, then in Lemma
8
we will generalize to the non-stopped process
\bm{\theta}_{k}
and
\bm{\Theta}_{t}
.

We shall use the following:

Definition 5

(Quadratic)
.

A function
q:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is quadratic if it may be written in the form

q(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\rm T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}^{\rm T}\mathbf{x}+c

for some
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Once again, for notational convenience we will denote
\sigma_{k+1}=\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\sigma(\left\langle\mathbf{x}_{k+1},\bm{\nu}_{k}\right\rangle-\epsilon_{k+1})
when it is clear.
Now if
q:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is quadratic, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{v}_{k+1})-q(\mathbf{v}_{k})=-\frac{\eta}{d}\nabla q(\mathbf{v}_{k})^{\rm T}\left(\sigma_{k+1}\right)+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2d^{2}}(\sigma_{k+1})^{\rm T}\nabla^{2}q(\mathbf{v}_{k})(\sigma_{k+1}).

(94)

Thus, taking its conditional expectation we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[q(\mathbf{v}_{k+1})-q(\mathbf{v}_{k})|\mathcal{F}_{k}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})}{d\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}\nabla q(\mathbf{v}_{k})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}+\frac{2\eta^{2}}{d^{2}\pi}\left\langle\nabla^{2}q(\mathbf{v}_{k}),\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}\right\rangle+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right).

(95)

By the Doob-decomposition, we have

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{v}_{k+1})-q(\mathbf{v}_{k})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})}{d\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}\nabla q(\mathbf{v}_{k})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}+\frac{2\eta^{2}}{d^{2}\pi}\left\langle\nabla^{2}q(\mathbf{v}_{k}),\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}\right\rangle

(96)

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right)+\Delta\mathcal{M}_{k+1}^{lin}+\Delta\mathcal{M}_{k+1}^{quad},

(97)

where

\Delta\mathcal{M}_{k+1}^{lin}=-\frac{\eta}{d}\nabla q(\mathbf{v}_{k})^{\rm T}\left(\sigma_{k+1}-\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{k+1}|\mathcal{F}_{k}]\right),

(98)

and

\Delta\mathcal{M}_{k+1}^{quad}=\frac{\eta^{2}}{2d^{2}}\left(\sigma_{k+1}^{\rm T}\nabla^{2}q(\mathbf{v}_{k})\sigma_{k+1}-\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{k+1}^{\rm T}\nabla^{2}q(\mathbf{v}_{k})\sigma_{k+1}|\mathcal{F}_{k}]\right).

(99)

Similarly, by Ito’s lemma on
\mathbf{V}_{t}
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}q(\mathbf{V}_{t})=\left(-\frac{\eta\varphi(\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{V}_{t}))}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{V}_{t})}}\nabla q(\mathbf{V}_{t})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{V}_{t}+\frac{2\eta^{2}}{\pi d}\left\langle\nabla^{2}q(V_{t}),\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}\right\rangle\right)\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}\mathcal{M}^{\sigma}_{t},

(100)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\sigma}=\eta\nabla q(\mathbf{V}_{t})^{\rm T}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}}{d\pi}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}\right).

(101)

Comparing (
97
) and (
100
), we see that predictable part of signSGD and the total variation part of HSGD depend only on
\nabla q(\mathbf{x})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{x}
and
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x})
. We capture these statistics in a “closed” manifold defined by

\displaystyle Q_{q}=\left\{\mathbf{x}^{\rm T}\mathbf{x},\,q(\mathbf{x}),\,\nabla q(\mathbf{x})^{\rm T}\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})\mathbf{x},\,\mathbf{x}^{\rm T}\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})^{\rm T}\nabla^{2}q(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{R}(y;\overline{\mathbf{K}})\mathbf{x};\,\,z,y\in\Gamma\right\}.

(102)

To be precise in our notion of closure, given any
g\in Q_{q}
, the predictable part of (
97
) (not accounting the error) and the drift part of (
100
) may be expressed via contour integral around
\Gamma
by a linear combination of functions from
Q_{q}
. Let us look at an example. Suppose
g(\mathbf{x})=\nabla q(\mathbf{x})^{\rm T}\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})\mathbf{x}
. It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla g(\mathbf{v}_{k})^{\rm T}(\mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{k})|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})}{d\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}\left(\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\rm T}\nabla^{2}q\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}+\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\rm T}\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})^{\rm T}\nabla^{2}q\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right)

(103)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})}{d\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}\left(\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\rm T}\nabla^{2}q\mathbf{v}_{k}\right)+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\eta\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})}{d\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}\underbrace{\left(z\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\rm T}\nabla^{2}q\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})\mathbf{v}_{k}+\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\rm T}\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})^{\rm T}\nabla^{2}q\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}\right)}_{p(\mathbf{v}_{k})}.

(104)

Notice that our error
O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right)
is independent to choice of
g
. This is because the resolvent
\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})
has uniformly bounded operator norm for all
z\in\Gamma
, thus the
\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{C^{2}}
is also uniformly bounded for all
g\in Q_{q}
. It then follows that

\nabla g(\mathbf{v}_{k})^{\rm T}\mathbf{E}_{k+1}\leq\left\|{\nabla g(\mathbf{v}_{k})}\right\|\left\|{\mathbf{E}_{k+1}}\right\|\leq\left\|{g}\right\|_{C^{2}}(1+\left\|{\mathbf{v}_{k}}\right\|)\left\|{\mathbf{E}_{k+1}}\right\|=O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right).

(105)

In addition, by the Cauchy’s integral theorem we may express
p(\mathbf{v}_{k})
by

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{v}_{k})=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}z\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\rm T}\mathbf{R}(y;\overline{\mathbf{K}})^{\rm T}\nabla^{2}q\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})\mathbf{v}_{k}+y\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\rm T}\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})^{\rm T}\nabla^{2}q\mathbf{R}(y;\overline{\mathbf{K}})\mathbf{v}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}y,

(106)

as well as

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{k}^{\rm T}\nabla^{2}q\mathbf{v}_{k}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\oint_{\Gamma}\oint_{\Gamma}\mathbf{v}^{\rm T}_{k}\mathbf{R}(z;\overline{\mathbf{K}})\nabla^{2}q\mathbf{R}(y;\overline{\mathbf{K}})\mathbf{v}_{k}\,\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}y.

(107)

Consequently, we see that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla g(\mathbf{v}_{k})^{\rm T}(\mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{k})|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})}{d\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}12\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|^{2}\max_{g\in Q_{q}}|g(\mathbf{v}_{k})|+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right)

(108)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{12\eta M_{\epsilon}}{d}\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|^{2}\max_{g\in Q_{q}}|g(\mathbf{v}_{k})|+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right),

(109)

where we applied Lemma
12
in the second inequality. Note the constant factor of
12\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|^{2}
depended on
g
. We may work around this quadratic dependent constant to obtain a uniform bound on (
109
) for all
g\in Q_{q}
with the following lemma:

Lemma 5
.

Let
Q_{q}
be defined as above then for all
n>0
there exists
{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muQ}_{q}\subset Q_{q}
such that
|{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muQ}_{q}|\leq C(\overline{\mathbf{K}})d^{4n}
and for all
g\in Q_{q}
, there exists
g_{0}\in{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muQ}_{q}
satisfying
\left\|{g-g_{0}}\right\|_{C^{2}}\leq d^{-2n}
.

The proof of Lemma
5
may be found in
Collins-Woodfin et al. (2024)
.

Lemma 6
.

There exists constants
C(\overline{\mathbf{K}}),M_{\epsilon}>0
such that for all
g\in Q_{q}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\rho\in(1/6+\delta_{0},1/4)
,

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla g(\mathbf{v}_{k})^{\rm T}(\mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{k})|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\right|\leq\frac{\eta M_{\epsilon}}{d}C(\overline{\mathbf{K}})\max_{g\in Q_{q}}|g(\mathbf{v}_{k})|+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right).

(110)

Proof.

Let
g\in Q_{q}
and
n>0
, then by Lemma
5
there exists
g_{0}\in{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muQ}_{q}
such that
\left\|{g-g_{0}}\right\|_{C^{2}}\leq d^{-2n}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla g(\mathbf{v}_{k})^{\rm T}(\mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{k})|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\eta\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})}{d\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}\nabla g(\mathbf{v}_{k})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}\right|+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right)

(111)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{k})}{d\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{k}}}\left(\left|\nabla(g-g_{0})(\mathbf{v}_{k})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}\right|+\left|\nabla g_{0}(\mathbf{v}_{k})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{k}\right|\right)

(112)

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right)

(113)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta M_{\epsilon}}{d}\left(\left\|{g-g_{0}}\right\|_{C^{2}}\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|\left\|{\mathbf{v}_{k}}\right\|^{2}+C_{g_{0}}(\overline{\mathbf{K}})\max_{g\in Q_{q}}|g(\mathbf{v}_{k})|\right)

(114)

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right)

(115)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta M_{\epsilon}}{d}\left(d^{-2n}\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\right\|+C_{g_{0}}(\overline{\mathbf{K}})\right)\max_{g\in Q_{q}}|g(\mathbf{v}_{k})|+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right),

(116)

where
C_{g_{0}}(\overline{\mathbf{K}})
is the choice dependent constant as in (
109
). By taking the max across our finite net
{}\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0muQ}_{q}
, there exists
C(\overline{\mathbf{K}})>0
such that for all
g\in Q_{q}
,

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla g(\mathbf{v}_{k})^{\rm T}(\mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_{k})|\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\right|\leq\frac{\eta M_{\epsilon}}{d}C(\overline{\mathbf{K}})\max_{g\in Q_{q}}|g(\mathbf{v}_{k})|+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right).

(117)

∎

We are now ready to prove our main result. It would be convenient to extend the indexing of
\mathbf{v}_{k}
from
\mathbb{N}
to
\mathbb{R}
by defining the sequence
t_{k}=k/d
. With some slight abuse of notation, let
\mathbf{v}_{t_{k}}=\mathbf{v}_{k}
. If

, then define
\mathbf{v}_{t}=\mathbf{v}_{t_{k-1}}
.

Lemma 7
.

Given
0<2p<d
and a quadratic
q
such that
||q||_{C^{2}}\leq 1
, define
Q=Q_{q}\cup Q_{\mathcal{R}}
, where
\mathcal{\mathcal{R}}
is the risk. For all
T>3
and
1/3<\delta<1/2
, there exists
C(\overline{\mathbf{K}},\epsilon)>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|q(\mathbf{v}_{t})-q(\mathbf{V}_{t})|\leq\frac{Td^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}\exp\left(C(\overline{\mathbf{K}},\epsilon)\left\|{\eta}\right\|_{\infty}T\right),

(118)

with probability at least
1-c(2p,\overline{\mathbf{K}})d^{p(1/3-\delta)}
.

Proof.

Let
g\in Q
, by (
97
), we see that

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{v}_{t})

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{v}_{0})-\frac{\eta}{d}\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor td\rfloor}\frac{\varphi(\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v}_{i}))}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v}_{i})}}\nabla g(\mathbf{v}_{i})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{i}

(119)

\displaystyle\quad+\lfloor td\rfloor O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right)+\frac{\eta^{2}}{d^{2}\pi}\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor td\rfloor}\left\langle\nabla^{2}g(\mathbf{v}_{k}),\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}\right\rangle+\mathcal{M}^{lin}_{t}+\mathcal{M}^{quad}_{t}

\displaystyle=g(\mathbf{v}_{0})-\eta\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\varphi(\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v}_{s}))}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v}_{s})}}\nabla g(\mathbf{v}_{s})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s

(120)

\displaystyle\quad+\lfloor td\rfloor O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d^{2}}\right)+\frac{2\eta^{2}}{d\pi}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\nabla^{2}g(\mathbf{v}_{s}),\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{M}^{lin}_{t}+\mathcal{M}^{quad}_{t}.

(121)

Taking the difference with
signHSGD
, we see that

\displaystyle|g(\mathbf{v}_{t})-g(\mathbf{V}_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq\eta\int_{0}^{t}\left|\frac{\varphi(\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v}_{s}))}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v}_{s})}}\nabla g(\mathbf{v}_{s})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{s}-\frac{\varphi(\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{V}_{s}))}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{V}_{s})}}\nabla g(\mathbf{V}_{s})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{V}_{s}\right|\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\left(|\mathcal{M}_{s}^{lin}|+|\mathcal{M}_{s}^{quad}|+|\mathcal{M}_{s}^{\sigma}|\right)+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d}\right)t.

(122)

However, Lemma

(123)

on a bounded domain is Lipschitz continuous with constant

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\varphi(\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v}_{s}))}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v}_{s})}}\nabla g(\mathbf{v}_{s})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{s}-\frac{\varphi(\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{V}_{s}))}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{V}_{s})}}\nabla g(\mathbf{V}_{s})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{V}_{s}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq L_{\epsilon}\sqrt{\left(\nabla g(\mathbf{v}_{s})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{v}_{s}-\nabla g(\mathbf{V}_{s})^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{V}_{s}\right)^{2}+\left(\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v}_{s})-\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{V}_{s})\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq L_{\epsilon}C(\overline{\mathbf{K}})\max_{g\in Q}|g(\mathbf{v}_{s})-g(\mathbf{V}_{s})|.

(124)

Plugging into (

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in Q}|g(\mathbf{v}_{t})-g(V_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\left(|\mathcal{M}_{s}^{lin}|+|\mathcal{M}_{s}^{quad}|+|\mathcal{M}_{s}^{\sigma}|\right)+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d}\right)t

\displaystyle+\eta L_{\epsilon}C(\overline{\mathbf{K}})\int_{0}^{t}\max_{g\in Q}|g(\mathbf{v}_{s})-g(\mathbf{V}_{s})|\,\mathrm{d}s.

(125)

By Gronwall’s inequality,

\sup_{g\in Q}|g(\mathbf{v}_{t})-g(\mathbf{V}_{t})|\leq\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\left(|\mathcal{M}_{s}^{lin}|+|\mathcal{M}_{s}^{quad}|+|\mathcal{M}_{s}^{\sigma}|\right)+O\left(\frac{d^{3\rho}}{d}\right)t\right)\exp\left(\eta L_{\epsilon}C(\overline{\mathbf{K}})t\right).

(126)

Lemmas

bounds the martingales by
\frac{d^{\delta}}{\sqrt{d}}

∎

We have now shown that the stopped processes satisfy the conclusion of Theorem

Lemma 8
.

For all

, there exists

Proof.

For
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\psi(\mathbf{V}_{t})

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{-2\eta\varphi(\mathcal{R}_{t})}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}_{t}}(1+\left\|{\mathbf{V}_{t}}\right\|^{2})}\mathbf{V}_{t}^{\rm T}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{V}_{t}-\frac{\eta^{2}}{d\pi(1+\left\|{\mathbf{V}_{t}}\right\|^{2})}\mathbf{V}_{t}^{\rm T}\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}\mathbf{V}_{t}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\left[\frac{2\eta^{2}}{d\pi(1+\left\|{\mathbf{V}_{t}}\right\|^{2})}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{K}_{\sigma})\right]\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{2\eta}{(1+\left\|{\mathbf{V}_{t}}\right\|^{2})}\mathbf{V}_{t}^{\rm T}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}}{d\pi}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}.

(128)

It is easy to check by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that the deterministic terms of may be uniformly bounded by some constant
C(\overline{\mathbf{K}},\mathbf{K}_{\sigma})>0

Denote the martingale term by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{M}^{\sigma-HSGD}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-C(\overline{\mathbf{K}},\mathbf{K}_{\sigma})^{2}Td\pi}{2\eta^{2}\left\|{\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}}\right\|}\right).

(131)

That is

\max_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\|{\mathbf{V}_{t}}\right\|\leq\exp\left(TC(\overline{\mathbf{K}},\mathbf{K}_{\sigma})\right),

(132)

with overwhelming probability.
∎

Lemma 12
.

If
\mu
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
with the property that there exists
a_{0}>0
such that
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}x}=g(x)
on
[-a_{0},a_{0}]
for
g\in C^{2}([-a_{0},a_{0}])
, then the map
\alpha:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

s\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y),

(146)

is bounded as well as Lipschitz.

Proof.

Notice that it suffices to show (
146
) is bounded and Lipschitz for
0<s<1
. Let
f_{s}(y)=\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt{s}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)
, as well as

G(y)=\mu((-\infty,y])
. Decomposing the integral into

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{s}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)=\int_{[-a_{0},a_{0}]}f_{s}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-a_{0},a_{0}]}f_{s}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y),

(147)

we see that the latter term may be easily bounded by

\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-a_{0},a_{0}]}f_{s}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\exp\left(\frac{-a_{0}^{2}}{2s}\right),

(148)

which decays to
0

. The former term we apply the integration by parts formula to get

\displaystyle\int_{[-a_{0},a_{0}]}f_{s}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)=f_{s}(y)G(y)\bigg|_{y=-a_{0}}^{y=a_{0}}+\int_{[-a_{0},a_{0}]}\frac{y}{s^{3/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)G(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

(149)

Further decomposing the latter integral into positive and negative regions we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a_{0}}\frac{y}{s^{3/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)G(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_{0}^{a_{0}}\frac{y}{s^{3/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)\left[G(-a_{0})+\mu((-a_{0},y])\right]\,\mathrm{d}y,

(150)

and

\displaystyle\int_{-a_{0}}^{0}\frac{y}{s^{3/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)G(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{-a_{0}}^{0}\frac{y}{s^{3/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)\left[G(-a_{0})+\mu((-a_{0},y])\right]\,\mathrm{d}y

(151)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{a_{0}}\frac{y}{s^{3/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)\left[G(-a_{0})+\mu((-a_{0},-y])\right]\,\mathrm{d}y.

(152)

Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{[-a_{0},a_{0}]}\frac{y}{s^{3/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)G(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{a_{0}}\frac{y}{s^{3/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)\mu((-y,y])\,\mathrm{d}y

(153)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{a_{0}}\frac{y^{2}}{s^{3/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y

(154)

\displaystyle=C\int_{0}^{a_{0}/\sqrt{s}}y^{2}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y.

(155)

Putting this all together, we conclude that
\varphi(s)
is uniformly bounded for all
s>0
. To see Lipschitz, we apply a similar argument. We first differentiate
f_{s}(y)
with respect to
s
to we get

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}f_{s}(y)=\frac{1}{2s^{5/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)\left(y^{2}-s\right).

(156)

Therefore,

\alpha^{\prime}(s)=\int_{[-a_{0},a_{0}]}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}f_{s}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-a_{0},a_{0}]}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}f_{s}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

(157)

There exists
s_{0}>0
such that if
s<s_{0}
, then
\sqrt{3s}<a_{0}
. It is easy to check that if
y>\sqrt{3s}
then
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}f_{s}(y)
is decreasing in
y

then
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}f_{s}(y)
is increasing in

\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-a_{0},a_{0}]}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}f_{s}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\leq\frac{1}{s^{5/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-a_{0}^{2}}{2s}\right)(a_{0}^{2}-s),

which decays to

.
Finally, we apply integration by parts once more to get

\displaystyle\int_{[-a_{0},a_{0}]}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}f_{s}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}f_{s}(y)G(y)\bigg|_{y=-a_{0}}^{y=a_{0}}

(159)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{a_{0}}\frac{1}{2s^{7/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)(y^{3}-3sy)\mu((-y,y])\,\mathrm{d}y.

(160)

Since

Plugging this into (

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{a_{0}}\frac{g(0)}{s^{7/2}}\exp\left(\frac{-y^{2}}{2s}\right)(y^{3}-3sy)y\,\mathrm{d}y\right|=\frac{g(0)a_{0}^{3}\exp\left(\frac{-a_{0}^{2}}{2s}\right)}{s^{5/2}},

(164)

Combining this with (

is uniformly bounded for all

.
∎

Lemma 13
.

Let
x\sim N(0,\mathbf{K})
such that
\mathbf{K}
is positive-definite. If
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then for all even moments
2k\leq d
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle a,\sigma(\mathbf{x})\right\rangle^{2k}\right]\leq C(2k,\mathbf{K})\|a\|^{2k}_{\infty}d^{4k/3},

(165)

where
C(2k,\mathbf{K})>0
depends only on
2k
,
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K})
and
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{K})
.

Proof.

We start by fixing a
\delta>0
and defining the smooth approximation of
\sigma(\mathbf{x})
to be
\sigma_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})=\rho_{\delta}\ast\sigma(\mathbf{x})
, where
\rho_{\delta}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the standard compactly-supported mollifier convolved entry-wise to
\sigma(\mathbf{x})
, i.e.

(\sigma_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}))_{i}=\rho_{\delta}\ast\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{i})
.
It follows that

\|\left\langle a,\sigma(\mathbf{x})\right\rangle\|_{L^{2k}}\leq\|\left\langle a,\sigma(\mathbf{x})-\sigma_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\right\rangle\|_{L^{2k}}+\|\left\langle a,\sigma_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\right\rangle\|_{L^{2k}}.

(166)

Note that
\rho_{\delta}
has support contained in
[-\delta,\delta]
, thus the entry-wise difference of
\sigma(\mathbf{x})-\sigma_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})
may be bounded by

|\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\sigma_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}_{i})|\leq\begin{cases}0&|\mathbf{x}_{i}|>2\delta\\
2&|\mathbf{x}_{i}|\leq 2\delta.\end{cases}

Define
N_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\mathbf{x}_{i}|\leq 2\delta\}}
to be the number of coordinates of
\mathbf{x}
within the interval
(-2\delta,2\delta)
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle a,\sigma(\mathbf{x})-\sigma_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\right\rangle^{2k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{a}\right\|_{\infty}^{2k}2^{2k}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})^{2k}\right]

(167)

\displaystyle=\left\|{a}\right\|_{\infty}^{2k}2^{2k}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbb{P}\left((\mathbf{x}_{i})_{i\in s^{\prime}}\in[-2\delta,2\delta]^{|s^{\prime}|}\right),

(168)

where
\mathcal{I}=\{1,\dots,d\}^{2k}
and
s^{\prime}
is set of distinct elements of
s
. Let
\mathbf{K}^{(s^{\prime})}=\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{i\in s^{\prime}}^{\otimes 2}\right]
, then
(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{i\in s^{\prime}}\sim N(0,\mathbf{K}^{(s^{\prime})})
. Recall that there exists a permutation matrix
\mathbf{P}
, such
\mathbf{K}^{(s^{\prime})}
forms the top
|s^{\prime}|\times|s^{\prime}|
sub-matrix of
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{P}^{-1}
. Given that
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{P}^{-1}
and
\mathbf{K}
are
similar, they share the same eigenvalues. Therefore, by the Cauchy interlacing-law,

\lambda_{\min}({\mathbf{K}})\leq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K}^{(s^{\prime})}).

(169)

In particular this implies

\det{\mathbf{K}^{(s^{\prime})}}=\prod_{i=1}^{|s^{\prime}|}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{K}^{(s^{\prime})})\geq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K})^{|s^{\prime}|}.

(170)

Plugging this back into (
168
) and choosing
\delta=d^{-r}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle a,\sigma(\mathbf{x})-\sigma_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\right\rangle^{2k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{a}\right\|_{\infty}^{2k}2^{2k}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{(4\delta)^{|s^{\prime}|}}{(2\pi\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K}))^{|s^{\prime}|/2}}

(171)

\displaystyle=\left\|{a}\right\|_{\infty}^{2k}2^{2k}\sum_{l=1}^{2k}\binom{d}{l}l!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{2k}{l}\frac{(4\delta)^{l}}{(2\pi\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K}))^{l/2}}

(172)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{a}\right\|_{\infty}^{2k}2^{2k}\max_{1\leq l\leq 2k}\left(l!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{2k}{l}\right)\sum_{l=1}^{2k}\left(\frac{ed}{l}\right)^{l}\frac{(4\delta)^{l}}{(2\pi\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K}))^{l/2}}

(173)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{a}\right\|_{\infty}^{2k}2^{2k}C(2k)\left(\frac{4ed^{1-r}}{\sqrt{2\pi\min\{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K}),1\}}}\right)^{2k}

(174)

\displaystyle=\left\|{a}\right\|_{\infty}^{2k}C(2k,\mathbf{K})d^{(1-r)2k},

(175)

where
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{2k}{l}
is Stirling’s number of a second-kind. For clarity of notation moving forward, we note that
C(2k,\mathbf{K})
may change up to factors of constants or powers of
k
from line to line, while always being independent to
d
.

To control the second term of Equation (
166
), we modify the proof of concentration of Lipschitz functions of Gaussian random variables. See Lemma 2.1.5 for more details
(
Adler & Taylor, 2007
)
. Let
G(\mathbf{x})=\left\langle a,\sigma_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\right\rangle
.
and
\mathbf{z}\sim N(0,\mathbf{K})
be independent to
\mathbf{x}
. Define the Gaussian interpolation
\mathbf{z}^{\alpha}

to be

\mathbf{z}^{(\alpha)}=\alpha\mathbf{x}+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\mathbf{z},

and note that
\mathbf{x}\overset{\operatorname{law}}{=}\mathbf{z}^{(\alpha)}
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Then by Lemma 2.1.4 in
(
Adler & Taylor, 2007
)
,

\mathbb{E}[G(\mathbf{x})^{2k}]=(2k-1)\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\mathbf{K}(a\odot\sigma^{\prime}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})),a\odot\sigma^{\prime}_{\delta}(\mathbf{z}^{(\alpha)})\right\rangle\cdot G^{2k-2}(\mathbf{x})\right]d\alpha,

(176)

where
\odot
represents the Hadamard product. Going forward, we will use Hölder’s inequality to break up the expectation and form a recursive equation. As such, consider

\mathbb{E}[\left\langle\mathbf{K}(a\odot\sigma^{\prime}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x}),a\odot\sigma^{\prime}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y}^{\alpha})\right\rangle^{2p}],

(177)

for some
p
. Standard linear algebra gives us

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\left\langle\mathbf{K}(a\odot\sigma_{\delta}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}),a\odot\sigma^{\prime}_{\delta}(\mathbf{z}^{(\alpha)}\right\rangle^{2p}]&\leq(\|\mathbf{K}\|\left\|{a}\right\|^{2}_{\infty})^{2p}\mathbb{E}[(\|\sigma^{\prime}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\|\|\sigma^{\prime}_{\delta}(\mathbf{z}^{(\alpha)})\|)^{2p}]\\
&\leq(\|\mathbf{K}\|\left\|{a}\right\|_{\infty}^{2})^{2p}\mathbb{E}[\|\sigma^{\prime}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\|\|^{4p}],\end{split}

(178)

with the last line following from Cauchy-Schwartz and equality in law of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{z}^{(\alpha)}
. Given that
\sigma_{\delta}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=0
for
\mathbf{x}_{i}\not\in[-2\delta,2\delta]
, as well as

|\sigma_{\delta}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}_{i})|\leq\frac{L_{\rho}}{\delta}
for
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in[-2\delta,2\delta]
and
L_{\rho}
a universal constant depending on our mollifier,

\mathbb{E}[\|\sigma^{\prime}_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\|\|^{4p}]\leq\frac{L^{4p}_{\rho}}{\delta^{4p}}\mathbb{E}[N_{\delta}^{2p}].

(179)

As we have seen in Equation (
175
),

\mathbb{E}[N_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})^{2p}]\leq C(p,\mathbf{K})d^{(1-r)2p}.

(180)

Therefore, up to absolute constants

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\left\langle\mathbf{K}(a\odot\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}),a\odot\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{y}^{\alpha})\right\rangle^{2p}]&\leq C(p,\mathbf{K})(\left\|{a}\right\|_{\infty}^{2})^{2p}\frac{L_{\rho}^{4p}}{\delta^{4p}}d^{(1-r)2p}\\
&\leq C(p,\mathbf{K})(\left\|{a}\right\|_{\infty}^{2})^{2p}d^{(1+r)2p}.\end{split}

(181)

Returning to (
176
) and choosing
2p=2k-1
, we see by Hölder’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G(\mathbf{x})^{2k}]

\displaystyle\leq(2k-1)\left\|{G(\mathbf{x})^{2k-2}}\right\|_{L^{\frac{2k-1}{2k-2}}}\left\|{[\left\langle\mathbf{K}(a\odot\sigma_{\delta}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}),a\odot\sigma^{\prime}_{\delta}(\mathbf{y}^{\alpha})\right\rangle}\right\|_{L^{2k-1}}

(182)

\displaystyle\leq(2k-1)\mathbb{E}[G(\mathbf{x})^{2k-1}]^{\frac{2k-2}{2k-1}}C(k,\mathbf{K})\left\|{a}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}d^{1+r}.

(183)

Iterating the same inequalities as above for
\mathbb{E}[G^{2k-1}]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G(\mathbf{x})^{2k}]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{2k-1}\left((2k-i)C(2k-i,\mathbf{K})\left\|{a}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}d^{1+r}\right)^{\frac{2k-i}{2k-1}}

(184)

\displaystyle\leq C(2k,\mathbf{K})\left\|{a}\right\|_{\infty}^{2k}d^{(1+r)k}.

(185)

Equations (
175
) and (
184
)combined give control over Equation (
166
),

\|\left\langle a,\sigma(\mathbf{x})\right\rangle\|_{L^{2k}}\leq C(2k,\mathbf{K})\|a\|_{\infty}\left(d^{\frac{1+r}{2}}+d^{1-r}\right).

(186)

Optimizing over
r
yields
r=1/3
leading to

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle a,\sigma(\mathbf{x})\right\rangle\|^{2k}\right]\leq C(2k,\mathbf{K})\|a\|_{\infty}^{2k}d^{4k/3},

(187)

as desired.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Let
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|\mathcal{F}_{k}]\right|^{2p}\right]

(201)

Lemma 15
.

Let

be a martingale such that

Appendix F
Heuristic for ADAM

In this section we derive a heuristic for
Adam

(
Kingma, 2014
)
. This is given, in our context, by:

Given:

\displaystyle\quad\eta:\text{learning rate}

\displaystyle\quad\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in[0,1):\text{exponential decay rates for moment estimates}

\displaystyle\quad\epsilon_{0}:\text{small constant for numerical stability}

Initialize:

\displaystyle\quad\bm{\theta}_{0}:\text{initial parameter vector}

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{m}_{0}\leftarrow\mathbf{0}:\text{1st moment vector}

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{v}_{0}\leftarrow\mathbf{0}:\text{2nd moment vector}

\displaystyle\quad k\leftarrow 0:\text{timestep}

Repeat until convergence:

\displaystyle\quad k\leftarrow k+1

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{g}_{k}\leftarrow\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k},y_{k})\quad\text{(Get gradients w.r.t. stochastic objective at timestep k)}

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{m}_{k}\leftarrow\beta_{1}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{k-1}+(1-\beta_{1})\cdot\mathbf{g}_{k}\quad\text{(Update biased first moment estimate)}

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{v}_{k}\leftarrow\beta_{2}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{k-1}+(1-\beta_{2})\cdot\mathbf{g}_{k}^{2}\quad\text{(Update biased second raw moment estimate)}

\displaystyle\quad\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{k}\leftarrow\mathbf{m}_{k}/(1-\beta_{1}^{k})\quad\text{(Compute bias-corrected first moment estimate)}

\displaystyle\quad\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\leftarrow\mathbf{v}_{k}/(1-\beta_{2}^{k})\quad\text{(Compute bias-corrected second raw moment estimate)}

\displaystyle\quad\bm{\theta}_{k}\leftarrow\bm{\theta}_{k-1}-\eta\hat{\mathbf{m}}_{k}/(\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}}+\epsilon_{0})\quad\text{(Update parameters)}

In a high-dimensional context, the first moment momentum
\beta_{1}
has been observed to be equivalent to an effective change of learning rate, without inducing other benefits on the dynamics (see
(
Paquette & Paquette, 2021
)
), and so we ignore it.

The role of the second moment, in contrast, should induce a preconditioner. If we assume that exponential decay rate of
\beta_{2}
is chosen sufficiently close to
1
, we would have

\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\approx_{\beta_{2}}\mathbb{E}(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k},y_{k}))^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{k-1}),

with the square applied entrywise. Using the definition of the stochastic gradient, we have

\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}=\mathbb{E}\left((\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}\left(\langle\mathbf{x}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{k-1}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}}\rangle+\epsilon_{k}\right)^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{k-1}\right).

This can be computed explicitly by Gaussian conditioning. Note that conditionally on the Gaussian
\bf{w}=\langle\mathbf{x}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{k-1}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}}\rangle
,
\mathbf{x}_{k}
develops a mean
\mathbf{K}(\bm{\theta}_{k-1}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}})
, which has norm
O(1)
. Hence provided it also has small
\operatorname{L}^{\infty}
norm, so too will all the variances of the entries of
\mathbf{x}_{k}
be nearly unaffected. Hence we essentially have independence, in that

\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\approx\mathbb{E}\left((\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\langle\mathbf{x}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{k-1}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}}\rangle+\epsilon_{k}\right)^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{k-1}\right)=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{K})(2\mathcal{P}).

Hence, we arrive at the approximate update rule for
Adam

\bm{\theta}_{k+1}=\bm{\theta}_{k}-\frac{\eta_{k}}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{P}(\bm{\theta}_{k})}}\mathbf{D}^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k+1},y_{k+1}).

(264)

The corresponding homogenized
Adam
equation is given by

\mathrm{d}\bm{\Theta}_{t}=-\frac{\eta_{t}}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{P}(\bm{\Theta}_{t})}}\overline{\mathbf{K}}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}})+\eta_{t}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\sqrt{\overline{\mathbf{K}}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}.

(265)

Appendix G
Weak-approximation and high-dimension

The idea of approximating discrete stochastic processes using differential equations is not new and can be traced back to
(
Robbins & Monro, 1951
)
. These approaches typically involved deriving an ODE by sending the step size of a stochastic difference equation to zero and demonstrating that the noise vanishes in the asymptotic limit. In recent years, SDEs have also been integrated into the study of SGD dynamics. In particular, the work of
(
Li et al., 2019
)
establishes a weak-approximation framework for tracking SGD across a wide range of suitable test functions.
(
Malladi et al., 2022
)
further builds on these ideas to derive SDEs for
Adam
and RMSprop. Simultaneously with our work,
(
Compagnoni et al., 2025
)
applies the weak-approximation framework to derive SDEs for
signSGD
. Their approach leverages the noisy gradient model, which allows them to extend their theory to a broader class of loss functions. In contrast, our approach follows the high-dimensional framework developed by
(
Paquette et al., 2022a
)
and
(
Collins-Woodfin & Paquette, 2023
)
.

In the high-dimensional setting, we are interested in studying a sequence of problems of growing complexity (as dimension goes to infinity). This influences our notion of convergence. As shown in Theorem
1
, we demonstrate that the risk curves of
signSGD
and its SDE,
signHSGD
, closely track each other in a pointwise sense. This enables us to directly study the behavior of
signSGD
by analyzing
signHSGD
, as seen in Theorems
3
and
4
. Additionally, the precision of this tracking improves as the dimension increases. In contrast, within the weak-approximation framework, for a fixed dimension, the notion of convergence is more akin to convergence in distribution between the SDE and the optimizer as the learning rate approaches zero. Below, we highlight some of the key differences between these two approaches.

In the following, we will show that
signHSGD
as defined in (
8
) is
almost
recoverable from the weak-approximation framework. The weak-approximation SDE of
(
Compagnoni et al., 2025
)
for
signSGD
with learning rate
\eta
and constant scheduler
\kappa
is described by

\mathrm{d}\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}=-\kappa(1-2\mathbb{P}\left(\nabla f_{\gamma}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA})<0\right))\mathrm{d}t+\kappa\sqrt{\eta\mathbf{\overline{\Sigma}}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t},

(266)

where
\nabla f_{\gamma}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA})=\mathbb{E}[\nabla f_{\gamma}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA})]+Z
, for
Z
the gradient noise, and
\mathbf{\overline{\Sigma}}
the covariance matrix of
\sigma(\nabla f_{\gamma}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}))-\mathbb{E}[\sigma(\nabla f_{\gamma}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}))].
Moreover, the probability and expectation are understood to be taken conditional on
\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}
. See
(
Malladi et al., 2022
)
for more on the noisy gradient model.
We consider the quadratic-loss function and to match our label-noise gradient to the noisy gradient, we set
Z({\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}})=\mathbf{x}_{t}(\left\langle\mathbf{x}_{t},\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}}\right\rangle-\epsilon_{t})-\mathbf{K}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}})
, for
\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim N(0,\mathbf{K})
and
\epsilon_{t}
independent to
\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}
.
Since the effective learning rate in high-dimension is
\eta/d
, choosing
\kappa=1/d

and plugging this into (
266
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{d}(1-2\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{t}\left(\left\langle\mathbf{x}_{t},\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}}\right\rangle-\epsilon_{t}\right)<0))\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{d}\sqrt{\eta\left(\frac{2\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}}{\pi}-\mathbf{M}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA})\right)}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}

(267)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{t}(\left\langle\mathbf{x}_{t},\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}}\right\rangle-\epsilon_{t}))\right]\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{d}\sqrt{\eta\left(\frac{2\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}}{\pi}-\mathbf{M}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA})\right)}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t},

(268)

where
\mathbf{M}(\bm{\Theta}_{t})=\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{t}(\left\langle\mathbf{x}_{t},\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}}\right\rangle-\epsilon_{t}))\right]^{\otimes 2}
. Computing the expectation of the signed gradient is not a straightforward task and to reduce it to a simplified form requires high-dimensional concentration. See Lemmas
1
,
3
and
4
. Thus,
taking the liberty of fixing the dimension
d
to be large, for
\rho<1/4
as specified in Lemma
4
,

\mathrm{d}\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}=-\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{\varphi(\mathcal{R}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}))}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA})}}\overline{\mathbf{K}}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}})+O\left(d^{3\rho-2}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{d}\sqrt{\eta\left(\frac{2\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}}{\pi}-\mathbf{M}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA})\right)}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}.

(269)

We present
signHSGD
for ease of reference,

\mathrm{d}\bm{\Theta}_{t}=-\eta\frac{\varphi(\mathcal{R}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}))}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{R}(\bm{\Theta}_{t})}}\overline{\mathbf{K}}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}-{\bm{\theta}_{*}})\mathrm{d}t+\eta\sqrt{\frac{2\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}}{\pi d}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}.

(270)

Most notable between
signHSGD
and (
269
) is the error
O\left(d^{3\rho-2}\right)
and
\mathbf{M}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA})
in the drift and diffusion term of (
269
) respectively. It is clear that the error in the drift term diminishes as the dimension increases. However, the error in the diffusion term is less obvious. We once again turn towards high-dimensional concentration. Indeed, by Lemmas
3
,
4
and
12
, we see that for all
1\leq i,j\leq d
,
[\mathbf{M}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA})]_{ij}=O\left(d^{2\rho-1}\right)
with high-probability.

Another important distinction between the two SDEs is the presence of
\eta
in both the drift and diffusion term of
signHSGD
. Whereas with
\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}
,
\eta
is only present in the diffusion term. This relates to the different notions of convergence within the respective frameworks.
In the weak-approximation setting,
\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}
is an order
1
approximate of
\bm{\theta}_{k}
in the sense of

\sup_{0\leq k\leq\lfloor{T/\eta}\rfloor}\left|\mathbb{E}[g(\bm{\theta}_{k})]-\mathbb{E}[g(\bm{\Theta}^{WA}_{k\eta})]\right|\leq C(d,T){\eta},

(271)

where
g
is any function with polynomial growth. We see that within a fixed dimension setting,
\bm{\Theta}_{t}^{WA}
has improved performance in approximating
\bm{\theta}_{k}
when
\eta\to 0
and the drift term dominates. On the contrary,
signHSGD
vanishes if we were to send
\eta\to 0
. Instead, we take the limit in dimension and show that

signHSGD
improves as dimension
d\to\infty
. See Theorem
1
. Comparing these two regimes, it is not immediately clear whether one can send both
\eta\to 0
and
d\to\infty
in (
271
) and still retain good approximation. For instance, if we
fix
\eta
and send
d\to\infty
in
(
269
), we see that the diffusion term persists as
\mathbf{K}_{\sigma}
grows with dimension. On the other hand, the drift term vanishes. The consideration of dimensionality as the problem scales is an important aspect of our work.

)
