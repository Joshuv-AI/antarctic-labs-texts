Abstract
. LLMs promise to democratize technical work in complex domains like programmatic data analysis, but not everyone benefits equally. We study how students with varied expertise use LLMs to complete Python-based data analysis in computational notebooks in a non-major course. Drawing on homework logs, recordings, and surveys from 36 students, we ask: Which expertise matters most, and how does it shape AI use? Our mixed-methods analysis shows that technical expertise – not AI familiarity or communication skills – remains a significant predictor of success. Students also vary widely in how they leverage LLMs, struggling at stages of forming intent, expressing inputs, interpreting outputs, and assessing results. We identify success and failure behaviors, such as providing context or decomposing prompts, that distinguish effective use. These findings inform AI literacy interventions, highlighting that lightweight demonstrations improve surface fluency but are insufficient; deeper training and scaffolds are needed to cultivate resilient AI use skills.



1.
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) hold the promise of democratizing complex technical tasks such as data analysis by assisting users in programming, analysis, and modeling
(
Ma et al. 2025
;
Pickering et al. 2025
)
.
Despite the grand vision, we continue to see mixed results in whether and how people benefit from AI
(
Paradis et al. 2025
)
. There have been conflicting reports on whether AI agents help developers improve programming efficiency
(
Becker et al. 2025
;
Song et al. 2024
)
; only some novices seem to perceive reduced barriers
(
Pickering et al. 2025
;
Kazemitabaar et al. 2023
)
.
In fact, performance often hinges on users’ prior expertise, ranging from domain knowledge to AI literacy
(
Chen et al. 2024
;
Nguyen et al. 2024a
;
Zi et al. 2025
)
. For instance, some work argues that less experienced developers benefit more from AI assistants
(
Liang et al. 2024
;
Peng et al. 2023
)
, and those with basic AI literacy may avoid common pitfalls in prompting or interpreting model outputs
(
Puppart and Aru 2025
;
Annapureddy et al. 2025
)
.
However, these findings remain fragmented across different populations and tasks,
making it challenging to weigh which types of expertise matter most
(e.g., whether we should focus on teaching domain knowledge or AI usage knowledge).
Moreover, most prior analyses measure only outcomes (e.g., task success) and are conducted in controlled, short-term lab settings
(
Kazemitabaar et al. 2024a
;
Pickering et al. 2025
;
Zheng 2023
)
. As a result, we have yet to demystify
how users’ AI interaction behaviors get shaped by their (lack of) certain expertise
in real-world workflows.

In this work, we aim to
examine how varied forms of expertise influence AI use and outcomes
in a naturalistic classroom setting: a graduate-level course, Data Science for Product Management (DSPM). The class offered a unique opportunity to study LLM-supported workflows: it included a diverse cohort of 40 students from CS majors to business students transitioning into technical roles, allowing us to examine varying domain expertise, LLM literacy, and communication skills. Students worked independently on a series of data analysis assignments (e.g., Data Cleaning) in Google Colab, had full access to the built-in Gemini LLM assistant (
Figure

1
), and their activity was captured through logs, surveys, and screen recordings.
Through this setup, we investigate three questions of increasing depth:

RQ1 (
Section

5
): Do LLMs close the performance gap between students with different expertise?

We first conduct quantitative analysis on the relationship between students’ self-perceived expertise (in technical, LLM, and communication domains) and their grades across four assignments.
Despite all students having equal access to Gemini, we find that self-reported technical expertise significantly predicted student grades, while LLM expertise and communication skills did not. This suggests that
LLMs do not fully close the expertise gap
— students with stronger programming and data science backgrounds outperform their peers when using AI. Many dimensions of expertise were highly correlated in our study, making it difficult to isolate their independent contributions. This indicates that
fully isolating expertise may require more deliberate sampling and experimental control in future work.

RQ2 (
Section

6
): How do differences in expertise translate to AI usage behaviors?

We analyze students’ interaction logs using a novel behavior-grounded codebook, developed via thematic analysis and scaled up through LLM-based annotation.
Our method segments over 7,000 raw log events into intent-driven episodes and steps, where each step is annotated with the presence (or absence) of effective AI behaviors, such as, decomposing tasks, providing contexts, and using AI for explanation or critique.
Our analysis shows that technical experts use AI more strategically. They write clearer prompts, are more likely to provide necessary context, and use AI proactively to explore alternative solutions or improve plans (e.g., asking for better visualizations). In contrast, novices rely more on AI to resolve immediate challenges, such as debugging errors or explaining confusing outputs.
These behavioral patterns offer
a more nuanced explanation of why expertise continues to matter
, even in AI-supported environments.

RQ3 (
Section

7
): What and how can we train students on AI usage?

We analyze missed opportunities for AI use by identifying log steps where AI could have been helpful but was not used. From this, we distilled a set of AI-use competencies spanning across four knowledge dimensions
Conceptual
,
Procedural
,
Metacognitive
,
Dispositional
.
We show that some AI-use skills can be improved via live coding demonstration and extended time-on-task (e.g., regarding prompt quality), while others likely require guided practice and feedback (e.g., regarding evaluation), and are hard to cover via demonstration (e.g., metacognitive and dispositional skills). In sum, we make three main contributions:

•

Empirical evidence on expertise vs. AI use
: We show that even in open-ended, LLM-supported workflows, technical expertise remains a significant predictor of success, and self-reported communication skill and AI familiarity alone do not suffice.

•

Complex log-based characterization of AI use
:
We introduce a behavior-oriented, LLM-based annotation schema that segments over 7,000 raw log events into semantically meaningful user stories. Compared to prior work that analyzes linear chat logs
(
Lee et al. 2022
)
, our setup in Google Colab involves multiple LLM-powered functions with different affordances and model access, resulting in more nuanced observations about when, how, and why students turn to AI support (e.g., comparing behaviors between AI-as-chat versus AI-as-inline-tool). We open-source all of our scripts, including log data collection, cleaning, annotation, and analysis:
https://github.com/mqo00/dspm

•

Pedagogical implications
: We identify which AI skills can be improved via lightweight instruction vs. require deeper pedagogical investment. This informs future curricula and tool design for training effective AI collaborators.

2.
Related Works

2.1.
Novice versus Expert Strategies in Programming with AI

HCI and educational research highlight systematic differences between novices and experts when collaborating with AI for programming and modeling. In agent-based modeling with ChatGPT, experts benefit more than novices and rely on stronger skills in task decomposition and evaluation of AI outputs
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
.
In data-science code comprehension, experts inspect the
data
deeply, verify assumptions about transformations, and navigate outputs with clear goals—behaviors novices underuse
(
Lum et al. 2025
)
. At the same time, studies document failure patterns such as “prompting rabbit holes,” where users iterate on prompts without convergence
(
Tie et al. 2024
)
.
However, most prior studies also focus on challenges brought by domain expertise differences, such as experts verifying and examining the data more deeply, and few focus on domain-agnostic LLM use patterns or skills regardless of expertise (
Section

2.3
).
Together, these findings motivate our research questions: measure whether LLMs truly reduce expertise gaps, and analyze
which behaviors
distinguish effective from ineffective AI use in authentic notebook workflows.

2.2.
Interfaces and Log Mining for AI-Assisted Data Analysis

AI-based computational notebook assistants have investigated a vast design space of scaffolding users’ interactions with LLMs during data analysis
(
Mcnutt et al. 2023
)
. For example, prior works have explored providing phasewise or stepwise decomposition interfaces
(
Kazemitabaar et al. 2024a
)
, structuring how users express intent and context with ephemeral UI
(
Cheng et al. 2024
)
, manipulating intent
(
Kim 2025
)
, and embedding explanations and documentation support
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
. These explorations brought different values to streamline the AI-assisted data analysis tasks, such as improving users’ perceived control
(
Kazemitabaar et al. 2024a
)
and lowering interaction barriers for novices
(
Wang et al. 2022
;
Cheng et al. 2024
)
. However, they do not necessarily improve objective performance measures such as task success or completion time, as UI scaffolds on their own do not ensure learning outcomes or allow us to analyze the AI-use skills students need.

Beyond interface design, many efforts have explored mining logs of user interactions to derive useful insights.
For example, recent works have explored logging and learning analytics in Jupyter notebooks to track students’ interactions with a chatbot AI
(
Valle Torre et al. 2025
)
or log novices’ data science workflow
(
Zhao et al. 2024
)
.
These approaches highlight the promise of leveraging naturally occurring clickstreams to study novice reasoning at scale
(
Claypool et al. 2001
;
Hijikata 2004
;
Huang et al. 2012
;
Chen et al. 2025b
)
, but also the challenges: raw logs are noisy, fragmented, and difficult to interpret without additional semantic structure e.g., a long pause may indicate either confusion or deep focus.
To enable higher-level reasoning, we take inspiration from recent work, to extract more
semantically meaningful insights from the raw logs
by utilizing LLMs’ reasoning and Theory-of-Mind capabilities for reasoning process
(
Viswanathan et al. 2024
;
Rabinowitz et al. 2018
;
Kosinski 2024
;
Strachan et al. 2024
)
.
To this end, we propose to combine log-based methods with behavior-grounded coding to identify success and failure patterns in AI-assisted notebook tasks, and identifying needed AI skills from the analysis. By semantically enriching logs into user story episodes and mapping them to concrete challenges, we aim to advance understanding of
why
some learners succeed while others fail, and what kinds of competencies need to be taught (
Section

7.1
,
7.2
).

2.3.
Training for AI-Assisted Data Analysis and Effective LLM Use

Prior works have also explored instructions specifically for AI-assisted data analysis tasks.
For example,
Zheng 2023
allowed ChatGPT use in a graduate data science task and found students reported that AI was more helpful for explaining code and less for open-ended problem-solving that needs context, especially on LLM-hard tasks where AI has limited capacities. LLM-enhanced feedback for an R-based data science tutoring system was also found to improve students’ performance, while the verbosity of AI explanations sometimes caused cognitive overload
(
Letteri and Vittorini 2025
)
.
Nonetheless, these works mostly focus on analyzing surveys and prompt exchanges, without behavioral log data that is important for capturing the full experiences and interactions with LLM
(
Kazemitabaar et al. 2024b
)
. Moreover, these studies may not represent the most authentic workflows of how students would use LLM for computational data analysis, when there are popular tools such as Google Colab
(
Google 2025
)
where AI is embedded in the notebook environment.

There have also been some works that focus on task-agnostic, domain-general AI-use skills, such as prompt engineering.
However, students do not learn from code feedback and self-experimentation
(
Nguyen et al. 2024b
)
; teaching specific prompt tips also may not be very effective at influencing students’ real prompting approaches and success, and also risks getting obsolete with model updates
(
Ma et al. 2025
)
. Some prior works have shown promise in teaching requirement specification
(
Ma et al. 2025
)
or requirement elicitation
(
Lojo et al. 2025
)
for LLMs.
However, no work has comprehensively mapped AI-use competencies across the full workflow or disentangled which skills are teachable via simple demonstrations versus guided practice and targeted feedback.
Our study fills this gap by linking fine-grained behaviors to outcomes and by quantifying post-demo shifts in skill acquisition.

3.
Course Structure and Task Setup

3.1.
Course Context

Our IRB-approved study was conducted in Spring 2025 in
Data Science for Product Managers
(DSPM) at an R1 University in the United States. The course serves students with highly diverse academic and professional backgrounds, including product managers, engineers, and graduates of non-technical disciplines.
This diversity creates a pedagogical tension: assignments that are trivial for some are overwhelming for others.
The setting therefore provides an ideal opportunity to examine whether and how LLM support can reduce expertise gaps in authentic workflows.

The class included four sequential assignments (HW1-HW4), each covering a primary data analysis stage: (1) data cleaning, (2) exploratory data analysis (EDA), (3) lightweight machine learning, and (4) data-driven storytelling.
All assignments used the same dataset (more details in

enabling continuity across tasks. Lectures covered corresponding data science topics and basic GenAI use with a demo (more details in

3.2.
Homework Design and Procedure

Assignments were distributed as Google Colab notebook templates with minimal instructions and starter code, requiring students to “fill in the blank” using Python.

Students first completed a
diagnostic
HW0 during lecture to familiarize themselves with the tools and task sequence. HW0 covered all four tasks but used a different dataset than HW1-HW4 (details in
Table

1
). We asked students to spend about 15 minutes on each task, where half the class completed the first two or last two tasks without using GenAI, providing a baseline for comparison. Students then completed HW1–HW4 over the semester on a bi-weekly basis, each focused on a different aforementioned task.
This setup enabled us to probe both short-term performance and longer-term workflow strategies, and to examine whether the LLM helps close the expertise gap.
Details regarding the dataset, task, and rubrics are presented in
Table

1
.

For each assignment, students submitted (1) a completed notebook, (2) exported log files of interactions, and (3) a post-survey reflecting on challenges and AI usage (details in
Section

4.2
and
Appendix

B
). For HW0, students recorded their screen via Zoom, creating paired video–log traces for deep qualitative analysis. For HW1–HW4, bonus credit was offered for optional 60-minute think-alouds facilitated by one author, during which students would verbalize their thoughts while doing homework as usual.

The assignments were graded by two teaching assistants (TAs) for the class. The TAs independently graded 20% of the student submissions in HW0 and achieved a Cohen’s kappa
\kappa=0.71
, which suggests substantial inter-rater reliability
(
McHugh 2012
)
. Weekly calibration meetings were held to align rubric interpretation.

Behavioral Logs

We deployed a custom instrumentation pipeline in Colab to capture both notebook interactions (code executions/errors), selected UI events (pressing explain-code/error buttons) and LLM use (prompts/responses). Students installed a Tampermonkey script that intercepted requests to Colab’s
converse
and
generateCode

In total, we collected 16,315 logged events (see example in
Figure

3

Logs captured a rich sequence of AI interactions, code edits, and executions, supporting both quantitative distributional analyses and qualitative episode-based coding.

RQ2: How do differences in expertise translate to AI usage behaviors?

The analysis in RQ1 showed that students’ self-perceived technical expertise continued to predict performance even when all students had access to the Colab Gemini assistant. Here, we dive into student log data to examine why:
how students use AI during workflows, and how it relates to different levels of technical expertise?

6.1.2.
Semantically meaningful log analysis.

Raw logs consist of fragmented sequences of actions that lack meaningful semantics. To recover actual student usage and rationale, we analyze user logs through a hierarchy of
episodes
,
steps
, and
log actions
(as shown in
Figure

4
B).

Specifically, we segmented logs into
episodes
— goal-driven units of activity (e.g., "remove duplicates in data column X").
Inspired by Norman’s Stages of Action for user cognitive processes
(
Tenner 2015
)
, we further broke each episode into four
steps
— forming
intent
, expressing the intent as an
input
(e.g., writing code or prompt), obtaining and

Develop the codebook.

To answer “how are users using LLMs”, we identify the core

Two authors thematically analyzed a subset of homework logs and screen recordings, following a hybrid of deductive and inductive thematic analysis approach
(

To maximize verifiability, we used five logs and screen recordings from HW0 and five logs and think-aloud recordings from HW1-4.
The AI code types in our codebook included both the AI use

.

Definition and Examples

Improve (Plan)

ai_breakdown_intent

Decompose a complex goal into manageable pieces to form the correct task intent. E.g., “How to improve performance of model. give step by step.”

ai_improve_prompt

Refine the wording of a prompt for clarity, specificity, and completeness before generation. E.g., “plz improve my prompt: [prompt]”

ai_suggest_steps_or_plan

Provide a step-by-step plan or workflow (e.g., data cleaning

\rightarrow

Code

ai_generate_code

Produce code that implements a clearly stated action to achieve the intended output. e.g., “Write the code to calculate the distribution of the cars by location.”

ai_edit_partial_code

Modify only a selected snippet or function instead of rewriting the whole cell or file. e.g., [Ask AI to only edit part of the code instead of regenerating the entire cell.]

Explain

ai_explain_bug_or_error

Explain an error or traceback and outline a concrete fix. E.g., Copy-paste error message and use "explain error" to understand and fix it.

ai_explain_code_or_api

Interpret code or clarify specific APIs (e.g., describe what a function does and how to use it). E.g., “Explain what pd.get_dummies does”

ai_explain_concepts

Provide plain-language explanations of data science or machine learning concepts. E.g., “How do data cleaning steps work?”

Eval

ai_critique_output

Evaluate results or insights for correctness, alignment with goals, and suggest improvements. E.g., “Check my insight and provide improvements”, “Is it a good model to work with?”

Prompt (Quality)

clear_instruction_in_prompt

Write prompts with explicit action verbs and desired outcomes (avoiding vague instructions like “fix it”). “Fix it” without saying what "it" is would be unclear.

decompose_task_in_prompt

State the problem as ordered sub-tasks or “step-by-step” instructions within the prompt. E.g., “This was my result array [copied output]” without intent would be too vague.

provide_context_in_prompt

Include necessary details (e.g., dataframe names, column names, prior steps) and avoid vague pronouns to prevent misinterpretation. E.g., “Don’t make up non-existent column name” without providing actual context does not help resolve hallucination.

7.
RQ3: What and how can we train students on AI usage?

The findings in RQ2 suggest that technical experts also use AI more strategically and effectively than novices. Here, we ask:
How can we train students to use AI more effectively
? What are some skills and learning opportunities for training better AI users?

7.1.
The effect of in-class demonstration and extended time-on-task

While we did not experiment with intervention mechanisms for training AI use skills (since the core learning objective was on computational data analysis), we did collect some preliminary signals on what AI use skills might be more easily acquirable than others via just in-class demonstrations and extended time-on-task.

Figure 8
.
Appropriate AI use ratio pre- to post-instructions. Ratio =

Method.

Between HW0 and HW1, the instructor provided a 90-minute live-coding demonstration of the HW0 data cleaning task.
The demonstration showcased examples of AI use behaviors in the codebook (
Table

3
), with explanations of when and why each behavior could be useful. For example, the instructor explained “If you find unfamiliar concepts, you can actually ask Gemini to get you started. We can ask,
What are some key steps in Data cleaning?
” while typing and sending the actual question and walking through the Gemini’s answer.
The demo also covered all the task environment space (e.g., overview of Colab as a product) as well as the AI context space (e.g., the code cell generation has access to the entire notebook, while the chatbot sidebar does not have access to code cell outputs).
Students could also re-watch the recording asynchronously.
Despite the limitations of lecturing (e.g., less cognitively engaging than active learning and may create an illusion of fluency without fostering durable understanding
(
Chi and Wylie 2014

Deslauriers et al. 2019

In addition to instructions on AI use (and regular lectures on programming and data science domain knowledge), students moved from HW0’s 15-minute time-limited tasks to HW1 and HW2’s bi-weekly assignments (
Table

1

Therefore, when we analyze students’ behavioral changes pre- to post-demo, any improvement is a result of both extended time on task and the instruction, and students’ room for improvement builds on top of gains already afforded by extended practice time and baseline instructional exposure.

Differentiate Knowledge Dimensions

Below, we provide some behavioral examples to help illustrate each AI use dimension. However, from our behavioral log data, we could not always identify which knowledge dimension exactly applies. For example, a student may know a particular way of using AI already; however, they may not be able to retrieve it properly at the time they need to use it (available but not accessible knowledge
(
Tulving and Pearlstone 1966
)
). Especially when a student did not use AI, we may not conclude if it was because they did not notice the appropriate time to apply this knowledge (
metacognitive
), did not have this knowledge stored (
conceptual
), failed to execute properly (
procedural
), or lacked the persistence to keep trying (
dispositional
). Future work may design more targeted assessments corresponding to each type of knowledge in order to differentiate them (
Section

Metacognitive.

Students often struggled with deciding
when to involve the AI and what to delegate
.
As we analyzed HW1&2 logs (after class instructions), we found multiple instances of students getting stuck in a loop of
explain_error
and
generate_code
without re-evaluating their approach (such as the novice’s code edit sequence
Table

5
). Specifically, these failures stem from repeated
KeyError
exceptions caused by the AI generating code with nonexistent columns — as Gemini in Colab was not able to retrieve the correct column names from the dataframe, the new code without augmented context kept hallucinating nonexistent columns.

This pattern could illustrate multiple missing AI use skills, such as a lack of metacognitive monitoring: the ability to recognize when a strategy is unproductive and to adjust. The students could avoid delegating AI to do the task that it has demonstrated unreliability and providing the correct column name themselves (such as the expert’s code-edit sequence
Table

5
), or alternatively ask AI to
breakdown
the debugging procedure and walk them through a step-by-step process to figure out what is wrong, or add the exact column name as
context_in_prompt
(such as the expert’s prompt-edit sequence
Table

5
). Effective metacognitive use would involve pausing to decide, for example, whether to ask the AI for a structured plan (e.g., “list all columns in my dataset”) before persisting with trial-and-error.

Many failures could stem from the lack of conceptual knowledge regarding the
AI’s task space (what it can do) and context space (what information it can access)

For

, most showed improvement in appropriate AI use (

If the demonstration did not covered some AI use, students may develop a wrong assumption of the AI task space. For example, the instructor did not cover

8.
Discussion and Future Work

8.1.
Design and Pedagogy Implications

Make AI mental models explicit.

Unlike simple chat interfaces, AI integrated in a notebook exposes multiple affordances (code cells, outputs, error panes, menu buttons, etc.).
We observed in the study that students transferred assumptions from more capable general-purpose AIs to their Colab working environment, miscalibrating what the embedded model could access or do. This produced confusion about AI
task space
(what to ask) and
context space
(what the model can “see”). As shown in
Table

5
, a novice during think-aloud prompted the model “Don’t use nonexistent columns, use what you have in the datasheet” after three repeated
KeyError
and commented “Perplexity can do that,” assuming that Colab Gemini would have access to the imported dataframe like other systems. It took them more than five repeated failures to realize that they should adjust their mental model for the system at hand, highlighting the need for explicit conceptual models and metacognitive updating abilities.
On the design side, systems with GenAI assistants should make their task and context scope more legible (e.g., what files and variables are visible to GenAI, what operations are supported, etc). Teaching “what to ask this specific AI” and “what this specific AI knows” is likely getting obsolete as models and systems rapidly evolve. More importantly, instructions are needed for
metacognitive abilities to quickly adjust mental models to different AI system capabilities
.

Facilitate evaluation by providing less information and scaffolding verification.

As we found in
Section

7.1
, using AI to gain additional, evaluative information was a consistent challenge. Asking AI to explain code, bugs, or critique outputs remained limited, despite its potential benefit to improve understanding. We especially observe that students may not persist in reading convoluted output, reflecting reluctance or difficulty in engaging with evaluative or complex information. While there are one-click affordances (explain error buttons) that aim to make it easier to start evaluation, the cognitive load of seeing a lengthy error explanation often creates resistance or reduces the chance for students to learn critical evaluation skills.

On one hand, we need instructions on
dispositional skills to help humans stick to evaluation and mitigate the risk of overreliance
. On the other hand, in terms of feedback design for novice students or users,
less is more.

Explicit instructions that focus exclusively on evaluating LLM outputs may alleviate this cognitive load in a complex task environment
(
Ma et al. 2024
)
.
Additionally, it is possible to lean on the rich log data to trigger real-time hints and output style changes. For example, repeated “fix it” after
KeyError
in short intervals suggests struggles of evaluation, so instead of providing a full code fix saying “If your column name is X, the code will look like Y [lengthy code] [lengthy explanation],” generating a diagnosis plan or hint for the user one step at a time might be more effective, e.g., “Run
df.columns
and check the output. Copy-paste the exact column name you want to use in code.”

In terms of design, generating UIs
(
Chen et al. 2025a
)
instead of lengthy text may help alleviate the evaluation burden. Designs that intentionally insert intermediate checkpoints can help decompose big evaluation plans into more manageable chunks, or frontload evaluation by asking users to provide requirements before generation, e.g., in the form of plan skeletons and verification checklists
(
Kazemitabaar et al. 2024a
)
. Pedagogy can mirror this by requiring learners to specify success criteria and requirements before requesting code
(
Ma et al. 2025
)
.

8.2.
Limitations and Future Work

Going deeper: ground in assessments and controlled experiment to study factors influencing effective LLM use

Our analyses suggest that students’ technical expertise significantly predicts performance during LLM use (similar to prior literature
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
), whereas LLM and communication expertise factors did not (
Section

5
). Other work also shows that LLMs can narrow gaps between novices and experts
(
Pickering et al. 2025
)
, or even impede expert performance under certain conditions
(
Becker et al. 2025
)
. This highlights that technical expertise is not the only factor shaping effective LLM use. Our log analyses revealed the importance of AI use strategies, which point toward the need to define and evaluate “AI use expertise” more directly, as what we have attempted in
Section

7.2
.

To probe the interplay between technical expertise and LLM use, we compared performance between experts and novices in the timed HW0 under conditions with and without LLMs (
Table

1
). A Mann–Whitney U test (as data violated normality assumptions)
(
McKnight and Najab 2010
)
showed a significant expertise gap when students did not use LLMs (
p=0.027<0.05
), but the gap disappeared when LLM was used (
p>0.05
). This suggests that under time pressure, LLMs may temporarily offset expertise advantages, indicating that factors such as time constraints also influence the effectiveness of LLM use. We also measured affective factors (motivation, self-efficacy) in self-reported survey items (
Appendix

B
), but none significantly predicted performance.

Our sample included 36 students in authentic classroom contexts. While this ensured ecological validity, it is a small sample and constrained our ability to isolate factors such as undisclosed external tool use (e.g., ChatGPT alongside Colab Gemini) or overlapping expertise dimensions. For example, Python and data science proficiency were moderately correlated in our sample, so we could not examine their independent effects on effective LLM use. Future studies should recruit distinct participant groups or employ pretests. More robust performance-based assessment and controlled experiments are needed to disentangle the interplay between more nuanced factors such as domain expertise, implementation expertise, AI-use expertise, affect, and situational factors like time pressure.

Going broader: extend to more diverse task contexts.

The tasks employed in our study were appropriate for classroom learning but were not “LLM-hard:” current models could achieve high accuracy. Future work could incorporate more complex tasks that better reveal the value of AI use skills and enable observations on how learners adapt when models struggle.

While the learning objectives for AI use skills (
Table

4
) were derived from programmatic data analysis tasks, they should extend beyond our study context. Many of the objectives are broadly relevant to problem-solving and higher-order thinking skills; for instance, clarifying the task space of any tool, communicating requirements effectively with decomposition, and verifying results. Future research could examine whether instruction around AI use transfers to these more general competencies, and explore how to evaluate AI use expertise more systematically (as self-rated LLM expertise may not be appropriate).

Finally, we chose Google Colab for the class because it embeds a free, in-house Gemini with different UIs, which enabled us to explore diverse behavioral AI use patterns in our research questions. However, our data collection relied on a custom logging script in the browser, which can be less robust than existing Jupyter notebook logging toolkits like JELAI
(
Valle Torre et al. 2025
)
. For example, our approach may capture an auto-completion as users’ code edits (although infrequent), and Colab’s frequently changing interface raises concerns about long-term replicability. Future work could build on established logging toolkits to improve data quality and generalizability.

9.
Conclusion

LLMs promise to broaden access to technical work, yet our study shows that not everyone benefits equally. Technical expertise remained the strongest predictor of success in homework, and experts engaged with AI more strategically than novices. Behavioral log analysis highlighted that experts provide clearer context, decompose tasks, and strategically use AI, while novices tend to rely on AI reactively in the face of challenges. While demonstrations and extended time helped students adopt clearer prompting practices, evaluative skills remained challenging.
These findings suggest that effective AI use is not just about producing good prompts, but also more about cultivating durable competencies in multiple dimensions.
We translate these insights into design and pedagogy implications for AI literacy: explicate mental models, clarify before generating, and scaffold verification.
Ultimately, effective AI use is not just better prompts — it is about better problem solving.

D.1.
Segmentation Prompt

Task
