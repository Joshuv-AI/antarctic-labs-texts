Pulsar timing array analysis in a Legendre polynomial basis
Bruce Allen, Arian L. von Blanckenburg, Ken D. Olum

Abstract
We use Legendre polynomials, previously employed in this context by Lee et al. Lee et al. 2012 , van Haasteren and Levin van Haasteren and Levin 2013 , and Pitrou and Cusin Pitrou and Cusin 2025 , to model signals in pulsar timing arrays (PTA). These replace the (Fourier mode) basis of trigonometric functions normally used for data analysis. The Legendre basis makes it simpler to incorporate pulsar modeling effects, which remove constant-, linear-, and quadratic-in-time terms from pulsar timing residuals. In the Legendre basis, this zeroes the amplitudes of the the first three Legendre polynomials. We use this basis to construct an optimal quadratic cross-correlation estimator μ ^ \hat{\mu} of the Hellings and Downs (HD) correlation and compute its variance σ μ ^ 2 \sigma^{2}_{\hat{\mu}} in the way described by Allen and Romano Allen and Romano 2025 . Remarkably, if the gravitational-wave background (GWB) and pulsar noise power spectra are (sums of) power laws in frequency, then in this basis one obtains analytic closed forms for many quantities of interest.



I
Introduction

Pulsar timing array (PTA) collaborations have observed pulse times of
arrival (TOAs) for many years. Recently, they reported varying levels
of evidence for a stochastic gravitational wave background (GWB)

Antoniadis et al. 2023
;
Agazie et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
Zic et al. 2023
;
Miles et al. 2025
.

To carry out this inference, PTAs compare the observed TOAs to models
which incorporate the effects of GWBs and intrinsic pulsar noise
(including both variations in the pulsar spin and in interstellar
propagation delays). These are typically modeled as independent
stationary and Gaussian stochastic random processes, with power-law
spectra. The GWB can be distinguished from intrinsic pulsar noise
because it induces a characteristic pattern of inter-pulsar
correlations, first predicted by Hellings and Downs (HD)
Hellings and Downs 1983
.

The analysis of the TOAs is done in terms of the residuals that remain
after subtracting a deterministic model of the pulsar arrival times in
the absence of a GWB. This so-called “timing model” always includes
a constant term which depends upon the pulsar’s rotational phase and
distance to the solar system barycenter (SSB), a linear term
determined by the pulsar spin frequency and velocity along the line of
sight, and a quadratic term determined by the pulsar’s intrinsic
spin-down rate and its acceleration along the line of sight. We call
these three terms the “universal terms”, because there may also be
other deterministic terms. For example, if the pulsar is in a binary,
the orbital motion of the pulsar around its companion results in a
periodically changing propagation path length.

The timing model parameters that characterize these terms are not
known independently. Rather, they are determined by fitting to the
observed TOAs. Thus, the analysis of the residuals must take into
account that this fitting process also subtracts away constant, linear
and quadratic effects that arise because of the GWB. (Fitting the
timing model also removes the other terms, but here we consider only
these three universal terms.)

A zero-mean multivariate Gaussian probability distribution is
completely determined by its covariance. Thus, a PTA GWB search needs
the covariance matrices (and their inverses) for the timing residuals
arising from pulsar intrinsic noise contributions, and from the
GWB
van Haasteren et al. 2025
. Both can be calculated from the data models, and
in principle this can be done in the same (time) domain as the PTA
data is collected. The standard approach is described
in
Gersbach et al. 2025
, but note that it incorrectly assumes that the
covariance matrices of stationary processes (for example,
H_{jk}
,
defined by
(
Allen and Romano 2025
, Eq. (16))
) are diagonal in the Fourier
basis.

For the multivariate Gaussian model, the log of the likelihood is a
quadratic form in the data; the inverse of the covariance matrix is
the “metric” or “kernel” of this quadratic form. Up to an overall
normalization, this likelihood is the probability that any specific
set of residuals would occur. However, generating posterior samples
with this likelihood is computationally intractable: the large number
(hundreds of thousands) of TOAs leads to enormous covariance matrices,
and computing their inverses is computationally very expensive.
Instead, the analysis uses a small basis (typically
\lesssim 100

elements) to write the covariance matrix in a low rank form. Then its
inverse and the log likelihood can be computed quickly.

To obtain this low rank form, most PTA analyses use a Fourier basis
whose components are exponential functions of time with oscillation
frequencies
j/T
, where
T
is the total time of observation, and
j

is a positive integer. In this paper, we explore an alternative
basis of Legendre polynomials. The advantage of this basis is
that the constant, linear, and quadratic terms in the timing model
span the same space as the first three Legendre polynomials.
Therefore to remove these timing model terms we can simply omit these
three polynomials from our basis.

The perturbations in pulse production, propagation, and observation
are described in terms of timing residuals. These are denoted by

\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)
, where
a
specifies the pulsar and
t
is the time of
observation. In the main body of this paper, the analysis is done in
terms of these timing residuals.

II
Fourier Basis

To begin, suppose that the pulsar timing residuals are continuously
observed on an interval
t\in[-T/2,T/2]
for some fixed
T>0
. In the
usual Fourier basis, we represent the timing residuals on this
interval by the Fourier series

\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)=\sum_{j}\mathcal{T}_{a}^{j}{\rm e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}f_{j}t}\text{ for }t\in[-T/2,T/2]\,,

(1)

where
f_{j}=j/T
are discrete Fourier frequencies,
T
is the
observation time,
\mathcal{T}_{a}^{j}
are complex expansion coefficients,
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Indices
a,b,c,d
will denote pulsars, and indices
j,k,\ell,m

will label modes in the Fourier basis.

The Fourier basis would be the preferred choice if we were analyzing
periodic signals with period
T
. In that case, the covariance matrix
of the Fourier modes,
\langle\mathcal{T}^{j}_{a}\mathcal{T}^{k*}_{b}\rangle
, would be
diagonal in
j
and
k
. That is not the case here, because the GWB
and pulsars are observed for a finite time and have components whose
frequencies do not correspond to an integer number of cycles within
the observation time. See the Conclusion of
Allen and Romano 2025
for
further discussion of this point.

van Haasteren and Levin 2013

We use conventional Legendre polynomials, which have normalization

\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}z\,P_{\mu}(z)P_{\nu}(z)=\frac{2}{2\mu+1}\delta_{\mu\nu}\,,

(3)

where
\delta_{\mu\nu}

appear in (

(
2

P_{\nu}(2t/T)
and integrating over

. Using
(

the sum, giving

\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}=\frac{2\mu+1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\mathrm{d}t\,P_{\mu}(2t/T)\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)\,.

(4)

Since the Legendre polynomials and
\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)
are real, the expansion
coefficients
\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}
are real.

There are many different families of orthogonal polynomials. Here, the
Legendre polynomials are preferred because (1) the corresponding
measure is uniform in time, and (2) the power term
z^{k}
is a linear
combination of Legendre polynomials
P_{\mu}
of order
\mu\leq k
and
consequently is orthogonal to polynomials of order
\mu>k~
2
2

2

One can construct the Legendre polynomials via a
Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization procedure on the interval
z\in[-1,1]

with inner product
(A,B)=\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}zA(z)B(z)
. The first
basis element is the constant function. The second basis element is
the linear function
z
, which is already orthogonal to the first
basis element. The third basis element is obtained from the quadratic
function
z^{2}
, after subtracting a constant term to make it
orthogonal to the first. And so on, with each element being made
orthogonal to its predecessors by subtracting appropriate multiples of
those with the same parity under reflection
z\to-z
.
. The first
condition is relevant because (in fitting parameters via pulsar
timing) the post-fit timing residuals are weighted uniformly in
time. The second condition isolates the effects of pulsar timing model
fitting to the first three coefficients in the expansion.

The equivalent of Parseval’s theorem is obtained by squaring (
2
)
and integrating over
t\in[-T/2,T/2]
. Using (
3
) converts the
double sum to a single sum, and dividing both sides by
T
gives

\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\mathrm{d}t\,\bigl[\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)\bigr]^{2}=\sum_{\mu}\frac{1}{2\mu+1}(\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu})^{2}\,.

(5)

Here, and going forward, we drop the summation range
\mu=0,1,\dots

from the summation.

This relation makes it easy to incorporate the effects of removing
constant-, linear-, and quadratic-in-time terms from
\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)
: we
will see that it corresponds to setting
\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}=0
for
\mu=0,1,2
. Thus, in many places in this paper, the summations over the
Legendre indices start at
\mu=3
.

IV
Effects of fitting a pulsar timing model

Pulsar timing residuals are typically obtained using

TEMPO

Nice et al. 2015
,
TEMPO2

Hobbs et al. 2006
, or

PINT

Luo et al. 2021
. Starting from the observed
pulse arrival times, these subtract deterministic terms to obtain the
“post-fit” timing residuals. The parameters of the deterministic
models are selected to minimize the sum of the squares of the
residuals, effectively minimizing the lhs of (
5
).

Adjusting the pulsar phase, its intrinsic rotation frequency, and
its intrinsic spindown, removes terms constant, linear and
quadratic in
t
from
\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)
. This is because the coefficients
of these terms are selected to minimize the mean-square post-fit
timing residuals, given by the lhs of (
5
). The post-fit
timing residuals are then given by

\check{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(t)\equiv(Q\mathcal{T}_{a})(t)

(6)

with
Q
denoting the linear operator which projects out terms
constant, linear and quadratic in
t
. We use the háček
accent
\check{\ }
to denote quantities after this operator has been
applied.

The beauty of the Legendre polynomial decomposition is that the
effects of this fitting process are very simple to describe. Because
the lhs of (
5
) is the mean-squared timing residual, it follows
from the rhs that removal of the constant term from
\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)
sets

\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}\to 0
for
\mu=0
, removal of the constant and linear terms sets
\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}\to 0
for
\mu=0,1
and removal of the constant, linear and quadratic terms sets
\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}\to 0
for
\mu=0,1,2
.
This is because (a) the Legendre polynomial
P_{\mu}(2t/T)
is a
polynomial of order
\mu
in
t
and (b) Parseval’s theorem (
5
)
shows that the minimization is achieved by driving the corresponding
coefficients to zero. Hence the components of the projection are

Q^{\mu}_{\nu}=\begin{cases}\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}&\text{for }3\leq\mu\text{ and }3\leq\nu\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(7)

in the Legendre basis.

Now we consider how the timing residuals in the
“short” observation time interval
T
are related to the underlying
stochastic processes. Those processes are stationary over
cosmological time scales (
10^{9}
years) which are much longer
than the observation time
T
(decades). Over the long time interval
the timing residuals (without any subtractions) may be written
as a Fourier integral

\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)=\int\mathrm{d}f\,\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(f)\,{\rm e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}ft}\,,

(8)

where the integral is over
f\in\mathbb{R}
, and a tilde

\tilde{}
over a time-domain function denotes its frequency-space
equivalent.

Since
\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)
is real, the resulting Fourier
amplitudes satisfy
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(f)=\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{a}^{*}(-f)
. If we substitute
(
8
) into (
4
), we obtain

\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}=\frac{2\mu+1}{T}\!\int\!\mathrm{d}f\,\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(f)\,\!\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\!\mathrm{d}t\,{\rm e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}ft}P_{\mu}(2t/T)\,.

(9)

The integral may be evaluated using
(
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2007
, Eq. (7.243.5))
, which is

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}{\rm e}^{i\alpha x}P_{\mu}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}^{\mu}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\alpha}}J_{\mu+1/2}(\alpha)

(10)

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{i}^{\mu}j_{\mu}(\alpha)\,.

The first equality assumes that
\alpha>0
. The second equality follows
from the definition of the spherical Bessel function
j_{\mu}(\alpha)
, and
holds for all real
\alpha

We evaluate (
9
) using (
10

\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}=(2\mu+1)\mathrm{i}^{\mu}\int\mathrm{d}f\,\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(f)\,j_{\mu}(\pi fT)\,.

(11)

This is similar to the corresponding Fourier basis equation given
in
(
Allen and Romano 2025
, Eq. (13))
, with the cardinal sine function
{\rm sinc}
replaced
by a spherical Bessel function and with timing residuals instead of redshifts.

VI
Evaluation of covariance matrix for power-law power spectra

The integrals in (
17
) can be computed in closed form for
power-law power spectra. For a fixed
\gamma
, take
H(f)
to be the
power law defined by
H(f)\propto|f|^{-\gamma+2}
(the offset in

\gamma

We can see that the integrals in (
17

\gamma

asymptotic behavior of the spherical Bessel functions for small

j_{\mu}(z)\approx\frac{z^{\mu}}{(2\mu+1)!!}\,.

(18)

Since projection (

\gamma<7

(-1)^{\dfrac{\mu+1}{2}}\left(\dfrac{\cos z}{z}\right)&\text{for }\mu\text{ odd}\,.\end{cases}

(19)

-1<\gamma

\sigma_{\hat{\mu}}^{2}=\frac{\mu_{\text{u}}^{2}(\gamma)}{\displaystyle\sum_{ab\in\gamma}\sum_{cd\in\gamma}\sum_{\mu,\nu,\lambda,\kappa}\mu_{ab}J^{\mu\nu}\,(D^{-1})^{ab,cd}_{\mu\nu,\lambda\kappa}\,\mu_{cd}J^{\lambda\kappa}}\,,

(38)

and

\rho^{2}=\displaystyle\sum_{ab\in\gamma}\sum_{cd\in\gamma}\sum_{\mu,\nu,\lambda,\kappa}\mu_{ab}J^{\mu\nu}\,(D_{0}^{-1})^{ab,cd}_{\mu\nu,\lambda\kappa}\,\mu_{cd}J^{\lambda\kappa}\,.

(39)

In these formulas, the Legendre component indices
\mu,\nu,\lambda

and
\kappa
are summed over all positive integers.

Writing
\sigma_{\hat{\mu}}^{2}
and
\rho^{2}
in the Legendre basis now makes it easy to
account for the effects of removing the constant, linear and quadratic
terms in the timing residuals. The effect of such removal is simple:
In (
38
) and (
39
) we have to replace

\displaystyle J^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\mapsto\check{J}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\equiv Q^{\mu}_{\lambda}J^{\lambda\kappa}Q^{\nu}_{\kappa}\,,

(40)

\displaystyle D_{ab,cd}^{\mu\nu,\lambda\kappa}

\displaystyle\mapsto\check{D}_{ab,cd}^{\mu\nu,\lambda\kappa}

\displaystyle\equiv Q^{\mu}_{\alpha}Q^{\nu}_{\beta}D_{ab,cd}^{\alpha\beta,\gamma\delta}Q^{\lambda}_{\gamma}Q^{\kappa}_{\delta}\,.

Note that taking the pseudoinverse and projecting are two operations that do not commute if applied to non-diagonal
{\boldsymbol{D}}
. The correct order here is to first project
{\boldsymbol{D}}
to
\boldsymbol{\check{D}}
and then use the pseudoinverse of
\boldsymbol{\check{D}}
(or
\boldsymbol{\check{D}}_{0}
) in (

) and (
39
). If
a<b
and

, the pseudoinverse

satisfies

\sum_{cd\in\gamma}\sum_{\lambda\kappa}(\check{D}\vphantom{D}^{-1})^{ab,cd}_{\mu\nu,\lambda\kappa}\check{D}_{cd,ef}^{\lambda\kappa,\alpha\beta}=\delta^{a}_{e}\delta^{b}_{f}Q^{\alpha}_{\mu}Q^{\beta}_{\nu}\,.

(41)

IX
Differing pulsar observation times

Up to this point, we have assumed that all pulsars are observed over
the same time interval
t\in[-T/2,T/2]
. However, this is not the
case for most PTAs, which are composed of pulsars that have different
observational time spans. In this section, we modify our calculations
to account for this.

Suppose that each pulsar is observed over a different epoch. Let
T_{a}

denote the total observing time span for pulsar
a
, and let

\Delta_{a}
denote the time at the center of that interval. So the
pulsar
a
observation interval is

\Delta_{a}-\frac{T_{a}}{2}\leq t\leq\Delta_{a}+\frac{T_{a}}{2}\,.

(42)

Our previous calculations correspond to the case where
T_{a}=T
and

\Delta_{a}=0
for all pulsars.

In the time interval (
42
), each pulsar’s timing residuals can be
expanded in Legendre polynomials, analogous to (
2
). Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu}\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}P_{\mu}(2(t-\Delta_{a})/T_{a})

(43)

\displaystyle\text{for }t\in\bigg[\Delta_{a}-\frac{T_{a}}{2},\Delta_{a}+\frac{T_{a}}{2}\bigg]\,.

The real coefficients
\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}
are obtained in the same way as for
(
4
), yielding

\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}=\frac{2\mu+1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}\mathrm{d}t\,P_{\mu}(2t/T_{a})\mathcal{T}_{a}(t+\Delta_{a})\,.

(44)

Substituting the Fourier integral (
8
) into (
44
) and
carrying out the integral with (
10
) gives the Legendre basis
coefficients in term of the Fourier amplitudes, as

\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}=(2\mu+1)\mathrm{i}^{\mu}\int\mathrm{d}f\,\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(f)\,j_{\mu}(\pi fT_{a})\,{\rm e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}f\Delta_{a}}\,.

(45)

This correctly reduces to (
11
) in the limit
\Delta_{a}\to 0

and
T_{a}\to T
.

The covariance of these Legendre coefficients is still given
by (
V
),
\langle\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}\mathcal{T}_{b}^{\nu}\rangle=J^{\mu\nu}\mu_{ab}+M_{a}^{\mu\nu}\delta_{ab}
, but the covariance matrices are
now

\displaystyle J^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=(2\mu+1)(2\nu+1)\mathrm{i}^{\mu-\nu}\int\mathrm{d}f\,\frac{H(f)}{\pi f^{2}}j_{\mu}(\pi fT_{a})j_{\nu}(\pi fT_{b})\,{\rm e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}f(\Delta_{a}-\Delta_{b})}

(46)

\displaystyle M_{a}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=(2\mu+1)(2\nu+1)\mathrm{i}^{\mu-\nu}\int\mathrm{d}f\,\frac{N_{a}(f)}{4\pi^{2}f^{2}}j_{\mu}(\pi fT_{a})j_{\nu}(\pi fT_{b})\,{\rm e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}f(\Delta_{a}-\Delta_{b})}\,.

Note that these matrices are real, but are no longer symmetric in
\mu
and
\nu
.

To write these as single-sided integrals, first consider
J^{\mu\nu}
.
For
\mu+\nu
even (implying
\mu-\nu
even) the quantity
H(f)j_{\mu}(\pi fT_{a})j_{\nu}(\pi fT_{b})/\pi f^{2}
is an even function of
f
, so only the real part
of the complex phase will contribute. If
\mu+\nu
is odd (implying
\mu-\nu

odd) then only the imaginary part contributes. So, we obtain

J^{\mu\nu}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 2(2\mu+1)(2\nu+1)(-1)^{\frac{\mu-\nu+1}{2}}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}f\,\frac{H(f)}{\pi f^{2}}j_{\mu}(\pi fT_{a})j_{\nu}(\pi fT_{b})\sin\bigl(2\pi f(\Delta_{a}-\Delta_{b})\bigr)&\text{ for }\mu+\nu\text{ odd}\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle 2(2\mu+1)(2\nu+1)(-1)^{\frac{\mu-\nu}{2}\phantom{+1}}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}f\,\frac{H(f)}{\pi f^{2}}j_{\mu}(\pi fT_{a})j_{\nu}(\pi fT_{b})\cos\bigl(2\pi f(\Delta_{a}-\Delta_{b})\bigr)&\text{ for }\mu+\nu\text{ even}\,.\end{cases}

(47)

The corresponding equations for
M_{a}^{\mu\nu}
are obtained by
dividing by
4\pi
and replacing
H(f)
with
N_{a}(f)
.

We now evaluate these integrals for the power-law form of
H(f)
given
in (
20
). In the case where
|\Delta_{a}-\Delta_{b}|
is small
compared to
T_{a}
and
T_{b}
, we can use
(
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2007
, Eq. 6.574.1)
to
express these in terms of the Gauss hypergeometric function

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;d;z)
. In the case where
|\Delta_{a}-\Delta_{b}|
is not
small, we can make use of
(
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2007
, Eq. 6.578.1)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!x^{\rho-1}J_{\lambda}(ax)\,J_{\mu}(bx)\,J_{\nu}(cx)\,\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{\rho-1}a^{\lambda}b^{\mu}c^{-\lambda-\mu-\rho}\Gamma(\frac{\lambda+\mu+\nu+\rho}{2})}{\Gamma(\lambda+1)\Gamma(\mu+1)\Gamma(1\!-\!\frac{\lambda-\mu+\nu-\rho}{2})}\times

(48)

\displaystyle F_{4}\left(\frac{\lambda+\mu-\nu+\rho}{2},\frac{\lambda+\mu+\nu+\rho}{2};\lambda+1,\,\mu+1;\frac{a^{2}}{c^{2}},\,\frac{b^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)\,

\displaystyle\text{assuming }\Re(\lambda+\mu+\nu+\rho)>0,

\displaystyle\Re(\rho)<\frac{5}{2},\,a>0,\,b>0,\,c>0,\,\text{ and }c>a+b\,,

where
F_{4}
denotes the Appell hypergeometric function. A notable
feature of this equality is that it is not explicitly symmetric under
interchange of the three Bessel functions, for example swapping
\mu

and
\nu
along with
b
and
c
.

To cast (
47
) into the form of (
48
), we make use of the
second equality in (
10
), and use

\displaystyle\sin(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\dfrac{\pi x}{2}}\,J_{1/2}(x)

(49)

\displaystyle\cos(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\dfrac{\pi x}{2}}\,J_{-1/2}(x)\,

to write the
\sin
and
\cos
functions as Bessel functions.

If we take different observation times into account,
\boldsymbol{J}
and
{\boldsymbol{M}}

are no longer symmetric (in either the Legendre or Fourier

{\boldsymbol{D}}
(timing residual equivalent
of

no longer

becomes invertible if the

and

X
Transmission functions

In this section, we calculate the covariance between timing residuals
after the universal terms have been removed by fitting a timing model.
As a special case, we recover “transmission functions” derived
numerically in
Hazboun et al. 2019
and analytically
in
Pitrou and Cusin 2025
.

Before fitting a timing model, the covariance between timing
residuals
\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)
and
\mathcal{T}_{b}(t^{\prime})
with
t\in[\Delta_{a}-T_{a}/2,\Delta_{a}+T_{a}/2]
and
t^{\prime}\in[\Delta_{b}-T_{b}/2,\Delta_{b}+T_{b}/2]

is given by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)\mathcal{T}_{b}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\!\int\mathrm{d}f\!\int\mathrm{d}f^{\prime}\langle\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(f)\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{b}^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(ft-f^{\prime}t^{\prime})}

(50)

\displaystyle=\!\int\mathrm{d}f\biggl[\frac{H(f)}{\pi f^{2}}\mu_{ab}+\frac{N_{a}(f)}{4\pi^{2}f^{2}}\delta_{ab}\biggr]\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}f(t-t^{\prime})}\,,

where the first equality follows from (
8
) and the second
from (
14
). The rhs is a function only of the time difference

t-t^{\prime}
, as must be the case for a stationary random process.

Suppose that we now fit a timing model, and subtract the universal
terms from the timing residuals. As discussed in

section

IV
, with the expansion (
43
), these
post-subtraction timing residuals are

\check{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(t)=\sum_{\mu=3}^{\infty}\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}P_{\mu}(2(t-\Delta_{a})/T_{a})\,.

(51)

The covariance between
\check{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(t)
and
\check{\mathcal{T}}_{b}(t^{\prime})
is given by

\displaystyle\langle\check{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(t)\check{\mathcal{T}}_{b}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu=3}^{\infty}\sum_{\nu=3}^{\infty}\langle\mathcal{T}_{a}^{\mu}\mathcal{T}_{b}^{\nu}\rangle P_{\mu}(2(t-\Delta_{a})/T_{a})P_{\nu}(2(t^{\prime}-\Delta_{b})/T_{b})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}f\,\biggl[\frac{H(f)}{\pi f^{2}}\mu_{ab}+\frac{N_{a}(f)}{4\pi^{2}f^{2}}\delta_{ab}\biggr]\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}f(\Delta_{a}-\Delta_{b})}\bigg(\sum_{\mu=3}^{\infty}(2\mu+1)\mathrm{i}^{\mu}j_{\mu}(\pi fT_{a})P_{\mu}(2(t-\Delta_{a})/T_{a})\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\bigg(\sum_{\nu=3}^{\infty}(2\nu+1)\mathrm{i}^{\nu}j_{\nu}(\pi fT_{b})P_{\nu}(2(t^{\prime}-\Delta_{b})/T_{b})\bigg)^{\!\!*}

(52)

where the first equality follows from (
51
), and the
second equality follows from (
V
) and (
46
) after
regrouping terms. We now use the
identity
(
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2007
, Eq. (8.511.4))

\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha x}=\sum_{\mu=0}^{\infty}(2\mu+1)\mathrm{i}^{\mu}j_{\mu}(\alpha)P_{\mu}(x)\,,

(53)

which holds for
-1\leq x\leq 1
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
. From (
53
), it follows that

\sum_{\mu=3}^{\infty}(2\mu+1)\mathrm{i}^{\mu}j_{\mu}(\pi fT_{a})P_{\mu}(2(t-\Delta_{a})/T_{a})=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}f(t-\Delta_{a})}S_{a}(f,t)\,,

(54)

where we defined

S_{a}(f,t)\equiv 1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}f(t-\Delta_{a})}\sum_{\mu=0}^{2}(2\mu+1)\mathrm{i}^{\mu}j_{\mu}(\pi fT_{a})P_{\mu}(2(t-\Delta_{a})/T_{a})\,.

(55)

To get from (
53
) to (
54
) first
replace
\alpha
with
\pi fT_{a}
and
x

with
2(t-\Delta_{a})/T_{a}
. Then, split the sum in the
terms
\nu=0,1,2
and
\nu=3,4,\dots
, isolate the sum
over
\nu=3,4,\dots
and factor out the phase
\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}f(t-\Delta_{a})}
.

Using (
54
) in (
X
), the
covariance of the post-fit timing residuals is

\langle\check{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(t)\check{\mathcal{T}}_{b}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}f\,\biggl[\frac{H(f)}{\pi f^{2}}\mu_{ab}+\frac{N_{a}(f)}{4\pi^{2}f^{2}}\delta_{ab}\biggr]S_{a}(f,t)S_{b}^{*}(f,t^{\prime})\,\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}f(t-t^{\prime})}\,,

(56)

Note that
S_{a}(f,t)S_{b}^{*}(f,t^{\prime})
is not a function of
t-t^{\prime}
,
hence the covariance
\langle\check{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(t)\check{\mathcal{T}}_{b}(t^{\prime})\rangle
is not a
function of
t-t^{\prime}
. This shows that the least-squares subtraction
of the constant/linear/quadratic timing-model terms makes the timing
residuals non-stationary. This point was first made
in
Lee et al. 2012
, which remarks that “such a fit is not a stationary
process”, and independently in
van Haasteren and Levin 2013
, which
remarks that “stationarity breaks down in the fitting process.” Both also
demonstrate that the subtraction of the universal timing model terms
may be implemented via a projection operation.

We define
R_{a}(f)
to be the time average of
\left|S_{a}(f,t)\right|^{2}

over
t\in[\Delta_{a}-T_{a}/2,\Delta_{a}+T_{a}/2]
. Using (
3
)
and the identity
(
Olver et al. 2025
, Eq. (10.60.12))

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(2n+1)j^{2}_{n}(z)=1\,,

(57)

we can show from (
54
) that

\displaystyle R_{a}(f)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{\Delta_{a}-T_{a}/2}^{\Delta_{a}+T_{a}/2}\mathrm{d}t\left|S_{a}(f,t)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{\mu=0}^{2}(2\mu+1)j_{\mu}^{2}(\pi fT_{a})\,.

(58)

If
|f|\ll 1/T_{a}
, then
R_{a}(f)\propto f^{6}
, as shown in
Figure

2
.

Figure 2:
The transmission function
R_{a}(f)
shows
low-frequency behavior identical to that
of
(
Hazboun et al. 2019
, Fig. 2a)
,
and
(
Pitrou and Cusin 2025
, Fig. 3)
.

As described in
Hazboun et al. 2019
, one can
interpret
R_{a}(f)
as a transmission function, which shows how
timing model subtraction effectively removes powers at low
frequencies. To see why, first consider the mean-squared
timing residuals before timing model subtraction, obtained by
setting
a=b
and
t=t^{\prime}
in (

\Bigl\langle\left(\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)\right)^{2}\Bigr\rangle=\int\!\!\mathrm{d}f\biggl[\frac{2H(f)}{3\pi f^{2}}+\frac{N_{a}(f)}{4\pi^{2}f^{2}}\biggr]\,.

(59)

Note that (as implied by stationarity) this is time independent. In
contrast, after the timing model subtraction, and also
setting
\Delta_{a}=0
, the time average of (

\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathrm{d}t\,\,\,\,\,\Bigl\langle\left(\check{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(t)\right)^{2}\Bigr\rangle=\int\!\mathrm{d}f\bigg[\frac{2H(f)}{3\pi f^{2}}+\frac{N_{a}(f)}{4\pi^{2}f^{2}}\bigg]R_{a}(f)\,.

(60)

The transmission function

, shown in
Figure

unity at high frequencies but falls off rapidly at frequencies
below

. This demonstrates that subtracting the
universal timing model terms effectively acts as a filter,
removing low-frequency components of both the
gravitational-wave background and the pulsar noise. The
transmission function is the response of that filter.
Equation (

), with (
X
) inserted, was
also obtained in
(
Pitrou and Cusin 2025
, Eqs. (119) and
(120))
.

Note that (
60

power-law
H(f)
(
20
) and
N_{a}(f)
(
21
)
if
\gamma<7
, provided that we impose a high-frequency
cutoff, restricting the integral to
\left|f\right|\leq f_{\rm m}
. This
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T_{a}}\int_{-T_{a}/2}^{T_{a}/2}\!\mathrm{d}t\left\langle\left[\check{\mathcal{T}}_{a}(t)\right]^{\!2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=D_{2}\!\left(A_{\rm gw}^{2}f_{\rm r}^{-3},\gamma_{\rm gw},f_{\rm m},f_{\rm r},T_{a}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+D_{2}\!\left(A_{\rm rn}^{2}f_{\rm r}^{-3},\gamma_{\rm rn},f_{\rm m},f_{\rm r},T_{a}\right),

(61)

with
D_{2}
defined in
Appendix

.

XI
Conclusion

In this paper we developed the use of Legendre polynomials as a basis
in PTA data analysis, as originally employed
by
Lee et al. 2012
;
van Haasteren and Levin 2013
;
Pitrou and Cusin 2025
. This basis makes it
straightforward to take account of the universal elements of pulsar
timing models: the constant, linear, and quadratic functions, simply
by dropping the first few Legendre polynomials. We showed how to use
this formalism to compute the covariance between coefficients.
Remarkably, it can be computed in closed form, even for bases adapted
to pulsars with different periods of observation. We showed also how
to go back and forth between Fourier and the Legendre basis.

If one uses a complete basis, any scalar result should be independent
of the basis. For example, Allen and Romano
Allen and Romano 2025

calculated the variance
\sigma^{2}_{\hat{\mu}}
of the HD estimator,
which may be expressed in terms of a scalar of the form

\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}
, where

\boldsymbol{v}
is a column vector and
\boldsymbol{C}
is a
covariance matrix. This scalar quantity is basis independent, so if
we compute
\sigma^{2}_{\hat{\mu}}
using the Legendre basis (
2
), we should
recover the same value as in
the Fourier basis (
1
). On the
other hand, if we drop terms with
\nu<3
in the Legendre basis, this corresponds
precisely
to a projection onto the modes which remain after pulsar timing
subtraction. Dropping these three terms from the summations reduces
the number of degrees of freedom by three.

This reduction of dimension is accomplished in the Bayesian analysis
by marginalizing over pulsar timing models. Here, this reduction of
dimension / projection onto a subspace / elimination of degrees of
freedom is accomplished by beginning the summations over basis
functions at
\mu,\nu,\lambda,\kappa=3
. This corresponds exactly to the Allen and
Romano analysis, which explicitly states (text after Eq. (16)
of
Allen and Romano 2025
) that if the determinants of
\boldsymbol{H}
or

\boldsymbol{P}_{a}
vanish, then their inverses are to be replaced by
the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverses.

The reduction of dimension can also be carried out in the Fourier
basis. However, in that basis, the terms linear-in-
t
and the
quadratic-in-
t
terms are represented by an infinite sum of basis
elements. In contrast, in the Legendre basis, those terms are
completely included in the
\nu=0
,
1
and
2
modes. So, if a
statistic is computed using a finite number of modes, then the Fourier
basis does not remove these terms completely.

In this work, our main application is to estimate the HD correlation

\hat{\mu}
in “position space”, meaning, as a function of the
inter-pulsar angle
\gamma
. One can also represent the HD
correlation in “harmonic space”, as
\mu(\gamma)=\sum_{l}c_{l}P_{l}(\cos\gamma)
, and estimate the coefficients
c_{l}
. Such
estimation was studied in
Nay et al. 2024
, and optimal
estimators
\hat{c}_{l}
are constructed
in
Pitrou and Cusin 2025
;
Allen and Romano 2024
. These optimal estimators
are scalars under basis transformations, so the methods of this paper
are applicable, and provide a simple way to account for the effects of
removing the universal timing terms from pulsar timing residuals.

Appendix C
Redshifts

Some of the literature, for example
Allen and Romano 2025
, describes
arrival time perturbations in terms of pulsar “redshifts”. These
redshifts are the derivatives of the timing residuals

Z_{a}(t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)\,.

(77)

A positive redshift means that the pulsar rotation frequency
(typically hundred of Hz) decreases slightly: the pulses come farther
and farther apart,
\mathcal{T}_{a}(t)
increases with time
t
, and

Z_{a}(t)>0
.

Below, we discuss how an analysis carried out in terms of timing
residuals could alternatively be done using redshifts, and how
redshifts are affected by the removal of the universal timing terms.

The term “redshift” is used figuratively rather than literally,
because the frequency shifts in question are not of visible
light. Rather, they refer to miniscule changes in the pulsar rotation
frequency, as observed at the solar system barycenter (SSB). The
pulsar’s rotation makes it act as a clock, and by the principle of
equivalence, all clocks are affected by gravity in the same way. So
even though a typical pulsar’s pulsation frequency is twelve orders of
magnitude smaller than for visible light, the fractional frequency
change produced by a gravitational wave is identical. Thus, the
literature uses the word “redshift” for both.

If
\lambda
denotes the spatial distance between successive pulses
arriving at the SSB, and the differences from the mean are
\delta\lambda
, then the redshift is
Z=\delta\lambda/\lambda=-\delta f/f
where
f
is the mean pulsar rotation frequency
Romano and Allen 2024
and

\delta f
is the difference from the mean.

In the same way as for timing residuals, we can expand the
time-dependent redshift (
77
) of pulsar

in a Legendre polynomial basis,
as

(78)

The relationship of the Legendre coefficients
