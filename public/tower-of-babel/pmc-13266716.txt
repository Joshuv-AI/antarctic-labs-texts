Clinical phenotypes of secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP) in different underlying lung diseases: a retrospective study and literature review on the clinical heterogeneity in this single entity
Authors: Hei-Shun Cheng, Charles Wong, Pui-Hing Chiu, Vinson Nelson Yew, Chi-Chung Jeffrey Wong, Chun-Wai Tong, Pui-Ling Flora Miu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical outcome of secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP) was mostly evaluated in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or interstitial lung disease (ILD) patients whereas data were limited in non-COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax. This study aimed to describe the clinical outcome of pneumothorax secondary to different pulmonary conditions.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted on SSP patients in a Hong Kong hospital. Clinical outcome of pneumothorax secondary to different underlying lung diseases including persistent air leak (PAL), 2-year post-discharge pneumothorax recurrence and all-cause mortality were evaluated. Logistic regression and cox regression analysis were applied for covariates adjustment.ResultsOf the 223 SSP patients enrolled, 32 (14.3%) had acute chest infection and 191 (85.7%) had chronic lung diseases. The acute chest infection group had higher risk of PAL >14 days (40.6% vs. 17.8%; P=0.003), longer hospitalization (24 vs. 13 days; P=0.001) and higher all-cause mortality rate (25.0% vs. 12.0%; P=0.049) than chronic disease group. Among those with chronic lung diseases, COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax led to higher PAL risk (>2 days: OR: 2.14, 95% CI: 1.01–4.54, P=0.046; >14 days: OR: 4.02, 95% CI: 1.49–10.88, P=0.006) and prolonged hospitalization (14 vs. 12 days; P=0.02) but similar post-discharge all-cause mortality and recurrence rate at two years compared to non-COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax. Higher proportion of COPD/ILD group was considered unfit for surgical pleurodesis. One-year recurrence rate was lower in asthma (8.3%) and bronchiectasis (0%) patients than other disease groups (15.8–33.3%).ConclusionsOur study demonstrated heterogeneous clinical phenotypes among different lung diseases with poorer outcome in acute chest infection and COPD/ILD population. Differentiation between infective and non-infective, COPD/ILD-related and non-COPD/ILD-related SSP may enable more personalized management.Keywords: Secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP), phenotypes, pneumothorax recurrence, persistent air leak (PAL)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Clinical outcomes were heterogeneous in secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP) due to different lung diseases.

• SSP due to acute chest infection had higher risk of persistent air leak (PAL), prolonged hospitalization and all-cause mortality than those with chronic lung diseases.

• Among those with chronic lung diseases, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)/interstitial lung disease (ILD)-related pneumothorax had higher risk of PAL and longer hospitalization than non-COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax.

What is known and what is new?

• Pneumothorax secondary to different lung diseases are collectively considered as SSP and this implies all underlying lung diseases share similar clinical outcome and treatment response.

• This study demonstrated that the clinical phenotypes were highly variable among SSP due to different lung conditions. Those with acute chest infection, COPD and ILD had poorer outcome.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• A better understanding of clinical phenotypes of SSP due to different lung conditions enables a more precise and individualized management plan. For example, a more definitive treatment approach should be considered early for COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax based on its poorer outcome while conservative approach may be deemed adequate for non-COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax such as asthma or bronchiectasis, in order to avoid unnecessary treatment-related complication. This study serves as an exploratory approach to examine the clinical heterogeneity and its result may deserve further validation by large-scale prospective research.

Introduction

Background

Secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP), a common complication in patients with underlying lung diseases (1), is usually associated with longer air leak, more prolonged hospitalization and higher rate of recurrence compared with primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP) (1-3). Pneumothoraces secondary to different lung conditions are usually considered collectively as SSP (4) which may imply all underlying lung diseases share similar clinical outcomes.

Patients included in previous research on SSP predominantly suffered from chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or interstitial lung disease (ILD) (3,5-10) and their treatment outcome or clinical presentation were more evaluated in details (11-14). Of note, the body of evidence of other pulmonary conditions is limited and mostly supported by studies with small sample size (15,16) or case reports only (17-19). While patients with COPD and ILD have a higher risk of persistent air leak (PAL) and recurrence due to more extensive lung parenchyma involvement and emphysematous change (20-22), it remains questionable whether other non-COPD/ILD lung conditions such as asthma or bronchiectasis share similar phenotypes of pneumothorax. In addition, although acute chest infection is commonly considered as an underlying cause of SSP (23,24), the pathology may be different from those in chronic lung disease population as the underlying lung parenchyma is usually considered normal before infection. Despite this, little data is available for their comparison.

Rationale and knowledge gap

It is increasingly recognized that heterogeneous clinical manifestation and treatment response exists among SSP patients with different lung diseases (25,26), yet a detailed and systematic evaluation in this area is lacking. A better understanding of clinical phenotypes, particularly PAL risk and recurrence rate, is imperative for SSP treatment guidance and precision medicine, which has emerged as an important milestone in other medical diseases. Since SSP patients are usually older with more comorbidities (1), a careful risk stratification is needed. Avoidance of either surgical or chemical pleurodesis in those with low risk of PAL and recurrence may be feasible after careful patient selection, in order to prevent unnecessary operation-related morbidity and mortality (14). Therefore, a thorough assessment on clinical behaviours of different lung diseases is believed to improve the quality of management.

Objective

Our study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcome of SSP patients secondary to different underlying lung diseases in a Hong Kong public hospital. To the best of authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to explore the clinical phenotypes of pneumothorax secondary to various lung conditions. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0443/rc).

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective observational study conducted on SSP patients who were admitted to Pamela Youde Nethersole Eastern Hospital (PYNEH), which serves 0.6 million Hong Kong population. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Central Institutional Review Board of Hong Kong Hospital Authority (No. CIRB-2024-277-4) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Patients with diagnostic coding of spontaneous pneumothorax were abstracted from the Hospital Authority (HA) Clinical Management System (CMS). Inclusion criteria were patients: (I) 18 years of age or older; (II) who suffered from SSP diagnosed on chest X-ray; and (III) who had been treated with chest drains.

Patients were excluded if they met the following criteria: (I) younger than 18 years of age; (II) PSP; (III) trauma-related or iatrogenic pneumothorax; (IV) pneumothorax not treated with chest drain insertion; (V) received mechanical ventilation (invasive or non-invasive) during the index pneumothorax episode; (VI) trapped lung; (VII) uncertain diagnosis of underlying lung disease; (VIII) uncertain duration of air leak; (IX) chest tube accidentally dislodged before pneumothorax resolution and not replaced; (X) wrong diagnostic label of pneumothorax.

Information on patient’s demographics including their underlying lung diseases, comorbidities and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) (27) were collected. Data of the index admission were retrieved including: (I) initial pneumothorax size by Collin’s method (28); (II) history of prior pneumothorax; (III) loculated pneumothorax; (IV) air leak duration; (V) serum albumin level closest to onset of pneumothorax; (VI) hospitalization duration; (VII) all-cause mortality; (VIII) pneumothorax-related mortality and (IX) pleurodesis methods. Data of two-year clinical outcomes including post-discharge mortality and pneumothorax recurrence were also analysed via review of patient’s medical record.

Underlying lung diseases were categorized as follow: (I) acute chest infection were divided into simple (pneumonia alone) or complicated chest infection such as lung abscess, necrotizing pneumonia and parapneumonic effusion including empyema; (II) chronic lung diseases included COPD, ILD, prior tuberculosis (TB), lung cancer, asthma and bronchiectasis. The definition of each lung disease for the purpose of this study was further described in the Appendix 1.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the clinical outcomes of SSP due to different underlying lung diseases. In view of potential high clinical heterogeneity leading to difficulty of data analysis, the clinical behaviours were evaluated in the following sequential orders:

Comparison between acute chest infection group and chronic lung disease group;

Comparison between acute chest infection group and chronic lung disease group;

Comparison between simple and complicated infection in the acute chest infection group;

Comparison between simple and complicated infection in the acute chest infection group;

Comparison among different underlying lung conditions in the chronic lung disease group.

Comparison among different underlying lung conditions in the chronic lung disease group.

The clinical data included: (I) incidence of PAL and all-cause mortality in the index pneumothorax episodes; (II) post-discharge pneumothorax recurrence and all-cause mortality up to two years. The data of PAL in this study was presented as both the number of days (continuous variable) and proportion of patients with air leak longer than a fixed period of two days, seven days and 14 days (categorized variables) based on the clinical relevance and definition supported by previous literatures (3,4,29-31). The reasons of analysing patients with a longer period of air leak were as follow:

PAL >7 days: surgical referral is suggested beyond 5–7 days of air leak (4) and 7–10 days (31). A single cutoff point of >7 days was adopted for the purpose of this study.

PAL >14 days: spontaneous remission of SSP without surgical intervention was observed in 79% of patients in a previous study favouring conservative approach within this period (3).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics was used to summarize the baseline patient demographic characteristics. Comparison of continuous variables was performed using Student’s t-test/Analysis of Variance or Mann-Whitney/Kruskal Wallis test as appropriate. Categorical variables between groups were compared by Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Post-hoc tests including Tukey’s HSD and Bonferroni correction methods were used to reduce type-one error arising from multiple comparisons as appropriate.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to assess the association of underlying lung diseases with PAL and mortality in the index hospitalization adjusting for covariates. Sample size estimation was based on the formula: N (sample size) >50 + 8x (number of independent variables) (32). After initial univariate analysis, risk factors with P value <0.2 and clinically important factors regardless of P value were recruited into the multivariate logistic regression analysis, in which P value <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. Hosmer & Lemeshow test and C-statistics were used to assess the quality of the results.

Cox regression analysis was used to evaluate the association of different lung diseases with post-discharge pneumothorax recurrence and mortality adjusting for covariates. Factors with P value <0.2 in the initial univariate analysis and clinically important factors regardless of P value were recruited into the Cox regression analysis. P value of <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. Missing data was handled with mean imputation. Results were calculated using the computer software Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, Version 22, for Windows).

Results

Of the 1140 patients with pneumothorax screened since 2009, 917 met the exclusion criteria (Table S1) and the medical records of the remaining 223 patients were retrieved for analysis (Figure 1). The evaluation of the last patient’s 2-year post-discharge clinical profile was completed in June, 2024. There were no missing data of patients during initial pneumothorax episodes and so mean imputation was not necessary. Of these 223 patients, 196 (87.9%) were male and the mean age was 68.7±15.6 years. The median air leak duration was 5 days (IQR, 2–12 days) and the mean initial pneumothorax size was 38.6%±25.4%. The median hospitalization duration was 14 days (IQR, 8–25 days). Sixty-five patients (29.1%) had a history of pneumothorax.

Flowchart of patients excluded and those included for analysis. PSP, primary spontaneous pneumothorax.

Among these 223 patients, 191 (85.7%) pneumothoraces were secondary to chronic lung diseases and the remaining 32 cases (14.3%) secondary to acute chest infection (Table 1, Table S2). The most common cause of chest infection was pneumonia (78.1%), and 46.9% were bacteria associated, followed by TB/non-TB mycobacterium (31.2%) (Table S3). Complication of pneumonia developed in 12 cases. The most common chronic underlying lung disease was COPD (51.3%), followed by prior TB (27.7%). There were 66 (34.6%) patients who had more than one lung diseases (Tables S4,S5).

Clinical outcome of index pneumothorax episodes

Comparison between acute chest infection (n=32) and chronic lung diseases (n=191)

The air leak duration tended to be longer in acute chest infection group compared with chronic lung diseases group (median 9.5 vs. 4 days; P=0.07). While the proportion of patients with PAL >2 days (81.3% vs. 80.1%; P=0.88) were similar, those with PAL >7 days (59.4% vs. 37.2%; P=0.02) and >14 days (40.6% vs. 17.8%; P=0.003) were significantly higher in the acute chest infection group (Table 1). The median hospitalization period was more prolonged in those with chest infection (24 vs. 13 days; P=0.001). Despite a lower CCI score (3 vs. 4; P=0.049) in the acute chest infection group, all-cause mortality of the index pneumothorax episode was higher (25.0% vs. 12.0%; P=0.049). Details of mortality was provided in Table S6.

Comparison between simple (n=20) and complicated chest infection (n=12)

Among the infection group, those with complications had longer air leak (median 16 vs. 3 days; P=0.04). The percentage of patients with PAL >2, >7 and >14 days tended to be higher in the complicated group without statistically significant difference. The hospitalization period of complicated infection was longer than that of simple infection (median 45.5 vs. 18.5 days; P=0.02) (Table S7) with similar all-cause mortality. Due to small subgroup size, multivariate logistic regression was not performed.

Comparison among different chronic lung diseases (n=191)

Among patients with chronic lung conditions, 125 had single lung disease. Asthma patients were significantly younger and a majority of them were non-smokers (75.0%), while COPD patients were predominantly male (98.2%), more likely to be active or ex-smokers (98.2%) with higher bulla prevalence (29.8%). The CCI score was highest in the lung cancer group (9; IQR, 7–10) in which there was a higher proportion of patients (50.0%) who had more than one non-respiratory comorbidity (Table S4).

Although the air leak durations were not statistically different among these groups, it was numerically longer in ILD and COPD patients. The proportion of patients with PAL >2 days was significantly higher in ILD group (100%; P=0.01) and lower in asthma group (50%; P=0.01). There was no statistically significant difference in the proportion of PAL >7 and >14 days but none of the patients with asthma or bronchiectasis had PAL >14 days (Table 2, Table S8).

Due to small population size in certain disease groups, our patients’ pneumothoraces were categorized as an exploratory approach into COPD/ILD related (patients with either COPD or ILD) and non-COPD/ILD related (patients with diseases other than COPD or ILD) based on their underlying respiratory diseases for further regression analysis. Those with COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax were more likely to be loculated (29.8% vs. 12.9%; P=0.008) and have bulla on the same side of pneumothorax (35.5% vs. 8.6%; P<0.001). The air leak duration in this group was significantly longer (mean 11.35±15.9 vs. 5.5±5.2 days; P<0.001; median 5 vs. 4 days; P=0.04). While the proportion of patients with PAL >2 and >7 days were similar between two groups, a higher percentage of COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax group had PAL >14 days (23.1% vs. 8.6%; P=0.01) with longer hospitalization period (14 vs. 12 days; P=0.02) (Table 3, Table S9). After taking into account of covariates in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax was associated with higher risk of PAL >2 days (OR: 2.14; 95% CI: 1.01–4.54; P=0.046) and PAL >14 days (OR: 4.02; 95% CI: 1.49–10.88; P=0.006). However, it did not heighten risk of all-cause mortality in the index episode (OR: 1.02; 95% CI: 0.34–3.13; P=0.97) (Table 4, Tables S9-S17, Figure S1-S4). Initial pneumothorax size (PAL >2 days: OR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.04, P=0.04; PAL >14 days: OR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.03, P=0.04) and those with more than one lung disease (PAL >2 days: OR: 2.83; 95% CI: 1.15–6.96; P=0.02) were also linked to increased risk of PAL in the regression analysis, whereas albumin level was negatively associated with all-cause mortality (OR: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.76–0.92; P<0.001).

Compared with non-COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax, chemical pleurodesis was more commonly given (56.2% vs. 27.1%; P<0.001) in patients with COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax and surgical pleurodesis was seldom offered (0.8% vs. 8.6%; P=0.01). There were also a higher percentage of them who were considered unfit for surgery (32.2% vs. 11.4%; P<0.001).

Clinical outcome of post-discharge SSP patients

Of the 223 SSP patients, 31 (13.9%) died in the index pneumothorax episodes. The most common cause of death was pneumonia (74.2%) followed by malignancy (19.4%). There were no pneumothorax-related deaths. Among the remaining 192 survived patients, 168 (87.5%) had underlying chronic lung diseases.

Comparison between acute chest infection (n=24) and chronic lung diseases (n=168)

There was a tendency of lower pneumothorax recurrence in patients with acute chest infection at three months (8.3% vs. 18.5%; P=0.38), one year (8.3% vs. 23.2%; P=0.12) and two years (12.5% vs. 28.0%; P=0.14) but these did not reach statistically significant difference (Table S2). The all-cause mortality at three months (0% vs. 7.7%; P=0.38), one year (12.5% vs. 23.8%; P=0.30) and two years (20.8% vs. 38.1%; P=0.10) also did not differ from those with chronic lung diseases.

Comparison between simple (n=16) and complicated chest infection (n=8)

There was no difference in the pneumothorax recurrence and all-cause mortality at three months, one year and two years post-discharge. Notably, there were no recurrent cases in the complicated chest infection group up to two years. Cox regression analysis was not performed due to small subgroup size.

Comparison among different chronic lung diseases (n=168)

Although statistically significant difference was not reached, a lower tendency of one-year pneumothorax recurrence was observed in asthma (8.3%) and bronchiectasis group (0%) compared with other lung diseases (COPD: 24.1%; ILD: 33.3%; Prior TB: 22.2%; lung cancer: 15.8%) (Table S8). After adjusting for covariates in cox regression analysis, COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax was not found to increase risk of post-discharge pneumothorax recurrence nor all-cause mortality at three months, one year and two years (Figure 2 and Tables S18-S29).

Discussion

It has been a common practice to identify SSP in patients who have underlying lung diseases or those who are older than 50 years of age with smoking history (4). The main consideration of differentiating between SSP and PSP is that the clinical behaviours and treatment responses are different (4,26). Although pneumothoraces due to different underlying lung diseases are collectively categorized into SSP, patients evaluated in previous research commonly suffered from COPD or ILD while those with other lung diseases were seldom studied individually. This brings into question whether pneumothorax secondary to other lung diseases share the similar phenotypes with COPD or ILD-related pneumothorax. Our study attempted to further clarify the effect of different underlying pulmonary diseases on pneumothorax outcome and we found that the clinical manifestations were heterogeneous. This points out the problem of oversimplification of the term “SSP” and highlights the need of a more refined classification to enable a precise treatment approach.

Key findings and literature review

Pneumothorax secondary to acute chest infection

Acute chest infection-related pneumothorax, commonly considered as SSP in previous literature (23,24), was associated with significantly higher risk of PAL, more prolonged hospitalization and higher all-cause mortality compared with chronic lung conditions. One study revealed co-existing pneumothorax and pneumonia as a poor prognostic factor (8). The poorer outcome observed in our study was driven mainly by complicated infection as the degree of parenchymal damage was considered to be more severe. There are case reports linking necrotizing pneumonia to development of pneumothorax (33-35), which proposed that necrosis of the lung parenchyma due to infection led to the formation of bronchopleural fistula resulting in pneumothorax (34). Active inflammation of the bronchioles or alveoli may impair the healing process of the defect resulting in PAL and higher risk of pleural infection (36). The mechanism of air leak in this group seems to be different from that in chronic pulmonary conditions although it could not be further evaluated in this study due to the retrospective nature. Therefore, it remains controversial whether infection-related pneumothorax should be classified as SSP indistinguishably with chronic lung diseases. Our cohort demonstrated that this group of patients had a higher rate of all-cause mortality despite a lower CCI score compared with chronic lung diseases group, possibly because of co-existing active infection and pneumothorax. In view of this, early recognition and intervention such as surgery should be considered in this population in addition to antibiotics and pleural drainage. One case report demonstrated success of surgical debridement of the necrotic tissue and sealing of fistula in a patient with pneumothorax complicating necrotizing pneumonia (34). The authors highlighted the importance of optimal timing of surgery and the need of balancing between uncontrolled ongoing infection and surgical risk, which was also echoed by another case series (37). Currently, there is no clear consensus addressing this issue.

After the index pneumothorax episode, a tendency of a lower pneumothorax recurrence rate was observed in this group compared with chronic lung diseases. Of note, none of the discharged patients with complicated chest infection suffered from a recurrence in two years. Although there were case reports and studies on chest infection-related pneumothorax (38-40), little focus was put on recurrence risk upon discharge. One study on pneumothorax in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-infected patients with either pneumocystis jirovecii or pulmonary TB showed that 4 out of 35 pneumothorax (11.4%) suffered from recurrence with a median time of five months after the first episode (41). This figure was similar to our data (8.3% at three months). Despite scarce literature evidence on the causal relationship, our result may suggest that once there is complete lung parenchymal recovery after resolution of active inflammation, the recurrence risk will be mitigated.

Pneumothorax secondary to chronic lung diseases

Our study revealed a considerably large variety of baseline characteristics and clinical outcome among different chronic lung conditions. Asthma patients were younger and predominantly non-smokers with low CCI score. Patients with COPD were more likely to be active or ex-smokers with higher prevalence of bulla, while lung cancer patients had the highest CCI score among all. Of note, we also demonstrated that compared to other lung conditions, COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax was linked to greater risk of PAL and prolonged hospitalization with similar all-cause mortality rate. The pneumothorax recurrence rate was also numerically higher despite its statistically insignificant difference. On the contrary, non-COPD/ILD pulmonary conditions were found to have lower risk of PAL. The diseases included in this group were asthma, bronchiectasis, prior TB and lung cancer.

It has been demonstrated in the past that COPD and ILD were associated with higher risk of pneumothorax and PAL (13,14,23,42,43). Both diseases are characterized by diffuse lung parenchyma damage (21,44). Emphysema and bullae are more common in COPD (45) whereas ILD is featured by fibrotic changes leading to alveolar epithelial and interstitial damage (46). This on-going chronic inflammation might possibly render the parenchyma more prone to air leak. While ruptured bulla is a possible explanation, whether increased pleural porosity could explain the air leak phenomenon as suggested in PSP requires further research (47). In our study, the 1-year pneumothorax recurrence rates were 24.1% in COPD and 33.3% in ILD, which are similar to those reported in an epidemiological study conducted in England (SSP in male: 27.4%; SSP in female: 24.9%) (1).

The one-year recurrence rates in other lung diseases were much lower, ranging from zero to 22.2% in our cohort. This was even lower than that observed in PSP (29.0%) as shown by a meta-analysis conducted in 2018 (48). Our proposed explanation is that these diseases usually lead to focal or minimal lung parenchymal damage only, as compared to COPD or ILD. The pathology of asthma and bronchiectasis predominantly involve airways although lung parenchyma damage can be present in severe diseases (49-51). Their relationship with pneumothorax was seldom studied. One Australian research on spontaneous pneumothorax suggested that asthma patients with SSP had similar pleural drainage time as PSP and non-asthma SSP was associated with prolonged drainage (OR: 4.1) (52). Regarding TB, one Spanish study did not find strong association between fibrotic TB and pneumothorax recurrence even in the presence of destroyed lungs (15). The authors suggested a more conservative approach might be appropriate for TB-associated SSP. The evidence on lung cancer-related SSP was much more limited. Consensus on mechanism of SSP in this group of patients has not been reached. Some case reports suggested direct invasion of the tumour into the pleural tissue causing air leak (53) while others proposed rupture of dilated alveolar space secondary to tumour obstruction in distal bronchioles (16,17).

Patients with multiple lung diseases are not uncommon in the real-world situation. The complex and mixed pathologies of various chronic lung diseases might have further complicated the result analysis of this study although a baseline analysis was performed separately for those with single and multiple lung diseases and that those with more than one lung disease were included as a covariate to be adjusted in the regression analysis. Nevertheless, the influence of multiple pulmonary conditions could still be a concern and further prospective studies are needed for evaluation.

Among those with chronic lung diseases, hypoalbuminemia was strongly associated with all-cause mortality. There have been studies suggesting this relationship in acute medical illness (54-56) and specifically lung diseases (57-59), but the linkage with pneumothorax was not clear. Albumin is usually considered as a negative acute phase protein (57,60) and hypoalbuminemia arises secondary to an interplay between inflammation, increased vascular permeability and hypercatabolism during acute illness while it is less associated with malnutrition (57,61). This study first showed that the albumin level at the onset of pneumothorax may serve a role in predicting prognosis, particularly mortality.

Implication: the need of SSP categorization

With such heterogeneity of the clinical outcome, it is difficult to adopt a single treatment approach to tackle the multifaceted problem as seen in SSP. For example, optimal management of a young non-smoking asthma patient with pneumothorax may be different from that of an old COPD heavy smoker. The consideration of treatment should include the PAL and recurrence risk of the underlying pulmonary diseases. A collective term of SSP may need to be revised as a more precise management approach is needed. The following suggestions may be one of the potential approaches to further classify SSP. As a result from this single centre retrospective study, it remains as a hypothesis with need of future validation rather than a practice-changing conclusion.

Acute chest infection related versus chronic lung disease related

Compared with those having chronic lung diseases, pneumothorax secondary to acute chest infection had a poorer outcome in the index pneumothorax episodes but a lower tendency to recur after discharge. The lung parenchyma is usually considered normal in this group and therefore post-discharge outcome should be similar to those with normal respiratory function if full recovery is achieved. As a result, it may be reasonable to separate this population from the SSP due to chronic lung diseases.

Chronic lung disease: COPD/ILD related versus non-COPD/ILD related

The reason of categorizing SSP into COPD/ILD-related or non-COPD/ILD-related in our study was based on the observation that these two diseases were the main representatives of SSP in previous research and both were characterized by diffuse parenchymal damage (21,44), in contrast to other pulmonary conditions which usually affect lung parenchyma focally and minimally. This is one of our purposes to evaluate whether non-COPD/ILD lung diseases share similar characteristics. In addition, COPD and ILD shared similarities in baseline characteristics such as numerically higher proportion of pneumothorax history, presence of bulla, loculated pneumothorax, and higher likelihood of receiving chemical or blood patch pleurodesis. Despite a significant difference demonstrated between the two groups, we acknowledge that the non-COPD/ILD group consisted of a limited variety but high clinical heterogeneity of diseases and this grouping method is an exploratory approach. The diseases in this group may not be pathologically related. There were also other pulmonary conditions that could not be examined, such as lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM) and Birg Hogg Dube (BHD) syndrome, which are rare yet known to be associated with pneumothorax. Further studies are warranted to determine if this phenotype categorization is clinically meaningful.

Nevertheless, some of the observations in this study deserves future validation. First, since COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax entails poorer clinical outcome, a more definitive treatment approach should be considered. There are studies evaluating surgical outcome in these two groups. Although patients with COPD seem to have a better surgical outcome than ILD patients (6,25,62), significant emphysema and pulmonary fibrosis were both found to be predictors of recurrence (14,63). Large-scale prospective studies are required to provide insights on careful patient selection for surgical pleurodesis.

Second, our current understanding on SSP clinical manifestation is mainly based on studies of COPD/ILD patients which may not represent other lung diseases. The outcome of non-COPD/ILD-related pneumothorax especially those secondary to asthma and bronchiectasis seemed to be better with lower PAL risk and recurrence. A more conservative approach may worth further attention by large-scale research. A clinical spectrum between PSP and SSP patients (64,65) is widely accepted and some PSP patients who are older than 50 years old with smoking history should be managed as SSP based on their resemblance in treatment response. Similarly, it may be possible for those SSP patients with mild parenchymal damage (e.g. asthma) to be managed as PSP. Pneumothorax classification according to presence of underlying lung diseases alone may need to be revised. The severity and extent of lung parenchymal damage (e.g., emphysema, fibrotic change or presence of bulla) or even radiographic phenotyping (26) could be an alternative direction of further evaluation on treatment response. A recently published study also highlighted the limitation of the conventional classification on surgical treatment guidance (66).

Strength and limitation

Our study has several strengths. First, this is one of the few studies that clearly defined underlying pulmonary diseases to investigate their clinical outcome. Localized diseases were all on the same side of the pneumothorax. Second, only those with chest drain insertion were included to allow accurate air leak assessment.

There are also several limitations in this study. First, this is a single-centre retrospective study that would affect the generalization of the result. Second, there was a large difference of population size in each disease group resulting in difficulty of putting them individually into regression analysis. This may reflect the difference in pneumothorax risk among various underlying lung diseases. Third, not all underlying lung diseases could be adequately evaluated to enable a better categorization of SSP. Some rare diseases such as LAM and BHD syndrome were not included. Besides, there could be significant variation of lung pathology within one single lung disease. For example, fibrotic or non-fibrotic ILD may implicate different clinical behaviours of SSP such as recurrence and mortality but the sample size of ILD in our study was too small for further analysis (Table S30). Fifth, those without chest drain treatment were excluded in order to allow a more accurate air leak measurement. Therefore, the clinical behaviours of SSP with no chest drains were not assessed in our study. Last but not least, the result was not applicable to severe pneumothorax requiring mechanical ventilation which was excluded.

Conclusions

In our study, pneumothorax secondary to acute chest infection, particularly those with complications, was associated with higher risk of PAL, prolonged hospitalization and all-cause mortality compared with chronic diseases, but tended to have a lower recurrence rate. Patients with COPD or ILD had significantly poorer clinical outcome in the index pneumothorax episodes and their recurrence rates tended to be higher than those with non-COPD/ILD pulmonary diseases. Further research on the SSP clinical heterogeneity is required to address its pathophysiological complexity of various lung conditions, so as to enable more precise management approach.
