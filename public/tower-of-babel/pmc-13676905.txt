Proteomic markers associated with blood pressure, hypertension, and treatment-resistant hypertension
Authors: Anna Dieden, Olle Melander, Manan Pareek, Lennart Råstam, Ulf Lindblad, Petri Gudmundsson, Bledar Daka, Margrét Leósdóttir, Peter M. Nilsson, Michael H. Olsen, Amra Jujic, Martin Magnusson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to investigate associations between 92 proteins and blood pressure (BP), prevalent hypertension (HT) and apparent treatment-resistant HT (aTRH) in a population-derived sub cohort.MethodsExcluding subjects with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) (n = 189), linear regression models assessed associations between proteins and systolic BP. Proteins showing significant associations (false discovery rate 5%) with systolic BP were analysed adjusting for clinically relevant covariates. Associated proteins were further analysed for associations with diastolic BP, prevalent HT, and aTRH, in multivariable regression models.ResultsComplete data was available in 1527 individuals (mean age 67.1 ± 6.0 years; 30.5% women). Interleukin-1 receptor type 2 (IL1R2) associated with systolic BP (β 2.79, p 0.008), diastolic BP (β 1.78, p < 0.001), HT (odds ratio (OR), 1.34 [95% CI, 1.01–1.78]) and aTRH (OR, 1.94 [1.11–3.39]) while chitinase-3-like-protein 1 (CHI3L1) associated with systolic BP (β 1.53, p 0.004) and prevalent HT (OR 1.20, [1.04–1.38]). Pathway analysis suggested “metabolism of angiotensinogen to angiotensins”, “neutrophil degranulation” and “peptide hormone metabolism” as significantly enriched, in relation to blood pressure.ConclusionsAmong 1527 individuals without established ASCVD, IL1R2 was associated with systolic and diastolic blood pressure, as well as with both HT and aTRH, while CHI3L1 associated with systolic blood pressure and prevalent HT. These findings may offer novel insights and inspire future research on HT and inflammation.

Introduction

Hypertension (HT) is a major global health concern and the leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1]. Globally, approximately 1.3 billion people have HT [2], yet only 32.5% treated for HT have controlled blood pressure (BP) [3]. Resistant HT (RH) is defined as uncontrolled BP despite ≥3 antihypertensive drug classes or controlled BP on 4 classes [4]. Apparent treatment RH (aTRH) is defined when out-of-office BP, medication dose or adherence data are missing. Individuals with aTRH have a higher risk of coronary heart disease and mortality than those with HT alone and are important to identify [5]. The American Heart Association has emphasized the need for better personalized treatment strategies in RH [4], underscoring the importance of improved understanding of the pathophysiological pathways underlying elevated BP, HT and aTRH. Although proteomics has been highlighted as a promising tool for advancing insight into BP regulation and HT [6], [7], there is a shortage of large-scale plasma proteomic studies in humans.

A prior African study used proteomics to examine vascular health phenotypes, including HT, identifying several biomarkers associated with prevalent HT, carotid intima media thickness (cIMT), and pulse wave velocity, many of them novel [8]. Earlier efforts have studied plasma proteomics in hypertensive and normotensive individuals [9], [10], as well as RH and controlled HT [11]. The studies, however, were constrained by a low number of participants. Lin et al. explored plasma proteomics and longitudinal changes in BP and found one protein significantly associated with BP progression in the discovery cohort, but not the validation cohort [12]. Although a limited number of large proteomic studies have explored BP progression, even fewer have specifically focused on HT. In two large German cohorts (KORA), proteomics identified 7 proteins associated with HT and 23 with systolic BP (SBP), with NT-proBNP, kidney injury molecure 1, and osteoprotegerin robustly linked to both [13].

To the best of our knowledge, no large population-based study has comprehensively examined proteomics and BP, HT and aTRH. Moreover, evidence suggests a close link between HT and inflammation [14] and the Olink CVD III panel consists of proteins with known or suspected links to cardiovascular disease and/or inflammation. Exploring associations between these proteins and BP phenotypes may improve our understanding of the biological pathways involved in HT and identify novel associations with disease burden and treatment resistance. Motivated by these knowledge gaps, we explored proteomics and BP regulation and HT phenotypes in a population-derived sub cohort. Our goal was not to propose diagnostic tools but to generate hypotheses on underlying pathophysiological mechanisms to guide future mechanistic or interventional studies.

Methods

The Malmö Preventive Project (MPP) is a population-based cohort, initiated in the 1970s to investigate CV risk factors, enrolling 33,346 Malmö residents (attendance rate 71%), born 1921–1949. A re-examination in 2002–2006 (MPP-RES) included 18,240 participants (attendance rate 72%), from which 1792 were randomly selected for echocardiography (MPP-RES-Echo), with oversampling of individuals with impaired or diabetic glucose metabolism to achieve balanced glucose status groups, as described previously [15]. This approach resulted in approximately 1/3 normoglycemic subjects, 1/3 with IFG, and 1/3 with diabetes. Proteomic data were available for 1737 individuals. For the present study, subjects with ASCVD were excluded (n = 189). Complete data on all variables was available in 1527 subjects (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flow-chart outlining inclusion in the study. Clinical assessment

Height and weight were measured with participants in light clothing and body mass index (BMI) was calculated (kg/m2). Following a 10-minute rest in a supine position, BP and heart rate (beats/min) were measured twice, using a sphygmomanometer with an appropriate cuff width and a mercury manometer [16]. The recorded values were then averaged.

Laboratory assays

Blood samples were collected after overnight fasting and centrifuged and stored at −80 °C. The Olink Proteomics Multiplex CVD III panel was employed to examine the biomarker profile of participants. Briefly, each of the 92 proteins is recognised by double oligonucleotide labelled antibodies, leading to quantification and detection using quantitative PCR. The final readout is expressed as normalised protein expression values on a log2 scale. NT-proBNP was measured with an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Elecsys; Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) at the Department of Clinical Chemistry, Akershus University Hospital, Lorenskog, Norway. Total cholesterol and Cystatin C plasma levels were assessed through standardised procedures at the Department of Clinical Chemistry, Skane University Hospital in Malmö.

Definitions

Hypertension was defined as systolic BP ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥90 mmHg, and/or use of antihypertensive treatment. Antihypertensive drug classes were defined as beta-blockers, RAAS inhibitors, calcium channel blockers, and diuretics (including loop diuretics, thiazide diuretics, and potassium-sparing agents). These variables were constructed from prescription codes recorded at baseline. Apparent TRH was defined as use of ≥4 antihypertensive drug classes, regardless of BP levels, or use of 3 classes with systolic BP ≥140 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥90 mmHg. Individuals on 1–2 drug classes, or on 3 classes with controlled BP (<140/90 mmHg), were considered non-resistant hypertensives [4]. Smoking habits were self-reported and were defined as current smoker (yes/no). Diabetes was defined by self-report, a diagnosis of diabetes in any of the local or national registries prior to study entry, diabetes medication use, or two distinct fasting plasma glucose measurements of ≥7.0 mmol/L. Statin treatment was self-reported at study entry. Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) was defined as having a history of prevalent coronary or stroke event and was register derived.

Statistical analysis

Variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians (first and third quartile) as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using student's t-test or, for non-normally distributed variables, Mann Whitney U test. Binary variables were evaluated using χ2 tests. Due to skewness, cystatin C was ln-transformed prior to analyses. Univariate linear regression models were carried out exploring associations between each of the 92 proteins and systolic BP with adjusted p-values calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, employing a 5% false discovery rate (FDR) [17]. FDR-adjusted significant associations between proteins and systolic BP were further analysed adjusted for age and sex (model 1) and thereafter adjusted for BMI, smoking, diabetes, heart rate, cystatin C, total cholesterol, statin treatment and antihypertensive treatment (model 2a) based on prior knowledge of their established associations with BP regulation and CV risk, with a nominal p-value <0.05 considered significant. Significantly associated proteins were thereafter explored for associations with diastolic BP, prevalent HT and aTRH in separate univariate linear (diastolic BP) or logistic (HT and aTRH) regression analyses, where the control group for aTRH were non-resistant HT. Significant associations were subsequently taken forward for analysis in model 1, thereafter model 2a for diastolic BP or model 2b (model 2a with the exclusion of antihypertensive treatment) for HT and aTRH. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Using the Spearman's rank correlation, the proteins significantly associated with systolic BP in fully adjusted models were analysed regarding their correlation to one another. A significant rho >0.6 was considered strong and correlations were considered significant at a two-sided p-value <0.01. A flowchart illustrating the main statistical analyses can be found in Fig. S1. Proteins significantly associated with SBP in fully adjusted models, were further explored in a pathway enrichment analysis, using Reactome over-representation analysis implemented in the ReactomePA package (R/Bioconductor). Enrichment was assessed against the full Reactome universe using a hypergeometric test. Statistical analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 (SPSS, Chicago, IL) and R (version 4.4.0).

When further exploring the six proteins' associations with diastolic BP IL1R2, TR-AP, CTSD and SELE significantly associated in unadjusted analysis (Table S4), as well as after adjusting for age and sex (Table S5). In fully adjusted models IL1R2, TR-AP and SELE remained significantly associated (Table 2).

Four proteins (SELE, CHI3L1, IL1R2, CTSD) significantly associated with HT in unadjusted (Table S6) and age- and sex adjusted analyses (Table S7), with two proteins (IL1R2, CHI3L1) associating in the fully adjusted model (Table 2). In unadjusted analysis, as well as after adjusting for age and sex, IL1R2 and CTSD were associated with aTRH (Tables S8 and S9). In the fully adjusted models, IL1R2 remained significantly associated with aTRH (Table 2). Further, we evaluated the discriminatory performance of IL1R2 for prevalent HT using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.533 (95% CI: 0.499–0.567; p = 0.056), indicating only marginal discrimination.

When further exploring the six proteins' correlation to one another, IL1R2 and APN revealed a strong correlation (rho 0.71, p < 0.001), while the other proteins all correlated with a rho <0.6 (Table S10).

Compared with individuals with non-resistant HT, those with aTRH had significantly higher BMI (p = 0.005), higher systolic BP (p < 0.001) and lower total cholesterol (p < 0.001). They were also more likely to have prevalent diabetes (p = 0.010) and to receive statin therapy (p = 0.003) (Supplementary Table S11).

The pathway enrichment analysis showed three significantly (FDR < 0.05) enriched pathways for the six proteins significantly associated with SBP, namely “metabolism of angiotensinogen to angiotensins”, “neutrophil degranulation” and “peptide hormone metabolism”.

Discussion

In this population-derived sub cohort study including 1527 older adults without established ASCVD, we found IL1R2 to be positively associated with higher systolic and diastolic BP, prevalent HT and aTRH, indicating relevance across a spectrum of hypertensive phenotypes. CHI3L1 was similarly associated with systolic BP and prevalent HT. Three pathways were significantly enriched for SBP: “metabolism of angiotensinogen to angiotensins”, “neutrophil degranulation” and “peptide hormone metabolism”. Several associations appear to be novel.

IL1R2 exists in both membrane-bound and soluble form. As IL1R2 was measured in plasma after removal of cellular components, the detected signal likely predominantly reflects the circulating soluble form. IL1R2 is a decoy receptor that inhibits and regulates IL-1β-mediated inflammation, and its soluble form is generated by alternative splicing or cleavage triggered by pro-inflammatory markers [18]. IL-1-mediated inflammatory activity in relation to HT has been suggested in murine models where IL1R1 deficiency limits BP increase in renin-angiotensin- system-dependent HT [19], and IL1R1 blockade reduced BP in salt-induced HT. [20] IL1R2 gene expression has been associated with HT. [21] A recent Mendelian randomization study investigated whether certain gut microbes causally influence the risk of developing HT and identified IL1R2 as a mediator between the two [22].

Growing evidence linking IL-pathways to vascular inflammation and atherothrombosis led to the extensive randomized controlled trial CANTOS, which showed that the IL-1 signalling contributes to adverse outcomes after myocardial infarction and persistent inflammatory response [23]. A sub-study showed no association between IL1β -inhibition and BP reduction, suggesting its CV benefits were BP-independent. However, patients in the highest BP quartile treated with IL1β -inhibition showed a trend toward fewer major CV events versus placebo [24]. In addition, IL-1 inhibition lowered BP in obese individuals [25], indicating a need for future studies to investigate the context-dependent effects of IL-1 inhibition.

Levels of IL1R2 associated with all outcomes in our study, suggesting relevance of the IL-pathway across the BP continuum. From an epidemiological perspective, consistency across outcomes strengthens the robustness and potential clinical relevance. Given that IL1R2 is a non-signalling decoy receptor, its associations with increasing BP and HT phenotypes likely reflect disease severity rather than causal involvement. In a recent large-scale proteome-wide Mendelian Randomization study, they sought causal associations between proteins and SBP and CVD [26]. IL1R2 was not associated with BP in Mendelian Randomization, lending support to the hypothesis that IL1R2 is unlikely to be a causal driver of BP. The lack of evidence of a causal role for IL1R2 in BP regulation does no exclude potential biological or clinical relevance. Non-causal biomarkers reflect disease activity or severity. IL1R2 may represent a marker of HT associated inflammatory activity rather than a direct mediator of BP regulation.

The strong correlation between IL1R2 and APN makes it difficult to determine which protein primarily drives the association with systolic BP. APN is a transmembrane protein peptidase and has been suggested to play a role in regulating BP [27] and a case-control study revealed associations between increased levels of soluble APN (sAPN) and essential HT. [28] It is noteworthy that sAPN, serves as an inflammatory indicator. In mice, sAPN was thought to upregulate the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β [29]. As both sAPN and sIL1R2 seem to influence IL-1β in different ways and are positively associated with systolic BP, this strengthens the theory of the role of an activated IL-1 pathway in BP regulation. More generally, the soluble forms of APN and IL1R2 act as inflammatory markers and may indicate an inflammatory state with higher systolic BP.

CHI3L1 is a glycoprotein produced and secreted into the blood by various inflammatory-related cells, and is believed to play a role in atherosclerosis and endothelial dysfunction [30]. In a Chinese nested case-control study, higher plasma CHI3L1 levels were significantly associated with HT incidence among healthy men [31] and higher levels of CHI3L1 were positively associated with incident HT among prehypertensive subjects [32]. Further, variants of the CHI3L1 gene were associated with incident HT. [33] Chen et al. found higher plasma levels of CHI3L1 in patients with idiopathic pulmonary HT (iPAH), compared to controls, and this was associated with clinical worsening [34]. Additionally, CHI3L1 demonstrated a significant association with endothelial dysfunction and HT in patients with obstructive sleep apnoea. Notably, this association was not observed in patients without obstructive sleep apnoea [35]. Exploring the 92 proteins association with vascular health in a South African population, CHI3L1 was significantly associated with cIMT (a marker of early alterations of vascular morphology) [8]. However, CHI3L1 was not associated with HT, possibly due to its younger cohort (mean age 45 years) and a high representation of Black Africans (45%). Whether the CHI3L1 associations with systolic BP and HT observed here relates to its roles in endothelial dysfunction or atherosclerosis calls for further investigation.

The proteins significantly associated with SBP revealed three significantly enriched pathways. “Metabolism of angiotensinogen to angiotensins” is central in the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone-System which is a key regulator of BP. Dysregulation of this pathway may lead to HT. “Metabolism of angiotensinogen to angiotensins” is a more specific pathway under the broader category “peptide hormone metabolism”. “Neutrophil degranulation” is consistent with findings of inflammation influencing BP [36].

Despite several strengths of our study such as a large sample size, the use of high throughput proteomics and the employment of rigorous statistical methods, one should be wary of the limitations that exist. The fundamental limitation of our study lies in its cross-sectional design, preventing any conclusions regarding causality. In addition, reverse causation cannot be excluded, as HT may have been present for many years before protein measurements were obtained. Chronic HT can induce vascular, renal, inflammatory and tissue remodelling changes that may influence circulating protein levels. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether circulating IL1R2 predicts incident HT. Our study sample is a sub-cohort stratified according to glucometabolic disturbances. While attempts were made to mitigate this by adjusting for diabetes, there was an issue of multicollinearity between diabetes and fasting plasma glucose in the regression analyses. Consequently, fasting plasma glucose could not be employed as a covariate. This may present a potential bias and associations found between proteins and BP could partly be driven or confounded by plasma glucose levels. One should therefore be cautious when interpreting these results on a general, unstratified population or a population with normoglycemic levels. Moreover, our population consists of mainly Swedish, white, older adults with established ASCVD, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. The observed associations may not be generalizable to younger populations, other ethnic groups, in individuals with severe kidney disease or with established CVD. Replication in cohorts with different demographic and clinical characteristics is therefore needed. As ambulatory BP was missing, office BP was used and thus, white-coat HT could not be detected. Even though up to 50% of subjects with white-coat RH seem to develop true RH after a 15-month period [37], it is preferable to measure true RH. Further, the influence of medication nonadherence, optimal medication dosage, inappropriate drug combinations and secondary causes of HT could not be evaluated. Consequently, some degree of misclassification cannot be excluded, and the observed association between IL1R2 and aTRH should not be interpreted as specific to biologically resistant HT. This was however addressed by not using the term “true” RH, but rather aTRH. Although IL1R2 and CHI3L1 were associated with HT and aTRH, the clinical value of these proteins as diagnostic tools is limited, particularly given the simplicity and reliability of direct BP measurement. Our analyses (including ROC curve evaluation) do not support their use as standalone biomarkers for HT detection. Rather, these proteins may reflect underlying inflammatory or endothelial mechanisms relevant to the pathogenesis of aTRH. Their potential role in sub phenotyping, risk stratification, or mechanistic research warrants further investigation, ideally in independent, unselected and more diverse cohorts before their potential clinical utility can be established.

Conclusions

Among 1527 older adults without established CVD, we found circulating IL1R2 to be significantly associated with higher BP, prevalent HT and aTRH. In addition, CHI3L1 was positively associated with higher systolic BP and prevalent HT. Our study provides valuable insights into the associations between inflammatory biomarkers and HT, shedding light on potential novel biomarkers within the area. Further studies are needed to elucidate the mechanisms behind the present findings as well as the potential clinical implications.
