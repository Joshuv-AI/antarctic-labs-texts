Diagnostic methods of rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease: a systematic review
Authors: Anna Lewandowska-Polak, Olga Brzezińska, Piotr Krawiec, Urszula Skalska, Magdalena Krajewska–Włodarczyk, Marcin Stajszczyk, Brygida Kwiatkowska, Zbigniew Żuber, Otylia Kowal-Bielecka, Bogdan Batko, Joanna S Makowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRheumatoid arthritis–associated interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD) is a potentially life-threatening extra-articular complication affecting 10–60% of patients with rheumatoid arthritis. Despite its clinical significance, the optimal diagnostic strategy remains uncertain. This systematic review assessed the diagnostic value of currently available methods for detecting RA-ILD. The review followed PRISMA guidelines. Databases were searched for studies evaluating imaging modalities (chest X-ray, high-resolution computed tomography [HRCT], lung ultrasound), pulmonary function tests (forced vital capacity [FVC], total lung capacity [TLC], diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide [DLCO]), clinical features, and serum biomarkers. The risk of bias was assessed using QUADAS-2 tool.Thirty-six studies were included. Risk of bias was low in 20, high in 1, and unclear in 15 studies. Applicability concerns were low in 18, unclear in 17 and high in 1 study. Lung ultrasound was most frequently evaluated, showing sensitivity of 70–100% and specificity of 56–97%. Only one HRCT study was identified; it demonstrated high specificity but moderate sensitivity for detecting a UIP pattern. No studies assessing lung volumes (FVC, TLC) were found. DLCO showed sensitivity for RA-ILD detection but limited specificity and uncertain optimal cut-off values. Among biomarkers, KL-6 was most studied (sensitivity 61–100%, specificity 78–91%), while other biomarkers demonstrated inconsistent results and lacked validation. Lung ultrasound appears diagnostically useful in RA-ILD. Evidence supporting pulmonary function tests is limited. Although KL-6 shows promise, biomarkers cannot currently serve as standalone diagnostic tools. Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is one of the most serious and potentially life-threatening manifestations of rheumatoid arthritis (RA). The global pooled prevalence of RA-associated ILD (RA-ILD) is estimated at 21.4%, with the highest rates observed in Africa and the lowest in Europe [1]. However, data from real-world cohorts indicate a much lower prevalence at diagnosis of RA, ranging from 0.09% in the Netherlands to 1.42% in Mexico [2]. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in case definitions, detection intensity, and disease duration. Notably, subclinical interstitial lung abnormalities may be present in up to 60% of patients with RA [3]. ILD can develop on any stage of the disease. In one longitudinal study, 49% of patients received the diagnosis of ILD either before or within 5 years of the RA diagnosis [4] with a mean RA duration of 12.4 years at the time ILD diagnosis. Interestingly, usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern tended to occur earlier after RA onset compared with nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), suggesting distinct pathogenic trajectories [4]. Data from a large Canadian cohort demonstrate that both the incidence and prevalence of RA-ILD increased significantly between 2000 and 2018, particularly among older adults. By 2018, the prevalence had reached 2.11% (21.1 per 1000 RA patients). Although RA-ILD-related mortality has decreased by over 50%, it remains a leading cause of death, accounting for 29% of fatalities among RA-ILD patients [5]. Overall, ILD contributes to almost 7% of all RA-related deaths [6]. Several studies have reported a five-year mortality rate of around 40%, with median survival after diagnosis ranging from 2.6 to 8 years [7]. Delayed diagnosis has been associated with increased mortality [8].

High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) is the imaging reference standard for detection and characterization of ILD in connective tissue diseases, including RA [9, 10]. It also provides critical prognostic information, as UIP pattern is associated with a 66% higher mortality risk compared with NSIP [11]. However, regular HRCT screening of all RA patients is not feasible due to high cost and risk of radiation exposure, especially when repeated scans are needed for patients who develop RA at younger age.

Moreover, even when diagnosed, the clinical course of RA-ILD is unpredictable, from stable lung function to rapid and progressive decline [12, 13]. Early identification of patients at risk of progression is therefore essential. All the above make diagnostics of RA-ILD very challenging.

Multiple screening methods have been proposed for the early ILD detection in RA, including physical examination (e.g., detection of inspiratory crackles), chest X-ray, pulmonary function tests, lung ultrasound (LUS), and different biomarkers. More recently, combined multimodal screening algorithms incorporating several of these tools have been explored to improve diagnostic yield. Among these, serum biomarkers have emerged as particularly promising tools, both for early screening and predicting disease progression [14].

The aim of this systematic review was to evaluate the reliability, validity, and clinical utility of the existing screening methods for RA-ILD, with a particular focus on early detection and identification of patients at risk of progression.

Methods

Protocol

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. A protocol for this systematic review was prospectively registered on PROSPERO and can be found at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251035610.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE, Scopus and Cochrane CENTRAL, covering studies published during last 20 years. The exact search period was from January 2005 to June 2025. Search terms were very broad to ensure that all relevant studies are identified. Search terms combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text words relating ILD and RA. The exact search terms were adapted for each database and were following: (“Lung Diseases, Interstitial“[Mesh]) AND “Arthritis, Rheumatoid“[Mesh]); “Lung Diseases, Interstitial” AND “Arthritis, Rheumatoid”; “Interstitial Lung Diseases” AND “Rheumatoid Arthritis”. Searches were restricted to peer-reviewed, full-text studies in English or Polish, with search terms in article abstract, title and keywords. The reference lists of eligible articles were also reviewed. All records were imported into EndNote (Clarivate Analytics), and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts. Full-text articles were retrieved for potentially relevant studies. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion between reviewers.

Study selection

Eligible for inclusion were only human studies on adult patients suspected of having RA-ILD/with ILD confirmed clinically and/or radiographically (HRCT preferred). Prospective or retrospective studies with a cross-sectional, cohort, case-control or diagnostic test design were eligible for selection. The diagnostic methods of interest were: lung imaging diagnostics (x-ray, HRCT, LUS); pulmonary function tests (forced vital capacity [FVC], diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide [DLCO]); clinical symptoms evaluation; and serum biomarkers’ evaluation. The comparator and control were any other diagnostic method (e.g., lung biopsy) and RA patients without ILD or healthy individuals.

Main outcome variables

The primary outcome was the identification and characterization of diagnostic modalities recommended for the detection of RA-ILD. Secondary outcomes included diagnostic performance measures – sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive likelihood ratio (LR+), and negative likelihood ratio (LR-).

and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

Data extraction was completed using a standardised form in Microsoft Excel. Study selection and data extraction were performed independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved by discussion. Extracted data included study characteristics (author, year, design, country), patient demographics, ILD subtype (e.g., UIP, NSIP), diagnostic method, biomarker assays, comparator groups, and diagnostic accuracy outcomes.

The QUADAS-2 tool was used to evaluate the quality and potential bias of selected studies [15].

Analysis

Extracted data were tabulated and compared in terms of study characteristics, diagnostic method, and diagnostic accuracy outcomes. Due to the heterogeneity in study designs, patient populations, reported outcomes of interest and their quantification, a narrative synthesis of selected studies has been conducted.

Results

Characteristic of included studies and risk of bias

A total of 36 studies met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed (Fig. 1). All studies were observational with various designs: 21 cross-sectional (including one study defined as diagnostic test accuracy study), 5 case-control, 3 prospective cohort, and 7 retrospective cohort studies. The studied patient populations were heterogenous in terms of age (ranging from 40 to 89 years), sex distribution (female percentage from 26 to 90%), with UIP/NSIP pattern reported in only 11 studies. The range of outcomes of interest reported in selected publications differed strongly with only three publications presenting all prespecified diagnostic accuracy metrics (sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, LR+, LR-, and AUC). Importantly, the mathematical and statistical measures applied for the quantification of outcomes of interest were not homogenous across the selected studies. Imaging methods–lung ultrasound

Nine observational prospective studies conducted in Italy, Spain, Egypt, Austria, Denmark, Argentina, Belgium, Greece, France, and Mexico were included in this systematic review. Among them, one study applied a cross-sectional diagnostic test accuracy design. A total of 352 patients with rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD), aged approximately 40 to 89 years, were analyzed in the included studies. The proportion of female participants ranged from 47% to 86%. The proportion of patients with UIP pattern was reported in only two studies and accounted for 40% [16] and 19% [17], respectively.

The sensitivity of LUS in detecting ILD among RA patients ranged from 70% to 100%, whereas specificity ranged from 56% to 97%. Surprisingly, in one study, the reported specificity was as low as 14.7%, compared to 94.7% for chest X-ray [18]. The AUC values reported across the included studies ranged from 0.63 to 1.0 [17–21]. The PPV ranged from 22% to 94%, and the NPV from 46% to 100%, as reported in seven studies [16–20, 22, 23]. The LR+ ranged from 1.15 to 29.4, and the LR- from 0 to 1.15, as reported in five studies [17, 18, 20, 22, 24].

Only three studies [17, 18, 20] reported all the prespecified diagnostic accuracy metrics (sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, LR+, LR-, and AUC). Overall, findings suggest promising but inconsistent diagnostic performance, precluding a definitive conclusion regarding the routine clinical use of LUS for RA-ILD screening.

Imaging methods–high-resolution computed tomography

One retrospective observational study from the United States compared HRCT with lung biopsy for the detection of UIP pattern in RA-ILD patients [25].

Among 69 patients (aged 50–75 years, 65% female), the prevalence of UIP pattern was 61%. When compared to histopathology, HRCT showed a sensitivity of 45% (95% CI: 13–61), and a specificity of 96% (95% CI: 81–100) with an AUC of 0.708 (95% CI: 0.623–0.792). The positive PPV and NPV were 95% and 53%, respectively.

Pulmonary function tests

Only two observational studies, from Italy and China, examined the utility of the DLCO in RA-ILD [26, 27]. A total of 54 RA-ILD patients (aged 50–75 years) were analyzed, with female participation reported in only one study (44%) [26]. Both studies demonstrated good diagnostic performance, with AUCs ranging from 0.81 to 0.87. A threshold of approximately 53% predicted provided 100% sensitivity but only moderate specificity (61%), indicating that DLCO functions best as a sensitive screening tool rather than a definitive diagnostic measure. No study evaluating FVC or TLC as a diagnostic index test for RA-ILD met the inclusion criteria, most likely because these parameters are used to assess disease severity and to monitor progression over time rather than to establish the diagnosis; pulmonary function tests should therefore be regarded as auxiliary tools for the non-invasive assessment of progression rather than as diagnostic methods for early detection.

Biomarkers

Twenty-four observational studies evaluated the diagnostic value of diverse serum biomarkers for RA-ILD including 1,276 RA-ILD patients aged 40–73 years, with female representation ranging from 26% to 97%. The proportion of RA-ILD patients with a UIP pattern was reported in eight studies, ranging from 26% to 59% [28–35]. The serum biomarkers identified are following: Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6), matrix metalloproteinases 7 (MMP-7), periostin, surfactant protein D and its ligand (SP-D, SP-DL), markers of endothelial or extracellular matrix activation (VCAM-1, ICAM-1, and E-selectin), cytokines (IL-22, IL-36α, IL-36γ), chemokines (CXCL-10, CCL-18, MCP-1), tumor-associated antigens (CA125, CA19-9, CA15-3, CEA), metabolomic markers (decanoic acid, glycerol, morpholine, asymmetric dimethylarginine [ADMA], uric acid), human epididymis protein 4 (HE4), endothelin-1 (ET-1), netrin-1, chitinase-3-like protein-1 (CHI3L1), anti-carbamylated protein antibodies (anti-CarPA) and anti-mutated citrullinated vimentin (anti-MCV). Given the marked heterogeneity of the underlying studies and of the level of supporting evidence, the identified biomarkers are presented below at separate levels — from the best-validated marker in current clinical use, through markers with moderate multi-study support, to single-study or exploratory candidates and, finally, unvalidated multi-marker panels.

Best-validated marker in current clinical use

Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6) was the most extensively studied biomarker (five studies) and showed the most consistent diagnostic accuracy, with high sensitivity (61–100%) and specificity (78–91%) and AUC values up to 0.92, consistently distinguishing RA-ILD from RA without lung involvement. It is currently the only biomarker used in clinical practice for this indication and therefore serves as the reference against which the remaining candidates should be judged.

Markers with moderate, multi-study support (adjunctive value)

Surfactant protein D and its ligand (SP-DL) demonstrated moderate diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.66–0.80), supporting their role as adjunctive rather than stand-alone tests [36–38]. Tumour-associated antigens (CA125, CA19-9, CA15-3, CEA) were frequently elevated in RA-ILD (sensitivity 56–79%, specificity 72–100%), but their limited specificity due to overlap with malignancy restricts clinical use [43–46]. Markers of endothelial or extracellular matrix activation (VCAM-1, ICAM-1, E-selectin) displayed moderate discriminative power (AUC 0.70–0.78), and chemokines (CXCL-10, CCL-18, MCP-1) achieved similar ranges (AUC 0.62–0.80), with CXCL-10 showing the best performance within this group [31, 40, 42]. Across this level, performance was moderate and inconsistent, and none of these markers has been validated as a stand-alone diagnostic test.

Single-study and exploratory candidates

Several markers rest on a single study or lack validation across independent cohorts and should be regarded as exploratory. These include periostin and MMP-7/TIMP-1 [30, 39]; individual cytokines (IL-22, IL-36α, IL-36γ; AUC 0.68–0.78) [35, 41]; and novel proteins such as HE4, ET-1, netrin-1, and CHI3L1, each of which achieved AUC values above 0.75 in individual studies [29, 47, 48]. The immature platelet fraction demonstrated limited diagnostic power (AUC 0.65) and likewise remains exploratory [49]. Two studies assessed autoantibodies — anti-carbamylated protein antibodies (anti-CarPA) and anti-mutated citrullinated vimentin (anti-MCV) — with moderate accuracy (sensitivity 79–80%, specificity 58–75%, AUC 0.67–0.82) [50, 51]; although elevated titres were more common in patients with ILD than in RA alone, these antibodies are not used routinely and lack independent validation. At present, all markers in this level should be considered exploratory rather than established diagnostic tools for RA-ILD.

Unvalidated multi-marker panels

The highest reported accuracy was achieved by combined signatures — combined MMP profiles (AUC approaching 0.99) [30, 39] and metabolite panels (decanoic acid, glycerol, morpholine, ADMA, uric acid; AUC ≈ 0.92, specificity up to 100%) [28, 31, 33]. However, these panels have not been externally validated and, despite their promise, cannot currently be recommended for clinical use. The summary of biomarkers is presented in Table 2.

Discussion

The present systematic review summarizes the current evidence on the diagnostic performance of various methods used for detecting interstitial lung disease (ILD) in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA). The included studies evaluated imaging techniques, pulmonary function tests, and wide range of serum biomarkers, with HRCT serving as the reference standard in most analyses.

The methodological quality of the included studies, assessed using the QUADAS-2 tool, was heterogeneous. While the overall risk of bias was judged as low in more than half of cases, 16 studies showed unclear and high (in 1 case) bias, mainly due to insufficient reporting on patient selection, index test interpretation, or reference standard procedures. Applicability concerns were low in 18 studies but unclear in 17 and high in 1, suggesting limited external validity. The studies also differed substantially in design, patient populations, reported outcomes of interest and their quantification, which limited comparability and precluded quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) – an approach inappropriate given the high heterogeneity of the studies.

Among imaging methods, LUS emerged as a promising, non-invasive, and radiation-free tool for ILD screening in RA patients. Reported sensitivity and specificity values varied widely (70–100% and 56–97%, respectively) [17–21]. Some studies demonstrated that LUS could outperform chest X-ray in early ILD detection [18], highlighting its potential role as a point-of-care screening modality. However, inconsistencies in scanning protocols, operator dependency, and lack of standardized scoring systems continue to limit its clinical implementation.

In contrast, HRCT remains the gold standard for identifying ILD patterns such as UIP. The only study directly comparing HRCT with lung biopsy [25] demonstrated high specificity (96%) but moderate sensitivity (45%), suggesting that HRCT can reliably confirm but not always exclude UIP. These findings underscore that HRCT, although highly accurate, may miss early or subtle fibrotic abnormalities.

As regards pulmonary function tests, particularly DLCO, have been evaluated in two studies and shown moderate diagnostic utility with AUC values ranging from 0.81 to 0.87 [26, 27]. Although the above results appear to confirm the usefulness of DLCO as a non-invasive functional screening tool, relatively low DLCO of 53% predictive value, which have shown best predictive value, preclude its use as a screening tool for early detection of RA-ILD. Moreover, reduced DLCO lacks specificity for ILD, as it may also occur in patients with emphysema, pulmonary hypertension, or anemia [20].

A considerable number of studies have examined the role of serum biomarkers in detecting RA-ILD. There were only two studies evaluating the diagnostic value of autoantibodies, and it should be emphasized that these were not antibodies routinely used in diagnostics.

Among other biomarkers, KL-6 has shown the most consistent diagnostic accuracy, with sensitivity ranging from 61% to 100% and specificity from 78% to 91% [19, 20, 22]. The levels of KL-6 were significantly higher in RA-ILD patients than in those with RA alone, supporting its role as a potential non-invasive screening biomarker.

Surfactant protein D and SP-DL demonstrated moderate diagnostic performance (AUC 0.66–0.80) [36–38], suggesting potential as adjunctive markers. Other molecules-such as periostin, MMP-7, tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase-1 (TIMP-1), and adhesion molecules including VCAM-1, ICAM-1, and E-selectin also showed moderate accuracy [30, 31, 39, 40], but results were inconsistent across studies and need further validation.

Several investigations also explored tumor-associated antigens such as CA125, CA19-9, CA15-3, and CEA, reporting AUC values often exceeding 0.70 [43–46]. While these markers may indicate lung involvement, their specificity is limited by overlap with malignancy-related elevations.

Emerging evidence also points to novel biomarkers, including HE4, ET-1, netrin-1, and CHI3L1, with some studies reporting high diagnostic accuracy (AUC > 0.80) 29,47,48]. These findings suggest that multi-marker panels or metabolomic approaches could enhance early ILD detection in RA in the future.

Overall, no single diagnostic tool, except for HRCT which has limitations such as cost and radiation risk, provides a definitive diagnosis of RA-ILD. A multimodal diagnostic approach combining clinical assessment, imaging, pulmonary function testing, and serum biomarkers appears most appropriate [18, 20]. Lung ultrasound may serve as an initial, accessible screening tool; HRCT remains essential for ILD pattern characterization; and selected biomarkers such as KL-6 and SP-D may help identify high-risk patients or monitor disease progression. Building on these observations and on current recommendations, we propose a pragmatic, sequential framework in which the individual modalities are positioned according to their principal clinical role — risk identification and screening, diagnostic confirmation, and severity assessment with longitudinal monitoring. At the first level, patients with RA should be evaluated for ILD risk on the basis of clinical assessment (inspiratory crackles, dyspnoea, unexplained cough) together with established risk factors such as older age, male sex, smoking history, and high-titre anti-citrullinated protein antibodies, in line with the 2023 ACR/CHEST guideline for screening and monitoring of ILD in systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases [9]. In this screening context, lung ultrasound may serve as an accessible, radiation-free, point-of-care tool to help rule out ILD and to select patients for further imaging, potentially complemented by KL-6 as an adjunctive marker. At the second level — diagnostic confirmation — HRCT remains the reference standard for detecting ILD and characterising the radiological pattern (UIP versus NSIP), with surgical lung biopsy reserved for atypical or diagnostically uncertain cases. It is essential to distinguish these two levels: lung ultrasound, pulmonary function tests, and biomarkers are screening or risk-stratification tools, whereas the diagnosis of RA-ILD is established by HRCT. At the third level, pulmonary function tests (DLCO, FVC) and selected biomarkers (KL-6, SP-D) are best suited to assessing disease severity and monitoring progression over time rather than to establishing the initial diagnosis, consistent with the 2026 ERS/EULAR clinical practice guidelines for connective tissue disease-associated ILD [10]. Importantly, this framework is a conceptual proposal derived from the reviewed evidence rather than a validated diagnostic protocol; given the heterogeneity of the included studies and the absence of prospective validation, the relative weight and optimal sequence of these modalities — and any diagnostic thresholds — will require confirmation in large, multicentre studies before such a pathway can be recommended for routine clinical use. For the same reason, we deliberately refrain from presenting it as a fixed diagnostic algorithm.

The present evidence is limited by the predominance of observational, single-center studies with small sample sizes and heterogeneous patient populations. Definitions of ILD and UIP patterns varied across studies, as did HRCT criteria and biomarker cut-off thresholds. In many reports, blinding between index tests and reference standards was not described, potentially introducing assessment bias. Furthermore, the scarcity of longitudinal studies precludes firm conclusions regarding prognostic or monitoring utility.

Taken together, very few studies evaluating HRCT in RA-ILD were identified, likely reflecting the well-established role of this modality in the diagnosis of interstitial lung disease in general and in the course of other systemic diseases. As also emphasized in the most recent EULAR guidelines [10], HRCT remains the gold standard, and current research efforts are primarily focused on identifying potential alternatives to HRCT rather than reassessing its diagnostic value. Lung ultrasound shows promise but inconsistent diagnostic accuracy across studies. Among biomarkers, KL-6 is the best validated and most reproducible marker for RA-ILD detection. Other biomarkers – including MMPs, surfactant proteins, tumor markers, and cytokines-show potential, but require further large-scale validation before they can be recommended for routine clinical use.

This review is further limited by the heterogeneity of study designs, patient populations, diagnostic thresholds, and reference standards, which prevented a formal meta-analysis and hindered comparability across studies. Many of the included analyses were small, single-centre studies with incomplete reporting of key methodological domains, resulting in unclear risk of bias and limited applicability. In addition, the lack of standardized imaging protocols and unified biomarker assays substantially restricts the generalizability of the findings.

Future research should focus on large, multicenter, prospective studies with standardized imaging protocols, unified biomarker assays, and integrated diagnostic algorithms that combine imaging and serological data. Such approaches could improve early detection of ILD in RA and facilitate timely initiation of targeted therapies, ultimately improving patient outcomes.
