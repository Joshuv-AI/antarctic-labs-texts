‘A return on investment’. How shared decision-making benefits doctors: a key informant interview study
Authors: Glyn Elwyn, Hannah B Leavitt, Mallory Vesling, Christopher Rex Jacobs, Lea Lund
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo elicit the benefits of shared decision-making to doctors who are champions of this approach.DesignA qualitative interview study that used practical thematic analysis.SettingWe identified a purposive international sample of doctors in active clinical practice who were recognised champions of shared decision-making, working in various clinical disciplines.Participants24 doctors in active clinical practice were interviewed; 14 were male and 10 were female; 20 had been in clinical practice for over 10 years (range 1–30). 12 practised in North America, 10 in Europe, 1 in South America and 1 in Asia; 4 doctors worked in internal medicine, 4 in primary care, 5 in surgery, 3 in paediatrics, 3 in oncology and 1 in each of the following disciplines: emergency medicine, palliative care, geriatrics, physical medicine and rehabilitation, anaesthesiology, and cardiology.ResultsThis selected sample of doctors consistently reported that shared decision-making provided benefits to themselves, their patients and their teams. Shared decision-making reinforced and enhanced their self-identity as ethical professionals, supporting patient autonomy, increasing their professional fulfilment and reducing their risk of burnout. These intrinsic benefits accompanied reports of other consequential benefits, namely, patients’ achieving better-informed, preference-sensitive decisions, a higher likelihood of improved patient outcomes, improved efficiency and team function. The doctors viewed the approach as providing connectedness, shared responsibility resulting in a lighter burden, acting as a buttress against moral injury and the emotional strain of clinical work and, where relevant, mitigation against becoming the second victim of a bad or unexpected outcome.Conclusions and relevanceDoctors who champion shared decision-making report significant benefits to themselves and their patients. These benefits have not been widely reported, which has implications for motivating doctors to adopt shared decision-making. Instead of addressing presumed gaps in communication skills, it might be better to highlight the positive impact on professional fulfilment and the protective effect of shared decision-making.

Introduction

Why have some doctors become strong advocates for shared decision-making (SDM) as a way to practise medicine?1 What do they gain by doing so? Over the last two decades, SDM has become an approach promoted in many countries at the policy level.2 SDM describes a collaboration where patients and doctors make health-related decisions together, informed by trustworthy evidence and by preferences: the goal is to deliver high-quality patient-centred care.3 4 Some clinicians are correctly concerned that SDM can be harmful, especially if the expressed wishes of patients informed by misinformation are supported. There is consensus, however, that SDM should be guided by evidence, and by the professional ethic of ‘do no harm’, in definitions that are widely accepted.5 6 Evaluations of SDM have mainly focused on patient-relevant outcomes,7 8 which have shown a positive impact on knowledge, satisfaction and decision confidence, and in specific settings, clinical outcomes and adherence to treatments have improved.7 8

Yet many doctors think that SDM is too idealistic,9 10 especially given the time constraints and workload pressures in most care delivery systems.1113 There is agreement that adoption and implementation have been slow.14 15 Given the low uptake, what drives some doctors to become champions? What motivates them to adopt a process many view as requiring extra time, new skills and a behaviour change?

While research exists on doctors’ views about SDM, such as their perceived barriers to implementing it in practice, much less attention has been paid to the reasons that lead some doctors to become strong advocates.121618 Evidence is emerging that SDM is associated with improved patient outcomes in specific settings,7 and, to a degree, this may be responsible for the broad interest in the approach.7 19 However, it is also possible that other factors play a part, such as validating professional identity, affecting doctor’s experience, and the potential to reduce burnout.20 Might researchers have paid too little attention to the benefits doctors themselves derive from undertaking SDM with patients?21

Research into the benefits of SDM for doctors has been largely absent; there has been more interest in understanding the barriers to adopting SDM.13 22 23 There is a significant knowledge gap: What are the benefits to doctors of adopting SDM? This study aims to elicit and explore the benefits SDM champions perceive for themselves and their patients. To achieve this aim, we conducted qualitative interviews with doctors recognised as champions of SDM and who are in active clinical practice.

Methods

Study design

We undertook a qualitative study to gain insight into doctors’ lived experiences, views and beliefs about their adoption of SDM as an approach to clinical practice. We collaborated to develop an interview guide (see box 1), piloted the process and added probes. We conducted semistructured interviews with purposively invited key informants using a videoconferencing platform to record and transcribe the conversations (figure 1). We planned to achieve data saturation, which is determined when additional interviews would not yield new insights about the benefits gained by undertaking SDM.24 We estimated this would occur after approximately 20 interviews, although we were prepared to continue if required. We included a patient (author CRJ) in the design, all aspects of data analysis and the development of the manuscript.

Our methods and results are reported using a Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research checklist (online supplemental appendix 1).25

Eligibility Criteria

We set the following eligibility criteria: (1) doctors should be active in clinical practice and (2) the doctors were asked to self-affirm themselves as SDM champions. Individuals were not eligible for interviews if they were not doctors or were doctors who had left clinical practice.

Invitations to participate

Interview invitations were based on a doctor’s advocacy for SDM in clinical practice. We obtained a list of the International Shared Decision Making Society members and identified individuals with medical qualifications who we considered to be champions of SDM, as judged by their profile in the SDM community, reputations and/or publications. We formed a recruitment matrix to guide our goal of achieving a purposive sample that represented a range of countries, genders, years of experience and clinical disciplines. We emailed the list of doctors, providing a study information document (online supplemental appendix 2 and 3). We scheduled online interviews after confirming that the doctor met the eligibility criteria. Before the interview, the participants’ verbal consent was confirmed and recorded. After introductions, the interviewers (HBL and MV) followed the semistructured interview guide (see Box 1), elaborating using prompts where relevant. Final drafts of the article were sent to interviewees, who were asked to inform us about misinterpretations and inaccuracies.

Interviewer characteristics

Two females (HBL and MV) with no previous contact with the doctors conducted the interviews: HBL is a researcher in her 20s with previous interview experience, and MV was a medical student in her 20s at the time of the study.

Analysis

We undertook a qualitative analysis of the transcripts following the practical thematic analysis method,1 using Atlas.TI software (V.24.1.1).26 Three steps were followed: reading, coding and theming.1 After reading all the transcripts and reflecting on the data, three coders (HBL, CRJ and MV) developed a preliminary phrase-based codebook. We initially inductively coded three interviews, and after a meeting where the preliminary coding was discussed, an updated codebook was finalised. We analysed the doctors’ responses to questions 3–7 in the interview guide. All the transcripts were coded independently by HBL and CRJ, checking for discordant data. At analysis meetings, themes and subthemes were proposed using complete thoughts or sentences, and relationships between candidate themes were discussed. A preliminary list of themes and subthemes was discussed by the research team (GE, HBL, CRJ and MV) and substantiated by selecting relevant participant statements from the transcripts. We asked interview participants to provide feedback on a near-final version of the manuscript.

Results

We invited 38 doctors, obtained 24 (63%) willing responses, and interviewed those doctors between January 2024 and May 2024 (22 by HBL and two by MV). No doctors withdrew their agreement to be interviewed. During this period, HBL became immersed in the data, summarised the responses to the study team and developed a phrase-based codebook. The interviewees’ responses were similar in terms of the perceived benefits to them and their patients. After the 10th interview, data saturation was achieved. The process of detailed independent coding by two people confirmed the emergence of data saturation even as we searched for discordant data. However, interviewees gave varying context-specific descriptions of the benefits to themselves and their patients. To confirm data saturation and exhaustion, we continued until we had completed 24 interviews. Interviewees did not report any data misinterpretations.

Participant characteristics

On average, the doctors we interviewed had been in practice for 16 years. Four had been in practice for less than 10 years, 12 for over 10 years and 8 for over 20 years: 14 were male and 10 were female. Direct patient contact time was reported to be at 50%–100% for seven, and at approximately 20% for the rest. We included 12 doctors from North America, 10 from Europe, 1 from South America and 1 from Asia. The mean duration of the interviews was 25 min (range, 14 –40 min). Four doctors worked in internal medicine, four worked in primary care, five were surgeons, three were paediatricians and three were medical oncologists. We interviewed one doctor from each of the following disciplines: emergency medicine, palliative care, geriatrics, physical medicine and rehabilitation, anaesthesiology and cardiology.

Thematic Analysis

The coders HBL, CRJ and MV categorised the data into four provisional themes describing benefits, namely: professional, personal, patient and academic. Further discussion led to merging the professional and personal into one professional benefits theme, in which we identified two subthemes: intrinsic and consequential benefits. In the intrinsic reward subtheme, we could identify three discrete areas of benefit arising from using SDM: (1) the reinforcement of professional identity, (2) increased satisfaction and fulfilment and (3) a reduced risk of burnout. For benefits that are consequences of SDM, we identified three discrete areas: (1) better informed preference-sensitive decisions, (2) better patient outcomes and (3) improved efficiency and team function. Two other themes were identified: SDM generates rewarding relationships with patients and expands academic networks and opportunities (see table 1).

A doctor recognised that some patients are “not used to having this kind of conversation.” Yet, the result was that they “are happier, and I’m happy. I feel I am doing my job right” (Family Practitioner, Argentina). A surgeon acknowledged that the process is “tough,” yet reported that finding alignment with patients’ personal beliefs was rewarding and was “about being good at your job” (Surgeon, United Kingdom).

Statements referred to ethics as an ‘imperative’ or a ‘driving factor’ (Paediatrician, USA). An emergency room doctor in Norway said that he tried to find the “best fit for this unique patient and how they live their life,” adding that “ethically, clinically, pragmatically… it’s the best way to do it” (Emergency Physician, Norway). In summary, these doctors said that SDM was, for the most part, “not optional” because SDM provides “intrinsic motivation… it’s the right thing to do” (Pulmonologist, Switzerland). SDM represented their values. A rheumatologist said, “Honesty is one of the things that makes somebody participate in SDM… Be upfront with your patient and their family.” (Rheumatologist, USA). The doctors tried to put themselves in their “patient’s shoes and think how you would like to be met… not as a number or diagnosis, but as a human being. That’s why I say it’s ethical.“ (Oncologist, Denmark).

Intrinsic benefits: (2) Increased satisfaction and professional fulfilment

SDM gave the doctors higher satisfaction in their work and a greater sense of professional fulfilment. See box 2 for statements that illustrate this theme. These benefits were linked to patients’ reactions to their curiosity, as this doctor reported knowing that her “patients have been heard.” (Gynaecologist, USA). The benefits were associated with developing connections, such as asking “what’s important” to her patients and eliciting “what they are hoping for” (Palliative Care, USA). The reports reflected a feedback loop generated by providing personalised plans, as this doctor in the Netherlands said, “It’s fun to know things about a patient’ and agreeing on a “plan that fits perfectly.” This, he said, makes him “happy… it empowers me to perform my job well” (Haematologist, Netherlands).

“[it’s about] professional satisfaction… a likelihood of success. I describe it as this kind of relief… of being able to be honest. That’s important. I like the moment when a decision is made, and you have a feeling, “Okay, we agree. That’s good, right?” Pumonology, Male, Switzerland, (23).

Similarly, the surgeon who provided options, risks, and benefits and then made sure the patient ‘made the right decision for themselves’ said he experienced ‘professional joy and satisfaction’ (Surgeon, USA). An endocrinologist reported results from randomised trials confirming the positive impact on clinician satisfaction and the ‘energising’ effect that ‘feeling good professionally’ has on intrinsic motivation. It gives “you energy to go on to the next patient.” (Endocrinologist, USA).

The doctors mention “internal reward” (Rehabilitation Physician, Taiwan) and more confidence that they are “doing an OK job.” These positive benefits are associated with the work involved in “developing a common understanding” or “getting invested” (Endocrinologist, USA) in “thinking about a plan that fits perfectly” (Haematologist, Netherlands).

While the doctors did not describe the cognitive work involved in SDM as a burden, they acknowledged that time was required to “provide choices” (Rehabilitation Physician, Taiwan) and to make sure that patients “understand other options” (Surgeon, USA). “Being honest” (Pulmonology, Switzerland) required emotional work and checking that people “have made the right decisions for themselves” (Surgeon, USA). Yet the doctors interviewed were consistent: the professional-level benefits justified the investment.

Intrinsic benefits: (3) Reduced risk of burnout

The doctors also reported that SDM reduced the risk of burnout. See box 3 for statements that illustrate this theme. For example, getting “closer” to patients was a route to more “professional confidence” and “less burnout” by giving more meaning to “life in a busy clinic” (Oncologist, Denmark). Another doctor said that SDM “feels right” and also prevents the “moral injury of doing something to somebody and then having them regret it,” removing the guilt “that you never informed them that this was even a possibility” (Family Practitioner, USA). SDM highlights the potential risks and insulates the doctor from becoming the second victim of poor outcomes.

An oncologist said that SDM provides “multiple benefits” and makes the “job easier” (Male Oncologist, Denmark). It reduces the risk of “burnout” (Female Oncologist, Denmark). A surgeon reflected on the challenge of bad outcomes, “It is painful for patients and families,” and admitted that it is “really hard to take care of people when things go poorly when we haven't clarified that this is right for the patient and the family” (Surgeon, USA). Another doctor described involving patients in decisions “as a kind of relief… of being able to be honest” (Pulmonologist, Switzerland). In summary, SDM was considered protective and rewarding: “It’s one of the reasons why I find joy and meaning in this work” (Emergency Care Physician, Norway).

Consequential benefits: (1) Patients achieving better-informed, preference-sensitive decisions

The interview data indicate that an important motivation of doctors who espouse SDM arises from a belief in the principle that patients should be well-informed and supported to determine if they wish to participate in choosing their future care. A selection of quotes that illustrate this is provided in box 4. The view that patients “deserve to have their preferences incorporated into their medical care” (Palliative Care Specialist, USA) exemplifies this stance. We also hear caution lest patients be abandoned to make decisions alone by doctors who prioritise information rather than offer support, “I definitely want clinicians to be seen as warm and kind, and I also want to make sure that the final decision reflects the patient’s goals and preferences” (Cardiologist, USA).

“I think everybody deserves to have their preferences incorporated into their medical care… So I think I would be doing shared decision-making regardless of what specialty I ended up in… It’s probably part of why I ended up as a palliative care doctor.” Palliative Care Specialist, Female, USA, (12)

“I definitely want clinicians to be seen as warm and kind, and I also want to make sure that the final decision reflects the patient’s goals and preferences.” Cardiologist, Female, USA, (10).

“Well, to me, the main benefit is that if things go badly, you know they [patients] have skin in the game. I always remember this patient with lung cancer. I didn’t feel comfortable giving two chemotherapy agents. But she was very clear and said, “Listen, I want this combined treatment.” Her family was there to hear [her preference]. Sure enough, after two cycles of the combined treatment, she had a massive stroke [and died]. But the family was not angry because they had all heard her take responsibility and realized that this was what she wanted to do. They knew she was ready to accept the consequences. So it’s not just the doctor, you have the patient invested in [(the decision]). I think it’s just a good way to practice medicine.” Oncologist, Male, Canada, (17).

“There’s a lot of things that I need to confirm with people or have their input. Medicine will not work well if we’re not being vulnerable about what we know. I think COVID-19 taught us that lesson. We’re not very forthcoming with information… And we don’t really give people options. So if we’re to build trust with people, especially people who’ve been marginalized… then we have to level about what we can do for people. We are connections to knowledge.” Rheumatologist, Female, USA, (2).

We heard examples where achieving informed preference-sensitive decisions led to ‘positive return on investment’. The oncologist, who, after telling his patient and her family that he felt it too risky to prescribe two chemotherapy agents, after talking, accepted her wish to go ahead. As he had feared, the patient died after two cycles of the combined agents. Yet, as he says, the family had no anger or regret “because they had all heard her take responsibility and realized that this was what she wanted to do” (Oncologist, Canada). This story and others show the balance of professional and patient agency that SDM demands. As this early-career doctor states,…if we're to build trust with people, especially people who’ve been marginalised… then we have to level about what we can do for people” (Rheumatologist, USA).

Consequential benefits: (2) Coproducing better patient outcomes

Doctors reported that SDM led to better patient outcomes. See box 5 for statements that illustrate this theme. SDM also identifies what patients might view as ‘unacceptable outcomes or experiences’. A palliative care specialist responsible for seriously ill patients said that she felt “even more of an obligation to make sure that the time they have left is lived in a way that is most acceptable to them… that they get the care they actually want.” (Palliative Care Specialist, USA). A sentiment echoed by a doctor who saw SDM as a way to avoid patient disappointment or complaints, “When I know that they have really thought about all their options, there’s not gonna be regret later… ” (Gynecologist, USA).

Another doctor described the benefit of patients who take on “responsibility” for their blood pressure control, “Sometimes, they don’t appear in the clinic for 2 years but still have good control by taking their medications” (Family Practitioner, Spain). Consequential benefits: (3) Improved efficiency and team function

Doctors who had continuity of care reported the efficiency gains that SDM generated, similar to “meeting an old friend,” where the relationship made it possible to “go directly to the core issues and discuss them at a deeper level because you trust each other” (Endocrinologist, USA). See box 6 for statements that illustrate this theme. It’s “a lot easier to take care of people when you actually know something about them,” said another doctor (Palliative Care Specialist, USA). Some acknowledged that efficiency gains required investment, “So, at first, it may take a little more time,” to save time “over the whole patient trajectory… because the decision will be much better” (Oncologist, Male, Denmark), a view repeated here, “It may cost you a couple of more minutes the first time, but then the second, third, and fourth time you meet with the same person… it will save you time ” (Female Oncologist, Denmark).

Discussion

Principal Findings

Interviews with these 24 medically qualified doctors in active clinical practice in ten countries, who viewed themselves to be SDM champions, reported benefits for themselves, their patients and their teams. SDM enhanced their self-identity as ethical professionals wishing to support patient autonomy, increased their work satisfaction, professional fulfilment and connectedness with patients. By achieving shared responsibility, SDM was viewed as reducing the risk of moral injury and burnout, and a mitigation from becoming the second victim of a bad or unexpected outcome. These intrinsic benefits were accompanied by their appreciation of other consequential benefits, such as patients achieving better-informed, preference-sensitive decisions and having a higher likelihood of better outcomes, including team function and efficiency.

The enhancement of professional identity and a boost to self-esteem were valued. Equally important was the view that the transparency involved in SDM reduced the risk of moral injury and burnout, mitigated against the emotional burden of clinical work and, where relevant, acted as a defence against the doctor becoming the second victim of a bad or unexpected outcome. The fact that SDM led to more rewarding relationships with patients and families was a significant benefit, along with possible expansion of academic opportunities.

Strengths and Weaknesses of the Method

The strengths of this study are the international sample of doctors and the approach to analysis. We purposefully selected doctors in active practice who could self-affirm to be SDM champions. The majority were known members of the International Shared Decision Making Society, established in 2018. We intentionally invited doctors from various disciplines, from family medicine to surgery. We interviewed doctors who trained in different countries and who had varying lengths of clinical experience. Our analysis of the interviews followed an approach called practical thematic analysis.1 We estimated data saturation at around the tenth interview but continued our interviews to approach data exhaustion. We all read the transcripts, and coding was done independently. During analytic discussions, we focused our theme identification on reported benefits. We acknowledge that we are at risk of bias, such as interviewees offering desired reports given their society membership. By interviewing only SDM champions, most of whom were in part-time contact with patients, we acknowledge that we cannot suggest that SDM has these benefits for other doctors.

Results in the context of other similar work

The doctors we interviewed valued the already established consequential benefits of SDM, such as patients gaining knowledge, satisfaction, complying with treatment recommendations, or establishing trust in older or ethnically diverse populations.2731 They also identified benefits with far-wider implications for professional well-being and acted as a defence against moral injury and the psychological stress that doctors experience when poor outcomes, possible litigation, serious adverse reactions or unexpected death occur, increasingly recognised as the second victim syndrome.32 33

Burnout and moral injury among doctors have become a widespread occupational concern.34 35 However, very little empirical work has been done on the benefits to doctors of humanistic practice, and specifically, whether doing SDM could be protective. This potential has been put forward with regard to burnout,20 and whether communication skills training could mitigate it.36 In a series of workshops, Horowitz et al reported that doctors ‘described humanistic interactions with patients as experiences that fulfilled them and confirmed their commitment to medicine’ and that ‘relationships deepened through recognising each person’s humanity’.37 Kern used surveys to investigate personal growth among medical faculty and reported that powerful experiences, helping relationships lead to introspection and positive development, and an ‘improved sense of self, increased productivity’.38 Adler suggested that the emotional and cognitive work required to construct a ‘caring doctor–patient relationship’ might lead to physiological benefits which he called sociophysiology and that such processes could influence the health of both patients and doctors.39 40 In a similar vein, Benson proposed benefits to clinicians of a patient-centred therapeutic approach based on Balint's ideas,41 and Baker et al evaluated the positive outcomes associated with continuity of care in primary care.42

The neurobiology of empathy and the role of mirror neurons is being investigated as part of the emergence of a ‘shared mind’.43 44 In teachers, the expression of empathy protects against burnout.45 There are studies in social psychology on motivation theory that also support these results.46 The arousal of intrinsic motivation increases job satisfaction, stimulates behaviour change and increases resilience.47 Nevertheless, these interviews provide evidence that these SDM champions report that by investing in the social, cognitive and emotional work of SDM, they achieve meaningful patient relationships that enhance their motivation and reduce their risk of burnout. That SDM took extra time, particularly during initial encounters, was acknowledged yet viewed as a worthwhile investment. These doctors did not see time as a hindrance, which is often mentioned as a significant barrier to SDM.12

Implications

These reported benefits require confirmation through studies conducted over extended periods, comparing doctors who practise SDM to those who do not. Additionally, we need to determine whether SDM offers similar benefits to other healthcare professionals who may have already adopted the principles. Even if we lack confirmation from other studies, the doctors in this study strongly felt that others should experience the professional and relational benefits they described and wanted to see changes in practice, training and policy, emphasising the reward of connecting in a ‘disconnected world’.48 This study also has implications for motivating doctors to adopt SDM. Past efforts to promote SDM have typically focused on addressing assumed deficiencies in communication skills or insufficient attention to patient-centred care, an approach that often generates defensiveness. A more successful strategy could be based on highlighting the beneficial protective effect of SDM, such as enhancing professional identity, improving patient relationships and reducing the risk of burnout.
