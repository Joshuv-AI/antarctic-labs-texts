Predicting 90-Day Mortality After Geriatric Hip Fracture Using Combined Preoperative and Perioperative Risk Factors
Authors: Murat Erem, Cihan Unyilmaz, Sinem Erem, Savas Yildirim, Esref Selcuk, Mert Ozcan, Cem Copuroglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHip fractures in the elderly are associated with high 90-day mortality rates. Most existing predictive models rely solely on preoperative variables. This study aimed to develop two parallel risk score models integrating preoperative and perioperative factors to predict 90-day mortality.MethodsMedical records of patients aged ≥65 years who underwent hip fracture surgery at our institution between January 2018 and December 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. Five preoperative risk factors were evaluated: Prognostic Nutritional Index <40, age ≥85 years, ≥4 comorbidities, surgical delay >5 days, and intracapsular fracture. The >5-day surgical delay threshold was determined empirically through receiver operating characteristic analysis, as the conventional 48-hour cut-off lacked discriminative capacity in this cohort where 86.8% of patients exceeded it. Model performance was assessed using logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic analysis.ResultsOf 388 patients enrolled, 387 constituted the analytical sample (60 deaths). The 90-day mortality rate was 15.5%. Individually, each factor showed weak to moderate discrimination. The preoperative five-factor score achieved an AUC of 0.722, and the combined six-factor score — which additionally incorporates ICU stay ≥5 days — achieved an AUC of 0.737. Each unit score increase raised mortality odds approximately 2.68-fold. In patients scoring ≥4, mortality exceeded 50%. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test confirmed adequate calibration for both models.ConclusionA two-stage scoring approach combining admission-available and postoperative variables demonstrated acceptable discrimination and calibration for 90-day mortality prediction in elderly hip fracture patients, supporting its use as a practical risk stratification tool at both preoperative and postoperative clinical decision points.Keywords: aged, hip fractures, mortality, nutritional status, risk assessment, prognostic nutritional index

Introduction

Hip fractures represent a major public health burden in the elderly population. Research indicates that hip fractures affect 681.35 per 100,000 individuals who are 55 years old or older, while the prevalence reaches 1,191.39 per 100,000. 1 The number of hip fracture cases is projected to increase to 4.5–6.26 million by 2050 compared to 1.26 million cases in 1990.2,3 Mortality rates after hip fracture are high; one-month mortality is estimated at 5–10% and one-year mortality at 12–27%3,4 and may reach 20–30% in the oldest patients. The Turkish research showed that patients who survived one year had a 29.49% annual mortality rate, whereas mortality reached 86.52% during the extended follow-up period, and it confirmed that hip fracture patients die at three times the rate of individuals without fractures. 5

The main risk factors affecting mortality include advanced age, male gender, ASA score of ≥3, institutional residence, metastatic cancer, and comorbidities. 3 Age-adjusted mortality rates are higher in men than in women, and deaths from cardiovascular, pulmonary diseases and cancer are more common. 5 The nutritional condition of patients determines their health status. Between 4% and 39.4% of elderly hip fracture patients are malnourished at admission, and research shows this condition raises their risk of dying within one year by 3.68 times. 4 The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) serves as a biomarker reflecting both nutritional and immune function; it has demonstrated value for mortality prediction, as patients with low PNI values have needed intensive care and experienced poor outcomes. 6 Previous meta-analyses have demonstrated that surgical delay is associated with significantly increased mortality in elderly hip fracture patients. A systematic review of 16 prospective studies involving 13,478 patients found that earlier surgery — irrespective of the 24, 48, or 72-hour cut-off — was associated with a 19% reduction in adjusted all-cause mortality. 7 A larger meta-analysis of 35 studies encompassing 191,873 participants confirmed that patients operated within 48 hours had significantly lower odds of death (pooled OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.67–0.81). 8 Accordingly, international guidelines recommend surgery within 24–48 hours of admission.4-9

Although various models have been developed in the current literature to predict mortality after hip fracture, most of these models focus only on preoperative factors. There is no clear conclusion about the superiority of models combining preoperative and perioperative factors. 1 The Area Under the Curve (AUC) values of the existing models vary between 0.710–0.964; however, predictive accuracy may decrease in elderly patients. 1 Although the role of PNI in predicting orthopaedic surgery outcomes has been demonstrated, the recommended cut-off values in different studies vary greatly between 26 and 49.2.4-6 Research has produced risk models to predict deep vein thrombosis before surgery in elderly patients who break their hips, 2 but there is a lack of studies that assess 90-day mortality using PNI alongside ICU admission duration and surgical delay as perioperative variables. The present study therefore aimed to construct and validate two complementary mortality prediction models for elderly hip fracture patients — one restricted to variables available at admission, and a second that extends this framework by incorporating postoperative intensive care data to refine risk estimates after surgery.

Methods

Study Design, Population and Sample

This retrospective cohort study was conducted between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2024, at Trakya University Faculty of Medicine, Department of Orthopaedics and Traumatology. As this was a retrospective observational study, clinical trial registration was not applicable. This study is reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guideline, a checklist of 22 items deemed essential for transparent reporting of prediction model studies 9 . The records of patients who underwent surgery for hip fracture in the same center, aged 65 years and over, whose preoperative serum albumin and lymphocyte results could be obtained, and whose 90-day survival status could be determined with certainty were scanned.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥65 years at the time of fracture; (2) radiologically confirmed proximal femoral fracture (intracapsular, intertrochanteric, or subtrochanteric); (3) surgical treatment performed at the study center; (4) preoperative serum albumin and total lymphocyte count available within 48 hours before surgery; and (5) 90-day survival status confirmed through hospital records or civil registry. Exclusion criteria were: (1) conservative (non-operative) management; (2) age <65 years; (3) missing preoperative albumin and/or lymphocyte values; and (4) loss to follow-up before the 90th postoperative day. Thus, the analysis cluster was determined as 388 patients. One patient had incomplete fracture classification data and was excluded from the regression and risk score analyses, yielding an analytical sample of 387 patients for those models.

A formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed, as this study enrolled all consecutive eligible patients within the study period. However, the sample of 388 patients with 59 deaths (15.2% event rate) met the commonly recommended minimum of 10 events per predictor variable for logistic regression models containing five to six variables (EPV = 9.8–11.8), supporting adequate statistical power for the primary analyses. The absence of a prospective power calculation is acknowledged as a limitation.

Age (years), gender, number of comorbidities, albumin (g/dL), lymphocyte count (/mm3), PNI, fracture type (intracapsular, intertrochanteric, or subtrochanteric), surgical procedure (proximal femoral nail antirotation, cemented or uncemented bipolar hemiarthroplasty, or other), length of intensive care unit stay (days), presence of ≥5 days of stay in the intensive care unit, time to surgery (days), and surgical delay of more than 5 days were defined. The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) was calculated with the formula [10 × albumin (g/dL)] + [0.005 × total lymphocytes (/mm3)]. 10 PNI <40 was accepted as the operative cut-off point for low nutritional status. 11

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was 90-day all-cause mortality following hip fracture surgery. Secondary outcomes included 12-month overall survival and the discriminative performance of the developed risk scores. Mortality was ascertained through the hospital information system and central civil registry records for the 90-day endpoint, and supplemented by outpatient clinic visits and telephone contact for the 12-month follow-up.

Operating Procedures

Demographic information, comorbidities, preoperative biochemistry results, fracture classification, surgery dates, intensive care needs, and length of stay were collected retrospectively through patient files and electronic record system. Albumin and lymphocyte measurements were taken within 48 hours before surgery and studied with an autoanalyzer in the hospital’s accredited clinical biochemistry laboratory; results were recorded in g/dL and/mm3.11-13 Until the postoperative 90th day, survival condition was confirmed by the hospital information system and the records of the central civil registry office. By the end of the twelfth month, survival information was completed through outpatient clinic visits and telephone. The data collection form was an abbreviated version of a clinically valid form used in previous hip fracture mortality studies; entries were made with double control.

Surgical Method and Intervention Protocol

Depending on the type of fracture and general condition, the patients underwent internal fixation, hemiarthroplasty or total hip replacement by the orthopaedic team. The time to reach surgery was calculated from the number of days between arrival at the emergency department and the incision. Although clinical guidelines recommend surgery within 24–48 hours of hospital admission (Ong and Vindlacheruvu, 2023) and meta-analytic evidence from 35 studies encompassing over 190,000 patients has demonstrated that early surgery is associated with significantly lower mortality (pooled OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.67–0.81) (Moja et al, 2012), the primary surgical delay threshold was defined as >5 days, determined empirically through ROC analysis (Youden-optimal point: 6.0 days) from the study dataset. This threshold was selected because 86.8% of patients in the cohort had surgical delay exceeding 48 hours (mean 6.4 ± 3.9 days), creating a highly imbalanced distribution that limited the discriminative capacity of the conventional 48-hour threshold (univariate OR 1.18, p = 0.707 vs OR 1.89, p = 0.029 for >5 days). Intensive care unit admission was defined as staying in the ICU for at least 5 days due to postoperative cardiac, pulmonary, neurological or metabolic problems, and this information was obtained from the files. The ≥5-day ICU threshold was derived empirically from the study data using ROC analysis and was consistent with the clinical observation that prolonged ICU stay in geriatric hip fracture patients typically reflects major postoperative complications requiring extended monitoring.

Variable Selection and Risk Score Construction

All candidate predictor variables were first screened using univariable logistic regression. Variables achieving statistical significance at the univariable level (p < 0.05) were considered for inclusion in the multivariable models. The final set of predictors was determined by combining univariable screening results with clinical reasoning and examination of potential collinearity between variables. Candidate predictor variables were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature on geriatric hip fracture mortality. Fracture type was included as a predictor because intracapsular fractures demonstrated significantly higher mortality due to disrupted femoral head blood supply and more complex surgical management. Surgical procedure type was not included as a model variable due to strong collinearity with fracture type, which would introduce multicollinearity and inflate variance in regression estimates. Six variables achieved statistical significance on univariable screening: ICU stay ≥5 days, age ≥85 years, ≥4 comorbidities, PNI <40, intracapsular fracture, and surgical delay >5 days. Similarly, ASA score and blood transfusion were evaluated at the univariate level but were not retained in the final models because ASA did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.063) and blood transfusion, while significant (p = 0.042), is a perioperative variable with substantial overlap with other model components.

A formal frailty index (e.g., Clinical Frailty Scale, Fried criteria) was not available in the retrospective dataset. However, the combination of age ≥85 years, ≥4 comorbidities, and PNI <40 captures key dimensions of frailty — physiological vulnerability, multimorbidity, and nutritional-immunological reserve — serving as a pragmatic surrogate. The absence of a dedicated frailty measure is acknowledged as a limitation.

Two Risk Score Models Were Constructed, Each Addressing a Different Clinical Decision Point

1. Preoperative model (score range 0–5): calculated at the time of admission using five variables available before surgery — PNI <40, age ≥85 years, ≥4 comorbidities, surgical delay >5 days, and intracapsular fracture. ICU stay was not included in this model as it is a postoperative variable unavailable at the time of admission. This model enables early risk stratification, informed consent discussions, and resource allocation planning.

1. Preoperative model (score range 0–5): calculated at the time of admission using five variables available before surgery — PNI <40, age ≥85 years, ≥4 comorbidities, surgical delay >5 days, and intracapsular fracture. ICU stay was not included in this model as it is a postoperative variable unavailable at the time of admission. This model enables early risk stratification, informed consent discussions, and resource allocation planning.

2. Combined model (score range 0–6): calculated after the postoperative period by adding ICU stay ≥5 days to the five preoperative variables. This model provides updated risk assessment incorporating the patient’s surgical course and allows postoperative care escalation decisions.

Each variable was assigned an equal weight of 1 point, creating unweighted integer-based scores. This equal-weighting approach was chosen over regression coefficient-based weighting for the following reasons: (1) all component odds ratios were of similar magnitude after dichotomization, reducing the benefit of differential weighting; (2) integer-based scores are simpler to calculate at the bedside without electronic tools; and (3) equal-weight scores have demonstrated comparable discrimination to complex weighted scores in similar geriatric prediction models. The trade-off between the logistic regression model AUC (preoperative 0.740, combined 0.759) and the simplified score AUC (preoperative 0.722, combined 0.737) was considered acceptable for clinical applicability.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., USA) and Python 3.9; packages included Statsmodels and Sklearn. Normality was checked with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were given as mean ± SD for clinical interpretability; the Mann-Whitney U test (two-sided) was used for group comparisons due to non-normal distributions. Categorical variables were presented as number and percentage and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test (two-sided) as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-sided; a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Precise p-values were reported to two decimal places for p ≥ 0.01 and to three decimal places for p < 0.01.

Candidate predictor variables were first screened by univariable logistic regression (Wald test, two-sided); variables achieving p < 0.05 were considered for inclusion in the multivariable models. To understand the relationship with mortality, univariate logistic regression (Wald test, two-sided) was performed first; then the preoperative model was built with five variables (PNI <40, age ≥85, ≥4 comorbidities, surgical delay >5 days, intracapsular fracture) and the combined model with six variables (preoperative variables plus ICU stay ≥5 days) entered simultaneously. For each variable, the ROC curve was drawn and AUC values were calculated.

The predictive power of PNI alone was also examined. In each multivariate model, a total risk score was created by giving 1 point to each factor; odds ratio was calculated to determine how the risk of mortality changed when the score increased by 1 point. Model calibration was checked with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and discrimination was checked with ROC-AUC, and a 95% confidence interval was given. Pseudo R2 was also reported. Brier scores were calculated to assess overall predictive accuracy. Survival was plotted with Kaplan-Meier curves stratified by preoperative risk score category (Low: 0–1, Moderate: 2–3, High: ≥4) and by PNI status (PNI <40 vs PNI ≥40), and the groups were compared with the log-rank test (two-sided). Internal validation was done with a thousand-sampling bootstrap and ten-fold cross-validation. Bootstrap optimism was calculated to assess model overfitting. Optimal cut-off points for risk stratification were determined using the maximum Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). Decision Curve Analysis was used to see clinical benefit across the 5%–50% threshold probability range. Separate ROC calculations were also performed in subgroup analyses for age, gender, PNI status, comorbidity burden, and fracture type. Confidence intervals for mortality rates were calculated using the Wilson score method. Since the missing data rate was less than five percent, list-wise exclusion was applied.

Ethical Considerations

This study entered into force with the approval of the Trakya University Faculty of Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee. Since it was retrospective, the written consent requirement was removed by the board, and patient IDs were completely deleted and processed in a coded manner. The whole process proceeded in accordance with the Helsinki principles and national legislation.

Results

General Characteristics of Patients and PNI Distribution

The average age of the 388 patients was around eighty. Women were the majority at seventy-three percent. The proportion of those with a PNI below forty was about two-thirds; the albumin and lymphocyte values of this group showed that they were eating worse. The mean number of comorbidities was 2.7, and the proportion of those with four or more comorbidities was 31.4%. The most common fracture type was intertrochanteric (63.1%), followed by intracapsular (32.7%) and subtrochanteric (4.1%). Surgical procedures were predominantly proximal femoral nail antirotation (62.4%) for extracapsular fractures and bipolar hemiarthroplasty (31.4%) for intracapsular fractures. While the proportion of those who stayed in the intensive care unit for more than five days was 19.1%, the mean preoperative time was 4.4 days. The rate of those who waited more than five days was 36.3%. This table revealed that the study group was an elderly population with multiple diseases and a late arrival for surgery (Table 1).

PNI Calculation and Clinical Threshold Value

PNI was calculated according to albumin and lymphocyte values: albumin was multiplied by 10, lymphocyte by 0.005 and summed. In the study, the threshold value was determined as forty. The mortality rate was found to be 17.7% in those with PNI below forty and 10.8% in those with PNI of forty or above. This difference suggested that low PNI may be associated with mortality. The mean PNI of all patients was 37.5, meaning that the vast majority were at risk in terms of nutrition and immunity (Table 1).

Univariate Predictors and Dual Model Development

Variables achieving statistical significance at the univariate level (p < 0.05) were considered candidate predictors for multivariate model development. Six variables were significantly associated with 90-day mortality on univariate analysis: ICU stay ≥5 days (OR 16.15, p < 0.001), age ≥85 years (OR 3.08, p < 0.001), ≥4 comorbidities (OR 2.81, p = 0.023), PNI <40 (OR 2.78, p = 0.036), intracapsular fracture (OR 2.37, p = 0.002), and surgical delay >5 days (OR 1.89, p = 0.029). Blood transfusion (OR 1.97, p = 0.042) was significant on univariate analysis but was not retained in the final models**; it was excluded owing to substantial overlap with other perioperative components and potential for collinearity. ASA score ≥3 did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.063) and was similarly excluded.** Two parallel multivariate models were developed to address the clinical need for both preoperative risk assessment and postoperative risk updating (Table 2).

Risk Score Distribution and Mortality Gradient

Of 388 enrolled patients, one was excluded from the risk score analysis due to missing fracture classification data, yielding an analytical sample of 387 patients with 60 deaths (15.5%). Each risk factor contributed one point: PNI <40, age ≥85 years, ≥4 comorbidities, surgical delay >5 days, and intracapsular fracture (preoperative score range 0–5); the combined score additionally included ICU stay ≥5 days (range 0–6). Mortality increased progressively with both scores: 0.0% at score 0, 5.3% at score 1, 12.2–12.8% at score 2, 20.0–22.6% at score 3, and 48.3–52.0% at score 4. In the combined model, four patients scored 5 and all died (100%). We divided patients into three categories: 0–1 points “low”, 2–3 points “moderate”, ≥4 points “high”. The mortality rate in the low-risk group was 4.3%, the moderate-risk group ranged from 15.5% to 17.0%, and high-risk patients had mortality of 52.0–54.5%. This distribution showed that both models successfully made the distinction between “low–moderate–high” risk and would be useful when making decisions in the clinic (Table 3) (Figure 1).

Model Validation

The preoperative risk score achieved an apparent AUC of 0.722, while the combined risk score achieved 0.737 (Figure 2). Both models demonstrated adequate calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow test: preoperative χ2 = 1.30, df = 3, p = 0.729; combined χ2 = 3.69, df = 4, p = 0.450). Internal validation confirmed model stability: bootstrap bias-corrected AUC was 0.721 and 0.736, ten-fold cross-validation AUC was 0.720 and 0.736, and bootstrap optimism was 0.001 and 0.000 for the preoperative and combined models, respectively. Brier scores were 0.117 (preoperative) and 0.112 (combined). The observed versus predicted mortality rates were closely aligned across all score levels, confirming that both models were well-calibrated and internally valid (Table 3).

At the twelve-month follow-up, those with high PNI lived longer. While survival reached 85% at the end of the 12th month in those with PNI ≥40, this rate remained at 70% in the PNI low group. The log-rank test found the difference significant (p = 0.036). In short, low PNI was associated with worse survival at both 90 days and one year (Table 4).

Subgroup Performance and Sensitivity Analysis

Both models performed consistently across clinically relevant subgroups. The combined model achieved higher AUC values in females (0.745 vs 0.716 in males), patients with PNI ≥40 (0.791), and those with intracapsular fractures (0.733). Performance was expectedly lower in the small subgroup with ≥4 comorbidities (AUC 0.596, n = 25), reflecting limited sample size rather than model failure (Table 5).

A sensitivity analysis comparing the >48-hour versus >5-day surgical delay threshold confirmed the superiority of the latter. In this cohort, 86.8% of patients had surgical delay exceeding 48 hours (mean 6.4 ± 3.9 days), resulting in a highly imbalanced distribution that limited the discriminative capacity of the 48-hour threshold (univariate OR 1.18, p = 0.707). In contrast, the >5-day threshold was significantly associated with mortality (univariate OR 1.89, p = 0.029) and yielded superior model performance (combined AUC 0.737 vs 0.720, ΔAUC = 0.017). The >5-day threshold was determined empirically by ROC analysis with a Youden-optimal point of 6.0 days (Table 5).

The PNI threshold of 40 was consistent with the existing literature, although its AUC alone was modest at 0.559. This confirmed that PNI functions as a contributory rather than standalone predictor, underscoring the value of the multi-component scoring approach (Table 2).

Twelve-Month Survival by Risk Category

Kaplan–Meier analysis stratified by preoperative risk score category demonstrated significant differences in 12-month overall survival across the three groups (log-rank p < 0.001) (Figure 4). Survival at 12 months was 86.1% in the low-risk group (score 0–1; n = 115), 68.8% in the moderate-risk group (score 2–3; n = 247), and 38.4% in the high-risk group (score ≥4; n = 25). The survival gap between risk categories widened progressively over the follow-up period, with the high-risk group reaching a 12-month mortality of 61.6% — a pattern consistent with the 90-day mortality gradient observed in Table 3 and suggesting that the preoperative score retains prognostic relevance beyond the primary endpoint.

Discussion

The research developed a risk assessment system which combines preoperative and perioperative elements to predict ninety-day mortality rates for elderly patients who have hip fractures. Although PNI alone demonstrated limited discriminative ability in this cohort, the multivariate model achieved acceptable clinical performance by integrating age, comorbidity burden, fracture type, surgical delay, and ICU length of stay. It should be noted that the risk factors included in this model — age, comorbidity burden, and baseline nutritional status — are largely non-modifiable. Therefore, this score is best understood as a prognostic tool for risk communication, expectation setting, and care planning, rather than as a means to directly modify patient outcomes. The risk score elevation enables doctors to detect patients with elevated mortality risk during early stages of treatment.

The researchers used this formula to calculate PNI through the following mathematical process: [10 × albumin (g/dL)] + [0.005 × total lymphocytes (/mm3)] with 40 as the threshold to detect patients with poor nutritional status. The mean PNI was 37.5 ± 6.4 in all patients, and 64.1% of the patients were below this threshold. The predictive performance of PNI alone in predicting 90-day mortality was found to be poor (AUC: 0.559). The AUC values reported for PNI in the literature are higher than our finding. The study by Kim et al determined total mortality AUC at 0.70 while identifying 39.2 as the optimal threshold value. 14 The study by Popp et al determined the 3-month mortality AUC at 0.66 while showing that 60.7% of patients had PNI values below 40. 15 The study by Arslan et al calculated an AUC of 0.660 for hospital death but used 32.5 as their threshold value and demonstrated PNI does not predict mortality independently but does predict the need for intensive care. 16 The study by Chen et al established PNI AUC at 0.727 for three-year follow-up and identified 45.275 as the best threshold value. 17 The high cut-off value in this study may be explained by the better nutritional status of the population (mean PNI: 47.5).

The low AUC value in our study may be related to the fact that the vast majority of our population is already at risk of malnutrition. The fact that the mean of PNI remains at a low level of 37.5 seems to have made it difficult for this index to adequately distinguish between risky patients. Similarly, Popp et al emphasized that high prevalence of malnutrition reduces the specificity of scores. 15 These findings highlight the limitations of using PNI alone and support the need for multivariate models in mortality prediction.

The present study developed two parallel risk score models serving distinct clinical purposes. The preoperative model (five components: PNI <40, age ≥85, ≥4 comorbidities, surgical delay >5 days, intracapsular fracture) achieved an AUC of 0.722 and applies at the time of admission for early risk stratification. The combined model, which additionally incorporates ICU stay ≥5 days as a postoperative variable, achieved an AUC of 0.737 and is applied after surgery to refine the risk estimate once perioperative data become available. The model calibration was found to be excellent (Hosmer-Lemeshow p = 0.450), and each 1-point increase in the risk score increased the risk of mortality by 2.68-fold (95% CI 1.96–3.68, p < 0.001) for the combined model. The individual component AUC values were as follows: age ≥85: 0.633; intracapsular fracture: 0.602; ICU ≥5 days: 0.577; surgical delay >5 days: 0.577; PNI <40: 0.559; ≥4 comorbidities: 0.541.

Our findings are consistent with other mortality prediction models in the literature. Hjelholt et al reported the AUC as 0.74 in the 1-year mortality model they developed using Danish national data and confirmed the superiority of combined modeling by emphasizing that even the strongest individual factors such as age have lower AUC values (0.65). 18 Cao et al similarly obtained AUC 0.74 in the logistic regression model they developed for 30-day mortality. 19 In the same study, it was reported that patients with an RCRI score of 3 had an OR 2,948-fold increased risk of mortality compared to patients with an RCRI score of 0, the reference group; this value is quite consistent with the finding of OR 2.68 per point in our model. 19 Forssten et al found the AUC of the logistic regression model to be 0.74 in their study comparing machine learning methods and showed that this value prevailed over other algorithms. 20 de Jong et al, on the other hand, showed slightly higher performance than our model with an AUC of 0.775 in the external validation cohort. 21

Perioperative factors in our model contribute significantly to risk. ICU hospitalization ≥5 days reached OR 9.37 (95% CI 2.63–33.38) as the strongest predictor in the combined model. We acknowledge that prolonged ICU stay is a postoperative variable that functions, at least in part, as a downstream outcome marker rather than a true preoperative risk factor. Its inclusion in the combined model may therefore introduce a degree of circularity and inflate model discrimination. For this reason, we present both a preoperative model (AUC: 0.722) and a combined model (AUC: 0.737) separately, allowing clinicians to use the preoperative score at admission and update the risk estimate postoperatively when ICU data become available. This two-stage approach ensures that the model retains clinical utility at both decision points. Although studies in the literature have mostly focused on preoperative factors, Cao et al reported OR 4,229 for ASA class 4; this supports the increase in mortality of high-risk patients. 19 Similarly, de Jong et al found OR 8.72 for ASA 4 and OR 3.18 for dementia. 21 These findings suggest that perioperative variables contribute significantly to mortality prediction and that combined models offer a more comprehensive evaluation than models based on preoperative factors alone.

The risk score created in our study divided the patients into low (0–1 points), moderate (2–3 points) and high (≥4 points) risk groups. This grouping showed clinically significant mortality differences: 90-day mortality was found at a rate of 4.3% in the low-risk group, 15.5% in the intermediate-risk group, and 54.5% in the high-risk group. Although the high-risk group consists of a small number of patients (n = 33), it represents a population that requires heightened prognostic awareness and may benefit from intensified care planning, goals-of-care discussions, and multidisciplinary coordination.

Studies in the literature have generally reported lower mortality rates. Schuijt et al found the 90-day mortality rate to be 15% in the high-risk group with VMS scoring and reported the AUC value as 0.65. 22 In the study conducted by the same authors in orthogeriatric fracture patients over the age of 85, the 30-day mortality remained at the level of 12%. 23 In the study in which the U-HIP model was developed, in-hospital mortality was reported as 4% and AUC was reported as 0.77. 24 In the external validation of this model, in-hospital mortality was found to be 4.4% and the c-statistic was 0.74 in the overall cohort. 25

The significantly higher mortality rates in our high-risk group (54.5%) are quite different from these values in the literature. This difference can be explained by the fact that our study targeted 90-day mortality and the high-risk group reflected a much more selective population.

Our Decision Curve Analysis results showed that the model is useful for clinical decision-making in the threshold probability range of 5–55%, with the highest net benefit in the range of 5–30%. However, DCA was not performed in the studies we compared.22,24,25 This suggests that the clinical benefit analysis of our model makes a unique contribution to the literature.

Score ≥3, which we have determined as the optimal cut-off, provides a sensitivity of 0.63 and specificity of 0.71 for the combined model (Youden index: 0.343). PPV ≥15% and NPV 91.3% have been reported in the literature at the Score 2 threshold for VMS, indicating that the score is useful in safely separating low-risk patients. 22 The Parker Mobility Score study established PMS ≤6 as the critical point which helps doctors decide if patients need rehabilitation or care facilities after hospital release. 26

The surgical delay threshold used in this study merits discussion. In our cohort, the mean time to surgery was 4.4 ± 3.4 days, and 86.8% of patients had surgical delays exceeding 48 hours. This high rate of delay limited the discriminative capacity of the commonly used 48-hour threshold: when the >48-hour cut-off was tested in sensitivity analysis, the univariate OR was 1.18 (95% CI 0.50–2.75, p = 0.707), and the combined model AUC decreased to 0.720. The >5-day threshold, determined empirically by ROC analysis (Youden-optimal point: 6.0 days), demonstrated superior discriminative performance (ΔAUC = 0.017 for the combined model). International guidelines generally recommend surgery within 24–48 hours. Simunovic et al, in their systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 prospective studies encompassing 13,478 patients, reported that early surgery was associated with a 19% reduction in all-cause mortality based on pooled adjusted estimates from five studies (4,208 patients, 721 deaths; RR 0.81, 95% CI 0.68–0.96, p = 0.01); early surgery was also associated with reduced rates of pneumonia (RR 0.59, 95% CI 0.37–0.93) and pressure sores (RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.34–0.69). 7 Klestil et al subsequently confirmed these findings in an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of 28 prospective observational studies encompassing 31,242 patients, showing that surgery within 48 hours was associated with a 20% lower 12-month mortality risk based on adjusted data from four studies (2,396 patients; RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.66–0.97); adjusted data also demonstrated fewer perioperative complications (8% vs. 17%) in patients who had early surgery. 27 While both meta-analyses support the 48-hour benchmark, these thresholds were derived from healthcare systems with substantially shorter time-to-surgery distributions. The prolonged delays observed in our setting likely reflect systemic barriers including limited operating room availability, preoperative optimization needs, and institutional resource constraints, which may explain why a longer threshold was required to achieve adequate discrimination in our population.

The 12-month survival rates between patients with PNI less than 40 and those with PNI 40 or higher showed a significant difference at 70% versus 85% respectively (log-rank p = 0.036). The results demonstrate that nutritional status acts as a significant prognostic factor for long-term patient survival outcomes. Although PNI did not retain independent significance in the multivariate combined model (OR 2.10, 95% CI 0.77–5.68, p = 0.146), the Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrates its prognostic value over a longer follow-up period, suggesting that the effect of malnutrition on mortality may accumulate beyond the 90-day window. Furthermore, Kaplan–Meier analysis stratified by preoperative risk score category demonstrated a pronounced gradient in 12-month survival: 86.1% in the low-risk group (score 0–1), 68.8% in the moderate-risk group (score 2–3), and 38.4% in the high-risk group (score ≥4) (log-rank p < 0.001; Figure 4). This survival gradient mirrors the 90-day mortality pattern observed across risk categories, and the separation between groups persisted through the full 12-month follow-up, suggesting that admission-stage risk classification carries meaningful prognostic information well beyond the primary outcome window. Dagnelie et al found the 1-year mortality of the patients they evaluated with MNA to be 15.4% in the malnourished group and 3.4% in the well-nourished group, showing that nutrition is a strong prognostic factor. 28 Malafarina et al also reported in their review that malnutrition increased 1-year mortality after hip fracture by up to 30%, and low BMI worsened survival by up to seven times. 29

The performance of our model differed according to the subgroups. While AUC was 0.737 in patients with fewer than 4 comorbidities, this value decreased to 0.596 in the small group with ≥4 comorbidities (n = 25). This can be partly explained by the sample size. However, the literature also shows that the burden of comorbidity complicates the estimation of mortality. Jürisson et al found that the 1-year risk of excess mortality increased to 30.3% in patients with high comorbidity (CCI ≥3) and that the effect of comorbidity appeared in a shorter time at older age. 30 Ensrud et al reported that the 5-year mortality reached 36.1% in women with ≥3 comorbidities. 31 These findings suggest that patients with multiple comorbidities constitute a more heterogeneous group and it is difficult for a single model to make adequate discrimination in this group.

Model performance (AUC: 0.745) was found to be higher in female patients than in male patients (AUC: 0.716). AUC differences by sex have not been specifically examined in the compared studies.28,29 However, it is known that the majority of hip fracture populations are women; Malafarina et al reported this rate as 73.5%. 29 Higher performance in women may be related to the higher number of female patients in the sample and therefore the better training of the model in this group.

This study has several important limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center design inherently limits the generalizability of the findings. Selection bias, information bias, and unmeasured confounding cannot be fully excluded in a retrospective cohort. External validation in a prospective, multicenter setting is necessary before the score can be recommended for routine clinical use.

Second, frailty — a well-established predictor of adverse outcomes in elderly hip fracture patients — was not assessed in this study. Validated frailty instruments such as the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) or the Frailty Index (FI) were not available in the retrospective dataset and therefore could not be included among the predictors. This is an important gap, as frailty may confound or modify the associations observed in our model, and future studies should incorporate standardized frailty assessments.

Third, a formal a priori sample size or power calculation was not performed. Although the events-per-variable ratio (60 events for 5–6 predictors, yielding approximately 10–12 EPV) meets the commonly cited minimum threshold, the study may be underpowered to detect smaller effect sizes, particularly in subgroup analyses where the high-risk group comprised only 33 patients.

Fourth, as discussed above, prolonged ICU stay (≥5 days) is a postoperative variable that may partly reflect disease severity and complications rather than serving as an independent preoperative predictor. Its inclusion in the combined model introduces potential circularity, as patients who are sicker are more likely both to have prolonged ICU stays and to die. We have addressed this by presenting the preoperative model separately, which excludes ICU data and can be applied at admission.

Fifth, certain variables that may influence mortality — such as ASA score, cognitive status, prefracture functional level, and specific surgical technique — were either not significant in univariate analysis (ASA ≥3: OR 1.79, p = 0.063) or were not included due to collinearity with existing predictors (e.g., surgical procedure type was strongly collinear with fracture type). The feature selection process was based on statistical significance thresholds and clinical judgment, which may have excluded potentially relevant confounders.

Sixth, no external validation was performed. While internal validation methods (bootstrap, 10-fold cross-validation) demonstrated minimal optimism (bias-corrected AUC: 0.736 vs. apparent AUC: 0.737), these methods cannot substitute for external validation in an independent population with potentially different case-mix and healthcare delivery characteristics.

Finally, the surgical delay threshold of >5 days used in this study deviates considerably from the 24–48 hour benchmarks recommended by international guidelines and supported by meta-analytic evidence (Simunovic et al, 2010; Klestil et al, 2018). While this threshold was empirically derived and demonstrated superior discriminative performance in our cohort, it reflects the specific clinical context of our institution and may not be generalizable to settings with shorter time-to-surgery distributions.

Despite these limitations, the study achieves several strengths through its model which combines preoperative and perioperative factors and demonstrates model consistency through internal validation analyses, decision curve analysis showing net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, and the provision of both a preoperative and combined scoring system to accommodate different clinical decision points. The model needs validation through upcoming research at different medical facilities to establish its usefulness for medical practice.

Conclusion

In this retrospective, single-center cohort of elderly hip fracture patients, a simple integer-based risk score combining nutritional, demographic, clinical, and perioperative factors was associated with acceptable discriminative performance and good calibration for predicting 90-day mortality. The model stratified patients into clinically meaningful risk categories with distinct mortality gradients across low-, moderate-, and high-risk groups. The preoperative five-factor model — incorporating PNI <40, age ≥85 years, ≥4 comorbidities, surgical delay >5 days, and intracapsular fracture — applies at admission, while the combined six-factor model, which adds ICU stay ≥5 days, applies postoperatively to update the risk estimate. However, because the included risk factors are largely non-modifiable, both scores should be regarded as prognostic tools for risk communication, expectation management, family counseling, and care planning — rather than as direct interventions to reduce mortality. The identification of high-risk patients at an early stage may facilitate timely goals-of-care discussions, multidisciplinary team involvement, and appropriate allocation of healthcare resources. Prospective, multicenter external validation — ideally incorporating frailty assessments and standardized surgical timing benchmarks — is needed before this score can be recommended for routine clinical implementation.
