An fNIRS hyperscanning dataset on the modulation of synchrony by social relationship during social touch
Authors: Yanan Chen, Huihui Niu, Na Ao, Jingwen Xu, Qiyue Ming, Xiyue Teng, Bo Yang, Ziyi Lian, Xiaowei Jiang, Yunkai Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Social touch from a close companion, such as a friend, can effectively buffer stress, an effect modulated by the nature of the social relationship. However, the neural mechanisms underlying this modulation, particularly the dynamics between interacting brains, remain poorly understood due to a lack of suitable open data. We present a functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) hyperscanning dataset from 47 female dyads (24 friend pairs, 23 stranger pairs) who jointly viewed negative and neutral images under touching and no-touching conditions. The dataset includes raw and preprocessed fNIRS data, trial-by-trial emotional valence and arousal ratings, and extensive trait- and state-based questionnaire data. Technical validation, including power spectral density analysis, confirms our preprocessing pipeline effectively yields high-quality signals. A preliminary validation analysis of the dataset confirms that interpersonal touch effectively buffers against negative emotional experiences by enhancing inter-brain synchrony. This rich, multi-modal dataset is publicly available on the Open Science Framework and provides a unique resource for investigating how social relationships shape the neurobehavioral dynamics of social support.

Background & Summary

Social touch, as a cornerstone of non-verbal communication, is central to human social connection and emotional regulation. From seeking comfort in infancy to expressing intimacy in adulthood, physical contact plays an indispensable role in shaping our psychological well-being, strengthening social bonds, and conveying complex emotional intentions1–3. A large body of research has confirmed that supportive touch from others can effectively buffer an individual’s physiological and psychological responses to stress and negative emotions, for instance, by reducing cortisol levels, slowing heart rate, and increasing subjective well-being4–6.

However, the effects of social touch are not universally uniform; the depth and valence of its impact depend largely on the social relationship between the individuals involved. A hug from an intimate partner or a friend may bring profound comfort and security, whereas the same act from a stranger might elicit discomfort or even alarm7,8. This phenomenon has received preliminary validation in neuroscience research: for example, when facing a threat, holding a husband’s hand reduces threat-related brain activity more effectively than holding a stranger’s hand9,10. This reveals a critical scientific question: How exactly does the degree of social closeness modulate brain activity to alter the emotional meaning of touch as a physical signal? Although we know that relationships are important, the underlying neural mechanisms, especially in realistic interactive contexts, remain a significant knowledge gap.

Traditional neuroscience paradigms, such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), typically place individuals in isolated settings for scanning, which presents a fundamental limitation when studying inherently interactive social behaviors. The neural underpinnings of social touch cannot be reduced to the processing of a single, “passive” brain but should be understood as the result of dynamic coupling between two brains during an interaction11,12. To overcome this bottleneck, functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) hyperscanning, a technique with higher ecological validity, has emerged. Compared to traditional single-brain imaging, fNIRS hyperscanning offers significant advantages in motion tolerance and ecological validity, making it particularly suitable for investigating social neuroscience in naturalistic, interactive scenarios13,14. In recent years, fNIRS hyperscanning has been widely applied to study various social interactions, for instance, in parent-child dyads, romantic partners, and collaborating peers15–22 — consistently revealing the phenomenon of inter-brain synchrony (IBS). Importantly, recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have confirmed that IBS detected via fNIRS hyperscanning in close relationships and naturalistic interaction contexts is robust, replicable, and consistent across studies23,24.

Although hyperscanning has brought a revolutionary method to social neuroscience, there is currently a lack of open datasets specifically designed to investigate how social relationships modulate the emotion-buffering effect of touch. To fill this gap, we constructed and now present this dataset. We employed fNIRS hyperscanning with high ecological validity to record the brain activity of 47 female dyads (comprising 24 pairs of close friends and 23 pairs of strangers) as they jointly experienced an emotional challenge (viewing negative pictures). We restricted the sample to females for two reasons. First, large-scale and experimental evidence indicates that women use and tolerate social touch more (or with greater diversity) than men25. Second, women exhibit higher social touch sensitivity—showing higher pleasantness ratings to CT-optimal stroking in meta-analytic data26 and lower thresholds in objective tactile tasks27. Limiting the sample reduces sex-related variance and increases power; generalization across sexes is reserved for future work. This experiment manipulated the core variable of “social touch”; in some trials, one participant was required to provide comfort to their partner by touching their hand. In addition to fNIRS data, this dataset also includes rich subjective emotional ratings, personality trait questionnaires, and relationship quality assessments, providing a unique resource for an in-depth exploration of the links between behavior, traits, and inter-brain neural dynamics. As demonstrated in the Technical Validation section, analyses of this dataset have successfully uncovered a neural mechanism for touch-based emotion regulation, proving its significant potential for future research28.

The value of this dataset lies in that it provides researchers, for the first time, with a means to directly test whether inter-brain synchrony is the key neural mechanism mediating the influence of social relationships on the effects of touch in a social support context. We believe that this open data resource will significantly advance the field of social neuroscience and deepen our understanding of the neural underpinnings of humanity’s most fundamental form of social comfort—supportive touch.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the ethics committee of Henan University (20200702002). All participants provided written informed consent before their participation. The informed consent form explicitly stated that participants’ personal information would be kept strictly confidential, all collected data would be processed in an anonymized format, and may be shared publicly for future scientific research.

Participants

A total of 47 female dyads were recruited via an online registration system and assigned to one of two groups: a friend group (24 dyads; Mage = 19.79, SD = 1.70) and a stranger group (23 dyads; Mage = 19.93, SD = 1.40). The recruitment criteria for friend dyads stipulated that partners must have known each other for a minimum of six months and mutually identified each other as one of their five closest friends. Stranger dyads consisted of participants who were randomly paired after confirming they had no prior acquaintance.

General inclusion criteria for all participants were right-handedness, heterosexual orientation, normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and no self-reported history of psychiatric disorders. Participants were instructed to refrain from consuming alcohol and to avoid sleep deprivation for 24 hours before the experiment. All individuals provided written informed consent prior to participation and received a compensation of 50 RMB upon completion.

Stimuli

Stimuli were selected from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS)29. The stimulus set comprised 80 negative and 80 neutral images, which were evenly divided between the two experimental sessions (touch and no-touch). After each image presentation, participants rated its valence (from negative to positive feelings) and arousal (from calm to excited)30. To validate the stimulus materials, we first confirmed that the normative ratings of the selected negative and neutral images were significantly different. As expected, negative images had significantly lower valence (M = 2.35, SD = 0.43) than neutral images (M = 5.09, SD = 0.52), t(158) = −36.70, p < 0.001. Conversely, negative images elicited significantly higher arousal (M = 5.78, SD = 0.79) than neutral images (M = 3.86, SD = 1.01), t(158) = −13.46, p < 0.001.

We then verified that the stimulus subsets used in the touch and no-touch sessions were equivalent. For negative images, there were no significant differences between the touch and no-touch subsets in either valence (Mtouch = 2.32, SD = 0.44 vs. Mno-touch = 2.38, SD = 0.43; t(78) = 0.61, p = 0.54) or arousal (Mtouch = 5.80, SD = 0.79 vs. Mno-touch = 5.81, SD = 0.80; t(78) = 0.30, p = 0.76). Similarly, for neutral images, there were no significant differences between the subsets in valence (Mtouch = 5.13, SD = 0.45 vs. Mno-touch = 5.04, SD = 0.57; t(78) = −0.79, p = 0.43) or arousal (Mtouch = 3.78, SD = 1.01 vs. Mno-touch = 3.93, SD = 0.95; t(78) = 0.67, p = 0.50).

Stimuli were presented using MATLAB 2020 on a 1920 × 1080 pixel screen. All environmental factors, such as room temperature and screen brightness, were held constant across all participants.

Interpersonal reactivity index (IRI-C)

Dispositional empathy was assessed using the Chinese version of the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI-C). The scale comprises 22 items rated on a five-point Likert scale (e.g., from 1 = “does not describe me well” to 5 = “describes me very well”), with no reverse-scored items. It measures four distinct dimensions of empathy: Perspective-Taking (PT), Fantasy (FS), Empathic Concern (EC), and Personal Distress (PD). The reported Cronbach’s α coefficients for the subscales in the Chinese validation study ranged from 0.72 to 0.7831.

Rosenberg self-esteem scale (RSES)

Global self-esteem was measured using a validated Chinese version of the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES). The scale consists of 10 items assessing an individual’s overall self-worth (e.g., “On the whole, I am satisfied with myself”). Participants responded on a four-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree”, with five items being reverse-scored. This finding is in line with the high reliability reported in large-scale international validations (e.g., Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.77 to 0.88)32.

Self-rating depression scale (SDS)

Depressive symptomatology was measured with a validated Chinese version of the Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS). This 20-item scale assesses both affective and somatic symptoms of depression on a four-point Likert scale (from “a little of the time” to “most of the time”). Ten items (2, 5, 6, 11, 12, 14, 16, 17, 18, 20) are reverse-scored. The SDS has demonstrated good internal reliability, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.82 and a split-half reliability of 0.79 (corrected for test length)33.

Self-rating anxiety scale (SAS)

Anxiety symptoms were assessed using a validated Chinese version of the Zung Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS)34. The scale comprises 20-items measuring both psychological and somatic symptoms of anxiety. Participants respond on a four-point Likert scale (from “a little of the time” to “most of the time”). Five of the items are positively worded and therefore reverse-scored before calculating a total score. The scale has demonstrated solid psychometric properties, with Cronbach’s α values ranging from 0.82 to 0.85 in various studies34.

Positive and negative affect schedule (PANAS)

State positive affect (PA) and negative affect (NA) were measured at three time points using a validated Chinese version of the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS)35. The scale requires participants to rate the extent to which they are currently experiencing 10 positive and 10 negative emotions on a five-point Likert scale (1 = “very slightly or not at all” to 5 = “extremely”). Internal consistency reliability for the PA and NA subscales ranges from 0.85 to 0.90 for PA and 0.84 to 0.87 for NA. Test-retest reliability over an 8-week period yielded correlations of 0.54 for PA and 0.45 for NA35.

Friendship quality scale (FQS)

Perceived friendship quality was assessed exclusively within the friend dyads using the Chinese version of the College Student Friendship Quality Scale36. The 15-item scale measures four dimensions of friendship: help and support, friend conflict, partnership, and intimacy. The α coefficient of the scale is 0.75, and the α coefficient of each dimension is between 0.585–0.71. The test-retest reliability is 0.7936.

Big five inventory-60 (BFI-60)

The Big Five personality traits were measured using a validated Chinese version of the 60-item Big Five Inventory (BFI-60)37. The scale assesses the dimensions of Extraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, Neuroticism, and Openness to Experience. The BFI-60 has been validated in multiple languages, demonstrating consistent factor structures across cultures, with reliability coefficients for the dimensions ranging from 0.75 to 0.8837.

Procedure

Upon arrival, participants provided informed consent and were situated in the experimental setup. The overall procedure consisted of three phases: a baseline assessment, the main fNIRS experiment, and a post-experiment assessment.

Experimental design and task

The study employed a 2 (touch: hand-holding vs. no-touch) × 2 (picture type: negative vs. neutral) × 2 (relationship: friend vs. stranger) mixed-factorial design. Touch and picture type were within-subject factors, while relationship was a between-subjects factor. The entire duration of the fNIRS acquisition was approximately 40 minutes.

During fNIRS recording, dyads were seated side-by-side, each facing an individual screen to prevent visual or verbal communication (Fig. 1B). In the hand-holding condition, participants were explicitly instructed to hold their partner’s hand continuously throughout each block, maintaining gentle contact without additional movement or communication. The experimenter monitored compliance in real time to ensure consistency across dyads. According to Hertenstein, even in the absence of visual communication, touch can transmit emotions1. The experiment consisted of two main sessions: a no-touching session followed by a touching session. fNIRS Data acquisition

The activity of the prefrontal cortex (PFC) was measured using the NIRX Scout 32 × 32 device (United States), as the PFC is a region of particular interest in studies of emotional regulation and interpersonal interactions. Data acquisition was performed using NIRStar 15.2 software. The device captured near-infrared light at wavelengths of 785 nm and 830 nm, corresponding to measurements of deoxyhemoglobin (HbR) and oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO), respectively. The setup maintained a consistent distance of 25–35 mm between the source and detector, with a data sampling rate set at 7.8125 Hz to optimally capture neural activity. A two-person prefrontal template (8 sources × 7 detectors) was used, covering the PFC with a total of 20 channels per individual, as depicted in Fig. 2. The channel configuration is based on the 10–20 international standard system of EEG, and analogous brain regions recorded by fNIRS were identified using this system39. Prior fNIRS researches have used the same approach for fNIRS channel configuration and brain region analogues10. Data preprocessing

Preprocessing of the fNIRS data was conducted using MNE-Python. Initially, raw optical intensity was converted to optical density to facilitate the assessment of data quality using the scalp coupling index (SCI)40. Channels with an SCI value less than 0.5 were marked as bad channels. Furthermore, all channels have a length between 1 and 3 cm41. Raw data were first transformed into optical density measurements for each participant. Temporal derivative distribution repair (TDDR) was then applied to remove artifacts from motion42.

Subsequently, optical density data were converted into concentrations of HbO and HbR using the Beer-Lambert law. A band-pass filter ranging from 0.01 Hz to 0.2 Hz was applied to remove low-frequency noise, such as slow drifts and heartbeat artifacts43. Physiological artifacts were further addressed by applying a negative correlation enhancement method44. Following Mayseless, oxyhemoglobin concentration (HbO) was considered a more sensitive indicator of changes in cerebral blood flow45. Therefore, epochs of 4 seconds were extracted, including 2 seconds before and 2 seconds after the image presentation. The 2 seconds before the image served as a baseline for baseline correction. Epochs with ΔHbO over 20μmol were excluded.

Technical Validation

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7) to determine the required sample size. Based on previous similar studies, we aimed to detect a medium effect size (f = 0.25) for the within-between interaction in a mixed-design ANOVA. With an α level of 0.05 and a desired power of 0.95, the analysis indicated that a total sample of at least 36 dyads was required. Our final sample of 47 dyads therefore provides excellent statistical power to detect the effects of interest. During preprocessing, channels with a SCI below 0.5 and epochs with ΔHbO exceeding 20 μmol were automatically marked as bad and excluded from further analysis. No channels were excluded, as all had an SCI value greater than 0.5. In total, 64 epochs were rejected across all participants based on the ΔHbO criterion. The effectiveness of this automated procedure was subsequently confirmed through manual visual inspection of the preprocessed data.

To validate the efficacy of our fNIRS data preprocessing pipeline, we performed a comparative analysis of the power spectral density (PSD) of the signal before and after preprocessing. The resulting visualization (see Fig. 3) clearly demonstrates that our pipeline, particularly the band-pass filtering step, successfully attenuated physiological noise and low-frequency signal drift present in the raw data. To further validate the quality and usability of the dataset, we conducted an illustrative empirical analysis using the same data, which has been reported in a separate preprint28. This analysis aimed to confirm that the behavioral and neural patterns derived from the dataset align with theoretically expected effects of interpersonal touch and affective regulation9,47. Specifically, we examined how interpersonal touch modulates subjective emotional experience and IBS within the prefrontal cortex.

Consistent with previous findings on social touch48,49, participants reported significantly lower arousal and higher valence toward negative emotional stimuli during the touch condition relative to the no-touch condition, suggesting a robust buffering effect of touch on negative affect50,51. In parallel, IBS showed a reliable increase in prefrontal synchrony when dyads engaged in hand-holding, reflecting enhanced neural coupling between partners. These effects were consistently observed across both friend and stranger dyads, supporting the stability of the measurement across social contexts38.

Beyond simple replication of expected patterns, mediation analyses revealed that IBS partially mediated the relationship between interpersonal touch and arousal to negative stimuli, indicating that greater neural coupling is a plausible pathway through which touch alleviates emotional reactivity52. This mediating pattern further demonstrates that the dataset not only captures high-quality signals but also enables advanced modeling of interpersonal and affective dynamics.

Together, these illustrative results confirm that the dataset yields theoretically coherent behavioral and neural outcomes, validating its potential for secondary analyses such as multilevel modeling of interpersonal coupling, cross-brain connectivity, or affective state prediction. Full analytic details and visualizations are reported in Chen et al.28.
