Implementation fidelity to the World Health Organization package of essential non-communicable disease interventions (PEN) during scale-up in Nepal’s primary healthcare system: a mixed methods study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe World Health Organization package of essential non-communicable disease interventions (PEN) comprises evidence-based interventions for primary healthcare systems in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Implementation fidelity, defined as the degree to which an intervention is delivered as intended, is crucial for intervention effectiveness. Yet, across LMICs, evidence on fidelity to PEN is scarce and mostly limited to pilot studies.MethodsWe evaluated fidelity to PEN during national scale-up in Nepal using a convergent parallel mixed methods study design based on the Conceptual Framework for Implementation Fidelity. Data were collected in 2021 from 105 randomly selected primary healthcare facilities across all seven provinces of Nepal. Using direct observation of 172 non-communicable disease (NCD) patient visits, we quantified health service providers’ adherence to PEN protocol 1 (hypertension and diabetes management) and protocol 2 (lifestyle counselling). We calculated overall fidelity scores and examined their potential determinants in multivariable regression models using generalized estimating equations. Moreover, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 47 providers and 33 NCD patients to elicit their views on factors affecting fidelity. We analyzed verbatim interview transcripts using thematic analysis.ResultsPEN-trained health service providers managing hypertension and diabetes patients (protocol 1) mainly focused on asking about medication (76% of patient visits), measuring blood pressure (82%), and adjusting medication (85%). Blood glucose was measured in 28% of eligible patient contacts. Other care steps, including cardiovascular risk estimation, were mostly skipped. Lifestyle counselling for NCD patients (protocol 2) most frequently entailed salt restriction advice (41%) and instructions on medications (62%) and follow-up appointments (69%). Overall fidelity scores for protocols 1 and 2 were 20.8 and 22.1 out of 100, respectively. In multivariable regressions, prior PEN training was associated with 25% (95%-CI: 1–54%; p = 0.04) and 32% (95%-CI: -4–80%; p = 0.08) higher fidelity scores for protocols 1 and 2, respectively. Qualitative data suggested that providers and patients were generally engaged by the intervention, though various factors impede fidelity, including negative user experiences and limited availability of medical supplies. Strategies to improve fidelity to PEN that participants suggested were intervention manuals, supervision and monitoring visits, brief refresher trainings, and community outreach programs.ConclusionsImplementation fidelity to PEN during scale-up in Nepal was often limited to a few diagnostic and therapeutic actions. Next to enhancing user experience, ensuring a reliable supply of diagnostic equipment and medicines will likely be crucial to sustainably improve NCD care. Background

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are the leading causes of mortality and disability worldwide [1]. There is an urgent need to strengthen health systems for the effective prevention and management of NCDs, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Primary healthcare is an essential platform for providing NCD services at scale while ensuring equitable access [2]. Therefore, the World Health Organization (WHO) created the package of noncommunicable disease interventions (PEN), a set of cost-effective, evidence-based interventions for NCD prevention and management within the primary healthcare system [3]. PEN consists of protocols specific to key health priorities, including cardiometabolic diseases and healthy lifestyles. In Nepal, where two-thirds of deaths are attributable to NCDs [4], health service coverage for diseases such as hypertension or type 2 diabetes (hereafter, diabetes) is low, with less than one in five patients receiving treatment [5, 6]. Nepal’s government therefore piloted PEN in October 2016 and continuously scaled up the intervention package nationwide through health service provider (HSP) trainings [7]. Yet, it remains unknown to what extent primary care providers in Nepal currently deliver PEN as intended.

Implementation fidelity, defined as the degree to which an evidence-based intervention is delivered as intended, is a prerequisite for intervention effectiveness [8]. Findings from a few LMICs suggest that PEN can be implemented with fidelity [9, 10] and result in improved health outcomes [11–14]. However, this evidence is limited to a few pilot studies in relatively controlled settings; data on implementation fidelity and effectiveness during scale-up is lacking [15]. To successfully reduce premature mortality from NCDs in line with Sustainable Development Goal 3.4, it is crucial that PEN can be reliably replicated and scaled up with fidelity.

We conducted a mixed-methods study evaluating implementation fidelity to PEN during national scale-up in Nepal’s primary healthcare system. In addition to quantifying adherence to PEN protocols across a geographically diverse sample of primary care facilities, we generated qualitative evidence on factors affecting implementation fidelity from provider and patient perspectives. Our study aims to enhance the scale-up of PEN in Nepal and guide the implementation of comparable NCD programs in other LMICs.

Methods

Setting

Qualitative and quantitative data were collected in primary care facilities (health posts and primary healthcare centers [PHCCs]) across all seven provinces of Nepal between March and August 2021 (Figure S1). PHCCs typically have clinical laboratories and are often led by a medical officer [16]. Health posts are exclusively staffed by health assistants, auxiliary health workers, and auxiliary nurse midwives who deliver basic services in communities countrywide. Health assistants can prescribe first-line medicines for hypertension and diabetes. Laboratory facilities are seldomly available at health posts, particularly in rural areas [16]. Basic PEN medicines, such as metformin, amlodipine, and atorvastatin are provided free of charge, though availability can be limited in some facilities [17]. Second-line NCD medicines are partly covered under a national social health insurance program that 10% of adults in Nepal are enrolled in [18]. Laboratory-based diagnostic tests, if available, are typically subject to user fees for uninsured patients; for blood glucose measurements, these are around 25–50 NPR (0.18–0.35 USD). Point-of-care glucose testing is usually provided free of charge. Secondary and tertiary hospitals function as referral points for patients with NCDs.

PEN training of HSPs in Nepal is delivered through four-day workshops, following a standardized manual and targeting one to two providers per facility [3]. The physicians and other experts who deliver PEN training in turn received training via a standardized, one-week workshop facilitated by WHO Nepal and the Ministry of Health and Population [19].

Study design

We used a convergent parallel mixed methods study design to assess implementation fidelity [20]. Cross-sectional quantitative and qualitative data were collected simultaneously and analyzed separately. We integrated data strands by critically assessing convergence, complementarity, and expansion [21].

Implementation science framework

We adapted the Conceptual Framework for Implementation Fidelity by Carroll et al. to guide data analysis (Fig. 1) [8]. The rationale for using this framework was to systematically structure our fidelity assessment, while clearly differentiating between fidelity itself and factors impacting fidelity. In the framework, adherence as a “bottom-line measurement” of implementation fidelity is distinguished from moderators that influence the degree of fidelity with which an intervention is implemented (Table 1) [22]. Adherence is further decomposed into intervention dose (frequency, coverage, and duration) and intervention content. Given constraints in reliably capturing dose metrics of large-scale interventions cross-sectionally [23], we limited our quantitative assessment of adherence to content, i.e., the degree to which HSPs followed PEN protocol steps. During qualitative data analysis, the availability of resources required for PEN delivery emerged as a key moderator of fidelity. We thus added resource availability to the four moderators originally proposed by Carroll et al. Quantitative component

Data collection and participants

As detailed elsewhere [24], we used multistage stratified random sampling to select 105 primary care facilities across all seven provinces of Nepal. In brief, from 31 districts that implemented PEN as of July 2019, we first randomly selected two districts per province for a total of 14 districts. Next, within each sampled district, we listed all public primary healthcare facilities, stratified the sampling frame by rural versus urban areas, and randomly sampled facilities within each stratum. Sample size calculation is reported elsewhere [24]. We oversampled PHCCs to ensure sufficient sample size for analyses stratified by facility type. Our final sample included 34 PHCCs and 71 health posts.

We used direct observation to assess adherence of HSPs to PEN protocols while managing NCD clients. The study coordinator (CSN) developed structured observation checklists for each PEN protocol, which comprised various items on health assessment, risk prediction, behavioral counselling, and treatment. These steps are supposed to be completed in all contacts with patients meeting PEN eligibility criteria [3]. Trained research assistants (RAs) observed all patient contacts occurring during one or two days at each facility’s outpatient department. In line with PEN eligibility in Nepal, we included all patients ≥ 30 years of age with diabetes and/or hypertension to assess protocol 1, and with any NCD covered in PEN (diabetes, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, suspected breast/cervical cancer) to assess protocol 2. We observed at least one consultation for either protocol at 47 of the 105 healthcare facilities. We did not include PEN protocol 3 (chronic respiratory disease management) and protocol 4 (cancer screening in women) in this study because only 24 and 9 eligible patients, respectively, were identified.

Data analysis

We calculated the proportions of eligible patient contacts in which HSPs followed each individual checklist item of PEN protocols 1 and 2. Moreover, we calculated fidelity scores for each protocol by: (1) computing average adherence levels across protocol items within each domain – multiplied by 100 – to obtain sub-scores; (2) weighting sub-scores in proportion to the number of patients eligible for the respective domain; (3) summing weighted sub-scores; and (4) dividing the resulting overall score by the sum of all weights so that its scale ranges from 0 to 100.

We stratified descriptive statistics by PEN training status of HSPs (PEN-trained versus not trained) and statistically tested for differences in fidelity using Fisher’s exact tests for individual protocol items and Wilcoxon rank sum tests for fidelity (sub-)scores. Lastly, we examined determinants of fidelity by regressing fidelity scores on various provider (provider type, PEN training status), facility (facility type, urban/rural area, geographic region), and patient (diagnosis) characteristics. Prior participation in the standardized, four-day PEN training workshop was assessed via self-report by HSPs. We ran univariable regressions for each variable and included all variables with Wald test p-values < 0.20 in the final, multivariable regression model [25]. All regression models were estimated using generalized estimating equations (GEEs) with a log link function and an independent correlation structure, clustering observations at the health facility level.

Qualitative component

Data collection and participants

Our study team conducted semi-structured interviews with 47 HSPs and 33 patients to elicit perspectives on NCD management and care, as well as the implementation of PEN. At various primary care facilities, we purposively sampled participants to ensure diversity across characteristics of HSPs (including professional training, facility type, remoteness of placement) and patients (including sex, age, education, and NCD diagnosis). We developed, pilot-tested, and iteratively refined two semi-structured interview guides, using the WHO building blocks framework [26] as a starting point for HSPs and the Health Belief Model [27] for patients. In addition, the interview guides included questions and probes on implementation fidelity.

Interviews were conducted by eight RAs and research officers who all are native Nepali speakers and trained in qualitative research methods. After each day of data collection, we held debriefing sessions to discuss emerging issues and topics and refine the interview guides by adding questions or probes. Data collection continued until no new issues or topics emerged in data analysis, i.e., code saturation was reached [28]. Median interview duration was 42 min (IQR: 32; 48) for HSPs and 30 min for patients (IQR: 25; 35). As some interviews were cut short due to interruptions and rush at facilities, we conducted additional interviews until we reached code saturation. All patient interviews and 35 of the 47 HSP interviews were conducted in person; the remaining 12 interviews were held via telephone or online due to COVID-19 travel restrictions.

Data analysis

All interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim in Nepali. Two study team members (CSN and SMal) verified the accuracy of transcripts against audio recordings and omitted identifying information. We used Dedoose version 9.0.54 to facilitate management, preparation and analysis of textual data. We conducted a thematic analysis, completing the following steps separately for patient and HSP data. First, two study team members (CSN and SMal for HSP interviews; SMal and SS for patient interviews) closely read all transcripts to further familiarize themselves with the data. Second, using one-third of transcripts, they developed an initial codebook with the study team (CSN, SMal, AS, ER, BP, ABi, SS, AA, and AD), which included codes developed through inductive and deductive strategies. The codebook entailed code definitions, as well as instructions and examples for the application of codes. Third, to ensure consistency in code application, the two respective coders individually coded the same three transcripts. Inconsistencies were resolved through discussions with the study team and further refinements of the codebook were made. Fourth, each of the remaining transcripts was coded by one coder. Fifth, we identified all codes relating to the five moderators of fidelity in our framework. Sixth, we wrote detailed descriptions for each identified code, contrasting diverse perspectives across different HSPs and patients. Seventh, for each of the five moderators, we integrated detailed descriptions of the relevant codes. Eighth, we repeatedly verified analytic findings against transcripts.

Our analysis aligned with Hennink’s approach to thematic analysis [29]. We adapted the approach by adding steps five (mapping codes onto framework) and seven (integrating code descriptions for each moderator of fidelity) to allow for applying the fidelity framework post hoc. Coding was conducted in Nepali. To enable collaboration on analysis and interpretation within our multilingual research team, coded segments from the provider data were translated to English, whereas for the patient data, detailed summaries were prepared in English for each code based on coded segments.

Results

Quantitative component

We observed health service providers consulting 144 patients eligible for PEN protocol 1 (integrated management of hypertension and diabetes) and 172 patients eligible for protocol 2 (health behavioral counselling). Around half of providers had received PEN training (Table S1). Among PEN-trained HSPs, fewer were medical officers (27.2%) and fewer worked at PHCCs (58.7%) compared with HSPs who did not receive PEN training (66.3% and 86.3%, respectively). Of 172 observed patients, 93 (54%) had hypertension, 33 (19%) diabetes, 18 (10%) both diabetes and hypertension, and 28 (16%) another NCD included in PEN.

Protocol 1: Integrated management of hypertension and diabetes

Adherence levels among PEN-trained HSPs varied across items of PEN protocol 1 (Fig. 2). The most common actions that occurred in over half of consultations included asking about current medication (76% of consultations), measuring blood pressure (82%), initiating, adjusting, or explicitly continuing pharmacological therapies (85%), and advising for a follow-up appointment (60%). In one-quarter or more of consultations, PEN-trained HSPs asked about previously diagnosed cardiometabolic conditions (34%) and new symptoms (45%), measured blood sugar (28%), and provided dietary advice (41%). Among patients with known diabetes, 54% received blood glucose measurement. The remaining protocol items were rarely followed, including care steps such as counselling on smoking cessation (8%) and physical activity (20%). The WHO cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk chart was used in 7% of consultations. Examinations and counselling specific to diabetes – other than glucose testing – were conducted in less than one-fifth of contacts with diabetes patients. The overall fidelity score for PEN protocol 1 among PEN-trained HSPs was 20.8 – on a scale ranging from 0 to 100 – with the highest sub-score of 37.2 found in the counselling and treatment domain and the lowest sub-score of 6.8 in the CVD risk prediction domain.Fig. 2Adherence to PEN protocol 1 (integrated hypertension and diabetes management)Notes: Patients aged 30 years and older were eligible for PEN protocol 1 if they had diabetes and/or hypertension. In unadjusted analyses (Fig. 2), adherence to protocol 1 was higher among HSPs who received PEN training compared with those who did not, including for overall fidelity scores (20.8 vs. 14.6; p = 0.001), three out of the six sub-scores, and some specific protocol items, namely measuring blood pressure (82% vs. 64%; p = 0.01), auscultating lungs (16% vs. 4%; p = 0.03), and providing dietary advice (41% vs. 23%; p = 0.03).

We identified PEN training status, provider type, health facility type, and urban/rural area as potential determinants of fidelity with p-values below the 0.2 threshold in univariable regression models (Table 2). Protocol 2: Health behavioral counselling

PEN protocol 2 is structured along the 5A-model of behavioral counselling (ask, advise, assess, assist, arrange) [30]. PEN-trained HSPs asked 46% of patients about previous missed follow-up appointments and 27% of patients about unhealthy diets. The remaining unhealthy behaviors were inquired on in less than one-fifth of cases (Fig. 3). Behavioral advice was mostly limited to instructions on medication use (62%) and follow-up appointments (69%). Counselling on other health behaviors, including various dietary recommendations, was provided in one-quarter or less of consultations. PEN-trained HSPs asked about at least one unhealthy behavior in 63 out of 92 (68%) observed patient contacts – compared with 36% among PEN-untrained HSPs. Of those 63 patients, 30 (48%) reported the presence of one or more unhealthy behaviors and were thus eligible for the final three steps of the 5A-model. In less than 10% of those 30 cases, PEN-trained HSPs assessed whether patients were willing and confident to change their behavior, and assisted in preparing and motivating for a quitting plan. A follow-up appointment to discuss progress in behavioral change was arranged with 37% of those 30 patients. The overall fidelity score for PEN protocol 2 among PEN-trained HSPs was 22.1, with the highest sub-score of 25.0 found in the advise domain and the lowest sub-score of 5.0 in the assess domain.

In unadjusted analyses (Fig. 3), we observed significantly higher adherence among PEN-trained HSPs compared with HSPs not trained in PEN for overall fidelity scores (22.1 vs. 12.5; p < 0.001), the ask (23.0 vs. 13.5; p < 0.001) and advise (25.0 vs. 13.4; p < 0.001) sub-scores, and various protocol items. For instance, PEN-trained HSPs more frequently asked about missed follow-up appointments (46% vs. 15%; p < 0.001), advised on salt restriction (41% vs. 20%; p = 0.003), and instructed patients on medications (62% vs. 31%; p < 0.001), adherence (38% vs. 16%; p = 0.002), and follow-up dates (69% vs. 45%; p = 0.002).

The same four variables were identified for inclusion in the final regression model as for protocol 1 (PEN training, provider type, facility type, area). In multivariable regression (Table 3), we found that overall fidelity scores for protocol 2 were 64% (95%-CI: 12–142%; p = 0.01) higher for auxiliary health workers compared with medical officers. PEN training status was associated with a 32% (95%-CI: −4–80%; p = 0.08) higher overall fidelity score. We found no indication of relevant multicollinearity in regression models. Participant responsiveness

Providers

Health service providers perceived the PEN intervention and training as generally useful for improving NCD services. In addition, PEN was considered relevant and well aligned with the needs of patient populations, given the shift in disease burden towards NCDs in Nepal. Next to building knowledge, PEN training appeared to serve a motivating function, and many HSPs reported an increased confidence in managing NCD cases after receiving training.“Earlier, before training, we used to refer patients directly to the doctor, we were not confident enough to diagnose and prescribe medicines to them; but after we received PEN training, we are able to diagnose the patient with hypertension and diabetes.” – Health assistant (HA), rural health post

“Earlier, before training, we used to refer patients directly to the doctor, we were not confident enough to diagnose and prescribe medicines to them; but after we received PEN training, we are able to diagnose the patient with hypertension and diabetes.” – Health assistant (HA), rural health post

HSPs also commented on the engagement of patients in NCD care and adherence to follow-up visits and medications. They mentioned several factors that discouraged patients from utilizing and adhering to NCD services, including (1) financial barriers and lack of health insurance; (2) lack of severe symptoms and disease awareness; (3) unavailability of medicines; (4) distrust in health services at public facilities; (5) fear that long-term drug therapies may be harmful; (6) long travel times and geographic inaccessibility.“Patients without health insurance have to pay out of pocket to conduct lab investigations. They don’t seek health services until their symptoms are painful and intolerable.” – Medical officer (MO), urban PHCC“Another challenge with drug compliance is disruption in the supply of medicines to the patients. […] When patients are unable to obtain services from our health facility, they won’t visit our health facility in the future.” – Senior HA, rural health post"Our communities don’t trust the primary healthcare delivery system. They think we only provide basic services and seek services from higher centers with medical doctors." – Senior auxiliary health worker (AHW), rural health post

Examples of such programs included screening camps and training of female community health volunteers (FCHVs).“Due to the active mobilization of FCHVs and school health programs, patients are more conscious about their health. They come to our health facility to get their blood pressure examined.” – HA, urban health post

In contrast to HSPs, most patients were unaware of the PEN program. However, they highly valued the availability of comprehensive NCD care near their home, including affordable medication, diagnostics, and counselling.“We have to take this medicine on a daily basis, and we have been saving 1000-1200 rupees [around 7–8 USD]. We are also saving time by being close to the health facility. We do not have to travel to the hospital. The health service providers have been giving advice and counseling services on what should we do and what should we eat. These all have been very helpful.” – Diabetes patient, male, age missing

We do not have to travel to the hospital. The health service providers have been giving advice and counseling services on what should we do and what should we eat. These all have been very helpful.” – Diabetes patient, male, age missing

Trust in health services depended not only on reliable medicine availability, but also on HSPs’ willingness to listen to patients’ concerns and address their individual needs. Common concerns included difficulties in comprehending treatment plans, potential side effects of long-term medications, emotional stress associated with chronic disease self-management, and transportation to healthcare facilities for appointments. Correspondingly, common patient needs were detailed information and clear explanations of their disease, treatment options, and medication. A trusting patient-provider relationship was seen as important for enhancing engagement with care and adherence to follow-ups.“I trust the services provided by the health facility, therefore, I come here for my checkup.” – Hypertension patient, female, 65 years

Lastly, some patients criticized the lack of appointment reminders and follow-ups after missed visits, which would improve their motivation for regular healthcare utilization.

Intervention complexity

Providers

HSPs generally considered the PEN intervention adequate in scope for primary care facilities and not overly complex. However, some HSPs raised concerns that the intervention, when delivered with fidelity, may be too time-intense for facilities with high patient flow, particularly PHCCs. When HSPs experienced such time pressure, they skipped care steps.“If I provide NCD services following all four protocols, it will take a lot of time and my patients lined up for examination will get impatient. Hence, I’m left with no choice but to skip some steps.” – Senior AHW, rural PHCC

“If I provide NCD services following all four protocols, it will take a lot of time and my patients lined up for examination will get impatient. Hence, I’m left with no choice but to skip some steps.” – Senior AHW, rural PHCC

HSPs typically found instructions in the PEN guideline clear and easy to follow. Care steps were described in detail, which was mostly seen as helpful for understanding the intervention.

Facilitation strategies

Providers

Various HSPs highlighted that physical or digital copies of the guideline made it easier to translate knowledge acquired during PEN training into clinical practice. Without these manuals, they found it difficult to follow all recommended care steps. PEN manuals were unavailable at many health facilities. To improve fidelity, HSPs thus advocated for distributing manuals to all training participants and primary care facilities.“It was easier for us to correlate theory and clinical practice with the help of the manual.“ – MO, urban PHCC

“It was easier for us to correlate theory and clinical practice with the help of the manual.“ – MO, urban PHCC

HSPs commonly suggested additional facilitation strategies, including (1) a brief refresher training to update knowledge of evidence-based NCD care and discuss challenges in PEN delivery, and (2) monitoring and supervision visits for NCDs to maintain motivation and engagement and improve service delivery. Across facilities, previous monitoring visits focused either on general management issues or specific infectious disease programs."The PEN program was initiated in [district] with much enthusiasm, but there were no follow-ups after the program initiation. We also didn't provide enough attention to the program. If we had continued program screening activities, we would have been successful in reducing deaths due to NCDs." – AHW, rural health post

"The PEN program was initiated in [district] with much enthusiasm, but there were no follow-ups after the program initiation. We also didn't provide enough attention to the program. If we had continued program screening activities, we would have been successful in reducing deaths due to NCDs." – AHW, rural health post

After receiving PEN training, HSPs typically oriented colleagues on PEN and some continued to sporadically provide informal peer-coaching, e.g. during staff meetings. However, to foster a greater sense of responsibility for NCDs and avoid disruptions in service delivery, some participants suggested extending PEN training to all HSPs across primary care facilities.

A range of HSPs openly shared that they consider the quality of NCD services at their facility suboptimal. HSPs deemed service quality as suboptimal if they could not adhere to all care steps included in PEN and if they did not meet the patients’ needs and expectations. A common example of not meeting patients’ needs and expectations was the inability to provide medicines regularly.“We started from zero and now we are doing whatever we can. Although we are not providing as per the patients' needs, we are doing our best.” – Senior AHW, rural PHCC"Not being able to provide services to NCD patients has been an issue in our health facility. Resource availability had an important influence on the type and quality of services provided. PHCCs were generally seen as better staffed and equipped than health posts, thus offering a broader range of diagnostic and therapeutic services. The COVID-19 pandemic further impeded the delivery of quality NCD services. HSPs highlighted disruptions in NCD drug procurement and reduced care-seeking by patients during lockdowns.

How patients evaluated the quality of NCD services largely depended on their user experience. Positive user experiences were driven by various factors, including courteous and respectful behavior of HSPs, clear communication, short wait times, and affordability of services. Positive user experience shaped satisfaction and trust in services, thus encouraging care uptake and retention. The behavioral advice patients reported receiving was generally in line with PEN guidelines (increasing physical activity, reducing salt intake, discontinuing smoking, adhering to medication). However, several patients mentioned receiving advice on avoiding spicy food, which is not recommended in PEN guidelines. Lastly, similar to HSPs, patients reported that COVID-19 disrupted access to quality services, such as routine check-ups and medication refills.

Resource availability

Providers

Most HSPs considered their health facility unprepared for the adoption of PEN at the time they received PEN training, except for some urban PHCCs. Facilities often received essential resources for PEN delivery delayed or not at all. Lacking resources included medicines, glucometers and test strips, recording and reporting tools, NCD tickets for outpatient departments, and patient education materials. Particularly in rural areas and at health posts, HSPs strongly emphasized the insufficient supply of NCD medicines and diagnostic equipment as key bottlenecks for delivering PEN with fidelity. Lacking NCD medicines caused frustration in both providers and patients, leading to care-seeking at and referrals to higher-level facilities.“Just having knowledge of things is not enough; we need resources. It’s disheartening when people return without services; they travel nearly 2 hours just to reach the health facility. If we got the instruments or medicines on time, that would make a lot of things better.” – HA, urban PHCC

“Just having knowledge of things is not enough; we need resources. It’s disheartening when people return without services; they travel nearly 2 hours just to reach the health facility. If we got the instruments or medicines on time, that would make a lot of things better.” – HA, urban PHCC

Regarding human resources, HSPs noted that many colleagues lacked PEN training, disrupting NCD services when trained staff was absent or transferred to other facilities. Staff transfers were particularly common in rural areas.

Patients

Lacking NCD medicines at primary care facilities were also a common complaint among patients. Stockouts at public facilities led patients to pay out-of-pocket for medicines bought at private pharmacies. The introduction of social health insurance was seen as having positively impacted access to medicines and health services, though only at PHCCs.“Insurance has opened access to all kinds of checkups, medicine, and other health-related services.” – Diabetes patient, male, 61 years“We have government insurance. They always say they are out of medicine. For the last two months they have been saying so. We have visited 2–3 times but there has not been available. So I have been buying medicine from private medical stores.” – Diabetes patient, female, 39 years

Similar to medicines, access to diagnostic services depended on the availability of equipment, such as weighing scales, measuring tapes, stethoscopes, and blood pressure cuffs. Patients visiting health posts reported receiving blood pressure measurements, whereas patients visiting PHCCs additionally could access blood glucose checks and urine testing. Insufficient diagnostic services at primary care facilities led patients to visit district hospitals to meet healthcare needs.

Discussion

In this mixed methods study evaluating implementation fidelity to the WHO package of noncommunicable disease interventions during scale-up in Nepal, we found that adherence to PEN protocols 1 and 2 was often limited to a few diagnostic and therapeutic actions – primarily blood pressure measurement and basic medication management. Health service providers who received PEN training followed PEN protocols more closely than those who did not, though overall fidelity scores remained low at around 20 out of 100. While providers and patients were generally engaged by the intervention, various factors might impede implementation fidelity, including limited availability of medicines and diagnostic equipment, and negative user experiences.

Data strands converged as low adherence levels found in quantitative analyses were confirmed by qualitative data, with HSPs openly discussing existing gaps in PEN delivery. Reasons for limited fidelity and further context and nuance emerging from interviews with patients and HSPs complemented the quantification of adherence. Moreover, in several instances, qualitative data expanded on quantitative findings by offering potential explanations [21]. For example, we found that hypertension and diabetes management in four of five cases entailed blood pressure measurement, whereas blood glucose was measured in less than a third of patients. Our qualitative interviews revealed that primary care facilities and especially health posts often lacked glucometers, test strips, and/or laboratory facilities, thus precluding glucose measurement.

A review including pilot studies from 10 LMICs considered protocol adherence the largest challenge for PEN implementation [15]. For example, patient follow-ups and referrals often did not align with PEN guidelines [9, 31], and, similar to our findings, CVD risk scores were rarely calculated [32]. Even in pilot studies, the limited availability of essential medicines, equipment, and human resources at primary care facilities was a common implementation barrier [15]. The only previous study evaluating PEN implementation during scale-up found that in Moldova, PEN training led to higher adherence to protocols 1 and 2, although substantial gaps remained [10]. Increased adherence was largely driven by medical assistants rather than family doctors, which – despite different health system contexts – was similar to our results indicating higher adherence among auxiliary health workers. This finding points to the importance of non-physician staff in scaling up PEN with fidelity.

Fidelity to original intervention design must be balanced with adaptations required to fit local contexts and meet specific population needs [33]. Deviating from intervention protocols can impede effectiveness or may enhance it by increasing program fit. During PEN implementation in Nepal, some HSPs might have adequately responded to time and resource constraints by prioritizing certain protocol items. To inform future adaptations of PEN, research is needed that distinguishes core functions of PEN from the “adaptable periphery”, i.e. intervention components that can be modified without compromising effectiveness [34].

Improving implementation fidelity to PEN in Nepal, and thus NCD management and behavioral counselling, likely requires expanding training to include more primary care providers at each facility. Additionally, increasing the availability of medicines and diagnostic equipment across primary care facilities is crucial. Next to initial investments in equipment and long-term assets, strengthening supply chains for medicines and other replenishable items is urgently needed. However, increasing resource availability and strengthening providers’ clinical competence may not, on their own, be sufficient to increase care uptake and retention. Our qualitative findings highlight the central role of positive user experience in promoting NCD service utilization and building trust in health services [35, 36]. Future implementation strategies could focus on enhancing user experience and trust, for instance through community outreach programs and appointment reminder systems.

Additional implementation strategies that emerged from our data included the provision of PEN manuals to help providers navigate intervention complexity without compromising fidelity. These manuals could potentially be disseminated to HSPs via the PEN mobile application provided by WHO [37], though feasibility in Nepal’s setting must be determined. Moreover, to enhance participant responsiveness and quality of delivery, brief refresher trainings, supervision and monitoring visits, and structured peer coaching could be promising facilitation strategies. Taken together, increasing resource availability, improving user experience, and institutionalizing continuous, high-quality training and feedback mechanisms can also enhance the long-term sustainability of PEN in Nepal’s health system. Other countries may consider similar strategies prior to PEN scale-up.

Beyond implications for policymakers and health authorities, our study can contribute to advancing implementation science. Current fidelity frameworks were developed in high-income country settings and rarely consider the availability of key implementation resources [8]. We recommend that fidelity assessments in resource-limited contexts should explicitly examine the availability of essential resources for intervention delivery, particularly during scale-up. Furthermore, the novel approach for calculating fidelity scores we developed can aid future studies evaluating fidelity to comprehensive, packaged interventions. Aggregating adherence to various individual items to an overall score allows for more sophisticated statistical analyses, such as using multivariable regression models to identify determinants of fidelity.

Our study leveraged qualitative and quantitative data to fill important knowledge gaps on the scale-up of PEN, which is a cornerstone of WHO’s strategy for reducing NCD mortality in LMICs. Additional strengths included the national sampling frame - enhancing generalizability across Nepal - and the triangulation of data from both patient and provider perspectives. Our study also had limitations. First, direct observation may have induced a Hawthorne effect, with HSPs following PEN protocols more closely than in unobserved patient contacts, particularly in facilities with few observed NCD consultations. However, HSPs were not aware of the purpose of our study, and, given low overall adherence levels, large observer effects appear unlikely [38]. Second, we were unable to reliably identify patients who had risk factors (smoking, obesity, family history of cardiometabolic diseases) but no manifest NCD, as RAs did not interfere with clinical interactions or independently assess patient histories. Protocol adherence in HSPs consulting those at-risk patients could diverge from observed adherence levels for hypertension and diabetes patients. Third, we collected quantitative data at a single timepoint and it was not feasible to randomize participation in PEN trainings. Differences in protocol adherence between PEN-trained and -untrained HSPs may be influenced by preexisting behavioral differences and residual confounding. Moreover, PEN trainings may have differed in quality despite standardization. Fourth, at around half of sampled facilities, no NCD patient visited the outpatient department during the observation period. Our adherence estimates might thus not generalize to facilities with low patient flow for NCDs. Fifth, the fidelity framework that we used for data analysis was not used for data collection and preparation. However, prior to data analysis, we verified that data and codes mapped well onto the framework. Sixth, we did not assess inter-rater reliability for direct observation of patient visits. To minimize measurement variability, research assistants followed item-by-item checklists and received extensive training prior to data collection.

Conclusions

During scale-up in Nepal, implementation fidelity to WHO PEN was often limited to a few actions, focusing primarily on blood pressure measurement and basic medication management. Health service providers who received PEN training more frequently adhered to various intervention components than those who did not, though overall fidelity scores for PEN protocols 1 and 2 remained low at around 20 out of 100. Promising strategies to improve fidelity to PEN might include the provision of manuals, supervision and monitoring visits, brief refresher trainings, and community outreach programs. In addition to enhancing user experience, ensuring a reliable supply of medicines and diagnostic equipment appears crucial to improve NCD care in Nepal. Future fidelity assessments in resource-limited settings should examine the availability of key implementation resources, particularly during scale-up.
