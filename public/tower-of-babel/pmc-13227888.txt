Aspirin alters molecular crosstalk between prostaglandin, apoptosis, and invasion pathways in colorectal cancer
Authors: Hanieh-Sadat Mostafavi-Fini, Arash Moradi, Erfan Gowdini, Ramtin Mohammadi, Tina Abdollahi, Faranak Jamshidian, Shahla Mohammad Ganji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) remains a significant cause of global cancer mortality. While aspirin demonstrates therapeutic benefits, the molecular determinants underlying patient response are poorly understood, hindering personalised treatment strategies. This study aimed to identify molecular subtypes in CRC based on prostaglandin pathway markers and assess their association with clinical outcomes and aspirin use.MethodsWe analysed paired tumour and adjacent normal tissues from 86 CRC patients (43 regular aspirin users, 43 aspirin-free) via qPCR for mRNA expression (PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, PTGER3) and immunohistochemistry for protein levels (COX-2, Caspase-8, MMP-9). Normalised expression data were correlated with clinicopathological features and survival. Unsupervised clustering algorithms (K-means, hierarchical, and hybrid) were applied independently to each cohort to identify distinct molecular subtypes using Z-score-normalised data across all seven markers.ResultsAspirin-treated patients showed significantly higher PTGS2 and PTGER3 mRNA, elevated Caspase-8 protein, and reduced MMP-9 protein compared to the aspirin-free group. Unsupervised clustering identified distinct molecular subtypes within both cohorts. In the Aspirin-Treated group, clinical stratification yielded a two-subtype solution (AT-1, AT-2), primarily driven by differential protein expression (Caspase-8, COX-2, MMP-9), which demonstrated a trend towards distinct survival outcomes (P = 0.073). In the Aspirin-Free group, a clinically relevant two-subtype solution (AF-1, AF-2) demonstrated significant survival differences (47.4% vs 16.7%, P = 0.016); the poor-prognosis subtype (AF-2) was characterised by higher PTGS2 mRNA, higher COX-2 protein, lower Caspase-8, and higher MMP-9 levels. Notably, strong correlations between PTGS2 mRNA and adverse clinical outcomes observed in the aspirin-free cohort were absent in the aspirin-treated group.ConclusionIntegrated prostaglandin pathway gene and protein expression analysis identifies distinct CRC molecular subtypes with prognostic significance, particularly related to aspirin use. Key protein markers (Caspase-8, COX-2, MMP-9) and PTGS2 mRNA levels differentiate these subtypes. Aspirin treatment disrupts specific molecular correlations linked to prognosis, suggesting modulation of post-transcriptional networks. These findings support the use of multi-marker molecular profiles to improve patient stratification and personalised aspirin therapy in CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15872-z.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Aspirin, Molecular subtypes, Prostaglandin pathway, PTGS2 (COX-2), Caspase-8, MMP-9, QPCR, Immunohistochemistry, Patient stratification

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) constitutes a significant global health burden, ranking as the third most prevalent and second most lethal malignancy worldwide [1]. Data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) indicate that over 1.9 million new CRC cases were diagnosed globally in 2022, resulting in approximately 900,000 deaths. Projections estimate an increase to 2.2 million cases and 1.1 million deaths by 2030, driven primarily by rising incidence rates in developing nations transitioning to westernised lifestyles [2]. This escalation in CRC incidence is attributed to a complex interplay of genetic and epigenetic modifications [3, 4], compounded by environmental factors such as sedentary behaviour, obesity, suboptimal dietary patterns, and chronic inflammation.

The pathogenesis of CRC is significantly influenced by environmental, genetic, and epigenetic determinants, with over 70% of cases classified as sporadic, lacking a clear hereditary aetiology. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), particularly ulcerative colitis, significantly increases CRC risk. In IBD patients, CRC manifests as colitis-associated cancer (CAC), with an incidence rate up to 60% higher than that of the general population [5]. Sporadic CRC frequently elicits a robust inflammatory response mediated by cytokine signalling and immune cell infiltration, termed tumour-induced inflammation. Chronic inflammation is a critical driver of CRC initiation and progression, creating a tumour-permissive microenvironment. In IBD, persistent inflammation underlies the development of CAC, mediated by pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), which activate key signalling pathways, including NF-κB and STAT3 [6]. Central to the pathogenesis of inflammation-driven colorectal cancer (CRC), the cyclooxygenase enzymes COX-1 and COX-2 catalyse the metabolism of arachidonic acid into prostaglandins. These lipid mediators are pivotal to both physiological homeostasis and pathological states, such as inflammation and tumourigenesis [7]. COX-1, encoded by PTGS1, is constitutively expressed across most tissues, sustaining baseline prostaglandin levels essential for mucosal integrity. In contrast, COX-2, encoded by PTGS2, is inducible, with its expression significantly upregulated by pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) and oncogenic signals within the CRC microenvironment. In CRC, COX-2 overexpression is consistently observed, driving the production of elevated prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), which promotes tumour cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and resistance to apoptosis [8]. This positions COX-2 as a therapeutic target, as evidenced by the chemopreventive effects of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as aspirin, in reducing CRC risk, particularly in inflammation-driven tumourigenesis [9]. These effects are mediated through downstream signalling of PGE2, a key COX-2-derived prostaglandin that exerts its oncogenic influence via specific receptor pathways.

PGE2, the predominant COX-2-derived prostaglandin, exerts its biological effects via four G-protein-coupled receptors, including EP1, EP2, EP3, and EP4 [10]. Recent evidence further demonstrates that COX-2-derived prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), acting via EP2, inhibits ferroptosis, a form of iron-dependent programmed cell death, in CRC cells, thereby promoting tumour growth and chemoresistance [11]. Despite the established chemopreventive benefits of aspirin, a major clinical challenge remains to identify reliable biomarkers that predict which patients will derive the greatest benefit from regular use. Recent molecular profiling efforts have highlighted the prognostic value of specific genetic alterations. Notably, while early observational studies suggested that patients harbouring PIK3CA-mutated colorectal tumours experience improved survival following post-diagnostic aspirin use [12], this has now been definitively confirmed by the ALASCCA double-blind, randomised clinical trial. This trial demonstrated that adjuvant low-dose aspirin (160 mg daily) significantly reduces the 3-year incidence of colorectal cancer recurrence in patients with somatic alterations in the PI3K signalling pathway, including PIK3CA hotspot mutations [13]. Furthermore, aspirin's chemopreventive and survival benefits appear more pronounced in tumours with wild-type KRAS and BRAF statuses, emphasising the highly context-dependent nature of its efficacy [14]. Alongside these genetic markers, existing studies show that aspirin directly modulates critical downstream networks, inducing apoptosis via Caspase-8 activation and inhibiting tumour invasion by preventing the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [15]. However, how these targeted apoptotic and invasive mechanisms correlate with the broader prostaglandin pathway across different patient profiles remains incompletely understood.

The selection of PTGS1 (encoding COX-1), PTGS2 (encoding COX-2), PTGER2 (encoding EP2), PTGER3 (encoding EP3), COX-2, Caspase-8, and MMP-9 was based on their established and emerging roles in CRC pathogenesis, inflammation, apoptosis, and invasion. Recent studies have highlighted the prognostic and predictive value of these markers in CRC and their potential involvement in mediating aspirin’s anti-cancer effects [16, 17].

Despite progress in understanding the roles of COX-2 and EP receptors in colorectal cancer (CRC), the concurrent expression of PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, and PTGER3 in a single patient cohort remains underexplored, as do their associations with clinicopathological features such as tumour stage, invasion, and metastasis. This study quantifies mRNA expression levels of these genes in paired tumour and normal tissues from 86 CRC patients using real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and examines their correlations with tumour stage, depth of invasion, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis.

The primary hypothesis of this study is that integrated expression profiles of prostaglandin pathway genes and related protein markers define molecular subtypes of CRC with differential survival and response to aspirin therapy. The specific objectives are: (1) to quantify PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, and PTGER3 mRNA and COX-2, Caspase-8, and MMP-9 protein expression in paired tumour and normal adjacent tissues; (2) to correlate these markers with clinicopathological features and survival; and (3) to use unsupervised clustering to identify molecular subtypes associated with aspirin response.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort and sample collection

This study examined CRC mRNA and protein expression profiles using paired tumour and adjacent normal tissue samples from 86 patients undergoing surgical resection at Imam Khomeini Hospital, Tehran, Iran, between January 2018 and December 2020. The research was conducted with approval from the National Institute of Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology ethics committee (Approval No. IR. NIGEB.1398.04.12), and informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with ethical guidelines. Patients were selected based on histologically confirmed CRC diagnoses from pathology records. Exclusion criteria included prior neoadjuvant therapy (chemotherapy or radiation), incomplete pathology reports, or unavailability of paired tissue samples, to ensure data integrity and sample quality.

Clinical and demographic data were systematically collected, encompassing sex, age, tumour stage (TNM classification: I–IV), depth of invasion (T1–T4), lymph node metastasis (N0–N3), distant metastasis (M0–M1), smoking status (current, former, or never), family history of CRC (first-degree relatives), and haemoglobin levels (g/dL). These variables were classified according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system (8th edition) [18]. Tissue specimens comprised tumour samples and adjacent normal mucosa, excised at least 5 cm from the tumour margin to minimise contamination by cancerous cells. Immediately following resection, samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until molecular analysis to preserve RNA integrity.

The study focused on quantifying the expression of PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, and PTGER3 in tumours relative to normal tissue. The normal control cohort, represented by adjacent normal tissue from all 86 patients, provided a baseline for comparative expression analysis. Survival status was monitored for three years post-surgery, with outcomes recorded as alive or dead due to CRC recurrence, facilitating the correlation of PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, and PTGER3 expression patterns with clinical progression in this representative CRC cohort. Notably, aspirin use was defined as regular intake of at least 75 mg daily for at least 6 months before surgery, as documented in patients' medical records and medication histories. Within the aspirin-treated cohort, the daily dosage ranged from 75 to 300 mg. The specific prescribed dose was highly dependent on the individual patient's clinical response to aspirin and the requirement to achieve desired therapeutic outcomes. The duration of use was at least six months, though a subset of patients had substantially longer treatment histories due to chronic, long-term aspirin use for cardiovascular disease prophylaxis.

As a point of clinical context, while once-daily low-dose aspirin (75 to 100 mg) is the current recommended antithrombotic regimen for patients with conditions such as essential thrombocythemia (ET), studies indicate that this baseline once-daily administration may not be sufficient to consistently inhibit platelet cyclooxygenase (COX)−1, often necessitating individualised dose adjustments. Compliance and dosing histories were inferred from prescription records and patient interviews [19]. A retrospective review of these records found no severe adverse events (e.g., major gastrointestinal bleeding) that led to treatment cessation, although mild adverse events were not systematically captured. Retrospective assessment may introduce some limitations in exposure classification.

RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from frozen tissue samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA, Cat. No. 15596026) following the manufacturer’s protocol, with modifications to optimise the procedure. Briefly, 50–100 mg of tissue was homogenised in 1 mL TRIzol using a TissueLyser II (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) at 25 Hz for 5 min with a 5-mm stainless steel bead. After phase separation with 0.2 mL chloroform (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, Cat. No. C2432), RNA was precipitated with 0.5 mL isopropanol (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. I9516), washed with 75% ethanol, and resuspended in 30 μL nuclease-free water (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat. No. AM9937). RNA quality was assessed by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel (w/v) prepared in 1 × TAE buffer (40 mM Tris–acetate, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0), stained with 0.5 μg/mL ethidium bromide. Three microliters of RNA, mixed with 1 μL of 6 × loading dye, were loaded per lane. The gels were run at 100 V for 30 min using a Mini-Sub Cell GT system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). RNA integrity was confirmed by visualising distinct 28S and 18S ribosomal RNA bands under UV transillumination using a Gel Doc XR + (Bio-Rad). RNA concentration and purity were quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with an A260/A280 ratio of 1.8–2.0 indicating acceptable purity. Samples with ratios outside this range or degraded RNA were excluded.

cDNA synthesis

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesised from 1 μg of total RNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. K1622). The reaction mixture (20 μL) contained 1 μg RNA, 1 μL oligo(dT)18 primer (50 μM), 4 μL 5 × Reaction Buffer, 1 μL RiboLock RNase Inhibitor (20 U/μL), 2 μL dNTP mix (10 mM each), and 1 μL RevertAid Reverse Transcriptase (200 U/μL), topped up with nuclease-free water. Reverse transcription was performed in a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad) under the following conditions: 42 °C for 60 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 70 °C for 5 min. cDNA quality was verified by conventional PCR amplification of the β-actin gene using cDNA-specific primers (Forward: 5’-CATGTACGTTGCTATCCAGGC-3’, Reverse: 5’-CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGAT-3’) designed to span an intron, ensuring no genomic DNA contamination. PCR products (expected size: 250 bp) were visualised on a 2% agarose gel.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Gene expression analysis of PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, PTGER3, and the housekeeping genes ACTB (encoding β-actin) and RAB7A was conducted using real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Reactions were prepared in triplicate with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Cat. No. 4309155), where each 20-μL reaction comprised 10 μL of 2 × SYBR Green mix, 1 μL each of forward and reverse primers at 10 μM, 2 μL of cDNA (diluted 1:10), and 6 μL of nuclease-free water. For ACTB, the forward primer sequence was 5’-CATGTACGTTGCTATCCAGGC-3’ and the reverse was 5’-CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGAT-3’; for RAB7A, the forward primer was 5’-GTCCTGGTTGGAAAGAAGCA-3’ and the reverse was 5’-CAGCATCCAGCATCAACTTC-3’; for PTGS1, the forward primer was 5’-ATGAGTTGTGGGAAAATTGCT-3’ and the reverse was 5’-GATCATCTCTGCCTGAGTATC-3’; for PTGS2, the forward primer was 5’-CCCTTGGGTGTCAAAGGTAA-3’ and the reverse was 5’-GCCCTCGCTTATGATCTGTC-3’; for PTGER2, the forward primer was 5’-CTGCTCCTCAAACAGCATCA-3’ and the reverse was 5’-GGTGCATGTATCTGGCTCCA-3’; and for PTGER3, the forward primer was 5’-CGCCTCAACCAAGATCATGT-3’ and the reverse was 5’-AGGAAGAGGTTTCGCCAAAC-3’. Amplification proceeded with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. Post-amplification, a melting curve analysis was performed (95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, ramping to 95 °C at 0.3 °C/s) to verify product specificity, as indicated by a single peak. Amplification efficiencies of 90–110% were confirmed using standard curves generated from five-fold serial dilutions of pooled cDNA.

Protein assay (Immunohistochemistry)

COX-2, Caspase-8, and MMP-9 protein expression levels were assessed using immunohistochemistry (IHC) on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections obtained from the patient cohort. Tissue blocks were sectioned into approximately 4 μm-thick slices. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by treating the slides with 3% hydrogen peroxide in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For antigen retrieval, sections were subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval using microwave irradiation in 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0). To minimise non-specific antibody binding, sections were incubated with a blocking solution, for example, 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), potentially overnight at 4 °C.

Following blocking, sections were incubated with primary antibodies specific for COX-2, Caspase-8, and MMP-9, diluted according to manufacturer recommendations or optimised concentrations, typically for 1 h at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies, including COX-2 (clone SP21, BIOCARE MEDICAL, cat# ACR306A, dilution 1:50), Caspase-8 (Rabbit Polyclonal, Biorbyt, cat# orb10241, dilution 1:100), and MMP-9 (Rabbit Polyclonal, Biorbyt, cat# orb100620, dilution 1:200), were used in this study. All antibodies were validated for IHC on FFPE tissue. After washing steps, usually with PBS, sections were incubated with an appropriate biotinylated secondary antibody (anti-rabbit IgG) diluted in buffer (1:5000 in 1% PBS) for about 30 min at room temperature. Sections were rewashed before application of the Streptavidin-HRP enzyme conjugate. Antibody binding was visualised using a chromogen substrate system, such as 3,3'-Diaminobenzidine (DAB), resulting in a brown precipitate at the site of protein expression. Slides were then counterstained with haematoxylin to visualise cell nuclei, dehydrated through graded ethanol and xylene, and cover-slipped using permanent mounting medium.

Stained slides were examined under the microscope by an experienced pathologist. Protein expression for COX-2, Caspase-8, and MMP-9 was quantified by determining the exact percentage of positively stained cells within representative tumour areas using ImageJ software. Additionally, the overall staining expression for all three markers was scored semi-quantitatively based on the proportion of positive cells. A score of 0 indicated a negative result with no staining or less than 1% positive cells. A score of 1 indicated weak positive staining with 1 to 30% positive cells. A score of 2 represented moderate positive staining with 31 to 70% positive cells. A score of 3 indicated strong positive staining, with more than 70% of cells positive. The resulting continuous staining percentages for COX-2, Caspase-8, and MMP-9 were utilised as the primary metrics for all subsequent statistical analyses, including comparisons between aspirin-treated and aspirin-free cohorts, correlation analyses, and unsupervised clustering algorithms. The semi-quantitative intensity scores were concurrently used to categorise representative histological fields.

Unsupervised clustering and molecular properties identification

Unsupervised clustering analyses were performed independently on the Aspirin-Treated (n = 43) and Aspirin-Free (n = 43) patient cohorts to identify potential molecular properties based on integrated marker profiles. The input dataset for each cohort comprised seven molecular features: relative mRNA expression levels (log2-transformed -ΔΔCT values) for PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, and PTGER3, and protein expression levels (immunohistochemistry staining percentages) for COX-2, Caspase-8, and MMP-9.

Before clustering, the combined seven-feature dataset for each cohort was standardised using Z-score normalisation (mean-centred and scaled to unit variance) to ensure equal contribution of each marker irrespective of its original scale or variance. Three distinct unsupervised clustering approaches were employed to evaluate patient stratification and determine the optimal number of clusters (k). The K-means Clustering method was applied to the Z-score normalised data. The optimal number of clusters (k) was determined by calculating the average silhouette score for potential k values ranging from 2 to 5. The k yielding the highest average silhouette score was considered optimal for this method. Silhouette plots were generated to visualise cluster cohesion and separation for each tested k. Furthermore, Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering was performed using Ward’s linkage method, which minimises the variance within merged clusters. Euclidean distance was used as the distance metric on the Z-score normalised data. Dendrograms were generated to visualise the hierarchical relationships between patients. Clusters were typically defined by cutting the dendrogram at the height corresponding to the optimal k identified by silhouette analysis, or at clinically relevant bifurcations supported by survival data. Finally, the Hybrid K-means/Hierarchical Clustering approach combined initial partitioning with hierarchical merging. First, K-means clustering was performed with a larger number of initial clusters (k = 10). Second, the centroids (mean profiles) of these 10 initial clusters were calculated. Finally, agglomerative hierarchical clustering (Ward's linkage, Euclidean distance) was applied to these 10 centroids. The optimal number of final clusters (k) for this hybrid method was determined by identifying the point of maximum acceleration (analogous to the elbow method) in the linkage distances of the resulting dendrogram.

Cluster visualisation and interpretation

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on each cohort's Z-score-normalised seven-feature dataset. The first two principal components were used to visualise the separation of patients assigned to different clusters (derived from K-means, hierarchical, or hybrid methods) in a reduced two-dimensional space. Heatmaps were also generated using Z-score-normalised data to visualise the molecular profiles characteristic of each identified cluster across the seven markers. Clustering analyses and visualisations were performed using Python (v3.9.18) with libraries including scikit-learn (for K-means, PCA, Z-score scaling, and silhouette score), SciPy (for hierarchical clustering and dendrograms), pandas for data manipulation, and Matplotlib/Seaborn for plotting.

Data analysis

Cycle threshold (Ct) values were calculated using the ABI 7500 software (version 2.3) with automatic baseline and threshold settings. Gene expression was initially normalised to the geometric mean of the Ct values for β-actin and RAB7A using the 2−ΔΔCT method to quantify relative fold changes in expression between tumour and normal tissues. To facilitate comparisons across patients, genes, and cohorts and to prepare data for clustering and clinicopathological correlation, the 2−ΔΔCT values were normalised in two steps and applied separately to the patients who used aspirin and the non-aspirin groups [20].

For each cohort, gene-specific minimum and maximum − ΔΔCT values were calculated using the cohort's expression data. For the aspirin-treated cohort, comprising CRC patients receiving regular aspirin therapy, these ranges were determined for each target gene (PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, PTGER3), capturing the variability in tumour expression under the influence of aspirin relative to paired normal tissue. Similarly, for the aspirin-free cohort of CRC patients not using aspirin, distinct minima and maxima were established to reflect expression patterns in the absence of aspirin treatment, relative to their paired normal tissue. The normal control cohort, which provides a baseline of non-cancerous tissue expression, was used to calculate the initial ΔΔCT values for both CRC cohorts using the housekeeping gene ACTB. However, it was not included in the min–max scaling to preserve its role as a reference. This normalisation process yielded values where 0 represents the lowest tumour expression relative to normal tissue, and 1 represents the highest within each CRC cohort, enabling comparisons across genes, patients, and treatment conditions. Min–max scaling was performed within each cohort to account for potential cohort-specific variability in expression due to aspirin exposure. This enables meaningful comparison of relative expression levels across genes and patients within each treatment group.

Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test in GraphPad Prism (v9.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Differential expression between tumour and normal tissues was analysed with REST2009 software (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), employing a pairwise fixed reallocation randomisation test (10,000 iterations) to assess the statistical significance of changes in PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, and PTGER3 expression. The suitability of ACTB and RAB7A as housekeeping genes was validated using one-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism, confirming no significant variation in expression across tissue types (P > 0.05). Associations between gene expression levels and clinicopathological factors were evaluated using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables (e.g., sex, tumour stage, survival status) in GraphPad Prism, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Patient demographics and clinicopathological characteristics

The study cohort comprised 86 patients with colorectal cancer, with a mean age of 54.2 years (standard deviation [SD] ± 16.0 years; range: 18–84 years). Demographic and clinicopathological characteristics are summarised in Table 1. Of the 86 patients, 40 were male (46.5%), and 46 were female (53.5%). Seventy-three patients (84.9%) were aged 50 years or older, while 13 (15.1%) were under 50 years. Smoking was reported in 19 patients (22.1%), with 67 (77.9%) identified as non-smokers. A family history of CRC was present in 12 patients (14.0%) and absent in 74 (86.0%). Haemoglobin levels indicated anaemia (< 12 g/dL) in 62 patients (72.1%), with 24 (27.9%) having levels ≥ 12 g/dL. The mean tumour size was 5.5 cm (SD ± 2.1 cm), with tumours < 5.5 cm in 38 patients (44.2%) and ≥ 5.5 cm in 48 (55.8%). TNM staging revealed advanced disease (Stages III and IV) in 45 patients (52.3%), with detailed distributions across T, N, and M categories.

RNA Quality and cDNA synthesis validation

RNA extracted from all 86 tumour and normal tissue pairs exhibited high integrity, with sharp 28S and 18S rRNA bands observed on agarose gels. The mean RNA concentration was 250 ng/μL (SD ± 45 ng/μL), and the A260/A280 ratio ranged from 1.85 to 2.05 (mean: 1.95 ± 0.07), confirming high purity. cDNA synthesis was successful in all samples, with β-actin PCR yielding a single 250-bp band, and no amplification in no-reverse-transcriptase controls, ruling out genomic DNA contamination.

Differential gene and protein expression between aspirin-treated and aspirin-free cohorts

To assess the impact of aspirin, use on key molecular markers, normalised mRNA expression levels (scaled 0–1 relative to paired normal tissue) of PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, and PTGER3 were compared between aspirin-treated (n = 43) and aspirin-free (n = 43) CRC patient cohorts using one-way ANOVA. The analysis revealed a significant (p < 0.001) difference for PTGS2 and PTGER3. Specifically, PTGS2 expression was higher in the Aspirin-Treated group (0.657 ± 0.170) compared to the Aspirin-Free group (0.335 ± 0.250). Similarly, PTGER3 expression was significantly (p < 0.001) elevated in the Aspirin-Treated group (0.527 ± 0.167) versus the Aspirin-Free group (0.335 ± 0.160). In contrast, no statistically significant difference (p = 0.055) was observed for PTGS1 expression between the treated (0.393 ± 0.201) and free (0.460 ± 0.102) groups. PTGER2 expression showed no difference between the treated (0.532 ± 0.167) and free (0.528 ± 0.307) cohorts (p = 0.938) (Fig. 1A).

Correlation patterns differ between aspirin-treated and aspirin-free groups

To elucidate the relationships between molecular markers and their potential association with clinical progression, Pearson correlation analyses were performed in both the aspirin-treated and aspirin-free cohorts. These analyses evaluated the associations between gene expression levels of PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, and PTGER3, protein staining percentages of COX-2, Caspase-8, and MMP-9, and key clinical variables, including tumour stage and survival status.

Distinct correlation networks regarding gene-protein interactions were observed between the two groups, as visualised in correlation heatmaps. In the aspirin-free cohort, PTGS2 mRNA expression showed a significant positive correlation with PTGS2 protein staining (r = 0.49) and with MMP9 staining (r = 0.62). PTGS2 mRNA expression also demonstrated a significant negative correlation with Caspase-8 staining (r = −0.53) in this group. Conversely, these correlations involving PTGS2 mRNA were non-significant in the aspirin-treated cohort. Both groups exhibited strong correlations among protein levels of COX-2 and Caspase-8 (Treated: r = −0.68; Free: r = −0.53) and positively correlated with MMP9 (Treated: r = 0.58; Free: r = 0.81), while Caspase-8 was strongly negatively correlated with MMP9 (Treated: r = −0.57; Free: r = −0.67) (Fig. 3 A and B, and Supplementary Table S1 and S2).

Correlation Patterns Between Gene Expression and Protein Levels in CRC Cohorts. A Pearson correlation matrix for normalized gene expression (PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, PTGER3) and protein staining levels (COX-2, Caspase-8, MMP-9) in the Aspirin-Treated cohort (n = 43). B Pearson correlation matrix for normalised gene expression and protein staining levels in the Aspirin-Free cohort (n = 43)

In the Aspirin-Treated group, none of the analysed gene expression levels significantly correlated with either tumour stage or survival status. However, all three protein markers exhibited highly significant correlations: COX-2 and MMP-9 positively correlated with higher tumour stage (r = 0.77, p < 0.001 and r = 0.75, p < 0.001, respectively) and negatively correlated with survival (r = −0.46, p = 0.0019 and r = −0.42, p = 0.0053, respectively), indicating association with poorer outcomes. Conversely, Caspase-8 staining showed a strong negative correlation with Stage (r = −0.81, p < 0.001) and a positive correlation with survival (r = 0.50, p < 0.001), suggesting an association with better outcomes. Furthermore, within the Aspirin-Free group, all three proteins show significant correlations with stage and survival status (COX-2: Stage r = 0.89, p < 0.001, Survival r = −0.64, p < 0.001; Caspase-8: Stage r = −0.75, p < 0.001, Survival r = 0.65, p < 0.001; MMP-9: Stage r = 0.89, p < 0.001, Survival r = −0.69, p < 0.001). Interestingly, PTGS2 gene expression also strongly correlated with adverse clinical features (stage: r = 0.68, p < 0.001; Survival: r = −0.58, p < 0.001). This suggests that the link between PTGS2 gene expression and clinical progression is particularly evident in the absence of aspirin treatment. These analyses reveal substantially different molecular correlation landscapes between aspirin-treated and aspirin-free CRC patients, particularly regarding the association between PTGS2 expression and protein levels and clinical outcomes (Supplementary Table S3).

Unsupervised clustering identifies distinct molecular subtypes defined by prostaglandin, apoptosis, and invasion marker profiles

To further investigate molecular heterogeneity and identify potential patient subgroups based on the combined expression profiles of prostaglandin pathway genes and related proteins, unsupervised clustering analyses were performed independently on the Aspirin-Treated (n = 43) and Aspirin-Free (n = 43) cohorts. The analyses utilised standardised data comprising mRNA expression levels (PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, PTGER3) and protein staining percentages (COX-2, Caspase-8, MMP-9), processed using Z-score normalisation.

Optimal cluster numbers (k) and patient stratification were evaluated using K-means clustering with silhouette analysis, agglomerative hierarchical clustering (Ward’s linkage), and a hybrid K-means/hierarchical approach. For K-means, the optimal k was determined as the value that yielded the highest average silhouette score across k values ranging from 2 to 5. The hybrid method identified the optimal k by the point of maximum acceleration in linkage distances during hierarchical clustering of initial K-means centroids (k = 10). The value "k = 10" here denotes the maximum candidate value for the hybrid method, not the final optimal number of clusters. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to visualise the separation of clusters in reduced dimensions.

For the Aspirin-Treated group, K-means clustering and silhouette analysis identified an optimal k of 2 (average silhouette score: 0.232, Supplemental Fig. 1A). In contrast, the hybrid clustering approach suggested a five-cluster solution (k = 5) (Fig. 4A). Although the hybrid method identified finer subdivisions (five clusters), both K-means and hierarchical clustering (Supplemental Fig. 2A) consistently indicated a primary bifurcation into two main patient groups, which was more consistent with the observed survival trends. Despite this, K-means and hierarchical clustering consistently supported a primary bifurcation into two main patient groups. This two-cluster stratification aligns with previously described subgroups, which exhibit a trend toward differential survival rates (71.4% vs 45.5%, P = 0.073). Characterisation of these two molecular subtypes revealed distinct profiles primarily driven by protein markers, consistent with the correlation patterns observed in this cohort. Subtype AT-1 was characterised by higher Caspase-8 protein levels and lower COX-2 and MMP-9 levels, indicating a more favourable prognosis under aspirin treatment. In contrast, Subtype AT-2 displayed lower Caspase-8 and higher COX-2 and MMP-9 protein levels, which were associated with less favourable outcomes. Differences in the mRNA markers (PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, PTGER3) appeared less prominent in defining these clusters within the treated group (Supplemental Fig. 3A and 4A).

The optimal number of clusters is identified by the maximum acceleration point in linkage distances. C, D PCA plots of final hybrid cluster assignments for each cohort, showing patient distribution in reduced dimensional space. The hybrid approach reveals additional substructure but supports a primary bifurcation consistent with survival trends

For the Aspirin-Free cohort, silhouette analysis for k-means clustering suggested a mathematical optimum of 3 clusters (average silhouette score: 0.268; Supplemental Fig. 1B), while the hybrid clustering approach indicated 5 clusters (Fig. 4B). However, to maintain adequate statistical power for survival analysis and prioritise clinical actionability, patients were stratified into a two-cluster solution (k = 2). This binary classification (AF-1 and AF-2) demonstrated significant survival differences (47.4% vs 16.7%, p = 0.016) and remains clinically relevant. However, the two-cluster solution (k = 2) previously validated in survival analyses (16.7% vs 47.4%, P = 0.016) remains clinically relevant. In this structure, Subtype AF-1 (associated with better survival, 47.4%) exhibited lower PTGS2 mRNA expression, lower COX-2 protein levels, higher Caspase-8, and lower MMP-9 protein levels compared to Subtype AF-2. Subtype AF-2 (poorer survival, 16.7%) was characterised by higher PTGS2 mRNA, higher COX-2, lower Caspase-8, and higher MMP-9 protein staining. The three-cluster solution suggested by silhouette analysis may represent further subdivision based on marker nuances, potentially isolating an intermediate group or highlighting combinations of the seven markers that are not fully captured by the binary survival split (Supplemental Figs. 3B and 4B). PCA visualisations confirmed that the combined seven molecular features (PTGS1, PTGS2, PTGER2, PTGER3 mRNA; COX-2, Caspase-8, MMP-9 protein) could segregate patients into these molecular subtypes in reduced-dimensional space, regardless of the clustering algorithm used (Fig. 4D and C).

Discussion

This study revealed significant differences in the expression profiles of key components of the prostaglandin pathway between aspirin-treated and aspirin-free CRC patients. Intriguingly, PTGS2 mRNA expression was significantly higher in the aspirin-treated group than in the aspirin-free group. While no significant difference was observed at the protein level (p = 0.070), COX-2 protein expression trended lower in aspirin-treated patients. This apparent discrepancy between transcriptional and translational regulation suggests complex post-transcriptional mechanisms in response to aspirin treatment. Aspirin binds to Ser530, the residue near the active site of COX-2, and perturbs COX-2 activity [21]. These findings align with a recent review demonstrating that aspirin-induced effects on COX-2 activity may operate primarily through post-translational modifications rather than direct transcriptional repression [22]. This phenomenon may be explained by aspirin's irreversible acetylation of COX-2, which inhibits its enzymatic function without necessarily reducing expression levels.

Furthermore, the increased PTGER3 mRNA expression in aspirin-treated patients represents a novel finding not previously reported in CRC cohorts. Previous studies have reported that PTGER1 and PTGER2 expression remain constant in CRC tissues compared to normal mucosa, whereas PTGER3 expression is significantly downregulated in CRC [21]. Our findings regarding PTGER2 and PTGER3 align perfectly with this established literature. EP3 receptor has demonstrated context-dependent roles in cancer, with some studies suggesting tumour-suppressive functions in colorectal adenomas [23, 24]. Our observations potentially indicate a compensatory upregulation of PTGER3 in response to reduced prostaglandin signalling under aspirin treatment [25], consistent with research demonstrating that PGE2 signalling can regulate receptor expression through negative feedback mechanisms [26].

Our observation of differential Caspase-8 and MMP-9 expression in aspirin-treated patients aligns with emerging mechanistic models of aspirin's multifaceted action. Beyond classical prostaglandin-mediated pathways, aspirin heavily modulates the WNT, cAMP-PKA, and β-catenin signalling networks, as well as non-epigenetically driven mismatch repair (MMR) deficiency [15]. The elevation of Caspase-8 is likely driven by aspirin's intersection with these parallel pathways; for instance, aspirin has been shown to induce apoptosis not only by triggering the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway but also by stimulating death receptors (such as Fas and TNFR1), which directly activate Caspase-8 [27]. Furthermore, aspirin negatively regulates the WNT pathway by inactivating the PPA2 protein to restore β-catenin degradation, subsequently promoting apoptotic cascades [28]. Similarly, the suppression of MMP-9, a crucial driver of extracellular matrix degradation, underscores aspirin’s potent inhibition of invasion. Activated platelets in the tumour microenvironment normally stimulate the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and promote extravasation; aspirin irreversibly inhibits platelet COX-1, effectively severing this platelet-tumour cross-talk [29]. Consequently, the disruption of typical biomarker correlations observed in our aspirin-treated cohort likely reflects this simultaneous modulation of both COX-dependent and COX-independent (WNT/β-catenin) signalling axes, suppressing tumour progression through a synergistic blockade of proliferation and invasion.

Our findings revealed a significant correlation between molecular markers and clinical outcomes, particularly distinguishing between cohorts treated with aspirin and those receiving aspirin-free administration. The aspirin-free cohort exhibited robust correlations between PTGS2 mRNA expression and various protein markers, including COX-2, MMP-9, and Caspase-8, as well as clinical parameters such as tumour stage and survival rates. Conversely, the aspirin-treated cohort disrupted these associations, with PTGS2 showing no correlation with clinical outcomes. This phenomenon suggests that aspirin administration may decouple the established relationship between cyclooxygenase expression and tumour progression. These findings align with recent investigations into aspirin's multifaceted mechanisms in CRC. Aspirin's anti-cancer effects extend beyond simple COX-2 inhibition, including modulation of multiple inflammatory pathways [30]. Similarly, research published in the Journal of Scientific Reports in 2024 revealed that aspirin can prevent colorectal cancer by regulating the gut microbiota and immune cell populations, offering alternative mechanisms for its protective effects [31]. Despite disrupting gene-protein associations in the aspirin-treated group, protein-level correlations with clinical outcomes in both cohorts suggest that aspirin may primarily modulate post-transcriptional regulatory networks. This interpretation is aligned with the fact that aspirin treatment significantly alters the colorectal cancer proteome independently of transcriptional changes [32].

Our unsupervised clustering analyses provided further insight into CRC molecular heterogeneity and its relationship with aspirin response. Using K-means clustering with silhouette analysis, we identified two optimal clusters (k = 2) in the aspirin-treated cohort and three optimal clusters (k = 3) in the aspirin-free cohort. In contrast, a hybrid K-means plus hierarchical clustering approach, which first partitions data into small clusters and then merges them based on linkage distances, identified five clusters (k = 5) as optimal for both cohorts, as determined by the point of maximum acceleration in the dendrogram linkage distances.

Although the hybrid clustering approach mathematically identified this optimal sub-division of five clusters (k = 5) based on fine-scale expression gradients, our subsequent analysis utilised the k = 2 (or k = 3) schema for clinical and prognostic evaluation. This transition was driven by both biological and statistical imperatives. Biologically, the five algorithmic subdivisions did not appear to represent fundamentally divergent functional pathways, but rather varying intensities of the same primary inflammatory and apoptotic states. Furthermore, from a statistical perspective, subdividing the relatively limited sample size (n = 43 per cohort) into five groups would have severely underpowered the Kaplan–Meier survival analyses, substantially increasing the risk of Type II errors. Therefore, aggregating the cohort into two or three primary phenotypes provided the optimal balance between mathematical algorithmic detection, biological relevance, and the statistical power required to reliably assess survival outcomes. We acknowledge, however, that these additional fine-scale clusters may represent meaningful biological heterogeneity, and future studies with larger sample sizes should explore their clinical significance.

In the aspirin-treated cohort, two subtypes emerged (AT-1 and AT-2), distinguished primarily by their protein marker profiles. Subtype AT-1, associated with a trend towards better survival, exhibited higher Caspase-8 and lower COX-2 and MMP-9 protein expression, suggesting enhanced apoptotic signalling and reduced invasion potential. The aspirin-free cohort yielded potentially two or three molecular subtypes, with the two-subtype stratification (AF-1 and AF-2) showing a significant association with survival outcomes. Notably, Subtype AF-2, characterised by higher PTGS2 mRNA and COX-2 protein expression, lower Caspase-8, and higher MMP-9 levels, was associated with significantly poorer survival (16.7% versus 47.4%, P = 0.016).

The existence of multiple optimal cluster solutions, as revealed by different unsupervised methods, highlights the complexity of CRC molecular heterogeneity and underscores the importance of methodological transparency in molecular subtype discovery. Our approach demonstrates that while K-means clustering provides clinically actionable subtypes with clear survival associations, more granular clustering (as identified by the hybrid approach) may uncover additional biologically relevant subgroups that warrant further investigation.

These findings align with the emerging concept of CRC molecular heterogeneity and differential response to aspirin. Studies have identified PIK3CA mutations as potential biomarkers for aspirin response, with mutated tumours showing enhanced sensitivity to aspirin therapy [33, 34]. Our molecular subtypes, defined by prostaglandin pathway markers, may represent an alternative stratification approach for identifying patients most likely to benefit from aspirin treatment. This concept is supported by a 2023 study demonstrating that the anticancer effects of COX-2 inhibitors are particularly pronounced in genetically predisposed individuals with specific molecular signatures [24].

This aligns perfectly with the recent ALASCCA randomised controlled trial, which provided definitive clinical evidence that adjuvant low-dose aspirin (160 mg daily) significantly reduces the 3-year risk of recurrence in CRC patients whose tumours harbour somatic alterations in PI3K pathway genes, such as PIK3CA [13]. The importance of identifying predictive biomarkers for aspirin response is increasingly recognised, with COX-2, PIK3CA mutations, and HLA class I antigens emerging as promising candidates [22]. Our observations of differential PTGS2 expression and distinct protein correlation networks between aspirin-treated and aspirin-free cohorts parallel findings that aspirin influences specific molecular profiles associated with tumour development [11]. The molecular subtypes we identified through unsupervised clustering share characteristics with previously reported aspirin-responsive profiles, particularly regarding COX-2 expression patterns, as studies have shown that aspirin reduces the risk of CRC predominantly in those cancers expressing higher COX-2 [35, 36]. Additionally, our findings of Caspase-8 upregulation in the aspirin-treated cohort align with research demonstrating aspirin's role in activating resolution pathways and reprogramming immune responses in CRC [35]. The identification of AT-1 and AT-2 subtypes in our aspirin-treated cohort, distinguished primarily by differential Caspase-8, COX-2, and MMP-9 expression with associated survival trends, provides further molecular stratification that could refine current approaches to aspirin chemoprevention, which is currently recommended by the United States Preventive Services Task Force for primary prevention of CRC in specific age groups [15]. Given that approximately one-third of CRC patients harbour PI3K pathway alterations and could benefit from adjuvant aspirin therapy [37], our integrated gene and protein expression analysis provides complementary biomarker profiles that may further personalise aspirin-based interventions for both prevention and treatment of CRC. Recent research has demonstrated that aspirin can reduce cancer mortality by 10% in PIK3CA-mutant tumours and by 15% in COX-2-overexpressing tumours [34]. Our study extends these observations by suggesting that integrated analysis of multiple markers across the prostaglandin pathway may provide a more comprehensive prediction model for aspirin response. The strong correlation between PTGS2 expression and clinical outcomes in aspirin-free patients, but not in aspirin-treated patients, particularly supports the potential utility of PTGS2 as a biomarker for aspirin response. Furthermore, our findings regarding aspirin's effects on Caspase-8 and MMP-9 expression suggest possible mechanisms for its anticancer activity beyond simple COX-2 inhibition. This is consistent with recent research demonstrating that aspirin can enhance the efficacy of other therapeutic agents through synergistic effects on apoptotic and invasive pathways [38].

Strengths and limitations

A notable strength of our study is the comprehensive analysis of gene and protein expression in paired tumour and normal tissues from a well-characterised CRC cohort, stratified by aspirin treatment status. Integrating multiple molecular markers across the prostaglandin pathway provides a more nuanced understanding of aspirin's effects than studies focusing solely on COX-2. Furthermore, identifying distinct molecular subtypes through unsupervised clustering represents a novel approach to patient stratification in the context of aspirin therapy.

Several limitations, however, should be acknowledged. The retrospective design and moderate sample size limit the generalisability of our findings. The cross-sectional nature of our tissue analysis does not capture the dynamic changes in gene and protein expression over time or in response to treatment. Crucially, because of the retrospective nature of this cohort, highly granular data on the precise duration, uninterrupted compliance, and exact dosage variations (which ranged widely from 75 to 300 mg daily) of aspirin use prior to surgery were not consistently documented in the clinical records. This broad dosage range may account for the lack of statistical significance observed in certain markers, such as COX-2 protein levels. Consequently, we were unable to perform an exploratory correlation analysis to determine the temporal or dose-dependent effects of aspirin on molecular marker expression. The binary classification of patients as "aspirin-treated" or "aspirin-free" may oversimplify these complex patterns of aspirin use. Given that variations in dosage and treatment duration likely influence these expression profiles, future prospective clinical trials with longitudinal sampling are necessary to evaluate these time- and dose-dependent molecular dynamics and dose–response relationships.

Future directions

Our findings highlight several avenues for future research. Prospective validation of the identified molecular subtypes in larger, multi-institutional cohorts would establish their clinical utility for predicting aspirin response. Longitudinal studies tracking changes in prostaglandin pathway markers before and after aspirin initiation would elucidate the temporal dynamics of aspirin's molecular effects. Integrating advanced genomic and proteomic approaches, such as single-cell analysis and spatial transcriptomics, would provide higher-resolution mapping of aspirin's effects on heterogeneous tumour cell populations. The potential synergistic effects of aspirin with other therapeutic agents, such as oleanolic acid [38] or glutaminase inhibitors like CB-839 [32], warrants further investigation. Our findings regarding distinct molecular subtypes suggest that specific combinations might be more effective in certain patient subgroups. Additionally, the unexpected findings regarding PTGER3 expression highlight the need for more detailed studies on prostaglandin receptor signalling in the context of aspirin therapy. The development of liquid biopsy approaches to monitor prostaglandin pathway markers in circulating tumour DNA or exosomes could facilitate non-invasive monitoring of aspirin response. Recent advances in circulating biomarker technologies make this an increasingly feasible approach for longitudinal patient monitoring. Finally, further mechanistic studies addressing the discrepancy between mRNA and protein expression of COX-2 in aspirin-treated patients would enhance our understanding of post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms in CRC. The integration of RNA-binding protein analyses and studies of microRNA regulation could provide insights into these complex regulatory networks.

Conclusion

Our retrospective study provides novel insights into the molecular effects of aspirin in colorectal cancer through the comprehensive analysis of prostaglandin pathway markers at both the gene and protein levels. The identification of distinct molecular subtypes with differential survival trends suggests preliminary potential for personalised approaches to aspirin therapy in CRC. The observed disruption of correlations between PTGS2 expression and clinical outcomes in aspirin-treated patients may highlight the complex mechanisms by which aspirin modulates cancer-related signalling networks beyond simple COX-2 inhibition. These findings contribute to support the growing evidence supporting aspirin's multifaceted anticancer activities and suggest that integrated molecular profiling has the potential to enhance patient selection for aspirin-based interventions. Future well-designed and large-scale prospective studies are warranted to validate these molecular subtypes and establish their putative clinical utility in guiding personalised treatment strategies for colorectal cancer patients.
