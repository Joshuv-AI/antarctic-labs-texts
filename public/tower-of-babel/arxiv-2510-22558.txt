Surface decomposition method for sensitivity analysis of first-passage dynamic reliability of linear systems
Jianhua Xian, Sai Hung Cheung, Cheng Su

Abstract
This work presents a novel surface decomposition method for the sensitivity analysis of first-passage dynamic reliability of linear systems subjected to Gaussian random excitations. The method decomposes the sensitivity of first-passage failure probability into a sum of surface integrals over the constrained component limit-state hypersurfaces. The evaluation of these surface integrals can be accomplished, owing to the availability of closed-form linear expressions of both the component limit-state functions and their sensitivities for linear systems. An importance sampling strategy is introduced to further enhance the efficiency for estimating the sum of these surface integrals. The number of function evaluations required for the reliability sensitivity analysis is typically on the order of 10 2 10^{2} to 10 3 10^{3} . The approach is particularly advantageous when a large number of design parameters are considered, as the results of function evaluations can be reused across different parameters. Two numerical examples are investigated to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method.

Introduction

Reliability analysis is fundamental for assessing the safety of engineering structures due to the ubiquitous uncertainties arising in structural parameters such as material properties and damping ratios, as well as in external loadings such as wind, waves and ground excitations due to earthquakes
ditlevsen1996structural
,
der1996structural
. In structural dynamics applications, the so-called first-passage dynamic reliability problems have been extensively investigated, where the system failure is defined as the event that any critical performance measure exceeds its prescribed threshold during the stochastic loading process
li2009stochastic
. The first-passage dynamic reliability problems can be addressed by the classical level-crossing-process-based methods with certain assumptions
chen1997study
,
kawano1999dynamic
, which, however, is generally limited to cases only involving a single response process. To overcome these limitations, various reliability approaches have been developed, such as the surrogate-modeling-based methods
wang2017equivalent
,
xian2024physics
,
kim2024dimensionality
, probability-density-evolution-based methods
chen2007extreme
,
chen2019direct
,
xian2021seismic
,
lyu2022unified
and sampling-based methods
au2001estimation
,
bansal2017evaluation
,
xian2024relaxation
.

For first-passage dynamic reliability problems of linear systems subjected to Gaussian random excitations, several advanced sampling techniques have been developed to achieve extremely efficient and highly accurate estimates of the failure probability
au2001first
,
misraji2020application
,
katafygiotis2006domain
,
valdebenito2021failure
. The common feature of these sampling techniques lies in that they fully exploit the system linearity and excitation Gaussianity. The efficient importance sampling
au2001first
constructs its importance density as a weighted sum of the theoretically optimal importance densities associated with the component limit-state functions, in which the weight coefficients are proportional to the component failure probabilities. This idea was further extended in directional sampling, leading to the directional importance sampling
misraji2020application
. The domain decomposition method
katafygiotis2006domain
decomposes the first-passage failure probability into a sum of domain integrals that can be evaluated by the direct Monte Carlo simulation, and an importance sampling scheme is then introduced to estimate their total contribution. The multidomain importance sampling
valdebenito2021failure
also leverages the idea of domain decomposition, where the resulting domain integrals are solved by line sampling, and a similar importance sampling strategy is adopted to estimate
their sum.

Reliability sensitivity analysis aims to quantify and understand the influence of small perturbations of system parameters on the probability of system failure
wu1994computational
,
lu2008reliability
,
papaioannou2018reliability
,
lacaze2015probability
,
talebiyan2020sampling
, which is essential in risk-informed decision making
song2009system
,
taflanidis2011simulation
and reliability-based design optimization
youn2004selecting
,
chun2016structural
. Sensitivity analysis of the first-passage dynamic reliability, which represents a challenging branch of reliability sensitivity analysis, has received increasing attention in recent years
au2005reliability
,
chun2015parameter
,
jensen2015reliability
,
yang2022structural
,
xian2023reliability
. Au
au2005reliability
presented an efficient simulation approach designed for dynamic-reliability-based sensitivity analysis involving only one or two parameters by artificially constructing an augmented reliability problem. Chun et al.
chun2015parameter
developed a sequential compounding method for the parametric sensitivity analysis of general system problems, and then applied this method to a first-passage dynamic reliability problem of a linear system. Jensen et al.
jensen2015reliability
investigated a subset-simulation-based approach to compute the sensitivities of first-excursion failure probability with respect to the distribution parameters of uncertain design variables. Yang et al.
yang2022structural
extended the probability density evolution method for evaluation of the first-passage dynamic reliability sensitivities of nonlinear structures with respect to deterministic design parameters. Xian and Su
xian2023reliability
developed an efficient approach for sensitivity analysis of the first-excursion dynamic reliability of fractionally-damped structures incorporating the explicit time-domain method, adjoint variable method and Poisson process method. Furthermore, building upon the developments confined to linear systems under Gaussian random excitations, the directional importance sampling and domain decomposition method have been extended from dynamic reliability analysis to reliability sensitivity analysis in
valdebenito2021sensitivity
and
misraji2025first
, respectively.

In essence, reliability sensitivity analysis entails the evaluation of a high-dimensional integral over the limit-state hypersurface, which is generally challenging to compute
papaioannou2018reliability
, particularly for first-passage dynamic reliability problems characterized by highly non-smooth and nonlinear limit-state hypersurfaces. Fortunately, in the case of linear systems subjected to Gaussian excitations, the above complex surface integrals can be evaluated with remarkable efficiency. This work presents a novel surface decomposition method to accomplish this purpose. The core concept of this method is to decompose the complex surface integral into a sum of simpler surface integrals over the constrained component limit-state hypersurfaces. The so-called constrained component hypersurface corresponds exactly to the active part of the corresponding component hypersurface that contributes to the system limit-state hypersurface. The effectiveness of this strategy lies in the ease of computing these component surface integrals, as closed-form linear expressions of both the component limit-state functions and their sensitivities are available. An importance sampling scheme is further introduced to estimate the total contribution of these component surface integrals. The sensitivity analyses of the first-passage dynamic reliability problems for an oscillator and a 20-degree-of-freedom shear-type structure are conducted to demonstrate the feasibility of the proposed method.

2
Explicit formulation of dynamic response and sensitivity for linear systems

2.1
Explicit expressions of dynamic responses

The equation of motion for a linear system can be expressed as

\boldsymbol{M}\ddot{\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{X},t)+\boldsymbol{C}\dot{\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{X},t)+\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{X},t)=\boldsymbol{L}F(\boldsymbol{X},t)

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{M}
,
\boldsymbol{C}
and
\boldsymbol{K}
are the mass, damping and stiffness matrices of the linear system, respectively;
\ddot{\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{X},t)
,
\dot{\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{X},t)
and
\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{X},t)
are the acceleration, velocity and displacement vectors of the linear system, respectively;
F(\boldsymbol{X},t)
is the external excitation assumed to be a zero-mean Gaussian random process;
\boldsymbol{X}
is the input random vector associated with the external excitation; and
\boldsymbol{L}
is the orientation vector of
F(\boldsymbol{X},t)
.

Suppose that the linear system is initially at rest. Then, from the discrete perspective of the convolution integral, any response
r(\boldsymbol{X},t)
of the linear system can be written in an explicit time-domain form as follows:

r_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})=a^{r}_{i,1}F_{1}(\boldsymbol{X})+a^{r}_{i,2}F_{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+\cdots+a^{r}_{i,i}F_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n)

(2)

where
n
is the number of time steps for the time-history analysis;
r_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})=r(\boldsymbol{X},t_{i})
with
t_{i}=i\Delta t
and
\Delta t
being the time step;
F_{j}(\boldsymbol{X})=F(\boldsymbol{X},t_{j})
with
t_{j}=j\Delta t\;(j=1,2,\dots,i)
;
a^{r}_{i,j}\;(j=1,2,\dots,i)
are the coefficients with respect to
r_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})
.

The physical meaning and computational scheme of the coefficients
a^{r}_{i,j}\;(1\leq j\leq i\leq n)
have been well discussed in
su2014random
. For these coefficients, only
a^{r}_{i,1}\;(1\leq i\leq n)
need to be calculated and stored, while the other coefficients can be determined from the recursive formula
a^{r}_{i,j}=a^{r}_{i-1,j-1}\;(2\leq j\leq i\leq n)
. The coefficients
a^{r}_{i,1}\;(1\leq i\leq n)
represent the response
r(\boldsymbol{X},t)
of the linear system at time
t=t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{n}
subjected to a unit impulse excitation applied at time
t=t_{1}
. Therefore, by performing a single response time-history analysis of the linear system shown in Eq.(
1
) with any type of time-domain integration methods, the coefficients
a^{r}_{i,j}\;(1\leq j\leq i\leq n)
with respect to any response
r(\boldsymbol{X},t)
of interest can be readily obtained.

The Gaussian random excitation
F(\boldsymbol{X},t)
can be characterized by either its correlation function
R_{F}(t,\tau)
or its power spectrum
S_{F}(\omega,t)
. Given the correlation function
R_{F}(t,\tau)
, the excitation at time
t=t_{i}
can be represented, based on the orthogonal decomposition method
grigoriu2006evaluation
, as

F_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})=\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\boldsymbol{X}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n)

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{X}
is a standard Gaussian random vector with a dimension of
d=n
; and
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
is the
i
-th row vector of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Psi}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1/2}
, in which
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
are respectively the orthonormal eigenvector matrix and the diagonal eigenvalue matrix of the following covariance matrix:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}R_{F}(t_{1},t_{1})&R_{F}(t_{1},t_{2})&\cdots&R_{F}(t_{1},t_{n})\\
R_{F}(t_{2},t_{1})&R_{F}(t_{2},t_{2})&\cdots&R_{F}(t_{2},t_{n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
R_{F}(t_{n},t_{1})&R_{F}(t_{n},t_{2})&\cdots&R_{F}(t_{n},t_{n})\end{bmatrix}

(4)

If the power spectrum
S_{F}(\omega,t)
is available, the excitation at time
t=t_{i}
can also be expressed in the same form as Eq.(
3
) utilizing the spectral representation method
deodatis2025spectral
. In this case,
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
is written as

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}=\sqrt{2\Delta\omega}\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{S_{F}(\omega_{1},t_{i})}\cos(\omega_{1}t_{i})\\
\sqrt{S_{F}(\omega_{2},t_{i})}\cos(\omega_{2}t_{i})\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{S_{F}(\omega_{q},t_{i})}\cos(\omega_{q}t_{i})\\
\sqrt{S_{F}(\omega_{1},t_{i})}\sin(\omega_{1}t_{i})\\
\sqrt{S_{F}(\omega_{2},t_{i})}\sin(\omega_{2}t_{i})\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{S_{F}(\omega_{q},t_{i})}\sin(\omega_{q}t_{i})\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n)

(5)

where
q
is the number of circular frequency intervals;
\Delta\omega=(\omega_{\max}-\omega_{\min})/q
is the circular frequency interval with
\omega_{\max}
and
\omega_{\min}
being the upper and lower cutoff circular frequencies, respectively; and
\omega_{k}=\omega_{\min}+(k-0.5)\Delta\omega\;(k=1,2,\dots,q)
is the circular frequency in the middle interval. Note that, when
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
is expressed in Eq.(
5
), the dimension of the standard Gaussian random vector
\boldsymbol{X}
is
d=2q
.

Substitution of Eq.(
3
) into Eq.(
2
) yields a more compact explicit expression of
r(\boldsymbol{X},t)
as follows:

r_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})=\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{r}\boldsymbol{X}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n)

(6)

where

\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{r}=a^{r}_{i,1}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}+a^{r}_{i,2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}+\cdots+a^{r}_{i,i}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}

(7)

is the coefficient vector with respect to
r_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})
. Eq.(
6
) indicates that the response of the linear system at any given time instant can be written as a linear combination of all the random variables contained in the input random vector
\boldsymbol{X}
.

It is noted that, for brevity, this study merely focuses on a single random excitation in the problem formulation. However, the extension to cases involving multiple random excitations is straightforward, with the number of response time-history analyses required for explicit formulation being equal to the number of random excitations.

2.2
Explicit expressions of response sensitivities

Suppose
\theta
is a design parameter of the linear system shown in Eq.(
1
). Then, differentiating all the terms in Eq.(
1
) with respect to the design parameter
\theta
, one can derive the sensitivity equation for the linear system as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}\frac{\partial\ddot{\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{X},t)}{\partial\theta}+\boldsymbol{C}\frac{\partial\dot{\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{X},t)}{\partial\theta}+\boldsymbol{K}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{X},t)}{\partial\theta}

(8)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{L}}{\partial\theta}F(\boldsymbol{X},t)-\left\{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{M}}{\partial\theta}\ddot{\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{X},t)+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{C}}{\partial\theta}\dot{\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{X},t)+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{K}}{\partial\theta}\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{X},t)\right\}

where
\partial\boldsymbol{M}/\partial\theta
,
\partial\boldsymbol{C}/\partial\theta
,
\partial\boldsymbol{K}/\partial\theta
and
\partial\boldsymbol{L}/\partial\theta
denote the sensitivities of the matrices
\boldsymbol{M}
,
\boldsymbol{C}
,
\boldsymbol{K}
and
\boldsymbol{L}
with respect to
\theta
, respectively; and
\partial\ddot{\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{X},t)/\partial\theta
,
\partial\dot{\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{X},t)/\partial\theta
and
\partial\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{X},t)/\partial\theta
denote the sensitivities of the response vectors
\ddot{\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{X},t)
,
\dot{\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{X},t)
and
\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{X},t)
with respect to
\theta
, respectively.

Differentiation of both sides of Eq.(
2
) with respect to the design parameter
\theta
yields the explicit time-domain form of the response sensitivity
\partial r(\boldsymbol{X},t)/\partial\theta
as follows:

\frac{\partial r_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})}{\partial\theta}=b^{r}_{i,1}F_{1}(\boldsymbol{X})+b^{r}_{i,2}F_{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+\cdots+b^{r}_{i,i}F_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n)

(9)

where
b^{r}_{i,j}=\partial a^{r}_{i,j}/\partial\theta\;(j=1,2,\dots,i)
are the coefficients with respect to
\partial r_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})/\partial\theta
.

The coefficients
b^{r}_{i,j}\;(1\leq j\leq i\leq n)
can be derived via direct differentiation of the coefficients
a^{r}_{i,j}\;(1\leq j\leq i\leq n)
in Eq.(
2
) with respect to
\theta
. However, this requires knowledge of the analytical formulas of
a^{r}_{i,j}\;(1\leq j\leq i\leq n)
. In fact, similar to the coefficients
a^{r}_{i,j}\;(1\leq j\leq i\leq n)
, the coefficients
b^{r}_{i,j}\;(1\leq j\leq i\leq n)
also possess inherent physical meanings, and a detailed computational scheme of these coefficients have been presented in
hu2016explicit
. Among these coefficients, only
b^{r}_{i,1}\;(1\leq i\leq n)
need to be calculated and stored, while a recursive formula
b^{r}_{i,j}=b^{r}_{i-1,j-1}\;(2\leq j\leq i\leq n)
can be adopted to determine the other coefficients. The coefficients
b^{r}_{i,1}\;(1\leq i\leq n)
represent the response sensitivity
\partial r(\boldsymbol{X},t)/\partial\theta
of the linear system at time
t=t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{n}
driven by a unit impulse excitation applied at time
t=t_{1}
. Therefore, by conducting a single response sensitivity time-history analysis based on the sensitivity equation of the linear system presented in Eq.(
8
), the coefficients
b^{r}_{i,j}\;(1\leq j\leq i\leq n)
can be easily obtained for the sensitivity of any response
\partial r(\boldsymbol{X},t)/\partial\theta
. The above response sensitivity time-history analysis can be achieved using any type of time-domain integration methods.

Similar to the derivation of Eq.(
6
), substitution of Eq.(
3
) into Eq.(
9
) results in the following compact explicit expression of
\partial r(\boldsymbol{X},t)/\partial\theta
as

\frac{\partial r_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})}{\partial\theta}=\boldsymbol{b}_{i}^{r}\boldsymbol{X}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n)

(10)

where

\boldsymbol{b}_{i}^{r}=b^{r}_{i,1}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}+b^{r}_{i,2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}+\cdots+b^{r}_{i,i}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}

(11)

is the coefficient vector with respect to
\partial r_{i}(\boldsymbol{X})/\partial\theta
.

It is noted that, when
\theta
is associated with the external excitation
F(\boldsymbol{X},t)
, for instance as a parameter defining the correlation function
R_{F}(t,\tau)
or the power spectrum
S_{F}(\omega,t)
, the explicit expression of
\partial r(\boldsymbol{X},t)/\partial\theta
can be obtained by direct differentiation of Eqs.(
6
) and (
7
), without performing any additional response sensitivity time-history analysis of the linear system.

Thus far, the explicit expressions for both the dynamic responses and their sensitivities have been constructed for the linear system, as shown in Eqs.(
6
) and (
10
), respectively. The availability of such concise closed-form expressions greatly facilitates the sensitivity analysis of first-passage dynamic reliability, which will be demonstrated in Section
3
.

3
Surface decomposition method for sensitivity analysis of first-passage

dynamic reliability

3.1
First-passage dynamic reliability

For first-passage dynamic reliability problems of linear systems, the system failure event is typically defined as the exceedance of the prescribed threshold by any response component of interest at any time instant within a specified time duration. The system failure event can be formulated as the union of the component failure events, i.e.

F=\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}F_{ki}

(12)

where
m
is the number of critical response components;
n
is the number of time steps for the given time interval; and
F_{ki}
is the component failure event corresponding to the
k
th critical response component
s_{k}(\boldsymbol{X},t)
at the time instant
t_{i}
, which can be defined as

F_{ki}=\left\{c_{k}-s_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{X})\leq 0\right\}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(13)

where
s_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{X})=s_{k}(\boldsymbol{X},t_{i})
; and
c_{k}
is the positive threshold for the
k
th critical response component. Note that the formulation is presented for up-crossing of positive thresholds, while extension to down-crossing of negative thresholds or simultaneous consideration of both cases is straightforward.

The probability of the component failure event
F_{ki}
is given by

\mathbb{P}_{ki}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{H}(-g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x}))f_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,d\boldsymbol{x}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(14)

where

g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=c_{k}-s_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{x})\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(15)

is the component limit-state function with respect to
F_{ki}
;
f_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the joint probability density function (PDF) of the standard Gaussian random vector
\boldsymbol{X}
; and
\mathcal{H}(\cdot)
denotes the Heaviside step function, with
\mathcal{H}(-g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x}))
taking 1 if
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0
and 0 otherwise.

Let
r(\boldsymbol{X},t)
be
s_{k}(\boldsymbol{X},t)
. Substituting Eq.(
6
) into Eq.(
15
), one has

g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=c_{k}-\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{s_{k}}\boldsymbol{x}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(16)

Since the limit-state hypersurface described by
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=c_{k}-\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{s_{k}}\boldsymbol{x}=0
is a hyperplane, closed-form solution to the failure probability
\mathbb{P}_{ki}
can be obtained as

\mathbb{P}_{ki}=\Phi(-\beta_{ki})\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(17)

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the standard Gaussian cumulative distribution function (CDF); and
\beta_{ki}
is the reliability index with respect to
F_{ki}
, which can be expressed as

\beta_{ki}=\frac{\mu_{g_{ki}}}{\sigma_{g_{ki}}}=\frac{c_{k}}{\left\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{s_{k}}\right\|}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(18)

where
\mu_{g_{ki}}
and
\sigma_{g_{ki}}
are the mean and standard deviation of the Gaussian random variable
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{X})
; and
\left\|\cdot\right\|
is the Euclidean norm.

The reliability index
\beta_{ki}
can be interpreted geometrically as the Euclidean distance between the origin and the design point
\boldsymbol{x}_{ki}^{\star}
. The design point
\boldsymbol{x}_{ki}^{\star}
, also referred to as the most probable point, is defined as the point on the limit-state hypersurface
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
that lies closest to the origin and can be expressed as

\boldsymbol{x}_{ki}^{\star}=\beta_{ki}\boldsymbol{u}_{ki}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(19)

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{ki}
is the unit vector pointing from the origin to the design point, which is aligned with the normalized gradient of the limit-state function
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})
and hence perpendicular to the hyperplane
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
. The unit vector is given by

\boldsymbol{u}_{ki}=-\frac{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\left\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\|}=\frac{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{s_{k}}}{\left\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{s_{k}}\right\|}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(20)

where
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})
denotes the gradient of
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

Although the probabilities of individual component failure events can be readily computed in closed form by Eqs.(
17
) and (
18
), an analytical evaluation of the system failure probability is not feasible. The probability of the system failure event
F
can be expressed as

\mathbb{P}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{H}(-G(\boldsymbol{x}))f_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,d\boldsymbol{x}

(21)

where
G(\boldsymbol{x})
is the system limit-state function written as

G(\boldsymbol{x})=\min_{k=1}^{m}\min_{i=1}^{n}g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})

(22)

By exploiting the system linearity and leveraging the analytical solutions to the component failure probabilities, certain advanced simulation techniques, such as the efficient importance sampling
au2001first
and the domain decomposition method
katafygiotis2006domain
, were proposed for solving the probability integral in Eq.(
21
) at high efficiency.

3.2
Sensitivity of first-passage dynamic reliability

Differentiation of the system failure probability formulated in Eq.(
21
) with respect to the design parameter
\theta
leads to the sensitivity of system failure probability as follows:

\frac{\partial\mathbb{P}}{\partial\theta}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\delta(-G(\boldsymbol{x}))\frac{\partial G(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\theta}f_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,d\boldsymbol{x}

(23)

where
\delta(\cdot)
denotes the Dirac delta function; and
\partial G(\boldsymbol{x})/\partial\theta
is the sensitivity of the system limit-state function
G(\boldsymbol{x})
with respect to
\theta
.

Considering the system limit-state function shown in Eq.(
22
), the term
\delta(-G(\boldsymbol{x}))
in Eq.(
23
) can be expanded as

\delta(-G(\boldsymbol{x}))=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta(-g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x}))\mathbb{I}_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})

(24)

where
\mathbb{I}_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})
denotes the indicator function that gives 1 if
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})
attains the minimum among all component limit-state functions, and 0 otherwise. The indicator function can be expressed as

\mathbb{I}_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=\min\limits_{j=1}^{m}\min\limits_{s=1}^{n}g_{js}(\boldsymbol{x}),\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(25)

The expansion presented in Eq.(
24
) decomposes the delta function of the system limit-state function into a sum of the delta functions of the individual component limit-state functions, with the indicator functions serving to partition the domain such that only the component reaching the minimum is active within each subdomain. It is noted that the indicator functions shown in Eq.(
25
) also provide an effective way to partition the system failure domain in the domain decomposition method for first-passage dynamic reliability analysis.

Differentiating the system limit-state function in Eq.(
22
) with respect to the design parameter
\theta
, and leveraging the indicator functions defined in Eq.(
25
), one can obtain the sensitivity of the system limit-state function as

\frac{\partial G(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\theta}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\theta}\mathbb{I}_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})

(26)

where
\partial g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})/\partial\theta
is the sensitivity of the component limit-state function
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})
with respect to
\theta
, which can be derived from Eq.(
15
) as follows:

\frac{\partial g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\theta}=-\frac{\partial s_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\theta}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(27)

where
\partial s_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{x})/\partial\theta
is the sensitivity of the
k
th critical response component
s_{k}(\boldsymbol{X},t)
at the time instant
t_{i}
with respect to
\theta
. Let
\partial r(\boldsymbol{X},t)/\partial\theta=\partial s_{k}(\boldsymbol{X},t)/\partial\theta
. Substituting Eq.(
10
) into Eq.(
27
), one gets

\frac{\partial g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\theta}=-\boldsymbol{b}_{i}^{s_{k}}\boldsymbol{x}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(28)

Substitution of Eqs.(
24
) and (
26
) into Eq.(
23
) yields the expansion for the sensitivity of system failure probability as follows:

\frac{\partial\mathbb{P}}{\partial\theta}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{ki}

(29)

where

\eta_{ki}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\delta(-g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x}))\frac{\partial g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\theta}\mathbb{I}_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})f_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,d\boldsymbol{x}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(30)

Since each component limit-state function
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})
is continuously differentiable with a non-zero constant gradient, the
d
-dimensional volume integral in Eq.(
30
) can be converted into a
(d-1)
-dimensional surface integral as follows
hormander2003analysis
:

\eta_{ki}=-\int_{g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=0}\frac{1}{\left\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\|}\frac{\partial g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial\theta}\mathbb{I}_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})f_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,dS(\boldsymbol{x})\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(31)

where
dS(\boldsymbol{x})
denotes the differential element of the
(d-1)
-dimensional hyperplane defined by
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
; and
\left\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\|
is the Euclidean norm of the gradient of the component limit-state function
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})
, which can be obtained from Eq.(
16
) as follows:

\left\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\|=\left\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{s_{k}}\right\|\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(32)

Since the integration in Eq.(
31
) is performed over the
(d-1)
-dimensional hyperplane
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
, the indicator function shown in Eq.(
25
) can be further expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }g_{js}(\boldsymbol{x})>0,\;\forall(j,s)\neq(k,i),\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(33)

Essentially, the indicator function
\mathbb{I}_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})
imposes additional constraints, restricting the integration to the portion of the hyperplane where all other component limit-state functions remain in their safe domains.

It should be noted that the constrained hyperplane
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
corresponds exactly to the portion of the system limit-state hypersurface contributed by the corresponding component limit-state hypersurface. The union of all these constrained hyperplanes, which are mutually exclusive, constitutes the complete system limit-state hypersurface.
Consequently, computing the sensitivity of the first-passage dynamic reliability amounts to integrating over a non-smooth system limit-state hypersurface. In this sense, Eqs.(
29
) and (
31
) can be interpreted as decomposing this complex surface integral into a set of simpler integrals over the constrained hyperplanes—a strategy referred to as the
surface decomposition method
. The basic idea of the proposed surface decomposition method is further illustrated in Figure
1
using a 2-dimensional toy problem.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of surface decomposition method using a 2-dimensional problem.
Consider a series system limit-state function
G(\boldsymbol{x})=\min_{k=1}^{4}g_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
. The four component limit-state functions are
g_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})=12-(-2x_{1}+x_{2})
,
g_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=18-(-x_{1}+3x_{2})
,
g_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})=36-(6x_{1}+7x_{2})
and
g_{4}(\boldsymbol{x})=10-(2x_{1}-x_{2})
, respectively. The system failure boundary is composed of four constrained component failure boundaries
g_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})=0\;(k=1,2,3,4)
, in which the indicator function
\mathbb{I}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
takes 1 if
g_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})>0,\forall j\neq k
, and 0 otherwise. The sensitivity of series system failure probability can be decomposed into four line integrals over these four constrained component failure boundaries, respectively. These line integrals will become surface integrals over the constrained component failure surfaces in higher-dimensional problems.

Thus far, substituting Eqs.(
28
) and (
32
) into Eq.(
31
), the surface integral
\eta_{ki}
can be directly estimated with the Monte Carlo simulation as follows:

\eta_{ki}\approx\frac{1}{N_{ki}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{ki}}\frac{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}^{s_{k}}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}{\left\|\boldsymbol{a}_{i}^{s_{k}}\right\|}\mathbb{I}_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\varphi(\beta_{ki})\quad(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)

(34)

where
\varphi(\cdot)
is the standard Gaussian PDF;
\beta_{ki}
is the reliability index associated with the component limit-state function
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})
, whose closed-form solution is given in Eq.(
18
); and
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\;(j=1,2,\dots,N_{ki})
are samples generated from the PDF
f_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})
lying on the hyperplane
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
, with
N_{ki}
being the number of samples.

Direct simulation of sample points on the hyperplane
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
can be accomplished as follows: i) generate a sample point
\boldsymbol{x}
based on the PDF
f_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})
; ii)
decompose
\boldsymbol{x}
into a component that aligns with the unit vector
\boldsymbol{u}_{ki}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{\parallel}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{u}_{ki})\boldsymbol{u}_{ki}
, and a component that is perpendicular to
\boldsymbol{x}_{\parallel}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{\perp}=\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\parallel}
; iii) construct
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}=\beta_{ki}\boldsymbol{u}_{ki}+\boldsymbol{x}_{\perp}
, which represents a sample point lying independently on the hyperplane
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
.

It should be noted that the computation of the surface integral
\eta_{ki}
via Monte Carlo simulation is trivial, as generating samples that fall into the mutual safe domain of all other component limit-state functions poses no particular difficulty. However, if the number of the surface integrals
\eta_{ki}\;(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)
is large, the computational cost for the sensitivity of system failure probability shown in Eq.(
29
) could still be significant. To reduce the computational effort, an importance sampling strategy that has been developed in the domain decomposition method
katafygiotis2006domain
for first-passage dynamic reliability analysis is introduced herein. The sum in Eq.(
29
) can be rewritten as

\frac{\partial\mathbb{P}}{\partial\theta}=mn\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{ki}f(k,i)

(35)

where
f(k,i)=1/(mn)
defines a discrete uniform probability mass function (PMF) over the index pairs
(k,i)\;(i=1,2,\dots,n;k=1,2,\dots,m)
.

Introducing an importance sampling PMF
h(k,i)
to Eq.(
35
), one yields

\frac{\partial\mathbb{P}}{\partial\theta}=mn\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{ki}\frac{f(k,i)}{h(k,i)}h(k,i)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{ki}\frac{1}{h(k,i)}h(k,i)\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\eta_{K_{j}I_{j}}\frac{1}{h(K_{j},I_{j})}

(36)

where
(K_{j},I_{j})\;(j=1,2,\dots,N)
are the index pairs sampled from
h(k,i)
, with
N
being the number of samples; and the surface integral
\eta_{K_{j}I_{j}}
can be estimated by Eq.(
34
).

There exists a theoretically optimal importance sampling PMF that results in a zero-variance estimate, i.e.

h^{\star}(k,i)=\frac{\eta_{ki}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\eta_{js}}

(37)

However, direct use of this optimal importance sampling PMF is not possible, since the denominator
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\eta_{js}
is exactly the sensitivity of system failure probability
\partial\mathbb{P}/\partial\theta
to be estimated.

A good choice of the importance sampling PMF is to construct it based on the closed-form solutions of the component failure probabilities, i.e.

h(k,i)=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{ki}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}_{js}}

(38)

The rationale of this importance sampling PMF is that a higher component failure probability
\mathbb{P}_{ki}
typically indicates a larger measure of the constrained hyperplane
g_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
. Consequently, the corresponding surface integral
\eta_{ki}
contributes more significantly to the sensitivity of system failure probability.

Finally, the sampling scheme described by Eqs.(
34
) and (
36
) can be combined into a single Monte Carlo estimator as follows:

\frac{\partial\mathbb{P}}{\partial\theta}\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{h(K_{j},I_{j})}\frac{\boldsymbol{b}_{I_{j}}^{s_{K_{j}}}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}{\left\|\boldsymbol{a}_{I_{j}}^{s_{K_{j}}}\right\|}\mathbb{I}_{K_{j}I_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\varphi(\beta_{K_{j}I_{j}})

(39)

where
N
is the number of samples;
(K_{j},I_{j})
is the index pair sampled from the importance sampling PMF
h(k,i)
in Eq.(
38
); and
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
is sampled from the hyperplane
g_{K_{j}I_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
.

It should be noted that evaluating the indicator function
\mathbb{I}_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})
in Eq.(
33
) is equivalent to calling the system limit-state function
G(\boldsymbol{x})
in Eq.(
22
), as both operations necessitate the computation of all component limit-state functions. Apart from evaluating the indicator function
\mathbb{I}_{ki}(\boldsymbol{x})
, the computational cost of the other operations in Eq.(
39
) is negligible. Therefore, the overall computational cost of the estimator in Eq.(
39
) amounts to
N
evaluations of the system limit-state function
G(\boldsymbol{x})
.

Furthermore, it can be seen from Eq.(
39
) that the results of the indicator functions can be reused across different design parameters, meaning that the computational cost does not increase as the number of design parameters increases. This feature is particularly advantageous when sensitivities with respect to a large number of design parameters are required, as is commonly the case in reliability-based topology optimization.

Conclusions

A novel surface decomposition method has been proposed for highly efficient and accurate sensitivity analysis of the first-passage dynamic reliability of linear systems subjected to Gaussian random excitations. To circumvent the direct computation of a complex integral over a non-smooth system limit-state hypersurface involved in the reliability sensitivity analysis, the proposed method decomposes this complex surface integral into a sum of simpler surface integrals over the constrained component hyperplanes. These component surface integrals can be evaluated at high efficiency for two main reasons: i) closed-form expressions are available for the component limit-state functions and their sensitivities for a linear system, which results in an explicit simulation of the component surface integral; ii) each constrained component hyperplane lies entirely within the mutual safe domain of all the other component limit-state functions, which leads to a straightforward generation of samples on the constrained component hyperplane. An effective importance sampling strategy is introduced to estimate the sum of all these component surface integrals, which is essential when a large number of component limit-state functions are involved.

A key advantage of the surface decomposition method lies in its ability to reuse function evaluation results for sensitivity analyses with respect to different design parameters, thereby substantially reducing the computational cost when a large number of parameters are considered.
The effectiveness of the proposed method has been validated by two first-passage dynamic reliability problems of linear systems. It has been found that, for the numerical examples studied, only less than 2000 function evaluations are required to obtain the sensitivity estimates of the first-passage failure probability with respect to all design parameters with a target coefficient of variation of 0.1. Furthermore, similar to the efficient importance sampling and the domain decomposition method for first-passage dynamic reliability analysis, the surface decomposition method for reliability sensitivity analysis also demonstrates a higher efficiency as the failure probability becomes smaller. The proposed method exhibits great potential for reliability-based parametric and topology optimization.
