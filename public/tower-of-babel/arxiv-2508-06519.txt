Comparative Simulation of CEvNS Recoil Observables in Geant4 Using Germanium, Argon, and Xenon Targets
Yusuf Havvat

Abstract




1
Abstract

Coherent Elastic Neutrino-Nucleus Scattering (CEvNS) is a low-energy process in which a neutrino interacts coherently with an entire nucleus, leading to a detectable nuclear recoil. This study presents a detailed Geant4-based simulation of CEvNS events for three commonly used target materials in neutrino detection: Germanium, Argon, and Xenon. A custom CEvNS process was developed and integrated into the Geant4 framework, incorporating realistic differential cross-section models and the Helm form factor. ROOT-based analysis was used to evaluate recoil energy spectra, angular distributions, and form factor suppression effects for each target. The results confirm expected physical trends: heavier nuclei like Xenon produce smaller recoil energies and exhibit stronger form factor suppression compared to lighter nuclei. This work demonstrates a complete end-to-end simulation of CEvNS from particle generation to recoil analysis and offers a reproducible framework that can support both theoretical modeling and experimental interpretation in neutrino physics and dark matter search experiments

İntroduction

Coherent Elastic Neutrino-Nucleus Scattering (CEvNS) is a neutral-current process predicted by the Standard Model, in which a neutrino scatters elastically off an entire nucleus via the exchange of a Z boson. The cross-section scales approximately with the square of the neutron number, making CEvNS a highly promising interaction for low-energy neutrino detection.

Although CEvNS was theoretically proposed by Freedman in 1974, it remained undetected for over four decades due to its low-energy nuclear recoil signatures, which are challenging to measure. The first observation was reported by the COHERENT collaboration in 2017 using a CsI(Tl) scintillator detector. Since then, experiments like CONNIE, MINER, and CONUS have explored various detector technologies and target materials.

The simulation and analysis of CEvNS are essential for designing future neutrino detectors and for interpreting results from dark matter experiments, where CEvNS represents an irreducible background. This study aims to provide a comparative modeling framework using Germanium, Argon, and Xenon—three of the most relevant target nuclei in current neutrino and dark matter research.

The primary objective of this study is to simulate and compare CEvNS interactions on different target nuclei—Germanium, Argon, and Xenon—under identical initial conditions using Geant4. By analyzing recoil energy, angular distribution, and form factor suppression, the work aims to evaluate the suitability of each material for low-energy neutrino detection. This simulation serves as a comparative model for selecting detector materials in future CEvNS or dark matter experiments.

3
Methodology

This study employs the Geant4 simulation framework to model the Coherent Elastic Neutrino-Nucleus Scattering (CEvNS) process for three different target nuclei: Germanium, Argon, and Xenon. The simulation architecture is designed to realistically reflect the kinematics and cross sections of CEvNS interactions at low energies.

A custom CEvNS process was implemented using C++ within the Geant4 framework. This process includes a differential cross section based on the Standard Model prediction, incorporating the weak nuclear charge, nuclear recoil energy, and the neutrino energy spectrum.

To account for nuclear structure effects, the Helm form factor is applied to the scattering amplitude. The form factor is expressed as:

F(q^{2})=3\times\left[\frac{j_{1}(qR_{0})}{qR_{0}}\right]\times\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}q^{2}s^{2}\right)

In this equation, j1 is the spherical Bessel function of the first kind, q is the momentum transfer, R0 is the effective nuclear radius, and s is the surface thickness parameter. This formulation reduces the cross section at high momentum transfer, particularly for heavy nuclei.

3.1
Detector Information

In the simulation environment, the detector is defined as a cubic volume. The detector volume is filled with the target material to be analyzed. Germanium (Ge), Argon (Ar), and Xenon (Xe) nuclei were defined separately, and simulations were run independently for each target material. The detector’s exterior is surrounded by a vacuum, and the outer volume is defined as the ”world volume.” The world volume was chosen to be large compared to the detector to prevent edge effects.

3.2
Particle Production and Energy Spectrum

Neutrino-like particles were created in the simulation to trigger the CEvNS process. These particles:
The electron-type neutrino is defined by its character (G4NeutrinoE). It is designed to have an energy spectrum that is close to constant or Gaussian, extending up to 10 MeV. The direction of momentum is defined as being along the z-axis and is directed towards the center of the detector. This process is configured with the PrimaryGeneratorAction class, and a single neutrino production is provided for each event.

3.3
Definition of the CEvNS Process

The CEvNS process is not defined by default in the Geant4 standard library. Therefore, a user-defined physical process called CEvNSProcess was specifically created within the scope of this study. This process:

It is based on the elastic scattering model. It operates with a single core per event. Includes differential cross-section information (optionally integrated with the Helm form factor). The Helm model was used as the form factor, and definitions were made that considered the nuclear structure of the cores. The momentum transfer q was used to calculate the form factor F(q²), which was then integrated to affect the scattering probability.

3.4
Physics List

The PhysicsList class used in the simulation is specifically configured. This list includes:
G4EmStandardPhysics: Electromagnetic processes
G4DecayPhysics: Unstable particle decays
CEvNSProcess: Coherent Elastic Neutrino-Nucleus Scattering process
Thanks to this structure, the CEvNS process was carried out in a controlled manner alongside other physical processes.

3.5
Data Recording and Analysis

At the end of each event, information such as: the kinetic energy of the recoiling nucleus, time information, position and momentum direction (theta), and the form factor used F(q²), is provided through the RootManager class. It has been saved to the root file. The data was then extracted via TTree and analyzed in ROOT using objects such as TCanvas, TH1F, and TH2F. Each graph is then saved using the SaveAs() command with the . It has been exported in PDF format.

5
Results

The simulation results highlight the distinct behavior of different target materials under CEvNS:

Recoil Energy Distributions: Germanium exhibits higher recoil energies compared to Xenon due to its lower mass. Xenon, while having a larger CEvNS cross-section due to a higher neutron number, yields lower energy recoils, often below 1 keV.

Angular Distributions: Recoil angles are primarily forward-peaked, consistent with elastic scattering kinematics. Heavier nuclei such as Xenon show tighter angular cones due to momentum conservation constraints.

Form Factor Effects: The Helm form factor introduces significant suppression in Xenon for large momentum transfers, while its effect on Germanium is less severe. This matches theoretical expectations and validates the modeling.

Time Distributions: The timing of the recoils does not show target-dependent deviations, reflecting the constant neutrino generation conditions in the simulation.

All results are presented graphically and show clear, physics-consistent trends across different nuclei.

6
Discussion

The comparative analysis among Germanium, Argon, and Xenon reveals that no single target is universally superior—each has unique advantages depending on the detector’s goals:

Germanium: Offers relatively high recoil energies and moderate neutron content. Its well-developed detector technology (e.g., HPGe) makes it ideal for low-threshold applications.

Argon: Cost-effective and scalable, with higher energy thresholds. It provides a balance between recoil detectability and target mass availability.

Xenon: Exhibits the largest CEvNS cross-section but generates the lowest recoil energies. The strong form factor suppression in Xenon makes detection more challenging, yet it remains a leading choice in dark matter experiments for its self-shielding and scalability.

These trends align with experimental findings and demonstrate that simulation tools like Geant4 can accurately reproduce CEvNS behavior across different detector materials.

These simulation trends align with experimental observations. The COHERENT experiment, which used CsI and later liquid Argon targets, reported similar recoil behaviors and confirmed the relevance of form factor suppression in heavier nuclei. Similarly, experiments such as CONNIE and CONUS, operating with Germanium detectors, have highlighted the material’s balance between recoil visibility and background control. Our results support these experimental preferences and provide theoretical reinforcement from a simulation perspective.

Energy Distribution Comparison

The recoil nucleus energies exhibited a low-energy distribution, reflecting the low-momentum transfer structure of CEvNS in all target nuclei. Average recoil is as follows:

Germanium: 16.3 keV

Argon: 19.8 keV

Xenon: 6.5 keV

These differences are related to the mass of the nucleus. In light nuclei (such as Argon), the recoiling nucleus gains more kinetic energy, while in heavy nuclei (such as Xenon), energy transfer is limited and the distribution shifts towards lower energies. This situation shows that the detector thresholds in the experiments play a critical role in material selection. Keeping the threshold value very low in heavy nuclei like xenon will enable more effective detection of CEvNS events.

6.2
Time-Energy Relationship

No significant time structure was observed in the time-energy histograms for any of the three cores. Although the narrow time structure of the neutrino source (such as pulse-type production) was not directly modeled in the simulation, the temporal stability of the distribution is a positive indicator in terms of the stability of the experimental timing systems (e.g., veto systems).

6.3
Scattering Angle (Theta) Distribution

The following trends were observed in the scattering angle distributions of recoiling nuclei:

Germanium: Density between 0–60°, average angles 30–40°

Argon: Wider distribution between 20–70°

Xenon: More pronounced accumulation at low angles (0–30°)

Due to the elastic nature of CEvNS, momentum transfer generally remains small. In heavy nuclei (Xenon), this results in a narrower distribution of scattering angles. This observation reveals that the angular resolution in detector design must be optimized relative to the target nucleus.

6.5
Experimental Applications and Material Selection

Based on these analyses:
Xenon, while increasing the event rate due to its high Z² value, requires lower-threshold, sensitive detectors because of its low recoil energies.
Germanium offers a balanced solution in terms of both event count and recoil energy, making it a frequent choice in CEvNS experiments.
Argon is a suitable candidate for large-volume detectors due to its low cost, stability, and relatively higher recoil energy (e.g., COHERENT, DUNE).

6.6
Conclusion

This study presents a full-scale simulation of Coherent Elastic Neutrino-Nucleus Scattering using custom processes in Geant4 and subsequent analysis in ROOT. By evaluating the CEvNS response of Germanium, Argon, and Xenon targets, the work establishes a flexible modeling framework that can support both neutrino physics and dark matter research.

The results highlight how nuclear mass and form factor behavior influence the recoil observables, guiding experimental design and material selection. Future improvements could include more realistic neutrino energy spectra, background modeling, and detector response simulation to fully prepare this framework for experimental integration.
