Medical students’ attitudes toward patient safety and medical error reporting in Syria
Authors: Aya Alkhdr, Ahmad Al-Bitar, Ibrahim Aljnide, Jawad Jalloul, Ali borji, M. Eyad Chatty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

This study assessed patient safety attitudes among undergraduate medical students in Syria and identified predictors of overall attitudes and error reporting behavior.MethodsA multi-center cross-sectional survey was conducted among medical students from four Syrian universities using the Attitudes to Patient Safety Questionnaire-III (APSQ-III). Background

Patient safety is a core competency in medical education. In conflict-affected and low-resource settings such as Syria, evidence on medical students’ safety attitudes and error reporting remains limited. The World Health Organization emphasizes that effective patient safety requires systems-based approaches and positive provider attitudes [1, 2]. Attitudes formed during undergraduate training influence future clinicians’ readiness to report errors, work in teams, and maintain vigilance under pressure [2].

The Attitudes to Patient Safety Questionnaire-III (APSQ-III) is a validated instrument assessing nine domains, including team functioning, working hours as error cause, and error reporting confidence [2]. International studies report moderately positive overall attitudes (mean scores 4.7–4.9 on 7-point scales), with consistently high scores in team functioning and working hours, and lower scores in domains such as professional incompetence and error reporting confidence [3–5].

A recurring concern is the gap between attitudes and behavior—particularly students’ tendency to witness errors without reporting them. In Middle Eastern contexts, although many students recognize patient safety concepts, only a minority report observed errors, citing barriers such as lack of confidence, fear of negative evaluation, and cultural norms [6, 7]. Underreporting undermines system learning and perpetuates adverse events.

Factors including gender, academic stage, GPA, and reporting confidence correlate with safety attitudes and behaviors, though findings vary by context [8, 9]. Structured patient safety education improves attitudes over time, suggesting curricular enhancements can produce measurable changes [3].

However, no large, multi-center assessment has been conducted in Syria, where medical education has been disrupted and baseline data are absent. We therefore surveyed 1,342 undergraduate medical students from four Syrian universities using the APSQ-III to assess patient safety attitudes, explore domain-level differences, and identify predictors of overall attitudes and error reporting behavior. These findings aim to inform targeted educational interventions to enhance patient safety culture in Syrian medical training.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted between January 2025 and July 2025 across four Syrian medical schools: Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, and Lattakia Universities. The study adhered to STROBE guidelines. Data were collected via a self-administered online questionnaire.

Participants

Undergraduate medical students enrolled in the 2024–2025 academic year who had completed at least one year were eligible. Exclusion criteria were graduate students, non-medical students, and incomplete submissions (> 20% missing data). Participants were recruited through non-probability convenience and snowball sampling via social media, class representatives, and lecture announcements.

Variables

Primary outcome

Overall patient safety attitudes, measured by the mean score on the Attitudes to Patient Safety Questionnaire-III (APSQ-III) (26 items, 7-point Likert scale, 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). Items indicating negative beliefs were reverse-coded. Higher scores indicate more positive attitudes.

Secondary outcomes

Domain-specific attitudes (nine domains) and error reporting behavior (binary: reported a witnessed error to a supervisor or not).

Independent variables

Age, gender, university, academic year, academic stage (pre-clinical years 1–3/clinical years 4–6), cumulative GPA, medical error exposure (witnessed error yes/no), and first-generation medical student status.

Data sources and measurement

The survey had two sections: (1) demographic and background information, and (2) the APSQ-III. The full list of APSQ-III items is provided in Additional file 1. The original English version was used, as all participants were proficient in medical English.

GPA was analyzed both continuously and categorically (≥ 3.5 vs. <3.5 on a 0–4 scale). This cutoff was chosen a priori as it represents the upper tertile in Syrian medical schools and aligns with previous regional studies. Error reporting was measured as a binary variable (yes/no); this simplification is acknowledged as a limitation.

Data collection

Google Forms was used with electronic informed consent. No personally identifiable information was collected. Completion time was 10–15 min.

Bias mitigation

Multiple recruitment channels were used to reduce selection bias. The validated APSQ-III, pilot testing, anonymity, and reverse-coded items minimized measurement, recall, and social desirability biases.

Sample size

The minimum required sample was 375 (Raosoft calculator: population 15,000, 95% confidence, 5% margin of error). Our final sample of 1,342 provided a margin of error of 2.7% and 80% power to detect small effects (d = 0.15).

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics (means, SDs, frequencies) were calculated. Group comparisons used independent t-tests, one-way ANOVA, or Mann-Whitney U as appropriate. Hierarchical multiple linear regression identified predictors of overall APSQ scores (assumptions checked: linearity, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals, VIF < 5). Binary logistic regression identified predictors of error reporting behavior (Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit). Missing data (< 10% for any variable) were handled using multiple imputation (MICE). Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed), with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d, odds ratios) are reported. Analyses used SPSS v26 and R v4.3.1.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in full accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Formal approval was obtained from the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (BMREC) at Damascus University (ID number: MD-011024-315-1*). Participation was entirely voluntary, and anonymity was guaranteed throughout the data collection and analysis process. The consent form was integrated into the questionnaire, and completion of the survey was considered implied consent.

Results

A total of 1,342 undergraduate medical students from four Syrian universities completed the survey. The overall mean APSQ-III score was 4.8 (± 0.67) on a 7-point scale, indicating generally positive attitudes toward patient safety.

Participant characteristics and overall attitudes

The sample comprised 58.0% female students. Statistically significant differences in overall APSQ scores were observed by university (p = 0.021), with Damascus University students scoring highest (4.9 ± 0.62); by academic stage (p = 0.003), with pre-clinical students (4.9 ± 0.65) scoring higher than clinical students (4.7 ± 0.69); and by GPA category (p = 0.015), with students having GPA ≥ 3.5 scoring higher (4.9 ± 0.63) than those with GPA < 3.5 (4.6 ± 0.70). No significant difference was found by gender (p = 0.142). Domain-specific attitudes

The highest scores were observed in “Working hours as error cause” (mean 5.72 ± 1.41) and “Team functioning” (5.68 ± 1.29), indicating strong agreement. The lowest score was “Professional incompetence as error cause” (3.52 ± 0.71), reflecting neutral to negative perceptions. (Complete domain scores and response distributions are provided in Additional File 2, Table S2).

Medical error exposure and reporting

Over one-third of participants (38.5%, n = 517) reported witnessing a medical error during training. However, only 28.5% of those who witnessed an error (n = 147) reported it to a supervisor, representing just 11.0% of the total sample.

Comparative analyses

Gender differences

Female students had significantly higher scores in three domains: “Patient safety training received” (p = 0.007), “Working hours as error cause” (p = 0.016), and “Error inevitability” (p = 0.017). Male students scored higher in “Patient involvement in reducing error” (p = 0.047). Effect sizes were small (Cohen’s d: 0.10–0.14).

Academic stage differences

Pre-clinical students scored significantly higher than clinical students in “Patient safety training received” (p = 0.014), “Working hours as error cause” (p = 0.003), and “Error inevitability” (p = 0.025). Effect sizes were small (Cohen’s d: 0.12–0.15). (Full comparative data are in Additional file 2, Tables S3–S4).

Multivariate analyses

Predictors of overall patient safety attitudes

Hierarchical multiple linear regression (Table S5, Additional File 2) identified significant independent predictors of higher overall APSQ scores: female gender (β = 0.17, p = 0.001), higher GPA (≥ 3.5) (β = 0.26, p < 0.001), having reported a witnessed error (β = 0.13, p = 0.030), and pre-clinical stage (β=−0.14 for clinical stage, p = 0.005). A significant gender × reporting interaction was found (β = 0.09, p = 0.025). The final model explained 6.3% of the variance (R²=0.063).

Predictors of error reporting behavior

Binary logistic regression (Tables 2 and 3) showed that female gender (OR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.08–2.28, p = 0.018), clinical academic stage (OR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.39–3.04, p < 0.001), higher GPA ≥ 3.5 (OR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.14–2.60, p = 0.010), higher “Error reporting confidence” domain score (OR = 1.52 per unit, 95% CI: 1.18–1.96, p = 0.001), and higher “Disclosure responsibility” domain score (OR = 1.42 per unit, 95% CI: 1.04–1.94, p = 0.029) were significant predictors of error reporting. Discussion

In this multi-center study of medical students from four Syrian universities, participants demonstrated generally positive attitudes toward patient safety, with an overall APSQ-III mean score of 4.8 (± 0.67). This is consistent with international evidence from Saudi Arabia, Malaysia, and Jordan [1, 2, 10]. High scores in “Working hours as error cause” and “Team functioning” further align with global patterns, reflecting widespread recognition of fatigue and teamwork as critical components of safe practice [11–14].

Domain-specific insights and systems thinking

The low score in “Professional incompetence as error cause” (3.52 ± 0.71) indicates less robust recognition among students of the multifactorial causes of error. Similar patterns have been observed in Thailand and China, where students more readily attribute errors to individual factors rather than systemic issues [15–17]. This finding underscores the need for curricula that emphasize systems thinking—the understanding that errors arise from complex interactions among individual, team, and organizational factors—particularly in Syrian medical education, where such concepts may be underexplored.

Error reporting behavior and the attitude-behavior gap

Although 38.5% of respondents witnessed a medical error during training, only 11.0% of the total sample reported it to a supervisor. This attitude-behavior gap echoes findings from other contexts, where students recognize the importance of reporting yet often fail to act due to hierarchical structures, fear of blame, and lack of clear reporting pathways [11–14]. In Syrian teaching hospitals, these barriers may be magnified by resource constraints and traditional hierarchies that discourage junior trainees from speaking up. Understanding and addressing this gap is essential, as underreporting limits opportunities for learning and system improvement.

Demographic and academic correlates

Our multivariate analysis identified several significant independent predictors of higher overall APSQ scores, including female gender, higher GPA, reporting a witnessed error, and pre-clinical stage enrollment. While gender effects vary across studies, our findings align with research in Jordan and Malaysia indicating gender differences in select patient safety domains [4, 6, 9]. Higher academic performance as a positive correlate likely reflects greater engagement with safety concepts. Pre-clinical students’ more positive attitudes may indicate early curriculum exposure effects, although longitudinal reinforcement throughout clinical years is needed [11].

Importantly, the regression model explained only 6.3% of the variance in overall APSQ scores, suggesting that unmeasured factors—such as institutional safety culture, faculty role-modeling, and prior exposure to adverse events—play substantial roles. The binary logistic regression for error reporting explained 18.4% of the variance (Nagelkerke R²), with confidence in error reporting and disclosure responsibility emerging as significant predictors. These findings reinforce evidence that self-efficacy and normative beliefs shape students’ willingness to act [16].

While gender differences reached statistical significance in several domains, effect sizes were small (Cohen’s d 0.10–0.14). Therefore, these differences should not be overinterpreted as clinically or educationally meaningful; they may reflect reporting patterns or socialization rather than true practical disparities.

Implications for Syrian medical education

Our findings support a growing body of evidence that patient safety education improves attitudes when systematically integrated into curricula. Interventions such as longitudinal patient safety modules, interactive case discussions, and structured feedback have been shown to produce sustained improvements in key APSQ domains over time [3, 7]. Emphasis on communication skills, error disclosure, and non-punitive reporting environments can further enhance students’ readiness to enact safety practices. In the Syrian context, practical next steps include developing structured error reporting simulations to build confidence (identified as a key predictor), training faculty to respond non-punitively to trainee error reports, and establishing clear, accessible reporting pathways with feedback loops to demonstrate system improvements. These interventions must be adapted to resource-limited and conflict-affected settings, where low-tech simulation and integration into existing case discussions may be more feasible than high-fidelity approaches.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference; longitudinal studies are needed to assess changes over time. Second, self-reported data may be subject to social desirability and recall bias. Third, convenience and snowball sampling may limit representativeness; findings may not generalize to all Syrian medical schools or other conflict-affected settings. Fourth, the binary measure of error reporting did not capture error severity, context, or specific barriers (e.g., fear of punishment, hierarchical pressure). Fifth, the low variance explained (R²=6.3% for attitudes, 18.4% for reporting) indicates important unmeasured confounders, which we acknowledge as a major limitation. Sixth, the GPA cutoff of ≥ 3.5, while chosen a priori, may not be universally applicable. Finally, we did not collect qualitative data; future research should include interviews or focus groups to explore the specific cultural and institutional barriers to reporting in Syrian hospitals.

Conclusion

This multi-center study demonstrates that undergraduate medical students in Syrian universities generally hold positive attitudes toward patient safety, particularly regarding teamwork and recognition of system-related contributors such as working hours. However, important gaps persist in domains related to professional incompetence and, critically, in the translation of positive attitudes into actual error reporting behaviors.

The low rate of error reporting despite frequent exposure to medical errors highlights the need for educational strategies that extend beyond awareness to skill development, confidence building, and cultural change. Demographic and academic predictors identified in this study—such as gender, academic stage, GPA, and reporting confidence—provide valuable insights for designing targeted interventions.

Strengthening patient safety education through longitudinal curricula, supportive clinical environments, and explicit training in error reporting and disclosure may help bridge the gap between attitudes and practice. These efforts are particularly crucial in resource-limited and conflict-affected settings, where cultivating a strong patient safety culture among future physicians is essential for improving healthcare quality and patient outcomes.
