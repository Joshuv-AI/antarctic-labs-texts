AI Feedback Enhances Community-Based Content Moderation through Engagement with Counterarguments
Saeedeh Mohammadi, Taha Yasseri

Abstract
Today, social media platforms are significant sources of news and political communication, but their role in spreading misinformation has raised significant concerns. In response, these platforms have implemented various content moderation strategies. One such method, Community Notes on X, relies on crowdsourced fact-checking and has gained traction, though it faces challenges such as partisan bias and delays in verification. This study explores an AI-assisted hybrid moderation framework in which participants receive AI-generated feedback —supportive, neutral, or argumentative —on their notes and are asked to revise them accordingly. The results show that incorporating feedback improves the quality of notes, with the most substantial gains resulting from argumentative feedback. This underscores the value of diverse perspectives and direct engagement in human-AI collective intelligence. The research contributes to ongoing discussions about AI’s role in political content moderation, highlighting the potential of generative AI and the importance of informed design.



Keywords—
Content Moderation; Human-AI Interaction; Large Language Models; Polarisation; Hybrid Intelligence

Introduction

The emergence of social media platforms has transformed how users consume and share information. Studies show that these platforms have become primary news sources for many individuals
[
1
,
2
]
and are widely used by politicians as campaign tools
[
3
]
. This shift presents challenges for platform owners in monitoring and verifying the information being shared. The widespread dissemination of misleading content and misinformation has become a primary concern for social media platforms. This issue is especially pronounced during significant socio-political events, such as the 2016 U.S. presidential election
[
4
]
and the Brexit referendum
[
5
]
. The spread of false information during times of crisis can seriously mislead the public. For example, the rapid circulation of misinformation about COVID-19 led the WHO’s Director-General to describe the situation as an ”infodemic”
[
6
]
. Another striking case is the January 6th, 2021, attack on the U.S. Capitol, where social media posts helped spread the misinformation that incited violence
[
7
]
. In response to these concerns, social media platforms have implemented various content moderation strategies, including both community-based and automated approaches.

On January 7, 2025, Mark Zuckerberg announced the end of Meta Platforms’ third-party fact-checking program, replacing it with Community Notes
[
8
]
. Launched initially on X (formerly Twitter) in January 2021, Community Notes represented the first large-scale, community-based initiative to moderate misleading content
[
9
]
. This shift in moderation strategy reflects the limitations of traditional fact-checking, which relies on human experts to review and classify large volumes of content. This approach is both expensive and challenging to scale
[
10
]
. Moreover, expert-driven moderation has faced criticism for potential political and cultural bias. A 2020 report found that 90% of Republicans in the United States believed social media platforms censor content based on political views
[
11
]
.

Community Notes rely on the wisdom of the crowd to identify misleading content and leverage the diversity of users on the platform to produce moderated outcomes. The process involves displaying specific posts to users in the Community Notes program and asking them to write a note that provides context for those posts. Users are also asked to indicate whether they believe the post is misleading and to provide reasoning for their judgment. Subsequently, other users rate the helpfulness of these notes. Initially, notes with high helpfulness ratings appeared beneath the posts in users’ timelines. However, early analyses revealed strong partisan behaviour. Yasseri and Menczer (2023) found that the network connecting note writers and raters mirrors the highly polarised friendship network on X
[
12
]
. Similarly, Allen et al. (2022) showed that users are more likely to write notes on posts by counter-partisans, label them as misleading, and rate notes from counter-partisans as unhelpful, while rating notes from co-partisans as helpful
[
13
]
.

To address partisan behaviour among Community Notes users, adjustments were made to the rating algorithm. Notes that receive helpful ratings from users with diverse perspectives, who typically rate content differently, are now prioritised over those endorsed only by ideologically similar users
[
9
]
. Although this change was intended to reduce polarised interactions, it has increased the time required for notes to be selected and attached to posts. A recent report found that 74% of accurate Community Notes addressing misinformation about the 2024 U.S. presidential election never reached users, allowing misleading content to go unchallenged. As a result, misinformation posts about the election spread 13 times faster than those accompanied by fact-checked notes
[
14
]
. These delays undermine the moderation process, as research shows that lag in content moderation significantly reduces its effectiveness
[
15
]
.

One approach to addressing the challenges of scale and speed in moderation is the use of automated algorithms. Early efforts in this area have focused on applying machine learning to assess the truthfulness of statements
[
16
]
. These systems typically rely on supervised learning and require pre-labelled datasets, such as LIAR
[
17
]
, which compiles fact-checked claims from dedicated websites, and FEVER
[
18
]
, which includes claims derived from Wikipedia. However, building high-performing models remains difficult due to the need for large, diverse labelled datasets—a process that demands significant labour from experts or crowdworkers
[
19
]
. The effectiveness of these automated systems is also limited by the quality and biases of their training data, which can perpetuate inaccuracies in classification
[
20
]
.

However, recent advances in generative AI offer promising solutions to the limitations of existing automated content moderation strategies
[
21
,
22
]
. Research shows that Large Language Models (LLMs) can effectively persuade individuals on political issues
[
23
]
and generate fact-checking messages that are more persuasive than those produced by human experts
[
24
]
. In addition, several studies demonstrate that LLMs can accurately assess the credibility of news sources
[
25
]
and verify factual claims
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
, with their accuracy improving when fine-tuned on augmented data
[
29
]
. In a recent example, De et al. (2025) fine-tuned an LLM on the Community Notes dataset to generate what they call “supernotes,” which were rated significantly more helpful than notes written by Community Notes users for the same posts
[
30
]
. These findings suggest that LLMs are not only capable of understanding complex content but may also outperform human-written moderation in certain cases. However, studies have also found that purely automated approaches can be less effective in the presence of social influence
[
31
]
. Moreover, people tend to place considerable trust in LLMs, even when the models make mistakes, raising concerns about overreliance on AI-generated content
[
32
]
.

An increasingly supported view in the literature is that the most effective content moderation strategies combine human oversight with AI assistance
[
33
]
. This hybrid approach leverages the complementary strengths of humans and machines, enabling moderation that is both more nuanced and scalable
[
34
]
. Schmitt et al. (2024) suggest that models integrating human judgment with AI can outperform either component alone
[
35
]
. However, even within hybrid systems, users often overestimate the accuracy of LLMs, placing trust in AI-generated assessments even when they are incorrect—a phenomenon known as automation bias or machine heuristics
[
36
,
37
]
. Moreover, some Human-AI frameworks risk reinforcing users’ existing beliefs, potentially deepening echo chambers and contributing to political polarisation
[
38
]
. To mitigate these risks, such systems must be carefully designed to avoid unintentionally amplifying bias and division on social media platforms.

Despite these risks, political diversity itself can be a valuable asset in improving community-based moderation efforts. One of the key strengths of Community Notes is its ability to harness ideological diversity—even polarisation—to produce more balanced and neutral notes
[
39
]
. A similar dynamic has been observed on Wikipedia, where research attributes the platform’s success to the variety of viewpoints among editors, along with its structured policies and guidelines
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
]
. Shi et al. (2019), for instance, found that higher levels of polarisation among editors correlate with higher-quality articles, especially on politically sensitive topics
[
42
]
. Moreover, studies suggest that exposure to cross-partisan messages can reduce both affective and issue-based polarisation
[
44
,
45
]
. These findings indicate that, when properly structured, political diversity not only does not hinder collaboration but also can even enhance it. In a recent study, researchers paired two participants to collaboratively write Community Notes. They found that duos produced more helpful notes than individuals, and that politically diverse groups were especially effective when evaluating posts with Republican-leaning content
[
46
]
. These results further support the idea that political diversity can strengthen the note-writing process in Community Notes, provided it is applied in a way that is both effective and efficient.

Building on these insights, this study proposes a human-AI collaboration framework that uses AI-generated feedback to simulate political diversity in content moderation. The aim is to combine the efficiency of AI with the interpretive strengths of human contributors, while ensuring that humans retain final decision-making authority. We investigate whether AI-generated feedback can emulate cross-partisan collaboration and enhance the quality of contributions on the Community Notes platform. Our focus is on how users revise their notes in response to different types of AI-generated feedback. Specifically, this study addresses the following research questions:

•

RQ1:
How does the perceived quality of notes vary by the type of feedback received (argumentative, supportive, or neutral)?

•

RQ2:
How does the impact of feedback on perceived note quality differ when participants evaluate co-partisan versus cross-partisan posts?

•

RQ3:
How does the effect of feedback on perceived quality change based on whether participants believe it was generated by a human expert or an AI model?

To investigate these research questions, we conducted an experiment modelled after the Community Notes process. Participants (
N=893
) were shown a post and asked to write a note providing additional context. Each note was then submitted to a GPT-4 language model, which was prompted to generate one of three types of feedback: argumentative, supportive, or neutral.

Argumentative feedback offers counterpoints to the original note, supportive feedback reinforces the note’s arguments, and neutral feedback simply restates its content. While all feedback was generated by the AI model, we randomly told some participants that the feedback was written by a human expert. This manipulation allows us to examine the roles of human favouritism
[
47
]
and automation bias
[
36
]
in shaping participants’ responses.

Participants were then asked to revise their notes in light of the feedback. In a second experiment (
N=1354
), we crowdsourced evaluations of note quality before and after revision. Self-identified Republicans and Democrats rated the notes on a 0–10 scale based on their helpfulness. Figure
1
provides a schematic overview of the experimental workflow.

Figure 1:
Diagram of the note-writing and note-evaluation framework.

The experimental workflow illustrates the process of note creation, feedback assignment, and evaluation. Participants (self-identified as Democrats or Republicans) wrote initial notes to provide context on posts authored by either Democrats or Republicans. After revising their notes in response to the feedback, a separate group of self-identified Democrats and Republicans rated the helpfulness of the original and revised notes. This design enables analysis of feedback effects on note quality and partisan differences in evaluation.

These contrasting outcomes highlight a key insight: the extent to which participants engage with AI-generated feedback significantly influences the effectiveness of their revisions.

Discussion

As Community Notes gains traction across major social media platforms as a leading approach to crowd-based content moderation
[
8
]
, concerns have been raised about its efficiency and long-term sustainability
[
13
,
48
,
15
]
. One emerging response to these challenges is the integration of generative AI—particularly large language models (LLMs)—into the Community Notes ecosystem. In this study, we introduce a framework that leverages GPT-4 to provide users with different types of feedback on their notes. This framework is designed both to evaluate the effectiveness of AI-assisted feedback and to deepen our understanding of the dynamics of human–AI collaboration in this context. We found that within this framework, the effectiveness of feedback in improving note quality depends not only on its type but also on the extent to which participants engage with it.

Studies have shown that two key factors in collective intelligence are the effort a group invests and how effectively members leverage each other’s knowledge and skills
[
49
]
. Our findings align with this: participants who engaged with AI-generated feedback more and incorporated it into their revisions produced better perceived notes. Specifically, the Feedback Acceptance rate (
FA
)—which reflects how closely participants’ final notes match the feedback—emerged as a significant predictor of note improvement for both Democratic and Republican raters (
I_{\mathrm{D}}
and
I_{\mathrm{R}}
). A high
FA
indicates strong engagement with the feedback, while a low
FA
often corresponds to users disregarding the feedback entirely, sometimes resulting in notes of lower quality than the original (See Figure SI10). This behaviour mirrors patterns observed on social media, where users often react defensively to content that challenges their political beliefs
[
50
]
. Our findings suggest that when participants move beyond this reflexive partisan response and actively engage with feedback, they produce higher-quality notes.

Among participants who actively engaged with the feedback, our findings show that argumentative feedback was more likely to lead to improvements in note quality. This may be because engaging with counterarguments encourages users to reconsider and refine their positions, resulting in notes that are more balanced, better justified, and ultimately more helpful. Prior research suggests that in problem-solving contexts, when AI systems are designed to complement human cognitive biases, they can prompt users to discover novel solutions, thereby improving outcomes
[
51
]
. Relatedly, Juncosa et al.
[
46
]
demonstrate that diversity among groups of note authors can enhance note quality under certain conditions. Our current findings suggest that argumentative AI feedback may function as a form of political diversity, exposing users to alternative viewpoints and, in doing so, fostering the production of higher-quality content.

In our second research question, we examine how the political alignment between participants and posts affects the perceived helpfulness of the notes. The results indicate that when participants and posts share the same political ideology (i.e., are co-partisan), participants are less likely to incorporate feedback into their notes, reducing the likelihood of producing a higher-quality note. This pattern holds for both argumentative and supportive feedback. One possible explanation for this behaviour is that when participants encounter a post aligned with their own beliefs, they may become less open to revision, regardless of the content or tone of the feedback
[
52
,
53
]
.

Lastly, in our third research question, we examined how the labelled source of feedback, whether an AI agent or a human expert, influences participant behaviour and, consequently, the quality of their notes. We find a slight preference for AI-labelled feedback when the post comes from the opposing political party (i.e., in cross-partisan contexts). In these cases, participants are more likely to engage with feedback they believe was generated by an AI agent. This finding is consistent with prior research on automated content moderation, which suggests that users tend to be more receptive to algorithmic decisions. This tendency, known as automation bias, reflects the inclination to overestimate the neutrality and competence of automated systems, even when they make errors
[
36
,
32
]
.

This study offers insights into the evolving role of AI in crowd-based content moderation. We demonstrate that integrating a large language model (LLM) into the Community Notes system can enhance its efficiency, provided the design preserves human agency and the platform’s core participatory nature. In our framework, the AI functions solely as a reviewer offering suggestions, while users retain full control over the content of their notes, helping to prevent the imposition of any algorithmic bias.

A key contribution of our approach is the introduction of political diversity at the note-writing stage, rather than relying solely on diverse raters at the evaluation stage. When participants engage with argumentative feedback, they are more likely to produce higher-quality notes, as rated by both Democratic and Republican evaluators. This suggests that such feedback not only promotes quicker production of helpful notes but also encourages users to consider counterarguments and revise their contributions in a more balanced and thoughtful way, ultimately discouraging the reinforcement of partisan narratives.

While our findings highlight the potential of integrating argumentative AI feedback into Community Notes, there are important limitations to consider.
First, we do not assess the objective truthfulness of the notes. Instead, note quality in this study is evaluated through crowdsourced ratings, with groups of Democrats and Republicans judging notes based on their perceived helpfulness. This method does not provide a direct measure of factual accuracy—for instance, a professional fact-checker might reach different conclusions. However, our approach reflects the current practice in Community Notes, where community members determine which notes are approved and displayed beneath posts. While some researchers question the reliability of crowd-based methods for identifying misinformation, it is important to note that the goal of Community Notes is not necessarily to debunk falsehoods, but to add context to potentially misleading posts. These posts may not contain explicit inaccuracies, but can still lead readers to incorrect conclusions due to missing or unbalanced information. In such cases, a politically diverse and balanced group of laypeople may be well positioned to assess whether the added context is informative and helpful.

Second, findings from an experimental setting may not fully generalise to real-world contexts. For example, participants in Community Notes are volunteers who may be motivated by political or ideological beliefs, whereas participants in our study were recruited and compensated for their involvement. Third, the “breaking news problem” remains a persistent challenge. For AI to provide meaningful feedback, it must have a sufficient understanding of the topic. When this is lacking, the AI may still offer feedback that encourages critical thinking, effectively acting as a nudging algorithm
[
54
]
. That said, recent advancements in large language models have enabled web-search capabilities, which may allow them to engage more effectively with current events in future applications.

Overall, this study provides a deeper understanding of human–AI interaction within a community-based content moderation system. Our findings suggest that AI can meaningfully enhance this process, not by replacing human judgment, but by offering targeted feedback. In particular, AI-generated argumentative feedback can introduce political diversity and improve note quality by prompting authors to consider opposing viewpoints. However, the presence of AI alone is insufficient to ensure effective collaboration. Its impact depends on active user engagement, which is shaped by factors such as the participant’s partisanship and the political alignment of the post.

Future work could extend these insights by examining longer-term interactions between users and AI to understand how engagement patterns evolve over time. Allowing users to interact with the AI across multiple sessions would provide a deeper understanding of sustained behaviour in this context. Additionally, while this study focused on political content, it would be valuable to explore how human–AI collaboration functions in apolitical or less polarising domains to assess whether similar engagement patterns emerge.

Methods

Data Collection

We collected data for this study through a series of experiments involving participants recruited via the online research platform Prolific. Eligibility was limited to residents of the United States. Participants received compensation for their time. The experiments were designed and implemented using the oTree framework (version 5.10.4), and GPT-4 was used as the AI agent in the note-writing task. In this section, we provide a detailed overview of the experimental procedures, including the creation of notes and their subsequent evaluation through crowd-based ratings.

Note Writing

In this experiment, participants first indicated their political affiliation using a nine-point slider ranging from ”Strongly Democrat” to ”Strongly Republican.” Those who identified as neutral were excluded from the study. Participants were then shown a political social media post and asked to write a contextual note and classify the post as either misleading or not. The instructions mirrored those provided to Community Notes users. After submitting their initial notes, participants received feedback labelled as coming from either an AI agent or a human expert, though in all cases, the feedback was generated by GPT-4. Participants were debriefed about the true source of the feedback at the end of the experiment.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of three feedback conditions. In the Argue group, the AI provided feedback that challenged the points made in the participant’s note while maintaining an opposing stance. In the Neutral group, the AI rephrased the note without introducing contradictions or additional evidence. In the Support group, the AI reinforced and expanded on the participants’ original arguments. Examples of AI-generated feedback for each condition are shown in Figure
4
, and the full prompts used to generate this feedback are included in the Supporting Information.

Figure 4:
AI-generated feedback across treatment conditions.
An example of a note created in the experiment and the responses the AI provides in each treatment.

After receiving feedback, participants were asked to revise their notes and were given the option to update their original classification of the post as misleading or not. Finally, they completed a short survey measuring their comfort level with emerging technologies. Additional details about the experiment are provided in the Supporting Information.

We used 40 social media posts with clear partisan messaging, all previously analysed by Community Notes users. After data cleaning, the final dataset included 893 participants: 455 self-identified Democrats and 438 self-identified Republicans. The study received ethical approval from the University College Dublin Office of Research Ethics (HS-LR-24-230-Mohammadi-Yasseri).

Note Evaluation

In the next phase of data collection, we recruited a new group of participants to evaluate the notes. After providing informed consent, participants first reported their political partisanship using a nine-point scale. They were then shown two different social media posts, each accompanied by 10 notes, and asked to evaluate the notes along two dimensions: helpfulness and favouritism. Helpfulness was rated on a scale from 0 to 10, reflecting the perceived utility of the note. Favouritism was assessed using a nine-point scale, indicating the extent to which a note appeared biased toward either political party, ranging from ”Favouring Democrats” to ”Favouring Republicans.” As shown in Figure SI11, each note received evaluations from at least three Democratic and three Republican participants. In total, we recruited 1354 participants for this evaluation phase, with each participant evaluating 20 notes across two posts (10 notes per post). This part of the study too received ethical approval from the University College Dublin Office of Research Ethics (HS-LR-24-188-Mohammadi-Yasseri).

Measurements

Helpfulness

The data collected from the note evaluation experiment provides the helpfulness scores assigned to each note by participants identified as Democrats and Republicans. As described earlier, each evaluator rates two posts and 10 notes per post. To standardise the scores and account for the rating habit of each user, we calculated z-scores of the notes associated with each post by a rater. The normalised score
\hat{H}^{T}_{u,i}
, representing helpfulness given by user
u
to note
i
for post
T
, is calculated as

\hat{H}^{T}_{u,i}=\frac{H^{T}_{u,i}-\mu^{T}_{u}}{\sigma^{T}_{u}},

where
\mu^{T}_{u}
is the mean of the scores assigned by user
u
to post
T
, and
\sigma^{T}_{u}
is the standard deviation of these scores.

To ensure reliability, we excluded the highest and lowest ratings for each note, provided the note had been rated by at least five Democrats and five Republicans (evaluated separately). We then calculate the final helpfulness score for each note by averaging the remaining normalised ratings.

Improvement is calculated as the relative percentage change in helpfulness scores from the initial to the final note. The improvement metric
I^{H}_{X}
, where
X
indicates Democrat or Republican raters, is defined as:

I^{H}_{X}=\frac{H_{\mathrm{final}}-H_{\mathrm{initial}}}{H_{\mathrm{initial}}}.

Notes were then categorised based on the percentage change in improvement. Notes with an improvement greater than 10% are classified as
improved
(
I=+1
). Those with a change between
-10\%
and
+10\%
are classified as
unchanged
(
I=0
). Notes with a decline greater than 10% are labelled as
declined
(
I=-1
).

Feedback Acceptance rate (
FA
)

To quantify the extent to which participants incorporated the feedback into their final notes, we calculated the semantic similarity between each piece of feedback and the corresponding revised note. This was measured using cosine similarity, implemented via the Scikit-learn package (version 0.0.post9). The resulting metric, referred to as the Feedback Acceptance rate (
FA
), captures the degree to which the feedback has been incorporated in the revised note. Figure SI8 displays the distribution of
FA
scores across the different feedback conditions.

The same semantic similarity method was also used to compute the similarity between the initial and revised versions of each note. This measure captures the extent to which a note changed during the revision process. Lower similarity scores indicate greater changes. Figure SI9 shows the distribution of similarity scores between the initial and final notes across the different feedback conditions.

Statistical Analysis

We conducted ordinal logistic regressions to examine changes in helpfulness scores, which could take values of 1 (”improved”), 0 (”unchanged”), or -1 (”declined”). The regressions were performed using the polr() function from the MASS package (version 7.3.57) in R. We report odds ratios, standard errors, and two-tailed p-values for each predictor. The p-values were calculated using the Wald test. To investigate the variables affecting the Feedback Acceptance rate (
FA
), we used ordinary least squares regressions (OLS). We used the lm() function from the stats package (version 4.2.1) in R to estimate the models. We report coefficient estimates, standard errors, and two-tailed p-values for each predictor. The p-values were calculated using the Wald test.

Note Evaluation Experiment

In the note evaluation experiment, participants were recruited to assess each note produced in the note writing experiment. Upon entering the study, participants first viewed an information sheet outlining the risks and benefits of participation, the purpose of the study, and ethics approval details. They then completed a consent form and provided their Prolific ID.

Afterwards, participants were asked to indicate their partisanship on a nine-point scale, consistent with the note-writing experiment. Those who selected the “neutral” option were excluded from the study.

In the main task of the experiment, participants were shown a social media post accompanied by 10 notes written about that post. They were asked to rate each note on two dimensions: overall helpfulness and favouritism. Overall helpfulness was defined as the quality and usefulness of the note in providing appropriate context for the post, rated on a scale from 0 to 10. Favouritism was described as the degree to which the note displayed bias favouring one political party beyond objectivity, measured on a nine-point scale ranging from strongly favouring Democrats to strongly favouring Republicans. Participants had 7 minutes to evaluate all 10 notes. Figure
SI7
shows a screenshot of the task description. Each participant evaluated two different social media posts, each with its own set of 10 notes.

Protocol for selecting the Social Media Posts

We selected 40 different posts from
X.com
for the note-writing experiment. These posts were drawn from the Community Notes dataset as of the end of August 2024. The selection followed the criteria below:

•

The post contains at least one of the following keywords: Democrat, Republican, Biden, or Trump.

•

It has at least two notes tagged as ”misinformed” in the Community Notes dataset.

•

The post is related to the United States.

•

The text is clear and easy to understand.

•

The post does not include a video or a link to an article required to understand its content.

Afterwards, the posts were classified as either pro-Democrat or pro-Republican based on their content. In total, we collected 20 pro-Democrat posts and 20 pro-Republican posts.

Feedback Prompts

We used the OpenAI API (version 1.25.0) to prompt the GPT-4 model for generating feedback in the note-writing experiment. The ”chat.completion” function was employed, with the system message set as: ”You are a helpful assistant who writes a note based on the instruction. The note should be limited to 280 characters. Do not use any hashtags or links in your response.” The temperature parameter was set to 1. The user message varied depending on the type of feedback. The specific prompts used are as follows:

•

Argue
: Read the following tweet:tweet. Read the comment made in response to the tweet:comment. Argue with the comment while maintaining a consistent opposing stance.

•

Support
: Read the following tweet:tweet. Read the comment made in response to the tweet: comment. Support the comment’s point. Help justify this comment, add more arguments if you can.

•

Neutral
: Read the following tweet:tweet. Read the comment made in response to the tweet: comment. Rephrase the comment. Do not add any evidence or contradict the comment in any way.

The ”tweet” refers to the content of the post, while the ”comment” is created by appending the sentence “The tweet is misleading” or “The tweet is not misleading” to the note written by the participant in the first task. This sentence is selected based on the participant’s classification of the post. The model is explicitly prompted to avoid including links, reducing the risk of hallucinated content generated by the AI.
