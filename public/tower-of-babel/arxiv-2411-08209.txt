Understanding failures in electronic structure methods arising from the geometric phase effect
Eirik F. Kjønstad, Henrik Koch

Abstract
We show that intermediate normalization of the electronic wave function, where a constant component is enforced, will lead to an asymptotic discontinuity at one point along any path that encloses a ground state conical intersection. For some electronic structure methods, this gives rise to severe global artifacts in the ground and excited state potential energy surfaces. We investigate how this affects two electronic structure methods: coupled cluster theory and Møller-Plesset perturbation theory. The analysis suggests that intermediate normalization is problematic not only in near-degenerate regions, such as in the vicinity of conical intersections. In particular, since problems will occur for any path that encloses a ground state intersection, the affected methods can unexpectedly break down in regions of internal coordinate space that are normally considered within their range of validity.

Traversing a loop in internal coordinate space that encloses a conical intersection can lead to a geometric phase effect.
(
Longuet-Higgins et al. 1958
;
Berry 1984
;
Domcke et al. 2004
;
Yarkony 1996
)
Starting from some initial point (or molecular geometry), and moving continuously along such a closed loop, one finds
that the electronic wave functions of the intersecting states have changed their sign upon returning to the initial geometry. This geometric phase effect occurs even when the path that encloses the degeneracy is nowhere near
the actual point of degeneracy; in particular, the effect is independent of the path and requires only that the path encloses a point of degeneracy.
(
Longuet-Higgins 1975
)
As a result, the phase effect has global consequences for the electronic wave functions.

A correct description of the phase effect, therefore
imposes constraints on the wave function parametrization used in electronic structure methods.
For instance, the overall sign of the closed-shell Hartree-Fock wave function

|\mathrm{HF}\rangle=\prod_{i}a^{\dagger}_{i\alpha}a^{\dagger}_{i\beta}|\mathrm{vac}\rangle

(1)

cannot be changed by a unitary transformation of the orbitals.
This wave function is therefore unable to account for the geometric phase effect.
Similarly, the phase effect cannot be captured by methods that adopt intermediate normalization.
Where the wave function
|\psi\rangle
is normalized with respect to some
|\varphi\rangle
(often the Hartree-Fock wave function) by requiring that

\displaystyle\langle\varphi\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\psi\rangle=1.

(2)

This normalization does not imply any loss of generality as long as
\langle\varphi\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\psi\rangle\neq 0
. However, if
|\psi\rangle
has a geometric phase effect, then
\langle\varphi\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\psi\rangle
will change sign continuously and pass through zero as we traverse around the conical intersection, regardless the choice of any phase-free
|\varphi\rangle
.
Indeed, if the wave function
|\psi\rangle
is to change its sign, all of its components (including the component along
|\varphi\rangle
) must go through zero at some point along the loop.
Renormalization of the wave function to enforce eq.
2
,

|\psi^{\prime}\rangle=|\psi\rangle/\langle\varphi\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\psi\rangle,

(3)

will therefore lead to asymptotic discontinuities in the expansion coefficients of

|\psi^{\prime}\rangle
. To see this, note that if the component along
|\varphi\rangle
is required to be 1, then the effective weight along
|\varphi\rangle
can only go to zero if the other components of
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle
diverge to infinity. Moreover, the change in the sign of the component along
|\varphi\rangle
, as it passes through zero, implies a discontinuous change of sign in the diverging components. These asymptotic discontinuities (see Figure
1
) form a subspace of dimension
N-1
, where
N
is the number of internal coordinates, which extends out from the conical intersection seam of dimension
N-2
.

Figure 1:
Asymptotically discontinuous components for intermediately normalized methods with
N=3

) and the space of discontinuous components forms a plane (

). From an arbitrary starting point, a loop enclosing the intersection seam will necessarily pass through a point of discontinuity where components of the wave function diverge.

We can therefore conclude that intermediate normalization is incompatible with a correct description of the geometric phase effect in the sense that there is no continuous description that correctly captures the change in sign. Nevertheless, intermediate normalization has been regarded as a convenient starting point for developing many electronic structure methods.
(
Helgaker et al. 2013
;
Helgaker et al. 2012
)
However, the implications of this choice have, to the best of our knowledge, not been discussed in detail before. This is the purpose of the present work.

Analytical model

It is instructive to first consider a two-state Hamiltonian where the two states are the ground and the first excited state. In the case of a real Hamiltonian, we write

\displaystyle\bm{H}=\begin{pmatrix}v&0\\
0&v\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}w&h\\
h&-w\end{pmatrix}=v\bm{I}+\bm{H}_{c}.

(4)

The eigenvectors of
\bm{H}
are uniquely determined by
\bm{H}_{c}
and the
v\bm{I}
term only provides a constant shift of the energies. We will therefore restrict our analysis to
\bm{H}_{c}
. Considering
\bm{H}_{c}
, we can express its eigenvectors and eigenvalues using polar coordinates,

\displaystyle w=r\cos 2\vartheta,\quad h=r\sin 2\vartheta.

(5)

Note that
\vartheta=0
to
\vartheta=\pi
corresponds to a full revolution about the origin.
The eigenvalues and eigenvectors are

\lambda_{\pm}=\pm r=\pm\sqrt{w^{2}+h^{2}}

and

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{-}(\vartheta)=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin\vartheta\\
\cos\vartheta\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{v}_{+}(\vartheta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\vartheta\\
\sin\vartheta\end{pmatrix}

(6)

respectively. This provides a useful framework for investigating the phase effect associated with degeneracies because a path about a degeneracy in internal coordinate space corresponds to a path about the
(w,h)
origin.
(
Williams et al. 2023
;
Mead 1992
;
Atchity et al. 1991
)
Indeed, by varying
\vartheta
from
0
to
\pi
(corresponding to a full revolution about the degeneracy), we find that the eigenvectors continuously change their sign upon returning to the same point:

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\pm}(\pi)=-\bm{v}_{\pm}(0).

(7)

This is the geometric phase effect. Intermediate normalization changes this analysis in important ways. When

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{v}}_{\pm}(\vartheta)=N_{\pm}(\vartheta)\bm{v}_{\pm}(\vartheta),\quad N_{-}(\vartheta)=-\frac{1}{\sin\vartheta},\quad N_{+}(\vartheta)=\frac{1}{\cos\vartheta},

(8)

we can ensure that the first component of both eigenvectors always has the same weight:

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{v}}_{-}(\vartheta)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-\cot\vartheta\end{pmatrix},\quad\tilde{\bm{v}}_{+}(\vartheta)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\tan\vartheta\end{pmatrix}.

(9)

However, the norm of these vectors diverges at the points where the first component of the normalized vectors goes through zero, and in these points, the sign of the diverging component changes discontinuously. These asymptotic discontinuities
are shown in Fig.
2
. We note that any path around the origin will pass through one such point. The result is a plane of asymptotic discontinuities (see also Fig.
1
).

Coupled cluster theory

In standard coupled cluster theory
(
Helgaker et al. 2013
;
Bartlett and Musiał 2007
)
we assume that the weight along the reference state, usually the closed-shell Hartree-Fock wave function
|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
, equals to one. We have that

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\psi\rangle&=\exp(T)|\mathrm{HF}\rangle\\
&=(1+T+\tfrac{1}{2}T^{2}+\ldots)|\mathrm{HF}\rangle,\\
\end{split}

(10)

where
\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\psi\rangle=1
and

\displaystyle T=\sum_{\mu}t_{\mu}\tau_{\mu}.

(11)

The
\tau_{\mu}
operators in
T
generate excitations from the reference wave function
|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
and are usually truncated at a given excitation order (for example,
T=T_{1}+T_{2}
in the singles and doubles model, i.e. CCSD
(
Purvis and Bartlett 1982
)
). Note that the cluster amplitudes
t_{\mu}
must diverge for the weight along the reference
|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
to effectively become zero.
Consequently, the geometric phase effect implies that the cluster amplitudes will diverge, showing an asymptotic discontinuity at a specific point along any path that encircles a ground state intersection.

This leads to severe artifacts in the potential energy surfaces unless the cluster operator is complete. To understand why, consider
|\psi\rangle
expressed as a configuration interaction expansion

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=(1+T+\tfrac{1}{2}T^{2}+\ldots)|\mathrm{HF}\rangle=(1+C)|\mathrm{HF}\rangle.

(12)

We may consider the operator
C
to be a function of
T
, defining a map from
T
to
C
. From the analysis above, we should expect a divergence in
C
with opposite signs on either side of the plane with asymptotic discontinuities. If we denote the wave function on either side of the plane by
|\psi_{+}\rangle
and
|\psi_{-}\rangle
, we thus expect that

\displaystyle|\psi_{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=(1+C_{+})|\mathrm{HF}\rangle=(1+C)|\mathrm{HF}\rangle

(13)

\displaystyle|\psi_{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=(1+C_{-})|\mathrm{HF}\rangle=(1-C)|\mathrm{HF}\rangle

(14)

as the plane is approached from either side.
Since the coefficients in
C
tend to infinity, these wave functions become asymptotically identical up to a sign when sufficiently close to the plane. That is,

\displaystyle|\psi_{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=(1+C)|\mathrm{HF}\rangle\approx C|\mathrm{HF}\rangle

(15)

\displaystyle|\psi_{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-C)|\mathrm{HF}\rangle\approx-C|\mathrm{HF}\rangle.

(16)

This is where the problem becomes apparent. There is in general no pair of cluster operators (
T_{+}
,
T_{-}
) that provides the same wave function up to a sign. The map from
T
to
C
is not invertible for truncated
T
and only in the complete
T
limit (full coupled cluster or full configuration interaction limit) will there exist a
T_{-}
and
T_{+}
such that the wave functions become
(1-C)|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
and
(1+C)|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
. From an equivalent perspective, the problem arises as the truncated cluster operator
T
does not have sufficient flexibility to control the sign of the high-order excitations that result from products of cluster operators in the expansion, see eq. (
12
).

This leads to a breakdown of the method in which the potential energy surfaces can even become multi-valued upon traversal around intersections between the ground and first excited states. We illustrate this behavior in the case of the pyramidalized ground state minimum energy conical intersection (MECI) in ethylene using the equation of motion CCSD method.
(
Koch and Jørgensen 1990
;
Stanton and Bartlett 1993
)
In these calculations, we have used the CCSD minimum energy conical intersection (
\varepsilon
-MECI) structure reported by Angelico, Kjønstad, and Koch
(
Angelico et al. 2024
)
in a recent work on MECI optimizations in coupled cluster theory. All calculations are performed using the
e^{T}
program,
(
Folkestad et al. 2020
)
with molecular gradients and derivative couplings (which are required to obtain the
\bm{g}
and
\bm{h}
vectors
(
Yarkony 1996
)
) evaluated using recent implementations.
(
Schnack-Petersen et al. 2022
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2023
)

The potential energy surfaces in the branching plane are shown in Figure
3
.
We find two solutions, one with a positive excitation energy and one with a negative excitation energy. These two solutions correspond to the two cases where the coupled cluster state
|\psi\rangle=\exp(T)|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
converges either to the ground state or the first excited state, respectively. Normally, the coupled cluster state is assumed to correspond to the ground state, but this is not guaranteed. The presence of multiple solutions is associated with singularities in the coupled cluster Jacobian matrix (corresponding to a vanishing excitation energy
(
Koch and Jørgensen 1990
;
Stanton and Bartlett 1993
)
) and are therefore expected intersections with the ground state. The existence of such solutions has been discussed previously in the literature.
(
Piecuch and Kowalski 2000
;
Sverrisdóttir and Faulstich 2024
)
Both solutions exhibit the same behavior in the cluster amplitudes, which tend to positive and negative infinity as the plane of discontinuities is approached in the clockwise and counter-clockwise directions. The breakdown covers a larger range in the case where the coupled cluster state represents the first excited state.
Only for the positive energy solution do we obtain multi-valued potential energy surfaces, where returning to the same point does not yield the same potential energies. For the negative energy solution, the breakdown occurs more quickly and the surfaces are therefore not multi-valued.

The existence of multi-valued solutions is due to the inability of the cluster operator to control the sign of the wave function. Upon a full revolution starting from a point close to the discontinuous plane, this leads to similar wave functions, up to a sign, but not identical. We should emphasize the generality of this result. Based on the above considerations, this incorrect behavior will be observed around all conical intersections between the ground and the first excited states in coupled cluster theory.

Møller-Plesset theory

In Møller-Plesset perturbation theory,
(
Helgaker et al. 2013
)
the ground state wave function is similarly expressed using intermediate normalization with respect to a reference wave function. If this reference is
|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
, then the wave function is given as

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=|\mathrm{HF}\rangle+|\psi^{(1)}\rangle+|\psi^{(2)}\rangle+\ldots,\quad\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\psi\rangle=1,

(17)

where the superscripts denote orders in the fluctuation potential.
Considering once again the point where the weight along the reference is zero, we see that the zero’th order wave function (
|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
) is not a component of the ground state wave function. Based on this, we might expect the sum of the Møller-Plesset corrections (
|\psi^{(1)}\rangle,|\psi^{(2)}\rangle,\ldots
) would tend to infinity as we approach this point, in order for the perturbation series to correctly converge to the ground state.

|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
vanishes in the ground state. In points where the Hartree-Fock reference dominates, instead, the perturbation series converges to the ground state. By continuity, this means that we obtain an extended region along the loop where the Møller-Plesset wave function
neither
represents the ground state nor the first excited state in any meaningful sense. Figure

\text{HeH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}

(

,

Surprisingly, the observed distortions of the potential energy surfaces become more pronounced at higher orders, where the Møller-Plesset potential energy surface sharply transitions from representing the ground state to representing the first excited state (around
\varphi=\pi
). This produces an unphysical artificial potential energy barrier and cusp in the ground state potential energy surface. Finally, we should mention that methods that depend on the Møller-Plesset ground state will also be affected by these issues. One example is the often-used algebraic diagrammatic construction method for excited states.
(
Dreuw et al. 2023
)

Conclusions

In this Letter, we have investigated the impact of the geometric phase effect on the description of ground state intersections in electronic structure methods. In particular, we have shown that enforcing a constant value on a wave function component (intermediate normalization) is incompatible with a correct description of the geometric phase effect, as it necessarily induces an asymptotic discontinuity along loops in internal coordinate space that surround an intersection. For some methods, this fact can produce complete breakdowns along arbitrary paths that surround a ground state conical intersection. We have demonstrated how such breakdowns can occur in the widely adopted coupled cluster and Møller-Plesset methods in regions where the weight along the reference wave function vanishes. This finding is especially worrisome, as the geometric phase effect is fundamentally a global effect: paths around the intersection are arbitrary
(
Longuet-Higgins 1975
)
and any path enclosing the intersection will have a point where the method breaks down. Although it remains to be seen how widespread and consequential these failures are, they may point towards a need to reconsider the parametrization of the ground state in single-reference theories such as coupled cluster theory.
(
Rossi et al. 2024
)
