Optimising transfusion practices in preterm neonates using gestational age and birth weight-based prediction model: a retrospective cohort study from India
Authors: Malavika Krishnakumar, Anjali Ajith, Georg Gutjahr, Sreerenjini Biju, Veena Shenoy, Dhanya A, Perraju Bendapudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreterm neonates, particularly those with extremely low birth weight and gestational age, are at high risk of requiring multiple blood transfusions during their stay in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Repeated transfusions result in multiple donor exposures, leading to increased risk of infections, alloimmunisation, worse neurodevelopmental outcomes and transfusion-related complications. Optimising transfusion practices through prediction-based strategies could enhance safety and resource efficiency, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.ObjectiveThis study aims to evaluate transfusion patterns among preterm neonates and develop a predictive model based on birth weight and gestational age to reduce donor exposure and improve transfusion efficiency.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included inborn preterm neonates (gestational age 23+0 to 32+0 weeks) admitted to a tertiary care NICU in South India in 2023 who received at least one packed red blood cell (PRBC) transfusion. Outborn babies who were transferred to the NICU beyond 48 hours of life and babies needing surgery at any point during their NICU stay were excluded. Data on gestational age, birth weight, transfusion frequency, donor exposure, clinical outcomes and blood usage were analysed. Predictive models were developed using Bayesian ordinal regression to estimate transfusion requirements.ResultsWe analysed 422 PRBC transfusions administered to 118 neonates. Lower gestational age and birth weight were significantly associated with increased transfusion frequency and donor exposure. Predictive models using gestational age and birth weight reduced the need for multiple donors. This approach, if used, would have also improved blood resource planning and highlighted the potential for reduced wastage.ConclusionIndividualised transfusion strategies may reduce donor exposure and improve transfusion safety in preterm neonates, especially in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Infant, Neonatology, Low and Middle Income Countries

Introduction

Recent advances in neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) care have significantly improved the survival rates of infants with low birth weight. Despite significant advances in neonatal intensive care, infants with low birth weight continue to face a high risk of developing anaemia, a common and often multifactorial condition in preterm and premature neonates. One of the primary causes is anaemia of prematurity, a physiological response characterised by reduced erythropoietin production, a shortened lifespan of red blood cells (RBCs), and rapid postnatal growth, all of which contribute to a relative deficiency in red cell mass.1 2 This vulnerability is further compounded by blood loss from iatrogenic sampling, birth trauma, or antenatal haemorrhage.3 In addition, haemolysis, whether immune-mediated, such as in Rh or ABO incompatibility, or non-immune, due to membrane or enzymatic disorders, can significantly accelerate red cell destruction.4 Suppressed erythropoiesis linked to infections; nutritional deficiencies (such as iron, folate or vitamin B12), and the intrinsic immaturity of the hematopoietic system in extremely preterm infants further limits red cell production.2 5 More often than not, these mechanisms overlap, leading to clinically significant anaemia that requires timely interventions, including RBC transfusions and hematinic support.1 6

More than 80% of preterm infants are transfused with RBCs during their initial hospitalisation.7,9 Frequent transfusions of packed red blood cells (PRBCs) from multiple donors present concerns related to infection risk, as well as complications such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia, retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) and necrotising enterocolitis (NEC) in neonates. These risks highlight the necessity of optimising transfusion protocols to minimise donor exposure and careful monitoring for adverse outcomes. In the neonatal population, blood transfusion is typically administered for two primary purposes: to act as a volume expander in cases of acute blood loss or hypovolemia and to enhance oxygen delivery by increasing haemoglobin concentration. While transfusions can be lifesaving, the excessive or inappropriate use of blood and blood products has been associated with adverse outcomes, including ROP, NEC and the risk of volume overload.10,12

Frequent transfusions not only increase the potential for exposure to multiple donors but also contribute to significant wastage of blood products. Each neonatal transfusion often involves a partial use of a blood pack, which contributes to blood wastage. Reserving satellite blood packs for individual babies could minimise waste and reduce donor exposure.13 Studies emphasise the importance of stringent transfusion thresholds14 15 to minimise transfusions. Adopting such evidence-based transfusion thresholds can optimise blood usage and minimise risks associated with excessive transfusions. Post hoc analysis of some studies has shown neurodevelopmental benefits of reduced donor exposure.16

Worldwide, multiple initiatives aim to minimise donor exposure in neonatal transfusions,13 17 primarily to reduce the risks of infections and immunological reactions associated with multiple donor interactions. The present study aims to develop a model based on gestational age and birth weight to predict the transfusion requirements of neonates in India. Such predictions can be used to reserve satellite blood packs in advance, thereby optimising blood utilisation, reducing potential risks and alleviating financial and health burdens associated with neonatal PRBC transfusions.

Methods

Study description

This retrospective cohort study was conducted among newborn infants in the NICU of a tertiary-level hospital in South India. The study aimed to estimate the average number of PRBC transfusions per neonate with adequate precision. Based on pilot data, the mean number of transfusions was four with an SD of 2. Using this variance estimate, the sample size was calculated to ensure that the 95% CI for the mean number of transfusions would have a total width of no more than one transfusion. Outborn babies who were transferred to the NICU beyond 48 hours of life and babies needing surgery at any point during their NICU stay were excluded. Infants who received at least one transfusion were included in the study. Live-born preterm babies between 23+0 weeks and 32+0 weeks of gestation who received blood transfusions were consecutively included in the study, stratified by gestational age strata, to maintain approximately equal sample sizes across the different strata. The Institutional Ethics Board approved the study (Dissertation Review/MD/MS/2023/89).

The data were collected from the blood order sheet, the hospital information system and the blood bank records. The collected data included birth weight, date of admission and discharge, length of stay in the NICU, discharge diagnosis, in-hospital mortality, need for surgery during the NICU stay, number of blood transfusions and both requested and administered blood volume. The unit has a restrictive transfusion policy and stringent guidelines for blood transfusions, based on gestational age, chronological age and the need for respiratory and oxygen support (Appendix 1). The average number of transfusions per neonate and the rate of donor exposure were calculated from the transfusion records. The donor exposure was defined as the unique number of donors for a baby. Bags that are reserved for blood transfusion but have not been utilised are referred to as return bags.

Standard clinical definitions were applied to classify neonatal conditions. Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), also known as hyaline membrane disease, is a surfactant-deficiency disorder seen primarily in preterm infants, leading to decreased lung compliance and impaired gas exchange.18 Transient tachypnoea of the newborn (TTN) is a self-limited condition due to delayed clearance of fetal lung fluid, typically affecting term or late preterm infants.19 NEC is an acquired gastrointestinal emergency in neonates, particularly preterm, characterised by inflammation and necrosis of the intestinal wall, with radiographic evidence such as pneumatosis intestinalis.20 ROP is a vasoproliferative retinal disorder in preterm neonates caused by abnormal vascular development, defined as per international criteria.21

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research. The study was based on a retrospective review of anonymised hospital records and transfusion data.

Current practices at the transfusion medicine department

A leukoreduced PRBC unit, typically 250 mL in volume and ABO/Rh matched or compatible, is crossmatched for the patient. The required small-volume aliquot for transfusion is aseptically transferred from the primary (mother) bag to a satellite bag using a sterile connection system. Parent blood units are reserved for up to 24 hours and will be utilised for other paediatric patients thereafter. A new PRBC unit is utilised for subsequent transfusions. Each donor unit has a unique identification number, which is the reference for all future communications between the neonatal clinical team and the Department of Transfusion Medicine. A single donor donates 450 mL of whole blood at a time, from which the 250 mL PRBC are separated. His subsequent donation will not be made for 3 months. In line with the NICU transfusion guidelines, babies are administered 15 mL/kg of PRBC when transfused. The blood volume is calculated using the working weight or the baby’s birth weight if it has not yet reached its birth weight. An additional 5 mL of blood is issued to account for the volume of the blood set (intravenous tubing used for transfusion of blood and blood products).

Statistical methods

We summarised patient characteristics using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the neonates were compared across gestational age groups using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were small. For continuous variables, comparisons of medians across gestational age groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Ordinary least squares (OLS), logistic and Cox regression models with spline terms for gestational age were used to estimate transfusion requirements, in-hospital mortality and the death-corrected number of transfusion events, respectively. A Bayesian network learnt with the MMHC algorithm was used to select the potential predictors and confounders from the baseline characteristics: sex, sepsis, respiratory distress, IVH, gestational age and birth weight. The number of transfusions is modelled using Bayesian ordinal regression models with the proportional odds assumption. Weakly informative priors were specified using an R2 prior with an expected proportion of explained variance of 20% to constrain the explanatory power of the predictors.22 Two models were fitted: Model A with the most predictive variable and Model B with all potential variables identified by the Bayesian network. Sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative R2 priors with expected proportions of explained variance of 20% and 75% and two additional frequentist models to assess the robustness of findings. The frequentist models for the sensitivity analysis were a proportional odds ordinal logistic regression model (LRM) and a generalised additive model (GAM). Model characteristics were summarised in terms of means and quantiles of the number of donor exposures and the number of return bags. A significant level of alpha=0.05 was used throughout the analysis. The statistical analysis was done in R V.4.4.1 using the rstanarm package V.2.32.1.

Results

A total of 422 PRBC transfusions were recorded for the 118 babies in this study. The median gestational age was 27.5 weeks (IQR, 25–29). The median birth weight was 900 g (IQR: 700–1200 g). The length of stay in the hospital showed substantial variability, with a median of 51 days (IQR: 36–69 days), though some infants experienced extended stays. The median donor exposure was 3 (IQR: 1–4). Of the 422 transfusions, 193 were given during the first month of admission to the NICU. Among the neonates included in the study, 31 received two transfusions, 10 received three, and 36 received four or more transfusions. The 77 (65%) neonates who were given multiple transfusions had a total of 257 transfusions, with a median of 3 (IQR 2, 5) and a mean of 4.34 (3.30). Five babies who received multiple transfusions were exposed to only a single donor, with all transfusions sourced from the same donor unit.

In the sensitivity analysis, four models were evaluated: a GAM, a proportional odds ordinal LRM, and two Bayesian linear models with alternative R2 priors (with expected proportions of explained variance of 20% and 75%, respectively). All models demonstrated a consistent decreasing trend in predicted transfusion requirements, from approximately four at the upper end of the distribution to around two at the lower end. The Bayesian model with an R2 prior of 0.2 consistently produced the most conservative estimates (3.67–1.65), whereas the model with an R2 prior of 0.75 yielded the highest predictions in the upper deciles (up to 4.85) before converging towards values similar to or below the frequentist models at the lower end. The GAM and LRM produced closely aligned intermediate estimates, with the GAM slightly higher overall. Overall, while the declining trend in transfusion requirements was robust across models, the absolute number of predicted transfusions varied depending on the modelling approach. Predictions were very similar across all models for gestational ages greater than 25 weeks, whereas for those less than or equal to 25 weeks, the Bayesian model with an R2 prior of 0.75 produced less variation in estimates compared with the weaker prior.

The predictive allocation models demonstrated a marked improvement in reducing donor exposures compared with the current policy, as shown in figure 2b. Under current practice, donor exposure was considerably higher, with only about 46 of 118 neonates (39%) receiving blood exclusively from a single donor, including 41 who required only one transfusion and five who had multiple transfusions but from the same donor. A significant number were exposed to two or more donors, including nearly 15% who were exposed to five or more. In contrast, Models A (gestational age-based) and B (gestational age plus birth weight) were calculated using the current requirement’s median plus two SD. They were significantly more effective, with over 90% of neonates in Model B receiving blood from a single donor. However, Models A and B resulted in the return of unused blood bags with a median of zero bags and a full range of zero to three bags. Models A and B predicted higher rates of returns, with nearly half of the neonates in Model B returning one or more bags, and some returning up to four.

Discussion

This study investigated the possibility of minimising donor exposure and blood wastage by setting aside blood bags based on the predicted requirement of preterm neonates. The analysis revealed a strong association between lower gestational age and a higher need for blood transfusions. This finding is consistent with previous studies.1415 23,25 The finding was further supported by the analysis of transfusion events adjusted for mortality, which indicated a similar trend.

Using the model created in this study, we can predict the transfusion requirements for each baby based on its gestational age at birth, enabling us to set aside the required number of paediatric transfusion packs from the same donor, which will help reduce donor exposure among the babies receiving PRBC transfusions. Notably, adding birth weight to the model did not offer a significant additional benefit, as both models performed similarly in predicting transfusion needs.

To reduce donor exposure in preterm neonates, we developed two predictive transfusion allocation models, Models A and B, based on gestational age alone and gestational age combined with birth weight, respectively. These models could enable the early estimation of transfusion needs, allowing pre-allocation of RBC units from a single donor, which can then be subdivided into small-volume aliquots. This proactive strategy has shown the potential to markedly reduce the number of donor exposures compared with standard practice. With current on-demand transfusion ordering, only 45% of neonates received blood exclusively from a single donor. In contrast, implementation of Model A increased this to 85%, while Model B achieved single-donor exposure in over 90% of cases. Although the use of predictive models led to a modest increase in unused blood returns, the significant reduction in donor exposure outweighs this drawback.

Reducing donor exposure in neonatal transfusions has been associated with several important clinical outcomes. The Preterm Erythropoietin Neuroprotection Trial (PENUT) trial reported that in extremely low gestational age neonates, a higher number of PRBC transfusions was associated with lower cognitive, motor and language scores at 2 years of age, suggesting a potential neurodevelopmental impact of transfusion burden.16 Multiple donor exposures also increase the risk of alloimmunisation and immune modulation, complicating future transfusions and transplant compatibility. Earlier studies, such as those by Baud et al and Wood et al, have demonstrated that single-donor programmes not only reduced exposure by more than 50% but also improved transfusion tolerance without increasing total transfusion volume.17 24 Furthermore, limiting donor exposures reduces the risk of transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease, a rare but often fatal complication, as shown in work by Strauss et al.13

In addition to these clinical benefits, donor reduction strategies contribute to more efficient and cost-effective use of blood. Programmes using satellite packs or aliquots derived from a single donor unit have consistently demonstrated lower wastage and reduced the frequency of cross-matching.13 26 27 Cost-analysis studies further support that the use of adult red cell packs and pedi-packs in NICUs reduces both procedural risks and resource use while maintaining clinical efficacy.28 These approaches align with global efforts to implement safer, more sustainable and economically viable transfusion practices in neonatology.

Following the proposed mode above requires storing blood, which may result in the return of a small number of blood packs. However, this might not be an issue, as innovations such as sterile connection devices and extended storage solutions have further facilitated the safe pre-storing of blood over extended durations. Vossoughi and Stotler give a recent overview of these storage solutions.29 The National Neonatology Forum (NNF) of India has also provided detailed guidelines on storage practices, advocating for stringent transfusion thresholds and the preferential use of single-donor aliquots to enhance safety profiles in neonates.30

Previous models in neonatal transfusion have focused on inventory management or predicting transfusion risk, rather than optimising donor assignment. For instance, Quinn et al simulated the effects of restricting RBC age. They found that providing fresher units could substantially strain inventory and increase the risk of donor exposure.31 Zhao et al developed an LRM using factors like gestational age, age under 1 month, mechanical ventilation and anaemia to predict which neonates are likely to require transfusion (AUC=0.936).32 Still, this model did not address donor allocation. More recently, Zhang et al, while employing prediction modelling, did not focus on reducing donor exposures.33 In contrast, our models, Model A, based on gestational age, and Model B, which combines gestational age with birth weight, offer a practical, data-driven strategy for improving donor assignment and achieving over 90% single-donor transfusions, representing a novel operational advance in neonatal transfusion practices.

The predictive models developed in this study offer a practical framework for reducing donor exposure in preterm neonates by enabling individualised transfusion planning at the time of admission. When a neonate is admitted to the ICU, their gestational age and birth weight, both routinely available, can be used to look up the predicted number of transfusions from the online supplemental tables 1 and 2. This predicted transfusion count can then be used to pre-allocate a sufficient number of blood bags from a single donor or from the minimum number of donors needed to meet the anticipated requirement. By doing so, hospitals can proactively group transfusion episodes from the same donor, thereby minimising the neonate’s exposure to multiple donors and the associated risks of alloimmunisation and transfusion-transmitted infections. Moreover, the model helps avoid under- or over-provisioning of blood products. In cases where predicted need exceeds standard thresholds, additional bags from the same donor can be requested in advance; in low-risk cases, unnecessary allocation can be avoided. This model-based approach supports a shift from reactive to proactive transfusion planning, aligning with patient safety goals and improving blood inventory management in NICUs.

While this study provides important insights into optimising neonatal transfusion practices, certain limitations should be considered. The relatively small sample size may limit generalisability, and infants who never received a transfusion were excluded, which may have introduced selection bias. Although the models were internally validated, prospective validation in the same NICU is in progress, and external validation in other centres is also necessary to confirm reproducibility. The retrospective design reflects real-world clinical practice but may limit causal interpretations. The primary limitation is the inability to establish causality; while associations such as the link between lower gestational age and increased transfusion requirements were observed, these may be influenced by unmeasured confounding factors, including institutional protocols, clinician practices and patient-level variability. Nevertheless, these results lay a strong foundation for future prospective and multicentre evaluations.

Our findings suggest that predictive models based on gestational age and birth weight may help anticipate transfusion requirements, thereby reducing blood wastage and donor exposure, while the use of satellite blood packs and sterile connection devices may further improve safety and efficiency. Future research should focus on prospectively validating such models in diverse clinical settings to confirm their reliability and generalisability, as well as on evaluating their integration into blood bank inventory systems and clinical workflows. These approaches may enhance transfusion safety, particularly in resource-limited NICUs.
