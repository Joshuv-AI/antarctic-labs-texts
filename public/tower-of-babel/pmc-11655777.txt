Identification of potential biomarkers for hepatocellular carcinoma based on machine learning and bioinformatics analysis
Authors: Chen Chen, Rui Peng, Shengjie Jin, Yuhong Tang, Huanxiang Liu, Daoyuan Tu, Bingbing Su, Shunyi Wang, Guoqing Jiang, Jun Cao, Chi Zhang, Dousheng Bai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetastasis is the major cause of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) mortality. But the effective biomarkers for HCC metastasis remain underexplored. Here we integrated GEO (Gene Expression Omnibus) and TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) datasets to screen candidate genes for hepatocellular carcinoma metastasis, a consensus metastasis-derived prognostic signature (MDPS) was constructed by machine learning. Based on the risk scores, HCC patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups. Comprehensive analyses were conducted to investigate various aspects including survival outcomes, clinical characteristics, immune cell infiltration, as well as in vitro experiments. Together, we develop a comprehensive machine learning-based program for constructing a consensus MDPS including four genes (SPP1, TYMS, HMMR and MYCN). Our findings revealed that four genes could serve as efficient prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in HCC. In addition, in vitro experiments showed that HMMR overregulation exacerbated tumor progression, including proliferation, migration and invasion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01667-w.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Metastasis, Machine learning, Prognosis, Bioinformatics

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the seventh most prevalent malignant tumor, is the fourth most frequent cause of tumor mortality [1]. Metastasis, the main contributor to cancer-related mortality, refers to the migration of cells from the primary tumor to distant locations in the body, where they establish secondary tumors. This process plays a critical role in the progression of cancer. Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), Cancer stem-like cells (CSCs) and autophagy have all been identified in the literature as mechanisms that contribute to cancer cell metastasis. Predicting metastasis in HCC is a difficult problem to solve because of the complex mechanisms and the lack of clarity of key genes in HCC.

Using GEO and TCGA databases has allowed researchers to discover several prognostic markers for tumors as a result of the advancement of microarray and high-throughput sequencing technologies. Databases like GEPIA, cBioPortal, and Human Protein Atlas, which are based on the TCGA dataset, are capable of analyzing and verifying hub gene expression and its effect on survival.

The genes identified play crucial roles in HCC proliferation and metastasis, thereby impacting patient survival and prognosis. Li S [2] identified TYMS as a potential biomarker of metastatic risk in HCC by using “limma” to screen differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and construction of PPI network, which is a single approach and lacks validation in external datasets. Not only did we incorporate a multicenter dataset, but the uniqueness and appropriateness of the modeling approach makes expression-based multigene characterization meaningful.

To develop an ideal biomarker, based on 36 metastasis genes, we constructed and multicenter validated a metastasis-derived prognostic signature via 77 machine-learning algorithm combinations. After incorporating five published signatures of HCC and a number of important clinicopathological features, our metastasis-derived prognostic signature (MDPS) also showed stable and dramatic improvements in prediction accuracy. The MDPS risk score consists of four genes involved, and the risk score was used to forecast tumor immune cell infiltration and immunotherapeutic efficacy with comprehensive validation. Furthermore, we selected HMMR for further validation.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and analysis methods

The gene expression profiling datasets (GSE28248 [2], GSE27635 [2], GSE14520 [3], GSE45114 [4], GSE76427 [5], GSE109211 [6], GSE104580 [7], and GSE149614 [8]) were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) [9]. For drug-related datasets, we obtained GSE109211 cohorts and GSE104580 cohorts. Utilizing these drug-related datasets, it was possible to evaluate how well MDPS performed at foretelling Sorafenib and TACE benefits in HCC. RNA-seq data were obtained from the TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and the ICGC (https://dcc.icgc.org) [10].

GSE149614 was used to acquire the single-cell sequencing data of 4 samples from HCC [11]. The “Seurat” R package in R Studio was utilized for the analysis of raw data obtained from GSE149614. This software allowed for comprehensive examination and exploration of the dataset, providing valuable insights and facilitating further analyses [12, 13]. We utilized markers from earlier studies along with CellMarker for the purpose of annotation [8].

Screening of DEGs

With a corrected P < 0.05 and a cutoff of |logFC|> 1, for the analysis of DEGs between tumors and normals, the “limma” [14] package was used. For further investigation, the shared DEGs in TCGA, GSE28248, and GSE27635 were screened.

Signatures generated by machine learning based integration methods

We formed a combination of 77 algorithms through 8 machine learning algorithms in order to explore a consensus MDPS with highly precise and stable performance. The integrative algorithms included RSF [15], Enet [16], Lasso [17], stepwise Cox [18], plsRcox [19], SuperPC [20], GBM [21], and survival-SVM [22]. The signature generation procedure was as follows: (a) The TCGA-LIHC cohort was able to identify genes associated with metastatic potential using Univariate Cox regression; (b) 77 algorithm combinations were then run on these genes to fit predictive models based on the leave one out cross validation (LOOCV) framework in the TCGA-LIHC cohort; (c) across three validation datasets (GSE14520, GSE45114 and GSE76427) were detected for all of the models; (d) all of the validation datasets for each model were merged to produce the Harrell coherence index (C-index), and the model with the highest mean C index was considered the best [23].

Clinical correlation analysis

Clinicopathologic data from the TCGA-LIHC cohort were gathered and categorized. The Wilcoxon test was used to analyze variations in riskscore distribution across subgroups sharing similar clinicopathological characteristics. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to evaluate potential independent prognostic factors, such as age, gender, stage, and risk score. To validate the nomogram and its integration of these prognostic factors, calibration and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were subsequently generated.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

We analyzed the primary enrichment pathways in two groups through GSEA analysis using the R package “clusterProfiler” for the sake of deeper understanding the underlying biological processes [24].

Immunogenomic landscape analysis

Researchers utilize the CIBERSORT [25] technique to measure the proportion of immune cells that have infiltrated a specific area (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) [26]. In addition, immune infiltration in 28 types of immune cells was assessed using single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) [27].

The role of MDPS in predicting efficacy of sorafenib and TACE

One of the most important treatment options for those with HCC is sorafenib and TACE therapy. Accordingly, investigating the role of risk score in evaluating the efficacy of Sorafenib and TACE holds significant clinical relevance. We evaluated the risk scores for individual samples in both GSE109211 and GSE104580, examining their utility in predicting responses to Sorafenib and TACE treatment modalities.

Cell–cell communication

To analyze cellular communication at the molecular level, we utilized the “Cellchat R package” [28]. By calculating network centrality metrics for each cell group, identify the main senders, receivers, mediators, and influencers within the intercellular communication network.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

QRT-PCR assay and IHC methods were conducted based on previous literature [29]. Total RNA was isolated from both HCC and corresponding paracancerous tissues using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Reverse transcription into cDNA was then carried out with the PrimeScriptTM RT Reagent Kit (Takara) [30]. The quantification was performed using the SYBR-Green PCR kit (Takara). The results of qRT-PCR were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt approach [30]. IHC was performed through steps of deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity, antibody incubation, signal detection, and finally dehydration, fixation, and mounting of slides. Proteintech (Wuhan, China) provided the main antibodies. The particular primers employed are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot

For Western blot analysis, Protein samples were obtained from the tissues using cell lysis buffer (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Cat. No. P0013), loaded onto an SDS/PAGE gel, 30 µg of protein per well, and subsequently transferred onto a PVDF membrane. Following pretreatment with methanol, the membrane was then blocked with 5% BSA (Bovine Serum Albumin, Beyotime) for a duration of 1 h [31]. Following incubation at 4 °C for 12 h, the membranes were treated with the primary antibodies anti-HMMR (Proteintech, Cat.No.15820–1-AP) at 1:5000, Beta Actin (Proteintech, Cat.No.66009–1-Ig) at 1:20000. After rinsing, the membranes were incubated with the secondary antibody (Cell Signaling, Cat. No. #7074) at a dilution of 1:2000, at room temperature for 1 h. Detection of signals was accomplished with an enhanced chemiluminescent reagent (NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China), then assessed on a Biorad Chemidoc XRS Gel Imaging System and analyzed with ImageJ software [29].

Cell lines

The human HCC cell lines SK-Hep1 [31], Huh7 [32], and PLC/PRF/5 [29] were obtained from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cell cultures were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The culture conditions involved maintaining cells at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator [29].

Lentivirus infection

Transfecting PLC/PRF/5 cells with the HMMR cDNA lentiviral vector and its corresponding control vector was performed using lentiviral vectors purchased from Shanghai Genomeditech Company. By treating the transfected cells with puromycin for 3 days, antibiotic-resistant cells were selectively selected.

Cell migration, invasion, wound healing and colony formation

The migration and invasion assays were conducted in 24-well plates using Transwell chambers. In the upper chamber lacking serum, 1 × 10^5 cells were seeded, while the lower chamber was filled with 500μL of complete medium. The cells were then fixed onto the plates using paraformaldehyde for 30 min, followed by staining with 0.1% crystal violet for an additional 30 min. In the wound healing assay, scratches were made in six-well plates, and photographs were taken at intervals of 0, 24, and 48 h to document wound healing progression. Cell lines stably transfected with HMMR overexpression and their corresponding control groups were cultured in six-well plates for 14 days. The fixation and staining protocols followed were the same as those used in the Transwell assays.

Statistical methods

R (version 4.3.2) was used for all data processing, statistical analysis, and generating plots. Pearson's correlation coefficients were used to assess the relationships between two variables that are measured on a continuous scale. The comparison of continuous variables was conducted using either the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or T test. These statistical analyses were employed to explore the relationships and differences among the variables of interest [33].

Results

20 matched HCC samples from lymph node metastasis patients were included in the GSE28248 gene expression profile, which also included both intratumoral and peritumoral tissue. GSE27635 contains 24 sets of intratumoral and peritumoral samples from individuals with HCC experiencing bone metastasis. 115 DEGs were found. The volcano plot and the heatmap of GSE28248 DEGs is shown in Fig. 1A. The volcano plot and the heatmap of GSE27635 DEGs is shown in Fig. 1B. From the TCGA-LIHC dataset, 7260 DEmRNAs were obtained. Then, we obtained overlapping DEGs for TCGA and two datasets (Fig. 1C).

Integrative construction of a consensus signature.

Univariate Cox analysis identified 36 prognostic genes based on the 67 genes associated with metastasis (Fig. 2A). To develop a consensus signature, we employed an integrative machine learning-based approach using 36 prognostic genes. The TCGA-LIHC dataset was utilized, and we implemented the LOOCV framework. During this process, a total of 77 distinct prediction models were fitted to the dataset, allowing for comprehensive analysis and optimization of the signature. By employing machine learning techniques and a rigorous validation framework, we aimed to create a robust and reliable signature for further analysis and interpretation. Next, we calculated the C-index for every model in all validation datasets (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Table 2). Based on our study, it appears that the final optimal model for our analysis was based on the Random Survival Forest (RSF) algorithm. This model achieved the highest mean C index of 0.662, indicating its good predictive performance in estimating survival outcomes. By using the RSF algorithm, we can identify the top 29 important variables among all the variables considered. These variables were considered to have the highest impact on the predicted survival outcomes. Then 29 variables were used to construct final model for survival analysis (Fig. 2C).

The swift advancement in high-throughput sequencing has illuminated the precise treatment and stratified management of tumors. In recent years, machine-learning algorithms like LASSO and SVM have led to the creation of several prognostic signatures for HCC, using extensive datasets of superior quality. Consequently, we gathered five existing prognostic signatures to assess and contrast the predictive precision of MDPS against these established models (Supplementary Table 3). We subsequently assessed the prognostic capability of MDPS and these five signatures by calculating the C-index within the TCGA cohort. Our MDPS demonstrated markedly higher accuracy compared to the other models (Fig. 3C). Patients with hepatocellular carcinoma were categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. Kaplan–Meier was carried out on the three subsets. The findings indicated a better prognosis in the low-risk group compared to the high-risk group (Fig. 3D–F). Furthermore, survival analysis using Kaplan–Meier was conducted on the ICGC dataset. This demonstrated a notably improved prognosis for the low-risk group in comparison to the high-risk group. (p = 0.047) (Fig. S1). ROC analysis revealed stable predictive performance of the signature across different subsets: for the TCGA-all subset, the AUCs for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival were 0.795, 0.699, and 0.702, respectively; for the TCGA-train subset, the corresponding AUCs were 0.797, 0.662, and 0.658; and for the TCGA-test subset, the AUCs were 0.788, 0.797, and 0.78, indicating consistent performance (Fig. 3G–I). Plots depicting survival outcomes showed increased mortality in high-risk patients, providing additional evidence of the accuracy of our MDPS (Fig. 4A–C). In both high- and low-risk groups, four signature genes showed consistent expression patterns across training and test sets, as illustrated by heatmaps (Fig. 4D–F).

Construction of a nomogram

To enhance the utility of the tool for patients, a nomogram was developed using age, gender, stage, and risk score (Fig. 4G). By summing all items, the resulting nomogram scores for each patient can forecast survival rates at 1, 3, and 5 years. Furthermore, calibration curves demonstrated the nomogram precise prediction accuracy for survival outcomes (Fig. 4H). In our study, we found that the risk score is an independent predictor of survival, while the nomogram is highly predictive of survival in patients with HCC.

Clinical correlation analysis of MDPS

To delve deeper into the clinical implications of the prognostic signature, correlations between age, gender, T stage, N stage, M stage, and overall stage with the prognostic signature were examined. Findings indicated elevated risk score in T4 compared to T1, T3 compared to T1, T2 compared to T1, stage III compared to stage I, and stage II compared to stage I (Fig. 5A–F). In general, our prognostic model, based on four distinctive genes, exhibited remarkable prognostic efficacy.

GSEA between high‑ and low‑risk groups

Both cohorts of high-risk and low-risk individuals took part in GSEA. We focused on the C2 collection, which is defined by MSigDB. In the high-risk cohort, we found that genes with a high-risk score were predominantly enriched in immune biological activities, ECM receptor interaction and MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 6A). Contemporaneously, in the low-risk score cohort, genes were predominantly enriched in pathways related to metabolism, including glycolysis gluconeogenesis, and valine leucine and isoleucine degradation (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Table 4). On the other hand, similarly, for HALLMARK gene sets, several immunological activities and metabolic processes were enriched in the different groups, respectively (Fig. 6C, D and Supplementary Table 5). Based on the aforementioned results, it can be inferred that the MDPS risk score holds great potential as a reliable indicator for assessing the diverse tumor microenvironment statuses in HCC.

Relationship between MDPS and tumor immune microenvironment (TIME)

By applying the CIBERSORT method, we generated 22 unique immune cell profiles in HCC patients. The proportions of immune subtypes infiltrating the tumor were analyzed to explore the relationship between the MDPS and the immune characteristics (Fig. 7A). According to the CIBERSORT algorithm, the level of native B cells and activated NK cells in the high risk score group was significantly lower than that in the low risk score, and the level of T cells regulatory (Tregs) and Macrophages M0 was increased in the high risk score group (Fig. 7B). The increase in Tregs and macrophages M0 may suppress the anti-tumor immune response, allowing the tumor to better evade immune surveillance. A decrease in the levels of native B cells and activated NK cells may indicate an insufficient anti-tumor immune response. This suggests that the high-risk score group may have stronger immunosuppressive characteristics and weaker immune-killing capability, indicating that tumors may grow and spread more easily in this environment. The ssGSEA algorithm results showed that the level of Immature dendritic cell, MDSC, Natural killer T cell, and Regulatory T cell was significantly higher in the high-risk compared with the low-risk group (Fig. 7C). The increase in MDSC and regulatory T cell (Treg) is typically associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment. MDSC and Tregs can inhibit the activity of other immune cells, making it more challenging for the immune system in the high-risk group to effectively recognize and attack tumor cells or infection sources. This suggests that a stronger immunosuppressive environment in the high-risk group, relatively weaker anti-tumor immunity, and a higher likelihood of disease progression or deterioration.

Prediction of immunotherapy, Sorafenib, and TACE efficacy by the MDPS

The utilization of immune checkpoint inhibitors has yielded favorable clinical outcomes in the treatment of diverse malignancies [35]. Through an examination of the relationship between genes in MDPS and prevalent immune checkpoints, our investigation revealed a noteworthy positive correlation between four genes and PD-1/PD-L1 expression (Fig. 8A). Furthermore, elevated TIDE scores were linked to inferior immune checkpoint blockade therapy and reduced survival rates. Furthermore, the group with higher TIDE scores and a high-risk score exhibited a reduced response to immunocheckpoint inhibitor treatment (Fig. 8B). Based on the results of GSE109211, A notable distinction in risk score was observed between the Sorafenib Response and Non-Response group, with the responders exhibiting a higher risk score (Fig. 8C). In the GSE109211 cohort, MDPS demonstrated a substantial ability to distinguish between responders and nonresponders of Sorafenib treatment (AUC = 0.739) (Fig. 8D). In the analysis of GSE104580, a significant disparity in risk score was identified between the TACE Response and Non-Response group (Fig. 8E), and MDPS could distinguish clearly between TACE respondents and nonresponders in GSE104580 cohort (AUC = 0.606) (Fig. 8F).

HMMR expression is an independent prognosis factor in HCC patients

We examined mRNA expression levels of specific genes in both HCC tumor and adjacent tissues using data from the TCGA database, observing that four genes showed notably higher mRNA expression in tumor tissues compared to adjacent ones (Fig. S2). Following this, we assessed mRNA levels for these four genes in a set of 10 matched HCC and adjacent tissue samples, where the data indicated that HMMR expression was markedly increased in tumor tissues relative to adjacent tissues (Fig. 11A). To further confirm these findings, we analyzed an additional 25 pairs of HCC tumors and peri-tumor samples. Results from qRT-PCR corroborated the bioinformatics analysis of the TCGA data (Fig. 11B). IHC analysis was performed on a tissue microarray slide that included samples from 106 patients diagnosed with HCC (Fig. 11C). This analysis aimed to further identify the heightened expression of HMMR in tumor tissues compared to adjacent peritumor normal tissues. Furthermore, we assessed the correlation between HMMR levels and clinicopathological characteristics in a cohort of 106 patients with HCC.HMMR levels were found to have a strong correlation with liver cirrhosis (P = 0.042), tumor number (P = 0.036), vascular tumor thrombus (P < 0.001), and TNM stage (P < 0.001, Table 1). When compared to HCC patients with low HMMR expression (n = 69), those with high HMMR expression (n = 37) tended to have shorter overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) (Fig. 11D, E).

HMMR modulates the proliferation, invasiveness and metastasis of HCC cells.

We assessed HMMR expression in three cell lines and found lower expression levels of HMMR in PLC cells, whereas SK-Hep1 cells exhibited higher expression levels (Fig. 12A). An investigation of the role of HMMR in invasive and metastatic of HCC cells was conducted, we overexpressed HMMR in PLC. The level of HMMR overexpression was validated by WB and qRT-PCR (Fig. 12B). Results from both the wound healing and Transwell assays demonstrated that HMMR overexpression significantly increased the migration and invasion potential of HCC cells (Fig. 12C, D). Additionally, the colony formation assay revealed that HMMR overexpression promoted the proliferative capacity of HCC cells (Fig. 12E).

Discussion

Tumor metastasis involves cancer cells spreading from their origin to other body regions through blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, or body cavities [36]. In addition, the development of metastases from malignant tumors is frequently the primary cause of treatment failure and the leading cause of death in most patients [36]. Detecting potential biomarkers early is critical to achieving early diagnosis and treatment. Our goal in this study was to discover genes that potentially influence HCC metastasis by analyzing comprehensive datasets from GEO and TCGA. Through the LOOCV framework, we fitted a total of 77 models to the TCGA dataset. Subsequently, validation on three independent datasets revealed that the RSF yielded the optimal model. In our study, the integration process entailed amalgamating various machine learning algorithms to formulate models that consistently predicted HCC prognosis. This approach not only reduced the dimensionality of variables but also contributed to simplifying and translating the models effectively. Ultimately, we constructed an MDPS comprising four genes associated with HCC metastasis: SPP1, TYMS, HMMR, and MYCN.

Furthermore, all samples were divided into low-risk and high-risk groups according to the calculated risk score. By performing the GSEA, we were able to find that the high-risk group was linked to signaling pathways that aid in the metastasis of cancer cells, such as EMT, TGF-beta signaling, and Wnt/beta-catenin signaling. EMT is a process in which epithelial cells are transformed to mesenchymal cells, epithelial cells lose acinar cell polarity, lose adhesion and acquire a mesenchymal cell phenotype, and acquire the ability to migrate as mesenchymal cells to promote metastasis and drug resistance. EMT is common in embryonic development, wound healing, organ fibrosis and tumor metastasis. The Wnt/beta-catenin signaling pathway controls the proliferation, differentiation, and homeostasis of cells during embryonic development and adult tissue maintenance. This further demonstrated the accuracy of our established MDPS.

In HCC, CD4+ T cells [37], CD8+T cells [38], and NK cells [39] have been shown to behave as protective factors. According to their role, significant decreases in CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells infiltration may also cause immune evasion of the tumor, ultimately leading to the growth of HCC, a high rate of recurrence and a poor prognosis [40]. Additionally, crucial cells in immunotherapy include NK cells, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells. Immunotherapy with PD-L1/PD-1 inhibition is most effective when CD4+ T cells are present [41]. IFN-γ is released by activated CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes. PDLl is expressed on tumor cells as a result of IFN-γ stimulation [42]. The effectiveness of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibition is influenced by NK cells and cytotoxic T cells [43]. Intriguingly, that we found that in the immunological microenvironment of patients with high-risk scores from the TCGA dataset, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, and NK cell infiltration were more abundant than in patients with a low-risk score. However, this immune activation does not necessarily translate to effective anti-tumor immunity, as immune evasion mechanisms can also develop in response to chronic exposure [44, 45]. Patients in the high-risk group may have a more complex immune microenvironment, where effector cell infiltration coexists with suppressive factors (such as MDSCs and Tregs), which can weaken the function of effector cells and make the disease more difficult to control. These findings imply that the prognostic outcome of patients might be influenced by the risk score through the TIME [46, 47]. In summary, the high-risk group exhibits potential immune imbalance, enhanced immune evasion, and limited response to immunotherapy.

Treatment options for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma include immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), chemotherapy, and TACE. Individuals with a higher sorafenib sensitivity had higher risk scores, whereas individuals with a lower TACE sensitivity had lower risk score. To validate the significance of risk scores as biomarkers for assessing immune status, we conducted additional investigations into the response rate of ICIs using TIDE scores. The elevated TIDE scores in the high-risk group suggest that this group might have a better response to ICI treatment. In light of this, we think that the risk score may serve as a crucial guide for hepatocellular carcinoma treatment, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and TACE. Sorafenib may be sensitive to patients with high-risk ratings, whereas TACE may be sensitive to patients with low-risk scores. Then, ROC analysis revealed that risk scores had a greater level of accuracy in estimating benefits based on sorafenib and TACE, suggesting that MDPS may be a practical tool for customized decision-making for hepatocellular carcinoma patients.

We then looked at the scRNA-seq profile of GSE149614 to further investigate the diversity in expression of the four genes that make up MDPS in diverse cells in HCC. We divided 14 cell populations into six different cell categories, including HCC, Macrophages, T cell, B cell, Fibroblast, Endothelial cell. The findings demonstrated that TYMS, SPP1, and HMMR could be expressed in almost all cell types, whereas the expression of MYCN was essentially absent. Nevertheless, the mechanism of how the four genes interact with these cells requires further study to explain their potential roles in hepatocellular carcinoma development.

In order to corroborate the aforementioned findings, qRT-PCR was conducted to reveal that the expression levels of HMMR, SPP1, TYMS were significantly elevated in HCC tissues compared to paracellular carcinoma tissues, with the most pronounced difference in HMMR, while there was no significant difference in MYCN gene. Moreover, we observed a significant and inverse correlation between HMMR expression and both HCC patient survival time and RFS. To establish stable cell lines with HMMR overexpression in PLC cells, we utilized lentiviral transfection technology. Our in vitro experiments validated that HMMR promotes HCC cell proliferation, invasion and potential metastasis. These combined results underscore the potential of HMMR as a promising predictive tumor marker for individuals diagnosed with HCC.

Conclusion

Taken together, we identified a consensus MDPS with the potential to function as a prognostic marker for HCC. The MDPS showed comparable performance in the prediction of prognosis, assessment of tumor immune cell infiltration and immunotherapy response. The analysis of the HMMR gene offers significant insights into its potential importance and role in HCC. This study, as a whole, establishes a basis for diagnosing and treating patients with HCC by combining transcriptomics data and computational algorithms.
