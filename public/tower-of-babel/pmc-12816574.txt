Integrated proteomics and metabolomics analysis revealed that macrophage-related signals may be potential biomarkers for oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Baogui Yang, Zhen Zhang, Yan Liu, Zhaojian Gong, Geng Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is a prevalent and aggressive malignancy with poor prognosis, yet the protein-level and metabolic alterations driving disease progression remain poorly characterized. Here, we integrated multi-omics analysis combining proteomics, metabolomics, spatial transcriptomics, and single-cell RNA sequencing data to systematically compare OSCC tumors with matched adjacent non-tumor tissues. Our proteomic profiling identified significant upregulation of immune-related proteins ARPC4 in OSCC, which correlated with poor patient survival. Notably, C1QC-positive macrophages (Macro_C1QC) were identified as the primary source of these dysregulated proteins, with high Macro_C1QC infiltration serving as an independent prognostic marker. Integrated proteo-metabolomic analysis revealed profound dysregulation of tryptophan metabolism pathways, suggesting a potential association with macrophage-mediated immunosuppression in the OSCC microenvironment. These findings collectively indicate that tryptophan metabolic imbalance might promote OSCC progression by reprogramming the phenotype of macrophages. In summary, our study not only delineates the proteomic and metabolomic landscape of OSCC but also identifies immune-related proteins ARPC4 along with Macro_C1QC macrophages as novel prognostic biomarkers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31899-x.Keywords: Proteomics, Metabolomics, Oral squamous cell carcinoma, Macrophages, C1QCSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology

Introduction

Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma (OSCC) is one of the most common malignant tumors in the head and neck region globally, exhibiting high morbidity and mortality rates within head and neck malignancies. According to World Health Organization (WHO) statistics, approximately 377,000 new cases of oral cancer and 177,000 deaths occurred worldwide in 2020, with incidence rates showing a continuous upward trend over the past decade1. The development of OSCC is closely associated with risk factors such as smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, HPV infection, and betel nut chewing2. Despite recent advancements in surgical, radiotherapeutic, and chemotherapeutic techniques, the 5-year survival rate for OSCC patients remains below 60% due to factors like significant tumor heterogeneity, a propensity for early metastasis, and treatment resistance, with outcomes being even poorer for advanced-stage patients3. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying OSCC occurrence and development, and identifying novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets, have become urgent clinical challenges.

In recent years, significant technological advancements in genomics and transcriptomics have substantially propelled the elucidation of OSCC’s molecular mechanisms. At the genomic level, whole-exome sequencing and targeted sequencing have identified significantly higher mutation frequencies in genes such as TP53, CDKN2A, and NOTCH1 compared to normal tissues, with TP53 mutation rates exceeding 80%, suggesting its pivotal role as a “molecular hub” in OSCC development4. Transcriptomic studies have further revealed the aberrant activation of the NOTCH signaling pathway, upregulation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related genes (e.g., SNAI1, VIM), and the expression of immune checkpoint molecules (e.g., PD-L1), all correlating with OSCC invasion and metastasis5,6. However, despite these findings providing a theoretical basis for targeted therapies, clinical translation continues to face significant challenges: Most drug targets focus on proteins or kinases (e.g., the EGFR inhibitor cetuximab), yet clinical trials demonstrate response rates below 20%, coupled with a high propensity for developing resistance7. Moreover, OSCC exhibits significant metabolic heterogeneity; for instance, some tumor regions rely on glycolysis for energy, while others maintain proliferation through oxidative phosphorylation. This metabolic plasticity renders single-targeted strategies inadequate for encompassing the entire patient population8.

Notably, current systematic studies on OSCC have predominantly been confined to genomic and transcriptomic dimensions, leaving the dynamic changes in proteins and metabolites relatively unexplored. Proteins, as the direct executors of life processes, reflect the real-time functional state of tumors more accurately through alterations in their expression levels, post-translational modifications, and interaction networks. Metabolomics, on the other hand, can reveal the characteristics of tumor cell energy metabolic reprogramming9. Liu et al. integrated multi-omics data, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, epigenetics, single-cell analysis, and spatial analysis, to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of lymph node metastasis driving OSCC progression, providing references for precise diagnosis and targeted therapy10. Bai et al. utilized metabolomics to uncover the heterogeneity of macrophages in diabetic nephropathy, identifying a shift in macrophage metabolism towards gluconeogenesis, which promoted the anti-inflammatory function of M2 macrophages and identified potential therapeutic targets for the disease, offering important insights into immune-related mechanisms during disease progression11.

This study aims to integrate proteomics and metabolomics data, incorporating single-cell sequencing data, spatial transcriptomics data, bulk transcriptomics data, and pathological examination data from public repositories such as the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), and Human Protein Atlas (HPA). By systematically analyzing protein expression, immune regulation, and metabolic differences between OSCC and adjacent non-tumor tissues, we seek to uncover novel molecular mechanisms driving OSCC pathogenesis. Our findings may provide potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for improving OSCC treatment strategies.

Results

Proteomic and metabolomic profiles of tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues in OSCC patients

Tumor tissues (n = 9) and adjacent non-tumor tissues (n = 5) were collected from 9 OSCC patients (Supplementary Table 1). Proteins and metabolites were extracted and analyzed for their types and abundances using LC-MS/MS, followed by bioinformatics analysis (Fig. 1A). Quality control of the proteomic data revealed that the error distribution between the observed and theoretical peptide masses followed a normal distribution, with all errors falling within ± 10 ppm (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Furthermore, the relative standard deviation (RSD) of protein quantification values between replicate samples in both groups was less than 0.3, indicating good quantitative reproducibility (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Subsequent analysis identified an average of 6241 proteins in tumor tissues and 4311 proteins in adjacent non-tumor tissues, suggesting a more disordered protein expression profile in the tumor tissues (Fig. 1B). However, no significant abundance differences were observed for these expressed proteins between tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 1C). Regarding the metabolomic data, high signal intensities for standard compounds were observed across different samples (Supplementary Fig. 2A), and the cumulative coefficient of variation (CV) curves for metabolite quantification values in replicate samples showed overall low CV values, indicating good quantitative reproducibility (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Subsequently, an average of 685 metabolites were detected in tumor tissues and 693 in adjacent non-tumor tissues, indicating a similar number of detected metabolites in both groups (Fig. 1D). Similarly, after normalization, the medians of metabolomic data across samples were relatively close (Fig. 1E). Next, cluster analysis was performed separately on the proteomic and metabolomic data from the 14 samples. Both datasets showed a tendency for samples within the same group to cluster together (Fig. 1F and G).

Differential protein analysis and validation between tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues in OSCC patients

Further analysis based on proteomic data was conducted using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The results demonstrated significant differences in the principal components (PCs) between tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissue samples, with samples within the same group showing more similar principal components (Fig. 2A). Subsequent differential expression analysis identified a total of 943 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), among which 351 were upregulated and 583 were downregulated in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 2). These differentially expressed proteins were primarily localized to the cytoplasm, mitochondria, and plasma membrane (Fig. 2C). Subsequently, we randomly selected DEPs including YTHDF2, VAV2, RAB13, FEN1, P4HB, JUND, OGN, PFKM, DLD, and ACADS, and utilized proteomic data from head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) available in the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database to validate their expression levels. The results showed that YTHDF2, VAV2, RAB13, and FEN1 were significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (P < 0.05), while JUND, OGN, PFKM, DLD, and ACADS were significantly higher in adjacent non-tumor tissues (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2D-E). Furthermore, we analyzed immunohistochemistry (IHC) results for RAB13, VAV2, and FEN1 proteins in OSCC tissues and normal mucosa tissues within the HPA database. We found that the expression levels of RAB13, VAV2, and FEN1 proteins were significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 2F), whereas JUND, OGN, and DLD showed higher expression in normal tissues than in tumor tissues (Fig. 2G). These results were consistent with the protein differential expression data obtained by our team in OSCC samples, demonstrating the reliability of our proteomic data.

To investigate the origin of these differentially expressed proteins, we further utilized previously published spatial transcriptomic data of OSCC (GSE220978) from our group for in-depth analysis. We first classified the tumor samples into tumor regions, stromal regions, and muscle/gland regions based on marker genes KRT17 (tumor marker), APOE (stromal cell marker), and DES (muscle, vascular, and glandular marker) (Fig. 2H). Subsequently, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) revealed that upregulated proteins were primarily enriched in the tumor region, whereas downregulated proteins were mainly enriched in the muscle, vascular, and glandular region (Fig. 2I).

Significantly enriched immune-related proteins are associated with prognosis in OSCC patients

The involvement of the immune system in tumor development is a central focus in tumor biology research12. Our functional analysis of the 351 proteins upregulated in OSCC tumor tissues revealed a significant immune component: 110 proteins were associated with the immune system and 30 with immune diseases (Fig. 3A), suggesting profound immune dysregulation in the tumor microenvironment. To identify specific immune pathways, we performed KEGG enrichment analysis on the upregulated proteins. This analysis highlighted two significantly enriched immune-related pathways: “Antigen processing and presentation” and “Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis” (Fig. 3B). A detailed examination of the “Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis” pathway identified 8 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) (Fig. 3C). Notably, this included VAV2, a protein previously reported to promote gastric cancer progression13 and was also upregulated in our cohort. Similarly, 8 proteins were mapped to the “Antigen processing and presentation” pathway (Fig. 3D). We then validated the expression of key proteins from these pathways using the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database. For the Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis pathway, the normalized protein expression (nRPX) values for ARPC4, CFL2, FCGR3B, PLPP3, and SYK were significantly different between tumor and normal tissues (Fig. 3E, P < 0.03). For the Antigen processing and presentation pathway, the nRPX values for HLA-DPA1, HSPA5, IFI30, PADI3, and PSME3 were significantly higher in tumor tissues (Fig. 3F, P < 0.00058).

Finally, we investigated the clinical relevance of these differentially expressed proteins. We found that high ARPC4 expression was significantly associated with poorer survival (P < 0.015), a finding validated in two independent GEO datasets, GSE42743 and GSE75538 (Fig. 3G). To further assess its prognostic value, we extended our analysis to the TCGA cohort for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC). High ARPC4 expression was correlated with significantly decreased overall survival (P = 0.017) and disease-free survival (P = 0.02) (Fig. 3H), underscoring its potential as a prognostic biomarker. It is noteworthy that while ARPC4 showed promise in univariate analysis, it failed to reach statistical significance as an independent predictor in the multivariate Cox regression model; this could be a consequence of the relatively small sample size, which may limit the statistical power to detect independent effects (Supplementary·Figure 3).

High infiltration levels of C1QC-positive macrophages in tumor tissues are significantly associated with poor prognosis in OSCC patients

To further investigate the sources of the differentially expressed immune-related proteins and their potential mechanisms of action, we downloaded a published OSCC single-cell RNA sequencing dataset (GSE181919). GSVA enrichment analysis of these genes revealed that they were primarily enriched in macrophages (Fig. 4A). Additionally, SYK and VAV2, two of the differentially expressed immune-related proteins identified in the proteomic analysis, were also differentially expressed in macrophages (Fig. 4B). This suggests that the differentially expressed immune-related proteins observed in OSCC tissue samples are primarily contributed by differentially expressed proteins within macrophages. This also implies that macrophages may play a crucial role in OSCC development and progression. Therefore, we performed a detailed re-clustering of the macrophage data. We identified a total of 7 cell clusters (Fig. 4C). Based on the marker gene expression profiles of each subcluster, they were ultimately annotated as cDC_CD1C, Macro_C1QC, Macro_FCN1, Macro_MKI67, and pDC_LILRA4 (Fig. 4D-E). Cell proportion data from the single-cell sequencing showed that the proportion of Macro_C1QC cells was significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 4F). The differentially expressed proteins SYK and ARPC4 identified by proteomics also exhibited high expression levels in the Macro_C1QC cluster (Fig. 4G-H). This suggests that the enrichment of Macro_C1QC in OSCC tissues might lead to alterations in the Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis and Antigen processing and presentation signaling pathways. Furthermore, using OSCC cohort data (GSE42743), C1QC was upregulated in tumor tissues, and its high expression level was significantly associated with poor prognosis in OSCC patients (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4I-L).

Metabolite differential analysis between OSCC tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues

Macrophages are regulated by various metabolites in the microenvironment14, and their metabolic patterns are closely related to their functions. To further investigate the metabolic characteristics of OSCC, we subsequently performed differential metabolite analysis on tumor tissues (n = 9) and adjacent non-tumor tissues (n = 5). Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) analysis showed that tumor tissue samples and adjacent non-tumor tissue samples clustered separately, demonstrating distinct differences (Fig. 5A). A total of 100 upregulated and 107 downregulated metabolites were identified in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Table 3).

Comparative metabolomic analysis revealed significant heterogeneity in seven major metabolite classes between tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 5C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated that these differentially expressed metabolites were primarily associated with cell growth/death and membrane transport functions. Notably, the human disease pathway most enriched with differential metabolites was “cancer: overview” (n = 9), while the most significantly enriched metabolic pathway was amino acid metabolism (n = 30) (Fig. 5D).

Network analysis of these differential metabolites identified distinct amino acid metabolic patterns in tumor tissues: upregulated metabolites were predominantly concentrated in cysteine and methionine metabolism and tryptophan metabolism pathways, while downregulated metabolites were mainly enriched in arginine and proline metabolism (Fig. 5E). Particularly noteworthy was tryptophan (Trp) metabolism, which plays crucial roles in immune regulation and gut homeostasis.

Integrated proteomic and metabolomic analysis reveals protein-metabolite regulatory networks in OSCC

To further analyze the synergistic mechanisms between proteins and metabolites in OSCC, we calculated the correlation between the identified differential metabolites and differential proteins using the Spearman method and generated a correlation heatmap (Fig. 6A). Top 200 protein-metabolite correlations with a correlation coefficient above 0.8 and Padj < 0.05 were selected for correlation network analysis (Supplementary Table 4). In tumor tissues, short peptides isoleucine (Ala-Ile and Ile-Val) and serine (Ser-Ile and Ser-Tyr) are negatively correlated with the abundance of most differentially expressed proteins, while L-Prolyl-L-isoleucine and N-Acetylhistidine are positively correlated with the abundance of more differentially expressed proteins. (Fig. 6B). To systematically identify protein-metabolite pairs with potential regulatory relationships, we performed Fisher’s exact test on integrated lists of differential proteins and metabolites (P < 0.05). We observed that the Tryptophan metabolism pathway remained significantly enriched with both metabolites and differential proteins (Fig. 6C). Further network analysis focusing on KEGG pathways enriched by both differential metabolites and proteins revealed that the process of chemical carcinogenesis contained the highest number of enriched differential metabolites and proteins. This process refers to the induction of malignant transformation in normal cells and the subsequent development into tumors by certain chemical substances through specific mechanisms. We noted that arecoline mixtures, a common inducer of OSCC, lead to the upregulation of genes such as KRAS15. Concurrently, the network also included the metabolic imbalance of L-kynurenine resulting from tryptophan metabolism dysregulation, which is a common metabolic disorder in tumor tissues16 (Fig. 6D). Subsequently, KEGG pathways commonly enriched by both proteomic and metabolomic data were visualized to further explore the regulatory relationships between differential proteins and metabolites within the same enriched pathway. This analysis revealed that up to 12 factors within the Tryptophan metabolism pathway were affected, indicating alterations in this metabolic route (Fig. 6E).

Materials and methods

Ethical compliance statement

This study involving human participants was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the 2024 World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Cancer Hospital (No. SBOLL-2024-060) and the Ethics Committee of Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (No. LYEC2024-K0105). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to tissue collection, with explicit disclosure of study objectives, potential risks (including tissue procurement discomfort and data anonymization limitations), and voluntary participation rights. All clinical investigations were performed following the principles of beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice, with special protections for vulnerable populations as stipulated in Article 23 of the Declaration. De-identified tissue samples and clinical data were stored in compliance with GDPR and China’s Personal Information Protection Law.

Sample collection and processing

Primary OSCC tissues and matched adjacent non-tumor tissues were collected from patients undergoing surgical resection at Hunan Cancer Hospital and Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. Tissue specimens were divided into tumor tissues (T) and adjacent non-tumor tissues (N) groups. The pathological types of all tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues were confirmed by the Department of Pathology, with the corresponding clinical characteristics of the samples detailed in Supplementary Table 1. All samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen within 30 min post-resection and stored at -80 °C until subsequent proteomic and metabolomic analyses.

Proteomic profiling and data analysis

Frozen tissue samples were homogenized in liquid nitrogen-cooled mortars and lysed in buffer containing 8 M urea and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay. Protein digestion was performed using a precipitation-based protocol involving trichloroacetic acid (TCA) precipitation followed by acetone washing, TEAB buffer resolubilization, and trypsin digestion (1:50 w/w ratio) at 37 °C overnight. The digested peptides were then reduced with 5 mM DTT at 56 °C for 30 min and alkylated with 15 mM iodoacetamide in the dark for 15 min. After acidification with 10% TFA (pH 2–3), samples were desalted using Strata X columns, lyophilized, and quantified using peptide calibration kits.

LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on a Thermo Scientific Vanquish Neo nanoLC system coupled to an Astral high-resolution mass spectrometer operating in positive ion mode. The mass spectrometer was set to scan from 380 to 980 m/z with MS1 resolution of 240,000 at 200 m/z (maximum injection time 5 ms, AGC 500%). For DIA analysis, 299 isolation windows of 2 m/z each were used, with MS2 spectra acquired at 120,000 resolution using 25 eV collision energy and 3 ms maximum injection time.

Raw data were processed using DIA-NN software with the following parameters: trypsin digestion allowing one missed cleavage, carbamidomethylation of cysteine as fixed modification, and methionine oxidation plus N-terminal acetylation as variable modifications. Protein identification was performed with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 1%. To stabilize variance and approximate a normal distribution, protein intensity values were log2-transformed. Differential expression analysis of proteins was performed using the Linear Models for Microarray and Omics Data (limma) package in R. A linear model was fitted to the data to identify proteins with significantly altered abundance between tumor and adjacent normal tissues. Proteins with log2|FC|>1 and adjusted P-value < 0.01 were considered to be differentially expressed. Bioinformatics analysis included functional annotation, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis17,18 using Fisher’s exact test (P < 0.05), and subcellular localization prediction using Wolf PSORT.

Public database analysis

Normalized protein expression data for head and neck squamous carcinoma (HNSC) and immunohistochemical data for RAB13, VAV2, FEN1, JUND, OGN, and DLD proteins were obtained from The Human Protein Atlas database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). The Human Protein Atlas database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) was accessed to download the nRPX values for proteomics and the immunohistochemistry data for RAB13, VAV2, FEN1, JUND, OGN, and DLD proteins.

Metabolomic profiling and data analysis

Metabolomic analysis was performed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) following standardized protocols from sample preparation to metabolite identification. Frozen tumor and adjacent tissues were homogenized and extracted with methanol containing internal standards. After centrifugation, the supernatants were lyophilized, reconstituted, and centrifuged again prior to LC-MS analysis, with pooled quality control (PQC) samples included for quality assessment. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Waters BEH C18 column with gradient elution at 40 °C. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted on a Q Exactive HF-X system in full scan mode coupled with data-dependent acquisition (DDA), with additional MS/MS spectra collected from PQC samples to facilitate metabolite annotation. Metabolite identification was performed using MS-DIAL software by matching against public and in-house databases, with validation based on retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and fragment ion patterns.

Raw data were processed through log2 transformation, median normalization, and missing value imputation. Method reproducibility was evaluated using PQC samples, while system stability was monitored via internal standards. Technical reproducibility was comprehensively assessed through coefficient of variation (CV) curves, principal component analysis (PCA), and Spearman correlation heatmaps. Metabolite functional classification was performed according to HMDB and KEGG databases. Differential metabolites were identified by combining univariate statistical testing (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < 0.05) with multivariate OPLS-DA, selecting metabolites meeting both variable importance in projection (VIP > 1) and statistical significance criteria. Functional enrichment analysis employed dual strategies including KEGG pathway Fisher’s exact test and metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA), with key metabolic pathways visualized through network diagrams to illustrate coordinated regulation patterns.

Integrated proteomic and metabolomic analysis

A multi-dimensional association framework was established to systematically investigate the interplay between differentially expressed proteins and metabolites. Following sample matching optimization, Spearman correlation analysis (|r|>0.8, Padj < 0.05) was performed to identify significant protein-metabolite pairs, with the top 200 correlated pairs selected to construct a bidirectional interaction network. Biochemical cascade analysis based on KEGG Reaction database enabled reconstruction of regulatory networks comprising enzymatic proteins and their catalyzed metabolites, with topological analysis identifying core regulatory nodes. To elucidate pathway-level associations, joint KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fisher’s exact test, P < 0.05) was conducted for both differential proteins and metabolites, followed by construction of multi-dimensional networks for co-enriched pathways. Pathway visualization was implemented using the pathview package to map protein fold-changes and metabolite alterations onto KEGG pathway maps, revealing coordinated molecular changes within specific metabolic routes. All heatmaps were plotted using the R package “pheatmap” (version 1.0.12, https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/index.html)19.

Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing data were obtained from the GEO database (accession GSE181919)20. Data preprocessing including quality control filtering, normalization (SCTransform/LogNormalize), and dimensionality reduction was performed using the Seurat package in R, with cell clusters visualized via UMAP. Differentially expressed genes were identified using Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while gene set enrichment analysis was conducted with the GSVA package. Cell type proportion visualization was generated using scRNAtoolVis. Spatial transcriptomic data were derived from our previously published dataset (GSE220978)21 and analyzed similarly using Seurat, with comparable GSVA-based gene set enrichment analysis performed for spatial domain characterization.

Survival analysis

Survival analysis for OSCC datasets GSE42743 and GSE75538, obtained from the GEO database, was performed using the BEST online tool (https://rookieutopia.com/app_direct/BEST/)22. This platform integrates patient survival time (overall survival) and event status (death/censorship) to generate Kaplan-Meier survival curves. The analysis evaluated the target gene’s potential as an independent prognostic factor, with hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals calculated to quantify its predictive value. Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the optimal cut-off value, which was determined by identifying the value with the most significant P-value. Survival differences between the two groups were assessed using the Log-rank test, with a statistical significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Discussion and conclusions

Our study systematically delineates the proteomic and metabolomic landscape of OSCC through integrated multi-omics analyses, providing a valuable data resource for future research. Importantly, we uncovered the critical role of immune-related protein dysregulation and its interplay with a specific macrophage subset (Macro_C1QC) in OSCC progression, proposing a novel mechanism by which tryptophan metabolism regulates macrophage functionality.

Key findings include the significant upregulation of immune-related proteins (VAV2, ARPC4, FCGR3B, SYK, and HSPA5) in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues, with their overexpression strongly correlating with malignant features. Notably, prior studies have demonstrated that ARPC4 promotes lymph node metastasis in bladder cancer23, and its knockdown suppresses proliferation, migration, and invasion in bladder cancer cells24. In colorectal cancer, ARPC4 facilitates tumor cell migration by nucleating actin cytoskeletal branching25, while in pancreatic cancer, ARPC4 silencing impairs cell motility, suggesting its therapeutic potential26. Although ARPC4 have been implicated in other cancers, their functional mechanisms in OSCC remain elusive. Our study first establishes their overexpression in OSCC and its correlation with poor clinical outcomes, suggesting their dual roles in fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment and enhancing tumor invasiveness. These findings expand the understanding of ARPC4 in OSCC and highlight their potential as therapeutic targets27,28. Collectively, our results underscore that OSCC progression is driven not only by tumor-intrinsic mutations but also by dynamic crosstalk with the immune microenvironment, particularly through dysregulation of immune-related genes. While our univariate analysis identified several molecules with significant prognostic value, their independent prognostic significance was not confirmed in the multivariate Cox model. This may be due to the limited sample size of our cohort. Therefore, the independent prognostic value of these factors warrants further investigation in larger, multi-center cohorts.

A pivotal discovery is the identification of the C1QC-positive macrophage subset (Macro_C1QC) as a key player in OSCC. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed its significant enrichment in tumors, with high infiltration levels predicting poor patient survival (shorter overall survival). This aligns with prior reports linking CD68 + macrophage abundance to adverse OSCC prognosis29,30, but our study specifically implicates Macro_C1QC as a prognostic biomarker. C1QC, a core subunit of the C1 complex, classically mediates antigen recognition in the complement cascade31, but also functions as an extracellular matrix protein to promote tumor cell adhesion, migration, angiogenesis, and metastasis32. We hypothesize that Macro_C1QC, as a tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) subset33, exacerbates OSCC progression by: (1) secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) to shape an immunosuppressive niche34,35; (2) releasing matrix-remodeling enzymes (e.g., MMP9) to facilitate extracellular matrix degradation and metastasis36. These mechanisms likely underlie its clinical association with aggressive disease.

Tryptophan (Trp) metabolic imbalance is strongly linked to cancer pathogenesis and can influence macrophage polarization37,38. We therefore hypothesized that this mechanism may contribute to OSCC progression. Our integrated proteomic and metabolomic analysis provides correlative evidence that aligns with this hypothesis, suggesting a plausible link that warrants further functional validation, revealing significant dysregulation of the Trp metabolism pathway in OSCC. We found marked alterations in Trp-related metabolites and identified 12 downregulated factors, including key enzymes in the IDO1/TDO-driven kynurenine axis. As this pathway is critical for establishing immune tolerance, its dysregulation could polarize Macro_C1QC cells toward a pro-tumor phenotype39. In turn, these reprogrammed macrophages might promote invasion by secreting inflammatory cytokines and matrix-remodeling factors40. Taken together, our data suggest a potential mechanism whereby Trp metabolism contributes to the phenotypic remodeling of macrophages, thereby facilitating OSCC occurrence and development.

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the high cost of multi-omics profiling restricted our proteomic and metabolomic analyses to a relatively small cohort of OSCC samples. Consequently, our findings require validation in larger, independent cohorts to establish their broader applicability. Second, the conclusions drawn from our integrated analysis should be interpreted as preliminary, given the limited sample size, and we caution against their overinterpretation. Finally, the potential mechanisms we have identified regarding macrophage involvement in OSCC progression are correlative and necessitate functional validation through both in vitro and in vivo experiments.

In summary, our work provides a comprehensive proteomic and metabolomic atlas of OSCC, identifying ARPC4 as upregulated immune-related proteins with significant prognostic value. We systematically identified Macro_C1QC macrophages as a key immune infiltrate strongly associated with poor patient outcomes. Integrated multi-omics analysis revealed that dysregulated tryptophan metabolism might promote OSCC progression by modulating macrophage functionality. These findings provide deeper insights into the complexity of the OSCC tumor microenvironment and offer promising directions for developing novel prognostic biomarkers and targeted therapeutic strategies.
