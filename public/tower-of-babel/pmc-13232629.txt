From farm to lab: gene-edited sheep transforming bone research
Authors: Raine Lunde-Young, Shannon Huggins, Jane H Pryor, Mark E Westhusin, Ken Muneoka, William J Murphy, Larry J Suva, Charles R Long, Dana Gaddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFor more than half a century, mice have been the workhorse of biomedical research. Their small size, rapid reproduction, and well-characterized genetics make them ideal disease models, and genome editing has enabled transgenic, knock-out, and knock-in lines that mimic numerous human conditions. These advances transformed modern biology, yielding fundamental insights into cancer, metabolism, immunity, and more. Their strengths notwithstanding, mouse models have important limitations, as biology does not scale neatly across species. Differences in physiology, size, and metabolism can obscure—or even distort—experimental outcomes. Nowhere is this clearer than in musculoskeletal research. Human bones are dynamic tissues that undergo Haversian remodeling, whereas mice exhibit limited Haversian remodeling and display distinct temporal growth trajectories. Moreover, mice have monophyodont dentition and craniofacial development diverges in ways that impact maxillofacial studies, and aging timelines differ. These differences limit our ability to understand human bone disorders from murine models alone. Biotechnology offers a new path forward: advances in genome sequencing, assembly and molecular engineering enable precise DNA editing in larger domesticated species—sheep, goats, and pigs—whose skeletal size, biomechanics, growth patterns, and remodeling dynamics more closely mirror humans. By introducing targeted, patient-relevant mutations, large-animal models can replicate mechanisms difficult to capture in mice and support longitudinal, clinically-relevant phenotyping—imaging, histomorphometry, serum biomarkers, and functional testing—in a translatable human-like context. The implications are profound. Large-animal models can validate disease pathways, refine biomarkers, and evaluate drugs, biologics, and implants, potentially improving treatment strategies and reducing clinical failures and costs. This shift does not diminish the value of mice, whose genetic tractability and cost-effectiveness ensure a central and continued important role in discovery. Rather, it adds a complementary strategy: expand to gene-edited large-animal models when human skeletal-like biology matters and where mice fall short, thereby bridging the gap between fundamental research and clinical reality.Keywords: rare bone disease, gene editing, bone remodeling, osteocyte, osteoblast, osteoclast, sheep, livestock, mutation

Introduction

The first “gene-edited” livestock (now referred to by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as livestock with intentional genomic alterations (IGAs)1) appeared in the literature in 1985.2 Intentional genomic alterations refer to heritable changes introduced into an animal’s genome through the application of modern molecular technologies. These interventions may be random or targeted and include nucleotide insertions, substitutions, or deletions, or other strategies that alter regulatory elements or introduce exogenous sequences to the genome of the animal. In 1997, the technology was first applied to model a human disease, namely retinitis pigmentosa,3 marking a conceptual shift. Since then, improved technical capabilities have expanded the utility of livestock as biomedical models of human disease. The field was transformed by somatic cell nuclear transfer from adult mammals, which generated “Dolly”4 and when combined with IGAs, “Polly” and “Molly.”5 Since the 1990s, IGA approaches have generated livestock that express novel therapeutic proteins6 as well as numerous examples where livestock model human disease.3,7,8 Methodologies, such as gene transfer via microinjection,2,9 lentiviral transgenesis,10 and inducible transgene expression,11 have expanded the range of large animals to model human disease conditions. Targeted genome editing via zinc finger or transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALEN) and most recently, clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)–CRISPR-associated (Cas) nuclease system have transformed modern biomedical research in both small and large animals. For example, CRISPR/Cas has been used to generate gene-edited sheep to interrogate gene function8,12,13 (Table 1) and to enhance economically important traits, such as muscle mass14 and wool fiber length,15–17 among many others.18 The rapid expansion of well-annotated and curated genomes for sheep, goats, and pigs has provided unprecedented sequence information for targeting regulatory regions, coding sequences, and allelic variants across major livestock species.19–22 Collectively, modern gene editing in livestock is redefining how human disease phenotypes are generated, studied, and evaluated. We are in a truly transformative moment with IGA of livestock models deployed to probe fundamental aspects of musculoskeletal biology.

Of mice and men

Since the emergence of mouse genetics in the 1950s, spontaneously presenting mutations with abnormal or debilitating phenotypes in laboratory stocks were recognized as valuable models of human genetic diseases. Classic murine examples include mutant alleles driving obesity, ob (now Lepob),23 and adiposity/high fat, ad (now Lepad).24 These mouse lines exemplify how both fortuitous and engineered mutations reshaped our understanding of genetic disease mechanisms and phenotyping, given the strong parallels later reported in human obesity. Subsequent intensive research into these and other related mutations led to the identification of the genes encoding the hormone leptin (LEP) and its cognate receptor (LEPR) in several mammalian species, including humans.25 As in mice, homozygous recessive loss-of-function mutations in either LEP or LEPR cause early-onset morbid obesity in humans. However, such human variants are extremely rare and most human obesity syndromes are multigenic in nature.26

A different series of mouse mutations was proposed as the genetic basis of human neuromuscular disease.27 Further investigation concluded that these mice did not faithfully recapitulate the human disorders, despite orthologous gene mutations in both species. In these and other reported instances where mouse phenotypes diverge from the human disease presentation (Table 2), an underlying central truth remains: no animal model is perfect and even mutation of the orthologous gene does not guarantee recapitulation of the complete human disease phenotype. Still, mouse models have yielded substantial biological insight and remain indispensable to modern research.

Because of their phylogenetic relatedness to humans, ease of maintenance and breeding, and the availability of many inbred strains, mice have long served as the primary model organism for human biology and disease.28 This is, however, a double-edged sword. On the one hand, rodents have proven excellent for elucidating both gene function and related signaling pathways in the bone disease setting (e.g., Wnt-signaling pathway,29 Fgf23-Kl-α axis,30,31 c-Src and c-Fos regulation of osteoclastogenesis,32,33 Runx2 haploinsufficiency,34 collagen modifications,35,36 and Tnfsf11,37,38 to name but a few). Mice have been very effective for studying basic biology and understanding pathophysiological mechanisms.39 Numerous mouse gene knockouts (e.g., Bach2, Csf-1, Kl-α, Nacc1, and Tnfsf11) capture many clinical features of rare human genetic diseases known to be caused by mutations in corresponding genes. Where mouse deletions replicate human disease phenotypes, these models are highly informative and effective. The significant cost and time advantages, along with detailed and extensive gene mapping and annotation in mice, ensures this species will remain the first choice for modeling this subset of human diseases.

On the other hand, more complex human disease phenotypes are not easily modeled in mice (Table 2). Across rare diseases (musculoskeletal and otherwise), gene deletions can produce a broad, multi-system phenotypic spectrum,40 yet many examples show incomplete or discordant translation. This does not diminish mouse research in any way: at the molecular level, cross-species congruence is substantial, and there are many examples of monogenic mouse mutations phenotypically resembling their human counterparts.36,41 However, knockouts of other murine genes—such as Ccn6,42 Ube3a,43,44 and Hprt45—show poor concordance with human phenotypes (e.g., pseudo progressive arthropathy of childhood (PPAC), Angelman’s syndrome, and Lesch-Nyhan syndrome) highlighting both the power and limitations of murine models (Table 2).

Large-animal models have also helped address current limitations of murine preclinical research by enabling refined drug development, dosage regimens, pharmacology, and rigorous testing of drug safety and efficacy, in addition to complex surgical procedures, anesthesia, clinical monitoring protocols, and behavioral studies.46 A common translational gap is illustrated by the oncology drug endostatin which elicits striking responses in mice but has shown limited or minimal efficacy in humans.47 Frequently, murine models reproduce only a subset of the human disease phenotype, may display more exaggerated symptom severity than human cases, or in some cases show no clinical phenotype at all48 (Table 2). This is particularly evident for metabolic, endocrine, or pharmacologic actions on the skeleton, where human genotype–phenotype relationships and gene functions frequently diverge from those of mice (Table 2). One functional example involves follicle stimulating hormone, which robustly activates osteoclast activity in mice,49,50 yet does not appear to directly regulate bone resorption in postmenopausal women.51 Similarly, the premature tooth loss observed in patients with odontohypophosphatasia (Odonto-HPP) is not fully recapitulated in murine models with a modest alkaline phosphatase deficiency,52 and mutant mice lack the characteristic deciduous tooth loss seen in patients.52 In contrast, HPP sheep have early dentition and deciduous tooth loss like HPP patients.53 In addition, the growth, maturation and aging of sheep long bones (up to 5-8 yr old) supports the use of sheep as a model for human age-related degeneration in the context of cancellous bone healing.54,55

From a comparative genomic standpoint, the genomes of mice and rats have evolved at nearly twice the nucleotide substitution rate as those of humans and most companion animals and livestock species.56 Similarly, accelerated rates of chromosome evolution in the murid rodent lineage have also substantially reorganized their gene order and regulatory architecture relative to most placental mammals.57 These radical genomic changes in the mouse have led to species-specific differences in anatomy, physiology, metabolic rates, lifespan, and size.43 It is also possible that an expanded view of how to better exploit rodent models, for example, by changing the housing environment, diet, and/or microbiome may provide better insight into human physiology. Even in scenarios, where rodent models do manifest the key phenotypic aspects of the human disease being modeled, they frequently pose preclinical limitations for applications, such as imaging, drug development, and in many cases surgical intervention.58 The bottom line is that while mice have been fundamental for elucidating gene pathways and improving our understanding of monogenic disorders in a mammalian setting, like all animal models, mice also have limitations with regard to recapitulating human disease phenotypes and have an even less robust history of predicting drug treatment efficacy.58,59 In such in vivo research, there remains a tendency to default to models that are expedient vs models that best represent the disease of interest. It is time to rethink this approach and strive to engineer animal models that best recapitulate the human disease of interest.

Rare bone diseases

Rare bone diseases are a diverse group of primarily genetic disorders that predominantly impact the skeleton, cartilage, and associated connective tissue.60 They encompass hundreds of clinical entities, from skeletal dysplasias to conditions of aberrant bone mineralization, or low bone mass and increased bone fragility, or abnormally high bone mass. Although uncommon by definition—individual disorders affect <5 in 10,000 individuals—they account for substantial morbidity worldwide (e.g., growth deficits and deformities, difficult mobility, fragility fractures, and pain) resulting in lifelong disability and markedly diminished quality of life.61,62 As such, rare bone diseases offer a unique opportunity to link specific molecular defects to skeletal phenotypes: conditions that arise from penetrant defects in specific molecular pathways can illuminate fundamental mechanisms of skeletal biology and mineral homeostasis, thereby accelerating the development of targeted therapies for both rare and common musculoskeletal disorders.63

In the context of rare bone diseases, selecting an optimal animal in which to model the appropriate human pathology is crucial. Large-animal models, particularly sheep (Ovis aries), offer specific structural advantages for translation. Sheep are domesticated, quadrupedal ruminants long associated with human civilization. Sheep and humans share relevant features in relation to their nervous system organization, in particular brain and spinal cord structure and function.46 Sheep also represent an excellent musculoskeletal model: they are larger and longer-lived (8-10 yr) than mice and have articular and growth plate cartilage whose size and architecture more closely approximate human bone and cartilage.64–66 In addition, sheep bone remodeling67,68 has well-developed Haversian systems like humans, and both sheep and humans have diphyodont dentition.53,69,70 Accordingly, we and others have prioritized livestock species, sheep in particular, for producing transgenic and gene-edited models of human disease (Table 1). Once established, bone (or other) disease-specific mutations in large animal models can clarify muscle/bone/tendon/ligament interactions, reveal new regulators of bone remodeling, and open opportunities for improved, mechanism-based therapies and diagnostics. Collectively, there is growing recognition that large-animal IGA models may more accurately recapitulate human skeletal phenotypes compared to mice in many contexts.

Why sheep?

Sheep are excellent models for investigating the musculoskeletal system, as mechanical load distribution across their joints during load-bearing activities closely mimics that of humans.71,72 Sheep also offer additional experimental advantages, such as allowing serial sampling from blood and synovial fluid, the utility to develop and test surgical techniques, and to evaluate spinal implants and orthopedic hardware comparable to those used clinically.71 Bone healing rates in sheep resemble those in humans,73–75 further supporting their value in orthopedic studies as well as for the investigation of osteoporosis.76 Beyond the skeleton, sheep exhibit abundant physiological and pathological characteristics that render them effective models for renal disorders77 and are uniquely suited to investigate the bone–kidney axis, owing to their similarities to humans in terms of size, multipapillary kidneys, and closely matched hemodynamic and coagulation profiles.78

Gene editing sheep

Recent advances in livestock IGAs have substantially broadened their utility, enabling new studies into disease mechanisms and therapeutic interventions that were previously not possible.3,8,19 Thus, we propose that strategically chosen domestic livestock species for modeling human bone disease will complement murine-driven research and will yield additional mechanistic insights relevant to musculoskeletal disease in both veterinary and human medicine. Selecting an appropriate animal model requires careful consideration of the research objectives, ethical standards, translational potential, and perhaps most importantly, the underlying experimental question.

The workflow for the development of engineered livestock species as biomedical models (Figure 1) is straightforward and has been applied across a multitude of livestock species (Table 1). The production of IGAs is, of course, based on modifying livestock genomes at early embryonic stages. There are 3 key stages: (1) in vitro validation of the IGA in target species cell lines; (2) production of IGA embryos via microinjection or electroporation of CRISPR/Cas reagents into the isolated zygotes or via somatic cell nuclear transfer of IGA cell lines; and (3) in vitro culture and subsequent transfer of embryos to recipients to obtain viable offspring. In parallel, a subset of embryos can be cultured in vitro and used for targeted next generation sequencing to confirm IGA efficiency. in vitro produced sheep embryos can either be implanted on embryonic day 2 into the oviduct or on day 6 into the uteri of surrogate ewes or cryopreserved for storage and later implantation. Founder lambs are immediately genotyped and appropriate assessments made to confirm the phenotype. Founder animals can be used for traditional breeding at approximately 10 mo of age to propagate the colony/herd using surgical embryo recovery after ovarian synchronization and multiple ovulation embryo transfer (MOET) into recipient ewes.79 Live progeny are used for continued confirmation and longitudinal evaluation of the phenotype of the modeled human disease.79

Workflow for generation of IGA sheep. (1) CRISPR/Cas reagents are first validated in vitro. Primary sheep fibroblasts are transfected or electroporated with CRISPR/Cas reagents, gene edited fibroblasts are expanded, and target loci are analyzed by next generation sequencing (NGS) to confirm Cas activity. (2) Validated reagents are then utilized to edit fertilized sheep embryos (zygotes). Donor ewes are super-ovulated, zygotes are collected, and CRISPR/Cas reagents are delivered by microinjection followed by in vitro culture incubation. After 2 or 6 d of incubation, CRISPR/Cas-edited embryos are either submitted for NGS to determine editing efficacy or transferred to estrus-synchronized recipient ewes. (3) Approximately 145 d after embryo production, lambs are born, genotyped by NGS, and evaluated for the targeted phenotype.

Sheep physiology in bone and mineral metabolism

For decades, pregnant sheep have been the model of choice for studying maternal–fetal interactions.80 Fundamental aspects of PTH-related protein-mediated placental calcium regulation were originally discerned with the use of chronically-cannulated twin sheep fetuses.81,82 Pregnant sheep have proven particularly useful for planning surgeries and for interventions that involve both the mother’s and fetal vascular systems,83 as well as in studies of compromised pregnancy.84 The sheep placenta is widely accepted as an appropriate model of the human placenta due to its many parallel, if not overlapping, features in vascular development, nutrient exchange mechanisms, and the feasibility of repeated blood collection from the mother, placenta, and fetus.46,83,85,86 Considerable evidence in this particular domain of the maternal–fetal interface demonstrates why livestock, particularly sheep, serve as uniquely powerful biomedical models for pregnancy and cell biology.87

Considering that the environments in which the mouse and human immune systems have evolved were (and are) quite different, it is conceivable that mouse models for immune conditions are particularly prone to diverge from humans. Moreover, given the strong intersection between the immune system and bone,88 skeletal disease is even more likely to manifest differently across species. Indeed, a core challenge in modeling human systemic inflammation in mice is the mismatch in injury and subsequent repair kinetics between species.39 Investigator decisions about sampling timepoints, analytical endpoints, and drug dosing often assume similar immune dynamics in mice and humans, despite substantial evidence showing not only mouse–human differences, but also strain-to-strain variability across mouse lines.39 Notably, bone marrow responses and immune activation differ meaningfully, suggesting that large animal models better reflect human immune system responses.39,40

As mentioned earlier, sheep provide practical advantages for surgical intervention studies (Figure 2). In the context of the immune system, lymphatic cannulation and serial collection of lymphoid cells are feasible in sheep but not in small rodents.89 Naturally occurring viral infections in sheep (e.g., endogenous retroviruses) provide additional opportunities to evaluate immune tolerance.90 Moreover, several chemokines produced by immune cells and associated with inflammatory activation differ across species. For example, the interleukin-8 (IL8) gene—also activated during bone resorption in humans91—is present in sheep and humans but absent in mice, underscoring an important translational gap in murine inflammation models.89

Utility of sheep for biomedical research

Livestock species (pigs, goats, and sheep) have been used extensively in biomedical research, in large part due to their physiology and anatomy being closely similar with humans92 (Figure 2). Sheep (and goat) models have served as mainstays in the orthopedic implant and osteoporosis research setting for decades.64,67,93–95 Their size enables clinically relevant imaging, implants and/or device testing (e.g., plates, screws, external fixators, and pacemakers), and surgical procedure development. These species support longitudinal sample collection—sometimes over years—mirroring patient follow-up. In addition, naturally occurring mutations in domestic livestock species have been widely studied as disease models, including Gaucher’s disease,96 hemophilia,97 cystic fibrosis,98 atherosclerosis,99 and connective-tissue disorders including an Ehlers-Danlos-like condition.100 Accordingly, the availability and generation of large-animal models that better phenocopy human diseases are highly valuable. In the context of bone disease, there are many clear parallels in bone cell function between livestock species and humans.

In addition to the well-accepted surgical utility of livestock species (particularly sheep for skeletal applications), multiple state-of-the-art molecular and functional assessment modalities are available in sheep (Figure 2). Indeed, clinical orthopedic examinations are a routine mode of assessment of the joints of these animals as is joint arthroscopy (Figure 2B and C). Given the docile nature of sheep, training to facilitate motion capture and the assessment of gait is also routine (Figure 2A). Furthermore, in the cardiovascular disease setting, exercised swine (trained to run on a treadmill) have been used for many years.101,102 The animals undergo a treadmill regimen (5 day/wk) in which speed and duration are progressively increased until the exercise training duration plateaus at week 12; training continues for 14 or 16 wk. By week 12, animals run at 6.0 mph for 5-8 min (speed) and at 4.0-4.5 mph for 60 min (endurance). Exercise-training regimen efficacy is evaluated by comparing heart-to-body weight ratios and skeletal muscle oxidative enzyme activity.103 These studies have discovered exercise-mediated signaling pathways that are directly relevant to humans.

With the continuing annotation of livestock species genomes and the continued use of sheep in biomedical research, modern molecular analyses are now commonplace (Figure 2D). In addition to conventional bone histology, the availability of multispecies antibodies has made immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence routine for domestic livestock (Figure 2Da and Dd). Establishment and annotation of the ovine reference genome104 have enabled extensive investigation into numerous domesticated breeds and wild species.105 Genome-wide association studies have now identified and characterized sequence variation including phenotype-associated functional variants.106,107 These resources provide key information for finding ovine homologs of human genes and enable subsequent design of gene targeting strategies for gene editing and/or other IGAs. These same resources enable state-of-the-art transcriptomic approaches to assess transcriptional responses due to induced genomic alterations. These recently reviewed methodologies108 are widely applied in agriculture and biomedical models of disease, and the ongoing technological and bioinformatics innovations continue to expand their utility.109–112

Sheep are also a leading large-animal model for FDA-oriented studies; virtually all bone-active bisphosphonates (and other agents) have been rigorously interrogated using sheep.67,68,94,113 Sheep provide accepted, reproducible platforms for testing therapies for osteoporosis,114–116 as well as osteonecrosis of the jaw.117 Histologic and histomorphometric analyses of sheep bone,94,118,119 together with clinical imaging and mechanical testing,120 validate sheep as a reliable, translatable model of the diseased human skeleton. In addition to their animal handling advantages, sheep bone size and anatomy facilitate high-quality imaging, with CT and DXA in routine use121–123 (Figure 2). Sheep have also been instrumental in establishing baseline parameters for DXA-based assessments of bone density and quality.124

Limitations of sheep (and large animal models)

In some circumstances, sheep may be readily available and cost-effective to purchase, but incur frequent, escalating maintenance costs, especially in long term aging studies typical of osteoporosis and other bone disorders64 (Table 3). Major drawbacks to adopting domestic livestock animal models for rare bone disease research include the need for larger, specialized housing and surgical facilities, longer reproductive cycles, and higher husbandry costs. Dedicated barns require construction, maintenance, inspection, and operating suites suitable for sterile surgery; highly skilled surgical teams and veterinary support before, during, and after any research manipulations further increase experimental costs19 (Table 4). Currently, the upfront costs to generate gene-edited mice or livestock are comparable (at least at our institution: ~$10,000 per mouse line vs ~$5,000 per large animal), but ongoing costs diverge sharply. For example, per diem costs for pasture-housed sheep can be as low as $1/day, whereas dry-lot or intensive housing substantially increases feed and labor expenses.

Comparison between murine and sheep models.

An important limitation of sheep models is the challenge of generating sufficient animal numbers for appropriate statistical comparisons, compounded by the fact that MOET is limited to 2 survival surgeries per animal. However, recent advances in reproductive technologies permit efficient use of ewes for mutant colony/herd expansion via in vitro embryo production (IVEP).79 In vitro embryo production utilizes laparoscopic oocyte pick-up (LOPU) from super-stimulated ovaries of mutant ewes followed by in vitro fertilization, and subsequent embryo transfer into recipient ewes. In sheep (and goats), the LOPU-IVEP platform permits production of more offspring from mutant females, as the procedure is minimally invasive and can be repeated more times frequently in the same founder animals compared with conventional surgical embryo recovery.79 Personnel demand also scales: managing a herd of >50 head of sheep typically demands more hands-on time than a colony of >500 mice. Beyond logistics, biochemical and molecular resources are less available (though availability is expanding; Figure 2) in sheep, and validated antibodies for routine immunohistochemistry are often less accessible and can be more costly. Collectively, these considerations and challenges are magnified in chronic, progressive bone studies, precisely where sheep models are most appropriate given their long lifespan, skeletal similarities, size, behavior, and tractability. In the current US funding climate, careful budgeting and thorough planning are essential for any disease model, be it murine or large animal. The global use of domestic animals in biomedical research continues to increase, notably for medical device testing and xenotransplantation studies.125 To enhance translational validity and scientific rigor, studies must reflect a deeper understanding of large-animal physiology and employ animal models that are physiologically relevant and appropriate—not merely convenient. High standards of animal welfare are imperative to minimize suffering and provide exemplary care. Procurement, maintenance, housing, and use of large animals in biomedical research must comply with all federal, state, and local laws and regulations, as well as institutional policies. In this regard, investigators with access to large animal facilities, such as the National Swine Resource and Research Center at which swine models and related resources are produced with NIH support for distribution to the broader scientific research community.126 These and other researchers in colleges of veterinary medicine and agricultural universities across the globe are strategically positioned to spearhead the continued and much-needed integration of IGA large-animal models into modern biomedical research.

Conclusions and the future

Despite ongoing and timely efforts to develop alternative, non-animal biomedical research methods, animal models—and the infrastructure required to support their welfare—remain essential for modeling human disease etiology, mechanisms, and therapeutic development/efficacy. As discussed here, sheep and other large-animal models currently provide the only comprehensive, whole-organism view of disease progression in bone (and beyond). Our own studies using these models have provided insight into the natural history of rare bone diseases, complex multi-system interactions, long-term outcomes, and multifactorial responses to treatments—all crucial for understanding complex disease conditions and developing effective therapies.8,72,127,128

The growing accessibility of genetically engineered large-animal models is reinvigorating rare bone disease research (and biomedical research broadly) by expanding experimental capabilities, enabling provocative new questions, and attracting new research funding along the way. Modern advances in livestock IGAs, coupled with well-annotated and curated genomes, are redefining how rare bone disease phenotypes are generated, studied, and evaluated. In effect, musculoskeletal research is entering a new era—one in which genetically engineered sheep (as well as goats and pigs) stand alongside—not instead of—mice as essential partners in discovery and therapeutic development. Together, these and other animal models are reshaping our ability to unravel the mechanisms of human bone disease and to design therapies with the very real potential to transform patient care.
