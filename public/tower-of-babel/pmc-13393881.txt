Successful treatment with plasmapheresis of severe hypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis complicated by fat overload syndrome caused by lipid injectable emulsion: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Dou-dou Li, Kai Liu, Ba yi Liu, Hua min Wang, Bing Lin, Hao Yang, Bao-ping Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis (HTG-AP) and fat overload syndrome caused by lipid injectable emulsion (ILE) are rare in clinical practice. Although the treatment of HTG-AP with plasmapheresis has previously been reported, only a few reports describe the application of plasmapheresis to treat HTG-AP and fat overload syndrome caused by ILE.Case presentationWe report a case diagnosed with severe HTG-AP and fat overload syndrome, based on laboratory findings and abdominal computed tomography, caused by intravenous infusion of ILE for about 3 weeks without monitoring of serum triglyceride (TG) levels in a 78-year-old Chinese female patient with a history of esophageal cancer. Conventional treatments were attempted; however, the patient progressively developed worsening abdominal pain and notably elevated inflammatory biomarkers, including C-reactive protein and procalcitonin levels. Plasmapheresis treatment was performed immediately to lower serum TG levels and prevent the complication of acute pancreatitis (AP) due to hypertriglyceridemia. After two consecutive plasmapheresis sessions, the patient’s TG and total cholesterol levels remarkably decreased to 13.57 and 8.62 mmol/L, respectively. She also showed considerable improvement in her abdominal symptoms.ConclusionHTG-AP and fat overload syndrome can be associated with intravenous infusion of ILE. Monitoring serum TG levels while utilizing ILE is necessary. Plasmapheresis should be considered an effective treatment measure for severe HTG-AP or fat overload syndrome caused by ILE, because it rapidly lowers serum TG levels and may prevent to be useful in ameliorating the course of acute pancreatitis.

Background

Fat overload syndrome results from elevated triglyceride (TG) levels that occur when intravenous lipid injectable emulsion (ILE) rates exceed the rate of free fatty acid uptake, hydrolysis and clearance [1]. It is widely advised that the daily intravenous administration of ILE should not surpass 2.5 g/kg. In conditions of critical illness or physiological stress, it is prudent, for safety considerations, to regulate the maximum dosage to ≤ 1.0 g/kg/day in order to prevent metabolic overload. Rapid deterioration of unexplained clinical symptoms such as sudden high fever, palpitations, tachypnea, nausea, vomiting, anorexia and loss of appetite in a patient receiving parenteral nutrition (PN) support containing ILE should be considered as suggestive of fat overload syndrome [1]. Relevant tests such as measurement of blood lipid levels, liver and kidney functions, routine blood testing, and electrolyte levels should be performed immediately for rapid assessment and diagnosis. The signs and symptoms of fat overload syndrome resolve once intravenous ILE is stopped. Hypertriglyceridemia acute pancreatitis (HTG-AP) is associated with fat overload syndrome and is rare in patients receiving PN therapy, with very few reports in the literature [2]. However, when HTG-AP does occur, it is usually severe [3]. Plasmapheresis may be considered an effective treatment measure when conventional conservative treatment does not successfully treat severe HTG-AP and fat overload syndrome. Plasmapheresis can rapidly reduce serum TG levels to prevent progression of fat overload syndrome or HTG-AP and life-threatening cardiovascular and central nervous system complications, including systemic inflammatory response syndrome and extensive organ damage [4, 5]. Here, we report a rare case of severe HTG-AP and fat overload syndrome in a patient with esophageal cancer who had received PN treatment with a 1440 mL fat emulsion, amino acids (17) and glucose (11%) injection containing 255 mL of intralipid fat emulsion daily for about 3 weeks. In this case, although conventional treatment for reduction of serum TG levels was not effective, the patient’s serum TG levels and clinical symptoms improved markedly after two rounds of plasmapheresis treatment. This case report highlights the importance of early recognition and treatment of HTG-AP and fat overload syndrome associated with ILE. In addition, given the potential for deterioration of HTG-AP, plasmapheresis should be considered in cases of severe HTG-AP where conventional treatment is unsuccessful.

Case presentation

A 78-year-old Chinese female patient with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma 4 years after radical resection was admitted to the hospital with dysphagia and tiredness for 2 weeks. Enhanced computed tomography (CT) had revealed multiple enlarged mediastinal and retroperitoneal lymph nodes, and bone metastases to the left ilium and sacrum 3 years ago. The patient received palliative intensity-modulated radiotherapy. For various reasons, the patient had not received the prescriptive treatment in accordance with the physician’s instructions. The patient had a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus for 6 years and received subcutaneous insulin injections to control blood glucose. A history of hypertension, coronary heart disease, hyperlipidemia, or other diseases including pancreatitis was denied by the patient. On clinical examination, her blood pressure, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation were normal, without any other pathological signs. The hematological, biochemical, blood lipid level, and inflammatory markers were normal. Due to dysphagia, she was unable to eat orally, she initially refused enteral nutrition (EN) and subsequently started PN support until enteral access for tube feedings was able to be established. The patient receives approximately 30% of total energy from oral intake, and the remaining 70% can be supplied by PN, requiring approximately 33–42 g of amino acids, 90–110 g of dextrose, and 40–60 g of lipid emulsion. A 1440-mL fat emulsion (20% intralipid fat emulsion), amino acids (17) and glucose (11%) injection was administered daily for 22 days (Fresenius Kabi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China). On day 23 of hospitalization, the patient developed unexplained abdominal pain, which was persistent and unbearable, accompanied by nausea, vomiting, obvious sweating, palpitation, pale complexion, and tiredness. Physical examination showed abdominal tenderness and rebound pain; her pulse rate was 124/min, respiratory rate 23/min, and blood pressure 108/78 mm Hg. Yellowing of skin or sclera was also observed, while assessment of the Murphy sign and nervous system showed no abnormalities. Routine blood tests revealed white blood cell (WBC) count of 2.36 × 109/L, hemoglobin of 86.4 g/L, and platelets (PLT) of 83 × 109/L. Evaluation of inflammatory markers showed a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 82.12 mg/L, procalcitonin level of 0.13 ng/mL, and interleukin 6 level of 205.50 pg/mL. Laboratory biochemistry investigations showed a serum total bilirubin concentration of 133.56 μmol/L, serum direct bilirubin concentration of 109.67 μmol/L, aspartate aminotransferase concentration of 152.16 U/L, alanine aminotransferase concentration of 151.43 U/L, serum triglyceride concentration of 31.10 mmol/L, serum total cholesterol concentration of 14.47 mmol/L, β-hydroxybutyrate concentration of 2.20 mmol/L, serum amylase level of 232.0 U/L, and serum lipase concentration of 290.1 U/L. The glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level was 6.7%. Myocardial enzyme, troponin, and B-type natriuretic peptide results were all within the normal range. Her laboratory results are listed in Table 2. Laboratory results and abdominal CT indicated severe HTG-AP and fat overload syndrome associated with intravenous ILE. Fat emulsion, amino acids (17) and glucose (11%) injection was immediately stopped, and the patient was administered conventional standard treatment with infusions of somatostatin, pantoprazole (40 mg every 8 h), gabexate mesilate (0.5 g, daily), glutathione (1.8 g daily), magnesium isoglycyrrhizinate (150 mg daily), and oral administration of fenofibrate, rosuvastatin, and ezetimibe tablets. She was also treated with fasting, aggressive intravenous hydration, subcutaneous injection of recombinant human granulocytic stimulating factor, intravenous infusion of 500 mL fresh-frozen plasma, insulin for unstable blood glucose level, and analgesics. The patient progressively developed deteriorating abdominal pain accompanied by polypnea and vomiting over the next day. Repeat serum TG and total cholesterol levels showed a slight drop, but CRP and procalcitonin levels were significantly elevated. She was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) and administered plasmapheresis treatment to reduce blood lipids on the same day. After the two consecutive plasmapheresis sessions (Fig. 2), the patient’s TG and total cholesterol levels decreased to 13.57 and 8.62 mmol/L, respectively. She recovered gradually from the plasmapheresis treatment and showed marked improvement in her abdominal symptoms. Meanwhile, her blood routine tests, inflammatory markers, coagulation function test, and liver and kidney function test also improved after the two plasmapheresis sessions. She was transferred from the ICU on day 25 in the hospital. For EN support, a nasojejunal feeding tube was inserted for post-pyloric feeding with a short peptide nutrient solution, 1000 mL daily. In the following days, her clinical symptoms improved further without apparent abdominal distension and pain. Repeat abdominal CT showed that exudation around the pancreatic head was significantly improved relative to the first test on the 28th day after admission. On the 39th day after admission, she was discharged from the hospital with a nasogastric tube in place for enteral nutrition to supplement energy. Her serum TG levels were 5.14 mmol/L and liver enzymes were slightly elevated; her kidney function parameters were within the normal range, as were her blood routine examination, blood biochemistry, and coagulation function test results (Fig. 3). Discussion

PN is mainly used when using oral or EN is not possible, especially among patients with advanced tumors. The fat emulsion, amino acids (17) and glucose (11%) injection can provide about 1000 kcal in total and improve the nutritional status and quality of life of patients with malnutrition, such as severe brain trauma, gastrointestinal tumor surgery, and chronic malnutrition who are unable to tolerate EN [2]. Common adverse reactions associated with PN include fever, shivering, nausea, and vomiting, and, less commonly, rare phlebitis, Wernicke encephalopathy, erectile dysfunction, acute liver and kidney injury, anaphylaxis, vein thrombosis, shortness of breath, fat embolism, and cardiac arrest [6–8].

Here, we report a rare case of HTG-AP and fat overload syndrome contributed by intravenous infusion of ILE. Although initial blood investigations revealed normal blood lipids, coagulation function, and biochemical parameters on hospitalization day one, the patient suffered from abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting after 3 weeks of intravenous infusion of lipid emulsion amino acid and dextrose solution, implying an obvious correlation between the time of administration of ILE and development of the adverse event. Although the drug package inserts do not mention the adverse reaction of pancreatitis, they clearly indicate that intravenous infusion of ILE can lead to HTG. Higher levels of TG can trigger the release of reactive oxygen species, inflammatory mediators, and pancreatic digestive enzymes within the pancreatic cells, leading to pancreatic inflammation. HTG further induces HTG-AP, which is positively associated with the risk of elevated TG levels [9, 10]. However, our patient had no previous episodes of pancreatitis, elevated lipase, or known hyperlipidemia, and we also ruled out HTG-AP and fat overload syndrome caused by other diseases and medications used at the time of hospitalization. Furthermore, HTG-AP or fat overload syndrome caused by infusion of ILE has been previously reported in the literature [2, 11, 12]. Therefore, we considered that the present case of HTG-AP and fat overload syndrome was exacerbated by intravenous ILE without weekly monitoring of triglycerides.

HTG-AP caused by infusion of ILE is rare clinically. A literature search of the PubMed and Web of science database revealed only four cases, three of which were in adults with acute ILE overdose [11–13], and the other case was a female patient diagnosed with acute lymphoblastic leukemia who had received long-term PN support for 78 days without monitoring of serum TG levels [2]. One of the cases was treated with double-filtration plasmapheresis [2]. All cases exhibited good recovery and a significant decrease in the TG level after treatment [2, 11–13]. In our case, a 1440-mL ILE, amino acids, and dextrose were administered daily for 23 days and ILE was prescribed at 1.0 g/kg/day, which ran at a total pump rate of 100 mL/h and did not exceed the recommended daily dose and infusion rate. Compared with the previous case report, although our patient had higher serum TG levels, her symptoms improved significantly after two sessions of plasma exchange.

The clinical features of patients with HTG-AP are generally not different from those of patients with AP due to other causes, and there is some evidence that HTG-AP is associated with a higher severity or a higher complication rate [14]. The mechanism underlying HTG-AP is postulated to involve the hydrolysis of TG by pancreatic lipase and the release of free fatty acids that induce free radical damage and inflammatory changes; however, the exact pathophysiology remains unclear [14]. Furthermore, higher free fatty acid concentrations can induce respiratory failure, kidney injury, and heart failure in patients with AP, and increase HTG-AP severity and the incidence rates of organ failure in patients [15, 16]. Lipase inhibitors may provide a new avenue for the treatment of HTG-AP by reducing the formation of free fatty acids [15].

HTG-AP and fat overload syndrome associated with infusion of ILE can be resolved by discontinuation of the infusion and symptomatic treatment. The current treatment protocols for HTG-AP include etiological treatment, conventional treatment, addressing complications, surgery, psychological therapy and rehabilitation [17]. Serum TG levels above 11.3 mmol/L are usually considered necessary to ascribe causation for AP, and with increased serum TG levels, the risk of multiple organ dysfunction in severe HTG-AP patients rises [14]. Hence, rapid reduction of serum TG levels below 5.65 mmol/L as the core treatment goal is recommended for HTG-AP and fat overload syndrome in order to eliminate the risk of systemic inflammatory response syndrome and prevent pancreatitis-related complications, especially multiple organ dysfunction [18, 19].

In most patients with HTG-AP or fat overload syndrome, it is possible to achieve a significant drop in serum TG level and good recovery after conventional treatment. However, for patients with severe refractory HTG-AP, plasmapheresis treatment should be considered as an effective therapeutic method when the decrease in serum TG level within 24 or 48 h is not significant and is accompanied by worsening clinical symptoms [20]. Moreover, studies have reported that, relative to conventional treatment, plasmapheresis can reduce serum TG levels more significantly and relieve the inflammatory response and abdominal pain symptoms to shorten hospital stay and improve prognosis [21]. Nakagawa et al. [13] and Wu et al. [2], respectively, reported a patient with acute lymphoblastic leukemia who developed severe HTG-AP. Total plasmapheresis and double-filtration plasmapheresis were performed, respectively, in the patients, and the serum triglyceride and total cholesterol levels of both patients decreased significantly after plasmapheresis [2, 13]. However, coincidentally, both patients had acute lymphocytic leukemia, so it is reasonable to speculate that acute lymphocytic leukemia may have been the cause of HTG-AP in both patients.

EN is the preferred route for patients with mild-to-moderate HTG-AP who can tolerate oral feeding, and its initiation as soon as possible is recommended [22]. The guideline recommends that the transition from hypocaloric feeding to standardized nutritional support enteral feeding can shorten hospital stay, maintain the integrity of the intestinal mucosa, and improve the prognosis of patients with severe pancreatitis. However, patients who are unable to tolerate EN or those with severe HTG-AP may require PN treatment [23, 24]. Patients with HTG-AP who have abdominal pain relief after 72 h of onset and serum TG level ≤ 500 mg/dL (5.65 mmol/L) can be cautiously treated with short- and medium-chain ILE, but serum TG levels should be monitored during intravenous administration [19]. Although the core treatment goal for HTG-AP is to rapidly eliminate elevated TGs, relevant studies have reported that omega-3 fatty acid (also called ω-3 fatty acids or n-3 fatty acids) emulsion can regulate fatty acid metabolism [25]. Intravenous administration of ILE containing omega-3 fatty acids may also ameliorate clinical symptoms, intraperitoneal pressure, and incidence of organ failure in patients with severe AP, while reducing ICU stays and enabling more rapid recovery [25]. Omega-3 fatty acid-containing lipid emulsion as an intravenous nutrition supplementation may, in the future, replace conventional ILE in patients who cannot tolerate EN therapy. It would be appropriate to note that such omega-3 fatty acid‑containing mixed lipid emulsions may also contain MCT oil, which may also assist with improved triglyceride clearance.

Conclusion

It is necessary to monitor serum TG levels at least once within 1 week in patients receiving PN therapy to prevent fat overload syndrome and progression to HTG-AP. Furthermore, HTG-AP may still occur, even when ILE intake is within manufacturer’s dosing recommendations. When nutritional therapy and drug therapy show little success in reducing serum TG levels in patients with severe HTG-AP or fat overload syndrome, TG can be physically removed from the body with timely plasmapheresis, enabling rapid relief from symptoms such as abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting.
