Abstract
. Current cybersecurity research increasingly acknowledges the human factor, yet remains fragmented, often treating user vulnerabilities as isolated and static traits. This paper introduces MORPHEUS, a holistic framework that operationalizes human-centric security as a dynamic and interconnected system. Grounded in the Cognition–Affect–Behavior (CAB) model and Attribution Theory, MORPHEUS consolidates 50 human factors influencing susceptibility to major cyberthreats, including phishing, malware, password management, and misconfigurations. Beyond factor identification, the framework systematically maps 295 documented interactions, revealing how cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and socio-organizational processes jointly shape security outcomes, and distills them into twelve recurring interaction mechanisms. MORPHEUS further links theory to practice through an inventory of 99 validated psychometric instruments, enabling empirical assessment and targeted intervention. We illustrate the framework’s applicability through concrete operational scenarios, spanning risk diagnosis, training, and interface design. Overall, MORPHEUS provides a rigorous yet actionable foundation for advancing human-centered cybersecurity research and practice.

1.
Introduction

Research increasingly demonstrates that cybersecurity is strongly shaped by human factors, with empirical findings indicating that almost 88% of breaches stem from human error
(
371
)
. Consequently, cybercriminals have refined their tactics to target individuals rather than focusing solely on technical vulnerabilities. Social engineering tactics, notably phishing and its variants (e.g., SMishing and spear-phishing), exploit psychological mechanisms such as trust, urgency, and fear to induce users to disclose sensitive information or execute malicious actions
(
27
)
. Besides social engineering, other human-related causes of security failure, such as a lack of good password management habits, bad system configurations, and accidental malware downloads, highlight the degree to which human choices influence cybersecurity posture. Despite technological advancements in security control measures, human factors remain one of the most persistent and exploitable weak points in defending against cyberthreats and an under-evaluated dimension of cybersecurity for individuals and organizations.

To identify and mitigate human-generated cyber risk, it is essential to develop a systematic process for identifying human-related aspects (e.g., cognitive, emotional) that underpin security breaches. The latter have been studied in the context of cognitive biases and emotional responses in cybersecurity
(
124
)
, as well as behavioral patterns and organizational situations that make such lapses more likely
(
21
;
429
;
257
;
341
)
. Other studies have attempted to classify human factors through frameworks such as the NIST Cybersecurity Framework
(
364
)
, analyses of insider threat and time-pressure variables, and models that synthesize these constructs
(
402
;
364
)
. These frameworks may be valuable, but they are narrow or excessively constraining, partitioning cognition, emotion, behavior, and social influences against each other instead of capturing the dynamic relationships that occur in real-world environments. In turn, these models provide an incomplete picture of the potential weaknesses and are insufficient for developing an overall plan to address human-based risks.

A further obstacle to effective mitigation is the lack of standardized methodologies for measuring and integrating human factors across various cyberthreats. The variability of modern attacks requires a unified paradigm that can categorize human weaknesses comprehensively and methodically. This kind of framework would serve as a platform for developing specific interventions and policies to minimize human-related cyber risks. The increasing complexity of digital ecosystems increases the pressing nature of this problem.

2.
Background and related work

2.1.
Frameworks of human factors in cybersecurity

Understanding human factors in cybersecurity helps address vulnerabilities arising from technical flaws and human errors and behaviors that may expose organizations to threats. Previous research in HCI and cybersecurity has proposed various frameworks to systematically analyze the impact of human factors on cybersecurity outcomes.

The
Drivers of Insider Threats Framework

(
147
)
categorizes the factors that lead to insider threats into themes such as technological awareness, leadership influence, and individual selfishness. Despite its structure, this framework offers too strict a compartmentalization of factors, failing to consider their exhaustive interactions. Additionally, it overlooks the indirect role of organizational culture in creating conditions that increase the likelihood of insider threats.

The
Time Pressure Cybersecurity Behavior Framework

(
81
)
also revolves around one factor that causes insecure behaviors, namely time pressure, and identifies scenarios, psychological mechanisms, and moderating circumstances, including task complexity, personal characteristics, and work environments. However, this solution does not account for other resource constraints, such as inadequate tools or expertise, which significantly impact security behaviors under pressure.

In contrast, the
NIST Cybersecurity Framework

(
364
)
integrates human factors into a broader framework by identifying twenty critical human behaviors, including password management and incident reporting. Although comprehensive, the framework makes several assumptions that human behaviors can be categorized conveniently, disregarding their more fluid and situation-specific occurrences. Such inflexibility does not allow the framework to adapt to behaviors, which include intentional deviations from standard practices.

Al-Darwish and Choe
(
11
)
presented an integrative information security framework explicitly addressing human factors alongside technical, organizational, and economic considerations. Their approach extends beyond traditional security methods, encompassing both direct human factors (e.g., errors, awareness, stress, negligence, training, and usability) and indirect factors (e.g., organizational culture, management support, communication, budgeting, and policy enforcement). The model focuses on human behavior as the primary factor in security breaches, but it overlooks important factors such as individual personality traits.

According to the
Cybersecurity Culture
(CSC) model
(
286
)
, which relies on the
Human Factor Diamond
framework, organizational and individual factors are divided into areas such as management, preparedness, and responsibility. Although the CSC model has an extensive scope, it places the most emphasis on formal arrangements (policies and leadership); therefore, it does not adequately account for human factors that could influence cybersecurity behavior, such as informal networks and emotional or personality-based factors.

Choong
(
79
)
designed a cognitive-behavioral model centered on the password management lifecycle (i.e., generation, maintenance, and authentication phases). Although a well-developed idea, this framework is based solely on password management, which reduces its transferability to other cybersecurity-related settings.

More recently, Khadka & Ullah
(
209
)
presented a comprehensive interdisciplinary review and propose a
Human-Centric Cybersecurity Framework
composed of four pillars: Psychological Resilience, which aims to strengthen individuals’ ability to deal with stress, fear, fatigue, and emotional manipulation; Adaptive Learning, which promotes dynamic and context-sensitive security education; Emotional Intelligence, which focus on enhancing users’ capacity to detect and interpret social engineering tactics; and Socio-Technical Integration, which aim is to ensure that organizational policies, technologies, and workflows are designed with human behavior in mind. Although comprehensive in scope, the framework does not provide a detailed analysis of how individual human factors influence cyberthreats, nor does it examine the interrelationships among these factors.

Despite the presence of several models and frameworks on human factors in cybersecurity, there is an evident lack of a holistic framework that provides a broader perspective on the human factors involved in the most common cyberthreats, that investigates the interaction among these factors, and that drives practitioners in measuring them. All of this is necessary to deliver actionable insights that can be used to construct a more robust cybersecurity defense.

2.2.
Existing Surveys or literature reviews on human factors in cybersecurity

One of the primary contributions of our study is a literature review aimed at identifying human factors in cybersecurity. Such an activity has been prompted by the fact that the available reviews have not been comprehensive regarding the integrated approach that we will be taking. For instance, Rahman et al.
(
333
)
investigated the human factors that significantly influence cybersecurity outcomes. The thorough examination of the concept of social influence is a notable feature of this work, as the authors emphasize the importance of users adopting secure practices in case people with similar habits support them. The study identifies a lack of established theoretical frameworks that could provide further insights into the nature of user behaviors and improve the prediction of such behaviors.

A comprehensive and systematic review, provided by Desolda et al., explores the interplay between human factors and phishing attacks
(
111
)
. The authors identified important issues affecting vulnerability to such attacks in groups, including a lack of security knowledge and training that keeps users uninformed about existing threats and how to protect themselves effectively. This work is supported by the thorough classification of essential factors, including the absence of security knowledge and overconfidence. However, the review’s primary focus on phishing leaves other types of cyberthreats unexplored.

In the overview of phishing training efficiency, Jampen et al.
(
187
)
identify various human and situational factors that contribute to phishing vulnerability. However, despite the thorough analysis offered by the authors, the findings on aspects such as age, gender, and user technical knowledge are somewhat contradictory in some cases, indicating that the issue requires further research to clarify these points.

A systematic review about the impact of HFs on malware and ransomware attacks has also been reported in
(
232
)
. According to this study, human traits play a significant role in the success of malware attacks. The actions of users, including browsing the internet, downloading files, and configuring security, directly influence malware vulnerability. Surprisingly, people with advanced computer knowledge proved to be more vulnerable, which is explained by their tendency to overestimate their knowledge and take more risks, or by being exposed to threats more frequently. It is also reported that peer-to-peer activity and high network usage can make the system more vulnerable, as well as specific behaviors, like failing to update the system or interacting with harmful websites.

Similarly, the systematic review by Nifakos and colleagues
(
295
)
examines the growing intersection between human behavior and cybersecurity in healthcare environments, acknowledging that digital transformation has significantly increased the sector’s vulnerability to cyberthreats. Phishing and other social engineering attacks have proven to be among the most prevalent and damaging types of cyberthreats in the healthcare sector.
One of the key findings from the review is the recognition that healthcare professionals often unwittingly serve as entry points for cybercriminals. This can partly be explained by the fact that the healthcare setting is a high-stress environment, and employees are often overburdened, distracted, or uninformed about the specific methods attackers can use to target them. The review notes that healthcare workers are particularly vulnerable to such schemes, not necessarily because they are careless, but because they often lack adequate knowledge about cybersecurity and its associated training.

The review by Allendoerfer
(
17
)
highlights various human factors that significantly impact the effectiveness of password security systems. Although human memory is strong, it is not resistant to lengthy and complex passwords, and a user must remember several distinct credentials to access various systems. It is also possible that cognitive constraints, including confusion among systems and difficulty remembering passwords that are not frequently used, may compromise password security.

Finally, Tornblad et al.
(
397
)
reviewed prior studies to identify 32 human factors predicting phishing susceptibility, grouped into seven categories: personality traits, demographics, education, cybersecurity experience and beliefs, platform experience, email behaviors, and work commitment style. The review found no single strong predictor, but combinations of traits, such as impulsivity, trust, low awareness, and habitual platform use, tend to increase vulnerability.

While the literature on human factors in cybersecurity is extensive and relevant to the community, no comprehensive work exists to identify and classify the most important human factors in various cyberthreats within a unified framework. Thus, with our study, we aim to fill this gap by identifying the most prevalent threats that exploit human errors and the human factors that may contribute to such errors. We will detail the information in the literature review in the following section.

3.
Methodology

To develop the MORPHEUS framework, we employed a multi-methodological approach tailored to address three research objectives: identifying the core human factors that play a crucial role in the most significant cyberthreats, mapping their complex interactions, and retrieving validated instruments to measure these factors.

To identify and define the foundational taxonomy of human factors and align them with existing theoretical models, we adopted a
Systematic Scoping Review
, ensuring qualitative depth and conceptual consistency (Section
3.1
). Subsequently, to address the scalability challenge of mapping the extensive network of potential interactions among these identified factors and retrieving specific psychometric tools, we implemented an
AI-assisted Systematic Screening

(
132
;
404
)
. This second phase integrated AI-driven semantic search with strict Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) validation to manage the high-dimensional search space without compromising rigor (Section
3.2
). The following subsections detail the procedural steps, inclusion criteria, and validation mechanisms for each strategy. All the datasets, protocols, and validation logs related to these activities are reported in the Additional Material.

3.1.
Identifying key human factors in cybersecurity: a systematic scoping review

A
systematic scoping review
has been carried out to answer the following research question:
“What human factors play a critical role in cyber-attacks?”
. This review focuses on the most significant attacks in which human behavior and decision-making are considered particularly impactful. These are
phishing, SMishing, spear phishing, malware download, password management, and misconfiguration

(
408
;
85
)

The scoping review approach has been selected because it allows for mapping the breadth of concepts across multidisciplinary fields, balancing methodological rigor with the need to cover a fragmented landscape
(

(

. Indeed, our approach is justified by the strategic choice to expand on some existing systematic reviews on phishing and its variants

3.1.1.
Data source and search strategy

We relied on
Google Scholar

Moreover, its choice was motivated by its ability to index publications across journals, conferences, workshops, and technical venues. This choice aligns with the goals of a scoping review to capture diverse literature and complements prior systematic reviews

The search was conducted between March and July 2025. No lower bound was imposed on the publication year, as our goal was to capture all potentially relevant studies that connect human factors to the selected cyberthreats.

The searches and terms used in the early searches were based on the terminology and citations from previous reviews. These existing reviews were more than background material; they also formed a starting point to determine proper keywords, citation trails, and under-researched subtopics. 3.1.2.
Inclusion Criteria

The selection procedure to include an article in our review was based on these inclusion criteria:

IN1

The publication must discuss the impact of at least one human factor, not in a generic sense, but related to one or more cyberthreats.

IN2

The publication must be peer-reviewed (we exclude unpublished articles, theses, etc.).

IN3

The publication must be a full paper (not a short paper, demo, poster, or workshop paper) published in top venues, specifically journals ranked at least Q3 in Scimago, or papers published in conferences ranked as at least class C in the Core Conference Portal.

IN4

The publication must be written in English.

To optimize the scoping process and avoid redundancy, as this technique recommends, we excluded papers that were already comprehensively analyzed in existing reviews

3.1.4.
Methodological considerations and limitations

The scoping review method supported the findings of relevant studies; however, some limitations should be considered.
Google Scholar
may have resulted in overlooking some relevant studies indexed in other databases or published in more specialized venues (although this is quite rare, it can still occur). Moreover, although our search string explicitly included the terms "human factors," "psychological traits," and "human aspects," we acknowledge that relevant studies may refer to individual-level constructs using alternative terminology (e.g., "individual differences," "user characteristics"). To mitigate these risks, backward and forward snowballing were systematically applied to every included paper, ensuring the inclusion of studies whose terminology differed from our initial search terms.

Additionally, focusing on recent literature and peer-reviewed sources, although they guarantee academic rigor, may have led to the exclusion of useful gray literature. Lastly, some variation in terminology and emphasis across papers was inevitable.

3.2.
AI-assisted Systematic Screening

While the identification of the 50 human factors (Section
3.1
) relied on a manual review, the subsequent tasks — mapping pairwise interactions among factors and identifying measurement instruments — presented prohibitive scalability challenges. We estimate that manually executing these tasks would have required more than 2,500 researcher-hours. Specifically:

•

For
interactions
, the challenge was
combinatorial volume
: managing over 2,500 interaction pairs would have required screening an estimated pool of 50,000+ titles/abstracts to determine relevance (
\approx
1,600 hours at 2 min/paper).

•

For
measurement instruments
, the challenge was
semantic depth
: distinguishing validated scales from theoretical discussions requires a deep methodological analysis. Processing just the top
\sim
55 relevant results for each of the 50 factors would have entailed examining
\approx
2,700 full texts (
\approx
900 hours at 20 min/paper).

To address these constraints in a manageable timeline while shifting human effort towards high-value validation, we employed an
AI-assisted Systematic Screening

(
132
;
404
)
. This approach integrates the semantic retrieval capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) with a strict Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) validation mechanism
(
239
;
363
)
.

We selected
Perplexity.ai
and
OpenAI Deep Research
as retrieval engines. This choice was driven by their capacity to minimize hallucinations compared to standard LLMs and their proven efficacy in complex scientific domains (e.g., health sciences and biology), where they have demonstrated superior accuracy in citation retrieval compared to keyword-based tools, while still needing heavy human supervision
(
241
;
399
)
. The search was conducted between March and July 2025.

The protocol follows a standardized workflow that comprises AI-assisted retrieval, a rigorous three-step validation process, and a snowballing phase, all of which are documented via a comprehensive audit trail.

3.2.1.
Phase 1: AI-assisted Retrieval

Before starting the search, we conducted a
pilot phase
to refine prompt engineering. Initially, generic prompts yielded broad but shallow results; we iteratively refined the structure to enforce
evidence-based constraints
, requiring the model to extract “direct quotes” and “methodological context” alongside the citation. This constraint significantly reduced the retrieval of generic associations.

The final retrieval strategy was adapted to the specific topology of each research question:

•

Mapping pairwise interactions among factors (Combinatorial Discovery):
To cover the dense search space of over 2,500 potential combinations, we adopted a
decomposed redundancy strategy
. We designed six distinct prompts—one for each of the human factor dimensions.

•

Identifying measurement instruments for the human factors (Targeted Retrieval):
Identifying psychometric tools required high precision. To address the
semantic filtering burden
and bypass the high signal-to-noise ratio of keyword searches, we employed a
single optimized prompt
structure designed to semantically extract validation studies (e.g., “validity”, “reliability”) for the specific identified factors.

In both cases, each prompt has been executed iteratively five times to mitigate the non-deterministic nature of LLMs. We observed a complementary behavior between the two engines: while
Perplexity.ai
excelled in retrieving recent open-access literature via direct web crawling,
Deep Research
demonstrated higher precision in parsing semantic relationships within older or paywalled abstracts, with a limited overlap in results that confirmed the necessity of a multi-engine approach. A total of 338 candidate publications (interactions = 251, measurements = 87) were retrieved. Prompts are reported in Appendix 4.

3.2.2.
Phase 2: HITL Validation and Audit Trail

Given the known risks of hallucination in generative models,
no AI-retrieved publication was accepted without passing a rigorous manual validation
. To ensure robustness, we adopted a
double-blind independent review protocol
. Two researchers independently executed each filtering step, and discrepancies were resolved through iterative consensus meetings at the conclusion of each activity before proceeding to the next stage. This prevented error propagation and ensured that only fully validated entries advanced through the pipeline.

To ensure transparency and replicability, the entire process was thoroughly documented in the Additional Material. Starting from the initial pool of raw candidate publications retrieved, we applied the following three-step filtering process:

(1)

Hallucination Check (Verification):
Each of the 338 candidate studies was cross-referenced independently by both researchers against authoritative indexing services (e.g., Google Scholar, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore). Entries that could not be verified or appeared to be “hallucinated” (i.e., non-existent titles or wrong authors) were immediately discarded. This step identified 31 hallucinated entries (interactions = 19, measurements = 12), resulting in an observed Hallucination Rate of 9.2% (interactions = 7.6%, measurements = 13.8%). This phase specifically targeted hallucinations manifested as
Fabricated References
. For the surviving valid publications, this step also served to correct any metadata inaccuracies.

(2)

Exclusion Criteria Filtering (Quality):
The remaining 307 verified papers (interactions = 232, measurements = 75) were subsequently assessed for bibliographic quality. We thus excluded 18 papers (interactions = 17, measurements = 1) that did not meet the publication standards (e.g., non-peer-reviewed, short papers, posters) or ranking thresholds (Scimago Q3+ or CORE Class C+).

(3)

Relevance Assurance (Scope):
The full texts of the surviving 289 publications (interactions = 215, measurements = 74) were independently examined by the two researchers to verify semantic consistency and identify
Content Misattribution
. This qualitative screening ensured that (i) the paper actually investigated the specific interaction or measurement instrument, and (ii) the finding was relevant to the context. Following the consensus discussion for this final stage, a total of 111 publications were excluded (interactions
= 102, measurements = 9).

Reliability

While rigorous consensus checks were performed at the end of each factual verification step (Steps 1 and 2) to resolve objective discrepancies, the inter-rater agreement metrics reported here specifically refer to the
qualitative eligibility decisions
performed in Step 3, where subjective interpretation of relevance was required. The agreement was substantial (Cohen’s
\kappa=.76
for interactions, Cohen’s
\kappa=.85
for measurement tools). After resolving disagreements, the remaining valid studies (N=178; interactions = 113, measurements = 65) were processed for the final corpus.

3.2.3.
Phase 3: Snowballing Augmentation

4.1.
A classification of resulting human factors

To explain how and why online behavior turns out to be secure or unsafe in a systematic way, the MORPHEUS framework integrates two psychological models: the Cognitive-Affective-Behavioral (CAB) model
(
55
)
and Heider’s Attribution Theory
(
163
)
. We utilize these theories to organize the 50 identified human factors by their functional roles—classifying them into
Direct Factors
and
Modulators
—and their attributional origins.

4.1.1.
Direct Factors (The CAB Core)

The CAB model is at the foundation of the framework, providing the proximal processing structure. We classify factors belonging to the
Cognitive
,
Emotional
, and
Behavioral
dimensions as
Direct Factors
. These elements reflect the interaction between what users believe, perceive, and think (cognitive), their emotional reactions and feelings (affective), and their actions and intentions (behavioral) in generating security practices
(
117
;
96
)
.
Direct factors have an immediate impact on behavior. They can manifest as cognitive failures (e.g., incomplete threat knowledge), emotional responses (e.g., fear, stress), or behavioral patterns (e.g., automatic or impulsive clicking on an email link)
(
63
;
152
)
. Consequently, interventions focusing on direct factors are often situational and short-term, such as awareness campaigns, nudges, UI redesign, and stress buffer features in the interface
(
157
;
234
)
.

4.1.2.
Modulators (Distal Antecedents)

Surrounding this core, we extended MORPHEUS by adding three dimensions—
Personality Traits
,
Demographic
, and
Social/Organizational
—which function as
Modulators
. These are distal antecedents that significantly contribute toward the magnitude, probability, or course of effects attributed to the direct factors. To systematize these modulators, we integrated Heider’s Attribution Theory, categorizing them into internal (dispositional) and external (situational) causes:

•

Internal Modulators (Personality Traits, Demographics):
These factors influence how an individual intrinsically regulates responses to a threat. For example, emotional reactivity and self-regulation are influenced by personality traits like neuroticism
(
402
;
266
;
347
)
. Similarly, demographic modulators (e.g., age, education) impact digital literacy, social anticipations, and inclinations towards trust
(
365
;
45
;
274
)
.

•

External Modulators (Social & Organizational):
These factors define the environment where the behavior occurs. External conditions, such as security culture, norms, workload, social pressure, and policy clarity, shape risk perception and compliance behavior
(
167
;
157
;
17
)
.

Interventions that mitigate modulator factors are systemic and longitudinal in nature, including role-based training, policy change, personality-based segmentation, or organizational workflow redesign.

It is worth noting that some factors (e.g.,
Risk attitude
or
Stress
) may overlap more than one dimension (e.g., Cognitive and Personality, or Emotional and Cognitive); in these cases, we consider the most relevant dimension as representative for each factor. Furthermore, examples such as workload, security culture, or policy clarity can be understood as conceptual antecedents of the social and organizational factors explicitly represented in our framework (e.g., lack of resources, security posture, norms). We therefore use these examples illustratively, while grounding the final taxonomy strictly in the 50 human factors identified through the literature review.

4.1.3.
Operational Causal System

To operationalize this theoretical distinction and avoid viewing these factors as an unstructured list, MORPHEUS is structured as a hierarchical causal system. As illustrated in Figure
2
, the framework identifies a directional flow of influence:

•

Layer 1 (Modulators):
These serve as the distal antecedents, categorized by their attributional origin. Internal characteristics, such as
Personality Traits
(e.g., Neuroticism),
Demographic traits
(e.g., Education), and external contextual factors (e.g.,
Organizational Norms
), set the baseline conditions for the user.

•

Layer 2 (Direct Factors):
This layer represents the proximal processing engine, aligned with the CAB model. It captures the user’s transient state—comprising Cognitive, Emotional, and Behavioral factors—which is the immediate driver of the security action.

•

Layer 3 (Outcome):
This represents the final susceptibility to specific threats (e.g., phishing, misconfiguration). Crucially, this outcome is precipitated by the specific
Behavioral Factors
enacted in Layer 2, which result from the interplay of modulators, cognitive-emotional states, and external
Adversarial Triggers
(e.g., time pressure).

This architectural view clarifies that distal factors rarely cause incidents in isolation; rather, they modulate the intensity of the cognitive and emotional processes that lead to the behavioral outcome.

It is worth noting that demographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, education) are classified as
internal modulators
in MORPHEUS, aligning with the dispositional perspective of attribution theory. However, functionally they differ from other internal traits: while they represent inherent attributes of the user, they cannot be modified through security training or behavioral correction. Instead, they serve as fixed constraints that systematically modulate susceptibility, requiring interventions focused on interface adaptation rather than user modification.

Such a layered framework will enable practitioners to develop sophisticated cybersecurity interventions, which may include tailored training programs and environmental or policy adjustments. However, some methodological issues might be associated with this approach. First, the direct and modulator types often overlap and require advanced analytic procedures, considering interactions
(
318
)
. Second, cyberthreat environments are dynamic and demand adaptive processes that combine different data streams and contextualize them to inform policy decision-making. These challenges require the systematic study of empirical data that can extrapolate the known models of the psychological domain in the particular area of cybersecurity
(
279
)
.

4.2.
Distinction between Human Factors and Adversarial Triggers

A fundamental theoretical contribution of MORPHEUS lies in the clear demarcation between
human factors
and
adversarial triggers
, which are external stimuli or tactics deployed to exploit these characteristics. While often conflated in cybersecurity literature, distinguishing the
stimulus
from the
response mechanism
is critical for accurate risk diagnosis. We identify three primary categories of adversarial triggers that function as amplifiers of human vulnerability.

Drawing from social psychology
(
84
)
,
persuasion principles
such as
Authority
,
Scarcity
, and
Reciprocity
are not human traits but manipulation tactics. Within our framework, these principles act as keys to unlock specific human factors. For instance, an attack leveraging
Authority
(tactic) targets the human factor of
Norms
(obedience) and
Trust
. Research
(
458
;
127
)
has confirmed that these principles—along with other adversarial triggers, such as Gragg’s psychological triggers
(
143
)
or Stajano’s principles
(
381
)
—are strategically embedded in phishing campaigns to bypass analytical processing (System 2) and force a reliance on heuristics.

Time pressure
is an environmental constraint imposed by the attacker (e.g., a phishing deadline) or the context (e.g., incident response), rather than a dispositional trait of the user
(
81
)
. In the MORPHEUS framework, time pressure functions as a cognitive inhibitor, restricting the resources available for
Vigilance
,
Cognitive reflectiveness
, and systematic processing (System 2 – slow, deliberate, and logical thinking
(
198
)
), thereby forcing a reliance on
Biases
and heuristics (System 1 - fast, automatic, and intuitive thinking
(
198
)
)
(
67
)
.

In threats such as malware downloads and system misconfigurations, attackers utilize
deceptive user interfaces
(also known as dark patterns) or contextual mimicry (such as spear-phishing pretexting). These are external design choices intended to maximize
Misperception
and exacerbate
Cognitive Fatigue
or
Frustration
. For example, a confusing security alert (trigger) exploits a user’s
Lack of Knowledge
or
Distraction
, leading to an insecure default choice
(
253
)
.

Analysts should consider these external triggers to move beyond generic "awareness" solutions toward targeted interventions that disrupt the specific interaction between tactic and trait.

5.
Human factors affecting the most prevalent threats

This section applies the multidimensional MORPHEUS framework to six of the most pervasive cyberthreats—phishing (and its spear-phishing and SMishing variants), malware downloads, password management errors, and system misconfigurations—to highlight how specific human factors drive vulnerability in each context. The analysis is grounded in an extensive review of 50 studies that cover demographic, personality, cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and organizational dimensions. The next sub-sections report the details of how each threat is influenced by the associated human factors.

5.1.
Phishing and variants

Phishing remains one of the most prevalent social-engineering threats, exploiting psychological biases and habitual decision processes through deceptive communication channels. Its targeted (spear-phishing) and mobile (SMishing) variants further amplify these mechanisms by leveraging personalization and immediacy cues. The empirical evidence from various studies consistently reveals that human susceptibility is systematically related to individual and contextual characteristics.

5.2.
Malware download

Malicious software, also known as malware, typically infiltrates systems by convincing users to open infected files or visit insecure websites. Human behavior underpins this vulnerability.

Demographic factors.

There is limited evidence in the literature regarding the influence of demographic factors on malware downloads. However, a study by Lévesque and colleagues found that middle-aged users (ages 25–40) were more susceptible than younger individuals
(
232

Personality factors.

A study by Yilmaz and colleagues
(

5.3.
Password management

The challenge of managing multiple unique and complex passwords for various digital services remains ongoing. In line with this, the spread of weak, recycled, or inadequately secured credentials is a critical weakness in individual and business cybersecurity settings. Moreover, the adoption of facilities, like password managers, is still limited by human factors, such as a lack of trust.

Demographic and Cognitive factors.

According to the results of Whitty et al.
(
429
)
, younger generations are significantly more likely to share their credentials.

Empirical evidence suggests that cognitive factors play a significant role in password management failures. Users’
cyber risk beliefs
can indeed lead them to underestimate the threats of identity theft, making them more likely to adopt poor password management behaviors
(
17
)
. This may also originate from a
misperception
of individuals that "nobody would target [them]" or that "[an attacker] could not do much damage anyway" if they had their password stolen
(
17
)
.

Cognitive fatigue
, mainly caused by the difficulty of maintaining multiple complex passwords, frequently prompts users to recycle credentials across different sites or to adopt predictable modification patterns
(
103
;
212
;
17
)
.

A
lack of awareness
regarding password security often leads users to underestimate the dangers of reusing passwords, inadequate password storage, and weak password choices. A common misconception among users is that small password changes (e.g., mere replacements) are much more effective in improving protection, without realizing that attackers can easily crack foreseeable alteration patterns
(
124
;
384
;
103
;
17
)
.

A
lack of knowledge
on best practices or techniques for strong and effective password creation (e.g., using mnemonics) consistently emerges as a central human factor contributing to insecure, weak, or non-compliant passwords
(
17
;
290
)
. Moreover, in
(
181
)
, the authors demonstrated that unclear or technical password rules leave users uncertain and with scarce knowledge about what constitutes a secure password, prompting insecure coping behaviors such as writing passwords down or using predictable patterns. Despite the adoption of password managers can alleviate these issues, users tend to mistrust and avoid these tools mainly because they do not understand how password managers work and their underlying security mechanisms
(
124
)
. There is evidence that expertise can be beneficial and lead to avoiding misbehavior, such as writing passwords down or using two-factor authentication, but experts may change their passwords much more rarely compared to non-experts

In the paper
(
124

, the authors highlight that

has a decisive influence on the adoption of password management tools: users who perceive themselves as more competent in the autonomous management of their credentials are less inclined to delegate security to a password manager, while those who doubt their abilities rely more on automated solutions. This result underscores how perceived self-efficacy in security practices guides behavioral choices and trust in technical support tools.

Emotional factors.

Frustration
is a critical emotion that can influence secure password behavior
(
181
;
17
)

As a consequence, users try alternative solutions such as password reuse, simplification, or external recording, often compromising overall security compliance and increasing vulnerability.

Behavioral factors.

In
(
103
)
, it was found that, due to the complexity of remembering passwords, users adopt simple and predictable rules for creating and changing passwords, mistakenly trusting that such changes are sufficient to guarantee security (
complacency

Impulsive behaviour
, both attentional and motor, emerges as a positive predictor of password reuse and sharing, revealing that acting quickly and without reflection can lead to poor choices in password management
(
154
)
. Moreover, a specific aspect of impulsivity, i.e., lack of perseverance, is associated with a greater likelihood of sharing passwords. This indicates a reduced ability to persevere with tedious and burdensome tasks, leading more easily to risky actions such as sharing credentials
(
429
)
.

Internet addiction
has been identified as a behavioral factor contributing to insecure password management, as users with this disorder tend to take greater risks to maintain online engagement, showing reduced compliance with security protocols and poorer password hygiene as a consequence of habitual and impulsive digital behavior
(

)
.

Laziness
can influence poor password management. In
(
429
)
, it was found that participants share passwords primarily for convenience and to reduce the effort to comply with security rules. This result suggests that avoiding effortful or tedious security behaviors can lead to unsafe practices, such as password reuse and disclosure.

Organizational and social factors.

Attitude toward policies
is associated with fewer risky behaviors. This implies that negative attitudes toward rules correlate with lax password practices (e.g., reuse, sharing)

;

;

;

The adoption of alternative wrong strategies to create and manage passwords can also be affected by a
Lack of communication

Lack of trust
in using password managers can be caused by fears of vendor access, cloud synchronization, and a “single point of failure” if the vault is breached
(

(

The

(technical, temporal, or cognitive) emerged as a critical cause of insecure password management. In
(

(

Compliance with

In
(

The

5.4.
Misconfiguration

Misconfigurations occur when systems or applications are configured, deployed, or provisioned incorrectly, thus exposing themself to unauthorized access or data breaches. The problem is rooted in multiple human factors, which are discussed in the following.

Demographic factors.

According to
(
284
)
,
education
might undermine systems’ security. Indeed, the authors found that "academic background was a notable factor associated with network instability". This is mainly because employees often lack the necessary qualifications and skills to properly configure the systems; for example, engineers commit fewer misconfigurations than administrators.

Cognitive factors.

Cognitive
biases
such as optimism and availability biases emerged as possible causes of misconfiguration
(
73
)
. They contribute to a false sense of adequacy, leading users to ignore critical options (e.g., alerts or spending limits) or misinterpret the implications of advanced configurations. Confirmation bias can also lead users to trust familiar names or services while overlooking malicious clues, which, when combined with alertness or fatigue, can result in incorrect configuration
(
332
)
.

Cognitive fatigue
amplifies misconfiguration for several reasons. Indeed, when reports fail to consider explanatory context, for instance, by visualizing only summary messages or error codes, they place the burden on professionals to independently research and interpret the cause of the problem and the most appropriate mitigation. These activities increase cognitive load, leading to errors, misunderstandings, or partial application of corrective measures
(
254
;
341
)
. Similarly,
(
332
)
demonstrated that implementing and maintaining security controls can lead to cognitive fatigue due to the high number of alerts, repetitive procedures, and complex technical decisions. These activities reduce user concentration and critical thinking. Moreover, they can lead users to automatic responses, omissions, and suboptimal choices that increase the likelihood of incorrect or incomplete configurations.

Different studies indicate that misconfiguration is often facilitated by a
lack of awareness
. For example, in the case of Non-Fungible Tokens (NFTs), lay users might not recognize relevant risks and configurations. At the same time, they tend to activate settings that they easily understand, leaving defaults unchanged, connect their wallet to marketplaces without preliminary checks, do not disconnect or log out, and ignore critical features such as 2FA
(
73
)
. This lack of awareness extends to backup and recovery, resulting in exposure to configuration errors and social engineering vectors. In the case of Border Gateway Protocol, administrators may not perceive the impact of their actions (e.g., incorrect filters or inconsistent policies), with errors persisting until they generate large-scale routing disruptions
(
250
)
. Additionally, the configuration of the containers’ orchestration system may be affected. Administrators of Kubernetes clusters often overlook the presence or severity of misconfigurations in manifests, as vulnerabilities are not immediately visible or reported by development tools
(
330
)
. This leads to an underestimation of risk and a failure to take corrective action, revealing the need to design solutions that better highlight the signs of insecure configuration. Finally, in the cloud environment, the importance of awareness and staff education in preventing configuration errors is emphasized
(
150
)
.

Lack of knowledge
in misconfiguration has been explored by different studies. For instance, overly descriptive reports force technicians to ‘fill in the information gaps’, indicating a lack of operational knowledge about the changes to be applied
(
254
)
. The previously mentioned study on Kubernetes configuration
(
330
)
highlighted that practitioners often lack the necessary knowledge to mitigate misconfigurations. Finally, the study on BGP configuration associated several BGP incidents to operators’ inadequate understanding of configuration semantics, pointing to a ‘lack of knowledge’ as the structural cause of misconfiguration at the network level
(
250
)
.

Also
misperception
emerged as a potential cause of misconfiguration. Specifically, in
(
332
)
the authors stated that during the implementation of security controls, many requirements are formulated in an abstract or ambiguous manner, leaving room for interpretation. This ambiguity fosters misperceptions, which in this case is a distorted perception of the meaning or priority of controls. As a consequence, final configurations may diverge from the original security intent, resulting in systematic misconfigurations due to incorrect assumptions or a partial understanding of control requirements.

Regarding
security self-efficacy
, the study reported in
(
254
)
proved that reports with actionable mitigations (explanations and operational snippets) increase accuracy and speed of correction, and reduce the need for external research. The authors also stated that such reports can “improve both the knowledge and skills” of administrators, thereby reducing the risk of systematic misconfigurations due to incorrect assumptions or errors in configuration tasks.

Emotional factors.

Frustation and shame emerged as the main emotional factors affecting misconfiguration. The study by Rahmati et al.
(
162
)
revealed that Wi-Fi configuration interfaces may cause significantly higher
frustration
levels, due to repetitive errors and confusing configuration steps. These problems lead users to adopt insecure shortcuts or bypass essential security controls, thereby increasing the likelihood of misconfigurations. The study reported in
(
341
)
revealed that
shame
emerged as a factor influencing the willingness to disclose and address security errors, thereby indirectly contributing to the persistence of misconfigurations.

Social and Organizational factors.

Lack of communication
can be a crucial factor for misconfiguration. For example, in
(
341
)
, the authors found that shame is associated with misconfiguration errors because it can hinder internal communication, leading to delays or omissions in reporting, thereby turning an isolated error into a systemic vulnerability. The same study highlights that shame-based responses within organizations foster a
lack of trust
: employees who feel monitored or publicly blamed after an incident perceive diminished credibility, while managers become more suspicious and controlling. This mutual erosion of trust limits an open dialogue and collaborative problem-solving, exacerbating the persistence and recurrence of configuration errors.

Several studies focused on the
lack of resources
, highlighting the importance of this factor. For example, missing information, time, tools, and training increase the likelihood that configurations will remain incorrect or be corrected late
(
254
)
. In an empirical validation, it has been proven that explanations and executable snippets serve as a cognitive resource that reduces time and errors, while their absence hinders remediation
(
253
)
. Also, limited budgets, inadequate tools, and insufficient human resources/training lead to flawed control implementations, resulting in misconfiguration and poor security hygiene
(
332
)
. In the study on Kubernetes manifests, it also emerged that practitioners report knowledge gaps due to a lack of training material that should help prevent and correct misconfigurations
(
330
)
. Finally, in the context of cloud analysis, the absence of periodic audits, continuous monitoring, and education paths leaves misconfigurations unresolved
(
150
)
.

Finally,
security posture
can impact misconfiguration. Rahman et al. demonstrated that configuration errors weaken the overall security posture of the system, making privilege escalations and unauthorized access more likely
(
330
)
. Similarly, during peak workloads and with limited resources, some administrators consciously accept violations of best practices (e.g., leaving default VLANs active) to “make the network work,” leaving it vulnerable to attacks; this represents a clear degradation of security posture due to misconfiguration and organizational choices
(
284
)
.

6.
Interactions among factors

A thorough comprehension of the human factors that affect security incidents is incomplete unless these factors are examined within an integrative framework that illustrates their interdependence. Cognitive biases, emotional states, behavioral patterns, personality factors, demographic factors, and social or organizational situations do not operate in isolation; instead, they interact with each other, magnifying exposure while also offering protective buffering processes simultaneously.

Applying the
AI-assisted Systematic Screening
detailed in Section
3.2

484

295
unique pairwise interactions. The interactions presented here are derived from heterogeneous studies. Consequently, unless a specific causal link is established by the source, these interactions should be interpreted as
statistical associations

Given the high dimensionality of this network, the exhaustive dimension-by-dimension analysis is provided in Appendix 2 and summarized in Tables

From the extensive network of interactions presented in Appendix 2, we have distilled
twelve
high-level
Key Interaction Mechanisms
. These represent the most salient and recurring causal patterns that emerged from our analysis. While not an exhaustive enumeration of every possible psychological dynamic latent in the dataset, these mechanisms serve as representative archetypes to illustrate how distal, proximal, and external factors systematically converge to drive vulnerability.

A critical interaction pattern emerges from the literature regarding how users process security information, heavily referencing the Heuristic-Systematic Model (HSM)

The interactions between

These interactions suggest that a "one-size-fits-all" approach to human factors in cybersecurity is insufficient, as the protective mechanisms for one demographic or personality type may serve as failure points for another. Therefore, individual demographic differences should be considered when designing security interventions.

5. The “Double-Edged Sword” of Desirable Traits.

A counterintuitive pattern emerges regarding traits typically considered positive in a general workforce context. For instance, while
conscientiousness
generally drives compliance with security policies
(

)
and strengthens the effect of positive attitudes
(

)
, it can manifest as insecure habits, such as diligently checking every communication, rendering them more susceptible to phishing through the over-processing of malicious emails
(

. Furthermore, studies indicate that among certain demographics, such as female users, high conscientiousness can actually predict riskier self-reported cybersecurity behaviors
(

Similarly,

facilitates policy compliance

but simultaneously acts as a vulnerability vector for social engineering. In fact, agreeable individuals are naturally more trusting and thus more vulnerable to

techniques

, also leading to lower vigilance levels
(

.

Finally, higher

does not linearly translate to higher security awareness. Surprisingly, evidence suggests that undergraduate students may possess better cybersecurity awareness than post-graduate students
(

, and highly educated employees may be less aware of password hygiene and organizational data protection
(

. This indicates that general academic knowledge does not automatically confer cybersecurity resilience.

6. The Cognitive-Emotional Bottleneck.

The interaction network identifies a critical system bottleneck centered on the interplay between
Stress
and
Cognitive Fatigue
. These two factors constitute a self-reinforcing feedback loop: the literature confirms that high

and

consume executive functions, leading to

,

(

and a heightened risk attitude

7. The Trust and Bias Overconfidence Trap.

Instead of a simple knowledge deficit, as the Dunning-Kruger effect might suggest

, our analysis reveals that

is driven by a convergence of dispositional and social factors. Specifically,

and

(inversely) are direct precursors: high trust and reliance on heuristics significantly increase overconfidence

and

8. Social Amplification and Silence Loops.

Social factors act as powerful amplifiers of individual risk. We identified a systemic “Silence Loop” driven by the interaction between
Lack of Communication
and
Shame
. The data shows that users who experience high levels of shame—often younger individuals
(

(

(

(

9. The Habitual Autopilot Loop.

Our analysis reveals a powerful inertial mechanism driven by the interaction between

and Cognitive factors.
Recurrence
(repetitive behavior) shows a modulation effect on

and
Bias

This mechanism explains why awareness campaigns often fail to change behavior in the long term: for example, the repetition of daily tasks creates a cognitive tunnel that desensitizes users to warning signals
(

;
199
)
.

10. The Resource-Constraint Cascade.

Organizational factors exert a cascading pressure on individual cognition. The analysis highlights that
Lack of Resources
(time, support) is a primary antecedent of
Stress

(
22
)
and is negatively associated with
Security Self-Efficacy

(
75

. This cascade demonstrates that human error is often a symptom of an under-resourced environment: forcing compliance without providing adequate resources leads to emotional burnout and a collapse of the user’s belief in their ability to perform security tasks effectively

11. The “Dark Traits” Risk Pathway.

Beyond accidental errors, the framework identifies a distinct pathway for negligence driven by antagonistic traits. Specifically,
Narcissism
and
Greed
bypass the typical cognitive filters. The interaction data reveals that
Narcissism
is positively associated with
Overconfidence

,

This pathway represents a dispositional vulnerability that might require behavioral monitoring rather than sole educational training.

12. The Compulsive Risk Spiral.

Distinct from the “Habitual Autopilot” driven by inertia, the interactions surrounding

define a pathological pathway to vulnerability. This factor shows strong positive feedback loops with both

and

7.1.
Organization of the assessment tools in three dimensions

To better frame the role and scope of the retrieved tools, we identified three main dimensions that focus on the type of data collected by the tool:
first-person
,
second-person
, and
third-person
. This classification is in line with well-established categorization often adopted in behavioral science and HCI, which distinguishes between these dimensions, offering insights into the cognitive processes and behavioral patterns relevant to cybersecurity.

First-person data: self-reported assessments

These data include the direct reports of individuals about their attributes, beliefs, attitudes, and experiences, which are collected through questionnaires, scales, and interviews. Structured psychometric instruments, such as the
Big Five Inventory

(
195
)
, provide insight into an individual’s personality traits. In contrast, Likert-scale self-reports of affective states, including fear, stress, and related factors, constitute a less structured yet equally standard tool
(
87
)
. Since such data reflect internal attitudes and perceived danger, first-person instruments provide valuable insights into long-term planning, organizational cultures, and behavioral tendencies. Their relative accessibility and scalability across heterogeneous populations make these tools easy to use for researchers who require a broad descriptive understanding. However, a clear downside of these methods is that they remain susceptible to
self-report bias
, necessitating caution when interpreting quantitative results.

Second-person data: behavioral observation and simulation

These data are the first-hand observations of the user activity in well-defined or semi-structured situations, such as experimental tasks, phishing simulations, decision-making games, and performance-based assessments. For example, a factor like
overconfidence
can be assessed through simulated investment scenarios
(
59
)
, while
vigilance
can be evaluated with attentional tasks such as the Mackworth Clock Test
(
248
)
. Since they can measure observable behavior, these measures are more objective than self-reported data. However, they tend to be ecologically valid only in experimental conditions; the control of an environment can often be artificial, which limits external reliability.

Third-person data: biometric and physiological indicators
. Thanks to more advanced sensor technologies, we can regularly collect third-person data, such as biometric and physiological measures in real-time. These instruments, such as electroencephalograms (EEG), eye-tracking gaze direction, heart-rate variability, and galvanic skin response, enable us to record involuntary and subconscious reactions associated with various human factors, including mental workload, stress, distraction, and fatigue
(
302
;
358
)
. The resulting temporal resolution and objective evidence are appealing; however, these methods generally require specialized apparatus and theories to interpret the results.

Although each data source offers unique strengths and limitations, the combined use of first, second, and third-person methods can yield a more comprehensive understanding of the human factors that influence cybersecurity behavior. Integrating data from various sources is essential for developing adaptive systems that can dynamically respond to the state, weaknesses, and local needs of users.

The following subsections present, for each dimension, the measurement strategies for each human factor related to that dimension, providing practical guidance for researchers and practitioners who aim to enhance cybersecurity through increased awareness of human risk.
Table
10
systematically maps each human factor to the related measurement instruments.

7.2.
Demographic Factors

Demographic factors such as
Age
,
Gender
, and
Education
are typically assessed through standard self-report demographic questionnaires. These instruments usually include a small set of items (e.g., year of birth, highest level of education attained, gender identity) and are administered alongside the main study measures. Although they do not require dedicated psychometric scales, demographic variables play a crucial role as control or moderating factors in cybersecurity research, as they systematically influence exposure, digital literacy, and preferences for protective strategies. Their simplicity and ubiquity make them easy to integrate into any empirical study that aims to contextualize human factors within broader population characteristics.

7.3.
Personality Traits

Personality traits (
Agreeableness
,
Conscientiousness
,
Extraversion
,
Openness
, and
Neuroticism
/
Emotional stability
) are commonly evaluated through validated questionnaires like the
Big Five Inventory
(BFI)
(
195
)
, the
International Personality Item Pool
(IPIP-FFM)
(
141
)
, both of which provide reliable assessments of personality dimensions. For example, the IPIP Big-Five scales correlate strongly with the commercial
NEO-PI-R measure

(
92
)
, a paid, licensed questionnaire. A more recent version of the BFI is the BFI-2
(
375
)
, which offers several advantages over the original version, including a robust hierarchical structure and enhanced predictive power. The BFI-2 was developed in three versions: a full 60-item version, a compact 30-item version, and an abbreviated 15-item version.
Another alternative is the
HEXACO-24

(
108
)
, a short self-reported questionnaire designed to assess personality traits, with four items per trait.

Greed
can be measured through the
Dispositional Greed Scale (DGS)

(
452
)
. It is a seven–item self-report instrument that measures the tendency to desire “more” and to experience persistent dissatisfaction with current possessions or achievements. An alternative tool is the
Multidimensional Dispositional Greed Assessment (MDGA)
, a questionnaire designed to measure adults’ dispositional greed, which encompasses the desire to acquire more than one has or retain what one has at all costs, and the tendency to never be satisfied
(
221
)
.

Narcissism
can be assessed by using different questionnaires. The first is the
Narcissistic Admiration and Rivalry Questionnaire (NARQ)

(
31
)
. It conceives narcissism as a dual process encompassing self-promoting and antagonistic tendencies:
admiration
, which reflects assertive self-enhancement and the pursuit of uniqueness, and
rivalry
, which captures defensive self-protection through devaluation of others. The second questionnaire is the
The Five-Factor Narcissism Inventory

(
140
)
, a self-report measure of the traits linked to grandiose and vulnerable narcissism, as well as narcissistic personality disorder. The last questionnaire is the
Narcissistic Personality Inventory
(NPI), one of the first tools used to assess non-clinical narcissism as a stable personality trait
(
335
)
. It captures key dimensions such as grandiosity, entitlement, and exhibitionism, and is among the most widely used measures of narcissistic traits in the general population.

7.4.
Cognitive Factors

Since cognitive
biases
are diverse, researchers often use different approaches. One useful tool is the
Adult Decision-Making Competence
(A-DMC) battery
(
59
)
, which includes seven tasks that users are required to perform to quantify their susceptibility to common biases (e.g., framing, overconfidence, resistance to sunk costs). Another quick gauge is the
Cognitive Reflection Test
(CRT)
(
52
)
, a 3-item test that assesses reflective versus intuitive thinking; lower CRT scores indicate a higher reliance on heuristic bias and have been associated with greater phishing susceptibility
(
6
)
. Finally,
electroencephalography
(EEG) has been proven effective in revealing heuristic biases in individuals
(
139
)
.

The Internet Users’ Information Privacy Concerns (IUIPC) scale is widely employed to operationalize
Cyber Risk Beliefs
when evaluating human factors in privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs) and investigating the privacy paradox. Specifically, it assesses users’ subjective views of fairness regarding data handling through the three dimensions of Control, Awareness, and Collection

(
149
)
.

Also, the
Cognitive fatigue
can be measured with questionnaires. The
Cognitive Failures Questionnaire
(CFQ)
(
57
)
is a tool that captures frequent lapses in memory and attention due to fatigue or overload. The CFQ is a 25-item scale that predicts real-world absent-minded errors (e.g., forgetting to save work). A high CFQ score implies cognitive fatigue and attentional lapses.
The
Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS)

(
216
)
is a 9-item scale that assesses the impact of fatigue on daily functioning. In more specific settings, mental workload indexes like the
NASA-TLX

(
160
)
(which includes a "mental fatigue" component) have also been used; the
NASA-TLX
is widely validated for subjective workload, including cognitive strain. Another viable solution is the
Fatigue Scale for Motor and Cognitive Functions (FSMC)

(
316
)
, a 20-item instrument (10 items each for cognitive and physical fatigue). Finally, physiological measurements such as EEG
(
302
)
,
Heart Rate Variability
(HRV)
(
358
)
, and pupil dilatation
(
280
)
can be used to assess this factor.

Cognitive reflectiveness
might be measured by using different questionnaires. An example is the
Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT)

(
131
)
, which measures an individual’s propensity to override intuitive, yet incorrect, heuristic responses in favor of deliberate reasoning. Higher CRT scores indicate greater engagement in analytic processing and have been linked to improved phishing detection and reduced reliance on superficial cues. Another example is the
Thoughtfully Reflective Decision Making
(TRDM) scale, which quantifies an individual’s tendency to collect information, generate alternatives, and systematically deliberate before making a decision, as opposed to relying on impulsive or ’gut feeling’ choices
(
310
)
. As an alternative, the
Elaboration scale
evaluates the degree to which the user actively connects received stimuli (e.g., an email) with prior knowledge
(
414
)
. Finally, the
Decision Styles Scale
(DSS) also measures an individual’s propensity towards an analytical decision-making process, characterized by systematic information gathering and thorough evaluation of alternatives
(
156
)
.

Regarding
Decision fatigue
, the
Decision Fatigue Scale
(DFS)
(
170
)
has been proposed as a tool to measure subjective decision fatigue.

General attentional distractibility (i.e.,
distraction
) can be assessed by the CFQ’s distractibility sub-score
(
57
)
or by mindfulness scales. Alternatively, the
Mindful Attention Awareness Scale
(MAAS)
(
58
)
(15 items, free) measures the tendency to be attentive versus distracted; it’s well-validated as an inverse measure of everyday distraction (higher mindfulness = less distraction). Other approaches include the
Attention-Related Cognitive Errors Scale
(ARCES)
(
77
)
, which is similar to the CFQ and validated for attention lapses. Each of these has been used in studies to quantify how easily one’s attention deviates, a factor linked to missing security cues. Eye-tracking
(
223
;
454
)
and EEG
(
19
;
206
)
can also detect distraction and mind-wandering episodes.

Security-specific
lack of awareness
and
lack of knowledge
are often measured with questionnaires. An example is the
Human Aspects of Information Security Questionnaire
(HAIS-Q)
(
309
)
, which assesses employees’ knowledge of and awareness of security policies and procedures. The HAIS-Q covers domains such as email, passwords, and internet use, effectively serving as a security awareness test (available for free for research purposes). The
Security Behavior Intentions Scale
(SeBIS)
(
120
)
, a 16-item questionnaire, and the Cybercrime Awareness Scale (CAS)
(
28
)
are two alternatives that measure the user’s level of awareness in the cybersecurity domain. A revised version of this scale has also been proposed
(
395
)
. In addition, the Situation Awareness Rating Technique (SART)
(
122
)
is a more general self-assessed tool for measuring situational awareness in human-machine environments, which can also be used in cybersecurity contexts
(
60
)
. Awareness and knowledge of cybersecurity incidents can be assessed through interviews, video games, focus groups, and attack simulations; a review of solutions on these is reported in
(
329
)
.

Misperception
is often evaluated through risk perception scales. For instance, Wilson et al. developed a questionnaire to measure risk perception
(
435
)
and validated a 21-item form
(
417
)
that includes four subscales: perceived affect, perceived exposure, perceived severity, and perceived susceptibility.

Overconfidence
is measured by comparing self-assessed ability to actual performance. In security, a typical measure is the
short overconfidence scale
(some exist in psychology, e.g., a 6-item scale validated by Schaefer et al.
(
357
)
), though these are less common. In all cases, evidence of validity comes from correlations between the overconfidence metric and actual errors (peer-reviewed studies consistently show that the most overconfident users are often the least skilled
(
420
)
).

A well-known validated instrument for assessing risky tendencies (i.e.,
risk attitude
) is the
Domain-Specific Risk-Taking Scale (DOSPERT)

(
44
;
43
)
. DOSPERT is a questionnaire that assesses the likelihood of engaging in risky activities across various domains (ethical, financial, health/safety, recreational, and social). Higher DOSPERT scores have been linked to risky online behavior
(
120
)
. Another measure is the
General Risk Propensity Scale
(GRiPS)
(
453
)
, a short unidimensional scale for overall risk-taking tendency. In studies of phishing, researchers have used
Nicholson’s risk propensity scale

(
294
)
and found that higher risk-taking scores correlate with greater phishing susceptibility
(
278
)
. The
Risky cybersecurity behaviours scale
(RScB)
(
154
)
is a 20-item scale that measures users’ risky cybersecurity behavior. Finally, research also proved that risk-taking behavior can be predicted through EEG analysis
(
405
)
.

Security self-efficacy
can be assessed using the
Self-Efficacy in Information Security Scale (SEIS)
(
344
)
. It includes items that evaluate users’ confidence in their ability to implement and manage protective actions, such as installing and updating security software, removing spyware, applying system patches, and configuring browser or privacy settings. This scale captures the cognitive belief that one can effectively protect personal or organizational digital assets, reflecting the motivational component that underpins proactive security engagement and resilience in the face of cyberthreats.

A standard instrument for
uncertainty
assessment is the
Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale
(IUS)
(
70
)
. The IUS (27-item or the short 12-item version) is a well-validated measure of one’s emotional/cognitive reactions to ambiguity. It has excellent validity and reliability across cultures
(
368
;
219
;
433
)
.

Vigilance
is typically measured via performance tasks, for example, the
Mackworth Clock Test

(
248
)
, the
Psychomotor Vigilance Task (PVT)

(
35
)
, or the
Continuous Performance Test

(
172
)
, where lapses in detecting signals indicate reduced vigilance. Additionally, the CFQ mentioned above
(
57
)
includes items for absent-mindedness that reflect vigilance lapses. High CFQ scores or poor sustained-attention task performance would signal low vigilance, consistent with known effects of fatigue and stress on vigilance. Decreased vigilance can also be identified using eye-tracking
(
46
;
268
)
(fixation duration, blink rate) and EEG alpha/theta wave patterns, which signal reduced cognitive engagement
(
10
;
39
)
.

7.5.
Emotional Factors

A common measure for
anxiousness
is the
State-Trait Anxiety Inventory
(STAI)
(
377
)
, a 40-item questionnaire that distinguishes between baseline anxiety tendency and the current state. The STAI is a gold standard with strong validity, but it is a licensed instrument. As free alternatives, the
Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)

(
36
)
(for clinical anxiety) and the
Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD-7)

(
379
)
scale are often used. The GAD-7 is a brief 7-item scale validated for measuring anxiety severity in the general population, as it shows good reliability and construct validity
(
244
)
. Moreover, affective reactions to cyberthreats (including anxiety-related negative affect) can be measured using the
Positive and Negative Affect Schedule
(PANAS) and its extended form PANAS-X
(
426
)
. For instance, it has been adopted to quantify changes in positive and negative affect before and after exposure to phishing emails, privacy or security threats, and security-related tasks
(
372
;
297
;
89
;
91
)
.
Other solutions to measure anxiousness are based on electrodermal activity
(
450
)
and reduced EEG activity
(
169
)
.

Concerning physiological assessment, anxiousness can also be detected through facial expression analysis
(
317
)
.

Digital anxiety
can be measured using the
Digitalisation Anxiety Scale (DAS)
, a questionnaire comprising 35 items that specifically measure anxiety related to digitalisation (as opposed to general or tech-specific anxiety)
(
434
)
. It can be used to identify triggers of digital anxiety within organizations for targeted interventions. Another tool is the
Computer Anxiety Rating Scale (CARS
), which focuses on the behavioral, cognitive, and affective components of computer anxiety
(
164
)
. During a computer interaction, greater computer anxiety was associated with lower expectations and poorer task performance, as well as with greater state anxiety, reported physiological arousal, and debilitative thoughts. Finally, the
Abbreviated Technology Anxiety Scale
(ATAS) can be used to measure this factor
(
434
)
.

A well-known instrument for assessing
fear
tendencies is the
Fear Questionnaire (FQ)

(
258
)
, which consists of a self-report scale to assess phobic fear and related avoidance, plus associated anxiety or depression. The
Cyber-Paranoia and Fear Scale

(
263
)
is a self-report questionnaire designed to measure individuals’ perceptions of threats related to information technology, encompassing both realistic and unrealistic fears, comprising 11 items. In the context of phishing or malware warnings, studies employ Likert items, such as "I am afraid I could lose important data," to gauge fear. These items are often drawn from the
Extended Parallel Process Model
(EPPM)
(
436
)
or
Protection Motivation Theory

(
47
)
constructs (perceived severity and vulnerability). Another approach involves detecting physiological proxies (e.g., heart rate
(
440
;
355
)
and skin conductance
(
432
)
) in laboratory studies.

The NASA-TLX questionnaire
(
160
)
is widely used to measure user
frustration
in addition to workload, thanks to its frustration component.
Frustration can also be measured through the
Frustration Discomfort Scale (FDS)
, a questionnaire developed and validated by Harrington
(
158
)
.
Besides self-assessment tools, facial expressions can reveal individuals’ frustration without the need for any survey
(
142
)
.

Shame
proneness can be measured by the
Test of Self-Conscious Affect 3 (TOSCA-3)

(
391
)
. It presents scenarios and asks how the respondent would feel; higher shame scores indicate a tendency to respond with shame. Alternatively, the
Experiential Shame Scale (ESS)

(
24
)
is another validated self-report that directly asks about feelings of shame in various situations. Finally, shame may also be detected through facial expression analysis
(
171
)
.

The
Perceived Stress Scale
(PSS)
(
87
;
86
)
is a classic 10-item scale for general
stress
appraisal. It measures how overwhelmed and stressed someone feels in the last month. A shorter 4-item version also exists. In cybersecurity research, PSS can be used to gauge chronic stress levels (e.g., job stress, which might correlate with errors). Another measure is the
Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS)

(
243
)
, which includes a stress subscale (available in both 42-item and 21-item forms).
Literature has also proved the efficacy of stress and anxiety assessment through EEG
(
174
;
362
;

)
, cortisol levels
(

;
325
)
, HRV
(
315
)
, Galvanic Skin Response (GSR)
(

)
, and electrocardiography (ECG)
(
390
)
.

7.6.
Behavioral Factors

The
Complacency-Potential Rating Scale (CPRS)

(
369
)
is a specific tool developed to measure an individual’s predisposition to automation
complacency
. It is a questionnaire that gauges attitudes like over-trust and low vigilance when using automated systems. Merrit et al. also proposed the
Automation Induced Complacency Potential-revised scale
(AICP-R)
(
272
)
, a 10-item questionnaire that focuses on attitudes about using and monitoring automation under high workload. In security contexts, high scores for automation can indicate that a user may become too complacent and overtrust the system to handle security tasks (e.g., dismissing warnings, failing to monitor for problems).

When referring to
compulsive behavior
, the
Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory
(OCI)
(
129
)
by Foa and colleagues can measure general compulsiveness. The
Compulsive Internet Use Scale
(CIUS)
(
270
)
is another targeted instrument consisting of a 14-item scale that has demonstrated good psychometric properties for assessing compulsive, potentially pathological internet use. Similarly, the
Online Cognition Scale
(OCS)
(
105
)
measures problematic Internet use using a 36-item questionnaire. Finally, the
Self-Report Habit Index
(SRHI) operationalizes the compulsive behavior by assessing the strength of habitual actions by measuring dimensions such as history of repetition, lack of control, and the psychological discomfort experienced when the behavior is inhibited
(
410
)
.

Concerning
impulsive behaviour
, a common measure is the
Barratt Impulsiveness Scale
(BIS-11)
(
311
;
312
)
, a validated 30-item questionnaire. Shorter scales like the
Abbreviated Impulsiveness Scale
(ABIS)
(
95
)
are also validated for quick assessment. Measures like the SeBIS
(
120
)
, which were introduced previously, also assess impulsivity and link it to risky cybersecurity behaviors. There is also evidence that impulsivity may be revealed by different EEG patterns of resting-state activity
(
226
)
.

The most widely used tool for measuring
internet addiction
is Young’s
Internet Addiction Test
(IAT)
(
448
)
, a 20-item questionnaire that assesses symptoms of excessive internet use (salience, withdrawal, tolerance, etc.). Higher IAT scores correlate with negative outcomes.

Internet usage
can be measured by adopting the
Internet Usage Scale (IUS)
. It has been developed specifically for adolescent populations and includes 26 items that measure participants’ beliefs about how their Internet usage impacts their behavior
(
277
)
.

There is no specific scale that measures
laziness
by that name, but this concept is inversely related to conscientiousness and proactive behavior
(
48
)
. Researchers often use procrastination scales as proxies. For example, Lay’s
General Procrastination Scale
(GPS)
(
222
)
is a validated 20-item measure of the tendency to delay tasks. A high procrastination score could indicate "laziness" in security upkeep (like postponing software updates).

Recurrence
is often measured through
longitudinal observation
. For instance, an organization might track how often the same individual repeats a security violation (clicks on phishing emails repeatedly over months). A high recurrence rate indicates this factor is present. Since “recurrence” is borrowed from clinical terminology (relapse in behavior
(
41
)
), its assessment is usually binary or frequency-based
(
207
)
(e.g., number of repeat incidents). However, a one-time approach is provided with the
Self-Report Habit Index (SRHI)

(
411
)
, a 12-item scale that measures the intensity of a given habit. A shorter version, the
Self-Report Behavioural Automaticity Index (SRBAI)

(
135
)
, comprises four items from the SRHI and was validated for capturing habitual behavior patterns. There is no standalone psychometric scale for recurrence, but its definition and measurement have been discussed in the literature on behavioral relapse
(
207
;
442
)
. If needed, diaries
(
181
)
or logs
(
190
)
are valid tools (a consistent pattern over time confirms the behavior’s recurrence).

Regarding
risk-taking
behaviour, the
Balloon Analogue Risk Task
(BART)
(
230
)
is a widely used behavioral measure for assessing this aspect. In the BART, participants inflate a virtual balloon to earn rewards, balancing potential gains against the risk of the balloon bursting, which would result in losing the accumulated earnings for that balloon. This task simulates real-world risk-taking by forcing participants to weigh uncertain outcomes. The test can predict real-world risk behaviors in domains like health, safety, and addiction.

7.7.
Social and Organizational Factors

Attitude towards policies
is typically measured by surveying users’ attitudes about following security rules. For example, Herath and Rao
(
167
)
used a set of Likert statements to capture policy attitudes (e.g. “If I follow the organization’s Information Security policies, I can make a difference in helping to secure [it].”) and reported high reliability in their scale. A recent instrument is the
Attitudes toward Security Recommendations (ASR)
scale, a 9-item survey designed to measure employees’ attitudes toward organizational security recommendations along two distinct dimensions: perceived legitimacy and effectiveness (how right, useful, legitimate the recommendations seem) and perceived rigor (how strict, demanding, or burdensome they are). The scale has demonstrated good psychometric properties and shows that these two dimensions differently predict people’s intentions to follow security recommendations
(
398
)
. The
Theory of Planned Behavior

(
9
)
can be used to construct policy compliance scales, as it has been done extensively in the literature
(
373
)
. Sommestad et al.
(
373
)
reviewed the literature and proposed a 50-item questionnaire that measures eleven variables regarding intention to comply with information security policies. Finally, the already cited HAIS-Q
(
309
)
can be used to measure employees’ attitude towards security policies.

Lack of resources
often refers to a lack of support and organizational learning, which can be estimated by using the
Perceived Organizational Support
(POS)
(
121
)
. It asks employees if they feel the organization genuinely cares about their well-being and helps them do their job effectively. While not security-specific, a high POS generally implies that the organization provides resources (training, help) — indirectly fostering learning. There are also “learning organization” surveys like the 43-item
Dimensions of the Learning Organization Questionnaire
(DLOQ)
(
260
)
and its shorter 21-item version
(
443
)
that measure continuous learning and support.

The concept of “
trust
” can refer to trust in technology or trust in people/organizations. In the interpersonal sense,
Rotter’s Interpersonal Trust Scale

(
349
)
is a classic, validated questionnaire comprising 25 items that assesses general trust in others. In an organizational context, the
trust inventory
by Mayer et al.
(
264
)
,
McAllister’s trust scale

(
265
)
, or
Organizational Trust Inventory (OTI)

(
213
)
can be used to measure employees’ trust in management and coworkers – these are validated in organizational psychology. For trust in technology (e.g., emails or software), scales have been adapted as well (e.g., Jian et al.’s
Trust in Automation
(TiA) scale
(
194
)
or
McKnight et al. measures

(
269
)
).

Adherence to
norms
is commonly evaluated via subjective items from models like the
Theory of Planned Behavior

(
9
)
. A typical approach is to ask respondents whether people important to them (or colleagues) expect them to behave securely, and whether others do so (descriptive norm). These 5-point Likert scale items have been used in many information security compliance studies and shown significant effects
(
166
)
. Another approach involves building measurement items based on the
Focus Theory of Normative Conduct
by Cialdini
(
82
)
, which categorizes norms into perceived common behavior (descriptive) or perceived social approval (injunctive). Additionally, the
Consumer Susceptibility to Interpersonal Influence
(CSII) scale
(
288
)
is a validated 12-item measure with two subscales: Normative influence (8 items) and Informational influence (4 items). This questionnaire, although originally devised for marketing purposes, may be repurposed to assess how the behaviors of workplace peers influence an individual.

Evaluation of
security posture
is a higher-level assessment typically done via audits or composite indices rather than a single survey. One example is
GoSafe

(
12
)
, an auditing and ranking system designed to assess an organization’s overall security posture. GoSafe produces a score based on multiple checkpoints (policies, controls, incident history). It is a more technical measure and a proprietary approach. The
Attitudes Towards Cybersecurity and cybercrime in Business
(ATC-IB) questionnaire
(
154
)
can measure an organization’s security posture with items such as "I don’t have the right skills to be able to protect the organization from cybercrime". Moreover, Parsons et al.
(
309
)
note that the HAIS-Q can be used collectively to gauge an organization’s human security posture. Finally, the
Global Cyber Risk Perception Survey
can be administered to organizations’ leaders to gain insights into the cybersecurity posture of their organizations
(
259
)
.

Social influence
can be measured by susceptibility to common persuasion tactics. The
Susceptibility to Persuasion-II scale
(StP-II)
(
276
)
is a psychometric tool that covers social influence mechanisms, designed to measure an individual’s general susceptibility to persuasion, particularly in the context of scams and fraudulent offers. It essentially reveals how easily an individual can be persuaded by various strategies, and it has been specifically used in cybersecurity research to understand who is more likely to be scammed. A shorter alternative questionnaire for this factor is the
Normative Influence subscale

(
288
)
. A different solution is the
Affective and Normative Commitment
scales, which assess the social influence factor, specifically focusing on organizational commitment and loyalty
(
16
)
. It measures the emotional attachment to the institution and the internalization of moral obligations regarding organizational loyalty.

Social proof
, a specific type of social influence where people follow the crowd, can be assessed with scenario-based questions. There is no standalone “social proof scale” by name, but the StP-II scale mentioned includes a sub-component for Social Influence susceptibility
(
276
)
.

8.
Analytical Walkthroughs of the Framework

While MORPHEUS serves as a robust conceptual framework, its true value can be better realized when it is applied to mitigate vulnerabilities in the complex reality of organizational security. To bridge the gap between theory and practice, we proposed a
Human-Centric Risk Assessment Process
. This logic guides analysts in identifying and mitigating vulnerabilities through three core phases, which can be adapted to specific operational workflows (e.g., prevention, forensics, or red teaming):

(1)

Factor Identification & Filtering:
The process begins by retrieving the full set of human factors associated with a specific threat via the framework’s mapping (Table
1
). The analyst then applies a
contextual filter
—based on environmental constraints (e.g., time pressure), user roles (e.g., experts vs. novices), or incident history—to prioritize the subset of factors most likely to be active drivers, thereby preventing analysis paralysis.

(2)

Quantification & Assessment:
The selected subset is assessed using the validated instruments provided in the Measurement Layer (Table
10
) or through qualitative methods (e.g., interviews, log analysis) appropriate for the context.

(3)

Systemic Analysis:
The interactions between these identified factors are analyzed using the network mechanisms (Section
6
) to identify and disrupt causal feedback loops.

To demonstrate this process, we present
eight
synthetic operational scenarios. These are illustrative vignettes constructed by synthesizing common patterns found in our literature review (e.g., healthcare workflows, administrative fatigue). They utilize a “Pattern Language” format to distinctly separate the procedural method from the narrative application.

Since MORPHEUS represents a comprehensive synthesis of validated constructs rather than a singular hypothesis, validating the entire framework in a single empirical study is infeasible. Therefore, the goal of this section is not empirical validation, but rather to demonstrate the framework’s descriptive power and operational utility. We employ analytical vignettes—synthesized from common patterns identified in our literature review (e.g.,
(
186
;
295
;
445
)
)—to demonstrate how MORPHEUS enables practitioners to identify complex risks that unidimensional models may overlook.

8.1.
Scenario A: Human-Centric Risk Diagnosis

Challenge:
Organizations often face “mystery recurrences”—incidents that keep happening despite robust technical controls. In these cases, MORPHEUS shifts the diagnostic lens from a generic “user error” label to specific environmental deficits.

Vignette: Phishing in a Clinical Setting

Context:
A high-pressure hospital environment faces repeated spear-phishing compromises despite technical filters. This context is consistent with findings on the high vulnerability of healthcare staff due to workload
(
186
;
295
)
.

Identification & Filtering:
Consulting Table
1
for “Phishing” yields over 20 potential factors. However, given the context of
emergency wards
and
shift work
, the security team deprioritizes general personality traits (which are stable and hard to change) and filters for situational modulators. They select
Vigilance
,
Stress
, and
Decision Fatigue
as the primary candidates for investigation
(
445
)
.

Diagnosis:
Qualitative validation (interviews) confirms that staff are knowledgeable but suffer from acute decision fatigue during night shifts.

Systemic Loop:
The interaction analysis reveals a critical feedback loop aligning with the
Cognitive-Emotional Bottleneck (Mechanism
6
)
: high
Stress
(Emotional) degrades
Vigilance
(Cognitive). This is exacerbated by rigid
Authority Norms
(Social) and fear of
Shame
that discourage junior staff from questioning doctors’ emails, triggering the
Silence Loop (Mechanism 8)

(
341
)
.

Outcome:
The intervention shifts from “more training” to environmental redesign: altering email gateways to flag external senders more aggressively during high-stress shifts.

8.2.
Scenario B: Guiding Targeted and Ethical Interventions

Challenge:
One-size-fits-all training is often inefficient and perceived as punitive. By distinguishing between direct drivers and modulators, MORPHEUS enables ethical, targeted interventions.

Vignette: Adaptive Training for University Staff

Context:
A university needs to reduce click-rates on phishing emails among administrative staff without disrupting workflows with heavy training
(
126
)
.

Identification & Filtering:
The team focuses on factors that drive
immediate
clicking behavior rather than deep psychological traits. They select
Impulsivity

(
154
)
and
Cognitive Reflectiveness
as the key factors.

Measurement:
Baselining the population with the
Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT)

(
131
)
reveals a specific user cluster characterized by high Impulsivity and low Reflectiveness
(
423
)
.

Outcome:
Instead of assigning generic training videos to everyone, the system deploys a specific technical control for this cluster: friction-inducing pop-ups (“Are you sure?”) that appear only when clicking links, designed to forcibly engage System 2 thinking and counteract the rapid processing typical of the
Habitual Autopilot Loop (Mechanism
6
)
.

8.3.
Scenario C: Risk Prioritization via Threat Mapping

Challenge:
Security resources are finite. Organizations need to prioritize mitigations based on the specific human factors that trigger their most critical attack vectors.

Vignette: Dashboard Design for Cloud Services

Context:
UX designers are building a new interface for cloud resource management used by sysadmins, a group prone to specific configuration errors
(
330
)
.

Identification & Filtering:
Using Table
1
for “Misconfiguration,” the team filters for factors relevant to
expert users
. They prioritize
Overconfidence
(common among experts
(
183
)
) and
Recurrence
, while deprioritizing
Lack of Knowledge
.

Mapping:
The design team maps the critical threat of accidental server deletion to these specific traits, identifying a susceptibility to the
Trust and Bias Overconfidence Trap (Mechanism
6
)
.

Outcome:
Recognizing that expert users are prone to
Overconfidence

(
420
)
, the team removes “one-click” deployments. They introduce a “cognitive wedge”—a mandatory confirmation dialog requiring the user to type the resource name with actionable mitigations
(
254
)
—to break the habit (

8.4.
Scenario D: Monitoring with Validated Tools

Challenge:
“Security culture” is often a buzzword. MORPHEUS transforms it into a measurable metric to track progress over time.

Vignette: Periodic Screening in Finance

Context:
A bank wants to assess the effectiveness of its security awareness program without causing employee burnout, a critical issue in high-compliance sectors
(
7
)
.

Measurement:
The Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) selects the
Big Five Inventory-2
and
HAIS-Q
(Table

(

Insight:
Hypothetical data reveal a specific department with high
Neuroticism
and low
Self-Efficacy

. This specific profile breaks the

Outcome:
The Chief Information Security Officer halts planned phishing simulations for that group (which would be counterproductive

8.5.
Scenario E: Psychological Red Teaming

Challenge:
Traditional Red Teaming focuses on technical flaws. MORPHEUS provides a menu of psychological vulnerabilities to test the “human surface.”

Vignette: The “Friday Afternoon” Attack

Design:
Instead of random attacks, the Red Team filters for factors exacerbated by the

They send CEO fraud emails timed strictly to arrive at 5:00 PM on Friday, leveraging the desire to clear the inbox (cognitive load)

Post-test interviews reveal that

8.6.
Scenario F: Blameless Post-Incident Forensics

Challenge:
Avoiding the “blame and shame” culture requires understanding the user’s psychological state at the moment of error.

Vignette: Ransomware Patient Zero

Reconstruction:
Forensic analysts use the MORPHEUS dimensions as a checklist. They identify that the incident occurred during a major software migration, a known stressor

Contextualization:
Filtering for factors relevant to

The root cause is identified not as individual negligence, but as a systemic failure involving a

8.7.
Scenario G: Breaking the Habitual Loop in Access Control

Challenge:
Employees persistently reuse passwords across internal and external services despite rigorous policy updates. Traditional enforcement leads to “shadow IT” workarounds.

Vignette: The Credential Stuffing Incident

Context:
Following a credential stuffing attack, audits reveal that 40% of staff reuse corporate passwords on personal sites.

Identification:
The CISO filters for behavioral factors and identifies
Recurrence
(Habits) as the primary driver, engaging the

Diagnosis:
Interviews reveal that password reuse is not due to

Outcome:
Instead of punitive training, the organization deploys a password manager with forced “contextual friction” (e.g., a UI that visually flags reused passwords in real-time). This disrupts the habitual loop at the moment of creation, forcing a System 2 decision
(

)
within the

8.8.
Scenario H: Insider Threat Pre-emption

Challenge:
Mitigating risks from “high-performing but deviant” insiders, where technical skill masks deeper behavioral vulnerabilities.

Vignette: The “Privileged” Data Leak

Context:
An expert system administrator unilaterally bypasses safety protocols to rush a deployment, believing standard procedures are unnecessary bureaucratic hurdles for someone of their skill level.

Identification:
Standard evaluations show high
Security Self-Efficacy
and perfect training scores (Knowledge). However, targeted profiling identifies high
Narcissism

Systemic Analysis:
This specific combination triggers the

)
. The framework reveals that the violation is driven by
narcissism
(specifically, a sense of entitlement) rather than ignorance: the user’s negative

stems from a belief that rules apply only to “lesser” users, not to experts

Outcome:
Recognizing that awareness training cannot correct personality-driven negligence, the strategy pivots from

8.9.
Limitations

It is essential to recognize that the proposed scenarios are theoretical vignettes. While grounded in empirical evidence, the framework has not yet undergone longitudinal ecological validation. Future work must address three critical challenges in operationalizing MORPHEUS:

•

Ethical Profiling:
Collecting psychometric data (e.g., Neuroticism in Scenario D) raises significant privacy concerns. Practitioners must ensure data is aggregated/anonymized to prevent discrimination and comply with regulations like GDPR.

•

Interdisciplinary Skill Gap:
Cybersecurity professionals may lack the expertise to interpret psychological scales correctly. Successful implementation likely requires collaboration with HR or organizational psychologists to avoid misdiagnosis.

•

Temporal Dynamics:
Factors like
Stress
or
Vigilance
are highly volatile. Interventions based on static measurements may lose effectiveness if not updated dynamically in response to real-time context (e.g., time of day, workload spikes).

9.
Conclusions and future works

This paper introduced MORPHEUS, a holistic framework that systematizes 50 human factors across cognitive, emotional, behavioral, personality, demographic, and social dimensions to address the human-centric vulnerabilities underlying the majority of security breaches. By synthesizing psychological theories—specifically, the CAB model and Attribution Theory—with cybersecurity practice, we mapped these factors to specific critical threats, identified 295 interactions, and consolidated 99 validated measurement tools. This extensive taxonomy, achieved through a novel hybrid methodology that combines AI-assisted analysis with expert validation, provides the operational granularity often lacking in previous high-level models.

Future research will focus on extending the framework’s ecological validity. A priority is the systematic mapping of external triggers (e.g., deceptive UI patterns, social engineering cues, environmental stressors) to specific human factors. Understanding this causality is crucial: while MORPHEUS currently models the user’s
internal
susceptibility, identifying how
external
stimuli activate these vulnerabilities will enable the design of context-aware defenses that can neutralize triggers before they precipitate a failure. Additionally, we plan to pursue empirical validation of the identified feedback loops in longitudinal settings and develop adaptive security interfaces that can respond to real-time biometric indicators.

APPENDIX 1: Description of the MORPHEUS human factors

In the following, each human factor is described to clarify its meaning in the context of this study. Of course, human factors whose meaning is trivial (i.e., age and gender) are not described.

A1.1 Demographic factors

Education
refers to the level of formal schooling acquired by an individual (e.g., high school, undergraduate, postgraduate) and the specific disciplinary field of study (e.g., STEM vs. non-STEM).

A1.2 Personality traits

Agreeableness
denotes a propensity for cooperative, empathetic, and trusting conduct in social contexts
(
455
)
. Individuals who rate high on this dimension are attached to interpersonal harmony and positive relations and are usually kind, considerate, and willing to help others
(
145
)
. Conversely, individuals exhibiting limited agreeableness tend to remain skeptical, critical, and competitive, which can influence relational interpretation, intent inference, and interpersonal responsiveness.

Conscientiousness
is concerned with organisation, self-discipline, and goal-directedness. Highly conscientious individuals tend to be reliable, structured, and task-oriented, with plans and a tendency to stick to regular practices
(
455
)
. These individuals possess high impulse control and a strong commitment to task completion over the long term. Low conscientiousness, conversely, is associated with spontaneity, reduced organization, and greater openness to flexible or adaptive decision-making.

Extraversion
denotes the degree to which individuals seek and sustain social engagement. Individuals scoring high in extraversion are characterised by vigour, extroverted demeanour, and assertiveness, actively pursuing external stimuli and interacting with large cohorts
(
455
)
. In comparison, introverts tend to prefer solitude or smaller, more intimate environments. This dimension influences the trends of social participation and the climate where the interaction takes place.

Greed
denotes a dispositional personality tendency to pursue material or symbolic rewards, such as money, prizes, or advantages, often prioritizing immediate gratification over cautious or secure behavior. Within cybersecurity contexts, higher levels of greed increase susceptibility to attacks that exploit promises of financial gain or exclusive benefits, such as prize-based phishing or fraudulent reward schemes.

Openness
to experience describes the extent to which an individual engages with imaginative, intellectually exploratory, emotionally expressive, art- and beauty-sensitive, and idea-oriented stimuli. High openness is associated with the enjoyment of abstract conceptualization, novel experiences, and creative endeavors, whereas low openness indicates a preference for routine, familiarity, and concrete thought. McCrae and Costa
(
94
)
delineate six facets within openness: fantasy, aesthetics, feelings, actions, ideas, and values.
This dimension also encompasses the construct of
sensation seeking
, which reflects a dispositional tendency to pursue novel, intense, or stimulating experiences.

Narcissism
denotes a personality trait characterized by an inflated view of the self, a heightened need for admiration, recognition, and status, and a sense of entitlement. It involves a focus on self-enhancement and maintaining a grandiose self-image.

Neuroticism
represents a dispositional tendency toward negative affective states such as anxiety, stress, mood instability, and self-doubt
(
34
)
. Conversely, low neuroticism—often referred to as
emotional stability
—reflects resilience, composure, and the ability to maintain equilibrium in the face of adverse or stressful conditions.

A1.3 Cognitive factors

Bias
, in general, denotes systematic departures from rational judgment that influence perception, memory, and decision-making processes
(
217
)
. Cognitive biases lead individuals to draw conclusions based on subjective or heuristic reasoning rather than objective evaluation. These biases influence information processing and can lead to persistent errors in judgment.

Cognitive fatigue
constitutes a state of diminished mental capability that manifests following prolonged engagement with cognitively demanding tasks. It is usually manifested through diminished attention, reduced processing speed, and overall decline in cognitive effectiveness
(
30
)
. Such performance losses may lead to a transition between deliberate and automatic operations, thereby impairing performance later.

Cognitive reflectiveness
denotes the propensity to override intuitive, heuristic-based judgments — commonly referred to as System 1 thinking, which operates automatically and rapidly — and to engage instead in deliberate, analytical reasoning (System 2 thinking), which is slower and more effortful
(
198
)
. Individuals with higher cognitive reflectiveness are less influenced by affect-driven or superficial cues and show improved discrimination between legitimate and fraudulent content, particularly in phishing contexts.

Cyber risk beliefs
denote individuals’ general beliefs about the likelihood and potential severity of cyber threats. Rather than reflecting procedural knowledge or momentary awareness, CRBs capture a stable cognitive stance toward cyber risk that shapes motivation and protective intentions.

Cybersecurity self-monitoring
(metacognitive).
Cybersecurity self-monitoring refers to an ongoing metacognitive process in which individuals track their own behavior, attention, and adherence to secure practices (e.g., verifying sender identity, checking URLs, flagging anomalies). Unlike
vigilance
, which concerns sustained attention to external cues, self-monitoring emphasizes reflexive oversight of one’s own actions and standards.

Decision fatigue
is operationally distinct yet conceptually proximate; it refers to diminished decision quality after a sequence of choices
(
324
)
. When resources are scarce, people struggle to effectively compare and contrast the available alternatives, and the risk of making an impulsive judgment or avoiding the decision increases.

Distraction
refers to an individual’s tendency to divert attention from a primary task. Such attentional shifts can arise from environmental stimuli, cognitive overload, or competing demands, thereby reducing the ability to maintain sustained concentration. Highly distractible individuals may struggle to concentrate, particularly in situations that require focused attention.

Lack of awareness
denotes an individual’s limited capacity to perceive salient elements of the environment or to observe aspects of their own behavior
(
118
)
. The causal mechanisms frequently involve a lack of exposure to the environment, inadequate training, or an inability to attend to relevant cues
(
40
)
. Awareness must be distinguished from knowledge, which refers to the explicit understanding, rather than perception.

Lack of knowledge
, conversely, signifies an absence of requisite information or expertise. Unlike misperception, the interpretation of a stimulus as other than it manifestly is, lack of knowledge reflects an inability to access or apply necessary information during decision-making.

Misperception
occurs when individuals interpret information incorrectly, resulting in discrepancies between perception and reality. This phenomenon can stem from cognitive biases, inadequate information processing, or external influences that distort understanding
(
192
)
. Misperception differs from lack of knowledge in that it involves an active distortion of information rather than a simple absence of comprehension.

Overconfidence
represents a cognitive bias characterized by an exaggerated belief in one’s abilities, judgments, or performance outcomes. It is usually expressed in the form of overestimates of the abilities, overrating in comparison with others, or great accuracy in the perceived accuracy
(
217
)
. By attenuating the inclination to solicit external validation or reassess one’s assumptions, overconfidence can foster inflexible decision-making.

Risk attitude
, by contrast, refers to an overarching dispositional orientation toward uncertainty, ranging from pronounced risk aversion to a propensity toward high-stakes or uncertain situations
(
168
)
. Whereas risk-taking denotes actions, risk attitude represents a broader personality trait that guides perceptions and strategies across multiple life domains.

Security self-efficacy
is an individual’s belief in their capability to recognize, avoid, and handle security threats such as phishing. Higher security self-efficacy supports systematic checking (sender identity, URL inspection, attachment types), fosters adherence to protective routines, and predicts better avoidance behavior
(
26
)
.

Uncertainty
denotes a state in which ambiguous outcomes or missing information render decision-making more complex. Individuals confronted with uncertainty may exhibit hesitation, rely on heuristics, or experience increased cognitive load, each of which influences how information is processed and responded to
(
224
)
.

Vigilance
denotes an individual’s capacity to maintain sustained attention and remain alert to relevant stimuli over extended periods. High vigilance is associated with strong attentional control and the capability to detect inconsistencies or patterns. Conversely, low vigilance may lead to lapses in focus and diminish the ability to recognize important cues
(
424
)
.

A1.4 Emotional factors

Anxiousness
refers to a future-oriented emotional state marked by heightened alertness, uncertainty, and physiological arousal. Anxiety is typically described as the worrying and expectation of negative future states, but it extends beyond the immediate dangers of the situation, influencing anticipatory appraisals and subsequent behavior
(
37
)
.

Digital anxiety
denotes apprehension, tension, or worry specifically associated with the use of digital systems, technologies, and online services. Elevated digital anxiety increases cognitive load and narrows attention to surface cues, fostering reliance on heuristics (e.g., urgency, familiarity) and hastier, less accurate judgments; as a result, it can impair the detection of deceptive content (e.g., phishing) and reduce willingness to engage with protective actions (e.g., verifying URLs, reporting).
Unlike
anxiousness
, which represents a broad, future-oriented emotional state related to general uncertainty or anticipation of negative outcomes,
digital anxiety
is context-specific and arises directly from the interaction with technology and online environments.

Fear
constitutes a fundamental defensive emotion elicited in response to the apprehension of immediate or proximal danger. The related physiological indicators are autonomic arousal, behavioural avoidance, and quick decision-making, aiming to reduce possible damage
(
37
)
. In contrast to anxiety, which is characterised by future orientation and broad, diffuse anticipation, fear is typically tied to discrete and imminent threats.

Frustration
arises when an individual’s efforts to attain a goal are repeatedly obstructed
(
191
)
. Associated with irritability and tension, particularly when affected persons perceive little control over impeding factors, frustration differs from stress in that it is specifically linked to blocked goal attainment rather than generalised pressure or demands.

Shame
, a self-conscious affect, is linked to perceptions of personal failure, inadequacy, and social disapproval
(
62
)
. Distinct from guilt, which is anchored to specific behaviours, shame carries a more encompassing self-evaluation. Intensity levels of shame exert influence over social engagement, disclosure behaviour, and self-perception.

Stress
, in turn, emerges from external or internal pressures that challenge an individual’s capacity to maintain equilibrium
(
380
)
. Although moderate stress can enhance alertness and problem-solving, excessive or chronic stress impairs cognitive processing, amplifies emotional reactivity, and leads to fatigue. Unlike anxiety, which stems from anticipation, stress is a reaction to specific present-day demands or contingencies.

A1.5 Behavioral factors

Complacency
involves a sense of self-satisfaction that causes the reduction of awareness of risks or deficiencies
(
272
)
. Unlike laziness, which arises through lack of motivation, complacency reflects an unfounded belief in the sufficiency of prior effort.

Compulsive behavior
is a repetitive action that is perceived as being compulsory by the individual, usually in relation to an intrusive thought or an urge to have control over a situation
(
98
)
. In contrast to impulsivity, which is spontaneous and unplanned, compulsive behavior is purposeful and regularly performed to mitigate discomfort. People with compulsive behaviors can feel that they do not need too many routines, but cannot stop them.

Impulsive behaviour
stems from a predisposition to act quickly without adequate deliberation of consequences and frequently involves difficulties in delayed gratification and resisting immediate urges
(
32
)
. Impulsive behavior differs from risk-taking: it is not necessarily driven by the anticipation of rewards, but rather stems from temporary difficulties in self-regulation.

Internet addiction
denotes a pathological behavioral pattern characterized by compulsive, excessive, and uncontrolled use of the Internet. Distinguished from high-frequency use (
Internet usage
), it involves specific psychological symptoms, such as salience, tolerance, withdrawal, and the persistence of online engagement despite negative consequences on daily life or psychosocial functioning
(
448
)
.

Internet usage
refers to the amount of time an individual spends engaging with online environments and digital services (e.g., email, social media, browsing, streaming). Higher Internet usage increases exposure to potential cyber threats and reinforces habitual message processing, thereby expanding the opportunity for deceptive contact unless counterbalanced by strong awareness and self-regulatory controls. Extreme levels of internet usage can degenerate into internet addiction behaviors.

Laziness
is characterized by an aversion to effortful activity despite having the capacity to perform it, a pattern associated with diminished productivity and influenced by factors such as perceived lack of control, task disinterest, or external circumstances that curtail engagement
(
249
)
.

Recurrence
designates the re-emergence of a previously exhibited behavioral pattern following a period of change or remission, a pattern frequently observable in individuals who relapse to unhealthy habits or emotional reactions
(
109
)
. The term differs with mere repetition in that it refers to the renewal of actions that have been repressed or altered.

Risk-taking
is defined as a behavioral orientation that involves engaging in actions susceptible to adverse consequences. It is often motivated by personal differences in reward sensitivity, whereby some people perceive gains as greater than losses
(
382
)
. Unlike
risk attitude
, which reflects a dispositional preference toward uncertainty and the cognitive evaluation of risky options,
risk-taking
denotes the actual enactment of such preferences in specific contexts, influenced by situational and psychological moderators such as impulsivity and perceived control
(
370
;
427
)
. This construct also diverges from impulsivity insofar as risk-taking can be deliberate rather than spontaneous.

A1.6 Organizational and social factors

Attitude toward policies
captures an individual’s evaluative stance toward formal regulations and organizational measures. This position is shaped by political beliefs, life experiences, and views of policy success
(
412
)
. Individuals who regard a policy as advantageous may actively support it, whereas those who deem it restrictive or ineffective may resist or challenge it
(
412
)
. Policy attitudes influence the manner in which individuals engage with institutional rules and the extent to which they comply with or contest regulations.

Lack of communication
occurs when information is neither shared nor interpreted effectively, thereby generating misunderstandings, inefficiency, and diminished coordination within collaborative contexts
(
118
)
. Communicative difficulties may arise due to unclear messages, unwillingness to share important details, or knowledge gaps.

Lack of resources
refers to conditions in which individuals or organizations do not possess the requisite tools, support structures, training opportunities, or informational assets necessary for effective and secure action
(
118
)
. This deficit can manifest as limited technical assistance, insufficient time, inadequate materials, or the absence of an institutional culture that promotes continuous learning and knowledge sharing. In contrast, environments that provide organizational support and foster collective learning enhance members’ ability to respond effectively to security challenges by developing adaptive strategies based on shared experience and expertise.

Lack of trust
denotes diminished confidence toward individuals, institutions, or systems. Collaboration and cooperation cannot occur without trust; without it, communication and effective decision-making are hindered
(
137
)
. These shortcomings can result from past negative experiences, inconsistent leadership, or system inefficiencies. A lack of trust in organizational systems or institutions can foster skepticism, rejection of directives, and withdrawal from collective endeavors.

Norms
denote the collective expectations operative within a group or society, providing guidelines that delineate acceptable from unacceptable conduct
(
118
)
. This is because such expectations create social stability and order. In organizational contexts, norms govern workplace interactions, decision-making processes, and adherence to policies. They are informal or formal rules that maintain uniformity among people who are in the same social or professional setting.

Online exposure
denotes the extent to which an individual’s personal information, credentials, or digital traces are accessible or stored across interconnected online systems and platforms. High exposure—whether through public sharing or centralized storage—heightens the likelihood of data exploitation, profiling, or targeted attacks (e.g., credential theft or account compromise), even when overall Internet usage remains limited.

Organizational tenure
indexes accumulated exposure to training, norms, and incident learning. Increased tenure can strengthen protective routines and pattern recognition for anomalies.

Security posture
refers to the overall security status of an individual or organization, based on the configuration, maintenance, and exposure of its digital assets and behaviors
(
12
)
. A robust security posture involves proactive risk management, sustained vigilance, and the implementation of best practices. Conversely, when security controls are perceived as inconvenient or effortful—due to complex configurations, frequent manual approvals, or excessive alerts—users may exhibit resistance, circumvention, or non-compliance, leading to weakened configuration hygiene and increased vulnerability.

Social influence
denotes the modification of behavior in response to external pressures from social groups, authority figures, or cultural expectations
(
83
)
. It encompasses conformity, compliance, and obedience, shaping individuals’ security practices through both explicit directives and implicit social norms.
Within organizational contexts, social influence also manifests through
organizational commitment and loyalty
, whereby individuals internalize collective values, identify with the institution, and align their behavior with its goals and security expectations.

Social proof
refers to the propensity for individuals to emulate the behavior observed in others
(
84
)
. People believe that most people possess a better understanding of the right lines of action. When uncertainty prevails, group behavior is often adopted to avert error. This inclination encourages conformity and strengthens patterns that have been largely accepted within a specific social environment.

Type of organization
influences exposure and control maturity through policies, training cadence, and incident learning culture, indirectly shaping users’ secure practices. Workers placed in institutions that have strict cybersecurity measures and frequent training sessions are likely to display safer practices compared to other workers.

Age interactions

Age was found to have several interactions with other human factors described in MORPHEUS.

Firstly, several studies have investigated the development of personality traits with age, yielding interesting findings. As one ages from adolescence to old age, traits such as conscientiousness and agreeableness tend to increase, while extraversion, openness, neuroticism, and narcissism decrease
(
376
;
15
;
229
)
. Moreover, a study on adolescents and young adults specifically observed a rise in openness and conscientiousness traits from 16 to 20 years old
(
407
)
, suggesting that openness does not decrease linearly across the lifespan.

Results from the literature highlight an unclear relationship between users’ age and their cybersecurity awareness and knowledge: some results highlight better knowledge and awareness among older people
(
353
;
365
;
457
;
339
)
, while others yield mixed results
(
54
)
or contradict this, e.g., with older women exhibiting the highest susceptibility
(
240
)
. Typically, older individuals self-report lower ICT skills and literacy, which may be due to them being under-confident in their cybersecurity knowledge
(
54
)
. The age of a user can also heighten their perception of the severity of cyberthreats and their perceived vulnerability
(
126
)
.
Age also has a direct effect on people’s mental resources. Cognitive fatigue, for example, is frequently reported in older adults
(
50
)
. Specifically, age was found to be an important factor in moderating the effects of cognitive issues (e.g., memory issues) according to personality traits. In older adults, lower extraversion, openness, and agreeableness were associated with more cognitive problems, together with higher neuroticism and conscientiousness, while this effect was reduced in middle-aged adults
(
29
)
. Nonetheless, older people have a greater tendency to perform accurate elaboration of information, e.g., reflecting more on phishing emails
(
136
)
, and generally be less impulsive
(
184
)
.

Education interactions

Education is another factor that can impact the cybersecurity resilience of users, particularly their awareness of cyberthreats. Surprisingly, a higher educational level does not correspond to higher awareness; in fact, there is evidence that the opposite is true, with undergraduate students showing better cybersecurity awareness than the post-graduate students

There is evidence that university education can even shape people’s personalities, indicating that university education can help develop traits of conscientiousness, agreeableness, and extraversion, especially in students from disadvantaged backgrounds

Finally, more educated people generally experience less anxiety while using technology

Bias and Cognitive reflectiveness interactions

Cognitive reflectiveness and Biases can be considered two intricately linked factors that affect susceptibility to cyberthreats. The Heuristic-Systematic Model of persuasion (HSM)
(
71
)
describes how people process persuasive information through either systematic or heuristic processing. The former involves a deep and careful analysis of the message’s arguments, whereas heuristic processing relies on mental shortcuts and simple cues (i.e., relying on biases).

Personality traits affect preferences towards using one or the other. Cognitive reflectiveness has been shown to be positively correlated with openness and conscientiousness
(
136
)
, while being negatively associated with low self-regulation levels (i.e., high neuroticism)
(
423
)
. Specifically, conscientious people are less likely to rely on heuristic processes in the context of phishing in social networks
(
130
)
. Other traits, including agreeableness, neuroticism, and openness, were instead found to be positively associated with both processing systems
(
130
)
.

Biased, heuristic-based processing is particularly relied upon during states of decision fatigue
(
324
;
383
)
or when time resources are limited
(
430
)
, which can lead to decisions yielding undesirable outcomes. On the other hand, cognitive reflectiveness lowers commitment to biases (such as conjunction fallacy and base-rate neglect biases)
(
20
;
80
)
and can affect the user’s cyber risk beliefs, heightening the perception of threats
(
285
)
. However, the cumulative effect of heuristic processing and recurrence of bad security habits may overwhelm the advantages coming from systematic processing
(
416
)
.

Technical knowledge and internet use have indeed been shown to increase cognitive reflection when elaborating emails
(
136
;
425
)
, as well as cybersecurity training
(
184
)
. Cognitive reflectiveness can be fostered by a perceived uncertainty when receiving suspicious emails in order to address missing information
(
416
)
. In economic contexts, irrational risk aversion and low-risk-taking behaviors can be limited by more cognitive reflection
(
235
)
.

Therefore, cognitive reflectiveness can enhance protection against cyber-attacks. However, it should be combined with security awareness messages or training
(
200
)
. Technical-based aids such as email alerts can indeed increase cognitive reflectiveness
(
431
)
.

Cognitive and decision fatigue interactions

In the context of phishing attacks, cognitive fatigue deriving from a high email load, combined with the recurrence of bad email habits, can increase user susceptibility
(
414
)
. Another relevant factor is vigilance (or sustained attention), which increases mental workload and cognitive fatigue over time
(
424
)
. However, focusing attention on specific elements, such as social influence or technical cues in phishing emails, can decrease the overall cognitive effort required for email processing
(
419
)
.
While too much cognitive fatigue can hinder decision-making, too little can have negative effects alike, leading to users self-interrupting or being distracted from their tasks if they have too many mental resources available
(
203
)
.

On the emotional side, anxiousness can contribute to decision fatigue, as socially anxious individuals are generally more hesitant when making decisions
(
165
)
. In general, anxiousness can occupy many cognitive resources, thereby increasing cognitive fatigue and hindering emotional and cognitive processing
(
49
)
.
Similarly, security fatigue—i.e., a state of mental and emotional exhaustion arising from repeated exposure to security demands—can lead to reduced productivity and increased anxiety
(
275
)
. Security fatigue can also lead to security-related decisions being driven by immediate motivations, relying on simplified heuristics, and acting impulsively
(
383
)
.

Cognitive fatigue can also affect individuals’ risk attitude, as mentally fatigued individuals tend to be more risk-averse when facing risk options in gambling scenarios
(
193
)
.
Finally, exposing users to training too frequently—especially when combined with other routine communications like health and safety information—can increase their workload and, subsequently, fatigue
(
338
;
431
)
.

Cybersecurity self-monitoring

People with higher levels of cybersecurity self-monitoring and self-regulation have been shown to be more likely to exhibit cognitive reflectiveness and resist impulsive reactions in the context of phishing susceptibility
(
423
)
. Moreover, increased self-monitoring can enhance resistance to social influence techniques and reduce reliance on biased heuristic processing
(
178
)
.

Cybersecurity self-monitoring is significantly influenced by security self-efficacy
(
220
)
; this means that the more users perceive they can handle threats, the more they will be able to control their cybersecurity behaviors. Additionally, the cyber risk beliefs of a user can increase their cybersecurity self-monitoring
(
220
;
261
)
. In the context of spear phishing, for example, expected negative outcomes such as feelings of fear or shame can improve behaviors and encourage users to report malicious emails more effectively
(
220
)
.

Cyber risk beliefs and Security self-efficacy interactions

Cyber risk beliefs and security self-efficacy are crucial factors that, in combination, shape individuals’ motivation to engage in cybersecurity behaviors (cf. Protection Motivation Theory
(
47
)
) and can lead to a more secure use of technology
(
220
;
228
;
409
)
. The cyber risk beliefs of a user can negatively affect their security self-efficacy
(
421
)
; this implies that, as perceived threats increase, the user will question their ability to cope effectively with them, while, if they are perceived as less severe, they may feel more assured in their ability to handle them.

Both security self-efficacy and cyber risk beliefs increase cognitive reflectiveness in users when dealing with suspicious emails
(
414
;
220

since more severely perceived threats increase the amount of cognitive resources users are willing to expend to stay safe. Security self-efficacy can reduce susceptibility to cyber threats, but the actual user’s knowledge and awareness significantly affect its impact
(
414
)
.
Moreover, security self-efficacy can be increased by workload (cognitive and decision fatigue), as more fatigued users perceive they can better comply with security regulations
(
445
)
, which may increase their susceptibility to cyberthreats.

At an organizational level, an employee’s security self-efficacy and cyber risk beliefs significantly impact their attitude towards security policies and compliance therewith
(
110
;
237
;
261
)
. Specifically, self-efficacy more effectively predicts a positive attitude toward policies for users with higher internet use experience
(
261
)
.
A very strong organizational security posture and large availability of security resources (e.g., specialist support) can lead employees to feel secure and perceive a low level of risk
(
431
)
. To improve their employees’ self-efficacy in phishing contexts, organizations can employ cybersecurity training
(
75
)
.

Lack of awareness and knowledge interactions

Users’ cybersecurity awareness and knowledge are crucial factors in determining susceptibility to cyber threats. User awareness of cyber threats affects their cyber risk beliefs in a complex manner, as it positively influences their perceived threat severity but negatively influences their perceived vulnerability
(
261
)
. This means that users with a high level of awareness may acknowledge the severity of a threat but not feel significantly threatened by it. Conversely, users are more aware of cyber threats that are perceived as genuinely dangerous to their personal security
(
338
)
.
On the other hand, high levels of knowledge and awareness are correlated with a high level of self-efficacy
(
261
;
26
)
.

There is proof that users who tend to use the Internet for personal reasons in the workplace may exhibit lower cybersecurity awareness
(
153
)
. Moreover, cybersecurity knowledge can also improve people’s behavior, as higher self-reported levels of security knowledge can lead to a decrease in taken risks
(
208
)
and be more vigilant about specific security indicators, such as phishing cues in emails
(
419
)
. A higher level of knowledge can even benefit users on an emotional level, as it can decrease their levels of digital anxiety
(
447
)
.

Technological support, such as security alerts for phishing emails, can increase users’ awareness of specific threats
(
431
)
. Moreover, employees tend to believe that educational support can benefit cybersecurity knowledge and awareness
(
342
)
. However, security training that is too frequent can lead to employees negatively reacting to educational resources and ultimately decreasing their security awareness levels
(
339
)
.
Even if employees’ knowledge is a critical factor in achieving better security overall, a review by Dawson and Thomson highlights that, in cybersecurity workforce contexts, users’ knowledge is not sufficient on its own but requires social intelligence (i.e., communication with peers) in combination
(
106
)
. An interesting finding suggests that exposure to the workplace (as little as one year) can negatively impact the cybersecurity behavior of employees and also affect their security knowledge
(
173
)
. This effect might be exacerbated for the long-tenure employees, such as managers
(
339
)
.

Overconfidence Interactions

From a personality perspective, overconfidence can be influenced by specific traits. Specifically, in the financial investments domain, there is evidence that people are more confident in their skills and rely less on unpredictable factors such as luck when they have higher values of conscientiousness
(
8
)
. Extraversion is another factor that influences overconfidence, with extroverted individuals generally being more optimistic about their knowledge, skills, and experience when making decisions
(
8
;
357
)
. Finally, neuroticism negatively influences overconfidence, as neurotic people generally possess lower self-esteem
(

)
and are less confident in their skills, thereby relying more on advice coming from others
(

)
.

In phishing email detection contexts, a user’s overconfidence can decrease if they are mentally fatigued
(

)
. Moreover, users who heavily rely on heuristic decision rules (i.e., biases) are more overconfident in their decisions
(

)
.

Uncertainty interactions

Uncertainty can lead to feelings of a lack of security in employees, who should instead be supported with actionable and concrete cybersecurity-related information (i.e., organizational resources)
(
342
)
, especially for employees with low organizational tenure, who may be more uncertain about work norms
(

)
.

Intolerance of uncertainty, i.e., the negative emotional attitude towards uncertain situations and events, can contribute to increased social anxiety
(
378
)
. Conversely, feelings of fear and anxiety can cause higher levels of uncertainty in users when dealing with phishing emails
(

)
.

Fear Interactions

Certain demographic groups are more prone to experiencing fear. In general, female individuals are more likely to be fearful than men
(
88
)
. Moreover, age affects the fear of cybercrime in a non-linear manner, with an increase from 18 years old until middle age and a decrease thereafter
(

)
.

Fear can have positive effects on the cybersecurity of an individual, such as more risk aversion

;

)
, reduced risk-taking
(

)
, and increased reliance on security organizational policies
(
7
)
. In this sense, fear can also be used by organizations as a strategy to foster compliance with information security policies through the use of
fear appeals
. However, their use is more effective when they are tailored to individual cognitive biases of employees
(

. Organizations also need to be aware of the side effects of fear appeals on long-term responses of employees, who can instead manifest a paralyzed attitude in response

)
. Moreover, the use of fear appeals can induce

On the other hand, a complete lack of fear in users can lead to low awareness and misperceptions, such as not feeling “important enough for anyone to want to take [their] information”

. Fear appeals can thus be useful to adjust employees’ cyber risk beliefs and security self-efficacy, if properly designed

Frustration interactions

Frustration is an emotional state that can severely compromise correct user behavior. Specifically, frustration was found to be associated with users’ cognitive and decision fatigue, with frustrated and fatigued users more likely to dismiss security warnings without due consideration
(
383
)
. Frustration can also negatively affect vigilance, with frustrated users being less likely to remain vigilant for sustained periods of time
(
424
)
.
Frustration also dictates whether an individual will self-interrupt during their tasks; in fact, some studies suggest that this can happen if the task is either too easy (in which case, the distraction happens out of boredom) or too hard (out of frustration)
(
203
)
.

When people have the misperception that they are not worth being attacked, they can feel frustrated if being told to protect themselves too frequently
(
383
)
.
In these cases, policies that are too frustrating or stringent can lead users to adopt lazy workarounds, such as writing passwords down on post-its
(
181
)
.
On the other hand, when users do not receive the proper supporting resources and information to use technological solutions, they can experience frustration, which can in turn decrease their self-confidence and self-efficacy
(
422
)
.

Shame interactions

Shame can be influenced by gender and age, with females being more prone to feelings of shame than males
(
252
)
, and younger individuals typically experiencing higher levels of shame
(
134
;
301
)
—although individuals over 50 may report increased shame compared to middle-aged individuals
(
301
)
.

Shame can lead to decreased communication when a person is too ashamed to ask for help, especially if they have a major lack of technical knowledge on the subject they need help with
(
422
)
. Sometimes employees may have a more positive attitude towards policies in order to avoid feelings of shame caused by non-compliance
(
159
)
. However, leveraging shame as a motivational factor does not generally improve the employee’s attitude
(
125
)
, and can even be counterproductive for the organization, resulting in self-protective or paralyzing behavior
(
56
)
. In fact, shame can lead to reduced levels of security self-efficacy
(
33
)
. Therefore, it is not generally recommended for organizations to use emotions such as guilt or shame as drivers for increased compliance.

Stress interactions

Stress can be chronically dictated by personality traits such as neuroticism, as neurotic individuals can experience stress at a higher intensity compared to other people
(
63
;
251
;
99
)
. Openness can also have a mitigating effect on stress
(
99
)
. However, Pflünger and colleagues argue that personality traits should be considered holistically to account for the finely granulated relationships; they indeed reported mixed effects of personality traits on stress, depending on the specific profile of individuals
(
323
)
.

Stress is not inherently a negative emotion, as it can actually serve as a driver for good behavior by increasing engagement with cybersecurity practices. This is the case of
challenge stress
or
eustress

(
366
)
, which proved to be correlated with emotions such as excitement and, ultimately, compliance with information security policies
(
72
;
7
)
. Moreover, eustress can also decrease emotions like anxiety and fear
(
72
)
.
In particular, stress has a complex relationship with anxiousness and affects the risk attitude of individuals based on their level of social anxiety: in low socially anxious people, stress fosters risk-taking, while in high socially anxious people, stress does not change their behavior and they remain risk-averse
(
165
)
.
In cybersecurity contexts, stress and anxiousness can jointly worsen cybersecurity behavior
(
275
)
.

However, stress in its negative sense (e.g.,
technostress
– the stress caused by technology) can decrease productivity, especially in male individuals
(
22
)
. Technostress, in particular, is more prevalent among younger, male individuals with low digital literacy
(
328
)
. Moreover, stress is strictly linked to uncertainty
(
22
)
and feelings of anxiousness
(
22
;
99
)
and fear
(
7
)
.

Stress is also strongly related to vigilance, as attentional tasks are very demanding for people, rapidly draining cognitive resources and increasing stress over time
(
424
)
. In fact, stress and cognitive fatigue are intertwined factors, as the former can increase the latter
(
49
;
99
;
22
)
and vice versa
(
392
)
. Frequent distractions and the norm of “being always available” can also increase stress in the workplace
(
392
)
.

On an organizational level, support and training resources can affect the stress of employees at two extremes: on the one hand, too frequent training can lead to user overload and possibly burnout; on the other hand, lack of support can increase stress, as employees may feel unable to deal with highly sophisticated technological solutions in the workplace
(
22
;
338
)
.

Impulsive behavior Interactions

Impulsive behaviors can stem from personality traits; in fact, impulsivity has been shown to be positively correlated with neuroticism, thereby leading to more impulsive behaviors
(
456
;
255
;
359
;
423
)
.
There is also evidence that gender may influence impulsive behaviors, as males tend to be more impulsive than females
(
113
)
.
Impulsivity is also significantly associated with increased internet addiction, as shown by several studies
(
113
;
351
;
262
)
.

Organizational tenure can reduce impulsive behaviors (e.g., when processing emails), as employed individuals may be more inclined to think carefully due to exposure to workplace norms, as opposed to students and unemployed users
(
184
)
.

Internet Addiction Interactions

Internet addiction has been widely studied in the psychology and cybersecurity literature.

Age has been found to be negatively associated with internet addiction, as younger people are the most affected
(
245
;
361
;
229
)
; however, this correlation might not be linear, as internet addiction was shown to increase in the 18–30 age range
(
113
)
. Gender also dictates internet addiction, as males are generally more susceptible to it than female users
(
113
;
196
;
138
)
, even if there is contrasting evidence
(
245
)
.

Under a personality perspective, neurotic people are generally more prone to exhibit increased internet addiction
(
327
;
298
;
196
;
205
;
394
;
361
)
. The other traits are instead generally correlated negatively with internet addiction
(
205
;
394
;
196
)
. However, there is some evidence showing that openness, extroversion, and conscientiousness can be heightening factors
(
298
)
.

Internet addiction has been shown to be correlated with higher levels of cognitive and decision fatigue
(
182
)
, shame
(
97
)
, anxiousness and stress
(
356
;
218
)
, but also with depression and compulsive behaviors such as substance abuse or online gambling
(
218
)
.
Other findings in the literature bear similar trends by showing positive correlations between social anxiety and internet addiction
(
116
)
. Internet addiction can also lead to social isolation and hinder communication skills
(
218
)
.

Risk-Taking Interactions

Impulsivity and internet addiction are factors that were shown to be significant predictors of riskier cybersecurity behaviors
(
154
;
456
)
. Moreover, more risk-taking behavior is linked to worse attitudes towards cybersecurity policies
(
154
;
155
)
.
Risk-taking behaviors have also been linked to lower levels of self-reported security knowledge
(
208
)
.

Laziness Interactions

Laziness can be manifested in various forms, such as procrastination. Studies have investigated the correlation between procrastination and the Big 5 traits, finding it is negatively correlated with agreeableness, conscientiousness, and extraversion, while being positively correlated with neuroticism
(
385
;
271
)
.

Attitude towards policies Interactions

As reported in the previous sections, employees’ attitudes towards their organization’s policies can be influenced by various factors. Another individual factor that can affect behavior includes the employees’ age, with younger users generally having a more positive attitude
(
292
)
.

Employees’ knowledge and awareness can improve their compliance with corporate policies
(
110
;
292
;
308
;
350
)
; a higher educational level among employees can further enhance the positive effect of their knowledge on policy compliance
(
292
)
.

To increase compliance, organizations must also address the lack of resources and support for their employees, for example, by creating training programs tailored to their roles and responsibilities, or by including the top management as an active part in security initiatives
(
110
)
.
Cognitive and cultural biases are also crucial factors in employee policy adherence and should thus be addressed individually by each organization
(
401
)
.
Finally, communication and relationships within the organization are critical in promoting more policy compliance
(
180
)
.

Lack of communication Interactions

Lack of communication with peers and technical support can increase susceptibility to cyberthreats. In fact, the combination of security self-efficacy, risk awareness, and social support has emerged as a powerful defensive synergy in the context of smartphone use
(
457
)
.

Communication is generally driven by the desire to warn or protect others from immediate threats that are either observed in others or experienced by oneself
(
104
)
. Therefore, communication can be encouraged by observing others’ behaviors (i.e., social norms), and can, in turn, improve social norms.

Support-seeking intentions can increase as organizations provide more training, leading to more frequent communications among peers
(
75
)
.
Emotions can hinder communication in the context of phishing reporting, as users might avoid reporting an email for which they fell victim to avoid embarrassment or for fear of getting colleagues into trouble (in case their account was compromised as part of a
lateral phishing
attack)
(
114
)
. Uncertainty about how to properly communicate a phishing attack may also prevent reporting
(
114
)
.

Lack of trust Interactions

Trust can be influenced by cognitive processes that occur naturally. The Truth-Default Theory poses that people tend to believe others by default, unless they have a reason to believe otherwise
(
233
)
. Previous experiences, in fact, create biases that influence trust in communications in online settings: a source that is considered trustworthy due to past communications will thus continue to be likely considered as such
(
430
)
. This can imply that familiarity with the sender and entities involved in a phishing email can increase trust and victims’ overconfidence in trusting the attacker
(
420
)
.

The level of trust in phishing emails can vary based on the recipient’s age (older users tend to be less trusting of emails) and the strength of their email habits and computer knowledge
(
67
;
13
)
. Moreover, the general perceptions and beliefs that people hold regarding the potential risks of online communications and technology in general—such as privacy concerns—can make users more suspicious
(
430
;
13
)
.

Emotional aspects also affect trust, as anxious individuals tend to be less trusting of others
(
348
)
.
Trust in security systems is strictly tied to employees’ perceived effectiveness of security measures and can ultimately increase their willingness to comply with policies
(
110
)
.

In social engineering contexts, trust is also affected by demographic characteristics of the attacker: for example, older individuals can appear to be more trustworthy and less likely to present a risk, while females are generally perceived as more trustworthy than males and can lead to more risk-taking in the victims
(
5
)
. However, these results can vary based on cultural factors that shape subjective perceptions.

Security posture Interactions

Security posture refers to the cybersecurity readiness/level of either an organization as a whole, or just considering its members (e.g., employees). The security posture of an organization can highly depend on whether top management provides proper support for security initiatives
(
176
)
.
Moreover, promoting good user behavior with solid organizational policies has a positive effect on employees’ attitude towards the organization and can increase security compliance
(
350
)
.

Social influence and Social Proof Interactions

Social influence is a complex aspect that can be influenced by several factors. Social influence techniques often leverage aspects such as time pressure (i.e., a lack of time resources), greed, and risk attitude of victims, who may be motivated by financial gains or a desire for challenge
(
430
)
.
Social influence techniques in social engineering attacks can lead target users to rely on heuristic processing to deal with, e.g., urgency and authority cues, and save cognitive effort
(
416
;
80
)
; in fact, they aim at diverting the victim’s attentional resources away from suspicious cues and leveraging biased processing strategies
(
414
)
. However, when social influence techniques trigger suspicion or use very convincing arguments, they can activate the more reflective cognitive system of users
(
80
;
285
;
414
)
.

Resistance to social influence can vary depending on the age of users, but the evidence is contradictory
(
386
;
389
)
; in fact, users of different ages may be susceptible to different social influence strategies
(
240
)
. Organizational tenure can also have varying effects on social influence susceptibility, with more senior employees being less susceptible to authority cues, but not to urgency cues, in contrast to middle-level employees
(
133
)
.

An individual’s personality traits can also increase their susceptibility to social influence. As already reported in Section
A2.1.2 Interactions with personality traits
, agreeable people are generally more vulnerable to social engineering due to their trusting nature
(
101
;
367
;
307
)
. Another trait that generally increases susceptibility to persuasion strategies and social influence is extraversion
(
307
;
101
)
, while people with higher values of neuroticism and openness are less affected by persuasion strategies
(
307
)
. However, there is evidence that higher traits of neuroticism, openness, and conscientiousness lead instead to increased susceptibility to social influence
(
306
)
.

Regarding social proof, a study by Coopamootoo and colleagues found that, when looking for security advice, women tend to rely more on social connections, while men prefer online content
(
90
)
. In general, social proof is a powerful factor in influencing employees’ behavior, especially those with low organizational tenure
(
431
)
. In fact, social pressure from peers and superiors impacts an employee’s behavior and positively (or negatively) influences their attitude and compliance with policies
(
180
;
76
;
350
)
. The social context and the impact of peers can also influence the beliefs and biases that people use for judgments, for example, when evaluating the quality of a web interface
(
374
)
.
Social proof can even impact people’s knowledge and security posture
(
173
)
; interestingly, this is especially true for individuals with higher education levels
(
173
)
.
