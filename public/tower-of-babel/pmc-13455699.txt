Early-season changes in concentric-only, long and short stretch–shortening cycle jump performance in highly trained youth basketball players
Authors: Tomás T Freitas, Stamatios Marinos, Georgios Koudounis, Konstantinos Spyrou, Elena Marín-Cascales
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn basketball, most studies analysing seasonal variations in neuromuscular performance through vertical jump testing rely solely on the countermovement jump (CMJ), a slow stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) test. This study aimed to analyse early-season changes in jumping performance in highly-trained youth basketball athletes, utilizing a comprehensive jump test battery to determine the time-course of adaptations in concentric-only qualities (using the squat jump, SJ), and slow (through the CMJ) and fast SCC function (via the drop jump and repeated jump tests, DJ and RJ).Materials and MethodsTwenty-six players (under-16: n = 14; age:14.9 ± 0.3 years; under-18: n = 12; age: 16.7 ± 0.4 years) from a high-level basketball academy volunteered to participate. In the first (T1) and final week (T2) of the pre-season, and four weeks into the in-season (i.e. week nine of the training period, T3), players completed the following tests on a contact mat on the same day: SJ, CMJ, DJ and RJ. To examine the progression of each vertical jump test across the selected different phases of the season, a repeated measures ANOVA with time (T1, T2 and T3) as factor was conducted.ResultsSJ and CMJ height improved only from T1 to T2 (both p < 0.001). DJ height remained constant, but shorter ground contact times (GCT) were observed in T2 and T3 when compared to T1 (p = 0.018 and p < 0.001, respectively). Moreover, significant and gradual increases were found in DJ reactive strength index (RSI) (T1 to T2: p = 0.039; T2 to T3: p < 0.030). In the RJ, significant improvements were found only for RSI between T2 and T3 (p = 0.005).ConclusionsReactive strength qualities and fast SSC performance peaked during the early in-season, while changes in concentric-only and slow SSC function (assessed with SJ and CMJ, respectively) occurred exclusively during the pre-season.KEY MESSAGESDifferent vertical jump tests showed distinct early-season changes in youth basketball.Squat (SJ) and Countermovement Jump (CMJ) performance improved only during the pre-season, while reactive strength qualities assessed via the Drop Jump (DJ) and Repeated Jumps peaked during the early in-season.DJ reactive strength index and ground contact times might be more sensitive than CMJ or SJ height to detect changes in neuromuscular capabilities in-season.Keywords: Integrative neuromuscular training, strength and conditioning, testing, vertical jump, team-sports

Introduction

Basketball is an intermittent team-sport that engages both the aerobic and anaerobic metabolic systems, in which players perform low-intensity movements (e.g. walking or jogging) interspersed with brief, high-intensity explosive actions (e.g. jumps, accelerations and decelerations, or changes of direction) during match-play [1–5]. It is for this reason that well-developed neuromuscular capabilities are fundamental in this sport, making a player’s ability to generate high power outputs during, for example, jumping actions a critical factor in determining performance outcomes [6]. Indeed, jumping ability is a crucial aspect in the game of basketball, as it significantly contributes to key essential actions such as shooting or rebounding [2]. Moreover, jumping performance has also been recently shown to discriminate players from different competitive levels (professional vs semi-professional players) and positional roles (guards vs forwards vs centers) [7,8]. Thus, vertical jump testing is common practice in real-world contexts, where sport scientists and practitioners collect data with the aim of obtaining meaningful information that could assist in player performance profiling, neuromuscular fatigue assessment and return to play processes [9].

In this regard, the countermovement jump (CMJ), squat jump (SJ), drop jump (DJ) and repeated jumps (RJ) are commonly used tests [10–13]. While the CMJ is the most widely employed for assessing jumping performance in basketball, due to its reliability, simplicity and time-efficiency [14], other jump tests, such as the SJ and DJ, may provide complementary insights into players’ jumping ability and stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) function [15,16]. In brief, and contrary to the CMJ, which evaluates slow SSC, the SJ isolates concentric-only capabilities, whereas the DJ and RJ assess fast SSC (i.e. <250 ms) and reactive strength [17], which are manifestations of force production more specific to the activity profile of basketball match-play. Therefore, using a combination of (complementary) jump tests might be a promising approach to gain a deeper knowledge of players’ neuromuscular capabilities (i.e. strength, power or SSC characteristics) and status (i.e. fatigue or readiness).

In sports, having information about performance fluctuations is essential for coaches to effectively manage training loads and optimize athletic performance [2,5,12,18,19]. Particularly in basketball, practitioners have long been interested in understanding whether vertical jump ability varies throughout the different phases of a competitive season, due to the different training goals and objectives associated with each phase. Previous research has shown that, as the season progresses (from pre-season to in-season), jump performance tends to improve [19–22]. However, most studies have focused on adult players and used exclusively the CMJ to evaluate jumping ability. Hence, limited evidence is available regarding how other jump types, such as the SJ or the DJ, fluctuate across the season, especially in high-level youth basketball players. These aspects are particularly relevant given the well-documented differences between youth and adults in several neuromuscular performance attributes (e.g. size-normalized strength and power, endurance, fatigability, and recovery) [23], which may contribute to age-related differences in the physiological adaptations to training as a consequence of maturation and hormonal regulation [24–27].

Undoubtedly, age and maturity play a significant role in athletes’ athletic and physical training development [28–31], in particular during adolescence. In this sensitive period, individuals experience rapid alterations due to natural growth and maturation and, while some may show remarkable increments in performance and adaptations, others may face difficulties and slower improvements trying to adapt to the physical demands of the game [29]. Therefore, a detailed analysis of how youth basketball players’ neuromuscular qualities change across a competitive season is paramount, and using a comprehensive jump test battery that assesses concentric-only capabilities (i.e. SJ), slow SSC (i.e. CMJ) and fast SSC and reactive strength (i.e. DJ and RJ) can offer valuable insights and help coaches design more effective and tailor-made training programs. However, to the best of author’s knowledge, no previous study has employed this approach in high-level academy basketball players.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to investigate the changes in jumping performance among highly-trained youth basketball players across different phases of a competitive basketball season (i.e. from pre-season to early in-season), utilizing a comprehensive testing battery that included the SJ, CMJ, DJ and RJ, to better understand the time-course of adaptations in concentric-only qualities, slow and fast SCC function, and reactive strength, respectively. It was hypothesized that SJ and CMJ would improve mainly from the beginning to the end of the pre-season, whereas DJ and RJ performance would peak during the early in-season.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed an observational, descriptive, repeated-measures design in which participants underwent a standardized testing battery at three distinct time-points of the basketball season (from August to October 2025): the start of the pre-season (week 1, T1), the end of the pre-season (week 5, T2), and during the early in-season phase (week 9, T3). Performance testing consisted of four vertical jump tests conducted on the same day with the following order: SJ, CMJ, DJ from a 40-cm height, and RJ. All tests were performed in the same indoor gym, under identical conditions, at the same time of the day, and using the same equipment. In T2 and T3, tests were scheduled 48 h after match day to ensure adequate recovery and minimize fatigue. Testing took place prior to the team’s scheduled resistance training session, in accordance with the weekly training plan. Athletes were familiarized with the vertical jumps performed as they were part of their routine assessments. Moreover, detailed instructions and feedback were provided to each individual to ensure proper technique and execution. Before the first test, a standardized general warm-up was completed, consisting of 10–15 min on-court activities, including full-body mobility exercises, dynamic movements across the court (i.e. running-based drills, such as, skipping, butt-kicks, or lateral shuffle), and bodyweight squats, lunges, and deadlifts. Participants

An a priori sample size estimation was performed on G*Power (version 3.1.9.2, Dusseldorf, Germany; http://www.gpower.hhu.de/) assuming a moderate effect size (ES) of f = 0.25 for a possible difference in CMJ height with respect to baseline, based on previous research [32]. Using a repeated-measures, within factors ANOVA as the statistical test, with an alpha level of 0.05, a power (1 − β) of 0.80, three measurements, and a correlation among repeated measures of 0.5, the estimated minimum sample size was 21 participants. For this reason, two youth basketball teams were recruited, and 32 adolescent male basketball players were initially included in the experimental procedures. For medical reasons or due to missing at least one testing session, 6 players were excluded. As a result, 26 post-peak height velocity players from both the U16 (n = 14; age: 14.9 ± 0.3 years; body height: 182.6 ± 6.8 cm; body mass: 72.5 ± 11.4 kg; training age: 6.4 ± 1.1 years) and U18 (n = 12; age: 16.7 ± 0.4 years; body height: 190 ± 6.7 cm; body mass: 82.8 ± 7.8 kg; training age: 8.1 ± 2.7 years) teams of a high-level European basketball academy participated in this study. All members of the U16 team competed in the highest-level National Domestic Basketball League (Ε.Σ.Κ.Α) of Athens, Greece. The players of the U18 team competed in both the National Domestic Basketball League (Ε.Σ.Κ.Α) of Athens and the Rising Stars League, a competition where top Greek teams play each other during the season alongside some selected teams from across Europe. Eligibility for study participation required valid medical certification, no major injuries sustained in the previous 6 months and full participation in all testing sessions. The study was approved by the UCAM Universidad Católica de Murcia Ethics Committee (code: CE102404) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to study enrollment, all athletes and their legal guardians received detailed written information outlining the study’s purpose, procedures, data privacy, participation requirements, and potential risks. As participants were minors, written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians, and written assent was obtained from all participating athletes.

Procedures

Squat jump

The SJ was the first test to be performed. Before starting it, players performed a jump-specific warm-up that consisted of three submaximal, progressive SJ trials. Following the warm-up, participants completed three maximal attempts on a contact platform (Chronojump Bosco System A2-DIN), separated by 15-s. For the correct execution of the test, athletes were instructed to hold a 90° knee flexion static position for ∼2-s, before executing a jump with no preparatory movement. During the aerial phase, actions that could artificially increase flight time (e.g. knee tuck) were not allowed. Arm swing was restricted throughout to isolate lower-extremity performance, and verbal encouragement was provided during all trials. Jump height was automatically calculated from flight time. The highest SJ was kept for analysis.

Countermovement jump

The CMJ was also performed after a jump-specific warm-up, on the same contact platform, with hands on hips. Athletes were instructed to perform a fast countermovement (to a self-selected depth) immediately followed by a complete extension of the legs and to land as close as possible to their initial take-off position. Jump height was calculated similarly to the SJ. A total of 3 trials were allowed, separated by 15-s of rest, and the best one was kept for analysis.

Drop jump

The DJ was executed from a 40-cm box. This height was selected because it was the one routinely used by the club to monitor its players. Moreover, a 40-cm drop height has been widely used in the literature to assess reactive strength and fast SSC in youth basketball players [33,34]. Players were instructed to step off the box with their preferred foot, to keep their hand on their hips, to land with both feet at the same time, and to jump ‘as high and fast as possible, while spending minimal time on the ground’ [35]. Three trials were completed and 15-s of recovery were allowed between them. The jump was performed on a contact platform (Chronojump Bosco System A2-DIN) and the following variables were recorded: jump height, ground contact time (GCT) and reactive strength index (RSI), obtained by dividing jump height by GCT. The DJ with the highest RSI was kept for analysis.

Repeated jumps

For the RJ test, players were required to perform 7 repeated maximal jumps on the contact platform, and given the same instruction as in the DJ: to jump ‘as high and fast as possible, while spending minimal time on the ground’ [35]. The metrics considered for analysis were the average JH, GCT and RSI from jumps 2 to 6 (i.e. the first and final jump were disregarded).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means and standard deviations. Although team-specific data are reported (i.e. U16 and U18, Table 2), the statistical procedures were applied to the pooled sample due to the a priori sample size estimation indicating that at least 21 participants were required. This decision was further supported by additional analyses including age group as a factor, which indicated no meaningful influence of age group on the overall pattern of temporal performance changes. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, the homogeneity of variances was checked with Levene’s test and sphericity with Mauchly’s test. When sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. To examine the progression of each vertical jump test across the selected different phases of the season, a repeated measures ANOVA with time (T1, T2 and T3) as factor was conducted using JASP statistical software (version 0.95.2; JASP Team, University of Amsterdam, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Statistical significance was established for a p-value of p < 0.05. When significant main effects were found, Bonferroni’s post-hoc were conducted for pairwise comparisons. ESs were reported as partial eta squared (η2ₚ), with values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively [36]. Additionally, Cohen’s d ES, and their respective 95% Confidence Interval (95%CI), were calculated and classified as: > 0.2 small, > 0.6 moderate, > 1.2 large, > 2.0 very large, and >4.0 nearly perfect, as recommended for team-sports [36]. Absolute and relative reliability of the data test was assessed through the coefficient of variation (CV) and the two-way random intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), respectively. CV values < 5% were considered excellent, whereas values <10% were considered good. ICC were interpreted as: < 0.5 poor, 0.5–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.9 good, > 0.9 excellent [37].

Results

Descriptive data of all jump tests and variables in T1, T2 and T3 are presented in Table 2. All metrics presented good to excellent reliability (i.e. ICC point estimates ranging from 0.77 to 0.92 and CV ranging from 3.3% to 8.8%).

Figure 1 displays the mean values and individual data points of SJ performance at each time-point. A significant time-effect was observed for SJ height (p < 0.001, F = 30.87, η2ₚ = 0.553). Specifically, significant increases were found from T1 to T2 (p < 0.001; ES [95%CI] = 0.77 [0.32; 1.21]), and jump height was also higher in T3 when compared to T1 (p < 0.001; ES = 0.97 [0.48; 1.45]). No differences were found between T2 and T3 (p = 0.346; ES = 0.20 [−0.12; 0.53]).

Similar results were found for CMJ performance (Figure 2) with a significant time-effect identified (p < 0.001, F = 27.81, η2ₚ = 0.527). Jump height improved significantly from T1 to T2 (p < 0.001; ES = 0.71 [0.30; 1.11]) and T1 to T3 (p < 0.001; ES = 0.88 [0.41; 1.35]), but no significant differences were observed when comparing T2 to T3 (p = 0.445; ES = 0.18 [−0.13; 0.49]).

Regarding DJ, no significant time-effects were found for jump height (p = 0.085, F = 2.877, η2ₚ = 0.103) (Figure 3(A)). Discussion

The purpose of this study was to examine the changes in vertical jump performance of U16 and U18 male basketball players during the first three months of the season (i.e. from the start of the pre-season until the early in-season period) through the application of a comprehensive test battery consisting of four distinct jumping tasks (i.e. SJ, CMJ, DJ and RJ). The main findings demonstrated that: (i) overall, significant neuromuscular adaptations occurred from T1 to T2 and T3, as reflected by performance gains across all jump tests; and (ii) RSI, in both the DJ and RJ, was the only metric to peak in early in-season (i.e. values in T3 were significantly greater than in T2). This suggests that RSI in fast SSC actions (e.g. DJ) could be especially useful for assessing neuromuscular capabilities during the competitive phase of the season in youth basketball players, as this metric seems to hold a higher discriminative value than SJ or CMJ height.

Previous studies in different team-sports such as basketball [16,38], volleyball [20,22], soccer [21,39] or futsal [40] have analyzed changes in vertical jump performance during the first months of the season with mixed findings. For example, Spyrou et al. [40] found no differences in CMJ height across the pre-season and early in-season in professional futsal players, with fluctuations of less than 2%, and suggested that neuromuscular adaptations that may occur during this period, might not be expressed by changes in jump height in the studied population. Conversely, Chiang et al. [38], in a sample of college-aged basketball players, reported that CMJ height increased from ∼43.5 cm to ∼48.5 cm during the initial phase of the training year, where higher training volumes were observed, but that no other significant differences were noted throughout the season. This is in line with the present results as SJ and CMJ height improved during the pre-season (i.e. from T1 to T2), a period characterized by enhanced training loads [41,42], but no changes were found during early in-season (i.e. from T2 to T3). The increased SJ and CMJ performances could be due, at least in part, to the characteristics and higher frequency of integrative neuromuscular training (i.e. strength-power training) in pre-season when compared to in-season (i.e. 3–4 vs 2 sessions, respectively). During the former period, the more strength-oriented stimuli, with a predominance of high-load, low-velocity exercises, might have led to enhancements in contractile element function and a more efficient use of the slow SSC, with the resulting improvements in SJ and CMJ height likely arising from concentric-biased gains in force production and motor unit coordination, firing and recruitment [43]. Nevertheless, in light of recent discussions on the underlying mechanisms of SJ and CMJ performance [12,44], further research is needed to better understand how resistance training may influence adaptations in these jump tasks, particularly in youth athletes.

A novel aspect of the current study is that a testing battery that included the SJ, CMJ, DJ and RJ was applied to determine the time-course of adaptations in concentric-only capabilities, slow and fast SCC function, and reactive strength qualities. Interestingly, although performance in most tests increased across the pre-season (i.e. from T1 to T2), RSI in the DJ and RJ tasks was the only variable that improved during the early in-season (i.e. from T2 to T3), which may have important practical implications. On the one hand, a systematic review and meta-analysis by Jarvis et al. [45] found well-established moderate-to-large associations between RSI and change of direction and short sprinting speed, athletic performance variables critical for basketball. On the other hand, a recent study [46] investigating longitudinal changes in CMJ metrics and their relationship with on-court basketball performance concluded that, although no individual jump metric was sufficient to predict game outcomes by itself, reactive strength qualities were associated to a better point differential (i.e. the difference between the total points a team scores and the points they allow). Of note, two other aspects are worth highlighting regarding the present findings. Firstly, RSI improved from T2 to T3 in two different tests (i.e. DJ and RJ), suggesting that the observed changes were not test-specific but reflected an actual increase in reactive strength capabilities. Secondly, because RSI is a ratio metric (obtained by dividing jump height by GCT), for a better understanding of players’ neuromuscular adaptations, it is crucial to track, not only RSI itself, but also its component parts [47]. Herein, DJ and RJ height remained constant across the three time-points, with changes observed solely in GCT, particularly in the former test. In other words, from pre-season to in-season, players were significantly faster off the ground when performing the DJ while still able to reach similar jump heights, thereby demonstrating a more effective utilization of fast SSCs [45], which is an essential quality for rapid force production during basketball-specific actions. Possible physiological and neuromuscular explanations for this specific adaptation include greater preactivation before ground contact, which may enhance muscle activation during the eccentric phase, thereby optimizing the timing of muscular action and facilitating a quicker transition from the eccentric to the concentric phase [48]. Moreover, according to Flanagan and Comyns [48], shorter GCT may also reflect greater neural potentiation of the muscle contractile machinery and a more pronounced contribution of the muscle spindle reflex. Collectively, these mechanisms may contribute to greater vertical stiffness [49] and a more efficient utilization of the SSC during reactive jumps, which are desirable adaptations in youth basketball players.

From a programming perspective, the observed increase in RSI might be due to, among other factors: i) the gradual shift in resistance training content from predominantly high-load, low-velocity lifts (e.g. back squats or deadlifts) to more power-oriented, low-load, high-velocity exercises emphasizing faster and more explosive actions (e.g. lower-body plyometrics or medicine ball throws), an approach shown to be effective to improve reactive strength [50]; and ii) the reduction in overall training volume (the total number of weekly on-court basketball-specific and gym-based sessions was lower in the competitive season in comparison with pre-season) which could have served as a tapering strategy that led to a decrement in accumulated fatigue and higher neuromuscular outputs, as previously reported in basketball research [38]. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of structured pre-season planning and consistent in-season monitoring for maintaining and advancing neuromuscular performance in youth basketball players.

Noteworthy, the results of the present study indicate that RSI in fast SSC actions seems to be more sensitive to detect changes in neuromuscular capabilities during the competitive period than CMJ or SJ height. According to Bishop et al. [17], players may modify jumping strategy in fatigued or potentiated states and still achieve similar jump outputs. Likewise, relevant performance gains may occur over time (e.g. a shorter propulsive phase duration during a CMJ) without corresponding increases in jump height. Therefore, time-based metrics, such as the RSI that relies on GCT, might be better suited to identify meaningful changes in neuromuscular qualities [9], particularly in contexts where force plates are not available and it is not possible to obtain complementary, phase-specific kinematic and kinetic metrics (e.g. concentric and eccentric duration, velocity, force or power). Based on all of the above and from an applied perspective, youth basketball practitioners are encouraged to include DJ or RJ assessments during the in-season period as a complement to the more traditional CMJ, as these tests allow obtaining time-based metrics (e.g. RSI and the corresponding GCT), even when using low-cost tools, such as contact platforms or smartphone apps.

The limitations of the present study must be acknowledged. Firstly, players from different age categories (i.e. U16 and U18) were included in the same analysis in a decision made to ensure greater statistical power. Thus, although all participants had already passed their peak height velocity, potential influences of chronological and biological age cannot be excluded. Secondly, as players were recruited from two teams, scheduling testing sessions within the same week for both teams throughout the entire season was not possible. Therefore, only one testing session was completed in-season (i.e. T3), which limited the ability to examine season-long fluctuations in the variables analyzed. Future research should incorporate additional assessment time-points while using a similar comprehensive testing battery to clarify how vertical jump performance evolves throughout the season in youth basketball players, ideally including a detailed analysis of SJ, CMJ, DJ and RJ kinetics and kinematics.

Conclusions

The present results indicate that performance changes in the SJ, CMJ, DJ and RJ followed distinct time-courses during the three first months of the basketball season in youth players. While concentric-only and slow SSC performance (assessed with SJ and CMJ height, respectively) improved solely during the pre-season, reactive strength qualities peaked during the early in-season, mainly due to players being faster off the ground when performing the DJ and RJ. Based on these findings, practitioners should consider that including these two jump tests in in-season monitoring practices, in addition to the more traditional CMJ, can provide important complementary information about their players’ athletic performance, particularly in the absence of force plate data. In this context, RSI and GCT derived from the DJ and RJ, time-based metrics easily obtained with low-cost equipment (e.g. contact platforms or smartphone apps), might be more sensitive to detect changes in neuromuscular capabilities during the competitive period when compared to CMJ or SJ height.
