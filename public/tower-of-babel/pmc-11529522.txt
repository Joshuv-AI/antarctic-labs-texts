Addition of prophylactic compression garments to standard care to prevent irreversible lower limb lymphoedema after gynaeco-oncological therapy (Gynolymph): protocol for a randomised controlled trial embedded within an observation cohort study
Authors: Tina Decorte, Vickie Van Besien, Charlotte Van Calster, Luc Vanden Bossche, Caren Randon, Nele Devoogdt, Chris Monten
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn female patients diagnosed with gynaecological cancer, the main priority is treating cancer itself. However, a significant number of these patients develop lower limb lymphoedema (LLL), with its incidence strongly influenced by the diagnostic cut-off (a difference in leg circumference between both legs of 5% or 10%). LLL significantly impacts patients’ quality of life (QoL) due to functional impairment, the daily time investment required for treatment, financial costs and affected body image. Additionally, it increases the risk of cellulitis, often leading to hospitalisation for intravenous antibiotics. Timely recognition and acknowledgement of symptoms are crucial first steps in improving the QoL for these women. LLL is a common and irreversible complication following treatment for gynaecological cancer. Despite its clinical significance, there are limited prospective studies investigating LLL incidence, risk factors, early detection and clinical course in this patient population. Therefore, the objective of this observational cohort study is to investigate the incidence rate of stage 0–1 or stage 2–3 LLL based on the International Society of Lymphology criteria in the first 2 years after gynaecological cancer treatment. The embedded randomised control study (RCT) examines the added value of prophylactic compression garments (class II) to standard care in preventing irreversible lymphoedema after gynaeco-oncological therapy.Methods and analysisIn June 2022, two university hospitals in Belgium initiated a multicentre observational cohort study, which also includes an embedded RCT. The study aims to enrol 400 patients before they begin cancer treatment or within 14 days of starting chemotherapy, radiotherapy or surgery. This cohort will be followed for up to 2 years. The embedded RCT will focus on patients who develop clinical lymphoedema stage 0–1 within the first 12 months following their initial cancer treatment. A total of 196 patients will be randomised into two groups: the intervention group (98 patients), receiving usual care plus prophylactic compression garments (compression class 2 (CCL2): 23–32 mm Hg), and the control group (98 patients), receiving only usual care. The RCT aims to assess the impact of adding prophylactic compression garments to standard care on further deterioration. Assessments will be conducted at baseline, and at 3, 12 and 24 months after initial cancer treatment. An additional assessment (T-visit) will be provided if there is a transition to LLL stage 0–1 or from stage 0–1 to stage 2–3 LLL, based on follow-up findings or patient initiative, using the predictive value of a validated self-report lower extremity questionnaire. This questionnaire is part of a lymph diary app provided to all patients. The primary outcome of the observational study is to determine the incidence rate of stage 0–1 or stage 2–3 LLL in the first 2 years after gynaecological cancer treatment. The primary outcome of the RCT is to evaluate the effect of wearing CCL2 preventive garments to avoid progression to higher-stage lymphoedema in patients who develop LLL within 1 year of treatment. Key secondary outcomes of the observational study include the timing of the development of LLL, risk factors for developing LLL and the impact of LLL on QoL and sexuality, as well as the evaluation of screening and diagnostic tools. The secondary outcomes of the RCT include tolerance and compliance with wearing the preventive compressive garments, the impact of the garments on limb volume and the patient’s time and financial investment.Ethics and disseminationThe study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the two academic hospitals: the University Hospital of Ghent in September 2021 and the University Hospital of Leuven in December 2021. Approval has been granted for the study protocol, informed consent forms and other related documents by the main Ethics Committee of Ghent (BC-09915) and the local Ethics Committee of Leuven (S65724). All patients will provide written informed consent before participating in the trial. The results will be shared through peer-reviewed journals and presentations.Trial registration numberNCT05469945.

Introduction

Improved knowledge in the diagnosis and treatment of gynaecological cancer has led to an increased number of cancer survivors. However, these survivors are faced with treatment-related complications that persist throughout their lives. Chronic conditions significantly affect daily functioning and overall quality of life (QoL).

Lymphoedema stands out as one of the most common and disabling complications. It affects QoL, physical functionality and mobility, and it can also have effects on social and psychological health.1 The damage caused by lymphoedema affects many aspects of daily life: it alters body image and complicates daily activities as basic as dressing, it increases patient-related healthcare expenditure for treatment and disease management, it reduces patient autonomy in daily functioning, and can even cause sensory or motor dysfunction.24

There is currently no definitive cure for lymphoedema: patients are limited to lifelong prevention, compression therapy and regular lymphatic drainage.5 6 Surgical repair may be an option in selected cases, but evidence on the different surgical techniques is limited and their long-term effects are still unknown.7 In addition, even after successful repair, conservative treatment is still required.

Most studies on lymphoedema are limited to upper limb lymphoedema (ULL), mainly following breast cancer treatment. Few publications focus on lower limb lymphoedema (LLL), despite its detrimental impact on QoL.1 8 9 This is attributed to the fact that LLL is more difficult to diagnose and treat than ULL. LLL often occurs bilaterally or at the level of the groin or genital region, making it difficult to diagnose by comparison.

The study aims to fill the evidence gap concerning early diagnostic techniques and interventions for LLL in patients with gynaecological cancer. Its primary objective is to determine the incidence of stage 0–1 or stage 2–3 LLL within the first 2 years after gynaecological cancer treatment. Diagnosis will be confirmed according to International Society of Lymphology (ISL) criteria through clinical examination and non-invasive technical measurements, while excluding other conditions that cause oedema. Additionally, a randomised trial will evaluate whether combining education and prevention with compression class 2 (CCL2) garments can prevent the worsening of LLL in patients exhibiting stage 0 and stage 1 symptoms. The role of high compression class III–IV stockings is well established in advanced lymphoedema. In this irreversible stage, the inconvenience of wearing such garments is well balanced against the burden of lymphoedema. However, in early-stage lymphoedema, the balance between inconvenience and benefit is less clear. CCL2 garments (23–33 mm Hg) may suffice to prevent progression. Cost-effectiveness will be evaluated based on patient and societal costs alongside lymphoedema reduction and QoL.

Methods and analysis

The manuscript follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guidelines, as detailed in online supplemental appendix A.10

Study design and setting

This is a multicentre, mixed observational cohort and randomised controlled trial (RCT) of lymphoedema after oncological treatment in women, led by the Department of Lymphatic Disorders of the University Hospital of Ghent. The observational cohort will include women diagnosed with gynaecological cancer prior to initiating any treatment for gynaecological cancer or within 14 days after starting chemotherapy, radiotherapy or surgery. Predefined follow-up assessments take place at 3 months, 12 months and 24 months after the last oncological treatment (chemotherapy, radiotherapy and/or surgery). Before including the patient, eligibility criteria are checked and an informed consent form is signed (online supplemental appendix B). After surgery, all participants receive standardised instructions for usual care. These instructions cover skin care and wound prevention. Additionally, participants are trained to use a study-specific lymphoedema app, which enables them to complete scheduled questionnaires and undergo repetitive screening for LLL through a dedicated screening questionnaire11 and registration of time as well as financial investment for the prevention or, in the event, the treatment of LLL.

If participants progress to early-stage LLL (stage 0–1) within the first year after treatment—either through scheduled clinical examinations or self-reported questionnaires—they are assigned to the interventional cohort. Within this cohort, they are randomly allocated to either the control group (continuing usual care) or the intervention group, where wearing compressive garments is added (see right part of figure 1). In case participants progress to an advanced stage of LLL (stage 2–3), either directly or as an evolution after early stage LLL (stage 0–1), they are treated with decongestive lymphatic therapy (DLT), including the continuation of usual care, applying multi-layer bandages if necessary, and wearing compressive garments of compression class III or IV. All patients will be followed up until 24 months after their last oncological treatment.

Intervention

Participants who develop clinically confirmed lymphoedema, based on ISL criteria (stage 0–1), are randomised to a subcohort. The purpose is to evaluate the effect of wearing preventive CCL2 compression garments in addition to usual care, aiming to prevent progression to higher-stage LLL. All patients with stage 0–1 receive standardised instructions on usual care for LLL. If randomised to the experimental group, the intervention involves adding CCL2 garments to their usual care. The selection of compression garment depends on the location of the lymphoedema. The range includes flat-knit garments tailored to various extents of coverage: ankle to groin (AG) covers the leg from the ankle up to the thigh, ankle to knee (AD) is designed to cover the leg from ankle to the knee. Abdominal to toe (AT) is a pantyhose garment that extends from toe and includes a waistband for full lower body support, if the patient has unilateral lymphoedema a mono AT, designed for one leg can be ad. A Bermuda short, femoral to toe (FT) offers a shorter coverage option, suitable for managing lymphoedema in the upper regions of the legs.

Objectives

Primary endpoints

The primary endpoint is to investigate the incidence rate of stage 0–1 or stage 2–3 LLL in the first 2 years after gynaecological cancer treatment, with a diagnosis of stage 0–1 and 2–3 LLL confirmed according to the ISL criteria by clinical examination and non-invasive technical measurements and excluding other conditions initiating oedema. The primary endpoint of the randomised subcohort group is to evaluate the effect of wearing CCL2 preventive garments to avoid progression to higher stage LLL in patients treated for gynaecological cancer who develop stage 0–1 LLL within 1 year of treatment.

Potential enrolment will be discussed during each centre’s multidisciplinary oncology meeting. Data collection, management and analysis

Patient assessment

Patients will be assessed at baseline (D0) and at 3 months (M3), 12 months (M12) and 24 months (M24) after the end of the last treatment (ie, surgery, chemotherapy or radiotherapy).

Tumour and treatment-related characteristics will be registered offline at D1 (after the last treatment).

If there is a transition from no LLL to stage 0–1 LLL or from stage 0–1 to stage 2–3 LLL (based on findings at follow-up or on patient initiative), the participant will be invited for an additional assessment.

Assessment of clinical stage of LLL

For the clinical evaluation we will use the ISL criteria12:

Stage 0: stage 0 lymphoedema is a subclinical or latent condition where swelling is not evident despite impaired lymphatic transport. Most patients are asymptomatic, but some report a feeling of heaviness in the limb. Stage 0 can be transitory and may exist for months or years before the onset of overt lymphoedema occurs (stages 1, 2 or 3 below). Assessment of early fluid can be accomplished using tissue dielectric constant (TDC) analysis.

Assessment of early fluid can be accomplished using tissue dielectric constant (TDC) analysis.

Stage 1: stage 1 lymphoedema is characterised by the accumulation of protein rich fluid that subsides with limb elevation, usually within 24 hours. The appearance is that of soft oedema that may pit, with no evidence of dermal fibrosis. This is sometimes called reversible oedema. Stage 1 corresponds to a mild grade of lymphoedema.

Stage 2: stage 2 lymphoedema does not resolve with limb elevation alone. This reflects the evolution of dermal fibrosis. As the fibrosis progresses, the limb may no longer pit on examination. This is sometimes called spontaneously irreversible lymphoedema. Stage 2 corresponds roughly to a moderate grade of lymphoedema.

Assessment of technical measurements

A perimeter will be used for the perimeter measurements, codeveloped with the University Hospital of Leuven, and allowing circumference measurement with a 4 cm interval between measurements along the leg.

Subcutaneous water percentage will be measured with a TDC metre at baseline, 3-month, 12-month and 24-month follow-up.13

If there is a transition from no LLL to stage 0–1 (ISL), patients will be randomised to the cohort group.

Within each of the two university centres, a single assessor with specialised knowledge in lower limb LLL will carry out all clinical assessments. Should an assessor be unavailable, a similarly qualified individual will take their place. This replacement will remain the same for any subsequent instances to ensure minimal bias affecting intra- and inter-rater reliability. All measurement procedures are standardised across both centres, with clear and detailed protocols that all assessors follow.

Assessment of ICG fluoroscopy and lymphoscintigraphy

If a patient develops clinical oedema, ICG fluoroscopy and lymphoscintigraphy will be performed to denote early diagnosis. Data from these investigations will be extracted to confirm the diagnosis of LLL. The lymphoscintigraphy procedure will be carried out within each hospital by the Department of Nuclear Medicine, which possesses the necessary expertise in detecting lymphatic insufficiency. The department will consider both minor and major criteria during the assessment. A written protocol detailing the lymphoscintigraphy procedure has been provided to both services. The ICG fluoroscopy will be performed in each centre by the same individual to ensure consistency in the assessment process.

Data management

REDCap will be used for data collection and management. This online tool will be used for randomisation, data collection, management and analysis. REDCap consists of a secure Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act compliant system. All data will be pseudonymised.

The datasets used and/or analysed during the current study will be available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Statistical analysis

Sample size

The Biostatistics Unit of the Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences of Ghent University performed the sample size calculation. The sample size is calculated for the interventional subcohort, to have at least 80% power to detect a difference between the compression group and the control group for progression of lymphoedema. An alpha has been set equal to 0.05. Sample size calculation is based on evaluation at year 1. No longitudinal analysis is foreseen as primary analysis, but this could be included as a secondary analysis using a generalised estimating equation analysis. Baseline measurement (no lymphoedema) is performed before start of any oncologic treatment or within 14 days.

Patients enter the interventional subcohort at ‘T1’ (=development of early-stage lymphoedema or ISL stage 0–1). Patients may enter T1 between the day of inclusion in the observational cohort and M12 (12 months after D0=last day of oncological treatment).

Spontaneous in-between measuring moments are possible, in case of patient-reported changes.

The primary analysis refers to the comparison within the interventional subcohort between control and compression at M12. The calculation of the required sample size is based on articles about lymphoedema after therapy of gynaecological tumours.1416

The following assumptions have been made for calculating the two subgroups: assumption of transition of 75% from observational group to interventional group and drop-out of 10% after 12 months.

To detect a difference, in the interventional subcohort 109 subjects are required per group (2×109=218 subjects in total for the two groups) to have at least 80% power. If 66% of the patients in the observational cohort will meet the inclusion criteria for the interventional randomised trial, at least 330 (=218/0.66) patients should be included in the observational cohort.14 To correct for drop out we planned to include 400 patients. The anticipated dropout rate is based on our own assumptions rather than prior literature. This assumption has been made after careful consideration of the specific context of our study and the characteristics of the participant population. If the number of subjects would be reached before the end of the planned recruitment period, recruitment will be stopped at 400 patients. If the number is not attained, the recruitment period will be prolonged. If the number of 218 subjects is reached in the interventional subcohort, randomisation will be stopped, and analysis starts. However, the observational cohort will continue accrual until 400 subjects are reached.

We assume a difference in true transition rates (T1 to T3) in both study arms, with 0.4 in the interventional group versus 0.75 in the control group. The recruitment of 109 patients in each treatment arm would allow the assessment of benefit of the intervention with 80% power, based on the 95% two-sided CI approach for the differences in two independent proportions.

An interim analysis is planned, using the O’Brien Fleming adjustment scenario for the statistical significance level. The interim analysis will be conducted when 56 patients in each group reached their follow-up period. This will allow the detection of an obvious treatment benefit of 35%, with 80% power at the 0.0052% significance level and will be applied as an early stopping rule for the trial. If the trial continues, the final analysis on the accumulating data will be performed at the 4.92% statistical significance level, to maintain an overall significance level of 5%.

Sample size calculations were performed using SAS Power and Sample Size (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Stratification was performed per centre, aiming for an equal distribution of the number of patients per centre. Enrolment and randomisation will be done in REDCap.

If 66% of the patients in the cohort meet the inclusion criteria for the randomised trial, a minimum of 330 (=218/0.66) patients should be included in the cohort.

To account for potential dropouts and uncertainties in the incidence rate of LLL, the sample size for this study has been increased to 400 patients. Power analysis indicates that with this sample size, the minimum transition rate required to achieve 80% statistical power is 55%. Should the observed transition rate be as low as 35%, the statistical power would decrease to 59%. Consequently, it is proposed to continuously monitor the transition rate throughout the study. Adjustments to the target sample size will be made as necessary and feasible. To ensure a reliable estimate, the transition rate will be evaluated once 300 patients have reached the 12-month follow-up point.

Statistical methods

Baseline variables will be described in each group, using means (SD), medians (IQR and range) and raw numbers (%). Since it is a randomised study, no p values will be reported for the comparison of baseline variables between both groups.

Different analysis sets will be defined. The intent-to-treat analysis set (ITT) contains all randomised patients grouped according to the allocated treatment. The modified ITT analysis set contains all randomised patients grouped according to the allocated treatment, but excluding patients who withdrew their consent. The as-treated analysis set also contains all randomised patients but grouping the patients according to their received treatment. The per-protocol analysis set contains all randomised patients who received the allocated treatment. The main analyses will be performed on the ITT analysis set. Results on the other analysis sets will be reported additionally.

The correlation between leg volume and body mass index will be assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. This analysis will focus on the affected leg. In cases where both legs are affected by lymphoedema, the leg with the more severe condition will be considered for the assessment.

The interobserver agreement of the different methods of measuring LLL is assessed by calculating the observed agreement and Cohen’s kappa coefficient.

Handling of missing data

A low drop-out rate is expected: trial follow-up moments collide with oncological follow-up, a completion of questionnaires is created through an interactive app, with reminders through pop-up if completion of questionnaire stays behind. Trial-participation guarantees close monitoring of symptoms suggestive of lymphoedema. For this reason, drop-out rate is estimated to remain low (maximum 10%).

For the analysis of primary and secondary outcomes, constrained longitudinal data analysis models will be used. These models will be fitted using a direct likelihood approach and therefore also include subjects with one or more missing measurements. For the analysis of the secondary endpoint, a multiple imputation will be considered to handle potential missing information.

Sensitivity analyses

As mentioned earlier, a low drop-out rate is expected. Eventual dropouts are very likely to occur in both control and experimental groups. If an asymmetric drop-out in over 10% of participants is observed, sensitivity analyses for the primary outcome analysis will be planned exploring models not assuming that the missingness mechanism is missing at random.

Outcomes

A constrained longitudinal data analysis using the baseline measurement and the follow-up measurements till 24 months as outcome will be used to compare the questionnaires and app results after 3 months, 12 months and 24 months based on a two-sided test with alpha=0.05. The choice of the covariance structure for the five measurements will be based on the Aikake criterion. Study site is added as a fixed factor in this model. Patients with a recurrence of cancer are considered as a drop-out.

Duration of the project and expected outcomes

The first patient was enrolled on 22 June 2022. Currently, there are 37 patients included in the observational cohort in Ghent, with five dropouts. In Leuven, 34 patients have been enrolled. For the randomised controlled trial (RCT), there are currently four patients in Ghent, with the first enrolment on 24 February 2024, and none in Leuven. Further recruitment of up to 400 patients is planned for 2025 and 2026. The initial results from the RCT are expected in 2027 and from the observational study in 2028. Patient and public involvement

To ensure the lymphoedema trial aligns with our patients’ needs and capabilities, we thoroughly discussed the protocol with our patients’ representatives, including a patient organisation supporting women diagnosed with gynaeco-oncological cancer. They reviewed the relevance of the research questions, the feasibility and acceptability of the methodology (including questionnaires, invasive and non-invasive measurements), the frequency of follow-ups, data collection through individual diaries and the development of an interactive app. Additionally, they evaluated the readability of the informed consent before presenting it to the ethics committee. During the project, an annual meeting with the patients’ representatives of the peer support group will take place to identify and resolve timely issues from the patient’s perspective.

Ethics and dissemination

The Gynolymph study will adhere to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, good clinical practice, and all applicable regulatory requirements. The study received approval from the Ethics Committees of the two academic hospitals: the University Hospital of Ghent in September 2021 and the University Hospital of Leuven in December 2021. A protocol amendment was submitted and approved by the Ethics Committees in January 2024. Approval has been granted for the study protocol, informed consent forms, and other related documents by the main Ethics Committee of Ghent (BC-09915) and the local Ethics Committee of Leuven (S65724). All patients will provide written informed consent before participating in the trial. The study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov in September 2021. The study is funded by Kom op Tegen Kanker (Stand up to Cancer), the Flemish cancer society (project ID: 12149).

The results of the study will be disseminated as soon as possible after completion. They will be shared through publications in peer-reviewed journals and presentations at conferences. Open access will be ensured for all peer-reviewed scientific publications related to the study’s results.

Discussion

So far, there is little knowledge regarding the early detection of LLL after gynaecological cancer treatment, with most of the evidence-based on retrospective analyses. This prospective observational study will provide more information regarding how often, at what moment and in whom LLL develops after treatment for gynaecological treatment. Moreover, the data collected will allow us to determine the evolution between the different stages of LLL, including early stage LLL. Through questionnaire-based patient reporting, the impact of LLL on QoL and on sexual well-being will be followed and the time and cost that patients invest in prevention and treatment will be collected. Data collection is facilitated by a user-friendly lymphoedema app designed for participants, ensuring easy and quick access for efficient data submission without being time-consuming. Data are injected in REDCap.

From the interventional randomised subcohort, we can evaluate in early stage LLL the effect of CCL2 garments on deterioration of the lymphatic flow impairment. The role of wearing these preventive compression garments on patients’ QoL, sexuality and limb volume will be evaluated, and tolerance as well as compliance will be recorded.

Patients who progress directly to stage 2–3 LLL, start immediately with decongestive lymphatic therapy, comprising intensive (daily) treatment with multi-layer bandaging if there is obvious pitting, or CCL3-4 compression garments measured by a compression specialist if pitting cannot be evoked. Type of compression garments depends on the location of the lymphoedema and may include flat knit stocking(s) thigh height (AG) or knee height (AD), panties (AT/monoAT), if necessary, a Bermuda short (FT) and if necessary, a toe cap, all of compression class III or IV (CCL3: 34–46 mm Hg or CCL4>49 mm Hg).

The study concept allows for evaluating the impact of LLL on patients, including QoL, sexual well-being, time investment and associated expenses. Additionally, the study will assess the role of CCL2 compression garments in managing early-stage LLL and explore patient tolerance.

While this study aims to provide valuable insights into the early diagnosis and prevention of LLL in patients with gynaecological cancer, several methodological limitations must be acknowledged. First, there is a potential for time-dependent bias, as patients who develop LLL early may receive longer compression therapy, leading to better outcomes on QoL questionnaires. This could impact the comparability of outcomes between those who develop LLL early and those who develop it later.

Additionally, the differing lengths of intervention periods among patients may affect the consistency of the results, making it challenging to compare outcomes accurately. The need to monitor and potentially adjust the sample size based on the observed transition rate introduces variability and may affect the study’s power and reliability. Consequently, it is proposed to continuously monitor the transition rate throughout the study. Adjustments to the target sample size will be made as necessary and feasible.

Despite these limitations, the study’s comprehensive plans for statistics, monitoring, data management and risk assessment aim to mitigate these challenges and ensure robust and reliable findings.
