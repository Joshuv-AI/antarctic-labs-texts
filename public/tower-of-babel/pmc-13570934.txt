Addressing Social and Cultural Needs with Lean Management in a National Sample of US Physician Practices
Authors: DOROTHY Y. HUNG, LILLIAN C. LEVY, KAREN E. SCHIFFERDECKER, DASHA E. ZERBONI, STEPHEN M. SHORTELL
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Policy Points This national study of US physician practices finds that lean management, an approach to operational excellence and continuous quality improvement, can support integration of social and cultural services in primary care.The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services and commercial payers can expand programs including value‐based, two‐sided risk and global budget payment models that incentivize social screening and follow‐up, language assistance, and workforce training to improve quality for vulnerable populations while reducing inequities in health care.The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services may also consider requirements that physician practices implement operational or performance excellence systems, such as lean management for quality and process improvement, as a condition for participating in value‐based programs. ContextHealth‐related social needs—including stable housing, food security, and interpersonal safety—and patient race, ethnicity, and language (REAL) are key contributors to health equality. Although primary care practices are well positioned to connect patients with resources and provide ongoing support, integrating interventions into clinical workflows remains challenging. This study examines how lean management, a quality improvement approach that emphasizes standardized work processes and respect for people, can support practices in addressing the needs of diverse populations.MethodsWe leveraged data gathered from a nationally representative sample of 1,245 US physician practices. Key independent variables included lean implementation and practice use of specific techniques such as value stream mapping, daily management systems with standard work, and rapid‐cycle process improvement. We conducted multivariate regressions to assess relationships between the extent of lean implementation and availability of social and cultural support in primary care.FindingsPractices that implemented lean displayed 2.72 times the odds of having social screening, referral, and follow‐up systems in place (95% CI, 1.20‐6.19), and 2.85 times the odds of training providers on how to care for underserved populations (95% CI, 1.53‐5.28). These practices also offered more language assistance (odds ratio [OR], 2.16; 95% CI, 1.22‐3.82), routine collection of patient REAL data (OR, 3.75; 95% CI, 1.98‐7.09), and more confidently addressed issues of race and/or discrimination among patients and staff (β: 0.12, P = .040). Value stream mapping, care team huddles, daily management, rapid‐cycle improvement, and use of analytic tools were associated with higher odds of all sociocultural support measures, except for clinician representativeness of patients in terms race/ethnicity.ConclusionsLean management can support efforts to address patient needs by facilitating social risk intervention and culturally responsive care. Attending to social and cultural needs in US physician practices may improve patient experiences and health outcomes, while reducing avoidable health care costs for individuals and populations.

Health‐related social needs (hrsns) impact patient outcomes and are increasingly recognized as key contributors to health equality. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, social factors such as stable housing, food security, transportation access, and interpersonal safety account for nearly half of all modifiable influences on health. 1 Proactive efforts to address social needs through screening and intervention can improve experiences and effectiveness of care by reducing cost and access barriers while increasing patient engagement and adherence to treatment plans. 1 , 2 Similarly, addressing patient needs with regard to race, ethnicity, and language (REAL) ensures delivery of more appropriate health services while optimizing clinical outcomes. For example, language‐concordant care and interpreter use enhance access, communication, and trust; compliance with care regimens; and patient safety among individuals with limited English proficiency. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 HRSNs that remain unmet, as well as persistent cultural barriers, inevitably lead to high utilization and associated costs through delayed care, preventable complications, and reduced use of appropriate services. 2 , 7 For these reasons, addressing social and cultural needs may help strengthen progress toward the Triple Aim by improving patient experiences and health outcomes, while reducing costs for individuals and populations. 8 , 9

Despite the importance of addressing sociocultural needs, identifying patient risks followed by targeted intervention remains uneven in clinical settings. Prior research shows that activities such as social risk screening and provision of culturally responsive care vary depending on local infrastructure and implementation capacity. For example, internal practice capabilities including health information technology and patient engagement strategies correlate more strongly with screening than broader health system characteristics, 10 while innovation capacity and focus on low‐income populations are associated with screening for a wider range of social risks. 11 Federally qualified health centers (FQHCs) likewise demonstrate widespread but uneven adoption of social risk screening, with frequency varying by key organizational factors such as resource availability. 12 Similarly, patient data capture on REAL characteristics requires coordinated organizational investment in data infrastructure, staff training, stakeholder engagement, and performance monitoring, 13 while culturally competent care initiatives are enabled by high readiness for change and leadership support. 14 , 15 Notably, risk intervention programs require not only screening or data collection tools themselves, but sustained workflow support, technical assistance, referral infrastructure, and follow‐up systems that are tightly integrated into clinical operations. 16 , 17

Because effective intervention relies on consistent documentation and follow‐up over time, primary care practices (PCPs) are uniquely positioned to connect patients with resources while offering continuous support. As a patient‐centered medical home, PCPs maintain frequent and ongoing relationships with patients, 18 thus creating opportunities to identify and monitor social and cultural needs that impact health outcomes. Despite this opportunity to support patients, incorporating interventions into primary care workflows remains challenging. Unlike screening for medical conditions, addressing sociocultural risks often requires multistep coordination and communication across care teams and community networks, which may strain already limited practice capacity. 19 , 20 In the absence of organized systems to facilitate these activities, efforts to address patient needs vary greatly depending on organizational resources, staffing priorities, and existing infrastructure. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17

Based on principles anchored in operational excellence and respect for people, lean management is a sociotechnical approach to quality improvement that aims to reduce variation in work processes while maximizing consistency in outcomes. 21 This approach to continuous improvement is increasingly used in health care settings to design clinical workflows that enhance value for patients. Although the effects of lean implementation on cost and efficiency are well‐documented, 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 its use to facilitate preventive services targeting social and cultural needs remains unexplored. Moreover, there is limited research on lean applications to reduce disparities in both patient and provider experiences within the health system. Understanding connections between lean implementation and availability of sociocultural support services is important to improve care for diverse populations. This study seeks to contribute new knowledge by examining the use of lean management to address social and cultural needs in US PCPs.

Methods

Data Source

Our study is based on cross‐sectional data gathered from the National Survey of Healthcare Organizations and Systems (NSHOS II), a nationally representative sample of 1,245 responding US physician practices drawn from the IQVIA OneKey database. 29 , 30 Details on the sampling methodology and survey response weighting are provided in the appendix. The NSHOS is stratified by ownership type, ranging from independently owned practices to those owned by an integrated delivery system, and includes clinicians specializing in family medicine, internal medicine, and geriatrics. 31 Practice managers or other clinic leaders were invited in June 2022 through February 2023 to complete the survey, which included information on organizational and geographic characteristics, revenue sources and value‐based payment, internal systems for operational and clinical quality improvement, approaches to care delivery, and assessment of sociocultural factors among patients and clinic staff.

Measures

Independent Variables

Our main independent variables captured the extent to which practices implemented various lean management techniques. Specifically, practice leaders were asked whether their clinic employed the following lean‐based processes: (1) A3 problem solving, (2) value stream mapping, (3) care team huddles, (4) daily management systems with standard work, (5) kaizen rapid‐cycle improvement, and (6) analysis tools for quality improvement. A3 problem solving is a structured approach to problem solving by which strategies for addressing a problem are summarized on a single sheet of A3‐sized paper (11 × 17 inches). A3 reports include a definition of the problem to be addressed, description of current conditions, desired future state (i.e., goal or target condition), root cause analysis, recommendations for intervention, and an implementation and sustainability plan. 32 Value stream mapping (VSM) is a technique that visually documents each step of a work process (e.g., patient rooming, service delivery, performance of a procedure) to understand the current state of operations, identify unnecessary or wasteful points in the process, and design a more ideal workflow. 33 Real‐time data analysis tools for quality improvement are used to monitor performance on operational or clinical targets. Examples include pareto charts that display data to visualize and highlight the most influential factors hindering performance, 34 and statistical process control to distinguish between common versus special cause variations indicating that a process is statistically aberrant. 35

Whereas these methodologies are more diagnostic in nature, other lean processes that were surveyed by the NSHOS II are commonly used to implement or sustain improvements to workflows. These include daily huddles, which are short, focused meetings among care team members to discuss key issues, coordinate care, and ensure alignment on patient needs, safety protocols, and work priorities. 36 A daily management system (DMS) consists of standardized work processes enabling staff and leaders to accomplish routine tasks in alignment with organizational goals. 37 Finally, kaizen continuous improvement events, also called rapid process improvement workshops, typically last a few days with the goal of conducting rapid‐cycle experiments to test new workflows that may be viable solutions to complex problems. 38 , 39 Based on these components of a lean management system, we created a binary variable reflecting whether PCPs utilized at least one of the six lean processes or none at all. Additionally, we generated a count variable of the number of lean processes used, ranging from 0 to 6, indicating the extent of lean implementation in each practice.

Dependent Variables

We measured seven outcomes capturing practice availability of social and cultural support services or capabilities. Of these study outcomes, three addressed HRSNs while four addressed issues of REAL.

HRSNs

Our first study outcome was the presence of social screening, referral, and follow‐up systems, measured as a composite indicator constructed from three related survey questions. The first question assessed whether practices have a system in place to routinely screen patients for social risk factors, including low health literacy, food insecurity, housing instability, utility needs, interpersonal violence, transportation, financial assistance, and Medicaid eligibility for low‐income patient populations. The second question asked if practices have a system to facilitate clinician referrals for social needs such as those listed above. The third assessed whether practices track patient follow‐through on referrals for social needs, including food, housing, and interpersonal violence. Responses for each HRSN item were binary coded (1 = yes/0 = no), and a composite score for each of the three survey questions was calculated by averaging across item responses. An overall summary score was then created by aggregating composite item scores across the three questions.

We also examined training requirements to reduce disparities in caring for socioeconomically disadvantaged patients. This was assessed via the survey question: “Does your practice require training for clinicians and staff on providing high‐quality care to socioeconomically disadvantaged populations?” There were separate response options for clinicians and staff; for each, responses were binary coded (1 = yes/0 = no) with a composite score generated by averaging the responses across groups. Similarly, we assessed whether practices conduct performance reviews of preventive service delivery stratified by payer type such as Medicaid, Medicare, or commercial insurance. This was also measured as a binary variable with responses of “Yes” coded as 1, and 0 if otherwise.

Race, Ethnicity and Language

We measured the frequency with which practices provide professional language services via the following question: “How often is your practice able to provide professional language assistance services for patients with limited English proficiency: Always, Often, Occasionally, Rarely, or Never?” This study outcome was treated as a binary variable with “Always” and “Often” coded as 1, and all other responses coded as 0. Another study outcome assessed clinician representativeness with respect to racial and ethnic characteristics of the practice's patient population. This was assessed on the survey via the following question, “To what extent are clinicians in your practice representative of the race/ethnicity of the patients you serve: Quite a lot, Some, A little, or Not at all?” Responses of “Quite a lot” were coded as 1, and all other responses were coded as 0.

We also assessed whether practices routinely collect patient data on REAL‐related characteristics. Responses were coded as a binary variable equal to 1 if information on both race/ethnicity and preferred language data were collected, and 0 if otherwise. A final survey question pertained to confidence in the practice's ability to address issues of race, racism, and/or discrimination among three groups: patients, clinicians, and staff. Confidence levels of “Quite a lot” were coded as 1, and responses of “Some,” “A little,” or “Not at all” were coded as 0. A composite score was generated by aggregating these responses across the three groups.

Covariates

We included organizational and geographic characteristics as control variables. Practice size was categorized based on the number of physicians in the practice: solo (1), small (2‐9), medium (10‐20), and large (21+). Ownership was classified as independent, physician group, hospital, health system, or FQHC. Affiliation with an independent practice association (IPA) and receipt of capitated payment were both measured as binary indicators. The survey also collected data on the amount of annual patient care revenue from payment sources such as commercial insurance, Medicare including dual beneficiaries, Medicaid, and uninsured/self‐pay/other (e.g., Tricare, Veterans Affairs, workers’ compensation). Each payment source was treated as a binary variable coded as 1 if the payer constituted a majority (≥50%) of a practice's annual patient care revenue, and 0 if otherwise.

To account for varying levels of participation in payment reform initiatives, we assessed the number of different shared‐risk accountable care organization (ACO) contracts held by a practice, including contractual arrangements with Medicare, Medicaid, and commercial payers. We also examined measures of rurality and geographic region to account for contextual factors that may influence practice operations. Rurality was determined using rural‐urban commuting area codes based on the practice's address and were categorized as: metropolitan, micropolitan, or rural/small town. Geographic region was similarly derived from the practice's address and categorized into four US regions: East, South, Midwest, and West.

Statistical Analysis

We first used t‐tests and chi‐square tests to compare PCPs that used lean management processes versus those that did not, in terms of their organizational and geographic features. We then examined unadjusted bivariate relationships between lean implementation status and each study outcome. This was followed by a series of multivariate regressions estimating the adjusted relationships between study outcomes and lean implementation, as well as the extent of implementation (i.e., total number of lean processes used), controlling for practice size, ownership, patient care revenue source, payment arrangements, and geographic characteristics. A final set of multivariate regressions examined relationships between study outcomes and each specific lean process, controlling for the use of all other lean processes as well as practice characteristics. All regressions accounted for sample and nonresponse weighting using the svyset command in R Studio (Integrated Development Environment for R, Boston, MA).

Results

Table 1 describes the status of lean implementation in this nationally representative, cross‐sectional sample of US PCPs. It also compares the organizational and geographic features of practices that reported using any lean management processes versus none. Compared to the latter group, physician practices that adopted lean management were larger in size (P = .031), owned by a health system or FQHC (P < .001), and received a greater share of revenue from Medicaid insurance for low‐income patients but a smaller share from Medicare for older adults (P < .006). Additionally, these practices were more likely to accept capitated payment (P = .001), participate in a higher number of ACO contracts (P < .001), and were more evenly distributed across geographic regions of the United States (P = .001).

Table 2 displays unadjusted, bivariate relationships between lean implementation and all study outcomes. In these preliminary analyses, practices that reported using lean management processes consistently offered more sociocultural support for patients, compared to those that did not use lean processes. For example, 13.2% of practices that used lean had systems in place for social screening, referral, and follow‐up compared to 5.8% of all other practices, and 41.6% required training for clinicians and staff on how to deliver high‐quality care to socioeconomically disadvantaged populations versus 20.2% of their counterparts. Similarly, 76.9% of lean practices provided frequent language assistance compared to 56.0% of all other practices. They were also more likely to collect data on patient race, ethnicity, and language (91.6% vs. 78.1%) and conduct performance reviews of preventive care delivery stratified by payment source (35.8% vs. 27.6%). These unadjusted patterns may signal the value of lean management in facilitating sociocultural support, or they may reflect lean implementation as a general marker of high performance in physician practices. There were no meaningful differences in the degree to which clinicians were representative of patient populations in terms of racial/ethnic composition.

Table 3 presents adjusted relationships as derived from multivariate logistic regressions. Controlling for organizational and geographic characteristics, lean implementation was strongly associated with practice support for HRSNs. For example, practices that used lean management processes were associated with 2.72 times the odds of having a system in place for HRSN screening, referral, and follow‐up (95% CI, 1.20‐6.19; P = .017). Similarly, these practices had 2.85 times the odds of requiring clinician and staff training on how to deliver high‐quality care to socioeconomically disadvantaged populations (95% CI, 1.53‐5.28; P = .0009). Practices that implemented lean management also showed higher odds of reviewing preventive care delivery stratified by payers, though this was not statistically significant (odds ratio [OR], 1.50; 95% CI, 0.85‐2.65; P = .159).

Finally, Table 5 shows that the extent of lean implementation, as measured by the number of lean processes used in a practice, was positively associated with nearly all sociocultural support measures. For example, use of each additional lean process was associated with a 45% increase in the odds of having social risk screening, referral, and follow‐up systems in place (OR, 1.45; 95% CI, 1.17‐1.80; P = .0008). In particular, VSM (OR, 2.46, 95% CI, 1.02‐5.93, P = .046), daily management systems with standard work (OR, 2.53; 95% CI, 1.01‐6.37; P = .048), and rapid process improvement (OR, 2.52; 95% CI, 1.08‐5.91; P = .03) were linked to increased presence of social risk screening and follow‐up systems. Similarly, more extensive lean implementation was associated with 62% higher odds of requiring workforce training on how to deliver high‐quality care to disadvantaged patients (OR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.33‐1.98; P < .0001). Specifically, VSM (OR, 4.13; 95% CI, 1.39‐12.3; P = .01), care team huddles (OR, 2.09; 95% CI, 1.14‐3.85; P = .02), and DMS (OR, 2.82; 95% CI, 1.60‐4.95; P = .0003) were linked to such training requirements. Likewise, increased use of lean management processes was associated with higher odds of conducting practice‐level reviews of preventive care delivery stratified by payer (OR, 1.24; 95% CI, 1.03‐1.50; P = .03). A daily management system was positively associated (OR, 2.35; 95% CI, 1.37‐4.01; P = .002), while rapid‐cycle improvement was inversely associated with payer‐stratified performance reviews (OR, 0.25; 95% CI, 0.10‐0.60; P = .002).

Discussion

This study is the first to examine relationships between lean management and efforts to address social and cultural needs in US physician practices. Based on cross‐sectional, nationally representative data, we found preliminary support for lean implementation and increased availability of sociocultural services in primary care settings. Lean organizations seek to create value as guided by a core principle of demonstrating respect for people. 21 As a result, there is deep attention to understanding the “voice of the customer” in order to improve outcomes and experiences among stakeholders. According to our study, practices that adopted lean processes were more systematic in addressing common barriers to high‐quality care. Findings suggest that lean management practices can strengthen efforts to serve patients by facilitating integration of social and cultural support into primary care workflows.

A distinctive feature of lean in health care is its emphasis on standardization of work to create and maintain reliable care processes. Standard work is foundational to lean methodology and shown to promote consistency and accountability in health care operations. 40 , 41 We found that lean techniques such as VSM, daily management, and kaizen process improvement corresponded with having established systems for social needs screening and follow‐up, which are not typically embedded in traditional primary care models but can influence health outcomes. 2 Since the effectiveness of risk screening depends on timely referrals and follow‐up, PCPs that adopt lean processes are better equipped for their role as both an entry point to the health system and consistent source of ongoing support for patients.

We found similar results for patient REAL preference, with lean practices reporting greater availability of language services and data collection on patient REAL‐related characteristics. In particular, care team huddles were positively associated with both activities, while use of analysis tools for quality improvement was linked to increased REAL data capture. Prior research underscores that without consistent gathering of patient information, care providers cannot reliably identify disparities or provide needed interventions to improve health outcomes. 42 , 43 , 44 Yet gaps in data capabilities remain barriers even when commitment to providing high‐quality care is strong. 45 Effective implementation will require a team‐based, participatory management approach along with robust information systems and monitoring. Lean management approaches that engage the workforce while leveraging real‐time data for quality improvement can be used to support the delivery of more patient‐centered, culturally responsive care. 46 , 47

Other related findings include FQHC reports of increased social and cultural interventions, including systems for HRSN screening and follow‐up, workforce training on how to improve care for socioeconomically disadvantaged patients, and data collection on patient REAL characteristics. These results reinforce a prior study of safety net settings that increased their capacity for health equity‐oriented care infrastructure following the COVID‐19 pandemic. 48 Together, study findings reflect a unique mission of the safety net to provide care for lower income and underserved populations, which requires deep attentiveness to sociocultural needs present in the community. On the other hand, we found that IPA‐affiliated practices reported providing fewer language services compared to nonaffiliated practices, as did independently owned practices and those with a higher proportion of commercially insured patients, while practices with higher Medicaid and dual‐eligible Medicare recipients conducted more reviews of preventive care delivery stratified by payer. These findings may highlight contrasting foci among practices serving different communities in terms of support offered to ensure health equity.

Even after controlling for practice characteristics including ownership status, payment sources, and value‐based incentives such as capitation and ACO participation, practices that implemented lean were more likely to address social risks while requiring staff training on how to care for underserved populations. Lean processes such as VSM, care team huddles, and daily management with standard work were particularly associated with these activities. Although studies that correlate lean management with equity‐related documentation and training are limited, lean organizations are commonly known for their workforce development initiatives to promote best practices and reduce process variation. 49 This combined with our current findings reinforce recommendations for targeted needs assessment and referral by trained staff, which is shown to enhance care quality by improving communication, patient trust, and clinical outcomes among disadvantaged populations. 50

Finally, PCPs that adopted a lean DMS and kaizen rapid improvement were more confident in their ability to address issues of race and/or discrimination among patients, clinicians, and staff. While there are no studies directly connecting lean with interventions to reduce racial discrimination, there is emerging research describing how lean leaders can advance health equity and workforce diversity as a priority in health systems. 51 These related study findings may reflect two core principles of a lean management approach, which emphasizes the importance of humble leadership and respect for people. 21 Moreover, findings suggest high competence among lean organizations in valuing diverse perspectives while creating environments where disparities can be identified and managed effectively.

Study results should be interpreted in light of the cross‐sectional nature of our data, which cannot rule out correlations between lean implementation and other practice features or interventions that might also impact outcomes. Although our analyses controlled for a number of factors, it is possible that associations are due to unmeasured confounders (e.g., available resources) that enable the use of lean management or other clinical workflows aimed at providing more patient‐centered care. While our study found several statistically significant associations between lean implementation and availability of sociocultural support, different study designs are needed to determine the causality of these relationships. For example, longitudinal data collected in multiple sites with distinct pre/postintervention times (e.g., interrupted time series, quasi‐experimental stepped wedge designs) may be ideal for research undertaken in real‐world settings where randomized controlled trials are not possible. Alternatively, mixed research methods that leverage both qualitative and quantitative data sources may provide more nuanced insights on how social and cultural risk interventions can be supported in clinical practice.

Conclusion

Recent efforts to strengthen primary care recognize that improving health outcomes depends on effectively addressing patients’ social and cultural needs. To promote high‐quality primary care, the National Academies recommends adopting more systematic approaches to serving patients from diverse socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds. 52 Our study examines how management models grounded in lean principles can create enabling environments to support more equitable, patient‐centered care. As a sociotechnical approach to quality and process improvement, lean management offers a framework that integrates operational reliability with an organizational commitment to respecting patients as stakeholders in receiving of care. Incorporating social and cultural interventions into primary care workflows will enable providers to more comprehensively meet the needs of diverse patient populations.
