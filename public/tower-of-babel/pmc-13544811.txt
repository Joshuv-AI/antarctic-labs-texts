Introduction

Adrenal myelolipoma (AML) is a rare, benign tumor composed of adipose tissue and hematopoietic (blood-forming) cells, which include both myeloid and erythroid components[1]. It comprises 0.08%–0.4% of all tumors, with increased incidence after the fifth decade of life[2]. However, with the widespread availability of ultrasonography (USG) and advancements in imaging modalities, incidental detection has become more common, now accounting for 10%–15% of adrenal masses[2,3]. After adrenocortical adenoma, AML is the second most common benign lesion of the adrenal gland. It is usually small (<4 cm in size in 80%–85% of cases), unilateral (95% of cases), non-secretory/non-functional (85%–90%), and asymptomatic, with equal incidence in males and females[1]. Myelolipoma may occur in extra-adrenal locations. Case presentation

A 58-year-old male with a known history of controlled HTN, on medication for 10 years, presented with non-specific abdominal discomfort and mild abdominal pain. He was otherwise asymptomatic, and the clinical examination revealed no remarkable findings. There was no history of allergies or similar illnesses in the family. During the diagnostic workup, an incidental left adrenal mass was identified on USG of the abdomen and pelvis. Subsequently, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the abdomen and pelvis was performed, revealing a well-defined, fat-containing lesion measuring approximately 7.9 × 7.3 × 7.2 cm in the left suprarenal region with high T2 signal intensity and mild heterogeneous post-contrast enhancement. The mass was in close proximity to adjacent structures, including the left kidney, tail of the pancreas, splenic vein, and body of the stomach. These findings were suggestive of AML or a fat-rich adenoma (Fig. 1). Figure 1.A well-defined lesion measuring 7.9 × 7.3 × 7.2 cm (orange arrow) is noted in the left suprarenal region. Hormonal evaluation, including plasma metanephrines, normetanephrines, and 3-methoxytyramine levels, was within normal limits, ruling out pheochromocytoma or other catecholamine-secreting tumors. ACTH, serum aldosterone, cortisol assessment (including the dexamethasone suppression test), aldosterone–renin ratio, DHEAS, potassium, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, renin, and testosterone were performed and were found to be within normal limits. Preoperative laboratory investigations were largely within normal limits, except for mild anemia (Hb 10.7 g/dL), mild transaminitis (ALT 144 U/L, AST 64 U/L), and elevated serum ferritin (662.0 ng/mL). Electrolyte disturbances included a slightly reduced serum potassium level. Echocardiography revealed mild mitral regurgitation with a preserved ejection fraction of 60%.

During the hospital stay, he was managed with antibiotics, analgesics, and proton pump inhibitors. Given the size of the lesion, its association with HTN, and its potential to cause future complications, surgical management was advised. Preoperative optimization included adequate blood pressure control with antihypertensive medication, correction and monitoring of electrolyte imbalance, anesthetic evaluation, and cardiac assessment prior to surgery. The patient underwent a laparoscopic left adrenalectomy under general anesthesia. Intraoperatively, an 8 × 7 cm left suprarenal mass with adhesions was noted and successfully excised. The postoperative period was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on 3rd postoperative day. Follow-up imaging and clinical assessments at 6 months showed normalization of blood pressure, even after discontinuing anti-hypertensive medications, with no evidence of recurrence. Histopathological examination of the excised adrenal mass showed a well-encapsulated lesion composed of mature adipocytes and trilineage hematopoietic elements, including erythroid, myeloid, and megakaryocytic precursors, with preserved maturation. Compressed residual adrenal cortical tissue was present at the periphery, with no mitotic activity or necrosis noted (Figs 2 and 3). These findings confirmed the diagnosis of a benign, non-functional AML. Discussion

In 1866, Arnold described this lesion as “adrenal lipoma.” Later, in 1905, Edgar von Gierke reported the presence of both adipose and hematopoietic cells in the lesion. Finally, the term “adrenal myelolipoma” was coined by the French pathologist Charles Oberling in 1929[5,6]. AML can be classified as Type I (predominantly composed of adipose tissue) and Type II (predominantly composed of hematopoietic cells).

Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and erythropoietin (EPO) are implicated in the pathogenesis of AML. In conditions associated with elevated ACTH levels – such as congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Cushing disease, obesity, and stress – chronic adrenal stimulation is thought to induce metaplasia of adrenal stromal cells, resulting in the development of adipose and hematopoietic tissue within the adrenal gland. Although ACTH levels are elevated in Addison’s disease, AML is not observed in this condition because there is insufficient functional adrenal tissue to respond to this stimulation. Similarly, in conditions leading to chronic anemia (such as thalassemias, hereditary spherocytosis, or sickle-cell anemia), increased erythropoietin (EPO) production promotes extramedullary hematopoiesis, which may contribute to the development of AML.

As discussed, most cases of AML are asymptomatic, but a few may present with symptoms of mass effect, such as mild abdominal discomfort, abdominal pain (as seen in our case), early satiety, abdominal fullness, back pain, flank pain, or positional shortness of breath[7]. Rarely, AML may be associated with HTN, retroperitoneal hemorrhage (due to the rupture of the tumor), or symptoms resulting from compression of adjacent structures. Retroperitoneal hemorrhage is the most common complication of AML, occurring mainly in lesions larger than 4 cm and in tumors composed of more than 50% adipose tissue. In functional (secretory) AML, HTN results from the release of catecholamines or aldosterone. However, as observed in our case, HTN can also occur in non-functional AML, a phenomenon rarely reported in the literature. Page kidney, also known as the Page phenomenon, is a rare condition in which external compression of the renal parenchyma leads to secondary HTN. It most commonly occurs due to a subcapsular hematoma or fluid collection compressing the kidney. Compression of the kidney results in reduced renal perfusion, which activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), leading to systemic HTN. In non-functional AML, a similar compressive mechanism has been proposed, wherein an enlarging adrenal mass exerts pressure on the adjacent renal parenchyma or vasculature, resulting in RAAS activation and subsequent HTN. The postoperative resolution of HTN in our patient further supports this possible pathophysiological mechanism. As AML may be associated with various endocrine disorders, a complete hormonal workup is recommended for all patients diagnosed with AML, regardless of size or imaging characteristics. This workup should include plasma ACTH, morning serum cortisol, aldosterone, androstenedione, 17-β-estradiol, metanephrine, normetanephrine, a 1-mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test, DHEAS, potassium, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, renin, and testosterone. Pheochromocytoma can be excluded by measuring plasma-free metanephrines or urinary fractionated metanephrines. Similarly, a normal serum 17-hydroxyprogesterone level can exclude congenital adrenal hyperplasia. In patients with concomitant HTN or unexplained hypokalemia, the use of the aldosterone/renin ratio is recommended to exclude primary aldosteronism.

AML should be differentiated from other lipomatous adrenal lesions like renal angiomyolipoma, adrenal lipoma, retroperitoneal liposarcoma, adrenocortical adenoma, or adrenocortical carcinoma. Although USG of the abdomen and pelvis is often the first-line investigation, non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) remains the most sensitive imaging modality. USG can help differentiate a supra-renal mass from the kidney, but it cannot confirm the diagnosis of AML[20]. On NCCT scans, myelolipomas appear as well-circumscribed, round or elliptical, hypodense, heterogeneous masses with attenuation values ranging from −20 to −120 HU. The attenuation value on CT increases as the myeloid component[1]. If NCCT shows a homogeneous mass smaller than 4 cm with an attenuation value of ≤ 10 HU, no further imaging is required[21]. If the adrenal mass is indeterminate on NCCT with normal hormonal levels, one of three options may be chosen: immediate additional imaging with another modality, interval imaging at 6–12 months (NCCT or MRI), or surgery without further delay[21]. MRI can further characterize the different structural components of myelolipomas and is useful for surgical planning, particularly to delineate tissue planes. In approximately 90% of cases, AML can be diagnosed based on imaging alone. In the remaining 10% of cases, where imaging is inconclusive, image-guided fine-needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB) may be performed, though it carries a higher risk of hemorrhage or tumor rupture[20]. The 2016 European Society of Endocrinology and European Network for the Study of Adrenal Tumors guidelines do not recommend the use of an adrenal biopsy in the diagnostic work-up of patients with adrenal masses unless there is a history of extra-adrenal malignancy.

The treatment of AML involves either watchful waiting or adrenalectomy, which can be performed via open versus laparoscopic approaches. Laparoscopic adrenalectomy is considered safe and effective compared to the open approach. It can be performed through either a transperitoneal or retroperitoneal route. Adrenalectomy is indicated if (a) the tumor is symptomatic; (b) the size is > 4–6 cm (due to an increased risk of retroperitoneal hemorrhage); (c) there are atypical features on imaging; (d) there is evidence of mass effect, inferior vena cava compression, hemorrhage, or rupture; (e) the tumor is functional, is rapidly increasing in size (>1 cm/year), or is suspicious for malignancy[7]. In all other cases, watchful waiting is preferred. Follow-up imaging is not required if the initial imaging shows a small lesion (<4 cm) with very low density (<10 HU on CT). If the lesion is greater than 4 cm or has indeterminate characteristics, repeat imaging is recommended every 12 months for 5 years[21].

Conclusion

In functional AMLs, HTN results from catecholamine or aldosterone secretion. However, HTN may also occur in non-functional AMLs, likely due to compression of the renal parenchyma, a mechanism rarely reported in the literature.
