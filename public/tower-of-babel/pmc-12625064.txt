A mixed effect hierarchical analysis to quantify variation on childhood diarrhea prevalence in Mauritania: evidence from the Mauritania demographic and health survey 2019–2021
Authors: Hailegebrael Birhan Biresaw, Solomon Sisay Mulugeta, Minilik Derseh Yismaw, Setegn Muche Fenta, Bezanesh Melese Masresha, Kenaw Derebe Fentaw, Gedam Derbew Addisia, Koyachew Bitew Abebe, Legesse Alamerie Ejigu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiarrhea remains a major global health concern, particularly among children under five. This study aims to assess the prevalence and determinants of diarrhea in children under five in Mauritania using nationally representative data.MethodsA secondary analysis of the Mauritania Demographic and Health Survey (2019–2021) was conducted using multilevel logistic regression to identify individual- and community-level determinants of diarrhea among children under five. The study included a weighted sample of 11,007 women aged 15–49 years for childhood diarrhea prevalence.ResultThe prevalence of childhood diarrhea was 13.8% (95% CI: 13.1–14.5). Higher odds of diarrhea were observed among children of parents with no education (AOR = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.07–1.52) and primary education (AOR = 1.24; 95% CI: 1.03–1.51), those living in rural areas (AOR = 1.94; 95% CI: 1.77–2.14), children of working mothers (AOR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.08–1.44), and children born small (AOR = 1.56; 95% CI: 1.02–2.38) or smaller than average (AOR = 2.06; 95% CI: 1.34–3.18). Lower odds were found among children aged 2–3 years (AOR = 0.56; 95% CI: 0.47–0.68) and 4–5 years (AOR = 0.37; 95% CI: 0.29–0.47), vaccinated children (AOR = 0.51; 95% CI: 0.33–0.71), and those from households with toilet facilities (AOR = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.08–0.54). Regional disparities were noted, with higher risk in Gorgol (AOR = 1.52; 95% CI: 1.14–2.02) and Tagant (AOR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.06–2.01) compared with Hodh Echargui.ConclusionThis study identified several individual and community-level factors associated with childhood diarrhea in Mauritania. Interventions should prioritize improving sanitation access, parental education, maternal support, and vaccination coverage, particularly in high-risk rural regions. Strengthening integrated child health programs targeting these determinants will be essential to reducing the burden of diarrhea among children under five.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25421-1.

Background

Diarrhea is defined as an abnormal increase in stool frequency and liquidity, or the passage of three or more loose stools within 24 h [1, 2]. Globally, an estimated 1.7 billion cases of childhood diarrhea occur each year [1], with most cases reported in areas with limited access to clean water, poor sanitation, inadequate healthcare, and among low-income or marginalized populations [3]. Diarrhea is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in children under five, accounting for 9% of global deaths in 2019 approximately 484,000 fatalities, or 1,300 deaths per day [4] mostly due to severe dehydration and fluid loss [1]. In Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, diarrhea is the major cause of childhood death, with rates ranging from 50 to 150 per 100,000 children, compared to 1–5 per 100,000 in other regions [5]. Notably, 90% of under-five diarrhea deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [6].

Diarrhea is a global health problem and, in some regions, causes higher mortality than all other causes combined [5]. It also accounts for 45 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) among children under five [7], increasing the risk of infectious diseases, malnutrition, and developmental delays [1, 5, 7]. Diarrhea places a substantial burden on healthcare systems, leading to hospitalizations, financial hardship, and lost productivity [8, 9]. For example, studies in Ghana and Zimbabwe reported hospital treatment costs of $133.88 and $293.74 per case, respectively [10, 11]. Diarrheal infections can be transmitted through contaminated food or water and behaviors such as children putting fingers in their mouths [1, 2]. Multiple pathogens—including rotavirus, Salmonella, Shigella, Escherichia coli, Entamoeba histolytica, Bacteroides fragilis, Campylobacter jejuni, Giardia spp., and Cryptosporidium spp.—can cause diarrhea, particularly in immunocompromised individuals [1, 3, 6]. To reduce diarrhea incidence and mortality in children by 2025, the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) launched the Global Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Pneumonia and Diarrhea (GAPPD) [12]. This strategy prioritizes child health by promoting WASH, preventing disease through immunization, and providing appropriate treatment, including oral rehydration solution (ORS) [12, 13].

Previous studies report the prevalence of acute diarrhea among children under five as 26% in Senegal [14], 22% in Ethiopia [15], 20% in Malawi [16], 16.44% in Cambodia [17], and 24.8%, 13.1%, and 13.91% in Burundi, Rwanda, and Tanzania, respectively [18]. Socio-demographic factors significantly associated with diarrhea include child’s age and sex [16, 17], maternal education [15, 18], maternal age, maternal employment status and occupation [16–19], residence [15, 16], size of family and awareness of caregivers [19], wealth index, breastfeeding and nutritional status [18, 20]. Additionally, water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) factors influence diarrhea prevalence, including maternal hand-washing practices [15, 20], availability and condition of toilet facilities [14, 16–18, 20], absence of hand-washing facilities, unsafe disposal of child stool [17], and source and distance of drinking water [14, 17, 18].

Poor WASH access and infrastructure remain challenges in Mauritania. In 2020, 14.8% of the population relied on unimproved water sources, and 13.5% had inadequate sanitation. Open defecation was practiced by 30.7%, while 11.8% used unimproved sanitation facilities [21] [21]. In 2019, Mauritania recorded 1,463,368 cases of diarrhea among children under five, resulting in 74,866 disability-adjusted life years and 1,454 deaths [22]. The under-five mortality rate was 40.5 per 1,000 live births in 2021, still above the Sustainable Development Goal target of 25 per 1,000 live births by 2030 [23, 24]. Diarrhea significantly increases childhood mortality, making it essential to understand both individual and environmental factors. The prevalence and determinants of diarrhea among children under five are poorly documented in Mauritania. Using data from the 2019–2021 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS), this study aims to identify its prevalence and contributing factors. Analyses of DHS data from Senegal, Mali and Niger [14, 25, 26] have shown that sanitation, maternal education, and regional disparities significantly influence childhood outcomes. Recent DHS data from Mauritania have not been analyzed in this context. This study addresses that gap, offering evidence for cross-country comparisons and guiding WASH and child health policies in the Sahel. The findings support efforts to reduce childhood diarrhea, lower under-five mortality, and advance progress toward SDG-3.

Methods

Source of data, study design and setting

This study used data from the 2019–2021 Mauritania Demographic and Health Survey (MDHS), which collected information on reproductive health, non-communicable diseases, child survival, maternal and child health, family planning, and nutritional status. The dataset was obtained from the MEASURE DHS database (http://dhsprogram.com/data/) with approval for the 2019–2021 MDHS. The survey included anemia testing for children aged 6–59 months and women aged 15–49, malaria testing for children aged 6–59 months, and hepatitis B testing for household members aged 1–59 years. MDHS data were also used to estimate adult and maternal mortality rates and the prevalence of female genital circumcision (FGC) among women aged 15–49 and girls aged 0–14.

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, data collection occurred in two phases. The first phase began on November 27, 2019, but fieldwork was halted in mid-March 2020, leaving only the capital, Nouakchott, incomplete. In total, 11,658 households, 15,714 women aged 15–49, and 5,673 men aged 15–59 were successfully interviewed. The 2019–2021 MDHS provides estimates at the national level, for Nouakchott, other urban areas, rural areas, and 14 study regions based on the country’s wilayas: Hodh Echargui, Hodh Gharbi, Assaba, Gorgol, Brakna, Trarza, Adrar, Dakhlet Nouadhibou, Tagant, Guidimagha, Tiris Zemour, Inchiri, Nouakchott North, Nouakchott West, and Nouakchott South [27].

Sampling methods

The 2019–2021 MDHS used a two-stage stratified sampling design. Each region was divided into urban and rural areas, forming 27 strata. In the first stage, 304 EAs (118 urban and 181 rural) were selected with probability proportional to size, though 3 were not visited and 2 were not defined. In the second stage, 38 households per cluster were systematically chosen from updated listings. A total weighted sample of 11,007 individuals was analyzed, as shown in Fig. 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included children under five living with their mothers or caregivers whose mothers (aged 15–49) participated in the 2019–2021 Mauritania DHS and provided complete data on diarrhea. Only records from 299 visited EAs were used, while children over five, unvisited or non-interviewed households, and “Do not know” responses (n = 621) were excluded.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The outcome variable was childhood diarrhea prevalence.

Independent variables

Predictor variables were selected based on previous research [1–4, 6, 8] and their availability in the 2019–2021 MDHS dataset. They were categorized into individual and community level variables.

Individual-level variables

Sex of child, current age, anemia level, birth order, type of birth, place of delivery, child vaccination, size at birth, breastfeeding duration, maternal age, maternal and paternal education, family size, wealth index (reclassified from DHS quintiles: poor = poorest + poor; middle = middle; rich = rich + richest), maternal marital status, maternal occupation, number of children under five, toilet facility (improved: flush/pour flush to piped sewer, septic tank, or pit latrine with slab; unimproved: pit without slab, open defecation, shared facilities), source of drinking water, household TV, and household radio.

Community-level variables

Region and place of residence, obtained directly from the DHS.

Data management and analysis

Data were extracted and recoded using SPSS, and analyses were conducted in STATA. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to characterize study participants. Sampling weights, strata, and cluster identifiers provided by DHS were applied in all descriptive analyses and rescaled for multilevel logistic regression following standard DHS guidelines to obtain nationally representative estimates. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Multilevel logistic regression analysis

A multilevel logistic regression model was used to examine diarrhea prevalence among children under five in Mauritania and identify contributing factors, accounting for the nesting of children within Enumeration Areas (EAs). Variables with p < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable model to avoid excluding potential confounders. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using multilevel logistic regression, accounting for clustering and applying rescaled sampling weights from the 2019–2021 MDHS data. Four hierarchical models were fitted: the null model (assessing contextual influence without covariates), Model I (individual-level effects), Model II (community-level effects), and Model III (full model) [28, 29]. Model comparison and measure of random effects

Model fit was compared using DIC, AIC and BIC; the model with the lowest information criterion was selected [30]. A random-intercept multilevel model was used to account for unobserved cluster-level variation while assuming consistent predictor–outcome relationships across clusters. This approach is commonly applied in childhood diarrhea studies [14, 16, 17]. Random-slope models were tested but did not improve fit (AIC/BIC), so the random-intercept model was retained [10, 31]. Random-effects measures (ICC, MOR, and PCV) were used to quantify between-cluster variation. Result

In this study, a weighted sample of 11,007 children under five years was included. The analysis was conducted using multilevel modelling based on 2019–2020 Mauritania DHS data.

Prevalence of childhood diarrhea and characteristics of study participants in Mauritania

The prevalence of childhood diarrhea in Mauritania was 13.8%. Higher risks were observed among male children, those living in rural areas, children of mothers who never breastfed, multiple births, children aged 2–3 years, and children residing in the Gorgol region. Most participants (92.45%) lacked health insurance, and 6.92% of households did not have a television. Additionally, the majority of homes lacked improved toilet facilities and access to improved water sources (Table 1).

The multilevel model selection, measure of variation and comparison statistics

Since the models were nested, multilevel logistic regression Model III was selected based on the fit statistics in Table 2, as it had the lowest AIC, BIC, and deviance values. Furthermore, the random-effects analysis indicated significant variation in diarrhea prevalence among children under five. The ICC showed that community-level factors accounted for 11.6% of the variability, while individual-level factors explained 89.4%, highlighting substantial differences across communities. The null model revealed that 32.72% of community-level variation (PCV) could be attributed to both individual- and community-level factors. Overall, the full model explained approximately 32.72% of the clustered variation in diarrhea prevalence. The MOR of 1.87 indicates that a child from a high-prevalence cluster is 1.87 times more likely to experience diarrhea than a child from a low-prevalence cluster, confirming significant between-community variation.

Compared to children aged 0–1 year, those aged 2–3 years (AOR = 0.56; 95% CI: 0.47–0.68) and 4–5 years (AOR = 0.37; 95% CI: 0.29–0.47) were less likely to develop diarrhea. Children from households with improved toilet facilities also had lower odds of diarrhea (AOR = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.08–0.54). Children with smaller than average birth size (AOR = 2.06; 95% CI: 1.34–3.18) or small birth size (AOR = 1.56; 95% CI: 1.02–2.38) were more likely to experience diarrhea than those with very large birth size.

Children living in rural areas had higher odds of diarrhea (AOR = 1.94; 95% CI: 1.77–2.14) compared to urban children. Regionally, children in Hodh Gharbi (AOR = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.49–0.90), Brakna (AOR = 0.49; 95% CI: 0.35–0.68), Dakhlet Nouadhibou (AOR = 0.23; 95% CI: 0.13–0.40), Nouakchott Ouest (AOR = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.26–0.68), and Nouakchott Sud (AOR = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.18–0.49) were less likely to experience diarrhea than those in Hodh Echargui. In contrast, children in Gorgol (AOR = 1.52; 95% CI: 1.14–2.02) and Tagant (AOR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.06–2.01) had higher odds of diarrhea compared to Hodh Echargui.

Discussion

A nationally representative Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) dataset (2019–2021) was used to assess the prevalence of diarrhea among children under five and its contributing factors. In Mauritania, childhood diarrhea exhibited clustering and was influenced by variables at both individual and community levels. At the individual level, significant factors included the child’s age and sex, maternal education and occupational status, and the number of under-five children in the household. At the community level, region and residence were significant determinants.

The study found a diarrhea prevalence of 13.84% in Mauritania. This is lower than reported in Pader District, Northern Uganda (29.1%) [33]; Northern Ethiopia [31]; Nouakchott City, Mauritania (22.4%) [34]; and Southern Ethiopia [35], but higher than in Tanzania (11.9%) [36], Ethiopia (12%) [37], and Malaysia (4.4%) [38]. Differences may be due to sample size, socio-demographic characteristics, study period, latrine coverage and usage, and access to clean water. The PCV value indicated that individual and community-level factors accounted for approximately 32.72% of the variation in childhood diarrhea, consistent with previous studies in East African countries of Burundi, Rwanda, and Tanzania [18, 36], and Ethiopia [37, 39]. Children of parents with lower educational attainment were more likely to experience diarrhea than those whose parents had higher education. This finding aligns with studies from Northwest Ethiopia [19, 31, 35] and Uganda [33] and suggests that education enhances household hygiene practices and parents’ understanding of diarrhea transmission and prevention. Children of unemployed mothers were less likely to experience diarrhea than those of employed mothers. This may be because employed mothers have less time for exclusive breastfeeding or continuous childcare and may rely on alternative caregivers or early complementary feeding, increasing diarrhea risk. This finding is consistent with studies in Ethiopia and Malaysia [35, 38] and Senegal [14]. Supporting this, research in Ethiopia showed that employed women were less likely than nonworking mothers to exclusively breastfeed their infants [40, 41].

The study found that children in households with two or three under-five children had a lower risk of diarrhea compared to those with one or no under-five children, consistent with a study in Ethiopia [41]. This may be due to increased attention to hygiene, as older children guide younger siblings. However, other studies in Ethiopia [42, 43] reported that children in households with three or more under-five children were four times more likely to develop diarrhea than those in households with two or fewer under-five children. The current study also found that childhood diarrhea was significantly associated with age, with children aged 0–1 years at higher risk than older children. This aligns with studies from Ethiopia [40, 41] and may be due to older children having stronger immunity, whereas younger children are more vulnerable to diarrhea from unsanitary diets and poor hygiene. This study also revealedthat there is a significant association between diarrhea and access to toilet facilities. Children from households with improved toilets had lower odds of diarrhea, suggesting that sanitation reduces fecal contamination and limits disease transmission. Improved sanitation also minimizes environmental pollution and prevents mechanical vectors from spreading pathogens, consistent with findings from Senegal [14]. However, studies from Cambodia, Ethiopia, Malawi, the USA, and Zimbabwe [10, 15–17, 31] reported no significant association, highlighting that the protective effect depends on proper use, maintenance, and hygiene practices. Evidence further shows that households that both possess and consistently use latrines experience lower diarrhea morbidity. Therefore, improving access to and promoting the correct use of sanitation facilities remain key strategies for reducing diarrhea among children under five.

The study found that smaller-than-average birth size was significantly associated with higher diarrhea incidence in children, especially in low-income countries where diarrhea is a major cause of infant mortality, consistent with a study in Ethiopia [44]. Unvaccinated children also had a higher risk of diarrhea, underscoring the protective effect of immunization, as supported by studies in Australia, Ethiopia, and Mauritania [19–21]. Furthermore, geographical region significantly predicted childhood diarrhea. Children in Hodh Gharbi, Brakna, Dakhlet Nouadhibou, Nouakchott Ouest, and Nouakchott Sud were less likely to experience diarrhea than those in Hodh Echargui, while children in Gorgol and Tagant were more likely. These differences may reflect disparities in healthcare, water, and sanitation services. Similar findings were reported in studies from the USA and Zimbabwe [6, 19]. Our findings align with Mauritania’s National Strategy for Sustainable Access to Water and Sanitation by 2030 (SNADEA-2030) and the National Child-Health Action Plan, which aim to reduce diarrheal morbidity through universal access to improved sanitation and hygiene promotion [45]. High-risk rural regions, such as Gorgol and Tagant, should be prioritized for community-led total sanitation and integrated immunization campaigns. Targeted investments in maternal education and rural water supply are essential to achieving SDG 3 and SDG 6 targets on child health and safe sanitation [45]. Children in rural areas of Mauritania had a higher risk of diarrhea than those in urban areas, likely due to limited access to clean water, sanitation, and toilet facilities. Similar findings were reported in Ethiopia [19, 31, 40]. This result also aligns with several U.S. studies indicating that cholera vaccines can reduce diarrhea-related morbidity and mortality [46, 47].

Limitation of the study

The study on childhood diarrhea in Mauritania has several limitations, including potential recall bias from self-reported data, the inability to infer causality due to the cross-sectional design, the absence of seasonal analysis, and the pooling of data across multiple time points. Although the use of a multilevel model accounted for data clustering and the nationally representative nature of the DHS data enhanced the accuracy and generalizability of our findings, the impact of unmeasured confounders such as dietary practices (beyond basic infant and young child feeding indicators), pathogen exposure, and caregiver hygiene behaviors was not comprehensively analyzed in this study and may influence the results.

Conclusion

The purpose of this study was to determine the prevalence of diarrhea among children under five and identify its contributing factors. The findings indicate that both individual- and community-level variables influence childhood diarrhea. Individual-level factors include the child’s age, the mother’s occupation, father education, the number of children under five in the household, access to toilet facilities, the child’s birth size, and immunization status. Community-level factors significantly associated with diarrhea were place of residence and geographic location. To reduce the high prevalence of childhood diarrhea in Mauritania, we recommend scaling up WASH (water, sanitation, and hygiene) infrastructure and ensuring access to safe water in high-risk regions (e.g., Gorgol and Tagant) through community-led total sanitation (CLTS) programs and public investment in piped water and improved latrines; strengthening rotavirus immunization by integrating vaccine delivery into routine Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) visits, conducting catch-up vaccination campaigns in rural areas, and training community health workers to address vaccine hesitancy; supporting maternal education on feeding, hygiene, and breastfeeding through behavior-change communication (BCC) interventions delivered via antenatal care, radio messaging in local languages, and peer-led mother groups focused on optimal breastfeeding, handwashing, and safe stool disposal; and enhancing disease surveillance and monitoring systems while implementing targeted regional interventions, accompanied by research to evaluate program impact.
