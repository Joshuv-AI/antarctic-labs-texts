AstaGen : Empirical Evaluation of Automated SATD Taxonomy Generation with LLMs
Sota Nakashima 1 , Yuta Ishimoto 1 , Masanari Kondo 1 , Tao Xiao 1 , Yasutaka Kamei 1

Abstract
Technical debt refers to suboptimal code that degrades software quality. When developers intentionally introduce such debt, it is called self-admitted technical debt (SATD) . Since SATD hinders maintenance, identifying its categories is key to uncovering quality issues. Traditionally, constructing such taxonomies requires manually inspecting SATD comments and surrounding code, which is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and often inconsistent due to annotator subjectivity. This study presents AstaGen , an initial step toward automating SATD taxonomy generation using large language models (LLMs). Given a comment and its surrounding code, AstaGen first generates a concise explanation for each SATD comment, then incrementally generates and updates categories to construct a taxonomy. We evaluate AstaGen on SATD datasets from three domains: quantum software, smart contracts, and machine learning. It successfully recovers domain-specific categories reported in prior work, such as Layer Configuration in machine learning. Compared to a naive use of an LLM, AstaGen produces more consistent category assignments due to its explanation-driven, iterative design. It also completes taxonomy generation in under two hours and for less than one USD, even on the largest dataset. These results suggest that while full automation remains challenging, AstaGen is able to support semi-automated taxonomy construction. Furthermore, our work opens up avenues for future work, such as automatic taxonomy generation in other areas.



I
Introduction

Developers sometimes introduce suboptimal code that degrades software quality; this is known as
technical debt

[
1
]
. In particular, when developers intentionally insert such code due to constraints such as budget or schedule, it is referred to as
Self-Admitted Technical Debt (SATD)

[
2
]
. In practice, developers explicitly mark SATD via code comments.

SATD can negatively affect software maintenance and operation. Consequently, identifying SATD categories and addressing them can improve code quality
[
3
,
4
]
. Previous studies therefore developed reusable SATD taxonomies that systematically organize these debts
[
3
,
5
,
6
]
.
For example, Maldonado and Shihab
[
3
]
defined a taxonomy for Java projects with five categories: design, requirement, defect, test, and documentation. Ishimoto et al.
[
5
]
extended this idea to quantum software by incorporating quantum-specific factors, and Ebrahimi et al.
[
6
]
developed a taxonomy for smart contracts.
Similarly, Obrien et al.
[
7
]
proposed a taxonomy tailored to machine learning systems.

While SATD taxonomies help in detecting and remedying debt, creating them is costly because it requires at least two researchers to manually analyze SATD comments
[
3
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
. While existing taxonomies can be reused within the same domain (e.g., Java), new domains (e.g., machine learning software) require building a new taxonomy. Moreover, manual analysis introduces the subjectivity of researchers and suffers from poor reproducibility; different teams may produce different taxonomies. Therefore, automating SATD taxonomy generation remains a critical research challenge.

To this end, we propose a large language model (LLM)-driven approach for SATD taxonomy generation, which we call
AstaGen
(
A
utomated
S
ATD
TA
xonomy
GEN
eration).
Traditional clustering techniques and learning-based classifiers struggle to (a) understand the issues raised by SATD comments and (b) generate new categories for them, even though they can group similar comments or fit them into existing categories.
To address these limitations, we design
AstaGen
as a two-phase LLM-based approach that
(a’) generates a concise explanation of the issue raised by each SATD comment, and
(b’) iteratively generates and updates categories based on these explanations to construct a coherent taxonomy.

We evaluated
AstaGen
across three domains (i.e., quantum software
[
5
]
, smart contracts
[
6
]
, and machine learning software
[
7
]
) using human-defined taxonomies as references.

AstaGen
successfully generated domain-specific categories that were semantically similar to those defined by humans, such as
Layer Configuration
in machine learning.
Compared to a naive use of an LLM,
AstaGen
generated more consistent taxonomy due to its explanation-driven, iterative design.
In addition,
AstaGen
completed taxonomy generation in under two hours and at a cost of less than one USD, even for the largest dataset containing 448 comments, highlighting its efficiency compared to manual approaches.

Our main contributions are as follows:
(1) We propose
AstaGen
, an automated SATD taxonomy generation approach using LLMs.
(2)
AstaGen
can generate domain-specific categories that are semantically similar to those defined by humans.
(3)
AstaGen
generates taxonomies that are more aligned with human-defined ones compared to a naive use of an LLM.
(4)
AstaGen
is a time- and cost-friendly approach; it runs in under two hours and for less than one USD.
(5) We suggest practical use cases for
AstaGen
in semi-automated taxonomy generation supported by our empirical results.

II
Related Work

II-A

Construction of SATD Taxonomies

To support the understanding of SATD, prior studies have investigated both the types and frequencies of SATD comments in source code
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
8
]
.
In particular, constructing a SATD taxonomy (i.e., classification scheme for SATD comments) has been recognized as essential for achieving a deeper understanding of technical debt in software projects.

Constructing such a taxonomy involves two main tasks. First, researchers identify SATD-related comments by applying keyword-based filters, typically regular expressions for markers such as
todo
and
fixme

[
2
,
3
]
, followed by manual validation to confirm their relevance.
Second, they categorize the filtered comments by manual inspection: at least two researchers read the comments, propose category labels, and reconcile their labels through discussion until consensus is reached.
For example, Ishimoto et al.
[
5
]
engaged three researchers to manually inspect 202 comments, identifying 193 SATD instances (including 88 quantum-specific cases) to construct a domain-tailored taxonomy.
Similarly, Ebrahimi et al.
[
6
]
had two authors manually review 287 SATD comments to extend existing taxonomies to smart contracts.

Although manual taxonomy construction is crucial for understanding SATD, it suffers from three major limitations.
(1) It is time-consuming:
Xiao et al.
[
8
]
required 120 person-hours to inspect 500 SATD comments.
(2) It does not scale: Kashiwa et al.
[
9
]
analyzed only 375 of 15,671 SATD comments to curb manual effort.
(3) It depends heavily on the prior knowledge and experience of the annotators: when independently labeling 50 comments, Ebrahimi et al.
[
6
]
observed 12 inconsistencies and relied on the second author, who had greater smart contract expertise, to resolve them.
These issues stem from the manual nature of taxonomy construction.
Therefore, we propose an initial approach toward automating SATD taxonomy generation using LLMs.

II-C

LLMs as a Replacement for Manual Annotation

Some prior studies have explored the potential of LLMs to replace human annotators in software engineering tasks
[
15
,
16
,
17

.

[

examined whether LLMs can replace human annotators in software engineering tasks.
Their study involves ten annotation tasks, including assessing the quality of code summaries, all of which are framed as multiple choice questions with predefined answer options.
Their results indicate that LLMs can replace human annotators for some tasks with comparable agreement levels.
Our target task, SATD taxonomy generation, is disjoint from theirs, as it requires generating category labels rather than selecting from predefined ones.
Indeed, they left such tasks (i.e., open coding) to future work.
We push this direction forward through the task of SATD taxonomy generation.

III
AstaGen: Automated SATD Taxonomy Generation by LLMs

III-A

Overview

Figure
2
illustrates the overall workflow of
AstaGen
.

Input and Output.

The input consists of a set of SATD comments and their corresponding source code.
The output is a SATD taxonomy generated by LLMs based on these inputs.
The resulting classification scheme is structured as a two-level tree.
We refer to the upper and lower levels of this hierarchy as the
main categories
and
subcategories
, respectively.
This structure aligns with most existing studies on SATD taxonomy construction
[
4
,
6
,
9
,
5
]
, which adopt trees with at least two hierarchical levels.
In addition to generating the taxonomy,
AstaGen
also assigns each input comment to a specific main category and subcategory in the resulting taxonomy.

Workflow.

AstaGen
consists of two phases:
(1) generating explanations for SATD, and
(2) generating and updating categories at each level of the taxonomy.
The two-phase design aims to efficiently generate the SATD taxonomy while accommodating the context length limitations of LLMs.
In the first phase, concise explanations serve as compressed representations of SATD comments and their corresponding source code.
In the second phase, the taxonomy is iteratively updated after each batch of comments, helping LLMs maintain consistency for generated categories.
This iterative refinement mimics the common practice in human-driven taxonomy generation, where categories are periodically reviewed after processing a certain number of comments
[
4
,
5
,
8
]
.
The following subsections describe each phase in detail.

III-B

Phase 1: Generating Explanations for SATD

In this phase, the LLM generates a concise explanation (typically 2–3 sentences) for each SATD comment, describing its content and context.
Prior studies have shown that the surrounding source code provides useful context for understanding SATD
[
14
,
12
]
.
Therefore, our prompts include not only the comment itself but also the relevant source code.
Specifically, we include the entire file in which the comment appears, enabling the LLM to generate explanations that reflect both the comment and the associated code issue.

III-C

Phase 2: Generating/Updating Categories at Each Level

In this phase, the LLM constructs a SATD taxonomy consisting of main and subcategories.
The taxonomy is built hierarchically: main categories are generated first, followed by subcategories.
Each level involves two steps—
category generation
and
update
—applied iteratively in batches, each containing a fixed number of SATD explanations.
This top-down structure allows the LLM to focus on a single level of abstraction at a time.
As batching proceeds, the list of categories is continuously updated until all comments are processed.
The output of this phase is a complete two-level taxonomy derived after processing all batches at the subcategory level.
The following describes the generation and update steps.

III-C
1
Generation Step

In this step, the LLM generates a category name of approximately 1–3 words for each comment included in the batch, based on its corresponding SATD explanation.
All explanations in the current batch are enumerated and presented together in a single prompt.
The LLM then produces a category name for each explanation individually.

III-C
2
Update Step

In this step, the categories generated in the current batch are integrated with those from previous batches to update the category list.

First, the LLM integrates newly generated categories with those from previous batches.
The prompt includes category names and their associated explanations: all explanations from the current batch, and up to three randomly sampled explanations per category from previous batches.
The LLM is instructed to merge semantically similar categories based on this information.
By comparing category names before and after integration, we obtain a mapping of name changes.

Second, the mapping is used to update the category list.
This ensures consistency across all processed comments.
Since this update involves only applying the name-change mapping, it does not require further use of the LLM.

Finally,
AstaGen
outputs a category assignment for each comment.
These categories are based on the SATD taxonomy generated by
AstaGen
, which is represented as a two-level taxonomy tree consisting of main categories and subcategories.

IV
Study Design

IV-A

Research Questions

RQ1

(Category name alignment)
Can
AstaGen
generate category names that are semantically similar to those defined by humans?

RQ2

(Taxonomy content alignment)

Does
AstaGen
achieve better alignment with human-defined taxonomies than a naive use of an LLM, in terms of comment assignment and category granularity?

RQ3

(Efficiency)
How much time and cost does
AstaGen
require?

RQ1 and RQ2 aim to compare the taxonomy generated by
AstaGen
with that created by humans.
RQ1 focuses on the similarity of category names, while RQ2 examines the consistency of the comments assigned to each category.
RQ3 assesses the practicality of
AstaGen
by measuring the execution time and monetary cost (i.e., API fees for LLMs) required to generate the SATD categories.

IV-B

Studied Datasets

We use SATD comments and corresponding taxonomies from prior studies as our datasets.
The SATD comments are input to
AstaGen
, while the human-defined taxonomies serve as references for evaluation.

We collected papers that constructed SATD taxonomies from two major digital libraries: IEEE Xplore and the ACM Digital Library.
Specifically, we used the following query to search for papers whose abstracts contained specific keywords:

("SATD" OR "self-admitted technical debt") AND ("taxonomy" OR "category" OR "coding")
.
The part before
AND
is intended to capture papers related to SATD, while the part after
AND
aims to capture those involving taxonomy or category construction.
After removing duplicates, this query returned 79 papers.

IV-C

Selected Models

In all experiments, we used Deepseek-V3
[
20
]
as the LLM, with the default temperature setting of 1.0.
Since the output of the LLM can vary across runs, we repeated all experiments ten times to assess the stability of the generated results.

In RQ1, we evaluate the semantic similarity between pairs of category names—those defined by humans and those generated by
AstaGen
.
We encode each category name into a 384-dimensional vector using the pre-trained sentence embedding model
all-MiniLM-L6-v2
,
2
2

2

https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2
and compute the cosine similarity between the resulting vectors.
We selected a sentence-level embedding model because category names often consist of multiple words, and such models are better suited for capturing their overall semantics.

IV-D

Prompt Design

In Phase 1 of
AstaGen
(Section
III-B
), we limited the source code context given to the LLM to 2,000 lines to comply with its context length limit.
If the file exceeded this limit, we included only the 1,000 lines before and after the SATD comment.
We used the line numbers available in the original datasets to accurately locate each comment.

In Phase 2 of
AstaGen
(Section
III-C
), we used a batch size of 20.
Specifically, SATD explanations were grouped into batches of 20, and each batch was processed with a separate prompt.

Evaluation Metrics

This section describes the evaluation metrics used in each RQ.

\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{M}
be the sets of human-defined and

-generated category names, respectively.
We denote individual category names from these sets as
H_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
and

.

RQ1

We compute the cosine similarity between the sentence embeddings for
H_{i}
and

, denoted as
sim(H_{i},M_{j})
.
We then compute
top\_sim(H_{i})=\max_{j}sim(H_{i},M_{j})
, which represents the highest similarity between
H_{i}
and any generated name.
This score quantifies how closely
AstaGen
can generate a category name for each human-defined category.

top\_sim(H_{i})
for each dataset and level to assess the alignment between human-defined and

-generated category names.

IV-E
2
RQ2

We evaluate whether similar comments are grouped into similar categories by humans and
AstaGen
.
Unlike classification problems,
H_{i}
and
M_{j}
are drawn from different sets (i.e.,
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{M}
), making conventional precision and recall inapplicable.
To address this, we define a
best-match precision and recall
for each human-defined category.
Let
C_{ij}
denote the number of comments assigned to both
H_{i}
(by humans) and
M_{j}
(by
AstaGen
).
The matrix
C
enables the construction of a Sankey diagram (e.g., Figure
3
) that visualizes the flow of comments between human-defined and
AstaGen
-generated categories.
4
4

4

The thickness of each flow from
H_{i}
to
M_{j}
is proportional to
C_{ij}
.

For each
H_{i}
, we define its
best-matched category
as
M_{j^{*}}
, where
j^{*}=\operatorname{argmax}_{j}C_{ij}
.
Using this best match, we compute:

\displaystyle\text{precision}(H_{i})=\frac{C_{ij^{*}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{H}|}C_{ij^{*}}},\quad\text{recall}(H_{i})=\frac{C_{ij^{*}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{M}|}C_{ij}}.

Here, precision reflects how exclusively the best-matched category corresponds to the human-defined category
H_{i}
, while recall indicates how well the comments in
H_{i}
are covered by the best-matched category.
We also report the F1 score as the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

Our proposed metrics quantify the correspondence between human-defined and
AstaGen
-generated categories as visualized in the Sankey diagram.
For example, in Figure
3
, the human-defined category
Backend
is best matched with
Quantum System Integration
from
AstaGen
, yielding a precision of 0.45 and a recall of 0.76.
As shown in Figure
3
,
Backend
category is largely covered by
Quantum System Integration
, indicating high recall.
On the other hand, since
Quantum System Integration
also includes comments from other human-defined categories, its precision is low.

While these metrics quantify the degree of comment-level alignment between human-defined and
AstaGen
-generated categories, they are sensitive to the total number of categories.
For instance, if
AstaGen
generates only a few categories, recall can become high because all the comments in a human-defined category may fall under a single generated category.
Therefore, we also report the difference in the number of generated and human-defined categories:
|\mathcal{M}|-|\mathcal{H}|
.
A smaller absolute value indicates that the number of categories generated by
AstaGen
more closely matches the human-defined categories. We refer to this aspect as
category granularity
.

VI
Use Case: Semi-Automated Taxonomy Generation

Although
AstaGen
may not fully automate SATD taxonomy generation—as RQ1 and RQ2 showed that its alignment with human-defined taxonomies was not always high—it remains a practical
assistant
for reducing human effort.
We propose two use cases for
AstaGen
:
(1) as a
virtual annotator
that can replace some human annotators in collaborative taxonomy construction, and
(2) as an
initial step
that provides category candidates to support annotators.
These use cases support semi-automated taxonomy generation.
By incorporating these human-in-the-loop workflows, it becomes possible to balance the efficiency of automation (as demonstrated in RQ3) with the quality of the resulting taxonomy through human review.

Notably,
AstaGen
has demonstrated the ability to generate domain-specific categories. This makes it especially valuable when researchers must construct a taxonomy in an unfamiliar domain. By incorporating
AstaGen
as one of the contributors, researchers can accelerate the early stages of taxonomy construction.
In addition to improving
AstaGen
, evaluating these use cases remains future work.

VII
Threats to Validity

Construct Validity.

In RQ2, we evaluate the alignment of
AstaGen
-generated taxonomies with human-defined ones using best-match precision and recall.
This evaluation is conducted in a relative manner using the naive variant, and therefore lacks an absolute evaluation.
Further investigation is needed to examine the correlation between these metric values and human preferences for each generated category.
This limitation is part of a broader challenge known as
alignment evaluation
for LLMs
[
21
]
.

Finally, we treat the final human-defined categories from the original studies as references, although these may be inconsistent or incomplete.

Internal Validity.

The outputs of
AstaGen
depend on the behavior of the underlying LLM (DeepSeek-V3), which is stochastic by nature.
To mitigate variability, we repeated each run ten times.
Still, some outcomes may be sensitive to prompt design or batch composition, particularly in Phase 2, where category merging occurs.

External Validity.

Our evaluation used SATD datasets from three domains—quantum software, smart contracts, and machine learning software.
While these cover diverse domains, they may not generalize to all types of software projects.

VIII
Conclusion

We present
AstaGen
, an initial step toward automating SATD taxonomy generation using LLMs.
Across three datasets,
AstaGen
(i) recovered domain-specific categories reported in prior studies (e.g.,
Layer Configuration
in the ML dataset), (ii) outperformed a naive baseline in comment assignment by up to 0.13 F1, and (iii) completed the entire process for 448 comments in under two hours at a cost of less than one USD even for the largest dataset.

Our findings suggest that fully automating SATD taxonomy generation remains a challenging task.
Nevertheless, employing
AstaGen
as a virtual annotator or as an initial step to propose candidate categories may help reduce human effort.
In future work, we plan to explore semi-automated, human-in-the-loop workflows and examine the trade-offs between the degree of automation and taxonomy quality.
Furthermore, extending automated taxonomy generation beyond SATD to other open coding tasks (e.g., a taxonomy of root causes of fuzzing build failure
[
22
]
) remains an promising direction for future research.
