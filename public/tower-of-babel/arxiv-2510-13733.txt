Internal Diffusion Limited Aggregation with Critical Branching Random Walks
Amine Asselah, Vittoria Silvestri, Lorenzo Taggi

Abstract
Internal Diffusion Limited Aggregation is an interacting particle system that describes the growth of a random cluster governed by the boundary harmonic measure seen from an internal point. Our paper studies IDLA in ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} driven by critical branching random walks. We prove that, unlike classical IDLA, this process exhibits a phase transition in the dimension. More precisely, we establish the existence of a spherical shape theorem in dimension d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 and the absence of a spherical shape theorem for d ≤ 2 d\leq 2 . Our bounds on the inner and outer worst deviations are of polynomial nature, which we expect to be a feature of this model.



1
Introduction

Internal Diffusion Limited Aggregation (IDLA) is a mathematical model aimed at describing erosion processes driven by diffusing particles. In its classical version on the cubic lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the process starts from an aggregate made of a single site, the origin
\{0\}
. We say that the origin is
occupied
, and the other sites are
empty
. At each iterative step, a new diffusing particle is started from the origin. The particle moves as a simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
until it finds an empty site, where it stops forever. We say that the particle
settles
at that site, and the site is added to the current aggregate.
Equivalently, the aggregate grows by swallowing boundary sites, one at the time, according to the
harmonic measure on the boundary seen from the origin
, namely the exit law from the aggregate of a simple random walk starting at
\{0\}
.

This model was introduced by Meakin and Deutch as an erosion process in 1986
[
26
]
, and independently by Diaconis and Fulton as an Abelian process in 1991
[
10
]
. We will come back later to the meaning of Abelianity in this context.
Right from its introduction, the first question to be addressed on IDLA was that of a shape theorem, namely whether rescaled IDLA aggregates approach a deterministic shape. This was established by Lawler, Bramson and Griffeath in 1992
[
20
]
: the shape is asymptotically an Euclidean ball on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for any
d\geq 1
. Nearly two decades later, IDLA maximal deviations from circularity were shown to be logarithmic in the radius of the limiting ball in any dimension
d\geq 2
by Asselah and Gaudillière and, independently, Jerison, Levine and Sheffield
[
1
,
2
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
.

In this paper we introduce a new variant of IDLA, in which the dynamics is driven by
Critical Branching Random Walks
in place of simple random walks.
Recall that, given a probability distribution
\nu
on
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2\dots\}
with strictly positive variance, a Branching Random Walk (BRW) on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
starting from a vertex
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is defined as follows. At time zero a single particle is placed at
z
. The particle has an exponential clock: when the clock rings, the particle splits into
a random number of indistinguishable particles at the same site (its
children
) with law
\nu
, which each take an independent and instantaneous simple random walk step on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. After that, each particle continues evolving as its parent, independently of the others. We refer the reader to Section
2.1
for a formal definition. If
\nu
has mean
1
, the BRW is said to be
critical
, and it is known that the associated branching process dies out in finite time almost surely.

Here we propose to use critical BRWs to drive IDLA dynamics. The (discrete time) BIDLA process
(A(t))_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is defined as follows. Set
A(0)=\emptyset
and
A(1)=\{0\}
. For all
t\geq 1
, the aggregate (or
cluster
)
A(t)
is a finite connected subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We say that sites in
A(t)
are
occupied
, while sites outside
A(t)
are
empty
. For each
t\geq 1
define
A(t+1)
from
A(t)
inductively, by
releasing a critical BRW from the origin, with the rule that when a particle reaches an empty site the particle stops there forever (in which case we say it
settled
). The site is then declared
occupied
, and the following particles stepping on it will continue their evolution until reaching a new empty site. Since the BRW is critical, after finitely many steps all particles will have settled. We call the resulting configuration the
BIDLA stabilization
of
A(t)+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{0}
, and denote it by
\mathscr{S}(A(t)+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{0})
. Then define

A(t+1):=\mathscr{S}(A(t)+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{0}).

In words, at each discrete time step
t\geq 1
a critical BRW is released from the origin in
A(t)
with settling upon visiting empty sites, and the settling locations are added to the cluster to make
A(t+1)
.
See Figure
1
below for simulations of BIDLA clusters on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, and Section
2.2
for a formal definition of the model.

Figure 1:
Four simulations of BIDLA clusters on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
at time
t=20000
, plotted together with a disc of radius
\sqrt{t/\pi}
. Here the different colours represent the arrival time of particles (where time is indexed by particle releases).

The phenomenology of Branching Random Walks with settling, and hence of BIDLA, is drastically different from that of simple random walks.
To start with, notice that while in classical IDLA launching one particle from the origin results in the addition of exactly one particle to the aggregate, in BIDLA new particles are created and some are lost due to the nature of the driving Branching Random Walks. Thus, even though

\mathbb{E}(|A(t)\setminus A(t-1)|)=1,

(see Lemma
2.2
below), in a single time step the cardinality of a cluster may, a priori, grow by an arbitrarily large amount. Furthermore, the exit locations of the arbitrarily many particles which are added to the cluster in one time step are correlated, and may be clumped together, with particles stepping on each other to reach sites which are far away from the cluster. These two new and crucial features of BIDLA play a fundamental role in the spacial growth of clusters, and cause classical approaches to the proof of shape theorems to fail in this setting.
To tackle this issue we introduce a new auxiliary process of independent interest called
Random Barrier Growth
, which can be coupled with BIDLA and whose growth is easier to bound.

1.1
Statement of results

We address the question of the existence of a deterministic limiting shape for BIDLA aggregates.
Our results hold under the following assumption on the offspring distribution
\nu
on
\mathbb{N}
, which is in force throughout the paper:

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k\nu(k)=1,\qquad\sigma^{2}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k-1)^{2}\nu(k)>0,\qquad\exists\lambda>0\;:\;\,\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{\lambda k}\nu(k)<\infty.

(H)

For
r>0
let
B_{r}:=\{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\|z\|<r\}

denote the Euclidean ball of radius
r
around the origin.
Then for any
\varepsilon>0
denote by

\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}
the set of
\varepsilon
-symmetric clusters, defined as

\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}:=\big\{A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\ \exists r>0\mbox{ s.t. }B_{(1-\varepsilon)r}\subseteq A\subseteq B_{(1+\varepsilon)r}\big\}.

(1.1)

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(A(t))_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
denote a BIDLA process on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
satisfying Assumption (
H
).

•

If
d=1,2
then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\big(A(t)\not\in\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}\mbox{ infinitely often}\big)=1.

•

If
d\geq 3
then
\forall\varepsilon>0

\mathbb{P}\big(A(t)\in\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}\mbox{ eventually}\big)=1.

This shows that BIDLA exhibits a
phase transition
, in that the existence of a spherical shape theorem depends on the dimension.
If
d\geq 3
the deterministic limiting shape is the same as classical IDLA on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. If
d=1
the barycenter of the aggregate (which on
\mathbb{Z}
is simply an interval) moves macroscopically infinitely often. Finally, if
d=2
our results would prove that there is no shape theorem provided one could rule out the existence of asymptotic shapes other than Euclidean balls.

In dimension three and more we are able to prove stronger estimates for the maximal deviations from circularity. To state them, for any
t\geq 1
let
r(t)
denote the radius of a Euclidean ball of volume
t
centered at the origin, so that by the shape theorem
A(t)
is close to
B_{r(t)}
eventually in
t
, almost surely.
We define

\begin{split}\delta^{I}(t)&:=\inf\{\delta\geq 0:B_{r(t)-\delta}\subseteq A(t)\}\\
\delta^{O}(t)&:=\inf\{\delta\geq 0:A(t)\subseteq B_{r(t)+\delta}\}.\end{split}

Then
\delta^{I}(t)
and
\delta^{O}(t)
measure respectively the inner and outer maximal deviation from circularity of
A(t)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(A(t))_{t\geq 0}
denote a BIDLA process in dimension
d>2
satisfying Assumption (
H
). Then for any
\epsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big\{\delta^{I}(t)>t^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}\Big\}\cup\Big\{\delta^{O}(t)>t^{1-\frac{1}{2d}+\epsilon}\Big\}\,\,\text{infinitely often in }t\Big)=0.

(1.2)

We conclude with a deviation result which is interesting on its own, expressing the fact that a number of particles proportional to the volume of a ball
B_{n}
covers it with overwhelming probability when
d>2
,
regardless of their specific placement inside
B_{n/2}
.
Moreover, the same holds with uniformly positive probability when
d=2
.

Let
\eta:\mathbb{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{N}
be an arbitrary particle configuration supported inside the ball
B_{n/2}
for some
n\geq 1
, where
\eta(z)
denotes the number of particles at
z
for all
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Recall that
\mathscr{S}(\eta)
denotes the BIDLA stabilization of
\eta
.
For a subset
K
of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we denote by
|K|
the cardinality of
K
, while if
\eta
is a particle configuration we let

|\eta|:=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\eta(x)

denote the total number of particles in
\eta
.

Lemma 1.3
.

For any
d>2
there exists a constant
\alpha
large enough such that for any integer
n
and any particle configuration
\eta
supported in
B_{n/2}
with
|\eta|\geq\alpha|B_{n}|
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\big(B_{n}\not\subseteq\mathscr{S}(\eta)\big)\leq\exp\Big(-C\frac{\alpha\cdot n}{\log n\cdot{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d=3}+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d>3}}\Big)

(1.3)

for some positive and finite constants
C
, depending only on the dimension.

Moreover, for any
d\geq 2
there exists a constant
\alpha
large enough such that for any integer
n
and any particle configuration
\eta
supported in
B_{n/2}
with
|\eta|\leq\alpha\cdot|B_{n}|
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\big(B_{n}\not\subseteq\mathscr{S}(\eta)\big)\geq\exp\Big(-c\cdot\alpha\cdot n^{d-2}\Big)

(1.4)

for some positive and finite constants
c
, depending only on the dimension.

In contrast, for classical IDLA, under the same hypotheses, the upper bound reads

\mathbb{P}\big(B_{n}\not\subseteq\mathscr{S}(\eta)\big)\leq\exp\Big(-C\frac{\alpha\cdot n^{2}}{\log n\cdot{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d=2}+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d>2}}\Big)

(1.5)

(see Lemma 1.3 of
[
2
]
).
This shows that, even though in dimension
d=3
both classical IDLA and BIDLA have a spherical shape theorem, the large deviation behaviours of the two processes differ significantly.

1.2
Sketch of the proof

We include here a brief overview of the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

1.2.1
No shape theorem in low dimension

In order to show that BIDLA is infinitely often asymmetric almost surely, it suffices to prove that once in a symmetric state, the probability that the process gets to an asymmetric state in one step
1
1

1

Here we consider the jump process associated to BIDLA, so that the cluster changes at every step, as explained at the beginning of Section
4
.
is uniformly positive. To this end, we show that when launching one BRW from the origin conditioned on exiting the current cluster, the following happens with positive probability:

(i)

If the cluster is close to a ball
B_{n}
, the BRW reaches the inner boundary of
B_{n}
with order
n^{2}
particles.

(ii)

These particles are clumped together.

(iii)

Settling the localised particles creates the claimed asymmetry.

Points (i) and (ii) are properties of a single BRW conditioned on travelling to distance
n
from its starting point, and are proved in Section
4.1
. The third statement (iii) accounts for settling, and it is proved by combining the classical approach of Lawler, Bramson and Griffeath with new second moment estimates on the local times of BRW. The necessity of controlling higher moments of the local times, rather than only the first moment, is a feature of Branching IDLA, and it stems from the randomness of the number of individuals during the time evolution of a single BRW. The needed second moment estimates are proved in Section
3.2
.

1.2.2
Shape theorem in high dimension

Our proof of the shape theorem for
d>2
consists of three steps, namely:

Step 1.

Establishing an
inner bound
, that is proving that the aggregate contains a Euclidean ball of roughly the right radius, even when particles are
frozen
upon reaching the boundary of this ball.

Step 2.

Bounding the number of frozen particles on the boundary of the filled ball.

Step 3.

Releasing the frozen particles in
waves
, and iteratively showing that each wave results in the settling of a positive proportion of the particles. This sets up a
contraction
in the number of particles which, after sufficiently many iterations, gives the desired outer bound.

Step 1 is carried out in Section
5
following the classical approach by Lawler, Bramson and Griffeath
[
20
]
together with an independence property observed first by Asselah and Gaudillière in
[
1
]
. We then take care of Step 2 in Proposition
5.2
, where we show that, on the event that the inner ball is filled (which has high probability by Step 1), only a small proportion of particles is frozen on the boundary of the ball. This requires the finite exponential moment assumption (
H
) on the offspring distribution
\nu
, which is in force throughout the paper.

The main technical novelty in our approach comes in Step 3, where we have to show that releasing the frozen particles does not cause the cluster to grow macroscopically. This is typically easy for classical IDLA (see the robust approaches of
[
20
]
and
[
11
]
), but for Branching IDLA it becomes far from trivial due to the creation/loss of particles, as well as the fact that close-by particles can help each other travel far within one BIDLA step. To control the growth of the aggregate we introduce a new auxiliary process called
Random Barrier Growth
, which is both easier to control than BIDLA and it
grows faster
than BIDLA itself. Hence, thanks to the Abelian property, from an outer bound on the auxiliary process, we can deduce an outer bound for BIDLA clusters.

In
Random Barrier Growth
settling is only allowed on spherical layers.
This restriction, while artificial at first sight, turns out to be a powerful tool, because the Green’s function of a random walk killed when leaving a ball is much better understood, thereby giving us a precise handle on the number of particles that settle on the layer.
A further crucial ingredient is that these spherical layers are not fixed in advance, but are sampled at random according to a carefully chosen distribution.
This randomness plays a decisive role in providing an upper bound for the number of particles which do not settle on the layer (see also Remark
6.6
).
Iterating this construction for successive layers, one obtains a contraction mechanism which ultimately yields the outer bound for BIDLA clusters.
This construction is carried out in Section
6
, with the crucial contraction bound stated in Proposition
6.5
.

1.3
Related models and open questions

As mentioned above, Internal DLA aggregates, or
clusters
, grow according to the harmonic measure on the cluster’s boundary seen from an
internal
point (the origin). The dynamics is therefore smoothing, in that arms grow slowly and fjords are filled quickly. This makes the shape theorem stable under small changes of the model, such as modifying the base graph or the transition probabilities of the driving simple random walks. See e.g.
[
11
,
25
,
33
,
7
]
and references therein for some results in this direction. We also mention a recent work by
Benjamini, Duminil-Copin, Kozma and Lucas, who
introduced a variant of IDLA where the starting point of a new random walk is chosen uniformly at random inside the aggregate, thereby proving that the limiting shape remains spherical
[
5
]
.

If, on the other hand, rather than using the internal harmonic measure one grows clusters according to the
external harmonic measure
on the boundary, i.e. the hitting measure of a simple random walk starting far away from the aggregate, one obtains
Diffusion Limited Aggregation
, in short DLA, a model which is notoriously hard to analyse
[
34
,
9
,
13
]
. DLA clusters are fractal-looking, with a few arms growing faster than the bulk. The mechanism for this reinforcement is still escaping a rigorous understanding, the only available result on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
dating back to 1987, when Kesten proved an upper bound for the growth rate of DLA clusters
[
19
]
.

In order to tame the reinforcement leading to fractality, one could grow clusters according to a
fractional
power of the external harmonic measure. This defines
Dielectric Breakdown Models
, and it reduces to the
Eden Model
when this power vanishes
[
27
,
12
]
. While the Eden model is well studied due to its coupling with
First Passage Percolation
, a mathematical description of Dielectric Breakdown aggregates is still far out of reach. We mention the recent work of Losev and Smirnov
[
24
]
, which provides bounds for the clusters’ growth rate à la Kesten.
Recent progress on the analysis of two-dimensional random aggregation models where the growth is driven by a fractional power of the (external) harmonic measure was achieved by working in the continuum, where clusters can be described via random conformal maps, and the correlation strength between those maps determines the boundary growth rate. See
[
13
,
28
,
29
]
and references therein on Hastings-Levitov models and Aggregate Loewner Evolution.

From the point of view of particle systems, Internal DLA belongs to a larger family of models called

Activated Random Walks
, where settling is neither eternal nor instantaneous:
a particle stops (or settles) at an exponential time of rate
\lambda
, called the
sleep parameter
, and it regains activity if another particle steps on its settling site. The dynamics of an ARW system depends on the parameter
\lambda
and on the initial configuration of particles. Classical IDLA corresponds to
\lambda=\infty
(particles settle, or fall asleep, instantaneously when at an empty site), and all particles started at the origin. When
\lambda<\infty
the analysis is more difficult due to the long-range correlations and the non-monotonicity of the system. According to the values of the sleep parameters and particle density, ARW in infinite volume undergoes a
phase transition
from eternal activity to stabilization, which has been object of extensive research
[
31
,
32
]
. The main interest in this model lies in the fact that it is widely believed to exhibit
Self Organised Criticality
and
Universality

[
4
,
30
,
23
]
, which was recently proved to be the case in dimension
d=1
by Hoffman, Johnson and Junge
[
15
]
.
One could also look at ARW dynamics as a growth process by releasing finitely many particles from the origin and asking whether the visited region until stabilization approaches a deterministic shape. A first result in this direction was obtained in dimension one by Levine and Silvestri
[
22
]
, followed by the proof of a shape theorem by Hoffman, Johnson and Junge
[
15
]
.

The problem of describing the aggregate’s growth has also been studied in the so-called Oil and Water model
[
6
]
, which is conjectured to satisfy a spherical shape theorem as in classical IDLA, though with a slower growth rate.
This conjecture has been partially resolved in one dimension by Candellero, Ganguly, Hoffman, Levine
[
14
]
.
As shown in
[
8
]
,
the model can be described in terms of BRW whose offspring law is random, mainly critical but occasionally subcritical.
We believe that the techniques introduced in the present article could contribute to further progress on Oil and Water and related models.

1.3.1
Open questions

We conclude with some open questions which we believe deserve further investigation.
In dimensions
d>2
, where a shape theorem is available, a central challenge is to determine the size of the fluctuations.
Unlike classical IDLA, we expect these fluctuations to grow on a polynomial scale, and pinning down their precise order would provide a deeper understanding of the model.
In two dimensions it would be very interesting to exclude the existence of a deterministic non-spherical limiting shape, thereby confirming a fundamental qualitative difference with the classical case. Is there a Markovian dynamics defined directly in the continuum which could be seen as the evolution in time of rescaled BIDLA in
d=2
?
Finally, the behaviour under sub-critical offspring distributions
\nu
remains largely unexplored. In this case we expect the limiting shape to lose the spherical symmetry, as sub-critical BRWs conditioned to survive up to a large distance from their origin would be ballistic rather than diffusive.

Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we provide a formal definition of Branching Random Walks
and Branching IDLA, and fix the notation.
In Section
3
we present new estimates for the second moment of the local time of BRW restricted to finite sets, and discuss a classical approach to inner bounds for IDLA.
In Section
4
we prove the absence of a spherical shape theorem in dimension
d=2
. The case
d=1
follows along the same lines, and we leave it to the reader since easier.
In Section
5
we establish an inner bound on the growth of the aggregate for
d>2
,
and derive an upper bound on the number of particles escaping such a bound.
In Section
6
we introduce the new auxiliary process,
the
Random Barrier Growth
, and
state our main result providing an outer bound on the growth of this process. We then conclude the proof of the shape theorem.

2
Definition of BIDLA

We start by recalling the definition of Branching Random Walks, which will later describe the dynamics of the particles, or
explorers
, in BIDLA.

2.1
Branching Random Walks

We let
\mathbf{\mathcal{T}}
be a critical Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree, whose offspring distribution
\nu
satisfies Assumption (
H
).
We think of
\mathbf{\mathcal{T}}
as a set of vertices, and call
E(\mathbf{\mathcal{T}})
the
edges of
\mathbf{\mathcal{T}}
, where there is one edge between an individual and each child.
A Branching Random Walk (BRW) is the associated tree-indexed random walk

(S_{u})_{u\in\mathbf{\mathcal{T}}}
, where
time is encoded by the tree
\mathbf{\mathcal{T}}
and
whose jump distribution is the uniform measure on the neighbours of the origin.
In other words, independent increments
(\xi(e))_{e\in E(\mathbf{\mathcal{T}})}

are associated to the edges of the tree, and if

[\emptyset,u]
is the sequence of edges between the root
\emptyset
and vertex
u
, then
we choose an initial position
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and set
S_{\emptyset}=z
and

S_{u}^{z}=z+\sum_{e\in[\emptyset,u]}\xi(e)\qquad\forall u\in\mathbf{\mathcal{T}}.

Given
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we define the time spent at
x
by the BRW as
the number of individuals
u
in the tree such that
S^{z}_{u}=x
, that is

\ell^{z}(x):=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{T}}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}(S^{z}_{u}=x),

(2.1)

and we refer to
\ell^{z}(x)
as the
local time
at
x
of a BRW started at
z
.
For a finite subset
K\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we denote the local time at
K
by

\ell^{z}(K):=\sum_{x\in K}\ell^{z}(x).

For
u,v\in\mathbf{\mathcal{T}}
, we say that
v
is an ancestor of
u
, denoted
v\prec u
,
if
v
belong to the geodesic between the root and
u
, excluding
u
. If we want to
include
u
, we say
v\preceq u
.

2.1.1
BRW restricted to a domain

It is convenient to consider the branching random walk
restricted to a domain
K\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Let

\mathcal{T}^{z}(K):=\{u\in\mathbf{\mathcal{T}}:\ \forall v\preceq u,\ S^{z}_{v}\in K\},

(2.2)

and observe that
\mathcal{T}^{z}(K)\subseteq K
. Denote the set of individuals on
\partial K
whose ancestors are all in
K
by

\partial_{K}\mathcal{T}^{z}:=\{u\in\mathcal{T}^{z}:\ S^{z}_{u}\in\partial K\,\mbox{ and }S^{z}_{v}\in K\;\;\forall v\prec u\}.

In words,
\mathcal{T}^{z}(K)
is the collection of individuals whose ancestral lines belong to
K
, whereas

\partial_{K}\mathcal{T}^{z}
is the set of so-called
pioneers
on
\partial K
, namely those individuals whose ancestors are all in
K
, but who have escaped
K
, and because of the nearest neighbour nature of
the increments of the BRW, they are on
\partial K
. We can define local times for the restricted
BRW
\mathcal{T}^{z}(K)
, as a set function on the whole of
K\cup\partial K
as follows:

\begin{split}\ell^{z}_{K}(x)&:=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{T}^{z}(K)}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}(S^{z}_{u}=x),\quad\mbox{for }x\in K,\\
\ell^{z}_{K}(x)&:=\sum_{u\in\partial_{K}\mathcal{T}^{z}}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}(S^{z}_{u}=x),\quad\mbox{for }x\in\partial K.\end{split}

(2.3)

The local time of a collection of independent Branching Random Walks restricted to
K
starting at locations

\eta=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is denoted by
\ell^{\eta}_{K}
.
When
K=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we simply use
\ell^{\eta}
.

2.2
Branching IDLA

We give here a formal definition of the BIDLA process using the formalism of particle systems. This definition may, at first sight, look different from that presented in the introduction. The equivalence between the two definitions follows from the Abelian property, which, roughly speaking, gives some freedom on how to stabilise particle configurations without altering their statistics. The fact that classical IDLA enjoys the Abelian property is well known
[
10
,
21
]
. We give a detailed proof of the Abelian property for BIDLA in Appendix
A
, which may be skipped upon first reading.

A BIDLA process on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is defined as follows.
A particle configuration on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a map
\eta:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{N}
, where
\eta(z)
denotes the number of particles at
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Particles are indistinguishable.
We build the aggregate through sequential
toppling
of
unstable
sites:
we say that the site
z
is
unstable
for the configuration
\eta
if
\eta(z)>1
, and
that
\eta
is
stable
if for any
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
\eta(z)\leq 1
(at most one particle per site). Choose an arbitrary ordering of the sites of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, for instance lexicographical ordering.
If a particle configuration
\eta
is unstable, we define its
BIDLA stabilization
as follows.
In
\eta
, take the first unstable site, and perform a
toppling
:
one particle at that site dies after producing a random number of children with law
\nu
at the same site and, instantaneously, each child takes an independent simple random walk step, thus moving to a random neighbouring site.
Since the offspring law
\nu
is critical, iterating this procedure will make the particle configuration stable after finitely many topplings, almost surely. We denote this stable configuration by
\mathscr{S}(\eta)
, and we call it the BIDLA stabilization of the configuration
\eta
.

We consider the evolution of an interacting particle system made of critical Branching Random Walks.
Each particle has two states:
settled
or
active
(i.e.
unsettled
). A settled particle stays in place forever, and there is at most one settled particle per site of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
The positions of the settled particles define a finite subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
called the
explored region
, and this region evolves until all particles are eventually settled, which happens almost surely.

Each unsettled particle has a
critical
branching mechanism: it produces a random number of children (or
offsprings
), and each child independently moves away from its parent’s position according to a simple random walk step.
We call this action a
toppling
. More formally, a toppling
consists of replacing a particle at an unstable site
x
(and unstable means
\eta(x)>1
) by
k
children with probability
\nu(k)
, where we recall that

\sum_{k\geq 0}k\cdot\nu(k)=1

(2.4)

by Assumption (
H
).
These
k
children are given independent simple random walk increments
(\xi_{j},\ j\leq k)
,
and the particle configuration
\eta
is replaced by

\eta-{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{x}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{x+\xi_{j}}.

The dynamics stops when no toppling is possible, that is when
\eta(x)\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and the resulting stable configuration is denoted by
\mathscr{S}(\eta)
.

With this notation in place, a BIDLA process
(A(t))_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is defined inductively by setting
A(0)=\emptyset
and, for each
t\geq 0
,

A(t+1):=\mathscr{S}(A(t)+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{0}).

In words, at each discrete time step
t\geq 1
add a particle at the origin and perform BIDLA stabilization.

Remark 2.1
.

We remark that the definition of BIDLA presented in the introduction simply corresponds to choosing a particular order of the topplings, the one dictated from the exponential clocks associated to the BRW particles. By the Abelian property, which is spelled out in Appendix
A
, the law of the stable configuration does not depend on the order of the topplings, thus the two definitions of BIDLA are equivalent.

Note that, since
\nu
is assumed to be critical, the total number of particles is a martingale. Moreover, the stabilization time is a stopping time in the natural filtration associated to each driving Branching Random Walk (or, alternatively, to each toppling). Thus, recalling that
|\eta|
denotes the number of particles in
\eta
, we have the following.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any dimension
d\geq 1
and any finite particle configuration
\eta
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}[|\mathscr{S}(\eta)|]=|\eta|.

(2.5)

2.3
Stabilization in a finite domain

As for classical IDLA, when working with BIDLA it is often convenient to
partially stabilize
a particle configuration
\eta
inside a given domain
K\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. This means that we only topple sites in
K
, and when a particles steps outside of
K
we declare it
frozen
, thus pausing its BRW evolution. Almost surely, after finitely many topplings this procedure will result in a stable configuration inside
K
, together with a collection of frozen particles on the exterior boundary
\partial K
. We denote this configuration by
\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{K}
. Hence by definition
\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{K}(z)\leq 1
for all
z\in K
, while if
z\in\partial K
then
\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{K}(z)\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the number of frozen particles at
z
. These particles are on standby, and their evolution may be continued at a later time. Importantly, by the Abelian property

\mathscr{S}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}^{\eta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathscr{S}(\eta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{law}}}{{=}}\mathscr{S}(\mathscr{S}_{K}^{\eta}).

In words, freezing particles and releasing them at a later time will result in a stable configuration which has the same statistics as the one obtained by stabilising without freezing. See Appendix
A
for a detailed discussion on the Abelian property.

3
Preliminaries

We collect in this section some preliminary results on BRWs and Green’s function estimates, which will be used throughout the paper. From here onwards, we denote by
c,C
constants which may depend only on the dimension
d
, and which are allowed to change from line to line.

3.1
On Branching Random Walks

Let
K\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a finite subset.
Recall from Section
2.1.1
that if
y\in K
then
\ell^{y}_{K}(z)
denotes the local time at
z
of a BRW starting at
y
with freezing upon exiting
K
. Then

\ell_{K}^{y}(\partial K):=\sum\limits_{z\in\partial K}\ell_{K}^{y}(z)

(3.1)

counts the number of frozen particles on
\partial K
. We call these particles
pioneers
, as they are the first individuals in the BRW process to explore the space outside
K
.

The first result of this section tells us that the probability that a BRW survives to distance
R
from its starting location is of order
\Theta(1/R^{2})
, independently of the dimension.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
3
]
, Lemma 3.8)
.

For any
d\geq 1
there exist constants
c,C>0
, only depending on the dimension
d
, such that for any
R\geq 1
,

\begin{split}&\inf_{y\in B_{R}}\mathbb{P}\big(\ell^{y}_{B_{R}}(\partial B_{R})>0\big)\geq\frac{c}{R^{2}},\\
&\sup_{y\in B_{R/2}}\mathbb{P}(\ell^{y}_{B_{R}}(\partial B_{R})>0)\leq\frac{C}{R^{2}}.\end{split}

Conditional on survival up to
\partial B_{R}
, one may ask how many pioneers are frozen on
\partial B_{R}
.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
3
]
, Theorem 1.3)
.

For any
d\geq 1
there exist positive constants
c
and
\lambda_{0}
, depending only on the dimension
d
, such that for each
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0}]

and for each
y\in B_{R}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\lambda\ell_{B_{R}}^{y}(\partial B_{R})/R^{2}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\leq e^{c\lambda/R^{2}}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\lambda\ell_{B_{R}}^{y}(\partial B_{R})/R^{2}\Big)\Big|\ell_{B_{R}}^{y}(\partial B_{R})>0\Big]

\displaystyle\leq e^{c\lambda}.

(3.3)

Moreover, writing
\bar{\ell}_{B_{R}}^{y}:=\ell_{B_{R}}^{y}(\partial B_{R})-\mathbb{E}[\ell_{B_{R}}^{y}(\partial B_{R})]
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\lambda\bar{\ell}_{B_{R}}^{y}/R^{2}\Big)\Big|\ell_{B_{R}}^{y}(\partial B_{R})>0\Big]\leq e^{c\lambda^{2}/R^{2}}.

(3.4)

Thus, on the event that the BRW survives to distance
R
, the number of particles
reaching the boundary of
B_{R}
is of order
\Theta(R^{2})
.
As a direct corollary of this result we get the following.

Corollary 3.3
.

For any
d\geq 1
there exists
\bar{\beta}(d)\in(0,\infty)
such that the following holds. For any
\alpha>0
and any
\beta\geq\bar{\beta}(d)
there exists a positive constant
c=c(\alpha,\beta,d)
such that for all
R>0

\mathbb{P}\big(\ell^{0}_{B_{R}}(\partial B_{R})\in[\alpha R^{2},\beta R^{2}]\big|\ell^{0}_{B_{R}}(\partial B_{R})>0\big)\geq c(\alpha,\beta,d)>0.

Proof.

Write
\ell_{R}
in place of
\ell^{0}_{B_{R}}(\partial B_{R})
for brevity. We use the conditional version of the Paley-Zygmund inequality: for any non-negative square-integrable random variable
X
, any event
E
with
\mathbb{P}(E)>0
and any
\vartheta\in[0,1]
, it holds

\mathbb{P}\big(X\geq\vartheta\mathbb{E}[X|E]|E\big)\geq(1-\vartheta)^{2}\frac{(\mathbb{E}[X|E])^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[X^{2}|E]}.

Taking
X=\ell_{R}
and
E=\{\ell_{R}>0\}
the above inequality rewrites as

\mathbb{P}\big(\ell_{R}\geq\vartheta\cdot\mathbb{E}[\ell_{R}|\ell_{R}>0]\,\big|\ell_{R}>0\big)\geq(1-\vartheta)^{2}\frac{(\mathbb{E}[\ell_{R}|\ell_{R}>0])^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[\ell_{R}^{2}|\ell_{R}>0]}.

Now by Lemma 3.8 of
[
3
]
we know that there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}\in(0,\infty)
, depending only on the dimension
d
, such that

\mathbb{E}[\ell_{R}|\ell_{R}>0]\geq C_{1}R^{2},\qquad\mathbb{E}[\ell_{R}^{2}|\ell_{R}>0]\leq C_{2}R^{4},

which gives

\mathbb{P}\big(\ell_{R}\geq\vartheta\mathbb{E}[\ell_{R}|\ell_{R}>0]\,\big|\ell_{R}>0\big)\geq(1-\vartheta)^{2}\frac{C_{1}^{2}}{C_{2}}.

Since, moreover,

\mathbb{E}[\ell_{R}|\ell_{R}>0]\leq C_{3}R^{2},

we conclude that

\mathbb{P}\big(\ell_{R}\geq C_{3}\vartheta R^{2}\big|\ell_{R}>0\big)\geq(1-\vartheta)^{2}\frac{C_{1}^{2}}{C_{2}}

for any
\vartheta\in[0,1]
. Take
\vartheta=\bar{\vartheta}(\alpha):=\alpha/(2\max\{C_{3},\alpha\})\in(0,1/2]
to get that

\mathbb{P}\big(\ell_{R}\geq\alpha R^{2}\big|\ell_{R}>0\big)\geq(1-\bar{\vartheta}(\alpha))^{2}\frac{C_{1}^{2}}{C_{2}}>0.

It remains to show that
\mathbb{P}(\ell_{R}\geq\beta R^{2}|\ell_{R}>0)
can be made arbitrarily small by taking
\beta
large enough. Indeed, recall that by Proposition
3.2
we have that for any
d\geq 1
there exist constants
c=c(d),\bar{\lambda}=\bar{\lambda}(d)>0
such that for any
\lambda\in(0,\bar{\lambda})
it holds that

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda\ell_{R}/R^{2}}|\ell_{R}>0]\leq e^{c\lambda}.

Taking
\lambda>0
as above we thus find

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\ell_{R}\geq\beta R^{2}|\ell_{R}>0)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda\ell_{R}/R^{2}}|\ell_{R}>0]\cdot e^{-\lambda\beta}\leq e^{-\lambda(\beta-c)},\end{split}

which can be made arbitrarily small by taking
\beta
large enough.
∎

3.2
Green’s function estimates

For
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, denote by
(S^{x}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
starting from
S^{x}_{0}=x
.
Given a finite set
K\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, let

H_{K}(S^{x}):=\inf\{t\geq 0:\,S^{x}_{t}\in K\}

denote the hitting time of
K
, and write simply
H_{K}
when no confusion occurs on the random walk.
For any
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, define the Green’s function by

G_{K}(x,y):=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{n=0}^{H_{K^{c}}}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}(S^{x}_{n}=y)\right],

where
K^{c}:=\mathbb{Z}^{d}\backslash K
.
Recall that
B_{R}
denotes the ball of radius
R
centered at the origin. We use the notation
G_{R}(x,y)
in place of
G_{B_{R}}(x,y)
.
Note that, with our definition, when
y\in\partial B_{R}

we have that
G_{R}(x,y)=\mathbb{P}\big(S^{x}(H_{\partial B_{R}})=y\big)
.

We start by recalling a classical bound of Asselah and Gaudillière.

Lemma 3.4

(
[
1
]
, Lemma 5.1)
.

Assume
d\geq 2
. Then there is an absolute constant
\kappa
such that for any
x\in B_{R}
and
y\in\partial B_{R}
it holds that

\mathbb{P}\big(S^{x}(H_{\partial B_{R}})=y\big)\leq\kappa\cdot\frac{1}{\|x-y\|^{d-1}}.

(3.5)

In dimension
d\geq 3
we need the following natural and crucial generalisation, which allows us to take
y
anywhere inside
B_{R}
. Write
a\wedge b
for the minimum between
a
and
b
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume that
d\geq 3
.
For arbitrary
R>0
let
x\in B_{R}
and
y\in B_{R}\cup\partial B_{R}
. Then there is an absolute constant
\kappa
such that

\mathbb{P}\big(H_{y}(S^{x})\leq H_{\partial B_{R}}(S^{x})\big)\leq\kappa\cdot\frac{1}{\|x-y\|^{d-2}}\cdot\Big(1\wedge\frac{(R-\|y\|+1)}{\|x-y\|}\Big)\cdot\Big(1\wedge\frac{(R-\|x\|+1)}{\|x-y\|}\Big).

(3.6)

Since we have not found a reference for this result in the extensive literature on random walks, we choose to include the proof.

Proof.

Define
R_{x}:=R-\|x\|
and
R_{y}:=R-\|y\|
.
Assume first that
\max(R_{x},R_{y})\leq r
where we set
r:=\|x-y\|/16
.
In other words, the points
x,y
are both much closer
to the boundary than to each other. In this case, we show that two simple random walks starting from
x
and
y
respectively must first get away from the
boundary by a distance of order
\|x-y\|
before they can meet.
To see this, define two points
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
outside
B_{R}
as follows.
Take
x^{\prime}
at a distance
1
from the line
\mathbb{R}\cdot x
and such that
x^{\prime}\in\partial B_{R+2r}
and

B_{R}\cap(x^{\prime}+B_{2r})=\emptyset
. The same construction holds for
y^{\prime}
when
the role of
x
and
y
are exchanged.
Then
x
is a distance less than
R_{x}+1
from
x^{\prime}+B_{2r}
,

y
is a distance less than
R_{y}+1
from
y^{\prime}+B_{2r}
, and

\|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\|\geq\|x-y\|-r=15r.

(3.7)

The point of this construction is that a path from
x
to
y
avoiding the complement of
B_{R}
must first reach
B_{1}:=x^{\prime}+B_{4r}
before
x^{\prime}+B_{2r}
, then reach
B_{2}:=y^{\prime}+B_{4r}
,
then go from
\partial B_{2}
to
y
without hitting
y^{\prime}+B_{2r}
.

Figure 2:
A depiction of the construction used in the proof.

Thus,

\{H_{y}(S^{x})<H_{\partial B_{R}}(S^{x})\big\}\subseteq\{H_{y}(S^{x})<\min(H_{x^{\prime}+B_{2r}},H_{y^{\prime}+B_{2r}})\},

where we have suppressed the random walk from the notation in the right hand side for brevity.
Now if we call
\tilde{x}
the
first site the walk
S^{x}
hits on
\partial B_{1}
, and
\tilde{y}
the last site it hits on

\partial B_{2}
before
y
, we can partition the event

\{H_{y}(S^{x})<\min(H_{x^{\prime}+B_{2r}},H_{y^{\prime}+B_{2r}})\big\}
according to the values of
\tilde{x}
and
\tilde{y}
, to get that its probability is bounded by

\begin{split}\sum_{\tilde{x},\tilde{y}}&\mathbb{P}\big(S^{x}(H_{\partial B_{1}})=\tilde{x},H_{\partial B_{1}}<H_{x^{\prime}+B_{2r}}\big)\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(H_{\tilde{y}}(S^{\tilde{x}})<\infty\big)\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(S^{y}(H_{\partial B_{2}})=\tilde{y},H_{\partial B_{2}}<H_{y^{\prime}+B_{2r}}\big)\\
&\leq\,\mathbb{P}\big(H_{\partial B_{1}}(S^{x})<H_{x^{\prime}+B_{2r}}\big)\cdot\sup_{\tilde{x}\in\partial B_{1},\,\tilde{y}\in\partial B_{2}}\mathbb{P}\big(H_{\tilde{y}}(S^{\tilde{x}})<\infty\big)\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(H_{\partial B_{2}}(S^{y})<H_{y^{\prime}+B_{2r}}\big).\end{split}

(3.8)

Here we used the strong Markov property and the reversibility of the random walk to obtain the second
line in (
3.8
).
Now, by a Gambler’s ruin estimate, there exists
\kappa>1
such that

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\big(H_{\partial B_{1}}(S^{x})<H_{x^{\prime}+B_{2r}}\big)\leq\kappa\cdot\frac{R_{x}+1}{\|x-y\|},\quad\text{and}\\
&\mathbb{P}\big(H_{\partial B_{2}}(S^{y})<H_{y^{\prime}+B_{2r}}\big)\leq\kappa\cdot\frac{R_{y}+1}{\|x-y\|}.\end{split}

(3.9)

Combining this with the classical estimate
\mathbb{P}\big(H_{\tilde{y}}(S^{\tilde{x}})<\infty\big)\leq\kappa\cdot\|\tilde{x}-\tilde{y}\|^{2-d}
and the fact that
\|\tilde{x}-\tilde{y}\|>7r
, we gather that for some constant
C>0

\mathbb{P}\big(H_{y}(S^{x})<H_{\partial B_{R}}(S^{x})\big)\leq C\frac{R_{x}+1}{\|x-y\|}\cdot\frac{R_{y}+1}{\|x-y\|}\cdot\frac{1}{\|x-y\|^{d-2}}.

(3.10)

This proves (
3.6
) in the case
\max(R_{x},R_{y})\leq r
.
It remains to treat the other cases.
If
R_{x}\leq\|x-y\|/16<R_{y}
, the only difference with the previous construction
is that once we reach
\partial B_{1}
, at
\tilde{x}
, we only require the walk to hit
y
, and this yields
the result. The case
R_{y}\leq\|x-y\|/16<R_{x}
is handled analogously, exchanging the role of
x
and
y
.
Finally, if
\min(R_{x},R_{y})>\|x-y\|/16
, then this means
that
x
and
y
are in the bulk of
B_{R}
, and
the unrestricted bound holds.
∎

In the remainder of this section we focus on obtaining upper bounds on the second moment of the local time of branching random walks restricted to a Euclidean ball.
Recall from Section
2.1
that if
\mathcal{T}
is a critical Galton-Watson tree indexing a BRW, for
R>0
and
x\in B_{R}
we denote by
\mathcal{T}^{x}(B_{R})
the set of vertices of
\mathcal{T}
whose ancestors are all mapped to
B_{R}
, when the root is mapped to
x
.
The vertices of
\mathcal{T}^{x}(B_{R})
mapped to
\partial B_{R}
are the pioneers.
When there is no confusion, we denote by
\mathcal{T}^{x}(B_{R})
both the set of vertices and their positions in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, with a slight abuse of notation. Recall that the local time at site
z\in B_{R}
of a BRW
(S^{x}_{u})_{u\in\mathcal{T}^{x}(B_{R})}
starting at
x
is defined by

\ell^{x}_{B_{R}}(z)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{T}^{x}(B_{R})}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}(S^{x}_{u}=z),

and

G_{R}(x,z)=\mathbb{E}[\ell^{x}_{B_{R}}(z)].

When
z\in\partial B_{R}
, the local time
\ell^{x}_{B_{R}}(z)
counts the number of pioneers which exit
B_{R}
at
z
, and

G_{R}(x,z)=\mathbb{P}\big(S^{x}(H_{\partial B_{R}})=z\big)
, so that
\mathbb{E}[\ell^{x}_{B_{R}}(z)]=G_{R}(x,z).

The following lemma bounds the second moment of the local time of a BRW restricted to
B_{R}
in all dimensions. Recall that
\sigma^{2}
denotes the variance of the offspring distribution, which is positive by Assumption (
H
).

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
d\geq 1
, any
R>0
,
x\in B_{R}
and
z\in\partial B_{R}
it holds

\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\ell^{x}_{B_{R}}(z)\big)^{2}\big]\leq G_{R}(x,z)+\sigma^{2}\sum_{y\in B_{R}}G_{R}(x,y)\cdot G_{R}^{2}(y,z).

(3.11)

Proof.

As we expand the square, the second moment corresponds to summing over
u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{x}
, and it is convenient to consider the youngest common ancestor, say
v
of
u
and
u^{\prime}
when they are different. Thus,
if
\mathcal{T}^{v}
is the subtree of
\mathcal{T}^{x}(B_{R})
having
v
as its root and including only the descendants of
v

\begin{split}\big(\ell^{x}_{B_{R}}(z)\big)^{2}=&\ell^{x}_{B_{R}}(z)+\sum_{u\not=u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{x}(B_{R})}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}(S^{u}=z=S^{u^{\prime}})\\
=&\ell^{x}_{B_{R}}(z)+\sum_{v\in\mathcal{T}^{x}(B_{R})}\sum_{u\not=u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{v}}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}(S^{u}=z=S^{u^{\prime}})\\
=&\ell^{x}_{B_{R}}(z)+\sum_{y\in B_{R}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{T}^{x}(B_{R})}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}(S^{v}=y)\sum_{u\not=u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{v}}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}(S^{u}=z=S^{u^{\prime}}).\end{split}

(3.12)

Now, the tree
\mathcal{T}^{v}
when
S^{v}=y
has the same law as
\mathcal{T}^{y}(B_{R})
.
Moreover, if
X_{v}
denotes the number of children of
v
, then
\mathbb{E}[{X\choose 2}]=\sigma^{2}/2<\infty
. Taking expectations throughout (
3.12
) we obtain (
3.11
).
∎

Remark 3.7
.

The same computation shows that if
z\in B_{R}
then

\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\ell^{x}_{B_{R}}(z)\big)^{2}\big]\leq G_{R}(x,z)\cdot(1+2G_{R}^{+}(z,z))+\sigma^{2}\sum_{y\in B_{R}}G_{R}(x,y)\cdot G_{R}^{2}(y,z),

where
G_{R}^{+}(z,z)
counts the expected number of returns to
z
before exiting
B_{R}
.

We now obtain upper bounds for the right hand side of (
3.11
).
Recall that

\mathbb{P}(H_{y}(S^{x})<H_{\partial B_{R}}\big)=\frac{G_{R}(x,y)}{G_{R}(y,y)}.

Moreover,

G_{R}(y,y)
is uniformly bounded in the transient case, whereas in the recurrent case
G_{R}(y,y)
can reach a value of order
\log R
, so extra care is needed.

We start by providing an estimate in the transient case.

Lemma 3.8
.

Assume
d\geq 3
.
There are finite constants
\{C_{d},d\geq 3\}
such that for any
R>0
,
x\in B_{R}
, and
z\in\partial B_{R}
it holds

\sum_{y\in B_{R}}G_{R}(x,y)G_{R}^{2}(y,z)\leq\frac{R-\|x\|+1}{\|x-z\|^{d}}\times\begin{cases}C_{3}\log\|x-z\|&\mbox{ if $d=3$,}\\
C_{d}&\mbox{ if $d\geq 4$.}\end{cases}

(3.13)

Proof.

Since

G_{R}(x,y)\leq G_{R}(0,0)\cdot\mathbb{P}(H_{y}(S^{x})<H_{\partial B_{R}})
,
and
G_{R}(0,0)
is uniformly bounded in the transient case, we have that
G_{R}(x,y)
is uniformly bounded for all
x,y\in B_{R}
. It thus suffices to bound

G_{R}(y,z)
for arbitrary
y\in B_{R}
and
z\in\partial B_{R}
. This can be achieved using Lemma

We split the sum over

into three regions such that

•

•

•

, i.e.
\mathcal{R}_{3}=B_{R}\setminus(\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2})
.

Recall the notation
R_{x}=R-\|x\|\leq\|x-z\|
.

In Region 1, we bound

by
\kappa\cdot R_{x}\cdot\|x-y\|
,
and

by

, for some finite constants

. This gives the bound

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}\frac{R_{x}}{\|x-y\|^{d-1}}\cdot\frac{1}{\|x-y\|^{2d-2}}\leq c\cdot\frac{R_{x}}{\|x-z\|^{2d-3}}\leq\frac{c}{\|x-z\|^{d-3}}\cdot\frac{R_{x}}{\|x-z\|^{d}}.

In Region 2,
G_{R}(y,z)=\mathcal{O}(\|x-z\|^{1-d})
, and the sum over
G_{R}(x,y)
in this region
is of order
R_{x}\cdot\|x-z\|
.
This gives the bound

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}\frac{R_{x}}{\|x-y\|^{d-1}}\cdot\frac{1}{\|x-z\|^{2d-2}}\leq c\cdot\frac{R_{x}}{\|x-z\|^{d-1}}\cdot\frac{1}{\|x-z\|^{d-2}}.

We are left with Region 3. For
h\leq 2\|x-z\|
and
k\leq 2\|x-z\|/h
, define the shells

\Sigma^{z}_{h,k}:=(z+B_{(k+1)h}\backslash B_{kh})\cap\{y:\ h\geq R_{y}>h-1\}
.
Note that

.
Thus, since in this region

, we end up with

\begin{split}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{R}_{3}}G_{R}(x,y)G_{R}^{2}(y,z)\leq&\,c\sum_{h=1}^{2\|x-z\|}\sum_{k=1}^{2\|x-z\|/h}\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{z}_{h,k}}\frac{h\cdot R_{x}}{\|x-y\|^{d}}\cdot\frac{1}{\|y-z\|^{2(d-1)}}\\
\leq&\,c\cdot\frac{R_{x}}{\|x-z\|^{d}}\sum_{h=1}^{2\|x-z\|}\frac{1}{h^{d-2}}\sum_{k=1}^{2\|x-z\|/h}\frac{1}{k^{d}}\\
&\leq c\cdot\frac{R_{x}}{\|x-z\|^{d}}\cdot\big(\log\|x-z\|{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d=3}+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d\geq 4}\big).\end{split}

(3.14)

Gathering the above estimates concludes the proof.
∎

1&\mbox{ if $d\geq 4$.}\end{cases}

(3.15)

We now move to the recurrent case, where the following holds.

Lemma 3.10
.

Assume

.
Then there exists a finite absolute constant

such that for any

Proof.

To start with, note that if

This implies that for any integer

for some

It follows that

By a Riemann integral approximation,

3.3
A classical approach to the inner bound

Here we recall a classical approach to proving the inner bound of a shape theorem, introduced by Lawler, Bramson and Griffeath in the seminal paper
[
20
]
, together with a key independence feature observed by Asselah and Gaudillière in
[
1
]
.
This approach will be used in Section
4
to prove a weak inner bound for BIDLA in dimension
d=2
, and in Section
5
to prove a strong inner bound for
d\geq 3
.

Let
\eta
be an arbitrary initial configuration, and recall that
|\eta|=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\eta(z)
denotes the number of particles in
\eta
. With a slight abuse of notation, we think of
\eta
also as a set of positions, and write

when summing over the positions of the
|\eta|
sites in
\eta
.

Assume that
\eta
is supported in a Euclidean ball
B_{R}
for some
R>0
.
We want to show that stabilising
\eta
with freezing on
\partial B_{R}
results in filling the ball
B_{R}
with high probability. To this end we need upper bounds for

\mathbb{P}(B_{R}\nsubseteq\mathscr{S}_{B_{R}}^{\eta})\leq\sum_{z\in B_{R}}\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{S}_{B_{R}}^{\eta}(z)=0).

The idea proposed by Lawler, Bramson and Griffeath is the following: let first each particle in
\eta
evolve as a BRW with settling upon reaching an empty site and freezing upon reaching
\partial B_{R}
.
The settling locations inside the ball are clearly correlated, since whether a site is empty or not at a given time depends on the trajectories of previous particles. However, if we
continue
the evolution of the BRWs
after settling
, which is sometimes referred to as introducing
ghost particles
, we can complete the particles’ trajectories to full
independent
BRWs.
Now, while the starting locations of the ghosts are correlated, there is at most one ghost per site inside the ball
B_{R}
. Thus we can only increase the chance of reaching a boundary site
z
if we release
exactly
one ghost per site. This allows one to recover independence.

To formalise this strategy, fix an arbitrary site
z\in B_{R}\cup\partial B_{R}
, and note that
\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)
is the local time at
z
in the BIDLA phase, i.e. the number of visits at
z
when releasing one particle from each site of
\eta
and letting them evolve as in BIDLA dynamics with freezing upon reaching
\partial B_{R}
.
If
z\in\partial B_{R}
then
\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)
denotes the number of particles frozen at
z
.

Write
\eta_{G}:=\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}\cap B_{R}
for the particle configuration obtained by BIDLA-stabilising
\eta
inside
B_{R}
. Then

\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)+\tilde{\ell}^{\eta_{G}}_{B_{R}}(z)=\ell^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z),

(3.19)

where
\tilde{\ell}^{\eta_{G}}_{B_{R}}(z)
denotes the local time of the
ghosts
, that is the number of visits to
z
when releasing exactly one BRW from each site of
\eta_{G}
with freezing on
\partial B_{R}
. The tilde notation is there to emphasize that, given their starting locations, the evolution of the ghosts is
independent
from that of the BIDLA particles. Now, since there is at most one ghost per site,

\tilde{\ell}^{\eta_{G}}_{B_{R}}(z)\leq\tilde{\ell}^{B_{R}}_{B_{R}}(z),

where
B_{R}
denotes the particle configuration made of exactly one particle at each site of
B_{R}
. This has the advantage that the dependence on the configuration
\eta_{G}
, and hence on the BIDLA particles, is lost. Therefore we get
the crucial inequality
2
2

2

Here (
3.19
) and (

and the inequality

in

]
, Section 3.

\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)+\tilde{\ell}^{B_{R}}_{B_{R}}(z)\geq\ell^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z),

It follows that, for any

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)=0)&\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\lambda\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)}]\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\lambda\tilde{\ell}^{B_{R}}_{B_{R}}(z)}\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\lambda\tilde{\ell}^{B_{R}}_{B_{R}}(z)}\big]}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\lambda\ell^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)}\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\lambda\tilde{\ell}^{B_{R}}_{B_{R}}(z)}\big]}\\

Here we have set

This is a new feature introduced by the branching.

c_{3}>0
. To this end, we prove that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{5\varepsilon n}}(z)=0\Big)+\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{5\varepsilon n}}\big(\partial B_{5\varepsilon n}\big)>\gamma n^{2}\Big)<1

(4.4)

by showing that the first probability in the right hand side is
<1
, while the second one can be made arbitrarily small by choosing
\gamma
large enough, depending on
\alpha
.

Recall the definition of
\sigma^{2}
from (
H
). As a first step, we claim that

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{5\varepsilon n}}(z)=0\big)\leq e^{-C^{\prime}\alpha/\varepsilon^{2}}

(4.5)

for some explicit constant
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(\sigma^{2})
depending only on
\sigma^{2}
.
Indeed, reasoning as in Section
3.3
, by (
3.22
) with
R=5\varepsilon n
and any
\lambda>0
we have that

\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)=0)\leq\exp\bigg(-\lambda\mathbb{E}\big[\ell^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)-\ell^{B_{R}}_{B_{R}}(z)\big]+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\sum_{y\in\eta}\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\bar{\ell}^{y}_{B_{R}}(z)\big)^{2}\big]\cdot e^{\lambda\mathbb{E}[\ell^{y}_{B_{R}}(z)]}\bigg).

Now, for any
y\in\eta\subseteq B_{R/2}
we can bound
\mathbb{E}\big[\ell^{y}_{B_{R}}(z)\big]=G_{R}(y,z)\leq C/R

for some absolute constant
C<\infty
(cf.
[
1
]
, Lemma 3.4). Moreover,
by Lemma
3.10
,

\sup_{y\in\eta}\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\bar{\ell}^{y}_{B_{R}}(z)\big)^{2}\big]\leq\sigma^{2}\sup_{y\in\eta}\sum_{w\in B_{R}}G_{R}(y,w)(G_{R}(w,z))^{2}\leq C^{\prime}

with
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(\sigma)=5C_{2}\sigma^{2}
with
C_{2}
as in (
3.16
).
We thus end up with

\prod_{y\in\eta}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\lambda\bar{\ell}^{y}_{B_{R}}(z)}\big]\leq\exp\Big(\alpha n^{2}\frac{C^{\prime}}{2}\lambda^{2}e^{\lambda C/R}\Big).

Plugging this into (
3.21
), together with

\mathbb{E}\big[\ell^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)\big]\geq\alpha n^{2}\inf_{y\in\eta}G_{R}(y,z)\geq\alpha n^{2}\frac{c}{R}=\frac{\alpha cn}{5\varepsilon}

with
c
absolute constant, and

\mathbb{E}\big[\ell^{B_{R}}_{B_{R}}(z)\big]=\sum_{y\in B_{R}}G_{R}(y,z)\leq c^{\prime}R\,\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\ell^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)\big]

for
\varepsilon
small enough,
we conclude that

\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)=0)\leq\exp\Big(\alpha n^{2}\frac{C^{\prime}}{2}\lambda^{2}e^{\lambda C/R}-\lambda\frac{\alpha cn}{5\varepsilon}\Big)=\exp\Big\{\lambda\alpha n\Big(\frac{C^{\prime}}{2}n\lambda e^{\lambda C/R}-\frac{c}{5\varepsilon}\Big)\Big\}.

Take
\lambda=\delta/n
with
\delta=c/(10\varepsilon C^{\prime})
to get that

\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)=0)\leq e^{-\alpha\delta c/(10\varepsilon)}\leq e^{-C^{\prime\prime}\alpha/\varepsilon^{2}}

for
C^{\prime\prime}
absolute constant.
This proves (
4.5
).

We now show that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}\big(\partial B_{R}\big)>\gamma n^{2}\Big)\leq\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma}.

(4.6)

To this end, note that

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}\big(\partial B_{R})\big]\leq\alpha n^{2}\sup_{y\in\eta}\mathbb{E}\big[\ell^{y}_{B_{R}}(\partial B_{R})\big]=\alpha n^{2}

since
\mathbb{E}\big[\ell^{y}_{B_{R}}(\partial B_{R})\big]=1
for all
y\in\eta
.
It follows that

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}\big(\partial B_{R}\big)>\gamma n^{2}\big)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}\big(\partial B_{R})\big]}{\gamma n^{2}}\leq\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}.

Plugging (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) into (
4.4
), and recalling that
R=5\varepsilon n
, we end up with

\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{5\varepsilon n}}(z)=0\Big)+\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{5\varepsilon n}}\big(\partial B_{5\varepsilon n}\big)>\gamma n^{2}\Big)\leq e^{-C^{\prime}\alpha/\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\alpha}{\gamma},

which can be made strictly smaller than
1
for any
\alpha>0
by taking
\gamma
large enough.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
\beta,\gamma,\varepsilon>0
there exists a positive constant
c_{4}=c_{4}(\beta,\gamma,\varepsilon)
such that
\mathbb{P}(E_{4}|E_{1}\cap E_{2}\cap E_{3})\geq c_{4}
.

Proof.

Note that, since there are at most
(\beta-\alpha+\gamma)n^{2}
particles that move independently up to distance
\varepsilon n
from their starting locations,

\mathbb{P}(E_{4}|E_{1}\cap E_{2}\cap E_{3})\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(\ell^{0}_{B_{5\varepsilon n}}(\partial B_{5\varepsilon n})=0\Big)^{(\beta+\gamma)n^{2}},

so it suffices to show that the latter probability is larger than
c_{4}
. Indeed, by Proposition
3.1
we have that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\ell^{0}_{B_{5\varepsilon n}}(\partial B_{5\varepsilon n})=0\Big)\geq 1-\frac{C}{(\varepsilon n)^{2}}

for some absolute constant
C>0
. Thus we find that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\ell^{0}_{B_{5\varepsilon n}}(\partial B_{5\varepsilon n})=0\Big)^{(\beta+\gamma)n^{2}}\geq\Big(1-\frac{C}{(\varepsilon n)^{2}}\Big)^{(\beta+\gamma)n^{2}}\geq c_{4}(\beta,\gamma,\varepsilon)>0,

as wanted.
∎

We can now prove that, once in a symmetric state, the (time-changed) BIDLA process becomes asymmetric in one step with uniformly positive probability.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}
be any
\varepsilon
-symmetric cluster, and write
\pi n^{2}:=|A|
for the volume of
A
.
Choose
\alpha>0
arbitrary and
\beta>0
large enough so that

\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\cap E_{2})\geq c_{1}(\alpha,\beta)\cdot c_{2}(\alpha,\beta)>0

by Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
above. Now choose
\gamma=\gamma(\alpha)
as in Lemma
4.4
above so that

\mathbb{P}(E_{3}|E_{1}\cap E_{2})\geq c_{3}(\alpha,\gamma)>0.

Finally, for these choices of
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
by Lemma
4.5

\mathbb{P}(E_{4}|E_{1}\cap E_{2}\cap E_{3})\geq c_{4}(\beta,\gamma,\varepsilon)>0,

which concludes the proof.
∎

5
The inner bound for
d>2

In this section we prove that, when releasing
|B_{n}|
BRWs with settling on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
d>2
, the resulting particle configuration covers the ball
B_{R}
for some
R=n-n^{\alpha}
with high probability. Here the constant
\alpha<1
is to be chosen later as small as possible.

To see this, we follow the classical approach to the inner bound sketched in Section
3.3
, together with necessary second moment bounds on the local time of critical BRW inside Euclidean balls.

5.1
Proof of the inner bound

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that
d>2
, and let
\eta:=|B_{n}|{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{0}
. There exists
c_{d}>0
such that,
for each any
\alpha>1/2
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{P}\big(B_{n-n^{\alpha}}\not\subseteq\mathscr{S}(\eta)\big)\leq n^{d}\cdot\exp\Big(-c_{d}\cdot n^{2\alpha-1}\Big(\frac{{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d=3}}{\log n}+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d>3}\Big)\Big).

Proof.

It suffices to prove the statement for
n
large enough.
Note that

\mathbb{P}\big(B_{n-n^{\alpha}}\not\subseteq\mathscr{S}(\eta)\big)\leq\sum_{z\in B_{n-n^{\alpha}}}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}(z)=0\big)=\sum_{R=1}^{n-n^{\alpha}}\sum_{z\in\partial B_{R}}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}(z)=0\big)

and
\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}(z)=0)\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{S}_{B_{R}}^{\eta}(z)=0)
. We now show that

\sup_{R\leq n-n^{\alpha}}\sup_{z\in\partial B_{R}}\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)=0)\leq\exp\Big(-c_{d}\cdot n^{2\alpha-1}\Big(\frac{{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d=3}}{\log n}+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d>3}\Big)\Big).

(5.1)

To see this, take
R\leq n-n^{\alpha}
arbitrary. Then by (
3.22
)
with
\eta=|B_{n}|{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{0}
, using that
|B_{n}|\leq 2\omega_{d}n^{d}
,
we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)=0)\leq\exp\bigg(-\lambda\mathbb{E}\big[\ell^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)-\ell^{B_{R}}_{B_{R}}(z)\big]+\lambda^{2}\omega_{d}n^{d}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\bar{\ell}^{0}_{B_{R}}(z)\big)^{2}\big]\cdot e^{\lambda\mathbb{E}[\ell^{0}_{B_{R}}(z)]}\bigg)

(5.2)

for any
z\in\partial B_{R}
.

For the first term we find

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\big[\ell^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)-\ell^{B_{R}}_{B_{R}}(z)\big]&=|B_{n}|G_{R}(0,z)-\sum_{y\in B_{R}}G_{R}(y,z)\\
&=(|B_{n}|-|B_{R}|)G_{R}(0,z)+\Big(|B_{R}|\cdot G_{R}(0,z)-\sum_{y\in B_{R}}G_{R}(y,z)\Big)\\
&\geq(n^{d}-(n-n^{\alpha})^{d})\cdot\frac{c}{R^{d-1}}-C\geq\kappa_{d}n^{\alpha},\end{split}

(5.3)

where the first inequality follows from Lemma
3.11
and the fact that
G_{R}(0,z)\geq cR^{1-d}
, while the last one holds for some constant
\kappa_{d}
depending only on the dimension.

For the second term we have that

\mathbb{E}[\ell^{0}_{B_{R}}(z)]=G_{R}(0,z)\leq C/R^{d-1}
while,
by Lemmas
3.6
and
3.8
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\bar{\ell}^{0}_{B_{R}}(z)\big)^{2}\big]&\leq G_{R}(0,z)+\sigma^{2}\sum_{y\in B_{R}}G_{R}(0,y)G_{R}^{2}(y,z)\\
&\leq C_{d,\sigma^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{R^{d-1}}\cdot\Big({\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d=3}\log R+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d>3}\Big)\end{split}

(5.4)

for some constant
C_{d,\sigma^{2}}
depending only on the dimension
d
and the offspring variance
\sigma^{2}
.
Plugging (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) into (
5.2
) we end up with

\sup_{R\leq n-n^{\alpha}}\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)=0)\leq\exp\bigg(-\lambda\kappa_{d}n^{\alpha}+\lambda^{2}C_{d,\sigma^{2}}\cdot n\cdot\Big({\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d=3}\log n+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d>3}\Big)\bigg).

Optimising over
\lambda
yields the desired result.
∎

Along the same lines one can prove the inner bound stated in Lemma
1.3
, dealing with a more general initial configuration. As the lemma will not be used in the remainder of the paper, we sketch the argument and leave the details to the reader.

Proof of Lemma
1.3
.

The lower bound relies on the scenario that no branching random walk starting inside
B_{n/2}
reaches
\partial B_{n}
before extinction, which has probability at least

\mathbb{P}(\text{out of $|\eta|$ branching random walks none reaches }\partial B_{n})\geq\Big(1-\frac{C}{n^{2}}\Big)^{\alpha\cdot 2\omega_{d}n^{d}}

by Proposition
3.1
, since
|\eta|\leq\alpha\cdot|B_{n}|=\alpha\cdot 2\omega_{d}n^{d}
.

For the upper bound take
\eta
supported in
B_{n/2}
with
|\eta|\geq\alpha|B_{n}|
. Reasoning as in the proof of Proposition
5.1
above, one shows that

\sup_{2n/3\leq R\leq n}\sup_{z\in\partial B_{R}}\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{R}}(z)=0)\leq\exp\Big(-C_{d}\frac{\alpha\cdot n}{\log n\cdot{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d=3}+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d>3}}\Big).

(5.5)

This proves that when releasing BIDLA particles from
\eta
with freezing on
\partial B_{n}
, the annulus
B_{n}\setminus B_{2n/3}
is covered with overwhelming probability. It remains to exclude holes inside
B_{2n/3}
.
To this end, we could iterate this procedure
k
times, for some large integer
k\geq\alpha
,
so as to cover each site of
B_{n}\setminus B_{2n/3}
with
k
individuals.
By the same argument, stabilising this configuration will result in covering the ball
B_{2n/3}
, with overwhelming probability.
∎

5.2
Number of particles frozen on
\partial B_{n}

We proved in the previous section that releasing
|B_{n}|
particles from the origin according to BIDLA dynamics one has that the event

\mathcal{E}_{n}(\alpha):=\{B_{n-n^{\alpha}}\subseteq\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}\}

holds with high probability as soon as
\alpha>1/2
. This section focuses on bounding the number of particles, or
pioneers
, frozen on
\partial B_{n}
. More precisely, we
show that on the good event
\mathcal{E}_{n}(\alpha)
there are at most

O(n^{d-1+\alpha})
frozen particles on
\partial B_{n}
. Note that, while this type of bound would be trivial for classical IDLA, the creation of particles in BIDLA means that, in principle, the number of pioneers could be arbitrarily large. We show that this number can be bounded by re-adapting the ghost particle approach in combination with the BIDLA inner bound proved in the previous section.

For an arbitrary particle configuration
\eta
write

\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})=\sum_{z\in\partial B_{n}}\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(z),\qquad\ell^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})=\sum_{z\in\partial B_{n}}\ell^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(z).

Thus
\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})
(respectively
\ell^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})
) counts the number of pioneers frozen on
\partial B_{n}
when releasing BIDLA particles (respectively independent BRWs) from each location of
\eta

with freezing on
\partial B_{n}
.

Now take
\eta=|B_{n}|{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{0}
. Setting
\eta_{G}:=\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}\cap B_{n}
, as in (
3.19
) we have

\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})+\tilde{\ell}_{B_{n}}^{\eta_{G}}(\partial B_{n})=\ell_{B_{n}}^{\eta}(\partial B_{n}).

(5.6)

Moreover, on the good event
\mathcal{E}_{n}(\alpha)
,

\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})+\tilde{\ell}_{B_{n}}^{B_{R}}(\partial B_{n})\leq\ell_{B_{n}}^{\eta}(\partial B_{n}),

(5.7)

where we have set
R=n-n^{\alpha}
for brevity. Notice that compared to (
3.20
) now the inequality is reversed, which is a consequence of the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}(\alpha)
holding true. This provides an upper bound for the number of pioneers
\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})
in terms of independent random variables, which can be used to deduce the following result.

Proposition 5.2
.

Assume
d>2
and take
\eta=|B_{n}|{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{0}
.
For any
\alpha>1/2
, and
\beta
large enough, we have for some
\kappa>0

and any
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}(\ \mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})>\beta\cdot n^{d-1+\alpha})\leq n^{d}\cdot\exp\Big(-c_{d}\cdot n^{2\alpha-1}\Big(\frac{{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d=3}}{\log n}+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{d>3}\Big)\Big).

(5.8)

Proof.

With
\mathcal{E}_{n}(\alpha)
defined as above, clearly

\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})>\beta\cdot n^{d-1+\alpha})\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}(\alpha),\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})>\beta\cdot n^{d-1+\alpha})+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}(\alpha)^{c}).

The term
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}(\alpha)^{c})
is dealt with in Proposition
5.1
, and will be the dominant term. For the other term use that

\mathcal{E}_{n}(\alpha)
and
\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})

are independent from
\tilde{\ell}_{B_{n}}^{B_{R}}(\partial B_{n})
to obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}(\alpha),\ \mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})>\beta n^{d-1+\alpha})&\leq e^{-\lambda\beta\cdot n^{d-1+\alpha}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}(\alpha)}\cdot\exp(\lambda\mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n}))]\cdot\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda\ell_{B_{n}}^{B_{R}}(\partial B_{n})}]}{\mathbb{E}[\exp\big(\lambda\ell_{B_{n}}^{B_{R}}(\partial B_{n})\big)]}\\
&\leq e^{-\lambda\cdot\beta\cdot n^{d-1+\alpha}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\exp\big(\lambda\ell_{B_{n}}^{\eta}(\partial B_{n})\big)]}{\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\big(\lambda\ell_{B_{n}}^{B_{R}}(\partial B_{n})\big)\big]}\\
&=e^{-\lambda\cdot\beta\cdot n^{d-1+\alpha}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\lambda\bar{\ell}_{B_{n}}^{\eta}(\partial B_{n})}\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\lambda\bar{\ell}_{B_{n}}^{B_{R}}(\partial B_{n})}\big]}e^{\lambda\mathbb{E}[\ell_{B_{n}}^{\eta}(\partial B_{n})-\ell_{B_{n}}^{B_{R}}(\partial B_{n})]}\\
&\leq e^{-\lambda\cdot\beta\cdot n^{d-1+\alpha}}\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\lambda\cdot\bar{\ell}_{B_{n}}^{0}(\partial B_{n})}\Big]\Big)^{|B_{n}|}\,\,e^{\lambda\mathbb{E}[\ell_{B_{n}}^{\eta}(\partial B_{n})-\ell_{B_{n}}^{B_{R}}(\partial B_{n})]}.\end{split}

(5.9)

Here again
\bar{\ell}_{B_{n}}^{0}(\partial B_{n}):=\ell_{B_{n}}^{0}(\partial B_{n})-\mathbb{E}[\ell_{B_{n}}^{0}(\partial B_{n})]
.
Write
\ell_{n}:=\ell_{B_{n}}^{0}(\partial B_{n})
and
\bar{\ell}_{n}:=\bar{\ell}_{B_{n}}^{0}(\partial B_{n})
to lighten the notation.

By
(
3.4
) in Proposition
3.2

we have that, for
\gamma
small enough,

\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(\gamma\bar{\ell}_{n}/n^{2}\Big)\Big]\leq\exp\Big(\kappa_{d}\gamma^{2}/n^{2}\Big)

(5.10)

for some constant
\kappa_{d}
depending only on
d
.
Moreover, since

G_{n}(0,\partial B_{n})=G_{n}(y,\partial B_{n})=1
for any
y\in B_{n}
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\ell_{B_{n}}^{\eta}(\partial B_{n})-\ell_{B_{n}}^{B_{R}}(\partial B_{n})]&=|B_{n}|G_{n}(0,\partial B_{n})-\sum_{y\in B_{R}}\sum_{z\in\partial B_{n}}G_{n}(y,z)\\
&=|B_{n}|-|B_{R}|=O(n^{d-1+\alpha}).\end{split}

It follows by choosing
\lambda=\gamma\cdot n^{-2}
that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}(\alpha),\ \mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})>\beta\cdot n^{d-1+\alpha})\leq\exp\big(-\gamma\beta n^{d-3+\alpha}+2\gamma^{2}\omega_{d}\kappa_{d}n^{d-2}+\frac{\gamma}{n^{2}}C_{d}n^{d-1+\alpha}\Big).

(5.11)

Thus, if we choose now
\beta\geq 2C_{d}
, and
\gamma
proportional to
n^{\alpha-1}
, we gather that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}(\alpha),\ \mathscr{S}^{\eta}_{B_{n}}(\partial B_{n})>\beta\cdot n^{d-1+\alpha})\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{\beta}{2}\cdot\gamma\cdot n^{d-3+\alpha}+2\gamma^{2}\omega_{d}\kappa_{d}n^{d-2}\Big)\leq e^{-\kappa\cdot\beta\cdot n^{d-3+2\alpha-1}}

(5.12)

for some
\kappa>0
, which concludes the proof.
∎

6
The outer bound for
d>2

In this section we provide an outer bound for the aggregate starting from
an arbitrary particle configuration.
By combining these results with those of
Section
5.2
,
this
will lead to the proof of the shape theorem in dimension
d\geq 3
.
The main result of this section is Theorem
6.1
below.
Recall that
\mathscr{S}(\eta)
denotes the aggregate of the BIDLA
process starting from a general initial particle configuration
\eta:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
d\geq 3
. There exists
\alpha_{0}>0
,
c>0
and
N_{0}<\infty
such that
the following holds.
For any
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
,
N>N_{0}
,

R\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{N}},

any particle configuration
\eta
with support

\partial B_{R}
and number of particles
N
, we have

\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathscr{S}(\eta)\not\subset B_{R+c\,\alpha^{2}\,{N}^{\frac{1}{d}}{\log(N)}^{\rho}}\Big)\leq\frac{1}{N^{\alpha}},

where
\rho=\frac{7}{2}
if
d=3
and
\rho=1
if
d>3
.

The proof is structured as follows. In Section
6.1
we consider one BRW starting inside a set
K
, and lower bound the support of the pioneers on
\partial K
in terms of the second moment of the local time (cf. Lemma
6.2
). In Section
6.2
we define the Random Barrier Growth process (in short RBG), and prove the main contraction result (cf. Proposition
6.5
). In Section
6.3
we upper bound the growth of the RBG process by considering a sequence of shells, large enough to extinct the process. We then conclude the proof of Theorem
6.1
in Section
6.4
. Finally, in Section
6.5
we conclude the proof of Theorem
1.1
by proving Theorem
1.2
, which implies it.

6.1
Expected number of particles settling on a layer

For any finite
K\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
x\in K
we define

\mathcal{R}_{K}^{x}:=\sum\limits_{z\in\partial K}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{\ell^{x}_{K}(z)>0\}},\quad\quad\Gamma^{x}_{K}:=\sum\limits_{z\in\partial K}(\ell_{K}^{x}(z))^{2}.

Here
\mathcal{R}_{K}^{x}
measures the support of the pioneers on
\partial K
.
The next lemma relates the expectations of the above random variables.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
and any
x\in K
we have that

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}_{K}^{x}]\geq\delta\big(1-\delta\,\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{K}^{x}]\big).

Proof.

Suppose that
x\in K
.
Recall the definition of
the number of pioneers
\ell_{K}^{x}(\partial K)
in
(
3.1
).
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have that

(\ell_{K}^{x}(\partial K))^{2}\leq\Gamma_{K}^{x}\,\mathcal{R}^{x}_{K}.

Thus,

\Gamma_{K}^{x}\geq\frac{(\ell_{K}^{x}(\partial K))^{2}}{\mathcal{R}^{x}_{K}}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{\mathcal{R}^{x}\leq\delta\,{\ell_{K}^{x}(\partial K)}\,\}}\geq\frac{1}{\delta}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{\mathcal{R}^{x}_{K}\leq\delta{\ell_{K}^{x}(\partial K)}\}}{{\ell_{K}^{x}(\partial K)}}.

(6.1)

This implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{x}_{K}

\displaystyle\geq\delta\,{\ell_{K}^{x}(\partial K)}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{\mathcal{R}_{K}^{x}>\delta\,{\ell_{K}^{x}(\partial K)}\}}

\displaystyle\geq\delta\big({\ell_{K}^{x}(\partial K)}\,-\,{\ell_{K}^{x}(\partial K)}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{\mathcal{R}_{K}^{x}\leq\delta\,{\ell_{K}^{x}(\partial K)}\}}\big)

\displaystyle\geq\delta\big({\ell_{K}^{x}(\partial K)}\,-\,\delta\,\Gamma_{K}^{x}\big).

Taking expectations on both sides and using that
\mathbb{E}[{\ell_{K}^{x}(\partial K)}]=1

we conclude the proof.
∎

The next proposition plays a central role in the proof of our
outer bound.

Proposition 6.3
.

There exists
c>0
such that,
for each
R>0
and
x\in B_{R}
,
we have

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum\limits_{z\in\partial B_{R}}\big(\mathcal{\ell}^{x}_{B_{R}}(z)-{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{\mathcal{\ell}_{B_{R}}^{x}(z)>0\}}\big)\bigg]\leq\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1-\frac{c}{\log(R-|x|)}&\mbox{ if $d=3$}\\
\displaystyle 1-c&\mbox{ if $d>3$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We use the fact that

\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{z\in\partial B_{R}}\mathcal{\ell}^{x}_{B_{R}}(z)\big]=1

and apply
Lemma
6.2

with
\delta=1/(2\mathbb{E}[\Gamma^{x}_{B_{R}})]

to obtain

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{z\in\partial B_{R}}\big(\mathcal{\ell}^{x}_{B_{R}}(z)-{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{\mathcal{\ell}_{B_{R}}^{x}(z)>0\}}\big)\Big]\leq 1-\frac{1}{4\mathbb{E}[\Gamma^{x}_{B_{R}}]}.

The claim then follows from Corollary

3.9
.
∎

6.2
The Random Barrier Growth

We now introduce the central object for bounding the growth of the aggregate, namely the
Random Barrier Growth
(RBG).
In RBG, settling occurs only on a spherical layer
chosen randomly within a shell of width
H
according to a certain distribution.
The choice of this distribution
fulfils a key property, namely
the expected number of offspring hitting
the random
layer within the current aggregate is proportional to the volume of the aggregate over
H^{d}
. By choosing
H
appropriately,
we obtain a contraction on the number of pioneers which do not settle. The key estimate is presented in
Proposition
6.5

below.

We now proceed with the formal definition of the RBG
in a shell.

Definition 6.4

(
Random Barrier Growth
)
.

Let
R_{1}<R_{2}
be two integer numbers,
set
H=R_{2}-R_{1}
.
Let
\eta:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{N}

be the initial particle configuration
with support in
\partial B_{R_{1}}

and number of particles
N_{1}=\sum_{z\in\partial B_{R_{1}}}\eta(z)
.
Let further
Z_{1},\ldots Z_{N_{1}}
be i.i.d. random variables with distribution
supported in
\{1,\ldots,H\}
given by

P(Z_{i}=h)=\frac{h^{d-1}}{\sum_{r=1}^{H}r^{d-1}},

(6.2)

The
\{Z_{i}\}
represent the sequence of heights at which the BRWs will probe the space for settlement.

The RBG consists of a discrete time
Markov chain
(A_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}

and a sequence of random variables

(\tilde{G}_{t},\tilde{R}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
,
defined as follows.
We set
A_{0}=\emptyset

and label the particles in
\eta
with integers from
1
to
N_{1}
in some arbitrary order.
At each step
t\in\{1,\ldots,N_{1}\}
,
we start, from the
t
-th particle of
\eta
, an independent BRW
stopped on
\partial B_{R_{1}+Z_{t}}

and let
\xi_{t}
be the configuration of pioneers on
\partial B_{R_{1}+Z_{t}}
.
We define the aggregate of the step
t
as
the union of the aggregate of the step
t-1
and
the support of the pioneers configuration, namely

A_{t}:=A_{t-1}\cup\{z\in\partial B_{R_{1}+Z_{t}}\,\,:\,\xi_{t}(z)>0\}.

After that we proceed as follows.

(a)

For each particle reaching
\partial B_{R_{1}+Z_{t}}
within

A_{t-1}
we start an independent critical branching random walk stopped on

\partial B_{R_{2}}
and call
\tilde{G}_{t}
the total number of pioneers
on
\partial B_{R_{2}}
, which we dub
green particles
.

(b)

For each vertex

x\in\partial B_{R_{1}+Z_{t}}\setminus A_{t-1}

with
\xi_{t}(x)>0
,
we let exactly one of the
\xi_{t}(x)
particles at
x
settle
and start

(\xi_{t}(x)-1){\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{\xi_{t}(x)>0\}}

independent critical branching random walks
stopped on
B_{R_{2}}
from
x
,
namely one critical Branching Random Walk from each particle which has not settled at
x
.
We call
\tilde{R}_{t}

be the total number of pioneers on
\partial B_{R_{2}}
,
which we dub
red particles
.

We then move to the step
t+1
and iterate the procedure
up to reaching
t=N_{1}
.

Thus,

N_{2}:=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{N_{1}}(\tilde{G}_{t}+\tilde{R}_{t})

(6.3)

corresponds to the total number of particles reaching

\partial B_{R_{2}}

in RBG, which we refer to as the
escaping particles.

The next proposition provides an upper bound on the expected number of escaping particles.

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
d\geq 3
.
There exist constants
c>0
,
J<\infty
depending only on the dimension such that the following holds.
Consider a RBG with integer radii
R_{2}>R_{1}
,
starting from an initial particle configuration
\eta

with support in
\partial{B}_{R_{1}}

and with
N_{1}=\sum_{x\in\partial{B}_{R_{1}}}\eta(x)
particles.
Then, with
H:=R_{2}-R_{1}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[N_{2}\big]\leq N_{1}\,\Big(J\,\frac{N_{1}}{H^{d}}+\,\,\gamma_{H}\Big)

where

\gamma_{H}:=\begin{cases}1-\frac{c}{\log H}&\mbox{ if $d=3$}\\
1-c&\mbox{ if $d>3$}.\end{cases}

This result is useful since, by setting the width of the shell

H
appropriately as a function of
N_{1}
, it implies
that only a fraction of the number of the particles of the initial particle configuration escapes without settling.

Proof.

We bound the expectation of
\tilde{G}_{t}
and
\tilde{R}_{t}

in (
6.3
) separately, starting from
\tilde{G}_{t}
.
Let
t\in\{1,\ldots N_{1}\}
be an arbitrary step of the procedure and let
y_{t}\in B_{R_{1}}
be the vertex where the
t
-th particle
of the initial configuration is located.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
be the
\sigma
-algebra
generated by the first
t
steps of the RBG.

The first observation is that the expectation of
\tilde{G}_{t}
,
the number of escaping green particles at the step
t
,
corresponds to the expected number of particles hitting
\partial B_{R_{1}+Z_{t}}

within
A_{t-1}
.
This holds since, once particles are stopped in
\partial B_{R_{1}+Z_{t}}\cap A_{t-1}
, they proceed to
\partial B_{R_{2}}

as independent BRWs stopped on
B_{R_{2}}
.
Hence,
summing over all possible realisations of the random radius at the step
t

we deduce that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\big[\tilde{G}_{t}\bigm|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]&=\sum\limits_{h=1}^{H}P(Z_{t}=h)\sum\limits_{x\in A_{t-1}\cap\partial B_{R_{1}+h}}G_{R_{1}+h}(y_{t},x)\\
&\leq\sum\limits_{h=1}^{H}\frac{h^{d-1}}{H^{d}}|A_{t-1}\cap\partial B_{R_{1}+h}|\frac{c}{h^{d-1}}\leq c\frac{|A_{t-1}|}{H^{d}}.\end{split}

(6.4)

Here we used (
6.2
),
Lemma
3.5
,
and
the fact that the sets
\{\partial B_{R_{1}+h}
:

h\in\mathbb{N}\}

are disjoint.
Using that
\mathbb{E}[|A_{t-1}|]\leq t-1
, we deduce that
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{G}_{t}]\leq ct/H^{d}
.

For the expected number of red particles
at the step
t
we have

\mathbb{E}\big[\tilde{R}_{t}\bigm|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]=\frac{1}{\sum_{r=1}^{H}r^{d-1}}\sum_{h=1}^{H}h^{d-1}\sum\limits_{z\in\partial B_{R_{1}+h}\setminus A_{t-1}}\mathbb{E}\Big[(\xi_{t}(z)-1){\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\xi_{t}(z)>0}\Big]\leq\gamma_{H},

where for the last step we summed over all vertices in
\partial B_{R_{1}+h}
and applied Proposition
6.3
.

Combining the two estimates above we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{t=1}^{N_{1}}\mathbb{E}\big[\tilde{G}_{t}+\tilde{R}_{t}\big]\leq\frac{c}{H^{d}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{N_{1}}t\,+\,\gamma_{H}N_{1}\leq c\frac{N_{1}^{2}}{H^{d}}+\,\,\gamma_{H}N_{1}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 6.6
.

The choice of the barrier distribution in (
6.2
)
is crucial for Proposition
6.5
.
Thanks to this distribution, the proportionality depends only on the size of
A_{t}
,
and not on its specific shape.

6.3
RBG through shells

The proof of Theorem
6.1

relies on analyzing a sequence of shells
and a sequence of RBG’s crossing each of them.
In this procedure, the width of each subsequent shell
is not fixed but random: it depends on the number of particles
that escape from the previous shell.
In light of Proposition
6.5
,
the width is chosen so that, in expectation,
only a fraction of the particles escaping the previous shell
will escape the next one.
This results in a contraction at a rate that can be uniformly bounded up to a certain blow-up time.
As a consequence, the total number of shells crossed before the process dies out is small.

Definition 6.7

(
Random Barrier Growth through shells
)
.

Fix an initial (integer) radius
R_{0}
and
an initial particle configuration
\eta_{0}
with support on

\partial B_{R_{0}}
.
Let
N_{0}:=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\eta_{0}(x)
be the total number of particles at time zero.
At each step
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
given the step
t-1
,
we set

R_{t}:=R_{t-1}+H_{t},

where

H_{t}:=\Big\lceil\,\Big(\kappa\,N_{t-1}\,\big(\log(N_{t-1}+1)\big)^{\beta}\Big)^{\frac{1}{d}}\,\Big\rceil

(6.5)

with
\beta=1
if
d=3
and
\beta=0
if
d>3
. Here
\kappa>0
is a large enough constant depending only on the dimension.
We start a RBG with radii
R_{t-1}
and
R_{t}
from the particle configuration

\eta_{t-1}

(which has support in
\partial B_{R_{t-1}})

and let
\eta_{t}
be the escaping particle configuration of such process
(which has support
in
\partial B_{R_{t}}
, recall Definition
6.4
).
We then move to the next step
t+1
and repeat the procedure.
Define for any
\alpha>0
the stopping time (with the convention that
\inf\emptyset=+\infty
)

\displaystyle T^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,:\,N_{t}>\alpha N_{0}\}.

(6.6)

The next result shows that, as long as
\{t<T^{(\alpha)}\}
,
the number of particles in the RBG decays exponentially fast in the number of shells which have been crossed, with a rate which might depend on
N_{0}
, on
\alpha
, and on the dimension
d\geq 3
.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
d\geq 3
.
In (
6.5
) we can choose
\kappa
large enough, depending only on the dimension, so that for any
\alpha>1
and

t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
we have

\mathbb{E}\big[N_{t}\,{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{t\leq T^{(\alpha)}}\big]\leq\begin{cases}e^{-\frac{c\,t\,}{\log(\alpha N_{0})}}N_{0}&\mbox{ if $d=3$}\\
e^{-c\,t\,}N_{0}&\mbox{ if $d>3$.}\end{cases}

(6.7)

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{t}
be the sigma-field
generated by the fist
t
steps of the RBG through Random Shells
and observe that
N_{t}
,

H_{t+1}
,

R_{t+1}
and

{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{T^{(\alpha)}\geq t+1\}}

are
\mathcal{G}_{t}
-measurable by construction.
B Proposition
6.5

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[N_{t}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{T^{(\alpha)}\geq t\}}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}\big[N_{t}\bigm|\mathcal{G}_{t-1}\big]{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{T^{(\alpha)}\geq t\}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(J\frac{N_{t-1}^{2}}{H_{t}^{d}}\,+\,\gamma_{H_{t}}N_{t-1}\Big){\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{T^{(\alpha)}\geq t\}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[N_{t-1}\Big(\,\frac{J}{\kappa}\cdot\frac{1}{(\log(N_{t-1}+1))^{\beta}}\,+\gamma_{H_{t}}\Big){\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{T^{(\alpha)}\geq t\}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[N_{t-1}e^{-\frac{c}{\log(N_{t-1}+1)}}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{T^{(\alpha)}\geq t\}}\Big]&\mbox{ if $d=3$}\\
\displaystyle e^{-c}\,\,\mathbb{E}\big[N_{t-1}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{T^{(\alpha)}\geq t\}}\big]&\mbox{ if $d>3$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}\displaystyle e^{-\frac{c}{\log(\alpha N_{0})}}\mathbb{E}\big[N_{t-1}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{T^{(\alpha)}\geq t-1\}}\big]&\mbox{ if $d=3$}\\
e^{-c}\,\mathbb{E}\big[N_{t-1}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{\{T^{(\alpha)}\geq t-1\}}\big]&\mbox{ if $d>3.$}\end{cases}

For the third inequality above
in dimension
d=3
we used that
\kappa
is a large enough constant such that if
N_{t-1}>0
, recalling that

\gamma_{H_{t}}=1-\frac{c}{\log H_{t}}\quad\mbox{ with }\quad H_{t}:=\Big\lceil\,\Big(\kappa\,N_{t-1}\,\log(N_{t-1}+1)\Big)^{\frac{1}{d}}\,\Big\rceil,

we have

\gamma_{H_{t}}+\frac{J}{\kappa}\cdot\frac{1}{\log(N_{t-1}+1)}\,\leq 1-\frac{cd}{\log\kappa+\log(N_{t-1}+1)+\log\log(N_{t-1}+1)}+\frac{J}{\kappa}\cdot\frac{1}{\log(N_{t-1}+1)}\\
\leq 1-\Big[\frac{c\,d}{2\log\kappa+2}-\frac{J}{\kappa}\Big]\cdot\frac{1}{\log(N_{t-1}+1)}\leq e^{-\frac{c}{\log(N_{t-1}+1)}}.

(6.8)

The proof then follows by iterating this bound.
∎

The previous lemma
is useful to stochastically bound from above the decay in the number of particles in the RBG when the number of crossed shells is large.
However, when this number is small, the previous lemma is powerless, and we need the following proposition, which relies on a stochastic comparison with independent BRWs restricted to a ball and large deviation estimates.

Proposition 6.9
.

There exists
c>0
such that,
for any large enough
\alpha
,
uniformly in
t,D,R_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
satisfying
R_{0}<D
, in the initial
particle configuration

\eta_{0}
with
N_{0}
particles and support in
\partial B_{R_{0}}
,
the RBG
starting from the particle configuration
\eta_{0}

satisfies

\mathbb{P}(N_{t}>\,\alpha\,N_{0},R_{t}<D)\leq D\,\exp\Big(-c\,\alpha\,\frac{N_{0}}{D^{2}}\Big).

Proof.

Fix arbitrary positive integers
D
and
t
.
By a union bound and the fact that
R_{t}\in\mathbb{N}

\mathbb{P}(N_{t}>\,\alpha\,N_{0},R_{t}<D)\leq\sum\limits_{r=R_{0}}^{D-1}\mathbb{P}(N_{t}>\,\alpha\,N_{0},R_{t}=r).

(6.9)

We now use that,
almost surely, we can dominate the RBG process
by independent critical BRWs.
Order the particles of the initial configuration
\eta_{0}
from
1
to
N_{0}
arbitrarily.
Fix
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
From each particle
we start a independent BRW restricted
to
B_{R_{0}+r}
,
and let
\ell^{(i)}_{B_{R_{0}+r}}(\partial B_{R_{0}+r})

denote the number of pioneers reaching the outer boundary of the ball
B_{R_{0}+r}
.
Moreover, let

\mathcal{N}_{r}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{0}}\ell^{(i)}_{B_{R_{0}+r}}(\partial B_{R_{0}+r})

denote the total number of pioneers reaching
\partial B_{R_{0}+r}
.
The
N_{0}
independent BRWs
starting from each particle in
\eta_{0}

and the RBG through random shells starting from
\eta_{0}

are defined on the same probability space and can be sampled by using the same instructions.
Hence, by monotonicity (see Corollary
A.4
in Appendix
A
), we have that

\{N_{t}>\alpha N_{0},R_{t}=r\}\subseteq\{\mathcal{N}_{r}>\alpha N_{0},R_{t}=r\}\subseteq\{\mathcal{N}_{r}>\alpha N_{0}\}.

By applying
Proposition
3.2

with small enough positive
\lambda
,
depending only on the dimension,
we obtain from from (
6.9
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N_{t}>\alpha\,N_{0},R_{t}<D)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{r=R_{0}}^{D-1}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{N}_{r}>\alpha N_{0}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{r=R_{0}}^{D-1}e^{\frac{-\lambda\alpha N_{0}}{(R_{0}+r)^{2}}}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{0}}\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\frac{\lambda}{(R_{0}+r)^{2}}\ell^{(i)}_{B_{R_{0}+r}}(\partial B_{R_{0}+r})}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{r=R_{0}}^{D-1}e^{\frac{\lambda(-\alpha+c)N_{0}}{(R_{0}+r)^{2}}}\leq De^{\frac{-\alpha\lambda N_{0}}{2D^{2}}}

where for the fourth
inequality we used that
R_{0}+r\leq D

and that
\alpha
is large enough.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.1

We can now conclude the proof of Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Let
\alpha>1
be a parameter to be chosen later large enough, and recall from (

) the definition of

. For some fixed constant

).

(6.10)

Appendix A
The Abelian property

One of the key properties of a large family of related models is the so-called Abelian property, which allows particles to be moved in any order – provided certain rules are respected – without altering the statistics of the final configuration.
In this section we establish this property for Branching IDLA, following the framework of Rolla and Sidoravicius
[
31
]
, and adapting their definitions and arguments to our context.

Particle configurations.

Given any particle configuration
\eta:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{N}
,
we say that the vertex
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is
stable
in
\eta
if
\eta(x)\leq 1
, and it is
unstable
otherwise.
The particle configuration
\eta
is stable if every vertex
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is stable in
\eta
.
We interpret
\eta(x)=0
as the site
x
being empty, and
\eta(x)=1
as the particle at
x
being settled.

Instructions.

To state the Abelian property for Branching IDLA, we first introduce a collection of instructions, that is, operators acting on a configuration of particles and producing a new configuration.
When a particle performs a Branching Random Walk step, it dies and creates offspring at its neighbouring sites.
Accordingly, we introduce birth-death instructions.
In Branching IDLA, a particle performs a BRW step only if it has not yet settled, that is, if the site currently hosts at least two particles.
In this case, we refer to the instruction as
legal
.
For the purpose of deriving a Least Action Principle and comparing Branching IDLA to the RBG process, however, it will be convenient to also allow settled particles to move. For that reason, we allow to use instructions at sites containing at least one particle, in which case the instruction is referred to as
acceptable
.

A
birth-death instruction
,
\mathfrak{A}^{x}_{\xi}
, is defined through a vector
\xi\in\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{N}^{n}
,
where
\mathcal{N}
is the set of sites which are neighbours of the origin.
If
\xi
has
k
-coordinates,
each entry is thought of as the step that one of the
k
children of an individual at
x
makes. More precisely, for any

\xi=(\xi(1),\dots,\xi(k))\in\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{N}^{n}
with
k
coordinates we have

\text{if}\quad\eta(x)\geq 1,\quad\mathfrak{A}^{x}_{\xi}\text{ is $\eta$-acceptable and}\quad\mathfrak{A}^{x}_{\xi}(\eta):=\eta-{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{x}+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{k>0}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{x+\xi(i)}.

In words, one individual at
x
produces
k\geq 0
children and dies, and the child numbered
i\leq k

is born at
x+\xi(i)
.
Let us denote by
\mathcal{I}_{x}
the set of all possible birth-death instructions at
x
,
namely

\mathcal{I}_{x}=\Big\{\mathfrak{A}^{x}_{\xi}\,:\,\xi\in\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\,\mathcal{N}^{n}\Big\}.

A birth-death instruction at
x
,
\mathfrak{A}^{x}\in\mathcal{I}_{x}
,
is
\eta
-
legal

if
\eta(x)>1
,
\eta
-
acceptable
if
\eta(x)\geq 1
and it is
\eta
-forbidden if
\eta(x)=0
.

The two key properties which make this representation Abelian are that, for any

\eta
-acceptable birth-death instruction at
x
,
\mathfrak{A}^{x}\in\mathcal{I}_{x}
, and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathfrak{A}^{x}\Big(\eta+n{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{x}\Big)=\mathfrak{A}^{x}(\eta)+n{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{x},

(A.1)

and that, for any
y\not=x
, and an
\eta
-acceptable birth-death instruction at
y
,
\mathfrak{A}^{y}\in\mathcal{I}_{y}
, we have

\mathfrak{A}^{y}(\eta)(x)\geq\eta(x).

(A.2)

These two properties have the following implication.
Assume that
\eta(x)\geq 1
(and, by (
A.2
), this implies that
\mathfrak{A}^{x}
is
\mathfrak{A}^{y}(\eta)
-acceptable),
and that
\mathfrak{A}^{y}
is
\eta
-acceptable. Then
\mathfrak{A}^{y}
is
\mathfrak{A}^{x}(\eta)
-acceptable by (
A.2
),
and

\mathfrak{A}^{x}\circ\mathfrak{A}^{y}(\eta)=\mathfrak{A}^{y}\circ\mathfrak{A}^{x}(\eta).

(A.3)

Stack of instructions and sequence of topplings.

In this framework, the instructions are assigned to sites rather than to particles.
For this reason, we introduce
stacks of instructions
, that is, sequences of birth–death instructions associated with each site.
More formally, a stack of instructions is a vector

\tau=(\tau_{x,j})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}},

where each element
\tau_{x,j}
belongs to
\mathcal{I}_{x}
, the set of instructions associated with the site
x
.
In order to sample a Branching IDLA process using stacks of instructions,
the elements
(\tau_{x,j})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}

are drawn at random as i.i.d. random variables
such that, for each birth-death instruction

\mathfrak{A}^{x}_{\xi}\in\mathcal{I}_{x}
producing
k\in\mathbb{N}
children, i.e.
\xi\in\mathcal{N}^{k}
,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{x,j}=\mathfrak{A}^{x}_{\xi})=\frac{1}{(2d)^{k}}\nu(k),

where we recall that
\nu
denotes the offspring distribution.
Given a stack of instructions
\tau
, we now define sequences of topplings.
Starting from an arbitrary initial particle configuration
\eta:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{N}
, we apply the instructions according to an ordered sequence of sites

\alpha=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}).

To keep track of the number of instructions used at each site, we introduce an
odometer field

h:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{N},

which records, for every site, how many instructions have been used.
A
toppling
at a site
x

is acceptable if
\eta(x)\geq 1

and legal if
\eta(x)>1
, and it is defined as

\Phi_{x}(\eta,h)=\bigl(\tau_{x,h(x)+1}\eta,\,h+\mathbf{1}_{x}\bigr).

A
sequence of topplings
along

\alpha
is acceptable (resp. legal) if, for each
0\leq k\leq n-1
,
the particle configuration of

\Phi_{x_{k}}\circ\dots\circ\Phi_{x_{1}}(\eta,h)

has at least one particle (resp. two particles) at
x_{k+1}
.
If
\alpha
is acceptable, then the sequence of topplings
\alpha
is defined by the following operation

\Phi_{\alpha}(\eta,h)=\Phi_{x_{n}}\circ\dots\circ\Phi_{x_{1}}(\eta,h).

We omit writing the odometer field
h
when it is not needed, and think of
\Phi_{\alpha}
as acting on configurations.
For any sequence
\alpha
, the
odometer
of
\alpha
,

m_{\alpha}
, is defined as

m_{\alpha}=\sum_{x\in\alpha}{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{x}.

Thus
m_{\alpha}
counts the number of appearances of the site
x
in the sequence
\alpha
.

Lemma A.1
.

If
\alpha,\beta
are
\eta
-acceptable finite sequences and
m_{\alpha}=m_{\beta}
, then for any odometer field
h
,

\Phi_{\alpha}(\eta,h)=\Phi_{\beta}(\eta,h)
.

Proof.

We write a proof by induction on the length of
\alpha
, that we denote with
|\alpha|
.
The property is obviously true for
|\alpha|=1
, since then
\alpha=\beta
.
Assume that
|\alpha|=n>1
and that

\alpha=(x,x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
, whereas
x
occurs in position
j+1>1
for the first
time in
\beta=(y_{1},\dots,y_{j},x,\dots)
, and
y_{1},\dots,y_{j}\not=x
.
Call now
\beta^{\prime}
the sequence obtained from
\beta
by moving the first occurrence of
x
to the front, namely
\beta^{\prime}=(x,y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{j},\ldots)
.
We will show that
\beta^{\prime}
is acceptable for
\eta
and that
\Phi_{\beta}(\eta,h)=\Phi_{\beta^{\prime}}(\eta,h)
.
Since the first site of

\alpha
and that of
\beta^{\prime}
are the same, and since
m_{\alpha}=m_{\beta^{\prime}}
,
we then deduce by induction that
\Phi_{\alpha}(\eta,h)=\Phi_{\beta^{\prime}}(\eta,h)=\Phi_{\beta}(\eta,h)=\Phi_{\beta^{\prime}}(\eta,h)
.

Since
\Phi_{\alpha}
is
\eta
-acceptable we have that
\eta(x)\geq 1
.
Call
\beta_{j}:=(y_{1},\dots,y_{j-1})
, and note that

\eta(x)\geq 1
implies that
\Phi_{\beta_{j}}(\eta)(x)\geq 1
, since, by property (
A.2
) mass cannot decrease at
x
when
toppling sites other than
x
.
By applying (
A.3
) we deduce that, if
\mathfrak{A}^{y_{j}}
and
\mathfrak{A}^{x}
are the instructions occurring
in
\beta
in the
j
and
j+1
positions respectively, then

\mathfrak{A}^{y_{j}}\ \text{ is }\mathfrak{A}^{x}(\Phi_{\beta_{j}}(\eta))\text{-acceptable}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{A}^{x}\circ\mathfrak{A}^{y_{j}}(\Phi_{\beta_{j}}(\eta))=\mathfrak{A}^{y_{j}}\circ\mathfrak{A}^{x}(\Phi_{\beta_{j}}(\eta)).

(A.4)

Now, by induction, we have that
\Phi_{\beta_{j+1}}
is
\Phi_{x}(\eta)
-acceptable and

\Phi_{x}\circ\Phi_{\beta_{j+1}}(\eta)=\Phi_{\beta_{j+1}}\circ\Phi_{x}(\eta).

(A.5)

This implies that
\Phi_{\beta^{\prime}}(\eta,h)=\Phi_{\beta}(\eta,h)
as desired, thus concluding the proof.
∎

The second lemma we present is the key result toward the Least Action Principle.
We write

m_{\beta}\prec m_{\alpha}

if
m_{\beta}(x)\leq m_{\alpha}(x)
for all

x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Lemma A.2
.

If
\alpha
is an
\eta
-acceptable sequence of topplings,
\Phi_{\alpha}(\eta)
is stable in the finite set
K\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
\beta
is a finite
\eta
-legal sequence of topplings in
K
, then

m_{\beta}\prec m_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

The proof is obtained by induction on
|\beta|
. The claim is obvious if
|\beta|=1
, so assume
|\eta|>1
. Let
y\in K
be
such that
\beta:=(y,y_{2},\dots)
.
By assumption, it is necessarily the case that
\eta(y)>1
.
Since
\alpha
stabilizes
\eta
, there must be a toppling which acts on
y
:
suppose that
y
occurs for the first time in position
j+1
in
\alpha
.
As in the previous proof, in
\Phi_{\alpha}
we can commute the instruction
\mathfrak{A}^{y}
with
the first
j
instructions. Call
\alpha_{1}
the sequence of topplings obtained by deleting the first site
y
occurring in the sequence
\alpha
,
\beta_{1}=(y_{2},\dots)
and
(\eta_{1},h_{1})=\Phi_{y}(\eta,h)
.
Now,
|\beta_{1}|=|\beta|-1
, the configuration
\Phi_{\alpha_{1}}(\eta_{1},h_{1})
is stable in
K
, and
\beta_{1}
is a
\eta_{1}
-legal sequence.
Thus by induction we deduce that
m_{\beta_{1}}\prec m_{\alpha_{1}}
, and since

m_{\beta}=m_{\beta_{1}}+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{y}
and
m_{\alpha}=m_{\alpha_{1}}+{\text{\Large$\mathfrak{1}$}}_{y}
,
we conclude that
m_{\beta}\prec m_{\alpha}
.
∎

We can now state the Least Action Principle, which is a direct corollary of Lemma
A.2
.
We say that the sequence
\alpha
, which is acceptable for
\eta
, stabilizes
\eta
in
K

if the particle configuration
\Phi_{\alpha}(\eta)
is stable in
K
.

Proposition A.3
.

If
\alpha
and
\beta
are both
\eta
-legal finite sequences which stabilize
\eta
in
K
, then
m_{\alpha}=m_{\beta}
, and consequently
\Phi_{\alpha}(\eta)=\Phi_{\beta}(\eta)
.
Furthermore, for any finite
K\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, any
K
-stabilizing
\eta
-acceptable finite sequence
\alpha
has an odometer
m_{\alpha}

above
m_{\beta}
, where
\beta
is any
\eta
-legal sequence in
K
which stabilizes
\eta
in
K
.

In summary, any successful
\eta
-legal finite sequence of topplings produces the same stable configuration, and its odometer is below the odometer of any stabilizing
\eta
-acceptable sequence of topplings.

Lemma
A.2
leads to the following useful corollary,
which is applied in Section

to compare
Branching IDLA with the Random Barrier Growth.
Indeed, Branching IDLA can be sampled by toppling any stabilizing

legal
sequence, whereas RBG can be sampled by
toppling an
acceptable
stabilizing sequence.
If the odometer of the RBG is known to vanish outside a large ball
(that is, no particle leaves that ball in the RBG),
which is precisely what we prove, then it follows that the odometer of
Branching IDLA also vanishes outside the same ball
(that is, no particle leaves the same ball in Branching IDLA).

Corollary A.4
.

Let
K
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
and let
\eta
be an arbitrary particle configuration with support contained in

.
Suppose that

is
\eta
-legal and stabilizes

in

,

\eta
-acceptable and stabilizes

in

,
