Effect of pregnancy intention on postpartum contraceptive use in Ethiopia: a national longitudinal study using propensity score methods
Authors: Birye Dessalegn Mekonnen, Vidanka Vasilevski, Ayele Geleto Bali, Linda Sweet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnintended pregnancy continues to be a major global public health challenge, with over three-fifths resulting in induced abortion. To reduce unintended pregnancies and associated consequences, providing effective postpartum contraception is crucial. Despite the high rates of unintended pregnancies and low postpartum contraceptive use in Ethiopia, the influence of pregnancy intention on postpartum contraceptive use remains inadequately explored. This study aimed to examine the effect of pregnancy intention on postpartum contraceptive use in Ethiopia at six weeks, six months, and one year postpartum.MethodsLongitudinal survey data from the Performance Monitoring for Action (PMA) Ethiopia survey were used for this analysis. The analysis included randomly selected pregnant women and those in the early postpartum period who participated in the baseline survey and completed the follow-up surveys. The data were collected from selected regions of Ethiopia between 2019 and 2021. We used inverse probability of treatment weighting based on propensity scores to address the imbalance of baseline confounders between women with intended pregnancies and those with unintended pregnancies. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated using a logistic regression model with inverse probability of treatment weights.ResultsWomen with intended pregnancies had higher odds of using postpartum contraceptives at six weeks (OR = 1.51, 95%CI: 1.05–2.17), and at six months postpartum (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.06–1.57). Similarly, women with intended pregnancies had a higher likelihood of using postpartum contraceptives at one year postpartum (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.13–1.96) compared with women with unintended pregnancies.ConclusionPostpartum modern contraceptive use remained suboptimal across the postpartum period, regardless of pregnancy intention. Women with intended pregnancies demonstrated higher odds of contraceptive uptake at six weeks, six months, and one year postpartum. Sexual and reproductive health services, particularly family planning access for all women, should be strengthened. Targeted strategies such as early identification and linkage to care are needed to address the specific contraceptive needs of those who have experienced unintended pregnancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02222-8.

Introduction

Prevention of unintended pregnancy has been prioritised as one of the first steps toward accelerating progress in maternal health [1]. Nevertheless, unintended pregnancy continues to be a major global public health challenge, with the highest burden in sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries, such as Ethiopia [2]. Globally, 61% of unintended pregnancies result in induced abortion, and approximately 45% of abortions are unsafe, increasing the risk of maternal death or morbidity [2, 3]. Analysis of multi-country data found that 30% of pregnancies in SSA were unintended [4], while a systematic review reported 34% in the same region [5]. In Ethiopia, the prevalence of unintended pregnancy ranges from 28% to 30% [6, 7].

Unnded pregnanciesinte result in low use of maternal healthcare services across the maternity continuum of care. For instance, unintended pregnancies are linked to delayed initiation and low use of antenatal care [8, 9], reduced rates of institutional childbirth [10], increased likelihood of home births, and limited access to postnatal care compared to intended pregnancies across the maternity continuum. Furthermore, studies found that women with unintended pregnancies were less likely to use postpartum contraceptives [11, 12], while others showed the opposite, where women with intended pregnancies were less likely to use postpartum contraceptives [13]. Studies have shown that postpartum contraceptive use was higher among women with mistimed pregnancies [14, 15], while evidence for unwanted pregnancies was mixed, with one study reporting higher use [16], and others finding no significant association [15, 17]. These differences may be due to variations in individual motivations, healthcare access, or cultural norms. Moreover, the limited and inconsistent findings highlight the need for further research.

Postpartum contraceptive use helps prevent pregnancy during the first year after childbirth, ultimately reducing the risk of maternal mortality through optimal birth spacing [18, 19]. The World Health Organization (WHO) advises a minimum gap of 24 months between pregnancies to enhance maternal and child health outcomes [20]. However, low use of postpartum contraceptives remains a maternal and child health concern. The use of postpartum contraceptives in low- and middle-income countries was 41.2% [21], and 37.41% in SSA [22]. In Ethiopia, postpartum modern contraceptive use was 45.44% to 45.79% [23, 24], and all methods of family planning use were at 48.11% [25]. To enhance postpartum contraceptive use, the continuum of care for maternal and child health offers opportunities for family planning services, providing information during antenatal care, childbirth, postnatal care, and child immunisation [26, 27]. Furthermore, several factors have been consistently associated with increased postpartum contraceptive use, including higher maternal education level, discussion with a husband or partner about family planning, antenatal care attendance, receipt of family planning counselling during antenatal care, use of postnatal care, resumption of menses, resumption of sexual activity, and prior contraceptive use [22, 23, 28].

Ethiopia faces high rates of unintended pregnancies and low postpartum contraceptive use [7, 23]. Although studies, as noted above, have examined the link between pregnancy intention and postpartum contraceptive use, the evidence remains mixed and inconclusive. This highlights a clear gap in understanding how pregnancy intention influences postpartum contraceptive use at different postpartum periods, which our study aims to address. Hence, to support interventions aimed at preventing unintended pregnancies throughout the postpartum period, it is essential to examine the effect of pregnancy intention on contraceptive use at six weeks, six months, and one year postpartum. Furthermore, robust studies that account for confounding factors and address baseline differences between women with intended and unintended pregnancies are needed to better understand contraceptive use decisions after childbirth. Propensity Score Methods (PSM) offer a robust approach to adjust for these confounders by balancing covariates between groups, enhancing comparability, and yielding stronger causal estimates [29]. This provides reliable evidence to inform family planning initiatives that could reduce unintended pregnancy. Thus, this study aimed to examine the effect of pregnancy intention on postpartum contraceptive use in Ethiopia, applying propensity score-based Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW).

Methods

Data source

Longitudinal survey data from the Performance Monitoring for Action (PMA) Ethiopia was used in this study. The PMA Ethiopia study is a nationally representative cohort study conducted in collaboration with Addis Ababa University (AAU), the Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health, and the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health (JHSPH) from October 2019 to August 2021 [30]. The study was conducted in six regions and one city administration of Ethiopia, including Afar, Tigray, Amhara, Oromia, Southern Nations Nationalities and Peoples’ (SNNP) regions, and Addis Ababa city administration. Using multistage sampling with proportional allocation, 217 data collection areas were selected, covering 90% of the Ethiopian population. A detailed description of the PMA Ethiopia survey methodology and design is available elsewhere [30].

In the PMA Ethiopia survey, a household census was conducted in each data collection area to identify all women aged 15–49 years who were either pregnant or less than six weeks postpartum. Eligible women who consented to participate were completed a baseline survey. They were subsequently surveyed at approximately six weeks, six months, and one year postpartum. Women were ineligible for the follow-up interviews if they reported a stillbirth, miscarriage, abortion, refused follow-up at any point, or had no live birth from the most recent pregnancy at the time of the interview. We used data collected at six weeks, six months, and one year about postpartum contraception use linked to baseline data. Women who were pregnant or within 0–4 weeks and 5–9 weeks postpartum at baseline completed the baseline survey. Women who were pregnant or up to 4 weeks postpartum at baseline (n = 2,578) completed the six-week follow-up survey about six weeks after giving birth, while those 5–9 weeks postpartum at baseline (n = 277) completed the six-week follow-up survey concurrently with their baseline interview. In total, 2,855 women completed the baseline interview and consented to follow-up interviews. Out of the 2,664 participants who completed the six-week postpartum interviews, 143 women were excluded due to non-live births (miscarriage, abortion, stillbirth) or missing data on birth outcomes, and 4 had no data on their pregnancy intention; thus, records of 2,517 women were included in the analysis. A total of 2,414 women completed the six-month postpartum interview, of whom the records of 2,409 women were included in the analysis after excluding five due to missing data on pregnancy intention. Meassure

Outcome variables

Postpartum modern contraceptive use was the outcome of interest, which was determined based on the woman’s self-reported use of modern contraceptives at six weeks, six months, and one-year surveys. Each interviewed woman was asked: “Are you or your partner currently doing something or using any family planning method to delay or avoid getting pregnant?” If the woman responded “Yes,” she was then asked: “Which method are you using?” A woman was classified as a modern contraceptive user if she stated using any of the following: male or female sterilisation, implant, intrauterine device, injectable, oral contraceptive or emergency contraceptive pills, male or female condom, or the lactational amenorrhea method (LAM) [31]. Women who reported LAM as their method of use during the one-year postpartum interview were not classified as modern contraceptive users, as they were at risk of pregnancy beyond six months after childbirth [32].

Exposure variables

The exposure variable in this study was women’s self-reported pregnancy intention. Pregnancy intention was determined by asking women whether their pregnancy was desired at the time of conception, whether they had wished to delay it to a later time, or whether they did not want any or more children at all. Based on their responses, pregnancy intention was categorised as ‘intended pregnancy’ if the response was ‘then/now,’ and as ‘unintended pregnancy’ if the response was ‘later’ (indicating a mistimed pregnancy) or ‘not at all’ (indicating an unwanted pregnancy).

Control variables

Potential covariates, including age, religion, marital status, educational level, parity, history of contraceptive use, household wealth quintile, location of residence, and study regions, were controlled during the analysis. Adjusting for these confounders was necessary to determine the unbiased effect of pregnancy intention on the use of postpartum contraceptives. A Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) was constructed to represent the assumed causal relationship between pregnancy intention and postpartum contraceptive use and to guide confounder selection. In the Directed Acyclic Graph, pregnancy intention was specified as the exposure and postpartum contraceptive use as the outcome (Supplementary file 1).

Statistical analysis

The weighted frequencies and percentages were computed to summarise the characteristics of the participants. Additionally, the distribution of postpartum modern contraceptive use and pregnancy intention across participant characteristics were described.

The propensity score method was employed to estimate the effect of pregnancy intentions on postpartum contraceptive use at six weeks, six months, and one year postpartum. Propensity score methods help reduce confounding effects when estimating the effect of exposure on outcomes in observational studies [33]. In observational studies, the non-randomised self-selection of participants into exposures can introduce bias influenced by sociodemographic factors, potentially confounding the relationship between exposures and outcomes [34]. Propensity score methods address these biases by balancing confounders, providing more accurate estimates of exposure effects [35]. In this analysis, we used IPTW based on propensity scores. The IPTW assigns weights to each observation to balance covariate distributions between the exposed and unexposed groups [29]. The propensity score represents the probability of being in the exposure group (in this case, having an intended pregnancy) given a set of observed covariates [36].

Propensity scores were derived using a logistic regression model, where pregnancy intention status (intended vs. unintended) served as the dependent variable while accounting for potential confounders. The model incorporated all available covariates associated with both the exposure and outcome variables. Following this, IPTW was calculated as 1/propensity score for those with intended pregnancies and 1/(1 − propensity score) for those with unintended pregnancies. The IPTW supports the precision of variance estimation for the main effect while maintaining the original sample size [29]. Covariate balance and the distribution of propensity scores were examined before and after weighting to evaluate the adequacy of the IPTW. To evaluate covariate balance between women with intended and unintended pregnancies, standardised mean differences were applied, with a threshold of 0.1 (10%) indicating a good balance [36, 37]. Additionally, kernel density plots were employed to visually illustrate the balance of propensity scores between the two groups, with closer proximity of the density lines indicating successful balance [37]. The estimation of propensity scores, computation of IPTW, and balance checks were conducted separately for the six-week, six-month, and one-year postpartum periods.

Separate logistic regression models were used to estimate the effect of pregnancy intention on postpartum contraceptive use at six weeks, six months, and one year postpartum, with the results expressed as odds ratios (OR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Survey weights were incorporated in all analyses to account for unequal sample allocation across different regions and for non-response. Because attrition occurred between baseline and endline, lost-to-follow-up weights were estimated to adjust for differential loss to follow-up and to preserve the representativeness. The final analytic weight was constructed as the product of the baseline survey weight, the IPTW, and the lost-to-follow-up weights. Stata 18 was used for all analyses.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

Records of 2,517 women at six weeks postpartum, 2,409 at six months postpartum, and 2,089 at one year postpartum were included in the analysis. Of the study sample, 934 (37.1%), 891 (37%), and 767 (36.7%) had unintended pregnancies at six weeks, six months, and one year postpartum, respectively. Postpartum contraceptive use

The proportion of women using postpartum modern contraceptives was 402 (16%) at six weeks, 865 (35.9%) at six months, and 832 (39.8%) at one year. Across all data collection points, most women reported using injectable contraceptives, followed by implants, oral contraceptive pills, and LAM (Supplementary file 2). The proportion of postpartum modern contraceptive use was relatively higher among women who had intended pregnancies. Higher postpartum modern contraceptive use was also observed among women aged 25–34 years, with primary education, a parity of 1–2, and previous family planning use, across all data collection points. Covariate balance

Covariate balance, measured using standardised mean differences for baseline covariates, was evaluated to determine whether the exposed (women with intended pregnancies) and unexposed (women with unintended pregnancies) groups were comparable on measured confounders. The standardised differences of covariates were less than 0.1 (10%) after weighting adjustments at six weeks, six months, and one year postpartum. This demonstrates good balance and comparability between women with intended and unintended pregnancies (Table 3).

Effect of pregnancy intention on postpartum contraceptive use

In the IPTW model without incorporating lost to follow-up weights, women with intended pregnancies appeared less likely to use postpartum contraceptives at six months postpartum (OR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.63–0.91; p = 0.003) and at one year postpartum (OR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.57–0.85; p < 0.001), with no significant association observed at six weeks postpartum (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.72–1.15; p = 0.437). However, after incorporating lost-to-follow-up weights to adjust for differential loss to follow-up, the direction of the association changed, indicating that intended pregnancy was associated with higher odds of postpartum contraceptive use. Accordingly, women with intended pregnancies had 51% higher odds of using postpartum contraceptives at six weeks (OR = 1.51, 95%CI: 1.05–2.17), 29% higher odds at six months (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.06–1.57), and 49% higher odds at one year postpartum (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.13–1.96) compared with women with unintended pregnancies (Table 4).

Discussion

This study examined the impact of pregnancy intention on postpartum contraceptive use in Ethiopia using nationally representative longitudinal data, offering valuable evidence for the prevention of unintended pregnancies. The findings showed that less than one-sixth of all women used modern contraceptives at six weeks, one-third at six months, and nearly two-fifths at one year postpartum. This implies that most women in Ethiopia may be at risk of unintended pregnancy during the postpartum period and could experience shorter interpregnancy intervals. Additionally, the final IPTW analysis indicated that intended pregnancies were associated with higher odds of postpartum contraceptive use at six weeks, six months and one year postpartum. This implies that women with unintended pregnancies have a lower likelihood of using modern contraceptives, which may increase the risk of repeated unintended pregnancies. This finding aligns with previous studies, which have indicated that women who had intended pregnancies had greater chances of postpartum contraceptive use [11, 12]. While differences in study design and analytical methods, particularly the use of cross-sectional and retrospective data in previous studies, make direct comparisons challenging.

At six weeks postpartum, contraceptive use was low among both women with intended pregnancies and those with unintended pregnancies, although the likelihood was significantly higher among women whose pregnancies were intended. Many women at six weeks postpartum may still be in the recovery phase, adjusting to new responsibilities and prioritising infant care over contraception [38, 39]. Furthermore, cultural or social norms, such as the belief that it is too early to use contraceptives, especially during breastfeeding, when fertility is assumed to be low, can contribute to low contraceptive use [40, 41]. These factors, particularly for women with unintended pregnancies, may lead to missed opportunities for counselling and delayed initiation of contraceptive methods during the early postpartum period.

Women with intended pregnancies were more likely to use postpartum contraceptives at six weeks, six months, and one year postpartum, highlighting the important role of pregnancy intention in shaping reproductive behaviour. This pattern underscores the need for tailored family planning interventions that address the specific barriers faced by women with unintended pregnancies. Women who plan their pregnancies may be more motivated to prevent closely spaced or unintended subsequent pregnancies due to greater readiness for parenthood, clearer fertility intentions, and proactive engagement with family planning information and services [42, 43]. Moreover, women with intended pregnancies often demonstrate better engagement with maternal health services across the continuum of care, which facilitates access to postpartum contraceptive counselling and improves their knowledge of contraceptive options [44, 45]. Conversely, women with unintended pregnancies may limit their engagement with maternal and reproductive health services due to social stigma, fear of judgment, lack of partner and family support, or experiences of emotional distress [40, 46–48].

This study has important implications for understanding the influence of pregnancy intention on postpartum contraceptive use within the Ethiopian context. The findings highlight the need to strengthen existing systems within the maternal, newborn, and infant continuum of care to ensure that postpartum services adequately address women’s contraceptive needs through collaborative efforts of policymakers, healthcare providers, and other stakeholders [49]. Policymakers could work to address barriers that limit access to quality postpartum contraceptive services, ensuring that all women receive comprehensive care and support after pregnancy. To improve postpartum contraceptive use regardless of pregnancy intention, healthcare providers could play a crucial role in educating women about the potential risks of unintended pregnancies and the benefits of family planning, thereby empowering them to make informed reproductive decisions [50]. In addition, women with unintended pregnancies should be identified early during pregnancy and linked to antenatal care, skilled birth attendants, and postnatal care, where they can receive comprehensive family planning counselling [51]. Furthermore, healthcare providers should ensure equitable and proactive provision of postpartum family planning services for women with unintended pregnancies, given their heightened vulnerability to repeat unintended pregnancies and their lower likelihood of timely contraceptive uptake. All postpartum women should be given access to the contraceptive methods of their choice, ensuring that reproductive autonomy is upheld for every individual [52]. Moreover, educating women about the limitations of LAM, particularly after six months postpartum, and promoting access to highly effective yet underutilised long-acting contraceptives, such as intrauterine device, is essential.

The study is strengthened by the use of data from a nationally representative cohort survey, which tracked women for a year postpartum, providing valuable insights into contraceptive adoption patterns at six weeks, six months, and one year. Additionally, the study used a relatively robust analysis method to adjust for potential confounders using the propensity based IPTW approach, which helps to reduce bias by balancing the exposed and unexposed groups. However, the study still has limitations. Firstly, other potential covariates that may be associated with both the exposure and outcomes, such as partners’ fertility preferences and approval, exposure to family planning messages, relationship factors, community-level norms, and health facility factors, were not covered in the PMA Ethiopia survey. Secondly, although the study employed propensity score methods, residual bias and unmeasured confounding may persist, as these methods cannot account for unobserved variables. Variables not captured in the dataset could have affected both pregnancy intention and postpartum contraceptive use, highlighting the need for future studies to incorporate a broader set of potential confounders. Thirdly, since pregnancy intention was self-reported and assessed post-conception, women may have altered their intentions during pregnancy. This potential bias could lead to misclassification of pregnancy intention, potentially underestimating or overestimating its true association with the outcomes. To address this, future studies should attempt to assess women’s intention to become pregnant prospectively before conception and consider changes in intention during pregnancy and after childbirth. Finally, the actual occurrence of unintended pregnancies might be underestimated in this analysis, as it includes only pregnancies that resulted in live births and excludes those that were terminated or resulted in miscarriage.

Conclusion

Postpartum contraceptive use was low regardless of pregnancy intention, with intended pregnancy associated with higher odds of postpartum modern contraceptive use at different points in the postpartum period. Existing sexual and reproductive health services, including family planning, should be strengthened for all women, with targeted strategies such as early identification and linkage to care to address the specific needs of those experiencing unintended pregnancies. Furthermore, integrating postpartum contraceptive counselling within the maternal and child health continuum of care, along with social and behavioural change initiatives at the community level, can ensure that postpartum services fully address women’s contraceptive needs. Further research is needed to explicitly explore why women with unintended pregnancies have a lower likelihood of using postpartum modern contraceptives.
