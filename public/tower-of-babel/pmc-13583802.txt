Role of systemic hypoxia in the progression of diabetic retinopathy: a propensity-matched retrospective cohort study
Authors: Luqman Munir, Meltem Yashar, Kathlyn Kim, Ayan Vaishnav, Yannis M Paulus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to assess the association between systemic hypoxia and progression from non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) to proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) and vision-threatening outcomes.Methods and analysisThis retrospective cohort study used the TriNetX Network to identify adults with NPDR with no prior PDR. Systemic hypoxia was defined using International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD-10) codes for anaemia, obstructive sleep apnoea, chronic respiratory failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pulmonary hypertension or interstitial lung disease. Both cohorts were propensity score-matched. The primary outcome was progression to PDR. Secondary outcomes included vitreous haemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment, legal blindness, diabetic macular oedema and therapeutic interventions. Time-to-event analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards models, and 5-year cumulative incidence was assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis.ResultsAfter matching, 92 454 patients were included in each cohort with well-balanced baseline characteristics (all standardised mean differences <0.1). Systemic hypoxia was associated with increased risk of PDR progression (HR 1.24; 95% CI 1.18 to 1.31; p<0.001). 5-year cumulative PDR incidence was higher in the hypoxia cohort (4.2% vs 2.6%; risk ratio 1.63). Patients with systemic hypoxia also had higher risks of vitreous haemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment, blindness and increased utilisation of panretinal photocoagulation and pars plana vitrectomy. Subgroup analyses identified anaemia as the primary driver of PDR progression, whereas respiratory aetiologies were not independently associated after matching.ConclusionsSystemic hypoxia, a diagnosis-code-based composite in which anaemia-associated phenotypes showed the strongest association, is associated with accelerated PDR progression and near-doubled legal blindness risk. Anaemia screening may offer an opportunity to reduce vision-threatening complications in diabetic patients. These findings are hypothesis-generating and warrant prospective validation before informing screening pathways, given the retrospective design and absence of measured oxygen saturation, haemoglobin and glycaemic data.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

For research, these findings justify prospective studies that pair direct hypoxia biomarkers (oxygen saturation, arterial blood gas, haemoglobin, ferritin, erythropoietin) with retinopathy outcomes and randomised trials testing whether correcting anaemia or hypoxia slows progression to proliferative disease. Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a leading cause of preventable blindness among working-age adults with long-standing diabetes mellitus.1 2 DR can progress from non-proliferative DR (NPDR), characterised by retinal capillary leakage, retinal ischaemia and retinal haemorrhages, to proliferative DR (PDR), which involves retinal neovascularisation and risk of vitreous or preretinal haemorrhage.3–7 In diabetic patients, retinal ischaemia activates hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), which upregulate the expression of vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGF) and drive disease progression.8 9 It is important to understand risk factors which could predict progression from NPDR to PDR, since patients with PDR are at significant risk of vision loss.

Systemic conditions causing chronic tissue hypoxia, including anaemia, obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), chronic respiratory failure and pulmonary hypertension, may theoretically exacerbate retinal ischaemia by reducing oxygen delivery. Prior studies have suggested associations between individual hypoxic conditions and DR severity,10–13 but these investigations have been limited by small sample sizes, single-centre designs and examination of conditions in isolation rather than comparative assessment. Additionally, substantial clinical overlap between conditions such as OSA and anaemia complicates interpretation of independent effects.

To address these gaps, we investigated whether systemic hypoxia influences the progression of NPDR to PDR and related complications, using data from a large, multicentre electronic health record (EHR) dataset that enabled simultaneous evaluation of multiple hypoxic aetiologies and comparative assessment of their relative contributions.

Materials and methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX (Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA) global federated health research network, which aggregates EHR (ICD-10-CM diagnoses, Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes and demographics) from participating healthcare organisations. We queried the US Collaborative Network which currently includes 110 healthcare organisations. The final analysis was performed on 12 December 2025. TriNetX provides deidentified data in compliance with patient privacy laws and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act.

Patient and public involvement

This study used deidentified, retrospective EHR data; patients were not directly involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this research. No patient advisory input was sought for this study. Dissemination of findings to study participants is not applicable.

The study population included patients with NPDR (ICD-10: E11.32, E11.33, E11.34, E11.349). The index event was defined as the first recorded NPDR diagnosis. Patients with PDR (ICD-10: E11.35) on or before the index date were excluded (online supplemental file 1).

The primary exposure was systemic hypoxia, defined as a composite of diagnoses predisposing to chronic tissue hypoxia, including chronic respiratory failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, OSA, pulmonary hypertension, interstitial lung disease and anaemia (aplastic, haemolytic, nutritional, chronic disease related and unspecified) (online supplemental file 2). This exposure definition is a claims/EHR-coded comorbidity composite. Exposure was defined by the presence of at least one qualifying diagnosis recorded on or before the index date (baseline ascertainment) to ensure exposure preceded retinopathy progression and to mitigate immortal time bias. Controls were patients with NPDR and no recorded history of these hypoxic conditions prior to the index event.

To control for baseline confounding, we performed 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) using a greedy nearest-neighbour algorithm with a calliper of 0.1 SD. Covariates included age at index, race, sex and comorbidities (hypertension, heart failure, ischaemic heart disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), obesity and nicotine dependence). Balance was assessed using standardised mean differences, that <0.1 indicating adequate covariate balance (online supplemental file 1).

The primary outcome was progression from NPDR to PDR (ICD-10: E11.35). Secondary outcomes included vitreous haemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment, diabetic macular oedema, legal blindness, endophthalmitis and therapeutic interventions (panretinal photocoagulation (PRP), vitrectomy, cataract extraction or intravitreal or subtenon injections). Outcomes were defined as the first occurrence after the index date. For each secondary endpoint, patients with a documented history of that endpoint on or before index were excluded from the endpoint-specific analysis.

Patients were followed from index until outcome occurrence, last recorded encounter or end of data availability. Time-to-event associations were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models, reported as HRs with 95% CIs. Kaplan-Meier cumulative incidence was evaluated at 5 years. Corresponding risk ratios (RR) were calculated using 5-year cumulative incidence estimates and reported as descriptive measures. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted for anaemia, OSA, respiratory failure/lung disorders, pulmonary hypertension and hypoxemia using separate propensity-matched cohort comparisons. Secondary subgroup analyses further stratified the anaemia cohort by aetiology, including iron deficiency anaemia, vitamin B12 deficiency anaemia, folate deficiency anaemia, other nutritional anaemias, aplastic anaemia, sickle cell disease, thalassaemia, anaemia of chronic disease, other unspecified anaemias and CKD related anaemia (defined as patients with CKD stage ≥3). Anaemia subtype analyses were performed at a separate time point; therefore, total sample sizes may differ slightly from primary analyses due to the dynamic nature of the TriNetX federated network. Subgroups were mutually exclusive (except CKD stage ≥3). Analyses were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

In the propensity-matched aggregate analysis, systemic hypoxia was associated with a higher risk of progression to PDR. The hazard of developing PDR was 24% greater in the hypoxia group than in controls (HR 1.24; 95% CI 1.18 to 1.31; p<0.001). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed clear separation, with a lower probability of remaining PDR-free over 5 years in the hypoxia cohort (log-rank p<0.001). At 5 years, the cumulative risk was 4.2% in the systemic hypoxia compared with 2.6% in the control cohort (RR: 1.63; 95% CI 1.55 to 1.72).

Patients with systemic hypoxia had higher risks of vision-threatening complications and ophthalmic interventions compared with controls (figure 2). The hypoxia cohort demonstrated increased risks of legal blindness/vision loss, tractional retinal detachment, vitreous haemorrhage and endophthalmitis, as well as higher rates of therapeutic interventions, including panretinal photocoagulation, pars plana vitrectomy, cataract extraction and intravitreal injections. Secondary subgroup analyses by anaemia aetiology revealed differential associations with PDR progression (table 3). Chronic disease-related anaemias demonstrated significant associations, while nutritional deficiency anaemias showed no significance. Folate deficiency (n=64), sickle cell disease (n=173), acquired haemolytic anaemia (n=33), aplastic anaemia (n=120) and thalassaemia (n=105) had insufficient sample sizes for robust analysis.

Discussion

This large-scale, multicentre retrospective cohort study using real-world data from the TriNetX network demonstrates that patients with NPDR and concurrent diagnoses predisposing to systemic hypoxia are at a significantly heightened risk for progression to PDR and vision-threatening complications. Our propensity-matched analysis revealed a 24% increased hazard of PDR progression (HR 1.24) and 63% increased cumulative risk (RR 1.63) in the systemic hypoxia cohort compared with controls. More concerning clinically, patients with systemic hypoxia demonstrated a near-doubling of legal blindness risk (RR 2.06), alongside significantly elevated rates of vitreous haemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment and need for surgical interventions including panretinal photocoagulation and pars plana vitrectomy (all p<0.001).

Critically, subgroup stratification revealed that among systemic hypoxic conditions examined, anaemia showed the strongest association with PDR progression (4.2% vs 2.7%; RR 1.52, p<0.001), closely matching the aggregate effect. This represents an important advance over prior studies treating anaemia as a single exposure without evaluating subtype-specific risks.11 12 14 15 Our secondary analysis of anaemia subtypes demonstrates clinically meaningful heterogeneity with significant implications for understanding disease mechanisms and identifying high-risk populations, consistent with a broader literature examining modifiable systemic and nutritional exposures in DR risk.16 17

Anaemia of chronic disease showed the strongest association with PDR progression (RR 2.58, p<0.0001), more than doubling risk compared with controls and substantially exceeding the effect of iron deficiency anaemia (RR 1.16, p=0.0662). This differential effect suggests that chronic, inflammation-driven anaemias confer greater risk than simple reductions in oxygen-carrying capacity alone. Anaemia of chronic disease, characterised by elevated inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) and hepcidin-mediated iron dysregulation,18 may exacerbate DR through multiple synergistic pathways: (1) sustained impairment in retinal oxygen delivery in the setting of already-compromised diabetic microangiopathy,14 (2) systemic endothelial dysfunction amplifying retinal vascular permeability14 19 and (3) chronic inflammatory states independently associated with VEGF upregulation.20 The chronic inflammatory state of diabetes further contributes to retinal injury through mechanisms including oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction.21 In contrast, nutritional deficiency anaemias showed no significant associations, suggesting that easily correctable, non-inflammatory anaemias may not substantially modify PDR risk.

The strong independent association between CKD (≥stage 3) and PDR progression (RR 1.52, p<0.0001) further supports the importance of chronic, multifactorial anaemia. CKD stage 3 and over has reduced erythropoietin production combined with uraemic inhibition of erythropoiesis and chronic inflammation,22 creating a sustained hypoxic and inflammatory milieu that may accelerate ischaemia-driven retinal neovascularisation. The comparable effect sizes between CKD-associated anaemia (RR 1.52) and the overall anaemia cohort (RR 1.52) suggest overlapping patient populations and shared pathophysiology.

These subtype-specific findings have important implications for risk stratification and for designing prospective studies. Rather than screening for anaemia indiscriminately, targeted evaluation of patients with chronic inflammatory conditions, CKD or unexplained anaemia refractory to nutritional supplementation may identify those at highest risk for accelerated PDR progression. The minimal association with iron deficiency anaemia suggests that routine iron supplementation in well-nourished diabetic patients may be unlikely to modify retinopathy outcomes, whereas addressing underlying chronic disease states may prove more beneficial, though this remains a hypothesis to be tested prospectively rather than an immediate practice recommendation.

By contrast, after stringent propensity matching, respiratory-related etiologies did not demonstrate significant excess risk: respiratory failure/lung disorders 2.2% vs 2.2% (p=0.746), OSA 2.3% vs 2.4% (p=0.460), pulmonary hypertension 3.0% vs 2.3% (p=0.311) and hypoxaemia 2.9% vs 2.5% (p=0.539). These null findings likely reflect substantial clinical overlap between conditions, particularly OSA and anaemia, rather than definitive absence of biological effect. OSA-induced intermittent hypoxaemia can contribute to anaemia through inflammatory pathways and erythropoietin dysregulation,10 23 while concurrent anaemia amplifies hypoxic burden. In our cohort, statistical models controlling for one condition necessarily exclude or adjust for patients with both diagnoses, limiting definitive separation of independent versus synergistic effects. Additionally, certain subgroups had insufficient sample sizes (obesity hypoventilation: n=65; cerebrovascular disease: n=33) to detect associations. Our data cannot definitively exclude clinically meaningful effects of respiratory conditions on PDR progression.

Our findings advance prior single-condition studies by simultaneously evaluating multiple systemic hypoxic conditions within a larger and multicentred cohort. Rahimy et al reported OSA’s association with elevated PDR risk (RR approximately 1.3) in 25 000 patients,24 while Li et al demonstrated dose-dependent anaemia effects (HR 1.29 to 1.43) in 70 000 patients using claims data.11 Our study’s 184 908 propensity-matched patients across more than 100 healthcare organisations enabled direct comparative assessment, identifying anaemia, particularly chronic, inflammatory subtype, as the primary clinically actionable risk factor while generating hypotheses about other hypoxic conditions that require prospective investigation.

Mechanistically, retinal ischaemia in NPDR stabilises HIF-1α, triggering VEGF upregulation and neovascularisation.19 25 Systemic hypoxic conditions theoretically amplify this pathway by reducing tissue-level oxygen delivery.26 Anaemia directly diminishes blood oxygen-carrying capacity11 24; in diabetic microangiopathy with already-compromised perfusion, this exacerbates retinal oxygen deficits and may accelerate ischaemic damage.14 The substantially greater effect of inflammatory anaemias compared with nutritional deficiencies suggests that chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, rather than haemoglobin levels alone, may be the critical modifiers of this pathway. The clinical efficacy of anti-VEGF therapy in PDR underscores the centrality of the hypoxia-VEGF axis, suggesting that patients with concurrent systemic hypoxic conditions may reach pathological neovascularisation thresholds more rapidly.14 19

While our observational study cannot establish causality, the subtype-specific differential effects (chronic disease anaemia RR 2.58 vs nutritional deficiency NS) combined with dose-dependent associations in prior literature11 suggest that chronic inflammatory anaemia warrants investigation as a potential therapeutic target in DR care beyond traditional glycaemic and blood pressure control.27 Randomised controlled trials are needed to determine whether anaemia correction influences PDR progression.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgement. First, the retrospective observational design precludes causal inference. Systemic hypoxia may not be solely responsible for DR progression, as unmeasured confounders such as overall health status or healthcare utilisation may also contribute. Second, reliance on ICD-10 coding introduces potential for misclassification, although key endpoints (PDR, vitrectomy, PRP) are specific billable procedures likely reliably documented. Patients with systemic hypoxia may also have more frequent healthcare contact and ophthalmological surveillance than controls, which could increase ascertainment of retinopathy progression and secondary outcomes independent of any true biological effect (surveillance bias). Third, variable follow-up duration and potential loss to follow-up, particularly if patients received care outside the network, may have introduced attrition bias.28 Fourth, we lacked granular data on disease severity (haemoglobin levels, oxygen saturation) and treatment status (continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) adherence, anaemia management), which may modify hypoxic burden and risk. We also lacked HbA1c, diabetes duration, anti-VEGF treatment history, optical coherence tomography findings and standardised retinal grading data, all of which influence DR progression and could confound or modify the associations reported here. Finally, small sample sizes in several subgroup categories (in some instances as few as 33 patients) limited statistical power to identify true independent effects. Despite these limitations, this large, multicentre real-world dataset enhances generalisability. Overall, systemic hypoxia was associated with faster NPDR progression and greater vision-threatening morbidity, findings that are biologically plausible and consistent with prior literature.

Prospective studies are needed to address key questions raised by our findings. Randomised controlled trials could evaluate whether anaemia correction influences PDR progression, incorporating serial haemoglobin and inflammatory biomarker measurements alongside retinal imaging to assess dose-response relationships. Prospective cohorts with detailed phenotyping (including sleep study parameters and longitudinal haemoglobin data) may help clarify the relative contributions of overlapping hypoxic conditions that could not be definitively separated in our retrospective analysis. Studies examining feasibility and cost-effectiveness of anaemia screening integration into DR care would inform potential clinical implementation. Further, research with granular physiological data is required to determine whether observed associations reflect causal relationships amenable to therapeutic intervention.

Conclusions

This multicentre analysis of propensity-matched patients demonstrates that systemic hypoxia an ICD-10 diagnosis-code-based comorbidity composite, with chronic inflammatory anaemia-associated phenotypes showing the strongest association is independently associated with accelerated PDR progression and near-doubling of legal blindness risk. Chronic disease-related anaemias (RR 2.58) showed substantially greater risk than nutritional deficiencies, suggesting that inflammation and chronicity, rather than haemoglobin levels alone, are critical disease modifiers, though these anaemia-aetiology subgroup findings were exploratory and not adjusted for multiple comparisons. These findings support prospective investigation of anaemia correction as a potential therapeutic target in DR care.
