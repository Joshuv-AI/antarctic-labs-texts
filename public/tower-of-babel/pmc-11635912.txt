In‐situ Reconstruction of Catalyst in Electrocatalysis
Authors: Jinxian Feng, Xuesen Wang, Hui Pan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractReconstruction of catalysts is now well recognized as a common phenomenon in electrocatalysis. As the reconstructed structure may promote or hamper the electrochemical performance, how to achieve the designed active surface for highly enhanced catalytic activity through the reconstruction needs to be carefully investigated. In this review, the genesis and electrochemical effects of reconstruction in various electrochemical catalytic processes, such as hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), oxygen evolution reaction (OER), carbon dioxide reduction reaction (CO2RR), and nitrate reduction reaction (NO3RR) are first described. Then, the strategies for optimizing the reconstruction, such as valence states control, active phase retention, phase evolution engineering, and surface poisoning prevention are comprehensively discussed. Finally, the general rules of reconstruction optimization are summarized and give perspectives for future study. It is believed that the review shall provide deep insights into electrocatalytic mechanisms and guide the design of pre‐catalysts with highly improved activity.

Introduction

The extreme use of fossil fuels makes the energy depletion and carbon dioxide (CO2) over emission, leading to serious energy and environmental crises.[ 1 ] Electricity from sustainable energy like wind, solar and tide has been a promising alternative.[ 2 ] Unfortunately, sustainable electricity is unstable, therefore hampering its wide applications.[ 2 ] Using chemical energy carriers to store the electricity and convert them back to electricity when needed, is suitable for improving the flexibility of sustainable powers.[ 3 ] The chemicals themselves can also be used as industrial feedstocks.[ 4 ] For example, hydrogen produced by using electricity from sustainable energies (called “green hydrogen”) is an efficient energy carrier. Water electrolysis driven by sustainable power sources is one of the important ways to produce high‐purity green hydrogen.[ 5 ] Carbon‐contained molecules like CO, HCOO−, CH4, CH3OH, C2H4, and high alcohols, produced from sustainable power sources through electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR)[ 6 ] are efficient energy carriers too.[ 7 ] Green ammonia production from the nitrate electrochemical reduction reaction (NO3RR) has been attracting extensive attention recently.[ 8 ] In addition, NO3RR is an efficient way to convert harmful nitrate into value‐added ammonia, which can be used in wastewater treatment.[ 9 ] In all the electrocatalytic reactions, the catalysts play critical roles in the performances, such as efficiency and stability. For example, the cost of the green hydrogen is still too high to be applied on a large scale because of the sluggish hydrogen evolution (HER) and oxygen evolution reactions (OER) in the water electrolysis,[ 10 ] due to the poor performance of electrocatalysts.[ 11 ] Although noble metals like Ru, Ir, and Pt, alloys and compounds, are highly efficient for the reactions,[ 5a ] the limited resources and high cost severely hinder their practical applications. The noble‐metal‐free elements like Ni, Co, Fe, and so on,[ 12 ] as well as their alloys and compounds, like oxides/hydroxides,[ 13 ] nitrides,[ 14 ] sulfides[ 15 ] and phosphides,[ 16 ] have been extensively investigated as alternatives due to their low‐cost and abundance. Similarly, CO2RR and NO3RR are facing low activity, selectively, and stability because of the same reasons.[ 17 ] Therefore, developing high‐efficient electrocatalysts is urgently needed for practical applications.

Based on experimental and theoretical studies, rational design of elemental composition and crystal structure for electrocatalysts has been regarded as an efficient way and a hallmark in fabrication. However, the interactions among the electrocatalysts and reactants, intermediates, products or electrolytes in reaction lead to surface/structure reconstruction, making the electrocatalytic performances deviated from the expectation.[ 2 , 18 ] The reconstruction may create new active sites, promoting the electrocatalytic process, or destroy the pre‐designed active site structures, resulting in reduced performance. Up to now, although various reconstructions have been reported, a systematical review of the strategies of designed surface/phase reconstruction for enhanced catalytic properties is unavailable.

In this review, we first introduce the genesis and electrochemical effects of typical reconstruction on the products in OER, HER, CO2RR, and NO3RR. Then we investigate the reconstruction optimization strategies, including valence state control, detachment hindrance, structure‐evolution engineering, and surface‐poisoning prevention, for achieving the robustness of the electrochemical performance. The perspectives on engineering the reconstruction for high catalytic performance are given at the end.

The Characteristics of Reconstruction

The materials before the electrocatalytic process are called pre‐catalysts. The reconstruction is highly relied on pre‐catalyst’ structure and composition, the electrolyte used, additive, temperature, applied potential/current density, and reaction time. Here we first discuss the factors, then the types of reconstructed structures and their effects on the electrocatalysis.

Factors of Reconstruction

The reconstruction happens at the solid‐electrolyte interface, which may penetrate inside of pre‐catalyst over time, leading to phase/structure change. Generally, the reconstruction rate is fast at the activation stage, then almost keep unchanged in the stable operation period. In the electrocatalytic condition, both the physical and chemical driving forces take part in the reconstruction (Figure 1 ). The physical driving forces mainly come from: i) gas bubble generation/breaking, ii) electrolyte convection, and iii) random atom immigration.[ 19 ] Physical driving forces lead to cracks, detachment of the catalytic layer, and change of the particle size,[ 20 ] while the chemical composition keeps unchanged. Therefore, the induced reconstruction results in the collapse of electrocatalyst and shall be prevented. The reconstruction introduced by the chemical driving force results in the change of valence state, leaching/redeposition, ion‐exchanging, and new phase. The typical chemical driving forces include: i) oxidation/reduction; ii) chemical adsorption/desorption and intercalation/de‐intercalation of ions/molecules; and iii) combination, hydrolysis, dissociation, and double decomposition of pre‐catalyst.[ 21 ] The influence of in‐situ reconstruction driven by chemical driving force on the electrochemical performance is complex. On the one hand, the reconstructed structures could provide open structures or create new active sites for electrocatalysis. On the other hand, the reconstructed structure may not be the active phase, which hampers the electrocatalytic reactions.

Reconstructed Structures of Pre‐Catalysts

The metals, alloys, oxide/hydroxides, oxygenated salts, metal–organic compounds (including metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), covalence organic frameworks (COFs), and metal–organic coordination compounds (MOCs)), nitrides (MNs), sulfides (MSs), and phosphides (MPs), as well as non‐metal materials, like carbon (graphite, amorphous carbon, graphene, carbon nanotubes, fullerenes) and black phosphorus (BP), have been used as electrocatalysts. All of them proceed with the reconstruction more or less, resulting in new valence states, reorganized crystal phases/shapes, and surface coordination compounds.

Oxides/Hydroxides

In OER, the metals convert into their oxides or hydroxides, and the metal ions are oxidized to high valence metal states (e. g. Ni4+, Fe4+) or peroxides at high potential.[ 22 ] The MNs, MSs, MPs, oxygenate salts and metal coordinate compounds undergo anion/ligands leaching and convert to oxide/hydroxides too. In HER, CO2RR, and NO3RR, the oxide or hydroxide may also be the active phase, but most of them are only keep stable at low overpotential.[ 23 ] The oxide/hydroxides derived from different pre‐catalysts and under various electrocatalytic conditions have special fine microstructures for the construction of active sites.[ 24 ]

Metallic Phases and Low‐Valence Compositions

Most of the transition metal oxides and hydroxides convert to single substrates in HER and CO2RR. Ti, V, and rare‐earth elements may convert to low‐valence states in their water‐stable compounds. The MNs, MSs, MPs, oxygenate salts and metal coordinate compounds may also convert to their low‐valence counterparts with the reduction of metallic ions.[ 18b ]

Reorganized Condense State

The shape and the crystallinity of pre‐catalyst may change in electrocatalysis, including: i) Morphology and size changing. For example, the small particles with high surface energy aggregate and convert to large ones, leading to reduced active sites. However, the particle sizes may also become smaller. Meanwhile, edges and high curvature sites may generate; ii) crystal phase evolution, which contains crystallinity change and element composition redistribution and high curvature sites. Typically, crystal lattice/planes arrangement, amorphization/re‐crystallization, and phase segregation are common in crystal phase evolution.[ 25 ]

Surface Decoration Species

The leached species of pre‐catalyst may adsorb on the reconstructed surface and become surface decoration species.[ 26 ] They combine with the metal/non‐metal ions, or the sites with high surface energy, like defect, corner/edge, and interface, that affecting the electronic state and take influence the electrocatalytic performance.

Surface Compounds

The OH−, CO3 2− or HCO3 − in the electrolyte, as well as the intermediates like adsorbed OH− (OHad), adsorbed H atoms (Hads) in cathodic reactions, CO in CO2RR and NOx in NO3RR, may bind with active sites on surface, generating surface compounds, such as coordinated hydroxyl coordinates (M‐OHx), carboxyl coordinates (M‐CO), and coordinated nitrogen‐oxygen compounds (M‐NOx). Specially, the Hads may generate coordinate compounds (M‐Hads) with the electrocatalytic sites with strong electrophilic characters, or penetrate in the crystal to form hydride (MHx) or metal‐hydrogen solid solution, like the Pd and Ti with high hydrogen/proton affinity.[ 27 ] In CO2RR, carbonates (M(OH)x(CO3)y, M = metal ions) may also generate, which may poison the active site, and provide feedstock for electrocatalysis to some extent.

Effects of In‐situ Reconstructed Structures

In‐situ Generated Metals/Alloys

Metals are the most stable species in HER, CO2RR or NO3RR. The reconstructed metals often show enlarged electrochemical active surface areas, unique surface decorations, abundant defects, and unsaturated coordinated sites, which may result in improved electrochemical performance. For example, Sang et al.[ 28 ] found that the P2O7 2− of Cu2P2O7 was leached, and the Cu2+ converted to highly‐active Cu(0) with unsaturated coordination sites in CO2RR (Figure 2a–e). Yao et al.[ 29 ] found that the Bi‐MOF proceeded multi‐step electrolyte‐mediated dissociation and conversion to Bi, which exhibited high activity, selectivity, and stability for the reduction of CO2 to HCOOH.

Some of the metal compounds may not be reduced to single substrates completely. The electronic interactions between reconstructed metal and unchanged counterpart shall benefit for HER, CO2RR or NO3RR. For example, Tian et al.[ 23 ] found that Ni/NiFe‐LDO was partially reduced to Ni0, and the electronic interaction between Ni0 and NiFe‐LDO was attributed to high HER activity and stability. Yuan et al.[ 30 ] found that in‐situ reduction of Co carbonate hydroxide/TiO2 hybrid generated Co0‐embedded TiO2 matrix, which was energetically favorable to HER. Pardo Pérez et al.[ 31 ] found that the SnInOx converted to Sn1‐xInx@In1‐ySnyOz core–shell hybrid structure in CO2RR, which played important roles in the CO2‐to‐HCOOH conversion. Zhou et al.[ 32 ] found that Cu(OH)2 on the cathode surface was partially reconstructed into Cu/Cu2O, where the electronic interaction between Cu and Cu2O resulted in high NO3RR performance. Ren et al.[ 33 ] found that Pd‐incorporated Cu‐based MOF (CuPd‐MOF) was in‐situ reconstructed to Cu/Pd/CuOx multi‐phase heterostructures, which triggered uneven spatial charge distribution for enhanced interface polarization and resulted in electron transfer from Pd to Cu (Figure 2f,g). So, the NO3 − adsorption on electron‐deficient Pd sites and further reduction on electron‐rich Cu sites were promoted, delivering good NO3RR performance. Yang et al.[ 34 ] found that metallic Bi° clusters and Ov were in‐situ generated in the BiFeO3, which promoted the conversion of *NO2 to *NO and enhanced the *H supply for the NO3 − hydrogenation, thus boosting NO3RR.

The non‐metal counterparts in pre‐catalysts may decorate on the reconstructed metal surface and take part in the active‐site construction. Fan et al.[ 35 ] found the S‐incorporated Co oxide underwent S leaching, followed by in‐situ Co ions reduction and S redeposited in HER, creating metallic Co scaffold and S‐coordinated Co𝜹+. The reconstructed structures improved the water dissociation kinetics and optimized the Hads adsorption, thus promoting HER consequently.

Generally, the metal phases are stable in HER, CO2RR, and NO3RR. The metal oxides and hydroxides, oxygenate salts and the coordination compounds may convert to metallic phases completely or partially. The in‐situ reconstructed structure with metals and oxides / hydroxide may promote the catalytic reaction greatly due to the electronic interaction among the multiple phases, as well as the optimized surface electronic structures. The reconstructed structures, such as crystallinity, surface decoration, defect, and phase, highly depend on the pre‐catalyst's structure and composition, and the electrocatalytic condition.

In‐situ Generated Oxides/Hydroxides

The transition metal oxides or hydroxides are stable in alkali OER. However, their metal ions may be further oxidized. Yao et al.[ 36 ] found that the edges of β‐Ni(OH)2 were reconstructed to form nanoporous Ni1−xO, resulting in Ni3+ species as the OER active sites. The surface decoration, distortion, vacancy and incorporation of the oxides/hydroxides derived from different pre‐catalysts are varied, endowing different OER performances. For example, Zuo et al.[ 24a ] found that the Ni‐Fe hydroxide with defects reconstructed from Ni‐Fe selenides showed higher OER performance than those from Ni‐Fe oxide and sulfide. Zhao et al.[ 37 ] found that high‐valent Co4+ species were obtained by using L‐CoS as a pre‐catalyst for alkaline OER, for which was more active than crystalline Co3O4 and Co LDH due to the special Co coordination by sulfur‐oxygen exchange in L‐CoS. Li et al.[ 24c ] discovered that glass–ceramic (Ni1.5Sn@triMPO4) transferred to Ni‐Fe oxyhydroxide in OER. The PO4 3− leaching generated oxygen vacancies (VO) on the surface, which optimized the electronic structure by regulating the d‐band center of Ni(Fe)OOH, leading to improved OER activity. The metal–organic compounds convert to hydroxide in OER with the organic coordinate leached. Yue et al.[ 24b ] found that the NDA group eliminated and the NiCoFe‐NDA (NDA = 2,6‐naphthalenedicarboxylic acid) pre‐catalyst showed high OER performance in the activation process, which was attributed to in‐situ formed NiCoFe hydroxide with inherited coordination structure.

Oxide or hydroxide may also be the active phases for HER, CO2RR, and NO3RR, but they are usually present together with their reduced single substrates or low‐valence compounds. He et al.[ 38 ] found that the dissolution of V in the amorphous V‐incorporated CoO led to the structurally rearranged CoO nanosheet for HER. Wang et al.[ 39 ] found that the C2O4 2− in CoC2O4@MXene leached and Co(OH)2@MXene was formed for HER. Feng et al.[ 40 ] fabricated highly active Ag/Cu/Cu2O heterostructure by in‐situ electrochemically reconstruction from Ag‐incorporated HKUST‐1, providing tandem active sites for the CO2‐to‐C2H4 conversion. Our group[ 41 ] also found that the spinel Co3‐xNixO4 reconstructed to cobalt‐nickel hydroxides (CoyNi1‐y(OH)2) as the active phase in the NO3RR.

Clearly, the metal oxide and hydroxide are active phases of OER. Especially, the Ru and Ir‐based oxides are stable in a wide pH range. For the non‐noble transition metals, their oxides and hydroxides are indissoluble in non‐acidic OER and NO3RR. The metals, oxygenate salts and the coordination compounds convert to metal oxide or hydroxide completely in OER. In the HER and CO2RR, the oxide or hydroxide may also be the active phases. However, they always be reduced partially and convert to metal phase completely at high overpotential. Similarly, the in‐situ reconstruction is highly dependent on the structure and composition of pre‐catalyst and the reaction condition.

Reorganized Crystal Structure

Crystal structure reorganization leads to changes of composition, crystal phase, morphology, and crystal size. The change of the element ratio in pre‐catalyst results in phase transition. Leaching leads to element redistribution, which may introduce open structures like pores and fissures, which expose more active sites and benefit from charge transfer and mass diffusion. For example, Pascuzzi et al.[ 42 ] found that the Mn species of MnTiRu oxide were leached out as Mn2+ (potential < 1.7 V) and MnO4 − (potential > 1.7 V), leading to high porosity, which benefited for acidic OER. Bo et al.[ 43 ] found that some of Cr species were oxidized into +6 valence state and leached out of NiFeCr hydroxide. As a result, the Fe‐sub‐β‐NiOOH with nanomesh‐like structure was formed, which could provide abundant active sites and promote OER. Element segregation leads to ununiformly distribution of element, which may accumulate the electrocatalytic‐active element on the surface, and then enhance electrocatalytic performance. Medhi et al.[ 44 ] found that the Fe accumulated on the surface layer that forms Fe‐enriched Ni hydroxide, offered low overpotential and high stability for OER. Pan et al.[ 45 ] found that the CoNiPt alloy converted to Pt‐enriched alloy on the surface and Co‐enriched cores in HER, promoting the water dissociation, facilitating the hydrogen adsorption, and accelerating alkaline HER kinetics synergistically. The element segregation introduces vacancy in the reduction condition, which becomes the active site for electrocatalysis, too. For example, nitrogen vacancies can be formed in NO3RR and work as active sites. Wei et al.[ 46 ] found that nitrogen vacancies could be formed in the potential‐driven reconstruction of Cu3N, which were active for the NO3 − adsorption, hydrogenation, and reduction.

Phase changing introduces the transform and amorphization of the crystal lattice. For example, the γ‐phase (interlayer distance of 7.2 Å) and α‐phase (interlayer distance of 7.8 Å) of NiFe LDH can transform from one to another, introducing different active sites for OER.[ 47 ] In‐situ micro imagination techniques showed that the edge and corner sites of the Co hydroxide changed at different potentials in the OER, which may provide different active sites for electrocatalysis.[ 48 ] The crystal phase reorganization of sulfide produces compounds with different metal/S ratios. Sun et al.[ 18b ] found that multivalent pyrite‐type nickel‐based sulfides (Fe‐incorporated or Fe‐free NiS2, α‐NiS, β‐NiS, and Ni3S4) underwent dynamic reconstruction propagating from surface to bulk in HER as mediated by the accumulation and diffusion of S vacancies, which resulted in Ni3S2 phase eventually (Figure 3c). In some cases, incompletely phase changes introduce hetero interfaces. Ding et al.[ 49 ] also found that NiS proceeded an in‐situ phase transition to an intimately mixed phase of Ni3S2 + NiO with abundant interfaces, leading to high HER (Figure 3d,e). The incorporated LDH may undergo phase segregation in OER and generate interface for electrocatalysis too. For example, Liu et al.[ 50 ] found that the Ce‐incorporated Ni‐Fe LDH converted to β/γ‐NiOOH phase interfaced with Ce(OH)3 in OER. The strong electron transfer between the Ce(OH)3 and β/γ‐NiOOH led to high catalytic activity (Figure 3d–h).

The intense interactions at the solid–liquid–gas interface distort the crystal lattice of pre‐catalyst, leading to amorphization. The amorphous phase itself can be the active phase for electrocatalysis. Fu et al.[ 51 ] found that the amorphization at the edge of the trace‐Ru (0.81 wt.%)‐incorporated NiPS3 nanosheets stabilized the Ru species through in‐situ formed amorphous layer with bridging S2 2− species, which could optimize the electronic state and achieve superior HER activity. Additionally, the partial amorphization creates abundant hetero‐interfaces for electrocatalysis. Zhao et al.[ 52 ] discovered that amorphous In‐hydroxide gel reconstructed to amorphous In‐OH species with crystalline In, resulting in amorphous/crystalline interface, which optimized the *HCOO generation and enhanced the CO2‐to‐HCOOH.

The sizes of electrocatalysts may become large (Ostwald ripening), which reduces the expose of active sites and hampers the electrocatalysis. On the contrary, the size of electrocatalysts may become small, even leading to atomic‐level dispersion. For example, Cu atoms were formed in the oxidation‐reduction of CO2RR, introducing empty 3d orbital and generating unsaturated single‐atomic sites,[ 53 ] which was the active phase to stabilize reaction intermediates on the surface and enhance the CO2RR electrocatalysis.

We see that the segregation of elements and rearrangement of crystallinity may happen in electrocatalysis. Usually, they are accompanied by the variation of chemical valence state. However, the reconstruction of crystal structure is a double‐blade sword: introducing new electrocatalytic sites or reducing the pre‐designed active sites/destroying the structure. There, the pre‐catalyst needs to be carefully designed to achieve the targeted reconstruction for high catalytic performance.

In‐situ Surface Decoration

The leached species can re‐deposit on to surface as in‐situ surface decoration species, including ions and small molecules, which adhere on the surface and keep stable in electrocatalysis. For example, the leached non‐metal oxygenate salt anions can be re‐adsorbed in OER. Shi et al.[ 54 ] also found that the Se in NiSe2 converted to SeO3 2− then to SeO4 2−. They accumulated on the surface in the OER and took part in the construction of electrocatalytic sites. Bao et al.[ 55 ] found that S and Te leached out from the CoTe@CoS heterojunction and formed soluble TeO3 2− and SO3 2−. They re‐adsorbed on reconstructed Co hydroxide, then converted into TeO4 2− and SO4 2−, improved the OER performance due to the synergistic effect together with the hydroxide (Figure 4a–c).

The leached ions can adhere to the cathode and take part in the surface decoration for HER. Our group[ 56 ] found that the Cr in multi‐metal oxides FeCoCrCuOx could be leached and re‐deposited as the Cr–OH. The synergistic effect of multiple metals and Cr–OH enhanced the proton concentration and promoted the HER kinetics. Du et al.[ 11b ] found that the Mo in the Ni4Mo alloy was dissolved in the form of MoO4 2− and then re‐adsorbed and polymerized to dimer Mo2O7 2− on the surface, so enhanced the HER activity and the stability in alkali (Figure 4d‐e). Moreover, the incorporated non‐metal heteroatom may convert to oxygenate ions that become surface decoration species in CO2RR. Wei et al.[ 57 ] found that the P of P‐incorporated In2O3 converted to (P‐O)δ− decorated on In (OD‐PIn), leading to a high In‐valence state. The OD‐PIn endowed strong interaction with K+(H2O)n hydride ions and accelerated water dissociation, thus providing protons and promoting the HCOO* formation in the CO2‐to‐HCOOH process.

As we can see, the redeposited leached species and the non‐metal counterparts in the pre‐catalysts may convert to surface decoration species. The bulk crystal structures of the surface‐decorated materials have negligible different from those without decoration. However, the surface decoration species change the electronic states and influence the charge/mass transfer. Therefore, the electrochemical performance can be influenced.

Surface Coordination Compounds and Carbonates

The reactants and intermediates may combine on the specific atomic site and generate surface coordination compounds, such as M‐OHx, peroxide, M‐Hx, M‐Hads, M‐CO, carbonate, and M‐NOx. In OER, M‐OHx may provide an OH source and enhance the kinetics.[ 59 ] Peroxides may be generated at the high OER potential. Radinger et al.[ 60 ] found that the NiII(OH)2 converted to NiIIIOOH and generated peroxide NiIVOO− at high OER potential. In CO2RR, the M‐OHx and the carbonates may poison the active sites. Hydrogen solid solution, surface coordination hydrogen compounds (M‐Hads) and hydride formation in HER, CO2RR and NO3RR may also lead to surface poisoning. However, they may also stabilize the H intermediates and provide hydrogen for feedstocks. For example, Jiang et al.[ 27a ] found that TiH2 could be in‐situ formed by electrochemical hydrogenation of TiO2 in acidic HER, which accelerated charge transfer and improved H coverage, enhancing HER performance significantly (Figure 4f–h). Abdellah et al.[ 27b ] found that the Pd converted to PdHx, which provided hydrogen for the CO2‐to‐HCOOH conversion. Luo et al.[ 61 ] also mentioned that the Hads on Fe nitride provided active hydrogen for the hydrogenation‐deoxygenation process in the NO3RR. The M‐CO may block the active sites, but may stabilize the CO intermediate and benefit to the basic reactions, like C─C coupling and CHO generation. For example, Zhou et al.[ 58 ] found that Cu NNAs were better for the CO adsorption than Cu NNs by accumulated K+ at high curvature nanotips, which stabilized *CO and enhanced the C–C coupling for the C2+ products (Figure 4i‐p). In NO3RR, the NO3 − and the intermediate NO2 − may also accumulate on the surface and provide feedstock, but may block the active sites for undesired competitive HER.[ 62 ]

Therefore, the surface coordination compounds may generate when the reactants have unpaired electrons or empty orbital, which may block the active sites so bring the poisoning effect. However, rationally design the electrocatalytic environment can alleviate the poisoning effect and make the poisoning species become feedstocks for electrocatalysis.

Strategies for Reconstruction Optimization

As discussed before, the surface structure reconstruction in‐situ happens in the catalytic reaction, which may promote or limit the performance. Therefore, how to achieve the targeted reconstructions for improved catalytic activity needs to be carefully investigated. Up to now, a few strategies for reconstruction optimizations have been proposed, including: i) valence‐state control, ii) active‐specie retention, iii) crystal‐phase‐evolution engineering, and iv) surface‐poisoning prevention. Properly designing the structures of the pre‐catalysts, selecting the electrolyte, and tuning the electrocatalytic condition can control the reconstructed structures and achieve the goal of performance improvement accordingly.

Valence‐State Control

Oxidation‐State Optimization

Metals, alloys, and metal compounds are oxidized to oxides or hydroxides in OER. Introducing heterojunctions can reduce the potential for generating active compounds with high‐oxidation state for OER. For example, Lu et al.[ 63 ] constructed a 2D p–n heterojunction by exfoliating 2D Sn nanosheets with NiFe‐LDH through electrodeposition. The p–n heterojunction guided electron migration from Ni and Fe to Sn, charging the NiFe‐LDH positively and promoting the generation of highly active Fe/Ni3+δOOH, thereby improving the OER performance. Leaching can trigger the generation of highly active NiOOH for OER at low potential. Liu et al.[ 64 ] found that the structural evolution of V‐incorporated Ni3S2 to active Ni(OH)2 and subsequently to OER‐active NiOOH was triggered by V leaching at low OER potential. The introduction of V‐induced charge redistributions among the surrounding S atoms disrupted the symmetry within Ni3S2, facilitating this transformation. In contrast, Ni3S2 without V was unable to transform into active NiOOH at such low potentials, resulting in poor OER activity. Anion additions create new solid–liquid interfaces that trigger the generation of optimal active phases. Tang et al.[ 65 ] added SO4 2−, CO3 2−, and NO3 − to the KOH electrolyte for OER, breaking the ion balance in the Helmholtz layer and enhancing the OH− release, leading to the generation of a 3–5 nm thick OER‐active amorphous Ni(OH)2 layer on LaNiO3‐δ.

The high overpotential may make the metal ions over oxidized to soluble high‐valence forms. For example, the Ru‐based materials converted to soluble ortho‐valent Ru species (e.g., RuO4) in the OER, resulting in the loss of active sites. Reducing the over‐accumulated positive charge on the main electrocatalytic sites, or enhancing the chemical bonding are the keys to the over‐oxidation prevention. Incorporation can enhance the chemical bonding by electron donation‐acceptance, therefore preventing the over‐oxidation and leaching. Recently, Liu et al.[ 66 ] found that Nb5+‐incorporated RuO2 introduced the electrons transfer from Nb to Ru through the bridge oxygen, which suppressed the over‐oxidation and prevented the dissolution of Ru by strengthening the Ru─O bonds, thus improving the activity and stability in acidic OER. The sulfide may convert to hydroxide completely in the OER, leading to reduced conductivity and hampering the charge transfer kinetics. Incorporation could prevent the over‐oxidation of the sulfides and keep the hydroxide/sulfide interface by enhancing metal‐S bonding. Niu et al.[ 67 ] found that the Fe‐incorporation resisted the over‐oxidation of Ni3S2 nanowires, resulting in S‐incorporated NiFeOOH shell@Fe‐Ni3S2 core hybrid structure (Figure 5a–e), promoting the OER kinetics (Figure 5f,g).

Over‐oxidation and leaching of Ir in the acidic OER can be alleviated by hybridizing Ce oxide, which acts as an electron buffer by eliminating excess positive charge. Dong et al.[ 68 ] constructed an Ir‐Ce oxide solid solution for generating CeO2 to store the excess positive charge through multiple and changeable valence states of Ce in OER, facilitating the charge transfer toward Ir ions. Therefore, the valence of Ir was lowered and the over‐oxidation and dissolution were effectively prevented, delivering remarkable acidic OER activity and stability.[ 68 ]

Over‐Reduction Prevention

The interactions between the species in different valence states may promote the electrochemical performance. However, the over‐reduction makes the metal ions reduce to a metal single substrate completely. Directional electron transfer by superlattice and high‐valence‐state stabilization by incorporation, encapsulation/hybridization, halogen addition, and potential control are effective for over‐reduction prevention.

Superlattice can lead to directional electron transfer and prevent the over‐reduction in CO2RR. Duan et al.[ 69 ] found that the active [Bi2O2]2+ sub‐layers in BiCuSeO superlattices retained the Bi oxidation states rather than self‐reduction in CO2RR because the rapid and selectively electron transfer through the conductive [Cu2Se2]2− sublayer prevented the Bi in BiCuSeO from reducing to metallic Bi. The Bi with high valence promoted the OCHO* generation, thus achieving a FE > 90% for the CO2‐to‐HCOOH conversion in a wide potential range (Figure 6a,b).

Inert‐oxide‐shell encapsulation can isolate the strong reductive intermediates for protecting the active Cu+ species inside. For example, Albertini et al.[ 70 ] used Al oxide (AlOx)‐organic hybrid composition as a protective layer, which could prevent the Cu2+‐to‐Cu° conversion, endowing excellent CO2RR performance. Hybridization can also prevent the Cu+ reduction by electronic interactions between matrixes. Yuan et al.[ 71 ] fabricated CuO particles on copper phyllosilicate lamella (CuSiO3) (CuO/CuSiO3). In the CO2RR process, part of Cu2+ in CuO/CuSiO3 was reduced to Cu0 and Cu+ ions, while the electron interactions between Cu/Cu+ and CuSiO3 made the Cu+ positively charged, therefore suppressing its complete reduction. The Cu0 promotes the CO2 activation, while Cu+ enhances the *CO adsorption, thus enhancing the kinetics of C─C coupling (Figure 6c–e). Al‐incorporation introduced amorphous Al oxide in CO2RR, which enhanced the stability of Cu+. Cheng et al.[ 72 ] found that incorporated Al in Cu oxide converted to a stable amorphous Al2O3 phase in CO2RR. The wide orbitals of amorphous Al2O3 provided lower energy levels for additional electrons, which could retain Cu+ and avoid the Cu formation, thereby promoting the formation of C─C bonds and improving the generation of C2+ products significantly.

The A‐O‐B (A, B = metal or non‐metal) sites in the oxides are active for CO2RR. However, metal ions in A and B sites may be reduced, leading to structure collapse.[ 73 ] Introducing Si in the B site and construct metal–O–Si asymmetry structure can prevent the structure from collapsing. Tan et al.[ 74 ] synthesized CuSiOx amorphous nanotube with abundant atomic Cu–O–Si sites. The strong electronic interactions between Cu and Si prevented the Cu from complete reduction. Additionally, the Si isolated the Cu sites and prevented the undesired C–C coupling, leading to high CO2‐to‐CH4 selectivity and stability (Figure 6f–h).

The halogen (X, X = Cl, Br, and I) in the electrolyte generates Cu(I) halogenide on the surface, and creates the Cu→CuX→Cu redox loop, which stabilizes the CO2RR‐active Cu+.[ 75 ] Therefore, the adsorption of *CO intermediate was enhanced, and C─C coupling was promoted, endowing high FE C2+ products in acidic CO2RR (Figure 6i–j). The leached halogen can play role as surface decoration species that suppress the complete reduction of Cuδ+. Yang et al.[ 78 ] unraveled that in‐situ I− leaching from AgI of AgI/CuO pre‐catalyst restrained the entire reduction of CuO to acquire stable active Cu0/Cu+ species for the C–C coupling in CO2RR. Besides, the residual I− on the Ag accumulated the electrons for accelerating the CO generation and spillover. The reconstructed tandem AgI‐Cu0/Cu+ bi‐functional site lowered the kinetic barrier of the C–C coupling, so enhanced the C2+ production.

Controlling the potential can trigger the surface state with different valence state and influence the intermediate adsorption, manipulating the products selectively. Wang et al.[ 76 ] found that the HCOOH/ethanol FE of CuO‐SnO2 was highly relative to the potential change due to different Cu/Sn valence states. The CuO/SnO2 converted to Cu2O/SnO2 at −0.85 V and benefited for the *OCOH adsorption, thereby endowed the CO2‐to‐HCOOH conversion (a FE of ≈54.81%). The CuO/SnO2 reconstructed to Cu/SnO2‐x at −1.05 V, showing an ethanol FE of 39.8%, and negligible HCOOH, because the reconstructed Cu/SnO2‐x was beneficial for the synergetic adsorptions of *COOH and *CHOCO intermediates and favored the C–C coupling for the ethanol generation (Figure 6i,j). Recently, Li et al.[ 79 ] reported that an Sb‐decorated Cu catalyst could selectively produce both CO and C2+ products in the CO2RR by tuning the potential. At a more positive potential, the oxygen species in Cu–Sb was favorable for the *COOH formation and reduced the adsorption of *CO, thereby promoting CO production. At a more negative potential, the oxygen in Cu‐Sb were removed completely, leading to enhanced CO‐adsorption and C–C coupling, resulting in an increased C2+ products. The potential can also affect the valence state of the reconstructed Cu surface, which affects the NO3RR selectively. Zhou et al.[ 77 ] found that the Cu2O pre‐catalyst reconstructed to oxide‐derived Cu0 (OD‐Cu) cube at high reduction potential, benefiting the NH3 generation. At low potential, only Cu2O was reduced partially and converted to Cu/Cu2O, favored the NO2 − generation kinetics (Figure 6m‐n).

Brief Summary

To overcome the kinetic barrier, the applied potential is always much higher than the thermodynamic potential, resulting in over‐oxidation or reduction. Therefore, the electronic structure of the reconstructed structure may deviate from the optimal state. The strategies for overcoming the over oxidation/reduction include: i) designing the electrocatalysts with robust crystal structure by heteroatom incorporation, ii) preventing the harsh electrocatalytic condition by encapsulation, iii) controlling the applied potential, and iv) optimizing the electron transfer pathways through hybrid‐structure construction.

Detachment Hindrance

The active species may be leached or detached in the electrocatalysis because of physical and chemical driving forces. Enhancing the binding between active phases and substrates, or isolate the active sites from the corrosive environments should be emphasized for the detachment of active species. The strategies include encapsulation, incorporation, alloying, and anchoring.

Encapsulation

Encapsulating the active species using a stable layer can prevent direct contact with the corrosive environments. Carbon is an ideal protective layer because of its high conductivity and chemical stability. Jeoung et al.[ 80 ] reported a graphene‐encapsulated NiMo alloy for realizing both high OER activity and stability. The Ce oxides can also be used as the protective layer. Obata et al.[ 81 ] introduced the CeOx layer on the NiFeOx, which selectively let OH− and O2 penetrate, endowing both high OER activity and stability (Figure 7a–e).

The Ru‐based oxide sheath can protect the active site and improve the activity. Chen et al.[ 82 ] found that the Ru‐based oxide nano‐sheath formed on the surface of Ru‐Fe nanoparticles on carbon felt (RuFe@CF) in OER, so suppressed the over‐oxidation of Ru species and inhibited the continuous metal dissolution (especially Fe) in acidic OER. Importantly, the in‐situ generated Ru‐Fe oxide itself had excellent electrochemical activity, and therefore balanced the activity and stability in the acidic OER. The “fence” layer can be the sheath of the active species, which not only improves the stability, but also enhances the activity. Huang et al.[ 83 ] found that the in‐situ generated CoS2 coating could be the unique “fence layer” on the external surface of highly active Co‐incorporated MoS2 (Co‐MoS2) in HER, which had high OH− adsorption ability and low water penetration kinetics barrier. Therefore, the OH− penetration was blocked and the MoS2 corrosion (MoS2 converts into MoO4 2− and SO3 2−) was inhibited, while the reactant H2O was favorable for passing through. All of those benefited for the HER kinetics synergistically.

Holes can be used as confinement, which can weaken the leaching by diffusion restriction. Additionally, its open side is beneficial to the pass‐through for reactants, intermediates, and products. Wang et al.[ 86 ] developed holy Co hydroxide with (10 ‐1 0101¯0) basal plane in the hole, whereas active Fe(OOH)1‐xClx species were strongly adsorbed. At the same time, the space‐confining effect slowed down the Fe diffusion from catalyst to electrolyte. As a result, high OER activity and stability were realized.

Incorporation/Alloying

Incorporation and alloying introduce heteroatoms with different electronegative, which may tune the chemical bonding and suppress the dissolution. Alkali‐ion incorporation introduces amorphous/crystalline domains, enhancing the bonding and improving the stability. Zhang et al.[ 84 ] found that the Na+‐incorporation introduced Ov‐enriched amorphous RuO2/crystalline RuO2 domains and optimized the Ru─O bonding, remarkably resisted the Ru leaching, leading to high OER performance under pH‐universal conditions (Figure 7f–h). Non‐meta‐atom incorporation can also tune the bonding states and prevent the excessive leaching of the active phases. Ping et al.[ 87 ] reported that the Si‐incorporation stabilized the lattice oxygen of RuO2, alleviating the leaching of highly active Ru sites, so endowed high activity and stability in acidic OER. Using the incorporated elements as a sacrificial reagent may generate anti‐corrosion compound on the surface, which stabilizes the active sites. Li et al.[ 88 ] found that the leaching of amphoteric V cation (V4+/V5+) from the bulk Fe‐incorporated Co2VO4 spinel oxide resulted in a highly active (Fe, V)‐incorporated CoOOH phase, and the remained V ions in the reconstructed CoOOH layer acted as the alkali‐resistant V2O3 phase for dynamic charge compensation, so prevented the excessive loss of lattice oxygen and Co dissolution, leading to high OER activity and stability.

The surface structure becomes more pronounced to structure‐dependent CO2RR, directly influencing catalytic performance. Cu has high surface mobility and is prone to either agglomeration or dissolution during the CO2RR process.[ 89 ] For example, Vavra et al.[ 90 ] found the Cu was easy to be dissolved in the electrolyte and redeposit into large nanoparticles. The Cu in Cu‐Au alloy was easily dissolved and aggregated, which was attributed to the mobility of Cu and the galvanic interaction between Cu and Au.[ 91 ] Zhu et al.[ 92 ] found that the Cu in the epitaxial Au–Cu heterostructure leached and redeposited, consequently formed AuCu alloy core and Cu shell. Therefore, the migration of Cu should be suppressed to maintain the surface structure. Jia et al.[ 85 ] found that the surface of three‐metallic Cu–Au–Zn alloy (3% Zn) with stable solid solution significantly suppressed the Cu dissolution by “vital few” effect: The low amount of Zn was alloyed with CuAu and strengthened the solid solution to suppress the migration of metal atoms, which improved both structural and performance stability. Benefiting from the consolidated structure, the trace Zn‐incorporated Cu–Au alloy exhibited high CO2‐to‐CO conversion efficiency with considerable stability (Figure 7i–l).

Interaction Between Loading/Support

The interactions between support (carbon, metal compounds, etc.) and loading can fix the active species by the electrostatic force or coordination/supermolecule interaction, which prevents the active species from detaching, dissolving, and immigrating. Joshi et al.[ 93 ] found that the interactions between the IrO2 nanoparticle and boron‐incorporated reduced graphene oxide (B‐rGO) diminished the rate and extent of Ir oxidation, so prevented the dissolution of nanoparticle and suppressed the oxidation of support, enhancing the acidic OER performance (Figure 8a–c). Liu et al.[ 94 ] reported that the Ni–N interaction between Ni loading and porous N‐incorporated carbon within the micropores prevented the Ni‐agglomeration. Furthermore, the plentiful micropores favored the adsorption of CO2, ensuring the instantaneous reactant supply and enhancing the CO2‐to‐CO reduction. The metal‐oxygen interactions in metal/metal oxide can stabilize the chemical structure in acidic OER as well. Chang et al.[ 95 ] fabricated Co3O4 loaded with short‐range Ru ion arrays. The Ru ions replaced the Co ions partially on the surface, resulting in near‐ideal Ru−Ru interatomic distances and a unique Ru−O hybridization state. Therefore, the lattice oxygen reaction was suppressed, thus the Ru leaching was alleviated.

Dissolution of NiFe layered double hydroxides (NiFe‐LDH) during operation not only leads to degradation of anode itself, but also deactivates cathode for water splitting, resulting in the decay of overall electrocatalytic performance. Liu et al.[ 96 ] founded that the leaching and segregation of metal ions (especially Fe) could be greatly reduced by adding the phosphate ions into the electrolyte because it migrated to the anode under the electric field, and bonded with high valence Ni and Fe sites to form stable metal hydroxy phosphates. Furthermore, the borates, sulfates, and carbonates additives showed similar results (Figure 8d–g). Although the non‐metal elements may tune the electronic structure for improved CO2‐to‐HCOOH conversion, they may be leached too. Peng et al.[ 98 ] found that the NaBiS2 converted to S‐incorporated Na+‐adsorbed Bi(110), while the strong Na‐S interaction prevented the S from leaching, resulting in a modified electronic structure for improved CO2‐to‐HCOOH conversion. Interaction between specific crystal phase and S species can avoid its leaching as well. Shen et al.[ 97 ] found that the π‐SnS with uniform rhombic dodecahedron structure could stabilize S by strong metal‐S interaction, effectively enhancing the *OCHO adsorption. Moreover, the *H binding strength was optimized, which could not only promote the hydrogenation reaction but also suppress the HER. Therefore, the CO2‐to‐HCOOH performance was enhanced (Figure 8g–i). The organic molecules decoration on catalyst's surface can stabilize the critical intermediate and improve the C2+ production yield in CO2RR. However, they may be leached in the electrocatalysis. Hydrophobic protective layer can isolate the contact between water and the decorated molecules, preventing the decorate species from leaching. Fu et al.[ 99 ] introduced a poly(vinylidene fluoride‐co‐hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF‐HFP) layer during the preparation of the gas diffusion electrode, where the hydrophobic layer was formed on the 4‐dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP)‐functionalized Cu‐catalyst. Therefore, the leaching of DMAP could be prevented and a high CO2‐to‐ethanol conversion was achieved.

Brief Summary

Based on these discussions, two strategies can be used to achieve the prevention of leaching and detachment. i) Constructing a chemically stable sheath that can isolate the electrolyte and stabilize the active sites. However, encapsulation may limit mass diffusion. ii) Strengthening the intrinsic crystal structure or enhancing the binding between the loading and substrate.

Crystal‐Phase‐Evolution Engineering

The electrocatalytic performance highly relies on the geometric or crystal structures, like special crystal facets, heterointerfaces, crystallinity, and exposed edges. However, those structures may be destroyed in harsh electrocatalytic conditions. Additionally, the active phase with special crystal facets and morphology can be selectively controlled by tuning the structures of pre‐catalysts. Keeping the structure, engineering the active phase, and avoiding the Ostwald ripening are ways for the reconstruction to improve the activity and stability.

Structure‐Skeleton Control

Incorporation, distortion, hetero‐domain construction, and additive addition are the strategies for stabilizing the as‐designed active phase and resisting the structure collapse. For example, Fe‐leaching of the Ni–Fe hydroxide may lead to segregation, thus reducing the activity. The NO3 −‐incorporation could alleviate the Fe leaching/migration by pinning effect. Liao et al.[ 100 ] found that the NO3 − coordinated with the unstable Fe sites of FeOOH and alleviated the Fe segregation, so the interface of FeOOH/Ni3(NO3)2(OH)4 (F‐NNH) remained. Notably, the Ni3(NO3)2(OH)4 lattice also avoided the fast leaching of NO3 −. All of those enhanced the OER activity and stability (Figure 9a–e). Introducing distortion can weaken the metal‐O covalence bonds, enhancing the lattice flexibility for high tolerance to the interface collapse by tension. Our group[ 101 ] used NH4F for dissolving Ni and Fe partially in the FeOOH/Ni hydroxide to introduce distortions, which could enhance lattice flexibility and stabilize the FeOOH/Ni hydroxide interfaces, benefiting the OER basic steps, like the OH− adsorption, transportation and intermediates dehydrogenation.

The CoOx skeletons in the spinel may collapse in the highly reductive HER condition. Introducing the crystalline/amorphous domains (nanocrystalline glass‐like structure) can stabilize the CoO6 and CoO4 of Co oxide. Our group[ 102 ] found that the cobalt oxide (CoOx) pre‐catalyst fabricated by using CrCl3‐CeCl3‐NaH2PO2 hybrid additive generated CoOx nanocrystalline glass with crystalline domain/amorphous hybrid structure, where the electronic interactions between crystalline/amorphous domains protected the CoO6 and CoO4 units of the spinel well. Therefore, the water molecule cleavage, hydrogen concentration, and transportation/conversion were improved, leading to high HER performance in alkali (Figure 9f–h).

Additives can stabilize certain electrochemical‐active crystal facets in the reconstructed structure. Han et al.[ 103 ] found that ethylenediamine tetramethylene phosphonic acid (EDTMPA) molecules preferentially adsorbed on Cu(110), stabilizing this facet with high *CO binding strength. Additionally, EDTMPA‐adsorbed Cu(110) promoted proton generation from water, accelerating *CHO generation on Cu(110) and resulting in excellent electrocatalytic CO2‐to‐CH4 performance (Figure 9i–k).

Ostwald‐Ripening Prevention

Ostwald ripening makes the nanoparticle size increase, and the curvature of the edge/corner decreases. Constructing stable non‐contact structures and anchoring the loaded species with strong attraction are effective for preventing Ostwald ripening.

1D hollow structures prevent contact between active sites, inhibiting particle growth. For example, Shi et al.[ 104 ] developed dendritic IrTe ultrathin nanotubes to isolate the active sites, which not only avoided Ostwald ripening, but also provided free paths for rapid electron transfer.

Enhancing the electronic interactions between the substrate and loading can prevent Ostwald ripening and optimize the electronic states, thereby improving electrochemical performance. Jing et al.[ 105 ] fabricated porous carbon nitride (CN) enriched with hydroxyl groups (─OH) for supporting atomic‐level dispersed FeOx (CN‐FeOx‐OH) as an OER electrocatalyst. The coordination effects among ─OH, CN, and Fe in FeOx not only suppressed the migration and aggregation of dispersed FeOx, but also optimized the electronic structure. Guo et al.[ 106 ] found that interactions between Bi nanoparticle and N‐incorporated carbon (Bi@C‐700‐4) could prevent Ostwald ripening and optimize the electronic states of Bi and adjacent N/C atoms, promoting the CO2 activation and *OCHO formation, thus improving the CO2‐to‐HCOOH conversion (Figure 10a–c). Liu et al.[ 107 ] developed Ru single atoms (SAs) on N‐incorporated Mo2C nanosheets (N‐Mo2C NSs), where the strong bonding between Ru atoms and Mo2C as regulated by incorporated N prevented the SA migration and alleviated Ostwald ripening in acidic HER. Xu et al.[ 108 ] created a RuIr@CoNC hybrid with strong electronic interactions between RuIr nanocrystal and CoNC support, resulting in homogeneous dispersion and firm immobilization of RuIr. Additionally, the electronic structures of RuIr and adjacent CoNC were optimized, leading to outstanding OER performance in various pH conditions (Figure 10d–i). Cao et al.[ 109 ] fabricated Ru SAs anchored on Co(OH)2 nanosheets for NO3RR. The electronic interactions between Ru SAs and Co(OH)2 stabilized the Ru species and prevented their aggregation, optimizing the free energies for the adsorption of NO3 ─ and intermediate.

Active‐Phase Control

Tuning the structure of pre‐catalyst can trigger special oxidation states or the formation of new phase in the reconstructed structure. Rationally choosing the non‐metal elements in pre‐catalyst and optimizing the morphology are effective for active‐phase control.

Optimizing the coordinate ligands can introduce special electronic states in MOF‐based pre‐catalysts, which could affect the electronic states in reconstructed structures. Zhang et al.[ 110 ] found that the electronic structures of coordinated Fe sites in MIL‐53(Fe)‐2OH were affected by the coordinate ligands, making the eg–t2g splitting of Fe 3d orbitals in reconstructed Ni‐Fe hydroxide, leading to the electroactive O sites. Therefore, the adsorption and conversion of reactants and intermediates at the rate‐determining step of OER were enhanced, resulting in lowered overpotential and enhanced stability (Figure 11a,b).

Halogen in pre‐catalyst can tune the reconstructed crystal plane and morphology. Wang et al.[ 111 ] demonstrated that different Cu halides (CuX, X = Cl, Br, or I) microcrystals underwent halide leaching and transformed into various Cu nanoarchitectures with different degrees of truncations at edges/vertices, favoring different CO2RR intermediates and product selectivity. For example, CuI‐derived Cu nanofibers favored the formation of C2/3 products with a particular selectivity toward ethane, while Cu nanocubes derived from CuCl showed high selectivity toward C1 products. CuBr‐derived Cu nanodendrites predominantly produced C2/3 products, favoring the formation of ethylene (Figure 11c–e). Yang et al.[ 112 ] found that BiOX (X = Cl, Br, and I) reconstructed to metallic Bi with different crystal planes for CO2RR, with BiOBr preferring to generate Bi(003), which was more favorable for CO2‐to‐HCOOH conversion than Bi(012) from BiOCl and Bi(003)+Bi(012) from BiOI. Metal incorporation can facilitate the creation of defects, like lattice mismatches and dislocations, providing active sites for NO3RR. Wang et al.[ 9 ] developed a novel grain boundary (GB)‐rich Sn‐Cu catalyst derived from the in‐situ electroreduction of Sn‐incorporated CuO nanoflowers (Sn‐CuO). Compared with pure Cu, the reconstructed Sn‐CuO introduced various GBs as the active sites for the *NO3 − activation. Additionally, Sn converted to Sn0 and Sn𝛿+, suppressing competitive HER and synergistically promoting NO3RR.

The coordinate states and the crystal facets of reconstructed structures may be inherited from the micro shape and crystallinity of pre‐catalyst. Fang et al.[ 21b ] found that the polygon facet number of Cu2O pre‐catalyst was relative to the coordination state of in‐situ reduced Cu. Notably, the in‐situ reduced Cu from twenty‐six facets Cu2O had a suitable coordination number for heightening the *CO adsorption, which could optimize the C–C coupling, therefore benefited the CO2‐to‐C2H4 conversion (Figure 11f–h). Hu et al.[ 8 ] found that the Cu(100)‐rich rugged Cu‐nanobelt (Cu‐NBs‐100) reconstructed from CuO nanobelts with dominant CuO(111) facets (denoted as CuO‐NBs‐111), facilitating the exposure of Cu(100) (Figure 11i–l). Compared with Cu nanoparticles with Cu(111)‐rich surfaces (termed Cu‐NPs‐111), the reconstructed Cu‐NBs‐100 could synergistically improve the adsorptions of NO3* and H*, thereby reducing the reaction barrier of NO3RR and suppressed the HER (Figure 11m,n).

Brief Summary

Controlling the internal and external factors may optimize the interaction at the solid–liquid–gas interface and engineer the reconstructed structures for improved catalytic performance. Regulating the internal structure of pre‐catalyst includes distortion, coordination control, optimization of loading‐support interaction, and incorporation of heteroatom. Manipulating the external factors include the addition of additives to stabilize the electrocatalytically‐active crystal facet and the formation of the active phase.

Surface‐Poisoning Alleviation

As we have described in 2.2.5 and 2.3.5, the anodic reaction (OER) and the cathodic reactions (HER, CO2RR, and NO3RR) involve various reactants and intermediates, which may lead to surface poisoning. Therefore, the strategies for solving the typical poisonings, such as the adsorptions of H+, OH−, and CO, the carbonate covering, and the NOx accumulation, need to be discussed.

H‐Adsorption

Water splitting generates Hads in HER, CO2RR, and NO3RR, which can serve as hydrogen sources, but may also lead to surface poisoning due to competitive adsorption on active site. For example, Ni hydride can lead to passive NiHx on the surface because the Ni–H binding is too strong to be dissociated. Harada et al.[ 113 ] prepared hydride‐free, fractally structured Ni films for HER by electrodeposition in the presence of the 3,5‐diamino‐1,2,4‐triazole (DAT) additive. DAT suppressed the diffusion of hydrogen into the Ni crystal, preventing the formation of NiHx, and resulting in high HER activity and stability. Non‐metal elements can firmly bind H, alleviating H poisoning on nearby metal sites. For instance, Xu et al.[ 114 ] found that the C‐incorporation in Co2P tailored the electronic structure through its strong electronegativity, introducing a two‐step H delivery pathway by forming the C–Had intermediate. This weakened the Co─H bonding, avoided the H‐poisoning on Co sites, and promoted the HER kinetics.

Introducing tandem functional sites with strong Hads affinity and water cleavage capability can alleviate the H‐poisoning and involve Hads in subsequent steps. Phase segregation can alleviate the H‐poisoning by weakening the metal–H interaction on the electrocatalytic sites, promoting the basic reactions through the tandem functional sites. Rajan et al.[ 115 ] developed a CoMoRu trimetallic alloy for alkaline HER. The Co and Mo converted to Co(OH)2/Co and MoOx, respectively, serving as water‐dissociation centers. These centers tune the electronic state of Ru and optimize the Ru‐H bonding. Consequently, the H2‐evolving entity was improved, and the H‐poisoning was avoided.

The above results show that Had may diffuse in the electrode and destroy the structure or poison the active sites. Constructing a passive layer to prevent H diffusion or introducing competitive adsorption sites can alleviate the H‐poisoning. Notably, introducing tandem functional sites could make the Had convert in time, thus improving the performance and stability of electrocatalyst in non‐acidic cathodic reactions.

OH−‐Adsorption

The strong OHad‐binding on the active site can lead to poisoning in non‐acidic cathodic reactions too. Weakening the OHad‐bonding can alleviate this poisoning by using support with high OHad‐affinity to construct competitive OH− adsorption. For example, tungsten carbide (WC) has a strong OHad‐affinity, which can be used for competitive OHad‐adsorption, thus alleviating the OHad‐poisoning. Lin et al.[ 116 ] found that the OHad‐binding energy of Ru could be weakened by WC, which punctured the OHad‐blockage. Additionally, the Ru/WC interfaces could accelerate the dissociation of water and optimize the H‐affinity, resulting in good HER performance. SnO2 also has a strong OHad‐affinity and can be used to eliminate the OHad‐poisoning through competitive OHad‐adsorption. Zhang et al.[ 117 ] constructed SnO2 nanoparticles incorporated with Ru single atoms (Ru SAs) on carbon (Ru SAs‐SnO2/C), where SnO2 regulated the interaction between Ru and OHad, thus alleviating the OHad‐poisoning of Ru sites (Figure 12a‐d). Constructing a “fence” layer by the decoration of small molecules is also efficient for preventing the OHad‐poisoning. Zhu et al.[ 118 ] found that ascorbic acid (VC) on Bi3S2 nanowires could stabilize the defective sites of Bi3S2 by trapping OHad in CO2RR, thereby protecting the critical active sites (Figure 12e–g). This enabled high CO2‐to‐HCOOH conversion and stability (Figure 12h,i).

Based on the above discussions, using materials with high OHad affinity as substrates and constructing fence layers can effectively manage and mitigate the OHad‐poisoning, thus improving the performance and stability of electrocatalysts in non‐acidic cathodic reactions.

CO‐Adsorption

Weakening the interactions between CO and active sites is essential to avoid the CO‐poisoning. Alloying and co‐adsorption can mitigate the CO‐poisoning by optimizing the d‐band center or promoting the CO conversion, thereby preventing the CO‐poisoning. For example, the coupling between Pd and Ag in mesoporous PdAg nanospheres [ 119 ] and the crystallographic ordering of Pd and Bi atoms within intermetallic Pd3Bi nanocrystal alloys [ 120 ] brought the d‐band center close to the Fermi level, suppressing the over‐binding of CO and enhancing the CO2‐to‐HCOOH conversion. Co‐adsorption of *OH and *CO can alleviate the CO‐poisoning by weakening the *CO binding. Herzog et al.[ 21a ] found that proper coverage of co‐adsorbed OHad on a Cu substrate formed on pre‐reduced Cu2O nanocubes, which alleviated the CO‐poisoning, enhancing the *CO‐conversion and C─C coupling, and boosting the CO2‐to‐ethanol. The co‐existence of metal‐H, hydrides, and *CO can also alleviate the CO‐poisoning by converting *CO into higher intermediates or products in a timely manner. Song et al.[ 121 ] found that the direct bonding between Pd nanoparticle (Pd NPs) and Cu increased the charge density at the Cu sites. The Pd NPs with hydrogen storage capability provided stable Hads adsorption by PdHx. Consequently, *CO at the Pd‐Cu interface was activated and converted to stable *CHO, facilitating the *CHO‐*CO coupling and promoting the CO2‐to‐C2H4 conversion.

Therefore, reducing the binding strength of M‐CO can alleviate the CO‐poisoning. It is also possible to avoid the poisoning effect of CO by managing the *CO adsorption via co‐adsorption, which can accelerate the *CO conversion.

Carbonate Covering

CO2 can combine with local/bulk OH− on the cathode to produce (bi)carbonate and CO3 2− can form insulating (hydro)carbonate (Ma(OH)x(CO3)y) compounds, leading to surface poisoning, resulting in sluggish electron transfer at the solid–liquid interface and significantly reduced CO2RR performance. It has been found that acidic media can hamper the formation of (bi)carbonate and Ma(OH)x(CO3)y on the cathode. However, the competitive HER in acid is high. Although adding trace amount of alkali metal can limit the HER, the (bi)carbonate precipitation and Ma(OH)x(CO3)y become severe. Modifying the electrolyte‐electrocatalyst microenvironment using surfactant can prevent carbonate precipitation. Wang et al.[ 123 ] used Ag as electrocatalyst and polystyrene sulfonate (PSS) as an addition in the mixed electrolyte of H2SO4 and K2SO4 for the CO2‐to‐CO conversion. The PSS‐K+ interaction reconstructed the interfacial H‐bond network on Ag and prevented the contact between CO3 2− and the interface by steric hindrance. Therefore, both the HER and carbonate precipitation were suppressed.

Changing the electronic structure of electrocatalyst may reduce the CO3 2−/OH− amount on the surface for alleviating the carbonate poisoning. Heteroatom incorporation can endow charge redistribution for reducing the CO3 2−/OH− affinity. Our group[ 124 ] found that the Cu‐incorporation could tune the electronic state of Zn oxide, weakening the CO3 2−/OH− adsorption, thereby preventing the formation of Zn5(CO3)2(OH)6 and Zn(OH)2. Concurrently, the reconstructed Cu‐incorporated Zn oxide generated stepped metallic Cu sites and increased the Ov concentration, benefiting the adsorption/activation of reactants and the stabilization of the intermediate for the CO2‐to‐CO conversion. Similar to Zn carbonate, Bi2O2CO3 is inactive and toxic in CO2RR. The construction of dynamic liquid surface can expose the fresh active surface in time, reducing the accumulated carbonate salts, which is one of strategies for preventing the carbonate‐poisoning. Hou et al.[ 125 ] developed a self‐healing electrocatalyst for the CO2‐to‐formate conversion using a Bi‐Ga‐based liquid metal alloy with a dynamic liquid metal surface. The fluidity of the alloy easily removed Bi2O2CO3, allowing Bi to segregate on the surface and refresh the surface. Consequently, the deactivated electrocatalyst was recovered.

Clearly, the strategies, including inhibiting the generation of carbonate by controlling the electrolyte, weakening the CO3 2− adsorption and isolating the contact between CO3 2− and electrocatalyst are effective for preventing the carbonate‐covering. In detail, adding additives can optimize the microenvironment, introducing heteroatom in the pre‐catalyst can tune the electronic states for weakening the CO3 2− affinity, and using liquid alloy can timely refresh the active surface.

NOx‐Accumulation

NOx species (NO3 − and NO2 −) in NO3RR are critical reactants or intermediates. However, the sluggish eight‐electron reaction process of NO3RR may hamper their conversion. Additionally, they are not easy to mitigate due to their large size, leading to surface poisoning.[ 126 ] Reducing the NOx adsorption and promoting its conversion is necessary.

Incorporation can optimize the binding state of NO2 − and alleviate the NO2 −‐poisoning through changing the electronic state of electrocatalyst. Wu et al.[ 127 ] founded that the Mn‐incorporation could prevent NO2 − from poisoning the Cu by enhancing the kinetic rate of NO2 −‐to‐NH3. The Mn‐incorporation induced a distinct configuration of *NO, enhanced the breaking of N─O bond in adsorbed NO2 −, and promoted the *NO‐to‐*NOH and the NO3RR accordingly. Defect can optimize the surface electronic state for regulating the NOx adsorption and alleviating the NOx‐poisoning. Huang et al.[ 128 ] reported that low‐coordinated Cu sites upshifted the Cu d‐band center to the Fermi Level, regulating the NO2‐adsorption in NO3RR. Hence, the NO2 − to NO2H conversion was enhanced, achieving high production of NH3. Interface can also alleviate the NO2 −‐poisoning by optimizing the NO2 − adsorption. Fu et al.[ 129 ] found that the electronic interaction between the Cu2O and Cu at the Cu2O/Cu interface increased the adsorption free energy of NO2 − and reduced the energy barrier of *NO2 to *NO, inhibiting the NO2 −‐poisoning for improved NO3RR.

Constructing tandem function sites for H or NOx spillover in multi‐element compound can lower the energy barrier of the NOx hydrogenation, which can combine the functional sites for water splitting (H2O + e−→ H + OH−) and NOx adsorption/hydrogenation by H spillover, resulting in fast hydrogenation. Li et al.[ 130 ] found that nickel cobalt phosphide (NiCoP) had two different kinds of hydrogens in NO3RR: catalytically‐inert adsorbed hydrogen (HI*) at Co‐hollow site and active HII* at secondary Co−P site. The dimerization between HI* and HII* for the H2 evolution was blocked, thereby the HII* drove timely the hydrogenation of nitrate. Therefore, the accumulation of nitrate was hampered, leading to high NO3RR performance.

Loading atomic‐dispersed Ru with high OH−‐affinity can accumulate water molecules through tuning the hydrogen bond networks, accelerating the NO3 −‐hydrogenation. For example, Hu et al.[ 122 ] found that the ‐OH group on oxyphilic Ru could accumulate water molecules, making the collision between water and NO3 − on Ru‐loaded Cu2O accelerated, which improved the NO3 −‐hydrogenation and alleviated the NO3 −‐poisoning. Hence, the Ru‐modified Cu2O could work at the high NO3 − concentration with high NH3 selectivity and yield rate (Figure 12h–k).

We see that modifying the NOx poisoning can be avoided by tuning the NOx‐adsorption mode and introducing defect, heteroatom, and interface. Additionally, constructing tandem functional sites and hydrogen‐bond network could accelerate the NOx‐hydrogenation.

Brief Summary

Taken all the discussions together, we can know that “passive defense” and “active conversion” strategies can alleviate surface poisoning. The “passive defense” methods, such as encapsulation and incorporation, aim to weaken the poisoning species adsorption. The “active conversion” methods, such as the construction of tandem functional sites and optimization of hydrogen‐bond network, aim to promote the poisoning species conversion, which is more favorable for electrocatalysis because many poisoning species are reactants or critical intermediates.

Conclusion and Outlooks

Conclusion

The in‐situ reconstruction is widely observed in various catalytic reactions. The fine reconstructed structures, such as crystallinity, defect, interface, surface decorations, and composition, shows the “vital few” effects in electrocatalysis. In particular, in‐situ reconstruction is a two‐blade sword: that may create new active sites for the electrocatalysis, or hamper the electrocatalytic reaction and make the electrocatalysts degraded (Tables 1 , 2 ).

Outlooks

The in‐situ reconstruction is unavoidable in electrocatalysis, and influenced by multi‐dimensional issues. The macrostructure of electrode and the electrolyte cell also affect the in‐situ reconstruction. The in‐situ reconstruction makes the structure of active sites apart from the theoretical design, resulting in the complexity of the structure‐electrochemical relationship. Fortunately, the in‐situ reconstruction can be rationally controlled through the design of pre‐catalyst and the selection of electrocatalytic conditions. The journey for the controlled in‐situ reconstruction is still at the beginning though many inspired results have been discovered. The challenges to be solved in the future include: The advanced operando characterization techniques and the algorithm for static analysis shall be developed. In‐situ characterizations have been instrumental in shedding light on in‐situ reconstruction. However, the signal of fine structure, such as distortion, surface decoration, and reconstructed phase, is greatly influenced by noise from substrate, electrolyte, and gas bubble. Therefore, effective and sensitive operando surface analysis techniques as well as algorithms that can extract the annihilated useful signal from noise should be developed.The reconstructions by physical factors and corrosion in electrocatalysis should be taken into notice. The physical factors may introduce the structure change by gas‐bubble generation and breaking, as well as the convection of electrolyte, leading to fissures and cracks on the electrocatalytic layer and current collectors gradually, and material fatigue and breaking. Four kinds of corrosion lead to structure collapse in electrocatalysis: 1) Chemical corrosion. The interaction among oxygen, water, and metallic current collector/electrocatalyst lead to chemical corrosion. 2) Galvanic corrosion. The close circuit of different compositions in electrocatalyst (especially metal and alloy) and the electrolyte form the galvanic battery, introducing galvanic corrosion. 3) Electrochemomechanical failure. Materials cleavage by repeatedly volume expansion/contraction through ion intercalation/deintercalation leads to electrochemomechanical failure. 4) Reversible current corrosion. Intermittent electrolysis generates oxidation at the cathode and reduction at the anode, potentially disrupting electrocatalytic conditions and leading to reversible current corrosion. However, the physical factors and corrosions for in‐situ reconstruction are seldom studied. Thus, the mechanisms of degradation by physical factors as well as corrosion shall be studied. Additionally, the robustness of electrocatalyst should be enhanced.The relations among the pre‐catalyst, electrocatalytic conditions, reconstructed structure, and electrochemical performance need to be studied. The relationship of structure‐electrochemical performance has been studied for decades, and impressive progress has been achieved. However, there is lack of consideration about the effects of in‐situ reconstruction on the electrochemical performance. It is well reported now that distortion, incorporation, surface decoration, and crystal phase in the reconstructed structures play role as the “real” active sites and determine the electrochemical performance. Therefore, relations among the pre‐catalyst, reconstructed structure, electrocatalytic condition, and electrochemical performance need to be systematically studied.
