Clinical and patient-reported outcomes of microneedling with ascorbic acid versus scalpel technique for gingival depigmentation: A randomized clinical trial
Authors: Anika Dawar, Jaya Pandey, Vikender Singh Yadav, Dolly Angral, Sumit Kumar Das, Anuppriyanka Ilango, Razia Haidrus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo compare gingival depigmentation achieved with microneedling and topical ascorbic acid (MN + AA) versus the conventional scalpel technique, along with evaluation of repigmentation and patient-reported outcomes (PROMs). Gingival thickness (GT) and dermal-gingival pigmentation associations were also explored.MethodsTwenty-six participants with maxillary physiologic gingival hyperpigmentation were randomized (1:1) to scalpel or MN + AA using an automated microneedling pen. Gingival pigmentation was assessed clinically (Dummett-Gupta Oral Pigmentation Index [DOPI], Gingival Pigmentation Index [GPI]); photographically (Pigmented Surface Area [PSA], Lightness [L]), and using a melanin meter (Gingival Melanin Meter Score, GMMS) at baseline, 1, 3, and 6 months. Primary outcome was change in DOPI at 1 month. Gingival Thickness (GT) and Dermal Melanin Meter Scores (DMMS) were recorded.ResultsTwenty-three participants completed the study. The scalpel group showed greater reduction in DOPI, GPI, PSA, GMMS, and a greater increase in L at 1 month (P < 0.05). Incomplete depigmentation was observed at some gingival sites with MN + AA. Repigmentation occurred in both groups; DOPI and GMMS were significantly lower in scalpel group at 3 months, with the difference in DOPI persisting at 6 months. Pain, itching, and discomfort scores were modestly higher in the scalpel group, with significant for discomfort on day 3. Color improvement and satisfaction scores were high in both groups. GT increased significantly in the MN + AA at 6 months. DMMS and GMMS were not correlated at any timepoint.ConclusionBoth treatments significantly reduced gingival pigmentation at 1 month, with the scalpel technique showing lower pigmentation intensity at 6 months. Both treatments were well tolerated with favorable PROMs.Clinical trial registration numberCTRI/2024/05/066602 (ctri.nic.in)

Introduction

Gingival color is an important determinant of smile esthetics. Physiological gingival hyperpigmentation is a brownish discoloration of the gingiva resulting from an increased melanin deposition by the melanocytes within the basal and suprabasal epithelial layers.1 Gingival depigmentation is a periodontal plastic procedure frequently sought by patients to improve the smile esthetics. Conventional techniques achieve this by removing the pigmented epithelial layer, allowing regeneration of less pigmented tissues, with commonly used modalities including scalpel surgery, laser ablation, bur abrasion, and electrocautery.2 Among these, scalpel depigmentation remains the gold standard owing to its simplicity, affordability, and predictable outcomes. However, surgical procedures are associated with disadvantages such as intraoperative bleeding, post-operative pain, delayed wound healing, patient discomfort, recurrence of pigmentation, and may be challenging in patients with a thin gingival phenotype because of the risk of gingival recession, injury to periosteum, or bone exposure.1,3 These limitations warrant exploration of other non-surgical alternatives.

Among the minimally invasive approaches, ascorbic acid (AA)/vitamin C, a widely used dermal depigmentation agent, has emerged as a promising option for gingival depigmentation.4,5 AA inhibits melanogenesis by reducing tyrosinase activity, thereby reducing dopaquinone formation, a key intermediate in melanin synthesis. Its antioxidant and reducing properties decrease melanocyte activity and convert oxidized melanin to lighter forms. It also interferes with melanosome transfer by modulating melanocyte-keratinocyte interactions and promoting collagen synthesis, making it suitable for gingival application.1,6,7 Both topical and injectable AA formulations have been explored for gingival depigmentation 1,6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11. While topical application is simple and painless, its effectiveness may be limited by salivary washout and restricted tissue penetration.6 Injectable AA (oral mesotherapy) overcomes these limitations through localized trans-epithelial delivery and has demonstrated depigmentation efficacy comparable to scalpel and laser techniques, with favorable patient-reported outcomes (PROMs) 1,10, 11, 12. However, injectable delivery remains technique-sensitive with limited control over penetration depth, and potential to cause tissue irritation or necrosis with excessive dosage or deeper penetration. Another consideration with conventional needles is product leakage in regions of thin gingival thickness such as canine eminence and the mucogingival junction.1,11 Therefore, a suitable approach for AA delivery in the gingival tissues is warranted, allowing effective tissue penetration with safety and ease of use.

In this context, microneedling (MN) is an emerging minimally invasive approach that creates transient microchannels within the epithelium using micron-sized needles.13 These microchannels enhance trans-epithelial delivery of topical agents while providing greater control over penetration depth than conventional injections.Additionally, MN induces collagen synthesis, potentially increasing gingival thickness, and may act synergistically with topical AA to enhance depigmentation 13, 14, 15. Although combination of MN and AA has been widely used in dermatology to treat hyperpigmentation conditions such as melasma,15,16 studies evaluating this intervention for gingival depigmentation are scarce. Previous studies are largely confined to pre- and post-treatment evaluation of MN + AA,17,18 while the few comparisons with the scalpel technique have focussed on short-term depigmentation outcomes assessed using clinical indices alone.19,20 Further, repigmentation, PROMs such as treatment tolerability and satisfaction, and the additional benefits and challenges associated with the dermatology-based MN device have not been evaluated together, precluding an overall assessment of this modality. Thus, the present randomized clinical trial primarily aimed to compare the gingival depigmentation achieved using a dermatology-based MN pen combined with topically applied AA with the conventional scalpel technique. Secondary objectives were to compare gingival repigmentation and PROMs between groups. Additionally, changes in gingival thickness and associations between dermal and gingival pigmentation across treatment groups were also explored.

Materials and methods

Study design

The present study was a parallel-group, single-blinded, randomized controlled clinical trial and conducted in accordance with CONSORT guidelines. Ethical approval was obtained from the institute's ethics committee (AIIMSA1242/22.4.24), and the trial was prospectively registered with the Clinical Trial Registry-India (CTRI/2024/05/066602). Each participant received detailed information regarding the study design and potential risks and benefits, following which written informed consent was obtained. The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (1975, revised in 2013).

Study participants

The participants were selected from patients attending the outpatient clinic of the Periodontics Division,Centre for Dental Education and Research, All India Institute of Medical Sciences, New Delhi between May 2024 and April 2025. The participants were included based on the following criteria:(a) adults aged 18-40 years; (b) physiologic melanin hyperpigmentation of the maxillary anterior gingiva; (c) seeking esthetic management of gingival hyperpigmentation, (d) availability for three treatment sessions and follow-up visits; and (e) systemically healthy. Exclusion criteria were: (a) pathologic/drug-induced gingival pigmentation; (b) current or former smokers; (c) presence of periodontal diseases; (d) patients taking AA supplements; (e) known hypersensitivity to AA ; (f) pregnant or lactating females.

Sample size

Sample size was calculated using the power.t.test function in R statistical software (version 4.6.0). Dummett Gupta oral pigmentation index (DOPI) was considered as the primary outcome. Previous studies have reported the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of DOPI at 1 month ranging from 0.15 ± 0.36 to 1.87 ± 0.38 in the control(scalpel) group, and 1.07 ± 0.31 for the MN + AA group. 15,18 Anticipating a mean difference of 0.6 units at 1 month between the two groups and considering the highest SD of 0.38, the minimum sample size was estimated to be 20 (10 per group) at a 5% level of significance with 90% power. Accounting for a 15% loss to follow-up, the final sample size was estimated at 26 participants (13 per group).

Randomization, allocation concealment and blinding

Participants meeting the eligibility criteria were randomly allocated (1:1) to the control (scalpel) or the test (MN + AA) group using a computer-generated random sequence (www.randomizer.org). Allocation concealment was achieved using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE), prepared by an individual not involved in the trial. Participants were enrolled by investigator DA, while the group allocation was disclosed immediately before treatment by investigator VSY, who was not involved in recruitment, treatment or outcome assessment. The outcome assessor (JP) was blinded to the group allocation; however, blinding of the treating clinician (AD) and the participants was not possible due to the nature of the interventions.

Examiner calibration

Intra-examiner reliability of the trained blinded outcome assessor (JP) was assessed in five pilot patients not included in the study, with reassessment of all parameters after 1 week. A weighted Cohen's kappa of >0.80 was obtained for clinical pigmentation indices, and an intraclass coefficient of >0.90 for other parameters.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of the study was the change in DOPI from baseline to 1-month post-treatment. Secondary outcomes included changes in GPI, PSA, L and GMMS at 1 month, repigmentation assessed using the above pigmentation parameters at 3 and 6 months, and PROMs comprising pain, itching, discomfort, perceived gingival color improvement, and overall patient satisfaction. Exploratory outcomes included changes in GT and the correlation between DMMS and GMMS.

Treatment procedures

All participants underwent full mouth supragingival and subgingival oral prophylaxis. Gingival depigmentation was performed in the maxillary esthetic zone extending from the first/second premolar to the same on the contralateral side by a single experienced periodontist (AD). Following baseline assessment of all parameters, participants received one of the following treatments according to the allocated groups:

Scalpel technique: Local infiltration (2% lignocaine containing 1:80,000 adrenaline) was administered. Using a No.15C blade, a partial thickness incision was made parallel to the long axis of the tooth to remove the hyperpigmented epithelium and a thin layer of connective tissue while avoiding bone exposure (Fig. 1g). Residual tissue tags were trimmed using microsurgical scissors to ensure complete removal of visible pigmentation (Fig. 1h). The site was irrigated with saline, and hemostasis was achieved with sterile gauze. Participants were advised to avoid brushing the surgical site and use analgesics as required and were reviewed at 1-week post-surgery(Fig. 1i).

MN + AA: A 1-mm layer of 20% benzocaine topical anesthetic gel (Progel-B, Septodent India) was applied to the target region. GT at each tooth was measured as described previously. The treatment was performed using an automated microneedling pen (Dr Pen auto microneedling system Ultima M8, China), with provision to adjust needle depth (0.25-2.5 mm) and fitted with a 16-pin stainless steel needle cartridge (0.18 mm needle diameter), in conjunction with topical AA solution (V-C injections, Vesco pharmaceuticals, Thailand) (Fig. 2a–c). The protocol adopted for gingival MN was similar to previous studies.17,19,23,24 The MN pen was operated at a low speed (level 1) of approximately 8000 RPM and needle penetration depth was set one increment lower than the measured pre-procedural GT at each tooth (0.25 mm for GT ≤ 0.5 mm, 0.5 mm for GT > 0.5-1 mm,1 mm for GT > 1-1.5 mm, and 1.5 mm for GT > 1.5-2.0 mm), to minimize the risk of overpenetration. Additional infiltration anesthesia (2% lignocaine containing 1:80,000 adrenaline) was administered when required. The device was applied perpendicular to the gingiva, using a stamping motion with about four to five overlapping passes across horizontal, vertical, and diagonal directions for 30-40 seconds per tooth, until uniform pinpoint bleeding points were achieved throughout the pigmented gingiva (Fig. 2d and e). The area was irrigated with saline. One ampoule of AA solution (1000 mg/5 mL) was dispended into a sterile steel bowl for use across the entire pigmented region per session. A cotton roll soaked in the AA solution was applied to the target pigmented site for 10 min (Fig. 2f and g). The needle cartridge was discarded after each treatment session. The procedure was repeated at a 1-week interval for three sessions, with participants reviewed at 1-week post-treatment completion (Fig. 2h and i) They were advised to avoid acidic, spicy, and highly pigmented foods and to take analgesics as required.Fig. 2Clinical steps of gingival depigmentation using the microneedling and ascorbic acid technique (MN + AA): (a) Baseline maxillary gingival pigmentation; (b)Armamentarium comprising the automated microneedling pen, disposable needle cartridge and ascorbic acid ampoule; (c) Disposable 16-pin stainless steel needle cartridge; (d)Microneedling pen placed on pigmented gingiva; (e)Pinpoint bleeding points following microneedling; (f) Ascorbic acid solution dispensed in a sterile steel bowl with cotton roll saturated with the solution; (g) Saturated cotton roll placed over the treated gingiva for 10 min; (h) Clinical appearance at 1week following the first treatment session; (i) Clinical appearance at 1 week following completion of three treatment sessions.

Notably, the scalpel group underwent a single session of depigmentation procedure, while those in the MN + AA group received three treatment sessions at 1-week intervals. Both groups were reviewed 1 week after treatment completion, and subsequently followed up and assessed at 1, 3 and 6 months post-treatment completion.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered in an excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Corp, Washington, USA) and analyzed using Stata 18.0 statistical software (Stata Corp, TX, USA). Categorical data were presented using frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution. Between-group comparisons were performed using the chi-Square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and the independent sample t-test or a Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. Changes over time and the group-by-time interactions were analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) with Gaussian family, identity link functions, exchangeable correlation and robust standard errors. Within-group changes over time were assessed using GEE analysis followed by post hoc pairwise comparison with a Bonferroni correction. The correlations were assessed using Spearman's correlation coefficient. The primary outcome was analyzed at 1month using both per-protocol (PP; scalpel: n = 12, MN + AA: n = 11) and intention-to-treat (ITT; scalpel: n = 13, MN + AA: n = 13) approaches. To perform ITT, missing outcome data were filled using the last last-digit-carried forward (LOCF) method. The between group differences of primary and all secondary outcomes at 1 month time point were obtained using appropriate technique. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

With the growing interest in minimally invasive approaches for gingival depigmentation, the present study compared the MN + AA with the scalpel technique for their depigmentation efficacy, PROMs, and repigmentation. Additionally, this is the first gingival depigmentation study to quantify both gingival and dermal melanin concentrations using a dermal melanin meter. These devices are employed in dermatology as non-invasive reflectance-based instruments that provide objective, reproducible quantification of epidermal melanin concentration and have been validated for monitoring treatment responses across pigmentary disorders.25 Objective gingival melanin assessment was incorporated in the current study as clinical indices are inherently subjective, and photographic assessments are influenced by lighting conditions, gingival hue, and examiner perception.26,27

Both modalities significantly reduced gingival pigmentation at 1 month; however, the scalpel group showed significantly greater improvement in both pigmentation intensity (DOPI, L*, GMMS) and extent (GPI, PSA). The 1-month timepoint was selected in accordance with previous MN and AA studies, allowing completion of re-epithelialization and stabilization of clinical color outcomes.1,7,10,11,15,19 While scalpel depigmentation immediately removes the pigmented epithelium in a single session, depigmentation with MN + AA may result from enhanced transepithelial delivery of AA combined with MN-induced epithelial remodeling. Potential mechanisms include regulation of melanosome transfer, transient basement membrane disruption facilitating melanin clearance, and accelerated epithelial turnover. The additional gain in GT may further reduce the clinical visibility of residual pigmentation.15,28 These synergistic effects of MN and AA observed in the current study are consistent with the findings of Mosby et al..and Maheshwari et al., who reported superior depigmentation with the scalpel technique, but differ from those of a split-mouth trial favoring MN + AA 15,17, 18, 19, 20. These differences may be attributable to variations in MN pen design, needle number and diameter, and AA formulation. Unlike previous studies using freshly prepared AA powder, current study employed a ready-to-use, standardized ampoule requiring no chair-side preparation. Notably, residual pigmentation was observed at few anatomically challenging gingival sites following MN + AA, possibly due to the limited adaptation of dermatology-based MN pen's needle cartridge and straight handpiece to narrow interdental papillae and premolar gingiva. In contrast, the scalpel technique allowed direct visualization and complete pigment removal from these sites. Repigmentation occurred following both treatment modalities, howeverthe scalpel group maintained lower DOPI at 3 and 6 months, likely reflecting more complete elimination of melanocyte-containing basal epithelium. Pigmentation extent (GPI, PSA), however, was comparable between groups during follow-up, suggesting that limited migration and proliferation of residual or adjacent melanocytes, the most widely accepted mechanism of repigmentation, may restore the pigmentation regardless of the technique.1,29 In addition, surgical procedure-induced inflammation and wound healing may stimulate melanogenesis through upregulation of cytokines and growth factors contributing to post-inflammatory pigmentation.30

PROMs were favorable in both groups. Pain, itching, and discomfort scores were modestly higher following scalpel depigmentation, reaching significance only for discomfort on day 3.Although previous studies have reported greater post-operative morbidity with the scalpel technique than with minimally invasive approaches such as MN, injectable AA, and laser therapy,1,3,15,19 our findings suggest that scalpel depigmentation was also well tolerated. Compared with injectable AA delivered through conventional hypodermic needle, MN + AA provides controlled transepithelial drug delivery through uniform microchannels, which may reduce localized deposition at discrete sites thus minimizing tissue distention, irritation or ulceration.30 Consistent with this, itching scores were low, contrasting with our previous preliminary case series of injectable AA, in which few participants experienced itching and ulceration.11 Furthermore, shallow MN penetration depths (<700 μm) avoid deeper vascular and neural structures while effectively breaching the epithelial barrier, which may explain why topical anesthesia alone was sufficient for some participants. Notably, one participant in the MN + AA group presented with a localized gingival abscess the day after the first session possibly related to localized tissue trauma, microbial ingress in microneedling channels, or local irritant reaction to AA. An overall PROMs were favorable for MN + AA, however, these findings are based on the first session only, consistent with the assessment window used in comparable previous studies,10,11,19 and should be interpreted accordingly. Further,this solitary adverse event also highlights the importance of post-operative monitoring and further safety evaluation of dermatology-based MN pen for gingival use.

From the patient's perspective, color improvement remained high in both groups throughout follow-up, despite incomplete depigmentation in some MN + AA cases and evidence of repigmentation. This suggests that patients may prioritize overall esthetic improvement, procedural comfort, and the minimally invasive treatment over complete pigment elimination. MN + AA showed favorable tolerability and satisfaction despite requiring three sessions, however, this unequal treatment exposure should be considered when interpreting efficacy relative to the single session scalpel depigmentation. Overall, no single technique is universally ideal, and treatment selection should be individualized. Scalpel therapy offers nearly complete cost-effective pigment removal, whereas MN + AA may provide additional gingival augmentation with favorable patient acceptance but requires specialized equipment, and recurring disposable cartridge costs. Thus, treatment modality for gingival depigmentation should be selected considering pigmentation intensity and extent, GT clinician's experience, available resources, and the patient's preferences.

GT was evaluated as an exploratory outcome and has not previously been assessed in MN-based gingival depigmentation trials. Both groups demonstrated a progressive increase in GT over time, reaching significance in MN + AA group at 6 months. The change in the scalpel group likely reflected normal wound healing, whereas the additional gain in the MN + AA group may be attributed to the biological effects of microneedling. Platelet activation, release of growth factors, fibroblast migration, and proliferation, neocollagenesis, and neovascularization may promote collagen induction in gingiva, similar to skin 14. Histologic studies in dermatology have demonstrated increased epidermal thickness and sustained collagen remodeling for up to 6 months following MN, with continued tissue maturation thereafter.13,14 Collagen-promoting effects of AA can further contribute to increased tissue thickness. These results are consistent with recent reports of increased GT following MN-based procedures.11,31 Such additional GT augmentation may be particularly relevant to thin gingival phenotypes, though potential benefit warrants confirmation in future studies supported by histologic and volumetric evaluation.

Constitutional dermal pigmentation is believed to be associated with baseline gingival pigmentation, commonly assessed using the visual Fitzpatrick skin phototype.32 To our knowledge, current study, for the first time explored, an association between baseline dermal melanin pigmentation and gingival pigmentation outcomes at different timepoints. Dermal melanin concentration was assessed objectively using the MILO melanin meter at the malar regions, a standardized facial site representative of visible facial complexion.33 The absence of a significant dermal-gingival (DMMS-GMMS) correlation at any follow-up timepoint, suggests that constitutional pigmentation may not be a major determinant of treatment outcomes. Other factors such as completeness of pigment removal, melanocyte migration from adjacent tissues, wound-healing dynamics, and local inflammatory mediators may play a greater role, although this warrants further investigation.

Strengths of the present study include objective melanin quantification using a melanin meter, combined PP and ITT analyses to enhance the assessment of depigmentation efficacy, documentation of PROMs, and anatomical limitations associated with the MN device. Exploration of GT and dermal-gingival associations further provide an insights beyond depigmentation efficacy alone. Limitations include a short follow-up period, exclusion of participants with missing data, multiple secondary comparisons, and limited validation of the melanin meter for gingival use with occasional challenges in measuring gingival melanin due to its large probe aperture. Possible assessor unblinding cannot be excluded, given visually distinguishable differences between interventions. The absence of AA-only or MN-only arms limit the ability to determine each component's individual contribution to the observed outcomes. Generalizability is limited to systemically healthy, non-smoking individuals with physiologic melanin pigmentation. Although this study has sufficient a priory and post hoc power, the finding should be interpreted with caution and validated in larger samples.

Conclusion

Within limitations of the study, both treatments significantly reduced gingival pigmentation at 1 month. The scalpel technique showed greater improvement in pigmentation intensity and extent and maintained lower pigmentation intensity at 6 months, though both groups showed repigmentation. Both treatments were well tolerated with favorable PROMs. The greater increase in GT observed with MN + AA and the absence of an association between baseline dermal and gingival pigmentation outcomes require confirmation in further studies. Future studies should incorporate longer follow-up and be directed towards developing gingival-specific microneedling devices and adjunctive therapies to delay repigmentation.
