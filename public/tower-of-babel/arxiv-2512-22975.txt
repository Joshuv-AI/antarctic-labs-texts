Computing parameters that generalize interval graphs using restricted modular partitions
Flavia Bonomo-Braberman, Eric Brandwein, Ignasi Sau

Abstract
Recently, Lafond and Luo [MFCS 2023] defined the 𝒢 \mathcal{G} -modular cardinality of a graph G G as the minimum size of a partition of V ⁡ ( G ) V(G) into modules that belong to a graph class 𝒢 \mathcal{G} . We analyze the complexity of calculating parameters that generalize interval graphs when parameterized by the 𝒢 \mathcal{G} -modular cardinality, where 𝒢 \mathcal{G} corresponds either to the class of interval graphs or to the union of complete graphs. Namely, we analyze the complexity of computing the thinness and the simultaneous interval number of a graph. We present a linear kernel for the Thinness problem parameterized by the interval -modular cardinality and an FPT algorithm for Simultaneous Interval Number when parameterized by the cluster -modular cardinality plus the solution size. The interval -modular cardinality of a graph is not greater than the cluster -modular cardinality, which in turn generalizes the neighborhood diversity and the twin-cover number. Thus, our results imply a linear kernel for Thinness when parameterized by the neighborhood diversity of the input graph, FPT algorithms for Thinness when parameterized by the twin-cover number and vertex cover number, and FPT algorithms for Simultaneous Interval Number when parameterized by the neighborhood diversity plus the solution size, twin-cover number, and vertex cover number. To the best of our knowledge, prior to our work no parameterized algorithms ( FPT or XP ) for computing the thinness or the simultaneous interval number were known. On the negative side, we observe that Thinness and Simultaneous Interval Number parameterized by treewidth, pathwidth, bandwidth, (linear) mim-width, clique-width, modular-width, or even the thinness or simultaneous interval number themselves, admit no polynomial kernels assuming 𝖭𝖯 ⊈ 𝖼𝗈𝖭𝖯 / 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 \mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{coNP}/\mathsf{poly} .



Keywords:
thinness, simultaneous interval number, modular cardinality, interval graphs, parameterized complexity.

Introduction

The class of interval graphs
[
30
]
has many useful properties that makes it amenable to fast algorithms for many common problems
[
33
,
34
]
. Thus, many graph parameters were defined aiming at quantifying the distance of a graph to an interval graph in some way, including the
interval number

[
23
]
, the
track number

[
24
]
, the
thinness

[
31
]
, and, more recently, the
simultaneous interval number

[
1
]
, among others. If one of these parameters is small for a class of graphs
\mathcal{G}
, some properties of interval graphs are possibly maintained on
\mathcal{G}
, and therefore some problems that are easy on interval graphs might be easy on
\mathcal{G}
as well. In this work we focus on two of these parameters: the thinness and the simultaneous interval number. In particular, we will be interested in computing them efficiently. We defer their formal definitions to
Section

2
. We now proceed to briefly motivate the study of these graph parameters.

Thinness.

The thinness of a graph was introduced by
31
to solve the
Maximum Independent Set
problem in polynomial time when a suitable representation of the input graph is given. The concept of thinness can be seen as a generalization of the characterization of interval graphs as those that admit an ordering
<
of the vertices such that for every triple of vertices
u<v<w
, if
(u,w)
is an edge, then
(v,w)
is also an edge
[
18
]
. The thinness of a graph is the minimum size of a partition of the vertices such that every triple of vertices
u<v<w
either meets the condition mentioned above, or
u
and
v
belong to different parts of the partition. Observe that interval graphs are exactly the graphs of thinness one.

Given a suitable representation for a graph with thinness bounded by
t
, many other problems including
Clique
were shown to be solvable in
XP
-time parameterized by
t
, while problems like
Capacitated
k
-Coloring
and
List
k
-Coloring
are
XP
parameterized by
t
plus the number of color classes
[
31
,
6
,
13
,
5
]
. It is worth noting that
Chromatic Number
is
NP
-complete for
t=2
, even if the representation is given
[
7
]
.

Simultaneous interval number.

The simultaneous interval number was recently introduced by
1
. It generalizes the class of interval graphs by assigning not only an interval to each vertex, but also a subset of
labels
from the set
\{1,\dots,d\}
, with
d\in\mathbb{N}
. The representation must be such that two vertices are adjacent in the graph if and only if their intervals
and
their sets of labels intersect. The simultaneous interval number is the minimum number
d
for which there exists such a representation.

In their work,
1
show that when given a suitable representation for a graph with simultaneous interval number bounded by
d
,
Clique
is
FPT
parameterized by
d
, and
Independent Set
and
Dominating Set
are
FPT
parameterized by
d
plus the solution size. On the other hand,
Chromatic Number
is
NP
-complete for
d=2
, even if the representation is given.

Unfortunately, determining the value of these two parameters is
NP
-hard on general graphs
[
1
,
35
]
. This prompts the investigation of algorithms for these two parameters on restricted classes of inputs. For a start,
Thinness
was solved in polynomial time for cographs by
5
and for trees by
8
. Some classes of graphs with bounded thinness, where a bounded-size consistent solution can be obtained in polynomial time, were identified by
9
. On the other hand, not much is known about the complexity of
Simultaneous Interval Number
on restricted graph classes.

Our contribution.

Given the inherent hardness discussed above, a natural approach is to search for parameterized algorithms for the
Thinness
and
Simultaneous Interval Number
problems, which ask to find the value of the corresponding parameters for a given graph. In particular, previous to this work there were no known
FPT
or even
XP
algorithms for computing any of the two parameters.

The field of parameterized complexity focuses on finding fast algorithms for problems when a natural number that is part of the input, the
parameter
, is small. In particular, one focus is on finding
XP
algorithms, which are algorithms that take time
f(k)\cdot n^{g(k)}
for some computable functions
f
and
g
, where
k
is the parameter and
n
is the size of the input. Another focus is on
FPT
algorithms, which instead take time
f(k)\cdot n^{c}
for some constant
c
. One way of finding an
FPT
algorithm for a problem is to find a
kernel
for it. A
kernelization
(or
kernel
) for a problem
Q
is a polynomial-time algorithm that transforms each input
(x,k)
into an
equivalent
instance
(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})
such that
\lvert x^{\prime}\rvert,k^{\prime}\leq g(k)
for some computable function
g
. We say that
g(k)
is the
size
of the kernel. Finding a kernel for a problem is equivalent to proving that it is

[
17
, Lemma 2]
. Kernels of polynomial size are of particular interest due to their practical applications.

The existence of
FPT
or even
XP
algorithms for
Thinness
or
Simultaneous Interval Number
parameterized by the solution size (the numbers
t
and
d
, respectively) is open. These would be the most natural parameterizations to consider, but unfortunately, these algorithms seem difficult to obtain. We thus focus instead on parameterizing the problems by other relatively natural parameters based on partitions of the input graph into modules.

The concept of

Graphs.

All graphs in this work are finite, undirected, and have no loops or multiple edges.

A graph

, if

A

Parameterized complexity.

The field of
parameterized complexity
seeks to find efficient algorithms for problems when the input is accompanied by a number that bounds a
parameter
of the input. Algorithms that take polynomial time when that parameter is fixed are of special interest. Formally, a
parameterized problem
is a language
Q\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times N
, and such a problem is
fixed-parameter tractable
(
FPT
) if there is an algorithm that decides membership of an instance
(x,k)
in time
f(k)\cdot\lvert x\rvert^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
for some computable function
f
. A problem is
slice-wise polynomial
(
XP
) if there is an algorithm that decides membership of an instance
(x,k)
in time
f(k)\cdot\lvert x\rvert^{f(k)}
for some computable function
f
.

Kernelization.

A
kernelization
(or
kernel
) for a parameterized problem
Q
is a polynomial-time algorithm that transforms each instance
(x,k)
into an instance
(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})
such that:

•

(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in Q
if and only if
(x,k)\in Q
, and

•

\lvert x^{\prime}\rvert,k^{\prime}\leq g(k)
for some computable function
g
.

We say that
g(k)
is the
size
of the kernel.

3
Reducing modules without changing the thinness

We begin by showing that if a sufficiently large group of vertices have the same neighborhood, some of them can be removed from a graph without affecting its thinness. This will be key in proving the kernelizations results for
Thinness
in
Section

5
, and when showing the linear time algorithm to compute the
interval
-modular cardinality in
Theorem

7
.

We state here a result by
10
on the thinness of a graph after contracting a complete module.

Lemma 2

(
[
10
, Theorem 35]
)
.

Let
H
be a module of a graph
G
, and
G|_{H}
be the graph obtained by contracting
H
into a vertex. If
H
is complete, then
{\textsf{{thin}}}(G)={\textsf{{thin}}}(G|_{H})
.

A similar lemma holds for modules that induce interval graphs that are not complete.

Lemma 3
.

Let
H
be a module of a graph
G
such that
G[H]
is an interval graph and
H
is not complete. Let
G^{\prime}
be the result of removing from
G
all vertices belonging to
H
except for two nonadjacent vertices. Then
{\textsf{{thin}}}(G)={\textsf{{thin}}}(G^{\prime})
.

Proof.

As
G^{\prime}
is an induced subgraph of
G
, we have
{\textsf{{thin}}}(G)\geq{\textsf{{thin}}}(G^{\prime})
. It remains to prove
{\textsf{{thin}}}(G)\leq{\textsf{{thin}}}(G^{\prime})
.

Starting from a consistent solution
(<^{\prime},S^{\prime})
on
{\textsf{{thin}}}(G^{\prime})
classes of the vertices of
G^{\prime}
, we will construct a consistent solution
(<,S)
of the vertices of
G
on no more than
{\textsf{{thin}}}(G^{\prime})
classes, and thus prove
{\textsf{{thin}}}(G)\leq{\textsf{{thin}}}(G^{\prime})
.

Let
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
denote the two remaining vertices belonging to
H
in
G^{\prime}
such that
h_{1}<^{\prime}h_{2}
. Take
<_{H}
to be an ordering of the vertices of
H
consistent with the partition
\{H\}
, which exists because
H
is an interval graph. We construct solution
(<,S)
by removing vertex
h_{2}
from
(<^{\prime},S^{\prime})
and replacing the vertex
h_{1}
in
<^{\prime}
with all the vertices in
H
in the order defined by
<_{H}
. Additionally, we assign all the vertices of
H
to the same class as
h_{1}
in
S^{\prime}
.

We claim that
(<,S)
is a consistent solution for
G
. We will prove this by analyzing every possible triple
u<v<w
of vertices in
G
, and showing that they form a consistent triple. We distinguish several cases
depending on which of the three vertices belong to

Theorem 4
.

An optimal
cluster
-modular partition of a graph

Proof.

First, we compute the modular decomposition tree
T

[

32

37
]
. Then, for each node
G_{t}\in T
(which is an induced subgraph of
G

cluster
-modular partition
S_{t}
of the vertices of
G_{t}
depending on the type of node
G_{t}
. Let
\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\{G_{1},\dots,G_{h}\}
be the children of
G_{t}
, if there are any.

•

Leaf
: The only class in
S_{t}
is formed by the only vertex in
G_{t}

•

Parallel or Series
: Let
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
be the children of
G_{t}
that are complete graphs. We define

, where

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigcup_{G_{i}\in\mathcal{K}}V(G_{i})

\displaystyle\mathsf{tail}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigcup_{G_{i}\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{K}}S_{t_{i}}.

In other words, we take the vertices of the child nodes

for which

is a complete graph, and join them into one class to build the first class of

. Then, we add the classes of all the other partitions as they are.

As shown by

, the only modules present in

. That is to say,

has no modules that contain some but not all vertices of some

, and some but not all vertices of some

Let us see first that this is a valid

and every element of
\mathsf{tail}
are modules. If

is a parallel node,

is a union of complete graphs, meaning, a cluster. If

is a series node,

is itself a complete graph, and so it is also a cluster. The elements of
\mathsf{tail}
are clusters, as they are part of the

G_{t}
. Moreover, all vertices of
G_{t}
are present in some set of the partition, and thus

is a valid
cluster
-modular partition.

Next, let us see that it is optimal. Suppose there is an optimal partition

of the vertices of
G_{t}
that uses fewer classes than

. As noted before, the classes of

must each be either a union of elements of
\mathcal{C}
, or submodules of elements of
\mathcal{C}
.

Suppose that some class

of

is a union of more than one element of
\mathcal{C}
, and that one of these elements

is not complete. We distinguish two cases:

–

G_{t}
is a parallel node
: Every element of
\mathcal{C}
is a connected graph. As

is not complete, it cannot be part of a cluster.

–

G_{t}
is a series node
: Let

be another element of
\mathcal{C}
present in

. Every vertex of

is adjacent to every vertex of

. As

is not complete, there are two nonadjacent vertices

and

that are each adjacent to a vertex

cannot be part of the same cluster.

These two cases show that the previous scenario cannot happen, and so every class of

is either a union of elements of

that are complete graphs, or submodules of elements of
\mathcal{C}
. Moreover, there cannot be more than one class of

that is a union of elements of

. Also, there cannot be a proper submodule of an element

of

that is a complete graph as a class in

to reduce the size of

that are complete graphs defines a class in

, the classes that have some vertex of a given graph

must form a partition of the vertices of

-modular partition of

. Partition

then contains an optimal cluster partition of each

, and necessarily has the same amount of classes as

.

•

Prime
: We define
S_{t}
to be the union of
S_{i}
for every

.

As shown by
21
, the proper modules of

are exactly the submodules of the associated graphs of children of

. Thus, an optimal
cluster
-modular partition of

must contain an optimal

-modular partition of every child

, and so

is optimal.

To achieve linear time on this algorithm, the partitions

are not actually computed. Instead, we set a flag on each child

of a series or parallel node

such that

belongs to

. Recognizing these children can be done with a simple bottom-up traversal of the tree that marks each node whose associated graph is complete (i.e., a parallel node whose children are leaves). Finally, a top-down traversal of the tree allows us to propagate to which class each of the leaf nodes belongs. These operations can be performed in time linear in the number of nodes in the tree, and thus this algorithm runs in linear time.
∎

We present two lemmas that will be useful to compute an optimal

Lemma 5

(Thinness of the union
[
5

)
.

Let

and

{\textsf{{thin}}}(G_{1}\cup G_{2})=\max\{{\textsf{{thin}}}(G_{1}),{\textsf{{thin}}}(G_{2})\}

Lemma 6

(Thinness of the join

Let

and

be graphs. If

In particular,

Theorem 7
.

An optimal

-modular partition of a graph

Proof.

The proof follows the structure of the one for

•

Leaf

•

Parallel

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigcup_{G_{i}\in\mathcal{I}}V(G_{i})

Note that

–

All the classes induce interval graphs, as

–

Each class is a module, a
C_{1}

Let us see that

is minimum. Again, as shown by

, the only modules of

are unions of children of

of

1.

by

2.

Partition

•

Series
: We define

as

, where

–

contains all vertices belonging to complete children of

–

If

.

By

induces an interval graph. Every class in

. On the other hand, notice that all classes induce modules of

is an

-modular partition.

Take another

of

.

As said earlier, the modules of

are unions of children of

, or submodules of children of

. For a module that is a join of two graphs to be an interval graph, it must be the join of a complete graph and an interval graph by

. Thus, if two or more children of

are joined in a class of

.

Furthermore,

is forced to contain

contains no more classes than

.

.

is not an interval graph, the partition must be the union of

It remains to show how to determine if
G_{t}
is an interval graph or not. In principle, one could run a linear-time algorithm that decides if a graph is interval or not on

, for example the one by
11
. The issue is that running a linear time algorithm for each of the prime nodes of the modular decomposition yields a runtime complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
, which is more than what we want.

To remedy this, we will utilize the lemmas presented in
Section

3
to reduce the number of vertices in

without changing the thinness. First, if any one of the children of

is not an interval graph, then, as the thinness is a hereditary property, graph

is also not an interval graph. Otherwise, we create a graph

by contracting every child that is a complete graph into a single vertex, and contracting every other child that is an interval graph into two nonadjacent vertices. By

and

, graph

is an interval graph if and only if

is an interval graph. Thus, we finish by running a linear-time algorithm to decide if

is an interval graph or not.

As the number of nodes in a modular decomposition tree is

, and the number of vertices in

is at most twice the number of children of

, taking into account that we need to store a flag for each node that signals if a node is a complete graph or not.∎

We are now ready to present the kernels for

Theorem 8
.

For every graph

{\textsf{{thin}}}(G)\leq 2\cdot\text{${\textsf{{interval}}}$-${\textsf{{mc}}}$}(G)
. In addition,

admits a linear kernel when parameterized by the

Proof.

We reduce an instance

of

is the

Lemma 12
.

For every graph

with twin-cover number

\text{${\textsf{{cluster}}}$-${\textsf{{mc}}}$}(G)\leq 2^{k}+k

Let

We first put each vertex

by the set of vertices in

that they are adjacent to. As there are

Sets

We first prove that they are modules. By definition, every vertex in

We will now show that if

and

are not adjacent. By definition of the first
