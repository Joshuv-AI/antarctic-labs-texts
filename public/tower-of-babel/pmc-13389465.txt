PIWIL3-piRNA pathway controls rabbit oogenesis and embryogenesis via broad regulation of the transcriptome and proteome
Authors: Yuanyuan Gong, Shuo Shi, Ling Li, Yuqiang Qian, Ting Lu, Zhaoran Zhang, Lichun Jiang, Guohui Liu, Meihua Cui, Shangang Li, Zhanjun Li, Haifan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFemale infertility often arises from oogenic defects, yet the underlying molecular mechanisms remain elusive. The Piwi-piRNA pathway is crucial for gametogenesis, but its role in mammalian female fertility remains unclear, partly due to reliance on mouse models lacking PIWIL3. PIWIL3 exits in most other placental mammals and is highly expressed in human oocytes, but its function remains largely unexplored. Here, we show that rabbit PIWIL3 closely resembles its human counterpart and is the predominant PIWI protein in oocytes. Using CRISPR-Cas9 knockout, we demonstrate that PIWIL3 is essential for female fertility in rabbits, its loss leads to severe defects in oogenesis. Embryos lacking maternal PIWIL3 arrest by the 8-cell stage. Mechanistically, PIWIL3 binds ~18-nucleotide piRNAs, supports piRNA biogenesis, and regulates transcriptomic, proteomic, and transposable element dynamics during oocyte maturation and early embryogenesis. These findings establish PIWIL3 as an essential regulator of female fertility in non-rodent mammals, potentially including humans.Subject terms: Embryogenesis, Piwi RNAs, Oogenesis

Introduction

In humans and other mammals, defects in oocyte number and quality, as well as in early embryogenesis are primary causes of female infertility—a persistent clinical and scientific challenge1,2. The generation of developmentally competent oocytes requires intricately orchestrated molecular events spanning transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and translational regulatory layers3–5. These complex programs precisely coordinate oocyte growth, meiotic maturation, and the subsequent embryonic development following fertilization6–14. Disruption of these regulatory networks can diminish ovarian reserve, impair oocyte quality, and lead to embryonic developmental arrest9–14. Despite significant progress, the master regulators responsible for coordinating multi-level gene expression dynamics during the oocyte-to-embryo transition (OET) remain incompletely understood.

The PIWI-piRNA pathway is a crucial regulator of germline biology, initially recognized for silencing transposable elements (TEs) to safeguard genomic integrity15–20. PIWI proteins bind Piwi-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), forming piRNA-induced silencing complexes (piRISCs) that mediate transcriptional and post-transcriptional gene regulation21–32. Beyond TE repression, this pathway also regulates protein-coding and non-coding RNAs during embryogenesis, tissue regeneration, and even oncogenesis33–41. However, functional diversification among PIWI paralogues has created species-specific regulatory landscapes, complicating generalization across mammals. For instance, mice—the predominant mammalian model—possess three PIWI genes (PIWIL1, PIWIL2, and PIWIL4) that are essential for spermatogenesis but have no detectable role in oogenesis or female fertility, either individually or collectively42–44. However, an additional PIWI gene, PIWIL3, is present in placental mammals but is absent in mice and rats. Recent studies in golden hamsters (Mesocricetus auratus), another rodent species, have revealed that PIWIL1-deficiency causes complete female sterility whereas PIWIL3-deficiency reduces female fertility by affecting early embryogenesis of progeny30,31,45, indicating an important role of PIWIL1 and a largely redundant role of PIWIL3 in female reproduction. Notably, neither PIWIL1 nor PIWIL3 has detectable function in oogenesis; especially, PIWIL3 has little effect even on gene expression and TE activity30,45.

Species-specific differences are further exemplified by piRNA populations within mammalian oocytes. Hamster oocytes harbor three distinct piRNA classes: 22-24 and 28-30 nucleotide (nt) piRNAs bound by PIWIL1 and an 18-20 nt piRNA subset bound by PIWIL330–32,45. By contrast, human oocytes predominantly express 18-21 nt piRNAs, which are primarily bound by PIWIL328,31,46. Correspondingly, PIWIL3 mRNA and protein are highly expressed as the predominant PIWI member in human oocytes27,31,47, whereas PIWIL1 is the principal PIWI in hamster oocytes29,31. Overall, this divergence presents an evolutionary paradox: although PIWIL3 is conserved across mammals, it exhibits only minor functional significance in female rodent reproduction, limiting the direct translation of rodent findings to human biology.

Addressing this translational gap requires identifying a model organism whose PIWI-piRNA dynamics more accurately reflect those in humans. We hypothesized that the phylogenetic proximity of certain species could provide a viable solution. The New Zealand White Rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus) emerged as a promising candidate based on two key factors: (1) Phylogenetic analysis reveals a closer evolutionary relationship between rabbit PIWI subfamily members and humans as compared to rodents. Notably, rabbit PIWIL3 shows a closer evolutionary distance to humans even than that of cattle and pigs, second only to rhesus monkeys (Macaca mulatta) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). (2) Tissue-specific expression profiling reveals a pronounced ovarian enrichment of PIWIL3 in rabbits, mirroring the expression pattern observed for human PIWIL327,31,45,47, whereas PIWIL1 and PIWIL2 remain testis-specific (Fig. 1a). These features position the rabbit as a compelling model to investigate the roles of PIWIL3 in female gametogenesis and early development, bridging the gap between rodent models and human reproductive biology.

Using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout and multi-omics analyses, we demonstrate the critical and pleiotropic roles of PIWIL3 during rabbit oogenesis and early embryogenesis. Our findings reveal that PIWIL3 orchestrates transcriptomic and proteomic reprogramming, is indispensable for piRNA biogenesis, and dynamically regulates TE expression throughout the OET. These insights significantly expand our understanding of PIWIL3 function beyond established rodent models and provide a comprehensive framework to investigate PIWI-piRNA pathway dysregulation in human female infertility.

Results

PIWIL3 is expressed in growing oocytes and early embryos

To investigate the role of PIWIL3 in rabbits, we first analyzed its expression across various rabbit tissues using quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR). PIWIL3 was specifically expressed in the oocyte, whereas the other three PIWI genes exhibited broader expression patterns, detected not only in oocytes but also in the testis and several other tissues (Fig. 1a, b). This finding underscores a unique role for PIWIL3 during rabbit oogenesis.

We next cloned ovarian PIWIL3 cDNAs and characterized their sequences, identifying four distinct PIWIL3 protein isoforms expressed in rabbit ovaries (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). These isoforms exhibited variations in their N-terminal regions compared to the sequences predicted by the Ensembl and UniProt databases. Isoform 1, the longest variant, had a coding sequence (CDS) comprising 20 exons and encoded a 1014-amino-acid protein that featured nine repeats of the nucleotide sequence encoding the GAAWERG motif. Isoform 2 consisted of a CDS of 19 exons encoding an 810-amino acid protein. Similarly, Isoform 3 also contained 19 exons but lacked a substantial portion of exon 3 found in Isoform 1, resulting in a shorter protein of 762 amino acids. Isoform 4 was the shortest, with a CDS of 13 exons encoding a protein of 537 amino acids.

To further characterize PIWIL3 protein expression and localization during rabbit ovarian development, we generated mouse monoclonal antibodies targeting the PIWIL3 protein (see Methods). Immunostaining demonstrated initial detection of PIWIL3 in ovarian tissues from 30-day-old rabbits, with PIWIL3-positive cells peaking in number at 60 days (Fig. 1c, d). Additionally, co-localization analyses with the germ cell marker VASA revealed specific expression of PIWIL3 in the cytoplasm of growing oocytes within primary and secondary follicles (Fig. 1e, f). Moreover, co-localization studies with the mitochondrial marker Apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF) showed abundant localization of PIWIL3 protein to mitochondria (Fig. 1g).

We then analyzed the mRNA and protein expression patterns of PIWIL3 and other PIWI genes throughout oocyte maturation stages (germinal vesicle (GV) to metaphase II (MII)) and during early embryogenesis, from the 1-cell (1 C) to 8-cell (8 C) stages (Fig. 1h, i). While the PIWIL3 mRNA level did not significantly exceed those of other PIWI genes in oocytes and early embryos, mass spectrometry analysis revealed substantially higher expression of the PIWIL3 protein relative to other PIWI proteins in GV-stage oocytes. Importantly, upon entry into germinal vesicle breakdown (GVBD), PIWIL3 became the sole PIWI protein detected in oocytes (Fig. 1i). This exclusive presence of PIWIL3 persisted through the MII stage and early embryogenesis, indicating a robust translational repression of all PIWI family mRNAs except PIWIL3 during these developmental periods.

Considering previous reports highlighting significant phosphorylation of PIWIL3 protein in golden hamster MII oocytes29, we treated rabbit MII oocyte lysates with calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase followed by SDS electrophoresis and western blotting to detect any potential down-shift of PIWIL3 due to dephosphorylation. However, the position of the PIWIL3 protein band remained unchanged at approximately 110 kDa (Fig. 1j). These data suggest that Isoform 1 is the predominant variant expressed in rabbit oocytes and that it is not detectably phosphorylated at the MII stage (Fig. 1j).

PIWIL3-null rabbits show oogenic and maternal-effect defects

To investigate the role of PIWIL3 in rabbit female reproduction, we generated two PIWIL3-knockout lines (PIWIL31/1 and PIWIL32/2) using CRISPR-Cas9 targeting exon 5 (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. 2a–e). Both homozygous mutant females were completely sterile. Despite successful copulation with wild-type males, pregnancy did not occur (Fig. 2b, c). Additionally, PIWIL3+/1 females showed reduced litter sizes when mated with PIWIL3+/- or PIWIL3-/- males, whereas PIWIL3+/2 females did not (Fig. 2c). This strain-specific PIWIL3 dosage effect is potentially due to genetic background differences.

To identify ovarian defects in PIWIL3 mutant females, we performed histological analyses of ovarian sections from PIWIL31/1 and PIWIL32/2 rabbits. Ovaries from 7-month-old sexually mature females showed a dramatic reduction in primary, secondary, and antral follicles containing growing oocytes, despite ovarian size and weight comparable to wild-type controls (Fig. 2d, e; Supplementary Fig. 2f, g). Importantly, the loss of PIWIL3 did not affect oogonia numbers at Day 0 or follicle numbers in Day 30, 60, and 120 prepubertal ovaries (Supplementary Fig. 2h–q). These data indicate an adult-specific requirement for PIWIL3 in follicle development and oocyte maturation.

Because PIWIL3 knockout ovaries still produced some mature oocytes, we examined whether these oocytes are capable of embryogenesis. When fertilized with wild-type sperm, the resulting embryos exhibited delayed development and asynchronous divisions, arresting variably between 2-8 cell stages (Fig. 2f, g). In addition, they appeared to suffer from extensive DNA double-stranded breaks, as indicated by a drastic increase in γH2AX signals (Fig. 2h). Most embryos had perished by 96 hours post-coitus (h.p.c.), when most wild-type embryos had reached the morula stage; all were dead by 120 h.p.c., when most wild-type embryos had developed into blastocysts (Fig. 2f, g). Hence, the maternal PIWIL3 is essential for early embryogenesis.

In contrast, PIWIL3-knockout males displayed normal testicular morphology, histology and fertility (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 2r, s), indicating a female-specific requirement for PIWIL3 in rabbits.

PIWIL3 regulates oocyte and zygotic mRNA reprogramming

Given the essential role of rabbit PIWIL3 in oogenesis and early embryogenesis, we examined whether PIWIL3 regulates transcriptomic changes occurring during OET. Using Smart-seq3, we profiled transcriptomes of single oocytes and early embryos from the GV to the 16 C stages.

In samples from PIWIL3+/+ or PIWIL3+/− females, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed stage‑specific transcriptome remodeling. In oocytes, the largest shift occurred at the GV‑to‑GVBD transition and a smaller shift at the GVBD-to-MII transition. Because transcription is minimal during meiotic resumption, differences between the GV and GVBD stages are expected to primarily reflect post-transcriptional remodeling of maternal mRNAs, including alterations in mRNA stability and poly(A) tail length during the GV-GVBD transition. Our Smart-seq3 data, generated using oligo(dT) priming, specifically capture the abundance of polyadenylated transcripts, thereby reflecting the outcome of these post-transcriptional regulations. In embryos, transcriptomes remained relatively stable from MII through the 4 C stage but underwent two major phases of changes at the 4C-to-8C and 8C-to-16C transitions, indicating zygotic genome activation (ZGA) and a decline of maternal mRNAs after the 4 C stage (Fig. 3a).

By contrast, PIWIL3-deficient oocytes exhibited a near-complete failure of transcriptomic reprogramming at the GV-to-GVBD transition. Transcriptomes from mutant GVBD oocytes clustered with GV-stage profile but did not advance to the GVBD stage of controls (Fig. 3a). This defect persisted, as MII mutant oocytes retained transcriptomic features of control GVBD oocytes, indicative of severe developmental delay. Furthermore, maternal-PIWIL3-deficient 8 C embryos clustered with control 4 C embryos. Pseudotime analysis also revealed a highly disorganized trajectory in PIWIL3-deficient samples, in contrast to the continuous and orderly developmental trajectory from GV to 16 C in controls (Fig. 3b). Together, these data indicate that PIWIL3 is required for maternal transcript remodeling during oocyte maturation and for the subsequent major ZGA of 8 C embryos.

To define PIWIL3-dependent transcriptomic program, we applied weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and identified eight modules (Fig. 3c). Two GV-expressing modules (turquoise and red) are normally silenced in GVBD and later stages, but failed to do so in PIWIL3-deficient GVBD and MII oocytes (Fig. 3c), indicating that the mutant oocytes are arrested at GV stage and that PIWIL3 is required to silence GV transcriptome for oocyte maturation to the GVBD (i.e. meiotic resumption) stage. These modules include cytoplasmic and mitochondrial ribosomal genes involved in protein transport, metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, and piRNA processing (Fig. 3c–e; with representative genes in Fig. 3g; Supplementary Fig. 3a, b), indicating the clearing of these mRNAs is integral to meiotic resumption.

Conversely, GVBD-expressing (green), MII-expressing (purple), and maternal clearance (brown) modules failed to be expressed timely in their corresponding stages in PIWIL3-deficient oocytes (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Specifically, the maternal clearance module is so named because it normally exhibits a maternal-clearance pattern: it is activated at the GVBD stage, maintained through the MII and 1 C stages, declines from the 2 C to 4 C stages, and is largely eliminated by the 8 C stage. However, in PIWIL3 mutants, its expression was delayed, beginning at the MII stage, peaking at the 1 C stage, and persisting through the 8 C stage—the most advanced stage we examined, rather than being downregulated after fertilization (Fig. 3c). The meiotic (GVBD and MII) modules comprise meiosis-associated transcripts enriched for transcription/chromatin regulation and cell-cycle/oocyte-meiosis function, consistent with their functions in meiosis (Fig. 3c, d, f, with representative genes in Fig. 3h). The maternal clearance module is enriched for genes annotated in RNA polymerase II transcription, cell division, and centrosome-related processes, suggesting that excessive expression of these genes in early embryos interferes with maternal-to-zygotic transition (MZT).

Overall, PIWIL3 is required during oocyte maturation for the stage-appropriate increase of 2345 genes (green, purple, brown modules) and decrease of 5921 genes (turquoise, red modules) around GVBD. Differential expression analysis (DESeq2) further confirmed that the GV-to-GVBD transition represents the most dynamic transcriptomic change (Supplementary Fig. 3a). In controls, 2243 transcripts increased and 2736 decreased at GVBD versus GV (FDR ≤ 0.05, fold change ≥ 2). In mutants, 83% of transcripts that normally are increased at GVBD failed to do so, and 80% of transcripts that normally are decreased failed to be down-regulated (Fig. 3d).

The three embryonic modules (yellow, pink, and blue) correspond to zygotically expressed, embryo-specific transcripts (Fig. 3c). These modules are enriched for pathways related to translation, mRNA splicing, oxidative phosphorylation, and cell division. Among these modules, the yellow module increased from the 1 C to 4 C stages, indicating that minor ZGA begins as early as the 1 C stage, with 1060 genes activated during this process. In contrast, the pink and blue modules were predominantly expressed only in 8 C and 16 C embryos, respectively, suggesting that major ZGA proceeds in two distinct phases. The first phase occurs at the 8 C stage and is characterized by the induction of a distinct group of 475 genes that are rapidly downregulated by the 16 C stage. The second phase occurs at the 16C stage and is characterised by the activation of a distinct cohort of 2359 genes.

In maternal PIWIL3-deficient embryos, both minor and major ZGA were completely abolished, with only 11 genes activated during major ZGA (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The affected pathways include RNA processing, protein synthesis, and mitochondrial energy metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Thus, PIWIL3 deficiency disrupts post-transcriptional remodeling of maternal mRNAs during oocyte maturation, leading to developmentally incompetent oocytes incapable of supporting ZGA.

PIWIL3 regulates oocyte and zygotic proteomic reprogramming

The dynamic regulation of protein synthesis, accumulation, and degradation is pivotal for oocyte maturation, fertilization, and early embryogenesis3,6,48–51. To test PIWIL3 proteomic dynamics during the OET, we performed stage-resolved, single-cell proteomics on control and PIWIL3-deficient oocytes/embryos, using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS).

In control (including PIWIL3+/+ and PIWIL3+/-) samples, PCA revealed a structured developmental trajectory marked by three major transitions: GV to GVBD, GVBD to MII, and MII to the 1 C stages. In contrast, later stages (1 C to 8 C) showed minimal separation (Fig. 4a). This was further supported by scatterplot analysis (Fig. 4b), which showed widespread upregulation of proteins during the GVBD-to-MII transition and subsequent downregulation from MII to the 1 C stage, highlighting the unique proteomic landscape at MII.

By contrast, PIWIL3-deficient samples showed an aberrant proteomic trajectory. First, the mutant GV oocytes showed a distinct profile between the control GV and GVBD. Second, the mutant GVBD, MII, 1C-8C embryos all collapsed to one cluster (Fig. 4a, b), indicating a failure to undergo the critical proteomic shift starting at the GVBD-to-MII transition. These findings demonstrate that PIWIL3 is indispensable for proper proteomic reprogramming during the OET, particularly around the MII stage.

To further resolve the temporal dynamics of the proteome during OET, we identified four PIWIL3-regulated protein co-expression modules (red, blue, turquoise, and pink) in oocytes (Fig. 4c). The red and blue modules were GV-enriched and sharply downregulated at GVBD, with the blue module partially re-accumulating at MII. Both modules were enriched for proteins in RNA splicing/processing, translational initiation, actin filament bundle assembly, and piRNA processing. In contrast, the pink and turquoise modules peaked at MII and were associated with mRNA splicing, glycolysis, and cell cycle/cell division functions (Fig. 4c). Collectively, these four modules comprise ~30% of detected proteins. PIWIL3 deficiency abolished the expression of most proteins in all four modules in MII oocytes (Fig. 4c), with only 58 of 828 proteins remaining expressed (Fig. 4d; examples in Fig. 4e). These data indicate that PIWIL3 regulates the expression of a broad spectrum of proteins during GVBD-to-MII transition. These proteins are primarily involved in cytoplasmic translation, mRNA processing/splicing, and chromatin remodeling (Fig. 4f), indicating that PIWIL3 enables the buildup of these proteins required for oocyte competence and early embryogenesis.

In addition, we identified a green module whose proteins increased significantly after the 1 C stage, coinciding with the onset of minor ZGA in early embryos. These proteins are likely translated from maternal mRNAs to facilitate ZGA. Maternal PIWIL3 deficiency affected their expression from the 1 C to 8 C stages (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Expectedly, these proteins are involved in diverse cellular processes, from metabolism to DNA repair, consistent with their presumed roles in early embryogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 4c). In summary, our findings establish PIWIL3 as a maternal regulator of proteomic reprogramming during oocyte maturation and ZGA.

PIWIL3 regulates target mRNA at RNA and protein levels

To investigate how PIWIL3 regulates transcriptomic and proteomic reprogramming during the OET, we performed RNA immunoprecipitation followed by mRNA sequencing (PIWIL3-RIP-mRNAseq) on GV to GVBD oocytes to identify direct mRNA targets of PIWIL3. 1,060 mRNAs were bound by PIWIL3 across biological replicates (Fig. 5a). KEGG analysis of these mRNAs revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to the spliceosome, cell cycle, mRNA surveillance, ribosome biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, cellular senescence, and oocyte meiosis (Fig. 5b).

We then examined the dynamics of these transcripts and their protein products during oocyte maturation. As the GV-to-GVBD transition represents the most dynamic window of transcriptomic reprogramming (Fig. 3a–d), we focused on this stage to assess the direct effects of PIWIL3. In control oocytes, numerous PIWIL3-bound mRNAs showed marked changes between GV and GVBD (Fig. 5c; Supplementary Fig. 5a). Transcripts associated with cell division, RNA splicing, mRNA processing, and chromatin organization were significantly upregulated, whereas those related to translation were downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c). In contrast, in PIWIL3-deficient oocytes, ~85% of the PIWIL3-bound mRNAs failed to show significant changes (Fig. 5c; Supplementary Fig. 5a). GO analyses indicated that transcripts failing to increase in mutants were enriched for cell division, RNA processing, and chromatin organization, whereas those failing to be degraded were mainly associated with cytoplasmic and mitochondrial translation (Fig. 5d). These results demonstrate that PIWIL3 directly regulates numerous mRNAs that act upstream in regulatory hierarchies during the GV-to-GVBD transition, positioning PIWIL3 as a key post-transcriptional regulator of transcriptomic reprogramming essential for oocyte maturation.

Since PIWIL3 deficiency blocks the extensive protein accumulation in the MII oocytes (Fig. 4a–d; Supplementary Fig. 4a, b), we investigated to what extent the blockage was due to direct PIWIL3 regulation by analyzing proteins encoded by PIWIL3-bound mRNAs. In controls, these proteins were generally upregulated from GVBD to MII (Fig. 5e). In mutants, however, levels of these proteins remained largely unchanged. These proteins are enriched in splicing factors, transcription factors, translation machinery, and chromatin remodelers (Fig. 5f, g), indicating that PIWIL3 directly controls the proteomic remodeling of many gene expression regulators during oocyte maturation.

Mechanistically, PIWIL3 appears to regulate protein abundance through the control of mRNA stability, translational efficiency, and protein stability. For some targets, reduced protein levels correlated with decreased mRNA abundance (Fig. 5h), indicating regulation of mRNA stability. For others, protein abundance was uncoupled from transcript levels (Fig. 5i), pointing to additional regulation at translational or post-translational levels.

PIWIL3 is essential for piRNA biogenesis

PIWI proteins typically partner with piRNAs to regulate gene expression at multiple levels. To test if this is also the case for PIWIL3, we conducted single-cell small RNA sequencing to profile piRNA dynamics across all stages of oocyte maturation and early embryogenesis (from GV to 8 C) and assessed the impact of PIWIL3 deficiency.

In control samples, 18-nt piRNAs dominated the small RNA landscape of oocytes and early embryos, increasing markedly from GV to MII and remaining abundant through the 8 C stage (Fig. 6a). PIWIL3 loss abolished 18-nt piRNAs and induced a transient 26-nt small RNA population in GV oocytes, which diminished by GVBD and was absent thereafter (Fig. 6a). These results indicate that PIWIL3 is essential for piRNA biogenesis in oocytes and early embryos.

We next analyzed piRNA species across OET stages with or without PIWIL3. In controls, GV oocytes contained equally complex 18-nt and 26-nt piRNA populations, though 18-nt piRNAs were far more abundant (Fig. 6b). In GVBD oocytes, the complexity of the 18-nt population was maintained, whereas 26-nt repertoire sharply declined. By the MII stage, 18-nt piRNA diversity doubled and 26-nt population became nearly undetectable (Fig. 6b), indicating a maturation-driven shift toward 18-nt piRNA biogenesis. These 18-nt piRNAs remained dominant throughout 1 C to 8 C. Upon zygotic genome activation around the 4 C stage, the diversity of both 18-nt and 26-nt piRNA populations significantly declined.

In contrast, PIWIL3-deficient oocytes and embryos contained mostly the 26-nt unique RNAs, with 18-nt population nearly absent (Fig. 6b). Specifically, mutant GV oocytes contained few species of 18-nt piRNAs but a complex population of the 26-nt piRNAs. As oocytes matured to the GVBD stage, the complexity of 26-nt piRNA significantly decreased. Eventually, few species of piRNAs were detected in MII oocytes (Fig. 6b). In maternal PIWIL3-deficient embryos, the 18-nt piRNA species continued to be absent, and the complexity of the 26-nt population gradually declined, paralleling trends observed in control embryos (Fig. 6b). Collectively, these findings confirm that PIWIL3 is essential for piRNA biogenesis and acts as a critical regulator of the entire small RNA landscape during rabbit oocyte maturation and early embryogenesis.

PIWIL3 binds to the 18-nt piRNAs enriched in 5’U

We then investigated whether PIWIL3-bound piRNAs correspond to the abundant 18-nt population and whether PIWIL3 forms complexes with these piRNAs to mediate gene regulation. We sequenced PIWIL3-bound small RNAs from rabbit GV and GVBD oocytes and found that PIWIL3-bound piRNAs predominantly consisted of 18-nt species (Fig. 6c). These piRNAs exhibited a strong 5’ uridine bias (Fig. 6d) but lacked significant adenine enrichment at position 10. This nucleotide signature, together with the predominant presence of PIWIL3 in oocytes and early embryos, indicates that PIWIL3-bound piRNAs are generated through a primary piRNA biogenesis pathway.

To further understand the piRNA biogenesis during oogenesis and early embryogenesis, we examined the expression of other canonical piRNA biogenesis factors. MAEL, ASZ1, TDRKH, TDRD1, TDRD5, DDX4, RNF17, PNLDC1, and HENMT1 were prominently expressed in GV oocytes, in addition to PIWI proteins (PIWIL1, PIWIL2, PIWIL4) (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Notably, PIWIL3 deficiency reduced protein levels of several core factors, including ASZ1, TDRKH, PNLDC1, HENMT1 and GTSF1 (Supplementary Fig. 6b). This reduction likely contributes to the defect in piRNA biogenesis. In addition, both PIWIL1 mRNA and protein were more highly expressed in mutants than in controls. This compensatory effect may account for the elevated abundance of 26-nt piRNAs in PIWIL3-deficient GV oocytes. This effect diminished beyond the GV stage, as PIWIL1 expression was reduced in both control and mutant oocytes after the GV stage.

Genomic mapping revealed that ~70% of PIWIL3-bound piRNAs originated from unannotated regions. Within the remaining sequences, 15.8% aligned antisense and 3.2% sense, respectively, to TEs, including long terminal repeats (LTRs, 65%), LINEs (28%) and SINEs (7%). In addition, 11.8% of PIWIL3-bound piRNAs mapped to protein-coding genes (Fig. 6e), markedly higher than in golden hamsters (3.5%)30. These data imply PIWIL3-piRNA complexes in rabbits may directly regulate the expression of mRNAs in maturing oocytes and early embryos.

PIWIL3 regulation of target mRNA appears to involve piRNA

We next asked whether PIWIL3-associated piRNAs contribute to the regulation of target mRNAs. Among 905 PIWIL3-bound transcripts dynamically regulated during the OET, 64.5% contained regions with perfect complementarity to the first 15 nucleotides (nt) of PIWIL3-bound piRNAs (Fig. 6f). These putative piRNA-targeted transcripts displayed stage-specific regulation: GV-specific mRNAs were down-regulated during the GV-to-GVBD transition; whereas meiosis-specific mRNAs were up-regulated. In addition, maternal clearance transcripts were upregulated during oocyte maturation but downregulated at ZGA. Finally, embryo-specific transcripts were strongly induced during minor and major ZGA (Fig. 6g).

At the protein level, 294 PIWIL3-regulated targets were identified, 60.3% of which are translated from mRNAs harboring perfect complementarity to the first 15 nt of PIWIL3-bound piRNAs (Fig. 6i). Maternal transcripts were mainly regulated at MII, whereas embryo-specific targets exhibited a marked increase at the 2 C stage during minor ZGA (Fig. 6j).

Overall, these findings are consistent with a major role of PIWIL3 in regulating oogenesis and early embryogenesis, which sharply contrasts with hamster oocytes, where PIWIL3 minimally influences mRNA regulation, and PIWIL1-bound piRNAs do not directly target mRNAs30,45.

We then compared piRNA targeting patterns between transcripts altered at the RNA versus protein level. Among PIWIL3-target mRNAs with dynamic changes, putative piRNA binding sites were distributed across 5’ UTRs, CDS, and 3’ UTRs in GV-specific transcripts that were down-regulated during GVBD. However, in meiosis-specific transcripts up-regulated by PIWIL3, putative piRNA binding sites were strongly enriched in 3’ UTRs (56%). In maternal clearance transcripts, nearly all sites (95%) were localized to 5’ UTRs, whereas in embryonic transcripts induced during ZGA, 3’ UTRs predominated (59%) (Fig. 6h). These distinct localization patterns might be indicative of different modes of regulation, since mRNA stability regulation can be achieved throughout the entire length of mRNA while translational regulation is known to involve 5’ UTR and 3’ UTR only.

Consistent with the above hypothesis, in mRNAs showing protein-level regulation, piRNA target sites were consistently enriched in 3’ UTRs (49-69%) across stages, with fewer sites in CDS (16-34%) and 5’ UTRs (17-29%) (Fig. 6k). These dynamic, stage-specific targeting patterns suggest that PIWIL3-piRNA complexes can achieve post-transcriptional regulation at multiple levels.

PIWIL3 regulates stage-specific expression of TEs

Given that PIWI proteins are well known for their role in silencing TEs, we investigated whether PIWIL3 regulates TEs in rabbit oocytes and early embryos. We analyzed SMART-seq3 data spanning from GV oocytes to 8 C embryos from both PIWIL3-deficient and control females; 16 C control embryos were also included to capture post-ZGA stages. PCA of TE expression profiles revealed a clear stage-dependent clustering from GV oocytes to 16 C embryos in control samples (Fig. 7a). In contrast, PIWIL3-deficient samples exhibited a disrupted progression: GV and GVBD oocytes clustered with control GV oocytes, while MII oocytes and embryos from 1 C to 8 C stages clustered tightly with the GVBD-4C cluster in controls (Fig. 7a). This disruption suggests that PIWIL3 ensures the proper progression of stage-specific expression of TEs during oocyte maturation and early embryogenesis.

To further characterize PIWIL3-dependent TE expression, we performed WGCNA and identified four distinct TE expression modules (Fig. 7b). The GV (Blue) module, composed of primarily LINE and some LTR elements (Fig. 7c), was primarily active in GV oocytes. However, in PIWIL3 mutant oocytes, it showed expanded activity at both GV and GVBD stages. The meiotic (Brown) module, enriched for SINE elements (Fig. 7c), displayed progressive expression from GV to MII stages and remained at low levels during the 1 C to 4 C stages (Fig. 7b). However, in PIWIL3 mutant oocytes, it was abnormally restricted to the MII stage at which it was expressed at significantly higher levels than controls (Fig. 7b). These findings indicate that PIWIL3 primarily functions to suppress premature or excessive activation of LINE, LTR, and SINE elements during oocyte maturation.

Two additional modules, the minor ZGA (Yellow) and major ZGA (Turquoise) modules, were activated post-fertilization in control embryos. Specifically, the minor ZGA module, composed primarily of LINE elements followed by SINEs and LTRs, was initially expressed in 1 C embryos and showed a marked increase from the 2 C to 4 C stages (Fig. 7b, c), coinciding with minor ZGA. The major ZGA module was similarly dominated by LINE elements, followed by SINEs, but contained relatively fewer LTRs compared to the minor ZGA module. It exhibited low expression at the 4 C stage and peaked at the 8 C and 16 C stages, coinciding with major ZGA (Fig. 7b, c). In maternally PIWIL3-deficient embryos, however, neither module was activated in embryos (Fig. 7b). Thus, PIWIL3 is required for ZGA-associated activation of LINE, LTR, and SINE elements that differ from those expressed during oogenesis.

Analysis of individual TE families further illustrated the dual effect of PIWIL3 (Fig. 7d, e). Embryo-specific X12_LINE was abnormally expressed in PIWIL3-deficient oocytes but failed to activate in embryos (Fig. 7e). L4_A_Mam, normally low in MII oocytes and high in embryos, became elevated in PIWIL3-deficient MII oocytes but failed to achieve high embryonic expression (Fig. 7e). These findings indicate that PIWIL3 is required for both repressing TEs during oogenesis and their timely activation during embryogenesis. Whether TE activation in early embryos reflects a direct role of PIWIL3, an indirect consequence of impaired oocyte maturation, or a combination of both remains to be determined.

Further analysis of de-regulated TEs revealed that LTR elements were most frequently dis-regulated upon loss of PIWIL3, particularly during embryogenesis (Fig. 7f, g). At the 4 C stage, upregulated TEs were primarily SINE elements (Fig. 7g). Interestingly, LINE elements constituted the majority of downregulated TEs from GVBD through the 4 C stage, while LTRs were the dominant downregulated subtype at the GV and 8 C stages (Fig. 7g, h). Together, these results establish that PIWIL3 exerts dual and stage-specific control over TE expression during rabbit oocyte maturation and early embryonic development, highlighting a unique role for PIWIL3 in the female germline.

PIWIL3 regulation of TEs also appears to involve piRNA

Given the known association between PIWI proteins and piRNAs in TE silencing, we examined whether PIWIL3-bound piRNAs were involved in regulating the differentially expressed TEs. Among all dysregulated TEs in PIWIL3-deficient samples, 67.4% harbored sequences with single-mismatch complementarity to PIWIL3-bound piRNAs within the first 15 nucleotides (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Both sense and antisense PIWIL3-bound piRNAs were broadly distributed across TEs that were either up- or downregulated (Supplementary Figs. 7b–d, 8a–d). Notably, PIWIL3-bound piRNAs were more enriched in TEs that were downregulated in PIWIL3-deficient MII oocytes than in those that were upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 7d). These findings are consistent with the involvement of piRNA in PIWIL3 regulation of TEs, which is in stark contrast to the minimal TE regulation observed in golden hamsters, highlighting the evolutionary plasticity of the piRNA pathway in the mammalian female germline.

Discussion

The role of PIWI proteins in mammalian female reproduction has long been underestimated, largely due to a historical reliance on mouse models. In mice, PIWIL3 is absent, and the loss of other PIWI members only impacts male fertility, leading to a prevailing assumption that the piRNA pathway is dispensable for the mammalian female germline42–44. While recent studies in golden hamsters—another rodent species—revealed that PIWIL1 and PIWIL3 are both expressed in oocytes, PIWIL1 remains the dominant player29,31. In hamsters, PIWIL3 appears to have a secondary role, yet neither gene is required for oogenesis30–32,45.

Our work in rabbits reveals a strikingly different and more stringent requirement for PIWIL3. We demonstrate that PIWIL3 is expressed in the oocytes of primary, secondary, and antral follicles during oogenesis, and is the sole PIWI protein expressed during oocyte maturation in rabbits, serving as an essential architect of the maternal transcriptome. Its loss triggers a profound cascade of failure: beginning with a depleted follicle pool and compromised developmental competence of the oocyte, followed by delayed maternal mRNA clearance and persistent maternal TE transcripts, but failure to activate zygotic TE expression. These transcriptome abnormalities likely represent a mixture of primary consequences of the loss of PIWIL3 and piRNAs, as well as secondary effects arising from compromised oocyte developmental competence. This oocyte-intrinsic dysregulation propagates through the OET, culminating in asynchronized cleavage divisions and developmental arrest variably between the 2-cell and 8-cell stages—the timing of minor and major ZGA in rabbits. These findings suggest that PIWIL3 establishes the molecular foundation in mature oocytes required for early embryonic genome reprogramming, a function far more critical than that observed in rodent counterparts.

A highlight of our work is the remarkable biochemical and functional alignment between the rabbit and human piRNA pathways. The predominant length of PIWIL3-associated piRNAs is similar, though not identical, across species, with rabbit, golden hamster, and human PIWIL3 binding to 18-nt, 19-nt, and 20-nt short piRNAs, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b)28,30–32. Among the three species, golden hamster oocytes not only contain PIWIL3-associated 19-nt short piRNAs, but the longer 23- and 29-nt piRNAs bound by PIWIL1. Our comparative analysis confirms that rabbit PIWIL3‑bound piRNAs closely mirror human oocyte piRNAs (os-piRNAs) in length distribution, sensitivity to NaIO₄ oxidation, and genomic cluster organization (Supplementary Fig. 9c–h). Furthermore, whereas PIWIL3 loss in hamsters does not significantly alter total piRNA complexity due to PIWIL1 compensation30–32, its loss in rabbits leads to a complete collapse of 18-nt piRNA biogenesis from the GVBD stage onward. These parallels support the rabbit as a valuable comparative model for investigating PIWIL3-associated piRNA biology in mammalian oocytes.

Mechanistically, we show that PIWIL3 achieves its oogenic and embryogenic functions by regulating an extensive network of mRNAs and proteins. Unlike the hamster model, where PIWIL3 plays no obvious role in gene expression30,45, rabbit PIWIL3 directly binds to 1,060 mRNAs, regulating the abundance of 905 of these transcripts and 294 of their encoded proteins. These 1,060 mRNAs are involved in cell division, metabolism, and chromatin remodeling (Figs. 5a, 6f, g, i, j). This broad functional spectrum is essential for normal oocyte maturation.

Bioinformatic analyses revealed stage-specific piRNA targeting of PIWIL3-associated mRNAs. For mRNAs regulated by PIWIL3 at the transcript level, piRNAs predominantly target CDSs in GV oocytes, shifting toward 3’UTRs during meiosis, to 5’UTRs during maternal clearance, and again to 3’ UTRs during embryonic stages (Fig. 6g, h). In contrast, for mRNAs regulated by PIWIL3 at the protein level, piRNAs consistently target the 3′ UTRs across all stages (Fig. 6j, k). This dynamic targeting pattern suggests that PIWIL3 employs multiple post-transcriptional mechanisms to coordinate the rapid molecular reprogramming required during OET.

Beyond its role in piRNA biogenesis and gene regulation, our study reveals that PIWIL3 is a critical regulator of TEs. While PIWI proteins are conventionally viewed as constitutive TE silencers, our data suggest a dual requirement: PIWIL3 is necessary for suppressing a vast array of TEs during oocyte maturation, yet its presence is also associated with the timely activation of specific TEs during early embryogenesis (Fig. 7b). Although we cannot yet formally determine whether the embryonic TE activation is a direct effect of PIWIL3 or a secondary consequence of compromised oocyte quality, the persistence of PIWIL3 protein and the high complementarity of its associated piRNAs to dysregulated TEs point toward a complex, stage-dependent regulatory role. This biphasic control may balance the need to maintain genome integrity in the oocyte with the requirement to activate certain TEs to facilitate ZGA. Future strategies capable of uncoupling oocyte and embryonic functions, such as embryo‑specific knockdown, maternal mRNA rescue, and piRNA mimic rescue, will be essential to resolve causality.

Our findings redefine PIWIL3 as a central orchestrator of female fertility, essential for piRNA biogenesis, post-transcriptional gene regulation, and stage-specific TE dynamics, which is in turn required for oocyte maturation and early embryogenesis (Fig. 8). By uncovering these comprehensive roles in a model that closely mirrors human oocyte biology, this work provides a much-needed framework to interpret human infertility cases characterized by oocyte maturation defects or early embryonic arrest. This conceptual advance defines the essential role of the PIWIL3–piRNA pathway in female reproductive success and paves the way for innovative diagnostic strategies.

Animals and ethics statement

All animal studies and experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of ShanghaiTech University (ID: 20220727001) and the Animal Welfare and Research Ethics Committee of Jilin University (ID: SY202306007). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the relevant ethical guidelines for the Care and Use of Experimental Animals. New Zealand white rabbits were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Jilin University (Changchun, China) and Shanghai Jiao Tong University (Shanghai, China). Female rabbits aged 0, 30, 60, and 120 days, as well as adult female and male rabbits, were included in this study. Rabbits were euthanized by intraperitoneal overdose of pentobarbital sodium (≥180 mg/kg), with death confirmed by cessation of respiration and heartbeat.

Generation of PIWIL3-deficient rabbit

One single sgRNA for PIWIL31/1 strain and one pair of sgRNAs for PIWIL32/2 strain were designed to knock out PIWIL3 according to a previous protocol52. Briefly, the gRNA sequences were cloned into pUC57-Simple-gRNA (Addgene ID 51306) vector and transcribed in vitro. To produce SpCas9 mRNA, the PCS2+Cas9 (Addgene ID 122,948) plasmid was linearized with NotI restriction digestion and used as a template for in vitro transcription. Microinjection of pronuclear-stage embryos was performed according to our established method53. Briefly, a mixture of Cas9 (200 ng/µl) and sgRNA (50 ng/µl) mRNA was co-injected into the cytoplasm of pronuclear-stage rabbit zygotes and then transferred into the recipient rabbits. Recipients were allowed to naturally deliver at around day 30 and raise their pups.

Rabbit genotyping

Genomic DNA was isolated from ear clips of newborn rabbits using the QuickExtract DNA Extraction Solution (Lucigen, QE09050) for PCR genotyping, followed by Sanger sequencing. All primers used for detection are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Collection of GV, GVBD, and MII oocytes, zygotes, as well as 2-cell, 4-cell, 8-cell and 16-cell embryos

GV and GVBD oocytes were collected from the ovaries of New Zealand white rabbits with an average body weight of 3 kg. Briefly, ovaries were finely minced in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the tissue was vigorously shaken in 15 mL of digestion buffer (2 mg/ml collagenase type IV (Gibco, 17104019), 2 mg/ml Dispase II (Sigma, D4693) and 50 IU/ml DNase I (Thermo Scientific, EN0523) in DMEM (Gibco, 11995073) plus 10% FBS (ExCell, FSP500) in a 37 °C orbital shaker for 15 min. Oocytes were then transferred into M2 medium (Sigma, M7167) under a dissecting microscope by means of sterile, plastic pipette tips.

MII oocytes, zygotes, 2-cell, 4-cell, 8-cell and 16-cell embryos were collected through surgical oviduct flushing from donors after superovulation treatment. Briefly, female New Zealand white rabbits (6-8 months old) in proestrus were administered six intramuscular injections of 50 IU FSH (NSHF, Follicle Stimulating Hormone for Injection) at 12-hour intervals, followed by a single intravenous injection of 100 IU HCG (NSHF, Chorionic Gonadotrophin for Injection) via the marginal ear vein. Ovulation was induced after HCG administration, either through vaginal stimulation for MII oocyte collection or natural mating for embryo collection. The rabbits were euthanized 18 h post-induction, and oviducts were flushed with 10 mL of Embryo manipulation medium (Medium 199, Hank’s supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum) to collect MII oocytes or pronuclear-stage zygotes. The zygotes were transferred into embryo culture medium (containing EBSS, supplemented with 1% nonessential amino acids and 2% essential amino acids. 0.0146% W/V glutamine, 0.0044% W/V sodium pyruvate and 10% of fetal bovine serum) and cultured at 38.5 °C under 5% CO2. Embryos at the 2-cell, 4-cell, 8-cell, and 16-cell stages were collected at 26, 36, 46, and 70 hours post-coitum, respectively.

For embryos, samples were fixed in Paraformaldehyde Solution (4% PFA) (Adamas Life, Cat#: F8011) for 2 h at room temperature upon being collected from rabbit oviducts. Fixed embryos were washed twice with PBS and then permeabilized with PBST buffer (0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 hour at room temperature. Permeabilized oocytes were blocked with PBST-BSA buffer (PBS containing 3% bovine serum albumin and 0.1% Triton X-100) for 2 hours at room temperature and subsequently subjected to antibody incubation. Oocytes were incubated with γH2AX primary antibodies (1:100, Abcam, Cat#: ab26350) in PBST-BSA overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times with PBST, oocytes were incubated with secondary antibodies and DAPI (1:200, Thermo Scientific, Cat#: 62248) for 2 hours at room temperature. After washing, samples were imaged and processed as described in the “Confocal microscopy image acquisition and processing” section.

Confocal microscopy image acquisition and processing

Fluorescence images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 710 confocal microscope (AxioObserver) equipped with Plan-Apochromat 20×/0.8 M27 and Plan-Apochromat 63X/1.4 Oil DIC M27 objective. For ovarian sections, images were collected at a pixel size of 0.21 × 0.21 μm (2048 ×2048 pixels, 8-bit) using 405 nm, 543 nm, and 633 nm laser lines. Emission was detected at 410-480 nm (DAPI), 548-640 nm (Alexa Fluor 555), and 638-755 nm (Alexa Fluor 647). Laser power was set to 2–3%, and signals were detected using PMT detectors with gain settings of 700–800. Frame acquisition time was 92.94 s with 4x line averaging. For embryos, z-stack images were acquired with a voxel size of 0.21 × 0.21 × 0.76 μm (2048 × 2048 pixels, 8-bit) using 405 nm and 633 nm laser lines. Emission was detected at 410-585 nm (DAPI) and 638-755 nm (Alexa Fluor 647). Z-stack images were acquired with 49–66 optical sections spanning 36.3–49.2 μm, depending on embryo size and focal depth. Laser power was set to 2–3%, with PMT gain values of 800-1000 and 2x line averaging.

All images were acquired under non-saturating conditions. Image processing was limited to linear adjustment of brightness and contrast applied uniformly across the entire image. No nonlinear adjustments were performed. Image analysis was conducted using ZEN (Zeiss) and ImageJ (v1.54 g).

Single-cell mRNA library construction

It was performed according to the previously described Smart-seq3 method with some modifications54. In brief, a single oocyte or embryo was incubated with lysis buffer mix (5% (v/v) Poly-ethylene Glycol 8000, 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100, 0.5 U/µL RNase Inhibitor, 0.5 µM OligodT30VN, 0.5 mM dNTPs) at 72 °C for 10 min to release RNA. After reverse transcription and PCR pre-amplification, cDNA products were purified using the Agencourt Ampure XP beads at a 0.6:1 ratio (Beckman Coulter, Cat#: A63881). Library preparation was performed using 0.5 ng of purified cDNA with the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, Cat#: 15032785), employing Tn5 transposase for tagmentation. The amplified libraries were purified with Agencourt AMPure XP beads at a 0.6:1 (bead: sample) ratio and sequenced in 2 × 150 bp paired-end mode on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform.

Processing of single-cell mRNA sequencing data

The zUMIs pipeline was used to process Smart-seq3 data55. Reads mapped to genes or TEs were counted by supplying either gene annotations or TE annotations in input yaml files. Down-sampled and normalized count tables were reported in dgecounts.rds for each feature type (exons, introns, intron+exon). Differentially expressed genes and TEs meeting the (FDR < 0.05, |fold change | ≥ 1.5) criteria were selected for downstream analysis. Pseudotime analysis was performed by monocle3 (http://cole-trapnell-lab.github.io/monocle3/) with the default parameters.

LC-MS/MS analysis

A total of 20 control samples and 24 PIWIL3-/- samples were analyzed, including GV, GVBD, MII, 1-cell, 2-cell, 4-cell, and 8-cell stage samples (control: n = 3, 3, 3, 3, 3, 2, and 3; PIWIL3-/-: n = 4, 4, 5, 3, 3, 3, and 2, respectively). All samples represented biological replicates. Every single oocyte or embryo was added first with SDT buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6) and then with DTT (Bio-Rad, Cat#: 161-0404) to the final concentration of 40 mM. The samples were mixed at 600 rpm for 1.5 hours at 37 °C to lyse cells. After the samples cooled to room temperature, Iodoacetamide (Bio-Rad, Cat#: 163-2109) was added to the mixture to a final concentration of 20mM to block reduced cysteine residues, and the samples were incubated for 30minutes in darkness. Next, the samples were transferred to the filters (Microcon units, 10 kDa), washed with 100 µl UA buffer (8 M urea, 150 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) three times and then with 100 µl 25 mM NH4HCO3 buffer twice. Finally, trypsin was added to the samples (the trypsin: protein (wt/wt) ratio was 1:50) and incubated at 37 °C for 15–18 h (overnight). The resulting peptides were collected as filtrates, desalted on C18 Cartridges (Empore™ SPE Cartridges MCX, 30 µm, Waters), concentrated by vacuum centrifugation, and reconstituted in 40 µl of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid. The peptide content was estimated by UV light spectral absorbance at 280 nm. Indexed retention time calibration peptides were spiked into the sample. The peptides from each sample were analyzed by Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) connected to a Vanquish Neo system liquid chromatography (Thermo Scientific) in the data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. Precursor ions were scanned at a mass range of 380-980 m/z, MS1 resolution was 240000 at 200 m/z, Normalized AGC Target: 500%, Maximum IT: 5 ms. 299 windows were set for DIA mode in MS2 scanning, Isolation Window: 2 m/z, HCD Collision Energy: 25 ev, Normalized AGC Target: 500%, Maximum IT: 3 ms.

Processing of LC-MS/MS data

DIA data was analyzed with DIA-NN 1.8.1. The protein sequence database was obtained from UniProtKB (Oryctolagus cuniculus, 2024_06_02). Main software parameters were set as follows: enzyme is trypsin, max missed cleavage is 1, fixed modification is carbamidomethyl(C), dynamic modification is oxidation(M) and acetyl (Protein N-term). All reported data were based on 99% confidence for protein identification as determined by false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 1%.

Single-cell small RNA library construction

Small RNA libraries from single oocyte or embryo were prepared following established protocols28 with several optimizations. Briefly, individual specimens underwent thermal treatment at 72 °C for 3 minutes to enhance RNA liberation and structural relaxation. Post 3’-adapter ligation, an optimized enzymatic digestion system (5 U λ exonuclease combined with 25 U 5’ deadenylase) demonstrated superior efficiency compared to conventional RecJf/deadenylase mixtures in eliminating excess adapters. Subsequent 5’-adapter ligation preceded reverse transcription to generate cDNA templates. A two-phase PCR amplification strategy was implemented, utilizing 1 µl of primary amplification product for final library enrichment. Quality-controlled libraries were size-selected through 6% denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, specifically targeting 140-200 bp fragments. Final paired-end sequencing (2×150 bp) was conducted on the MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform.

Small-RNA spike-in information

To normalize between different data sets, six synthetic small-RNA spike-in controls (three unmethylated and three methylated variants) were commercially synthesized (Integrated DNA Technologies) and combined into a mixture as described previously28. A defined quantity (5 × 10-6 pmol) of this spike-in cocktail was introduced into each reaction system during single oocyte/embryo processing. Detailed nucleotide sequences of these synthetic RNA standards were obtained from a previous study28.

Processing of single-cell small RNA sequencing data

Small RNA sequences were processed with TrimGalore (version 0.6.10; http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/) with the default adapter trimming mode to auto-detect adapters in the 150 bp long sequencing reads. A size cutoff using 15 to 42 nt was applied to retain small RNA reads of suitable length. Subsequently, trimmed reads were mapped onto the rabbit genome using Bowtie1 (version 1.2.2) with the parameters -v 0 -n 0 -l 18 -k 1, allowing perfect matches and reporting 1 alignment. Genome-mapped reads were sequentially mapped to the libraries of miRNA, tRNA, rRNA, snRNA, snoRNA, piRNA, and repetitive elements. The reads in each library were normalized to the counts of exogenous spike-ins. The raw read counts for both the spike-ins and the total reads are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Mapping of PIWIL3-bound piRNAs to mRNAs and TEs

PIWIL3-bound piRNAs were mapped to the reverse complementary sequence of differentially expressed mRNAs, proteins, or TEs using Bowtie1 (v1.2.2) with parameters -n 0 -l 15 -a (perfect matches) or -n 1 -l 15 -a (allowing one mismatch).

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was performed on single-cell mRNA, TE and protein expression data using the WGCNA R package (v1.72; https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/WGCNA/index.html). The analysis was conducted with the following parameters: Network type: Signed, Soft-thresholding power (β): 9 for mRNA and TE data, 8 for protein data (determined by scale-free topology criterion with R² > 0.85), Minimum module size: 30 genes, Module merging threshold: 0.25, Dynamic tree cutting with deepSplit = 2, Intramodular connectivity (kME) > 0.8, All reported modules met significance criteria: module-trait correlation |r | > 0.5, P < 0.01.

Source of genome annotations

The annotation of the New Zealand white rabbit genome was downloaded from UCSC genome annotation database (https://genome.ucsc.edu/index.html) for the Apr. 2009 (Broad/oryCun2) assembly of the rabbit genome (oryCun2, Broad Institute oryCun2 (NCBI project 12819, AAGW00000000)). Gene annotations were also downloaded from UCSC. Functional RNA from New Zealand white rabbit was retrieved from the following databases: miRNAs were from genome annotation (Ensembl GTF); tRNAs were from GtRNAdb (https://gtrnadb.ucsc.edu/); snoRNAs and snRNAs were from RNAcentral v17 (https://rnacentral.org); rRNAs were compiled from both genome annotation (Ensembl GTF) and RNAcentral. TE annotations were downloaded from RepeatMasker (https://www.repeatmasker.org/). piRNA clusters were annotated and classified using proTRAC (v2.4.2) based on genome-aligned small RNA reads (15-42 nt) retained after miRNA depletion in MII-stage rabbit oocytes56. Commands for proTRAC were: perl proTRAC_2.4.3.pl -map ${map_files} -genome oryCun2.fa -geneset oryCun2.ensGene.sorted.gtf -repeatmasker oryCun2.RepeatMasker_original -pimin 15 -pimax 34. $mapfile was the mapped output using sRNAmapper (version 1.0.4)57. Repeat-derived small RNA libraries were obtained from the RepeatMasker annotation in UCSC genome browser. Any overlapping clusters were merged into one cluster. piRNA candidates were identified by mapping onto rabbit piRNA clusters.

Genomic annotation of piRNA

piRNA sequences were first mapped to previously defined piRNA clusters using Bowtie with the parameters -v 0 -n 0 -l 18 -a, allowing perfect matches and reporting all possible alignments. The resulting BAM files were converted to BED format, with coordinates adjusted to genomic positions. For each piRNA, a fractional weight of 1/n was assigned to each mapped location, where n is the total number of mapping sites for that sequence.

The weighted BED file was then intersected with various genomic annotation datasets, including RepeatMasker elements, protein-coding genes, lncRNAs, and pseudogenes. If a locus overlapped multiple annotations, it was assigned a feature label based on the following priority: (1) repeat_region_antisense, (2) repeat_region_sense, (3) protein_coding_sense, (4) lncRNA_sense, (5) pseudogenes_sense, and (6) processed_pseudogenes_sense. Additionally, we annotated piRNAs based on specific TE subfamilies and sub-genic regions within protein-coding genes (i.e., CDS, 3′UTR, and 5′UTR), using the same weighted BED approach. For each annotation category, the total count was computed by summing the assigned weights across all mapped piRNAs.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

For each condition, 200 GV/GVBD rabbit oocytes were lysed in RIP lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% (v/v) IGEPAL 630 (SIGMA, I8896), 0.5 mM DTT, cOmpleteTM EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Tablets (MERCK, 4693132001), PhosStop Tablets (MERCK, 4906837001) and SUPER In RNase Inhibitor (Thermo, AM2694)). Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#: 10004D) were washed once with lysis buffer and coupled with 5 µg of homemade anti-PIWIL3 or mouse IgG (Millipore, Cat#: 12-371) antibodies at 4 °C for 4 h. Oocyte lysates were mixed with antibody-coupled beads and rotated gently at 4 °C overnight. The beads were then washed twice with RIP wash buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 200 mM NaCl, 0.05% (v/v) IGEPAL 630 and 0.5 mM DTT) and two times with RIP high wash buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 0.05% (v/v) IGEPAL 630 and 0.5 mM DTT). The RNA-containing supernatant was recovered using a magnetic holder and processed for both single-cell small RNA and smart-seq3 library construction, as described above.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine the sample size. Sample sizes were selected based on prior experience and in accordance with field standards to ensure sufficient statistical power. Data were excluded only in cases of technical failure. High-throughput sequencing and mass spectrometry experiments were conducted in a blinded manner, and samples were processed and analyzed in a randomized order based on genotype-defined group allocation to minimize potential batch effects. All data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1.2). For comparisons between data groups, two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used. Exact P values are shown in the figures. Data were presented as the mean ± SD or mean ± SEM and P < 0.05 was considered significant. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to calculate the regression and correlation between two groups. As indicated in the figure legends, all assays were performed at least three biological replicates unless stated otherwise. Representative micrographs and Western blots shown in figures were repeated three times independently with similar results.
