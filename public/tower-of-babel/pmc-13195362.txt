Ultrasound-assisted extraction of total saponins from Ardisia mamillata Hance and their effects on the gut microbiota of mice fed a higher-fat and -sugar diet
Authors: Jinhua Shao, Junyang Liu, Jiaxin Huang, Yuqing Zhu, Changhui Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArdisia mamillata Hance is a traditional Chinese medicinal plant rich in saponins and shows considerable potential for application in functional foods. However, systematic investigations into efficient extraction strategies and the biological activities of its constituents remain limited. To address this gap, the present study optimized an extraction process for total saponins from Ardisia mamillata Hance and evaluated their regulatory effects on gut microbiota dysbiosis induced by a high-fat, high-sugar diet in mice. Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE), combined with a Box–Behnken response surface design, was employed to optimize extraction parameters. The optimal conditions were determined to be an ethanol concentration of 71% (v/v), an ultrasonic time of 31 min, an ultrasonic temperature of 61 °C, and a solid–liquid ratio of 1:32 (g/mL). Under these conditions, the yield of total saponins reached (27.52 ± 0.16) mg/g, demonstrating superior extraction efficiency compared with conventional ethanol heating methods. In a diet-induced mouse model, administration of total saponins from Ardisia mamillata Hance partially inhibited body weight gain and alleviated excessive adipose accumulation, hepatocellular injury, and lipid metabolism disorders associated with the high-fat, high-sugar diet. Furthermore, the saponins contributed to the restoration of intestinal homeostasis by increasing the relative abundance of Muribaculaceae and Alloprevotella, enhancing short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, reducing the abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136 and Lactobacillus, and modulating the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio. Overall, this study not only establishes an efficient extraction protocol for total saponins from Ardisia mamillata Hance but also highlights their potential to mitigate metabolic disorders through modulation of the “gut microbiota–SCFAs” axis. These findings provide a scientific foundation for the development of Ardisia mamillata Hance as a functional food ingredient or a promising prebiotic candidate.Keywords: Ardisia mamillata hance, Gut microbiota, Total saponins, Ultrasound-assisted extraction

Introduction

With ongoing shifts in dietary patterns, high-fat diets have emerged as a major driver of hepatocellular steatosis, which may progress to metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) (Man et al., 2023) and increase the risk of obesity (Xu et al., 2024). Increasing evidence indicates that the gut microbiota, along with its collective genome, plays a pivotal role in nutrient absorption, energy homeostasis, and lipid storage (Li et al., 2019). As a key determinant of host health, the intestinal microbiota is intricately involved in immune regulation and the development of various diseases (Chen et al., 2023). Under physiological conditions, the gut microbiome exists in a dynamic state of equilibrium. However, microbial dysbiosis disrupts both its composition and functional capacity, thereby impairing normal physiological processes and contributing to disease onset (Ding et al., 2022). In this context, plant-derived bioactive compounds have attracted considerable attention for their potential to modulate gut microbiota and ameliorate metabolic disorders. For example, polysaccharides have been shown to partially restore microbial balance (Shi, 2023); saponins derived from quinoa husks alleviate hyperlipidemia in mice through modulation of gut microbial communities (Yu et al., 2023); total flavonoids extracted from hawthorn leaves reduce blood lipid levels in hyperlipidemic rats by influencing both lipid metabolism and microbiota composition (Hu et al., 2022); and berberine increases the relative abundance of the beneficial bacterium Akkermansia while decreasing the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio, thereby regulating bile acid metabolism and exerting lipid-lowering effects (Jin, 2020). In addition, several medicinal plants, including rhubarb, Polygonum cuspidatum (Hu Zhang), and Alisma species, have been reported to remodel gut microbial structure, promote the growth of beneficial bacteria, and restore microbial homeostasis, collectively contributing to improvements in lipid metabolism disorders (Liu et al., 2023).

Ardisia mamillata Hance, a species belonging to the genus Ardisia within the family Myrsinaceae (Ruxin, 2022), is known by several vernacular names, including maogaogang, hongdan, dwarf tea, shanzhupa, and hongzhantan (Ling et al., 2019a). It is widely distributed across multiple regions of China, including Guangdong, Fujian, Sichuan, Guangxi, Hunan, and Yunnan provinces (Tuo, 2013). The entire plant possesses medicinal value and, according to traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) theory, is associated with the Lung, Liver, Kidney, and Gallbladder meridians. In traditional and folk medicine, A. mamillata is commonly used for its therapeutic properties, such as clearing heat and promoting diuresis, activating blood circulation to achieve hemostasis, and facilitating tissue regeneration through the removal of necrotic material. Clinically, it has been employed in the treatment of conditions including pulmonary tuberculosis with hemoptysis, hepatitis, and cholecystitis (Lin et al., 2005). Modern phytochemical investigations have demonstrated that A. mamillata contains a wide range of bioactive constituents, including alkaloids (Ling et al., 2007), polysaccharides (Ling and Zeng, 2007), flavonoids (Lou et al., 2016), and saponins (Ling et al., 2019b). Among these, saponins are considered the principal pharmacologically active components. Saponins represent a structurally diverse class of naturally occurring compounds widely distributed in plants and are known to exhibit multiple biological activities, including anticancer, anti-inflammatory, anti-allergic, antileukemic, antiviral, hypoglycemic, and cardioprotective effects (Jun et al., 2024; Navarro del Hierro et al., 2018a). In addition, saponins have been reported to inhibit digestive enzyme activity (Zheng et al., 2007), regulate appetite (Karu et al., 2007), modulate lipid metabolism and energy expenditure (Han and Zhang, 2021), and alter the composition of the gut microbiota (Lee et al., 2015). Despite these advances, systematic studies investigating both the optimized extraction of saponins from A. mamillata and their potential regulatory effects on gut microbiota remain lacking.

The efficient extraction of total saponins from Ardisia mamillata Hance represents both a key research objective and a prerequisite for subsequent pharmacological investigations. Currently, a variety of techniques are employed for the extraction of plant-derived saponins, including alkaline extraction, acid extraction, hot water extraction, microwave-assisted extraction (MAE), and enzyme-assisted extraction (EAE) (Mo et al., 2025). Among these approaches, ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) offers several notable advantages. Its cavitation effect disrupts plant cell structures, thereby enhancing the release of intracellular bioactive compounds, while simultaneously reducing extraction time and energy consumption. These features make UAE a highly efficient method for isolating plant-derived bioactive constituents (Zheng et al., 2024). In the present study, whole A. mamillata plants were used as the raw material, and UAE combined with a Box–Behnken response surface design was employed to optimize the extraction conditions. In parallel, male C57BL/6 mice were administered total saponins derived from A. mamillata via oral gavage to evaluate their effects on gut microbiota composition. This experimental design aimed to assess the potential of A. mamillata saponins in modulating gut microecology and in the prevention or mitigation of obesity. Overall, this study not only provides a scientific basis for the efficient extraction and utilization of A. mamillata resources but also offers new insights into the interactions between plant-derived bioactive compounds and the gut microbiota. The findings may contribute to the development of functional foods and natural product–based therapeutics, facilitate the modernization of TCM, and support innovation within the health industry, while also serving as a valuable reference for related fields of research.

Materials and methods

Materials

Ardisia mamillata Hance was collected in Hunan Province, China, in September. The whole plants were vacuum-dried at 60 °C, ground into powder, passed through a 60-mesh sieve, and stored for subsequent use.

Experimental animals: Specific pathogen-free (SPF) male C57BL/6J mice (6 weeks old, weighing 18–21 g) were obtained from Hunan Silaike Jingda Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. (certificate No. 430727231102079742). All experimental procedures complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The research project was ethically supervised by the Academic Ethics Committee of Hunan University of Science and Engineering.

Methods

Preparation of the standard curve

Standard curve preparation: With slight modifications to the method described in reference (Xu et al., 2021; Man et al., 2023)0.4 mg of ginsenoside Rg1 standard was accurately weighed, dissolved in anhydrous ethanol using ultrasonication, and diluted to a final volume of 10.0 mL to obtain a stock solution. Aliquots ranging from 0.1 to 1.0 mL (i.e., 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mL) were transferred into 10 mL stoppered test tubes and evaporated to dryness in an 80 °C thermostatic water bath. After complete solvent evaporation, 0.2 mL of freshly prepared 5% vanillin–glacial acetic acid solution and 0.8 mL of perchloric acid were added to each tube. The mixtures were thoroughly mixed and incubated at 65 °C for 20 min to allow color development. Subsequently, the reaction mixtures were cooled to room temperature, diluted to 10.0 mL with glacial acetic acid, and mixed thoroughly. A reagent blank was used as the control. Absorbance was measured at 581 nm. A standard curve was constructed by plotting absorbance (y-axis) against ginsenoside Rg1 concentration (x-axis), yielding the regression equation: y = 2.4369x + 0.18918 (R2 = 0.99413).

Determination of total saponin content from Ardisia mamillata hance

Determination of total saponin content: A 1.0 mL aliquot of the ethanol extract supernatant was transferred into a 10 mL stoppered test tube and evaporated to dryness in an 80 °C thermostatic water bath. Subsequently, 0.2 mL of freshly prepared 5% vanillin–glacial acetic acid solution and 0.8 mL of perchloric acid were added to each tube. The mixture was thoroughly mixed and incubated at 65 °C for 20 min to allow color development, followed by rapid cooling in an ice bath. After cooling, the reaction solution was diluted to 10.0 mL with glacial acetic acid and mixed thoroughly. The absorbance was then measured at 581 nm using a reagent blank as the control. The saponin concentration in the extract was calculated based on the standard curve described in Section 2.2.1. Single-factor experiments

Impact of ethanol concentration on the of total saponins’ yield from A. mamillata

Single-factor experiment on ethanol concentration: A 20.0 g sample of A. mamillata dry powder was accurately weighed for extraction. Using the extraction yield of total saponins as the response variable, UAE was carried out under the following fixed conditions: ultrasonic power of 200 W, intermittent ultrasonication (3 s on/3 s off), extraction temperature of 70 °C, a solid–liquid ratio of 1:20 (g/mL), and an extraction time of 35 min. Ethanol concentration was varied at 60%, 65%, 70%, 75%, and 80% to evaluate its effect on total saponin yield. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Impact of ultrasonic time on the of total saponins’ yield from A. mamillata

Single-factor experiment on ultrasonic time: A 20.0 g sample of A. mamillata dry powder was accurately weighed for extraction. Using the extraction yield of total saponins as the response variable, UAE was performed under the following fixed conditions: ultrasonic power of 200 W, intermittent ultrasonication (3 s on/3 s off), extraction temperature of 70 °C, solid–liquid ratio of 1:20 (g/mL), and ethanol concentration of 70%. Ultrasonic extraction time was varied at 25, 30, 35, 40, and 45 min to investigate its effect on total saponin yield. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Effect of ultrasonic temperature on the yield of total saponins from A. mamillata

Single-factor experiment on ultrasonic temperature: A 20.0 g sample of A. mamillata dry powder was accurately weighed for extraction. Using the extraction yield of total saponins as the response variable, UAE was performed under the following fixed conditions: ultrasonic power of 200 W, intermittent ultrasonication (3 s on/3 s off), solid–liquid ratio of 1:20 (g/mL), ethanol concentration of 70%, and extraction time of 30 min. Ultrasonic temperature was varied at 50, 55, 60, 65, and 70 °C to evaluate its effect on total saponin yield. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Effect of material-to-liquid ratio on the yield of total saponins from A. mamillata

Single-factor experiment on material-to-liquid ratio: A 20.0 g sample of A. mamillata dry powder was accurately weighed for extraction. Using the extraction yield of total saponins as the response variable, UAE was performed under the following fixed conditions: ultrasonic power of 200 W, intermittent ultrasonication (3 s on/3 s off), extraction temperature of 60 °C, ethanol concentration of 70%, and extraction time of 30 min. The material-to-liquid ratio was varied at 1:15, 1:20, 1:25, 1:30, and 1:35 (g/mL) to investigate its effect on total saponin yield. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Response surface experimental design

Response surface methodology (RSM) design: Based on the results of the single-factor experiments, RSM with a Box–Behnken design was employed to develop a mathematical model describing the relationship between four independent variables, ethanol concentration (A, %), ultrasonic time (B, min), ultrasonic temperature (C, °C), and liquid-to-material ratio (D, mL/g), and the extraction yield of total saponins. Model analysis was performed to assess the relative contribution of each factor to the total saponin yield from A. mamillata and to determine the optimal combination of extraction parameters. Comparative extraction experiments

Ethanol heating (reflux) extraction: A 20.0 g sample of A. mamillata dry powder was accurately weighed and extracted with 71% (v/v) ethanol solution at a solid–liquid ratio of 1:32 (g/mL). Reflux extraction was performed at 90 °C for 1.5 h. All experiments were conducted in triplicate. The total saponin content was determined according to Sections 2.2.1–2.2.3.

Ultrasound-Assisted Extraction (UAE): A 20.0 g sample of A. mamillata dry powder was accurately weighed and extracted with 71% (v/v) ethanol solution at a solid–liquid ratio of 1:32 (g/mL). UAE was performed under the following optimized conditions: extraction temperature of 61 °C, extraction time of 31 min, ultrasonic power of 200 W, and intermittent ultrasonication (3 s on/3 s off). All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the total saponin content was determined according to Sections 2.2.1–2.2.3.

Preliminary purification of total saponins from Ardisia mamillata hance

Extraction of total saponins from A. mamillata: Whole-plant material of A. mamillata (vacuum-dried at 60 °C, ground, and passed through a 60-mesh sieve) was accurately weighed (20.0 g) and transferred into a round-bottom flask. A total of 640 mL of 71% (v/v) ethanol was added, and UAE was performed at 61 °C for 31 min (ultrasonic power of 200 W; intermittent ultrasonication, 3 s on/3 s off). The extract was vacuum-filtered, and the resulting supernatant was collected. The filtrate was then concentrated under reduced pressure until no ethanol odor remained. The residue was re-dissolved in distilled water and sequentially partitioned with petroleum ether (3 × 50 mL), and the organic phases were discarded. The aqueous phase was subsequently extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 50 mL), and these organic fractions were also discarded. Finally, the aqueous phase was extracted with n-butanol (3 × 50 mL). The combined n-butanol fractions were concentrated under reduced pressure and vacuum-dried to obtain the total saponins of A. mamillata.

UPLC-MS/MS

Chromatographic conditions

An ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) was employed for analysis. The flow rate was set at 0.3 mL min−1, and the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. The injection volume was 4 μL. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, while mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–1.5 min, 95% A; 1.5–2.5 min, A decreased from 95% to 90% and was held for 1.0 min; 2.5–14 min, A decreased from 90% to 60%; 14–22 min, A decreased from 60% to 5%; 22–25 min, 5% A; 25–26 min, A increased from 5% to 95%; and 26–30 min, 95% A.

Mass spectrometry conditions

An electrospray ionization (ESI) source was employed with the following parameters: nebulizer gas (GS1) pressure of 60 psi, auxiliary gas pressure of 60 psi, curtain gas pressure of 35 psi, ion source temperature of 550 °C, and spray voltage of 5500 V in positive ion mode and −4500 V in negative ion mode.

Animal grouping and gavage

Animal grouping and treatment: Eighteen SPF adult male mice were used in this study. After a 1-week acclimation period on a standard chow diet, the mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 6 per group). Animals were housed under controlled environmental conditions (22–26 °C, 55%–70% relative humidity) with ad libitum access to food and water. The experimental groups were as follows: Control group (Con): fed a standard maintenance diet for 13 weeks. High-fat and high-sugar diet group (Mod): fed a high-fat and high-sugar diet for 13 weeks. Treatment group (Ard): fed a high-fat and high-sugar diet for 12 weeks, followed by daily oral gavage of A. mamillata total saponins from week 9 to week 13 at a dose of 108 mg/kg for 5 weeks.

Mouse feces collection

Fecal sample collection: One day prior to the end of the experiment, fresh fecal samples were collected from each mouse into sterile Eppendorf (EP) tubes. The samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Measurement of mouse body weight

The body weight of mice in the control group (Con), high-fat and high-sugar diet group (Mod), and treatment group (Ard) was measured once weekly, and longitudinal changes in body weight were recorded.

Measurement of mouse organ fat weight

At the end of the experiment, mice were fasted for 12 h, after which blood samples were collected and the animals were euthanized. Perirenal, inguinal, dorsal, and epididymal adipose tissues were rapidly excised, carefully trimmed to remove surrounding impurities, and weighed.

Hematoxylineosin (HE) staining of liver and epididymal adipose tissue

Fresh liver and epididymal adipose tissue samples from the control group (Con), high-fat and high-sugar diet group (Mod), and treatment group (Ard) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. The tissues were subsequently processed into paraffin blocks using standard histological procedures, including dehydration through a graded ethanol series, clearing in xylene, and paraffin infiltration. Paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm, followed by dewaxing and rehydration. Sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). After staining, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with coverslips. Morphological changes in liver and epididymal adipose tissues were examined under a light microscope, and representative images were captured for analysis.

Detection of ALT, AST, TC, and TG levels in mouse serum

Serum levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total cholesterol (TC), and triglycerides (TG) in the control group (Con), high-fat and high-sugar diet group (Mod), and treatment group (Ard) were measured according to the manufacturer's instructions provided with the respective assay kits.

Effects of A. mamillata total saponins on the gut microbiota of mice

16S rRNA high-throughput sequencing

PCR amplification of 16S rRNA genes: The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified using genomic DNA extracted from mouse fecal samples as the template. The following primers were used: forward primer (F), 5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′, and reverse primer (R), 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′. The PCR reaction mixture (25 μL total volume) consisted of 1.0 μL each of forward and reverse primers (5 μmol/L), 3.0 μL bovine serum albumin (BSA; 2 ng/μL), 12.5 μL of 2 × Taq Plus Master Mix, approximately 30 ng of template DNA, and sterile water to adjust the final volume. PCR amplification was performed under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min; 28 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min.

High-throughput sequencing and data processing: Each sample was amplified in triplicate, and the resulting PCR products from the same sample were pooled. Amplicons were verified by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel, after which the target bands were excised and purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen). The purified DNA was subsequently quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA).

Sequencing libraries were constructed and subjected to high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform. Raw FASTQ reads were initially processed using Trimmomatic for quality filtering, and paired-end reads were merged using PEAR. Chimeric sequences were identified and removed using UCHIME against a reference database, with additional de novo chimera detection performed to eliminate sequences not present in the reference. Low-quality and short reads were further filtered out to obtain high-quality sequences. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at a 97% sequence similarity threshold, and subsequent bioinformatic analyses were conducted using QIIME.

Determination of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) content

Extraction of SCFAs from feces

A 0.20 g sample of mouse feces stored at −80 °C was suspended in 1.60 mL of sterile deionized water. The mixture was thoroughly vortexed and allowed to stand at room temperature for 20 min, followed by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully transferred to a new EP tube. An additional 1.60 mL of sterile deionized water was added to the remaining fecal pellet, and the extraction procedure was repeated. The resulting supernatants were combined and mixed thoroughly. After filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane filter, a 0.20 mL aliquot of the filtrate was mixed with 0.70 mL of sterile water and 0.10 mL of n-butanol solution (100 μg/mL) as an internal standard for subsequent GC analysis.

Gas chromatography (GC) detection of SCFAs

(1)Detection conditions

Detection conditions

A Nexis GC-2030 gas chromatograph (Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with an InterCap WAX highly polar capillary column (60 m × 0.32 mm × 0.25 μm) was used for analysis. The inlet temperature was set at 280 °C with a split ratio of 20:1. The oven temperature program was as follows: initial temperature of 60 °C held for 2.5 min, increased to 140 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, and further increased to 170 °C at a rate of 3 °C/min (Xu et al., 2024). Detection was performed using a flame ionization detector (FID) maintained at 300 °C, and the injection volume was 1 μL.

(2)Preparation of the standard curve

Preparation of the standard curve

Preparation of standard solutions and calibration curves: Analytical standards of acetic acid, propionic acid, n-butyric acid, isobutyric acid, n-valeric acid, and isovaleric acid were accurately weighed and dissolved in ultrapure water to prepare individual stock solutions at a concentration of 1000 μg/mL for each compound. The six standard solutions were then mixed at a volume ratio of 20:10:5:2:2:1 to obtain a mixed standard solution containing the six SCFAs at final concentrations of 500 μg/mL, 250 μg/mL, 125 μg/mL, 50 μg/mL, 50 μg/mL, and 25 μg/mL, respectively. This mixed standard solution was further diluted with ultrapure water to generate five concentration gradients (1 × , 0.6 × , 0.4 × , 0.2 × , and 0.1 × ). For example, the corresponding concentrations of acetic acid in these diluted solutions were 500, 300, 200, 100, and 50 μg/mL, respectively. For GC analysis, 200 μL of the mixed standard solution was combined with 700 μL of ultrapure water and 100 μL of n-butanol internal standard solution, resulting in a final n-butanol concentration of 100 μg/mL. The average peak area of n-butanol, obtained from multiple injections, was used to correct for inter-batch variation. Calibration curves were constructed by plotting the concentration of each standard acid (x-axis) against its corresponding peak area (y-axis).

Data analysis

Experimental data were processed and analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2007, Origin, and Design-Expert 8.0.6. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS 22.0, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Optimization of UAE process for total saponins from Ardisia mamillata Hance

Results of single-factor experiments

As shown in Fig. 1(A), the extraction yield of total saponins from Ardisia mamillata Hance initially increased and then decreased with rising ethanol concentration, reaching a maximum at 70%. This behavior can be attributed to the amphiphilic nature of saponins, which contain both hydrophobic aglycone moieties and hydrophilic glycosidic chains. Changes in ethanol concentration alter the solvent polarity, and according to the principle of “like dissolves like,” 70% ethanol provides an optimal polarity that best matches the physicochemical characteristics of the saponins in A. mamillata, thereby facilitating their dissolution and release (Xing et al., 2024). When the ethanol concentration exceeded 70%, the extraction efficiency decreased, likely due to reduced solubility of more polar saponin fractions under lower solvent polarity conditions (Akbari et al., 2019).

Effects of individual factors on the Extraction amount of total saponins from A. mamillata.

As shown in Fig. 1(B), the extraction yield of total saponins increased progressively with increasing ultrasonic time. This trend is likely due to acoustic cavitation, which disrupts plant cell structures and enhances the release of intracellular compounds. In addition, ultrasonic energy is partially converted into heat, increasing the medium temperature and accelerating molecular motion, thereby further promoting mass transfer and saponin release (Fan et al., 2015). The yield reached its maximum at 30 min; however, further extension of extraction time resulted in a decline, possibly due to the degradation of saponin aglycone structures under prolonged ultrasonic exposure, leading to reduced overall yield (Belwal et al., 2019).

As shown in Fig. 1(C), the extraction yield of total saponins increased with rising ultrasonic temperature but decreased when the temperature exceeded 60 °C. This trend may be explained by the fact that moderate temperature elevation enhances molecular motion and improves solute solubility, thereby facilitating saponin extraction. However, temperatures above 60 °C may induce thermal degradation of heat-sensitive saponin constituents, resulting in reduced yield (Ruilin et al., 2017).

As shown in Fig. 1(D), increasing the solvent volume initially promoted the extraction yield of total saponins, whereas further increases led to a decline. This phenomenon may be attributed to changes in the effective polarity and mass transfer dynamics of the extraction system. At an optimal range, higher solvent volumes enhance solvent–matrix contact and improve saponin release. However, excessive solvent volume may promote the co-extraction of polyphenols, flavonoids, lipophilic impurities, carbohydrates, proteins, amino acids, and tannins (Liu et al., 2022; Wong et al., 2020; Ligor et al., 2018; Navarro del Hierro et al., 2018b). These compounds may form non-covalent complexes with saponins or encapsulate their molecular structures (Guo et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025), thereby reducing their apparent solubility in the solvent system and ultimately decreasing the total saponin yield (Wen et al., 2019).

The steepness of the response surface reflects the sensitivity of the response to changes in each factor, while the contour shape indicates the strength of interaction between two variables. Circular contours suggest weak or negligible interactions, whereas elliptical contours indicate stronger interactions. In this study, the interaction between ethanol concentration (A) and ultrasonic temperature (C) produced a relatively steep response surface, and the corresponding contour plots tended toward an elliptical shape, suggesting a relatively strong interaction between these two variables.

Validation of the extraction process for total saponins from A. mamillata

By solving the quadratic polynomial regression equation, the optimal extraction conditions were determined as follows: ethanol concentration of 70.50% (v/v), ultrasonic time of 30.92 min, ultrasonic temperature of 61.26 °C, and material-to-liquid ratio of 1:31.96 (g/mL), with a predicted extraction yield of 27.89 mg/g. Considering practical operability, these conditions were adjusted to ethanol concentration of 71% (v/v), ultrasonic time of 31 min, ultrasonic temperature of 61 °C, and material-to-liquid ratio of 1:32 (g/mL). Three independent validation experiments were then conducted, each performed in triplicate. As shown in Table 4, the mean extraction yield of total saponins was (27.52 ± 0.16) mg/g, which closely matched the predicted value and demonstrated good reproducibility. This confirms a strong agreement between the response surface model and the experimental validation, indicating that the optimized extraction process is reliable and practically feasible.

Comparison of different extraction methods

As shown in Table 5, ethanol heating (reflux) extraction yielded (23.56 ± 0.203) mg/g of total saponins, whereas UAE achieved a significantly higher yield of (27.32 ± 0.128) mg/g. Although both methods employed ethanol as the extraction solvent, UAE operated at a lower temperature and shorter extraction time, yet increased the extraction yield of total saponins from A. mamillata by 13.73%. These results are consistent with previous reports. Du et al. (2016) reported that under 74% (v/v) ethanol and an ultrasonic time of 33 min, the yield of quinoa saponins reached 23.37 mg/g. Liang et al. (Xiang et al., 2017) obtained a lower yield of 15.8 mg/g using ethanol reflux extraction. Similarly, Feng (2017) compared UAE, methanol reflux extraction, and ethanol heating extraction of quinoa bran and found that UAE was the most efficient method. Chen et al. (2021) further demonstrated that UAE increased the extraction yield of Ziziphus jujuba seeds by 14.25% compared with ethanol heating extraction. Collectively, these findings indicate that UAE offers higher extraction efficiency, shorter processing time, and lower energy consumption, while utilizing a relatively safe and environmentally friendly solvent system. This method not only enhances saponin yield but also demonstrates strong potential for industrial-scale application, providing a useful reference for the extraction of bioactive compounds from other medicinal plants.

α-Diversity analysis

α-Diversity reflects the overall diversity of the gut microbiota. The ACE and Chao1 indices quantify community richness, whereas the Shannon and Simpson indices reflect community diversity (Wang et al., 2023). Fig. 8 presents the α-diversity indices across all experimental groups. Regarding community richness, both the ACE (P = 0.0188, P < 0.05) and Chao1 (P = 0.0203, P < 0.05) indices were significantly higher in the high-fat and high-sugar group compared with the normal diet group, indicating that a high-fat and high-sugar diet markedly increased gut microbial richness in mice. The treatment group also exhibited higher richness values than the normal diet group; however, no significant difference was observed when compared with the high-fat and high-sugar group. These results suggest that administration of A. mamillata total saponins did not significantly reduce microbial richness, but also did not further exacerbate the diet-induced increase. Regarding community diversity, the Shannon index was significantly higher in the treatment group than in the high-fat and high-sugar group (P = 0.0371, P < 0.05). Although no significant difference was observed between the high-fat and high-sugar group and the normal diet group, the increase in the treatment group suggests that A. mamillata total saponins may have induced a modest improvement in microbial diversity. In contrast, the Simpson index showed no significant differences among groups, indicating that overall diversity remained relatively stable regardless of diet or saponin intervention. Taken together, these findings indicate that a high-fat and high-sugar diet significantly increased gut microbial richness, while treatment with A. mamillata total saponins exerted a limited but measurable effect on microbial composition. Specifically, it partially modulated richness and modestly enhanced diversity without inducing broad shifts in overall community diversity.

β-Diversity analysis

As shown in the principal component analysis (PCA) plot (Fig. 9), samples from the normal diet group and the high-fat and high-sugar group were clearly separated, indicating distinct differences in gut microbial community structure. The treatment group clustered between these two groups and was positioned closer to the normal diet group, suggesting that A. mamillata total saponins exerted a modulatory effect by shifting the gut microbiota toward a composition more similar to that of the normal diet group. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distance yielded consistent results. By projecting high-dimensional data into a lower-dimensional space while preserving pairwise sample dissimilarities, the PCoA plot clearly demonstrated differences in microbial community structure among the groups. The treatment group again clustered more closely with the normal diet group, further supporting the modulatory effect of A. mamillata total saponins. Similarly, non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis based on Bray–Curtis distance revealed comparable patterns. As a nonlinear ordination method well suited for ecological data, NMDS arranges samples according to their compositional similarity. In this analysis, the treatment group was located between the normal diet and high-fat and high-sugar groups, and closer to the normal diet group. This further confirms that A. mamillata total saponins contribute to the partial restoration of gut microbial composition disrupted by a high-fat and high-sugar diet.

Analysis of gut microbial community structure

As shown in Fig. 10(A), phylum-level analysis of the gut microbial community revealed that Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were the dominant phyla across all groups. In the normal diet group, their relative abundances were 40% and 53%, respectively; in the high-fat and high-sugar group, 64% and 30%; and in the treatment group, 53% and 39%. The F/B ratios were 0.75, 2.13, and 1.36, respectively. The human gut microbiota contains approximately 104 bacterial species, with Firmicutes (Gram-positive) and Bacteroidetes (Gram-negative) together accounting for nearly 90% of the total microbial population (Shi, 2023). Previous studies have reported that an increased abundance of Firmicutes, a reduced abundance of Bacteroidetes, or an elevated F/B ratio is associated with obesity (Fei and Zhao, 2013; Cotillard et al., 2013; Cai et al., 2025). Following administration of A. mamillata total saponins, the relative abundance of Firmicutes decreased, while that of Bacteroidetes increased, resulting in a lower F/B ratio compared with the high-fat and high-sugar group. These findings suggest that A. mamillata total saponins may help regulate the F/B ratio by suppressing Firmicutes and promoting Bacteroidetes, thereby potentially alleviating high-fat diet–induced obesity in mice. Fig. 10(B) presents the genus-level microbial community structure. The dominant genera in the normal diet, high-fat and high-sugar, and treatment groups included Muribaculaceae (32%, 23%, and 28%, respectively), Lachnospiraceae (6%, 15%, and 11%), the NK4A136 group (2%, 17%, and 8%), Lactobacillus (7%, 9%, and 2%), and Alloprevotella (7%, 2%, and 4%). Administration of A. mamillata total saponins increased the relative abundance of Muribaculaceae and Alloprevotella, while decreasing Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136 and Lactobacillus. Muribaculaceae, belonging to the Bacteroidetes phylum (Ruxin, 2022), are dominant members of the rodent gut microbiota. They are highly oxygen-sensitive and require strictly anaerobic conditions for growth. Their taxonomy and functional roles have only recently been elucidated through genomic analyses. Muribaculaceae are associated with host metabolic health and have been linked to reduced risks of inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, and diabetes (Takeuchi et al., 2023). Alloprevotella can produce SCFAs, and its abundance is negatively associated with metabolic syndrome and diabetes (Zhang et al., 2015). In contrast, certain members of Firmicutes, such as Lachnospiraceae, have been implicated in the development of obesity and type 2 diabetes (Yang et al., 2025). Fig. 10(C) presents a heatmap of genus-level abundance. The color gradient (red to blue) represents increasing abundance, while branch lengths indicate similarity in abundance profiles among groups. The normal diet and high-fat and high-sugar groups clustered relatively closely, whereas the treatment group formed a distinct cluster, suggesting that A. mamillata total saponins significantly reshaped the genus-level gut microbial community structure in high-fat diet–induced mice.

Discussion

Recent studies have demonstrated that plant-derived saponins exhibit a range of biological activities, including the scavenging of lipid peroxidation products, enhancement of hepatic energy storage, hepatocyte protection, and modulation of the gut microbiota (Liu et al., 2015; Su, 2024). Accordingly, the development of an efficient and rapid extraction method is a critical prerequisite for fully realizing the application potential of A. mamillata total saponins. Although conventional reflux extraction is operationally straightforward, its limitations, including prolonged extraction time, high energy consumption, and the potential degradation of thermolabile constituents under elevated temperatures, restrict its suitability for industrial-scale applications (Luo et al., 2025). In the present study, an UAE process was optimized. Under the optimal conditions (71% ethanol, solid–liquid ratio of 1:32 g/mL, 61 °C, 31 min), the yield of total saponins from A. mamillata reached 27.52 mg/g, representing a significant increase of 13.73% compared with the conventional ethanol reflux method. This result is consistent with the findings of Du et al. (Liu et al., 2022) and Chen et al. (Navarro del Hierro et al., 2018b) in studies on quinoa and Ziziphus jujuba seed saponins, further confirming that ultrasonic cavitation can effectively disrupt plant cell walls and facilitate the release of bioactive compounds. Importantly, the optimized process achieved a high extraction yield under relatively mild thermal conditions. Such low-temperature efficiency helps minimize the degradation of saponin glycosidic bonds induced by excessive heat, thereby better preserving their native structure and biological activity. In summary, the UAE method developed in this study demonstrates clear advantages over conventional approaches in terms of extraction efficiency, energy consumption, and preservation of active constituents, providing an efficient, energy-saving, and environmentally friendly strategy for the industrial utilization of A. mamillata resources.

The gut microbiota comprises a complex community of microorganisms inhabiting the human intestinal tract and typically maintains a dynamic ecological balance with both the host and the external environment. Disruption of this equilibrium can impair multiple host physiological functions and contribute to the development of various diseases (Peng et al., 2022). Approximately 70% of hepatic blood flow is derived directly from the gastrointestinal tract via the portal vein, rendering the liver the first organ exposed to gut-derived metabolites and microbial products. Consequently, gut microbiota dysbiosis and impairment of the intestinal mucosal barrier may expose hepatocytes, Kupffer cells, and hepatic stellate cells to microbial-derived factors, thereby inducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines that play a central role in the initiation and progression of MAFLD (Barber et al., 2023). Recent studies have demonstrated that saponins can modulate the gut microbiota, restore intestinal microecological balance, and contribute to the prevention and treatment of MAFLD (Malnick and Michael, 2023). Microbial α-diversity reflects the richness and diversity within microbial communities, where the Chao index estimates species richness and the Shannon index reflects overall diversity. Zhong et al. (2022) reported that total saponins from Gynostemma pentaphyllum could ameliorate gut dysbiosis, restore microbial diversity, and improve MAFLD. Consistently, in the present study, total saponins from A. mamillata modulated microbial richness (Chao index) and diversity (Shannon index) in mice, restoring these parameters toward levels observed in the control group. Accumulating evidence supports a key role for the gut microbiota in metabolic disorders such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. At the phylum level, Firmicutes are often increased in obese individuals, whereas Bacteroidetes are reduced (Zhou et al., 2016). Certain members of Firmicutes, such as the Lachnospiraceae family, have been implicated in obesity and type 2 diabetes (Kahn and Davidson, 2014; Selph et al., 2025). Consistent with these findings, A. mamillata saponins increased the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes while decreasing that of Firmicutes, thereby reducing the F/B ratio. At the genus level, A. mamillata total saponins increased the relative abundance of Muribaculaceae and Alloprevotella, while decreasing Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136 and Lactobacillus. Muribaculaceae and Alloprevotella are important producers of SCFAs in the gut (Tang et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2025). Muribaculaceae have been shown to play a key role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis, suppressing inflammation, and alleviating metabolic syndrome (Takeuchi et al., 2023), while increased abundance of Alloprevotella is generally associated with a healthier metabolic profile (Zhang et al., 2015). The observed enrichment of these beneficial taxa provides a microbial basis for the subsequent increase in SCFA production. In contrast, a high-fat diet significantly increased the abundance of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136, a group that has been associated with insulin resistance and obesity in several studies (Yang et al., 2025). Although Lactobacillus is commonly regarded as a probiotic genus, emerging evidence suggests that under conditions of diet-induced metabolic dysregulation, its overrepresentation may not be uniformly beneficial and could be associated with specific metabolic disturbances (Tang et al., 2021; Kwon et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2021). Collectively, these findings suggest that A. mamillata total saponins may contribute to the remodeling of gut microbial communities by suppressing taxa that become overabundant under pathological metabolic conditions.

The gut microbiota ferments dietary fiber to produce SCFAs, which not only serve as the primary energy source for colonocytes but can also enter the liver via the portal vein, where they participate in systemic energy metabolism and immune regulation (Hu et al., 2018; Kim, 2023; Tan et al., 2023). In the present study, intervention with A. mamillata total saponins significantly increased the levels of acetate and butyrate, markedly elevated propionate and valerate, and restored total SCFA levels. As a natural product, A. mamillata total saponins are poorly digestible and absorbable by the host; however, they may function as potential prebiotic-like compounds. These saponins can be selectively utilized by beneficial bacteria, such as Muribaculaceae and Alloprevotella, thereby promoting their proliferation and enhancing SCFA-producing capacity (Chen et al., 2015; Luo et al., 2020). Notably, the significant increase in valerate was closely associated with the enrichment of Alloprevotella. As a potent histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, valerate may contribute to the maintenance of metabolic homeostasis through epigenetic regulation of gene expression (Paciolla et al., 2025). Among SCFAs, butyrate plays a key role in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and attenuating hepatic inflammation by reducing endotoxin translocation into the circulation (Fan et al., 2021). Acetate and propionate, after hepatic uptake, may suppress de novo lipogenesis through activation of the AMPK signaling pathway or inhibition of key lipogenic enzymes (Li et al., 2013; Iannucci et al., 2016). These mechanisms are consistent with the observed reductions in serum TG and TC levels, as well as the improvement in hepatic steatosis in this study, suggesting that A. mamillata total saponins may exert lipid-lowering effects via modulation of the “gut microbiota–SCFAs–host metabolism” axis. Collectively, these findings provide a mechanistic foundation for future studies and may offer novel strategies for the prevention and treatment of metabolic diseases.

Although this study provides meaningful insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the effects of A. mamillata total saponins on the gut microbiota were evaluated exclusively in a murine model, and the underlying molecular mechanisms were not fully elucidated. In addition, the pharmacological activities of individual bioactive constituents within the total saponin fraction remain to be characterized in detail. Therefore, future studies should focus on elucidating the mechanistic pathways involved, isolating and purifying the active compounds, and conducting systematic pharmacological evaluations. Such investigations will provide stronger experimental evidence to support the potential development of A. mamillata total saponins as functional food ingredients or therapeutic agents.

Conclusions

In summary, UAE is an efficient method for obtaining total saponins from A. mamillata. The optimal extraction conditions were determined to be 71% ethanol, 31 min of ultrasonication, 61 °C, and a solid–liquid ratio of 1:32 (g/mL). Furthermore, A. mamillata total saponins were shown to inhibit body weight gain in obese mice to a certain extent and to alleviate hepatic fat accumulation, hepatocyte injury, and lipid metabolism disorders induced by a high-fat and high-sugar diet. Mechanistically, A. mamillata total saponins improved intestinal homeostasis by increasing the abundance of Muribaculaceae and Alloprevotella, elevating SCFA levels, suppressing Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136 and Lactobacillus, and modulating the F/B ratio. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of A. mamillata total saponins in modulating the gut microbiota and provide important insights into their possible mechanisms for ameliorating metabolic disorders.
