Ruptured Peritoneal Dissemination of Hepatocellular Carcinoma Successfully Treated with Transcatheter Arterial Embolization: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Ryo Aoki, Miyuki Kambe, Tomohiro Numata, Ritsuko Oishi, Manabu Morimoto, Zenjiro Sekikawa, Daisuke Utsunomiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRupture of peritoneal dissemination from hepatocellular carcinoma is a rare, life-threatening condition. A 66-year-old man who had previously undergone treatment for hepatocellular carcinoma presented with hemorrhagic shock due to a ruptured peritoneal nodule. Emergency transcatheter arterial embolization was performed. A triaxial system was used to navigate the tortuous omental artery. Successful hemostasis was achieved using an N-butyl cyanoacrylate-lipiodol mixture and gelatin sponge. A literature review identified only eight prior cases, revealing a mortality rate of 33.3%. Although surgical resection is often preferred because of preoperative diagnostic challenges and difficult arterial access, our case demonstrates that transcatheter arterial embolization using a triaxial system is a viable, minimally invasive alternative. Accurate identification of the feeding artery and strategic device selection are essential.Keywords: rupture, peritoneal dissemination, hepatocellular carcinoma, transcatheter arterial embolization

Introduction

Rupture of HCC is a critical condition that results in massive hemoperitoneum and is associated with high mortality rates. While rupture of the primary hepatic lesion is well-documented, spontaneous rupture of peritoneal dissemination is rare and presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. We herein report the case of a 66-year-old man with a ruptured peritoneal dissemination nodule in the omentum that was successfully treated with TAE. In addition to describing the case, we provide a literature review of previously reported cases to discuss the clinical characteristics and management of this rare entity.

Case Report

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report. The patient was a 66-year-old male with a history of HCC arising from non-alcoholic steatohepatitis-related cirrhosis. He had undergone a right posterior segmentectomy for moderately differentiated HCC 10 years prior, followed by multiple sessions of radiofrequency ablation and transcatheter arterial chemoembolization for recurrent multifocal intrahepatic lesions. Although peritoneal dissemination had emerged during follow-up and was managed with systemic chemotherapy, the response was poor, and a transition to best supportive care had been planned. His comorbidities included hypertension and diabetes mellitus.

The patient was transported to our emergency department following the sudden onset of abdominal pain. On arrival, his vital signs and laboratory data were as follows: blood pressure, 80/58 mmHg; heart rate, 70 beats/min; hemoglobin level, 7.5 g/dL; platelet count, 17.5 × 104/μL; total bilirubin, 0.6 mg/dL; albumin, 3.1 g/dL; fibrinogen level, 227 mg/dL; prothrombin time, 13.4 s; activated partial thromboplastin time, 28.5 s; and alpha-fetoprotein, 2,025 ng/mL. Additionally, the serum lactate level was elevated at 6.4 mmol/L, and the arterial blood pH was 7.321, indicating metabolic acidosis due to hemorrhagic shock.

Multiphase contrast-enhanced CT revealed active extravasation from a peritoneal dissemination nodule (Figure 1 and Video S1), accompanied by massive hemoperitoneum. Based on these findings, emergency TAE was performed. A 6-Fr, 45-cm guiding sheath (Destination; Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) was inserted through the right common femoral artery. A 5-Fr, 70-cm Cobra-type catheter (Hanako Excellent EN catheter; Hanaco Medical, Saitama, Japan) and a 0.035-inch, 150-cm guidewire (Radifocus; Terumo) were advanced into the gastroduodenal artery, followed by the placement of the guiding sheath into the same artery. After removing the Cobra-type catheter, a triaxial system was constructed using a 2.6-Fr, 120-cm high-flow microcatheter (Carry Leon high-flow; UTM, Aichi, Japan) and a 1.5-Fr, 160-cm selective microcatheter (Carry Leon selective; UTM). Using this system, the catheters were advanced into the omental artery through the right gastroepiploic artery to reach the feeding artery of the ruptured peritoneal dissemination lesion. A 0.014-inch, 180-cm microguidewire (ASAHI Meister S14; Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan) was used for this procedure.

The use of this system enabled both microcatheters to be advanced to the immediate vicinity of the HCC lesion. Angiography confirmed active extravasation from the tumor (Figure 2a). Embolization was performed using a 25% NBCA (Histoacryl; B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany)-lipiodol (Guerbet, Villepinte, France) mixture (1:3 ratio) (Figure 2b), which resulted in the complete disappearance of the extravasation (Figure 2c). As residual tumor staining persisted, additional embolization was performed using gelatin sponge slurry (Serescue; Nippon Kayaku, Tokyo, Japan) (Figure 2d). No complications, such as omental infarction or gastrointestinal necrosis, were observed. The patient was discharged eight days after the procedure and was subsequently managed with palliative care. No episodes of rebleeding from the peritoneal dissemination were observed during the 4-month follow-up period, and no additional TAE was required.

Discussion

Rupture of HCC is a life-threatening condition requiring immediate intervention because it leads to rapid intraperitoneal hemorrhage. A comprehensive literature search was conducted using MEDLINE (via PubMed) and Web of Science for articles published through January 2026. The search terms used were (Hepatocellular carcinoma) AND (dissemination OR peritoneal OR seeding) AND “rupture”. This search identified eight cases of ruptured peritoneal dissemination from HCC resulting in hemoperitoneum [1-8]. The clinical data for these cases, including the present case, for a total of nine cases, are summarized in Table 1. It should be noted that this review primarily focused on peer-reviewed articles indexed in major international databases; thus, some cases published in regional or non-indexed journals may not have been captured.

Among the nine cases, three patients (33.3%) died as a result of the rupture, underscoring the high mortality rate associated with this condition. Of the seven patients who underwent invasive intervention, surgical resection was selected in five cases, whereas TAE was performed in only two cases, including the present case. This preference for surgery may be attributed to the inherent difficulty in identifying the feeding vessels of disseminated nodules [7], which often complicates arterial access. In fact, excluding our case, the only other patient treated with TAE involved a ruptured lesion below the left diaphragm, for which the left gastric and left inferior phrenic arteries were embolized. In that case, the distance from the arterial origin to the target lesion was relatively short, making catheterization more straightforward than in our case. Furthermore, as seen in Case 3 [3], in which appendicitis was initially suspected, ruptured peritoneal dissemination can be challenging to diagnose accurately preoperatively, sometimes being identified only through intraoperative findings. While TAE is generally considered the least invasive method for achieving hemostasis in ruptured HCC [9], surgical resection may be preferred for patients with well-controlled intrahepatic lesions, as it offers the potential for a more favorable long-term prognosis than TAE [7]. In the present case, TAE was selected because it was less invasive than surgery, making it a suitable option for a patient with treatment-resistant multiple HCC who was already receiving best supportive care. For rupture of peritoneal dissemination from HCC, TAE should be regarded primarily as an effective hemostatic treatment rather than an oncologically definitive therapy, because the ruptured disseminated lesion remains in situ after bleeding control. However, in selected patients, it may provide durable hemorrhage control, as no rebleeding occurred during the 4-month follow-up period in the present case. For patients with a limited disease burden who are acceptable operative candidates for surgery, surgical resection after stabilization may offer more definitive local control and eliminate the source of future bleeding. In the present patient, however, treatment-resistant multifocal HCC, known peritoneal dissemination, and the previous decision to transition to best supportive care made invasive surgery inappropriate; therefore, TAE alone was selected as the least invasive and most appropriate means of achieving hemostasis.

Spontaneous rupture of a peritoneal dissemination nodule is markedly less frequent than rupture of the primary hepatic lesion; its optimal therapeutic strategy and prognosis remain poorly defined. Disseminated nodules lack the protective confinement of the liver parenchyma, making them more susceptible to the effects of exophytic growth. Specifically, when such nodules rupture, the absence of the tamponade effect typically provided by the liver parenchyma can result in massive, uncontrolled hemorrhage.

In this case, embolization of the distal omental branch was required. The omental arteries arise from the arcade formed by the anastomosis of the right and left gastroepiploic arteries. Accessing these arteries often requires navigating long, tortuous, and small-caliber vessels, which may prevent the catheter from reaching the target lesion. When distal access is unachievable, proximal embolization using NBCA or gelatin sponge may be considered [10]. However, the omentum is supplied by numerous arteries branching from the gastroepiploic arcade; thus, proximal embolization may not provide sufficient hemostasis. Furthermore, because the omental arteries may communicate with branches of the superior mesenteric artery, such as the middle colic artery, via the omental arc (Arc of Barkow) [11], non-selective embolization should be avoided whenever possible to prevent complications such as bowel ischemia. The use of advanced imaging modalities such as a hybrid angiography-CT system or cone-beam CT can provide a detailed understanding of the vascular anatomy, potentially further reducing the risk of non-target embolization during complex procedures. Furthermore, selective embolization is preferable for tumors supplied by the omental artery. Recent advancements in catheter technology have enabled access to increasingly distal and peripheral arteries. To reach the feeding artery, strategic device selection is essential to ensure adequate backup support and minimize redundant catheter length. If the guiding sheath is unnecessarily long, the effective length of the coaxially inserted microcatheter may be insufficient to reach the bleeding point. The same principle applies when using a guiding catheter. In the present case, we advanced a 45-cm guiding sheath into the gastroduodenal artery and coaxially inserted two microcatheters. This configuration allowed us to advance both a high-flow microcatheter and a selective microcatheter to the vicinity of the tumor, enabling effective and safe embolization. Whereas treatment could potentially be performed using a standard coaxial system, the use of a triaxial system is crucial when employing NBCA to prevent the migration of the NBCA cast into non-target arteries [12]. Furthermore, because the microcatheter must be removed immediately following NBCA injection, this system is also essential for facilitating follow-up angiography to confirm hemostasis and providing secondary access should additional embolic agents be required.

In the present case, NBCA was initially employed to control active hemorrhage. The choice of NBCA was based on both the clinical urgency and the vascular anatomy. The patient presented with hemorrhagic shock and active extravasation from a distal omental branch, requiring rapid and definitive hemostasis, whereas proximal embolization alone might have been insufficient because of potential collateral perfusion. NBCA is recognized as an effective embolic agent for HCC rupture [13]; it enables cast-like occlusion from the extravasation point back to the feeding artery, ensuring definitive hemostasis even in patients with massive hemoperitoneum or coagulopathy associated with liver failure. However, NBCA requires a high level of technical expertise and carries a risk of non-target embolization due to potential over-injection. Although a non-adhesive liquid embolic agent based on an ethylene-vinyl alcohol copolymer, such as the Lava Liquid Embolic System, may have been an attractive option for this lesion, it was not available at our institution; therefore, NBCA was selected as the most appropriate liquid embolic agent in this emergency setting. Following initial hemostasis, we performed additional embolization with gelatin sponge slurry to address the residual tumor staining. This decision was primarily prophylactic and based on the operator’s clinical judgment, considering the possibility of occult micro-active bleeding masked by the tumor staining. We selected gelatin sponge slurry because it allows for more controlled adjustment of the embolization than NBCA; however, this approach also involves inherent risks of vascular recanalization and potential non-target embolization.

In conclusion, although rupture of peritoneal dissemination from HCC is a rare and life-threatening condition, TAE can be a feasible and effective hemostatic option in selected cases. In particular, this case highlights the clinical utility of a triaxial catheter system, which can facilitate distal access through tortuous feeder vessels and enable safe and selective embolization. Careful identification of the feeding arteries and strategic device selection are essential for successful treatment.
