Artificial intelligence in dermatology: A literature review of current evidence and clinical implementation
Authors: Jacob Mørck Munch Christensen, Marielle Nyheim-Tømmerås, Bjørn Kromann Hansen, Morten Bahrt Haulrig, Mihai Manolache, Marianne Bengtson Løvendorf, Beatrice Dyring-Andersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence is emerging as a transformative and rapidly developing technology, with growing implications for the healthcare sector, including dermatology. We conducted a literature review using PubMed and EMBASE. Included studies primarily focused on the application of artificial intelligence for image-based classification. These artificial intelligence systems showed remarkable performance in controlled settings, even matching dermatologist-level accuracy for specific narrow tasks. Several common limitations were identified, including restricted dataset sizes, limited diagnostic diversity, potential selection bias, and inconsistencies in model evaluation. Clinical implementation should require careful attention to validation rigor, dataset diversity, implementation strategies, ethical considerations, and evidence of real-world impact.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a term used to describe technologies that enable machines and computers to simulate human learning, reasoning, and decision-making. In dermatology, these technologies represent an expanding research area with the potential to enhance diagnostics, improve skin cancer detection, reduce human error, optimize time management, and contribute to dermatology education. In addition, AI has the potential to improve access to dermatological care in areas with low density of dermatologists by using AI-powered platforms to triage patients or assist nonspecialist physicians. This can be especially useful in countries such as Taiwan, where the density of dermatologists has been estimated at 4.27 per 100,000 people (Dai et al, 2018). In comparison, the density of dermatologists in the United States has been reported to be 3.65 per 10,000 people, showing a considerable difference worldwide (Feng et al, 2018). Several Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved dermatology AI tools are already available, including one that supports melanoma detection by recommending biopsy or monitoring based on lesion morphology (Nahm et al, 2025).

AI technologies in clinical context

AI is a broad field encompassing areas such as machine learning (ML) and natural language processing (NLP) (Omiye et al, 2023). ML involves analyzing data to identify patterns, make predictions, and improve performance without explicit programming. ML includes the subfield of deep learning (DL), which is modeled after human neurobiological processing through the use of artificial neural networks. These networks contain multiple processing layers (hence “deep”), enabling recognition of increasingly complex patterns. Unlike traditional ML, which requires converting raw data into features for pattern recognition, DL can recognize complex patterns directly from raw data. This enables DL models to excel at complex tasks such as image analysis and recognition (Omiye et al, 2023).

A convolutional neural network (CNN) is a DL model that is particularly well-suited for image analysis. Images are analyzed within multiple layers of filters that highlight basic features, such as edges, lines, and colors. The network combines these simple features into more complex ones, like shapes, textures, or even specific skin lesions, and can detect them regardless of location, recognizing patterns whether they appear at the center or edge of an image. Multiple public frameworks of CNNs exist, including ResNet, VGG-16, and DenseNet, which can be trained within image databases (Murphree et al, 2024).

NLP is another subfield of AI focused on interpreting and analyzing human language (Natural Language Understanding), as well as generating it (Natural Language Generation). This includes the development of large language models (LLMs).

Machine learning training

Training is key to developing AI models and can be performed through supervised, unsupervised, or reinforced learning. In supervised learning, the model is trained on labeled data with known answers (ground truths), whereas in unsupervised learning, it is trained on unlabeled data without predefined answers. In unsupervised learning, the model identifies patterns and clusters them together in an attempt to discover hidden structures in data (Omiye et al, 2023). In reinforced learning, the algorithm improves its performance through feedback in the form of rewards or penalties from its environment and users. For diseases with limited or absent labeled training data, zero-shot and few-shot learning offer practical alternatives. In zero-shot learning, a model classifies previously unseen categories by drawing on knowledge acquired during pretraining, without requiring any task-specific examples (Sivarajkumar and Wang, 2023). Few-shot learning extends this principle by providing only a small number of labeled examples, with one-shot learning representing the limiting case of a single example. Both approaches depend critically on the breadth and quality of the model’s pre-existing knowledge representations, which form the foundation from which novel categories are inferred (Irfan et al, 2025).

Convolutional neural network usage

Dermatology is widely regarded as a visual specialty in which the diagnosis of skin diseases relies heavily on recognizing visual patterns and interpreting clinical images. Therefore, visual models, such as CNNs, hold significant potential for diagnostic processes within the field.

For example, CNNs can support binary or multiclass image classification and segmentation, the latter involving the isolation of meaningful image regions from their background, such as identifying nevi in the surrounding skin.

Performance evaluation of AI models

Model performance can be assessed using metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operator curve (ROC). Accuracy is the proportion of correct predictions or diagnoses made by the AI model. It can be measured as the likelihood of the AI correctly identifying the single most probable diagnosis, known as top-1 accuracy, or the likelihood of the correct diagnosis being among the 3 most likely predictions, known as top-3 accuracy.

Although several reviews, including those by Jeong et al (2023) and Jairath et al (2024), have summarized the progress of AI in dermatology, the rapid evolution of the field underscores the need for an updated and comprehensive review. This review provides an overview of current research on the performance and applications of AI models and tools in dermatology. We focus on disease categorization using multimodal models, CNNs, public LLMs, and clinical support tools, while also investigating additional outcomes, including image segmentation and diagnostic suggestions.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

We included original research articles and clinical implementation studies that evaluated AI applications in dermatology. Reviews, conference abstracts, and opinion pieces were excluded.

Search strategy and data extraction

We searched PubMed and EMBASE for English-language studies with available abstracts, published between January 2015 and June 2025, and included articles in which the specified search terms appeared in the titles, using the following title-based search terms: “artificial intelligence,” “AI,” “machine learning,” “deep learning,” “neural networks,” “automated decision making,” “algorithmic analysis,” “computer vision,” “image analysis,” “pattern recognition,” “federated learning,” “Large Language Models,” “Large Language Model,” “LLM,” “LM,” “Language Model,” “Language Models,” “Agentic AI,” “convolutional neural network,” “CNN,” “GAN,” “Generative Adversarial Network,” “computational intelligence,” AND “dermatology,” “skin diseases,” “cutaneous medicine,” “dermatoses,” “cutaneous disorders,” “epidermal disorders,” “skin pathology,” “dermatopathology,” “cosmetic dermatology,” “clinical dermatology,” and “dermatological image.” We also screened reference lists of included articles to identify additional relevant articles.

We removed article duplicates using Covidence (https://www.covidence.org/). Two independent reviewers extracted the PubMed ID, year, author, primary objective, study design, dermatological domain, AI model, results/performance metrics, clinical validation results, real-world implementation outcomes, and identified study limitations. Discrepancies during data extraction were resolved by consensus.

Most of the included studies focused on AI in classifying skin diseases from images, with limited integration of clinical history data. Additional studies investigated image segmentation and database development.

AI model development and training

We examined AI model development and training studies to assess variation in study designs, disease categories, and evaluation approaches (Supplementary Table S1). A total of 41 predominantly retrospective, single-center studies on AI development and training were published between 2017 and 2025, most building AI models for binary or multiclass classification of specific skin conditions. Eighteen studies compared AI disease classification performance with that of physicians. The majority of studies used CNN models, often based on established frameworks such as ResNet, VGG, and Inception. CNNs were frequently trained, validated, and tested on the same dataset through partitioning. Dataset sizes for training and evaluation varied substantially, ranging between 120 and 235,268 images, and predominantly included photographic or dermatoscopic images. Models performed variably across multiple datasets depending on dataset characteristics and were reported to perform worse when tested on additional or external datasets (Han et al, 2020; Daneshjou et al, 2022; Rikhye et al, 2025). Most studies assessed performance using accuracy (often stated as top-1 or top-3 accuracy), sensitivity, specificity, or ROC curves. Overall, studies on AI model development and training differed considerably in their designs, types of skin diseases, and evaluation methodologies.

Comparison of diagnostic performance between AI and physicians

Eighteen studies compared AI models with physicians in their ability to diagnose skin diseases (Supplementary Table S1). Most of these studies evaluated performance on fewer than 14 diseases and relied predominantly on images (photographic, dermoscopic, high-frequency ultrasound, or H&E-stained whole-slide images) with only a few studies assessing a broader range of skin diseases and incorporating clinical history. The studies included between 3 and 280 board-certified dermatologists (Rikhye et al, 2025; Zhu et al, 2021). Final evaluation of the reported methods was commonly performed on internal datasets (datasets from the same institute that was used in training). Notably, most studies reported that AI performed comparably to, or even outperformed, physicians (Zhu et al, 2021; Esteva et al, 2017; Huang et al, 2024; Haenssle et al, 2018; Huang et al, 2021; Han et al, 2018b; Liu et al, 2020; Dulmage et al, 2021; Gao et al, 2021). For example, Zhu et al (2021) evaluated the diagnostic performance of a CNN model on 8 disease classes using a test set of 200 cases with clinical and dermoscopic images, reporting comparable performance to that of 280 physicians. In a different example, Huang et al (2021) evaluated their model, Xy-SkinNet, against 31 dermatologists of varying seniority, using 100 external images and no time constraints. In a 6-diagnosis classification task, their model’s top-1 and top-3 accuracies were comparable to the average performance of dermatologists. However, their model identified 100 images in 10 seconds, compared with over 1212 seconds for dermatologists (Huang et al, 2021). To gain deeper insight, 3 studies stratified physicians by specialty and experience, showing that AI models outperformed general practitioners and dermatology residents but not experienced dermatologists (Zhu et al, 2024; Han et al, 2020; Liu et al, 2020). In the study by Liu et al (2020), their deep learning system model was evaluated against 18 clinicians, including dermatologists, primary care physicians (PCPs), and nurse practitioners, in the diagnostic classification of 26 skin diseases. Using 963 internal cases, the model showed comparable top-1 accuracy to dermatologists (0.67 vs 0.63) and higher top-3 accuracy (0.90 vs 0.75) (Liu et al, 2020).

Some studies compared AI models, such as SE-ResNet-18s, with physicians on specific diagnostic tasks. Huang et al (2024) tested 98 dermatologists from 21 hospitals using a questionnaire with clinical photos of hypopigmented dermatoses. Dermatologists were grouped by experience (<5 years to >10 years). The AI model, SE-ResNet-18s, achieved 98% accuracy, significantly outperforming all dermatologists. Overall, these studies show that AI models can perform comparably to dermatologists in specific classification tasks and sometimes even outperform dermatologists.

Several studies evaluated AI binary classification tasks against physician performance (Supplementary Table S1). Esteva et al (2017) compared a CNN model with 21 dermatologists across 2 binary classification tasks: distinguishing keratinocyte carcinomas from benign seborrheic keratoses and malignant melanomas from benign nevi. The authors reported that AI performed comparably to physicians when tested on their internal dataset. Han et al (2018a) compared the performance of a CNN model with 42 dermatologists, including 34 dermatologists with more than 10 years of experience, and 57 non-dermatological practitioners in a binary task to identify the presence or absence of onychomycosis. The study used images from multiple external datasets. The model was trained on clinical images from one dataset and evaluated on 2 additional datasets. In the 2 evaluation datasets, the model outperformed nearly all physicians. On average, only 0.7 ± 0.6 dermatologists outperformed the CNN model when tested multiple times in the first dataset, and 6.0 ± 1.7 dermatologists when tested multiple times in the second. Together, these findings demonstrate that AI can match or surpass dermatologist performance in binary classification tasks, often outperforming even experienced specialists.

AI models for clinical assistance

Several studies investigated physicians’ diagnostic performance with AI assistance. For example, Li et al (2024) tested the diagnostic ability of 10 junior dermatologists, with and without AI assistance, on lupus erythematosus subtypes and other primarily inflammatory skin diseases in a 13-diagnosis classification test. Dermatologists were provided with an external, independent dataset with skin lesion images, medical history, symptoms, available laboratory test results, and histopathology conclusions. The authors reported that AI assistance improved junior dermatologists’ diagnostic accuracy from 66.9% (± 6.9%) to 81.3% (± 4.2%). Similarly, Wang et al (2024) grouped 13 physicians based on clinical experience in diagnosing genital skin diseases (<3 years or >10 years) and provided them with 115 pairs of clinical images and corresponding medical records. The AI model was trained on 520 patients and evaluated on 115 patients from other institutes. The authors evaluated precision, defined as the proportion of true positives among all positive predictions. Physicians with less than 3 years of experience benefited the most from AI assistance and showed a 17% increase in average precision, whereas the precision of physicians with over 10 years of experience improved by 2%.

Diagnostic performance of standalone AI models without physician comparison

Twenty-three studies developed or trained AI models for tasks such as diagnostic classification, image processing, image segmentation, and database creation, without physician comparison or assistance. Performance of AI models was evaluated using technical metrics, such as top-1 and top-3 accuracy.

Diagnostic classification

Several studies found that model performance improved when multiple diagnostic suggestions were allowed. For example, Liu et al (2020) reported accuracy rising from 0.71 (top-1 accuracy) to 0.93 (top-3 accuracy) across 26 common skin conditions, such as psoriasis, eczema, and melanoma. Similarly, evaluated the top-1, top-3, and top-5 accuracy of an AI model in classifying 59 skin diseases with multiple photographs per patient. They reported accuracy increasing from 0.579 (top-1) to 0.793 (top-3), reaching 0.863 with top-5. In addition, a study by Hossen et al (2023) showed that federated learning, a privacy-preserving technique in which institutions collaboratively train AI models by exchanging model updates rather than patient data, improved the collective model’s accuracy from 81.2% to 94.1% with more participating institutions (ranging from 1000 to 2500 institutions) (Hossen et al, 2023).

Comparison of publicly available AI models

We next reviewed studies evaluating publicly available AI models and identified 11 relevant publications (Supplementary Table S2). The performance was evaluated in skin disease classification (n = 5), prediction of adverse events to medication (n = 1), image generation (n = 1), and dermatology-related questions (n = 4). The latter included residency exam questions (n = 2), topics covering dermatology, venereology and leprosy (n = 1), and dermatopathology (n = 1) (Supplementary Table S2). Eight studies tested LLMs and multimodal models, 2 evaluated CNNs, and 1 tested other machine learning algorithms. Tested LLMs and multimodal models included ChatGPT (OpenAI) (n = 7), with 3 studies comparing different versions, along with Microsoft Bing Chat (Microsoft) (n = 1), Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic) (n = 2), Gemini 1.5 Pro (Google DeepMind) (n = 2), and other multimodal models. The primary outcome measured was diagnostic accuracy and/or correct response rate. Comparative studies of LLMs and multimodal models showed conflicting results. Cirkel et al (2025) reported that ChatGPT v4 was the most accurate in diagnosing psoriasis, vitiligo, erysipelas, and rosacea, outperforming Gemini 1.5 Pro, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Llama3.2-90B (Meta). Murthy et al (2025) reported that Microsoft Bing Chat outperformed ChatGPT v3.5 on 60 dermatology, venereology, and leprosy questions, while Yamamura et al (2025) found Claude 3.5 Sonnet outperforming ChatGPT v4 in diagnosing 30 medical cases. Comparisons across ChatGPT versions consistently showed that ChatGPT v4 outperformed earlier versions (Shetty et al, 2023; McCrary et al, 2024; Göçer Gürok and Öztürk, 2025). Studies using CNNs and machine learning algorithms mainly focused on disease classification (n = 2) (Lu et al, 2023; Kamulegeya et al, 2023). Konda and Rani Balmuri (2020) applied 9 machine learning algorithms to predict adverse events associated with glucocorticoid treatment.

Evaluating public AI models against dermatologists

Several studies (n = 4) compared model performance with that of dermatologists (Murthy et al, 2025; Yamamura et al, 2025; Göçer Gürok and Öztürk, 2025; Stoneham et al, 2024). Murthy et al (2025) found that Microsoft Bing Chat outperformed 12 final-year postgraduates on 60 questions in dermatology, venereology, and leprosy, achieving a correct response rate of 78.2% compared with 43% for the students. Yamamura et al (2025) reported that AI models achieved diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of dermatologists in 30 medical cases. Göçer Gürokand Öztürk (2025) reported that ChatGPT v4 performed comparably to first- and third-year senior dermatology residents in multiple-choice questions and passed a Turkish third-year dermatology residency exam with the correct response rate above the 60% passing threshold. However, the model showed lower performance compared with second- and fourth-year senior residents. In another study, Shetty et al (2023) reported that the model was able to correctly answer 85% of the questions in a dermatology specialty certificate examination.

The diagnostic accuracy of ChatGPT v4 ranged from 56% to 70% in cases or images of neoplastic, inflammatory, and vascular diseases (Yamamura et al, 2025; Cirkel et al, 2025; Stoneham et al, 2024). Stoneham et al (2024) reported that ChatGPT v4 achieved a diagnostic accuracy of 56% in 36 patient cases, outperforming nonspecialists (36%) but remaining lower than that of dermatologists (83%). In addition, ChatGPT showed lower diagnostic performance on patient data obtained by nonspecialist physicians (39%).

AI-enabled mobile health applications designed to support skin disease diagnosis

Following the assessment of publicly available AI models, we next turned to studies exploring AI integration into mobile health applications. We identified 4 studies that have developed and evaluated AI-based mobile applications for dermatological diagnosis, highlighting their potential as diagnostic support tools across a range of skin conditions (Supplementary Table S3) (Wu et al, 2020; Goceri, 2021; Mehta et al, 2024; Lee et al, 2023).

Wu et al (2020) developed a smartphone platform, artificial intelligence dermatology diagnosis assistant, capable of distinguishing healthy skin from psoriasis and eczema or atopic dermatitis, achieving high accuracy (95.8 ± 0.09), sensitivity (94.4 ± 0.12), and specificity (97.2 ± 0.06). At the time of the study, the application was already available to doctors in China, with more than 7000 registered physicians using it in 2020. Similarly, Goceri (2021) introduced a smartphone application designed to diagnose 5 skin diseases: hemangioma, acne vulgaris, psoriasis, rosacea, and seborrheic dermatitis. The application was implemented using a CNN architecture and trained on 725 photographs, with the best-performing framework, modified MobileNet, achieving an accuracy of 94.76%. Mehta et al (2024) assessed AI-based mobile tools in the context of genital diseases, comparing their performance with that of PCPs and dermatologists using 257 photos. The application significantly outperformed PCPs and performed comparably to dermatologists in genital disease classification (Mehta et al, 2024).

In addition, 4 studies evaluated the performance of clinical support tools (Panagoulias et al, 2025; Nervil et al, 2023; Felmingham et al, 2022; Majidian et al, 2022). Panagoulias et al (2025) developed a system to improve clinical workflow by integrating an image analysis model with a machine learning tool for segmentation and measuring tasks, thereby providing clinical decision support. The model was evaluated using an objective structured clinical examination-inspired method that incorporated both clinical information and images. The AI model provided a description of symptoms along with a diagnosis, which was subsequently rated by an expert with more than 15 years of clinical experience. The model achieved a weighted accuracy of 87%.

Nervil et al (2023) investigated whether training in skin cancer diagnostics with an AI algorithm could improve the diagnostic ability and confidence of PCPs. In this study, 115 PCPs were randomly assigned to either an intervention group (training) or a control group (no training) in a 3:1 ratio. Participants completed a multiple-choice questionnaire before and after the training to assess performance. PCPs in the intervention group significantly improved their diagnostic scores and confidence levels compared with the control group, whose performance did not change.

Majidian et al (2022) evaluated the role of AI in telemedicine triage and diagnostics using 100 clinical images of skin diseases, encompassing both malignant and benign lesions. The performance of the AI system was compared with that of 3 board-certified dermatologists (with an average of 18 years of experience), with biopsy results serving as the reference standard. Diagnostic performance was measured using top-3 accuracy, and no significant difference was observed between the AI system and the dermatologists. Furthermore, the AI showed comparable performance to the dermatologists in distinguishing benign lesions from basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and malignant melanoma.

Limitations of the use of AI in dermatology

Limitations were often, but not always, stated in the publications. The most commonly reported constraint was the limited size of the datasets used for model training and evaluation. These training datasets were also often restricted in the range of diagnoses and severity of disease, typically including conditions such as psoriasis, eczema, and melanoma, while rarely encompassing less prevalent skin diseases. In addition, the training datasets did not always encompass the full spectrum of skin types. The importance of including different skin types has been addressed by Kamulegeya et al (2023), who reported that AI achieved a top-5 accuracy of 17% for Fitzpatrick 6 skin types, compared with 69.9% for a dataset with primarily Fitzpatrick 1 and 2 skin types, which reflected the distribution used during model training. Training, validation, and testing were frequently conducted on the same dataset, potentially overestimating performance. Multiple studies were conducted in China using images from Chinese patients, which could influence how well the AI model performs on patients from other demographic or ethnic groups (Zhu et al, 2024). The images used in datasets were not standardized and varied in size, angle, and lighting, which complicates comparisons across studies. However, such variability can also be advantageous, as it may improve the generalizability of AI models to real-world clinical settings. Several studies did not include metadata, such as clinical history, and relied exclusively on clinical and/or dermoscopic images. In some studies, classification tasks were limited to binary outcomes, which do not capture the complexity of clinical practice, where multiple differential diagnoses are often considered. Some studies did not use histopathology as the reference standard for ground truth labeling, and errors or inconsistencies in diagnostic labeling could compromise the reliability and validity of model outcomes. This problem was stated in a study by Stutz et al (2025), where they also used statistical models to account for ground truth uncertainty.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that the use of AI in dermatology is a rapidly growing field of research, especially in its capacity to classify skin conditions. Among the 60 studies reviewed, multiple studies of AI models demonstrated diagnostic performance comparable to that of dermatologists within controlled, well-defined and limited settings, consistent with previous reviews (Karimzadhagh et al, 2026; Jeong et al, 2023). However, the methodological landscape reveals that challenges to clinical translation persist as a natural reflection of a field still finding its footing, and there is room to move toward more standardized approaches to study design, evaluation, and reporting.

Building representative and inclusive datasets, diagnostic labeling, and ground truth

The foundation of any effective AI system is its training data, and the quality, size, and diversity of datasets remain among the most significant limiting factors identified in this review. Effective model training requires images spanning the full clinical spectrum of a condition across all skin types (Daneshjou et al, 2022). Without balanced, representative datasets, AI models risk systematic underperformance on underrepresented populations, which is a concern with direct clinical consequences. Kamulegeya et al (2023) illustrate this, reporting a top-5 accuracy of 17% for Fitzpatrick type 6 images, compared with 69.9% for the model training dataset, which primarily included Fitzpatrick skin types 1 and 2. Furthermore, dermatologist-assigned labels used to train these models also demonstrate reduced accuracy for darker skin tones (Daneshjou et al, 2022), meaning that bias can be introduced at the level of ground truth itself. This combination of human and algorithmic bias underscores the importance of ensuring that both image datasets and diagnostic labeling are representative across all skin types.

The datasets reviewed were also frequently limited in diagnostic scope, predominantly covering common conditions such as psoriasis, eczema, and melanoma, while rarely including less prevalent skin diseases or capturing the full spectrum of disease severity. Multiple studies were conducted in single centers using images from specific demographic groups, limiting generalizability. Image standardization was also inconsistently applied across studies, with variation in size, angle, and lighting—although such variability may, in some contexts, improve model robustness in real—world settings.

Efforts to build more inclusive datasets, such as the Diverse Dermatology Images dataset (Daneshjou et al, 2022) and International Skin Imaging Collaboration archive (https://challenge.isic-archive.com), represent meaningful steps forward. Federated learning, in which model updates rather than raw patient data are shared across institutions, offers a promising strategy for overcoming privacy barriers while enabling training on geographically and demographically diverse data. Hossen et al (2023) demonstrated that model accuracy improved from 81.2% to 94.1% as the number of participating institutions increased, illustrating the scalability of this approach. Further development of federated learning frameworks holds considerable promise for the field.

A methodologically underappreciated issue is how ground truth is established in AI training and evaluation datasets. A number of studies used clinical images or dermatologist-assigned diagnoses as the reference standard. This approach, while common in clinical practice, differs from histopathological confirmation. Errors and inconsistencies in diagnostic labeling can compromise the validity of model outcomes, and Stutz et al (2025) took the important step of applying statistical models to explicitly account for ground truth uncertainty. Ideally, future studies would clearly define their reference standard and distinguish between biopsy-confirmed diagnosis, biopsy combined with clinical data, and expert consensus, and consider incorporating uncertainty quantifications.

The need for standardized evaluation approaches

A recurring limitation observed in this review is considerable variability in how AI models are evaluated. Studies differed in their choice of outcome metrics, test dataset composition, and comparative benchmarks. Training, validation, and testing were frequently conducted on partitions of the same dataset, which may inflate reported performance. Consistent with this concern, several studies found that models performed worse when tested on additional or external datasets (Han et al, 2020; Daneshjou et al, 2022; Rikhye et al, 2025). Together, these observations suggest that external validation should be considered an important standard for the field.

To enable more meaningful cross-study comparison, the field might benefit from large, easily accessible validation datasets against which AI models in dermatology could be assessed. Such datasets would ideally include high-quality images with histopathology-confirmed diagnoses, broad coverage of skin conditions and skin types, and comprehensive metadata. Public repositories or equivalent platforms could potentially host this resource, provided image selection criteria, labeling methodology, and ground truth procedures are transparently documented (Daneshjou and Kittler, 2024).

When AI performance is compared directly with clinicians, a methodological strength present in 18 of the 41 model development studies reviewed, evaluations are ideally conducted on identical image sets under clearly defined conditions. Reporting the seniority and specialty of physician comparators adds valuable context, as the present review found that AI models generally outperformed general practitioners and residents but not experienced dermatologists (Zhu et al, 2024; Han et al, 2020; Liu et al, 2020). This stratification helps clarify where in the clinical hierarchy AI assistance may be most beneficial.

Evaluating publicly available AI systems

A subset of studies evaluated publicly available AI systems, including ChatGPT, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 1.5 Pro, and Microsoft Bing Chat, on dermatological classification and examination questions. Results were mixed, and comparisons across studies were confounded by differences in test sets, question formats, and model versions. ChatGPT v4 diagnostic accuracy ranged from 56% to 70% across neoplastic, inflammatory, and vascular conditions (Yamamura et al, 2025; Cirkel et al, 2025; Stoneham et al, 2024). Stoneham et al (2024) found that ChatGPT v4 outperformed nonspecialists but did not reach the diagnostic performance of experienced dermatologists. Comparisons across ChatGPT versions consistently showed improvement with newer iterations, underscoring the rapid evolution of these systems. The role of LLMs in dermatology is distinct from that of CNN-based image classifiers, and their strengths lie in synthesizing clinical history, generating differential diagnoses, and supporting patient communication rather than in image interpretation per se. Future evaluations should be designed with this distinction in mind, using clinically realistic test cases that integrate both visual and contextual information.

Clinical context and the path to implementation

An increased orientation toward clinical relevance may be one valuable direction to consider. As an increasing number of AI models demonstrate improved diagnostic performance, attention should shift toward their integration into clinical practice. This focus is essential for advancing the field toward clinical implementation, which, for many models, could constitute the primary endpoint where relevant. For these studies, it would be a strength if the intended clinical use of AI models is described and a suggested approach for implementation is provided.

It is worth acknowledging, however, that the field is still evolving, and so is the collective understanding of how best to develop and deploy AI in clinical practice. Studies demonstrating AI performance in artificial or controlled settings, even without a clearly defined clinical pathway, represent a natural and necessary phase in this stage, and their contribution to the field should not be understated. That said, as the evidence and knowledge base matures, greater emphasis on clinical context becomes increasingly important. Many reviewed studies demonstrated AI model performance without articulating how the model addresses a clinical need or would function within a care pathway, and high accuracy on a curated test set, while valuable in the development of these systems and tools, remains insufficient proof of clinical utility on its own.

AI could be well suited to support clinical decisions rather than be a replacement for clinical judgment. AI assistance improved diagnostic accuracy among junior dermatologists from 66.9% to 81.3% (Li et al, 2024) and increased diagnostic precision in physicians with fewer than 3 years of experience by 17% (Wang et al, 2024). AI-enabled training improved the diagnostic scores and confidence of PCPs in skin cancer detection (Nervil et al, 2023). FDA-approved tools such as MelaFind and DermaSensor demonstrate that AI clinical decision support can reach regulatory approval for specific, well-defined use cases (Nahm et al, 2025; Venkatesh et al, 2024). However, regulatory approval requires demonstration of safety rather than optimal diagnostic balance, and both tools have so far demonstrated limited specificity in practice (Venkatesh et al, 2024).

An important and underexplored dimension is the differential suitability of AI architectures across disease categories. Single-lesion classification tasks, such as distinguishing melanoma from atypical nevus, are well-suited to CNN-based image classifiers, which excel at extracting fine-grained visual features such as asymmetry, border irregularity, and color variation from dermoscopic images. In contrast, diagnosing generalized inflammatory dermatoses, such as distinguishing drug eruption from atopic dermatitis, relies heavily on the distribution and morphology of lesions across the body, the temporal evolution of symptoms, and integration of clinical history, factors that a single-image CNN may be poorly positioned to capture. These tasks may be better served by multimodal architectures that combine image analysis with structured clinical data, or by large language models capable of reasoning across history, symptoms, and laboratory findings.

While a classifier determines whether a condition is present, a segmentation model delineates the boundaries of affected skin, enabling quantification of lesional area across body regions. This distinction has direct clinical relevance in, for example, severity scoring tools such as the PASI, and assessments of vitiligo subtype and treatment response depend on accurate measurement of affected surface area over time, tasks that align naturally with the capabilities of segmentation models and that extend well beyond simple disease identification.

Rare diseases with limited training data present yet another challenge. Future research should investigate which AI methodologies are best suited to each diagnostic paradigm.

Toward a framework for future studies

Drawing on the evidence reviewed and the gaps identified, we suggest that future studies in AI-assisted dermatological diagnosis would benefit from working toward a shared set of methodological standards (Supplementary Table S4, Figure 2). Ideally, datasets used for training and validation would be large, diverse, and inclusive of all skin types, with histopathologically confirmed diagnoses where feasible. Training and test data should be clearly distinguished, and where public datasets are used, the specific images and selection criteria ought to be reported. External validation, whether on an independent public benchmark dataset or through prospective clinical testing, is increasingly important and should be encouraged as the field evolves. Where performance is compared with clinicians, identical image sets should be used, physician seniority and specialty should be specified, and assessment procedures should be clearly defined. Making code, model weights, and datasets publicly available would further strengthen reproducibility. Clearly stated limitations should be considered an expected component of any study. It may also be worth considering whether journals could play a role in encouraging adherence to such criteria, not as a barrier to publication, but as a shared commitment to the methodological standards that would allow this research to ultimately benefit patients. As the field matures, the central question is no longer whether AI can classify skin disease in optimized settings, but whether it can do so reliably, equitably, and usefully across the full diversity of clinical practice.

This review has certain limitations related to the literature search and selection process. Although multiple keywords were used to search PubMed and EMBASE, some relevant studies may not have been identified. The search was restricted to English-language publications and to titles containing AI- and dermatology-related terms, which may have led to the exclusion of relevant studies published in other languages or without these specific keywords.

Strengths of this study include measures taken to minimize these risks. Two reviewers independently screened all articles, and highly cited and relevant references were included to further reduce the likelihood of omissions.

Conclusion

This review highlights the potential of AI to transform dermatology, especially in image-based diagnosis and clinical decision support. At the same time, it underscores important barriers to clinical implementation that still need to be addressed. Future research should prioritize the development of more diverse and representative datasets that encompass all skin types and conditions, external validation in real-world clinical settings, and the integration of clinical history with image analysis. In addition, these systems should employ transparent processes that explain how diagnostic suggestions are generated, fostering clinician trust while protecting patient safety and privacy. The theoretical promise of AI can only be translated into practical improvements in dermatological care if these fundamental challenges are addressed.
