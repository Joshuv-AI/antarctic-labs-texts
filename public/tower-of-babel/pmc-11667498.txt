Timing of Alzheimer's disease biomarker progressions: A two‐decade observational study from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)
Authors: Tamar Schaap, Pamela Thropp, Duygu Tosun, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAlzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) has been pivotal in identifying and refining Alzheimer's disease (AD) biomarkers for clinical trials. This study leverages longitudinal data from participants who have progressed to amyloid‐positivity during their study participation to track evolution of biomarkers and cognitive function.METHODSWe modeled AD biomarker (positron emission tomography [PET], structural, cerebrospinal fluid [CSF], cognition) trajectories before and after observed amyloid‐positivity onset time to detect time at which each biomarker had detectable trajectory changes.RESULTSAnalysis of a sub‐cohort of the 20‐year ADNI study (N = 90) recapitulated Alzheimer's progression beginning with amyloid alterations ‐4.8 to ‐5.3 years relative to amyloid‐positivity, succeeded by neurodegeneration (t = ‐4.0 to ‐4.1 years), and CSF tau (t = ‐0.4 to ‐0.5 years). Cognitive decline was observed to significantly correspond with emergence of amyloid‐positivity (t = 0.2 to 2.4 years).DISCUSSIONOur results corroborate temporal progression curves of AD biomarkers, providing insights on earliest detectable changes in objective and subjective cognitive function assessments.Keywords: amyloid phenotype progression, amyloid positivity, amyloid time, biomarker progression, subjective cognitive decline

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD), a neurodegenerative disorder of insidious onset and progressive nature, is characterized by cognitive decline and dementia. Its distinct neuropathological hallmarks, amyloid‐beta (Aβ) plaques, and tau neurofibrillary tangles, are widely recognized as pivotal players in neuronal loss and synaptic dysfunction. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 The trajectory of these pathophysiological events and their relation to clinical disease progression was originally introduced as the amyloid‐cascade model 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 and later elegantly delineated by Jack et al. who modeled the temporal progression of major AD biomarkers. 5 , 6 This model, instrumental in shaping our understanding of AD progression, posits that abnormal amyloid‐pathology accumulation starts approximately two decades before symptomatic onset, followed by a cascade of events including tau‐pathology, neurodegeneration, and cognitive decline.

Substantial efforts have been invested in validating and recapitulating the AD biomarker temporal progression curves through longitudinal observations in cohort studies. Since 1948, over 50 longitudinal cohort studies have been established worldwide to connect symptoms with pathology and cognitive decline (reviewed in 7 , 8 ). Most of these cohorts have less than 500 individuals, ages range from 70 to 75 years, all observe at least two measures, and the average length of follow‐up is 3–5 years. Biomarker measures include different combinations of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), cerebral spinal fluid (CSF), genetics, and plasma, along with functional, neuropsychiatric, and cognitive evaluations. 7 Non‐standardized approaches (e.g., MRI acquisition protocols) curb comparative, cross‐cohort analysis, and the temporal scope and selective use of biomarkers often falls short of the long, progressive duration of AD, thereby limiting a comprehensive understanding of disease progression.

Only a few longitudinal cohorts have persisted for longer than two decades, the length of time estimated from the onset of amyloid‐plaque deposition to the realization of cognitive symptoms. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Recently, Jia et al. presented a longitudinal study on changes in AD biomarkers spanning 20 years preceding AD diagnosis. 12 The China Cognition and Aging Study (COAST) compared AD‐related cognitive, hippocampal, and CSF biomarker changes between those who eventually were diagnosed with AD to those who never converted. Using clinical diagnosis as a time factor, they found that CSF Aβ42 diagnostic levels changed first, 18 years before AD diagnosis, followed by p‐tau181 (∼11 years before), neurofilament light chain (∼9.5 years), hippocampal volume (∼8 years), and finally cognitive decline (∼6 years) before AD diagnosis. 12 To our knowledge, this is the only AD study that projects AD biomarker changes resembling the temporal order presented by Jack et al. and stretches 20 years before diagnosis.

In this study, using time‐to and ‐from amyloid‐positivity (in contrast to time‐to‐symptom‐onset) as a biological disease clock, we used data from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) study to map the progression of several AD biomarkers over 20 years. The ADNI is a large multisite, longitudinal observational cohort study that has been tracking progression of cognitive function and AD biomarkers from cognitively unimpaired (CU) elderly, individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and individuals with clinical diagnoses of dementia due to AD. The ADNI is a unique cohort not only in size (> 2800 participants), but also the number of biomarkers analyzed. Collaborators from over 60 sites work with standardized protocols to allow for comparative datasets from several entities including MRI, PET, CSF, plasma, genetics, and cognitive analysis allowing for a more comprehensive picture across the AD continuum.

As the ADNI celebrates its 20th anniversary, a unique opportunity arises to examine the long‐term progression of AD in a subset of participants who have been observed through different ADNI‐cycles and transitioned from amyloid− to amyloid+ (i.e., amyloid‐phenoconverters). This rich, longitudinal dataset allows for a more nuanced understanding of the natural history of AD. Leveraging this unique sub‐cohort from the ADNI and the ability to observe the amyloid‐phenoconversion, this study aims to explore the progression of AD‐related biomarkers and clinical measures, offering new insights into the long‐term trajectory of this devastating disease.

METHODS

Participants

Data were obtained from the ADNI. Participants were selected if they had amyloid‐phenoconversion during their participation in ADNI using pre‐established amyloid‐PET positivity cutoff of Centiloid ≥ 20. Participants were excluded if their Centiloids were volatile (became amyloid− after being amyloid+; N = 25). The selection process, with inclusion and exclusion criteria, are diagrammed in Figure 1.

Flowchart showing selection process for participants. Participants must have had more than one amyloid PET scan and phenoconverted from amyloid− to amyloid+ during their participation in ADNI to be included in this study. ADNI, Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative; PET, positron emission tomography.

Outcome measures of interest

All data were downloaded from https//ida.loni.usc.edu. We investigated the trajectory of several biomarkers across the time into amyloid‐positivity. These biomarkers included: (1) biomarker measures of amyloid‐PET Centiloid 13 ; (2) CSF Aβ42, total‐tau, and p‐tau181 using the Roche Elecsys immunoassays 14 ; (3) measures of neurodegeneration—hippocampal volume and meta‐region‐of‐interest (meta‐ROI; entorhinal, inferior temporal, middle temporal, and fusiform) cortical thickness measured by structural‐MRI 15 using FreeSurfer framework 16 ; (4) meta‐ROI (bilateral angular gyri, posterior cingulate gyrus, and the inferior temporal gyri normalized to pons) glucose metabolism estimated from FDG‐PET 17 ; (5) measures of global cognition: the Clinical Dementia Rating – Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB), Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale‐Cognitive Subscale 13‐item (ADAS‐Cog13), Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE), and Preclinical Alzheimer Cognitive Composite (PACC) 18 ; and (6) subjective cognitive decline measures of self‐reported everyday cognition (Ecog–Subject), and study partner‐reported everyday cognition (Ecog–Partner). In Ecog–Subject and Ecog–Partner, values answered as “I don't know” were considered as missing data, and global scores were estimated using previously published methods. 19

Hippocampal volume and meta‐ROI from structural‐MRIs were limited to scans that had been acquired using the ADNI‐GO/2/3 3‐Tesla MRI protocols. To account for variance due to differences in MRI acquisitions across different ADNI‐cycles and the FreeSurfer version used in each cycle, 20 hippocampal volume measures were first adjusted for intracranial volume (ICV), within each ADNI‐cycle (i.e., ADNI‐GO/2 versus ADNI‐3) separately, using a residual modeling method based on data from CU individuals. 21 Meta‐ROI cortical thickness and ICV‐adjusted hippocampal volume measures were then harmonized across ADNI‐cycles using ComBAT‐GAM, 22 while preserving biological variance due to age and sex, with age as a nonlinear factor. Finally, normal aging effects on hippocampal volume (ICV‐adjusted and harmonized) and meta‐ROI cortical thickness (harmonized) were estimated within amyloid− CU individuals and regressed out from the corresponding measures from all individuals, using age and quadratic age as predictors. The steps for ICV‐adjustment, ComBAT‐GAM harmonization, and adjusting for normal effects of age utilized all available ADNI‐GO/2/3 data for unbiased modeling.

Statistical analysis

Each individual's amyloid‐phenoconversion date was estimated as the weighted midpoint between their last amyloid‐PET− (Centiloid < 20) date and first amyloid‐PET+ (Centiloid≥20) date, where the weights were inversely proportional to the absolute difference from Centiloid = 20. For each individual, their t = 0 was set to amyloid‐phenoconversion date, and time‐to‐amyloid‐positivity (referred as amyloid‐time hereon) was estimated for each biomarker and cognitive measure observations.

We estimated the trajectory of each biomarker over amyloid‐time using Generalized Additive Mixed‐effects Models (GAMs). 23 Amyloid‐time was included as an unpenalized smoothing term with four knots in each model. Additionally, we controlled for age (for outcomes that were not age‐adjusted), gender, apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype, and years of education. The models for clinical and cognitive outcome measures were further adjusted to include global dementia status (CDR) to control for variations in cognitive function that occur independently of biological biomarker levels. APOE genotype was simplified for the analysis; homozygotes were obscured: ε2/ε3 participants (N = 5) were considered ε2; ε3/ε3 (N = 51) were considered ε3; ε3/ε4 (N = 32) or ε4/ε4 (N = 2) were considered ε4. Finally, we included a random intercept to account for repeated measures. Fitted GAMs were then assessed to identify time of detectable changes in its trajectory from amyloid‐positivity. Specifically, we employed a finite differencing approach to calculate the slopes of the GAMs as a function of years from amyloid‐positivity, representing the rate of change in the outcome measures per unit increase in years from amyloid‐positivity. Change in outcome measure was deemed significantly different from zero when their estimated confidence intervals did not include zero.

RESULTS

Cohort characteristics

Records from 90 participants (57–93 years) were used in the current study. Of these, 54.4% were female (38.9% CU, 14.4% MCI, 1.1% Dementia) and 45.6% were male (31.1% CU, 13.3% MCI, 1.1% Dementia), as detailed in Table 1. The APOE ε2 allele was least common and only heterozygous with ε3 (5.6%), while most participants (56.7%) were homozygous for APOE ε3. The APOE ε4 allele was more commonly paired with ε3 (35.6%), and two individuals were ε4/ε4. Most participants (95.6%) were white, 4.4% were Black or mixed race (all CU), 5.6% were Hispanic or Latino (all CU), and 94.4% of participants were not Hispanic or Latino.

Data for all available outcome measures from participants were analyzed. While all participants had cognitive measures available, 92.2% of participants had MRI, 85.6% had CSF, and 85.6% had FDG‐PET available. The number of timepoints and duration of logged data differed by biomarker, with cognitive data having the longest mean duration of logged observations and the most amount of timepoints (Table 1).

AD biomarker and cognitive trajectories

AD biomarker trajectories were modeled based on time‐to‐amyloid‐positivity, spanning roughly 20 years of data collection. While not all participants in the current study have 20 consecutive years of data, they do have several years of acquired data that spans the time of their own amyloid‐phenoconversion. Their varying longitudinal data allowed us to collectively model roughly 10 years before and after amyloid‐phenoconversion. Finite differencing was used to determine the time of detectable increase or decrease in biomarker trajectories as functions of amyloid‐time (Figures 2, 3). Detectable changes in amyloid occur first, followed by neurodegeneration, tau, and eventually cognitive decline on the path of AD progression.

Specifically, increase in amyloid‐PET uptake and decrease in CSF Aβ42 are evident first and become significant 5.3 and 4.8 years before amyloid‐positivity is detected, respectively (Figure 2A‐B). Hippocampal volume and cortical thickness decrease significantly around 4 years before amyloid‐positivity (Figure 2E‐F). Approximately 3.5 years later, CSF p‐tau181 and total‐tau begin to increase significantly just before amyloid‐positivity is detected (Figure 2C (−0.5 years), 2D (−0.4 years)). FDG‐PET meta‐ROI does not significantly change during the timeline captured in this study (Figure 2G).

Significant changes in measures of global cognition become evident after time of amyloid‐positivity onset, starting with MMSE at 0.2 years (Figure 3C), followed by CDR‐SB at 1.4 years (Figure 3A), PACC at 1.9 years (Figure 3D), and ADAS‐Cog13 at 2.0 years (Figure 3B). Study partners reported significant change in Ecog‐Partner 1.4 years after amyloid‐positivity onset (Figure 3E), while subjects themselves started reporting significant changes in their cognition 2.4 years after amyloid‐positivity onset (Figure 3F).

DISCUSSION

The longitudinal data collected over a span of two decades from the ADNI study presents an unparalleled opportunity to deepen our understanding of the temporal progression of biomarker and cognitive measures, particularly during the amyloid‐phenoconversion. Our study leverages this extensive dataset where the true timing of amyloid‐phenoconversion was observed to shed light on the natural progression of AD‐related biomarkers and clinical measures, providing valuable insights into the earliest detectable changes in the trajectory of this neurodegenerative disorder. We analyzed multimodal data spanning a 20‐year period obtained from a cohort of 90 ADNI participants, for whom we observed a clear transition from amyloid− to amyloid+ phases. Our findings indicate that the progression of AD initiates with detectable amyloid alterations occurring many years prior to the onset of amyloid‐positivity; amyloid‐PET Centiloid begins to significantly increase roughly 5.3 years before amyloid‐positivity, and CSF Aβ42 starts significantly decreasing around 4.8 years prior to amyloid‐positivity. Subsequently, neurodegeneration becomes evident around 4 years before amyloid‐positivity, followed by tau pathology approximately 0.5 years prior. Further, our study demonstrated a significant measurable cognitive decline across six outcomes with the emergence of amyloid‐positivity. Specifically, we observed the earliest decline in cognitive function with MMSE, occurring as early as 0.2 years after the onset of amyloid‐positivity. Additionally, subjective cognitive decline assessments indicated detectable cognitive decline between 1.4 to 2.4 years following the emergence of amyloid‐positivity.

Our findings align with the fundamental premise of the AD biomarker progression curves, 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 underscoring the pivotal role of amyloid‐pathology as an early event in the disease process. Except for two individuals diagnosed with AD Dementia, the remaining 88 individuals in this sub‐cohort were either CU (N = 63) or MCI (N = 25) at the time of amyloid‐phenoconversion. Thus, our results show measurable, significant changes in biomarkers and cognitive measures reflective of early changes in the AD spectrum with relation to an amyloid‐positivity clock.

All cognitive measures became significant within three years of amyloid‐positivity detection in this sub‐cohort (Figure 3). The subtle changes among cognitive measures (Figure 3A‐D), and particularly between the Ecog analyses (Figure 3E‐F) supports early stages of AD, where cognitive decline is evident. Using clinical diagnosis as a time measure (time‐to‐dementia), Jia et al reported that CDR‐SB changes occur nearly a decade after CSF Aβ42 changes. 12 This supports our results for early stages and suggests that these individuals are entering the phase of clinically meaningful cognitive decline.

Our study extends the existing temporal model for AD biomarker progression by providing empirical evidence based on long‐term observations, accentuating the progressive nature of AD. Our results, specifically with amyloid‐PET (Figure 2A), are in line with a recent study of AD onset using amyloid‐PET data from three large cohorts, including the ADNI. Specifically, amyloid‐PET values first start to become abnormal 2.3–2.8 years before the amyloid‐PET positivity threshold of 20 Centiloids. 24 This critical aspect of disease progression has often been inadequately captured in shorter‐term cohort studies, 25 , 26 highlighting the unique contribution of our analysis.

Several studies have used longitudinal data to examine the progression of AD biomarkers in the context of amyloid‐chronicity or amyloid‐time based on data from amyloid‐PET. 9 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 This approach has been useful because trajectories can show changes in biomarker outcomes spanning a time period when someone amyloid‐phenoconverts. This method also helps to alleviate concerns regarding harmonized approaches of amyloid‐PET interpretation. 32 , 33 , 34 An important and novel aspect of our study is that we analyzed all outcome measures within the same individuals, spanning the window of time when they amyloid‐phenoconverted, more accurately representing biological trends across amyloid‐phenoconversion.

It is worth noting that the detection of neurodegeneration prior to an increase in CSF tau measures observed in our study does not entirely align with the expected sequence of events proposed by others. 6 , 12 The earlier emergence of neurodegeneration observed in our study might be due to neuroimaging measures of neurodegeneration capturing a broader spectrum of brain changes including those due to other neurodegenerative processes, vascular factors, and even neuroinflammation, which might not be immediately reflected by changes in CSF tau levels. The larger proportion of participants with MCI at the time of amyloid‐PET phenoconversion and the older age of participants at phenoconversion (> 70 years on average) also support the possibility of co‐occurring non‐AD pathologies. These factors may contribute to an accelerated or atypical progression of neurodegeneration independent of classical amyloid and tau pathologies.

However, we acknowledge certain limitations. Even though the ADNI offers a unique opportunity to observe long‐term disease progression, the findings are drawn from a sub‐cohort of participants who amyloid‐phenoconverted. Therefore, generalizing these findings to all AD patients should be approached with caution. In addition, not all individuals in this sub‐cohort had data in all outcome measures. In particular, we may not have had adequate power to detect significant changes in FDG‐PET meta‐ROI SUVR due to small sample size and limited follow‐up observations per subject. The lack of ethnoracial diversity in the ADNI is a limitation, despite its widespread geographical reach. Additionally, some participants’ PET scan dates were quite far apart. While most scans took place within a time frame that allowed us to estimate amyloid‐phenoconversion time within relative certainty (2.36 ± 0.83 years), there were also participants whose estimated amyloid‐phenoconversion times might have greater uncertainty (e.g., N = 10; 11.1% for whom their last amyloid− scan and their first amyloid+ scan were more than four years apart). Finally, an important limitation of our study is the use of CSF p‐tau181 as a surrogate biomarker for tau‐pathology, since tau‐PET was available for only a limited number of participants, making it difficult to thoroughly investigate the temporal progression of tau‐pathology as a function of amyloid‐time.
