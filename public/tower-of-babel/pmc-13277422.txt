Evaluation of proximal ulna morphology: a systematic review of varus angulation of the proximal ulna
Authors: Chan M Khin, Julian Okorocha, Flavia Stettler, Fiona L Ashton, Joideep S Phadnis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVarus angulation (VA) of the proximal ulna is a key morphological feature influencing fracture reduction, implant design, and elbow biomechanics. Despite its clinical significance, reported values and measurement techniques vary widely. The aim of this study was to systematically review and synthesize the existing literature on proximal ulnar VA in adults, establish normative values, evaluate methodological variation, and recommend standardized assessment approaches and values for the VA.MethodsFollowing Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, electronic databases (MEDLINE, Embase, and Cochrane Library) were searched from January 2000 to November 2024. Eligible studies reported quantitative measurements of VA in adults without pathology. Data on study design, measurement technique, imaging modality, and risk of bias (assessed with the Anatomical Quality Assessment Tool) were extracted and analyzed.ResultsSeventeen studies were included. Imaging modalities used to measure VA were computed tomography (CT) (n = 7), X-ray (n = 1), and cadaveric analysis (n = 9). Reported VA means ranged from 6.6° to 17.7°, with a pooled mean of 12.4° ± 2.9°. Tip-to-varus apex distance ranged from 47.8 mm to 99.7 mm (pooled mean 77.6 mm ± 4.2 mm). Significant methodological heterogeneity was observed, particularly in anatomical landmarks (eg, olecranon tip, posterior cortex, triceps insertion) and measurement lines (axis-based vs. vertical). Analysis of variance comparing VA across CT, X-ray, and cadaveric studies demonstrated no statistically significant difference between modalities (F ≈ 0.83, P > .40). This indicates that modality choice did not meaningfully influence reported VA means.ConclusionReported proximal ulnar varus angulation and apex position demonstrate a reproducible central tendency despite substantial between-study heterogeneity driven primarily by landmark and axis definition rather than imaging modality. For clinical reconstruction, restoring approximately 10–15° of varus with the apex located 70–90 mm distal to the olecranon tip is a pragmatic target range. CT-based, axis-defined methods offer the most consistent and reproducible estimates and should be adopted as the preferred reference standard for research reporting and pre-operative planning.Keywords: Proximal ulna, Varus angulation, Elbow anatomy, Imaging modalities, Computed tomography, Cadaveric study, Orthopedic implant design, Systematic review

Fractures of the proximal ulna are relatively common, with a reported incidence of 21.4 per 100,000 patients over a 7-year period.26 Fracture patterns range from simple isolated proximal ulna fractures to highly complex multifragmentary fractures with other osseo-ligamentous injuries.

Fixation of these injuries is a challenge, not least because the proximal ulna has a variable anatomy in the coronal, sagittal, and axial plane. Pre-contoured plates aim to match this anatomy but cannot be correct for every patient. The 2 most studied angles in the proximal ulna are the proximal ulnar dorsal angulation and varus angulation (VA).16,18,22 Failure to restore these anatomical parameters intraoperatively has been associated with a measurable reduction in elbow range of motion and forearm rotation, with average losses of 6° in each plane.18 Altered varus or dorsal angulation can also disrupt the congruency of the trochlear notch and radial head articulation, increasing joint contact pressures and contributing to long-term instability and stiffness.16,20

A thorough understanding of proximal ulnar morphology is essential for optimizing surgical outcomes, implant design, and pre-operative planning. The VA is defined as the medial deviation of the proximal ulna relative to the ulna shaft. Despite its clinical importance, significant variability exists in the definition, measurement, and reporting of VA across published studies. This means interpretation of data between individual studies may not be reliable and could lead to errors in implant design.

The aim of this systematic review is to critically appraise and consolidate the existing literature on proximal ulnar varus angulation in adults. This review seeks to establish normative values and ranges for varus angulation, evaluate the variability introduced by different imaging modalities and measurement techniques, and address inconsistencies in reported values. Furthermore, it aims to provide evidence-based recommendations to standardize the assessment of varus angulation, ultimately enhancing surgical planning, implant design, and biomechanical research.

Materials and methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines,14 to identify studies that have undertaken quantitative measurement of proximal ulnar varus angulation (VA) to define the morphology of the proximal ulna.

An ethics application was submitted to the Brighton and Sussex Medical School Research Governance and Ethics Committee and approved without board review, as this was a systematic review. All cadaveric images used were obtained with permission under the auspices of the anatomy HTA license held by Brighton and Sussex Medical School.

Data sources and search strategy

A systematic and comprehensive literature search was conducted across 3 electronic databases: MEDLINE ALL (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), and The Cochrane Library, covering the period from January 1, 2000, to November 1, 2024. The search strategy combined relevant keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) using Boolean operators, including terms related to the proximal ulna anatomy (“Proximal Ulna,” “Olecranon,” “Coronoid,” “Radial Notch,” “Trochlear Notch,” “Ulna Anatomy”) combined with morphological descriptors (“Morphology,” “Measurement,” “Angulation,” “Varus Angulation”). The detailed search strategy is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Unpublished studies and gray literature were not included in this review. Additionally, manual searches of the reference lists of relevant systematic reviews, narrative reviews, meta-analyses, and included articles were performed to identify any additional eligible studies. All identified records were imported into EndNote reference management software and deduplicated before screening.

This systematic review protocol was prospectively registered with International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (registration number CRD420251088051).

Study selection

Titles of journal articles retrieved from the initial search were screened for relevance. Abstracts of all remaining studies were reviewed to identify references to varus angulation (VA). In cases where the abstract did not provide sufficient detail, full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. To minimize selection bias, the search, screening, and final selection were independently performed by 3 reviewers (J.O., F.A., and J.P.). Consensus from all 3 reviewers was required for final article inclusion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure relevance and methodological consistency.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: studies of healthy human ulna in those aged 18 years or older, reporting quantitative measurements of proximal ulna varus angulation. Variations in terminology describing coronal plane angulation, such as proximal ulna varus and diaphyseal alignment angle, were considered within the scope of varus angulation. Eligible studies were published in English or another language that could be reliably translated using Google Translate. Exclusion criteria included studies examining non-human ulnae; those with pre-existing pathologies—including previous trauma, congenital or degenerative deformity, neoplastic, metabolic, or genetic elbow pathologies; studies that did not report quantitative VA measurements; or publication types including letters to the editor, conference abstracts, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias for the included studies was assessed using the Anatomical Quality Assessment (AQUA) Tool,9 which is specifically designed for evaluating anatomical and morphological studies. This tool examines 5 domains: (1) Objectives and Subject Characteristics, (2) Study Design, (3) Methodology Characterization, (4) Descriptive Anatomy, and (5) Reporting of Results. Each domain was assessed as low, moderate, or high risk of bias, and an overall risk rating was assigned accordingly. Two reviewers conducted the assessment independently, and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Data extraction

For each eligible study, we recorded authorship, year, country, journal of publication, sample size, and participant demographics, including age and gender. Methodological details including study type (clinical vs cadaveric), anatomical landmarks, and imaging or measurement modalities (eg, radiographs, 3-dimensional [3D] computed tomography [CT], cadaveric assessment). Varus angulation (VA) and tip-to-varus apex distance (TTA) measurements were extracted in degrees and millimeters, respectively.

Statistical methods

Varus angulation (VA) was recorded in degrees (°), with mean values and standard deviations extracted and tabulated. Weighted means were calculated to account for sample size and methodology. Analysis of variance with post-hoc Tukey tests assessed group differences, using P < .05 for significance. Summary estimates and visualizations of pooled means were generated using Meta-Analysis Online7 (https://www.metaanalysisonline.com), a web-based meta-analysis platform. Random-effects models (DerSimonian–Laird method) were applied to calculate pooled means and 95% confidence intervals for continuous data (varus angulation in degrees and TTA in millimeters). Standard deviations were used as reported or approximated from ranges where necessary. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic.

Results

Initial searches identified a total of 1,374 articles. After initially removing 60 nonrelevant articles, 1,314 titles and abstracts were screened, with 64 full-text articles assessed for eligibility. Seventeen studies met inclusion criteria, including 7 identified through reference hand searching. The detailed study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA14 flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Varus angulation measurements

Varus angulation was reported in all included studies, ranging from 6.6° to 17.7°, with a pooled mean of 12.4° ± 2.9° (Fig. 2A). Approximately 60% of studies reported mean VA values within 1 standard deviation of the pooled estimate (9.5°–15.3°), indicating a relatively tight central distribution despite methodological heterogeneity. A small number of studies produced values around 1.5 standard deviations or more from the pooled mean and were therefore considered methodological outliers: 219,22 reporting lower mean VAs (6.6° and 8.5°) and 38,23,24 reporting higher means (16.1°-17.7°). Lower values originated from CT-based studies using alternative definitions of the ulnar bow apex, whereas higher values were typically derived from cadaveric measurements referencing the posterior border of the ulna.

Tip-to-varus apex distance measurements

TTA was reported in 141,3,6,8,10,11,15,17,19,21, 22, 23, 24, 25 of 17 studies (82%), ranging from 47.8 mm to 99.7 mm, with a pooled mean of 77.6 ± 4.2 mm (Fig. 2B). Approximately 80% of studies reported mean TTA values between 70 mm and 85 mm, consistent with the pooled average range. Two studies reported distances that deviated substantially from the pooled central estimate and were considered methodological outliers: 1 study10 reporting a markedly shorter distance of 47.8 mm and another19 reporting a longer distance of 99.7 mm. The lower value originated from a CT-based study using a proximal olecranon reference point, whereas the higher value was derived from a 3D reconstruction technique that measured from more distal and posterior landmarks.

For 2 studies11,25 that expressed the varus apex as a percentage of total ulnar length, absolute TTA values were calculated using the pooled mean ulnar length of 257.1 mm (95% CI: 254.8–259.4 mm). These adjusted data were incorporated into the pooled mean to ensure comprehensive inclusion across all 14 studies.

Terminology and grouping of measurements

Across studies, several overlapping terms were used to describe the same parameters. To ensure comparability, equivalent descriptors were grouped as follows:

Varus angulation (VA): encompassed terms such as proximal ulnar varus, varus angle, and diaphyseal alignment angle, all representing medial deviation of the proximal ulna relative to its shaft in the coronal plane.

TTA: unified terms including varus apex distance, point of maximum varus angulation (PMVA) distance, and bow apex distance, all describing the linear distance from the olecranon tip (or proximal reference point) to the apex of maximum varus curvature.

Grouping these descriptors allowed consistent pooling of data and direct comparison between studies despite variations in terminology and measurement definition.

Although cadaveric studies showed a slightly wider range of reported VA values (8.5°–17.7°), the weighted means were nearly identical across modalities (CT 12.3°, X-ray 12.7°, cadaver 12.2°), and statistical testing demonstrated no significant modality effect.

A Welch's t-test comparing TTA between CT and cadaveric studies demonstrated a statistically significant difference (t = −4.72, df ≈ 798, P < .001), with CT-based methods reporting slightly shorter distances.

These findings indicate that while both modalities identify similar central estimates of proximal ulnar morphology, CT-based measurements converged on slightly lower TTA values, whereas differences in landmark definition likely explain most of the residual variability between modalities.

Discussion

This systematic review consolidates evidence on proximal ulnar varus angulation (VA) and TTA, summarizing measurement techniques, normative values, and variability. VA demonstrated a consistent anatomical pattern across studies, despite wide variation in measurement techniques. Although CT, X-ray, and cadaveric analyses differed in their methodological approaches, the overall magnitude of the varus angle was comparable between modalities.

Variation in reported values was largely attributable to heterogeneity in measurement methods and anatomical landmarks. Studies using axis-based definitions with clear reference points (typically the olecranon tip and ulnar shaft axis) produced the most consistent results, yielding mean values of approximately 12° for varus angulation and around 78 mm for TTA, closely matching the pooled means of this review. In contrast, posterior border, triceps insertion, and PMVA-based methods showed measurement patterns that did not align with the majority of comparable studies, and vertical or nonaligned approaches displayed wider variation. Cadaveric measurements also showed greater heterogeneity than CT-based analyses, which could plausibly be attributed to positional variation, tissue distortion, and inconsistent landmark interpretation.

The forest plots showed markedly greater spread of values among studies of the VA than for TTA. This indicates that varus angle measurements are highly sensitive to methodological factors such as axis construction and imaging orientation, which can shift angular values even with minor landmark differences. In contrast, TTA represents a linear parameter which may be less influenced by rotational alignment, resulting in tighter clustering and less apparent variability. These findings suggest that the location of the varus apex is relatively consistent, whereas the measured degree of varus is more dependent on the specific measurement technique employed.

Similar methodological variability has been reported in other descriptions of proximal ulna and elbow morphology. Studies assessing ulnar torsion and rotational profiles have shown that even defining the “ulnar axis” is not consistent across authors and that reported angles depend on which anatomical line is chosen.4 Work describing proximal ulna anatomy for fixation and plating has also emphasized that curvature, angulation, and dorsal contour are characterized using different landmark definitions and terminology between studies, which complicates attempts to generalize “normal” morphology.5 In addition, a recent systematic review of coronoid height and fracture measurement techniques found substantial variation in how investigators define the reference points and measurement direction for the coronoid, with nearly 20 distinct methods reported in the literature; this lack of standardization directly limited meaningful comparison between studies.12 The heterogeneity we observed in defining varus angulation and TTA is therefore not isolated but part of a broader problem in proximal ulna morphometrics: key angles and distances are being measured against nonuniform landmarks, planes, and axes. In this systematic review, various studies use different terminologies to describe VA and the VA apex, leading to inconsistencies in the literature. This variability in terminology creates challenges when comparing studies and methods, highlighting the need for standardized definitions and measurement protocols in anatomical research. Future studies should adopt consistent terms for varus angulation, varus apex, and TTA, ideally aligning with anatomical landmarks that are universally recognized, to improve reliability and facilitate better comparisons across research. Clearly defining the terms will also enhance the clarity and applicability of these measurements in clinical settings.

Restoration of proximal ulnar coronal and sagittal contours is important for maintaining normal elbow kinematics and forearm rotation. A key implication of this review is that proximal ulnar varus angulation and apex position represent consistent anatomical features rather than incidental variations. Failure to restore this native coronal curvature—whether during fracture reconstruction or application of pre-contoured fixation—risks flattening the physiological bow of the ulna and altering joint congruency. Although pooled estimates suggest a typical angulation of approximately 10–15° with the apex located about 7–9 cm distal to the olecranon tip, alignment strategies should focus on preserving the overall contour of the proximal ulna rather than precise reproduction of any single value. Fixation constructs should therefore accommodate, rather than override, this anatomy, recognizing that restoration of curvature and apex position is likely more important than exact angular matching.

This study has some limitations. Many of the included studies showed moderate to high risk of bias due to incomplete methods and inconsistent landmark definitions. As with any review of this nature, it is limited by substantial data heterogeneity, which reduces the precision of pooled estimates and precludes more granular subgroup meta-analyses, potential publication and language bias, and the exclusion of studies without VA data. Despite this, however, the pooled findings are surprisingly consistent for the majority of individuals.

Future work should prioritize standardization and validation of measurement techniques for proximal ulnar morphology. Studies should adopt CT-based, axis-defined methods with clearly reported anatomical landmarks to reduce interobserver variability and enable direct comparison across populations. Establishing sex-, side-, and ethnicity-specific normative data would further refine implant sizing and contouring.

Large-scale 3D morphometric modeling and finite element analysis could be used to correlate variations in varus angulation and apex position with stress distribution and fixation stability. In addition, clinical outcome studies are needed to determine how precisely restoration of native varus influences post-operative elbow motion, union rates, and implant longevity. Integrating these anatomical parameters into patient-specific pre-operative planning and computer-assisted implant design will be the next major step toward improving accuracy and reproducibility in proximal ulna fracture management.

Conclusions

This systematic review demonstrates that the proximal ulna consistently exhibits approximately 12° of varus angulation, with the apex located about 8 cm distal to the olecranon tip, despite wide methodological variation across studies. CT-based, axis-defined measurements provide the most reliable and reproducible assessment of these parameters. Standardizing landmark definitions and measurement techniques will improve comparability across studies and ensure that implant design and surgical reconstruction more accurately restore native ulnar anatomy. Future research should build on these findings through high-resolution 3D imaging and outcome-based validation to translate anatomical precision into improved clinical results.
