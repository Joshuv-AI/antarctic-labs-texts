The critical role of 16S rRNA sequencing in diagnosis and management of an odontogenic deep neck abscess: A case report
Authors: Annachiara Ghiotti, Jacopo Monticelli, Marina Busetti, Michele Maglione, Stefano Di Bella, Fabio Barbone, Raffaella Koncan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Odontogenic abscesses are characteristically polymicrobial infections where fastidious anaerobes play a critical role. Conventional culture methods often fail to identify these pathogens, leading to diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. We present a case of a severe deep neck and metastatic lung abscess following third molar extraction. While the initial culture of the abscess drainage was deceiving, 16S rRNA sequencing revealed the infection's real anaerobic nature. Therapy was successfully targeted, leading to full clinical resolution. This case highlights the limitations of culture methods and the importance of molecular diagnosis in guiding optimal management of complicated odontogenic infections.

Introduction

One of the most frequent procedures for oral and maxillofacial surgery is the surgical extraction of third molars. Although typically safe, there is a known risk of post-operative infection, which can occasionally lead to life-threatening conditions if they are not promptly diagnosed and treated, such as sepsis, cavernous sinus thrombosis, mediastinitis [[1], [2], [3], [4]].

Odontogenic abscesses are characteristically polymicrobial and comprised of anaerobes, which can be fastidious to identify with conventional culture methods, resulting in challenges in diagnostics and treatments [5]. This is significant since anaerobic bacteria like Bacteroides spp. and P. micra are recognized for their potential to cause bacteraemia and metastatic complications following dental procedures [6].

The advent of 16S rRNA gene sequencing has revolutionized microbiological diagnosis in such scenarios, since it allows for the precise identification of bacteria without the need for culture.

We present a case of a severe deep neck and lung abscess following a dental extraction, where initial cultures were misleading and led to ineffective antibiotic treatment. A definitive diagnosis was only achieved through 16S rRNA gene sequencing, supporting a prompt utilization of advanced microbiological diagnostics in case of difficult infections to adjust the pharmacological choice.

Clinical report

In June 2025, a 26-year-old female with no significant past medical history presented to the emergency department of our Hospital (Azienda Ospedaliera Universitaria Giuliano Isontina, Trieste, Italy) with fever, facial oedema, and trismus, 12 days after the surgical extraction of tooth #38 (mandibular left third molar). After a few days, she developed local pain and swelling, for which she received a brief course of amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (1 g tid orally).

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a multi-chambered abscess within the lateral pterygoid muscles extending to the angle of the mandible, and subcutaneous emphysema in the cervical region.

After a tracheostomy was necessary to ensure safe orotracheal patency due to severe trismus, the patient underwent transoral surgical drainage of the abscess. The abscess drainage was submitted for microbiological testing. An initial culture yielded sparse colonies of Enterobacter cloacae, resistant to ampicillin and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, but a subsequent culture showed no growth. Antibiotic therapy was escalated to intravenous cefepime and metronidazole (2 g/day), but shifted to intravenous cefepime and oral clindamycin (450 mg/8 h) due to partial intolerance to metronidazole.

A follow-up CT scan demonstrated improvement of the orofacial abscess, but revealed a new 20-mm apical lung abscess. To investigate a potential source of haematogenous spread, a transthoracic echocardiogram was performed, which showed no evidence of infective endocarditis. A subsequent bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was negative for common bacterial pathogens and for mycobacteria using routine methods.

At this time, the sample from the oral abscess drainage was subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing using Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) and analysed using Qiime2 [7] and the expanded Human Oral Microbiome Database (eHOMD, v4.1Φ). This technology is not routinely available in our hospital for clinical diagnostic purposes; however, the sample was sequenced as part of an ongoing collaborative research project between the Hygiene and Preventive Medicine unit and the Odontostomatology unit, aimed at characterizing the perigingival microbiome in patients following third-molar extractions. This method identified a predominantly anaerobic microbial community (Fig. 1), comprised mainly of bacteria from the Bacteroidales (36.4%) and Tissierellales (18.7%) orders, specifically of the phylotype Bacteroidetes bacterium HMT-365 and the species Parvimonas micra, respectively. Based on these findings, the previously isolated E. cloacae was deemed a likely contaminant and the antimicrobial therapy was targeted against anaerobic pathogens. It was shifted to oral clindamycin (450 mg/8 h) and moxifloxacin (400 mg/day), the latter subsequently discontinued due to intolerance. The total duration of anaerobic therapy was 56 days, in line with recommended treatment plan for a lung abscess and acute osteomyelitis of the jaw.

The patient achieved complete clinical resolution of the infection, as confirmed by a CT scan obtained three months after completion of antibiotic therapy, and by follow-up visits. She remained asymptomatic with no clinical signs suggestive of recurrent infection.

Discussion

This case illustrates the severe potential of odontogenic infections to lead to life-threatening complications and underscores the critical limitations of conventional diagnostic methods.

Odontogenic deep neck abscesses, originating typically from the first lower molars, include parapharyngeal and retropharyngeal abscesses [1,2]. Odontogenic abscesses are characteristically polymicrobial and comprised predominantly of anaerobic bacteria [5]. Our case aligns perfectly with this pattern. While initial standard culture was inconclusive, identifying sparse colonies Enterobacter cloacae, 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed the true anaerobic nature of the infection.

The main identified pathogens are recognized members of the oral microbiome and have been individually implicated in periodontal diseases and odontogenic abscesses [5,[8], [9], [10]]. The dominance of B. bacterium and P. micra is particularly notable.

While P. micra is an obligate anaerobe, Gram-positive coccus of clinical importance as it is known for causing various systemic infections [6,11,12], B. bacterium has been observed as pathogenic only in few cases [9]. However, the broader order of Bacteroidales has been associated with numerous types of infections, both odontogenic and systemic [[13], [14], [15]]. Given their localization in the oral microflora, the main risk factors and routes of systemic infection are dental treatments such as tooth extractions [16].

A central challenge in this case was the failure of conventional culture to identify the causative anaerobes. This is a well-known limitation, as many anaerobic bacteria are difficult to culture due to their fastidious growth requirements and prior exposure to antibiotics [5,17]. It is important to explicitly acknowledge that, in the present case, the 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed retrospectively as part of an experimental research protocol, rather than as a real-time clinical decision-making tool. The sequencing results were not available to guide the initial or even the early phase of antibiotic management; the therapeutic adjustments were made empirically based on clinical suspicion and later confirmed by the molecular findings. This distinction is critical, as it highlights the current gap between the proven diagnostic power of molecular techniques and their practical accessibility at the point of care.

The practical application of 16S rRNA gene sequencing in this case went beyond mere identification; it was pivotal in altering the clinical trajectory. While our initial culture pointed toward a single pathogen (E. cloacae), the molecular results reframed the entire clinical picture by revealing a complex, anaerobic polymicrobial community. This distinction has direct therapeutic consequences, allowing for correct de-escalation from broad-spectrum antibiotics to a targeted oral regimen, thereby reducing the risk of adverse drug reactions and antimicrobial resistance.

Furthermore, the argument that molecular diagnostics are purely "research tools" is becoming increasingly obsolete. Recent technological advancements, including simplified library preparation, reduced costs, and the availability of rapid platforms, which delivered results within a single day in our case, have made turnaround times significantly shorter than traditional culture methods, which often require prolonged incubation for fastidious anaerobes. While logistical and standardization challenges remain, the clinical benefit demonstrated here provides a compelling rationale for integrating these rapid molecular techniques into routine hospital diagnostic workflows for life-threatening odontogenic infections.

Our findings support the increasing amount of research suggesting that molecular techniques like 16S rRNA gene sequencing are essential for accurately identifying the microbiology of deep-seated odontogenic infections, allowing for targeted antibiotic treatment. Without this advanced diagnostic, therapy would have remained misdirected at a contaminant, potentially leading to treatment failure.

The progression of our patient's infection to a metastatic lung abscess warrants further consideration. While haematogenous spread from the initial dental focus is possible, the temporal association with a major surgical intervention (tracheostomy) must be acknowledged. Organisms such as Bacteroidales spp., P. micra and D. pneumosintes, while part of the oral flora, have often been associated with lung infections, either following surgical procedures that disrupt anatomical barriers, or because of aspiration pneumonia [6,18,19]. We postulate that the lung abscess may have resulted from perioperative aspiration or direct inoculation of the oral anaerobic consortium into the lower respiratory tract during the tracheostomy procedure itself, but we could not confirm this hypothesis because of the lack of 16S rRNA sequencing of the BAL sample and because of inconclusive routine identification methods. If our hypothesis is correct, it highlights a serious, though rare, complication of necessary airway management in the setting of a severe odontogenic infection.

Conclusions

Our case of odontogenic deep neck abscesses and a lung abscess due to fastidious anaerobes, following a dental extraction, underscores several key lessons: the polymicrobial anaerobic etiology of odontogenic infections, the diagnostic superiority of molecular methods over traditional culture in such scenarios, and the potential for otherwise commensal oral anaerobes to cause significant local and metastatic disease, particularly following surgical interventions. This report highlights the urgent need to translate these powerful molecular techniques from the research laboratory into the routine clinical workflow. While we acknowledge the logistical and regulatory barriers that currently prevent their widespread adoption, the clinical benefit demonstrated in this case—the correction of a misdirected antibiotic regimen—provides a compelling rationale for developing rapid, clinically accessible molecular diagnostic algorithms for life-threatening odontogenic and orofacial infections. A high index of suspicion and the utilization of advanced diagnostics are crucial for the effective management of these complex infections.
