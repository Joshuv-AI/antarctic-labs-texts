Spatiotemporal dynamics of neuronal subtypes and their interactions with glia following intracortical electrode implantation
Authors: Zhi Zhao, Xiaoge Duan, Jiaqi Luo, Zhaoyuan He, Yuxue Zhang, Meiting Wang, Jiaoqin Qin, Sen Lin, Hailan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronically implanted electrodes offer a promising approach for treating neurological disorders via brain-computer interfaces, yet their long-term efficacy is compromised by the neuroinflammatory foreign body response. While neurons are central to both electrode function and inflammatory regulation, their specific responses post-implantation remain poorly characterized. Background

Implantable neural electrodes hold transformative potential for treating a range of neurological disorders, from Parkinson’s disease to spinal cord injury, by restoring damaged neural circuits through electrical stimulation and recording [1–3]. Clinically applied neural electrode system, such as cochlear implants (CI) for hearing restoration and vagus nerve stimulators for insulin secretion, underscore this promise [4, 5]. However, the long-term stability and functionality of these interfaces are severely compromised by the foreign body response characterizing with persistent neuroinflammatory reaction triggered by the implanted device [6, 7]. This response involves complex interactions among glial cells (astrocytes and microglia), leading to glial scar formation, neurodegeneration, and ultimately, signal attenuation and electrode failure [8, 9].

While glial responses have been extensively studied, the role of neurons, the primary functional units of the brain and the intended partners of the electrode, remains surprisingly overlooked. Neurons are not passive bystanders during neuroinflammation, but actively participate in immune modulation and tissue repair through bidirectional communication with glial cells [10–12]. Existing evidence suggests that electrode implantation alters neuronal excitability and gene expression, which in turn can drive astrocyte activation [13–15]. Nevertheless, our understanding of neuronal contributions has been constrained by the limitations of bulk tissue analysis, which obscures the responses of specific neuronal subtypes and their unique molecular mechanisms. However, it is still unknow that how do heterogeneous neuronal populations at the implantation site dynamically remodel their structure and function, and how do they specifically communicate with glial cells to influence the neuroinflammatory trajectory.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) provides a powerful tool to resolve this complexity of cell populations and dynamic gene transcription [16]. It enables the dissection of cellular heterogeneity and the identification of subtype-specific transcriptional changes within complex tissues like the brain [17]. This approach has been successfully applied to reveal nuanced cell-type-specific responses in neurological diseases such as multiple sclerosis and Alzheimer’s disease [18], but its application to the study of the brain-electrode interface remains limited.

In this study, we employed snRNA-seq combined with immunofluorescence to map the temporal dynamics of neuronal subtypes in the rat motor cortex at 3, 25, and 50 days after neural electrode implantation. We aimed to: (1) identify distinct injury-responsive neuronal subpopulations and their characteristic gene expression profiles; (2) delineate the temporal trajectory of neuronal transcriptional remodeling; and (3) uncover specific ligand-receptor-mediated communication pathways between neurons and glial cells, particularly astrocytes, during the neuroinflammatory response.

Methods

Electrodes and animals

Silicon-based Michigan type implantable neural electrodes were obtained from Diagnostic Biochips (Glen Burnie, USA) with shank length of 8.3 mm and shank with of 48 μm. A total of 30 adult female Sprague Dawley rats (aged 8–10 weeks, weight 200–220 g) were provided by Sibeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (license no. SCXK (Jing) 2024-0001) (Beijing, China). All rats were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with controlled temperature (22 ± 1℃), humidity (55 ± 5%), and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangxi University (Approval No. GXU-2022-223) and conducted in strict accordance with the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Standard laboratory chow and water were provided ad libitum throughout the acclimatization and experimental periods.

Surgical procedure for neural electrode implantation

Rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) and adequate anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of pedal and corneal reflexes. Anesthetized rats were placed on a stereotaxic brain positioning injector to maintain a constant head level. The scalp was shaved and disinfected with iodophor and 75% ethanol sequentially. After a midline incision, the skull was exposed, and a craniotomy (3 mm in diameter) was performed above the primary motor cortex (M1). A Silicon-based Michigan type implantable neural electrode was implanted into the M1 region (coordinates: AP + 3.0 mm, ML -3.0 mm, DV -3.5 mm from bregma) at a rate of 100 μm/s. The electrode was secured to the skull using three anchor screws and Vetbond tissue adhesive (3 M Company, MN, USA). Post-operatively, buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg) was administered for analgesia every 12 h for 48 h.

Tissue collection and processing

For snRNA-seq, animals (n = 2 per time point) were euthanized at 3, 25, and 50 days post-implantation. The brain was rapidly extracted, and the cortical tissue surrounding the electrode tract (~ 5 mm³) was dissected on ice and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent nuclei isolation.

For immunofluorescence, a separate cohort of animals (n = 3 per time point) was transcardially perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brain was harvested, post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight, and embedded in paraffin.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq)

Nuclei were isolated on ice from the snap-frozen cortical tissues using a detergent-based lysis buffer (NP40) followed by density gradient centrifugation through a 29% Optiprep cushion. The purified nuclei were resuspended in nuclei storage buffer supplemented with RNase inhibitor. Nuclei count and integrity were verified under a microscope. Approximately 10,000 nuclei per sample were loaded onto a 10x Chromium Controller chip (10x Genomics) to generate single-nucleus gel bead-in-emulsions (GEMs). Libraries were constructed using the Chromium Single Cell 3’ Library and Gel Bead Kit v3.3 according to the manufacturer’s protocol and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with a target depth of 50,000 reads per nucleus.

snRNA-seq data analysis

Raw sequencing data (FASTQ files) were processed using CellRanger software (v7.1.0) for demultiplexing, alignment to the rat reference genome (GRCh38_2020-A), and feature counting. Downstream analysis was performed in R using the Seurat package (v4.3.0). Cells were filtered based on the following quality control metrics: number of detected genes between 200 and 5000, mitochondrial gene ratio below 10%, and hemoglobin reads less than 5%. Data normalization and scaling were performed using the SCTransform function. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on highly variable genes, and the top 30 principal components were used for graph-based clustering and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) visualization (resolution = 0.8). Cell types were annotated based on the expression of canonical marker genes (Table 1).

Differential expression and enrichment analysis

Differential expression genes (DEGs) analysis between conditions was performed in Seurat using the FindMarkers function with a Wilcoxon Rank Sum test. Genes were considered differentially expressed if they exhibited a log2 fold change > 1 (or < -1) and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted on the differentially expressed gene sets using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.6.2).

Trajectory analysis

Cellular differentiation trajectories and pseudotime ordering of neuronal subclusters were inferred using Monocle2 (v2.9.0). The developmental potential of neuronal clusters was independently assessed using CytoTRACE (v0.3.3).

Cell communication analysis

Cell-cell communication analysis was performed with the CellChat package (v1.1.3) using the default database to identify significant ligand-receptor interactions and signaling pathways between defined cell types.

Protein-protein interaction network analysis

The protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis of differential genes was performed with the search tool for the retrieval of interacting genes/proteins (STRING) database (http://www.string-db.org/), and the PPI network model was drawn using the Cytoscape 3.10.0.

Immunofluorescences staining

Immunofluorescence were performed according to previously described [19] with modifications. Briefly, 3 μm thick paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Sections were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin. They were then incubated overnight at 4℃ with a primary antibody against Neun (monoclonal mouse anti-Neun, 1:100, Servicebio). After washing, sections were incubated with a fluorescently conjugated secondary antibody (goat anti-mouse IgG H&L, 1:100, Servicebio) for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Stained sections were imaged using a Vectra Polaris automated imaging system (Akoya Biosciences), and the relative fluorescence intensity of Neun was quantified using 3DHISTECH’s Slide Converter software. For quantification, three animals per group were analyzed, with representative images shown from one animal per group.

Power spectral density analysis

Local field potential (LFP) signals recorded from all electrode channels were analyzed to quantify changes in spectral power over time. Raw signals were segmented and transformed using a fast Fourier transform (FFT), followed by calculation of the power spectral density (PSD). PSD values were then averaged within standard frequency bands, including δ (0.5–4 Hz), θ (4–8 Hz), α (8–12 Hz), β (12–30 Hz), low-γ (30–80 Hz), high-γ (80–120 Hz), and the total band (0.5–120 Hz). The PSD power of each band was computed for every electrode channel on Days 3, 25, and 50 post-implantation. The results were plotted to visualize the spatial distribution of neural spectral activity across channels and to characterize temporal changes associated with the electrode–tissue interface.

Result

snRNA-seq delineates the cellular landscape of the neuroinflammatory response to electrode implantation

To comprehensively characterize the cellular responses to intracortical electrode implantation, we performed snRNA-seq on brain tissue surrounding the implant site at three critical time points: acute (3 days), sub-chronic (25 days), and chronic (50 days) phases (Fig. 1A). After quality control, we obtained a total of 125,701 high-quality nuclei for further analysis. Unsupervised clustering identified 27 distinct clusters (Fig. 1B), which were annotated into six major cell types based on well-established marker genes (Fig. 1C, D; Table 1): astrocytes (clusters 19–23), microglia (clusters 17,18), oligodendrocytes (clusters 24), oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs; clusters 12–14), pericytes (clusters 5) and neurons (clusters 0–4,6,8–11,15,16,25). This dataset provides a high-resolution foundation for investigating cell-type-specific dynamics throughout the implantation period.

Neural electrode implantation induces the emergence of injury-responsive neuronal subtypes

Given the central role of neurons in both signal transduction and neuroinflammation, we focused our subsequent analysis on neuronal populations. Sub-clustering of all neuronal nuclei across timepoints identified 22 transcriptionally distinct neuronal subpopulations (Fig. 2A). In addition, these neuronal subclusters were functionally annotated based on differential expression of marker genes (Table S1). The proportional abundance of these subpopulations shifted dramatically over time (Fig. 2B), indicating a dynamic and phased neuronal response to implantation. Notably, at the acute 3-day time point, we observed a significant expansion of subclusters 5, 6, and 8 (Fig. 2B). Analysis of the top marker genes for these subclusters revealed a striking and specific upregulation of Tmsb4x in clusters 5 and 6 at day 3 (Fig. 2C-E). As Tmsb4x is implicated in actin sequestration, cell migration, and tissue repair, these data pinpoint a specific subset of neurons that adopt a plastic, injury-responsive state immediately following electrode insertion.

Temporal transcriptomic profiling reveals phased neuronal remodeling and activation of repair pathways

We next sought to define the global transcriptional changes in neurons over time. DEGs analysis identified 274, 573, 370 genes (log2 FC ≧ 0.1, p < 0.05) significantly upregulated in neurons at day 3, 25, and 50, respectively, compared to unimplanted controls (Fig. 3A). In addition, DEGs analysis shows that the electrode implantation induced down-regulation of expression of a large number of genes, which may represent neuronal injury. PPI network analysis of the day 3 upregulated genes highlighted a central network dominated by ribosomal proteins (e.g., RPL/RPS family) and translation-associated factors (e.g., UBA52, RACK1) (Fig. 3C), suggesting a rapid increase in protein synthesis capacity to meet the demands of acute injury response. A core set of 29 genes was consistently upregulated across all time points (Fig. 3B). Functional enrichment analysis of these temporal gene sets revealed sustained activation of key biological processes, including GTPase activity, GTP binding, transcription factor activity, and proton transmembrane transporter activity, and significant involvement in pathways such as dopaminergic synapse, glutamatergic synapse, and chemokine signaling (Fig. 3D, E).

Immunofluorescence validation confirms acute neuronal loss and subsequent recovery

To validate our transcriptomic findings at the tissue level, we performed immunofluorescence staining for the neuronal marker Neun. Qualitative and quantitative analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in neuronal density at 3 days post-implantation, followed by a gradual recovery at days 25 and 50 (Fig. 4A, B). This pattern of acute loss and chronic recovery aligns with the known neuroinflammatory trajectory. Intriguingly, while cell numbers decreased acutely, snRNA-seq data indicated a marked increase in the number of detected neuronal nuclei and transcriptional diversity at day 3 (Fig. 4C). This apparent discrepancy suggests that the initial injury triggers a robust transcriptional activation in the surviving neuronal population, driving the phenotypic remodeling observed in our subcluster analysis.

Progressive changes in raw neural activity and spectral power after electrode implantation

The raw LFP traces recorded at different time points after implantation (Fig. S1) and PSD-based power distribution across channels (Fig. 5) revealed substantial temporal changes in neural activity. Power in all frequency bands remained relatively uniform across channels, mirroring the stable and evenly distributed raw signal morphology at day 3 after electrode implantation. However, δ-band power became markedly heterogeneous, matching the emergence of large-amplitude bursts in the raw traces. Channels with high δ-power corresponded to those showing strong signal fluctuations at day 25 and day 50 after electrode implantation. Together, these results consistent with immunofluorescence indicate that neuronal activity loss at early stages is primarily driven by acute neuronal damage and local neuroinflammatory responses triggered by electrode implantation.

Pseudotime analysis reconstructs a trajectory of injury-responsive neuronal activation

To understand the transcriptional transition from normal to the inflammation types of neurons, we performed an unsupervised trajectory analysis. To model the transition of neurons from a homeostatic to an injury-adapted state, we performed pseudotime trajectory analysis. CytoTRACE analysis predicted that subclusters 5, 6, and 8 possessed a lower differentiation score, indicative of a more plastic state (Fig. 6A). Monocle2 analysis reconstructed a trajectory branching into five distinct states (Fig. 6B). Neurons residing in State 2, which was highly enriched at day 3, exhibited a pronounced upregulation of genes related to mitochondrial function (e.g., Cox-series, Nduf-series) and cytoskeletal remodeling (e.g., Calm3, Tmsb4x) (Fig. 6C). The distribution of cells along this trajectory confirmed that the day 3 time point and subclusters 5, 6, and 8 were overwhelmingly localized to State 2 (Fig. 6D, E). This defines State 2 as a metabolically active, injury-responsive neuronal state critical for the early adaptive response to implantation.

Cell-cell communication analysis identifies enhanced neuron-astrocyte signaling pathways

Finally, we investigated how neurons communicate with glial cells to shape the neuroinflammatory microenvironment. Using CellChat, we inferred ligand-receptor interactions. The number and strength of significant signaling pathways between neurons and astrocytes increased from implantation, with 10, 9, and 13 pathways identified in control, day 3, and day 25 groups, respectively (Fig. 7A-C). Comparative analysis revealed a specific enhancement of several key ligand-receptor pairs post-implantation (Fig. 7D, E), including pleiotrophin (PTN)-syndecan-4 (SDC4), slit guidance ligand 1/2 (SLIT1/SLIT2)-roundabout guidance receptor 1 (ROBO1), and interleukin-34 (IL34)/colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1)–colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) etc. Most notably, the Ptn-Sdc4 and Il34/Csf1-Csf1R signaling axes were predicted to emerge as prominent communication channels from neurons to astrocytes. These findings suggest that neurons actively participate in modulating astrocyte activity and the broader immune response through these specific pathways following electrode implantation.

Discussion

The long-term performance of intracortical electrodes is critically limited by the neuroinflammatory foreign body response [20]. While glial activation has been extensively documented, the dynamic role of heterogeneous neuronal populations in this process has remained elusive. In the current study, we leveraged snRNA-seq to construct a high-resolution temporal atlas of the neuronal response to chronic electrode implantation. We identified a previously unrecognized, injury-responsive neuronal state characterized by the acute upregulation of Tmsb4x, and revealed potential specific ligand-receptor pairs, namely PTN-SDC4 and Il34/Csf1-Csf1R, that mediate novel communication channels from neurons to astrocytes. These findings position neurons not as passive victims but as active participants in shaping the peri-electrode microenvironment, offering fresh insights for improving interface biocompatibility.

Neurons respond to electrode implantation with dynamic and plastic changes, a consequence of the initial mechanical trauma [21]. Our integrated analysis, combining snRNA-seq with electrophysiology and immunofluorescence, delineates a biphasic neuronal response: an initial phase of acute loss followed by gradual recovery. The significant reduction in neuronal density at day 3, consistent with previous observations of decreased spine density [22], underscores the severity of the initial mechanical injury. In addition, we observed a large number of downregulated and some upregulated gene expression in neurons with electrode implantation. Similarly, some downregulated transcripts co-occurring with selective upregulation of injury-associated pathways were also reported in brain injury scRNA-seq [23]. We speculate that this reflects a compensatory response in which injured neurons transiently suppress non-essential transcriptional programs while preserving or enhancing the capacity for selective protein synthesis required for cytoskeletal remodeling, cellular repair, and recovery. Some research supports this claim, mRNA downregulation in multiple injury and stress paradigms [24, 25].

However, the concurrent transcriptional activation and the emergence of specific subclusters (5, 6, 8) suggest a robust compensatory effort by surviving neurons. This is powerfully exemplified by the specific and acute upregulation of Tmsb4x within these clusters. As a key regulator of actin sequestration, cell migration, and anti-inflammatory responses [26], Tmsb4x is ideally positioned to orchestrate cytoskeletal remodeling and repair processes in neurons navigating the inflammatory milieu. Actually, many studies have reported that Tmsb4x plays a key role in neuronal responses to stress and injury [27, 28]. The concept of distinct neuronal subtypes playing unique physiological roles is well-established [29, 30]. Our data advance this concept by identifying clusters 5, 6, and 8 as a specialized, plastic neuronal subtype dedicated to early-stage adaptation and repair. These clusters were enriched not only for Tmsb4x but also for genes involved in neuronal plasticity, cytoskeletal remodeling, and mitochondrial activity (e.g., Cox2, Ndufa4, ND1, and ND4), indicating a concerted effort towards energy production and metabolic support for repair. The presence of a small population of these clusters in the control group suggests a baseline level of homeostatic maintenance and plasticity. Following implantation, however, their marked expansion implies a massive recruitment of neurons into this injury-responsive, reparative state. Specifically, clusters 5 and 6, characterized by elevated Tmsb4x expression, appear to be central to cellular migration, cytoskeletal reorganization, and potentially, the immunomodulation of the cortical repair microenvironment in the acute phase.

The pseudotime trajectory analysis further solidified this notion, placing these Tmsb4x-expressing clusters within a distinct, metabolically active state (State 2) enriched for mitochondrial and ribosomal genes. This transcriptional profile indicates a substantial investment in energy production and protein synthesis, likely fueling the demands of structural remodeling and synaptic reorganization. The enrichment of this state almost exclusively at the acute 3-day time point highlights the transient yet critical nature of this adaptive response. It appears that a subset of neurons swiftly transitions into a high-energy, repair-competent state to mitigate damage and initiate recovery, a dynamic process that bulk tissue analyses have previously missed.

Perhaps our most intriguing finding is the specific neuron-to-astrocyte signaling pathways that are potentiated after implantation. The Ptn-Sdc4 and Il34/Csf1-Csf1R axes represent compelling mechanisms for neuron-glia cross-talk. PTN, signaling through its receptor SDC4, is known to influence neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, and glial activation. Our data suggest that neurons may use the PTN-SDC4 pathway to modulate astrocytic behavior, potentially steering them toward a supportive rather than a reactive phenotype. Even more suggestive is the role of the IL34/CSF1-CSF1R pathway, while classically associated with microglial homeostasis and macrophage differentiation [31, 32], its upregulation in neuron-astrocyte communication here is novel in the context of neural implants. We hypothesize that neurons may be leveraging this pathway to indirectly influence the broader neuroimmune landscape, as astrocytes can in turn modulate microglial activity. This positions neurons higher up in the signaling hierarchy of the neuroinflammatory cascade than previously appreciated.

Despite this study demonstrating that the Tmsb4x specific upregulation in injury-responsive neuronal state and proposed PTN-SDC4 and Il34/Csf1-Csf1R play a key role in communication from neurons to astrocytes, there are still some limitations. To test our findings in detail, it would require extra work at the molecular and protein expression level, such as validation of critical genes involved. Second, our study found the key signaling pathways that were carried out based on data mining and bioinformatics analysis, lacking experimental verification of protein expression. The ligand-receptor colocalization and performing functional assays are important in future research to further understanding the neuron-to-glial signaling pathways.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study provides an unprecedented, single-cell-resolution view of neuronal plasticity and communication in the brain’s response to a chronic implant. We move beyond the paradigm of neurons as mere targets of inflammation and instead depict them as active regulators that undergo metabolic reprogramming, activate repair-associated genes like Tmsb4x, and engage in sophisticated molecular dialogues with glial cells possibly via Ptn-Sdc4 and Il34/Csf1-Csf1R pathways. Our work delineates a new mechanistic framework for understanding the brain-electrode interface. Targeting these newly identified neuronal subtypes and communication pathways holds promising potential for developing next-generation neural interfaces with enhanced integration and long-term stability.
