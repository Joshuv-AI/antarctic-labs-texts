Prevalence, Trends, and Associated Factors of Malnutrition Among Under Six‐Month‐Old Infants in Bangladesh: A Pooled Analysis of DHS Data (2011–2022)
Authors: Imteaz Mahmud, Roxanne Assies, Rukhsana Haider, Azaz Bin Sharif, Kedir Teji Roba, Marko Kerac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMalnutrition among infants aged under 6 months (u6m) is a global public health problem. As countries begin implementing 2023 WHO malnutrition guidelines, local prevalence and epidemiology must be well understood. We analysed Bangladesh Demographic Health Survey data (2011–2022), describing the prevalence and time trends of infant u6m wasting (weight‐for‐length z‐score [WLZ] < −2), stunting (length‐for‐age z‐score [LAZ] < −2), and underweight (weight‐for‐age z‐score [WAZ] < −2). In bivariate and multivariate analyses, we tested the association between wasting and underweight and established risk factors for malnutrition. Over the last 10 years, Bangladesh has seen some reduction in stunting and underweight, while wasting has remained relatively stable. In 2022, out of 440 infants u6m, 10.2% were wasted, 11.2% stunted, 11.3% underweight, 0.2% concurrently wasted/stunted. This translates to 178,394 infants u6m being wasted, 195,884 stunted, and 197,633 underweight, in 2022. Between the years 2011 and 2022, 30.3% of participants had at least one form of anthropometric deficit. Length (171) had more missing values than weight (79). WAZ had the lowest percentage of flagged data (0.5%) compared to WLZ (6.1%) and LAZ (2.5%). Underweight was associated with residence, wealth status, maternal age at delivery, maternal BMI, maternal height, maternal level of education, exclusive breastfeeding status and fever in the past 2 weeks. Factors associated with wasting were survey year, the sex of the household head, wealth status, maternal educational level, maternal age at delivery, infant age, and place of delivery. Our findings support the urgent need to roll out the 2023 WHO Guidelines in Bangladesh. They are also consistent with other recent literature highlighting potential advantages of weight‐for‐age (WAZ) as a measure of undernutrition. Given multiple associated factors, packages of care tackling a wide range of potential underlying causes are important for effective local interventions tailored to this age group.Keywords: Bangladesh, Demographic Health Survey (DHS), infants under 6 months, malnutrition, stunted, undernutrition, underweight, wasted

Introduction

Malnutrition remains a major global public health challenge, disproportionately affecting low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), particularly in Africa and Asia (Kerac et al. 2020; UNICEF, W 2025). Approximately 2.4 million under‐five deaths are attributed to malnutrition annually, which is half of all global child deaths (Ritchie 2024). Among survivors, malnutrition has long‐term consequences, including impaired physical and cognitive development (Bhutta et al. 2017; Black et al. 2013; Kirolos et al. 2022) and an increased risk of later‐life non‐communicable diseases (Grey et al. 2021; Lelijveld et al. 2016). According to the 2025 Joint Child Malnutrition Estimates, an estimated 150.2 million (23.2%) children under 5 years of age are stunted, and 42.8 million (6.6%) are wasted globally (UNICEF, W 2025).

Infants aged under 6 months (u6m) are increasingly recognised as a highly vulnerable subgroup. A recent analysis of 56 Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) estimated that 10.3 million (17.4%) infants u6m are underweight, 9.2 million (15.5%) are wasted, 11.8 million (19.9%) are stunted, and 8.9 million (15.0%) are born with low birth weight (Kerac et al. 2025).

Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 2: Zero Hunger, underscores the critical importance of addressing malnutrition (Sustainable Development Goals: Goal 2: Zero Hunger n.d.). However, progress toward achieving a 50% reduction in undernutrition by 2030 has been severely disrupted by the COVID‐19 pandemic, global conflicts, and climate crises (UNDESA 2022). To counteract this setback, a more aggressive, effective and targeted approach is needed, particularly focusing on infants u6m, who have been historically overlooked in nutrition policy and programming. The World Health Organisation (WHO) recently emphasised the importance of infants u6m by including them as a key group in the new 2023 wasting guideline (WHO 2023). Termed ‘infants at risk of poor growth and development’, the criteria for programme enrolment now go beyond wasting alone and include low weight‐for‐age z‐score (WAZ < −2), low weight‐for‐length z‐score (WLZ < −2), and low mid‐upper arm circumference (MUAC < 110 mm, for infants aged over 6 weeks to 6 months) (WHO 2023). Whilst it is a major step forward that infants u6m are emphasised in the new guideline, the recommendations are based on conditional and low‐certainty evidence. Robust data from diverse settings is needed to strengthen policies and intervention strategies. Among the key questions arising is which anthropometric and other criteria should be used to identify the highest‐risk infants u6m for priority enrolment and treatment. There has been particular recent interest in possible advantages of weight‐for‐age over weight‐for‐length as an anthropometric measure best able to identify the highest‐risk infants (Ahmed et al. 2025; Hoehn et al. 2021).

Bangladesh, one of the most densely populated countries in the world, faces a significant burden of malnutrition, with 24% of children aged under 5 years stunted, 22% underweight, and 11% wasted (NIPORT 2023). Despite ongoing public health efforts, child malnutrition persists due to a complex interplay of poverty, food insecurity, inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) practices, frequent natural disasters, suboptimal maternal and child health practices, and limited healthcare access (Ahmed et al. 2015; Faruque et al. 2008; Mondal et al. 2024). While much research has focused on malnutrition in older children, data specific to infants u6m remain scarce.

Methods

Study Design

In this secondary analysis, we used the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Surveys (BDHS) dataset from 2011 to 2022 (ICF 2025). The DHS programme has conducted standardised household surveys in multiple countries since 1984, providing reliable data on health and nutrition. In Bangladesh, these cross‐sectional, nationally representative surveys were conducted every 3–4 years from 1993 to 2022. This dataset is publicly available. As part of the original survey protocol, all data were anonymised prior to download from repositories to protect participant privacy. This study focuses on infants u6m, a population group that has received limited attention in nutrition research despite its recognised vulnerability.

Sampling Strategy and Data Collection

The BDHS employed a two‐stage cluster sampling strategy to ensure a nationally representative sample (NIPORT 2024). The sampling frame was based on the most recent census Enumeration Areas (EAs), each containing approximately 120 households serving as primary sampling units (PSUs). In stage one, EAs were stratified by urban and rural residence, and a random selection of PSUs was made using probability proportional to size. An average of 30 households per EA were randomly selected for data collection in stage two.

Trained interviewers visited selected households and collected demographic and anthropometric data using the DHS standard household questionnaire. Children under 5 years were identified, and their weight and length were measured using standardised procedures. Weight was measured using a digital scale to the nearest 0.1 kg, and length was measured using a length board; children < 2 years or < 85 cm were measured lying down. Additional maternal and household information was collected during the survey.

Variables

Infants u6m were identified from the children's dataset. Weight (kg) and length (cm) were measured to one decimal place. Data quality checks included generating histograms and scatter plots to assess distribution and identify outliers. Anthropometric indices (z‐scores) were calculated using the WHO growth reference standards via the zscore06 package (Leroy 2011). To examine the factors associated with malnutrition, we selected key infant, maternal, and household factors based on UNICEF's Analytical Framework for COVID‐19 and Malnutrition (UNICEF, USAID, WHO, & Nutrition, U. A 2022). Variables were recoded according to established definitions. For instance, exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) was defined according to the WHO criteria (WHO & UNICEF 2021). Explanatory variables were classified into survey year (2011, 2014, 2017, 2022), infant, maternal, and household characteristics. Infant characteristics included age (< 1 month/1 to < 2 months/2 to < 4 months/4 to < 6 months), sex (male/female), number of antenatal care (ANC) visits (< 4/ > = 4), place of delivery (home/health facility), received postnatal care (PNC) within 2 months of delivery (yes/no), time of breastfeeding initiation (< 1 h/> 1 h), EBF (yes/no), and recent illness (cough, fever, diarrhoea) (yes/no). Maternal characteristics included maternal age at delivery (12–19 years/20–34 years/> =35 years), education level (no education/primary/secondary/higher), maternal height (< 145 cm/> =145 cm), BMI (normal/underweight/overweight or obese), employment status (employed/unemployed), and total births (< 3/ > = 3). Household characteristics included residence type (urban/rural), sex of the household head (male/female), religion (Muslim/Hindu and others), and wealth quintiles (poorest/poorer/middle/richer/richest). All variables were captured using standard DHS methodologies, with detailed descriptions available in the official DHS manual (DHS Manuals n.d.).

Statistical Analysis

We performed statistical analysis using STATA 16 (StataCorp 2017). Sampling weights were used to estimate frequencies and percentages to adjust for under‐ and oversampling. To account for the complex survey design, we applied svyset command to obtain robust standard errors. We explored the distribution of different anthropometric measurements of malnutrition and their concurrence across age groups using the combined 2011 to 2022 dataset. We assessed the prevalence of malnutrition indicators (wasting, stunting, underweight, and WaSt) across 2011, 2014, 2017, and 2022 to evaluate trends over time. Based on the latest 2022 prevalence, we estimated the current caseload of malnutrition in this age group in Bangladesh. Using combined data (2011–2022), we examined crude associations (unadjusted odds ratios) between infant nutritional status (wasted and underweight) and associated factors using bivariate logistic regression. Finally, we performed multivariable logistic regression to find the factors associated with malnutrition among infants u6m. All the variables were selected based on epidemiological relevance. The survey year was included as a categorical variable to account for temporal differences across DHS rounds. Variables with high missing values were excluded from the analysis. Missing values were kept as missing and were not imputed or recoded. In the multivariable models, observations with missing values were excluded from the regression, resulting in a complete‐case analysis. Before fitting the model, multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). No evidence of multicollinearity was observed (all VIF values < 5). All the selected covariates were entered simultaneously into the final models, and no further automated variable selection procedure was applied. Model stability was assessed by comparing the crude and adjusted models during model building. Multivariable models for both wasting and underweight as outcomes included a total of 2,585 observations. The corresponding weighted population size was 2611.4. All the numbers mentioned here were based on weighted analysis, except the study participants' flow chart (Figure S1).

Ethics Statement

This study used publicly available, de‐identified data from the BDHS, accessed with permission from the DHS Programme. The BDHS surveys are ethically approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of ICF International and the Bangladesh Medical Research Council (BMRC). The project was further approved by the LSHTM Research Ethics Committee (reference 15051).

Results

Study Participants

This analysis included four rounds of BDHS datasets from 2011 to 2022 (Figure S1). After excluding infants with missing or flagged data, a total of 2615 (unweighted) infants u6m were included in the final analysis. The distribution of the sample population across the eight districts of Bangladesh is shown in Table S1. Around half of the participants were from the Dhaka and Chattogram divisions. Sylhet division had the lowest representation (3.6%) in this analysis.

Quality of the Data

Figure S1 shows the distribution of missing and flagged (out‐of‐range) anthropometric data among the collected sample. There were 171 missing length values and 79 weight data points. 32 length data were less than 45 cm and were not suitable for assessing any anthropometric indices. WLZ had markedly more flagged values (n = 170, 6.1%) than LAZ (n = 63, 2.5%) and WAZ (n = 14, 0.5%). A total of 184 (6.6%) participants had at least one flagged anthropometric index.

Of the 2638 infants (weighted) aged 0–5 months, 47.4% were female. The sex distribution was similar in all age groups. Infants were almost equally distributed across age groups, with the highest number in the 4 to 5‐month age group (34.3%). Among the included infants, 11.9% (95% CI: 10.4–13.7) were wasted, 16.4% (95% CI: 14.7–18.3) were stunted, 14.5% (12.9–16.2) were underweight, and 0.9% (95% CI: 0.6–1.4) were concurrently wasted and stunted (WaSt). In the year 2022, 440 infants aged 0–5 months were included. Overall, sex distribution was almost equal (49.2% female). Among the included infants, 10.2% (95% CI: 7.6–13.6) were wasted, 11.2% (95% CI: 8.0–15.5) were stunted, 11.3% (95% CI: 8.3–15.1) were underweight, and 0.2% (95% CI: 0.0–0.8) were concurrently wasted and stunted (WaSt).

Table 1 also shows the extrapolated caseload of malnutrition among all u6m old infants in Bangladesh. The country had an estimated total of 1,748,963 infants u6m. Among them, 178,394 were classified as wasted, 195,884 as stunted, 197,633 as underweight, and 3498 as WaSt.

Anthropometric Deficit Over Time

Figure 3 illustrates the prevalence of different anthropometric deficits from 2011 to 2022. Stunting declined from 2011 (18.0%; 95% CI: 14.7%–21.7%) to 2014 (14.7%; 95% CI: 10.6%–20.0%), then increased to 19.0% (95% CI: 16.4%–21.8%) in 2017. In the latest 2022 data, it decreased to 11.2% (95% CI: 8.0%–15.5%). The prevalence of underweight increased from 2011 to 2014, then decreased steadily from 2014 to 2022. In 2011, the prevalence of underweight was 15.0% (95% CI: 12.0%–18.5%). However, in 2014, it increased to 17.3% (95% CI: 13.4%–22.0%). In 2017 and 2022, it again reduced to 13.8% (95% CI: 11.6%–16.5%) and 11.3% (95% CI: 8.3%–15.1%), respectively. Wasting was at 12.8% (95% CI: 9.9%–16.4%) in 2011. In 2014, it rose to 17.7% (95% CI: 13.3%–23.1%). Then, in 2017, it decreased to 8.1% (95% CI: 6.1%–10.6%) and remained low at 10.2% (95% CI: 7.6%–13.6%) in 2022. Over the years, an inverse relationship was observed between wasting and stunting. Concurrent wasting and stunting were below 2% throughout this period. Overall, the rates of wasting, stunting, and underweight declined over the years, with increases in wasting and underweight in 2014 and stunting in 2017.

Factors Associated With Wasted and Underweight Infants

Table 2 shows the bivariate and multivariable analyses of different factors and their associations with wasting and underweight. In bivariate analysis, survey year, sex of the household head, mother's level of education, infant age categories, and place of delivery were found to have good evidence of association with wasting. Mother's age at delivery, mother's employment status, and PNC within 2 months showed weak evidence of association with wasting. In terms of underweight, strong evidence of association was observed with rural residence, poverty/low wealth index, advanced mothers' age at delivery, low mother's BMI, low mother's height, low mother's level of education, the place of delivery, and EBF. Good evidence was observed with mother's employment status, infant age category, PNC within 2 months and number of ANC visits.

Survey Year

The survey year was found to be associated with wasting. In 2014, there was 1.66 times the odds of wasting compared with 2011 (aOR = 1.66; 95% CI: 1.08–2.55). However, no association was observed with infants' underweight status.

Household Characteristics

Infants residing in rural areas had 1.56 times the odds of being underweight compared with those in urban areas (aOR = 1.56; 95% CI: 1.08–2.23). Infants coming from richer households were 38% less likely to be underweight than those from middle‐income households (aOR = 0.62; 95% CI: 0.39–0.96). On the other hand, infants in the richest households had 1.82 times the odds of wasting (aOR = 1.82; 95% CI: 1.1–3.01) compared with those in middle‐income households. Additionally, infants from female‐headed families were 1.81 times more likely to be wasted than those from male‐headed families (aOR = 1.81; 95% CI: 1.18–2.78).

Maternal Characteristics

Maternal age at delivery was found to be a statistically significant factor for both the infant's underweight status and wasting. Infants born to mothers whose age at delivery was 35 years or more had 2.70 (aOR = 2.70; 95% CI: 1.46–4.99) times the odds of being underweight and 2.11 (aOR = 2.11; 95% CI: 1.11–4.00) times the odds of being wasted than those with the age at delivery of 20–34 years. Additionally, mothers aged 12 −19 years (at the time of delivery) had a 1.33 (aOR = 1.33; 95% CI: 0.97–1.84) times higher chance of an underweight infant compared to those aged 20–34 years at delivery. Though the evidence was weak (p = 0.077). Infants born to mothers with a BMI indicating underweight were 1.65 (aOR = 1.65; 95% CI: 1.15–2.36) times more likely to be underweight, compared to those born to mothers with a normal weight. On the other hand, infants born to mothers who were overweight or obese were 31% less likely to be underweight (aOR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.47–1.02) compared with infants born to mothers of normal weight; however, the evidence for this finding is not strong (p = 0.067). A strong association was observed between maternal height and infant underweight status. Infants whose mothers had a height of less than 145 cm had 2.26 times (aOR = 2.26; 95% CI: 1.59 – 3.22) higher chance of being underweight than those with taller mothers. Strong evidence of association was observed between infant malnutrition and maternal education. Compared with infants of mothers with no education, infants of mothers with completed primary education had a 49% lower chance of being underweight (aOR = 0.51; 95% CI: 0.31–0.82) and a 39% lower chance of being wasted (aOR = 0.61; 95% CI: 0.36–1.04). Though the evidence was not strong (p = 0.068). Mothers with secondary education had 45% lower odds of having underweight infants (aOR = 0.55; 95% CI: 0.35–0.88) and 42% lower odds of having infants with wasting (aOR = 0.58; 95% CI: 0.33–0.99). Mothers with higher education (beyond secondary) had 46% lower odds of having underweight infants (aOR = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.29–1.01), although the evidence was weak (p = 0.055). Good evidence of association was observed between maternal employment status and infant underweight. Infants with employed mothers had a 41% lower likelihood of being underweight compared with infants of unemployed mothers (aOR = 0.59; 95% CI: 0.39–0.88).

Infant Characteristics

Strong evidence of association was observed between infant age and malnutrition status. With ageing, the likelihood of wasting and underweight decreased. Infants in the 4 to < 6 months and 2 to < 4 months age groups were 66% (aOR = 0.34; 95% CI: 0.20–0.58) and 39% (aOR = 0.61; 95% CI: 0.40–0.91) less likely to be wasted, respectively, compared to infants less than 1 month old. Similarly, in the 4 to < 6 months age group, infants were 35% less likely to be underweight (aOR = 0.65; 95% CI: 0.39–1.07), though the evidence was weak (p = 0.09). The place of delivery was also significantly associated with child health outcomes. Infants born with home‐based deliveries had 1.47 times the odds of being wasted (aOR = 1.47; 95% CI: 1.02–2.12) than those born with health facility‐based deliveries. Regarding underweight, the association with place of delivery was attenuated in the adjusted model. To further assess the robustness of this finding, a sensitivity analysis using sequentially adjusted models for groups of potential confounders was performed (Table S2). The association between wasting and place of delivery remained relatively stable after adjustment for household and maternal characteristics (aOR = 1.36; 95% CI: 0.98–1.90; aOR = 1.32; 95% CI: 0.94–1.85, respectively). In the final model, it became statistically significant (aOR = 1.47; 95% CI: 1.02–2.12). For underweight, a statistically significant association with the place of delivery was observed in the crude analysis (aOR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.11–1.92). However, after adjustment for socioeconomic characteristics, the association was substantially attenuated (aOR = 1.17; 95% CI: 0.86–1.58). After adjustment of maternal and infant covariates, there were modest changes in association (aOR = 1.18; 95% CI: 0.86–1.61; and aOR = 1.25; 95% CI: 0.87–1.77, respectively). Strong evidence of association was observed between EBF status and infant underweight. Infants who were not exclusively breastfed had 1.61 times odds of being underweight than not exclusively breastfed infants (aOR = 1.61; 95% CI: 1.15–2.25). Infants with a fever within the past 2 weeks were associated with underweight status, with an odds ratio of 1.46 (95% CI: 1.07–1.99) compared to those without fever. In the adjusted analysis, the total number of births, infant sex, number of ANC visits, early initiation of breastfeeding, having a cough, or diarrhoea showed no significant association with wasting or being underweight.

Discussion

In Bangladesh, DHS survey data indicate that regardless of anthropometric measures, infant u6m malnutrition was common. Over the last decade (2011–2022), 11.9% of infants 0‐5 months were wasted, 16.4% were stunted, and 14.5% were underweight. It remains a common problem, according to the latest 2022 data, with 10.2% wasted, 11.2% stunted, and 11.3% underweight, underscoring the need for treatment and prevention programming, consistent with the latest 2023 WHO Malnutrition guidelines (WHO 2023). This status of malnutrition among infants u6m is, however, better than the overall world prevalence (Kerac et al. 2025). Across different anthropometric measurements, WLZ had the highest number of flagged data points, followed by LAZ and WAZ. Other studies reported similar WAZ values, with the lowest flagged data (Grijalva‐Eternod et al. 2017; Mahmud et al. 2024). Studies have indicated that length is particularly prone to measurement errors due to the natural/infantile position of the legs, which must be straightened during measurement (Mwangome and Berkley 2014). This makes the measurement more complicated and time‐consuming for the health worker and may be less acceptable for the mother, as it requires moving the infant into this position (Lelijveld and Kerac 2017). Recent evidence suggests that WAZ has an advantage over other measures in routine nutritional surveys and monitoring of nutrition programmes in this age group of infants (Hoehn et al. 2021; Kerac et al. 2025). Recent WHO‐led multi‐country cohort analysis also highlights potential practical advantages of WAZ in identifying infants at highest risk of mortality (Ahmed et al. 2025). Whilst our analysis is consistent with the wider literature on the WAZ‐informed identification of high‐risk infant u6m, we do, however, highlight that our data is cross‐sectional in nature and cannot show this directly. Our analysis does not, for example, assess clinical outcomes, such as morbidity, mortality, or treatment outcomes, and therefore cannot determine the predictive superiority of any anthropometric indicator. Another critical finding was that concurrence of malnutrition was common in this age group of infants; around one third of the infants u6m had at least one form of anthropometric deficit, and those who were concurrently wasted and stunted were also underweight. A similar finding was observed in other studies as well (Mahmud et al. 2024; Myatt et al. 2018). It is important to note that concurrent wasting and stunting often flags the highest‐risk infants—another reason why WAZ might be a good measure of risk—since it also captures lots of that overlap (Myatt et al. 2018).

In terms of wasting, stunting and underweight trends, improvements over the last 10 years were observed. The highest progress was observed for stunting, while the prevalence of wasting has declined modestly. This progress may have been possible due to the government's multisectoral interventions that focused on both nutrition‐specific and nutrition‐sensitive approaches (Suri 2019). Regional disparities were observed across Bangladesh, with Rangpur as the most affected division, showing the highest levels of stunting and underweight. Addressing malnutrition among this age group might require prioritisation and a targeted approach to the most affected regions.

Several factors were associated with wasting and underweight. This suggests a need for packages of care that address multiple underlying risk factors rather than one alone (Grey et al. 2021). Infants surveyed in 2014 had significantly higher odds of wasting than those surveyed in 2011. However, this should be interpreted cautiously. As wasting reflects acute or recent nutritional deprivation and is influenced by recent infant illnesses, sudden food price hikes, household food insecurity, seasonal variation of food availability or sudden economic crisis (Mohsena et al. 2018). There could also be residual confounding, differences in population characteristics or any unmeasured characteristics between survey rounds. Although we observed a sudden increase in wasting prevalence among infants under 6 months from 12.8% in 2011 to 17.7% in 2014, no major change was observed in wasting prevalence among children under 5 years between these 2 survey years (NIPORT, Associates, M. a., & International, I 2013, 2016).

Strong evidence was found for an association between the place of delivery and infant wasting. Infants delivered at home had higher odds of being wasted compared to infants delivered at a hospital. This finding is consistent with other evidence indicating that institutional delivery is associated with better health outcomes (Akombi et al. 2017; Olusanya and Renner 2012; Wali et al. 2021). Whilst from our data we can only observe associations rather than infer causation between infant wasting and place of delivery, it could be that this reflects a “continuum of care” deficit. Mothers who delivered their infants in health facilities are likely to have received antenatal care, nutrition counselling, knowledge of child care, and postnatal support, all of which are important determinants of infant wasting (Rahman et al. 2009). This association may also reflect that mothers who delivered at the health facility were more health‐conscious, had better access to health care, and differed in ways not fully captured in this analysis. On the other hand, no significant association was observed between the place of delivery and infant underweight status after adjustment for household characteristics. This signifies that the crude association was mediated by underlying socioeconomic status. Nonetheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution, given potential residual confounding and the indication for hospital‐ or home‐based deliveries. Cross‐sectional DHS data cannot alone provide a complete picture of household decision‐making processes or any obstetric complications that lead to those delivery decisions. Future longitudinal studies would be required to identify the pathway of place of delivery and infant undernutrition.

In our analysis, maternal age at delivery was associated with infant underweight. The association between maternal age and infant malnutrition often follows a U‐shaped distribution where mothers at both ends of the reproductive age spectrum, which is adolescent (12–19 years) and advanced maternal age (AMA) (35 and more), face a significantly higher risk of adverse infant nutritional outcomes than the 20–34 years age cohort (Welch et al. 2024). This disadvantage can be explained by the combination of biological and socio‐economic disadvantages. Adolescent mothers are often still physically developing, and pregnancy causes competition between the mother's own physiological needs and the developing foetus for nutrition. They often deliver preterm and low‐birth‐weight babies (Ganchimeg et al. 2014). Subsequently, they cannot nurse their babies during the newborn and infancy stages. Moreover, teenage mothers often have limited income, household decision‐making power, and access to healthcare, which ultimately affect their children's nutritional status (Nguyen et al. 2021; Wemakor et al. 2018). On the contrary, AMA is a well‐documented risk factor of childhood malnutrition (Khoshnood et al. 2005). Older mothers develop gestational diabetes and pregnancy‐induced hypertension, compromising the intrauterine environment for the foetus (Huang et al. 2023). They often have preterm deliveries and low birth weight babies, leading to underweight infants later in life.

Maternal BMI was positively associated with underweight. This finding is consistent with previous studies in Bangladesh and other low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) (Goudet et al. 2011; Haque et al. 2022). The observed association can be explained by the intergenerational cycle of malnutrition. Underweight mothers are more likely to have inadequate nutritional reserve, which ultimately affects foetal growth, nutrient reserves, and poor postnatal weight gain, leading to underweight infants (Black et al. 2013). On the other hand, infants of overweight or obese mothers had a lower likelihood of being underweight compared to the infants of normal‐weight mothers. This concurs with other surveys (Casapía et al. 2007; Dekker et al. 2010; Felisbino‐Mendes et al. 2014; Hasan et al. 2016; Rayhan and Hayat Khan 2006). There is, however, also a risk that these children may develop overweight, obesity and non‐communicable diseases in early childhood or later in life (Agras et al. 2004; Salsberry and Reagan 2005).

Short maternal stature (under 145 cm) was associated with infant u6m underweight. This is also biologically plausible since maternal height is an indicator of intergenerational risk as well as a mother's own childhood nutrition and health. A secondary analysis of BDHS data showed that maternal short stature was associated with 1.3‐ and 1.4‐times higher risks of wasting and severe wasting, respectively. This study also found that a one‐centimetre increase in maternal height was associated with a 1.4% decrease in wasting (Khatun et al. 2019). Shorter maternal stature, an indicator of chronic undernutrition, leading to suboptimal nutrient stores, impaired foetal growth and underweight in infancy (Khanam et al. 2019; Kozuki et al. 2015; Muhihi et al. 2016; Rush 2000) (Black et al. 2013; Hart 1993; Van Roosmalen and Brand 1992). Also, short maternal stature is linked to poverty, limited access to healthcare and poor living conditions, which can contribute to underweight infants.

Maternal education is consistently recognised as one of the strongest determinants of a child's nutritional status. We also found a similar association. Mothers who had primary, secondary, or higher levels of education had a lower chance of their infants being identified as underweight compared to mothers with no education. This finding is consistent with other studies conducted in Bangladesh and other LMICs (Hasan et al. 2016; Hossain and Khan 2018; Rezaeizadeh et al. 2024; Vollmer et al. 2017). Glewwe developed a conceptual model describing how parental education can impact child health (Glewwe 1999). Parental education leads to changes in parental values and cognitive skills. Cognitive skills lead to changes in parental knowledge about health and household income. Ultimately, all these changes lead to the allocation of resources towards child health and nutrition. In Bangladesh, the mother is the primary caregiver, and the father is the primary decision‐maker (Shehan 2016). Apparently, educated parents can provide better protection for their child against malnutrition.

In our study, maternal employment status was found to be associated with infant malnutrition, with infants of employed mothers less likely to be classified as underweight. Before interpreting this finding, it is important to consider various factors, such as the nature of employment and socioeconomic status. Also, previous studies were based on older children. There may be residual confounding in our analysis that warrants consideration. This finding aligns with the ‘income effect’ hypothesis, which states that mothers joining the labour force increases the household's purchasing power, thereby improving access to quality health care services. However, the literature available in Bangladesh presents nuanced, often contradictory findings. Many studies have identified maternal employment as a risk factor for child malnutrition, particularly in resource‐limited settings (Khan et al. 2020). This is attributed to the ‘care‐time trade‐off’, in which mothers' absence from home due to work leads to reduced breastfeeding duration and suboptimal feeding (Kabir and Maitrot 2017). Our findings can be explained by the supportive system in terms of care and the type of work mothers are involved in. For mothers who can access extended‐family support or quality childcare, the positive income effect dominates the negative care‐time trade‐off effect (Win et al. 2022). Mothers who live in a nuclear family or work in demanding physical labour, their children mostly suffer from malnutrition (Das and Gulshan 2017).

In our study, infant age was also a statistically significant factor, with older infants (2‐ < 4 months and 4‐ < 6 months) showing a lower likelihood of being identified as wasted than those in the earliest stages of infancy. Studies analysing large‐scale data, such as the National Family Health Survey (NFHS‐4) of India, show that infants in the younger age group are more prone to severe wasting than those in older age groups (u6m) (Choudhary et al. 2019). This trend indicates that, with ageing, infants' physiological stability improves or caregiving practices become more established, thereby reducing the prevalence of wasting.

EBF up to 6 months of age is a foundational protective factor against child malnutrition. In this study, EBF was found to be associated with infant underweight. Infants who were not exclusively breastfed had higher odds of being identified as underweight compared with those who were exclusively breastfed. Similar findings were observed in studies conducted in Bangladesh and other LMICs (Khan and Islam 2017; Kuchenbecker et al. 2015; Nigatu et al. 2019). The immunological and balanced nutritional properties of breastmilk contribute to adequate nutritional status, proper growth, and the prevention of infant morbidity. Also, by reducing the chances of contamination from formula feeding, EBF reduces the risk of morbidity and mortality (Field 2005; Lamberti et al. 2011).

We also revealed a positive link between female‐headed households and infant wasting. This mirrors findings from others (Ashagidigbi et al. 2022). However, this association should be interpreted cautiously. Female‐headed households may serve as a proxy for broader social and structural circumstances, such as socioeconomic conditions, caregiving arrangements, household structure, and access to health care. In Bangladesh, most families are male‐headed, and female‐headed households often consist of widows or single parents who lack a male provider to assist with generating income. Often, women's wages are lower than those of their male counterparts, which may further affect infant nutritional status (Rahman and Al‐Hasan 2019). Being the head of the household might also lead to an increased need to work outside the home, which affects their ability to spend time with, care for, and breastfeed their babies (Hasan et al. 2020). Another finding of our study is that infants in the richest households had higher odds of wasting than those in middle‐income households, while no statistical association was observed with other wealth categories. This finding differs from the available literature (Kerac et al. 2025). However, it should not be overinterpreted. As we estimate multiple associations, false positives can readily occur, leading to counterintuitive findings. We performed the ‘Test of trend’ for the wealth index and wasting and observed no significant linear trend in wasting across different wealth quintiles. In terms of underweight status, infants from richer households were less likely to be identified as underweight, consistent with other literature (Kerac et al. 2025).

Fever in the last 2 weeks was also associated with underweight and is a marker of infections, which directly and indirectly affect infant growth (Weisz et al. 2011). However, this relationship may be bidirectional, as malnourished infants are also susceptible to infections. Thus, fever may reflect both a potential contributing factor and a consequence of poor nutrition. The practical implication of the finding is that it is important to support infant health and not just nutritional intake.

Strengths and Limitations

Since we rely on observational, cross‐sectional data, we acknowledge several limitations to our work. The key risks are unknown confounding and bias; these should be considered when interpreting our results. Even when biologically plausible, associations between risk factors and WLZ and WAZ should be interpreted cautiously, as hypothesis‐generating. Secondly, exposure may be misclassified due to recall bias during interviews with the mother. This may be less of a problem for infant u6m‐related information since the time lag is less than 6 months. It may, however, be problematic when a mother replies with socially desirable answers; this might, for example explain why EBF was not associated with decreased wasting in our analysis, as might have been expected (albeit it was associated with decreased underweight). Mothers may have answered positively while not actually breastfeeding. Measurement bias is another challenge. Although the interviewers were trained similarly and given the same information for interviews and measurements, there may still be variable responses across interviewers and inaccurate measurements. In addition, several variables in this study may be endogenous or serve as proxies rather than direct measures of exposure. Variables such as healthcare utilisation (e.g., place of delivery, PNC), recent illnesses, or feeding practices may reflect underlying vulnerability, access to care or responses to existing child health conditions. These complexities limit the ability to interpret observed associations as independent effects. The choice of the DHS dataset may also introduce selection bias. The children's dataset was used, which included infants whose mothers were home and eligible for an interview. The study did not include infants and mothers who were not at home during the household survey. This could mean that the mother and/or infant were hospitalised during the survey and have poorer health and nutritional outcomes. Another reason a mother might not be present could be that the infant was an orphan or that the mother was working. These children may have a different nutritional status than the children with an interviewed mother. Not including these infants in our analysis may mean our prevalence estimates are lower than the true prevalence, especially for severe wasting, which often requires inpatient treatment (WHO 2023). In this analysis, we combined data from 2011 to 2022 and assessed various factors associated with infant malnutrition. Although BDHS follows a standardised sampling and measurement protocol, there may be minor differences in survey implementation. When comparing across survey rounds, we should be cautious. It would have been ideal to evaluate factors for each dataset individually. However, due to the insufficient sample size, we were unable to do so. Nonetheless, we do not think it will change the actual message we wanted to convey. As this is a cross‐sectional study, we cannot establish temporality. Some of the changes we observed could be due to reverse causality. Also, we used various variable categorisations, which might have introduced residual confounding that should be considered when interpreting the findings of this analysis.

Despite the limitations, our study also has strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first study to estimate the total caseload of different forms of malnutrition among infants aged 6 months or younger in Bangladesh. This will be useful to local policymakers and programme managers. The basic analytical framework used in this study can be easily and rapidly replicated for other countries seeking to better understand the local epidemiology of infant u6m malnutrition. The generalisability of the approach to similar settings is thus high. Another strength of DHS data is that all measurements were collected from a representative population sample via a household survey with a high response rate and a two‐stage cluster sampling strategy. No intervention or new measurement technique has been introduced in this study. Therefore, the results reflect the current ‘real life’ situation.

Future Study

For future research, it would be important to examine differences in the associations between moderate and severe wasting or underweight with different risk factors. Ideally, the association between WLZ and WAZ should be assessed against mortality outcomes in a prospective study in a community setting. A prospective study can also help us better understand the risk factors for infant malnutrition. After checking their anthropometric indicators, follow‐up of these infants is needed to understand the mortality and nutritional risk for wasted infants who would not be included in the nutritional intervention when WAZ is used instead of WLZ. The reason why the prevalence of wasted and underweight infants increased in 2014 and stunting in 2017 needs to be evaluated. In this study, we found that female‐headed households were associated with higher odds of wasting. Longitudinal and qualitative studies would be important for understanding the link between household structure and infant nutrition in Bangladesh.

Conclusions

Over the past decade, Bangladesh has reduced various forms of malnutrition among infants u6m. However, the prevalence and overall burden are still high. Our analysis highlights the importance of focusing not just on nutritional intake but also on broader infant, maternal, and social factors that may underlie undernutrition. From a policy perspective, to address this issue effectively, packages of care focusing on mother‐newborn‐infant nutrition are needed. Future prospective studies are needed to determine which nutritional interventions and anthropometric indices most effectively identify, treat, and prevent malnutrition in infants.
