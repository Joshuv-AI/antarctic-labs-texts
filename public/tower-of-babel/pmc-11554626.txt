Platyhelminthes of common buzzard (Buteo buteo): Checklist of species in Europe and new parasite-host associations in Romania
Authors: Katarzyna Anna Hołówka, Angela Monica Ionică, Mihai Ilea, Alexandru Cristian Poșa, Vlad Dan Cotuțiu, Alexandru Bulacu, Jilji Sitko, Oana Cristina Vasiliu, Andrei Daniel Mihalca, Călin Mircea Gherman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlatyhelminthes are common parasites of diurnal birds of prey, including common buzzard (Buteo buteo). They are widely distributed in Europe. Despite the extensive study of flatworms across the continent, this study represents the first report for Romania and extends the biogeographic knowledge of Platyhelminthes in diurnal raptors. It also identifies common buzzards as a new host of Parastrigea flexilis in Europe.Materials and methodsBetween 2017 and 2020, 63 common buzzards (Buteo buteo) found road killed or dead due to other causes were collected from Romania. All specimens were necropsied and examined for internal helminths, which were preserved in ethanol for molecular identification and formaldehyde for morphological identification.ResultsSpecies identified included Cestoda: Cladotaenia globifera (33.3%) and five species of Trematoda: Neodiplostomum attenuatum (12.7%), Strigea falconis (6.4%), Parastrigea flexilis (3.2%), Neodiplostomum spathoides (3.2%) and Brachylaima fuscata (1.6%).ConclusionsThis study provides new data about diversity, phylogenetics and geographical distribution of Platyhelminthes parasitizing Buteo buteo in Romania. To our best knowledge, it identifies B. buteo as a new host of Parastrigea flexilis and reports the first genetic sequence of Neodiplostomum spathoides.

Introduction

The common buzzard, Buteo buteo is the most abundant diurnal raptor in Europe. The species is listed as least concerned by IUCN, with an increasing European population estimated to be between 2.1 and 3.7 million mature individuals (BirdsInternational, 2021). In Romania, the population is estimated to be between 20,000 and 50,000 breeding pairs. National distribution covers most of the country, from the lowlands of Danube Delta to the high mountain areas (Societatea Ornitologică Română, 2024).

Endoparasitic infections are common in the wild raptor birds (Krone and Cooper, 2002; 2007) Buzzards are exposed to parasites through their wide distribution in Europe, ecological role, and predatory behaviour. Common buzzards prey on various small mammals, amphibians, reptiles, and birds, mostly small passerines (Selas et al., 2007; Graham et al., 1995). Consequently, prey can serve as intermediate hosts to various heteroxenous parasites (Niewiadomska, 2002; Krone and Cooper, 2002; 2007).

Despite the abundance of common buzzards in the country, there is no available data on the internal parasites infecting this species in Romania. Therefore, the aim of this study was to provide data on the diversity, genetics, distribution, and epidemiology of trematodes and cestodes in common buzzards in Romania, along with updating the checklist of Platyhelminthes for this host across Europe.

Materials and methods

The carcasses of sixty-tree common buzzards (Buteo buteo), resulting from roadkills or natural causes of death, were collected in collaboration with the Romanian Wilderness Society between 2017 and 2020. For each carcass, the following data was recorded: GPS coordinates, sex (M/F/unknown), and age (juvenile - less than 1 year old, subadult - between 1 and 2 years old and adult older than 2 years old) (Zuberogoitia et al., 2005).

Carcasses were stored at −18 °C until examination, which involved a full parasitological necropsy. For this study, only flatworms were included. All internal organs were removed and separated; afterward, the gastrointestinal tract was opened, and the content was flushed with saline solution into Petri dishes and checked under a stereomicroscope (Olympus SZ). All remained organs were opened on a glass plate and examined for parasites under the stereomicroscope (Olympus SZ).

All flatworms were collected into Petri dishes with saline. Based on a preliminary morphological assessment, individuals from each morphotype were divided equally in two groups, preserved in 4% formaldehyde solution for morphological examination or 100% ethanol for molecular studies.

Morphological identification of cestodes was done using keys and descriptions provided by Kornyushin (1989). Keys included in Gibson et al. (2002),detailed descriptions given by Niewiadomska (2010) for Strigeidea and measurements given by Dubois (1964) for genus Neodiplostomum were used for trematodes identification. Additionally, cestodes and trematodes collected in formaldehyde were stained with haematoxylin (Dumendiak et al., 2024). Identification and photographs were performed with an Olympus BX61 light microscope, and pictures were made with dedicated software cellSens. Following the morphological identification, all specimens were counted.

DNA was extracted from 13 specimens preserved in ethanol using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. Due to the preservation in formaldehyde the DNA from C. globifera and P. flexilis was not extracted. A ∼780 bp fragment of the 18S rDNA gene was amplified using the C for/A rev primer pair (Routtu et al., 2014).Slight modifications of the originally published PCR amplification protocol: after single 2 min denaturation (94 °C) instead of 15 s, the time of denaturation (94 °C), annealing (60 °C) and elongation (72 °C) were extended by 15 s each, and the number of cycles, from 30 was increased to 40 repetitions, followed by 1 min elongation (72 °C). The PCR products were visualized following electrophoresis in 2% agarose gels stained with EcoSafe nucleic acid staining solution (Pacific Image Electronics, Taiwan), and their molecular weight was assessed by comparison to a molecular marker (HyperLadder™ 100 bp, meridian Bioscience, UK). All bands of the expected size were excised from the gels, purified using a commercial kit (Gel/PCR DNA Fragments Kit, Geneaid Biotech, Taiwan), and bidirectionally sequenced using an external service (performed by Macrogen Europe B.V., the Netherlands). The chromatograms were assembled and edited using geneious software version 4.8 (Biomatters Ltd., New Zealand), and the consensus sequences were compared to those available in the GenBank® by using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) analysis.

The phylogenetic trees were constructed using Bayesian inference (BI), as implemented in the MrBayes version 3.2.6 software (Huelsenbeck and Ronquist, 2001). The evolutionary history was inferred by using the GTR model, with non-uniformity of evolutionary rates among sites modelled by using a Gamma distribution. The analysis was run for 100,000 generations, with a tree sampled every 10 generations. The first 500 sampled trees were discarded as burn-in.

Visualization of data was done using ArcGIS Pro software version 3.22 (ESRI, Canada, 2023) using the World Imagery Base map. (Fig. 2).

Checklist of common buzzards Platyhelminthes was prepared based on extensive literature search published from historical times to the June 2024. Databases as PubMed, Research Gate, Web of Science, Science Direct, Host-Parasite Database of the Natural History Museum (London), along with web search engine Google Scholar were used. Parasites are listed in the table in the alphabetical order of families and species. Names of species are updated with current taxonomy. For each species country of report and references in chronological order are provided. If there were more than one report originating in one country references are listed in chronological order respectively.

The prevalence (P) and confidence interval (CI 95%) were calculated with Epitools Epidemiological Calculators (Sergeant, ESG, 2018).

The study was conducted under the permit numbers 229 from September 19, 2020 and 364 from March 06, 2023 granted by the bioethics committee of USAMV Cluj-Napoca.

Results

Thirty out of 63 examined buzzards were infected with Platyhelminthes. The overall recorded prevalence (P) was 47.6% (n = 30; P = 47.6%; CI 95% = 35.8–59.7%). Out of all buzzards, 21 (n = 21; P = 33.3%; CI 95% = 23.0–45.6%) were infected with tapeworms, and 12 (n = 12; P = 19.0%; CI 95% = 11.3–30.4%) with trematodes. In three birds (n = 3; P = 4.8%; CI 95% = 1.6–13.1%) both tapeworms and trematodes were found. Out of the 30 infected buzzards, 26 (n = 26; P = 41.3%; CI 95% = 30,0–53.6%) were infected with one species of flatworm, two (n = 2; P = 3.2%; CI 95% = 0.9–10.9%) with two species, one (n = 1; P = 1.6%; CI 95% = 0.3–8.5%) with three species and one (n = 1; P = 1.6%; CI95% = 0.3–8.5%) with four species. All flatworms were found only in the small intestine.

The morphological identification revealed one species of cestode, Cladotaenia globifera (Batsch, 1786) (Fig. 1a and b), and five species of trematodes in three families: Strigea falconis, Szidat, 1928 (Fig. 1c), Parastrigea flexilis (Dubois, 1934) (Strigeidae) (Fig. 1d), Neodiplostomum attenuatum (Linstow, 1906) (Fig. 1e), Neodiplostomum spathoides Dubois, 1937(Diplostomidae) (Fig. 1f) and Brachylaima fuscata (Rudolphi, 1819) (Brachylaimidae) (Fig. 1g and h). Discussion

In Europe, the diversity of endoparasites in common buzzards was well studied over the past 20 years (Sanmartin et al., 2004; Santoro et al., 2010, 2012; Tezel et al., 2015). A synoptic checklist of all Platyhelminthes reported in common buzzard in Europe is given in Table 2. However, prior to this study, there was no information on the Platyhelminthes fauna's structure, prevalence, and distribution in B. buteo from Romania. Platyhelminthes reported in Buteo buteo in Europe.

Cladotaenia globifera, the only cestode species collected in the present study, is typically found in several raptor species such as rough legged buzzard (B.lagopus), Eurasian sparrow hawk (Accipiter nisus), northern goshawk (A. gentilis), western marsh harrier (Circus aeruginosus), hen harrier (Circus cyaneus), common kestrel (Falco tinnunculus), and tawny eagle (Aquila rapax) (Sitko and Rysavy 1995). This is not unexpected, as its intermediate hosts are small mammals, especially voles, which represent a major component of buzzards’ diet (Selas et al., 2007). The prevalence reported in Romania was similar to other studies in Central Europe (Komorová et al., 2017) but higher than in Southern Europe (Santoro et al., 2012; Sanmartin et al., 2004).

Our study identified six species of Platyhelminthes in buzzards from Romania and reports B. buteo for the first time as a host for Parastrigea flexilis. The general prevalence of trematodes in our study was lower than previously reported in common buzzards: 71.42% in Turkey (Tazel et al., 2015) or 40.33% in Slovakia (Komorová et al., 2017).

Out of all trematode species obtained from B. buteo, N. attenuatum was the most prevalent, similarly to with other studies (Santoro et al., 2012; Krone, 2000). Common buzzards, as well as other birds of prey: European honey buzzard (Pernis apivorus), rough-legged buzzard (B. lagopus), long-legged buzzard (B. rufinus) (Komorová et al., 2016), Eurasian sparrow hawk (A. nisus), northern goshawk (A. gentilis), Montagu's harrier (C. pygargus), and peregrine falcon (Falco peregrinus) (Sanmartin et al., 2004) serve as definitive hosts, meanwhile amphibians are intermediate host, while reptiles and small mammals can act as paratenic hosts (Niewiadomska, 2002).

N. spathoides was previously recorded in common buzzards in several countries: Czechia, Hungary, Italy, and Slovenia (Sitko, 1994; Sey, 1973; Santoro et al., 2012; Berglez, 1997). This trematode can affect different species of birds of prey: Eurasian goshawk (A. gentilis), Eurasian sparrow hawk (A. nisus), long-legged buzzard (B. rufinus), marsh harrier (C. aeruginosus), common kestrel (F. tinnunculus), lesser spotted eagle (Clanga pomarina), or short-toed snake eagle (Circaetus gallicus) (Krone, 2000; Borgsteede et al., 2003; Sitko et al., 2006; Komorová et al., 2016; 2017). In this study, the prevalence was lower than Santoro's et al. (2012).

S. falconis was the second most common trematode in our study, as found also by Santoro et al. (2012) but with lower prevalence than in other studies (Komorová et al., 2017; Krone, 2000; Ferrer et al., 2004).

P. flexilis is rarely reported in buzzards (Buteo spp.), being more common in harriers (Circus spp.) (Sitko, 1998). The first report of this trematode in hosts of genus Buteo was in long-legged buzzards (B. rufinus) (Komorová et al., 2017) in Slovakia. Our finding represents the second report in the genus Buteo and the first on in the common buzzard, B. buteo, highlighting the possible role of buzzards in its natural cycle in Europe.

B. fuscata, is commonly found in Passeriformes, but it is uncommon in birds of prey (Heneberg et al., 2016). In our study, 14 specimens were collected from a single common buzzard. This trematode was first reported in one common buzzard from the Netherlands (Borgsteede et al., 2003). Afterward, it was reported in common buzzards form Italy and Czechia. (Santoro et al., 2012; Sitko 2024).

Analyses of phylogenetic tree of Diplostomidae showed closed genetically relation of identified Brachylaima fustcata to the sequence obtained in Australia. Position of sequence of N. attenuatum in the same cluster with one from Czechia revealed relationship between those specimens. Besides the fact, that sequence on Neodiplostomum spathoides showed 98.4% similarity to Neodiplostomum reflexum (Achatz et al., 2022) its position in phylogenetic tree indicates another species more genetically similar to N. attenuatum, N. microcotule, N. vaucheri and N. spathula. In addition, on the phylogenetic tree of Strigeaidae close resemblance to sequences of S. falconis from ones from Czechia and Poland.

Conclusion

Our studies provide new insights into the diversity, phylogenetics and geographical distribution of Platyhelminthes parasitizing Buteo buteo in Romania. Our study reports B. buteo as a new host of Parastrigea flexilis and obtained unique sequence of Neodiplostomum spathoides, which was added to the GenBank®.
