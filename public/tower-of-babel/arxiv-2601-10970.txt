Abstract
. Couples therapy, or relationship counseling, helps partners resolve conflicts, improve satisfaction, and foster psychological growth. Traditional approaches to training couples therapists, such as textbooks and roleplay, often fail to capture the complexity and emotional nuance of real couple dynamics. We present a novel multimodal, multi-agent simulation system that models multi-party interactions in couples therapy. Informed by our systematic research, this system creates a low-stakes environment for trainee therapists to gain valuable practical experience dealing with the critical demand-withdraw communication cycle across six couple-interaction stages. In an evaluation study involving 21 US-based licensed therapists, participants blind to conditions identified the engineered agent behaviors (i.e., the stages and the demand-withdraw cycle) and rated overall realism and agent responses higher for the experimental system than the baseline. As the first known multi-agent framework for training couples therapists, our work builds the foundation for future research that fuses HCI technologies with couples therapy.

Couples therapy is a core modality in mental health care with evidence for improving relationship distress and supporting individual well-being

36

Trainees must track two perspectives simultaneously, manage rapid emotional shifts, and intervene in live interaction cycles—skills that are hard to cultivate using textbooks and videos alone and imperfectly approximated by role-play due to issues with ecological validity, bias, cost, and scalability

Across approaches and settings, communication problems are among the most frequently reported presenting concerns for couples experiencing relationship difficulties, conflict, or emotional strain

Our paper introduces a multimodal, multi-agent simulation system for couples therapy that models the dynamics of two virtual patients and enables experiential practice with the complexities of multi-party dialogue.
Our system models multi-party interaction with two virtual patients and a module that controls the stage of dialogue (the

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Couples Therapy

In the field of mental health, most therapy takes the form of individual sessions where one person seeks support. Yet, beyond the one-on-one encounter lies the powerful domain of multi-party therapy, such as couples therapy, where the room holds more than one story. Couples therapy is a form of psychotherapy that involves both members of a committed partnership, whether married or unmarried, heterosexual or homosexual, and focuses on the patterns of interaction between partners
(
37
)
. Also known as marital therapy or relationship counseling, couples therapy is a psychotherapeutic process in which a trained clinician works with two individuals involved in a romantic relationship to resolve conflicts, improve relationship satisfaction, and promote emotional and psychological growth within the partnership
(
5
;
16
;
17
)
.

Couples therapy has gained growing recognition in mental health care because of its three unique features, including the high prevalence of relationship distress, its impact on partners and their families, and strong evidence supporting the effectiveness of couples therapy—not only in addressing relationship distress but also as a primary or complementary intervention for a range of individual emotional, behavioral, and physical health challenges
(
36
)
.

2.1.1.
Treatments in Couples Therapy

Building on this recognition and demonstrated effectiveness, the field has converged on several evidence-based approaches that operationalize couples therapy into structured interventions. Among these, three interventions are well-established for couples therapy
(
11
)
, including Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy (EFT), an empirically supported treatment for couples experiencing relationship distress
(
18
;
20
)
, Behavioral Couple Therapy (BCT), where therapist use behavioral techniques based on principles of operant conditioning, such as reinforcement
(
14
)
, and Integrative behavioral couple therapy (IBCT), an evolution of BCT that designed to help couples achieve improved satisfaction
(
16
)
.

2.1.2.
Common Presenting Problems in Couples Therapy

Across treatment approaches, couples therapy consistently targets a core set of difficulties, most of which can be grouped into two domains: specific relationship problems (e.g., extramarital affairs) or mental and physical health problems (e.g., mood disorders such as depression)
(
40
;
36
)
. Based on research, approximately 1.5% to 4% of married individuals will engage in extramarital sexual activity in any given year
(
2
)
. Also, approximately 17% of individuals experience a major depressive episode at some point in their lives
(
21
)
.

Among the diverse challenges that couples bring to therapy, problems in communication stand out as the most frequently acknowledged source of conflict
(
12
;
26
;
40
)
.
The core dynamic underlying problematic communication is often a negative interaction cycle known as
demand–withdraw
, referred to in Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT) as the
pursue–withdraw
cycle. Demand-withdraw pattern is prevalent among couples experiencing distress. In this cycle, “One member (the demander) criticizes, nags, and makes demands of the other, while the partner (the withdrawer) avoids confrontation, withdraws, and becomes defensive”
(
13
)
. This pattern of behavior can intensify conflicts and fuel negative affect both during interaction between partners and the aftermath
(
8
;
25
)
. EFT therapists use this cycle to track and attend to couples’ patterns of interaction and attachment behaviors
(
18
)
.

2.1.3.
Training Challenges in Couples Therapy

Despite the widespread practice of couples therapy in the U.S., most mental health training programs provide little to no formal preparation in this domain. Outside of specialized marriage therapy programs, exposure to couples therapy is typically limited to elective coursework or minimal supervised experience. Senior practitioners in couples therapy have long raised concerns about how the practice unfolds in everyday clinical contexts
(
10
)
.

In the training of couples therapists and practitioners, traditional methods such as textbook-centric learning, video demonstrations, and roleplay have long been the standard practice
(
27
;
9
)
. Although textbooks and videos provide theoretical grounding, their static and linear presentation can limit trainees’ ability to grasp the complex emotional and relational dynamics essential for effective therapeutic practice. Roleplay, another widely adopted technique, allows trainees to practice in simulated therapeutic sessions with peers or supervisors to build up their skills and self-efficacy in relationship counseling
(
31
)
. However, this may cause confusion from role switching and multiple characters, or expose potential bias, especially when their peers play the role of a client
(
35
)
. A major concern is that roleplay often lacks the complexity and emotional nuance of actual couple interactions. Researchers observe that roleplay can lead to oversimplified scenarios that do not accurately represent the dynamics encountered in real-life sessions
(
28
)
. Another concern is that roleplay usually requires substantial time and human resources, as each session requires trained supervisors, available peers, and repeated enactments to approximate the variability of real therapy encounters
(
22
)
. This reliance on human availability makes training less scalable and often uneven in quality across programs.
Importantly, therapists themselves have voiced concerns that inappropriate interventions during role-play sessions may inadvertently cause harm, such as triggering emotional distress in role-players
(
32
)
, even in a training context.

There is a clear need for alternative training systems that preserve the firsthand benefits of practice while minimizing the associated costs and risks. Such systems could simulate a real couples therapy session and provide a low-stakes environment where trainees can safely experiment with interventions and refine their therapeutic skills at any time, with lower cost, and independent of peer or supervisor availability. The field of HCI has offered a wide selection of research methods and tools that allow us to design, implement, and evaluate a multi-agent couples therapist training system that can realistically simulate multi-party therapist-patient interactions.

2.2.
Virtual Patients in Mental Health

Simulated patients have been shown to be effective in medical training, improving trainees’ self-efficacy and communication skills
(
42
;
34
;
30
)
. In parallel, large language models (LLMs) now function as conversational agents that adopt diverse personas and sustain coherent dialogue
(
23
)
. LLMs can carry out fluent and meaningful conversations, presenting a great opportunity to simulate a couples therapy session for training future therapists. Recent work utilizes large language models (LLMs) to generate authentic, lower-cost patient–clinician interactions and to role-play virtual patients that scaffold novice therapists’ counseling practice
(
7
;
24
;
38
)
. However, most systems simulate only a single patient agent at one time. While multi-agent approaches have appeared in mental-health applications, they typically assign distinct roles (e.g., therapist, patient, or assessor) rather than modeling multiple clients interacting with one another
(
19
;
3
)
. Consequently, existing platforms rarely capture multi-party client dynamics that are central to couples therapy and relationship counseling, motivating our focus on modeling multi-party interactions for this setting.

3.1.
Identifying Design Goals and Strategies

To understand how to design a simulation system that realistically simulates couples therapy, we conducted systematic research following three steps: (1) reviewing prior literature, (2) conducting interviews with domain-experts, and (3) performing validation analysis of real-world psychotherapy sessions. This three-step process is illustrated in Figure

Specifically, we first conducted a literature review on couples therapy and identified the demand–withdraw pattern, a well-documented dynamic central to couple conflict. To connect theory with practice, we then carried out semi-structured interviews with two experienced psychotherapists to understand their clinical experiences in couples therapy. One expert (T1) is a licensed psychologist (Ph.D.) in the United States with 10 years of experience in Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), Interpersonal Therapy (IPT), and Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy (EFT). The other expert (T2) is a supervising psychotherapist (M.Phil.) at a university-affiliated hospital in China with 9 years of experience in IPT, EFT, and Systemic Family Therapy. Finally, to triangulate our findings, we analyzed seven anonymized transcripts (1,621 conversation turns) from the Alexander Street Counseling and Psychotherapy Transcripts corpus

, which confirmed insights from the earlier stages.

3.1.1.
Demand–Withdraw Patterns

The demand–withdraw pattern is a well-documented dynamic central to couple conflict. This dyadic pattern features a
demander
who seeks change, discussion, or resolution of the issue and a
withdrawer
who seeks to avoid or terminate the discussion. It typically appears in distressed couples and is linked to heightened negative emotions (e.g., anger and sadness)
(
29
)
.

Crenshaw et al. (2017)
(
8
)
studied how couples communicate at three points in conflict episodes: when a problem arises, during its discussion, and after the discussion. Within these phases, demand–withdraw behaviors are expressed in ways such as initiating (demander) versus avoiding discussion (withdrawer), nagging or criticizing (demander) versus resisting or defending (withdrawer), and pressuring for change (demander) versus disengaging afterward (withdrawer)
(
8
)
. This framing allowed us to model demand–withdraw not as a one-off exchange but as a dynamic pattern that can surface across different phases of interaction.

Through the interviews, our expert participants described in detail how a couples therapy session typically unfolds across six recurrent stages:

T2 affirmed that the six stages accurately captured the structure and flow of therapy sessions in practice, providing validation that our framework aligned with authentic therapeutic encounters.

The experts’ suggestions are well aligned with the literature, which also suggests that escalation and de-escalation are critical stages for couple conflicts. Within conflict episodes, escalation reflects intensifying disagreements, broadening topics, rising hostility, and repeated counter-complaints

(

Including enactments as one of the stages allowed us to mirror a key therapeutic intervention for problematic patterns that fosters emotional accessibility, secure attachment, and improved relational functioning

In addition, experts also offered design recommendations for simulation systems: (1) therapists commonly alternate between speaking to both partners together and to each individually; (2) the system should provide concise background on the couple and their presenting problems; and (3) difficulty levels could be calibrated to the unfolding interaction (e.g., higher difficulty features greater resistance and more persistent negative cycles). These recommendations directly informed the feature set of our simulation system.

3.1.3.
Validation of the findings

To validate our findings from both literature and expert interviews, we performed a validation analysis using the Alexander Street (Counseling and Psychotherapy Transcripts) dataset, a public corpus contains anonymized transcripts spanning multiple therapeutic approaches and client populations. From this corpus, we identified transcripts from seven couples therapy sessions (including 1,621 conversation turns) and analyzed them to surface recurring interaction stages and demand-withdraw pattern.

Transcripts were independently annotated by two researchers using a stage-based coding scheme as well as the demand–withdraw dynamics, both derived from prior literature and expert interviews. Coders identified the presence of each stage and demand–withdraw pattern in every transcript, reaching strong agreement (Cohen’s
\kappa=0.88
). For example, we found that greeting occurred in 5 out of 7 conversations, problem raising in 7 out of 7, escalation in 5 out of 7, de-escalation in 4 out of 7, enactment occurred in 5 out of 7, and wrap-up in 7 out of 7, confirming that the six stages identified in our expert interviews were consistently observable in authentic sessions. In addition, demand–withdraw dynamics were evident in five of the seven sessions, further confirming their prevalence in the interactions. These findings validate that the stages and dynamics identified through prior literature and expert interviews are consistently observable in real-world couples therapy sessions.

Building on the insights gathered from our research, we designed the
Couple Agents
: two agents powered by large language models (LLMs) to simulate the dynamics of real couples in conflict
. The agents are designed to provide trainee therapists with a low-stakes setting to practice counseling skills and experiment with interventions. The system incorporates the demand–withdraw pattern alongside the six interaction stages identified in our earlier analysis, enabling structured simulations of distressed couple interactions.

The system architecture consists of three core components: (1) two LLM-powered virtual patients embodying the demand-withdraw dynamic, (2) a stage-based interaction controller that manages stage progression, and (3) agent-to-agent interactions.

3.2.1.

To simulate the core dynamics of distressed couples, we instantiated two LLM-based agents as virtual patients, named

and

and

withdraws, becomes silent, defends, resists apologizing,

The actual behaviors of the agents are tuned to the current interaction stage as well as their demand/withdraw characteristics.
Alex plays the role of a demander, who always raises problems, expresses dissatisfaction, and verbalizes explicit demands for change; Jordan assumes the withdrawer role, characterized by avoidance behaviors, emotional withdrawal, or defensive reactions. This dyadic interplay creates a vicious cycle in which demand reinforces withdrawal and vice versa, until therapeutic intervention from the trainee disrupts the loop. At each interaction stage, both agents follow a consistent set of behavioral rules to simulate a distressed couple, as encoded in a set of structured prompts that include stage-specific behavioral instructions shown in Table

3.2.2.
Stage-based Interaction Controller

The two virtual patients adjust their behaviors based on the current interaction stage. Informed by our research into the literature and with expert psychotherapists, we designed a stage-based interaction controller that determines the next interaction stage based on the conversation and stage history, following the rules specified in Table
3
. These rules are derived from what our research revealed about the definitions of the stages and the roles that a therapist plays at each stage, as shown in Table
1
. In a therapeutic session, the two virtual patients and the therapist take turns to speak. After the therapist responds to either patient, the controller will analyze the current context (the most recent messages from the couple agents and the therapist’s response) and determine the next stage following the prompt shown in Appendix B1, which may activate different instructions for agent behaviors in subsequent conversations. With the controller, we are able to simulate a therapeutic session with six distinct stages, some of which may be revisited in a non-linear order while the session is live.

Here is how the interactions are structured and how the controller works. All sessions begin with the
greeting
stage, where the two virtual patients wait for the therapist to initiate a small talk with a friendly line, such as “
How are you doing?
” Then, instructed to simulate an aggressive demander, Alex will reply with an emotional response insinuating the relationship problems that troubled the couple. The therapist is likely to respond with a request asking for more description, but
before the conversation continues
, the controller will recognize these stage transition cues and decide to advance the session into the
problem raising
stage, where the two agents will follow
new

Since the two agents encode contrasting characteristics and behavior patterns (demanding vs. withdrawing), disputes and conflicts are unavoidable, and are intentional for psychotherapy training. As the conflicts intensify, the controller will advance the session into the
escalation
stage, where the two agents follow the behavioral rules specific to “Escalation” listed in Table
3
and exacerbate the demand-withdraw divide. If the conversation stagnates at the problem raising stage
for more than seven conversation turns

As revealed by our literature research, the demand-withdraw interaction cycle is a prevalent behavior pattern observed in distressed couples, representing a critical therapeutic challenge that an effective training tool should enable trainee therapists to practice addressing. Our interviews with expert therapists also suggest that the demand-withdraw pattern typically emerges during the problem raising and escalation stages characterized by partner-to-partner interactions. Therefore, we explicitly simulate demand-withdraw conversations in those two stages by promoting agent-to-agent interactions.

Our system constantly predicts who the next speaker is based on five most recent conversation turns and the current interaction stage, following the prompt shown in Appendix B2. Normally, when the therapist is leading the conversation, both agents only respond to the therapist but not to each other, and the therapist is the predicted next speaker after either agent. During the problem raising and escalation stages, however, we explicitly instruct the agents to begin to address each other whenever accusatory expressions, such as “you always” or “you never”, appear. These accusatory expressions are likely to show up in the conversation sooner or later because Alex is endowed with an inflammatory character. This realistically mimics the language and dynamics of a real therapy session, where couples often engage in direct exchanges during heated moments. Once the system determines that the agents are addressing each other, the conversation will enter an agent-to-agent loop, which will last for three turns in the problem raising stage and five turns in the escalation stage unless interrupted by the therapist. The therapist can step in and interrupt the heated exchanges at any time by talking to either or both agents to steer the conversation.

3.2.4.
Iterative Design

We conducted a pilot study with five licensed therapists to validate the system design. Each participant interacted with the system for 15 minutes and was interviewed to provide feedback. One notable problem that surfaced from the pilot study was that, while our system simulates realistic and chaotic partner-to-partner conflicts during the escalation stage,
some therapists experienced a longer escalation stage and did not get a chance to sufficiently explore later stages, such as de-escalation or enactment. To ensure every trainee therapist can go through the full simulated therapeutic session and benefit from the practice experiences at all stages, we add a special stage transition rule that instructs the stage controller to advance the stage to de-escalation after the therapist has made
two
attempts at steering the conversation that is spinning out of control. Since our system allows non-linear progression among the stages, the trainee therapists may revisit the escalation stage following some practice in the de-escalation stage, if they are still learning but have not yet mastered how to effectively manage conflicts.

3.3.
Full System Implementation

We designed and implemented a full training system for couples therapy by integrating the couple agents, along with agent personas, multimodal outputs, training scenarios, difficulty settings, and features that allow trainees to address specific clients. Figure
3
shows a screenshot of the interface.

3.3.1.
Agent Persona Design

In a real therapeutic session, patients not only communicate their thoughts but also their emotions (which surface even faster). Prior literature and our secondary analysis of real sessions also reveal the prominence of negative affect in distressed couples. Therefore, we explicitly encode these emotions in the two virtual patient agents to enhance the realism of the simulation.

Specifically, Alex, the aggressive demander, is instructed to express emotions that feel
Neutral
,
Sad
,
Angry
,
Hopeful
,
Vulnerable
, and
Relieved
in each of the six interaction stages. On the other hand, Jordan, the defensive withdrawer, is instructed to show emotions that feel
Neutral
,
Anxious
,
Sad
,
Cautious
,
Open
, and
Calm
. These emotional shifts are intended to serve as nonverbal cues that guide trainees’ interventions.

To ensure the trainee therapist can observe the expressed emotions as in a real therapeutic session, we designed two animated avatars that visually express emotional states across different interaction stages. The avatars convey emotional cues through changes in color palettes and vocal tone as shown in Box 1 of Figure
3
.

3.3.2.
Text and Voice Output

By listening to what patients have said, therapists can use subtle vocal cues to manage the flow of a therapeutic session. To simulate realistic therapeutic interactions, we rendered agent behaviors via both text and voice, enabling the trainees to perceive and respond to subtle verbal cues. Trainees provide input through text, while the agents reply with both text and synthesized speech. For voice output, we employed OpenAI’s Text-to-Speech model with separate instructions (detailed in Appendix C) for different expressed emotions to bring nuance and realism in each agent’s response. The demander’s voice carries the weight of emotional investment, while the withdrawer’s tone reflects the protective distance of someone conditioned to expect conflict. The tone and style of speech shift according to the expressed emotion at each stage, with tailored prompts for different emotions. This results in more dynamic and realistic tonal variation and transitions.

3.3.3.
Scenario Design

The system supports custom scenarios but also provides two pre-defined scenarios adapted from authentic couples’ session transcripts in the Alexander Street (AS) dataset. The two original scenarios, shown below, involve affairs and depression-related challenges—two highly common problems in couples therapy. Both epitomize a complex therapeutic challenge that naturally elicits demand-withdraw patterns. Trainers can modify the scenarios to focus on specific therapeutic skills or relationship issues.

(1)

AS Scenario 1:
“The couple talks about one partner’s worsening depression, inability to cope with the depression, increased thoughts of suicide and self-harm, and the exhausting emotional toll it is taking on both individuals in the relationship.”

(2)

AS Scenario 2:
“The couple is seeking counseling due to strain in their relationship after the one partner had an affair.”

3.3.4.
Three-Tiered Difficulty Level

To accommodate trainees with varying levels of experience and to simulate differing degrees of client resistance, we implemented a three-tiered difficulty system by utilizing different prompts for both the discussion control flow and the agent behavior, depending on the selected level:

(1)

Easy:
"You are open to the trainee’s interventions and show some flexibility."

(2)

Normal:
"You respond realistically to the trainee’s interventions with moderate resistance."

(3)

Hard:
"You are highly resistant to the trainee’s interventions, very slow to change, and deeply entrenched in their patterns."

In addition to using distinct prompts, the “hard” difficulty level features a more resistant conversation flow, allowing agents to interrupt each other even when one is being addressed directly. This introduces greater unpredictability into the interaction.

3.3.5.
Addressing the specific patient (Alex, Jordan, or both)

According to domain experts, it is common for the therapist to address the patients both individually and collectively. In our system, the trainee can choose to address either or both virtual patients by clicking on the settings named “Alex”, “Jordan” or “Both”. The trainee can also address one of the patients by referring to that person by name (e.g., “Alex, what do you think?”), which prompts Alex to respond individually or initiates an agent-to-agent conversation with Jordan, depending on the current stage of interaction. This feature allows trainees to practice both individual and couple-focused interventions.

3.3.6.
Implementation Details

Implemented in React, the front-end uses web-socket communication to allow the user to interrupt the conversation flow between the two agents. The interface also displays information about the interaction stage and which agent plays the role of
Pursuer
or
Withdrawer
, providing more guidance and information for training purposes. The backend system integrates the agent and session behavior outlined in the prior section through the Python framework Flask and web-socket communication.

4.
Evaluation Method

4.1.
Evaluation Goals

The primary objective of our evaluation is to assess the perceived realism of the couple simulation system. Specifically, we aim to: (1) evaluate whether the couple agents’ demand-withdrawal cycle dynamics are perceived as authentic and believable, (2) assess whether the couple agents exhibit behaviors consistent with those of actual couples across different stages of therapy sessions, and (3) determine whether, overall, our couple agents are perceived as realistic by expert participants.

4.2.
Study Design

We conducted a within-subjects user study with licensed therapists to evaluate system realism. Participants experienced two conditions: an experimental system with couple agents incorporating demand-withdrawal patterns and stage-specific behaviors, and a baseline system with couple agents lacking these features. The baseline system prompts each agent only with a role and scenario:
“You are [name], a member of a couple in therapy. The scenario is:[scenario[difficulty]]”
. Both systems shared identical scenarios and interfaces to control for potential confounds. Stage indicators and agent personality information were removed from the interface to avoid leading participants toward specific dynamics.

We recruited
N=21
licensed therapists with couples therapy experience ranging from 1 to 27.5 years, all practicing in the United States. Participants were blind to experimental conditions and the specific engineered agent behaviors being evaluated. We randomly assigned the order of the experimental and baseline systems for each participant to mitigate learning effects. Although the system supports custom scenarios, to reduce confounding variables, we elaborated on the couples therapy scenarios shown in Section
3.3.3
and provided all participants with two consistent scenarios, both having a
normal
difficulty level:

(1)

User-study Scenario 1:
“The couple has been struggling as Jordan’s depression has significantly worsened over the past several months. Jordan describes feeling trapped in a cycle of hopelessness, experiencing frequent intrusive thoughts of suicide and self-harm, and an overwhelming inability to cope with daily life. Simple tasks have become monumental challenges, and Jordan often isolates in bed for hours or days at a time.”

(2)

User-study Scenario 2:
“The couple sought counseling after the revelation that Alex had been involved in an affair several months ago. The betrayal has shaken Jordan’s sense of safety in the relationship to the core. Jordan reports feeling devastated, angry, and uncertain about whether trust can be rebuilt, often replaying the events in their mind and questioning Alex’s honesty.”

4.3.
Procedure

Each participant attended a single 90-minute session structured as follows: 10 minutes for onboarding, 30 minutes of interaction (i.e., 15 minutes with the experimental couple agents and 15 minutes with the control couple agents in random order), 40 minutes for survey completion, and 10 minutes for wrap-up. During each 15 minute system interaction, participants received prompts at three checkpoints (every 5 minutes) to complete brief questionnaires assessing their real-time perceptions of the interaction. Participants were compensated at a rate of $150 per hour. All responses were collected through Qualtrics for systematic quantitative and qualitative analysis.

4.4.
Measures

4.4.1.
Checkpoint Measures (administered every five minutes during each conversation with the couple agents).

At every checkpoint, participants (1) labeled the interaction stage they perceived (multiple choice questions), in order to determine whether the stage that the agents were actually presenting was realistic enough to be perceived. For each stage they labeled, they rated (2) the realism of the couple agents’ emotions, behaviors, and interactions during the stage; (3) role fidelity—the extent to which the agents’ responses aligned with the ongoing scenario context; (4) response realism for the most recent exchanges; (5) the perceived extent of demand–withdraw dynamics; and finally, (6) the overall realism of the couple agents, reflecting how closely the agents resembled real couples. Except for the first measure, which asked participants to label the current stage, all checkpoint measures utilized 5-point Likert scales.

4.4.2.
Post-Session Measures (administered after each conversation with the couple agents).

After each 15-minute interaction with a system, participants rated the overall realism of agents on a 5-point Likert scale, and provided feedback identifying specific moments that felt realistic or unrealistic.

4.4.3.
Comparative Measures (administered after interacting with both systems).

After participants interacted with both systems, they
evaluated (1) which system (experimental or baseline) is more realistic and (2) the perceived effectiveness of both systems for novice couple therapy training (5-point Likert scales). In addition, (3) they answered open-ended questions regarding training effectiveness and instructional value of the couple agents, and (4) open-ended questions about their suggestions for improvement.

6.
Discussion

Across manipulation checks, realism judgments, and perceived training effectiveness, results consistently favored the experimental couples simulation. Participants blind to condition reliably recognized the engineered interaction stages and the demand-withdraw cycle, and they rated overall realism, agent-response realism, and training value higher than for the baseline. Realism also varied systematically by stage—especially during
problem raising
,
escalation
, and
de-escalation
—together constituting a clear proof-of-concept that the simulation works as intended and shows strong promise for improving counselor training.

Therapists judged the experimental system as more realistic overall than a baseline without engineered stage and cycle logic and reported that it would be more useful for training couples therapists. We speculate that these advantages arise because the system simulates behavioral patterns that are common in real couples therapy—how problems are raised, how demand-withdraw escalation unfolds, and how softening and repair can be achieved. By making these trajectories explicit and dynamically responsive to the trainee’s moves, the environment offers practice that feels both familiar and consequential, thereby strengthening its validity.

Crucially, gains concentrated in
problem raising
,
escalation
, and
de-escalation
—the phases where timing, wording, and turn-taking most strongly shape client responses. These are precisely the moments in which novice therapists often struggle with session management: setting the frame, distributing airtime, interrupting productively, and choosing prompts that invite each partner’s perspective without amplifying conflict. The simulation’s stage logic appears to scaffold these micro-skills by giving immediate, context-sensitive consequences to therapist choices, thereby cultivating the capacity to get couples talking productively within a single session.

At the same time, we are cautious about the scope of what such a simulation can teach. Our 15
-
minute sessions are a pragmatic proxy for the much longer courses of couples therapy that often unfold over weeks and months. Within that constraint, the present results suggest benefits primarily for in
-
session process management (e.g., reducing in
-
the
-
moment conflict and getting partners to listen to each other), yet it remains unclear whether the current design alone prepares trainees to formulate and intervene on deeper relational problems (e.g., attachment injuries, longstanding patterns outside the hour). Moreover, although the simulator affords practice and opportunities for self
-
reflection, the current system does not yet provide formative feedback about what trainees are doing or structured opportunities for guided self
-
reflection. Addressing these broader training goals likely requires extended, multi
-
session arcs, integration with case
-
conceptualization frameworks, and supervision or automated coaching that explicitly links simulator practice to change processes that develop over time. This delineation points to a practical division of labor: use the simulator to build rapid, in
-
room fluency with session
-
management skills, and pair it with complementary training and feedback mechanisms to target underlying relational dynamics.

Several therapist suggestions point to near
-
term enhancements that could deepen realism, add feedback, and broaden skill coverage. First, adding voice input would allow trainees to speak with agents, practicing prosody, pacing, interruptions, and supportive backchannels that are central to couple work but not captured by text alone. Second, layering unobtrusive nonverbal annotations into the transcript (e.g., “Alex is eye rolling,” “Jordan glances at the therapist, then looks down and shrugs slightly,” “avoids eye contact”) would surface affect, alignment, and power signals that guide the timing and selection of interventions. Third, incorporating structured feedback and reflection—such as post-session dashboards that summarize stage transitions and demand-withdraw intensity, track talk-time balance and interruptions, and provide moment-linked detection of micro-skills (e.g., accurate reflection, softening, validation) with suggestions for alternatives—would further strengthen the system’s instructional value.

This work introduces the first framework to realistically simulate couples therapy conversations. Our contributions are threefold: we developed a couples therapy simulator that models six common interaction stages and the demand-withdraw cycle, giving trainees practice with realistic session dynamics; we grounded the system in theory, expert interviews, and real-world session data to ensure that the modeled stages, client behaviors, and transitions are clear and usable; and we conducted an evaluation with licensed therapists showing that they could recognize the simulated stages and cycles, rated the system as more realistic and useful than a baseline, and highlighted especially strong effects in
problem raising
,
escalation
, and
de-escalation
. Together, these contributions demonstrate how a couples therapy simulator can support trainees in practicing session-management skills and provide a foundation for future training tools.

Appendix B1. Stage Transition

Given the following couple therapy conversation and previous stage history identify
which stage the session is currently in.

Stages:
- Greeting: Initial hellos, small talk, or opening "how are you" questions.
→ Not Greeting if: Partners have started discussing issues or problems.

- Problem Raising: One partner introduces a complaint or relationship issue.
Discussion focuses on what brought
them to therapy.
→ Switch to Escalation stage when: Discussion shifts from introducing to
actively arguing or escalating.

- Escalation: Conflict intensifies. Alex becomes more critical or demanding. Jordan
becomes defensive, withdrawn, or counter-attacks. Blame is assigned; pursue-withdraw
patterns emerge.
→ Do NOT stay in Escalation if one partner begins sharing vulnerable emotions —
in that case, choose Enactment.

- De-Escalation: Therapist validates the opinions of Alex and/or Jordan. Therapist
actively intervenes to calm a highly confrontational situation—validating both sides,
reframing, or helping partners understand each other, or interrupts a heated argument.
Key words may include, "let’s slow it down", "stop", "calm down", "let’s take a break",
OR the therapist starts asking questions that help the couple share more vulnerable feelings.
→ Switch to Enactment stage when: One partner begins to express vulnerable
emotions to the other.

- Enactment: One partner directly expresses and communicates their vulnerable
feelings to their partner (not just to the therapist). Examples include hurt, sadness,
fear, loneliness, longing, disappointment, shame, guilt, or need for closeness.
Therapist may facilitate one partner turning to the other to share these emotions.
→ Prioritize Enactment over Escalation if vulnerable emotions are shared, even if some
conflict words remain.
→ Examples: “I feel hurt when…”, “I’m scared you’ll leave…”, “I need you to…”,
“I just want you to care…”

- Wrap-up: The session is ending. The therapist summarizes progress, mentions time ending,
or schedules the next session. Partners may also express appreciation or relief.
(Examples: ’let’s wrap up’, ’We are out of time’, ’Good bye’,
’We learned a lot this session’, etc.)

Respond with only the stage name.

Appendix B2. Next Speaker Determination

You are deciding who speaks next in a couple’s therapy conversation between Alex, Jordan,
and the Therapist.

If the current speaker addresses another (Example:
"Alex, what do you think?", "Jordan, could you tell me more", "Jordan, have you tried
taking any small steps to help Alex out?")
return the person being addressed.

Decision rules:
1. If the therapist sends any message and you are not ignoring them
(per resistance rules) → respond "therapist".
2. If Alex says "you" in reference to Jordan’s actions → "Jordan".
3. If Jordan says "you" in reference to Alex’s actions → "Alex".
4. If Alex is speaking directly to Jordan (without therapist intervention) → "Jordan".
5. If Jordan is speaking directly to Alex (without therapist intervention) → "Alex".
6. If Alex or Jordan speak without addressing the other directly → "therapist".
7. The therapist never replies to themselves — if the last message is from
the therapist, only "Alex", "Jordan", or "both" are valid.

8. If the last message is from the therapist and it isn’t directed return "both".
9. If the therapist directly addresses one of the patients
(e.g., "Jordan, what do you think?", "What brings you in today Alex?") → that patient.

Valid answers: Alex, Jordan, both, therapist.
Return only one word.
