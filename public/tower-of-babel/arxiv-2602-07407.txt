On two-dimensional steady compactly supported Euler flows with constant vorticity


Abstract
In this paper, we mainly construct local solution curves for the two-dimensional steady compactly supported incompressible Euler equations with free boundaries and constant vorticity. Our work is distinguished from most existing studies on two-dimensional steady water waves by its focus on perturbations near annular flows, rather than laminar flows. More precisely, we consider three classes of steady Euler flows with compact support, corresponding to partially overdetermined, two-phase overdetermined, and overdetermined elliptic problems. The primary contribution of our work is threefold. For each class, we first establish the flexibility result (i.e., the existence of nontrivial admissible domains) via shape derivatives and local bifurcation theory. Second, we give and discuss the corresponding rigidity result respectively. Third, we apply the implicit function theorem to demonstrate the stability of standard annular flows under perturbations of the Neumann boundary condition. Our results also offer novel insights into the theory of elliptic overdetermined problems.

Introduction and main results

We first consider two-dimensional steady incompressible Euler equations with vorticity, which is known as the water waves problem
[
10
, (2.1)-(2.2)]
. In such problem, the fluid domain
\Omega
is bounded by a fixed boundary
\mathcal{B}
and a free sufficiently smooth non-self-intersecting curve

\mathcal{S}(s):=\{x(s),y(s):s\in\mathbb{R}\}

and one seeks a smooth stream function
\psi(x,y)
defined on
\Omega
, whose gradient gives the velocity field
(\psi_{y},-\psi_{x})
, satisfying the following system of equations and boundary conditions:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta\psi=\gamma(\psi)&\text{in}\,\,\Omega,\\
\psi=0&\text{on}\,\,\mathcal{S},\\
\psi=m&\text{on}\,\,\mathcal{B},\\
|\nabla\psi|^{2}+2gy=Q&\text{on}\,\,\mathcal{S},\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

where
\gamma(\psi)
is the vorticity function,
g
denotes the
gravitational constant of acceleration, and the constants
m
and

Q
represent the relative mass flux and the total head,
respectively. Although system (
1.1
) with non-closed
streamlines
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{S}
, referred to as steady
water waves problem, has been widely studied

[
8
,
10
,
9
,
17
,
53
,
54
,
56
]
,
the closed streamline configuration has received considerably less
attention. Our work aims to address this gap by constructing local
curves of steady solutions for the closed streamline case. To gain a
better focus on the topic, we restrict ourselves to discussing
steady solutions in the following introduction. The mathematical
study of steady water waves dates back over two centuries to the
pioneering work of Laplace and Lagrange in the late 18th century.
Early investigations conducted by
mathematicians such as Cauchy, Poisson, and Airy, often relied on
linearizing the equations near still water. A
particularly remarkable contribution came from Gerstner, who
derived an exact family of steady solutions to the fully nonlinear
system. Another significant advance was made by Stokes, who employed
perturbation expansions in wave amplitude to analyze traveling water
waves. Stokes
[
52
]
also studied waves of large amplitude
and proposed a famous conjecture: the existence of wave
of greatest height, featuring sharp crests with an included angle of

2\pi/3
.

In the last century, most research remained confined to
two-dimensional and irrotational flows. In this setting, the stream
function
\psi
is harmonic throughout the fluid domain. By
employing the Green’s function of the Laplacian, the problem can be
reformulated as an integral equation on the free boundary curve

\mathcal{S}
. Nekrasov
[
41
]
pioneered this approach,
constructing the first rigorous small-amplitude periodic solutions
for water waves over infinite depth. He expanded a presumed solution
in powers of the amplitude and established a positive radius of
convergence for the series. Shortly thereafter, Levi-Civita

[
40
]
independently derived similar results using a more
direct power-series expansion, avoiding the use of Green’s
functions. All these early results pertained to waves of small
amplitude. A major advance came with the work of Keady and Norbury

[
38
]
, who applied global bifurcation theory to obtain a
continuous branch of large-amplitude solutions. Subsequently, Amick,
Fraenkel, and Toland
[
3
]
analyzed the limit along this
branch to rigorously establish the existence of the Stokes extreme
wave. At the turn of the century, the inclusion of vorticity posed a
significant challenge: it was unclear how to move beyond small
perturbations of a flat surface. A breakthrough was achieved by
Constantin and Strauss
[
8
]
, who adapted the
semi-hodograph transformation and bifurcation theory to establish
the existence of large-amplitude water waves with arbitrary smooth
vorticity distributions. However, this method inherently excludes
overhanging wave profiles, stagnation points, and critical layers.
To overcome these limitations and allow for stagnation points and
critical layers, many subsequent studies adopted a flattening
transformation, which scales the vertical coordinate within each
vertical ray to a fixed interval (see, e.g.

[
15
,
36
,
54
,
53
]
). A main drawback of this
approach is that it requires the wave profile to be a graph, thereby
still excluding overhanging waves. To this end, Constantin et al.

[
10
,
9
]
introduced a conformal mapping
approach, representing the fluid domain as the image of a horizontal
strip. This technique enabled the construction of both small- and
large-amplitude waves with constant vorticity, without imposing a
priori geometric constraints on the free surface or the stream
function. As a result, it accommodates solutions with critical
layers, stagnation points, and overhanging profiles. The versatility
of this framework
[
9
]
has been further demonstrated
through its extensions to stratified waves
[
33
]
,
capillary-gravity waves
[
57
]
, and electrohydrodynamic waves

[
12
]
. More recently, Wahlén and Weber

[
55
,
56
]
also employed a conformal change of
variables and bifurcation theory to prove the existence of
large-amplitude capillary-gravity and gravity waves with a
prescribed but arbitrary vorticity distribution, allowing for
stagnation points, critical layers, and overhanging profiles. A key
innovation in their works is the removal of any structural
assumptions on the vorticity distribution.

However, the steady solutions studied above are confined to
strip-shaped domains. In many practical situations, the free surface
may instead be closed, as in the case of liquid drops or water
columns. It is therefore natural to investigate the steady water
wave problem (
1.1
) in bounded domains. This motivates us to
study Euler flows with both a closed surface and a closed bed. Since
the generalized vorticity function presents not only complex
physical phenomena but also considerable mathematical challenges,
our analysis here is restricted to constant vorticity as in

[
10
]
, i.e.,
\gamma(\psi)=\gamma
. More precisely, we
investigate the following system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta\psi=\gamma&\text{in}\,\,\Omega\setminus\overline{D_{0}},\\
\psi=0&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\\
\psi=1&\text{on}\,\,\partial D_{0},\\
|\nabla\psi|^{2}=Q>0&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

on a ring-shaped domain
\Omega\setminus D_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

where

\lambda

\partial\Omega

\psi
is the stream function,

gravitational potential energy

1.2

boundary problem
[
34

The second model we examine builds upon recent research
[
31

Here we extend this framework to the case of closed streamlines.
That is, we further

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta\psi_{1}=\gamma_{1}&\text{in}\,\,D,\\
\Delta\psi_{2}=\gamma_{2}&\text{in}\,\,\Omega\setminus\overline{D},\\
\psi_{1}=\psi_{2}&\text{on}\,\,\partial D,\\
\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}\partial_{\nu}\psi_{1}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}}\partial_{\nu}\psi_{2}&\text{on}\,\,\partial D,\\
\psi_{2}=0&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\\
|\nabla\psi_{2}|^{2}=Q>0&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

on a disk-shaped domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where the
domain

is the core of
\Omega
,
\partial D
and

\partial\Omega
are two free boundaries. Here,
\psi_{1}
and

\psi_{2}
are stream functions on

\Omega\setminus\overline{D}

\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}

denote two distinct constant vorticities,
\nu
is the outer unit

is the Bernoulli constant.

At last, we consider a similar two-dimensional steady incompressible Euler equations with constant vorticity near circular
flows as problem (

(1.4)

on a ring-shaped domain
\Omega\setminus D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

2.2
Shape derivatives

We first introduce the concept of shape derivatives

[
7
,
35
]
. We consider the following class of smooth
perturbations:

\mathcal{A}:=\{\Phi\in C^{2}\left([0,1),C^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)\right)~|~\Phi(0,\cdot)\equiv 0\}.

For
\Phi\in\mathcal{A}
,
t\in[0,1)
and an arbitrary domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we set

\Phi(t):=\Phi(t,\cdot),\quad\Omega_{t}:=(Id+\Phi(t))\Omega=\{x+\Phi(t,x)~|~x\in\Omega\}.

By assumption, for a given
\Phi\in\mathcal{A}
, there exists some smooth vector field
h
such that the following expansion
holds:

\Phi(t)=th+\circ(t),\quad\text{as}~t\rightarrow 0.

(2.15)

For any shape functional
J
, domain
\Omega
and deformation field
\Phi\in\mathcal{A}
, we define the shape derivative of
J
with respect to
\Phi
on
\Omega
as the following quantity

J^{\prime}(\Phi):=\frac{d}{dt}J((Id+\Phi(t))\Omega)\Big|_{t=0}=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{J((Id+\Phi(t))\Omega)-J(\Omega)}{t}.

Here we give the definition of both shape derivative and material
derivative of a state function. Fix a perturbation

\Phi\in\mathcal{A}
and let
v=v(t,x)\in C^{1}([0,1),C^{1}(\Omega_{t},\mathbb{R}))
. The shape derivative of the state
function is defined as the following partial derivative with respect
to
t
at a fixed point
x\in\Omega
:

v^{\prime}(t_{0},x):=\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}(t_{0},x),\quad\text{for}~~x\in\Omega,~t_{0}\in[0,1).

On the other hand, differentiating along the trajectories gives rise to the material derivative:

\dot{v}(t_{0},x):=\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}(t_{0},x),\quad\text{for}~~x\in\Omega,~t_{0}\in[0,1),

where
w(t,x):=v(t,x+\Phi(t,x))
. It follows from (
2.15
) and the definition of shape derivative and
material derivative that

v^{\prime}=\dot{v}-\nabla v\cdot h.

(2.16)

The concept of shape derivative can be applied to shape functionals that take values
in a general Banach space too. Here we are interested in the case where

v(t,\cdot):=u_{t},

(2.17)

where
u_{t}
is the solution to some boundary value problems defined
on the perturbed domain
\Omega_{t}:=(Id+th)\Omega
.
Since each
u_{t}
lives in a different domain
\Omega_{t}
, it follows from (
2.16
) that the shape
derivative
u^{\prime}
can be defined in an indirect way

u^{\prime}=\dot{u}-\nabla u\cdot h,

(2.18)

which is our main formula in this paper.

2.3
Spherical harmonics

We now recall the spherical harmonics in
[
5
]
. The real
spherical harmonics of degree
k\geq 0
are the eigenfunctions of
the negative spherical Laplace-Beltrami operator

-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}
corresponding to the eigenvalue

\lambda_{k}=k(N+k-2)
, that is,

-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}Y^{i}_{k}=\lambda_{k}Y^{i}_{k}.

Each eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
has multiplicity
2k+1
for each
k\geq 0
. The set of all spherical harmonics
\{Y_{k}^{i}:k\geq 0,|i\leq k|\}
would form a complete orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{N-1})
and they can be normalized by

\|Y^{i}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{N-1})}=1.

Thus, for any
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{N-1})
, we have that

u=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{|i|\leq k}\alpha_{k}^{i}Y_{k}^{i},\quad\alpha_{k}^{i}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}uY_{k}^{i}~d\mathbb{S}^{N-1}.

For each
k\geq 0
we define the linear subspace

\mathbf{Y}_{k}:=\text{span}\{Y_{k}^{i}:|i|\leq k\}

with any
u\in\mathbf{Y}_{k}
being such that
-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}u=\lambda_{k}u
.
Notice that the eigenspace corresponding to
the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}=0
is the one-dimensional space of constant functions on
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
. Moreover, such constant function term does not appear in the expansion of a function
of zero average on
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
.
In particular, when
N=2
, for any
(u,v)\in\widetilde{X}
where the space
\widetilde{X}
is defined in (
2.2
), it follows
[
13
]
that

u(\theta)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\alpha_{k}\cos(k\theta),\quad\alpha_{k}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}u\cos(k\theta)~d\theta

(2.19)

and

v(\lambda\theta)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\beta_{k}\cos(k\theta),\quad\beta_{k}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}v\cos(k\theta)~d\theta

(2.20)

where
\theta=\frac{x}{|x|}
and
\alpha_{k}\neq\beta_{k}
are real coefficients.

3.1
The trivial solution of (

We first describe this trivial branch of solutions explicitly in the

Lemma 3.1
.

For some
\lambda\in(0,1)
and any
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, there exist unique value
Q^{\gamma}
given by

Q^{\gamma}=\left(\frac{4+\left(1-\lambda^{2}\right)\gamma}{4\ln\lambda}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{2}

such that for
\Omega\setminus B_{\lambda}=B_{1}\setminus B_{\lambda}
the problem (
1.2

\psi=\psi^{\gamma}_{tri}
with boundary conditions

\psi=1~~\text{on}~~\partial B_{\lambda},\quad\psi=0~~\text{on}~~\partial B_{1},\quad|\nabla\psi|^{2}=Q^{\gamma}~~\text{on}~~\partial B_{1}.

The solution is radially symmetric and given by

\psi^{\gamma}_{tri}(|x|)=\frac{4+\left(1-\lambda^{2}\right)\gamma}{4\ln\lambda}\ln|x|-\frac{\gamma\left(1-|x|^{2}\right)}{4}.

Proof.

If
\psi=\psi(r)
, where
r=|x|
, is a radially symmetric solution to (

\psi
satisfies the ODE

\psi_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}\psi_{r}=\gamma

where the prime denotes differentiation with respect to

\psi_{r}(r)=\frac{C}{r}+\frac{\gamma}{2}r

By a simple integration and Dirichlet boundary conditions, we obtain

\psi(r)=C\ln r-\frac{\gamma\left(1-r^{2}\right)}{4}

with

C=\frac{4+\left(1-\lambda^{2}\right)\gamma}{4\ln\lambda}.

Thus we obtain the trivial solution depending on
\gamma

\psi(r)=\frac{4+\left(1-\lambda^{2}\right)\gamma}{4\ln\lambda}\ln|x|-\frac{\gamma\left(1-|x|^{2}\right)}{4}:=\psi^{\gamma}_{tri}(|x|).

Taking this trivial solution into the last boundary condition of (
1.2
), we have that

Q=\left(\frac{4+\left(1-\lambda^{2}\right)\gamma}{4\ln\lambda}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)^{2}:=Q^{\gamma}.

The proof is now complete.
∎

The differentiability of the map
