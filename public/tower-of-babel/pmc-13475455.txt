Implementing a RPM-enabled transitional care pathway for adults with poorly controlled diabetes: a quality improvement initiative
Authors: Michelle Elsener, Cigi Mathew, Farrukh N Jafri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdults discharged from the hospital with poorly controlled diabetes face elevated readmission risk, yet health systems lack practical models for integrating remote glucose monitoring into post-discharge care. Existing approaches often exclude patients at the highest risk who have limited English proficiency, adverse social determinants of health or limited smartphone access.Local problemAt a 292-bed community hospital in White Plains New York, patients discharged with haemoglobin A1c ≥8% showed higher rates of hospital utilisation. Local mortality data revealed disparities affecting Black/African American and Hispanic/Latino residents, prompting a programme design with equity as a core objective.MethodsWe integrated remote patient monitoring (RPM) into our nurse-led transitional care programme through five Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles from April 2024- to January 2025. Patients received RPM services and care coordination for 90 days. Enrolment, appointment attendance, A1c completion and demographic representation were evaluated through each cycle.ResultsAmong 442 screened patients, 46 were enrolled (enrolment grew from 10.5% to 39.7%, p<0.001). Enrolled patients closely matched the eligible population across targeted demographic groups. Follow-up appointment attendance reached 78.3% for primary care and 69.6% for endocrinology. A1c testing completion improved to 48% in the final cycle. The most substantial gains in feasibility occurred after redistributing monitoring to a team-based model.ConclusionsIterative PDSA cycles enabled the development of an equitable RPM-enabled transitional care pathway for diabetes, achieving proportional enrolment across all targeted demographic groups. Key implementation insights included workforce capacity as the primary constraint to enrolment, workflow simplification through continuous glucose monitoring standardisation and the importance of pre-arranged ambulatory follow-up. Health systems implementing RPM for transitional care should prioritise adequate monitoring workforce, streamline device protocols and coordinate ambulatory integration from programme inception to enable scalable, equitable implementation.Keywords: Quality improvement, Transitions in care, DIABETES MELLITUS, Nurses, PDSA

Introduction

Diabetes affects more than 37 million adults in the USA and is a leading contributor to preventable morbidity, mortality and healthcare utilisation.1–3 Adults hospitalised with diabetes face elevated risk during the transition from hospital to home.4 Gaps in glucose monitoring and health literacy, difficulties obtaining medications and challenges securing timely follow-up appointments all contribute to unplanned utilisation and readmissions.4–6 Additionally, local and national data highlight persistent equity gaps with Black/African American and Hispanic/Latino adults experiencing markedly higher diabetes mortality and poorer outcomes than their White counterparts.6–11 These disparities are further compounded when hyperglycaemia remains uncontrolled after discharge, increasing the risk of long-term complications without early, structured follow-up.12–14

Current literature supports the effectiveness of transitional care programmes in reducing complications and improving care continuity for chronic conditions.6 13 15–18 However, many transitional care models rely on episodic follow-up and patient self-report, limiting their ability to detect early physiologic deterioration after discharge.19 Remote patient monitoring (RPM) offers a scalable approach to extend transitional care beyond discrete encounters by enabling continuous capture and review of patient-generated physiologic data between visits.5 In diabetes care, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) represents a form of RPM with the potential to identify glycaemic instability early and inform timely intervention. Advances in CGM have enhanced diabetes management by enabling real-time glycaemic feedback and enabling clinicians to identify glycaemic abnormalities to offer patient-centred, clinically relevant guidance.13 16 20 21 Despite these advances, existing studies around the benefits of CGM with RPM occur in outpatient settings and rarely include individuals with technology barriers, limited English proficiency or unmet social needs.22–27 Despite growing adoption, evidence describing how CGM-enabled RPM functions within transitional care workflows and its impact during the post-discharge period remains limited.

Embedding CGM within an RPM-enabled transitional care model would shift its use from individual self-management towards structured, team-based clinical oversight during high-risk periods, necessitating further research on implementation and adoption.22 28–30

Integration, though, introduces substantial operational challenges including the need for hands-on device onboarding, staffing models for daily data review, defined escalation pathways and workflow adaptation to support device logistics, documentation and coordination across care settings.5 6 31 32

While nurse (RN)-led interventions have demonstrated effectiveness in diabetes self-management education and telephone-based follow-up,20 33–35 few models describe how to operationalise RN-coordinated RPM within post-discharge transitional care for diabetic patients. Even fewer address how these approaches can be adapted with purposeful consideration of existing inequities among patients facing technological, language or social barriers, or how nursing workforce capacity affects programme reach and equity.

WPH Cares, an established RN-led transitional care programme at White Plains Hospital has demonstrated improvements in hospital utilisation, access, care coordination and overall patient experience through telehealth-supported outreach.36 Motivated by local disparities and existing operational strengths, the team sought to design and iteratively refine an RPM-enabled transitional care model capable of supporting adults discharged with poorly controlled diabetes, including those with limited technological access or unmet social determinants of health (SDOH) needs. This initiative was designed not to test the efficacy of CGM, but to examine how CGM-enabled RPM could be operationalised within a real-world, RN-led transitional care programme serving high-risk patients.

Specific aims

The primary aim of this quality improvement initiative was to design, implement and iteratively refine an RPM-enabled transitional care pathway for adults with poorly controlled diabetes following hospital discharge and to improve enrolment from a baseline rate of 10% in cycle 1 to ≥30% of eligible patients, while ensuring proportional enrolment across demographic and social risk groups. Across five Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles, the team sought to improve workflow reliability, enhance the feasibility of onboarding CGM or Bluetooth glucometers and establish a consistent process for RN-led RPM for glucose monitoring. Secondary aims were to evaluate key process and implementation measures, including onboarding success (confirmed data transmission during home visit), follow-up appointment attendance (primary care provider (PCP) within 30 days and endocrinology within 30 days), A1c completion at 60–120 days post-discharge (baseline 0% in cycle 1), early graduation and equity of enrolment across race, ethnicity, preferred language, insurance status and SDOH. These process measures are reported in this manuscript. Detailed analysis of clinical outcomes, including glycaemic control, readmissions and utilisation, will be presented separately in a subsequent outcomes-focused manuscript.

Methods

This report adheres to Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence 2.0 guidelines and describes the rationale, implementation strategy, iterative adaptation and process outcomes of an RPM-enabled transitional care pathway.

Context

This quality improvement initiative was conducted at White Plains Hospital, a 292-bed community hospital in Westchester County, New York. The hospital serves a diverse population, including communities with elevated diabetes mortality rates among Black/African American and Hispanic/Latino residents. The WPH Cares programme, a transitional care service, provides structured telephonic outreach for high-risk adults after discharge from the hospital setting; it consists of an interdisciplinary team of RNs, advanced practice providers (APPs), clinical pharmacists, social workers (SW) and access referral navigators who all work together to offer patient-centred, diagnosis-informed care to patients. In addition, WPH Cares partners with community paramedicine services to support the transition from hospital to home, enabling in-person assessments and escalations when needed under the medical direction of the programme. This initiative integrated RPM into existing transitional care workflows for patients with poorly controlled diabetes transitioning from hospital to home.37

Intervention

Post-discharge identification and enrolment

Patients were identified by running a daily report within the electronic health record (EHR) that highlights adults discharged with haemoglobin A1c ≥8% within 90 days of their admission (online supplemental figure 1). Nurses completed a brief chart review for eligibility (age ≥18, in-network ambulatory provider, discharged home) and contacted eligible patients by telephone within 24–48 hours of hospital discharge. Enrolment calls confirmed eligibility, described the programme and administered SDOH screening using the EHR 9-domain assessment tool (housing stability and safety, food insecurity, caregiver strain, financial resources, utilities, legal assistance, violence and transportation). Identified barriers were flagged for SW or access navigator referral.

Home visit and device setup

During enrolment, patients with CGM-compatible smartphones chose between CGM or a Bluetooth glucometer with a cellular hub for data transmission (fingersticks by protocol (FBP)). Those without compatible devices received the FBP option. Because setup could not be completed in the inpatient setting or virtually, community paramedics conducted home visits for device setup, training with teach-back and data transmission verification. Patients received manufacturer instructions and diabetes education materials in their preferred language (English or Spanish). CGM devices were provided through a manufacturer supply programme; ongoing supply required insurance coverage or patient assistance programmes.

Daily remote Patient monitoring

Nurses reviewed glucose readings daily (8:00 AM–12:00 PM, 7 days/week) via a web-based platform. Immediate outreach occurred for glucose <40 mg/dL (hypoglycaemia), >300 mg/dL (hyperglycaemia), no data transmitted for three consecutive days or the patient was unreachable after 1 week despite ≥3 attempts (online supplemental figure 2). Nurses assessed patient safety, provided guidance (eg, consuming fast-acting carbohydrates for hypoglycaemia, evaluating contributing factors for hyperglycaemia) and escalated to programme APPs, clinical pharmacists or outpatient providers when medication changes were needed. All contacts were documented in the EHR using standardised templates.

Clinical escalation and care coordination

WPH Cares APPs managed urgent clinical issues requiring immediate intervention (persistent hyperglycaemia, bridging prescriptions when appointments were delayed, etc). SWs addressed SDOH barriers, particularly transportation and food insecurity. Access navigators assisted with appointment scheduling for both PCP, endocrinology and nutritionist visits. Clinical pharmacists provided medication consultations and recommendations to ambulatory providers for complex cases.

Timely follow-up was a core focus of the programme, with RNs coordinating closely with access navigators to ensure appointment scheduling, provide reminders and address barriers to attendance.

Scheduled Patient outreach

Beyond daily RPM escalation contacts, patients also received scheduled outreaches: weekly for the first 30 days, biweekly for days 31–90. These phone calls consisted of a review of glucose trends, patient-identified goals, diabetes self-management education, medication adherence reinforcement and appointment confirmation.

Programme duration

The programme duration was 90 days from hospital discharge. At completion, ambulatory providers received CGM data summaries documenting glucose trends and care recommendations. Each patient’s established ambulatory provider determined if ongoing CGM coverage was clinically indicated after programme completion.

Study of the intervention

Implementation was evaluated across PDSA cycles from April 2024 to January 2025. Workflow performance, onboarding reliability, engagement patterns and operational barriers were reviewed during multidisciplinary meetings between cycles (online supplemental material 1). Documentation audits and EHR review assessed fidelity to intervention components. Qualitative feedback from RNs, community paramedics, APPs and SWs informed the contextual factors affecting implementation. These insights, combined with cycle-level trends in enrolment, onboarding success and early graduation, guided refinements to scripting, outreach timing, language support, device education and follow-up coordination.

Measures

The team used an Ishikawa diagram to identify root causes of failure to improve glycaemic control across six domains: patient and caregiver factors, technology, equity and SDOH, inpatient discharge processes, nursing and interdisciplinary team factors, and follow-up coordination (figure 1a). A driver diagram was then developed to link the primary aim to key drivers and specific change concepts (figure 1b). These diagnostic tools informed the selection of process measures that included enrolment rate among eligible patients (calculated per PDSA cycle), onboarding success (confirmed data transmission during home visit), follow-up appointment attendance (PCP within 30 days, endocrinology within 30 days, nutrition within 90 days), follow-up A1c completion (lab drawn 60–120 days post-discharge, reflecting reliance on outpatient providers and real-world scheduling constraints) and early disenrolment with documented reasons.

Additionally, we reviewed equity in enrolment which included race, ethnicity, preferred language, insurance and SDOH burden compared with the eligible population. Barriers to enrolment among eligible patients were documented, including cohort capacity constraints, inability to contact, patient declined, technology barriers and operational issues.

All measures were obtained through routine transitional care documentation and EHR review.

Analysis

Run charts displayed trends in key process measures across PDSA cycles. Given the quality improvement focus and small sample sizes per cycle, analyses were exploratory and descriptive rather than inferential. Enrolment rates, onboarding success, appointment attendance, A1c completion and early disenrolment were calculated per PDSA cycle and overall.

Descriptive statistics summarised patient characteristics and implementation outcomes (online supplemental table 1a). Standardised mean differences (SMD) compared demographic and clinical characteristics between enrolled and non-enrolled patients, with SMD <0.10 indicating a negligible difference. To assess enrolment equity, we compared the distribution of race, ethnicity, language and insurance status among enrolled patients with those of the eligible population using SMD and χ2 tests.

All analyses were conducted using R V.4.3.1 (online supplemental material 2).

Patient and public involvement

Patients were involved in the conduct and iterative refinement of this quality improvement initiative through informal feedback provided during enrolment, device onboarding and follow-up outreach. Nurses and community paramedics solicited patient perspectives regarding device usability, clarity of education materials, language needs, scheduling preferences and perceived barriers to participation. This feedback informed PDSA refinements to outreach scripting, educational approaches, follow-up cadence and workflow logistics. Patients did not participate in the initial study design, data analysis, manuscript preparation or dissemination planning, and no formal patient advisory group was established.

Results

Across five PDSA cycles (April 2024-January 2025), the team iteratively refined the CGM-enabled transitional care pathways in four key areas: (1) reliable patient identification and enrolment, (2) patient engagement in glycaemic management, (3) ambulatory integration and (4) access to technology and supportive resources. Reliable patient identification and enrolment

Of 442 patients screened across five PDSA cycles, 218 (49.3%) met eligibility criteria. Of those eligible, 46 (21.1%) enrolled in the CGM-enabled transitional care pathway (figure 2) with the final cycle achieving 39.7% enrolment rate (online supplemental figure 3a). The most common barriers were cohort capacity constraints (n=78), reflecting a deliberate approach to starting small and scaling up during the first three cycles. Other barriers included inability to contact patient to enrol (n=52), and patients that declined participation (n=24).

Flow diagram depicting patient identification, eligibility assessment, enrolment and participation in the RPM-enabled transitional care pathway across five PDSA cycles. Adults discharged from the hospital with haemoglobin A1c ≥8% were screened for eligibility, contacted for enrolment and assigned to the 90-day RPM intervention. Reasons for non-enrolment, including cohort capacity constraints, inability to contact, patient declination and operational barriers, are shown.

Of the 46 enrolled patients, 41 (89.1%) used Dexcom G7 CGM devices, while 5 (10.9%) used FBP devices (online supplemental figure 3b). FBP was discontinued after cycle 3 due to unreliable Bluetooth connectivity and insufficient reading frequency preventing clinically relevant monitoring. Community paramedics successfully confirmed data transmission during home visits in all completed setups, though scheduling coordination often extended the target 72-hour window.

Patient engagement in glycaemic management

All 46 enrolled patients were assigned a 90-day programme duration. Days with active glucose monitoring data ranged from 7 to 90 days (mean 47.5, median 51.0), reflecting gaps due to device supply issues, technological barriers and patient engagement. Patients who used FBP devices remained engaged for an average of 32 days, while those using CGM devices remained engaged for an average of 49 days (p=0.27). Over the 90-day period, patients had an average of five contacts with WPH Cares RNs, four with APPs and fewer than one with SWs. Patients with identified SDOH needs received more SW engagement due to embedded escalation and referral workflows. Device supply interruptions due to insurance authorisation delays or lack of coverage were the primary reason for disenrolment among uninsured patients.

Ambulatory integration

Among enrolled patients, follow-up care utilisation within 30 days of hospital discharge was substantially higher compared with non-enrolled patients. Specifically, 78.3% of enrolled patients (36/46) attended at least one PCP appointment, compared with 40.7% of non-enrolled patients (161/396) (OR 5.26, CI 2.50 to 10.89, p<0.001). Similarly, 69.6% of enrolled patients (32/46) attended an endocrinology appointment versus 29.8% of non-enrolled patients (118/396), representing increased likelihood of endocrinology follow-up (OR 5.41, CI 2.82 to 10.40, p<0.001). Nutrition visit attendance was also higher among enrolled patients, with 23.9% (11/46) completing a nutrition appointment compared with 4.0% of non-enrolled patients (16/396) (OR 7.50, CI 3.16 to 17.78, p<0.001). 33 patients (71.7%) completed at least one visit with either their PCP or an endocrinologist within 30 days of hospital discharge. Enrolled patients were significantly more likely to complete a follow-up appointment within 30 days compared with eligible patients who were not enrolled (71.7% vs 48.3%; χ² p=0.008, OR 2.72, 95% CI 1.30 to 5.70). Appointment scheduling challenges decreased across cycles following the extension of the target appointment window from 14 to 30 days in cycle 3. Trends in outpatient follow-up attendance across PDSA cycles are shown in online supplemental figure 3c.

Follow-up A1c testing at 60–120 days post-discharge was completed for 21 of 46 patients (45.7%). A1c completion improved across PDSA cycles from 0% (cycle 1, n=2) and 20% (cycle 2, n=5) to 66.7% (cycle 3, n=3), 55.6% (cycle 4, n=9) and 48.1% (cycle 5, n=27), following implementation of day-60 escalation protocols in cycle 4 (online supplemental figure 3d).

Access to technology and supportive resources

Enrolled patients were proportionally representative of eligible patients across all demographic groups (online supplemental table 1b). Among enrolled patients, 30.4% identified as Black/African American (compared with 26.6% of eligible patients, SMD=0.08), 37.0% identified as Hispanic/Latino (vs 36.2% eligible, SMD=0.02), 30.4% preferred Spanish language (vs 24.8% eligible, SMD=0.13) and 21.7% were uninsured or had emergency Medicaid (vs 16.5% eligible, SMD=0.13). All SMDs were <0.20, indicating balanced enrolment across demographic groups. χ2 tests confirmed no significant differences in enrolment rates across race (p=0.73), ethnicity (p=0.96), language (p=0.34) or insurance status (p=0.52), supporting proportional enrolment.

During enrolment screening, 17/46 patients (37%) reported at least one SDOH concern. Transportation barriers affected 7 patients (15.2%), food insecurity affected 10 patients (21.7%), housing instability affected 4 patients (8.7%) and financial resource strain affected 3 patients (6.5%). SWs connected with all patients who identified barriers, helping them access community resources, including food assistance programmes and transportation services when available. Despite SW support, patients with SDOH barriers had significantly shorter engagement (mean 37 days vs 53 days for patients without barriers; p=0.049), suggesting that SDOH concerns may have limited their ability to sustain participation, even with SW support.

Safety and adverse events

No serious adverse events related to CGM use were reported. Nurses monitored for device-related concerns, including skin irritation, sensor failure and connectivity issues. Among enrolled patients, 6 (13.0%) were readmitted within 30 days of discharge, and 11 (23.9%) returned to the emergency department (ED) within 30 days, though causality assessment was beyond the scope of this implementation study.

Discussion

This quality improvement initiative achieved three implementation successes: enrolment rates improved nearly fourfold (from 10.5% in cycle 1 to 39.7% in cycle 5); equity was maintained across racial, ethnic, linguistic and socioeconomic groups; and follow-up appointment attendance (78.3% PCP, 69.6% endocrinology), which exceeded typical post-discharge rates. These outcomes emerged from systematic evolution towards team-based monitoring and proactive equity design.

Key implementation insights from Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles

The increase in enrolment from 10.5% to 39.7% demonstrated that programme capacity rather than patient interest or technology access was the primary limiting factor (online supplemental figure 3a). Early cycles were intentionally designed to start small; however, this constrained initial enrolment due to cohort capacity limits. Because the intervention was designed as a 90-day programme and early PDSA cycles occurred approximately 30 days apart, the full downstream effects of initial cohorts could not be assessed until later cycles. Enrolment and monitoring capacity improved as daily RPM responsibilities were distributed across the care team, enabling higher throughput while maintaining clinical oversight. These iterations underscored that sustained RPM delivery requires either a dedicated role or an explicitly team-based model; a single individual cannot feasibly manage enrolment, daily monitoring, care coordination and longitudinal outreach at scale when layered onto existing operational responsibilities.

Technology choices also proved critical. We added FBP, intending to reduce digital barriers for patients without smartphones. Instead, FBP created problems including Bluetooth connectivity failures, delayed data transmission, patient challenges with using the technology and insufficient reading frequency (one to two times daily vs continuous data). FBP patients remained in the programme shorter (mean 32 days vs 49 days for CGM) and were less engaged. After three cycles of troubleshooting and obtaining feedback from patients and staff, FBP was discontinued. Enrolment increased in later cycles, suggesting that focusing resources on one reliable technology outperformed offering a suboptimal alternative.

Appointment scheduling required realistic target-setting. Initial 14-day goals for PCP and endocrinology proved unattainable due to scheduling backlogs and insurance authorisation timelines. Extending to 30 days (cycle 3) aligned expectations with system capacity, improving attendance without compromising clinical appropriateness. Quality improvement must work within real-world constraints rather than aspirational timelines.

Achieving equitable enrolment

Proportional enrolment across demographic groups contrasts with typical digital health implementation, which often exacerbates disparities. Several design choices contributed. First, SDOH screening at enrolment enabled proactive identification of barriers and immediate SW referrals for 37% of patients, addressing transportation, food insecurity, housing and financial concerns before they derailed engagement. Second, alternate-language materials and interpreter services were embedded from the start as part of the standard of care to ensure patients received information in their preferred language. Third, flexible scheduling for home visits and phone calls respected patients’ personal schedules. Fourth, real-time equity metric monitoring kept demographic representation visible to the team, prompting adjustments when Black/African American enrolment initially lagged. This ‘inclusion by design’ approach differs from treating equity as an afterthought.

Comparison to current literature

This model builds on established frameworks in transitional care, RPM and CGM by integrating real-time glucose data into RN-led, diagnosis-informed care coordination. Although the observed improvements cannot be attributed to CGM alone or comprehensive care coordination, embedding technology within structured transitional care appears to support effective post-discharge management rather than deploying technology as a standalone intervention.

Implementation barriers

Some challenges persisted. Securing ongoing CGM supply for uninsured patients remained difficult beyond the manufacturer-provided starter period, requiring insurance coverage or patient assistance programmes. Follow-up A1c completion improved from 0% in cycle 1 to 48% in cycle 5 through proactive day-60 escalation, but 48% remains suboptimal. 30% of eligible patients never answered calls despite multiple attempts, a persistent barrier for any telephonic outreach programme. Transportation limitations affected 15% of patients; we connected them with SWs but could not provide transportation vouchers ourselves. These challenges require system-level or policy solutions beyond hospital-based quality improvement.

Limitations

This initiative has several important limitations.

First, the sample size was small (46 enrolled patients), limiting our ability to detect meaningful patterns in some subgroups and restricting statistical precision.

Second, this was a single-centre study in a safety-net hospital with a specific population profile; generalisability to other settings, particularly non-safety-net hospitals, rural areas or regions with different demographic compositions, requires evaluation.

Third, the non-randomised design and lack of a concurrent control group mean we cannot definitively attribute outcomes to the intervention alone, rather than to concurrent quality improvement activities or secular trends in diabetes care.

Fourth, selection bias is likely present, as patients enrolled were those who answered our calls, were available for home visits and agreed to participate. Enrolled patients had slightly higher A1c values and were more likely to screen positive for SDOH barriers than those not enrolled, suggesting they may have represented a higher-risk or more motivated subgroup.

Fifth, follow-up A1c completion was only 46%, limiting our ability to assess glycaemic impact even descriptively. While this improved in later cycles, it remained suboptimal and reflects real-world challenges in obtaining laboratory data from post-discharge patients that future programmes will need to address.

Sixth, we did not systematically track intervention fidelity metrics beyond onboarding success and completion of scheduled calls. Variability in how RNs responded to glucose alerts, the depth of diabetes education provided or adherence to escalation protocols may have influenced outcomes, but was not quantified.

Seventh, the programme relied on manufacturer-provided CGM devices through a hospital discharge programme; sustainability and scalability beyond this supply arrangement are uncertain, particularly for uninsured patients.

Finally, this implementation study was not designed to evaluate clinical effectiveness. While we observed readmission and ED return rates descriptively (13% and 24% at 30 days, respectively), without a control group, we cannot determine whether these rates would have been lower without the intervention. An outcomes analysis using a propensity score-matched cohort will be reported in another paper, and a forthcoming randomised controlled trial will further evaluate questions of clinical effectiveness that this implementation study could not answer.

Conclusions

Iterative PDSA refinement of an RPM-enabled transitional care pathway achieved substantial implementation success: enrolment rates improved nearly fourfold; proportional representation was maintained across racial, ethnic, linguistic and socioeconomic groups; and follow-up appointment attendance exceeded typical post-discharge rates. Three implementation insights proved critical. First, distributing daily monitoring responsibilities across a nursing team rather than a single coordinator removed the primary constraint on enrolment capacity and enabled scalability. Second, discontinuing fingerstick glucometers paradoxically improved implementation by enabling focus on CGM deployment, which offered more consistent data flow and streamlined training. Third, aligning appointment targets with real-world scheduling capacity (30-day windows rather than aspirational 14-day targets) dramatically improved attendance without compromising clinical appropriateness.

For hospitals implementing similar programmes, we recommend embedding SDOH screening at enrolment to enable proactive barrier mitigation; establish team-based monitoring from the start rather than scaling from single-provider models; set appointment and completion targets based on system capacity, not guidelines alone; and monitor equity metrics continuously with explicit enrolment goals. Persistent challenges remain that require system-level or policy solutions beyond hospital-based quality improvement. For example, it will be important to establish long-term CGM supply for uninsured patients, ensure completion of follow-up laboratory tests, and manage cohort capacity constraints during scale-up.

This initiative provides a practical, replicable framework for hospitals seeking to integrate RPM and CGM into transitional care workflows while maintaining equitable access. A randomised controlled trial is warranted to evaluate clinical effectiveness and determine whether this feasible, equity-enhancing approach leads to meaningful improvements in outcomes, including acute care utilisation, primary care follow-up and glycaemic control.
