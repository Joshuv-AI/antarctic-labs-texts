Comparison of the effects of four commercially available prescription diet regimens on the fecal microbiome in healthy cats
Authors: Takumi KOMIYA, Kaoru KOYAMA, Ran AKIYAMA, Hitomi ODA, Kureha GOKITA, Toshinori SAKO, Akihiro MORI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiet significantly influences the gut microbiota, with variations in macronutrient content (proteins, fats, and carbohydrates) and the presence of non-digestible carbohydrates, such as fiber, playing crucial roles in shaping microbial composition. This study investigated the effects of four commercial prescription diets (low-carbohydrate, weight-loss, high soluble fiber, and renal) on the fecal microbiome of five healthy domestic cats using a 4 × 4 Latin square design. Fecal samples were analyzed using Illumina sequencing of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene to assess bacterial community structure and diversity. Results showed that cats fed the renal diet (low protein, high carbohydrate, high fat) were associated with higher relative abundances of the class Actinobacteria, particularly Bifidobacterium. The low-carbohydrate diet was associated with higher relative abundances of Adlercreutzia, Collinsella, and Slackia. The high-soluble fiber diet was associated with a higher relative proportion of Streptococcaceae. In contrast, the renal diet was associated with lower relative abundances of Blautia and Erysipelotrichales. No significant differences were observed in alpha-diversity indices among diets, whereas beta-diversity analysis showed significant differences in microbial community composition among dietary groups. These findings suggest that dietary composition may influence the fecal microbiome in healthy cats. However, given the exploratory design and compositional nature of the sequencing data, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating.Keywords: feces, feline, prescription diet

INTRODUCTION

Diet is a critical factor influencing the gut microbiota. Specifically, the quantity and quality of the three major nutrients (proteins, fats, and carbohydrates) have been shown to significantly impact the gut microbiota in various studies involving humans, dogs, and cats [5, 18, 25, 27, 35, 44]. Non-digestible carbohydrates, such as fiber, are fermented by colonic bacteria, which alters the composition of the gut microbiota and leads to the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including acetate, propionate, and butyrate. These SCFAs are vital for maintaining the host’s gut health [31, 41, 45].

In our previous study, we fed four types of therapeutic prescription diets to healthy dogs and revealed diverse changes in the gut microbiota depending on the diet, thereby elucidating the effects of therapeutic diets on the gut microbiota [26]. Commercial prescription diets are widely used to manage various medical conditions in pet cats, with their composition (including carbohydrate, protein, fat, vitamin, and mineral content) tailored to address specific conditions. However, despite their prevalence, the effects of these diets on the intestinal microbiota of cats remain poorly understood. This study aims to assess the impact of four distinct commercial prescription diets, each characterized by unique nutritional profiles, on the fecal microbiomes of healthy cats.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Five healthy domestic cats aged 1–3 years were maintained in our laboratory for this study. The group included one castrated male, two intact males, and two females, with body weights ranging from 2.9–5.8 kg. All cats had a body condition score (BCS) of 3 on a five-point scale (1: thin, 2: lean, 3: optimal, 4: overweight, 5: obese). Prior to the experiment, all cats were fed a commercial diet (WU or HF, Blue Buffalo Japan, K.K., Tokyo, Japan) twice daily (8 am and 6 pm) with caloric intake set at 1.0–1.4 × RER (BW0.75 × 70) to maintain ideal body weight, where RER is the resting energy requirement and BW is body weight. Although baseline microbiota may have been influenced by the prior diet, all cats were maintained under identical feeding conditions before experimental allocation. This potential baseline influence is acknowledged as a limitation. The coefficient (1.0–1.4) was adjusted according to individual metabolic variation and activity levels to maintain stable body weight throughout the study period. Complete blood counts and comprehensive serum biochemistry analyses were performed, including glucose, total protein, albumin, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, lipase activity, triglycerides, total cholesterol, and electrolytes (Na, K, Cl). Urinalysis was also conducted in all cats. All laboratory values were within normal reference ranges. In addition, fecal examinations and imaging diagnostics were performed to exclude underlying diseases. Based on these comprehensive assessments, all cats were confirmed to be clinically healthy prior to enrollment. The study protocol was approved by the Animal Research Committee of Nippon Veterinary and Life Science University (approval number: 30S-27). The care and use of experimental animals complied with local animal welfare laws, guidelines, and policies.

Diets

The prescription diets compared in this study were as follows: low-carbohydrate diet (diabetic; high protein, medium fat, low carbohydrate, and medium fiber) weight-loss diet (satiety-support; high protein, low fat, low carbohydrate, and high fiber), high soluble fiber diet (Fibre response; medium protein, medium fat, medium carbohydrate, and medium fiber), renal diet (Renal; low protein, high fat, high carbohydrate, and medium fiber). All diets were from Royal Canin, Japon. Each diet has a particular aim suggested by the manufacturer. The chemical composition of each diet is summarized in Table 1.

Each prescription diet was fed twice daily (9 am and 5 pm). Caloric intake was adjusted individually according to body weight and condition to maintain ideal body weight throughout the study period.

Experimental design

Each of the four diets was offered to the five healthy cats for 21 days in a replicated 4 × 4 Latin square design. Two cats followed one diet sequence, and the remaining three cats followed the reverse sequence, ensuring that each cat received all four diets across four experimental periods while helping to minimize potential period effects. Following a 19-day adaptation period, one fresh fecal sample was collected within 15 min of defecation on either day 19–21 of each period. Samples were collected within 15 min of defecation to minimize environmental exposure and oxidative changes while ensuring practical feasibility in a laboratory setting [6]. The feces were stored at −80°C until further analysis. Fecal samples were sent to Anicom Advanced Medical Research Institute, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan) for gut microbiota analysis.

Fecal DNA extraction

Frozen fecal samples were thawed prior to DNA extraction. For each sample, 810 µL of a pre-prepared mixture of lysis buffer and Proteinase K was added to 200 µL of fecal material. Mechanical disruption was performed using a bead-beating method at 6,000 rpm for 20 sec, followed by a 30-sec interval and an additional 20-sec disruption.

The samples were then incubated at 70°C for 10 min and subsequently heated at 95°C for 5 min to terminate enzymatic reactions. The resulting lysates were transferred to a 96-well Deep Well Plate and subjected to automated DNA purification using the CMG-1076 kit (chemagic DNA Stool Kit, PerkinElmer Japan G.K., Yokohama, Japan) and the chemagic 360 (PerkinElmer Japan G.K.) automated nucleic acid extraction system, following the manufacturer’s dedicated protocol (chemagic DNA Stool_CMG-1076). During the elution step, elution buffer was added and DNA was finally recovered in a volume of 100 µL.

PCR amplification and library preparation

Amplification of bacterial DNA targeting the 16S rRNA gene was performed according to our in-house standard operating procedure (SOP, version 2.9.2).

The primers used for amplicon PCR were as follows: Illumina_16S_341F 5′-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGA CAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′ Illumina_16S_805R 5′-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGGACTACH VGGGTWTCTAAT-3′ Prior to amplicon PCR, genomic DNA from each sample was normalized to a concentration of 1 ng/µL. The first PCR (1st PCR; total reaction volume: 25 µL) was prepared by mixing 12.5 µL of 2× KAPA amplification kit (F. Hoffmann-La Roche, Ltd., Basel, Switzerland), 9.9 µL of PCR-grade water, 0.05 µL of forward amplicon primer, 0.05 µL of reverse amplicon primer, and 2.5 µL of template DNA (1 ng/µL). PCR amplification was performed with an initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles consisting of denaturation at 95°C for 30 sec, annealing at 55°C for 30 sec, and extension at 72°C for 30 sec. A final extension was carried out at 72°C for 5 min. The PCR products were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) and subsequently quantified. The purified amplicon PCR products were then normalized to 10 ng/µL. The second PCR (2nd PCR; index PCR, total reaction volume: 25 µL) was prepared by mixing 12.5 µL of 2× KAPA amplification kit, 5.0 µL of PCR-grade water, 2.5 µL of forward index primer, 2.5 µL of reverse index primer, and 2.5 µL of template DNA (10 ng/µL). PCR was performed with an initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 12 cycles consisting of denaturation at 95°C for 30 sec, annealing at 55°C for 30 sec, and extension at 72°C for 30 sec, with a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. The index PCR products were purified using AMPure XP beads and quantified. Libraries were adjusted to a final concentration of 9 pM. Subsequently, 850 µL of the prepared library was mixed with 150 µL of PhiX control at the same concentration and subjected to sequencing using the Illumina MiSeq platform with the MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 (600-cycle) (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Sequencing analysis

The sequence data were processed using QIIME 2 version 2018.8.0. The DADA2 software package version 2018.8.0 incorporated into QIIME 2 was used to correct the amplicon sequence errors and yield high quality reads. To rarefy the data, we used 1,000 high quality reads from each sample. Unique ASVs of the 16S rRNA gene and its abundances were summarized in an ASV abundance table, and the alpha and beta diversities were calculated using QIIME2. Microbial taxonomy was assigned using a Naïve Bayes classifier trained on the greengenes 13_8 database.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as medians (minimum–maximum range) for relative abundances and as means ± standard deviation where appropriate. Taxonomic distributions at various levels (phylum, class, order, family, and genus) were compared across diet periods using the Kruskal–Wallis test (GraphPad Prism 6, GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) as an exploratory non-parametric approach. Because amplicon-based 16S rRNA gene sequencing generates compositional data expressed as relative abundances, results from these exploratory analyses should be interpreted with caution. Alpha diversity indices, including Shannon index, Observed OTUs (Operational Taxonomic Units), Pielou’s evenness, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (Faith_PD), were compared among dietary groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Beta diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis distance matrices generated from the microbial abundance table. Differences in overall microbial community composition among dietary groups were evaluated using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA; 999 permutations) and analysis of similarities (ANOSIM; 999 permutations) implemented in the vegan package in R (version 4.5.2). Individual cat identity was included in the PERMANOVA model to account for the repeated-measures study design. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and hierarchical clustering were performed using QIIME2 for visualization of microbial community structure.

* Indicates significant difference (P<0.05, Kruskal-Wallis test).

The phylum Actinobacteria was higher in relative abundance in cats fed the low-carbohydrate and renal diets compared to the other diets (exploratory Kruskal–Wallis P<0.05). Within Actinobacteria, the order Bifidobacteriales, family Bifidobacteriaceae, and genus Bifidobacterium were higher in relative abundance in the renal diet group compared to the other groups (P<0.05). The genera Adlercreutzia, Collinsella, and Slackia, belonging to the family Coriobacteriaceae, were elevated in the low-carbohydrate diet group compared to other diets (P<0.05).

No significant differences were observed in the phylum Bacteroidetes and its downstream taxa across the four diets.

Within the phylum Firmicutes, the proportion of Bacilli-Lactobacillales showed lower relative abundance (exploratory Kruskal–Wallis P<0.05) in the weight-loss diet group compared to the other three groups. In contrast, the proportion of the family Streptococcaceae showed higher relative abundance (exploratory Kruskal–Wallis P<0.05) in the high soluble fiber diet group compared to the other groups. The genus Blautia, belonging to the family Lachnospiraceae, showed lower relative abundance in the renal diet group compared to the other groups (P<0.05). Additionally, the proportion of Peptococcaceae-Peptococcus was increased in the low-carbohydrate diet group compared to the other groups, while the proportion of Erysipelotrichi-Erysipelotrichales showed lower relative abundance (exploratory Kruskal–Wallis P<0.05) in the renal diet group compared to the other groups.

No significant differences were found in the phylum Fusobacteria and its downstream taxa across the four diets.

Within the phylum Proteobacteria, the proportion of Betaproteobacteria-Burkholderiales-Alcaligenaceae-Sutterella showed higher relative abundance (exploratory Kruskal–Wallis P<0.05) in the weight-loss diet group compared to the other groups.

No significant differences were observed in alpha-diversity indices among the four dietary groups. The Shannon index (P=0.088), observed OTUs (P=0.074), Pielou’s evenness (P=0.234), and Faith_PD index (P=0.273) did not differ significantly among diets (Kruskal–Wallis test; Table 3).

DISCUSSION

This exploratory study suggests that different commercial prescription diets may be associated with variation in the fecal microbiome of healthy cats. In this study, members of the phylum Actinobacteria, particularly Bifidobacterium, showed higher relative abundance in cats fed the renal diet (low protein, high carbohydrate and high fat). Previous studies have demonstrated that high-carbohydrate diets promote the growth of Bifidobacterium, as carbohydrate fermentation by gut bacteria supports its proliferation [31]. In contrast, diets high in protein are often associated with a reduced proportion of Bifidobacterium [36]. Thus, the higher levels of Bifidobacterium observed in cats on the renal diet may result from its high carbohydrate and low protein content.

In the group fed a low-carbohydrate diet, there was an increase in the genera Adlercreutzia, Collinsella, and Slackia, all belonging to the family Coriobacteriaceae. Adlercreutzia and Slackia are known as equol-producing bacteria [33], and equol (a metabolite produced by gut flora) has been associated with various health benefits, particularly for women’s health [13, 16]. However, the specific effects of these bacteria in cats are not well understood and warrant further investigation. Additionally, Collinsella is thought to influence cholesterol and lipid metabolism, and its association with type 2 diabetes and rheumatoid arthritis has also been reported in humans [10]. There are studies reporting a decrease in Collinsella abundance with high-protein or high-fiber diets, which contradicts the findings of this study [4, 17], as the low-carbohydrate diet used in this study is high in protein and moderate in fiber. Meanwhile, studies have linked the presence of Collinsella and Slackia to improved fecal scores in cats with spontaneous chronic diarrhea, suggesting potential benefits of these genera for gut health in cats [30]. However, given the compositional nature of relative abundance data, the observed increase may also reflect proportional shifts rather than absolute expansion [9]. Therefore, the biological relevance of these observations in healthy cats remains unclear, and the present findings should be considered exploratory.

No statistically significant differences were detected in the phyla Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes among the four diets. However, the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio numerically varied across diets: 1.270 for the low-carbohydrate diet, 0.638 for the weight-loss diet, 1.315 for the high-soluble fiber diet, and 1.658 for the renal diet (exploratory Kruskal–Wallis P=0.0414; data were not shown in results). An increased F/B ratio has been reported in genetically obese mice (ob/ob) and obese humans [19, 20, 40]. Conversely, plant-rich, high-fiber diets have been shown to induce a lower F/B ratio in human children [7]. Thus, the low F/B ratio observed for the weight-loss diet in this study may be related to its high fiber content.

The proportion of the family Streptococcaceae belonging to the Lactobacillales-Bacilli-Firmicutes showed higher relative abundance (exploratory Kruskal–Wallis P<0.05) in the high soluble fiber diet group compared to other groups (P<0.05; Kruskal-Wallis test). Previous research has shown that Streptococcus abundance is elevated in dogs with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [24], and quantitative analysis of Streptococcus is included in the dysbiosis index assessment [1]. Similarly, changes in Streptococcus levels have been observed in cats with IBD compared to healthy cats, suggesting a potential role in the pathogenesis of IBD in felines [14]. Furthermore, Streptococcus is included in the feline fecal dysbiosis index, indicating its relevance in dysbiosis-associated microbial shifts in cats [38]. Given that the high-soluble fiber diet is often used for managing constipation, the observed increase in fecal streptococcus warrants further investigation.

Blautia (genus) belonging to the Lachnospiraceae-Clostridiales-Clostridia-Firmicutes was decreased in the renal diet group. Previous studies have shown that Blautia is influenced by dietary protein-to-carbohydrate ratios. Blautia increased in kittens fed a high-protein, low-carbohydrate diet, which is consistent with our research findings [12]. Blautia is involved in glucose metabolism and produces SCFAs like acetic and butyric acids, which are crucial for maintaining gut health [22, 23]. These SCFAs enhance intestinal barrier function and have anti-inflammatory effects by inducing regulatory T cells [2].

Clostridium hiranonis is an important bacterial species incorporated into the feline dysbiosis index [38]. In the present study, the relative abundance of reads classified as C. hiranonis based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing differed among groups (medians, min–max): low-carbohydrate diet (1.05, 0.52–3.33), weight-loss diet (0.86, 0.31–3.40), high soluble fiber diet (1.15, 0.10–1.52), and renal diet (0.00, 0.00–0.05), although these data are not shown in the Results section. However, species-level resolution using the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene is limited, and accurate identification and quantification of C. hiranonis remain constrained. This bacterium is known to play a critical role in bile acid metabolism; nevertheless, the findings of this study are based on 16S-derived taxonomic assignment and may therefore be subject to underestimation or misclassification. Further validation using species-specific quantitative PCR or shotgun metagenomic approaches is warranted.

The proportion of Erysipelotrichales (order) within Erysipelotrichi (class) belonging to the Firmicutes was reduced in the renal diet group. The proportion of Erysipelotrichaceae has been positively correlated with protein digestibility in dogs [3]. The renal diet used in this study contained only 23% protein (Table 1), which is lower than in the other diets, and featured highly digestible protein content. Enhanced protein digestibility may reduce the risk of food hypersensitivity [28, 42], potentially contributing to the observed reduction in Erysipelotrichales in the renal diet group.

Sutterella, an anaerobic or microaerophilic gram-negative bacterium, is thought to regulate inflammatory responses in the gut [15]. It has been demonstrated that the number of Sutterella spp. is increased in gastrointestinal biopsies obtained from children with autism spectrum disorder [43]. Meanwhile, Sutterella is less abundant in the microbiomes of dogs with inflammatory bowel disease [37] or steroid-responsive enteropathy [29] compared to healthy controls. Therefore, the present finding should not be interpreted as evidence of a beneficial or detrimental effect of this taxon.

An important methodological limitation of this study is that amplicon-based 16S rRNA gene sequencing generates compositional data. Because taxa are expressed as relative abundances and are not statistically independent, standard non-parametric tests do not inherently account for compositional constraints [9]. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as exploratory. The small sample size (n=5) and the inclusion of healthy cats only further limit generalizability. The absence of diseased or antibiotic-treated comparison groups restricts contextual interpretation of microbiome variation. In addition, inter-individual variability may have influenced the observed relative abundance shifts. Next-generation sequencing also lacks complete analytical standardization across laboratories; differences in DNA extraction methods, primer sets, sequencing platforms, and bioinformatic pipelines may contribute to variability between studies and limit direct comparability [8, 11, 32]. Furthermore, analytical validation and cross-platform reproducibility remain incomplete, which constrains its immediate translational applicability. Therefore, integration of sequencing results with metabolomic profiling and validated quantitative assays will be necessary to strengthen biological interpretation [21, 34, 39]. Finally, baseline fecal microbiota may have been influenced by the pre-study diet (Blue Buffalo WU or HF). Although all cats were maintained under identical conditions before experimental allocation, this potential carryover effect should be considered a limitation.

In conclusion, the four commercially available prescription diets were associated with differing fecal microbial profiles in healthy cats. However, because of the exploratory design, very small sample size, repeated-measures structure, and compositional limitations of 16S relative abundance data, these observations should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis-generating.
