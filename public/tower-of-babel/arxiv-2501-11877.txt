From Drafts to Answers: Unlocking LLM Potential via Aggregation Fine-Tuning
Yafu Li, Zhilin Wang, Tingchen Fu, Ganqu Cui, Sen Yang, Yu Cheng

Abstract
Scaling data and model size has been proven effective for boosting the performance of large language models. In addition to training-time scaling, recent studies have revealed that increasing test-time computational resources can further improve performance. In this work, we introduce Aggregation Fine-Tuning (AFT), a supervised fine-tuning paradigm where the model learns to synthesize multiple draft responses, referred to as proposals , into a single, refined answer, termed aggregation . At inference time, a propose-and-aggregate strategy further boosts performance by iteratively generating proposals and aggregating them. Empirical evaluations on benchmark datasets show that AFT-trained models substantially outperform standard SFT. Notably, an AFT model, fine-tuned from Llama3.1-8B-Base with only 64k data , achieves a 41.3% LC win rate on AlpacaEval 2, surpassing significantly larger LLMs such as Llama3.1-405B-Instruct and GPT-4. By combining sequential refinement and parallel sampling, the propose-and-aggregate framework scales inference-time computation in a flexible manner. Overall, These findings position AFT as a promising approach to unlocking additional capabilities of LLMs without resorting to increasing data volume or model size. Our code is publicly available at https://github.com/Linzwcs/AFT .

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs)
(
Dubey et al. 2024
;
OpenAI 2023
)
have demonstrated remarkable success in applications ranging from coding
(
Li et al. 2023a
;
Roziere et al. 2023
)
and reasoning
(
Yuan et al. 2023
;
Qin et al. 2024a
)
to AI assistance
(
Chiang et al. 2023
)
, driven by the expansion of both data and model capacity during pre-training
(
Hoffmann et al. 2022
;
Kaplan et al. 2020
)
and supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
(
Yuan et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
.
Beyond training-time scaling, recent studies
(
Snell et al. 2024a
;
Zhang et al. 2024c
;
Wang et al. 2024a
)
indicate that allocating additional compute at inference can further boost performance, enabling smaller models to rival larger ones.
Correspondingly, a variety of inference-time scaling algorithms
(
Madaan et al. 2023
;
Brown et al. 2024
;
Świechowski et al. 2023
)
have emerged, including sequentially revising generations or selecting among parallel samples.

Figure 1
:

Aggregation Fine-Tuning: Learning to generate a refined response by synthesizing multiple drafts alongside the query, different from the direct query-response mapping of traditional supervised fine-tuning.

Despite the growing complexity of inference algorithms, typical SFT pipelines still learn a direct mapping from a user query to a reference response.
To this end, a line of work attempts to integrate the intermediate inference process such as rationale
(
Zelikman et al. 2022
)
and reasoning traces
(
Tian et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
, into supervised training to improve test-time performance.
While these intermediate steps have been shown effectiveness in reasoning tasks such as coding and math, they are less straightforward to extend to tasks such as writing or open-ended dialogue.
The intermediate process for general tasks remains less discussed.

In this work, we seek to find an inference process for instruction-following and integrate it into supervised training to facilitate test-time scaling.
Recently,
Wang et al. 2024a
propose mixture-of-agents (MoA) which aggregates diverse response from multiple agents to improve generation quality.
Drawing inspiration from the mixture-of-agents (MoA) framework
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
, which aggregates diverse outputs from multiple LLMs, we propose to treat
aggregation
itself as a general-purpose inference process for instruction following.
This leads to a new supervised training framework, which we term
aggregation fine-tuning
(
AFT
).
As illustrated in Figure
1
, under AFT the model is trained to
aggregate
multiple draft responses into a single, refined answer rather than directly mapping a query to a response.
During inference, the model can itself generate and aggregate draft responses to produce a refined response.

To perform aggregation fine-tuning, we begin by constructing a specialized training dataset in which each instance comprises a query, multiple draft responses (i.e.,
proposals
), and a refined response (i.e.,
aggregation
).
We consider two types of proposals: off-policy and on-policy proposals, differing on whether or not the proposals are sampled from the model to be trained.
Subsequently, the model is trained to aggregate proposals to an aggregation given the query, thereby conventional fine-tuning of query-to-response alignment.
During inference, the model can perform
propose-and-aggregate
in a multi-layer fashion, which can be regarded as MoA
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
with only one agent.
At each layer, the model aggregates its proposals at the previous layer and generate proposals for the next layer.
This iterative process of proposal generation and aggregation allows the model to leverage both parallel sampling and sequential revision effectively.

Empirical results on Llama3.1-8B-Base and Mistral-7B-v0.1-Base demonstrate that AFT substantially surpasses standard SFT in various benchmark datasets.
On AlpacaEval 2, AFT models improve LC win rates by up to 13.9% on average,
with on-policy AFT models exhibiting particularly significant improvements.
Further performance gains are achieved through propose-and-aggregate at inference time, elevating Mistral-based models above a 30% LC win rate and Llama-based models beyond 40%.
Notably, the top-performing model, fine-tuned on only
64k
training examples
without preference alignment
, attains an LC win rate of
41.3%
, surpassing strong competitors such as Llama3.1-405B-Instruct and GPT4.
In MT-Bench, propose-and-aggregate pushed the best-performing mode to a score of 8.1.
Additionally, AFT shows enhancements over SFT in downstream tasks such as GSM8K and IFEval.

Analytic findings reveal that by fitting answers conditioned on drafts, aggregation learning benefits from shaping a low-perplexity region, where the model converges in a faster and more stable way.
We also observe that both the diversity and quality of proposals contribute to the aggregation quality.
In addition, the propose-and-aggregate method effectively combines sequential refinement and parallel sampling to scale inference-time computation, producing consistent improvements across multiple tasks with manageable computational overhead.
Overall, these insights highlight aggregation learning and propose-and-aggregate inference as a flexible, efficient strategy for unlocking the full potential of LLMs.
We will release the code, data and model after the anonymous period.

Related Work

Supervised Fine-tuning of LLMs.

Supervised fine-tuning plays a pivotal role in enhancing the instruction-following ability of LLM
(
Li et al. 2023b
;
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Ouyang et al. 2022a
;
Qin et al. 2024b
;
Jiang et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2023b
)
.
Previous works find that high-quality instruction-following data is essential for the success of SFT
(
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024a
;
Xu et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2023a
;
Li et al. 2024a
)
and various data-curation techniques are proposed
(
Wang et al. 2023
;
Du et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024a
;
Li et al. 2024b
;
Sun et al. 2023
)
.
More specifically, including complex constrained
(
Sun et al. 2024
;
He et al. 2024
)
, multi-faced
(
Lou et al. 2024
)
, self-refined
(
Zelikman et al. 2022
;
Cheng et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024a
)
and sequential
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
data could enhance the instruction-following ability of LLM.
Additionally, several studies on reasoning suggest incorporating intermediate processes (e.g., refinements in sequential revision
(
Cheng et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024a
)
or search traces in step-level graph search
(
Qin et al. 2024a
;
Xi et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
) into the SFT data to enhance performance in reasoning tasks.
The proposed aggregation fine-tuning differs in that it uses aggregation as the inference process of general-purpose instruction-following tasks to improve test-time performance.

Inference Scaling for LLMs.

Recent research has shifted attention from training-time computation to test-time scaling.

Snell et al. 2024a
show that allocating more compute at inference can sometimes prove more effective than enlarging model size.
Indeed, with existing inference-time algorithms
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Yao et al. 2023
;
Besta et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Brown et al. 2024
)
, smaller models can surpass larger ones when both share a fixed test-time budget
(
Wu et al. 2024b
)
.
According to
Welleck et al. 2024
, inference scaling can be broadly categorized into:

sequential revision
, where an LLM functions as a callable module to iteratively refine its prior outputs
(
Dohan et al. 2022
;
Khattab et al. 2024
;
Madaan et al. 2023
;
Shinn et al. 2023
;
Welleck et al. 2023
;
Havrilla et al. 2024
)
;

parallel sampling
, which generates multiple candidate responses concurrently and selects the best via a ranker
(
Brown et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2023b
)
;
and
step-level graph search
, wherein a complex query is decomposed into intermediate steps explored through tree-search
(
Świechowski et al. 2023
)
or graph-search algorithms
(
Yao et al. 2023
;
Besta et al. 2024
;
Tian et al. 2024
;
Chi et al. 2024
)
.
While most of these methods focus on a single model’s test-time scaling,
Wang et al. 2024a
introduce a
mixture-of-agents
approach that integrates multiple LLMs and aggregates their diverse outputs to boost performance.
In this study, we bring MoA’s aggregation concept into a single-model setting for both training and inference, where
aggregation learning
uncovers an LLM’s latent potential, and
propose-and-aggregate
further elevates performance on par with models of substantially larger scale.
In particular, we view aggregation learning as an intermediate process for general instruction following, while propose-and-aggregate unifies the strengths of sequential revision and parallel sampling at test time.

3
Method

In a traditional supervised fine-tuning setup, a language model
f_{\theta}:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{R}
is trained to map each query
q\in\mathcal{Q}
directly to a single reference response
r^{*}\in\mathcal{R}
.
In contrast, we propose a supervised training framework wherein a language model aggregates multiple draft responses, referred to as
proposals
, into a refined final response, termed
aggregation
.
We define this process as
aggregation fine-tuning
.
Let
q\in\mathcal{Q}
be a query, and
P=\{r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{K}\}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
represent a set of
K
proposals.
Given
(q,P)
, the goal of AFT is to train an aggregator
A_{\theta}:\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{R}^{K}\to\mathcal{R}
, parameterized by
\theta
, to produce a refined response
\hat{r}=A_{\theta}(q,P)
that approximates a high-quality reference response
r^{*}\in\mathcal{R}
.
During inference, the model can
iteratively
perform aggregation
A_{\theta}:\mathcal{Q}\times\mathcal{R}^{K}_{t-1}\to\mathcal{R}_{t}
to refine responses, where
t
is the iteration step or aggregation layer.

This process contrasts with traditional supervised fine-tuning, which directly maps a query
q
to a single reference response
r^{*}
.
By explicitly conditioning on the drafts
P
, the model requires a meta-skill which goes beyond producing an output for a query.
It learns a reasoning process that evaluates, critiques, and improves upon initial solutions.
The overall framework is illustrated in Figure
2
.
We provide a detailed illustration of data construction, model training, and inference in the following sections.

3.1
Data Construction

Constructing training data for aggregation fine-tuning involves collecting model proposals and a reference aggregation response, for each user query.

Proposal Collection.

We categorize proposals into two types:
off-policy
proposals and
on-policy
proposals.

Off-policy
proposals are derived from existing preference alignment datasets, such as UltraFeedback
(
Cui et al. 2023
)
, where each query is accompanied by multiple responses generated by models different from the one being fine-tuned.

On-policy
proposals are obtained by leveraging in-context learning (ICL) with demonstrations, prompting the base LLM to generate multiple responses for a given query
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
. Since these proposals are sampled from the same LLM that will be fine-tuned, they are referred to as
on-policy
proposals.
In this way, the model learns how to revise and aggregate its own generations.
Both methods result in a set of
K
proposals, denoted as
P=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{K}\}
, for each query
q
.

Aggregation Construction.

We use a dedicated prompt (Appendix
A
) to prompt a stronger language model to aggregate the proposals
P
into a reference response
r^{*}
that serves as the ground truth for training.
This procedure ensures that
r^{*}
demonstrates an ideal aggregation behavior, incorporating information from
P
while correcting errors and improving quality.
The data construction details can be found in Section
4
.

3.2
Training

The goal of aggregation fine-tuning is to maximize the probability that the model’s aggregator
A_{\theta}
assigns to the reference aggregated response
r^{*}
. Formally, for a training dataset

\{(q^{(i)},P^{(i)},r^{*(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where each example consists of a query
q^{(i)}
, a set of proposals
P^{(i)}
, and a high-quality reference aggregation
r^{*(i)}
, we seek:

\max_{\theta}\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{\theta}\bigl(r^{*(i)}\!\mid\!q^{(i)},\,P^{(i)}\bigr).

(1)

In contrast, conventional supervised training learns a mapping function
f
from a query to reference response directly:

\max_{\theta}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{\theta}(r^{*(i)}\mid q^{(i)})

(2)

We use the same prompt as outlined in Section
3.1
to incorporate proposals.
Rather than producing an answer from scratch given only the query, the aggregator can build upon the semantic cues, linguistic structures, and partial solutions embedded in the drafts.
As a result, the model’s generation perplexity is significantly reduced, reflecting its ability to more efficiently predict the reference tokens due to the guidance offered by the existing candidates.
Therefore, this enriched learning signal leads to a more stable and smooth training curve, as demonstrated in Section
6.1
.

3.3
Inference

During inference, the AFT model can perform
propose-and-aggregate
to scale inference-time computing to boost performance.
The process of propose-and-aggregate involves iteratively aggregating proposals from the previous layer and generating aggregations, forming the proposals for the next layer.

Initial Proposal Generation.

Given a query
q
, the model draws
K
initial proposals
P^{(0)}=\{r^{(0)}_{1},\dots,r^{(0)}_{K}\}
by sampling:

r^{(0)}_{i}\sim A_{\theta}(r|q)\quad i=1,2,\ldots K.

(3)

Choosing a single proposal from this set as the final response is equivalent to standard generation (i.e.,
A_{\theta}(q)
) which does not scale inference-time computation.

Figure 3
:
Test-time scaling of propose-and-aggregate compared with sequential revision and parallel sampling.

Iterative Aggregation (Optional).

Given the initial proposals, the model can optionally produce a refined response in a multi-layer setup.
Specifically, the model can take in the aggregated responses from the previous layer
\ell
(e.g., initial proposals at the first layer
P^{(0)}
) as input proposals:

P^{(\ell)}\;=\;\bigl\{\,r^{(\ell)}_{1},\,\dots,\,r^{(\ell)}_{K}\bigr\}\quad\ell=0,1,\dots,L,

(4)

where
L
is the maximum aggregation layer.
Subsequently, the model performs aggregation on these proposals to further enhance generation quality:

r^{(\ell+1)}_{i}\;\sim\;A_{\theta}\bigl(r|q,\;P^{(\ell)}\bigr)\quad i=1,2,\ldots K,

(5)

forming the proposals for the next layer:
P^{(\ell+1)}
.
This iterative procedure continues until a predefined number of layers is reached.

Iterative propose-and-aggregate inference thus emulates a recursive reasoning process in which the model refines its responses over multiple stages.
As noted by
Snell et al. 2024b
, LLMs can scale test-time computing via sequential revision or parallel sampling.
As presented in Figure
3
, sequential revision sequentially refines model outputs to incrementally improve quality
(
Qu et al. 2024
)
, whereas parallel sampling explores the sampling space under the guidance of a verifier
(
Lightman et al. 2024
)
.
Propose-and-aggregate combines the advantages of depth-first search (sequential revision) and breadth-first search (parallel sampling) by iteratively refining diverse aggregated generations from prior iterations.
We demonstrate how our method scales along both depth and breadth axes in Section
6.3
.

4
Experimental Setup

Data.

We construct training data based on UltraFeedback
(
Cui et al. 2023
)
.
UltraFeedback consists of 64k instances, each of which containing multiple model responses to a user query, providing a natural testbed for
off-policy
proposals.
Among these responses, we select the response with the highest score as the reference for training
SFT
baselines.
To generate
on-policy
proposals, we utilize in-context learning to prompt base LLMs (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B-base and Mistral-7B-v0.1-Base) for sampling 10 responses to a query in UltraFeedback, subsequently employing a reward model to select the top 5 highest-ranked samples.
We adopt the ICL template from prior research
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
, and the reward model utilized is
sfairXC/FsfairX-LLaMA3-RM-v0.1

(
Dong et al. 2023
)
.
To promote generation diversity, we set the temperature to 1.0, top_k to 50, and top_p to 0.95, as recommended by
Wang et al. 2024b
.
We use Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct
(
Hui et al. 2024
)
to generate aggregated responses (aggregations) given queries and proposals following
Wang et al. 2024a
.

Training.

We perform fine-tuning on two families of base LLMs: Llama-3.1-8B-Base
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
and Mistral-7B-v0.1-Base
(
Jiang et al. 2023a
)
.
For both SFT and AFT models, training is conducted using Llama-Factory
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
.
All models are fine-tuned with LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
, employing a rank of 8 and a total batch size of 128 across four A100 GPUs.
The learning rate is set to
1\times 10^{-4}
with a cosine schedule featuring 10% warmup steps for one epoch.

Evaluation.

We primarily evaluate our models using two prominent open-ended instruction-following benchmarks: the single-turn dialogue evaluation AlpacaEval 2
(
Li et al. 2023b
)
and the multi-turn dialogue evaluation MT-Bench
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
.
These benchmarks assess the models’ conversational capabilities across a diverse array of queries spanning various domains and tasks.
Scores are reported according to each benchmark’s official evaluation protocol.
For AlpacaEval 2, we present both the raw win rate (WR) and length-controlled win rate (LC)
(
Dubois et al. 2024
)
.
For MT-Bench, we provide the average score with GPT-4 serving as the judging model.
Additionally, we also evaluate performance on downstream tasks, which encompass general knowledge (MMLU
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
), reasoning abilities (ARC-c
(
Clark et al. 2018
)
and StrategyQA
(
Geva et al. 2021
)
), mathematical skills (GSM8K
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
)
), and instruction following (IFEval
(
Zhou et al. 2023b
)
).
All evaluations adhere to evaluation configurations under OpenCompass
(
Contributors 2023
)
, where we adpot few-shot generation configurations for MMLU, ARC-c, StrategyQA, and GSM8K, while zero-shot for IFEval.
For IFEval, we report the average across all four scores.

Inference.

For proposal generation and intermediate aggregation, we set the temperature to 0.7 and top_p to 0.95 across all experiments.
By default, we set the number of proposals to 5 and configure the aggregation layers to 2 for AlpacaEval 2 and MT-Bench, while employing a single-layer aggregation for GSM8K and IFEval.
For final response generation (i.e., last-layer aggregation), we configure settings according to standard routing practices.
For AlpacaEval 2, we utilize a temperature of 0.9 for Llama-based models and 0.7 for Mistral-based models following previous work
(
Meng et al. 2024
)
.
For MT-Bench, we adhere to the official decoding setup that prescribes varying sampling temperatures based on distinct categories.
For downstream tasks, we follow the OpenCompass configuration.

The results on
AlpacaEval 2
are presented in Table

.

Downstream Task Performance

The evaluation results for downstream tasks are summarized in Table
3
(MMLU, ARC-cA, and StrategyQA) and Figure
5
(GSM8K and IFEval).
AFT models demonstrate comparable performance to the SFT baseline on MMLU while consistently improving outcomes in reasoning tasks such as ARC-c and StrategyQA.
Due to the limited information provided in the answers for these tasks—specifically, single-choice options or yes/no responses—the propose-and-aggregate method is not applied.
For GSM8K and IFEval, we perform propose-and-aggregate for three runs and report mean performance and standard errors.
As shown in Figure

, AFT models consistently outperform SFT counterparts, and the advantages are further expanded with propose-and-aggregate.

We analyze the computational overhead of the propose-and-aggregate framework and compare with parallel sampling.
We approximate inference FLOPs following previous work
(
Brown et al. 2024
)
(Details in Appendix
C
).
The primary additional computational cost arises from the aggregation step, which processes all proposals from the previous layer as input prompts.
The FLOPs for propose-and-aggregate, denoted as
\hat{F}
, can be approximated as:

\hat{F}\approx L\cdot(2\cdot N\cdot F)+F
,
where
F
represents the FLOPs for vanilla generation,
L
is the number of aggregation layers and
N
denotes the number of parallel proposals.
A common parallel sampling baseline is Best-of-N (BoN), which uses an external reward model to rank multiple generated responses.
Assuming the reward model is the same size as the policy model, the FLOPs for BoN can be approximated by
\bar{F}\;\approx\;2\,\cdot\,N\,\cdot\,F
.

compares propose-and-aggregate to BoN under comparative FLOPs.
Even when equipped with BoN, an SFT model cannot surpass the performance of an AFT model.
Furthermore, propose-and-aggregate offers greater improvements on AFT compared to BoN, indicating the benefit of expanding along both width (parallel proposals) and depth (iterative aggregation).
Notably, propose-and-aggregate does not rely on an external reward model to achieve test-time scaling, thereby simplifying deployment while retaining robust performance gains.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced
aggregation fine-tuning
as a paradigm that teaches language models to aggregate multiple draft responses (i.e., proposals) into a refined final answer (i.e., aggregation).
By conditioning on diverse proposals, AFT encourages higher-order reasoning and demonstrates consistent gains over standard supervised fine-tuning across both instruction-following benchmarks and downstream tasks.
Furthermore, our
propose-and-aggregate
method leverages iterative inference to further improve performance without additional training, combining the merits of both sequential refinement and parallel sampling.
Analysis showed that AFT reduces perplexity, stabilizes convergence, and benefits from carefully balancing proposal quality and diversity, making it a flexible and cost-effective approach to unlocking latent model capabilities.

Impact Statement

This work aims to advance the field of Machine Learning by providing a new training and inference paradigm, i.e., Aggregation Fine-Tuning, that refines multiple candidate answers into a stronger overall solution.
While we do not foresee any immediate or significant societal harms unique to this approach, it is possible that more powerful language generation techniques could be misused for deceptive or harmful purposes (e.g., generating misleading content).
We encourage future efforts to incorporate robust filtering and responsible usage guidelines for such systems.
Beyond these considerations, the potential positive societal impacts include more accurate, reliable, and resource-efficient language models, which could benefit a wide range of applications from education to healthcare.

Appendix C
Computational Overhead

Following
Brown et al. 2024
, the FLOPs for generation can be approximated as:

\text{FLOPs per token}\approx 2\times(\text{num parameters}+2\times\text{num layers}\times\text{token dim}\times\text{context length}),

and the total inference FLOPs are estimated as:

\text{total inference FLOPs}\approx(\text{num prompt tokens}+\text{num decoded tokens})\times\text{FLOPs per token}.

For simplicity and without loss of generality, we omit the FLOPs for the query prompt tokens and focus on the computational overhead contributed by prompt tokens accommodating previous-layer proposals. Under this assumption:

For vanilla generation, the total inference FLOPs can be approximated as:

\text{total inference FLOPs}\approx\text{num decoded tokens}\times\text{FLOPs per token}.

For parallel sampling with a reward model, the FLOPs include the cost of generating multiple proposals in parallel and evaluating them using the RM. The total FLOPs, denoted as
\bar{F}
, can be approximated as:

\bar{F}\approx 2\cdot(\text{num proposals})\cdot(\text{num decoded tokens})\cdot\text{FLOPs per token}.

Here, the factor of 2 accounts for the additional computational cost of evaluating the proposals using an RM, which is typically comparable in size to the policy model.

For the propose-and-aggregate framework, the additional overhead comes from the aggregation step, where all proposals from the previous layer are included as part of the input prompt. Considering
the practical implementations of KV Cache that enable keys and values of contextual tokens (i.e., proposals from the previous layer) to be reusable, the total inference FLOPs, denoted as
\hat{F}
, can be approximated as:

\hat{F}\approx(\text{num aggregation layers})\cdot(2\cdot\text{num proposals}\cdot\text{num decoded tokens}\cdot\text{FLOPs per token})

+(\text{num decoded tokens}\cdot\text{FLOPs per token}),

where
(2\cdot\text{num aggregation layers}\cdot\text{num proposals}\cdot\text{num decoded tokens}\cdot\text{FLOPs per token})
represents the cost of generating and processing proposals in multiple aggregation layers, while
(\text{num decoded tokens}\cdot\text{FLOPs per token})
corresponds to the final generation step.
