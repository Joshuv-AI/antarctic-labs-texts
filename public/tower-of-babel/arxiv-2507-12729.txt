Tensor-Tensor Products, Group Representations, and Semidefinite Programming
Alex Dunbar ∗,1,2 and Elizabeth Newman †,1,3

Abstract
. The ⋆ M \star_{M} -family of tensor-tensor products is a framework which generalizes many properties from linear algebra to third order tensors. Here, we investigate positive semidefiniteness and semidefinite programming under the ⋆ M \star_{M} -product. Critical to our investigation is a connection between the choice of matrix M M in the ⋆ M \star_{M} -product and the representation theory of an underlying group action. Using this framework, third order tensors equipped with the ⋆ M \star_{M} -product are a natural setting for the study of invariant semidefinite programs. As applications of the M M -SDP framework, we provide a characterization of certain nonnegative quadratic forms and solve low-rank tensor completion problems.



1.
Introduction

Multiway operators or
tensors
are central objects in many fields of pure and applied mathematics, including data science applications
[
VT02
,
FO17
]
, multidimensional scientific simulations
[
BLP20
]
, and tensor fields (i.e., multiway-array-valued functions) for Riemannian manifolds
[
DP91
]
and mechanical stress tensors
[
Irg08
]
.
The breadth of multilinear applications has generated numerous factorization strategies to extract multidimensional features and operate in high dimensions; see, e.g.,
[
Bal25
,
KB09
]
for comprehensive reviews.
Three foundational tensor factorizations that emulate properties of the matrix singular value decomposition (SVD)
[
GV13
]
are Canonical Polyadic (CP)
[
Hit27
,
Har70
,
CC70
]
, higher-order SVD (HOSVD)
[
Tuc66
,
DDV00
]
, and tensor train
[
Ose11
]
.
Each tensor decomposition brings distinct advantages and algebraic consequences.
The CP format offers uniqueness and interpretability of the extracted features, but common linear algebra computations become NP-hard (e.g., determining rank and eigenvalues)
[
HL13
]
.
The HOSVD excels at data compression, but lacks Schmidt-Eckart-Young-Mirsky-like guarantees for optimal low-rank approximations
[
Sch07
,
EY36
,
Mir60
]
.
The tensor train format is designed to approximate high-dimensional operators arising in quantum chemistry
[
Orú14
]
and high-dimensional partial differential equations
[
Bac23
]
, but is often viewed as a powerful and strategic computational tool rather than analyzed from a linear algebraic perspective.
The algebraic sacrifices of these classical tensor decompositions can make revealing underlying symmetries, invariants, and other geometric structures challenging in high dimensions and potentially eliminate the opportunity to exploit such structures for efficient algorithm development.

Recent work has introduced a family of tensor operators, called the
\star_{M}
-product (the prefix is pronounced “star-M”), that extends traditional matrix-matrix multiplication to multidimensional arrays
[
KKA15
,
KBHH13
,
KM11
]
.
This tensor operation gives rise to provably optimal multiway data representations
[
KHAN21
]
and has demonstrated empirical success across applications in imaging
[
NK20
,
SKH16
]
, tensor completion
[
ZA17
,
KLL21
]
, and machine learning
[
NHAK24
]
.
The key perspective of the
\star_{M}
-product is to consider an order-three tensor to be a two-dimensional matrix with vector-valued entries.
The
\star_{M}
-product thereby multiplies two tensors by replacing the underlying notion of scalar multiplication with a vector-vector product defined by an invertible matrix,
M
.
The original
t
-product
[
KM11
]
considered
M
as a discrete Fourier transform matrix, giving rise to analysis of numerical algorithms under the algebra of circulants
[
KBHH13
,
GGV13
]
.
Connections between the
\star_{M}
-product and other tensor factorization strategies were introduced in
[
KHAN21
]
.

A strength of the
\star_{M}
-product framework is that it allows for tensor properties and tensor decompositions which are analogous to familiar matrix decompositions in numerical linear algebra, such as the existence of a singular value decomposition
[
KM11
,
KHAN21
]
. One matrix property which is widely applicable in both pure and applied mathematics is positive semidefiniteness. In convex optimization, positive semidefiniteness is used to define
semidefinite programming problems
–optimization problems whose decision variables are positive semidefinite matrices. Semidefinite programming is well-studied; see e.g.,
[
VB96
]
, and has applications in polynomial optimization
[
Las01
,
Par03
]
, relaxations of integer programs
[
GW95
]
, and applied algebraic geometry
[
BPT12
]
among others. Semidefiniteness has been studied in the case of the original
t
-product
[
KM11
]
in
[
ZHW21
,
ZHH22
,
MKY24
]
, but not for the general
\star_{M}
-product.

1.1.
Our Contributions

In this paper, we develop the theory of
\star_{M}
-semidefinite tensors and corresponding optimization problems. As is common in the
\star_{M}
-product literature, the condition of positive semidefiniteness can be checked on slices in the transform domain; that is, the tensor obtained by changing basis on the space of tubes according to the matrix
M
. Viewing this as a block diagonalization result, we make a connection to group invariant semidefinite programs, which requires a representation-theoretic perspective on the
\star_{M}
-product. Thus far, the mathematics behind the
\star_{M}
-product has been primarily discussed in the language of matrix algebras, which has enabled elegant extensions of numerical linear algebra tools and algorithms to multilinear settings.
Our primary achievement is providing the first unified view of the
\star_{M}
-product from algebraic and geometric perspectives which gives interpretability to the matrix
M
.
Notably, we completely characterize the geometric space of tensors which represent group equivariant transformations under the
\star_{M}
-product for a given matrix
M
.
Group equivariance, the property that a group action on a function input is equivalent to a group action on a function output, is a symmetry-preserving property that has wide utility in statistical inference
[
LC98
]
, differential equations
[
OR87
]
, and machine learning
[
BBCV21
,
LN23
,
CEEOSS21
,
MWF22
]
.
With a group representation interpretation, we are able to exploit group-theoretic tools on tensor-tensor products.

We briefly highlight key results presented in our paper.

•

We introduce
\star_{M}
-semidefinite programming, the first use of a general
\star_{M}
-product for this application.
In the process, we provide rigorous theoretical analysis of
\star_{M}
-semidefinite tensors, extending numerous classic equivalent definitions to the tensor case.

•

We connect
\star_{M}
-semidefinite programming to group invariant semidefinite programs. In the process, we present new algebraic and geometric insight into the
\star_{M}
-product from a representation theoretic perspective. Specifically, we highlight the importance of the choice of matrix
M
via an interpretation through Schur’s Lemma.

•

To complement the new theoretical advancements and demonstrate the practicality of our approach, we provide numerical experiments of solving
\star_{M}
-semidefinite programs on both video data and hyperspectral data.
Code to reproduce all experiments is provided publicly at
https://github.com/elizabethnewman/tsdp
.

2.0.1.
Vectorization

To avoid ambiguity with tubes, we denote column vectors with non-bold lowercase letters; e.g.,
a\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{3}}
.
To map between tubes and vectors, we define two isomorphisms,
\tube:\mathbb{F}^{n_{3}}\to\mathbb{F}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
and
\mathop{\rm{vec}}:\mathbb{F}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}\to\mathbb{F}^{n_{3}}
, defined in coordinates by

(1)

\displaystyle\tube(x)_{1,1,k}=x_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{x})_{k}=\mathbf{x}_{1,1,k}\qquad\text{for }k\in[n_{3}].

For ease of notation, we will sometimes write
\mathbf{x}\equiv x
if
\mathbf{x}=\tube(x)
.

2.0.2.
Tubal Transformations

To apply linear transformations along the tubes of a tensor, we use the mode-
3
product,
\times_{3}:\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}\times\mathbb{F}^{p\times n_{3}}\to\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times p}
, denoted and defined as

(2)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}\times_{3}M)_{i,j,:}=\tube(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}_{ij}))

for
i\in[n_{1}]
and
j\in[n_{2}]
.
When the matrix
M
is clear from context, we denote
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{A}\times_{3}M
and refer to
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
as the image of the tensor
\mathcal{A}
in the
transform domain
.

2.0.3.
Facewise Operations

We often will discuss the structure of a tensor and implement algorithms facewise (i.e., by properties or actions of the frontal slices).
A tensor
\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
is
facewise diagonal
or
f-diagonal
if each frontal slice is a diagonal matrix; i.e., for each
k\in[n_{3}]
, the matrix
\mathcal{D}_{:,:,k}\in\Dgroup_{n_{1},n_{2}}(\mathbb{F})
where
\Dgroup_{n_{1},n_{2}}(\mathbb{F})
denotes the set of
n_{1}\times n_{2}
diagonal matrices over
\mathbb{F}
.
If
n=n_{1}=n_{2}
, we write the set of diagonal matrices as
\Dgroup_{n}(\mathbb{F})
.
The facewise identity tensor
\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
is an f-diagonal tensor where each frontal slice is the
n\times n
identity matrix; i.e.,
\mathcal{I}_{:,:,k}=I_{n}
for all
k\in[n_{3}]
.
We can multiply two tensors facewise by multiplying corresponding frontal slices.
Specifically, the
facewise product
,
\smalltriangleup:\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times m\times n_{3}}\times\mathbb{F}^{m\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}\to\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
, computes
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\smalltriangleup\mathcal{B}
where
\mathcal{C}_{:,:,k}=\mathcal{A}_{:,:,k}\mathcal{B}_{:,:,k}
for
k\in[n_{3}]
.
Computationally, the facewise product can be parallelized over the frontal slices. In terms of linear algebra, we can phrase the facewise product in terms of block diagonal matrices. Specifically, for a tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
, define its
block-diagonalization
as

(3)

\mathop{\rm bdiag}(\mathcal{A})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{:,:,1}&&&\\
&\mathcal{A}_{:,:,2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\mathcal{A}_{:,:,n_{3}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}n_{3}\times n_{2}n_{3}}

In this notation, we have that
\mathop{\rm bdiag}(\mathcal{A}\smalltriangleup\mathcal{B})=\mathop{\rm bdiag}(\mathcal{A})\mathop{\rm bdiag}(\mathcal{B})
.

2.1.
An Algebraic Ring Perspective of the Tubal
\star_{M}
-Product

Following the works
[
KKA15
,
KM11
]
, we provide a brief overview of core properties of the
\star_{M}
-framework, starting from the foundation of algebraically-motivated tensor-tensor products (the
\star_{M}
-product), subsequent linear algebraic properties induced by the
\star_{M}
-product, and concluding with a
\star_{M}
-analog of the singular value decomposition (SVD).

Fundamentally, the
\star_{M}
-product defines a multiplication rule for third-order tensors that is parameterized by an invertible matrix
M
; that is,
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
where
\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
is the general linear group over
\mathbb{F}
or the set of invertible
n_{3}\times n_{3}
matrices with entries in
\mathbb{F}
.
In some cases, we will restrict ourselves to unitary or orthogonal matrices
M
with
M^{H}M=I_{n_{3}}
.
We denote as
M\in\Ogroup_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
where
\Ogroup_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
is the group of
n_{3}\times n_{3}
unitary or orthogonal matrices, when
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{R}
, respectively.

We start by defining the
\star_{M}
-product on tubes.
Let
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
and fix
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
.
The operation
\star_{M}:\mathbb{F}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}\times\mathbb{F}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}\to\mathbb{F}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
is defined as

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}\star_{M}\mathbf{b}=\left((\mathbf{a}\times_{3}M)\odot(\mathbf{b}\times_{3}M)\right)\times_{3}M^{-1}

where
\odot
is the Hadamard pointwise product.

From the isomorphisms in (
1
), an equivalent vectorized presentation of the tubal
\star_{M}
-product in (
4
) is

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}\star_{M}\mathbf{b}=\tube(M^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{b}))

where
\mathop{\rm diag}:\mathbb{F}^{n_{3}}\to\Dgroup_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
is a bijection that maps the entries of a vector to the diagonal entries of a diagonal matrix.

Note that multiplictaion by a tube
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear transformation. We denote
this transformation
T_{\mathbf{a}}:\mathbb{F}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}\to\mathbb{F}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
; that is,
T_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{a}\star_{M}\mathbf{x}
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
.
Under the identification in (
5
),
T_{\mathbf{a}}
has a matrix representative

(6)

\displaystyle M^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))M=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{3}}a_{i}M^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(M(:,i))M.

In other words,
T_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x})\equiv A\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{x})
where
A
is the matrix in (
6
).
We provide a concrete example of the
\star_{M}
-algebraic structure in
Example

2.1
.

Example 2.1

(The matrix representations of the
t
-product
[
KM11
]
)
.

The
t
-product is the
\star_{M}
-product with
M
taken to be the (unnormalized) discrete Fourier matrix
F\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{3}\times n_{3}}
.
Each entry of
F
is a power of a complex root of unity; that is,
F_{j+1,k+1}=e^{2\pi\mathbbm{i}jk/n_{3}}
for
j,k=0,\ldots,n_{3}-1
where
\mathbbm{i}=\sqrt{-1}
.
The matrix representative of the linear transformation
T_{\mathbf{a}}:\mathbb{C}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}\to\mathbb{C}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
is the circulant matrix

(7)

\displaystyle F^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(F\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))F=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&a_{n_{3}}&\dots&a_{2}\\
a_{2}&a_{1}&\dots&a_{3}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{n_{3}}&a_{n_{3}-1}&\dots&a_{1}\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{3}}a_{i}Z^{i-1}

where
Z
is the circulant downshift matrix with the property
Z^{n_{3}}=I
; i.e.,

(8)

\displaystyle Z=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&1\\
1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&1&0\end{bmatrix}.

As a result of the matrix representation of tubal multiplication, the
\star_{M}
-product gives
\mathbb{F}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
the structure of a
\mathbb{F}
-algebra with multiplicative identity

\mathbf{e}_{M}=\tube(M^{-1}\mathbbm{1}_{n_{3}})
, where
\mathbbm{1}_{n_{3}}
is the
n_{3}\times 1
all ones vector
[
KKA15
]
.
We denote the ring of tubes given by the
\star_{M}
-product over the field
\mathbb{F}
by
\mathbb{F}_{M}
. In what follows, it will sometimes be advantageous to “forget” the ring structure on
\mathbb{F}_{M}
, and only emphasize the
\mathbb{F}
-vector space strucutre, in which case we use the notation
\mathbb{F}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
.
We note that the rings
\mathbb{F}_{M}
for
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
are all isomorphic to one another in the category of
\mathbb{F}
-algebras.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
and
I
be the
n_{3}\times n_{3}
identity matrix. Then,
\mathbb{F}_{M}\simeq\mathbb{F}_{I}
as
\mathbb{F}
-algebras.

Proof.

The map
\phi:\mathbb{F}_{M}\to\mathbb{F}_{I}
defined by
\phi(\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{a}\times_{3}M
is bijective and
\mathbb{F}
-linear since
M
is invertible. Following (
5
), it is a
\mathbb{F}
-algebra homomorphism because

(9a)

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{a}\star_{M}\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle=\tube(M^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{b}))\times_{3}M

(9b)

\displaystyle=\tube(\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{b}))

(9c)

\displaystyle=\phi(\mathbf{a})\star_{I}\phi(\mathbf{b}).

∎

The result of Proposition
2.1
is that the commutative algebra alone is too coarse of a framework to effectively study the family of
\star_{M}
-products. However, we notice that the map
\phi
used in the proof of Proposition
2.1
is simply a change of (
\mathbb{F}
-vector space) basis on
\mathbb{F}_{M}
. So, it is natural to ask “What is a good choice of basis?” We work towards an representation-theoretic answer to this question in Section
4
.

2.1.1.
Squares in the Ring
\mathbb{R}_{M}

Nonnegative elements of the ring
\mathbb{R}
are central to the theory of semidefinite matrices and squares in the ring
\mathbb{R}_{M}
are the natural generalizations to the
\star_{M}
-setting.
Thus, we characterize elements of the ring
\mathbb{R}_{M}
which are squares.

Lemma 2.2
.

Fix
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
. Then,
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}
is a square if and only if
\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\widehat{\mathbf{a}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}_{+}
where
\widehat{\mathbf{a}}=\mathbf{a}\times_{3}M
.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}
is a square, then
\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a})=M^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(Mx)Mx
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
. Therefore,
\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\widehat{\mathbf{a}})=\mathop{\rm diag}(Mx)Mx\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}_{+}
.
Conversely, if
\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\widehat{\mathbf{a}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}_{+}
, then
\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\widehat{\mathbf{a}})=\mathop{\rm diag}(y)y
for some
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
.
It follows that
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{x}\star_{M}\mathbf{x}
where
\mathbf{x}=\tube(M^{-1}y)
.
∎

2.2.
The
\star_{M}
-Product

As depicted in
Figure

1
, tensors can be viewed as “matrices of tubes.”
Thus, the ring structure on the space of tubes suggests a natural tensor-tensor product for third-order tensors.
Specifically, we consider a tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
to be an
\mathbb{F}_{M}
-linear transformation of free modules
(\mathbb{F}_{M})^{n_{2}}\to(\mathbb{F}_{M})^{n_{1}}
.
We thereby define the
\star_{M}
-product of tensors analogously to matrix-matrix products.

Definition 2.3

(
\star_{M}
-product)
.

Fix
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
.
Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n_{1}\times m}
and
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{m\times n_{2}}
.
Then,
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
where

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{i,j}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\mathcal{A}_{i,\ell}\star_{M}\mathcal{B}_{\ell,j}.

for
i\in[n_{1}]
and
j\in[n_{2}]
.

Consequently, the
\star_{M}
-product
\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{B}
is the composition
(\mathbb{F}_{M})^{n_{2}}\overset{\mathcal{B}}{\to}(\mathbb{F}_{M})^{m}\overset{\mathcal{A}}{\to}(\mathbb{F}_{M})^{n_{1}}
.
In practice, the
\star_{M}
-product is implemented following
Algorithm

1
.

Data:

\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times m\times n_{3}},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n_{2}\times n_{3}},M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
.

Result:

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}\times_{3}M
,
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}\times_{3}M
;

1

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\leftarrow\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\smalltriangleup\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
;

2

\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\times_{3}M^{-1}
.

Algorithm 1

\star_{M}
-product

Algorithm

1
shows that in the transform domain, the
\star_{M}
product operates independently on frontal slices. In terms of block diagonal matrices,
\mathop{\rm bdiag}(\hat{\mathcal{C}})=\mathop{\rm bdiag}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})\mathop{\rm bdiag}(\hat{\mathcal{B}})
. This provides one interpretation of the
\star_{M}
product in which the choice of matrix
M
and passage to the transform domain amounts to a block diagonalization.

An additional perspective on the
\star_{M}
-product as a structured, block matrix operation will prove useful in subsequent theoretical analysis.
We define the following tensor matricization operation.
Let
\mymat_{M}:\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\to\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}n_{3}\times n_{2}n_{3}}
be defined as

(11)

\displaystyle\mymat_{M}(\mathcal{A})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathcal{A}_{1,1,:}))&\cdots&\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathcal{A}_{1,n_{2},:}))\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathcal{A}_{n_{1},1,:}))&\cdots&\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathcal{A}_{n_{1},n_{2},:}))\end{bmatrix}.

The map
\mymat_{M}
is an isomorphism onto its image, which consists of the block matrices with
n_{1}\times n_{2}
elements of
D_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
. Using (
11
), we extend the matrix representative tubes in (
6
) to an
\mathbb{F}
-linear transformation of the underlying
\mathbb{F}
-vector spaces
\mathbb{F}^{n_{2}\times 1\times n_{3}}\to\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times 1\times n_{3}}
.
Specifically, given
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
, let
T_{\mathcal{A}}:\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n_{2}}\to\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n_{1}}
be defined as
T_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{X})=\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}
for any
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n_{2}}
.
Let
x=\mymat_{I}(\mathcal{X})\mathbbm{1}_{n_{2}n_{3}}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{2}n_{3}}
where
I
is the
n_{3}\times n_{3}
identity matrix; effectively,
x
is a specific vectorization of
\mathcal{X}
.
Then,

(12)

\displaystyle T_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{X})\equiv(I_{n_{1}}\otimes M^{-1})\mymat_{M}(\mathcal{A})(I_{n_{2}}\otimes M)x

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product
[
PP12
, Section 10.2]
.

2.3.
A Matrix Mimetic Algebra

Because of the similarities between the
\star_{M}
-product and traditional matrix-matrix multiplication, we say the
\star_{M}
-product is
matrix mimetic
.
We can extend this similarity to other familiar linear algebraic properties. For example, the notions of multiplicative identity tensor and the transpose of a tensor make sense in the
\star_{M}
setting.
The definitions follow from
[
KHAN21
]
, presented here in the language of rings.

Definition 2.4

(
\star_{M}
-identity tensor)
.

The
\star_{M}
-identity tensor, denoted
\mathcal{I}_{M}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n\times n}
, has tubes

(13)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{I}_{M})_{i,j,:}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{e}_{M}&i=j,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for
i,j\in[n]
where
\mathbf{e}_{M}=\tube(M^{-1}\mathbbm{1}_{n_{3}})
is the
\star_{M}
-multiplicative identity in
\mathbb{F}_{M}
.
The
\star_{M}
-identity tensor satisfies
\mathcal{I}_{M}\star_{M}\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{I}_{M}=\mathcal{A}
for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n\times n}
.

Definition 2.5

(
\star_{M}
-conjugate transpose/
\star_{M}
-transpose)
.

Fix
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
.
The
\star_{M}
-conjugate transpose of
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
, denoted
\mathcal{A}^{H}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}}
, is uniquely defined as

(14)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}^{H}\times_{3}M)_{:,:,k}=\left((\mathcal{A}\times_{3}M)_{:,:,k}\right)^{H}

for each
k\in[n_{3}]
.
If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
, the notion of
\star_{M}
-transpose, denoted
\mathcal{A}^{\top}
, reduces to permuting the indices of tubes or equivalently transposing the frontal slices of
\mathcal{A}
.

We note that the
\star_{M}
-conjugate transpose satisfies matrix mimetic properties such as reversal of the order of the
\star_{M}
-product under transposition; i.e.,
(\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{B})^{H}=\mathcal{B}^{H}\star_{M}\mathcal{A}^{H}
.
Further note that transposition commutes with the tensor matricization operation in (
11
); that is,
\mymat_{M}(\mathcal{A}^{H})=(\mymat_{M}(\mathcal{A}))^{H}
.

Definition 2.6

(
\star_{M}
-Hermitian/
\star_{M}
-symmetric)
.

Fix
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
. A tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n\times n}
is
\star_{M}
-Hermitian if
\mathcal{A}^{H}=\mathcal{A}
.
If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\top}=\mathcal{A}
, we call the tensor
\star_{M}
-symmetric.

The definition of a
\star_{M}
-symmetric tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}_{M}
provided in Definition
2.6
is consistent with our point of view that third-order tensors are matrices of tubes. However, this differs from the notion of a symmetric order 3 tensor as an element of the symmetric power
\mathrm{Sym}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
encountered elsewhere.

Definition 2.7

(
\star_{M}
-unitary/
\star_{M}
-orthogonal)
.

For a fixed
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
, a tensor
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n\times n}
is
\star_{M}
-unitary if
\mathcal{Q}^{H}\star_{M}\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}\star_{M}\mathcal{Q}^{H}=\mathcal{I}_{M}
.
If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
, we call a
\star_{M}
-unitary tensor
\star_{M}
-orthogonal.

A foundational result in the
\star_{M}
-product literature is the existence of a tensor singular value decomposition (SVD) analogous to the matrix SVD.

2.8
Existence of the
\star_{M}
-SVD
[
KKA15
,
KHAN21
]
.

Fix
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
and let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
.
There exist
\star_{M}
-unitary tensors
\mathcal{U}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n_{1}\times n_{1}}
and
\mathcal{V}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n_{2}\times n_{2}}
and an f-diagonal tensor
\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
such that

(
\star_{M}
-SVD)

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{U}\star_{M}\mathcal{S}\star_{M}\mathcal{V}^{H}.

The
\star_{M}
-SVD leads to a notion of tensor rank.

Definition 2.9

(
\star_{M}
-rank)
.

Fix
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
and let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
with
\star_{M}
-SVD given by
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{U}\star_{M}\mathcal{S}\star_{M}\mathcal{V}^{H}
.
The
\star_{M}
-rank is the number of nonzero singular tubes in
\mathcal{S}
; that is,

(15)

\displaystyle\starMrank(\mathcal{A})=\#\{\mathcal{S}_{i,i,:}\mid\mathcal{S}_{i,i,:}\not={\bf 0}\text{ for }i\in[\min(n_{1},n_{2})]\}.

Algorithmically, the
\star_{M}
-SVD is formed by computing matrix SVDs facewise in the transform domain. Specifically, for each
k\in[n_{3}]
, we compute the matrix SVD

(16)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{:,:,k}=\widehat{\mathcal{U}}_{:,:,k}\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{:,:,k}\widehat{V}_{:,:,k}^{H}

for each
k\in[n_{3}]
where
\widehat{\cdot}=\cdot\times_{3}M
.
Note that the properties of the matrix SVD hold for each frontal slice in the transform domain; in particular,
\widehat{\mathcal{U}}_{:,:,k}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{:,:,k}
are unitary and
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{:,:,k}
is diagonal with nonnegative, ordered diagonal entries
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{1,1,k}\geq\cdots\geq\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{r,r,k}\geq 0
where
r=\starMrank(\mathcal{A})
.

Remark 2.1
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is
\star_{M}
-symmetric, then we can take
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{V}
in the decomposition (

⋆

M

-SVD
). This follows from the fact that the SVD of
\mathcal{A}
yields SVDs of the frontal slices of
\mathcal{A}\times_{3}M
.

Remark 2.2
.

For expositional clarity, we may use
\star_{M}
- or
M
- when describing the properties of a tensor.
We may omit the prefix when there is no ambiguity.

2.4.
Inner Product of Tensors Under the
\star_{M}
-Product

Central to constructing
\star_{M}
-based tensor semidefinite programming is the inner product between tensors.
We define the Frobenius-norm-induced inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}\times\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}\to\mathbb{F}
as

(17)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{3}}\overline{\mathcal{A}_{i,j,k}}\mathcal{B}_{i,j,k}

where
\overline{z}
denotes the complex conjugate of a scalar
z\in\mathbb{F}
.
The Frobenius norm of a tensor induced by (
17
) is thereby
\|\mathcal{A}\|_{F}=\sqrt{\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}\rangle}
.

Note that the definition of a tensor inner product is consistent with that of matrices (i.e., when
n_{3}=1
), which is alternatively presented as
\langle A,B\rangle=\mytrace(A^{H}B)
for
A,B\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
.
This observation gives rise to a facewise interpretation of (
17
), given by

(18)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{3}}\langle\mathcal{A}_{:,:,k},\mathcal{B}_{:,:,k}\rangle.

For completeness, we additionally provide a tubal perspective of (
17
) given by

(19)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\langle\mathcal{A}_{i,j,:},\mathcal{B}_{i,j,:}\rangle

where
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle=\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a})^{H}\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{b})
for tubes
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
.

We next establish some useful identities about the inner product of real tensors and
\star_{M}
multiplication given by an orthogonal matrix
M
. These identities generalize familiar properties of the inner product of matrices. We note that the orthogonality of
M
is a necessary hypothesis for the identities to hold.

Lemma 2.10
.

Fix
M\in\Ogroup_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
and let
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1\times n_{3}}
.

(1)

If
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n\times n_{3}}
, then

(20)

\langle\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{X},(\mathcal{A}^{\top}\star_{M}\mathcal{B})\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\rangle.

(2)

If
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
, then

(21)

\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{X}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}^{\top}\rangle.

(3)

For any tensors
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
, we have that

(22)

\langle\mathcal{A}\times_{3}M,\mathcal{B}\times_{3}M\rangle=\langle\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\rangle.

Proof.

The proofs are calculations which we defer to
B
.
∎

3.
Properties of
\star_{M}
-semidefinite tensors

In this section, we provide a definition of
M
-positive semidefinite (
\star_{M}
-PSD) third-order tensors which generalizes the definition of
t
-PSD tensors
[
ZHH22
, Definition 3(ii)]
.
We then prove basic properties of
\star_{M}
-PSD tensors, showing that many properties from PSD matrices generalize to the
\star_{M}
-product setting, and conclude with a discussion of
\star_{M}
-semidefinite programming problems.
Since our ultimate goal is the formulation of convex optimization problems, we restrict our attention to real-valued tensors with square frontal slices, i.e.
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n\times n}
for
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
.
We start with an inner-product-based definition of
\star_{M}
-PSD.

Definition 3.1

(
\star_{M}
-positive semidefinite tensor)
.

Fix
M\in GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
.
A symmetric tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n\times n}
is
\star_{M}
-positive semidefinite (
\star_{M}
-PSD) if for all
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
, we have
\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\rangle\geq 0
.

We denote the set of all
\star_{M}
-positive semidefinite tensors belonging to
\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n\times n}
by
\myPSD_{M}^{n}
. We write
\mathcal{A}\succeq_{M}0
if
\mathcal{A}\in\myPSD_{M}^{n}
.

3.0.1.
Dependence on
M

Note that Definition
3.1
depends on the choice of matrix
M
. For example, the
2\times 2\times 2
tensor
\mathcal{X}
with frontal slices

(23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{:,:,1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and }\quad\mathcal{X}_{:,:,2}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}

is
I
-PSD, but not
H
-PSD for
H=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{bmatrix}
. This is discussed in more detail in Example
3.1
.

3.0.2.
Convexity of
\myPSD_{M}^{n}

From the
\mathbb{R}
-linearity of the
\star_{M}
-product and the bilinearity of inner products, we see that if
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
are nonnegative constants and
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\myPSD_{M}^{n}
, then for any
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
,

(24)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{X},(\lambda\mathcal{A}+\mu\mathcal{B})\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\rangle=\lambda\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\rangle+\mu\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\rangle\geq 0.

Therefore
\myPSD_{M}^{n}
is a
convex cone
, i.e. a convex set invariant under nonnegative scaling.

3.1.
Properties of
\star_{M}
-PSD Tensors

We derive basic properties of
\star_{M}
-PSD tensors, emphasizing connections to the familiar theory of positive semidefinite matrices; see e.g.,
[
BPT12
, Appendix A.1]
for details.
In
Proposition

3.2
, we show the matrix representative of an
M
-PSD tensor
\mathcal{A}
is a PSD matrix.
In
Proposition

3.3
, we show that each frontal slice of
\mathcal{A}\times_{3}M
is a PSD matrix.
In
Proposition

3.5
, we show the existence of a tensor factorization
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}\star_{M}\mathcal{B}^{\top}
.
In
Proposition

3.6
, we show that the
\star_{M}
-principal minors of
\mathcal{A}
are squares in
\mathbb{R}_{M}
.
We conclude with
Proposition

, which contains a brief discussion of eigentubes.
Similar properties were shown for the

-product in
[

]
, and our work generalizes these properties to any orthogonal

3.1.1.
Positive Semidefinite Matrix Representative

In a reasonable notion of
\star_{M}
-positive semidefiniteness, we would expect the matrix representative of the underlying
\mathbb{R}
-linear transformation of a
\star_{M}
-PSD tensor to be a PSD matrix itself.
We prove this property below using the matrix representative in (
12
).

Proposition 3.2

(PSD Matrix Representative)
.

Fix
M\in\Ogroup_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
.
Given a symmetric tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n\times n}
, we have that
\mathcal{A}\in\myPSD_{M}^{n}
if and only if
(I_{n}\otimes M^{\top})\mymat_{M}(\mathcal{A})(I_{n}\otimes M)
is positive semidefinite.

Proof.

For any
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
, let
x\equiv\mymat_{I}(\mathcal{X})\mathbbm{1}_{nn_{3}}\in\mathbb{R}^{nn_{3}}
.
Then, we have

(25a)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\mathcal{X}_{i,1,:},(\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{X})_{i,1,:}\rangle

(25b)

\displaystyle=\langle x,(I_{n}\otimes M^{\top})\mymat_{M}(\mathcal{A})(I_{n}\otimes M)x\rangle,

where we use the block structure of
x
and
(I_{n}\otimes M^{\top})\mymat_{M}(\mathcal{A})(I_{n}\otimes M)
in (
25b
). The equivalence of the tensor and matricized inner products combined with Definition
3.1
and properties of PSD matrices in
[
BPT12
, Appendix A.1]
completes the bidirectional proof.
∎

3.1.2.
Semindefiniteness in the Transform Domain

Most
\star_{M}
-based algorithms are applied facewise in the transform domain, as this allows for independent matrix operations which can be parallelized.
We show below that
\mathcal{A}\succeq_{M}0
if and only if the frontal slices of
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
are PSD matrices.

Proposition 3.3

(PSD Transformed Frontal Slices)
.

Fix
M\in\Ogroup_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n\times n}
be symmetric. Then,
\mathcal{A}
is
\star_{M}
-PSD if and only if
\mathop{\rm bdiag}(\widehat{\mathcal{A}})
is a positive semidefinite matrix.

Proof.

Recall that a block diagonal matrix is PSD if and only if each block along the diagonal is PSD. The bidirectional proof proceeds as follows:

(\Longrightarrow)
:

If
\mathcal{A}
is
\star_{M}
-PSD, then
\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\rangle\geq 0
for all
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
.
Given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
k\in[n_{3}]
, construct
\mathcal{X}^{(k)}=\widehat{\mathcal{X}}^{(k)}\times_{3}M^{\top}
where

(26)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{:,:,\ell}^{(k)}=\begin{cases}x,&\ell=k\\
0,&\ell\not=k\end{cases}.

Then, by
Lemma

2.10
and (
18
),
\langle\mathcal{X}^{(k)},\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}^{(k)}\rangle=\langle x,\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{:,:,k}x\rangle\geq 0
for all
k\in[n_{3}]
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Thus,
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{:,:,k}\succeq 0
for all
k\in[n_{3}]
.

(\Longleftarrow)
:

If each frontal slice of
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
is PSD, then
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}
is
\star_{I}
-PSD by (
18
). So, for
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{M}
, we have by
Lemma

2.10
that

(27)

\displaystyle 0\leq\langle\mathcal{X},\hat{\mathcal{A}}\star_{I}\mathcal{X}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{X},(\mathcal{A}\star_{M}(\mathcal{X}\times_{3}M^{\top}))\times_{3}M\rangle=\langle\mathcal{X}\times_{3}M^{\top},\mathcal{A}\star_{M}(\mathcal{X}\times_{3}M^{\top})\rangle.

It follows that
\mathcal{A}
is
\star_{M}
-PSD.

∎

3.1.3.
Factorization of
\star_{M}
-PSD tensors

Classical factorizations for PSD matrices, such as the Cholesky factorization, are central to mathematics.
We provide a similar factorization here, starting with the case of
f
-diagonal tensors.
Recall that a diagonal matrix
D\in D_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is positive semidefinite if and only if each element along the diagonal is nonnegative, or equivalently, each element along the diagonal is a square in
\mathbb{R}
. Since the ring of tubes
\mathbb{R}_{M}
does not have an ordering, the analogous result for
f
-diagonal tensors asserts that the tubes along the diagonal are squares in the ring
\mathbb{R}_{M}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Fix an orthogonal matrix
M\in\Ogroup_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
. If
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n\times n}
is an f-diagonal tensor with tubes
\mathcal{A}_{i,i,:}=\mathbf{a}_{i}
then
\mathcal{A}
is
\star_{M}
-PSD if and only if each
\mathbf{a}_{i}
is a square in the ring of tubes
\mathbb{R}_{M}
.

Proof.

The bidirectional proof proceeds as follows.

(\Longrightarrow)
:

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is
\star_{M}
-PSD. Fix
i\in[n]
and
\ell\in[n_{3}]
and set
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1\times n_{3}}
to have tubes

(28)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{j}=\begin{cases}\tube(M^{\top}e_{\ell}),&j=i\\
0,&j\not=i\end{cases}.

where
e_{\ell}
is the
\ell^{\text{th}}
standard basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
. Note that the tubes
\mathbf{x}_{j}
are defined so that the only nonzero entry of
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
is
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{i,1,\ell}=1
.
Then,

(29)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\rangle=(M^{\top}e_{\ell})^{\top}M^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}_{i}))e_{\ell}=e_{\ell}^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}_{i}))e_{\ell}.

Since
\mathcal{A}
is
\star_{M}
-PSD, it follows that
(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}_{i}))_{\ell}\geq 0
. Because
\ell
was arbitrary, this implies that
\mathbf{a}_{i}
is a square in the ring of tubes by Lemma
2.2
.

(\Longleftarrow)
:

If each
\mathbf{a}_{i}=\mathbf{b}_{i}\star_{M}\mathbf{b}_{i}
for some
\mathbf{b}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}
, then for any
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
, we have

(30)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{a}_{i}\star_{M}\mathbf{x}_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\mathbf{b}_{i}\star_{M}\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{i}\star_{M}\mathbf{x}_{i}\rangle=\|\mathbf{b}_{i}\star_{M}\mathbf{x}_{i}\|^{2}\geq 0.

∎

By passing to the
\star_{M}
-SVD of
\mathcal{A}
, we are able to conclude that all
\star_{M}
-PSD tensors have the desired factorization. Additionally, passing to the
\star_{M}
-SVD of
\mathcal{A}
connects the decomposition
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}\star_{M}\mathcal{B}^{\top}
to the
\star_{M}
-rank of
\mathcal{A}
in a way that is analogous to PSD matrices.

Proposition 3.5

(
\star_{M}
-PSD Square Root)
.

Fix
M\in\Ogroup_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
. Then, a
\star_{M}
-symmetric tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n\times n}
is
\star_{M}
-PSD if and only if
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}\star_{M}\mathcal{B}^{\top}
for some tensor
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n\times r}
, where
r=\starMrank(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

The bidirectional proof proceeds as follows.

(\Longrightarrow)
:

From
Theorem

2.8
, the
\star_{M}

3.1.4.

One important fact about PSD matrices is that all principal minors are nonnegative. This provides a system of polynomial inequalities which describes the PSD cone.
In order to generalize this fact to tensors equipped with the

Fix

The sign of a permutation

For example, in

Note that

We now have the tools to define a

Proposition 3.6

(Square

Proof.

Let

By (

3.1.5.
Eigentubes of
\star_{M}
-PSD Tensors

A final characterization of positive semidefinite matrices is that all eigenvalues are nonnegative. However, because the rings
\mathbb{R}_{M}
have zero-divisors (nonzero elements whose product is zero), the
\star_{M}
-PSD case is more subtle. We obtain a weaker statement regarding the relationship between
\star_{M}
-PSD tensors and tubes which act analogously to eigenvalues. The spectral theory of tensors equipped with the
t
-product was developed in
[
EJRR23
]
; see also
[
GGV13
]
. Note also that a notion of spectral theory for higher order tensors which does not depend on a tensor-tensor product has been studied in the literature; see e.g.
[
QL17
]
for an overview.

Proposition 3.7
.

Fix
M\in\Ogroup_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{A}\in\myPSD_{M}^{n}
. Let
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}
and
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}_{M}^{n}
be such that
\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}=\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}
and that all frontal slices of
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
are nonzero. Then
\mathbf{\lambda}
is a square in
\mathbb{R}_{M}
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}=\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}
, then in the transform domain, we have
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\smalltriangleup\widehat{\mathcal{X}}=\widehat{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$}}\smalltriangleup\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
, where
\smalltriangleup
denotes the facewise product. It then follows that for each
k\in[n_{3}]
,

(36)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{:,:,k}\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{:,1,k}=\widehat{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$}}_{1,1,k}\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{:,1,k}.

By the assumption that all frontal slices of
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
are nonzero, this implies that
\widehat{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$}}_{1,1,k}
is an eigenvalue of
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{:,:,k}
for each
\ell\in[n_{3}]
.
By
Proposition

3.3
, the matrix
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{:,:,k}
is positive semidefinite and therefore
\widehat{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$}}_{1,1,k}\geq 0
. It therefore follows from
Lemma

2.2
that
\mathbf{\lambda}
is a square in
\mathbb{R}_{M}
.
∎

Note that the requirement that all frontal slices of
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
are nonzero is necessary for the conclusion of Proposition

to hold. For example, let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2\times 2}
and
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 1\times 2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{:,:,1}=\mathcal{A}_{:,:,2}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathcal{X}_{:,1,1}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{X}_{:,1,2}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\

Then,

, where

is the

identity matrix, and

for any

For example,

4.
Invariant SDPs and a Representation Theoretic Interpretation of the
\star_{M}
-product

A line of work in semidefinite programming, initiated by Gaterman and Parrilo
[
GP04
]
, studies semidefinite programs that are invariant under a group action.
Such invariant presentations introduce new theoretical insights and numerical efficiencies.
In particular, invariant SDPs can be naturally block-diagonalized, resulting in reduced computational cost.

In this section, we connect (
M-SDP
) to such invariant semidefinite programs.
To do so, we develop an interpretation of the
\star_{M}
-product based on the representation theory of finite groups.
Specifically, we derive conditions on a finite group
G
such that the
\star_{M}
-product is equivariant under group actions; that is, for any
g\in G
and under (left) group action denoted by
\cdot
, we have
\mathcal{A}\star_{M}(g\cdot\mathcal{B})=g\cdot(\mathcal{A}\star_{M}\mathcal{B})
for any compatibly-sized tensors
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
.
These conditions are concrete and can be interpreted in terms of the solution to a linear system involving the transformation matrix
M
.
The representation theoretic results may be of independent interest, so our presentation works in greater generality than is needed for
M
-SDP.

4.1.
Brief Background on Group Representations

To preface the discussion of invariant
M
-SDPs, we briefly review representations of finite groups (see, e.g., standard references
[
Ser77
,
FH04
]
).

4.1.1.

Let

A

2

A representation is

3

Properties of representations are sensitive to the underlying field. A representation

If

Schur’s Lemma gives places strong structure on equivariant linear transformations. Since we work primarily with matrix representatives of linear transformations, we need an appropriate choice of ordered basis to make this structure apparent.

Definition 4.2

(Symmetry-Adapted Basis)

Let

be a linear representation of a finite group and

the decomposition of

(2)

\begin{bmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}&&\\

Theorem

provides a characterization of the relationship between a group

4.3
.

Fix a finite group
G
, a representation
\rho:G\to GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
, and
M\in GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
.
Then, the multiplication map
T_{\mathbf{a}}:\mathbb{F}_{M}\to\mathbb{F}_{M}
is
\rho
-equivariant for all
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}
if and only if
M\rho(g)M^{-1}
is diagonal for each
g\in G
.

Proof.

The bidirectional proof proceeds as follows.

(\Longrightarrow):

Suppose that
M\rho(g)M^{-1}
is diagonal for each
g\in G
. Let
g\in G
and
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}
.
Then, because diagonal matrices commute, we have

(48a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}\star_{M}(g\cdot\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle\equiv\left(M^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))M\rho(g)\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{b})\right)

(48b)

\displaystyle\equiv\left(M^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))M\rho(g)M^{-1}M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{b})\right)

(48c)

\displaystyle=\left(M^{-1}M\rho(g)M^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{b})\right)

(48d)

\displaystyle\equiv g\cdot(\mathbf{a}\star_{M}\mathbf{b}).

(\Longleftarrow)
:

Conversely suppose that
T_{\mathbf{a}}
is
\rho
-equivariant for all
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}
.
Then, for any
g\in G
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}
, we have
T_{\mathbf{a}}(g\cdot\mathbf{b})=g\cdot T_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{b})
, or equivalently

(49)

\displaystyle M^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))M\rho(g)\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{b})=\rho(g)M^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{b}).

Because this holds for any
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}
, we have

(50)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))M\rho(g)M^{-1}=M\rho(g)M^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a})).

Since
M
is invertible, any diagonal
n_{3}\times n_{3}
matrix can be written in the form
\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))
.
In particular, for
\ell\in[n_{3}]
, let
E_{\ell}
be the diagonal matrix with entry
e_{\ell\ell}=1
and all other entries zero.
It follows that

(51)

\displaystyle E_{\ell}M\rho(g)M^{-1}=M\rho(g)M^{-1}E_{\ell}

for all
\ell\in[n_{3}]
.
To satisfy these
n_{3}
equations,
M\rho(g)M^{-1}
must be diagonal.

∎

For many representations and groups of interest, the matrices
\rho(g)
for
g\in G
will not be simultaneously diagonalizable. Indeed, simultaneously diagonalizable matrices commute, but the group operation in many groups is noncommutative (e.g., the symmetric group
S_{n}
for
n\geq 3
).
Thus, the multiplication map
T_{\mathbf{a}}
will only be
\rho
-equivariant for some subset of tubes.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
G
be a nonabelian group and
\rho
a faithful representation of
G
. For any
M\in\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{F})
, there exist tubes
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{M}
for which the multiplication map
T_{\mathbf{a}}
is
not

\rho
-equivariant.

Proof.

If
\rho
is a faithful representation of
G
and
g,h\in G
have
gh\not=hg
, then
\rho(g)\rho(h)\not=\rho(h)\rho(g)
. Because the matrices
\rho(g)
and
\rho(h)
do not commute, they are not simultaneously diagonalizable
and the result follows by Theorem
4.3
.
∎

Theorem
4.3
characterizes the representations
\rho
and transformation matrices

where

T_{\mathbf{a}}
are
\rho
-equivariant. In light of Corollary
4.4

Recall from Section

4.5
.

Fix a finite group
G
and a representation
\rho:G\to\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{C})
. Suppose that the decomposition of
\mathbb{C}^{n_{3}}
into irreducibles is given by

(53)

\mathbb{C}^{n_{3}}\simeq V_{1}\oplus V_{2}\oplus\dots\oplus V_{m},

where
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{j})=d_{j}
for

. Let

represent a change of basis to a symmetry-adapted basis. Then, there is a vector subspace
W_{\rho}\subseteq\mathbb{C}_{M}
for which

is
\rho
-equivariant if and only if
\mathbf{a}\in W_{\rho}
.
W_{\rho}
is given explicitly by

(54)

W_{\rho}=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}\;\middle|\;\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{x}))=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}I_{d_{1}}&&&\\
&c_{2}I_{d_{2}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&c_{m}I_{d_{m}}\end{bmatrix},\enskip c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{m}\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.

Proof.

The map
T_{\mathbf{a}}
is
\rho
-equivariant if and only if

(55)

M^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))M\rho(g)=\rho(g)M^{-1}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))M,

which is equivalent to
\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))
and
M\rho(g)M^{-1}
commuting. As a result, if
\hat{\rho}:G\to\GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{C})
is the representation with
\hat{\rho}(g)=M\rho(g)M^{-1}

\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))
is
\hat{\rho}
-equivariant. Note that since

is a change of basis to a symmetry-adapted basis, the decomposition of

into
\hat{\rho}
-irreducibles is given by subspaces spanned by standard basis vectors:

(56)

\mathbb{C}^{n_{3}}\simeq\mathop{\rm span}\nolimits\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d_{1}}\}\oplus\mathop{\rm span}\nolimits\{e_{d_{1}+1},\ldots e_{d_{1}+d_{2}}\}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathop{\rm span}\nolimits\left\{e_{i}\;\middle|\;\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}d_{j}\right)+1\leq i\leq n_{3}\right\}.

By Schur’s Lemma (Lemma

\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))
map is

-equivariant if and only if the restrictions to each

-irreducible are given by multiplication by a constant. This happens if and only if
\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))
has the desired block structure in (
54

∎

Theorem

gives explicit conditions linear conditions on the space of tubes for

to be equivariant in terms of the rows of the matrix

&\mathbbm{1}_{d_{2}}\\
&&\rotatebox{-60.0}{$\cdots$}&\\

\tube(a)\in W_{\rho}
if and only if we can find coefficients

such that

4.3.
Connection to Invariant SDPs

We now use the representation theoretic interpretation of the
\star_{M}
-product to study invariant semidefinite programs. The equality constraints in (
M-SDP
) allow one to enforce that multiplication by the tensor
\mathcal{X}
satisfies group equivariance properties. Indeed, if
\rho:G\to GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
and
M
are as in Theorem
4.5
, then the condition that multiplication is
\rho
-equivariant is a linear condition on the space of tubes and can therefore be written using equalities of the form
\langle\mathcal{A}^{(i)},\mathcal{X}\rangle=0
for some symmetric tensors
\mathcal{A}^{(i)}
. In particular, group equivariance can be encoded in the constraints of (
M-SDP
), and such cases yield invariant semidefinite programs.

4.3.1.
Invariant Matrix SDPs

Recall, a matrix SDP is the optimization of a linear functional over an affine slice of the cone of positive semidefinite matrices, which lies in the vector space

\mathbb{S}^{n}=\{Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\mid X^{\top}=X\}
of all
n\times n
real-valued symmetric matrices.
A representation
\rho:G\to GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
gives
\mathbb{S}^{n}
the structure of a representation via the action

(69)

\displaystyle g\cdot X=\rho(g)^{\top}X\rho(g).

A matrix SDP is called
invariant
if the objective function remains constant and the feasible region is fixed under group actions. That is, if
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{n}
is the feasible region and
C
is the cost matrix,
then
\Omega=g\cdot\Omega:=\{g\cdot X\;|\;X\in\Omega\}
for each
g\in G
and
\langle C,g\cdot X\rangle=\langle C,X\rangle
for each
g\in G
and
X\in\Omega
.

A central result of
[
GP04
]
is that an SDP which is invariant to a totally real representation of a group
G
can be block-diagonalized by changing basis.
Specifically, if
\rho(g)
is orthogonal for every
g\in G
, then a symmetric matrix
X
is fixed under the action of
G
if and only if
\rho(g)X=X\rho(g)
.
In this setting, the linear transformation
T_{X}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
with
T_{X}(v)=Xv
is
\rho
-equivariant.
By permuting a symmetry adapted basis as in Definition
4.2
, one obtains a basis for which
X
is block diagonalized.

4.3.2.
M
-SDPs and Invariant Matrix SDPs

The results of Section
3
imply that
M
-SDP can be viewed as a block-diagonalization of large semidefinite programs. Section
4.2
provides an interpretation of the
\star_{M}
-product in terms of representation theory. We connect these two ideas using the framework of invariant SDPs to characterize the case where these points of view are comptaible. That is, the case where the block diagonalization arising from the
\star_{M}
-product structure agrees with the structure of an invariant SDP.

4.6
.

(70)

\displaystyle\max\enskip\langle\mathcal{C},\mathcal{X}\rangle\enskip\text{s.t. }\langle\mathcal{A}^{(\ell)},\mathcal{X}\rangle=b^{(\ell)}\text{ for }\ell\in[L],\enskip\mathcal{X}\in\mathrm{PSD}_{M}^{n}\cap U

is an invariant

Proof.

(71)

\displaystyle\hat{X}

\displaystyle=(I_{n}\otimes M^{\top})\mymat_{M}(\mathcal{X})(I_{n}\otimes M).

Note that
\hat{X}
is a
nn_{3}\times nn_{3}
block matrix with blocks
M^{\top}D_{i,j}M
, where
D_{i,j}=\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{x}_{i,j}))\in\Dgroup_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
.
Now, if
\mathcal{X}\in U
, then
\mathbf{x}_{i,j}\in W_{\rho}
for each
i,j
and therefore
M^{-1}D_{i,j}M
commutes with
\rho(g)
for any
g\in G
. Since
\rho(g)^{\top}=(\rho(g))^{-1}=\rho(g^{-1})
, we have

(72)

\rho(g)^{\top}M^{\top}D_{i,j}M=M^{\top}D_{i,j}M\rho(g)^{\top}.

It then follows that for any
g\in G
,

(73)

\hat{\rho}(g)^{\top}\hat{X}\hat{\rho}(g)=\hat{X}\hat{\rho}(g)^{\top}\hat{\rho}(g)=\hat{X}.

To conclude, we need to show that if
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
is a symmetric tensor and
\hat{B}
is its matrix representative, then
\langle\mathcal{B},\mathcal{X}\rangle=\mathop{\rm trace}(\hat{B}\hat{X})
. Note that if
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
are tubes, then, because
M
is orthogonal,

(74)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle&=\langle M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}),M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{b})\rangle\\
&=\mathop{\rm trace}(\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{b})))\\
&=\mathop{\rm trace}(M^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{a}))MM^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{b}))M).\end{split}

It then follows that

(75)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle\mathcal{B},\mathcal{X}\rangle&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle\mathbf{b}_{i,k},\mathbf{x}_{k,i}\rangle\right)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathop{\rm trace}(M^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{b}_{i,k}))MM^{\top}\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{x}_{k,i}))M)\right)\\
&=\mathop{\rm trace}(\hat{B}\hat{X}).\end{split}

∎

We conclude this section with a simple example to highlight the connection between

-SDPs and invariant matrix SDPs.

5.
Applications

In this section, we describe two applications of the
M
-SDP framework. Our first application is a description of certain group invariant sums of squares polynomials. Our other application is an
M
-SDP formulation of low rank tensor completion problems. These applications highlight that the
M
-SDP framework has theoretical and computational applications analogous to those of PSD matrices.

5.1.
Application 1:
M
-SOS Polynomials

A well-known application of semidefinite programming is as a relaxation of polynomial optimization problems.
The central idea is to search for nonnegativity certificates of a polynomial
f
in the form of a decomposition of
f
into a sum of squares (SOS); that is, if
\deg(f)=2d
, one seeks degree-
d
polynomials
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}
such that
f=\sum_{i=1}^{k}g_{i}^{2}
; see, e.g.,
[
Las01
,
Par03
]
for additional details.
The maximum value of
\gamma
such that
f(x)-\gamma
is SOS is then a (possibly strict) lower bound on
\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)
.
Additionally, a polynomial
f(x)-\gamma
is SOS if and only if there is a positive semidefinite Gram matrix
Q
such that
f(x)-\gamma=\xi^{\top}Q\xi
, where
\xi
is a vector of all monomials of degree at most
d
. Searching for the maximum
\gamma
such that
f(x)-\gamma
is SOS is therefore a semidefinite programming problem.
Here, we use
\star_{M}
-PSD tensors to study a subset of SOS polynomials.

SOS relaxations for structured polynomial optimization problems were studied using the tensor
t
-product in
[
ZHH22
,
MKY24
]
and we extend to the general
\star_{M}
-product below.
The group-theoretic interpretation developed in Section
4
provides further insight into the structure of SOS polynomials defined through
\star_{M}
-products.

It will be convenient to define a reshaping operator that partitions an
nn_{3}\times 1
vector into
n
blocks of length
n_{3}
and converts the blocks into tubes of a lateral slice.
More precisely, we define
\fold_{n_{3}}:\mathbb{R}^{nn_{3}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1\times n_{3}}
such that if
v=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}^{\top}&v_{2}^{\top}&\dots&v_{n}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
, where
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
for each
i\in[n]
, then

(80)

\fold_{n_{3}}(v)=\begin{bmatrix}\tube(v_{1})\\
\tube(v_{2})\\
\vdots\\
\tube(v_{n})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1\times n_{3}}.

We now define an M-SOS polynomial using the
\star_{M}
-product and the folding operation in (
80
).

Definition 5.1

(
M
-SOS Polynomial, Gram Tensor)
.

Consider the ring of polynomials in
k
variables with real coefficients and collect all possible monomials up to degree
d
in the length
N={k+d\choose d}
vector
\xi=\begin{bmatrix}1&x_{1}&x_{2}&\dots&x_{k}&x_{1}^{2}&x_{1}x_{2}&\dots&x_{k}^{d}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
.
Let
N=nn_{3}
for some positive integers
n
and
n_{3}
and let
\mathcal{X}=\fold_{n_{3}}(\xi)
be an
n\times 1\times n_{3}
array of variables.
Given
M\in\Ogroup_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
, we say that a real polynomial
f
in
k
variables of degree at most
2d
is
M
-SOS
if there is a tensor
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathrm{PSD}_{M}^{n}
such that

(81)

f(x)=\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Q}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\rangle.

Such a tensor
\mathcal{Q}
is called a
Gram tensor
for
f
.

We note that
M
-SOS polynomials are sums of squares polynomials. Indeed, if
\mathcal{Q}
is a Gram tensor for
f
, then by Proposition
3.5
,
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{B}^{\top}\star_{M}\mathcal{B}
for some tensor
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n\times n_{3}}
. It then follows that

(82)

f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\langle B_{i,:,:}\star_{M}\mathcal{X},B_{i,:,:}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|B_{i,:,:}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}\right\|^{2},

which is a sum of squares. Alternatively, the matrix representative of
\mathcal{Q}
is PSD by Proposition
3.2
.

5.1.1.
Invariant Polynomials

There has been recent interest in sums of squares polynomials which are invariant under a group action on the variables; see e.g.,
[
GP04
,
RTAL13
,
HHS21
]
. In this setting, the theory of invariant SDPs is leveraged to better understand the SDPs which certify that an invariant SOS polynomial is a sum of squares.

Since the results of Section
4
relate the
\star_{M}
-product to invariant SDPs, we work towards an analogy in the tensor case. Specifically, Theorem
4.6
shows that if
\rho:G\to GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
is a totally real representation, then an
M
-SDP with variables in
\mathrm{PSD}_{M}^{m}
and equivariance constraints on tubes leads to an SDP invariant with respect to the representation on
\mathbb{R}^{mn_{3}}
where
g\in G
acts by
(I_{m}\otimes\rho(g))
. So, we expect that if
f
is an
M
-SOS polynomial with a Gram tensor which has tubes representing
\rho
-equivariant transformations, then
f
will be invariant under the action of
(I_{m}\otimes\rho(g))
for
g\in G
. Unwinding the Kronecker product structure, this means that
f
is invariant under the representation
\rho
acting on
groups
of variables of size
n_{3}
. While the existence of a Gram tensor satisfying equivariance constraints on tubes results in an invariant polynomial, the converse is not true without an additional assumption on the multiplicity of the irreducible representations appearing in the decomposition of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
.

5.2
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
\rho:G\to GL_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
be a totally real representation with each
\rho(g)
orthogonal, and set
M\in\mathrm{O}_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
and
W_{\rho}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{M}
to be as in the statement of Theorem
4.5
.

Consider a quadratic form in
mn_{3}
variables
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1,1},x_{1,2},\ldots,x_{m,n_{3}}]_{2}
and collect all variables in the vector
\xi=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1,1}&x_{1,2}&\ldots&x_{1,n_{3}}&x_{2,1}&\dots&x_{m,n_{3}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
.

If
f
is
M
-SOS and there is a Gram tensor
\mathcal{Q}
for
f
with tubes in
W_{\rho}
, then
f
is SOS and invariant under the action of
G
on
\mathbb{R}[x_{1,1},x_{1,2},\ldots,x_{m,n_{3}}]
given by
g\cdot\xi=(I_{m}\otimes\rho(g))\xi
. The converse holds if the multiplicity of each irreducible representation in the decomposition of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
appears with multiplicity at most one.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
4.6
, we note that if
\mathcal{Q}
is a Gram tensor for
f
with tubes in
W_{\rho}
and

(83)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}=(I_{m}\otimes M^{\top})\mymat_{M}(\mathcal{Q})(I_{m}\otimes M)

is the matrix representative of the underlying linear transformation, then
(I_{m}\otimes\rho(g))^{\top}\hat{Q}(I_{m}\otimes\rho(g))=\hat{Q}
for each
g\in G
. In particular, it follows that

(84)

(g\cdot f)(x)=((I_{m}\otimes\rho(g))\xi)^{\top}\hat{Q}((I_{m}\otimes\rho(g))\xi)=\xi^{\top}\hat{Q}\xi=f(x).

Conversely, suppose that
f
is SOS and invariant under the action of
G
. Then, there is a
(mn_{3})\times(mn_{3})
positive semidefinite matrix
Q
such that
f(x)=\xi^{\top}Q\xi
and
(I_{m}\otimes\rho(g))^{\top}Q(I_{m}\otimes\rho(g))=Q
. Denote blocks of
Q
by

(85)

Q=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{1,1}&Q_{1,2}&\dots&Q_{1,m}\\
Q_{2,1}&Q_{2,2}&\dots&Q_{2,m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
Q_{m,1}&Q_{m,2}&\dots&Q_{m,m}\end{bmatrix},

where each
Q_{i,j}
is
n_{3}\times n_{3}
. Now, for each
i,j\in[m]
and each
g\in G
,
\rho(g)^{\top}Q_{i,j}\rho(g)=Q_{i,j}
since
(I_{m}\otimes\rho(g))^{\top}Q(I_{m}\otimes\rho(g))=Q
. By hypothesis, the decomposition of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
into irreducibles is given by

(86)

\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}\simeq V_{1}\oplus V_{2}\oplus\dots\oplus V_{s},

where each
V_{i}
is unique. Since
M
corresponds to a symmetry adapted basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}}
it follows from Schur’s Lemma (Lemma
4.1
) that

(87)

MQ_{i,j}M^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}I_{d_{1}}&&&\\
&c_{2}I_{d_{2}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&c_{s}I_{d_{s}}\end{bmatrix}

where
d_{t}=\dim V_{t}
. By Theorem
4.5
, there exists a tube
\mathbf{q}_{i,j}\in W_{\rho}
such that
MQ_{i,j}M^{-1}=\mathop{\rm diag}(M\mathop{\rm{vec}}(\mathbf{q}_{i,j}))
. So, there is a tensor
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}_{M}
with

(88)

Q=(I_{m}\otimes M^{\top})\mymat_{M}(\mathcal{Q})(I_{m}\otimes M)

and such that
f=\langle\mathcal{X}_{1}^{(n_{3})},\mathcal{Q}\star_{M}\mathcal{X}_{1}^{(n_{3})}\rangle
.
∎

5.2.
Application 2: Low Rank Tensor Completion

A common application of the
\star_{M}
-product is low-rank completion of tensors by minimizing the tensor nuclear norm (e.g.,
[

,
ZN19
,
ZA17
,
ZEAHK14
]
). In this setting, we consider a
\star_{M}
-nuclear norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
which depends on the choice of orthogonal matrix
M

[
SNZ20
]
:

(95)

\|\mathcal{X}\|_{M,*}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{3}}\|(\mathcal{X}\times_{3}M)_{:,:,k}\|_{*},

where for a matrix
A
with singular values
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\sigma_{r}\geq 0
,
\|A\|_{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sigma_{j}
is the nuclear norm. It is known that the value of the matrix nuclear norm of

can be computed via an SDP (see e.g.,
[
RFP10
]

[
BPT12
, Section 2.1]
), with value given by the following primal-dual pair:

(96)

\displaystyle\max_{Y}

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm trace}(A^{\top}Y)

\displaystyle\min_{W_{1},W_{2}}\,\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathop{\rm trace}W_{1}+\mathop{\rm trace}W_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}I&Y\\
Y^{\top}&I\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}&A\\
A^{\top}&W_{2}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0

5.2.1.
M
-SDP Formulation of (
95
)

We show below that the SDP formulation of the matrix nuclear norm generalizes to compute (
95
) using an
M
-SDP.
This will allow us to formulate the tensor completion problem as an
M
-SDP.

Proposition 5.3
.

Fix an
M\in\Ogroup_{n_{3}}(\mathbb{R})
and let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
. The
M
-nuclear norm
\|\mathcal{A}\|_{M,*}
can be computed by solving the following
M
-SDP

(97)

\min_{\mathcal{W}_{1},\mathcal{W}_{2}}\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{I}_{M}&0\\
0&\mathcal{I}_{M}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{W}_{1}&\mathcal{A}\\
\mathcal{A}^{\top}&\mathcal{W}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle\quad\subjectto\quad\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{W}_{1}&\mathcal{A}\\
\mathcal{A}^{\top}&\mathcal{W}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\succeq_{M}0

for
\mathcal{W}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}
and
\mathcal{W}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
.

Proof.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{A}\times_{3}M
and let
W_{1}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{1}}
and
W_{2}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{2}}
solve the independent matrix SDPs

(98)

\displaystyle\min_{W_{1},W_{2}}\,

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathop{\rm trace}W_{1}+\mathop{\rm trace}W_{2}\right)\quad\text{s.t.}\,\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}&\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{:,:,\ell}\\
(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{:,:,\ell})^{\top}&W_{2}\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0

for each
\ell\in[n_{3}]
.
Define
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
such that
(\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{1})_{:,:,\ell}=W_{1}^{(\ell)}
for
\ell\in[n_{3}]
and let
\mathcal{W}_{1}=\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\times_{3}M^{\top}
.
Follow a similar procedure to construct
\mathcal{W}_{2}
.
It follows from
Proposition

3.3
that
\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{W}_{1}&\mathcal{A}\\
\mathcal{A}^{\top}&\mathcal{W}_{2}\end{bmatrix}
is
M
-PSD.
Moreover, the corresponding objective value is

(99a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{I}_{M}&0\\
0&\mathcal{I}_{M}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{W}_{1}&\mathcal{A}\\
\mathcal{A}^{\top}&\mathcal{W}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{I}_{M}\times_{3}M&0\\
0&\mathcal{I}_{M}\times_{3}M\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{W}_{1}&\mathcal{A}\\
\mathcal{A}^{\top}&\mathcal{W}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\times_{3}M\right\rangle

(99b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{3}}\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
0&I\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}^{(\ell)}&\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{:,:,\ell}\\
(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{:,:,\ell})^{\top}&W_{2}^{(\ell)}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle

(99c)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{3}}(\mathop{\rm trace}W_{1}^{(\ell)}+\mathop{\rm trace}W_{2}^{(\ell)})

(99d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{3}}\|\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{:,:,\ell}\|_{*}

(99e)

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{A}\|_{M,*}.

To show the
\star_{M}
-nuclear norm is indeed the solution, consider any tensors
\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}
and
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
such that
\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\prime}&\mathcal{A}\\
\mathcal{A}^{\top}&\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}
is M-PSD.
By Proposition
3.3
, any
M
-PSD tensor with block format yields feasible solutions to (
96
) by taking frontal slices in the transform domain. Moreover, the corresponding objective value satisfies

(100a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{I}_{M}&0\\
0&\mathcal{I}_{M}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\prime}&\mathcal{A}\\
\mathcal{A}^{\top}&\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{3}}(\mathop{\rm trace}(\mathcal{W}_{1}^{\prime}\times_{3}M)_{:,:,\ell}+\mathop{\rm trace}(\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\prime}\times_{3}M)_{:,:,\ell})

(100b)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{3}}\|(\mathcal{A}\times_{3}M)_{:,:,\ell}\|_{*}

(100c)

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{A}\|_{M,*}.

Thus, solving (
97
) computes
\|\mathcal{A}\|_{M,*}
.

5.2.2.
Tensor Completion

We now consider the tensor completion problem. A tensor
\mathcal{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
is
partially specified
if the entries
\mathcal{Y}_{i,j,k}
are known for
(i,j,k)\in\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{3}
and unknown
\mathcal{Y}_{i,j,k}
for
(i,j,k)\not\in\Omega
. We seek a low
M
-rank solution to the tensor completion problem, using the
M
-nuclear norm (
95
) as a proxy for rank in the objective function:

(101)

\min_{\mathcal{A}}\|\mathcal{A}\|_{M,*}\,\text{ s.t. }\,\mathcal{A}_{i,j,k}=\mathcal{Y}_{i,j,k}\text{ for all }(i,j,k)\in\Omega.

We rewrite (
101
) as an
M
-SDP:

(102)

\min_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{W}_{1},\mathcal{W}_{2}}\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{I}_{M}&0\\
0&\mathcal{I}_{M}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{W}_{1}&\mathcal{A}\\
\mathcal{A}^{\top}&\mathcal{W}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathcal{A}_{i,j,k}=\mathcal{Y}_{i,j,k}\text{ for all }(i,j,k)\in\Omega,\,\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{W}_{1}&\mathcal{A}\\
\mathcal{A}^{\top}&\mathcal{W}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\succeq_{M}0.

Note that if
\mathcal{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
, then the tensors involved in (
102
) have sizes
\mathcal{W}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{1}\times n_{3}},\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}},
and
\mathcal{W}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
.

7.
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we developed the theory of
\star_{M}
-PSD tensors and the corresponding semidefinite programming problems. Additionally, we connected the
\star_{M}
-product of third order tensors to the representation theory of finite groups, explicitly relating the choice of transformation matrix
M
and underlying group action. This connection allowed us to interpret
M
-semidefinite programming problems in terms of group invariant SDP. The framework of
\star_{M}
-semidefinite programming has applications which are analogous to those of standard semidefinite programming, and we use the framework to describe sums of squares quadratic forms and tensor completion problems. Proof-of-concept computations involving tensor completion problems suggest that the
M
-SDP framework is able to scale to larger problems than a naïve matrix SDP approach and that some problems have a non-trivial dependence on the choice of transformation matrix
M
.

In our computational experiments, we used an out of the box solver in
cvxpy

[
DB16
]
. Future work could develop specialized numerical methods for
M
-SDP which take advantage of tensor structure. Additionally, an interesting avenue to pursue is a bilevel optimization framework for problems such as tensor completion, where the inner level is an
M
-SDP and the outer level is optimization over the transformation matrix
M
.
