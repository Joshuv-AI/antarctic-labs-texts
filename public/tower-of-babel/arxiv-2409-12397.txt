Learning to Coordinate without Communication under Incomplete Information
Shenghui Chen, Shufang Zhu, Giuseppe De Giacomo, Ufuk Topcu

Abstract
Achieving seamless coordination in cooperative games is a crucial challenge in artificial intelligence, particularly when players operate under incomplete information. A common strategy to mitigate this information asymmetry involves leveraging explicit communication. However, direct (verbal) communication is not always feasible due to factors such as transmission loss. Leveraging the game Gnomes at Night TM {}^{\textit{TM}} , we explore how effective coordination can be achieved without verbal communication, relying solely on observing each other’s actions. We demonstrate how an autonomous agent can learn to cooperate by interpreting its partner’s sequences of actions, which are used to hint at its intents. Our approach generates a non-Markovian strategy for the agent by learning a deterministic finite automaton for each possible action and integrating these automata into a finite-state transducer. Experimental results in a Gnomes at Night TM {}^{\textit{TM}} testbed show that, even without direct communication, one can learn effective cooperation strategies. Such strategies achieve significantly higher success rates and require fewer steps to complete the game compared to uncoordinated ones, and perform almost as well as in the case direct communication is allowed.

Introduction

In artificial intelligence, autonomous agents either compete or cooperate with one another, reflecting the diverse nature of real-world interactions. Games are often employed as models to study these interactions, offering structured environments where the dynamics of agent behavior can be observed and analyzed.
Much of the research has focused on adversarial games, where agents act as protagonists striving to accomplish specific tasks despite the adversarial behaviors of the environment
(
Cimatti, Roveri, and Traverso 1998
;
Cimatti et al. 2003
;
Geffner and Bonet 2013
)
.
Conversely, in games requiring cooperation
(
Dafoe et al. 2021
)
, multiple agents must collaborate to achieve a shared goal. The challenge here lies in how agents effectively understand and anticipate each other’s intentions to coordinate seamlessly. An example of this is the card game Hanabi
(
Bard et al. 2020
)
, where players must communicate private information within strict constraints on what they can convey and when.
These cooperative games become particularly challenging when players have incomplete or different information. This information asymmetry, often from partial observations or a limited understanding of game dynamics, can lead to misaligned or suboptimal actions.

Such cooperative game dynamics with incomplete information typically require effective communication during decision-making, as the helper must understand the seeker’s guidance and make corresponding moves for successful coordination.
Direct communication offers a solution by enabling the exchange of relevant information between players.
Recent research has explored the use of large language models to communicate and interpret intentions and predict actions through natural language, enhancing alignment and coordination within the human-AI team

In this paper, we consider scenarios where direct verbal communication is unattainable. In
Gnomes at Night
{}^{\textit{TM}}
, this happens when the two players cannot talk to each other during gameplay. Without direct communication, the helper must rely on minimal information derived from observing the seeker’s physical movements, changes, or simple signals. This observation-based interaction raises the question: How can the helper learn to cooperate effectively with only the observed trajectory to infer the seeker’s intentions? The core challenge is in determining how the helper can deliberate on courses of action (i.e., strategies) that align with the seeker’s intentions. For instance, the seeker might express “help needed with moving up when I cannot continue” by repeating an “up-down” motion three times. The helper must analyze the seeker’s previous action sequence and strategically respond by moving the seeker up when it detects that the seeker cannot proceed, cf.
Figure

2
.

Standard strategies are often Markovian, where actions depend on the specific state of the game. However, when communication is disallowed, the helper would have no clue to understand what the seeker intends to do from a single step. Instead, it must observe the seeker’s action over time and base its own action on the observed sequence.

To capture this formally, we introduce a variant of
shared-control games

(
Chen, Fried, and Topcu 2024
)
that allows seeker multi-step dynamics and, in this framework, we learn a non-Markovian strategy for the helper that depends on (key aspects of) the history observed so far.
Specifically, we learn such a non-Markovian strategy in the form of a finite-state transducer obtained by combining several deterministic finite-word automata (DFAs), each corresponding to a specific action of the helper. Each DFA is learned using a variant of Angluin’s L* automata learning algorithm
(
Angluin 1987
)
. Then, these DFAs are combined into a finite state transducer in a polynomial way, avoiding the explicit construction of their Cartesian product, which would result in an exponential blowup in the number of helper’s actions.

We simulate gameplays with a coordination mechanism that allows the helper to infer the seeker’s intentions through observation.
To learn such automata, the helper observes sequences of seeker moves, followed by its own move. The helper bases its decisions on the seeker’s moves since its last action. The helper learns a DFA for each of its actions, which accepts sequences that align with the seeker’s intentions and rejects those that misalign with respect to that action.

We evaluate our proposed solution on
Gnomes at Night
{}^{\textit{TM}}
empirically. We implement a seeker with path-planning capabilities and memory for wall constraints. We compare the helper’s performance in our no-communication coordination approach with two other cases: a worst-case scenario where the helper does not try to coordinate at all and a best-case scenario where the helper coordinates through direct communication. We measure success rates and the number of steps to complete the game across a given number of trials and different maze configurations. We test on
9\times 9
and larger
12\times 12
mazes to assess the solution’s ability to generalize across maze sizes.
Results show that no-communication coordination improves success rates by
61.54\%
in
9\times 9
mazes and
72.84\%
in
12\times 12
mazes compared to no coordination. Its performance closely matches that of direct-communication coordination with only a
3.92\%
difference in
9\times 9
mazes and
7.48\%
difference in
12\times 12
mazes. It also reduces the number of steps needed to complete the game and decreases both the number of walls the seeker memorizes and the wall error rate, cutting both by more than half compared to no coordination.

Preliminaries

Deterministic finite-word automata.

A deterministic finite-word automaton (DFA) is represented as a tuple
\mathcal{D}=(2^{\mathrm{Prop}},Q,q_{0},\delta,F)
, where
\mathrm{Prop}
is the alphabet,
Q
is a finite set of states,
q_{0}\in Q
is the initial state,
\delta:Q\times 2^{\mathrm{Prop}}\rightarrow Q
is the transition function, and
F\subseteq Q
is a set of accepting states. Let
\sigma=\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}\in(2^{\mathrm{Prop}})^{+}
be a finite trace. The run of
\mathcal{D}
on
\sigma
is a finite sequence of states
\mathrm{run}(\mathcal{D},\sigma)=q_{0}q_{1}\cdots q_{n+1}
such that
q_{0}
is the initial state of
\mathcal{D}
and
q_{i+1}=\delta(q_{i},\sigma_{i})
for every
0\leq i\leq n
.
A finite trace
\sigma\in(2^{\mathrm{Prop}})^{+}
is accepted by a DFA
\mathcal{D}
, if the run
\mathrm{run}(\mathcal{D},\sigma)=q_{0}q_{1}\cdots q_{n+1}
ends at an accepting state, i.e.,
q_{n+1}\in F
. Given a DFA
\mathcal{D}
, we denote by
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D})
the set of traces accepted by
\mathcal{D}
.

Angluin’s L* algorithm for learning DFAs.

Automata learning, or model learning, aims to construct black-box state models by providing inputs and observing outputs. In this paper, we utilize Angluin’s L* algorithm
(
Angluin 1987
)
to learn a DFA, which works by allowing a learner to ask two types of queries to a teacher, who already has the DFA to be learned in mind.

•

Membership queries: The learner can try different traces by asking “Can trace
\sigma
be accepted by the DFA?” to have an initial idea of what the DFA looks like.

•

Equivalence queries: Once the learner has a guess (a hypothesis) of the DFA, it asks, “Is my guess correct?” If the guess is wrong, the teacher provides a counterexample that the learner can use to refine the guess.

The L* algorithm can learn DFAs by asking a polynomial number of membership and equivalence queries. Despite the development of faster algorithms, the most efficient learning methods today still follow Angluin’s minimally adequate teacher approach.

Formal Framework

Our framework models interactions using
shared-control games

(
Chen, Fried, and Topcu 2024
)
, where two players, the
seeker
and the
helper
, collectively control a single
token
to achieve a goal under incomplete information.
Any action by either player counts as a step in the game.

In the original shared-control game, the token control switches after every step. However, without communication, this step-by-step alternation is too strict since the helper cannot understand what the seeker intends to do in a single step. To overcome this, we extend this game to enable the seeker to retain control and transfer it to the helper only when support is needed. Formally, the game we consider is as follows:

Shared-control game under incomplete information with seeker multi-step dynamics.

S
and helper
H

\Gamma=(\mathcal{S},s_{\text{init}},s_{\text{final}},\mathcal{A}^{\textbf{S}},\mathcal{A}^{\textbf{H}},\mathcal{T}^{\textbf{S}},\mathcal{T}^{\textbf{H}})
, where
\mathcal{S}
is a finite set of states;
s_{\text{init}}\in\mathcal{S}
is an initial state;
s_{\text{final}}\in\mathcal{S}
is a final state;
\mathcal{A}^{i}
is a finite set of actions available to each player

;
\mathcal{T}^{\textbf{S}}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}^{i}\to\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{T}^{\textbf{H}}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}^{i}\to\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\textbf{S}}_{*}(s_{t},\mathbf{a}^{i}_{t+1})

Through this extension

5
Solution Technique

The key challenge for the helper to effectively coordinate with the seeker lies in recognizing the seeker’s behavior to determine the action the seeker expects. Since communication is disallowed, both players can only observe each other’s action sequences. The seeker expresses its intentions solely through these sequences, and the helper must reason based on them to determine its actions.
We propose an automata-learning-based approach where the helper constructs deterministic finite-word automata (DFAs) for each of its possible actions. These DFAs, termed
intent-response DFAs
, are known only to the helper and unaware by the seeker. They are constructed to recognize the intent information in the seeker’s behavior that implies a specific expected helper action.
Finally, we combine these DFAs into a non-Markovian finite-state transducer representing a helper strategy that suggests actions to assist the seeker.

Learning Helper’s Intent-Response DFAs

Note that the seeker pre-determines a policy
\pi^{\textbf{S}}
to express its intentions through action sequences while moving in a game. The helper must learn to strategically perform the actions expected by the seeker when the seeker cannot proceed. To develop a corresponding strategy
\pi^{\textbf{H}}
for the helper, we introduce an automata-learning-based technique. The key insight is that when the seeker does not need assistance, it will naturally follow the shortest path. In this case, if the action sequence taken deviates from the shortest path, the extra actions taken are interpreted as intent information. Additionally, there should be consistent patterns in this intent information when the seeker expresses the same intention. To capture this consistency, we use deterministic finite-word automata (DFAs), associating each helper action with a DFA that accepts segments of action sequences indicating that specific action. We leverage automata learning, e.g., Angluin’s L* algorithm, to obtain these DFAs.

Note that Angluin’s L* algorithm relies on membership and equivalence queries. We now explain how to realize both queries within our setting, where the seeker acts as the “teacher” and the helper as the “student.” Specifically, the helper learns multiple DFAs—one for each action—simultaneously by querying the seeker.

Membership query.

The membership queries are completed as follows: During gameplay, the seeker generates an action sequence, knowing which action it expects the helper to perform.
The helper extracts intent segments from the observed action sequence through a “capping” process and performs an action.
The seeker responds with “Yes” only if the helper performs the expected action.
Therefore, with a “Yes” response, the helper has a positive answer for the membership queries of all the segments on the intent-response DFA
\mathcal{D}^{a}
, where
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\textbf{H}}
is the action that the helper performed. Moreover, the helper also has a negative answer for the membership queries of these segments on all other intent-response DFAs. If the seeker responds with “No”, i.e., the helper performs an unexpected action, the helper then gets a negative answer for the membership queries of all the segments on the intent-response DFA associated with the performed action.

We now elaborate on how to abstract intent information from the seeker’s action sequence. By counterfactual intuition, the helper assumes that if no coordination is needed, the seeker would naturally follow the shortest path. Hence, redundancies in the sequence suggest that the seeker’s intent is embedded in segments outside this shortest path. To identify these “informative” segments, the helper first constructs a subgraph based on the seeker’s visited states and then calculates the shortest path from the previous state to the stopped state within this subgraph. Next, the helper applies a function called
Capping
to match and remove the identified shortest path, thereby isolating the informative segments. If the shortest path is empty, the function returns the original action sequence. If the shortest path is a contiguous substring, the function removes it and returns the remaining actions. Otherwise, the function returns only the non-matching actions, breaking the sequence into segments.

Equivalence query.

For the equivalence query, we do not explicitly require the seeker to compare the learned DFA with the oracle DFAs it has in mind, as the seeker’s strategy
\pi^{\textbf{S}}
inherently embeds these oracles. Instead, we relax the equivalence check by performing a fixed number of membership queries. Once this fixed number is reached, we conclude that the learned DFAs, called
intent-response DFAs
and denoted as
\textbf{D}=\{\mathcal{D}^{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}^{\textbf{H}}}
where
\mathcal{D}^{a}=\{{\mathcal{A}^{\textbf{S}}},Q,q_{0},\delta,F\}
, for each helper action
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\textbf{H}}
, are equivalent to the oracle DFAs.

These learned DFAs
D
allow the helper to recognize the seeker’s intent solely by analyzing the seeker’s action sequences. When the seeker cannot proceed, it becomes the helper’s turn to provide assistance. To do this, the helper must identify the assistance expected by the seeker through careful analysis of the action sequences produced. The DFAs enable the helper to observe the action sequence, extract informative segments, and interpret the seeker’s intent embedded within those segments.

Helper’s Strategy Construction

It is worth noting that every helper’s intent-response DFA
\mathcal{D}^{a}=\{2^{\mathcal{A}^{\textbf{S}}},Q^{a},q_{0}^{a},\delta^{a},F^{a}\}

D
are defined only based on the seeker’s actions. Consequently, as long as the seeker utilizes the same policy
\pi^{\textbf{S}}
to express its intentions, we can apply these DFAs
D
across various games that share the same action space of both players. Given a game
\Gamma=(\mathcal{S},s_{\text{init}},s_{\text{final}},\mathcal{A}^{\textbf{S}},\mathcal{A}^{\textbf{H}},\mathcal{T}^{\textbf{S}},\mathcal{T}^{\textbf{H}})

for the helper to solve Problem

6
Experimental Analysis

Since our techniques do not guarantee optimality in general, we evaluate it empirically, comparing it with the case of no coordination at all and full coordination through direct communication. More precisely, we consider three coordination types, each encompassing different levels of information exchange:

•

No coordination (NC)
: The seeker behaves as described in the previous subsection. The helper attempts to guess the seeker’s desired next action, but due to a lack of communication, its actions are essentially random.

•

Direct communication coordination (DCC)
: Both players have a clear communication channel, allowing the seeker to directly inform the helper of its desired next action, which the helper then executes on its turn.

•

No communication coordination (NCC)*
: The seeker incorporates its intended next action into its trajectory using the proposed DFA-based approach. The helper interprets the seeker’s action sequence and chooses its next action based on the perceived intent.

For a fair comparison, we use a consistent seeker implementation while varying the helper implementation accordingly.

Seeker Implementation

The seeker implementation is based on the A* algorithm to generate a path from its current position to the goal. A* algorithm is a widely used graph traversal algorithm that finds the shortest path in a graph by combining the actual distance from a start node to a goal. It selects the path that minimizes
f(n)=g(n)+h(n)
, where
g(n)
is the cost from the start node to
n
, and
h(n)
is the heuristic estimate from
n
to the goal. A* guarantees optimal and complete solutions as long as the heuristic does not overestimate the actual cost
(
Hart, Nilsson, and Raphael 1968
)
.

In particular, the seeker utilizes a modified A* algorithm (
Algorithm

2
) to generate a path that leads to the goal while minimizing wall violations and considering walls that it believes are present for its partner as hard constraints. These constraints are strictly enforced during re-planning. We show an example of such a generated path in
Figure

4
. The algorithm produces an action sequence marked with violation points, and the seeker follows the sequence until the next wall violation. The seeker then inserts intent-expressing segments to indicate the next action it desires from the helper to cross the wall. If the seeker notices the helper’s actions do not match its intended actions, it assumes an unknown wall is blocking the helper and adds this wall to its memory as a new hard constraint.

operates similarly to the standard A* algorithm, using a priority queue

The algorithm then enters a loop, selecting the path with the lowest combined cost and violations from
H

s_{\text{final}}

For each possible action, the algorithm checks if the move stays within bounds and does not violate any hard constraints
\mathcal{C}

c^{\prime}

This process continues until the algorithm either finds a valid path to the final state or exhausts all possibilities. If no valid path exists under the given constraints, the algorithm returns an empty list, indicating that the goal is unreachable.

Related Works

The problem of achieving coordination in multi-agent systems involves enabling multiple autonomous agents to work together effectively to accomplish shared goals. This problem is widely studied in fields such as distributed artificial intelligence
(
Genesereth, Ginsberg, and Rosenschein 1988
)
, swarm intelligence (stigmergy
(
Marsh and Onof 2008
)
), and game theory (correlated equilibrium
(
Aumann 1974
)
).
However, in many settings, all agents are aware of the goal. In contrast, this paper focuses on agent-agent coordination, in which only one agent possesses information about the goal.

A common approach to address these challenges under incomplete information is through explicit communication, using discrete signals as in Hanabi
(
Bard et al. 2020
)
, or natural language in negotiation and coordination games like
Deal-or-No-Deal

(
Lewis et al. 2017
;
He et al. 2018
)
,
Diplomacy

(
Paquette et al. 2019
)
, and
MutualFriends

(
He et al. 2017
)
. Recently,
Gnomes at Night
{}^{\textit{TM}}
was used to highlight the challenges of shared control under incomplete information, when leveraging natural language dialogue for communication
(
Chen, Fried, and Topcu 2024
)
. In contrast, our study examines coordination without direct communication, using a mute version of
Gnomes at Night
{}^{\textit{TM}}
.

Another approach to understanding coordination is through multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), where agents learn cooperative strategies via trial and error in complex environments, particularly through self-play and opponent modeling
(
Foerster et al. 2018
)
. However, most MARL approaches use neural networks to represent policies, which often obscure the intent inference process within the learning model. The automata-learning-based solution technique proposed in this paper provides a more explicit representation, potentially offering better explainability.

Pedestrian trajectory prediction
is another relevant problem, which uses data and models to anticipate pedestrian movements based on factors like past behavior, environmental conditions, and interactions with others. This is analogous to the helper’s task of inferring the seeker’s desired actions. Common approaches include the knowledge-based approach
(
Helbing and Molnár 1995
)
and supervised deep learning methods
(
Alahi et al. 2016
)
.

A process mirroring the challenge of helper attempting to infer the seeker’s intended actions is
plan recognition
in planning
(
Kautz, Allen et al. 1986
)
. Goal recognition involves identifying all potential goals an agent might pursue based on a sequence of observed actions
(
Vilain 1990
;
Charniak and Goldman 1993
;
Lesh and Etzioni 1995
;
Goldman, Geib, and Miller 1999
;
Avrahami-Zilberbrand and Kaminka 2005
;
Ramırez and Geffner 2009
)
. In this context, the domain is entirely visible, allowing for the calculation of possible goals that can be achieved through an optimal policy aligning with these observations. However, in our setting, the helper lacks information on the seeker’s domain. An efficient coordination could help in mutual understanding of each player’s domain. Exploring how to develop such a coordination aligns with the focus of this study.

9
Conclusion and Future Works

In this paper, we investigate how an autonomous agent (helper) can learn to coordinate with a seeker in games requiring cooperation when communication is disallowed. We propose an automata-learning-based technique that obtains a strategy for the helper by constructing a DFA for each helper action to recognize the seeker’s intentions in its behavior. Using
Gnomes at Night
{}^{\textit{TM}}
as a testbed, our experiments show that this approach nearly matches the performance of an oracle baseline with direct communication. For future work, we would like to explore an iterative version of our automata-learning-based technique that continuously refines the helper’s strategy.
Another direction is to extend our approach from standard reachability objectives to temporal objectives, which would necessitate more structured planning for both the seeker and the helper.
Additionally, we see potential in adapting this method to handle games with greater non-determinism, such as those involving human or environmental interventions.
