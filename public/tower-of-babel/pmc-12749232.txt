Analysis of toxicity and mechanisms of aspartame in kidney stones with network toxicology and molecular docking strategy
Authors: Kailiang Xu, Qiuqiu Zhang, Zhihao Shen, Jinmin Zeng, Yixiang Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aspartame, an artificial sweetener, has been implicated in the formation of kidney stones, although the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study aims to analyze the toxicity of aspartame on kidney stone development using network toxicology and molecular docking strategies. We used ChEMBL, SwissTargetPrediction, and STITCH databases to identify 145 unique targets of aspartame action, and GeneCards and OMIM databases to identify 776 unique targets related to kidney stones. Integration of these datasets revealed 19 common targets for aspartame-induced kidney stones. A Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) network was constructed utilizing the STRING database, and the topological properties of this network were evaluated using Cytoscape software. This analysis revealed five key targets: ACE, IL1B, REN, CASP3, and NOS3. To further understand the biological significance of these targets, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the DAVID database. The results indicated that core targets are primarily involved in endocrine processes, endopeptidase activity, and aspartic-type endopeptidase activity. Notably, signaling pathways such as the renin–angiotensin system (RAS) demonstrated significant associations with aspartame-related toxicity. Molecular docking analysis using Autodock Vina revealed a positive affinity between the proteins and aspartame. Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations revealed stable interactions and optimized binding conformations for all targets, suggesting significant roles for specific residues like GLU384 in ACE, ARG341 in CASP3, ARG183 in NOS3, and ARG98 in IL1B. This study successfully identifies and characterizes the key molecular targets and pathways associated with aspartame-induced kidney stones, providing a foundational understanding of the underlying biological mechanisms. Additional experiments are necessary to verify these findings and explore therapeutic approaches targeting these pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29822-5.

Introduction

The ubiquity of artificial sweeteners in modern diets necessitates a thorough understanding of their potential health impacts. Aspartame, one of the most commonly used artificial sweeteners globally, has been a subject of contention concerning its safety profile since its introduction1,2. Aspartame metabolism results in the production of phenylalanine, aspartic acid, and methanol, which have been implicated in various physiological toxicities, including oxidative stress, neurotoxicity, and metabolic disruptions3–6. Furthermore, researches have indicated that aspartame and its metabolic by-products, such as diketopiperazinecan, may induce hepatotoxicity, disrupt glucose homeostasis, and potentially increase the risk of cancers7,8. Despite the general consensus of its safety in moderation by numerous health authorities (10.2903/j.efsa.2013.3496), emerging studies suggest potential adverse effects, particularly concerning renal health9. Some studies suggest that habitual consumption of beverages containing non-nutritive sweeteners is positively associated with an increased incidence of nephrolithiasis10. Nevertheless, mechanistic or clinical investigations specifically examining aspartame-induced promotion of kidney stone formation remain scarce.

Kidney stones, or renal calculi, represent a significant health burden, with a rising prevalence globally11. This condition affects 2% to 20% of people and recurs in roughly 30% to 50% of cases over five years12,13. Notably, the prevalence of kidney stones has been shown to be increasing not only in adults but also in children14,this trend is strongly linked to contemporary shifts in dietary composition and lifestyle. Converging epidemiological and experimental evidence implicates the increasing prevalence of diets high in animal protein, sodium, and free sugars, coupled with inadequate total water intake, as pivotal modifiable determinants of this upward trajectory in stone formation15. The convergence of these factors exerts a cumulative deleterious effect on renal health in adults and poses a latent threat to the developing urinary systems in children. Consequently, enhancing public awareness and adherence to evidence-based dietary recommendations is indispensable for the primary prevention of nephrolithiasis.

Toxicological assessments have traditionally relied on animal models and in vitro studies to gauge the safety of compounds such as aspartame9. Recent research has examined the potential link between food additives, including artificial sweeteners, like aspartame, and the risk of developing kidney stones. For example, research conducted by Ferraro et al. indicated that diets high in specific sweeteners could be linked to a higher risk of developing stones16. Additionally, epidemiological studies have suggested a correlation between the consumption of diet sodas, which often contain aspartame, and an elevated risk of nephrolithiasis17. Furthermore, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis has underscored the need for more robust longitudinal studies to understand the long-term impacts of artificial sweeteners on kidney health18.

Recent advancements in computational biology offer new paradigms for toxicological evaluation. Network toxicology, an interdisciplinary approach combining toxicology with network biology, enables the holistic examination of complex biological interactions and pathways involved in toxicity19. This approach integrates multi-omics datasets—such as genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics—to reconstruct biomolecular interaction networks, thereby elucidating the toxicological mechanisms of chemical agents. Network toxicology not only facilitates the identification of pivotal toxicity-related targets and pathways but also enables the prediction of latent toxic effects, providing a theoretical framework for subsequent experimental investigations. For instance, network toxicology-driven analyses can generate hierarchical network models characterized by “multiple components, multiple targets, and multiple pathways” thereby illuminating key molecular events within adverse outcome pathways and their associated interaction networks20. Additionally, molecular docking studies provide insights into the interactions at the molecular level between toxins and biological macromolecules, elucidating potential mechanisms underlying observed toxic effects21. These approaches can more precisely identify how aspartame and its metabolites may contribute to kidney stone pathophysiology by disturbing metabolic and regulatory networks in renal cells.

The objective of our research is to combine network toxicology with molecular docking techniques to investigate the possible toxicity and mechanisms of aspartame related to kidney stone development. By leveraging computational tools, we can systematically dissect the biological networks perturbed by aspartame exposure and pinpoint critical molecular interactions that may predispose individuals to development of kidney stone. This holistic approach not only broadens our understanding of aspartame’s safety profile but also augments our capacity to predict and mitigate its potential adverse effects on renal health. This investigation will bridge existing knowledge gaps by employing a comprehensive and mechanistic approach to evaluate aspartame’s toxicological effects on the kidneys. The findings of this study could potentially inform regulatory policies and guidelines regarding artificial sweetener consumption, thereby enhancing public health protection.

Materials and methods

Collection of aspartame targets

The structural configuration of Aspartame and its Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) representation was determined via a comprehensive search for “Aspartame” within the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Leveraging the search outcomes, potential biological targets of Aspartame were predicted using databases such as ChEMBL (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/), STITCH (http://stitch.embl.de/cgi/input.pl), and SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), with the search parameters restricted to the keyword “aspartame” and the species “Homo sapiens”. For ChEMBL, targets were selected with activity values (IC50, EC50, or Ki) ≤ 10 μM. For STITCH, only interactions with a combined confidence score ≥ 0.400 were included. For SwissTargetPrediction, we retained targets with a probability score ≥ 0.1, representing moderate-to-high prediction confidence. These thresholds were chosen to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the predicted targets across different databases. The results of our search were meticulously reviewed and cross-referenced to ensure consistency and accuracy. The identifiers from ChEMBL, SwissTargetPrediction, and STITCH corresponding to the potential targets were aggregated, and any duplicates were removed. This refined list of targets was then employed to construct a repository of potential targets for Aspartame.

Collection of kidney stones targets

Targets associated with kidney stones were identified through a comprehensive search of the GeneCard (https://www.genecards.org/) and OMIM databases (https://omim.org/) using the keyword “kidney stones”. Subsequently, Venn diagrams were employed to filter and identify common potential targets between aspartame and kidney stones, with the intersecting regions considered as potential targets for aspartame.

Screening of core targets and construction of protein interaction networks

Data on potential aspartame-kidney stone links were input into STRING (v12.0) (https://cn.string-db.org/) for “Homo sapiens” with a minimum required interaction score (confidence level) of 0.400 (medium confidence), excluding disconnected nodes. This cut-off value was selected according to standard practices in network toxicology studies to balance coverage and interaction reliability.

The resulting target genes were analyzed, and the information was imported into Cytoscape (v3.10.1) (https://cytoscape.org/) to create a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. Core targets were identified using the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape based on multiple topological analysis algorithms to improve the robustness of hub gene selection. Specifically, we applied four centrality metrics—Degree, Betweenness, Closeness, and Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC)—to rank the importance of each node in the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. For each algorithm, the top 10 ranked genes were selected, and the overlapping targets among these four ranking lists were identified as the final set of hub genes. This integrative approach ensures that the identified core targets represent nodes with both high connectivity and topological significance within the PPI network. All analyses were conducted using Cytoscape version 3.10.1, and the visualization of hub genes was generated through the CytoHubba module for clarity.

GO function and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

To explore the biological functions of potential targets implicated in aspartame-induced kidney stones, we utilized the DAVID database to conduct Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses22. The GO analysis encompassed evaluations of biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) to elucidate the primary biological roles of these targets. Concurrently, KEGG enrichment analysis was employed to discern significant pathways linked to the potential targets of aspartame-induced kidney stones, with the objective of identifying key toxic pathways interacting with these targets. Additionally, we conducted GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses specifically on the core targets associated with aspartame-induced kidney stones using the DAVID database. The overarching aim of this study was to deepen our understanding of the pathways related to kidney stones by examining the targets, thereby illuminating critical signaling pathways involved in relevant biological processes. Finally, visualization techniques were applied to effectively interpret and present the findings from the GO and KEGG analyses.

Molecular docking of aspartame to core targets

To examine the binding characteristics and molecular interactions of Aspartame with target proteins, computational docking was performed using AutoDock Vina (v1.2.2)23. The ligand structure of Aspartame was acquired from PubChem Compound database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The three-dimensional coordinates of the proteins ACE (PDB ID: 1O86; resolution: 2.00 Å), REN (PDB ID: 1BIL; resolution: 2.40 Å), IL1B (PDB ID: IL1B; resolution: 2.00 Å), CASP3 (PDB ID: 1NME; resolution: 1.6 Å), and NOS3 (PDB ID: 1M9J; resolution: 2.43 Å) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (http://www.rcsb.org/pdb/home/home.do). Prior to docking, protein structures underwent preprocessing, including solvent molecule removal and polar hydrogen addition, with both ligands and receptors converted to PDBQT format. The molecular docking protocol employed 30 Å3 cubic grid boxes centered on active sites, maintaining a 0.05 nm grid spacing to ensure comprehensive sampling of ligand conformations. All simulations were executed under default parameters using the AutoDock Vina platform, with binding affinity calculations and pose analysis conducted according to established molecular docking protocols.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using the noncommercial version 2022.1 of Desmond/Maestro software (available at https://www.deshawresearch.com/downloads/download_desmond.cgi/). To prepare the systems, TIP3P water molecules were incorporated, followed by neutralization with a 0.15 M NaCl solution. After minimizing and relaxing the system, a production simulation was executed for 100 ns within an isothermal-isobaric ensemble at a temperature of 300 K and a pressure of 1 bar. Trajectory coordinates were recorded every 100 picoseconds. During the trajectory analysis, multiple structural parameters were evaluated to comprehensively assess the stability and conformational dynamics of the complexes, including root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF). These analyses were performed using the Simulation Interaction Diagram feature of Desmond, and the binding free energies were calculated with the MM/GBSA method implemented in the Schrödinger 2022.1 software suite.

These results indicate a positive affinity between the compounds and their respective targets. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

ACE-aspartame

During the simulation, the Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) of the ACE remained consistently below 3 Å, ultimately stabilizing around 2.4 Å. Similarly, the aspartame’s RMSD fluctuated initially before stabilizing within the range of 1.6–2.4 Å. These observations indicate that both the protein and the ligand exhibit minimal deviations from their initial conformations, underscoring their structural stability and robustness (Fig. 7a). The Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) values of the ACE residues involved in interactions with the aspartame (highlighted in green lines in Fig. 7b) and those of the aspartame’s atoms (Fig. 7c) are all within acceptable ranges, further supporting the stability of the complex. Throughout the simulation, various interaction forces were observed between the ACE and the aspartame (Fig. 7d). Notably, hydrogen bonds involving GLU384 were found to have formation frequencies of 88% and 99%, which exceed the 50% occupancy threshold for stable interactions (Fig. 7e). These results underscore GLU384’s pivotal role in the binding process. Additionally, hydrophobic interactions and water bridges were identified as key contributors to the binding affinity between the protein and ligand. To gain a comprehensive understanding of the binding free energy, solvation and entropy effects were also considered. Solvation effects were characterized by the formation of water bridges, which help stabilize the ACE-aspartame complex. Entropy effects were evaluated through the analysis of conformational flexibility, revealing that the ACE and aspartame maintain a favorable balance between rigidity and mobility, further enhancing the binding stability. In conclusion, the findings from the molecular dynamics simulation demonstrate that the ACE-aspartame complex exhibits high structural stability, with strong affinities between the protein and ligand. The MM/GBSA calculation for the ACE complex reveals a total binding free energy of − 43.79 kcal/mol. IL1B-aspartame

During the simulation, the RMSD of IL1B remained consistently below the 3 Å stability threshold, fluctuating within the range of 1.8–2.4 Å, indicating conformational stability. In contrast, the aspartame exhibited significant conformational deviations, with its RMSD stabilizing at approximately 21.0 Å, which exceeds the stability threshold (Fig. 8a). These observations highlight the relatively weak stability of the initial aspartame conformation compared to IL1B. Notably, the simulation revealed the formation of a more stable binding conformation based on the original structure. The RMSF values of IL1B residues involved in aspartame interactions (highlighted in green, Fig. 8b) were relatively low, indicating minor conformational changes during binding. However, the RMSF values for aspartame’s atoms were substantially larger (Fig. 8c), underscoring that the ligand experienced greater flexibility during the simulation. Throughout the simulation, various interaction forces were observed between the IL1B and the aspartame (Fig. 8d). Hydrogen bonds involving ARG98 showed formation frequencies of 41% and 42%, which, while below the 50% threshold for stable interactions, still highlight ARG98’s role in the binding process (Fig. 8e). Additionally, solvation effects (e.g., water bridges) and entropic contributions (e.g., ligand flexibility) were found to play significant roles in stabilizing the interaction, reducing the overall binding free energy. In summary, these findings demonstrate that the initial conformation was optimized through molecular dynamics simulations to achieve greater stability, with the protein and ligand exhibiting enhanced affinity under this conformation. For the IL1B complex, the MM/GBSA analysis indicates a total binding free energy of − 31.44 kcal/mol. REN-aspartame

During the simulation, the RMSD of REN remained consistently below the 3 Å stability threshold, fluctuating within the range of 1.5–2.1 Å, indicative of conformational stability. Meanwhile, the aspartame showed considerable deviation from its initial conformation, with its RMSD stabilizing at approximately 11.0 Å, which exceeds the stability threshold (Fig. 9a). This suggests moderate stability in the initial conformation of the aspartame. The simulation also demonstrated that a more stable binding conformation emerged based on the original structure. The RMSF values of REN residues involved in aspartame interactions (highlighted in green, Fig. 9b) were relatively low, implying minimal conformational changes during binding. Discussion

In this study, we have identified 19 common targets potentially involved in aspartame-induced kidney stones by integrating data from multiple bioinformatics databases. Our PPI network analysis highlighted five core targets: ACE, IL1B, REN, CASP3, and NOS3. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that these targets are primarily associated with endopeptidase activity, endocrine processes, and metabolic pathways, including the renin–angiotensin system. Molecular docking demonstrated strong affinities between aspartame and these core targets, with binding energies indicating significant interactions. Furthermore, MD simulations confirmed the stability and optimized binding conformations of these interactions, underscoring the pivotal roles of specific residues in the binding process. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that aspartame-induced nephrolithiasis is not attributable to the dysregulation of a single molecular target but arises from the synergistic perturbation of multiple critical targets and their interconnected signaling cascades. They thereby furnish a comprehensive mechanistic framework for the pathogenesis of aspartame-associated renal stone formation and nominate tractable therapeutic nodes for subsequent translational investigation.

Network toxicology is an emerging field that integrates principles from systems biology, computational modeling, and toxicology to better understand the complex interactions between various biological networks and toxic substances. Traditional toxicology often focuses on single pathways or limited sets of interactions, but network toxicology aims to capture the broader picture by examining how toxins perturb biological networks at multiple levels, including molecular, cellular, and organismal levels24. This holistic approach allows for a more comprehensive understanding of toxic effects, mechanisms of action, and potential therapeutic interventions. Aspartame is a widely used artificial sweetener that has been the subject of extensive toxicological research due to concerns about its safety. Numerous studies have investigated various aspects of aspartame’s impact on health, including its metabolic, neurological, and carcinogenic effects25–28. For instance, some studies have highlighted its nephrotoxic effects, suggesting that prolonged consumption may lead to an increase in free radical production within renal tissues, which can ultimately result in kidney damage29.

While direct research on the association between aspartame and kidney stones is limited, some studies on sweeteners and kidney-related diseases have provided indirect evidence. In research examining the relationship between added sugars and kidney stones, researchers analyzed epidemiological data from 28,303 adult men and women using data from the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning 2007–201830. Participants self-reported a history of kidney stones. The daily added sugar intake of each participant was estimated through two recalls of recent food and beverage consumption: a face-to-face interview and a telephone interview conducted 3–10 days later. The results showed that, after adjusting for a range of confounding factors (including gender, age, race or ethnicity, relative income, body mass index, Healthy Eating Index score, smoking status, and whether participants had a history of diabetes), the percentage of energy intake from added sugars was positively correlated with kidney stones. For example, participants in the highest 25% of added sugar intake had a 39% higher risk of developing kidney stones during the study period compared to others. Similarly, participants whose added sugar energy accounted for more than 25% of total energy intake had an 88% higher risk of kidney stones than those whose added sugar energy accounted for less than 5% of total energy intake. Aspartame, an artificial sweetener, is often used as a substitute for traditional sugars in foods and beverages. Although this study focused on added sugars, it to some extent suggests a potential association between sweetener intake and kidney stones. Although current data do not definitively establish a direct causal relationship between aspartame and kidney stones, these epidemiological data provide important clues for further research on the relationship between the two, and more targeted studies are still needed to clarify their association. These findings suggest that aspartame may have broader biological effects than previously understood, warranting further investigation using network-based approaches.

Upon ingestion, aspartame is hydrolyzed to yield three principal metabolites: aspartic acid, phenylalanine, and methanol. Methanol is subsequently oxidized to formaldehyde and then to formic acid, both of which are established toxicants capable of inducing oxidative stress and cellular injury31. Phenylalanine, an essential amino acid under physiological conditions, may accumulate to toxic concentrations in individuals with inborn errors of metabolism such as phenylketonuria (PKU), in which phenylalanine hydroxylase activity is deficient. Although direct nephrotoxicity attributable to phenylalanine itself has not been conclusively demonstrated in humans, sustained elevations in plasma phenylalanine could indirectly compromise renal homeostasis32. High local concentrations of aspartic acid have been shown to exert cytotoxic effects, particularly when acting synergistically with other aspartame metabolites33. While no controlled clinical trials have yet isolated the renal impact of individual aspartame metabolites in humans, the potential for chronic high-dose aspartame consumption to confer adverse renal outcomes remains a scientifically plausible concern.

The relationship between the core genes (ACE, IL1B, REN, CASP3, and NOS3) and the formation of kidney stones involves a complex interplay of regulatory mechanisms and pathophysiological processes. The ACE gene is essential for regulating blood pressure and maintaining fluid and electrolyte balance, as it is a key component of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS). The ACE gene is responsible for encoding an enzyme that converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II, a potent vasoconstrictor that increases blood pressure. The high C/A ratios observed for Aspartame with targets like ACE suggest a potential for influencing blood pressure regulation. This gene has been extensively studied for its polymorphisms, particularly the insertion/deletion (I/D) polymorphism, which has been linked to various cardiovascular, renal and metabolic conditions. For instance, studies have shown that the ACE I/D polymorphism can influence the risk of kidney stone formation by affecting the intrarenal renin–angiotensin system (RAS) activity, which is crucial in maintaining renal hemodynamics and electrolyte balance34,35. Our findings support the notion that aspartame’s impact on ACE activity may contribute to renal dysfunction and increased kidney stone risk by enhancing RAS activation.

IL1B is a cytokine that plays a central role in the inflammatory response. It is released by various cell types, including macrophages, in response to infection or injury. IL1B mediates the inflammatory cascade by upregulating other pro-inflammatory cytokines and by promoting apoptosis in renal cells36. In the context of kidney stones, IL1B has been implicated in both the initiation and propagation of kidney stone formation, as it influences the renal microenvironment and increases susceptibility to crystal deposition. IL1B’s upregulation is frequently observed in kidney stone patients, particularly in those with associated renal injury37. Our results, which show a strong binding affinity between IL1B and aspartame, suggest that aspartame may enhance inflammation within the kidney, contributing to an inflammatory milieu conducive to stone growth.

Renin is the enzyme responsible for initiating the RAS, a crucial regulator of blood pressure and fluid balance. Renin is produced by the juxtaglomerular cells located in the kidneys when there is a decrease in blood pressure, a low concentration of sodium, or activation of the sympathetic nervous system. Its primary function is to convert angiotensinogen, a protein produced by the liver, into angiotensin I. This angiotensin I is then transformed into angiotensin II through the action of ACE. Angiotensin II plays a crucial role in regulating blood pressure and fluid balance in the body38. Research has shown that mutations in the REN gene can lead to autosomal dominant tubulointerstitial kidney disease (ADTKD-REN), characterized by early-onset anemia, hyperuricemia, and progressive renal failure. These conditions can create an environment conducive to kidney stone formation due to altered renal function and metabolic imbalances39. Furthermore, studies have identified that certain REN gene polymorphisms, such as the rs1464816 polymorphism, are associated with the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD) in patients with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD), which can also predispose individuals to kidney stones due to changes in kidney structure and function40,41. Our molecular docking results confirm that aspartame interacts with renin, suggesting that aspartame may exacerbate kidney stone formation by activating the renin–angiotensin pathway.

CASP3, or caspase-3, is a central effector of apoptosis, the process of programmed cell death. It is activated in response to cellular stress, injury, and damage, playing a critical role in maintaining cellular homeostasis42. During the progression of cancer, particularly in the context of gastric cancer development, the apoptotic pathway frequently becomes dysregulated, a characteristic commonly observed across various malignant tumors. The inhibition of caspase-3 (CASP3) activity can result in an increased resistance of gastric cancer cells to specific chemotherapeutic agents, thereby diminishing the efficacy of treatment43. Furthermore, the intricate interplay between tumor cells and their microenvironment—including adjacent non-tumor cells, the extracellular matrix, and signaling molecules—may be influenced by aberrant CASP3 function44. A study on the Chinese Han population demonstrated that certain genetic variants involved in the crystallization pathway, which includes genes like CASP3, are associated with calcium nephrolithiasis, a common type of kidney stone45. Furthermore, the role of CASP3 in apoptosis suggests that it might influence the cellular processes that contribute to stone formation, such as the death of renal tubular cells, which can lead to the release of cellular debris that acts as a nidus for stone formation46. Our findings align with this hypothesis, as molecular dynamics simulations revealed a significant binding affinity between aspartame and CASP3, indicating a role for caspase-mediated cellular stress in kidney stone formation.

NOS3, commonly referred to as endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), is crucial for the synthesis of nitric oxide (NO) within endothelial cells. Nitric oxide is essential for several physiological processes, including vasodilation, which helps regulate blood vessel diameter and blood flow. Additionally, NO is vital for maintaining renal blood flow and offers protective effects against endothelial damage, thereby contributing to overall cardiovascular health47. NO also inhibits renal crystal aggregation, which is a key factor in preventing kidney stone formation48. Recent studies have suggested that genetic polymorphisms in the NOS3 gene may influence the susceptibility to kidney stone formation. For instance, polymorphisms such as G894T and T-786C in the NOS3 gene have been investigated for their potential role in renal pathophysiology, including their impact on kidney stone disease49,50. Our findings suggest that aspartame may impair NOS3 function, potentially disrupting renal hemodynamics and contributing to an environment that promotes kidney stone formation.

These targets converge on four key processes: (1) RAS dysregulation (ACE/REN), which disrupts electrolyte balance and renal perfusion. One of the key mechanisms by which the RAS may contribute to nephrolithiasis is through its effect on calcium homeostasis. High dietary sodium intake has been shown to suppress aldosterone, a component of the RAS, which in turn decreases serum calcium levels and increases urinary calcium excretion, thereby elevating the risk of kidney stone formation51. This relationship underscores the importance of dietary sodium restriction as a preventive measure for kidney stones. Furthermore, the activation of the RAS in conditions such as hyperuricemia has been suggested to mimic the effects of xanthine oxidase inhibitors, which are known to reduce uric acid levels and potentially influence stone formation52. (2) IL1B-mediated inflammation alters the renal microenvironment to promote crystal adhesion. Several studies have highlighted the role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in this process, which is activated by oxidative stress and contributes to the release of IL1B and other inflammatory cytokines, thereby exacerbating renal damage and promoting stone formation53–55. (3) CASP3-induced apoptosis. Apoptosis, a form of programmed cell death, is crucial in maintaining cellular homeostasis and is mediated by various caspases, including CASP3. In the context of nephrolithiasis, CASP3-related apoptosis has been implicated in the pathogenesis of kidney stones, particularly calcium oxalate (CaOx) nephrolithiasis, through its role in oxidative stress and inflammation56. (4) NOS3 dysfunction exacerbates crystal aggregation. Dysfunction in NOS3 can lead to reduced NO bioavailability, which has been implicated in various renal pathologies, including nephrolithiasis. The provided reference on the role of reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation in calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis offers valuable insights into the inflammatory pathways that may intersect with NOS3 dysfunction to exacerbate kidney stone formation57. Overall, the dysregulation of these core genes highlights their significant roles in the multifactorial nature of kidney stone disease, showcasing the interconnected pathways that contribute to the disease’s complex pathology.

The molecular interaction between aspartame and core proteins involved in kidney stone formation, as well as other biological functions, has been extensively studied through molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. These computational techniques provide valuable insights into the binding affinity, stability, and dynamic behavior of protein–ligand interactions, which are critical for understanding the mechanistic pathways of aspartame’s toxicity. Molecular docking results have revealed a positive affinity between the core proteins and aspartame, with binding energies ranging from − 4.299 to − 7.264 kcal/mol. For example, aspartame exhibits a particularly strong binding affinity with NOS3 (− 7.264 kcal/mol), suggesting a potential disruption in nitric oxide production and an impact on renal blood flow25. Similarly, docking results with ACE and REN indicated moderate binding affinities, implying that aspartame may interfere with these proteins’ functions in the renin–angiotensin system, which regulates blood pressure and fluid balance, potentially contributing to kidney stone formation41. This interaction with key proteins emphasizes aspartame’s potential to modulate vascular and renal functions, which are critical for maintaining kidney health.

MD simulations further explored the dynamic nature of the interactions between aspartame and the core proteins identified in the docking studies. MD simulations provide insights into the stability and conformational changes of the protein–ligand complexes over time. For instance, the interaction between aspartame and ACE showed that the ligand–protein complex remained stable throughout the simulation period, with the Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) stabilizing at around 2.4 Å, indicating that the binding was highly stable. This stability suggests that aspartame could potentially affect ACE’s enzymatic activity, disrupting its role in regulating blood pressure and fluid homeostasis, which may contribute to kidney dysfunction and stone formation. The MD simulations also revealed key conformational changes in the core proteins upon aspartame binding. For instance, in the case of CASP3, aspartame binding induced significant structural rearrangements, which could activate the protein’s apoptotic function. This finding is consistent with previous studies linking apoptosis to kidney stone formation, where excessive cell death and tissue remodeling promote stone deposition58. Similarly, the interaction between aspartame and IL1B revealed that binding could modulate the inflammatory response, with potential implications for kidney inflammation and stone formation. The ability of aspartame to induce conformational changes in these proteins highlights its potential to disrupt normal protein function, further supporting its role in kidney stone pathogenesis.

In summary, the findings of this study carry the following key clinical implications: the core targets (ACE, IL1B, REN, CASP3, and NOS3) and their associated pathways (e.g., the renin–angiotensin system) provide actionable candidates for developing preventive or therapeutic interventions against aspartame-induced kidney stones. For example, targeting ACE or REN to modulate the renin–angiotensin system could help restore renal hemodynamics and electrolyte balance, thereby reducing lithogenic risk. By clarifying the mechanistic links between aspartame and kidney stone formation, the results may contribute to refining dietary recommendations for artificial sweetener consumption, particularly for individuals at high risk of nephrolithiasis (e.g., those with a history of kidney stones or renal dysfunction). The identified core targets (e.g., ACE polymorphisms, NOS3 variants) could serve as biomarkers to identify populations susceptible to aspartame-induced renal toxicity, enabling personalized risk assessment and early intervention.

According to the recommendations of the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), the Acceptable Daily Intake (ADI) of aspartame is set at 40 mg/kg body weight. For instance, an adult weighing 60 kg should not exceed an intake of 2400 mg of aspartame per day. In practical consumption, aspartame is primarily used in low-sugar beverages and foods, with a typical content of approximately 180 mg of aspartame in a 330-ml can of diet beverage. Therefore, an adult would need to consume approximately 13 cans of such beverages daily to reach the upper limit of the ADI2. Therefore, for the majority of individuals, the daily intake of aspartame falls within a relatively safe dose range. However, there are inter-individual differences in the metabolic capacity for aspartame, which may affect its concentration and action in the body. For instance, certain individuals may be more sensitive to aspartame due to genetic factors or health conditions. Thus, if one were to investigate the dose–response relationship of aspartame in inducing the aforementioned key target-related mechanisms, it would be necessary to take into account individual differences and potentially conduct long-term clinical studies for validation.

Despite the intriguing findings and their implications, this study is not without limitations. One significant limitation is the reliance on computational analyses to infer biological interactions. While network analysis and molecular docking provide valuable insights, they are inherently predictive and cannot substitute for empirical validation. Experimental studies are essential to confirm the identified pathways and targets’ roles in aspartame-induced nephrolithiasis. Additionally, this study focused on a limited number of targets and did not account for potential genetic variability in individual responses to aspartame. Genetic polymorphisms, particularly in enzymes involved in metabolic pathways, could influence susceptibility to aspartame’s effects and subsequent kidney stone formation59. Moreover, the direct translation of these simulation results into physiological effects in the real world necessitates the consideration of multiple factors, including individual differences, metabolic rates, and the impact of long-term exposure. While our simulation results indicate that aspartame may promote the formation of kidney stones by affecting specific targets, the actual physiological effects may vary depending on an individual’s metabolic capacity and intake dosage. Future research should incorporate diverse methodologies, including in vitro and in vivo studies, to comprehensively understand how aspartame influences renal health and disease.

Conclusion

This study employed a thorough bioinformatics approach to elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms underlying aspartame-induced kidney stone formation. Key mechanistic evidence suggests that RAS may act as a pivotal pathway in aspartame-induced nephrolithiasis. Dysregulation of RAS can promote kidney stone formation by perturbing electrolyte homeostasis and impairing renal perfusion. The practical significance of these findings lies in their ability to provide a foundational molecular framework that links aspartame to kidney stone formation and offer promising targets for future intervention strategies, including preventive measures and therapeutic development. Nonetheless, it is necessary to conduct additional experimental validation to verify these results and gain a deeper insight into the mechanisms involved.
