Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate a parabolic-ODE SIS epidemic model with no-flux boundary conditions in a heterogeneous environment. The model incorporates a saturated infection mechanism S ​ I / ( m ⁡ ( x ) + S + I ) {SI}/(m(x)+S+I) with m ≥ , ≢ 0 m\geq,\,\not\equiv 0 . This study is motivated by disease control strategies, such as quarantine and lockdown, that limit population movement. We examine two scenarios: one where the movement of the susceptible population is restricted, and another where the movement of the infected population is neglected. We establish the long-term dynamics of the solutions in each scenario. Compared to previous studies that assume the absence of a saturated incidence function (i.e., m ≡ 0 m\equiv 0 ), our findings highlight the novel and significant interplay between total population size, transmission risk level, and the saturated incidence function in influencing disease persistence, extinction, and spatial distribution. Numerical simulations are performed to validate the theoretical results, and the implications of the results are discussed in the context of disease control and eradication strategies. Keywords: Parabolic-ODE SIS epidemic model, saturated incidence mechanism, long-term dynamics, disease persistence/extinction. MSC 2020: 35M13, 35B40, 92D25.



1.
Introduction

Traditional epidemic models often assume a well-mixed environment: every individual in a certain area has an equal chance of interacting with any other individual. This simplifies the modeling process by adopting ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to describe disease transmissions
[
30
]
. However, this assumption frequently fails to accurately represent real-world situations, especially in geographically fragmented populations. In such cases, individual interactions are naturally constrained by geographical barriers, even though movement between regions remains possible. This mobility introduces spatial heterogeneity into disease transmission dynamics, leading to region-specific variations in transmission patterns and population characteristics
[
62
]
. The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the crucial importance of understanding environmental heterogeneity to fully comprehend the complex dynamics of disease transmissions
[
6
,
7
,
40
]
. This pandemic has also demonstrated the significant influence of population movement on disease spread
[
29
,
14
]
. Additionally, it has also shown the effectiveness of disease control measures such as lockdown and quarantines in reducing disease transmissions.

To address the complexities arising from spatial heterogeneity, researchers have complemented the study of traditional ODE models with investigations of reaction-diffusion equations. This approach provides a more comprehensive framework by accounting for continuous spatial and temporal variations, enabling a more accurate representation of disease spread across heterogeneous environments
[
47
]
. These systems have become increasingly popular in recent studies
[
19
]
.
Building on this conceptual framework, Allen et al.
[
1
]
conducted a pioneering work on a reaction-diffusion SIS (susceptible-infected-susceptible) epidemic model with frequency-dependent incidence function
SI/(S+I)
in spatially heterogeneous environment:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{t}S-d_{S}\Delta S=-\frac{\beta(x)SI}{S+I}+\gamma(x)I,&x\in\Omega,\ t>0,\\
\displaystyle\partial_{t}I-d_{I}\Delta I=\frac{\beta(x)SI}{S+I}-\gamma(x)I,&x\in\Omega,\ t>0,\\
\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}S=\partial_{\nu}I=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\ t>0,\\
S(0,x)=S_{0}(x)\geq 0,\ I(0,x)=I_{0}(x)\geq 0,&x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

(1.1)

In this model, the domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n\geq 1
) is bounded with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
,

\nu
is the outward unit normal to
\partial\Omega
,
\beta,\,\gamma\in C(\bar{\Omega})
, and
\beta,\,\gamma>0
on
\bar{\Omega}
.
The variables
S(t,x)
and
I(t,x)
denote the densities of susceptible and infected individuals at time
t
and location
x
, respectively. The positive constants
d_{S}
and
d_{I}
represent the mobility rates of susceptible and infected individuals. The functions
\beta(x)
and
\gamma(x)
are the rates of disease transmission and recovery. The homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions ensure no population flux across the boundary. Inspired by the foundational work of Allen et al.
[
1
]
, an extensive body of research has emerged focusing on reaction-diffusion epidemic models; see

Diffusive SIS epidemic models with a modified frequency-dependent infection mechanism, represented as
{SI}/{(m+S+I)}
, have garnered considerable research interest recently
[
2
,
12
,
13
,
24
]
. This model incorporates a nonnegative saturated incidence function
m
to account for the limitations on the number of effective contacts an individual can make. These limitations are influenced by various factors, including population density, spatial distribution, and time constraints. Furthermore,
m
may represent a subpopulation with natural resistance to specific infections, potentially due to genetic mutations or other biological factors. This approach has been particularly influential in studies by Diekmann and Kretzschmar
[
20
]
and Roberts
[
55
]
. For example, a notable COVID-19 human challenge trial
[
28
]
demonstrated that 16 out of 36 participants inoculated with SARS-CoV-2 did not become infected. Such natural resistance has also been observed in other infectious diseases, such as HIV/AIDS, malaria, and norovirus, underscoring the importance of incorporating these factors into epidemic modeling. Additional studies, such as those by Anderson and May
[
3
]
and Heesterbeek
[
26
]
, further emphasize the significance of considering variable susceptibility and resistance in understanding epidemic dynamics.

Specifically, the authors in
[
24
]
explored the following SIS model featuring a saturated incidence function in a spatially heterogeneous environment:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{t}S-d_{S}\Delta S=-\frac{\beta(x)SI}{m(x)+S+I}+\gamma(x)I,&x\in\Omega,\ t>0,\\
\displaystyle\partial_{t}I-d_{I}\Delta I=\frac{\beta(x)SI}{m(x)+S+I}-\gamma(x)I,&x\in\Omega,\ t>0,\\
\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}S=\partial_{\nu}I=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\ t>0,\\
S(0,x)=S_{0}(x)\geq 0,\ I(0,x)=I_{0}(x)\geq 0,&x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

(1.2)

Here, the saturated incidence function
m\in C(\bar{\Omega})
satisfies
m\geq 0
and
m\not\equiv 0
, and
all other parameters and state variables have the same epidemiological meanings as in (
1.1
) (the authors also considered the scenario where parameters
\beta
,
\gamma
, and
m
are time-periodic).

Taking into account the realistic implications, it is assumed that the initial data
{S}_{0}
and
I_{0}
are both nonnegative continuous functions on
\bar{\Omega}
with
I_{0}\geq,\not\equiv 0
. Thus, by the standard theory for parabolic equations, (
1.2
) admits a unique classical solution
S,I\in C^{1,2}((0,\infty)\times\bar{\Omega}
). Moreover, the strong maximum principle and Hopf boundary Lemma for parabolic equations ensure that
S(t,x)>0
and
I(t,x)>0
for all
x\in\bar{\Omega}
and
t\in(0,\infty)
.

Adding the first two equations in (
1.2
) and integrating the resulting equation over
\Omega
, one can derive that:

\int_{\Omega}(S(t,x)+I(t,x))\,\textrm{d}x\equiv\int_{\Omega}(S_{0}(x)+I_{0}(x))\,\textrm{d}x=:N,\quad\forall t\geq 0,

(1.3)

indicating that the total population
N
is conserved. Throughout this paper,
N
is supposed to be a fixed positive constant.

In
[
24
]
, the authors analyzed the threshold dynamics of the model (
1.2
), establishing conditions for disease extinction and persistence based on the basic reproduction number. They demonstrated the global attractivity of both the disease-free equilibrium and the endemic equilibrium by constructing suitable Lyapunov functions under certain conditions. Additionally, they determined the spatial distribution of the disease when the dispersal rates
d_{S}
or/and
d_{I}
is sufficiently small.
Compared to previous works on model (
1.1
), their results suggest that the presence of a saturation effect reduces the transmission risk of the disease. This effect enables the total population size to significantly influence disease dynamics, potentially leading to substantial alterations in the spatial distribution of the disease under specific conditions.

Quarantine and lockdown measures significantly limit population movement, reducing individual interactions and transmission opportunities. In the diffusive SIS models, this is reflected by reducing the movement rates
d_{S}
of the susceptible population and

d_{I}
of the infected population. Formally, when the movement rate
d_{S}
is controlled to approach zero, system (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) reduces to the following parabolic-ODE coupled system:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{t}S=\gamma(x)I-\frac{\beta(x)SI}{m(x)+S+I},&x\in\Omega,\,t>0,\\
\displaystyle\partial_{t}I=d_{I}\Delta I-\gamma(x)I+\frac{\beta(x)SI}{m(x)+S+I},&x\in\Omega,\,t>0,\\
\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}I=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\,t>0,\\
\displaystyle S(0,x)=S_{0}(x)\geq 0,\ \ I(0,x)=I_{0}(x)\geq 0,&x\in\Omega,\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(S(t,x)+I(t,x))dx=N,&t\geq 0.\end{cases}

(1.4)

Similarly, as the movement rate
d_{I}
of the infected individuals approaches zero, system (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) reduces to the following parabolic-ODE system:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{t}S=d_{S}\Delta S+\gamma(x)I-\frac{\beta(x)SI}{m(x)+S+I},&x\in\Omega,\,t>0,\\
\displaystyle\partial_{t}I=-\gamma(x)I+\frac{\beta(x)SI}{m(x)+S+I},&x\in\Omega,\,t>0,\\
\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}S=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\,t>0,\\
\displaystyle S(0,x)=S_{0}(x)\geq 0,\ \ I(0,x)=I_{0}(x)\geq 0,&x\in\Omega,\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(S(t,x)+I(t,x))dx=N,&t\geq 0.\end{cases}

(1.5)

In
[
41
,
57
]
, the authors investigated the long-term dynamics of both models (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) when
m\equiv 0
. The primary aim of this paper is to address (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) when
m\not\equiv 0
and to understand how the saturated incidence function
m
affects disease persistence, extinction, and spatial distribution. It turns out that many techniques in
[
57
]
cannot be used to investigate (
1.4
) and (
1.5
). Therefore, novel analytical techniques are needed to overcome the inherent mathematical challenges.

Our results demonstrate that the saturated incidence function
m
, combined with the transmission risk level—determined by the difference between disease transmission and recovery rates—and the total population size, plays a crucial role in the global dynamics of (
1.4
) and (
1.5
). In future work, we plan to rigorously verify that the solution to (
1.2
) converges to that of (
1.4
) as
d_{S}\to 0
and to (
1.5
) as
d_{I}\to 0
, respectively. We will provide a more detailed discussion of our theoretical results and their implications in the discussion section, highlighting the critical insights gained from our analysis. We would like to mention that the works
[
8
,
9
]
employ another approach to investigate the impact of population movement constraints on disease spread; they studied the concentration phenomenon in an epidemic model by establishing an asymptotic shape of the stationary solution for a small diffusion parameter (but not completely zero).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present the main theoretical results for both systems (
1.4
) and (
1.5
). Section 3 is dedicated to the proofs of the main results for system (
1.4
), and the proofs of the results for system (

Since the constant
c_{*}
in (
3.47
) is independent of any sequence,
\omega(I)
contains only one positive number. Since
\omega(I)
is connected,
\omega(I)=\{c_{*}\}
and
\|I(t,\cdot)-c^{*}\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. Recalling that

\partial_{t}S=(\beta-\gamma)\Big[\frac{\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}(m+I)-S\Big]\frac{I}{m+S+I},\quad t>0,x\in\bar{\Omega},

by a simple perturbation argument, we can establish that

\|S(t,\cdot)-\frac{\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}(m+c_{*})\|_{\infty}\to 0\ \ \text{as}\ t\to\infty.

Therefore, (ii) holds in this case.

Case 2:

\omega(I)=\{0\}
.

In this case, we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}=0.

(3.48)

It remains to show the convergence of
S
and proves (i). Observe that
S(t,x)
satisfies

\partial_{t}S=(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))\Big[\frac{\gamma(x)(m(x)+I(t,x))}{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}-S(t,x)\Big]\frac{I(t,x)}{m(x)+S(t,x)+I(t,x)}.

(3.49)

By the comparison principle for ODEs, for any given
t_{0}>0
,

S(t,x)\geq\min\Big\{\frac{\gamma(x)m(x)}{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)},S(t_{0},x)\Big\}>0,\quad\forall\,t>t_{0}>0,\ x\in\bar{\Omega}.

Therefore, we have

\liminf_{t\to\infty}S(t,x)>0,\quad\forall\,x\in M^{+}.

(3.50)

For simplicity, let us define

J:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}dt.

Subcase 2-1:

J<\infty
.

By the equation of
S
in (
1.4
),

\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\partial_{t}S(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}dt\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big\|\gamma-\frac{\beta S}{m+S+I}\Big\|_{\infty}\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}dt\leq(\|\gamma\|_{\infty}+\|\beta\|_{\infty})J<\infty.

Hence, there holds

S(t,\cdot)\to S_{0}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{t}S(t,\cdot)dt=:S^{*}\ \ \mbox{in}\ C(\bar{\Omega})\ \,\mbox{ as}\ t\to\infty.

Clearly,
S^{*}\in C^{+}(\bar{\Omega})
. By (
3.50
), we have
S^{*}>0
on
M^{+}
.

Subcase 2-2:

J=\infty
.

Fix
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
. By (
3.48
), there is
t_{\varepsilon}\gg 1
such that

\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon,\quad\forall\,t>t_{\varepsilon}.

(3.51)

By the equation of
S
in (
1.4
),

\partial_{t}S(t,x)\geq\Big[\frac{\gamma(x)(m(x)-\varepsilon)}{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}-S(t,x)\Big]\frac{(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))I(t,x)}{m(x)+S(t,x)+I(t,x)},\quad\forall\,t>t_{\varepsilon},\,x\in\bar{\Omega}.

We can now invoke the comparison principle for ODEs to conclude that

S(t,x)\geq\frac{\gamma(x)(m(x)-\varepsilon)}{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}+\Big[S(t_{\varepsilon},x)-\frac{\gamma(x)(m(x)-\varepsilon)}{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}\Big]e^{-\int_{t_{\varepsilon}}^{t}\frac{(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))I(s,x)}{{m(x)+S(s,x)+I(s,x)}}ds}

(3.52)

for all
t>t_{\varepsilon},\,x\in\bar{\Omega}
.

Since
J=\infty
, it follows from (
3.2
), (
3.3
) and (
3.8
) that

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}\int_{t_{\varepsilon}}^{t}\frac{I(s,x)}{{m(x)+S(s,x)+I(s,x)}}ds\geq\frac{1}{(\|m\|_{\infty}+M+\bar{S}^{*})c_{1}}\int_{t_{\varepsilon}}^{t}\|I(s,\cdot)\|_{\infty}ds\to\infty

(3.53)

as
t\to\infty
. Therefore, by (
3.8
) and (
3.52
), we have

\liminf_{t\to\infty}S(t,x)\geq\frac{\gamma(x)(m(x)-\varepsilon)}{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}\quad\text{uniformly on }\ x\in\bar{\Omega}.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we obtain that

\liminf_{t\to\infty}S(t,x)\geq\frac{\gamma(x)m(x)}{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}\quad\text{uniformly on}\ x\in\bar{\Omega}.

(3.54)

Similarly,

\partial_{t}S(t,x)\leq\Big[\frac{\gamma(x)(m(x)+\varepsilon)}{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}-S(t,x)\Big]\frac{(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))I(t,x)}{m(x)+S(t,x)+I(t,x)},\quad\forall\,t>t_{\varepsilon}.

We can now invoke the comparison principle for ODEs to conclude that

S(t,x)\leq\frac{\gamma(x)(m(x)+\varepsilon)}{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}+\Big[S(t_{\varepsilon},x)-\frac{\gamma(x)(m(x)+\varepsilon)}{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}\Big]e^{-\int_{t_{\varepsilon}}^{t}\frac{(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))I(s,x)}{{m(x)+S(s,x)+I(s,x)}}ds}

for all
t>t_{\varepsilon},\,x\in\bar{\Omega}
. Using the above inequality, we can show

\limsup_{t\to\infty}S(t,x)\leq\frac{\gamma(x)m(x)}{\beta(x)+\gamma(x)}\quad\text{uniformly on}\ x\in\bar{\Omega}.

Combining this with (
3.54
), we obtain

\lim_{t\to\infty}S(t,x)=\frac{\gamma(x)m(x)}{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}=:S^{*}\quad\text{uniformly on}\ x\in\bar{\Omega}.

This finishes the proof for Subcase 2-2.

Finally, it is easy to see that if
N\leq\int_{\Omega}\frac{m\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}
, then Case 2 happens and (i) holds.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

Suppose that
\beta>\gamma
on
\bar{\Omega}

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}U=(I-U)F_{1}(t,x),&x\in\bar{\Omega},\ t>0,\cr\partial_{t}I=d_{I}\Delta I+(U-I)F_{2}(t,x),&x\in\Omega,\ t>0,\cr\partial_{\nu}I=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\ t>0,\end{cases}

(3.55)

where

F_{1}(t,x)=\frac{(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))I(t,x)}{m(x)+S(t,x)+I(t,x)}>0,\ \ F_{2}(t,x)=\frac{\gamma(x)I(t,x)}{m(x)+S(t,x)+I(t,x)}>0

for all
t>0
and
x\in\bar{\Omega}
.

(i) Suppose that
S_{0}\geq\frac{m\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}
. Then,
U(0,x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\bar{\Omega}
. Since
I(t,\cdot)>0
on
\bar{\Omega}
for all
t>0

3.10
) and the comparison principle for ODEs that
U(t,\cdot)>0
on
\bar{\Omega}
for all
t>0
. Let

c(t)=\min\{\min_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}I(t,x),\ \min_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}U(t,x)\}>0,\quad\forall\,t>0.

For every
t_{0}>0
, the constant function
(\underline{U}(t,x),\underline{I}(t,x))=(c(t_{0}),c(t_{0}))
,
t>0
, solves the cooperative system (
3.55
) and satisfies

\underline{U}(t_{0},x)\leq U(t_{0},x)\quad\text{and}\quad\underline{I}(t_{0},x)\leq I(t_{0},x),\quad x\in\bar{\Omega}.

Therefore, by the comparison principle for cooperative systems, we have

c(t_{0})=\underline{U}(t,x)\leq U(t,x)\quad\text{and}\quad c(t_{0})=\underline{I}(t,x)\leq I(t,x),\quad\forall\,t>t_{0},\ x\in\bar{\Omega}.

Hence,

for all
t\geq t_{0}>0

\inf_{t>t_{0}}\min_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}I(t,x)\geq c(t_{0})>0,\quad\forall\,t_{0}>0.

So
\liminf_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)>0
uniformly for
x\in\bar{\Omega}
. Consequently, Theorem
2.1
-(ii) holds.

(ii) Suppose that
S_{0}\leq\frac{m\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}
and
\|I_{0}\|_{\infty}<\frac{N-\int_{\Omega}\frac{m\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}}{\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}}
. Define

\hat{U}(t,\cdot)=\|I_{0}\|_{\infty}-U(t,\cdot)\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{I}(t,\cdot)=\|I_{0}\|_{\infty}-I(t,\cdot),\quad t\geq 0.

Then
(\hat{U}(t,x),\hat{I}(t,x))
solves the cooperative system (
3.55
). Observe also that

\hat{I}(0,\cdot)\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{U}(0,\cdot)=\|I_{0}\|_{\infty}-\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}\Big(S_{0}-\frac{m\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}\Big)>0.

Therefore, by the comparison principle for cooperative systems,

\hat{U}(t,\cdot)>0
and
\hat{I}(t,\cdot)>0
for all
t>0
.

As a result, we find that

S(t,\cdot)=\frac{\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}[U(t,\cdot)+m]=\frac{\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}[\|I_{0}\|_{\infty}-\hat{U}(t,\cdot)+m]<\frac{\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}(\|I_{0}\|_{\infty}+m),\quad\forall\ t>0.

This shows that, for all

,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}I(t,x)=

\displaystyle N-\int_{\Omega}S(t,x)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle N-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}(m+\|I_{0}\|_{\infty})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{N-\int_{\Omega}\frac{m\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}}{\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}}-\|I_{0}\|_{\infty}\right)\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{\beta-\gamma},

which along with (
3.45
) gives that

\liminf_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\geq\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\left(\frac{N-\int_{\Omega}\frac{m\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}}{\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}}-\|I_{0}\|_{\infty}\right)\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}>0.

Hence, Theorem
2.1
-(ii) holds.

Next we will present the proof for Theorem

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

We first prove the assertion (i). To the end, we pick an interior point
x_{0}
of
H^{0}\cap M^{+}
. Then there exists a closed ball
B\subset H^{0}
centered at
x_{0}
such that
m>0
on
B
.

As in the proof of Lemma
3.3
, the following Harnack’s inequality holds:

\max_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}I(t,x)\leq C\min_{x\in\bar{\Omega}}I(t,x),\ \ x\in\bar{\Omega},\,t\geq 1

(3.56)

for some constant
C>1
.

Since
\beta\geq\gamma
on
\bar{\Omega}
, we have

\partial_{t}S=\frac{(-\beta+\gamma)S+\gamma I+\gamma m}{m+S+I}I\leq\gamma\frac{I^{2}+mI}{m+S+I},\ \ t>0,\,x\in\bar{\Omega}.

Given any
x\in\bar{\Omega}
, the comparison principle for parabolic equations ensures that
S(x,t)\leq\bar{S}(t)
for all
t\geq 1
, where
\bar{S}
is the solution of the following ODE problem:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\bar{S}^{\prime}(t)=\gamma(x)\frac{I^{2}(t,x)+m(x)I(t,x)}{m(x)+\bar{S}+I(t,x)},&t>1,\\
\bar{S}(1)=S(1,x).&\end{cases}

(3.57)

We can show that
KS(x_{0},t)
is an upper solution of (
3.57
) for some large
K>0
. Indeed, we just need to check

K\partial_{t}S(t,x_{0})=K\gamma(x_{0})\frac{I^{2}(t,x_{0})+m(x_{0})I(t,x_{0})}{m(x_{0})+S(t,x_{0})+I(t,x_{0})}\geq\gamma(x)\frac{I^{2}(t,x)+m(x)I(t,x)}{m(x)+KS(t,x_{0})+I(t,x)}.

By (
3.56
), it is sufficient to check the following inequality:

K\gamma(x_{0})\frac{I^{2}(t,x)/C^{2}+m(x_{0})I(t,x)/C}{m(x_{0})+S(t,x_{0})+{\frac{I(t,x)}{C}}}\geq\gamma(x)\frac{I^{2}(t,x)+m(x)I(t,x)}{m(x)+KS(t,x_{0})+I(t,x)}.

Some elementary computation shows there is a large constant
K>0
depending on
m|_{B}
such that the above inequality holds. It then follows from the parabolic comparison principle that

KS(t,x_{0})\geq\bar{S}(t)\geq S(t,x),\ \ \forall t\geq 1.

By interchanging
x_{0}
and

, we have

S(t,x_{0})/K\leq S(t,x)\leq KS(t,x_{0}),\ \ \ \forall x\in B,\ t\geq 1.

(3.58)

Using (
3.58
), we deduce

N\geq\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)\geq\int_{B}S(t,\cdot)\geq\int_{B}\frac{S(t,x_{0})}{K}=\frac{|B|S(t,x_{0})}{K}

for all
t\geq 1
. Therefore, we have

S(t,x_{0})\leq KN/|B|\ \ \text{and}\ \ S(t,x)\leq K^{2}N/|B|,\ \ \forall x\in\bar{\Omega},t\geq 1.

This implies that there exists
M>0
depending on (the
L^{\infty}
norm of) initial data such that

\|S(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq M,\ \ \forall t\geq 1.

(3.59)

By (
3.56
) and
\int_{\Omega}I\leq N
, there is
M^{\prime}>0
such that
0\leq I(t,x)\leq M^{\prime}
for all
x\in\bar{\Omega}
and
t\geq 1
.
Therefore, this and (
3.59
) yield

\partial_{t}S=\frac{\gamma I^{2}+\gamma mI}{m+S+I}\geq\frac{a}{\|m\|_{\infty}+M+M^{\prime}}\min_{y\in B}I(t,y),\ \ \forall x\in B,t>1,

where
a=\min_{B}\gamma m
.
Integrating the above inequality over
B\times(1,\infty)
and noticing that
H^{0}
has positive measure, by (
3.56
), we have

\int_{1}^{\infty}\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}dt<\infty.

(3.60)

Since
0\leq I(x,t)\leq M^{\prime}
for all
x\in\bar{\Omega}
and
t\geq 1
, by the
L^{p}
estimate and the
I
-equation of (
1.4
),

is Hölder continuous on
\bar{\Omega}\times[1,\infty)
. Thus, it follows from Lemma
3.1
and (
3.60
) that
\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

By the equation of
S
and (
3.60
), we infer

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\|\partial_{t}S(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}dt\leq

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\Big\|\frac{\beta S(t,\cdot)I(t,\cdot)}{m+S(t,\cdot)+I(t,\cdot)}-\gamma I(t,\cdot)\Big\|_{\infty}dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\|\beta\|_{\infty}+\|\gamma\|_{\infty})\int_{1}^{\infty}\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}dt<\infty.

It follows that
S(t,\cdot)\to S^{*}:=S(0,\cdot)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{t}S(t,\cdot)dt\in C(\bar{\Omega})
uniformly on
\Omega
as
t\to\infty
.

To finish the proof of the assertion (i), it remains to show

on

. The proof of

is the same as in that of Theorem

(i) (using (

, from the first equation of (

), we see that

\partial_{t}S(t,x)=\frac{\gamma(x)m(x)+\gamma(x)I(t,x)}{m(x)+S(t,x)+I(t,x)}I(t,x)>0,\ \ \forall t>0.

Thus,
S(t,x)
is increasing in

for such

, and hence

on

. Therefore,

on

Using (

[

Proof of Proposition

\varphi>0

\varphi\in C^{2}({\mathcal{O}})\cap C(\bar{\mathcal{O}})
.

(S,I)

(S_{0},I_{0})\in[C^{+}(\Omega)]^{2}

I_{0}\geq,\neq 0

\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\to 0

\|S(t,\cdot)-S^{*}\|_{\infty}\to 0

t\to\infty

S^{*}\in C^{+}(\bar{\Omega})
.

\partial_{\nu}\varphi<0
on
\partial\mathcal{O}
. Since
I(t,\cdot)>0
on
\bar{\mathcal{O}}
for all
t>0

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal{O}}{\varphi}I=

\displaystyle d_{I}\int_{\mathcal{O}}{\varphi}\Delta I+\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Big(\frac{\beta S}{S+I}-\gamma\Big){\varphi}I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{I}\left(\int_{\mathcal{O}}I\Delta{\varphi}+\int_{\partial\mathcal{O}}\varphi\partial_{\nu}I-\int_{\partial\mathcal{O}}I\partial_{\nu}{\varphi}\right)+\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Big(\frac{\beta S}{S+I}-\gamma\Big){\varphi}I

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle d_{I}\int_{\mathcal{O}}I\Delta\varphi+\int_{\mathcal{O}}\Big(\frac{\beta S}{S+I}-\gamma\Big)\varphi I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\lambda}_{0}\int_{\mathcal{O}}{\varphi}I-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\frac{\beta I}{S+I}{\varphi}I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big({\lambda}_{0}-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\frac{\beta I}{S+I}\frac{{\varphi}I}{\int_{\mathcal{O}}{\varphi}I}\Big)\int_{\mathcal{O}}{\varphi}I,\quad t>0.

By the comparison principle for ODEs,

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\varphi(x)I(t,x)dx\geq\int_{\mathcal{O}}\varphi I(1,x)e^{\int_{1}^{t}\Big[{\lambda}_{0}-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\frac{\beta I(s,y)}{S(s,y)+I(s,y)}\frac{{\varphi}I(s,y)}{\int_{\mathcal{O}}{\varphi}(y)I(s,y)dy}dy\Big]ds}dx,\quad t\geq 1.

Since
\int_{\mathcal{O}}I(1,\cdot)\varphi>0

\int_{\mathcal{O}}I(1,\cdot)\varphi
and then take
\log

\frac{1}{t}\ln\Big(\frac{\int_{\mathcal{O}}{\varphi}I(t,x)}{\int_{\mathcal{O}}{\varphi}I(1,x)}\Big)\geq\frac{{\lambda}_{0}(t-1)}{t}-\frac{1}{t}\int_{1}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\frac{\beta I(s,x)}{S(s,x+I(s,x)}\frac{{\varphi}I(s,x)}{\int_{\mathcal{O}}{\varphi}(y)I(s,y)dy}dxds,\quad t>1.

(3.66)

\varepsilon>0
, there is
t_{\varepsilon}\gg 1
such that

\frac{(N-\bar{c}^{*}-\varepsilon)_{+}}{|\Omega|}<S(t,\cdot)<\frac{(N-\underline{c}^{*})+\varepsilon}{|\Omega|},\quad\forall\,t\geq t_{\varepsilon}.

(4.28)

Recall that if
I_{0}(x_{0})=0
for some
x_{0}\in\bar{\Omega}
, then
I(t,x_{0})=0
for all
t\geq 0
, and if
I_{0}(x_{0})>0
for some
x_{0}\in\bar{\Omega}
, then
I(t,x_{0})>0
for all
t\geq 0
. Thus, to determine the limit of
I
as
t\to\infty
, it suffices to work on the set
\{I_{0}>0\}:=\{x\in\bar{\Omega}:\ I_{0}(x)>0\}
.

From (
4.28
) it follows that

\partial_{t}I\leq\left[\frac{\beta\frac{(N-\underline{c}^{*}+\varepsilon)}{|\Omega|}}{m+\frac{(N-\bar{c}^{*}+\varepsilon)}{|\Omega|}+I}-\gamma\right]I,\ \quad\forall\,x\in\{I_{0}>0\},\,t\geq t_{\varepsilon}.

Hence, by a comparison principle for ODEs, we have that

\limsup_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\leq\left[\frac{(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))(N-\underline{c}^{*}+\varepsilon)}{|\Omega|\gamma(x)}-m(x)\right]_{+},\quad\forall\,x\in\{I_{0}>0\}.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we get

\limsup_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\leq{\left[\frac{(N-\underline{c}^{*})}{|\Omega|}-\frac{m(x)\gamma(x)}{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}\right]_{+}}\frac{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}{\gamma(x)},\quad\forall\,x\in\{I_{0}>0\}.

(4.29)

Similarly, we can proceed to establish that

\liminf_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\geq{\left[\frac{(N-\bar{c}^{*})}{|\Omega|}-\frac{m(x)\gamma(x)}{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}\right]_{+}}\frac{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}{\gamma(x)},\quad\forall\,x\in\{I_{0}>0\}.

(4.30)

It is clear that (
2.5
) follows from (
4.29
).

Thanks to (
4.29
), Fatou Lemma, and the uniform boundedness of
\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}
, we have

\bar{c}^{*}=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}I(t,x)\leq\int_{\Omega}\limsup_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\leq\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\Big[\frac{N(\beta-\gamma)}{|\Omega|\gamma}-m\Big]_{+}.

Therefore, by means of (
4.30
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\geq

\displaystyle\left[\frac{N-\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\Big(\frac{N(\beta-\gamma)}{|\Omega|\gamma}-m\Big)}{|\Omega|}-\frac{\gamma m}{\beta-\gamma}\right]\frac{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}{\gamma(x)}

(4.31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[N+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}m-\frac{N}{|\Omega|}\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\frac{(\beta-\gamma)}{\gamma}-\frac{|\Omega|m\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}\right]\frac{\beta(x)-\gamma(x)}{|\Omega|\gamma(x)}

(4.32)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 0,\quad\forall\,x\in\{I_{0}>0\}.

(4.33)

Then, we deduce from (
4.31
) and (
4.27
) that

\lim_{t\to\infty}(S(t,\cdot)-Z(t,\cdot))=0\quad\text{a.e. in}\ \{I_{0}>0\}.

(4.34)

Let
\{t_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
be a sequence of positive numbers converging to infinity. By (
4.25
) and Proposition
4.1
, possibly after passing to a subsequence, there is a positive number
\hat{S}
such that
\|S(t_{j},\cdot)-\hat{S}\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
. By the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem, (
4.34
), and Proposition
4.2
, we have

Z(t_{j},\cdot)\to\hat{S}
as
j\to\infty
in
L^{p}(\{I_{0}>0\})
for all
p\geq 1
.

Hence

I(t_{j},\cdot)=\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}Z(t_{j},\cdot)-m\to\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}\hat{S}-m\quad\text{as}\ j\to\infty,

in
L^{p}(\{I_{0}>0\})
for any
p\geq 1
. Thus

\displaystyle N=

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}[S(t_{j},\cdot)+I(t_{j},\cdot)]=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}S(t_{j},\cdot)+\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}I(t_{j},\cdot)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\Omega|\hat{S}+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\Big(\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}\hat{S}-m\Big)

(4.35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\Omega|\hat{S}+\hat{S}\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}-\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}m.

(4.36)

Solving for
\hat{S}
, we get

\hat{S}=\frac{N+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}m}{|\Omega|+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}}.

Since
\hat{S}
is independent of the chosen sequence, we conclude that

\|S(t,\cdot)-\hat{S}\|_{\infty}\to 0\ \ \text{as}\ t\to\infty.

Furthermore, by the equation of
I
,

\Big\|I(t,\cdot)-\hat{I}(\cdot)\Big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\to 0\ \ \text{as}\ t\to\infty

for any
p\geq 1
, where
\hat{I}
is given in (
2.5
)(i). This proves (i) in Case 1.

Case 2:
Suppose that
\frac{m\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}\Big(|\Omega|+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}\Big)<N
.

In view of (
4.25
) and Proposition
4.1
, for any sequence
\{t_{j}^{\prime}\}
with
t_{j}^{\prime}\to\infty
as
j\to\infty
, we can find a subsequence (labeled by itself without loss of generality), and
a positive number
S_{*}
such that
\|S(t_{j}^{\prime},\cdot)-S_{*}\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
. Here the positive constant
S_{*}
may depend on the choice of the sequence
\{t_{j}^{\prime}\}
.

Due to (
4.27
), we may assume that

S(t_{j}^{\prime},x)-Z(t_{j}^{\prime},x)\to 0\ \ \ \mbox{for}\ x\in\Omega_{*}\subset\Omega\ \ \mbox{as}\ j\to\infty,

(4.37)

and

I(t_{j}^{\prime},x)\to 0\ \ \ \mbox{for}\ \ x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{*}\ \ \mbox{as}\ j\to\infty,

(4.38)

where
\Omega_{*}
may be an empty set and depend on the choice of the sequence
\{t_{j}^{\prime}\}
.
Arguing as in Case 1, combined with Proposition
4.2
, we deduce from (
4.37
) that

Z(t_{j}^{\prime},\cdot)\to S_{*}
as
j\to\infty
in
L^{p}(\Omega_{*})
for all
p\geq 1
.

(4.39)

Thus, it follows from (
4.39
) that

I(t_{j}^{\prime},\cdot)=\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}Z(t_{j}^{\prime},\cdot)-m\to\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}S_{*}-m\quad\text{as}\ j\to\infty,

(4.40)

in
L^{p}(\{I_{0}>0\}\cap\Omega_{*})
for any
p\geq 1
.

Since
\int_{\Omega}[S(t_{j}^{\prime},\cdot)+I(t_{j}^{\prime},\cdot)]=N
for each
j
, as in Case 1, using (
4.38
) and (
4.40
), together with the fact of
I(t_{j}^{\prime},x)=0
for each

and
x\in\{I_{0}=0\}
, we get

S_{*}=\frac{N+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}\cap\Omega_{*}}m}{|\Omega|+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}\cap\Omega_{*}}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}}\geq\frac{N}{|\Omega|+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}}.

Notice that
\frac{N}{|\Omega|+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}}
is independent of the choice of the sequence
\{t_{j}^{\prime}\}
. This implies that for any given
\epsilon>0
, there is a large

such that

S(t,x)\geq\frac{N}{|\Omega|+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}}-\epsilon,\ \ \ \forall t\geq t_{\epsilon,*},\,x\in\bar{\Omega}.

As a result, we see from the
I
-equation that

\partial_{t}I\geq\left[\frac{\beta\Big(\frac{N}{|\Omega|+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}}-\epsilon\Big)}{m+\frac{N}{|\Omega|+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}}-\epsilon+I}-\gamma\right]I,\ \quad\forall\,x\in\{I_{0}>0\},\,t\geq t_{\epsilon,*}.

Then an application of the comparison principle for ODEs yields

\liminf_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\geq\left[\frac{(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))}{\gamma(x)}\Big(\frac{N}{|\Omega|+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}}-\epsilon\Big)-m(x)\right]_{+},\quad\forall\,x\in\{I_{0}>0\}.

By sending
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we get

\liminf_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\geq\left[\frac{(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))}{\gamma(x)}\Big(\frac{N}{|\Omega|+\int_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\frac{\beta-\gamma}{\gamma}}\Big)-m(x)\right]>0,\quad\forall\,x\in\{I_{0}>0\}

(4.41)

due to our assumption.

With the help of (
4.41
), we can use the same analysis as in Case 1 to conclude the desired assertion.

(ii) Suppose that
\frac{\gamma m}{\beta-\gamma}\geq\frac{N}{|\Omega|}
. It follows from (
2.5
) that
I(t,\cdot)\to 0
uniformly on
\bar{\Omega}
as
t\to\infty
. Hence
\int_{\Omega}S(t,x)dx\to N
as

. This along with (
2.3
) yields (ii).
∎

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
2.6
.

Proof of Theorem
2.6
.

Thanks to Proposition
4.2
, there is
L>0
such that

\|S(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}+\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leq L.

Fix
x\in\bar{\Omega}
such that
\beta(x)\leq\gamma(x)
. Then

\partial_{t}I(t,x)\leq-\frac{\gamma I^{2}(t,x)}{m(x)+S(t,x)+I(t,x)}\leq-\frac{\gamma I^{2}(t,x)}{m(x)+L},\quad\forall\,t>0.

Then by the comparison principle for ODEs, we obtain

I(t,x)\leq\frac{m(x)+L}{\frac{m(x)+L}{I_{0}(x)}+\gamma t}\to 0\quad\text{as}\ t\to\infty.

(4.42)

(i) Suppose that
\beta\leq\gamma
on
\bar{\Omega}
. Then, it follows from (
4.42
) that

\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\to 0\quad\mbox{as}\ t\to\infty.

Hence,
\|(\beta S/(m+S+I)-\gamma)I(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\to 0
and
\int_{\Omega}S=N-\int_{\Omega}I\to N
as
t\to\infty
. By the first equation of (
1.5
) and standard results on the large time behavior of solutions of the heat equations subject to the homogeneous boundary conditions, we can easily conclude that
\|S(t,\cdot)-\frac{N}{|\Omega|}\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

(ii) Let
l_{1}>0
be the positive number given by (
4.3
), which is independent of
m
and
N
.
Let
K\subset H^{+}
be a compact set and set

N_{*}:=\max_{x\in K}\frac{2m(x)\gamma(x)}{(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))l_{1}}.

It is clear that
N_{*}=0
if
m=0
on
K
. Note also that
N_{*}
is independent of initial data and
N
.

Thanks to (
4.3
), there is
t_{1}>0
such that

S(t,x)\geq\frac{Nl_{1}}{2},\quad\forall\,x\in\bar{\Omega},\,t\geq t_{1}.

Thus, for every
N>N_{*}
and
x\in K
, there holds that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}I=

\displaystyle\Big[\frac{\beta S}{m+S+I}-\gamma\Big]I

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Big[\frac{\frac{\beta Nl_{1}}{2}}{m+\frac{Nl_{1}}{2}+I}-\gamma\Big]I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big[\frac{(\beta-\gamma)l_{1}}{2\gamma}\Big(N-\frac{2m\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}\Big)-I\Big]\frac{\gamma I}{m+\frac{Nl_{1}}{2}+I}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Big\{(N-N_{*})l_{1}\Big[\min_{x\in K}\frac{(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))}{2\gamma(x)}\Big]-I\Big\}\frac{\gamma I}{m+\frac{Nl_{1}}{2}+I},\quad t>t_{1}.

It then follows from the comparison principle for ODEs that

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\min_{x\in K}I(t,x)\geq(N-N_{*})l_{1}\Big[\min_{x\in K}\frac{(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))}{2\gamma(x)}\Big]>0,

which yields the desired result.
∎

In order to establish Proposition
2.5
, we need to prepare some preliminary results. The first one reads as follows.

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix
d_{S}>0
and let
(S,I)
be the solution of (
1.5
). Then for each
x\in\bar{\Omega}
,

((R-1)Nl_{1}-m)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\leq((R-1)l_{2}-m)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}},

(4.43)

where
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
are given in Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
, respectively.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
, we have

Nl_{1}\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}S(t,x)\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}S(t,x)\leq l_{2},\quad\text{uniformly for}\ x\in\bar{\Omega}.

(4.44)

So for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is
t_{\varepsilon}\gg 1
such that

Nl_{1}-\varepsilon<S(t,x)<l_{2}+\varepsilon,\quad\forall\,t>t_{\varepsilon},\,x\in\bar{\Omega},

which in turn implies that

\partial_{t}I\leq\Big[\frac{\beta(l_{2}+\varepsilon)}{m+(l_{2}+\varepsilon)+I}-\gamma\Big]I,\ \quad x\in\bar{\Omega},\ t\geq t_{\varepsilon}.

Hence, by the comparison principle for ODEs, we have that

\limsup_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\leq\Big[(R(x)-1)(l_{2}+\varepsilon)-m(x)\Big]_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}},\quad\forall\,x\in\bar{\Omega}.

Taking
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we get

\limsup_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\leq[(R-1)l_{2}-m]_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}},\quad\forall\,x\in\bar{\Omega}.

Similarly, we can proceed to establish that

\liminf_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\geq[(R(x)-1)Nl_{1}-m(x)]_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}},\quad\forall\,x\in\bar{\Omega}.

Thus, the desired results are proved.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Fix
d_{S}>0
and suppose that
1/(\beta-\gamma)\in L^{1}(H^{+}\cap\{I_{0}>0\})
. Let
(S,I)
be the solution of (
1.5
). Then we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\Big\|S(t,\cdot)-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)\Big\|_{\infty}=0.

(4.45)

Proof.

Define

V(t):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}S^{2}+\int_{H^{+}\cap\{I_{0}>0\}}\frac{\gamma(I+m)^{2}}{2(\beta-\gamma)},\ \ t>0.

Due to
1/(\beta-\gamma)\in L^{1}(H^{+}\cap\{I_{0}>0\})
,
V(t)
is well defined for all
t>0
. It is easy to check that

\displaystyle\frac{dV}{dt}=

\displaystyle-d_{S}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla S|^{2}-\int_{H^{+}\cap\{I_{0}>0\}}\frac{I((\beta-\gamma)S-\gamma I-\gamma m)^{2}}{(\beta-\gamma)(S+I+m)}

(4.46)

\displaystyle\ +\int_{H^{-}\cup H^{0}\cup\{I_{0}=0\}}\left(\frac{\beta S}{S+I+m}-\gamma\right)SI,\ \ \forall\,t>0.

(4.47)

By the equation of
I
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{H^{-}\cup H^{0}\cup\{I_{0}=0\}}\left(\frac{\beta S}{S+I+m}-\gamma\right)I=\int_{H^{-}\cup H^{0}\cup\{I_{0}=0\}}(I-I_{0}).

If
x\in H^{-}\cup H^{0}\cup\{I_{0}=0\}
, then

\left(\frac{\beta S}{S+I+m}-\gamma\right)I\leq 0.

Hence,

0\leq-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{H^{-}\cup H^{0}\cup\{I_{0}=0\}}\left(\frac{\beta S}{S+I+m}-\gamma\right)I<\infty.

Due to Proposition
4.1
, we further have

0\leq-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{H^{-}\cup H^{0}\cup\{I_{0}=0\}}\left(\frac{\beta S}{S+I+m}-\gamma\right)SI<\infty.

Therefore, integrating (
4.46
) over
\Omega\times(0,\infty)
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla S|^{2}<\infty.

(4.48)

By Proposition
4.2
, we have
\sup_{t\geq 0}\|(\beta S(t,\cdot)/(m+S+I)-\gamma)I\|_{\infty}<\infty
. By the regularity theory for parabolic equations, the mapping
[1,\infty)\ni t\mapsto S(t,\cdot)\in C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
is Holder’s continuous. In particular,
\|\nabla S(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is Hölder’s continuous on
[1,\infty)
. So, we can conclude from (
4.48
) and Lemma
3.1
that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\||\nabla S(t,\cdot)|\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0.

(4.49)

By virtue of (
4.49
), the Poincaré inequality and the regularity of
S(t,\cdot)
, we can proceed as in (
3.45
) to establish that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\Big\|S(t,\cdot)-\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)\Big\|_{C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})}=0,

which proves (
4.45
).
∎

With the above preparation, we are now ready to verify Proposition
2.5
.

Proof of Proposition
2.5
.

Since
0\leq\int_{\Omega}S\leq N
for all
t\geq 0
, there exist constants
\zeta,\eta\in[0,N/|\Omega|]
with
\zeta\leq\eta
such that

\zeta|\Omega|=\liminf_{t\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)=\eta|\Omega|.

(4.50)

For any
\epsilon>0
, by Lemma
4.3
, there exists
t_{\epsilon}>0
such that

\zeta-\epsilon\leq S(t,x)\leq\eta+\epsilon,\ \ \forall x\in\bar{\Omega},t\geq t_{\epsilon}.

Similarly to the proof of Lemma
4.2
, for each
x\in\bar{\Omega}
,

((R-1)\zeta-m)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}I(t,x)\leq((R-1)\eta-m)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}}.

(4.51)

By (
4.2
) and Fatou’s lemma, we have

|\Omega|\eta=\limsup_{t\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}S=N-\liminf_{t\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}I\leq N-\int_{\Omega}((R-1)\zeta-m)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}}

and

|\Omega|\zeta=\liminf_{t\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}S=N-\limsup_{t\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}I\geq N-\int_{\Omega}((R-1)\eta-m)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}}.

It follows that

|\Omega|\eta-\int_{\Omega}((R-1)\eta-m)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\leq|\Omega|\zeta-\int_{\Omega}((R-1)\zeta-m)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}}.

(4.52)

Define the function

f(\tau):=|\Omega|\tau-\int_{\Omega}((R-1)\tau-m)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}},\ \ \tau\in[0,N/|\Omega|].

We claim that
f
is strictly increasing. Indeed, if
\sigma>0
, then

\displaystyle f(\tau+\sigma)-f(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\Omega|\sigma-\left[\int_{\Omega}((\tau+\sigma)(R-1)-m)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}}-\int_{\Omega}(\tau(R-1)-m)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sigma\left(|\Omega|-\int_{\Omega}(R-1)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}}\right)>0,

where we have used the inequality
a_{+}+b_{+}\geq(a+b)_{+}
and the assumption
|\Omega|-\int_{\Omega}(R-1)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}}>0
.

Since
f
is increasing and
\zeta\leq\eta
, we have
f(\zeta)\leq f(\eta)
. However, (
4.52
) is equivalent to
f(\eta)\leq f(\zeta)
. Hence,
f(\eta)=f(\zeta)
and
\eta=\zeta=:S^{*}
is the unique solution of

S^{*}|\Omega|+\int_{\Omega}((R-1)S^{*}-m)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}}=N.

Thus, Proposition
2.5
is a direct consequence of (
4.50
) and (
4.51
).
∎

Proof of Corollary
2.2
.

It is easy to see that the unique solution
S^{*}\in(0,N/|\Omega|]
of the algebraic equation

S^{*}|\Omega|+\int_{\Omega}((R-1)S^{*}-m)_{+}\chi_{\{I_{0}>0\}}=N

is
S^{*}=N/|\Omega|
if and only if

(R-1)N/|\Omega|-m\leq 0
on
H^{+}\cap\{I_{0}>0\}
.

Then the desired results readily follow from Proposition
2.5
.
∎

6.
Discussion and Conclusion

The SIS model serves as one of the most fundamental mathematical frameworks for understanding infectious disease dynamics.
This paper presents a comprehensive analysis of the SIS systems (
1.4
) and (
1.5
), offering insights into the dynamics of infectious disease spread and control.

6.1.
For system (
1.4
): control on the susceptible’s movement

System (
1.4
) reflects the strategic movement constrains imposed to the susceptible population. If the habitat is entirely composed of high-risk areas (
\beta>\gamma
on
\Omega
) and the saturated incidence exists (i.e.
m\not\equiv 0
), Theorem
2.1
indicates that the fate of a disease—whether it diminishes or persists—depends significantly on the total population size
N
. Specifically, the disease will persist across the habitat in the long term if
N
exceeds the threshold value
\int_{\Omega}\frac{m\gamma}{\beta-\gamma}
. Conversely, if
N
falls below this threshold (considering the saturated incidence factor), the disease is destined for eradication. In contrast, if no saturated incidence effect exists (i.e.,
m\equiv 0
),
[
57
, Theorem 4.2(ii)]
demonstrates that the disease will persist throughout the habitat in the long run, regardless of the total population size.

Theorem
2.2
suggests that in habitats consisting solely of high-risk and moderate-risk locations, disease extinction can be achieved in the long run if the movement of susceptible individuals is sufficiently restricted. Similarly, Theorem
2.3
demonstrates that the presence of low-risk areas, coupled with movement restrictions, can lead to the eventual extinction of the disease. Theorems
2.2
-
2.3
imply that controlling the mobility of susceptible individuals is a viable strategy for disease eradication as long as moderate-risk or low-risk locations are created.

Furthermore, the presence of saturated incidence effects significantly impacts the distribution of the susceptible population. The susceptible population can persist in areas where the risk is moderate or low, as well as in regions where saturated incidence effects are present. In particular, when this effect is consistently present throughout the habitat, the susceptible population will persist on the entire habitat. In contrast, Proposition
2.2
and Corollary
2.1
suggest that the susceptible population cannot occupy all the high-risk places where the saturated incidence effect is negligible, particularly with low infected population mobility.

6.2.
For system (
1.5
): control on the infected’s movement

System (
1.5
) reflects the strategic movement restrictions imposed to the infected population.
According to Theorem
2.4
, restricting the movement of the infected population allows the susceptible population to occupy the entire habitat. This outcome holds regardless of transmission and recovery rates or the presence of a saturated incidence effect. Theorem
2.5
demonstrates that in high-risk environments, restricting the movement of infected individuals can result in a homogeneous distribution of the susceptible population. The persistence or extinction of the infectious disease, however, depends on the total population size and the presence of the saturated incidence effect. In the absence of this effect, the disease persists. Conversely, when the saturated incidence effect is considered, a larger total population size tends to support disease persistence, while a smaller population size increases the likelihood of eventual extinction.

Theorem
2.6
demonstrates that in low- and moderate-risk regions, infections will eventually be eradicated. However, in high-risk regions with a large total population, eradication of the infection is not possible. Proposition
2.5
and Corollary
2.2
further reveal that as the population increases, the area where the disease can persist also expands. This highlights the importance of population control in managing disease spread, particularly in high-risk regions. These results surprisingly differ from
[
57
, Theorem 4.6(i)]
for the model without saturated incidence effect, which indicates that the disease will persist exactly on high-risk areas when the mobility of the infected population is sufficiently controlled.

6.3.
Control on both susceptible and infected’s movements

If we restrict the movement of both susceptible and infected populations, that is, as
d_{S},d_{I}
approach zero in (
1.2
), formally we are led to the following system of ODEs:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\dfrac{dS}{dt}=-\dfrac{\beta(x)SI}{m(x)+S+I}+\gamma(x)I,\ \ &t>0,\,x\in\bar{\Omega},\\
\displaystyle\dfrac{dI}{dt}=\dfrac{\beta(x)SI}{m(x)+S+I}-\gamma(x)I,&t>0,\,x\in\bar{\Omega},\\
\displaystyle S(0,x)+I(0,x)=n(x)\geq 0,&\,x\in\bar{\Omega}\end{cases}

(6.1)

with
\int_{\Omega}n=N>0
.

We define the local basic reproduction number
\mathcal{R}_{0}(x)
of system (
6.1
) by

\mathcal{R}_{0}(x)=\frac{\beta(x)n(x)}{\gamma(x)(m(x)+n(x))},\ \ \ x\in\bar{\Omega}.

Note that if a point
x\in\bar{\Omega}
is of low risk (i.e.,
\beta(x)<\gamma(x)
), then
\mathcal{R}_{0}(x)<1
, regardless of the distribution of the population
n(x)
. Moreover, if
m(x)=0
for some
x\in\bar{\Omega}
, then the point
x
is of low-risk if and only if
\mathcal{R}_{0}(x)<1
. According to
[
24
, Theorem A.1]
, the following statements hold:

(i)

If
\mathcal{R}_{0}(x)\leq 1
, then
(S(t,x),I(t,x))\to(n(x),0)
as
t\to\infty
;

(ii)

If
\mathcal{R}_{0}(x)>1
, then
(S(t,x),I(t,x))\to\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}_{0}(x)}n(x),\left(1-\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}_{0}(x)}\right)n(x)\right)
as
t\to\infty
.

These results reveal that the long-term dynamics of system (
6.1
) are determined by the local basic reproduction number
\mathcal{R}_{0}
. Specifically, at position
x
, if
\mathcal{R}_{0}(x)>1
(equivalently,
(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))n(x)>m(x)\gamma(x)
), the disease will persist; if
\mathcal{R}_{0}(x)\leq 1
(equivalently,
(\beta(x)-\gamma(x))n(x)\leq m(x)\gamma(x)
), the disease will eventually become extinct.
Thus, in each moderate- or low-risk location, controlling the movement of both susceptible and infected populations can lead to disease eradication. However, in every high-risk location, the success of such control measures in achieving disease extinction depends on factors such as the total population size and the presence of a saturated incidence effect. Specifically, a large total population size and the absence of a saturated incidence effect tend to support disease persistence, whereas a small total population size and the presence of a saturated incidence effect can promote disease extinction.
These findings also suggest that merely creating moderate- or low-risk sub-areas is insufficient for eradicating the disease across the entire habitat, when the movement of both populations is strictly limited. This outcome is similar to the results observed for system (
1.5
), when the movement of only infected population is controlled. In contrast, the results on system (
1.4
) suggest that limiting the movement of susceptible population may eliminate the disease if moderate- or low-risk sub-areas exist. Henceforth, restricting the movement of both populations may not be the most effective strategy for eradicating the disease.

Indeed, targeting the movement of either the susceptible or infected population can sometimes yield better results than addressing the movement of both simultaneously. This can be explained by several possible reasons. (1) Targeted intervention: Focusing on the susceptible population can significantly reduce new infections, as shown in the systematic review by Balcan et al.
[
5
]
, where restricting movement into high-infection areas lowers transmission rates. (2) Resource allocation: Managing both populations may dilute intervention effectiveness; concentrating resources on one group allows for more efficient strategies
[
46
]
. (3) Behavioral dynamics: The behavior of individuals affects disease spread; if infected individuals are less mobile, their impact on transmission diminishes
[
29
]
.

6.4.
Summary

Existing research has emphasized the importance of tailored approaches involving multiple intervention strategies to contain the spread of infectious diseases
[
5
,
14
,
23
,
22
,
38
,
46
]
. This study focuses on the role of movement restrictions on either susceptible or infected population in controlling infectious diseases. By analyzing systems (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) compared with (
6.1
), it offers some insights into developing effective public health strategies, during pandemics like COVID-19. The findings underscore the necessity of adaptive strategies that consider regional variations of transmission risk and targeted interventions to achieve disease eradication.
