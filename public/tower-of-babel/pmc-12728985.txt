Modified intracardiac echocardiography (ICE)-guided transseptal puncture under ablation catheter guidance
Authors: Pan Hou, Jiayu Dai, Rong Wang, Li Liu, Feng Lin, Lewei He, Shifang Ding, Qing Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundZero-fluoroscopy procedures have become increasingly popular in electrophysiological interventional surgery. As a core technology for achieving zero-fluoroscopy, traditional transseptal puncture (TSP) under intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) guidance has several limitations that hinder its widespread application and further development. This study aims to introduce and evaluate the efficacy and safety of a modified TSP technique developed based on clinical practice, which has the potential to overcome the shortcomings of traditional ICE-guided TSP.MethodsA total of 77 patients who underwent radiofrequency ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF) between March 1, 2022 and February 28, 2023 were enrolled. Among them, 44 patients underwent traditional ICE-guided TSP, and 33 patients underwent modified ICE-guided TSP. The success rate, numbers of puncture attempts, puncture duration, proportion of ideal puncture location and incidence of puncture-related complications were recorded.ResultsThere were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups. Compared with the traditional group, the modified group had a significantly lower number of puncture attempts (1.25 ± 0.44 vs.1.06 ± 0.24, p = 0.018) and shorter puncture duration (2.52 ± 0.83 vs.3.94 ± 2.15 min, p = 0.001). No significant differences were observed in TSP success rate, proportion of ideal puncture location or incidence of puncture-related complications between the two groups.ConclusionsThis study presents a modified ICE-guided TSP technique using an ablation catheter for guidance. This technique simplifies the operation of the puncture component and eliminates the need for ICE view tracking. It offers advantages including high success rate, favorable safety profile, simple procedural steps, ease of use, and a short learning curve, making it worthy of clinical promotion and application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05340-0.Keywords: Intracardiac echocardiography guidance, Ablation catheter guidance, Transseptal puncture.

Introduction

Transseptal puncture (TSP) is one of the most commonly used procedure in interventional cardiology. It is widely applied in the ablation of left atrial arrhythmias, percutaneous closure of the left atrial appendage, and interventional treatment of mitral valve disease. This procedure was first described by Ross and Cope in 1958 [1, 2]. In the 1980 s, with the extensive development of percutaneous valve replacement and pulmonary vein isolation, TSP was gradually popularized [3].

Traditional TSP is usually performed under X-ray guidance. However, under this modality, the puncture site is determined based on anatomic relationships, which imposes extremely high requirements on the operator’s anatomical knowledge and procedural skills. Meanwhile, radiation exposure remains a critical concern for both patients and operators. In recent years, with the widespread application of new technologies and devices such as intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) and three-dimensional (3D) electroanatomic mapping (EAM), the concept of 3D zero-fluoroscopy TSP has gradually gained recognition. Zero-fluoroscopy TSP techniques guided by ICE and EAM have been reported worldwide and their safety and effectiveness have been extensively validated [4].

Compared with traditional X-ray guided TSP, ICE-guided TSP enables real-time visualization of the fossa ovalis (FO), adjacent anatomical structures, and puncture components, thereby reducing X-ray exposure and improving the success rate of puncture, especially in cases with complex anatomical variations. EAM-guided TSP can directly provide a 3D perspective and accurately locate the FO through potential mapping, significantly improving puncture success rate and safety [5]. However, ICE-guided TSP relies on a two-dimensional ultrasound view, which makes it difficult to track the puncture components and may lead to potential safety risks. EAM-guided TSP depends on FO potential mapping, which requires high technical accuracy and is not suitable for patients with small differences between the central and peripheral potentials of the FO.

Based on these considerations and previous studies, we developed a modified ICE-guided TSP technique using an ablation catheter for guidance. This approach integrates the advantages of 2D ultrasound and 3D modeling, along with simple manipulation, 3D visualization, and high safety, as detailed below.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This study was a single-center, non-randomized controlled observational study. Eligible patients were aged 18–80 years old and met the criteria for AF ablation. Patients with the following conditions were excluded: (1) left atrial appendage thrombosis; (2) left atrium anteroposterior diameter>50 mm; (3) Structural heart diseases (atrial septal defect after occlusion, severe valvular disease, tetralogy of Fallot, etc.) (4) other conditions that are unsuitable for ablation: including fever, severe heart failure, advanced tumors, etc. Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this study. Two different ICE guided TSP techniques (traditional and modified) were used, as described below.

Modified ICE-Guided TSP under ablation catheter guidance

The modified ICE-guided TSP with ablation catheter guidance is an original technical developed by our team. The procedural workflow is described below and illustrated in Fig.1.

3D model construction and ideal puncture site selection via ICE catheter

An ICE catheter(SOUNDSTAR, Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA, USA) was sent to the middle of the right atrium (RA) via the right/left femoral vein. A 3D-model of the RA, FO, coronary sinus (CS), left and right pulmonary veins(L/RPV), esophagus (ESO) and left atrium (LA) were constructed respectively along the short axis view of the LA. When the ICE view was aligned with the middle of the left pulmonary vein (LPV) through the FO (LPV view), the mid-inferior region of the FO was selected as the preset puncture site. This site was marked on both the ICE view and 3D model, with confirmation that the preset puncture site was inferior to the anterior inferior rim of the right inferior pulmonary ostium. Sufficient space should be reserved between the ideal puncture site and the anterior rim of the right inferior pulmonary vein for the manipulation of the ablation catheter.

Localization of the ablation catheter on the FO via 3D-2D interactive

With reference to the LA 3D model, the ICE catheter was positioned to display the LPV view (characterized by the “rabbit ear sign” shown in Fig. 1, Step 3), and an appropriate posterior loop was applied to reserve sufficient operational space for the ablation catheter and puncture needle at the atrial septum. The entire atrial septum and FO should be fully visualized in the ICE view. Subsequently, an ablation catheter (Thermocool Smart Touch, Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA, USA) was sent to the mid-upper RA via a transseptal guiding introducer (SL1, Abbott, Abbott Park, IL, USA), and then directly delivered to the FO under 3D model guidance. The tip of the ablation catheter was adjusted and tightly positioned against the preset puncture site marked on the FO. (Tip of the ablation catheter and obvious tenting of the FO were seen on the ICE view). Meanwhile, the ICE view was maintained in coaxial alignment with the ablation catheter in the 3D model.

Outer sheath advancement to the preset FO puncture site along the ablation catheter

The ablation catheter was kept stable, and the outer sheath was slowly advanced along the ablation catheter to the mid- FO. In the 3D model, 1–2 electrodes at the tip of the ablation catheter were gradually covered by the outer sheath. Under the ICE view, tip of the outer sheath was advanced to the FO puncture site until a mild FO tenting was visible. The ablation catheter was then withdrawn with forward traction of the outer sheath to maintain the tenting. During this process, rotation of the ICE view and outer sheath should be avoided. After ablation catheter withdrawn, the double-track appearance of the outer sheath (red arrow in Fig. 1, step7) and FO puncture site tenting sign were visible on the ICE view.

Delivery of the puncture needle and inner sheath to the mid- FO

The puncture needle was preloaded into the inner sheath. The outer sheath tip was maintained in contact with the FO to preserve the tenting sign, and the needle and inner sheath were advanced to the mid-FO via outer sheath. At this point, the tenting sign remained visible, and the outer sheath double track sign disappeared from the ICE view. The puncture site was fine-tune according to the preset ideal location: (1)ICE view alignment with the left atrial appendage, indicated an excessively anterior position; (2)alignment with the LA posterior wall indicated an excessively posterior position; (3)alignment with the LPV view indicated an optimal position; (4)tenting near the FO upper edge indicated an excessively high position; (5)tenting near the FO lower edge indicated an excessively low position.

Puncture through the atrial septum into the LA

After confirming the puncture site and visualizing the complete sheath shape on the ICE view, the needle was advanced forward. The tenting sign disappeared upon atrial septum penetration. On the ICE view, the position of the needle tip in the LA was visualized; injection of heparinized saline through the needle resulted in an eddy current (bubble-like phenomenon), which further confirmed LA entry and localized the needle tip. The puncture needle was stabilized and the inner sheath was advanced forward-during which a second tenting sign was visible. After inner sheath entry into the LA, the second tenting sign disappears, and the needle tip was covered. Subsequently, the outer sheath was advanced while withdrawing the needle and inner sheath, with visualization of the outer sheath double-track sign on the ICE view. Finally, a guidewire into the LA through the outer sheath.

Traditional ICE-guided TSP

The traditional ICE-guided TSP procedure was performed as previously described [6]. After 3D model construction, a J-type long guide wire was sent into the superior vena cave (SVC) via the SL1 sheath and confirmed by ICE. The SL1 sheath with a dilator was delivered into the SVC via guide wire guidance under ICE. The guide wire was then withdrawn and the puncture needle was placed inside the dilator. Heparinized saline is injected into the needle to confirm its position. While the TSP assembly was slowly pulled down to the typical 4 to 7 o’clock position (based on LA anteroposterior diameter and operator experience), the ICE catheter was slightly adjusted to continuously monitor the position of the TSP assembly tip, When the TSP assembly was pulled down into the FO, the tenting sign was visible on ICE. Subsequent steps were identical to those described in Section. 2.2.5.

Outcomes

The following TSP-related outcomes were evaluated: (1) TSP success rate: Failure was defined as > 3 puncture attempts, TSP duration > 5 min, requirement for operator replacement, or conversion to X-ray fluoroscopy plus ICE-guided TSP. (2) Ideal puncture site: Defined as an actual puncture site within 3 mm of the preset site. (3) TSP duration: Interval from completion of LA 3D model to outer sheath flushing with heparinized saline in the LA. (4) Safety: Incidence of major TSP-related complications (e.g., cardiac tamponade, pericardial effusion, aortic root perforation, stroke/transient ischemic attack).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (Version 21). Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, and non-uniformly distributed variables were expressed as medians (Q1 and Q3). Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons of normally distributed continuous variables were performed using the independent-samples t-test, and comparisons of non-normally distributed continuous variables were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Outcomes comparisons between groups

TSP success rate, number of puncture attempts, TSP duration, ideal puncture site rate, and TSP-related complications were compared between the two techniques. As shown in Table 2, the TSP success rate was 100% in both groups, with no complications reported. The modified group had a significantly lower number of puncture attempts than the traditional group (1.06 ± 0.24 vs. 1.25 ± 0.44 times, p = 0.018) and a significantly shorter average TSP duration (2.52 ± 0.83 min vs. 3.94 ± 2.15 min, p = 0.001). No significant difference was observed in the ideal puncture site rate between the two groups (97.0% [32/33] vs. 90.9% [40/44], p = 0.548).

Disscusion

TSP is an essential skill for electrophysiologists and an ideal TSP technique should be safe and easy to implement. Over the past few decades, X-ray guided TSP has been widely adopted with a mature workflow and high clinical acceptance. However, X-ray guided TSP infers the puncture site through 2D anatomical structures, making it challenging to perform in patients with anatomical variations [7]. Additionally, radiation exposure remains a critical concern, as it poses health risk to patients, operators, and operating room staff. With the advancement of technologies such as ICE and EAM, an increasing number of electrophysiologists recognize that radiation-free surgery is an inevitable trend TSP and even electrophysiological interventional diagnosis and treatment [8].

ICE enables real-time dynamic visualization of cardiac structure and adjacent anatomical features. Under ICE guidance, radiation-free TSP has become safer and more reliable. The use of ICE-guided TSP during AF ablation is “Recommended” in clinical practice, as it effectively improves TSP success rates and reduces complication rates, especially in cases of abnormally sized atria (overly large or undersized) and atrial septal anatomical variations [9]. Currently, ICE-guided TSP mainly includes three approaches: ICE view tracking, ablation catheter guided steerable sheath to the FO [4], and ICE combined with EAM [10]. Based on previous experience, our study innovatively proposes a modified ICE-guided TSP under ablation catheter guidance, which offers several distinct advantages over existing TSP methods.

ICE modeling simplifies operation and optimizes anatomical details

Baykaner et al. [4] previously described an ablation catheter-guided TSP similar to our modified method, the key difference lies in the modeling approach. According to their report, the ablation catheter was guided via introducer sheath to the right cardiac chamber for making volumetric map of the RA, SVC, and CS. In our modified technique, after completing 3D model with the ICE catheter, the ablation catheter can be directly delivered to FO under the 2D and 3D interaction. Compared with ablation catheter modeling, ICE catheter modeling provides more realistic and anatomically accurate visualization of cardiac structures, facilitating precise positioning and puncture [11]. This improvement fully leverages the advantages of ICE and eliminates redundant steps such as guide wire placement for modeling.

Simplified operation of puncture modules

Most current ICE-guided TSP require direct manipulation of the puncture module to locate the puncture points. “ICE view tracking” requires guiding the puncture modules along the guide wire to the SVC and then pulling it down to the FO. However, guide wire placement can be difficult in patients with right atrium anatomical variations. “Ablation catheter guided puncture modules” deliver puncture modules to the root of SVC via an ablation catheter. Although the difficult problem of wire placement can be solved, the puncture module still needs to be gradually withdrawn to locate the FO puncture site, and precise control of the withdrawal angle is relatively demanding. The ablation catheter-guided steerable sheath technique can guide the puncture module sheath to the FO directly, but the use of steerable sheath and special TSP needle increase the costs [12]. Studies have shown that TSP failure is mainly attributed to the inability to accurately located the FO [13]. The modified technique in our study locates the FO using an ablation catheter under ICE guidance, transforming “puncture module operation” into “ablation catheter operation”. As ablation catheters are more maneuverable, this modification significantly improves procedural success rates and safety. Subsequently, the puncture needle is directly positioned at the target site along the ablation catheter and the outer sheath, requiring only minor adjustments to complete the puncture.

Fixed ICE view eliminates the need for real-time tracking

“ICE view tracking” is a widely used TSP procedure in which the ICE view must real-time track the puncture module from the SVC to the FO. This process requires constant adjustment of the ICE catheter and passively “search” for the puncture modules, which easily leads to sector shift and loss of the sheath on ICE view. This not only imposes higher technical requirements on surgeons and the assistants but also increase safety risks. In contrast, our modified TSP technique only requires one fixed ICE view throughout the puncture process. The entire procedure can be completed under direct visualization without excessive manipulation or adjustment of the ICE catheter, effectively avoiding TSP failure caused by the inability of the ICE view to synchronize with the movement of the needle sheath.

Simplified workflow, high success rate and shortened learning curve

As described above, the operation of the ICE, ablation catheter and puncture modules are relatively independent and simplified in our modified technique. This significantly improves TSP success rates and facilitates the implementation of zero-fluoroscopy. Simplification of the procedural workflow and reduction in technical requirement are expected to lower complication rates. In clinical practice at our center, the modified technique achieved a 100% success rate with no post-procedural complications. Overall, the modified technique is more user-friendly and has a shorter learning curve for beginners. After proficiency is achieved, the entire puncture process can be completed by a single operator, making it simpler, more convenient, and more reliable in clinical settings.

Limitations

First, the limited sample size—especially the small number of patients with anatomical variations—makes it difficult to fully evaluate the performance of the modified TSP technique in complex clinical scenarios. Second, this is a single-center, non-randomized observational study that only compares technical parameters between the two groups. It cannot directly analyze differences in long-term success rates, complication rates, or learning curves with sufficient statistical power. Future studies with rigorous experimental designs are needed to further validate the clinical value of this technique.

Conclusion

The modified ICE-guided TSP technique using an ablation catheter for guidance simplifies the operation of puncture modules and eliminates the need for ICE view tracking. It offers distinct advantages including high success rate, favorable safety profile, ease of use, and a shortened learning curve, making it worthy of clinical promotion and application.
