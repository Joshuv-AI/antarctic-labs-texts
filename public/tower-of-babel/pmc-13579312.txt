A transdermal CRISPR/Cas9 strategy using biodegradable microneedles to target YB-1 in melanoma
Authors: Xiazhou Fu, Zhaoyi Chen, Ziru Zhang, Yufei Jiang, Xiaomeng Jiang, Yuxin Yang, Hongsheng Zhang, Xinyun Zou, Jingwen Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Melanoma's aggressiveness and therapeutic resistance highlight the need for innovative strategies. This study developed a CRISPR/Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) system targeting the oncogenic YB-1 gene, combined with doxorubicin (DOX), delivered by chondroitin sulfate (CS)-based microneedles (MN) incorporating designed nanoparticles (CNPs). YB-1, a critical oncogene driving melanoma progression, metastasis, and drug resistance, was targeted to enhance therapy efficacy. In vitro experiments demonstrated that CNPs enhanced cellular uptake efficiency, achieving a dual-drug delivery rate of 74.4% and mediating 65.32% gene editing efficiency. The application of CNPs@DOX@RNP reduced tumor cell viability to just 3.51%, while simultaneously lowering off-target toxicity. In vivo C57BL/6 melanoma models showed significant tumor growth inhibition, increased apoptosis, and suppressed proliferation/angiogenesis. Following treatment with CNPs@DOX@RNP/MN, tumor weight exhibited a 92.56% reduction relative to the untreated control. Biocompatibility assays confirmed safety with minimal organ damage. This MN platform offers a synergistic, safe, and effective strategy for combined YB-1 gene editing and chemotherapy, addressing melanoma's therapeutic challenges.

Introduction

Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat - associated nuclease 9 (CRISPR/Cas9) system is composed of single guide RNA (sgRNA) and the Cas9 nuclease. [1,2]. Ribonucleoprotein (RNP) is a pre-assembled complex of Cas9 nuclease and sgRNA that directly executes DNA cleavage within cells, bypassing the need for cellular transcription and translation processes [3,4]. This complex produces the fastest genome-editing effect; moreover, it avoids the problems of mRNA degradation and plasmid DNA integration into the host genome [5]. However, the lack of efficient delivery vehicles for transporting RNP to specific cells or tissues hinders the translation of this biotechnology into clinical applications [6]. Polymeric nanoparticles (NPs) have been extensively studied [7,8] as attractive platforms for non-viral delivery of CRISPR/Cas9, owing to their unique properties such as high stability, versatility, and sustained release [9]. Lo et al. used porous NPs for RNP delivery and achieved efficient knockdown of target genes in vitro and in vivo [10]. Chae et al. designed multifunctional NPs with pH-sensitive PEG derivatives and targeting peptides to achieve efficient delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 and docetaxel to human TNBC MDA-MB-231 cells and tumor-bearing mice for the treatment of triple negative breast cancer [11]. However, NPs are also limiteded by finite delivery efficiency and susceptibility to off-target effects [12]. Therefore, enhancing the delivery efficiency and local concentration of NPs within tumor cells while reducing their off-target toxicity represents a critical challenge that urgently needs to be addressed.

Biodegradable microneedle (MN) technology offers a compelling solution to these delivery barriers. As a painless transdermal system, MNs bypass the stratum corneum and hepatic first-pass metabolism, delivering drugs directly to the lesion site to maximize local concentration and patient compliance [[13], [14], [15]]. Although MNs are widely used for skin tumors like melanoma [16], their broader application has been restricted by limited drug-loading capacity [17]. To overcome this, integrating NPs into MNs has proven effective in enhancing chemotherapeutic delivery [18]. Recent studies have demonstrated that the combination of MNs with NPs achieves higher chemotherapeutic delivery efficiency than MNs alone for effective treatment of inflammatory skin diseases (ISD) [19]. Previously, We designed and synthesized a novel polymer polyethyleneimine-oleic acid (PEI-OA) for the combined delivery of monoclonal antibodies and chemotherapeutic drugs. The PEI-OA complex significantly enhanced the drug-loading capacity and encapsulation efficiency of the nanoplatform [20]. Similarly, Teng et al. utilized the PEI derivatives to effectively deliver siRNA into SK-HEP-1 cells, successfully inducing downregulation of luciferase reporter gene expression [21]. Building on this, we employ PEI-OA as the core nanocarrier, encapsulated within MNs to achieve the coordinated delivery of RNP complexes, which exerts potent therapeutic effects at an extremely low dose. We selected chondroitin sulfate (CS) over the commonly used hyaluronic acid (HA) [22]. CS is a sulfated glycosaminoglycan [23,24] and a fundamental component of the extracellular matrix in connective tissues [25,26]. Beyond its exceptional biocompatibility and controlled biodegradability, CS exhibits intrinsic anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antitumor properties [27]. These biological activities, combined with minimal immunogenicity, make CS a superior candidate for fabricating biodegradable MNs tailored for tumor therapy. Simultaneously, we modified the NP surface with CS to form CNPs, enabling enhanced targeting of the CD44 receptor on tumor cells.

Y- box binding protein 1 (YB-1) [28] has been identified as a critical regulator of melanoma cell growth, differentiation, and oncogenic transformation [[29], [30], [31]]. As one of the most conserved proteins in evolution, YB-1 has long been considered a potential cancer target [32]. In normal cells, YB-1 is typically poorly expressed and performs cellular functions such as DNA repair, pre-mRNA splicing, and mRNA stability [33]. However, in cancer cells, YB-1 is phosphorylated by kinases like protein kinase B, 70 kDa ribosomal protein S6 kinase, and 90 kDa Ribosomal S6 kinase, leading to increased expression and nuclear translocation [34,35]. Evidence suggests that the nuclear localization and overexpression of YB-1 can predict poor outcomes in patients with over 20 different types of tumors. Li et al. reported a polyamine-modified thermosensitive hydrogel for transdermal delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 targeting YB-1 for effective gene therapy of melanoma. It was confirmed that the knockout of YB-1 could have a therapeutic effect on melanoma [36]. Therefore, we selected the YB-1 gene as the therapeutic target to evaluate its feasibility for melanoma treatment. Since chemotherapy remains the first-line treatment for inoperable cancers [37], combination therapy strategies are currently showing great promise [38]. In this study, we propose a combination therapy integrating CRISPR/Cas9-based YB-1 gene editing with DOX [39] based chemotherapy to target highly metastatic melanoma.

Collectively, this study reports a biodegradable MN patch for the transdermal co-delivery of RNP and DOX (Fig. 1). The system utilizes a CS-based MN matrix reinforced with PEG6000, embedded with PEI-OA-based core-shell nanoparticles (CNPs). In this design, DOX is encapsulated in the hydrophobic core, while RNP complexes are adsorbed onto the cationic surface and stabilized by an outer CS coating. Upon application, the MNs mechanically penetrate the skin barrier and dissolve at physiological temperature, releasing CNPs. The surface CS facilitates CD44 receptor-mediated targeting and intracellular uptake in melanoma cells. Once internalized, the CNPs disassemble to release their payload: the RNP complex mediates YB-1 gene knockout in the nucleus, while DOX induces apoptosis via DNA intercalation. This novel MN-based strategy aims to overcome current delivery bottlenecks, offering a potent and precise therapeutic approach for melanoma.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of CNPs@DOX@RNP/MN, illustrating its preparation, in vivo delivery, and intracellular drug delivery mechanism.

Materials & methods

Expression and purification of Cas9 and Cas9-EGFP fusion proteins

Primers were designed to construct the nuclear localization signal (NLS) contained pET28a-Cas9-6×His-2NLS and pET28a-Cas9-EGFP-6×His-2NLS recombinant plasmids to enhance the ability of protein to enter the nucleus [40]. The plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) competent cells. Protein expression was induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) when the bacterial culture reached OD600 ≈ 0.8. Cells were collected by centrifugation, resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and lysed using a low-temperature ultra-high-pressure continuous flow cell disruptor (JNBIO, Guangzhou, China). The supernatant was collected via centrifugation and loaded onto Ni-NTA affinity chromatography columns. Cas9 proteins were purified by stepwise elution with imidazole solutions (100 mM, 200 mM, 300 mM).

RNP preparation

Two YB-1-targeting single-guide RNA (sgYB-1) sequences were designed. sgYB-1 sequences containing T7 promoters were obtained by PCR amplification followed by in vitro transcription using the Thermo Scientific™ TranscriptAid™ T7 High Yield Transcription Kit. The Cas9 protein was mixed with sgYB-1 in a molar ratio of 1:3, incubated at 37 °C for 10 min to form an RNP complex.

NP preparation and characterization

NPs were prepared via the thin-film hydration method. PEI-OA, Solutol® HS 15 (HS15) and DOX were mixed in ethanol at a 6.2:30:1 mass ratio. Organic solvents were removed by rotary evaporation at 37 °C, followed by hydration with 60 °C reverse osmosis (RO) water to obtain DOX loaded NPs (NPs@DOX). Particle size and zeta potential were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZSP (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). NPs@DOX and RNP were mixed at a 10:1 mass ratio and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min to form DOX and RNP loaded NPs (NPs@DOX@RNP). NPs@DOX@RNP and CS (M.W.: 50-80 kDa) were then combined at a 1:2 mass ratio and incubated for 10 min to yield CS-coated NPs@DOX@RNP (CNPs@DOX@RNP).

HA, DOX, and RNP were mixed and incubated at room temperature for 10 min to prepare DOX and RNP loaded HA (HA@DOX@RNP). CS, DOX, and RNP were combined and incubated under identical conditions to obtain DOX and RNP loaded CS (CS@DOX@RNP).

To confirm dual loading of DOX and RNP, RNP loaded NPs (NPs@RNP), CS-coated NPs@RNP (CNPs@RNP), CNPs@DOX@RNP, and HA@DOX@RNP were prepared. Particle size and zeta potential of all six formulations were analyzed using the Zetasizer Nano ZSP. Morphology of PEI-OA, CS@DOX@RNP, and CNPs@DOX@RNP was examined by 200 kV transmission electron microscope (TEM, 200 kV, Thermo, USA).

In vitro release study

Fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled bovine serum albumin (FITC-BSA) replaced RNP for fluorescence quantification. PBS containing FITC-BSA (PBS@FITC-BSA), HA loaded with FITC-BSA (HA@FITC-BSA), CS loaded with FITC-BSA (CS@FITC-BSA), NPs loaded with FITC-BSA (NPs@FITC-BSA) and CNPs loaded with FITC-BSA (CNPs@FITC-BSA) (150 μL each, containing 40 μg FITC-BSA) were loaded onto 8-layer gauze. PBS containing DOX (PBS@DOX), HA loaded with DOX (HA@DOX), CS loaded with DOX (CS@DOX), NPs loaded with DOX (NPs@DOX) and CNPs loaded with DOX (CNPs@DOX) (1 mL each, containing 20 μg DOX) were placed in dialysis bags (MWCO 1 kDa). Samples were immersed in 40 mL PBS (pH 7.4) in amber wide-mouth bottles and incubated at 37 °C with shaking (100 rpm). At 1, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48 and 72 h, 2 mL aliquots were collected and replaced with fresh PBS. FITC-BSA release was quantified fluorometrically at 470 nm excitation wavelength, while DOX release was measured at 525 nm excitation wavelength using SpectraMax M2e (Molecular Devices, USA). Cumulative release (%) was calculated based on standard curves:Cumulativerelease(%)=(Cn×40+∑i=1n‐1Ci×2)A×100Note: Cn: Drug concentration at time point n; Ci: Drug concentration of the sample i; A: The total amount of drug in the hydrogel.

MN patch preparation and characterization

MN patches were fabricated using silicone molds with pyramidal cavities (410 × 410 μm base, 800 μm height) arranged in an 11 × 11 array. Three different types of MNs were prepared: MNs made with HA as the main needle tip material (HA/MN), MNs made with CS as the main needle tip material (CS/MN), and MNs made with CNPs as the main needle tip material (CNPs/MN). In the absence of drug loading, the needle tip composition of HA/MN consists of 10 wt% HA and 1 wt‰ PEG6000, whereas the tips of CS/MN and CNPs/MN are composed of 50 wt% CS and 5 wt‰ PEG6000. The rest of the MN is ultra-pure water, and the drug in the drug-loaded MN is also dissolved in water. The fabrication process of MN patch involves the following steps. 150 μL MN solution was evenly spread over the mold. Vacuum degassing ensured cavity penetration, followed by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 2 min. The mold was rotated 180° and centrifuged again. After drying at 4 °C for 1 h, 500 μL 10% HA backing solution was added. Complete drying occurred at 4 °C for 48 h before demolding. For mechanical strength testing, depilated BALB/c mouse dorsal skin was pressed with HA/MN without PEG6000 (HA/MN(−)), CS/MN without PEG6000 (CS/MN(−)), HA/MN and CS/MN for 3 min. After euthanasia, skin samples were stained with 0.4% trypan blue for 20 min, washed, and penetration sites were examined under an IX73 inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan). HA/MN and CS/MN were imaged using a high-resolution smartphone (Huawei Mate60 Pro). CS/MN microstructure was characterized by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; OPTON, Beijing, China). All animal protocols were in accordance with institutional and local ethical regulations and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Hubei University (No.20240102, Hubei, China).

Fluorescence imaging of drug-loaded MNs

Based on the requirements of visualization research, we employed DiD dye instead of DOX and used FITC-BSA to replace RNP. To visualize drug loading within MN needle tips, DiD and FITC-BSA loaded HA/MN (HA@DiD@FITC-BSA/MN), and DiD and FITC-BSA loaded CNPs/MN (CNPs@DiD@FITC) formulations were prepared (containing 40 μg FITC-BSA and 20 μg DiD per MN). After fabrication, fluorescence images were acquired using a two-photon high-speed ultra-high resolution imaging system (LSM980) (ZEISS, Germany).

Evaluation of transdermal drug delivery behavior

To track drug release dynamics post-MN administration, CNPs@DiD@FITC-BSA/MN patches, 20 μg DiD and 40 μg FITC-BSA per MN, were applied to BALB/c mouse skin. BALB/c mice were sacrificed at 0.5, 4, 8 and 24 h post-application. Skin sections were prepared and imaged using LSM980.

Drug delivery and tumor targeting ability of MNs in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo drug delivery capacity and tumor targeting ability of different MNs, three groups of MN formulations with two fluorescent labels were prepared: HA@DiD@FITC-BSA/MN, DiD and FITC-BSA loaded CS/MN (CS@DiD@FITC-BSA/MN), and CNPs@DiD@FITC-BSA/MN. The depilated BALB/c mouse back was divided into three areas, each treated with one of these formulations. Fluorescence intensity was monitored at 4, 24, 48, and 72 h using an IVIS Lumina III imaging system (PerkinElmer, MA, USA).

Skin healing assessment

Following depilation, MN patches were applied to BALB/c mouse dorsa. Skin samples collected at 0, 4, and 12 h were sectioned, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and examined under an IX73 inverted microscope.

Cell transfection studies

To visually track the intracellular distribution of RNP, we expressed and purified Cas9 protein fused with green fluorescent protein (Cas9-EGFP) in E.coli. B16F10 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 per well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, B16F10 cells were treated with DiD and Cas9-EGFP PBS solution (PBS@DiD@Cas9-EGFP), DiD and Cas9-EGFP loaded HA matrix solution (HA@DiD@Cas9-EGFP), DiD and Cas9-EGFP loaded CS matrix solution (CS@DiD@Cas9-EGFP), DiD and Cas9-EGFP loaded CS and PEI matrix solution (CP@DiD@Cas9-EGFP, the amount of PEI is the same as the amount of PEI contained in NP), DiD and Cas9-EGFP loaded NPs (NPs@DiD@Cas9-EGFP), DiD and Cas9-EGFP loaded CNPs (CNPs@DiD@Cas9-EGFP), and DiD and Cas9-EGFP loaded Lipofectamine CRISPRMax™ (LIPO, Thermo Fisher, USA) (LIPO@DiD@Cas9-EGFP, the amount of LIPO is 5 μL/well). Each formulation contained 0.5 μg DiD and 4 μg Cas9-EGFP in DMEM. After 6 h incubation, cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde, stained with DAPI, and imaged by LSM980.

Additionally, the aforementioned formulation was applied to B16F10 cells seeded in a 6-well plate. After incubation for 6 h, the cells were washed three times with PBS, then digested with trypsin, centrifuged at 900×g for 3 min, resuspended in 500 μL of PBS, and finally analyzed using a flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, NJ, USA).

Lysosomal escape assay

B16F10 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 per well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, B16F10 cells were treated with Cas9-EGFP contained PBS (PBS@Cas9-EGFP), Cas9-EGFP loaded HA matrix solution (HA@Cas9-EGFP), Cas9-EGFP loaded CS matrix solution (CS@Cas9-EGFP) and Cas9-EGFP loaded CNPs matrix solution (CNPs@Cas9-EGFP) (4 μg Cas9-EGFP per group in lysosome-tracker-containing DMEM). After 2 h and 6 h, cells were fixed, DAPI-stained, and analyzed by LSM980.

GFP knockout assessment

To confirm the gene-editing capability of the delivered RNP, we designed and prepared a GFP-targeting single-guide RNA (sgGFP). Cas9 protein was assembled with sgGFP to form RNPGFP. 293T-GFP cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 per well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, 293T-GFP cells were treated with PBS@RNPGFP, HA@RNPGFP, CS@RNPGFP, CNPs@RNPGFP and LIPO@RNPGFP in fresh DMEM. GFP knockout efficiency was detected at 0, 8, 24, and 48 h using IX73 inverted microscope.

T7 endonuclease I (T7EI) assay

B16F10 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 per well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, B16F10 cells were treated with RNP contained PBS (PBS@RNP), RNP loaded HA matrix solution (HA@RNP), RNP loaded CS matrix solution (CS@RNP), and CNPs@RNP for 24 h at 37 °C. Genomic DNA was extracted by Genome-Editing Mutation Detection Kit. Target regions were amplified by PCR. 200 ng PCR products were digested with T7EI at 37 °C for 30 min. Cleaved fragments were separated on 2% agarose gels using a GelDoc XR + system (Bio-Rad, USA). Mutation frequency was quantified by ImageLab 5.0 software.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of the blank carrier against B16F10 and 293T cells was evaluated, while the cytotoxicity of the DOX-loaded formulation and the formulation containing both DOX and RNP against B16F10 cells was also measured. B16F10 and 293T cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 per well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, cells in 96-well plates were treated with above formulations in 2% fetal bovine serum and 0.2% penicillin-streptomycin containing DMEM for 24 h. Methylthiazolyldiphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) solution (20 μL, 5 mg/mL) was added for 4 h. Formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO (150 μL/well). The absorbance at 570 nm was measured using SpectraMax M2, with cell viability (%) calculated relative to 2% DMEM-treated controls using the following formula.CellViability(%)=(ODs‐ODb)/(ODc‐ODb)×100Note: ODs: Optical density (OD) of experimental group; ODc: OD of control group; ODb: OD of blank group.

Colony formation assay

B16F10 cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 per well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, mixtures of PBS containing DOX and RNP (PBS@DOX@RNP), HA@DOX@RNP, CS@DOX@RNP, and CNPs@DOX@RNP in fresh DMEM were added. After 6 h incubation at 37 °C, cells were trypsinized and reseeded into 12-well plates. Culture was terminated when colonies in the PBS@DOX@RNP group reached ≥ 50 cells per clone. Cells were washed with PBS, stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min, imaged, and colony areas were quantified using ImageJ 1.53c software.

Apoptosis assay

B16F10 cells were seeded in a 12-well plate at 1 × 105 cells/well. After 24 h, the cells were treated for 6 h with culture media containing PBS@DOX@RNP, HA@DOX@RNP, CNPs, CNPs@DOX, or CNPs@DOX@RNP. Cells were then washed twice with PBS, trypsinized, and collected (5 × 104–1 × 105 cells per group) into 2 mL EP tubes. After centrifugation at 1000×g for 5 min, supernatants were removed, and cell pellets were resuspended in 195 μL of binding buffer. Annexin V-FITC (5 μL) and PI (10 μL) were added, mixed, and incubated for 10-20 min at room temperature in the dark. Aliquots of stained cells were examined under an inverted fluorescence microscope for visualization, while the remainder was analyzed by flow cytometry.

The PBS@DOX@RNP, HA@DOX@RNP, CS@DOX@RNP, CNPs@DOX@RNP, and DOX and RNP loaded LIPO (LIPO@DOX@RNP) groups were set up and subjected to the same procedures as described above. Finally, the cells in the EP tubes were transferred to culture dishes and resuspended by pipetting. Cell apoptosis was then analyzed using an IX73 inverted microscope.

Anti-tumor efficacy

Melanoma murine models were established by shaving dorsal hair and subcutaneously injecting 5 × 106 B16F10 cells to C57BL/6 mice. Twenty-five C57BL/6 mice were divided into control group, DOX loaded HA/MN (HA@DOX/MN), DOX loaded CS/MN (CS@DOX/MN), DOX loaded CNPs/MN (CNPs@DOX/MN), DOX and RNP loaded CNPs/MN (CNPs@DOX@RNP/MN). Each CNPs@DOX@RNP/MN delivered 20 μg Cas9 protein and 12 μg sgRNA. Each treatment group encapsulated 50 μg DOX per MN. The first treatment was given on the fifth day after tumor cell inoculation. During administration, the MN patch was pressed on the surface of the tumor for 3 min, and the MN patch was further fixed with medical tape to ensure continuous drug release. The mice were treated on day 5, 8, and 11. Body weight and tumor volume (V = 0.5×length×width2) were recorded every 2 days. On day 23, C57BL/6 mice were euthanized after tumors were photographed. Tumor weight was recorded. Blood were collected for hematological analysis. Tumors and major organs were collected for histological analysis. Tumor sections were subjected to immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for Ki67, CD31 and PD-L1, along with Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase mediated dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL), YB-1 Immunofluorescence staining and H&E staining. Stained sections were imaged by LSM710 confocal microscope (LSM710) (ZEISS, Germany). The main tissues and organs were sectioned and stained with H&E and T7EI assay to evaluate the toxicity of the preparation on normal tissues.

Statistical analysis

Difference between two groups was calculated by using student's t-test. When the comparison was performed among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance was performed. The p value was denoted by * for p < 0.05, ** for p < 0.01, *** for p < 0.001 and **** for p < 0.0001. When there are two independent variables, two-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparison test was performed. GraphPad Prism (Version8.02, GraphPad Software, Inc.) was used.

Cas9 and Cas9-EGFP proteins were expressed and purified. Primers (Table S1) were designed to construct pET28a-Cas9-6×His-2NLS (Fig. S3) and pET28a-Cas9-EGFP-6×His-2NLS recombinant plasmids (Fig. S4). Protein expression was carried out in E.coli BL21 competent cells, followed by bacterial harvesting and protein purification. After extracting cDNA from B16F10 cells, the YBX1 (Table S2) fragment was amplified and ligated into the pUC18 plasmid (Fig. S5) for subsequent in vitro restriction enzyme digestion assays to screen sgYB-1. Two sgYB-1s were designed (Table S2), with T7 promoter-containing sequences obtained via PCR and in vitro transcription. RNP complexes were formed by incubating Cas9 protein with sgYB-1 at 37 °C for 10 min. In vitro cleavage assays (Fig. S6) confirmed the superior efficiency of sgYB-1-1, which was thus selected for subsequent experiments.

Zeta potential and particle size of NPs@RNP, CNPs@RNP, CNPs@DOX@RNP, and HA@DOX@RNP are presented in Fig. 2C and D. CNPs@DOX@RNP exhibited a zeta potential of −5.37 mV. All formulations had diameters < 200 nm, with NPs@RNP, CNPs@RNP, and CNPs@DOX@RNP demonstrating progressively increasing sizes, confirming successful electrostatic binding of RNP and CS to NPs. The NPs were incubated in 10% serum-containing medium at 37 °C to evaluate their serum stability. As shown in Fig. S7, there was no significant change in the zeta potential, and the particle size remained stable (<200 nm) over 48 h. This indicates good colloidal stability, despite the relatively neutral zeta. The RNP encapsulation efficiencies of different NPs were determined using a Coomassie G-250 protein standard curve (Fig. S8A). The encapsulation efficiency of CNPs reached 78.88%, showing a significant difference compared to the 57.23% observed for NPs (Fig. 2E). This indicates that the incorporation of CS enhances the RNP encapsulation capacity of NPs. Consequently, this allows for a reduction in NP dosage during drug delivery, thereby minimizing potential toxicity to normal cells.

We employed FITC-BSA, a commonly used model protein in research, as a surrogate for Cas9 to investigate the in vitro release rate. The physicochemical properties of Cas9 and BSA are presented in Table S3. To investigate the in vitro drug release behaviors of the NPs, we first established standard curves for FITC-BSA (Fig. S8B) and DOX (Fig. S8C) using data obtained from a microplate reader. Subsequently, the cumulative release rates of PBS@FITC-BSA, HA@FITC-BSA, CS@FITC-BSA, CNPs@FITC-BSA, PBS@DOX, HA@DOX, CS@DOX, and CNPs@DOX were calculated at various time points. The release of FITC-BSA from CNPs@FITC-BSA exhibited a gradual upward trend, reaching 72.83% at 72 h (Fig. 2F). Similarly, the release of DOX from CNPs@DOX showed a slow and sustained increase, achieving a release rate of 66.31% at 72 h (Fig. 2G). These results demonstrate that CNPs possess a certain sustained-release effect. We hypothesize that the CS coating on the NP surface likely retards the release of FITC-BSA and DOX from the NP interior to some extent. The study of in vitro release kinetics theoretically confirms that CNPs exert a sustained-release effect in delivering both DOX and RNP. By prolonging the drug release duration and achieving release amounts exceeding 65% for both agents, CNPs can maintain stable drug concentrations to enhance therapeutic efficacy. This sustained release profile reduces dosing frequency, improves patient compliance, and optimizes drug utilization, thereby lowering the total administered dose and drug load, which ultimately alleviates the body's overall metabolic burden and the risk of long-term accumulation. TEM imaging (Fig. 2H) revealed that CNPs displayed uniform spherical morphology and smaller dimensions. In contrast, CS@DOX@RNP exhibited ill-defined boundaries and aggregation tendencies, with no distinct NPs observed. A clear interface was evident between the NPs and the MN matrix in the CNPs@DOX@RNP group, suggesting structural integrity within the MN material. It was an advantageous feature for drug delivery applications.

The amphiphilic, cationic NPs were selected as the nanoplatform core due to their favorable charge characteristics, biocompatibility [41], and capacity for RNP integration via electrostatic interactions. CS coating was applied to mitigate off-target toxicity and enhance in vivo safety. The fabrication of CNPs combines simplicity, environmental compatibility, and excellent scalability for industrial production.

Preparation and characterization of MNs

The backing material for MNs was selected as 10% HA. MN needle tips require sufficient mechanical strength and good biocompatibility. Monocomponent HA and CS MNs exhibit poor formability and brittleness, necessitating the addition of excipients. Based on existing research, PEG6000 possesses excellent biocompatibility and enhances sustained drug release, is widely used as a thickener [42]. We prepared HA/MN, CS/MN, HA/MN(−) and CS/MN(−). Mechanical strength testing on BALB/c mouse skin compared to HA/MN(−) and CS/MN(−) showed that HA/MN and CS/MN demonstrated adequate mechanical strength, uniform needle tip shape, and excellent transdermal delivery capability (Fig. S9). The morphology of the MN was observed after 1 min of dissolution at 32 °C (simulating skin surface temperature [43]) and 37 °C. As shown in Fig. S10, the MN tips exhibited significant dissolution at both temperatures, with only minor differences observed. This suggests that temperature has a limited effect on the release rate. Therefore, an application time of 2 min on mouse skin was set to ensure sufficient drug delivery from the MN tips.

Photographs of HA/MN and CS/MN (Fig. 3A) show intact, conical needle tips conforming to mold specifications. For detailed needle tip observation, SEM imaging was performed (Fig. 3B). Effective genome editing by CNPs@RNP

To assess RNP delivery efficiency, we conducted a GFP knockout assay using sgGFP (Table S2) in 293T-GFP cells treated with various formulations (PBS@RNPGFP, HA@RNPGFP, CS@RNPGFP, CNPs@RNPGFP, LIPO@RNPGFP). Time-course fluorescence imaging (Fig. 5B) showed striking differences in editing efficacy: control groups (PBS@RNPGFP, HA@RNPGFP, CS@RNPGFP) displayed minimal fluorescence reduction with full signal recovery by 48 h, while LIPO@RNPGFP mediated only partial knockout. In contrast, CNPs@RNPGFP achieved rapid and durable gene editing. This superior performance stems from our multi-step targeting mechanism: (1) CNPs-facilitated cellular RNP delivery, (2) NLS-guided Cas9 transport, and (3) sgRNA-directed target recognition and binding [40]. Having validated this precision editing platform, we next applied structure-optimized RNPs targeting therapeutically relevant YB-1 for comprehensive functional characterization.

We investigated the effects of different material-delivered RNP on YB-1 expression in B16F10 cells using Western blotting. The results showed that the Cas9 protein transfection in cells treated with CNPs@RNP was significantly higher than other groups (Fig. 5C). At the same time, the expression of YB-1 in B16F10 cells treated with CNPs@RNP was significantly reduced compared with other groups (Fig. 5D). It was shown that CNPs successfully transfected RNP into B16F10 cells and effectively reduced the expression of YB-1 gene.

The T7EI assay was conducted to assess the gene editing efficiency of CNPs@RNP (Fig. 5E). Genomic DNA extracted from B16F10 cells treated with PBS@RNP, HA@RNP, CS@RNP and CNPs@RNP was analyzed using the T7EI kit. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated YB-1 gene disruption was most efficient with CNPs@RNP. Gel electrophoresis analysis showed that CNPs@RNP yielded the faintest uncut band intensity alongside a prominent cleaved product at 103 bp, confirming efficient target DNA cleavage. Quantitative analysis revealed significantly higher editing efficiency for CNPs@RNP (65.32%) versus control groups: CS@RNP (7.95%), HA@RNP (1.25%), and PBS@RNP (normalized to 1%) (p < 0.001). Moreover, CNPs@RNP successfully disrupted the YB-1 gene.

In summary, the CNPs delivery platform exhibited superior performance by: (1) enabling efficient RNP delivery targeting both reporter (GFP) and therapeutic (YB-1) genes in multiple cell models (B16F10 and 293T-GFP); (2) promoting effective lysosomal escape; and (3) accomplishing high-fidelity genome editing in vitro. These findings collectively demonstrate CNPs as a versatile and potent vehicle for CRISPR/Cas9 RNP delivery and genome editing applications.

Nevertheless, the measured AST values in the remaining four groups exceeded the normal limits. This elevation is presumably attributed to the impact of excessive tumor growth. We also tested the editing efficiency of YB-1 in major organs of mice treated with CNPs@DOX@RNP/MN by T7EI. The results showed that CNPs@DOX@RNP/MN did not affect major organs (Fig. S16). CS modification reduced NP toxicity while enhancing biocompatibility, thereby preventing organ damage and inflammation. These results collectively demonstrate the high safety and biocompatibility profile of our delivery system. Even with repeated administrations, no significant adverse effects were observed, including: (1) minimal body weight fluctuations (<5% change vs. controls); (2) no histopathological abnormalities (as assessed by H&E staining of major organs); (3) stable serum biochemical parameters (ALT, AST, UREA, and CREA remaining within normal ranges).

In conclusion, the CNPs@DOX@RNP/MN platform achieves potent anti-melanoma effects through transdermal co-delivery of YB-1-targeting RNPs and DOX, demonstrating synergistic therapeutic efficacy alongside excellent biosafety. This dual-action system simultaneously disrupts oncogenic pathways via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated YB-1 knockout and induces chemotoxic DNA damage, while the MN array ensures localized delivery that minimizes systemic exposure. The combined pharmacological and genetic intervention represents a promising strategy for melanoma treatment, particularly for inoperable lesions or as a neoadjuvant approach prior to surgical resection. However, further studies, including survival analysis, immune profiling, and metastasis assessment, are required to fully evaluate its translational potential.

Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully developed a multifunctional MN delivery system for the transdermal co-delivery of YB-1-targeting RNP and DOX for the treatment of murine melanoma. We engineered a two-stage platform in which RNP- and DOX-loaded CNPs were depot-loaded into MNs composed of CS and PEG6000. Mechanistically, the CNPs@DOX@RNP/MN system demonstrated strong internalization by melanoma cells. Upon skin insertion, the highly biocompatible MNs created micrometer-scale pores, facilitating the efficient delivery of drug-loaded CNPs directly to the melanoma tissue, thereby enabling intracellular gene editing and chemotherapy. This approach effectively suppressed the proliferation and metastasis of B16F10 cells.

In vitro results collectively indicated that CNPs offered superior delivery performance, achieving 74.4% cellular uptake of both drugs and over 60% gene editing efficiency. This translated into potent antitumor activity, with CNPs@DOX@RNP suppressing cell survival to as low as 3.51%, alongside reduced off-target effects. Crucially, in vivo application of the CNPs@DOX@RNP/MN system proved highly effective, resulting in a 92.56% decrease in final tumor weights as compared with untreated controls. Overall, this novel multifunctional MN system provides a promising synergistic therapeutic strategy for melanoma and serves as a valuable blueprint for the development of safe and effective in vivo RNP delivery platforms.

Looking forward, future iterations of this system could incorporate stimuli-responsive mechanisms, such as pH or photothermal sensitivity, to achieve more precise and controllable drug release, thereby further enhancing antitumor efficacy. This nanoparticle - microneedle delivery system can be extended to other cutaneous indications, including psoriasis and skin senescence. Additionally, the tumor model employed in this study has certain limitations; future investigations using clinically more relevant models are warranted to further validate these findings.
