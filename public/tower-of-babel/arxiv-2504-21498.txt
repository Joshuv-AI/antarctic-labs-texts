Domain Wall as Cosmological Oscillator
Bo-Qiang Lu

Abstract
In this study, we examine the domain wall within the framework of a cosmological harmonic oscillator. We investigate the interaction between the domain wall and a periodic background field, which can induce perturbations in the oscillatory behavior of the wall. We propose a novel mechanism for resolving the domain wall problem through the phenomenon of resonant oscillation. Resonant oscillation occurs when the frequency of the external driving force aligns with the intrinsic frequency of the domain wall. This synchrony can significantly amplify the amplitude of the oscillation. If the amplitude of oscillation exceeds a predetermined critical deformation threshold, the domain wall may be deconstructed. Furthermore, we demonstrate that this mechanism remains valid in models that preserve discrete symmetry.

Introduction

Domain walls are topological defects that emerge due to spontaneous symmetry breaking in the early universe
Kibble:1976sj
. During phase transitions, as the universe cooled below critical temperatures, scalar fields settled into distinct vacuum states separated by energy barriers. When the symmetry breaking involves a discrete symmetry, such as
Z_{2}
, adjacent regions of space may adopt different vacuum configurations, leading to the formation of stable two-dimensional structures known as domain walls at their boundaries. For instance, in the Two-Higgs-Doublet Model
Lee:1973iz
;
Preskill:1991kd
;
Gunion:2002zf
, the spontaneous breakdown of
Z_{2}
symmetry during electroweak symmetry breaking results in the formation of domain walls between regions characterized by distinct vacua. Similarly, early-universe phase transitions involving exotic fields, including axions
Sikivie:1982qv
;
Preskill:1991kd
, axion-like particles, or dark matter
Chao:2017vrq
;
Huang:2017kzu
;
Grzadkowski:2018nbc
;
Chiang:2019oms
;
Chiang:2020yym
, may generate extensive networks of domain walls.

The presence of domain walls presents significant cosmological challenges, as they can dominate the universe’s energy density and leave observable cosmological signatures. For instance, if domain walls persist long enough to account for approximately
10\%
of the total energy density, their annihilation at the QCD scale could provide potential signals for recent observations by pulsar timing arrays (PTAs)
Chiang:2020aui
;
Lu:2023mcz
, including findings from the NANOGrav 15-year dataset
NANOGrav:2023gor
and IPTA-DR2
Antoniadis:2022pcn
. Recently, we demonstrated that isocurvature perturbations arising from Poisson fluctuations in domain wall networks can seed horizon-sized overdensities, leading to the formation of primordial black holes through the collapse of these overdensities
Lu:2024ngi
;
Lu:2024szr
. Additionally, the curvature perturbation from the domain wall network has the potential to induce primordial gravitational wave radiation, as shown in Ref.
Lu:2024dzj
.

A primary solution to the so-called “cosmological catastrophe”, which describes the scenario in which stable domain walls dominate the universe’s energy density, involves the introduction of energy bias terms into the scalar field potential
Dine:1993yw
;
Dvali:1994wv
. These terms explicitly break the discrete symmetry responsible for domain wall formation, thereby destabilizing the network and facilitating its annihilation. For example, in models characterized by the scalar field potential
V(\phi)=\frac{\lambda}{4}(\phi^{2}-v^{2})^{2}+\mu\phi^{3}
, the bias term
\mu\phi^{3}
disrupts the
Z_{2}
symmetry and generates a preference for one vacuum state over others. This asymmetry leads to the collapse of domain walls, which subsequently decay into gravitational waves or radiation prior to dominating the universe’s energy budget. Numerical simulations of collapsing domain wall networks corroborate that such mechanisms produce exponential decay of false vacuum volume
Kawasaki:2014sqa
, with annihilation timescales consistent with observational limits.

In addition to bias-induced annihilation, various other mechanisms have been proposed to resolve the domain wall issue. Metastable domain walls may decay via quantum tunneling, enabling them to surmount energy barriers that are classically insurmountable, leading to their annihilation
Kibble:1982dd
;
Preskill:1992ck
. The collapse or fragmentation of domain wall networks can occur through the nucleation of string-bounded holes or interactions with cosmic strings
Dunsky:2021tih
. During temperature-driven phase transitions, the wall’s surface tension may exhibit temperature dependence, leading to the melting of the wall as the universe expands and the temperature decreases
Babichev:2021uvl
.

In this work, we propose to treat the domain wall as an oscillator, with its elasticity determined by its surface tension and thickness. The interaction between the domain wall and the background field can act as an exotic force, inducing oscillations in the wall. The background field itself may also oscillate periodically. Resonant oscillation of the wall occurs when the oscillation frequency of the background field matches the natural frequency of the wall, potentially resulting in destructive oscillations.
In the context of particle physics, scalar fields that undergo periodic oscillations, expressed as
\phi=\phi_{0}\cos(\omega t)
, play crucial roles in numerous theoretical models, including the inflaton field from cosmic inflation
Khlebnikov:1996mc
, axion fields to address the strong CP problem
DiLuzio:2020wdo
, moduli fields in string theory
Thorn:1988hm
or after compactification in higher-dimensional spacetimes
Otsuka:2022vgf
, and quintessence fields as models for dark energy
Tsujikawa:2013fta
. Consequently, the existence of a periodic force is anticipated across a broad theoretical framework.

2
Domain Walls Interacting with Periodic Background Fields

By establishing interaction between the domain wall and a temporally periodic background field, a periodic driving force can be effectively induced. The frequency
\omega
of this driving force serves as a direct characterization of the temporal period of the driving mechanism. A detailed analysis of this phenomenon is presented as follows.

2.1
Model Setup

In cosmological contexts, the equation of motion governing a scalar field is rigorously derived from its Lagrangian density within the theoretical framework provided by the Einstein field equations. The line element characterizing a homogeneous, expanding Friedmann-Robertson-Walker (FRW) universe is mathematically expressed as:

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)(dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}),

(1)

Here,
x,y,z
denote comoving spatial coordinates, while
t
represents cosmic time and
a(t)
represents the universe’s scale factor.

Consider a domain wall field
\phi(x,t)
whose dynamics are governed by the following Lagrangian density:

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi-V(\phi),

(2)

The contravariant components of the FRW metric are given by:

g^{\mu\nu}=\text{diag}\left(-1,\frac{1}{a(t)^{2}},\frac{1}{a(t)^{2}},\frac{1}{a(t)^{2}}\right),

(3)

The total potential of the domain wall is expressed as
V(\phi)=V_{0}(\phi)+V_{\text{int}}(\phi)
. The kinetic portion of the Lagrangian density can be expanded as:

-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}-\frac{1}{2a(t)^{2}}(\nabla\phi)^{2},

(4)

where
\dot{\phi}=\partial_{t}\phi
represents the time derivative and
\nabla\phi
denotes the spatial gradient.

To simplify the analysis, we adopt a
Z_{2}
-symmetric double-well potential:

V_{0}(\phi)=\frac{\lambda}{4}(\phi^{2}-v^{2})^{2}

(5)

Furthermore, we introduce a coupling between the domain wall field
\phi(x)
and a background field through the interaction potential:

V_{\text{int}}(\phi,t)=\epsilon\phi\psi(t)=\epsilon\phi\psi_{0}\cos\left(\omega t\right),

(6)

where
\epsilon
is a dimension-2 coupling constant. The background scalar field
\psi(t)
exhibits spatial homogeneity and temporal periodicity, specifically:

\psi(t)=\psi_{0}\cos\left(\omega t\right),

(7)

with
\omega
denoting the angular frequency of the field.
It should be noted that the introduced interaction term (
6
) explicitly breaks the
Z_{2}
symmetry of the system.

2.2
Equation of Motion for the Scalar Field

In curved spacetime, the dynamics of a scalar field are governed by the Euler-Lagrange equation:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\mu}\left(\sqrt{-g}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\left(\partial_{\mu}\phi\right)}\right)=0

(8)

Substituting the Lagrangian density (
2
) and utilizing the relations:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\left(\partial_{\mu}\phi\right)}=-g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\phi,\penalty\ \penalty\ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi}=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial\phi},

(9)

yields the equation of motion:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\mu}\left(\sqrt{-g}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\phi\right)+\frac{\partial V}{\partial\phi}=0.

(10)

Within the FRW metric framework,
\sqrt{-g}=a(t)^{3}
. Upon substitution and expansion, we obtain:

\frac{1}{a(t)^{3}}\partial_{t}\left(a(t)^{3}\dot{\phi}\right)-\frac{1}{a(t)^{2}}\nabla^{2}\phi+\lambda\phi(\phi^{2}-v^{2})+\epsilon\psi_{0}\cos\left(\omega t\right)=0.

(11)

Further expanding the time derivative term:

\frac{1}{a(t)^{3}}\partial_{t}\left(a(t)^{3}\dot{\phi}\right)=\ddot{\phi}+3H\dot{\phi},

(12)

where
H=\dot{a}/a
denotes the Hubble parameter.
Collecting all terms, the equation of motion for the scalar field
\phi
in an expanding FRW universe is:

\boxed{\ddot{\phi}+3H\dot{\phi}-\frac{1}{a(t)^{2}}\nabla^{2}\phi+\lambda\phi(\phi^{2}-v^{2})+\epsilon\psi_{0}\cos\left(\omega t\right)=0.}

(13)

Key components of this equation include:

•

Hubble damping term
3H\dot{\phi}
: This term arises from the energy dilution effect of cosmic expansion, acting as a damping mechanism on the scalar field’s kinetic energy.

•

Spatial derivative correction
-\frac{1}{a(t)^{2}}\nabla^{2}\phi
: This term reflects the impact of cosmic expansion on spatial coordinates. The gradient energy contribution decays in proportion to
a(t)
due to the stretching of spatial dimensions.

•

Periodic driving term
\epsilon\psi_{0}\cos\left(\omega t\right)
: This term introduces a temporally periodic modulation of the domain wall. Resonant oscillations may be induced if the driving frequency aligns with the domain wall’s natural oscillation frequency.

3
Derivation of Domain Wall Motion Equation

The perturbation caused by the external force results in vibrations of the domain wall around its equilibrium position. In this section, we will expand the perturbed potential around the static solution and derive the collective coordinate equation of motion for the domain wall. This approach will allow us to analyze the oscillatory behavior of the wall in response to the applied perturbations and to characterize the dynamics governing its vibrations.

3.1
Perturbation Expansion of Scalar Field Potential

In the absence of the interaction between the domain wall and the periodic field
\psi(t)
when
\epsilon=0
, the static solution
\phi_{0}(x)
satisfies:

-\frac{1}{a(t)^{2}}\partial_{x}^{2}\phi_{0}+\lambda\phi_{0}(\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})=0.

(14)

The corresponding domain wall solution is given by:

\phi_{0}(x)=v\tanh\left(\frac{x}{\delta}\right),\quad\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda}}\frac{1}{a(t)v}.

(15)

This solution interpolates between the two vacuum states
\phi=\pm v
and possesses a wall thickness
\delta
.

Under the assumption of weak periodic modulation (
\epsilon\psi_{0}\ll\lambda v^{3}
), we decompose the scalar field into a static solution and a perturbation term:

\displaystyle\phi(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(x-\xi(t))+\delta\phi(x,t)

(16)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(x)-\xi\partial_{x}\phi_{0}(x)+\delta\phi+\mathcal{O}(\xi^{2},\delta\phi^{2}).

Here,
\xi(t)
denotes the minor displacement of the domain wall position in the comoving coordinate (collective coordinate), and
\delta\phi(x,t)
represents local field fluctuations, which are assumed to be small and negligible to leading order.
Substituting the decomposed field into the equation of motion (
13
) and noting that
\partial_{t}\phi_{0}=0
, the derivative terms can be approximated as:

1.

Time derivative terms:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\dot{\xi}\phi_{0}^{\prime}+\partial_{t}\delta\phi+\mathcal{O}(\xi^{2},\delta\phi^{2}),

(17)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\phi

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\ddot{\xi}\phi_{0}^{\prime}+\dot{\xi}^{2}\phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\partial_{t}^{2}\delta\phi+\mathcal{O}(\xi^{3},\delta\phi^{2}),

(18)

2.

Spatial derivative term:

\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)\approx\phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\delta\phi^{\prime\prime}+\xi\partial_{x}^{3}\phi_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\xi^{2},\delta\phi^{2}),

(19)

where
\phi_{0}^{\prime}\equiv\partial_{x}\phi_{0}
and
\phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}\equiv\partial_{x}^{2}\phi_{0}
.

We proceed to consider the effects of small displacements and wavefunction deformations on the scalar potential. Expanding the potential energy around the static solution
\phi_{0}
up to second order yields:

V(\phi)\approx V(\phi_{0})+\left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial\phi}\right|_{\phi_{0}}(\delta\phi-\xi\phi_{0}^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial\phi^{2}}\right|_{\phi_{0}}(\delta\phi-\xi\phi_{0}^{\prime})^{2}.

(20)

The individual terms are calculated as follows:

•

Zeroth-order term:

V(\phi_{0})=\frac{\lambda}{4}(\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})^{2}+\epsilon\phi_{0}\psi(t).

(21)

•

First-order term:

\left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial\phi}\right|_{\phi_{0}}(\delta\phi-\xi\phi_{0}^{\prime})=[\lambda\phi_{0}(\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})+\epsilon\psi(t)](\delta\phi-\xi\phi_{0}^{\prime}).

(22)

•

Second-order term:

\frac{1}{2}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial\phi^{2}}\right|_{\phi_{0}}(\delta\phi-\xi\phi_{0}^{\prime})^{2}=\frac{\lambda}{2}(3\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})(\delta\phi-\xi\phi_{0}^{\prime})^{2}.

(23)

The total potential expansion up to second order comprises the following components:

V(\phi)=V(\phi_{0})+\Delta V_{\text{disp}}+\Delta V_{\text{defo}}+\Delta V_{\text{cross}},

(24)

where

•

Displacement-related terms:

\Delta V_{\text{disp}}=-\xi\phi_{0}^{\prime}[\lambda\phi_{0}(\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})+\psi(t)].

(25)

These terms arise from the displacement of the potential in position and contribute to a restoring force on the system.

•

Deformation-related terms:

\Delta V_{\text{defo}}=\lambda\phi_{0}(\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})\delta\phi+\frac{\lambda}{2}(3\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})\delta\phi^{2}+\epsilon\delta\phi\psi(t).

(26)

These terms describe the energy contribution from wavefunction deformation
\delta\phi
, corresponding to fluctuation modes in the field equation.

•

Cross terms:

\Delta V_{\text{cross}}=-\lambda(3\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})\xi\phi_{0}^{\prime}\delta\phi.

(27)

These terms represent the nonlinear coupling between displacement and deformation, which may lead to resonance enhancement or damping effects.

In this work, we restrict our analysis to the zeroth-order term and the first term on the right-hand side of the displacement-related terms. Consequently, the potential of the wall is approximated as:

V(\phi_{0})\simeq\frac{\lambda}{4}(\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})^{2}-\xi\phi_{0}^{\prime}[\lambda\phi_{0}(\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})+\psi(t)]+\epsilon\phi_{0}\psi_{0}\cos\left(\omega t\right).

(28)

The properties of deformation-related terms and their influence on the wall’s evolution will be investigated in Section
4
.

3.2
Collective Coordinate Equation of Motion

Substituting the potential (
28
) and the derivative terms (
17
)-(
19
) into the equation of motion (
13
) yields:

\dot{\xi}^{2}\phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}+3H\dot{\xi}\phi_{0}^{\prime}+\ddot{\xi}\phi_{0}^{\prime}-\frac{\phi_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{a^{2}}+\lambda\phi_{0}(\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})-\xi\phi_{0}^{\prime}\lambda(3\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})+\epsilon\psi_{0}\cos\left(\omega t\right)=0,

(29)

where the second-order perturbation terms, such as
\delta\phi^{\prime\prime}
and
\dot{\xi}\delta\phi^{\prime}
, have been neglected.
By invoking the static equation (
14
), equation (
94
) simplifies to:

\ddot{\xi}\phi_{0}^{\prime}+3H\dot{\xi}\phi_{0}^{\prime}-\xi\phi_{0}^{\prime}\lambda(3\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})+\epsilon\psi_{0}\cos\left(\omega t\right)=0,

(30)

with the
\dot{\xi}^{2}
term being neglected due to its second-order perturbative nature.

To project the equation onto the translation mode of the static solution
\phi_{0}^{\prime}
(the mode function), we perform the following steps:

1.

Weighting function: Multiply both sides by
\phi_{0}^{\prime}
and integrate over all space
(-\infty,\infty)
.

2.

Orthogonality: Utilize the orthogonality conditions
\int\phi_{0}\phi_{0}^{\prime}dx=0
and
\int(\phi_{0}^{\prime})^{2}dx=\text{constant}
, with higher-order fluctuation terms
\delta\phi
being negligible.

This procedure results in the equation of motion for the collective coordinate:

\sigma_{w}\ddot{\xi}+3H\sigma_{w}\dot{\xi}+k\xi=F_{\text{ext}},

(31)

where the surface tension of the wall is given by

\sigma_{w}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\phi_{0}^{\prime})^{2}dx=\frac{4v^{2}}{3\delta},

(32)

the elastic coefficient
k
is defined as the second derivative of potential energy with respect to displacement:

k=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\phi_{0}^{\prime})^{2}\lambda(3\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})dx=\frac{8\lambda v^{4}}{15\delta},

(33)

and the external force term is:

F_{\text{ext}}=-\epsilon\psi_{0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi_{0}^{\prime}\cos\left(\omega t\right)dx=-2\epsilon v\psi_{0}\cos(\omega t).

(34)

Substituting the external force term into Eq. (
31
), we derive the collective coordinate equation for the domain wall:

\boxed{\ddot{\xi}+\gamma\dot{\xi}+\omega_{0}^{2}\xi=F_{0}\cos\left(\omega t\right)},

(35)

where the domain wall’s natural frequency and the amplitude of the external force are given by:

\omega_{0}^{2}=\frac{k}{\sigma_{w}}=\frac{2}{5}\lambda v^{2}\quad{\rm and}\quad F_{0}=\frac{-2\epsilon v\psi_{0}}{\sigma_{w}},

(36)

respectively.
The damping coefficient
\gamma
arises from two energy dissipation mechanisms:

1.

Radiation damping
\gamma_{\rm rad}
.

2.

Cosmological damping
\gamma_{H}=3H
.

The total damping is expressed as:

\gamma=\gamma_{\text{rad}}+\gamma_{\text{H}}.

(37)

In the collective coordinate equation (
95
), the cosmological damping due to Hubble expansion is explicitly included.
The subsequent section will provide detailed calculations of the damping coefficient attributed to the radiation effect.

4
Damped Harmonic Oscillator Driven by Periodic External Force

Equation (
95
) represents the equation of motion for a damped harmonic oscillator that is subjected to a periodic external force. We will now examine the general solution of this equation and investigate the radiation emitted by the harmonic oscillator as a result of forced vibrations. This analysis will provide insights into the dynamical response of the oscillator and the characteristics of the radiation produced during its oscillatory motion.

4.1
Solution to the Equation

The corresponding homogeneous equation is
\ddot{\xi}+2\beta\dot{\xi}+\omega_{0}^{2}\xi=0
(where
\beta=\gamma/2
), with the characteristic equation:

r^{2}+2\beta r+\omega_{0}^{2}=0\implies r_{1,2}=-\beta\pm\sqrt{\beta^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}}.

(38)

Three distinct solutions emerge based on the relationship between
\beta
and
\omega_{0}
:

•

Underdamping (
\beta<\omega_{0}
)
:

\xi_{h}(t)=e^{-\beta t}\left[C_{1}\cos(\omega_{d}t)+C_{2}\sin(\omega_{d}t)\right],\quad\omega_{d}=\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}-\beta^{2}}.

(39)

The system exhibits oscillatory motion with exponentially decaying amplitude.

•

Overdamping (
\beta>\omega_{0}
)
:

\xi_{h}(t)=C_{1}e^{r_{1}t}+C_{2}e^{r_{2}t}.

(40)

The displacement decays exponentially without oscillations.

•

Critical damping (
\beta=\omega_{0}
)
:

\xi_{h}(t)=e^{-\beta t}\left(C_{1}+C_{2}t\right).

(41)

The system returns to equilibrium as quickly as possible without oscillating.

We now analyze the particular solution corresponding to the steady-state response.
Assuming the particular solution takes the form:

\xi_{p}(t)=\xi_{0}\cos(\omega t-\varphi_{0}).

(42)

Substitution into the equation yields the amplitude:

\xi_{0}=\frac{|F_{0}|}{\sqrt{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega^{2})^{2}+4(\beta\omega)^{2}}},\quad\tan\varphi_{0}=\frac{2\beta\omega}{\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega^{2}}.

(43)

Thus, the general solution for the harmonic oscillator is obtained by superimposing the homogeneous and particular solutions:

\xi(t)=\xi_{h}(t)+\xi_{p}(t).

(44)

4.2
Steady-State Power and Radiation Damping

We note that the homogeneous solution is exponentially suppressed by the factor
e^{-\beta t}
. Consequently, after a characteristic time
t\gtrsim 1/\beta
, the dynamics of the oscillator are dominated by the particular solution, which describes the steady-state behavior. The velocity associated with this steady-state solution is given by:

v(t)=\frac{d\xi_{p}}{dt}=-\xi_{0}\omega\sin(\omega t-\varphi_{0}).

(45)

The damping force,
f_{\rm damp}(t)=-M_{w}\gamma v(t)
, where
M_{w}=a(t)\sigma_{w}L_{w}^{2}
represents the physical mass of the domain wall and
L_{w}
denotes its physical length, plays a crucial role in dissipating energy into radiation. Therefore, the radiated power is determined by the instantaneous power associated with the damping force:

P(t)=-f_{\rm damp}\cdot v(t)=M_{w}\gamma v(t)^{2}=M_{w}\gamma\xi_{0}^{2}\omega^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega t-\varphi_{0}).

(46)

The time-averaged radiated power over one oscillation cycle is:

P_{\rm rad}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}P(t)dt=\frac{1}{2}M_{w}\gamma\xi_{0}^{2}\omega^{2}.

(47)

By substituting the expression for
\xi_{0}
, we can express the radiated power as:

P_{\rm rad}=\frac{\gamma M_{w}F_{0}^{2}\omega^{2}}{2\left[(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega^{2})^{2}+(\gamma\omega)^{2}\right]}.

(48)

The detailed derivation of the radiation coefficient will be provided in the subsequent section.

4.3
Determination of radiation damping coefficient

The vibration of domain walls results in energy radiation through scalar field perturbations, manifesting as wave propagation and subsequent energy dissipation within the system. The radiation damping coefficient
\gamma_{\text{rad}}
quantifies this rate of energy loss.

In scalar field theory, the energy-momentum tensor
T_{\mu\nu}
, which encapsulates the distribution and flow of energy and momentum, is defined for a real scalar field
\phi(x)
as:

T_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}\left(\partial_{\alpha}\phi\partial^{\alpha}\phi+2V(\phi)\right).

(49)

By substituting
\phi
with
\delta\phi
, we obtain the energy-momentum tensor corresponding to the scalar field deformation
\delta\phi
. The potential for the deformation field, derived from terms proportional to
\delta\phi
in Eqs. (
26
)-(
27
), is given by:

V(\delta\phi)=\frac{\lambda}{2}(3\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})\delta\phi^{2}+\lambda\phi_{0}(\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})\delta\phi+\epsilon\delta\phi\psi(t)-\lambda(3\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})\xi\phi_{0}^{\prime}\delta\phi.

(50)

To compute the radiation power, we extract the radial energy flux density
T_{0r}
, which signifies the energy flow rate in the radial direction. In spherical coordinates, this corresponds to the mixed component
T^{0}_{r}
of the energy-momentum tensor:

T^{0}_{r}=\partial^{0}\delta\phi\partial_{r}\delta\phi-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{0}_{r}\left(\partial_{\alpha}\delta\phi\partial^{\alpha}\delta\phi+2V(\delta\phi)\right).

(51)

Given that
\delta^{0}_{r}=0
and the potential term
V(\delta\phi)
is negligible in the far-field radiation zone (as
\delta\phi
resides near the potential minimum), this simplifies to:

T^{0}_{r}=\partial_{t}\delta\phi\partial_{r}\delta\phi.

(52)

Assuming domain wall vibrations excite scalar waves, the far-field (
r\to\infty
) perturbation can be approximated as an outgoing spherical wave:

\delta\phi(r,t)=\frac{aA}{r}e^{i(kr-\omega t)},

(53)

where the amplitude
A
correlates with the wall’s displacement
\xi(t)
. In the far-field approximation (
r\gg 1/\omega
):

\partial_{t}\delta\phi=-i\omega\frac{aA}{r}e^{i(kr-\omega t)}+\frac{aHA}{r}e^{i(kr-\omega t)}\approx-i\omega\frac{aA}{r}e^{i(kr-\omega t)}

(54)

and

\partial_{r}\delta\phi=ik\frac{aA}{r}e^{i(kr-\omega t)}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}e^{i(kr-\omega t)}\approx ik\frac{aA}{r}e^{i(kr-\omega t)}.

(55)

In this work, we focus on the condition
\omega\gtrsim H
, where the external force from the interaction between the wall and the periodic field
\psi(t)
dominates the oscillation. Under this condition, the friction from the Hubble expansion is negligible, allowing us to disregard the
\propto H
term in Eq. (
54
). The radial energy flux density simplifies to:

T_{r}^{0}\approx\frac{k\omega a^{2}|A|^{2}}{r^{2}}.

(56)

This indicates that the energy flux density is proportional to the square of the time derivative of the wave profile and diminishes with the square of the distance. The total radiation power is obtained by integrating
T^{0}_{r}
over a spherical surface:

P_{\text{rad}}=\int_{S^{2}}T^{0}_{r}\,r^{2}d\Omega=4\pi k\omega a^{2}|A|^{2}.

(57)

To determine
A
, we match the far-field scalar wave solution to the near-field source. Substituting the potential (
50
) into the Euler-Lagrange equation (
8
), we obtain:

\ddot{\delta\phi}+3H\dot{\delta\phi}-\frac{1}{a(t)^{2}}\nabla^{2}\delta\phi+\lambda(3\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})\delta\phi+\lambda\phi_{0}(\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})+\epsilon\psi(t)-\lambda(3\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})\xi\phi_{0}^{\prime}=0.

(58)

By invoking Eq. (
30
) and neglecting the Hubble expansion term
3H\dot{\xi}\phi_{0}^{\prime}
(justified by the prompt radiation assumption), Eq. (
58
) simplifies to:

\ddot{\delta\phi}-\frac{1}{a(t)^{2}}\nabla^{2}\delta\phi+\partial_{\phi}^{2}V(\phi_{0})\delta\phi+\lambda\phi_{0}(\phi_{0}^{2}-v^{2})=\ddot{\xi}\phi_{0}^{\prime}.

(59)

Here, the Hubble expansion term
3H\dot{\delta\phi}
is also neglected, consistent with the
\omega\gtrsim H
condition. The term
\ddot{\xi}\phi_{0}^{\prime}
acts as the source term for the perturbation. Approximating this source term as:

\text{Source term}\simeq\frac{v}{\delta}\ddot{\xi}(t)\delta^{(3)}\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}}{a\delta}\right),

(60)

where
\mathbf{x}=a\bm{x}
denotes the physical coordinate in three dimensions, and recognizing that the third term on the RHS of Eq. (
59
) vanishes, the equation of motion for the vibration becomes:

\Box\delta\phi+\partial_{\phi}^{2}V(\phi_{0})\delta\phi=\frac{v}{\delta}(a\delta)^{3}\ddot{\xi}(t)\delta^{(3)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right),

(61)

with
\mathbf{x}
in the Dirac delta function and the D’Alembert operator
\Box=\partial_{t}^{2}-\nabla^{2}
representing physical distance. Employing the Green’s Function Method, the scalar wave solution corresponds to a retarded potential:

\delta\phi(r,t)=\int\frac{\text{source term}(t-|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|)}{4\pi|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|}d^{3}x^{\prime}.

(62)

For the point source (
60
), the solution becomes (with detailed derivations provided in Appendix
A
and Appendix
B
):

\delta\phi(r,t)=-\frac{va^{3}\delta^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}}{4\pi r}\cos\left(\omega t-kr\right),

(63)

where
k=\sqrt{\omega^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2}}
(with
m_{\phi}^{2}=\lambda v^{2}
) and the negative sign indicates an outgoing wave. By comparing this with the steady-state solution (
42
) and the far-field form
\delta\phi\sim\frac{aA}{r}e^{i(kr-\omega t)}
, we find:

A=-\frac{(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}}{4\pi}.

(64)

This confirms that the amplitude
A
is dimensionless.

The damped oscillation of the wall can be analogized to an electric dipole. For a domain wall oscillating as
\xi(t)=\xi_{0}\cos(\omega t)
, the equivalent dipole moment is given by:

p(t)=q\xi(t)\quad\text{with}\quad q=\int\phi_{0}^{\prime}(x)dx=2v.

(65)

Energy conservation dictates that the radiation power magnitude must match that of the damping force,
P_{\rm rad}=-P_{\rm damp}
, leading to:

4\pi k\omega a^{2}|A|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{\rm rad}M_{w}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}^{2}.

(66)

Solving this yields:

\gamma_{\rm rad}=\frac{8\pi ka^{2}|A|^{2}}{M_{w}\omega\xi_{0}^{2}}=\frac{2a^{6}\delta^{4}k\omega^{5}}{\pi M_{w}}.

(67)

5
Elastic Limit of Domain Walls

The domain wall may experience destruction if the oscillation amplitude surpasses its elastic limit. In this section, we will compute the critical strain of the wall. This calculation is essential for determining the threshold at which the domain wall can no longer maintain its integrity under oscillatory stress, thereby providing insights into the conditions that lead to its destabilization and eventual annihilation.

5.1
Introduction of Deformation and Strain Definition

Assuming a displacement of the domain wall in the transverse direction
y
by
u(y)
, the deformed field configuration is given by:

\phi_{0}(x,y)=v\tanh\left(\frac{x-u(y)}{\delta}\right).

(68)

The local strain, defined as the gradient of the displacement, is:

\zeta(y)=\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}.

(69)

The energy density after deformation is expressed as:

\mathcal{E}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial x}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial y}\right)^{2}+V_{0}(\phi_{0}),

(70)

where terms proportional to
\zeta
have been neglected. Expanding and retaining terms up to second order in strain
\zeta
, we have:

1.

Kinetic Energy Term:

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial x}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{v}{\delta}\text{sech}^{2}\left(\frac{x-u}{\delta}\right)\right)^{2}.

(71)

2.

Additional Kinetic Energy from Deformation:

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial\phi_{0}}{\partial y}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{v}{\delta}\text{sech}^{2}\left(\frac{x-u}{\delta}\right)\cdot\zeta(y)\right)^{2}.

(72)

3.

Potential Energy Term:

V(\phi_{0})=\frac{\lambda}{4}\left(v^{2}\tanh^{2}\left(\frac{x-u}{\delta}\right)-v^{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{\lambda v^{4}}{4}\text{sech}^{4}\left(\frac{x-u}{\delta}\right).

(73)

Integrating over
x
yields the deformation energy per unit area:

\Delta\mathcal{E}=\frac{1}{a}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{v^{2}}{2\delta^{2}}\zeta^{2}(y)\cdot\text{sech}^{4}\left(\frac{x}{\delta}\right)dx=\frac{v^{2}}{2\delta^{2}}\zeta^{2}(y)\cdot\left(\frac{4\delta}{3}\right)=\frac{2v^{2}}{3\delta}\zeta^{2}(y),

(74)

where the factor
1/a
accounts for the transformation from comoving to physical coordinates. With Eq. (
32
), the elastic potential energy density is given by:

U_{\text{elastic}}=\frac{\sigma_{w}}{2a}\zeta^{2}(y).

(75)

5.2
Barrier Penetration Condition and Critical Strain

When the strain
\zeta
is sufficiently large, higher-order terms (such as
\zeta^{4}
) cannot be neglected. In this case, deformation may cause the field
\phi
to cross the potential barrier (from
+\!v
to
-\!v
), leading to domain wall rupture.
The critical condition is analyzed through the balance between
elastic restoring energy density
U_{\text{elastic}}
and barrier energy density

U_{\text{barrier}}\sim V_{\text{max}}\cdot a\delta=\frac{\lambda v^{4}}{4}\cdot a\delta.

(76)

When
U_{\text{elastic}}\gtrsim U_{\text{barrier}}
, the deformation energy overcomes the barrier, causing domain wall rupture:

\frac{\sigma}{2a}\zeta_{c}^{2}=\frac{\lambda v^{4}}{4}a\delta,

(77)

substituting
\sigma=\frac{4v^{2}}{3\delta}
and
\lambda=\frac{2}{a^{2}\delta^{2}v^{2}}
, we solve:

\zeta_{c}^{2}=\frac{3\lambda v^{4}a^{2}\delta^{2}}{8v^{2}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\zeta_{c}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}.

(78)

Then, the critical deformation amplitude is given by

A_{c}\sim\zeta_{c}\cdot\delta=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\delta.

(79)

When the local strain induced by external forces exceeds this threshold, the domain wall’s elastic restoring force cannot counteract the deformation energy, leading to structural rupture.

5.3
Destruction by Resonant Oscillation

When the oscillator’s amplitude
\xi_{0}
surpasses the critical deformation amplitude
A_{c}
, the domain wall is destabilized due to an energy imbalance. This leads to the condition:

\frac{|F_{0}|}{\sqrt{(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega^{2})^{2}+4(\beta\omega)^{2}}}\approx\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\delta.

(80)

In the case of resonant oscillation, we have:

\omega=\omega_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{5}\lambda}v\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma=\gamma_{H}=3H.

(81)

Given that
\omega_{0}<m_{\phi}
, there is no particle radiation during resonant oscillation, implying
\gamma_{\text{rad}}=0
. This results in:

\epsilon\approx\sqrt{\frac{6}{5}\lambda}\frac{Hv^{2}}{\psi_{0}}.

(82)

It should be noted that the perturbation condition
\epsilon\ll\lambda v^{2}
necessitates
H\ll\psi_{0}
.

The perturbation interaction (
6
) also breaks the
Z_{2}
symmetry and introduces a bias potential:

\Delta V\approx 2\epsilon v\psi_{0}.

(83)

Domain walls can also be annihilated by the bias potential when
\Delta V\simeq a\sigma_{w}H
, leading to:

\epsilon\approx\sqrt{2\lambda}\frac{Hv^{2}}{\psi_{0}}.

(84)

Comparing this condition with Eq. (
82
), it is evident that the destruction of the domain wall via resonant oscillation can precede domain wall annihilation.

6
Dissipation by Radiation

When the oscillation frequency exceeds the scalar particle mass, specifically
\omega\gtrsim m_{\phi}
, particle radiation from wall oscillations becomes possible, leading to energy dissipation. We focus on the regime:

\omega\gg\omega_{0},\gamma\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma\simeq\gamma_{\rm rad}.

(85)

Using Eqs. (
43
) and (
48
), the power spectrum can be approximated as:

P_{\rm rad}\approx\frac{1}{\pi}a^{6}\delta^{4}k\omega^{3}F_{0}^{2}.

(86)

To ensure a significant reduction in domain wall energy density within a Hubble time
H^{-1}
, the condition:

P_{\text{rad}}\gtrsim\rho_{w}L_{w}^{3}\cdot H,

(87)

must be satisfied. Substituting
\rho_{w}\simeq\sigma_{w}H/a
and considering horizon-scale domain walls
L_{w}\simeq H^{-1}
, we derive:

\epsilon^{2}\psi_{0}^{2}\gtrsim\frac{4\sqrt{2}\pi\lambda^{5/2}v^{11}}{27Hk\omega^{3}}.

(88)

This condition can be met when the energy density of the driving source is sufficiently high. In such scenarios, particle radiation effectively dissipates the energy of the domain wall, thereby preventing it from dominating the energy budget of the universe and offering a novel solution to the domain wall problem.

By comparing conditions (
84
) and (
88
), we find that the destruction of the wall due to resonant oscillations is particularly pertinent in the late universe, characterized by a small Hubble parameter (
H
). Conversely, radiation-driven dissipation of the domain wall is more significant in the early universe, where the Hubble parameter (
H
) is large. This distinction underscores the varying mechanisms influencing domain wall dynamics across different cosmic epochs and elucidates their implications for the evolution of domain walls in the universe.

8
Conclusion and Discussion

Domain walls that arise from the spontaneous symmetry breaking of a discrete symmetry in new particle physics models may result in cosmological disasters due to their relatively slow energy density decay rates. In this work, we propose a novel mechanism for the destruction of the domain wall network through resonant oscillation. Utilizing quantum field perturbation theory, we demonstrate that perturbations on the domain wall can induce oscillations of the wall itself. We derive the equation of motion for the oscillating domain wall from the perturbed potential and determine both the elastic coefficient and the radiation damping coefficient of the domain wall oscillator.

We consider the coupling of the domain wall to a periodically oscillating background field, which acts as the external force governing the domain wall’s oscillation. When the frequency of the external force matches the intrinsic frequency of the wall, resonant oscillation occurs, significantly enhancing the amplitude of the oscillations. Should the oscillation amplitude exceed a critical deformation threshold, the domain wall will be destroyed due to energy imbalances.

In scenarios involving
Z_{2}
breaking interactions, we find that the parameter space for resonant destruction can extend beyond that of domain wall annihilation. Conversely, in
Z_{2}
conserving interactions, we show that the resonant oscillation amplitude can grow over time, especially when the Hubble expansion rate is sufficiently small (
H\lesssim 0.01\omega_{0}
). Additionally, we discuss the dissipation of the domain wall through particle radiation during the oscillatory process. Consequently, we establish that our resonant destruction mechanism is applicable even within models that conserve discrete symmetries.

It is worth noting that our mechanism requires alignment between the intrinsic frequency of the wall and the oscillating frequency of the background field, which may lead to what is often referred to as the fine-tuning problem. However, we can anticipate that the background field possesses a range of oscillatory modes with a broad frequency distribution. When one of these modes aligns with the domain wall’s intrinsic frequency, it can trigger resonant oscillation, establishing itself as the dominant oscillation amplitude. Thus, our proposed scenario is quite general within the context of new particle physics frameworks.

Appendix A
Solution for Vibration Field

The equation of motion governing the scalar field
\phi(r,t)
is given by:

\Box\delta\phi(r,t)-\lambda v^{2}\left(3\delta^{3}a^{3}\delta^{(3)}(r)-1\right)\delta\phi(r,t)+(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}a\cos(\omega t)\delta^{(3)}(r)=0

(97)

Here,
\Box=\partial_{t}^{2}-\nabla^{2}
represents the d’Alembert operator, and
\xi_{0}
,
\omega
,
v
,
\delta
,
\lambda
are constants. The term
\delta^{(3)}(r)
denotes the three-dimensional Dirac delta function. The solution process for Eq. (
107
) is outlined below:

1. Equation Simplification

The equation can be rewritten as:

\Box\delta\phi+\lambda v^{2}\delta\phi=3\lambda v^{2}(\delta a)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(r)\delta\phi+(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}a\cos(\omega t)\delta^{(3)}(r)

(98)

The left-hand side corresponds to the Klein-Gordon equation, while the right-hand side includes a term proportional to
\delta\phi
and an external source term.

2. Assuming a Harmonic Solution

Given the time-dependent source term
\cos(\omega t)
, we seek a time-harmonic solution of the form:

\delta\phi(r,t)=\delta\psi(r)a(t)\cos(\omega t)

(99)

Substituting this ansatz into the equation separates the time dependence, resulting in the spatial equation:

\nabla^{2}\delta\psi+\left(\omega^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\delta\psi+3\lambda v^{2}(\delta a)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(r)\delta\psi=(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}\delta^{(3)}(r)

(100)

3. Solving the Spatial Equation

For
r\neq 0
, the equation reduces to the homogeneous Helmholtz equation:

\nabla^{2}\delta\psi+k^{2}\delta\psi=0,\quad k=\sqrt{\omega^{2}-\lambda v^{2}}

(101)

The spherically symmetric solution to this equation is:

\delta\psi(r)=A\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}+B\frac{e^{-ikr}}{r}

(102)

Selecting the outgoing wave solution (appropriate for physical considerations), we set
B=0
, yielding:

\delta\psi(r)=A\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}

(103)

4. Matching Boundary Conditions at the Origin

Integrating the equation around the origin and applying the properties of the Dirac delta function, we obtain:

-4\pi A+3\lambda(\delta a)^{3}v^{2}\delta\psi(0)=(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}

(104)

Due to the divergence of
\delta\psi(r)
as
r\to 0
, regularization or renormalization techniques are employed to determine the coefficient
A
. Neglecting higher-order terms, the solution yields:

A=-\frac{(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}}{4\pi},

(105)

where
\xi_{0}
has been appropriately normalized (
\xi_{0}\equiv\xi_{0}^{\text{ren}}
). Detailed regularization and renormalization procedures are provided in Appendix
B
.

5. Final Form of the Solution

Substituting
k=\sqrt{\omega^{2}-\lambda v^{2}}
into the solution, the scalar field perturbation is:

\delta\phi(r,t)=-\frac{(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}}{4\pi}a\cos\left(\omega t-\sqrt{\omega^{2}-\lambda v^{2}}\,r\right)

(106)

Appendix B
Regularization and Renormalization in Scalar Field Equations

The equation of motion for the scalar field
\phi(r,t)
is given by:

\Box\delta\phi(r,t)-\lambda v^{2}\left(3\delta^{3}a^{3}\delta^{(3)}(r)-1\right)\delta\phi(r,t)+(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}a\cos(\omega t)\delta^{(3)}(r)=0

(107)

Here,
\Box=\partial_{t}^{2}-\nabla^{2}
denotes the d’Alembert operator, and
\xi_{0}
,
\omega
,
v
,
\delta
,
\lambda
are constants. The term
\delta^{(3)}(r)
represents the three-dimensional Dirac delta function. Below is a detailed explanation of the process to physically determine the coefficient
A
using regularization and renormalization:

1. Regularization: Introducing a Cutoff Factor

First, introduce a spatial cutoff scale
\varepsilon
(for example, restricting the integration region near the origin to
r\geq\varepsilon
) to avoid directly handling divergent terms. Integrate the original equation within a small sphere of radius
\varepsilon
:

\int_{r\leq\varepsilon}\left[\nabla^{2}\delta\psi+\left(\omega^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\delta\psi+3\lambda v^{2}(\delta a)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(r)\delta\psi\right]d^{3}r=\int_{r\leq\varepsilon}(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}\delta^{(3)}(r)d^{3}r.

(108)

Let us handle the terms on the left-hand side (LHS) of Eq. (
108
):

•

First Term (
\nabla^{2}\delta\psi
integral): Using Gauss’s theorem, convert the volume integral to a surface integral:

\int_{r\leq\varepsilon}\nabla^{2}\delta\psi\,d^{3}r=\oint_{r=\varepsilon}\nabla\delta\psi\cdot d\mathbf{S}

(109)

For the spherically symmetric solution
\delta\psi(r)=Ae^{ikr}/r
, the gradient is:

\nabla\delta\psi=\frac{d\delta\psi}{dr}\hat{r}=A\left(\frac{ike^{ikr}}{r}-\frac{e^{ikr}}{r^{2}}\right)\hat{r}.

(110)

At
r=\varepsilon
, the surface integral becomes:

\oint_{r=\varepsilon}\nabla\delta\psi\cdot d\mathbf{S}=4\pi\varepsilon^{2}\left.\frac{d\delta\psi}{dr}\right|_{r=\varepsilon}=4\pi\varepsilon^{2}\left[A\left(\frac{ike^{ik\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}-\frac{e^{ik\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\right].

(111)

Expanding
e^{ik\varepsilon}\approx 1+ik\varepsilon
(small
\varepsilon
approximation), we get:

\text{Eq.\eqref{eq:RR11}'s RHS}=4\pi A\left(ik\varepsilon-1\right).

(112)

Therefore, the integral of the gradient yields a factor of
-4\pi A
.

•

Second Term (
\left(\omega^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\delta\psi
integral):
Since
\delta\psi\sim 1/r
, the integral diverges. However, after regularization:

\left(\omega^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\int_{r\leq\varepsilon}\delta\psi\,d^{3}r\approx\left(\omega^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\cdot 4\pi A\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}r\,dr=2\pi A\left(\omega^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\varepsilon^{2}

(113)

This term can be neglected as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

•

Third Term (
3\lambda v^{2}(\delta a)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(r)\delta\psi
integral):
Using the property of the Dirac delta function:

3\lambda v^{2}(\delta a)^{3}\int_{r\leq\varepsilon}\delta^{(3)}(r)\delta\psi(r)d^{3}r=3\lambda v^{2}(\delta a)^{3}\delta\psi(0)

(114)

However,
\delta\psi(0)\sim A/\varepsilon
diverges and needs to be retained.

Finally, integrating the right-hand side (RHS) of Eq. (
108
) gives:

\int_{r\leq\varepsilon}(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}\delta^{(3)}(r)d^{3}r=(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}.

(115)

We observe that the divergence arises from the integration of the third term on the RHS of Eq. (
108
).

2. Renormalization: Divergence Cancellation

Substituting the integral results into the original equation:

4\pi A\left(ik\varepsilon-1\right)+3\lambda v^{2}(\delta a)^{3}\frac{A}{\varepsilon}=(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}

(116)

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, the divergent term is:

-\underbrace{4\pi A}_{\text{finite}}+\underbrace{3\lambda v^{2}(\delta a)^{3}\frac{A}{\varepsilon}}_{\text{divergent}}=(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}.

(117)

To eliminate the divergence, we combine the divergent term with a redefinition of the theoretical parameters. This can be accomplished by adjusting parameters such as
\lambda
or
\xi_{0}
to absorb the divergence effectively. As
\varepsilon
approaches zero, the oscillator ceases to be treated as a point source, necessitating a renormalization of the source parameter
\xi_{0}
. It is important to note that since
\xi_{0}
is proportional to
\epsilon
, this procedure effectively corresponds to a renormalization of the coupling constant
\epsilon
. This approach allows for the consistent elimination of divergences while maintaining the integrity of the theoretical framework.

The renormalization approach involves matching the divergent term with the source term to ensure finiteness as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Re-write Eq. (
117
) as:

3\lambda v^{2}(\delta a)^{3}\frac{A}{\varepsilon}=(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}+4\pi A.

(118)

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, the left-hand side diverges, so the right-hand side must also possess a corresponding divergence. For example, define the bare source term as:

(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}^{\text{bare}}=(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}^{\text{ren}}+3\lambda v^{2}(\delta a)^{3}\frac{A}{\varepsilon}.

(119)

The divergence is then canceled, yielding:

A=-\frac{(\delta a)^{2}\omega^{2}\xi_{0}^{\text{ren}}}{4\pi}.

(120)

The final answer is expressed in terms of renormalized parameters, with divergences absorbed into the redefined physical parameters, resulting in a finite and physically meaningful solution.
