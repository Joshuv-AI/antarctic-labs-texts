Plasmonic electrophosphorescence: “Feeling” blue
Authors: Qiyin Chen, Sichao Ji, Xulin Chen, Long Chen, Guohua Xie
Published: 2026

Main text

Surface plasmon polaritons (SPPs), excited at the interface between the metallic electrode and dielectric layer, are commonly considered detrimental to the performance of organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs).1,2,3 Approximately 20%–40% of photons are confined within the SPP mode, resulting in exciton annihilation and energy dissipation. Compared with the low internal quantum efficiency (IQE) of fluorescent materials and the unpleasant efficiency roll-off of thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF)-based devices, phosphorescent materials have received increasing attention for blue OLEDs, owing to their near-unity IQE and relatively better stability. Owing to the prolonged blue triplet lifetimes and slow radiative decay rates, the phosphorescent molecules are prone to dissociation under electrical excitation. This results in the excessively short operational lifetimes for blue phosphorescent OLEDs (PHOLEDs). Inspirationally, the stability of blue phosphorescent devices with plasmonic structures has been demonstrated as a big breakthrough by utilizing the polariton-enhanced Purcell (PEP) effect to accelerate radiative decay, using metallic nanoparticles (MNPs) to extract energy from the SPP mode.

Blue phosphorescence

First-generation OLEDs employ fluorescent emitters with a low IQE of only 25%, thereby facing the dilemma of inherent performance limitations. Although the state-of-the-art TADF emitters can achieve up to 100% IQE, TADF devices face significant external quantum efficiency (EQE) roll-off and inferior stability because of the accumulation and annihilation of excitons caused by inefficient reverse intersystem crossing. In contrast, heavy-metal-based phosphorescent materials, capable of reaching an IQE of 100% as well, exhibit faster radiative decay of triplet excitons due to the heavy-atom effect. While high-performance green and red PHOLEDs have been employed in commercial displays, blue PHOLEDs still remain constrained by their short operational lifetimes and high cost.1,2 Serious nonradiative recombination processes, such as triplet-triplet annihilation (TTA) and triplet-polaron annihilation (TPA), frequently rule blue PHOLEDs.3,4

To tackle this issue, researchers have exploited lots of strategies for luminescent materials and emission layers (EMLs). On the materials front, some useful strategies include developing more rigid molecular structures to suppress vibrational relaxation and enhancing chemical bond stability through methods such as deuteration. For the EML, some unique approaches, such as gradient doping of host and guest, mixed hosts, and hot excited state management, have been employed to reduce triplet exciton density and accelerate exciton decay. In addition, EML systems, such as TTA upconversion, hyperfluorescence, inverted singlet-triplet system, and phosphor-assisted TADF-sensitized fluorescence, have been developed to enhance device stability. However, the effectiveness of these methods diminishes rapidly in deep-blue devices due to the increased exciton energy, which triggers molecular degradation processes more easily.

Recently, Forrest’s group demonstrated a significant improvement in the operational lifetimes of blue plasmonic PHOLEDs with different planar-cavity architectures, such as single-junction devices and tandem devices, manipulating the PEP effect.2,4,5 They achieved a long lifetime of LT90 = 830 ± 30 h with an initial luminance of L0 = 500 cd m−2 in blue planar tandem plasmonic PHOLEDs.5 The lifetime enhancement was nearly 250 times that of conventional single-junction devices. To be specific, such a strategy generally employs optical tuning of a microcavity to actively induce resonance between the SPP mode and excitons within the adjacent organic layer, such as the electron transport layer and hole transport layer, respectively, thereby forming strongly coupled plasmon-exciton polaritons. Then, it accelerates the radiative decay rate (kr) of excitons and reduces the triplet radiative lifetime, fundamentally reducing the probability of TTA and TPA.

Mechanism of blue plasmonic PHOLEDs

The physical essence of the Purcell effect is that in microcavity OLEDs, the radiative decay rate of excitons is accelerated by enhancing the optical density of states.2,4 The enhancement intensity is quantified by the Purcell factor. Increasing the Purcell factor and reducing the singlet and triplet densities are effective methods to alleviate device degradation, which can be realized by utilizing the SPP mode in microcavity OLEDs.

Green plasmonic PHOLEDs with Ag nanocubes, as shown in Figure 1C, have been proven as an effective way to outcouple photons.1 The exciton decay rate was improved via the PEP effect to boost operational stability, while the Ag nanocubes extracted energy from the SPP mode to maintain high efficiency. They confirmed the practicality of the PEP effect and provided an effective strategy for using MNPs to improve the device stability.

In a conventional PHOLED, although the triplet energy can be transferred to the SPP mode to induce exciton decay, its electromagnetic field is strongly localized within the nanoscale range of the metal surface (ca. 15 nm; Figure 1A) and decays exponentially, making it difficult to effectively reduce the triplet density in the EML. The core of the PEP effect lies in transcending the scope of the weakly coupled Purcell effect and entering the strongly coupled region with a high Purcell factor in plasmonic PHOLEDs.4,5 When the excitons in the adjacent dielectric layer resonate with the SPP mode on a metal electrode (such as Ag) and the coupling strength exceeds their intrinsic losses, the system enters the strong coupling region, forming new hybrid quasiparticles known as plasmon-exciton polaritons. It can provide a significantly enhanced optical density of states that extends the Purcell effect enhancement tens of nanometers (up to 50 nm; Figure 1B) into the EML, thus substantially reducing triplet radiative decay lifetime and triplet density.

Challenges and prospects

The significant role of the PEP effect in enhancing the operational lifetime of blue plasmonic PHOLEDs has been confirmed. However, their current lifetime remains insufficient to meet commercialization requirements. From our point of view, the challenges and prospects in developing blue plasmonic PHOLEDs for future commercialization should encompass the following.

Materials

Although TTA fluorescent emitters are used in commercial blue OLEDs, their theoretical IQE is about 62.5%, whereas phosphorescent materials can achieve 100% IQE, enabling blue PHOLEDs to exhibit superior electroluminescence efficiency. In order to rapidly consume triplet excitons to enhance the operational lifetime and color purity of blue PHOLEDs, the strategy of using phosphorescent materials to sensitize multi-resonance TADF (MR-TADF) emitters through intramolecular energy transfer (ET) has been confirmed.4 MR-TADF emitters exhibit high color purity due to the restricted structural relaxation, which is essential for display panels. In this case, it would prolong the lifetime of narrow electroluminescence‌ blue plasmonic PHOLEDs and simplify the fabrication of EMLs, as shown in Figure 1D, constructing emitters with intramolecular ET by chemically bonding blue MR-TADF units with blue phosphorescent sensitization units (e.g., tetradentate Pt(II) moiety).

Devices

Dielectric nanoparticles such as SiO2 and TiO2 have been shown to enhance light outcoupling in OLEDs and other LEDs by Mie scattering and resonance effects. However, they have little effect on shaping the surface plasmon in a microcavity of OLED. In contrast, MNPs are an emerging class of materials that effectively regulate plasmonic resonance behavior. As proposed in Figure 1E, the type of MNPs (such as shape, size, and composition) and the position of the micro-nano structure play crucial roles in regulating the plasmonic resonance behavior. Therefore, for blue plasmonic PHOLEDs, the specific MNPs adapted to blue light wavelengths between 430 and 475 nm are the most urgently needed to be determined. Furthermore, in order to achieve high-performance plasmonic PHOLEDs, the following three aspects should be carefully considered: (1) the selection of the transport layer and metallic contact to ensure efficient formation of plasmon-exciton polaritons with strong coupling, (2) the position of the EML to form the strong excitons coupling region, and (3) the construction of an optical cavity to maximize the Purcell enhancement with superior light out-coupling efficiency.

Processes

Perfectly integrating MNPs or other micro-nano structures into devices without influencing the molecular stacks remains a significant challenge. Since chemically synthesized MNPs are generally kept in organic solutions, preparing an MNP film requires a solution-processed approach, for example, spin coating or inkjet printing (Figure 1F). However, these processes may cause the degradation of the underlying film due to solvent penetration. Combining these processes with transfer printing could effectively resolve the growth of MNPs. Micro-nano processing, such as laser etching and chemical etching (Figure 1F), is another effective way to avoid damaging the film stacks. This process can achieve structurally ordered and customizable structures through precise pattern design to manipulate plasmonic PHOLEDs, and the size of micro-nano structures can range from nanometers to micrometers.

Conclusion

Plasmonic PHOLEDs based on the PEP effect offer a novel pathway to overcome the operational lifetime challenges of blue PHOLEDs. Building on this foundation, micro-nano structural engineering demonstrates potential to further enhance the lifetime and efficiency of blue plasmonic PHOLEDs. Currently, major display companies have already commenced research into plasmonic PHOLEDs. We believe that after integrating these two approaches and addressing the relevant key challenges of micro-nano-structured plasmonic devices, the commercial application of blue plasmonic PHOLEDs will be realized in the near future. Furthermore, the organic plasmonic laser also holds significant promise by overcoming the challenge of device stability at high current densities and constructing a high-Q optical microcavity based on long-lifetime plasmonic devices.
