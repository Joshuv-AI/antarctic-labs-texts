Modulation of satellite cell function to alleviate age-related sarcopenia: Electrical stimulation & Ca²⁺ signaling combined approach
Authors: Canyu Chen, Yersen Mulat, Dongsheng Jiang, Haodong Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAge-related sarcopenia is a degenerative condition characterized by loss of muscle mass and strength. Satellite cells (SCs), the stem cells of skeletal muscle, decline in number and function with age, contributing to sarcopenia. Enhancing SC function represents a promising therapeutic strategy. Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) induces passive muscle contraction and improves SC activity, partly through calcium-dependent mechanisms. Calcium signaling is essential not only for excitation-contraction coupling but also for SC proliferation and differentiation. This review summarizes age-related changes in SCs, current sarcopenia treatments, and the therapeutic potential of NMES. We propose a novel combined strategy integrating NMES with calcium signaling modulation to synergistically enhance SC function. This approach offers a promising avenue for treating age-related sarcopenia and warrants further investigation.

Introduction

As the global population continues to age, age-related diseases are imposing an increasing burden on healthcare systems. Among these, age-related sarcopenia is a primary concern for the musculoskeletal health of the elderly. This condition not only leads to a decline in motor function but also increases the risk of secondary injuries such as falls and fractures.1,2,3,4 According to the European working group on sarcopenia in older people 2 (EWGSOP2), age-related sarcopenia is defined by reduced muscle strength and decreased muscle mass.1 In addition to these clinical features, a range of histological changes have been observed in aging muscle, including muscle fiber atrophy, increased fibrosis, enhanced fat infiltration, motor unit remodeling, and loss of satellite cells.5,6,7

For managing age-related sarcopenia, EWGSOP2 recommends nutritional supplementation and regular physical exercise as preferred approaches.1,8 Over the past years, novel therapeutic approaches have also been explored, such as administration of pharmacological agents and the application of neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES). Some of these approaches have demonstrated encouraging outcomes in clinical studies.

Satellite cells, the resident stem cells in skeletal muscle, play a central role in muscle repair and regeneration.9 Under normal conditions, these cells remain a mitotically quiescent stage. However, in response to stimuli such as exercise or injury, satellite cells are activated and then proliferate and differentiate to form new muscle fibers that replace or repair the damaged tissue.10,11,12 With advancing age, both the number and regenerative capacity of satellite cells are gradually diminished, contributing to impaired muscle repair and regeneration capacity.13,14,15,16,17 Although the causal relationship between satellite cell dysfunction and sarcopenia remains under debate,18,19,20,21 enhancing satellite cell number and function is recognized as a promising therapeutic approach and has become an area of active research.22

NMES is a rehabilitation technique that improves muscle quality and function by inducing passive muscle contractions, thereby stimulating the process of physical exercise.23,24 It has been proven to be effective in counteracting sarcopenia caused by conditions, such as chronic heart failure,25 chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD),26 and critical ill.27 Similarly, NMES has been used to treat aging muscles and has shown an effect on improving muscle mass, strength, and mobility in the elderly.28,29,30 Notably, several studies have reported that NMES also enhances the quantity and function of satellite cells in stimulated muscle.28,31,32,33,34

It is widely acknowledged that NMES induces muscle contraction by simulating neural signals, leading to the activation of calcium channels in muscle cells. This triggers calcium-dependent excitation–contraction (EC) coupling, emphasizing the essential role of calcium signaling in the mechanism of NMES.35 Also, calcium signaling is involved in the regulatory process of satellite cell proliferation and differentiation.36,37,38 However, despite these findings, there remains a lack of studies specifically addressing whether NMES or calcium signaling modulation can directly improve satellite cell function to treat age-related sarcopenia.

In summary, the proliferation and differentiation capacity of satellite cells is essential for muscle regeneration and represents a potential therapeutic target for sarcopenia. Therefore, this review will summarize recent advances in strategies aimed at enhancing satellite cell function in aging muscle, and focusing on the role of NMES and calcium signaling and the possibility of combining NMES with calcium signal regulation to improve satellite cell activity for the treatment of age-related sarcopenia.

Characteristics of satellite cells and age-related changes

The characteristics and heterogeneity of satellite cells

Satellite cells are a type of stem cell located between the basal lamina and the sarcolemma of skeletal muscle fibers, which will undergo proliferation and differentiation to form skeletal muscle tissue under specific conditions.39 In normal states, satellite cells remain mitotically quiescent, corresponding to the G0 phase of the cell cycle, during which they express high levels of PAX7. When triggered by conditions such as muscle injury or inflammation, quiescent satellite cells (QSCs) are activated and enter the cell cycle. Some activated satellite cells (ASCs) undergo symmetric division to expand the satellite cell pool, while others undergo asymmetric division to generate one satellite cell (Pax7+/Myf5−) and one myogenic committed cell (Pax7+/Myf5+). The myogenic committed cells subsequently proliferate and differentiate into myoblasts, undergoing the stage of myocytes and myotubes, and eventually fusing to form new myofibers. Meanwhile, a portion of satellite cells that do not participate in fiber formation return to quiescence to replenish the satellite cell pool (Figure 1).

QSCs are characterized by the specific expression of Pax7 in skeletal muscle.In response to external stimuli, such as muscle injury, satellite cells become activated and undergo proliferation. A proportion of these cells self-renews and returns to a quiescent state, thereby maintaining the size of the satellite cell pool, whereas the remaining cells commit to the myogenic lineage. These committed cells are marked by the expression of Mrf5 and further differentiate into myoblasts, which express MyoD, Myogenin, and Mrf4, indicating the transition from quiescence to muscle repairing stage. Subsequently, myoblasts differentiate into myocytes, which fuse to form myotubes. Finally, these myotubes mature and integrate into myofibers, a terminal differentiation process characterized by the expression of myosin heavy chain (MHC).

However, the traditional view of satellite cells as a homogeneous population defined principally by PAX7 expression has been revised by single-cell transcriptomic analyses. These studies demonstrate that satellite cells exist as a dynamic continuum of transcriptionally and functionally distinct subpopulations in both homeostatic and regenerating muscle, with notable species-specific features.

In homeostatic mouse muscle, satellite cells occupy distinct states according to scRNA-seq evidence. Two primary clusters have been identified as follows. One of them is a close-to- quiescence population enriched in transcripts associated with stemness and quiescence, which specifically expresses Pax7, Calcr, the Notch target Hes1. The other one is an “early-activation” population exhibiting elevated Myod and ribosomal gene expression that remains translationally repressed at the protein level.40 Also, a subpopulation characterized by elevated Notch2 and Sdc4 expression has also been identified, comprising approximately 23% of homeostatic satellite cells and enriched for G1-phase markers.41

During muscle regeneration, this heterogeneity expands into a functionally specialized subpopulation network. At day 4 post-injury, myogenic cells resolve into five transcriptionally distinct subpopulations: a primary cluster retaining high Pax7 expression; an ECM cluster characterized by elevated collagen expression; a proliferation cluster representing transit-amplifying myoblasts; a differentiation cluster expressing Myog and the Notch ligand Dll1; and a Notch2-high cluster that persists from the homeostatic state.41 Critically, DLL1 from differentiating cells activates NOTCH2 signaling in the Notch2-high subpopulation to drive self-renewal—a feedback mechanism that maintains the satellite cell pool during proportional regeneration.41

In human skeletal muscle, single-cell analysis of CXCR4+/CD29+/CD56+ satellite cells from healthy adult muscle identified twelve transcriptionally distinct clusters.42 A subpopulation marked by elevated CAV1 (Caveolin-1) expression exhibits distinctive functional properties: delayed first division, lower MYOD expression in vitro, and superior engraftment capacity in xenotransplantation models, contributing the majority of regenerated myofibers and repopulated satellite niches.42 This contrasts with mouse satellite cells, where Cav1 is more uniformly expressed, highlighting a species-specific distinction.

Factors leading to the age-related changes of satellite cells

During the process of aging and muscle degeneration, satellite cells exhibit aging-related changes, including a reduced population and intrinsic cellular senescence.

On one hand, the chronic activation of QSCs caused by aging muscle-derived cytokines leads to the depletion of the satellite cell pool. The PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, as the key pathway regulating the transition of QSCs into ASCs, can activate mTORC1 and inhibit FOXO,43 thereby enhancing proliferation and differentiation while inhibiting the return to quiescence.44 However, persistent activation of PI3K-Akt pathway induced by increased levels of insulin and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) provided by the aging muscle niche,45,46 or chronic activation of mTORC1 induced by aging,47 can result in persistent transition of QSCs into ASCs while impairing their self-renewal, thereby causing the depletion of satellite cell pool. Similarly, increased production of fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) by aged myofibers also cause persistent activation of QSCs and contribute to the exhaustion of satellite cells.13 Recent studies have shown that reduced levels of Agrin in skeletal muscle of aging mice can activate Yap signaling path, which promotes the apoptosis and consumption of satellite cells, thereby disrupting their quiescence and depleting the satellite cell pool.48

On the other hand, with the aging of satellite cells, their physiological process of activation and proliferation were impaired.49,50 Numerous studies have demonstrated that QSCs rely on autophagy to maintain their stemness and activation potential.50,51 However, aging-related reductions in autophagic flux or cellular senescence can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, enhanced reactive oxygen species (ROS), and increased DNA damage, which will not only impair satellite cell activation and proliferation but may also induce the overexpression of the cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitor P16INK4A,52 thereby restricting the self-renewal and the ability to return to quiescence of satellite cells.53 Also, the AMPK/P27 signaling pathway is involved in maintaining the activation potential and proliferation capacity of satellite cells by suppressing cellular senescence and apoptosis and maintaining autophagy. However, downregulation of P27 was detected in aged satellite cells, diminishing AMPK/P27 activity and impairing satellite cell activation and proliferation.54

In recent years, multiple studies using single-cell RNA-seq have identified novel macrophage subsets within the aged muscle microenvironment that influence satellite cell behavior. For example, a novel subset of interferon-responsive macrophages (IFRMs) has been identified and proved to be capable of secreting CXCL10 upon IFN-γ stimulation, which binds to CXCR3 on satellite cells and activates ERK1/2 and MAPKp38 signaling pathways, thereby promoting satellite cell proliferation. However, the IFN-γ response is diminished in aged muscle, potentially reducing CXCL10 secretion by IFRMs and impairing satellite cell function.55 Also, another macrophage subset named Mac_1 promotes satellite cell proliferation by secreting hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), which activates the HGF/c-Met pathway and suppresses Cdkn1b expression. Impaired Mac_1 function in aged muscle may attenuate HGF/c-Met signaling, thereby reducing satellite cell proliferation.56

Critical knowledge gaps in satellite cell regulation during aging

Despite substantial progress in understanding satellite cell biology, several fundamental questions regarding satellite cell regulation in aging muscle remain unresolved. First, the causal relationship between satellite cell dysfunction and sarcopenia continues to be debated. While satellite cell depletion and impaired regenerative capacity are consistently observed in aged muscle,13,52 inducible depletion of satellite cells in adult sedentary mice impairs muscle regenerative capacity without affecting the development of sarcopenia under baseline conditions.21 This suggests that satellite cell loss alone may be insufficient to drive age-related muscle wasting in the absence of injury or degenerative stimuli, and that other factors—including neuromuscular junction degeneration, chronic inflammation, and niche dysfunction—may be primary drivers of sarcopenia.20

On the other hand, the signaling pathways affecting the transition between quiescence, activation, proliferation, and self-renewal are well characterized in young muscle, but how these pathways are quantitatively and qualitatively altered in aged satellite cells is less clear. For example, the PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway regulates the quiescence-to-activation transition, but whether age-related alterations in this pathway reflect adaptive responses to chronic niche signals or maladaptive cell-intrinsic changes is unresolved.20,44 Similarly, Notch signaling is essential for maintaining satellite cell quiescence and self-renewal,57,58 but the specific roles of individual Notch receptors and ligands in aged satellite cell dysfunction have not been systematically dissected.

Characteristics and underlying mechanisms of age-related sarcopenia

Neurogenic and myogenic changes in age-related sarcopenia

Age-related sarcopenia, caused by skeletal muscle aging,59 manifests as reduced muscle mass and strength, decreased flexibility, increased fatigue, and a heightened risk of falls and fractures.1,60 Also, a range of histological changes have been observed in aging muscle, including muscle fiber atrophy and disorganization, degeneration of neuromuscular junctions (NMJs), motor unit remodeling, increased fat infiltration, and a reduction in satellite cells.5,7

One characteristic of aged muscle is the remodeling of motor units, which involves alterations in motor neurons61 and muscle fiber composition.62 On the one hand, aging leads to a decline in both the number and function of α-motor neurons in the spinal cord,61 resulting in denervation of muscle fibers62 and disruption of the EC coupling in muscle cells,63 along with changing the organization and function of mitochondria, thereby leading to atrophy and functional decline in aged muscle.35 On the other hand, this denervation mainly affects fast-twitch (type II) muscle fibers.64,65 Concurrently, skeletal muscle senescence is accompanied by a pronounced depletion of the satellite cell pools residing specifically within these type II fibers,6 thereby profoundly compromising the intrinsic regenerative capacity of the tissue. Consequently, aged skeletal muscle exhibits preferential atrophy of type II fibers,66,67 which directly translates to impaired rapid contractile mechanics and diminished explosive power, ultimately manifesting as a generalized clinical decline in muscular strength. In a compensatory physiological attempt to mitigate this specific loss, slow-twitch (type I) and intermediate fibers frequently exhibit an aberrant expression of type II fiber-associated phenotypic markers; however, their absolute quantities remain largely unaltered.68 Ultimately, this complex structural and functional remodeling results in a shifted histological profile characterized by an elevated proportion of type I fibers and a corresponding reduction in type II fibers.

The association between satellite cells and age-related sarcopenia

As mentioned before, satellite cell depletion and cellular senescence are widely detected in aged muscle. These changes impair the regenerative capacity of skeletal muscle with aging and are major contributors to the development of age-related sarcopenia.20

NMJs, which innervate muscle fibers, are essential for maintaining muscle function.69 During aging, NMJs progressively degenerate, contributing to muscle atrophy. The age-related loss of satellite cells surrounding NMJs further accelerates the degeneration of NMJ and exacerbates muscle denervation.70 Moreover, studies have shown that satellite cells can mitigate age-related denervation by enhancing the viability of α-motor neurons.71,72 Thus, satellite cells play a critical role in maintaining NMJ integrity, preventing age-related NMJ degeneration, and alleviating skeletal muscle denervation in the elderly,73 thereby mitigating sarcopenia.

Recent studies have revealed a connection between satellite cells and age-related muscle fibrosis. With aging and satellite cell depletion, extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition around muscle fibers increases, leading to age-related muscle fibrosis.21 A potential mechanism involves elevated expression of Cyr61, which activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, driving satellite cells toward a profibrotic phenotype. This shift promotes excessive ECM accumulation and contributes to the process muscle fibrosis.74

Therapeutic strategies for age-related sarcopenia

Exercise-induced satellite cell activation and its potential to mitigate sarcopenia

Exercise confers numerous health benefits, including enhancing bone strength, reducing obesity, improving cardiovascular function, and boosting immune responses. Among these, exercise also plays a significant role in improving muscle function.75 As a form of physiological stress, exercise induces micro-injuries in muscle tissue, similar to the response following muscle damage.39 These micro-injuries can activate QSCs, triggering their proliferation and differentiation, suggesting a potential strategy for alleviating age-related sarcopenia.

Exercise regimens are generally categorized into resistance exercise and aerobic exercise, both of which can induce satellite cell activation, promote muscle fiber hypertrophy, and mitigate age-related sarcopenia. Daniel et al. conducted a study in older men and found that low-load resistance exercise increased the cross-sectional area of both type I and type II fibers in aging muscle, as well as the size of type II fibers and myonuclei. Concurrently, the population of satellite cells in both type I and type II fibers was also improved.66 In another study, Tim et al. implemented a 12-week exercise training program in older men, consisting of resistance exercise performed twice per week and high-intensity interval training once per week. This regimen improved muscle strength and aerobic capacity and significantly increased the number of QSCs and ASCs in both type I and type II muscle fibers.67

Amelioration of age-related sarcopenia via the modulation of satellite cell function by pharmacological agents or biomaterials

As a critical cell population in skeletal muscle, fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) are closely associated with muscle regeneration. Single-cell RNA sequencing of regenerating aged skeletal muscle revealed a tight spatiotemporal relationship between FAPs and satellite cell activation and muscle regeneration. FAPs promote muscle regeneration by secreting complement component C3 and communicating with monocytes/macrophages.80 A study in progeroid mice further showed that aging skeletal muscle exhibits increased numbers of FAPs and decreased numbers of QSCs, along with elevated levels of senescence markers and mTORC1 activation in both cell types, while senescent FAPs impair the proliferative and differentiative capacity of satellite cells. However, treatment with senolytic agents, such as fisetin, can induce apoptosis in senescent FAPs, thereby increasing satellite cell numbers and alleviating age-related muscle atrophy in aging mice.81

In addition, other studies have explored the effects of melatonin on satellite cells. Intraperitoneal injection of melatonin in aged mice was found to improve grip strength, alleviate muscle atrophy and fibrosis, and restore the satellite cell pool. Further investigations revealed that melatonin enhances the myogenic potential of aged satellite cells, suppresses the expression of fibrotic markers, and reduces extracellular matrix (ECM) secretion. These effects are potentially mediated through inhibition of the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 pathway.76 Moreover, oral administration of melatonin combined with endurance training was shown to reduce satellite cell senescence by mitigating oxidative stress, improve myogenic capacity, thereby restore muscle morphology, and increase satellite cell population in aged mice.82

Several novel pharmacological and biomaterial-based interventions for sarcopenia have also been proposed in latest studies. For example, Dai et al. designed a hierarchically injectable hydrogel capable of sequentially delivery of antagomiR-467a-3p-loaded and antagomiR-874-5p-loaded satellite-cell-targeting bioengineered extracellular vesicles. This system enhanced satellite cell stemness and myogenic potential, effectively mitigating muscle loss in progeroid mice.49 In another study, Kitora et al. isolated satellite cells from the tibialis anterior muscle of mice and transplanted them into the tibialis anterior muscle after mixing with Matrigel. This intervention increased muscle mass and suppressed fibrosis, establishing a novel strategy for cell-based therapy of muscle atrophy.83

NMES as an emerging strategy for age-related sarcopenia

Technical parameters of NMES

NMES delivers electrical impulses through surface or implanted electrodes to depolarize motor nerves and induce muscle contraction. Despite its clinical use in rehabilitation, the field lacks standardized protocols for sarcopenia applications, and the influence of individual stimulation parameters on satellite cell biology remains poorly characterized. A systematic review of NMES for older adults found that common parameters include frequencies of 50–100 Hz with variable intensity and pulse duration, though parameter selection is frequently empirical rather than mechanistically justified.84

The parameters of NMES can be systemically described in several distinct aspects as follows. First, the frequency of NMES determines the rate of action potential firing. Low-frequency stimulation (≤20 Hz) produces twitch contractions and is typically used for endurance-oriented protocols. Higher frequencies (50–100 Hz) generate fused tetanic contractions required for strength gains. Kim et al. reported that 500 Hz stimulation produced more pronounced improvement in myogenic potential and reduction of aging markers in aged human skeletal muscle cells compared to lower frequencies, suggesting that optimal frequency may increase with age.32 Cheng et al. similarly found that higher-frequency stimulation (50–100 Hz) more effectively enhanced satellite cell proliferation and differentiation.85 Second, the pulse width of NMES determines the duration of membrane depolarization. Typical NMES protocols employ pulse durations of 200–500 μs. Wider pulses may recruit a greater proportion of fast-twitch (type II) fibers, which are preferentially affected in sarcopenia. Third, most NMES devices deliver the waveform of biphasic rectangular pulsed currents, which offer advantages in charge balance and tissue safety compared to monophasic waveforms. Fourth, the work-rest cycle of NMES determines the balance between stimulation and recovery. Typical strengthening protocols employ ON times of 6–15 s and OFF times of 30–50 s to allow metabolic recovery, while endurance-oriented protocols utilize shorter rest periods. Fifth, the intensity of NMES is typically adjusted to match individual maximum tolerated levels or achieve visible muscle contraction. Surface electrodes placed over motor points are standard. Finally, the electrode size and positioning of NMES devices influence current density and the spatial pattern of fiber recruitment. NMES as a therapeutic intervention for sarcopenia

Effects of NMES on aged muscle and satellite cells

As previously mentioned, aging skeletal muscle is characterized by a decline in muscle strength, type II fiber atrophy. Multiple studies have shown that NMES can enhance muscle strength and function, induce hypertrophy, increase the cross-sectional area of type II fibers,32,87 and elevate the proportion of type I fibers in aged muscle.28,34 These findings indicate the potential of NMES as a therapeutic strategy for sarcopenia. Moreover, several studies have examined the effects of NMES on satellite cells in aged muscle, demonstrating that NMES can improve their proliferation and differentiation, reduce apoptosis,34 and mitigate oxidative stress28—thereby restoring satellite cell function in aged muscle.

The AMPK signaling pathway plays a crucial role in regulating muscle metabolism and satellite cell physiology.39 When NMES is applied to atrophic muscle, it activates AMPK, which in turn activates TBC1D4, promoting the translocation of GLUT4 and enhancing glucose uptake, thereby improve the glucose metabolism of atrophic muscle. Furthermore, AMPK activation via NMES can suppress FOXO3 activity, thereby reducing the expression of muscle atrophy–related proteins Atrogin-1 and MuRF1, and inhibiting protein degradation in skeletal muscle.86 In addition, AMPK activation by NMES has also been reported to stimulate ULK1, thereby inducing autophagy in satellite cells and promoting their myogenic differentiation, which contributes to the restoration of muscle mass and function in aged or atrophic muscle.31 Moreover, a study by Madoka et al. demonstrated that the combination of NMES with blood flow restriction upregulates the ERK and Akt-mTOR signaling pathways in muscle tissue, suggesting that NMES may also improve skeletal muscle mass and function by enhancing protein synthesis.87

The role of calcium signaling in skeletal muscle contraction and satellite cell function

It is well known that calcium ions, as a key intracellular messenger, play an essential role in the physiological processes of skeletal muscle contraction and relaxation. When the muscle membrane receives an electrical signal and depolarizes, the signal is transmitted through the transverse tubule (TT) membrane to the interior of the cell. Then, this signal is received by the L-type calcium channels located on the sarcolemma or transverse tubules (TT), namely the dihydropyridine receptor (DHPR) and is transmitted to the calcium release channels of the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), specifically the ryanodine receptor (RYR), thereby triggers the release of a large amount of calcium ions from the SR into the myoplasm. The calcium ions will bind to troponin and initiate the sliding of myofilaments, leading to the contraction of skeletal muscle.88,89 As the myoplasmic calcium concentration increases, the sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase (SERCA) is activated, which facilitates the reabsorption of calcium ions into the SR, resulting in skeletal muscle relaxation. This process is known as EC coupling, where calcium signaling and the channel proteins DHPR and RYR play critical roles.90

In addition, calcium ions and corresponding calcium channels are crucial in regulating satellite cell proliferation and differentiation. For instance, PIEZO1, a mechanosensitive ion channel activated by membrane tension, is highly expressed in undifferentiated satellite cells and mediates calcium ion influx, thereby regulating satellite cell proliferation and activation.91 Furthermore, the transient receptor potential canonical (TRPC1) is also highly expressed in satellite cells and plays a role in mediating calcium ion influx. This process is activated by FGF2 and ultimately promotes satellite cell activation and differentiation through the nuclear translocation of NFATc2 and NFATc3.38 Additionally, recent studies have found that PIP5K1α (type I phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate 5-kinase) is involved in the regulation of calcium ion concentration in satellite cells. It enhances the levels of PIP2 (phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate), thereby increasing production of IP3 (inositol 1,4,5-triphosphate), which can combine with the calcium ion channel IP3 receptor (IP3R) on endoplasmic reticulum and induces the release of calcium ions. This results in an increase in cytosolic calcium concentration and ultimately promotes the myogenic differentiation of satellite cells.36,92

Potential synergy of NMES and calcium signaling modulation in combating age-related sarcopenia

Current evidence and hypothesis

The hypothesis that NMES enhances satellite cell function through calcium-dependent mechanisms is supported by correlative but not causal evidence. Di Filippo et al. reported that an 8-week NMES intervention in healthy elderly subjects increased cytoplasmic free Ca2+ levels in undifferentiated satellite cells, enhanced MYOD and MYOG expression, and improved satellite cell fusion with myofibers.28 In aged mouse muscle, Kim et al. demonstrated that NMES application elevated intracellular calcium flux, enhanced myotube differentiation, and upregulated DHPR and RYR1 expression.32 However, neither study established a direct causal link between NMES-induced calcium transients and specific satellite cell outcomes, nor did they identify the specific calcium channels mediating these effects. Whether the observed calcium increases represent a primary mechanism of NMES action or a secondary consequence of myofiber contraction and paracrine signaling remains unclear.

Based on the available evidence, we have proposed three hypothetical pathways through which NMES affects the calcium dynamics of cells. First, NMES-induced depolarization of motor nerves triggers myofiber action potentials and excitation-contraction coupling, involving DHPR-mediated sensing of membrane potential and RYR1-mediated calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.90 This contraction releases paracrine factors—including FGF2, HGF, and IGF-1—that bind satellite cell surface receptors and activate downstream calcium channels such as TRPC1.38 Second, myofiber contraction generates mechanical strain in the satellite cell niche, which may activate the mechanosensitive calcium ion channel PIEXO1 on the surface of satellite cells and mediate calcium ion influx. Satellite cell activation and myogenic differentiation during skeletal muscle regeneration

Mechanisms of NMES-induced calcium signaling in skeletal muscle cells and satellite cells

NMES triggers the opening of DHPR on the skeletal muscle cell surface, mediating both extracellular Ca2+ influx and intracellular Ca2+ release. This calcium surge facilitates muscle contraction through EC coupling. Simultaneously, it activates the CaMKK2-AMPK signaling axis; downstream effectors, including FOXO3 and TBC1D4, are subsequently engaged to enhance muscle oxidative metabolism. In parallel, NMES promotes Ca2+ entry into satellite cells via calcium channels such as PIEZO1 and TRPC1. This influx activates the CaMKK2-AMPK-ULK1 pathway to modulate cellular autophagy. Additionally, the Ca2+ signal drives the nuclear translocation of NFATc2 and NFATc3, thereby promoting satellite cell proliferation.

Potential of combining NMES and calcium signaling modulation

If NMES does indeed enhance satellite cell function through calcium-dependent pathways, then pharmacological modulation of specific calcium channels could synergistically amplify therapeutic benefits. For example, applying calcium channel agonists may enhance mechanosensitive calcium entry in quiescent satellite cells. Meanwhile, RYR1 stabilizers may restore efficient calcium release in aged muscle while preventing deleterious calcium leak.93 However, it must be emphasized that the hypothesis of synergistic efficacy remains entirely speculative at present.

Clinical applying the strategy of NMES combining calcium modulation requires addressing challenges in both electrical and pharmacological delivery. For electrical stimulation, surface electrodes are non-invasive and well-tolerated, but produce heterogeneous current distribution and may insufficiently activate deep muscle regions. Implantable stimulators or minimally invasive percutaneous electrodes could provide more precise spatial targeting but introduce surgical risks. Emerging electroceutical technologies incorporating flexible, stretchable electrode arrays may enable personalized protocols that adapt to individual muscle architecture.32 For pharmacological delivery, calcium channel modulators require to be targeted and delivered to satellite cells. The feasibility of localized delivery approaches, such as injectable hydrogels for sustained release or electrical-responsive drug release system, has not been established for this application and remains speculative.

In realistic clinical situation, NMES alone for has already be applied in treating elderly patients with mobility limitations, such as during prolonged hospitalization, or in advanced frailty.28,30 Based on this, for elderly patients with sarcopenia who have confirmed depletion of satellite cells through biomarkers and other methods, the strategy of NMES combining calcium modulation may be applied to enhance the therapeutic effect of NMES after the confirmation of safety and efficacy.

Conclusion and future directions

Age-related sarcopenia is a degenerative condition of the musculoskeletal system in the elderly, characterized by a decline in muscle strength and a reduction in muscle mass. Satellite cells, as resident stem cells in skeletal muscle, undergo age-associated decreases in both number and function. This process is influenced by chronic activation of satellite cells within aged muscle, intrinsic cellular senescence, and the involvement of other cell types such as macrophages and FAPs. Moreover, satellite cells are closely associated with age-related microstructural alterations in skeletal muscle, including motor unit remodeling, denervation, and fibrosis. Based on these observations, enhancing satellite cell function has become a key therapeutic focus in the management of age-related sarcopenia. Conventional treatment strategies mainly include physical training and pharmacological interventions targeting specific molecular pathways. However, as potential therapeutic strategies, NMES and calcium signaling modulation have received relatively little attention. NMES has the potential to improve muscle strength and mass in aged skeletal muscle by inducing passive contraction or modulating satellite cell activity through simulating neural electrical signals. In parallel, calcium ions play a pivotal role not only in EC coupling but also in regulating the proliferation and differentiation of satellite cells. Thus, targeting intracellular and extracellular calcium homeostasis may serve as an additional therapeutic strategy for ameliorating muscle atrophy. In summary, this review proposes a combined therapeutic approach combining NMES and calcium signaling modulation—such as the use of calcium channel agonists—as a promising and synergistic strategy for improving satellite cell function and alleviating age-related sarcopenia. This integrative approach holds considerable potential for clinical application and warrants further investigation.

Despite the therapeutic potential of combining NMES with calcium channel modulation, significant biological and technical knowledge gaps remain in clinical settings. Specifically, the optimal NMES parameters required to activate satellite cells in senescent skeletal muscle, alongside the precise administration routes and dosages of calcium channel modulators, have yet to be established. Furthermore, the therapeutic window—balancing safety and efficacy for this novel strategy—remains undefined. Consequently, these unresolved critical issues impede the clinical translation of this dual-intervention strategy for age-related sarcopenia. To systematically bridge these field gaps, future investigations should adopt multi-phase translational research. First of all, comprehensive in vitro studies are necessitated to identify the specific calcium channels expressed within satellite cell subpopulations derived from aged human and animal models. Concurrently, these in vitro models must be utilized to screen for effective NMES parameters and determine the pharmacological safety and efficacy profiles of calcium channel agonists. Based on this foundational data, the subsequent phase entails the engineering of integrated therapeutic platforms that combine advanced NMES devices with targeted drug delivery systems. These platforms must undergo rigorous validation and optimization in aged in vivo models to mechanistically elucidate their impact on biological activity of satellite cells and pathology of sarcopenia, thereby establishing robust safety and intervention parameters. Finally, this progressive validation will culminate in the development of a clinical-grade therapeutic platform. Subsequent well-designed clinical trials involving carefully screened cohorts of patients with sarcopenia will be imperative to evaluate human-specific effects on satellite cell function and overall disease mitigation, facilitating the successful bench-to-bedside translation of this combinatory therapeutic strategy.
