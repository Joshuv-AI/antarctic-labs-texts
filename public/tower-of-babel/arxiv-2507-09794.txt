Joint Scheduling of Deferrable and Nondeferrable Demand with Colocated Stochastic Supply
Minjae Jeon, Lang Tong, Qing Zhao

Abstract
We address the problem of optimal joint scheduling of deferrable and nondeferrable demand involving colocated stochastic supply. Deferrable demand can be delayed within its service deadline, whereas nondeferrable demand must be scheduled immediately. Under a finite-horizon stochastic dynamic programming formulation, we show that the optimal scheduling policy is a “procrastination policy” that delays scheduling as much as possible and is characterized by three procrastination parameters. Exploiting the low-dimensional parameterization of the optimal policy, we propose a Procrastination Threshold Reinforcement Learning algorithm. Numerical experiments based on real-world test data confirm that the threshold-learning algorithm closely approximates the optimal policy and outperforms standard benchmarks.



I
Introduction

We study the joint scheduling of deferrable and nondeferrable demand with colocated stochastic supply. A deferrable demand (job) is stochastic with random arrival, random demanded quantity, and a deadline for completion. A deferrable demand can be delayed subject to a penalty if it is not completed by the deadline. The nondeferrable demand must be scheduled upon arrival. However, the quantity served is elastic with respect to both cost and availability of supply. In addition to the traditional supply available to meet demand at a cost, a special feature of the problem is the presence of colocated stochastic supply that is free but with a random amount. The objective of the co-optimized demand scheduling is to maximize the surplus, defined as the consumption benefit minus the cost.

Applications involving this type of scheduling are typical in energy systems, sometimes referred to as the
colocation problem
. A prototype example is the scheduling of (deferrable) electric vehicle (EV) charging, along with other nondeferrable household consumptions. The colocated supply is the rooftop solar that provides a random amount of power. In addition to the free colocated solar, EV charging and household demand can also be served by electricity purchased from the grid at cost. Other applications include renewable colocated commercial and industrial demand, such as data centers serving time-sensitive and flexible computation tasks and a manufacturing facility with deferrable and nondeferrable tasks on a production line.

The optimal scheduling of deferrable demand is challenging for two main reasons. First, the stochasticity of demand and supply calls for a stochastic dynamic programming formulation. Without special structural properties, obtaining an optimal scheduling policy faces the curse of dimensionality, especially when the action space is large and possibly uncountable. Second, the underlying dynamic programming model is often unknown or inaccurately specified. Although reinforcement learning methods can be employed, their learning complexity and performance are difficult to quantify.

This paper aims to examine the structure of the optimization and obtain a low-dimensional characterization of the optimal policy for efficient learning and implementation. To this end, we couch the optimal joint deferrable and nondeferrable scheduling problem in the context of a household energy management problem involving deferrable EV charging and nondeferrable household consumption. The connection with other types of colocation problems is immediate.

I-B

Summary of results and contributions

The main contribution of this work is twofold. First, we establish the optimality of the
procrastination policy
—a threshold policy parameterized by three “procrastination parameters” under a Markovian model for colocated supply and other simplifying assumptions.

Second, we propose a
Procrastination Threshold Reinforcement Learning
(PTRL) algorithm that learns the three dimensional procrastination parameters. Although the reinforcement learning of procrastination parameters cannot be guaranteed to converge to the optimal value, and the assumptions made for the optimality of procrastination policy may not hold in practice, we demonstrate using out-of-sample real-world data traces that PTRL outperforms standard reinforcement learning and model predictive control benchmarks.

Finally, this work builds on an earlier conference publication
[
31
]
with significant extensions. Specifically, we relax the assumption of time-independent renewables and time-of-use (ToU) prices by considering a Markovian stochastic process and general time varying prices, respectivley. In addition, we propose a learning algorithm that addresses issues arising from unknown or inaccurate models.

II-A

The Energy Management and Control

The household energy consumption is scheduled by an energy management system (EMS) under a
T
-horizon scheduling model indexed by
t=1,\cdots,T
, with

t=T+1
the (non-scheduling) terminal interval.

Two types of household energy-consuming devices are considered: one has the deferrable demand (such as EV charging) whose total demand needs to be met by a specific deadline. The other type includes a set of nondeferrable demands that must be served in each interval
t
. At the beginning of interval
t
, the EMS measures the available DG level
g_{t}
and sends a control signal
\bm{a}_{t}=(\bm{d}_{t},v_{t})
to these devices.

With local generation
g_{t}
offsetting the gross consumption, the
net consumption

z_{t}
of the household is given by

z_{t}=v_{t}+{\mathbf{1}}^{\top}{\bm{d}}_{t}-g_{t}.

(1)

The EMS’s control policy makes the household a
prosumer
: a paying
consumer
to the distribution utility when
z_{t}>0
, a paid

by the utility when

, and a

The goal of the EMS is to maximize the household surplus defined by the total benefits minus the payment to the utility. Demand and supply models are detailed next.

II-C

Supply and costs

Two power supplies are used to serve both types of the demand. One is the purchased power from the grid—a decision variable with cost; the other is the behind meter DG (rooftop solar), whose production
g_{t}
in interval
t
can be measured at the beginning of interval

(g_{t})
is modeled as a non-homogeneous Markovian process with a conditional probability density function

g_{t+1}\sim f_{t}(\cdot\,|\,g_{t}).

(5)

The EMS controls on the usage of

; it may export a fraction of unused

to the grid for payment.

The cost of household consumption comes from the power purchased from the grid and the terminal cost of incompletion. We assume that the household is served by a distribution utility under a regulated tariff. A widley adopted tariff is the Net Energy Metering (NEM) that charges or credits the household based on their net consumption

.

The NEM tariff is deterministic, possibly time varying, and known to all retail customers. In interval

The standard NEM payment function

P_{\bm{\pi}_{t}}(z_{t})=(z_{t}\pi_{t}^{+})\mathbbm{1}(z_{t}\geq 0)+(z_{t}\pi_{t}^{-})\mathbbm{1}(z_{t}<0),

(6)

where
\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)

\pi_{t}^{+}
by the consumer no smaller than the export rate,
\pi_{t}^{+}\geq\pi_{t}^{-}

z_{t}

II-E

Model Assumptions

We summarize key model assumptions with comments and justifications.

A1)

The utility function of nondeferrable consumption is concave increasing and known.

A2)

The set of NEM price vectors
\{\bm{\pi}_{t}=(\pi_{t}^{+},\pi_{t}^{-})\}
are deterministic and known to the EMS, satisfying

\big(\pi_{t}^{+}\geq\pi_{t}^{-},\forall t\big)~\mbox{and}~\big(\min\{\pi_{t}^{+}\}\geq\max\{\pi_{t}^{-}\}\big).

(11)

A3)

The penalty function
q
for incomplete deferrable demand is strictly increasing and convex with
q(0)=0
and
\frac{dq}{dy}(0)>\max\{\pi_{t}^{+}\}
.

A4)

Without loss of generality,
y_{1}\leq T\bar{v}
.

A1 is standard in microeconomic theory of consumer behavior. In practice, the consumer utility function may not be known, and inverse optimization techniques (
e.g.,
[
32
]
) can be deployed. A2 is necessary to avoid risk-free arbitrage. It holds for all practical NEM tariffs. Assumption A3 eliminates the trivial case that it is more economic not serving the deferrable demand than serving it with purchased power. If A4 is not satisfied, there will be at least
q(y_{1}-T\bar{v})
penalty regardless of scheduling algorithm. Therefore, we only need to consider serving deferrable demand of size
y_{1}\leq T\bar{v}
. By asssumption A4, the completion of charging demand is always feasible.

III
Structures of the Optimal Policy

In this section, we introduce the procrastination policy and establish its optimality.

III-A

The procrastination intuition

We motivate the procrastination policy by considering the simple scenario that schedules only the deferrable demand (EV charging) and the NEM tariff rates are time invariant,
i.e.,
\bm{\pi}_{t}=(\pi^{+},\pi^{-})
for all
t
. Because the marginal cost of DG is zero, it is intuitive to avoid purchasing power (at the price of
\pi^{+}
) by delaying (procrastinating) any purchase unless it is necessary. The timing of such purchasing decisions needs to be made precise.

The procrastination policy can be stated as follows: in interval
t
with remaining demand of
y_{t}
,

1.

use all DG production
g_{t}
to meet the demand and export surplus.

2.

delay (procrastinate) purchasing until it is no longer possible to fulfill the deferrable demand by the end of interval
T
.

The intuition of procrastination policy is as follows.
Item 1) prioritizes using DG
g_{t}
to serve the demand because DG has zero marginal cost. Serving demand with
g_{t}
has a higher marginal value (
\pi^{+}
) than that gained from exporting
g_{t}
to the grid. Item (2) means that there is no purchase of power from the grid if the remaining demand can still be served by the deadline.

We establish formally (Appendix
B
) the optimality and the threshold structure of the procrastination policy
(\mu_{t}^{\text{p}})
.

(Optimality of Procrastination)
.

Under the time-invariant NEM pricing and in the absence of non-deferrable demand, the procrastination policy
\mu_{t}^{\text{p}}:\bm{x}_{t}\to v_{t}^{*}
, with procrastination threshold
\theta_{t}=(T-t)\bar{v}
is optimal :

\displaystyle v^{*}_{t}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}y_{t},&0<y_{t}\leq\min\{\bar{v},\;g_{t}\}\\
\min\{\bar{v},\;g_{t}\},&\min\{\bar{v},\;g_{t}\}<y_{t}\leq\theta_{t}+\min\{\bar{v},g_{t}\}\\
\min\{y_{t}-\theta_{t},\bar{v}\}&\theta_{t}+\min\{\bar{v},\;g_{t}\}<y_{t}\leq\theta_{t}+\bar{v}.\end{cases}

(12)

V-A

The evaluations of the proposed scheduling policy and comparison benchmarks were based on two real-world datasets. We used data collected from the Adaptive Charging Network (ACN) dataset

V-A

Demand

We considered a single household with nondeferrable and deferrable demands. Two types of nondeferrable demand were HVAC and standard consumption, and their utility functions were assumed to be quadratic with parameters learned from historical retail price and household electricity usage data in the Pecan Street dataset. For simplicity, we assumed time-independent utility functions.

The deferrable demand was in the form of a sequence of EV charging requests defined by three random attributes: arrival time of the charging request, kWh demanded, and completion deadline; these parameters were assumed to be known at the time of the request. The nondeferrable demand was part of the optimization. The maximum capacity of the charger was 7.2 kW, typical for a level-2 charger.

V-A

The household demands were met by two sources: the local renewable generation and grid-purchased electricity from the distribution utility. Renewable generation has zero marginal cost, whereas purchased electricity is subject to the Time-of-Use (ToU) Net Energy Management (NEM) tariff of the distribution utility. In the simulation, we used the Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E) ToU retail rate with on-peak hours defined as 4 PM to 9 PM. The NEM import-export rates

V-A

Training Strategy

The optimal scheduling derived in Section
III
assumes Markovian DG. The tests conducted in this work involved field-collected rooftop solar datasets that did not necessarily satisfy this assumption.

A significant limitation of implementing RL was the limited training data. A standard approach to circumvent data limitations is to use the available training data to learn a parametric generative model from which unlimited training data could be produced. So long as the test data are field-collected and statistically independent of the training data, the test results are justified.

To this end, we used the historical data to fit two models. One model was a first-order Gaussian-Markov (autoregressive) model, whose parameters were estimated from training data. The other was a time independent generation model with a truncated Gaussian distribution whose parameters were estimated from training data.

V-A
7
Performance Evaluation

The performance of learning is evaluated in two ways; one is based on the out-of-sample synthetic data generated under the same model used in training. Such tests demonstrate the performance under ideal conditions, where the data model in training matches perfectly with that in testing.

A more realistic evaluation can be achieved by using field-collected data to assess the performance of RL solutions trained under independent or Markovian models. VI
Conclusion

This paper develops joint optimization and control of deferrable and nondeferrable demand with colocated stochastic supply. This problem is a prototype for many applications in the energy sector where operators of data centers, manufacture plants, and EV charging facilities, face rapidly increasing costs of electricity. Colocating stochastic distributed generation such as wind and solar can bring significant economic and environmental benefits. The randomness of the stochastic generation brings technical challenges that standard MDP formulations requires detailed operation and resource models that are often unavailable or inaccurate, and there is a major computational barrier from the curse of dimensionality for MDP. The main contribution of this work is the low-dimensional characterization of the underlying MDP, making it possible for a much simpler and more effective reinforcement learning strategy.
