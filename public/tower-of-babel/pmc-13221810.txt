Mid-Field Cardiovascular MRI in Class III Obesity
Authors: Katarzyna E Gil, Katherine Binzel, Akash Goyal, Daniel Giese, Ning Jin, Yingmin Liu, Chong Chen, Yu Ding, Xavier Sieber, Ruud B van Heeswijk, Matthias Stuber, Rizwan Ahmad, Yuchi Han, Matthew S Tong, Orlando P Simonetti, Juliet Varghese

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is a cornerstone for assessing cardiac function and tissue characterization but is often inaccessible to patients with class III obesity because of the bore size and table weight limits of conventional 1.5-T/3-T systems.Case SeriesFive patients with body mass index ≥40 kg/m2 underwent clinically indicated CMR on a 0.55-T 80-cm wide-bore scanner using prototype cine, parametric mapping, phase-contrast flow, late gadolinium enhancement, and thoracic MR angiography sequences.DiscussionDiagnostic image quality was achieved in all cases, enabling comprehensive evaluation of ventricular function, myocardial tissue, valvular morphology, and aortic anatomy. Findings guided diagnosis, pharmacotherapy optimization, and procedural planning.Take-Home MessagesWide-bore 0.55-T CMR enables diagnostic imaging in patients with class III obesity, who are often excluded from conventional MR systems. Mid-Field, Wide-Bore CMR/MRA in Patients With Cardiac Comorbidities and Class III Obesity

CMR = cardiovascular magnetic resonance; MRA = magnetic resonance angiography.

Background

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is the noninvasive imaging gold standard to assess cardiac function and to characterize myocardial tissue. However, bore sizes of 60 to 70 cm on systems with conventional field strengths of 1.5-T and 3-T often preclude CMR in patients with class III obesity (body mass index [BMI] ≥40 kg/m2).1 Affecting 9.4% of U.S. adults (2021-2023), class III obesity significantly increases cardiovascular morbidity, with a 2 to 3 times higher risk of hypertension, heart failure, and ischemic heart disease compared to normal-weight individuals.2,3

We report 5 nonconsecutive CMR cases in which transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was either nondiagnostic or the patients were unable to undergo imaging in narrower 1.5-T/3-T scanners owing to body habitus and/or claustrophobia, and where an 80-cm wide-bore 0.55-T system provided diagnostic-quality imaging.

Take-Home Messages

•Wide-bore (80-cm), mid-field (0.55-T) CMR can provide diagnostic-quality imaging in patients with class III obesity, who are often excluded from narrower bore, higher field-strength MR systems.

Methods

A commercial 80 cm–bore whole-body 0.55-T system with 700-lb table weight limit (MAGNETOM Free.Max, Siemens Healthineers) was used to accommodate patients with larger body habitus. The system gradients have a maximum amplitude of 26 mT/m and a slew rate of 45 T/m/s. An anteriorly positioned 12-element body array coil was combined with a posteriorly positioned 6-element body array coil for MR signal reception. An external monitor (Expression MR400 In Vivo, Philips) was used for electrocardiogram (ECG) triggering. A comprehensive set of prototype CMR techniques for myocardial parametric mapping, cine, phase contrast flow, late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), and gadolinium- and ferumoxytol-enhanced MR angiography (MRA) of the thoracic aorta has been previously developed to address the lower signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and the limited gradient performance of this system.4,5 Here, we apply these CMR tools in the clinical assessment of patients with class III obesity.

Cardiac function was assessed with breath-held segmented compressed sensing–based balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) or gradient-recalled echo (GRE) cine. Aortic flow was assessed with breath-held segmented phase-contrast flow with GRAPPA (GeneRalized Autocalibrating Partial Parallel Acquisition) acceleration. Breath-held single-shot T2-prepared bSSFP images for T2 mapping and modified Look Locker inversion recovery (MOLLI) inversion recovery–prepared bSSFP images for T1 mapping were used. LGE images were acquired with a breath-held segmented inversion recovery–prepared bSSFP sequence after administration of gadolinium. Breath-held ungated GRAPPA-accelerated or breath-held ECG-triggered compressed sensing–accelerated GRE sequences were used for gadolinium-enhanced MRA acquisitions. A free-breathing inversion-recovery–prepared GRE sequence with image navigator for respiratory motion compensation6 was used for ferumoxytol-enhanced MRA assessment.

The CMR imaging workflow was similar to that of higher field systems.7 To maintain adequate temporal resolution, fewer lines of k-space per heartbeat were acquired for segmented scans such as cine, phase-contrast flow, MRA, and LGE. Additional source images were acquired for single-shot imaging applications such as parametric mapping to boost SNR. These system-specific protocol adjustments lengthened scan times for most techniques at mid-field by a few heartbeats. Detailed sequence parameters are listed in Table 1. Images were processed using SuiteHEART (Neosoft) and were interpreted by the assigned CMR reader of the day (American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association level 3–certified cardiologist). Normative reference ranges (mean ± 2 SDs) for myocardial relaxation times were defined from institutional control data as follows: native T1 = 671.9 ± 16.9 ms (638-705 ms), T2 = 60.9 ± 3.6 ms (54-68 ms), and extracellular volume fraction (ECV) = 23.9% ± 2.5% (18.9%-28.9%).

Patient 1. Heart Failure Etiology

A 54-year-old man (BMI: 41 kg/m2), with a history of patent foramen ovale closure, stroke, and supraventricular tachycardia presented with progressive shortness of breath and chest discomfort. TTE showed preserved biventricular systolic function, no significant valvular heart disease, and normal left ventricular diastolic function, though limited by large body habitus. Coronary computed tomography angiography revealed mild nonobstructive coronary artery disease. Right heart catheterization confirmed new-onset heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (severely elevated biventricular filling pressures, mean right atrial pressure: 25 mm Hg, mean pulmonary arterial pressure: 38 mm Hg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure: 28 mm Hg) and normal to high normal cardiac output (Fick cardiac output: 10.7 L/min, Fick cardiac index: 4.2 L/min/m2).

After initiation of guideline-directed medical therapy and intravenous diuresis, CMR was performed to further assess heart failure etiology. Biventricular size (left ventricular end-diastolic volume index [LVEDVI]: 91 mL/m2, right ventricular end-diastolic volume index [RVEDVI]: 84 mL/m2) and systolic function (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF]: 62%, right ventricular ejection fraction [RVEF]: 64%) were normal on bSSFP cine (Figure 1A, midventricular short-axis view; Figure 1B, horizontal long-axis view showing interatrial septal occluder device; Video 1). CMR did not show any features suggestive of inflammatory or infiltrative cardiomyopathy, precluding the need for additional targeted treatment. Native T1 (Figure 1C; 702 ms) and T2 (Figures 1D and 1E; global T2: 59 ms) relaxation times (measured on myocardial regions without artifacts) were normal, with no evidence of diffuse interstitial expansion (ECV: 17%) or myocardial edema/inflammation. While heterogeneity was visually observed on T2 maps, regional T2 values (52-61 ms) were all within the normative range. There was no nonischemic myocardial fibrosis or infarct scar on LGE (Figures 1F and 1G).

Genetic testing, including a targeted panel for idiopathic or hereditary restrictive cardiomyopathies, identified no significant pathogenic variants. The patient subsequently enrolled in a randomized controlled trial to explore the possible benefit of left atrial offloading via shunt creation between the left atrium and the coronary sinus.

Patient 2. Ventricular Arrhythmia Etiology

A 52-year-old man with BMI 44 kg/m2, hypertension, and palpitations was found to have a high burden of unifocal premature ventricular contractions (PVCs; 22%) and nonsustained ventricular tachycardia with left bundle branch morphology on Holter monitoring. Exercise stress TTE demonstrated a significant increase in arrhythmia burden with activity, despite no evidence of coronary artery disease on coronary computed tomography angiography.

The patient was referred from an out-of-state tertiary health system for CMR to assess the etiology of ventricular arrhythmia. Biventricular size and systolic function were normal (LVEDVI: 98 mL/m2, RVEDVI: 102 mL/m2, LVEF: 55%, RVEF: 54%) on bSSFP cine imaging (Figures 2A to 2C, Video 2). Frequent PVCs resulted in mild interventricular septal dyssynchrony (Video 2) and caused cardiac motion artifacts on some images. LGE imaging (Figures 2D to 2F) suggested presence of focal linear right ventricular outflow tract fibrosis near the basal anterior interventricular septum (Figures 2A, 2D, and 2F, white arrows), consistent with the suspected source of ventricular arrhythmia. LGE was also visible on the cine images, as they were acquired after gadolinium contrast administration. Native T1 and T2 values were not elevated, with no evidence of diffuse interstitial expansion (Figure 2G; native T1: 660 ms, ECV: 23%) or myocardial edema/inflammation (Figure 2H; global T2: 64 ms). Midmyocardial fat was present in the apical septum, as observed on cine (Video 2), LGE, and out-of-phase GRE images (Figure 2B, 2C, 2E, 2F, and 2I, black arrows).

After identification of the myocardial fibrosis, the patient underwent ablation for PVCs originating from the right ventricular outflow tract and the aortic valve at the junction between the right and left coronary cusps, resulting in a decreased arrhythmia burden and symptomatic improvement.

Patient 3. Congenital Valve Defect: Bicuspid Aortic Valve

A 34-year-old man (BMI: 49 kg/m2) with hypertension and nonexertional chest pain underwent TTE, which raised concern for significant aortic dilation, but aortic valve morphology and function could not be evaluated given poor image quality.

CMR and MRA were performed to assess biventricular systolic function, aortic valve, and thoracic aorta. Biventricular size and systolic function were normal (LVEDVI: 76 mL/m2, RVEDVI: 67 mL/m2, LVEF: 62%, RVEF: 62%) on bSSFP cine (Figure 3A and 3B, Video 3), with no features suggestive of inflammatory or infiltrative cardiomyopathy. Native T1 and T2 values were normal, with no evidence of diffuse interstitial expansion (Figure 3C; native T1: 661 ms, ECV: 21%) or myocardial edema/inflammation (Figure 3F; global T2: 58 ms). There was no nonischemic myocardial fibrosis or infarct scar on LGE imaging (Figures 3D and 3E).

The aortic valve was bicuspid (Sievert classification type 0, no visualized raphe) (Figure 3G and Video 3, asterisks on GRE cine) with no evidence of significant flow acceleration during systole (peak aortic velocity: 2.0 m/s) (Figure 3H, phase contrast flow). Asymmetric valve opening (Figure 3I, arrow; bSSFP cine systolic phase, note increased flow dephasing and enhanced jet visualization from longer repetition time of 4.22 ms and echo time of 1.75 ms) and trivial aortic regurgitation were demonstrated (Figure 3J, asterisk; bSSFP cine diastolic phase) (Video 3). There was no systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve. Three-dimensional time-of-flight thoracic MRA (Figure 3K) revealed mild aortic root dilation (3.9 cm measured sinus to commissure) and near-aneurysmal dilation of the proximal ascending aorta up to 4.9 cm (Figure 3K, inset).

Blood pressure control was optimized, and the patient was referred to the weight management program in anticipation of potential aortic intervention. After lifestyle and pharmacologic interventions, aortic dimensions remained stable over 2 years, as assessed by follow-up MRA acquired on a 70 cm–bore 3-T system after the patient lost 30 lb of body weight.

Patient 4. Postrepair Monitoring in Congenital Heart Disease

A 33-year-old man with BMI 58 kg/m2, DiGeorge syndrome, and history of interrupted aortic arch after having undergone end-to-end anastomosis followed by aortic graft repair, bicuspid aortic valve with aortic regurgitation after undergoing mechanical aortic valve replacement, and ventricular septal defect after undergoing patch repair, was referred for annual surveillance TTE at 7 years postrepair. Although TTE yielded suboptimal diagnostic quality, large body habitus and claustrophobia precluded the use of standard MR scanners.

CMR and MRA were performed to assess the prosthetic aortic valve, biventricular systolic function, thoracic aorta, and to exclude residual intracardiac shunting. Images were acquired after infusion of 4 mg/kg ideal body weight of ferumoxytol, in consideration of the excellent blood pool signal homogeneity that can be achieved in imaging congenital heart disease indications and patients with metallic implants. Biventricular size was normal, with low-normal biventricular systolic function (LVEDVI: 92 mL/m2, RVEDVI: 103 mL/m2, LVEF: 51%, RVEF: 45%) on GRE cine (Figures 4A and 4B, Video 4). Phase-contrast imaging (peak aortic velocity: 1.5 m/s) showed no evidence of mechanical aortic prosthesis obstruction or significant residual intracardiac shunting (Qp:Qs = 1). Image-based navigator GRE thoracic MRA (Figure 4C) demonstrated an intact aortic graft with mild aortic root dilation (Figure 4C, inset; maximum diameter: 4.3 cm measured sinus to commissure). Free-breathing, self-gated, whole-heart 5D cine8 images (Figures 4D to 4F) showed reduced valve artifact with clearly depicted blood pool contrast and prosthetic leaflet motion within the cage compared to the 2D GRE cine (Figures 4G to 4I, black arrows; Video 4), likely owing to the smaller voxel size and shorter echo time, which contributes to reduced signal dephasing and susceptibility artifacts. The volumetric 5D cine acquisition additionally boosts SNR. Regular follow-up assessments using the 0.55-T scanner were planned considering the patient's comfort and compliance, and the high quality of the CMR/MRA images.

Patient 5. Congenital Valve Defect: Unicuspid Aortic Valve

A 51-year-old woman with BMI 51 kg/m2, hypertension, and a systolic murmur underwent a TTE study that was technically difficult with suboptimal image quality but raised concern for significant aortic valve regurgitation and stenosis. CMR was performed to further assess aortic valve morphology and function and was prioritized over transesophageal echocardiography to avoid sedation, invasive procedural risks, and for optimal volumetric ventricular and valvular assessment.

Normal biventricular size and systolic function (LVEDVI: 75 mL/m2, RVEDVI: 60 mL/m2, LVEF: 55%, RVEF: 48%) were measured on bSSFP cine (Figures 5A and 5B, Video 5). The aortic valve was unicuspid with an acommissural pin-hole shape (Figure 5C, midsystole bSSFP cine), with moderate stenosis (aortic valve area: 1.4 cm2 by planimetry). Lack of leaflet coaptation (Figure 5D, asterisk; end diastole bSSFP cine, Video 5) resulted in mild to moderate central aortic regurgitation (Figure 5E, asterisk; regurgitant jet, diastole phase contrast image of aortic valve), with a regurgitant volume of 16 mL and a regurgitant fraction of 29% (Figure 5F, flow through aortic valve). Aortic dimensions were assessed as normal on breath-held ECG-triggered gadolinium contrast–enhanced thoracic MRA (Figure 5G, asterisk; aortic root), with no evidence of aortic coarctation. Contrast-enhanced MRA was chosen over noncontrast MRA because of the higher SNR and blood signal homogeneity, and to streamline study workflow that required administration of gadolinium contrast for myocardial tissue characterization.

The patient was referred to the adult congenital heart disease clinic for further follow-up and management. To optimize the result of a potential future aortic valve replacement and decrease the risk of surgical complications, glucagon-like peptide-1 agonist was introduced to achieve weight loss. Family screening with transthoracic echocardiography was recommended.

Discussion

Obesity presents a growing challenge in cardiovascular care given its associated comorbidities and significant limitations on diagnostic imaging. For patients with large body habitus, TTE quality suffers from poor acoustic windows,9 while cardiac computed tomography may involve higher radiation exposure when assessing ventricular and valvular function—an important consideration for younger patients and those who require serial imaging.

This case series presents the use of an 80-cm wide-bore 0.55-T system for diagnostic-quality CMR examinations in patients with class III obesity. The patients had either undergone a TTE with poor imaging quality limiting diagnosis, computed tomography was not preferred by the health care provider given high radiation exposure concern, or use of narrower bore MR systems were limited by body habitus and/or claustrophobia. Tailoring imaging strategies and optimizing modality selection are therefore essential for high-quality cardiovascular care in this population.

The cases demonstrate technical applications of precontrast CMR techniques, including parametric mapping, cine, phase-contrast MRI, and postcontrast techniques including gadolinium-enhanced MRA, LGE, and ferumoxytol-enhanced assessment of the aorta. They also demonstrate the capabilities of the 0.55-T system to assess heart failure, ventricular arrhythmia etiology, aortic valve morphology, mechanical prosthesis function, and aortic dimensions. The 0.55-T CMR examination provided imaging evidence that was further integrated into the individual patient's clinical decision-making process.

Although limited imaging cases are presented here, they represent a range of clinical indications, highlighting the possibility of obtaining diagnostic CMR results in a population where the alternative is less optimal. Other limitations of the study include no direct comparison to higher field systems, as the patients could not be accommodated in those scanners. However, prior studies have validated CMR techniques at mid-field with respect to higher field-strength systems, proving diagnostic reliability.10, 11, 12, 13 Technical and patient-specific challenges include slightly longer imaging protocols and breath-hold durations. For example, a single-slice 2D cine acquisition required 6 heartbeats at 0.55-T, compared with 3 heartbeats at 1.5-T/3-T. Similarly, T1 and T2 mapping protocols at 0.55-T were longer by 3 and 5 heartbeats, respectively. This may contribute to increased respiratory and cardiac motion artifacts in the images. Pulse gating was used when ECG triggering was challenging or unreliable in individual patients. Relatively lower SNR was observed compared with images obtained from individuals with lower BMI, potentially contributing to greater pixel-wise heterogeneity in quantitative maps and wider normative ranges. Dedicated receiver coils designed to provide greater penetration depth and surface area coverage may further improve cardiac imaging in larger patients and increase diagnostic sensitivity. Higher relaxivity for ferumoxytol has been demonstrated at mid-field; a lower dose than the 4 mg/kg used at higher fields and in this study may thus be sufficient, but this needs to be investigated further.

Conclusions

Examinations representing a range of CMR indications are presented from 5 patients with class III obesity who underwent clinical assessment on an 80 cm–bore 0.55-T MR system. The results provided information toward clinical decision-making in these patients. These cases highlight the potential for wider bore, mid-field MR systems to expand accessibility to CMR-based diagnosis and management for the growing population of severely obese patients, a group for whom alternative imaging modalities may be less effective and CMR may be challenging or inaccessible.
