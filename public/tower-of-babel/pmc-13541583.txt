A curious change in QRS morphology during epicardial pacing
Authors: Udbhav Kharad, Ahmed Almarzuqi, George J. Klein
Published: 2026

Case presentation

A 66-year-old male with junctional rhythm and a baseline right bundle branch block (RBBB) was admitted to the intensive care unit following cardiac surgery. Temporary epicardial pacing wires were secured to the right atrium (RA) and right ventricle (RV). The patient was paced in DDD mode at a rate of 89 bpm with a programmed atrioventricular interval (AVI) of 240 ms.

Postoperative telemetry demonstrated abrupt, transient transitions from a relatively narrow QRS to a wide complex rhythm on multiple occasions. One such example is shown in Fig. 1.

What is the likely mechanism behind the initiation, persistence, and eventual termination of this wide-complex rhythm?

Discussion

The proposed mechanism underscores the interaction between pacing and the intrinsic conduction network.

Pseudo-normalization of the RBBB via fusion with RV pacing

At baseline, the patient demonstrates a RBBB pattern on his ECG (Fig. 2A), consistent with delayed RV activation following left ventricular (LV) activation via the left bundle branch (LBB). With RV epicardial pacing in DDD mode and at an AVI of 240 ms, the paced RV impulse occurs earlier than the native right-sided activation. The paced impulse occurs synchronously with intrinsic LV activation through the LBB. This produces ventricular fusion (Fig. 2B), resulting in a “pseudo-normalized” QRS complex that is interestingly narrower than both the intrinsic RBBB QRS and a fully RV-paced QRS. Analysis of the overarching rhythm

Several key observations emerge when the rhythm strip is examined from a broader perspective. First, the ventricular cycle length remains constant across both the narrow, fused complexes and the wide-complex segment, interrupted only briefly by PVCs at either end of the wide-complex episode. Second, this rate matches the programmed DDD pacing rate of 89 bpm. Third, a strict 1:1 relationship between P waves and QRS complexes is maintained during the wide-complex rhythm. From a probabilistic standpoint, these observations favour a single atrially-paced rhythm with intermittent variations in QRS morphology triggered by PVCs, rather than multiple independent rhythms.

Initiation of the wide complex rhythm via PVCs

The transition from fused complexes (Fig. 3A) to a wide, LBBB-like morphology is consistently triggered by premature ventricular contractions (PVCs). These PVCs either originate in the LBB (given the RBBB-like appearance in lead V) or arise from the myocardium but retrogradely invade the LBB (Fig. 3B), rendering it refractory to subsequent antegrade impulses. When the next atrially-paced impulse arrives at the His bundle, it encounters the LBB in a state of functional refractoriness (Fig. 3C). As a result, ventricular activation occurs exclusively via RV epicardial pacing, without contribution from the intrinsic conduction system. This produces manifest RV pacing and a wide QRS with characteristic LBBB-like morphology.

Persistence: the linking phenomenon and concealed conduction

While the initial beat of pure RV pacing is explained by functional LBBB secondary to retrograde PVC invasion of the LBB, a hallmark of this case is the stability of the wide-complex rhythm once initiated. This persistence is explained by the linking phenomenon, an electrophysiologic mechanism by which a functional block in one conduction pathway is maintained throughout repeated retrograde penetration of impulses from another pathway, thereby preserving functional antegrade block over successive beats [3].

In this case, each subsequent RV-paced impulse traverses the interventricular septum (transseptal conduction) and retrogradely conceals into the LBB, perpetuating functional LBBB for the following atrial impulses (Fig. 3D). This repetitive “bottom-up” activation keeps the LBB refractory for each “top-down” atrial impulse. As long as RV pacing continues and transseptal conduction occurs before the next antegrade impulse arrives, the LBB remains functionally linked in a blocked state, allowing manifest RV pacing to persist indefinitely.

Termination of the functional block

Termination of the manifest pure RV pacing morphology therefore requires resolution of the functional LBBB maintained by the linking phenomenon. In this tracing, the block recovers via “peeling back of refractoriness” of the LBB. Peeling back of refractoriness is a key mechanism for resolving functional conduction blocks, in which early activation of a previously blocked pathway advances its refractory period and allows recovery of conduction [1].

In this tracing, an early PVC (Fig. 3E) retrogradely activates the LBB. By activating the LBB earlier, the PVC shortens the action potential duration in the LBB due to cardiac restitution [4]. This effectively lengthens the interval between LBB activation and the next atrial-paced impulse. This allows the LBB sufficient time to fully recover and participate in subsequent antegrade conduction, thereby restoring the original pattern of ventricular fusion.

During this patient's postoperative course, other mechanisms of allowing the LBB more time to recover were observed. These included post-extrasystolic pauses from early-coupled premature atrial complexes blocked in the AV node, as well as direct disruption of linking via disconnection of the RV pacing wire.

Conclusion

This case highlights several important insights into the dynamic behaviour of functional blocks in the cardiac conduction system under the influence of artificial pacing and extrasystoles. It illustrates key electrophysiologic phenomena, including fusion, linking, and peeling back of refractoriness.

Editorial commentary (Martin Green)

This case beautifully illustrates the importance of understanding “concealed conduction” as a mechanism of ongoing refractoriness in the conduction system. In this case, each beat that blocks in the right bundle branch leaves the distal right bundle branch available for concealed retrograde conduction up to the point where the previous anterograde beat was blocked! The PVC can depolarize the distal right bundle earlier than it would have been depolarized with the current timing of the concealed retrograde penetration, and thus allow the right bundle branch to be available for anterograde conduction via the next anterograde beat. This “peeling back” of the refractoriness of the right bundle branch proves that the right bundle branch block observed was a function of concealed retrograde penetration of the right bundle rather than a prolonged refractory period of the right bundle branch.
