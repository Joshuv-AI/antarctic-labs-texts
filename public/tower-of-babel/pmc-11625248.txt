Enhanced antireflection and absorption in thin film GaAs solar cells using dielectric composite nanostructures
Authors: Yanyan Wang, Jiali Chen, Ruiying Zhang, Min Qian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the application of dielectric composite nanostructures (DCNs) to enhance both antireflection and absorption properties in thin film GaAs solar cells, which are crucial for reducing production costs and improving energy conversion efficiency in photovoltaic devices. Building upon previous experimental validations, this work systematically explores the underlying theoretical mechanisms using the finite difference time domain (FDTD) method to analyze the light interaction with the proposed DCNs. The results show that the combination of Mie resonance, Fabry–Perot resonance, and guided resonance, induced by the surface structuring of the DCNs, significantly enhances light absorption in the active layer, particularly at longer wavelengths. For solar cells featuring a 500-nm-thick absorber layer, SARL-decorated solar cells demonstrated an average reflectivity of 12.18 %, whereas those incorporating DCNs exhibited a significantly reduced average reflectivity of 4.52 %. These findings indicate that DCNs structures are highly effective in enhancing the performance of thin and ultra-thin GaAs solar cells by minimizing surface reflection and increasing photon utilization, offering a promising solution for high efficiency, cost effective photovoltaic devices.Keywords: Dielectric composite nanostructures, Surface antireflection, Absorption enhancement, FDTD, GaAs solar cells

Introduction

Gallium arsenide (GaAs) solar cells have been widely employed in space applications due to their direct bandgap, excellent matching with the solar spectrum, high electron mobility, and high radiation hardness [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. However, high production costs and toxicity have hindered their widespread use in terrestrial photovoltaics. One approach to mitigate these issues is to reduce the thickness of the active layer, which introduces a series of challenges such as insufficient absorption of long-wavelength incident light, high surface reflection, and surface recombination [2,3]. Effective light management strategies, including the evaporation of optical antireflection layers or textured nanostructures on the front side and back scattering layers with metallic mirrors on the rear side, have been studied both theoretically and experimentally to reduce surface reflection in GaAs solar cells [[4], [5], [6], [7]].

Recent research has focused on enhancing the efficiency and reducing the costs associated with thin film GaAs solar cells through advanced light management strategies and material selection. Plasmonic nanostructures, such as nanorings and nanoparticles, have been integrated to exploit surface plasmons, significantly enhancing light absorption by scattering and trapping light within the absorber layer, thus improving short-circuit current density and overall efficiency [[8], [9], [10]]. Additionally, multi-layered dielectric antireflection coatings (ARCs) have been developed to minimize reflection losses across a broad spectrum, enhancing absorption in both the visible and near-infrared regions [11,12]. The use of nanostructured back reflectors, such as silver nanostructured mirrors, further increases the optical path length of photons within the thin absorber layer, improving photon absorption likelihood [[13], [14], [15]]. Dielectric nanostructures made from materials like SiO2, Si3N4, and TiO2 act as both antireflection layers and surface passivators, reducing surface recombination and enhancing light trapping [[16], [17], [18]]. Transparent conducting oxides (TCOs) like indium tin oxide (ITO) are employed for their high electrical conductivity and optical transparency, serving as efficient back reflectors and electrode materials [19,20]. Despite these advancements, several challenges remain. Reducing surface recombination rates is critical for improving efficiency, and surface passivation techniques using dielectric layers have shown promise in mitigating these losses. The implementation of advanced light trapping structures often involves complex fabrication processes, which can increase production costs, and ensuring the long-term stability of these materials under varying environmental conditions is essential for the durability of the solar cells.

Our group has previously demonstrated the successful application of DCNs on single-junction GaInP solar cells, where we observed significant improvements in both antireflection performance and surface passivation [21,22]. Building on this foundation, we have selected DCNs for this current study as a continuation of our previous work. These structures have proven effective not only in achieving broadband antireflection but also in mitigating surface carrier recombination, thereby improving open-circuit voltage. In this study, we apply DCNs to thin film GaAs solar cells and use the finite difference time domain (FDTD) method to systematically analyze light interaction mechanisms at the front surface and within the active region. Our results confirm that DCNs are highly effective in reducing surface reflection and extending the optical path length within the absorber layer, leading to enhanced light absorption. Integrating advanced light management strategies and carefully selecting materials are crucial for overcoming the challenges associated with thin film GaAs solar cells. Future research should focus on optimizing these strategies and developing cost-effective fabrication methods to enhance the commercial viability of these high-efficiency photovoltaic devices.

Structural model and simulation method

In this study, the simulated structure model, as depicted in Fig. 1(a), consisted of closely-packed hexagonal SiNx nanodomes with a period of 600 nm, a height of 560 nm, a diameter of 340 nm at the top, and a bottom diameter of 600 nm. Beneath these nanodomes lies a 60-nm-thick SiNx film, under which resides a planar single-junction GaAs solar cell.

(a) Perspective view, (b) XY view, and (c) YZ view of the GaAs solar cell with DCNs decoration, and (d) YZ view of the GaAs solar cell with SARL decoration.

The selection of SiNx as the material for the DCNs is based on several key considerations. SiNx exhibits high density, coupled with superior thermal and chemical stability, which makes it an excellent candidate for applications in photovoltaic devices. Its remarkable hydrophobicity and low density, in combination with its strong resistance to moisture, humidity, and elevated temperatures, renders it highly impermeable to both alkaline substances and water vapor. Furthermore, the crack resistance further contributes to enhancing solar cell efficiency and prolonging the device's lifespan. In addition, the high optical transparency of SiNx in the visible spectrum makes it an optimal material for use in both passivation layers and antireflection coatings, where light management is crucial for maximizing photovoltaic efficiency [23,24].

The dimensions of the structure model, including layer thicknesses and DCN parameters (such as the nanodome period and height), are primarily based on prior experimental results [22]. Specifically, the 60-nm-thick SiNx film was introduced to resolve the over-etching issue encountered in previous fabrication process using reactive ion etching (RIE) technology [21]. This layer acts as a protective buffer, improving the reliability of the fabrication process while enhancing surface passivation. The planar single-junction GaAs solar cell, from top to bottom, is composed of a 20-nm-thick AlInP window layer, a GaAs active layer with a thickness denoted as t, a 100-nm-thick Al0.49Ga0.51As backside field layer, a 300-nm-thick GaAs buffer layer, and a 75-nm-thick Au contact. Additionally, the single antireflection layer (SARL) decorated GaAs solar cell model was also simulated for comparison, with a 60-nm-thick SiNx film on top of the planar GaAs solar cell as shown in Fig. 1(d).

This simulation is based on practical design parameters derived from experimental data and provides a solid foundation for further refinement in subsequent efforts aimed at enhancing the performance of GaAs solar cells. By systematically addressing critical challenges associated with etching precision and surface passivation, this design contributes to improved fabrication reliability and increased overall efficiency of the solar cells.

The FDTD method was employed to simulate the electric field distribution, absorptance, and reflection spectra for the DCNs-decorated solar cells. The solid frames in Fig. 1(b) and (c) represent the smallest simulation area. Periodic boundary conditions were applied to the x and y directions, and perfect match layer (PML) boundary conditions to the z direction. It was assumed that all incident photons could be absorbed by the GaAs active region. The refractive indices and extinction coefficient values of each material layer used in the simulation model were measured using an ellipsometer. A plane wave source with a wavelength range from 300 nm to 920 nm and zero polar and azimuthal angles was used. The electric field distribution, absorptance, and reflection spectra were obtained through frequency-domain field and power monitors in Lumerical FDTD software. The grid accuracy of the simulations for x, y, and z was set to 4 nm to ensure convergence and accuracy of the modeling.

Results and discussions

To elucidate the antireflection characteristics of the DCNs, the reflection spectra for DCNs-decorated GaAs solar cells, with varying absorber layer thicknesses, were simulated. The resulting spectra are presented in Fig. 2.

(a) Simulated reflection spectra for GaAs solar cells with DCNs decoration and varying absorber layer thicknesses. (b) Reflection spectra comparison between SARL-decorated and DCNs-decorated GaAs solar cells, both with 500-nm-thick absorber layers.

As depicted in Fig. 2(a), the reflection spectra show an overlapping trend for incident wavelengths shorter than 530 nm, which diverges at longer wavelengths. This initial overlap is ascribed to the large extinction coefficient and the adequate thickness of the GaAs material, ensuring that nearly all incident light is absorbed by the active region. Consequently, the identical textured surface produces similar front interface reflections. However, for longer wavelengths, both the front interface reflection, caused by variations in the refractive index, and the back reflection from the bottom Au contact significantly influence surface reflection. Thinner GaAs absorber layers exhibit a greater susceptibility to back reflection, resulting in higher surface reflectivity, as corroborated by the simulated reflection spectra. Therefore, optical strategies play a pivotal role in minimizing surface reflection, particularly for long-wavelength incident light, which is not fully absorbed.

Fig. 2(b) provides a comparative analysis of the reflection spectra for GaAs solar cells decorated with either SARL or DCNs, both featuring a 500-nm-thick absorber layer. For incident wavelengths below 530 nm, surface reflection is predominantly influenced by front interface reflection. The SARL-decorated solar cells exhibit a gradual decline in reflectivity due to destructive interference. In contrast, the DCNs-decorated solar cells consistently exhibit enhanced antireflection performance over this wavelength range, effectively reducing front surface reflection.

For incident wavelengths exceeding 530 nm, the reflection spectra of the SARL-decorated solar cells exhibit quasi-periodic oscillations, primarily attributable to back reflection. In contrast, the reflection spectra of the DCNs-decorated solar cells display a combination of quasi-periodic oscillations, originating from back reflection, and disordered oscillations from the front interface. This interaction leads to a significant reduction in surface reflection, as most incident light is efficiently trapped within the absorber layer.

The average reflectivity of various GaAs solar cell configurations is summarized in Table 1. A comparative analysis between SARL-decorated and DCN-decorated solar cells, both utilizing a 500-nm-thick absorber layer, indicates that the average reflectivity of SARL-decorated solar cells is 12.18 %, while DCN-decorated cells exhibit a markedly lower reflectivity of only 4.52 %. These findings clearly indicate that, under identical absorber layer conditions, DCNs demonstrate superior antireflection properties.

Additionally, as the absorber layer thickness increases in DCN-decorated solar cells, the average reflectivity gradually decreases, which can be attributed to the reduced backside reflection effect from the bottom contact. This observed trend is consistent with above findings, further underscoring the effectiveness of DCNs in reducing surface reflection and enhancing the overall optical performance of GaAs solar cells.

To further understand the antireflection behaviors, the electric field distribution was simulated for the SARL- and DCNs-decorated GaAs solar cells. High electric field intensity was observed above the SARL-decorated solar cells compared to the DCNs-decorated ones, aligning with the reflection spectra in Fig. 2(b). In the absorber layer, the SARL-decorated cells exhibited striped patterns due to interference between incident light and reflected light from the bottom contact, except at an incident wavelength of 300 nm. For the DCNs-decorated solar cells, similar electric field distribution was seen above the four solar cells with different absorber layer thicknesses when λ ≤ 530 nm, owing to sufficient light absorption. The phenomenon within the absorber layers differed, except at an incident wavelength of 300 nm. With a redshift in wavelength, Mie resonance excited by SiNx nanostructures began to affect the absorber layers. At 530 nm, insufficient absorption length brought light interaction within the 500-nm-thick absorber layer, and at 610 nm for the 1000-nm-thick, 710 nm for the 2000-nm-thick solar cells, as shown in Fig. 3(b)–(c), and Fig. 3(d) respectively. Specifically, not only Mie resonance but combined resonance modes composed of Fabry–Perot resonance from the vertical direction and guided resonance from the horizontal direction affected the absorber layer. Furthermore, the thinner the absorber layer, the more pronounced the resonance impacts and the stronger the electric field intensity within the active region, particularly at an incident wavelength of 920 nm.

As shown, the light absorptance for the four solar cells before the wavelength of 530 nm all achieved above 98 % due to the strong light absorption ability of GaAs material. In other words, surface reflection is the only absorption loss in this wavelength domain. Therefore, by decorating antireflection nanostructures to allow more incident light to reach the absorber layer, light utilization can be enhanced. The absorptivity value decreased rapidly for the 500-nm-thick solar cells once the wavelength exceeded 530 nm, while the 3000-nm-thick solar cells still achieved 98 % before the incident wavelength of 810 nm. Thus, decorating surface antireflection structures for thin and especially ultra-thin film solar cells to enhance light absorption can be a beneficial strategy to improve photoelectric conversion efficiency.

In addition, the light absorptance for the DCNs-decorated solar cells was simulated using the FDTD method and shown in Fig. 5, with the calculated Yablonovitch limit for the planar solar cells as a comparison. When the incident wavelength is < 530 nm, the absorptance curves exhibit the same trend for the four DCNs-decorated solar cells due to similar surface reflection, leading to the same light flux to the absorber layer and excellent light absorption ability of GaAs material, ensuring adequate photon utilization. With a redshift in wavelength, the absorptance for the DCNs-decorated solar cells maintained a relatively high value due to the combined effect of multi-resonance modes. For instance, in Fig. 5(a), compared to the rapid decrease until the absorptance curves of the planar solar cells drop to zero, the value for the DCNs-decorated solar cells remained above 90 % before a wavelength of 800 nm and even 30 % at 900 nm, demonstrating superior absorption enhancement brought by the DCNs. Issues such as insufficient light absorption and optical transmission for long-wavelength light are also addressed with these structures. The simulation results presented in Fig. 5(a) are consistent with the electric field distribution in Fig. 3(a) and (b), further demonstrating that the DCNs-decorated solar cells can achieve high-efficiency surface antireflection and absorption enhancement within the active region.

Comparison of the Yablonovitch limit for planar GaAs solar cells with the light absorptance for DCNs-decorated GaAs solar cells with absorber layer thicknesses of (a) 500 nm, (b) 1000 nm, (c) 2000 nm, and (d) 3000 nm.

For the solar cells with thicker absorber layers, adequate absorption length and a large material absorption coefficient ensure that most incident photons are absorbed in a single pass, even for the long-wavelength domain, as seen in Fig. 5(d), leading to weak light interaction within the active region. This also explains the phenomenon presented in Fig. 3, where the electric field intensity for the 500-nm-thick solar cells is much stronger than that for the 3000-nm-thick ones. Therefore, such DCNs structures are more meaningful for thin and especially ultra-thin film solar cells to reduce surface reflection, achieve absorption enhancement, passivate the semiconductor, and improve photoelectric conversion efficiency.

To further investigate the optical antireflection and light-trapping mechanisms, a MOVIE monitor was used to record the light interaction before and after entering the absorber layer of the 500-nm-thick solar cells. The results are presented in Fig. 6. This simulation only includes one incident and one reflection of the incident light.

Electric field distribution during light propagation through DCNs-decorated GaAs solar cells.

As shown in Fig. 6(a)–6(c), plane waves propagate from above the nanostructures at first, causing electric field distortion when approaching these structures. Mie resonance is excited inside the nanostructures due to their typical length scales, which are equal to or smaller than the wavelength of light, causing the electromagnetic wave to concentrate in the middle of the structures. Furthermore, prominent electric field enhancement existed around the nanostructures, arising from the multi-reflection effect, where reflected light between the nanostructures could be guided into adjacent ones again, resulting in a high-intensity electric field in the central region of the structures. Thereafter, this incident light was gradually introduced to the absorber layer, as shown in Fig. 6(d)–6(h). As observed, almost all incident light can pass through the DCNs, with only a few reflecting back to the air above the interface, proving an outstanding antireflection property for the DCNs. This incident light continues to propagate vertically and attenuates due to absorption by the active region. The remaining light with larger wavelengths that reach the bottom contact reflects once more, as shown in Fig. 6(g)–6(i), thus exciting the resonance modes to achieve sufficient utilization for long-wavelength light. In summary, DCNs structures demonstrate significant potential for application in thin film solar cells, maximizing light trapping efficiency while further reducing production costs.

Conclusions

Dielectric composite nanostructures have demonstrated brilliant broadband antireflection and absorption enhancement properties. The antireflective mechanism for single-junction GaAs solar cells with varying absorber layer thicknesses has been studied. From the simulated results of reflection spectra and electric field distribution, it can be deduced that surface reflection is merely related to the thickness of the absorber layer when the incident wavelength is less than 530 nm, due to the sufficient absorption length and large absorption coefficient of GaAs material. Hence, applying DCNs structures on the surface of the solar cells can effectively enhance the incident light flux to the absorber layer. Furthermore, absorption enhancement resulting from multi-resonance modes, including Mie resonance, Fabry–Perot resonance, and guided resonance, has been demonstrated when the incident wavelength is ≥ 530 nm. Combined with the comparison of the absorptance curves between the planar solar cells and the DCNs-decorated solar cells, it can be concluded that these DCNs structures are more meaningful for thin and especially ultra-thin film solar cells to reduce surface reflection, achieve absorption enhancement, passivate the semiconductor, and ultimately reduce production costs.
