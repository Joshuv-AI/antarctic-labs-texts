Unveiling the beam-wake interactions in plasma wakefield acceleration
Authors: Yang Wan, Sheroy Tata, Omri Seemann, Igor A Andriyash, Bo Peng, Tao Jing, Eitan Y Levine, Eyal Kroupp, Victor Malka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlasma wakefield accelerators, powered by high-current particle beams, promise compact and cost-effective sources of energetic leptons for future light sources and particle colliders. The interplay between the driving particle bunch and the plasma wakefields fundamentally determines accelerator performance. Gaining direct insight into these interactions is therefore key to realizing high-efficiency, high-quality particle acceleration. Using femtosecond ultrarelativistic electron microscopy, we characterize in situ both the wakefield and the driving bunch from a single-shot image with unprecedented detail. By systematically varying the driver charge, we capture the transitions from linear to nonlinear wakefield regimes. We also observe beam-wake coupling manifested through wakefield flattening and transverse dynamics of the driving bunch. These observations provide crucial real-time insights into the highly transient and complex dynamics of beam-plasma interactions, paving the way for precise control of the acceleration process.Subject terms: Imaging techniques, Plasma-based accelerators, Laser-produced plasmas

Introduction

Plasma is an ionized medium capable of sustaining extreme conditions, including high energy densities, intense electric and magnetic fields, and nonlinear relativistic waves1. Today, plasmas produced in laboratories are unique tools to explore astrophysical phenomena2, investigate thermonuclear fusion3, and deliver beams of ultra-relativistic particles4,5. The latter are now known as the plasma accelerators—devices in which energetic pulses from intense lasers or particle accelerators excite powerful plasma waves with electric fields reaching tens to hundreds of gigavolts per meter5–7. The accelerating gradients in these plasma-based systems far surpass those of conventional radio-frequency accelerators, offering transformative potential for next-generation linear colliders and coherent X-ray sources 8,9.

Plasma accelerators are commonly categorized into laser wakefield accelerators (LWFA)6 and plasma wakefield accelerators (PWFA)7, depending on whether the plasma waves are driven by laser pulses or particle bunches, respectively. LWFA typically employs laser systems ranging from a few terawatts to multiple petawatts and holds the promise of compact designs. In contrast, PWFA is generally pursued at large-scale accelerator facilities. Nevertheless, an electron bunch generated from LWFA, if strong enough, can also drive its own wake in the so-called hybrid LWFA-PWFA regime10,11. Major advantages of the particle bunch driver over the laser one are that plasma does not slow the driver down, and that the transverse fields in the plasma wake can keep the bunch focused over the long interaction distances, thus allowing for very high energy transfer efficiencies12.

As a particle bunch drives the plasma wave, its space-charge field shapes the wake, which in its turn acts on the beam through the wakefields. This interplay commonly manifests as beam loading and the associated flattening of the longitudinal plasma field that affects the acceleration13–15 and may also modulate the so-called betatron transverse motion of the particles, leading to the beam pinching, beam scalloping or plasma lensing16–18. In PWFA, this collective transverse feedback often gives rise to such deteriorating phenomena as beam hosing or beam-breakup instabilities, or it can produce additional transverse beam loading and bunch filamentation19–23. These highly transient, bidirectional effects can significantly impact the performance and stability of PWFA in ways that are challenging to predict or interpret from currently available measurements (for details, see the review22). With active development of PWFA, it has become essential to obtain direct, simultaneous experimental insights into the states of both the beam and the wake24.

Experimentally, information about the wakefield can be gathered through direct imaging of the plasma wave using optical25–28 or particle-based29–32 probes. Alternatively, fields can be mapped using witness particles in scanning33–35 or single-shot measurement setups14,36,37. In the most general case, imaging techniques become the primary option, as they provide the instantaneous and in situ visualizations of either the wake electromagnetic fields (via particle deflectometry) or the associated density structures (via optical interferometry or shadowgraphy). This work utilizes a specific type of particle deflectometry that we have recently developed and termed femtosecond ultrarelativistic electron microscopy (FREM)38. FREM employs an ultrashort, high-energy electron bunch generated by an auxiliary laser-plasma accelerator to probe the wakefield at nearly 90∘ angle. Although being challenging to implement, FREM provides detailed information about the fields within nonlinear plasma waves, offering unique insights that are not accessible through other methods. Previously, this technique has been used to image and characterize the linear patterns in a weak laser wakefield32, the rear electron sheath of a highly nonlinear laser wakefield29, and the envelope evolution of electron beams in plasma39.

In this work, we focus on the intrinsic beam-plasma interaction dynamics in the uncharted transitional regimes of PWFA. FREM has enabled us to investigate two key physical processes: first, the distinct transition from linear to nonlinear beam-driven plasma wave patterns as a function of the driver charge, a process central to PWFA but not previously visualized in such detail. The second is the strong transient interplay between the beam and its self-generated plasma wake in the nonlinear regime, directly revealing how the tail of the bunch loads and flattens the longitudinal wakefield, and how the transverse wakefield in turn focuses the tail. This study provides a direct, in-situ view of the beam-wake interaction that governs beam quality and stability of PWFA.

Results

Experimental platform for imaging beam-wake interactions

The LWFA-PWFA experiment was conducted using the HIGGINS dual 100 TW laser system at the Weizmann Institute of Science40. As shown in Fig. 1, Laser 1 with 0.6 J on-target energy and 30 fs duration was focused by a 2 m focal-length off-axis parabolic mirror (to a ~37 μm FWHM spot) into a 3 mm-long supersonic helium gas jet doped with 1% nitrogen (referred to as Jet 1). The peak plasma density, assuming full ionization, was approximately 8 × 1018 cm−3. The laser pulse underwent strong self-focusing and drove the plasma wake. The electron beam got injected into the plasma wake through the mechanism of ionization injection41,42 and was accelerated to relativistic energies. After sufficient propagation, as the laser pulse diffracted and depleted, the laser wakefield weakened, and the plasma wakefield driven by the accelerated electron bunch started to dominate, which is the central topic of this study. After exiting Jet 1, the driver electron bunch was then characterized by an energy spectrometer located around 850 mm downstream of the gas jet.

Experimental setup.

Laser 1 of 0.6 J, 30 fs was focused into a gaseous target (Jet 1) and accelerated an electron bunch through the wakefield launched by the laser pulse. After some propagation, the laser intensity became too weak, and the electron bunch started to drive its own wakefield. This was diagnosed by another electron bunch as a femtosecond probe generated by Laser 2 of 1.2 J, 30 fs in gas Jet 2. After crossing the beam-driven wakefield and gaining some momentum modulation, the probe bunch drifted a further 6 mm distance and developed into a corresponding density modulation that contains the key information of the investigated wakefield (see the raw probe image). After exiting the Jet 1, the electron driver was diagnosed by an energy spectrometer consisting of a dipole and a scintillator screen located 850 mm downstream of the gas jet.

To characterize the plasma wakefield, we have implemented FREM using a second 30 fs laser pulse with 1.2 J energy (referred to as Laser 2) that was focused into another supersonic gas jet (referred to as Jet 2) of the same mixture and generated a high-energy electron probe with a few-femtoseconds duration. Detailed characterization of the probe beam parameters can be found in the Methods. After exiting Jet 2, the probe bunch drifted in the vacuum for 10 cm to expand its spatial size into the millimeter scale, and then intercepted the plasma wakefield perpendicularly. The electromagnetic fields from both the driver bunch and the driven wakefield deflected the probe bunch. Following an additional 6 mm drift, these deflections evolved into periodic density variations, and were then captured by a 50 μm-thick Ce:YAG scintillator (protected by a 100 μm-thick stainless steel foil) together with a high-resolution imaging system.

Charge-dependent PWFA transition and wakefield reshaping

The plasma wakefield was imaged along the slowly varying low-density downramp, where the depleted or diffracted laser, though still being able to ionize the gas, was already too weak to drive a noticeable wakefield. This was verified by measuring a pure laser wakefield at the probed position, whose strength was much smaller than that of the beam-driven wakes (More details can be found in Supplementary Fig. 1 and also “Methods”). Figure 2 presents four representative shots obtained at different on-target laser energies ranging from approximately 280 to 550 mJ, showing the energy spectra of the laser-accelerated electrons in Fig. 2a and the corresponding FREM images taken at around 2 mm downstream of the gas jet center in Fig. 2b, where Δn/n0 represents the relative density modulation with Δn the absolute density variation and n0 the density background. The plasma density in the probed region was also estimated via a wavelet transform of the on-axis wakefield pattern of the FREM image, which yielded a decrease from 2.2 × 1017 cm−3 at the FREM image left boundary to around 1 × 1017 cm−3 at the driver position (see Supplementary Fig. 2). The spectrometer data indicates that the charge and maximum energy of electrons increase with laser energy. The dark structure that appears ahead of a periodic bright-dark pattern in FREM images indicates a typical particle beam-driven plasma wakefield 39. It is clearly seen that the intensity of the periodic structure is significantly enhanced with the increase of the driver's total charge. Additionally, one may note that for the stronger driver, the wakefield appears curved transversely, together with the slight elongation of the wakefield period, indicating a transition from linear to nonlinear PWFA regime.

To quantify the linear to nonlinear transition, we examined two representative FREM snapshots (1) and (4) from Fig. 2, as depicted in Fig. 3a, b, and reconstructed their field distributions. The front dark elliptical structure in probe density modulation is mainly produced by the space-charge fields of the drive bunch, and it can be shown to be directly correlated with the bunch’s transverse charge-density distribution σb39, i.e., Δn/n0 ∝ σb. Based on this understanding, both the drivers’ total charge and transverse dimensions can be quantitatively calculated from the FREM images. Assuming a bi-Gaussian distribution, the drive bunch in Fig. 3a is estimated to contain a charge of 21 ± 2 pC, with transverse root-mean-square (RMS) sizes of σx = 7.4 ± 0.6 μm (horizontal) and σy = 7.6 ± 0.3 μm (vertical). By comparison, the bunch in Fig. 3b is estimated to have a much higher charge of 128 ± 13 pC with slightly larger sizes σx = 9.3 ± 0.8 μm and σy = 8.1 ± 0.3 μm.

The Wakefield characterization was performed using a newly developed retrieval algorithm that incorporates differences in probe particle coordinates between object and image planes, offering improved accuracy over the earlier method43. We also filtered out minor contributions from the linear laser wakefield. More details about the PWFA field retrieval can be found in the Methods. The reconstructed longitudinal electric fields (Ez) are presented in Fig. 3c, d, with on-axis lineouts shown as blue solid curves in Fig. 3e, f. We have also plotted a series of vertical dashed lines in Fig. 3e, f to mark the positions of the Ez field peaks and valleys. Comparing the two figures, one can see that the left panel (Fig. 3e) reveals a sinusoidal pattern characteristic of a classic linear wave, whereas the right panel (Fig. 3f) displays a skewed field profile persisting over at least two periods, indicating the emergence of nonlinearity 34,44. The measured field strength in Fig. 3f is around 6.7 GV/m, well below the theoretical wavebreaking limit in cold plasma 45(~30 GV/m for the density of 1 × 1017 cm−3), suggesting a weakly nonlinear regime. We note that our retrieval algorithm cannot distinguish the probe electrons deflections caused by the driver bunch and the ones induced by the plasma wake, thus mistaking the combined pattern for the wakefield and generating a non-physical negative field at the front (blue dashed curves in Fig. 3e, f).

In Fig. 3f, the FREM measurement reveals that, aside from the characteristic sawtooth waveform, the electric field in the first period exhibits a significant flattening, which can be related to the phenomenon of beam loading13,15,46,47. In accelerator science, beam loading commonly signifies the reduction of the accelerating gradient induced by the charge of a slice on the subsequent slices. Flattening of the longitudinal field by the space-charge can be controlled by shaping the bunch current profile, and may be considered for improving the bunch energy spread. In our experiment, the continuous ionization injection is likely to produce a low-density tail of the bunch, also known as the dark current48, that can indeed load and reshape the field in the first wake period.

The observed plasma wave transition and indications of beam space-charge effects were reproduced numerically using the quasi-cylindrical pseudo-spectral particle-in-cell code FBPIC49 (more details can be found in the “Methods”). The case of a linear plasma wave presented in the left panel of Fig. 3 was reproduced using the 18 pC driver bunch, with 8 μm RMS radius, and 10 fs duration, and the simulated Ez field closely aligns with the experimental data (see the orange curve in Fig. 3e).

For the weakly nonlinear regime (right panels in Fig. 3), the driver charge was increased to 100 pC. In Fig. 4a, we show that such a driver expels around two-thirds of the plasma electrons and creates a dense rear spike, yielding a weakly nonlinear wave with a characteristic sawtooth profile of the longitudinal electric field. However, using the same bunch duration of 10 fs fails to reproduce the field flattening observed in the first wave period (blue curve in Fig. 3f). In order to reproduce the space-charge effect, we have positioned an additional 120 fs low-density electron bunch behind the short driver, mimicking the long tail dark current. Since the bunch tail in Fig. 3b is not directly visualized, its transverse size must be comparable to or even larger than that of the wakefield. We therefore set the trailing low-density bunch to have a wide transverse size. The resulting plasma density distribution is presented in Fig. 4b, and the corresponding Ez field is presented by the orange solid curve of Fig. 3f. One may see that, with the dark current, the plasma wake gets slightly extended and the associated Ez field becomes flatter, akin to the experimental observations. To ensure consistent comparison between the simulated cases and experimental observations, we have simulated the FREM measurement numerically, considering a high-energy electron probe propagating through the wakefield. The simulated FREM images and their on-axis lineouts for both configurations are shown in Fig. 4c–f. Comparing these cases, one may see that in the beam loaded case Fig. 4d, f, the intensity peak in the first wakefield period shifts backward with respect to Fig. 4c, e, aligning with the experimental data in Fig. 3b. Although indirectly, these observations support the interpretation that the occurrence of field flattening is caused by a long, wide tail of the drive bunch in FREM images. More details about the acceleration and FREM simulation setups can be found in the “Methods”.

Transverse focusing of the drive bunch tail

To see how the bunch-wake interaction develops, we have taken another measurement about 600 μm downstream from the previous observation point for the same laser parameters as considered in Fig. 3b. The experimental data, presented in Fig. 5a, reveals a thin, elongated structure confined within the first wave period, located at the region where a wide long bunch tail was indicated in Fig. 3b. This feature was reproduced in the multiple shots under identical conditions (see Supplementary Fig. 3), and can be attributed to the focusing of the long tail dark current produced by the plasma transverse wakefield. The dark current electrons are typically injected with a large transverse size, and therefore are strongly mismatched to the transverse wakefield, in particular in the observed transition regime. This mismatch results in the collective betatron oscillations of the transverse bunch envelope, eventually reaching high electron density on axis. As discussed above, in the relevant PWFA case of a long particle bunch, such strong focusing in the non-uniform field often leads to bunch pinching or scalloping17,21, and may potentially trigger deteriorating phenomena such as hosing or beam-breakup instabilities or produce additional transverse beam loading19–22.

The value represents the estimated probe density modulation caused only by the long tail bunch.

To test this interpretation, we simulated the driver propagating an additional 500 μm and generated a synthetic FREM image based on a configuration similar to that in Fig. 4b, adjusted with a slightly longer tail duration to match the experimental shot. The simulated electron density distribution and corresponding FREM image are shown in Fig. 5b. c, respectively, and they demonstrate good agreement with the experimental observations. In addition to the above simulation, starting with a pre-formed electron beam configuration outside the plasma, we have also performed a start-to-end simulation that includes the entire chain from laser propagating into plasma and exciting LWFA to its transition to a PWFA driven by the LWFA-generated electron bunch. The results are presented in the Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5. A large amount of dark-current electrons is continuously injected into the wake bucket along the rear-side downramp of the gas jet. As the wake bucket expands, many of these electrons, especially those near the tail, are injected off-axis with considerable displacement, forming a bunch with a large transverse width (within the green dashed box in S-Fig. 4). These electrons are not perfectly transversely phase-matched to the evolving wake. Owing to the strong focusing force inside the wake, they are first pushed transversely toward the axis, resulting in strong focusing of the bunch, and later move outward away from the axis, causing bunch re-expansion. Our two experimental snapshots seem to capture the initial bunch focusing process, and the later-time betatron oscillation could be an interesting topic that will be further investigated using a dedicated experimental design in the future.

Assuming cylindrical symmetry and luminal velocity, we also quantitatively analyzed the transverse modulation of the imaged long bunch tail (vertical dashed lineout in Fig. 5a), depicted in the pink shaded region of Fig. 5d. By distinguishing the wakefield and bunch space-charge contributions (see “Methods”), we derived the transverse profile of the bunch tail, illustrated in Fig. 5e. This profile conforms to a Gaussian distribution with an RMS width of 5.2 ± 0.1 μm, indicating noticeable width reduction compared to its prior state in Fig. 3b. The total length of the bunch tail is estimated to be around 60 μm. Furthermore, the dashed black line in Fig. 5d, e reveals that the bunch tail’s center is slightly offset from the wakefield’s propagation axis. This subtle offset of roughly 3 μm, captured by FREM, could arise from an off-axis injection of the bunch tail50, or the start of a beam-hosing instability19,20.

Discussion

In summary, we have experimentally investigated the transient dynamics of a particle-bunch-driven plasma wakefield. By applying the FREM technique, both the in situ information of the driver and the wakefield were obtained from a single-shot measurement. As the charge of the driver increased, we observed the transition from a linear to a weakly nonlinear plasma wave. Supported by PIC and synthetic-FREM simulations, we also captured the flattening of the wakefield induced by beam-tail loading and consequent transverse bunch focusing. These findings provide valuable quantitative insights into the complex interactions between ultrarelativistic electron beams and plasma. As such, they can be used to configure and guide the PWFA research at large-scale facilities. For example, the approach enables reliable reconstruction of highly nonlinear wakefield structures in the blowout regime driven by high-charge beams, as well as the identification of key processes in PWFA that are difficult to visualize in situ and in real time. These potentially include beam loading induced by a witness bunch, the development of hosing instability in a long drive bunch, and mismatches between the drive/witness bunch and the plasma wakefield that lead to bunch envelope oscillations. Given these capabilities, we believe FREM will make an important contribution to PWFA studies. We are actively implementing this technique directly for PWFA platforms at linac facilities, where greater flexibility in controlling drive and witness beam parameters is available. This will allow us to further advance this technique and access even more fascinating dynamics in beam-plasma physics, ultimately contributing to the optimization of accelerator performance.

Methods

Laser system

The HIGGINS dual 100 TW laser system at Weizmann Institute of Science 40 was utilized for this experiment, which delivers up to 7 J of laser energy at 1 Hz. After being split into two parts and compressed independently to around 30 fs duration, Laser 1 was focused by a 2 m-focal-length off-axis parabola (OAP) for the generation of a PWFA electron driver and Laser 2 was focused by a 1.5 m-focal-length OAP for generating a high-energy electron probe. In our experiment, with account of all losses, the maximum on-target laser energies for 1 and 2 were ~0.6 J and ~1.2 J, respectively.

Probe electron beam generation

The probe electron bunch was produced by focusing Laser 2 into a supersonic flow from a 3 mm diameter nozzle with a gaseous mixture of 99% helium and 1% nitrogen. Peak plasma density along the laser path was around 1.1 × 1019 cm−3 as measured by an off-line Mach-Zehnder interferometer and assuming complete ionization of both helium and nitrogen. Angular profile of the accelerated electrons was imaged with a Lanex scintillator 855 mm downstream of the gas jet, and their spectra were characterized right after this screen, using an energy spectrometer consisting of a 10 cm long magnetic dipole with 1 T induction and another DRZ screen. In the experiment, quasi-monoenergetic bunches were observed with a peak energy of 300 ± 33 MeV and a 50 ± 25 MeV FWHM energy spread. Their angular RMS divergences were 3.5 ± 1.3 mrad in the horizontal direction (laser polarization direction), and 2.8 ± 0.6 mrad in the vertical direction, respectively and the total charge above 200 MeV was more than 250 pC.

High-resolution image system

After crossing the PWFA, the probe profile was measured using a 50 μm thick Ce:YAG scintillating screen placed 6 mm away from the Jet 1. This screen was shielded from the residual laser light and betatron x-rays by a 100 μm thick stainless steel foil. The light from Ce:YAG was collected and transmitted by a long working-distance microscope objective (Mitutoyo 10X) and an 200 mm-focal length achromatic lens (Thorlabs) to a 16-bit sCMOS Hamamatsu camera. The spatial resolution of this imaging system is determined by the combined effects of the numerical aperture of the imaging system (evaluated via ZEMAX) and the scattering of electrons from the protection foil and YAG crystal (checked via Monte Carlo simulations)29, and the final spatial resolution after accounting all these effects, defined as the RMS size of point-spread function, was about 1 μm, which is sufficiently smaller than the measured beam sizes and also the observed bunch tail offset.

Particle-in-cell simulations

PIC simulations were performed to simulate the PWFA dynamics using the pseudo-spectral code FBPIC49 with a quasi-cylindrical geometry simulated with Nm = 2 azimuthal modes. The cylindrical simulation box was set to 400 μm in length and 200 μm in radius, with the mesh sizes of Δz = 200 nm and Δr = 500 nm. The plasma profile was set as pre-ionized plasma with a 150 μm up-ramp followed by a density down-ramp extracted from the estimation of plasma period based on experimental wakefield snapshots, with the peak density of 1.5 × 1017 cm−3. Two configurations were set for the PWFA electron driver. For the ultrashort bunch cases, the electron driver has a RMS pulse duration of 10 fs and transverse RMS sizes of 8 μm, containing a total charge of 18 pC for Fig. 3e and 100 pC for Fig. 4a. For the case of drive bunch with a long tail, we generated two bunches to model it, an ultrashort bunch with the same parameters as the previous case, and a second bunch with much longer pulse duration of 130 fs. The total charge of the second bunch was 100 pC, with transverse RMS sizes of 10 μm.

FREM simulations

In order to speed up the FREM simulation, we developed a dedicated code for tracking probe particles as they traverse plasma fields, eliminating the need to directly incorporate a probe bunch into the FBPIC simulation. The methodology is outlined below.

First, the complete electromagnetic field distribution to be probed is extracted from the PIC simulation and imported into the tracking code. The field’s propagation velocity can be set to any value; for simplicity, we adopt the speed of light in vacuum. Next, a probe electron bunch with predefined parameters is introduced, crossing the field at arbitrary angles. A Boris pusher is implemented to model the field’s deflection of the probe particles. This code is optimized for NVIDIA GPUs, achieving a computational speed at least five times faster than the full PIC simulation for equivalent configurations. Subsequently, the six-dimensional phase space information of all probe particles is extracted from the FREM simulation. This data is used to simulate a free-space drift over a specified distance, ultimately generating the desired density modulation map, referred to as the FREM image, for comparison with experimental results.

In our FREM simulations, the probe bunch was initialized with a peak energy of 300 MeV, a 20% FWHM energy spread, a pulse duration of 10 fs, a source size of 2 μm, and an angular divergence of 3 mrad, comprising a total of 2 × 107 macro-particles. The bunch propagated 10 cm in vacuum before interacting with the wakefield at a 90-degree angle. Following the interaction, it drifted an additional 7 mm, yielding the final FREM image.

FREM field reconstruction

Previously, an algorithm for reconstructing the wakefield structure was developed 43, based on a perturbation theory derived from the fluid continuity equation. However, this approach does not account for variations in particle coordinate and thus is mostly valid for very small density perturbations of the probe beam (i.e.,∣Δn/n0∣ ≪ 1). This means that it may fail under conditions of very large field strength or long drift distance after the probe interacts with the wakefield. In addition to these two models that describe the general wakefield shape, we have developed other models to explain specific features observed in FREM images under particular conditions, such as the bright rings observed in highly nonlinear wakefields in the blowout regime29, or the effects of the bunch space-charge fields on probe particles39.

LWFA contribution subtraction from retrieved fields

In our experiment, the retrieved field Etotal from each FREM image comprises both PWFA and LWFA components, with the LWFA contribution being minimal. To isolate the PWFA field, we developed a three-step procedure to subtract the LWFA contribution.

First, we gradually reduce the laser energy to the threshold where ionization injection is just suppressed. In our setup, this reference laser energy, Wref, is approximately 230 mJ. Second, we reconstruct the laser wakefield, determining its field strength, Eref, to be approximately 0.6 GV/m. Third, given that the LWFA at the probed position operates in a linear regime, its field strength scales proportionally with the drive laser energy. Using the pure LWFA case as a reference (0.6 GV/m for 230 mJ), we calculate the LWFA field strength, Elwfa, for each case in Fig. 3b by scaling Eref with the ratio of the applied laser energy to Wref. Finally, the PWFA field is derived using the expression Etotal × (1 − Elwfa/Etotal).

Characterization of the bunch long tail based on the FREM image

Assuming cylindrical symmetry and considering that the structure’s velocity approaches the speed of light, we can integrate the fields along the probe particles’ paths. Consequently, the FREM image represents a projection of the convolution of the structure’s longitudinal and transverse charge-density distributions. For a highly elongated structure, such as the first period in Fig. 5a, this projection closely approximates the long tail’s (z-r) slice profile. In Fig. 5d, the relative probe density modulations in the pink shaded region result from the superposition of the wakefield cavity and the bunch tail’s space-charge field, where the former focuses the probe electrons and the latter repels them contrarily. By assuming a constant wakefield contribution for simplicity (here we take the value 0.09) and subtracting it, we derive the modulation from experimental data, shown in Fig. 5e. Fitting this with a Gaussian function reveals that the bunch tail at this position has an RMS size of 5.2 ± 0.1 μm.
