Deciphering inter-organ communication: The multi-organ-bone axis in osteoporosis and emerging therapeutic strategies
Authors: Ruiming Wen, Haixia Wang, Songtao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoporosis has long been considered a disorder confined to bone tissue. Growing evidence now highlights its systemic origins, governed by intricate communication between multiple organs. The multi-organ-bone axis (MOBA) reframes osteoporosis pathogenesis as disrupted communication between bone and peripheral organs. Although dysfunction in organs such as the gut, adipose tissue, and nervous system has been linked to bone loss, a comprehensive integration of this axis and its therapeutic implications remain incomplete. Clarifying this multi-organ network is essential for devising innovative systemic treatment strategies.MethodsThis review synthesizes current evidence on the MOBA by conducting a comprehensive literature search in PubMed and Web of Science from inception up to March 2025. Search terms included combinations of “osteoporosis,” “multi-organ bone axis,” “gut-bone axis,” “adipose-bone axis,” “neuro-bone axis,” “kidney-bone axis,” “muscle-bone axis,” “probiotics,” “prebiotics,” “natural products,” “small molecule inhibitors,” and “exercise.” Eligible studies included original research articles, clinical trials, and high-quality reviews published in English that addressed inter-organ communication in bone metabolism.ResultsThe MOBA framework indicates that bone homeostasis is governed by dynamic interactions among the gut, adipose tissue, nervous system, kidney and skeletal muscle. Dysfunction in these organs perturbs hormonal, inflammatory, immune and neural signaling, thereby driving bone loss through convergent mechanisms. On the basis of this framework, emerging therapeutic strategies can be grouped into five categories: microbiota-directed interventions, natural products, small-molecule inhibitors or agonists, synthetic compounds and exercise. Rather than acting exclusively on bone cells, these approaches aim to restore systemic homeostasis by reprogramming organ crosstalk.ConclusionsThe MOBA model offers an integrative framework that conceptualizes osteoporosis as a systemic disorder involving disrupted inter-organ communication. While axis-targeted interventions show preclinical promise, their clinical translation is challenged by mechanistic complexity, model limitations, and insufficient clinical validation. Future research should map precise inter-organ circuits in humans and validate these strategies in patient populations to advance the development of effective systemic osteoporosis therapies.The translational potential of this article is as followsThis Review positions osteoporosis as a systemic disease driven by disordered inter-organ communication. By outlining the MOBA framework and classifying emerging multi-organ-targeted interventions, it shifts the therapeutic focus from bone-centred treatment to system-level recalibration, offering a conceptual basis for more effective prevention and treatment strategies.Keywords: Bone homeostasis, Inter-organ communication, Multi-organ-bone axis, Osteoporosis, Targeted therapy, Therapeutic strategies

Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) is a metabolic bone disease characterized by low bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture, resulting in an increased risk of fracture. Its fundamental pathology lies in an imbalance in bone remodeling, in which osteoclast (OC)-mediated bone resorption exceeds osteoblast (OB)-driven bone formation [[1], [2], [3]]. Traditionally, research and therapeutic development in OP have followed a bone-centric paradigm focused on dysregulation of local bone metabolic signaling pathways [4]. Although this framework has been instrumental in defining the cellular and molecular basis of skeletal remodeling, it does not fully explain why OP frequently coexists with disorders of other organs, nor does it adequately account for secondary OP, in which bone loss arises from dysfunction outside the skeleton.

Over the past two decades, accumulating evidence has revealed that bone metabolism is regulated by an integrated network of systemic signals. Early studies identified individual organ-bone axes, each operating through distinct mediators. For instance, the gut-bone axis primarily modulates bone resorption through microbiota-mediated immune regulation [5]; the adipose-bone axis couples energy metabolism to bone formation via adipokines [6,7]; the neuro-bone axis mediates bidirectional regulation of bone remodeling through sympathetic and sensory nerves [8,9]; the kidney-bone axis regulates calcium-phosphate homeostasis and bone mineralization through the fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23)-Klotho-vitamin D axis [10]; the muscle-bone axis promotes bone formation via myokines [11]. In recent years, advances in this field have led to the emergence of a new paradigm: the multi-organ-bone axis (MOBA) [12]. This framework posits that bone homeostasis is sustained by continuous communication between the skeleton and distant organs, forming a complex regulatory network integrating hormonal signaling, immune-inflammatory mediators, and neuro-metabolic coupling. Dysregulation at any level can lead to OP [13,14]. Subsequent studies have shown that these axes do not operate in isolation but interact through interconnected metabolic, immune, and neuroendocrine networks, collectively forming a systemic regulatory network governing bone homeostasis [15].

In this Review, we focus on five core organ systems: the intestine, adipose tissue, nervous system, kidney, and skeletal muscle (Fig. 1). These were selected because they are supported by the strongest mechanistic, translational, and clinical evidence linking organ dysfunction to altered bone metabolism and osteoporotic outcomes. Other organs, including the liver, also contribute to skeletal homeostasis through pathways such as vitamin D metabolism, bile acid signaling, and inflammatory regulation [16,17]. However, compared with the five core systems emphasized here, their integration into a unified translational framework for OP remains less well defined. Our intention is therefore not to exclude these organs, but to center the discussion on the axes for which evidence is currently most mature and conceptually cohesive. Although this review focuses primarily on OP, related conditions such as obesity-associated bone loss, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) bone impairment, chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD) and osteosarcopenia are discussed as representative models of systemic bone disorders that share overlapping mechanisms of multi-organ regulation. Building on this framework, therapeutic strategies for OP are also evolving. Rather than exclusively targeting bone cells or isolated skeletal pathways, emerging interventions increasingly aim to restore systemic homeostasis by modulating organ crosstalk. These include microbiota-directed approaches, natural products, small-molecule inhibitors or agonists, synthetic compounds, and exercise-based interventions [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. At the same time, clinical translation remains constrained by challenges in mechanistic validation, evidence integration, biomarker development, patient stratification, and safety assessment.

The MOBA regulatory network in osteoporosis. The skeleton and peripheral organs regulate bone homeostasis through complex bidirectional communication. Metabolites derived from the GM, adipokines and energy metabolism signals secreted by adipose tissue, regulatory inputs from sympathetic and sensory nerves, the renal FGF23-Klotho-vitamin D axis, and myokines produced by muscle collectively maintain skeletal balance. However, dysfunction in any single organ can disrupt this intricate network, tipping the balance in favor of bone resorption over formation. This imbalance leads to bone loss, deterioration of bone microarchitecture, and increased fracture risk.

Accordingly, this Review has four aims: (1) to define the conceptual basis of MOBA and its theoretical evolution from earlier organ-bone axis models; (2) to summarize the cross-cutting mechanisms and organ-specific pathological alterations that drive OP within this framework; (3) to evaluate emerging MOBA-targeted therapeutic strategies and their translational maturity; (4) to identify the major obstacles and future directions for bringing this systems-based perspective into clinical practice. By reframing OP as a disorder of disrupted inter-organ communication rather than a purely skeletal disease, the MOBA framework provides a more integrative foundation for understanding pathogenesis and developing next-generation therapies.

Pathophysiological foundations of MOBA

The core regulatory architecture of MOBA comprises three cross-cutting layers: hormonal signaling, immune-inflammatory mediators, and neuro-metabolic coupling, which operate across multiple organs to coordinate bone homeostasis under physiological conditions. Their dysregulation constitutes the common pathogenic basis for the organ-specific pathologies.

Hormonal signaling and inter-organ communication

Hormonal signaling constitutes the foundational regulatory layer of the MOBA framework. A fundamental principle of this system is that bone tissue is not merely a passive recipient of signals but functions as an endocrine organ, actively participating in bidirectional feedback loops with distant organs through the secretion of osteocrine factors [25]. For instance, OB-derived osteocalcin (OCN) can modulate pancreatic insulin secretion and stimulate adiponectin (ADPN) release from adipose tissue, thereby establishing a “bone-pancreas-adipose” metabolic axis [26]. Wang et al. demonstrated that the neuropeptide Y (NPY) secreted by osteocyte precursors exerts dual regulatory functions. It mediates glucocorticoid (GC)-induced impairment of mineral apposition and marrow adipogenesis via post-translational modification of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) [27], and targets the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus to suppress sympathetic tone, thereby modulating bone formation and illustrating the bidirectional nature of the neuro-bone axis [28]. Furthermore, the kidney-derived hormone 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 (1.25(OH)2D3) serves as a master regulator of calcium-phosphate homeostasis. Substantial evidence indicates that 1.25(OH)2D3, via its nuclear receptor (VDR), directly regulates the expression of genes critical for OB differentiation and engages in a negative feedback loop with parathyroid hormone (PTH) [29,30]. Notably, while early clinical studies suggested that it exerts a direct stimulatory effect on OB function and OCN production without apparent direct effects on OC activity [31], subsequent studies have revealed that its regulation of OC activity is primarily mediated indirectly through OB-derived receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) [32], with direct VDR signaling in OC precursors also reported under certain conditions [29,30]. This dichotomy underscores the multifaceted nature of vitamin D signaling in bone remodeling.

The regulatory complexity is further enriched by the intricate crosstalk between hormones and neuropeptides, which adds another dimension to the neuro-bone axis. Leptin serves as a prime example. Elefteriou et al. confirmed that leptin exerts an inhibitory effect on OB via the hypothalamic-sympathetic nervous pathway, a process strictly dependent on the structural integrity of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) [33]. By contrast, Fu et al. showed that leptin can also rely on sensory nerve signaling to directly accelerate OB proliferation and bone formation by upregulating activator protein-1 (AP-1) expression and promoting Cyclin D1 synthesis [34]. These opposing effects underscore the complex role of leptin in the context-dependent regulation of bone formation. The concerted actions of these hormonal signals not only reveal the extensive cross-system communication between bone and organs such as the pancreas, adipose tissue, nerves, and kidneys, but also underscore the foundational role of the hormonal network in maintaining MOBA homeostasis.

Immune-inflammatory mediators and osteoimmune crosstalk

Immune-inflammatory mediators function as the dynamic response layer of the MOBA, playing a central role in mediating the initiation and progression of cascading bone loss under pathological conditions [35,36]. Specifically, functional imbalance in T-cell subsets constitutes a core element in inflammatory bone destruction. The seminal study by Takayanagi et al. first demonstrated that interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secreted by T cells inhibits OC differentiation by interfering with RANKL signaling [37]. However, it is interleukin-17 A (IL-17 A), produced by T helper 17 (Th17) cells, that acts as the pivotal driver of pathological bone erosion, exerting potent bone-destructive effects particularly in autoimmune diseases like rheumatoid arthritis [38,39]. Concurrently, Zhao et al. revealed that tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) not only acts directly on OC and their precursors but also exhibits a significant synergistic effect with RANKL, collectively amplifying the bone resorption signal [40]. Conflicting findings regarding the roles of key signaling molecules such as IL-17 A, TNF-α, and serotonin indicate that their functions are not unidirectional but rather depend on concentration, cellular context, and the presence of co-factors.

Mechanistically, within the chronic inflammatory microenvironment, macrophage-derived TNF-α activates the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway, inducing stromal cells to highly express RANKL while suppressing osteoprotegerin (OPG) production, thereby disrupting the bone remodeling balance [41]. The RANKL/OPG axis serves as a trans-organ regulatory hub, with its influence extending far beyond the local bone milieu. In addition to immune-cell-derived signals, extra-skeletal sources of RANKL may also contribute to systemic bone loss under specific pathological conditions [42,43]. Conversely, semaphorin 3 A, secreted by bone stromal cells, functions as an immunomodulatory factor that simultaneously inhibits OC fusion and promotes regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation, demonstrating dual osteoprotective and immunoregulatory effects in postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMOP) models [44]. Notably, research on the mechanisms by which inflammation suppresses bone formation remains relatively limited. In normal coupled bone remodeling, enhanced bone resorption is accompanied by the recruitment of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) and their differentiation into OB. This process, however, is significantly impaired in the presence of inflammatory cytokines [45], suggesting that inflammation exacerbates bone metabolic imbalance by inhibiting osteogenic differentiation. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms of action of inflammatory cytokines on MSCs, mature OB, and osteocyte warrant further investigation, indicating that future therapeutic strategies for PMOP should incorporate the modulation of underlying inflammation.

Neuro-metabolic coupling and energy regulation

Neuro-metabolic coupling serves as a critical regulatory node within the MOBA, enabling rapid response and adaptation to energy metabolism. Its dysregulation can disrupt bone homeostasis [46]. Hanyu et al. demonstrated that norepinephrine (NE) released from sympathetic nerve terminals suppresses bone formation and enhances bone resorption by activating the β2-adrenergic receptor (β2-AR) and downstream cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)/protein kinase A (PKA) signaling in osteocytes, constituting a key molecular mechanism of stress-induced OP [47]. β-Sitosterol restores neuro-skeletal homeostasis by boosting 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT)-mediated suppression of SNS activity, thereby improving bone health [48]. However, the role of 5-HT in bone metabolism remains debated: brain-derived serotonin promotes osteoblast proliferation, whereas duodenal-derived serotonin suppresses it, suggesting compartment-specific and context-dependent effects [49]. These discrepancies underscore the complexity of neuro-skeletal crosstalk and highlight the need for further research to resolve these contradictions. Conversely, the mechanical microenvironment can activate Piezo2/voltage-gated calcium channel (VGCC) ion channels, mediating calcium influx in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons. This, in turn, induces the release of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) from sensory nerves via the PI3K-AKT/RAS pathway, thereby enhancing the osteogenic differentiation potential of bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) [50]. This bidirectional neural regulation of bone metabolism illustrates the intricate integration of neural and metabolic signals.

At the metabolic level, Tang et al. showed that the loss of Kindlin-2 in adipose tissue accelerates fatty acid synthase (FAS) degradation, reduces PPARγ activation and subsequent fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4) expression, thereby improving insulin sensitivity and indirectly increasing bone mass [51]. Conversely, suppression of the FNDC5-Irisin axis in diabetic states disrupts energy transfer along the muscle-bone axis, contributing to secondary OP [22]. These neuro-metabolic factors collectively maintain the dynamic equilibrium of the MOBA through multiple mechanisms.

Organ-specific pathological alterations and cascading effects in MOBA dysregulation

Dysregulation of the MOBA originates from organ-specific pathological insults. These alterations disrupt the cross-organ regulatory balance of bone homeostasis by modifying hormonal secretion, the immune microenvironment, and metabolic signaling [52,53]. In contrast to the traditional view, organ pathologies in OP do not exist in isolation. Clinically, multi-organ diseases frequently coexist with OP, supporting the notion that systemic conditions converge to influence skeletal health (Table 1). Rather, they exert cascading and amplified effects through shared molecular carriers: compromised intestinal barrier integrity triggers systemic inflammation, adipose tissue expansion potentiates sympathetic output, and kidney metabolic dysfunction leads to secondary calcium-phosphate imbalance. These disparate pathological streams ultimately converge within the bone microenvironment, causing irreversible dysregulation of the OB-OC coupling [[54], [55], [56]]. The following sections will systematically elaborate on the pathologies of the five core organs and their cascading effects within the MOBA network.

Intestinal pathology

Clinical evidence

The intestine serves as a pivotal hub within the MOBA network, where its structural and functional integrity is essential for maintaining bone homeostasis. Emerging evidence indicates that structural or organic lesions can directly compromise the absorption of critical nutrients. A meta-analysis by Oh et al. confirmed that anatomical alterations following gastrectomy significantly reduce bone mineral density (BMD), an effect attributed to impaired absorption of calcium and vitamin D [58]. In addition, chronic intestinal inflammation directly disrupts bone remodeling balance through the release of inflammatory mediators. Studies have consistently demonstrated that in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), the persistent inflammatory cascade activates OC while simultaneously inhibiting OB function, thereby driving progressive bone loss [57]. In parallel, studies in IBD mouse models have shown that modulating calcium-phosphorus balance and altering the abundance of specific bacterial taxa can ameliorate IBD-associated bone metabolic abnormalities [54]. Beyond IBD, other intestinal disorders also contribute to skeletal deterioration. A cross-sectional study reported that individuals with celiac disease (CD) face a higher risk of developing lumbar OP in middle age [59], and dietary gluten withdrawal enhances calcium and vitamin D absorption, thereby improving bone metabolism [60]. Furthermore, patients with gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (GEP-NETs) exhibit evidence of increased susceptibility to fragility fractures at diagnosis, often accompanied by vitamin D deficiency [61].

Preclinical evidence

Intestinal pathology is an important driver of OP through multiple mechanisms involving the “microbiota-barrier-immunity-metabolic axis”, encompassing gut microbiota (GM) ecological imbalance, intestinal barrier damage, disruption of mucosal immunity, and alterations in endocrine and metabolic signaling [81]. Clarke et al. found that propionate, produced by GM fermentation, can suppress growth hormone (GH) synthesis in the pituitary gland via the GPR41/43-cAMP signaling axis, thereby influencing the hormonal regulation of bone metabolism [82]. Li et al. demonstrated that butyrate derived from the GM promotes the expansion of bone marrow regulatory T cells via GPR43-dependent signaling in dendritic cells and GPR43-independent pathways in T cells. This, in turn, stimulates CD8+ T cells to produce Wnt10 b, thereby activating Wnt-dependent bone formation. Notably, this mechanism is essential for the anabolic effects of parathyroid hormone [83]. Beyond their endocrine effects, gut microbial metabolites also shape the local immune landscape. For instance, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) modulate the composition and function of intestinal Tregs, which in turn attenuate inflammatory bone loss in PMOP [84]. Moreover, pathogenic CD4+ T cells primed in gut-associated lymphoid tissues can migrate to the bone marrow and promote osteoclastogenesis through IL-4 signaling, establishing a direct link between intestinal mucosal immunity and skeletal homeostasis [85]. Beyond SCFAs, GM-derived metabolites such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and bile acids also contribute to bone metabolism. In ovariectomized (OVX) mice, gut dysbiosis characterized by an increased Firmicutes: Bacteroidetes ratio leads to elevated serum LPS levels, which promote systemic inflammation and bone loss. Notably, antibiotic-induced GM depletion reverses OVX-induced bone loss, whereas transplantation of OVX-adapted microbiota recapitulates the osteoporotic phenotype. Mechanistically, the G-protein-coupled bile acid receptor (TGR5) plays a critical role in mediating these effects [86]. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) inhibits excessive osteoclastogenesis and prevents ovariectomy-induced bone loss, an effect mediated by correcting GM dysbiosis, improving SCFAs levels, and optimizing intestinal permeability [87]. These findings highlight the importance of the GM-bile acid-TGR5 signaling axis in postmenopausal OP and establish GM metabolites as key signaling molecules that directly participate in the precise regulation of bone metabolism through both immune and endocrine pathways.

Adipose pathology

Clinical evidence

Adipose tissue is not merely an energy storage organ but also an active endocrine organ. Its dysfunction contributes to abnormal bone metabolism through multiple pathways, including adipokine imbalance, chronic low-grade inflammation, aberrant visceral fat accumulation, and remodeling of bone marrow adiposity [88]. Clinical studies have shown that, in postmenopausal women, higher circulating ADPN levels and lower leptin levels are each independently associated with the risk of osteoporotic fractures at multiple skeletal sites [62]. Notably, the combined assessment of IL-6 and leptin may serve as a serum biomarker that improves fracture risk prediction in postmenopausal women with low bone mass [63], supporting a direct regulatory role for adipose endocrine dysfunction in bone homeostasis. Obesity is not invariably protective for the skeleton. Among overweight or obese postmenopausal women, a higher baseline body mass index (BMI) was associated with an increased risk of incident morphometric vertebral fractures [64]. This finding suggests that the so-called obesity paradox is more appropriately interpreted as impaired bone quality in the setting of increased body weight, rather than as a simple gain in BMD. Consistent with this view, an inverse association between visceral adiposity and whole body BMD has been reported, indicating that abnormal fat distribution, rather than body weight itself, is a key determinant of skeletal fragility [65]. Increased bone marrow adiposity also has clear clinical relevance. A higher bone marrow fat (BMF) fraction was closely associated with lower BMD and was also significantly associated with an increased risk of major osteoporotic fractures, hip fractures, and vertebral fractures [66]. Thus, BMF infiltration is a key local mechanism linking adipose pathology to impaired bone quality. Prospective cohort studies have shown that, within 6 to 12 months after sleeve gastrectomy, patients may experience declines in bone mass, bone microarchitecture, and bone strength, with more pronounced effects in postmenopausal women [67], suggesting that rapid weight loss and adipose remodeling may accelerate bone loss.

Preclinical evidence

Adipose tissue pathology influences bone homeostasis through a metabolically inflamed vicious cycle, primarily involving adipocyte hypertrophy, dysregulated adipokine secretion, and bone marrow adipose infiltration. This pathological triad constitutes the foundation of obesity-associated OP [89]. Specifically, De et al. found that abnormal expansion of adipose tissue volume not only exacerbates local hypoxia but also prompts adipocytes to secrete a spectrum of adipokines and inflammatory mediators, thereby disrupting systemic metabolic balance and setting the stage for bone metabolic disorders [90]. Importantly, Wolf et al. discovered that in obesity, although leptin secretion increases, leptin receptor resistance prevents it from normally promoting bone formation; instead, it inhibits bone anabolism via central nervous system pathways [91]. Concurrently, Rajput et al. confirmed that reduced ADPN levels diminish its protective effects on bone metabolism [92], whereas Shang et al. demonstrated that resistin promotes bone repair and improves bone formation by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis [93]. Another adipose-derived cytokine, omentin-1, exhibits inverse correlation with obesity and exerts protective effects on both the vasculature and skeleton. Mechanistically, omentin-1 modulates the RANKL/OPG balance in both vascular smooth muscle cells and OB, promoting OPG expression while suppressing RANKL production through activation of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. Blockade of this pathway abolishes the effects of omentin-1. In vivo, adenovirus-mediated overexpression of omentin-1 attenuates arterial calcification and ameliorates bone loss in OPG−/− mice, indicating a dual protective role in the vasculature and skeleton. These findings suggest that omentin-1 ameliorates bone loss through regulation of the RANK signaling pathway, further expanding the repertoire of adipose-derived factors that coordinate bone homeostasis [94]. These findings underscore the critical role of adipose tissue as an energy reservoir that actively participates in the regulation of bone marrow niche function and skeletal integrity. Furthermore, infiltrating adipocytes release substances such as fatty acids that disrupt the skeletal microenvironment, thereby suppressing bone matrix mineralization and trabecular formation [11,95]. Together, pathological alterations in adipose tissue—namely chronic inflammation triggered by adipocyte hypertrophy, signaling disruption caused by dysregulated adipokine secretion, and direct inhibitory effects resulting from bone marrow adipose infiltration, collectively constitute the intrinsic driving factors for the high incidence of OP in the obese population.

Neural pathology

Clinical evidence

Recent clinical studies have provided increasingly clear support for a link between neurological pathology and OP, indicating that neurodegenerative disorders, cerebrovascular events, and spinal cord injury not only increase the risk of falls but also exacerbate skeletal fragility through reduced mobility, regional disuse, and disturbed neural regulation [69,96]. In the context of neurodegenerative disease, a nationwide retrospective cohort study involving 10,333 patients with Parkinson's disease (PD) showed that the overall fracture risk was significantly higher than that in matched controls, with a linear increase according to disease severity; among all fracture types, the rise in hip fracture risk was most pronounced [68]. These findings suggest that deterioration of neurological function is itself an important determinant of skeletal fragility. In cerebrovascular disease, a prospective study found that patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) or transient ischemic attack (TIA) experienced a markedly increased incidence of fractures within one year after the event, and post-stroke fractures were significantly associated with higher mortality, loss of walking ability, and poorer quality of life [69]. About 8.4% of ischemic stroke patients aged 50 and above experienced low-trauma fractures within 12 months, and the FRAC stroke score had good predictive performance for post-stroke fracture risk [70], indicating substantial value for early risk stratification and intervention after neurological injury. In the setting of more severe central nervous system injury, patients with acute traumatic spinal cord injury (t-SCI) continued to show rapid declines in BMD at the hip and key periarticular regions around the knee despite early treatment with zoledronic acid [71], highlighting the rapid progression and limited reversibility of spinal cord injury-related bone loss.

Preclinical evidence

The neuro-bone axis maintains bone homeostasis through bidirectional regulation by sympathetic and sensory nerves. Abnormal elevation in sympathetic nervous activity has been identified as a core element mediating bone metabolic disorders. Notably, sympathetic overactivation exerts its effects via NE release from nerve terminals, which acts on β2-ARs on bone cell surfaces, subsequently modulating downstream signaling networks. This mechanism constitutes a key pathophysiological basis for stress-induced and metabolic bone diseases [97]. Specifically, dysregulation of neural pathways contributes to the pathological progression of OP through cascading effects. The core mechanism involves a bidirectional imbalance between sympathetic and sensory nerves, leading to persistent bone loss [98]. Sensory nerves primarily exert protective roles in maintaining bone homeostasis. For instance, Chen et al. reported that calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) secreted from these nerves simultaneously accelerates bone angiogenesis and bone formation in vivo and in vitro [99]. Furthermore, integration dysfunction in higher central nuclei, such as the hypothalamus, can trigger secretion imbalances of critical hormones including PTH and GH, thereby indirectly disrupting the dynamic balance of bone remodeling [100,101]. The complexity of the neural regulatory network is further illustrated by recent findings at both peripheral and central levels. Chen et al. revealed that diminished cholinergic signaling and reduced secretion of the neurotrophic factor neurturin (NRTN) in the sympathetic nerves of OVX mice are closely associated with bone loss. Exogenous NRTN was shown to dose-dependently promote bone formation and BMSCs osteogenic differentiation, thereby reversing this pathological process [102]. Meanwhile, GABAergic neural circuits in the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) have been implicated in the regulation of chronic stress-induced bone loss [103]. Collectively, these abnormalities, acting in concert through the neuro-endocrine network, significantly increase the risk and severity of OP.

Kidney pathology

Clinical evidence

The clinical link between renal pathology and OP has been further strengthened by recent original studies, indicating that declining kidney function not only disrupts calcium and phosphate homeostasis and acid-base balance, but is also frequently accompanied by secondary hyperparathyroidism and abnormal bone turnover, which together promote skeletal fragility [72]. In patients with CKD, progressive deterioration of renal function is closely associated with an increased risk of hip and vertebral fractures, whereas higher parathyroid hormone levels, lower serum calcium, and lower bicarbonate levels are each independently associated with fracture risk [72], indicating that CKD-MBD related metabolic disturbances are key clinical drivers of skeletal fragility. Recent studies have further shown that early changes in BMD after kidney transplantation are markedly heterogeneous, and that persistent elevation or delayed decline of bone turnover markers is associated with ongoing bone loss [73], suggesting that a high bone turnover state is a major determinant of post-transplant bone disease. Changes in BMD after transplantation also show clear site specificity, with continued glucocorticoid exposure being independently associated with bone loss, whereas glucocorticoid withdrawal is associated with improvement in BMD at selected skeletal sites [74]. These findings indicate that post-transplant bone disease is not only persistent but also clinically modifiable. Recent high-quality cohort studies have additionally shown that denosumab treatment is associated with a substantially increased risk of severe hypocalcaemia, underscoring the need to carefully evaluate disturbances in mineral metabolism and drug safety when selecting OP therapies in the setting of advanced renal insufficiency [75].

Preclinical evidence

The kidney serves as the pivotal regulator of calcium-phosphate metabolism and bone mineralization balance within the MOBA network. Its functional impairment primarily drives the disruption of bone metabolic homeostasis through dysregulation of the FGF23-Klotho-Vitamin D axis and subsequent mineral metabolism disorders, constituting a key etiology for the characteristic bone phenotype in CKD-MBD [104]. Specifically, kidney pathology contributes to OP through the following mechanisms: Impaired kidney function directly leads to phosphate excretion deficiency, causing hyperphosphatemia and compensatory hypocalcemia. To maintain calcium homeostasis, the body is compelled to mobilize skeletal calcium stores, thereby directly accelerating bone loss [105]. Secondly, decreased kidney 1α-hydroxylase activity results in severely insufficient synthesis of 1.25(OH)2D3. This not only compromises intestinal calcium absorption, further exacerbating negative calcium balance, but also attenuates the direct stimulatory effect of vitamin D on bone formation [106]. Subsequently, the combined state of hypocalcemia and low 1.25(OH)2D3 triggers secondary hyperparathyroidism. Persistently elevated PTH excessively stimulates OC activity, leading to enhanced bone resorption and irreversible deterioration of bone microstructure [107].

Other factors also contribute to bone loss. Metabolic acidosis induced by kidney disease promotes bone calcium mobilization to buffer the acidic environment. Certain therapeutic agents interfere with calcium absorption and excretion. Abnormal changes in cytokine levels (such as transforming growth factor-beta and IGF-1) following renal impairment collectively constitute multiple driving factors for bone loss. These interconnected pathways significantly elevate the prevalence of OP and fracture risk in CKD patients [[108], [109], [110]]. Beyond these classical pathways, emerging evidence has revealed additional molecular mechanisms by which the kidney-bone axis regulates skeletal homeostasis. Klotho, a hormone primarily secreted by the kidney, participates in the regulation of mineral metabolism by influencing the synthesis of FGF23 within bone [10]. The FGF23-Klotho complex directly modulates osteocyte function, thereby regulating bone remodeling processes and controlling the progression of bone mineralization [111]. In the context of renal osteodystrophy, specific extracellular vesicle-derived microRNAs play pivotal roles in promoting inflammation and OC differentiation [112]. Additionally, chronic renal insufficiency leads to elevated serum phosphate levels, which in turn suppress OC differentiation and development [113]. Notably, a soluble form of Klotho (sKlotho) exists in the circulation, generated by proteolytic cleavage of the membrane-bound Klotho extracellular domain. This soluble variant enables Klotho to function as a circulating endocrine factor as well as a local autocrine or paracrine regulator [114], further expanding the repertoire of kidney-derived signals that coordinate bone homeostasis.

Skeletal muscle pathology

Clinical evidence

Extensive evidence indicates that skeletal muscle pathology, including sarcopenia, reduced muscle strength, impaired physical performance, and pathological intramuscular fat infiltration, collectively contributes to skeletal fragility [[76], [77], [78]]. Previous studies have shown that both presarcopenia and sarcopenia are associated with increased risks of overall fractures and major osteoporotic fractures, and that definitions of sarcopenia based on muscle strength and gait speed predict fracture risk more effectively than those based solely on muscle mass, indicating that impaired muscle function has more direct clinical relevance to skeletal fragility [76,77]. In postmenopausal women, higher appendicular lean mass index and greater handgrip strength are associated with more favorable cortical area and trabecular volumetric BMD, whereas in those with T2DM, sarcopenia or sarcopenic obesity is accompanied by deterioration of both cortical and trabecular microarchitecture even in the absence of an obvious reduction in areal BMD, suggesting that skeletal muscle pathology may increase fracture susceptibility primarily through impairment of bone quality rather than through a simple reduction in BMD [78]. Notably, the pathological significance of intramuscular fat infiltration has also received clinical support. In older women with hip fractures, lower muscle density and greater fat infiltration in the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus are associated with an increased risk of subsequent hip fracture. In patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures, fatty infiltration of the paraspinal muscles has likewise been identified as an independent predictor of recurrent vertebral fracture, suggesting that myosteatosis may persistently impair bone quality by weakening local mechanical support and stability [79,80]. Taken together, skeletal muscle pathology contributes to the onset and progression of OP through multiple pathways, including reduced muscle strength, insufficient mechanical loading, deterioration of bone microarchitecture, and intramuscular fat infiltration.

Preclinical evidence

Skeletal muscle serves as a critical hub integrating mechanical stimuli and biochemical signaling. Its pathological alterations disrupt bone homeostasis through dual “biomechanical-endocrine” pathways, constituting a core mechanism underlying the high prevalence of osteosarcopenia (SOPS) [115]. Specifically, as a mechanosensitive tissue, bone exhibits suppressed OB activity under conditions of insufficient mechanical loading. This results in a deceleration of bone formation, ultimately leading to progressive bone loss over time [116]. Furthermore, dysregulation of muscle endocrine function exacerbates bone metabolic imbalance. Under physiological conditions, contracting skeletal muscle secretes various myokines that effectively promote OB proliferation and differentiation. In pathological states, however, the secretion of beneficial myokines is reduced, leading to inadequate activation of osteogenic signaling pathways. This creates a relative enhancement of OC activity, ultimately disrupting the OB-OC coupling balance [117,118].

Additionally, pathological adipose tissue infiltration into muscle impairs skeletal health through a dual mechanism. On one hand, adipocytes displace functional myocytes, further diminishing mechanical stimulation derived from muscle. On the other hand, the infiltrating adipose tissue itself secretes adipokines such as leptin and TNF-α, which directly disrupt osteocyte function, promote OC generation, and inhibit OB activity [11]. Collectively, these pathological changes—diminished mechanical stimulation, dysregulated myokine secretion, and adipose infiltration—synergistically impact bone metabolism through combined biomechanical and endocrine pathways, serving as key drivers in the pathogenesis of SOPS. Large-scale epidemiological evidence further confirms that men with pre-sarcopenia and women with sarcopenia exhibit a significantly elevated risk of developing OP [119]. This clinically substantiates the etiological link between muscle-bone axis dysfunction and abnormal bone metabolism.

Notably, recent evidence suggests that musculoskeletal degeneration occurs earlier than many other organ systems during aging, positioning the musculoskeletal system as a potential driver of systemic aging [120]. Beyond its local effects, bone deterioration may contribute to dysfunction in distant organs through endocrine signals. For instance, osteocalcin secreted by OB regulates insulin sensitivity and energy metabolism in the pancreas and adipose tissue [121], while FGF23 derived from osteocyte modulates renal phosphate handling and cardiovascular function [122]. Conversely, age-related muscle wasting (sarcopenia) impairs systemic metabolism and increases inflammatory burden, further exacerbating organ dysfunction [123]. These findings raise the intriguing possibility that skeletal and muscle degeneration may serve as an initiating event in multi-organ failure, highlighting the need for further investigation into the causal relationships between musculoskeletal health and systemic organ function.

Targeted intervention strategies and clinical translation

Based on the MOBA framework, interventional strategies for OP have expanded from traditional single-target drugs to systemic modulation targeting multi-organ networks. In this section, we classify emerging therapeutic approaches into five categories according to their primary intervention modality and mechanism of action: microbial modulation (probiotics and prebiotics), which acts through exogenous manipulation of the GM and the gut-immune-bone axis; small-molecule inhibitors and agonists, which precisely target key signaling nodes within specific organ-bone axes with high selectivity; natural products, which leverage multi-component, multi-target properties to coordinately regulate inflammation, metabolism, and GM; synthetic compounds, which combine well-defined chemical structures with tunable pharmacokinetics and systemic regulatory effects; and exercise, a non-pharmacological intervention that integrates myokine signaling, exosome-mediated communication, and GM remodeling (Table 2).

Microbial modulation: prebiotics and probiotics

According to the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) consensus, “probiotics” are defined as “live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host” [155], whereas “prebiotics” are “a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit” [156]. Recent studies indicate that probiotics and prebiotics regulate bone metabolism through exogenous modulation of GM composition and function, promoting beneficial microbial metabolites, inhibiting pathogen colonization, and enhancing intestinal barrier integrity [18,19]. They exert protective effects against OP via cross-system pathways such as the “gut-immune-bone axis” [83].

Regarding probiotic interventions, different strains demonstrate potential for ameliorating bone loss through specific modulation of the “gut-immune-skeletal” axis. A randomized controlled trial confirmed that Limosilactobacillus reuteri 6475 effectively attenuates the decline in BMD in both early postmenopausal and elderly women, providing clinical evidence for its use in PMOP [124,125]. Lactiplantibacillus plantarum GMNL-662 and Bacillus coagulans have been shown to improve bone microarchitecture in OVX mice by modulating the Bregs-Tregs-Th17 immune axis, increasing levels of anti-inflammatory factors in peripheral blood and SCFAs in the gut [19]. Furthermore, Ricoldi et al. found that a compound preparation containing Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus could suppress the proliferation of aerobic pathogens, promote the colonization of anaerobic bacteria, and reduce systemic inflammation levels, thereby indirectly mitigating alveolar and systemic bone loss in a periodontitis model [157].

Prebiotics, serving as “nutritional substrates” for the GM, drive structural optimization of the microbiota and remodeling of its metabolic functions, thereby positively influencing bone metabolism. A clinical trial demonstrated that prebiotics extracted from Ganoderma lucidum and Pleurotus ostreatus promoted the proliferation of SCFAs-producing bacteria such as Fusobacterium prausnitzii, significantly increased fecal butyrate concentration, and modulated the serum RANKL/OPG ratio, consequently rebalancing bone remodeling [126]. Xylooligosaccharides (XOS), either alone or as part of a synbiotic formulation with probiotics, effectively ameliorated systemic metabolic abnormalities and bone microstructural damage in HFD-induced OP rats. This effect was closely associated with the suppression of intestinal inflammation and improved insulin sensitivity [127]. Notably, a study developing the compound preparation SBD111-A showed that it dose-dependently enriched SCFAs-producing and vitamin K2-synthesizing functional bacteria, significantly elevated fecal SCFAs and vitamin K2 levels, reduced bone resorption markers, and consequently prevented bone loss in OVX mice [128]. Despite these promising results, inconsistencies exist among studies. These inconsistencies highlight the importance of considering host-specific factors and experimental conditions when interpreting results and designing future clinical trials.

Although existing basic and some clinical research has confirmed the beneficial effects of prebiotics and probiotics in OP management, their clinical translation remains challenging. Major current issues include the significant strain-specificity of probiotic effects, with substantial variations in the ability of different strains to modulate the GM and improve bone metabolism. Furthermore, consensus standards regarding optimal dosage, intervention duration, and target populations have not been established. Therefore, future efforts require more large-scale, long-term, multi-center randomized controlled trials to clarify their mechanisms of action and establish standardized clinical application protocols.

Small-molecule inhibitors and agonists

Small-molecule inhibitors and agonists demonstrate considerable potential for the precise intervention of OP due to their high selectivity and tunability towards key signaling pathways in bone metabolism [158]. This class of compounds enables multidimensional regulation of bone homeostasis through specific targeting of critical nodes, exhibiting well-defined mechanisms of action and validated efficacy across various models [159,160].

Small-molecule inhibitors have demonstrated substantial efficacy across multiple organ-bone axes. In the context of the adipose-bone metabolic axis, PPARγ antagonists suppress the transcriptional activity of this nuclear receptor in BMSCs, thereby blocking their adipogenic differentiation program and activating osteogenic pathways, ultimately leading to significant improvement of bone microarchitecture. Notably, this process is accompanied by concurrent downregulation of adipokine levels, including ADPN and leptin [[129], [130], [131]], indicating their dual efficacy in remodeling bone-adipose balance. Furthermore, insulin sensitizers such as vildagliptin have been shown to ameliorate insulin resistance and abnormal cholesterol metabolism in HFD rats, while simultaneously enhancing BMD and bone microstructural quality [132]. Significant progress has also been made in the realms of neuro-bone regulation and inflammatory intervention. For example, Adrenomedullin 2 enhances diabetic bone regeneration during distraction osteogenesis, by attenuating advanced glycation end products (AGE)-induced imbalances in macrophage polarization, partly through PPARγ/NF-κB signaling, and improving AGE-impaired osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs simultaneously [133]. Important breakthroughs have also been achieved in correcting calcium-phosphate metabolic disorders via the kidney-bone axis. Research demonstrates that FGF23-neutralizing antibodies can competitively bind FGF23 and block its interaction with the renal Klotho/FGFR complex, thereby restoring renal tubular phosphate reabsorption, elevating vitamin D levels, and ultimately significantly improving chronic kidney disease-associated secondary hyperparathyroidism and bone metabolic abnormalities [135].

Small-molecule agonists exert their effects by activating positive regulatory nodes within the neuro-bone axis, metabolic-bone axis, and hormonal signaling pathways. The osteometabolic benefits of different agonists vary significantly depending on their target specificity [161,162]. It should be noted, however, that not all agonists are beneficial; β2-adrenergic receptor agonists can paradoxically promote bone resorption [136], a finding that underscores their potential pathological role in sympathetic overactivity-related bone loss. Conversely, prostaglandin E2 receptor EP4 agonists activate the sensory nerve-OB pathway in an EP4 receptor-dependent manner, promoting OB proliferation, differentiation, and bone microarchitecture improvement [98]. These findings reveal the potential value of bidirectional fine-tuning of the neuro-bone axis. In the context of metabolic and hormone receptor regulation, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) agonists significantly enhance OB activity while suppressing OC differentiation and bone resorption markers in OVX mice, effectively retarding bone microstructural deterioration [137]. Furthermore, selective estrogen receptor modulators preserve bone mass in estrogen deficiency without inducing severe endocrine side effects [138]. Additionally, GPR133 agonists inhibit OC generation via the cAMP-PKA-NFATc1 axis, consequently improving bone microarchitecture [139].

Despite demonstrating clear pathway specificity and bone metabolic improvements in preclinical studies, the clinical translation of small-molecule inhibitors and agonists faces significant challenges. A primary concern is the insufficient tissue specificity of certain agents. For instance, while modulating the bone-fat balance, PPARγ antagonists may cause off-target effects on systemic glucose metabolism, potentially leading to adverse effects. These findings underscore the necessity of incorporating potential bone metabolic impacts into comprehensive safety assessments during drug development. Moreover, the translational potential of small-molecule agents is complicated by conflicting preclinical data. For example, while PPARγ antagonists consistently improve bone mass in rodent models, their effects in humans remain uncertain due to species differences in PPARγ signaling and the potential for off-target metabolic effects. Similarly, β2-AR agonists have shown both protective and detrimental effects on bone depending on the specific agonist, dosing regimen, and model system used [136]. These discrepancies underscore the need for more rigorous preclinical models that better recapitulate human disease and for well-designed clinical trials to validate efficacy and safety.

Natural products

In recent years, natural products have garnered significant attention in the prevention and management of OP due to their unique advantages of multi-target actions and low toxicity. Preclinical studies suggest that various plant extracts and traditional compound formulations can regulate bone homeostasis through coordinated effects on inflammation, metabolism, GM, and bone cell function [[163], [164], [165]]. Their mechanisms of action involve multiple levels, including repairing the intestinal barrier, modulating microbial community structure, influencing host metabolism, and directly regulating OB/OC activity. Studies on single herbs have revealed their multi-faceted mechanisms for bone protection. Feng et al. [20] found that quercetin significantly alters GM structure, elevates SCFAs levels, restores intestinal barrier function, and reduces inflammatory cytokine release, effectively mitigating bone loss. Similarly, Mei et al. [140] demonstrated that polyphenols from areca nut seeds can maintain the Paneth cell population in estrogen-deficient hosts, increase lysozyme (LYZ) expression, and ameliorate OP by regulating GM and controlling inflammatory responses. Importantly, soy-whey dual protein exerts bone-protective effects by reducing serum OCN and PTH levels, decreasing bone marrow adipocyte numbers, increasing OB numbers, and upregulating OPG expression in OVX rats [141].

Research on compound formulations has also advanced. Multi-omics and bioinformatics analyses revealed that Roucongrong Pill (RCRP) alleviates bone loss in PMOP rats by modulating GM and related metabolites, influencing key metabolic pathways such as taurine/hypotaurine metabolism and steroid hormone biosynthesis [142]. Notably, Kim et al. found that Pueraria lobata exhibit phytoestrogen-like activity [143]. Without affecting liver function or estradiol levels, they induced dose-dependent improvements in bone markers and endometrial thickness, further expanding the potential of compound formulations in metabolic regulation and hormone-like actions. Given these multifaceted mechanisms, natural products offer unique advantages in OP prevention and treatment by virtue of their multi-component, multi-target properties. Their mechanisms encompass multiple aspects of the “microbiota-metabolism-immune” axis, including modulation of the gut microecology and systemic inflammation, as well as direct regulation of osteocyte differentiation and bone metabolic balance.

Synthetic compounds

Synthetic compounds exhibit unique advantages in the systemic regulation of bone metabolism due to their well-defined chemical structures, tunable pharmacokinetic properties, and established clinical development pathways [166]. These compounds achieve precise regulation of bone homeostasis by intervening in multi-organ signaling networks such as the “gut-bone axis," “immune-bone axis,” and “metabolic-bone axis” [167,168]. At the metabolic regulation level, multiple studies have confirmed that synthetic compounds can effectively ameliorate bone loss associated with metabolic disorders. Metformin activates the AMPK signaling pathway to regulate PPARγ expression, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation through the endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) pathway and effectively preventing hyperglycemia-induced bone metabolic abnormalities [144]. Similarly, isoflavone aglycones inhibit adipogenesis and inflammatory response, effectively improving bone loss in OVX rats [146]. Furthermore, Sebastian et al. [145] demonstrated that alkaline agents can reduce compensatory bone calcium mobilization by buffering the acidic state of the bone microenvironment.

Recent research has unveiled novel mechanisms through which synthetic compounds exert bone-protective effects via the “gut-bone axis.” Kang et al. [147] found that Korean red ginseng extract regulates GM diversity and intestinal permeability and is effective at preventing dysbiosis-induced bone loss in mice. Similarly, Chen et al. [148] confirmed that icariin significantly ameliorates bone loss in OVX mice by restoring intestinal barrier function, remodeling microbial community structure, and regulating host immune status. Thus, synthetic compounds provide diversified strategies for treating bone metabolic diseases through multi-target, multi-organ synergistic regulation. From traditional anti-resorptive agents to novel bone-forming agents, and from metabolic regulation to immunomodulation, synthetic compounds demonstrate multi-level, systemic interventional effects in maintaining bone homeostasis.

Exercise

Exercise serves as a non-pharmacological intervention that systemically regulates bone metabolic homeostasis through coordinated multi-organ networks. Unlike drug-based strategies, exercise exerts its effects through integrated physiological stimulation, mediated by myokine secretion, exosome-mediated signaling, and GM remodeling, establishing bidirectional muscle-bone and gut-bone regulatory axes [118,169,170]. Myokines released from skeletal muscle in response to exercise constitute crucial mediators of bone metabolism regulation. Irisin, as an important exercise-responsive factor, suppresses IL-6 production in bone marrow adipocytes through the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby alleviating obesity-associated bone loss [149]. Exercise-induced irisin also ameliorates T2DM-related bone deterioration and inflammatory responses via the miR-150/FNDC5/pyroptosis axis [23]. Mechanistically, exercise promotes polyunsaturated fatty acid metabolism in skeletal muscle, enhances PGC1α expression, and stimulates FNDC5 transcription. The secreted irisin targets OB through exosomes, binds to caveolin-1 (Cav1) protein, activates the AMPK pathway, and synergistically promotes OB proliferation while inhibiting ferroptosis [[150], [151], [152]]. Additionally, the exercise-derived metabolite β-aminoisobutyric acid (BAIBA) promotes bone progenitor cell differentiation by activating NADPH oxidase/reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling [153]. Its L-isomer, L-BAIBA, mediated by the SLC6A6 transporter, downregulates the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB pathway and activates the NRF2 antioxidant system, effectively inhibiting osteoclastogenesis [24].

Notably, exercise-mediated regulation of bone metabolism extends beyond direct muscle-bone communication, with gut microecological remodeling representing another crucial component. Exercise exerts systemic bone-protective effects by reshaping the gut microbial ecosystem, acting synergistically with myokine regulation. Both physical activity and exercise-derived fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) enhance intestinal barrier integrity. Specifically, exercise-derived FMT alleviates systemic inflammation by activating the apelin signaling pathway, thereby attenuating estrogen deficiency-induced OP progression [154]. Concurrently, characteristic alterations in the gut bile acid profile observed in exercise models significantly correlate with enhanced bone mass [154].

Collectively, exercise regulates bone metabolism through a multidimensional, multi-organ network. Myokines, exosomes, and GM metabolites jointly contribute to these effects through direct and indirect signaling pathways. Myokines (irisin/BAIBA) directly modulate bone cell differentiation and function via classical signaling pathways; exercise-derived exosomes serve as novel signaling vehicles mediating inter-organ communication; GM and their metabolites (e.g., bile acids) indirectly influence bone homeostasis through immune-metabolic axes. Future investigations should refine the dose-response relationships between exercise parameters (intensity/frequency/mode) and bone protective effects, explore synergistic effects of personalized exercise prescriptions with nutritional and pharmacological interventions, and advance the clinical application of exercise interventions in the precision prevention and management of bone metabolic diseases (Fig. 2).

Multi-modal targeted intervention strategies for osteoporosis based on the MOBA framework. These strategies systematically target dysfunctional organ networks to restore bone homeostasis in OP, transcending conventional bone-centric therapeutic approaches. Microbial modulation remodels GM structure and repairs the intestinal mucosal barrier, thereby blocking pathological signal transduction along the gut-bone axis. Natural products and synthetic compounds exert trans-axial regulation of adipose tissue inflammation and correct renal calcium-phosphate imbalances. Signaling pathway targeting precisely interrupts pathological transmission in the neuro-bone and immune-bone axes. Exercise intervention induces myokine secretion, activates the muscle-gut axis, and augments positive signaling through the muscle-bone axis. These strategies collectively converge on the bone marrow microenvironment, rebalancing the bone remodeling process by suppressing OC activity and/or enhancing OB function, ultimately promoting the restoration of bone mass and microarchitecture.

Translational perspectives and future directions

Clinical translation: evidence and barriers

At present, the central challenge in MOBA-targeted interventions is no longer confined to whether individual strategies exert biological effects, but rather whether their levels of evidence, target populations, and safety profiles are sufficient to support clinical translation. From a translational perspective, interventions supported by clinical studies with defined endpoints such as BMD or fracture outcomes should be considered high-maturity approaches. For example, romosozumab has demonstrated consistent gains in BMD and reduction in incident vertebral fractures in postmenopausal women with OP and mild to moderate renal impairment, indicating that the repurposing and refined application of approved drugs in specific MOBA subpopulations is highly feasible [171]. In contrast, microbiome-based interventions, although evaluated in randomized controlled trials, have yielded inconsistent results. Notably, long-term administration of Limosilactobacillus reuteri 6475 in early postmenopausal women did not produce a consistent anti-bone-loss effect, suggesting that such approaches are more appropriately classified as intermediate-maturity strategies with preliminary clinical signals but insufficient validation [125]. Lifestyle and exercise interventions represent a distinct translational category, characterized by well-defined systemic benefits and favorable safety profiles. Recent randomized trials have shown that such interventions can attenuate the loss of BMD associated with weight reduction in older women, supporting their role as one of the most immediately implementable non-pharmacological strategies [172]. Three priority directions for future development can be identified: first, the precision repurposing of approved therapies in defined MOBA subgroups; second, structured exercise and lifestyle interventions; and third, microbiome modulation guided by host stratification. By contrast, most natural products and organ axis-specific small molecules remain predominantly supported by preclinical evidence and should be regarded as early-stage strategies [125,171,172] (Table 3).

The principal barriers to MOBA translation do not lie in insufficient efficacy alone, but in the absence of an integrated evidence framework that connects mechanistic insights, biomarker development, patient stratification, safety monitoring, and regulatory endpoints [75,173]. A major limitation is the lack of validated biomarkers for precision translation. Although combined assessment of IL-6 and leptin has shown potential for predicting fracture risk in postmenopausal women with low bone mass [63], such biomarkers remain insufficiently validated across large-scale, multi-center, and external cohorts, limiting their immediate clinical applicability. Future MOBA studies should therefore incorporate organ-network phenotyping, including GM profiles, adipose distribution patterns, renal function status, neuromuscular performance, and fracture history, to enable more precise patient stratification. Safety considerations must also be prioritized, particularly in high-risk populations. For example, the increased risk of severe hypocalcaemia associated with denosumab in dialysis-dependent patients highlights that interventions validated in primary OP cannot be directly extrapolated to all forms of secondary OP [75]. In such populations, proactive safety monitoring and dynamic risk management are essential. Overall, addressing these interrelated challenges through multi-center biomarker validation, organ network-based stratification, proactive safety assessment, and regulatory-aligned trial design will accelerate the clinical translation of MOBA-targeted therapies and expand therapeutic options for patients with OP and related systemic skeletal disorders.

Emerging platforms and future directions

Beyond conventional animal models, multi-organ-on-chip systems and bone-related organoids are emerging as critical platforms for MOBA research and translation. These human-relevant microphysiological systems can recapitulate inter-organ interactions under controlled fluidic conditions, mechanical cues, and multicellular co-culture environments, thereby more closely reflecting the dynamic, bidirectional, and systemic regulatory features of MOBA [174,175]. Recent studies have demonstrated that bone and joint-on-chip platforms can model key aspects of the bone and joint microenvironment and are applicable to mechanistic studies, drug screening, and patient-specific modeling, underscoring their experimental utility and translational potential in skeletal research [175]. In parallel, advanced bone marrow organoids derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells have successfully reconstructed essential cellular compositions, spatial architecture, and molecular features of the bone marrow niche, providing a scalable human platform for integrating gut, kidney, muscle, and immune modules into composite MOBA models [176]. Importantly, organ-on-chip technologies are evolving from single-organ systems toward interconnected multi-organ platforms with increased throughput. This shift enables not only mechanistic dissection of causal pathways but also integrated evaluation of drug efficacy and toxicity, as well as patient-specific modeling. As such, these platforms are poised to serve as key intermediaries linking fundamental MOBA mechanisms with drug development and precision medicine applications [177,178]. Building on these developments, recent advances in organoids and organs-on-chips (OoCs) further enhance the translational relevance of the MOBA framework by providing human-relevant platforms that recapitulate multi-tissue crosstalk and disease heterogeneity. These systems have demonstrated potential for modeling disease mechanisms and therapeutic responses, while also emerging as regulatory-relevant tools that may complement or partially replace animal studies [179].

Beyond these challenges, the field is characterized by notable contradictions and unresolved controversies that warrant systematic investigation. First, the effects of many interventions are highly context-dependent, varying with disease stage, model system, and experimental conditions. For instance, conflicting findings regarding the roles of key signaling molecules such as IL-17 A, TNF-α, and serotonin indicate that their functions are not unidirectional but rather depend on concentration, cellular context, and the presence of co-factors. Second, the translation of promising preclinical results to clinical settings has been hampered by species differences in organ crosstalk mechanisms and microbiota composition. Addressing these contradictions will require standardized experimental protocols, multi-center studies, and the use of humanized or patient-derived models to better recapitulate disease heterogeneity.

Future research should focus on three strategic directions: Mechanistically, advanced technologies such as spatial transcriptomics and single-cell sequencing should be employed to map high-resolution dynamic communication networks between organs and identify novel key signaling molecules and regulatory nodes. Technologically, efforts should prioritize developing intelligent drug delivery systems, organoids, and multi-organ-chip models to overcome delivery challenges posed by multi-organ microenvironmental heterogeneity. Clinically, there is an urgent need to establish an OP precision subtyping system based on organ network profiling, integrating multidimensional data including GM profiles, circulating adipokine patterns, and neuroendocrine markers to achieve refined patient stratification and personalized treatment strategies. Ultimately, the OP prevention and treatment paradigm will transition from traditional “local bone repair” to “systemic ecosystem reconstruction,” achieving holistic recovery of bone homeostasis through multi-organ modulation. This paradigm shift not only provides novel insights for OP management but also offers a reference for systematic research on other metabolic bone diseases. Future research outcomes are expected to advance the application of precision medicine in bone metabolic disorders, laying a solid foundation for developing highly effective and safe OP therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, MOBA is not a simple linear organ-bone relationship, but an integrated regulatory network involving hormonal signaling, immune-inflammatory mediators, and neuro-metabolic coupling. Together, these layers maintain bone homeostasis. Hormonal signaling provides the core framework for inter-organ communication, forming bidirectional feedback pathways between bone and organs such as the pancreas, kidneys, and nervous system, and plays a central role in maintaining bone metabolic homeostasis. Immune-inflammatory mediators function as a critical response mechanism that, under pathological conditions such as chronic inflammation or autoimmunity, triggers cascading bone loss through osteoimmune crosstalk, contributing substantially to OP progression. Neuro-metabolic coupling serves as a key adaptive mechanism, perceiving mechanical stimuli and regulating energy metabolism to enable real-time adaptation of bone tissue to physiological changes, thereby fine-tuning bone homeostasis.

Two key features of this network are bidirectional communication and functional redundancy. On one hand, bone is not a passive recipient of regulation but actively secretes “osteocrine signals” such as OCN and NPY, which in turn regulate the function of distant organs, contributing to a bidirectional organ-bone interaction network. On the other hand, functional disruption in any single axis can be partially compensated by other axes; however, prolonged imbalance triggers a cascading amplification effect. Together, these features indicate that OP arises from synergistic interactions across multiple organs and pathways, helping to explain the limited efficacy of single-target interventions. Based on the MOBA framework, intervention strategies for OP have shifted from traditional single-target drugs toward systemic regulation of multi-organ networks. This review has summarized five major intervention strategies within the MOBA framework: microbial modulation protects bone by remodeling the “microbiota-immune-skeletal” axis; natural products regulate inflammatory and metabolic homeostasis leveraging their multi-component, multi-target advantages; small-molecule inhibitors/agonists precisely target key signaling nodes; synthetic compounds remodel bone homeostasis through dual metabolic and immune regulation; and exercise intervention exerts non-pharmacological protective effects via myokine regulation and gut microecological modulation. Collectively, these strategies constitute a novel systemic framework for OP prevention and treatment.
