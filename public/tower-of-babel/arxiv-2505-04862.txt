Accurate Prediction of Sequential Tensor Properties Using Equivariant Graph Neural Network
Ting-Wei Hsu, Zhenyao Fang, Arun Bansil, Qimin Yan

Abstract
Optical spectroscopies provide a powerful tool for harnessing light-matter interactions for unraveling complex electronic features such as the flat bands and nontrivial topologies of materials. These insights are crucial for the development and optimization of optoelectronic devices, including solar cells, light-emitting diodes, and photodetectors, where device performance is closely connected with the nature of the underlying electronic spectrum. Realistic modeling of tensor optical responses in materials, which are computationally quite demanding, however, remains challenging. Here we introduce the Sequential-tensorial-properties Equivariant Neural Network (StepENN), which is a graph neural network architecture that maps crystal structures directly to their full photon-frequency-dependent optical tensors. By encoding the isotropic sequential scalar components along with the anisotropic sequential tensor components into ℓ = 0 \ell=0 and ℓ = 2 \ell=2 spherical tensor components, StepENN ensures symmetry-aware predictions that are consistent with the constraints of crystalline symmetries of materials. Trained on a dataset of frequency-dependent permittivity tensors of 1,432 bulk semiconductors computed using first-principles methods, our model achieves a mean absolute error (MAE) of 24.216 millifarads per meter (mF/m), demonstrating its potential for efficient modeling of other related properties such as the optical conductivities. Our framework opens new avenues for rational data-driven design of anisotropic optical responses for accelerating materials discovery for advancing optoelectronic applications.

Optical spectroscopies, which involve excitation of a material with external electromagnetic fields, provide a powerful tool for probing properties of materials. We propose the Sequential-tensorial-properties Equivariant Neural Network (StepENN), which represents Cartesian tensors in spherical tensor components, which leverages the symmetry-aware capabilities of E3NN to model complex directional dependencies of functional properties of materials. Our method utilizes
\ell=0

\ell=2

We demonstrate the capabilities of StepENN, by considering the frequency-dependent permittivity tensors of a dataset of 1,432 nonmagnetic semiconductors, which were calculated via first-principles methods using OpenMX

\ell=0

By training StepENN on the aforementioned dataset, we achieve a MAE of 24.216 mF/m on the predicted permittivity spectra, with 85.7% of predictions exhibiting less than 10% relative error in the test set. The high prediction accuracy enables the reconstruction of the real part of permittivity tensor via the Karmers-Kronig (K-K) relations, allowing us to compute additional optical properties derived from the spectra. Accurately predicting the full tensor while preserving its symmetry opens new opportunities for designing materials with tailored anisotropic responses, benefiting applications such as photovoltaics, optical modulators, and sensing technologies.

To predict the permittivity tensor using an equivariant neural network architecture, we expand the target tensors in a spherical harmonic basis, which naturally encodes rotational symmetry. Let
v_{i}
denote the Cartesian unit vectors with
i\in\{x,y,z\}
, and
Y_{1}^{\alpha}
the spherical harmonic basis vectors with
\alpha\in\{0,\pm 1\}
. Due to the intrinsic symmetry of the permittivity tensor,
\chi_{ij}=\chi_{ji}
, it is sufficient to consider only the upper triangular components in the Cartesian basis. The permittivity tensor can then be expressed as the outer product of two rank-1 vectors
a_{i}v_{i}
and
b_{j}v_{j}
, leading to:

\chi_{ij}=a_{i}b_{j}\,v_{i}\otimes v_{j}=T_{ij}\,v_{i}v_{j}=T_{ij}\,v_{ij},

(1)

where
T_{ij}=a_{i}b_{j}
are the physical tensor coefficients, and
v_{ij}=v_{i}\otimes v_{j}=v_{i}v_{j}
is the rank-2 Cartesian tensor basis.

To transform this representation into the spherical harmonic basis, we first convert the Cartesian vectors into covariant canonical components using the unitary transformation
v_{\alpha}=C_{i\alpha}v_{i}
, and equivalently
v_{i}=C_{i\alpha}^{*}v_{\alpha}
.
The rank-1 components transform as
v_{\alpha=0}=v_{z}
and
v_{\alpha=\pm 1}=\mp\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(v_{x}\pm iv_{y})
. Accordingly, the rank-2 Cartesian basis transforms as
v_{ij}=C_{i\alpha}^{*}C_{j\beta}^{*}v_{\alpha}v_{\beta},
and the permittivity tensor becomes:

\chi_{ij}=T_{ij}v_{ij}=T_{ij}C_{i\alpha}^{*}C_{j\beta}^{*}v_{\alpha}v_{\beta}=T_{\alpha\beta}v_{\alpha}v_{\beta},

(2)

where
T_{\alpha\beta}=C_{i\alpha}^{*}C_{j\beta}^{*}T_{ij}
are the same physical tensor components, re-expressed in the spherical harmonic basis.
Since the canonical components
v_{\alpha}
are equivalent to the
\ell=1
spherical harmonics
Y_{1}^{\alpha}
, we can identify
v_{\alpha}v_{\beta}=Y_{1}^{\alpha}\otimes Y_{1}^{\beta}=Y_{1}^{\alpha}Y_{1}^{\beta}
, and thus:

\chi_{ij}=T_{\alpha\beta}\,Y_{1}^{\alpha}Y_{1}^{\beta}.

(3)

To complete the transformation, we decompose the product of two spherical harmonics into irreducible representations using Clebsh-Gordan coefficients:

Y_{l_{1}}^{m_{1}}Y_{l_{2}}^{m_{2}}=\sum_{\ell}(2\ell+1)(-1)^{m_{1}+m_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell&l_{1}&l_{2}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\ell&l_{1}&l_{2}\\
-(m_{1}+m_{2})&m_{1}&m_{2}\end{pmatrix}Y_{\ell}^{m_{1}+m_{2}},

(4)

where

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}l_{1}&l_{2}&l_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{array}\right)
is the Wigner
3j
symbols which enforce angular-momentum selection rules. In the specific case of the tensor product
Y_{1}^{\alpha}Y_{1}^{\beta}
, the allowed values of total angular momentum are limited to
\ell=0
and
\ell=2
. This results in the decomposition:

\chi_{ij}=T_{\alpha\beta}\,Y_{1}^{\alpha}Y_{1}^{\beta}=\chi^{(0)}\oplus\chi^{(2)}=\sum_{l\in\{0,2\}}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\chi_{(l)}^{m}Y_{l}^{m},

(5)

which corresponds to the trace (
\ell=0
) and the traceless symmetric (
\ell=2
) parts of the permittivity tensor, respectively.

This approach transforms the tensors from the Cartesian basis into the spherical harmonic basis, making it easier to work with tensor properties in systems with rotational symmetry. In Eq. (
5
), note that a rank-2 Cartesian tensor can be decomposed into
\ell=0
and
\ell=2

2\ell+1

2.2
Equivariant Graph Neural Network Architecture

Our StepENN model extends the e3nn and GNNOpt frameworks, both of which have demonstrated strong performance in sequential prediction tasks such as electron and phonon densities of states, as well as in predicting traces of optical spectra
MingdaPhonon
;
hungEnsembleEmbeddingGraphNeural2024
;
okabe2024virtual
. While StepENN shares the underlying principles of equivariant GNNs inherited from e3nn, it introduces key extensions that differentiate it from GNNOpt, particularly since we leverage higher-order spherical harmonic components to accurately capture anisotropy of tensor properties (Figure
3
). Accordingly, we construct a GNN that represents each material as a crystal graph, where the atomic and structural information is encoded through node and edge features. Each atomic node
i
is initially represented by a one-hot encoding of its atomic species
z_{\text{type},i}=\text{onehot}(\text{atomic number})\in\mathbb{R}^{118},

corresponding to the 118 known chemical elements
xieCrystalGraphConvolutional2018a
. These atomic types are embedded via a multilayer perceptron to produce initial node-level features
f_{i}
.

StepENN enforces equivariance to 3D Euclidean transformations by adopting the formalism of tensor field networks
thomas2018tensorfieldnetworksrotation
. Translational equivariance is achieved through the use of relative atomic positions in message passing, while rotational equivariance is enforced by constraining intermediate features to lie within irreducible representations of the rotation group
\mathrm{SO}(3)
. These are indexed by rotation order
\ell\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
and parity
p\in\{-1,1\}
. Each feature tensor takes the form
V_{cm}^{(\ell,p)}
, where
c
indexes feature channels and
m\in[-\ell,\ell]
indexes spherical harmonic components. Thus, a representation at degree
\ell
occupies a tensor space of dimension
\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{channels}}\times(2\ell+1)}
.

These representations are combined using equivariant tensor product operations
\otimes
to construct the pointwise convolution layers. This operation can be applied iteratively
L
times, following the Clebsch–Gordan decomposition as implemented in
e3nn
:

\left(\mathbf{U}^{(\ell_{1},p_{1})}\otimes\mathbf{V}^{(\ell_{2},p_{2})}\right)^{(\ell_{0},p_{0})}_{cm_{0}}=\sum_{m_{1}=-\ell_{1}}^{\ell_{1}}\sum_{m_{2}=-\ell_{2}}^{\ell_{2}}C^{(\ell_{0},m_{0})}_{(\ell_{1},m_{1})(\ell_{2},m_{2})}\,U^{(\ell_{1},p_{1})}_{cm_{1}}\,V^{(\ell_{2},p_{2})}_{cm_{2}},

(6)

where
\ell_{0}\in\left[|\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}|,\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\right]
and
p_{0}=p_{1}p_{2}
. In our implementation, the maximum rotation order is restricted to
\ell\leq 2
. For brevity, we omit the indices
\ell
,
p
,
c
, and
m
when referring to these tensors in the remainder of this paper.

After the final pointwise convolution layer, we apply mean pooling over all nodes to obtain a global representation of the material. The network output is then partitioned into two parts: a scalar component corresponding to
\ell=0
and a tensor component corresponding to
\ell=2
. To train the model, we define a composite loss function
\mathcal{L}
, which separately evaluates the scalar and tensor components of the predicted optical spectra. Let
N
denote the number of materials and
n_{\omega}
the number of frequency points. For each material entry
i
,
\hat{y}_{0e}^{(i)}(\omega)
and
\hat{y}_{2e}^{(i)}(\omega,m)
denote the predicted
\ell=0
and
\ell=2
components, respectively, and
y_{0e}^{(i)}(\omega)
and
y_{2e}^{(i)}(\omega,m)
denote the corresponding ground-truth values. The total loss is defined as:

\mathcal{L}=\text{MAE}(\ell=0)+\text{MAE}(\ell=2),

(7)

with component-wise definitions:

\text{MAE}(\ell=0)=\frac{1}{Nn_{\omega}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\omega}|\hat{y}_{0e}^{(i)}(\omega)-y_{0e}^{(i)}(\omega)|,

(8)

\text{MAE}(\ell=2)=\frac{1}{Nn_{\omega}M_{\ell=2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\omega}\sum_{m=-2}^{2}|\hat{y}_{2e}^{(i)}(\omega,m)-y_{2e}^{(i)}(\omega,m)|,

(9)

where
M_{\ell=2}=2\cdot 2+1=5
is the number of components for the rank-2 irreducible representation. The model used for prediction was selected based on the best performance according to the validation loss. After obtaining the predicted spherical tensor components from the best model, we transformed them back into the Cartesian tensor representation.

To evaluate model’s performance, we compress the sequential tensorial outputs into scalar quantities for both the prediction and the target. Specifically, for each sample, we first average the tensor components over the frequency range and then compute the mean over the tensor indices to obtain a single scalar value, denoted as
\bar{\bar{\chi}}^{(k)}
with
k\in\{\text{prediction},\text{target}\}
.
The scalar
\bar{\bar{\chi}}^{(k)}
is computed using,

\bar{\bar{\chi}}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{6}\frac{1}{n_{\omega}}\sum_{i\leq j}\sum_{\omega}\chi_{ij}^{(k)}(\omega),

(10)

where
n_{\omega}
is the total number of sampled frequency points, and
\omega
denotes the discrete frequency points. The summation is restricted to
i\leq j
to account for the intrinsic symmetry of the permittivity tensor,
\chi_{ij}=\chi_{ji}
, resulting in six independent components in the three-dimensional Cartesian representation: the three diagonal terms
\chi_{xx}
,
\chi_{yy}
,
\chi_{zz}
, and the three off-diagonal terms
\chi_{xy}
,
\chi_{xz}
, and
\chi_{yz}
. This compression preserves the original physical units of the spectra (F/m) and provides a consistent and physically meaningful evaluation of the model predictions without introducing redundancy from symmetric tensor elements.

Following this compression, we assess the model’s performance by evaluating and reporting the MAE to directly quantify the average prediction error in original physical units, defined as

\text{MAE}=\left|\bar{\bar{\chi}}^{\text{prediction}}-\bar{\bar{\chi}}^{\text{target}}\right|.

(11)

We also compute the relative error, defined as

\text{Relative Error}=\frac{|\bar{\bar{\chi}}^{\text{prediction}}-\bar{\bar{\chi}}^{\text{target}}|}{|\bar{\bar{\chi}}^{\text{target}}|},

(12)

which provides a normalized measure of the prediction accuracy relative to the magnitude of the true tensor response.

These distinct tensorial symmetries are also reflected in the spherical harmonic decomposition. Each crystal system preserves different spherical tensor channels depending on its symmetry, and this is directly observable in the spherical tensor plots. As already pointed out, in the spherical tensor representation, the permittivity tensor can be decomposed into
\ell=0
and
\ell=2

\ell=0

However, because both the input crystal structure and the corresponding permittivity tensor are inherently symmetry-consistent, the model preserves the underlying symmetry constraints during training. As a result, it outputs only the physically allowed tensor components, maintaining the correct form of the spherical tensor decomposition. This symmetry-preserving behavior ensures that no spurious mixing occurs between channels and that the reconstructed Cartesian tensor contains only the symmetry-permitted elements. Consequently, the model captures the intrinsic symmetry of materials without violating crystallographic constraints.

3
Discussion

A major strength of our StepENN model lies in its equivariant architecture, which preserves the symmetries of the input crystal structure. This property is validated through symmetry-mask analysis of the predicted permittivity tensors, particularly in the triclinic systems where all components, including the off-diagonal terms, are expected to be nonzero. As a result, the model effectively captures directional couplings (e.g.,
x\rightarrow z
,
x\rightarrow y
) that are often missed or underestimated by the graph neural network in the absence of the equivariance architecture. Compared to first-principles methods such as the Kubo–Greenwood formalism, StepENN offers a data-driven, computationally efficient alternative, enabling rapid screening of materials with pronounced anisotropic optical responses. In addition to maintaining a low mean relative error, the model respects both physical and symmetry constraints. For instance, all predicted spectra decay toward zero as the photon energy approaches 30 eV, a behavior consistent with the asymptotic vanishing of interband transitions at high frequencies. This trend ensures compliance with the
f
-sum rule, which dictates that the integral over the optical conductivity (or dielectric function) must satisfy a conserved quantity related to the total number of electrons. Such behavior enables the evaluation of derived quantities governed by the
f
-sum rule, including the generalized quantum weights
PhysRevX.14.011052
. Symmetry constraints are verified using energy-resolved mask comparisons across the frequency axis, confirming that the
\ell=0
components of the spherical tensor decomposition capture isotropic contributions, along with the
\ell=2
components which represent anisotropic effects. For example, the model correctly reflects the symmetry-enforced relations such as

To further investigate the model’s treatment of symmetry, we systematically examined the importance of the input structure satisfying crystal symmetry constraints. \hat{\chi}_{xx}=\hat{\chi}_{yy}\neq\hat{\chi}_{zz}

While the primary focus of this work is on predicting the imaginary part of the permittivity tensor, we also demonstrate that the real part can be accurately reconstructed by using K-K relations. This yields a complete complex permittivity tensor, which in turn enables the derivation of a broad range of optical response functions, including optical conductivity. Such predictive capability is particularly valuable for designing materials for photovoltaics, sensors, and nonlinear optical applications. Triclinic crystals, for example, with both

4.1
DFT Calculations

DFT calculations were performed using OpenMX
openmxfirst
;
openmxsecond
;
openmxthird
;
openmxfourth
, which employs numerical pseudo-atomic orbitals as the basis set to expand the one-particle Kohn-Sham wave functions. The high-throughput calculations involve three main steps: generating input files, submitting jobs, and monitoring their progress. The input files were generated using
ASE
package, which provides a predefined lookup table for optimized basis selection. However, this table has not been updated since 2013, leading to missing entries for some elements with heavy
f
-orbital character. We used the PBE-GGA functional throughout the calculations. We use a custom workflow for job submission and monitoring, implemented with
Atomate

MATHEW2017140
. We ensured self-consistent field (SCF) convergence to
3.67\times 10^{-8}
Hartree or
10^{-6}
eV. All other settings followed the default OpenMX parameters. The SCF
k
-point grid was generated using the
pymatgen
automatic density function with a target density of 2000
k
-points per atom (kppa). This method selects
\Gamma
-centered meshes for hexagonal and face-centered lattices, and Monkhorst–Pack grids for all other crystal systems, ensuring consistent and resolution-adaptive Brillouin zone sampling.

4.2
Tight-Binding Calculations

After achieving SCF convergence with OpenMX, we extracted the Hamiltonian, overlap matrices, and position operators to evaluate the permittivity tensorial function. The relative permittivity can be calculated by following the Kubo formula:

\frac{\chi_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)}{\epsilon_{0}}=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{n,m}\frac{f_{nm}r_{nm}^{\alpha}r_{mn}^{\beta}}{\omega_{mn}-\omega-i\eta},

(13)

where
\hbar\omega_{nm}=E_{n}-E_{m}
denotes the energy difference between bands
n
and
m
, corresponding to the photon energy.
f_{nm}=f(E_{n})-f(E_{m})
is the difference in Fermi–Dirac occupation factors and
r^{\alpha}_{nm}
is the Berry connection, defined as

r^{\alpha}_{nm}=i\left\langle u_{n\mathbf{k}}\middle|\partial_{\alpha}u_{m\mathbf{k}}\right\rangle,

with
\partial_{\alpha}=\partial/\partial k_{\alpha}
representing the derivative with respect to the Cartesian momentum coordinate
\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}
. Further details can be found in
wangFirstprinciplesCalculationOptical2019
;
wangFirstprinciplesCalculationNonlinear2017
;
sipeNonlinearOpticalResponse1993
;
marzariMaximallyLocalizedGeneralized1997
;
marzariMaximallyLocalizedWannier2012
. A uniform
k
-point mesh was constructed using a reciprocal-space resolution of
0.05~\text{\AA}^{-1}
along each reciprocal lattice vector, ensuring consistent Brillouin zone sampling across all materials. The smearing parameter
\eta
, introduced to improve numerical convergence in the evaluation of the optical response functions, was fixed at 0.05 eV for all calculations.

4.3
Symmetry perseverance validation

Group theoretical analysis reveals five distinct cases determined by the symmetry properties of a material system
MOZRZYMAS197429
;
NonlinearOpticsRobert
;
powellSymmetryGroupTheory2010
. This framework enables us to construct a mask
\mathbf{M}
that identifies the symmetry-allowed, non-vanishing components of a tensor. The mask is derived by initializing a
3\times 3
matrix of ones,
\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}
, and applying the set of rotation operations
\mathcal{O}=\{R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{N_{R}}\}
associated with the space group symmetry of the crystal. The final mask is computed by averaging over the transformed matrices:

\mathbf{M}=\frac{1}{N_{R}}\sum_{R_{i}\in\mathcal{O}}R_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}R_{i}.

For example, in a monoclinic system, this process yields the following mask:

\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
1&0&1\end{pmatrix}

(14)

This mask highlights the non-vanishing tensor components, such as
xx
,
yy
,
zz
, and
xz
, as dictated by the symmetry constraints. To identify any symmetry violations, we define the residual mask as

\text{Residual mask}=\epsilon(\mathbf{1}_{3\times 3}-\mathbf{M})=\begin{pmatrix}0&\epsilon&0\\
\epsilon&0&\epsilon\\
0&\epsilon&0\end{pmatrix},

where
\epsilon=10^{-6}~\mathrm{F/m}
serves as the detection threshold. We apply this residual mask across the photon energy grid to verify the input tensors before training and the predicted tensors after training. Our results confirm that all tensors respect the imposed symmetry constraints, demonstrating that the model maintains equivariance throughout training without symmetry breaking.

4.4
Data preparation for model training

The spectra were computed with a resolution of 0.01 eV, with a total of 3,001 points. Including all points in the training process would significantly increase computational cost. To make training more efficient, we performed downsampling by interpolating the data along the photon energy grid to 301 points, which still captures most of the spectral response and enables a practical training approach, see Section 1 of the Supplementary Information for details. We used an 80:10:10 split for training, validation, and testing datasets. Given our focus on symmetry with respect to crystal systems, we implemented balanced loading by analyzing the distribution of crystal systems across all splits to ensure even representation, see Section 4 of Supplementary Information for details.

4.5
Optimization of hyperparameters

We performed a comprehensive hyperparameter search to optimize performance of the model. The multiplicity of irreducible representations
m
used in the first two convolution layers was varied from 16 to 64, with 32 selected as the optimal value. The number of pointwise convolution layers
L
was tuned between 1 and 4, with the final model using 2. The embedding feature vector length was explored from 16 to 160, with 64 yielding the best performance. Dropout probability was tested at 0, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.4 with 0.4 selected. Batch sizes of 1, 2, and 4 were evaluated, and a size of 4 was chosen as the best tradeoff between efficiency and stability. The hyperparameters of the AdamW optimizer were carefully tuned. Learning rates between
5\times 10^{-4}
and
10^{-2}
were explored, with
5\times 10^{-3}
achieving the lowest validation loss. To further enhance convergence, we applied PyTorch’s
CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts
scheduler, which gradually reduces the learning rate according to a cosine decay schedule. The initial number of iterations before the first restart was set to
T_{0}=10
, with subsequent settings kept at their default values. This smooth, non-monotonic decay enables larger updates during the early training stages and finer adjustments at later stages, helping the model escape shallow minima and converge more robustly
loshchilov2017sgdrstochasticgradientdescent
.
