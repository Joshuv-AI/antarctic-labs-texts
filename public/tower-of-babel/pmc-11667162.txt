Identification and validation of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD)-associated targets for monascin from the extracts of Monascus purpureus-fermented rice: Compound preparation, high-throughput genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis
Authors: Xilinqiqige Bao, Hanqing Li, Jiayin Xu, Xiaoqing Liu, Runa A, Jianming Chen, Fengzheng Chen, Nan Ya, Rigula Sa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the remarkable bioactivities exhibited by monascin against several tumors, its therapeutic targets remain unexplored. In this study, our objective is to identify therapeutic targets based on the antitumor activity of monascin against lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) A549 cells. The compound monascin was derived from extracts of Monascus purpureus-fermented rice. Its chemical structure was determined using spectroscopic methods, while the physicochemical properties of monascin were investigated through thermal behavior analysis. Herein, antitumor studies indicated that monascin significantly inhibited the viability of A549 cells, with an IC50 value of 2.05 μM; RNA-sequencing revealed 12 up-regulated and 29 down-regulated genes as differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for A549 cells in response to monascin; RT-qPCR validation supported the plausibility of the RNA-sequencing results. Moreover, utilizing data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses provided evidence supporting GPR37 and FAM83A as potential candidate genes for predicting disease progression in LUAD patients. Additionally, the LASSO Cox regression and nomogram analyses confirmed that FAM83A and GPR37 genes had the potential to predict overall survival for LUAD patients. Finally, molecular docking studies suggested that monascin could interact with both GPR37 and FAM83A proteins through the hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions; Western blotting assays indicated that monascin inhibited the expression levels of both GPR37 and FAM83A proteins. Overall, the clinical prognosis of GPR37 and FAM83A highlights the rationale for targeting these specific genes with monascin. Additionally, there findings provide compelling evidence for the polypharmacological properties of monascin against LUAD.Keywords: Monascin, RNA-sequencing, Antitumor, Lung adenocarcinoma, GPR37, FAM83A

Introduction

Lung cancer is a major contributor to the morbidity and mortality associated with cancer, posing a global health threat worldwide(Bade and Dela Cruz, 2020). Among all subtypes of lung cancer, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) stands out as the most common subtype(Rafei et al., 2017). Over the past two decades(Herbst et al., 2018), numerous researchers have focused on identifying novel targeted therapeutic drugs that have shown promising results in reducing the mortality rates among some LUAD patients. For example, selective epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors like erlotinib and gefitinib have demonstrated clinical feasibility in treatment for LUAD(Miyawaki et al., 2021, Nagano et al., 2019). However, the 5-year survival rate of LUAD patients remains alarmingly low, especially in cases of metastatic LUAD(Akhtar-Danesh et al., 2019). The main reasons behind this phenomenon are the metabolic adaptability of tumor cells and immune suppression in the tumor microenvironment, which frequently result in resistance to targeted therapies (Altorki et al., 2019, Ettinger et al., 2017). Therefore, it is crucial to explore novel targeted therapeutic agents for LUAD in order to combat current drug resistance and improve patient survival.

The Monascus-fermented products show great potential as a source for exploring targeted agents, as they have been proven to possess significant pharmacological effects against tumors in animal models (Hsu and Pan, 2012, Kiss et al., 2023, Yang et al., 2014). The fungus Monascus has a long history of producing dietary supplements(Adin et al., 2023, Cheng et al., 2022), some of which are currently being investigated as potential ingredients for tumor prevention (Fujihashi et al., 2018). Monascin, an azaphilonoid pigment found in rice fermented by Monascus purpureu (known as red rice or Hong-Qu)(Wu et al., 2020a), has garnered significant attention due to its diverse range of biological activities, including its ability to inhibit tumor growth (Akihisa et al., 2005a, Chen et al., 2020), reduce inflammation (Lin et al., 2020, Tan et al., 2014), and modulate the immune system(Hsu et al., 2013). Furthermore, monascin specifically targets several disease-associated inflammatory factors such as NF-κB, IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and COX2(Shi and Chen, 2020) that contribute to tumor angiogenesis and metastasis.

Despite the advancements made in the development of monascin for tumor treatment, the identification of its therapeutic target remains a significant bottleneck in early-stage drug discovery(Ashrafizadeh et al., 2021, Farha and Brown, 2016). High-throughput genome sequencing has emerged as a valuable approach for exploring potential targets for tumors through analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)(Kinker et al., 2020). By utilizing DEG data, several target genes associated with LUAD can be identified at the cellular level(Kim et al., 2020). Intrigued by the challenges associated with identifying therapeutic targets, we performed mRNA-sequencing analysis on LUAD A549 cells treated with monascin. However, it is important to note that the results of this in vitro assay may provide a comprehensive list of DEGs for monascin but not all of them can be directly extrapolated to humans (Isgut et al., 2018).

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data are widely recognized as the largest and most commonly used public resource in human cancer research. This invaluable resource provides comprehensive genomic sequencing and copy number variation datasets representing over 11,000 individuals across more than 30 different types of cancer(Wang et al., 2016a). Increasing evidence suggests that gene signatures at the mRNA level hold significant potential for predicting tumor prognosis(Liu et al., 2021b). The comprehensive exploration of tumor gene signatures from TCGA has demonstrated its efficacy as a proficient approach in identifying therapeutic targets, thereby providing valuable guidance for the discovery of novel targeted agents (Kotol et al., 2023). By visualizing gene expression data alongside clinical features, researchers can gain insights into the impact of genomic alterations on phenotypes. Additionally, integrating Kaplan–Meier survival analysis based on prognostic gene signatures (Yang et al., 2023) facilitates the identification of therapeutic targets.

To further our ongoing investigation into the potential targets of monascin in LUAD, we will employ high-throughput RNA-sequencing and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis to identify DEGs in A549 cells treated with monascin. Subsequently, utilizing LUAD data, both univariate and multivariate analyses will be conducted to pinpoint two candidate genes. Additionally, molecular docking studies will be undertaken to gain valuable insights into the potential therapeutic targets in LUAD, which will then be validated through Western blotting.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

The celecoxib (batch number orb322937) was purchased from Biorbyt (Wuhan, China). Deionized water used throughout the study was purified on a Milli-Q system (Millipore, Bedford, USA). All other reagents were of analytical grade and were obtained from Sinopharm Beijing Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd., China.

Isolation of monascin

The crude pigments extracted from rice fermented with Monascus purpureus were separated using ethanol and purified through silica gel column chromatography (200–300 mesh, Qingdao Ocean Chemical Co., Ltd, China)(Hsu et al., 2010). The yield of the extracted monascin was approximately 0.008%. Monascin (batch number 2020060, purity greater than 98%) was obtained through the recrystallization method (Xu et al., 2024).

High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) for purity analysis

The purity analysis of monascin was conducted using a Waters ACQUITY TUV system equipped with an Agilent C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, inner diameter 5 μm). The separation was achieved by employing the following elution gradient and mobile phase composition: 0–20.0 min, 60 – 90 % B; 20.0–20.1 min, 90 – 60 % B; and finally, from 20.1 to 25.0 min, maintaining a constant concentration of mobile phase B at 60 % (where mobile phase A refers to water and mobile phase B refers to acetonitrile), all operated at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The detection wavelength was set at a specific vale of λ = 220 nm for monitoring purposes during the analysis process. Each injection consisted of an accurately measured volume of sample solution equaling to 5.0 μL.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments

The NMR experiments were performed on a Bruker AVANCE III HD 400 NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Switzerland). 1H and 13C NMR resonance frequencies were set at 400 and 100 MHz, respectively. Chemical shifts are reported in parts per million (δ, ppm) relative to the signal of tetramethylsilane (TMS), which was used as an internal reference. Peak multiplicity in the 1H NMR spectra is denoted by s (singlet), d (doublet), t (triplet), m (multiplet), and dd (doublet).

Fourier transform infrared spectrum (FT-IR) spectroscopy

The FT-IR spectra were recorded using KBr particles on a Bruker ALPHA II spectrometer. The acquisition range was 4000–400 cm−1 with 10 scans at a resolution of 4 cm−1.

Thermogravimetric (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analyses

Thermogravimetric (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analyses were conducted using a TGA/DSC1 instrument (Mettler-Toledo, Germany). The sample, weighing precisely 6.7 mg, was placed in a sealed aluminum crucible with a pinhole of 40 μL capacity. It was then heated from 30 to 300 °C at a constant ramp rate of 10 °C/min under a dry nitrogen flow of 30 mL/min. The obtained TGA/DSC data were recorded and analyzed using STARe SW 14.0 software.

Cell lines and cultures

The human LUAD cell line (A549) and the human breast cancer cell line (MCF-7) were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co. Ltd (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in RPMI1640 (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Stock solutions of monascin (1000 μg/mL) were prepared using dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich), which was then diluted with the cell culture medium to achieve desired concentrations (final DMSO concentration ≤ 0.1%).

Cell viability assay

The cell viability of monascin and celecoxib was assessed using the standard colorimetric assay of EnoGeneCell™ Counting Kit-8 (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). Briefly, A549 and MCF-7 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well on a plate with 96 wells and incubated for 24h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Subsequently, an equal volume of additional medium containing either the test compound or DMSO control was added to achieve the desired final concentration. The treated cells were further incubated for 48h. Cell viability was determined by comparing the optical density (OD) at 450 nm between test compound-treated cells and DMSO control-treated cells using an MK3 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The cell inhibition rate was calculated according to equation(Wang et al., 2019): cell inhibition rate = [(control well OD − experimental well OD)/control well OD] × 100%. Data analysis was performed using Phoenix WinNonlin software (version 8.1, Certara, L.P., USA) [I = Imax × Conc/(IC50 + Conc), where I represents the cellular inhibition rate; Imax represents the maximum inhibition rate; Conc represents the test compound concentration; IC50 represents the concentration at half of the maximum inhibition rate].

mRNA-sequencing

The A549 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well on 6-well plates and incubated overnight for attachment. Subsequently, the cells were treated with either DMSO control or monascin at a concentration of 3.69 μM for 24 or 48h before RNA isolation. Total cellular RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (TIANGEN, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. Biomarker Technologies (Beijing, China) performed mRNA-sequencing analysis was performed on an Illumina HiSeqTM 4000 platform. Cutadapt software (version 1.10) was used to filter reads containing aptamer contamination, poly-N sequences and poor-quality bases from the raw data. The filtered data were mapped to the Homo sapiens reference genome using a combination of Bowtie2 (version 2.3), STAR (version 2.7) and Cufflink (version 2.2) programs. The reference genome and gene annotation files were downloaded from Ensembl (https://asia.ensembl.org)(Howe et al., 2020). Pairs of clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (GRCh38.p13) using STAR software (version 2.7)(Dobin et al., 2013). After obtaining the final transcriptome, mRNA abundance was analyzed by StringTie software (version 2.1), quantified as fragments per kilobase per million mappings (FPKM), which takes into account sequencing depth and gene length in read counts estimation for gene expression levels assessment. The DESeq2 R package (version 1.24) provided statistical analysis for differential gene expression between DMSO control and monascin-treated samples. DEGs between sample pairs were identified based on adjusted P (Padj) values < 0.001 and absolute log2 fold change (FC) ≥ 1(Tsoucas et al., 2019). Volcano plots were generated using bioinformatics online tool (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn). Heat maps and Venn diagrams were created utilizing Cloud Platform online tool (https://www.ehbio.com/ImageGP).

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis

The A549 cells were treated with either DMSO control or monascin at concentrations of 0.78, 3.13 and 12.50 μM for 48 h before RNA isolation. Total mRNA isolation from the cells was performed using TRIzol as previously described in the mRNA-sequencing process. One microgram of extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript® III RT SuperMix kit (Transgen, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. A total of 19 genes were selected based on RT-qPCR analysis and gene-specific PCR primers were synthesized according to the sequences provided in Table 1. Gene expression quantification was carried out using an ABI PRISM 7500 fast instrument (Applied Biosystems, USA) under the following reaction conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min followed by amplification consisting of 40 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s, and then annealing at 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

LUAD patient-related survival data

The LUAD patient-related survival data were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-based UCSC Xena web data mining platform (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/)(Goldman et al., 2020). The survival data for 490 patients with LUAD included overall survival time, survival status, age, gender, pathological stage (T, N and M), tumor stage, and gene expression level. Univariate, multivariate and Lasso Cox regression analyses were performed on a bioinformatics online web-based data mining platform (http://vip.sangerbox.com)(Shen et al., 2022) to explore the correlation between each variable and overall patient survival. Variables were considered statistically significant at a cut-off of P < 0.05. Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves were applied to evaluate the predictive value of each variable. Patients were categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median value of each variable expression(Liu et al., 2019).

Molecular docking

The potential interaction pattern between monascin and gene-encoded proteins was investigated through molecular docking studies using the LeDock program (https://www.lephar.com/)(Wang et al., 2016b). X-ray crystal structures were obtained from the Research Collaboration for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB) Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/)(Rellos et al., 2010), while homology model structures of the target proteins were downloaded from the fully automated Protein Structure Homology Model Server (https://swissmodel.expasy.org)(Waterhouse et al., 2018). CavityPlus (https://repharma.pku.edu.cn/cavityplus)(Xu et al., 2018) was employed to detect protein cavities and identify potential binding sites on the protein surface based on ligandness and druggability scores. The optimal conformation was determined through clustering analysis with a root mean square deviation (RMSD) threshold of 1.0 Å, and graphical analysis focused on selecting the lowest energy conformation out of 20 docking results. LigPlot+ (version 2.2)(Laskowski and Swindells, 2011) was used to generate two-dimensional ligand-target interaction maps.

Western blot assay

Proteintech (Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co., LTD, Hubei, China) supplied the GPR37 polyclonal antibody (#14820–1-AP, 1:1000) and FAM83A polyclonal antibody (#20618–1-AP, 1:1000). ABclonal Technologies (Wuhan, Hubei, China) provided the β-Actin Rabbit monoclonal antibody High Dilution (#AC026, 1:4000), horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (#AS014, 1:4000), and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (#AS003, 1:4000). Merck Millipore Chemical Technology (Shanghai, China) supplied the polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane with a pore size of 0.45 um (#IPVH00010).

The proteins were extracted from A549 cells after treating them with either DMSO control or monascin at concentrations of 0.78, 3.13, and 12.50 μM for 48 h using a RIPA lysis buffer (#MA0151, Dalian meilune Biotechnology Co., LTD) containing the phosphatase inhibitor Phynomethanesulfonyl fluoride (100 mM) (#MA0001, Dalian meilune Biotechnology Co., LTD). The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein determination kit. Subsequently, the proteins were separated by 4 % − 12 % sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (FuturePAGETM, Nanjing ACE Biotechnology Co., LTD). They were then transferred onto a PVDF membrane and incubated with 5 % albumin bovine (#A600669, Guanzhou Saiguo Biotech Co., LTD) for 2.5h at room temperature. The primary antibody was applied to the PVDF membrane overnight at 4 °C, followed by the secondary antibodies for 2h. Finally, the bands were visualized using a Bio-red Gel imaging system, and their optical density was analyzed using Image J software.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). The student's t-test was used to analyze differences between experimental groups; levels of statistical significance are denoted as * and # P < 0.05; ** and ## P < 0.01. Apart from these assays, specific values are used to express levels of significance in other data analysis.

Results

Purity

The purity of monascin was confirmed by means of HPLC, with a representative chromatogram depicted in Fig. 1a. Monascin exhibited a retention time of 15.46 min and co-eluted with at least one unidentified compound at 15.74 min. HPLC analysis revealed that the purity of monascin exceeded 98%.

Thermal behavior

The TGA/DSC curves for monascin are presented in Fig. 1b. The initial weight loss of 1.12% observed in TGA curve between 0 and 200 °C can be attributed to water evaporation, while the subsequent weight loss of 7.83% before reaching 300 °C may indicate molecular binding of monascin. Additionally, the DSC curve exhibits an endothermic peak at approximately 149.6 °C (− 65.70 J/g), corresponding to monascin's melting point(Inouye et al., 1962). There findings further confirm the high purity of the prepared monascin sample.

Inhibition of cell viability

Monascin has been reported to exhibit antitumor activity through its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects(El-Far et al., 2018). In this study, we aim to further investigate the inhibitory potential of monascin on A549 and MCF-7 cells. As celecoxib has previously demonstrated inhibition of A549 and MCF-7 cells(Abdelgawad et al., 2017), it was selected as a positive control. Fig. 2 illustrates the inhibitory effects of both celecoxib and monascin on tumor cells. As depicted in Fig. 2a and c, the Imax values for celecoxib at 129.17 μM (50 μg/mL) against MCF-7 and A549 cells were found to be 62.0% and 63.9%, respectively; with corresponding IC50 values of 1.66 μM for MCF-7 cells and 2.23 μM for A549 cells. On the other hand, Fig. 2b and d demonstrate that monascin at a concentration of 139.50 μM (50 μg/mL) exhibited an Imax value of approximately 82.3% against MCF-7 (Fig. 2b) as well as an Imax value of around 79.7% against A549 cells; with respective IC50 values measured at 1.55 μM for MCF-7 cells and 2.05 μM for A549 cells. These results obtained from our in vitro viability assay confirmed that monascin had the inhibitory effects on both MCF-7 and A549 cell lines, suggesting that it exhibited comparable potency to celecoxib. Furthermore, this assay provided evidence supporting the potential development of monascin as a promising chemoprotection agent for patients with lung cancer(Akihisa et al., 2005a, Akihisa et al., 2005b).

The inhibitory effects of celecoxib (a and c, circles) and monascin (b and d, triangles) on MCF-7 (a and b) and A549 (c and d) cells (n = 5).

Differentially expressed mRNA genes

The inhibitory effect of monascin was observed after treatment of A549 cells. Consequently, A549 cells were subsequently selected and exposed to 3.69 μM of monascin for mRNA-sequencing analysis. The results of transcriptome sequencing are summarized in Table 2. After normalizing and filtering the mRNA-sequencing data, a total of 48720914, 47632558, 52432450 and 51127326 clean reads were obtained from C24 (24h-DMSO), C48 (48h-DMSO), H24 (24h-monascin), H48 (48h-monascin)-treated A549 cells, respectively. The quality values (Q30) for clean bases ranged from 92.92% to 93.80%. The mRNA-sequencing mapping data for the clean reads are presented in Table 3. The overall mapping rates were approximately 96.06% and 96.4% for the C24 and H24-treated samples, respectively; while approximately 96.08% and 96.24% for the C48 and H48-treated samples, respectively. Finally, a total of 28640 expressed genes were identified, including 28451 known genes as well as 189 novel genes. Additionally, a total of 128231 expressed transcripts were detected, comprising 119278 known transcripts and 8953 novel transcripts.

As depicted in Fig. 3, mRNA-sequencing reveals distinct effects on gene expression between DMSO control and monascin-treated A549 cells. Two volcano plots were generated to visually represent the distribution of DEGs between DMSO control and monascin-treated A549 cells (Fig. 3a and 3b). The heatmap of DMSO control and monascin-treated samples demonstrated highly consistent transcriptional changes (Fig. 3c). The Venn diagrams illustrated unique and overlapping gene expression patterns between DMSO control and monascin treatment groups (Fig. 3d). Upon comparing the C24 and H24 treatment groups (group A), we identified a total of 421 genes (158 up-regulated and 263 down-regulated) in A549 cells (Fig. 3a). Notably, there were 12 up-regulated as well as 29 down-regulated DEGs that overlapped in both comparisons (Fig. 3d and Table 4). By utilizing the high-throughput genome sequencing technique, we have successfully identified significant genomic alterations influenced by monascin treatment. This underscores the promising potential of mRNA-sequencing analysis in pinpointing targets of monascin.

RT-qPCR validation

To validate the reliability of mRNA-sequencing results, we randomly selected 5 up-regulated and 14 down-regulated genes for quantification using the RT-qPCR method (Fig. 4). Comparative analysis revealed statistically significant alterations in the relative expression levels of NPNT, CRISPLD1, OGDHL, PTCHD4, CDK14 and OR51E2 genes upon treatment with monascin at concentrations of 0.78, 3.13 and 12.50 μM compared to DMSO control-treated genes. Similarly, both 3.13 and 12.50 μM concentrations of monascin resulted in statistically significant changes in the relative expression levels of MAGEA6, EREG, FAP, FAM83A and MME genes. Notably, only at concentrations of either 3.13 or 12.50 μM did monascin cause notable changes in the relative expression levels of GPR37, TMTC1, SNCA, METTL7A, HSPA6 and ADH4 genes. Additionally, a concentration-dependent effect was observed for SLX1B and AQP7 genes upon monascin treatment; however, no statistical significance was found regarding their expression alterations. Overall, RT-qPCR validation supported the plausibility of the mRNA-sequencing results (Table 4) obtained from this study, despite some quantitative discrepancies in relative expression levels.

Identification of candidate genes for monascin in LUAD tissues

To identify candidate genes for monascin in human LUAD tissues, we collected TCGA dataset to evaluate the differential gene expression between tumor and normal tissues (Fig. 5). Initially, we compared the DEGs (Table 4 and Fig. 4) obtained from A549 cells with those in TCGA dataset, revealing that 16 genes were significantly differentially expressed in LUAD tissues (Fig. 5a). In comparison to normal lung tissues, SLX1B, ATF3, MME, NPNT, TMTC1, METTL7A, KRT4, SNCA, and CDK14 were significantly down-regulated in tumor tissues (P < 0.0001), while HSPA6, HMGA2, GPR37, FAP, FAM83A, MAGEA6, and OGDHL displayed substantial up-regulation in tumor tissues (P < 0.0001). Among these up-regulation DEGs, they represent potential genes for the development of natural products or new chemical entity with antitumor properties. Consequently, HMGA2, GPR37, FAP, FAM83A, MAGEA6, and OGDHL were exclusively found to be down-regulated genes upon treatment of A549 cells with monascin (Fig. 5a and Table 4).

Expression of DEGs in LUAD tissues and their correlations with prognosis. Expression levels of 16 significant DEGs in LUAD and normal lung tissues (a). Forest map of univariate Cox regression analysis (b). Forest map of multivariate Cox regression analysis (c). Kaplan–Meier survival curves of patients with FAM83A (d), GPR37 (e), and HMGA2 (f) expression in LUAD tissues.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed on the 16 significant DEGs (Fig. 5b and c), revealing that FAM83A [P < 8.7e-6, Hazard ratio (1.25, 95% CI: 1.13–1.38)] and GPR37 [P < 0.02, Hazard ratio (1.16, 95% CI: 1.02–1.33)], HMGA2 [P < 0.05, Hazard ratio (1.14, 95% CI: 1.00–1.31)] exhibited potential as risk genes for disease progression in LUAD patients. To further validate their association with disease risk, patients were stratified into low and high expression level groups based on the median value of gene expression levels (Fig. 5d to 5f). Kaplan-Meier survival curves demonstrated that LUAD patients with low expression levels of FAM83A [Fig. 5d, P = 2.3e-3, Hazard ratio (1.58, 95% CI: 1.17–2.13)] and GPR37 [Fig. 5e, P = 2.7e-3, Hazard ratio (1.57, 95% CI: 1.17–2.11)] had significantly longer overall survival compared to those with high expression levels; this finding is consistent with previous findings(Richtmann et al., 2019, Xie et al., 2022). However, HMGA2 gene [Fig. 5e, P = 0.15, Hazard ratio (1.24, 95% CI: 0.93–1.67)] did not exhibit a significant difference in overall survival between the low and high expression level groups; this observation aligns with findings reported in human colorectal cancer(Leung et al., 2019) and ovarian cancer(Huang et al., 2018a) studies. The expression of both FAM83A and GPR37 genes demonstrated strong prognostic implications for overall survival in LUAD patients. Therefore, these two genes, FAM83A and GPR37 genes, hold great potential as promising candidate genes for monascin.

Prognosis analysis of candidate genes

To investigate the potential of candidate genes as independent risk predictors for LUAD patients, we initially conducted regression analyses based on overall survival to explore the interdependence between key clinical features and candidate genes (Fig. 6). The key clinical features consisted of Tumor_stage (grade 1 to 9), Pathlogical_T (grade 1 to 9), Pathlogical_N (grade 1 to 5), Pathlogical_M (grade 1 to 5), age (33 to 88 years), and gender (grade 0 and 1). The candidate genes under consideration were FAM83A and GPR37. Subsequently, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed on these eight indicators (Fig. 6a and b), revealing that Tumor stage showed significant association with patient prognosis (P < 3.4e-4 in Fig. 6a and P < 4.2e-9 in Fig. 6b). Moreover, the expression levels of GPR37 gene also demonstrated a strong correlation with patient outcomes (P < 2.0e-3 in Fig. 6a and P < 1.0e-3 in Fig. 6b). Similarly, FAM83A gene expression was found to be an independent risk predictor for LUAD patients (P < 2.5e-3 in Fig. 6a and P < 2.7e-3 in Fig. 6b).

Coefficient profiles of Tumor_stage, FAM83A and GPR37 gene expression in Lasso-Cox model (c). Cross validation diagram for Lasso-Cox model (d). Kaplan–Meier survival curves of LUAD patients in the high-risk and low-risk groups according to the risk score (e). The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression analysis was performed to investigate the impact of Tumor stage, FAM83A and GPR37 gene expression on LUAD prognosis (Fig. 6c to e). A prognostic model was established using data from 290 patients, and the risk score was calculated as follows: Risk score = 0.1877 × Tumor_stage + 0.1638 × FAM83A gene expression + 0.1937 × GPR37 gene expression. According to this formula, the risk score for each patient was calculated, and subsequently, they were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median risk score (Fig. 6e), confirming that a combination of a two-gene signature with Tumor stage has potential in predict overall survival for patients [P < 2.4e-10, Hazard ratio (2.64, 95% CI: 1.93–3.62)]. The high-risk group exhibited significantly reduced overall survival compared to the low-risk group, with respective median survival times of 950 and 2261 days for high-risk and low-risk groups (Fig. 6e). We analyzed the distribution of risk scores, survival status, and three risk features (Fig. 6f), which revealed a significant decrease in patient survival with increasing risk scores; patients in the high-risk group had notably worse outcomes than those in the low-risk group. More importantly, Tumor stage, FAM83A and GPR37 genes demonstrated an up-regulated expression trend with increasing risk scores (Fig. 6f).

A nomogram was constructed incorporating a two-gene signature along with Tumor stage to further predict the outcome of LUAD patients (Fig. 6g). When Tumor stage (P < 4.2e-9), FAM83A (P < 1.0e-3) and GPR37 (P < 2.7e-3) genes were expressed at their average levels, they contributed 41.00, 40.37 and 17.92 points respectively towards the overall score calculation. With a total score of 99.29, it is evident that two-gene signature holds significant prognostic potential for LUAD patients as it contributes the most to overall survival (58.29 points). Hence, it can be concluded that the combination of FAM83A and GPR37 in a two-gene signature exhibits promising prognostic value for LUAD patients.

Molecular docking for FAM83A and GPR37 proteins

The potential of both FAM83A and GPR37 proteins as multiple targets of monascin will be further confirmed through molecular docking analysis. Fig. 7 illustrates the detailed docking interactions between monascin and these two proteins. Initially, we conducted molecular docking to identify the key amino acid residues in GPR37 that interacted with monascin (Fig. 7a and b). The obtained docking results were compared to those of hyocrellin B, which has been previously reported as the bioactive agent of GPR37(Xie et al., 2022). Interestingly, some of the binding sites of monascin (Fig. 7a) overlapped with those hyocrellin B, supporting a similar binding pattern between monascin and hypocrellin B (Fig. 7b). Similar to hyocrellin B, monascin can form hydrogen bonds with Asn512 residue and engage in hydrophobic interactions with Glu508, Tyr446, Leu443, Arg442, Ile421, Thr515, Arg401, Glu397, Glu341, and Ser537 residues with GPR37′s structure. Additionally, monascin also established hydrophobic interactions with Leu345, Asn531 and Gln535 residues. Subsequently, molecular docking was employed to investigate the key amino acid residues in FAM83A that interacted with monascin, in comparison with erlotinib as bioactive agent of FAM83A. The overlapping binding sites of monascin and erlotinib within the structure of FAM83A protein(Richtmann et al., 2019) (Fig. 7c) revealed their identical binding patterns (Fig. 7d). Monascin exhibited hydrogen bonding interactions with Lys243 and Ser537 residues, as well as hydrophobic interactions involving Phe282, Glu242, Phe244 and Gly255 residues for its interaction with FAM83A. In addition to these shared interactions, monascin specifically engaged with FAM83A through the hydrogen bonding interactions involving Arg408 and Thr407 residues, along with hydrophobic interactions involving Ser256 and Pro406 residues.

Effects of monascin on the expression levels of both GPR37 and FAM83A proteins

To further investigate the impact of monascin on both GPR37 and FAM83A, A549 cells were co-incubated with monascin. The concentration-dependent analysis of GPR37 and FAM83A was conducted using a Western blot assay. As depicted in Fig. 8a, monascin significantly suppressed GPR37 at both concentrations of 3.13 and 12.50 μM, leading to a notable deduction in the ratio of GPR37 to β-Actin by 38.15% (3.13 μM-group vs. DMSO-group, P < 0.05) and 40.35% (12.50 μM-group vs. DMSO-group, P < 0.05). Likewise, as shown in Fig. 8b, consistent with previous mRNA expression results, treatment of A549 cells with monascin at both concentrations of 3.13 and 12.50 μM resulted in lower expression levels of FAM83A compared to DMSO-treated cells (3.13 μM-group vs. DMSO-group, P < 0.01; 12.50 μM-group vs. DMSO-group, P < 0.01). These findings indicated that that both GPR37 and FAM83A proteins were potential targets for monascin in LUAD.

Discussion

The metastasis of LUAD cells to distant tissue sites poses a significant clinical challenge, leading to high mortality rates and a substantial proportion of patients experiencing therapeutic failure (Huang et al., 2023). Approximately 70% of diagnosed LUAD patients are at an advanced stage characterized by both local and distant metastasis(Belloni et al., 2023, Hu et al., 2023). Among these patients, they have a median overall survival time of about 8.0 months (95% CI: 6.1 – 9.9) (Xu et al., 2021). Undoubtedly, the metabolic adaptability of tumor cells has been proposed as one of the most significant processes in tumor metastasis (Chen et al., 2023). Moreover, the tumor microenvironment employs immune suppression to facilitate immune evasion, proliferation, and metastasis of tumor cells (de Sauvage et al., 2023). Hence, it is crucial to explore novel targeted therapeutic agents for mitigating LUAD metastasis. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to identify LUAD-associated targets for monascin from extracts derived from Monascus purpureus-fermented rice.

In China, Monascus purpureus is the predominant species used for the production of Monascus-fermented red rice(Pharmacopoeia committee of the People's Republic of China, 2020). Throughout history, ancient Chinese employed Monascus-fermented red rice as a natural food coloring agent(Huang et al., 2018b, Su et al., 2005). Recently, extracts derived from Monascus-fermented red rice have been investigated as potential chemotherapy agents for treating tumors due to their pigments such as ankaflavin, monascin, and rubropunctatin(Cheng and Pan, 2016, Zheng et al., 2016). Among these pigments, monascin exhibits certain antitumor and anti-inflammatory properties(Hsu et al., 2014). Consequently, several recent studies have focused on investigating the antitumor effects of Monascus metabolites. The fermented red rice contains a mixture of six major substances including orange pigments (monascorubrin and rubropunctatin), red pigments (monascorubramine and rubropunctamine), as well as yellow pigments (ankaflavin and monascin)(Gao et al., 2013, Wu et al., 2020a). However, there are limited reports available regarding the biological targets of these pigments against tumors. Kung-Yen et al.(Chen et al., 2020) demonstrated that monascin prevented breast cancer metastasis while Ruei-Lan et al.(Tsai et al., 2011) indicated that red mold rice mitigated oral carcinogenesis in Hamsters. Here we have demonstrated that monascin, one of the major isolated pigments in our study, has the potential to act as a targeted therapeutic agent for GPR37 and FAM83A. This compound effectively suppresses the proliferation of LUAD A549 cells in vitro.

In the mRNA-sequencing study, the significant DEGs of A549 cells in response to monascin treatment (Table 4) have the potential to serve as predictive, prognostic, and therapeutic biomarkers for cancer patients. For instance, alcohol dehydrogenase 4 (ADH4), a member of the ADH family, exhibits high expression levels and may serve as a more reliable prognostic biomarker for patients with non-small cell lung cancer(Wang et al., 2018). Heat shock protein family A member 6 (HSPA6), belonging to the HSP70 family, may act as a cytoprotective factor associated with cancer(Sojka et al., 2021). Membrane metallo-endopeptidase (MME), a key zinc-dependent metallopeptidase in natriuretic peptide and kinin systems, is strongly linked to poor overall survival in patients with non-small cell lung cancer(Leithner et al., 2014). Fibroblast-activated protein alpha (FAP) is highly expressed in stromal fibroblasts and promotes the proliferation of breast cancer cells(Jia et al., 2018). Aquaporin 7 (AQP7) is considered a novel regulator of breast cancer and predicts overall patient survival(Dai et al., 2020). Methyltransferase-like enzyme 7A (METTL7A), located within the inner retina membrane, has potential as a therapeutic biomarker for breast cancer(Yang et al., 2021). However, it is important to acknowledge that these significant genomic alterations observed in A549 cells cannot be directly extrapolated as predictive, prognostic, and therapeutic effects for LUAD patients in clinical settings. Therefore, an approach is required to identify DEGs found at the cellular level as potential target genes of monascin for LUAD patients.

The field of bioinformatics is widely recognized for its role in storing, analyzing, and utilizing information from multiomics data, which holds immense potential for unraveling complex biological systems(Pathak et al., 2022). The bioinformatics approach integrates various statistical methods, such as univariate and multivariate Cox analyses, LASSO Cox regression analysis, and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, to identify signature genes (Yarlagadda and Giorgio, 2023). Despite significant changes in gene expression of ADH4 (Padjust < 0.0001), HSPA6 (Padjust < 0.0001), MME (Padjust < 0.0001), AQP7 (Padjust < 0.0001), and METTL7A (Padjust < 0.0001) observed upon monascin treatment in A549 cells (Table 4), it is worth noting that neither ADH4 nor AQP7 were down-regulated DEGs, and METTL7A was not an up-regulated DEG in LUAD patients (Fig. 5a); subsequent univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses revealed that neither HSPA6 (P = 0.82) nor MME (P = 0.57) were associated with an increased risk in LUAD patients (Fig. 5b). Conversely, both FAM83A and GPR37 genes have emerged as promising candidate genes influenced by monascin treatment. This is supported by their highly significant levels observed in both A549 cells (Padjust < 0.0001 for both FAM83A and GPR37 genes in Table 4) and LUAD patients (P < 0.0001 for both FAM83A and GPR37 genes in Fig. 5a). Furthermore, it should be noted that the overexpression of both FAM83A and GPR37 genes is associated with an unfavorable prognosis for LUAD patients(Chen et al., 2024, Liang et al., 2023, Liu et al., 2022).

Identification of signature genes with significantly increased expression in LUAD cells is crucial for overcoming resistance to current targeted therapeutic drugs. The upregulation of FAM83A gene has been observed in various cancer specimens(Liu et al., 2021a, Liu et al., 2022), indicating its potential role as a key regulator in the EGFR pathway and its contribution to the development of resistance against EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (Lee et al., 2012). Overexpression of the FAM83A gene has been associated with early lymph node metastasis and poor overall survival in patients with LUAD(Liu et al., 2022, Richtmann et al., 2019). Similarly, the expression of GPR37 exhibited a significant increase in specimens of human colorectal cancer liver metastasis, which was correlated with an unfavorable prognosis(Zhou et al., 2023). Knockdown of the GPR37 gene hindered neutrophil recruitment within the tumor microenvironment of colorectal cancer liver metastasis, negatively affecting tumorigenesis and suppressing tumor cell proliferation in patients with colorectal cancer(Zhou et al., 2023). Additionally, GPR37 was validated as a prognostic biomarker playing a key role in tumor progression and its upregulation is associated with a poor prognosis for LUAD patients(Xie et al., 2022). Clearly, both the FAM83A and GPR37 genes are involved in multiple regulatory feedback loops that govern tumor metastasis. Therefore, it can be observed that monascin targets both the signature genes of FAM83A and GPR37, potentially altering LUAD metastasis.

The current study has several limitations that need to be acknowledged. First and foremost, our investigation primarily focused on data from LUAD, so caution should be exercised when applying our findings to other types of cancer. Secondly, it is crucial to externally validate the prognostic effects of dual gene signatures in conjunction with a single clinical feature using additional independent cohorts. Lastly, due to the relatively low bioavailability (Wu et al., 2020b), we did not conduct any in vivo experiments in this study.

Conclusions

This study establishes the foundation for future optimization of the monascin scaffold in order to enhance binding potency and improve drug-like properties as a potential chemoprevention agent. Moreover, the identification of novel targets will facilitate the development of new agents to overcome the resistance observed with current targeted therapies for LUAD treatment. Both FAM83A and GPR37 proteins are considered promising targets for prediction, prognosis and treatment of LUAD. Undoubtedly, the identification of these potential targets will greatly facilitate future research on the antitumor mechanisms of monascin, thereby paving the way for developing targeted therapies and optimizing its chemical structure.
