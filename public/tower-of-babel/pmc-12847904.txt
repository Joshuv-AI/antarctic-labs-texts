The impact of peer support groups on the psychological well-being of patients with rheumatoid arthritis: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Maryam Davoodifard, Seyyed Abolfazl Vagharseyyedin, Zeinab Saremi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with rheumatoid arthritis have higher rates of mental health problems and lower levels of psychological well-being compared to the general population. This study examines the impact of these factors on the psychological well-being of patients with rheumatoid arthritis. In the trial conducted in 2024, a total of 78 patients were enrolled. They first completed a demographic characteristics form and Ryff’s Psychological Well-being Scale. Subsequently, the patients were randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the control group. The control group received routine care, while the intervention group participated in a peer support group program consisting of 1.5-h weekly sessions over a period of six weeks. These sessions were conducted using the social media platform BALE. The data collection tool was completed by participants immediately after the intervention and again two months after its completion. Data analysis included chi-square, Mann–Whitney, and Friedman tests, as well as Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction. A significantly higher score of psychological well-being was observed in the intervention group compared to the control group at the posttest (P < 0.001) and follow-up test (P < 0.03). In terms of aspects of psychological well-being, participants in the intervention group had significantly higher scores on environmental mastery (P = 0.008), positive relations (P = 0.03), self-acceptance (P = 0.007), and personal growth (P = 0.03) compared to patients in the control group at the posttest. Moreover, scores for environmental mastery (P = 0.002) and self-acceptance (P = 0.01) were significantly higher in the intervention group than in the control group at the follow-up assessment. Peer support groups can be a helpful tool for improving the psychological well-being of individuals with rheumatoid arthritis. To further enhance the long-term benefits of these groups, the implementation of a booster program is recommended. Future research could explore and compare the impact of various delivery approaches for peer support groups, such as hybrid models, on the psychological well-being of patients with rheumatoid arthritis.Trial registration This trial is registered on the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials website with the identifier IRCT20140519017756N49 (Date: 14/10/2023).Keywords: Peer group, Psychological well-being, Rheumatoid arthritis

Background

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is the most prevalent inflammatory joint disorder, significantly affecting patients’ quality of life and their capacity to engage in societal activities1. The pathogenesis of RA is regarded as a multifaceted interplay of genetic, epigenetic, and environmental influences, many of which are not yet fully comprehended2. In 2020, it was estimated that RA impacted 17.6 million individuals globally, with a worldwide age-adjusted prevalence of 0.21%, according to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study. This figure represents a 14.1% increase compared to the prevalence recorded in 19903,4.

Patients with RA have higher rates of mental health problems compared to the general population. For example, Vestergaard et al. (2024) found a high prevalence of anxiety and depression symptoms among patients with RA5. Consequently, the recently published guidelines for self-management in rheumatic and musculoskeletal diseases by the European Alliance for Associations of Rheumatology highlight the need for regular mental health assessments throughout the disease course6.

The symptoms and stressors associated with RA can lead to adverse changes in both professional and personal life, resulting in feelings of isolation, deprivation, and the loss of meaningful activities, which in turn can negatively affect psychological well-being (PWB). As a result, individuals with RA frequently experience symptoms of depression and anxiety7–9. In contrast, experiencing high levels of PWB has been linked with a range of health outcomes, including improved subjective health, a decrease in sleep problems, and lower inflammation levels10.

According to Ryff (2013), PWB reflects the level to which individuals perceive their lives as meaningful, purposeful, and directed (purpose in life); their perception of living in alignment with their own beliefs (autonomy); the degree to which they are utilizing their talents and potential (personal growth); their ability to handle life circumstances effectively (environmental mastery); the quality of their relationships with significant others (positive relationships); and their understanding and acknowledgment of themselves, including recognition of personal limitations (self-acceptance)11.

It is notable that the association between RA symptoms and psychological distress is bidirectional. Previous research suggests that psychological factors such as mental health and well-being can influence disease activity, pain processing, and quality of life in patients with RA12. Thus, promoting PWB may be a cost-effective approach for the long-term management of RA. An enhanced PWB can prevent higher levels of disease activity in the future, thereby reducing the necessity for more aggressive therapeutic strategies13.

Considering the potential of peer support groups, it seems that participation in these groups may be a useful tool in promoting PWB in patients with RA. The concept of peer support generally refers to the provision of social and emotional support by individuals who share similar experiences and characteristics with those they are helping. Peer supporters may be either volunteers or individuals who receive financial compensation for their role14. The fundamental principle of peer support is to connect individuals with others who have faced similar life events. Furthermore, these peers can offer guidance rooted in their personal experiences, rather than relying solely on theoretical knowledge15.

Social support is recognized as a crucial mechanism underpinning the benefits of peer support16. The benefits of social support operate through two mechanisms: buffering against the negative impacts of stress and promoting positive health behaviors17. It is anticipated that receiving social support is linked to enhancing specific dimensions of SWB, particularly positive relationships and environmental mastery. These improvements may subsequently contribute to personal growth.

Bandura’s self-efficacy theory proposes a second mechanism. According to Bandura, self-efficacy can be developed through several ways, such as observing role models18. Therefore, it is plausible that observing peers as role models may lead to enhancements in the environmental mastery and autonomy dimensions of SWB.

Lastly, social comparison theory suggests another mechanism underpinning the benefits of peer support. Social comparison refers to the process of relating information about other individuals to the self19. Finding similarities between the self and others is one way to utilize this information. Additionally, social comparison is a process by which an individual can form a sense of identity within a group of similar others16. It can be inferred that this process may contribute to enhanced self-acceptance and positive relationships, which are important dimensions of SWB.

Nevertheless, the impact of peer support groups on mental health outcomes such as PWB is still inconsistent. Geertz et al. (2023), for instance, conducted a systematic review and found that supportive and psychological peer-group interventions are beneficial for children and adolescents with parents who have cancer. The authors concluded that these interventions can lead to improved PWB among participants20. Contrary to this finding, a recent meta-analysis found limited support for positive mental health outcomes following structured community-based friendship groups including peer support groups21.

Notably, there is scarce and inconsistent evidence in the literature regarding the impact of peer support groups on the health outcomes of patients with RA. One of the relevant studies was conducted by Lopez-Olivo et al. (2022). They found that the participation of patients with RA in an online social network using Facebook® led to higher peer support satisfaction among participants. However, this did not result in a reduction in negative emotions such as depression and anxiety22. In another study, Shadick et al. (2018) described the feasibility of a telephone peer support program among RA patients. They concluded that the participants felt less alone, calmer, and less sad than patients receiving standard care. However, after 6 months, non-significant improvements were observed in study outcomes such as fatigue, pain, self-efficacy, functional status, flare frequency, and medication adherence23. Ultimately, in a narrative review of literature by Karp et al. (2023), the authors found that peer support interventions are generally associated with positive impacts on health-related quality of life metrics among patients with RA. However, these differences are not always statistically significant24. These varying findings can be attributed to contextual factors, such as cultural background, that influence how patients feel about diseases and perceive peer support services25. On the other hand, these discrepancies can be explained, in part, by the format of peer support, which may impact its effectiveness26.

To the best of our knowledge, there is a lack of research investigating the effectiveness of peer support groups in enhancing the PWB of individuals with RA. On the other hand, different cultural contexts influence how people give and receive support from others25. Therefore, findings derived from studies conducted in countries with different cultural backgrounds may not be generalizable to populations, such as Iranian RA patients. Additionally, previous interventions focusing on peer support have been characterized by lengthy duration (e.g., 26 weeks)22 or one-to-one delivery methods23. These features may make the above-mentioned interventions difficult to deliver.

To address the issues mentioned above and narrow the existing gaps in peer support group outcomes among patients with RA, this study aimed to examine the impact of six-week peer support groups on the PWB of patients with RA.

Methods

The aim, design, and setting of the study

To address the issues mentioned above and narrow the existing gaps in peer support group outcomes among patients with RA, this two-arm parallel randomized controlled trial was designed to examine the impact of these groups on the PWB of patients with RA. The accessible population for the current study included all patients with RA referred to the rheumatology clinic affiliated with Birjand University of Medical Sciences in 2024.

The characteristics of participants and description of materials

Drawing on a prior study by Saadat et al. (2020)27, the mean difference formula was utilized to determine the sample size required for the current study. Taking into account m1 = 56.41, m2 = 62.21, SD1 = 9.17, SD2 = 8.31, α = 0.05, β = 0.8, and accounting for a 15% drop-out rate, a total of 39 patients were planned for each group. Reluctance or refusal to continue participation was considered an exclusion criterion. Sampling was performed in January 2024. Following the completion of data collection tools (T0), a research assistant randomly assigned selected patients to either the intervention group (39 individuals) or the control group (39 individuals). Simple block randomization with variable block sizes was conducted using an online generator, and allocation concealment was achieved via sealed opaque envelopes (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of study number of patients.

In the current study, demographic and clinical information was collected using a designated form, which included gender and age, occupation type, marital status, education level, duration of disease, family income adequacy for living expenses, joint involvement, RA treatment, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), rheumatoid factor (RF), C-reactive protein (CRP), and antinuclear antibodies (ANA) positivity. The Disease Activity Score using 28 joint counts (DAS28) was used to assess disease activity in patients. It is calculated from four components: tender joint count, swollen joint count, visual analogue scale (VAS) score of the patient’s global health, and the laboratory parameter ESR28. The Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale-21 Items (DASS-21) was utilized to assess depression, anxiety, and stress. It consists of three subscales: depression, anxiety, and stress. Each subscale consists of seven questions graded on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 to 3 (0 = “Did not apply to me at all,” to 3 = “Applied to me very much, or most of the time”)28. This scale has been used among patients with rheumatoid arthritis in various countries, including Iran and has shown good validity and reliability29,30. In this study, The Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.81, 0.79, and 0.76 for the depression, anxiety, and stress subscales, respectively, indicating good reliability.

The PWB was assessed using the 18-item Persian version of Ryff’s PWB scale. This self-report tool includes of 18 items that evaluates six aspects of PWB: autonomy, environmental mastery, positive relations with others, self-acceptance, personal growth, and purpose in life. Respondents rate each item using a 6-point Likert scale, where 1 indicates strong disagreement and 6 indicates strong agreement. Therefore, the total score can range from 18 to 108, with higher scores indicating a higher level of well-being31,32. This scale has been utilized in numerous studies in Iran and its psychometric properties have been deemed acceptable32–34. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha for subscales ranged from 0.70 to 0.76, and that of the total scale was 0.78.

Intervention

The intervention group was randomly divided into four groups: three groups of 10 patients and one group of 9 patients. The same leader was appointed for all intervention groups. In the process of selecting a leader for the groups, specified criteria were considered. These criteria encompassed having strong verbal and non-verbal communication skills, shared experiences with group members, and the ability to effectively manage group sessions. Considering these criteria, the rheumatologist recommended a 47-year-old woman who had been living with RA for approximately 7 years and had been using methotrexate for the past 5 years.

A two-hour training session supervised by the research team was held for the leader. During this session, the nature of the leader’s role and responsibilities in peer support groups were highlighted and effective communication methods were emphasized. The main responsibilities of the leader included introducing topics for each session, guiding participants to share their experiences related to the identified topics, preventing marginalization within the sessions, encouraging shy, quiet, and untalkative group members to express their individual experiences, and summarizing the topics discussed. Additionally, the leader provided guidance to group members who had question.

The intervention consisted of a 1.5-h weekly session over six weeks. Sessions were delivered through the social media platform BALE, which is an Iranian social network. All sessions were supervised by two research team members (MD and SAV). The topics for each session were chosen based on a review of existing literature22, consideration of the primary concerns expressed by participants, and in collaboration with the rheumatologist (ZS); (Table 1).

The control group received routine care. At the end of six weeks (T1) and two months after intervention completion (T2), data collection tool was completed again by both the intervention group and the control group. Follow-ups were conducted in May 2024.

Statistical analysis

The data was analyzed using SPSS software (version 26). An intention-to-treat approach was used in this study. Descriptive statistics (e.g., mean, standard deviation, and frequency) were utilized for data description. The Chi-square and Mann–Whitney U tests were employed to compare demographic characteristics between two groups, including gender, marital status, level of education, occupation type, age, and duration of the disease. Due to the non-normal distribution of PWB scores and its aspects, the Mann–Whitney U test was conducted to compare scores of PWB and its aspects at each measurement time point between the two groups. The Friedman test was used to evaluate changes in PWB scores within each group across three time points. Significant results from the Friedman test were followed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction. For the Mann–Whitney, Friedman, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, the rank-biserial correlation coefficients, Kendall’s W coefficients of concordance, and matched rank biserial correlations were estimated, respectively. It is notable that to reduce detection bias during statistical analysis, the data analyst was blinded to group allocation. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered for all analyses.

Results

In total, 39 participants were assigned to the intervention group and 39 to the control group. All patients in the peer support groups completed the intervention. The mean age in the intervention group was 52.69 ± 10.08 years, and in the control group, it was 51.02 ± 10.10 years (P = 0.48). Additionally, the mean duration of the disease was 4.02 ± 3.35 years in the intervention group and 3.61 ± 2.07 years in the control group (P = 0.90). Other differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups were also not significantly different (Table 2).

In terms of aspects of PWB, participants in the intervention group had significantly higher scores on environmental mastery (P = 0.008), positive relations (P = 0.03), and self-acceptance (P = 0.007) compared to patients in the control group at the posttest, all with moderate effect sizes. At posttest, participants in the intervention group showed more personal growth than those in the control group (P = 0.03), with a modest effect size of 0.24. Finally, scores for environmental mastery (P = 0.002, EF = 0.36) and self-acceptance (P = 0.01, EF = 0.28) were significantly higher in patients in the intervention group compared to those in the control group at the follow-up assessment (Table 3).

Further analysis of the changes in PWB scores over the three time points using the Friedman test showed a significant overall improvement in PSW only in the intervention group (P = 0.003), with an effect size of 0.23, indicating a relatively modest effect (EF = 0.23). Subsequent Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction revealed that in the intervention group, the PWB score at posttest was significantly higher than at pretest (P < 0.001), with a small effect of 0.1. Conversely, there were no significant differences between the PWB scores at follow-up compared to pretest (P = 0.03) and between follow-up and posttest (P = 0.19).

Within the intervention group, some aspects of the PWB including positive relations (P = 0.001), personal growth (P = 0.001), and self-acceptance (P = 0.009) exhibited significant changes across the three time points, all with small effect sizes (Table 3). Post hoc analysis indicated that there was a significant increase in positive relations (P < 0.001, EF = 0.15) and personal growth (P = 0.002, EF = 0.10) scores from pretest to posttest. However, at follow-up, both positive relations (P = 0.01, EF = 0.06) and personal growth (P = 0.003, EF = 0.13) scores decreased significantly compared to posttest. Furthermore, self-acceptance demonstrated a significant improvement at posttest compared to pretest (P = 0.003) with a small effect size of 0.1.

In the control group, the Friedman test revealed a statistically significant decrease in environmental mastery (P = 0.01) and purpose in life (P = 0.02) of PWB over time. The effect sizes for these changes were small, as shown in Table 3. Nevertheless, subsequent Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction did not show any pairwise comparisons reaching significance (P > 0.01).

Discussion

The current study aimed to investigate the influence of peer support groups on the PWB of patients with RA. Our findings indicate that patients’ engagement in peer support groups led to a modest improvement in PWB, which was not sustained at follow-up.

Consistent with our findings, Karp and et al. (2023) in a narrative literature review stated that patients living with rheumatic diseases generally view peer support as a favorable and necessary tool for helping manage their conditions24. Better self-management can have a positive effect on PWB in patients with rheumatic diseases35. Also, a study conducted by Shadick et al. (2018) found that a telephone peer support program did not lead to improvements in study outcomes for patients with RA, including fatigue, pain, self-efficacy, and medication adherence. Nevertheless, participants reported several benefits of peer support, such as reduced feelings of isolation and sadness, increased sense of calmness, and enhanced support from their family and friends23. On the contrary, Lopez-Olivo et al. (2022) compared the negative emotions experienced by RA patients who participated in an online Facebook group moderated by peer leaders with those of a control group. They found no differences in negative emotions (i.e., depression, anxiety, and stress) between the two groups over time22. This discrepancy may be partly attributed to sociocultural differences. Lopez-Olivo et al. have acknowledged that most of their patients were educated white women. Thus, their results may not be generalizable22.

In our study, the estimated effect size for improvement in PWS was interpreted based on Cohen’s standard values36. Some researchers believe that these decontextualized criteria are nonsensical in psychological research. In psychology a given effect size that falls in the small range according to Cohen (2013) could indicate a medium effect that is of some explanatory and practical use even in the short run37. When interpreting the estimated effect size, it is necessary that the particular context of participants can be considered38. As expressed previously, many individuals with RA frequently experience negative emotions such as depression and anxiety that dramatically affect their PSW7,9. Thus, even a modest improvement in PSW in patients with RA can be important. Finally, the impact of an experimental research in psychology could in many cases be expected to accumulate into important implications over time even if its effect size is small in any particular instance37.

The enhancement of PWB in this study can be attributed to various factors. Firstly, peer support interventions are highlighted as pivotal in offering social support39. Social support encompasses four key dimensions: informational support (providing information to others), emotional support (offering care, empathy, love, and trust), instrumental support (supplying tangible goods, services, or assistance), and appraisal support (providing information relevant to self-evaluation rather than problem-solving)40. Social support serves as a factor that can alter the impact of stressful life events and is linked to positive health outcomes, such as reduced psychological distress41,42. Therefore, the improvement in PWB can be partially attributed to the benefits of social support.

Given the various aspects of PWB, it seems plausible that the provision of social support can contribute to the improvement of positive relations and personal growth, an assertion that was supported by the results of the present study. It is worth noting that positive relations and personal growth were significantly higher in the intervention group compared to the control group at the posttest assessment. However, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in these aspects of PSW at the follow-up. This lack of difference can be attributed to a decline in positive relations and personal growth from the posttest to the follow-up assessment in the intervention group. Previous research indicates that patients with RA often express feelings of being misunderstood or lacking trust from their significant others, and some even perceive prejudice43. Engaging in peer support groups provides RA patients with a supportive environment where they can voice their concerns and feel understood44, potentially leading to improved positive relationships with others. Also, in line with our finding, Weiss (2004) noted that connecting with other patients who have had similar experiences is a significant and unique opportunity for personal growth for cancer patients. Weiss observed that patients view those who have gone through the trauma of breast cancer as a valuable resource, while they may perceive advice from other sources as trivial and unhelpful45.

Secondly, participants engaged in peer support groups can derive various benefits from providing assistance to their peers, as outlined in the helper therapy principle. This principle posits that providing help to others can enhance one’s interpersonal competence through positively impacting the lives of others, while also boosting one’s sense of self and personal value through the social approval received from those they have helped46. One can assume that peer support through the helper therapy principle will influence various aspects of PSW, such as positive relations and self-acceptance. In this study, a significant increase in self-acceptance from pretest to posttest in the intervention group was another finding. Also, participants in the intervention group showed significant improvements in self-acceptance compared to those in the control group at posttest and follow-up assessments. Pohan et al. (2021) determined that social is the most important factor in fostering self-acceptance among older adults residing in nursing homes47. This finding aligns with our research, highlighting the significant influence of peer support groups on enhancing participants’ self-acceptance. Previous research shows that RA patients actually experience feelings of embarrassment and perceive negative judgment from their social environment for utilizing the disease as an excuse to avoid responsibilities48. Peer support groups provide a non-judgmental environment where individuals are encouraged to openly discuss their limitations and concerns. It can be assumed that engaging in these groups is useful to facilitate self-acceptance by helping participants acknowledge and embrace their strengths and weaknesses.

Lastly, researchers believe that peer support groups offer an ideal basis for the social comparison process, through which an individual can form a sense of identity within a group of similar others. This process can help to shape a sense of identity within a group of similar others and can also influence individuals’ evaluation of their abilities16,49. It appears that the social comparison process can have a positive influence on self-acceptance and positive relationships. It is noteworthy that both of these aspects improved in participants of the intervention group over time.

In our research, we found that the impact of peer support groups on PWB in the intervention group was not sustained at follow-up. Furthermore, scores of positive relations and personal growth aspects decreased significantly from immediately after the intervention to follow-up. These findings are partly in line with the study by Sandhu et al. (2013), which found that engagement in a 12-week peer support program led to enhancements in health-related quality of life, coping, and social support among participants, including those with RA, immediately after program completion. Nevertheless, none of these variables showed sustained improvements at the 3-month follow-up assessment50. The lack of sustainable effects at the follow-up assessment in this study can be attributed to various factors such as the absence of continued group interaction, participants’ emotional dependency on temporary support, and lack of booster sessions.

In the present study, the control group showed a decline in purpose in life and environmental mastery over time. However, the pairwise comparisons did not yield any statistically significant differences, suggesting that the observed changes were minimal. Participants in the control group reported significantly lower scores on the environmental mastery aspect than those in the intervention group at both the posttest and follow-up assessment. Previous studies have indicated that functional status, quality of social roles, and negative emotions are associated with purpose in life among individuals with RA51. It is probable that participants in the control group, who were confronted with health-related limitations and concerns, experienced a reduction in their purpose in life score over time.

Regarding environmental mastery, individuals who have difficulty managing everyday affairs, and lack a sense of control over the external world are considered as those with low scores in this domain52. The decrease in environmental mastery observed in the control group may be attributed, in part, to the unmanaged stress stemming from RA symptoms. The presence of frequent and intense stressors can adversely impact perceptions of environmental mastery53.

In summary, the intervention implemented in this study offer valuable insights for clinicians who are attempting to develop peer support programs to improve the mental health of patients with RA. The study’s intervention features, including study design, duration, number of follow-ups, and timing, can serve as a guide for designing effective peer support initiatives. The findings of this study also have important implications for healthcare policy makers. It is recommended that policymakers incorporate education on peer support principles and types into the in-service training programs for clinicians involved in the care of patients with RA. Additionally, healthcare policies should prioritize the provision of sufficient support and resources for interventions that offer ongoing support to individuals with RA.

This study has several limitations. The participants were recruited from a single rheumatology clinic, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to a wider population of patients with RA, given the specific cultural and social context of the participants. Another limitation is the relatively small sample size. To address these limitations, further research should be multicenter and have larger sample sizes. Additionally, the lack of objective outcome measures and the use of self-reported data in this study raise concerns about potential self-reporting bias. For example, social desirability may have influenced participants’ responses to the data collection tool. In future studies, researchers should consider incorporating more objective measures to enhance the robustness of their findings.

Conclusions

Peer support groups can be a beneficial tool to enhance PWB in individuals with RA. To optimize the long-term benefits of such groups, the implementation of a booster program is recommended. Subsequent research endeavors could explore and compare various delivery methods of peer support groups, such as hybrid peer support models, as well as develop longer interventions to assess their impact on the PWB of RA patients.
