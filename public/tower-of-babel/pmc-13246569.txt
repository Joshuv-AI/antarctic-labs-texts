Cuscuta chinensis Lam. extract alleviates ovariectomy-induced osteoporosis by inhibiting VCP-mediated METTL3 protein degradation
Authors: Hua Jiang, Liangyu Ma, Yong Liu, Mufan Li, Jinghua Sun, Junwei Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the therapeutic effects of Cuscuta chinensis Lam. (CCL) on ovariectomy (OVX)-induced osteoporosis (OP) and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, focusing on the VCP-METTL3 axis. The bioactive components of CCL were identified using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry. An OVX-induced OP mouse model was established and treated with CCL. Bone microarchitecture was assessed by micro-CT, and bone metabolism markers were measured by ELISA. In vitro, bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were used to assess osteogenic differentiation (alkaline phosphatase and alizarin red S staining). N6-methyladenosine (m6A) levels were quantified colorimetrically. Protein expression and interactions were analyzed by Western blot, co-immunoprecipitation, and immunohistochemistry. Protein stability and ubiquitination levels were assessed following cycloheximide (CHX) and MG132 treatments. Potential targets were screened via bioinformatics. CCL alleviated bone loss in OVX mice, improving bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), while reducing trabecular separation/spacing (Tb.Sp). It also restored the balance between bone formation and resorption. In BMSCs, CCL enhanced osteogenic differentiation and increased m6A levels by stabilizing METTL3, extending its half-life. Mechanistically, CCL targeted VCP, inhibiting its interaction with METTL3. VCP, in concert with the E3 ligase BARD1, promoted METTL3 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Knockdown of METTL3 attenuated the pro-osteogenic effects of CCL. Conversely, METTL3 overexpression rescued the inhibitory effects of VCP overexpression on osteogenesis. CCL alleviates OP by targeting VCP to inhibit BARD1-mediated METTL3 ubiquitination and degradation, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation. These findings highlight the VCP/METTL3 axis as a potential therapeutic target for OP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10735-026-10846-8.Keywords: Osteoporosis, Cuscuta chinensis Lam. extract, VCP, METTL3, BARD1

Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) is a systemic metabolic bone disorder characterized by low bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture (Adejuyigbe et al. 2023). Its core pathological mechanism involves an imbalance between weakened osteoblastic bone formation and enhanced osteoclastic bone resorption (Aibar-Almazan et al. 2022). In postmenopausal women, estrogen deficiency resulting from declined ovarian function represents the primary trigger for OP (Walker and Shane 2023). Approximately one-third of postmenopausal women experience fragility fractures due to this condition, significantly impairing their quality of life and increasing mortality risk (Calaf-Alsina et al. 2023). Although first-line clinical therapeutics, such as bisphosphonates and calcitonin, can delay bone loss, long-term administration is associated with serious adverse effects, including osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femoral fractures (Reid and Billington 2022; Yang et al. 2025). Moreover, these agents are incapable of fundamentally repairing the compromised bone microstructure (Li et al. 2021). Consequently, identifying alternative therapies that are safe, act via defined mechanisms, and can synergistically promote bone formation constitutes a critical need in current OP research.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has a long history of application in treating bone metabolic diseases (Li et al. 2023a, b; Cai et al. 2025). Cuscuta chinensis Lam. (CCL), a classic TCM herb renowned for tonifying the liver and kidney, replenishing essence and blood, and strengthening tendons and bones, has been found to possess a broad spectrum of biological activities, including anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antiviral, antidepressant, and organ protective effects (Wang et al. 2022). Importantly, CCL has demonstrated in previous studies to ameliorate experimental OP by modulating osteoblast proliferation and differentiation and inhibiting osteoclast activity (Mo et al. 2019; Tao et al. 2021; Li et al. 2025). However, its precise molecular targets and signaling pathways remain elusive, which significantly limits its potential for clinical translation.

In recent years, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification, the most abundant epitranscriptomic mark in eukaryotic mRNAs, has been identified as a key molecular switch regulating bone metabolic homeostasis (Huang et al. 2021). Methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), the core catalytic subunit of the m6A methyltransferase complex, promotes the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) by enhancing the m6A modification levels of osteogenesis-related genes, such as Runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) and collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1) (Li et al. 2023a, b; Sheng et al. 2024; Liang et al. 2025). Notably, METTL3 protein expression has been shown significantly downregulated in ovariectomy (OVX)-induced OP models (Wang et al. 2023a, b); however, the molecular mechanisms underlying this downregulation, particularly regulation at the protein degradation level, have not been reported.

Valosin-containing protein (VCP), an AAA⁺ ATPase, participates in crucial cellular processes such as cell cycle regulation and protein quality control by recognizing ubiquitinated target proteins and facilitating their degradation via the proteasome (Pontifex et al. 2024). Emerging evidence indicates that VCP can influence the pathogenesis of various diseases by modulating the interaction between E3 ubiquitin (Ub) ligases and their substrates, including cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative diseases, and cancers (Wang et al. 2021; Chu et al. 2023; Rakhe and Bhatt 2024). Moreover, a dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry study has reveal bone loss and secondary OP progression in VCP multisystem proteinopathy 1, which is a rare disorder caused by missense variants in the VCP gene (Columbres et al. 2024). Nevertheless, whether VCP participates in METTL3 protein degradation and thereby regulates bone metabolism remains entirely unexplored.

Based on this background, we hypothesize that CCL may target VCP to inhibit VCP-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of METTL3, thereby stabilizing METTL3 protein, upregulating m6A modification levels, and ultimately ameliorating OVX-induced OP. To test this hypothesis, we will first identify the active components of CCL and screen for drug-like compounds. We will then validate the effects of CCL on bone microarchitecture and bone metabolism markers in an OVX mouse model. Finally, employing a combination of in vitro and in vivo experiments, we aim to systematically elucidate the role of the CCL-VCP-METTL3 axis in regulating osteogenic differentiation and mitigating OP. This study seeks to provide novel molecular targets and experimental evidence supporting TCM-based interventions for OP.

Experimental methods

Preparation of CCL extract

The CCL extract was prepared by initially slicing and soaking the starting material. Subsequent extraction was performed three times at 80 °C, with each extraction lasting 1.5 h. The combined liquid extracts were then concentrated under reduced pressure. Following centrifugation to eliminate impurities, the concentrated solution was processed by spray drying to yield fine powder. The CCL extract shows excellent water solubility and can be completely dissolved in sterile double-distilled water or physiological saline at a concentration of up to 200 mg/mL without precipitation or stratification at room temperature.

Chemical composition analysis of CCL

For compositional analysis, 1 mg of the CCL extract was dissolved in methanol (500 μL). The solution was subjected to 30-min ultrasonication followed by 10-min centrifugation (13,000 rpm). The resulting supernatant was collected for liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS), which was conducted within 24 h of preparation using an UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system coupled to a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Separation was achieved on a Thermo Hypersil GOLD C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.9 μm) with a heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source. The injection volume was 5 μL, and the mobile phase, delivered at 0.3 mL/min, consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (eluent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (eluent B). The mass spectrometer was operated under conditions as follows: ion source temperature, 310 °C; capillary temperature, 320 °C; sheath gas flow, 30 arbitrary units; auxiliary gas flow, 10 arbitrary units; and spray voltage set to 3.0 kV for positive mode and 2.8 kV for negative mode. A data-dependent acquisition method was employed with a top-10 precursor selection cycle. Full MS scans were obtained at a resolution of 70,000 over the m/z range of 100–1,500, with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 3e6 and a maximum injection time of 200 ms. Subsequent MS/MS scans were performed at a resolution of 17,500, with an AGC target of 1e5 and a maximum injection time of 50 ms, using a stepped normalized collision energy of 10, 28, and 35 eV. Structural identification of the detected compounds was performed by Hangzhou Yanqu Information Technology Co., Ltd.

Screening of bioactive components

The compounds identified by MS were assigned standardized nomenclature and simplified molecular-input line-entry system (SMILES) notations using the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Subsequently, these SMILES notations were processed in a batch format through the SwissADME database (http://www.swissadme.ch/) to predict key pharmacokinetic properties for each compound. The evaluated parameters included gastrointestinal (GI) absorption, logarithm of the partition coefficient (LogP), topological polar surface area (TPSA), compliance with Lipinski’s rule of five, and bioavailability score. A final screening was applied to select compounds that met the following criteria: adherence to at least three of Lipinski’s rules, high GI absorption, and a molecular weight (MW) of less than 500 daltons.

OP induction and treatment in mice

C57BL/6 mice (female, 8-week-old) were bought from Shulaibao (Wuhan, China). All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions, maintained at a controlled temperature of 20–26 °C, a relative humidity of 50%-60%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. All animal procedures were carried out following the protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of China Medical University (Ethics Number: CMU2021141). Following acclimatization, the mice were randomized into four groups (n = 8/group): Sham, OVX, OVX + CCL-L (low-dose CCL extract), and OVX + CCL-H (high-dose CCL extract). Surgical procedures were performed under gas-induced anesthesia. Mice were positioned in a prone posture on a thermostatically controlled surgical platform (37 °C) to undergo bilateral OVX. The Sham group received an identical surgical intervention, including incision and suturing, but their ovaries were left intact. Starting from the second week post-surgery, the treatment groups received daily oral gavage of CCL extract at a fixed volume of 0.1 mL/10 g body weight. The CCL-L group received CCL extract dissolved in normal saline at a concentration of 10 mg/mL (equivalent to 100 mg/kg), while the CCL-H group received a concentration of 20 mg/mL (equivalent to 200 mg/kg), doses (100 and 200 mg/kg) previously established to be safe and effective in rodent osteoporosis models (Maimaiti et al. 2021), for a duration of three weeks (Mo et al. 2019). The control groups received an equal volume of saline.All mice were euthanized eight weeks after the initial modeling surgery. To evaluate the potential hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity of Cuscuta chinensis Lam. (CCL) extract, liver and kidney tissues were collected for hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and histopathological examination.

Micro-CT

Following fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), the isolated femora from each group were scanned employing a Skyscan 1176 micro-CT instrument (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium). For quantitative analysis of the femoral region, micro-CT data were processed using the official dedicated companion software suite provided by Pingsheng Medical Technology Co., Ltd. (all software modules were version 2.2.14.1). Raw data acquisition was performed using Cruiser software, image reconstruction was carried out with Recon software, and three-dimensional visualization and post-processing of the femoral region were completed using Avatar software. The following microarchitectural parameters were determined: bone volume (BV)/total volume (TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation/spacing (Tb.Sp).

Histological and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Bone tissue samples were subjected to 4% PFA fixation and EDTA decalcification. Subsequently, the specimens were paraffin-embedded and sectioned (thickness 5 μm). The sections were then processed for deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through ethanol. For histological examination, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE; C0105S, Beyotime) and Masson’s trichrome (I774801, Aladdin), mounted with neutral resin, and imaged under a microscope.

IHC staining was performed following a standardized protocol (Shi et al. 2023). Antigen retrieval was conducted on the deparaffinized and rehydrated sections via heating in citrate buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched through incubation with H₂O₂ for 20 min. After blocking non-specific binding sites with 5% BSA, the sections were subjected to overnight incubation (4 °C) with a primary antibody against METTL3 (1:500, ab195352, Abcam). On next day, an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was used. Staining was visualized using a DAB substrate kit (P0203, Beyotime), and the sections were hematoxylin-counterstained before final imaging.

ELISA

Serum levels of bone turnover markers, including C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX-I), tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase 5b (TRAP5b), procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (P1NP), and osteocalcin (OCN), were quantified using specific commercial ELISA kits (CTX-I: ABIN6955118, antibodies-online; TRAP5b: EK248953, Thermo Fisher Scientific; P1NP: EEL220, Invitrogen; OCN: ABIN6958335, antibodies-online) in strict accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols.

Isolation and culture of primary cells

Commercially available human BMSCs (PCS-500–012, ATCC) were cultured in α-MEM (Gibco) added with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin within a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator maintained at 37 °C. To induce osteogenic differentiation, BMSCs (6 × 104 cells/cm2) were seeded in tissue culture-treated plates. The cells were then treated with an osteogenic induction cocktail comprising L-ascorbic acid (50 μg/mL), β-glycerophosphate (10 mM), and dexamethasone (100 nM), in either the presence or absence of the CCL extract.

CCK-8 assay

The potential cytotoxic effects of CCL on BMSCs were assessed using a CCK-8 (HY-K0301, MCE). Following a 24-h treatment with CCL at 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2 mg/mL, the CCK-8 reagent was applied to the culture wells. The plate was subsequently returned to the incubator for an additional 2-h incubation period. The absorbance (450 nm) was then measured using a microplate reader to quantify relative cell viability.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and alizarin red S (ARS) staining

ALP and ARS staining were performed on days 7 and 14, respectively, of the osteogenic differentiation protocol. After fixing the cells with 4% PFA and washing with PBS, the staining procedures were carried out following the manufacturers’ instructions using a commercial ALP staining kit (G1087, Servicebio) and ARS solution (A5533, Sigma-Aldrich). The resulting staining patterns were visualized and documented under a microscope.

Colorimetric quantification of m6A levels in cells

The global m6A levels in RNA were measured using the EpiQuik m6A RNA methylation quantification kit (P-9005–96, Epigentek) via a colorimetric assay. Briefly, total RNA was isolated with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). The extracted RNA was then bound to the wells of a high-binding affinity plate using a specialized RNA binding solution. Subsequent incubations were performed sequentially with an m6A-specific capture antibody followed by a detection antibody. After the addition of a color-developing substrate, the absorbance (450 nm) was recorded with a microplate reader for determining the relative m6A methylation levels.

RT-qPCR assay

Following total RNA extraction from cultured cells, RT-qPCR was carried out with the SuperScript™ III Platinum™ one-step qRT-PCR kit (11,732,088, Invitrogen). The target gene mRNA expression was normalized to β-actin, which served as an internal control, and relative quantification was conducted based on the 2−ΔΔCt method. The nucleotide sequences of the specific primers used for amplification included: METTL3 forward, 5’-CTATCTCCTGGCACTCGCAAGA-3’, and reverse, 5’-GCTTGAACCGTGCAACCACATC-3’; COL1A1 forward, 5’-GATTCCCTGGACCTAAAGGTGC-3’, and reverse, 5’-AGCCTCTCCATCTTTGCCAGCA-3’; RUNX2 forward, 5’-CCTGAACTCTGCACCAAGTCCT-3’, and reverse, 5’-TCATCTGGCTCAGATAGGAGGG-3’; BGLAP forward, 5’-CGCTACCTGTATCAATGGCTGG-3’, and reverse, 5’-CTCCTGAAAGCCGATGTGGTCA-3’; ACTB forward, 5’-CACCATTGGCAATGAGCGGTTC-3’, and reverse, 5’-AGGTCTTTGCGGATGTCCACGT-3’.

Western blot (WB) analysis

Cellular proteins were lysed using RIPA buffer (20101ES60, Yeasen) supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor and 1% phosphatase inhibitor. The supernatant was collected for protein concentration determination using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (P0009, Beyotime). After separation by 12% SDS-PAGE, protein samples were transferred onto PVDF membranes (FFP39, Beyotime). Following 1-h blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were subjected to an overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies: METTL3 (1:1,000, ab195352, Abcam), COL1A1 (1:1,000, 72,026, Cell Signaling Technology), RUNX2 (1:1,000, 82,636–2-RR, Proteintech), OCN (1:1,000, 16,157–1-AP, Proteintech), VCP (1:1,000, ab109240, Abcam), BARD1 (1:1,000, A300-263A-T, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and β-actin (1:5,000, ab8227, Abcam). The following day, after washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:10,000, ab205718, Abcam) for 1 h. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using a hypersensitive ECL detection kit (HY-K2005, MedChemExpress). The relative protein expression was determined by densitometric analysis with β-actin serving as the loading control.

Lentiviral infection

To achieve genetic manipulation in BMSCs, lentiviral vectors carrying short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting METTL3 (sh-METTL3) and BARD1 (sh-BARD1) for gene knockdown, as well as overexpression vectors for VCP (oe-VCP), BARD1 (oe-BARD1), and METTL3 (oe-METTL3), were obtained from VectorBuilder Inc. The detailed nucleotide sequences of sh-METTL3 are summarized in Table S2. Prior to treatment, BMSCs were harvested and seeded into culture dishes, followed by infection with the corresponding lentiviruses. After 48 h of incubation, the cells were collected for subsequent experimental analyses.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cellular lysates were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer. Following centrifugation of the lysates at high speed and 4 °C, the resulting supernatants were collected and incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against VCP (1:100, ab109240, Abcam), BARD1 (1:200, A300-263A-T, Thermo Fisher Scientific), or a control normal IgG (02–6102, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, agarose beads were added to each antibody-lysate mixture and incubation continued for an additional 4 h at 4 °C. The agarose bead-antibody-protein complexes were then collected, boiled in loading buffer, and subjected to SDS-PAGE to detect the enrichment of target proteins within the immunoprecipitated complexes.

Protein stability assay

To assess the impact on METTL3 protein stability, cells from different experimental groups were treated with 50 μg/mL cycloheximide (CHX; S7418, Selleck). Cellular proteins were harvested at specified time points (0, 0.5, 1, and 2 h) following CHX administration. The extracted proteins were then subjected to WB analysis to evaluate the changes in METTL3 protein levels.

Ubiquitination assay

To evaluate the ubiquitination levels of METTL3 across different experimental groups, cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection and lysed using RIPA buffer. Following centrifugation of the cellular lysates, the supernatants were subjected to IP using an anti-HA antibody (1:200, 81,290–1-RR, Proteintech). The immunoprecipitated proteins were then analyzed via WB with antibodies specific for His-tag (1:200, ab9108, Abcam) and HA-tag (1:5000, 81,290–1-RR, Proteintech) to detect ubiquitinated METTL3 species.

Data analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software. The results were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Two-group comparisons were conducted applying the t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were carried out via one/two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Experimental results

Bioactive components of CCL

A systematic analysis of the chemical composition of CCL was conducted using high-resolution LC–MS (HRLC-MS), with the resulting base peak chromatograms presented in Fig. 1. Following a screening process for absorption capacity and drug-likeness, 46 chemical constituents were identified (Table S1). These bioactive components are all natural compounds, primarily encompassing flavonoids, phenolic acids, fatty acids, and their derivatives.

CCL alleviates OVX-induced OP

To evaluate the therapeutic effect of CCL on OP, an OVX-induced OP mouse model was established and treated with CCL. The impact of CCL on bone microstructure was assessed by micro-CT scanning of the femur. The results demonstrated that OVX induction significantly reduced BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th, while markedly increasing Tb.Sp in mice. Notably, these OVX-induced alterations were partially reversed by CCL treatment (Fig. 2A-B). HE staining revealed substantial bone loss in OVX mice, which was improved following CCL treatment (Fig. 2C). Concurrently, Masson’s staining indicated impaired collagen organization in the OVX group, which was restored upon CCL intervention (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, compared to the Sham group, serum levels of bone resorption markers (CTX-I and TRAP5b) were elevated, while levels of bone formation markers (P1NP and OCN) were decreased. CCL treatment significantly increased osteogenic marker levels and reduced bone resorption marker levels (Fig. 2E). These findings indicate that CCL extract alleviates OVX-induced OP.

CCL stabilizes METTL3 to promote bone formation

We next evaluated the effect of CCL on osteogenic differentiation in vitro. Results from the CCK-8 assay indicated no cytotoxic effects of CCL on BMSCs at concentrations ranging from 0 to 1 mg/mL (Fig. 3A). Based on this, concentrations of 0.5 and 1 mg/mL CCL were selected for subsequent in vitro experiments. ALP and ARS staining performed on days 7 and 14 of osteogenic induction revealed that CCL-treated cells exhibited enhanced ALP activity and increased mineralized matrix deposition, respectively (Fig. 3B-C). Given the established correlation between OP progression and reduced m6A modification accompanied by downregulation of METTL3 (Xiao et al. 2025), we investigated the influence of CCL on global m6A levels and METTL3 expression in BMSCs. CCL treatment significantly increased the total m6A content in BMSC RNA (Fig. 3D). Data from RT-qPCR and WB analyses demonstrated that CCL primarily upregulated the protein expression of METTL3 in BMSCs, without significantly altering its mRNA levels (Fig. 3E-F). To assess the impact of CCL on METTL3 protein stability, cells were treated with CHX to inhibit new protein synthesis. This experiment showed that CCL treatment prolonged the protein half-life of METTL3 (Fig. 3G). IHC staining of bone tissue sections yielded consistent results, indicating that METTL3 expression, which was decreased in OVX mice compared to the Sham group, was restored following CCL treatment (Fig. 3H). Furthermore, no obvious histopathological damage, inflammatory infiltration, or structural abnormalities were observed in the liver or kidney tissues of mice in the Sham, OVX, OVX + CCL-L (100 mg/kg), or OVX + CCL-H (200 mg/kg) groups. Collectively, these results demonstrated that CCL extract at the administered doses did not induce obvious hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity in OVX mice (Fig. S1).

METTL3 knockdown attenuates the pro-osteogenic effects of CCL

To investigate the role of METTL3 in mediating the pro-osteogenic effects of CCL, METTL3 expression was knocked down in BMSCs using shRNAs. Transduction efficiency was confirmed by both RT-qPCR and WB analysis (Fig. 4A-B). The shRNA construct yielding the highest knockdown efficiency (sh-METTL3-1#) was selected for all subsequent experiments. ALP staining demonstrated a marked reduction in positive staining upon METTL3 knockdown, indicating diminished early osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 4C). Concurrently, ARS staining revealed a significant decrease in the formation of mineralized nodules in the METTL3-knockdown groups (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, RT-qPCR and WB analyses indicated that METTL3 silencing led to a substantial downregulation in the expression levels of key osteogenic markers, including COL1A1, RUNX2, and OCN, at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4E-F).

VCP is a potential target of CCL

To elucidate the mechanism of action of CCL in OP, potential targets of its active components were predicted using the SwissTargetPrediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), including all targets with a probability greater than 0. OP-related targets were retrieved from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) with a relevance score threshold greater than 1. Additionally, METTL3-interacting proteins were collected from the BioGRID database (https://thebiogrid.org/) (Fig. S2A). An intersection of these three sets was performed to identify potential targets through which CCL modulates METTL3 in the context of OP (Fig. S2B). A drug-component-disease target network was subsequently constructed using Cytoscape software (version 3.10.2), revealing 24 active components corresponding to 12 potential targets (Fig. S2C). Given that CCL primarily modulates METTL3 protein stability, we analyzed the core targets and found that VCP mediates the degradation of polyubiquitinated substrates by the proteasome (Meyer and Weihl 2014). We hypothesized that CCL might inhibit the Ub-mediated degradation of METTL3 by targeting VCP. Co-IP assays confirmed an interaction between VCP and METTL3, which was attenuated upon CCL treatment (Fig. 5A). Overexpression of VCP led to a decrease in METTL3 protein levels, an effect that was reversed by the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 5B). To further clarify the role of VCP in regulating METTL3 stability, we assessed the impact of VCP overexpression on METTL3 protein half-life. The results showed that METTL3 protein half-life was shortened following VCP overexpression (Fig. 5C). Subsequently, we measured the ubiquitination level of METTL3 and found that VCP increased METTL3 ubiquitination, which was suppressed by CCL treatment (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, assessment of METTL3 K48-linked ubiquitination showed a corresponding trend (Fig. 5E).

VCP promotes METTL3 ubiquitination and degradation through BARD1

When associated with ubiquitination enzymes, VCP can facilitate the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of target proteins (Ahlstedt et al. 2022). To identify the relevant E3 Ub ligase, VCP-interacting proteins were retrieved from the BioGRID database (Fig. S3A), while potential E3 Ub ligases for METTL3 were obtained from UbiBrowser 2.0 (http://ubibrowser.bio-it.cn/ubibrowser_v3) (Fig. S3B). Intersection analysis of these two datasets identified two candidate E3 ligases: BARD1 and SYVN1 (Fig. S3C). BARD1 was prioritized for further investigation due to its higher prediction confidence score (Fig. S3D). To experimentally validate the E3 ligase responsible for METTL3 regulation, we overexpressed BARD1 and SYVN1 separately and assessed METTL3 protein levels. The results demonstrated that BARD1 overexpression led to a more pronounced reduction in METTL3 levels, which was effectively rescued by treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, both VCP and METTL3 were detected in the protein complex pulled down by BARD1 (Fig. 6B), and VCP overexpression was found to enhance the interaction between BARD1 and METTL3 (Fig. 6C). Knockdown of BARD1 significantly prolonged the protein half-life of METTL3 (Fig. 6D). As anticipated, BARD1 knockdown markedly reduced METTL3 polyubiquitination (Fig. 6E). Consistently, we observed that BARD1 depletion also attenuated K48-linked ubiquitination of METTL3 (Fig. 6F).

CCL promotes osteogenic differentiation by targeting VCP to inhibit METTL3 ubiquitination and degradation

To validate that CCL promotes osteogenic differentiation through the VCP/METTL3 signaling axis, we established BMSC cell lines overexpressing VCP alone or co-overexpressing both VCP and METTL3. VCP overexpression successfully increased VCP protein levels while concurrently reducing METTL3 expression. This suppressive effect on METTL3 was effectively rescued by concomitant METTL3 overexpression (Fig. 7A). Following VCP overexpression, a marked reduction in ALP activity and mineralized nodule formation was observed. Notably, the inhibitory effect of VCP on osteogenic differentiation was significantly attenuated upon METTL3 co-overexpression (Fig. 7B-C). Consistent with these phenotypic observations, the expression levels of key osteogenic marker proteins were decreased by VCP overexpression but were subsequently increased when METTL3 was overexpressed, as confirmed by both RT-qPCR and WB analyses (Fig. 7D-E).

Discussion

This study systematically investigated the mechanism by which CCL extract ameliorates OP induced by OVX. Through a combination of in vivo and in vitro experiments, multi-omics analysis, and validation of molecular interactions, we constructed a complete mechanistic pathway, the CCL-VCP-BARD1-METTL3 axis that regulates osteogenic differentiation, thereby providing a novel theoretical and experimental foundation for TCM in OP treatment.

CCL, a classic TCM herb for tonifying the liver and kidney and strengthening tendons and bones, has demonstrated potential anti-OP effects (Li et al. 2020). Previous studies confirmed that its extract promotes osteogenesis by modulating pathways such as RANKL/OPG and NF-κB/IκBα (Mo et al. 2019; Li et al. 2025). However, the unclear active components and core targets have limited its clinical translation. Our study first identified 46 potential active components in CCL via LC–MS coupled with ADME screening, encompassing flavonoids, phenolic acids, and fatty acids, and their derivatives. Among these, flavonoids (e.g., quercetin and its glycosides) are known to exert anti-OP effects by inhibiting osteoclast activity and activating osteogenic signaling pathways (Chen et al. 2023), while phenolic acids possess anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and pro-osteogenic properties (Dong et al. 2024). This identification establishes the material basis for CCL’s efficacy. In vivo, CCL significantly reversed the OVX-induced deterioration of bone microarchitecture. Micro-CT analysis revealed that CCL treatment markedly increased the BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th, while decreasing Tb.Sp in the femur of mice. Furthermore, HE and Masson’s trichrome staining confirmed that CCL repaired bone loss and collagen damage. ELISA results indicated that CCL downregulated serum levels of bone resorption markers (CTX-I, TRAP5b) and upregulated levels of bone formation markers (P1NP, OCN). In vitro, CCK-8 assays confirmed that 0.5–1 mg/mL CCL was non-cytotoxic to BMSCs, while ALP and ARS staining demonstrated that CCL significantly enhanced ALP activity and mineralized nodule formation in BMSCs. The consistency between in vivo and in vitro results not only validates the anti-osteoporotic efficacy of CCL but also establishes a progressive evidence chain linking “active components-in vivo efficacy-in vitro function”, providing experimental support for the traditional application of CCL. Furthermore, HE staining of liver and kidney tissues showed no obvious histopathological lesions or drug‑related toxicity in CCL‑treated mice, indicating the safety of CCL extract at the administered doses.

m6A RNA modification is a key epitranscriptomic mechanism regulating bone metabolic homeostasis (Zhong et al. 2024). The core methyltransferase METTL3 promotes osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs by enhancing the m6A modification levels of osteogenesis-related genes, such as RUNX2 and COL1A1 (Chen et al. 2025). We found that METTL3 protein expression was significantly downregulated in bone tissue from OVX mice, and CCL treatment effectively restored its levels. Notably, RT-qPCR analysis showed that CCL did not significantly affect METTL3 mRNA levels. However, CHX chase assays demonstrated that CCL substantially prolonged the half-life of METTL3 protein, indicating that CCL regulates METTL3 at the post-translational level via protein degradation rather than transcription. This discovery addresses a gap in the understanding of how TCMs regulate m6A modification in OP. While previous research has focused on the impact of TCMs on METTL3-mediated m6A methylation (Tian et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024), our study is the first to demonstrate that a TCM can exert anti-OP effects by stabilizing the target protein. To further confirm the necessity of METTL3 in CCL-mediated osteogenesis, we employed lentivirus-mediated METTL3 knockdown. Silencing METTL3 expression significantly reversed CCL’s promotive effects on BMSC ALP activity, mineralization capacity, and the expression of osteogenic markers (COL1A1, RUNX2, OCN), directly establishing METTL3 as an essential effector molecule for CCL’s anti-OP action. Combined with the finding that CCL upregulated global m6A levels in BMSC RNA, we conclude that CCL stabilizes METTL3 protein, enhances its m6A methyltransferase activity, and consequently activates the osteogenic differentiation program. This mechanism provides a new paradigm for the connection among TCM, epitranscriptomic modification, and bone metabolism.

To identify the specific molecular target through which CCL stabilizes METTL3, we screened potential targets of CCL’s active components using the SwissTargetPrediction database. By intersecting these results with OP-related targets from the GeneCard database and METTL3-interacting proteins from the BioGRID database, we identified VCP as a core candidate. VCP, a member of the AAA⁺ ATPase family, recognizes ubiquitinated target proteins and facilitates their proteasomal degradation, playing crucial roles in cell cycle regulation and protein quality control (Sun and Qiu 2020). Its role in bone metabolism has been implicated, for instance, by a report that missense mutations in VCP can lead to multisystem proteinopathy 1, which includes Paget’s disease of bone (Boock et al. 2023). Our CO-IP experiments confirmed an endogenous interaction between VCP and METTL3, which was significantly inhibited by CCL treatment. Functional assays revealed that VCP overexpression markedly reduced METTL3 protein levels, an effect reversible by the proteasome inhibitor MG132, suggesting that VCP promotes METTL3 degradation via the proteasome pathway. Furthermore, ubiquitination assays demonstrated that VCP overexpression significantly enhanced both the total and K48-linked ubiquitination of METTL3, and CCL effectively suppressed this process. Given that K48-linked ubiquitination is a canonical signal for proteasomal degradation (Tracz and Bialek 2021), these results directly elucidate the molecular mechanism by which VCP promotes METTL3 degradation and confirm that CCL inhibits this pathway by targeting VCP.

E3 Ub ligases are critical determinants of substrate specificity in ubiquitination (Sosic et al. 2022). To identify the specific E3 ligase involved in VCP-mediated METTL3 ubiquitination, we intersected VCP-interacting proteins from the BioGRID database with METTL3-associated E3 ligases from the UbiBrowser 2.0 database, identifying BARD1 and SYVN1 as candidates, with BARD1 showing higher confidence. BARD1 is a RING-type ubiquitin E3 ligase that interacts with many proteins to function in various biological processes, including DNA repair (Wang et al. 2023a, b). It has been implicated in multiple cancers, such as breast cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, and pancreatic cancer (Sniadecki et al. 2020; Jain et al. 2022; Patel et al. 2025), but its role in OP had not been reported. Our functional validation showed that BARD1 overexpression significantly decreased METTL3 protein levels, an effect reversed by MG132, whereas SYVN1 overexpression had no significant impact. CO-IP experiments confirmed that BARD1 forms a ternary complex with VCP and METTL3, and VCP overexpression enhanced the BARD1-METTL3 interaction. Moreover, BARD1 knockdown significantly extended the METTL3 protein half-life and reduced its total and K48-linked ubiquitination levels. These findings collectively reveal that VCP recruits the E3 ubiquitin ligase BARD1 to form a VCP-BARD1 complex that specifically mediates K48-linked ubiquitination and degradation of METTL3. CCL inhibits this process by disrupting the formation of this complex. To ultimately validate the functional significance of the CCL-VCP-METTL3 axis, we established BMSC cell lines with VCP overexpression and VCP/METTL3 co-overexpression. The results demonstrated that VCP overexpression inhibited BMSC osteogenic differentiation, and this inhibition was rescued by concurrent METTL3 overexpression, directly confirming that CCL exerts its pro-osteogenic effect by targeting VCP to stabilize METTL3.

Despite systematically elucidating the core mechanism of CCL against OP, this study has limitations for future research. First, while we identified 46 active components in CCL, the specific molecule(s) that directly bind VCP and inhibit its function remain to be pinpointed. Techniques such as molecular docking, surface plasmon resonance, and drug affinity responsive target stability should be employed to identify the key active molecule(s) for precise drug development. Second, although we confirmed that CCL upregulates m6A levels via METTL3, the specific osteogenic genes whose m6A modification is regulated were not identified. Future studies integrating m6A-seq and RNA-seq are needed to identify METTL3-dependent m6A target genes and validate changes in their translation efficiency, thereby refining the m6A modification-osteogenesis network. Third, the study utilized only the OVX mouse model, and validation in other OP models, such as senescence-induced or glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis, is necessary to assess the generalizability of CCL’s effects. Fourth, beyond the VCP-BARD1-METTL3 axis, CCL may act through other signaling pathways (e.g., RANKL/OPG, NF-κB/IκBα) (Mo et al. 2019; Li et al. 2025). Comprehensive analyses using transcriptomics and proteomics are warranted to systematically map the signaling networks modulated by CCL and elucidate the synergistic mechanisms of different pathways.

In conclusion, this study is the first to reveal that CCL extract ameliorates OVX-induced OP by inhibiting the VCP-BARD1-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination and degradation of METTL3, thereby maintaining METTL3 protein stability, upregulating m6A modification levels, and ultimately promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. The innovative value of this research lies in its integration of a traditional herbal medicine with modern molecular mechanisms involving m6A modification and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, offering a novel therapeutic paradigm for TCM in OP treatment. The identification of the VCP-BARD1 axis as a key pathway for METTL3 degradation provides a new molecular target for OP therapy. Through multi-level validation encompassing in vivo, in vitro, and molecular interaction studies, we construct a rigorous mechanistic evidence chain. This work not only provides a scientific basis for the clinical application of CCL but also, by elucidating the function of the CCL-VCP-BARD1-METTL3 axis, offers new insights and an experimental foundation for the precision treatment of OP.
