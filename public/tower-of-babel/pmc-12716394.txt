Topoisomerase III-beta protects from immune dysregulation and tumorigenesis
Authors: Md Rasel Al Mahmud, Simone Andrea Baechler, Anjali Dhall, Sourav Saha, Hongliang Zhang, Laura Bassel, Shuling Zhang, Min-Jung Lee, Nahoko Sato, Shraddha Rastogi, Suresh Kumar, Muhammad S. Alam, Liton Kumar Saha, Beverly A. Mock, Valentina M. Factor, Yves Pommier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Topoisomerase III-beta (Top3b) reduces nucleic acid torsional stress and intertwining generated during RNA and DNA metabolism while protecting the genome from pathological R-loops, which otherwise result in DNA breakage and genome instability. By studying Top3b knockout mice (Top3b-KO), we find that the loss of Top3b accelerates the development of spontaneous atypical lymphoid hyperplasia and lymphomas arising in spleens and lymph nodes, organs with prominent Top3b expression. Aging Top3b-KO mice also display splenomegaly and systemic immune alterations including neutrophilia and lymphopenia consistent with chronic inflammation. At the molecular level, Top3b deficiency causes genome-wide R-loop accumulation in splenocytes as measured by CUT&Tag sequencing. Increased R-loops are associated with genomic breaks and activation of immune signaling pathways including innate and adaptive immune cell signaling, IL-4 signaling, FAK signaling, and cGAS-STING. In addition, knocking-out Top3b promotes the rapid development of syngeneic EL4 T cell lymphomas. In conclusion, our work implies that Top3b protects from lymphoma, tumorigenesis, and immune dysregulations.

INTRODUCTION

Topoisomerases are pivotal for preventing and resolving the nucleic acid topological constraints arising from fundamental molecular processes including DNA replication, transcription, translation, meiotic recombination, and chromosomal segregation (1–4). Among the six metazoan topoisomerases (TOP1, TOP1MT, TOP2A, TOP2B, TOP3A, and TOP3B), only topoisomerase III-beta (TOP3B) can resolve RNA topological problems, in addition to its activity on single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) (3, 5).

Eukaryotic TOP3B forms heterotrimeric complexes with the scaffolding protein Tudor domain-containing protein 3 (TDRD3) and fragile X mental retardation protein (6, 7). TOP3B is endowed with a wide range of functions facilitating nucleic acid metabolic processes (5–13). It plays a prominent role in suppressing cellular R-loops (3, 13–15), either through relaxation of hypernegative supercoils or by resolving R-loops (8) in coordination with the helicase DDX5 (3). TOP3B’s auxiliary factor TDRD3, via “its” “Tudor domain,” can also recognize asymmetric dimethylated arginine marks in core histones (H3R17me2a and H4R3me2a) and RNA POL2 (R1810me2a), targeting TOP3B to CpG island containing active promoters to resolve R-loops (13). The TOP3B-mediated suppression of R-loops regulates transcription in cancer cells and neurons (13, 16). In addition, Top3b accelerates RNA translation both in cancer cells and neurons (5–7, 9, 12, 17). It is also associated with the stabilization of RNAs independently of its catalytic activity (9).

R-loops are DNA-RNA hybrid structures with a displaced DNA single strand and variable RNA-DNA base pairs ranging between 300 and 2500 base pairs (3, 18–20). They are highly dynamic with an average limited half-life of 10 to 15 min (18, 21, 22). R-loops are essential for maintaining important physiological processes, including class-switch recombination (23), mitochondrial replication (24, 25), protection against promoter methylation (26–28), transcription termination (29), chromatin remodeling and chromosome segregation (30, 31), double-strand break (DSB) repair (32, 33), DNA repair in transcribed regions (28), telomeres (34), and centromeric functions (28, 35). However, unscheduled and persistent R-loops can lead to a range of deleterious effects, including replication stress, replication fork collapse, stalling of transcription forks, replication-transcription conflicts, DSBs, and genome instability (3). As a result, R-loops are associated with a number of diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis type 4, fragile X syndrome, autoimmune diseases, and cancer (3).

Notably, Top3b–knockout (KO) mice are viable, and studies of their phenotype have begun to reveal the importance of TOP3B for human health (10, 36–38). Top3b inactivation has been associated with neurological defects, including increased anxiety, reduced spatial learning, defective synaptic transmission, and deficient adult neurogenesis. Top3b-KO mice also have a shorter life span (38–40), infertility after successive generations of homozygous pair breeding (37), transcriptional and behavioral impairment (4, 10), and accumulation of chromosomal aberrations and are prone to autoimmune disease (36). However, little is known about R-loops, immune- and cancer-related phenotypes of Top3b-KO mice. Here, we report previously unknown features of the Top3b-KO mouse phenotype by combining a combination of morphological, histological, immunohistochemical (IHC), molecular, and sequencing approaches demonstrating that unresolved and/or excessive R-loops are associated with immune dysregulation and increased lymphoma development.

RESULTS

Loss of Top3b accelerates spontaneous lymphomagenesis in mice

The Top3b-KO mouse line (38) (a gift from A. C. Shaw, Yale University) was rederived before introduction into the National Cancer Institute (NCI) animal facility. Consistent with previous report, our Top3b-KO mice show reduced life span (fig. S1A), increased incidence of ulcerative dermatitis (fig. S1, B and C), and extensive immune cell infiltration across different organs (fig. S1D) (38).

Because lymphoma is the most common causal factor contributing to mortality of aging C57BL/6 mice (41), we investigated the impact of Tob3b loss on the prevalence of lymphoma in mice from two different age groups (Fig. 1, A and B). At 11 to 15 months of age, the histological analysis of spleens revealed atypical lymphocyte hyperplasia in 23% of the Top3b-KO mice (3 of 13), whereas no such changes were observed in age-matched wild-type (WT) littermates (Fig. 1A). In affected mice, white pulp regions were moderately expanded by lymphocytes, with coalescence of adjacent follicles and absence of polarization and discernable germinal centers. IHC analysis showed that hyperplastic regions were primarily comprised of B cells intermixed with T cells (Fig. 1C). At higher magnification, lymphocytes from Top3b-KO mice were larger than those from WT controls, with increased cytoplasm, enlarged nuclei, dispersed chromatin, prominent nucleoli, and a greater number of mitotic figures (Fig. 1D).

As the mice aged, lymphomas were observed more frequently in Top3b-KO mice compared to WT controls, arising in various lymphoid tissues including the spleen, Peyer’s patches, and lymph nodes; diffusely effacing the surrounding tissues; and occasionally spreading to the liver or kidney. Among 35 Top3b-KO female mice necropsied between 18 and 24 months of age, lymphomas were identified in 15 animals (43%) compared to 3 of the 21 WT females (14.3%) (P < 0.05). Similarly, Top3b-KO male mice developed lymphomas at a higher frequency than WT males, although at a lower rate than the KO females (Fig. 1B) as previously reported for aged C57BL/6 mice (41). There were no observed differences in tumor histology, anatomical distribution, or tumor type between Top3b-KO female and male mice. Neoplastic lymphocytes were pleomorphic with round to oval nuclei, containing one or more prominent nucleoli, and abundant mitotic figures (Fig. 1E), while normal lymphoid architecture was observed in the age-matched WT (fig. S1E). IHC analysis identified lymphomas with either B220+ B cell predominance or CD3+ T cell predominance. Figure 1 (E and F) shows a representative B cell–predominant tumor characterized by larger B220+ B cells interspersed with smaller CD3+ T cells (Fig. 1, F and G). Together, these data demonstrate that the loss of Top3b is associated with lymphoproliferation that can progress to lymphoma, supporting a tumor suppressor role for Top3b.

Splenomegaly and inflammatory phenotype in Top3b-KO mice

Systematic investigation of Top3b-KO and WT littermates over time showed an elevation in spleen-to-body weight (SBW) ratios from 6 months of age onward (Fig. 2A and fig. S2). By 18 to 24 months of age, Top3b-KO female SBWs were, on average, more than fourfold higher (P < 0.0001) than in the age-matched WT female littermates (Fig. 2A) and fivefold (P < 0.01) higher than in Top3b-KO male mice (Fig. 2A and fig. S2E). Given the greater and more universal spleen enlargement in the Top3b-KO female mice than in Top3b-KO males, we used females in most of the following experiments.

In the Top3b-KO mice progressing toward splenomegaly, the total number of splenocytes was increased, reaching more than a twofold difference by 11 to 15 months of age compared to WT controls (Fig. 2B). The higher splenic cellularity was caused by an expansion of both B and T lymphocytes (Fig. 2B) as assessed by flow cytometry analysis (fig. S3).

Because splenomegaly in rodents can arise from systemic inflammation (42), we performed clinical hematology analyses at different ages. Complete blood cell counts analyses revealed a progressive accumulation of neutrophils and concurrent reduction in lymphocytes, resulting in a statistically significant increase in the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) (Fig. 2C). Top3b-KO females with enlarged spleen also showed a significant elevation in serum interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels (Fig. 2D). Inflammatory phenotypes observed in the spleen prompted us to examine inflammation-associated immune cell infiltration in livers. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis of livers in Top3b-KO female mice at 15 to 19 months of age (fig. S4, A and B) showed a marked accumulation of lymphoid cells as shown by increased CD3+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T and CD19+ B cells, and myeloid cells (Fig. 2E), implicating a wide range of systemic inflammation (43). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Top3b loss leads to splenomegaly and an inflammatory phenotype.

Top3b deletion results in genome-wide R-loop formation

To gain insight into the molecular mechanisms promoting the tumor predisposition phenotype of Top3b-KO mice and because we had recently shown in human cell line models that TOP3B inactivation leads to R-loop accumulation and DNA damage (8), we explored R-loops and the transcriptome of splenocytes from 3-month-old young adult mice (Fig. 3A). We compared the abundance of R-loops in WT and Top3b-KO splenocytes by two different methods, R-loop slot blot (8, 11) and R-loop Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation (R-Loop CUT&Tag) (Fig. 3A). A twofold elevation of R-loops was found in KO splenocytes compared to WT (Fig. 3, B and C). The specificity of the R-loop signals observed with S9.6 antibody was confirmed by the treatment of the genomic DNA samples with ribonuclease (RNase) H before slot blot analysis (Fig. 3B).

(C) Quantitation of three independent slot blot experiments. R-loop signals are normalized to double-stranded DNA (dsDNA). Data are presented as means ± SEM. n = 3 per genotype. Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t test (*P < 0.05 and **P < 0.01). (D) Heatmap of R-loop CUT&Tag-seq signals in WT and KO splenocytes within ±2 kb of R-loop centers. Color intensity represents signal strength. (E) Coverage plot of normalized R-loop peak signals across 31,922 genes. Enrichment was observed in 23,553 genes (top left, red), while depletion was noted in 8369 genes (bottom right, blue) in Top3b-KO mice. FPKM: fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads. (F) Distribution of R-loop peak intensity across different genomic features. RNase, ribonuclease; A.U., arbitrary unit; TTS, transcription termination site.

Genome-wide native R-loop profiling by R-Loop CUT&Tag confirmed that Top3b-KO splenocytes displayed an increase in R-loop peak intensities across 72% of the total R-loop peaks (fig. S5). The average peak intensity was elevated approximately twofold in Top3b-KO splenocytes as shown in the metaplot of Fig. 3D (top panel) and the heatmap of Fig. 3D (bottom panel). Next, we analyzed the raw R-loop peak intensities assigned to the corresponding genes. In total, we analyzed R-loops across 31,922 genes and found increased R-loop accumulation across 73.8% of all gene loci in the Top3b-KO splenocytes (Fig. 3E). These results confirm the R-loop slot blot data (Fig. 3, C and E). Differential peak analysis [fold change (FC) > 1.2 or FC < −1.2, P value < 0.05], showed the highest R-loop signal enrichment at promoter regions (Fig. 3F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Top3b prevents genome-wide accumulation of R-loops with the greatest impact at gene promoters.

Top3b deletion is associated with dysregulation of the immunotranscriptome

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of Top3b-KO splenocytes performed in parallel with the R-loop experiments identified 1371 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (dataset S1) using the Linear Model for Microarray Data (limma) (fig. S6A) (44). Among them, 509 (32%) were up-regulated and 862 genes (63%) were down-regulated (Fig. 4, A and B). The functional annotation of significantly altered pathways performed using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) on DEGs that were either up-regulated or down-regulated by at least 1.5-fold revealed a significant enrichment of immune signaling pathways including “communication between innate and adaptive immune cells,” “IL-4 signaling,” “T cell receptor signaling,” “focal adhesion kinase (FAK) signaling,” and suppression of “neutrophil degranulation” (Fig. 4C and dataset S1). Further analyses of differentially expressed immune genes revealed alterations in T cell and B cell receptor genes (fig. S6B), regulation of T cell activation by CD28 family (fig. S6C), signaling by interleukins (fig. S6D), and Fcgamma receptor–dependent phagocytosis (fig. S6E) reinforcing immune dysregulation in Top3b-KO splenocytes. These analyses support the conclusion that Top3b loss is associated with immune dysregulation.

(B) Volcano plots showing some of the DEGs in Top3b-KO splenocytes. DEGs were analyzed using limma. To evaluate the relationship between R-loops and transcriptomic alterations in Top3b-KO splenocytes, we plotted R-loops levels [FC, KO/WT] against RNA expression [FC, KO/WT] (Fig. 5A). The lack of correlation indicates that R-loop elevation in Top3b-KO splenocytes does not affect steady-state RNA expression (Fig. 5A). The lack of correlation was further demonstrated by Integrative Genome Viewer (IGV) tracings of three representative gene loci including Myc, Elof1, and Ppm1f (Fig. 5B). The graphs show that the R-loop signals were enhanced in the absence of Top3b, while the transcript levels were relatively similar. Notably, when we plotted the R-loop levels against gene length, both down-regulated (fig. S6F) and up-regulated (fig. S6G) groups showed a tendency for longer genes to accumulate more R-loops.

These data demonstrate that R-loops do not directly affect genome-wide transcripts. However, longer genes tend to accumulate more R-loop in the absence of Top3b.

DNA breaks and innate immune activation in Top3b-deleted splenocytes

To assess whether R-loop accumulation is associated with DNA damage (30, 45), we performed immunofluorescence staining of 3-month-old young adult splenocytes with γH2AX antibody, a well-established sensitive molecular marker of DSBs (46). Figure 5C shows increased γH2AX levels in Top3b-KO splenocytes. The quantitation by Random-Forest ML pixel-classifier also showed a significant increase in γH2AX foci number and signal intensity (Fig. 5D and fig. S7) in Top3b-KO as compared to WT splenocytes controls. Western blotting experiments confirmed elevation of the γH2AX signal in Top3b-KO splenocytes (Fig. 5E).

Since aberrant R-loop formation is known to cause cytoplasmic accumulation of DNA/RNA/RNA-DNA hybrid species leading to the activation of cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) signaling (47, 48), one of the major cytosolic DNA sensors in mammalian cells with diverse functions in host immunity and production of proinflammatory cytokines (49, 50), we performed Western blotting of cGAS (Fig. 5F) and STING (Fig. 5G), which showed that both were significantly increased in KO splenocytes. These results suggest a previously unappreciated role of Top3b in preventing innate immune activation.

Top3b deficiency promotes syngeneic EL4 lymphoma growth

Given that activation of the cGAS-STING pathway has been associated with peripheral T cell lymphomas (51) and that we found the Top3b-KO mice to be immune-compromised, we used a lymphoma EL4 syngeneic model (Fig. 6A) to compare tumor development in Top3b-KO and WT mice. Since EL4 cells were derived from a malignant T cell lymphoma induced in a C57BL/6 inbred mouse strain, this model allowed us to evaluate the impact of a functional (WT) and Top3b-deficient microenvironment on lymphoma growth in immune-proficient mice.

Data are representative of two independent biological experiments with consistent results. (B) Tumor growth kinetics. Tumor size was measured twice weekly using a digital caliper. Data were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. *P < 0.05. (C) Tumor weights 21 days after EL4 cell transplantation. Representative tumor images (right) show size differences at the endpoint. Data are shown as means ± SEM. **P < 0.01, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. (D) Representative bioluminescence images 21 days posttransplantation. (E) Quantification of bioluminescence imaging as total flux (photons/second). Data are shown as means ± SEM. *P < 0.05, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. IVIS, in vivo imaging system.

WT and Top3b-KO mice were challenged with subcutaneous injections of 10,000 EL4 cells stably expressing a luciferase reporter gene. EL4 lymphoma cells were readily engrafted in both WT and Top3b-KO recipients; notably, tumors grew faster and were bigger in Top3b-KO mice than in WT mice (Fig. 6B). As a result, syngeneic EL4 lymphomas in Top3b-KO recipients reached an experimental tumor size limit of 20 mm at 21 days after transplantation, significantly earlier than in the WT control mice (Fig. 6C). Bioluminescence imaging on day 21 showed, on average, a threefold increase in tumor mass in Top3b-KO recipients (Fig. 6, D and E). The transplantation caused no toxicity as evaluated by the mouse weights (fig. S8). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the loss of Top3b promotes tumor growth.

DISCUSSION

Here, we report that genetic inactivation of Top3b results in atypical lymphoproliferation and splenomegaly in middle-aged mice (11 to 15 months old) and leads to increased incidence of spontaneous lymphomas in aging mice (18 to 24 months old) (Figs. 1 and 2). We also demonstrate that Top3b prevents syngeneic lymphoma in young adult mice (2 to 3 months old) (Fig. 6). At the molecular level, we provide evidence that the loss of Top3b in mice leads to R-loop accumulation (Fig. 3), genomic DSBs (Fig. 5), and immune dysregulation (Figs. 2, 4, and 5). We propose that genomic instability, in combination with early-life impaired immune function renders Top3b-KO mice susceptible to lymphoproliferation and subsequent lymphomagenesis later in life. These results add to the known functions of Top3b in ensuring normal neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity (6, 7, 10).

Our findings in the mouse model confirm that lack of Top3b promotes R-loop accumulation with elevated DNA damage (2, 11, 14) and reveals inflammatory signaling in the absence of Top3b, suggesting a potential mechanistic link relevant to tumorigenesis. While these observations are correlative, they are supported by prior studies in human cancer cell lines showing that TOP3B is critical for suppressing R-loops possibly by relieving hypernegative supercoiling and enabling their resolution (8, 13–15, 30). Furthermore, mounting evidence indicates that unresolved R-loops promote DNA damage by inducing replication-transcription collisions and the cleavage of the R-loops by endonucleases [such as xeroderma pigmentosum group G-complementing protein (XPG) and xeroderma pigmentosum group F-complementing protein (XPF) or flap endonuclease 1 (FEN1) and XPF] (52–56), which in turn can drive activation of the cGAS-STING pathway through the accumulation of cytoplasmic RNA-DNA hybrids or ssDNA, leading to the induction of inflammatory genes (30, 56). Supporting this, matrin 3 (MATR3)-deficient near haploid human cell line, HAP1 cells exhibit reduced TOP3B levels, elevated cytoplasmic RNA-DNA hybrids, and robust cGAS-STING pathway activation (57). Together, these findings suggest that R-loop–mediated innate immune activation may be a consequence of TOP3B deficiency conserved across species. Future studies in human cell and murine models are warranted to directly test the causality of this pathway with associated molecular alterations and to delineate the contribution of R-loop–induced DNA damage to inflammation in the context of TOP3B loss.

Lymphomas are among the most common naturally occurring malignancies in many strains of mice with a higher incidence in females (41, 58–60) and a major cause of mortality in C57BL/6 mice (41). In agreement with published studies (38), we find that Top3b-KO mice display a shortened life span, which we attribute, at least in part, to increased lymphoma burden (fig. S1A), especially in female mice (Fig. 1).

Upon aging, we find that Top3b-KO mice exhibit features of immune system dysfunctions including splenomegaly, immune cell infiltration in different organs, and ulcerative dermatitis (fig. S1, C and D), which is consistent with a prior report (36). In peripheral blood, we find that Top3b deficiency causes a significant increase in the NLR (Fig. 1E), a marker of systemic inflammation and of diseases including infectious and autoimmune disorders (61), schizophrenia (62), and cancer (63, 64). These findings highlight a critical role of Top3b in preventing systemic inflammation and lymphomagenesis.

Our study further establishes that Top3b acts as a main suppressor of deleterious R-loops driving genomic instability in vivo (3) and in human renal cancer (14). Our findings are consistent with the observed elevation of R-loops and DSBs at the c-Myc and Ig heavy chain (Igh) loci in mice lacking Tdrd3, the scaffolding partner of Top3b (2, 15). Impaired Top3b activity has also been related to mammary gland cancer (65), bilateral renal cancer (14, 66), lymphoma (67), and melanoma (68). The characterization of Top3b-KO splenocytes isolated from 2- to 3-month-old mice revealed accumulation of R-loops with a parallel increase in DNA damage reflected by the presence of γH2AX. The analysis of R-loops across the entire genome demonstrates enrichment of R-loop signals at promoter regions (Fig. 3F).

Among the hallmark features of impaired processing of R-loops is activation of cGAS-STING-mediated inflammatory response through exposure to self-DNA/RNA/DNA-RNA hybrid (45, 47). Accordingly, we found increased cGAS-STING protein levels in Top3b-KO splenocytes (Fig. 5, F and G). Furthermore, the pathway analysis of the DEGs showed a statistically significant enrichment of the major inflammatory pathways, including but not limited to T helper type 2 (TH2) pathway (dataset S1) and IL-4 signaling (Fig. 4C) in Top3b-deficient splenocytes. IL-4 signaling activation favors atopic dermatitis (69) and lymphoma (70–73). In addition to that, elevated FAK signaling pathways (Fig. 4C) include IL-7 receptor (IL-7R). IL-7 transgenic mice suffer lymphoproliferative diseases such as B and T cell lymphoma (74, 75). Supporting this, T cell lymphoma– and B cell lymphoma–associated accelerated mortality was observed in IL-7 transgenic mice (76, 77). In our study, Top3b deletion led to IL-4 and FAK signaling in splenocytes from 2- to 3-month-old female mice (Fig. 6), well before lymphoma development. Therefore, one possible scenario is that loss of Top3b activates lymphoma-promoting immune signaling, which in turn contributes to spontaneous and syngeneic lymphoma progression. A more detailed phenotypic and molecular characterization of lymphoma is warranted including clonality of immunoglobulin and T cell receptor variable regions, in conjunction with flow cytometric analysis.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that in addition to a neurological phenotype (10), Top3b-KO mice develop a phenotype linking Top3b deficiency with R-loop–mediated genomic instability that triggers immune dysregulation and lymphoma-prone phenotype.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell line

EL4 cell line purchased from American Type Culture Collection (catalog no. TIB-39) and was grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (GeminiBio, West Sacramento, CA, 100-106), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15140122), and 55 mM 2-mercaptoethanol at 37°C in humidified 5% CO2 chamber. All experiments were performed within 25 passages from thawing, and cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma (Lonza, LT07-318) contamination.

Mouse model

The Top3b-KO mouse line (38) (a gift from A. C. Shaw, Yale University) was rederived before introducing into our facility. The mouse colony was maintained in an outbred C57BL/6 background under standard housing conditions (21° ± 1°C) with artificial light (12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle). Mice were kept in isolated cages. To maintain a specific pathogen–free environment in the cage, controlled ventilation was performed using high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters. Mating of individual pairs was initiated when the animals reached 8 weeks of age, and the litter size from each individual pair was monitored over a period of 8 to 10 months. All mice were genotyped at weaning by commercial vendor (Transnetyx, Cordova, TN).

H&E and immunocytochemistry staining

All animal work was performed in compliance with relevant regulatory standards and was approved by the Animal Research Committee of NCI, National Institutes of Health (NIH) Animal Care and Use Committee (ACUC). To prepare paraffin block for histological analysis, organs were fixed overnight in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5-μm slices by microtome for either hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for routine histology or immunostaining staining with antibodies against B cell, T cell, and macrophages with F4/80. All immunostainings were performed in house or by Histoserv Inc. (Gaithersburg) and/or Molecular Histopathology Laboratory, NCI (Frederick National Laboratory) using standard procedure. All slides were reviewed by veterinary pathologists.

Genetic modification of the EL4 cell line expresses the Luciferase gene

Lentivirus was produced by transfection of human embryonic kidney (HEK)–293T cells with lentiviral plasmid of interest, Pax2 (Adgene, 35002), and vesicular stomatitis virus G glycoprotein (VSV.G) (Adgene, 14888), and lentiviral plasmid pLenti CNV V5-LUC Blast (Adgene, 21474) for luciferase. The viral production was performed according to the NIH ACUC guidance. The HEK-293T cells were seeded in DMEM + 10% FBS on day −1 transfected using TurboFectin 8.0 on day 0 (OrgiGene, TF81001), and the virus was collected after 48 to 72 hours of posttransfection. The luciferase viral transduction was achieved by seeding 1 to 2 million EL4 cells with 1 ml of viral soup, along with of polybrene (10 μl/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific), by centrifuging at 1300g for 2 hours at 24°C and followed by incubation overnight at 37°C in a CO2 incubator. Cells were washed and cultured in EL4 cell media supplemented with blasticidin (5 μl/ml) for a week. Luciferase cells were reduced to maintenance of 1 μg/ml and eventually taken off blasticidin entirely.

EL4 LUC xenograft model in WT and Top3b-KO C57BL/6 mice

Two to 3-month-old females WT and Top3b-KO mice were used in this experiment. All procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the ACUC of the NCI, NIH. The cells were negative for mycoplasma, and all the viruses were tested. EL4 cells (1 × 104 cells per mouse) were subcutaneously transplanted into the right flank of experimental mice in a 1:1 mix of Hanks’ balanced salt solution and Matrigel. Tumor size and mouse weight were assessed every 3 days until day −21. Tumor volume was calculated by the formula: volume = 0.5 × length × (width)2. At day −21, mice were injected with d-luciferin (150 mg/kg body weight) at the right quadrant of the abdomen intraperitoneally for bioluminescence imaging. Images were taken 10 min after injection using a Xenogen in vivo imaging system (IVIS) System. Signal intensity quantification was performed using the Living Image software (Xenogen).

R-loop dot blot

For R-loop detection by slot blot, genomic DNA was extracted from isolated splenocytes using protocol as described previously (8, 78, 79). Briefly, cells were lysed in tris-EDTA (TE) buffer containing SDS and proteinase K [at 37°C overnight, Invitrogen Proteinase K Solution (20 mg/ml)], phase-separated using phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1), ethanol-precipitated, and resuspended in TE buffer. Genomic DNA was digested using cocktail of restriction enzymes (Hind III, Ssp I, Eco RI, Bsr GI, and Xba I; 30 U each), treated with RNase T1 (2 U; Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. EN0542) and shortcut RNase III (2 U; New England Biolabs, catalog no. M0245L), and again purified by phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) extraction. Increasing concentrations of genomic DNA were spotted on a nitrocellulose membrane, cross-linked with ultraviolet light (120 mJ/cm2), blocked with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)–Tween (0.1%) buffer and 5% non-fat milk [room temperature (RT) for 1 hour], and incubated with mouse S9.6 antibody (1:500 dilution, overnight at 4°C; MilliporeSigma, catalog no. MABE1095). After washing with PBS–Tween (0.1%; PBS-T), membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody, washed, and developed with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) techniques. In case of RNase H (New England Biolabs, catalog no. M0297L)–treated control, 10 μg of genomic DNA was preincubated with 20 U of RNase H for 3 hours at 37°C (8).

Western blotting

Splenocytes were lysed in 100 μl of SDS buffer containing 25 mM tris-HCl (pH 6.5), 1% SDS, 0.24 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 0.1% bromophenol blue, and 5% glycerol and cOmplete, Mini, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, catalog no. 11836170001). Whole-cell extracts were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel, transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes, and blocked in 5% skimmed milk dissolved in 0.1% Tween 20 in PBS. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by washing with 0.1% Tween 20 in PBS. Primary antibodies used in this study are as follows: rabbit polyclonal anti-γH2AX (1:1000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, catalog no. 9718), rabbit monoclonal anti-STING (1:1000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, catalog no. 13647), and rabbit mAb anti-GAPDH (1:2000 dilution; clone 14C10, Cell Signaling Technology, catalog no. 2118). Membranes were incubated with anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) ECL, HRP conjugated (1:4000 dilution; GE Healthcare, catalog no. NA9340) at RT for 1 hour and washed thrice, and signals were detected by ECL chemiluminescence reaction (SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, catalog no. 34095).

R-loop CUT&Tag sequencing

The CUT&Tag protocol was carried out following Zhang et al. (80). Briefly, 2 μl of 2XCB [0.1 M tris (pH 8.0), 0.3 M NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 25% glycerol], 2 μl of pA-Tn5 transposome (at 4 μM), and 2 μl of S9.6 antibody were mixed in a microcentrifuge tube (mixture A) and kept on ice. One million cells were harvested and transferred to a clean 1.5-ml tube. Cells were washed once with 1 ml of PBS, suspended in 1 ml of 1XCB, and incubated on ice for 10 min. The cells were spun down and suspended in 1 ml of 1XCB. An aliquot of 50 μl was added to mixture A and incubated at RT for 60 min with rotation. A 500 μl of wash buffer [50 mM tris (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Triton X-100] was added to each tube and centrifuged. The pellets were washed two more times with 500 μl of wash buffer and then suspended in 100 μl of wash buffer. A 1 μl of 1 M MgCl2 was added to start the transposome reaction and incubated at 37°C for 60 min. A 4 μl of 0.5 M EDTA, 2 μl of 10% SDS, and 1 μl of proteinase K (20 mg/ml) were added and incubated at 55°C for 60 min. The DNA was purified with ChIP DNA clean & concentrator (Zymo Research). Library was constructed following the manufacturer’s protocol (Illumina) and sequenced on Next-seq.

Total RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted by TRIzol (Invitrogen) and then purified using the PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, catalog no. 12183018A) with deoxyribonuclease treatment (QIAGEN). The 2100 Expert (Agilent) was used for RNA quality and quantity assessment. The RNA integrity number of all samples was above 9.0.

Library was prepared for splenocytes using the NEBNext rRNA Depletion Kit v2 (human/mouse/rat) (NEB, #E7400) and the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit from Illumina (NEB, #E7760). For polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification (13 cycles), NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (96 unique dual index primer pairs, NEB, E6440) was used. Dual index (101 × 8 × 8 × 101) pair-end sequencing of the final 750 pM library was performed using the Illumina NextSeq2000 P2 Reagents (200 cycles) on the Illumina NextSeq2000 instrument. The total number of reads was 550 M.

Biotinylated, target-specific oligos combined with Ribo-Zero ribosomal RNA (rRNA) removal beads were used for the removal of rRNA library construction for splenocytes. The Illumina TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Preparation Kit and paired-end sequencing of 6 pooled RNA-seq samples were performed on HiSeq. Following RNA fragmentation and cleavage, small pieces are converted to cDNA with reverse transcriptase and random primers and, consequently, second strand cDNA synthesis with DNA polymerase I and RNase H. A standard Illumina library prep with end-repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification being performed to give a sequencing ready library from the double strand cDNA as input. After that, the purified product was quantified using quantitative PCR, followed by cluster generation and pair-end sequencing on HiSeq.

Data preprocessing and bioinformatics analysis

R-loop CUT&Tag sequencing fastq files for WT and Top3b-KO were generated from splenocytes of young female mice. Sequencing quality was assessed using FASTQC (version 0.12.1), and duplicate reads were identified using Picard (version 3.2.0). After duplicates were removed, the fastq files were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm39) using Burrows-Wheeler Alignment (BWA) aligner (version 0.7.17). Peak calling was conducted with MACS2 (version 2.2.7.1), and normalized bigwig files were produced using the BAMscale “scale” module (81). In addition, the coverage of aligned Binary Alignment Map (BAM) files was calculated with BAMscale “cov” (version 0.0.6), applying the FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) normalization method. Peaks annotations and differential peak analysis were performed using HOMER software (version 5.1) using “annotatePeaks.pl” and “getDifferentialPeaks” modules. Average signal intensity heatmaps were generated using plotHeatmap module, and the read coverage of WT and Top3b-KO bigWig files was calculated using “multiBigwigSummary” module of deepTools (version 3.5.5) (82). R-loop peaks were identified using MACS2 (q value < 0.05) from DRIP-seq data generated in WT and Top3b-KO splenocytes. A total of 45,990 peaks were called in WT and 60,275 peaks in Top3b-KO, indicating increased R-loop accumulation in the absence of Top3b. Peak overlap analysis was performed using bedtools intersect, revealing 29,779 shared peaks, 16,211 peaks unique to WT, and 30,496 unique to Top3b-KO. R-loop signal intensities used in plots (Fig. 3D and fig. S5) were calculated by quantifying normalized read coverage over these peak regions.

In addition, RNA-seq fastq files from WT and Top3b-KO female mouse splenocytes were aligned to mm39 reference genome using STAR aligner (version 2.7.10b) (83). SAMtools (v 1.17) was used to sort and index-aligned BAM files. Read counts per gene were computed with the STAR “–quantMode GeneCounts” option, and raw reads were normalized to RPKM (reads per kilobase per million mapped reads) values. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using “limma” package [https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/limma.html in R (version 4.2.3)]. DEGs were used to perform pathway analysis using IPA software (Ingenuity Systems, QIAGEN, China Co. Ltd.).

γH2AX immunostaining

For γH2AX detection, total splenocytes (0.5 × 106 cells) were collected from spleens of 2- to 3-month-old females using cytospin and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min at RT, followed by permeabilization for 15 min with Triton X-100 (0.5%) in PBS. After that, blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 0.05% PBS-T for 60 min at RT, splenocytes were incubated overnight at 4°C with α-γH2AX (1:500; rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, catalog no. 20E3) primary antibody. After washing with PBS, followed by a 1-hour incubation with a goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 568–conjugated IgG (1:500; Invitrogen, catalog no. A11011) at RT. After washing several times with PBS, the section was dried and mounted in 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole–containing media. Images were captured with a Zeiss LSM 780 confocal microscope. The quantification of γH2AX foci intensity and number was performed using custom-trained Cellpose deep-learning model and Random-Forest ML pixel-classifier.

Flow cytometry of the spleen

All anti-mouse antibodies used were CD8-phycoerythrin (catalog no. 552094), CD4–fluorescein isothiocyanate (catalog no. 553650), CD3e–allophycocyanin (APC) (catalog no. 553066), and B220-APC-Cy7 (catalog no. 552094), all purchased from BD Pharmingen. Spleen cells were isolated, and red blood cells were removed using ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) lysis buffer. The stained cells were resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS with 0.5% BSA) to a final concentration of 107 cells/ml. A 100 μl of aliquot of the cell suspension was blocked with Fc blocker (anti-CD16/32) for 15 min at 4°C and then incubated with either single antibodies or an antibody cocktail for 30 min at 4°C. After incubation, cells were washed once with FACS buffer. Stained cell populations were analyzed using a BD LSRFortessa and FlowJo 10. Flow cytometry gating strategies are shown in fig. S3.

Flow cytometry of immune subset of liver

Freshly resected mouse liver tissue samples were stored in the magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) tissue storage solution (Miltenyi, #130-100-008) at 4°C not longer than 24 hours before dissociation. Liver tissues were mechanically dissociated using mouse liver dissociation kit (Miltenyi, #130-105-807) using gentleMACS dissociator (Miltenyi, #130-093-926), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Single-cell suspension dissociated from liver tissues was processed for multiparameter flow cytometric analysis. Cells were incubated with Live/Dead fixable Aqua dead cell stain kit (Invitrogen, #L34957). Before antibody staining, cells were blocked using Fc receptor blocking agent, mouse (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-092-575), followed by incubation with antibodies for surface staining for 20 min at 4°C. The antibodies used were CD4 (RM4-5, BioLegend, #100542), CD8 (53-6.7, BioLegend, #100733), CD3 (17A2, BioLegend, #100237), CD11b (1D3/39, BioLegend, #152408), CD11c (N418, BioLegend, #117310), Ly6C (HK1.4, BioLegend, #128031), Ly6G (1A8, BioLegend, #127618), major histocompatibility complex class II (REA813, Miltenyi, #130-112-391), CD45 (30-F11, BioLegend, #103151), and CD19 (1D3/CD19, BioLegend, #152413). All flow cytometric analyses were performed using a MACSQuant Analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software version 10.6.1. (FlowJo, LLC). Flow cytometry gating strategies are shown in fig. S4B.

IL-6 measurements

Before euthanasia, aliquots of whole blood were obtained from anesthetized mice for hematocrit determination and plasma preparation. IL-6 level was measured by BioTechne Luminex Mouse Discovery Assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Complete blood count

The mice were anesthetized using isoflurane anesthesia, and then blood samples were collected through cardiac puncture before mice were euthanized. A total of 100 μl of blood was collected and placed in the 0.5-ml EDTA-coated tube and shipped to Molecular Histopathology Lab (MHL), NCI, Frederick National Laboratory for CBC.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism V.10.4.1 (GraphPad Software Inc.). Two-sided Student’s t test, two-sided Welch’s t test, Mann Whitney test, and log-rank test were used for statistical comparisons. Data are presented as the means ± SEM, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001).
