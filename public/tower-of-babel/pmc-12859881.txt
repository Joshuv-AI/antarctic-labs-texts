Building ultrasound-guided procedure skills in regional anesthesiology: simulation training with a formalin-embalmed cadaver model
Authors: Nena Lundgreen Mason, Edan Cohen, Kiran Ganga, Isain Zapata, Aaron Hawke, Meredith Peck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe use of ultrasound guidance in regional anesthesiology procedures has become the standard of care. Using ultrasound to guide a needle is a highly technical skill that requires deliberate training and repetitive hands-on practice to master. Cost effective and anatomically realistic simulation training opportunities are needed to allow medical trainees to develop these skills prior to performing procedures on patients. Simulation training with formalin-embalmed cadavers, which are the most widely available type of body donor, may provide appropriate and effective needle-guidance training opportunities without risk to patients.MethodsNineteen first-year medical students viewed an orientation video and attended a femoral nerve block simulation training workshop using formalin-embalmed cadavers. Participants received 20-minutes of one-on-one training, followed by a 5-minute skills test. Pre- and post-training surveys were also conducted.ResultsParticipant self-confidence in their ability to perform all aspects of the procedure increased significantly following training (p = < 0.001). During the skills assessment, all participants but one were able to independently guide their needle into the target zone for a femoral nerve block in an average of 106 s. Participants needed an average of 1.9 attempts to complete the procedure. Ultrasound images of formalin-embalmed tissue were clear and relevant structures were easy to visualize during needle guidance.ConclusionsBrief simulation training with formalin-embalmed cadavers can effectively allow novice medical trainees to build and demonstrate the crucial skills needed to accurately guide a needle with ultrasound in regional anesthesiology procedures. Background

Femoral nerve blocks (FNBs) are a key clinical procedure used across many disciplines, especially anesthesiology and emergency medicine. FNBs provide effective analgesia for multiple indications, including femoral and hip fractures, total knee arthroplasties, and anterior cruciate ligament repairs, while also reducing opioid consumption and associated adverse effects [1–5]. Ultrasound (US) guidance has improved regional anesthesia by allowing direct visualization of neural structures, anatomy, and needle trajectory, enhancing safety and success compared to traditional methods [6].

Initial training in FNBs lacks standardization but typically occurs during residency using patients at bedside [7, 8]. New residents report proficiency in basic clinical procedures but state lack of exposure to more advanced techniques such as regional anesthesia [9, 10]. This training gap creates challenges when students and novice clinicians first attempt these techniques on actual patients, potentially causing complications like hematomas, arterial puncture, paresthesia, block failure, nerve injury, and local systemic anesthetic toxicity (LAST) [11].

While various methods, such as commercially available synthetic nerve block models, provide a controlled learning environment, they lack realistic tissue characteristics, have limited anatomical variations, and are often expensive [12]. In comparison, human cadavers can provide a model for low-stress simulation training that more accurately represents the relationship of the femoral nerve to surrounding structures [13, 14]. Recent studies show that formalin-embalmed cadavers are an effective model for US-guided peripheral nerve blocks and other US-guided procedures due to their tissue durability, simple preparation, and minimal maintenance [15–19]. Despite their accessibility in most medical training institutions, skepticism persists regarding their utility in US-guided anesthesia procedures due to concerns about tissue desiccation and poor visibility of neural structures [14].

This feasibility study aims to assess the educational utility of using formalin-embalmed cadavers for teaching US-guided FNBs to novice medial trainees. Pre- and post-training surveys will assess participant confidence, and an objective skills test will evaluate procedural competence of study participants following simulation training with formalin-embalmed cadavers. This project seeks to expand the use of existing resources to develop low-risk training environments for trainees to learn US-guided femoral nerve blocks before performing them on patients.

Methods

Study setting, participant recruitment, and IRB approval

This study was conducted in the anatomy laboratory facility at the Geisel School of Medicine at Dartmouth in Hanover, New Hampshireand was determined to be exempt by the Dartmouth College Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects (STUDY00033177). Participants were recruited via email invitation sent to the 96 students in the first-year medical student class. Interested students enrolled on a first-come, first-served basis. Nineteen first-year medical students volunteered to participate in the study and signed a consent form.

Equipment

Three Mindray MX7 ultrasound systems equipped with L12-3RC linear transducers (Mindray Global, Shenzhen China) were used to guide 21 g 2-inch hypodermic needles and syringes during needle guidance training.

Cadavers

Four cadaveric specimens were utilized for simulation training activities in this study. Specimens were prepared using standard formalin-embalming procedures commonly used around the world for the preparation of body donors for anatomical dissection, or burial. Formalin-embalmed cadavers are the most readily available type of cadaveric specimen because the tissue integrity holds up the longest and they are the cheapest to produce and maintain when compared to alternatives like fresh frozen, and soft-embalmed specimens. Cadaver age at death, embalming date, and demographic information were not utilized to select the four specimens used in training activities, rather specimens were selected based on the sonographic clarity of each specimen’s femoral triangle structures.

Study design

Participants first viewed a 15-minute instructional video outlining the fundamental US technique for performing a US-guided femoral nerve block (FNB) in cadaver tissue. Immediately afterward, each participant attended a 20-minute, one-on-one hands-on training workshop in the anatomy lab using formalin-embalmed cadavers. During the session, an instructor taught participants to use a linear US probe to identify the femoral nerve and relevant adjacent anatomical structures, including the femoral artery, femoral vein, fascia lata, and fascia iliaca. They were then instructed through the process of guiding the needle tip into the target zone for a FNB approximately within 1 cm lateral to the femoral nerve and deep to the fascia iliaca. Participants were encouraged to guide the needle tip into the target zone with continuous visualization of the needle tip as many times as they could until their allotted training time ended. Immediately following the workshop, participants underwent a five-minute skills assessment with an instructor they did not learn from to evaluate their ability to independently perform an US-guided femoral nerve block (excluding the fluid administration step) without help from an instructor. Instructors recorded the, procedure duration, any observed complications, and number of needle sticks needed to complete the procedure during skills testing. Instructors also used a previously published global rating scale (GRS) rubric (supplementary file 1) to rate participants performance of the procedure [20]. Pre- and post-training surveys (supplementary file 1), which were developed specifically for this study, were administered to assess the self-reported confidence of participants.

Procedure for needle placement for FNB

To preserve the integrity of cadaveric tissue across multiple participants, students were not permitted to redirect the needle within the cadaver tissue, inject fluid into the femoral nerve space, or perform sterile technique during the assessment. Although fluid injections are well-visualized in formalin-embalmed cadaver tissue and would be feasible in small-group instruction, repeated injections of fluid distort the tissue planes limiting their reusability with a larger number of learners. These actions were therefore restricted in this study.

Instructors

Instruction was provided by one of four educators. All instructors used a teaching rubric to standardize what was taught and the order in which topics were covered as much as possible across all learners. The instructors included; a full time US educator and clinical anatomist with 11 years’ experience teaching US skills to learners at all levels of training, two D.O. Regional Anesthesiology Fellows, and a third-year medical student who had completed three sessions of US needle guidance training and received specific training in the performance of FNBs. Statistical analysis was performed to evaluate for any difference in participant performance outcomes associated with the type of instructor a participant learned from.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive assessment was performed for categorical variables reporting frequencies and percentages and for continuous variables reporting means and standard deviations. Confidence levels were assessed on numerically converted Likert scales. Confidence and skill assessments were evaluated using Generalized Linear Models (GLMs). For confidence assessment models, dependent variables were: their confidence to identify the fascia iliaca, confidence to identify femoral artery, confidence to identify femoral nerve, confidence to identify the optimal site, and confidence to guide the needle. The dependent variable was the time point (pre vs. post). For skill assessment models, dependent variables were: respect for tissue, time and motion, instrument handling, flow of procedure, knowledge of procedure and the overall performance. Independent variables were: number of procedures completed, the instructor, the time in seconds it took to complete the task, the number of attempts needed and if they used US before. All modeling was performed using SAS v.9.4 (SAS Institute Inc. Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was declared at p ≤ 0.05.

Participants and instructors

All 19 participants in this study were first-year medical students. Three of the 19 participants reported having prior experience using US to guide a needle. Two of these participants had been taught to guide a needle into a gel block model, and one had received training in US-guided IV catheter placement. There were four instructors with varying levels of teaching and US related expertise that taught participants the simulation training workshop. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences between participant self-confidence or skills test related outcomes and the specific instructor a participant learned from.

Self-confidence surveys

Following training, participant self-confidence increased significantly across all aspects of the procedure (p = < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Pre-training self-confidence scores ranged from 0.4 to 1 and post-training they increased from a range of 2.8 to 3.2. Results from the post-training survey showed that 89.5% of participants thought that this type of cadaveric simulation training was “extremely useful” or “quite useful” (options 4 and 5 on the 5-point Likert scale) to their personal ability to perform US-guided procedures on patients in future. All participants agreed that this type of cadaveric simulation training would be “extremely useful” or “quite useful” for any medical student to have before they are asked to perform US-guided procedures on live patients.

Training period and skills testing

Data collected related to skills training and testing is depicted in Fig. 3. Study participants were able to successfully use US to visually guide their needle into the target zone an average of 5 (range 2–11) times with the guidance of an instructor during the 20-minute training period. Eighteen of the 19 participants successfully completed the skills test in the allotted time. Participants completed the skills test in an average time of 106 s using an average of 1.9 attempts (needle sticks) to complete the procedure. The one participant who failed the skills test placed the needle off center of the transducer so that tissue movement was visible on the US screen during needle insertion, but the hyperechoic needle was not visible during needle placement. The instructor thought the needle tip was placed close enough to the target zone to achieve a successful block if fluid had been pushed, but because the participant did not have adequate needle visualization during needle placement, they did not pass the skills assessment. Average global rating scale (GRS) scores (supplementary file 1) that the instructors assigned to participant procedural performance ranged from 4.2 to 4.6. Figure 4 shows that analysis via generalized linear models showed significant relationships between students with higher GRS scores in the categories of “respect for tissue” “flow procedure”, “knowledge of procedure”, and “overall performance” with significantly higher participant opinions of the usefulness of simulation training with cadavers (p = < 0.05).

Discussion

Ultrasound imaging

The sonographic images of the cadaveric femoral triangle region show that formalin-embalmed tissue yields US images that are adequate to teach learners to perform FNBs (Fig. 1 panels B and C). In this study, participants used US to locate relevant anatomy and carefully guide thneedle tip into the appropriate target zone for a femoral nerve block albeit without administration of fluid in order to preserve specimen integrity. It’s worth noting, however, that if each femoral region in a cadaver were to be used to train only one or two learners and the tissue was not to be immediately reused repeatedly to train multiple learners, allowing the trainee to push saline into the target zone of a formalin-enabled cadaver does allow for clear and sonographic visualization of fluid spread around the nerve and appropriate distribution can be easily confirmed using US.

Needle-guidance training

US guidance makes nerve blocks safer for patients by reducing the number of needle sticks, amount of local anesthetic, and total time required to complete each procedure [21–23]. However, anesthesia trainees make procedural mistakes frequently while learning, which could potentially harm patients [24]. New medical graduates and even anesthesiology residents report feeling unprepared to do basic clinical procedures and would like more simulation training opportunities [25–27]. This study shows that novice medical trainees can build skills and self-confidence through anatomically accurate simulation training and repeated practice with needle-guidance using formalin-embalmed cadavers. These data support other studies that show simulation training to be an effective means of building procedural skills, increasing trainee confidence, and decreasing compilation rates in procedures performed on patients [28–31].

Participants in this study significantly increased self-confidence related to their ability to perform all measured aspects of the femoral nerve block procedure. The ability to repeatedly practice and build the hands-on tactile skills required to successfully guide a needle with US prior to performing procedures on patients may help learners approach these procedures in the clinical environment with increased confidence and hopefully with some measure of improved procedural performance. A study by Smith et al. supports the early introduction of US-guided procedure training to support the improvement of clinical performance in US-guided procedures by anesthesiology trainees [32], Additionally, Studies have shown that simulation training increases self-confidence which can positively impact procedural performance in regional anesthesia [33, 34].

Limitations

A model of any kind can’t 100% mimic the clinical experience of performing a FNB procedure accurately (no artery pulsation, patient positioning or respiratory movement) and therefore it cannot directly translate to clinical performance. It is also important to note that self-assessed confidence is not a true measure of procedural competence and any clinical assessment that has instructors judging a learner’s competence will be subjective in nature. This study did not teach sterile technique or allow participants to administer fluid around the femoral nerve, reasons for this are provided above. Nineteen is a low number of participants to drive statistical power, a difficulty caused by the time consuming and labor-intensive nature of providing one-on-one training like this with a large group of learners. Additionally, this study lacks a control group which weakened the study methodology. However, in this case, a control group would mean asking a medical student to perform a needle-based procedure without any training which would likely result in undue damage to the expensive US equipment, the cadaver tissue, and potentially a needle stick injury making the risks outweigh the potential benefit of using a control group. Lastly these participants volunteered which may have introduced a self-selection bias to the pool of participants.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that formalin-embalmed cadaver tissue yields clear sonographic images of the femoral triangle region and can be a low cost and effective teaching model for novice medical trainees learning ultrasound-guided femoral nerve blocks. After a brief training video and cadaveric procedure simulation session, participants reported significant increases in their confidence and were able to successfully identify pertinent anatomy and independently maneuver a needle into the target zone under ultrasound-guidance. The opportunity to participate in simulation training with formalin-embalmed cadavers that are both anatomically accurate and realistic, prior to performing nerve blocks on patients may be beneficial in increasing trainee self-confidence and procedural competence.
