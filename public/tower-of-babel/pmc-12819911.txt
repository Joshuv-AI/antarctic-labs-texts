Investigating the causal relationship between insomnia and ovarian cancer risk: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Yuan Ren, Ni Yang, Tingyan Shi, Rong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe epidemiological association between insomnia and ovarian cancer remains controversial, as observational studies are inherently susceptible to confounding factors and reverse causation bias. While neuroendocrine dysregulation may contribute to the pathogenesis of hormone-sensitive malignancies, there is a paucity of genetic evidence supporting a causal relationship between sleep disturbances and ovarian carcinogenesis.MethodsWe conducted a two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis using summary-level genetic data obtained from separate genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of insomnia and ovarian cancer in European populations. The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method served as our primary analytical approach, with complementary validation through MR-Egger regression, weighted median estimation, and MR-PRESSO sensitivity analyses. Instrumental variables were stringently selected based on genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸), linkage disequilibrium thresholds (r² < 0.001), and instrument strength quantification using F-statistics , with all retained SNPs demonstrating strong instrument validity (F > 30). Horizontal pleiotropy was systematically evaluated through Egger intercept testing and MR-PRESSO outlier correction procedures.ResultsThe IVW method found a statistically significant but clinically marginal association between insomnia and ovarian cancer risk (OR = 1.00, 95%CI: 1.00-1.01, p= 0.02). Nonetheless, both the MR-Egger analysis (OR = 1.00, 95%CI: 0.99-1.01, p= 0.68) and the weighted median analysis (OR = 1.00, 95%CI: 0.99-1.01,p= 0.21) produced non-significant results. The instruments are strong (mean F-statistic = 38.8). No evidence of horizontal pleiotropy or heterogeneity was found (MR-PRESSO p= 0.731; Egger intercept p= 0.986; Cochran’s Q p= 0.643).ConclusionThe study reveals a modest association between insomnia and ovarian cancer incidence, though this relationship demonstrates limited clinical relevance. Genetic analyses indicate that insomnia does not independently contribute to elevated ovarian cancer risk.Keywords: Insomnia, Ovarian cancer, Mendelian randomization, Genetic causal association, Horizontal pleiotropy

Introduction

Insomnia affects about 10% of people globally and another 20% get trouble on occasion. The prevalence is consistently higher in women than in men [1]. Recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a major public health challenge, insomnia not only significantly impairs quality of life but also imposes substantial medical and economic burdens [2]. Concurrently, ovarian cancer exhibits the highest mortality rate among gynecologic malignancies, particularly in developed countries where it accounts for over two-thirds of gynecologic cancer deaths, underscoring its epidemiological severity [3].

The neuroendocrine mechanisms underlying insomnia are complex. Studies demonstrate enhanced hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activity in insomnia patients, characterized by elevated cortisol levels and disrupted circadian rhythms [4]. Core circadian genes such as PER1 and PER2 possess dual tumor-suppressive functions, with their expression significantly downregulated in multiple cancers including gastric, hepatic, and non-small cell lung carcinomas [5, 6]. These genes inhibit tumor growth and metastasis through regulation of cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and DNA repair mechanisms [7, 8]. Sleep disturbances are highly prevalent in ovarian cancer patients, with approximately 36% reporting sleep aid usage and 67% experiencing poor sleep quality [9]. Insomnia symptoms persist throughout the first year post-diagnosis independent of chemotherapy status, suggesting chronic characteristics unrelated to treatment phase [10]. Emerging evidence indicates potential associations between sleep quality and ovarian cancer risk [11, 12].

Insomnia-ovarian cancer’s relationship has observational studies, which have two methodological issues, as follows. First, there is incomplete control for confounding factors, such as obesity and hormone therapy. Second, there may also be reverse causation bias. This is because certain ovarian cancer symptoms may themselves induce a secondary sleep disorder. For instance, ascites may cause discomfort, while pain may call for opioid therapy, which is sedative. Neuroendocrine mechanisms may play a role in hormone-sensitive tumor development, although previous studies have focused mostly on epidemiological correlation rather than causation or mechanism. The goal of this study is to assess the potential causal effects of insomnia on ovarian cancer through genetic instruments selected from large scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Our mendelian randomization approach is able to circumvent the confounding and reverse causation biases that are commonly unresolvable in observational studies. These methods will also provide useful grounds to further deduce the biological plausibility of the insomnia-ovarian cancer association.

Methods

Data sources

Genetic instruments for insomnia were derived from GWAS conducted by UK Biobank and 23andMe (GWAS ID: ukb-a-13). Epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) data were obtained from the Ovarian Cancer Association Consortium (OCAC) GWAS (GWAS ID: ieu-b-4963). All datasets were accessed through the OpenGWAS platform. Although OCAC data are available via OpenGWAS, their original sources remain completely independent from UK Biobank and 23andMe datasets.

All the study populations were of European ancestry to limit the confounding effect of population stratification through genetic homogeneity. However, we recognize that the results may not apply to non-European populations. Ethical approval was obtained by the original studies from the respective institutional review boards with written informed consent from all participants. This secondary analysis did not need approvals and used de-identified summary-level statistics. It did not gain access to individual-level identifiable information. It also met the Declaration of Helsinki and EU General Data Protection Regulation requirements for secondary use of data. Detailed dataset characteristics are presented in Table 1.

MR method selection

This study employed a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) framework, with inverse-variance weighted (IVW) as the primary analytical method to assess the causal relationship between insomnia and ovarian cancer. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using MR-Egger regression and weighted median methods. The IVW method integrates causal effect estimates from all valid instrumental variables (weighted by the inverse of standard errors), providing optimal unbiased estimates when the assumptions of instrument strength (F-statistic > 10) and absence of significant horizontal pleiotropy are satisfied. Given potential pleiotropic bias in genetic instruments, MR-Egger regression was simultaneously applied for validation. This method detects horizontal pleiotropy through regression intercept analysis, though its statistical power is substantially affected by weak instrument bias. The robustness of causal inference was ultimately established by evaluating directional consistency across different methods (e.g., concordance in coefficient signs between IVW and MR-Egger) combined with instrument strength metrics.

Data processing and analytical procedures

Overview

This two-sample MR study investigated the potential causal relationship between insomnia and ovarian cancer, with the overall study design illustrated in Fig. 1. Initial data filtering removed single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) located in the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region, rare SNPs (minor allele frequency < 0.01), and palindromic SNPs. Instrumental variables were rigorously selected (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸, linkage disequilibrium r² <0.001) to ensure quality and relevance. Subsequent data harmonization and IV selection employed a 1000 kb window size with consistent effect alleles and directions. Instrument strength was evaluated using F-statistics (all SNPs F > 30, mean F = 38.8) to eliminate weak instrument bias. The MR analysis utilized IVW as the primary method, supplemented by MR-Egger regression, weighted median method, and MR-PRESSO testing. Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-out analysis, horizontal pleiotropy assessment (Egger intercept test), MR-PRESSO global test, and heterogeneity evaluation through Cochran’s Q test.

Analytical flowchart of the mendelian randomization approach investigating the association between insomnia and ovarian cancer

SNP selection and data quality control

We extracted genetic variant data from insomnia GWAS (ukb-a-13) and ovarian cancer GWAS (ieu-b-4963). Through a rigorous quality control pipeline, we removed SNPs in the MHC region, rare SNPs with MAF < 0.01, and palindromic SNPs. After validating independence via LD analysis (r² <0.001, window size 1,000 kb), 30 high-quality SNPs were retained. The strength of genetic instruments was assessed using the F-statistic, calculated as F = βₓ²/SEₓ². All SNPs exhibited F-values > 30 (mean 38.8, range 30.1–150.8), significantly exceeding the weak instrument threshold (F > 10), confirming sufficient instrument strength.

Pleiotropy testing

To rigorously evaluate the specificity of genetic instruments (SNPs), we systematically conducted pleiotropy testing to exclude potential bias arising from SNPs indirectly influencing ovarian cancer through non-insomnia pathways (e.g., other phenotypes or biological mechanisms). Specifically, MR-Egger regression was employed to quantify horizontal pleiotropic effects, with directional pleiotropy determined by the significance of the intercept term (p < 0.05).To assess the heterogeneity among the SNPs, Cochran’s Q test was used in further validating the assumption of homogeneity. The MR-PRESSO (Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier) analysis was performed to test for global pleiotropy using the Global Test, identify potential outlier SNPs, and rerun the estimate on corrected data. We performed a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis by removing an individual SNP at a time and repeating the MR analyses to identify main outliers that could influence the causal estimates. This showed that the estimates were robust against such removal. Analytical error in pleiotropy testing avoided by performing data harmonization and choosing an IV.

Analytical methods

To make the causal effect estimates more robust and reliable, five MR methods were employed in a complementary manner to form an evidence chain. The IVW method provided core effect estimates assuming all SNPs are valid instruments. MR-Egger regression incorporated non-zero intercepts to adjust for directional pleiotropy. Weighted median technique was applied so that resistance to outliers was enhanced under the condition that ≥50% of SNPs are valid. We further combined simple mode and weighted mode (based on SNP-exposure effect distribution) to address instrument heterogeneity. The strength of genetic instruments was assessed using F-statistics. The F-values of all SNPs used in our analysis were >30 (mean =38.8, range =30.1–150.8). This outcome suggests that the instruments were strong enough to prevent weak instrument bias. The verification of causal associations was consistent across all methods, with all the analyses performed being adjusted for multiple testing (using Bonferroni correction). In addition, radial MR and F-statistics were used to determine the validity of the instruments, and the MR-PRESSO Distortion Test was used to evaluate the stability of the causal effect when outlier SNPs were detected.

Interpretation of results

When interpreting IVW and IVM results, we prioritized biological plausibility of effect direction, statistical significance, and potential bias sources. By comparing effect estimates from both methods, we evaluated compliance with instrument variable assumptions (e.g., relevance, exclusion restriction, independence) and explored potential biological mechanisms linking insomnia to ovarian cancer (e.g., hormonal regulation, inflammatory pathways, circadian disruption). Sensitivity analyses further examined pleiotropy, weak instrument bias, and heterogeneity to confirm the robustness of primary findings. Additionally, we integrated existing epidemiological evidence to discuss the clinical implications of insomnia as a risk factor for ovarian cancer and proposed critical questions for future research (e.g., dose-response relationships or subgroup variations).

Results of Mendelian randomization analysis for individual SNPs

Consistency of results across MR methods

The IVW method yielded an effect size of 0.0096 (p = 0.0252), indicating a statistically significant positive association between insomnia and ovarian cancer. In contrast, MR-Egger analysis produced an effect size of 0.0098 (p = 0.4645), which did not reach statistical significance. The weighted median method showed an effect size of 0.0103 (p = 0.0955), also non-significant. Although MR-Egger regression demonstrates robustness to horizontal pleiotropy and the weighted median method tolerates some invalid instrumental variables, both approaches exhibit relatively lower statistical power and less stable estimates. Conversely, the IVW method provides higher efficiency and stability under the assumption of valid instrumental variables, making it the primary analytical framework for this study. Based on IVW results, a significant positive correlation was observed between insomnia and ovarian cancer risk (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.01, p = 0.02), where OR > 1 suggests insomnia may increase ovarian cancer risk. Results from different MR methods are illustrated in Fig. 3.

Horizontal lines are the 95% confidence intervals for the estimated effect. Most estimates were close to 0.010 and the confidence intervals mostly ruled out zero. MR leave-one-out sensitivity analysis results

The thirty SNPs assessed presented a mean F-statistic of 38.8 (30.1–150.8), which certainly demonstrated strong instrument strength (i.e., F < 10 suggests weak instruments).Thus, the differences that we observed between the IVW and other MR estimators are unlikely due to weak instrument bias. It is important to note that though IVW was significant (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.01, p= 0.02), the effect size was very small (β = 0.0089) and non-significant in MR-Egger and weighted median means doubt on clinical utility. Current evidence, alongside sensitivity analyses, suggests insignificant evidence for a possible association between insomnia and ovarian cancer risk requiring larger studies for confirmation.

Effect estimates across MR methods and sensitivity analyses

For the evaluation of potential horizontal pleiotropy effect, we applied Egger intercept test. The significant value of the intercept Egger was 0.9863, which was much higher than 0.05 meaning there was no horizontal pleiotropy. Horizontal pleiotropy occurs when a genetic variant influences both the exposure (insomnia) and the outcome (ovarian cancer) through a pathway independent of the exposure. Due to the insignificant Egger test, we can interpret the MR results with more confidence. Thus, the insomnia-ovarian cancer association is likely to reflect a causal association rather one due to confounding or pleiotropic bias.Furthermore, Fig. 5 demonstrates consistent effect estimates across multiple MR approaches, including IVW, MR Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode, reinforcing the robustness of our findings.

Effect estimates and sensitivity analysis of the association between insomnia and ovarian cancer using different MR methods

Instrument strength was assessed using F-statistics, with all SNPs exhibiting F-values ranging from 30.1 to 150.8 (mean: 38.8), well above the weak instrument threshold (F > 10). This definitively excludes weak instrument bias as a confounding factor for the IVW results. Although IVW demonstrated statistical significance (p = 0.026), the absolute effect size was minimal (β = 0.0089, OR = 1.009), and non-significant results from alternative methods warrant cautious interpretation of clinical relevance. In sensitivity analyses, leave-one-out procedures revealed stable effect estimates clustered around 0.010 (95% CI: 0.005–0.015), with no evidence of influential outlier SNPs.

The robustness of the results was further validated through MR-PRESSO analysis. The global test revealed no significant horizontal pleiotropy (RSSobs = 25.97, p = 0.731), and no outlier SNPs were detected (Outlier Test: NULL), indicating that the IVW estimates were not influenced by pleiotropy-driven outliers. The Egger intercept test yielded a p-value of 0.9863, substantially exceeding the 0.05 significance threshold, further supporting the absence of directional pleiotropic bias. In heterogeneity analyses, both the IVW method (Cochran’s Q = 23.76, p = 0.643) and MR-Egger method (Cochran’s Q = 23.76, p = 0.589) showed no significant heterogeneity, suggesting consistency in SNP effect directions.

Although sensitivity analyses revealed no evidence of pleiotropy or heterogeneity, the discrepancies in results across MR methods warrant cautious interpretation. The IVW method demonstrated a significant positive association between insomnia and ovarian cancer (β = 0.0089, p = 0.026), whereas MR-Egger (β = 0.0098, p = 0.4645) and weighted median methods (β = 0.0103, p = 0.0955) failed to reach statistical significance. These discrepancies may stem from the lower statistical power of the latter two methods, particularly when the true effect size is small (OR ≈ 1.01). Consequently, we emphasize that the significance of IVW results might be susceptible to weak instrument bias or undetected non-directional pleiotropy, and the current evidence strength remains insufficient to support definitive causal conclusions.

Discussion

The global health impact of insomnia on ovarian cancer is primarily manifested through its significant negative effects on patients’ quality of life, particularly among elderly patients and long-term survivors [13]. Although the direct causal relationship between insomnia and ovarian cancer remains unconfirmed [14], effective sleep management and interventions can substantially improve the overall health status of ovarian cancer patients [14, 15].This study implemented an MR analysis using genetic variants of insomnia as instrumental variables to investigate their causal link to ovarian cancer. The value of this can help dodge reverse causation biases and over-claims made by observational studies by providing researchers a great means of telling a causal inference that separates out the biological mechanisms that connect them. Moreover, this study not only augments the robustness of causal inference. Furthermore, by using GWAS summary data within two-sample MR framework, it also provides novel insights for future biological mechanism research.

The primary finding of this study is a statistically significant positive association between insomnia and ovarian cancer risk identified through IVW analysis (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.01, p = 0.02), suggesting that insomnia may increase ovarian cancer risk through specific biological pathways. However, results from MR-Egger (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.99–1.01, p = 0.68) and weighted median methods (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.99–1.01, p = 0.21) did not reach statistical significance. These discrepancies may be attributed to multiple factors including sample size, genetic background, and the strength/quantity of instrumental variables. While MR-Egger demonstrates robustness against horizontal pleiotropy, its relatively low statistical power may lead to unstable effect estimates. Similarly, although the weighted median method can tolerate partially invalid instruments, the limited number or effect size of instruments in this study might have contributed to the lack of statistical significance. Such methodological heterogeneity necessitates cautious interpretation of causal effects and underscores the need for subsequent studies to expand sample sizes, optimize instrument selection strategies, and validate findings across multi-ethnic populations to further elucidate the insomnia-ovarian cancer relationship.Our MR analysis revealed significant genetic associations between insomnia-related variants (e.g., rs10087341, rs11152363) and elevated ovarian cancer risk (IVW OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.01, p = 0.02), suggesting that insomnia may influence ovarian carcinogenesis through multi-system interactions.

Insomnia, a prevalent sleep disorder, is associated with hyperactivation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and elevated cortisol levels, particularly during evening and nocturnal periods [4]. SNPs such as rs10087341 and rs11152363 are located within or near genes including NR3C1 (glucocorticoid receptor) and CRHBP (corticotropin-releasing hormone binding protein), suggesting potential genetic links between insomnia and cortisol rhythm dysregulation [16]. The HPA axis, a pivotal neuroendocrine system, regulates physiological stress responses through cortisol secretion [17]. Disruption of HPA axis rhythmicity leads to cortisol-melatonin imbalance, which abnormally activates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway via ERα phosphorylation. Such circadian disturbances may induce HPA axis dysfunction, subsequently contributing to various psychiatric and metabolic disorders [18, 19]. Chronic insomnia downregulates aromatase activity through the HPA axis-melatonin-CYP19A1 axis, resulting in estrogen accumulation and activation of the ERα/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, significantly increasing ovarian cancer risk (2.1-fold elevation in ERα phosphorylation and 60% increased tumor incidence in animal models, P < 0.05). These biological mechanisms have been validated in observational studies and experimental models [20, 21]. However, MR analysis only reveals genetic associations without demonstrating direct SNP-mediated regulation of HPA axis function, necessitating functional validation experiments. The potential indirect effects of HPA axis dysregulation on ovarian cancer through estrogen pathways warrant re-evaluation of insomnia’s independent effects after controlling for estrogen levels.

The association between insomnia and elevated systemic inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6) has been established in multiple studies, with chronic inflammation recognized as a key driver of ovarian carcinogenesis [22–24]. Emerging evidence suggests potential genomic co-localization between insomnia-associated SNPs (e.g., rs1925501, rs3808937) and inflammatory genes such as IL6R and NLRP3 [25, 26], revealing a potential pathway through which genetic variants may promote tumor microenvironment formation via inflammasome activation mechanisms. Insomnia-related SNPs enhance NLRP3 inflammasome activity, inducing release of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β and IL-18, thereby perpetuating chronic inflammatory states [27]. Inflammasome activation and amplified IL-6 signaling may promote infiltration of immunosuppressive cells (e.g., Tregs and MDSCs), suppressing anti-tumor immune responses [28]. Chronic inflammation potentially facilitates tumor angiogenesis through upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), supporting tumor growth and metastasis [29].Our MR analyses did not explicitly assess indices of inflammation, so we cannot tell from current evidence whether the inflammation arises from the local tumor microenvironment inflammation or systemic inflammation. Mediation analysis would be conducted in future to elaborate mechanism.

Circadian rhythm genes regulate diverse physiological processes including cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis through biological clock modulation [30]. Core clock genes such as PER2 and CRY1 govern cell cycle-related gene expression via transcription/translation feedback loops [30]. SNPs may influence circadian gene expression by altering functional elements in promoters, enhancers, or other regulatory regions. The PER2 SNP rs934945 demonstrates significant association with human chronotype, suggesting its potential role in modulating circadian function through PER2 expression levels [31]. The rs1056560 polymorphism (T/G genotypes) differentially affects miRNA binding (e.g., miR-381 and miR-300) to the CRY1 3’UTR, thereby regulating CRY1 mRNA expression [32]. In gastric cancer tissues, rs1056560 genotypes correlate significantly with CRY1 mRNA levels, further supporting SNP-mediated regulation of CRY1 expression [32]. Circadian disruption suppresses transcriptional regulation of BRCA1/2 promoters by core clock genes (PER1/2, CRY1, BMAL1, CLOCK), markedly reducing BRCA1/2 protein expression and causing homologous recombination repair (HRR) deficiency, thereby increasing breast cancer risk [33]. Given that BRCA1/2 mutations and HRR defects constitute shared genetic susceptibility foundations for breast and ovarian cancers, and considering ovarian cancer tissues similarly exhibit circadian gene regulation of BRCA1/2 transcriptional activity, these mechanisms hold critical pathogenic significance in ovarian carcinogenesis [34].

The clinical relevance of insomnia as an ovarian cancer risk factor has gained support from epidemiological studies. Sleep disorders, including insomnia, have been associated with increased cancer risk, particularly for ovarian malignancies [35]. A population-based cohort study reported significantly higher ovarian cancer incidence among sleep disorder patients compared to controls, with an adjusted incidence rate ratio (IRR) of 1.30 (95% CI: 1.10–1.52) [35]. Our MR analysis (IVW OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.01, p = 0.02) provides genetic evidence supporting the insomnia-ovarian cancer association, complementing previous observational studies: while the Korean National Health Insurance Service (KNHIS) cohort showed reduced ovarian cancer risk in insomniac women (aHR = 0.856, p = 0.0344) [36], the U.S. Women’s Health Initiative (WHI) study identified elevated risk of invasive serous ovarian cancer associated with sleep disturbances. These findings suggest insomnia may promote specific subtypes through neuroendocrine-immune-inflammatory pathways, underscoring the need for heightened attention in public health strategies [11].

Notably, insomnia-associated genetic variants may influence ovarian cancer risk through pleiotropic effects. GWAS indicate that SNPs such as rs10087341 and rs11152363 are implicated in sleep regulation [37]. Furthermore, insomnia-related SNPs demonstrate genome-wide level indirect associations with major depressive disorder risk and BMI, supporting the mediating role of metabolic pathways [14].The results indicate that variants implicated in insomnia may contribute to ovarian cancer development through neuroendocrine, metabolic, and behavioral changes, rather than simply through sleep disturbance and its consequences on the body and mind.

It is particularly noteworthy that although the IVW method revealed a statistically significant association between insomnia and ovarian cancer risk (OR = 1.00, 95% CI 1.00–1.01), the corresponding absolute risk increase falls within the 0–1% range. At the individual level, this implies that insomnia alone may not constitute sufficient clinical indication for intervention. From a public health perspective, even assuming causal relationships, sleep intervention strategies based on this effect size would have minimal impact on reducing population-level ovarian cancer burden. This minimal effect suggests three critical implications: First, insomnia may exert clinical effects through synergistic interactions with other risk factors (e.g., obesity, chronic inflammation). Second, the higher risk ratios reported in observational studies (e.g., IRR = 1.30) might be influenced by residual confounding factors. Third, the genetic effects captured by MR analysis may primarily reflect cumulative biological consequences of chronic insomnia rather than direct impacts of transient sleep disturbances.

This study has several limitations. First, although MR-PRESSO and Egger intercept tests detected no significant horizontal pleiotropy, discrepancies between IVW and supplementary methods suggest potential undetected non-directional pleiotropy. Second, the limited variance in exposure explained by instrumental variables may introduce weak instrument bias. Additionally, the OR value of 1.01 requires cautious clinical interpretation considering biological plausibility. While European population genetic data effectively controlled for population stratification bias, this limits generalizability to non-European populations and concurrently restricts the instruments’ capacity to elucidate candidate biological pathways.

Future research should focus on several key directions. First, multinational collaborations should establish cross-ethnic genetic databases to validate instrument generalizability across populations. Second, integrating genome-wide functional annotation with colocalization analysis could optimize instrument selection strategies to enhance exposure variation interpretation. Furthermore, combining single-cell sequencing with multi-omics data within MR frameworks could facilitate mediation analyses to delineate specific pathways of candidate mechanisms such as the HPA axis-estrogen axis. Concurrently, developing multidimensional multivariable MR (MVMR) models incorporating traits like depression, BMI, smoking, and alcohol consumption is essential. Additionally, functional validation experiments employing organoid models and gene-editing technologies are needed to clarify biological effects of key SNPs in ovarian carcinogenesis. Finally, establishing prospective cohorts to track insomnia phenotype dynamics and developing novel MR methods distinguishing direct effects from confounding pathways are recommended.

Conclusion

This study employed a two-sample MR analysis to reveal for the first time a positive genetic association between insomnia and ovarian cancer risk (IVW OR = 1.00, p = 0.02). The findings suggest that chronic insomnia may influence ovarian carcinogenesis through potential mechanisms involving the HPA axis-estrogen pathway, inflammatory microenvironment remodeling, and circadian rhythm gene regulation. Although MR Egger and weighted median methods did not reach statistical significance, multi-methodological evidence supports a limited causal effect of insomnia-related genetic variants on ovarian cancer risk. The current findings are constrained by insufficient instrumental variable explanation power and the generalizability limitations of European population genetic data. Future research should integrate multi-omics mediation analyses, cross-ethnic validation, and functional experiments to further elucidate the spatiotemporal dynamics of insomnia-ovarian cancer associations and their clinical translational value.
