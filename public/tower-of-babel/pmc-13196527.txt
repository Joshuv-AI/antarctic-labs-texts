Nutritional and bioactive properties of native chicken eggs: Global perspectives on human health implications
Authors: Joko Sujiwo, Hyo-Jun Choo, Hee-Jin Kim, Jun Heon Lee, Ok-Hwan Lee, Cho Hee Kim, Aera Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEggs are increasingly recognized not only as nutrient-dense foods but also as sources of bioactive compounds with potential physiological relevance. Native chicken eggs, produced under diverse genetic backgrounds and often under low-input production systems, may exhibit compositional characteristics that differ from those of commercial layer eggs. This review summarizes the major bioactive compounds of native chicken eggs and critically evaluates the current evidence regarding their possible implications for human health. Yolk phospholipids and omega-3 fatty acids have been associated with pathways related to lipid metabolism and membrane function, whereas carotenoids such as lutein and zeaxanthin exhibit antioxidant properties and have been discussed in relation to visual function. Albumen proteins, including lysozyme, ovotransferrin, and ovomucoid, have demonstrated antimicrobial and immunomodulatory activities mainly in experimental settings. Some studies have reported that native chicken eggs contain higher levels of antioxidant micronutrients and distinctive lipid profiles compared with commercial eggs, although such differences are not consistent and may vary with breed, diet, and production systems. Overall, native chicken eggs represent a potentially valuable food resource beyond basic nutrition. However, current evidence for their proposed cardiometabolic, antioxidant, and immune-related effects is derived largely from compositional analyses and from in vitro or animal studies, with only limited evidence in humans. Future research integrating multi-omics approaches, standardized comparative designs, and well-controlled human studies is needed to assess whether the compositional and experimental findings reported for native chicken eggs translate into meaningful benefits in humans and to their development as value-added functional food products.Keywords: Native chicken egg, Bioactive compound, Functional food, Human health

Introduction

Eggs have gained considerable attention as functional foods because they provide a dense package of essential nutrients and diverse bioactive compounds. In this review, the term “bioactive compounds” refers to egg-derived components that, beyond their role in basic nutrition, have been identified in compositional studies and, in some cases, reported to influence biological processes or health-related outcomes in in vitro, animal, or human studies (Vettorazzi et al., 2020; Weaver, 2014). Because the strength of evidence varies substantially across the literature, compositional findings and results from in vitro, animal, and human studies are considered separately, and the term does not imply established clinical efficacy in humans (Weaver, 2014). Functional foods are dietary components that confer physiological benefits beyond basic nutrition and may contribute to disease risk reduction and health promotion (Milner, 2000). Eggs are notable sources of high-quality proteins, vitamins (e.g., A, B12, and riboflavin), choline, and minerals such as iron, zinc, and calcium (Réhault-Godbert et al., 2019). Beyond their nutrient contribution, egg-derived molecules have been reported to exhibit antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory activities, with evidence derived primarily from in vitro or cell-based assays, and to a lesser extent from animal models (Pellegrini et al., 2004; Meram and Wu, 2017; Sujiwo et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2022; Jung et al., 2025). By contrast, human studies have evaluated egg consumption and related biomarkers or health outcomes, but direct evidence linking specific egg-derived bioactive molecules to physiological effects in humans remains limited (Iannotti et al., 2017a; Thomas et al., 2022). These activities are primarily attributed to protein- and lipid-derived constituents, whose profiles vary with genetics, feeding regimes, freshness, and post-processing conditions. Consistent with this distribution of bioactive compounds between the albumen and yolk (Fig. 1), antimicrobial and defense-related proteins are predominant in the albumen, whereas lipid-soluble compounds are concentrated in the yolk, a pattern that may contribute to the breed- and production system-dependent differences observed in native chicken eggs.

Recent analytical advances have highlighted the molecular complexity of egg-derived bioactives. Untargeted lipidomic studies have shown that egg yolk contains a wide range of lipid species, including multiple phospholipid classes and hundreds of distinct molecular species, with docosahexaenoic acid enriched at the sn-2 position of several phospholipids (Cheng et al., 2024). This structural detail is potentially important because both phospholipid class and fatty-acid position can affect lipid bioaccessibility, membrane-related functions, and the nutritional relevance of yolk lipids. At the same time, peptidomic analyses have shown that major egg proteins, such as ovotransferrin, ovalbumin, and ovomucin, can release peptide fragments with reported antioxidant and angiotensin-converting enzyme-inhibitory activities during simulated digestion (Song et al., 2024). Together, these findings suggest that digestion influences not only protein degradation but also the generation of potentially functional compounds from eggs. However, these omics-based observations remain primarily compositional and mechanistic, and their physiological relevance in humans has yet to be established.

Alongside growing interest in functional foods, global attention has increasingly focused on conserving agricultural biodiversity, including native chicken breeds. In this review, native chickens refer to locally adapted breeds commonly described as indigenous, whose origin, adaptation, and use over the long term are tied to a specific geographical region (FAO, 2001). Native chickens raised in villages are typically maintained in low-input extensive systems and represent important genetic resources that support adaptation to local climates and endemic disease pressures (Lawal and Hanotte, 2021). Compared with those from commercial layers, eggs from native breeds often differ in size, yolk-to-albumen ratio, shell characteristics, nutrient composition, and techno-functional properties relevant to processing (e.g., foaming and emulsification) (Gumułka et al., 2022; Mulyani et al., 2021a; Sirri et al., 2018; Sokołowicz et al., 2019). Accumulating evidence suggests that some native chicken eggs may show higher antioxidant capacity in chemical assays and related in vitro experimental systems than eggs from commercial layer hens under certain conditions. For example, yolks from a Korean native chicken line (Woorimatdag No. 2) showed higher 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical-scavenging activity, 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) radical-scavenging activity, and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) values than those from Hy-Line Brown in a recent comparative study (Jung et al., 2025). Similarly, a proteomic comparison between Tibetan native and commercial eggs reported greater in vitro antioxidant capacity in yolk proteins from the Tibetan breed, which the authors linked to oxidative stress-related pathways, particularly HIF-1 signaling, glutathione metabolism, and arachidonic acid metabolism, with glutathione peroxidase 3 identified as the main candidate protein (Liu et al., 2020). However, these findings reflect differences in measured antioxidant-related properties rather than confirmed antioxidant effects in humans. Such differences may reflect both genotype and production context. In a free-range comparison using Mos and Isa Brown hens under three diets, Mos eggs showed higher fat (9.53 % vs. 7.58 %), protein (12.31 % vs. 11.66 %), and ash (1.10 % vs. 1.04 %) than Isa Brown eggs, while diet also significantly affected the fatty acid profile (Franco et al., 2020). Likewise, under the same organic production system, yolk cholesterol differed between Italian breeds and hybrids (27.0–27.3 vs. 25.2–25.4 mg/g), and yolk n-3 PUFA varied by genotype from 1.97 to 2.34 % (Rizzi and Chiericato, 2010).

Consumer preferences align with sustainability and quality narratives. Surveys in multiple countries indicate a willingness to pay for welfare-friendly and/or native breed eggs, and several studies have reported distinctive sensory attributes in eggs from endangered native breeds (Buddle et al., 2025; González Ariza et al., 2019; Kralik et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2017; Ndenga et al., 2017; Spain et al., 2018; Wulandari et al., 2020). Collectively, these market signals support premium niches for eggs produced by native breeds and alternative production systems.

Despite these ecological, nutritional, and economic drivers, the literature remains strongly weighted toward commercial chickens. In a scientometric review of 22,451 articles published in Poultry Science over 1921–2020, broilers and laying hens accounted for 6,156 and 3,472 articles, respectively, whereas studies indexed under indigenous chickens, a category broadly corresponding to native chickens in the present review, accounted for only 229, indicating a substantial evidence imbalance (Vaziri et al., 2022). This disparity limits robust synthesis of whether, and under what conditions, native chicken eggs provide distinctive nutritional or bioactive advantages.

In addition to poultry and egg quality, the bioactive compounds of eggs are increasingly being discussed in relation to human health outcomes, particularly cardiometabolic, antioxidant, and immune-related endpoints. However, much of the current evidence is based on compositional and preclinical studies, and the extent to which native chicken eggs confer distinct health effects on humans has not been established.

Accordingly, this review synthesizes and critically evaluates the available evidence on the nutritional composition and bioactive compounds of native chicken eggs, including protein- and lipid-derived compounds and their reported antioxidant, antimicrobial, antihypertensive, and immunomodulatory activities. Emphasis is placed on distinguishing compositional observations from in vitro, animal, and human evidence, and on comparing native chicken eggs with commercial layer eggs within the context of breed, diet, and production systems. By identifying areas of consistency, uncertainty, and methodological limitation, this review aims to clarify the current state of knowledge and to outline priorities for future research, product development, and the conservation of native chicken genetic resources.

Methodology of the review

This article presents a structured narrative review based on a transparent literature search and predefined eligibility criteria. The review aimed to summarize the nutritional composition and bioactive compounds of native chicken eggs and to critically assess their potential relevance to human health, with particular attention to differences among native breeds and between native and commercial eggs.

The literature search was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science as the primary databases, with Google Scholar screened only as a supplementary source to identify additional relevant studies. The final search was completed in January 2026, and the search covered records from database inception to that date. Search terms were combined across concept groups related to native chicken descriptors (“native chicken,” “traditional chicken,” “indigenous chicken,” “local chicken,” and “village chicken”), egg-related terms (“egg,” “egg yolk,” “egg white,” and “albumen”), bioactive compounds (“bioactive compounds,” “egg proteins,” “egg peptides,” “egg yolk lipids,” “carotenoids,” “phospholipids,” and “fatty acids”), and health relevance (“functional food” and “human health”). Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to combine search terms, and the reference lists of selected articles were manually screened to identify additional relevant studies.

Eligible studies were peer-reviewed English-language articles reporting data on the composition, nutritional quality, bioactive compounds, or health-related properties of eggs from native, indigenous, or locally adapted chicken breeds. Comparative studies involving commercial layer eggs were included when they informed breed-related differences. Compositional, in vitro, animal, and human studies were considered when they addressed the biological activities or nutritional implications of egg-derived compounds. Review articles were used to contextualize mechanisms and identify additional primary literature, but were not treated as substitutes for original evidence. Studies were excluded if they focused exclusively on non-chicken eggs, addressed poultry production traits without relevant egg-composition or bioactivity data, lacked sufficient methodological detail, or were available only as abstracts, conference proceedings, or duplicate reports. Non-English or non-peer-reviewed sources were used only for illustrative purposes and were not included in the analytical synthesis.

Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible articles. Extracted information included country or region, breed or genotype, production system, dietary background, egg fraction analyzed, target nutrients or bioactive compounds, study design, comparator group, and principal findings. To strengthen interpretation, evidence was classified as compositional, in vitro, animal, or human. Studies were also appraised qualitatively according to breed identification, description of diet and production system, adequacy of analytical methods, appropriateness of comparator groups, and alignment between study design and the strength of the conclusions. Greater interpretive weight was given to studies with clearly defined breed identity, appropriate comparator groups, and adequately described dietary and production-system conditions. Findings from studies with limited contextual or methodological detail were interpreted more cautiously.

Because the available literature was highly heterogeneous in breed, feeding strategy, production system, analytical methodology, and reported outcomes, formal meta-analysis was not considered appropriate. Findings were therefore synthesized narratively and weighted according to the strength and translational relevance of the evidence. Results from compositional and preclinical studies were not interpreted as equivalent to confirmed physiological effects in humans. Claims regarding the health relevance of native chicken eggs were framed cautiously, especially when human data were limited, inconsistent, or absent.

Quality and nutritional background

Egg quality is typically assessed from shell characteristics and the physical state of the albumen and yolk. Because the studies summarized in this section differ in comparator groups and in the degree of control over diet, age, and management, the findings should be interpreted according to the study design used in each study. Some studies provide head-to-head comparisons between native and commercial layers under similar conditions, whereas others compare only native breeds or reflect combined effects of breed, feeding, and production systems. Accordingly, the patterns discussed below should be interpreted as context-dependent rather than as universal attributes of all native chicken eggs. Shell thickness was likewise heterogeneous. Several native breeds raised under extensive conditions, such as Egyptian Fayoumi and Turkish village hens, had thicker shells than commercial layers, a trait associated with a dense calcium-rich palisade layer (Cicek and Kartalkanat, 2009; Fathi et al., 2019). In contrast, Black Castellana and Woorimatdag eggs were comparable to or slightly thinner than Lohmann Classic and Hy-Line Brown eggs (Jung et al., 2025; Romero et al., 2024). Table 2 illustrates this variability, Turkish village eggs showed greater shell thickness than the commercial comparator (0.37 vs 0.34 mm), whereas Black Castellana (0.336–0.342 mm) and Woorimatdag-related Korean native lines (0.38–0.40 mm) were comparable to or thinner than Lohmann Classic (0.361–0.364 mm) and Hy-Line Brown (0.43 mm), respectively. Thus, shell robustness should be evaluated case by case because it is influenced by genetics, diet, hen age, and housing systems.

Evidence from a controlled comparison under the same extensive management supports a strong age effect on egg physical traits. In Polish native breeds (Polish Liliputy Bantams, Polish Crested/CP-22, and Gold Laced Polish Chickens) and Hy-Line Brown, egg weight and the proportions of major fractions increased with hen age (33 to 55 weeks), whereas albumen quality (albumen height and Haugh unit (HU)) and eggshell quality (including thickness and strength) declined with age (Lewko et al., 2024). Relative to Hy-Line Brown, eggs from native breeds showed yolk proportion and yolk color that exceeded the hybrid, whereas albumen height, HU, and several shell traits were lower than Hy-Line Brown; however, albumen height and HU in older native hens remained within commonly accepted ranges for marketable table eggs reported in that study (Lewko et al., 2024).

Because HU is derived from egg weight and albumen height, is a widely used indicator of albumen quality and freshness. Under identical free-range conditions in Türkiye, eggs from the domestic ATAK-S line showed lower HU values than Lohmann Brown eggs but values comparable to those of Lohmann Sandy eggs (Aygun et al., 2025). Another study covering nine European breeds reported mean HU values that matched or slightly exceeded those of ISA Brown commercial layers (Lordelo et al., 2020). Together, these findings indicate that albumen quality in some native breeds can approach that of commercial layers, but the direction and magnitude of differences remain breed- and context-dependent. Table 2 illustrates this variability: HU values were lower in Indian native breeds than in White Leghorn (85.24–86.99 vs 94.98) and lower in Black Castellana than in Lohmann Classic (79.9–81.2 vs 86.1–90.3), but were similar to or slightly higher than the comparator in Polish Crested versus Hy-Line Brown (88.0 vs 87.3) and Turkish village eggs versus commercial eggs (85.82 vs 82.64). Even under identical intensive management and the same commercial diet, HU can still differ among native ecotypes, as shown in Beninese native chickens (Houngbo et al., 2026).

Yolk color is influenced by both breed and diet because carotenoid-rich feed promotes xanthophyll deposition in the yolk. Several studies reported deeper yolk color or higher Roche yolk color scores in native chicken eggs than in commercial eggs (Cicek and Kartalkanat, 2009; Gumułka et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024b). However, this pattern is not universal and depends strongly on carotenoid intake and production system. For example, in Table 2 yolk color values reached 11.94 in Turkish village eggs compared with 7.80 in commercial eggs, and 11.65 in Taihe silky fowl eggs compared with 5.92 in Hy-Line Brown. In contrast, Portuguese native breeds showed lower yolk color scores (8.64–9.55) than Tetra Brown (13.33). Shell pigmentation, by comparison, reflects deposition of pigments such as protoporphyrin IX and biliverdin and contributes to consumer preferences that vary across countries (Aygun et al., 2025).

The perception of egg quality among consumers extends beyond laboratory metrics. In various cultural contexts, darker yolks are often associated with rural production and perceived as more nutritious. This cultural association significantly affects the native egg market value. Some pigments (carotenoids and xanthophylls) have antioxidant activities and provide nutritional benefits (Crupi et al., 2023). Knowing how breed and feed affect shell and yolk traits will help producers meet niche market demands.

Whole eggs contain approximately three-quarters water and supply approximately 12 g/100 g protein and 10 g/100 g lipid, delivering complete essential amino acids alongside triglycerides and phospholipids such as phosphatidylcholine (PC) (Réhault-Godbert et al., 2019). Comparative studies indicate that some native and commercial eggs can show similar macro- and selected micronutrient profiles, particularly when diets are comparable. For example, Taihe black-boned silky fowl eggs matched Hy-Line Brown layers in crude protein, fat, triglyceride, and cholesterol content, differing mainly in minor amino and fatty acid profiles (Zhang et al., 2024b). Likewise, a nutrient survey in rural Malawi found similar calorie, protein, and water-soluble vitamin contents per 100 g basis in village-chicken and commercial layer eggs (Werner et al., 2019). However, these findings should not be generalized across all native breeds and production systems.

Although macro- and micro-nutrient profiles may converge under comparable diets, yolk lipid composition can still vary markedly by breed. Importantly, not all studies in this area compare native breeds directly with commercial layers. For example, in three Sicilian native breeds reared under similar semi-extensive conditions (Siciliana, Cornuta di Caltanissetta, and Valplatani), Cornuta eggs showed lower saturated fatty acid and higher monounsaturated fatty acid proportions, with oleic acid reaching 50.74 % versus 39.92 % in Siciliana and 43.36 % in Valplatani (Lo Presti et al., 2025). Similarly, a comparison among four native South Indian breeds (Aseel, Aseel hybrid, Siruvidai, and Peruvidai) reported breed-dependent differences in internal egg composition, even though no commercial layer eggs were included (Radha and Amritha, 2025). These within-native comparisons are informative for illustrating heterogeneity among native breeds, but they should not be interpreted as direct evidence that native chicken eggs are uniformly superior to commercial eggs.

Albumen contains approximately 88 % water and 10–11 % protein (Réhault-Godbert et al., 2019). Its major proteins include ovalbumin, ovotransferrin, ovomucoid, ovomucin, and lysozyme, each of which contributes to antimicrobial activity, nutrient binding, and foaming properties. Egg yolk contains approximately 15–16 g protein/100 g (Réhault-Godbert et al., 2019), mainly in the form of lipoproteins. Approximately two-thirds of yolk proteins are packaged as low-density lipoproteins (LDL) and one-sixth as high-density lipoproteins (HDL), with smaller amounts of phosvitin and livetins (Oladimeji and Gebhardt, 2023). LDL act as natural emulsifiers, whereas HDL contribute to gel formation, and phosvitin binds minerals and exhibits antioxidant activity.

Approximately one-third of yolk dry matter (DM) consists of fat, two-thirds of which are neutral lipids and one-third phospholipids, dominated by PC and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) (Abeyrathne et al., 2022). These phospholipids are embedded in LDL and HDL micelles, stabilize emulsions, and supply essential fatty acids. The fatty acid profile is diet-dependent; fish oil and flaxseed increase omega‑3 levels, while corn-based diets raise omega‑6 fatty acids. Thus, PC and PE may exert hepatoprotective and anti‑inflammatory effects (Abeyrathne et al., 2022).

Eggs contain nearly every vitamin except vitamin C. The yolk is rich in fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K, and several B vitamins, while the albumen contains riboflavin and niacin (Réhault-Godbert et al., 2019). Eggs also contain bioavailable minerals. Two eggs cover 10–30 % of the daily requirements for many micronutrients. Eggs are among the richest natural sources of choline, primarily PC.

Eggs supply macro‑ and trace minerals vital for bone health, oxygen transport, and enzyme function. Most minerals accumulate in the yolk, whereas the albumen contains more potassium and sodium. A study conducted in Portugal found that eggs from four native chicken breeds had mineral levels similar to or higher than those in commercially produced eggs (Lordelo et al., 2020). However, this observation was derived from a specific comparative setting and should not be interpreted as evidence of a universal mineral advantage across all native breeds.

Egg proteins and lipids provide foods with desirable textures that are essential for many culinary applications. These properties underpin techniques such as soufflés, custards, and mayonnaise. Understanding these functional properties (foaming, emulsifying, gelation, and coagulation) will help chefs design products with desirable textures and nutrient retention. When albumen entrains air, it creates a foam. Albumen proteins unfold and orient at the air–water interface; hydrophobic regions face the air, and hydrophilic regions remain in the liquid (Lomakina and Míková, 2006). This orientation lowers surface tension and allows bubbles to be stabilized by a protein film. Foam stability is limited by drainage and bubble coalescence. Interactions among globulins, lysozyme, and ovomucin increase elasticity and prolong foam life.

Egg yolk is a potent emulsifier because of its dispersed lipoproteins and phospholipids. LDL micelles in the plasma fraction effectively stabilize oil-in-water emulsions and form the basis for mayonnaise (Abeyrathne et al., 2022). The granule fraction, consisting of HDL and phosvitin, has lower solubility and emulsifying power; however, treatments such as pH adjustment or salt addition improve its performance (Oladimeji and Gebhardt, 2023). Differences in yolk composition among breeds may influence emulsifying behavior, but direct comparative evidence for superior emulsion stability in native chicken eggs remains limited.

Heating eggs causes the proteins to denature and aggregate to form gels that trap water and fat. Gelation involves unfolded proteins associating through hydrogen and hydrophobic bonds to create an irreversible network that defines the texture of cooked eggs (Zang et al., 2023). Egg yolk granules form heat-resistant gels because of their high HDL and phosvitin contents (Oladimeji and Gebhardt, 2023). Gel strength is affected by pH, salt, sugar, and heating rate, and breeds with more phosvitin may produce firmer gels.

In addition to their macronutrients, eggs harbor bioactive molecules. Proteins, such as ovotransferrin, lysozyme, ovomucin, and phosvitin, exhibit antimicrobial and antioxidant activities (Réhault-Godbert et al., 2019). Enzymatic digestion can release peptides with antihypertensive and anti-inflammatory effects. Yolk phospholipids provide cardiovascular and liver benefits, whereas carotenoids such as lutein support eye health (Abeyrathne et al., 2022). The abundance and functional relevance of these compounds depend on multiple factors, including breed, diet, and production systems, with breed-related differences in yolk lipoprotein composition also having been reported (Oladimeji and Gebhardt, 2023). Accordingly, native chicken eggs should be considered a potentially valuable source of bioactive compounds, but their health-related advantages require evaluation in breed-authenticated and well-controlled comparative studies.

Bioactive proteins and peptides in albumen and yolk

Egg proteins are a source of molecules with diverse reported bioactivities beyond their nutritional value, although the supporting evidence varies substantially across biochemical, in vitro, animal, and human studies. The major constituents of albumen, including ovalbumin, ovotransferrin, ovomucoid, ovomucin, lysozyme, ovoinhibitor, ovomacroglobulin, cystatin, and avidin, contribute to the egg’s natural defense system and potential therapeutic uses (Kovacs-Nolan et al., 2005). These proteins and their derived peptides have been associated with antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, antihypertensive, antioxidant, and anticancer activities, whereas antiviral effects have been described for selected egg proteins (Kovacs-Nolan et al., 2005; Mann and Ndung’u, 2020). However, in the context of human nutrition, these reported activities arise from heterogeneous evidence bases and should not be interpreted as equivalent evidence of efficacy in humans.

Although albumen proteins have antimicrobial and structural roles, egg yolk proteins contribute to diverse nutrient- and bioactivity-related functions. Specifically, the principal protein groups of egg yolk include LDL (with apovitellins/apovitellenins as the apoproteins) and HDL composed of lipovitellin, serum-derived livetins (α-, β-, and γ), and phosvitin (Hatta et al., 2008). The minor constituents include egg yolk riboflavin-binding protein and other binding proteins for biotin, thiamine, vitamin B12, retinol, and yolk transferrin (Hatta et al., 2008). These proteins have distinct roles; livetins (including IgY) contribute to immune defense, lipovitellin serves as a nutrient reservoir, phosvitin contributes to metal chelation, antioxidant protection, and mineral transport, and LDL supplies lipid-rich energy while enhancing emulsifying capacity. However, for yolk proteins as well, broader health-related bioactivities should be interpreted according to evidence level, as many reported effects have been demonstrated primarily in isolated fractions, hydrolysates, or experimental models rather than in human studies.

From a nutritional and translational perspective, these albumen proteins are unlikely to contribute equally to human health. Ovalbumin, as the most abundant albumen protein, is likely the most quantitatively relevant component, whereas lysozyme represents a smaller but still substantial albumen fraction and avidin is only a minor constituent (Nimalaratne and Wu, 2015; Khorshidian et al., 2022; Nau et al., 2007). Ovalbumin may be particularly important as a precursor of bioactive peptides released after hydrolysis rather than as an intact bioactive protein itself (Pellegrini et al., 2004; Benedé and Molina, 2020). In contrast, ovotransferrin and lysozyme are more strongly supported as natural antimicrobial proteins within the egg matrix. In addition, culinary heating denatures and aggregates egg proteins, whereas gastrointestinal digestion may further modify their structure while also releasing peptide fragments (Zang et al., 2023; Song et al., 2024). Accordingly, the presence of a bioactive protein in raw egg white should not be interpreted as direct evidence of bioavailability or physiological efficacy after normal cooking and consumption, and for most albumen proteins these aspects remain insufficiently defined.

Ovotransferrin. Ovotransferrin (conalbumin) is a multifunctional albumen protein. Antiviral, antifungal, immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and anticancer effects have been reported in experimental systems, although the strength of evidence differs across in vitro, cell-based, and animal studies (Valenti et al., 1985; Zhu et al., 2018; Fijałkowski et al., 2025). It restricts bacterial growth by sequestering iron (Fe3+) (Rathnapala et al., 2021) and disrupting bacterial membranes (Fijałkowski et al., 2025). Ovotransferrin shows antifungal activity against Candida in vitro, likely through direct interaction with fungal cells (Valenti et al., 1985). Its potential antiviral relevance to SARS-CoV-2 has been proposed in a review context, but direct effects against this virus remain unconfirmed (Mann and Ndung’u, 2020). In experimental systems, autocleaved ovotransferrin has shown pro-apoptotic activity in human colon and breast cancer cell lines through caspase-9 and -6 activation (Ibrahim and Kiyono, 2009), whereas immunomodulatory effects have been reported in a cyclophosphamide-induced mouse model, including altered cytokine expression and enhanced intestinal immune responses (Zhu et al., 2018). Antioxidant-related activity has also been linked to redox-dependent self-cleavage of ovotransferrin into peptides with superoxide dismutase activity under experimental conditions (Ibrahim et al., 2006). However, much of the evidence derives from purified or experimentally modified ovotransferrin, including autocleaved forms and derived peptides, rather than from the protein as consumed in ordinary foods. In addition, the extent to which ovotransferrin remains bioactive after culinary heating and gastrointestinal digestion, or yields peptides with meaningful physiological effects in humans, remains insufficiently defined.

Chicken albumen ovotransferrin occurs as three electrophoretic variants: OTFA, OTFB, and OTFC. Kinoshita et al. (2016) explained that the primary difference between the OTFB and OTFC variants is a single amino acid substitution at residue 500, where aspartic acid in OTFB is replaced by asparagine in OTFC, altering the protein's net surface charge and electrophoretic mobility. However, in the Asian native population, OTFA and OTFC are very low to undetectable (Kinoshita et al., 2016). Kinoshita et al. (2016) revealed that in commercial layer eggs, the OTFB allele was fixed, whereas several Japanese and Taiwanese native chicken eggs were polymorphic and carried OTFB, OTFB/C, and OTFC. In Kinoshita et al. (2016), OTFB was fixed in both commercial layer populations, whereas several native populations remained polymorphic, with OTFC allele frequencies ranging from 0.048 to 0.238 across Satsumadori, Hua-Tung, and Ju-Chi populations; however, purified OTFB and OTFC showed no significant difference in antibacterial activity against S. aureus or E. coli. The diversity of ovotransferrin variants among native chickens may affect its bioactivity, with isoform co-expression in B/C heterozygotes being a potential mechanism underlying differences relative to commercial lines. However, confirming this hypothesis requires testing across an expanded range of pathogens and native albumen matrices.

Breed-dependent variations in albumen antimicrobial protein concentrations, including ovotransferrin, have been documented in native chickens. A multibreed study of 23 native chickens revealed that variations in albumen ovotransferrin and lysozyme levels were primarily influenced by breed and shell pigmentation, whereas sampling year and breeder profile played a minor role (Javůrková et al., 2019). These results suggest that intrinsic factors, including breed genetics and shell color phenotypes, contribute to variability in albumen antimicrobial proteins, including ovotransferrin, in native breeds. Pigmentation explained 66 % of the overall variability in ovotransferrin concentration, compared with 29 % for year of collection and only 5 % for breeder identity; moreover, ovotransferrin concentration was higher in tinted eggs than in blue, white, and brown eggs, whereas dark-brown eggs did not differ significantly from tinted eggs (Javůrková et al., 2019). However, a proteomic comparison of native large breeds (Araucana, Czech Golden-penciled, Minorca) with small breeds (Booted Bantam, Rosecomb Bantam) revealed that ovotransferrin was not among the proteins that differed significantly between classes, even though other antimicrobial albumen proteins were more abundant in large breeds (Javůrková et al., 2019).

Lysozyme. Lysozyme is an alkaline disulfide-stabilized muramidase-type glycosidase (isoelectric point = 10.7; 129 amino acids; 14.3 kDa) (Baron et al., 2016). It is ubiquitous in biological fluids such as tears, saliva, and milk; hen albumen is a major source, comprising approximately 3.5 % of albumen proteins (3.5 g/L), or roughly 0.3 to 0.4 g/egg (Khorshidian et al., 2022).

Lysozyme from hen albumen is widely recognized for antimicrobial activity, particularly against Gram-positive bacteria, by hydrolyzing β-(1,4) glycosidic linkages in bacterial peptidoglycan through catalytic residues Glu35 and Asp52 (Khorshidian et al., 2022). Additional immunomodulatory, antifungal, and antiviral activities have also been reported, although the supporting evidence is mainly preclinical. In review-based discussions, lysozyme has been described as having immunomodulatory potential through reduced neutrophil chemotaxis and oxidative burst, downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, and binding of advanced glycation end-products (Mann and Ndung’u, 2020). Antifungal activity has been demonstrated in vitro against Candida albicans biofilms, in which lysozyme affected biofilm development and fungal cell-associated properties (Sebaa et al., 2017). Antiviral effects have likewise been discussed mainly in mechanistic and review contexts, including possible interference with viral entry, binding to nucleic acids or viral particles, inhibition of virus-induced cell fusion, and modulation of signaling pathways such as NF-κB (Mann and Ndung’u, 2020).

In native chicken eggs, lysozyme content varies with genotype, shell color, age, and husbandry practices. In the comparative analysis of albumen lysozyme parameters, eggs from native Polish breeds, particularly Gold-laced Polish Chickens for overall lysozyme activity and Polish Liliputy Bantams for thin-albumen lysozyme content, demonstrated significantly higher values than those from the commercial Hy‑Line Brown hybrid (Lewko et al., 2024). Native strain differences were also observed in a multi-strain comparison under a genetic-resource program in Poland; eggs from Green-legged Partridge, Yellowleg Partridge, and Rhode Island Red lines had a higher lysozyme content, whereas Leghorn showed the lowest (Lewko et al., 2021). However, these studies quantified lysozyme concentration and enzymatic activity rather than direct antimicrobial outcomes in food matrices or human studies; therefore, the extent to which these differences translate into applied food preservation or human health effects remains uncertain.

Lewko et al. (2021) also reported that eggs with tinted or light-brown shells tended to show higher albumen lysozyme concentrations and enzymatic activity than those with white or blue shells. Native chicken eggs with tinted and dark brown shells contain lysozyme levels that positively correlate with protoporphyrin IX pigment concentrations in the eggshell cuticle, a pattern not observed in other shell color groups (Javůrková et al., 2019). Older layers produce eggs with greater lysozyme concentrations and enzymatic activity, regardless of genotype (Lewko et al., 2021). Under the same management conditions, thin-albumen lysozyme content and activity in Polish traditional breeds ranged from 0.25 to 0.39 % and 52,728 to 82,279 U/mL, respectively, compared with 0.34 % and 70,802 U/mL in Hy-Line Brown, with Gold Laced Polish Chickens showing the highest values (Lewko et al., 2024). Across 23 traditional breeds, eggshell pigmentation explained 52 % of total lysozyme variability, with higher concentrations in dark-brown and tinted eggs than in white and brown eggs (Javůrková et al., 2019). Quantitatively, lysozymes may differ across breeds, shell color, and age; however, their protein profile often remains unchanged (monomorphic) in some native populations, while genetic variation can still occur without modifying the protein itself (Myint et al., 2010; Yan et al., 2017).

Ovalbumin. Ovalbumin is the principal glycoprotein of chicken albumen, accounting for ∼54 % of the total albumen protein, comprising 385 amino acids (Nimalaratne and Wu, 2015). Ovalbumin has been studied mainly as a precursor of bioactive peptides, with reported antimicrobial and antihypertensive activities attributed largely to derived peptides rather than to the intact protein itself (Kovacs-Nolan et al., 2005). Intact ovalbumin is not generally regarded as a strong antioxidant; however, enzymatic hydrolysis generates low-molecular-weight peptides with antioxidant activity through free-radical scavenging and transition-metal chelation, thereby helping to mitigate lipid oxidation and oxidative stress (Maggonage et al., 2025). Consistently, several identified ovalbumin-derived peptides have shown antioxidant capacity, whereas tryptic fragments such as SALAM, SALAMVY, YPILPEYLQ, and ELINSW have exhibited antimicrobial activity against Bacillus subtilis in vitro (Pellegrini et al., 2004; Benedé and Molina, 2020). Immunomodulatory effects have also been reported for ovalbumin-derived peptides in experimental formulations, such as selenium nanoparticles, under preclinical conditions (Zeng et al., 2025).

However, research specifically focusing on ovalbumin characteristics, including bioactivity, in native chicken breeds remains limited. In Japanese native breeds, the ovalbumin (Ov) locus is polymorphic, with OvA dominant, and even fixed, in several breeds, and high inter-breed differentiation (genetic differentiation index ≈ 0.25), indicating strong breed-specific structure (Myint et al., 2010). In Asian native populations spanning eight countries, five albumen loci (Ov, G3, G2, G1, and TfEW) are polymorphic, and the overall genetic variability in native populations exceeds that of commercial lines (Kinoshita et al., 2002). Complementing these genetic studies, a proteomics-guided comparison of native European breeds (large: Araucana, Czech Golden-penciled, Minorca; small: Booted Bantam, Rosecomb Bantam) showed higher abundance of ovalbumin-related protein X (OVALX), rather than direct evidence of altered ovalbumin bioactivity itself, in large-breed eggs (Bílková et al., 2018). This gap underscores the need for targeted work on ovalbumin bioactivity in native breeds to inform the nutritional evaluation and conservation of genetic resources in traditional poultry systems.

Avidin. Avidin is a tetrameric glycoprotein secreted from the hen’s oviduct into the egg albumen and comprises approximately 0.05 % of the total protein, equivalent to approximately 50 mg/L (Nau et al., 2007; Beck et al., 2025). It has an exceptionally high affinity for biotin (Kd of approximately 10−15 M), forming an almost irreversible complex with a half-life of approximately 200 days (Moreau et al., 2022). Oviductal expression of avidin genes is induced by progesterone or bacterial infections. By sequestering biotin (vitamin B7), avidin can limit microbial growth in experimental systems; for example, it inhibits the growth of biotin-dependent Escherichia coli mutants and can bind several Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, independent of its biotin-binding capacity (Campbell et al., 1972). Because of its extraordinary biotin affinity and stability, the avidin–biotin system has been widely exploited in diagnostics and targeted delivery platforms. However, these biomedical applications of purified avidin–biotin interactions should not be interpreted as evidence that avidin in consumed eggs confers comparable health effects in humans. In the egg itself, avidin is considered part of the natural antimicrobial defense system through biotin sequestration. Beyond this role, additional anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antihypertensive, and antimicrobial applications have been described mainly in biomedical or experimental contexts rather than as established effects of egg consumption in humans (Zhang et al., 2021). Comparative studies indicate that avidins isolated from chicken, duck, goose, ostrich, and turkey eggs have similar tetrameric structures and glycosylation, but display distinct immunological cross-reactivity (Hytönen et al., 2003). However, information on avidin levels and glycosylation in native chicken breeds remains scarce, and further research is needed to determine whether native genetic background and extensive production systems influence avidin abundance, glycosylation, or functional properties.

Defensin. Defensins are small, cationic host-defense peptides characterized by six conserved cysteines forming three disulfide bonds; in birds, the predominant family is avian β-defensins (AvBD), which contribute to innate immunity at mucosal surfaces and within eggs (van Dijk et al., 2008). In hens, antimicrobial peptides, including AvBD, are present in multiple egg compartments (eggshell matrix, albumen, and vitelline membranes) and help protect the embryo (Hervé-Grépinet et al., 2010).

Functionally, defensins are primarily recognized for broad antimicrobial activity, especially through membrane disruption, whereas immunomodulatory functions such as chemotaxis and cytokine regulation have been described mainly in innate-defense and in vitro experimental contexts (Sugiarto and Yu, 2004). A distinct, egg-adapted clade of ovodefensins includes gallin, which is highly expressed in the magnum (site of albumen synthesis) and recovered from albumen. Structural studies and in vitro assays have confirmed its antimicrobial activity (Gong et al., 2010). Another key peptide, AvBD11, is an atypical, egg-specific β-defensin purified from vitelline membranes and present in the egg. Comparative analyses highlight its unique sequence features among AvBD (Hervé-Grépinet et al., 2010).

Evidence relevant to native chickens remains limited and currently derives mainly from expression-level observations rather than direct protein quantification in eggs. A recent breed-comparison study reported age- and breed-dependent differences in AvBD transcript abundance in yolk sac tissues (e.g., Beijing You versus Hy-Line Gray), indicating that defensin-related immune signaling in the egg environment may vary with genetic background (Ayalew et al., 2024). However, direct quantification of ovodefensins and AvBD proteins in eggs from native breeds remains scarce, and targeted proteomic and functional studies are needed to determine whether expression differences translate into differences in antimicrobial potency.

Egg yolk proteins

Table 4 summarizes the principal egg-yolk bioactive proteins, including phosvitin and livetins (α, β, and γ). These proteins have well-established biochemical and nutritional roles in the egg, including metal binding, emulsification, nutrient storage, vitamin transport, and passive immune protection. However, the strength of evidence for additional health-related effects differs substantially across studies. For phosvitin and livetins, reported antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and osteogenic activities derive predominantly from compositional analyses and in vitro, ex vivo, or animal studies, whereas direct human evidence remains limited. In addition, the study by Lee et al. (2022) demonstrated cellular antioxidant activity and modulation of Th1/Th2 cytokine responses for egg-yolk protein extracts, but this finding reflects cell-based evidence rather than established physiological effects in humans. Emerging evidence indicates that some yolk bioactives vary with breed and production system; however, detailed α- and β-livetin as well as phosvitin profiling in native chicken remains a key research need.

From a nutritional and translational perspective, yolk proteins are also unlikely to contribute equally to human health. Phosvitin and livetins are quantitatively relevant yolk proteins, but the evidence supporting their broader health-related effects is not equivalent: phosvitin is particularly notable for its metal-binding and physicochemical functions, whereas many reported antioxidant, osteogenic, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects for both phosvitin- and livetin-related fractions derive from isolated proteins, hydrolysates, or experimental systems rather than from direct dietary evidence in humans. In addition, processing and digestion are likely to influence the behavior of these proteins, because heat, pH, salts, and hydrolysis affect yolk protein functionality, while gastrointestinal digestion may further modify protein structure and release peptide fragments. Accordingly, the presence of a bioactive yolk protein in raw egg should not be interpreted as direct evidence of bioavailability or physiological efficacy after normal cooking and consumption, and for most yolk proteins these aspects remain insufficiently defined.

Phosvitin. Phosvitin is the principal phosphoprotein of egg yolk and is among the most heavily phosphorylated proteins found in nature. It is generated from vitellogenin precursors during yolk formation (Lv, 2017; Ishwarya et al., 2023) and is exceptionally rich in phosphoserine clusters that underlie its unusual chemistry and bioactivity. In hen eggs, phosvitin accounts for approximately 11 % of yolk proteins or 4 % of yolk DM, and carries the vast majority of yolk phosphorus, reflecting its extreme phosphorylation density (Yilmaz and Ağagündüz, 2020).

Structurally, phosvitin contains dense clusters of phosphorylated serine residues that confer strong affinity for metal ions such as Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, and Ca²⁺ (Lu and Baker, 1986). This metal-chelating property is a well-established biochemical feature and underlies its ability to inhibit iron-catalyzed lipid oxidation and protect phospholipids under controlled experimental conditions (Benedé and Molina, 2020). Antioxidant activity of phosvitin hydrolysates, including radical-scavenging effects associated with residues such as histidine, methionine, and tyrosine, has been demonstrated mainly in in vitro digestion and model-system studies (Xu et al., 2007). Reported antimicrobial effects likewise derive largely from mechanistic or experimental evidence, in which iron sequestration may restrict microbial growth (Yilmaz and Ağagündüz, 2020). Additional osteogenic and anticancer activities have been described at the preclinical level: phosvitin-derived phosphopeptides promoted osteoblast differentiation and osteogenic signaling in cell and animal models (Chakrabarti et al., 2020), whereas anticancer effects have been reported mainly in experimental systems involving cancer cells and oxidative DNA-damage models rather than in humans (Moon et al., 2014). By contrast, its emulsifying function is directly supported as a physicochemical property relevant to food processing (Jiang et al., 2019).

To our knowledge, breed-specific studies directly characterizing intact phosvitin in native chicken eggs are currently lacking, and native-versus-commercial comparisons have not been reported. Existing studies have focused mainly on mechanisms and processing variables, including iron and copper chelation, Fenton suppression, and the effects of heat, pH, salts, and hydrolysis, without breed attribution (Taborsky, 1980; Albright et al., 1984; Lu and Baker, 1986, 1987; Lee et al., 2002; Ishikawa et al., 2004; Castellani et al., 2004). Likewise, studies using food models and phosvitin hydrolysates have not examined native breeds or direct comparisons with commercial layers (Lee et al., 2002; Xu et al., 2007; Yoo et al., 2017). Collectively, these studies support phosvitin bioactivity, but they do not resolve breed-specific variation, phosphorylation patterns, or isoform differences. Future research should focus on comparing native and commercial chicken samples under controlled diets and age. Key priorities include rapid metal removal, inhibition of phosphatases, and advanced phosphoproteomic techniques, such as site-occupancy analysis and top-down mass spectrometry, to resolve isoform ladders. Additional research directions involve standardized assays for metal-catalyzed oxidation and antimicrobial activity conducted under precisely defined iron conditions with robust cross-breed replication protocols.

Livetins. Livetin is a soluble protein fraction of egg-yolk plasma. In fresh yolk, livetins represent a distinct fraction of yolk proteins, accounting for approximately 10% of yolk DM (Zhang et al., 2024a). Livetins are water-soluble and separated from lipid-rich granules. They include α-livetin, which corresponds to serum albumin; β-livetins, which are glycoproteins derived from vitellogenins; and γ-livetin, which corresponds to immunoglobulin Y (IgY) and accounts for approximately 41 % of total livetin proteins (Zambrowicz et al., 2023). IgY is transferred from the hen’s blood to the oocyte and provides passive immunity to the developing embryo (Schade and Chacana, 2007).

Several properties have been described for egg-yolk livetins, and the level of evidence differs across studies. The livetin fraction has documented physicochemical and functional properties, including high solubility over a broad pH range as well as foaming and emulsifying behavior, supporting its potential use as a functional ingredient in food, nutraceutical, and pharmaceutical applications (Chalamaiah et al., 2017). Additional bioactivities have also been reported. In lipopolysaccharide-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages, purified livetin fractions and their pepsin- or Alcalase-derived hydrolysates reduced nitric oxide production, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, and iNOS expression; Alcalase hydrolysate also reduced PGE2 production and COX-2 expression, while phagocytic activity was enhanced (Meram and Wu, 2017). Zambrowicz et al. (2023) further reviewed evidence that yolk-plasma proteins such as yolkin and YGP40 possess immunomodulatory properties, including stimulation of cytokine production in human whole-blood cells and murine macrophage models. For γ-livetin, IgY is well established as a mediator of passive immune protection in the egg, and Lee et al. (2021) reviewed its diagnostic and therapeutic applications, which have been reported mainly in animal models of infectious disease and, in some cases, in studies using purified pathogen-specific oral IgY preparations. However, evidence from purified pathogen-specific oral IgY preparations should not be equated with the effects of consuming intact yolk proteins in conventional egg foods. Taken together, these findings suggest potential functional, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory potential for livetin-related yolk proteins, although current evidence remains based largely on in vitro and animal studies rather than direct dietary studies in humans.

Evidence relevant to native chicken eggs remains limited and mostly preliminary. An Indonesian study compared the water extracts of yolks from Kampung chickens with those from commercial eggs. Kampung extract significantly increased immunoglobulin M production (5.91‑fold in HB4C5 cells) and stimulated IgA, IgG, and IgM secretion in mouse splenocytes compared with the commercial extract (4.70-fold in HB4C5 cells). The active fraction was precipitated at 80 % ammonium sulfate saturation (Mulyani et al., 2021b). However, the study did not isolate specific livetin fractions, and the findings were limited to in vitro cell-based assays and mouse primary splenocyte assays. Similarly, a veterinary study found that native Iranian chickens produced higher total antibodies and IgY titers (4.18–4.52 log2) in response to sheep red blood cells than did commercial Ross 308 strains (2.95 log2) (Khazali et al., 2025), indicating greater humoral immune responsiveness in live birds. Since γ-livetin is the egg-yolk form of IgY, these results support the possibility of breed-related variation in yolk immune-related components, but they do not directly establish superior immunoprotective effects of native chicken eggs in human nutrition. However, information on α- and β-livetin profiles in native breeds is scarce. Overall, current evidence suggests potential immunomodulatory relevance of livetin-related yolk proteins in native chicken eggs, although this evidence remains preliminary and is based mainly on in vitro and animal studies.

Lipid-soluble bioactive compounds

The egg yolk is a rich repository of lipophilic nutrients, including carotenoids, fat-soluble vitamins, and complex lipids. These compounds are nutritionally relevant, but their physiological effects in humans depend on concentration, serving size, cooking-induced changes, bioaccessibility during digestion, and the broader diet. However, the strength of evidence varies across compositional, mechanistic, animal, and human studies. This section reviews the major groups of lipid-soluble bioactives found in eggs, including carotenoids, fat-soluble vitamins, and bioactive lipids.

Carotenoids

The Taihe black‑boned silky fowl produces yolks that naturally accumulate large amounts of carotenoids. Even without dietary supplementation, silky fowl yolks exhibit higher carotenoid accumulation, suggesting a breed-specific metabolic trait that favors carotenoid deposition. This was demonstrated in a study in which silky hens accumulated more lutein and zeaxanthin in the yolk than did Rhode Island Reds (Kojima et al., 2022). This difference is attributed to the higher lipid content of silky eggs, which increases yolk capacity for fat-soluble carotenoid storage. This difference may also reflect breed-related variation in yolk lipid deposition and carotenoid handling, although the underlying mechanisms remain uncertain.

Native scavenging hens in Bangladesh forage for insects, grasses, and green leaves, allowing them to ingest preformed carotenoids. A comprehensive study of eggs from scavenging, semi-intensive, and intensive systems found that yolks from native scavenging hens contained significantly higher lutein levels (8.0–11.8 mg/kg yolk) compared with those from semi-intensive or intensive systems (Islam et al., 2017). In contrast, eggs from intensive systems where hens were fed formulated diets rich in corn and synthetic pigments exhibited higher zeaxanthin concentrations (4.9–6.3 mg/kg). Total carotenoid content was highest in eggs from native scavenging systems compared with those from other production systems. Hens raised under scavenging systems tend to have greater access to carotenoid-rich dietary sources such as herbivorous insects (Eeva et al., 2010).

Evidence from other native breeds has illustrated the influence of genetics and husbandry practices on carotenoid deposition. A comparative study of eggs from Romagnola hens, an Italian local breed, and commercial Hy‑Line Brown layers found that the native breed produced eggs with a significantly higher yolk‑to‑egg ratio (31.1 % vs 24.9 %; p < 0.01) and carotenoid (β‑carotene) concentration (36.8 vs 20.2 ppm; p < 0.01) (Sirri et al., 2018). This advantage likely stems from the stronger capacity of the Romagnola genotype to absorb and deposit carotenoids, coupled with a lower laying rate, which limits pigment dilution. Active pasture foraging can further boost carotenoid intake (Sirri et al., 2018). Additionally, Romagnola hens may exploit pastures more effectively, thereby increasing their natural carotenoid intake. This example shows that some native breeds can naturally deposit more carotenoids, including β‑carotene, than commercial layers.

Two main factors explain observed breed differences. The first is carotenoid availability in the diet, and the second is the physiological capacity for the yolk absorption of carotenoids. Pasture and scavenging systems provide access to plant pigments that are absent in grain-based diets. For example, eggs from pasture-raised hens contain approximately twice as many carotenoids as those from cage-free hens (Sergin et al., 2022). Breed-specific differences in lipid metabolism also contribute to this phenomenon. For example, silky and Romagnola hens produce yolks with a higher lipid content, higher yolk-to-egg ratio, and lower laying rate, thus incorporating more carotenoids (Kojima et al., 2022; Sirri et al., 2018). Beyond natural variation driven by genotype and forage access, evidence from controlled feeding trials—including a meta-analysis—indicates that dietary supplementation with natural carotenoid sources increases yolk carotenoid concentration and intensifies yolk color (Yunitasari et al., 2023).

Lutein and its isomer, zeaxanthin, belong to the xanthophyll class of carotenoids found in eggs and are deposited in human tissues, particularly in the retinal macula, where they act as blue-light filters and antioxidants. Broader review-level literature has associated these carotenoids with visual-health relevance, including improved visual acuity and reduced risk of age-related macular degeneration and cataracts (Zaheer, 2017). Beyond visual health, additional biological effects have been reported, but the level of evidence varies across studies. In an experimental cell model, lutein downregulated inflammatory mediators, including NF-κB, COX-2, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, in primary chondrocytes (Qiao et al., 2018). Ahn and Kim (2021) further reviewed possible cardioprotective and vascular anti-inflammatory actions of lutein across epidemiological, in vitro, animal, and limited clinical evidence. Evidence for brain- and skin-related effects is also mixed; Tan et al. (2017) reported neuroprotective effects in a rat traumatic brain injury model, Lee et al. (2004) demonstrated protection against ultraviolet-radiation-induced skin responses in mice, and Juturu et al. (2016) reported improved skin tone and minimal erythemal dose in a placebo-controlled human trial. Taken together, these findings support the nutritional relevance of lutein and zeaxanthin to visual health and suggest broader biological potential, but evidence for anti-inflammatory, cardiovascular, neuroprotective, and skin-related effects remains drawn from a mixture of cell studies, animal models, reviews, and limited human trials rather than direct dietary studies of native chicken eggs. Their nutritional significance also depends on the amount of egg consumed, retention during preparation, and bioaccessibility after digestion rather than on yolk concentration alone.

Fat-soluble vitamins

Fat-soluble vitamins accumulate in egg yolks and are nutritionally important because they contribute to physiological functions such as vision, calcium homeostasis, antioxidant protection, and blood coagulation. Unlike water-soluble vitamins, these compounds are stored in body tissues, and their concentrations in eggs are influenced by the hen’s diet, sun exposure, and genetic background. Reported differences between native and commercial eggs vary by vitamin and study conditions, and therefore should be interpreted considering compositional evidence rather than assumed as uniform functional advantages. Moreover, the nutritional relevance of these differences depends on the amounts actually consumed within the overall diet rather than on breed designation alone.

Vitamin A refers to a group of compounds including retinol, retinal, and retinyl esters. Egg yolk is an important dietary source of preformed retinol, which is highly bioavailable. Vitamin A content depends on several factors, including breed genetics, housing systems, and feeding practices. Native hens that forage on carotenoid‑rich vegetation convert dietary β‑carotene into retinol, which is then deposited in the yolk. Comparative findings for vitamin A are inconsistent across breeds, diets, and production systems. In Bangladeshi native hens, eggs from scavenging systems are rich in β-carotene and lutein, reflecting greater vitamin A precursor intake (Islam et al., 2017). Similarly, eggs from the Italian Romagnola breed contain ≈ 36.8 ppm of β-carotene, significantly exceeding the 20.2 ppm found in commercial hybrid eggs (Sirri et al., 2018). Polish Green-legged Partridge hens also lay eggs with significantly higher vitamin A levels than those of Hy-Line Brown layers (p < 0.05), while Araucana hens exhibit levels comparable to those of the commercial breed (Sokołowicz et al., 2019). Conversely, a study from Malawi reported a vitamin A content of 150 µg retinol activity equivalents/100 g in commercial eggs, compared with 102 µg retinol activity equivalents/100 g in native village-chicken eggs, representing a 47 % higher concentration (Werner et al., 2019).

Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is synthesized in the skin when 7‑dehydrocholesterol is exposed to ultraviolet B radiation and can also be obtained through the diet (Chaiprasongsuk et al., 2020). Hens exposed to sunlight produce more vitamin D3, which is transported to the yolk as 25‑hydroxyvitamin D3 (25‑OH‑D3) (Schutkowski et al., 2013). A comparative study of Taihe silky, silky fowl crossbred, and Hy-Line Brown hens under similar diet and management conditions found higher yolk 25-OH-D3 concentrations in Taihe Silky and the crossbred line than in Hy-Line Brown (approximately 21.60–23.51 vs 15.18 ng/g) (Zhang et al., 2024b). However, other studies have reported higher vitamin D levels in commercial chickens than in native chickens under less clearly described dietary and management conditions (Onyenweaku et al., 2018).

In addition to its classical role in calcium homeostasis and bone health, vitamin D has broader immunological relevance. Immune cells, including B and T lymphocytes, natural killer cells, dendritic cells, and monocytes, express the vitamin D receptor. Fenercioglu (2024) reviewed evidence that vitamin D regulates gene expression, modulates cytokine production, inhibits pro-inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK, reduces IL-2 and interferon-γ expression, and promotes anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10. Vitamin D also influences immune cell differentiation and maturation. These findings support the biological plausibility that vitamin D content in eggs may be nutritionally relevant to immune function; however, they are derived from broader vitamin D literature and should not be interpreted as direct evidence that native chicken eggs confer immune-support effects in humans.

Vitamin E (tocopherols and tocotrienols) acts as a lipid-phase antioxidant that protects polyunsaturated fats in cell membranes from oxidative damage. Hen diets strongly influence vitamin E levels because birds cannot synthesize it. Recent comparative studies demonstrated that the eggs of native chicken breeds do not consistently exhibit high vitamin E concentrations. In Poland, native Polish Crested eggs contained significantly greater amounts of yolk α-tocopherol than those from the commercial Hy-Line Brown when both were provided the same diet, indicating a possible genotype-related advantage (Gumułka et al., 2022). Conversely, a controlled comparison in China reported similar vitamin E contents in Taihe silky and Hy-Line Brown yolks, while a silky fowl crossbred line exhibited the highest concentrations (Taihe silky = 2.68 µg/g, Hy-Line Brown = 3.33 µg/g, silky fowl crossbreed = 6.61 µg/g yolk; p < 0.01), suggesting that native breeds are not necessarily superior in this parameter (Zhang et al., 2024b). A free-range trial in Spain likewise reported lower yolk α-tocopherol levels in the native Black Castellana compared with those in Lohmann LSL under both control and omega-3-enriched diets (111 vs. 194 and 98 vs 132 µg/g on a DM basis, respectively), further supporting this observation (Romero et al., 2024). Within-breed variation can also be substantial; for example, the Green-legged Partridge demonstrated yolk vitamin E contents ranging from approximately 36 µg/g in backyard systems to 84–91 µg/g in intensive and organic systems, indicating that management and dietary factors strongly influence vitamin E deposition (Krawczyk, 2009). Environmental conditions also play a role, as demonstrated in a study from the Guatemalan highlands, where vitamin E in whole cooked eggs increased from approximately 2.07 to 2.47 mg/100 g of the edible portion between the wet and dry seasons (Wallace et al., 2023). Collectively, these findings indicate that while breed may contribute to differences in vitamin E content, dietary composition, production system, and seasonal factors exert a greater influence, and native breeds are not inherently richer in vitamin E without supportive nutritional and management conditions.

Vitamin K (phylloquinones and menaquinones) is less studied in eggs, but it plays a critical role in blood clotting and bone metabolism. Recent comparative research has indicated that native chicken breeds do not consistently contain high vitamin K concentrations in their eggs. In a controlled study using a standardized corn–soybean diet, yolks from Taihe Silkie, a crossbred black-boned line, and Hy-Line Brown contained comparable vitamin K concentrations (approximately 2.6–2.8 ng/g), with no significant differences among breeds (p = 0.55) (Zhang et al., 2024b). Similarly, a broader multibreed comparison that included native genotypes, such as Ayam Cemani and Green-legged Partridge, reported no significant differences in yolk vitamin K when all hens were maintained under uniform housing and dietary conditions (Hejdysz et al., 2024). These findings suggest that breed plays a minimal role in determining vitamin K content and that dietary composition is the predominant factor. Indeed, dietary supplementation with menadione (vitamin K3) substantially increases yolk menaquinone-4 levels to approximately 46–51 µg/100 g whole egg, with menaquinone-4 representing over 90 % of total vitamin K content (O’Sullivan et al., 2020).

Phospholipids, sphingolipids, and fatty acids

Egg yolk contains a dense matrix of lipids, including triacylglycerols, phospholipids, and sphingolipids. Triacylglycerols provide energy, whereas phospholipids and sphingolipids function primarily as membrane constituents and nutritionally relevant lipid classes with recognized roles in cell structure and signaling (Abeyrathne et al., 2022; Réhault-Godbert et al., 2019). Recent lipidomic studies have further highlighted the complexity of yolk lipids, revealing at least 19 phospholipid classes and 275 molecular species (Cheng et al., 2024). In an experimental feeding study, microalgae supplementation altered the yolk lipidome by increasing plasmalogens and shifting long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) to the sn-2 position in PE, phosphatidylinositol, and phosphatidic acid, changes interpreted as potentially relevant to antioxidant capacity and membrane-mediated signaling.

Approximately one-third of yolk lipids are phospholipids (glycerophospholipids), dominated by PC and PE, with smaller amounts of sphingomyelin, plasmalogen, and phosphatidylinositol (Abeyrathne et al., 2022). These molecules are packaged in LDL and HDL, contributing to yolk emulsifying properties and lipid bioaccessibility during digestion. PC is also a major dietary source of choline, which is important for methyl-group metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis, and liver lipid handling (Van Parys et al., 2022). Broader experimental and review literature has linked yolk phospholipids to hepatoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects, including attenuation of oxidative stress and modulation of inflammatory signaling in model systems; however, these findings have not yet been directly confirmed in human studies using native chicken eggs.

Native hens with a higher yolk-to-egg ratio may yield greater absolute amounts of phospholipids per egg. For instance, Romagnola hens and four Portuguese native breeds produce proportionally larger yolks than those in commercial hens (Sirri et al., 2018; Lordelo et al., 2020), although the specific phospholipid composition of these yolks is yet to be investigated. In contrast, another study provided stronger evidence that a higher yolk percentage in silky hens was associated with increased phospholipid content (Zhang et al., 2024b). Gerzilov et al. (2015) reported that the phospholipid content in eggs from Katunitsa, a native Bulgarian chicken breed, ranged from 30.56 to 36.77 mg/g of yolk. In comparison, Zhang et al. (2024b) found that silky hens had PC and PE levels of 1.60 and 11.28 mg/g, while Hy-Line brown hens showed lower values of 1.03 and 8.92 mg/g, respectively. Additionally, Antova et al. (2019) reported phospholipid concentrations across various chicken breeds, including both native and commercial lines, of 389–573 and 219 to 355 g/kg for PC and PE, respectively.

Egg yolk contains sphingolipids, which account for a substantial proportion of yolk phospholipids. Seven sphingolipid subclasses have been identified, including ceramides, galactosylceramides, glucosylceramides, lactosylceramides, gangliosides, sphingomyelin, and sphingosine (Cheng et al., 2024). In broader biological literature, ceramides, lactosylceramides, and gangliosides have recognized roles in cell signaling, immune regulation, neural communication, and brain development (Liu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2021). Comparative egg-composition studies have suggested that some native or specialty lines may contain higher levels of specific sphingolipids. For example, ceramide and sphingomyelin concentrations were higher in silky-hen eggs than in commercial eggs (Zhang et al., 2024b). Another study, conducted under uncontrolled dietary and environmental conditions, also reported higher ceramide and sphingomyelin levels in eggs from Nixi chickens and Silky hens than in commercial eggs (Zhao, 2023). These findings indicate compositional variation among breeds and production systems, but they do not directly establish superior nutritional or physiological effects in humans.

Yolk PUFA, particularly DHA (C22:6n3) and ARA (C20:4n6), are nutritionally salient because they feed on the eicosanoid and docosanoid pathways, and are incorporated into neural and retinal membranes (EFSA, 2008). When diet and housing were controlled for, the study outcomes differed. Some studies reported similar total PUFA levels and only minor differences in DHA and ARA levels between native and commercial eggs (Lordelo et al., 2020; Nalyvayko et al., 2021; Gumułka et al., 2022). In contrast, other studies have identified clear breed-related effects, with certain native or crossbred lines showing higher DHA or ARA levels and distinct PUFA profiles than those in commercial layers (Ji et al., 2023; Hejdysz et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024b; Jung et al., 2025). When diet and husbandry practices were not controlled for, the differences observed were more likely to reflect production system effects rather than genetic variations. For example, a study in Sri Lanka found that “country/forager” eggs exhibited higher yolk DHA, while “farm/intensive” eggs contained more linoleic acid (Ediriweera, 2023). Collectively, “native” does not automatically imply higher DHA and ARA or superior PUFA profiles. Instead, targeted feeding strategies, such as the inclusion of flaxseed, fish oil, microalgae, or pasture-based diets, play a primary role in shaping PUFA composition, which may influence the extent and direction of these dietary effects (Sergin et al., 2022).

Culinary heat can significantly oxidize PUFA and alter phospholipid structures, even after brief exposure. For instance, frying for just 5 min at 160 °C (Zhao et al., 2023) or boiling for 1 h at 100 °C (Wang et al., 2023) can cause notable changes. Accordingly, compositional differences in raw eggs should not be interpreted as equivalent differences in absorbed dose or physiological effect after cooking and within mixed diets. Emulsified egg yolk formats, such as soft-cooked preparations, preserve phospholipid functionality and support mixed micelle formation in the gastrointestinal tract, thereby enhancing fatty acid and carotenoid absorption (Anton, 2013; Abeyrathne et al., 2022). As many native production systems rely on scavenging or pasture, seasonal fluctuations in feedstuffs can alter fatty acid profiles and antioxidant buffering capacity. Therefore, pairing omega-3 enrichment strategies with natural antioxidants in the diet is advisable for maintaining oxidative stability across systems and breeds (Vlaicu et al., 2021).

When diet and environment are controlled, the differences in bioactive lipids between native and commercial eggs are generally small and inconsistent across studies. Several native lines match commercial layers in crude lipid, triglyceride, cholesterol, and broad fatty acid profiles, with occasional breed-specific deviations in lipid or fatty acid profiles (Lordelo et al., 2020; Nalyvayko et al., 2021; Gumułka et al., 2022; Ji et al., 2023; Hejdysz et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024b; Jung et al., 2025). Under free-range or pasture conditions, native systems can display higher omega-3 content and antioxidant capacities; however, these advantages have been attributed to feed ecology rather than an inherent breed effect. A key practical point is that native hens do not inherently have higher levels of PUFA or phospholipids. Diet composition, access to forage, and management choices dominate these outcomes, with genotype acting as a meaningful but secondary modifier.

The following three research priorities were identified: First, controlled trials with a constant diet, age, and environment are required to isolate true genotype effects on phospholipid and sphingolipid species, including newly cataloged plasmalogens and glycosphingolipids in yolk (Cheng et al., 2024). Second, integrative work should connect lipidomics with functional results, such as antioxidant status, lipid peroxidation resistance, and hepatic or vascular health biomarkers in humans, including clinically relevant models. Third, studies should test feed strategies that leverage the foraging ecology of native systems to reproducibly increase omega-3 levels and protect these lipids from oxidation, thereby enabling fair comparisons with fortified commercial eggs. Overall, current evidence does not support the conclusion that native chicken eggs are uniformly superior sources of lipid-soluble bioactives. Rather, some native lines show higher concentrations of specific compounds under particular feeding and production conditions, whereas under controlled diets many differences are small, inconsistent, or attributable primarily to diet and management rather than breed alone.

Health implications

Native chickens are an important food resource in many rural and smallholder systems, and eggs are nutrient-dense foods that provide high-quality proteins, essential fatty acids, choline, vitamins A and B-12, and several minerals. This nutrient profile makes eggs a valuable source of nutrients in low-income settings. Human intervention studies in Ethiopia, Burkina Faso, and Ecuador have reported favorable outcomes from egg-based interventions, including improvements in growth, hematologic status, nutrient biomarkers, safety-related outcomes, and early childhood development (Chapnick et al., 2021; Ernyey et al., 2025; Iannotti et al., 2017a, 2017b; McKune et al., 2020; Omer et al., 2022, 2023). Additional human studies from Malawi, Uganda, Thailand, South Africa, and Honduras support the general benefits or safety of egg consumption, although these studies differ in design and primary outcomes (Baum et al., 2017; Lutter et al., 2021; Nakiranda et al., 2025; Palacios et al., 2024; Suta et al., 2022). However, these studies primarily provide evidence for egg consumption in general rather than for breed-authenticated native chicken eggs specifically, because most did not isolate the contribution of native chicken eggs from that of commercially sourced eggs.

Anthropometric benefits were the most consistent finding. A cluster‑randomized trial in Ethiopia, where households were provided several hens and encouraged to feed one egg daily to children aged 6–18 months, improved weight‑for‑age z‑scores (WAZ) by +0.38 and weight‑for‑height z‑scores (WHZ) by +0.43 within 6 months (Omer et al., 2022). The same trial also recorded an increase in hemoglobin levels (discussed below). In Ecuador, the Lulun Project, named for “egg” in Kichwa, was a randomized controlled trial conducted in rural indigenous communities. The program combined a daily egg intervention with a social marketing strategy to support adherence and empower caregivers. Infants aged 6–9 months received 1 egg per day for 6 months and showed a significant improvement in length‑for‑age z‑scores (LAZ), with an average gain of approximately +0.63 z‑score (Iannotti et al., 2017b). These growth effects are among the notable findings that have stimulated follow-up trials in other regions.

School-based programs and community interventions further underscore the benefits of egg consumption for supporting growth. In a study in Uganda, primary schools provided children with 0, 1, or 2 eggs per day for 6 months; children receiving 2 eggs showed greater increases in height, weight, and mid-upper arm circumference than those in the no-egg group, while 1 egg daily was sufficient to improve arm circumference (Baum et al., 2017). A cluster trial in Thailand offering 10 eggs per week to students aged 8–14 years also reported gains in height and weight compared with those in the controls (Suta et al., 2022). In Honduras, a community-based microenterprise supplied 1 egg per day for 1 year, which increased egg consumption and reduced the risk of respiratory tract infection. However, anthropometric outcomes did not change significantly (Palacios et al., 2024). An individual trial in Malawi that provided 1 egg per day highlighted increased energy intake without the displacement of other complementary foods (Lutter et al., 2021); however, growth data were not fully detailed. Overall, growth improvements were most evident when eggs were consumed consistently within supportive dietary contexts, with the strongest evidence coming from trials in Ethiopia and Ecuador (Iannotti et al., 2017b; Omer et al., 2022).

The Lulun Project in Ecuador assessed not only growth but also nutrient-related biomarkers. Daily egg consumption increased plasma choline, betaine, methionine, DHA, DMA, and TMAO, although vitamin B-12 and retinol did not differ significantly between groups (Iannotti et al., 2017a). Likewise, a randomized crossover study in U.S. adults with metabolic syndrome, conducted with commercial eggs, reported higher HDL cholesterol, plasma choline, and plasma zeaxanthin after consumption of two eggs per day compared with an egg-substitute breakfast (Thomas et al., 2022). These human studies support egg consumption as a source of selected nutrients and biomarker improvements, but they do not isolate breed-specific effects of native chicken eggs.

Separate from this human evidence, compositional and experimental studies suggest that some native lines differ in antioxidant-related and lipid-quality traits. In Korean native chickens, Jung et al. (2025) reported greater antioxidant power in selected yolk samples as assessed by DPPH, ABTS, and FRAP, reflecting in vitro antioxidant potential rather than demonstrated in vivo effects in humans. Within-native compositional differences have also been reported among Sicilian breeds, with Cornuta eggs showing lower atherogenic and thrombogenic indices and a higher hypocholesterolaemic/hypercholesterolaemic ratio than Valplatani and Siciliana (Lo Presti et al., 2025). These findings are useful for breed characterization and hypothesis generation, but they should be distinguished from direct human evidence. Overall, current evidence indicates that egg consumption can improve selected biomarkers in humans, whereas evidence for superior health effects of native chicken eggs remains indirect and is based mainly on compositional comparisons and in vitro assays rather than head-to-head dietary trials in humans.

Eggs are rich in choline and DHA, which are essential nutrients for brain development and neural function. A follow-up of the Un Oeuf trial in Burkina Faso, which provided 4 hens and a behavior-change package for 10 months, found that children aged 18–33 months who consistently consumed eggs were less likely to fall below the cutoff for gross motor development (odds ratio 0.13; p = 0.02) and showed improved personal-social scores (Ernyey et al., 2025). Likewise, an Ethiopian trial reported improvements in motor skills as secondary outcomes, alongside anthropometric gains (Omer et al., 2022). Although evidence remains limited, these findings suggest that egg consumption may contribute to neurodevelopment through the provision of choline, DHA, and other nutrients. However, the available studies do not establish that native chicken eggs confer greater neurodevelopmental benefits than eggs in general, and this possibility remains hypothetical pending direct comparative human studies.

The introduction of eggs at an early age has raised concerns regarding allergies. However, data from indigenous populations indicate a low risk. A sub-study of the Lulun Project measured immunoglobulin E (IgE) specific to ovomucoid and ovalbumin as indicators of allergy, and the results suggested that introducing native chicken eggs at 6–9 months of age was safe under close monitoring (Chapnick et al., 2021). A randomized trial in South Africa that provided 1 egg per day for 6–9 months reported reduced sensitization to common food allergens after 6 months (Nakiranda et al., 2025). No severe adverse events were reported across these studies, and clinical monitoring was incorporated to manage potential reactions. However, the allergy-related implications of introducing eggs should not be conflated with claims of breed-specific hypoallergenicity. Evidence from a comparative study using sera from egg-allergic children showed that eggs from native chicken breeds (Araucana and Maran) exhibited IgE-binding and functional activity similar to those of commercial laying hybrids, providing no support for the assumption that native chicken eggs are inherently less allergenic (Egger et al., 2011). Therefore, while early egg introduction may support tolerance development at the population level, evidence for reduced allergenicity of native chicken eggs remains insufficient.

Across Latin America, Africa, and Asia, interventions that combine production support, behavioral change strategies, and improved access have generally shown beneficial effects of egg consumption on child growth, nutrient status, or safety-related outcomes. However, many studies that reference “indigenous/native chickens” rely on proxy production systems such as backyard, rural, or village production, with most failing to specify breeds or analyze egg nutrient composition. Thus, the current human evidence supports the health relevance of egg consumption in general, rather than the superiority of native chicken eggs specifically. Separate compositional studies suggest that some native or free-range eggs may contain higher concentrations of nutrients such as choline, carotenoids, vitamins D and E, and n-3 fatty acids (Gałązka-Czarnecka et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2017; Karsten et al., 2010; Werner et al., 2019). Nevertheless, compositional differences alone do not establish greater health benefits in humans, and the superiority of native chicken eggs for human health remains unproven in the absence of direct head-to-head dietary trials using breed-authenticated eggs.

Research gaps in native breeds and future directions

Fig. 2 summarizes current evidence imbalances and reporting gaps while outlining a staged research agenda, from standardized head-to-head comparisons, multi-omics, and in vitro assays to human trials and, ultimately, conservation strategies for safeguarding native chicken germplasm. Research on eggs from native chickens remains limited compared with the extensive literature on commercial breeds. A scientometric review spanning a century of poultry publications showed that research has been dominated by commercial broilers and layers, whereas studies explicitly addressing “indigenous/native chickens” remain scarce, constraining comparative evaluation of nutrient and bioactive profiles between local and commercial eggs (Vaziri et al., 2022). Although interest in local chicken eggs is increasing, the evidence base remains fragmented. Publications on native chickens rose more than threefold in 2011–2020 compared with 2001–2010 (164 vs. 49 articles), likely reflecting growing concern for poultry biodiversity (Fathi et al., 2017), the vulnerability of local chickens to environmental stressors (Mignon-Grasteau et al., 2015), the need for genetic and phenotypic performance evaluation (Ghorbani et al., 2013), and increasing interest in outdoor-based production systems (Stadig et al., 2016). Together, these scientometric trends highlight persistent evidence gaps between native and commercial eggs and underscore the need for systematic comparative research.

It highlights how inconsistent metadata and units currently limit cross-study comparisons and sets out a standardized trial blueprint and conservation pathway so that bioactive quality in native chicken eggs can be quantified, clinically validated, and protected.

Reporting gaps in nutrient and bioactive composition are the first barrier to address before advancing clinical evidence. Several publications compare “native/backyard/free-range” versus commercial eggs but do not specify breed identity, hen age, season, and diet composition, even though these factors strongly shift the contents of carotenoids, fat‑soluble vitamins (D/E/K), choline, phospholipids, and sphingolipids, as well as fatty‑acid profiles (Franco et al., 2020; Gumułka et al., 2022; Hejdysz et al., 2024; Islam et al., 2017; Lordelo et al., 2020; Rizzi and Chiericato, 2010; Sirri et al., 2018). A lack of uniform units and analyte forms is also common; vitamin D is reported as D3 or 25‑OH‑D3 with mixed units (µg/100 g or ng/g), carotenoids are often given as totals without speciation (lutein/zeaxanthin), and bioactive lipids are frequently presented only as relative percentages rather than as absolute contents (e.g., mg/100 g or mg/egg). This lack of uniform unit–biased comparisons occurs when yolk-to-white ratios differ across breeds (Lordelo et al., 2020; Schutkowski et al., 2013; Sirri et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2024b).

For proteins and peptides, cross‑breed quantification of lysozyme, ovotransferrin, ovomucin, and phosvitin under controlled conditions is rare. Breed-specific proteomics and peptidomics of native chicken eggs also remain limited. Nevertheless, immunomodulatory and antioxidant activities have been reported for native chicken egg fractions (Meram and Wu, 2017; Mulyani et al., 2021b). Postharvest and thermal conditions are also poorly standardized (temperature, time, and medium) despite known modifications to phospholipids/PUFA and the yolk metabolome (Wang et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2023). Priority improvements include head-to-head compositional studies that maintain a constant diet, age, and season, coupled with harmonized reporting of units and analyte forms, both absolute and relative outputs, and cross-laboratory analytical quality control.

Once a compositional baseline has been established, the next gap is the paucity of human evidence. Most claims regarding the benefits of native chicken eggs rely on composition analyses and in vitro and in vivo assays. Clinical validation is scarce, especially head‑to‑head comparisons of native versus commercial eggs that assess health‑relevant outcomes. Randomized controlled trials lasting at least 12–24 weeks are recommended. These studies should authenticate the origin of local breeds through genotyping or pedigree data and clearly describe the associated husbandry systems. Furthermore, doses for intervention should be standardized (for example, 1 egg per day), and a consistent serving format should be employed. Test eggs should be chemically profiled in advance for specified carotenoids, vitamins D and E, choline, phospholipid species such as PC and PE, sphingomyelin and ceramide, and n‑3 fatty acids. Clinically meaningful primary endpoints must be defined, including choline and one‑carbon metabolites, plasma lutein and zeaxanthin, 25‑OH‑D, blood lipids (functional HDL and oxidized LDL), inflammatory markers such as hs‑CRP and IL‑6, and child growth indices (LAZ, WAZ, WHZ) in low- and middle-income country contexts. Trials should also assess safety, including allergy, gastrointestinal tolerance, and adherence. Finally, the interactions between diet and breed, such as the effects of microalgae or flaxseed supplementation in native strains with higher yolk ratios, should be explored. With such a design, the hypothesis of native chicken eggs providing stronger health benefits owing to higher levels of specific bioactive compounds can be tested rigorously and transparently.

Multidisciplinary studies are essential to ensure that compositional and clinical findings are mutually explanatory. At the genomic level, quantitative trait loci/single-nucleotide polymorphism mapping for health‑related egg traits (carotenoid deposition, 25‑OH‑D3, PC:PE ratios, sphingomyelin/ceramide, yolk size/ratios) will link the genetic diversity of native breeds to egg phenotypes. Lipidomics and metabolomics are needed to uncover PC/PE species (including plasmalogens) and sphingolipid/ceramide pathways, as well as postprandial metabolites, following the consumption of native versus commercial eggs. Proteomics and peptidomics can map the bioactive peptides derived from ovalbumin, ovotransferrin, ovomucin, and phosvitin, followed by in vitro digestion bioavailability assays and small-scale human pharmacokinetic studies. Microbiomics should assess the modulation of eubiosis, short-chain fatty acids, and trimethylamine N‑oxide levels. Cross-omics integration with clinical endpoints enables precise breeding that prioritizes bioactive quality without sacrificing diversity.

Conservation strategies are fundamental because the bioactive value of native chicken eggs depends on genetic diversity. In situ community-based breeding programs, smallholder incentive schemes, and geographical indication (GI)/protected geographical indication (PGI) labeling must be paired with traceability to ensure that the value of bioactive quality is reflected in the price. Ex situ, cryobanking primordial germ cells (PGC) in poultry is essential to secure the W chromosome and mitochondrial DNA that semen banks do not cover. Current PGC techniques enable “genome-complete” conservation and provide insurance against population loss due to outbreaks or disasters (Nakamura, 2016). Along the supply chain, consistent feed guidelines (e.g., microalgae for n‑3 and plasmalogens) and the inclusion of feed antioxidants should be established to preserve bioactive stability. Downstream, low-temperature processing and storage standards will minimize lipid oxidation and vitamin degradation.

Germplasm preservation is not merely conservation; it is also an investment to substantiate health benefits. In some contexts, eggs from native breeds can match or surpass the quality of commercial eggs (e.g., internal quality and levels of minerals and carotenoids) (Sirri et al., 2018; Lordelo et al., 2020). To validate this claim, an integrated research package is needed, comprising head-to-head composition studies, randomized clinical trials in humans with clinical and functional endpoints, multi-omics platforms linked to clinical data, and a sustainable ecosystem for conservation and utilization. Thus, the narrative of native chicken eggs as functional foods shifts from descriptive to evidence-based claims that can be translated into policy and markets.

Conclusion

This review indicates that the bioactive profile of native chicken eggs can differ from that of commercial eggs, with several comparisons reporting greater concentrations of carotenoids and selected phospholipid classes. Crucially, these differences are frequently driven by diet composition and foraging access inherent to extensive production systems, rather than by breed genetics alone. Therefore, “native” should not be treated as a universal marker of nutritional superiority but as a proxy for a specific husbandry context that may favor deposition of particular bioactive compounds. A principal limitation is the very limited number of human clinical trials using breed-authenticated native chicken eggs. Consequently, many health implications are inferred from compositional evidence, mechanistic studies, or interventions using conventional market/commercial eggs, and breed-specific functional claims remain hypotheses. Although consumer preferences for specific attributes—such as yolk pigmentation and rearing systems—often drive market dynamics, these cues do not consistently correlate with verified compositional superiority. Consequently, market premiums should be substantiated by empirical evidence rather than market perception.
