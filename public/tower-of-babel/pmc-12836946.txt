Nutritional and inflammatory biomarkers for COVID-19 susceptibility: a focus on vitamin D, potassium, and hematological indices
Authors: Yaser Khajebishak, Ali Soleimani, Sadra Madani, Asghar Tanomand, Marziyeh Ashrafi, Younes Badri, Ashraf Norouz Yeganeh, Laleh Payahoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSince its emergence, COVID-19 has caused a global health crisis with multi-systemic complications, particularly affecting the respiratory and cardiovascular systems. Identifying potential risk factors is important for early detection of disease and may improve patients survival. This study was carried out to evaluate inflammatory, hematological and nutritional indicators to determine the risk of COVID-19 infection.Materials and methodsThis case-control study conducted on 234 COVID-19 cases and 160 non-COVID-19 controls from 2021 to 2023 in Maragheh, northwest of Iran. Participants were recurited from referrals to local hospitals and healthcare centers of Maragheh University of Medical Sciences. Laboratory methods were employed to measure serum vitamin D, potassium, CRP, and the hematological indices. Standard statistical analysis were performed in SPSS version 21, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsNo significant difference was observed in gender distribution between the two groups (54% vs. 52% female; p = 0.858). The median serum CRP level was significantly higher in COVID-19 patients (60.5 mg/L, IQR: 40.0–85.0) compared to the non-COVID-19 group (37.5 mg/L, IQR: 20.0–65.0, p < 0.001). Creatinine levels were significantly lower in the COVID-19 group (p = 0.033). Additionally, the median vitamin D level was significantly lower in the COVID-19 group (33.0 ng/mL, IQR: 22.0–45.0; p < 0.001). A significant protective effect of vitamin D, potassium, creatinine, and lymphocyte-to-CRP (Lym/CRP) ratio against COVID-19 was observed, while age, CRP, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) showed a positive association with increased risk of COVID-19 risk.ConclusionA practical clinical approach to potentially reduce the risk of COVID-19 could be assessing inflammatory (CRP and NLR) and nutritional biomarkers (vitamin D and potassium).Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

COVID-19, the disease caused by SARS-CoV-2, was first detected in December 2019 in Wuhan, China. It spread rapidly, emerging as a critical global issue. Throughout the pandemic, it became clear that COVID-19 affects more than the lungs, provoking various health implications, including immune, cardiovascular, neurological, and gastrointestinal disruptions. Many patients diagnosed with COVID-19 exhibit a range of symptoms, including fever, anorexia, cough, fatigue, and lymphopenia [1–3].

Given the evidence, the role of optimized nutrition in accelerating recovery from respiratory diseases and supporting the immune system has been confirmed [4, 5]. While optimal nutritional status and a strong immune system play a critical role in survival from COVID-19 [6–8], nutritional deficiency worsens patient status and raises mortality rates by prolonging hospitalization, delaying recovery, and increasing complication rates of [9–11]. This has generated an interest towards vitamins and minerals, and how deficiencies may exacerbate diseases like COVID-19 [12].

Vitamin D, a fat-soluble immunomodulatory vitamin, plays a major role in inflammation [13]. and has been shown to reduce the inflammatory cytokine storm through various mechanisms [14].

Imbalances in electrolytes and vitamin levels are often obesrved in COVID-19 patients [15, 16]. Hypokalemia, a major complication of the disease, exacerbates respiratory distress and heart injury through various mechanisms [17, 18]. Magnesium plays multiple important roles in the human body, such as in the transport and activation of vitamins [19]. For proper function, both the vitamin D receptor (VDR) and vitamin D binding protein (VDBP) require magnesium as a cofactor [19–21].

Along with nutritional deficiencies, the main causes of mortality in these patients are inflammatory cytokine storm and the immune system dysfunction [22, 23]. Cytokine storms can result in lymphopenia [24–26] and myocardial injury, which aggravates mortality in COVID-19 patients [27–29]. Two other biomarkers of systemic inflammation, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and the CRP-to-albumin ratio (CAR), further complicate their condition [30, 31].

While previous studies have focused on biomarkers predicting disease severity in hospitalized COVID-19 patients, fewer have examined their role in predicting susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection. This study was specifically designed to identify biomarkers associated with the risk of COVID-19, rather than predicting severity among already infected individuals. We aimed to investigate whether serum levels of vitamin D, potassium, CRP, and hematological indices could serve as indicators of infection susceptibility at Maragheh University of Medical Sciences, Iran.

Materials and methods

We conducted an observational case-control study, recruiting 394 participants (234 COVID-19 patients and 160 non-COVID-19 controls) aged 30 to 75 years between 2021 and 2023 at healthcare centers of Maragheh University of Medical Sciences, northwest of Iran. Maragheh is the second-largest city in the southern East Azerbaijan Province and serves as a major healthcare hub for at least five neighboring cities. During the COVID-19 pandemic, its hospitals were designated as the main referral centers for the area.

Two groups of participants were selected. The case group consisted of individuals with laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 infection and severe COVID-19 who required intensive care, in accordance with both World Health Organization (WHO) criteria and our institutional clinical protocol. Severe disease was characterized by substantial pulmonary involvement (> 25%) on chest computed tomography (CT) scan, as evaluated by a specialist radiologist, and/or an oxygen saturation (SpO₂) of < 90% on room air at the time of admission. Controls were selected from asymptomatic individuals attending the same healthcare centers for routine COVID-19 screening during the same period. All control participants tested negative for COVID-19 by RT-PCR at the time of enrollment. Individuals with a known history of previous COVID-19 infection were excluded from the control group. The median duration of hospitalization was 7 days among the enrolled patients. People with acute illnesses, including kidney and liver diseases, hypertension, diabetes, heart diseases, and mental or degenerative neurological diseases, were excluded from both groups to minimize confounding effects of comorbidities on the studied biomarkers. Participants provided written informed consent and completed a demographic questionnaire completed at baseline. The regional ethics committee approved the study protocol (Approval number: 5/4/2787).

For patients who required a minimum of 5 days of hospitalization, blood samples were collected within 24 to 48 h of admission to capture acute-phase reactant levels during the initial critical period of illness. Five mL of venous blood was collected from each participant for biochemical testing. For serum separation, samples were immediately centrifuged (3000 rpm, 10 min), then aliquoted and preserved at -80 °C until assay. A Sysmex XP300 analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) was used to measure complete blood counts, from which the NLR was determined. Serum albumin was measured using the Parsazmon kit and vitamin D levels were determined through an automated assay (Vidas PC analyzer, BioMerieux, France). Additional measurements included CRP (commercial ELISA kit), potassium and magnesium (AutoAnalyzer system), and creatinine (enzymatic method). Sample size was calculated based on the CRP variable from a similar study (males: mean = 89.29 mg/L, SD = 73.4; females: mean = 50.70 mg/L, SD = 54.14). By considering 90% power and α = 0.05, the minimum sample size was calculated as 71 participants per group [32].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 21.0, Chicago, IL, USA). After assessing data normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, normally distributed data are presented as mean (SD), and non-normal distributed data as median (IQR). Frequencies and percentages represented categorical variables. Group comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for numerical data and chi-square tests for categorical factors. Multiple logistic regression was used to adjust for confounding factors and to calculate odds ratios for COVID-19 predictive variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The median vitamin D level was significantly lower in COVID-19 patients (33.0 ng/mL, IQR: 22.0–45.0) compared to control group (p < 0.001). Figure 1 shows the serum vitamin D levels in both groups. Multivariate logistic regression confirmed a significant protective role for vitamin D, potassium, creatinine, and the lymphocyte-to-CRP ratio against COVID-19. Specifically, the risk of COVID-19 decreased by 3% for each unit increase in vitamin D and by 56% for each unit increase in potassium. Conversely, age, CRP, and NLR showed a positive association with COVID-19 risk. Each one-year increase in age was associated with a 4% increase in risk, while each unit increase in CRP and NLR increased the risk by 3% and 6%, respectively. Discussion

COVID-19 is a respiratory infection that can present clinically in a wide range of severities, from no symptoms to severe complications [33, 34]. This study addresses a distinct aspect of COVID-19 by focusing on infection risk rather than disease severity. While most cited literature examined the prognostic factors in hospitalized patients, this study specifically identifies biomarkers associated with susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection in a community setting. It is a vital point because the mechanisms underlying susceptibility to infection may differ from those which lead to the progression of the disease.

Our study provides specific evidence that low vitamin D is an independent risk factor for infection. Similar to our findings, Merzon et al. showed an independent relationship between low plasma 25-hydroxy vitamin D levels and the risk of both developing COVID-19 and hospitalization [35]. Other cohort studies have also noted increased mortality in patients with vitamin D deficiency [36, 37]. Contrary to our results, Cereda et al. discovered that although vitamin D deficiency was common among hospitalized COVID-19 patients, low vitamin D levels were not related to worse clinical outcomes [38]. Furthermore, Povaliaeva et al. (2022) demonstrated that COVID-19 could disturb vitamin D metabolism [39]. These observations can be explained by various mechanisms. Vitamin D prevents the progression of COVID-19 through processes that improve immune control and reduce aberrant inflammatory responses. It enhances the body’s first line of defense by promoting the production of substances like cathelicidin and defensins which can directly inhibit viral replication and aid clearance [40–42]. Vitamin D also controls the balances between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory signals, thereby modulating adaptive immune responses. This immunomodulatory effect reduces the likelihood of a cytokine storm- a possibly fatal hyperinflammatory reaction associated with severe COVID-19 outcomes [40, 43, 44]. Moreover, by upregulating angiotensin-converting enzyme receptor (ACE2), the primary cellular receptor for SARS-CoV-2 entry, vitamin D offers pulmonary protection by mitigating the pathogenic consequences of the virus on the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) [42, 45]. Furthermore, vitamin D reduces oxidative stress and maintains respiratory epithelial integrity, minimizing pulmonary tissue damage [43, 46]. While causation must be proven through randomized trials [42, 47], current observational and meta-analytic data consistently links adequate vitamin D levels to lower infection rates, less severe disease progression, and fewer ICU admissions. The effects of vitamin D on immune regulation, inflammation management, and tissue protection may prevent COVID-19 from advancing to more severe stages [40–43, 45].

Our findings indicate that hypokalemia was an independent risk factor for COVID-19. Previous studies have shown that hypokalemia is among the most common electrolyte abnormalities in COVID-19 patients [48–52]. Hypokalemia was frequently observed in hospitalized COVID-19 patients and served as an independent predictor of the need for invasive mechanical ventilation, indicating its utility as a marker of disease risk and severity rather than mortality [49, 51, 53, 54]. However, one prospective study found no significant difference in serum potassium levels between COVID-19 patients and controls [55]. some research has shown that both low and high potassium levels during hospitalization are associated with increased mortality, therefore suggesting a J-shaped relationship and highlighting the importance of maintaining potassium within a normal range [51, 56–60].

The underlying mechanism for this association may be two ways. A pre-existing state of hypokalemia could impair immune cell function, such as lymphocyte activation and proliferation, thereby weakening the initial immune defense against the virus. In contrast, if infection occurs, the binding of COVID-19 to ACE2 receptors disrupts the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), increasing angiotensin II activity. This promotes renal sodium reabsorption and potassium excretion, creating a vicious cycle of progressive potassium depletion that may exacerbate disease severity [49, 61, 62]. Because the degree of hypokalaemia correlated with indicators of systemic inflammation and disease severity, it is a sensitive biomarker for progression in COVID-19 [51, 54, 62]. Hypokalaemia also increases the risk of cardiac arrhythmias, particularly when combined with specific medications or direct viral impacts on the heart [49, 54, 61]. Therefore, in severe COVID-19 cases, monitoring and controlling potassium levels are vital for prognosis and complication prevention [49, 51, 54].

The current research highlights the potential of the CRP-to-albumin ratio (CAR) as an inflammatory indicator for assessing disease risk, showing significantly higher CAR in COVID-19 patients compared to non-infected controls. The biological basis of CAR’s prognostic significance is its combination of two critical markers: CRP, an acute phase reactant reflecting systemic inflammation and immune activation, and albumin, a negative acute phase protein whose levels decline with inflammation and inadequate nutritional status [63–67]. Studies show comparable predictive power among these indicators, with some finding CAR to predict death and severe disease better than CRP or albumin alone [63, 65, 66, 68–70]. An elevated CAR is a strong prognostic marker in COVID-19, closely linked to greater disease risk, higher ICU admission rates, need for mechanical ventilation and increased mortality [63–66, 68–71]. Significantly, no studies to date have reported opposing results, thereby validating the consistency and reliability of our findings. High CAR reflects both increased inflammatory response and impaired nutritional or hepatic reserves, both of which are related to COVID-19 progression and organ dysfunction [63–66].

Mechanistically, elevated CRP levels indicate cytokine driven inflammation, whereas hypoalbuminemia may reflect capillary leakage, liver damage, or malnutrition-all contributing to immune dysregulation and adverse clinical outcomes [63–66]. Variations in ideal CAR cutoffs and predictive accuracy could be explained by methodological differences, including patient populations, timing of measurement, and outcome definitions [70, 72, 73]. Overall, the evidence supports CAR as a strong, readily available biomarker for early risk stratification in COVID-19, though more prospective studies and standardization of cutoff values are required to optimize its clinical use [63–66, 68–71].

This study found notable changes in hematological indicators between non-infected controls and COVID-19 patients, including a rise in NLR and a corresponding drop in LCR in COVID-19 group. These findings suggest a shift toward greater systemic inflammation and compromised immunological regulation, indicating that both NLR and LCR may be useful indicators in the context of COVID-19.

Independent of age or comorbidities, several recent meta-analyses and large cohort studies have shown that a high NLR is a strong predictor of severe COVID-19 illness and death, with higher NLR values correlating with worse outcomes [74–78]. Similarly, diminished LCR has been strongly associated with higher risk of ICU admission and death; some studies even indicate that LCR may outperform NLR in predicting 28-day mortality [79–81]. Furthermore, while NLR and LCR levels typically stabilize during recovery, patients with poor prognosis exhibit persistently abnormal values [80, 82]. These results highlight the clinical utility of NLR and LCR as affordable, readily available indicators for early risk stratification and management of COVID-19 patients, as well as the key role of immune and inflammatory dysregulation in disease progression [74, 77, 79, 81, 82].

The inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers examined in this study also have differential diagnosis and patient management implications. For instance, raised CRP and NLR are generally considered features of systemic inflammation, but they are not unique to COVID-19 and may be found in other respiratory infections as well, such as influenza and community-acquired pneumonia [83]. Hence, these parameters should be considered as adjuncts to support decision-making regarding risk stratification and triage rather than being used as diagnostic markers of certainty. At the same time, detecting deficiencies such as hypokalemia or vitamin D shortage at the time of admission not only enables the provision of necessary supplements but can also help to improve clinical outcomes and guide personalized patient management in conjunction with standard treatments.

Our research elucidates a set of biomarkers that are associated with the susceptibility to COVID-19, as the pandemic changes the background, new layers are being added to the context. The immune responses to SARS-CoV-2 have been significantly changed by the widespread COVID-19 vaccinations, which may affect the interaction between the nutritional status, inflammatory markers, and the risk of infection [84, 85].Moreover, the differences in the pathogenicity of the new viral variants may lead to changes in the clinical significance of the biomarkers that we have identified. Furthermore, it is very important to focus on Long COVID as well, a situation in which the symptoms persist after the acute infection. There is a necessity for the subsequent studies to figure out if vitamin D, potassium, or hematological indices at baseline can predict the risk of Long COVID, thereby extending their prognostic utility beyond the initial infection stage [86, 87].

Limitations

Our study has several critical limitations that may affect our results and conclusions. Its case-control design precludes causal inferences, and we lacked data on participants’ dietary intake and, crucially, their vaccination status and the specific SARS-CoV-2 variants involved in the infections. The current literature suggests that both vaccination and viral evolution are critical confounders that can modulate immune responses and the clinical relevance of the biomarkers we identified [84, 85]. Therefore, future prospective studies must account for vaccine status and viral evolution to clarify the independent role of these biomarkers in the current phase of the pandemic and to determine if our findings remain robust in vaccinated populations and against circulating variants.

It is worth noticing that further studies should be directed towards the use of non-invasive biomarkers combined with the readily available hematological and nutritional indices that we have discussed. The development of such composite models can go far in the early detection of infection and the assessment of the risk of COVID-19 in various community and healthcare environments [88–90].

Furthermore, future research should assess how other nutritional and biochemical markers influence the susceptibility and survival of this respiratory infectious disease. We recommend designing large-scale prospective cohort studies that collect comprehensive data such as vaccination status and dietary habits to confirm these potential relationships and establish causality. Conclusion

In this case-control study, we identified several inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers independently associated with COVID-19 infection. Elevated CRP and NLR may increase infection risk, while higher vitamin D, potassium, creatinine, and LCR demonstrated protective effects. These readily available biomarkers show potential for COVID-19 risk stratification, suggesting the utility of monitoring both inflammatory and nutritional parameters in clinical practice.
