Stereophotographic Assessment of Intraocular Pressure Reduction in Myopic Eyes with Glaucoma-Compatible Optic Neuropathy
Authors: Yang Zhang, Ailing Bian, Qi Zhou, Lüe Li, Yu Jin, Zixi Sun, Youxin Chen, Gangwei Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe aim of our work is to evaluate stereoscopic photographic changes in the optic nerve head among patients with glaucoma-compatible optic neuropathy in myopia (GCON-M) after intraocular pressure (IOP) reduction and to determine whether these changes represent true progressive glaucomatous damage.MethodsThis self-controlled, observational cohort study included 41 patients with bilateral normal-tension GCON-M. The eye with the worse baseline visual field (VF) mean deviation received intraocular pressure (IOP)-lowering treatment targeting a 30% reduction, while the fellow eye served as an untreated control. Participants underwent serial VF testing and stereophotographic imaging over a median follow-up of 48 months. Structural and functional progression was compared between the treated and control eyes.ResultsAmong the 41 patients (82 eyes), the treatment group achieved a mean IOP reduction of 31.1%, significantly greater than that in the control group (P < 0.001). There was no significant difference between the groups in the change in VF mean deviation (P = 0.25) or structural progression scores (P = 0.37). Disease progression occurred in 9.7% of treated and 12.2% of control eyes. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no significant difference in progression-free survival between the groups (P = 0.76), which was further confirmed by multivariate Cox regression analysis (P = 0.67).ConclusionsThe long-term stability of GCON-M in many untreated eyes suggests that these changes may represent non-progressive remodeling rather than true glaucomatous damage. These findings argue against routine glaucoma therapy and support an individualized monitoring strategy to avoid overtreatment.

Introduction

The prevalence of myopia has increased sharply across East and Southeast Asia, now affecting more than 80% of young adults and nearly 40% of adults over age 40 [1, 2]. This rising trend presents a significant public health challenge, as myopia not only causes reversible visual impairment due to uncorrected refractive errors but also heightens the risk of irreversible complications such as myopic macular degeneration and glaucoma.

Glaucoma remains a leading cause of irreversible blindness globally [3], but its diagnosis and management are particularly challenging in myopic eyes, which often exhibit structural alterations including axial elongation, optic disc tilting and rotation, lamina cribrosa deformation, and peripapillary atrophy [4, 5]. These features complicate the distinction between early glaucomatous damage and anatomical variations related to myopia alone.

A substantial proportion of myopic patients, particularly those with optic discs displaying glaucomatous-like features but statistically normal intraocular pressure (IOP), are often diagnosed with normal-tension glaucoma (NTG) and treated accordingly. However, whether these changes indicate early glaucomatous damage or benign myopic remodeling remains uncertain. Misdiagnosing benign myopic changes as glaucoma may result in unnecessary lifelong treatment, leading to psychological and socioeconomic burdens and exposing patients to potential treatment-related complications. In some cases, overtreatment may inadvertently increase the risk of vision loss rather than preventing it [6, 7].

This study aims to investigate whether optic disc changes in patients with glaucoma-compatible optic neuropathy in myopia (GCON-M) with normal IOP reflect true glaucomatous progression requiring intervention, or benign structural alterations that do not benefit from IOP-lowering therapy. To address this, we conducted a self-controlled longitudinal study involving patients with GCON-M. In this study, one eye of each patient received medical IOP-lowering therapy, while the fellow eye remained untreated. Through stereophotographic follow-up, we assessed whether standard IOP-lowering treatment improved the optic disc changes in GCON-M eyes. Our findings will provide valuable insights to inform clinical decision-making in the management of GCON-M patients.

Methods

This was a single-center, self-controlled observational study conducted at Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) in Beijing, China. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of PUMCH and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

A total of 41 patients normal-tension GCON-M were enrolled and followed longitudinally. Inclusion criteria included: age 18–50 years; bilateral glaucomatous-like optic discs were defined as a vertical cup-to-disc ratio ≥ 0.5 in both eyes, accompanied by localized retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) defects, with or without notching or rim thinning in the inferotemporal and/or superotemporal disc regions, and in the absence of other identifiable causes; spherical equivalent ≥ − 3.00 D, IOP between 11–21 mmHg without IOP-lowering treatment across at least two visits, open anterior chamber angle confirmed by Goldmann single-mirror gonioscopy (GW Cheng), and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 20/30 or better.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) advanced visual field (VF) loss, defined as a mean deviation (MD) < − 16 dB or sensitivity ≤ 10 dB at any of the two points closest to fixation; (2) prior antiglaucoma treatment, ocular surgery, laser procedures, or trauma; (3) unreliable VF testing or other conditions affecting visual fields; (4) retinal diseases, optic neuropathies, neovascular disorders, or other non-glaucomatous causes of VF defects; and (5) pregnancy or lactation.

All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic evaluation, including BCVA, IOP measured using a Goldmann applanation tonometer, axial length, fundus stereophotography, and VF testing. Pupils were dilated prior to acquiring stereophotographs using a Kowa Nonmyd WX camera, which captures stereo pairs simultaneously for standardized optic disc evaluation.

Optic nerve alterations were assessed from stereoscopic optic disc photographs according to the 5R principle (Table 1). The photographs were independently assessed and scored by two experienced glaucoma specialists (Y Zhang and GW Cheng). VF testing was conducted using the Humphrey Field Analyzer with the 24–2 SITA standard strategy. Reliable VF tests were defined as those with < 15% false-positive and false-negative rates, and < 20% fixation losses. Unreliable tests were repeated after a rest period of at least 30 min, with up to five attempts allowed to obtain a minimum of two reliable tests per eye.

The eye with the worse MD was designated the intervention eye and received treatment targeting a 30% IOP reduction. Latanoprost 0.005% was used as first-line therapy; if not tolerated, an alternative prostaglandin analogue was prescribed. If the target IOP reduction was not achieved within 3 months, brinzolamide 1% was added. Beta-blockers and alpha agonists were avoided due to potential systemic side effects. IOP was reassessed one week after each medication change. Patients who did not reach the target IOP were excluded to avoid selection bias related to uncontrolled IOP.

Participants were followed every 3 months in the first year and every 6 months thereafter. The primary endpoint was VF progression, with or without structural changes. VF progression was defined as a ≥ 10 dB decline in two adjacent points within a defect, or a new defect involving ≥ 3 contiguous points. This definition was adapted to balance sensitivity and specificity by requiring both magnitude and spatial contiguity. Progression was confirmed on a subsequent VF test to ensure reproducibility. Once an endpoint was reached, treatment adjustments were made at the clinician’s discretion.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Premium v21 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range. Categorical variables as counts and percentages. Normality was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Between-group comparisons used t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests; within-subject comparisons used paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Kaplan–Meier and Cox proportional hazards models were employed to evaluate progression risk. BCVA was converted to the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR) for statistical analyses. A two-sided P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Based on the study’s endpoint criteria, disease progression occurred in 12.2% (n = 5) of control eyes and 9.7% (n = 4) of treated eyes. Four eyes exhibited VF progression without changes in optic disc morphology, characterized by isolated enlargement of pre-existing RNFL defects. Two eyes showed progression without any detectable structural alterations. Seven eyes demonstrated localized progression within previously affected hemifields. During the follow-up period, disc hemorrhage occurred in seven eyes across both groups. Parapapillary zone beta expansion was observed in five treated and three control eyes.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed no significant difference in progression-free survival between the groups (P = 0.758, Fig. 1). To further validate this finding, a Cox proportional hazards model was constructed, which included treatment assignment along with several covariates, including zone beta expansion, disc hemorrhage, cup-to-disc ratio and change in the five R’s rule scores. Discussion

Numerous studies, including the Blue Mountains Eye Study, have established a strong association between high myopia and glaucoma [8–10]. However, the evaluation of highly myopic eyes remains a clinical challenge. Many of these patients, despite having normal IOP, exhibit glaucomatous-like optic disc appearances [11]. Despite growing recognition that optic disc alterations in high myopia follow a pathophysiological course distinct from that of primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) or NTG, existing diagnostic frameworks continue to apply criteria developed for non-myopic populations. This raises critical questions: How can we distinguish true glaucomatous damage in myopic eyes from structural or functional changes related to myopia alone? More importantly, when should IOP-lowering treatment be initiated, and for which patients?

Optic nerve head evaluation remains a cornerstone in glaucoma diagnosis and monitoring. Stereophotography offers distinct advantages over optical coherence tomography (OCT) in highly myopic eyes. By capturing side-by-side images at a fixed angle and under consistent exposure conditions, it generates standardized images that more accurately reflect the morphological characteristics of the optic disc. Stereophotography preserves the three-dimensional perception of the optic nerve head, enabling a more detailed assessment of features like rim thinning, notching, and RNFL defects-features that may be overlooked or misclassified by OCT or non-stereoscopic imaging techniques [12, 13]. In this study, all included eyes were confirmed to meet the structural criteria for GCON-M through stereophotography, and changes in their follow-up structural parameters were monitored. To ensure the comprehensiveness and consistency of optic disc evaluations, all assessments adhered to the five R’s rules. After unilateral IOP-lowering treatment and a 4-year follow-up, no significant differences were observed in changes of optic nerve structural parameters or five R’s rule scores between the treated eye and the untreated fellow eye.

In this study, only 10% of eyes with GCON-M exhibited VF progression, primarily as an expansion of pre-existing, well-demarcated, and steep-edged defects (Fig. 2), rather than new defects appearing in previously unaffected hemifields, especially those corresponding to preserved rim areas. The appearance of new defects in these areas is more characteristic of classical POAG and should raise suspicion for true glaucomatous conversion, prompting intervention [14–16]. Such steep-edged or “dense island” defects may represent a characteristic VF pattern in highly myopic eyes, potentially reflecting biomechanical distortion rather than progressive axonal loss, further distinguishing them from the arcuate or nasal step patterns characteristic of glaucomatous progression [17]. Sawada et al. demonstrated that new localized VF defects in previously unaffected regions are more indicative of glaucomatous progression than the diffuse deepening of pre-existing defects [15]. Our observation was that VF changes did not correlate with treatment response. Among the nine eyes that demonstrated VF progression, six exhibited deterioration without corresponding structural changes at the optic disc or showed only isolated enlargement of pre-existing RNFL defects. This frequent dissociation between structural and functional damage appears to be a characteristic feature of GCON-M. It may be attributed to non-glaucomatous mechanisms such as scleral stretching, optic disc tilting, retinoschisis, or compromised microcirculation-biomechanical and vascular factors commonly associated with high myopia [18–22].

In myopic glaucoma, a higher incidence of lamina cribrosa (LC) defects has been observed, often aligned with corresponding VF loss, and histological data reveal significant differences in LC morphology that may alter the biomechanical response to IOP-related stress [23]. These defects are believed to result from axial elongation–induced stretching and deformation of the optic disc, rather than from focal rim excavation typical of glaucomatous optic neuropathy [24]. This may explain cases exhibiting disc cupping and RNFL thinning in the absence of rim notching or focal rim narrowing. Notably, many LC defects remain structurally stable and do not lead to progressive VF deterioration. Once axial elongation stabilizes, deformation of the optic nerve head may plateau, producing structural changes that mimic glaucomatous cupping without associated functional decline [18, 25]. These dichotomous progression patterns imply that myopia with glaucomatous-like features constitutes a heterogeneous clinical entity, distinct from classical glaucoma.

While NTG treatment typically targets IOP reduction, our data suggest that this strategy may be ineffective in eyes with GCON-M. Despite achieving a 30% reduction in IOP, the treated group did not demonstrate better functional or structural outcomes compared to the control group. This suggests that IOP may not be the sole or primary driver of damage in myopic optic neuropathy, though delayed treatment response or other contributing factors cannot be excluded, or that standard therapeutic targets (e.g., 30% reduction) are insufficient [26]. This lack of treatment response raises challenges to the traditional paradigm of initiating therapy for GCON-M.

The dissociation between traditional glaucomatous progression markers, such as optic disc hemorrhage and parapapillary zone beta enlargement, and functional decline in our cohort warrants particular attention [27]. Although these features are well-established predictors of progression in glaucoma, their high prevalence in highly myopic eyes may reflect underlying structural fragility or biomechanical instability rather than active axonal loss. This interpretation is supported by recent imaging studies demonstrating that such findings are common in myopic eyes regardless of disease status [27, 28]. Consequently, their prognostic value in distinguishing true glaucomatous conversion may be limited, especially in the absence of confirmed functional deterioration. These observations underscore the need for myopia-specific progression markers, potentially informed by advanced techniques such as deep learning-based analysis of dynamic scleral strain.

In light of our findings, the routine initiation of IOP-lowering therapy in young, mildly affected patients with GCON-M may warrant reconsideration. In this patient group, structural features that mimic glaucomatous damage are common, yet functional decline is either absent or progresses very slowly. This raises concerns regarding overdiagnosis and overtreatment—both of which may subject patients to unnecessary medication burden, psychological stress, and healthcare costs without clear benefit. A precision medicine approach is needed, one that integrates multimodal imaging, biomechanical analysis, and careful follow up to identify patients truly at risk of progression. In some cases, adopting a more conservative management strategy, emphasizing longitudinal monitoring and individualized risk assessment rather than immediate treatment, may not only be safer but also more appropriate.

Study limitations include the small sample size, relatively young cohort, and limited follow-up duration. While sufficient to assess early changes, a longer observation period may be necessary to capture the slow and heterogeneous progression of GCON-M. Although all included eyes maintained statistically normal IOP, the potential influence of transient IOP fluctuations, diurnal variation, or biomechanically amplified IOP-related stress cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

In summary, many cases of GCON-M represent a distinct clinical entity characterized by glaucomatous-appearing optic discs that remain stable, non-progressive, and unresponsive to conventional IOP-lowering therapy. These findings challenge the direct application of NTG treatment strategies in this population, suggesting that such structural changes may reflect biomechanical remodeling rather than active neurodegeneration. Particularly in younger individuals with mild or ambiguous findings, immediate intervention may not be necessary. Instead, a personalized management approach focused on longitudinal monitoring and individualized risk stratification, with cautious follow-up, remains the most appropriate strategy for stable GCON-M cases, helping to prevent overtreatment and reduce unnecessary patient burden.
