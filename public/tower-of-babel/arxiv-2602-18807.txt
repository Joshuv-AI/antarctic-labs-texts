Chat-Based Support Alone May Not Be Enough: Comparing Conversational and Embedded LLM Feedback for Mathematical Proof Learning
Eason Chen, Sophia Judicke, Kayla Beigh, Xinyi Tang, Isabel Wang, Nina Yuan, Zimo Xiao, Chuangji Li, Shizhuo Li, Reed Luttmer, Shreya Singh, Maria Yampolsky, Naman Parikh, Yvonne Zhao, Meiyi Chen, Scarlett Huang, Anishka Mohanty, Gregory Johnson, John Mackey, Jionghao Lin, Ken Koedinger

Abstract
We evaluate GPTutor , an LLM-powered tutoring system for an undergraduate discrete mathematics course. It integrates two LLM-supported tools: a structured proof-review tool that provides embedded feedback on students’ written proof attempts, and a chatbot for math questions. In a staggered-access study with 148 students, earlier access was associated with higher homework performance during the interval when only the experimental group could use the system, while we did not observe this performance increase transfer to exam scores. Usage logs show that students with lower self-efficacy and prior exam performance used both components more frequently. Session-level behavioral labels, produced by human coding and scaled using an automated classifier, characterize how students engaged with the chatbot (e.g., answer-seeking or help-seeking). In models controlling for prior performance and self-efficacy, higher chatbot usage and answer-seeking behavior were negatively associated with subsequent midterm performance, whereas proof-review usage showed no detectable independent association. Together, the findings suggest that chatbot-based support alone may not reliably support transfer to independent assessment of math proof-learning outcomes, whereas work-anchored, structured feedback appears less associated with reduced learning.

System Developments

We developed
GPTutor
to support students’ engagement with open-ended proof-writing homework.
Rather than replacing students’ reasoning processes, GPTutor is designed to scaffold reflection on logical structure while preserving opportunities for productive struggle.
GPTutor provides high-quality, timely feedback on open-ended proof-writing assignments and supports students in asking math-related questions.
It features two core functionalities: (1) a Proof-Review-GPTutor that provides structured feedback on students’ written proofs, and (2) a chatbot that allows students to engage in real-time, math-focused dialogue.

3.2
AI Chatbot for Answering Students’ Questions

Figure 2:
Screenshot of the chatbot interface illustrating answer-seeking and escalation behaviors. Left: A student requests an explanation, and the chatbot responds with step-by-step guidance and a reflection prompt. Right: Despite receiving pedagogical scaffolding, the student continues asking for the answer to the same problem, illustrating escalation behavior where students bypass guided reasoning to seek direct solutions.

Given students’ widespread familiarity with chatbot-based LLM interfaces,
GPTutor
also includes a built-in chatbot powered by GPT-4o to enable conversational, mathematics-focused support.
The interface follows a standard chat format (message-by-message dialogue), minimizing onboarding friction and allowing consistent logging of interaction traces.
The chatbot does not have access to course-specific materials; instead, it is guided by a system prompt that constrains the conversation to mathematics-related discussion.

During preliminary testing, we observed that unconstrained chatbot interactions often led to direct answers.
To mitigate answer outsourcing, the chatbot prompt instructs the model to avoid directly providing final answers or complete proof steps.
Instead, it uses Socratic questioning, scaffolding prompts, and partial hints to support students in constructing their own solutions.

4
Methods

We conducted a quantitative study to examine (1) whether earlier access to
GPTutor
is associated with differences in homework and exam performance, and (2) how students’ reliance on two system components (chatbot vs. proof-review) relates to self-efficacy and subsequent outcomes. Data sources included course performance records, system interaction logs, and self-efficacy surveys.

4.1
Participants and Study Design

This IRB-approved study was conducted at a large US research university, deploying
GPTutor
in an undergraduate discrete mathematics course (308 enrolled students). The course covered logic, sets, relations, and proof writing. Homework and exams followed standard course procedures, and students were not permitted to use AI tools during exams.

Students were invited to provide consent and complete a baseline survey after Midterm 1. Participation was voluntary and did not affect course grades. After consent, participants were randomly assigned at the individual level to an experimental or control condition. The experimental group received access to
GPTutor
earlier (after Midterm 1 and HW4), while the control group received access later (after Midterm 2). This staggered-access design provided descriptive comparisons of performance during periods when access differed, while ensuring all participants ultimately received access.
In total, 155 students consented (77 experimental, 78 control). After the university’s withdrawal period, 73 experimental and 75 control students remained; by the final exam, the control group decreased to 72 due to attrition.

4.2
GPTutor Use in the Course

Homework assignments were submitted in
L
a
T
e
X
, and students were encouraged (but not required) to paste drafts into the Proof-Review-GPTutor prior to submission. Students could also use the chatbot for mathematics-related questions while working on homework or studying. Course policies did not restrict students’ use of other external resources outside exams.

4.3
Measures and Data Sources

Academic Performance.

Performance outcomes included scores from ten homework assignments and three midterm exams (Midterm 1–3). Homework and exams were graded by course staff using standard procedures. Some assessments contained bonus items; thus, standardized scores could exceed 100 points.

System Interaction Logs.

GPTutor
logged all interactions with the Proof-Review-GPTutor and the chatbot. For each participant, we recorded timestamps and counts of interactions, including (1) the number of Proof-Review-GPTutor requests and (2) the number of chatbot messages sent by the student. We aggregated usage by exam intervals to align reliance with subsequent assessments. For example, analyses predicting Midterm 3 used interactions between Midterm 2 and Midterm 3. We operationalized reliance as frequency-based measures within each interval:
Proof-review usage frequency
is the number of proof submissions for structured feedback, and
chatbot usage frequency
is the number of student messages to the chatbot.

Chat behavior labeling.

We collected 659 chatbot sessions during the Midterm 2
\rightarrow
Midterm 3 interval, two undergraduate coders independently labeled a random sample of 80 sessions across three dimensions: student intent (answer-seeking, help-seeking, other;
\kappa=0.780
), topic type (conceptual vs. procedural;
\kappa=0.732
), and follow-up type (engage in conversation, escalate for getting answer, stop, other;
\kappa=0.702
). A PhD researcher then adjudicated disagreements to form a consensus label set. We evaluated an LLM-based classifier against these consensus labels and observed substantial to excellent agreement (studentIntent
\kappa=0.84
, topicType
\kappa=0.76
, followUpType
\kappa=0.68
). After this validation, we use the LLM-based classifier to label the full interval and aggregate student-level proportions for supplementary analyses.

Survey Measures.

At baseline (after Midterm 1), participants
completed a self-efficacy scale adapted from prior validated instruments
[
19
]
. Internal consistency was high (Cronbach’s
\alpha=0.91
).

4.4
Analysis Strategy

All quantitative analyses were conducted in Python. We organized analyses to align with the paper’s core questions about access patterns, adoption/help-seeking, outcome associations, and mediation associations.

Access-level comparisons.

To characterize performance differences during periods when access differed, we examined homework and exam outcomes under the staggered-access deployment using regression-based models. For homework, we fit mixed-effects regressions with student as a random intercept and prior performance as a covariate, focusing on the interval when only the experimental group had access. For exams, we used ordinary least squares regressions controlling for baseline midterm performance.

Adoption and help-seeking (RQ1).

To test whether reliance reflects help-seeking, we examined associations between baseline self-efficacy, prior performance (e.g., Midterm 2), and subsequent usage frequency of each component.

Outcome associations beyond prior performance (RQ2).

We conducted hierarchical multiple regression analyses predicting Midterm 3 scores from chatbot usage frequency and the proof-review component, while controlling for self-efficacy and prior performance (e.g., Midterm 1 and Midterm 2), consistent with common analytic practice in educational research.

Mediation associations (RQ3).

We tested whether chatbot usage mediates links from (a) self-efficacy to Midterm 3 performance and (b) Midterm 2 to Midterm 3 performance using bootstrapped confidence intervals for indirect effects
[
20
]
. Mediation analyses focus on the Midterm 2
\rightarrow
Midterm 3 interval, when all participants had access to
GPTutor
, maximizing sample size and comparability.

6
Discussion

This study examined how students engaged with two forms of LLM-based support for proof writing and how both access and reliance relate to outcomes in an authentic undergraduate course. Taken together, the results suggest that access alone is an incomplete indicator of impact: who uses which interface, and how that usage is situated within students’ prior performance and self-efficacy, is critical for interpretation. These results align with theories of generative processing and productive struggle, suggesting that interface constraints shape whether AI support preserves learner sensemaking.

6.1
Access aligns with improved homework but not exam transfer

Earlier access to
GPTutor
coincided with higher homework performance during the period when only the experimental group could use the system, whereas midterm exam performance did not differ by access condition. This contrast suggests a boundary on what LLM-based support influences in this setting. Homework affords iterative drafting and revision, aligning with repeated feedback cycles. Exams, in contrast, require independent proof construction without access to tools. The absence of exam differences therefore points to limited transfer from AI-supported practice to independent assessment performance, at least as captured by midterm exams in this course.

6.2
Conversational reliance tracks difficulty and constitutes a risk pathway

Across analyses, students with lower self-efficacy and lower prior performance used
GPTutor
more, indicating that reliance is not uniform adoption. Importantly, the serial mediation model clarifies this pattern in a temporally consistent way: lower self-efficacy predicted lower Midterm 2 performance, and both lower self-efficacy and lower Midterm 2 performance uniquely predicted higher chatbot usage during the Midterm 2
\rightarrow
Midterm 3 interval. Chatbot usage, in turn, was negatively associated with Midterm 3 performance even after accounting for self-efficacy and prior performance.

Beyond usage frequency, our session-level behavior labels provide a more specific lens on what conversational reliance looks like in practice. Students who ever exhibited answer-seeking intent or escalation behaviors tended to score lower on Midterm 3, and in the full sample these associations remained significant when controlling for Midterm 2. At the same time, within chatbot users only, these differences were no longer statistically reliable after controlling for Midterm 2, consistent with selection effects among users. Taken together, conversational reliance appears to function as a risk signal: both frequent use and answer-oriented behaviors concentrate among students experiencing difficulty, and these observable patterns are associated with poorer subsequent exam performance.

These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that chatbot use causes lower scores. A plausible alternative is that students who are already struggling are both more likely to seek conversational support and more likely to perform worse. However, the persistence of associations in models that control for prior performance suggests that conversational traces can be useful for identifying students who may need additional instructional support or different scaffolds.

6.3
Interface constraints decouple reliance from performance risk

A central contribution of this study is the contrast between two LLM tutoring interfaces. Although lower self-efficacy predicted greater use of both tools, only chatbot use was associated with exam outcomes and led to a negative performance pathway. In contrast, proof-review usage appears primarily selection-based: students with lower self-efficacy were more likely to use proof-review, and lower self-efficacy also directly predicts lower exam performance. Once these individual differences are accounted for, proof-review usage is not associated with subsequent Midterm 3 performance. This pattern suggests that structured feedback anchored in students’ own proof attempts may absorb help-seeking demand without amplifying performance risk, whereas conversational support can remain more tightly coupled to difficulty and poorer subsequent performance.

This divergence highlights that simply using LLMs as a chatbot is not sufficient. For proof learning, interface and task constraints likely shape whether AI support preserves student generative processing. Proof-review requires students to first produce a proof attempt and then engages them with localized, structured feedback tied to their own work, which may reduce opportunities for short-circuiting. Conversational support, even when guided by a pedagogical prompt, can still invite repeated reliance under uncertainty. Designing LLM-based tutors for proofs may therefore require not only answer-withholding prompts, but additional scaffolds that regulate usage and promote consolidation, such as self-explanation prompts before receiving hints, reflection checkpoints after receiving feedback, or usage-aware interventions that redirect students to independent practice when usage is unusually high.

The contrast between these two interfaces can be understood through an analogy to traditional help-seeking contexts. Chatbot interactions resemble visiting office hours to ask a teaching assistant for help: the open-ended conversational format naturally invites requests for answers or step-by-step solutions, even when students are instructed to engage pedagogically. In contrast, the proof-review interface resembles submitting a draft for feedback: students must first produce their own work, and the system responds with targeted comments anchored to specific parts of their attempt. This work-anchored design constrains the interaction toward instructional feedback rather than direct answers. Our data support this framing: a substantial proportion of chatbot sessions exhibited answer-seeking intent, and students who engaged in answer-seeking or escalation behaviors performed worse on subsequent exams even after controlling for prior performance. These findings align with prior work on overreliance risks in conversational AI systems
[
31
,
21
]
and suggest that the affordances of chat-based interfaces may inherently promote answer-oriented help-seeking in ways that structured, work-anchored feedback does not.

Additionally, answer-seeking and escalation behaviors may serve as actionable triggers for adaptive scaffolding, such as prompting students to articulate a next-step justification before receiving further hints, or redirecting repeated escalations toward structured proof-review and independent practice.

One notable tension in our findings is that higher chatbot usage was negatively associated with exam performance at the individual level, yet the experimental group as a whole did not perform worse on midterms compared to the control group. One possible interpretation is that the negative association with chatbot reliance may have been offset by benefits from the proof-review component, which showed no independent negative association with outcomes. If so, future work comparing access to proof-review alone versus combined access could clarify whether work-anchored feedback yields more consistently positive effects when not bundled with conversational support.

6.4
Limitations and future work

Our access-level comparisons are descriptive and do not support causal claims. We do not measure (1) time-on-task between Midterm 2 and Midterm 3, (2) displacement/substitution (e.g., whether chatbot use replaced independent practice, office hours, or other AI tools apart from GPTutor), or (3) within-student change in self-efficacy, which was measured once and assumed stable; we cannot rule out reciprocal effects between usage and self-efficacy.

Future work should combine richer process measures (e.g., self-reported study diaries or instrumented writing environments) with interventions that redirect conversational reliance toward generative processing and evaluate transfer on independent assessments.
Our findings suggest that LLM integration in proof learning should move beyond generic chat interfaces toward designs that anchor feedback in student work and actively regulate reliance.
Such designs may better preserve generative processing while providing scalable support in math education.
