Dust dispersion and prevention in deep concave open-pit coal mines
Authors: Yifang Long, Ziling Song, Leng Wu, Yu Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address dust diffusion in deep concave open-pit coal mines, this study examined Fushun East open-pit coal mine in Liaoning Province, a deep concave open-pit coal mine. Employing theoretical analyses, field testing, numerical simulation, and experimental validation, we comprehensively investigated the mechanisms of dust generation, migration, and effective control strategies, revealing the mine dust migration patterns and providing practical foundations for dust control systems. Based on field measurements, road transportation was identified as the primary source of dust emissions. Atmospheric flow within the deep concave open-pit mine follows a complex recirculation pattern. Using COMSOL numerical simulation software, a fluid-solid coupling dust dispersion model was established based on the scale conditions of deep concave open-pit mines, revealing the formation mechanism of negative pressure dust accumulation zones induced by the inclination angle of the pit’s lateral slopes, while the pit’s shape illustrated dust migration trajectories. The air flow inside the pit was vortex-induced, primarily concentrated near the intake air areas of the pit. The study simulated air flow and dust particle trajectories in the flow field around mining vehicles, determining that the air disturbance range during vehicle movement extends to 1.5 times the vehicle’s volume. It is found that under low-speed driving conditions, dust dispersion behavior is mainly controlled by external wind speed and direction, with relative speed playing a dominant role in affecting the dust dispersion rate around mining vehicles. This in-depth study of dust sources, generation mechanisms, dispersion patterns, and precise control measures in deep concave open-pit coal mines has significant implications for achieving coordinated development between economic benefits and environmental protection.

Introduction

“Dust pollution is a pervasive challenge in surface mining, especially within deep open-pit coal mines beneath the surface closure zone. The extensive internal airflow area renders dust dispersion highly susceptible to local eddies and variations in atmospheric flow. As a result of the complex interplay between dust source attributes, particle properties, and site-specific conditions, dust diffusion is characterized by multi-point emissions, irregular dispersion patterns, and suboptimal control efficiency.”

Dust dispersion in open-pit coal mines involves multiple fields such as mining, environmental science, and engineering technology. Research on dust dispersion patterns in mining pits primarily employs numerical simulations and laboratory experiments to analyze how dust disperses under various meteorological conditions1. By revealing the influence of factors such as wind speed, wind direction, and atmospheric stability on dust dispersion in mining pits, improvements can be made to dust prevention measures under actual conditions2. Based on research into the properties of open-pit coal mine dust, dust suppression control technologies specific to these properties, as well as dust dispersion and migration3, dust mitigation and suppression measures of open-pit coal mines in foreign countries are highly targeted, including restricting blasting to suppress dust generation at the source, limiting dispersion by applying external conditions during the process to reduce dust spread.International dust control practices generally emphasize technical strategies that integrate source prevention and control with process management4. Taking the Anjialing open-pit mine as the research subject, Li Lin utilized drone and satellite monitoring technology, numerical simulations, and case validation to determine the dispersion patterns of particulate matter, such as PM10 and TSP, generated during various production stages in open-pit coal mining. Research by Zhou Gang et al. indicated that in an enclosed space, dust concentration gradually decreases in the downwind direction, and the stress experienced by dust particles could be derived from a gas-particle turbulence model5–9. Zhang Ruixin et al. used Fluent software to analyze dust dispersion patterns and validated their findings with field data. The results showed that the dust concentration was highest in the areas surrounding the excavation and loading shovels10, and by rationally adjusting dust suppression equipment according to the wind direction11, the dust concentration during operations was effectively reduced12–14.“Prior studies on dust dispersion have predominantly been conducted within confined environments, where dust accumulation is substantial and poses acute health risks. In these settings, phenomena like particle agglomeration and sedimentation are readily observable due to low external interference and high regularity, allowing for effective application of research outcomes. However, such results are often not transferable to open-pit coal mines due to the lack of research in expansive, open areas. While dust concentrations in open-pit mines may be lower, the massive scale of dust generation and wide dispersion range lead to significant environmental consequences15. Additionally, localized dust eddies present a notable hazard to personnel. Moreover, the failure to adequately account for objective on-site environmental factors in previous models hinders their ability to accurately reflect actual conditions16.”

Focusing on the dust dispersion in Fushun East open-pit mine, this paper employs theoretical analysis, field testing, numerical simulations, and experimental validation to comprehensively analyze dust generation, dispersion, and control measures and clarify the dust migration and dispersion patterns within the mine. On-site measurements reveal that while blasting operations generate the largest amount of dust instantaneously over a short duration, activities such as excavation, loading, and transportation produce smaller quantities of dust over extended periods. Dust from blasting operations has a high proportion of large-diameter particles that settle easily. The analysis concludes that road transportation is the primary source of dust. From the practical perspective of mine environmental management and precise protection, this paper provides accurate data for developing scientific strategies for dust mitigation and suppression.

Materials and methods

Dust sample collection and preparation

Fushun East open-pit coal mine is located between 123°77’27” and 123°79’14” east longitude, between 41°70’42” and 41°71’07” north latitude. The mine area coordinates are from E4400 to E10400 and S700 to N2000. Sampling points include the primary dust-generating locations within the deep concave open-pit coal mine, such as the blasting area, the excavation and loading area, and the vehicle transportation area. The test results can be used to set the basic parameters for the dust dispersion prediction model and to formulate appropriate dust control measures. The experimental equipment required for testing the basic characteristics of the dust includes: an AKFC-92 A mining dust sampler, polyvinyl chloride fiber filter membranes for dust collection, glass collection plates (φ27mm colorless quartz wafers), a balance scale, silicone oil, tweezers, and a drying oven. The sampling procedure is as follows: Install the sampler and correctly place the sampling medium (filter membrane) inside it. Turn on the sampler and collect dust according to the predetermined sampling time and flow rate. The sampling locations are shown in Fig. 2.

Field testing of mine dust properties

Based on the properties of the dust, tests were conducted on the dust generated from various workplaces around the Fushun East open-pit coal mine to obtain data on the dust’s dispersity and concentration in the air before dispersion, providing reference data for the subsequent simulation of the dust dispersion process and the formulation of dust control measures17.

Dust concentration test

The AKFC-92 A mining dust sampler was used to collect dust.

Multiple sampling campaigns were conducted across sites with varying dust concentrations to assess dust dispersibility. Accounting for ambient humidity, temperature, and wind speed, dust concentration, dispersibility, adhesion, wettability, and electrostatic charging were quantified, and comparative analyses were performed to identify the dominant dust-source types in deep open-pit mining areas. Sampling procedures and gravimetric determinations followed the Chinese occupational health standards GBZ 159 and the GBZ/T 192 series.

Dust adhesion test

Metal plates, glass plates, and plastic plates were selected to test dust adhesion. The surface of each test plate was thoroughly cleaned. Each test plate was weighed and numbered before the experiment. A powder spraying device was used to evenly coat the test plates with dust, controlling the pressure and distance of the spray. The dust-coated test plates were placed in a controlled environment with a room temperature of 27 °C and an indoor humidity of 47%. The plates were left undisturbed for 20 min to allow the dust to adhere stably, and the dust adhesion on the plates was observed. A precision electronic balance was used to weigh each plate again and record data, including the material of each test plate, the mass of the applied dust, and the change in mass before and after the experiment. Based on the experimental data, the adhesion rate of the dust on the test plates of different materials was calculated.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis of dust

A Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) was employed to observe the surface morphology of the prepared samples. The main instrumental parameters were set as follows: a magnification range of 2,000–100,000 times, an accelerating voltage of 30–200 keV, and detectors including a secondary electron detector, a backscattered electron detector, and an EDS energy spectrum detector.During the test, the powdered samples were directly adhered to conductive adhesive tape and mounted on the sample stage. The stage was then automatically transported into the microscope chamber, which was subsequently sealed and evacuated. Once the required vacuum level was reached, images of the sample morphology were captured.Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS, Bruker Nano GmbH, Germany) was utilized to analyze the surface elemental distribution of each sample. The experiments were conducted using combined SEM-EDS testing, with the accelerating voltage for EDS scanning set at 15 kV.

Particle size analysis of dust

The laser particle size analyzer used in this study was a Mastersizer 3000 (Malvern Panalytical, UK). The principle of the laser particle size analyzer involves measuring the intensity of scattered light at different angles. By substituting these measurements into an appropriate scattering theory, the particle size distribution of the sample can be calculated.

Dust wettability test

To macroscopically verify the aforementioned conclusions, a contact angle experiment was conducted. First, the dust samples were dried at 105 °C for 2 h. Using an HY-12 type tablet press, a continuous pressure of 20 MPa was applied for 5 min, after which the sample was removed, having been formed into a circular wafer. An SCI3000 Contact Angle Measuring Instrument was used to measure the contact angle. Each experiment was measured three times, and the average value was taken as the final result. A smaller contact angle indicates better dust wettability, while a larger contact angle indicates greater dust hydrophobicity.

Dust chargeability test

Place the filter membrane containing the collected dust into a drying oven and dry it at 105 °C for 2 h to remove any moisture. Use an electronic balance to accurately weigh the mass of the dried filter membrane and the dust it contains. Uniformly disperse the dust sample in an electrolyte solution to form a suspension. Proceed with the experiment by mounting the electrode cell containing the dust suspension onto a macroscopic electrophoresis apparatus. Set parameters such as the electric field strength and the duration of the experiment. Activate the electrophoresis apparatus, observe the movement of the dust particles within the electric field, and record the displacement distance of the particles at different time points. Measure parameters such as the electric field strength and the distance between the electrodes.

Dust chargeability refers to the electrical charge carried by dust particles, which typically manifests as either positive or negative polarity. Such charges are primarily generated through contact or triboelectric charging between dust particles and other substances, including air, liquids, or solid surfaces. The magnitude and polarity of the acquired charge are governed by several key factors, such as particle size, morphology, material composition, surface structure, humidity, and temperature.The presence of charge enhances the stability of dust particles in the atmosphere, hindering their sedimentation. This phenomenon occurs because electrostatic repulsion between similarly charged particles prevents agglomeration and subsequent settling. Furthermore, charged dust exhibits a stronger tendency to adhere to surfaces such as pipes, machinery, and monitoring equipment. This adhesion can disrupt sensor measurements and interfere with the operation of autonomous trucks. Consequently, the suspension time of dust is prolonged, which exacerbates visibility issues and poses greater risks to respiratory health.

Study on dust generation and dispersion patterns in deep concave open-pit coal mines based on COMSOL

COMSOL is a multi-physics modeling software suitable for creating complex numerical models, widely used in simulating mine dust dispersion. The entire simulation process is as follows: first, use the geometric modeling tools in COMSOL to construct a three-dimensional geometric model of the mine site, including elements such as the pit, ore stockpiles, and yards; second, the appropriate physics fields for mine dust dispersion simulation were selected, specifically covering both gas dynamics and particle tracing.

The analysis of dust dispersion patterns in the open-pit coal mine is conducted in two steps. First, the flow field within the computational domain is solved. After the flow field solution converges, the particle tracing physics field is added. We will start to analyze the velocity and pressure fields within the computational domain, then the dust particle trajectory and migration, finally the factors influencing dust dispersion and migration within the mine pit.

Analysis of factors influencing dust dispersion and migration within the mine pit

A deep concave open-pit coal mine is a complex spatial environment where the dust dispersion patterns and movement trajectories are not simple linear processes but are jointly influenced by numerous factors, such as wind speed, airflow direction, ambient temperature, and particle properties. Specifically, wind speed and wind direction are two crucial factors. The magnitude of the wind speed directly determines the aerodynamic forces acting on the dust particles, thereby affecting the range and velocity of dust dispersion. While the direction of the airflow determines the diffusion path of the dust particles, further defining their spatial distribution pattern. Therefore, this paper conducts a sensitivity analysis on wind speed and wind direction to clarify their impact on dust dispersion patterns and movement trajectories.

COMSOL Multiphysics 6.0 offers a robust set of modeling tools and extensive customization capabilities.The pit was modeled with dimensions of 3000 m (length) × 800 m (width) × 400 m (depth), incorporating realistic topographic features. The left and right slopes were assigned distinct angles of 30° and 45°, respectively. The airflow domain adjacent to the upper ground surface was defined as a rectangular region measuring 3800 × 1500 × 400 m.

Mesh generation parameters included a maximum cell size of 45.3 m, a minimum cell size of 4.9 m, a maximum cell growth rate of 1.1, a curvature factor of 0.4, and a narrow region resolution of 0.9. Furthermore, to account for the relatively regular geometry of the computational domain, angle refinement was applied to boundaries and corners, with a minimum boundary angle of 240° and a cell size scaling factor of 0.35. The entire domain was discretized using an unstructured tetrahedral mesh. To resolve flow field details and particle behavior near the walls, five layers of boundary cells were implemented.

(2) Development of the particle tracking mathematical model and boundary conditions.

After the flow field calculation was completed and converged, the particle transport physics was added. Particle release followed the Newtonian principle, with a specified mass flow rate of 0.6 kg/h and a maximum number of secondary particles set to 1000. The particles were assumed to consist of sandstone or quartz, with a density of 2800 kg/m³ and a uniform diameter of 100 μm. Based on charge measurements, the particles were assumed to be uncharged. The initial particle positions were determined using the density method, with a particle refinement factor of 1. The particle velocity was derived from the flow field results, following a cone-based velocity increment rule with 200 velocity space ions. The inlet boundary was set as a velocity inlet, coupled with the turbulent flow field. The outlet was defined as a pressure outlet with 0 Pa. The inner walls of the pit were set as rebound boundaries, while the air region above the pit was configured as both rebound and adhesion boundaries.

Analysis and results of on-site mine dust property testing

Analysis of dust concentration test results

Fig. 3Dust sampling records for blasting, loading and unloading, and transportation areas.

Dust sampling records for blasting, loading and unloading, and transportation areas.

As shown in Fig. 3, extremely high concentrations of dust are generated when conducting blasting operations because rocks, coal, etc. are instantly crushed, releasing a large number of fine particles, causing the dust concentration to rise sharply. While larger particles settle quickly, fine particles remain suspended in the air. Subsequent activities, such as secondary blasting or operational disturbances, can re-suspend the settled dust, showing intermittent peak values and significant fluctuations in concentration18. In mining and loading operations, the continuous excavation and handling of materials by mechanical equipment constitute a persistent dust emission source, maintaining a consistently elevated ambient dust concentration. Without effective dust suppression measures, concentrations can progressively increase, with fluctuations corresponding to operational intermittency. In transportation areas, dust is primarily generated from the friction between materials and equipment, as well as disturbances from vehicle movement on haulage roads. These activities generate a steady stream of dust, yielding a relatively stable concentration, which may increase in areas with heavy vehicle traffic or poor road conditions. Continuous haulage operations make it difficult to reduce the dust concentration quickly.

As shown in Table 1, the dust adhesion in different working areas is different. Dust from the blasting area exhibits weak adhesion due to its coarser particles, rough surface texture, and high kinetic energy, making it less likely to adhere and resulting in a short suspension time. Dust adhesion is moderate in the mining and loading area because the particles are smaller with smoother surfaces, and electrostatic effects from equipment may increase adhesion, but natural airflow in the open-pit environment tends to reduce its adhesion. Dust adhesion is strong in the transportation area.19 The particles are fine and readily adsorb onto vehicle surfaces, road signs, and into water vapor. Frequent vehicle and personnel activity, along with rough road surfaces, increases dust accumulation and contact area. Therefore, dust adhesion is strongest in the transportation area, thus we recommend optimizing road control technologies for deep concave open-pit coal mines specifically targeting these characteristics.

Calculation results of dust charge and charge to mass ratio.

Figure 7 and Table 2 shows the calculated results for dust charge and charge-to-mass ratio. Dust from the blasting area (samples 16–18, 22–24, 26–27) exhibits a coexistence of positive and negative charges because particles are charged by high-temperature, high-pressure gas flows and frictional collisions, with smaller particles carrying a larger charge, resulting in a non-uniform charge distribution. The charge amount can be reduced by controlling the blasting intensity and optimizing blasting methods to minimize dust generation and friction. In the loading and unloading area (samples 4–7, 10–12, 19–21), the dust also exhibits bipolar charging, which is principally induced by friction between machinery and the handled materials. Specifically, coal dust tends to acquire a negative charge through contact with metallic surfaces and a positive charge through friction with homologous particles. The overall charge magnitude is less than that observed in the blasting area, yet the charge-to-mass ratio is higher. Optimizing the materials of mechanical components and refining operational workflows can mitigate friction, thereby lowering the charge magnitude and enhancing the stability of the charge-to-mass ratio. Dust from the transportation area (samples 1–3, 7–9, 13–14, 17) predominantly carries a slight net negative charge due to friction with equipment and aerodynamic collisions. This dust possesses the lowest charge magnitude but the highest charge-to-mass ratio. Improvements to transport equipment and the implementation of anti-static measures can minimize friction, thus reducing the charge or rendering the dust particles effectively neutral.

Velocity field analysis and results

As shown in Fig. 8, where each observation direction along the airflow is divided into 5 cross-sections, the velocity field in the computational domain shows a clear stratified distribution. Specifically, the low-velocity fluid is mainly concentrated inside the mine pit, where the velocity is relatively low, reflecting the relatively static and stable environment within the pit, which corresponds to the actual conditions of a deep concave open-pit coal mine. In contrast, the high-velocity fluid is mainly concentrated in the airflow area above the pit, driven primarily by external wind, forming a distinct flow layer. A further comparison of Fig. 8(a) and (b) indicates that within the flow field above the pit, the fluid velocity is consistently maintained at approximately 0.1 m/s, signifying the dominant influence of the external wind. However, when the fluid enters the pit, its velocity decreases significantly to approximately 0.08 m/s, and the velocity interface between the high- and low-speed areas is nearly horizontal. As the observation cross-section moves further inward, as shown in (c) and (d), the fluid velocity at the bottom of the pit becomes non-uniformly distributed, and its value gradually decreases from approximately 0.04 m/s to 0.02 m/s. The comparative analysis also shows that the interface between the high- and low-speed areas gradually transforms from a horizontal shape to a transverse “S” shape, which is likely due to the influence of vortex flow. As the observation cross-section moves further forward (d), the interface gradually returns to a horizontal trend, and the velocity distribution at the interface becomes complex, showing an interface area with multiple velocity values compared to the inlet area20.

Simultaneously, perpendicular to the direction of vertical wind flow, the interface between high-speed and low-speed fluids gradually exhibits a bending tendency. Such rotational motion not only alters the flow position of the fluid but also exerts a significant influence on its flow direction. Based on specific on-site conditions of the deep concave open-pit coal mine, including topographical variations within the pit and fluctuations in the speed of transport vehicles, the aforementioned vortex flow may be induced by the combined effects of multiple factors.

Conclusion

(1) Dust emissions in deep concave open-pit mines are predominantly attributed to blasting and transportation operations. Specifically, blasting dictates the initial particle size distribution and dispersion range, whereas transportation dynamics modulate the subsequent extent of dust propagation. Experimental analysis reveals that dust within the transportation zone exhibits the strongest adhesive properties and wettability. In contrast, blasting-derived dust demonstrates relatively poor wettability, which may be attributed to the presence of hydrophobic substances. Characterization of electrostatic properties indicates that blasting-generated dust carries the highest charge, while transportation-related dust possesses the lowest. A quantitative assessment of dust contribution rates ranks the sources in the order: blasting > transportation > loading. Although blasting serves as an instantaneous source with a confined diffusion range, its environmental impact is substantial. However, due to its transient nature, blasting-induced dust disperses over a smaller spatial scale and for a shorter duration compared to road dust, which tends to pervade the entire pit. Consequently, this study focuses on the dust generation mechanism during truck transportation, with specific emphasis on secondary dust emission and sustained diffusion processes.

(2) Dust migration within the deep-pit open-pit mine is accompanied by vortex formation. The airflow in the upper air domain is relatively gentle, with vortices concentrated in the air inlet area, where dust particles predominantly move in a clockwise rotational pattern. This finding lays a foundation for investigating the mechanisms of dust deposition and diffusion.Variations in wind speed along the long-axis direction affect dust accumulation in the distal region. With increasing wind speed, dust retention on the windward slope increases, vortex stability decreases, and the vortex core evolves from a single-core to a multi-core structure, accompanied by the downward shift of dust trajectories toward the pit bottom. This indicates that dust suppression measures on the windward slope and at the pit bottom should be strengthened under high wind speed conditions, providing a basis for formulating wind-speed-adaptive dust control strategies.Under the influence of natural wind along the short-axis direction, dust movement in low-slope areas is dominated by near-surface transport, where vortex generation is dependent on relatively high wind speeds. In contrast, vortices in high-slope areas concentrate around the pit center, and dust particles tend to form spiral trajectories climbing toward the slope surface. Accordingly, it is recommended that near-surface ventilation and dust suppression facilities be optimized in low-slope areas, while dust control at the pit center and measures to mitigate dust climbing be enhanced in high-slope areas.

(3) Dust diffusion velocity exhibits a positive correlation with truck driving speed. Under low wind speed conditions, the dust cloud forms a stepped distribution pattern and settles rapidly. While increased wind velocity shortens the duration required to reach diffusion equilibrium, it simultaneously expands the diffusion range and decelerates the sedimentation rate. Particle size is inversely related to diffusion velocity; smaller particles disperse more extensively. Temporal variations in dust concentration follow a similar trend across different scenarios, with localized concentrations remaining relatively uniform. An elevated dust generation rate primarily elevates local concentrations but exerts a negligible influence on the overall diffusion velocity and range. Notably, the concentration escalation near the source is more pronounced than in the wake of the truck. Consequently, dust control strategies should prioritize preventive measures over remedial actions.
