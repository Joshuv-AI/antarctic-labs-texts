Forest islands sustain more temporally stable insect metacommunities in a heterogeneous tropical mountaintop landscape
Authors: Pedro Giovâni da Silva, Flávio Camarota, Marina do Vale Beirão, Flávio Siqueira de Castro, Geraldo Wilson Fernandes, Frederico Neves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe evaluated how habitat type influences community temporal dynamics in a heterogeneous tropical mountain landscape. We sampled ants, dung beetles, and fruit-feeding butterflies across two mountaintop habitats: campo rupestre and forest islands in the Southern Espinhaço Range, Brazil. We quantified temporal dynamics by assessing variations in species richness across sampling years and in species composition, focusing on species gains and losses. Species richness did not differ between environments and most species were unique to either campo rupestre or forest islands. We found no consistent pattern of species gains or losses among insect communities or habitat types, with such temporal dynamics being more prevalent for butterflies in the campo rupestre. In the campo rupestre, recent periods were dominated by colonization-driven homogenization following widespread species losses. In forest islands, extirpations prevailed for ants, colonizations for butterflies, and processes were balanced for dung beetles. The campo rupestre showed greater temporal dynamics in species richness and composition, particularly for butterflies and dung beetles, resulting in more frequent species gains. Habitat type shapes the temporal dynamics of insect communities in heterogeneous tropical mountaintop landscapes. Forest islands sustain more temporally stable insect metacommunities, while open environments and more mobile organisms have greater temporal dynamics.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Zoology

Understanding how biological communities change over time has become a paramount task as the Earth advances in the Anthropocene1,2. While broad-scale evidence indicates widespread changes in species composition without a consistent trend of species loss1,3,4, these changes are spatially structured, with numerous locations either gaining or losing species4,5. Therefore, ecologists increasingly need to monitor, analyze and predict temporal dynamics in the structure of biological communities concerning ongoing shifts in climate, land use, and other abiotic and biotic drivers6,7. This task is critical in mountainous systems where the upper-adapted species inhabiting there have nowhere to go.

Mountaintops are environmentally and biologically heterogeneous, housing a high biodiversity and providing vital ecosystem services, such as water supply, hydroelectric power generation, ecotourism, medicinal plant resources, native forage production, and belowground carbon storage8,9. They are the upper distributional limit for many species and are home to numerous endemic species, making them crucial reservoirs of unique ecological and evolutionary processes9–12. Research in mountainous regions often focuses on species distribution along elevational gradients7,13,14, while accessing diversity patterns among different environments present on mountaintops is less common15,16. In heterogeneous mountainous landscapes, species can exhibit varying levels of habitat specialization17, with a few effectively utilizing all environments. For instance, forest-dependent and open habitat-tolerant species may respond differently to environmental changes in mountain landscapes18,19, as predicted by the Habitat Templet Hypothesis20,21. This hypothesis suggests that environmental conditions associated with each habitat type act as selective pressures, shaping the traits and behaviors of organisms20,21. Therefore, one might expect biological communities inhabiting different environments at the same elevation belt to exhibit contrasting temporal patterns due to differences in microclimatic conditions and resource availability. Understanding whether such changes are mediated differently by the type of environment and whether they are leading to processes of biotic homogenization (decreasing dissimilarity over time) or differentiation (increasing dissimilarity over time) is an urgent need to establish appropriate conservation strategies4,22–24. Tropical mountains act as cradles (speciation spots) and museums (persistence spots) of biodiversity11, facing many threats ranging from climate change to direct habitat degradation8,25.

Forest and open environments provide different microclimatic conditions to organisms26, with forest interiors attenuating extreme temperatures and maintaining humidity compared to open environments27. This is particularly relevant in mountainous ecosystems, where climate variables, especially temperature, can be limiting factors28,29. In addition, these two types of environments have distinct structural complexity (i.e., vegetation layering, spatial heterogeneity, and microhabitat diversity)30, which provides different shelter and food resources to their species. Soil features, such as depth, texture, and granulometric structure, are frequently different between forest and open environments in mountainous landscapes31. Savannah and rupestrian grasslands usually occupy shallow soils with low nutritional status32–34, whereas forests grow in areas with deeper, eutrophic soils, such as watercourses35. Forests can then be limited by these conditions and can form natural archipelagos immersed in a grassland matrix35,36. While contrasting environmental conditions are expected to shape species traits and their behavioral responses, as suggested by the Habitat Templet Hypothesis20,21, it remains unclear whether such differences extend their influence on the temporal dynamics of organisms. Therefore, mountaintops provide an ideal setting to investigate the effect of landscape heterogeneity on metacommunity dynamics.

Many terrestrial ecosystems are facing insect species losses, specially of sensitive groups, such as rare or highly specialized species37,38. The diversity–stability hypothesis suggests that the ongoing loss of biodiversity can weaken the ecosystem’s capacity to buffer the impacts of environmental disturbances on its functioning39–42. This is particularly concerning as insects are the most abundant and diverse organisms on Earth and provide essential ecosystem services, such as nutrient cycling and biological control43,44. Higher elevations are characterized by decreased insect richness of many groups14,29,45. Besides, some lowland insect species are expanding their ranges to higher elevations due to climate change46–48, which can cause new biological interactions with probable negative effects on sensitive groups. Furthermore, distinct biological groups may differ in natural history traits and dispersal ability7,49,50. Therefore, evaluating the dynamics of mountaintop communities is a first step to unravel how biodiversity loss or change over time could affect the ability of ecosystems to maintain themselves.

Here we evaluated the temporal dynamics (2013–2020) of ants, fruit-feeding butterflies, and dung beetles occurring in two habitats in a tropical heterogenous mountaintop system: rupestrian grasslands (campo rupestre) and forest islands. Ants, fruit-feeding butterflies, and dung beetles are widely recognized as bioindicators51–53, which encompass a range of traits (e.g., body mass, trophic guild, type of locomotion, and evolutionary history) that influence both dispersal and foraging activity54. We hypothesize that temporal dynamics in insect communities are environment-specific, reflecting distinct selective pressures imposed by open (campo rupestre) versus forested (forest island) environments. We expect greater variation in community dynamics (richness variation, compositional change, species gains and losses) in the campo rupestre when compared to forest islands, because open environments are characterized by harsher abiotic conditions (e.g., higher temperature, solar radiation) than forest islands27,55. Low-vagility organisms, such as ants and dung beetles, are expected to experience biotic differentiation over time because they are more restricted to their original habitats. In contrast, highly vagile organisms, such as butterflies, are more likely to experience biotic homogenization, where widespread and generalist species increasingly dominate communities over time49,50.

Results

Diversity patterns

We sampled 326 insect species, including 189 ants (123 in campo rupestre, 104 in forest islands), 81 dung beetles (51 in campo rupestre, 50 in forest islands), and 56 fruit-feeding butterflies (29 in campo rupestre, 41 in forest islands). Over the years, ant species ranged between 62–72 in campo rupestre (average ± sd: 67.5 ± 5.3) and 53–72 in forest islands (62.3 ± 7.9), dung beetle species ranged between 27–33 in campo rupestre sites (30.3 ± 2.8) and 26–35 in forest islands (29.8 ± 3.9), while fruit-feeding butterfly species varied between 7–19 in campo rupestre sites (11.8 ± 5.1) and 20–25 in forest islands (21.8 ± 2.4) (Table S1). We found 38 species of ants shared between campo rupestre and forest islands, 85 species unique to campo rupestre, and 66 species unique to forest islands (Fig. S2). A total of 20 species of dung beetles were shared between campo rupestre and forest islands, 31 species unique to campo rupestre and 30 unique to forest islands (Fig. S2). For butterflies, 14 species were shared between both habitat types, 15 species unique to campo rupestre and 27 species unique to forest islands (Fig. S2). The rarefaction and extrapolation curves revealed that species richness of all insect groups was similar in both habitats for extrapolated values at the same sample coverage (Fig. 1; see also Table S2).

Temporal β-diversity

We found compositional changes between pairwise combinations of sampling years in both habitats for ants, dung beetles, and fruit-feeding butterflies (Fig. 2). In the campo rupestre, ants had detectable compositional changes only between 2017–2019 (the last two sampling periods), mainly dominated by species gains. In forest islands, ants had detectable compositional changes between 2014–2015 and 2015–2018, initially dominated by gain and then loss of species (Fig. 2, upper panels). Dung beetles had perceptible compositional changes only between 2014 and 2017 in the campo rupestre, dominated by species losses. However, in forest islands, dung beetles showed no perceptible compositional change (Fig. 2, central panels). Fruit-feeding butterflies had detectable compositional changes between all pairwise combinations of sampling years in the campo rupestre. Between 2013–2014 and 2017–2019, butterflies showed increased gains of species, while between 2014–2017, we found considerable species losses in almost all campo rupestre sites. In forest islands, fruit-feeding butterflies had detectable compositional changes only between 2018 and 2020, represented mainly through species gains in nearly all forest islands (Fig. 2, lower panels).

The partitioning of β-diversity into dynamic components revealed that both colonizations and extirpations played essential roles in insect compositional changes between the survey years in both habitats (Fig. 3). In the campo rupestre, colonization-resultant homogenization of more frequent ant species has been the dominant process in the last years of sampling (2017–2019). In forest islands, however, there was a greater gain of both more frequent and rare species in the first years of sampling, followed by a considerable loss of both groups. In the last samplings, the four processes balanced each other, with a small dominance of colonization processes similar to the previous period (Fig. 3, upper panels). Dung beetles from the campo rupestre had perceptible changes only between 2014 and 2017, represented by losses of rare species, leading to homogenizing effects. However, colonization and extirpation components balanced each other in the first two and last sampling periods, with a slightly greater contribution from colonization of more frequent species. Both colonization and extirpation processes in forest islands, leading to either biotic homogenization or differentiation, balanced each other over time. This explains the lack of perceptible compositional changes of dung beetles inhabiting the forest islands (Fig. 3, central panels). For fruit-feeding butterflies inhabiting the campo rupestre, increased species gains found between 2013–2014 and 2017–2019 were represented by more frequent species, leading to homogenizing effects. Between 2014 and 2017, the prominent species losses occurred for both more frequent and rare species, with a slightly greater contribution from the first group. In forest islands, butterflies had more species between 2018 and 2020, represented mainly by colonization-result homogenizing effects. Both extirpations of either more or less frequent species decreased greatly from the first to the last samplings (Fig. 3, lower panels).

Effect of habitat type on species richness and temporal dynamics in species composition

The effect of habitat varied among metrics and insect groups, with campo rupestre generally presenting more temporally dynamic insect communities than forest islands. We observed a greater variation in species richness of dung beetles and fruit-feeding butterflies in the campo rupestre but no differences between habitats for ants (Table 1, Fig. 4). We also found a greater temporal dynamics in species composition (TBI) and higher species gains in the campo rupestre for ants and fruit-feeding butterflies, but no differences between habitats for dung beetles (Table 1, Fig. 4). Finally, we did not observe differences between habitats for species losses for the three insect groups (Table 1, Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this study, we provided insights into the dynamics of communities of ants, dung beetles, and fruit-feeding butterflies by exploring the direct influence of habitat type (campo rupestre and forest islands) on these communities’ temporal dynamics. Whereas extrapolated species richness was similar between the two habitats, most species were unique to either campo rupestre or forest islands, with a smaller proportion of shared species, reflecting the accentuated differences between open and forested habitats in the tropical heterogenous mountaintop landscape. These species are either rare or among the most abundant (dung beetles and fruit-feeding butterflies) or of high occurrence (ants). We found compositional changes over time for all insect groups in both campo rupestre and forest islands (except for dung beetles in forest islands), with gains and losses contributing to these patterns. Critically, we detected notable differences between habitat types, and the campo rupestre communities were more temporarily dynamic than the forest ones. Indeed, there was a higher variation in species richness (dung beetles and fruit-feeding butterflies), compositional change, and species gains over time (ants and fruit-feeding butterflies) in campo rupestre than in forest islands. We also detected a particular importance of common (widespread) species, leading to more biotic homogenization than differentiation trends in the last years of sampling in the campo rupestre. Overall, our study gives critical insights into the role of environmental differences and organism dispersal capacity in shaping the temporal dynamics of biological communities in the highly diverse, heterogeneous, and threatened tropical mountaintops.

Probably due to microclimatic, environmental, and resource-related differences33,35, we found that the campo rupestre and forest islands had more unique than shared insect species. However, we found no differences in extrapolated species richness of all insect groups between habitats at comparable sample coverage56. This indicates that both habitats contribute similarly to the gamma diversity of the mountaintop insect metacommunity, highlighting the potential of these species to be used as bioindicators of further environmental changes in mountain environments57.

According to the Habitat Templet Hypothesis20,21, the physical and biological conditions of an environment act as a selective force that influences the traits and behaviors of organisms, leading to the development of specific adaptations suited to those conditions. In campo rupestre, harsh abiotic conditions (temperature extremes, high radiation, wind, and water limitation) may favor traits linked to stress tolerance, such as small or flattened bodies, thicker cuticles to reduce desiccation, thermal tolerance, and behaviors like nocturnal activity58–61. Life cycles are often synchronized with short favorable periods, and dispersal ability may be high to cope with patchy resources62. In forest islands, more stable and humid conditions favor adaptations related to structural complexity63 and biotic interactions64, including larger body size65, specialization on host plants66 or litter resources, longer activity periods, and reduced dispersal67. Therefore, it is not surprising that both environments have more species associated with each other than shared, even though a portion of the insect communities use both environments. Differences in thermal tolerance among fruit-feeding butterflies have been observed in a study in the campo rupestre and the forest islands16. In the campo rupestre, all butterfly species showed similar thermal tolerance due to the homogeneity of environmental conditions. In contrast, forest islands exhibited two distinct butterfly assemblages—thermally resistant and non-resistant—likely due to the environmental heterogeneity created by the vertical structure of forest islands, such as variations in canopy openness and temperature differences between the canopy and understory16.

Furthermore, the sink habitat may be more temporally dynamic for habitat-generalist species inhabiting the mountaintop landscape since more adequate conditions and resources can be found during different seasons in the campo rupestre and forest islands18. For instance, many habitat-generalist species of dung beetles that are very abundant in the campo rupestre during the summer can be more abundant in the forest islands than in the campo rupestre in the winter, where these forest islands serve as temporary refuges18. In addition, both environments may have contrasting responses to anthropogenic pressures33,35,68. Therefore, conserving both environments is essential for maintaining the unique insect biodiversity and their associated ecosystem functions.

We found compositional changes between pairwise periods for all insect groups in both campo rupestre and forest islands (except dung beetles in forest islands), with either gains or losses dominating the patterns. Significant compositional changes between pairwise sampling periods, higher variation in species richness (dung beetles and fruit-feeding butterflies), compositional change, and species gains over time (ants and fruit-feeding butterflies) occurred more in the campo rupestre than in forest islands, as we expected. Both colonization and extirpation played important roles in insect compositional changes over time in both environments. In the campo rupestre, colonization-resultant biotic homogenization of more frequent species was the dominant pattern in the last sampling periods, preceded by a high extirpation of both common and less frequent species of all insect groups in the middle of the sampling period. This weak homogenizing trend led by widespread species is a recurring global pattern in studies with longer durations and larger spatial scales4. In forest islands, we observed contrasting patterns: extirpations predominate over colonizations for ants with an opposite pattern for fruit-feeding butterflies, leading to homogenizing effects; the processes remained balanced for dung beetles. These results are associated with the greater harshness found in the campo rupestre. Open environments are characterized by harsher abiotic conditions when compared to forest islands, such as higher temperature, solar radiation, wind speed, and fire occurrency27,55,68,69. Therefore, forest environments are capable of buffering some harsh conditions69,70. Indeed, various ecological drivers can influence the biotic homogenization or differentiation of communities across spatial and temporal scales, including some critical environmental disturbances, such as fire24. Nevertheless, the precise role of such disturbances in shaping the processes of community homogenization and differentiation in ecosystems such as campo rupestre and forest islands remains to be determined.

Fruit-feeding butterflies exhibited significant changes between all pairwise sampling periods in the campo rupestre, coupled with the higher average variation in species richness, temporal beta-diversity index (TBI), and species gains. However, contrary to our expectations, the colonization of common species over the years leading to a trend of biotic homogenization in the last sampling periods was the predominant pattern among all insect groups in the campo rupestre. This pattern was consistent in both environments for fruit-feeding butterflies only. Fruit-feeding butterflies are vagile organisms that easily move between patches, and they may take advantage of that to improve food resource acquisition and seek better habitat conditions71. On the other hand, most species of ants and dung beetles are expected to be less vagile than butterflies due to their morphological traits and behavior72–75. Many ant species (mostly ground-dwelling ones) construct large and deep nests, which provide considerable protection against abiotic conditions, including fires68,76,77. Local adaptation and plastic response to temperature78 can also shape the ant nest architecture. Dung beetles use mammal feces, carrion, and decaying plant matter to build their nests in the soil; a small group of species live within the food source79. Both situations provide protection against abiotic conditions for dung beetles. Some authors sampled dung beetles before and after fire events in the campo rupestre, and they found that dung beetle community metrics and composition are not affected by fire occurrence, time since the last fire, and fire frequency80. Therefore, fruit-feeding butterflies can experience harsher conditions (or “less protection”) than ants and dung beetles in the campo rupestre, since they depend on vegetation structure for appropriate nesting and feeding sites81. These conditions include microclimate (e.g., temperature, wind, and rain) and the presence of host plants for caterpillars and food resources for adults53,82.

Differences in life-history traits among the insect groups could help us to understand the role of dispersal ability in shaping metacommunities over time7,49,50,83. We acknowledge that we did not directly measure the dispersal of organisms and that we used the known distinct vagility of the insect groups52,71,73,74 as a proxy for interpreting our results. However, this approach has been used robustly and satisfactorily in other studies15,49,50, providing promising insights in the absence of direct measures. Future efforts are needed to verify the role of specific morphological and behavioral traits to refine the understanding of such response patterns.

Some observed variation in insect temporal dynamics is probably driven by a combination of phenological shifts and demographic processes operating across multiple temporal scales. Species-specific phenology, including the timing of emergence, reproduction, and activity, can respond sensitively to short-term (annual) fluctuations in environmental conditions such as temperature, precipitation, and resource availability, leading to marked temporal dynamics in activity and community composition7,84. At the same time, demographic dynamics, such as recruitment, survival, and local extirpation, may be shaped by longer-term (interannual) environmental variability, which can alter population trajectories and influence community persistence over time. Interannual variation in climate regimes may affect habitat quality, trophic interactions, and disturbance frequency, thereby modulating population growth rates and turnover7,84. Together, these processes can generate complex temporal patterns in insect communities, with observed dynamics reflecting the interplay between phenological responses to immediate environmental cues and demographic adjustments to longer-term environmental variability7.

This research is part of an ongoing long-term ecological project (PELD Campos Rupestres da Serra do Cipó), and we acknowledge that the number of sampling periods is still limited6. However, the results demonstrate insightful patterns that can be improved as the surveys continue. Notably, our study is one of the few that have investigated species exchange trends within a heterogeneous tropical mountaintop landscape, revealing the complex temporal patterns and outcomes. Despite certain limitations, such as the potential negative effects on species populations due to individuals’ removal, we assert that our samplings seem robust enough to picture the temporal dynamics of these insect metacommunities. Except for the first two surveys, which were conducted in consecutive years, sampling events were spaced at 2–3-year intervals. These longer intervals likely reduced potential negative impacts of repeated sampling and allowed sufficient time for insect populations to recover and re-establish, especially for rare species. This highlights the relevance of long-term monitoring studies for a deeper understanding of temporal trends of biodiversity84,85.

The heterogeneous mountaintop landscape in the Espinhaço range faces many threats9,33,35,68. As with any other mountaintop worldwide, climate and land use changes are among the more pervasive threats86,87. Long-term human activities in the campo rupestre include extensive fire, mining, livestock production, uncontrolled urbanization, and tourism expansion8,9,33. Moreover, the campo rupestre has been historically overlooked in public policies despite its high diversity and key associated ecosystem services8,9. Being immersed in the campo rupestre, the forest islands may experience even worse effects from the increase in changes resulting from human activities9,68.

In this study, we present findings from comprehensive field samplings conducted over multiple years, focusing on ants, dung beetles, and fruit-feeding butterflies within a critically endangered and highly biodiverse mountaintop landscape. These insect groups exhibited a complex temporal dynamics in species richness and composition, with notable differences between habitat types. More temporally dynamic metacommunities were found in the open habitat (campo rupestre) than in forest islands. Also, fruit-feeding butterflies—the most vagile insect group—proved to be more temporal dynamics than ants and dung beetles. We also observed the importance of common (widespread) species leading to more biotic homogenization than differentiation processes in the last years of sampling in the campo rupestre. Our results align with previous studies on temporal variation in species composition, which observed strong fluctuations but showed either no consistent trend towards species loss over time or a slight tendency towards biotic homogenization led by the spread of common species4,24, predominantly in the campo rupestre. This comprehensive approach, involving multiple sampling periods and diverse insect groups, enhances our understanding of the intricate dynamics shaping tropical metacommunities in response to environmental conditions and global changes on mountaintops.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted in the southern portion of the Espinhaço mountain range, specifically within Serra do Cipó, located in the state of Minas Gerais, southeastern Brazil (19°14’19” S, 43°31’35” W; Fig. S1). The Espinhaço range spans around 1200 km north-to-south and is distinguished by its remarkable biodiversity and high endemism8,33. The southern Espinhaço acts as a biogeographical barrier that delineates the Cerrado (savannah) biome to the west from the Atlantic Forest biome to the east. Above 900 m, the landscape primarily comprises two habitat types: the continuous matrix of campo rupestre and scattered natural relict forest islands (known as capões de mata)35. These two habitats display contrasting levels of productivity and distinct plant ecological strategies88. The campo rupestre is classified as an old, climatically-buffered, infertile landscape (OCBIL)89, characterized by open grassy areas with regular burning and nutrient-poor soils31,34. Key plant families include Velloziaceae (Vellozia, Barbacenia), Eriocaulaceae (Paepalanthus, Syngonanthus), Xyridaceae (Xyris), Melastomataceae (Microlicia, Lavoisiera), Asteraceae (Lychnophora, Vernonia), Bromeliaceae (Dyckia, Encholirium), Orchidaceae (Habenaria, Epidendrum), Fabaceae (Chamaecrista, Mimosa), and Poaceae (Andropogon, Axonopus)28,33. Campo rupestre vegetation is predominantly low-statured, with most herbs and subshrubs ranging from 5–50 cm, and shrubs typically reach 0.5–2 m in height28,33. In contrast, the forest islands are productive forest patches on mountaintops, mostly found above 1000 m, generally associated with headwaters and small streams, and a floristic composition like the semi-deciduous forests of the Atlantic Forest domain35. The most frequent tree species in these forest islands are Protium spruceanum (Burseraceae), Geonoma schottiana (Arecaceae), Tibouchina sellowiana (Melastomataceae), Casearia decandra (Salicaceae), and Guatteria australis (Annonaceae). The richest tree families are Myrtaceae, Lauraceae, Melastomataceae, Fabaceae, Asteraceae, and Rubiaceae. The canopy varies between 12–20 m, with emergent individuals reaching 25–30 m in the central part of the forest islands, while at the edge, the average height varies between 6–8 m35,36. Forest islands ranged between 1.2–35.8 ha, with an average of 7.7 ± 9.3 ha (mean ± sd). The region is characterized by an average annual precipitation ranging from 1300 to 1600 mm, with most rainfall occurring from October to March.

Sampling design

The study was conducted between 2013 and 2020 across two habitats located above 1000 m in elevation. A total of 26 sites were sampled, encompassing an area of 66 km², including 11 forest islands and 15 campo rupestre sites, each separated by at least 250 m (Fig. S1). This minimum distance of 50–100 m has been acknowledged to ensure spatial independence between consecutive samples for the insect groups sampled73,90–92. Sampling occurred in both habitats during the rainy season (October to March). In the campo rupestre sites, sampling was performed in 2013, 2014, 2017, and 2019 (last weeks of November or first weeks of December), while in the forest islands, it occurred in 2014, 2015, 2018, and 2020 (last weeks of January or first weeks of February), during 7–10 days in each environment. Sampling was conducted during periods that avoided intense rainfall and coincided with peak insect activity, ensuring optimal sampling efficiency and representativeness93.

Ant sampling utilized non-baited pitfall traps consisting of plastic containers (9 cm deep × 15 cm in diameter) filled with 250 ml of a 5% salt and detergent solution and protected from rain19,94. Dung beetles were sampled using similar traps, with the addition of 25 g of human feces as bait in a wire-suspended smaller container (4 cm deep × 5 cm in diameter)18,95. Each pitfall trap (for ants and dung beetles) was deployed in the field for 48 h during each sampling event. Fruit-feeding butterfly sampling employed Van Someren-Rydon traps baited with a mixture of fermented banana and sugar cane juice, with each trap left in the field for five days and checked daily71.

In the campo rupestre sites, transects were spaced 250 m apart. Five pitfall traps were placed along each transect for ant sampling, with 50 m between them. Dung beetle traps were spaced 100 m apart, with three traps per transect73,96. Butterfly traps were spaced 50 m apart, with four traps per transect. Within the central region of each forest island, a 20 × 50-m rectangular plot was established, where traps for each taxon were placed at all four corners, with an additional ant trap positioned in the center15,50. Consequently, transects served as sampling units for campo rupestre sites, while each 20 × 50-m plot was used as the sampling unit for forest islands. Differences in trap arrangement between campo rupestre and forest islands reflect the spatial characteristics of each habitat, since the limited size of forest islands makes the establishment of 250 m transects unfeasible.

Following each sampling, we preserved all ant and dung beetle samples in 70% alcohol. Butterflies were stored in individual paper envelopes. Individuals were identified to the most precise taxonomic level achievable using identification guides97–100 and through consultation with taxonomists (see Acknowledgements). The sampled material was deposited in the Centro de Coleções Taxonômicas at the Instituto de Ciências Biológicas at the Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais, in the Zoological Collection of the Universidade Federal do Paraná (some ants), in the Universidade Estadual de Campinas (some butterflies), and the Universidade Federal de Mato Grosso (some dung beetles). All necessary sampling permits were issued by Instituto Chico Mendes de Conservação da Biodiversidade (SISBIO #74826-1).

Data analysis

We carried out the analyses using the R analytical environment v. 4.3.3101 and specific packages, as described below. First, we generated rarefaction and extrapolation curves following the iNEXT four-steps approach56 for ants, dung beetles, and fruit-feeding butterflies per habitat (campo rupestre vs. forest islands). This analysis allowed us to evaluate diversity profiles and sample completeness per habitat and insect group, which were calculated with 100 bootstrap replications using the “iNEXT.4steps” package102. Data of all groups were assessed using incidence frequency, which is more adequate than abundance because of the aggregation effect56 caused by behavior (ant recruiting) or bait attraction. Extrapolation curves were constructed with the number of samples set at twice the actual reference size56.

The compositional dissimilarity of each insect group was measured per habitat between Time 1 and Time 2 (i.e., temporal β-diversity) according to the sequence of samplings (i.e., pairs of years, 2013–2014, 2014–2017, 2017–2019 for campo rupestre sites; 2014–2015, 2015–2018, 2018–2020 for forest islands) using the temporal beta-diversity index (TBI) approach103. We used the Sorensen dissimilarity for incidence data to calculate the pairwise dissimilarities. The TBI value was then decomposed into two components: B (species losses) and C (species gains)103. The mean of the differences between the B and C statistics was also computed across all sites per group. A positive value of [C – B] indicates that the habitat was dominated by species gains, whereas a negative value denotes overall species losses103. The [C – B] difference across all sites per habitat was tested for statistical differences using a permutational paired t-test (N = 999 permutations). The calculations were carried out using the “TBI” function of the “adespatial” package104. Violin plots were constructed using the “ggplot2” package105 to illustrate the values of TBI components per insect group and habitat.

We also partitioned the temporal dynamics in spatial β-diversity into dynamic components of colonization and extirpation to evaluate their influence on homogenization and differentiation processes for each insect group and habitat23,106. This approach is called Temporal change in β-diversity (ΔβTotal = β’ − β), where β and β’ are β-diversity at Time 1 and Time 2, respectively23. Differently from TBI’s approach, which partitions the colonization and extirpation processes within a single community over time, this method partitions the two processes in altering the spatial variation across multiple communities over time23,106. In our case, between pairwise sampling years per habitat (i.e., 2013–2014, 2014–2017, 2017–2019 for campo rupestre sites; 2014–2015, 2015–2018, 2018–2020 for forest islands). Taking into account the number of unique and shared species, temporal β-diversity was calculated for (1) species that were present in each sampling year, (2) species that went locally extinct from each site between two sampling years (i.e., extirpationβ-diversity), and (3) sets of species that newly colonized each site between two sampling years (i.e., colonization β-diversity)106. These combinations lead either to homogenization (colonization-resultant homogenization [ΔβC−] and extirpation-resultant homogenization [ΔβE−]) or differentiation (colonization-resultant differentiation [ΔβC+] and extirpation-resultant differentiation [ΔβE+]) of communities. We used the function “ecopart.multi” of the “ecopart” package107 for these calculations. Barplots were used to illustrate the Temporal change in β-diversity per insect group and habitat.

Generalized linear models (GLMs) were used to test for differences in the variation of species richness over time between habitat for each insect group. We used the Gaussian distribution with identity link for these models and reported pseudo R-squared using the function “pR2” of the “pscl” package108. The models were: variation of species richness of ants, dung beetles, or fruit-feeding butterflies [response variable] ~ Habitat [predictor variable]. We also used separate generalized linear mixed-effect models (GLMMs) to test for differences in TBI, species gains, and species losses between habitats for each insect group. The identity of sites was used as a random factor. We used the Gaussian distribution with identity link for the temporal β-diversity (TBI) and its components of gains and losses after residuals check. The models were: TBI or Gains or Losses [response variables] ~ Habitat [predictor variable] + (1|site identity) [random effect]. We reported marginal and conditional pseudo R-squared for these models109, which were calculated using the “r.squaredGLMM” function of the “MuMIn” package110. We used the “glm” and “lmer” functions of the “stats” and “lme4” packages, respectively111, for these models and checked the residuals using the “DHARMa” package112.
