Efficient Discovery of Potent AKR1B10 Inhibitors Using Co-folding Model Boltz‑2
Authors: Shinya Kawano, Akira Ikari, Satoshi Endo, Kentaro Tomii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although affinity prediction of protein–ligand binding remains an important challenge, cofolding models are expected to make virtual screening more effective for drug discovery and development. To verify the effectiveness of selecting a tractable number of candidates from a compound library using cofolding models, we strove to identify a novel inhibitory active compound using Boltz-2, a representative cofolding model, for aldo-keto reductase 1B10 (AKR1B10), which is highly expressed in various cancers. Consequently, of the 40 candidate compounds obtained after narrowing-down 867 candidates from a chemically diverse library based on prediction results by Boltz-2 and candidate selection with sufficient diversity, 70% (28 of 40 tested) compounds at IC50 < 10 μM were found to have inhibitory activity and to provide identification of multiple submicromolar inhibitors exhibiting novel scaffolds. Our results demonstrate that our sparse selection approach using Boltz-2 is helpful for enhancing AI-driven drug discovery. Moreover, the findings highlight its potential applicability for translating AI-generated predictions into experimentally actionable hits.

Introduction

The field of in silico virtual screening has undergone a major paradigm shift during the past decade, transitioning from classical physics-based docking approaches to deep learning-driven structure prediction and generation frameworks. , Conventionally used docking tools such as AutoDock Vina and Glide have long been centrally important for structure-based drug design. However, these methods often rely on simplified scoring functions and static receptor representations, consequently limiting their ability to capture induced-fit effects, long–range interactions, and the complex energetic landscape governing protein–ligand recognition. ,

Recent advances in “co-folding” models, as exemplified by AlphaFold 3, RoseTTAFold All-Atom (RFAA), and the Boltz-1/2 series, have expanded the scope of computational drug discovery considerably by enabling the joint modeling of protein–ligand­(−cofactor) complexes directly from sequence and SMILES representations. − These models employ invariant point attention and related architectures to generate three-dimensional atomic coordinates while accounting for backbone and side-chain flexibility. ,, In principle, such frameworks offer a unified solution to structure prediction and ligand pose generation. Among them, Boltz-2 can reportedly predict protein–ligand binding affinity approximately 1000 times faster than standard free-energy perturbation (FEP) calculations, while maintaining comparable accuracy to that provided by FEP, based on large amounts of data for the binding affinity.

Despite their impressive predictive capabilities, a crucially important limitation of those models has been reported: The good predictability and accuracy of the cofolding models are limited to cases that are similar to the trained data set, i.e., known protein–ligand interactions. Based on results of recent rigorous benchmarks, a salient concern is that those models might depend heavily on memorization of training data for protein–ligand interactions. This requirement can hamper effective applications for de novo drug design and can pose a major bottleneck in translating AI-generated predictions into experimentally actionable hits. However, according to a recent report, Boltz-2 demonstrated superior ability to distinguish true positives from false ones.

For this study, we set a biologically meaningful task as an example of the prediction target, Aldo-keto reductase 1B10 (AKR1B10), to confirm the effectiveness of Boltz-2 for prediction of binding affinity, especially for novel compounds. AKR1B10 is a cytosolic enzyme with a conserved (α/β)8-barrel fold. It is involved in the detoxification of reactive carbonyl species and lipid metabolism. It has emerged as a clinically relevant biomarker and therapeutic target in multiple malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma and non-small cell lung cancer. − Moreover, AKR1B10 has been demonstrated to play a crucially important role in cancer cell proliferation, migration, progression, and the development of anticancer drug resistance. − Consequently, this enzyme has attracted considerable attention as a promising therapeutic target for cancer treatment.

During recent decades, our laboratory has established a comprehensive chemical library of AKR1B10 inhibitors, identifying a wide spectrum of potent and selective agents. These include natural products such as unsaturated fatty acids, mangostins, terpenoids, curcuminoids, and steroids, as well as synthetic scaffolds including chromene derivatives ,, and caffeic acid derivatives. It is noteworthy that AKR1B10 inhibitors derived from natural products are not regarded as drug-like and that challenges persist in their clinical application. Novel anticancer drugs are also expected to increase the sensitivity of cancer cells to existing drugs and to overcome resistance to them.

Therefore, we attempted to identify an inhibitory active and ‘novel’ compound with a slightly modified approach of standard virtual screening using Boltz-2 for AKR1B10 as a proof of concept. For this study, we verified the effectiveness of selecting candidate compounds from the library using Boltz-2, with the aim of using it in accurate and detailed assays for a small number of compounds (a few dozen), mainly in the early to midstage of drug discovery. Specifically, of the 40 candidate compounds obtained by narrowing down candidates from a chemically diverse library of 50,240 compounds with thresholds for predictive values determined based on results of benchmarks for AKR1B10 and candidate selection with sufficient diversity, 70% (28 out of 40 tested compounds, derived from the 867 Boltz-2 candidates through score-based and diversity-based filtering) at IC50 < 10 μM and 17.5% (7 out of 40) at IC50 < 1 μM were shown to have inhibitory activity and to identify multiple inhibitors exhibiting novel scaffolds. Interaction between these 40 candidate compounds and the target protein was also monitored using thermal shift assay. This orthogonal assay further validates the prediction results provided by Boltz-2 and allows us to confirm that the selected compounds contain not only simple binders but also potent inhibitors for AKR1B10. Our results demonstrate that our sparse selection approach based on identified compounds with Boltz-2 can enhance hit enrichment considerably, mitigate the so-called "dry–wet" gap, and transform all-atom generative models into robust tools for complex enzymatic targets.

Results

Retrospective Benchmarking Using Boltz-2

To evaluate the ability of Boltz-2 to discriminate AKR1B10 inhibitors from nonbinders, we first performed a retrospective benchmark analysis. A data set was assembled consisting of 132 known inhibitors curated from ChEMBL and 6600 decoy molecules generated using the DUD-E protocol. , Among 132 inhibitors, only 43 compounds with pChEMBL values ≥7.0 against AKR1B10 (corresponding to ≤100 nM potency for IC50/K i) were regarded as actives, whereas compounds with pChEMBL values below this threshold were treated as negatives in the retrospective benchmark (Figure A). When multiple activity annotations were available for a given compound, the most potent reported value (maximum pChEMBL) was used for label assignments.

Distance-based constraints for targeting the catalytic residues Tyr49 and His111, along with the adjacent Trp112 which dictates pocket specificity, were derived from our extensive discovery data and were introduced to the screening calculations by Boltz-2 (Figure B). In addition, residues within 4.5 Å of the cocrystallized NADP+ were used to define the cofactor-binding pocket (Figure C). For internal validation of the constrained inference process, tolrestat (IC50 = 54 ± 4 nM) was evaluated as a reference compound. Across replicate Boltz-2 runs, tolrestat consistently yielded extremely high scores, confirming both the robustness of the model and the validity of the imposed catalytic constraints (Figure A). ,

Under these conditions, Boltz-2 demonstrated robust classification performance, achieving an overall ROC-AUC value of 0.84 (Figure B and Table ) in terms of binary classification of active compounds vs inactive ones. It is noteworthy that all 132 known inhibitors obtained from ChEMBL used as the active set can be fully included in the Boltz-2 affinity-head training data (ChEMBL_34, 2024–03 release; 132/132 compounds appear in ChEMBL with document_year ≤2023; Table S1). Accordingly, the retrospective ROC-AUC of 0.84 and EF1% of 8.7 should be regarded as a sanity check, not as evidence of generalization to unseen actives. This performance is comparable to, or exceeds, that of conventional physics-based docking approaches, which often struggle with AKR family enzymes because of the pronounced flexibility of the specificity pocket. , Consistent with the ROC analysis, Enrichment Factor evaluation showed effective prioritization of active compounds in the top-ranked fraction (EF1% = 8.7).

To define a stringent and nonarbitrary criterion for prospective screening, the affinity_probability_binary threshold t was scanned systematically from 1.00 to 0.00. Examination of the ROC-derived performance profile revealed a local optimum at t = 0.64 (Figure C). Such ROC-guided threshold selection strategies have been demonstrated as effective for balancing false-positive suppression and hit recovery in highly imbalanced virtual screening settings. − Therefore, we confirmed that the enrichment of true actives is expected when using affinity_probability_binary = 0.64 and affinity_pred_value = 0 as thresholds. Because the same data set was used both to tune t and to calculate the retrospective metrics, these values should be viewed as threshold-optimized estimates without held-out validation; an independent test of t = 0.64 is its prospective application to the Enamine Discovery Diversity Set described below.

It is noteworthy that the selected compounds exhibited low mutual similarity based on Morgan2/Tanimoto fingerprints, thereby indicating marked topological diversity, while displaying moderate similarity in terms of extended reduced graph (ErG) fingerprints (Figure S2). ErG fingerprints emphasize pharmacophoric feature arrangements rather than scaffold identity. Therefore, this pattern suggests that Boltz-2 did not converge on candidates based on simple 2D structural proximity. Instead, the model preferentially identified chemically diverse scaffolds that satisfied the spatial and functional interaction patterns required by the AKR1B10 active site, as described in 2.4 hereinafter.

Experimental Validation

Enzymatic assay and thermal shift assay (TSA) were conducted to ascertain which of the 40 identified compounds were true hits (active compounds). These analyses reveal that the predictions produced by Boltz-2 extend beyond mere binding compounds, encompassing compounds that exhibit inhibitory activity.

Enzymatic assays for the 40 compounds revealed an exceptionally high hit rate of 70% (28 out of 40 compounds) at a screening concentration of 10 μM, as defined by an inhibition rate of at least 50% (Figure A). It is noteworthy that this 70% value represents the hit rate within the 40 compounds derived from the 867 primary Boltz-2 candidates through score-based and diversity-based filtering, not an unbiased estimate of the full 867-compound pool. This rate markedly exceeds the 1–10% hit rates commonly reported for traditional structure-based virtual screening campaigns. Even at 1 μM, 7 of the 40 compounds (17.5%) retained measurable inhibitory activity. Several submicromolar inhibitors were identified, including AICE003 (AICE stands for AKR1B10 Inhibitor Candidate Enrichment), with an IC50 value of 197 ± 12 nM (Figures B,C, S3, and Table ).

The complexity of interpreting binding events for deep-learning-derived hits was highlighted further by the TSA results. Although several potent inhibitors such as AICE003, AICE001, and AICE032 induced clear protein stabilization, others exhibited decreased thermal stability (e.g., AICE023 and AICE024) or negligible ΔT m shifts (e.g., AICE036 and AICE038) (Figure S4). These discrepancies observed between the TSA profiles and enzymatic inhibition potencies are consistent with the findings of Kabir et al. on aldo-keto reductase family members. That study established that, although thermal shift assays (ΔT m) can track the thermodynamic stabilization of AKR1B10, they do not necessarily yield a strict, quantitative linear correlation with enzymatic IC50 values. This nonlinear relation stems from the distinct chemical scaffolds of the hits, which can induce diverse conformational stabilization profiles or affect the binding environment of the NADP+ cofactor differentially. , The primary strategic objective of incorporating TSA in this workflow was to obtain direct, orthogonal evidence verifying that the predicted ligands physically bind to the target protein, thereby confirming genuine target engagement and ruling out assay artifacts. Therefore, TSA was positioned as a key qualitative filter to substantiate physical interactions, whereas the definitive determination of functional inhibitory efficacy was driven rigorously by steady-state enzymatic IC50 evaluation.

Structural Novelty of Hit Compounds and Predicted Binding Modes

Similarity heatmap analyses (Figure ) demonstrated that the AICE compound series is topologically distinct from earlier reported inhibitors registered in ChEMBL. This observation was supported further by Tversky similarity analysis (Figures S5 and S6). , The low Morgan2-based Tanimoto coefficient values (T < 0.35) show that Boltz-2 achieved scaffold hopping successfully. , At the same time, moderate similarity in ErG fingerprint space suggests preservation of pharmacophoric arrangements that are important for target engagement. By integrating both topology-based and feature-based similarity metrics, we confirm that the protocol can identify structurally novel scaffolds and exclude compounds that would otherwise occupy the binding pocket in catalytically inert orientations. ,

Inspection of predicted binding modes for representative compounds AICE003, AICE023, and AICE036 (Figure A–C) confirmed preservation of hydrogen bonds with anionic site residues within the AKR1B10 active site. In each case, polar functional groups were positioned at distances that were compatible with hydride transfer relative to the nicotinamide ring of NADP+. The groups were stabilized by interactions with Tyr49 and His111. Consistent with the native structural features of the enzyme, the top-ranked Boltz-2 prediction models for AICE003, AICE023, and AICE036 showed that all these ligands leave the Trp112 side chain in its original, unflipped conformation, designated as the “B10-position”. In these models, the “B10-position” is highly stabilized by a specific Gln114-centered hydrogen-bond network (Figure D). By contrast, the reference crystal structure of the AKR1B10–tolrestat complex (PDB ID: 1ZUA) shows that the bulky tolrestat induces an atypical side-chain flip of Trp112 into the “B1-position”. This flipped state mimics the expanded pocket topology of the closely related AKR1B1 isoform and often compromises isoform selectivity. As the AICE series binds while preserving the native “B10-position” without triggering this cross-reactive induced-fit adaptation, it is thought that these potent compounds preferentially exploit the ground-state architecture unique to AKR1B10. This allows them to function as isoform-specific inhibitors. ,

To test this structural hypothesis by experimentation, AKR1B1 inhibition assays were performed on the eight AICE compounds (AICE001, 003, 017, 023, 024, 032, 036 and 038) with confirmed IC50 values under conditions matched to the AKR1B10 assay. The measured AKR1B1/AKR1B10 selectivity ratios were divided clearly according to the predicted Trp112 conformation by Boltz-2: The six B10-conformation compounds yielded a geometric mean selectivity ratio of 6.47 (range 1.39–231) in favor of AKR1B10, whereas the two B1-conformation compounds yielded a geometric mean ratio of 0.62 (range 0.48–0.80) in favor of AKR1B1. The two conformational groups therefore differ by a systematic 10.4-fold shift in selectivity (Figure E,F and Table ). AICE023 (B10-position) exhibited 231-fold AKR1B10 selectivity, whereas AICE038 (B1-position) showed inverse selectivity at 0.48-fold. This selectivity provides direct experiment-derived support for the previously described structural rationale. The single nonselective B10 example (AICE032, 1.39-fold) suggests the presence of a secondary determinant beyond Trp112, possibly loop-C tip residues (Val301 and Gly302). This particular point is expected to be the subject of follow-up work. It is noteworthy that the Trp112 χ1 torsion angle, which distinguishes the two conformations, is not restrained by the Boltz-2 pocket contact. Therefore, the B10/B1 split is a ligand-dependent free prediction by the model. Moreover, its alignment with the measured selectivity is a nontrivial validation. These results support the predicted binding modes of AICE compounds.

Robustness of the Prospective Hit Rate

To ascertain whether the 70% prospective hit rate reflects Boltz-2 enrichment rather than an effect of diversity selection or library curation, we performed three complementary analyses. First, stratification of the 40 tested compounds by their cluster size (Table S2) as small (≤5), medium (6-15), and large (≥16) respectively yielded hit rates of 81.2%, 60.0% and 66.7%, all above 50%, with fundamentally no correlation between cluster size (logarithmic scale) and percentage inhibition at 10 μM (r = −0.020, n = 40; Figure S7). Second, a cluster-independent nearest-neighbor density metric (Tanimoto T = 0.3–0.7) showed |r| < 0.30 with percentage inhibition at 10 μM across all five thresholds (Figure S8), indicating that the effect is not specific to the Morgan2/Tanimoto/k = 51 clustering scheme. Third, to separate Boltz-2-specific enrichment from any effect of library curation, we extended the 51-cluster solution to 251 clusters across the full 50,240-compound library. After exclusion of the 40 AICE compounds, the threshold-pass rate (binary probability ≥0.64 and predicted log10 IC50 < 0) was 8.80% (784/8911) in the 40 AICE-occupied clusters, compared with 0.10% (43/41,076) in the 211 AICE-free clusters, corresponding to an 88-fold enrichment (one-sided Mann–Whitney U test, p = 6.4 × 10–42; Figure A). The AICE-free clusters showed a pass rate 17-fold below the library-wide value (1.65%, excluding the 40 AICE compounds), whereas the AICE-occupied clusters (8951 compounds; 17.8% of the library) included 94.8% of all threshold-passing compounds. The distribution of AICE-occupied regions across chemical space is shown in Figure B. Consistent with this, hit-anchored hierarchical clustering of the seven sub-μM AICE inhibitors and their nearest library neighbors placed the anchors on independent branches, with a median Tanimoto coefficient of approximately 0.10 to the full library (Figures S9 and S10).

Correlation of Boltz-2 Scores and Confidence Metrics with Experimental Activity

Correlation analysis between experimentally measured inhibition rates and Boltz-2 prediction scores revealed only weak linear relations, with Pearson’s r values of −0.35 to 0.08 (Figure ). This analysis was extended to eight Boltz-2 confidence metrics (confidence_score, ptm, iptm, ligand_iptm, complex_plddt, complex_iplddt, complex_pde, and complex_ipde) for the 40 AICE compounds and the 132 ChEMBL retrospective compounds, correlated with TSA ΔT m, percentage inhibition at 10 μM and 1 μM, pIC50, and pK i . No strong positive correlation was found. ptm and complex_plddt showed moderate negative correlation with TSA ΔT m (ptm r = −0.50, complex_plddt ρ = −0.52; Figure S11), whereas correlation with IC50 and pK i was consistently weak (|r| < 0.30 throughout; Figure S12 for pK i of the 132 known inhibitors).

Discussion

True Hit Rate

Of the 40 tractable candidate compounds obtained by narrowing-down with sufficient diversity from the top hits using Boltz-2, 70% (28 compounds) exhibited inhibitory activity at IC50 < 10 μM and 17.5% (7 compounds) at IC50 < 1 μM. Is the success rate attributable to overlapping with the training data? Regarding the viewpoint of ligands, at least 40 compounds possess novel scaffolds against known AKR1B10 inhibitors, according to similarity analysis of them. At the same time, moderate similarity in ErG fingerprint space indicates that the pharmacophoric arrangements are preserved to some extent. This preservation suggests that known inhibitor–protein interaction patterns can yield useful information related to the spatial arrangements and constraints for scaffold hopping using Boltz-2.

The robustness analyses indicate that the reported hit rate reflects Boltz-2 enrichment within a chemically coherent subspace, rather than reflecting other factors. The hit rate was independent of the cluster size. The conclusion was unchanged across clustering methods. Moreover, the 88-fold enrichment of threshold-passing compounds in AICE-occupied versus AICE-free clusters showed that the lead-/drug-like curation of the Enamine library alone is insufficient to explain the observed performance, although the diversity selection might improve chemical-space coverage.

Pose Prediction

In the aldo-keto reductase (AKR) superfamily, productive catalysis requires precise positioning of the substrate relative to the nicotinamide ring of the NADP+ cofactor to enable hydride transfer within a narrow distance and angular window. , Empirical insights gained from our historical inhibitor discovery campaigns served as the foundation for the construction of our constraint-guided predictive framework, ensuring that the AI-driven search space remained aligned with the functional requirements of AKR1B10. Specifically, AKR1B10 uses catalytically important residues, Tyr49 and His111, , along with the adjacent Trp112 dictating pocket specificity, which are often overlooked by generalized generative models. By enforcing proximity to Tyr49, His111, and Trp112 explicitly, the present approach might exclude compounds that would otherwise occupy the binding pocket in catalytically inert orientations. Therefore, those three residues are used for setting constraints in this study. This approach restricts ligand placement to the intended binding site in most cases.

Despite extensive efforts, achieving high isoform selectivity against the closely related AKR1B1, which shares approximately 71% sequence identity, remains challenging. , However, as described above, the protocol enriched compounds possessing essential binding features while maintaining scaffold diversity, thereby suppressing nonproductive binding modes that typically contribute to the dry–wet gap. Even minor deviations in ligand orientation can abolish catalytic competence, rendering many predicted binding modes functionally inert. As such, AKR enzymes represent a stringent benchmark for evaluating the functional fidelity of generative structure prediction models. Although the present study specifically examines AKR1B10, the constraint-guided framework is readily extensible to other enzyme targets for which catalytic geometry and electrostatic compatibility are critical determinants of productive binding.

Limitations of Score-Based Ranking

Weak correlation between the Boltz-2 scores/confidence metrics and the experimental activity described above is partly attributable to the compressed dynamic range of the tested data set because all evaluated compounds were drawn from the high-score regime (t ≥ 0.64). Under such input-variable variance compression, Pearson r and Spearman ρ tend to be attenuated relative to the true population values. Accordingly, the correlation observed for the AICE 40 set should be interpreted as lower-bound estimates of the predictive value of the confidence metrics rather than direct measurements of it. A more rigorous evaluation that includes both true binders and true nonbinders remains as future work. In addition, single-point inhibition measurements at 10 μM tend to approach saturation, thereby masking more subtle differences in binding affinity. This behavior exemplifies the well-documented “scoring function paradox”, whereby computational models classify binders versus nonbinders effectively but fail to rank relative affinities accurately among active compounds. − Such limitations are often attributed to incomplete treatment of solvation effects and entropy–enthalpy compensation in tight-binding regimes. ,,

In fact, the primary strength of our approach lies in hit enrichment rather than fine-grained affinity ranking. As described above, quantitative affinity predictions remain challenging to the degree that our approach to maximize chemical space coverage while minimizing redundancy among top hits detected by Boltz-2 or other cofolding models presents an appropriate and practical way to find novel bioactive chemotypes in drug discovery.

Conclusion

For this study using a tractable number 40 of compounds selected from the library by narrowing down the plausible hits by Boltz-2, we achieved an experimentally validated hit rate of 70% within the diversity-filtered and score-filtered representative set, greatly exceeding those typically reported for conventional virtual screening approaches. The successful identification of submicromolar inhibitors, including AICE003, with topologically novel scaffolds highlights the capacity of this approach to achieve scaffold hopping while preserving catalytic compatibility. Furthermore, AKR1B1 inhibition assays of the eight AICE compounds showed a 10.4-fold difference in selectivity between the predicted B10 and B1 conformation groups, providing experiment-derived support for the Trp112-based selectivity rationale.

Although the present model is more effective at classifying active versus inactive compounds than at accurately ranking binding affinities, its ability to narrow the dry–wet gap considerably represents a meaningful advance for practical AI-driven drug discovery. More broadly, this work underscores the importance of integrating earlier biochemical and mechanistic knowledge into generative AI workflows. Our approach constitutes a rational and extensible strategy for aligning high-capacity deep learning models with the functional requirements of enzymatic targets. Future efforts will specifically examine refinement of the scoring metrics and applying this approach to other cofactor-dependent enzyme systems where catalytic geometry is a dominant determinant of ligand efficacy.

Materials and Methods

Reference Structure and Constraint Definition

The crystal structure of human AKR1B10 in complex with NADP+ (PDB ID: 1ZUA) was used as a reference structure. To guide the generative model toward biologically relevant conformations, two distinct categories of distance-based constraints based on this structure were introduced during Boltz-2 inference.

First, cofactor constraints were defined by identifying all amino acid residues located within 4.5 Å of the cocrystallized NADP+ molecule (CCD code: NAP), thereby delineating the cofactor-binding pocket. Second, ligand constraints were imposed by designating key residues involved in catalytic hydride transfer (Tyr49, His111, and Trp112) as ligand-binding anchors. These constraints were designed to restrict ligand placement to orientations that are compatible with the AKR1B10 catalytic environment.

Boltz-2 Prediction Protocol

Virtual screening was conducted using Boltz-2 (ver. 2.2.0; https://github.com/jwohlwend/boltz/commit SHA: ac1c8582d61a728bee899451ff95e6e93be8922a). For each candidate compound, a YAML input file (Figure S14) was generated containing the AKR1B10 amino acid sequence, the ligand SMILES representation, and the NADP+ cofactor. Multiple sequence alignments were generated using MMseqs2 (Release 17-b804f) against the UniRef90 database (ver. 2025_03). All Boltz-2 inference runs were executed on the ABCI supercomputing infrastructure using default settings, with the potential-based energy term deliberately disabled (use_potentials = off) to keep virtual screening of approximately 50,000 compounds computationally tractable. The physical feasibility of the resulting poses (Table S4), assessed by steric-clash and intraligand overlap checks, and a comparison of use_potentials on versus off (Figures S15 and S16), are provided as Supporting Information.

Performance Metrics and Threshold Determination

To define an objective threshold used for prospective screening, a retrospective benchmark was performed using a curated data set of 132 known AKR1B10 inhibitors obtained from ChEMBL and 6600 decoy compounds generated using the Directory of Useful Decoys (DUD-E) protocol. Screening performance was quantified using the Enrichment Factor at x % (EF x ), defined as shown below. 1 EFx=Hitsx/NxHitstotal/Ntotal

Therein, Hits x and N x respectively denote the number of hits (active compounds, here) and the number of compounds, identified within the top x % of the ranked list. Hitstotal and N total respectively denote the number of active compounds and the number of compounds in the library. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted. The affinity_probability_binary threshold was scanned across its full range from 0.00 to 1.00 in 0.01 increments. The final selection threshold was set as t = 0.64, corresponding to the first local maximum of the ROC-area under the curve (ROC-AUC) profile. , Separately, compounds were considered predicted actives when their affinity_pred_value was ≤0, a cutoff calibrated to correspond to IC50 ≤ 1 μM.

Materials

All 40 test compounds (Table S5) were purchased from Enamine Ltd. with stated purity of at least 96%. The purity and identity of the eight AICE compounds were confirmed by LC–MS analysis provided by the supplier. They were dissolved in methanol. The compound library used for the virtual screening was “Enamine Discovery Diversity Set” (ver. 31 October 2023), which contains 50,240 "lead-like" compounds without PAINS. Recombinant human AKR1B10 protein was prepared as described earlier. NADPH, NADP+, and pyridine-3-aldehyde (P3A) were obtained from commercial suppliers. They were used without further purification.

Enzymatic Inhibition Assay

AKR1B10/AKR1B1 inhibitory activity was evaluated by monitoring NADPH consumption at 340 nm using a spectrophotometric assay. Experiments were conducted in 96-well flat-bottom plates using a Multiskan SkyHigh microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). The reaction mixture consisted of AKR1B10/AKR1B1 enzyme, 100 μM NADPH, and the test compound in phosphate-buffered saline (0.1 M, pH 7.4). After preincubation at 25 °C for 15 min, the reaction was initiated by the addition of P3A as the substrate. Absorbance at 340 nm was recorded for 10 min at 25 °C. Percentage inhibition was calculated relative to a solvent control containing methanol only. Half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were determined from seven-point dose–response curves generated using 2-fold serial dilution and fitted to a simple logistic model. All experiments were performed independently at least three times (n ≥ 3). Then data were reported as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Thermal Shift Assay (TSA)

Thermal stability measurements were taken using a real-time PCR system (QuantStudio 1; Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) as described earlier. Each 20 μL reaction mixture contained 0.2 mg/mL AKR1B10, 25 μM test compound, 100 μM NADP+, and 4× SYPRO Orange dye in PBS (pH 7.4). Samples were preincubated at 25 °C for 15 min, followed by a temperature ramp from 25.0 to 99.9 °C at a rate of 0.05 °C/s. The melting temperature (T m) was found from the first derivative of the fluorescence intensity curve.
