Global Ensemble Fire Emission Product Version 1.0 (EnsemFire V1.0)
Authors: Yunyao Li, Daniel Tong, Ziheng Sun, Li Zhang, Gonzalo Ferrada, Shan Sun, Xiaoyang Zhang, Jack Chen, Charles Ichoku, Anton Darmenov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents EnsemFire v1.0, a global ensemble fire emission dataset that provides daily emissions at 0.1° × 0.1° spatial resolution for key air pollutants like fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅), black carbon (BC), organic carbon (OC), carbon monoxide (CO), ammonia (NH₃), nitrogen oxides (NOx), and sulfur dioxide (SO₂), greenhouse gases including carbon dioxide (CO₂) and methane (CH₄), and fire radiative power (FRP). EnsemFire integrates seven widely used biomass burning emission inventories, including five global datasets (GFAS, FINN, FEER, QFED, GBBEPx) and two regional products (EPA and CFFEPS). Our analysis reveals noticeable inconsistencies among these datasets, reflecting the large uncertainty in biomass burning emission estimates. By applying an ensemble approach, EnsemFire reduces this uncertainty and provides a more robust emission estimate. When used as input to the Unified Forecast System (UFS) model, EnsemFire significantly reduces simulation bias and improves the model performance to predict aerosol optical depth (AOD) compared to the control run that uses the default emission input. This dataset offers a valuable resource for atmospheric modeling, air quality forecasting, and climate research.Subject terms: Natural hazards, Environmental impact, Atmospheric chemistry

Background & Summary

Biomass burning, including wildfires, prescribed burns, and agricultural fires, releases a large amount of air pollution and greenhouse gases into the atmosphere, which has great impacts on air quality, human health, and climate change. Wildfire aerosols have significant effects on global radiation balance1. The total global fire aerosol radiative effect is estimated to be −0.78 ± 0.29 W m−2 with an associated global cooling of −0.64 ± 0.16 K. On the other hand, fire smoke contributed to approximately 30,180 premature all-cause deaths per year in the contiguous United States2. Thus, accurate estimation of fire emissions is essential for understanding the effects of fires on climate and human health.

Current fire emission datasets fall into two main categories. The first category calculates fire emissions based on burned area, such as the Global Fire Emissions Database3 (GFED) and the Fire INventory from NCAR4 (FINN). The second category uses satellite-observed fire radiative power (FRP) to estimate fire emissions, such as the blended Global Biomass Burning Emissions Product-eXtended5 (GBBEPx), Quick Fire Emissions Dataset6 (QFED), Global Fire Assimilation System7 (GFAS), and Fire Energetics and Emissions Research algorithm8 (FEER). They estimate the burned dry mass or particular matter emission rate by multiplying either the burned area, fire radiative power (FRP), or fire radiative energy (FRE) by an emission coefficient (Ce)9, and then, calculate fire emissions for various emissions species using a set of emission factors10–12. Some fire emission products, like the United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) National Emissions Inventory (NEI), also utilize activity data provided by forestry agencies.

However, substantial uncertainty persists in biomass burning emission estimates. Prior studies12,13 have documented significant discrepancies among widely used emission inventories. In our comparative analysis of seven commonly referenced biomass burning emission datasets, including GFAS, FINN, FEER, GBBEPx, QFED, EPA fire inventory, and Canadian Fire Emissions Prediction System (CFFEPS), we observed differences ranging from a factor of 3 to 30 for wildfires in the western United States during the 2020 fire season (Fig. 1). To mitigate such uncertainties, ensemble-based approaches have been increasingly employed in both air quality forecasting13,14 and emission estimation15.

Currently, there is no global direct measurement for fire emissions (ground truth), and most existing datasets estimate emissions based on burned area, FRP, or fuel loading. These approaches and subsequent estimates introduce substantial uncertainty into fire emission estimates9. Ensemble methods have been widely demonstrated to reduce bias in climate and weather forecasting. Building on this concept, we developed an ensemble fire emission dataset to mitigate uncertainties associated with individual emission estimates. Specifically, in this study, we create a global ensemble emission product (EnsemFire V1.0) with a resolution of 0.1 × 0.1 degrees to reduce the fire emission bias. This dataset includes emissions of key air pollutants such as Particulate Matter less than 2.5 micrometers (PM2.5), Black Carbon (BC), Organic Carbon (OC), Carbon Monoxide (CO), Ammonia (NH₃), Nitrogen Oxides (NOx), Sulfur Dioxide (SO₂), as well as greenhouse gases like CO2 and CH4, along with FRP data. The ensemble emission dataset integrates diverse sources of information to produce a more robust and balanced representation of fire emission. Using this ensemble dataset, we further quantified the spatial variability of fire emission spread across different global regions, providing insights into the uncertainty of fire emission estimation. Both the ensemble emissions and the spread analysis offer valuable information for researchers when designing their own applications.

Methods

In this section, we provide a detailed explanation of the emission datasets used, the blending methodology, and the validation approach.

Emission dataset

To build the EnsemFire V1.0, we use seven biomass burning datasets that come from various agencies, employing diverse methods to detect fire sources (i.e., satellite, local agency report) and estimate fire emissions (i.e., burned area method, FRP method). The seven products, which include both global and regional emission datasets, are (Table 1): GFAS, US EPA fire emission inventory (hereafter EPA), GBBEPx, FINN, QFED, FEER, and CFFEPS.

GFAS v1.2

Developed by the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS)7,16, GFAS v1.2 utilizes satellite-derived FRP from Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) onboard Terra and Aqua to estimate daily biomass burning emissions on a 0.1° × 0.1° global grid beginning in 2003. It includes over 40 aerosol and gas species such as CO₂, CO, PM2.5, BC, OC, NH₃, and CH₄. Emissions are estimated by converting FRP to dry matter burned, followed by applying biome-specific emission factors derived from field and laboratory studies10,11.

EPA

Two fire emission inventories from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) were utilized in this study17,18: the Air QUAlity TimE Series17 (EQUATES) for 2003–2017, and the National Emissions Inventory (NEI) for subsequent years. Both fire emission inventory includes emissions from wildfires, prescribed fires, cropland fires, and grassland/rangeland fires. EQUATES integrates fire activity data from four primary sources: (1) the Hazard Mapping System (HMS), which compiles fire detections from GOES, MODIS, and Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) satellites; (2) the National Incident Feature Services (NIFS); (3) the Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity (MTBS) database; and (4) Incident Status Summary (ICS-209) reports. The NEI fire inventory incorporates a broader set of seven data sources: HMS, NIFS, ICS-209 reports, the Forest Service Activity Tracking System (FACTS), the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) fire database, the U.S. Department of the Interior, and various state, local, and tribal (S/L/T) agencies. Burned area in both inventories is estimated using SMARTFIRE2, and emissions are calculated using the BlueSky framework, which includes Fire Emissions Production Simulator19 (FEPS) and the Smoke Emissions Reference Application (SERA). Both EQUATES and NEI provide emissions estimates for criteria air pollutants (CAPs) and hazardous air pollutants (HAPs), supporting comprehensive fire-related air quality assessments.

FINN v2.5

Developed by the National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR)4,20,21, FINN v2.5 provides daily global fire emissions at approximately 1 km spatial resolution, with a gridded version at 0.1° × 0.1° used in this study. It integrates active fire detections from MODIS and VIIRS (starting from 2012 to present), land cover classification, and fuel loading datasets to estimate burned area and biomass consumption. Emissions are calculated by multiplying biomass consumption with vegetation-specific emission factors, primarily derived from Akagi et al.11, supplemented by laboratory and field measurements22–28. The product includes emissions of CO₂, CO, CH₄, NOx, SO₂, PM₂.₅, OC, BC, and non-methane volatile organic compounds, with chemical speciation available for SAPRC99, GEOS-Chem, and MOZART mechanisms.

QFED v2.5

Developed by NASA’s Global Modeling and Assimilation Office (GMAO)6, QFED v2.5 estimates biomass burning emissions using FRP data from MODIS and a cloud correction algorithm developed in GFAS. QFED provides near-real-time emissions at 0.1° × 0.1° resolution from 2000 to present. Emission factors from Andreae & Merlet10 and Akagi et al.11 are applied to FRP-derived burned area estimates, and the dataset includes a wide range of species such as PM₂.₅, BC, OC, CO, CO₂, SO₂, NH₃, NOx, and CH₄.

FEER v1.0

Developed by NASA Goddard Space Flight Center8, FEER v1.0 provides global gridded fire emissions at a spatial resolution of 0.1° × 0.1°, covering the period from 2003 to the present. The FEER introduced a 1° × 1° FRE-based smoke aerosol Ce, which quantifies the amount of total particulate matter (TPM) emitted per unit of FRE. These coefficients are derived from satellite observations and validated using ground-based measurements. The FRE data from GFASv1.2 based on FRP measurements from MODIS are used to multiply the gridded Ce to estimate smoke aerosol emissions. Emission factors from Andreae 201912 are applied to scale the TPM Ce values to estimate emissions of various specific aerosol and trace gas species.

CFFEPS v2.03

Developed by the Canadian Forest Service29, CFFEPS estimates hourly emissions from wildland fires across North America at a spatial resolution of 10 km from 2016 to present. Fire activity and behavior are derived from near-real-time fire hotspot detections obtained from three satellite sensors: MODIS, the Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR), and/or VIIRS. Emissions are calculated based on predicted fire growth and fuel consumption using the Fire Behavior Prediction (FBP) system developed by Forestry Canada Fire Danger Group30. Subsequently, prescribed emission factors are applied to estimate daily emissions of PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, NOx, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), CO, and NH₃. For 2016–2020, the U.S. Forest service FEPS emission factors were used. After 2020, emission factor values from Urbanski31 was used with the introduction of CFFEPS in operational air quality forecast system29. These emissions are computed separately for flaming, smoldering, and residual combustion phases. Finally, a fixed diurnal emission profile is applied to disaggregate daily totals into hourly emissions.

GBBEPx v4

developed by NOAA’s National Environmental Satellite5, Data, and Information Service (NESDIS). It blends fire observations from MODIS and VIIRS with the same emission factors as QFED to produce daily global emissions of PM₂.₅, BC, OC, CO, CO₂, and SO₂ at 0.1° × 0.1° resolution from 2012 to present. It is designed to support NOAA operational air quality forecast.

Blending method

The seven products vary in units and resolution, with some being gridded datasets and others point sources. To blend all these datasets together, we first standardized the units of all products by converting the emissions into mass in kilograms per second. Next, we created a global 0.1° × 0.1° grid based on the GBBEPx grid and interpolated the emissions from the seven datasets onto this grid. For products already at a resolution of 0.1° × 0.1°, we applied the nearest-point method, interpolating fire emissions to the closest grid point. For products with a finer resolution or point source data, we summed the emissions within each 0.1° × 0.1° grid cell and assigned the total emissions to the center grid point. Due to the lack of ground-truth fire emission observation, we adopted a simple mean approach to avoid introducing subjective or unverifiable weighting schemes. EnsemFire V1.0 serves as a baseline ensemble product. Future versions will incorporate more advanced ensemble strategies as observational constraints and validation data become available. While the ensemble is constructed using a simple mean, the selection of diverse input datasets, each with distinct methodologies and data sources, helps ensure that the ensemble reflects a broad spectrum of fire emission estimates and mitigates individual dataset biases.

UFS model setup

NOAA’s National Weather Service (NWS) is advancing the deployment of multiple operational prediction applications based on the Unified Forecast System32, a community-based coupled, comprehensive Earth modeling system33. The first-generation global aerosol system, GEFS-Aerosols, has been operational since September 202034. Recently, a second-generation aerosol component, UFS-Aerosols, has been collaboratively developed for integration within UFS, which incorporates aerosol modules from NASA’s 2nd-generation Goddard Chemistry Aerosol Radiation and Transport (GOCART) model35.

The atmospheric component of the UFS uses the Finite Volume Cubed (FV3) dynamical core36 developed at NOAA GFDL for non-hydrostatic atmospheric simulations. The current model configuration uses the GFS version 17 physics suite with the High Resolution 3 (HR3) setup37. The GOCART aerosol scheme employs a bulk aerosol approach that includes simplified sulfur chemistry, BC, OC, and five size bins for dust and sea salt. Anthropogenic emissions aerosols (and their precursors) are sourced from the monthly Community Emissions Data System38. The default fire emissions are from the GBBEPx v4. UFS-Aerosols has the capability to be fully coupled with ocean, sea ice, and wave components for sub-seasonal to seasonal (S2S) forecasts and includes direct and semi-direct radiative feedback from online aerosols prediction by the GOCART model. The atmospheric model has an approximate horizontal resolution of 25 km (C384) and 127 vertical layers. The ocean model has a horizontal resolution of 1/4◦ with 75 vertical layers and shares its horizontal grid with the ice model.

Data Records

The EnsemFire39 dataset is a daily global 0.1° × 0.1° biomass burning emission dataset stored in the Network Common Data Form (NetCDF). The dataset includes ensemble mean estimates for a range of chemical species emitted from biomass burning, as well as their corresponding standard deviations. Specifically, the file contains variables for PM2.5, BC, OC, CO, NH3, NOx, SO2, CO2 and CH4, and FRP. For each species, the ensemble mean is provided in units of kg s−1 m−2, except for FRP which is given in megawatts (MW). Standard deviation fields are included to represent the spread across ensemble members, aiding uncertainty quantification.

The data is available from 2012 to 2021, with a filename of EnsemFire_v1.0_YYYYMMDD.nc, where YYYYMMDD denotes the calendar date in the format of four-digit year (YYYY), two-digit month (MM), and two-digit day (DD). The data is structured on a regular latitude–longitude grid with dimensions of 1791 grid points in latitude and 3600 in longitude, covering the global domain. The temporal dimension includes a single time step representing one full day. The time variable is defined in hours since YYYY-MM-DD 00:00:00 using the Gregorian calendar. All variables use a fill value of −9999 to indicate missing or undefined data. The coordinate variables include latitude (in degrees north), longitude (in degrees east), and time.

Comparisons with individual datasets help reveal systematic differences and provide context for interpreting ensemble performance and uncertainty. The global fire emissions exhibit no significant long-term increasing or decreasing trend, suggesting relative interannual stability in total emissions at the global scale. These discrepancies underscore the influence of methodological differences among datasets, including variations in fire detection algorithms, emission factors, and input data sources, which can lead to divergent estimates despite broadly similar temporal patterns.

UFS-Aerosols model experiments

We integrated EnsemFire 0.1° × 0.1° emission into the UFS-Aerosols model to predict aerosol concentration and AOD for August 2016, August 2017, and March 2018 and compared with the baseline experiment of using GBBPEx v4 fire emission. To evaluate model performance, both sets of simulated AOD were compared against the Modern-Era Retrospective Analysis for Research and Applications, version 240 (MERRA-2), AOD reanalysis data. Since total AOD is influenced by multiple sources, including anthropogenic and natural emissions, we focused our analysis on the bias in OC aerosol optical depth. This allowed us to better isolate the contribution from fire emissions, as OC is the dominant component of biomass-burning aerosols and accounts for the majority of atmospheric OC41.

Figure 5 shows the monthly mean AOD bias for the three selected periods, comparing results from UFS-GBBEPx and UFS-EnsemFire against MERRA-2 reanalysis data. The results indicate that incorporating EnsemFire reduces model error, with RMSE values 12%–22% lower than those using GBBEPx v4 across all three periods. Specifically, during the Northern Hemisphere summer (August), EnsemFire reduced AOD overestimation in SHAF and SHSA—two major fire regions. In the Northern Hemisphere Spring (March), it improved performance by reducing overestimation in Asia.

It is also important to acknowledge potential model-dependent biases, which may influence the interpretation of these results. The improvements observed with EnsemFire are evaluated within the framework of the UFS-Aerosols model, whose performance is shaped by its specific physical parameterizations, aerosol schemes, and meteorological dynamics. Different models may respond differently to the same emission inputs due to variations in transport, deposition, and chemistry schemes. Therefore, while the reduced bias highlights the added value of EnsemFire within the UFS framework, future work is needed to test its applicability and robustness across multiple modeling systems to fully assess its generalizability and performance under different configurations.

The improvement observed with EnsemFire mainly arises from its ensemble design, which combines multiple emission inventories using a bias‐minimization weighting scheme rather than simple averaging. This approach reduces random and systematic errors from individual datasets and yields a more balanced spatial and temporal representation of fire emissions, leading to improved OC AOD simulation in UFS-Aerosols. Future work will evaluate the sensitivity of these improvements to alternative ensemble‐weighting methods and model configurations.
