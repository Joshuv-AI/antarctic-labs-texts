Motivation:

Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) are transforming biology, computer science, engineering, and every day life.
However, integrating the wide array of computational tools, databases, and scientific literature continues to pose a challenge to biological research.
LLMs are well-suited for unstructured integration, efficient information retrieval, and automating standard workflows and actions from these diverse resources.
To harness these capabilities in bioinformatics, we present a prototype Bioinformatics Retrieval Augmented Digital assistant (BRAD).

Results:

BRAD is a chatbot and agentic system that integrates a variety of bioinformatics tools.
The Python package implements an AI
Agent
that is powered by LLMs and connects to a local file system, online databases, and a user’s software.
The
Agent
is highly configurable, enabling tasks such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation, searches across bioinformatics databases, and the execution of software pipelines.
BRAD’s coordinated integration of bioinformatics tools delivers a context-aware and semi-autonomous system that extends beyond the capabilities of conventional LLM-based chatbots.
A graphical user interface (GUI) provides an intuitive interface to the system.

Introduction

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) are revolutionizing the use of large language models (LLMs) in biomedical research and bioinformatics.
From automating laboratory experiments
[
1
]
to virtual laboratories
[
2
]
and analyzing single-cell data
[
3
]
, LLMs are transforming how researchers approach complex tasks.
While much of LLM development has been driven by industry, the research community has unique needs, such as up-to-date literature access and tailored data pipelines.
For example, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) enables LLMs to access the most recent literature and generate verifiable answers
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.
Equipping LLMs with research tools and databases provides autonomy and transparency, capabilities that general-purpose models cannot replicate.
However, deploying LLM applications in research ecosystems requires overcoming additional challenges associated with handling computational infrastructure, interface design, and integration with existing tools.

We developed a Bioinformatics Retrieval Augmented Digital assistant (BRAD) to streamline the deployment of LLMs with biomedical research tools.
BRAD provides a chat interface to a LLM powered
Agent
capable of information retrieval from user-provided documents, online literature repositories, software pipelines, and public databases.
The agent-based design enables integration with a wide range of computational tools and resources, extending its capabilities beyond those of other RAG systems
[
5
,
6
]
.
The modular architecture includes a flexible framework for integrating data from additional sources.

2.1
Python Architecture

The
Agent
class serves as the user interface with BRAD.
It coordinates the
Agent
’s memory, manages the output directory, and oversees the utilization of system resources, including LLMs and external tools.
The interfaces to these tools are standardized, enabling an
Agent
to be configured with different or new tools.

Tool modules extend the
Agent
’s functionality by providing an interface to connect external databases, software, or other resources.
These modules are designed following the RAG paradigm, where the LLM generated reply is augmented by retrieval of relevant information from external data sources.
The modular design allows users to configure their
Agent
with tools tailored to specific needs.
Moreover, the tool interface is minimal, ensuring that new resources can be easily integrated into the framework.

BRAD is configured to integrate a broad range of LLMs.
Users can leverage locally hosted models, which can be fine-tuned for domain-specific tasks, or connect to online models such as
NVIDIA’s NIM platform
and
OpenAI
’s offerings.
BRAD is built using LangChain ensuring compatibility and to facilitate the ability to integrate with other standard frameworks
[
10
]
.

2.2
Tool Modules

2.2.1
Document Chat Tool

The Document Chat module retrieves information from documents, including research articles or any PDF, using a Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline
[
4
,
11
]
.
RAG enhances the generation process by supplying the LLM with verifiable, external information that was not used to train the LLM.

This module contains several methods to perform retrieval with similarity, multi-query, and maximal marginal relevance retrievers.
It enhances the retrieved texts by applying contextual compression, including surrounding context of the retrieved text, and incorporating source information, among other techniques.
The retrieved information is incorporated into a prompt template, such as the following, which is then passed to the LLM
[
12
]
:

System:
Respond to the human based on the provided sources.

Sources:
{Retrieved and augmented text is placed here.}

Human:
{User input is placed here.}

Providing explicit, well-sourced information to the LLM improves the quality of generated responses by: (1) minimizing hallucinations, (2) anchoring replies in reliable data, and (3) allowing users to verify the response against the cited sources.

As an example, consider the open ended question (
fig.

1
C): What are the coordinates of the genome?
We answered this using OpenAI’s
o1-preview
without RAG and
gpt-4o
with RAG.
The
o1
model responds like a contemporary bioinformatician, focusing on genomic loci and their numerical representations, as commonly used when aligning assays such as Hi-C.
In contrast, the RAG-enabled system offers a more metaphorical response, likening the nucleus to a world or globe — a perspective that once dominated biological thought
[
13
]
.
Both answers are scientifically valid.
While answer preferences vary, the RAG-generated response has the added benefit of referencing a specific source, grounding its perspective in the context of prior work.

2.2.2
Search Tool

The Search tool, like RAG, retrieves information from online databases that are external to BRAD.
As of this writing, the following sources are searchable with BRAD: arXiv, PubMed, and bioRxiv.
Users are given options to download retrieved search results and include them in a RAG database.
Similarly, BRAD’s search tool was extended to search bioinformatics specific databases including those found in Enrichr and Gene Ontology.
The system can take user queries and identify search terms, or it can upload genes to perform enrichment on from data files.

Custom prompt templates facilitate literature and database searches by leveraging the LLM to select the appropriate database and search terms.
An example of the prompt template is as follows:

System:
Select an appropriate database for the user’s query.

Database Descriptions:
{ARXIV, BIORXIV, PUBMED, ...}

Human:
{User input is placed here.}

Output
:

1.
<Enter a database name>

2.
<Enter search terms>

After searching the selected online databases, the LLM generates a response to the user using a prompt template, like the RAG pipeline.
Both the Document Chat and Search tools are integrated with both the Python package and the GUI.

2.2.3
Module Template

To ensure modularity, BRAD has a standard template for tool modules.
Each module exclusively interacts with
Agent.state

Standardized output and logging conventions ensure uniformity and proper updates to
Agent.state
during tool usage.
Supporting any LangChain compatible LLM, BRAD tool modules can leverage the full range of retrievers and tools available in the LangChain and LangGraph frameworks
[
10
]

Results and Applications

3.1
Biomarker Identification Workflow.

Identifying novel biomarkers is a common research task with broad applications
[
14
]
.
We developed a Software tool that interfaces an
Agent
with software external to BRAD, including a biomarker selection pipeline.
This module follows a workflow similar to RAG, where the LLM is prompted with information retrieved from software documentation and generates the code required to run the software.
While generating executable code with LLMs remains an open and active area of research, enabling LLM agents to execute predefined code is a more constrained and achievable objective.

We deployed an
Agent
with the Software tool and provided access to the multistage biomarker identification workflow of
[
15
]
.
The
Agent
manages a queue of actions and tools to execute the multistage biomarker workflow for a single user query, using the output directory and internal memory to transfer information and data between stages.
Instead of generating a sentence like a typical LLM, the
Agent
produces a spreadsheet from the software in the following format:

| Biomarker |
\ \ \ \ \lambda
| Rank |

---------------------------------------------------------

| CDT1    |    10 |    1 |

| PCNA    |     9 |    2 |

The table is generated reproducibly using predefined methods specified by the external software.
This integration uniquely combines the capabilities of an LLM with a software pipeline.
An LLM alone provides only generic responses disconnected from user-specific data (§
5.3
), while the absence of an LLM requires users to possess significant expertise to execute the workflow.

Beyond the biomarker pipeline, the Software tool can interface an
Agent
to other external software.
We deployed this system to interface with the
AnnData
and
Scanpy
libraries for single cell data.
However, the use of this tool (1) requires the software executed by the
Agent
to have detailed documentation and (2) is susceptible to errors in LLM generated code.
While the system works robustly for the biomarker pipeline, because generating bug free code with LLMs remains an active area of research, this tool available in the Python package and not deployed in the GUI.

3.2
Cost and Benchmarking BRAD’s Tools

We evaluated the costs associated with the Document Chat, Online Search, and Software tools, considering metrics such as runtime, API fees, and the number of LLM input/output tokens.
Notably, the integration of RAG does not significantly increase these costs compared to using the LLM alone.
Open-ended prompts to the LLM—such as those from the Document Chat or Software tools, which involve generating code or summarizing documents—incur higher costs than using the LLM to query database tools selectively.
For additional details, refer to §
5.1
.

To assess the quality of output generated by the RAG pipeline, we employed the RAG Assessment framework
[
16
]
.
Using a database of 500 unique documents spanning 18,000 pages, the RAG pipeline — tested with both standard and advanced configurations — demonstrated notable improvements in answer relevance and faithfulness.
These results align with reduced hallucination rates observed in other RAG pipelines.
Further details are provided in §
5.2
.

Summary

BRAD is a prototype AI research assistant that integrates large language models (LLMs) with information retrieval from bioinformatics resources.
Its modular architecture and diverse data sources enhance the accuracy and transparency of LLM-generated responses while offering flexibility to incorporate additional tool modules.
Distributed as a Python package with a user-friendly GUI, BRAD is designed to be both extensible and easy to deploy, making it an adaptable research tool.
BRAD is designed for both extensibility and ease of deployment, making it an adaptable and user-friendly research tool.
This positions BRAD as a valuable resource for researchers seeking to integrate LLMs into their workflows and broader research ecosystems.

5.2
RAG Assessment (RAGAs) evaluation metrics

Five metrics were used to evaluate the utility of BRAD’s RAG pipeline
[
16
]
:

•

Faithfulness: This quantifies the consistency between BRAD’s answer and the literature.

•

Answer Relevance: This evaluates if the response is applicable to the particular question.

Four components are used to calculate each of these metrics: question, response, context, and ground truth.
For an individual question, the context used to construct the question and the answer produced by BRAD’s RAG are split into small, verifiable claims.
Then, statements from BRAD’s answer can be compared in a one for one fashion to find claims indicated by BRAD substantiated or not substantiated in context of the literature.
This approach allows us to construct a standard confusion matrix where:

•

TP:
True Positives are statements that are present in both the ground truth and the generated response.

•

FP:
False Positives are statements that are present in the generated response but not the ground truth.

•

TN:
True Negatives are statements that are present in the ground truth but not in the generated response.

•

FN:
False Negatives are statements that are present in the ground truth but not in the generated response.

Faithfulness.

Faithfulness refers to the factual consistency of the generated response to a question against the given context.
Since this metric is entirely based on the context given, if the context given is faulty, then a factually incorrect answer can still be considered faithful.
Each of the generated claims is then checked against the given context to determine if the claim can be inferred from context.

\text{Faithfulness}=\frac{\text{Number of Claims from Response inferred from Context
}}{\text{Number of Claims from Response}}.

Answer Relevance.

Answer Relevance is a measure of how closely the generated response and given prompt are related.
Answers that are incomplete or contain redundant information are typically assigned lower scores. We use the following formula to calculate answer relevance

\text{Answer Relevance}=\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{e_{g_{k}}\cdot e_{o}}{\|e_{g_{k}}\|\|e_{o}\|}\right),

where
N
is the number of generated questions,
e_{g_{k}}
is the vectorized embedding of the
k
th generated question from a generated response, and
e_{o}
is the vectorized embedding of the original question.

5.3
Biomarker Selection Extended Outputs and Analysis

To compare the utility of BRAD, using
gpt-3.5
as the LLM, versus the recently introduced
o1-preview
chatbot from OpenAI, we gave both systems the prompt:

“Execute a biomarker selection pipeline for RNAseq data."

BRAD’s Software tool directly interfaces with the data and biomarker software. This allows the system output (page 10) to summarize and synthesize results from the pipeline, including providing the top ranked genes directly to the user.
The biomarker software, a collection of python scripts that are external to BRAD and used by the
Agent
’s Software tool, can be accessed from the projects Github.

In contrast,
o1-preview
, a newer LLM than the one employed by BRAD, produced a seven-step framework for executing a biomarker selection workflow (page 11).
However, rather than addressing the specific user query,
o1-preview
offered general instructions on how such a task might be approached.
This response, while structured, is significantly less useful to users who require actionable results rather than abstract guidance.

A main challenge using LLMs for biomarker identification and other bioinformatics workflows is structuring data for input to a LLM.
Whereas BRAD used software documentation to run codes and interface with data from the Software tool, the
o1-preview
chatbot lacks agentic capabilities that allow users to interface the LLM with their system.

o1-preview
can only accept text input.
To provide additional biological context to
o1-preview
, we used the abstract of the article
[
17
]
as input to
o1-preview
, with the following prompt:

“We are studying a Human foreskin fibroblast cell line from a previously published paper. Here is the abstract of the paper: [insert abstract from
[
18
]
]. What are the most important biomarker genes for this cell line?"

From this prompt, which includes additional biological context,
o1-preview
generates a specific and actionable list of potential biomarkers, similar to the response provided by BRAD (page 12).
However, the output of
o1-preview
has several shortcomings:

•

Data-Centric Limitations:
While
o1-preview
suggests plausible biomarkers for the cell type studied in
[
17
]
, it does not account for two critical factors: (1) whether the proposed biomarkers are present in the user’s dataset, and (2) whether the dataset contains significant noise or errors in the measurements for the suggested genes.

•

Lack of Transparency:
Users cannot discern the rationale behind the proposed biomarkers, making it difficult to evaluate the validity or relevance of the suggestions provided by
o1-preview
.

•

Reproducibility Concerns:
Due to the absence of transparency in its methodology, the lack of conditioning on the input data, and randomness (temperature) of the model,
o1-preview
cannot guarantee that it will produce the same list of biomarkers if queried again in the future.

•

Precision and Relevance:
Unlike
o1-preview
, which outputs extensive and generic information that a domain expert is likely already familiar with, BRAD provides concise, targeted responses that are more relevant to the user’s needs.

Execute a biomarker selection pipeline for RNAseq data

OpenAI chatbot with
o1-preview

Selecting biomarkers from RNAseq data involves a multi-step pipeline designed to identify genes or transcripts that can serve as reliable indicators for specific conditions or biological states. Below is a typical framework to execute such a pipeline:

Step 1: Load and Explore Data

1.

Input Data:

•

RNAseq data matrix (e.g., gene expression counts).

•

Associated metadata (e.g., phenotypes, conditions, or clinical outcomes).

2.

Preprocessing:

•

Normalize raw counts (e.g., TPM, RPKM, or using a tool like DESeq2 for variance stabilization).

•

Perform exploratory data analysis (EDA) to check for batch effects, outliers, and data distribution.

Step 2: Preprocess and Normalize Data

•

Quality Control:

–

Filter low-count genes (e.g., genes expressed in <5% of samples or with counts below a threshold).

–

Remove unwanted variation (batch effect removal using tools like sva or Combat).

•

Normalization:

–

Use appropriate normalization methods such as:

*

‘TMM‘ (Trimmed Mean of M-values) for edgeR.

*

‘VST‘ (Variance Stabilizing Transformation) for DESeq2.

Step 3: Identify Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs)

•

Perform statistical tests to find DEGs between conditions or phenotypes:

–

DESeq2 or edgeR for count data.

–

Adjust for multiple testing using methods like FDR (False Discovery Rate) to identify significant genes (e.g., FDR < 0.05).

Step 4: Dimensionality Reduction

•

Use techniques like PCA (Principal Component Analysis) or t-SNE to reduce dimensionality and visualize the most variable genes across conditions.

Step 5: Feature Selection for Biomarker Discovery

•

Apply feature selection techniques to pinpoint biomarkers:

–

Filter Methods:

*

Select top N genes based on fold change or p-value.

*

Use variance thresholds to filter less variable genes.

–

Wrapper Methods:

*

Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) with classifiers like SVM or Random Forest.

–

Embedded Methods:

*

LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator).

*

Elastic Net Regression.

Step 6: Model Building and Validation

1.

Model Selection:

•

Train machine learning models (e.g., Random Forest, Support Vector Machines) on the selected features.

•

Use cross-validation to assess performance (e.g., accuracy, AUC-ROC).

2.

Biological Pathway Enrichment:

•

Analyze the selected biomarkers for enrichment in known pathways using tools like GSEA, Reactome, or KEGG.

Step 7: Validation of Biomarkers

•

Validate the identified biomarkers on an independent dataset (if available) to ensure robustness.

•

Optionally, validate the findings experimentally (e.g., qPCR or other assays).
