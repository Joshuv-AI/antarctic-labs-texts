A single-arm, single-center phase II clinical study of concurrent brain radiotherapy combined with tislelizumab and chemotherapy in patients with small-cell lung cancer and brain metastases
Authors: Shuhao Que, Enhui Dai, Jingjing Wang, Qi Mo, Huan Xu, Yiqin Qiu, Xu Liang, Shuwei Zhai, Yuetong Li, Guoqiang Zhong, Wei Feng, Chao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrain metastases from small cell lung cancer (SCLC) are characterized by high malignancy and poor prognosis. Emerging evidence demonstrates that combining brain radiotherapy (BRT) and immunotherapy may improve outcomes; however, the optimal strategy for combining these therapies remains undefined. This study was conducted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of concurrent BRT combined with tislelizumab and chemotherapy in patients with SCLC and brain metastases.MethodsPatients with treatment-naive or previously treated SCLC and brain metastases who were admitted to Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (Hangzhou, China) between September 2023 and November 2025 were enrolled. All patients received one cycle of chemotherapy combined with tislelizumab, followed by initiation of BRT within one week. Chemotherapy plus immunotherapy was subsequently continued, followed by maintenance immunotherapy until disease progression. The primary endpoint was intracranial objective response rate (iORR). Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), disease control rate (DCR), and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs).ResultsTwenty-five patients were included. Among them, the iORR was 76.0%, and intracranial DCR was 100.0%. The systemic ORR and DCR were 56.0% and 80.0%, respectively. The median OS was 11.1 months [95% confidence interval (CI): 7.4–14.8], the median PFS was 5.6 months (95% CI: 3.1–8.1), and the median intracranial PFS was 7.6 months (95% CI: 5.7–9.5). TRAEs of any grade were identified in all patients, while grade ≥3 adverse events were found in 36.0% of patients. Hematological toxicity was most common (88.0%).ConclusionsConcurrent BRT combined with tislelizumab and chemotherapy demonstrated promising intracranial and systemic efficacy with manageable toxicity in SCLC patients with brain metastases and warrants further validation in larger prospective studies.Keywords: Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), brain metastases, radiotherapy, immunotherapy

Highlight box

Key findings

• Concurrent brain radiotherapy (BRT) combined with tislelizumab and chemotherapy demonstrated promising intracranial and systemic efficacy with manageable toxicity in small cell lung cancer (SCLC) patients with brain metastases.

What is known and what is new?

• Chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy is the first-line treatment option for extensive-stage SCLC, but the efficacy of systemic treatment alone for patients with brain metastases remains controversial. The optimal combination of BRT and systemic therapy is still unclear.

• Concurrent BRT combined with tislelizumab and chemotherapy can bring certain benefits to patients with brain metastases from SCLC.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Concurrent BRT combined with tislelizumab and chemotherapy is a new treatment option for patients with brain metastases from SCLC. Concurrent therapy has the value of further exploration.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide (1). Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), accounting for approximately 15% of all lung cancer cases, is characterized by rapid progression and poor prognosis (2). The brain is a frequent site of metastasis in SCLC, and the development of brain metastases is associated with particularly unfavorable outcomes. The median survival of these patients is approximately 6 months, and the 5-year survival rate is typically less than 7% (3,4).

For patients with extensive-stage SCLC (ES-SCLC), the current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend chemotherapy plus immunotherapy as the first-line treatment (5,6). Tislelizumab, a humanized immunoglobulin G subclass 4 (IgG4) anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) monoclonal antibody, is widely utilized in clinical practice. The RATIONALE-312 study (7) demonstrated that chemotherapy combined tislelizumab significantly prolonged overall survival (OS) [hazard ratio (HR) =0.75, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.61–0.93, P=0.004] and progression-free survival (PFS) (HR =0.64, 95% CI: 0.52–0.78, P<0.0001) in patients with ES-SCLC. However, despite the overall benefit of immunotherapy in ES-SCLC, its efficacy in patients with brain metastases remains uncertain. In large-scale clinical trials, including KEYNOTE-604 and IMpower133, no survival benefit of immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy was consistently reported in the subgroup of patients with brain metastases (8,9).

Brain radiotherapy (BRT), including whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT) and stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT), remains a cornerstone of local treatment for patients with SCLC and brain metastases (10). Nevertheless, the outcomes remain suboptimal. Even when combined with chemotherapy, the median OS (mOS) of patients with brain metastases is approximately 7 months (11), highlighting the limitations of current treatment strategies.

Emerging evidence demonstrates that the combination of BRT with immunotherapy and chemotherapy may improve outcomes in patients (12). In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the concurrent use of immunotherapy and radiotherapy has gained increasing acceptance and has exhibited to be both feasible and effective in patients with brain metastases (13,14). These findings provide a strong biological and clinical rationale for exploring concurrent immunoradiotherapy in SCLC and brain metastases. Hence, a single-arm, single-center phase II clinical study was conducted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of concurrent BRT combined with tislelizumab and chemotherapy in patients with SCLC and brain metastases. We present this article in accordance with the TREND reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0387/rc).

Methods

Study design and patients

Patients with either initial treatment-naive or previously treated SCLC and brain metastases who were admitted to Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (Hangzhou, China) between September 2023 and November 2025 were enrolled. Inclusion criteria were as follows:

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (No. IRB-2023-278). The study was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (identifier: ChiCTR2300070273). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. All participants were fully informed of the study objectives, treatment procedures, as well as potential benefits and risks. Patient confidentiality was strictly protected throughout the study, and all clinical data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Treatment protocol

After enrollment, all patients underwent baseline magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and routine clinical examinations, based on which individualized treatment plans were established. Immunotherapy consisted of intravenous administration of tislelizumab at a dose of 200 mg every 3 weeks. In case of poor physical condition during the second treatment cycle, administration of tislelizumab could be delayed by 1–2 weeks.

Chemotherapy was administered concurrently with immunotherapy. The chemotherapy regimens included the following:

Etoposide plus cisplatin (EP): cisplatin 75 mg/m2 on day 1 and etoposide 100 mg/m2 on days 1–3, which is repeated every 3 weeks;

Etoposide plus cisplatin (EP): cisplatin 75 mg/m2 on day 1 and etoposide 100 mg/m2 on days 1–3, which is repeated every 3 weeks;

Irinotecan plus cisplatin (IP): cisplatin 30 mg/m2 on days 1–3 and irinotecan 60 mg/m2 on days 1 and 8, which is repeated every 3 weeks;

Irinotecan plus cisplatin (IP): cisplatin 30 mg/m2 on days 1–3 and irinotecan 60 mg/m2 on days 1 and 8, which is repeated every 3 weeks;

Paclitaxel plus cisplatin (TP): cisplatin 30 mg/m2 on days 1–3 and paclitaxel 135 mg/m2 on day 1, which is repeated every 3 weeks;

Paclitaxel plus cisplatin (TP): cisplatin 30 mg/m2 on days 1–3 and paclitaxel 135 mg/m2 on day 1, which is repeated every 3 weeks;

Other clinically appropriate chemotherapy regimens.

Other clinically appropriate chemotherapy regimens.

Chemotherapy plus tislelizumab was administered every 21 days for a minimum of two cycles. For patients who tolerated treatment well, 4–6 cycles were recommended, followed by maintenance therapy with tislelizumab alone. Treatment was continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, withdrawal of informed consent, an investigator’s decision to discontinue, or completion of a maximum of 2 years of tislelizumab.

Radiotherapy options included WBRT at a dose of 30 Gy in 10 fractions or 40 Gy in 20 fractions, WBRT with a simultaneous integrated boost (SIB) of 10–20 Gy, hippocampal-sparing WBRT, or SRT. Based on previous studies, the biologically effective dose (BED)-10 of SRT in this study was approximately 50 Gy (15). Radiotherapy was typically initiated on day 7 after the commencement of chemotherapy combined with tislelizumab. For patients who experienced significant treatment-related adverse reactions, radiotherapy was initiated within one week after completing chemotherapy. Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was intracranial objective response rate (iORR). Secondary endpoints included intracranial disease control rate (iDCR), intracranial PFS (iPFS), PFS, disease control rate (DCR), objective response rate (ORR), and mOS.

Notably, OS was defined as the time from enrollment to death from any cause or the date of the last follow-up. mOS was calculated accordingly. PFS was defined as the time from enrollment to disease progression, death from any cause, or the last follow-up, whichever occurred first. In addition, iPFS was defined as the time from enrollment to intracranial disease progression, death from any cause, or the last follow-up, whichever occurred first. Patients were followed up until November 30, 2025.

Efficacy and adverse event assessment

Intracranial lesions were evaluated according to the Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (RANO) criteria, while systemic lesions were assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. Tumor responses were categorized as follows:

❖ Complete response (CR): complete disappearance of all lesions on imaging and endoscopic evaluation;

❖ Complete response (CR): complete disappearance of all lesions on imaging and endoscopic evaluation;

❖ Partial response (PR): ≥30% reduction in tumor size;

❖ Partial response (PR): ≥30% reduction in tumor size;

❖ Progressive disease (PD): ≥20% increase in tumor size or appearance of new lesions;

❖ Progressive disease (PD): ≥20% increase in tumor size or appearance of new lesions;

❖ Stable disease (SD): neither sufficient tumor shrinkage to qualify for PR nor sufficient increase to qualify for PD.

❖ Stable disease (SD): neither sufficient tumor shrinkage to qualify for PR nor sufficient increase to qualify for PD.

The ORR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving CR or PR, and the DCR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving CR, PR, or SD.

Treatment response was assessed based on serial imaging comparisons with baseline and prior scans, and categorized as CR, PR, SD, or PD according to standard radiological criteria. ORR and DCR were determined based on the best overall response observed during follow-up.

Adverse events were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) version 5.0. Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) occurring during hospitalization were documented and graded by attending physicians, while those occurring after discharge were primarily collected through patient self-reporting and telephone follow-up. Radiation-induced neurotoxicity was assessed using the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) acute radiation injury grading criteria for the central nervous system (CNS) (16), and brain computed tomography or MRI was carried out as clinically indicated. Brain imaging follow-up was generally performed using MRI at approximately 9-week intervals (with allowable variation depending on clinical conditions), while thoracic and systemic evaluations using CT were typically conducted every 6 weeks.

Data collection

The documented data of the included patients included age, sex, smoking history, initial disease stage, underlying disease (refers to common chronic comorbidities, e.g., hypertension, diabetes, and coronary heart disease). Patients were classified as “Yes” if at least one condition was present, stage at enrollment [tumor (T) and lymph node (N) classification], presence of synchronous brain metastases, prior treatments, number of brain metastases, comorbidities, presence of extracranial metastases, neurological symptoms, treatment regimens after brain metastasis, post-progression treatments, intracranial and systemic responses, median survival following the diagnosis of brain metastasis, PFS, and intracranial and systemic adverse events.

Sample size estimation

This study was designed as a single-arm phase II clinical trial. Previous studies have reported an intracranial ORR of 50–65% with WBRT in patients with SCLC and brain metastases (17). Accordingly, an iORR of 60% was defined as the null response rate (p0). The anticipated intracranial ORR for WBRT combined with tislelizumab and chemotherapy was 75% (Pp1=0.75).

A Simon two-stage optimal design was applied with a one-sided significance level of α=0.05 and a type II error rate of β=0.20, corresponding to a statistical power of 80%. In the first stage, 30 evaluable patients were planned for enrollment (n1=30). If 18 or fewer patients achieved an intracranial objective response (X1≤18), the trial would be terminated early due to futility. Conversely, if 19 or more responses were found (X1≥19), the study would progress to the second stage, with a total planned sample size of 62 evaluable patients (N=62). At the final analysis, the null hypothesis would be rejected if at least 44 patients achieved an intracranial objective response (X≥44).

Should the predefined efficacy threshold (X1≥19) be met prior to the enrollment of 30 evaluable patients, progression to the second stage could be considered based on ethical considerations, feasibility, and observed efficacy signals. Any potential bias arising from this protocol deviation was comprehensively assessed following the completion of both study stages.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 29.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Sample size estimation was conducted using PASS software (version 2023). All statistical tests were one-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Furthermore, CIs were calculated at the 95% level. Disease control and survival outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

A total of 25 patients were enrolled in the study (Table 1). The median age was 62.0 years, and more than 80% of the cohort were male. Nearly 80% of the patients have a history of smoking. The median number of brain metastases was 6. Neurological symptoms were present in 52.0% of patients. Extracranial metastases were identified in 9 patients, including liver metastases (n=3), bone metastases (n=4), and adrenal metastases (n=4). Moreover, 9 patients presented with treatment-naïve brain metastases, while among the 16 patients with previously treated disease, 8 had received prior immunotherapy. Notably, 3 patients had undergone prior thoracic surgery. More than 80% of the patients had a KPS score of 80 or above at the time of enrollment. A Graded Prognostic Assessment (GPA) score ≤2 was found in 76% of patients.

All 25 patients received planned chemotherapy combined with tislelizumab and concurrent BRT (Table 2). Among them, 23 patients underwent WBRT (30 Gy/10 fractions), with dose reduction in 2 patients and WBRT-SIB in 1 patient. Efficacy

With a median follow-up of 23.8 months, the iORR was 76.0%, and the iDCR was 100%. The systemic ORR was 56.0%, and the systemic DCR was 80.0% (Tables 3,4). mOS was 11.1 months (95% CI: 7.4–14.8), median PFS was 5.6 months (95% CI: 3.1–8.1), and median iPFS (miPFS) was 7.6 months (95% CI: 5.7–9.5) (Figure 2). A swimmer plot was generated to illustrate OS and treatment response (Figure 3). The longest OS was 26.4 months, and the patient remained alive at the last follow-up.

Adverse events

All patients experienced TRAEs. Hematological toxicities were the most frequently observed adverse effects, occurring in 88.0% of patients. Among them, over 80% of the patients developed anemia, and 60% of the patients had a decrease in white blood cells. Abnormal liver function was found in 52.0% of patients, and thyroid dysfunction in 48.0%. Moreover, 3 patients experienced grade 1 elevations in myocardial enzymes. Grade ≥3 adverse events occurred in 36.0% of patients, mainly including hematological toxicities. Among them, 5 patients presented with grade 3 or above neutropenia, 5 patients had grade 3 or above thrombocytopenia, and nearly 10% of the patients developed severe anemia. One patient developed a grade 3 rash. According to the RTOG acute central nervous system (CNS) radiation injury criteria, 4 patients had scores of ≥2, while no grade 3 or 4 toxicities were observed (Table 5). Four patients used dexamethasone for symptom relief.

Discussion

This study explored the efficacy and safety of chemotherapy combined with tislelizumab and concurrent BRT in patients with treatment-naïve or previously treated SCLC with brain metastases. The results revealed a high iORR of 76.0% and an iDCR of 100%. The mOS was 11.1 months (95% CI: 7.4–14.8), the PFS was 5.6 months (95% CI: 3.1–8.1), and the miPFS was 7.6 months (95% CI: 5.7–9.5).

The present study provided supportive evidence for the use of combined chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and yBRT in patients with brain metastases. Prior to the advent of immunotherapy, the conventional treatment for SCLC with brain metastases was a combination of BRT and chemotherapy, which achieved an iORR of 56–82% (11) and a mOS of approximately 8 months (18). In some studies, the number of brain metastases, typically fewer than 10, has been used as a criterion for selecting stereotactic radiotherapy as a BRT technique. The mOS reported in these studies is generally 8 to 10 months (15,19,20). In the present study, the mOS of 11.1 months is indicative of a clinically meaningful improvement compared with historical outcomes achieved using conventional therapies. Prior research (21) has demonstrated that the addition of immunotherapy failed to prolong survival in patients with SCLC and brain metastases (mOS =5.3 months in the immunotherapy group), which could be attributable to the insufficient use of BRT. In contrast, accumulating evidence indicates that immunotherapy could be advantageous for patients with brain metastases. For instance, a large-scale retrospective analysis (22) of 398 patients with SCLC and brain metastases found that the immunotherapy group exhibited significantly improved OS compared with the non-immunotherapy group (HR =0.77, 95% CI: 0.67–0.88, P<0.001). Another study (10) involving 187 patients with baseline SCLC brain metastases found that chemotherapy, immunotherapy combined with BRT significantly improved OS compared with either immunotherapy alone or BRT alone. A study of 153 patients (23) indicated similar results (15.5 vs. 9.5 months, P=0.0001). In the present study, the 1-year survival rate was 48.9%, which was significantly higher than the 21.6% reported in a population-based analysis (24) of more than 7,000 patients with SCLC and brain metastases who were treated with conventional chemoradiotherapy between 2010 and 2019. Collectively, these findings indicate that the combination of chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and BRT can significantly improve OS in patients with SCLC and brain metastases. In terms of local control, a study (25) demonstrated an iORR of 72.0% and an iDCR of 93.6% for traditional treatment, consisting of chemotherapy plus BRT. In comparison, the combination of chemotherapy with tislelizumab and concurrent BRT in this study yielded higher iORR and iDCR; however, the degree of improvement was modest, which could be attributable to the limited sample size.

On the mechanistic level, the synergistic effects of radiotherapy and immunotherapy may be noteworthy in SCLC patients with brain metastases. Tislelizumab, a PD-1 monoclonal antibody, blocks the interaction between PD-1 and PD-L1, thereby regulating T cell-mediated anti-tumor effects (26). However, the efficacy of immunotherapy may vary across different molecular subtypes of SCLC. According to the transcriptomic profiling, SCLC can be classified into four molecular subtypes: ASCL1 (SCLC-A), NEUROD1 (SCLC-N), POU2F3 (SCLC-P), and the inflammatory subtype (SCLC-I). Accumulating evidence indicated that the SCLC-I subtype is more likely to derive benefit from immunotherapy (27,28). However, due to economic and other practical limitations, transcriptomic profiling is not routinely performed in the majority of patients with SCLC, leaving molecular subtype information unavailable in clinical practice. For these patients, radiotherapy may be a rational therapeutic option, as it has the potential to enhance the immunotherapeutic sensitivity in subtypes that are less responsive to immunotherapy (29). The peak of radiotherapy-induced tumor cell death occurs within 24 hours and persists for up to 7 days (30), while tumor-associated antigen exposure can increase within 10 days, which is directly associated with the sensitivity of tumor immunotherapy (31,32). Although the above-mentioned studies did not specifically investigate the combined effects of radiotherapy and immunotherapy for intracranial SCLC, and primarily evaluated immune responses outside the brain, they still provide valuable insights into the treatment of patients with brain involvement. Thus, radiotherapy may transiently enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy, providing a theoretical rationale for the potential benefit of concurrent immunotherapy and radiotherapy.

The identification of the optimal combination strategy for BRT and immunotherapy was the main concentration of this study. A retrospective study (12) of more than 70 patients with brain metastases revealed that the timing of the interval between immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and BRT, was not significantly associated with OS or PFS. This study further demonstrated that BRT administered prior to undergoing ICI was associated with a poorer PFS compared with BRT administered after ICI (HR=2.32, CI: 1.16–4.66, P=0.018). Another study (10) categorized the combination of immunotherapy and BRT into sequential (with an interval between BRT and ICI >1 month) and concurrent (with an interval ≤1 month) strategies. The results revealed that the sequential approach exhibited a notable advantage in terms of both PFS and iPFS for patients with baseline SCLC brain metastases. Importantly, both studies indicated no significant differences in OS based on the treatment sequence. However, a study involving 49 patients demonstrated that BRT administered prior to ICI was associated with a longer OS compared with BRT administered after ICI (23.3 vs. 34.8 months, P=0.02) (33). The majority of related studies currently lack stratified analyses of different combination strategies for BRT and immunotherapy. Instead, heterogeneous treatment approaches are generally pooled together, with only limited subgroup analyses yielding inconsistent conclusions. In the present study, the combination of chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and concurrent BRT was assessed, which achieved a median PFS of 5.6 months and a miPFS of 7.6 months. These are comparable to, and in some respects more favorable than, those reported for conventional chemotherapy combined with BRT (34), which yielded median PFS and miPFS of 5 months and 6 months, respectively. Previous research indicated that an unstratified combination of immunotherapy and BRT resulted in an miPFS of 10.7 months (25). The slightly lower miPFS found in the present study could be attributable to the prior exposure to immunotherapy in a subset of patients (35). Notably, after excluding 8 patients with prior immunotherapy, the remaining 17 patients had a miPFS of 11.2 months, being comparable to the unstratified immunotherapy cohort. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that concurrent treatment remains a promising strategy and warrants further investigation in the future.

Notably, the swimmer plot suggests that intracranial response was not strongly correlated with OS, indicating that survival may depend more on extracranial disease control. Therefore, the benefit of adding immunotherapy may lie primarily in improving systemic disease control. Radiotherapy may also synergize with immunotherapy by modulating the tumor microenvironment and inducing systemic immune responses (29), although this remains to be further validated, particularly in the intracranial setting.

In terms of adverse events, the incidence of any grade TRAEs was 100%, in which hematological toxicity was found in 88.0% of patients, and grade ≥3 hematological toxicity in 32.0%. These findings are consistent with those of a previous study (10), which reported hematological toxicity rates exceeding 90% across the ICI-alone, BRT-alone, and ICI + BRT groups, all three groups of patients were treated with chemotherapy. This indicates that concurrent BRT with tislelizumab and chemotherapy does not significantly exacerbate hematological toxicities in SCLC patients with brain metastases. A previous study (36) analyzing adverse events in 42 patients who received WBRT (including WBRT-SIB) combined with immunotherapy for SCLC patients with brain metastases found no significant difference in the incidence of radiation-induced brain necrosis between WBRT and WBRT-SIB. In this study, one patient underwent WBRT-SIB, with no significant increase in RTOG scores. These findings demonstrate that, in the context of immunotherapy, WBRT-SIB may not exacerbate radiation-related brain injury compared with conventional WBRT. Further research is thus required to confirm these findings. Overall, the adverse events associated with concurrent chemotherapy, tislelizumab, and BRT are within an acceptable range compared with BRT combined with chemotherapy.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Firstly, the relatively low incidence of SCLC, combined with eligibility criteria based on patient performance status and brain metastasis size, resulted in a small sample size and a disproportionate representation of male patients, reflecting both epidemiological patterns and treatment-seeking behaviors. Secondly, in the first phase, 25 evaluable patients were enrolled, with 19 demonstrating intracranial responses, thereby exceeding the threshold required to advance to phase two. While the smaller sample size might modestly diminish statistical power under the alternative hypothesis, it did not elevate the risk of type I error. Considering the feasibility of the study, ethical considerations, and observed efficacy, progression to the second phase was regarded as appropriate, including plans for a more comprehensive analysis of potential biases to be conducted upon completion of patient enrollment. Third, the inclusion of different radiotherapy modalities (SRT, WBRT, hippocampal-sparing WBRT, and WBRT with SIB) may have introduced treatment heterogeneity and potential confounding, given their known differences in local control. Moreover, no stratified analysis by radiotherapy modality was performed, which may limit the interpretation of the results. Finally, although both intracranial and systemic responses were evaluated, the impact of extracranial disease control on survival outcomes was not analyzed separately, which represents another limitation of this study.

Conclusions

Concurrent BRT combined with tislelizumab and chemotherapy could provide clinical benefits for SCLC patients with brain metastases, with radiation-induced brain injury and TRAEs falling within an acceptable range. This approach further supports the potential of combining immunotherapy and radiotherapy in brain metastasis patients. Compared with unstratified immunotherapy plus BRT, the concurrent treatment exhibited no evidence of significant inferiority and warrants further investigation in future combination therapy studies.
