Hospital burden of Respiratory Syncytial Virus in children in Spain (2016–2022): A retrospective study
Authors: Ruth Gil-Prieto, Rafael García-Carretero, Valentín Hernandez-Barrera, Patricia Marín-García, Alba González-Escalada, Ángel Gil de Miguel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRespiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) is a leading cause of acute lower respiratory tract infections, particularly in children under two years of age. This study aimed to estimate the population-based burden of RSV-related hospitalizations in individuals under 19 y in Spain from 2016 to 2022. A retrospective analysis was conducted using national hospital discharge data. Hospitalization rates, mortality, case fatality, and associated healthcare costs were analyzed by age group and year. A total of 166,046 RSV-related hospitalizations were recorded (57.36% male), with 94.60% in children under 5 y and 66.22% in infants under 1 y. The highest mean hospitalization rate was observed in infants <1 y (4,367.87 per 100,000), increasing to 5882.06 in 2022. Mortality was highest in infants <1 y (2.9 per 100,000), while the highest case fatality rates occurred in the 10–14 (0.89%) and 15–19 (0.92%) age groups. Children <5 y with comorbidities had significantly higher case fatality (0.76%) compared to those without (0.02%; OR=31.76). Hospitalization and mortality rates were lowest during the COVID-19 pandemic years. The mean cost per hospitalization was €3,169.63 for children <5 y and €3,276.50 for infants <1 y. The total cost exceeded €497 million, with €360 million attributed to infants <1 y. RSV imposes a substantial burden on the pediatric population in Spain, particularly among infants. These findings underscore the need for effective preventive strategies, including immunization and early intervention.KEYWORDS: Hospitalizations, Respiratory Syncytial Virus, infant, children, epidemiology, Spain

Introduction

Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) is a highly contagious pathogen and the leading cause of acute lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs), particularly in infants under two years of age.1,2 RSV exhibits seasonal patterns with annual epidemics, typically peaking during colder months in temperate climates.3,4 According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study, RSV affected approximately 24.8 million individuals globally in 2016, resulting in 76,000 deaths.5 In 2019, an estimated 33 million episodes of RSV-associated LRTIs occurred worldwide, leading to 3.6 million hospital admissions, 26,300 in-hospital deaths, and 101,400 overall deaths among children under five years.6

By the age of one, around 60–70% of children have been infected with RSV, increasing to 90% by age two.3,4 This widespread infection contributes significantly to infant hospitalizations, morbidity, and postnatal mortality, especially in low-resource settings.7 In Spain, retrospective studies have reported hospitalization rates of 4137 per 100,000 children under one year between 1997 and 2011, with 80% of deaths occurring in this age group,8 and rates ranging from 3998 to 5453 per 100,000 among infants aged 0–5 months between 2016 and 2019.9

Risk factors for severe RSV outcomes include prematurity, chronic lung disease, congenital heart defects, immunosuppression, and neuromuscular disorders.10 Severe RSV infection in infancy is associated with increased respiratory morbidity in the short to medium term, leading to elevated healthcare utilization for up to two years post-infection.11 Additionally, RSV infection has been linked to recurrent wheezing and the development of asthma into adolescence and early adulthood.11–13

Treatment for RSV is primarily supportive, as the infection is self-limiting and does not confer lasting immunity.7,13,14 Passive immunization is considered a promising preventive strategy due to the immature immune systems of infants. Palivizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the RSV fusion protein, is approved for high-risk infants but is costly and limited in availability.15,16 Nirsevimab, a long-acting monoclonal antibody, is indicated for all infants under six months and those at risk up to 24 months, offering protection throughout the RSV season with a single dose.13,17,18 It was authorized in the EU in October 2022, and Spain has since recommended its use for high-risk infants and those born during the RSV season.19

Maternal vaccination is another preventive strategy, recently approved by the European Medicines Agency (EMA), though not yet available in Spain.20,21

This retrospective study aims to estimate the population-based burden of RSV-related hospitalizations among children and adolescents up to 19 y of age in Spain from 2016 to 2022, including the COVID-19 pandemic period. It also seeks to identify changes in RSV seasonality and risk factors to inform national immunization strategies.

Methods

This retrospective study was performed using the information obtained from the Spanish National Hospital Data Information System (Conjunto Mínimo Básico de Datos; CMBD) maintained by the Ministry of Health.22 This system is a valuable system for epidemiological analysis,22 and according to the 10th Clinical Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10-CM)23: i) covers an estimated 98% of public hospitals and provides a complete hospitalization registry, and ii) since compulsory health insurance covers an estimated 99.5% of the Spanish population, and even persons not covered by it can be treated in the hospitals of the Health Care System, information collected is representative of the entire population; and iii) the Spanish Ministry of Health validates the CMBD for data quality and overall methodology.

The Rey Juan Carlos University Ethics Committee determined that obtaining ethical approval for this study was not required. All retrospective cases diagnosed with RSV in children up to 19 y old between January 2016 and December 2022 were collected. Children were grouped and studied according to age, with special emphasis on those under 1 and 5 y. According to the ICD-10-CM,23 the code J21.0,J21.9,B97.4,J20.5,J20.9,J12.1- RSV infection was used. The necessary codes related to the risk factors of interest were also considered. (Suppl 1)

The increased risk in patients with comorbidity was analyzed. ORs by risk factor were calculated for these patients exposed to a given risk factor: Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), solid tumors, neoplasms, immunosuppression, diabetes, arterial hypertension (AHT), cardiopulmonary disease, chronic lower respiratory tract disease, cerebrovascular disease, asthma and acute renal failure.24

Statistical analysis

It was assumed that the distribution by age of the population covered by these public hospitals was equal to that of the general population. Patient data were anonymized and de-identified prior to analysis.

The average number of hospitalizations per year, the annual incidence of hospital admissions (per 100,000 population), and the rate of death (per 100,000 population) were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for all the study period, per year and season. Corrected population data from municipal records extracted from the National Institute of Statistics were used as the denominator for the hospitalization rates.

For the analyses by season, seasson was defined as from October 1st to September 30th.

The average length of hospital stay (ALOS) was calculated as the total length of stay divided by the total number of hospitalizations. The Ministry of Health estimated the cost of hospitalizations to the healthcare system data. The case fatality rate (%), calculated by dividing the number of deaths by the total number of hospitalizations, reflected the severity of cases.

Poisson regression models were used to measure the differences in hospitalization and mortality rates during the study period by age group and sex.

Logistic regression was used to assess the differences in case fatality rates during the study period by age group and sex and risk factor associated to case fatality rate.

For all tests, the significance level was set at p < .05. Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS 29 and Stata 16.1 software.

Results

A total of 166,046 cases in children and pediatric population <19 y old, 57.36% male (95,244 children) and 42.64% female (70,802 children), were admitted for RSV from 2016 to 2022. Most of them, 157,080 (94.60%), were <5 y old (90,265 male [57.46%] and 66,815 female [42.54%]), and 109,949 (66.22%) were <1 y (63,983 male [58.19%] and 45,966 female [41.81%]). The ALOS for children <5 and <1 y old was the same, 4 d, but with IQR of 4.00 and 3.00, respectively. The mean (SD) cost of hospitalization was 3169.63€ (2,904.21)/hospitalization for children <5 y and 3276.50€ (3,089.40)/hospitalization for children <1 y. The total cost was more than €497 million for children <5 y, most of it due to the hospitalization of children <1 y (€ 360 million, € 51,43 million/year). Among all the RSV related hospitalizations in children, 0.68% were coinfected with influenza virus and 1.30% with COVID.

The mean annual hospitalization rate between 2016 and 2022 was significantly different (p < .001) among the age groups (Figure 1). The highest mean hospitalization rate was observed for children <1 y, 4367.87/100,000 population (CI 95%: 4342.62–4,393.12), being higher for males than for females (4,947.74/100,000 population [CI 95%: 4910.36–4,985.12] vs 3755.24/100,000 population [3,721.56–3,788.92]), and decreased with age. Significant differences (p = .000) were also observed by sex, higher for males for all ages except for the age range of 15–19 y old, in which no differences were observed (p = .823).

Significant differences (p < .001) by year were found in hospitalization rates in most age groups (Figure 2(A) and Table 1), except for the age range of 5–9 y (p-value = .157). In addition, an increase was observed in children <1 y in 2022, compared with previous years before the pandemic (5,882.06/100,000 population; CI 95%: 5800.62–5,963.50). Significant differences (p < .001) were also recorded according to seasons, except for the age range of 10–14 y (p-value = .285). The highest hospitalization rate was found again for children <1 y (2,229.67/100,000 population [CI 95%: 2184.03–2,275.31]) in the 2018–2019 season (Figure 2(B) and Table 2). More frequent comorbidities were: respiratory failure, not classified elsewhere (5.76%); other respiratory disorders (2.42%); other respiratory disorders originating in the perinatal period (1.78%) and pneumonia, microorganism not specified (1.34%).

From 2016 to 2022, the total number of deaths was 147 patients, 77 males and 70 females. The mean mortality rate during the study period was significantly higher (p 0.000) in children <1 y (2.90/100,000 population [CI 95%: 2.23–3.57]) compared with other ages, with no differences by sex (Figure 3(A)). However, the mean case fatality rates were significantly different (p < .001) among age ranges in the study period. It started to rise at 5 y old, and dramatically increased from 9 y old, being the highest one observed in the age ranges of 10–14 y old (0.89% [CI 95%: 0.89–0.89]) and 15–19 y old (0.92% [CI 95%: 0.92–0.92]) (Figure 3(B)). No differences were found by sex.

No significant differences were recorded in the mortality rate according to the seasons (Figure 4(A) and Table 3). However, the lowest mortality rate was found in the 2020–2021 season, matching the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 and 2021, and the highest one was observed in children <1 y (3.43/100,000 population [1.40–5.46]) in the 2022–2023 season (only 2022 months included). Regarding the case fatality rates by season, only significant differences (p = .032) were found for children 1 y old. The highest case fatality rates were found for 10–14 y in 2017–2018 and 2020–2021, being 2.05% (CI 95%: 0.27–3.83) and 2.8% (CI 95%: −0.33–5.93), respectively; and for 15–19 y old in 2015–2016 (only 2016 months) and, again, in 2021–2021 season, being 1.83% (CI 95%: −0.69–4.35) and 1.45% (CI 95%: −1.37–4.27), respectively (Figure 4(B) and Table 3). Of the 166,046 cases of RSV hospitalization recorded for children and pediatric population <19 y old between 2016 and 2022, 157,080 cases were reported in children <5 y, of which 5.66% presented with at least one underlying condition that was considered a risk factor for the disease. The most common risk factor was chronic lower respiratory tract disease (1.46%), followed by asthma (1.32%), cardiopulmonary disease (1.17%) and cancer (1.05%). Thus, the case-fatality rate among children <5 y with at least one comorbidity was significantly higher (p = .016) than that among those without underlying conditions, 0.76% vs 0.02%, respectively; with a meaningful much higher probability of dying with (OR = 31.76 [CI 95%: 21.31–47.34]) (Table 4) in those with associated comorbidities. The most important risk factors for death were acute renal failure (OR = 431.47), cerebrovascular disease (OR = 161.42), cardiopulmonary disease (OR = 125.80), AHT essential (OR = 106.77), immunosuppression (OR = 69.83) and AHT disease (OR = 62.80).

The rate of ICU admission and ventilator support among children with severe RSV has remained similar before and after the COVID-19 pandemic (p = .376; data not shown), though seasonal patterns and total case numbers have fluctuated due to changes in viral circulation and mitigation measures.

Discussion

This retrospective analysis assessed the hospitalization burden of RSV among children under 19 y in Spain between 2016 and 2022. The highest hospitalization rates were observed in infants under one year, with a notable increase in 2022 (5,882.06 per 100,000; CI 95%: 5800.62–5963.5), resulting in over €360 million in healthcare costs. Mortality rates were also significantly higher in this age group (2.90 per 100,000; CI 95%: 2.23–3.57), although case fatality rates increased with age, peaking in adolescents aged 10–19 y. Additionally, 5.66% of children under five had comorbidities that elevated their risk of severe outcomes.

Consistent with previous studies,7,13,25,26 our findings reaffirm that infants under one year are the most vulnerable to RSV, comprising 66.22% of hospitalizations. Hospitalization rates remained stable compared to earlier Spanish studies,8,9 and similar trends were reported across the EU.27 Although rates decline with age, the burden in older children may be underestimated due to nonspecific symptoms and diagnostic limitations.9,28

Significant year-to-year and seasonal variations were observed, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, which disrupted RSV epidemiology.2,29–33 Restrictions during the pandemic led to a temporary decline in RSV cases, but post-pandemic seasons saw atypical peaks, including spring-summer outbreaks in Spain and other regions.30,34,35 Behavioral changes and climatic factors may have contributed to these shifts.2

The rise in hospitalizations in 2022 prompted concern from the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control.36 While increased severity or virulence is unlikely,37 factors such as improved diagnostics, healthcare-seeking behavior, and reduced maternal immunity due to RSV circulation gaps may explain the surge.30,38

Hospitalization and RSV incidence are likely underestimated, especially in older children.9,39 A study by Haeberer et al.9 suggested rates could be up to 6.5 times higher. Ares-Gómez et al.11 found that RSV significantly increases healthcare utilization for up to two years post-hospitalization. Our study estimated a mean hospitalization cost of €3,276.50 for infants under one year, totaling over €360 million. Including outpatient care and indirect costs would further highlight RSV’s economic impact.

RSV-related deaths during hospitalization in children can either be directly caused by severe infections such as bronchiolitis, pneumonia, or respiratory distress in otherwise healthy patients, or may result from underlying health problems being worsened by RSV. The majority of fatalities occur in children with complex chronic illnesses (e.g., cancer, heart or lung disease, immunosuppression, prematurity), where RSV acts as a trigger that worsens their condition. The study highlights that having comorbidities is a strong, independent risk factor for death in children hospitalized with RSV, and that when determining cause of death, RSV may be listed as the main cause, a contributing factor, or simply coexisting, depending on each case’s circumstances. Mortality was highest in infants, consistent with global data,6,7,25,40 due to immature respiratory and immune systems.13 However, case fatality rates rose sharply in older children with comorbidities, such as acute renal failure (OR = 431.47), cerebrovascular disease (OR = 161.42), and cardiopulmonary conditions (OR = 125.80). The large variation in year-to-year case fatality rates among RSV-hospitalized children aged 10–14 and 15–19 could be explained by several factors, such as the small number of cases that case can cause large fluctuations in the calculated case fatality rate from year to year, the variation of the underlying conditions, or coinfections and possible changes in hospital codings. These findings underscore the importance of targeted prevention strategies, that include both immunological approaches (maternal vaccination and long-acting monoclonal antibodies) and public health measures, such as frequent washing of hands for children, family members, and caregivers; environmental cleaning; and reducing exposures. These strategies could reduce overall RSV transmission.

Palivizumab, though effective, is costly and limited in scope.15,16,41 Nirsevimab offers broader protection and has demonstrated high efficacy in clinical trials.13,42–44 At present, although the Spanish health administration has already included RSV in the list of preventable diseases,45 still does not have a defined strategy to combat RSV for the entire population. However, since the 2023–2024 season, the Vaccination Program and Registry Committee, and following the statement of the Spanish Society of Paediatric Infectious Disease (SEIP),46 has issued a report on recommendations for using nirsevimab against RSV for each season.19 Spain has begun implementing passive immunization using nirsevimab, with promising early results from regions like Galicia, Madrid, Navarre, and Catalonia.47–50 A national modeling study projected significant reductions in RSV-related healthcare utilization and costs with universal nirsevimab immunization.51

Our study underscores the significant health and economic impact of RSV infection in infants. Historically, preventive options were limited to palivizumab, a monoclonal antibody that, while effective in reducing disease severity, was costly and not feasible for widespread use. More recently, nirsevimab has emerged as a promising alternative, demonstrating strong efficacy in reducing RSV-related illness in infants during phase III clinical trials. Additionally, three vaccines – Abrysvo, Arexvy, and mRESVIA – have been approved for use in older adults, with several other candidates showing encouraging results in clinical development. The adult vaccines may decrease community-based spread of RSV. These advancements mark a substantial leap forward in RSV prevention, offering enhanced protection for high-risk groups. As a result, the landscape of RSV management is undergoing rapid transformation.52

This study has some limitations. Primarily, it includes only hospitalized cases, excluding milder RSV infections typically managed in primary care. As a result, the overall burden of RSV may be underestimated. Since confirmation of RSV infection was not available for all patients in the study cohort, there may be cases of bronchiolitis from other causes within the analyzed group. As a result, the true number of hospitalizations specifically attributable to RSV may be overestimated. Nonetheless, a key strength lies in the use of the MBDS database, which captures nearly all admissions across public and private hospitals in Spain, offering robust statistical power and strong external validity,53 despite potential variability in coding and clinical documentation.54 Although ICD-10 codes that are specific to RSV can help portray the epidemiological trends of the virus, relying solely on these codes results in an undercount of the true number of RSV infections.55 In Spain, RSV testing practices are influenced by both age and illness severity: infants and critically ill children are most often tested, while routine testing in older children and mild cases is less common unless there are enough resources or specific surveillance protocols. This targeted strategy may fail to capture the actual burden of RSV in older children and those who are not hospitalized. Expanding access to multiplex PCR testing could improve detection rates for all age groups. Another limitation is the absence of data from seasons following the introduction of nirsevimab, meaning the study does not reflect the impact of this new immunization strategy. Covering a seven-year period (2016–2022), including the COVID-19 pandemic, this study provides a robust baseline for future evaluations of RSV trends and preventive measures. Despite its limitations, it offers a comprehensive overview of RSV burden and underscores the need for research assessing the long-term impact of immunization programs. These findings will be essential for retrospective studies evaluating maternal vaccination and universal prophylaxis with monoclonal antibodies.

Conclusion

Our study confirmed that RSV is still a major problem in terms of hospitalization rate, morbidity, and mortality, especially in children <1y, which results in a great expense for the health systems. Thus, the impact and cost-effectiveness of RSV prevention programs should be considered. Based on the results of the first studies detailing promising preliminary results of the passive immunization plan using Nirsevimab, widespread use of monoclonal antibodies in newborns may play a key role in reducing the health and economic burden of RSV infection in Spain. The results of this study show the need for preventive measures for managing RSV infection in children.

Biography

Ruth Gil-Prieto is a Preventive Medicine and Public Health professor at Rey Juan Carlos University in Madrid. Trained as a biochemist, she completed her PhD in epidemiology and public health. Because of her interest in humanitarian medicine and development, she quickly turned her attention to infectious disease epidemiology, particularly vaccines. After serving as an adjunct professor, she completed research stays in the pharmaceutical industry, closely following the clinical development of several vaccines. In 2010, she obtained her Tenure at the University Rey Juan Carlos (URJC) Madrid, where she taught epidemiology and served 7 y as the director of the International Cooperation and Volunteering Program.
