Preferences, factors, and needs involved in decision-making on medication use during lactation among parents and healthcare providers: a Canada-wide cross-sectional survey study
Authors: Uma Yakandawala, Laurie Proulx, Christina Raimondi, Katherine Kearns, Angela Blouin, Karine Toupin April, Dana Turcotte, Lauren E. Kelly, Christine Leong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDecision-making around medication use during lactation is often challenging for both parents and healthcare providers. The purpose of this study is to understand preferences, factors, and needs related to decision-making of parents and healthcare providers regarding medication use during lactation.MethodTwo separate online surveys were disseminated to Canadian mothers/birthing parents and healthcare providers from November 2021 to February 2022. Participants were recruited through advertisements on social media. The surveys collected demographic information and participants’ decision-making factors regarding medication use during infant feeding.ResultsThere were 165 parents and 47 healthcare providers (n = 44 physicians; n = 2 nurses; n = 1 pharmacist) who completed the survey. Over half (54.7%) of parent participants found decision-making on medication use while nursing to be difficult and very few participants felt certain when making decisions (8.6%). Parents and healthcare providers ranked the leading factor impeding decision-making to be lacking information.ConclusionMany parents and healthcare providers identified a lack of information available to help guide the decision to use medication during lactation. Findings highlight the need for better evidence and a shared decision-making tool on medication use during lactation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13006-026-00847-4.

Background

Parents may require the use of medications during lactation to treat acute or chronic health conditions which may be crucial for their physical health and well-being. More than half of women receive a prescription medication in the postpartum period [1, 2]. The use of medications during lactation is an identified barrier to continued breast/chestfeeding, due to concerns of exposure risks to the infant [3]. This is concerning as there is evidence for improved maternal and infant health outcomes associated with breast/chestfeeding for at least 6 months and uncertainty surrounding the safety of medications during this period can increase the risk for early discontinuation of breast/chestfeeding. One systematic review found that among women who required medication, breast/chestfeeding discontinuation rates were as high as 58% in women with chronic conditions compared to 2–18% in the general population [4]. One potential contributing factor is the limited information available in this population to guide decisions around medication use due to the historical exclusion of lactating women from research and drug development. One study found only 19.5% of women agreed that many drugs can be used during breastfeeding, while the majority (67.8%) disagreed and 12.5% did not know [5].

Parents depend on healthcare providers as a reliable source for breastfeeding information. However, it has been reported that women felt they were provided with contradictory information about the safety of drugs during breastfeeding from different health professionals [6]. It is critical for healthcare professionals to offer advice based on scientific evidence, however, there is often limited evidence available [7]. Furthermore, there is variability in healthcare providers’ training on medication use during lactation. In a previous study, healthcare providers stated they felt frustration and uncertainty around making clinical recommendations when there was a lack of safety data and clear guidelines [8]. It is important to understand the barriers for healthcare providers when advising on medication decisions during lactation, while also considering the preferences and experiences of the parent.

There is limited evidence on the decision-making process of parents and the role healthcare providers play in meeting their decisional needs. The decisions parents have to make may be more complex than deciding to take or not to take a medication, including whether to start, stop or alter their infant feeding practices. The process may involve consideration of different options and various factors such as the potential health effects on the infant, the mother’s health needs, values, and preferences, and the feasibility of altering the timing of medication or method of infant feeding. Therefore, it is important to evaluate the type of decisions parents have made and what factors were prioritized that led to those decisions. This will help us better understand how to best support parents and physicians to make evidence-based decisions about the risks of drug exposure and the benefits of human milk for their children.

The objectives of this study are to understand (1) preferences, factors, and needs of parents that contribute to the decision to take medication during breastfeeding; and (2) healthcare provider’s views on parents’ decisional needs and preferences regarding medication use during breastfeeding.

Methods

This study included two surveys for the following populations (1) mothers/birthing parents (2) healthcare providers (e.g., physicians, nurses, pharmacists). The survey was administered between November 2021 to February 2022. The study was approved by the Health Research Ethics Board of the University of Manitoba (approval number HS24663 (H2021:069)).

Survey development

We used the Ottawa Decision Support Framework (ODSF) [9] as the framework to guide this study. ODSF “conceptualizes the support needed by patients, families, and their practitioners for ‘difficult’ decisions with multiple options whose features are valued differently”. It guides practitioners and researchers to assess participants’ decisional needs and provide decision support interventions (e.g., patient decision tools). The workbook by Jacobsen et al: Decisional needs assessment in populations for assessing patients’ and practitioners’ decision-making needs, was used as a guide to develop the questions for the surveys in this study [10].

The survey was developed with input from an advisory steering committee and parent volunteers. Survey items collected information on impeding and facilitating factors for decision-making, and comfort level of parents’ using a medication and healthcare providers’ recommendations on medications. Communication between healthcare providers and parents was also explored. Finally, questions were included on sources of information and types of resources that would be beneficial for decision making. An advisory steering committee of parents with lived experience, physicians, pharmacists, researchers, and nurses who have an interest in research related to medications in human milk was assembled to help guide this study and provided input during the survey development. This involved two meetings. The first meeting focused on identifying priority areas of focus for the survey questions that would help inform the development of a decision aid. During this meeting, preferences and factors related to making decisions around starting, continuing or stopping medications that were identified from the literature were reviewed. Feedback was provided on the relevance of capturing specific data (e.g., asking about the specific levels of evidence using Brigg’s or Hale’s on decision making) and whether the information captured would provide meaningful input in practice. The second meeting provided an opportunity for the steering committee to provide feedback of the wording survey questions and whether they adequately address the knowledge gaps required to guide decision aid development. Furthermore, we pilot tested the surveys with six parent participants who provided verbal feedback to help us further develop and refine the survey items. This was to ensure the wording of the questions would be understood by participants and the information collected aligns with the objectives. After this phase, the wording of the survey questions was revised to improve the clarity of the questions. No formal content validity testing was done.

Participant and setting

The survey distribution was restricted to participants residing in Canada who were 18 years of age and older. Parents were eligible to participate if they were pregnant or birthed children regardless of their medication during pregnancy or lactation. We aimed to include any individual who had experienced or who has the potential to experience making decisions about using medications during lactation. We did not restrict the population to those with breastfeeding experience only as we wanted to capture the experiences of those who may have chosen not to breastfeed due to medication exposure risk concerns. Canadian healthcare providers including physicians, nurses, pharmacists and other practitioners who provide clinical services to parents with young children around medication use while infant feeding were also eligible to participate.

Recruitment

Participants for both studies were recruited through online recruitment methods using social media platforms such as parent and healthcare provider websites/groups on Facebook and Instagram. Several infant and parent groups were contacted to help advertise the surveys on their social media/web pages. Recruitment sources include Winnipeg Breastfeeding Support Group, Southeast Breastfeeding Support Group, La Leche League Canada, Winnipeg Breastfeeding Centre (@MBMilkDocs Instagram account), Healthy Start, The Birth Centre, Women’s Health Clinic, MBLB (Manitoba Lactation and Breastfeeding) Support network Facebook page, BC (British Columbia) Lactation Consultant association Facebook page, Douglas College breastfeeding course Blackboard communication system, Youville and Canadian Arthritis Patient Alliance. We also employed a snowballing method for recruitment to expand our sample and reach a broader demographic group. The survey link was shared with the steering committee and Manitoba Interdisciplinary Lactation group at the University of Manitoba to help distribute and asked to share with colleagues and parents. The survey advertisement also asked participants to share the link with others to participate in the study.

Sample size

No restrictions were made on the number of participants who can participate in this survey. The sample size is determined based on recruitment and the voluntary response of individuals.

Data collection

The survey responses were anonymous, self-reported and web-based. The questions included a mix of categorical, ranking, and free-text response questions. The survey was administered using the secure online application Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap). Data was collected over three months between November 9, 2021, to February 9, 2022. Both surveys took an estimated 20–30 min to complete and consisted of a section on background information on medication(s) used and the condition(s) they treat, demographic information, and participant’s attitudes, and preferences regarding medication use during infant feeding. Medication use in both the pregnancy and breast/chestfeeding period was captured as perspectives on medication use during breast/chestfeeding may be different between those who received a medication during pregnancy and those who did not. To capture information about preferences, parents and healthcare providers were asked to rank eight statements from most (1) to least (8) important concerning taking a medication while nursing. The parent survey also captured information about breast/chestfeeding experiences, including the intended and/or actual duration of breast/chestfeeding, challenges experienced during this period, and whether participants felt they met their breast/chestfeeding goals. This was captured to gain an understanding of the extent of current breast/chestfeeding challenges when interpreting the data on decisions about medication use during lactation. Parents also ranked statements that would make decision-making difficult with respect to medication use while breast/chestfeeding.

Data analysis

The data from the surveys were exported from REDCap and analyzed using password-protected statistical software (Microsoft Excel®). Descriptive statistics were shown as frequency, percentage and means (and standardized deviations, SD) as applicable. Qualitative data (free response questions) were quoted for questions that allow participants to add their additional comments or specify any advice they were given about medications during breast/chestfeeding (parents) and counseling experiences (healthcare providers), the content was analyzed for patterns and themes to allow a deeper understanding of the input from participants. A final meeting with the steering committee was held to review the study findings and input on the interpretation of results.

Results

Parent survey results

There were 165 parent participants who completed the survey where data was collected for analysis.

Demographic characteristics

The majority of respondents were in the age category between 30 and 39 years old (70.9%) and of white/European descent (89.1%) (Table 1). Many participants had formal education with completed college/university (45.5%). Most of the respondents had a household income higher than $80,000 (68.9%), resided in Manitoba (64.0%) and lived with a partner (94.4%). In addition, most of the parents had one (57.9%) or two (32.9%) biological children. The majority of participants (93.9%) indicated they had fed directly from the breast/chest within three months postpartum, with 56.4% using pumped milk in bottles and 35.2% using formula.

Medications and medical conditions

Over half (55.2%) of the participants indicated they did not have a chronic health condition during pregnancy or postpartum. Slightly over one-fourth (26.7%) of respondents had a chronic health condition during both pregnancy and postpartum, with 6.7% indicated they experienced a chronic health condition during pregnancy only and 10.3% with a chronic health condition during the post-postpartum period only (Table 1). The majority of parents took two medications (excluding products) during pregnancy and within six months postpartum (31.5% and 32.1%, respectively; Table 2).

Factors influencing parents’ comfort towards medication use during breast/chestfeeding

Parents indicated that they were very uncomfortable (51.8%) or uncomfortable (27.3%) about using a medication when there were no studies on the risk of infant exposure through breast/chest-feeding (Fig. 1a). Parents were also very uncomfortable (15.1%) or uncomfortable (44.6%) about using a medication if it was their first time using the medication. In comparison, if they had used the medication during pregnancy, a small percentage was very uncomfortable (2.9%) or uncomfortable (8.6%) with using the medication. When the medication had been taken by many people without evidence of adverse effects on nursing infants’ parents were comfortable (48.9%) and very comfortable (18.7%) with using the medication. Parents were mostly comfortable (44.9%) or very comfortable (17.4%) and neutral (31.9%) about taking a medication if their healthcare provider thinks the medication is a good choice for them. The majority of parents were neutral/comfortable using prescription medication if it is used to treat a chronic (50.0%/28.6%) or acute (42.4%/42.4%) illness.

Parent’s decision-making on medication use during breast/chestfeeding

More than half (n = 76/139, 54.7%) of respondents found the decision to take a medication while breast/chestfeeding difficult. When asked about how they feel when making decisions about taking medications during nursing, almost half (n = 68/140, 48.6%) of the parents worried about what could go wrong, followed by wavering between choices or changing your mind (n = 49/140, 35.0%) and unsure about what to do (n = 44/140, 31.4%) (Fig. 2). One-third of parents (n = 45/140, 32.1%) responded they were comfortable making the decision and few parents selected they felt certain about what to do (n = 12/140, 8.6%).

Healthcare provider survey results

Forty-seven healthcare providers completed the survey where data was collected for analysis.

Demographic characteristics

The majority of respondents were in the age category between 30 and 39 (70.2%), identified as a woman (89.4%), and were primarily of white/European descent (68.9%) (Table 1). The majority of the participants practiced in an urban setting (76.6%) at outpatient clinics (68.9%) followed by hospital settings (46.7%). Almost all the participants were physicians (93.6%) and over half (57.8%) of the participant’s area of practice was in primary care/family medicine. Over half (53.2%) of the respondent had less than 5 years in practice and a little over one-third (36.2%) had between 5 and 10 years of experience. Around 40% of the providers interacted with patients’ with young children less than 25% of the time in their practice, and almost a quarter of the providers interacted with these patients between 25 and 49% of the time in their practice.

Factors influencing healthcare providers’ comfort towards medication use during breast/chestfeeding

Most of the providers indicated they were uncomfortable (65.5%) or very uncomfortable (20.7%) recommending a prescription medication when there were no studies on the risk of infant exposure through breast/chestfeeding (Fig. 1b). When the medication had been taken by many people without evidence of adverse effects on nursing infants, providers were mostly comfortable (58.6%) recommending a medication. Practitioners were very comfortable (51.7%) and comfortable (37.9%) recommending using a medication if controlled studies have not shown evidence of risk. If parents had used the medication in the past while nursing with no evidence of adverse effects on the nursing infant, practitioners were mostly neutral (44.8%) or comfortable (37.9%) about recommending using the medication. Practitioners felt generally comfortable (35.7%) and neutral (28.6%) making recommendations about using a medication if parents are able to space the timing of the medication during infant feeding.

Healthcare providers’ communication with parents on medication use during lactation was explored with a series of questions (Supplemental Fig. 1). Providers would routinely always (24.1%), often (27.6%) and sometimes (37.9%) ask parents if they were breast/chestfeeding when discussing a medication. Almost half of providers would often (34.5%) or always (13.8%) ask parents if they were breast/chestfeeding if the parent had a child older than 12 months. Almost all the providers often (51.7%) or always (44.8%) consider parents’ values when giving recommendations. Largely, providers’ advice on medication use while nursing rarely (34.5%) or sometimes (44.8%) varies based on the infant’s age.

Healthcare provider’s decision-making on medication use during breast/chestfeeding

When recommending a medication, 65.5% of healthcare providers indicated they share the decision with parents and 58.6% indicated they provide support/advice for parents to make the decision on their own. None of the providers who participated in the survey indicated that they made the decision for the parents.

The most important factors that make it difficult for parents to make a decision about medication use during lactation from the perspective of healthcare providers included, “lacking information about options, benefits, and risks of medication” followed by “parental fear/anxiety/expectations/ideas/feelings on medication use while lactation”, and “conflicting information from healthcare providers” (Table 3). Social pressure to not take a medication was fifth and pressure from family and friends was rated eighth. “Feeling unsupported in decision-making” was selected as the least important.

Healthcare providers’ confidence on medication use during breast/chestfeeding

Out of the twenty-nine participants that responded, around half (51.7%) answered that there are specific medications or medication classes that they are not confident in providing counselling to parents. Specific classes of medications mentioned include psychiatric medications (5 quotations), pain medications (3 quotations), immunosuppressants (3 quotations), and blood pressure medications (2 quotations) (Supplemental Table 1).

Discussion

Findings from this study provided important insight into Canadian parents’ and healthcare practitioners’ preferences and decision-making factors around medication use during lactation. This study was able to explore gaps, specifically evaluating factors that were considered important to parents’ decision-making process.

Over half of the parents in this study found decision-making on medication use while nursing to be difficult and almost half of parents indicated that they worried about what could go wrong when making decisions about taking a medication during nursing. These findings highlight the challenges parents face when it comes to making decisions about medications during this period and the potential impact this might have on the wellbeing of caregivers should be explored further. This study also found more than half of participants deliberately did not start or stopped a medication during breast/chestfeeding. These findings were consistent with previous research and may indicate that parents who breast/chestfeed could be at risk of not being adequately treated for a health condition [4, 11]. Understanding the difficulties surrounding medication use during lactation is important as the trend in chronic disease burden on pregnant and postpartum individuals is increasing [12, 13]. It is evident that while lactating individuals have historically been excluded in clinical trials, many breast/chestfeeding individuals may require medications during the postpartum period in the real-world [14].

Our study also found that almost a quarter of participants stopped breast/chestfeeding in order to use a medication. Given the challenges many parents encountered during lactation, it is possible that uncertainty about medication safety during nursing could be a contributing factor to the decision to discontinue nursing, even if not the primary reason [15]. It is unsurprising that prescription medications with no available studies on the risk of infant exposure through human milk created the most discomfort about using or recommending the use of a medication during lactation for both parents and healthcare providers. Both parents and healthcare providers indicated the lack of information about options, benefits and risks of medications as the most important factor that make decision-making around medication use during this period difficult. This should signal the need for regulatory mandates to ensure pharmaceutical companies provide parents and healthcare providers with safety and efficacy data relevant to lactation. While discussing the risk versus benefit of medications is considered an important component of medication counseling for parents who are breast/chestfeeding [16], this conversation with patients would be difficult with a lack of available evidence to substantiate the potential risks relative to benefits of medications during breastfeeding. Psychiatric and immunosuppressant medications were more commonly identified by healthcare providers as medications where they were the most uncertain about during lactation. Improved evidence, education, and decision-aid tools may greatly help providers in this area. Findings from this study can help prioritize future research efforts on medications that are most used by parents along with medications healthcare providers feel less confident in providing advice.

The majority of parents in our study reported receiving medication advice during lactation by a family doctor with approximately a third of parents receiving advice from their pharmacist. However, a few parents in our study indicated they received conflicting advice from healthcare providers, and conflicting information from healthcare providers was ranked high among healthcare providers as contributing to the difficulty in making decisions about medications. These findings support the need for adequate training in this area among family physicians and pharmacists to ensure education from healthcare providers provide consistent information to patients and based on best available evidence.

There are a few limitations of this survey study that should be noted. Although efforts were made to recruit a diverse population by contacting various parent groups and organizations, the majority of the participants were of white ethnicity, highly educated, had a high household income and lived in Manitoba. This may limit the generalizability of our findings to those from minoritized populations. However, this also highlights that parent participants included in this study were from high socioeconomic backgrounds and education achievement, and had challenges during lactation in addition to feeling uncomfortable with decisions related to medication use. The impact of these issues in parents of lower socioeconomic status or lower health literacy is unknown. There could be disparity in rates of early breast/chestfeeding cessation and increased chronic disease burden by economic and education status that warrants further study. Future studies should specifically target underrepresented groups to include more people from different demographics and future versions of this survey can include a version in other languages to reach additional communities. Another limitation of the survey was that it was only delivered online, therefore we were not able to reach individuals that did not have access to the internet. Moreover, there may be potential for selection bias towards recruiting those with stronger feelings about medication use during lactation being more likely to respond from social media recruitment. There was also a small sample size for the healthcare provider survey, and almost all the healthcare providers were physicians, which may limit the generalizability to non-physician healthcare providers. It is possible that fewer pharmacists and nurses interact with the social media and websites within our advertising and dissemination strategy. Moreover, most healthcare participants included in this study were 30–39 years old in family medicine, yet they reported being uncomfortable in their knowledge and decisions about medication use in lactation. For future versions of the survey, we can contact the appropriate associations for the different healthcare providers’ specialties to distribute the survey in an attempt to capture a variety of practitioners who work with post-partum parents. Lastly, we did not specifically capture the role of lactation consultants as a resource in both surveys, which could be an important influence on breast/chestfeeding continuation and possibly medication decision-making. However, based on feedback from the advisory committee it was unclear how much medication-specific recommendations are made by lactation consultants. This could be considered in future research.

The strength of this study is that the survey study design allowed us to gather data from the free-text responses to gain a deeper understanding of parents’ and healthcare providers’ thoughts and experiences. This study also used an advisory group, which included individuals with lived experience, to help inform the survey design. The study findings support the need for reliable and consistent resources on medication use during lactation and were a recurring theme by both parents and healthcare providers. Parents valued healthcare providers as reliable sources for information and trusted their advice when decision-making. Therefore, a shared decision-making tool that can facilitate better communication of evidence-based information between parents and their healthcare providers, in addition to taking into account parent values and preferences, could improve parents’ decision qualities on medication use during lactation. Future studies could focus on assessing decisional needs of commonly used medications by postpartum parents and medications that were changed postpartum to develop decision aids that can be used in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study evaluated parents’ decision-making process on medication use during lactation and healthcare providers’ role in parents’ decision-making. Specifically, this study was able to examine the factors considered important for parents during decision-making. Parents and healthcare providers (most respondents were physicians) would benefit from improved educational material and reliable sources for information on medication use during lactation to help improve communication in this area. Priority should be made to assess the reliability and consistency of the available resources on commonly used medication by postpartum parents and assess the decisional needs of parents and healthcare providers, including taking parents’ values and preferences into account during these decisions. Findings from this study also support the need for higher quality evidence for studying medications during lactation as many individuals report using a medication during this period. This is important as many individuals require medications during lactation and may be using medication without enough information on the relative risks versus benefits of taking the medication during this period. Future versions of this survey should focus on reaching underrepresented demographic groups. Findings from this study suggest a shared decision-making tool could be beneficial for clinical practice, particularly for psychiatric and immunosuppressant medications. A shared decision-making tool will help clarify parent’s values and preferences and provide decision support.
