Mapping cellular interactions and communication landscapes in cervical cancer via single-cell transcriptomics
Authors: Shaoli Zhuang, Xiaoxiao Deng, Furong Fu, Zhang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical cancer is among the prevalent gynecological malignancies affecting women worldwide. The diverse cellular populations and their intricate communication patterns within the tumor environment significantly influence cancer development and metastasis. Our research focused on elucidating the cellular diversity, intercellular signaling networks, and underlying molecular processes in cervical cancer tissues via single-cell transcriptomic approaches.MethodsPublicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data for cervical cancer (GSE236738) were re-analysed to delineate the cellular composition and transcriptional programs of the tumour microenvironment. We applied UMAP and t-SNE algorithms for dimensionality reduction, alongside intercellular communication mapping, MIF pathway analysis, temporal trajectory modeling, and pathway enrichment studies, to comprehensively characterize the cellular architecture and molecular profiles within cervical cancer tissues. In addition, paired tumour and adjacent non-tumour cervical tissues from 20 patients were subjected to quantitative real-time PCR and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay to confirm the transcriptional and protein-level expression of key genes identified by scRNA-seq.ResultsAnalysis revealed substantial cellular and molecular diversity within cervical cancer specimens. Ten distinct cellular populations were characterized, including NK lymphocytes, epithelial components, and macrophage subsets, indicating pronounced cellular heterogeneity. Intercellular communication mapping revealed that stromal and epithelial populations are the primary coordinators of cell-to-cell signaling. MIF pathway activity peaked in cancer-associated fibroblasts, which exhibited distinct cell type distributions. Temporal analysis revealed dynamic cellular states and pinpointed critical regulatory elements, including ISG15. Pathway analysis emphasized the importance of neutrophil activity, mitochondrial respiration, and cell adhesion mechanisms in cancer progression. qPCR confirmed that ISG15 and TNFRSF18 were significantly upregulated in tumor tissues, while SDF4 was downregulated and NOC2L showed a moderate increase. ELISA results were consistent with transcript-level findings, demonstrating significant protein overexpression of ISG15 and TNFRSF18 in tumor tissues compared with controls.ConclusionOur investigation comprehensively characterized the cellular diversity and molecular features of the cervical cancer microenvironments.

Introduction

Cervical carcinoma is one of the most common cancers in women worldwide, presenting significant health challenges, particularly in areas with limited resources. Understanding the complex molecular mechanisms underlying the development of cervical cancer is crucial for devising effective therapeutic approaches, even while preventive measures such as HPV vaccination and cytological screening have shown promise [1–3]. By enabling the examination of malignant tissues at the single-cell level, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revolutionized tumor biology research. This technology provides a comprehensive assessment of individual cellular components within tumor ecosystems by revealing the cellular diversity and intricate intercellular interactions that traditional bulk analysis approaches fail to detect [4–6].

Malignant epithelial cells, diverse immune cell populations, supportive stromal elements, and vascular cells constitute the remarkably complex tissue architecture of cervical cancer. Understanding how these distinct cell types interact and coordinate signaling is critical for elucidating cancer progression and developing potential therapies [7–10]. Particular attention has been directed toward intercellular communication systems and molecular pathways, notably the macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) cascade, which may contribute significantly to tumor initiation and development. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of stromal interactions and immune cell infiltration in cervical cancer progression. However, a comprehensive understanding of the cellular composition, developmental trajectories, and signaling pathways within the cervical cancer microenvironment is still lacking [11–13]. Furthermore, additional studies are needed to uncover the molecular mechanisms underlying these interactions and their therapeutic potential.

In this study, we extensively characterized the cervical cancer microenvironment using scRNA-seq combined with advanced computational approaches. Our goal was to develop a comprehensive understanding of the cellular and molecular architecture of cervical cancer by integrating several analytical strategies, including dimensionality reduction, intercellular communication mapping, developmental trajectory analysis, and functional pathway enrichment. This systematic approach enabled us to identify key cellular populations, delineate their interactions, and uncover potential therapeutic targets that could guide the development of more effective treatment strategies.

Our research advances the basic understanding of the mechanisms underlying cervical cancer and may provide insights for the design of tailored treatment plans and targeted therapies. By closely examining the intricate cellular and molecular networks within tumor ecosystems, we sought to identify novel therapeutic avenues that could improve clinical outcomes in cervical cancer.

Methods

Data source and tissue processing

Publicly available single-cell RNA-seq data for cervical cancer were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE236738. Raw data were processed and analyzed as described below. In addition, A total of 20 cervical cancer tissue samples were collected from patients undergoing surgery or biopsy. Tumor tissues were histologically confirmed by pathologists, and a group of normal cervical tissues was used as controls, obtained from patients without cancer who underwent hysterectomy for benign conditions. Fresh tissues were transported on ice in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin and processed within 2 h. Samples were minced into ~ 1 mm³ pieces and enzymatically dissociated using 1 mg/mL collagenase IV (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Roche, Switzerland) in RPMI-1640 at 37 °C for 30 min with gentle agitation. The digested cell suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer, washed twice with PBS containing 0.04% BSA, and subjected to dead cell removal using the Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). Viability was assessed by trypan blue exclusion, and only samples with a viability > 85% were processed further. These tissue samples were used exclusively for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to validate selected candidate genes and cytokines identified from the scRNA-seq analysis.

Single-cell library construction and sequencing

Single-cell RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits v3 (10x Genomics, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (paired-end 150 bp) with a target depth of at least 50,000 reads per cell.

Data quality assessment and initial processing

We used a single-cell RNA profiling approach to examine the tissue microenvironment of cervical cancer. Sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (v6.1.1, 10x Genomics) for demultiplexing, alignment to the GRCh38 human reference genome, and UMI counting. Downstream analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.2) using the Seurat package (v4.3.0). Cells expressing fewer than 200 genes, more than 2,500 genes, or >10% mitochondrial transcripts were excluded. Genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells were removed. Highly variable genes were identified using the “vst” method. Batch effects between samples were corrected using Harmony (v0.1.0). In order to identify genes with high variability by normalized variance computations, the analytical workflow began with a comprehensive quality assessment of transcriptome datasets, which included gene expression variance assessments [14, 15].In order to characterize basic molecular features, hierarchical grouping techniques were used in addition to principal component analysis to assess expression patterns. A number of important variable genes were identified by this research, including MZB1, GNLY, CCL4L2, TRBV28, and CD79A. These genes are mostly associated with B lymphocyte activity and immunological responses.

Cell type identification and dimensionality reduction

Two complementary dimensional reduction strategies were used in the cellular diversity examination: the t-SNE algorithm and the UMAP algorithm. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed, and the first 30 PCs were selected based on an elbow plot and JackStraw test. Cells were clustered using the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 0.5. Ten different cellular populations were successfully identified and classified in tumor environments thanks to these approaches. These populations included natural killer lymphocytes, epithelial components, macrophage subsets, neuron-like populations, cancer-associated fibroblasts, plasma cells, stromal elements, endothelial components, neutrophil populations, and regulatory T lymphocytes [16, 17].Significant variations between PC1–PC15 were found by statistical analysis of principal component importance (PC1: p = 1.424e-230, PC2: p = 5.61e-121, PC3: p = 1.9e-133).

Cell type annotation

Cell clusters were annotated based on canonical marker genes, including EPCAM and KRT19 (epithelial cells), CD3D and CD8A (T cells), CD68 and CD163 (macrophages), COL1A1 (fibroblasts), and PECAM1 (endothelial cells). Marker selection was guided by the CellMarker and PanglaoDB databases. Annotation was further cross-validated using the SingleR package (v1.10.0).

Intercellular signaling network examination

In-depth studies of intercellular signaling networks were used to examine patterns of cellular interaction. Cell–cell communication was inferred using CellPhoneDB (v2.1.7) with 1,000 permutations. Only ligand–receptor pairs with p < 0.05 were considered significant. MIF pathway–specific analyses were performed using CellChat (v1.5.0) with the built-in ligand–receptor database. With a focus on stromal and epithelial components, which exhibit the strongest interconnections, this method measures the frequency and strength of connections between different cellular populations [18–20]. To find unique interaction features, cell-specific analyses were carried out, concentrating on key populations such as macrophages, stromal cells, epithelial cells, and cancer-associated fibroblasts.

MIF pathway network investigation

Circular network visualization was used in MIF signaling cascade research to show the linkages between pathways in different cellular populations.The strength of intercellular communication was measured using heatmap representations, which also looked at the expression patterns of key MIF pathway receptors, including as MIF, CD74, CXCR4, CD44, and CXCR2, across various cellular groupings. Cell-specific signaling traits were identified by this analysis, with cancer-associated fibroblasts exhibiting the highest levels of MIF expression.

Pseudotime and trajectory analysis

Pseudotime trajectories were reconstructed using Monocle 2 (v2.24.0). Genes with high dispersion were selected for ordering, and DDRTree was used for dimensionality reduction. Branch points and state assignments were identified using the BEAM method. Developmental trajectory analysis was used to study cellular state evolution, shedding light on the advancement of cellular states in cervical cancer. This method entails tracking changes in the expression of six important genes under various cellular circumstances: ISG15, NOC2L, SDF4, TNFRSF18, TNFRSF4, and UBE2J2. The research showed the distinct temporal distribution features of different cellular populations along developmental trajectories and revealed three main differentiation pathways [21].

Differential gene expression and functional enrichment

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between clusters were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in Seurat (v4.3.0), with log2 fold change >0.25 and adjusted p < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction). GO enrichment analysis for Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC) was conducted using clusterProfiler (v4.6.2) with the GO.db (v3.15.0) database. The methodology for functional enrichment analysis was performed following established practices [22]. To identify functionally related gene modules, gene expression clustering was applied, which included groups associated with cytokine/chemokine factors, immune response components, and extracellular matrix and collagen components. The analysis demonstrated unique temporal properties of different genetic modules as well as cell-specific expression characteristics.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis for key gene expression

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent, and RNA purity was assessed spectrophotometrically, with A260/A280 ratios ranging from 1.8 to 2.0. RNA integrity was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Genomic DNA was removed, and RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan) in a 20 µL reaction mixture incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. qPCR was performed using SYBR Green chemistry in a 20 µL reaction volume. The amplification protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was performed at the end of the reaction to confirm amplification specificity.

The target genes analyzed included ISG15 (120 bp; F: CTCTGAGCATCCTGGTGAGGAA, R: AAGGTCAGCCAGAACAGGTCGT), NOC2L (132 bp; F: CGGCACAAGAAGGACACTTTCC, R: TCCGTCAAGGTCCACTGCATGA), SDF4 (110 bp; F: GGAGAGCAAGACACACTTCCGC, R: GAGTTCCTCGTTGAGCCTGATG), and TNFRSF18 (150 bp; F: CCAGTGTATCGACTGTGCCTCG, R: CACAGCGTTGTGGGTCTTGTTC). The housekeeping gene GAPDH (119 bp; F: GGGCTTTATTGACAAGATTGCT, R: TCGGTAGTCTGACTGAGTGGC) was used as an internal control. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method based on Ct values.

Enzyme-Linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Fresh cervical cancer and matched adjacent non-tumor tissues were homogenized in PBS (pH 7.4) containing protease inhibitors on ice. Homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and total protein in the supernatants was quantified using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

ELISA kits for MIF, CXCL12, and TGF-β1 (R&D Systems, USA) were used according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, equal amounts of total protein (100 µg/mL) from each sample were loaded in triplicate wells of 96-well plates, incubated with capture and detection antibodies, followed by HRP-streptavidin, and developed with TMB substrate. Absorbance was read at 450 nm (BioTek Synergy H1, USA), and cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves and normalized to total protein (pg/mg).

Statistical methods

Throughout the inquiry, appropriate statistical techniques were used to guarantee the relevance and dependability of the results. Statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.2) and GraphPad Prism (v9.0). Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA was used for group comparisons, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

The cellular and molecular landscapes of cervical cancer habitats are thoroughly understood thanks to this integrated analytical approach, which also offers valuable insights into the mechanisms behind the disease’s progression and possible treatment options.

Validation of key gene expression

Quantitative PCR was performed to assess the expression levels of ISG15, NOC2L, SDF4, and TNFRSF18 in cervical cancer tissues and adjacent normal tissues. ISG15 was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues (P < 0.01), suggesting a potential role in tumor cell proliferation and immune regulation, consistent with the regulatory role indicated by pseudotime trajectory analysis. NOC2L was also elevated in cancer tissues (P < 0.05), which may be associated with transcriptional repression during tumor development. In contrast, SDF4 was significantly downregulated (P < 0.05), implying a potential role in endoplasmic reticulum calcium homeostasis and stress responses. As a key receptor involved in T cell modulation, TNFRSF18 was markedly overexpressed in tumor samples (P < 0.01), indicating its possible contribution to immune evasion and the formation of a pro-inflammatory microenvironment (Fig. 9). Collectively, the qPCR results validated the dynamic expression patterns of ISG15, NOC2L, SDF4, and TNFRSF18 in cervical cancer tissues, supporting their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets in cervical cancer progression.

Discussion

The research of cervical cancer using single-cell sequencing (scRNA-seq) has advanced significantly in recent years. This method can provide novel insights for cervical cancer diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis by revealing the heterogeneity of tumor cells, the complexity of the tumor microenvironment (TME), and the dynamic changes in immune cells at the single-cell level [23–25].

The deep heterogeneity of cervical cancer cells, including varying gene expression patterns and biological roles, has been made visible by single-cell sequencing technologies. In cervical cancer, for instance, research has shown several tumor cell subpopulations, some of which exhibit significant expression of genes linked to EMT, suggesting a higher risk of invasion and metastasis [26–28].

Ten different cell types were identified by our single-cell RNA sequencing research, which demonstrated the cervical cancer microenvironment’s amazing biological variety. These various cell types, such as stromal cells, immune cells, and cancer-associated fibroblasts, have been identified, which highlights the TME’s complexity. An active immune response within the tumor microenvironment was suggested by the notable presence of a significant fraction of immune cells. This result supports earlier research showing the role of immune surveillance in the development of cervical cancer. These findings are in agreement with recent single-cell studies in cervical cancer, which have delineated immune cell remodeling, epithelial heterogeneity, and stromal–tumor crosstalk using similar analytical approaches [29–31].

ISG15 (interferon-stimulated gene 15), a crucial regulatory gene, has garnered a lot of attention in our study of the intercellular interactions and communication landscape of cervical cancer due to its role and mechanism in malignancies. ISG15, which has ubiquitin-like modifying capabilities, was initially recognized as a crucial molecule in the antiviral response. It can use ISGylation modification to control the stability and functionality of target proteins. However, more and more research indicates that ISG15 has a far wider function in cancer than only antiviral activity. We discovered that ISG15 has two roles in different types of cancer: ISG15 can, on the one hand, encourage the formation of tumors by controlling the cell cycle, encouraging cell division, and preventing apoptosis; However, by strengthening the immune system’s anti-tumor reaction, ISG15 may help prevent tumor growth. Our single-cell RNA sequencing data in cervical cancer showed that tumor cells, immune cells, and stromal cells exhibit substantial variation in ISG15 expression patterns across several cellular states.

According to our research, there may be a significant role for ISG15 in boosting tumor cell proliferation because of the favorable correlation between its high expression in the tumor microenvironment and the expression of cell proliferation markers like Ki-67.Furthermore, the overexpression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 is linked to high expression of ISG15, suggesting that it may improve tumor cell survival by blocking the apoptotic pathway. More significantly, ISG15’s involvement in tumor cell invasion and migration cannot be disregarded. We found that the expression of genes associated to the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), including Vimentin and N-cadherin, was strongly correlated with the overexpression of ISG15 expression. This suggests that ISG15 may have a regulatory role in encouraging cell invasion and migration.

Analysis of the cell communication network showed complex patterns of interaction, with stromal and epithelial cells showing up as important intercellular communication mediators.

Strong connections between stromal cells and other cell types indicate that they play a vital part in coordinating the TME. These discoveries offer fresh perspectives on the intricate cellular relationships that could affect the course of tumors and possibly suggest new targets for treatment.

The highest MIF expression was found in cancer-associated fibroblasts, according to our investigation of the MIF signaling pathway, which also revealed cell type-specific expression patterns. This finding implies that cancer-associated fibroblasts might be essential in using MIF signaling to modify the tumor microenvironment. Our observation is consistent with clinical and experimental evidence that the MIF–CD74 axis in tumor-associated macrophages and epithelial cells promotes immune evasion and correlates with poor prognosis in cervical cancer, and that CD74 blockade can enhance responses to PD-1 inhibition [32–35]. A complicated regulatory network that may be the focus of therapeutic intervention is indicated by the varied expression of MIF receptors in different cell types.

Single-cell sequencing technique can be utilized to examine immune cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells in the tumor microenvironment in addition to tumor cells. In the cervical cancer microenvironment, for instance, research has identified several immune cell subpopulations, such as germinal center B cells (GCBs), clonally expanded T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs). Tumor immune tolerance and antitumor immune responses are significantly influenced by these cell subpopulations. Furthermore, the metabolic features of the tumor microenvironment have been uncovered by single-cell sequencing, including elevated expression of genes linked to metabolism in tumor-associated endothelial cells (TECs).

Dynamic cell state transitions and the temporal advancement of several cell types during tumor formation were both discovered using pseudotime analysis. The finding that neutrophils and regulatory T cells predominate in later stages while macrophages and endothelial cells dominate in early stages points to a coordinated series of biological events in tumor growth. The discovery of important regulatory genes, namely ISG15, offers possible targets for treatment at different phases of the course of the illness. The development of targeted therapeutics may benefit from a thorough description of the tumor microenvironment and the identification of important cellular connections. The qPCR results showed that ISG15 and TNFRSF18 were significantly upregulated in tumor samples, supporting their roles in cell proliferation and immune regulation. NOC2L also exhibited an upward trend, suggesting its involvement in transcriptional regulation. In contrast, SDF4 was downregulated, which may be associated with impaired endoplasmic reticulum stress-related functions. These findings are highly consistent with the single-cell analysis and further reinforce the potential roles of these genes in cervical cancer progression. Our results further align with recent evidence that tumor-derived ISG15 not only regulates protein stability through ISGylation but also modulates natural killer (NK) cell activity, thereby contributing to immune evasion [36, 37]. In breast and colorectal cancers, elevated ISG15 has been shown to dampen NK-mediated cytotoxicity and reshape the tumor immune microenvironment toward a tolerogenic phenotype [38]. Similar mechanisms may be operative in cervical cancer, as suggested by the co-occurrence of high ISG15 expression and elevated levels of immunosuppressive cytokines such as TGF-β1 and IL-10 in our dataset. These findings are supported by direct clinical evidence of ISG15 overexpression promoting proliferation and invasion in cervical cancer [39] and by mechanistic studies showing that ISGylation modulates key tumor suppressors, such as SIRT1, thereby affecting p53 acetylation and tumor progression [40].

In addition, recent reviews highlight the functional plasticity of γδ T cells and regulatory T cells in shaping the tumor immune landscape and influencing responses to immune checkpoint blockade, underscoring the need to consider these subsets in therapeutic design [41, 42]. Equally important, inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages profoundly modulate the cervical cancer microenvironment, driving immune evasion, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling [43–45].

Our findings also have important translational implications. Integrating single-cell transcriptomics with other multi-omics technologies, such as epigenomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, could provide a more comprehensive understanding of tumor heterogeneity and identify robust biomarkers for patient stratification. Such integrative approaches hold promise for uncovering therapeutic vulnerabilities that may not be apparent from transcriptomic data alone. Moreover, coupling multi-omics analyses with drug development pipelines has the potential to accelerate the discovery of novel therapeutic targets and optimize strategies for precision medicine [46, 47]. By contextualizing our cell–cell communication networks within this broader framework, our study underscores the clinical value of leveraging multi-omics integration to translate molecular insights into actionable therapeutic interventions in cervical cancer.

Future studies should concentrate on confirming these results in broader patient groups and examining the therapeutic possibilities of focusing on particular cell types or signaling pathways that were found in this investigation. Furthermore, the temporal dynamics of cell state transitions indicate how crucial it is to take timing into account when developing therapeutic approaches.

Limitations

Despite giving us a high-resolution record of biological heterogeneity, single-cell RNA sequencing has some drawbacks. Our validation cohort included 20 cervical cancer patients (n = 20), which may not fully represent the diversity of the disease across broader populations. As a result of our small sample size, the research findings might not accurately reflect the diversity of cervical cancer. In large-sample research, the biological variations between patients may be more fully represented. Second, technical noise and deviation are significant limiting factors that can impact the data’s accuracy and repeatability. Despite the remarkable sensitivity of single-cell RNA sequencing technologies, processing and analysis invariably involve some mistakes. Therefore, to ensure the robustness and broad applicability of the research findings, larger-scale, multi-center research and the combination of additional sophisticated tools will be required in the future to validate and expand our findings.

Conclusion

To sum up, our research offers a thorough description of the milieu surrounding cervical cancer, exposing intricate molecular relationships and possible treatment targets.
