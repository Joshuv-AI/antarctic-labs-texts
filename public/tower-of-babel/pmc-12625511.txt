Prevalence of infertility and its risk factors in Sub-Saharan Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Getachew Muluye Gedef, Eden Bishaw Taye, Osman Yimer Mohammed, Marta Yimam Abegaz, Melaku Hunie Asratie, Fantahun Andualem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfertility is a significant public health concern affecting millions of couples globally, with a particularly high prevalence in Sub-Saharan Africa, where its social and psychological impacts are profound. Despite advances in reproductive health research, comprehensive data on infertility in the region remain limited, and existing studies often yield inconsistent and inconclusive findings. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis conducted to estimate the prevalence of infertility and identify its risk factors in Sub-Saharan Africa.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search of electronic databases and grey literature using predefined search terms. Original articles published in English between January 1, 2000, and May 30, 2024, reporting the prevalence and/or associated factors of infertility in Sub-Saharan Africa were included. The review followed PRISMA guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023428778). Data were extracted using the Joanna Briggs Institute tool. A random-effects meta-analysis using STATA version 14 generated pooled prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q and I² statistics; publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test.ResultSixteen primary studies from ten Sub-Saharan African countries, comprising a total of 22,303 participants were included in the review. The pooled prevalence of infertility in the region was 16.98% (95% CI: 11.62, 22.33%). Factors such as obstetric complications (OR = 7.94, 95% CI: 4.74, 13.30), STIs / PID (OR = 3.24; 95% CI: 2.33, 4.52), substance use (OR = 3.84, 95% CI: 1.71, 8.63), and women’s age (OR = 2.19, CI: 1.48, 3.22) were identified as significant risk factors of infertility.ConclusionsNearly one in six couples in Sub-Saharan Africa experience infertility. Key contributing factors include STIs/PID, substance use, and obstetric complications. These findings highlight the need for targeted, context-specific reproductive health interventions and policies to mitigate the burden of infertility in the region.Keywords: Infertility, Prevalence, Risk factors, Meta-analysis, Sub-Saharan Africa

Introduction

Infertility is defined as the inability of a heterosexual couple to conceive after 12 months or more of regular unprotected sexual intercourse [1]. It can be primary when a pregnancy has never been achieved by a person or secondary infertility when at least one prior pregnancy has been achieved.

Infertility is a major public health issue, affecting approximately one in six people of reproductive age globally during their lifetime. The prevalence of infertility ranges from 10% to 15% of couples worldwide, with noticeable regional variations. Low- and middle-income countries face unique challenges, as fertility care is often limited, and treatments such as assisted reproductive technology (ART) is not universally accessible​ [2].

Infertility represents a significant personal, social, and public health challenge, particularly in developing countries. It often results in psychological distress and social stigma, including exclusion, abuse, and marital discord. Especially for women, it can diminish quality of life and increase the risk of engaging with multiple sexual partners, and exposure to sexually transmitted infections and sexual dysfunction [3–5].

In Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), infertility poses a significant challenge, with prevalence rates reaching as high as 30% in some populations. Cultural stigma further exacerbates the issue, often resulting in social isolation and psychological distress [6–8]. In Ethiopia, it is a pressing concern, affecting a considerable number of couples, with prevalence rates estimated between 10% and 20% due to various biological and socio-economic factors [9, 10].

The primary underlying cause of high infertility rates in SSA is attributed to infections, particularly sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Other contributing factors include advanced maternal age, malnutrition, and inadequate access to healthcare during pregnancy and childbirth. Furthermore, a lack of awareness regarding reproductive health and limited access to fertility treatments further complicate the case in the region [6–8].

Despite advancements in reproductive health research, comprehensive data on infertility in SSA remain limited, with existing individual studies often yielding inconsistent and inconclusive results. To address this gap, the current study undertakes a systematic review and meta-analysis designed to synthesize these diverse findings and provide a more reliable estimate.

Although a systematic review has been conducted at the continental level, there remains a need to explore regional variations. Furthermore, the last review in the region was published more than two decades ago (2000). Hence, region-specific meta-analyses focusing on SSA and reflecting more recent trends in reproductive health are warranted.

By synthesizing evidence on both the prevalence and predictors of infertility, this study aims to generate insights that can guide regionally tailored interventions and inform health policies addressing the root causes of infertility in SSA.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11]. The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD42023428778).

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, and African Journals Online, as well as search engines (Google/Google Scholar) and grey literature sources. We also searched African Index Medicus to capture studies potentially underrepresented in global databases.

Keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to infertility were used individually and in combination using Boolean operators “OR” and “AND”. The search was limited to human studies published in English from January 1, 2000, to May 30, 2024.

The searches were conducted using the following search terms derived from the study questions: (Infertility) OR (Childlessness) AND ((Prevalence) OR (Incidence) OR (Magnitude) OR (Proportion) OR (Epidemiology)) AND (“Associated Factors” OR “Risk Factors” OR “Determinants” OR “Risk factors” OR “Etiology”) AND (“Sub-Saharan Africa” OR “Countries”), limited to English language studies.

Additionally, the references of retrieved articles were examined to identify further relevant articles and a manual search for grey literature was performed by the investigators.

Eligibility criteria

We included original research articles published in English between January 1, 2000, and May 30, 2024, that reported on the prevalence of infertility and/or its associated factors in Sub-Saharan African countries. All observational studies addressing infertility prevalence and/or associated factors were considered eligible. Studies were excluded if they were reviews or if the full text was inaccessible (i.e., if the authors were contacted via email but did not respond within two weeks).

Study selection process

First, all retrieved studies were exported to EndNote to check for duplication; then duplicated articles were excluded from the study. Two authors screened independently by carefully reading their titles and abstracts. Studies referencing the outcomes of the review (infertility prevalence and/or risk factors) in their titles and abstracts were evaluated in full based on the objectives, methods and key findings.

Two authors (GMG and EBT) independently assessed study quality using a standardized checklist. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram. (Fig. 1)

Following study selection, GMG and EBT independently extracted data using a standardized data extraction template adapted from Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI), and managed in Microsoft Excel 2016. The template included the first author’s name, publication year, study setting, study design, study participants, sample size, prevalence of infertility, and associated factors (Table 1). To ensure data accuracy and consistency, the authors compared extractions from a random for a random 30% subset of the included studies.

Quality appraisal (quality assessment of the included study)

The inclusion criteria for all retrieved articles were initially appraised using their titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text screening to evaluate the quality of each study before final selection. The quality of the included studies was evaluated using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklists for observational studies [12, 13]. Based on total scores ranging from 1 to 9, studies were classified as high quality (>7 criteria met), medium quality ( [5] – [6]), or low quality (< 5). All included studies were rated as either high or medium quality according to the JBI assessment criteria.

Study risk of bias management

To minimize risk of bias, we conducted a comprehensive search strategy involving electronic databases, manual screening, and grey literature.

Data analysis

After we extracted the data in Microsoft excel, we imported into STATA statistical software for analysis. A random-effects model was employed to calculate pooled prevalence estimates, given the substantial heterogeneity among studies (I² = 99.4%, χ² = 1249.69, p < 0.001). Results are presented in text, tables, and forest plots with 95% confidence intervals. Subgroup analyses were performed to explore variations in infertility prevalence across specific study characteristics.

Publication bias was assessed using both visual inspection of funnel plot symmetry and Egger’s weighted regression test (p < 0.05 considered significant). Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with values above 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis was performed to test the robustness of the pooled estimates by omitting studies one by one.

Outcomes and effect measures

In light of the study objectives, this study primarily focused on estimating the prevalence of infertility and identifying its risk factors. Infertility prevalence was calculated as the proportion of individuals experiencing infertility within the study population. For the second objective, to assess risk factors, we identified variables associated with increased or decreased infertility risk. Odds ratios (ORs) were calculated to quantify these associations.

Results

This systematic review and meta-analysis examined infertility prevalence and risk factors across 16 studies conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia, Ghana, Nigeria, Senegal, Cameroon, Malawi, Rwanda, Sudan, and Tanzania. The studies utilized nine cross-sectional, five case-control, and two cohort designs. The total sample size was 22,303 participants: primarily females aged 15–49, with some studies also including males. The prevalence of infertility reported in the studies ranged from 5.5% to 27.6%. The time frame spanning from 2000 to 2024, enables an exploration of trends in infertility over time.

Overall, the diversity in study designs, geographic coverage, participant demographics, and adequate time span enriches the findings and provides valuable insights into infertility prevalence and risk factors in the region. (Table 1)

Heterogeneity and publication bias

We assessed statistical heterogeneity using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic, revealing substantial variation among the included studies (I² = 99.4%, p < 0.001). Publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot and Egger’s regression test. The funnel plot showed a symmetrical distribution, with all data points falling within the expected region, suggesting no significant publication bias (Fig. 3). Egger’s regression test also showed no statistically significant small-study effects (p = 0.24, greater than the significance threshold) (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

The heterogeneity analysis revealed notable variability in effect sizes across countries. For example, Cameroon showed very high heterogeneity (I² = 97.7%), indicating significant variability across its studies. In contrast, Ethiopia showed moderate heterogeneity (I² = 58.2%), while Ghana exhibited low heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%) (Fig. 4). Infertility prevalence was highest in studies involving females only (21.36%, I² = 98.4%), followed by both-gender studies (13.72%, I² = 99.6%), and male-only studies (13.0%). No heterogeneity was calculated for male-only studies due to a single study being available (Fig. 5).

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the impact of each study on the overall prevalence of infertility by omitting one study at a time in this systematic review and meta-analysis. The pooled effect size remained relatively consistent across all scenarios, suggesting that no single study disproportionately influenced the overall result. Although minor fluctuations were observed, all estimates remained within the 95% confidence interval range. Therefore, the overall pooled estimate of 16.98% (11.62–22.33%) remained robust and unchanged despite the omission of individual studies. Heterogeneity remained high (98.1%–99.4%) in all cases, suggesting that no single study accounted for the observed variability. Instead, the persistent high heterogeneity (I² ~99%) suggests systematic differences among the studies, such as variations in populations, methodologies, or setting. Omitting individual studies did not significantly reduce heterogeneity, and the consistently significant P-values (p < 0.001) confirm that the observed variability is unlikely due to chance (Table 3; Fig. 6).

Risk factors of infertility

This meta-analysis explores risk factors of infertility, including female reproductive tract issues, sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), multiple sexual partners, abortion and its procedures, substance use (lifestyle factors), women’s age (age at first pregnancy), male factors (sperm viability, impotence), and obstetric complications in the last pregnancy. The overall pooled effect size (ES = 3.54, 95% CI: 2.80, 4.46) indicates a strong association between these risk factors and infertility. Notably, categories such as obstetric related complications (OR = 7.94, 95% CI: 4.74, 13.30), STI/PID (OR = 3.24, 95% CI: 2.33, 4.51), substance use (OR = 3.84, 95% CI: 1.71, 8.63), and women’s age (OR = 2.19, 95% CI: 1.48, 3.22) were all significantly associated with infertility, each reaching statistical significance with p < 0.05.

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 79.8%), likely due to variations in populations, study settings, and methodological approaches. Heterogeneity was particularly high for categories such as substance use and abortion, suggesting that the influence of these factors may differ by context or population. Nonetheless, the overall test of significance (z = 10.63, p < 0.001) confirms the relevance of these risk factors in relation to infertility (Fig. 7).

Discussion

There is a critical need for region-specific evidence on infertility in SSA, where sociocultural, economic, and healthcare contexts differ markedly from other African regions. Although infertility has been studied across the continent, data specific to SSA remain limited. A WHO-supported study [29] highlighted the rising burden of infection-related infertility in the region and the lack of data on behavioral and obstetric risk factors. By synthesizing data on the prevalence and risk factors of infertility in SSA, this study aimed to inform targeted interventions and context-specific policies.

In this meta-analysis, the pooled prevalence of infertility was 16.98% (95% CI: 11.62, 22.33%), with key risk factors identified, including obstetrics (pregnancy and childbirth related) complications, STIs/PID, substance use, and age. These findings highlight infertility as a critical and multifaceted health issue in the region.

Our findings are comparable to WHO’s 2023 global estimate of infertility prevalence, which reported that 17.5% of individuals worldwide experience infertility, with relatively stable rates across regions—17.8% in high-income and 16.5% in low- and middle-income countries [30]. This similarity highlights infertility as a consistent public health issue and emphasizes the need for increased attention to fertility treatments, support, and policy interventions, particularly in low-resource settings.

The pooled prevalence rate observed in this analysis is higher than reported rates from Sudan [31] and Iran [32]. Several factors could explain this discrepancy, including differences in healthcare infrastructure, cultural views on infertility, and environmental conditions. Countries like Iran may have more advanced and accessible healthcare systems, including fertility care. In contrast, SSA faces inconsistent healthcare access and a higher burden of STIs, which may contribute to the observed differences. In addition, socio-economic conditions, such as poverty and a lack of awareness in some parts of Sub-Saharan Africa, may contribute to higher infertility rates.

Comparison with prior African reviews, the SSA prevalence is lower than the continent-wide estimates [33], this difference suggests broader fertility challenges at the continental level, particularly in regions with weak reproductive healthcare and, where weak maternal health services and higher STI prevalence amplify infertility risks.

Obstetric complications emerged as a major risk-factor of infertility. Women experiencing pregnancy- and childbirth-related complications had nearly eight times higher odds of infertility (OR = 7.94, 95% CI: 4.74–13.30). Inadequate maternal healthcare during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period can lead to complications such as infections and uterine trauma which in turn damage the reproductive organs and disrupting normal reproductive functions and contributing to infertility. These results emphasize the lasting consequences of inadequate maternal healthcare and highlight the urgent need for safer obstetric services in SSA.

STIs/PID increased infertility risk by over threefold (OR = 3.24, 95% CI: 2.33–4.52). This is consistent with earlier studies that showed the impact of untreated infections on fertility outcomes [15, 34–42]. Studies from African centers reporting rates more than three times higher than those in other regions [41]. Most cases of tubal factor infertility are caused by untreated sexually transmitted infections, particularly gonorrhea and chlamydia, which ascend the reproductive tract, leading to tubal inflammation, damage, and scarring [35, 39, 40]. This can block or impair the movement of eggs, reducing the chances of conception or increasing the risk of ectopic pregnancy. This study emphasizes the need for preventive health measures, early diagnosis, and treatment of STIs to reduce infertility rates, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa, where access to reproductive health services may be limited.

Substance use including smoking, alcohol, and khat use were another significant predictors, with a pooled effect size of 3.84. This association was particularly strong for male infertility and supported by a previous review that concluded substance abuse affects human male fertility. Substance use can negatively affect fertility by disrupts spermatogenesis, srperm quality, egg development, hormone regulation, and ultimately imparing conception [43–47]. Evidence showed that both drug misuse and male infertility are currently increasing globally, raising serious concerns about the potential for drug-induced male sub-fertility. This trend may reflect growing exposure to substances and lack of preventative health education programs addressing modifiable lifestyle risk factors for reproductive health [43, 46]. Advice on modifiable lifestyle factors, such as “no smoking, no alcohol, and no street drugs,” should be emphasized for patients seeking fertility treatment, as a healthy lifestyle can improve outcomes [43].

As in global literature, womens’ age was an independent predictor of infertility but this finding highlights the prominence of healthcare and infection-related risk factors in Sub-Saharan Africa. Indicating that global recommendations on infertility prevention may need modification to meet the specific needs of the region.

Limitations

Our analysis revealed considerable heterogeneity, likely due to differences in study design, definitions of infertility, population characteristics, and regional or cultural contexts. This necessitates cautious interpretation of the pooled estimate and suggests that infertility risk factors may differ across settings, highlighting the need for locally adapted health recommendations. In addition, the review included studies from only a subset of countries in Sub-Saharan Africa, which may not fully represent the region. Non-English publications were excluded, potentially omitting relevant evidence. Both clinically diagnosed and self-reported infertility were included, which could affect consistency in estimates. Finally, male infertility was rarely addressed, underscoring the need for more male-focused research in the region.

Implications for intervention and policy

These findings emphasize that infertility in SSA is shaped by both poor maternal healthcare and infections. Addressing infertility in the region requires integrated strategies: strengthening maternal and reproductive health services, expanding STI prevention and treatment programs, and promoting public awareness of modifiable risk factors such as substance use. Educational campaigns with culturally appropriate messaging tailored to specific SSA contexts are essential to improve prevention and treatment outcomes.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis provides a robust estimate of infertility prevalence in SSA and also highlights the critical role of preventable factors, such as STIs/PID, substance use, and obstetric complications. Our findings advocate for reproductive health policies that target these specific risk factors and emphasize the importance of maternal healthcare utilization, safe reproductive health practices, and lifestyle modifications. These findings support the development of tailored interventions to reduce infertility and improve reproductive health outcomes in Sub-Saharan Africa, while also informing global health policy on regional priorities and challenges.
