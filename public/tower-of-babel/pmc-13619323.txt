The impact of improved air quality on low birth weight and health inequalities in Spain from 2010 to 2018
Authors: Stefan Sieber, Joan Ballester, Mark Nieuwenhuijsen, Andrea Pozzer, Cathryn Tonne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe aimed to estimate the association of air pollution and term low birth weight (LBW) by maternal education and assess the distributional impact of meeting European Union and World Health Organization air pollution targets and universal tertiary maternal education on LBW cases.MethodsWe analyzed administrative data on 1 409 084 term births in Spain (2010–18) and high spatial resolution estimates of maternal particulate matter with a diameter of 2.5 micrometers or less (PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) exposure. Single-exposure distributed lag non-linear models were used to estimate exposure–response functions (ERF) for LBW (<2500 g) overall and by maternal education. We then applied G-computation to estimate changes in LBW cases under counterfactual scenarios: target levels of PM2.5 (10 and 5 µg/m³) and NO2 (20 and 10 µg/m³), and universal tertiary maternal education.ResultsA 10 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 and NO2 was associated with 6% [95% confidence interval (CI): 0%–11%] and 1% (95% CI: 0%–3%) higher odds of LBW. Relative to tertiary education, secondary and primary education were associated with increased LBW odds [1.32 (95% CI: 1.30–1.35); 1.76 (95% CI: 1.71–1.82)]. Meeting the 10 and 5 µg/m³ PM2.5 limits would have prevented 662 (95% CI: −4 to 1271) and 2032 (95% CI: −74 to 3900) LBW cases in 2010–18. Meeting the 20 and 10 µg/m³ NO2 limits would have prevented 432 (95% CI: −16 to 848) and 938 (95% CI: −50 to 1860) LBW cases in 2010–18. Universal attainment of tertiary education would have prevented over 9200 LBW cases. The primary maternal education group showed the highest shares of LBW cases, but they also experienced the largest relative reductions under air pollution intervention scenarios.ConclusionsExpanding access to higher education may yield large reductions in the burden of LBW. Reducing air pollution exposure could also meaningfully reduce LBW burden, particularly among infants from lower educated mothers, and contribute to narrowing related health inequalities.Keywords: air pollution, low birth weight, NO2, PM2.5, maternal education, health inequality

Introduction

Low birth weight (LBW) has profound and lasting implications across the lifespan, affecting health and development from infancy through adulthood. While some of its effects, such as poorer school performance and suboptimal growth, diminish over time, others, including mild cognitive, attention, and neuromotor issues, persist into later childhood and adolescence [1, 2]. LBW is also a significant risk factor for chronic conditions in adulthood, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and chronic kidney disease. These long-term risks are partly attributed to the fetus adapting to scarcity, which can become maladaptive decades later, such as underlying insulin resistance that begins at birth [3–6]. Furthermore, LBW increases the likelihood of asthma, cerebral palsy, and osteoporosis, while being associated with earlier menopause in women [7, 8]. Early preventive interventions targeting modifiable environmental exposures and social determinants can provide large cumulative benefits across the life course [1, 2].

Air pollution is a major global public health concern, with serious implications for maternal and infant health. Exposure to pollutants like particulate matter with a diameter of 2.5 micrometers or less (PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) during pregnancy has been linked to LBW, preterm birth (PTB), and small for gestational age (SGA) infants [9–13]. These outcomes increase the risk of long-term health issues and poorer economic attainment [14, 15]. Reducing pollution levels could help reduce these risks. A previous study documented that a 10 µg/m³ reduction in NO2 could prevent 8% of LBW cases [12].

The burden of air pollution, however, is not equally distributed across the population. Lower socioeconomic groups are disproportionately affected by exposure to air pollutants and the consequent adverse outcomes [17]. Moreover, mothers with lower educational attainment are particularly susceptible to the negative effects of air pollution on birth outcomes [18]. This heightened susceptibility reflects the combined influence of chronic stress, poorer nutrition, and reduced healthcare, which may exacerbate placental oxidative stress and inflammatory responses triggered by air pollution exposure [19], highlighting the interplay between environmental and social determinants of health [20].

Our objectives were to (i) quantify the association of long-term air pollution (NO2 and PM2.5) exposure and LBW according to maternal education, (ii) quantify the impact of meeting air pollution targets on cases of LBW and on socioeconomic inequalities in LBW, and (iii) compare the impact of meeting air pollution targets with other potential policy options, specifically increasing maternal education.

Methods

Study population

We used birth registry data provided by the National Statistics Institute, including all births on Spanish territory except the Canary Islands, Ceuta, and Melilla between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2018. The study population included a total of 1 409 084 term births between 37 and 40 weeks of gestation. The registry included geocoded maternal home addresses with added spatial noise of about 40 m to pseudonymize the data.

Low birth weight

Birth weight was measured in grams. LBW at term was defined as a birth weight <2500 g.

Air pollution exposure

Air pollution data for NO2 and fine particulate matter (PM2.5) were derived from geographically and temporally weighted regression models developed for Europe from 2000 to 2019 as part of the EXPANSE project and described in detail elsewhere [21, 22]. Briefly, models integrated routine monitoring data with spatiotemporal predictors such as land use, satellite data, and meteorological variables to predict monthly air pollution at a high spatial resolution of 100 m × 100 m (see Extended Methods in Supplementary Material).

We defined the pregnancy period for each birth using the reported date of birth and gestational age, dividing it into consecutive four-week intervals (with a shorter final interval for weeks 37 and 40). Monthly air pollution concentrations were assigned to geocoded maternal home addresses via spatial linkage with pollution grids. In utero exposure for each interval was calculated as a weighted average of monthly concentrations based on the number of gestational days within each month. Supplementary Fig. S1 shows the mapped temporal evolution of in utero air pollution exposure between 2010 and 2018.

Maternal education

Maternal education was measured in 10 categories at the time of birth. The variable was recoded according to the International Standard Classification of Education 2011 (ISCED 2011) scale and subsequently categorized into “primary,” “secondary,” and “tertiary” education (see Supplementary Table S1) [23].

Statistical analyses

The statistical analyses were performed in two steps. First, we estimated single-exposure associations between air pollution and LBW to obtain exposure–response functions (ERF) overall and by individual-level maternal education level, as well as the association between maternal education and LBW. We estimated single-exposure models to avoid multicollinearity and to ensure stable estimation of the ERF used for the G-computation analyses in the next step. Second, we predicted LBW cases under different exposure scenarios consistent with air quality and education targets.

Associations between air pollution and low birth weight

We used single-exposure distributed lag non-linear models (DLNM) with a binomial link function to estimate the ERF between monthly average PM2.5 and NO2 exposure during pregnancy and LBW at term, overall and stratified by maternal education [16]. Models estimated (i) the overall cumulative ERF of PM2.5 or NO2 and LBW and (ii) the association of air pollution and LBW at each lag (i.e. 10 four-week intervals or pregnancy months) to identify windows of susceptibility [9]. The association of maternal education with LBW was estimated from the overall model.

Model selection for the exposure-lag-response functions was guided by prior literature, visual inspection of resulting graphs, and the Bayesian Information Criterion and Akaike Information Criterion (Supplementary Table S2) [9, 24, 25]. The final selected models included linear functions for the exposure–response and the lag-response for both PM2.5 and NO2. Effect modification by maternal education was tested using an air pollution cross-basis × education interaction and comparing it to a model without interaction with a likelihood ratio test.

All models were adjusted for covariates identified by using a directed acyclic graph (DAG) and from previous literature (Supplementary Fig. S2) [9, 26–28]: Birth year to adjust for secular trend, infant’s sex (female/male), singleton (yes/no), maternal age at birth in years, maternal marital status (married/not married), maternal citizenship status (Spanish/not Spanish), parity (nulliparous/multiparous), paternal citizenship status (Spanish/not Spanish), degree of urbanization of maternal residence’s municipality (city/town or suburb/rural) [29], 2011 area-level deprivation index [30], yearly mean normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) in a 300-m buffer for years 2010, 2015, 2020 (births were assigned the closest value to date of birth). Monthly mean temperature was modeled using a natural cubic spline with 2 df for the exposure–response and a penalized spline for the lag-response consistent with previous literature [30, 31]. We also included a random intercept at the NUTS 1 level (i.e. autonomous communities grouped into regions: North-West, North-East, Madrid, Center, East, South) to account for spatial autocorrelation. The overall model was additionally adjusted for maternal education level (primary/secondary/tertiary). While the DAG informed the minimally sufficient confounder set, we additionally adjusted for established predictors of LBW (infant sex, singleton status, maternal age, and parity) to improve model precision and account for baseline risk differences.

In an exploratory analysis, we ran models for continuous birth weight [see Supplementary Material, Appendix A1, for PM2.5 and birth weight ERF (Supplementary Fig. S7) and lag response (Supplementary Fig. S8), and NO2 and birth weight ERF (Supplementary Fig. S9) and lag response (Supplementary Fig. S10)].

Hypothetical intervention scenarios

We considered hypothetical interventions to retrospectively reduce air pollution concentrations and limit pregnancy air pollution exposure according to the revised EU Ambient Air Quality Directive (AAQD) and 2021 WHO Air Quality Guidelines (AQG) (Table 1) [31, 32]. We also considered a social intervention in which all mothers attained tertiary education (see Supplementary Table S1).

G-computation was implemented in the DLNM models that generated the exposure–response relationships to predict the probability of LBW under counterfactual exposure levels [33–35]. First, we obtained the predicted log-odds of LBW for each infant in the “reference” scenario (i.e. under observed exposure, Table 1) among all infants and stratified by maternal education (see Extended Methods in Supplementary Material).

Second, to evaluate the air quality interventions, we capped each infant’s in utero exposure at the corresponding EU AAQD and WHO AQG limits, setting observed lag-specific exposures to the guideline maximum where exceeded. With this altered exposure matrix, we recomputed the cross-basis, which was then used to predict the probability of LBW for each infant. The sum of these probabilities yielded the predicted LBW cases under the improved air quality scenarios for all infants and each stratum of maternal education. To account for parameter uncertainty, we applied a parametric simulation approach to calculate confidence intervals (CIs) (see Extended Methods in Supplementary Material).

Third, we evaluated a social intervention, changing all mothers’ education level to “tertiary.” We predicted the absolute number and change and percent change of LBW cases under each scenario overall and stratified by maternal education level.

Analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2, with models fitted using the dlnm and mgcv packages [36–38].

Results

Table 2 presents descriptive statistics overall and by maternal education. Among 1 409 084 term births, 11.2%, 38.7%, and 50.1% were to mothers with primary, secondary, and tertiary education, respectively. Mean pregnancy PM2.5 was similar across groups [11.41 (SD: 2.24), 11.44 (SD: 2.27), and 11.60 (SD: 2.30) µg/m³ for primary, secondary, and tertiary, respectively). Mean NO2 exposure was comparable for primary and secondary [21.31 (SD: 9.16) and 21.88 (SD: 9.68) µg/m³] but higher for tertiary education [24.06 (SD: 10.57) µg/m³] (Supplementary Fig. S3). Exposures remained stable throughout pregnancy (Supplementary Fig. S4). LBW prevalence decreased with increasing maternal education (5.3%, 4.9%, and 4.3% for primary, secondary, and tertiary, respectively).

Associations between air pollution, maternal education, and low birth weight

Main results are presented as exposure–response curves showing the cumulative association of PM2.5 or NO2 exposure (with the reference being 0 µg/m³ exposure) separately and the overall odds ratios (ORs) for LBW across the entire lag period [16, 24]. Results for the “windows of susceptibility” can be found in the Supplementary Material (see lag–response relationships).

PM2.5 and low birth weight

We found a positive linear association between PM2.5 and LBW in the total sample, with the CI including the null (OR: 1.06; 95% CI: 1.00, 1.11 per 10 µg/m³ increase in exposure) (Fig. 1A). The association differed by maternal education (likelihood ratio test P-value = .02); CI for primary, but not for other education groups, excluded the null (Fig. 1B).

Odds ratios for low birth weight per PM2.5 exposure, cumulative over the entire pregnancy, compared to 0 µg/m³, for all infants (A) and stratified by maternal education (B).

NO2 and low birth weight

A 10 µg/m³ increase in NO2 was associated with a 1% (95% CI: 0%–3%) increased odds of LBW (Fig. 2A), with no difference according to maternal education (likelihood ratio test P-value = .98) (Fig. 2B).

Odds ratios for low birth weight per NO2 exposure, cumulative over the entire pregnancy, compared to 0 µg/m³, for all infants (A) and stratified by maternal education (B).

Maternal education and low birth weight

Compared with infants of mothers with tertiary education, infants of mothers with secondary and primary education had 32% (95% CI: 30%–35%) and 76% (95% CI: 71%–82%) higher odds of LBW, respectively.

Predicted low birth weight cases under hypothetical intervention scenarios

Between 2010 and 2018, we predicted 65 036 LBW cases (95% CI: 64 584–65 507) under the reference PM2.5 scenario (Fig. 3A) and 65 045 (95% CI: 64 576–65 556) under the reference NO2 scenario (Fig. 3B).

Under PM2.5 counterfactuals, limiting exposure to 10 and 5 µg/m³ was associated with reductions of 662 (95% CI: −4 to 1271) and 2032 (95% CI: −74 to 3900) LBW cases (Fig. 4A), corresponding to a decrease in LBW cases from 4.62% (95% CI: 4.58%–4.65%) to 4.47% (95% CI: 4.34%–4.62%) (Fig. 5A). Under NO2 counterfactuals, limits of 20 and 10 µg/m³ were associated with reductions of 432 (95% CI: −16 to 848) and 938 (95% CI: −50 to 1860) LBW cases (Fig. 4B), corresponding to a decrease from 4.62% (95% CI: 4.58%–4.65%) to 4.55% (95% CI: 4.47%–4.63%) (Fig. 5B). CI for these reductions included the null.

In contrast, the social intervention scenario (all mothers attaining tertiary education) was associated with reductions of 9246 (95% CI: 8777–9737) LBW cases in the PM2.5 model (Fig. 4A) and 9275 (95% CI: 8743–9726) in the NO2 model (Fig. 4B), corresponding to a decrease in LBW cases from 4.62% (95% CI: 4.58%–4.65%) to 3.96% (95% CI: 3.91%–4.00%) (Fig. 5A and B).

Stratified analyses (Fig. 6A and B) indicated the largest relative reductions among infants of mothers with primary education, whereas estimates for secondary and tertiary education groups were relatively smaller and included the null. For PM2.5, estimated reductions among the primary educated group were 225 cases (95% CI: 7–435) at 10 µg/m³ and 759 cases (95% CI: 81–1387) at 5 µg/m³ (Fig. 4A), corresponding to decreases of 0.14 and 0.48 percentage points, respectively (Fig. 5A). For NO2, estimated reductions were 148 cases (95% CI: 23–286) at 20 µg/m³ and 376 cases (95% CI: 67–702) at 10 µg/m³ (Fig. 4B), corresponding to decreases of 0.09 and 0.23 percentage points (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

Our analysis led to several key findings. First, we observed positive linear, but imprecise, exposure–response relationships between PM2.5 and NO2 and LBW. Infants born to mothers with the lowest educational attainment experienced the highest odds of LBW due to PM2.5 exposure compared with other educational groups. Differences in the odds of LBW due to NO2 exposure were less conclusive. Second, our findings provide evidence that reductions in PM2.5 and NO2 exposure during pregnancy could lead to significant declines in LBW cases, with the greatest benefits observed among mothers with lower educational attainment. Third, increasing maternal education led to larger reductions in LBW cases compared to achieving air pollution targets, although reaching these targets could improve social inequalities in LBW cases.

Our findings related to the first objective add to the mixed results observed in previous epidemiological studies reporting on the relationship between air pollution and LBW. Existing evidence linking PM2.5 exposure to LBW is inconsistent [9, 10, 28]; our study observed an association by a small margin due to large CIs, consistent with the results of a recent study in a similar population reporting an OR of 0.99 (95% CI: 0.96–1.01) per 5 µg/m³ exposure increment [25]. Similarly, we observed no relationship between NO2 and LBW due to large CIs, which contrasts with two meta-analyses reporting small but positive associations: one found an OR of 1.05 (95% CI: 1.00–1.09) per 20 ppb increase in NO2 [39], and another reported 1.03 (95% CI: 1.01–1.05) per 10 ppb increase [40]. However, these represent larger exposure increments in terms of mass concentration than the exposure reductions modeled in our intervention scenarios.

Few studies have examined air pollution effects on birth outcomes by individual-level socioeconomic position. In Spain, PM10 exposure was associated with higher LBW risk among infants of mothers with lower education [25]. Similarly, a study in São Paulo reported stronger associations between PM10 and NO2 with LBW among less educated mothers, aligning with our findings of consistently greater impacts among infants born to mothers with lower educational attainment [41]. Other indicators of social disadvantage—including household income, race, and area-level deprivation—have also been shown to amplify pollution-related risks, highlighting greater vulnerability in socioeconomically disadvantaged populations [41–44]. Consistent with this evidence, we observed stronger adverse effects of PM2.5 on LBW among mothers with lower educational attainment despite comparable exposure levels. This heightened vulnerability may reflect compounding stressors, including nutritional deficiencies and poorer prenatal care access [41, 45, 46], alongside poorer maternal health and chronic stress, which may limit the capacity to buffer pollution-related insults [47].

In relation to the second objective, Arroyo et al. reported 8% of LBW cases (9385) in Spain (2001–09) could have been prevented with a 10-µg/m³ reduction in NO2 [12]. In comparison, achieving the WHO 10-µg/m³ guideline in our study period (2010–18) could have prevented approximately 938 cases (1.4%). This difference is likely due to the higher OR reported by Arroyo et al.: 1.09 (95% CI: 1.06–1.12), which was based on aggregate rather than individual-level data [12]. Additionally, the uncertainty intervals in our study included the null value. Our study extends existing literature by estimating PM2.5 and NO2 reductions by maternal education, suggesting larger benefits among infants of lower-educated mothers and potential reductions in social inequalities in LBW through improved air quality.

Findings for the third objective indicated greater reductions in LBW cases under improved maternal education than under air pollution targets. Previous research, including a study across 12 European countries, has reported elevated risks of PTB and SGA among mothers with lower education [48]. Education may influence pregnancy health through pathways such as nutrition, smoking, genital tract infections, prenatal care access and quality, environmental exposures, stress, and other psychosocial factors [49, 50].

A key strength of this study is its application of G-computation, which allows for causal inference under hypothetical intervention scenarios while accounting for complex exposure–response relationships and confounders. Additionally, our use of high-resolution air pollution estimates provides robust exposure assessments at the individual level. Nonetheless, several limitations should be considered. Residual confounding by unmeasured factors (e.g. maternal nutrition, stress, genetic predisposition) cannot be excluded. Exposure misclassification may have occurred because air pollution was assigned at residential addresses at delivery and monthly exposure data were aligned to weekly gestational windows; this likely resulted in non-differential error and attenuation of associations. We assumed stable exposure–response relationships across time and regions, did not adjust simultaneously for co-pollutants to avoid multicollinearity, and specified distributed lag models that are sensitive to smoothing choices. These decisions may introduce modeling uncertainty and limit inference on independent pollutant effects. Finally, the counterfactual scenarios represent hypothetical exposure shifts and do not specify the policy mechanisms or costs required to achieve air quality improvements or universal tertiary education. Thus, the estimates quantify potential health gains under defined exposure changes but do not assess feasibility or implementation pathways.

While our scenarios are not policy-specific, they illustrate potential reductions in LBW from meeting air quality targets; implications for socioeconomic inequalities; and how environmental interventions compare with other policy options like expanding access to higher education. Given LBW’s long-lasting health consequences across the life course, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and chronic kidney disease, minimizing in utero air pollution exposure is crucial [7, 8]. Our findings support calls for aligning EU air quality directives with WHO guidelines to maximize health benefits [51]. Additionally, integrating air pollution reduction efforts with social policies aimed at improving maternal education and healthcare access may yield synergistic benefits, further reducing disparities in birth outcomes. Future research should explore the cost-effectiveness of these interventions and their long-term health and economic impacts.

In conclusion, this study highlights the benefits of air pollution reduction and increased maternal education for low birth weight in Spain, particularly among vulnerable populations. Continued efforts to improve air quality and improve access to higher education could play an important role in reducing the burden of low birth weight infants and concomitant health complications and improving health equity.

Administrative birth data is owned by the Spanish National Statistics Institute and was provided by permission. Data will be shared on request to the corresponding author with permission of the Spanish National Statistics Institute.

Use of artificial intelligence (AI) tools

ChatGPT was used to proofread the manuscript for grammar and clarity. All scientific aspects of the study, including the design, data analysis, interpretation of results, and image generation, were conducted by the authors without the use of AI.
