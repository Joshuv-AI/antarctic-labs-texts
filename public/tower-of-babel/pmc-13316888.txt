Determinants of Health-Related Quality of Life Among Patients with Psoriasis Vulgaris
Authors: Bui Long, Nguyen Thi Kim Huong, Le Huu Doanh, Nguyen Hoang Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivepsoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated disease that significantly impacts patients' health-related quality of life (HRQOL). While effective treatments exist, the burden of psoriasis on HRQOL and the factors influencing it remain underexplored in Vietnam. To assess HRQOL and its associated factors among patients with psoriasis vulgaris in a tertiary hospital in Vietnam.Methoda cross-sectional study was conducted from March to December 2023, including 149 patients with psoriasis vulgaris. HRQOL was evaluated using the EQ-5D-5L tool, and disease severity was assessed with the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI). Data analysis included descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and stepwise multivariate Tobit regression to identify factors associated with the EQ-5D index.Resultsthe mean EQ-5D index was 0.85 (SD = 0.17), with patients reporting the fewest problems in mobility (88.6% no issues) and self-care (81.2% no issues). However, pain/discomfort (68.5%) and anxiety/depression (36.2%) were significant concerns. The EQ-5D index declined with increasing PASI scores (mild: 0.87, severe: 0.68, p < 0.01). Factors negatively associated with HRQOL included older age (Coef. = -0.003, p = 0.026), severe redness (Coef. = -0.199, p = 0.001), and pain symptoms (Coef. = -0.108, p = 0.031). Male sex was independently associated with higher HRQOL (Coef. = 0.084, p = 0.015). Fraizeron treatment was positively associated with higher HRQOL (Coef. = 0.164, p = 0.048).Conclusionspsoriasis substantially impacts HRQOL, particularly through pain and emotional burden. Older age, severe redness, and pain symptoms were associated with poorer HRQOL, whereas male sex and Fraizeron treatment were associated with higher HRQOL. Comprehensive management strategies addressing both physical symptoms and psychological well-being may help improve HRQOL.Keywords: psoriasis vulgaris, health-related quality of life, pain, psychology

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease characterized by persistent involvement of the skin and, in some cases, the joints (Boehncke, 2015; Parisi R et al., 2020). Recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a serious non-communicable disease, psoriasis imposes considerable individual and societal burdens. Globally, more than 100 million people are affected, with prevalence estimates ranging from 0.09% to 11.43% (Mehrmal S et al., 2021). Beyond its visible dermatological manifestations, psoriasis is increasingly understood as a systemic inflammatory condition associated with metabolic and cardiovascular comorbidities, including insulin resistance, type II diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular events (Augustin M et al., 2010). Importantly, the impact of psoriasis extends beyond physical morbidity; individuals frequently experience psychological distress, social stigma, interpersonal difficulties, and diminished overall well-being, underscoring the need to evaluate its broader effects on quality of life (Belinchón I et al., 2016).

To better understand this broader impact, it is essential to distinguish between quality of life (QoL) and health-related quality of life (HRQOL). QoL is a multidimensional construct that encompasses individuals’ perceptions of their position in life within their cultural and value systems and in relation to their goals, expectations, and concerns (Haraldstad et al., 2019). It includes domains such as autonomy, social relationships, emotional comfort, daily activities, spirituality, and financial security (Van Leeuwen et al., 2019). Health is considered one determinant among many contributing to overall QoL rather than its sole component (Haraldstad et al., 2019; Sarvimäki & Stenbock-Hult, 2000). Within this broader framework, HRQOL specifically captures the perceived impact of disease and treatment on physical, psychological, and social functioning (Guyatt et al., 1993; Testa & Simonson, 1996). It is inherently subjective and multidimensional, reflecting symptom burden, role functioning, and emotional well-being (Ferrans et al., 2005; Martínez-Martín, 2017). Given the chronic, visible, and often stigmatizing nature of psoriasis, HRQOL has become a central outcome in both clinical research and patient-centered care.

Consistent with this conceptual framework, international research has documented substantial HRQOL impairment among patients with psoriasis across diverse cultural settings. In Ethiopia, a clinic- based study used the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), which is a 10-item dermatology-specific questionnaire assessing the impact of skin disease on symptoms, daily activities, leisure, work/school, personal relationships, and treatment burden (Finlay & Khan, 1994). The study reported a mean DLQI score of 6.25, indicating a moderate impact on daily life, particularly in symptom and emotional domains (Kumsa et al., 2021). Similar findings were observed in Germany, where 26.2% of patients experienced very large to extremely large impairment (Da Silva et al., 2022). In populations with more severe disease, such as those included in biologic trials, baseline DLQI scores have reached 12.1, reflecting moderate to very large impact (Karpińska-Mirecka et al., 2020). Generic instruments further corroborate these impairments: Japanese registry data showed reduced physical functioning and general health scores on the Short Form-36 (SF-36), a generic HRQOL instrument measuring eight domains of physical and mental health (Shinichi et al., 2024), while Norwegian patients demonstrated significantly lower scores across all SF-36 domains, particularly in role-emotional functioning (Wahl et al., 2000). Together, these findings illustrate that psoriasis affects not only physical capacity but also emotional and social dimensions of health.

Disease severity has emerged as one of the most consistent determinants of HRQOL impairment. Real-world registry data from the United States demonstrated a stepwise decline in DLQI and EQ-VAS scores, which is a 0–100 self-rated measure of overall health status (EuroQol Research Foundation, 2017), with increasing body surface area and Investigator Global Assessment severity (Strober et al., 2019). Systematic European reviews similarly confirm poorer HRQOL among patients with more severe disease, visible lesions, pruritus, and skin discomfort, whereas systemic and biologic therapies are associated with improvements (Karpińska-Mirecka et al., 2020; Obradors et al., 2016; Strober et al., 2019). Beyond clinical severity, demographic and psychosocial factors also contribute. Female sex, lower income, and limited education have been linked to worse HRQOL (Da Silva et al., 2022; Kumsa et al., 2021; Obradors et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2023), while pruritus, cutaneous pain, and visible lesions, especially in exposed areas, are associated with greater psychological burden and higher DLQI scores (Da Silva et al., 2022; Kotewicz et al., 2024; Obradors et al., 2016). These findings underscore the multidimensional and biopsychosocial nature of HRQOL impairment in psoriasis.

Given the need for standardized and comparable measurement, the EQ-5D-5L has become a widely used generic preference-based instrument for assessing HRQOL (EuroQol Research Foundation, 2017). It evaluates five dimensions, mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, each across five severity levels, along with a visual analogue scale for overall health (Hernández et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2017). Utility scores derived from country-specific tariffs allow for economic and clinical comparability (Poór et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2017). Evidence supports its validity in psoriasis populations, demonstrating reduced ceiling effects, stronger correlation with diseases severity, and good known-groups validity compared with earlier versions (Poór et al., 2017). Furthermore, multinational studies show significant HRQOL deficits across all five EQ-5D dimensions among dermatology patients, with psoriasis ranking among the most impactful conditions (Balieva et al., 2017).

Despite this robust international evidence, research from Vietnam remains limited, particularly studies integrating clinical severity, symptom burden (e.g., redness and pain), and psychological dimensions within a unified HRQOL framework. Cultural context may shape how individuals perceive visible skin disease, stigma, and social evaluation, potentially influencing reported emotional distress and social functioning. Differences in healthcare access, treatment patterns, and health literacy may further affect patient-reported outcomes. Consequently, findings from Western or high-income settings may not fully reflect the lived experience of Vietnamese patients. Rather than aiming to estimate national prevalence, the present study seeks to provide a context-specific examination of HRQOL patterns and associated factors within a tertiary-care sample. Based on existing literature, we hypothesized that (1) greater clinical severity would be associated with lower HRQOL; (2) pain symptoms and severe redness would independently predict poorer HRQOL; and (3) demographic factors, particularly age and sex, would contribute to variability in HRQOL outcomes. By applying the validated EQ-5D-5L instrument, this study aims to clarify the clinical and psychological determinants of HRQOL in Vietnamese patients with psoriasis vulgaris and to inform culturally sensitive, patient-centered management strategies.

Materials and method

Study design

This study utilized a cross-sectional descriptive design and was conducted at the National Dermatology Hospital and Friendship Hospital in Hanoi, Vietnam, from March 1, 2023, to December 31, 2023. Participants included all patients diagnosed with psoriasis vulgaris (standard type) who were referred to these hospitals for examination and treatment during the study period. Eligibility criteria required participants to: (1) be at least 18 years old; (2) have a confirmed diagnosis of psoriasis vulgaris; (3) complete all clinical and paraclinical assessments; (4) not be undergoing statin treatment; and (5) provide consent to participate. A convenience sampling method was employed, resulting in the enrollment of 146 patients meeting the criteria. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hanoi Medical University (Approval Code: 859/GCN-HDDDNCYSHDHYHN). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data collection method

Participant data related to the diagnosis of psoriasis vulgaris were extracted from medical records. The collected information included demographic characteristics such as age, gender, and occupation, as well as medical history, encompassing illness type and duration, family and personal medical history, comorbidities, body mass index (BMI), and clinical symptoms such as itching, pain, and skin redness. The severity of redness was classified as mild, moderate, or severe. Additional clinical data included information about associated injuries (e.g., fingernails, toenails, joints) and details of ongoing treatment regimens.

The severity of psoriasis vulgaris was evaluated using the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI), a comprehensive scoring tool that assesses psoriasis severity across four anatomical regions: the head, arms, trunk, and legs. Each region is scored based on three plaque characteristics: erythema (redness), induration (thickness), and desquamation (scaling). The scores for each characteristic within each region are totaled and weighted according to the region's proportion of the total body surface area. The weighted scores are then combined to produce a final composite score ranging from 0 to 72. Psoriasis severity is categorized as follows: Mild severity: PASI < 10; Moderate severity: 10 ≤ PASI < 20; Severe severity: PASI ≥ 20 (Fredriksson & Pettersson, 1978).

The assessment of participants' HRQOL was conducted using the EQ-5D-5L instrument, a standardized, preference-based measure of generic health status (Reips & Funke, 2008). The EQ-5D-5L consists of two components: (1) a descriptive system comprising five single-item dimensions, including Mobility, Self-care, Usual Activities, Pain/Discomfort, and Anxiety/Depression, and (2) a visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS) that records the respondent’s self-rated overall health on a scale from 0 (the worst health imaginable) to 100 (the best health imaginable) (EuroQol Research Foundation, 2017). Each of the five dimensions is rated at five levels of severity, ranging from 1 (no problems) to 5 (extreme problems), resulting in 3,125 possible health states, from 11111 (full health) to 55555 (most severe impairment). The instrument was administered as a self-report questionnaire, with participants completing the form independently; assistance was provided when necessary to ensure comprehension. Health states were converted into a single utility index using the Vietnamese value set, with scores ranging from -0.5112 (health states considered worse than death) to 1 (full health) (Mai et al., 2020a). Higher utility scores indicate better perceived HRQOL. The Vietnamese version of the EQ-5D-5L has demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability in prior psychometric evaluations (Mai et al., 2020b), supporting its use in this study population.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics were first performed to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables, including age, duration of disease, body mass index (BMI), PASI score, and EQ-5D utility index, were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Bivariate analyses were undertaken to examine differences in the EQ-5D index across demographic and clinical characteristics. For dichotomous variables (e.g., gender, family history of psoriasis, itching, pain symptoms, joint involvement, and specific treatment modalities), independent samples t-tests were used to compare mean EQ-5D scores between groups. For variables with more than two categories (e.g., occupation, severity of redness, nail involvement, and PASI severity categories), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. Chi-square tests were additionally used to assess associations between PASI severity and selected categorical variables where appropriate.

To identify independent factors associated with HRQOL, a multivariable Tobit regression model was fitted with the EQ-5D utility index as the dependent variable. The Tobit model was chosen because the EQ-5D index is a censored variable bounded at 1 (representing full health), which may violate the assumptions of ordinary least squares regression due to potential ceiling effects. A stepwise forward selection procedure was applied to construct the final model. Variables were entered sequentially based on the likelihood ratio test, with a p-value < 0.2 considered as the entry criterion for inclusion in the model. The initial “full” candidate set included demographic variables (gender; occupation; family history of psoriasis), clinical characteristics and symptoms (psoriasis type; comorbid conditions; absence of clinical symptoms; itching symptom; pain symptom; redness symptom; severity of redness; nail involvement; joint involvement), treatment variables (topical treatment; UV therapy; methotrexate treatment; cyclosporine treatment; acitretin treatment; Fraizeron treatment; Humira treatment), disease severity indicators (psoriasis severity category based on PASI: mild, moderate, severe; and PASI score as a continuous variable), and continuous covariates (age, duration of disease, and BMI). Categorical predictors were entered using indicator (dummy) coding with clinically meaningful reference categories (e.g., female; farmer; guttate psoriasis; mild redness; no symptoms/treatment exposure), and continuous variables were modeled per unit increase (years for age and disease duration; kg/m2for BMI; points for PASI score). To minimize redundancy and collinearity in severity measurement, PASI category and PASI score were not simultaneously retained in the final model if they conveyed overlapping information with other severity-related variables. After stepwise forward selection, the final Tobit model retained the predictors including age, gender, BMI, disease duration, family history of psoriasis, severity of redness, pain symptom, and Fraizeron treatment. Model results are presented as regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and corresponding p-values. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values below 10 indicating acceptable levels. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 149 patients were included in the study. The sample was predominantly male (61.7%), with a mean age of 43.2 years. Most participants were freelance workers or engaged in other occupations (61.8%), and 16.1% reported a family history of psoriasis. Clinically, plaque psoriasis was the most common subtype (88.6%). Itching was reported by 38.9% of patients, and 59.7% experienced skin redness, which was mainly mild (51.7%) or moderate (36.2%) in severity. Nail involvement was observed in 69.1% of cases, most commonly affecting both fingernails and toenails (63.1%), while 32.9% had joint involvement. Based on PASI classification, 59.7% of patients had mild disease, with a mean PASI score of 9.4 ± 6.1. The mean disease duration was 12.5 years, and the average BMI was 22.3 kg/m2. Treatment regimens varied, with 36.9% receiving methotrexate and 30.9% using topical therapy; smaller proportions were treated with acitretin (21.5%), UV therapy (6.0%), or biologic agents such as Fraizeron (5.4%) and Humira (0.7%) (table 1).

The mean EQ-5D index was 0.85 (SD = 0.17). When stratified by disease severity (figure 1), HRQOL declined progressively with increasing PASI scores. Patients with mild psoriasis had the highest mean EQ-5D index (0.87) and the lowest proportion of reported problems, whereas those with severe psoriasis had the lowest index (0.68), with markedly higher proportions of impairment across all five domains (F=5.14, p < 0.01). In severe cases, pain/discomfort (87.5%) and usual activity limitations (87.5%) were most prevalent, followed by anxiety/depression (75.0%) and mobility problems (62.5%).

Bivariate analyses (table 3 and table 4) showed that males had slightly higher EQ-5D scores than females. Patients without a family history of psoriasis reported significantly higher HRQOL (p = 0.011). While itching was associated with higher EQ-5D scores (p = 0.049), pain symptoms, severe redness, and joint involvement were significantly associated with lower HRQOL (p < 0.01). Discussion

This study showed that psoriasis was associated with reduced HRQOL, with the greatest problems reported in pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression, especially among patients with more severe disease. The decrease in EQ-5D index with increasing PASI scores indicates an inverse relationship between clinical severity and HRQOL. Older age, severe redness, and pain symptoms were independently associated with lower HRQOL, reflecting the broad impact of psoriasis on patients’ daily lives. In contrast, males and patients receiving Fraizeron treatment reported higher EQ-5D scores, suggesting possible gender differences and treatment-related benefits. These findings highlight that HRQOL in psoriasis is influenced by both clinical and demographic factors. Importantly, by examining PASI severity, specific symptoms (redness and pain), and patient-reported health utility together, this study provides a more integrated view of HRQOL determinants.

The use of the EQ-5D-5L in this study allowed HRQOL to be measured using a standardized health utility scale that can be compared across diseases and populations. The EQ-5D-5L assesses five areas of health (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression) and includes a visual scale of overall health (EQ-VAS), giving a concise summary of patients’ physical and emotional health. Compared with the older 3-level version, the 5-level EQ-5D has better ability to distinguish different levels of health problems, which is useful in dermatology patients who may otherwise appear to have minimal impairment on generic scales (Poór et al., 2017). Because it provides a preference-based utility score, EQ-5D-5L results can also be used in future cost-effectiveness or health-policy analyses. Therefore, its use in this study supports interpretation of HRQOL in a broader health-economic context rather than replacing dermatology-specific tools such as the DLQI.

The pattern of HRQOL impairment observed here is consistent with previous studies. Although mobility and self-care were largely unaffected, substantial problems were reported in usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Similar patterns were reported in an Iranian study, where pain/discomfort and anxiety/ depression were the most affected domains (Moradi et al., 2015). The mean EQ-5D index of 0.85 in this study indicates a moderate impact and falls within the range reported in systematic reviews (0.52–0.9), suggesting HRQOL impairment comparable to other chronic diseases (Møller et al., 2015). Studies in China also reported relatively high EQ-5D-5L scores, whereas studies using the older EQ-5D-3L reported lower values, highlighting the influence of measurement instruments (Liu et al., 2018; Souza et al., 2019). However, in this Vietnamese tertiary-care sample, patients with higher PASI severity consistently reported lower EQ-5D utility, a pattern similar to previous international findings. This suggests that the relationship between disease severity and HRQOL is also observable in this clinical setting, although larger or population-based studies in Vietnam are needed to confirm it.

Several factors were significantly associated with the EQ-5D index, reflecting the relationship between psoriasis and HRQOL. Older age was linked to a slight decline in HRQOL, a finding consistent with studies showing that older psoriasis patients often experience more significant impairments due to cumulative disease burden, comorbidities, and psychological distress (Jankovic et al., 2011; Kimball et al., 2010). Older adults, especially women, report higher levels of anxiety and depression, further exacerbating their quality of life decline (Sampogna et al., 2006). Gender differences in HRQOL are notable, with women generally reporting greater psychological distress and a more pronounced impact on their quality of life despite comparable clinical severity. For instance, studies in Italy and Japan demonstrated that female patients experienced a significantly greater impact on QOL scores than males, both at baseline and after treatment, even when PASI scores were similar (Colombo et al., 2022; Mabuchi et al., 2012). In our multivariable analysis, male sex remained independently associated with higher EQ-5D scores after adjustment for clinical factors, suggesting that gender disparities in HRQOL may not be fully explained by disease severity alone. Several explanations may account for this finding. Women may experience greater distress related to visible skin lesions and social stigma. Differences in social roles and daily responsibilities may also increase the impact of symptoms on usual activities. In addition, women may be more likely to report emotional distress and pain, whereas men may underreport these symptoms. Differences in treatment expectations or satisfaction may also contribute. These possibilities require further investigation in studies measuring stigma, coping, and social support.

Severe redness and pain symptoms were strongly associated with lower EQ-5D scores, emphasizing their substantial impact on patient well-being. Redness, often measured as erythema in the PASI score, correlates closely with HRQOL outcomes. While PASI scores as a whole may not directly align with EQ-5D scores in this study, specific components such as redness demonstrate significant associations. For instance, longitudinal studies in Sweden and Korea revealed that higher PASI scores, including pronounced erythema and exposed lesions, were tied to poorer HRQOL outcomes (Geale et al., 2017; Youn et al., 2018). By isolating redness severity as an independent predictor in the regression model, this study suggests that a clinically observable component of plaque activity may be particularly relevant to perceived health utility. Moreover, proactive management of redness has been shown to improve patient-reported outcomes compared to reactive approaches (Jalili et al., 2022), reinforcing its clinical relevance.

Pain symptoms, particularly skin and musculoskeletal pain, were also linked to lower HRQOL, highlighting their dual physical and psychological burden. Pain contributes to sleep disturbances and heightened psychological distress, including anxiety and depression, which in turn further impair HRQOL (Ljosaa et al., 2012). Musculoskeletal pain, whether linked to psoriatic arthritis or not, amplifies this decline, as patients with pain report poorer quality of life and higher self-assessed disease severity than those without pain (Felbo et al., 2021). Our findings indicate that pain remained statistically associated with health utility even after adjusting for severity and demographic variables, underscoring its central role in patient-perceived health burden. This supports a multidisciplinary management approach that integrates dermatologic treatment with pain control and psychosocial support (Coimbra et al., 2011).

The findings emphasize the need to address pain and emotional health in psoriasis care. Fraizeron treatment was associated with higher HRQOL scores in this study. However, because of the cross-sectional design and possible confounding by treatment selection, this association should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal treatment effect. Instead, it suggests a potential treatment-related benefit that should be evaluated in longitudinal or controlled studies. Overall, by identifying both clinical and psychosocial factors in one model, this study provides context-specific evidence to support patient-centered psoriasis management in Vietnam.

This study has limitations. The cross-sectional design prevents causal interpretation. Convenience sampling may limit generalizability. Self-reported measures may introduce bias. The sample size may have limited detection of weaker associations. Nevertheless, the study provides new context-specific evidence on HRQOL correlates in Vietnamese psoriasis patients, helping address an important regional evidence gap.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant impact of psoriasis on HRQOL, particularly in domains such as pain, discomfort, and emotional well-being. Disease severity, pain symptoms, and redness were identified as key determinants of reduced HRQOL, while treatments like Fraizeron showed a potential for improvement. These findings emphasize the need for comprehensive management strategies that address both physical and emotional aspects of psoriasis to improve patient quality of life. Future longitudinal studies with larger samples are recommended to further explore these associations and evaluate intervention outcomes.
