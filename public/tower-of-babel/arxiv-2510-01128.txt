How many channels can a photonic system support?
Paul Virally, Pengning Chao, Alessio Amaolo, Alejandro Rodriguez, Sean Molesky

Abstract
We develop a general method to bound the ordered singular values (channel amplitudes) of the electromagnetic Green function for arbitrarily structured linear photonic systems. The approach yields computable, quantitatively predictive, upper bounds on the n t ​ h n^{th} singular value that capture the complexity of multi-channel tradeoffs from the device perspective. As an illustration of the practical value of the framework, indexed channel bounds are obtained for multi-wavelength scale three-dimensional volumes (up to 64 ​ λ 3 64\,\lambda^{3} ) and applied to common application classes related to waveguides, metasurfaces, and planewave detection. These results are immediately applicable to the calculation of information theoretic objectives such as Shannon capacity and Fisher information.

Against this backdrop, the abstraction of singular value decomposition (SVD) stands as a natural language for linear system design.

Specifically, we provide the first method to generally answer the following question that properly captures at least some of the tradeoffs imposed by multiple inputs on device design.

Given some volumes of space that may contain arbitrarily structured materials (having known susceptibilities), how large can the
n^{th}
singular value of the electromagnetic Green function be?

The figure illustrates the meaning of an electromagnetic channel in the sender-mediator-receiver convention used throughout the article.
Each channel consists of a pair of connected input-output fields (one in the sender and one in the receiver) that are orthogonal to the fields of every other channel, along with a channel amplitude (the singular value
\sigma_{k}

Working in terms of channels leads to a completely decomposed description of communication (transmission).
The lower part of the figure shows four physical phenomena, among many, that are described by functional sums over channel amplitudes: heat transfer

, Purcell enhancement

Beyond its immediate connection to multi-functionality, this metric is seen to be deeply intertwined with a number of physical quantities of interest.
In particular, under an additive noise model (detector dominated noise), the number of singular values above a threshold strongly dictates Shannon capacity
Ehrenborg and Gustafsson 2020
, and is hence closely related to mutual information
Pinkard et al. 2025
and Fisher information (see §Applications).
Consequently, there are a range of scenarios related to imaging and classification where it has significant implications.
More broadly, essentially any trace formulation can be reframed as a functional sum over singular values, and in this way channel counts above a cut-off provide an interesting point of comparison for phenomena such as Purcell enhancement, Casimir forces, fluorescence, scattering cross sections, and thermal radiation
Hamam et al. 2007
,
Krüger et al. 2012
,
Yao et al. 2022
, Fig. 1.

The results presented below show that, by combining the Courant-Fischer-Weyl min-max principle with convex relaxation bounds
Chao et al. 2022
, quantitatively accurate limits on the maximum (or minimum) of the
n^{th}
singular value of the electromagnetic Green function are computable for moderately sized wavelength scale systems (up to
64~\lambda^{3}
).
After briefly sketching the main idea behind these limits and some notable relations, three illustrative application examples are explored.
First, bounds on the number of channels having amplitude greater or equal to a specified value are given for an increasingly long design region inserted between thin, unstructured, sender and receiver volumes—investigating the extent to which structuring a material similar to silicon can enhance transmission over free space communication.
As may be anticipated the results are fairly dramatic: with the mediator present, a separation distance of
5\lambda
compares favorably with free space communication down to separation distances smaller than
\lambda/4
.
Next, singular value bounds are given for metasurface like configurations of a wide-thin design region inserted between a wide-thin, unstructured, sender and receiver pair with varying aspect ratios and separations.
Here we find that while the largest singular values remain relatively stable, increasing the volume of the mediating design region can greatly increase the number of usable channels (qualified as amplitude bounds larger than
~0.1
).
However, for small separations this effect saturates with increasing device area, suggesting that while larger “non-local” cell volumes are likely a key to realizing greater functionality in metasurfaces
Kwon et al. 2018
,
Overvig et al. 2020
,
Chen et al. 2025
there is likely also a point of diminishing returns.
Finally, we examine a scenario mimicking angle sensing and laser awareness
Li et al. 2023

30^{\circ}
cone of incidence.
Again, the physical extent of the designable regions is seen to have strong effects.
As the width of the mediator increases from
2\lambda
to
16\lambda

0.1

I
§Formulation

The underpinning of our proposed framework is the Courant-Fischer-Weyl (CFW) min-max principle, which restates the
n^{th}
singular value of a linear operator as a min-max or max-min optimization.
Intuitively, the first
n
right singular vectors of a compact linear operator
\bm{\mathrm{A}}:X\rightarrow Y
form an
n
-dimensional subspace,
U_{n}
.
Take
Q_{n-1}
to be any
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace of
X
and
Q^{\perp}_{n-1}
to be its orthogonal complement.
Because
X=Q_{n-1}\oplus Q^{\perp}_{n-1}
, some linear combination of elements of
U_{n}
must reside in
Q^{\perp}_{n-1}
—
Q_{n-1}\cap U_{n}
is at best an
\left(n-1\right)
-dimensional subspace, and so a direction orthogonal to
Q_{n-1}\cap U_{n}
within
U_{n}
always exists.
Take
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
to be the unit vector along this direction.
As the image of each component of
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
will be scaled by a factor of at least
\sigma_{n}
,
\max_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in Y}\Re\left(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{\mathrm{x}}\right)
, with
\lVert\bm{\mathrm{y}}\rVert_{2}=1
, will be at least as large as
\sigma_{n}
.

Min-max principle
—Let
\bm{\mathrm{A}}:X\rightarrow Y
be a compact linear operator between complex inner product spaces with ordered singular values
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots
.

\bm{\mathrm{A}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}
is then a compact self-adjoint operator with ordered eigenvalues
\sigma_{1}^{2}\geq\sigma_{2}^{2}\geq\cdots
, and the
n^{th}
eigenvalue of
\bm{\mathrm{A}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}
is equal to the following min-max (max-min) optimization over the
n
-dimensional linear subspace
Q_{n}\subseteq X
and its orthogonal complement
Q_{n}^{\perp}

Teschl 2014
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{Q_{n-1}\subseteq X}\max_{~\bm{x}\in Q_{n-1}^{\perp}}\bm{x}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{x}

(1a)

\displaystyle=\max_{Q_{n}\subseteq X}\min_{\bm{x}\in Q_{n}}\bm{x}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{x},

(1b)

with
\lVert\bm{\mathrm{x}}\rVert_{2}=1
.
Stripping away the outer optimization gives an upper (resp. lower) bound on
\sigma_{n}^{2}
:

\min_{\bm{x}\in Q_{n}}\bm{x}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{x}\leq\sigma_{n}^{2}\leq\max_{\bm{x}\in Q_{n-1}^{\perp}}\bm{x}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{x}.

(2)

Equivalent bounds can also be stated in terms of
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
under an additional optimization over a unit vectors
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in Y
:

\sigma_{n}\leq\max_{\bm{x}\in Q_{n-1}^{\perp}}\max_{\bm{y}\in Y}~\bm{y}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{x}.

(3)

In what follows, the CFW min-max principle is applied to derive bounds on
n^{th}
singular value of the electromagnetic Green function based on the
\bm{\mathrm{T}}
-operator expansion

\bm{\mathrm{G}}=\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}+\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}\bm{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ},

(4)

with
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}
the free space Green function.
Depending on the application in question, this is done by either asserting
Q_{n-1}
to be formed by some collection of the singular vector basis of
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}
and then applying a convex relaxation over a quadratically constrained quadratic program describing the possible response characteristics of
\bm{\mathrm{T}}
(see Supplementary), or by first performing these steps and then freely optimizing over
Q_{n-1}
.

As indicated by the laser awareness example, this CFW procedure is also applicable to settings wherein there is no source region, but rather only incident waves, as well as design problems wherein detection is restricted to a particular subspace (e.g. the detector only registers certain field patterns).
In the first instance, this is done by factoring Eq. (
4
) as
\left(\bm{\mathrm{I}}+\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}\bm{\mathrm{T}}\right)\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}
and then replacing the
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}
appearing on the right with the appropriate incident fields.
Adding a restriction to detection may be implemented in a similar manner via the inclusion of subspace projector to the left of the Green function.
Through these type of compositions, and use of the fundamental relations of scattering theory, it is possible to handle many cases of practical interest for all major scattering operators (Supplementary).
However, there is no clear prescription for satisfactorily treating a receiver that can only measure intensities—the root issue being that the mapping from fields to intensities is nonlinear.
More technically, although relations do exist between singular values and Hadamard products, we are not presently aware of any argument that properly captures the intuition that occluding phase information must cause certain distinct communication channels to merge (compatible with the present CFW strategy).
While it is fairly obvious that phase sensitive detection is ultimately superior to intensity only detection, and that as such any derived limits for measurement sensitivity or information theoretic quantities likely remain valid, it is less clear what the consequences are at the level of individual channel bounds.
Developing this understanding is an evident direction for future work.

II
§Applications

II.1
Waveguide and Metasurface (like) Channel Bounds

As a waveguide provides a near ideal example of communication via channels—by extruding some material pattern, power can be efficiently coupled between a pair of (largely) separated volumes through a small set of discrete, low-loss modes—we being by examining channel bounds in the context of waveguide like mediators.
Directly, Fig.
2
(a) bounds the number of independently addressable channels a photonic system of given size can support above a chosen cutoff threshold
c
: For instance, focusing on the upper frame, no matter what structuring is employed, no
1.0\times 1.0\times 5.0\;\lambda
mediator derived from a silicon-like material (
\chi=13.6+0.05i
) can support more than twenty-five unity amplitude channels.
One of the most interesting aspects of this data is the astounding extent to which the bounds suggest that it may be possible to multiplex medium distance communication using a wavelength scale structure.
Towards the
l=5\lambda
right edge of the upper plot, all but the
c=0.10
cutoff line appear to be rapidly tending towards near stable asymptotics.
Although the appearance of these plateaus may in part be due to numerical artifacts (see Supplementary), the recorded channel counts are much larger than what is realized in typical waveguides.

The metasurface-style limits depicted in Fig.
2
(b) apply to the alternative (commonly encountered) situation of needing to transmit information across some fixed distance using a comparitively thin mediator: e.g., beam shaping
Yang et al. 2018
, mode conversion
Akgol et al. 2018
, and (de)multiplexing
Frellsen et al. 2016
.
A number of noteworthy features can be identified across the four illustrated settings.
First, as a function of the side length parameter
h
, the number of channels above any given cutoff is seen to rapidly increase and then effectively saturate; in going from
1.0\times 1.0\times 1.0\lambda
to
4.0\times 4.0\times 1.0\lambda
the maximal number of realizable channels having at least unity amplitude at a separation distance of
d=\lambda/4
grows from
\approx 8
to
\approx 32
, exhibiting scaling roughly proportional to the increase in area; in going from
4.0\times 4.0\times 1.0\lambda
to
8.0\times 8.0\times 1.0\lambda
only minor changes in the bounds occur until the separation reaches
\approx 5\lambda
, which may be simply attributed to the larger solid angle covered by the larger components at a fixed distance.
While great care should always be taken in inferring absolute trends from limits—the bounds may simply be loose—the suggested implications for metasurface design are both sensible and non-trivial.
At any given separation there is likely much to be gained in regards to achievable multi-functionality by designing over larger “non-local” cell volumes.
Yet, these improvements do not persist indefinitely, at least as far as can be extrapolated from channel bounds on the relatively small mediators we have studied.
Second, as highlighted by the relative differences between the
4.0\times 4.0\times 4.0\lambda
bounds and the
8.0\times 8.0\times 1.0\lambda
bounds, the ratio of thickness to area plays an important role in setting potentially accessible channel characteristics.
Although the two system volumes are equal, thicker component regions presumably allows for the realization of a greater number of large amplitude channels until a separation distance of
\approx 5.0\lambda
, beyond which the channel bounds for smaller area system decay much more quickly.
Noting that the thickness of the mediator is linked to the thickness of the sender and receiver regions, it is not possible to disentangle whether these difference are primarily an effect of the distinct device geometries offered by altering the mediator design volume, or whether they are mainly caused by changes in the types of fields that can be excited and detected.
However, regardless of the precise origin, the bounds would seem to imply that increasing thickness near the wavelength scale provides access to important design possibilities
Miller 2023
.

II.2
Fisher Information for a Laser Detection Metasurface

Bounds on the minimum singular value
\sigma_{-1}
are of interest to the design of photonic information processing systems.
As a final application, we model a linear photonic information processing system as
\bm{y}=\bm{\mathrm{H}}\bm{x}+\bm{n}
, with input
\bm{x}
, output
\bm{y}
, linear transfer matrix
\bm{\mathrm{H}}
and additive noise
\bm{n}
.
Practical examples of such systems include waveguide multiplexers where
\bm{x}
,
\bm{y}
are the modal coefficients of the input and output waveguides, as well as on chip spectrometers as investigated in
Ma et al. 2025
.
The design of such devices centers on engineering
\bm{\mathrm{H}}
in order to induce robustness to noise, a design goal commonly quantified by the condition number
\text{cond}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\right)=\sigma_{1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\right)/\sigma_{-1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\right)
.
Intuitively,
\text{cond}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\right)
is a measure of the sensitivity of the inferred input
\bm{x}
to the observed noisy output
\bm{y}
.
Minimizing
\text{cond}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\right)
, trivially, amounts to minimizing
\sigma_{1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\right)
and maximizing
\sigma_{-1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\right)
.
However, minimizing
\sigma_{1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\right)
is frequently undesirable, since the largest channel amplitude corresponds to the maximum strength of a transmitted signal, which should be kept as high as possible.
Consequently, maximization of
\sigma_{-1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\right)
is often the true driver for minimization of
\text{cond}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\right)
, and upper bounds on
\sigma_{-1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\right)
effectively capture the scaling of noise robustness with regards to design parameters such as material susceptibility, device footprint, and the number of usable channels.

Another perspective on the importance of
\sigma_{-1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\right)
is given by the Fisher information matrix of the measured output
\bm{y}
.
Supposing additive Gaussian noise
\bm{n}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,N\right)
,
\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\bm{x},N\right)
, and the Fisher information matrix of
\bm{y}
about
\bm{x}
is
I_{\bm{y}}\left(\bm{x}\right)=\bm{\mathrm{H}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{H}}/N
.
Taking measurement to be formally described as an estimation of the value of
\bm{x}
given the observed
\bm{y}
,
I_{\bm{y}}\left(\bm{x}\right)
then sets the minimum estimator covariance via the Cramér-Rao bound: for any unbiased estimator
T\left(\bm{y}\right)
of
\bm{x}
,
\text{cov}\left(\bm{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{y}\right)\right)\succeq I_{\bm{y}}\left(\bm{x}\right)^{-1}=N(\bm{\mathrm{H}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{H}})^{-1}
.
Given that less estimator covariance means more estimation certainty, it is desirable to make
(\bm{\mathrm{H}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{H}})^{-1}
small in some sense, and maximizing
\sigma_{-1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\right)
is equivalent to minimizing the spectral norm of
(\bm{\mathrm{H}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{H}})^{-1}
.

\sigma_{1}(\bm{\mathrm{H}})
, in this Fisher information setting, has no significance.

As a demonstration of the
\sigma_{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{H}})
metric, Fig.
3
examines incident planewave detection via a structurable metasurface mediator and designable detector (receiver), schematically illustrated in Fig.
3
(top).
In more detail, the electric fields produced along the detector region are used to infer a plausible decomposition of the incident field in terms of various planewaves along different directions:
\bm{\mathrm{H}}=\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rf}\bm{\mathrm{S}}_{ff}
, where the columns of
\bm{\mathrm{S}}_{ff}
are unit planewave sources in the far-field with various incident angles
\phi
.
The metasurface
\epsilon(\bm{r})
is designed to maximize
\sigma_{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{H}}(\epsilon(\bm{r})))
which, as discussed above, is equivalent to minimizing the spectral norm of the ideal estimator covariance.
Bounds on
\sigma_{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{H}})
are computed using subspace optimization; essentially, for any probing source
\bm{s}
, QCQP upper bounds are computed on
\bm{s}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{H}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{H}}\bm{s}
which always bounds
\sigma_{-1}^{2}(\bm{\mathrm{H}})
.
The tightest such bound is then found by minimizing over
\bm{s}
.

Topology optimization with various proxy objectives to maximize
\sigma_{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{H}})
were performed to discover high-performing metasurfaces for comparison with the bounds. Directly maximizing
\sigma_{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{H}})
is found to be challenging and often leads to the optimization stalling out, likely because of the non-differentiability of
\sigma_{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{H}})
when
\bm{\mathrm{H}}
has degenerate minimum singular vectors. A proxy objective of minimizing
\sum 1/\sigma_{j}
(as used also in Ref.
Ma et al. 2025
) was found to be the most effective.

This threshold increases roughly linearly with

III
§Outlook

The most intriguing, and in some sense surprising, aspect of the proposed CFW method is how well it appears to align with realizable functionality.
For the three-dimensional examples, calculated results are similar enough to vacuum that, barring cases where the channel amplitudes are too small to be of practical interest, duality gaps substantially larger than an order of magnitude are unlikely (if not impossible).
For the laser awareness example, topology optimization generally finds designs within a factor of unity of the computed bounds, and in the situations in which larger differences are seen there are plausible explanations as to why such behaviour could be expected.
Most importantly, while the
Q_{n-1}
subspace selection implies that distinct channels can not fully reuse a single resonance—inputs and outputs must remain orthogonal—consistency of the implied material profile has not been enforced in any of the considered examples.
That is, although a methodology for asserting such a consistency condition completely compatible with the described CFW approach has been previously reported
Molesky et al. 2022
, in favour of reducing computational costs and pushing to more relevant design sizes, the bounds shown in
§Applications
do not guarantee a single structure having
n
singular values above a certain threshold.
If structural consistency constraints were included, the bounds given in
§Applications
could well be reduced by significant factors, particularly when multiple large amplitude channels are in principle accessible (the idea being that simultaneously activating many large amplitude channels in a single device of a moderate size will inescapably lead to performance reducing tradeoffs).
Moreover, in the three-dimensional examples there is no optimization over the CFW exclusion subspace
Q_{n-1}^{\perp}
(Eq. (
1a
)) beyond a possible reordering of the singular vectors of
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}
.
The depicted bounds, as detailed further in the supplementary information, are the result of an educated guess for
Q_{n-1}
, conceived with the express idea of properly limiting the decay characteristics of any included scattering medium.
Correspondingly, there is almost certainly a persistent overestimation of the attainable channel amplitude at large channel indices: to achieve the tightest bounds,
Q_{n-1}
should be selected to balance
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}
against
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}\bm{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}
in Eq. (
4
) and this has not been done.
Similar to the inclusion of structural consistency constraints, this shortcoming could also be mitigated at the cost of additional computational effort.

The presence of these outstanding directions for future work makes the agreement observed in
§Applications
all the more interesting.
Setting aside the physical context in which the findings of this article are based, there does not seem to be a convincing reason to suppose that use of the CFW theorem would lead to practically accurate performance predictions in view of a weakly constrained optimization in the way that we have used it.
Critically, the (outer) excluded subspace
Q^{\perp}_{n-1}
is always selected prior to the (inner) structural optimization from which bounds are determined.
Supposing the existence of at least two devices capable of mapping multiple, distinct, orthogonal inputs to multiple, distinct, orthogonal outputs, it is easy to imagine how the number of channels above a given cutoff cannot be reasonably bounded in this manner.
For example, let devices
(a)-(j)
all have an equal number of channels above any particular cutoff.
If the first
j
largest channels of device
(a)
do not align with the first
j
channels of any of the other devices, then the CFW approach we have explored could not indicate any decay over the first
j
indices, even if each device can only support one large channel.
The fact that such trends are not encountered highlights the central role that the physics of scattering is playing in the viability of Eq. (
1a
).
Because the free space Green function encapsulates the scattering
\bm{\mathrm{T}}
-operator, Eq.  (
4
), arbitrary material structuring can not create arbitrary channel characteristics.
No matter what is done to shape the material, convex relaxation proves that the features of the vacuum background necessarily persist to a large extent.
Echoing the conclusions of other recent works on fundamental limits in device engineering
Chao et al. 2022
,
Guo and Fan 2024
,
Kuang et al. 2025
,
Chao et al. 2025
, we take this as evidence that the mathematics of scattering are more intricate than one might initially assume.

V
§Prior Methods

We are aware of only two previous quantitative methods for bounding the
n^{th}
singular value of an electromagnetic transfer function allowing for arbitrarily structured materials: separation bounds and Frobenius norm bounds.
Although both of these approaches remain valuable in terms of conceptual implications and ease of application, sample calculations have found the method described herein to be consistently (and often orders of magnitude) tighter than either of these approaches.
In the case of separation bounds, this follows mathematically.
The
1|n
singular value bound (see below) from which these limits follow can be derived by combining a particular choice of
Q_{n-1}
with an additional relaxation in the Courant-Fischer-Weyl (CFW) min-max principle.
The final form is essentially equivalent to assuming that every pair of singular vectors is (respectively) perfectly resonant in the sender and receiver.
In the case of Frobenius norm bounds, the distinct nature of the approximations used makes proving a general ordering more difficult.
However, the square-root decay behaviour characteristic of a Frobenius approach is unphysically slow based on comparison with either separation bounds or CFW.
At large values of
n
, it is almost certain that the Frobenius norm will vastly overestimate achievable singular values.

V.1
Separation Bounds

In Ref.
Kuang et al. 2025
, shape-independent upper bounds on
\sigma_{n}
for current-current communication between two volumes (that can be separated by a spherical surface) are derived based on the following
1|n
inequality
.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{A}}:X\rightarrow Y
and
\bm{\mathrm{B}}:Y\rightarrow Z
be linear operators between complex vector spaces.
Take
\sigma_{n}\left(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\right)
to denote the
n^{th}
singular value of the linear operator
\bm{\mathrm{C}}
.

\sigma_{n}\left(\bm{\mathrm{AB}}\right)\leq\min\left\{\sigma_{1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{A}}\right)\sigma_{n}\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}\right),\sigma_{n}\left(\bm{\mathrm{A}}\right)\sigma_{1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}\right)\right\}.

(5)

Proof of
1|n
inequality.

Select the first
n-1
right singular vectors of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}
to form
Q_{n-1}
.
By CFW,

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}\left(\bm{\mathrm{AB}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in Q^{\perp}_{n-1}}\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{B}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{\mathrm{B}}\bm{\mathrm{x}}

(6)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{1}^{2}\left(\bm{\mathrm{A}}\right)\max_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in Q^{\perp}_{n-1}}\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{B}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{B}}\bm{\mathrm{x}}=\sigma_{1}^{2}\left(\bm{\mathrm{A}}\right)\sigma_{n}^{2}\left(\bm{\mathrm{B}}\right).

The second inequality forming the minimum follows by replacing
\bm{\mathrm{AB}}
with
\bm{\mathrm{B}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{A}}^{\dagger}
.
∎

Next, taking
s
and
r
subscripts to denote restrictions into the sender and receiver volumes, consider the formal description of the
\bm{\mathrm{W}}
-operator of the system—the linear operator mapping any initial polarization current (a source) to the total (final) polarization current consistent with the scattering characteristics of the system
Chao et al. 2022
—in block form:

\bm{\mathrm{W}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{rr}-\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}_{rr}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{rr}&-\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}_{rr}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{rs}\\
-\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}_{ss}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{sr}&\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{ss}-\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}_{ss}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{ss}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}

(7)

with
\bm{\mathrm{I}}
the identity operator (e.g.
\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{rr}
the identity operator restricted to the volume of the receiver),
\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}
the material susceptibility taking electromagnetic fields to polarization currents (e.g., the electric susceptibility,
\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}\bm{\mathrm{f}}=\int_{\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}}\chi_{ij}\left(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime},\omega\right)f_{j}\left(\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}\right)=\left(1-\epsilon\left(\omega\right)\right)f_{i}\left(\bm{\mathrm{r}}^{\prime}\right)
in the case of an isotropic, non-magnetic, medium), and
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}
the vacuum Green function, which we define without the usual frequency dependence—
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}
inverts the Maxwell operator of the domain with radiative boundary conditions.
Using block matrix inversion,

\bm{\mathrm{W}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{rr}&\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{rr}^{(i)}\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}_{rr}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{rs}\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{ss}\\
\bm{\mathrm{W}}^{(i)}_{ss}\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}_{ss}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{sr}\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{rr}&\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{ss}\end{bmatrix},

(8)

where
\bm{\mathrm{W}}^{(i)}
is the
\bm{\mathrm{W}}
operator for the appropriate isolated object (e.g.,
\bm{\mathrm{W}}^{(i)}_{ss}
is the
\bm{\mathrm{W}}
operator for the source in the absence of any receiver), and
\bm{\mathrm{W}}
is the dressed operator accounting for the presence of both scattering volumes (e.g.,
\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{ss}
maps initial currents in the source volume to self-consistent polarization currents in the source volume, accounting for all scattering from the receiver region).

With these two results established the bounds given in Ref.
Kuang et al. 2025
follow directly from prior results.
Current-current communication between the source and receiver volumes corresponds to the source-receiver entry of Eq. (
8
),
\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{rs}=\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{rr}^{(i)}\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}_{rr}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{rs}\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{ss}
.
Applying the
1|n
inequality twice to this relation,

\sigma_{n}\left(\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{rs}\right)\leq\sigma_{1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{rr}^{(i)}\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}_{rr}\right)\sigma_{1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{ss}\right)\sigma_{n}\left(\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{rs}\right).

(9)

Because both
\sigma_{1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{rr}^{(i)}\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}_{rr}\right)
and
\sigma_{1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{ss}\right)
must be bounded for any physical material, c.f. Ref.
Miller et al. 2014
and Ref.
Molesky et al. 2020b
, Eq. (
9
) proves the crucial point that communication is always limited by free space propagation across the vacuum separation.
No matter how the collection of objects comprising the sender and receiver are structured, the number of free space channels available between the two volumes ultimately restrict achievable communication.

However, in considering electromagnetic interactions between wavelength scale volumes, which constitutes the most meaningful application of Eq. (
9
) in light of the singular value growth characteristics elucidated in Ref.
Miller et al. 2025
, the procedure described in Ref.
Kuang et al. 2025
for applying Eq. (
9
) will almost certainly be quite loose.
First, as further detailed in Ref.
Molesky et al. 2020b
, the necessity of coupling power from the source to the receiver is incompatible with the assumption that both volumes optimally amplify each left and right singular vector: a careful analysis of the interplay between
\sigma_{1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{rr}^{(i)}\right)
and
\sigma_{1}\left(\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{ss}\right)
is required to accurately approximate the value of these terms.
Second, there are many practical situations in which the main concern is structuring an intermediate (mediator) volume, which is neither part of the source or the receiver.
Lumping the mediator into one side of the two volume description of Eq. (
9
) (e.g. as a super-source) will typically lead to a substantial over-estimation of the singular values of
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{rs}
—the volumes will be too large, and the separation will be too small.
Finally, the blanket assumption of
\sigma_{1}\left(\#\right)
response for
\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{rr}^{(i)}
and
\bm{\mathrm{W}}_{ss}
obviates the difficulties of supporting multiple channels from the perspective of the source and receiver.
For large devices, this relaxation could well be accurate (e.g. near perfect absorbers do exist
Molesky et al. 2019
).
However, for near wavelength scale design volumes, it is likely a very strong assumption
Molesky et al. 2022
.

With this said, the understanding provided Eq. (
9
) is highly useful.
Although an incomplete picture, the characteristics of the vacuum Green function between the design volumes will play a meaningful role in determining the sort of functionality that can be realized, regardless of how the materials are structured.

V.2
Frobenius Norm Bounds

Bounds on the
n^{th}
singular value of the Green function are also set by the
n^{th}
partial Frobenius norm, which can be computed based on techniques described in Refs.
Miller et al. 2016
,
Molesky et al. 2020b
.
Namely, if
c_{1}\geq c_{2}\geq\ldots\geq c_{n}\geq 0
, and
\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}=v
, then the largest
c_{n}
can be is
v/n
.
Since the squared singular values of any linear operator
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
respect this conditions,

\sigma_{n}\leq\lVert\bm{\mathrm{A}}\rVert_{\mathtt{F}_{n}}/\sqrt{n}.

(10)

Replacing
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
with
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rs}
, Eq. (
10
) bounds the
n^{th}
singular value of the Green function, supposing a bound on the partial Frobenius norm
\lVert\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rs}\rVert_{\mathtt{F}_{n}}
.

While any number of more exact optimization based approaches could be applied, c.f.
Chao et al. 2022
, the principle method that has been used to determine bounds on the partial Frobenius norm of the Green function in past literature is the von Neumann
trace inequality
.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{A}}:X\rightarrow Y
and
\bm{\mathrm{B}}:Y\rightarrow X
be linear operators between the complex vector spaces
X
of dimension
l
and
Y
of dimension
m
.
Take
\bm{\mathrm{A}}=\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{a}\bm{\mathrm{D}}_{a}\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{a}^{\dagger}
and
\bm{\mathrm{B}}=\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{b}\bm{\mathrm{D}}_{b}\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{b}^{\dagger}
to be SVDs of
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{B}}
.

\left|\tr\bm{\mathrm{B}}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\right|\leq\sum^{\min\left\{l,m\right\}}_{k=1}\sigma^{b}_{k}\sigma^{a}_{k},

(11)

where
\left\{\sigma_{1}^{a},\ldots,\sigma_{l}^{a}\right\}
is the set of singular values of
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
and
\left\{\sigma_{1}^{b},\ldots,\sigma_{m}^{b}\right\}
is the set of singular values of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}
.

Eq. (
11
) may be interpreted as a statement regarding freely chosen basis vectors: if
\bm{\mathrm{D}}_{a}
and
\bm{\mathrm{D}}_{b}
are fixed, and
\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{a},\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{b},\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{a}
, and
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{b}
may be freely selected within appropriate operator spaces in order maximize
\left|\tr\bm{\mathrm{B}}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\right|
, then
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{b}=\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{a}
and
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{a}=\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{b}
is an optimal choice.
More generally,
\left|\tr\bm{\mathrm{C}}\right|
of a finite composition
\bm{\mathrm{C}}=\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{q}\ldots\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{2}\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{1}
is maximized when the input and output bases of
\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{k+1}
and
\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{k}
are identical,
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{k+1}=\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{k}
.

Proof of trace inequality.

Assume that
\bm{\mathrm{B}}
is rank one,
\bm{\mathrm{B}}=\sigma^{b}\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{b}\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{b}^{\dagger}
with
\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{b}
and
\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{b}
unit vectors in
X
and
Y
and
\sigma^{b}>0
.
Take
c_{1},...,c_{l}
to be the coefficients of expansion of
\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{b}
in the basis of
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{a}
. and
d_{1},...,d_{m}
to be the coefficients of expansion of
\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{b}
in the basis of
\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{a}
.

\displaystyle\left|\tr\bm{\mathrm{B}}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\right|=\sigma^{b}\left|\sum_{k,j,i}\sigma^{a}_{k}\left(\bm{\mathrm{v}}^{a\dagger}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{v}}^{a}_{k}\right)\left(\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{k}^{a\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{j}^{a}\right)\left(c_{j}d_{k}^{*}\right)\right|=

\displaystyle\sigma^{b}\left|\sum_{k}\sigma^{a}_{k}c_{k}d_{k}^{*}\right|\leq\sigma^{b}\sqrt{\left|\sum_{k}\sigma^{a}_{k}c_{k}c_{k}^{*}\right|~\left|\sum_{k}\sigma^{a}_{k}d_{k}d_{k}^{*}\right|}\leq

\displaystyle\sigma^{b}\sigma_{1}^{a}\sqrt{\left|\sum_{k}c_{k}c_{k}^{*}\right|~\left|\sum_{k}d_{k}d_{k}^{*}\right|}=\sigma^{b}\sigma^{a}_{1},

with the third relation following from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
Setting
c_{1}=d_{1}=1
,
\left|\tr\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{\mathrm{B}}\right|=\sigma^{a}_{1}\sigma^{b}
.

Suppose that the proposition has been verified for the composition of any rank
n-1
linear operator with
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
.
Let
\bm{\mathrm{B}}=\bm{\mathrm{E}}+\bm{\mathrm{F}}
, with
\bm{\mathrm{F}}=\sigma^{b}_{n}\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{b}\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{b}^{\dagger}
and
\bm{\mathrm{E}}=\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{b}\left(\bm{\mathrm{D}}_{b}-\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}\sigma_{n}^{b}\right)\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{b}^{\dagger}
.
Here,
\bm{\mathrm{I}}_{n}
is the identity operator with zeros replacing the usual ones at all positions along the main diagonal greater than
n
—the identity restricted to the first
n
singular values.
Extending the conventions used above,

\displaystyle\left|\tr\bm{\mathrm{F}}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sigma^{b}_{n}\left|\sum_{q}\sum_{k,j,i}\sigma^{a}_{k}\left(\bm{\mathrm{v}}^{a\dagger}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{v}}^{a}_{k}\right)\left(\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{k}^{a\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{j}^{a}\right)\left(c_{j}^{q}d_{k}^{q*}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sigma^{b}_{n}\left|\sum_{k}\sigma_{k}^{a}\left(\sum_{q}c_{k}^{q}d_{k}^{q*}\right)\right|\leq\sum_{k}\sigma^{b}_{n}\sigma^{a}_{k}\left|\sum_{q}c_{k}^{q}d_{k}^{q*}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}\sigma^{b}_{n}\sigma^{a}_{k},

with the third relation following from the triangle inequality, and the fourth relation following from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
Therefore, using the fact that
\left|\tr\bm{\mathrm{E}}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\right|\leq\sum_{k}\sigma^{d}_{k}\sigma^{a}_{k}
because
\bm{\mathrm{E}}
is of rank
n-1
,

\displaystyle\left|\tr\bm{\mathrm{B}}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}\sigma^{d}_{k}\sigma^{a}_{k}+\sum_{k}\sigma^{b}_{n}\sigma_{k}^{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left(\sigma_{k}^{d}+\sigma_{n}^{b}\right)\sigma_{k}^{a}=\sum_{k}\sigma^{b}_{k}\sigma^{a}_{k}.

Setting
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{b}=\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{a}
and
\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{b}=\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{a}
the inequality is saturated, proving that these choices are optimal.
∎

As a consequence of Eq. (
11
) selecting
\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{b}=\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{a}
and
\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{b}=\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{a}
maximizes every partial trace of
\left|\tr\bm{\mathrm{B}}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\right|
:

\lVert\bm{\mathrm{B}}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\rVert_{\mathtt{F}_{n}}^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma^{a}_{k}\sigma^{b}_{k},

with
\lVert\bm{\mathrm{B}}\bm{\mathrm{A}}\rVert_{\mathtt{F}_{k}}^{2}
denoting the Frobenius norm of
\bm{\mathrm{B}}\bm{\mathrm{A}}
restricted to the subspace spanned by the first
n
(left or right) singular vectors—the
n^{th}
partial Frobenius norm.

In Ref.
Molesky et al. 2020b
we have shown that eq. (
11
) implies that the square of the
n^{th}
partial Frobenius norm of the electromagnetic Green function, at a fixed frequency, between an arbitrarily shaped sender
s
and receiver
r
, with local linear electric susceptibilities
\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}_{s}
and
\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}_{r}
, is bounded by

\lVert\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rs}\rVert_{\mathtt{F}_{n}}^{2}\leq\frac{\eta}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{4\kappa_{k}}{\left(1+\kappa_{k}\right)^{2}}+\Theta\left(\kappa_{k}-1\right)\frac{\left(1-\kappa_{k}\right)^{2}}{\left(1+\kappa_{k}\right)^{2}},

(12)

where
\eta=\left(\Im\chi_{r}\Im\chi_{s}\right)^{-1}
,
\kappa_{k}=\zeta_{r}\zeta_{s}\gamma_{k}^{\circ rs2}
,
\zeta_{r}=\lVert\chi_{r}\rVert^{2}/\Im\chi_{r}
and
\zeta_{s}=\lVert\chi_{s}\rVert^{2}/\Im\chi_{s}
are the material factors associated with
\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}_{r}
and
\bm{\mathrm{\chi}}_{s}
,
\Theta:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq}
is the Heaviside step function, and
\gamma^{\circ rs}_{k}
is the
k^{th}
singular value of
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{rs}
—the vacuum Green function between the predefined design volumes containing the two bodies.
As in our prior work, all Green functions appearing here are defined without the usual frequency dependence:
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
is the inverse of the usual Maxwell operator scaled by frequency.

VI.1
Green function singular values

The basic paradigm of electromagnetic communication used throughout the main text consists of a set of channels mapping fields between sender and receiver volumes
1
1

1

Actual communication also requires encoding the source current and decoding the received field.
These additional conversions must be composed with the Green function to properly model physical systems.
.
The relevant Green function for such a sender-mediator-receiver configuration is

\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rs}=\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{rs}+\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{rd}\bm{\mathrm{T}}_{dd}\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{ds},

(13)

with
\circ
subscripts denoting bare (empty space) operators and
\bm{\mathrm{T}}_{dd}(\bm{\epsilon})
the scattering

-operator (physically connecting vacuum incident fields with the induced polarization within the scattering material) of the designable part of the communication system—which necessarily contains the design volume of the mediator, and may contain some part of the receiver and source (c.f. Ref.

For our purposes
\bm{\epsilon}
amounts to the portion of the system where the design profile

can be freely controlled:
the variable of the optimization problem

\displaystyle\underset{\epsilon(\bm{\mathrm{r}})}{\max/\min}\quad\sigma_{k}\{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rs}(\epsilon(\bm{\mathrm{r}}))\}

\displaystyle~\text{s.t.}~\left(\omega^{-2}\nabla\times\nabla\times-\epsilon\left(\bm{\mathrm{r}}\right)\right)\bm{\mathrm{G}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\bm{\mathrm{r^{\prime}}}\right)=\bm{\mathrm{I}}\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}}-\bm{\mathrm{r^{\prime}}})

\displaystyle~\epsilon(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\begin{cases}\text{fixed}&\bm{\mathrm{r}}\notin D\\
1\text{ or }\chi&\bm{\mathrm{r}}\in D\end{cases}.

(14)

Per the second equation of the main text, let
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\perp}

\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
a column grouping of orthonormal basis vectors for

\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{\perp}=\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\bm{\mathrm{Q}}

Setting
\lVert\bm{\mathrm{s}}\rVert_{2}=1

An upper bound for

\displaystyle\max\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\perp}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rs}^{\circ\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rs}^{\circ}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\perp}+2\Re\left\{\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\perp}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rs}^{\circ\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rd}^{\circ}\bm{\mathrm{t}}\right\}+\bm{\mathrm{t}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rd}^{\circ\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rd}^{\circ}\bm{\mathrm{t}}

\displaystyle~\text{s.t.}~\lVert\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\perp}\rVert_{2}^{2}=0\text{~\&~}\lVert\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\perp}\rVert_{2}^{2}=1.

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\perp},\bm{\mathrm{t}}}\,\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\dagger}_{\perp}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rs}^{\circ\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rs}^{\circ}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\perp}+2\Re\left\{\bm{\mathrm{q}}^{\dagger}_{\perp}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rs}^{\circ\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rd}^{\circ}\bm{\mathrm{t}}\right\}+\bm{\mathrm{t}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rd}^{\circ\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rd}^{\circ}\bm{\mathrm{t}}

VI.2
Subspace Optimization

Eq. (
16
) gives bounds on
\sigma_{n}
regardless of the chosen subspace represented by
Q_{n-1}
.
As an extension, it is natural to consider optimizing the subspace to obtain the tightest bound.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{u}}
be the concatenated vector formed from
\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\perp}
and
\bm{\mathrm{t}}
.
Taking
\bm{\mathrm{H}}_{o}
to be the Hermitian matrix associated with the objective of Eq. (
16
),
\bm{\mathrm{H}}_{j}
to be the Hermitian matrix associated with the
j^{th}
constraint defined by Eq. (
15
),
\bm{\mathrm{H}}_{Q}
to be the Hermitian matrix associated with the subspace orthogonality condition
\lVert\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\perp}\rVert_{2}^{2}=0
and
\bm{\mathrm{H}}_{1}
to be the matrix associated with the normalization constraint
\lVert\bm{\mathrm{q}}_{\perp}\rVert_{2}^{2}=1
, the Lagrangian of Eq. (
16
) is

\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\mathrm{u}},\bm{\mathrm{\mu}},\mu_{Q},\bm{\mathrm{H}}_{Q}\right)=\bm{\mathrm{u}}^{\dagger}\left(\bm{\mathrm{H}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{\mu}}\right)-\mu_{Q}\bm{\mathrm{H}}_{Q}\right)\bm{\mathrm{u}}+\mu_{1},

(18)

where
\bm{\mathrm{H}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{\mu}}\right)=\bm{\mathrm{H}}_{o}-\mu_{1}\bm{\mathrm{H}}_{1}-\sum_{j}\mu_{j}\bm{\mathrm{H}}_{j}
.
(Additional details on this transformation to purely quadratic forms are given in the supplementary information of Ref.
Kuang et al. 2020
and Ref.
Molesky et al. 2025
).
The dual of eq. (
18
), upper bounding the value of
\sigma_{n}
, is

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\bm{\mathrm{\mu}},\mu_{Q},\bm{\mathrm{H}}_{Q})\equiv\max_{\bm{\mathrm{u}}}~\mathcal{L}\left(\bm{\mathrm{u}},\bm{\mathrm{\mu}},\mu_{Q},\bm{\mathrm{H}}_{Q}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mu_{1}&\bm{\mathrm{H}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{\mu}}\right)-\mu_{Q}\bm{\mathrm{H}}_{Q}\preceq 0\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

VII.2
Randomized Singular Value Decomposition

Performing a singular value decomposition of the vacuum operators
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{xy}
for the different regions
xy\in\left\{rs,rd,dd,ds\right\}
is, in general, a non-trivial task due to the size associated with the discretized integral operators.
For a three dimensional problem discretized on a cuboid grid of
N_{i}
cells in each direction
i\in\left\{x,y,z\right\}
, the size of the operator is
(3N_{x}N_{y}N_{z})\times(3N_{x}N_{y}N_{z})
.
This
\mathcal{O}\left(N^{6}\right)
scaling in memory precludes explicit storage and effectively requires matrix-free methods for any reasonably large system.
For the examples presented in the main text, we have used randomized singular value decomposition (RSVD) following Algorithm 4.4 of
Halko et al. 2011
with power iterations and oversampling.

Concretely, to return
k
singular components,
\ell=k+p
test vectors are drawn (here,
p=2k
) following
q
power iterations (here,
q=14
).
The method constructs a small matrix
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{xy}\ni\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{xy}\bm{\mathrm{Q}}^{\dagger}_{xy}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{xy}\approx\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{xy}
and decomposes
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{xy}\approx\bm{\mathrm{V}}_{xy}\bm{\mathrm{D}}_{xy}\bm{\mathrm{U}}^{\dagger}_{xy}=\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{xy}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{V}}}_{xy}\bm{\mathrm{D}}_{xy}\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{xy}^{\dagger}
where
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{xy}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{xy}=\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{V}}}_{xy}\bm{\mathrm{D}}_{xy}\bm{\mathrm{U}}_{xy}^{\dagger}
is the standard singular value decomposition of a tall-and-skinny matrix which can be done efficiently on a dense matrix.
The matrix with orthonormal columns,
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}_{xy}
, turns out to be easily computed by orthonormalizing the matrix
\bm{\mathrm{Y}}_{xy}=\left(\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{xy}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ\dagger}_{xy}\right)^{q}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{xy}\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}
where
\bm{\mathrm{\Omega}}
is a tall-and-skinny matrix with
\ell

The use of
q\geq 0
power iterations sharpens the singular value spectrum.
Intuitively, random test vectors probe all directions; after application by
\mathbf{G}^{\circ}_{xy}
(and power iterations), their images concentrate along the dominant column space, so
\mathbf{Q}_{xy}
captures the top left singular subspace with high probability (oversampling provides a safety margin
Halko et al. 2011

The particular choice of
q=14
and
p=2k
used here was made by ensuring the relative RSVD dual residuals,
\lVert\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ\dagger}\bm{\mathrm{v}}_{i}-\sigma_{i}\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{i}\rVert_{2}/\sigma_{i}
were smaller than

and by comparing the largest singular value of
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{rs}\bm{\mathrm{U}}^{\perp}_{n-1}
and
\bm{\mathrm{V}}^{\perp}_{n-1}\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{rs}
against

The target rank

For instance, in the

mediator result, a rank of

targeted to use up the 80  GB of VRAM available on an NVIDIA H100, courtesy of the Digital Alliance of Canada. Note that at the discretization used of 32 768 voxels per cubic wavelength, the associated operator would have an actual rank of 3 145 728.

VII.3
Numerical Fidelity of the Bounds

Due to the inherently randomized nature of the scheme for computing the ordered singular value bounds in three dimensions, the main lessons that should be drawn from Fig. 2 are comparative: it is better to focus on spectral trends (decay rates, relative spacing, and cross-scenario ordering) of
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{rs}
over the absolute magnitudes of any given singular value.
Although we firmly believe that the current bounds are quantitatively useful, variations within an order of unity would not be surprising.

Further, as the present three-dimensional results only covered the possibility of a structured mediator, it is reasonable to wonder how the bounds might be affected if all three regions—the sender, mediator, and receiver—are considered to be designable.
The current issue with treating such full structuring scenarios lies in the fact that our singular value chain approach relies on the vacuum Green function basis connecting either the source or the receiver with the design region.
That is, simultaneous overlap between the designable region, the source region, and the receiver region leads to a pathology with the use of the randomized singular value decomposition algorithm.
Precisely, in the presence of mutual overlap, there does not seem to be an obvious way of eliminate highly localized “dark” channels, related to the singular part of the Green function, that cannot be effectively leveraged for communication but nevertheless possess large amplitudes.
The optimization must eliminate each of these possibilities before getting to useful channels, which for even modest design sizes becomes computationally intractable.

While we have not yet developed an acceptable scheme for removing these problematic terms without compromising the integrity of the bounds, the CFW approach may still potentially be used to estimate optimal channel characteristics when the sender, mediator and receiver can all be structured by simply ignoring any overlap when constructing the singular vector basis of either
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{rd}
or
\bm{\mathrm{G}}^{\circ}_{ds}
.
If bounds are absolutely necessary, a likely very loose form could also be found by combining the CFW method with the Frobenius norm technique.
The basic steps of this constructions are as follows.
Begin by supposing that only the mediator can be structured, producing a chain of singular value bounds.
The full structuring case can then be reduced to the arbitrary sender-receiver situation studied in Ref.
Molesky et al. 2020b
by replacing the usual background Green function with the Green function determined by the presence of the mediator.
Consequently, Eq. (
12
) can then be used to produce a bound on the full structuring scenario by replacing each
\gamma_{k}^{\circ rs}
with the appropriate bound.
