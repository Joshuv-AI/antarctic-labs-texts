Endoscopic retrograde appendiceal irrigation for children with recurrent abdominal pain and appendiceal fecaliths: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Fengge Wang, Zhenni Zhu, Chijun Hu, Qin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrent abdominal pain (RAP) in children with appendiceal fecaliths and mesenteric lymphadenitis poses a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, often mimicking appendicitis. The role of interventional endoscopy in this setting is undefined.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed children with RAP, sonographically confirmed appendiceal fecaliths, and ileocecal lymphadenitis who underwent ultrasound-guided endoscopic retrograde appendiceal irrigation (ERAI) from 2021 to 2023. Technical success, complications, immediate symptom resolution, and follow-up outcomes were assessed.ResultsTotally, 73 patients (mean age 8.3 years) who underwent successful ERAI (100% technical success) were included in this study for safety analysis. Three were lost to follow-up, leaving 70 for efficacy analysis. Immediate pain resolution was reported in 98.6% (69/70) of patients with follow-up. Minor complications such as self-limiting bleeding and procedure-related discomfort occurred in 8.2% (6/73) and 9.6% (7/73) of patients, respectively. There were no perforations or major adverse events. During a median follow-up of 15.5 months, the abdominal pain recurrence rate was 18.6%. The sonographic prevalence of mesenteric lymphadenitis decreased significantly from 100% preoperatively to 21.9% postoperatively (p<0.01).ConclusionsERAI appears to be a feasible and safe super-minimally invasive procedure for selected children with RAP associated with appendiceal fecaliths and lymphadenitis, and was associated with rapid symptomatic relief and a significant reduction in lymphadenitis.Keywords: Child Health, Gastroenterology

Introduction

Recurrent abdominal pain (RAP) affects approximately 10%–15% of school-aged children, representing a substantial clinical burden and diagnostic challenge.1 While often classified within functional gastrointestinal disorders, a significant subset of children may have an underlying organic component. This is particularly so for those presenting with a clinical syndrome characterized by recurrent right lower quadrant (RLQ) pain, sonographic evidence of an appendiceal fecalith, and frequently coexisting mesenteric lymphadenitis in the absence of criteria for acute appendicitis.2 Traditional management strategies, primarily focused on symptomatic relief and reassurance, frequently result in persistent symptoms, recurrent healthcare visits, and reduced quality of life.3

Appendiceal fecaliths are increasingly recognized as incidental findings in children undergoing abdominal imaging for RAP.4 Once considered clinically insignificant, emerging evidence suggests that these concretions may serve as a nidus for chronic, low-grade inflammation within the appendiceal lumen.5 We hypothesize that retained fecaliths create a cycle of persistent luminal irritation, localized lymphatic engagement, and neuroimmune signaling, providing a plausible anatomical basis for recurrent episodes of pain in susceptible children.

A paradigm shift toward organ preservation in appendiceal pathology, driven by recognition of the role of the appendix in gut immunity,6 has spurred the development of minimally invasive alternatives to appendectomy. Building on endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy (ERAT),7 8 we developed endoscopic retrograde appendiceal irrigation (ERAI) as a targeted procedure to mechanically remove fecaliths in children with RAP. This retrospective cohort study is the first systematic evaluation of the feasibility of ERAI, its safety, and clinical outcomes in this specific pediatric population.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

We reviewed the electronic medical records of all pediatric patients (aged ≤18 years) who underwent ERAI for suspected appendiceal fecalith-related abdominal pain at the Department of Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hubei Maternal and Child Health Hospital, between August 2021 and June 2023.

To address potential sources of bias, we employed the following strategies: selection bias was minimized by applying consistent inclusion and exclusion criteria; information bias was reduced by using standardized ultrasound diagnostic criteria and a structured data collection form; and confounding was addressed by excluding patients with other identifiable causes of abdominal pain. All endoscopic procedures were performed by the same team of experienced pediatric gastroenterologists to ensure procedural consistency. Sample size was determined by including all consecutive eligible patients who underwent ERAI during the study period (August 2021 to June 2023), without a pre-specified sample size calculation, as this was an exploratory retrospective cohort study aimed at evaluating the feasibility and initial outcomes of this novel procedure.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) A history of recurrent or persistent RLQ abdominal pain (duration ≥2 months with ≥3 episodes of pain). (2) Ultrasonographic confirmation of an appendiceal fecalith (hyperechoic focus with posterior acoustic shadowing within the appendix), with or without appendiceal lumen dilatation. Ultrasound was the primary diagnostic tool. Computed tomography (CT) was reserved for cases with equivocal ultrasound findings, suspicion of complicated appendicitis, or to evaluate alternative diagnoses. (3) Absence of clinical and imaging features indicative of acute appendicitis, specifically including absence of fever (body temperature <38 °C), no leukocytosis (white blood cell count <10×10⁹/L), and no neutrophilia (neutrophil percentage <75%); ultrasonographic findings demonstrating appendiceal wall thickness ≤3 mm, appendiceal compressibility, absence of periappendiceal fat echogenicity, and no intraperitoneal free fluid; CT scan (if performed) demonstrating no periappendiceal inflammatory changes.9 (4) Concurrent sonographic evidence of mesenteric lymphadenitis, defined as ≥3 lymph nodes in the ileocecal region, with the largest node having a longitudinal diameter >10 mm, a transverse diameter >5 mm, and a longitudinal-to-transverse ratio >2.10

Exclusion criteria included: (1) Diagnosis of acute or suppurative appendicitis requiring immediate appendectomy or standard ERAT. Specific exclusion indicators included: clinical presentation of migratory right lower quadrant pain accompanied by fever, leukocytosis, or neutrophilia; imaging findings demonstrating appendiceal wall thickness >3 mm, non-compressible appendix, increased periappendiceal fat echogenicity, or periappendiceal fluid collection. (2) Other identified causes of abdominal pain (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease, Meckel’s diverticulum, and intussusception). (3) Incomplete medical records or loss to follow-up.

ERAI procedure

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia by experienced pediatric gastroenterologists. The technique was adapted from the principles of ERAT7 and comprised the following steps.

Standardized bowel preparation prior to the procedure

All patients received a liquid diet on the day before the procedure, followed by oral administration of polyethylene glycol electrolyte powder (dose adjusted according to body weight) for bowel cleansing on the evening prior to the intervention.

Colonoscopic examination and cannulation

A colonoscope fitted with a transparent cap was advanced to the cecum. The terminal ileum and ileocecal region were inspected to exclude other pathologies. Gerlach’s valve was gently pushed aside using the cap to expose the appendiceal orifice, which was examined for signs of inflammation. A guidewire-assisted cannulation of the appendiceal lumen was then performed using an endoscopic retrograde rholangiopancreatography (ERCP) sphincterotome.

Ultrasound-guided assessment and aspiration

With real-time bedside ultrasound monitoring, a 20 mL syringe was used to aspirate any retained fluid or debris from the appendiceal cavity via the catheter. Sulfur hexafluoride microbubble contrast was injected to perform an appendiceal contrast study to visualize the lumen anatomy, identify filling defects (fecaliths), and rule out perforation.

Targeted irrigation and fecalith extraction

The lumen was copiously irrigated with normal saline under simultaneous suction until the effluent was clear. If contrast imaging revealed persistent, non-displaceable fecaliths, they were extracted using a retrieval basket or balloon under combined endoscopic and ultrasonic guidance.

Final evaluation

A final contrast study was performed to confirm complete irrigation and luminal patency. A plastic stent was placed only in cases with significant luminal stenosis or purulent debris, at the operator’s discretion.

Data collection and outcomes

Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, primary and accompanying symptoms, physical examination findings, and antibiotic usage prior to ERAI, were extracted from medical records. Intra-procedural data included endoscopic findings (e.g., orifice morphology) and ultrasonographic features (e.g., fecalith size and lymph node characteristics).

Primary outcomes were as follows. Technical success rate was defined as successful cannulation and completion of the irrigation procedure. Procedure time was the median time from cannulation to scope withdrawal. Safety was evaluated by the incidence of intraoperative and post-procedural complications (e.g., bleeding and perforation, infection) within 30 days.

Secondary outcomes, aligned with our pathophysiological hypothesis, were as follows. Clinical efficacy was defined as significant resolution of the presenting abdominal pain (reported by patient or parents and confirmed on physical examination) at the first post-procedure follow-up (within 2–4 weeks). Recurrence rate was calculated astThe proportion of patients experiencing a recurrence of similar abdominal pain episodes during the follow-up period, leading to a clinic visit or re-investigation. Recurrence was defined as a patient-initiated clinic visit or investigation for abdominal pain of similar character to the presenting complaint. Scheduled follow-up was conducted at 2–4 weeks with clinical assessment and ultrasound. Subsequent follow-up was conducted via clinic visits or telephone contact prompted by symptom recurrence.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (V.27.0). Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean±standard deviation and were compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages and were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missing data were minimal. As noted below, three patients were lost to follow-up, and hence, these data were missing. These patients were excluded from the efficacy analysis but included in the safety analysis (intention-to-treat). No other variables had missing values exceeding 5%, and no imputation methods were applied.

Results

Patient enrollment

Patient enrollment process is summarized in figure 1. During the study period, 89 children with suspected appendiceal fecalith-related RAP were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 16 were excluded based on predefined criteria, leaving 73 patients who underwent ERAI. All 73 patients (100%) had baseline sonographic lymphadenitis. Three patients were lost to follow-up, resulting in 70 patients available for efficacy analysis, while all 73 were included in the safety analysis (intention-to-treat principle).

Patient enrollment flowchart. ERAI, endoscopic retrograde appendiceal irrigation; RAP, recurrent abdominal pain.

Patient characteristics

A total of 73 pediatric patients (29 male, 44 female) who met the inclusion criteria underwent ERAI during the study period. The mean age was 8.3±2.7 years. All patients presented with a history of RLQ abdominal pain. On detailed evaluation, the pain was localized to a fixed point in the RLQ with tenderness in 13 patients (17.8%), while the remaining 60 patients (82.2%) described initial or concomitant upper abdominal or periumbilical pain that localized to the RLQ. Accompanying symptoms included constipation in 45 patients (61.6%) and nausea or vomiting in 21 patients (28.8%). Pre-procedural abdominal imaging confirmed appendiceal fecaliths in all patients. In addition, appendiceal dilation or elongation was noted on CT scan in 27 patients (37.0%). Concurrent mesenteric lymphadenitis, as per our predefined ultrasound criteria,10 was identified in all 73 patients at baseline.

Procedural outcomes and safety

All 73 procedures were completed successfully under ultrasound guidance, yielding a technical success rate of 100%. The median procedure time was 45 min (IQR 25–65 min). Endoscopic examination of the appendiceal orifice revealed significant congestion and edema in 24 patients (32.9%), mild changes in 8 patients (11.0%), and a normal appearance in 41 patients (56.2%). Lavage effluent from the appendiceal cavity predominantly consisted of flocculent debris and fecal residue (with or without pus) in 66 patients (90.4%). Impacted fecaliths requiring basket or balloon extraction were encountered in 5 patients (6.8%), and foreign bodies (e.g., food residues) were found in 2 patients (2.7%).

Procedure-related complications were minor and transient. Localized bleeding at the cannulation site (approximately 2 mL), which ceased spontaneously after saline irrigation, occurred in six patients (8.2%). Abdominal discomfort attributed to colonic insufflation was reported in seven patients (9.6%) and resolved promptly post-procedure. No serious adverse events, such as perforation, clinically significant infection, or contrast agent-related reactions, were observed.11 Antibiotics were administered in the peri-procedural period to 18 patients (24.7%).

Efficacy and follow-up

Post-procedural contrast ultrasound confirmed successful clearance of the appendiceal lumen in all cases, with no residual filling defects or signs of perforation. Clinical follow-up data were available for 70 patients (95.9%), with a median follow-up duration of 15.5 months (IQR 1–30 months). Follow-up modalities included outpatient clinic visits (n=48, 68.6%), telephone interviews (n=15, 21.4%), and combined clinic and telephone follow-up (n=7, 10.0%). The primary outcome of clinical efficacy, defined as significant resolution of the index abdominal pain episode at the first follow-up, was achieved in 69 of the 70 patients with follow-up data (98.6%). Of note, 92.9% (65/70) of patients reported significant pain relief within 72 hours post-procedure or by discharge.

Regarding secondary outcomes, the recurrence of episodes of RLQ abdominal pain (of similar character) within 1-year post-ERAI was documented in 13 patients (18.6% of those followed). The proportion of patients with sonographic evidence of mesenteric lymphadenitis decreased significantly from 100% (73/73) at baseline to 21.9% (16/73) at the last available follow-up imaging (p<0.01). Three patients (4.3%) showed sonographic evidence of recurrent or new appendiceal fecaliths during follow-up, with a median time to detection of 2.5 months.

Discussion

This retrospective study describes the application and outcomes of ERAI in a specific pediatric cohort presenting with recurrent RLQ abdominal pain, sonographic evidence of appendiceal fecaliths, and frequently coexisting mesenteric lymphadenitis. Endoscopic findings of congestion or dilation in the absence of systemic or radiological acute signs likely represent a spectrum of chronic irritation, which is central to our pathophysiological hypothesis, although distinguishing this from early acute appendicitis remains a diagnostic challenge. Our findings suggest that ERAI is a technically feasible and safe procedure in this context, associated with high rates of immediate symptom resolution and a significant reduction in the sonographic prevalence of lymphadenitis during follow-up.

The pathophysiology of RAP in children is complex. Mesenteric lymphadenitis is a well-recognized contributor, often diagnosed after excluding other organic causes.12 Its pathogenesis is linked to the immunological reactivity of the mesenteric lymphatic system, particularly in the ileocecal region, which is rich in lymphoid tissue and acts as a barrier against pathogens from the terminal ileum.5 This can lead to reactive enlargement of lymph nodes and pain, often mimicking appendicitis.6 In our cohort, the invariable finding of an appendiceal fecalith adds another dimension to this clinical picture. Fecaliths, composed of inspissated fecal material, salts, and inorganic matter, are recognized as a key factor in the pathogenesis of acute appendicitis by causing luminal obstruction, elevated intraluminal pressure, bacterial overgrowth, and subsequent inflammation.7 13 Of note, fecaliths are more commonly identified in pediatric compared with adult appendicitis.7 We hypothesize that in the context of recurrent, non-acute pain, a fecalith may act as a persistent source of low-grade luminal irritation or intermittent subclinical obstruction. This could trigger localized neuro-immune signaling and lymphatic engagement in the adjacent ileocecal mesentery, providing a plausible link between the anatomic finding (fecalith), functional symptoms (recurrent pain), and sonographic sign (lymphadenitis). This conceptual framework justifies a targeted intervention aimed at removing the presumed irritant. However, it must be emphasized that a causal relationship between fecaliths and mesenteric lymphadenitis has not been established. These two conditions may exist independently or exert mutual influence; our study design precludes differentiation between these possibilities. This conceptual framework provides a theoretical basis for exploratory investigation.

ERAI, adapted from the principles of ERAT,7 9 served this purpose effectively. Despite the majority of patients (56.2%) lacking significant endoscopic inflammation at the appendiceal orifice, the lavage consistently yielded inflammatory debris and, in some cases, impacted fecaliths. The subsequent high rate of symptom resolution (98.6%) may be associated with mechanical clearance of the lumen alleviating a source of somatic stimulation. Furthermore, the significant decrease in the proportion of patients with sonographic lymphadenitis post-procedure (from 100% to 21.9%, p<0.01) offers indirect evidence consistent with the proposed pathophysiological link. However, given the potential confounders such as constipation and bowel preparation, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. By removing the fecalith and associated debris, ERAI may interrupt a cycle of chronic low-grade inflammation and lymphatic reactivity.

Furthermore, the high prevalence of constipation (61.6%) in our cohort and the standardized bowel preparation required for colonoscopy represent a significant potential confounder. Symptom improvement may be partly attributable to effective colonic clearance. This attribution bias is an important limitation of our study design. We attempted outcome stratification by constipation status, but the small subgroup sizes (n=45 with vs n=28 without constipation) precluded meaningful statistical comparison. Peri-procedural antibiotic use represents another potential confounder for symptom resolution.

The five patients (6.8%) in our series with impacted fecaliths requiring mechanical extraction and the three patients (4.3%) with early recurrence of fecaliths might represent a subgroup with an anatomical predisposition, such as a narrower appendiceal orifice or a more tortuous lumen. Future studies with detailed luminal imaging could help identify such anatomical predictors of treatment success or failure.

The procedural success rate of 100% in our series compares favorably to reported cannulation success rates in pediatric ERAT, which vary widely.11 13 This high success rate may be attributed to the use of a transparent cap, meticulous technique, and possibly the less acute, non-edematous state of the appendiceal orifice in our elective patient population. It should be noted that these outcomes were achieved in a specialized tertiary center by an experienced endoscopic team, and the technical success rate may not be directly generalizable to less experienced settings. The safety profile was excellent, with only minor, self-limiting complications observed, consistent with the known safety of ERAT.7

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate ERAI specifically for children with RAP associated with appendiceal fecaliths. We employed standardized ultrasound criteria for lymphadenitis,10 achieved complete follow-up data for 95.9% of patients, and provided a comprehensive assessment including technical success, safety, and mid-term outcomes. The use of ultrasound guidance eliminated ionizing radiation exposure.

The retrospective, single-center design and lack of a control group limit causal inference and generalizability. Follow-up duration was variable (1–30 months), and symptom assessment relied on clinical records rather than standardized pain scales. While we observed a significant reduction in lymphadenitis, clinical correlation requires further validation. Most importantly, the lack of a control group precludes definitive conclusions regarding the causal effect of ERAI versus the natural history of the condition or other concurrent management (e.g., bowel preparation). This study was exploratory in nature, with the sample size determined by available cases rather than statistical calculation. Future large-scale, multicenter, randomized controlled trials employing standardized patient-reported outcome measures and rigorously controlling for confounders such as constipation and antibiotic use are warranted to establish optimal patient selection criteria and quantify long-term benefits.

Clinical implications

Our findings suggest that ERAI could be integrated into the diagnostic-therapeutic algorithm for selected children with RAP and sonographic evidence of appendiceal fecaliths with lymphadenitis. This approach may (1) provide rapid symptomatic relief, (2) reduce unnecessary appendectomies, (3) aid in differential diagnosis by confirming the presence of removable luminal contents, and (4) potentially modify the natural history of recurrent pain episodes. ERAI represents a valuable addition to the armamentarium of the pediatric surgeon for managing this ambiguous clinical entity.

In conclusion, beyond its established role in treating acute appendicitis, ERAT-based techniques like ERAI may have a diagnostic and therapeutic role in a select subset of children with RAP associated with appendiceal fecaliths and mesenteric lymphadenitis. By directly addressing a potential anatomical culprit, this ultra-minimally invasive approach offers a promising organ-preserving alternative. It may provide symptomatic relief, aid in differential diagnosis, and potentially modify clinical course in these challenging cases. These findings, though promising, require validation in controlled studies. Therefore, ERAI should be considered only after conservative measures fail, in highly selected patients with clear sonographic evidence of an appendiceal fecalith and persistent, localized symptoms. It represents a diagnostic-therapeutic option rather than standard therapy. Further prospective, controlled trials are warranted to define optimal patient selection and quantify long-term benefits.
