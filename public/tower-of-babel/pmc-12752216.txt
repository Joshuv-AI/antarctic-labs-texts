Exploring a mutation-based signature to predict the benefits of immune checkpoint inhibitors in oncogene-addicted subsets of non-small cell lung cancer: a retrospective study
Authors: Jing Huang, Jinsong Lei, Baoyou Xiao, Wenyan Kang, Zhilin Sui, Youjun Deng, Ran Jia, Chujian Huang, Songhua Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the effectiveness of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy in cancer treatment, oncogene-addicted subsets of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) typically derive limited benefits. Herein, we established and evaluated a mutation-based signature to predict ICB efficacy in patients with both non-mutant and oncogene-mutant NSCLC.MethodsThe predictive power of the signature was evaluated and validated across advanced NSCLCs cases with or without targetable oncogene alterations, using four public and one in-house immunotherapy cohort. NSCLC cohorts from The Cancer Genome Atlas were used to explore the underlying mechanisms of the signature. Herein, we defined a gene mutation number (GMN) based on the signature and classified patients into high and low GNM groups (GMN-H and GMN-L, respectively).ResultsThe GMN-H group exhibited significantly improved overall survival (OS) (hazard ratio [HR], 0.23; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.14–0.37; P < 0.001 in the discovery cohort) and progression-free survival (HR, 0.43; 95% CI: 0.28–0.65; P < 0.001 in the discovery cohort; HR, 0.41; 95% CI: 0.26–0.66; P = 0.0011 in the validation cohort) after ICB therapy. The survival advantage of GMN-H persisted in patients with KRAS mutations, whereas only trends toward improved survival outcomes were observed in those with EGFR, HER2, or MET alterations. Moreover, in the KRASG12C- and KRASG12V-mutant subgroups, patients with GMN-H exhibited a better OS. Compared with GMN-L, GMN-H was associated with higher levels of neoantigens, enrichment of WNT and MAPK pathways, and increased intra-tumoral CD8 + T cell infiltration.ConclusionsThe mutation-based signature established herein represents a robust biomarker for identifying patients with advanced NSCLCs who are most likely to benefit from ICB therapy, including those with targetable oncogene alterations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03429-w.Keywords: Tumor mutational burden, Gene mutational number, Immune checkpoint blockade therapy, Biomarker, Oncogene-addicted non-small cell lung cancer

Background

Advancements in immunotherapy have led to a paradigm shift in cancer treatment. By stimulating host T cells to eliminate cancer cells, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy confers promising survival advantages to patients across multiple cancer types, including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [1–5]. However, 60–80% of patients with advanced cancers derive no sustained benefits from ICB treatment [6, 7], while incurring substantial economic costs. Therefore, reliable biomarkers are urgently required for the early identification of patients who are likely to respond to ICB therapies.

Patients with oncogene-addicted NSCLC reportedly exhibit heterogeneous but modest response to ICB treatment; however, most evidence was derived from retrospective studies of ICB monotherapy [8–10]. Specifically, patients with BRAF or KRAS mutations exhibit responses comparable with those with non-mutant NSCLC [11, 12], whereas EGFR, HER2, or ALK alterations are typically associated with ICB resistance [13–15]. Moreover, data regarding ICB efficacy in patients with MET mutation and ROS1 or RET fusions are scarce and inconsistent [16–18]. Collectively, these findings indicate that immunotherapy may not an optimal treatment for unselected oncogene-addicted NSCLC. However, given the potential beneficial outcomes of ICB therapy and the increasing number of patients progressing on targeted therapy such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) [19], developing tools to identify patients with oncogene-addicted NSCLC who can benefits from ICB therapy is clinically meaningful.

To date, the predictive value of PD-L1 and tumor mutational burden (TMB) in NSCLC with targetable oncogene alterations remains unclear. While some studies have demonstrated that PD-L1 status can predict ICB efficacy in oncogene-driven NSCLC [20, 21], others have reported minimal benefits from immunotherapy in NSCLC with EGFR, MET, or ALK alterations, despite high PD-L1 expression [22, 23]. However, recent studies have identified several mutation-based signatures that demonstrate excellent performance in predicting survival benefits after immunotherapy in NSCLC [24, 25]. In addition, co-mutations in TP53 or STK11 have been shown to greatly influence the clinical sensitivity of KRAS-mutant NSCLCs to ICB therapy [26, 27]. Thus, we aimed to explore and establish a mutation-based signature to identify subsets of patients with oncogene mutations who can benefit from with immunotherapy.

Herein, we first filtered out certain genes whose mutations were likely to promote antitumor immune responses and constructed a corresponding mutation-based signature. We hypothesized that this signature could serve as a reliable surrogate biomarker for predicting ICB efficacy in patients with non-mutant and oncogene-mutant NSCLC.

Methods

Clinical cohorts and data sources

The in-house cohort included patients with NSCLC who underwent treatment between January 2019 and November 2023 at the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) advanced NSCLC not amenable to treatment, (2) genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing, and (3) administration of ICB as first- or second-line therapy. Patients who received ICB as neoadjuvant therapy were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics and Research Committee of the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, and all patients provided written informed consent.

Publicly available clinical data of patients with NSCLC—utilized as the discovery and validation cohorts herein—were obtained from the cBioPortal database (https://www.cbioportal.org) and previous studies [28–30]. These included four ICB-treated cohorts: 1661 patients who underwent ICB therapy in a study by Samstein et al. [31], 35 patients with NSCLC who underwent treatment with anti-PD-1 in a 2015 study by Rizvi et al. [32], 240 patients with NSCLC who underwent treatment with anti-PD-(L)1 in a 2018 study by Rizvi et al. [33], and 75 patients with NSCLC who underwent combination therapy utilizing anti-PD-1 and anticytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 in a study by Hellmann et al. [34].

For prognosis comparison and mechanistic exploration, we included three non-ICI-treated cohorts: 566 patients with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [35], 487 patients with lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) patients from TCGA [35], and 10,336 patients with advanced tumors from a study by Zehir et al. [36].

Eligible patients were recruited and consolidated from the aforementioned cohorts, based on the criteria described in Appendix Fig. 1. Clinical information of patients analyzed in this study is summarized in Appendix Table 1.

Gene lists and definitions of TMB and gene mutation number (GMN)

In the discovery cohort, which included 350 patients with NSCLC from the cohort of Samstein et al., we identified potential genes that were associated with better ICB treatment outcomes. The eligibility criteria were as follows: (1) candidate genes with a significantly higher mutation frequency in surviving patients than in deceased patients (p ≤ 0.10), with a relaxed threshold used to include more potential genes; and (2) a mutation frequency of at least 3/350. We hypothesized that genes associated with improved survival may contribute to tumor neo-antigen generation or directly influence immune-related pathways, specifically the PD-L1 and PD-1 checkpoint pathways.

GMN was defined as the total number of non-synonymous mutations in the selected genes that met the criteria above. Patients were classified into high and low GMN subgroups (GMN-High (GMN-H) and GMN-Low (GMN-L), respectively) based on an optimal cutoff value of 3, which was determined using the maximally selected rank statistics method implemented in the surv-cutpoint package of R. TMB was defined as the total number of non-synonymous mutations per megabase of the genome examined. As approved by the US FDA, a cutoff value of ten was used to distinguish patients into the TMB-H and TMB-L groups [37].

Immune pathway analysis using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA can help identify biological pathways enriched in patients with tumors based on RNA expression data [38]. To explore whether immune pathways were differentially dysregulated between the GMN-H and GMN-L groups, we performed GSEA using RNA expression data obtained from patients with NSCLC in TCGA.

Evaluation of intra-tumoral immune cell infiltration with CIBERSORT

We utilized the CIBERSORT (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/cshome.php) tool to compare differences in immune cell infiltration between the GMN-H and GMN-L groups based on RNA expression data from a TCGA cohort [39]. Specifically, we focused on differences in CD8 + T cell and Treg cell infiltration between the two GMN subgroups.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS v.26.0 (SPSS, Chicago, Illinois, USA) and R software (version 4.2.0). Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test while continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Multivariable analysis was performed using Cox proportional hazard regression with inclusion of variables significant on univariate regression including age, sex, histology, smoking history, PD-L1 status, and treatment drugs. Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier curves. P-values, hazard ratios (HR), and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using the log-rank test.

Results

Identification and validation of the mutation-based signature

In the discovery cohort, which included 350 patients with NSCLC and was sequenced using the MSK-IMPACT panel, we first filtered out candidate genes whose mutations could affect immunotherapy efficacy based on the selection criteria described in the Methods section. This yielded a list of 41 genes (Appendix Table 2), many of which were already known to be closely related to ICB response [40–42]. Notably, the quality of the candidate gene lists may be further improved by assessing larger cohorts.

We then integrated the gene sets to construct a mutation-based signature and defined the GMN as the total number of nonsynonymous somatic, coding, base substitution, and indel mutations in 41 genes in patients with tumors. In the discovery cohort, 57.7% of patients had at least one of the 41 gene mutations, and the GMN ranged from 0 to 18, with a median of 1 (Appendix Table 3). We speculated that a higher GMN might indicate a greater possibility that the patient would benefit from ICB treatment (Fig. 1A).

In the discovery cohort, patients in GMN-H group exhibited longer overall survival (OS) than those in the GMN-L group (P < 0.0001; HR, 0.23; 95% CI: 0.14–0.37) (Fig. 1B). Additionally, we compared the influence of the signature on progression-free survival (PFS) in a sub-cohort of 205 patients with NSCLC. As expected, the GMN-H group showed an increased PFS compared with the GMN-L group (P < 0.0001; HR, 0.43; 95% CI: 0.28–0.65) (Fig. 1C).

Next, we attempted to confirm the predictive value of GMN in an independent cohort of 144 patients with NSCLC collected from three previous studies. When using the same cutoff of 3, the patients in the GMN-H group showed an increased PFS compared with those in the GMN-L group (P = 0.00011; HR, 0.41; 95% CI: 0.26–0.66) (Fig. 1D). A trend toward prolonged PFS in the GMN-H group was consistently observed across all three datasets in the validation cohort (Fig. 1E). Notably, we only included 35 patients from the 2018 study by Rizvi et al. in the validation cohort, as the remaining 205 patients were included in the discovery cohort. When assessing the predictive power of GMN in all patients from the 2018 study by Rizvi et al. (n = 240), the improved PFS in the GMN-H group was more noticeable compared with that in the GMN-L group (P < 0.0001) (Appendix Fig. 2). Therefore, the insignificant P-value in Fig. 1E may have been due to the limited sample size. The validation results were remarkably similar to those of the PFS analysis in the discovery cohort, verifying the consistency of the association between GMN-H and favorable ICB benefits across both the discovery and validation cohorts.

The ICB response rates between GMN-H and GMN-L subgroups were further compared. The response subsets were 37.0% in the GMN-H group versus only 15.1% in the GMN-L group (Fisher’s exact test; P = 0.003) in the discovery cohort (Fig. 2A). Similar results were obtained in the validation cohort (56.8% vs. 23.1%, P = 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Moreover, the GMN-H group showed a significantly higher proportion of long-term clinical benefits than the GMN-L group (66.7% vs. 29.8%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2C).

In conclusion, these data indicate that this mutation-based signature may be a promising surrogate for predicting ICB survival outcomes and responses in advanced NSCLC.

Comparison of predictive efficacy between the signature, individual gene mutations, and TMB

We systematically compared the predictive power of the mutation-based signature with that of known individual mutated genes associated with ICB response, including TP53, KRAS [43, 44], STK11 [27], EPHA5 [45], and B2M [46]. Our results showed that the C-index of GMN was 0.77, which was greater than those for KRAS (0.52), TP53 (0.57), STK11 (0.58), EPHA5 (0.68), and B2M (0.69) mutations in the discovery cohort (Fig. 2D). A higher C-index indicates better model performance, highlighting that the predictive ability of GMN was more promising than that of any single gene.

TMB is a validated biomarker for predicting immunotherapy efficacy. In our discovery cohort, the TMB-H group (TMB ≥ 10) exhibited superior OS compared with the TMB-L group (P = 0.0074) (Appendix Fig. 3A). Next, we compared the relative predictive powers of the signature and TMB. The 350 pateinst with NSCLC patients divided into the following four subgroups: those with GMN ≥ 3 and TMB > 10, those with GMN ≥ 3 and TMB < 10, those with GMN < 3 and TMB > 10, and those with GMN < 3 and TMB < 10. The results revealed that patients with TMB < 10 and high GMN had significantly improved OS compared with those with low GMN and either high or low TMB (Fig. 2E). We further examined whether the GMN was a promising predictor beyond TMB in the validation cohort. Among the 144 patients with NSCLC, 40 were in the TMB-H group and showed more improved PFS outcomes than those in the TMB-L group (Appendix Fig. 3B). To compare the predictive power of TMB-H and GMN-H, 144 NSCLC patients in the validation datasets were stratified by GMN and TMB levels and divided into four levels as in the discovery cohort. In the TMB-H subgroup, GMN-H was associated with a significantly longer PFS than GMN-L (P = 0.039). Moreover, there was a trend toward longer PFS in GMN-H than in GMN-L in the TMB-L subgroup (Fig. 2F). Notably, 47 and 40 patients belonged to the GMN-H and TMB-H groups, respectively. The C-index was used to compare the performance of the GMN with that of TMB. The results showed that the predictive power of the GMN was not lower than that of TMB in the discovery or validation cohorts (Fig. 2G).

Altogether, our analysis revealed the non-inferiority of GMN to TMB in predicting the benefits of immunotherapy.

GMN was not a prognostic factor in the non-ICB-treated cohort

To confirm that the observed clinical benefits in GMN-H patients were not attributable to a general prognostic nature, we studied the survival difference between GMN patients in two non-ICI-treated cohorts. In the TCGA cohort, no survival benefits were observed in the GMN-H group compared to those in the GMN-L group (Appendix Fig. 4A). Given that patients with NSCLC in the TCGA datasets were in relatively earlier stages of cancer, we further evaluated the effects of GMN in the cohort of Zehir et al., which comprised patients with more advanced disease. However, no survival benefit was observed in the GMN-H group (Appendix Fig. 4B).

Moreover, we explored whether GMN is an independent predictor of the response to immunotherapy. In both the discovery cohort and validation cohorts, univariate Cox regression analysis showed that GMN was correlated with OS (Appendix Table 4). After adjusting for drug type, GMN remained an independent predictive factor based on multivariate Cox regression analysis, confirming its robustness for independently predicting ICB prognosis.

Overall, we conclude that the GMN has predictive power for immunotherapy rather than a general prognostic impact.

The mutation-based signature predicted immunotherapy outcomes in oncogene-addicted NSCLC

Next, we investigated whether this signature is suitable for advanced NSCLC with actionable oncogenes. Herein, we focused on patients with KRAS, EGFR, BRAF, HER2, or MET mutations as they account for at least 3% of all LUAD cases [8]. No significant difference in survival analysis was observed among the patients harboring these in both discovery and validation cohorts; however, there was a trend toward longer median OS and PFS in KRAS-mutant patients than in EGFR-mutant patients (Fig. 3A-C).

Using the same cut-off value of 3, we divided oncogene-driven NSCLCs into two subgroups and explored the differences between the groups in terms of ICB response and survival benefits. As shown in Fig. 3D-H, irrespective of the mutation, the GMN-H group showed a higher response rate than the other groups; however, the findings were not statistically significant. In the survival analysis, improved OS (P < 0.0001 in the discovery cohort) and PFS (P = 0.0028 in the discovery cohort and P < 0.0001 in the validation cohort) were observed in the GMN-H groups than in the GMN-L groups in KRAS-mutant patients (Fig. 3I-K and Appendix Fig. 5). In patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLCs, the GMN-H group exhibited greater OS outcomes in the discovery cohort (P = 0.013) (Fig. 3L) and longer PFS (P > 0.05) in both the discovery and validation cohorts (Fig. 3M-N). Interestingly, this signature might not apply to BRAF-mutant patients, for whom a worse PFS was observed in the GMN-H group in the validation cohort (Fig. 3O-Q). For patients with HER2 or MET mutations, results similar to those for EGFR-mutant NSCLCs were obtained (Fig. 3R-W). However, there was only one patient with a HER2 alteration, and validation in larger cohorts is needed.

The signature predicts immunotherapy outcomes in different KRAS- or EGFR-mutant NSCLC

Accumulating evidence indicates that KRAS and EGFR mutations are not uniform entities and exhibit notable biological heterogeneity. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate whether NSCLCs with different sub-alleles influence the performance of the signature. In both the discovery and validation cohorts, no obvious differences in survival outcomes after ICB therapy were observed among patients with KRAS-wild, KRASG12C-, KRASG12D-, KRASG12V-, and other KRAS allele-mutant (Fig. 4A-B). In the discovery cohort, the GMN-H group exhibited improved OS compared with the GMN-L group for all KRAS-mutant alleles (P = 0.0017 for KRASG12C-mutant; P = 0.66 for KRASG12D-mutant; P = 0.012 for KRASG12V-mutant; and P = 0.022 for the rest of KRAS-mutants) (Fig. 4C-F and Appendix Fig. 6). Consistent with the results in the discovery cohort, superior survival outcomes persisted in the GMN-H group among the different KRAS-mutant patients in the validation cohort (Fig. 4G-J). Notably, GMN-H patients with KRASG12D-mutation showed a trend toward longer OS in the discovery cohort (Fig. 4D). In addition, an insufficient number of patients with KRASG12D-mutation was utilized for survival analysis in the validation cohort (Fig. 4H). Collectively, the signature may be applicable to patients with KRASG12C, KRASG12V, or other KRAS allele mutations; however, uncertainties remain regarding patients with in KRASG12D-mutations.

A similar analysis was conducted for EGFR-mutant subsets. Compared with patients with EGFR-wild-type, no difference in OS was observed among patients with EGFRL858R-, EGFRE746−A750del-, or other EGFR mutations in the discovery cohort (Fig. 4K). Patients with EGFR-wild-type had better PFS than patients with EGFRL858R-mutations in the validation cohort (Fig. 4L). After grouping patients into different GMN subsets, we noticed longer median OS or PFS in the GMN-H group in both the discovery and validation cohorts; however, the results were not statistically significant due to limited patient numbers (Fig. 4M).

In summary, a mutation-based signature may also be useful in patients with different KRAS or EGFR mutant alleles. Further validation using additional clinical cohorts is required.

Validation of the signature in both non-mutant and oncogene-mutant NSCLC patients in an in-house cohort

We retrospectively collected real-world clinical data from Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in detail in the Methods section. After integration, 24 patients with EGFR mutation, 16 with KRAS-mutations, six with BRAF-mutations, and 64 without these mutations were included for internal validation. Notably, the next-generation sequencing panel utilized contained 312 genes (Appendix Table 5), lacking several genes in the signature, such as TET1, MGA, EPHA7, and so on. To avoid excessive underestimation of the efficacy of our signature, we performed survival analysis based on two cutoff values.

When using the cutoff value of 3 as in the publicly available cohorts, the GMN-H group maintained a trend toward improved OS than the GMN-L group, regardless of whether they were in the wild type, KRAS-mutant, or EGFR-mutant cohorts (Fig. 5A-C). Consistent with the results in the publicly available cohorts, the difference in OS between the GMN-H and GMN-L groups was not obvious in patients with BRAF-mutations (Fig. 5D). When the cut-off value was set to 1, we noticed a significant OS improvement in the GMN-H group than the other groups in both wild-type and KRAS-mutant cohorts (P = 0.027 in the wild cohort; P = 0.031 in the KRAS-mutant cohort) (Fig. 5E-F). The GMN-H group exhibited improved OS in the EGFR-mutant cohort and worse OS in the BRAF-mutant cohort (Fig. 5G-H).

In summary, the in-house validation supported the potential utilization of the signature in advanced NSCLCs without driver mutations, as well as with KRAS and EGFR mutations, to identify candidates who would benefit from immunotherapy.

Potential mechanisms associated with the signature in predicting ICB treatment efficacy

One reason some individuals respond to ICB therapy is that the tumor acquires sufficient antigenicity after interaction with the immune system, known as “cancer immunoediting.” Thus, to uncover the underlying mechanism, we compared the neoantigens between the GMN-H and GMN-L subgroups in all immunotherapy cohorts with neoantigen data available. In 34 patients with NSCLC from the 2015 study by Rizvi et al., there was a trend toward higher neoantigen levels in the GMN-H group than in the GMN-L group (P = 0.09) (Fig. 6A). In addition, in 75 patients with NSCLC from the 2018 study by Hellmann, the GMN-H group had higher neoantigen levels than the GMN-L group (P = 0.0001). When these two cohorts were combined, we observed that the neo-antigen levels were significantly different (P < 0.0001) between the GMN-H and GMN-L groups.

We then investigated whether any biological pathways were altered in the GMN-H group compared with the GMN-L group. GSEA was applied to the TCGA cohort to identify differently enriched pathways between the two GMN subgroups. The results revealed that several pathways, such as the Wnt signaling, MAPK signaling, and the mismatch repair pathways, were highly enriched in the GMN-H group compared with the GMN-L group in all NSCLC, LUSC, and LUAD cohorts (Fig. 6B and Appendix Tables 6, 7 and 8). These pathways are well known to be related to immunotherapy response.

To further test whether patients with GMN-H harbor an altered tumor immune microenvironment, we used the CIBERSORT tool to evaluate immune cell infiltration. In our analysis, the GMN-H exhibited greater intra-tumoral CD8 + T cell infiltration in the NSCLC, LUSC, and LUAD cohorts (Fig. 6C). Conversely, the GMN-H group showed significantly lower Treg infiltration in the NSCLC, LUSC, and LUAD cohorts (Fig. 6D).

In summary, these results suggested that patients with GMN-H have an immune-inflamed microenvironment. Mechanistically, the increased intra-tumoral infiltration of CD8 + cells in patients with GMN-H patients might be due to the generation of more tumor neoantigens and altered pathways associated with immunotherapy responses.

Discussion

In this proof-of-concept study, we identified a mutation-based signature and demonstrated that GMN-H was associated with improved survival outcomes following ICB therapy in advanced NSCLC, including oncogene-addicted subsets with KRAS, EGFR, HER2, and MET mutations. These results were validated using publicly available data, as well as internal cohorts.

We observed that the GMN, defined from much smaller gene panels, exhibited efficacy comparable with TMB in predicting survival benefits after ICB treatment in both the discovery and validation cohorts. One possible explanation is that not all non-synonymous mutations can produce immunogenic neoantigens [47, 48], which is the premise of TMB as a biomarker in ICB therapy. Several smaller gene panels have been developed as alternatives to whole-exome panels for estimating clinically relevant TMB in NSCLC [49, 50]. Moreover, several mutation-based signatures have demonstrated superior predictive performance compared with TMB. Pan et al. reported that a compound mutation signature with two or more of 52 candidate mutated genes performed better than TMB-H in predicting ICB outcomes [25]. Additionally, Long et al. [24] established a mutation-based gene set that demonstrated greater prognostic ability than TMB. Although TMB has been approved for clinical use, these findings highlight opportunities for improvements. Our results suggest that GMN may be a promising alternative to TMB.

Interestingly, the GMN acted as a potential tool to identify patients likely to benefit from ICB treatment in KRAS-mutant, EGFR-mutant, HER2-mutant, or MET-mutant NSCLCs, but not in BRAF-mutant NSCLC. Whether immunotherapy can be recommended as the first-line option for patients with these driver mutations remains unclear, especially when targeted drugs are limited. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop predictive biomarkers that can provide accurate ICB treatment guidance for these patients. However, to our knowledge, few studies have identified effective ICB biomarkers in patients with oncogene-dependent NSCLC. Our study addresses this gap in knowledge by introducing a potential biomarker tailored to this clinical context.

Furthermore, we employed two publicly available clinical trial datasets and the TCGA dataset to explore the biological underpinnings of high GMN. In patients with GMN-H, the elevated neo-antigen characteristics of TMB-H were preserved. However, the Wnt, MAPK, and mismatch repair pathways were significantly dysregulated between the GMN-H and GMN-L groups across the TCGA-NSCLC, TCGA-LUSC, and TCGA-LUAD cohorts. Defects in the interferon signaling pathway and its associated effectors are well known to correlate with the ICB response [51]. A prior study reported that activating mutations in the Wnt pathway influenced the ICB response by altering the expression of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in cancers [52]. In addition, patients with mutations in the components of the RAS-MAPK pathway exhibited longer OS after ICB therapy than control patients with gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma [53]. Recently, a study identified several genes and pathways, including MAPK signaling pathway, as predictors of immunotherapy response [54]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the improved outcomes of ICB therapy in patients with GMN-H may be due to both the high neoantigen content and the altered biological pathways closely related to the ICB response.

The use of GMN for predicting the outcomes of ICB treatment has several potential advantages for clinical implementation. First, the concept of GMN is simpler than that of TMB in clinical use. Second, TMB is typically calculated using whole exome sequencing (WES) or other targeted panels such as F1CDx or MSK-IMPACT [55]. Thus, the use of different platforms influences TMB measurement [56], while GMN estimation is less prone to such biases as it only considers the mutation status of selected genes. Third, GMN may be applicable to oncogene-addicted NSCLCs, providing patients with an additional treatment option after progression on targeted therapy. Finally, with the accumulation of more clinical data and experimental investigations, the gene panel can be improved, and the underlying mechanism can be elucidated, thus further bolstering the predictive efficacy of GMN.

This study has several limitations. First, potential statistical bias may arise because of the retrospective nature of the study, especially in oncogene-addicted groups with fewer patients; however, we have attempted to minimize this bias by obtaining consistent results across multiple cohorts. Second, the lack of certain clinical data—such as detailed treatment histories, comorbidities, and ECOG performance status—may affect the accurate interpretation of the GMN’s predictive value. Thus, larger cohorts are required to validate the generalizability of our findings. Third, although many mutated genes in the gene list have already been implicated in the response to immunotherapy, such as ZFHX3 [57], STAT3 [58], and TET1 [40], the synergistic effects of these mutated genes remain unclear. Mechanistic interpretations of GMN require further evaluation by experimental molecular studies. Lastly, as combining different biomarkers has been reported to improve the prediction of ICB response in NSCLC [59], future studies exploring the joint utility of PD-L1 expression and GMN are warranted.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that GMN-H, defined as the presence of a high number of mutated genes, is associated with improved survival in patients with NSCLC, both with and without driver mutations, after ICB therapy. Further prospective studies are required to validate and improve the GMN prior to its implementation in clinical settings.
