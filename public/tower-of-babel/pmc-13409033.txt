Heat and Dust Impacts on the Health of Refugees in Zaatari Refugee Camp
Authors: Samer Makahleh, Maha Al‐Harahsheh, Ka Wai Sit, Natasha Hessami, Wael K. Al‐Delaimy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate‐linked heatwaves and dust storms can exacerbate non‐communicable disease (NCD) morbidity, yet quantitative evidence from displacement settings remains limited. We assessed short‐term impacts of heat and dust events on symptom burden among Syrian refugees with non‐communicable diseases (NCDs) in Jordan's Zaatari Camp. We conducted a prospective study of 660 adults receiving care at King Salman Humanitarian Aid and Relief Clinics (KS‐Relief) clinics in Zaatari across three periods aligned to seasonal heatwaves. Self‐reported symptoms following heatwaves and dust storms were collected via structured interviews. Repeated measures were analyzed using generalized linear mixed‐effects models and generalized estimating equations with prespecified covariates. Heatwaves and dust storms were associated with increased symptom burden among participants with NCDs. Symptom burden was highest in Phase 1, the hottest period of 2024 in the Mafraq region (mean daily max 37.8°C; peak 43°C; multiple heatwave events and dust storm days), with more dyspnea, fatigue, and hypertension symptoms, especially among those with asthma or hypertension. Phase 2 (moderate heat with intermittent dust; mean daily max 34.1°C; peak 37°C) had lower odds of heatwave‐ and dust storm–related impacts than Phase 1 (odds ratio 0.54 and 0.62, respectively) with similar symptom profiles. Phase 3 (moderate heat; fewer dust storms; mean daily max 32.6°C) showed no significant change compared to Phase 2. Across phases, women, larger households, and asthma were associated with higher odds of adverse health impacts. In a displaced, low‐resource setting, heatwaves and dust storms impose measurable, recurrent symptom burdens on adults living with NCDs, with heightened vulnerability among those with asthma and in larger households, and generally higher reported impacts among women. Camp‐appropriate adaptation (improved cooling, shading, dust‐ mitigation) and targeted patient education and surveillance may reduce climate‐ sensitive NCD morbidity.

Introduction

Changes in climate events, such as increased average temperature, droughts, floods, and dust storms, are contributing to the development and exacerbation of non‐communicable diseases (NCDs) by influencing key risk factors such as air pollution, psychosocial stress, clean water accessibility, and reduced capacity for physical activity (Al‐Delaimy et al., 2020; Hunter et al., 2024). These challenges exist globally, including in well‐resourced urban settings of developed countries, but are significantly magnified and more harmful in the context of forced displacement, where healthcare infrastructure and access are fragmented or insufficient (WHO, 2025).

Apart from some regions in Africa where infectious diseases still predominate, NCDs are the leading cause of global mortality. As of 2021, NCDs are responsible for about 43 million deaths each year. Some 77% of these deaths, inclusive of premature deaths before age 70, occur in low‐ and middle‐income countries (WHO, 2024a, 2024b). Socioeconomic factors shape risk for and management of NCDs, with factors like rapid urbanization, poor access to healthy food, tobacco exposure, and barriers to health care access, contributing to the disproportionate burden of NCD mortality and morbidity worldwide (FAO et al., 2024).

High temperatures account for over 150,000 deaths and 3.4 million disability‐adjusted life years annually among those with NCDs, with nearly 70% of this burden concentrated in South Asia, North Africa, and the Middle East (Zhang, Cheng, et al., 2024; Zhang, Zhou, & Lin, 2024). Global deaths from extreme heat have increased sixfold since 1990, disproportionately affecting populations in low‐income and climate‐vulnerable regions. Separately, refugee status has been identified as a risk factor for diagnosis with an NCD and confers worse morbidity (Ngaruiya et al., 2022). The compounding risk of living in a heat‐vulnerable environment has been insufficiently accounted for in regional climate adaptation plans (ICRC, 2023). Older adults and those with NCDs face the highest mortality risk (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2024). The suggested mitigation strategies in the ICRC (2023) report, such as shaded infrastructure, air‐conditioned spaces, and improved emergency access, remain exceedingly limited and inadequate in displaced settings.

Air quality further compounds climate‐linked NCD risks (Karim et al., 2024). Acute exposure to particulate pollution increases cardiovascular and respiratory events by 1%–3% (Gouveia et al., 2024), and long‐term exposure may elevate chronic disease burden by as much as 10% (Orellano et al., 2024). The linear relationship between particulate matter and mortality suggests health improvements can be realized through even modest reductions in air pollution (Breitner‐Busch et al., 2023). Evidence from other humanitarian contexts suggests clean cookstove interventions are underutilized in refugee camp settings, compounding the exposure risk (Vianello, 2016). This escalating burden unfolds within the backdrop of unprecedented human displacement. In 2024, an estimated 123.2 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide. This figure has doubled in the last decade (UNHCR, 2024a, 2024b). Of this staggering number of displaced people, 39.3 million crossed international borders to seek safety as refugees. Although camps are meant as temporary solutions, they are increasingly becoming semi‐permanent as major crises last on average 20 years (European Commission, 2024), with refugee populations residing in camps for years or even decades. Camp construction is necessarily rapid and often occurs on marginal, ecologically fragile land unsuitable for self‐reliance activities, leaving residents exposed to heightened environmental vulnerabilities (Bose, 2024; Fransen et al., 2023).

Zaatari Camp in Northern Jordan exemplifies both the promise and the challenge inherent to established refugee camps. Zaatari boasts the both the largest solar plant and largest water network in any refugee camp, successfully reducing 13,000 metric tons of CO2 emissions and providing drinking water to each household (UNHCR Jordan, 2024). Yet, as Bose (2024) describes, residents face psychological, spatial, and economic limitations that are necessary to appropriately adapt to climate stressors. This disparity illustrates how infrastructure alone is insufficient to solve the interplay between environment, health, and displacement. The camp's desert location brings chronic water shortages and exposure to climate threats, including dust storms, heatwaves, and cold snaps. Thermal stress is a daily reality with limited means to regulate indoor temperatures, with 73% of families in Zaatari reporting dissatisfaction with summer heat (Albadra et al., 2017; Ashour et al., 2023). Refugee camps in other regions of the world are faced with their own risks, such as the flood‐prone region of South Sudan, where 35,000 refugees were dislocated after the Alganaa refugee camp flooded in 2021 (Fransen et al., 2023). The combination of spatial and socioeconomic exclusion that refugees are subjected to make them more vulnerable to climate events, which then contributes to further erosion of their well‐being, ultimately increasing disenfranchisement and marginalization within their host country (Ashour et al., 2023; Fransen et al., 2023).

Zaatari refugee camp had nine medical facilities in the camp that provide primary, selective secondary and tertiary healthcare services free of charge, which include a 24 hr emergency service and maternity facilities (UNHCR, 2024a, 2024b). Overall satisfaction with healthcare services has been reported as high as 72.5%, but notably services for elderly, ease of referral to hospitals, and lack of follow up were cited as least acceptable domains—all of which are core to the care of chronic conditions (Salim et al., 2021). Numerous studies have identified challenges in obtaining and sustaining NCD care as a dire need and top priority in the provisioning of healthcare for Syrian refugees in Jordan, with identified gaps including follow‐up, continuity of care and management of complex medication needs (Akik et al., 2022; El Arab & Sagbakken, 2018; Naja et al., 2019). The extent of inadequate treatment of NCDs is well demonstrated in Hammad et al. (2022) retrospective cross‐sectional study on drug‐therapy problems among Syrian Refugees in Zaatari who had been diagnosed with a chronic illness, where they found 90.16% of diabetic patients have uncontrolled diabetes and 97.1% of hypertensive patients have uncontrolled hypertension.

Given the paucity of data on NCD outcomes in refugee camps facing climate extremes, this study seeks to quantify the burden of heatwaves and dust storms on the health status of NCD patients in Zaatari camp. By identifying modifiable risk factors, our findings will inform the development of targeted interventions to mitigate the impact of climate on NCD morbidity in displaced populations.

Methods

A prospective study was conducted to understand the impact of climate change on the health status of patients with NCDs residing in the Al Zaatari refugee camp in northern Jordan. Zaatari refugee camp is the largest Syrian refugee camp globally, with 81,000 refugees. It is located east of the northern Jordanian city of Mafraq in a desert environment that has gradually evolved into a permanent settlement.

For this study, we selected King Salman Humanitarian Aid and Relief Clinics (KS‐Relief), as the primary recruitment site. The study population included adult (18+) NCD patients registered at KS‐Relief clinics. We recruited and trained a survey team consisting of eight data collectors to administer survey tools. Participants were initially contacted by telephone or in person to seek their informed consent, and we enrolled a consecutive convenience sample of 660 eligible patients who were part of the population served by the clinic.

Ethical Considerations

All study procedures were reviewed and approved by the UNHCR and Jordanian Ministry of Health (JMOH). Participants were explicitly informed that their involvement was voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. Participants were also informed that their involvement in the study would not confer additional medical benefit. Participant names were replaced with unique code numbers, and data were entered into a secure electronic database with personally identifiable information removed. We restricted access to the database to the research team.

Inclusion in Global Research Statement

This research was conducted in collaboration with local partners in Jordan and we obtained approvals from UNHCR and the JMOH. Local collaborators are included as co‐authors consistent with AGU authorship criteria. Data collection and interpretation benefited from local clinical staff who advised on culturally relevant symptom groupings and patient engagement. End‐users include humanitarian health providers and camp authorities responsible for climate adaptation planning.

Data Collection Procedures

Participants who met the criteria were recruited from King Salman Humanitarian Aid and Relief Clinics (KSR) in Zaatari Camps in Jordan, which represent 30% of the total NCD patients inside the camp. All patients with NCD diseases and above the age of 18 who expressed interest in participating were eligible to participate. After obtaining informed consent from the participants, we interviewed them and asked about their health status.

Data on participants' health status were collected using a structured questionnaire developed by the research team, which included questions on disease profiles and sociodemographic characteristics. The survey captured information on gender, age, education level, income, and medical history. Full details of the questionnaire are provided in Appendix A. During each interview, participants were allowed to report multiple symptoms for each exposure event. Heatwave‐related and dust storm‐related symptoms were captured in separate questions, each referring to the most recent event within the recall period. Initial responses were recorded in free‐text format, after which interviewers used a structured symptom checklist to ensure completeness and consistency. The recall period for all symptom‐related questions was within 2 weeks of the last heatwave/dust storm.

Data collection was conducted in three distinct Phases, each correlating with a heatwave in Zaatari Camp (Table 1). These Phases were intentionally aligned with elevated temperatures to capture potential differences in self‐reported symptom patterns based on heat intensity, allowing for comparative analysis. While this design enabled comparisons across gradations of heat and dust exposure, it did not include a low‐temperature or dust‐free baseline period. Importantly, symptom differences were observed even between moderate temperature ranges, highlighting the sensitivity of this population to relatively small increases in environmental stressors. Future studies incorporating cooler season data would allow for clearer estimation of baseline symptom burden. Phase 1 (Phase 1, 21 June–9 July 2024) corresponded with the most severe heatwave, with temperatures peaking at 43°C. Phase 2 (Phase 2, 26 July–10 August 2024) corresponded with a moderate heatwave, with maximum temperatures reaching 37°C. Phase 3 (Phase 3, 26 August–15 September 2024) corresponded with a moderate heatwave, with temperatures peaking at 35°C.

Data Cleaning

We analyzed participants' responses about symptoms they experienced following a heatwave or dust storm by extracting and cleaning the free‐text entries for quantitative analysis. All symptom data were self‐reported; no objective clinical measurements (e.g., blood pressure readings) were taken during interviews to verify these reports. We conducted a frequency analysis of all symptoms and identified the six most reported (dyspnea, fatigue, headache, sweating, allergy, and high blood pressure). These were retained as standalone categories. Less frequently reported symptoms were grouped into broader, clinically meaningful syndromic categories (see Table 2). These groupings were reviewed and refined by medical personnel working in the camp to ensure they reflected both clinical and cultural context.

Statistical Analysis

For this study, we used R (with RStudio), version 4.2 to calculate the 95% confidence intervals. The coefficient was calculated ±1.96 times its standard error and then exponentiated those limits. We obtained p‐values directly from the Wald test output of the Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE).

We used binary logistic regression to investigate the univariate relationships between important clinical and demographic factors and exposure to heatwaves and dust storms, supplementing the multivariate longitudinal studies.

Repeated measures at three survey Phases (Phase 1, Phase 2, Phase 3) were analyzed using two complementary frameworks: subject‐specific generalized linear mixed‐effects models and population‐averaged generalized estimating equations (GEE). Details of these frameworks and the choices we followed in the analyses are in the supplementary files. We modeled binary symptom outcomes (e.g., dyspnea, fatigue, headache, sweating, allergy) with a binomial family and logit link, separately for heatwave and dust storm exposures. All models were adjusted for prespecified covariates: age group, gender, family‐size group, monthly‐income group, household area, and Index NCD category (hypertension, asthma, thyroid disease). Within‐subject correlation was handled by indexing repeated observations with the survey phase. All 660 enrolled participants completed all three survey phases and there were no missing data on the variables included in the models; therefore, the complete‐case analyses corresponded to the full analytic sample, and missingness did not vary by phase or by key covariates.

We modeled phase variable as a three‐level categorical variable (Phase 1, Phase 2, Phase 3). For each outcome, our primary interest was in comparing symptom prevalence between phases, therefore we estimated three pairwise contrasts (Phase 2 vs. Phase 1, Phase 3 vs. Phase 1, and Phase 3 vs. Phase 2) from the fitted models. We report these contrasts as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals, obtained by transforming the model estimates from the log‐odds scale. We report two‐sided, unadjusted p‐values. We did not apply a formal global multiple‐testing correction across the full set of binary symptom outcomes, which were defined a priori from questionnaire items and are clinically related; instead, we focused on the magnitude and consistency of effect estimates across outcomes rather than on isolated p‐values.

We assessed multicollinearity among covariates using variance inflation factors; all VIF values were low (maximum ≈ 1.4), indicating no problematic multicollinearity. We also examined residuals and influence diagnostics using simulation‐based methods and did not identify influential observations or lack of fit that materially affected model estimates.

Binary Logistics Regression Analysis

Binary logistic regression was conducted to examine factors associated with self‐reported effects of dust storms and heatwaves. For dust storm exposure, gender (p < 0.001) and asthma (p < 0.001) emerged as highly significant predictors, while other variables such as age group, family size, income, house size, hypertension, thyroid disorders, and other comorbidities were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

In relation to heatwave exposure, family size (p = 0.030), gender (p = 0.007), and asthma (p = 0.009) showed significant associations, whereas the remaining demographic and clinical variables did not demonstrate statistical significance.

Overall, asthma consistently appeared as a strong risk factor in both environmental conditions, suggesting heightened vulnerability of patients with respiratory conditions. Gender differences were also evident across both models, while family size showed a unique contribution under heatwave conditions, possibly reflecting differences in household‐level exposure or coping strategies.

Guided by prior studies and our a priori confounding structure, we retained established confounders for multivariable modeling regardless of their univariate p‐values (table 5).

Heatwave Effects

The likelihood of being affected by heatwaves fell sharply from Phase 1 to Phase 2 (OR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.47–0.63; p < 0.01) and remained lower at Phase 3 (OR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.41–0.54; p < 0.01), with no significant difference between Phase 2 and Phase 3 (p = 0.05). Male respondents had lower odds (OR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.69–0.91; p < 0.01) than females to report being affected heatwaves. Larger household size was associated with higher odds (e.g., 3–5 members: OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.01–1.52; p = 0.04; 9+ members: OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.06–1.65; p = 0.01), while asthma (single NCD) was modestly associated with being affected by heatwaves (OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.02–1.50; p = 0.03). Age, income, household area, and thyroid disease were not associated with heatwaves.

Condition‐specific heatwave impacts showed similar Phase patterns. Dyspnea decreased from Phase 1 to Phase 2 (OR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.45–0.69; p < 0.001) and remained lower in Phase 3 versus Phase 1 (OR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.52–0.80; p < 0.001), with no additional change between Phases 2 and 3 (p = 0.21). Fatigue declined from Phase 1 to Phase 2 (OR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.57–0.93; p = 0.01) and further decreased by Phase 3 versus Phase 1 (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.45–0.74; p < 0.01), with no statistically significant difference between Phases 2 and 3 (p = 0.08). Hypertension similarly fell at Phase 2 versus Phase 1 (OR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.35–0.64; p < 0.001) and at Phase 3 versus Phase 1 (OR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.32–0.59; p < 0.001), with no further change from Phase 2 to Phase 3 (p = 0.56). Participants with asthma had more than twice the odds of reporting heatwave‐related dyspnea (OR = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.78–2.96; p < 0.001). Participants with hypertension were more likely to report heatwave‐related hypertension symptoms (OR = 1.93, 95% CI: 1.39–2.68; p < 0.001). Fatigue was more common in larger households (e.g., 9+: OR = 2.22, 95% CI: 1.49–3.31; p < 0.01) and was less common in the 100–200 JD income group versus the reference (<100 JD) (OR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.57–0.91; p < 0.01) (Table 6).

Dust Storm Effects

Reported effects from dust storms also showed a decline from Phase 1 to Phase 2 (OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.53–0.72; p < 0.01) and sustained reduction at Phase 3 (OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.52–0.69, p < 0.01), with no difference between Phase 2 and Phase 3 (p = 0.68). Men had lower odds of reporting these effects than females (OR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.62–0.85; p < 0.01), while asthma patients (Single NCD) increased the odds of reported impacts of dust storms (OR = 1.72, 95% 304 CI: 1.41–2.10; p < 0.01).

Among dust storm–related conditions, dyspnea and allergy followed the same temporal pattern (e.g., dyspnea Phase 2 vs. Phase 1: OR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.51–0.73; p < 0.01; Phase 3 vs. Phase 1: OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.48–0.70; p < 0.01). Allergy continued to decline from Phase 2 to Phase 3 (Phase 2 vs. Phase 3: OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.02–1.68; p = 0.04; i.e., lower odds at Phase 3). History of asthma (Single NCD) raised the odds of both dyspnea (OR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.52–2.41; p < 0.01) and allergy (OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.01–1.74; p = 0.04) (Table 7).

Discussion

In this population of displaced Syrian refugees living in the largest camp in Jordan and suffering from NCDs, we found that heatwaves and dust storms were significantly associated with negative impacts on those with noncommunicable diseases and their symptoms. The prospective design of the study allowed for three measurement points during the summer and early fall and demonstrated a clear trend: high temperatures and more frequent dust storms during mid‐summer were associated with a significantly higher frequency of adverse impacts on those with NCDs than in late summer and early fall. Heatwaves had more clear and significant adverse impacts on those with asthma and hypertension, as well as reported symptoms of dyspnea or fatigue. In contrast, dust storms significantly impacted only those with asthma, dyspnea, and allergies. These findings underscore the sensitivity of NCD symptoms to short‐term environmental variability in displaced, low‐resource settings.

Our findings align with the literature demonstrating the impacts of heatwaves on NCDs (World Health Organization [WHO], 2024a, 2024b), including hypertension and respiratory diseases (Zhang, Cheng, et al., 2024; Zhang, Zhou, & Lin, 2024). Higher temperatures cause fluid loss and disrupt thermal regulation (Alahmad et al., 2023) among elderly populations, especially those with existing NCDs (Zhang, Cheng, et al., 2024; Zhang, Zhou, & Lin, 2024). People with hypertension, heart failure, diabetes, or renal diseases depend on medications to regulate metabolism (WHO, 2023), fluid retention and excretion, and blood flow to maintain a functional life (Zhang, Cheng, et al., 2024; Zhang, Zhou, & Lin, 2024). However, extreme temperatures and prolonged heatwaves pose a risk (Achebak et al., 2023) for poor health control, potentially leading to hospitalization and death (Zhang, Cheng, et al., 2024; Zhang, Zhou, & Lin, 2024). These risks are significantly heightened among elderly NCD patients living as refugees (UNHCR, 2024a, 2024b) in camps that often lack the infrastructure and facilities available in urban areas (Zhang, Cheng, et al., 2024; Zhang, Zhou, & Lin, 2024). Moreover, data on these risks in such populations remain limited and scarce (Dampha et al., 2025).

Most research on refugee health focuses on immediate humanitarian needs without considering the long‐term environmental impacts on those with NCDs. Therefore, our findings are important in highlighting a clear, consistent pattern of adverse heat‐related impacts on residents of the Zaatari refugee camp in northern Jordan. The camp has no reliable electricity, which is supplied on average for only 9 hr per day (UNHCR, 2022). Housing consists of non‐insulated fabricated caravans that become extremely hot in summer and very cold in winter, complicating heat coping in an arid, desert‐like environment (Alshawawreh & Rush, 2024). Complaints about heat impacts decreased as temperatures improved and weather cooled from the hottest month to early fall, when all regions of Jordan became more temperate. Those with a history of asthma and hypertension reported the most significant heat‐related impacts, while individuals with thyroid or other cardiovascular diseases did not. Hypertension was the most common NCD in this population (20%), while only a few participants had other cardiovascular diseases, possibly explaining the limited adverse heat‐related health impacts observed in that group. Symptoms like dyspnea and fatigue, which can indicate respiratory or cardiovascular issues, were consistently reported during the initial summer period compared to the subsequent two reporting periods, suggesting heightened physiological stress during peak heat exposure.

The Zaatari camp is situated in a remote area near the Syrian–Jordanian border. The surrounding land and landscape are mostly barren desert, except for some agricultural areas and a small nearby town. This creates ideal conditions for dust storms in spring and summer, which sweep through the unprotected caravans of the camp and expose residents to constant dusty environments. These localized dust storms became less frequent as the weather cooled in late summer and early fall. Our study population was asked about how these dusty conditions affect their health and symptoms. As expected, individuals with asthma reported a significantly greater impact of dust storms on their dyspnea and allergy symptoms. Additionally, those who experienced dyspnea or allergies from dust storms reported higher frequency of these issues during the first data collection in summer compared to later collections. There is a higher risk for asthmatics from climate‐related events and dust storms (Zheng et al., 2025), as reported by several studies from around the globe. Additionally, the dust can carry fungi, bacteria, or allergens (Papatheodorou et al., 2025) that can further trigger asthmatic patients and worsen their dyspnea (Zhang, Cheng, et al., 2024; Zhang, Zhou, & Lin, 2024). Allergens are also carried during dust storms, increasing the suffering and symptoms of asthmatics and allergy‐sensitized individuals (Zhang, Cheng, et al., 2024; Zhang, Zhou, & Lin, 2024). The Syrian refugee population, who have endured heat and dust in Zaatari camp for more than 14 years, have limited measures to protect themselves from frequent dust storms, which are combined with heat and rising desert temperatures (Alshawawreh & Rush, 2024). There is no electricity for air conditioning (Sandwell et al., 2025), and their poor housing conditions prevent them from insulating themselves against these harsh conditions. Our results confirmed this, demonstrating that those with other health conditions not related to asthma and allergies are less affected. Based on the data collected, it is important to note that this population is relatively young, with only 19% over the age of 60. They mostly live in extreme poverty and rely on UN aid for survival, along with some labor jobs outside the camp that require daily permits to leave (UNHCR & Partners, 2024). Overcrowding in housing is also an issue, as residents in larger households may experience higher levels of fatigue and discomfort during heatwaves due to increased sources of heat and dust exposure, limited personal space and more challenging ventilation conditions. In addition to household size, women in this population reported a higher frequency of adverse health impacts compared to men. This may be due to several potential factors: women often spend more time indoors, engage in caregiving roles such as looking after children or elderly family members, and may have greater sensitivity to internal symptoms or a higher tendency to report health issues. It is important to note that these explanations are hypothetical and were not directly tested in this study.

The study has several advantages related to its prospective design, which allowed us to identify trends concerning temperatures. These trends confirmed the expectation that higher temperatures would result in more complaints among participants. We also had access to the participants because of their affiliation with the clinic that provides their healthcare, which prevented attrition during follow‐up, especially since they are confined to the camp and cannot leave for other regions in Jordan. Therefore, their clinical diagnosis was verified clinically. However, the limitations include the absence of objective measures of the effects of heat or dust on health impacts reported. This may introduce some bias in responses and the familiarity with the questions after repeated data collection. This would likely lead to an underestimation of the true risks from heat or dust.

Conclusion

In this prospective study of refugees diagnosed with NCDs in Zaatari Refugee Camp, both heatwaves and dust storms were associated with increased self‐reported symptom burden, with the highest impact observed during peak summer conditions. Even moderate reductions in temperature and dust exposure were associated with measurable decreases in symptom reporting, suggesting that relatively small environmental changes meaningfully affect health in protracted displacement settings. Those with pre‐existing NCDs (most significantly, asthma), female gender, and larger household size were consistently associated with higher vulnerability. To our knowledge, this is the first study looking at climate impacts on NCDs among such group of patients living in refugee camps. This has implications for other displaced and vulnerable populations impacted by similar extreme weather events and have high disease risks. Our study highlights the need for prevention and education measures targeting these vulnerable populations, who will endure long‐term, slow adverse effects. Targeted, context‐specific interventions, such as improved cooling, shading, and dust mitigation, to reduce climate‐sensitive morbidity in displaced populations are some of the measures that can be taken by decision makers and health care providers for such group of patients. Interventions in low‐resource and humanitarian settings should prioritize scalable environmental adaptations that address both heat and dust exposure, particularly for the high‐risk subgroups identified in this study.
