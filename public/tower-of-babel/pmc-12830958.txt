Detection of energetic equivalence depends on food web architecture and estimators of energy use
Authors: Poppy Joaquina Romera, Benoit Gauzens, Ana Carolina Antunes, Ulrich Brose, Nico Eisenhauer, Olga Ferlian, Myriam R Hirt, Malte Jochum, Grace Mitchell, David Ott, Anton Potapov, Bibishan Rai, Stefan Scheu, Kiri Joy Wallace, Andrew D Barnes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEcologists have long debated the universality of the energetic equivalence rule, which posits that population energy use should be invariant with average body size due to negative size–density scaling. We explore size–density and size–energy use scaling across 183 geographically–distributed soil invertebrate food webs (comprising 55,054 individual soil invertebrates) to investigate the universality of these fundamental energetic equivalence rule assumptions across trophic levels and varying food web structure. Additionally, we compare two measures of energy use to investigate size–energy use relationships: population metabolism and energy fluxes. We find that size–density scaling does not support energetic equivalence in soil communities. Furthermore, evidence of energetic equivalence is dependent on the estimate of energy use applied, the trophic level of consumers, and food web properties. Our study demonstrates a need to integrate food web energetics and trophic structure to better understand how energetic constraints shape the body size structure of terrestrial ecosystems.Subject terms: Food webs, Ecological modelling

Introduction

The scaling of body size with organism metabolism and abundance are some of the most widely observed, studied, and debated biological relationships1. Yet, there is persisting discord around the generality of the energetic equivalence rule (EER) across ecosystems. This partly stems from variation in the underlying biological assumptions of the EER, differences in approaches used to estimate community body size–energy use relationships, and limited consideration of how EER scaling depends on organism trophic position and food web architecture. To date, few studies have quantified the influence of these underlying expectations and ecological properties on the scaling of organism body size and population energy use estimates, despite early advancements in size–density scaling to understand energetic constraints of food webs over two decades ago2,3.

Assessing the EER is fundamentally reliant on determining the scaling relationship between population body size and energy use. The EER is a central expectation of the Metabolic Theory of Ecology (MTE), which predicts that population n energy use (hereafter, En, the total energy consumption required to support population (density) n biomass) should be invariant with respect to population–average body size, that is, En∝Mn0. This is based on the underlying assumptions that 1) individual organism energy use i (Ei) scales with whole–organism body size Mi with a positive ¾ (Ei∝Mi0.75) exponent, and 2) the abundance (i.e., density) N of individuals scales with population body size M with a negative ¾ (N∝M–0.75) exponent2. A zero exponent of body size–energy use scaling of populations in a given community (i.e., En∝Mn0) should only be met if whole organism body size–energy use (i.e., Ei∝Mi) and size–density (N∝Mb) inverse relationships have opposite exponent values4 (e.g., Ei∝Mi0.75 and N∝M–0.75). Numerous studies have found a –0.75 size–density scaling exponent (N∝M–0.75) among populations within communities1,5–7, though many others have found this varies considerably across taxa1,8,9. These observed deviations likely arise from taxonomic variation in the allometric exponent of basal metabolic rates and varying energetic constraints across trophic levels, life history traits, environmental conditions, and spatial scale10–13. Nevertheless, an expectation of a –0.75 size–density exponent is still widely employed1 at both local and macro–ecological scales14. Despite the fundamental assumptions of the EER, few studies have analysed both size–density and size–energy use relationships to detect energetic equivalence5,6,15, and most assume energetic equivalence based only on size–density (N∝M–0.75) or whole–organism body size–energy use (Ei∝Mi 0.75) scaling7,16,17. Furthermore, describing size–energy use scaling relationships poses the challenge of assessing population energy use, which is typically achieved by quantifying population metabolism. This approach, however, does not consider additional energetic expenditure due to the inefficiency of trophic energy transfer, losses to predation from higher trophic levels, and other trophic–level–dependent variation in energy loss4,11. Although the EER is derived from macro–scale patterns (the GSDR)14, we should still expect this rule to apply for communities at carrying capacity, which would theoretically be evident from a size–density scaling relationship exponent of –0.75. Arguably, however, a more robust test of the EER at the local community scale would be to examine the population energy use–body size relationship, as this accounts for differences in energy use due to factors like taxonomic composition and trophic structure.

Size–density and size–energy use scaling relationships have been shown to vary systematically across trophic levels (Fig. 1)1,3,4,18. While some studies have explored body size scaling relationships within a food web context6,19,20, they lack further synthesis into the comparison between these size–density scaling exponents on their resulting size–energy use scaling. Energy availability decreases with increasing trophic level and body size, due to inefficient energy transfer from resources to consumers18,21. Therefore, the scaling of size–density and size–energy use should be N∝M<–0.75 (Fig. 1a) and En∝Mn<0 (Fig. 1b), respectively, across all trophic levels, compared with N∝M–0.75 and En∝Mn0 within single trophic levels18 that share the same energy pool14. However, secondary consumers typically span multiple trophic levels that do not directly access basal resources and tend to prefer larger prey21, so they should have the steepest size–density and size–energy use scaling exponents (Fig. 1a, b) due to greater energetic constraints18 (although there are documented exceptions across aquatic versus terrestrial systems22). Systematic deviations from expected scaling exponents related to trophic positioning of species question the theoretical foundation of the EER and call for a food web approach that discriminates among trophic levels, as information on energetic constraints could be obscured by ignoring trophic differentiation19.

Whilst there has been research into environmental drivers of size–density scaling relationships underlying the EER10–13,23–26, how variation in food web architecture and estimators of energy use affect these relationships remains unexplored (but see Loeuille & Loreau27 which examines how body size affects species interactions). Alternative measures of energy use, such as trophic energy flux, account for total transfer of energy in and out of a population and can therefore describe total energetic losses (and thus expenditure) due to metabolism, assimilation efficiency, and consumption by higher trophic levels (i.e., predation)28,29. These losses must be balanced by energetic gains and are therefore directly related to population energy use28. Thus, despite uncertainty that is inherently introduced by modelling energy fluxes based on published input parameters, measures of total energy consumption (i.e., influx of energy into a given food web node before assimilation) should provide more precise estimates of population energy use that can be integrated into analyses of size–energy use scaling relationships in food webs (Fig. 1)29.

Here we analyse size–density (N∝Mb) and size–energy use (En∝Mnb) scaling using a comprehensive dataset of individual body sizes (body mass in mg) in soil invertebrate food webs (i.e., local communities; Fig. 2) across four geographical locations (i.e., 183 food webs comprising 55,054 individual invertebrates). We investigate the universality of size–density scaling relationships (based on individual size distributions as opposed to population–level size–density scaling relationships19) and their likelihood of indicating energetic equivalence in soil communities of primary and secondary consumers. We compare two different estimates of energy use to investigate differences in size–energy use relationships, population metabolism (In∝Mn) and energy fluxes (Fn∝Mn), and investigate whether these scaling exponents are reflected by size–density relationships. Additionally, we investigate the marginal effects of node richness (i.e., number of nodes within each food web, where a node is defined as organism taxonomic order), trophic similarity (i.e., mean similarity between nodes per food web), and omnivory (i.e., trophic level omnivory of secondary consumers) on the deviation of food webs from predicted size–metabolism or size–energy flux exponents. We hypothesise that (1) N∝Mb and En∝Mnb scaling across all trophic levels (i.e., entire food webs) and for secondary consumers will be more negative than the MTE expectations of scaling exponents (i.e., N∝M<–0.75 and En∝Mn<0) due to attenuating trophic transfer efficiency at higher trophic levels. However, we acknowledge that increasing energetic constraints at higher trophic positions may also be partially relaxed by increasing prey richness, available energy sources, and spatial scale of movement of larger–bodied consumers21,30. In contrast, we hypothesise that (2) primary consumers will exhibit N∝M–0.75 and En∝Mn0 scaling because individuals within their trophic guilds feed from and compete over the same basal resource base18,21. We also hypothesise that (3) En∝Mnb scaling using metabolism I as an estimate of energy use will be less likely to produce scaling exponents that meet expectations compared to energy flux F, as metabolism does not account for additional energetic expenditure due to assimilation (in)efficiency and losses to predation by higher trophic level consumers. Finally, we hypothesise that deviations from EER expectations of En∝Mn0 scaling will be smaller in food webs with higher node richness and trophic similarity due to greater resource competition imposing proportionately stronger energetic constraints on larger–bodied consumers. Conversely, we expect increasing deviations from En∝Mn0 scaling with omnivory as energetic constraints may be relaxed when consumers feed across more energy sources21, leading to En∝Mn0 scaling exponents > 0. However, we also assume these effects of food web properties on deviations from EER expectations will vary across trophic levels due to differences discussed above31,32.

Results

Testing the energetic equivalence rule without trophic differentiation

Scaling exponents of soil invertebrate consumers across all trophic levels in soil food webs were, on average, more positive than expected for size–density (i.e., N∝M>–0.75, mean = –0.174, median = –0.152, σ2 = 0.05), size–metabolism (i.e., In∝Mn>0, mean = 0.134, median = 0.164, σ2 = 0.07), and size–energy flux (i.e., Fn∝Mn>0, mean = 0.204, median = 0.195, σ2 = 0.09) relationships (Fig. 3). Nevertheless, scaling exponents derived from both measures of energy use (In∝Mn and Fn∝Mn) appeared to have a similar central tendency (kurtosis = 4, and In∝Mn σ2 = 0.07, Fn∝Mn σ2 = 0.09) around values expected by the MTE, although we found significantly different probability distributions of estimated In∝Mn and Fn∝Mn scaling exponents (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test D = 0.148, p = 0.037). Overall, we found that only 1.1% of all soil food webs met size–density expectations, in that they contained –0.75 within the confidence interval of exponent estimates (Fig. 3a). At the whole food web level, metabolism as an estimate of energy use appeared to better fit EER expectations compared to energy flux, as 50.3% of all soil food webs met scaling expectations for size–metabolism relationships (Fig. 3b) compared to 41.5% for size–energy flux relationships (Fig. 3c). There was a significant positive and negative effect of node richness on the deviation of food webs from the hypothesised size–metabolism and size–energy flux exponent (p < 0.01, Fig. 3; Fig. S3a, d, Table S4), respectively. We also found a significant negative effect of trophic similarity (i.e., the mean similarity among all nodes in the trophic level (primary consumers or predators) on the deviation of whole food webs from both the hypothesised size–metabolism (In∝Mn0) and size–energy flux (Fn∝Mn0) exponents (p < 0.01, Fig. 6; Fig. S3b, e, Table S4).

Variation in energetic scaling patterns across trophic levels and food web structure

Similar to the trends in EER scaling relationships observed for whole food webs, primary consumers produced scaling exponents that were, on average, more positive than expected for size–density (N∝M>–0.75, mean = –0.191, median = –0.109, σ2 = 0.16), size–metabolism (In∝Mn>0, mean = 0.261, median = 0.268, σ2 = 0.12), and size–energy flux (Fn∝Mn>0 mean = 0.761, median = 0.646, σ2 = 0.51) relationships (Fig. 4a–c). Secondary consumer scaling exponents were, on average, more positive than expected for size–density (N∝M>–0.75, mean = –0.218, median = –0.201, σ2 = 0.06) relationships (Fig. 4d). However, as we predicted, they were more negative for size–metabolism (In∝Mn<0, mean = –0.039, median = –0.003, σ2 = 0.14) and size–energy flux (Fn∝Mn<0, mean = –0.394, median = –0.524, σ2 = 0.57) relationships (Fig. 4e, f). Out of 173 primary consumer communities, 1.1% met scaling expectations for size–density (N∝M–0.75), 42.9% met size–metabolism (In∝Mn0) expectations, and 46.3% met size–energy flux (Fn∝Mn0) scaling expectations of the MTE. Comparatively, out of 180 secondary consumer communities, 2.8% met scaling expectations for size–density, 41.4% met size–metabolism scaling expectations, and 54.1% met size–energy flux scaling expectations of the MTE. We also found less variation (i.e., higher kurtosis and variance of the respective density functions for scaling exponent estimates) around the mean and median exponents for the scaling of body size with energy fluxes (Fn∝Mn) compared to metabolism (In∝Mn) for secondary consumers, though the opposite was true for primary consumer kurtosis values. These observed differences between measures of energy use were further corroborated by significant differences in the probability distributions of estimated metabolism versus energy flux exponents for both primary (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test D = 0.411, p < 0.01) and secondary consumers (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test D = 0.406, p < 0.01). Contrary to our hypothesis (Fig. 1b, d), we found that mean and median exponent values for In∝Mn and Fn∝Mn relationships were closer to zero for secondary consumers than for primary consumers. To ensure that our results were not notably biased by differences among datasets in the measurement of environmental temperature, we recalculated metabolic rates and energy fluxes with temperatures extracted from WorldClim33 and re–ran analyses to test for the sensitivity of EER scaling results to variations in environmental temperature, which yielded no qualitative changes in our results (Figs. S1 & S2).

We only found a significant positive effect of trophic similarity within secondary consumer food webs on the deviation from size–metabolism scaling expectations (In∝Mn0, Fig. 6b). In contrast, for size–energy use scaling based on energy fluxes, we found a significant positive effect of node richness and negative effects of trophic similarity and omnivory on the deviation from size–energy flux scaling expectations for secondary consumers (Fn∝Mn0, p < 0.01, Fig. 6d–f, Table S4). Discussion

Using a comprehensive dataset of individual body sizes in soil food webs to date, we found broadly contrasting evidence for energetic equivalence in soil ecosystems across the 183 food webs. Inference of energetic equivalence was especially variable between scaling exponents from size–density, size–metabolism, and size–energy flux scaling. Very few soil food webs (1.1%) met size–density scaling expectations (i.e., exponent of –0.75), whereas 50.3 and 41.5% of food webs also met size–metabolism and size–energy flux expectations (i.e., exponent of zero) derived from the MTE, respectively (Figs. 4, 6; also supported by RMA model fitting; Fig. S4). Greater than expected size–density scaling exponents (using an ISD approach) are in line with findings from studies19 whereby scaling exponents were much greater in forests compared to other systems, though we still lack a mechanistic explanation for this phenomenon. Nevertheless, our analyses suggest that metabolism better fits EER expectations at the whole food web level (Fig. 3), but size–energy use scaling relationships using energy flux produced exponents closer to zero expectations for secondary consumer communities (Figs. 4 and 5)18. Our results demonstrate notable differences in size–density and size–energy use scaling relationships within and across primary and secondary consumer trophic levels. Thus, each scaling relationship presents different crucial pieces of information about the energetic structure of food webs18.

The disparity between size–density and size–energy use scaling relationships clearly demonstrates that size–density scaling exponents near –0.75 may not equate to energetic equivalence in local communities14,19. Even though size–density scaling is related to energy use within a community, it is unlikely a reliable indicator of energetic equivalence15,34. Indeed, our findings suggest that many past studies may have inaccurately assumed a lack of community energetic equivalence based on size–density scaling exponents that do not equal –0.757,16,17. Size–density scaling exponents of –0.75 due to energetic equivalence are contingent on a whole organism size–metabolism allometric exponent of 0.7516,19, which is known to be untrue for many taxa1,35 and may contribute to the disparity in findings between size–density and size–energy use exponents. However, direct comparisons between size-density and size-energy use scaling from our analyses must be made with considerable caution, due to the necessary different model fitting approaches used to estimate scaling exponents (i.e., ML estimation of ISDs versus OLS estimation of scaling between individual size bins and energy use). Nevertheless, our study revealed that energy flux may provide a more precise estimation of the energetic demands within trophic levels, especially for secondary consumers in food webs (Figs. 4 and 5). In contrast to metabolism, energy flux captures losses in energy from assimilation and transfer efficiency, in addition to metabolic losses, especially in secondary consumers that experience greater energetic constraints than primary consumers31. Primary consumers are also highly dependent on ecosystem–specific primary productivity which varies across ecosystems from sub–arctic to tropical regions, such as those analysed in our study32. Furthermore, ecosystems at various stages of succession following subsequent natural or human disturbances are unlikely to be at demographic equilibrium as assumed by MTE and, hence, are unlikely to meet EER expectations4,10,15,36. Many constraints under natural conditions pose significant challenges to proving energetic equivalence of communities. Simplified, controlled experiments are therefore needed where energy inputs into the system and individual energy intake within a food web are quantifiable. Taken together, our findings suggest that estimates of energy use other than metabolism are likely required for accurate estimates of trophic level–specific size–energy use scaling relationships and, therefore, the detection of energetic equivalence across ecosystems.

Our finding that primary consumers had EER exponents significantly flatter than predicted values (i.e., In∝Mn>0, t = 7.779, p < 0.01 or Fn∝Mn>0, t = 14.727, p < 0.01; Figs. 4–5 & Fig. S5) compared to secondary consumer EER exponents (i.e., In∝Mn<0 or Fn∝Mn<0, Figs. 4–5 & Fig. S5) is likely due to weaker energetic constraints imposed on primary consumers that directly access energy from a less limiting basal resource pool (see also18). Additionally, this phenomenon follows the food web subsidy hypothesis where organisms with access to resource subsidies, such as millipedes and earthworms (i.e., primary consumers), will have size–energy use scaling exponents more positive than predicted by the MTE. Primary consumers may also be more size compartmentalised than secondary consumers, allowing for resource subsidisation of large primary consumers and relaxing energetic constraints within food webs37. However, the secondary consumers in our study comprised organisms from multiple trophic levels. These results support the hypothesis that size–energy use scaling across communities made up of multiple trophic levels should be more negative than those within a singular trophic level18. The slightly more negative size–density exponents seen in secondary (mean = –0.218) versus primary (mean = –0.191) consumers (although this difference was not statistically significant; p > 0.05) are also consistent with the resource–thinning hypothesis in soil food webs proposed by Ehnes15,22, where the abundance of organisms decreases with body size even more than expected with increasing trophic level because of increasing energetic constraints (Fig. 4a, d). Additionally, the less negative size–density and more positive size–energy use scaling exponents than expected for soil invertebrate consumers across all trophic levels further suggests that the allometric exponent of basal metabolic rates varies among taxa. Therefore, a broader synthesis of EER scaling relationships spanning ecosystems and biogeographic regions is needed to gain a more general understanding of the ecological and evolutionary factors that influence body size structure and energetic constraints of food webs (see Damuth5,38). Interestingly, our results differ from previous studies that suggest organism size does not offer an energetic advantage, such that energy use is independent of body size38. Instead, we find that eco–evolutionary mechanisms likely influence the body size structure of soil food webs, whereby population body size covaries systematically with population energy use. Our findings therefore suggest that soil food webs do not follow expectations of the EER, possibly due to underlying evolutionary drivers of body size in belowground systems.

Past studies have explored abiotic sources of variation in EER scaling relationships10,23,25, but none have investigated the role of food web architecture. We found various food web properties were important predictors of energetic equivalence in soil food webs. Increased node richness resulted in whole soil food webs being significantly less likely to meet size–metabolism and more likely to meet size–energy flux predictions (Fig. S3a, d). Increased trophic similarity resulted in significantly less deviation from both size–metabolism and size–energy flux predictions (Fig. S3b, e). Increased node richness of secondary consumers in soil food webs resulted in significantly greater deviations from size–energy flux expectations (Fig. 6), whereas food webs with lower trophic similarity and omnivory of secondary consumers were more likely to meet size–energy flux expectations (Fig. 6) derived by the MTE. As node richness increases within food webs, competition should also increase due to niche overlap31,32,39, which should in turn increase energetic constraints and the likelihood of meeting EER expectations (Fig. S3)18. Ulrich et al.40 similarly found higher trophic levels (predators) were more likely to meet EER expectations compared to decomposers (i.e., primary consumers). In addition, increasing secondary consumer node richness should allow for greater trophic complementarity39 due to more trophic niches that support increased niche partitioning31,32. This should relax competition for resources31,32, reducing energetic constraints on secondary consumers and, therefore, observed deviations from size–energy flux expectations. However, the whole food web deviations do not seem congruent with those within trophic levels. Interestingly, deviations from EER expectations were greater when node richness was high and trophic similarity and omnivory were low (i.e., high taxonomic and trophic diversity). These deviations could be due to especially high niche complementarity and facilitation that could relax energetic constraints, allowing for larger than expected populations for a given body size. Ultimately, our results provide evidence that food webs with more secondary consumer nodes (i.e., greater predator diversity) are more diverse in their trophic niches, experience less competition, and may therefore be less likely to meet EER expectations.

Our study raises important questions about our understanding of the transfer of energy within food webs. By employing an energetic food web approach, across 183 soil food webs consisting of 55,054 individual soil invertebrates, our study sheds light on systematic variation in energetic constraints across trophic levels. While metabolism appeared to be a better indicator of energetic equivalence at the whole food web level, energy flux provided a better estimate of energy use for indicating energetic equivalence within trophic levels, especially for secondary consumers. Our results suggest the EER in local communities, according to the classic MTE expectations of negative size-density scaling, should be rejected for soil food webs. In contrast, we found frequent support for energetic equivalence based on size-energy use scaling, particularly for secondary consumers. We therefore urge future studies to employ an energetic food web approach that considers trophic structuring and food web energy use to explore energetic equivalence across ecosystems.

Methods

Soil invertebrate community sampling

Soil invertebrate community data was collected from four locations (Aotearoa New Zealand, Indonesia, Canada, and U.S.A) through sampling efforts of three large–scale projects (Fig. 2). (1) The Aotearoa New Zealand plots (People Cities & Nature programme) were established in forest patches within nine cities spanning ~9o of latitude (Fig. 2, Table S1). These were part of a 60–year–old planted and primary old–growth urban forest chronosequence (n = 73). (2) The Indonesian (EFForTS project) study plots (n = 30, Fig. 2) were established in remnant tropical rainforest, jungle rubber agroforest systems, rubber monocultures, and oil palm monocultures across two landscapes surrounding Bukit Duabelas and Harapan rainforest reserves in the Jambi Province, Sumatra, Indonesia10,41. The ECOWORM project study plots (n = 80, Fig. 2) in (3) the United States (St John’s forest, Minnesota) and (4) Canada (Barrier Lake North, Barrier Lake South, and Bull Creek Hills in the Canadian Rocky Mountains, Kananaskis Valley, Alberta) were established in maple and aspen forest sites, respectively, whereby each of these forests contained a low–invasion and a high–invasion exotic earthworm area–per forest (10 plots in low– and 10 plots in high–invasion areas)10,42.

Soil invertebrate community data collection and preparation

Aotearoa New Zealand soil invertebrates were sampled between November 2019–February 2020 (austral summer) from three 50 × 50 cm subplots by taking one macrofauna soil core (22 cm diameter, 10 cm deep from the mineral soil layers) and one mesofauna soil core (5 cm diameter, 10 cm deep mineral soil layers) per subplot. Macro– and mesofauna were extracted from the soil cores using heat extraction and identified in the laboratory to family or order level (i.e., node)43. Individual body length (mm) was converted to fresh body size (mg) using length–size scaling relationships according to different invertebrate phylogenetic groups from published literature (Table S2)44–46. Soil invertebrate collection from all other geographic regions followed similar procedures, whereby mesofauna were collected either from small cores (5 cm diameter, 10 cm deep) or 16 × 16 cm quadrats (5 cm deep), and macrofauna were collected from large soil cores (20 cm diameter, 10 cm deep), leaf litter sieving (0.5 m2 ECOWORM and 1 m2 EFForTS), and mustard extraction for earthworms (0.25 m2). Invertebrate body size ranged from 4.82 × 10–5 mg – 5290 mg. For more detailed methods see Antunes10.

To analyse size–energy use relationships, we took a size–binning (rather than populations of competing organisms) approach by taking the natural logarithm of all body sizes, across all taxonomic groups, and grouped them into 0.25 ln(mg) size class bins (following Reuman20). This size binning approach was used for two reasons. Firstly, population-level information (i.e., species level taxonomic resolution) was not always available for our data, though individual body sizes were. Secondly, these methods have both yielded similar size–density scaling relationships within many food webs studied19, so we can assume this consistency also applies to tests of size–energy use scaling relationships. We took the median of the natural logarithm of body size of each size class bin per plot, across all taxonomic groups. Abundance was then calculated as the number of individuals in each size class bin (and per node type for analysing within trophic levels) per plot and scaled to 1 m2. For analysing size–density relationships, the counts of soil invertebrate fresh body sizes (mg) across and within trophic levels were scaled up to 1 m2. Food web reconstruction and consumer energy intake calculation

To reconstruct soil food webs, nodes were defined according to organism taxonomic order (or family level for Hymenoptera and Coleoptera) for which information on their general feeding preferences was obtained. We constructed a meta–matrix of binary consumer–resource interactions, whereby feeding links were assigned based on feeding preferences obtained from literature43,48. Energy influx in J day–1 into each consumer node within a local food web (i.e., plot) was calculated using the food–web energetics approach as described by Barnes28 and Gauzens29 to quantify the energy intake of organisms across the 183 local food webs. Statistical analysis

To analyse size–density relationships (N∝Mb) across whole soil invertebrate food webs and within primary and secondary consumers, we used maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to estimate the exponent b (i.e., λ + 1)19,52 derived from individual size distributions (ISD) of all individual body sizes (scaled to 1 m2) within soil invertebrate communities using the sizeSpectra package in R (see Edwards53). To check the fit of models against observed body size data (x), rank–frequency plots were produced giving the natural logarithm–rank of the number of observations ≥ x against the value of x (See supplementary code to perform MLE model cheques)53.

To analyse size–metabolism (In∝Mn), and size–energy flux (Fn∝Mn) scaling relationships across whole soil invertebrate food webs and within primary and secondary consumers, we used ordinary least squared (OLS) regression using logarithm median body mass data (scaled to 1 m2). MLE and OLS regression models were run across all communities (i.e., plots) within the four geographic regions. The 95% confidence intervals around the exponent values were also calculated, and exponents were considered to meet N∝M–0.75, In∝Mn0, or Fn∝Mn0 expectations if these confidence intervals included the expected exponent value. We excluded all resulting models with less than 10 body size class bins or individuals per food web from our analysis due to insufficient data for estimating reliable model fits. To evaluate the distribution of scaling exponents around the mean and median values of our models, we calculated the kurtosis of their probability density functions using the kurtosis function in R54. In this regard, higher kurtosis values indicate greater deviation from a normal distribution, reflecting increased variation around the mean and median exponent values. We also ran a two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, to determine if our two sample distributions (e.g., metabolism and energy flux exponents) come from the same probability distribution using the ks.test function in R54. To analyse size–metabolism (In∝Mn), and size–energy flux (Fn∝Mn) scaling relationships across whole soil invertebrate food webs and within primary and secondary consumers, we also used ranged major axis (RMA) regression using the lmodel2 package in R to analyse these relationships55. RMA regression is used when both x and y variables (e.g., median body size, abundance, metabolism, and energy flux) in the regression equation are random55. RMA regression models were run across all communities (i.e., plots) within the four geographic regions. We excluded all resulting models with less than 10 body size class bins or individuals per food web from our analysis due to insufficient data for estimating reliable model fits. As a result, our analyses yielded a total of 183 food webs (i.e., comprising both primary and secondary consumers) and 360 trophic level models (180 models for primary consumers and 180 for secondary consumer food webs). Additionally, we investigated whether the likelihood of size–density and size–energy use relationships to meet EER expectations could be explained by variation in food web structure. To do so, for all food webs, we constructed a normal linear model testing for the response of the absolute logarithm–deviation of observed exponents from expected size–energy use exponents (In∝Mn0 and Fn∝Mn0) to omnivory, node richness, and trophic similarity per food web and per trophic level (i.e., primary and secondary consumers). This produced six linear models in total. All statistical analyses were performed in R version R 4.4.054.
