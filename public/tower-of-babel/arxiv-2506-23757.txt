Training of Spiking Neural Networks with Expectation-Propagation
Dan Yao, Steve McLaughlin, and Yoann Altmann

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a unifying message-passing framework for training spiking neural networks (SNNs) using Expectation-Propagation. Our gradient-free method is capable of learning the marginal distributions of network parameters and simultaneously marginalizes nuisance parameters, such as the outputs of hidden layers. This framework allows for the first time, training of discrete and continuous weights, for deterministic and stochastic spiking networks, using batches of training samples. Although its convergence is not ensured, the algorithm converges in practice faster than gradient-based methods, without requiring a large number of passes through the training data. The classification and regression results presented pave the way for new efficient training methods for deep Bayesian networks.



I
Introduction

Spiking neural networks (SNNs), as the third generation of neural network models
[
1
]
, have emerged as a powerful approach to information processing inspired by biological brains
[
2
,
3
]
. Unlike conventional artificial neural networks (ANNs)
that transmit information using continuous values, SNNs operate on binary spike trains. By employing spiking neurons as the computational units, temporal information is encoded in the dynamics of the spikes, which can mimic the behavior of real biological neurons in response to external stimuli. Such characteristics make SNNs particularly suited to perform energy efficient computation
[
4
,
2
,
5
]
.
In recent years, SNNs have demonstrated promising potential in a variety of applications where higher energy efficiency and lower computational cost is important, such as image classification
[
6
]
, spiked-based object detection
[
7
]
, robot control
[
8
]
and
neuromorphic simultaneous localization and
mapping (SLAM)
[
9
,
10
]
.

However, SNN training remains challenging and has impeded their more widespread deployment. One of the main difficulties arises from the non-differentiability of the typical spiking activation. To address this challenge, a variety of SNN training methods have been proposed and they can be categorized into two main types. Indirect training methods typically train traditional (non-spiking) ANNs and convert the architectures into SNNs
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. Conversely, most direct training methods employ classical surrogate gradient methods
[
15
,
16
]
, leaving the original training loss unchanged. However, it is possible to reformulate the training task, using probabilistic models and methods
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
, to derive new objective functions that are either differentiable, or do not rely on gradient-based optimization. Moreover, Bayesian methods can also potentially provide uncertainty quantification tools such as parameter posterior distributions that can be particularly useful to assess relevance of features
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. The method proposed in this paper belongs to this last family of direct training methods.

SNN architectures can also be divided in two families based on their neuron types. The most popular SNNs for computer vision tasks are networks whose activation functions are deterministic, e.g. the Heaviside function, with parameters/weights treated as deterministic variables. We refer to those networks as deterministic SNNs. Stochastic SNNs on the other hand, rely on recent studies in cognitive science and neuroscience that have shown that neurons and synapses are inherently stochastic
[
24
,
25
]
. In this work, we propose a framework able to handle deterministic and stochastic SNN training using Bayesian inference, in a similar fashion to
[
26
]
. This allows prior knowledge such as pre-training results or weight constraints to be incorporated in a principled manner.

As for most deep-learning architectures, exact Bayesian computation is not tractable given the large number of network unknowns and in this work, we propose a flexible network based on Expectation-Propagation (EP)
[
27
,
28
]
. EP-based methods are a family of variational inference tools which allow for message-passing type of updates. In contrast to most variational Bayes methods which also rely on Kullback-Leibler divergences as similarity metrics between distributions, EP does not require distributions to be differentiable.
From a methodological viewpoint, EP-based methods have been recently proposed to train deep Bayesian networks
[
29
,
30
]
, including spiking networks
[
31
]
and networks with binary/discrete weights
[
32
]
. However, methods such as probabilistic backpropagation
[
33
]
are not explicitly designed to allow multiple passes (epochs) through the data or process simultaneously multiple training samples, as intended here. Thus, they tend to provide unreliable uncertainty estimates. We combine EP and stochastic EP (SEP)
[
34
,
35
]
in a modular fashion and the method, tailored here to train SNNs, can be generalized to other, potentially recurrent, neural networks. The resulting algorithm still preserves the notions of forward and backward passes through the network, as in probabilistic backpropagation
[
33
]
.

The main contributions of the paper can be summarized as follows.

•

We derive an EP-based method able to train feedforward neural networks using mini-batches. This method, applied to spiking architectures without recurrent neurons, serves as a building block to train recurrent SNNs.

•

The method is able to handle, in a unified framework, deterministic and stochastic SNNs as well as the estimation of discrete and continuous weights.

•

In addition to point estimates, the method provides marginal weight uncertainties and allows the computation of predictive distributions at inference time without resorting to Monte Carlo sampling (of the network weights or stochastic activation functions).

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section
II
introduces the neuron model and SNN architectures considered. Section
III
derives the likelihood associated with SNNs and the resulting posterior distribution of the network parameters. Section
IV
details how to compute approximating distributions by combining stochastic/average EP updates. Section
V

discusses convergence and the use of large datasets.
The potential of the method is illustrated using results obtained for classification and regression problems in Section
VI
. Conclusions and potential future work are finally reported in Section
VII
.

II
Preliminaries

We consider a general supervised training task where a set of i.i.d. input/output data pairs
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}
is available. In the context of SNNs, the input
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
is typically a binary set of inputs with multiple timestamps, but in this paper we focus on single timestamps. In contrast,
\boldsymbol{y}_{n}
corresponds to the network output, which can be binary values (spiking output, e.g. for classification problems), real values (e.g. for regression problems), or a combination of those, depending on the activation function used in the output layer (see Section
II-A
below).

II-A

Spiking neurons under Spike Response Model

In this work, we consider a simplified Spike Response Model (SRM)
[
36
]
for our spiking neurons. Following SRM, the spiking activity of neuron
i
is determined by a scalar internal state, known as membrane potential
u_{i,n}
, which can be expressed as

u_{i,n}=\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{P}_{i}}w_{ij}v_{j,n},

(1)

where
w_{ij}
is the synaptic weight connecting neuron
i
to its presynaptic neuron
j
and
\mathcal{P}_{i}=\{j|j\text{ presynaptic to }i\}
. Moreover,
v_{j,n}
represents the
feedforward
(spiking) signal from the neuron
j
of the previous layer, for sample
n
. Note that to simplify the algorithm description, Eq. (
1
) does not include biases, but these can easily be included.

The spiking output of each neuron
v_{i,n}
is modeled using a stochastic, potentially degenerate distribution
f(v_{i,n}|u_{i,n})
, conditioned on the neuron membrane potential
u_{i,n}
.
The typical options in this paper for
f(v_{i,n}|u_{i,n})
are

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}f(v_{i,n}|u_{i,n})=&\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\delta(v_{i,n}-1)\penalty\ \penalty\ \textrm{if}\penalty\ \penalty\ u_{i,n}\geq 0\\
\delta(v_{i,n})\penalty\ \penalty\ \textrm{if}\penalty\ \penalty\ u_{i,n}<0\end{array}\right.,\\
f(v_{i,n}|u_{i,n})=&\mathcal{B}ern\left(\sigma(u_{i,n})\right)\\
\end{array}\right.,

where
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac delta function and
\sigma(u_{i,n})=(1+\text{exp}^{-u_{i,n}})^{-1}
is the usual sigmoid function. These options correspond to classical deterministic SNNs (with Heaviside activation) and probabilistic SNNs
[
17
]
, respectively. However, other activation functions can also be used. For instance, if the output of the network is continuous,
f(v_{i,n}|u_{i,n})
can be set as a Gaussian distribution.

II-B

Network architecture and training task

In this work, we consider layered network architectures. More precisely, the feedforward networks consist of
L
fully connected layers.
Each layer
\ell
has
V_{\ell}
spiking neurons, for
\ell=1,\dots,L
. At the
\ell
-th layer, we denote by
\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{V_{\ell}\times V_{\ell-1}}
the weight matrix, which leads to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}^{(\ell-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{V_{\ell}\times 1},

(7)

where
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}^{(\ell-1)}
denotes the forward signal (or output) of layer
(\ell-1)
for samples
n
and where the same convention is used to define
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{(\ell)}
(with
\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
).

The main training task addressed in this paper consists of estimating (the distribution of) the network trainable parameters
\mathcal{W}=\{\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
. However, since the mappings
f(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell)}|\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}^{(\ell-1)})
can be stochastic, the (spiking) output of each layer is a random vector, which propagates uncertainties in the subsequent layers of the network. Thus, the estimation of
\mathcal{W}
also requires the estimation or marginalization of a range of nuisance parameters, as will be discussed in the next section.

III
Bayesian model for SNN training

III-A

Forward model for a single training sample

The layered structure presented in the previous section is particularly well suited to derive a statistical model relating the network inputs to its outputs.

Using Eqs. (
II-A
) and (
7
), the stochastic model linking the input
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}^{(\ell-1)}
to the output
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}^{(\ell)}
of layer
\ell
can be expressed as
f(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell)}|\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}^{(\ell-1)})
.
However, to facilitate inference using message passing, we extend this model, using
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{(\ell)}
as auxiliary variable, such that

f(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell)},\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{(\ell)}|\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}^{(\ell-1)})=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell)}|\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{(\ell)})f(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{(\ell)}|\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}^{(\ell-1)}),

(8)

with

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{(\ell)}|\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}^{(\ell-1)})=\delta\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{(\ell)}-\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell-1)}\right).

(9)

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}=\{\{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L},\{\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L-1}\}
gather all the unknown latent variables associated with the
n
-th training sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
. When feeding the network with
\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
and
\mathcal{W}=\{\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}\}_{l=1}^{L}
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n},\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(L)}|\mathcal{W},\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(0)})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}f(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell)},\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{(\ell)}|\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}^{(\ell-1)}).

(10)

III-B

Forward model for the whole training set

A few additional notations are needed to simplify the presentation of the joint forward model, namely
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\}_{n}
, as well as
\boldsymbol{U}^{(\ell)}=\{\boldsymbol{u}_{n}^{(\ell)}\}_{n}
,
\boldsymbol{V}^{(\ell)}=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell)}\}_{n}
,
\boldsymbol{U}=\{\boldsymbol{U}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
and
\boldsymbol{V}=\{\boldsymbol{V}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1}^{L-1}
, such that
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}=\{\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{U}\}
. Note that the outputs of the last layer are not included in
\boldsymbol{V}
, since they are known/fixed during training, and are thus not treated as latent variables.
The network weights
\mathcal{W}
are global parameters shared by all the training samples. Assuming the training data are mutually independent, Eq. (
10
) leads to

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}},\boldsymbol{V}^{(L)}|\mathcal{W},\boldsymbol{V}^{(0)})=\prod_{n=1}^{N}f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n},\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(L)}|\mathcal{W},\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(0)})

(11)

III-C

Bayesian training of SNNs

Adopting a Bayesian approach,
\mathcal{W}
can be assigned a prior
p_{0}(\mathcal{W})
and one can conceptually obtain the marginal posterior distribution of
\mathcal{W}
given the training dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N}
,

p\left(\mathcal{W}|\mathcal{D}_{N}\right)\propto p_{0}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)\left[\displaystyle\int f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}},\boldsymbol{V}^{(L)}|\mathcal{W},\boldsymbol{V}^{(0)})d\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\right],

(12)

where the local/nuisance latent parameters in
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
are marginalised. However, the high dimensional integral in Eq. (
12
) is intractable, in particular because the dimension of
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
grows linearly with the number of training samples. Even if the integral was analytically tractable, computing marginal moments from
p\left(\mathcal{W}|\mathcal{D}_{N}\right)
would remain challenging, irrespective of whether the weights are considered as continuous or discrete random variables. Thus approximate methods are needed. In the remainder of this paper, the prior model
p_{0}(\mathcal{W})
is assumed to be fully separable and known (user-defined).

IV
Proposed EP-based training method

In this section, we derive an EP-based method to approximate

\displaystyle p\left(\mathcal{W},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}|\mathcal{D}_{N}\right)\propto p_{0}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)f(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}},\boldsymbol{V}^{(L)}|\mathcal{W},\boldsymbol{V}^{(0)}),

(13)

by a surrogate distribution
q(\mathcal{W},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}})
, whose marginal moments are easier to manipulate than those of
p\left(\mathcal{W},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}|\mathcal{D}_{N}\right)
. In a traditional EP fashion, the factorization of
q(\mathcal{W},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}})=q(\mathcal{W})q(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}})
will mimic the factorization of
p\left(\mathcal{W},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}|\mathcal{D}_{N}\right)
.

Algorithm 1

EP SNN training (single batch)

Given the graph structure illustrated in Fig.
1
, the sequential updates of the approximating distributions
\{q_{i}(\cdot)\}_{i=0}^{1}
can be achieved using a series of forward and backward passes through the factor graph, as summarized in Algo.
1
. More precisely, during the forward pass, from layer
\ell=1
to layer
\ell=L
, the message
q_{0}(\boldsymbol{V}^{(\ell-1)})
is sent to the mixing block to update
q_{1}(\boldsymbol{U}^{(\ell)})
. That message is then sent to the activation block of that layer to update
q_{0}(\boldsymbol{V}^{(\ell)})
. During the backward pass, from layer
\ell=L
to layer
\ell=1
,
q_{0}(\boldsymbol{U}^{(\ell)})
is updated and sent to the mixing block of layer
\ell
. This message is used to update
q_{1}(\boldsymbol{V}^{(\ell-1)})
and
q_{1}(\boldsymbol{W}^{(\ell)})
.

If
p_{0}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)
is in the same family of parametric distributions as
q_{0}(\mathcal{W})
,
then
q_{0}(\mathcal{W})
can be initialised as
q_{0}(\mathcal{W})=p_{0}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)
and does not need to be updated during the forward or backward passes. If
p_{0}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)
is not Gaussian or categorical, then
q_{0}(\mathcal{W})
can updated either during the forward or backward pass. Here we update it in the backward pass if needed. Typical examples where

is not Gaussian arise when promoting sparsity, using for instance Bernoulli-Gaussian, Gaussian mixture or Laplace priors

37
,
38
]
.

It is interesting to note that the backward pass, also used in
[

]
for single samples, is a message passing equivalent of gradient backpropagation. The convergence speed of this process can be adjusted by controlling the amount of damping during EP-based updates (see discussion in Section

). In the next paragraphs, we discuss how messages are updated within mixing and activation blocks of each layer.

IV-B

Updates for mixing blocks

For the mixing block of layer
\ell
, we first introduce the set of variables
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(\ell)}=\{\boldsymbol{U}^{(\ell)},\boldsymbol{V}^{(\ell-1)},\boldsymbol{W}^{(\ell)}\}
. the traditional block-EP update consists of minimizing the following KL divergence

\displaystyle KL\left[g(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(\ell)})q_{0}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(\ell)})||q\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(\ell)}\right)\right].

(18)

where

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\left(\boldsymbol{U}^{(\ell)}-\boldsymbol{W}^{(\ell)}\boldsymbol{V}^{(\ell-1)}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n,i}\delta\left(u_{n,i}-\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell-1)}\right),

where, in this paragraph
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)}
denotes the row
i
of
\boldsymbol{W}^{(\ell)}
, and for sample
n
,
\boldsymbol{s}_{n}^{(\ell)}
is the forward trace of layer
\ell
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell-1)}
the spiking ouput of layer
(\ell-1)
.

The product of delta functions in
g(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(\ell)})
does not allow easy computation of the marginal distributions associated with the tilted distribution
g(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(\ell)})q_{0}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(\ell)})
. However, handling individual delta functions is simpler, e.g. using stochastic EP updates.
It is worth noting that each
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)}
is involved in
N
delta functions (because
N
samples are considered in the factor graph) and each
\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell-1)}
is involved in
V_{\ell}
delta functions, whereas each
u_{n,i}
is involved in a single delta function. In a similar fashion to the vanilla stochastic EP, we thus define local cavity distributions

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}q_{c}(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell)})&\propto q_{0}(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell)})\left[q_{1,old}(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell)})\right]^{\frac{V_{\ell}-1}{V_{\ell}}},\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall n\\
q_{c}(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)})&\propto q_{0}(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)})\left[q_{1,old}(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)})\right]^{\frac{N-1}{N}},\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall i\\
q_{c}(u_{n,i}^{(\ell)})&=q_{0}(u_{n,i}^{(\ell)}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall(n,i)\end{array}\right.

where
q_{1,old}(\cdot)
denotes the previous estimate of
q_{1}(\cdot)
. Defining
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,n}^{(\ell)}=\{u_{n,i},\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)},\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell-1)}\}
, the divergence to be minimized for each delta function becomes

\displaystyle KL\left[\tilde{g}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,n}^{(\ell)})q_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,n}^{(\ell)})||q\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,n}^{(\ell)}\right)\right],

(24)

with
\tilde{g}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,n}^{(\ell)})=\delta\left(u_{n,i}-\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell-1)}\right)
.
The marginal tilted moments of
u_{n,i}^{(\ell)}
can be approximated as in
[
32
]
, by approximating
\eta_{u}=\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell-1)}
as a Gaussian random variable with

\displaystyle q_{c}(\eta_{u})=\mathcal{N}\left(\eta;\mathbb{E}_{q_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,n}^{(\ell)})}\left[\eta_{u}\right],\textrm{Var}_{q_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,n}^{(\ell)})}\left[\eta_{u}\right]\right).

(25)

The marginal moments of
\int\delta(u_{n,i}-\eta_{u})q_{c}(\eta_{u})q_{c}(u_{n,i})\textrm{d}\eta_{u}
can then be obtained in closed-form
[
39
]
and
q_{1}(u_{n,i})
can finally be obtained by moment matching.

The update for the other variables in
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,n}^{(\ell)}
requires an additional approximation. This is explained here for one weight of
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)}
, denoted
w_{i,j}^{(\ell)}
but the same process applies to the other variables. Let
\boldsymbol{w}_{i,\backslash j}^{(\ell)}
contain all the elements of
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)}
but
w_{i,j}^{(\ell)}
. If
v_{n,j}^{(\ell-1)}\neq 0
,
\tilde{g}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,n}^{(\ell)})
can also be expressed
\tilde{g}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,n}^{(\ell)})=\delta(w_{i,j}^{(\ell)}-\eta_{w})
, where

\displaystyle\eta_{w}=\dfrac{u_{i,j}^{(\ell)}-\boldsymbol{w}_{i,\backslash j}^{(\ell)}\boldsymbol{v}_{n,\backslash j}^{(\ell-1)}}{v_{n,j}^{(\ell-1)}}.

(26)

In contrast to
\eta_{u}
, the moments of
\eta_{w}
under
q_{c}
are generally not tractable because the denominator in (
26
) is a random variable (except for
\ell=1
). They could be estimated, e.g. via Monte Carlo simulation, but this would significantly increase the overall computational cost of the algorithm. Instead, we fix
v_{n,j}^{(\ell-1)}
to its mean under
q_{c}
and define

\displaystyle\eta_{w}^{*}=\dfrac{u_{i,j}^{(\ell)}-\boldsymbol{w}_{i,\backslash j}^{(\ell)}\boldsymbol{v}_{n,\backslash j}^{(\ell-1)}}{\mathbb{E}_{q_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,n}^{(\ell)})}\left[v_{n,j}^{(\ell-1)}\right]}.

(27)

The distribution of
\eta_{w}^{*}
is then approximated using

\displaystyle q_{c}(\eta_{w}^{*})=\mathcal{N}\left(\eta;\mathbb{E}_{q_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,n}^{(\ell)})}\left[\eta_{w}^{*}\right],\textrm{Var}_{q_{c}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i,n}^{(\ell)})}\left[\eta_{w}^{*}\right]\right).

(28)

In a similar fashion to
u_{i,n}^{(\ell)}
, the marginal moments of
\int\delta(w_{i,j}^{(\ell)}-\eta_{u})q_{c}(\eta_{u})q_{c}(w_{i,j}^{(\ell)})\textrm{d}\eta_{u}
can be obtained in closed-form and the local approximation
q_{1,n}(w_{i,j}^{(\ell)})
can then be obtained by moment matching.

The local marginal tilted distributions can be computed independently for each delta function factor. It is possible to update
q_{1}
and recompute the cavities
q_{c}
after approximation of each of the
NV_{\ell}
factors (vanilla SEP), or groups of factors (parallel SEP with mini-batches) but to reduce the number of sequential updates, we used average EP, where
q_{1}
is updated after having computed the local approximations of the
NV_{\ell}
factors.
As mentioned previously,
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)}
is involved in
N
delta functions, each leading to, after moment matching, a new approximation
q_{1,n}(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)})
. These approximations are combined to lead to the new approximation

\displaystyle q_{1,new}(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)})\propto\prod_{n=1}^{N}q_{1,n}(\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{(\ell)}).

(29)

Similarly, each
\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell-1)}
is involved in
V_{\ell}
delta functions, each leading, after moment matching, to new approximations
q_{1,j}(\boldsymbol{s}_{n}^{(\ell)})
and
q_{1,j}(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell)})
. These approximations are combined to lead to the new approximation

\displaystyle q_{1,new}(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{j=1}^{V_{\ell}}q_{1,j}(\boldsymbol{v}_{n}^{(\ell)}).

(30)

IV-C

Updates for activation blocks

At the layer
\ell
, the update consists of minimizing the following KL divergence

\displaystyle KL\left[f\left(\boldsymbol{V}^{(\ell)}|\boldsymbol{U}^{(\ell)}\right)q_{0}(\boldsymbol{V}^{(\ell)},\boldsymbol{U}^{(\ell)})||q\left(\boldsymbol{V}^{(\ell)},\boldsymbol{U}^{(\ell)}\right)\right].

(31)

For the activation functions considered in Section
II-A
, the first argument of the KL divergence (called the tilted distribution), as well as
q(\cdot)
, are fully separable. Thus, this problem reduces to solving
NV_{\ell}
independent KL minimization problems with 1D distributions, which reduces to computing the mean and variances of the marginal tilted distributions. For the sigmoid activation function, the moments of the marginal distribution for the membrane potentials have been discussed in
[
40
]
. For the Heaviside activation, the moments can be computed using
[
41
, Chap. 3.1]
. Using the moments of the tilted distributions, one can then compute the marginal moments of
q_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{V}^{(\ell)}\right)
and/or
q_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{U}^{(\ell)}\right)
, potentially under additional constraints and damping (see Section
V-B

V-A

Inference with large datasets

As discussed in Section
IV
, if all the training data can be processed in a single batch, the proposed method consists of a main loop running EP-based message passing forward and backward through the network layers. To handle mini-batches, here we propose to use SEP
[
34
]
and partition the training dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N}
into a set of
B
mini-batches
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{\mathcal{D}_{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
, with
M=N/B
training samples per batch.

Let
q(\mathcal{W})
be the final approximation of the marginal posterior of the network weights. Using the SEP formalism,
q(\mathcal{W})
can be factorized as

q\left(\mathcal{W}\right)\propto\left[q_{1}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)\right]^{B}q_{0}\left(\mathcal{W}\right),

(32)

where
q_{1}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)
represents the average contribution of each batch to the posterior distribution of the weights. The SEP method we use here starts from an initial guess for
q_{1}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)
, which will be updated sequentially using the
B
batches. It is often required to process the
B
batches several times (i.e. runs multiple epochs of SEP), however this does not lead to over-fitting as SEP automatically accounts for the total amount of data while updating
q_{1}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)
.
To process a batch
b
, SEP first creates a cavity distribution

\displaystyle q_{c}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\left[q_{1,old}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)\right]^{B-1}q_{0}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)

(33)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle q\left(\mathcal{W}\right)/q_{1,old}\left(\mathcal{W}\right).

This cavity distribution is then used as a local prior model for
\mathcal{W}
to be trained with
\mathcal{D}_{b}
, essentially replacing
p0\left(\mathcal{W}\right)
by
q_{c}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)
in Section
IV
. This leads to a local approximated posterior distribution denoted
q_{b}(\mathcal{W})
, which will be in the same parametric family of distributions as
q(\mathcal{W})
. In that case,
q_{1,b}(\mathcal{W})\propto q_{b}(\mathcal{W})/q_{c}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)
can be used to update
q_{1}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)
via

\displaystyle q_{1,new}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)=\left[q_{1,old}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)\right]^{1-1/B}\left[q_{1,b}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)\right]^{1/B}.

(34)

The update of
q_{0}(\mathcal{W})
can then be performed after each batch, in a similar fashion to Section
IV
, but using
\left[q_{1,old}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)\right]^{B-1}
as cavity distribution.

V-B

Convergence

While convergence of the proposed method cannot be ensured, a series of tools are available to stabilize Bayesian learning. Optimal settings will be architecture-dependent but we report here a few empirical results to help future practitioners. Careful initialization of approximating factors can help stabilize the algorithm especially during the first epochs or first batches. The shallow architectures considered in this paper were trained from scratch, but for deeper networks, pre-training might help converge to better solutions.

Another important tool typically used in EP-based method is damping, which consists of limiting the changes of approximating distributions between successive updates (using weighted geometric averages)
[
37
]
. In this work, damping is used in the outer loop of SEP in Algo.
3
and also used in the inner loop of AEP in Algo.
2
. The damping parameter used in SEP is set to be
1/B
following
[
34
]
(see (
34
)), and set to 0.7 in AEP (Algo.
2
).

We also observed that regions of the networks which are rarely activated can be particularly prone to variances becoming negative if not constrained. This often occurs within EP and can be mitigated by adding constraints to the KL minimization problems
[
37
]
. We observed that, especially during the early epochs, bounding the variances of Gaussian approximating factors might not be sufficient. Thus we propose to bound the two natural parameters of the Gaussian factors. The derivation of the iterative algorithm is similar to that in
[
37
]
and omitted here due to space constraints, together with the constrained minimization for categorical distributions.

V-C

Message passing at inference time

While we introduced our EP method for training SNNs, The same EP framework can be used at inference time, to predict the marginal means and variances of the network outputs without resorting to Monte Carlo sampling, e.g. without generating multiple random realizations of the weights nor passing data multiple times through the network as in
[
26
]
to obtain ensemble estimates. After training, it is possible to consider a factor graph similar to that in Fig.
1
, where all the approximating factors but those associated with the network weights are set to uninformative messages (uniform distributions for the Bernoulli variables and Gaussians with large variances for the Gaussian factors). The initial prior factors for the weights can then be replaced by the marginal posteriors obtained after training. At inference time, it is sufficient to run a single forward pass.

Experiments

This section evaluates the performance of SNNs trained by our method, referred to as
EPSNN
, and a benchmark method for a classification and a regression task. For quantitative comparisons, we consider as baseline the PyTorch implementation of the method in
[
42

[
26

VI-A

Performance metrics and losses

For consistency purposes, MLE and EPSNN are trained with similar output likelihoods. More precisely, for the classification problem, the binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss is used for MLE, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textrm{BCE}_{i,n}=y_{i,n}\log(\sigma(u_{i,n}))+(1-y_{i,n})\log(1-\sigma(u_{i,n})),

where
u_{i,n}
is the membrane potential of the
i
th
output of the network, for the
n
th sample.

Since EPSNN does not estimate a single set of weights but their marginal distribution, the loss reported is the posterior expected BCE (PeBCE)

\displaystyle\textrm{PeBCE}_{i,n}=\mathbb{E}_{q(u)}\left[\textrm{BCE}_{i,n}\right].

where the expectation is taken with respect to the variational approximation of the distribution of
u_{i,n}
(see Section
V-C
).
We also report the classification accuracy, where the output with the lowest BCE/PeBCE is selected as estimated class.

For the regression problem, we use the mean squared error (MSE)
between the membrane potential of the output layer and the ground truth as loss for the MLE-based method. While we presented spiking likelihoods in (
II-A
), replacing them by a Gaussian likelihood for the output layer is straightforward and this is the option adopted here for regression problems. For EPSNN, we report the MSE
computed using the mean of
q(u_{i,n})
at inference time.

VII
Conclusions and Future Work

We have proposed a unifying framework based on Expectation-Propagation (EP) for training spiking neural networks. In contrast to existing EP-based methods for Bayesian neural network training, we have proposed a combination of stochastic and average EP updates to handle mini-batches. Due to its message-passing interpretation, the proposed framework is modular and flexible, can be used for continuous and discrete weights, as well as deterministic and stochastic spiking networks. We conducted experiments with architectures including one and two hidden layers and the results are in line with those obtained using existing methods. We also observed that the algorithm generally converges rapidly without the need of a large number of passes through the training data.
The neuron models and architectures investigated in this paper need to be generalized in the future, in particular for use with recurrent neurons and to include feedback mechanisms
[
17
,
45
]
and winner-take-all units
[
46
,
47
]
. For computer vision tasks, it would also be interesting to derive update rules for convolutional layers.
Another interesting route to explore is the stability of the training process. Although the global convergence of the method cannot be ensured, damping and batching are generally sufficient for the algorithm to converge. However, the impact of the additional constraints of the local divergence minimization problems and the initialization of the approximating distributions are worthy of deeper analysis.
