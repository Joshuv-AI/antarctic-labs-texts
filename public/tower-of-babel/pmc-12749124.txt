Developing a new prevention model for pediatric respiratory infection
Authors: JianFu Zhou, YiBing Zhang, Ruiling Li, Yue-Cune Chang, Niang-Huei Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPediatric infections are often closely linked to infections in families, schools, and communities, illustrating the importance of developing a holistic model of pediatric respiratory infection prevention. Research purposes were to construct a new preventive model for pediatric respiratory infection prevention and to clarify the relationships among impact factors in this model. Research method was a cross-sectional survey. A structured questionnaire was used to measure model variables, including “parental prevention measures (PPM),” “concern about pediatric vaccination (CPV),” “school precautionary measures (SPM),” and “children’s self-protection practices (CSPP).” Structural equation modeling analysis was performed to test four proposed hypotheses and identify the relationships among these variables. Research participants were 2420 parents with one or more 3-16-year-old children. Results identified five paths in research model. (1) “Parental prevention measures, PPM” directly affects “concerns about pediatric vaccination, CPV” [direct effect: 0.354], “school precautionary measures, SPM” [direct effect: 0.354], and “children’s self-protection practices, CSPP” [direct effect: 0.354]. (2) PPM affects CPV through the mediating effect of SPM (indirect effect: 0.04), resulting in a total effect of 0.394. (3) PPM affects CSPP through the mediating effect of SPM (indirect effect: 0.3), resulting in a total effect of 0.655. All these effects were statistically significant. Results strongly suggested that coordinating prevention strategies between families and schoolteachers is most effective in equipping children with the knowledge and behaviors to avoid infectious disease. Results confirmed that the newly constructed model for preventing pediatric respiratory infection was well fitted as a double mediation model. Further studies are needed to pursue the family-school health education model in the prevention of pediatric infectious disease. Key words: Parental prevention measures; Concern about pediatric vaccination; School precautionary measures; Children’s self-protection practices; Pediatric Respiratory Infection. Introduction

The pediatric population is susceptible to respiratory infectious diseases, such as COVID-19, swine influenza, respiratory syncytial virus, mycoplasma infections, etc. Moreover, respiratory infectious diseases in children significantly and seriously affect their health, subsequently leading to various respiratory complications1,2. In recent years, such infections have caused outbreaks, epidemics, and even pandemics, leading to elevated mortality rates around the world1,2. Thus, it is crucial to strengthen the development of effective prevention and control policies for respiratory infectious disease in children.

Most respiratory infections share similar transmission, and prevention methods.

According to existing epidemiological studies, pediatric respiratory infections are often closely linked to infections in families, schools, and communities, illustrating the importance of developing a holistic model of respiratory infection prevention for children3–6. For young children, experts suggest several strategies, including (1) parental efforts to maintain household hygiene and to guide children’s behaviors to reduce risk factors6–9, (2) school precautionary strategies to reduce risk of transmission7–9, (3) personal self-protection measures taught to young children8–12, and (4) pediatric vaccination to reduce the risk of infections13–16. Parents and schools play an important role in these preventive measures by not only educating children on hygienic behaviors but also inculcating protective habits in their daily lives11–14. However, most previous studies have focused either on investigating the effectiveness of parental prevention measures or on enhancing school precautionary measures3–5,12,14–16, with a lack of research investigating the impacting relationships among parental prevention measures, school precautionary measures, and children’s self-protective practices. Integrating different preventive measures and evaluating relationships among these measures to demonstrate integrated efficacy has become the most important concern in pediatric infectious disease prevention. An integrated prevention model is necessary to enhance cooperation among parents, schools and communities to prevent further outbreaks of pediatric respiratory infectious disease3,5,8,9.

Purposes

Research aimed to identify the relationships among existing pediatric respiratory infection prevention measures (parental prevention measures, school precautionary measures, pediatric vaccination concerns, and children’s self-protection practices) and to construct a new prevention model for pediatric respiratory infectious diseases.

Methods

Research design and participants

Research method was a cross-sectional survey. The inclusion criteria were Chinese parents aged 18 to 50 years who (1) had one or more 3–16-year-old children, (2) were living with their children and, (3) could read Chinese. Research data were collected electronically through structured questionnaires posted on the Sojump platform. The snowball sampling method was used to recruit participants. First, the inclusion criteria and QR code for the online questionnaire were posted in the researchers’ WeChat Moments to recruit participants who met the inclusion criteria. Second, the recruitment information was subsequently posted on the WeChat Moments of currently enrolled participants and recommended to other potential participants who met the inclusion criteria for the study. Since previous studies indicated that different lifestyles, habits, and climate may impact epidemic control, research setting included cities in both the north and south of mainland China. Northern Chinese cities are usually cold and sometimes snowy during the winter, whereas cities in the south are usually significantly warmer. Five provinces around Henan province represented the north and five provinces around Fujian province represented the south, and participants were divided into subgroups by area.

Research model and research hypotheses

Based on existing literature, we proposed a double mediation model of pediatric respiratory infection prevention (Fig. 1) to test the following five hypotheses: (1) parental prevention measures (PPM) would be positively associated with concerns about pediatric vaccination (CPV), (2) parental prevention measures (PPM) would be positively associated with school precautionary measures (SPM), (3) parental prevention measures (PPM) would be positively associated with children’s self-protection practices (CSPP), (4) school precautionary measures (SPM) mediates the effect of parental prevention measures (PPM) on concerns about pediatric vaccination (CPV), and (5) school precautionary measures (SPM) mediates the relationship between parental prevention measures (PPM) and children’s self-protection practices (CSPP).

Reliability and validity

The content validity index (CVI) of the research questionnaire was 0.84 as evaluated by a panel of seven experts including five clinical experts (2 pediatricians and 3 senior nurses) and two pediatric scholars. Internal consistency reliability was estimated with overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient as 0.95, 0.865 for the section on “parental prevention measures,” 0.83 for the section on “school precautionary measures,” 0.912 for the section on “children’s self-protection measures,” and 0.893 for the section on “concerns about pediatric vaccination.”

Ethical considerations

This research was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Putian University before collecting data. The registration number of the Human Research Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Putian University is ECAHPU2023-047. All research methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations issued by the Human Research Ethics Committee. Questionnaires were anonymous and the participants were assured confidentiality. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians before data collection.

Statistical analysis

Research data were analyzed by IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 and IBM SPSS AMOS version 24. Data were described by number and frequency for categorical data, as well as means and standard deviations for continuous variables. The Pearson’s R-squared was used to describe the bivariate correlation of research variables. Structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis was used to explore the possible relationships among research variables. Model fits representing how a SEM performance fits the sample data were assessed by seven indices: the chi-square test, the goodness-of-fit index (GFI), the adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI), the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), the root-mean-squared error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root-mean-square residual (SRMR). We used the guideline proposed by Hooper et al.17 to determine the model fit in SEM. That is, a SEM model was well fitted if: the chi-square test (p> 0.05), SRMR < 0.05, RMSEA < 0.06, GFI > 0.95, IFI > 0.95, CFI > 0.95, NFI > 0.95, and TLI > 0.9517.A p-value less than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Sample size consideration

Although SEM analysis is considered to require a large sample size18,19, many scholars have recently advocated that researchers should consider the acceptable sample size estimates as a range, rather than focusing on a single required minimum value19. –20 According to the research hypothesis, research model includes 11 parameters and 4 observed variables. Based on 20 participants per parameter and 15 participants per study observed variable, the minimum sample size was 280. After considering the factors of research population and model complexity of this study, the acceptable sample size was in the range of 280–2500.

Research results

Demographics of participants

Data were collected from December 2022 to May 2023. A total of 4030 participants completed the online questionnaire. After review, 1610 questionnaires were excluded due to duplications and failure to meet inclusive criteria of sampling, for a final total of 2420 responses (60% response rate), including 1288 fathers (53.2%) and 1132 mothers (46.8%). Of these, 881 (36.4%) were recruited from five southern provinces and 1539 (63.6%) were recruited from five northern provinces in mainland China. Most participants were clearly aware of the importance of wearing masks to prevent infectious agents from entering their body and claimed to be familiar with the techniques of facemask use. However, the majority incorrectly believed that the proper duration of handwashing was 10 s and that “covering the nose and mouth with hands while sneezing or coughing is appropriate” (Question 21).

Regarding COVID-19 vaccination, most participants wished to be informed of the side effects and efficacy of vaccination before considering having their children vaccinated. Additionally, most also proposed that their child be examined prior to receiving the vaccination (Table 1).

In the face of the pandemic, many participants responded that they intended to strengthen their children’s self-protective measures before going to school and to cooperate with schools to protect their children.

Additionally, there were highly significantly positive relationships between “concerns about pediatric vaccination (CPV)” and “parental prevention measures (PPM)” (r = 0.394, P < 0.001), and between “concerns about pediatric vaccination (CPV)” and “school precautionary measures (SPM)” (r = 0.276, P < 0.001). Discussion

Demographics of participants

Research participants were recruited from twenty cities, ten cities each in southern and northern area mainland China. Most were well-educated and self-identified as middle class. Based on these demographics, their responses were sufficiently representative to test the research hypotheses.

It is best to teach children from an early age to develop good hygiene habits and infection-prevention strategies and translate these into daily habits. Parents and schoolteachers play an important role in teaching these knowledge and skills3–6,22. Children over 16 years old have already developed their daily hygiene habits, and from this point they are mostly influenced by peer relationships. In addition, in China, most children over 16 attend boarding schools, and the influence of their families is limited. Therefore, our research sampling focused on parents with children aged 3–16.

Discussion of participants’ responses on questionnaire

Although the COVID-19 epidemic has been brought under control, it is suggested that an urgent need exists to construct and verify the preventive model of pediatric respiratory infection to help families and schools work together to guide children to adopt self-protective practices and receive vaccinations against infectious diseases.

Participants strongly agreed with most preventive measures, such as facemask use, regular handwashing, and maintaining a clean, and well-ventilated home. These preventive measures are similar to those widely promoted in Chinese media. However, most participants mistakenly advocate “covering mouth and nose with our hands when coughing or sneezing,” which can lead to contamination of the individual’s hands with pathogens and to the spread of the pathogens to the environment. Additionally, people often neglect proper handwashing practices, potentially increasing the spread of respiratory infections in the home environment (Table 1)4,22. –23 These results are likely consistent with previous studies3,5.

Discussion of measurement model and five hypotheses validations

Research results confirmed that the newly constructed model for preventing pediatric respiratory infection was well fitted as a double mediation model and demonstrated in detail the relationships among study variables in the model.

In summary, when participants reported better parental prevention measures, it positively affected their concern about the effectiveness of pediatric vaccinations. Additionally, participants’ attitude toward cooperating with their children’s schoolteachers to promote pediatric preventive measures indirectly strengthened the above causal relationship. Stronger parental prevention measures had a directly positive impact on children’s self-protective practices. Parental prevention measures at home, coupled with school precautionary measures, further enhanced the reported adoption of children’s self-protective practices. Results strongly suggested that coordinating prevention strategies between families and schoolteachers is most effective in equipping children with the knowledge and behaviors to avoid infectious disease.

Application of new prevention model

Many countries adopted prevention measures such as school closures and home quarantine during the COVID-19 pandemic10–16. Although these measures may have been effective, they also led to a pandemic immunity gap among children and were only temporary24–26. Based on results, we suggest that the Public Health Department equip schoolteachers with the knowledge and preventive behaviors to prevent and control infectious disease as well as to convey this correct information to students and their families, so that school and families together can provide students a hygienic and safe environment and also cultivate self-protective behaviors and a sense of responsibility to prevent the spread of infectious disease. Further studies should focus on promoting the cooperative model of family-school collaboration from the earliest stages of a pandemic to the periods of home quarantine and prevention in daily life.

Limitations

Our study is subject to some limitations. First, an online survey may induce a selection bias. Additionally, since most research participants came from provinces and cities in Fujian and Henan provinces, this may affect the representativeness of the research results. Second, results may be limited by the questionnaire-based approach, with participant’s responses limited by the structure of the questions. Third, due to the use of snowball sampling in this study, there are referral bias exists and the research results are prone to a lack of diversity. To reduce referral bias and increase the diversity of research results, we used electronic questionnaires to expand the collection of research data from Fujian and Henan provinces to ten surrounding provinces. Nevertheless, the infectious disease prevention behaviors, in this study, are more oriented toward the prevention of COVID-19 which may skew research results. Further studies are needed to enlarge research setting, increase sample size, and add qualitative research data to further explore the attitudes and behaviors of parents in preventing and managing pediatric infectious diseases. Moreover, future research could further expand the concept of infectious disease prevention behavior to explore the impact of prevention of other pediatric infectious diseases on the quality of life of children and their families.

Conclusion

Research results confirmed that the newly constructed model for preventing pediatric respiratory infection was well fitted as a double mediation model. Results strongly suggest that coordinating prevention efforts between families and schoolteachers is most effective in equipping children with the knowledge and behaviors to avoid infectious disease. The Public Health Department should equip schoolteachers with the knowledge of infectious disease preventive behaviors to transmit to students and their families. Further studies are needed to pursue the family-school health education connection in the prevention of pediatric infectious disease.
