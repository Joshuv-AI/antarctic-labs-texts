Immunomodulatory 3D-printed conductive scaffold combined with electrostimulation: a dual-strategy approach to enhance bone regeneration
Authors: Lijuan Wang, Chenxu Wang, Dejian Li, Xiaojun Zhou, Chuanglong He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional conductive scaffolds lack the capacity to dynamically coordinate the immuno-osteogenic coupling that is critical for effective bone repair. In this study, we developed a three-dimensional (3D) printed immunomodulatory conductive scaffold that integrates anti-inflammatory macrophage induction with electrical signal transmission. This scaffold achieves a combination of available electrical conductivity and immunomodulatory function through poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) (PEDOT) doping and controlled ion release. In vitro experiments demonstrated that the scaffold enhanced M2 macrophage polarization by potential regulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway mediated by bioactive ions, even under conditions of electrical stimulation (ES). Furthermore, it synergistically promoted the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) in the presence of ES. In vivo evaluation using a rat cranial defect model revealed that the scaffold combined with ES accelerated bone regeneration via the convergence of immunomodulation and electrical cues, as evidenced by coordinated M2 macrophage infiltration and early angiogenesis. This study provides a promising strategy that integrates conductive network and bioactive ion release to establish an electrostimulation and immunoregulation microenvironment for the treatment of bone defect.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04784-6.Keywords: 3D-printed conductive scaffold, Ion release, Immunomodulation, Electrical stimulation, Bone repair

Introduction

Reconstructing the electrophysiological microenvironment is essential for effective bone repair. As a pivotal strategy in tissue engineering, three-dimensional (3D) printed conductive hydrogel scaffolds facilitate the transmission of electrical signals while simultaneously providing mechanical support and a conducive environment for cellular proliferation. For example, Huang et al. developed a double-layer ionic conductive skin scaffold via 3D bioprinting, employing a novel ionic conductive biomimetic bioink [1]. The zwitterionic 2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine component of the bioink emulates the electrical signaling characteristics of natural skin, enabling the fabrication of skin-like constructs comprising an epidermal layer and a vascularized dermal layer, which maintain high shape fidelity and cell viability. To address the limited neuronal differentiation of transplanted neural stem cells (NSC) in spinal cord injury (SCI) treatment, Zhang et al. utilized 3D bioprinting to produce conductive biomimetic scaffolds exhibiting mechanical and electrical properties analogous to those of native spinal cord tissue [2, 3]. These scaffolds enhance NSC survival, promote neuronal differentiation, and facilitate myelin regeneration, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes in SCI repair. Additionally, Shi et al. engineered a conductive hydrogel bioink incorporating ionic liquid for 3D printing anisotropic cardiac tissue scaffolds that replicate the spiral and circular architectures of native heart tissue [4]. Collectively, these 3D-printed conductive hydrogels represent a versatile platform for advanced biomedical applications. Although 3D printing enables the fabrication of complex hydrogel architectures, the electrical performance of these scaffolds is contingent upon the conductive fillers or polymers integrated within the hydrogel matrix, which govern conductivity, stability, and sensing capabilities.

Among conductive materials such as metals, carbon nanotubes, and conductive polymers, poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene): poly(styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT: PSS) is a widely utilized aqueous conductive polymer dispersion in biomedical applications [5]. Its notable chemical modifiability and processability render it suitable for use in biosensors [6, 7], soft actuators [8, 9], nanogenerators [8, 10] and tissue engineering scaffold [11–13]. The appropriateness of PEDOT: PSS for scaffolds depends on achieving an optimal balance among conductivity, mechanical properties, and biocompatibility [14]. Concentrations below 0.1% (w/v) demonstrate low cytotoxicity and conductivity, making them suitable for tissues with minimal conductivity requirements or as bioactive coatings [13, 15]. The conventional “safe window” of 0.1–0.5% (w/v) enhances intercellular electrical signaling while preserving cell viability, thereby being ideal for the repair of conductive tissues such as nerves, cardiac, and skeletal muscle [16–19]. Higher concentrations (> 0.5% w/v) offer increased conductivity for applications including cardiac patches and nerve catheters [20–23]; however, they also elevate cytotoxicity and alter mechanical properties, necessitating complex modifications or purification processes. Cytotoxicity is influenced not only by concentration but also by factors such as purification methods, substrate characteristics, and target cell types. The trade-off between biocompatibility and conductivity arises primarily from the presence of PSS, which facilitates PEDOT dispersion but generates cytotoxic degradation by-products and diminishes conductivity due to its insulating propertie [24, 25]. Approaches to mitigate these issues include secondary doping with polar solvents [26, 27], in situ polymerization of EDOT in polysaccharide solutions [28], and grafting hydrophilic groups onto EDOT [29, 30]. Nevertheless, these methods often involve harsh conditions that are incompatible with 3D-printed hydrogel scaffolds used in tissue engineering. Consequently, PEDOT-based conductive hydrogels continue to face challenges in simultaneously optimizing mechanical strength, conductivity, and biocompatibility.

The dynamic immune microenvironment has been proved crucial for bone repair, as tissue damage, scaffolds, and electrical stimulation (ES) influence inflammatory responses [31]. Macrophages are key regulators, in which M1 macrophages release pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α to clear pathogens and necrotic tissue but can worsen inflammation if overactivated [32]. Conversely, M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, promoting angiogenesis, osteoblast differentiation, and enhancing ES effects on osteoblasts [33]. Therefore, the synergy between ES and M2 macrophages may accelerate bone repair. However, combining ES delivery with scaffold-mediated immunomodulation remains underexplored, underscoring the urgent need to develop immunomodulatory conductive scaffolds for the treatment of bone defect.

In this study, a composite bioink composed of gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA), poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene): alginate (PEDOT: Alg), and magnesium-doped bioactive glass (BG) was formulated to fabricate an immunomodulatory and conductive scaffold for bone repair. Accordingly, a promising strategy that integrates conductive network, bioactive ion release, and ES was presented for the treatment of bone defect. Particularly, PEDOT: Alg was integrated into the GelMA-based bioink to introduce electrical conductivity for stimulating osteogenesis, while BG was embedded to enable immunomodulatory function via ion release. Following the confirmation of the scaffold’s conductive property, ion release characteristic, and in vitro biocompatibility, the influence of the scaffold on macrophage polarization and bone regeneration was systematically assessed using in vitro and in vivo assays. In summary, this study introduces the conductive scaffold with immunomodulatory and ES properties as a therapeutic platform for bone defect repair through the synergistic effect of efficient electrical signal transmission and bioactive ion release.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

GelMA and lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) were procured from EFL-Tech Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). BG was supplied by Kunshan Chinese Technology New Materials Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). PEDOT: PSS was obtained from HERAEUS (Hanau, Germany). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12), fetal bovine serum (FBS), trypsin, and penicillin-streptomycin were purchased from Gibco Life Technologies (Grand Island, USA). Lipopolysaccharides (LPS), Calcein-AM, the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining solution, Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) Assay Kit, and BCA Protein Assay Kit were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated anti-mouse CD86 and allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated anti-mouse CD206 antibodies were supplied by Elabscience Biotechnology (Wuhan, China).

Preparation of bioink

The PEDOT: Alg solution was prepared in accordance with a previously reported method [34]. Equal volumes of 1.5 wt% PEDOT: PSS and 1.5 wt% Alg solutions were combined and stirred at room temperature for 20 min. The resulting mixture was then cast into a polytetrafluoroethylene mold and immersed in a 2 wt% CaCl2 solution for 10 min to induce hydrogel formation. Subsequently, the hydrogel was thoroughly washed with water to remove displaced PSS chains and the excess of Alg chains that are not involved in cross-linking or combined with PEDOT. The hydrogel was further immersed in a 3 wt% NaCl solution and dialyzed to yield the PEDOT: Alg solution. Separately, GelMA (6% w/v) and LAP (0.5% w/v) were dissolved in deionized water. The PEDOT: Alg solution was incorporated into this mixture to achieve a final concentration of 0.3% (w/v), and Magnesium-doped bioactive glass was dispersed at 0.8% (w/v). After thorough stirring, the bioink was cooled at 4 °C for 10 min. Using a Bio-Architect SR bioprinter (Regenovo Biotechnology, Hangzhou, China) maintained at 20 °C, the bioink was printed into a cylindrical grid structure with filament diameters of 250 μm and spacing of 800 μm. Printing operations were controlled via BioPrinter software, and the scaffolds were crosslinked under visible light at 405 nm. The scaffold designated “Gm” comprised solely GelMA; “GmPa” contained GelMA and PEDOT: Alg; “GmPaBg” included GelMA, PEDOT: Alg, and BG; and “GmPp” consisted of GelMA and PEDOT: PSS.

Physicochemical properties of the scaffold

The chemical composition of the scaffold was characterized using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet-670, Thermo), while scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS, SU8010, Hitachi, Japan) was employed to analyze the scaffold’s morphology and elemental distribution. Moreover, to evaluate the mechanical property of scaffolds, cylindrical hydrogel specimens were fabricated using a custom-designed Teflon mold. All samples were equilibrated in PBS (pH 7.4) at 25 °C for 24 h to achieve complete hydration. Uniaxial compression tests were conducted using a universal testing machine (HY-940FS, Shanghai, China) equipped with a 200 N load cell. Compression was performed at a constant strain rate of 2 mm/min until a maximum strain of 80% was attained.

Rheological property and printability of the bioink

The rheological properties of the prepared bioink were assessed using a rheometer (MCR 302e, Anton Paar, Austria) equipped with parallel plate geometry (25 mm diameter, 1 mm gap). To prevent dehydration, samples were coated with silicone oil immediately after being placed on the plate. Shear viscosity measurements were performed over a shear rate range of 0.1 s− 1 to 100 s− 1. Oscillatory strain sweep tests were conducted at a fixed frequency of 1 Hz, with strain varying from 0.01% to 100%. Oscillatory frequency sweep tests were carried out at a constant strain of 1%, with frequency ranging from 0.1 rad/s to 100 rad/s. The oscillatory temperature sweep test was performed at 1% strain and 1 Hz frequency, with temperature increasing from 7 °C to 38 °C at a rate of 2 °C/min. Additionally, a three-interval thixotropy test (3ITT) was implemented at 1% strain to simulate the 3D printing process: the first interval applied 1 Hz for 120 s to represent the bioink state prior to printing; the second interval applied 10 Hz for 60 s to mimic the printing process; and the third interval applied 1 Hz for 120 s to characterize the bioink state post-printing. Furthermore, a cube with dimensions of 10 mm ×10 mm ×2.5 mm was fabricated to demonstrate the printability of the bioink.

Swelling, degradation, and ion release behavior of the scaffold

The swelling behavior and degradation rate of the scaffold were assessed using a gravimetric method. For the swelling test, dried scaffolds were immersed in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C and incubated on a shaker at 100 rpm. At predetermined time points (1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 12, and 24 h), the scaffolds were removed, surface moisture was gently blotted with filter paper, and the samples were weighed (Ws). The swelling rate (SR) was calculated using the following formula: SR = (Ws-W0)/W0 × 100%, where W0 is the initial dry weight. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate. For the degradation study, dried scaffolds (W0) were immersed in PBS and incubated at 37 °C on a shaker at 100 rpm. At specified intervals (3, 7, 15, 21, 30, 45 and 60 days), scaffolds were removed, rinsed with distilled water, freeze-dried, and weighed (Wt). The degradation rate was calculated as follows: Degradation Rate = (W0-Wt)/W0 × 100%.

To assess the kinetics of ion release, the scaffold was immersed in 10 mL of PBS maintained at 37 °C and pH 7.4, and subsequently placed on a constant-temperature shaker operating at 100 rpm/min. At predetermined time intervals (1, 4, 7, 10, 14, 21 and 30 days), 1 mL aliquots of the release medium were withdrawn and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS pre-equilibrated to the same temperature. The concentrations of Ca, Si, and Mg ions were quantified using inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES, Prodigy-ICP, LEEMAN, USA) to determine the cumulative ion release.

Electrical and electrochemical characterization of scaffolds

The electrical conductivity of hydrogels was systematically evaluated through multiple techniques, including diode circuit testing, four-point probe measurements, cyclic voltammetry (CV), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). A straightforward direct current (DC) circuit was constructed by connecting hydrogel samples in series with a 3 V power supply and a blue light-emitting diode (LED); the continuous illumination of the LED indicated effective charge transport within the hydrogels. Bulk conductivity (σ, S/m) was quantified using a four-point probe system (MCP-T370, Mitsubishi Chemical Analytech Co., Ltd., Japan) with a probe spacing of 1.0 mm. Electrochemical activity was investigated employing a three-electrode configuration comprising an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, a platinum counter electrode, and the hydrogel as the working electrode. CV scans were performed over a potential range of −1 to +1.5 V at scan rates between 5 and 100 mV/s in 0.1 M PBS. Additionally, EIS measurements were conducted over a frequency range from 1 MHz to 0.1 Hz at open circuit potential, applying a 10 mV root mean square (RMS) perturbation.

Cytotoxicity assessment of scaffolds

The cytotoxicity of 3D-printed hydrogel scaffolds was evaluated using the leaching method. Scaffolds were immersed in complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) at a concentration of 20 mg/mL, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h with gentle agitation at 100 rpm/min. Subsequently, the supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter (Millipore). The RAW264.7 macrophages were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were isolated from the femurs and tibias of 2-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats and maintained in DMEM/F12 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. BMSCs and RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and cultured for 24 h under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). The culture medium was then replaced with the scaffold extracts. Cell viability was assessed at 48 h post-treatment using the CCK-8 assay. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured to assess cell viability using a microplate reader (Multiskan GO, Thermo, USA).

BMSCs and RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded onto scaffolds at a density of 1 × 106 cells per scaffold and cultured at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The culture medium was replaced every 48 h. At 1, 3, and 7 days post-seeding, the scaffolds were rinsed twice with pre-warmed PBS, incubated with Calcein-AM solution for 45 min at 37 °C, subsequently rinsed to remove excess dye, and then imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (DMi8, Leica, Germany).

The effect of scaffolds on macrophage polarization

RAW264.7 macrophages and scaffold extracts were utilized to evaluate the immunomodulatory effects of the scaffolds. Cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Consistent with previous studies [35], 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide was administered to induce M0-to-M1 macrophage polarization over an 8-h period, thereby simulating acute inflammation following bone injury. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with scaffold extracts, and the cells were cultured for an additional 3 days. Moreover, to assess the ability of the scaffolds to promote M1-to-M2 polarization under ES, cells exposed to the GmPaBg extract were additionally subjected to ES using a custom-made ES cell culture device (9130B, B&K Precision, America, Fig. S1). A current of 20 µA was applied for 20 min daily until the endpoint analysis. Macrophage phenotypes were characterized using immunofluorescence, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), and flow cytometry, while Western blot analysis was employed to elucidate the mechanisms by which the scaffold promotes M2 polarization.

For immunofluorescence staining, the macrophages treated with scaffold extracts were fixed with paraformaldehyde, permeabilized using Triton X-100, and subsequently blocked with BSA. The cells were incubated overnight with primary antibodies, followed by one-hour incubation. After washing with PBS, protein expression was visualized via confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; Carl Zeiss LSM 700, Germany). For qRT-PCR, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent on days 3. Complementary DNA synthesis was performed utilizing a reverse transcription kit. Quantitative PCR was conducted with SYBR Green Master Mix on a QuantStudio 7 Flex system. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

After incubation with scaffold extracts, the macrophages were harvested into centrifuge tubes and subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Following blocked with goat serum for 30 min, the cells were incubated with flow cytometry antibodies for 1 h. Cell aggregates were then passed through a mesh filter, and macrophage phenotypes were analyzed using flow cytometry.

Whole-cell protein lysates for Western blot analysis were prepared using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies (phosphorylated p65 (p-p65), phosphorylated IκBα (p-IκBα), Arg-1, IL-10, and β-actin). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence and quantified by densitometric analysis with ImageJ software.

Effect of macrophage-derived paracrine factors on angiogenesis

To examine the influence of various scaffolds on macrophage-mediated angiogenesis, we evaluated the effects of conditioned media derived from macrophages co-cultured with scaffolds on HUVECs angiogenic activity, employing scratch and Matrigel tube formation assays. Conditioned medium was prepared following macrophage culture and induction of the M1 phenotype (see Sect. "The effect of scaffolds on macrophage polarization"). HUVECs were purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China) and maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. After three days of stimulation with scaffold extracts and electrical current, the supernatant was collected. The conditioned media were designated as CMControl, CMGm, CMGmPa, CMGmPaBg, and CMGmPaBg@ES. HUVECs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured until reaching 90–100% confluence. A standardized scratch wound was created using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip, followed by the removal of detached cells through washing with PBS. Subsequently, cells were subjected to various treatments. Wound closure was assessed at 24 h using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (DMi8, Leica, Germany), and the migration distance was quantified utilizing ImageJ software.

For the tube formation assay, 48-well plates were coated with 100 µL of Matrigel and allowed to polymerize at 37 °C for 30 min. HUVECs were seeded onto the Matrigel at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in various extract conditions. After an incubation period of 8 h, capillary-like structures were imaged and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ’s Angiogenesis Analyzer software, assessing the number of junctions, master segments and meshes.

In vitro evaluation of osteogenesis

BMSCs were isolated from Sprague-Dawley rats (2 weeks old) and cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells at passage 3–5 were used for experiments. All groups were induced with osteogenic medium (OM: DMEM/F12 medium +10% FBS, 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 100 nM dexamethasone) containing scaffold extracts. And cells in GmPaBg@ES group were subjected to a direct electrical stimulus with the current of 20 µA for 20 min daily until endpoint analysis.

Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min following 7 and 14 days of culture, then stained using the BCIP/NBT ALP kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For ALP quantification, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer, and ALP activity was measured using the Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit. Absorbance at 405 nm was normalized to total protein content, which was determined by the BCA assay. Data are presented as micromoles of p-nitrophenol (pNP) per milligram of protein (µmol/mg protein). After 21 days, mineralized nodules were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 2% ARS (pH 4.2) for 30 min. PCR assays were conducted following the procedures described in Sect. "The effect of scaffolds on macrophage polarization", targeting Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), ALP, osteopontin (OPN), Osteocalcin (OCN), and Collagen type I alpha 1 (Col1α), with GAPDH serving as the housekeeping gene. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Calcium ion influx and Western blot assays were performed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. For the calcium influx assay, cells were incubated with Fluo-4 AM for 30 min at 37 °C following the removal of the culture medium, and images were acquired using a Leica DMi8 live-cell imaging system. Western blot analysis, as described in Sect. "The effect of scaffolds on macrophage polarization", was conducted to detect p-CaMKII, p-CREB, Runx2, and OPN.

In vivo evaluation of bone regeneration

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Donghua University (Approval No.: DHUEC-NSFC-2022-27) and conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines. Thirty healthy 8-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 6 per group), including Control, Gm, GmPa, GmPaBg, and GmPaBg@ES.

Rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium at a dosage of 40 mg/kg. A 5 mm full-thickness bone defect was created centrally in each parietal bone using a trephine bur under continuous saline irrigation. Materials were implanted according to the designated group assignments. The periosteum and skin were subsequently closed with absorbable sutures. Postoperative analgesia and antibiotic treatments were administered in accordance with established protocols. In the GmPaBg@ES group, ES (20 µA/5 min, three times per week) commenced one week after surgery and continued for one month. Animals were euthanized at 6 and 12 weeks post-operation. Prior to euthanasia at 12 weeks, blood samples were collected from the abdominal aorta for analysis. Following euthanasia, cranial defect sites, adjacent bone tissue, and major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidney, were harvested for further examination.

Cranial samples were harvested and scanned using a high-resolution micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) system (Quantum GX, PerkinElmer, USA). to generate 3D reconstructions and to quantify the bone volume to total volume ratio (BV/TV) within the defect region utilizing the system’s proprietary software. Subsequently, the samples were fixed, decalcified, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Masson’s Trichrome, as well as subjected to immunofluorescence labeling for CD206 (a marker of M2 macrophages), Alpha-Smooth Muscle Actin (α-SMA, a marker of vascular smooth muscle cells), and OCN (an osteoblast marker). Cell nucleus counterstaining was performed using DAPI.

Whole blood samples collected at 12 weeks were analyzed using an automated hematology analyzer (BC-30Vet, Mindray, China) to determine complete blood count (CBC) parameters. Serum was separated by centrifugation, and biochemical parameters including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (Urea), and creatinine (Crea), were measured using an automated biochemistry analyzer (Chemray 240, Rayto Life and Analytical Sciences Co., Ltd., China).

The heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, stained with H&E staining, and examined using a light microscope to evaluate systemic toxicity and pathological alterations.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using Origin software. Differences among groups were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Statistical significance was considered at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Given the critical role of ES and M2-type macrophages in bone repair, a dual-functional scaffold composed of GelMA, PEDOT: Alg, and BG was formulated via 3D-bioprinting to combine ES delivery with scaffold-mediated immunomodulation. As illustrated in Fig. 1, PEDOT: Alg enhance the connectivity of the conductive network, where the flexible polymer chains of Alg helps to reduce PEDOT aggregation. The ES transmitted by the scaffold promotes osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs through a possible molecular correlate associated with the calcium ion signaling pathway. Meanwhile, the ions released by the scaffold enhance M2 polarization of RAW264.7 cells by regulating the NF-κB signaling pathway. Additionally, M2 macrophages boost angiogenesis by secreting vascular-associated factors, which, combined with electrically stimulated osteogenesis, drives effective bone regeneration.

Physicochemical properties of bioink

The successful synthesis of PEDOT: Alg was initially verified using FTIR spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 2A, the infrared spectrum of PEDOT: Alg exhibits characteristic absorption bands at 1610 cm− 1 and 1415 cm− 1, corresponding to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the carboxylate group (-COO−) in Alg. Additionally, distinct peaks at 1520 cm− 1 and 1350 cm− 1 are attributed to the C = C stretching vibrations of the thiophene ring and the C-C inter-ring vibrations in PEDOT, respectively. As expected, higher concentrations of PEDOT: Alg improved the conductivity of hydrogels without compromising its biocompatibility (Fig. S2), the following hydrogel scaffold was prepared using the bioink containing 0.3% (w/v) PEDOT: Alg. The infrared spectrum of the scaffold components is depicted in Fig. 2B. Notably, absorption peaks at 1650 cm− 1 (amide I, C = O stretching vibration) and 1550 cm− 1 (amide II, N-H bending) confirm the presence of collagen derivatives. Furthermore, a broad peak at 1080 cm− 1 is assigned to the asymmetric stretching vibrations of Si-O-Si, while the peak around 470 cm− 1 corresponds to the bending vibration of Si-O, indicating the preservation of the bioactive glass network structure. Magnesium doping did not induce significant shifts in the characteristic peak positions within the siloxane framework but led to variations in peak intensities.

Mechanical strength constitutes a critical property of scaffolds employed in bone tissue engineering. As demonstrated by the stress-strain curve (Fig. 2C), the incorporation of PEDOT enhances the toughness of the material, while bioactive glass particles function as a reinforcing phase that effectively distributes stress and reduces the occurrence of brittle fracture. The compression modulus of the GmPaBg scaffold attains a value of 115.34 kPa (Fig. S3). Furthermore, the scaffold’s internal porous architecture facilitates cell infiltration and nutrient transport. As shown in Fig. 2D, E, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis reveals a highly interconnected porous network within the GmPaBg scaffold, while energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis confirms a homogeneous distribution of calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), and silicon (Si) elements throughout the scaffold’s cross-sectional surface, indicating BG particles are uniformly embedded within the polymer matrix.

The swelling and degradation behaviors of tissue engineering scaffolds are critical determinants of their biocompatibility and the effectiveness of bone repair in vivo. As depicted in Fig. 2F, the GmPaBg scaffold exhibited a swelling rate of 643.63% after 24 h of immersion in PBS. This pronounced swelling is attributed to the hydrophilic properties and porous structure of GelMA and Alg, which facilitate nutrient diffusion without imposing compressive stress on adjacent tissues. Degradation assessments revealed that, following two months of incubation in PBS at 37 °C, the GmPaBg scaffold experienced approximately 65% mass loss, whereas the GelMA scaffold underwent complete degradation, as illustrated in Fig. 2G. These results indicate that the incorporation of bioactive glass effectively slows the degradation rate, thereby enhancing the scaffold’s structural integrity. Moreover, BG endows the scaffold with ion release capabilities. ICP-AES analyses demonstrated a sustained release of Ca, Mg, and Si ions from the GmPaBg scaffold (Figs. 2H, I), with ES further promoting ion release. This ion release is advantageous in attenuating inflammatory responses and facilitating the polarization of macrophages from pro-inflammatory to anti-inflammatory phenotypes [36–41].

Printability of the bioink and conductivity of the scaffold

Rheological analyses were conducted to assess the printability of bioinks. Initially, the sol-gel transition behavior of the bioink was investigated through temperature scanning. As depicted in Fig. 3 A, the storage modulus (G’) is significantly lower than the loss modulus (G”) within the temperature range of 30 °C to 40 °C, indicating that the system remains in a sol state characterized by typical viscous fluid behavior. At this stage, GelMA molecular chains exhibit high mobility with weak intermolecular interactions, which are insufficient to form permanent network structures. Upon decreasing the temperature, both G’ and G” increase; however, G’ rises at a substantially faster rate than G”. When the temperature approaches approximately 25 °C, G’ equals G”, signifying the transition from a viscosity-dominated sol state to an elasticity-dominated gel state. This transition is primarily attributed to the temperature-induced enhancement of hydrophobic interactions among GelMA molecular chains [42, 43]. When the temperature falls below 20 °C, the G’ consistently exceeds the G”, indicating the establishment of a stable elastic gel network. Collectively, these findings confirm the temperature sensitivity of GelMA bioink, wherein the high-temperature sol state is conducive to cell mixing and printing operations, while the low-temperature gelation enables rapid network formation post-extrusion, rendering it suitable for temperature-controlled printing environments.

Viscosity is a crucial parameter influencing the smoothness of the printing process. In this study, following confirming the linear viscoelastic region (Fig. S4), the shear viscosity properties of bioinks were evaluated through steady-state shear scanning. As depicted in Fig. 3B, the viscosity of the bioinks is strongly dependent on the applied shear rate. At low shear rates, which typically correspond to ink storage in cartridges or slow movement, the viscosity is elevated, indicating the presence of a stable network structure within the bioink, such as physical entanglements and hydrogen bonding. This stability is essential to prevent sagging or leakage of the bioink prior to printing. Conversely, as the shear rate increases, simulating high-shear flow conditions within the printing needle, the viscosity decreases markedly, thereby substantially reducing the extrusion force required. This reduction facilitates the smooth flow of the ink through micro-needles, minimizing potential shear-induced damage to encapsulated cells and decreasing the risk of clogging. The pronounced shear-thinning behavior observed constitutes a significant advantage of this bioink for extrusion-based bioprinting applications.

The 3ITT thixotropic test was performed to evaluate the changes in the modulus of the bioink during the recovery phase as shear rates decreased from high to low values. As shown in Fig. 3 C, the storage modulus of the bioink rapidly returned to levels approximating the initial state following the cessation of shear forces, indicating that the bioink’s structure maintains thixotropic properties after shear removal. This rapid and adequate structural recovery is critical for preserving the shape of extruded filaments and facilitating precise layer-by-layer stacking. Moreover, the elevated G’ values observed in the low-frequency range (Fig. 3D) confirm the bioink’s sufficient structural strength, thereby ensuring the stability of the final construct.

The results presented above indicate that the developed bioink possesses optimal rheological properties critical for high-performance biofabrication. Its pronounced temperature sensitivity enhances cell loading and printing procedures, while significant shear-thinning behavior promotes efficient extrusion. Additionally, the bioink exhibits excellent thixotropic characteristics, which contribute to improved shape fidelity and print resolution post-extrusion. Together, these features establish a strong basis for the application of this bioink in high-precision 3D bioprinting of complex structures.

Figure 3E depicts a porous scaffold fabricated using extrusion-based 3D printing. The scaffold exhibits significant structural integrity, evidenced by uniformly sized filaments and tightly interwoven layers, with no observable collapse or fractures. The square apertures within the mesh maintain a consistent geometric morphology, indicating that the bioink demonstrates excellent shape fidelity. This finding further supports the stability of the bioink’s rheological properties under shear stress.

The conductive properties of the scaffold were assessed through multiple analytical approaches. As depicted in Fig. 3 F, incorporation of the scaffold into a closed circuit with an LED resulted in successful illumination of the diode, providing direct evidence of the continuity and integrity of the internal conductive pathways. This finding indicates that PEDOT: Alg effectively prevents circuit disruption caused by heterogeneous material dispersion, thereby enabling the formation of a continuous 3D conductive network throughout the scaffold. Figure 3G illustrates that the CV curve area for samples containing the PEDOT group (GmPa, GmPp, GmPaBg) is significantly larger than that of the Gm group, suggesting that PEDOT plays a critical role in enhancing the conductivity of the hydrogels. Moreover, four-probe measurements revealed that GmPa exhibited superior conductivity compared to GmPp, although it was inferior to GmPaBg (Fig. 3H). This difference is attributed to the 3D network structure of alginate, which facilitates more uniform dispersion of PEDOT relative to the linear structure of PSS, thereby reducing insulating regions [34]. Furthermore, the bioactive glass embeeded in GmPaBg may serves as an ion source to improve ionic conductivity. Through optimization of conductive network structures and ionic environments, these materials achieved a stepwise enhancement in conductivity. EIS curves further corroborated these findings, as evidenced by progressively larger high-frequency X-axis intercepts for GmPaBg, GmPa, GmPp, and Gm (Fig. 3I).

Good biocompatibility is a fundamental requirement for the application of tissue engineering scaffolds. Initially, the cytotoxicity of various scaffold extracts was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. As illustrated in Fig. 4A, cell viability remained above 90% across all groups, with the GmPa and GmPaBg groups showing higher levels than the control group. These results suggest that all the scaffold groups exhibit favorable cytocompatibility.

Subsequently, cell proliferation on the scaffold was assessed using Calcein-AM staining. As illustrated in Fig. 4D, E, cells in the Gm group were sparsely distributed, predominantly localized at the scaffold edges, exhibiting a slightly contracted morphology. In contrast, cells in the GmPa group displayed a more uniform extension and increased density, consistent with previous finding [44], which demonstrated that PEDOT enhances cell adhesion. Cells in the GmPaBg group were fully spread, forming a continuous layered structure with pronounced pseudopodial extensions, and exhibited significantly greater scaffold coverage compared to the other two groups. Moreover, quantitative analysis via the CCK-8 assay corroborated the proliferation patterns observed with Calcein-AM staining. As shown in Fig. 4B, C, cell numbers, indicated by absorbance at 450 nm, increased over time, with all material-treated groups surpassing the control at each time point. Collectively, these findings indicate that the GmPaBg scaffold enhances cell adhesion and proliferation through the release of bioactive ions, highlighting its potential for bone tissue engineering applications.

The effect of the scaffold on macrophage polarization

Tissue injury or scaffold implantation typically elicits immune responses. The functional polarization of macrophages, predominantly characterized by the classical pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype and the alternative anti-inflammatory (M2) repair phenotype, constitutes a critical factor influencing the success of bone regeneration. This study investigated the effects of scaffolds on macrophage polarization using immunofluorescence staining, flow cytometry, qRT-PCR, and western blotting, with the objective of elucidating the immunomodulatory properties of the scaffolds and providing mechanistic insights into their enhanced osteoinductive capabilities.

As illustrated in Fig. 5A, cells in the control group exhibited high expression of the M1 marker iNOS (green) and low expression of the M2 marker arginase-1 (Arg-1, red), indicative of a typical pro-inflammatory phenotype. In contrast, cells treated with extracts from the Gm and GmPa scaffolds demonstrated reduced iNOS signaling alongside a modest increase in Arg-1 levels, suggesting partial inhibition of M1 polarization with limited M2 phenotype induction. Notably, cells exposed to extracts from the GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES scaffolds, showed markedly suppressed iNOS expression and substantially elevated Arg-1 levels, consistent with a pronounced M2 phenotype. Furthermore, the fluorescence area of Arg-1 in the GmPaBg@ES and GmPaBg groups was larger than that in the GmPa group, while no significant difference was detected between the GmPaBg@ES and GmPaBg groups. Conversely, the expression pattern of iNOS showed an opposite tendency (Fig. S5). Collectively, these results suggest that the incorporation of ES did not compromise the immunoregulatory function of the scaffold. Subsequently, flow cytometry was employed to quantitatively assess the ratio of macrophages exhibiting M1 versus M2 phenotypes. As shown in Fig. 5B, the percentage of CD206-positive cells in the GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES groups was substantially higher than that in the other groups, reaching 43.9% and 50.1%, respectively. Additionally, histogram analysis and the quantification of mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) showed that the expression level of CD86 in GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES groups was significantly lower than that in other groups while CD206 in these groups was markedly elevated (Fig. 5C-E). These findings underscore the robust immunomodulatory capacity of the GmPaBg scaffold, which is maintained even under external ES.

Consistent with the findings from immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry, the mRNA expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (CD86, iNOS, IL-6) in the GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES groups were significantly lower than those observed in the other groups (Fig. 6A). Conversely, the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines (Arg-1, CD206, IL-10) was markedly upregulated in these groups (Fig. 6B), thereby corroborating the beneficial role of the electroactive property in immune regulation. To elucidate the underlying mechanism, Western blot analysis was conducted. As illustrated in Fig. 6C-G, the control group exhibited elevated levels of p-p65 and p-IκBα, indicative of the correlation with the NF-κB pathway. Although p-p65 expression was partially reduced in the Gm and GmPa groups, no significant differences were detected between these scaffold groups and the control. In contrast, treatment with GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES resulted in a significant reduction in p-IκBα and p-p65 protein levels, suggesting suppression of NF-κB nuclear translocation via blockade of IκBα phosphorylation. Notably, the inhibitory effect on p-p65 in the GmPaBg@ES group was comparable to that in the GmPaBg group, indicating that ES did not have obvious influence on the NF-κB pathway. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the GmPaBg scaffold effectively induces macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby establishing a favorable immune microenvironment conducive to efficient subsequent bone repair.

The influence of scaffold materials on the capacity of macrophages to facilitate angiogenesis

Furthermore, to evaluate the impact of sequential polarization of macrophages from M1 to M2 phenotypes on angiogenesis, the mRNA expression levels of PDGF-BB and SDF-1α in macrophages were measured following stimulation (Fig. 6H, I). Consistent with expectations, the results demonstrated that both GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES enhanced the secretion of PDGF-BB and SDF-1α, which are critical factors in angiogenesis during bone regeneration.

The migration of HUVECs induced by macrophage-conditioned medium under various conditions revealed that the GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES groups exhibited significantly enhanced wound healing rates compared to the other groups, achieving 45.80% and 53.34%, respectively, after 24 h (Fig. 7A, C). Consistent with the results of the scratch assay, HUVECs in both the GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES groups formed continuous, dense network structures on Matrigel, demonstrating significantly greater number of junctions, master segments and meshes than those observed in the other groups (Fig. 7B, D-F). In contrast, the lumen structures of HUVECs in the remaining three groups were fragmented and exhibited sparse networking. The expression levels of angiogenesis-related genes exhibited the same trend (Fig. 7G, H). These findings suggest that the ion release functionality of the scaffold facilitates the induction of 3D vascular network formation by polarizing macrophages toward the angiogenic M2 phenotype, a process that is further enhanced under ES.

Evaluation of in vitro osteogenesis

The osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs represents a critical mechanism in bone repair. After establishing the scaffold’s beneficial immunomodulatory effects, its direct influence on osteogenesis was systematically evaluated through ALP activity assays, ARS staining, and the analysis of the expression levels of key osteogenic genes, including Runx2, Col1α, OPN, and OCN.

ALP expression, a marker of early osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs, was assessed both qualitatively and quantitatively. As illustrated in Fig. 8A, the GmPaBg group exhibited markedly intensified staining relative to groups lacking bioactive glass. The BMSCs in the GmPaBg@ES group demonstrated the highest ALP activity, with staining intensity and coverage surpassing all other groups, including the GmPaBg group without ES. Quantitative analysis corroborated these observations, revealing significantly elevated ALP concentrations in the GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES groups compared to other experimental conditions (Fig. 8C). Furthermore, mineralized nodule formation, indicative of late-stage osteogenic differentiation, was evaluated via alizarin red staining. As shown in Fig. 8B, the alizarin red-positive area increased progressively from day 14 to day 21 across all groups. Notably, the control group consistently exhibited sparse orange-red complexes, whereas the scaffold groups displayed dense clusters or lamellar deposits. On day 21, the GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES groups presented extensive brick-red lamellar regions covering nearly the entire microscopic field, with the GmPaBg@ES group showing stacked dark red lamellae. Subsequent gene expression analysis of osteoblast-related markers (Runx2, OCN, OPN, Col1α) further confirmed the robust osteogenic differentiation induced by GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES. As depicted in Figs. 8D-G, expression levels of all examined genes were significantly upregulated in these groups relative to controls.

(C) Quantitative measurement of ALP activity. (D-G) qRT-PCR analysis of the expression of osteogenic marker genes: Runx2 (D), Col1α (E), OPN (F) and OCN (G). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001

To elucidate the underlying mechanism, Fluo-4 AM staining assay and Western blotting were performed. As illustrated in Fig. 9 A and Fig. S6), treatment with GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES resulted in a significantly elevated intracellular calcium ion concentration compared to other groups, as evidenced by markedly increased fluorescence intensity relative to the control group. Correspondingly, the expression levels of downstream proteins p-CaMKII, p-CREB, Runx2, and OPN exhibited similar upregulation trends (Fig. 9B-F). These findings suggest that GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES facilitate the upregulation of protein expression associated with the Ca2+/CaMKII/CREB signaling pathway, thereby promoting osteoblast differentiation.

In summary, our in vitro results provide robust evidence that the immunomodulatory 3D-printed scaffold markedly enhances the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. The observed increases in ALP activity, accelerated matrix mineralization, and elevated expression of osteogenic-specific markers collectively substantiate its potent pro-osteogenic properties. Importantly, we propose that this effect arises not solely from the scaffold’s osteoconductive characteristics but also from the immunomodulatory microenvironment it establishes. By fostering an anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative milieu, the scaffold creates a favorable niche that directs BMSCs toward osteogenic commitment. These findings strongly support our central hypothesis and highlight the superior potential of immunomodulatory scaffold designs compared to traditional inert materials in bone tissue engineering.

Evaluation of bone repair in vivo

Following the confirmation of the scaffold’s immunomodulatory and osteoinductive properties in vitro, the performance of various scaffold materials, particularly GmPaBg, along with their synergistic effects with ES in vivo, was evaluated using a Sprague-Dawley rat skull defect model. As shown in Fig. S7, S8, no significant differences were observed between the control group and GmPaBg@ES group in either blood tests or H&E staining of visceral tissues, demonstrating the biocompatibility of the scaffold combined with ES. Figure 10 comprehensively presents the results of micro-CT scans, histomorphometric measurements, histological assessments, and immunofluorescence analyses conducted at 12 weeks postoperatively.

Evaluation of cranial bone defect repair and associated osteo-immunomodulatory effects in vivo. (A) Representative 3D reconstructed CT images of the cranial bone defects at the designated time point. (B) Quantitative analysis of BV/TV from the CT data. (C) Histological analysis of decalcified cranial bone sections stained with H&E and Masson’s trichrome. (D) Representative immunofluorescence staining of cranial bone sections for CD206, α-SMA, and OCN. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (blue). (E-G) Quantitative analysis of the immunofluorescence staining shown in (D), including the percentage of CD206+ area (E), the percentage of α-SMA+ area (F), and the percentage of OCN+ area (G). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001

Micro-CT 3D reconstructions of rat cranial defects (Fig. 10A, B) enabled direct visualization of new bone formation. The Control group exhibited minimal and discontinuous callus formation within the defect area, with BV/TV values significantly lower than those observed in all experimental groups. Both the Gm and GmPa groups demonstrated limited new bone formation capacity, with BV/TV values only marginally higher than the Control group, indicating their restricted osteoinductive potential. In contrast, the GmPaBg group showed markedly enhanced new bone formation, as evidenced by significantly higher BV/TV values compared to Gm and GmPa. The most notable outcome was observed in the GmPaBg@ES group, wherein the bone defect was nearly completely repaired. This group exhibited new bone tissue surpassing all others in both volume and density, achieving the highest BV/TV values and demonstrating statistically significant improvements relative to the GmPaBg group alone. Collectively, these findings indicate that GmPaBg possesses inherently strong osteoinductive properties, which are further potentiated by exogenous ES, suggesting a significant synergistic interaction between these factors.

Histological analysis using H&E staining and Masson’s Trichrome staining provided detailed insights into the tissue architecture and composition within the defect site. As illustrated in Fig. 10C, the defect area in the control group was predominantly occupied by fibrous tissue, whereas the GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES groups exhibited a more organized extracellular matrix characterized by a lamellar pattern at the periphery. Importantly, the interface between the newly formed bone and the host bone demonstrated seamless integration with minimal gaps, indicating ongoing remodeling toward functional cortical bone. Masson’s Trichrome staining further substantiated this maturation process by revealing abundant mature collagen deposition in these two groups. Notably, the GmPaBg@ES group displayed the most mature and well-integrated bone matrix, reflecting advanced bone remodeling. In contrast, a substantial portion of the defect in the other groups remained occupied by connective tissue, with only isolated regions of immature bone formation.

The process of bone regeneration is intricately linked to immune responses, particularly the phenotypic polarization of macrophages. To elucidate the immune mechanisms underlying the observed osteogenic differences, we assessed the immune microenvironment within the defect site through immunofluorescence staining for CD206, a marker indicative of M2-type macrophages (Fig. 10D, E). The results demonstrated weak CD206-positive signals in the Control, Gm, and GmPa groups, suggesting limited infiltration of M2 macrophages in these microenvironments. In contrast, the GmPaBg group exhibited the most intense CD206 fluorescence signal, with a significantly greater positive area relative to the other groups. Notably, this pronounced M2 polarization persisted even under exogenous ES (GmPaBg@ES). The CD206-positive area in the GmPaBg@ES group did not differ significantly from that of the GmPaBg group but remained substantially higher than in the control groups. This critical finding robustly confirms that GmPaBg exerts potent and dominant immunoregulatory effects, effectively directing host macrophages toward M2 phenotype polarization, which facilitates tissue repair. Furthermore, this immunomodulatory effect is neither diminished nor inhibited by concurrent ES.

To elucidate the downstream effects of this immunomodulation, we directly evaluated vascularization and osteogenesis. Immunofluorescence staining for α-SMA (Fig. 10D, F), a marker indicative of vascular smooth muscle cells, was employed to identify mature blood vessels. The density of α-SMA-positive vessels was significantly greater in the GmPaBg group compared to the other material groups and the control. The most pronounced pro-angiogenic effect was observed in the GmPaBg@ES group, indicating a potent synergistic interaction between the M2-favorable microenvironment established by GmPaBg and the well-documented pro-angiogenic properties of ES [45]. Concurrently, staining for Osteocalcin (Fig. 10D, G), a specific marker of mature osteoblasts, demonstrated a similar pattern. New bone formation, as indicated by a strong OCN signal, was most prominent in the GmPaBg and GmPaBg@ES groups, with the latter exhibiting the highest osteogenic activity. In conclusion, the data presented in Fig. 10 collectively supported that GmPaBg functions as a potent immunomodulatory scaffold which promotes M2 macrophage polarization. Moreover, GmPaBg serves as an optimal platform for integration with ES, leading to the enhancement of both angiogenesis and osteogenesis. The remarkable bone regeneration induced by GmPaBg, particularly when combined with ES, is not merely attributable to passive osteoconduction but is actively mediated through immunomodulatory mechanism.

As reported previously, ES has shown potential in promoting bone regeneration [46, 47]. Highly conductive material is one of the prerequisites for external ES to work. Besides, some studies have demonstrated the critical role of immunomodulation in bone repair process [48, 49]. Therefore, constructing bioactive scaffolds to modulate the immune microenvironment in conjunction with ES to enhance osteogenesis will significantly promote bone defect repair. In the design of GmPaBg scaffold, PEDOT: Alg was employed to improve electrical signal transmission for achieving enhanced conductivity. The LED circuit assay confirmed that the PEDOT: Alg composite scaffold exhibited superior conductivity. ES from an external power source was applied and demonstrated to significantly promote osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Additionally, the incorporation of BG enabled sustained release of Si and Mg ions from the scaffold, thereby stimulating macrophage polarization toward M2 phenotype and facilitating vascularization, thus creating a favorable microenvironment for bone regeneration. Finally, in a rat skull defect model, this conductive scaffold combining ES and immunomodulation demonstrated the efficacy in promoting bone defect repair, providing an effective strategy for the development of bone repair scaffolds. Although stable ES can be accessible through external power sources, practical applications still suffer from limited convenience. Future studies could focus on developing self-powered bone repair scaffolds, such as those based on the principle of electrochemical reactions.

Conclusion

In summary, this study proposed to construct a bioactive scaffold that integrated ES and immunomodulatory functions by 3D printing for promoting bone repair. To improve both conductivity and biocompatibility, a PEDOT: Alg composite was prepared by substituting PSS with alginate, which contributed to the dispersion of PEDOT within hydrogels. This scaffold was demonstrated to possess excellent biocompatibility and conductivity, while effectively induced macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype through Mg and Si ions release, even under ES. Furthermore, the M2 macrophages facilitated by the scaffold synergized with ES to markedly promote pro-angiogenic activity and osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Moreover, in vivo results suggested that this dual-strategy approach strongly enhanced vascularized bone regeneration, resulting in better ourtcome of bone defect repair. The GmPaBg scaffold, distinguished by its superior printability, conductivity, and bioactivity, offers an optimal platform to create a favorable regenerative microenvironment under ES treatment for bone regeneration through a synergistic effect of electrical stimulation and bioactive ions. Overall, this study establishes a therapeutic strategy based on combined actions of electrostimulation and immunoregulation for bone repair.
