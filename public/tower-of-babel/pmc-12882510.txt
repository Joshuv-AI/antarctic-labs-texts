Diagnosis of EBV and HCMV infections in pediatric heart transplant recipients
Authors: Fatemeh Gabeleh, Mohammad Mahdavi, Mohammad Hadi Karbalaie Niya, Mehrdad Ravanshad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundViral infections, including human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) and Epstein–Barr Virus (EBV), are significant agents that can cause serious complications and death in heart transplant recipients. This study aims to detect CMV and EBV infections in pediatric heart transplant recipients.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study involving 28 hospitalized children who were candidates for heart transplantation. Plasma samples were collected at three time points: during surgery, one month after transplantation, and two months after transplantation. Urine samples were collected two months post-transplant. Heart tissue specimens from donors were taken from the aorta wall, pulmonary artery, and left atrium. Molecular tests were performed to detect EBV and CMV infections and viral loads. The patients’ clinical information was recorded and analyzed.ResultsAll 28 patients were CMV seropositive (100%), and 64.28% had EBV-IgG. CMV infection was detected in 10.7%, 7.1%, and 7.1% of plasma samples at the three time points, respectively, and in 7.1% of urine samples two months post-transplant. EBV infection in plasma was more frequent than CMV, with detection rates of 25%, 22.2%, and 25% at the three time points, respectively. Two recipients (7.1%) received CMV-infected heart tissue, and four patients (14.3%) received EBV-infected heart tissue.ConclusionThe immunosuppressive state in solid organ transplantation can trigger the reactivation and re-infection of CMV and EBV, leading to CMV disease and EBV-associated complications in heart transplant recipients. Our findings emphasize the need for long-term monitoring, effective prophylactic strategies, and a balanced approach to immunosuppression to mitigate the risks associated with these viral infections.Keywords: Heart transplantation, Pediatric, HCMV, EBV, Immunosuppressive

Background

Infection agents account for one of the leading cause of serious complications and mortality in heart transplantation (HTx) [1]. Among these, viral infections human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) are considered main threats to heart transplant recipients [2]. The occurrence of cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV) resulted in impaired coronary endothelial function of the graft organ that correlates with postoperative CMV infection [3, 4]. The CMV infection plays a role in reducing postoperative survival and the development of episodes of organ rejection [5, 6]. The risk of acquisition of CMV infection postoperative is associated with the serology status of the donor and recipient. There is a high risk of postoperative CMV infections when transplantation in the HCMV immunoglobulin G (IgG) mismatched context (D + and R-) was done [4, 7]. EBV infection and immunosuppressive medications after solid organ transplantation are the key causes involved in the occurrence of post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD) [8]. The risk of developing PTLD is 6–76 times that it is attributed to EBV [9]. In latently infected patients, the use of immunosuppressive drugs post-transplantation triggers an imbalance between the immune response and latent form of EBV and then reactivates it [9]. Therefore, the anti-viral treatments like Valganciclovir as preemptive strategies for reduction of rate of CMV infection and CMV disease have been recommended [7, 10]. In EBV-mismatched conditions (R+/D-), the prevention approaches and use of prophylactic anti-viral therapies for EBV infection require additional investigations, and there is inadequate evidence for them [9]. Monitoring and detection of EBV infection in solid organ transplanted aids in diagnosing and management of EBV/PTLD cases as well as being used to inform preemptive therapy with reduction of immunosuppression and the most effective intervention for prevention of and treatment for PTLD. In this study, we surveyed the prevalence of CMV and EBV infections post heart transplant in pediatric recipients.

Methods

Recipients

A selection of 28 hospitalized pediatric heart transplant recipients was conducted in the Shaheed Rajaei Cardiovascular Medical and Research Centre. These patients were diagnosed with cardiac disorders such as dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), restrictive cardiomyopathy (RCM), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), and tetralogy of Fallot (TF) and selected for heart transplant. Sample collection was done from March 2021 to May 2022. Blood samples were collected at three time points, including at the time of surgery, 1 and 2 months post-transplant, and a urine sample two months post-transplant. Ultimately, the demographic, clinical, and laboratory findings of patients were recorded. The inclusion criteria included children aged 18 years with congestive heart failure, subacute coronary artery disease, reduced left ventricular function, and ischemic cardiomyopathy, and infants with heart failure or complex congenital features who are undergoing heart transplantation, and the exclusion criteria included individuals with pre-transplant immunodeficiency, active malignancy, irreversible hypertension, and adults.

Donors

In this study, due to the confidentiality of donor information, we could not obtain any additional demographic and clinical data, including seropositivity status for EBV and CMV. Heart tissue samples from donors were collected during the preparation and washing of the heart for transplantation, including samples from the aorta wall, pulmonary artery, and left atrium.

Molecular diagnosis of viral infections

The nucleic acid of the specimens, which included plasma (three time points), urine, and heart tissue of donors, was extracted using an advanced viral DNA/RNA extraction kit (GeneALL/South Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The Real-Time PCR CMV and EBV kit (GeneProof, Czech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions confirmed the presence of CMV and EBV in the mentioned samples. The target sequences of the IE antigen and EBNA1 by GeneProof, CMV, and EBV PCR kit primers and probes were amplified, respectively. The PCR assay was done with diagnostic sensitivity for CMV and EBV of 98.32% and 99.10% and diagnostic specificity of 94.21% and 94.19%, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Statistical assessments were conducted using Fisher’s exact or chi-square tests to determine the significant relationship between the type of infection and positive and negative cases. P <.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed with SPSS software version 22.0.

Results

We studied 28 pediatric patients who were candidates for heart transplants. The mean age of children was 11.5 ± 4.5 years (2–18). There were 57.1% females and 42.9% males. The clinical history and demographic information of recipients were recorded and summarized in Table 1. The induction regimen for all patients consisted of anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG), intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and methylprednisolone for days one and two post-transplantation, followed by initiation of tacrolimus and mycophenolate mofetil (CellCept) after extubation on the third day. Immunosuppressive medications continued with tacrolimus and methylprednisolone from the fourth day onwards. Followed prophylactic measures for all patients with intravenous ganciclovir immediately after transplantation was started. In case of negative PCR results, oral valganciclovir (Valcyte) was not initiated, mainly due to the minimal availability of valganciclovir syrup in Iran. With respect to the patients’ clinical conditions, CMV/EBV-positive PCR test, and their viral load, the physician decided to start treatment with Valcyte for Pts 6, 12, 14, and 23 during hospitalization.

Discussion

Our study investigated the prevalence and implications of CMV and EBV infections in pediatric heart transplant recipients. The results deepen the understanding of viral infection dynamics in this vulnerable population and demonstrate the value of vigilant monitoring and preemptive strategies in managing post-transplant infections.

Our results indicate a substantial prevalence of both CMV and EBV infections among pediatric heart transplant recipients. The serostatus of patients pre-transplant showed high CMV-IgG positivity (100%), indicating prior exposure or latent infection, with a lower CMV-IgM positivity(7.1%), and suggesting recent or ongoing infection in a minority of cases. For EBV, pre-transplant IgG and IgM positivity were 64.28% and 10.7%, respectively. These findings are consistent with existing literature, which reports high CMV seroprevalence in transplant candidates due to their immunocompromised status and previous exposures [11–13].

Our results showed CMV was detected in plasma in 10.7% of patients at the time of surgery, decreasing slightly to 7.1% two months post-transplant. This persistence of CMV highlights its potential for causing post-transplant complications despite prophylactic measures. In addition, EBV was detected in 25% of patients’ plasma at the time of surgery and maintained a similar prevalence two months post-transplant. This high prevalence of EBV aligns with the known risk of EBV reactivation in immunosuppressed patients [14].

In our study, according to patients’ conditions, 14.28% received Valcyte, and none of them showed any CMV infection despite being seropositive. This supports the efficacy of preemptive antiviral therapy in managing CMV infections, as previously documented [15]. However, Pts 6 and 23 showed EBV infection. In the case of Pt 6, a 7-year-old girl who was EBV seronegative, the patient’s blood sample at the time of transplantation showed a high viral load (1,204,973 copies/mL). This patient received intravenous ganciclovir on the day of transplantation and, due to the transplant conditions and high viral load, was prescribed oral Valcyte for six months. In addition, for this patient, from the 15th day after transplantation, the dose of tacrolimus was gradually reduced until the 30th day after transplantation, and intravenous immunoglobulin was prescribed for the patient for two weeks. Receiving Valcyte and changing the dose and type of immunosuppressive drug may be reasons for limiting EBV replication. Regarding the effectiveness of valganciclovir in reducing EBV infection in Pt 6, in Yager et al.’s study (2017), valganciclovir could suppress EBV replication and oral shedding, and it mitigated the proportion of days with EBV detected from 61.3% to 17.8% compared with placebo [16]. Gill et al. (2014) observed the suppression of EBV reactivation by valganciclovir during alemtuzumab therapy. It could be beneficial to use prophylaxis in populations with immunocompromised patients who have a high risk of EBV reactivation [17]. On the contrary, Pt 23 received an EBV-infected donor’s heart tissue, and during 2 months post-transplantation, EBV infection was detected in blood samples. It is possible that transplantation of an EBV-infected donor’s heart tissue was a reason for the increase in EBV viral loads in Pt 23. However, the high prevalence of EBV despite prophylaxis underscores the need for more effective or additional preventive measures against EBV reactivation.

A study documented a case of CMV reactivation occurring within the first year post-HTx, with the patient experiencing up to six additional episodes, notably affecting the eyes. The patient was administered oral ganciclovir, and the immunosuppressive regimen was modified multiple times. This case presented showed how immunosuppression played a significant role in some infections, despite the availability of suitable antiviral treatment [18]. Kuo’s study (2021) described a case involving heart transplantation and ECMO. The patient received valganciclovir. CMV reactivation occurred 9 days after heart transplantation (HTx), and the patient experienced another episode of CMV reactivation within the first year, with up to six additional episodes affecting his eyes. The patient received oral ganciclovir, and the immunosuppression was altered several times after transplantation, and then progressed to CMV enterocolitis. Repeated septic shock, severe gastrointestinal tract complications, and CMV hepatitis caused the patient’s death [19]. In our results, Pts 14, 18, and 21 underwent ECMO, and we did not detect EBV or CMV infections in them. Meanwhile, Pt 18 received CMV-infected heart tissue, and Pt 21, on the day of transplantation, showed CMV infection. Altogether, the exact changes in the dosage of immunosuppressive drugs and prophylactic treatments in cases of CMV reactivation must be managed.

Gardiner et al. (2023) detected a 17% CMV infection rate at a median of 2.2 months post-heart transplant in recipients. The authors highlighted the effect of valganciclovir in reducing CMV infection, advising the routine use of antiviral prophylaxis after heart transplantation [20]. In comparison to our findings with Gardiner’s study, we reported a low frequency (7.1%) of CMV infection after two months. In addition, Immohr et al. (2021) observed that during the first year, among 18 heart transplant recipients who developed CMV infection, the rates were 31.6% in D+/R-, 31.9% in D-/R+, 3.4% in D-/R-, and 11.1% in D+/R+ [21].

In Hyun’s study (2019), among 199 pediatric kidney recipients, 46 cases (23.1%) showed EBV viremia approximately 6 months after transplantation. In addition, seven patients developed PTLD at a median of 8.2 months (2.8–98.9 months) after transplantation. The authors showed that the incidence of PTLD had a correlation with higher tacrolimus levels [22].

Our study also highlights the role of immunosuppressive therapy in viral reactivation. All patients in the current study received tacrolimus and prednisolone, which are essential for preventing graft rejection but also facilitate viral reactivation by suppressing the immune response. The balance between adequate immunosuppression and minimizing infection risk remains a critical challenge in post-transplant care.

Our current study had some limitations, such as in sampling and follow-up, due to the specific conditions of the patients and the COVID-19 pandemic, which prevented us from conducting prolonged monitoring. Additionally, since donor information was completely confidential and inaccessible, we were not aware of the EBV and CMV serological status of the donors.

Conclusion

The findings advocate for enhanced surveillance protocols for both CMV and EBV post-transplant. Regular monitoring of viral loads and serostatus can guide timely interventions and improve patient outcomes. The data indicate that vigilant monitoring, effective prophylactic strategies, and a balanced approach to immunosuppression are necessary to mitigate the risks associated with these viral infections. According to our findings, we recommend continuous monitoring of recipients for on-time molecular diagnosis of EBV and CMV infections post-transplantation.
