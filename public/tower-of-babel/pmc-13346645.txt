In-vehicle 3D vision for perceiving dangerous driving behaviors
Authors: Wuhuan Li, Jun Lu, Kanlun Tan, Fei Kuang, Zhenming Chen, Wenhao He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate identification of dangerous driving behaviors is critical for accident prevention and occupant protection. However, most existing in-vehicle driver monitoring systems rely primarily on facial or head motion analysis, which fails to capture full-body driving behaviors and raises privacy concerns due to dependence on RGB or near-infrared imaging. In addition, these systems often exhibit limited robustness under low-light conditions. To address these limitations, this study proposes a comprehensive depth-based framework for in-vehicle 3D human pose estimation and dangerous driving posture recognition. First, a large-scale dual-view 3D pose dataset encompassing ten typical driving behaviors is constructed using a Time-of-Flight (ToF) camera. Based on this dataset, we develop a lightweight end-to-end pipeline in which an anchor-based regression model estimates the 3D poses of 16 driver keypoints, followed by an enhanced ST-GCN++ architecture for skeleton-based action recognition. By integrating pose estimation with graph-based temporal modeling, the proposed method effectively distinguishes visually similar hazardous behaviors. To facilitate real-world deployment, the algorithm is further integrated into a software system that enables closed-loop pose monitoring and hierarchical intervention. Experimental results verify that the proposed method achieves 96.02% accuracy in 3D pose estimation and 98.0% accuracy in behavior recognition. With a computational cost of only 1.49 G FLOPs and an inference latency of 0.0375 s per sample, the system achieves real-time performance (27–28 FPS) on an automotive embedded platform, making it well suited for practical in-vehicle safety applications.

Introduction

Distracted driving has become an increasingly critical issue in road safety, driven by the growing use of in-vehicle electronic devices and insufficient safety awareness. Behaviors such as answering calls, using mobile phones, and smoking while driving significantly contribute to traffic accidents.Consequently, real-time and accurate monitoring of driver states has become essential for enhancing road safety.

With the rapid advancement of intelligent vehicle technologies, Driver Monitoring System (DMS) has have become crucial for detecting fatigue, assessing attention, and identifying risky behaviors1. Traditional passive DMS mainly rely on indirect indicators, such as steering patterns and seat pressure, which are costly, less intelligent, and face challenges in seamless integration with cabin systems. In contrast, active DMS utilize visual sensors to directly capture the driver’s facial expressions2 and movements, employing deep learning to recognize fatigue, attention levels, and distractions, which represent the primary trend in DMS development. With regulations tightening, such as the EU GSR requiring fatigue monitoring systems in new vehicles by 2024 and distracted driving monitoring systems by 2026, and the 2024 version of the CNCAP integrating DMS into its scoring for the first time, the adoption of DMS in vehicles is rapidly progressing towards becoming a standard feature.

Current driver monitoring systems (DMSs) predominantly rely on RGB and near-infrared (NIR) cameras, either individually or in combination. RGB systems are susceptible to overexposure in bright light and noise in low light because of their reliance on ambient light. Although NIR can improve imaging in low light, it faces challenges in maintaining consistent quality under intricate lighting conditions. Additionally, concerns arise regarding compliance with strict privacy regulations owing to the detailed facial and appearance information captured in RGB/NIR images, where even slight blurring can affect the recognition accuracy. Most existing DMS studies concentrate on the head pose or facial regions, lacking a comprehensive capture of the driver’s overall three-dimensional pose. This limitation hinders a comprehensive assessment of potential operational risks. In contrast, the time-of-flight (ToF) depth camera, which operates as an active stereo vision sensor, presents unique benefits. It demonstrates resilience to bright light, performs well under low-light conditions, guarantees consistent imaging, and eliminates identifiable textures in depth maps. By accurately recording the driver’s three-dimensional structural information while safeguarding privacy and security, the ToF depth camera is ideal for monitoring human poses inside vehicles3.

Although ToF technology offers significant advantages, research on in-vehicle perception based on ToF is relatively limited. Additionally, insufficient attention has been given to estimating dangerous driver postures, with existing studies mostly focusing on 2D joint estimation. In reality, the driver’s three-dimensional structural information–including head orientation, torso posture, and hand position–is crucial for identifying risky behaviors4, directly impacting assisted driving strategies and passive safety responses during collisions. Abnormal postures, such as leaning forward, leaning to the side, or excessive backward tilt, may weaken the effectiveness of seat belts and affect the deployment of airbags, highlighting the significance of accurately capturing the driver’s three-dimensional posture in dynamic driving environments. However, this task faces several challenges, such as imaging instability owing to lighting variations, limited computational capabilities of automotive-grade chips, high costs associated with depth data collection and annotation, and the complexity of in-vehicle posture changes.

In response to these challenges, this study focuses on in-vehicle driver pose perception. A three-dimensional pose dataset was constructed using a 3D-ToF depth camera, an end-to-end 3D pose estimation method based on depth images is proposed, a deep learning recognition algorithm tailored for in-vehicle scenarios is developed, and an integrated, real-time driver pose monitoring system is ultimately realized. Related works

In this section, we first review the development of driver monitoring system(DMS). We then introduce recent studies on 3D driver pose estimation using deep learning methods.

Driver monitoring systems

With the development of intelligent transportation systems, ensuring drivers’ alertness and safety during driving has become a core challenge. DMSs are advanced technological solutions designed to improve road safety by monitoring driver behavior to detect fatigue, distraction, or impairment. These systems employ advanced cameras, including driver monitoring camera technology, and utilize complex algorithms to observe driver behavior, detect indicators of fatigue or distraction, and ensure that drivers maintain their attention while driving. To improve the performance of DMSs, deep learning methods have been extensively applied in these systems. For example, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are commonly employed to classify eye and mouth states5,6. Advanced models like the YOLO series, such as YOLOv8, excel in rapid and precise object detection, directly recognizing conditions like ”open eyes” and ”closed eyes.” To address temporal features such as continuous blinking patterns, Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and their variations, such as Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory networks (Bi-LSTMs)7 are utilized to capture the dynamic changes in fatigue states8. Furthermore, various features were leveraged to detect fatigue, distraction, and hazardous conditions. For instance, the eye aspect ratio was computed9 to differentiate between open and closed eyes. If the system detects multiple consecutive frames with closed eyes, it infers driver fatigue or drowsiness and prompts an alert. Similarly, the frequency of yawning is a crucial indicator of fatigue. Facial images offer a wealth of information extraction possibilities, including identity, emotion, and gaze. It is evident that a single indicator is insufficient and unreliable for analyzing the attention status of drivers. Nearly all existing solutions involve training multiple independent models to identify various aspects of a driver’s facial state. This poses challenges for in-vehicle safety systems, including hardware resources, software compatibility, and real-time responsiveness10.In terms of visual information sources, early systems mainly relied on single visual information. However, in real driving environments, issues such as lighting changes, extreme head poses, and occlusions are challenging. Multimodal fusion has become an important trend for obtaining more reliable and comprehensive judgments. In addition to RGB cameras, systems have begun to integrate more sensor data. For instance, a deep learning-based gaze detection method using a NIR camera sensor was proposed to consider the driver’s head and eye movements11. Depth information was utilized to mitigate motion-induced artifacts in a moving vehicle3 and to improve the processing of posture and occlusion issues12. However, this research field is relatively new, and studies addressing these topics are scarce. Furthermore, deep learning-based monitoring systems have evolved from singular fatigue detection to a comprehensive evaluation of the overall state of the driver. For example, these systems can detect whether the driver is engaging in secondary activities, such as using a mobile phone or an in-vehicle entertainment system. Additionally, they can assess the driver’s emotional state and determine whether they are excited or depressed, which may compromise safe driving practices.

Overall, these studies highlight the promising potential of deep learning in DMS for detecting driving behavior and assessing driver status. Although existing research demonstrates the significant capabilities of deep learning in the DMS domain, challenges remain in achieving real-time performance and addressing the complexities of varying lighting conditions and driver postures in the DMS. Furthermore, legal and ethical considerations, particularly regarding data privacy and user informed consent, must be thoroughly addressed during the design and deployment of such systems.

Deep learning methods

The task of human pose estimation (HPE) has evolved over the past several decades. Recently, the advent of deep learning13,14 has significantly accelerated the advancement of this field. Notably, the estimation of 3D human poses from single 2D images has garnered considerable attention in research15,16. This approach has become the prevailing method for such studies. Initially, 2D joint point coordinates are extracted from the image, after which these coordinates are transformed into 3D space using a deep neural network.The challenge of this method lies in the fact that some image information is lost during the process of mapping from 2D to 3D, which may lead to limited final accuracy. Recent studies17,18 have aimed to bypass intermediate 2D representations and directly infer three-dimensional joint heat maps or voxel representations from images. One approach involves discretizing the three-dimensional space into a voxel mesh and predicting a three-dimensional heat map for each joint, with the peak position of the heat map indicating the joint coordinates.The advantage of this method is that it can better preserve spatial information. However, less attention has been paid to the pose estimation of drivers in vehicles. In addition, for continuous video streams, some recent methods use 3D convolutional neural networks (CNN) or recurrent neural networks (RNN) to model the continuity of human motion between frames, thereby obtaining smoother and more accurate three-dimensional pose sequences19. Meanwhile, with the rapid development of intelligent perception and autonomous driving technologies, visual SLAM and collaborative perception methods have also provided new ideas for environmental modeling and state sensing in vehicle cabins.In particular, transfer learning-based meta-learning20, multi-object classification under dynamic scenes, and lightweight collaborative SLAM21 have improved the efficiency and robustness of visual perception, which inspires the design of our lightweight in-vehicle pose estimation system.

Recent studies22–24 have proposed enhancing the utilization of constraint relationships in human kinematics by incorporating attention mechanisms, such as the transformer module or specialized Dual Attention. These mechanisms aim to explicitly capture both global and local relationships among joints, thereby enhancing estimation accuracy, particularly in scenarios involving occlusion.Furthermore, the research community has devoted significant efforts to constructing high-quality and diverse driving datasets. However, these endeavors have primarily focused on 2D pose estimation, which is insufficient for more advanced applications. Some studies have utilized Kinect depth sensors for 3D human pose estimation25, yet most of these systems can only operate in highly constrained environments and lack applications tailored to specific scenarios. Acquiring training data for 3D human pose estimation is notably more challenging than for 2D pose estimation. Most existing 3D datasets are limited in scope and lack generalizability. To improve the generalization capabilities of the model and reduce its dependency on extensive and costly 3D labeled data, researchers have employed multitasking learning approaches that incorporate attitude estimation and action recognition. Additionally, they utilized weak supervision and learning strategies, such as leveraging two-dimensional annotations and training on signals that are consistent across multiple viewpoints19,26.Despite significant advancements in general 3D pose estimation technology, its implementation in an in-vehicle setting encounters unique challenges. First, within the confined space of a vehicle, objects such as the steering wheel, seat backrests, and center console often obstruct the driver’s body parts, particularly the trunk and lower body. Second, existing publicly available large-scale 3D human pose datasets do not accurately reflect the postures and backgrounds typical of real driving situations. The scarcity of comprehensive and high-fidelity three-dimensional posture datasets tailored for in-vehicle drivers is a primary obstacle to technological progress. Furthermore, in-vehicle systems impose stringent demands on computational resources and real-time performance requirements. Many advanced models, such as certain transformer-based models, possess extensive parameters and intricate computations, rendering real-time execution challenging on embedded in-vehicle devices. Developing a streamlined network architecture has emerged as a fundamental focus area for specialized applications such as driver monitoring.

System overall design

System structure

The system utilizes a three-layer architecture, comprising the Perception, Algorithm, and Application layers, to create a comprehensive closed-loop framework for managing dangerous driving behaviors. This framework covers the entire workflow, from data acquisition and preprocessing to intelligent, real-time recognition and active system intervention. To illustrate the overall architecture, a graphical flowchart shown in Fig. 1 for the implementation procedure is provided. In the Perception Layer, a data acquisition platform is established using depth sensors with a dual-view configuration. This design effectively overcomes the limitations of conventional RGB cameras, including their high sensitivity to lighting conditions and increased risk of privacy leakage. The collected video data are subsequently preprocessed to generate image frames and skeletal representations.

In the Algorithm Layer, the preprocessed dual-view depth data and initial skeleton information are fed into the proposed lightweight 3D pose estimation and behavior recognition pipeline for real-time processing. The 3D pose estimation module leverages a dual-path adaptive fusion mechanism to combine multi-view depth features, enabling robust extraction of the driver’s three-dimensional skeletal structure even under partial occlusions from the steering wheel or center console. The extracted skeletal joint coordinates are then processed through a temporal smoothing strategy to generate continuous, stable motion sequences. Subsequently, these motion features are analyzed by the skeleton-based action recognition model to identify hazardous driving behaviors, achieving accurate classification with low computational overhead. This integrated processing flow ensures that the system meets both high-precision and real-time requirements for in-vehicle embedded deployment.

Finally, in the Application Layer, the system employs a hierarchical control strategy to interface with the vehicle’s active and passive safety subsystems. This interaction enables a graded response to detected risks, including early auditory and visual warnings for distracted driving, and targeted active interventions such as seat belt pretensioning and brake pre-charging, thereby realizing a closed-loop safety monitoring workflow that effectively prevents accidents and protects occupant safety.Fig. 1System technology pipeline from data acquisition to risk warning.

Sensing devices and installation layouts

Depth perception devices commonly used in intelligent cabin systems can be broadly classified into three categories: RGB stereo cameras, structured light sensors, and ToF cameras. As summarized in Table 1, a comparison of these three depth sensors shows that RGB stereo cameras function as passive ranging devices that estimate depth by matching visual features across paired images and applying triangulation principles. However, their performance in in-vehicle environments is severely constrained by variations in cabin illumination. For example, RGB stereo systems often fail under low-light nighttime conditions and are highly susceptible to interference from diverse clothing textures, which can degrade the feature-matching accuracy. In contrast, structured light sensors are active ranging systems that project encoded light patterns onto target surfaces and infer depth by analyzing distortions in the reflected patterns in combination with triangulation. This approach reduces the sensitivity to variations in ambient lighting and surface texture differences. Nevertheless, structured light systems present notable limitations for in-cabin applications: their depth reconstruction process is computationally intensive, and their achievable frame rates are relatively low, limiting their suitability for real-time monitoring.Fig. 2Schematic diagram of sensor position. Data processing and algorithm details

Data preprocessing process

To process the driver-centered depth images,IR data, and synchronized time-series signals captured by the Azure Kinect ToF camera, this study designs a multidimensional preprocessing pipeline. The pipeline aims to suppress noise, standardize data formats, and enhance informative features, thereby providing high-quality input for downstream pose estimation and behavior recognition.

First, multimodal synchronization. A star-topology synchronization hub is used to connect the primary and secondary devices. During acquisition, the secondary device is started first and remains in standby mode. When the primary device is activated, the hub issues a recording command to the secondary device to enable simultaneous capture. The same sequence is used to terminate the recording. This procedure introduces small temporal offsets at the beginning and end of both streams. To mitigate these offsets, an offline timestamp-alignment step is applied after recording: each ToF stream is converted into timestamped frames, and the depth and IR frames are paired based on their timestamps. The resulting synchronization error is constrained within 10 ms, yielding aligned frame-sequence pairs for subsequent processing.

Next, depth enhancement and region cropping. To address the noise and missing/invalid pixels commonly observed in ToF depth images, a combined enhancement strategy is adopted. Median filtering is applied to suppress salt-and-pepper noise while preserving the edge structure. Contrast stretching then linearly expands the depth-value dynamic range to improve the separability in low-texture regions. To reduce the computational cost and focus on the driver’s upper body, target detection is performed, and the frames are cropped to the region of interest. This step removes background pixels, reduces data dimensionality, and substantially improves the efficiency of the downstream algorithms.Fig. 4Schematic diagram of the joint point. Skeleton data are also preprocessed before analysis. Using the skeleton-tracking module in the Azure Kinect SDK, we extract the 3D coordinates of 16 driver joints, including the left eye, right eye, left ear, right ear, nose, neck, chest, shoulders, elbows, wrists, and hips, as shown in Fig. 4. To improve the data quality, missing frames are filled using linear interpolation. In addition, joints with implausible positions caused by occlusion are manually corrected. These steps improve the completeness and reliability of the skeletal time series.

Detailed algorithm description

In the 3D driving posture estimation of the system, many existing depth-based approaches exhibit three major limitations. First, methods that convert depth images into point cloud representations require additional preprocessing steps and introduce a substantial runtime overhead. Operations such as point cloud sampling and normalization increase the algorithmic complexity and may degrade the estimation accuracy owing to the loss of geometric information during sampling. Second, voxel-based approaches suffer from high computational and memory costs. The dimensional expansion introduced by voxelization significantly increases the training burden of 3D convolutional neural networks, resulting in strict hardware requirements and poor suitability for deployment in resource-constrained environments. Third, some methods that operate directly on depth images adopt multi-stage pipelines. These typically obtain two-dimensional joint coordinates using a 2D keypoint detection model and subsequently map them into a three-dimensional space through regression. Because such pipelines are not fully end-to-end, they introduce redundant processing steps and allow errors to accumulate across the stages. Consequently, it is difficult to simultaneously achieve high computational efficiency and stable performance.

To address these limitations, we adopt A2J27, an anchor-driven keypoint regression network, as the baseline framework. As illustrated in Fig. 5, we further design a lightweight backbone network28 and an adaptive dual-path fusion module on top of this baseline. These improvements, together with the anchor-based regression strategy, enhance the robustness while maintaining computational efficiency. Consequently, the proposed method achieves a lightweight, fully end-to-end design.Fig. 5Overall structure and workflow of 3D pose estimation network. The depth image is input, the lightweight backbone network extracts the shallow and deep features of the image, and then the two features are fused and adaptively assigned to the regression features and classification features by the adaptive dualpath fusion module to their respective detection heads. Finally, the weighted sum is performed to obtain the final predicted 3D coordinates.

A 9-layer ST-GCN++ network is employed for action feature extraction, with the first three layers having 64 output channels, the middle three layers having 128 channels, and the last three layers having 256 channels. Residual connections are incorporated to mitigate the gradient-vanishing problem. For the convolution kernel design, an ergonomic partitioning strategy is adopted to categorize neighboring nodes into stationary, centripetal, and centrifugal points, with distinct weights assigned to enhance the feature representation capability. Meanwhile, to address the confusion between similar actions (e.g., smoking and drinking), a fused decision mechanism of ”object detection + skeleton classification” is proposed,as shown in Fig. 6. If the confidence level of the target detection exceeds the threshold, the target detection result is adopted. Otherwise, the skeleton classification results are used (the confidence threshold for this study was 0.65). If neither reaches their respective thresholds, the behavior is classified as normal driving. Additionally, a dual-smoothing strategy combining sliding window smoothing (window size = 30 frames) and Exponential Moving Average (EMA, smoothing factor = 0.8) is implemented to reduce the impact of short-term fluctuations on recognition results. Experiments demonstrate that this strategy effectively reduces the volatility of recognition outcomes and lowers the false detection rate.Fig. 6Logical workflow of fusion-based action recognition for ambiguous skeletal actions.

Analysis of experimental results and design of software system

Dataset construction

Prior to data collection, all procedures involving human participants are approved by the Ethics Committees of Chongqing Normal University and Chongqing Changan Automobile Co., Ltd. All methods are performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.Written informed consent is obtained from all volunteers. Informed consent is obtained from all subjects to publish anonymized depth images and skeleton visualizations in open-access journals.To protect participant privacy, all personal data are replaced with anonymous identifiers. The ToF depth maps inherently eliminate identifiable texture information and are further processed to remove any potentially identifying features. All anonymized datasets are stored on password-protected servers with access restricted to the research team.

After constructing the dual-view ToF data acquisition platform, we have recruited 25 volunteers (15 males and 10 females) aged 22–45 years. The participants cover a broad range of body mass index (BMI) and body types, including slim, normal, and overweight figures, and exhibit diverse driving habits to ensure diverse sample distribution. The data collection scenarios includes 10 driving-related behaviors, such as normal driving, making or receiving phone calls, smoking, and drinking water. Environmental variations are also considered, including different illumination conditions during daytime and nighttime, as well as diverse clothing styles such as T-shirts, sweaters, and jackets.

In total, more than 900 video clips are collected, as summarized in Table 2. Each clip has a duration of approximately 20 to 60 seconds. The videos are segmented into individual frames, resulting in approximately 300,000 paired depth and infrared image sequences. Using the skeleton-tracking module provided by Microsoft, the corresponding 3D skeleton data are extracted for each frame to create the base dataset. The dataset is divided into training and test sets with a ratio of 8:2. Both automatic and manual annotation strategies are employed. A self-developed depth-skeleton annotation correction tool is used to manually calibrate joint positions, with bilinear interpolation applied to refine the final annotations. Experimental results

In the training environment, all experiments are conducted on a Windows 10 operating system computer equipped with Intel Core i7-8700 CPU, 24 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU, which provides enough computing power to support the training and inference of all compared models.To comprehensively evaluate the proposed method and existing mainstream approaches, several performance metrics are employed. The proportion of successful frames is used to assess the accuracy of the 3D human skeleton estimation, whereas the action recognition accuracy measured the behavioral classification performance. The real-time efficiency is evaluated using the inference time and floating-point operations (FLOPs). In addition, the model complexity and deployment feasibility are quantified using the total number of model parameters.

First, ablation experiments are conducted on a 3D human keypoint recognition algorithm using a self-built dataset. The ST-GCN++ fusion model is then evaluated against several mainstream methods, including GCN(Graph Convolutional Network), DNN(Deep Neural Network), BLS(Broad Learning System), and 1D-CNN(One-Dimensional Convolutional Neural Network), on the test set. As reported in Table 4, ST-GCN++ achieved an accuracy of 98.0%, substantially outperforming GCN (74.5%), 1D-CNN (83.4%), DNN (92.6%), and BLS (93.5%). In terms of inference efficiency, ST-GCN++ required only 0.0375 s per sample, which is markedly faster than GCN at 4.356 s, 1D-CNN at 1.481 s, DNN at 0.432 s, and BLS at 0.151 s. Regarding the model size, the proposed network contains 507 KB of parameters. Although this is larger than the GCN with 81 KB, it is significantly smaller than the DNN with 1128 KB and 1D-CNN with 809 KB, and only slightly larger than the BLS with 364 KB. Overall, these results indicate that ST-GCN++ effectively balances high recognition accuracy, low inference latency, and moderate model complexity, making it well-suited for embedded deployment.

In the real-vehicle demonstration, the depth sensor continuously streams depth images to the processing module. The system detects the driver’s 3D joint positions in real time and analyzes the resulting joint sequences using a sliding window strategy to identify hazardous driving behaviors. The proposed framework achieves real-time performance at approximately 27–28 FPS on automotive-grade embedded platforms, with an inference latency of 0.0375 seconds per sample and computational complexity of 1.49 GFLOPs for 3D pose estimation. Despite the stable overall performance, several typical failure cases are observed in real scenarios.These mainly include partial body occlusion caused by the steering wheel or center console, high visual similarity between fine-grained actions (e.g., drinking and eating), and minor depth noise under strong direct sunlight. Such conditions may lead to temporary confidence fluctuations, which are effectively suppressed by the proposed dual-smoothing strategy and object-skeleton fusion decision mechanism. Software system

To assist researchers in exploring ToF camera applications in vehicle monitoring, we develop two python-based graphical user interface (GUI): the ”Dangerous Driving Behavior Recognition System” and the ”Control Strategy Demonstration System”. Its core components and libraries are illustrated in Fig. 11. Built on the QT framework, the application executes seamlessly within a Python environment, ensuring cross-platform compatibility. These systems interface with the Kinect camera via the pyk4a library to capture infrared and depth images of the driver. Core image processing tasks are performed using the OpenCV and NumPy libraries, while model inference is handled by the ONNX Runtime library. Behavioral decisions are made based on confidence scores from object detection and behavior recognition models. Additionally, a hierarchical behavior recognition module further refines the outputs of these models. Figure 9 presents the main GUI window of the system.

The proposed driver monitoring system features a flexible visualization and logging module to support both real-time supervision and post-event analysis. It offers two display modes for different application scenarios: a complete view combining depth images with overlaid 3D skeletons, and a simplified skeleton-only view for reduced bandwidth usage. During operation, the system continuously records key information including the type of detected dangerous behavior, corresponding confidence scores, precise timestamps, and the full 3D joint coordinate sequence of the driver. All data can be exported in standard Excel format for further statistical analysis and system optimization. The complete interface of the proposed Control Strategy Demonstration System is illustrated in Fig. 10. Figure 12 illustrates the multi-model workflow for driver posture detection and hazardous behavior recognition.The workflow is as follows: First, the driver’s infrared image is input into the object detection model, which outputs the bounding box coordinates and detection confidence scores. The corresponding depth image is then cropped using these coordinates. Next, the cropped depth image is processed by the 3D pose estimation model to extract the 3D skeleton data of the driver’s upper body. This skeleton data is subsequently fed into the behavior recognition model to generate confidence scores for behavior classification. The final classification of the driver’s behavior is determined based on the combined confidence scores from both the object detection and behavior recognition models.

Specifically, if the confidence level of object detection exceeds a predefined threshold, the result is prioritized. Otherwise, the results of behavior recognition are used. To further refine the system’s response, a time-series coordination mechanism is employed to implement a three-level hierarchical control strategy that dynamically adjusts the intervention intensity based on the duration of dangerous behaviors. If the behavior duration is less than 5 seconds, no intervention is triggered, and only the behavior data is recorded. When the duration is between 5 and 15 seconds, a warning level is activated, triggering voice prompts (e.g., ”For your driving safety, please adjust to the correct sitting posture”) and flashing dashboard lights. If the duration of the behavior exceeded 15 s, an active intervention level is initiated, activating the seatbelt pre-tensioning system and intelligent driving system to mitigate potential risks.
