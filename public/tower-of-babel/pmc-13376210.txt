Biomechanical comparison of two-screw and four-screw zero-profile fusion devices in anterior cervical discectomy and fusion using finite element analysis
Authors: Xingjin Wang, Minghe Yao, Chengyi Huang, Tingkui Wu, Kangkang Huang, Hao Liu, Beiyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBoth two-screw and four-screw zero-profile fusion devices are commonly used in anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), yet their biomechanical differences remain unclear. This study compared their biomechanical performance using a validated three-dimensional finite element (FE) model of the C2–T1 cervical spine reconstructed from computed tomography data. The devices were implanted at the C5/6 segment to simulate ACDF under various motion conditions. A 75 N follower load and a 1.0 N·m moment were applied to induce flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation. The two-screw zero-profile device demonstrated higher von Mises stress on the cage and plate but lower stress on the screws than the four-screw device. Stress distributions on the cage, screws, and adjacent vertebral bodies were comparable across models. After fusion, both devices exhibited similar stress characteristics. Overall, the two-screw zero-profile fusion device may achieve biomechanical behavior comparable to that of the four-screw zero-profile fusion device under the investigated static loading scenario. Further long-term clinical and biomechanical investigations are warranted to clarify its biomechanical behavior and potential implications following ACDF.Keywords: Zero-profile fusion device, Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, Finite element analysis, Biomechanics, Implant–bone interfaceSubject terms: Engineering, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

The anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is the most classical surgical procedure in cervical spine surgery and is widely accepted as the golden standard to treat symptomatic cervical degenerative disc disease1. The advantages of ACDF include thorough decompression via the natural canal, reliable surgical decompression efficiency, and restoration and maintenance of cervical sagittal alignment 2. Moreover, the continuously developing concept of intervertebral fusion promotes the evolution of ACDF, giving rise to the zero-profile intervertebral fusion device3. Designed to provide immediate postoperative stability and avoid dysphagia from plate-soft tissue contact4,5, the zero-profile fusion device is increasingly preferred over traditional anterior plates and cages6.

According to the low-profile concept, the anterior plate of zero-profile fusion device is attached to the polyether-ether-ketone (PEEK) cage to make the whole device placed into the intervertebral space3. Screws are then inserted through the holes in the anterior plate for fixation. Based on clinical application results, zero-profile fusion devices could offer similar clinical outcomes and shorten blood loss and operative time compared with plate and cage system4,5,7. However, the use of zero-profile fusion devices may be associated with potential complications, including loss of cervical sagittal alignment, reduced biomechanical stability, and an increased risk of implant subsidence 4,8,9. Among various zero-profile fusion devices, Zero-P (Synthes GmbH, Oberdorf, Switzerland) is the typical four-screw device and widely used in clinical practice10. Zero-P consists of an anterior plate with four screw holes and a PEEK body3. The four fixed-angle screws stabilize the adjacent vertebrae, though the need for precise angulation can make screw placement challenging, particularly near the mandible3,11. Additionally, rigid early fixation following ACDF may cause stress shielding, potentially impacting the bony fusion process12. To address these concerns, the Zero-P VA (Synthes GmbH, Oberdorf, Switzerland) fusion device was introduced, featuring two variable-angle screws and a diagonal limited depth design. Previous studies have demonstrated that Zero-P VA achieves clinical outcomes comparable to those of Zero-P and traditional plate-and-cage constructs8,11,13.

Although both zero-profile fusion devices provide stable outcomes in ACDF procedures, differences exist in their plate designs, screw trajectories, and biomechanical interactions with the vertebral bodies. However, few studies have specifically investigated these differences or offered evidence-based recommendations for clinical practice. Apart from this, no finite element studies have explored the biomechanical differences between the two fusion devices, particularly with respect to load-sharing evolution between early fixation and fully fused conditions using identical implant geometry. Therefore, the objectives of this finite element study were: (1) to compare the biomechanical behavior of the two-screw and four-screw zero-profile fusion devices, and (2) to provide practical guidance for their clinical application.

Materials and methods

Construction of the cervical spine model and instruments

The three-dimensional finite element (FE) model of cervical spine segments (C2-T1) was constructed and validated based on the computed tomography (CT) images of a 28-year-old healthy man volunteer (165 cm, 65 kg). The CT scans in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format were obtained by a CT scanner (SOMATOM Definition AS+, Siemens, Germany), and the slice thickness and interval of the images were 0.75 mm and 0.69 mm, respectively. Then the CT images were imported to Mimics 17.0 software (Materialize Corporation, Belgium) to generate C2-T1 spine FE models. Subsequently, the models were processed using Geomagic Studio 2015 (Raindrop Geomagic Inc., USA) to perform denoising, surfacing, and smoothing. Next, the refined models (Fig. 1) were imported into Hypermesh 14.0 (Altair Corporation, USA) to generate mesh structures. Finally, ABAQUS 6.9.1 (Dassault Systèmes Corporation) was employed to set boundary conditions and conduct the analysis.

The finite element of a healthy cervical spine (C2-T1), Zero-P and Zero-P VA fusion devices.

Based on previous studies14–16, the cervical spine model consisted of cortical bone, cancellous bone, and bony endplates. The cortical bone and bony endplates were modeled as thin solid layers covering the cancellous bone with a uniform thickness of 0.4 mm16,17. The intervertebral disc was partitioned into the annulus fibrosus and nucleus pulposus, maintaining a volume ratio of 6:416,17.The annulus fibrosus was modeled as a solid annulus matrix (C3D4 elements) reinforced by embedded tension-only collagen fibers represented using T3D2 truss elements. Fibers accounted for approximately 19% of the annulus volume and were oriented at 15°–30° relative to the transverse plane17. The cartilage layer, covering the 0.5 mm facet joint space, was modeled as a nonlinear surface-to-surface contact, while frictionless contact was established between the articular surfaces of the facet joints. The endplates and intervertebral disc were designated as a tie connection to ensure stability. Furthermore, the ligaments—including the anterior longitudinal ligament (ALL), posterior longitudinal ligament (PLL), ligamentum flavum (LF), interspinous ligament (IL), supraspinous ligament (SL), and capsular ligament (CL)—were defined as tension-only truss elements and attached to the corresponding vertebrae. The interbody fusion devices for ACDF were constructed according to the four-screw zero-profile fusion device18 and two-screw zero-profile fusion device16. The four-screw zero-profile fusion device incorporated a cage measuring 15 mm in width, 16 mm in length, and 6 mm in height, with self-tapping screws 16 mm in length. The two-screw zero-profile fusion device featured a cage with dimensions of 13.6 mm (width), 17.5 mm (length), and 5 mm (height), and was similarly equipped with 16 mm self-tapping screws.

Following implantation of the zero-profile fusion device, two postoperative conditions were simulated: the immediate postoperative state and the fused state. In the immediate postoperative model, the interface between the fusion device and vertebral endplates was defined using surface-to-surface nonbonded contact, allowing separation and sliding, with a friction coefficient of 0.3, consistent with previous FE studies15. In the fused model, complete osseointegration was assumed. Bonded (tie) constraints were applied at the cage–endplate and grafted bone–endplate interfaces to represent solid fusion. The interaction between the centrally grafted bone and the inferior endplate of C5, as well as the newly formed bone surrounding the cage, was defined as fully bonded. Material-specific implant–endplate contacts were retained in the fused condition, with friction coefficients of 0.1 for the anterior titanium plate–endplate interface and 0.5 for the posterior PEEK cage–endplate interface. The screw–vertebra interfaces were modeled using tie constraints, and shared nodes were assigned at the screw–plate interfaces to prevent relative motion16.

Eventually, four surgical models were constructed: ACDF surgical model with four-screw zero-profile fusion device implantation at the C5/6 segment and simulated bone fusion (Model 4-F); ACDF surgical model with four-screw zero-profile fusion device implantation at the C5/6 segment and simulated immediate postoperative status (Model 4-NF); ACDF surgical model with two-screw zero-profile fusion device implantation at the C5/6 segment and simulated bone fusion (Model 2-F); ACDF surgical model with two-screw zero-profile fusion device implantation at the C5/6 segment and simulated immediate postoperative status (Model 2-NF). All material properties were set according to previous studies15,16. The material properties and the numbers of nodes and elements of aforementioned components are presented in Table 1 and 2.

Biomechanical simulation

To simulate human cervical spine movement, the FE models were constrained at the inferior endplate of T1. A follower load of 75 N and a pure moment of 1.0 N·m were both applied at the odontoid process of C2 to represent physiologic compressive loading and to induce flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation, respectively15,19. Self-weight state is defined as the state of the cervical spine model when it remains in a neutral position and is subjected to the follower-load, without any movement or tendency to move in other directions. The following parameters were subsequently analyzed: (1) The maximum stress on the vertebrae at the operative and adjacent segments in the models incorporating four-screw and two-screw zero-profile fusion devices; (2) The maximum stress experienced by the intervertebral disc at both the operative and adjacent segments across different models; (3) The magnitude and distribution of stress at the surface of the implant components, including screws, the titanium plate, and the cage.

Results

Validation of the developed FE cervical spine model

To validate the accuracy of the present FE model, the simulated range of motion (ROM) in the intact cervical spine model was compared with previously published in vitro experimental results by Panjabi et al.20. and FE analysis results by Lee et al21. across the cervical spine segments C2–C7 (Fig. 2). In the current study, the flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation values of the present model were found to be generally within one standard deviation of the mean values reported by Panjabi et al.20, and closely aligned with the data from Lee et al.21, confirming the consistency and physiological reliability of the present study’s outcomes.

Comparison of the predicted range of motion with previous published studies.

Maximum stress of the zero-profile fusion devices and vertebral bodies

Across all motion conditions, 2-NF exhibited the highest stress concentration on the fusion device (Fig. 3), with peak values observed in right axial rotation (20.813 MPa) and left lateral bending (19.249 MPa). In contrast, 4-F consistently showed the lowest stresses, with a maximum value of 15.889 MPa during left rotation. Under self-weight condition, two-screw zero-profile fusion device showed higher von Mises stress concentration on the cage and plate, while lower von Mises stress concentration on the screws compared with four-screw zero-profile fusion device (Fig. 4). And the trends of stress distribution on the surfaces of interbody fusion cage and screws were similar under 6-degree movement.

The maximum stress of the cages under self-weight, flexion, extension, lateral bending and axial rotation in four FE models.

The von Mises stress distribution on the surface of plates, cages and screws among the four FE models in all loading conditions.

In the inferior vertebral body of C5, 2-NF recorded the highest stresses under all motion states, reaching 4.02 MPa in right axial rotation, while 4-F remained the lowest at 3.238 MPa (Fig. 5). Similarly, for the superior vertebral body of C6, 2-NF again showed the highest stress (4.113 MPa in right rotation) compared to 4-F (3.213 MPa). Nevertheless, the stress distribution of inferior vertebral body of C5 and superior vertebral body of C6 was similar when the implant and vertebral reached fusion status in 4-F and 2-F. As for C5 and C6 vertebral bodies, all implanted models showed increased stress compared to the intact model (Fig. 6). For instance, in C6 during right axial rotation, stress in the intact model was 5.416 MPa, whereas it reached 10.291 MPa in 2-NF—an increase of nearly 90%. In C5, 2-NF exhibited peak stress (8.737 MPa) under right rotation, significantly higher than 4-F (6.247 MPa) and the intact model (4.442 MPa).

The maximum von Mises stress of the inferior vertebral body of C5 and superior vertebral body of C6 in all loading conditions.

The maximum von Mises stress vertebrae bodies at surgical levels (C5, C6) in all loading conditions.

Maximum stress of the adjacent vertebrae bodies

At the C4 level, compared to the intact model, all models exhibited increased stress values under self-weight and all motion conditions (Fig. 7). Under flexion, the highest stress was observed in 2-NF (4.465 MPa), followed by 4-NF (4.317 MPa). A similar pattern was found under extension and lateral bending. Notably, during left axial rotation, 2-NF (4.412 MPa) and 4-NF (4.317 MPa) again demonstrated the highest stress, markedly exceeding the intact model (2.414 MPa).

The maximum von Mises stress of the adjacent vertebrae bodies in all loading conditions.

At the C7 level, the stress distribution trends were consistent with the C4 level. Under self-weight, 2-NF (2.745 MPa) and 4-NF (2.654 MPa) exhibited the highest stress values. During flexion and extension, 2-NF reached 4.047 MPa and 3.837 MPa, respectively, exceeding the other models and the intact spine. In lateral bending and axial rotation conditions, 2-NF and 4-NF consistently showed higher stress values compared to 4-F and 2-F. Specifically, under right axial rotation, 4-NF (5.106 MPa) exhibited the highest stress, followed by 2-NF (5.049 MPa), both significantly greater than the intact model (2.596 MPa).

Maximum stress of adjacent intervertebral discs

Adjacent intervertebral disc stresses also increased following implantation compared with the intact condition (Fig. 8), particularly in two-screw fusion device implantation model. At the C4/5 level, stress values increased across all models compared with the intact condition (0.997 MPa under self-weight). The 2-NF demonstrated the highest stress values under most loading conditions, with values reaching 3.83 MPa under flexion, 3.148 MPa under extension, and 3.874 MPa under left axial rotation. 4-NF followed closely, with stresses of 3.611 MPa and 3.668 MPa in flexion and left axial rotation, respectively. The complete fusion models (4-F and 2-F) consistently exhibited lower stress values compared to their corresponding implantation models, though still higher than the intact condition. At the C6/7 level, similar trends were observed. The intact model showed stress values ranging from 1.07 MPa (self-weight) to 2.663 MPa (left axial rotation). 2-NF again presented the highest stresses, reaching 3.971 MPa under flexion and 4.173 MPa under left axial rotation, while 4-NF displayed comparable high values (3.824 MPa and 3.88 MPa respectively). The complete fusion models had relatively lower stresses, particularly 4-F, which exhibited 3.173 MPa under flexion and 3.483 MPa under left axial rotation.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to investigate the biomechanical behavior of two zero-profile cervical implant systems—Zero-P, a four-screw design with rigid fixation, and Zero-P VA, a two-screw system that allows micromotion—under various physiological loading conditions, and provide evidence for the ACDF clinical practice. Our results suggested that both four-screw zero-profile fusion device and two-screw zero-profile fusion device with complete fusion can provide favorable stress distribution across the fusion cage, vertebral bodies, and adjacent intervertebral discs. However, the two-screw zero-profile fusion device exhibited higher stress values across nearly all structures at the postoperative immediate status compared with four-screw zero-profile fusion device. The two-screw with variable angle design allows for micromotion between the anterior plate and screws at the early stage after implantation, which may offer load sharing on the cage and endplate, and enhance bone remodeling and promote fusion in some cases18; however, this mobility also results in elevated biomechanical loads on adjacent segments and posterior elements22.

Zero-profile implant systems are widely accepted in ACDF due to the promising clinical and radiological outcomes and advantages of the low-profile design3,6,11,13. Additionally, the integrated design and flexible screws insertion avoid overexposure of surgical area, which significantly shorten operation time and reduce intraoperative blood loss6,7. However, the biomechanical changes after the implantation of the four-screw and two-screw zero-profile fusion device in ACDF are not fully elucidated. In this study, the von mises stress was higher on two-screw zero-profile fusion device compared with four-screw zero-profile fusion device after the implantation under self-weight and six-degree movement condition, and the trends were similar in the vertebral bodies at the surgical level. The postoperative stability of the implant and vertebrae plays an important role in maintaining the functional spinal unit height, cervical sagittal alignment, and bone formation and remodeling process10,12,23,24, while the rigid fixation with the implant might result in stress shielding12,22. Thus, the balance between the implant fixation and intervertebral stability is of particular interest given the intimate biomechanical relationship. The two kinds of zero-profile implants can provide similar contact force after insertion, but the von mises stress is higher in the two-screw zero-profile fusion device. Huang et al.11 analyzed the clinical outcomes and fusion speed in ACDF patients with Zero-P and Zero-P VA. According to their results, the patients who were implant with Zero-P VA showed higher fusion rates and similar incidence of complications postoperatively compared with Zero-P. Besides, Reis et al.25 compared the stability of Zero-P VA, Zero-P, and plate and cage in cadaveric specimens, and they revealed that Zero-P VA could offer less rigid construct than the others. Appropriate micromotions (112 ± 176 µm) at the bone-implant interface may facilitate the osseointegration process, whereas excessive micromotions can impair it26. At the early stage after ACDF, the nonrigid screw fixation allows more load sharing between the vertebral body and implant cages, and the contact stress caused by micromotion might promote postoperative bone formation11,13. Although the faster fusion speed will be beneficial to patients’ prognosis, these findings suggest that the two-screw zero-profile fusion device imposes greater biomechanical demands on the surgical segment.

Theoretically, the biomechanical stress, cervical lordosis, and intervertebral disc height works cooperatively to maintain the cervical sagittal alignment and stability10,12. Hence, the stress of adjacent segment structure also worth our attention. Although the adjacent segment vertebral body experiences greater stress after implantation of a two-screw zero-profile fusion device compared to a four-screw device in the unfused state, this difference diminishes post-fusion. After fusion, the stress on the adjacent segment prosthesis under both self-weight and motion conditions becomes comparable, with no significant differences observed. The same is true for the adjacent segment intervertebral disc, but the stress is greater during left and right lateral bending. Hua et al27 performed a FE analysis to evaluated adjacent segment biomechanical changes after ACDF, and increased range of motion and intradiscal pressure were observed at the adjacent segments of the fused segments in ACDF, regardless of whether zero-profile devices or cage plus plate was used. Reis et al25 also reported Zero-P VA consistently allowed greater ROM in flexion, extension and axial rotation than standard plate and cage, and greater ROM than the Zero-P during extension and axial rotation in cadaveric study. Furthermore, both the lax zone and stiff zone exhibited similar trends to the ROM findings, with the two-screw cage showing significantly greater lax zone and stiff zone across all directions of loading than the other two constructs. After implantation, the nonrigid configuration of Zero-P VA will contribute to load sharing between the bone graft cage and vertebral body. However, the adjacent segment structures will bear higher stress and less stability25. Zhao et al13 retrospectively reviewed 13 patients implanted with Zero-P VA and their results showed a significant decline in the Cobb angle values of the fused segment at 3-months follow-up visit. Huang el al11 also mentioned that the allowance for greater micromotion with two-screw fixation increased the likelihood of lordosis loss or kyphosis. Although the micromotion property of Zero-P VA will be beneficial to enhance bone formation and promote bone fusion process, the less rigid fixation also increases the biomechanical stress on adjacent vertebrae and intervertebral discs. These results highlight the considerable biomechanical influence of the implant system on adjacent segments22, indicating a potential elevated risk for adjacent segment degeneration with the two-screw zero-profile fusion device. Future clinical studies are warrant to further analyses the long-term biomechanical and radiological changes of two-screw zero-profile fusion device after ACDF.

The design of the diagonal depth limit of the front plate, the reduction of the number of screws, and the design of diagonal screw implantation have changed the implantation method of the zero-profile system and the biomechanical properties produced after implantation in the cervical spine. Compared to sagittal screw implantation, a diagonal trajectory enables the screw to traverse a longer path within the vertebral body, thereby increasing its contact area with the cancellous bone, which may help reduce the risk of screw loosening or pullout following ACDF28,29. The two diagonally implanted screws form a stable construct with the fusion cage, thereby enhancing the stability of the implant25. Additionally, as only one screw is implanted into each vertebra, this design reduces the risk of vertebral fracture and irritation of endplate during implantation30, particularly in multi-level cervical spondylosis cases. However, due to the relatively less rigid fixation and potential for postoperative micromotion, the application of two-screw zero-profile fusion device should be approached with caution in patients with multi-segmental cervical spondylosis, flexion-distraction injuries, fractures, and osteoporosis30–33.

However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, inherent methodological constraints of FE analysis, together with the use of a single-subject cervical spine model, may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although FE modeling provides an effective tool for predicting biomechanical responses under controlled conditions, it cannot fully replicate the complex in vivo environment following surgery. Important factors such as active cervical musculature, physiological ligament and facet joint interactions, patient-specific material heterogeneity, and postoperative biological remodeling processes cannot be accurately reproduced within the current modeling framework. Second, simplified assumptions regarding material properties, contact definitions, and boundary conditions were adopted to ensure numerical stability and computational feasibility. While these assumptions are commonly used and supported by prior biomechanical literature, they may influence absolute stress magnitudes and ROM predictions. In addition, although a follower load combined with pure moment loading was adopted in this study to simulate physiological compressive effects of head weight and cervical musculature, differences remain between this loading strategy and other commonly used experimental or validation protocols (e.g., preload combined with pure moment loading). Compared with simple axial preload, follower loads maintain compressive forces along the curvature of the cervical spine and reduce artificial bending moments, which may alter the magnitude of predicted ROM and stress distribution. Variations in load application paths and constraint definitions may contribute to discrepancies in absolute ROM and stress magnitudes across studies. However, because identical boundary and loading conditions were consistently applied across all simulated models, the present analysis remains suitable for evaluating relative biomechanical differences between the two-screw and four-screw zero-profile fusion constructs. Therefore, the present analysis was primarily intended to compare relative biomechanical differences between the two zero-profile fusion devices rather than to simulate the complete physiological loading environment. Third, there is considerable variety among zero-profile implant systems, with differing anchoring methods, screw thread feature and structural designs. In this study, only four-screw and two-screw zero-profile fusion devices were evaluated, and differences in screw thread features between the two devices may have influenced the biomechanical results. In addition, although the present FE model included the C2–T1 cervical spine, model validation was conducted by comparing segmental ROM at the overlapping levels (C2–C7) with widely cited experimental data. This approach has been commonly adopted in previous cervical spine FE studies due to the limited availability of experimental datasets covering the entire C2–T1 segment under identical loading conditions. Nevertheless, differences in modeled spinal levels and boundary conditions may influence absolute ROM values. Therefore, ROM-based validation in this study serves primarily to verify global kinematic behavior, while stress-related outcomes were interpreted in a relative and comparative manner. Further experimental validation using specimens and loading protocols more closely matching the modeled segment is warranted. Finally, the cervical spine exhibits anatomical and biomechanical variability across different segments, and findings derived from the C5/6 segment may not fully represent all clinical scenarios. Future studies incorporating multiple subject-specific models, sensitivity and convergence analyses, in vitro biomechanical experiments, and longer-term clinical follow-up are warranted to further validate and extend the conclusions of this work.

Conclusion

This FE analysis evaluated the biomechanical behavior of four-screw and two-screw zero-profile fusion devices under identical loading conditions, considering both the immediate postoperative state and the fully fused state. In the immediate postoperative condition, the two-screw device exhibited relatively higher stress levels at the operated segment and adjacent segments compared with the four-screw construct, indicating different load-sharing characteristics during the early fixation stage. After complete fusion was simulated, the stress differences between the two implant configurations were notably reduced, and both devices demonstrated comparable stress distributions across the implant and surrounding vertebral structures under the same loading conditions. Based on these findings, the two-screw zero-profile fusion device may achieve biomechanical behavior comparable to that of the four-screw zero-profile fusion device after complete fusion under the investigated static loading scenario. Nonetheless, the present findings reflect relative biomechanical behavior rather than clinical superiority. Further long-term clinical trials and in vitro biomechanical investigations are therefore required to comprehensively evaluate the biomechanical implications of two-screw zero-profile fusion device implantation following ACDF.
