Delayed C5 palsy following thoracolumbar deformity surgery: a case report
Authors: Alyssa Barré, Charles-Antoine Mechas, Ahmad Alhankawi, Mohamed Sarraj, Gokul Arunmaithurai, Ahmad Nassr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:C5 palsy is a known complication following decompressive cervical spine surgery, most often attributed to factors such as foraminal stenosis, cord drift, or traction. While its incidence after cervical procedures is well-documented, C5 palsy occurring after thoracolumbar deformity surgery has not been previously described.Case:A 70-year-old woman with a prior C2-T4 posterior cervical spinal fusion underwent thoracolumbar deformity correction with T4-pelvis fusion. Two months postoperatively, she developed sudden right deltoid and biceps weakness. Laboratory and imaging workups ruled out other pathological causes, and electromyography confirmed a new C5 palsy. She was treated with physical therapy and electrical stimulation and achieved complete recovery by 6 months post-thoracolumbar surgery.Conclusion:This case describes isolated delayed C5 palsy following thoracolumbar deformity surgery in a patient with prior cervical spine fusion 2 years earlier. Potential contributing factors include global alignment changes, altered neural tension, and intrinsic anatomic fragility of the C5 nerve root. This presentation expands the clinical context of postoperative C5 palsy and highlights the importance of monitoring for cervical neurologic deficits, even in the absence of cervical intervention.Level of Evidence:5 – Case Report

Introduction

C5 palsy is a well-recognized complication of anterior and posterior cervical decompression surgeries, characterized by painless dysfunction of the C5 myotome[1]. The incidence is approximately 4–6 percent following cervical decompression surgeries, with reported rates of 4.3% following anterior and 4.7% following posterior cervical decompressions[2–4]. Despite the frequency with which it occurs, there is a discrepancy in the literature regarding the clinical definition of C5 palsy[9]. Proposed definitions include a decline in deltoid/biceps muscle strength by one or more grades in standard manual muscle testing (MMT) within 6 weeks after surgery[10], new-onset deltoid weakness demonstrated by MMT on two consecutive postoperative visits[11], and deltoid and/or biceps weakness on MMT of greater than one level without other myelopathic symptoms[8,12,13]. Regardless of the definition, C5 palsy typically presents within a few days to weeks postoperatively[14], most often as unilateral weakness in the deltoid and biceps brachii with associated numbness in the C5 dermatome[14–16]. Multiple etiologic theories exist, including ischemia to the anterior horn cells, mechanical traction on the C5 nerve root due to cord migration, and hypoperfusion of the radicular arteries[17–19]. Static imaging often fails to demonstrate definitive correlates, and no single mechanism has been conclusively established.

Prognosis for postoperative C5 palsy is generally favorable, with most patients improving within a few months; however, reported treatment approaches and recovery timelines vary widely[6,15]. This variability shows the complexity of C5 palsies and emphasizes the need to understand atypical presentations.

Importantly, existing literature focuses almost exclusively on C5 palsy following cervical surgery. To date, no published cases describe isolated C5 palsy occurring after thoracolumbar deformity correction. We present a unique case of a patient who developed a delayed-onset C5 palsy 2 months after thoracolumbar deformity surgery in a patient with a remote history of cervical fusion. This case highlights an unusual presentation and provides an opportunity to explore potential mechanisms of C5 palsy in patients with long-segment fusion constructs. As such constructs and global alignment corrections become increasingly common, understanding how thoracolumbar procedures influence cervical biomechanics is essential, particularly in patients with previously fused cervical segments.

Case report

A 70-year-old female with a history of hypertension, hyperparathyroidism, remote breast cancer, cervical myelopathy, and thoracolumbar adult spinal deformity presented to our academic center and initially underwent C2-T4 posterior spinal decompression and fusion for treatment of cervical deformity and degenerative myelopathy. Surgery included foraminotomies and partial facetectomies on the left (C4-5, C5-6, C6-7) and right (C5-6, C6-7, C7-T1). She was noted to have significant thoracolumbar deformity, which was relatively asymptomatic at that time (Fig. 1). Her postoperative course was uncomplicated. Two years later, she presented with worsening back pain, progressive leg pain aggravated by prolonged ambulation or standing, and progression of her thoracolumbar deformity. After the failure of conservative management, a decision was made to pursue surgery. Given the patient’s extensive thoracolumbar deformity and prior surgery to T4, we elected for a T4-pelvis instrumented fusion, including ALIF from L3-S1 and TLIF L1-L3. She ultimately underwent this surgery with a final uninterrupted spinal construct from C2-pelvis (Fig. 2). Shoulder traction was not used for the procedure. Imaging reviewed postoperatively demonstrated stable cervical alignment across the prior C2-T4 fusion without interval change in cervical lordosis, coronal alignment, foraminal stenosis, or hardware position. Approximately 2 months postoperatively, after the thoracolumbar surgery, the patient developed sudden-onset right arm weakness. The differential for new upper extremity weakness after a non-cervical spine surgery includes perioperative stroke, cervical epidural hematoma, implant migration, positional/tractional brachial plexopathy, and metabolic derangements. These possibilities were systematically excluded. Neurological evaluation revealed no abnormalities on head computed tomography and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Cervical spine MRI (Fig. 3) showed no significant central or foraminal stenosis at any level, no epidural hematoma, and no instrumentation failure or screw penetration. Laboratory evaluation revealed no nutritional or metabolic abnormalities. Electromyography findings indicated chronic right-sided C5-T1 radiculopathies consistent with her prior cervical pathology, but importantly, revealed new subacute changes localized to the right C5 nerve root distribution, confirming a new C5 palsy. At 3 months post-operatively, the patient exhibited a dense motor palsy affecting the right C5 myotome, characterized by MMT of 1/5 deltoid strength and 3/5 biceps strength, without significant sensory deficit. She was prescribed physical therapy for shoulder range of motion and electrical stimulation therapy. At 6 months following thoracolumbar surgery (5 months after the onset of C5 palsy), she reported full functional recovery. Examination revealed complete resolution of the motor deficits, with 5/5 symmetric deltoid and biceps muscle strength and full active and passive shoulder range of motion.

Discussion

Postoperative C5 palsy is a known complication of cervical decompression procedures, with debated etiology including regional mechanical and perfusion-related factors. Existing literature frames C5 palsy as a complication arising from regional cervical changes such as cord drift, foraminal stenosis, or direct insult[1–4]. This case extends the clinical context to a scenario in which no cervical exposure occurred, and the only recent operative intervention involved the thoracolumbar spine in a patient with pre-existing C2-T4 fusion and no apparent change in cervical spine alignment. Importantly, because of the abnormal deficit, a broad differential diagnosis was considered. Once all metabolic, vascular, and compressive causes were excluded, C5 palsy became the most consistent explanation and was supported by findings on electrodiagnostic studies.

We hypothesize that our patient’s rigid C2–T4 fusion likely limited the cervical spine’s ability to compensate for changes in global posture after thoracolumbar reconstruction, creating a mechanical environment in which small shifts in spinal cord and nerve root tension or neural stress may have been absorbed disproportionately by the exiting cervical roots. This may help explain why the C5 nerve root, which is already structurally predisposed to traction-related dysfunction, became symptomatic despite no radiographic alignment change. C5 is the most cranial root of the brachial plexus and experiences stress during shoulder motion. Anatomic studies have shown that C5 has a shorter intradural course, a steeper exit against the prominent C6 carotid tubercle, tighter bony corridors, and the smallest amount of excursion, which predisposes it to traction risk[16,21]. Previous studies have shown that intraoperative shoulder traction can be a risk factor for C5 palsy[22], and while this was not utilized during our patient’s operation, it does support the traction mechanism theory.

The patient’s recovery pattern is also consistent with existing C5 palsy literature. Although she presented with a dense deficit (MMT 1/5 deltoid, 3/5 biceps), she achieved full neurologic recovery with conservative management. Published series report generally favorable outcomes, with more than 70% of patients recovering within 6 months[6], although recovery timelines may vary and range from 1 month to 1.5 years[15]. In the study by Thompson et al, management strategies ranged from observation to formal therapy, and outcomes included complete resolution in just over half of patients, partial recovery in one quarter, and persistent symptoms in less than one-fifth[15]. These data help contextualize our patient’s favorable course and shows that even profound initial weakness can improve meaningfully over time.

Conclusion

This case illustrates an unusual instance of delayed C5 palsy following thoracolumbar deformity surgery in a patient with a rigid, previously fused cervical spine. Although C5 palsy is classically associated with cervical procedures, this report suggests that global alignment changes in long-segment constructs can influence cervical nerve root physiology in select patients with prior cervical fusion. Thorough evaluation to exclude metabolic, vascular, and compressive causes is essential. This case broadens the clinical context in which C5 palsies may arise and highlights the need for further studies into how global spine alignment and pre-existing cervical rigidity may interact to affect cervical nerve root function.
