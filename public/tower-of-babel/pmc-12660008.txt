Skin and Soft Tissue Mucormycosis in Patients Receiving Bruton Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors: Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Eduardo Aparicio-Minguijón, Nieves López-Muñoz, Jorge Boán, María Asunción Pérez-Jacoiste Asín, Javier de la Serna, Ana Pérez-Ayala, Ana Belén Enguita, Isabel Rodríguez-Goncer, Mario Fernández-Ruiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKis) have been associated with an increased risk of invasive fungal disease, with skin and soft tissue mucormycosis accounting for a minority of cases. We present a 72-year-old patient with chronic lymphocytic leukemia who developed necrotizing fasciitis and myositis due to Lichtheimia spp after 4 months of therapy with the second-generation BTKi zanubrutinib, with a fatal outcome despite aggressive treatment. Our literature review identified 7 additional cases of BTKi-associated skin and soft tissue mucormycosis, revealing an all-cause mortality rate of 37.5%. Two-thirds of the patients had a previous trauma or skin lesion.Keywords: Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, infection, skin and soft tissue mucormycosis, zanubrutinib

Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKis) have emerged in recent years as promising therapies for B-cell malignancies such as chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and certain types of lymphomas [1]. Hypogammaglobulinemia, immune impairment due to clonal lymphocyte proliferation, and cytotoxic chemotherapy often lead to profound immunosuppression in patients with CLL that can be exacerbated by BTKis [2]. Treatment with ibrutinib, the first approved BTKi, was first associated with an unexpectedly high incidence of invasive fungal disease (IFD), particularly central nervous system aspergillosis [3, 4]. Cumulative use over the past years, however, has led to a more accurate risk assessment [5]. The occurrence of IFD has been also reported with novel second-generation BTKi agents, such as zanubrutinib, although the experience is still limited [4, 5]. Skin and soft tissue (SST) mucormycosis represents a minor proportion of these cases [5]. We present the second reported case of cutaneous mucormycosis in a patient receiving zanubrutinib and review the available literature regarding mucormycosis with primary involvement of SST structures associated with BTKi therapy.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 72-year-old male patient was diagnosed with CLL with high-risk cytogenetic features (del[17p]) 4 years before the current episode. He initially received ibrutinib therapy for 2 years, which was interrupted due to intolerance. Treatment was then switched to rituximab and venetoclax for 2 further years, achieving complete remission. Disease relapse was detected 4 months prior to admission, and therapy with rituximab (375 mg/m2 of body surface area once weekly for 4 doses) was reinitiated with the addition of zanubrutinib at a dose of 160 mg twice a day.

His medical history was notable for chronic heart failure, atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and interstitial lung disease (nonspecific interstitial pneumonia due to overlap syndrome of systemic sclerosis and polymyositis), as well as a latent tuberculosis infection for which the patient had received isoniazid 10 years prior to the current episode. The chronic treatment included antimicrobial prophylaxis with double-strength cotrimoxazole (160/800 mg 3 days a week) and acyclovir (800 mg once a day [QD]). He was not taking any other immunosuppressive agent.

The patient initially presented with a recent-onset history of low-grade fever, marked peripheral edema, and a painful erythematous plaque on the right leg. He denied any apparent trauma or arthropod bite. He was diagnosed with decompensated heart failure and bacterial cellulitis and treated with furosemide and antibiotic therapy (vancomycin followed by oral linezolid), with rapid clinical response. Therefore, he was subsequently discharged. However, the patient returned to the emergency department 5 days later due to worsening dyspnea, cough, and persistent edema and pain in his right leg. Physical examination revealed significant edema and a discolored plaque, which was mildly painful on palpation. Polymerase chain reaction on nasopharyngeal swab was positive for respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and the chest x-ray revealed no lung infiltrates. He was admitted with a presumptive diagnosis of acute heart failure decompensation, RSV respiratory tract infection, and recurrent cellulitis. Broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy with meropenem and linezolid was initiated, as well as treatment with bronchodilators, methylprednisolone, and ribavirin. Zanubrutinib was discontinued.

Seventy-two hours after admission, the patient developed fever, and his clinical condition deteriorated, with a marked worsening of leg pain. The plaque on the right leg turned violaceous, and intense pain was noticed upon palpation (Figure 1). Computed tomography scanning showed extensive inflammation of the subcutaneous tissue and signs of myositis, with fluid collection in the semimembranosus muscle (Figure 2). The patient underwent emergent surgical debridement due to the clinical suspicion of necrotizing fasciitis. Intraoperative findings included devitalized tissue and necrosis in the anterior and lateral compartments of the leg. The patient required mechanical ventilation and vasopressors in the early postoperative period, and he was admitted to the surgical intensive care unit.

Violaceous lesion in the pretibial and lateral regions of the right leg that had evolved from the previous erythematous plaque.

Computed tomography scan reveals inflammation of the subcutaneous fat and muscular tissue of the right leg with fascial involvement and fluid collection in the semimembranosus muscle (A, B) and myositis of the anterolateral leg compartment (C).

Microscopic examination of the surgical samples revealed numerous fungal structures with wide, nonseptate hyphae consistent with mucormycosis (Figure 3). Liposomal amphotericin B (7.5 mg/kg QD) and isavuconazole (200 mg 3 times a day) for 48 hours, followed by 200 mg QD, were added to the patient's treatment regimen. Fungal cultures eventually identified Lichtheimia spp in several surgical samples with no bacteria isolation. A second intervention was performed within the next 48 hours, revealing progressive expansion of necrosis into all muscle compartments, which led to an above-knee amputation. Despite intensive vasopressor and renal replacement therapy, the patient developed acute respiratory distress syndrome and died 24 hours later due to irreversible multiorgan failure. Histopathologic examination of all tissue samples from the amputated limb confirmed the presence of muscle and fascia necrosis with fungal structures that invaded the blood vessels. These findings were consistent with necrotizing fasciitis and myositis due to mucormycosis.

Laboratory findings: A, direct microscopic examination of the surgical samples shows wide nonseptate hyphae; B, fungal culture under magnifying glass examination. Histopathologic findings of amputated tissue samples show muscle and fascia necrosis with fungal structures in granulation tissue (C) and fungal vascular invasion (D).

LITERATURE REVIEW

We conducted a PubMed search from inception to 16 July 2025 for reports of SST mucormycosis in patients receiving BTKi therapy. The following MeSH terms were used: (“Mucorales Infection” OR “Infection, Mucorales” OR “Mucormycoses” OR “Mucormycosis” OR “Zygomycosis”) AND (“Bruton Tyrosine Kinase inhibitor” OR “BTK inhibitor” OR “Ibrutinib” OR “Acalabrutinib” OR “Zanubrutinib” OR “Tirabrutinib” OR “Orelabrutinib” OR “Pirtobrutinib”). The search results were manually screened to identify those cases with primary SST involvement. References of the selected articles and those cited in selected reviews and meta-analyses [4, 5] were scrutinized for any potentially related study. Since the first report from Castro in 2016 [6], we identified 7 further cases [7–12]. The clinical and microbiological characteristics and outcomes of the previous case reports and the present one are summarized in Tables 1 and 2.

DISCUSSION

The increased susceptibility to fungal pathogens is increasingly recognized in patients undergoing BTKi therapy. Following the initial reports of cerebral aspergillosis in the phase 1b clinical trial of ibrutinib for central nervous system lymphoma [13], a wider range of pathogens and clinical presentations has been documented [2–5]. As Bruton tyrosine kinase predominantly mediates B-cell function, the pathophysiology of ibrutinib-associated IFD was initially thought to be related to the off-target inhibition of other kinases, such as TEC kinases or the epidermal growth factor receptor, impairing the function of macrophages and neutrophils [14]. However, the occurrence of IFD has also been reported with second-generation BTKis, such as acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib, which have a more specific affinity for the Bruton tyrosine kinase [1, 15]. As recently suggested, this kinase appears to play a role in innate immunity and neutrophil function, beyond its well-established function in B-cell activation and differentiation [16].

The most commonly reported IFDs associated with BTKi therapy are invasive candidiasis, invasive aspergillosis, and Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, with an estimated weighted prevalence of 3% in a recent meta-analysis [5]. Mucormycosis is a life-threatening IFD caused by filamentous fungi belonging to the order Mucorales, which typically affects patients who are severely immunocompromised [17]. Since rituximab-induced B-cell depletion and the humoral immune impairment observed in patients with CCL are not among the factors recognized to increase the susceptibility to mucormycosis, patients receiving BTKi therapy should now be considered an emergent at-risk group [4, 5]. In a systematic review, Gibert et al found that mucormycosis accounted for 10.9% of cases of IFD in hematologic patients receiving BTKi therapy and 11.6% in the specific subgroup of patients with CLL [5].

BTKi-associated mucormycosis has mainly been reported with ibrutinib, with only 1 previous case in a patient receiving zanubrutinib [12]. To our knowledge, ours is the second reported case of zanubrutinib-associated mucormycosis with primary SST involvement and the first with microbiological documentation.

Around 20% of cases of mucormycosis primarily affect the skin and skin structures, particularly in the setting of health care–associated infections or following high-energy penetrating trauma [17–19]. Skin involvement as the initial manifestation is rare in hematologic patients (<10% of cases) and is more common in the presence of disseminated infection [17]. Nguyen et al recently performed a systematic review that yielded 15 cases of mucormycosis in patients receiving BTKi therapy, with different forms of presentation that included pulmonary, central nervous system, rhinosinusal, and disseminated infection [8]. Of note, they reported a higher-than-expected proportion of SST involvement, accounting for over a third of the cases [8].

The present case exemplifies the most severe form of the clinical spectrum of SST mucormycosis, with the development of necrotizing fasciitis and myositis (Table 1). His clinical presentation was similar to that reported by Maggioni et al [12]: both patients had CLL, were in their seventh decade of life, presented with concurrent respiratory symptoms, and had a fatal outcome despite receiving antifungal therapy and surgical debridement.

Our patient corroborates the male predominance observed in this entity (87.5%). Most cases (75.0%) were associated with ibrutinib therapy, whereas 2 patients (25.0%) were receiving zanubrutinib at infection onset. We identified no cases with other BTKis, such as acalabrutinib or pirtobrutinib, which might suggest agent-specific differences in the risk or simply reflect more limited experience of use. The median time from BTKi initiation to the diagnosis was 20 months, a later onset as compared with BTKi-associated aspergillosis. Gibert et al reported a median interval of 17 and 3 months to the diagnosis of mucormycosis and other types of IFD, respectively, a difference that achieved statistical significance (P < .001) in their systematic review [5]. Only 37.5% of patients were concomitantly receiving corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents. A previous trauma or predisposing skin lesion was reported in two-thirds of the cases (62.5%). In the remaining patients, the pathogenic mechanism was assumed to be hematogenous or dissemination from a cutaneous entry point that remained undetected. Three patients (37.5%) developed respiratory symptoms, which were mainly attributed to other causes (Table 2). In our patient, respiratory symptoms were thought to be related to RSV infection. However, dissemination of IFD could not be completely ruled out, as chest computed tomography was not performed due to his rapidly worsening condition with clinical instability.

Most patients received antifungal therapy with amphotericin B associated with a broad-spectrum triazole (either in combination or sequentially). Surgical debridement was performed in 37.5% of cases, likely reflecting a delayed diagnosis or a poor patient condition that prevented intervention. Six patients (75.0%) achieved clinical cure, while 3 (37.5%) died, with 2 of these deaths attributed to IFD. This relatively favorable outcome in comparison with other IFDs is in line with previous series of posttraumatic mucormycosis in predominantly nonhematologic patients [19]. The figure was similar to the overall survival rate (65.2%) found by Gibert et al for the spectrum of BTKi-associated mucormycosis [5].

Our study contributes to the existing literature—including 2 recent reviews [5, 8]—by providing additional information of the differential impact of newer agents, such as zanubrutinib, on the risk of IFD. Further studies are needed to assess the incidence of mucormycosis in patients receiving the most selective second- and third-generation BTKis (eg, pirtobrutinib), as cumulative experience will emerge during the next years. We also offer a detailed description of an uncommon and often unrecognized form of mucormycosis with primary SST involvement, in contrast to the overall epidemiology derived from clinical trials and cohorts that often lack clinical granularity [5]. In our opinion, the reported prevalence of BTKi-related IFD (<5%) is not high enough to recommend universal antifungal prophylaxis [5]. However, this approach should be considered in patients with CCL with additional risk factors, such as neutropenia or chronic corticosteroid therapy [3]. Regardless of the actual magnitude of this association, the dismal prognosis observed justifies an aggressive diagnostic and therapeutic approach. Any patient undergoing BTKi therapy with new-onset cutaneous lesions should be promptly evaluated and sampled for histopathologic and microbiological examination, including direct microscopy. To avoid rapid progression of infection, the threshold for initiating mold-active antifungal therapy should be low, as well as for early surgical debridement [20]. Despite none of the patients in our review being treated with an immune checkpoint inhibitor, its adjuvant role in SST mucormycosis is promising, with higher potential benefit in patients who are immunocompromised [21].

In conclusion, SST mucormycosis is an emergent complication associated with BTKi therapy. This form of IFD has been reported with ibrutinib and zanubrutinib. Since early diagnosis is crucial to improve the otherwise dismal outcome, a high index of suspicion should be maintained by including mucormycosis in the differential diagnosis of patients with CCL who are taking BTKis and presenting with cutaneous lesions, even in the absence of other classical risk factors.
