Immunomodulatory and regenerative effects of human umbilical cord MSC-EVs in Cutaneous Leishmaniasis

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from human umbilical cord Mesenchymal Stem Cells (hUCMSC-EVs) are emerging as promising immunomodulatory agents in inflammatory disorders. However, their potential for infectious diseases remains poorly explored.MethodsWe investigated the effects of hUCMSC-EVs in experimental Cutaneous Leishmaniasis (CL) caused by Leishmania braziliensis using both in vitro and in vivo approaches. EVs were isolated and characterized according to MISEV guidelines. Their immunomodulatory properties were evaluated in murine macrophages, and their therapeutic efficacy was tested in a preclinical model of CL.ResultsTreatment with hUCMSC-EV modulated the phenotype of L. braziliensis-infected bone marrow-derived macrophages toward a mixed M1/M2 profile, enhancing TNF-α and IL-10 secretion and reducing intracellular parasite load. Protemic analysis of the EV content was correlated with response to wounding and wound healing biological processes, which were confirmed in scratch and cell migration assays. In vivo, intravenous administration of hUCMSC-EV to infected mice reduced lesion thickness and collagen deposition at the infection site.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that stimulation with hUCMSC-EVs enhanced Leishmania killing, inducing the upregulation of immune mediators. hUCMSC-EVs also promoted wound closure and keratinocyte migration. In vivo, intravenous injection of hUCMSC-EVs impaired lesion development, supporting their potential as an adjunctive therapy for CL. This study lays the groundwork for further investigation into MSC-EVs in host-directed therapies for infectious diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04759-8.

Introduction

Immunotherapy using Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) has emerged as a promising approach for tissue regeneration, owing to their immunomodulatory and regenerative properties [1]. These biological properties of MSCs are attributed to soluble factors and Extracellular Vesicles (EVs) present in their secretome (rev in [2]). Depending on their size and biogenesis, EVs are classified into microvesicles, apoptotic bodies, and exosomes [3]. They are defined as lipid bilayer–delimited particles released by MSCs [4] that are loaded with bioactive molecules, such as peptides, mRNAs, and growth factors [5], and participate in processes including cell-to-cell communication [6]. Regarding clinical applications, EVs offer advantages over MSCs, given their low immunogenicity, stability in circulation, and ability to cross biological barriers [7]. Moreover, EVs can be engineered, allowing for modification/enrichment of their internal content to enhance the therapeutic potential [8, 9].

The therapeutic potential of MSC-derived EVs has been investigated in various models of inflammatory diseases. In a type 2 diabetes mellitus animal model, treatment with hUCMSC-derived exosomes significantly reduced blood glucose levels, β-cell apoptosis, and serum TNF-α levels, while improving insulin sensitivity [10]. Similarly, Xin Chen et al. (2025) reported that human Umbilical Cord MSC-EVs (hUCMSC-EVs) alleviated hyperglycemia, restored glucose tolerance, and downregulated the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [11]. Moreover, topically injected hUCMSC-EVs accelerated wound healing by reducing TNF-α levels, promoting M2 Polarization of macrophages, and enhancing angiogenesis [12]. Employing a colitis model, treatment with hUCMSC-EV inhibited weight loss and preserved tissue integrity while reducing inflammatory cell infiltration, TNF-α, and IL-1β levels [13]. Building on this, Bihua Wu and collaborators demonstrated that reduced IL-1β levels were associated with the inhibition of inflammasome activation, a key pathway implicated in tissue damage [14]. Finally, we have demonstrated that hUCMSC-EVs produced under GMP conditions reduced alveolar damage and pulmonary inflammation in a model of LPS-induced acute lung injury [4]. Therefore, these studies demonstrate that MSC-EVs protect tissue integrity in various inflammatory diseases by downmodulating Th1-associated cytokines, supporting their potential as immunomodulatory and therapeutic agents.

Cutaneous Leishmaniasis (CL) is an infectious disease caused by Leishmania parasites, which are transmitted by insect vectors. CL manifests as skin ulcers at the site of parasite inoculation by the sand fly. In CL, pathogenesis is driven by the strong inflammatory response that develops, and hallmarks include the intense production of IFN-γ and TNF, key cytokines involved in macrophage activation and parasite killing [15, 16]. In CL caused by Leishmania braziliensis, CD8 + T cells contribute to the pathogenesis through cytotoxic activity, which leads to tissue destruction and the development of particularly disfiguring ulcers [17]. Since a strong immune response is associated with disease severity in CL, using host-directed immunomodulatory therapies represents a potential alternative or adjunct to chemotherapy [18]. In this context, MSC-based therapy has been evaluated in experimental CL with mixed results, ranging from lesion improvement and cytokine modulation to increased parasite load, depending on the study design and administration route [19–21]. However, the potential of EVs derived from MSCs in CL remains unexplored. Given their lower immunogenicity, stability, and ability to cross biological barriers, MSC-EVs may represent a safer, more targeted alternative to whole-cell therapy.

In the present work, we have explored the effect of EVs derived from MSCs in an experimental model of CL. In vivo, inoculation of hUCMSC-EVs significantly decreased the disease burden without changing the parasite load. Our findings also demonstrate that exposure of Leishmania-infected macrophages to hUCMSC-EVs reduces the parasite load in vitro and modulates cytokine production. Together, these findings support the potential OF hUCMSC-EVs as an adjunctive therapy for CL.

Materials and methods

Production of hUCMSC-derived EVs

MSCs were isolated from Wharton’s jelly of human umbilical cords following standard protocols [4, 22]. MSCs were expanded in low-glucose DMEM (ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with 3% human platelet lysate (hPL), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 200 mM L-glutamine (all from ThermoFisher Scientific). Cultures were maintained up to passage 3 and subsequently plated at passage 4 for experimental analyses at 4000 cells/cm2 density. Characterization of the MSCs included flow cytometric immunophenotyping, tri-lineage differentiation assays, G-banding karyotype analysis, mycoplasma testing, and assessment of endotoxin levels, as described elsewhere [22].

Passage 4–5 MSCs were cultured for 48 h in low-glucose DMEM without hPL for EV production. The conditioned medium was then collected, and EVs were isolated by sequential centrifugation and filtration, following the protocol detailed in [4]. EV characterization, including nanoparticle tracking analysis, transmission electron microscopy, and evaluation of exosomal markers, was performed as previously reported [4, 23] and are provided in Supplemental Material 1.

Cell culture

HaCaT (immortalized human keratinocytes) were cultured in 5% CO2 at 37 °C in regular Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM—Gibco), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μL/mL streptomycin (Thermo Scientific) [24].

Parasite culture

Leishmania braziliensis parasites (strain MHOM/BR/01/BA788) were grown in Schneider’s insect medium (Sigma Aldrich) supplemented with 20% heat-inactivated FBS, 2 mM glutamine, 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 µL/mL of streptomycin (all from Thermo Scientific) at 26 °C. Stationary-phase Leishmania promastigotes were used in experiments. To confirm the stationary phase, parasite numbers were monitored daily, and morphology was evaluated. After 5 days, stationary-phase Leishmania promastigotes were used in the infection of cells and animals [25]. L. braziliensis GFP⁺/LUC⁺ promastigotes (1 × 105/well) were incubated with hUCMSC-EVs (50 µg/mL) in 96-well plates to assess cytotoxicity. After 24 h, luminescence was measured using the One-Glo™ Luciferase Assay (Promega). Controls consisted of parasite cultures grown in the absence of EVs. Cultures were performed in triplicate.

Animals

Six- to eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were obtained from the IGM-FIOCRUZ animal facility, where they were maintained under pathogen-free conditions. Mice were housed in a temperature-controlled environment (23 ± 2 °C) with free access to food and water ad libitum under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. All experimental animal procedures followed the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 and were conducted according to the Guidelines for Animal Experimentation established by the Brazilian Council for Animal Experimentation Control (CONCEA). The local Institutional Review Board approved all the procedures involving animals for Animal Care and Experimentation (CEUA) [IGM-FIOCRUZ-013/2022].

Stimulation of bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDM) with hUCMSC-EVs and infection with Leishmania

Bone marrow cells were isolated from BALB/c mice as described [26]. Briefly, the femurs and tibias were aseptically collected. Both epiphyses were cut and the bone marrow was extracted by flushing using a 1-mL syringe fitted with a 27-gauge needle and filled with RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL of penicillin, 100 µL/mL of streptomycin, and 2.5% HEPES (all from Invitrogen) and 30% L929-cell condition medium as a source of granulocyte/macrophage colony stimulating factor [27].

After differentiation, Bone Marrow-Derived Macrophages (BMDM) were seeded at 3 × 105 cells/500 μL/coverslip per well in 24-well plates. BMDM monolayers were washed to remove non-adherent cells and infected with stationary-phase Leishmania promastigotes at a 10:1 parasite/host cell ratio in supplemented RPMI 1640 /well. Plates were incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 24 h. Coverslips were extensively washed to remove non-internalized Leishmania and simulated with hUCMSC-EVs (50 µg/mL) for 48 h. Coverslips were fixed in methanol and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The parasite load was assessed by observing 200 macrophages, and the number of cells with and without Leishmania and the total number of intracellular parasites were counted using optical microscopy (magnification × 1000). Controls consisted of infected cells that were unexposed to hUCMSC-EVs. Alternatively, coverslips were washed after 24 h of infection and cultured in supplemented RPMI 1640 for 72 h. Cells were washed and subsequently cultured in supplemented Schneider’s insect medium for 5 days at 26 °C. The number of promastigotes was determined on day 5 by counting them using a hemocytometer. In some experiments, cells were stimulated with hUCMSC-EVs (50 µg/mL) for 24 h and then infected with Leishmania, as described above. In this case, controls consisted of infected cells unexposed to hUCMSC-EVs.

Cytokine quantification and macrophage phenotype

BMDM were stimulated with hUCMSC-EVs (50 µg/mL) for 24 h and infected with Leishmania promastigotes as described above. Cell culture supernatants were collected after 48 h. Cytokine levels were determined by sandwich ELISA, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen). Alternatively, cells were seeded and stimulated with hUCMSC-EVs (50 µg/mL) for 48 h. Cells were then blocked and permeabilized, followed by incubation with primary antibodies (anti-TNF-α, anti-CD86, anti-IL-10, anti-CD206, anti-arginase, anti-CD165, anti-CXCL9, and anti-CXCL10) for fluorescence labeling. Slides were mounted with Fluoroshield Mounting Medium containing DAPI, followed by image acquisition using the Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope. Fluorescence quantification was carried out on approximately 50 cells per group using Fiji software (Version 2.16.0).

Proteomic analysis

Proteins were extracted in a buffer (4% SDS, 0.1 M DTT, 0.1 M Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, protease inhibitors). Ten micrograms were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE, stained with Coomassie, excised, reduced, alkylated, and digested with trypsin. Peptides were extracted, concentrated, purified (StageTips-C18), dried, and resuspended in 0.1% formic acid, 5% DMSO, 5% acetonitrile. Peptide analysis was performed by LC–MS/MS (Eksigent NanoLC 1D plus, 60 min gradient, 15 cm × 75 μm C18, 3 μm particles) on an LTQ Orbitrap XL ETD (Thermo Scientific), fragmenting the top 10 ions (CID30, 30 s exclusion), and more details in Supplementary File 1.

Differential gene expression analysis

Bulk RNA-seq data from skin biopsies of patients with cutaneous leishmaniasis were retrieved from the GEO repository (GSE214397). Samples were classified as lesion tissue (CL) or healthy intact skin (HS). Raw counts were loaded with gene symbols as row identifiers, and the columns were reordered to match the metadata. Lowly expressed genes were filtered, retaining only those with counts per million (CPM) > 1 in at least the smallest sample group. Filtered counts were normalized using the TMM method (edgeR) and transformed with voom (limma) to model the mean–variance relationship for linear modeling. A linear model was fitted to compare CL and HS samples, and a contrast matrix was used for the comparison between CL and HS. Bayesian statistics were computed using eBayes, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (logFC) > 1. DEGs were compiled for downstream functional analyses and visualization.

Enrichment analysis and protein–protein interaction network

The proteomic data obtained from hUCMSC-EVs, deposited in the PRIDE repository (ID: PXD051984), were subjected to functional enrichment analysis using the g:Profiler tool (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost). A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied to select enriched terms. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING platform [28], with an interaction score threshold greater than 0.9. The resulting network was then visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape.

Migration assay

Cell migration was assessed as described in [29]. Briefly, Keratinocytes (HaCaT) (2 × 105/well) were cultured for 24 h in DMEM medium lacking FBS (Gibco) (starvation). Starved cells were seeded in the top chamber of the transwell insert, while the lower chamber was filled with DMEM supplemented with FBS (10%), 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 µL/mL of streptomycin. Cells were exposed to hUCMSC-EVs (50 µg/mL) for 48 h, detached using trypsin, and placed in inserts containing serum-supplemented medium to allow migration for 5 h. Following migration, inserts were removed, and cells from the upper chamber were collected. Cells that migrated to the lower chamber were labeled with DAPI and counted by confocal microscopy. Controls consisted of cells not exposed to hUCMSC-EVs. Alternatively, HaCaT cells were cultured in supplemented DMEM in 96-well plates (5 × 105 cells/well) for 48 h until confluency. A scratch was inflicted in the cell monolayer using a 10 μL sterile pipette tip, as described with minor modifications [30]. Cells were rinsed gently with PBS to remove any remaining cell debris. Subsequently, cells were exposed to hUCMSC-EVs (50 µg/mL) for 24 h. Controls consisted of cells not exposed to hUCMSC-EV. Wells were photographed before (time zero) and 24 h after the addition of EVs. The scratch area was measured by ImageJ software (ImageJ v1.53).

In vivo treatment with hUCMSC-EVs in a preclinical CL model

BALB/c mice (n = 8) were anesthetized with Ketamine/xylazine (70 mg/kg ketamine + 5 mg/kg xylazine) and were inoculated in the left ear with 105 stationary-phase L. braziliensis promastigotes, using a 27G needle. Ear thickness was measured weekly using a digital caliper (Kroeplin IP65). Three weeks post-infection, infected mice were randomized into two groups (experimental and control). Group allocation was performed using a random sequence generated by Random.org (https://www.random.org/sequences/), which provides accurate random numbers based on atmospheric noise. Mice in the experimental group were injected intravenously with hUCMSC-EVs (60 µg/mL in 100 µL Plasmalyte (Baxter) mice in the control group were injected intravenously with Plasmalyte alone. Ear thickness continued to be monitored weekly. Six weeks post-infection (3 weeks after EV inoculation), mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation. The infected ear and the draining lymph nodes (dLNs) were excised, and parasite load was evaluated by limiting dilution analysis, as previously described [31]. Briefly, for limiting dilution, tissues were homogenized in supplemented Schneider’s medium; eight serial dilutions were plated in 96-well plates and incubated at 26 °C for 10 days. Parasite load was defined as the highest positive dilution. In parallel, dLNs cells were resuspended in RPMI medium supplemented with FBS (10%), 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 µL/mL of streptomycin. Cells (1 × 106) were seeded in 48-well plates and stimulated with Soluble Leishmania Antigen (10 μg/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 h, at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Control cells were left unstimulated. Cytokine production was assayed in culture supernatants by commercial ELISA kits, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Alternatively, six weeks post-infection (3 weeks after EV inoculation), mice were euthanized, and their ears were processed and embedded in paraffin. Tissue Sects. (5 μm) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and analyzed by light microscopy. Sections were also stained with Masson's Trichrome to evaluate collagen deposition.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons between two groups were performed using Mann–Whitney non-parametric t-tests and comparisons among more than two groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc tests to determine which specific groups differ from each other, as described in Figure Legends. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) or standard deviation of the mean (SD), as indicated in figure legends. Analyses were conducted using Prism (V.10.0, GraphPad) and a p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Characterization of hUCMSCs and hUCMSC-EVs

At passage three, hUCMSCs exhibited classic fibroblast-like morphology, and their multipotency was confirmed by successful differentiation into adipocytes (lipid droplets stained with Oil Red O), osteoblasts (mineralized matrix stained with Alizarin Red), and chondroblasts (cartilage matrix stained with Alcian Blue) (Fig. 1A-D). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated robust expression of MSC markers CD90, CD105, and CD73. In contrast, hematopoietic and immune markers (Fig. 1E) and G-banding karyotype analysis did not reveal chromosomal aberrations in the passages used for EV production (Fig. 1F). hUCMSC-EVs were isolated and thoroughly characterized using multiple methodologies. NTA demonstrated a size distribution between 50 and 200 nm, consistent with a population of small EVs, and an average concentration of approximately 3.5 × 101⁰ particles/mL (Fig. 1G, H). A multiplex bead-based immunoassay confirmed the presence of the tetraspanins CD9, CD63, and CD81, which are characteristic markers of EVs. The cytosolic protein syntenin-1, commonly associated with endosomal biogenesis, was also detected, further supporting the vesicular origin. In addition, the adhesion molecule VLA-4, known to be expressed by MSCs and their EVs, was present. By contrast, the negative control marker cytochrome c exhibited only minimal detection, indicating low contamination from cellular or mitochondrial components. Nanoflow cytometry further validated robust expression of the tetraspanins CD63 and CD81, as well as CD90, a marker indicative of MSC origin (Fig. 1J). Transmission electron microscopy revealed vesicles with the typical cup-shaped morphology characteristic of EVs (Fig. 1K). Functionally, hUCMSC-EVs markedly inhibited both the proliferation and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF and IFN-γ) by activated PBMCs, compared to untreated controls, across all concentrations tested (p < 0.001). These findings demonstrate a strong, concentration-dependent immunomodulatory effect (Fig. 1L).

Stimulation with hUCMSC-EVs modulates BMDM responses to Leishmania

A well-defined ulcer with elevated borders and a sharp, crater-like appearance characterizes CL. The lesion results from the immune response to infected macrophages, which harbor and support the replication of Leishmania parasites. We first investigated whether exposure to hUCMSC-EVs induced changes in the response of Leishmania-infected BMDM. Stimulation of BMDMs with hUCMSC-EVs alone significantly increased the production of TNF (Fig. 2A), IL-1b (Fig. 2B), and IL-10 (Fig. 2C). When cells were infected with Leishmania and then exposed to hUCMSC-EVs, TNF production was significantly increased in comparison to infected cells alone (Fig. 2A) and in comparison, to cells stimulated with hUCMSC-EVs alone (Fig. 2A). The production of IL-1β (Fig. 2B) and IL-10 (Fig. 2C) was also significantly higher in infected cells exposed to hUCMSC-EVs. However, for these two cytokines, levels were significantly lower in comparison to cells stimulated with hUCMSC-EVs alone (Fig. 2B and C). hUCMSC-EVs induce a mixed phenotype in Leishmania-infected macrophages

Previous studies have shown that macrophages exposed to MSCs-derived EVs undergo a shift from an M1 to M2 phenotype [33]. To investigate this in the context of Leishmania infection, BMDMs were infected and treated with hUCMSC-EVs, and the expression of M1- and M2-associated markers was evaluated by confocal microscopy. Among M1 markers, CD86 (Fig. 5A) and TNF (Fig. 5B) were upregulated, CXCL10 was downregulated (Fig. 5C), and CXCL9 remained unchanged (Fig. 5D). Regarding M2 markers, CD206 (Fig. 5E) and IL-10 (Fig. 5F) were upregulated, whereas arginase was downregulated (Fig. 5G), and CD163 remained unchanged (Fig. 5H). Overall, hUCMSC-EV treatment induced significant changes in the expression of both M1 and M2 markers, including up- and downregulation (Fig. 5I). Protein–protein interaction networks reveal roles for hUCMSC-EV in wound repair

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the action of EVs in tissue repair, we evaluated the potential interactions between proteins identified in the hUCMSC-EV proteome and genes differentially expressed in lesions caused by Leishmania braziliensis. The protocol for hUCMSC-EV isolation and characterization was detailed in a previous study [4]. A total of 718 proteins were identified in hUCMSC-EVs according to their UniProt accession numbers. Additionally, the complete EV proteome, obtained by mass spectrometry, was made available. Complementarily, Amorim et al. [34, 35] performed transcriptome profiling of CL lesions and identified a set of highly variable genes associated with L. braziliensis infection. We confirmed the identification of 2978 upregulated genes (LFC > 1 and FDR < 0.05) and 2075 downregulated genes (LFC < –1 and FDR < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 1). For our analyses, we specifically focused on upregulated genes. The integration of these datasets provides a robust framework to explore relevant biological processes and potential repair mechanisms mediated by EVs.

Gene ontology (GO) analysis of the hUCMSC-EV proteome revealed key biological processes potentially associated with their wound-healing capacity and immunomodulatory activity (Fig. 6A). Enrichment analysis of the proteome identified a total of 46 non-redundant GO categories, comprising 17 biological processes, 8 cellular components, and 21 molecular functions, along with 30 KEGG pathways. Among the enriched GO terms, “response to stress” and “protein catabolic process” were prominent, encompassing child terms related to wound healing regulation and response to wounding. Additional relevant biological processes included “regulation of immune response” and “defense response” (Fig. 6B). Based on the EV proteomic profile, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis was performed by integrating proteins enriched in wound-healing processes with proteins encoded by DEGs present in CL lesions [35]. The PPI resulted in a network with 401 nodes (EV proteins and genes from CL lesions) and 1184 edges. Considering only high-confidence interactions (score > 0.9), the analysis revealed direct connections between FN1, TIMP1, ANXA1, ANXA2, and SERPINE1 found in EVs and proteins encoded by genes in CL, including COL3A1, COL5A1, and COL5A2, as well as several matrix metalloproteinases (MMP1, MMP2, MMP3, MMP10, and MMP14) involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [36]. Notably, these EV-associated proteins also interacted with TNF and TGFB, key mediators in neutrophil recruitment following platelet activation (Fig. 6C) [37]. Furthermore, interactions involving TNF and IL-10 in CL, as well as proteins such as DCN and UBC in hUCMSC-EVs, were identified. These proteins participate in multiple signaling pathways related to inflammatory responses and ECM organization (Fig. 6D) [38]. hUCMSC-EVs modulate disease development in a preclinical model of CL

Our results demonstrated that huMSC-EVs can reduce parasite burden, modulate immune responses, and promote cell migration in vitro. To investigate whether these effects translate in vivo, we employed a preclinical model of CL [31]. In this model, Leishmania parasites are inoculated into the ear dermis of mice and lesions develop progressively over a course of 2–5 weeks [31]. Thereafter, lesions heal spontaneously and return to baseline levels, as observed prior to infection. While topical delivery can maximize local bioavailability and minimize systemic exposure, intravenous administration an ables EVs to reach the lesion via the circulation and exert broader immunomodulatory effects potentially rebalancing systemic immune responses that influence local pathology, as observed in the central nervous system and other inflammatory models [23]. Therefore, we opted to probe the ability of hUCMSC-EVs to modulate CL development following a single systemic administration.

Three weeks post Leishmania infection, mice received a single intravenous dose of HuCMSC-EVs. Mice were monitored weekly, and three weeks post-inoculation of hUCMSC-EVs, a significant decrease in ear thickness was observed (Fig. 8A). Analysis of ear thickness variation from baseline (Δ) confirmed that hUCMSC-EV administration significantly attenuated disease progression (Fig. 8B). Histological evaluation revealed pronounced inflammation in control mice, which was markedly reduced in hUCMSC-EV–treated animals (Fig. 8C). Discussion

EVs act as natural carriers of bioactive molecules, including microRNAs, cytokines, and growth factors [4]. In this study, we investigated whether MSC-EVs modulate disease development in a model of CL. We observed that EVs isolated from hUCMSCs exert robust immunomodulatory effects in vitro and in vivo, supporting their functional role as mediators of immune regulation [40].

Stimulation of Leishmania-infected macrophages with hUCMSC-EVs led to upregulation in the production of TNF, IL-1β, and IL-10. Macrophages are the primary host cells for Leishmania, and parasite killing occurs through the synergistic action of IFN-γ and TNF, as well as the production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species [32, 41]. Thus, hUCMSC-Evs enhanced the microbicidal function of macrophages, significantly impairing parasite survival, an unexpected finding, associated only with intracellular stages of the parasite, as direct exposure of promastigotes to hUCMSC-EVs did not affect viability.

Exposure of Leishmania-infected macrophages to AD-MSCs did not lead to increased parasite killing. However, higher levels of both TNF and IL-10 were detected [20], suggesting that the killing effect results from the presence of hUCMSC-EVs. Further studies are necessary to investigate whether hUCMSC-EVs interact with the parasitophorous vacuole containing Leishmania and enhance the production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, which are essential for microbial killing. Another question arises from the production of IL-1β observed herein, a hallmark mediator of NLRP3 activation [42]. Indeed, inflammasome-derived IL-1β induces Leishmania killing, mediated by NO [43]. Our results show that stimulation of Leishmania-infected macrophages with hUCMSC-EVs modulates the cytokine response, producing both pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators without a clear polarization towards the M1 or M2 phenotype. We highlight the ability of hUCMSC-EVs to significantly decrease the production of CXCL10, a chemokine associated with the recruitment of activated T cells and crucial in the pathogenesis of CL, as well as an enzyme linked to disease susceptibility, as demonstrated in experimental and human CL [44].

Several abundant extracellular matrix (ECM)-associated proteins were identified in the proteome of hUCMSC-EVs, including TIMP1, TIMP2, FN1, SERPINE1, and ANXA1 [4, 45]. Tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) are well-recognized for their ability to directly inhibit matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), thereby preventing excessive ECM degradation and holding promise as therapeutic agents in tissue repair [46, 47]. SERPINE1, another highly abundant ECM regulator, modulates proteolytic activity by inhibiting plasminogen activators, consequently limiting plasmin-mediated ECM breakdown. Complementing these ECM-related mechanisms, Annexin A1 (ANXA1) exerts potent immunomodulatory effects, promoting the polarization of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This shift, along with the molecule´s ability to enhance cell migration and stimulate tubular structure formation, contributes to the accelerated wound healing observed in animal models [48, 49]. The presence of these proteins in the proteome of hUCMSC-EVs can thus be correlated with the results we obtained regarding wound closure and cell migration.

In vivo, intravenous inoculation of hUCMSC-EVs significantly impaired disease development, without altering the parasite load or the recall cellular response to Leishmania. In situ, overall signs of inflammation decreased (as shown by histopathological analysis of tissue sections) and collagen deposition increased. These results highlight the potential of hUCMSC-EVs to act at distal sites, ameliorating CL. The choice between systemic and topical administration of EVs is a critical consideration in developing effective therapies for localized diseases such as CL. Topical delivery can maximize local bioavailability and minimize systemic exposure but is often limited by barriers to penetration and variable absorption through the skin. Moreover, the literature indicates that the use of Tofacitinib [50] or Anakinra [51] given systemically can decrease pathology in experimental CL. This evidence prompted us to use a similar route to test hUCMSC-EVs. Our results show that systemic administration of hUCMSC-EVs modulated lesions developing at the site of parasite inoculation, without significantly modifying the parasite load. We did not expect parasite load to be reduced following administration of hUC-MSC-EVs, per se. To achieve this outcome, we would likely combine hUCMSC-EVs with a leishmanicidal compound. While we have observed parasite killing upon stimulation of Leishmania-infected macrophages, it is unlikely that such an effect would be recapitulated in vivo, given the complexities of the lesion site, cellular composition, and other factors. Indeed, intravenous administration of hUCMSC-EVs did not modulate cytokine production, unlike in vitro experiments. Our results align with our previous observations that hUCMSC-EVs reduce lung damage and inflammation in a model of LPS-induced acute lung injury [4].

The immunomodulatory effect of MSC-derived EVs on CL has been tested previously, yielding distinct results. Intravenous injection of adipose-tissue MSCs (AD-MSCs) improved wound healing and decreased parasite load in L. major-infected mice [52]. Intralesional injection of MSC also modulated lesion development when given in combination with a leishmanicidal agent [53]. In L. amazonensis-infected mice, however, injection of AD-MSCs partially controlled lesion development, without a significant effect on parasite load [20]. Intravenous injection of bone-marrow-derived MSCs, on the contrary, increased parasite load in this same experimental model [54]. Therefore, the source of MSCs (adipose tissue or bone marrow) and the experimental model of CL employed can yield distinct results. We build on these findings by associating the modulatory effects with EVs derived from human mesenchymal stem cells (huMSCs) instead of MSCs, in the context of Leishmania infection. Notably, the EVs used herein have been shown to reduce lung damage in a model of Acute Lung Injury [4]. While further work is necessary to optimize the route of delivery, we pave the way for the use of hUCMSC-EVs as an immunomodulatory agent, aimed at host-directed therapies in diseases characterized by overt and localized inflammatory reactions.

Conclusion

In vivo, a single treatment with hUCMSC-EVs reduced lesion development, decreased local inflammation, and promoted tissue repair. We unexpectedly found that stimulation with hUCMSC-EVs enhances microbial killing in vitro, a finding that warrants further investigation. This study lays the groundwork for further exploration of MSCs-EVs in host-directed therapies for infectious diseases.
