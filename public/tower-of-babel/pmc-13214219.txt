Spatially fractionated radiation therapy for bulky tumors: a systematic review of clinical outcomes and dosimetric challenges
Authors: R Di Franco, R Mottareale, D Pezzulla, S Mercogliano, V Borzillo, E Scipilliti, G Silvestro, G De Palma, M Serra, G Ametrano, F Buonanno, C Arrichiello, V d’Alesio, M A di Franco, S Cilla, A Cuomo, E Cavalcanti, E Maranzano, C Donati, F Cellini, V Ravo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional radiotherapy for bulky tumors is often limited by normal tissue tolerance, restricting dose escalation and increasing toxicity. Spatially Fractionated Radiation Therapy (SFRT), including GRID and Lattice Radiotherapy (LRT), delivers intentionally heterogeneous dose distributions to overcome these limitations. This systematic review summarizes the clinical application, outcomes, and dosimetric variability of SFRT in the treatment of bulky tumors. A systematic literature search of the MEDLINE database was performed for studies published between January 2010 and June 2025. Eligible English-language studies reporting original clinical data on SFRT, including case reports, case series, retrospective analyses, and prospective trials, were included. Data on patient and tumor characteristics, treatment intent, SFRT technique, clinical response, and toxicity were extracted, and methodological quality was assessed using the ROBINS-I framework. Twenty-nine studies involving 513 patients and 553 lesions were included. The most commonly treated sites were the thorax (28.8%), pelvis (24.7%) and abdomen (23.3%), with sarcomas (48.0%) carcinomas (14.5%) and non–small cell lung cancer (10.4%) being the predominant histologies. SFRT was mainly delivered with palliative intent (216 cases). Tumor shrinkage was reported in approximately 80% of lesions, with consistent symptom relief. Treatment-related toxicity was generally mild, with most adverse events graded as 1–2; severe toxicities (Grade ≥ 3) were rare. Despite favorable clinical outcomes and an acceptable safety profile, substantial heterogeneity in dosimetric parameters, including valley-to-peak dose ratio and fractionation schemes, was observed across studies. SFRT appears to be a feasible and effective option for bulky tumors, particularly in the palliative setting. Introduction

Radiotherapy is the treatment of choice for large tumor lesions, which are often inoperable or poorly responsive to systemic therapies [1–3]. It represents a minimally invasive therapeutic strategy that plays a crucial role in achieving local disease control and improving patients’ quality of life. Advanced external beam radiotherapy techniques, such as Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy (VMAT) and tomotherapy, enable highly conformal dose distributions, thereby enhancing the balance between target coverage and sparing of healthy tissues [4–9]. VMAT allows continuous modulation of beam intensity during the rotation of the linear accelerator, optimizing dose homogeneity while reducing treatment time. Similarly, tomotherapy integrates external beam radiotherapy with a computed tomography–like delivery modality, ensuring accurate dose distribution across large and complex tumor volumes, with optimal protection of organs at risk [10, 11]. Despite their high degree of conformality, conventional treatments of bulky lesions may be limited by normal tissue tolerance, leading to an increased risk of toxicity and compromising local tumor control. These limitations have prompted the development of alternative strategies, such as Spatially Fractionated Radiation Therapy (SFRT), which enables the selective delivery of high doses within the tumor volume, thereby potentially improving therapeutic efficacy without increasing systemic toxicity [12–16]. Historically, SFRT was developed to overcome dose limitations and toxicity particularly cutaneous toxicity-associated with early radiotherapy technologies [17]. With the advent of modern linear accelerators, SFRT has evolved to allow the safe delivery of high doses, thereby improving local control and symptomatic response, especially in the palliative setting [18, 19]. In contrast to conventional radiotherapy, which aims to achieve a uniform dose distribution within the target volume, SFRT deliberately generates a heterogeneous dose distribution within the lesion, characterized by steep gradients alternating between high-dose peaks (so-called “hot spots” or vertices) and low-dose regions (“valleys”) [20, 21]. This approach enables ablative doses to be delivered to circumscribed regions while maintaining lower doses across the remainder of the tumor and surrounding organs at risk [21, 22]. Several radiobiological mechanisms have been proposed to explain the efficacy of SFRT. Therapeutic effects are thought to derive not only from the direct cytotoxic action of high doses, but also from biological responses within the tumor microenvironment [23, 24]. Proposed mechanisms include the “bystander effect” (where irradiated cells transmit damage signals to non-irradiated cells), damage to the tumor microvasculature, and, importantly, the induction of a systemic antitumor immune response [25, 26]. This latter aspect is of growing interest in the immunotherapy era, as SFRT may act as an “immunological primer” (in situ radio-vaccination), enhancing immune system activation against tumor cells both at the irradiated site and at distant locations (abscopal effect) [27, 28]. The SFRT techniques most commonly described in the literature include GRID Therapy and Lattice Radiotherapy (LRT) [12, 22]. GRID Therapy employs a non-uniform dose distribution with a two-dimensional “grid-like” pattern, whereas LRT first introduced by Wu et al. in 2010 [29] represents its three-dimensional evolution, utilizing intensity-modulated beam delivery techniques to generate a volumetric configuration of intratumoral hot spots [12, 30]. Both approaches have shown promising results, with significant symptomatic improvements and volumetric reductions in bulky tumors across various anatomical sites, including sarcomas, non-small cell lung cancer, and head-and-neck carcinomas [13, 15, 31, 32]. Although current evidence is largely limited to retrospective studies and case reports, available data suggest that SFRT is a safe and effective strategy to overcome the clinical challenges associated with large tumors, providing a therapeutic option where conventional approaches would otherwise be ineffective [12, 13]. In 2020, based on a decade of clinical and experimental experience, Wu et al. [21] published a comprehensive review consolidating the dosimetric and physical principles underlying LRT, describing its clinical implementation and providing practical guidelines for its use. As reported by Wu et al. [21], within the context of photon-based radiotherapy although some authors have suggested extending LRT to charged particle beams in hadron therapy [33, 34] stereotactic delivery systems such as VMAT with flattening filter–free (FFF) beams using conventional linear accelerators, or dedicated platforms such as CyberKnife, are generally well suited for LRT treatment planning.

Despite these promising results, the widespread clinical implementation of SFRT faces several challenges, including the requirement for high precision in treatment planning and dosimetry, the lack of standardized treatment protocols across institutions, and an incomplete understanding of the underlying biological mechanisms [13]. The aim of this systematic review is to explore the available evidence in clinical practice on SFRT. By providing a comprehensive overview of associated dosimetric and clinical outcomes, this analysis seeks to establish highlight clinical practice standards and guide future research directions in the management of bulky tumors.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and study selection

A systematic review was conducted to identify and synthesize the available evidences on SFRT. A comprehensive literature search was performed in the MEDLINE database by three authors (RDF, RM and DP), covering the period from January 2010 to June 2025. The search strategy combined the following keywords and Boolean operators: “(palliative OR radioresistant) AND/OR radiotherapy AND (Spatially Fractionated OR Lattice OR GRID)” + “(”Neoplasms”[MeSH] OR Cancer OR Tumor OR Neoplasm OR Malignancy) AND (”Radiotherapy”[MeSH] OR Radiotherapy OR “Radiation Therapy”) AND (”Spatially Fractionated” OR Lattice OR “GRID radiotherapy” OR “SFRT”) AND (Palliative OR Radioresistant OR Advanced OR Bulky)”. In addition, a manual search of the reference lists of all eligible articles, relevant review papers, meeting abstracts, and book bibliographies was undertaken to identify further potentially eligible studies. Studies were considered eligible if they met the following inclusion criteria: publication in the English language, availability of full-text, and reporting original clinical data as case reports, case series, retrospective analyses, or prospective clinical trials. Exclusion criteria were abstracts without full-text availability, letters to the editor, conference proceedings, editorials, expert opinions, narrative reviews without original data, duplicate or overlapping datasets, animal studies, and cellular or preclinical investigations. All retrieved records underwent an initial screening based on title and abstract, conducted by two reviewers (DP and SM) independently, followed by full-text review for final eligibility assessment. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented. Disagreements during the selection process were resolved through discussion. Data extraction was performed using a standardized form by a dedicated team (RDF, RM, DP) and cross verified to ensure accuracy (SM, VB).

Data extraction and quality control

Data extraction was systematically performed using a custom-built electronic spreadsheet designed to capture both clinical outcomes and high-granularity technical parameters. To ensure a rigorous and transparent methodology, a comprehensive framework detailing all extracted variables and their definitions is provided in the Supplementary Materials (Table S1). This structured framework facilitated a consistent, transparent, and replicable synthesis of the heterogeneous clinical and technical data reported across the vertex-based SFRT literature.

Data synthesis and analysis

Due to the substantial heterogeneity in study designs, patient populations, and dosimetric reporting, a formal meta-analysis was not performed. Instead, a descriptive synthesis was conducted to summarize clinical and technical outcomes. Quantitative data, such as tumor volumes, lattice geometric parameters, and dosimetric metrics, were summarized using descriptive statistics, including means ± standard deviation (SD), medians, and ranges. Categorical variables, including clinical response rates and toxicity grades, were reported as absolute numbers and percentages.

Methodological quality and risk of bias assessment

To evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies, a structured risk-of-bias assessment using the ROBINS-I (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions) framework was conducted. For each study, and for each reported outcome (toxicity, local control, and overall survival when available), all seven ROBINS-I domains were systematically assessed: bias due to confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selective reporting. Domain-level judgments were assigned in accordance with ROBINS-I guidance, and an overall risk-of-bias rating was subsequently derived for each outcome.

Review registration

The review was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF), accessible at the following link: https://osf.io/3kyr2/overview?view_only=38873797ec7b47e3a550c33eeb9e54ee (created on 20 April 2026).

Results

Study selection

A total of 1728 citations were identified through the initial database search. After removing duplicates, the titles and abstracts of the remaining records were screened for eligibility. During this phase, studies were excluded for various reasons, including not being in English, being reviews or overviews, focusing on cell lines or animal models, not being related to radiotherapy, not utilizing SFRT techniques, and being pediatric studies. Following the full-text review, a final total of 29 papers were selected for inclusion in this systematic review. Of the 29 studies included, 12 were case reports and 17 were case series. Tumor location and histology

Lesions were most frequently located in the chest (N = 101, 28.8%), followed by pelvis (N = 87, 24.7%), the abdomen (N = 82, 23.3%), head and neck (N = 60, 17.1%) and extremities (N = 21, 6.0%). The largest included cohort largely influenced these distributions, while smaller studies more commonly focused on pelvic, gynaecologic, and head and neck malignancies. The included cases spanned a wide range of histologies, with sarcomas (48.0%), carcinomas (14.5%) and non–small cell lung cancer (10.4%) being the most frequently reported; however, no single predominant primary tumor type was identified across the literature.

Treatment intent and outcomes

Treatment intent was variably reported and was most often palliative or cytoreductive in nature. Response assessment and follow-up were heterogeneous across studies, with radiographic evaluation commonly performed within the first 3–12 months after treatment, although several reports included longer-term outcomes. Among studies reporting imaging response, most treated lesions demonstrated tumor regression or volume reduction, while complete responses were infrequently observed but documented in selected cases. Symptom control was consistently favourable across studies, with several series reporting high rates of pain relief or functional improvement and one large cohort describing rapid symptom relief in 87% of treated patients.

Toxicity

Based on the studies reported, it is possible to estimate the number of high-grade toxicity cases, which generally remained low despite the aggressive treatment approaches. Only one study, by D. Owen et al. [52], reported a Grade 5 toxicity event, which corresponds to an estimated 1 case. The total number of Grade 4 cases across the studies is estimated to be approximately 7. These cases were reported by three different studies: one case of urosepsis in Duriseti et al. [11], an estimated one case in J.A. Knight et al. [42], and a portion of the combined Grade 3 or 4 events reported by Ahmed et al. [36]. Similarly, the total number of Grade 3 cases can be estimated to be between 9 and 14, with the exact number being uncertain due to the combined reporting of Grade 3 and 4 in one study. The contributing cases are from Majercakova et al. [34] (2 cases), Choi et al. [27] (an estimated 5 cases), J.A. Knight et al. [42] (an estimated 2 cases), and the remainder of the events from Ahmed et al. [36].

Treatment delivery platforms

VMAT-LRT represents the predominant modality, implemented in most studies [10, 11, 17, 25, 30–41, 46, 49, 50, 52, 53]. Varian TrueBeam, Trilogy™, and Edge™ linacs were most frequently employed, often combined with RapidArc volumetric arc delivery. Eclipse TPS (versions 13.7–15.6) with AAA or Acuros dose engines was standard for VMAT-LRT planning [15, 16, 22, 30, 35, 45, 46, 48, 51, 52, 55, 57]. Elekta Infinity linear accelerator equipped with Monaco TPS (version 5.11) [47], RayStation [38, 56], and ViewRay MRIdian TPS [40, 43], were also employed. The CyberKnife VSI system was used for SRS/SBRT-LRT approaches [42], employing the Multiplan TPS (Accuray), enabling high-precision robotic delivery for small, discrete lattice vertices. ZAP-X Surgical System was also employed [41]. Across studies, photon energies ranged predominantly from 6 MV FFF to 10 MV, often delivered with multiple arcs (ranging from 2 to 7 arcs). IMRT-GRID techniques were delivered primarily with conventional C-arm linacs [55], often using static MLC-based apertures [31, 32]. GRID delivery incorporated hexagonal or rod-like geometries, with isocenter spacings ranging from 1 to 2 cm, and MLC or proton spot-scanning optimization for uniform peak-valley dose distribution [31, 32, 48, 54–56]. IMRT GRID plans utilized up to six coplanar gantry angles with collimator rotations up to 90°, while proton GRID techniques employed two-step spot optimization to maintain sharp dose fall-off and preserve tubular high-dose regions. Proton-based SFRT, though less frequent [48, 54, 56], utilized single static field therapy with spot-scanning optimization to generate tubular dose regions of approximately 1 cm radius. SFRT geometrical features

SFRT geometrical parameters varied according to tumor volume and planning objectives (Table 4). Across VMAT-LRT studies, spherical vertices were the dominant design. The median number of vertices across these studies was approximately 7 (range 1–18), with diameters ranging 0.8–2 cm (median 1.5 cm; mean 1.2 ± 0.2 cm). Center-to-center spacing was typically 2–6 cm (median 3.5 cm; mean 3.8 ± 1.1), with edge-to-edge distances designed to maintain sufficient valley regions and spare adjacent OARs. Volumes of individual vertices (VTV) were generally small relative to the GTV, ranging from 0.5 cc to 26.5 cc, and VTV/GTV ratios varied between 0.02% and 2.3% [15, 30, 35, 39, 50, 53, 54]. For GRID techniques, geometrical configurations included rod-like patterns with hole diameters 1–1.4 cm, and isocenter spacings of 2–2.1 cm [54, 55]. Notably, the reproducibility of lattice placement was achieved via imaging guidance, including CBCT and SGRT for VMAT-LRT, ensuring accurate alignment of high-dose vertices with tumor subvolumes. Few studies [40, 44, 49] incorporated functional imaging (e.g., ADC maps) to identify hypoxic or radioresistant tumor regions, guiding vertex placement and optimizing oxygen-guided targeting. Adjacent organ-at-risk (OAR) constraints were consistently respected, with minimum vertex-OAR distances maintained at ≥1–2 cm in most cases [30, 50].

Prescription dose and fractionation

An overview of fractionation schedules and corresponding vertex (VTV) and valley/periphery doses per fraction reported in published clinical and preclinical studies is presented in Table 5. Duriseti et al. [16, 52] and Schiff et al. [36] employed VTV doses of 66.7 Gy delivered in 5 fractions, with periphery/valley doses of 20 Gy/5f, highlighting a high-dose, hypofractionated approach targeting the tumor vertices while sparing surrounding tissue. Price et al. [40] confirmed these parameters in MRIdian MR-linac treatments, reporting VTV doses of 66.7 Gy/5f and periphery/valley 20 Gy/5f across two patients. Other VMAT-LRT applications utilized lower doses or single-fraction regimens, reflecting variations in tumor location, size, and prior treatments. Ahmed et al. [54] described VTV doses ranging from 16 to 20 Gy in a single fraction, with subsequent EBRT post-SFRT delivering 40 Gy administered in 10 fractions (median EQD2 α/β = 4, 43 Gy; BED α/β = 10, 56 Gy₁₀, range 12–92 Gy₁₀). Ferini et al. [50] delivered VTV doses of 15–18 Gy in a single fraction, followed by EBRT of 30 Gy (range 22.5–40.05 Gy) across 5–15 fractions. Montero et al. [38] delivered 18 Gy/1f to VTV with pre-SFRT EBRT of 40 Gy/20f, maintaining consistent dosimetric objectives. Furthermore, Amendola et al. [15, 35] consistently employed single-fraction vertex doses of 18 Gy with periphery doses of 3 Gy, while in a retrospective study in 2020 [51] they prescribed 24 Gy/3f to the VTV and 9 Gy/3f to the periphery. This approach was reclaimed analogously by Suarez et al. [37] who used 27 Gy/3f for VTV and 9 Gy/3f for the periphery.

IMRT-based GRID SFRT demonstrates a more uniform single-fraction approach, with I. Choi et al. [32] delivering up to 20 Gy in 1 fraction, while Snider et al. [31] and Knight et al. From a statistical perspective, across all VMAT-LRT studies, the median VTV dose was approximately 18–66.7 Gy, with the most commonly reported regimen being 66.7 Gy/5f in large tumor targets. Single-fraction VTV doses ranged from 15 Gy to 27 Gy, with standard deviations difficult to calculate precisely due to heterogeneous reporting and small sample sizes, but indicative of considerable institutional variability. Periphery/valley doses ranged from 3 Gy/1f to 30 Gy/5f, with median values clustering around 20 Gy/5f. EBRT adjuvant or interdigitated treatments displayed median cumulative doses of 30–50 Gy, with extremes from 9 Gy to 83.5 Gy in single cases, reflecting individualized dose adaptation.

Dosimetric objectives and parameters

The available literature on SFRT highlights substantial variability in dosimetric parameters across techniques and clinical settings, reflecting the complexity of balancing high intratumoral dose deposition with sparing of surrounding tissues (Supplementary Table S2). Pronounced intratumoral dose heterogeneity was consistently reported and was primarily characterized using PVDR or its reciprocal, VPDR, as well as related percentile-based metrics.

In studies reporting PVDR, values around ~4 were consistently described, including Yilmaz et al. (PVDR 4) [41], Xu et al. and Owen et al. (PVDR ~4.32 using D10%/D90%) [47, 48], while Studer et al. reported PVDR values spanning 30–100% according to their reporting approach [53]. Conversely, studies reporting VPDR showed a wide range of valley-to-peak relationships, from very low values in VMAT-based implementations (Ferini et al.: valley dose ~0.19 Gy; VPDR 0.04) [50] to moderate values around 0.20–0.30 (Hatoum et al.: VPDR 0.20; Montero et al.: VPDR 0.3; Price et al.: VPDR ~0.29–0.30) [38–40]. Jiang et al. reported VPDR based on a D_valley/D_peak definition of ~25% [42].

Across reports, target-oriented plan quality metrics generally indicated high coverage with variable conformity and gradients. Yilmaz et al. reported CI ~1.1 and HI ~1.2 with a GI of 6.9 [41], while Duriseti and Kavanaugh et al. observed V95%Rx near 100% with a higher gradient index (V50%Rx/V100%Rx ~20.65) [52]. Consistently high coverage was also reported by Price et al. (V95%Rx ~99–100% for both vertex and valley structures, CI ~1.2) [40], whereas lower conformity was described by Ferini et al. (CI ~ 0.74) in association with very low valley doses and VPDR [50]. Dose heterogeneity within boosted subvolumes was further reflected by reported dose–volume points, including Jiang et al. (VTV D98% ~21 Gy vs. D5% ~27.9 Gy) [42] and Montero et al. (vertex maxima ~23 Gy with low-dose regions reflected by GTV_lattice D99% ~1.7 Gy and inter-sphere doses in the 6–8 Gy range) [38]. Peak doses substantially exceeded mean target doses in some implementations (e.g., Grams et al.: GTV mean 6–7.9 Gy with GTV maximum up to 31.2 Gy) [22].

Biological/summary dose descriptors, when reported, also varied widely. Owen et al. reported EUD values of 3.6 Gy and EQD2 up to 76.3 Gy [48]. In combined SFRT + EBRT strategies, Amendola et al. reported higher VTV/GTV dose ratios (up to 2.2) and EQD2 values approaching ~115 Gy for VTV in some cohorts [15], with additional series reporting BED values > 90 Gy₁₀ and EQD2 summaries (e.g., mean EQD2 ~79.5 Gy) alongside concurrent reporting of peripheral PTV doses and selected OAR dose summaries (e.g., rectum/bladder maximum per fraction) [51], and BED values up to 120 Gy in other reports [35]. Delivery and efficiency parameters were variably provided, including beam-on times of 6–11 minutes in Grams et al. [22] and longer delivery times with MU reporting in Price et al. [40], as well as MU/Gy reporting in Duriseti et al. [52].

OAR reporting was heterogeneous but generally demonstrated feasibility of respecting constraints across sites. Detailed thoracic OAR metrics were provided by Iori et al. (e.g., lung V20 ~1.3%, spinal cord Dmax ~16.9 Gy, esophagus Dmax ~24.9 Gy) [30], while site-specific constraints and achieved limits were reported in individual series (e.g., Jiang et al.: spinal cord max 2.67 Gy [42]; Montero et al.: spinal cord max ≤ 18 Gy with multiple abdominal/thoracic constraints reported [38]; Dincer et al.: liver, stomach, kidneys, and bowel metrics reported across cases [43]). Hatoum et al. additionally emphasized that vertex dose escalation was confined to a small fraction of GTV (e.g., V12 Gy ~1.9% of GTV) [39].

Overview of reporting consistency across SFRT studies, highlighting the emerging role of LRT

To systematically characterize heterogeneity in reporting across the current SFRT literature, a comparative evidence table was developed to summarize key clinical, imaging, physics, and treatment planning parameters reported in published studies of GRID and, more prominently, LRT. The methodological framework used to construct this table was informed by the consensus-based approach described by Mayr et al. [58], which outlines a structured procedure for literature review, parameter extraction, and evidence tabulation in SFRT research. Following a similar strategy, available clinical series were reviewed and data were extracted across predefined domains considered critical for the evaluation of SFRT protocols, including baseline clinical assessment, toxicity and follow-up reporting, patient-reported outcomes, imaging modalities, functional imaging techniques, dosimetric descriptors (including PVDR/VPDR), geometric lattice configuration, contouring strategies, and treatment planning methodology. Given the rapid expansion and increasing clinical adoption of three-dimensional LRT techniques within the broader SFRT spectrum, particular emphasis was placed on parameters specific to lattice design and implementation. The resulting Table 6 provides a structured synthesis of reporting consistency across studies and identifies current gaps that may warrant standardization in future SFRT investigations.

Methodological risk-of-bias assessment

To evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies, a structured risk-of-bias assessment was conducted using the ROBINS-I framework (Supplementary Table S3). For each study and for each reported outcome (toxicity, local control, and overall survival, when available), all seven ROBINS-I domains were systematically appraised: bias due to confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selective reporting. Domain-level judgments were assigned in accordance with ROBINS-I guidance, and an overall risk-of-bias rating was subsequently derived for each outcome.

The ROBINS-I assessment of studies investigating LRT reveals a coherent methodological profile consistent with both the developmental stage of this innovative radiotherapy technique and the predominantly palliative clinical settings in which it has been applied. As summarized in the evidence table, the overall risk of bias was generally rated as Moderate for toxicity outcomes and Critical for efficacy outcomes, particularly local control and overall survival. This distribution reflects the nature of the available evidence and the inherent challenges of evaluating emerging therapeutic strategies based primarily on early retrospective experiences.

Future perspectives: ongoing clinical trials in SFRT/LRT

To address the current scarcity of high-level evidence, a targeted search of the ClinicalTrials.gov database was performed using ‘lattice radiotherapy’, identifying several ongoing prospective trials (Table 7). A significant shift in research focus is evident, with the initiation of the first randomized Phase III trial comparing LRT to conventional radiotherapy for palliation (NCT07444775, reg. date: 2026–03-03) and multiple Phase II trials integrating patient-reported outcomes (PROMs) and translational immunomodulatory assessments (NCT07000162, reg. date: 2025–06-02; NCT06416007, reg. date: 2024–05-16; NCT05837767, reg. date: 2023–05-01). Furthermore, the scope of SFRT is expanding beyond palliative debulking. Ongoing investigations include MRI-guided focal lattice boosts for prostate cancer (NCT02307058, reg. date: 2014–12-03; NCT06111313, reg. date: 2023–11-01) and response-adapted induction chemo-immunotherapy combinations for head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (NCT07428148, reg. date: 2026–02-23; NCT07335380, reg. date: 2026–01-13). These studies will be instrumental in defining the optimal biological dose and the synergistic potential of SFRT with modern systemic agents.

Discussion

Clinical and technical implementation

The collected clinical evidence, encompassing both traditional GRID and contemporary LRT techniques, supports SFRT as a feasible and generally well-tolerated modality for the management of bulky, anatomically complex, and clinically challenging tumors. Series consistently report clinically meaningful tumor regression and rapid symptomatic relief, often surpassing conventional EBRT results despite the delivery of lower mean doses to the GTV. This suggests therapeutic mechanisms beyond classical dose-response relationships, likely involving microvascular injury, tumor microenvironment modulation, and antitumor immune activation—factors of high relevance in the immunotherapy era. Overall toxicity remains favorable, with most adverse events limited to Grade 1–2, although Grade ≥ 3 events have been described, such as severe cutaneous toxicity in head and neck tumors [32] and postoperative complications after neoadjuvant GRID boosts [31], underscoring the need for meticulous patient selection, individualized planning, and prudent incorporation of SFRT within multimodality strategies to mitigate risk. Consequently, SFRT is rarely deployed as a stand-alone intervention and is typically integrated as a boost or debulking component with EBRT or systemic therapy, showing partial remission rates often exceeding 50%.

Despite these favorable outcomes, substantial heterogeneity persists across delivery platforms (VMAT, IMRT, SBRT, protons) and fractionation schedules. Currently, fractionation varies from single fraction to hypofractionated regimens, with a mean of 2.3 ± 1.8 fractions, reflecting tumor volume, anatomical site, and prior irradiation history. Specifically, VMAT-based LRT characteristically applies high-dose vertex targeting with median doses per fraction to the vertex/VTV of 15 Gy (range 8–20 Gy; mean 15.0 ± 3.8 Gy), with cumulative vertex doses reaching up to 66.7 Gy over five fractions, while sparing peripheral/valley regions (median 4 Gy per fraction; range 3–6 Gy; mean 3.9 ± 0.9 Gy) to preserve normal tissue tolerance and generate steep intratumoral gradients. Most protocols integrate SFRT with EBRT delivered either before or after lattice treatment, tailoring cumulative doses to site-specific constraints and prior therapy. Stereotactic precision, achievable with both robotic and LINAC-based systems, ensures high conformity and safe delivery in anatomically critical locations. Technically, GRID SFRT employs a non-uniform grid-like pattern of high-dose peaks and low-dose valleys to treat bulky, radioresistant tumors, often in palliative scenarios where it may trigger bystander and abscopal phenomena [59]; in contrast, LRT offers greater geometric flexibility to stimulate immune-mediated effects. It embeds high-dose spherical vertices within a lattice, maintaining safe distances from organs at risk (OARs) through tumor-geometry–adapted reticular [60] or alternative diamond-shaped arrangements [52]. LRT planning commonly specifies three dose levels: peak (10–20 Gy), valley (minimum within lattice), and a peripheral tumor dose (usually < 3 Gy). Following the 2024 Radiosurgery Society (RSS) Working Group clinical and dosimetric guidelines for LRT implementation [59, 61], optimization goals include high PVDR (50–80%) and VPDR values around 0.29 using SRS/SBRT-capable systems such as VMAT-FFF and CyberKnife. Finally, Tomotherapy-based LRT (Tomo-LRT), leveraging helical delivery, may further enhance spatial dose modulation for large lesions. However, while tomotherapy-based GRID SFRT has been explored previously, Tomo-LRT remains novel, with initial feasibility data by Seol et al. [62] providing early support for further clinical investigations. These advancements reinforce SFRT’s dual objective: achieving highly heterogeneous, biologically optimized dose deposition in tumor subvolumes while preserving normal tissue integrity through precise modulation of fractionation and spatial doses according to the patient’s clinical history.

Dosimetric discussion

The dosimetric evidence reinforces that SFRT is a family of implementations rather than a single standardized technique. Across published experiences, spatial modulation is consistently achieved, but the magnitude of modulation and the way it is quantified differs substantially. This is particularly relevant for PVDR/VPDR reporting, where the same conceptual endpoint (peak–valley separation) may reflect different constructs depending on whether it is defined geometrically, derived from DVH percentiles, or normalized to dose prescription or subvolume averages [38, 40–42, 47, 48, 53]. As a consequence, modulation ratios are informative within a given planning framework but risk being misleading for cross-study comparisons unless definitions and sampling methods are harmonized.

A second theme emerging from the dosimetric synthesis is that high target coverage is frequently feasible even with strongly heterogeneous intratumoral dose distributions; however, the cost function used in planning differs across studies, leading to variable trade-offs among conformity, gradients, and valley doses. Plans often meet high-coverage metrics while exhibiting different levels of spatial sharpness or spill [41, 52]. Importantly, approaches that drive valley dose very low can coincide with reduced conformity in some reports [50], suggesting that maximal heterogeneity is not automatically equivalent to optimal plan quality, but rather represents one end of a planning spectrum. This supports the interpretation that SFRT planning is intrinsically multi-objective: the intended biological effect of peaks/valleys must be balanced against geometric deliverability and the robustness of dose fall-off, particularly for large or irregular targets.

The reviewed studies also highlight a recurring challenge in SFRT interpretation: dose escalation is often described using non-equivalent descriptors (peak dose, mean tumor dose, vertex dose, DVH percentiles, EUD, BED, EQD2), which do not scale linearly across techniques [15, 22, 35, 38, 42, 48, 51]. High intratumoral maxima can coexist with modest mean target doses, and biological conversions (BED/EQD2) are heavily influenced by fractionation and α/β assumptions [15, 35, 48, 51]. From a comparative standpoint, this means that SFRT “intensification” should be discussed with explicit reference to the dose construct used: peak-driven descriptions capture spatial escalation, whereas EUD/EQD2/BED are more interpretable for global tumor burden and combined-modality regimens. Without this clarity, different studies may appear concordant while actually describing different therapeutic intents.

With respect to normal tissue exposure, reported OAR outcomes are generally compatible with the feasibility of delivering SFRT while respecting common constraints, including in anatomically complex thoracic and abdominal settings [30, 38, 42, 43]. Nevertheless, OAR reporting is itself heterogeneous, ranging from detailed multi-structure DVH endpoints to selective constraint-based summaries. This inconsistency complicates efforts to relate specific modulation regimes (e.g., very low vs moderate valley dose) to clinical toxicity risk. While confining escalation to small boosted subvolumes is a pragmatic risk-mitigation strategy [39], broader comparability requires consistent, site-relevant DVH reporting linked to clinical outcomes.

Taken together, these observations support the need for standardization in SFRT dosimetric reporting to enable meaningful synthesis across studies and to accelerate clinical translation. A pragmatic minimum dataset would include: a formal PVDR/VPDR definition (including sampling method), absolute peak and valley doses (not ratios alone), vertex geometry (size/spacing and boosted volume fraction), a consistent set of coverage and gradient metrics with clearly defined denominators, and a core group of OAR DVH endpoints tailored by anatomical site. Such harmonization will distinguish genuine technique-dependent differences from reporting conventions, facilitating future efforts to correlate spatial modulation with clinical efficacy and toxicity profiles.

Delivery systems/TPS/planning strategies

SFRT has been implemented across a broad spectrum of delivery platforms and planning environments, underscoring both its technical adaptability and the heterogeneity of contemporary practice. The reviewed literature indicates that VMAT-based lattice treatments are predominantly delivered on conventional linacs (notably Varian TrueBeam equipped with high-resolution MLCs) often using 6–10 MV FFF photon beams. Planning is commonly performed in Eclipse (Acuros XB or AAA engines) and supported by structured pre-treatment verification (e.g., EPID-based checks, ArcCHECK, and phantom measurements) consistent with established QA expectations including AAPM TG-218–oriented criteria [22, 46, 48].

To address complex geometries and large targets, several authors have successfully employed multi-arc and non-coplanar strategies with couch rotations, reflecting a deliberate use of beam arrangement to preserve dose gradients and conformality without compromising deliverability [16, 52]. Beyond conventional linacs, robotic radiosurgery platforms, notably CyberKnife systems, have also been employed to exploit highly flexible beam incidence for spatial modulation in anatomically constrained settings. Several reports even describe mixed-platform utilization (e.g., TrueBeam and Elekta linacs combined with CyberKnife) to tailor delivery to patient-specific geometry and institutional capability [42, 44]. Beyond contemporary VMAT/SRS infrastructures, SFRT has also been implemented using conventional high-energy linacs and physical GRID blocks, as well as multi-isocenter lattice sphere techniques, illustrating that the core SFRT concept is not confined to a single technology class [31, 32, 41]. Emerging platforms—including MR-guided linacs such as MRIdian—extend SFRT feasibility into workflows with superior soft-tissue visualization and the potential for online adaptation, delivered via step-and-shoot or pseudo-arc strategies, while proton-based approaches have been explored using static-field delivery, further emphasizing modality-level diversity [40, 43, 59].

Regardless of the system chosen, planning strategies converge on common principles: integrating SFRT with conventional EBRT through single-fraction or hypofractionated regimens to compartmentalize dose into high-dose vertices (or peaks) while sparing valley/peripheral regions to sustain steep intratumoral gradients while maintaining organ-at-risk tolerance [15, 35, 37, 51]. In this context, stereotactic-level precision, achieved through image guidance (CBCT-based IGRT, SGRT or MRI), functions as an enabling requirement rather than a technical refinement. Because geometric uncertainty directly threatens the intended peak–valley architecture, platform selection, TPS algorithms, QA rigor and arc/beam arrangement should be interpreted as interdependent elements of a single implementation pipeline aimed at reliably delivering heterogeneous, biologically motivated intratumoral dose distributions.

From feasibility to standardization: reporting challenges in lattice radiotherapy

Across the studies reviewed, the application and reporting of lattice and SFRT radiotherapy techniques demonstrate substantial heterogeneity, underscoring the need for methodological standardization within the field. While baseline clinical assessments and post-treatment toxicity monitoring are generally well documented, ensuring adequate characterization of patient status and early safety signals, other key domains remain inconsistently addressed. Notably, patient-reported outcomes are largely absent, limiting the capacity to evaluate the patient-centered impact of treatment. Imaging modalities—both pre- and post-treatment—are commonly reported but often without sufficient detail to enable reproducibility or cross-study comparison. Furthermore, the integration of functional imaging into lattice design is infrequently described, despite its potential to enhance biologically guided treatment personalization. The limited incorporation of functional imaging into lattice design may be partly attributable to patient selection and disease stage. In many reported experiences, candidates for LRT present with advanced disease and are treated with palliative intent, a clinical context in which advanced functional imaging is often unavailable, not routinely indicated, or impractical to obtain. This may partially explain the paucity of reports integrating biologically informed imaging into lattice planning, despite its recognized potential to support treatment personalization. Although geometric fixed lattice approaches are more consistently reported, dosimetric metrics such as the PVDR/VPDR, essential for understanding dose heterogeneity and its biological implications, are often missing or only partially detailed. Contouring strategies also exhibit variability in reporting, reflecting a lack of consensus on target and subvolume delineation. By contrast, planning techniques tend to be clearly documented, likely due to their centrality in radiotherapy workflow and established institutional protocols. Overall, the pattern of reporting reveals strong emphasis on clinical feasibility and treatment planning, yet limited attention to biological optimization and patient-reported outcomes. Addressing these gaps through standardized reporting frameworks and prospective incorporation of functional imaging and QoL measures will be critical for advancing evidence-based implementation of LRT.

Implications of the risk-of-bias assessment

Across the included studies, bias due to confounding and selection of participants emerged as the principal contributors to elevated overall risk-of-bias ratings for efficacy endpoints. This pattern is expected in the context of Lattice based-SFRT, which has been predominantly employed in patients with large, heterogeneous, and clinically complex tumors, frequently after multiple prior treatments or within multimodality therapeutic regimens. Patient populations treated with LRT typically represent subsets with limited standard therapeutic options, substantial tumor burden, or urgent symptomatic needs. Consequently, baseline prognostic variables—such as performance status, tumor biology, prior therapies, and metastatic burden—are intrinsically imbalanced and only infrequently controlled for in the available studies. Given that most reports consist of uncontrolled observational series or single-institution cohorts, the ability to attribute survival or local control outcomes directly to the lattice intervention remains inherently limited. Accordingly, confounding for efficacy outcomes was consistently rated as Critical, and selection bias as Serious, across nearly all studies.

In contrast, toxicity outcomes demonstrated a more favorable and consistent risk-of-bias profile, generally within the Moderate range. Several factors contributed to this comparatively lower risk. Acute toxicity outcomes are less sensitive to baseline prognostic heterogeneity and are more directly related to treatment delivery parameters, including dose, fractionation, and normal tissue exposure, which were typically well documented. In addition, reporting completeness was adequate in most series, and classification of interventions was uniformly reliable due to the technical specificity of SFRT planning and delivery. Although the absence of blinded outcome assessment represents a potential source of measurement bias, toxicity grading relies largely on standardized clinical criteria and objective clinical findings, thereby mitigating the impact of non-blinded evaluation. As a result, the robustness of toxicity reporting appears proportionally greater than that of survival or tumor control endpoints.

A further consistent observation across studies concerned the classification of interventions, which was uniformly rated as Low risk of bias. This reflects the well-defined dosimetric and geometric characteristics of SFRT techniques—such as vertex diameter, center-to-center spacing, and peak-to-valley dose gradients—which were clearly described across reports. Similarly, the extent of missing data was generally limited for both toxicity and clinical outcomes, particularly during early follow-up, despite the retrospective design of most cohorts.

The ROBINS-I evaluation should be interpreted within the broader clinical and methodological context of LRT as an emerging spatially modulated radiotherapy approach undergoing early clinical adoption. The existing literature represents foundational clinical experience aimed at establishing feasibility, acute safety, and preliminary antitumor activity. The predominance of uncontrolled series, small patient cohorts, and palliative treatment indications is an expected and largely unavoidable characteristic of early-phase clinical implementation. Consequently, the high risk of bias observed for efficacy outcomes does not necessarily indicate methodological inadequacy, but rather reflects the intrinsic limitations of exploratory and compassionate-use evidence in a rapidly evolving field.

Taken together, the ROBINS-I findings indicate that the current evidence base provides a reliable initial characterization of LRT toxicity, supported by consistent reporting and low-to-moderate risk-of-bias ratings. In contrast, estimates of survival and local control should be interpreted with caution, not due to a lack of promising early results, but because the methodological structure of existing studies does not yet permit causal inference. These observations underscore the need for prospective clinical trials, harmonized reporting standards, and controlled comparative study designs, which will be essential to robustly quantify the therapeutic benefit of LRT beyond the encouraging preliminary signals reported to date. In summary, the ROBINS-I analysis reflects the anticipated methodological landscape of an emerging radiotherapy technique, characterized by reproducible safety findings, early but methodologically constrained efficacy observations, and a clearly defined pathway for future high-quality evidence generation.

Evolving landscape: rationale and clinical implications

The necessity of this systematic review is underscored by the rapid evolution and growing clinical adoption of SFRT. While recent literature has addressed specific aspects of the field, such as focused meta-analyses on LRT [63, 64] or technical evaluations of automated planning tools [65], the present work provides a more comprehensive integration of the SFRT landscape. By encompassing both GRID and LRT techniques, this review offers a holistic perspective on spatially fractionated approaches for bulky tumors. Furthermore, with the inclusion of 29 studies and a total of 553 lesions, this analysis represents one of the largest clinical syntheses to date, providing a robust evidence base that significantly expands upon earlier, more circumscribed reports. A distinctive aim of this study is the systematic correlation between clinical outcomes and dosimetric variability within the current SFRT literature. This dual focus bridges the gap between technical planning challenges and clinical safety, uniquely complemented by a comprehensive mapping of the ongoing investigative pipeline identified via ClinicalTrials.gov. By synthesizing retrospective safety profiles with emerging prospective protocols—including the first randomized Phase III and immunotherapy-combination trials—this review provides updated insights through June 2025 that are fundamental for the development of standardized, evidence-based protocols.

Conclusions

Collectively, this systematic review delineates both the reproducible strengths and the current evidentiary constraints of SFRT for bulky tumors: across heterogeneous implementations, SFRT appears technically deliverable and generally well tolerated, with consistent early signals of rapid symptom relief and tumor regression that warrant further investigation, yet efficacy estimates remain non-causal given the predominance of confounded, non-comparative cohorts. By identifying the principal sources of clinical and dosimetric heterogeneity—particularly inconsistent PVDR/VPDR definitions, variable vertex geometry reporting, and non-uniform OAR DVH endpoints—this work may provide a pragmatic framework for harmonized reporting and trial design. Prospective, standardized studies integrating core dosimetric metrics, patient-reported outcomes, and explicit dosimetry–toxicity/response linkage are now required to determine the true magnitude of benefit and to optimize SFRT integration within multimodality care pathways.
