Relationship between Lumbar Spinal Movement and the Location of Type 1 Modic Changes in Elite Athletes
Authors: Saori Soeda, Masatoshi Morimoto, Takafumi Ohshima, Junzo Fujitani, Takayuki Kitahara, Daiki Nakajima, Naoto Ono, Yuji Nagao, Kosuke Sugiura, Hiroaki Manabe, Fumitake Tezuka, Sudharshan Tripathi, Koichi Sairyo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction Type 1 Modic change (MC1) is strongly associated with chronic low back pain, but it is unclear whether its anatomical distribution reflects sport-specific spinal loading in elite athletes. Methods We retrospectively reviewed elite athletes with chronic low back pain and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-confirmed MC1 who had a history of low back pain for >3 months and concordant pain relief after fluoroscopy-guided discography or selective disc block at the MC1 level. Athletes aged >60 years and those with a history of lumbar surgery, scoliosis >10°, or sagittal translation >4 mm were excluded. Three blinded reviewers classified the dominant spinal movement of each athlete during competition (flexion, extension, right/left lateral bending, or axial loading). The distribution pattern of MC1 (anterior, posterior, right, left, or entire) was determined on short-tau inversion recovery MRI scans. Correspondence was assessed only when all reviewers agreed on the dominant movement. Results Eighteen athletes (19 levels) with a mean age of 34.5 years were included. MC1 (63%) was most frequently observed at L5-S1. MC1 was localized anteriorly in 42%, to the right in 26%, to the left in 16%, and involved the entire endplate in 16%. There were no posterior-only lesions. Rater consensus on the dominant movement was obtained for 10 athletes (involved in seven sports). Nine of these 10 athletes (90%) showed correspondence between the dominant movement and the distribution of MC1, including flexion with anterior MC1 and right lateral bending with right-sided MC1. Conclusions Sports with a single dominant repetitive spinal movement often showed a match between that movement and the location of MC1. This finding supports a focal, load-related mechanism linking sport-specific spinal loading with MC1 in elite athletes with chronic low back pain.

Introduction

Modic change (MC) refers to alterations in the bone marrow at the lumbar endplate and is confirmed on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans1,2). These changes are classified into three types based on their MRI characteristics3). Type 1 MC (MC1), which shows hypointense signals on T1-weighted and hyperintense signals on T2-weighted images, reflects active inflammation and is most strongly associated with chronic low back pain4,5). While traditionally viewed as a feature of generalized degeneration, MC1 is increasingly recognized as a focal response to localized mechanical overuse6-9). Previous studies have shown that MC1 signals are distributed asymmetrically in patients with scoliosis or disc herniation, where mechanical loads are non-uniformly concentrated across the endplate10,11). These findings suggest that specific, localized mechanical triggers determine the anatomical distribution of MC1.

Elite athletes serve as a coherent clinical model for investigating this mechanical pathway due to their repetitive, sport-specific spinal loading12). Athletes exposed to high loads exhibit a markedly higher prevalence of MC1 compared to the general population, with rates reaching up to 46% in sports like weightlifting13,14). This high frequency, often observed in younger populations, underscores the significant impact of professional-level physical demands on the vertebral endplate.

However, the direct relationship between sport-specific movements and the regional distribution of MC1 remains unknown. Although focal mechanical stress is a recognized driver15), it has not been elucidated whether the localized MRI signals reflect the prevailing direction of spinal loading in different sports. Identifying this link is essential to confirm whether focal mechanical overuse is the primary mechanism behind symptomatic MC1 in athletes.

The purpose of this study was to determine whether the dominant spinal movement in elite athletes corresponds to the anatomical distribution of MC1 on MRI. We hypothesized that repetitive, direction-specific loading during competition concentrates stress on discrete endplate regions, triggering the focal inflammation visualized as MC1.

Materials and Methods

Study design and participants (Figure 1)

Study inclusion and analysis flowchart.

The 18 elite athletes included in the study were grouped based on unanimous rater consensus on dominant spinal movement into an Agreement group (n=10) and a Non-agreement group (n=8). Correspondence analysis was subsequently performed only on the Agreement group to assess the match with the MC1 distribution.

MC1: type 1 Modic change

This retrospective cohort study reviewed 18 patients diagnosed with chronic low back pain associated with MC1 between April 2018 and March 2023. We included only elite athletes competing at or above the national level. To verify MC1 as the primary pain generator, all patients underwent fluoroscopy-guided discography or selective disc blocks, and only those with significant, concordant pain relief were included.

Exclusion criteria were age over 60 years, prior lumbar surgery, autoimmune disease, scoliosis (Cobb angle >10°), or lumbar instability (anterior slippage >4 mm). These criteria ensured the analysis focused on sport-specific mechanical stress rather than age-related degeneration or global instability.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data, including age at diagnosis of MC1, sex, type of sport, history of lumbar surgery, Cobb angle on standing lumbar radiographs, and vertebral slippage distance on flexion views, were extracted from the clinical records.

Definition of Frequent spinal movements

For each sport, the most frequent spinal movement was defined by reviewing one complete competition per athlete. Three independent reviewers (two spine surgeons and one sports trainer) who were blinded to all MRI findings classified each athlete's predominant movement into one of the following five prespecified categories: flexion, extension, right lateral bend, left lateral bend, or axial loading. Sports for which all three raters selected the same movement were assigned to the agreement group, and those without concordance were assigned to the non-agreement group. Four movement categories were prespecified: flexion (repeated forward bending), lateral bending (repeated side-bending, specified as right-dominant or left-dominant), extension (repeated backward bending), and axial loading (predominantly vertical loading throughout the spine).

Definition of the distribution pattern of MC on MRI

MC1 was classified as anterior, posterior, right, left, or entire based on its location within the vertebral body as identified on mid-sagittal MRI images obtained at the time of diagnosis (Figure 2). The central planes were used to divide the vertebral body into anterior–posterior and left–right segments.

Analysis of correspondence between the most common movement and the MC1 distribution pattern

This analysis was performed only in the agreement group. Correspondence was considered present when the distribution pattern of MC1 matched the predefined region associated with the most frequent movement identified; specifically, flexion with anterior localization, extension with posterior localization, right lateral bending with right localization, left lateral bending with left localization, and axial loading with involvement of the entire region. Cases in the non-agreement group were not included in this correspondence analysis.

Finite element analysis

A validated nonlinear finite element model of the L4-L5 functional spinal unit was used to simulate endplate stress distributions. The model, constructed using ABAQUS software (Dassault Systèmes, Johnston, RI, USA), incorporated cortical and cancellous bone, an intervertebral disc with a fiber-reinforced annulus, and all relevant ligamentous structures, following previously published protocols16,17). The material properties of each component are summarized in Table 1. The model was validated by comparing its range of motion under a 10 Nm moment with previously reported experimental data18,19). For the current study, von Mises stress distributions on the L4 and L5 endplates were calculated under pure flexion, extension, and lateral bending moments. These results were exported as color maps for qualitative comparison with the clinical distribution of MC1 observed on MRI.

Results

Patient characteristics

The patient demographics and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 2. Eighteen elite athletes (15 men, three women) with 19 affected intervertebral levels were analyzed. The mean age was 34.5 years. All patients presented with chronic low back pain in the absence of radicular symptoms, and none had a history of lumbar spine surgery. The most frequently involved level was L5-S1 (12/19 levels, 63%). Twelve sports were represented: golf, baseball, rhythmic gymnastics, soccer, speed skating, cycling, mixed martial arts, wrestling, volleyball, hammer throw, sumo wrestling, and horse racing jockey.

Definition of frequent spinal movements

The three blinded raters achieved consensus on the most frequent spinal movement for seven of the 12 types of sport (58.3%; Table 3). The agreed-upon dominant movements included flexion (horse racing jockey, Keirin cycling, wrestling, and speed skating), axial loading (sumo wrestling), extension (rhythmic gymnastics), and right lateral bending (dominant-hand golf). Representative cases from this agreement group include a right-handed golfer (Figure 3), a sumo wrestler subjected to axial loading (Figure 4), and a Keirin cycling athlete (Figure 5). The inter-rater reliability for these classifications yielded a Fleiss κ score of 0.57, indicating moderate agreement. Distribution of MC

The distribution of MC1 was as follows: eight levels (42%) in the anterior group, five levels (26%) in the right group, three levels (16%) in the left group, and three levels (16%) in the entire group (Table 2). There were no cases in the posterior group.

Correspondence between frequent movements and distribution of MC1

The three raters reached consensus on the dominant spinal movement in 10 of the 18 athletes. Within this agreement group, nine of 10 athletes (90.0%) showed correspondence between the agreed dominant spinal movement and the predefined MC1 quadrant (Table 3). The concordant cases are summarized as follows. The speed skater showed a flexion-dominant pattern and anterior MC1. The two racing cyclists showed a flexion-dominant pattern and anterior MC1. The sumo wrestler also showed a flexion-dominant pattern and anterior MC1. All three golfers were right-handed and showed right lateral bending and right-sided MC1. The sumo wrestler also showed axial loading with involvement of the entire endplate. The jockey showed a flexion-dominant pattern and anterior MC1. The single non-correspondent consensus case was a rhythmic gymnast in whom the dominant movement was considered to be extension, with MC1 localized anteriorly. Sports that involve heterogeneous, task-dependent mechanics, namely, baseball, soccer, women's volleyball, and hammer throw, did not achieve a higher consensus and were not analyzed for correspondence.

In the L4-L5 finite element model, a pure flexion moment shifted compressive von Mises stress toward the anterior aspect of the inferior endplate, whereas right and left lateral bending increased stresses predominantly on the ipsilateral side. In contrast, near-pure axial loading produced a more diffuse, circumferential stress pattern involving almost the entire endplate. Discussion

Summary of findings

To our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate that MC1, previously regarded as an imaging feature alone, corresponds to the dominant spinal movement performed during sport. Correspondence between the dominant spinal movement and the regional distribution of MC1 was observed in nine of 19 (47%) cases. Notably, within the agreement group, which was defined as athletes for whom all three raters unanimously identified the same dominant movement, nine of 10 (90%) showed concordance between the dominant movement and the regional distribution of MC1. These findings suggest that the anatomical distribution of MC1 tends to mirror the prevailing mechanical demand on the lumbar spine in sports that do not require complex multidirectional movement patterns or multiple positional roles. In contrast, sports that involve complex, task-dependent mechanics or multiple positions seldom yield a stable single identifiable dominant movement and therefore show no consistent mapping.

Methodological considerations for frequent movement and rater reliability

The inter-rater reliability yielded a Fleiss κ score of 0.57, which is considered “moderate agreement.” To maintain high methodological rigor, we restricted our analysis to the “agreement group” in which all three blinded raters reached a unanimous consensus.

The frequent movements defined in this group are strongly supported by established biomechanical literature. Regarding golf, Mefford et al.7) reported that the dominant spinal movement in right-handed professional golfers involves repetitive right lateral bending during the swing, creating asymmetrical stress on the right side of the endplate. Similarly, the classification of “flexion” in road cycling is consistent with Usabiaga et al.20), showing that professional cyclists maintain a sustained, deep trunk-flexed posture. Regarding sumo wrestling, Nakagawa et al.21) documented massive vertical impact forces and repetitive axial loading during the tachi-ai, supporting our consensus on axial loading.

Regarding golf, Mefford et al.7) reported that the dominant spinal movement in right-handed professional golfers involves repetitive right lateral bending during the swing, which creates asymmetrical stress concentration on the right side of the vertebral endplate. Similarly, the classification of “flexion” as the dominant movement in road cycling is consistent with the report by Usabiaga et al.20), which showed that professional cyclists maintain a sustained, deep trunk-flexed posture on the bicycle.

While our findings align with established biomechanical literature, competition-specific movements alone do not represent the total mechanical load experienced by athletes. Although these movements reflect high-intensity loading patterns that likely drive endplate stress, the lack of quantitative data on training volume and variety remains a significant constraint. For instance, the discordant rhythmic gymnastics case suggests that unmeasured training-related movements may influence MC1 localization more than competition-specific extension. Future studies incorporating longitudinal tracking of both competition and practice-related movements are essential to develop more precise predictive models for localized MC1.

Spinal movement and distribution of stress on vertebral endplates

Interpretation of our present findings requires consideration of how spinal movement concentrates mechanical load on specific regions of the vertebral endplate. The finite element model of L4-L5 created at our institution demonstrated that a pure flexion moment shifts compressive von Mises stresses towards the anterior aspect of the inferior endplate, whereas right and left lateral bending increases stresses predominantly on the ipsilateral side; in contrast, near-pure axial loading produces a more diffuse, circumferential stress pattern involving almost the entire endplate.

These patterns are consistent with recent multiaxial finite element analyses of the lumbar spine, which have found that flexion and lateral bending generate the highest von Mises stresses on the superior and anterior regions of the vertebral endplates, with asymmetric ipsilateral loading during lateral bending, while extension redistributes the load away from the endplate towards the posterior bony elements, and axial loading yields more centrally distributed but relatively uniform endplate loading22,23).

On this biomechanical basis, we prespecified our correspondence rules, namely, flexion with anterior MC1, lateral bending with right or left MC1, and axial loading with entire-endplate MC1. The high concordance observed in the agreement group is consistent with the concept that MC1 arises preferentially in regions subjected to peak cumulative endplate stress rather than being randomly distributed. It is noteworthy that no posterior MC1 was observed among the athletes in this study. Biomechanically, lumbar extension shifts the center of pressure posteriorly, but the majority of the resulting compressive load is transmitted to the posterior segments—particularly the facet joints and pars interarticularis—which can bear up to 40% of the axial load in extended postures24,25). We hypothesize that this load-sharing mechanism effectively shields the posterior vertebral endplate from the high-magnitude focal stress required to trigger MC1. Consequently, elite athletes in extension-dominant sports may be more clinically predisposed to facet arthropathy or spondylolysis rather than posterior endplate lesions.

Consideration of other mechanical mechanisms underlying type 1 MC

Other mechanical factors, such as degeneration, slippage, and scoliosis, are believed to be among the leading etiologies of MC. Mu et al.15) investigated 263 patients with MC and demonstrated significantly greater angular and translational motion on flexion–extension radiographs in segments with MC1 and MC2 than in those with MC3, indicating that these types of lesions are closely associated with segmental instability in the lumbar spine. Li et al.26) examined 626 patients with lumbar spondylolisthesis and found that MC were more frequent in isthmic spondylolisthesis than in degenerative spondylolisthesis and that the prevalence of these changes increased with slip grade and intervertebral disc degeneration, indicating a stress concentration mechanism at the slipped level. Wu et al.11) found that MC was significantly more prevalent in patients with degenerative lumbar scoliosis than in controls without scoliosis and tended to localize on the concave side at the curve apex, where asymmetric loading is maximal. However, all athletes in the present study were younger than 50 years and had no radiographic scoliosis, spondylolisthesis, or obvious dynamic instability, suggesting that such gross mechanical pathologies contributed little to the localized MC observed in our cohort.

Future perspectives for exercise therapy

Accurate identification of the “pain generator” is essential for the management of low back pain in elite athletes. For athletes with MC1, identifying the localized distribution of the lesion allows for a transition from general treatment to mechanism-based rehabilitation. Based on our findings, we have implemented targeted strategies such as movement training with the non-dominant hand to balance trunk loading and motor control exercises based on the Pilates method to improve segmental stability. Early clinical reports suggest that such motor control exercises can effectively reduce pain in patients with MC, potentially by limiting mechanical stress on the affected endplate region27).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the overall sample size was small, as was the number of athletes in each type of sport. Moreover, we did not include athletes with MC1 who were asymptomatic. Investigating both symptomatic and asymptomatic cohorts would be necessary to establish a more definitive causal relationship between repetitive loading and the development of MC1. Second, although the dominant spinal movement was determined through unanimous rater consensus to minimize bias, some degree of subjectivity is inherent in video-based classification. Furthermore, our analysis focused on competition-specific movements, which may not fully account for the total mechanical burden, including the duration, intensity, and variety of movements experienced during daily practice sessions. Future studies incorporating quantitative motion analysis and longitudinal tracking of training volume are essential to refine these associations. Third, we used a single generic L4-L5 finite element model to represent the biomechanics of the lumbar spine. While the L4-L5 functional spinal unit is a widely validated model for standard lumbar biomechanics, it may not fully capture the unique environment of the lumbosacral junction (L5-S1), where 63% of our clinical cases occurred. The lumbosacral region is influenced by specific anatomical factors, such as the iliolumbar ligaments and the sacral slope, which may alter stress distribution compared to mid-lumbar levels. Therefore, our biomechanical findings should be extrapolated to L5-S1 with caution, and future research using level-specific models is warranted. Finally, this study did not include tissue sampling or bacterial culture of the intervertebral disc or endplate. Therefore, the possible role of inflammatory stimuli, such as bacterial infection, in determining the distribution of MC could not be evaluated.

Conclusions

This study suggests that the distribution of MC1 in elite athletes with relatively simple movement corresponds to the spinal movement most frequently performed during sport. This finding may pave the way for the development of prevention programs and rehabilitation strategies for MC1-related low back pain based on the mechanism of movement.
