Intention to quit smoking and associated factors among outpatients at a tertiary hospital in semi-urban Nepal: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Bihari Sharan Kuikel, Archana Shrestha, Bikram Adhikari, Rabin Dhital, Nripa Raj Dangaura, Biraj Man Karmacharya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionQuitting tobacco is challenging and often requires multiple attempts. Effective tobacco cessation programmes depend on understanding the factors associated with the intention to quit in specific contexts. The study aimed to determine the prevalence of intention to quit and its associated factors among people who smoke currently visiting the outpatient department of a tertiary hospital in a semi-urban area of Nepal.MethodsAn analytical cross-sectional study was conducted from 1 May to 30 June 2021, among 375 people who smoke currently selected using purposive sampling from the outpatient department of Dhulikhel Hospital. Intention to quit was assessed using a question on whether participants planned to quit smoking, with response options: (1) within the next month, (2) within the next 6 months, (3) sometime in the future but not within 6 months and (4) not planning to quit. Data on socio-demographic characteristics, nicotine dependence, motivational factors and awareness of cessation aids and services were collected using a standard structured questionnaire. Multivariate logistic regression was used to determine the factors associated with intention to quit smoking.ResultsOverall, 72.5% (95% CI 67.8% to 76.8%) of participants reported an intention to quit smoking, with 64.8% in the pre-contemplation stage, 24.8% in the contemplation stage and 10.4% in the preparation stage. Higher education (high school or above), longer duration of smoking and moderate nicotine dependence were significantly associated with higher intention to quit, whereas having a history of family member smoking was associated with lower likelihood of intending to quit.ConclusionsA substantial proportion of patients reported an intention to quit, with most in the pre-contemplation stage. These findings highlight the need for targeted outpatient cessation strategies, including brief advice from healthcare workers, mobile or telephone-based quit-line services and family-centred educational campaigns, to support quitting efforts and inform culturally appropriate tobacco control interventions in Nepal.Keywords: Cross-Sectional Studies, Epidemiology, Public Health

Introduction

Globally, cigarette smoking remains one of the most significant public health challenges. It is responsible for poor health in millions of people and approximately 8 million premature deaths each year.1 Around 80% of the world’s 1.3 billion tobacco users live in low- and middle-income countries, where limited health services contribute to a substantial burden of tobacco-related illness, deaths and the financial costs.2 In 2018, the economic burden of cigarette smoking in the USA was estimated at over US$600 billion, encompassing healthcare expenditures, productivity losses from smoking-related diseases, premature deaths and deaths caused by secondhand smoke exposure.3

Nepal bears a substantial share of this burden. The 2019 WHO STEPS survey reported that 28.9% of adults aged 15–69 years were current tobacco users in any form (smoking or smokeless).4 About 14.5% of adults (24.2% of men and 5.9% of women) were people who currently smoke cigarettes, while 6.5% were people who smoked previously, including 8.8% of men and 4.5% of women. Tobacco was the third leading risk factor for deaths and disabilities in Nepal in 2021. It was estimated to be linked to approximately 28.1 thousand deaths, representing about 23.7% of all fatalities. Of these tobacco-related deaths, 24 000 (85.7%) were attributed to smoking, while 4500 (16.2%) were caused by secondhand smoke.5 Besides the disease burden, treatment of tobacco-related disease has also put an economic burden on the health services and raised the out-of-pocket expenditure in healthcare.6 Globally, smoking imposes substantial direct and indirect economic costs through healthcare expenditures and productivity losses. The WHO estimates that tobacco-related economic costs amount to approximately US$1.4 trillion annually, equivalent to about 1.8% of global gross domestic product (GDP). In Nepal, the economic burden is also substantial, with losses estimated at approximately NPR (Nepalese rupee) 39.25 billion in 2020, equivalent to nearly 1.5% of the national GDP.7

Quitting tobacco is a challenging process, often requiring multiple attempts on average, 30 times before achieving long-term success. The intention to quit smoking is considered the first step toward cessation, and varies by people who smoke’s characteristics.8 A number of factors affect the intention to quit among people who smoke. Studies have shown that sex, marital status, not smoking to kill time, occupation, family pressure, having a religious affiliation and a higher self-efficacy, having health problems in the past, smoking duration and smoking fewer cigarettes per day, duration of past attempts, outcome expectancy of quitting, worry about future health and overall opinion of smoking were associated with intentions to quit smoking.9–13

International studies show wide variation in intention-to-quit rates. In Vietnam, 50.3% (2020) and 48.4% (2022–2023) of people who smoke currently reported an intention to quit.8,14 In India, rates were 70% in a tertiary care outpatient setting15 and 76.3% in a rural community-based study.10 In South Korea, 61.7% of people who smoke currently reported an intention to quit,16 while in China, 59.60% of the people who smoke visiting cessation clinics planned to quit within the next 7-day period.17 In Nepal, only 19.4% of the people who smoke currently reported a quit attempt in the past year,4 although willingness to quit with support is reported to be as high as 90.5%.18

Despite multiple national policies, such as awareness programmes, product warnings, public smoking bans and quit-line counselling service,19 utilisation of cessation services in Nepal remains poorly documented. Furthermore, little is known about quit intentions among individuals actively seeking healthcare, who may have greater motivation and opportunity to quit.

Dhulikhel Hospital is a non-profit, community-based tertiary care hospital located in Dhulikhel Municipality in central Nepal. The hospital provides outpatient and emergency services for a broad range of diseases and health conditions. It serves a population of approximately 2.5 million people from Kavrepalanchok, Sindhupalchowk, Dolakha, Sindhuli, Ramechhap, Bhaktapur and surrounding districts. In 2023/2024, the hospital provided outpatient and emergency services to more than 500 000 people. The hospital has also provided services to people from more than 50 of Nepal’s 75 districts, reflecting its broad geographical catchment. As a university hospital associated with Kathmandu University, Dhulikhel Hospital provides a range of general and specialised healthcare services.20 The outpatient department (OPD) is often the first point of contact between patients and healthcare providers, offering a valuable opportunity to assess smoking behaviours.21 Patients in this setting may be more receptive to smoking cessation interventions than the general population, as they are already engaged with healthcare services.22 For those who smoke and wish to quit, healthcare providers are well-positioned to initiate and support cessation efforts.23 Understanding quit intentions in this group can provide insights in developing hospital-based interventions and inform broader national strategies. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the prevalence of intention to quit smoking and determine associated factors among the people who smoke currently attending the OPD of a tertiary care hospital in Nepal. The study will not only inform national strategies but also contribute to the global evidence base by highlighting challenges faced in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study design and participants

An analytical cross-sectional study was conducted from 1 May to 30 June 2021 among patients attending the OPD of Dhulikhel Hospital. The study included adults aged 18–65 years who self-reported as a person who smokes currently, defined as having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and at least one cigarette in the past 30 days at the time of interview.24 Individuals classified as people who smoked previously, as well as those unable to participate due to medical reasons such as hearing impairment, critical illness or mental illness, were excluded.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on a previously reported prevalence of 58% of people who smoke currently in Nepal having attempted to quit smoking.18 Using this prevalence (p=58%), the required sample size was determined using the formula:

n = Z²PQ / e²

where Q = 1 − P,

Z=1.96 for a 95% CI, and e=5% (margin of error).

Based on these parameters, 375 participants were required for the study.

Sampling technique

Participants were recruited from the medicine OPD at Dhulikhel Hospital using purposive sampling until the required sample size was reached. Data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire in the Nepali language. Eligible participants were approached by trained research enumerators in the medicine OPD. After providing written informed consent, participants were interviewed using the structured questionnaire and the enumerators recorded their responses. The interviews were conducted in a separate room within the hospital to ensure privacy and confidentiality. Each interview took approximately 20–30 min to complete. Purposive sampling was particularly chosen due to the restricted patient flow caused by the second wave of COVID-19, which limited opportunities for random sampling. All safety precautions were strictly followed throughout the recruitment process, ensuring the safety of both the study team and the patients.

Outcome variable

Transtheoretical Model (TTM) was used to assess the intention to quit.25 The TTM, also known as the stages of behavioural change model, is a widely used framework in smoking cessation that guides interventions based on an individual’s readiness to change.

Intention to quit smoking was assessed by asking the question, ‘Are you planning to quit smoking?’ with the following response options: (a) within the next month, (b) within the next 6 months, (c) sometime in the future, but not within 6 months and (d) not planning to quit. This classification corresponds with the TTM stages: participants not considering quitting within the next 6 months were categorised in the pre-contemplation stage, those thinking of quitting within the next 6 months were in the contemplation stage, and those planning to quit within the next month were in the preparation stage.

For the purpose of analysis, participants who selected options a, b or c were grouped as having the intention to quit (coded as 1), while those selecting option d were categorised as not having the intention to quit (coded as 0).26

Independent variables

The socio-demographic variables consisted of age (18–25/26–35/36–45/46–55/>55 years), gender (male/female), marital status (married/unmarried), ethnicity (Brahmin or Chhetri/Janjati and others), religion (Hindu/non-Hindu), education (no formal education/less than high school/high school or more), occupation (employed/unemployed), residence (rural/urban) and family type (nuclear/joint), history of smoking among family members (yes/no), age of smoking initiation (years) and duration of smoking years).

Nicotine dependence was measured using Fagerström Tolerance Questionnaire27 based on people who smoke’s response to the following five questions: (1) How soon after you wake up do you smoke your first cigarette? (after 60 min (0 point), 31–60 min (1 point), 6–30 min (2 points), within 5 min (3 points)); (2) Do you find it difficult to refrain from smoking in places where it is forbidden? (no (0 point), yes (1 point)); (3) Which cigarette would you hate most to give up? (the first in the morning (1 point), any others (0 point)); (4) How many cigarettes do you smoke per day? (10 or less (0 point), 11–20 (1 point), 21–30 (2 points), 31 or more (3 points)); and (5) Do you smoke more frequently during the first hours after waking than during the rest of the day? (no (0 point), yes (1 point)). A total score for nicotine dependence (Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND)) was created as the sum of the above given points ranging from 0 to 9. The total scores of this tool was interpreted as low dependence (0–2), low to moderate dependence (3–4), moderate dependence (5–7) and high dependence (8–9). For analysis, we considered the score of 5 or higher as high nicotine dependence (≥5), moderate dependence (3–4) and low dependence (0–2).

Motivational variables consisted of five questions adapted from a previous study conducted in China and Africa.13,26 These covered: outcome expectancy of quitting, worries about health damage, favourable attitudes about smoking, smoking as an important part of life and overall opinion about smoking.

Outcome expectancy of quitting was assessed by asking: ‘How much do you think you would benefit from health and other gains if you were to quit smoking permanently in the next 6 months?’ Response options were: not at all, a little, very much and do not know.

Worries about future health were measured with the question: ‘How worried are you, if at all, that smoking will damage your health in the future?’ Response options were: not at all, a little, very much and do not know.

Favourable attitude towards smoking was assessed by asking respondents to rate their agreement with the statement: ‘You enjoy smoking too much to give it up’. Options were: disagree (strongly disagree, disagree), neutral (neither disagree nor agree) and agree (agree, strongly agree).

Importance of smoking in life was measured by asking for agreement with the statement: ‘Smoking is an important part of your life’. Options were the same as above: disagree, neutral and agree.

Overall opinion about smoking was assessed with the question: ‘What is your overall opinion of smoking?’ Response options were: good (very good, good), neither good nor bad and bad (bad, very bad).

Awareness of smoking cessation aids and services was assessed using three questions: whether the respondent had noticed health warnings on cigarette packages (yes/no), was aware of the law banning smoking in public places in Nepal (yes/no) and had heard about tobacco quit-line services (yes/no).4

The questions were translated into Nepali and reviewed for clarity, relevance and cultural appropriateness in the Nepalese context. The translated questionnaire was pilot-tested among a small group of people who smoke to assess the clarity and comprehensibility of the questions. Feedback from the pilot testing was used to refine the wording of the questions before data collection.

Data analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using Stata V.14 (StataCorp). Continuous variables were summarised using the mean and SD, and categorical variables using frequency and percentage. The χ2 test was used to assess the associations between categorical variables. Factors associated with the outcome were examined through multivariable logistic regression, reporting adjusted OR (aOR) with 95% CIs. All covariates were entered simultaneously into the model. Covariates included in the multivariable model were selected based on prior literature, theoretical relevance and variables showing a p value<0.1 in bivariate analysis to ensure important predictors were considered while maintaining statistical rigour. No stepwise approach was used. A p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The overall fit of the models was assessed using likelihood ratio tests and Pseudo R². Potential two-way interactions between key independent variables (such as education×nicotine dependence, worried health×favourable attitude and family smoking×duration of smoking) were also tested to explore whether the effect of one variable depended on another. Variables showing perfect prediction or collinearity were noted and excluded from the model as appropriate. Regarding missing data, variables with missing values were excluded from the analysis to ensure accuracy and consistency of the results.

Patient and public involvement

There was no involvement of the patients and the public in this study.

Results

Prevalence of intention to quit smoking among the study participants

Of the 375 participants, 64.80% of the participants were in the pre-contemplation stage (27.47% with no intention to quit and 37.33% intending to quit sometime in the future but not within the next 6 months). About one-quarter (24.80%) were in the contemplation stage, intending to quit within the next 6 months and 10.40% were in the preparation stage, planning to quit within the next month. Overall, 72.53% of the participants reported an intention to quit smoking (table 1).

Factors associated with intention to quit smoking among the study participants (n=375)

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several factors significantly associated with the intention to quit smoking (table 5). Participants with a high school education or higher had greater odds of intending to quit compared with those with no formal education (aOR=2.34; 95% CI 1.03 to 5.27). A longer duration of smoking was also associated with higher odds of intending to quit (aOR=1.03; 95% CI 1.00 to 1.05) compared with a shorter duration. Regarding nicotine dependence, participants with moderate dependence were more likely to intend to quit than those with low or high dependence (aOR=2.14; 95% CI 1.04 to 4.43). In contrast, history of smoking among family members was associated with lower odds of intending to quit (aOR=0.24; 95% CI 0.13 to 0.44).

Discussion

This study assessed the intention to quit smoking and its associated factors among people who smoke currently visiting the outpatient department of a tertiary hospital in Nepal. In our study, 72.53% of participants expressed an intention to quit smoking. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in the OPDs of a tertiary care hospital in India15 and a community-based study in India,10 which reported intention-to-quit rates of 70% and 76.3% among participants, respectively. However, another community-based study conducted in North India28 reported a lower intention to quit, with 52.4% of people who smoke currently expressing such intent. Similar levels of intention to quit were observed in studies conducted in Spain (45.6%)29 and Vietnam (50.3%).8

The variation in the proportion of people who smoke intending to quit may be attributed to differences in study settings and sample sizes. Additionally, since our study was conducted in a hospital setting, participants may have been more health-conscious due to their existing medical conditions. They may also have received counselling from healthcare professionals, contributing to a higher intention to quit smoking.

Our study found that participants with higher education levels had significantly greater odds of intending to quit smoking. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in India,30 China,31 South Korea32 and Vietnam,8 which also reported a positive association between higher education and intention to quit. This association may be explained by the fact that individuals with higher education are generally more aware of the health hazards associated with smoking and possess greater knowledge about the benefits of quitting. In addition, they are more likely to have better access to information on cessation programmes, policies, campaigns and services.

Another important finding of this study was the lower level of intention to quit among participants with a history of family member smoking. The presence of people who smoke in the household may reduce the likelihood of abstinence among other family members. For instance, children may imitate smoking behaviours of their parents, making it difficult for parents to discourage smoking and, in turn, challenging for them to quit themselves. Moreover, individuals may feel less motivated to quit if they observe family members smoking without experiencing apparent health consequences. In contrast, studies conducted in Iran33 and the USA34 have shown that family members can play a positive role in increasing an individual’s intention to quit. Specifically, household smoking restrictions and expressing negative attitudes toward tobacco use have been found to motivate individuals to consider cessation.

Contrary to our findings, a study conducted in China reported that participants with a longer duration of smoking were less likely to intend to quit.17 The higher level of intention to quit observed in our study could be attributed to greater awareness among long-term people who smoke of their increased risk for various health consequences. Prolonged nicotine exposure may heighten concern about the potential health damage caused by extended smoking duration, thereby motivating a stronger intention to quit.

Similarly, nicotine dependence was found to be a significant factor associated with a higher level of intention to quit. This finding was consistent with a previous study conducted in China17, which reported that participants with moderate nicotine dependence had higher odds of intending to quit compared with those with low dependence. However, another study from China35 and one from India36 found that moderate dependence was significantly associated with a lower intention to quit. This difference may be due to differences in sample sizes and study setting, or the cut-off values used to define dependence levels. In general, heavy nicotine dependence is often linked to a lower intention to quit, as it is characterised by tolerance to nicotine and the likelihood of experiencing withdrawal symptoms during a quit attempt.37 So, individuals with higher nicotine dependence are less likely to intend to quit. In our study, which was conducted in the outpatient setting of a tertiary hospital, participants with moderate dependence may have shown a higher level of intention to quit due to increased self-awareness of their health risks and the benefits of cessation. Moreover, because they were not heavily dependent, they may have been more capable of seeking professional advice and support, further enhancing their intention to quit. These results highlight the importance of considering people who smoke’s levels of nicotine dependence when developing smoking cessation programmes.

Our results also support the growing evidence that the setting can influence the level of intention to quit. In our study, the hospital environment provided an opportunity to enhance participants’ intention to quit smoking by increasing their health awareness and facilitating access to professional support. Similarly, a study conducted in Turkiye38 demonstrated that a smoking cessation intervention delivered in a hospital setting can lead to higher quit rates. These findings underscore the potential benefits of developing hospital-based cessation interventions and integrating them into routine care.

Strength and limitation of the study

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to investigate in depth the intention to quit smoking among the Nepalese population, providing important context-specific insights in a setting where evidence is limited and informing future interventions. Nicotine dependence was measured using a five-item version of the FTND, which, while slightly shorter than the validated six-item version, provides a reliable assessment of dependence in this context. Data were collected using a structured and standardised questionnaire, ensuring validity and precision. The study employed rigorous statistical methods, examining a wide range of factors including socio-demographics, nicotine dependence, awareness of smoking cessation aids and services and motivational variables. The results reflect independent associations after adjusting for potential confounders, which strengthens the robustness of the findings.

The study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish temporality and causal relationships between factors and intention to quit; further in-depth studies are needed to draw conclusions about causality. Second, data were self-reported but collected through an interviewer-administered questionnaire, and this may have introduced social desirability bias, potentially leading to over-reporting of intentions to quit. Third, the study used non-probability sampling. Fourth, it was conducted at a single location, limiting generalisability and the relatively small sample size may reduce precision for some subgroup analyses. Some CIs were wide due to small numbers in certain groups, and these estimates should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, certain potentially relevant variables, such as exposure to media campaigns, prior cessation attempts and access to cessation support, were not assessed. There may also be residual confounding from unmeasured or imperfectly measured factors, which could influence the observed associations. Finally, the validated six-item version of the FTND was not fully administered, as the sixth item was inadvertently omitted. Consequently, the total dependence score was based on five items rather than the standard six, which may affect score range, precision and comparability with other studies using the complete instrument. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Moreover, the psychometric properties and categorisation of the FTND in the Nepalese context are not well established, which may limit comparability of our findings with other studies.

Conclusion

The present study assessed the intention to quit smoking among patients visiting the outpatient department of a tertiary hospital in Nepal. A substantial proportion of participants reported an intention to quit, with most (64.8%) in the pre-contemplation stage, 24.8% in the contemplation stage and 10.4% in the preparation stage, indicating the need for prompt action. Key factors associated with intention to quit included higher education, longer smoking duration, moderate nicotine dependence and absence of a family history of smoking. These findings highlight the need for further research to determine which tobacco cessation approaches are feasible and effective in this setting. This study provides context-specific evidence on factors associated with intention to quit among people who smoke attending outpatient services, which may inform future research and the development of tobacco cessation interventions.
