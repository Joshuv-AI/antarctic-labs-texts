ABSTRACT
PurposeDespite growing evidence that immunosuppression (IS) influences aetiology and prognosis of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), there remains a lack of data from representative multicentre studies.MethodsThe multinational prospective CAPNETZ study includes patients with CAP and HIV since 2017, and with other causes of IS since 2020. We present the first comprehensive analysis of patients with CAP and IS (HIV with low CD4 cells, systemic steroids, immunosuppressive drugs, antineoplastic treatment, neutropenia, primary immunodeficiency, transplantation, haematological neoplasia) included within CAPNETZ until December 2023. IS cases were matched 1:1 with non-IS patients. The primary end-point was 30-day mortality and/or organ replacement therapy. Additionally, cases of CAP caused by non-core respiratory pathogens (NCRPs) according to an international consensus were evaluated. Univariate and multivariate risk factor analyses and evaluation of predictive scores by receiver operating characteristic analysis were performed.ResultsWe included 232 patients with IS, most commonly after antineoplastic therapy (37.5%), systemic steroids (36.2%) and immunosuppressive drugs (35.8%). 10.3% reached the primary end-point. Overall, IS was not associated with prognosis compared to matched non-IS patients, but patients with immunosuppressive drugs had a significantly lower risk. The qSOFA, CRB-65 and CURB-65 scores showed limited prediction (area under the curve 0.57–0.64) and insufficient sensitivity for the primary end-point. CAP caused by NCRPs occurred in 16.4% in IS and was associated with transplantation, previous anti-infective treatment and intensive care unit admission, but SARS-CoV-2, Haemophilus influenzae and Streptococcus pneumoniae were the most common pathogens in both groups.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the relevance and heterogeneity of CAP in IS and highlights the need for individualised risk stratification and treatment.

Introduction

The relevance and clinical heterogeneity of patients with immunosuppression (IS) have been increasingly recognised in recent years. While earlier data described a population prevalence of IS of 2–4% [1–5], more recent studies reported an increase of people with IS in the Western population of up to 6–7% in the last decade [6, 7]. In specialised centres evaluating hospitalised patients with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), IS rates of up to 18–24% have been described [8, 9]. Nonetheless, patients with CAP and IS are still insufficiently represented in global guidelines and recommendations due to the extended spectrum of respiratory pathogens, the marked variation in clinical presentation of IS and their limited inclusion in large prospective CAP studies [10–12]. Given the continuous development of antineoplastic therapies, the increasing number of available immunosuppressants and the expanding indications for immunosuppressive and immunomodulatory medications as well as the increasing number of solid and stem cell transplantations, the proportion of people with IS is likely to rise further in the coming years [6, 13, 14].

IS represents one of the most important risk factors for CAP, as well as for CAP-associated mortality [6]. Yet there exists no universal risk stratification strategy for this patient group.

According to a recent international consensus classification, IS in CAP is specifically characterised by a higher risk of infections with so-called non-core respiratory pathogens (NCRPs), which are not covered by empirical anti-infective treatment recommendations for CAP [11]. In this context, the specific cause of IS is crucial for the individually expected spectrum of respiratory pathogens [12, 15, 16]. Furthermore, recent data showed a higher risk for severe respiratory insufficiency in CAP caused by specific NCRPs in patients with IS [17].

Together, these data demonstrate the urgent need for individualised risk stratification and empiric treatment recommendations based on high-quality multicentre data in patients with CAP associated with different causes of IS. Therefore, we conducted the current study, which provides the first comprehensive analysis of all patients with CAP and IS from the multicentre, multinational, prospective CAPNETZ cohort.

Methods

Study cohort and definitions

Since April 2001 the multinational prospective CAPNETZ study (study registration NCT 02139163) prospectively collects data on patients with CAP in >40 local clinical centres in central Europe (https://capnetz.de).

Criteria for inclusion in CAPNETZ are age ≥18 years and radiologically and clinically confirmed pneumonia; a detailed description of the CAPNETZ methodology is published elsewhere [18, 19].

Since January 2017, HIV-infected patients with or without associated IS, and since April 2020, patients with all other forms of IS have also been eligible for inclusion.

IS within CAPNETZ is defined according to the criteria of Ramirez et al. [11] as presence of at least one of the following criteria: - systemic steroid therapy with ≥20 mg prednisone equivalent for >4 weeks or ≥5 mg prednisone equivalent for >6 months;- antineoplastic therapy within the past 3 months (excluding immune-checkpoint inhibition monotherapy and/or exclusive anti-resorptive therapy for bone metastases) or within the last 12 months in case of B-cell depletion treatment;- neutropenia defined as a neutrophil count <1000 µL−1;- IS according to stem cell or solid organ transplantation;- active haematological neoplasia;- primary immunodeficiency disease (PID);- intake of biologicals or other immunosuppressive drugs within the past 3 months (or within 12 months in case of B-cell depleting agents; excluding biologicals for bronchial asthma); or- HIV infection with a documented CD4 cell count <200 cells·μL−1 and/or percentage <14%.Documented data of all cases included within the CAPNETZ database with the above-defined label of IS were individually rechecked and classified into IS subgroups according to these strict criteria. Definition of end-points, risk factors, scores and pathogen status

The primary outcome was defined as combined 30-day mortality and/or initiation of any new organ replacement therapy (ORT) during CAP. ORT was defined as noninvasive or invasive ventilation, vasopressor treatment or acute renal replacement treatment. The secondary outcome was defined as 30-day mortality only. Other end-points included the individual ORTs and intensive care unit (ICU) admission.

Evaluated risk factor analysis regarding the primary and secondary end-point included demographic data, the different immunosuppressive conditions as defined above, pre-existing comorbidities, vital and laboratory parameters on hospital admission, previous and/or current anti-infective treatment or prophylaxis, detection of multilobar infiltrates or a fungus-specific infiltrate on chest computed tomography (CT), and respiratory pathogen detection according to the definitions below. Missing values are clearly documented. The vaccination status against SARS-CoV-2 has only been documented since 25 June 2021.

The prognostic qSOFA, CRB-65 and CURB-65 scores were calculated for all patients as described before [20–23]. For score calculation, missing values for urea were set as missing; missing values for respiratory rate or blood pressure were set as negative criterion.

Pathogens were evaluated as core and NCRPs according to Ramirez et al. [11]. Pathogen evaluation for core respiratory pathogens was done according to the established standard operating procedure of CAPNETZ as described before [19, 24, 25]. Every core pathogen was accepted in case of proven or likely pathogen detection. If an NCRP was detected, the relevance of the pathogen during CAP was reevaluated according to the diagnostic criteria shown under “Diagnostic criteria for NCRP” in supplementary Item S1.

In case of a polymicrobial infection with detection of at least one NCRP, the pathogen status was defined as NCRP.

Matching of non-immunosuppressed control cases

For evaluation of risk factors for NCRPs and of prognosis in comparison to a contemporary cohort of CAPNETZ patients without IS, cases with IS were matched with an immunocompetent control group from the same CAPNETZ period. Patients with implausible IS status and/or HIV-infected patients without reduced CD4 cell counts were also excluded from matching to the control group. Cases were matched 1:1 by using exact matching for variables sex and diagnostic quarter of the CAP episode, and nearest neighbour for age and hospitalisation status using R software (R version 4.4.0) (figure 1).

Statistical analysis

For univariate risk factor analyses the Pearson chi-squared test was performed for categorial variables and the Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables, which were presented as median with interquartile range. For risk factors with missing values of <5% or 10% (described in the corresponding table 2, supplementary tables S2 and S10) and which were statistically significantly associated with the corresponding end-point after univariate analysis, a multivariable logistic regression analysis with stepwise forward selection (inclusion level 0.05, exclusion level 0.1) was performed. For statistically significant risk factors the odds ratio (OR) with associated 95% confidence interval (95% CI) was described.

To evaluate prognostic performance of risk scores, receiver operating characteristic analyses (ROC) were performed for the qSOFA, CRB-65 and CURB-65 scores to evaluate their prognostic properties. Sensitivity, specificity, predictive values and likelihood ratios, each with 95% CI, were presented.

Furthermore, a Kaplan–Meier survival curve for 30-day mortality was calculated to compare the immunocompromised and immunocompetent cohort using the log-rank test.

The statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 30.0.0.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc version 23.2.1 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium) software.

The level of significance was stated with a two-sided p-value of <0.05.

Prognostic end-point analyses

Primary end-point

A total of 24 patients (10.3%) experienced the primary end-point of 30-day mortality and/or any new ORT (table 1).

Whereas age was not associated with the outcome (p=0.231), vascular and cerebrovascular comorbidities (p=0.028), being on dialysis (p=0.003), pneumonia-associated confusion (p=0.001) and higher respiratory rates (p=0.006) were significantly associated with the primary end-point.

Regarding underlying immunosuppressive conditions, patients on immunosuppressive medication were fewer among those with the outcome (p=0.039).

The multivariable regression analysis confirmed the relative lower risk for patients after intake of immunosuppressive drugs within the past 3 months (p=0.037; table 2).

However, among patients with >1 positive criterion of any of the scores, the positive predictive value (PPV) for the primary end-point was at least 19% suggesting clinically relevant high-risk prediction (especially for a qSOFA of >1 with a PPV of 40%).

Secondary end-point analysis

Overall, 17 patients (7.3%) died within 30 days. Results of univariable and multivariable analyses and prognostic evaluations of the included scores are shown in supplementary tables S1–3.

The multivariable regression analysis confirmed a negative association for immunosuppressive drugs (p=0.049), and the evaluated scores again showed limited negative predictive values.

Multiple immunosuppressive conditions

48.3% of the patients had two or more underlying IS conditions (supplementary table S4); the combination of active haematological neoplasia with antineoplastic therapy or transplantation, intake of immunosuppressive drugs and steroids were most common.

Patients with multiple IS conditions were significantly more often under anti-infective prophylactic treatment (p<0.001) and had a trend towards a higher NCRP detection and ICU admission (supplementary table S5).

Analysis of CAP with IS compared to CAP without IS

Patient characteristics in comparison to the matched immunocompetent cohort are shown in supplementary table S6. The outcome analysis according to immunosuppressive status showed no significant differences between the two groups for either the primary (10.3% versus 9.9%) and the secondary end-point (7.3% versus 4.3%). This finding is also reflected in the Kaplan–Meier survival curve (figure 2).

Kaplan–Meier curve for 30-day mortality of immunosuppressed patients compared with matched, immunocompetent control group. There were no statistical differences between both groups by calculation using the log-rank test (Mantel Cox) with p=0.165.

In a combined analysis of the total cohort including patients with IS and matched patients without IS, with respect to the primary end-point (supplementary table S7), no significant association was detected between the primary end-point and IS, either overall or in any IS subgroup.

For the secondary end-point (supplementary table S8), only antineoplastic treatment and HIV infection with IS were associated with death, whereas overall IS and all other subgroups were not.

Pathogen analysis

Pathogen detection in patients with IS

In 128 (55.2%) of 232 patients with IS a pathogen was detected (table 4). Of these, 21 cases (16.4%) involved at least one NCRP, most frequently Pneumocystis jirovecii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (figure 3b). Polymicrobial infection was identified in 47 of these 128 patients (36.7%). Among patients with at least one NCRP, 11 individuals (52.4%) also had a co-infection with one or more core respiratory pathogens (supplementary table S9).

When patients with IS were compared according to NCRP detection (table 4), transplantation was the only IS condition significantly associated (p<0.001), especially stem cell transplantation (p=0.001). >50% of all NCRP infections occurred within this patient group and NCRPs accounted for approximately one third (12 out of 33) of all pathogen detections within this patient group. NCRPs were also detected in 22% of patients receiving immunosuppressive drugs. Another risk factor for NCRP-associated CAP was any documented treatment for an infection within the past 12 months (66.7% versus 34.0%, p=0.006), which remained the only significant risk factor after multivariable regression analysis (supplementary table S10).

Regarding treatment outcomes, CAP caused by NCRPs showed a trend towards a poorer prognosis and was significantly associated with ICU admission (28.6% versus 9.3%, p=0.015; table 4).

Pathogen detection in patients with IS compared to matched patients without IS

When pathogen detections were compared between CAP patients with and without IS, detection of NCRPs was clearly associated with IS (9.1% (n=21) versus 0.4% (n=1), p<0.001). Polymicrobial infections were identified in 20.3% (n=47) of patients with IS and 16.8% (n=39) of patients without IS.

Core respiratory pathogens were most frequently isolated in both groups (figure 3a). Whereas respiratory viruses were detected more often in patients with IS, SARS-CoV-2 was the most often isolated virus in both groups (36 versus 30 cases).

Regarding bacterial CAP, Streptococcus pneumoniae was the most common Gram-positive core bacterium in immunocompetent patients (n=46) but was detected less frequently in patients with IS (n=26). Gram-negative core bacteria were often detected in both groups, particularly Haemophilus influenzae (nno-IS=40, nIS=38). Atypical bacteria were described less frequently in both groups (nno-IS=17, nIS=11).

Discussion

Our comprehensive evaluation of patients with CAP and IS within CAPNETZ, representing the first analysis of this patient group within this database, revealed the following key findings: 1) Among a wide range of different immunosuppressive conditions, only the use of immunosuppressive drugs was associated with a better prognosis; otherwise, outcomes across different causes of IS were quite similar.2) The commonly used CAP severity scores CRB-65, qSOFA and CURB-65 had a low negative predictive value for excluding poor outcomes in patients with IS, even at low score values, but high PPV for >1 score point.3) Compared with matched patients without IS, a trend towards higher mortality was observed in immunosuppressed CAP patients, but overall prognosis was broadly similar.4) NCRPs caused CAP in 16.4% of patients with IS with pathogen detection (and 9.1% overall), were often isolated together with other respiratory pathogens and particularly frequent in patients after stem cell transplantation or treated for infections within the past 12 months.5) NCRP detection was strongly associated with IS and a higher rate of ICU admission; nevertheless, SARS-CoV-2, H. influenzae and S. pneumoniae remained the most frequently identified pathogens in both groups.In comparison to recent population-based immunocompetent cohorts with CAP [26], our immunocompromised study cohort was considerably younger with a median age of 64.5 years overall and 71 years in deceased patients. Additionally, <1% lived in a nursing home and/or were chronically bedridden. This reflects data from previous publications in IS patients, but also a potential selection bias seen in prospective studies compared to non-selected population-based data [8, 13, 17, 27–29]. Regarding comorbidities, patients with IS in our cohort suffered significantly more often from malignant (56.5% versus 22%) and autoimmune diseases (21.6% versus 8.2%) compared to patients without IS, which seems plausible because of frequently needed treatments associated with IS in this patient group.

Other very recent publications describing IS cohorts with CAP reported mortality rates from 5.2% up to 13.2%, comparable to our rate of 7.3% [13, 27, 28, 30, 31]. A large population-based study from Germany showed that IS is not only one of the most critical risk factors for CAP (HR 2.4, 95% CI 2.3–2.5), but also for subsequent death (HR 1.9, 95% CI 1.8–2.1) [6]. In contrast to this study, we observed only a trend towards a higher mortality for patients with IS with 7.3% versus 4.3%. As predicting and managing critical disease with ORT is of major prognostic impact in CAP with IS [17], we choose a combined severe pneumonia end-point. Whereas recent European retrospective studies involving more selected patient groups after solid organ transplantation reported rates of severe pneumonia of up to 26.1% [27, 30, 31], our rates were lower. This may reflect a selection bias inherent to a prospectively recruited cohort as well as a broader definition of IS in our cohort. Accordingly, we found a significantly lower risk for reaching both the primary (OR 0.192) and the secondary end-points (OR 0.11) in patients with IS due to intake of immunosuppressive drugs, a subgroup that constituted a substantial proportion of our cohort. This complements data from a recent population-based study showing that most causes of IS like antineoplastic treatment, active haematological disease and solid organ or stem cell transplantation were associated with a higher 30-day all-cause mortality, whereas intake of immunosuppressants was not [6]. We can only speculate about the cause of this association. Patients with intake of immunosuppressive drugs were significantly younger than patients with other causes of IS in our cohort (median 61 versus 67 years, p<0.01), but the association remained significant after accounting for age in the multivariable analysis. Potentially, underlying diseases were of minor prognostic impact in patients receiving IS medications compared to the often malignant diseases underlying other causes of IS per definition, and confounding by non-pneumonia related causes of death cannot be excluded.

We additionally evaluated the predictive performance of the commonly used CAP severity scores CRB-65, qSOFA and CURB-65. Our results showed lower AUCs for all evaluated scores in patients with IS when compared with their documented predictive performance in CAP patients without IS [32–34]. Other studies have reported comparable findings [27, 28, 31, 35]. In contrast to immunocompetent individuals with CAP, the age criterion within the CRB-65 score has recently been shown to lack predictive power in an IS cohort [28], a finding that was also confirmed in our cohort.

Concordant with findings of recent studies, the major drawback of these scores is their inability to reliably predict a low risk for deterioration. Overall, the negative predictive value in our study was slightly higher or nearly similar to that reported in previous publications involving IS cohorts [28, 31]. This means that nine of 99 for CRB-65, six of 72 for CURB-65 and 11 of 150 for qSOFA reached the primary end-point despite a score of 0, corresponding to up to 45.8% of all patients who experienced the outcome. However, among patients with >1 positive criterion in any of the scores, the PPV for the primary end-point was at least 19%, suggesting clinically relevant high-risk prediction.

In our analysis, a respiratory pathogen was detected in more than half of the patients with IS. Among them, 16.4% had CAP caused by NCRPs (corresponding to 9.1% of the overall IS cohort) with P. jirovecii and P. aeruginosa being most frequent. These results are similar or lower to other published studies in patients with IS, some of them including more selected patient populations after transplantation only [8, 27, 28, 30]. As expected, detection of NCRPs was highly significantly associated with IS and most frequent after transplantation, especially stem cell transplantation. Other risk factors for NCRP detection included the presence of fungus-specific infiltrates on CT and treatment of previous infections within the past 12 months, although only the latter remained significant after multivariable analysis. Additionally, ∼50% of all NCRP cases showed co-isolation of at least one other core respiratory pathogen. A recently published study reported polymicrobial infections in 7.9%, which is less than half of the 20.3% in case of IS in our study [30]. In contrast, Schleenvoigt et al. [29] reported more co-infections in 42.5% in patients living with HIV. The clinical meaning of these polymicrobial infections currently is unclear, and data from larger cohorts are needed. NCRP detection overall showed a clear trend towards a poorer prognosis with more than doubled rates of mortality and ORT and a significantly higher ICU admission rate, a finding consistent with previous reports [17].

Pneumococci have previously been identified among the most common bacterial pathogens in CAP in both patients with and without IS [8]. This was confirmed by our study, as core respiratory pathogens were also most frequently isolated in both immunocompetent and immunosuppressed patients. In accordance with former studies, in the IS cohort the most common core pathogens were S. pneumoniae [8, 27, 29], H. influenzae [29, 30] and SARS-CoV-2 [27], highlighting the clinical relevance of core respiratory pathogens for treatment and prevention also in IS patients with CAP.

Our study period included the COVID-19 pandemic, thus the observed epidemiology is probably not fully transferable to the future. We detected 36 COVID-19 infections in IS patients, 16.7% (n=6 out of 36) achieved the primary end-point. According to the availability of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, the corresponding vaccination status has been documented only since mid-2021.

By including a wide range of different IS conditions, some clinically relevant differences between these could be elucidated. We found a better prognosis for patients after intake of IS medications compared to other causes of IS, a high share of IS-associated mortality of 52.9% attributable to patients after antineoplastic treatment and a higher rate of NCRP detection in patients after transplantation, especially stem cell transplantation. However, larger cohorts are needed to confirm these findings and allow more differential subgroup analyses.

Strengths and limitations

The current study represents the first comprehensive analysis of patients with CAP and IS within the multinational, prospective CAPNETZ cohort.

The major strength of our study is its prospective and multi-centric study design with extensive and standardised data collection from patients with CAP across >40 local clinical centres throughout central Europe. In addition, we included a comprehensive spectrum of different IS conditions, rigorously defined according to recently published criteria [11], and with a high causative pathogen detection rate of 55% in patients with IS.

However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting our results. Due to the prospective enrolment process requiring individual patient consent, selection bias towards milder cases of CAP cannot be excluded; this is reflected by the generally lower mortality rate within CAPNETZ compared to population-based cohorts. Additionally, fewer than 2% (n=4) of ambulatory patients with CAP and IS were included, precluding any conclusions for this subgroup. Whereas the inclusion of a large variety of different IS causes can be regarded as a strength of our study, it may also introduce bias by failing to adequately account for its biological and prognostic heterogeneity within the study results. Accordingly, subgroup analyses regarding different causes of IS or the detection of NCRPs are limited by sample size. Additionally, by chance, we were unable to include patients with PID. Due to missing data, we were not able to evaluate additional severity scores like the Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) and the American Thoracic Society (ATS) minor criteria. Most pathogen detections were performed in local laboratories, and diagnostic standards may have varied across centres. This might especially have influenced the detection of NCRPs, as this is largely dependent on the individual decision to test. Some variables like urea and history of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination had to be excluded from some of the multivariable analyses due to missing data, as clearly stated for each parameter in the Results section. In the HIV subcohort, missing values of CD4 cells at CAP onset led to exclusion of 66 out of 99 patients and therefore limit the results for this subgroup. Finally, data on causes of death were not available, and therefore comorbidity-associated and pneumonia-associated mortality could not be differentiated in the analyses.

Conclusion

Our comprehensive analysis of a concurrent multicentre cohort of CAP patients with diverse IS conditions provides important insights into the epidemiology, prognosis and risk factors of this increasingly relevant yet frequently underrepresented patient population in clinical trials. Our study confirmed that core respiratory pathogens – especially SARS-CoV-2, H. influenzae and S. pneumoniae – were the most frequently detected pathogens in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent patients. However, the isolation of NCRPs, especially P. jirovecii and P. aeruginosa, was frequent in patients after organ or stem cell transplantation or anti-infective treatment within the preceding year and associated with an increased ICU admission. While the prognosis in patients with CAP and IS did not differ significantly from that of age-matched CAP patients without IS, commonly used CAP severity scores (CRB-65, CURB-65 and qSOFA) showed insufficient ability to reliably predict low risk. Thus, intensive monitoring and clinical expertise are essential for individual risk stratification in this population.

In conclusion, our study highlights the heterogeneity and clinical relevance of CAP in patients with IS and underscores the need for additional clinical data from multicentre studies, including subgroup analyses of different IS conditions in order to develop and adjust empiric diagnostic, risk stratification tools, and treatment recommendations and guidelines.
