Exploring the role of intelectin-1 in modulating asthma through the gut–bone–lung axis
Authors: Shiyue He, Xinyue Hu, Xiaoxiao Gong, Qiyun Cheng, Sheng Zhu, Ya Chen, Yi Luo, Yiyi Wang, Guoqiang Zhu, Tengfei Wan, Chunyuan Chen, Hui Li, Pinhua Pan, Hui Xie, Zhenxing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe development of extra-intestinal diseases is often accompanied by disruptions in intestinal microbiota and its metabolites, yet the mechanistic link between the gut microbiota and asthma remains unclear. We investigated whether intelectin-1 (ITLN1) mitigates allergic asthma through the gut‒bone‒lung axis as a regulator of gut microbial homeostasis.MethodsSerum samples from 53 asthmatic patients and 34 healthy subjects were analyzed to assess the association between asthma and ITLN1 levels. Mechanism studies were conducted using a house dust mite (HDM)-induced asthma model in Itln1-knockout and Itln1-overexpression mice. Fecal samples were subjected to 16S rRNA sequencing, while lung tissues were analyzed using metabolomics and mRNA sequencing. Butyrate and CCL8-neutralizing antibody were used in the intervention studies, and human serum CCL8 levels were evaluated for clinical relevance.ResultsSerum ITLN1 levels inversely correlated with blood eosinophils in asthmatic patients. Itln1 deficiency exacerbated HDM-induced allergic lung inflammation, particularly type 2 inflammation, while Itln1 overexpression attenuated these effects. Fecal 16S rRNA analysis revealed reduced levels of Bifidobacteriaceae, Erysipelotrichaceae, and Muribaculaceae in Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice, mainly due to the loss of agglutination effects of ITLN1. Moreover, Itln1 deficiency correlated with decreased lung butyrate levels, further enhanced macrophage-derived CCL8 production using the NF-κB pathway and then activated CCR3 expression in CD4+ T cells from bone marrow. Noteworthily, butyrate supplementation or CCL8 neutralization effectively attenuated type 2 inflammation, especially in Itln1 deficiency condition. Additionally, human serum CCL8 levels exhibited a negative correlation with FEV1/FVC, FEV1, and FVC and a positive correlation with neutrophils under asthmatic conditions.ConclusionITLN1 is a promising therapeutic target linking gut microbial metabolism to asthma immune regulation using the gut‒bone‒lung axis.

Introduction

Asthma is a complex and heterogeneous disease characterized by airway inflammation, remodeling, bronchial hyperresponsiveness, and mucus hypersecretion that affects approximately 350 million people worldwide.1-3 Despite advancements in understanding the pathophysiology of asthma and improvements in management strategies, approximately 10% of asthma patients exhibit poor responses to standard therapies.4 Over the past few decades, notable progress has been made in elucidating the mechanisms of eosinophil infiltration and type 2 inflammation, such as the interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5, and IL-13 pathways, as well as immunoglobulin E (IgE) production, which are key points in asthma.5

In addition to the local pulmonary microenvironment, interest in the gut‒lung axis, which refers to the bidirectional immune interactions between the gut microbiota and respiratory mucosa, has increased.6 Dysbiosis of the gut microbiome, particularly when it occurs early in life, has been linked to increased asthma susceptibility,7 highlighting the need for further exploration to understand the detailed mechanisms involved in the gut microbiota and asthma. Recent studies have shown that oral administration of Lactobacillus reuteri in an allergic mouse model reduces airway inflammation through augmenting regulatory T (Treg) cell production and IL-10 secretion while suppressing the levels of monocyte chemoattractant protein 1, IL-5, and IL-13.8 Additionally, the use of antibiotics, which are known to induce significant changes in the intestinal microbiota, has further highlighted this connection. Specifically, azithromycin has been reported to alter the gut microbiome and attenuate airway inflammation in allergic asthma.9 These findings suggest that specific gut microbe–immune cell crosstalk represents a novel approach for understanding and potentially treating asthma.

Intelectin-1 (ITLN1), also known as omentin-1,10 has emerged as a significant adipokine with potential implications for asthma. ITLN1 is expressed primarily in human omental and subcutaneous adipose tissues, as well as in the small intestine, particularly within intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), goblet cells (in humans), and Paneth cells (in mice).11 The role of ITLN1 in the gut microbiota has been well-documented, and its specific expression in airway mucous cells suggests a potential link to eosinophilic airway inflammation in asthma.12 Several studies have reported that ITLN1 plays a protective role in inhibiting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α).13 Additionally, the overexpression of ITLN1 has been associated with reduced extracellular matrix deposition and fibrosis resolution in lung fibrosis models.14,15 Given these multifaceted roles, ITLN1 is a molecule of interest in asthma research. Notably, studies have shown that knockout of ITLN1 can result in an increased abundance of bacterial communities such as Burkholderiales, Prevotellaceae, and Anaplasmataceae,11 which are related to asthmatic conditions. This observation, coupled with the high expression of ITLN1 in the gut, suggests that ITLN1 may influence asthma development through its regulatory effects on the gut microbiota. Recent research has highlighted the importance of postbiotics, particularly the microbial metabolites short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid, in mediating the effects of the gut microbiome on distant organs, including the lungs.16-18 Infants with low levels of SCFAs present an increased risk of developing asthma and allergic diseases later in life.19 While supplementation with SCFAs or SCFA-producing bacteria has been shown to alleviate allergic airway inflammation.19-21

Thus, we hypothesize that ITLN1 plays a critical role in asthma pathogenesis by modulating the gut microbiota and its metabolites, particularly SCFAs. To test this hypothesis, we conducted comprehensive 16S rRNA sequencing of fecal samples and SCFAs detection in lung samples from wild-type and Itln1 knockout mice subjected to an HDM-induced asthma model. Our results identified butyric acid as a key metabolite affected by ITLN1 during asthma. Furthermore, transcriptomic sequence analysis showed that ITLN1 deficiency led to increased macrophage CCL8 production, which subsequently attracted lymphocytes through the CCL8/CCR3 axis and enhanced group 2 innate lymphoid cell (ILC2) activation in the lungs. These findings provide a foundation for developing potential intervention/treatment strategies for asthma targeting ITLN1 and its downstream effects on the metabolome.

Results

Reduced ITLN1 levels in asthma negatively correlates with airway inflammation

To investigate the relationship between ITLN1 levels and asthma, we evaluated serum ITLN1 concentrations, lung functions, and immune cell profiles in cohorts of healthy individuals and asthmatic patients. The demographic and clinical data are summarized in Figure 1A. Serum ITLN1 levels were significantly reduced in asthmatic patients compared to healthy subjects (Figure 1B). This finding is consistent with a previous study22 and extends these observations to a larger cohort, suggesting a potential role for ITLN1 in asthma pathogenesis.

We further assessed whether serum ITLN1 levels were correlated with the lung function parameters, including forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), FEV1 percent predicted (FEV1% pred), forced vital capacity (FVC), and FVC percent predicted (FVC% pred). No significant correlations were identified between ITLN1 levels and these lung function metrics (Figure 1C, D). Given the heterogeneous nature of asthma, which is characterized by chronic airway inflammation predominantly driven by eosinophils (Eos), we examined the association between ITLN1 levels and eosinophilic inflammation. Serum ITLN1 levels were inversely correlated with both the percentage and count of blood Eos (Figure 1E), while no significant correlation was observed with neutrophils (Neu; Figure 1F).

To further delineate the role of ITLN1, we stratified asthmatic patients into low and high ITLN1 groups. Patients with lower ITLN1 levels exhibited significantly elevated Eos percentage and counts of Eos than higher ITLN1 cohorts (Figure 1G), while no significant differences in Neu levels were detected between the groups (Figure 1H). Taken together, these findings indicate that reduced ITLN1 levels are associated with increased airway inflammation in asthma.

ITLN1 modulates HDM-induced allergic lung inflammation

To elucidate the role and underlying mechanisms of ITLN1 in asthma, we investigated the impact of differential ITLN1 expression on allergic lung inflammation. Initially, HDM-induced asthma models were generated on wild-type (WT) and Itln1 knockout (Itln1−/−) mice. Histopathological analysis with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid-schiff (PAS) staining revealed that Itln1−/− mice exhibited significantly greater inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue damage in the lungs compared to WT mice, while mucus production remained unaffected (Figure 2A, B). Total cell counts in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid were significantly elevated in Itln1−/− mice, indicating an exacerbated inflammatory response (Figure 2C). Serum levels of HDM-specific IgG1 and IgE were markedly increased in Itln1−/− asthmatic mice, along with elevated lymphocyte (Lym) counts, as determined by ELISA and flow cytometry (Figure 2D, E). There were no significant differences in Eos, Neu, or macrophage (Mac) populations in BAL fluid between the groups (Figure 2E). These findings suggest that ITLN1 deficiency amplifies allergic inflammation.

To further assess the therapeutic potential of ITLN1, we employed a systemic overexpression approach by administering adenoviral Itln1 (Ad-Itln1) to HDM-induced asthmatic mice (Figure 3A and S1A–D). Itln1 overexpression attenuated lung inflammation, as evidenced by reduced inflammatory infiltration in H&E staining, although mucus production was unaffected by PAS staining (Figure 3B, C). Moreover, Ad-Itln1 treatment led to decreased serum IgG1 levels in trend (Figure 3D) and significantly reduced total BAL cells, Eos, and Lym numbers compared to Ad-Mock-treated asthmatic mice (Figure 3E). These data support that ITLN1 overexpression attenuates the allergic inflammation, underscoring the potential of ITLN1 as a therapeutic target for asthma.

ITLN1 deficiency exacerbates HDM-induced type 2 inflammation

Our observation indicated that ITLN1 reduction significantly led to severe lung tissue inflammation and increased BAL Lym numbers. To further characterize this effect, we analyzed parameters associated with type 2 inflammation. As shown in Figure 4A, HDM exposure triggered the production of T helper 2 (Th2) cytokines, specifically IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 in BAL fluid. ITLN1-deficient asthma mice exhibited significantly elevated BAL IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 levels compared to WT controls (Figure 4A), demonstrating an intensified type 2 inflammatory response in the absence of ITLN1. The key cellular orchestrators for the secretion of type 2 effector cytokines are adaptive Th2 cells and ILC2 activation.23,24 Flow cytometry analysis revealed increased proportions of lung CD4+ T cells in HDM-treated asthmatic mice compared to PBS-treated controls, with the increase being more pronounced in Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice (Figure 4B). Specifically, Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice presented a significant increase in IL-4 production by lung CD4+ T cells, while IL-5 and IL-13 production remained unchanged in lung CD4+ T cells (Figure 4C).

To better understand the impact of ITLN1 deletion on CD4+ T cell dynamics in asthma conditions, we analyzed bone marrow-derived CD4+ T cells, which are a major source of these cells. Although WT asthmatic mice exhibited a slight increase in CD3+CD4+ T cells in the bone marrow, Itln1-deficient mice showed a significantly greater proportion of these cells, particularly under asthmatic conditions (Figure 4D). Additionally, Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice presented a marked increase in lung ILC2 (ST2+Thy1.2+) numbers (Figure 4E).

Together, these results confirm a critical role for ITLN1 in mediating type 2 immune responses. The absence of ITLN1 amplifies type 2 inflammation in response to lung allergens, potentially by activating ILC2 and recruiting CD4+ T cells from the bone marrow.

Gut microbiota dysbiosis reduces butyrate production in the ITLN1-deficient asthma model

Although ITLN1 is expressed primarily in the intestine, previous studies have reported its upregulation in the lung during asthma.12 In our study, we also observed that ITLN1 expression specifically localized to airway goblet cells after HDM exposure, with no detectable expression in other lung regions (Figure S2A). Importantly, ITLN1 was highly expressed in the colon, but its levels were significantly reduced under asthmatic conditions (Figure S2A-B), paralleling the decrease in serum ITLN1. Moreover, to investigate whether ITLN1 directly modulates airway inflammation in asthma condition, recombinant ITLN1 protein was used to treat an HDM-exposed human bronchial epithelial HBE cell line and a murine alveolar macrophage MH-S cell line. As shown in Figure S2C-D, ITLN1 treatment did not significantly reduce the levels of inflammatory markers induced by HDM (IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP in HBE cells; Il-1β, Il-6, and Tnf-α in MH-S cells). Notably, ITLN1 treatment exhibited pro-inflammatory effects on MH-S cells in the absence of HDM induction (Figure S2E). In addition, although elevated levels of IL-4 expression were observed in CD4+ T cells from the lung tissues of Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice (Figure 4C), in vitro studies revealed that recombinant ITLN1 protein does not directly regulate IL-4 expression in isolated primary spleen CD4+ T cells (Figure S2F-G). These results suggest that ITLN1 does not directly mitigate allergic airway inflammation, indicating a possible link between ITLN1 levels and intestinal function in asthma.

Given the predominant expression of ITLN1 in the colon and the lack of direct anti-inflammatory effects in the lung, we explored its role in regulating the intestinal microenvironment during asthma. ITLN1 is known to specifically recognize microbial glycans, which suggests that it may modulate microbial composition. To assess this, we performed 16S rRNA sequencing on fecal samples from WT and Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice (Table S1−S2). Principal co-ordinate analysis (PCoA) showed distinct microbiota profiles between two groups (Figure S3G). Although community richness indices (Observed, Chao1, and ACE) were similar between two groups (Figure S3A–C), Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice exhibited increased Shannon value and decreased Simpon value in compared to WT asthmatic mice, indicating heightened microbial diversity in the absence of ITLN1 under asthma condition (Figure S3D–F).

At the family level, Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice displayed decreased abundances of Muribaculaceae, Erysipelotrichaceae, Atopobiaceae, Bifidobacteriaceae, and Sutterellaceae, while the abundance of Alcholeplasmataceae, Oscillospiraceae, and Tannerellaceae were elevated compared to WT asthmatic mice (Figure S3H). These trends were further confirmed in absolute quantification (Figure 5A, B). Three of the most diverse flora, including Bifidobacteriaceae, Erysipelotrichaceae, and Muribaculaceae, were selected for verification by RT-qPCR (Figure 5C). Additionally, no obvious changes in Bifidobacteriaceae, Erysipelotrichaceae, and Muribaculaceae abundance were observed in WT and Itln1-deficient mice under normal condition (Figure S3I), indicating that ITLN1 specifically regulates these bacterial families in asthma.

Previous research established that ITLN1 can specifically bind to certain gut microbes (e.g., Streptococcus pneumoniae).25 Therefore, a bacterial agglutination assay was conducted by co-incubating the recombinant ITLN1 protein and the gut microbiota of solvent- or HDM-treated mice. As shown in Figure S3J, ITLN1 was observed to agglutinate a greater number of components of the gut microbiota under HDM conditions than in controls. After co-culturing the recombinant ITLN1 protein with the gut microbiota suspension, RT-qPCR of residual bacterial showed a significant increase in the relative abundance of Bifidobacteriaceae, Erysipelotrichaceae, and Muribaculaceae (Figure S3K‒M).

METACYC pathway analysis, based on 16S rRNA data, revealed suppression of multiple metabolic pathways, including the fatty acid biosynthesis initiation pathway, in Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice (Figure S4). Given the role of Bifidobacteriaceae, Erysipelotrichaceae, and Muribaculaceae in the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs),26-28 we then examined SCFAs levels in lung tissues by GC–MS. PCA analysis revealed apparent differences between the two groups (Figure 5D). Moreover, SCFAs levels were decreased in Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice compared to WT asthmatic mice (Figure 5E). Considering the abundance of metabolites, we further analyzed the levels of acetic acid, butyric acid, and propionic acid. As shown in Figure 5F, G, butyric acid levels were apparently decreased in Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice compared to WT asthmatic mice. In addition, we measured the levels of butyrate in clinical samples and found that serum butyrate levels were significantly lower in asthma patients compared to healthy subjects (Figure 5H). These findings indicate that ITLN1 may influence allergic inflammation possibly by modulating butyrate produced through its effects on specific gut microbial populations.

Butyrate supplementation alleviates allergic inflammation in ITLN1-deficient asthma

To validate the role of butyrate in ITLN1-mediated regulation of asthma, butyrate was used to treat WT and Itln1−/− asthmatic mice (Figure 6A). Butyrate treatment resulted in a mild reduction in lung inflammation in WT asthmatic mice but apparently alleviated lung inflammation and goblet cell metaplasia in Itln1−/− asthmatic mice, as assessed by H&E and PAS staining (Figure 6B, C). In Itln1−/− asthmatic mice, butyrate supplementation also led to a substantial reduction in serum HDM-specific IgG1 and IgE levels, while the effects were less pronounced in WT asthmatic mice (Figure 6D). Consistent with histopathological observations, butyrate treatment produced a greater reduction in total BAL cell counts and Eos numbers in Itln1−/− asthmatic mice compared to WT asthmatic mice (Figure 6E). Additionally, butyrate decreased BAL IL-5 and IL-13 levels in both WT and Itln1−/− asthmatic mice, while significantly reduced BAL IL-4 levels only in Itln1−/− asthmatic mice (Figure 6F).

We further investigated whether butyrate modulates the type 2 immune response. Flow cytometry analysis showed that butyrate significantly reduced the elevated ILC2 proportions observed in Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice, while no significant changes were seen in WT mice (Figure 6G). Notably, there was no significant alterations in bone marrow CD3+CD4+ T cell populations in either WT or Itln1−/− asthmatic mice (Figure 6H).

Subsequent KEGG analysis of mRNA sequencing data from WT and Itln1−/− asthmatic lung tissues indicated that pathways involved in fatty acid metabolism, elongation, and butanoate metabolism were influenced by ITLN1 expression (Figure S5A–C). Given that ITLN1 deficiency is associated with reduced butyrate levels, we found that Acsm1, a gene associated with fatty acid metabolism, was downregulated in Itln1−/− asthmatic lung tissues (Figure S5D). RT-qPCR further confirmed that Acsm1 mRNA levels were significantly lower in Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice compared to WT controls (Figure S5E).

Collectively, these findings indicate that butyrate supplementation effectively mitigated type 2 immune responses in Itln1-deficient asthmatic conditions, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic strategy to alleviate asthma exacerbations related to ITLN1 deficiency.

CCL8-CCR3 interaction recruits CD4+ T cells to augment allergic inflammation in ITLN1 deficiency

To elucidate the mechanism by which ITLN1 modulates type 2 immune responses, we performed mRNA sequencing and Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of lung tissues from WT and Itln1−/− asthmatic mice. As shown in Figure 7A, biological processes associated with lymphocyte chemotaxis, migration, activation, and differentiation were significantly enriched. Among the DEGs, several key regulators of lymphocyte-related pathways, including Adam8, Ccl8, Ccl9, Mzb1, and Ccl6, were prominently represented (Figure 7B). We subsequently verified the mRNA levels of the top five significant genes, including Ccl6, Ccl8, and Ccl9, using RT-qPCR. Notably, Ccl6 and Ccl8 tended to be upregulated in asthmatic patients compared to healthy controls, with ITLN1 deficiency further augmenting their expression, particularly Ccl8 (Figure 7C). Moreover, analysis of BAL fluid showed significantly elevated CCL8 levels in asthmatic mice but increased CCL8 levels in Itln1-deficient mice (Figure 7D).

Given the critical role of macrophages in producing CCL8,29,30 we performed immunofluorescence co-staining for F4/80 (a macrophage marker) and CCL8. HDM exposure led to increased CCL8 expression by macrophages, which was further augmented in Itln1-deficient mice (Figure 7E). These findings suggest that ITLN1 modulates type 2 immune responses using the regulation of CCL8 secreted by macrophages. This effect was confirmed in bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) stimulated with HDM, where butyrate downregulated Ccl8 mRNA levels (Figure 7F), likely through the suppression of NF-κB pathway activation using the inhibition of its phosphorylation (Figure S6).

To further understand the involvement of CCL8 in type 2 inflammation, its receptors, such as CCR3, CCR5, and CCR8, on bone marrow CD3+CD4+ T cells were detected by flow cytometry. ITLN1 deficiency significantly upregulated CCR3 expression, which was subsequently reduced by butyrate treatment, while no significant changes in CCR5 and CCR8 expression were observed (Figure 7G). Taken together, these data support the hypothesis that ITLN1 deficiency exacerbates the type 2 immune response by reducing lung butyrate levels and subsequently enhancing macrophage CCL8-CCR3 axis to promote CD4+ T cell recruitment.

CCL8 blockade reverses type 2 inflammation in ITLN1-deficient asthma

To investigate whether ITLN1 deficiency exacerbates lung inflammatory infiltration and type 2 immune responses via CCL8, we administered a CCL8 neutralization antibody via intravenous tail injection in both WT and Itln1−/− asthmatic mice (Figure 8A). As shown in Figure 8B, C, CCL8 blockade significantly alleviated lung inflammation and mucus production in Itln1−/− asthmatic mice, as evaluated by H&E and PAS staining, while no significant effects were observed in WT asthmatic mice. In addition, CCL8 blockade led to a significant reduction in serum HDM-specific IgG1 and IgE levels in Itln1−/− asthmatic mice, with unobvious influence in WT asthmatic mice (Figure 8D). Flow cytometry analysis showed that CCL8 blockade also reduced total BAL cell counts, Eos, and Neu numbers in Itln1−/− asthmatic mice (Figure 8E). Moreover, CCL8 antibody treatment had a more pronounced therapeutic effect on type 2 immune responses in patients with Itln1-deficient asthma. Specifically, after blocking CCL8, the BAL levels of IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 were decreased in Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice, while in WT mice, the levels of IL-4 and IL-5 were reduced, but no significant change in the level of IL-13 was observed (Figure 8F). Additionally, CCL8 blockade significantly reduces the number of lung ILC2s in Itln1−/− asthmatic mice, with no obvious changes observed under WT asthma conditions (Figure 8G).

We further validated these findings in human subjects and demonstrated that serum CCL8 levels were elevated in asthma patients compared to healthy controls (Figure 8H). Notably, serum CCL8 levels were negatively correlated with pulmonary function measures, including the FEV1/FVC ratio, FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC% pred, FEV1% pred, and FVC% pred (Figure 8I–K and S7A–D). Additionally, serum CCL8 levels have no significant correlation with blood Eos (Figure S7E, F), while were positively correlated with Neu (Figure S7G, H).

These findings suggest that CCL8 blockade effectively mitigates lung inflammatory infiltration and type 2 immune responses in ITLN1 deficiency asthma, highlighting CCL8 as a potential therapeutic target for modulating type 2 inflammation in this condition.

Discussion

This study integrated human clinical data, metabolomics and transcriptomic analysis, and murine models to elucidate the role of ITLN1 in asthma pathogenesis (graphical abstract). Our findings showed that Itln1 deficiency exacerbated HDM-induced allergic lung inflammation, particularly type 2 inflammation, while adenovirus-mediated overexpression of ITLN1 attenuated these effects. Notably, fecal 16S rRNA analysis revealed a significant reduction in the abundance of Bifidobacteriaceae, Erysipelotrichaceae and Muribaculaceae in Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice, accompanied by decreased butyric acid levels in the lungs. Butyrate acid is a key metabolite of SCFAs closely associated with these three gut microbiome families. Furthermore, mRNA sequencing of asthmatic lung tissues showed elevated Ccl8 expression in Itln1-deficient mice, implicating CCL8 in the recruitment of CD4+ T lymphocytes from the bone marrow to the lungs via CCR3 signaling pathways. Notably, supplementation with butyrate and blockade of CCL8 significantly ameliorated type 2 inflammation in Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting these pathways.

ITLN-1 has been considered as an anti-inflammatory adipokine.13,31,32 Our previous studies have demonstrated that it plays a role in attenuating inflammation in conditions such as benign prostatic hyperplasia and osteoporosis.13,33 Interestingly, while ITLN1 expression is diminished in several inflammatory diseases, including acute lung injury, osteoarthritis, and inflammatory bowel disease,34,35 studies on asthma are more complex. ITLN1 levels are elevated in BAL fluid, bronchial epithelial brushings, and induced sputum in both asthmatic patients and murine models.12,36-38 While both prior research37 and our study confirmed reduced circulating ITLN1 in asthma patients versus healthy controls, the functional implications of this deficiency remain incompletely characterized. Critically, previous studies lacked analysis of potential correlations between ITLN1 levels and clinical measures of lung function or specific immune cell subpopulations in systemic circulation. Moreover, reduced ITLN1 expression is correlated with obesity, smoking, and increased body susceptibility to anxiety and depressive-like behaviors.39-41 This discrepancy suggests that the role of ITLN1 in asthma may be context-dependent, potentially influenced by factors such as obesity, smoking, and stress, all of which are known risk factors for asthma.42-44 Given the anti-inflammatory role of ITLN1 in other diseases, identifying the impact of ITLN1 on asthma will provide new insight for the treatment of asthma.

Our study corroborates the protective role of ITLN1 in asthma. Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice exhibited more severe lung inflammation than WT mice, while systemic supplementation with Ad-Itln1 led to substantial attenuation of these pathological changes. Additionally, considering the correlation between ITLN1 and type 2-related parameters, including FeNO and IgE,37 we further detected the type 2 immune response in wild-type and Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice. The observed increase in lung IL-4 in CD4+ T cells, bone marrow CD4+ T cells and lung ILC2s in Itln1-deficient mice further implicates ITLN1 in modulating the type 2 immune response in asthma, likely by influencing ILC2 proliferation and the recruitment of CD4+ T cells from the bone marrow. Given the specific expression pattern of ITLN1, which was decreased in the intestine and increased in airway goblet cells under asthmatic conditions, direct treatment with recombinant ITLN1 existed pro-inflammatory effect on MH-S cells in the absence of HDM, where as it had no effect on CD4+ T cells under the same condition or on HDM-induced inflammation in both MH-S or HBE cells. It is plausible that ITLN1 affects type 2 inflammation by altering the gut microbiota composition and metabolism.

ITLN1 has emerged as a significant regulator of the gut microbiota. Evidence suggests that ITLN1 deficiency leads to a decreased abundance of key bacterial families such as Erysipelotrichaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Lactobacillaceae and an increased abundance of potential pathogenies like Anaplasmataceae and Burkholderiaceae.11 ITLN1 has been demonstrated to bind to specific gut microbes,11 and our results indicate that ITLN1 exerts a direct regulatory effect on the composition of the gut microbiota by selectively agglutinating specific bacterial populations, thereby providing competitive advantages for the remaining commensal communities. Studies have also indicated that supplementation with probiotics, specifically Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum CECT7765 or lactoferrin, both of which are known to regulate gut microbiome dysbiosis, results in increased ITLN1 expression or improved gut microbiome homeostasis under conditions of obesity or intestinal dysfunction, respectively.45,46 These alterations in the gut microbiota composition are particularly relevant to asthma, as previous studies have linked gut dysbiosis with increased asthma risk.47,48 In our study, ITLN1 deficiency was associated with significant reductions in the Erysipelotrichaceae and Bifidobacteriaceae families. A neonatal study showed that a lower abundance of Bifidobacterium in the gut microbiome contributes to a high risk of asthma.49 Additionally, the relative abundance of the Erysipelotrichaceae family increased in a mixed granulocytic asthma model.50 Thus, Erysipelotrichaceae and Bifidobacteriaceae might play an important role in ITLN1 deficiency-induced severe lung inflammation.

The gut microbiome plays a direct role in gastrointestinal-related diseases, yet its influence extends beyond the intestinal tract, primarily because of metabolites like extracellular vesicles, proteins, and SCFAs.51 SCFAs, in particular, exert significant effects on mucosal and systemic immune responses, and lower SCFAs levels are directly associated with susceptibility to allergic disease.52,53 Erysipelotrichaceae are crucial butyrate-producing bacteria in the gut.54 Bifidobacteriaceae seemed to be a butyrogenic bacterium or one that supported the growth of butyrogenic bacteria, thereby contributing to the increased butyrate level.55,56 In addition, Muribaculaceae was also associated with butyrate.57 In our results, GC–MS analysis showed significantly lower lung SCFAs levels (particularly butyric acid) in Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice versus WT mice. Therefore, we suggested that ITLN1 aggravated asthma through butyrate which is directly or indirectly produced by Erysipelotrichaceae, Muribaculaceae, and Bifidobacteriaceae.

Additionally, butyric acid can be directly transported into various cell lineages, including immune cells, through widely expressed transporters like MCT1 and SMCT1 under asthmatic conditions.58 Interestingly, we found that HDM stimulation increased Acsm1, which is a gene related to propanoate and butanoate metabolism, while ITLN1 deficiency reduced Acsm1 in asthmatic conditions. Thus, ITLN1 deficiency aggravates asthma lung inflammation might via Acsm1 regulated butyric acid. Consistently with previous research,58 we also observed that butyrate supplementation could moderately relieve lung inflammation while exerting more significant effects on both lung inflammation infiltration and type 2 immune responses in ITLN1-deficient asthmatic mice.

Given the association between ITLN1 and the type 2 immune response in asthma,37 we explored the crucial molecules involved in this process. The results of mRNA sequencing showed that higher lung Ccl8 expression was associated with lymphocyte-related functions in ITLN1 deficiency asthmatic mice compared to wild-type mice. Consistent with previous studies,59,60 we also observed increased serum CCL8 levels in asthmatic patients and mice. Meanwhile,serum CCL8 levels were negatively correlated with lung function and positively correlated with blood neutrophil numbers and percentages. Moreover, a previous study showed that butyrate ameliorates asthma by targeting the NF-κB pathway and that activation of the NF-κB pathway promotes CCL8 expression in macrophages, which is consistent with our results.53,61,62 CCL8 is a CC chemokine of the monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP) family that can activate chemotactic T cells and CD4+ T cells.63-68 Several studies have demonstrated that CCL8 is closely associated with type 2 inflammation in atopic dermatitis and that HDM induce type 2 inflammation during allergen exposure.29,69,70 Furthermore, CCL8 plays a critical role in pulmonary ILC2 accumulation and function like the development of airway inflammation.30,71,72 Thus, ITLN1 deficiency might aggravate asthma type 2 inflammation via enhancing CCL8 expression.

In this study, CCL8 neutralization antibody treatment reduced lung inflammation in wild-type asthmatic mice, while showed a more apparent therapeutic effect in Itln1 deficiency asthmatic mice which owing to increased CCL8 expression in ITLN1 deficiency condition. Furthermore, the BAL IL-4, IL5, and IL-13 levels and proportions of ILC2 were significantly reduced in Itln1-deficient asthmatic mice after blocking CCL8. CCL8, a key mediator of Th2 and ILC2 response,29,30,73 showed dynamics of expression distinct from those of CCR3, CCR5 and CCR8 ligands. Our results showed increased CCR3 expression in bone marrow CD4+T cells, and butyrate treatment decreased CCR3 expression, as no significant changes in CCR5 and CCR8 expression were detected. Because HDM exposure can drive increased CCL8 expression by macrophages,74,75 our study revealed that ITLN1 deficiency could increase macrophage CCL8 expression under asthma conditions. Additionally, we also observed decreased macrophage CCL8 production in butyrate-treated ITLN1 deficiency asthmatic mice. One study showed that butyrate decreased the levels of several chemokines, including CCL8, and subsequently reduced inflammation.76 Regardless, we can conclude from our data that ITLN1 deficiency caused reduced lung butyric acid levels via decreasing Erysipelotrichaceae, Muribaculaceae and Bifidobacteriaceae and then upregulated CCL8/CCR3 axis activation, leading to the aggravation of type 2 inflammation in asthma.

In conclusion, our findings highlight the importance and specific mechanisms by which ITLN1 modulates the gut microbiota and their metabolites to regulate macrophage CCL8 expression and the type 2 immune response under asthmatic conditions. More importantly, this study also identifies three potential and promising intervention strategies, including Ad-Itln1, butyrate, and CCL8 neutralizing antibodies, for asthma treatment, particularly in those lacking ITLN1. This study advances our understanding of the gut‒lung axis in asthma and highlights ITLN1 as a potential therapeutic target for modulating the gut microbiota and metabolic pathways to mitigate asthma severity.
