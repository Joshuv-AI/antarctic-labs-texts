Integrated Histological and Transcriptomic Characterization of Prolonged Starvation Responses in the Cavefish Triplophysa rosa
Authors: Zechen Wu, Luyun Ni, Yuan Xu, Yongming Wang, Feng Shao, Zuogang Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Simple SummaryCaves are dark, isolated, and food-poor habitats. Animals living in caves must therefore make careful use of limited energy to survive. The cavefish Triplophysa rosa lives in such an environment, but little is known about how it copes when food is unavailable for a long time. In this study, we combined growth measurements, liver and spleen histology, transcriptomic analysis, quantitative reverse transcription PCR, and transmission electron microscopy to examine T. rosa responses to 30, 60, and 90 days of starvation. Prolonged starvation reduced growth and liver reserve indicators and was accompanied by coordinated changes in metabolic gene expression. In the spleen, early immune-related transcriptional changes were followed by greater prominence of melano-macrophage centers, suggesting increasing involvement of cellular clearance and tissue maintenance during prolonged starvation. Autophagy-related gene expression and ultrastructural observations were consistent with increased involvement of intracellular recycling, which may support basic cellular maintenance during prolonged food deprivation. In summary, prolonged starvation in T. rosa involved coordinated temporal changes in metabolism, splenic function, and cellular maintenance. By providing an integrated characterization of prolonged starvation responses in a cave-restricted fish, this study may provide useful physiological information for future conservation and management studies of cave-dwelling species.AbstractFood scarcity is a major ecological challenge for cavefish; however, their coordinated physiological and molecular responses to prolonged starvation remain poorly understood. Here, we characterized the responses of Triplophysa rosa to 30, 60, and 90 days of food deprivation under controlled laboratory conditions by integrating growth measurements, histology, transcriptomics, quantitative reverse transcription PCR, and transmission electron microscopy. Starvation inhibited growth, reduced the condition factor and hepatosomatic index, and induced hepatocyte shrinkage, consistent with mobilization of hepatic energy reserves. Liver genes with declining temporal expression were enriched mainly in lipid biosynthesis, cholesterol metabolism, ribosomal function, and cell-cycle pathways, consistent with reduced biosynthetic activity. In the spleen, immune-related transcription increased during early starvation and declined at later stages, while the greater prominence of melano-macrophage centers was consistent with greater involvement of cellular clearance and tissue maintenance during prolonged starvation. Prolonged starvation also increased the expression of autophagy-related genes in the liver and spleen, and autophagy-related structures became more prominent in representative transmission electron microscopy images, consistent with increased involvement of intracellular recycling. Together, these findings reveal a coordinated temporal response involving metabolic adjustment, stage-dependent reorganization of splenic immune-related processes, and autophagy-associated cellular maintenance in T. rosa. This integrated approach improves our understanding of physiological maintenance during sustained nutrient limitation in cave-restricted fish.

1. Introduction

Environmental and seasonal fluctuations can lead to temporal and spatial variability in food resources and recurrent food deprivation in fish [1]. Although several fish species can survive food scarcity for weeks or even months, their physiological state may be severely impacted [2]. Starvation affects many vital processes in fish—including energy allocation, immune regulation, and stress responses—prompting extensive research on the effects of starvation on fish growth, morphology, physiological and biochemical indicators, and compensatory growth after refeeding. For example, starvation reduces metabolic rate, body mass, and energy reserves and can alter antioxidant activity, endoplasmic reticulum function, and immune responses [3,4]. Although short-term starvation may have limited effects on immune function, prolonged starvation can reduce immune responses in fish [5,6]. In Sinibrama taeniatus, short-term starvation induced innate immunity and partially suppressed adaptive immunity [5]; transcriptomic analysis showed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with innate immunity were upregulated in the liver during early starvation but became downregulated as starvation progressed. During early starvation in Coilia nasus and Salmo salar, stored lipids support the enhanced innate immune response required for rapid defense against external pathogens; however, continued starvation depletes these energy reserves, leading to a decline in immune functions [7,8]. Immune activation requires substantial energy and increased metabolic activity, and its efficiency depends partly on available energy reserves. In addition to metabolic and immune regulation, autophagy may also represent an important intracellular recycling mechanism that supports cellular homeostasis during prolonged nutrient deprivation [9,10].

Caves represent unique environments characterized by limited external material and energy inputs; thus, food scarcity poses a notable obstacle for cave-dwelling organisms, which depend heavily on external energy sources from bats and seasonal floods [11]. Restricted access to food destabilizes cave ecosystems and forces cave-dwelling organisms to allocate limited energy among different physiological functions, resulting in trade-offs among immune responses, growth, and reproduction [12]. Prioritizing resources to one function inevitably diminishes their availability for others. The cavefish Triplophysa rosa, which is exclusively found in the Wulong Karst Cave in Chongqing, China, belongs to the order Cypriniformes, family Nemacheilidae, and genus Triplophysa [13]. Previous research on this species has explored its morphology, eye degeneration mechanisms, immune system, metabolism, and evolutionary characteristics [14,15,16,17].

Persistent food scarcity influences both metabolism and immune function in cavefish. However, immune regulation during prolonged nutrient deprivation remains poorly understood [18,19]. Although malnutrition commonly causes thymic atrophy and degeneration in fish [20], adult T. rosa retain a well-developed thymus under the food-limited conditions of their natural habitat [14]. This finding provides important insights into the long-term preservation of immune architecture in T. rosa, but it remains unclear how the liver and spleen respond dynamically to experimentally defined prolonged starvation or how metabolic adjustment, immune-related processes, and cellular recycling are coordinated across tissues. To address this gap, we adopted an integrated approach combining growth measurements, liver and spleen histology, transcriptomic profiling, quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) validation, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to characterize the responses of T. rosa to 30, 60, and 90 days of starvation.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Fish and Experimental Design

T. rosa is a cave-restricted wild fish species, and a stable captive breeding population was not available at the time of this study. All the experimental fish were collected from a cave in Wulong District, Chongqing, China. Because suitable wild individuals were difficult to obtain, only fish with similar body sizes were selected for this study. A total of 175 individuals with a mean total length of 5.15 ± 0.72 cm and a mean body weight of 0.98 ± 0.26 g were included.

After one week of acclimation under laboratory conditions, fish were randomly assigned to seven groups, with each group of 25 fish maintained in one tank. Fish sampled immediately after the acclimation period on Day 0 were designated as the baseline control group and are referred to as CON throughout the manuscript. The remaining fish were assigned to the feeding groups FD30, FD60, and FD90 or the starvation groups SV30, SV60, and SV90. Feeding and starvation groups were sampled after 30, 60, and 90 days of their respective treatments. Fish in the feeding groups received a commercial frozen bloodworm diet twice daily at 09:00 and 18:00. At each feeding, two frozen blocks were provided to each tank containing 25 fish. Visible residual feed remained after 30 min, indicating apparent satiation, and was promptly removed by siphoning to maintain water quality. The diet contained 88% moisture, 8.5% crude protein, 1.2% crude fat, and 0.8% crude fiber on an as-fed basis, as well as multivitamin ingredients. Fish in the starvation groups received no feed.

All the fish were maintained in tanks of identical specifications under shaded conditions. One-third of the tank water was replaced weekly, and groundwater was fully aerated before use. Water temperature was maintained at 17 ± 1 °C throughout the experiment. No starvation-induced mortality was observed during the experiment. All the animal handling procedures were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Southwest University, Chongqing, China (IACUC Approval No. IACUC–20210415–02), and all the experiments were performed in accordance with committee guidelines.

2.2. Growth Index Analysis

Nine fish were randomly selected from each group for growth measurements and subsequent histological analyses. The total length, body length, and body weight were measured following anesthesia with tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222; Cat. No. HY-W011777; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). The weight gain rate (WGR), weight loss rate (WLR), condition factor (CF), and hepatosomatic index (HSI) were determined as follows:WGR(%)=(final weight − initial weight)initial weight × 100WLR(%)=(initial weight − final weight)initial weight × 100CF=weightbody length3×100HSI=liver weightbody weight × 100

Growth data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference test for multiple comparisons, and the results were visualized using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

2.3. Histological Analysis

Liver and spleen tissues were collected from the same nine fish selected for growth measurements in each group and subsequently processed for histological analysis. Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Cat. No. P1110; Solarbio, Beijing, China) at room temperature for 24 h, then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Paraffin-embedded tissues were cut into 5-μm sections and stained using a Hematoxylin-Eosin Stain Kit (Cat. No. G1120; Solarbio, Beijing, China). Sections were observed and photographed using a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and whole-slide imaging was performed using a Pannoramic DESK digital microscope (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary). The hepatocyte area was quantified using ImageJ v1.54r (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For each fish, three sections were examined, and 10 hepatocytes were randomly selected for area measurement in each section.

2.4. Transcriptome Sequencing

For transcriptomic profiling, liver and spleen tissues were collected from six fish in each group and pooled separately by tissue type for RNA extraction and library construction. Total RNA was extracted from each pooled liver and spleen sample using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity and quality were assessed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). Subsequently, eukaryotic mRNA was enriched using Oligo(dT) beads, fragmented in fragmentation buffer, and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using random primers. Second-strand cDNA was synthesized using DNA polymerase I, RNase H, and dNTPs. The cDNA fragments were ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters following end-repair. Finally, the products were purified, amplified, and sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq 2500 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Raw sequencing data have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive under BioProject accession PRJCA065274.

2.5. Transcriptome Data Analysis

Raw data obtained from Illumina sequencing were subjected to quality control using fastp v1.0.1 software to eliminate sequencing adapter sequences, reads with unknown bases (N content > 10%), and low-quality reads. Subsequently, clean data were further evaluated using FastQC v0.12.1 software. Clean reads were aligned to the available T. rosa reference genome using HISAT2 v2.2.1, followed by quantitative analysis of gene expression in each sample using StringTie v3.0.3 [21,22]. The edgeR package v3.36.0 in R v4.1.3 was used to identify DEGs [23]. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change of at least 1 and an FDR of no more than 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Candidate DEGs were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses.

2.6. Short Time-Series Expression Miner Analysis

The short time-series expression profiles of DEGs identified across the CON, SV30, SV60, and SV90 groups were analyzed using the short time-series expression miner (STEM) program v1.3.13, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 [24]. The CON, SV30, SV60, and SV90 groups were included in the STEM analysis to characterize coherent temporal gene expression trajectories during continuous food deprivation relative to the common initial physiological state. This analysis was designed to identify coordinated early, progressive, and late expression patterns across the full starvation period. Transcriptomic data from FD30, FD60, and FD90 were used for descriptive analysis of selected autophagy-related genes, whereas the principal STEM analysis focused on the continuous starvation series.

The clusterProfiler package v4.2.2 in R was used to perform GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the gene sets that exhibited significant trends. The results were visualized using bubble charts generated with the ggplot2 package v3.4.2. For autophagy-related heatmaps, the normalized transcript abundance was converted to row Z-scores and visualized using the heatmap package in R.

2.7. RT-qPCR Validation

To validate the transcriptome results, RT-qPCR was used to analyze the expression levels of 16 DEGs using the QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The identified DEGs were ACACA, APOB, ATG4D, ATG5, BECN1, C8β, C9-like, CD8α, Dnmt1, ERp72-1, ERp72-2, IL10, SIK2, SYK, TCRβ, and ULK1. Expression levels were assessed to confirm their consistency with the transcriptome data. Each group comprised nine samples, with three samples pooled to form three replicates. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by first-strand cDNA synthesis using the RevertAid First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The reaction mixture was prepared by combining 5 μL of 2X SYBR Green Abstart PCR Mix (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), 0.3 μL of each forward and reverse primer, and 1 μL of the template, with ddH2O added to reach a total volume of 10 μL. The PCR protocol was as follows: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of amplification at 95 °C for 15 s, 58 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 45 s. The expression stability values for β-actin, EF1α, 18S, and GAPDH, evaluated across all the groups using NormFinder v0.953 (Department of Molecular Medicine, Aarhus University Hospital, Aarhus, Denmark), were 0.215, 0.303, 1.417, and 1.503, respectively. As β-actin had the lowest NormFinder stability value, this reference gene was selected for RT-qPCR normalization. Detailed information on the primers used in this study is shown in Table 1. The relative expression levels of target genes in T. rosa were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method, as described by Livak and Schmittgen [25], with the results visualized in GraphPad Prism 9.

2.8. TEM Analysis

TEM was used to examine autophagy-related ultrastructural features in the liver and spleen [26]. TEM was performed using three biological replicates from each of the CON, SV30, and SV90 groups. Liver and spleen fragments were fixed in 2.5% EM-grade glutaraldehyde (Cat. No. P1126; Solarbio, Beijing, China) for TEM. These groups represented the initial condition, early starvation, and prolonged starvation, respectively, and were selected to compare the early ultrastructural response with the prolonged starvation endpoint. Samples were then dehydrated through a graded acetone series, infiltrated, embedded, and cut into ultrathin sections. After staining, autophagy-related structures in hepatocytes and splenic macrophages were observed and photographed using a JEM-1400-FLASH transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Three TEM images were examined for each sample, and representative images were selected for presentation.

3. Results

3.1. Starvation Effects on the Growth of T. rosa

Growth-related indicators were assessed in all the groups (Figure 1). Relative to CON, body weight, condition factor, and hepatosomatic index increased in the fed groups and decreased in the starved groups. Starvation therefore markedly inhibited growth in T. rosa. In the starved groups, the WLR after 30 days was 13%, whereas WLR values after 60 and 90 days ranged from 12% to 18%. CF and HSI tended to decrease with starvation duration, although the differences among the starved groups were not significant. These changes are consistent with suppressed somatic growth and mobilization of hepatic reserves under starvation conditions.

3.2. Effects of Starvation on Liver Histology

The liver histology results are shown in Figure 2. Compared with the fed groups, the control group showed relatively denser hepatocyte distribution and smaller hepatocyte size, while its overall morphology was similar to that of the starved groups, particularly SV30 (Figure 2A,B). In the fed groups, the liver structure was relatively intact, with swollen polygonal cells containing abundant cytoplasmic vacuoles. The nuclei were mostly positioned at the cell periphery, and the nuclear boundaries were unclear (Figure 2C–H). After prolonged starvation (SV60 and SV90), the liver tissue exhibited increased looseness, expanded intercellular spaces, and disorganized hepatic cords. Hepatocytes underwent significant shrinkage, with reduced cell volume, a transition from polygonal to round or oval shapes, and less cytoplasm (Figure 2I–N).

Hepatocyte area was significantly larger in the fed groups than in the control group (p < 0.05). However, differences in hepatocyte area among the fed groups were not significant. Conversely, hepatocyte area was significantly smaller in the starved groups than in the control group (p < 0.05). Hepatocyte area did not differ significantly among the SV30, SV60, and SV90 groups (p > 0.05; Figure 3).

3.3. Effects of Starvation on Spleen Histology

Compared with the control group, the spleen capsule in the fed groups remained thin, with no obvious changes in cellular composition or signs of congestion or inflammation (Figure 4). Moreover, no prominent melano-macrophage centers were observed. In the starved groups, the splenic capsule also remained thin, with no apparent cellular proliferation, cell loss, or inflammatory cell infiltration. However, melano-macrophage centers became more prominent after 60 and 90 days of starvation. No obvious pathological alterations were observed in CON.

3.4. Effects of Starvation on the Liver Transcriptome

After sequencing and filtering, 560,680,466 clean reads were obtained from the liver samples. Subsequent FastQC quality control analysis showed that Q20 and Q30 values exceeded 95% and 89%, respectively (Table 2). When aligned to the reference genome, more than 90% of reads were successfully mapped to the T. rosa reference genome (Table S1), indicating a high-quality liver transcriptomic dataset.

Temporal expression analysis was performed on liver DEGs under different durations of starvation, using the control group as the reference, to characterize dynamic changes in gene expression over time. Genes with similar temporal dynamics were clustered using STEM, with a line chart depicting overall gene expression trends within each module. Profiles with statistically significant expression patterns (p < 0.05) are shown in color in Figure 5. STEM analysis grouped 1541 liver DEGs into 10 profiles based on gene expression patterns across time points, with only profiles 1 and 9 showing significant expression patterns. Profile 1 demonstrated a decreasing trend after 30, 60, and 90 days of starvation, whereas profile 9 exhibited an increasing trend.

Subsequently, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the profiles with significant temporal expression patterns (Figure 6). The DEGs in profile 1, which exhibited a decreasing trend, were notably enriched in biosynthetic processes related to cholesterol and steroids, metabolic processes including neutral lipid and cholesterol metabolism, and fatty acid elongation. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that the DEGs in profile 1 were notably enriched in steroid biosynthesis, ribosomes, fatty acid biosynthesis, cholesterol metabolism, protein digestion and absorption, and cell-cycle pathways. The DEGs in profile 9, which showed an increasing trend, were mainly enriched in terms related to endoplasmic reticulum stress, metabolism, and cellular homeostasis. The first category was linked to endoplasmic reticulum stress, encompassing terms such as response to unfolded proteins, response to endoplasmic reticulum stress, and autophagy. The second category focused on metabolism, including processes such as oligosaccharide catabolism and glycine metabolism. The third category included other terms such as cation homeostasis. Additionally, KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment in protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum and AMPK signaling pathways. Together, these enrichment patterns associated the declining profile with biosynthetic and proliferative processes and the increasing profile with cellular stress responses and homeostatic processes during prolonged starvation.

3.5. Effects of Starvation on the Spleen Transcriptome

Following sequencing and filtering, a total of 601,156,350 clean reads were obtained from spleen tissues. FastQC analysis showed that Q20 and Q30 values exceeded 96% and 90%, respectively (Table 3). Additionally, more than 89% of reads were successfully mapped to the T. rosa reference genome (Table S2), indicating a high-quality spleen transcriptomic dataset.

For spleen starvation time-series analysis, we used STEM to analyze 3291 DEGs from control, SV30, SV60, and SV90 groups to explore dynamic expression patterns during food deprivation. Among the 10 profiles examined, four showed significant expression patterns (profiles 0, 1, 7, and 9) (Figure 7). Profile 0 decreased in SV30, increased in SV60, and subsequently declined in SV90. Profile 7 initially increased in SV30, followed by a declining trend in SV60 and SV90 groups. Profiles 9 and 1 showed upward and downward trends, respectively. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted on the four profiles with significant temporal expression patterns.

Most significantly enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways in profile 0 were associated with spliceosome-related processes (Figure 8). In profile 7, gene expression initially increased, then decreased, and many of the top 20 enriched GO terms were related to immunity. These terms included regulation of CD8-positive alpha-beta T cell activation, complement activation, regulation of the inflammatory response to antigenic stimuli, antigen processing and presentation of exogenous peptide antigen via MHC class I, and positive regulation of interleukin 10 production. Regarding KEGG pathways, genes in profile 7 were significantly enriched in antigen processing and presentation, T cell receptor signaling, phagosome, and Fc epsilon RI signaling pathways. The DEGs in profile 9, which exhibited increased gene expression, were notably associated with GO terms such as SRP-dependent co-translational protein targeting to membranes and endoplasmic reticulum polarization. These DEGs were also linked to proteasome and protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum pathways. Conversely, profile 1 showed significant enrichment in GO terms associated with decreasing trends, such as cell cycle, regulation of DNA replication, and organ growth. Furthermore, KEGG pathway analysis revealed enrichment of pathways including ribosomes, DNA replication, and the intestinal immune network for IgA production. Together, the enriched profiles were associated with stage-dependent changes in immune-related processes, protein synthesis, DNA replication, and growth-associated processes in the spleen during prolonged starvation.

3.6. Effects of Starvation on Autophagy in the Liver and Spleen

After observing autophagy-related enrichment in the starvation time-series analysis, we further examined the expression patterns of autophagy-related genes across the control, fed, and starved groups. In the liver, AMPKα-2 showed higher relative expression after 60 and 90 days of starvation (Figure 9). TSC1 and TSC2—upstream negative regulators of mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)—also showed higher relative expression in the starved groups, particularly SV90. Key genes involved in the autophagy pathway, including ULK1, ULK2, BECN1, ATG4b, ATG5, ATG12, ATG13, and LC3, showed higher relative expression in SV90. Similarly, in the spleen, AMPKα-2 showed higher relative expression after 60 and 90 days of starvation, accompanied by higher relative expression of TSC1 and TSC2. Notably, the key autophagy-related genes ULK1, ULK2, BECN1, ATG4b, ATG5, ATG12, and ATG13, together with both LC3 isoforms, exhibited higher relative expression in SV90. Overall, these descriptive expression patterns are consistent with increased autophagy-related transcription in the liver and spleen during prolonged starvation.

3.7. TEM Observations of Autophagy in the Liver and Spleen

Transcriptomic analysis showed increased expression of autophagy-related genes in the liver and spleen during prolonged starvation. TEM was therefore used to examine corresponding ultrastructural features in hepatocytes and splenic macrophages from the CON, SV30, and SV90 groups (Figure 10). Hepatocytes in the control group showed intact organelle structures and few autophagy-related structures. The SV30 group showed double-membrane autophagosome-like structures and single-membrane autolysosome-like structures containing partially degraded cytoplasmic material. These structures were more prominent in representative hepatocyte images of SV90. Similarly, autophagy-related structures were observed in splenic macrophages of SV30 and became more prominent in representative images of SV90. Together, these ultrastructural observations are consistent with increased involvement of autophagy-associated intracellular recycling during prolonged starvation.

3.8. Validation of Gene Expression via RT-qPCR Analysis

According to NormFinder analysis, β-actin had the lowest stability value among the four candidate reference genes and was therefore selected for RT-qPCR normalization (Table S3). RT-qPCR and RNA sequencing showed largely consistent expression trends for the selected genes, supporting the consistency of these transcriptomic expression patterns (Figure 11).

4. Discussion

4.1. Effect of Starvation on T. rosa Growth

Food scarcity poses a substantial challenge to cave-dwelling organisms owing to limited and unpredictable food sources [11]. Cavefish exhibit diverse physiological and metabolic responses to persistent food limitation. For example, cave-dwelling Astyanax mexicanus show increased appetite when food is available but exhibit hypertrophic fat cells that reduce weight loss during starvation, which improves starvation tolerance [27,28]. In this study, T. rosa experienced significant decreases in body weight, CF, HSI, and hepatocyte area under starvation. Body weight loss during starvation (12–18%) was similar to that reported in cave populations of A. mexicanus. Megalobrama amblycephala lost 18.25% of its body weight after 28 days of starvation [29], whereas juvenile Erythroculter ilishaeformis showed a WLR of 21.36% after 16 days of starvation [30]. Under our experimental conditions, T. rosa survived 90 days of food deprivation, with WLR values of approximately 12–18%. This observation documents sustained survival during prolonged starvation, while its comparative significance remains to be evaluated using closely related surface-dwelling species. Under starvation stress, fish generally rely on stored energy reserves, particularly hepatic lipid reserves, to meet their energy demands [31]. In T. rosa, food deprivation was accompanied by decreased hepatocyte size, consistent with mobilization of hepatic reserves. This pattern is consistent with findings in other fish species, such as black rockfish [32]. Therefore, the observed changes in body weight and hepatocyte size are consistent with progressive mobilization of hepatic reserves during prolonged starvation.

4.2. Effect of Starvation on T. rosa Metabolism

Time-series analysis of liver DEGs after starvation revealed marked changes in energy metabolism and allocation. Genes with declining temporal expression were enriched in fatty acid biosynthesis, cholesterol metabolism, protein digestion and absorption, ribosomal function, and cell-cycle pathways. These findings are consistent with previous liver transcriptome analyses in zebrafish and larval C. nasus following starvation treatment [8,33]. During nutrient scarcity, animals mobilize stored nutrients to meet energy demands and actively regulate their metabolic pathways to support survival [34]. Because biosynthetic and cell-cycle processes require substantial energy, the declining expression of genes associated with these pathways is consistent with reduced investment in biosynthetic and proliferative activity during nutrient limitation. Together, these results indicate that prolonged starvation was accompanied by lipid reserve mobilization and broader adjustments in hepatic biosynthetic and proliferative processes.

4.3. Effect of Starvation on T. rosa Immunity

Most starvation studies have focused on physiological and metabolic responses, with relatively little research on immune regulation under starvation [35]. In this study, we investigated starvation-associated changes in splenic immune-related processes in T. rosa by analyzing the spleen, a major secondary immune organ in fish. Although the thymus and head kidney are also important immune organs, their small size and the limited body size of T. rosa hindered consistent sampling. Therefore, the spleen was selected for histological and transcriptomic analyses. Histological observations showed that melano-macrophage centers became more prominent after prolonged starvation, whereas no obvious inflammatory infiltration or severe pathological alterations were observed.

Time-series transcriptome analysis further revealed dynamic changes in splenic immune-related transcription during starvation. Genes assigned to the transient expression profile were enriched in antigen processing and presentation, T cell receptor signaling, complement activation, inflammatory regulation, and phagosome-related pathways; their expression increased during early starvation and declined at later stages. Genes associated with protein synthesis, DNA replication, and organ growth showed declining temporal expression with increasing starvation duration. These patterns suggest that splenic immune-related signaling and biosynthetic activity were dynamically adjusted as nutrient limitation progressed.

The effects of food deprivation on immunity depend on its duration and severity; short-term starvation may induce transient immune-related responses, whereas prolonged nutrient limitation can alter immune regulation and tissue maintenance in fish and mammals [6,36,37,38]. In T. rosa under starvation conditions, the early increase in immune-related transcription was followed by a greater prominence of melano-macrophage centers with prolonged starvation. Melano-macrophage centers are involved in phagocytic processing and the sequestration of cellular debris, damaged erythrocytes, pigments, and oxidized materials. Their increased prominence is therefore consistent with chronic physiological stress and greater involvement of immune-related clearance and tissue maintenance. Together, these findings indicate a staged splenic response in which early immune-related signaling was followed by increasing involvement of cellular clearance and tissue homeostasis with progressive starvation.

4.4. Effect of Starvation on T. rosa Autophagy

Starvation increases reactive oxygen species generation, leading to oxidative stress and subsequent endoplasmic reticulum stress. This stress response can activate autophagy, helping to eliminate oxidatively damaged molecules within cells through autophagic lysosomes. In this process, organelles undergo self-digestion and degradation, which in turn helps mitigate endoplasmic reticulum stress, safeguard cell survival, and maintain overall homeostasis [10]. Notably, we observed enrichment of autophagy-related terms in the transcriptome. Further examination of autophagy-related genes showed higher relative expression of AMPKα-2, the upstream negative regulators of mTOR, TSC1 and TSC2, and several key autophagy-related genes after 90 days of starvation. Under conditions of diminished ATP levels, AMPK can phosphorylate TSC2, which inhibits mTOR complex activity and promotes autophagy [39]. The coordinated expression patterns of AMPKα-2, TSC1, TSC2, and downstream autophagy-related genes suggest the involvement of AMPK and mTOR-associated regulation in the autophagy-related response of T. rosa to prolonged starvation.

Representative TEM images show that autophagy-related structures became more prominent in hepatocytes and splenic macrophages during prolonged starvation, consistent with the transcriptomic expression patterns. Similar starvation-associated changes in autophagy have been reported in zebrafish embryonic cells and skeletal muscle [40]. Autophagy is an important survival mechanism during nutrient scarcity because it recycles proteins and damaged organelles through autolysosomes, providing substrates for cellular maintenance and energy production [41]. Together, the transcriptional and ultrastructural observations support an increasing contribution of autophagy-associated intracellular recycling to cellular maintenance during prolonged starvation.

In summary, prolonged starvation in T. rosa was accompanied by coordinated temporal changes across tissues. Mobilization of hepatic reserves and reduced biosynthetic activity occurred alongside stage-dependent changes in splenic immune-related processes, as well as increasing involvement of melano-macrophage centers in cellular clearance and tissue maintenance with prolonged starvation. Concurrent changes in autophagy-related gene expression and ultrastructures further support an increasing contribution of intracellular recycling to cellular maintenance. This cross-tissue framework links organismal condition with tissue-specific molecular remodeling, and provides a basis for future comparative and functional studies of prolonged nutrient limitation in cavefish.

4.5. Limitations of the Study

This study presents an integrated temporal framework for understanding starvation responses in a poorly studied cave-restricted fish species; however, several aspects limit the scope of its interpretation. First, no closely related surface-dwelling species was examined under equal experimental conditions. Therefore, the evolutionary specificity of the observed responses requires further investigation through direct comparative studies. Second, T. rosa was collected from its natural cave habitat because a stable captive breeding population has not yet been established. The limited availability of suitable wild individuals constrained the scale of experimental sampling and the extent of time-matched comparisons. Establishing a captive breeding system will allow future studies to expand sample availability and refine comparative experimental designs. Finally, we used transcriptomic and ultrastructural analyses to identify metabolic, immune-related, and autophagy-related processes associated with prolonged starvation, but did not experimentally examine the functional contributions of these responses. Therefore, comparative studies with related surface-dwelling species and targeted manipulation of key regulatory pathways will be important for defining the evolutionary and physiological significance of the observed responses.

5. Conclusions

In this study, we characterized the temporal physiological and molecular responses of T. rosa to 30, 60, and 90 days of food deprivation by integrating growth measurements, histology, transcriptomics, RT-qPCR, and TEM. Prolonged starvation was accompanied by reduced growth indices, hepatocyte shrinkage, and declining expression of genes associated with lipid biosynthesis, cholesterol metabolism, ribosomal function, and the cell cycle, consistent with hepatic reserve mobilization and reduced biosynthetic activity. In the spleen, immune-related transcription showed a stage-dependent pattern, while the later prominence of melano-macrophage centers was consistent with increasing involvement of cellular clearance and tissue homeostasis. In parallel, increased expression of autophagy-related genes and the greater prominence of autophagy-related structures were consistent with an increasing contribution of intracellular recycling during prolonged starvation. Together, these findings connect organismal condition with tissue-specific molecular remodeling and reveal a coordinated shift from growth and biosynthetic activity toward cellular clearance, tissue maintenance, and intracellular recycling. This integrated temporal framework provides a basis for understanding physiological maintenance during sustained nutrient limitation in a cave-restricted fish species. Comparative studies with closely related surface species and targeted functional experiments will be important for defining the evolutionary specificity and functional contribution of these responses.
