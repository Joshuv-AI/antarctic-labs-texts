Body mass index, prebiotic supplementation during pregnancy and gestational diabetes mellitus risk: an effect modification analysis from a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Rachelle A Pretorius, Thomas R Sullivan, Summer V M Walker, Johan Garssen, Jeffrey A Keelan, Desiree Silva, Susan L Prescott, Debra J Palmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrebiotic dietary supplementation has been shown to improve glucose homeostasis in type 2 diabetes patients. The aim of this analysis was to determine whether pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) modifies the effect of prebiotic supplementation from mid-pregnancy on reducing the risk of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM).MethodsIn a double-blinded, randomised controlled trial, pregnant women < 21 gestational weeks were randomly assigned (1:1) to consume daily prebiotics (14.2 g galacto-oligosaccharides and fructo-oligosaccharides) or placebo (8.7 g maltodextrin) powder. An effect modification analysis was performed to assess the heterogeneity of the effect of prebiotic supplementation in relation to pre-pregnancy BMI on GDM diagnosis.ResultsBetween June 2016 and November 2021, 329 women were assigned to the prebiotic group (50.4%), and 323 (49.5%) were assigned to the placebo group. Overall, 288 of 652 women (44.2%) were classified as overweight/obese prior to pregnancy (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2). The distribution was balanced, with 146/329 (44.4%) randomised to the prebiotics group and 142/323 (44.0%) in the placebo group. Pre-pregnancy BMI modified the effect of prebiotic supplementation, with the intervention reducing GDM rates in women with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (prebiotic group 11.0% vs. control group 21.8%; adjusted relative risk 0.50; 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.28 to 0.89) but not in women with a pre-pregnancy BMI < 25 kg/m2 (7.7–7% vs. 4.4%; adjusted relative risk 1.72; 95% CI 0.70 to 4.19; interaction p = 0.02).ConclusionPre-pregnancy BMI was found to modify the effect of prebiotic supplementation on GDM rates, with benefits observed in overweight and obese women. Our results highlight a target population for future randomised controlled trials to further investigate the effects of prebiotic supplementation during pregnancy on reducing the risk of GDM. Introduction

Prebiotics are a type of dietary fibre that selectively stimulates the growth of beneficial gut microbiota [1] and produces anti-inflammatory short-chain fatty acids through fermentation. Two of the most studied prebiotics used for dietary supplementation are galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS) (produced either by synthesis or by extraction and hydrolysis of plant sources such as pulses or lactose) and fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS) (produced either by enzymatic synthesis or by hydrolysis of inulin from natural sources, mainly from the roots of chicory, artichoke, yacon, dahlia or agave) [2].

We conducted a double-blinded, randomised controlled trial (RCT) (n = 652) involving supplementation with maternal prebiotics (GOS and FOS) from < 21 weeks gestation [3]. The trial was designed to determine the effects of maternal prebiotic supplementation during pregnancy and lactation on child allergic disease outcomes [3]. After half the participants had given birth, an interim safety monitoring analysis was undertaken to compare the rates of any pregnancy complications between the two dietary supplementation groups. In this interim analysis (unpublished data), a trend towards a lower incidence of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) in the prebiotic-supplemented group than in the placebo group was noted. These findings are particularly important given the increasing global prevalence of GDM, which poses short- and long-term health consequences for both mothers and their children [4].

The daily dose amounts of each study powder for the RCT were designed to ensure a matching energy content of 143 kilojoules per day. The interim analysis revealed no differences in gestational weight gain between the two groups. Importantly, our trial’s maternal faecal microbiota analysis [5] revealed maternal microbiome changes in the prebiotic group but not in the placebo group. The findings from this study show that prebiotic supplementation was associated with an increased abundance of commensal Bifidobacteria and a reduction in the abundance of Negativicutes, along with increased stool acetic acid concentrations [5].

To date, no interventional studies have specifically examined the effect of prebiotic fibre supplementation on GDM. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis on the effects of microbiome-modulating probiotics, prebiotics and synbiotics on glucose homeostasis in type 2 diabetes patients revealed that pro-/synbiotic supplementation improved glucose homeostasis in diabetic patients [6]. Interestingly, the effect of prebiotics of − 0.45% on HbA1c (assessed by 12 trials) was more promising than the effect of multistrain probiotics of − 0.28% on HbA1c (assessed by 16 trials). Similarly, another 2022 network meta-analysis revealed that prebiotic interventions reduced HbA1c levels in individuals with type 2 diabetes compared with those in control individuals (standardised mean difference − 0.43; 95% CI − 0.77 to − 0.08; P = 0.02) [6]. These findings suggest that prebiotic dietary supplementation may be an effective strategy for treating patients with type 2 diabetes.

During pregnancy, two randomised controlled trials [7, 8] have investigated the effects of maternal dietary supplementation with 24 g dietary fibre powder per day compared with usual care (control group) from 20–24+ 6 weeks gestation on GDM diagnosis. Cao and colleagues [7] randomised women (n = 295) with a triglyceride‒glucose index ≥ 8.5 and reported 11.2% GDM in the fibre group compared with 23.7% in the control group (P = 0.02). Zhang and colleagues [8] randomised women (n = 98) with a pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) ≥ 24 kg/m2 and reported 8.3% GDM in the fibre group compared with 24.0% in the control group (P = 0.04). Hence, as the women in our trial were stratified by pre-pregnancy BMI (< 25 or ≥ 25 kg/m2), we undertook an exploratory analysis to examine whether pre-pregnancy BMI modified the effect of prebiotic supplementation on the outcome of GDM.

Methods

Study design and setting

This trial was a two-arm (1:1 allocation), parallel-design, double-blinded, randomised controlled trial conducted at one site in Perth, Western Australia. The trial was nested within the ORIGINS Project birth cohort [9], and the sponsoring institution was The Kids Research Institute Australia, Western Australia. The trial protocol and primary outcome results for infants with medically diagnosed eczema have been previously published [3, 10]. The trial was registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trial Registry: ACTRN12615001075572, https://www.anzctr.org.au/on13/10/2015. The manuscript was written according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines (see supplementary data 2).

Participants

The trial protocol, including inclusion and exclusion criteria, has been previously published [3, 10]. In brief, and relevant to this analysis, the participants were pregnant women < 21 weeks of gestation, aged ≥ 18 years, non-smokers during pregnancy and not already consuming prebiotic supplements more than twice per week. Baseline data were recorded, including age, ethnicity, education, any prior prebiotic or probiotic supplement use, pre-pregnancy weight, height, and current weight from all particpants who provided formal consent.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from The Joondalup Health Campus (approval number 1451r) and University of Western Australia (RA/4/1/8137) Human Research Ethics Committees. This study was conducted based on the ethical guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration. Once eligibility was confirmed, written informed consent was obtained prior to randomisation (for full process refer to published protocol and primary outcome results [3, 10]. This study was conducted based on the ethical guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration. We affirm that this manuscript presents an honest, accurate, and transparent account of the study. The trial was registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trial Registry: ACTRN12615001075572, https://www.anzctr.org.au/on13/10/2015.

Intervention

The participating women were randomised to either a prebiotic group and asked to consume 14.2 g per day of prebiotic powder (GOS and FOS ratio by weight 9:1) or a placebo control group and asked to consume 8.7 g per day of maltodextrin powder [3,10]. To maintain blinding, both the prebiotic and placebo powders were packaged in identical tins. Both study powders were similar in taste, texture and colour. The differences in daily dose weights were accommodated by powders of different sizes, which were concealed within the tins to ensure blinding. All the study powders were manufactured, packaged, and labelled (with a unique study ID) by an independent company, Danone, Netherlands, according to Good Manufacturing Procedures. The trial was investigator-initiated, and Danone had no other involvement other than the provision of study powders. The participants, research staff and data analysts were blinded to the randomisation group allocation.

The trial randomisation process has been previously published [3, 10], of relevance to this effect modification analysis presented here, participants were randomised with stratification by pre-pregnancy BMI (< 25 or ≥ 25 kg/m2). The World Health Organization considers individuals with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 to be classified as overweight or obese. Women who enter pregnancy with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 may be at increased risk of GDM [11]. The daily dose amounts of each study powder were designed to ensure a matching energy content of 143 kilojoules per day. Both study powders were mixed with food or beverages and consumed orally by the participating women once per day. The intervention period commenced from < 21 weeks gestation, and the women were asked not to consume any other prebiotic products during the intervention period.

Assessments

The research team maintained regular (in-person or telephone) monthly contact with participants to monitor study powder consumption adherence. Women attended an appointment at 18–20 weeks (randomisation baseline) and at 28 weeks gestation, during which their weight was measured. Medically diagnosed GDM during pregnancy data (classified as a binary yes/no variable) was collected via results from the oral glucose tolerance test routinely administered to pregnant women at approximately 28 weeks gestation in Australia, or determined based on documented medical diagnosis during the routine antenatal care (reported in medical records) or by participant self-reporting during a telephone call after birth (if the oral glucose tolerance test results or access to medical records were not available). Some participants completed the Australian Eating Survey Food Frequency Questionnaire [12] dietary intake assessment at trial entry baseline at 18–20 weeks gestation.

Statistical analysis

Effect modification on GDM by pre-pregnancy BMI was assessed in a subgroup analysis undertaken on an intention-to-treat basis. Analysis was performed by fitting a log binomial model that included an interaction term between the treatment group and pre-pregnancy BMI, along with corresponding main effects, with treatment effects described as relative risks with 95% confidence intervals. Pre-pregnancy BMI was modelled as a categorical variable (< 25 or ≥ 25 kg/m2) per the trial stratification, while adjustments were made for maternal allergy status (history of medically diagnosed asthma, allergic rhinitis, eczema and/or food allergy), which was the other trial stratification variable. For consistency with primary trial analyses, missing data on GDM were addressed using multiple imputation implemented under a missing-at-random assumption, with 100 complete datasets generated for analysis. Imputation was performed separately by a randomised group using identically specified logistic chained equations. Further details on the imputation approach have been published previously [10]. As a sensitivity analysis, effect modification by pre-pregnancy BMI was also evaluated in women with observed GDM data. In addition to GDM, we also considered a subgroup analysis for weight gain from baseline to 28 weeks gestation, with the observed data analysed via linear regression and effects described as the mean differences. All analyses were performed via the R statistical environment, version 4.3.1 (R Core Team, 2023).

Characteristics of the participants at baseline

Baseline dietary intake data was collected for 216 of 652 participants (33.1%) at 18–20 weeks gestation (113 in the prebiotic group and 103 in the placebo group). Baseline mean (± standard deviation) daily energy intake was 8517 kJ (± 2707) for the prebiotic group and 8586 kJ (± 2547) for the placebo group. Baseline daily median (IQR) dietary fibre intakes were 27.0 g (IQR 15.219, 38.9) for the prebiotic group and 26.3 g (IQR 16.9, 35.8) for the placebo group.

Overall, 288/652 (44.2%) women were classified as overweight/obese with a pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, with 146/329 (44.4%) randomised to the prebiotics group and 142/323 (44.0%) in the placebo group. Twenty-two women in the prebiotic group and twenty-three women in the placebo group had missing data on GDM outcomes (6.9% missing data overall), while a further three were excluded from GDM analyses due to the occurrence of a stillbirth.

For pre-pregnancy overweight/obese women (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2), the rate of GDM was 47/287 (16.4%), whereas it was 22/362 (6.1%) for women with a pre-pregnancy BMI < 25 kg/m2. Pre-pregnancy BMI modified the effect of prebiotic supplementation (interaction P = 0.02), with supplementation reducing GDM rates in women with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (prebiotic group 11.0% vs. control group 21.8%; adjusted relative risk 0.50; 95% CI 0.28 to 0.89) but not in those with a BMI < 25 kg/m2 (7.7% vs. 4.4%; adjusted relative risk 1.72; 95% CI 0.70 to 4.19; Table 3). Conversely, there was little evidence of effect modification on maternal weight gain per week from randomisation to 28 weeks of gestation (interaction P = 0.65; Table S1, see supplementary data 2).

Discussion

Our results suggest that overweight/obese women with a pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 could benefit from prebiotic dietary supplementation during pregnancy to reduce the risk of developing GDM. This is an important finding, especially in our Australian setting, where the proportion of women who were overweight/obese before pregnancy in 2022 was 50% [4] and where GDM rates have been increasing over the past decade from 8.3% in 2014 to 17% in 2022 [4]. The overall total rate of 10.6% GDM in our trial participants is representative of our Western Australian population during the trial period, where the rate of GDM increased from 9.3% in 2016 to 12.8% in 2021 [4].

Our results in the overweight/obese female subgroup (n = 287) with 11.0% GDM in the prebiotic group compared with 21.8% in the control group were similar in magnitude to those in the 24 g/day fibre supplementation trial by Zhang and colleagues [8], which randomised women (n = 98) with a pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2 and found 8.3% GDM in the fibre group compared with 24.0% in the usual care control group. Our prebiotic powder dose was lower at 14.2 g/day, which may be important for practical implementation, although both trials reported similar powder consumption compliance rates. Our trial had an average of 85.7% compliance from 18 to 20 weeks to 28 weeks of gestation, and in the shorter intervention period trial by Zhang and colleagues [8], they reported 94.7% powder consumption between 20 and 24+ 6 weeks of gestation.

To date, our trial is the largest completed maternal prebiotic dietary supplementation during pregnancy trial. A strength of our trial design was the use of study powders with matching energy content (143 kilojoules/day), which likely explains our finding of no difference in weekly weight gain between the prebiotic and control powder groups. Another strength of our trial was the double-blinded design, which reduced possible bias from the participating women, who might otherwise have changed their dietary intake if they had become aware of their study group allocation. However, we acknowledge that a limitation of our trial was the lack of other dietary intake data collected during this period.

Our trial was designed to determine the effects of maternal prebiotic supplementation on child allergic disease outcomes. The inclusion of pregnancy complication outcomes, such as GDM, was not the main trial focus. Hence, a limitation for further interpretation of the results from this exploratory analysis is that other GDM-related baseline and outcome data, such as glucose metabolism and HbA1c level data, were not collected as part of this trial. We also acknowledge that the use of maltodextrin as a placebo powder would not have been the ideal choice for a trial focused on a GDM primary outcome, as it has a higher glycaemic index. We chose maltodextrin as the placebo, as this trial was designed to investigate maternal prebiotic dietary supplementation as an allergy prevention strategy. Previous infant formula trials, which investigated the effects of prebiotic supplementation on child allergic disease outcomes, also used maltodextrin as a placebo [13, 14]. Maltodextrin was also chosen to enhance blinding to group allocation in our trial because its taste, texture, and appearance are similar to those of the prebiotic powder. In 2016, when our trial commenced, maltodextrin was widely used as a food additive and commonly used as a placebo powder in clinical trials. Notably, maltodextrin has been widely used as a placebo in prebiotic intervention trials, including those investigating its effects on type 1 and type 2 diabetes outcomes. Across four meta-analyses published between 2015 and 2022 [6, 15–17], 50–75% of the included studies with diabetes outcomes used maltodextrin as a placebo. A 2022 systematic review raised the question regarding the use of maltodextrin as a placebo because of its potential effects on the gut microbiota [18]. However, in our 2024 stool microbiome study [5], we were able to demonstrate maternal microbiome changes in the prebiotic group but not in the maltodextrin placebo group.

We acknowledge the exploratory (not prespecified) nature of this effect modification subgroup analysis, which focused on the outcome of GDM; however, our results provide justification for further investigation. We also recognise that a limitation of our trial participant population was that it was from one Australian city and had predominantly Caucasian/White participants; hence, our results may not be generalizable to women in other geographical settings and of other ethnicities. Therefore, future trials investigating the effects of prebiotic dietary supplementation to reduce the risk of GDM should be multisite and have varied ethnic diversity within the study population to increase the generalizability of the results.

In conclusion, dietary supplementation with prebiotics (GOS and FOS) during pregnancy reduced GDM rates in overweight/obese pregnant women. Our results provide valuable pilot data and highlight a target population of overweight/obese women for future randomised controlled trials to investigate the effects of prebiotic dietary supplementation during pregnancy on glucose metabolism and the risk of GDM development. We recommend that future trials investigating the effects of maternal prebiotic dietary supplementation on GDM as the primary outcome consider using an alternative placebo powder (such as cellulose, a nonfermentable type of dietary fibre), measuring multiple glucose metabolism biomarkers, and conducting detailed assessments of participant dietary intake.
