Recurrent primary hyperparathyroidism with ipsilateral multifocal papillary thyroid carcinoma of classical, warthin-like, and follicular variants harboring distinct BRAF and NRAS mutations: a rare case report
Authors: Shaima Ali Maghdi, Jobran M Moshi, Alanoud Awaji Hakami, Mutaz Abdulrahman S Alahdal, Aamir Gazali M Ishaq, Ahmed Mohsen Safhi, Fahd Alaajam, Ali Baity, Siddig Ibrahim Abdelwahab, Humaid O Al-Shamsi

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe coexistence of primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) and papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) occurs in 2–5% of surgical series. Recurrent PHPT following parathyroidectomy presents additional clinical challenges. While synchronous PHPT and PTC have been documented, the simultaneous occurrence of multifocal PTC exhibiting three distinct histological variants with different oncogenic driver mutations, accompanied by ipsilateral parathyroid pathology, appears not to have been previously reported in the available literature.Case presentationA 48-year-old Saudi female with hypothyroidism underwent left inferior parathyroidectomy and left hemithyroidectomy for biochemically confirmed PHPT. Initial histopathology revealed Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and parathyroid follicular cell lesion. Six months postoperatively, she developed biochemical recurrence (serum calcium 2.77 mmol/L; PTH 21 pg/mL). Subsequent neck ultrasound demonstrated a suspicious right thyroid nodule (TIRADS 5), confirmed as Bethesda category V on fine-needle aspiration cytology. Completion right thyroidectomy with right inferior parathyroidectomy revealed multifocal PTC comprising classical variant foci, a 1.2 cm Warthin-like variant harboring BRAF V600E mutation, and a 0.2 cm follicular variant with NRAS G13R mutation. Concurrent right inferior parathyroid adenoma/hyperplasia was identified. Molecular testing (Idylla Biocartis platform) confirmed distinct mutational profiles, establishing independent clonal origins.ConclusionTo our knowledge, after review of the available literature, this appears to be the first reported case of recurrent PHPT with synchronous ipsilateral parathyroid adenoma/hyperplasia and multifocal PTC comprising three histological variants with distinct molecular alterations. This unique presentation underscores the necessity for comprehensive thyroid evaluation in PHPT patients, the utility of molecular profiling in establishing tumor clonality, and the importance of individualized surgical planning in complex endocrine neoplasia.Keywords: Primary hyperparathyroidism, Papillary thyroid carcinoma, Warthin-like variant, Multifocal thyroid cancer, Parathyroid adenoma

Introduction

Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) represents the most prevalent parathyroid disorder, characterized by autonomous parathyroid hormone (PTH) secretion resulting in hypercalcemia [1, 2]. While solitary parathyroid adenomas account for approximately 85% of cases, multiglandular hyperplasia (10–15%) and parathyroid carcinoma (< 1%) constitute less frequent etiologies [3–5]. Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), representing more than 80% of differentiated thyroid malignancies, exhibits substantial histological heterogeneity with multiple recognized variants including classical, follicular, tall-cell, solid, diffuse sclerosing, cribriform-morular, columnar cell, hobnail, and Warthin-like subtypes [4, 6, 7].

The concurrent presentation of PHPT and PTC has been documented in 2–5% of surgical series, with considerable institutional variation (range 2.8–47.1%) [1, 8]. This association raises important questions regarding shared pathophysiological mechanisms versus incidental co-occurrence [8]. Proposed mechanisms include chromosomal instability, common embryological origin from neural crest derivatives, radiation exposure, calcium dysregulation promoting thyroid cell proliferation, and shared genetic susceptibility factors [9, 10].

Recurrent PHPT, defined as reappearance of biochemical hyperparathyroidism more than six months following initial parathyroidectomy, presents diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. It occurs in 5–10% of cases following initial surgery and typically results from incomplete excision, multiglandular disease, or supernumerary glands [11].

While the synchronous occurrence of PHPT and PTC has been established in contemporary literature, to our knowledge, no prior reports have described multifocal PTC exhibiting three distinct histological variants with different oncogenic driver mutations coexisting with ipsilateral parathyroid pathology and recurrent biochemical PHPT. Herein, we present what appears to be the first reported case in the available medical literature, emphasizing its diagnostic complexity, molecular characteristics, and clinical implications for endocrine surgical practice.

Case presentation

Diagnostic assessment – first episode

Neck ultrasonography revealed a 1.6 cm isoechoic nodule in the left thyroid lobe with regular margins, no microcalcifications, and peripheral vascularity on Doppler imaging (TIRADS 2 classification). The nodule had been stable in size over 18 months of prior surveillance. The right thyroid lobe demonstrated diffusely heterogeneous echotexture consistent with chronic thyroiditis, without discrete nodules at this initial evaluation. Technetium-99 m sestamibi scintigraphy yielded negative results. Magnetic resonance imaging of the neck demonstrated a 1.3 × 1.4 cm lesion posterior to the left thyroid lobe, consistent with left inferior parathyroid adenoma. Fine-needle aspiration cytology of the left thyroid nodule revealed benign findings consistent with colloid goiter (Bethesda category II). Technetium-99 m sestamibi parathyroid scintigraphy with SPECT/CT demonstrated bilateral cervical radiotracer uptake, which in the setting of chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis may partly reflect thyroid retention in addition to possible hyperfunctioning parathyroid tissue (Figs. 2 and 3).

Clinical implications and unique features

To our knowledge, this case represents the first reported instance in the available literature combining all of the following features: (1) recurrent primary hyperparathyroidism requiring reoperation, (2) synchronous multifocal papillary thyroid carcinoma, (3) three distinct histological PTC variants within a single gland, (4) molecularly confirmed independent clonal origins via distinct driver mutations, (5) ipsilateral parathyroid pathology, and (6) background chronic autoimmune thyroiditis. Each individual component has been previously described in the medical literature; however, their simultaneous occurrence in a single patient has not been reported.

The clinical implications of This highly unusual presentation include several important considerations for endocrine surgical practice:

All patients with PHPT should undergo detailed thyroid assessment including high-resolution ultrasonography with systematic evaluation of thyroid parenchyma.

An important learning point from this case pertains to the surgical extent at initial operation. While the decision for left hemithyroidectomy was appropriate based on available preoperative data (benign left thyroid nodule cytology, absence of discrete right lobe nodules), the subsequent identification of contralateral multifocal PTC highlights the potential benefit of total thyroidectomy in selected PHPT patients. Risk factors that may favor more extensive thyroid resection include: (1) concurrent Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, which demonstrates established association with PTC (particularly Warthin-like variant); (2) family history of thyroid cancer; (3) prior neck irradiation; (4) bilateral thyroid abnormalities on imaging; and (5) inability to adequately surveil the contralateral lobe postoperatively. The threshold for total thyroidectomy in PHPT patients with thyroid pathology should be individualized based on comprehensive risk assessment and informed patient discussion. This case underscores that even benign-appearing thyroid pathology in the setting of PHPT and autoimmune thyroiditis warrants careful consideration of surgical extent.

Diagnostic Re-evaluation

Repeat neck ultrasonography identified a 1.8 cm hypoechoic nodule within the right thyroid lobe exhibiting microcalcifications, irregular margins, and taller-than-wide morphology (TIRADS 5 classification) (Fig. 4). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the neck confirmed a 1.2 cm right lobe nodule with punctate calcifications without evidence of cervical lymphadenopathy or extrathyroidal extension. Fiber-optic laryngoscopy demonstrated bilateral vocal cord mobility.

Therapeutic intervention – second surgical procedure (May 2025)

Following informed consent and multidisciplinary team discussion, completion thyroidectomy with right inferior parathyroidectomy was performed. The surgical procedure proceeded without complications, with meticulous preservation of the recurrent laryngeal nerves and identification of the remaining parathyroid glands. The patient tolerated the procedure well without perioperative complications. The patient experienced an uncomplicated postoperative recovery. Calcium and thyroid hormone replacement therapy were initiated. She was discharged on postoperative day three with scheduled endocrinology follow-up. At three-month follow-up, she remained eucalcemic with appropriate thyroid function on replacement therapy.

Discussion

This case represents an exceptionally rare and, to our knowledge, rarely reported combination of recurrent primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT), ipsilateral parathyroid adenoma/hyperplasia, and synchronous multifocal papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) composed of three distinct histological variants—classical, Warthin-like, and follicular—within the same thyroid lobe. The distinct BRAF V600E and NRAS G13R alterations identified in the Warthin-like and follicular components, which are typically mutually exclusive in papillary thyroid carcinoma, strongly support the likelihood of independent primary tumor clones rather than intrathyroidal metastatic spread, favoring de novo multifocal tumorigenesis and field cancerization within the thyroid gland [12–14]. This molecular heterogeneity expands the recognized spectrum of multifocal PTC biology [15]. A limitation of the molecular analysis is that testing was restricted to a targeted PCR-based mutation panel without broader next-generation sequencing or phylogenetic assessment; therefore, the clonality inference should be interpreted cautiously as supportive rather than definitive evidence [13].

During the first surgery, intraoperative PTH unexpectedly increased from 0.9 pg/mL at baseline to 8 pg/mL after excision, rather than demonstrating the expected > 50% decline. Although the absolute values remained low, this biochemical pattern raised suspicion for residual hyperfunctioning parathyroid tissue, later supported by biochemical recurrence.

The coexistence of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, particularly with the Warthin-like variant, further supports a microenvironment permissive to multifocal neoplastic transformation [15, 16]. Chronic lymphocytic inflammation may promote genomic instability through oxidative DNA damage, cytokine-driven proliferation, and altered immune surveillance. Previous studies have reported an increased prevalence of thyroid malignancy in patients with PHPT, with coexistence rates generally ranging from 5% to 15%, although higher rates have been described in surgically selected cohorts [17, 18]. Rare reports have also described the coexistence of two histological PTC variants within the same thyroid gland, most commonly classical with follicular or tall-cell variants, supporting the concept of intrathyroidal molecular heterogeneity [14, 15]. However, the presence of three distinct variants within the same lobe, combined with separate driver mutations and recurrent PHPT, makes the present case highly unusual.

The Warthin-like variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma is well known to show a particularly strong association with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, more consistently than many other PTC subtypes. This relationship is thought to reflect chronic inflammation–driven epithelial injury, lymphoid stromal enrichment, and enhanced susceptibility to oncogenic transformation within an autoimmune thyroid background.

Several mechanisms may explain the PHPT–PTC association observed in this patient (Table 1), including chronic hypercalcemia–mediated thyroid follicular proliferation, shared embryological susceptibility of adjacent endocrine tissues, and overlapping genetic instability pathways [17]. In addition, rare case reports have described the coexistence of two histological PTC variants within the same thyroid gland, most commonly classical with follicular or tall-cell variants, supporting the concept of intrathyroidal heterogeneity and multifocal independent tumorigenesis. However, the presence of three distinct variants within the same thyroid lobe, as observed in the present case, remains exceedingly uncommon. The initial diagnosis of atypical parathyroid adenoma may also be clinically relevant, as atypical histological features can reflect less predictable biological behavior and may contribute to an increased risk of recurrence compared with conventional adenoma, supporting the need for prolonged biochemical surveillance. Although the intraoperative PTH decline during the first operation was suboptimal, the patient demonstrated initial postoperative calcium normalization, and histopathology confirmed removal of a pathological left inferior parathyroid lesion. In the absence of persistent immediate postoperative hypercalcemia, close biochemical surveillance was favored over early re-exploration because of the recognized risks of reoperative neck surgery. Definitive reoperation was appropriately undertaken once biochemical recurrence was confirmed at six months, supported by repeat imaging and the simultaneous emergence of a suspicious contralateral thyroid lesion.

From a clinical perspective, this case supports consideration of systematic thyroid ultrasonography in PHPT patients, particularly when autoimmune thyroiditis or thyroid nodularity is present, as well as the potential value of molecular profiling in multifocal thyroid carcinomas with divergent histological patterns [17, 20]. The identification of distinct driver mutations strongly supported the likelihood of independent primary tumors, thereby informing pathological interpretation and long-term surveillance strategy [21]. Overall, this case broadens current understanding of thyroid–parathyroid neoplastic interplay and highlights the importance of individualized surgical planning, cautious molecular interpretation, and prolonged postoperative biochemical follow-up in complex endocrine neoplasia.

A limitation of this report is the relatively short postoperative follow-up period of three months; therefore, continued long-term biochemical and imaging surveillance is warranted to monitor for potential recurrence [22].

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this case report describes the first reported occurrence in the available literature of recurrent primary hyperparathyroidism with concurrent ipsilateral parathyroid adenoma/hyperplasia and synchronous multifocal papillary thyroid carcinoma exhibiting three distinct histological variants—classical, Warthin-like, and follicular—with molecularly confirmed independent clonal origins via BRAF V600E and NRAS G13R mutations. This highly unusual combination of rare pathological and molecular features highlights the complexity of thyroid-parathyroid interactions in endocrine neoplasia.

The clinical implications of this case emphasize the importance of comprehensive thyroid evaluation in all PHPT patients, the utility of molecular profiling in establishing tumor clonality and guiding management decisions, and the necessity for individualized surgical planning and long-term surveillance in complex endocrine neoplastic conditions. This case expands the recognized spectrum of thyroid-parathyroid associations and underscores the continued need for molecular characterization of seemingly distinct neoplastic processes arising within anatomically proximate endocrine tissues.
