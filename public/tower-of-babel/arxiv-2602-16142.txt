Spencer Compton, Gábor Lugosi, Jaouad Mourtada, Jian Qian

Abstract
We study density estimation in Kullback-Leibler divergence: given an i.i.d. sample from an unknown density p ⋆ p^{\star} , the goal is to construct an estimator p ^ \widehat{p} such that KL ⁡ ( p ⋆ , p ^ ) \mathrm{KL}(p^{\star},\widehat{p}) is small with high probability. We consider two fundamental settings involving a finite dictionary of densities: (i) model aggregation , where p ⋆ p^{\star} belongs to the dictionary, and (ii) convex aggregation (mixture density estimation), where p ⋆ p^{\star} is a mixture of densities from the dictionary. Crucially, we make no assumption on the base densities: their ratios may be unbounded and their supports may differ. For both problems, we identify the best possible high-probability guarantees in terms of the dictionary size, sample size, and confidence level. These optimal rates are higher than those achievable when density ratios are bounded by absolute constants; for mixture density estimation, they match existing lower bounds in the special case of discrete distributions. Our analysis of the mixture case hinges on two new covering results. First, we provide a sharp, distribution-free upper bound on the local Hellinger entropy of the class of mixtures of M M distributions. Second, we prove an optimal ratio covering theorem for convex sets: for every convex compact set K ⊂ ℝ + d K\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d} , there exists a subset A ⊂ K A\subset K with at most 2 O ⁡ ( d ) 2^{O(d)} elements such that each element of K K is coordinate-wise dominated by an element of A A up to a universal constant factor. This geometric result is of independent interest; notably, it yields new cardinality estimates for ε \varepsilon -approximate Pareto sets in multi-objective optimization when the feasible objective set is convex.

1.1
Our contributions

Our main statistical contributions are optimal high-probability guarantees for both model aggregation and convex aggregation (as well as optimal in-expectation rates for convex aggregation), for arbitrary dictionary of densities.
Here and in what follows
a\lesssim b

c>0
such that
a\leqslant cb

For Model Aggregation we present an estimator
\widehat{p}_{\operatorname{MA}}
, and for Convex Aggregation an estimator
\widehat{p}_{\operatorname{CA}}

\operatorname{KL}\big(p^{\star},\widehat{p}_{\operatorname{MA}}\big)\lesssim\frac{\log M\log(1/\delta)}{n},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{KL}\big(p^{\star},\widehat{p}_{\operatorname{CA}}\big)\lesssim\frac{M+\log M\log(1/\delta)}{n}.

These guarantees are stated in

Both rates are unimprovable in general, up to multiplicative constant factors.

A standard approach to these problems is to use
metric entropy
estimates: one derives statistical guarantees by analyzing coverings of the relevant space of distributions. As mentioned above, existing implementations of this strategy have not yielded sharp guarantees in our setting. In this work, we develop tools that make a metric-entropy approach sharp. There are two main obstacles: (i) we do not have sharp covering numbers for mixture models in KL divergence, and (ii) even with sharp covering numbers, the usual reductions would still not lead to sharp statistical bounds.

Our approach relies on three ingredients.
First, for both problems we construct a preliminary estimator that is close to
p^{\star}
in Hellinger distance.
This metric is more amenable to entropy methods, but we need stronger local covering bounds than were previously available (to the best of our knowledge, this remained open; see
3

3

3

Second, we construct an
\varepsilon
-cover in KL divergence
within
the resulting local Hellinger ball.
Third, we treat this KL cover as a finite list of candidates and feed it into the recent estimator of
53
under bounded density ratios, which returns a distribution whose KL risk is nearly as good as the best candidate in the list.
We briefly gloss over some additional technicalities. In particular, we mix candidates in a specific way to satisfy the conditions required by the estimator. For completeness, we provide a self-contained (less general) version of this estimator in
Appendix

The second ingredient, namely constructing a KL cover of a local Hellinger ball, is a key result of the paper.
For reasons explained later, it reduces to the following more general geometric problem.

We say that a subset

1.2
Further applications of ratio covers

Theorem
1
also appears useful beyond our two main estimation results. We outline a few further applications here, and develop them in detail in Section
4.2
and Section
6
.

Approximate Pareto curves.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
represent attainable values in a multi-objective optimization problem. For instance, a point
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in K
could represent producing
x_{1}
units of product 1,
x_{2}
units of product 2, and so on. In general, the Pareto-optimal set may be infinite. The influential work of Papadimitriou and Yannakakis
46
shows that there exists a small subset of
K
that
approximately
dominates all points in
K
. In our terminology, a set that
(1+\varepsilon)
-dominates is exactly a
(1+\varepsilon)
-ratio cover. Their bounds for general
K
depend on
d,\varepsilon
and on the range parameter

R=\max_{i\in[d]}\frac{\max_{x\in K}x_{i}}{\min_{y\in K}y_{i}}
, where here and in what follows
[d]=\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
In Section
4.2
, we use our ratio cover machinery (under convexity of
K
) to remove the dependence on
R
. We expect this to be useful in settings where
K
is convex, for example when
K
is the feasible region of a linear program.

Expected error guarantees via sharpening Yang–Barron.

As remarked earlier, Yang and Barron provide a general recipe for converting KL covering numbers into in-expectation error bounds. While very general, their method often introduces an unnecessary logarithmic factor. In Section
6.1
, we sharpen the Yang–Barron guarantee in settings where the class is convex and amenable to our ratio-cover approach. The plan is: (i) obtain an estimator with squared Hellinger error
\varepsilon
(often via entropy methods), (ii) use ratio covers to build a KL cover of the local Hellinger ball, and (iii) run the Yang–Barron estimator using this local KL cover. The key improvement is that we only need a KL cover
locally
, and the ratio-cover structure remains helpful even when the initial Hellinger estimator occasionally lands outside the target
\varepsilon
-ball. This yields our in-expectation guarantee for convex aggregation, scaling as
\frac{M}{n}
up to constants.

KL covering numbers.

The recent PhD thesis “Divergence Coverings” of Tang
50
develops techniques for constructing KL covers and highlights the problem of bounding the KL covering number of the
d
-dimensional simplex. In Section
6.2
, we review this line of work and show how ratio covers sharpen the KL covering number for the simplex. We emphasize that this section is mainly an illustration of how our techniques can sharpen recent results: in fact, our approach extends immediately beyond the simplex to the convex hull of an arbitrary dictionary of
d
distributions, and can be used to control corresponding local covering numbers.

Related literature

The literature on aggregation under logarithmic loss is vast; we only highlight lines of work that are closest to our setting.

Sequential prediction, logarithmic loss, and universal coding.

Sequential prediction with logarithmic loss lies at the intersection of online learning, universal coding, and Bayesian mixture methods.
In universal coding, predictors such as the Krichevsky–Trofimov estimator yield sharp nonasymptotic redundancy guarantees under log-loss

In online learning, log-loss is

Statistical aggregation and progressive mixtures.

In model aggregation for density estimation,

These developments connect to earlier work on Bayes rules and information consistency

Likelihood methods, entropy, and geometry.

A central tool in nonparametric density estimation is the control of likelihood-based procedures through metric entropy and likelihood-ratio inequalities, as in sieve MLE analyses

Estimator selection under Hellinger-type risks and test-based constructions has been developed in

2
Model aggregation

In this section, we provide a complete analysis of model aggregation over
M
individual densities, where no assumptions are made on the individual densities.
Despite its general novelty, this result is technically less involved and can be seen as a preliminary building block for a more complicated case involving the convex hull of arbitrary densities. In particular, even at the geometry level, we might not need to use the Birgé-Le Cam tournaments, but rather exploit simple properties of the MLE in this case.

Theorem 2
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be an i.i.d. sample from some unknown density
p^{\star}
that belongs to a known class of arbitrary densities
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{M}\}
defined on the space
\mathcal{X}
with respect to the measure
\mu
. There is a density estimator
\widehat{p}
such that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\operatorname{KL}\left(p^{\star},\widehat{p}\right)\leqslant\min\left\{\frac{75\log(2M)\log(2/\delta)}{n},\log(M)\right\}.

This bound is optimal in the sense that there are
M
distributions such that no estimator can perform better up to multiplicative constant factors as shown in
44
;
52
.

For the sake of presentation we assume that we are given a sample of size
2n
. In general, provided that the sample size is at least
2
we can split the sample into two equally sized independent parts, while dropping at most one element of the original sample.
This will only lead to a larger absolute constant in the final statement. We describe the estimator
\widehat{p}
as follows:

•

Use the first
n
observations to construct a data-dependent set of almost empirical risk minimizers

\mathcal{W}=\left\{p\in\mathcal{P}:\widehat{L}_{n}(p)-\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}}\widehat{L}_{n}(q)\leqslant\frac{2\log(4M/\delta)}{n}\right\}.

•

Construct a (random) density class

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}=\left\{\frac{1}{2}p+\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{W}|}\sum\limits_{q\in\mathcal{W}}q:p\in\mathcal{W}\right\}.

(4)

•

Using the second half of the sample, run a version of the progressive density estimator of
Lemma

2
over
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
to output the final density
\widehat{p}
. Finally, if
n\leqslant 75\log(2/\delta)
output instead
\widehat{p}=\frac{1}{M}\sum\limits_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p
.

Let us quickly explain why the procedure works. We first show that the construction of the set
\mathcal{W}
of almost empirical likelihood maximizers provides a set of small Hellinger radius that contains
p^{\star}
. However, closeness in Hellinger radius does not imply closeness in the Kullback–Leibler divergence.
This is where the smoothed class
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
helps: the construction does not inflate the Hellinger distance, but it allows one to control the density ratio. However, as an inevitable price of smoothing
p^{\star}
does not have to belong to
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
. This is where now the regret-based online-to-batch conversion techniques help, since they provide both the optimal tail bounds and allow to bypass the problem
p^{\star}\notin\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
. Remarkably, the final estimator is optimal up to multiplicative constant factors.

The proof of Theorem
2
requires a preliminary lemma. The first result is standard and similar derivations can be found in e.g.
59
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{M}\}
be a finite set of densities with respect to a measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
.
Fix
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}
and let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. distributed according to
p^{\star}
.
For
\delta\in(0,1)
, set

\mathcal{W}=\bigg\{p\in\mathcal{P}:\ \widehat{L}_{n}(p)-\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}}\widehat{L}_{n}(q)\leqslant\frac{2\log(2M/\delta)}{n}\bigg\}.

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, one has
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{W}
and

\max_{p\in\mathcal{W}}\operatorname{H}^{2}(p^{\star},p)\leqslant\frac{2\log(2M/\delta)}{n}.

Proof.

Fix
t>0
. For any
p\in\mathcal{P}
, by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(\widehat{L}_{n}(p)-\widehat{L}_{n}(p^{\star})\leqslant t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(X_{i})}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}p^{\star}(X_{i})}}\geqslant\exp(-nt/2)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp(nt/2)\,{\mathbf{E}}\left[\sqrt{\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(X_{i})}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}p^{\star}(X_{i})}}\right]

\displaystyle=\exp(nt/2)\,\big(1-\operatorname{H}^{2}(p^{\star},p)\big)^{n}\leqslant\exp\Big(\tfrac{nt}{2}-n\operatorname{H}^{2}(p^{\star},p)\Big),

where we used
1-u\leqslant\exp(-u)
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{t}=\{p\in\mathcal{P}:\operatorname{H}^{2}(p^{\star},p)\geqslant t\}
. By a union bound,

\mathbf{P}\left(\exists p\in\mathcal{P}_{t}:\ \widehat{L}_{n}(p)-\widehat{L}_{n}(p^{\star})\leqslant t\right)\leqslant\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{t}}\exp(-nt/2)\leqslant M\,\exp(-nt/2).

Note that for any
p\in\mathcal{P}
,

\widehat{L}_{n}(p)-\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}}\widehat{L}_{n}(q)\leqslant t
implies
\widehat{L}_{n}(p)-\widehat{L}_{n}(p^{\star})\leqslant t
,
since
\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}}\widehat{L}_{n}(q)\leqslant\widehat{L}_{n}(p^{\star})
. Therefore,

\mathbf{P}\left(\exists p\in\mathcal{P}:\widehat{L}_{n}(p)-\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}}\widehat{L}_{n}(q)\leqslant t\ \text{and}\ \operatorname{H}^{2}(p^{\star},p)\geqslant t\right)\leqslant M\exp(-nt/2).

For the second part, apply the same argument with
-t
in place of
t
. For any
p\in\mathcal{P}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(\widehat{L}_{n}(p)-\widehat{L}_{n}(p^{\star})\leqslant-t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(X_{i})}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}p^{\star}(X_{i})}}\geqslant\exp(nt/2)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp(-nt/2)\,\Big(\int\sqrt{p(x)p^{\star}(x)}\,d\mu(x)\Big)^{n}\leqslant\exp(-nt/2).

By a union bound,

\mathbf{P}\left(\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}}\widehat{L}_{n}(q)\leqslant\widehat{L}_{n}(p^{\star})-t\right)\leqslant\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{P}\left(\widehat{L}_{n}(p)-\widehat{L}_{n}(p^{\star})\leqslant-t\right)\leqslant M\exp(-nt/2).

Equivalently, we have

\mathbf{P}\left(\widehat{L}_{n}(p^{\star})-\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}}\widehat{L}_{n}(q)>t\right)\leqslant M\exp(-nt/2).

Choose
t=\frac{2\log(2M/\delta)}{n}
. Then each of the two bad events above has probability at most
\delta/2
, so by a union bound they fail simultaneously with probability at most
\delta
. Hence, with probability at least
1-\delta
, every
p\in\mathcal{W}
satisfies
\operatorname{H}^{2}(p^{\star},p)<t
and also
\widehat{L}_{n}(p^{\star})-\min_{q\in\mathcal{P}}\widehat{L}_{n}(q)\leqslant t
, that is,
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{W}
. This yields the claim.

\widetilde{p}=\frac{1}{2}p^{\star}+\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{W}|}\sum\limits_{q\in\mathcal{W}}q
. Note that
\widetilde{p}\in\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
given by (
4
). Since we aim to use
Lemma

2
to bound
\operatorname{KL}({p^{\star}},{\widehat{p}})
, we will first prove its condition on the densities.
Observe that by construction for each
p,q\in\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
we have

\sup_{p,q\in\mathcal{W}^{\prime}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\log\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right)\leqslant\log\bigl(1+|\mathcal{W}|\bigr)\leqslant\log(2M).

Therefore, by
Lemma

2
we have, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\operatorname{KL}({p^{\star}},{\widehat{p}})\leqslant\operatorname{KL}({p^{\star}},{\widetilde{p}})+\frac{2\log(M)+\frac{25}{4}\left(e-2\right)\left(\log(2M)+1\right)\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}{n}.

(5)

Finally, we have by Lemma
1
and the convexity of the Hellinger distance squared, observing that the ratio of
p^{\star}
and
\frac{1}{2}p^{\star}+\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{W}|}\sum\limits_{q\in\mathcal{W}}q
is bounded by
2
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},\widetilde{p})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\max\{1,\log(2)\}}{(\sqrt{e}-1)^{2}}\operatorname{H}^{2}\left(p^{\star},\frac{1}{2}p^{\star}+\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{W}|}\sum\limits_{q\in\mathcal{W}}q\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{(\sqrt{e}-1)^{2}}\operatorname{H}^{2}\left(p^{\star},\frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}|}\sum\limits_{q\in\mathcal{W}}q\right)\leqslant\frac{2\log(4M/\delta)}{(\sqrt{e}-1)^{2}n}.

Plugging this into (
5
) and using the union bound, we have with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},\widehat{p})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\log(M)+\frac{25}{4}\left(e-2\right)\left(\log(2M)+1\right)\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}{n}+\frac{2\log(4M/\delta)}{(\sqrt{e}-1)^{2}n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{25\log(2M)\log(2/\delta)}{n}.

Note that we used a sample of size
2n
; for any original sample size at least
2
, splitting into two equal parts (dropping at most one observation) increases the absolute constant by at most a factor of
3
(the worst case is a total sample size of
3
). Finally, if
n\leqslant 75\log(2/\delta)
we output
\widehat{p}=\frac{1}{M}\sum\limits_{p\in\mathcal{P}}p
, which trivially gives the upper bound
\log(M)
.
The claim follows.∎

3
Local Hellinger entropy for mixture models

In this section we study the local Hellinger geometry of convex hulls of densities. This result already suffices to establish the optimal rate for
estimation of mixture distributions under Hellinger loss.
By contrast, the best previously known statistical rates incur additional logarithmic factors due to reliance on VC-type covering arguments; see, for instance,
(
3
, Proposition 7 and Corollary 3)
.
Our estimation guarantee in Hellinger distance is given by the bound (
9
) below.

We first introduce notation. Let
M\geqslant 2
and, as above, let
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{M}\}
be a set of densities on
\mathcal{X}
with respect to
\mu
.
Given a set of densities
\mathcal{P}
, define its local Hellinger entropy by

N_{\mathsf{loc}}(\mathcal{P},\varepsilon)=\sup\limits_{p^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{\eta\geqslant\varepsilon}\ N_{\operatorname{H}}\big(B_{\operatorname{H}}(p^{\star},\eta)\cap\mathcal{P},\eta/2\big),

(6)

where
B_{\operatorname{H}}(p,r)=\{q\text{ is a density on }(\mathcal{X},\mu):\operatorname{H}(p,q)\leqslant r\}
and
N_{\operatorname{H}}(\mathcal{F},\varepsilon)
is the covering number of
\mathcal{F}
in the Hellinger metric
\operatorname{H}
at scale
\varepsilon
. For any
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M})\in\Delta_{M-1}
, where in what follows

\Delta_{M-1}=\bigg\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}_{+}:\sum_{j=1}^{M}\theta_{j}=1\bigg\},

we define
p_{\theta}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\theta_{j}p_{j}
. It is convenient to define the pseudo-metric
d_{\operatorname{H}}
on the simplex
\Delta_{M-1}
, which is the Hellinger distance between mixtures, namely for any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Delta_{M-1}
, one has
d_{\operatorname{H}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\operatorname{H}(p_{\theta},p_{\theta^{\prime}})
. The following result provides a universal upper bound on local Hellinger covering numbers of the class of mixtures of
M
densities.

Theorem 3
.

For any finite dictionary of densities
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{M}\}
on
\mathcal{X}
with respect to the measure
\mu
and any
\varepsilon>0
, one has

N_{\mathsf{loc}}\big(\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(\mathcal{P}),\varepsilon\big)\leqslant 64^{M-1}.

Proof.

Fix
p^{\star}\in\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(\mathcal{P})
and
\eta\geqslant\varepsilon
, and choose
\theta_{0}\in\Delta_{M-1}
such that
p^{\star}=p_{\theta_{0}}
. Set

\mathcal{B}=\{\theta\in\Delta_{M-1}:d_{\operatorname{H}}(\theta,\theta_{0})\leqslant\eta\}.

Then

B_{\operatorname{H}}(p^{\star},\eta)\cap\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(\mathcal{P})=\{p_{\theta}:\theta\in\mathcal{B}\},

and moreover, for
A\subseteq\Delta_{M-1}
and
\delta>0
, let
N_{d_{\operatorname{H}}}(A,\delta)
denote the covering number of
A
in the pseudo-metric
d_{\operatorname{H}}
at scale
\delta
. Then

N_{\operatorname{H}}\big(B_{\operatorname{H}}(p^{\star},\eta)\cap\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(\mathcal{P}),\eta/2\big)\leqslant N_{d_{\operatorname{H}}}(\mathcal{B},\eta/2).

(7)

Since
d_{\operatorname{H}}\leqslant 1
, if
\eta>1
then
\mathcal{B}=\Delta_{M-1}
and by monotonicity in the radius
N_{d_{\operatorname{H}}}(\mathcal{B},\eta/2)\leqslant N_{d_{\operatorname{H}}}(\Delta_{M-1},1/2)
, so it suffices to prove the bound below for
0<\eta\leqslant 1
.

Assume
0<\eta\leqslant 1
. By definition,
\mathcal{B}=\{\theta\in\Delta_{M-1}:\operatorname{H}^{2}(p_{\theta_{0}},p_{\theta})\leqslant\eta^{2}\}
; the map
\theta\mapsto p_{\theta}
is affine and
q\mapsto\operatorname{H}^{2}(p_{\theta_{0}},q)
is continuous and convex, so
\mathcal{B}
is convex and compact. Moreover, for any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Delta_{M-1}
and any
t\in[0,1]
, convexity of
\operatorname{H}^{2}(p_{\theta},\cdot)
gives

\operatorname{H}^{2}\left(p_{\theta},p_{(1-t)\theta+t\theta^{\prime}}\right)=\operatorname{H}^{2}\left(p_{\theta},(1-t)p_{\theta}+tp_{\theta^{\prime}}\right)\leqslant t\operatorname{H}^{2}(p_{\theta},p_{\theta^{\prime}}),

and therefore

d_{\operatorname{H}}\big(\theta,(1-t)\theta+t\theta^{\prime}\big)\leqslant\sqrt{t}\,d_{\operatorname{H}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime}).

(8)

Using
d_{\operatorname{H}}\leqslant 1
and (
8
) with
\theta=\theta_{0}
shows that
(1-\eta^{2})\theta_{0}+\eta^{2}\Delta_{M-1}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
, hence (with
\mathrm{Vol}_{M-1}
the Lebesgue measure on the affine hull
\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}:\sum_{j=1}^{M}\theta_{j}=1\}
) one has

\mathrm{Vol}_{M-1}(\mathcal{B})\geqslant\mathrm{Vol}_{M-1}\big((1-\eta^{2})\theta_{0}+\eta^{2}\Delta_{M-1}\big)=\eta^{2(M-1)}\mathrm{Vol}_{M-1}(\Delta_{M-1})>0.

Let
\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N}\}\subset\mathcal{B}
be a maximal
(\eta/2)
-separated set in
(\mathcal{B},d_{\operatorname{H}})
. By the standard property we have
N_{d_{\operatorname{H}}}(\mathcal{B},\eta/2)\leqslant N
. Fix
\lambda=1/64
and define
A_{i}=(1-\lambda)\theta_{i}+\lambda\mathcal{B}
for
i=1,\dots,N
. Since
\mathcal{B}
is convex and
\theta_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
, we have
A_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
. If
\theta\in A_{i}
and
\theta^{\prime}\in A_{j}
with
i\neq j
, write
\theta=(1-\lambda)\theta_{i}+\lambda\widetilde{\theta}
and
\theta^{\prime}=(1-\lambda)\theta_{j}+\lambda\widetilde{\theta}^{\prime}
for some
\widetilde{\theta},\widetilde{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
; then (
8
) with
t=\lambda
and the triangle inequality yield

d_{\operatorname{H}}(\theta_{i},\theta)\leqslant\sqrt{\lambda}\,d_{\operatorname{H}}(\theta_{i},\widetilde{\theta})\leqslant\sqrt{\lambda}\big(d_{\operatorname{H}}(\theta_{i},\theta_{0})+d_{\operatorname{H}}(\theta_{0},\widetilde{\theta})\big)\leqslant 2\eta\sqrt{\lambda},

and similarly
d_{\operatorname{H}}(\theta_{j},\theta^{\prime})\leqslant 2\eta\sqrt{\lambda}
. Therefore,

d_{\operatorname{H}}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\geqslant d_{\operatorname{H}}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})-d_{\operatorname{H}}(\theta_{i},\theta)-d_{\operatorname{H}}(\theta_{j},\theta^{\prime})>\frac{\eta}{2}-4\eta\sqrt{\lambda}=0,

since
\sqrt{\lambda}=1/8
; hence
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset
. Observe that
\mathrm{Vol}_{M-1}(A_{i})=\lambda^{M-1}\mathrm{Vol}_{M-1}(\mathcal{B})
. As the sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{N}
are pairwise disjoint and contained in
\mathcal{B}
,

\mathrm{Vol}_{M-1}(\mathcal{B})\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{Vol}_{M-1}(A_{i})=N\,\lambda^{M-1}\mathrm{Vol}_{M-1}(\mathcal{B}),

and since
\mathrm{Vol}_{M-1}(\mathcal{B})>0
we conclude
N\leqslant\lambda^{-(M-1)}=64^{M-1}
. Thus
N_{d_{\operatorname{H}}}(\mathcal{B},\eta/2)\leqslant 64^{M-1}
, and combining with (
7
) gives

N_{\operatorname{H}}\big(B_{\operatorname{H}}(p^{\star},\eta)\cap\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(\mathcal{P}),\eta/2\big)\leqslant 64^{M-1}\quad\text{for all }p^{\star}\in\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(\mathcal{P}),\ \eta\geqslant\varepsilon.

Taking the suprema in (
6
) yields
N_{\mathsf{loc}}(\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(\mathcal{P}),\varepsilon)\leqslant 64^{M-1}
.
∎

4
Ratio covers of convex sets

After approximating the target distribution in Hellinger distance (as we did above, for instance in the special case of finite mixtures), a central question in this work is how to convert this control into a
small
set of candidate distributions such that at least one is close to
p^{\star}
in KL divergence. We recall from Lemma
1
that the KL divergence
\operatorname{KL}({p},{q})
can be bounded in terms of the Hellinger distance together with a factor depending on the maximal likelihood ratio
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}
. In particular, if
p
and
q
lie in the same squared Hellinger ball of radius
\varepsilon
and the ratio
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}
is bounded by a constant, then one obtains a bound of the form
\operatorname{KL}({p},{q})\leqslant C\,\varepsilon
for some constant
C>0
depending only on that ratio bound. This motivates the notion of a
ratio cover
, which we will later apply to Hellinger balls to obtain the KL covers needed in our applications.

Definition 2
.

Let
K
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
and
\alpha\geqslant 1
.
We say that a subset
A\subset K
is an
\alpha
-ratio cover
of
K
if for every
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d})\in K
, there exists an element
\phi=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{d})\in A
such that
\theta_{j}\leqslant\alpha\,\phi_{j}
for every
j=1,\dots,d
.

Our goal is to show the existence of
C_{1}
-ratio covers of size
(C_{2})^{d}
for convex sets
K
, with constants
C_{1},C_{2}\geqslant 1
. In our proof of Theorem
6
for mixture models, we will ultimately pay a term proportional to
\log(|A|)/n
for a ratio cover of size
|A|
: hence even a ratio cover of size
|A|=(\log d)^{d}
would yield a suboptimal upper bound, and similarly any super-constant ratio
\alpha
would yield suboptimal results (both issues are also suboptimal for our other applications). With this motivation, we state one of our main results.

See
1

In the remainder of this section, we first briefly comment on the theorem statement, and then we discuss an idea of the proof of Theorem
1
. In Subsection
4.1
, we prove Theorem
1
.

First, the constraint
A\subset K
is essential: without it, there is a trivial “cover” obtained by choosing the single vector

\phi\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
with coordinates
\phi_{j}=\max_{\theta\in K}\theta_{j}
, which need not belong to
K
and is not useful for our constructions. Second, the definition must be one-sided. Indeed, if one required the two-sided condition
\phi_{j}/\alpha\leqslant\theta_{j}\leqslant\alpha\phi_{j}
, then even in dimension
d=1
the set
K=[1,L]
with
L\geqslant 2
would require at least
\log_{\alpha}L
points. Third, convexity is required: for
d=2
, the nonconvex set

K=\{(x,1/x):\ 1/L\leqslant x\leqslant L\}

also requires at least
\log_{\alpha}L
points for any fixed
\alpha
. If desired, one could drop convexity but then relax the requirement to
A\subset\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}(K)
; we note, however, that this relaxation will not be useful for the applications discussed in this paper. Finally, even for convex
K
, an exponential dependence on
d
is sometimes unavoidable for constant
\alpha
(e.g.,
K=\Delta_{d-1}
, the simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
). A simple illustration is
\Delta_{1}=\{(t,1-t):t\in[0,1]\}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
: if
\alpha<2
(say
\alpha=1.9
), no single point
\phi=(a,1-a)\in\Delta_{1}
can
\alpha
-ratio cover both extreme points
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
. Hence any
1.9
-ratio cover of
\Delta_{1}
must have size at least
2
. Consequently, if
d
is even, a
1.9
-ratio cover for the product set
\prod_{i=1}^{d/2}\Delta_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
must have size at least
2^{d/2}
: each block
\Delta_{1}
contributes an independent binary choice, so one needs a distinct cover point for each of the
2^{d/2}
combinations.

We provide two different approaches to the proof of
Theorem

1
. The first is somewhat longer, but fully constructive and deterministic, based on first principles and exploiting the convexity of the set. The second proof, presented in
Section

4.3
, is shorter and relies on a reduction to
\varepsilon
-net arguments presented in
27
together with the volumetric argument used in the proof of
Theorem

3
.

Proof roadmap for
Theorem

1
.

We outline the main ideas of the proof. We begin with two warm-ups that highlight the basic geometry behind ratio covers, and then explain how the full argument combines discretization, averaging, and induction to obtain a
C_{1}
-ratio cover of size
C_{2}^{d}
.

Warm-up 1: a
d
-ratio cover with a single point.

For each coordinate
j\in[d]
, let
m_{j}=\max_{\theta\in K}\theta_{j}
(the maximum exists since
K
is compact), and pick
\overline{\theta}^{(j)}\in K
such that
\overline{\theta}^{(j)}_{j}=m_{j}
.
Define

\phi^{(1)}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\overline{\theta}^{(j)}\in K,

where
\phi^{(1)}\in K
by convexity.
Then for any
\theta\in K
and any
i\in[d]
,

\theta_{i}\leqslant m_{i}=\overline{\theta}^{(i)}_{i}\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{d}\overline{\theta}^{(j)}_{i}=d\,\phi^{(1)}_{i}.

Thus
\{\phi^{(1)}\}
is a
d
-ratio cover of
K
.

Warm-up 2: a
4
-ratio cover with
|A|\leqslant(\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil+1)^{d}
.

Warm-up 1 is too coarse since the ratio factor grows with
d
.
To reduce the ratio, we discretize each coordinate of
\theta
into dyadic bins relative to
\phi^{(1)}
.
Fix
\theta\in K
. If
\theta_{i}\leqslant\phi^{(1)}_{i}
, then coordinate
i
is already
1
-covered by
\phi^{(1)}
.
Otherwise,
\theta_{i}>\phi^{(1)}_{i}
, and the bound from Warm-up 1 implies
\theta_{i}\leqslant d\,\phi^{(1)}_{i}
.
(If
\phi^{(1)}_{i}=0
, then necessarily
m_{i}=0
and hence
\theta_{i}=0
for all
\theta\in K
, so only the first case can occur.)
Therefore each coordinate
i
must satisfy either

\theta_{i}\leqslant\phi^{(1)}_{i},\quad\text{or}\quad\theta_{i}\in(2^{j}\phi^{(1)}_{i},\,2^{j+1}\phi^{(1)}_{i}]\ \text{for some }j\in\{0,\dots,\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil-1\}.

This yields at most
(\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil+1)^{d}
possible bin patterns across all coordinates.
Let
A_{2}\subset K
contain one representative
\phi^{(2)}
for each bin pattern that occurs among points of
K
, and define

A=\frac{\phi^{(1)}+A_{2}}{2}=\left\{\frac{\phi^{(1)}+\phi^{(2)}}{2}:\ \phi^{(2)}\in A_{2}\right\}.

Given
\theta\in K
, pick
\phi^{(2)}\in A_{2}
with the same bin pattern.
If
\theta_{i}\leqslant\phi^{(1)}_{i}
, then

\theta_{i}\leqslant\phi^{(1)}_{i}\leqslant\phi^{(1)}_{i}+\phi^{(2)}_{i}=2\frac{\phi^{(1)}_{i}+\phi^{(2)}_{i}}{2}.

Otherwise, for some
j
we have
\theta_{i}\in(2^{j}\phi^{(1)}_{i},2^{j+1}\phi^{(1)}_{i}]
, and the matching bin pattern implies
\phi^{(2)}_{i}>2^{j}\phi^{(1)}_{i}
, hence

\theta_{i}\leqslant 2^{j+1}\phi^{(1)}_{i}\leqslant 2\,\phi^{(2)}_{i}\leqslant 4\frac{\phi^{(1)}_{i}+\phi^{(2)}_{i}}{2}.

Therefore
A
is a
4
-ratio cover of
K
, and

|A|=|A_{2}|\leqslant(\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil+1)^{d}
.
We note that the idea of discretizing by dyadic scales and selecting one representative per pattern also appears in
46
.

Idea of the full theorem.

The warm-ups still fall short: we need a constant ratio
C_{1}
and cover size
C_{2}^{d}
.
After discretizing
K
as above, the key additional observation is that
averaging
a finite set of discretized representatives typically provides slack on many coordinates: for a large fraction of discretized points, the average already constant-covers at least half of the coordinates.
This enables the following high-level strategy:

1.

Discretize points of
K
into finitely many profiles (as in Warm-up 2), and pick one representative per profile.

2.

Iteratively take averages of the remaining representatives; at each step, keep the average and discard all representatives that are already constant-covered by it on at least half of the coordinates.

3.

For the uncovered coordinates (at most half), apply the induction hypothesis to a suitable lower-dimensional projection.

4.

Combine the averaging-based candidates with the inductive covers so that every coordinate is covered with an overall constant ratio.

4.1
Constructive proof of Theorem
1

We proceed by induction on
d\geqslant 1
.

Base case
d=1
.

This case follows simply. Let
\theta^{(0)}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{max}}_{\theta\in K}\theta
(which exists by compactness of
K
).
Then,
A=\{\theta^{(0)}\}\subset K
is a
1
-ratio cover of
K
with one element.

Induction.

Otherwise, let
d\geqslant 2
; we assume that the property holds for any dimension
d^{\prime}<d
, and proceed to show it in dimension
d
.

We will define below four finite subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{4}\subset K
such that

A\subset(A_{1}+\dots+A_{4})/4=\{(\phi^{(1)}+\dots+\phi^{(4)})/4:\phi^{(1)}\in A_{1},\dots,\phi^{(4)}\in A_{4}\}

is a
32
-ratio cover of
K
.

Definition of
A_{1}
.

A_{1}
is constructed exactly as in the first warm-up. For each
j=1,\dots,d
, define
m_{j}=\sup_{\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d})\in K}\theta_{j}
, which is finite and attained by compactness of
K
.
Then, for each
j=1,\dots,d
, pick
\overline{\theta}^{(j)}=(\overline{\theta}_{1}^{(j)},\dots,\overline{\theta}_{d}^{(j)})\in K
such that
\overline{\theta}_{j}^{(j)}=m_{j}
, and define
\phi^{(1)}=(\overline{\theta}^{(1)}+\dots+\overline{\theta}^{(d)})/d\in K
.
We then simply take
A_{1}=\{\phi^{(1)}\}
.

For every
\theta=(\theta_{j})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\in K

J_{1}(\theta)=\{1\leqslant j\leqslant d:\theta_{j}\leqslant m_{j}/d\}.

Then, by definition of
\phi^{(1)}
, for each

\theta_{j}\leqslant m_{j}/d\leqslant\phi_{j}^{(1)}
,
i.e., the
j

\theta
is
1
-covered by that of
\phi^{(1)}
.

Definition of
A_{2}
.

We now proceed to cover some of the remaining coordinates.
For every subset
J\subseteq[d]
, define

K_{1}(J)=\{\theta\in K:[d]\setminus J_{1}(\theta)=J\},

the set of vectors in
K
whose set of “remaining coordinates” is
J
.
We will now define a subset
A_{2}(J)\subset K
that covers at least half of the coordinates for every vector
\theta\in K_{1}(J)
.

If
J=\emptyset
(meaning that there are no coordinates left to cover), then arbitrarily set
A_{2}(J)=\{\phi^{(1)}\}
;
if
K_{1}(J)=\emptyset
(meaning that there are no vectors to cover), then set
A_{2}(J)=\emptyset
.
Now, let
J\neq\emptyset
such that
K_{1}(J)\neq\emptyset
.
By definition of
K_{1}(J)
, for every
\theta\in K_{1}(J)
and

, we know

m_{j}/d<\theta_{j}\leqslant m_{j}
.

We first discretize the vectors
\{(\theta_{j})_{j\in J}:\theta\in K_{1}(J)\}
by powers of
2
of their coordinates.
Let us formalize this: we define the

of a vector
\theta\in K_{1}(J)
as the vector

(k_{j})_{j\in J}\in\mathbb{N}^{J}\textrm{ such that }m_{j}2^{-k_{j}-1}<\theta_{j}\leqslant m_{j}2^{-k_{j}}\textrm{ for every }j\in J.

Since
m_{j}/d<\theta_{j}\leqslant m_{j}

0\leqslant k_{j}\leqslant\left\lceil\log_{2}d\right\rceil-1\leqslant\log_{2}d
.

We say that a profile

K_{1}(J)
has this profile.
We then define a subset
S\subset K_{1}(J)

Note that the cardinality of

is at most
|S|\leqslant|\{0,\dots,\left\lceil\log_{2}d\right\rceil-1\}^{J}|\leqslant(\log_{2}d+1)^{d}
.

So far, we have produced a finite set of representatives that represent the (possibly infinite) remaining values of
\theta

\theta
has its coordinates approximated by its representative, our intuition is that producing a ratio-cover for this set of representatives will yield a ratio-cover for all
\theta
.
In this sense, we have essentially reduced the task of ratio-covering a convex set
K
, to the task of providing a ratio cover for a set with bounded size. At first, this may not seem much easier; particularly because the set is still quite large. However, let us first consider a weaker goal, where we only ratio-cover half of the coordinates:

Definition 3

(Half-
c
-cover)
.

For
c\geqslant 1
, we say that a vector
\theta
is half-
c
-covered by a vector

\theta_{j}\leqslant c\mu_{j}
holds for at least half of the indices

.

Even producing a half-
c
-cover for our bounded-size set is not immediately obvious. However, we will argue that
the average of the set
S
is a half-
4
-cover for at least half of the elements in
S

Claim 1
.

Given a non-empty set of indices
J\subseteq[d]
, let
S^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}

\mu^{\prime}=\frac{1}{|S^{\prime}|}\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}}\theta^{\prime}

Then, at least half of the elements of
S^{\prime}

4

\mu^{\prime}

Proof of Claim
1
.

Denote by
B\subset S^{\prime}
the set of

\mu^{\prime}
.
For each

|\{\theta\in S^{\prime}:\theta_{j}>4\mu^{\prime}_{j}\}|\leqslant|S^{\prime}|/4
.
Hence,

\frac{|S^{\prime}|}{4}\geqslant\frac{1}{|J|}\sum_{j\in J}\sum_{\theta\in S^{\prime}}\bm{1}(\theta_{j}>4\mu^{\prime}_{j})=\sum_{\theta\in S^{\prime}}\frac{\lvert\{j\in J:\theta_{j}>4\mu_{j}^{\prime}\}\rvert}{|J|}\geqslant|B|\cdot\frac{|J|/2}{|J|}=\frac{|B|}{2}\,,

so that
|B|\leqslant|S^{\prime}|/2
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Our plan is to invoke this claim repeatedly to produce a half-
c
-cover for all of
S
. Concretely, we recursively define elements
\mu^{(k)}\in K
and subsets
S_{k}\subset S
as follows. Let
S_{1}=S
. Then, for each
k\geqslant 1
:

•

Define
\mu^{(k)}=\frac{1}{|S_{k}|}\sum_{\theta\in S_{k}}\theta\in K
.

•

Let
S_{k+1}
be the set of vectors in
S_{k}
that are not half-
4
-covered by
\mu^{(k)}
.

•

If
S_{k+1}=\emptyset
, then stop; otherwise, turn to step
k+1
.

By Claim
1
, one has
|S_{k+1}|\leqslant|S_{k}|/2
, hence the loop stops after

r\leqslant\left\lfloor\log_{2}|S|\right\rfloor+1\leqslant d\log_{2}(\log_{2}d+1)+1\leqslant 2^{d}

iterations.
We define
A_{2}(J)=\{\mu^{(1)},\dots,\mu^{(r)}\}
, the set of partial means, such that
|A_{2}(J)|\leqslant 2^{d}
by the above.

By construction of the means
\mu^{(k)}
and sets
S_{k}
above, each
\theta\in S
is half-
4
-covered by an element of
A_{2}(J)
.
In addition, by definition of
S
, each vector
\theta\in K_{1}(J)
has all its coordinates
j\in J
within a factor of
2
of those of an element in
S
. Hence,
\theta
is also half-
8
-covered by an element
\phi^{(2)}=\phi^{(2)}(\theta)\in A_{2}(J)
.
We denote the coordinates handled by this step by

J_{2}(\theta)=\{j\in J:\theta_{j}\leqslant 8\phi_{j}^{(2)}\}.

Since this covers at least half of the coordinates for any
\theta
, we know
|J_{2}(\theta)|\geqslant|J|/2
. Thus, the number of remaining unhandled coordinates is at most

|[d]\backslash(J_{1}(\theta)\cup J_{2}(\theta))|\leqslant d-|J_{1}(\theta)|-|[d]\backslash J_{1}(\theta)|/2\leqslant d/2.

In summary, define
A_{2}=\bigcup_{J\subseteq[d]}A_{2}(J)
, such that
|A_{2}|\leqslant\sum_{J\subseteq[d]}2^{d}=2^{2d}
.
Since
K=\bigcup_{J\subseteq[d]}K_{1}(J)
, it follows from the above that for every
\theta\in K
, there exists
\phi^{(2)}=\phi^{(2)}(\theta)\in A_{2}
such that all coordinates

j\in J_{2}(\theta)
of
\theta
are
8
-covered by the corresponding ones of
\phi^{(2)}
.

Definition of
A_{3}
.

For any
\theta\in K
, let
J^{\prime}(\theta)
denote the coordinates not handled by
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
:

J^{\prime}(\theta)=[d]\backslash(J_{1}(\theta)\cup J_{2}(\theta)).

As noted before, the number of unhandled coordinates is at most
d/2
.
In what follows, we will handle the remaining coordinates
J^{\prime}(\theta)
by induction, together with an argument to avoid degrading the ratio factor.

For every subset
J\subset[d]
with
|J|\leqslant d/2
, we define
\pi_{J}(K)
as the projection of
K
to the coordinates
J
, meaning

\pi_{J}(K)=\{(\theta_{j})_{j\in J}:(\theta_{j})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\in K\}.

The set
\pi_{J}(K)
is a convex compact subset in dimension
d^{\prime}\leqslant d/2<d
; hence, by the induction hypothesis, it admits a subset with at most
2^{8d^{\prime}}\leqslant 2^{4d}
elements that is a
32
-ratio cover.

We define the corresponding subset
A_{3}(J)\subset K
, with at most
2^{4d}
elements, where
\pi_{J}(A_{3}(J))
is a 32-ratio cover of
\pi_{J}(K)
. For
J=\emptyset
, we set
A_{3}(\emptyset)=\{\phi^{(1)}\}
.
By the ratio cover property, for every
\theta\in K
, there exists
\phi^{(3)}=\phi^{(3)}(\theta)\in A_{3}(J^{\prime}(\theta))
such that
\theta_{j}\leqslant 32\,\phi_{j}^{(3)}
for every
j\in J^{\prime}(\theta)
.
From here, we define
A_{3}=\bigcup_{J\,:\,|J|\leqslant d/2}A_{3}(J)
, with cardinality
|A_{3}|\leqslant\sum_{J\,:\,|J|\leqslant d/2}|A_{3}(J)|\leqslant 2^{d}\times 2^{4d}=2^{5d}
.

At this point, it is worth noting that achieving a
32
-covering from
\phi^{(3)}
over the coordinates
j\in J^{\prime}(\theta)
is not sufficient for our purposes, since the vector
\phi^{(3)}
is to be averaged with previous vectors
\phi^{(1)},\phi^{(2)}
to cover coordinates
j\in[d]\setminus J^{\prime}(\theta)
.
This averaging operation will degrade the constant
32
in the ratio factor, thus breaking the induction.

In order to address this issue, we distinguish between two types of coordinates.
For
\theta\in K
, let
\phi^{(3)}=\phi^{(3)}(\theta)\in A_{3}(J^{\prime}(\theta))
be as above.
We define

J_{3}(\theta)=\{j\in J^{\prime}(\theta):\theta_{j}\leqslant 8\,\phi_{j}^{(3)}\}\textrm{ and }J_{4}(\theta)=J^{\prime}(\theta)\setminus J_{3}(\theta)=[d]\setminus(J_{1}(\theta)\cup J_{2}(\theta)\cup J_{3}(\theta)).

By definition, coordinates
j\in J_{3}(\theta)
are
8
-covered, which provides the necessary slack to maintain a
32
-ratio covering after the averaging operation.
It thus remains to handle the coordinates in
J_{4}(\theta)
.

Definition of
\phi^{(4)}
.
We will not actually define a set
A_{4}
as our earlier definition suggests, but instead will define a function
\phi^{(4)}(\theta)
as before.

The final idea of the proof is the following: coordinates
j\in J_{4}(\theta)
are
32
-covered but not
8
-covered by
\phi^{(3)}(\theta)
; meaning, the value of
\theta_{j}
is known up to a factor of
4
, and can thus be separately encoded. Specifically, for any
\phi^{(3)}\in A_{3}
and subset
J\subset[d]
, let

C(\phi^{(3)},J)=\{\theta\in K:8\phi_{j}^{(3)}<\theta_{j}\leqslant 32\phi_{j}^{(3)},\ \textrm{for all}\ j\in J\}.

Using this, let
R(\phi^{(3)},J)\in C(\phi^{(3)},J)
be an arbitrary representative chosen for
C(\phi^{(3)},J)
(as long as the set is nonempty). For any
\theta\in K
, let
\phi^{(3)}=\phi^{(3)}(\theta)
and
J_{4}=J_{4}(\theta)
as above.
By definition of
J_{4}(\theta)
, we know
\theta\in C(\phi^{(3)},J_{4})
; hence, the latter set is nonempty, and we choose
\phi^{(4)}(\theta)=R(\phi^{(3)},J_{4})
.
This ensures that
\theta_{j}\leqslant 32\phi_{j}^{(3)}\leqslant 4\phi_{j}^{(4)}
for every
j\in J_{4}
.

Conclusion
.

We let

A=\bigg\{\frac{\phi^{(1)}+\phi^{(2)}(\theta)+\phi^{(3)}(\theta)+\phi^{(4)}(\theta)}{4}:\theta\in K\bigg\}\,,

which is contained in
K
by convexity.
In addition, for every
\theta\in K
, letting
\phi=(\phi^{(1)}+\phi^{(2)}(\theta)+\phi^{(3)}(\theta)+\phi^{(4)}(\theta))/4=(\phi_{j})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}
and
J_{k}=J_{k}(\theta)
for
k=1,\dots,4
, we have by the above that:

\theta_{j}\leqslant 4\phi_{j}\text{ for }j\in J_{1};\quad\theta_{j}\leqslant 32\phi_{j}\text{ for }j\in J_{2};\quad\theta_{j}\leqslant 32\phi_{j}\text{ for }j\in J_{3};\quad\theta_{j}\leqslant 16\phi_{j}\text{ for }j\in J_{4}.

Since
[d]=J_{1}\cup J_{2}\cup J_{3}\cup J_{4}
, we deduce that
A
is a
32
-ratio cover of
K
.

It remains to control the cardinality of
A
.
There are at most
|A_{2}|\leqslant 2^{2d}
possible values of
\phi^{(2)}(\theta)\in A_{2}
, and at most
|A_{3}|\leqslant 2^{5d}
possible values for
\phi^{(3)}(\theta)\in A_{3}
; in addition, since
\phi^{(4)}(\theta)=R(\phi^{(3)}(\theta),J)
for some
J\subset[d]
, there are at most
2^{d}
possible values of
\phi^{(4)}(\theta)
given
\phi^{(3)}(\theta)
.
Hence, there are at most
2^{2d}\times 2^{5d}\times 2^{d}\leqslant 2^{8d}
possible values for
(\phi^{(2)}(\theta),\phi^{(3)}(\theta),\phi^{(4)}(\theta))
for
\theta\in K
, and therefore
|A|\leqslant 2^{8d}
. ∎

4.2
Approximate Pareto curves

Consider a setting where a set
K\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
represents the attainable objective values in a multi-objective optimization problem.
For instance, one may think of producing
d
different products: a point
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in K
indicates that it is possible to simultaneously produce
x_{j}
units of product
j
for each
j\in[d]
.
Such problems typically involve tradeoffs between objectives, and a common object of interest is the
Pareto curve
(or Pareto frontier), namely the set of points in
K
that are not dominated coordinatewise by any other point in
K
. In general, the Pareto curve may be infinite.
Papadimitriou and Yannakakis
46
showed that one can nevertheless extract a finite
\varepsilon
-approximate Pareto curve
, meaning a subset
A\subset K
such that for every
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d})\in K
there exists
\phi=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{d})\in A
with

\theta_{j}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\,\phi_{j}\quad\text{for all }j\in[d].

In our terminology, this is exactly a
(1+\varepsilon)
-ratio cover of
K
.
Their result does not assume convexity of
K
, but it does assume that each coordinate ranges over a bounded interval.

Theorem 4

(Rephrased Theorem 1 of Papadimitriou and Yannakakis
46
)
.

For every set
K\subset[\frac{1}{R},R]^{d}
, every
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, and every
R\geqslant 1
, there exists a subset
A\subset K
with

|A|\leqslant\left(c\,\frac{\log R}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d-1}

that is an
\varepsilon
-approximate Pareto curve (equivalently, a
(1+\varepsilon)
-ratio cover) for
K
, where
c>0
is a universal constant.

Their short proof is essentially the same dyadic discretization idea that we used in our second warm-up.
A key feature of
Theorem

4
is its dependence on the range parameter
R
.
If one adds the assumption that
K
is convex, then the averaging idea from our first warm-up yields a simple way to remove this dependence.

Corollary 1

(Corollary of
Theorem

4
under convexity)
.

For every convex and compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
and every
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, there exists a subset
A\subset K
with

|A|\leqslant\left(c\,\frac{\log d}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d-1}

that is an
\varepsilon
-approximate Pareto curve for
K
, where
c>0
is a universal constant.

By comparison, we now use our ratio cover theorem to obtain an
\varepsilon
-approximate Pareto curve whose size has no dependence on
R
and whose base does not involve
d
.
The convexity assumption is essential for this stronger statement (see the discussion following
Theorem

1
), and it is natural in many applications, for example when
K
is the feasible region of a linear program.

Corollary 2

(of
Theorem

1
)
.

For every convex and compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
and every
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-approximate Pareto curve
A\subset K
with

|A|\leqslant 2^{8d}\left(3+\frac{4\log_{2}(128/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d-1}.

In particular, this implies
|A|\leqslant\left(\frac{c_{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{c_{2}d}
for large enough absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
. The proof of
Corollary

2
is deferred to
Appendix

D
.
A substantial literature following Papadimitriou and Yannakakis develops algorithms that construct
\varepsilon
-approximate Pareto curves of near-minimal size (see, e.g.,
54
;
26
;
27
;
6
;
24
).
Our result shows that, under convexity, there always exists an
\varepsilon
-approximate Pareto curve of smaller size, and therefore yields improved existential upper bounds for what such approximation algorithms can aim to achieve in this setting.

4.3
Proof of a version of
Theorem

1
based on the reduction to
\varepsilon
-nets

In this section, we provide an alternative proof of
Theorem

1
based on
\varepsilon
-net arguments in the spirit of Haussler and Welzl
31
, introduced in the context of Pareto curves by Diakonikolas and Yannakakis
27
for general sets
K
.
We then combine this approach with convexity via a volumetric argument similar to the one used in the proof of
Theorem

3
. We state the resulting bound as a separate theorem because it yields a different dependence on
\varepsilon
in the language of
Corollary

2
.

Theorem 5
.

For every convex and compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
with nonempty interior and every
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-approximate Pareto curve
A\subset K
with

|A|\leqslant\left\lceil 8d\left(1+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d}\log\left(13\left(1+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d}\right)\right\rceil.

Note that, in full generality, the dependence on
\varepsilon
in
Theorem

5
differs from that in
Corollary

2
. In the natural regime of fixed
d
and sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, the bound of
Corollary

2
is stronger because it has exponent
d-1
rather than
d
. Despite not having the same dependence on
\varepsilon
as
Corollary

2
, the result of
Theorem

5
implies a
32
-ratio cover of size at most

\left\lceil 8d\left(\frac{32}{31}\right)^{d}\log\left(13\left(\frac{32}{31}\right)^{d}\right)\right\rceil\leqslant 2^{5d},

for all
d\geqslant 1
.
Since optimization of the numerical constants is not our priority, we mostly use the simple integer form of
Theorem

1
.

We also note that the construction in our first proof of
Corollary

2
is deterministic and constructive, whereas the proof of
Theorem

5
uses a Vapnik–Chervonenkis style probabilistic argument.

Proof.

The nonempty interior assumption implies
\mathrm{Vol}(K)>0
. For each
\theta\in K
, set

R_{\theta}(\varepsilon)=\{\phi\in K:\theta_{j}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\phi_{j}\ \textrm{for all}\ j\in[d]\}.

As observed in
(
27
, Section 4.1)
,
A
is an
\varepsilon
-approximate Pareto curve if and only if, for all
\theta\in K
,

R_{\theta}(\varepsilon)\cap A\neq\emptyset.

Indeed, this means that for any
\theta\in K
there exists
\phi\in A
such that
\theta_{j}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\phi_{j}
for all
j\in[d]
.

Now define the collection of sets
\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon)=\{R_{\theta}(\varepsilon):\theta\in K\}
. Following
31
, the VC dimension of the
range space

(K,\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon))
is the largest integer
v
such that
\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon)
shatters some set
X\subset K
of size
v
, where shattering means that for every subset
Y\subseteq X
there exists a range
R\in\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon)
such that
R\cap X=Y
. The following result is proved in
27
:

Lemma 4

(Lemma 4.5 in
27
)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, the VC dimension of the range space
(K,\mathcal{R}(\varepsilon))
is at most
d
.

Note that
Lemma

4
does not require
K
to be convex. We now depart from the reduction in
27
and exploit convexity. For each
\theta\in K
set

Q_{\theta}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\theta+\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}K.

By convexity of
K
, we have
Q_{\theta}(\varepsilon)\subset K
for every
\theta\in K
. Moreover, for every
\theta\in K
it holds that

Q_{\theta}(\varepsilon)\subset R_{\theta}(\varepsilon).

Indeed, any
\phi\in Q_{\theta}(\varepsilon)
can be written as
\phi=\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\theta+\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\nu
with
\nu\in K
, and then for all
j\in[d]
,

\theta_{j}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\left(\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\theta_{j}+\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\nu_{j}\right)=(1+\varepsilon)\phi_{j},

so
\phi\in R_{\theta}(\varepsilon)
. Let
\mu
be the uniform probability measure on
K
, which is well-defined since
\mathrm{Vol}(K)>0
. Then for any
\theta\in K
, a volumetric argument gives

\mu(R_{\theta}(\varepsilon))\geqslant\mu(Q_{\theta}(\varepsilon))=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\right)^{d}.

Thus, it suffices to construct a set
A
such that for all
\theta\in K
one has
A\cap R_{\theta}(\varepsilon)\neq\emptyset
, and such that every range satisfies the uniform lower bound
\mu(R_{\theta}(\varepsilon))\geqslant\eta
, where

\eta=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\right)^{d}.

By the standard
\varepsilon
-net argument (see
31
and
(
14
, Theorem 2.1)
), if we sample

\left\lceil\frac{8d}{\eta}\log\left(\frac{13}{\eta}\right)\right\rceil

points independently according to
\mu
and let
A
be the resulting set, then with probability at least
1/2
we have
A\cap R_{\theta}(\varepsilon)\neq\emptyset
for all
\theta\in K
. Plugging in the value of
\eta
yields the stated bound. Existence follows since this event has positive probability.
∎

5
Mixture models (convex aggregation)

We are ready to state our second main statistical result.

Theorem 6
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
with
n\geqslant 2
be an i.i.d. sample from some unknown density
p^{\star}
that belongs to the convex hull of a known class of arbitrary densities
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{M}\}
, namely
\mathcal{P}=\operatorname{conv}\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{M}\}
defined on the space
\mathcal{X}
with respect to the measure
\mu
and
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}
. There is a density estimator
\widehat{p}
such that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\operatorname{KL}\left(p^{\star},\widehat{p}\right)\leqslant\frac{870030\,M+45000\log(M)\log\left(4/\delta\right)}{n}.

In the regime
n\geqslant\log(1/\delta)
, provided that
n\geqslant M\geqslant 5000
, this bound is optimal in the sense that there are
M
distributions such that no estimator can perform better up to absolute constant factors, as shown in
(
44
, Theorem 4)
.

As above, for the sake of presentation we assume that we are given a sample of size
2n
(we translate back to the original sample size at the end). In general, provided that the sample size is at least
2
we can split the sample into two equally sized independent parts, while dropping at most one element of the original sample. This will only lead to a larger absolute constant in the final statement. We describe the estimator
\widehat{p}
of Theorem
6
as follows:

•

Use the first
n
observations to run a Birgé-Le Cam tournament (see Theorem
9
) over
\mathcal{P}
to find a convex closed set
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathcal{P}
such that, with probability
1-\delta/2
, it satisfies

\sup\limits_{p,q\in\mathcal{W}}\operatorname{H}^{2}(p,q)\leqslant\frac{3528\log(64)\,M+512\log(4/\delta)}{n},\quad\textrm{and}\quad p^{\star}\in\mathcal{W}.

(9)

•

Let
\Delta
be the simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Consider the closed convex set
\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq\Delta
defined as follows:

\Delta^{\prime}=\left\{\alpha\in\Delta:\sum_{j=1}^{M}\alpha_{j}p_{j}\in\mathcal{W}\right\},

and construct a
32
-ratio cover of
\Delta^{\prime}
, which will be denoted as
\Delta^{\prime}_{c}
.

•

Choose any
\beta^{(1)},\ldots,\beta^{(M)}\in\Delta^{\prime}
such that for all
j\in[M]
,
\beta^{(j)}_{j}=\sup\{\alpha_{j}:\alpha\in\Delta^{\prime}\}
, i.e., each vector
\beta^{(j)}
witnesses the largest
j
-th coordinate in
\Delta^{\prime}
. Construct a (random) density class

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M}\alpha_{j}p_{j}+\frac{1}{2M}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{M}\beta^{(j)}_{k}p_{k}:\alpha\in\Delta^{\prime}_{c}\right\}.

(10)

•

Using the second half of the sample run a version of the progressive density estimator of
Lemma

2
over
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
to output the final density
\widehat{p}
.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
. Combining the Birgé-Le Cam tournament result (Theorem
9
) and the local Hellinger entropy bound of Theorem
3
, we construct the desired convex set
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathcal{P}
satisfying, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\sup\limits_{p,q\in\mathcal{W}}\operatorname{H}^{2}(p,q)\leqslant\frac{3528\log(64)\,M+512\log(4/\delta)}{n},\quad\textrm{and}\quad p^{\star}\in\mathcal{W}.

(11)

From now on we work on this event and focus on the second half of the sample. Using the second half of the sample we output the density
\widehat{p}
obtained using the procedure of
Lemma

2
with respect to
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
defined by (
10
). Let
p^{\star}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M}\alpha_{j}p_{j}
for some
\alpha\in\Delta^{\prime}
. On the event where
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{W}
, by the definition of
\Delta^{\prime}_{c}
there is a vector
\gamma\in\Delta^{\prime}_{c}
such that for the density

p^{\star}_{c}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M}\gamma_{j}p_{j},\quad\textrm{it holds that}\quad\max_{j,\gamma_{j}>0}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\gamma_{j}}\leqslant 32.

(12)

Denote
\widetilde{p}=\frac{1}{2}p^{\star}_{c}+\frac{1}{2M}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{M}\beta^{(j)}_{k}p_{k}
. Note that
\widetilde{p}\in\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
by (
10
). We aim to use
Lemma

2
to bound
\operatorname{KL}({p^{\star}},{\widehat{p}})
, so we will first prove its condition on the densities.
Writing any
p\in\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
as
p_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}(\alpha_{k}+\bar{\beta}_{k})p_{k}
with
\bar{\beta}_{k}=\tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{j}\beta^{(j)}_{k}
and noting
\alpha_{k}\leqslant\sum_{j}\beta^{(j)}_{k}=M\bar{\beta}_{k}
, we get

\sup_{p_{\alpha},p_{\alpha^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{W}^{\prime}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\log\left(\frac{p_{\alpha}(x)}{p_{\alpha^{\prime}}(x)}\right)\leqslant\log\left(1+\frac{\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}p_{k}(x)}{\sum_{k}\bar{\beta}_{k}p_{k}(x)}\right)\leqslant\log\left(1+M\right)\leqslant\log(2M).

Therefore, by
Lemma

2
, and since by Theorem
1
it holds that
|\mathcal{W}^{\prime}|\leqslant 256^{M}
, we have, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
,

\operatorname{KL}({p^{\star}},{\widehat{p}})\leqslant\operatorname{KL}({p^{\star}},{\widetilde{p}})+\frac{2M\log(256)+\frac{25}{4}\left(e-2\right)\left(\log(2M)+1\right)\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}{n}.

(13)

Finally, by Lemma
1
and the convexity of the Hellinger distance squared, and observing that the ratio of
p^{\star}
to
\frac{1}{2}p^{\star}_{c}+\frac{1}{2M}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{M}\beta^{(j)}_{k}p_{k}
is bounded by
64
by (
12
), we note explicitly that since
\alpha_{j}\leqslant 32\,\gamma_{j}
coordinatewise,

\sum_{j=1}^{M}\alpha_{j}p_{j}(x)\leqslant 32\sum_{j=1}^{M}\gamma_{j}p_{j}(x)=32\,p_{c}^{\star}(x),\quad\text{so}\quad\frac{p^{\star}(x)}{\widetilde{p}(x)}\leqslant\frac{32p_{c}^{\star}(x)}{\frac{1}{2}p_{c}^{\star}(x)}=64.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},\widetilde{p})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\max\{1,\log(64)\}}{(\sqrt{e}-1)^{2}}\operatorname{H}^{2}\left(p^{\star},\frac{1}{2}p^{\star}_{c}+\frac{1}{2M}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{M}\beta^{(j)}_{k}p_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\log(64)\left(3528\log(64)\,M+512\log(4/\delta)\right)}{(\sqrt{e}-1)^{2}\,n},

where in the last line we used that
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{W}
and
\frac{1}{2}p^{\star}_{c}+\frac{1}{2M}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{M}\beta^{(j)}_{k}p_{k}\in\mathcal{W}
combined with (
11
). Plugging this into (
13
) and using the union bound, we have with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},\widehat{p})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2M\log(256)+\frac{25}{4}\left(e-2\right)\left(\log(2M)+1\right)\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}{n}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2\log(64)\left(3528\log(64)\,M+512\log\left(4/\delta\right)\right)}{(\sqrt{e}-1)^{2}n}.

Note that we used a sample of size
2n
, which leads to an increased absolute constant by at most a factor of
3
(e.g., in the worst case of an odd total sample size such as
3
one observation is dropped).
The claim follows.∎

6.1
Sharpening the Yang–Barron bound (expected error guarantees)

The celebrated result of Yang and Barron
60
shows how to turn a finite KL cover into an
expected
KL risk bound via a progressive mixture construction. In this section, we explain how our ratio-cover approach yields a sharper guarantee when the underlying density class is convex and we may construct appropriate ratio covers. We use the following form of the Yang–Barron bound.

Theorem 7

(Yang–Barron; see the proof of Theorem 32.1 in Section 32.2.1 of
47
)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. from a density
p^{\star}
on
(\mathcal{X},\mu)
, and let
q_{1},\dots,q_{N}
be densities such that

\min_{j\in[N]}\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},q_{j})\leqslant\varepsilon.

Define the progressive-mixture estimator
\widehat{p}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}q_{j}
, where

w_{j}=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\frac{\prod_{t=1}^{i-1}q_{j}(X_{t})}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\prod_{t=1}^{i-1}q_{k}(X_{t})}.

Then

{\mathbf{E}}\big[\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},\widehat{p})\big]\leqslant\varepsilon+\frac{\log N}{n+1}.

Two issues prevent a direct use of
Theorem

7
in our setting. First, constructing KL covers is substantially more delicate than constructing Hellinger covers. Second, even when one has sharp KL covering numbers (e.g., for the simplex), applying Yang–Barron with a
global
cover introduces unnecessary logarithmic factors, since it does not exploit localization.

Our approach is to localize the construction. We first build an estimator that is close to
p^{\star}
in Hellinger distance using the Le Cam–Birgé tournament (
Theorem

9
). We then construct a finite candidate list that KL-covers the resulting local Hellinger uncertainty region using ratio covers (
Section

4
), and finally apply
Theorem

7
to this local list. The remaining technical point is that the Hellinger control is in expectation, so
p^{\star}
may fall outside the chosen ball; we address this by showing that the KL quality of the ratio-based cover degrades gracefully with
\operatorname{H}^{2}(p^{\star},\widehat{p})
(see
Lemma

5
).

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a convex class of densities on
\mathcal{X}
with respect to
\mu
, fix
p\in\mathcal{P}
,
\varepsilon>0
, and
\alpha\geqslant 2
.
Set

\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{P}\cap B_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}(p,\varepsilon),\qquad B_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}(p,\varepsilon)=\{q\ \text{is a density}:\ \operatorname{H}^{2}(p,q)\leqslant\varepsilon\}.

Assume that
S\subset\mathcal{Q}
is finite and has the following property: for every
q\in\mathcal{Q}
there exists
\widetilde{q}\in S
such that

q(x)\leqslant\alpha\,\widetilde{q}(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{X}.

Then for every
q\in\mathcal{P}
,

\min_{\widetilde{q}\in S}\operatorname{KL}(q,\widetilde{q})\leqslant 30\,\bigl(\varepsilon+\operatorname{H}^{2}(p,q)\bigr)\,\log\Bigl(\alpha\Bigl(1\vee\frac{\operatorname{H}^{2}(p,q)}{\varepsilon}\Bigr)\Bigr).

The proof of this result is deferred to
Appendix

D
. Combined with the tail bounds on the Hellinger estimator, we obtain an expected KL guarantee for convex classes admitting suitable ratio covers.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that
n
is even.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a convex class of densities and assume that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. from some
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}
.
Let
\varepsilon_{n/2}>0
satisfy

\frac{n}{2}\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}\geqslant\log N_{\mathsf{loc}}(\mathcal{P},\varepsilon_{n/2})\,\lor\,1.

Assume that there exist
\alpha\geqslant 2
and an integer
N_{S}\geqslant 1
such that for every
p\in\mathcal{P}
one can find a finite set

S(p)\subset\mathcal{P}\cap B_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}(p,\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2})
which is an
\alpha
-ratio cover of
\mathcal{P}\cap B_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}(p,\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2})
(in the sense of
Lemma

5
) and satisfies
|S(p)|\leqslant N_{S}
.
Then, there exists an estimator
\widehat{p}
such that

{\mathbf{E}}\big[\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},\widehat{p})\big]\leqslant 366609\,\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}\log(\alpha)+\frac{\log(N_{S})}{n/2+1}.

Proof.

Split the sample into two independent halves.
Let
\widehat{p}_{\operatorname{H}}
be the Le Cam–Birgé estimator (Theorem
9
) built from the first
n/2
observations, and let
\widehat{p}
be the Yang–Barron mixture (Theorem
7
) built from the remaining
n/2
observations over the candidate set
S(\widehat{p}_{\operatorname{H}})
with the uniform prior. Conditioning on the first half and applying Theorem
7
to the second half yields

{\mathbf{E}}\big[\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},\widehat{p})\big]\leqslant{\mathbf{E}}\Big[\min_{q\in S(\widehat{p}_{\operatorname{H}})}\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},q)\Big]+\frac{\log(N_{S})}{n/2+1}.

By Lemma
5
with
p=\widehat{p}_{\operatorname{H}}
and
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}
,

\min_{q\in S(\widehat{p}_{\operatorname{H}})}\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},q)\leqslant 30\Bigl(\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}+\operatorname{H}^{2}(\widehat{p}_{\operatorname{H}},p^{\star})\Bigr)\log\Bigl(\alpha\Bigl(1\vee\frac{\operatorname{H}^{2}(\widehat{p}_{\operatorname{H}},p^{\star})}{\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigr).

For
x\in(0,1)
, Theorem
9
applied with sample size
n/2
gives, with probability at least
1-x
,

\operatorname{H}^{2}(\widehat{p}_{\operatorname{H}},p^{\star})\leqslant 882\,\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}+\frac{256\log(2/x)}{n}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\big[\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},\widehat{p})\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant 30\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(883\,\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}+\frac{256\log(2/x)}{n}\Bigr)\log\left(\alpha\Bigl(882+\frac{256\log(2/x)}{\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}\,n}\Bigr)\right)\,dx+\frac{\log(N_{S})}{n/2+1}.

Since
(n/2)\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}\geqslant 1
, we have
\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}\geqslant 2/n
, and hence

\frac{256\log(2/x)}{\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}\,n}\leqslant 128\log(2/x),\qquad\frac{256\log(2/x)}{n}\leqslant 128\,\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}\log(2/x).

Thus, we have

{\mathbf{E}}\big[\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},\widehat{p})\big]\leqslant 30\,\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(883+128\log(2/x)\Bigr)\,\log\left(\alpha\Bigl(882+128\log(2/x)\Bigr)\right)\,dx+\frac{\log(N_{S})}{n/2+1}.

Evaluating the integral yields

{\mathbf{E}}\big[\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},\widehat{p})\big]\leqslant 33000\,\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}\log(\alpha)+231240\,\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}+\frac{\log(N_{S})}{n/2+1}.

Since
\alpha\geqslant 2
, we have
\log(\alpha)\geqslant\log 2
, so

231240\,\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}\leqslant\frac{231240}{\log 2}\,\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}\log(\alpha)
.
This proves the claim.∎

This immediately implies the following sharp result based on the progressive mixture rule of Yang and Barron applied to a localized set of densities.

Corollary 3
.

In the setup of
Theorem

6
let
n
be even.
There exists a density estimator
\widehat{p}
such that

{\mathbf{E}}\big[\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},\widehat{p})\big]\leqslant\frac{10568316\,M}{n}.

Proof.

Apply Proposition
1
with
\mathcal{P}=\mathop{\mathrm{conv}}\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{M}\}
and
\alpha=32
.
By Theorem
3
,
N_{\mathsf{loc}}(\mathcal{P},\varepsilon)\leqslant 64^{M-1}
for all
\varepsilon>0
, so we may take

\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}=\frac{2M\log(64)}{n},

which satisfies
(n/2)\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}\geqslant\log N_{\mathsf{loc}}(\mathcal{P},\varepsilon_{n/2})
. Fix any
p\in\mathcal{P}
and write
p=p_{v}
for some
v\in\Delta_{M-1}
, where
p_{v}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}v_{j}p_{j}
.
The set of mixture weights

K=\Bigl\{u\in\Delta_{M-1}:\ p_{u}\in\mathcal{P}\cap B_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}(p,\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2})\Bigr\}

is convex and compact. By Theorem
1
, it admits a

2^{8M}

{\mathbf{E}}\big[\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},\widehat{p})\big]\leqslant 366609\,\varepsilon_{n/2}^{2}\log(32)+\frac{8M\log 2}{n/2+1}.

Using

and

\frac{366609\cdot 2\log(64)\cdot\log(32)}{n}\,M+\frac{16\log 2}{n}\,M\leqslant\frac{10568316\,M}{n},

6.2
KL covering numbers

Constructing covers in Kullback–Leibler divergence is substantially less developed than for metrics such as Hellinger distance.
Tang’s PhD thesis
50
highlights the KL covering number of the simplex as a natural benchmark problem. This benchmark is directly tied to discrete distribution estimation: in this section,
\Delta_{d-1}
is the convex hull of the
d
Dirac masses on
[d]
, equivalently the class of mixtures of a finite dictionary of
d
point masses.

We note that the results of this section are mainly illustrative. We focus on the simplex to mirror the setting studied in
50
, but global KL covering numbers for the simplex are too crude for our statistical applications, where we crucially exploit localization. More broadly, our ratio-cover approach applies to convex hulls of arbitrary collections of densities, and our focus is always on local covering numbers.

Since KL is a directed divergence, we use the one-sided notion of covering that matches our risk
\operatorname{KL}(p^{\star},\widehat{p})
and is the notion used in the work of Yang and Barron
60
: for
\varepsilon>0
,

N_{\operatorname{KL}}(\Delta_{d-1},\varepsilon)=\min\Bigl\{N:\ \exists\,q^{(1)},\dots,q^{(N)}\in\Delta_{d-1}\ \text{such that}\ \sup_{p\in\Delta_{d-1}}\ \min_{k\in[N]}\ \operatorname{KL}({p},{q^{(k)}})\leqslant\varepsilon\Bigr\}.

Tang’s KL covers for the simplex
50
.

Tang gives a general lower bound (via a reduction to total variation covering numbers), which yields

N_{\operatorname{KL}}(\Delta_{d-1},\varepsilon)\geqslant\left(\frac{1}{8\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}.

(14)

On the other hand, for any
p\in\Delta_{d-1}
, the uniform distribution
u=(1/d,\dots,1/d)
satisfies
\operatorname{KL}({p},{u})\leqslant\log d
, so
N_{\operatorname{KL}}(\Delta_{d-1},\varepsilon)=1
whenever
\varepsilon\geqslant\log d
.
For
0<\varepsilon<\log d
, Tang’s Theorem 2 gives the upper bound

N_{\operatorname{KL}}(\Delta_{d-1},\varepsilon)\leqslant\left(C\frac{\log d}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}

for a universal constant
C>0
, using a Hellinger cover together with a uniform lower bound on coordinates to control likelihood ratios.
In the small-
\varepsilon
regime
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{4(d+1)^{2}}
, Tang’s Theorem 3 gives the sharper bound

N_{\operatorname{KL}}(\Delta_{d-1},\varepsilon)\leqslant\left(\frac{C}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon},

via a probabilistic construction, namely the proof uses a
\chi^{2}
-type argument based on a modification of Jeffreys prior. These results motivate the following question: for a fixed accuracy level (say
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
), can one match the lower bound
(
14
) up to universal constants, i.e., obtain an upper bound of the form

N_{\operatorname{KL}}(\Delta_{d-1},\varepsilon)\leqslant\left(\frac{C}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\qquad(0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1)

for some universal constant
C>0
?
We answer this question in the affirmative using ratio covers (see
Proposition

2
below).
We note that Tang also analyzes additional regimes where

varies with

Our next result improves the KL covering number upper bound in the regime where

is at most an absolute constant. We also state a general reduction from KL covers of the simplex to local Hellinger covers, combined with ratio covers.

Proposition 2
.

Let
N_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}(\Delta_{d-1},r)
denote the covering number of the simplex
\Delta_{d-1}
in squared Hellinger distance at scale
r
.
Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

N_{\operatorname{KL}}(\Delta_{d-1},\varepsilon)\leqslant N_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}\left(\Delta_{d-1},\frac{\varepsilon}{70}\right)\cdot 2^{8d}.

In particular, for every
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
,

N_{\operatorname{KL}}(\Delta_{d-1},\varepsilon)\leqslant\left(\frac{5040\cdot 2^{32}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and set
R=\varepsilon/70
.
Let
\{q^{(1)},\dots,q^{(N)}\}\subset\Delta_{d-1}
be an
R
-cover of
\Delta_{d-1}
in squared Hellinger distance, so that
for every
p\in\Delta_{d-1}
there exists
k\in[N]
with
\operatorname{H}^{2}(p,q^{(k)})\leqslant R
, and
N=N_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}(\Delta_{d-1},R)
. For each
k\in[N]
, define the (squared-Hellinger) ball intersection

K_{k}=\Delta_{d-1}\cap B_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}(q^{(k)},R)\qquad\text{where}\qquad B_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}(q,R)=\{p\in\Delta_{d-1}:\ \operatorname{H}^{2}(p,q)\leqslant R\}.

Applying
Theorem

1
to a convex and compact set
K_{k}
, we obtain a
32
-ratio cover
A_{k}\subset K_{k}
with
|A_{k}|\leqslant 2^{8d}
.
Define
\mathcal{A}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}
, so

|\mathcal{A}|\leqslant N_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}(\Delta_{d-1},R)\cdot 2^{8d}.

Now fix
p\in\Delta_{d-1}
and pick
k\in[N]
such that
\operatorname{H}^{2}(p,q^{(k)})\leqslant R
.
Since
p\in K_{k}
and
A_{k}
is a
32
-ratio cover of
K_{k}
, there exists
q^{\star}\in A_{k}
such that
p_{i}\leqslant 32\,q^{\star}_{i}
for all
i\in[d]
.
In particular, whenever
p_{i}>0
we have
q^{\star}_{i}>0
and
\log(p_{i}/q^{\star}_{i})\leqslant\log 32
, hence

\max_{i\in[d]}\log\frac{p_{i}}{q^{\star}_{i}}\leqslant\log 32.

Moreover, since
q^{\star}\in A_{k}\subset K_{k}\subset B_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}(q^{(k)},R)
, we also have
\operatorname{H}^{2}(q^{(k)},q^{\star})\leqslant R
.
Using the triangle inequality for
\operatorname{H}
,

\operatorname{H}(p,q^{\star})\leqslant\operatorname{H}\bigl(p,q^{(k)}\bigr)+\operatorname{H}\bigl(q^{(k)},q^{\star}\bigr)\leqslant\sqrt{R}+\sqrt{R}=2\sqrt{R},

and thus
\operatorname{H}^{2}(p,q^{\star})=\operatorname{H}(p,q^{\star})^{2}\leqslant 4R
.
By
Lemma

1
,

\operatorname{KL}({p},{q^{\star}})\leqslant\frac{2}{(\sqrt{e}-1)^{2}}\operatorname{H}^{2}(p,q^{\star})\max\left\{1,\max_{i\in[d]}\log\frac{p_{i}}{q^{\star}_{i}}\right\}\leqslant\frac{8}{(\sqrt{e}-1)^{2}}\max\{1,\log 32\}\,R.

The numerical constant satisfies
\frac{8}{(\sqrt{e}-1)^{2}}\max\{1,\log 32\}<70
, so with
R=\varepsilon/70
we have
\operatorname{KL}({p},{q^{\star}})\leqslant\varepsilon
.
Since
p\in\Delta_{d-1}
was arbitrary,
\mathcal{A}
is a KL
\varepsilon
-cover of
\Delta_{d-1}
, and therefore

N_{\operatorname{KL}}(\Delta_{d-1},\varepsilon)\leqslant N_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}\left(\Delta_{d-1},\frac{\varepsilon}{70}\right)\cdot 2^{8d}.

It remains to derive the stated
(C/\varepsilon)^{(d-1)/2}
bound when
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
.
Consider the bijection
p\mapsto\sqrt{p}=(\sqrt{p_{1}},\dots,\sqrt{p_{d}})
between
\Delta_{d-1}
and the positive orthant of the unit sphere

{S}^{d-1}_{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}:\ \|x\|_{2}=1\}.

For
p,q\in\Delta_{d-1}
, we have

\operatorname{H}^{2}(p,q)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl\|\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{q}\bigr\|_{2}^{2},

which implies

N_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}(\Delta_{d-1},r)=N_{\ell_{2}}\left({S}^{d-1}_{+},\sqrt{2r}\right)\leqslant N_{\ell_{2}}\left({S}^{d-1},\sqrt{2r}\right),

where
N_{\ell_{2}}({S}^{d-1},t)
denotes the
\ell_{2}
covering number of the unit sphere at radius
t
.
For
0<t\leqslant 1
, a standard shell-volumetric argument yields

N_{\ell_{2}}({S}^{d-1},t)\leqslant\left(\frac{12}{t}\right)^{d-1}.

Fix
r=\varepsilon/70
. For
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, we have
\sqrt{2r}=\sqrt{2\varepsilon/70}\leqslant 1
, hence

N_{\operatorname{H}^{2}}\left(\Delta_{d-1},\frac{\varepsilon}{70}\right)\leqslant\left(\frac{5040}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}.

Combining this with the first part of the proof, we prove the claim.

Aggregation in the misspecified case

In this section we discuss aggregation under misspecification, where we do not assume that
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}
. This problem is also of interest in the aggregation literature. The results in this section have the following features:

•

the analysis relies on more standard tools;

•

•

;

•

at the price of additional polylogarithmic factors in

Thus, in the broader context of our results, the hardest remaining question appears to be obtaining optimal rates up to multiplicative constant factors when

Proposition 3
.

\mathcal{X}

Compared to
Theorem

2
the bound contains an additional dependence on

under the logarithm. The estimator and the analysis are presented in
Appendix

D
. The idea behind the estimator is to run the predictor of

2
on a deterministically smoothed class

obtained from the given set

.

Our next result is an analog of

in the misspecified case.

Proposition 4
.

•

Construct a class of densities
