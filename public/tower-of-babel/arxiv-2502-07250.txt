Scaling Online Complex Event Detection with Synthetic Supervision and Mamba-Based Neural Algorithmic Reasoning
Liying Han liying98@g.ucla.edu, Gaofeng Dong gfdong@g.ucla.edu, Xiaomin Ouyang xmouyang@cse.ust.hk, Kang Yang kyang73@g.ucla.edu, Lance Kaplan lance.m.kaplan.civ@army.mil, Federico Cerutti federico.cerutti@unibs.it, Mani Srivastava mbs@ucla.edu

Abstract
Modern machine learning models excel at detecting individual actions, sounds, or scene attributes from short, localized observations. However, many real-world tasks, such as in smart cities and healthcare, require reasoning over high-level complex events ( CE s): spatiotemporal, rule-governed patterns of short-term atomic events ( AE s). Complex event detection (CED) is challenging due to long temporal dependencies, generalization beyond the training horizon, sparse CE -level supervision without temporally aligned fine-grained AE labels, and cognitively demanding annotation, as CE labels often depend on ordering, duration, negation, and completion-time semantics. These challenges are further amplified in an online setting that requires causal, streaming inference with limited computation. We identify the primary bottleneck in online CED as learning robust CE rules, and propose a Neural Algorithmic Reasoning framework that decouples rule learning from low-level sensor semantics by (i) generating large-scale synthetic AE -level concept traces to pretrain a Mamba-based CE -rule reasoner, and (ii) introducing an adapter that learns to map raw sensor inputs into the reasoner’s latent space using limited, labeled sensor data. We introduce a controlled simulator-generated online multilabel CED testbed built from real-world multimodal sensor clips and rule-generated CE labels, with stress-test settings that vary sensor noise, distribution shift, and the window size used to segment streaming sensor sequences. Experiments on this controlled benchmark show that NAROCE is competitive with the strongest baselines and often outperforms them under these stress tests and longer-horizon generalization, while using 5× fewer labeled sensor sequences and 10–20× fewer FLOPs than all non-Mamba baselines. Code and dataset available at: https://github.com/nesl/naroce_dailyoce .

Introduction

Recent advances in machine learning have greatly improved low-level sensory perception tasks such as action recognition and sound event detection. However, many real-world intelligent systems must go beyond recognizing short-lived patterns in sensor streams: they need to
reason over high-level event patterns
that unfold over extended temporal (and often spatial) horizons and reflect meaningful scenarios or critical changes in the underlying state of the world.

This capability is commonly formalized as
complex event detection (CED)

(
Cugola and Margara, 2012
;
Xing et al., 2019
;
Roig Vilamala et al., 2023
;
Wang et al., 2024
;
Han et al., 2025
)
. A
complex event (CE)
is a rule-governed pattern composed of multiple
atomic events (AEs)
.
AE
s are low-level semantic events that can be inferred from short, fixed-duration perception windows (typically seconds). In contrast,
CE
s capture higher-level (spatio)temporal relationships among
AE
s, such as ordering, duration, repetition, temporal gaps, overlap, and negation, and may span minutes or hours.
For example, in a restaurant, a
CE
such as a
sanitary protocol violation
may be defined as an employee using the restroom and subsequently touching kitchen surfaces without an intervening hand-washing action. In a smart-city setting, a
CE
such as a
coordinated security threat
may be inferred from distributed observations, such as two baggage drop-offs at different locations separated by tens of minutes, followed by a vehicle pick-up captured by another camera. In both cases, the target
CE
cannot be inferred from any single observation; it emerges from the temporal composition and ordering of multiple
AE
s over long horizons. Another common characteristic of CED is that annotations are typically provided at the level of
high-level
CE
occurrences
, while fine-grained, temporally aligned annotations of the underlying
AE
s are
unavailable
due to the labor-intensive annotation burden.

Detecting
CE
s from noisy sensory data poses several fundamental challenges.
First, a model needs to robustly identify a small number of key
AE
s while
ignoring large amounts of irrelevant activity
, often formalized as “don’t-care” elements (denoted as
X
). For instance, a sanitary protocol may be expressed as

``\text{Use restroom}"\rightarrow X\rightarrow``\text{Wash hands}"\rightarrow X\rightarrow``\text{Touch kitchen surfaces}",

where
X
may include unrelated actions such as walking or sitting. Allowing such flexibility dramatically enlarges the space of acceptable sequences, especially as temporal horizons grow.

Second,
CE
s exhibit
variable and long-range temporal dependencies
. The time gap between two critical
AE
s may range from seconds to hours, yet the semantic rule defining the
CE
remains unchanged. As a result, learning-based systems must extrapolate
CE
patterns beyond the fixed-length traces observed during training and remain robust to temporal variability at inference time, which is an inherently difficult requirement for models trained on finite data traces.

Third, many application domains demand
online detection
. In scenarios such as eldercare, workplace safety, or building security monitoring, a
CE
must be recognized as it unfolds rather than through offline post hoc analysis. This requires maintaining a faithful internal state that summarizes relevant historical context and updating it continuously as new sensory observations arrive.

Beyond modeling challenges,
dataset scale and supervision present a major bottleneck
. As a representative example, prior work
(
Han et al., 2025
)
reports learning-based methods trained on the order of 10,000 labeled 5-minute
CE

sequences, amounting to over 800 hours of sensor recordings, or approximately
4.8\times 10^{10}
raw samples for audio at 16 kHz. Annotating a
CE
label is also cognitively demanding, requiring reasoning over long, structured event sequences spanning minutes or hours. Moreover, because many sensor streams (e.g., IMU or audio) are not directly human-interpretable, large-scale annotation is difficult without strict collection protocols or auxiliary modalities such as vision. Even the most relevant existing real-world event datasets
(
Oh et al., 2011
)
typically target loosely structured activities that do not require explicit long-horizon rule-based reasoning, and often contain only tens of instances per event. As a result, collecting realistic, large-scale
CE
data is prohibitive. This motivates two complementary goals pursued in this work: developing a methodology to reduce reliance on labeled sensor sequences, and building a controlled, scalable evaluation testbed that preserves key ingredients for online CED reasoning—real sensor observations, long-horizon temporal composition, and diverse
CE
types—while systematically varying sensor noise and distribution shift.

Related Work

Complex Event Detection (CED).
Traditional CED has been extensively studied in structured databases, where mature rule-based engines detect symbolic event patterns from clean and structured inputs
(
Schultz-Møller et al., 2009
;
Debar and Wespi, 2001
;
Cugola and Margara, 2012
)
. Recent efforts extend CED to unstructured, high-dimensional sensor data. For example,
Wang et al. (2024)
extracts symbolic labels (e.g., object types, attributes, bounding boxes) from the video and passes them to hand-written rules, but this approach is brittle to perception errors. Neurosymbolic methods
(
Xing et al., 2020
;
Roig Vilamala et al., 2023
)
improve robustness by integrating soft neural predictions with differentiable logic frameworks
(
De Raedt et al., 2007
;
Manhaeve et al., 2018
;
Manhaeve et al., 2019
)
, but still rely on user-defined rules and scale poorly.
Han et al. (2025)
explores fully data-driven methods for learning
CE
rules, showing that SSMs
(
Gu et al., 2022
)
like Mamba
(
Gu and Dao, 2024
;
Dao and Gu, 2024
)
are well-suited. However, their reliance on massive labeled sensor data remains a major drawback.

Neural Algorithmic Reasoners (NAR).
NAR models learn classical algorithms using neural networks, enabling generalization on structured reasoning tasks such as sorting, shortest path, and graph traversal
(
Veličković et al., 2022
;
Ibarz et al., 2022
;
Velickovic and Blundell, 2021
)
. Recent work combines a pretrained graph neural network (GNN)-based NAR with transformers to extend algorithmic reasoning to language tasks
(
Bounsi et al., 2024
)
. However, these methods typically operate on clean, structured inputs and do not directly address noisy sensory observations. Inspired by NAR, we view online
CE
-rule detection as an algorithmic procedure, and train an NAR on synthetic
AE
-level concept traces to learn these rules. We then bridge the gap to raw sensor data through a learned sensor adapter, thereby decoupling high-level rule reasoning from low-level sensor semantic learning to improve data efficiency and generalization.

Online Action Recognition.
Tasks such as Online Action/Action Start Detection, Temporal Action Localization, and Segmentation focus on real-time recognition of frame-level actions in streaming videos. Recent work improves long-range modeling using advanced temporal architectures and memory modules
(
An et al., 2023
;
Wang et al., 2023
;
Cao et al., 2023
;
Zhao and Krähenbühl, 2022
;
Chen et al., 2022a
;
Xu et al., 2021b
;
Wang et al., 2021
)
. However, these methods target
flat, low-level, temporally localized
activities
(
Fabian Caba Heilbron and Niebles, 2015
;
Jiang et al., 2014
)
rather than activities or events that require structured and rule-based reasoning over ordering and long temporal gaps. Our task addresses an
orthogonal and complementary
challenge: rule-based reasoning over long sequences of
AE
s under sparse
CE
-only supervision.

3
Problem Formalization

3.1
Complex Event Definitions

Definition 3.1
.

An
atomic event
(
AE
) is a short-horizon, low-level semantic event inferred from a fixed
local
perception window over sensor data. The window length is application-dependent (e.g., seconds for human activity; tens of milliseconds for speech/audio). Let
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
denote the set of
AE
types.

Definition 3.2
.

A
complex event
(
CE
) is a high-level event defined by a temporal and/or logical rule over multiple
AE
s that may span long horizons. Let
E=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
denote the set of
CE
types of interest.

Definition 3.3
.

Each
CE

e_{i}\in E
is specified by a
pattern function

R_{i}
over a subset of relevant
AE
types
A_{i}\subseteq A
. Let
z_{t}\in A
denote the
AE
observed at window
t
. To ignore irrelevant
AE
s, define a mapping

g_{i}:A\rightarrow A_{i}\cup\{X\}

that maps any
a\in A_{i}
to itself and any
a\notin A_{i}
to a
don’t-care
symbol
X
(i.e., ignored by
R_{i}
).

Let
\tilde{z}_{t}=g_{i}(z_{t})
and
\tilde{z}_{1:t}=(\tilde{z}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{z}_{t})\in(A_{i}\cup\{X\})^{*}
. The pattern function evaluates the
AE
trace online:

R_{i}(\tilde{z}_{1:t})\in\{0,1\},\quad t=1,2,\ldots

(1)

where
R_{i}(\cdot)=1
indicates that the temporal/logical pattern for
e_{i}
is satisfied by the observed prefix up to window
t
. The
occurrence time

t_{e_{i}}
is the smallest window index
t
such that
R_{i}(\tilde{z}_{1:t})=1
, i.e., the exact window when the pattern becomes satisfied.

We follow the pattern taxonomy in prior work
(
Han et al., 2025
)
and consider four primary categories:
Sequential Ordering
,
Temporal Duration
,
Repetition
, and
Combination
. All patterns
R_{i}
considered here are
finite-state
and can be represented as finite state machines (FSMs). Formal definitions and examples are provided in Table
9
.

3.2
Online & Multilabel Complex Event Detection

Assume a system receives a multimodal sensor stream
\mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{X}^{(m)}\}_{m\in M}
, where modality
m
is sampled at rate
r_{m}
. The stream is processed in windows of duration
W
. At window
t
, the segment for modality
m
is

\mathbf{D}_{t}^{(m)}=\mathbf{X}^{(m)}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{(r_{m}\cdot W)\times d_{m}},

(2)

where
d_{m}
is the feature dimension of modality
m
.
We use the same window boundaries for all modalities and denote the multimodal input at window
t
as
\mathbf{D}_{t}=\{\mathbf{D}_{t}^{(m)}\}_{m\in M}
.

Prior work
(
Han et al., 2025
)
formulates detection as a
multiclass
problem with mutually exclusive labels per window, assigning a single
CE
label only at the window where the corresponding pattern becomes satisfied; the rule then resets to enable detecting subsequent occurrences. However, in real-world scenarios, occurrences of different
CE
s can overlap in time and multiple
CE
types may be detected in the same window; therefore, we extend the formulation to
online multilabel CED
. Accordingly, each window
t
is labeled with a binary vector
\mathbf{y}_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{|E|}
, where
\mathbf{y}_{t}[i]=1
indicates that
CE

e_{i}
is detected at window
t
; multiple entries can be
1
in the same window, representing co-occurring
CE
s. We adopt the same satisfaction-time labeling semantics, i.e., if an
e_{i}
-pattern spans from window
t_{1}
to
t_{2}
, then only
\mathbf{y}_{t_{2}}[i]=1
while
\mathbf{y}_{t_{1}:t_{2}-1}[i]=0
, and
\mathbf{y}_{t_{2}+1}[i]=0
due to the reset for detecting subsequent occurrences. We refer to the interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]
as the
CE
temporal span
. As a result, online
CE
labels are
temporally sparse and rare
.

The goal of an online CED model
f
is to minimize the discrepancy between the predicted label
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
and the ground-truth label
\mathbf{y}_{t}
at each window using current and past inputs:

\min_{f}\ \ell(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t},\mathbf{y}_{t}),\quad\text{with }\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=f(\mathbf{D}_{1:t}),\quad t=1,2,\ldots

(3)

where
\ell(\cdot)
is a multilabel classification loss.

Only coarse
CE
labels
\mathbf{y}_{t}
are provided on the long-horizon sensor traces; temporally aligned, fine-grained
AE
annotations are not available for these traces. Consequently, the model must learn both
AE
-relevant semantics and long-horizon
CE
rules under
distant & weak supervision
from nonlocal, sparse, and high-level
CE
labels, in an
online

multilabel
setting.

3.3
Online Inference Pipeline

A standard online CED pipeline
(
Han et al., 2025
)
first segments the continuous stream into non-overlapping decision windows of length
W
, yielding
\{s_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
.
A
feature encoder

m
, typically pretrained separately on short sensor clips using either supervised or self-supervised objectives, then extracts each (multimodal) sensor window
s_{t}
into a latent embedding
v_{t}=m(s_{t})
, producing an embedding stream
\{v_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
.
Lastly, an
online complex event detector

g
takes the embedding history and outputs the prediction for window
t
:

\vskip-3.00003pt\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t}=g(v_{1:t}),\quad t=1,2,\ldots

(4)

Our focus is designing
g
to learn robust long-horizon
CE
rules and the
AE
-relevant latent semantics needed for
CE
reasoning from sparse
CE
-level supervision with limited labeled sensor data.

We build

4.1
Sensor Trace Generation and Labeling

We generate long multimodal traces of daily human routines by composing real-world IMU and audio clips under a stochastic activity simulator that mimics natural behavior:
AE
durations are sampled from realistic ranges and
AE
-to-
AE
transitions follow probabilistic patterns (details in Appendix
C
). We source IMU clips from WISDM
(
Weiss, 2019
)
and audio clips from ESC-70
(
Moreaux et al., 2019
)
, and we additionally collect in-house audio clips to cover all 9
AE
types. Each
AE
instance is instantiated by sampling a
W_{\text{clip}}=2.5
s IMU/audio clip from its class and concatenating clips to form long sensor traces.

Online
CE
labels are generated by applying an expert-designed FSM per
CE
rule to the ground-truth
AE
trace (example code in Appendix
G
). This simulator supports (i)
cross-subject
evaluation by generating test traces from held-out subjects and (ii)
CE
rule generalization by varying
AE
durations and inter-
AE
gaps at test time while ensuring the target
CE
patterns remain satisfied. We generate 5-minute traces for training, and 5/15/30-minute traces for evaluation.

Axis I: Multilabel
CE
composition.

Unlike
Han et al. (2025)
, which uses mutually exclusive per-window labels and at most one active
CE
per trace,
DailyOCE
allows temporal spans of different
CE
classes to overlap in time. Consequently, multiple
CE
s may be detected in the same window, yielding multilabel supervision.

Axis II: Windowing settings.

Fig.
2
(A) illustrates how simulator-generated continuous sensor traces are segmented into online
decision windows
of length
W
for labeling and evaluation under different windowing regimes. Note that the resulting long-horizon
DailyOCE
dataset provides only
CE
labels; temporally aligned
AE
labels are used only internally by the simulator to derive the
CE
labels.

•

Idealized windowing.
The decision-window
W
matches the underlying
AE
clip length (
W=2.5
s), providing a controlled setting that removes window–
AE
length mismatch. We include: (i)
Aligned
windowing; (ii)
Phase-shifted
windowing, which keeps the same
W
but offsets window start times by
\phi
to test sensitivity to window-boundary placement; and (iii)
Adjacent-window fusion
, a controlled temporal-smoothing variant that mixes each window’s feature embedding with the previous window using a weight
\lambda
.

•

Realistic windowing.
The decision-window
W
differs from
W_{\text{clip}}
, reflecting reality where segmentation boundaries rarely match underlying
AE
boundaries. We first assign ground-truth
AE
labels at a finer resolution
\delta t
(e.g.,
0.5
s), then derive each decision-window
AE
label by majority vote over its
\delta t
segments;
CE
labels are computed by applying the
CE
FSMs to the resulting decision-window
AE
trace. This induces boundary mixing and label noise when a decision window overlaps multiple
AE
s. We evaluate multiple window sizes
W\neq W_{\text{clip}}
to stress-test sensitivity to segmentation choices.

5
Methodology

Our design of
NAROCE
is guided by two hypotheses:

•

Hypothesis I
:

CE
rule reasoning is the primary bottleneck; decoupling rule reasoning from sensor semantics reduces reliance on expensive labeled sensor data.

•

Hypothesis II
:
Pretraining on large-scale synthetic
AE
-level concept traces improves
CE
rule extrapolation to longer horizons and robustness under distribution shift.

Inspired by Neural Algorithmic Reasoning (NAR)
(
Veličković et al., 2022
;
Velickovic and Blundell, 2021
;
Ibarz et al., 2022
)
, which uses neural networks to represent and learn algorithms from symbolic input-output pairs, we view each
CE
rule as an algorithm executed over an
AE
sequence. We use Mamba as the long-context reasoning backbone, CE-NAR, and train
NAROCE
in three stages, shown in Fig.
1
:

•

Stage 1: Rule pretraining.
Train a Mamba-based CE-NAR on large-scale, low-cost synthetic
AE
-level concept traces to learn
CE
rules independently of sensor data.

•

Stage 2: Sensor adaptation.
With a small labeled sensor set, train the Sensor Adapter to map sensor embeddings into the latent reasoning space of CE-NAR.

•

Stage 3: Joint finetuning.
Unfreeze CE-NAR, and finetune it jointly with the Sensor Adapter for a few epochs using the same labeled sensor set.

Note that before these
NAROCE
training stages, we use a separate
Stage 0: Shared upstream feature extractor
.
The Pretrained Feature Extractor in Fig.
1
, corresponding to the feature encoder
m
in Eq.
4
, is pretrained separately on isolated
AE
-labeled sensor clips to produce multimodal latent embeddings, then frozen and shared across
NAROCE
and all baseline methods.
Our
CE
-only supervision claim applies to Stages 1–3, where no temporally aligned
AE
annotations of the long-horizon sensor traces are used.

5.1
Stage 1 - Training CE-NAR on Concept Traces

Synthetic
AE
-level concept traces.

These text traces can be generated by any principled stochastic simulator. In this work, we design an LLM-based synthesizer that generates stochastic simulator programs, which are then used to produce concept traces at the decision-window granularity (one
AE
per window of length
W
) via structured prompting. Concretely, it follows a four-step procedure:
(1) organizing
AE
s into
semantic groups
(e.g., hygiene, work);
(2) defining
probabilistic transitions
between groups and between
AE
s within each group;
(3) assigning
variable durations
to both groups and individual
AE
s; and
(4) dynamically adjusting transition probabilities to increase the likelihood of the target
CE
occurring.
For each
CE
rule, we use GPT-4o with temperature sampling set to 0.7 to generate 10 simulator programs, diversifying trace distributions and reducing bias.
Appendix
B
provides full prompt details.

Labeling.

To ensure accurate and consistent online
CE
labels, we deterministically label each concept trace by applying an expert-designed FSM per
CE
pattern (Section
4.1
). LLMs are not used here to avoid hallucinations.

Pretraining CE-NAR.

We pretrain CE-NAR as follows:

(1)

Generate a large set of synthetic
AE
-level concept traces of length
T
windows.
Tokenize each trace
\{a_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
with
AE
Tokenizer, which maps each
AE
concept
a_{t}
to a discrete token
x_{t}
via a fixed lookup vocabulary, producing
\{x_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.

(2)

Embed each token
x_{t}
with a learnable embedding encoder, Embedding Encoder
f
, into a 128-dimensional latent space.

(3)

Train Embedding Encoder
f
jointly with CE-NAR on the concept trace dataset, then freeze CE-NAR for use as the
CE
-rule reasoner in later stages.

5.2
Stage 2 - Training the Sensor Adapter

We train the Sensor Adapter
f^{\prime}
to map the sensor embedding
v_{t}
into CE-NAR’s latent reasoning space.
Here
v_{t}
is a 128-dimensional embedding extracted from the multimodal sensor window by a Pretrained Feature Encoder.

(1)

Remove
f
from Stage 1 and introduce
f^{\prime}
, which takes
v_{t}
and outputs a 128-dimensional vector that serves as input to CE-NAR for window
t
.

(2)

Keep CE-NAR frozen and train only
f^{\prime}
using labeled online
CE
sensor data.

We instantiate
f^{\prime}
as a small Mamba-based adapter to model short-range temporal structure within each window.

5.3
Stage 3 - Joint Finetuning

Finally, we unfreeze CE-NAR and finetune it jointly with the Sensor Adapter
f^{\prime}
for a few epochs using the same labeled sensor set. This stage provides light end-to-end adaptation, primarily refining the pretrained rule prior to the target
CE
-pattern distribution, while also allowing minor sensor-semantic adjustment beyond Stage 2.

Inference.

At inference time, the pipeline only runs the Pretrained Feature Encoder, then the Sensor Adapter
f^{\prime}
, followed by CE-NAR to produce online
CE
predictions.

5.4
Training Objective

Online
CE
supervision is extremely sparse (Section
3.2
): in most windows, the
CE
label vector
\mathbf{y}_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{|E|}
is all zeros, leading to severe class imbalance. We therefore adopt a
multilabel
version of Focal Loss to handle sparse supervision while supporting co-occurring
CE
s
(
Lin et al., 2017
)
. Let
\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{t}\in(0,1)^{|E|}
be the predicted probabilities. The loss over a dataset of
N
sequences is

L_{\mathrm{FL}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t}\sum_{c=1}^{|E|}\ell_{\mathrm{FL}}\!\bigl(y_{i,t,c},\hat{p}_{i,t,c}\bigr),

(5)

where the per-label loss for class
c
at window
t
is

\ell_{\mathrm{FL}}(y,\hat{p})=\alpha\,y\,(1-\hat{p})^{\gamma}\log(\hat{p})+(1-\alpha)(1-y)\,\hat{p}^{\gamma}\log(1-\hat{p}).

(6)

\gamma
is the focusing parameter that reduces the contribution of frequent classes, and
\alpha
is the class weight. We use
\gamma=2
as recommended
(
Lin et al., 2017
)
and select
\alpha=0.8
for all positive
CE
classes via a small grid search. We apply this objective in all training stages of
NAROCE
.

6
Experiments

Research Questions.

Our evaluation examines the two hypotheses introduced in Section
5
through four research questions.

RQ1. Data efficiency.
Does concept-trace pretraining reduce the amount of labeled sensor data required for online CED?

RQ2. Long-horizon generalization.
Does the pretrained
CE
reasoner improve generalization to sequences longer than those observed during training, and how does this behavior scale with the amount of synthetic concept-trace pretraining?

RQ3. Robustness.
How robust is
NAROCE
to cross-subject distribution shift, sensor perturbations, window-boundary misalignment, and changes in decision-window size?

RQ4. Learned alignment.
Does the sensor adapter map sensor observations into a structured latent space that reflects the underlying
AE
semantics despite receiving only
CE
-level supervision?

Datasets.

We
only
use 5-min labeled sensor data for training and validation, with a full budget of 10,000 training examples per idealized and realistic windowing setting in
DailyOCE
.

NAROCE
additionally pretrains on 40,000 5-min
AE
-level concept traces, which are easy and cheap to generate.
For evaluation, the
DailyOCE
simulator generates test sets using held-out subjects:
the 5-min test set measures cross-subject generalization, while the 15/30-min sets further increase
AE
durations and inter-
AE
gaps, requiring rule generalization beyond the training horizon.

Pretrained Feature Encoder.

As introduced in Stage 0, we use
m
in Eq.
4
as a pretrained, frozen encoder shared across
NAROCE
and all baseline methods. We train
m
on isolated
AE
-labeled sensor clips segmented with the same decision window
W
used at inference and keep it fixed throughout downstream CED training. Specifically, we use 1,000
AE
-labeled clips across the nine classes. Although self-supervised or unsupervised pretraining could also be used in principle, we adopt
AE
-supervised pretraining because it provides a strong perceptual representation for the Neural AE+FSM and Neural AE+ProbLog FSM baselines, whose performance depends directly on
AE
recognition accuracy; this design therefore favors these symbolic baselines. Architecture and training details are provided in Appendix
D
.

Baselines.

We compare against several complex event detector choices
g
in Eq.
4
.
All baselines operate causally, using feature embedding extracted by
m
up to window
t
, to prevent future leakage.
For neural architectures, we additionally ensure the receptive field exceeds the longest
CE
temporal span, so the model can observe complete patterns.
Baselines fall into three categories:

•

Standard end-to-end baselines.
These models take the sensor embeddings
\{v_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
as input and are trained end-to-end using online
CE
labels. We consider causal sequence models over sensor embeddings: a
unidirectional LSTM

(
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997
)
, a
causal TCN

(
Bai et al., 2018
)
, two
causal Transformer encoders
using either standard sinusoidal positional encoding or ALiBi positional biases
(
Press et al., 2022
)
and
Mamba

(
Gu and Dao, 2024
)
.

•

Online action detection baseline.
We include
MiniROAD

(
An et al., 2023
)
, a top-ranked GRU-based model on benchmarks from the OAD community
(
Jiang et al., 2014
;
Geest et al., 2016
)
, and the strongest publicly available method adaptable to our multimodal setting.

•

Neurosymbolic baselines.
These baselines apply the
ground-truth

CE
rules, so performance is limited mainly by
AE
prediction errors. We include them as a reference point.
Neural AE + FSM
first predicts an
AE
label per window using an MLP classifier, then applies one FSM per
CE
rule (Section
4.1
). We also explore a variant that propagates uncertainty,
Neural AE + ProbLog FSM
, implemented using the probabilistic programming language ProbLog
(
De Raedt et al., 2007
)
, which takes softmax outputs from an
AE
classifier and represents FSM states as probability distributions. When the probability of an accepting state, one that corresponds to a satisfied
CE
pattern, exceeds a preset threshold, the
ProbLog FSM
is reset to its initial state.

Implementation Details.
All models are optimized using AdamW and trained with focal loss; early stopping is based on validation loss. We report mean performance over 10 random seeds. Full baseline configurations,
NAROCE
configurations, and training details are provided in Appendix
E
.

Evaluation Metrics.

We report two aggregate metrics:

(1)

Macro F1
(
F1
): For each
CE
class (
e_{1}
–
e_{10}
), we threshold its predicted probability at
0.5
to obtain binary predictions at each window and compute F1; we then report the unweighted average over classes.

(2)

Mean Average Precision
(
mAP
): a threshold-free ranking metric. For each
CE
class, we compute average precision (AP) over all windows using the predicted probabilities, and report the mean AP over classes.

We use F1 as our primary metric and report mAP as a complementary threshold-free score.

6.1
Results and Analysis – Idealized Windowing

In idealized windowing, the decision window length matches the underlying sensor clip unit (
W=2.5
s), so windows align exactly with the ground-truth
AE
boundaries. Starting from this aligned setting, we further introduce controlled boundary perturbations to study robustness to sensor noise and window misalignment.

NAROCE
achieves the best performance with substantially less labeled sensor data.

Fig.
3
reports performance under
aligned
windowing as we vary the amount of labeled sensor training data.
NAROCE
consistently attains the highest F1 score and reaches near-peak performance with only 2k labeled sensor sequences. The advantage is most pronounced on the 15- and 30-minute test sets, where
NAROCE
maintains a clear margin over all baselines as the temporal horizon increases. Notably, the causal Transformer degrades sharply at longer horizons, likely due to the fragility of sinusoidal positional encoding when extrapolating to long contexts. The Neural AE + ProbLog FSM baseline, while conceptually appealing, also performs poorly in this setting, suggesting the need for better probabilistic FSM designs. These results support
RQ1
and
RQ2
: concept-trace pretraining substantially reduces the amount of labeled sensor data required for online CED, and improves generalization beyond the training horizon.

Robustness to window offsets and fusion perturbations with minimal labeled sensor data.

To probe sensitivity to windowing, we evaluate two controlled variants at inference time: (i)
Phase-shifted windowing
offsets the decision windows by a shift ratio
\phi
relative to the underlying
AE
transition boundaries. (ii)
Adjacent-window fusion
mixes neighboring windows with weight
\lambda
,

v_{t}^{\prime}=\lambda v_{t}+(1-\lambda)v_{t-1}
,
as a simple temporal smoothing mechanism.

Results are shown in Fig.
4
. For clarity, Mamba-10k denotes a Mamba model trained on 10k labeled sensor sequences, and
NAROCE
-2k denotes
NAROCE
trained on 2k sensor sequences (similarly for other models). Across both perturbations,
NAROCE
-2k remains better than baselines trained with the full 10k labeled set, especially on 15/30-minute horizons, supporting
RQ3
. We also observe that a small amount of fusion (
\lambda\approx 0.1
–
0.3
) improves most models, suggesting that mild temporal smoothing helps reduce sensitivity to local noise, whereas overly large fusion or shifts degrade performance. However, Neural AE + FSM degrades dramatically in both cases, highlighting that deterministic FSM is highly sensitive to
AE
misclassification caused by noise.

Ablation study.

We assess the contribution of key
NAROCE
design choices via the ablations in Table
3
: (i) removing Stage 1 and training CE-NAR and Sensor Adapter jointly from scratch; (ii) skipping the final joint finetuning of Sensor Adapter and CE-NAR after Stage 2; and (iii) replacing Focal Loss with standard multilabel cross-entropy loss in all training stages. All three ablations reduce performance. The largest drop comes from removing Stage 1, highlighting the importance of concept-trace pretraining for rule generalization. Skipping Stage 3 also hurts, indicating that joint finetuning is needed to adapt the pretrained rule prior to the target
CE
-pattern distribution. Replacing Focal Loss lowers F1, confirming the need to handle
CE
label sparsity in the loss function.

Efficiency, Scaling, and Representation Analysis

Scalability in compute.
We report model size and FLOPs in Table

.

NAROCE
adds only a small overhead over Mamba in both 5-minute full-trace inference and streaming per-step inference, while remaining 10-20

NAROCE

Scaling behavior of concept traces.

We study the effect of synthetic

Hypothesis I & II

7
Discussion & Conclusion

Scope of learned
CE
behavior.

The
CE
definitions evaluated in this work are predefined and are used to generate the Stage 1 concept-trace labels and the
DailyOCE
benchmark labels. However, the executable rules themselves are not provided to
NAROCE
or the other learning-based baselines; these models receive only labeled examples and must inductively learn the corresponding
CE
behavior. Thus, our experiments evaluate the setting of inducing predefined-but-hidden
CE
behavior from rule-generated labels, rather than learning
CE
behavior from annotations obtained without predefined executable symbolic rules. However, explicitly specified rules are not an inherent requirement of
NAROCE
. In principle, annotations could instead be provided by human annotators or other domain-specific labeling processes. We do not evaluate this broader setting in the present work and leave it for future investigation.

Limitations.

First, our evaluation is conducted on a controlled simulator-generated benchmark built from real-world sensor clips rather than naturally collected long-horizon
CE
sequences. Although this design enables systematic evaluation of rule extrapolation, sensor noise, distribution shift, and temporal windowing, real-world
CE
data may introduce additional sources of variability not captured here. Second, standard Transformer and TCN do not support efficient per-step streaming inference; exploring stateful or streaming designs
(
Gulati et al., 2020
)
is an important direction. Third, while
NAROCE
substantially reduces annotation requirements, 2k labeled sequences still require nontrivial data collection effort (
\approx
166 hours in our setting). Stronger data augmentation or self-supervised pretraining could further reduce annotation needs toward the hundreds, easing the burden of collecting real-world
CE
sequences. Finally, extending both the benchmark and
NAROCE
to additional sensing domains (e.g., video, WiFi), as well as collecting real-world
CE
evaluation data under privacy-preserving protocols, are important directions for future work.

Conclusion.

We present
NAROCE
, a neural algorithmic reasoning framework for efficient and robust online CED. By decoupling
CE
rule learning from sensor-specific perception,
NAROCE
reduces reliance on large-scale labeled sensor data. We also introduce
DailyOCE
, a controlled and scalable simulator-generated multimodal testbed for online multilabel CED built from real-world sensor clips, to serve as a practical proxy for studying long-horizon CED before real-world deployment, when
CE
data are prohibitively difficult to collect and annotate. Experiments show that
NAROCE
is competitive with the strongest baselines and often outperforms them with substantially fewer labeled sensor data, and lower FLOPs than all non-Mamba baselines, and that pretraining on large-scale, low-cost synthetic
AE
-level concept traces improves generalization to longer sequences as well as robustness to sensor noise and distribution shift. These findings provide evidence for the potential of
NAROCE
in online CED, while evaluation on naturally collected real-world CED data remains necessary to validate its real-world effectiveness and generalizability.

Broader Impact Statement

This work advances methods and benchmarks for online complex event detection from multimodal sensor streams. Potential positive impacts include more reliable monitoring and assistance in smart-health and safety applications, especially when labeled data are limited. However, models that infer high-level routines from sensor data may raise privacy concerns or be misused for surveillance. Moreover, reducing labeled-data requirements may lower the barrier to deploying such behavior-monitoring systems. We therefore emphasize that deployments should follow informed consent, data minimization, and secure on-device or privacy-preserving processing when possible. Our benchmark is simulator-based and intended for research. However, using LLMs to synthesize simulator programs may introduce LLM-related biases and other ethical concerns. Any real-world deployment should be evaluated for bias, robustness, and safety in the target population and environment.

Appendix D
Pretrained Feature Encoder

Fig.
8
illustrates the architecture of our pretrained feature encoder. Since the benchmark is multimodal, containing both IMU and audio streams, the encoder performs early fusion to produce a single fused embedding for each sensor window of length
W
.

D.1
Early Fusion Model

Multimodal fusion strategies typically fall into early, late, or hybrid paradigms
Baltrusaitis et al. (2017)
. We adopt
early fusion
, combining modalities immediately after feature extraction (Fig.
8
).

Given an audio and IMU window of length
W
, we extract modality-specific embeddings using pretrained encoders: BEATs
Chen et al. (2022b)
for audio and LIMU-BERT
Xu et al. (2021a)
for IMU. Each embedding stream is passed through a GRU, projecting to 128 dimensions. We then concatenate the two 128-d embeddings into a 256-d vector and apply a fusion layer to obtain a joint 128-d representation. During pretraining, we attach a one-layer MLP
AE
classifier head. At inference, we discard the classifier head and use the remaining network as the
pretrained feature extractor
, whose window embeddings are fed into downstream complex event detectors.

Figure 8
:
Overview of the multimodal fusion module.
In the figure, a rectangular block represents data or an embedding vector, while a rounded corner rectangular block represents a neural network. The dashed-lined block indicates that it is omitted during the inference phase.

D.2
Training

The BEATs model used for the audio module is already pre-trained, while the LIMU-Bert model for IMU module is pre-trained on large datasets. We freeze the parameters of both models and focus on training the other components of the multimodal fusion module. For training and testing the fusion module, we utilize the multimodal
AE
dataset introduced in Table
C.2
. The training process minimizes the cross-entropy loss:

\min_{\theta}L_{m}=-\min_{\theta}\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{enc}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{enc}}}c_{i}\cdot\log\left(\hat{c}_{i}\right)\right),\qquad\hat{c}_{i}=m_{\theta}(\mathbf{d}_{i}).

(7)

where
\mathbf{d}_{i}
is the multimodal sensor data of window size
W
,
\hat{c}_{i}
is the predicted
AE
label,
c_{i}
is the corresponding ground-truth label, and
N_{\mathrm{enc}}
denotes the number of samples used to train the feature encoder. In our experiments,
N_{\mathrm{enc}}=1000
.

Appendix E
Experiment Implementations

E.1
Baseline Model Details

Standard End-to-end Baselines:

•

Unidirectional LSTM
: 5 LSTM layers with a hidden dimension of 256.

•

Causal TCN
: We use a temporal convolutional network with causal convolutions to prevent future leakage. The network has 9 convolutional levels with channel sizes
\{128,128,256,256,256,256,256,128,128\}
and kernel size
k=3
. Following the standard TCN design, dilation grows exponentially across levels, and the largest dilation is
d_{\max}=256
. The resulting receptive field is
\mathrm{RF}=1+(k-1)\sum_{\ell=0}^{8}d_{\ell}=1+2(2^{9}-1)=1023
windows, i.e.,
1023\times W=1023\times 2.5\text{s}=2557.5\text{s}\approx 42.6
minutes,
which is larger than the longest 30-minute
CE
test case.

•

Causal Transformer Encoder
: with a triangular attention mask to restrict the model’s self-attention to previous timestamps only, excluding information from future timestamps. The causal transformer encoder uses 6 encoder layers with a hidden dimension 128, multi-head attention with 8 heads, and positional encoding
Vaswani et al. (2017)
.

•

Mamba
: We use a Mamba model with 12 SSM blocks. We made this design choice because the original Mamba paper
Gu and Dao (2024)
states that two Mamba blocks are equivalent to one transformer layer.

Neurosymbolic Baselines.

We train an MLP
AE
classifier on 2.5 s multimodal
AE
clips, achieving 95% accuracy on the
AE
test set.

•

Neural AE + FSM
uses one-hot
AE
predictions from the classifier.

•

Neural AE + ProbLog FSM
uses the classifier softmax probabilities. The accepting-state threshold is set to
1/|\mathcal{S}|
, where
|\mathcal{S}|
is the number of FSM states.

Online Action Detection (OAD) Baseline:

While OAD addresses a different task than online CED, its methods for long-term dependency modeling are relevant. Many recent approaches
Wang et al. (2023)
;
Chen et al. (2022a)
rely on vision-specific modules (e.g., spatial attention) or task-specific components (e.g., start/end detectors, label smoothing) that are incompatible with online CED. GateHUB
Chen et al. (2022a)
is promising but not open-source.

We adopt MiniROAD
An et al. (2023)
, a recent top-performing GRU-based model on standard OAD benchmarks
Jiang et al. (2014)
;
Geest et al. (2016)
, and the strongest publicly available option adaptable to our setup. MiniROAD identifies that RNNs underperform in long-term OAD due to a mismatch between training (short, reset clips) and inference (continuous streams). To address this, it applies a non-uniform loss weighting strategy that supervises more the final time step of each clip, aligning training behavior with inference conditions.

We adapt MiniROAD from frame-level video inputs to window-level IMU+audio segments by replacing its original visual encoder with our Pretrained Feature Encoder. While MiniROAD uses a multi-label classification loss, we replace it with our Focal Loss tailored for the online CED task, while retaining their non-uniform loss weighting strategy. We use a 4-layer MiniROAD model with a hidden dimension of 256.

For CE-NAR, we use a Mamba model with 12 SSM blocks and hidden dimension 128.
For Sensor Adapter, we use a smaller Mamba model with 6 SSM blocks and hidden dimension 128.

E.3
Training Details

All experiments run on one NVIDIA H100 GPU and use AdamW.

Optimization Hyperparameters.

For neural baselines, we use learning rate
1\times 10^{-3}
, weight decay
0.1
, and batch size
256
.
For
NAROCE
, we use stage-specific learning rates:
5\times 10^{-4}
for Stage 1 (CE-NAR pretraining),

5\times 10^{-3}
for Stage 2 (Sensor Adapter training), and
5\times 10^{-5}
for Stage 3 (joint finetuning).
We use dropout
0.1
in the idealized setting to reduce overfitting and dropout
0
in the realistic setting.
In the idealized setting, we use a 3-layer Mamba Sensor Adapter to reduce overfitting.
Baseline architectures are kept fixed across windowing regimes: after an architecture search, we use the same selected configurations in both settings, as we found baselines consistently benefit from larger model sizes.

We train with multilabel Focal Loss to address severe label imbalance.
Early stopping is based on validation loss with a fixed patience.
We report results over 10 random seeds.

Training Schedule.

Stage 1 (CE-NAR pretraining) is trained for 5,000 epochs.
Stage 2 (Sensor Adapter training) is trained for a total of 200,000,000 sensor-sample iterations
(i.e., the total number of per-sample update steps accumulated over repeated passes through the dataset).
All neural baselines are trained with the same budget of 200,000,000 sensor-sample iterations.
Stage 3 (joint finetuning) is trained for 10 epochs.
