From Micro-Distributions to Macro-Regularities: A Critique and Reconstruction of the Production Function Based on the Maximum Entropy Principle
Jihyuan Liuh

Abstract
This paper aims to provide a micro-foundation for the Cobb-Douglas production function based on statistical physics, and to launch a critique of its political-economic implications. By introducing the Maximum Entropy Principle and an axiom of scale invariance, we prove that in an economic system with incomplete information, the most unbiased distribution of micro-level technical coefficients must take the form of a truncated power law. Based on this, statistical aggregation naturally leads to the emergence of a constant-returns-to-scale Cobb-Douglas function at the macro level. This result not only provides a micro-foundation for neoclassical growth models that does not rely on a representative agent or value aggregation of capital but, more importantly, reveals that the aggregate production function is essentially a lossy compression of micro-level information. In this compression process, the social-historical relations embedded in distribution parameters are ’naturalized’ into seemingly eternal technical laws, which is the manifestation of Marx’s critique of ’fetishism’ at the level of mathematical logic. This paper further deepens the understanding of the production function as a statistical phenomenon rather than a technical law through dialogues with Marx, the Cambridge School, and Shaikh. Keywords: Cobb-Douglas Production Function; Maximum Entropy Principle; Statistical Emergence; Capital Fetishism; Cambridge Capital Controversy JEL Codes: O33.



1
Introduction

In the theoretical edifice of modern macroeconomics, the aggregate Cobb-Douglas (C-D) production function undoubtedly occupies a cornerstone position. Since its proposal in 1928, its simple mathematical form and its remarkable ability to fit the ’constant factor shares’ stylized fact (Kaldor Facts) have made it the lingua franca of growth theory, business cycle models, and development accounting. In the neoclassical narrative, the aggregate production function is treated as a physical law describing technical characteristics; its exponents
\alpha
and
1-\alpha
are interpreted as the output elasticities of capital and labor in the production process, thereby providing legitimacy for income distribution under marginal productivity theory.

However, the foundation of this macro edifice has never been truly secure. The mid-20th century ”Cambridge Capital Controversy” exposed the fatal logical flaw of the aggregate production function: in a model with heterogeneous capital, unless one assumes an extremely restrictive one-commodity world, it is impossible to define the capital aggregate
K
without prior knowledge of the interest rate, leading to a logical circularity of ”using the interest rate to determine the interest rate” (Robinson, 1953; Sraffa, 1960). Furthermore, Shaikh (1974, 2005) proposed the famous ”humbug” hypothesis, arguing that the so-called C-D production function might merely be an algebraic disguise of the national income identity
Y\equiv wL+rK
under relatively stable factor shares, rather than reflecting any real production technology. These critiques suggest that the regularities we observe in macro data may not stem from the physical properties of technology but from statistical artifacts of the aggregation process or constraints imposed by accounting identities.

Faced with this theoretical dilemma, Houthakker (1955) offered a highly enlightening way out. He demonstrated that if the technical efficiency of micro production units follows a Pareto distribution, then aggregating these fixed-proportion (Leontief) technologies would naturally lead to the emergence of a C-D function at the macro level. This discovery hints that macro regularities might be a form of ”statistical emergence” rather than a simple scaling up of micro agents. However, Houthakker’s work left a question: Why must the technical distribution necessarily be Pareto? Is this distribution an arbitrary assumption or a necessary consequence of some deeper mechanism? More importantly, what does this statistical aggregation perspective imply for understanding the nature and social attributes of capital?

This paper aims to establish a complete axiomatic system to answer these questions and, based on this, launch a political-economic critique of the aggregate production function. We introduce the ”Maximum Entropy Principle” from statistical physics and the ”Scale Invariance” of the system as core axioms. We prove that under the axiomatic framework satisfying scale invariance, physical constraints, and the maximum entropy principle, the maximum entropy distribution (i.e., the distribution with minimal information and maximal unbiasedness) of micro technical coefficients must be a truncated power law.

On this basis, this paper constructs a statistical equilibrium model incorporating heterogeneous production units and physical capacity constraints. The derivation shows that this model sidesteps the core difficulty of the Cambridge Capital Controversy: we do not need to assume a representative firm, nor do we need to aggregate heterogeneous capital goods via the interest rate (i.e., value aggregation of capital). Instead, based solely on the statistical distribution of technical coefficients, the physical capacity aggregation of fixed factors, and the screening mechanism of market competition, we can rigorously derive a constant-returns-to-scale C-D production function at the macro level. This result not only provides a solid micro-statistical foundation for the C-D function but, more crucially, offers a new perspective for re-examining the Cambridge Controversy and Marx’s ”commodity fetishism.”

The core argument of this paper is that the aggregate production function is essentially an information compression of a micro complex system. Constant factor shares do not arise from physical necessity of technology but from the steady-state distribution of micro-heterogeneity under specific statistical constraints. Neoclassical theory interprets this statistical regularity as a technical law, thereby naturalizing the social-historical attributes of capital (embodied in distribution parameters) into properties of things. This is precisely the manifestation of Marx’s critique of ”fetishism” at the level of mathematical logic — a specific historical form of social relations is disguised, through the aggregation process, as an eternal natural law.

The structure of this paper is as follows: Part II establishes the axiomatic system based on scale invariance and the maximum entropy principle, deriving the joint distribution of technical coefficients; Part III performs statistical aggregation under market competition, proving the emergence mechanism of the C-D function and constant shares; Part IV engages in theoretical dialogue, using this mathematical framework to respond to the Cambridge Capital Controversy, deepen Shaikh’s humbug theory, and explore the formation mechanism of capital fetishism; and finally, the Conclusion.

2
Axiomatic System and Micro-Foundations

This section is dedicated to establishing an axiomatic framework starting from the micro distribution of techniques. Unlike traditional literature (e.g., Houthakker, 1955) which directly assumes a Pareto distribution, this paper strives to provide a deeper, principle-based explanation for this distributional form. Directly assuming a distribution, while concise, is ’arbitrary’—it itself constitutes a black box requiring explanation. This paper adopts a ’reverse-engineering’ strategy: we first identify the most basic, indisputable symmetries and information principles that the economic system should satisfy (Axioms 1 and 3), then solve for the most unbiased probability distribution under these constraints via variational calculus. The derived distribution is thus no longer an arbitrarily chosen assumption but a logical necessity under the system’s intrinsic constraints. This greatly enhances the robustness and explanatory power of the model’s foundation.

2.1
Setup of Micro Production Units

Consider an economic system composed of a large number of heterogeneous production units. Each unit
j
has the following characteristics:

1.

Fixed-Proportion (Leontief) Technology:
Producing one unit of output requires
a_{1,j}
units of the first variable input (e.g., raw materials, variable capital) and
a_{2,j}
units of the second variable input (e.g., labor). We call the vector
\mathbf{a}_{j}=(a_{1,j},a_{2,j})
the unit’s
micro technical coefficient
.

2.

Capacity Constraint:
Each production unit is subject to a physical limit from fixed capital (e.g., machinery, land, or managerial capacity), with a maximum output capacity
\phi_{j}
.

In the short run, the technical coefficients
\mathbf{a}_{j}
and capacity
\phi_{j}
are given. Production units are price takers. Given the output price
p_{0}
(normalized to 1) and input prices
p_{1},p_{2}
, a unit’s decision is to produce at full capacity (if unit profit
\pi_{j}=1-a_{1,j}p_{1}-a_{2,j}p_{2}>0
) or shut down (if
\pi_{j}<0
).

We view the entire economic system as a probability field over the state space of technical coefficients
(a_{1},a_{2})
. Let
p(a_{1},a_{2})
be the joint probability density function of technical coefficients in the economy. Our core task is to determine the form of
p(a_{1},a_{2})
.

2.2
Fundamental Axioms

To determine the distributional form without introducing arbitrary assumptions, we introduce the following three axioms.

Axiom 1 (Scale Invariance):
Technical efficiency is a relative concept; the functional form of its probability distribution should not depend on the units of measurement for inputs.

Economic Interpretation:
Whether we measure labor in ”man-hours” or ”man-days,” and raw materials in ”tons” or ”kilograms,” the statistical regularities of the technology distribution (e.g., degree of inequality) should remain consistent. Mathematically, this implies that if we transform the random variables
a_{i}\to k_{i}a_{i}
, the transformed density function should belong to the same family as the original, with only parameters changing. This strongly suggests the distribution should have a Power Law characteristic.

Axiom 2 (Physical Constraints):
Technological progress has physical limits.

For each input
i
, there exists a theoretical minimum efficiency (i.e., maximum input coefficient)
A_{i}^{\max}>0
, such that all existing technologies must satisfy
0<a_{i}\leq A_{i}^{\max}
.

Note:
While a physical maximum efficiency (i.e., a lower bound
a_{i}^{\min}>0
) also exists, in the frontier economies of interest here, we assume the observation scale is much larger than this physical lower bound (the asymptotic frontier assumption, detailed later), so mathematically we let the lower bound tend to 0.

Axiom 3 (Maximum Entropy Principle):
Given known macro constraints, the probability distribution containing the least information (i.e., with maximum entropy) should be chosen.

Methodological Explanation:
When we lack complete information about micro processes (e.g., specific R&D history, enterprise management details), any assumption beyond known constraints introduces subjective bias. Jaynes (1957) argued that the maximum entropy distribution is the only inference result guaranteeing ”maximal unbiasedness” under such circumstances.

2.3
Derivation of the Technology Distribution

Based on the above axioms, we derive
p(a_{1},a_{2})
by solving a variational problem.

First, according to Axiom 1 (scale invariance), the most natural scale for measuring technical level is the logarithmic scale. Technological progress is typically multiplicative (e.g., a 10% efficiency increase), not additive. Therefore, the first-moment constraints we impose should be on the logarithmic variables:

\mathbb{E}[\ln a_{1}]=\iint\ln a_{1}\cdot p(a_{1},a_{2})\,da_{1}da_{2}=\mu_{1}

\mathbb{E}[\ln a_{2}]=\iint\ln a_{2}\cdot p(a_{1},a_{2})\,da_{1}da_{2}=\mu_{2}

where
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
represent the average position of the technological frontier in the economic system.

Our goal is to maximize the Shannon Entropy over the domain
\mathcal{D}=\{(a_{1},a_{2})|0<a_{i}\leq A_{i}^{\max}\}
:

H[p]=-\iint_{\mathcal{D}}p(a_{1},a_{2})\ln p(a_{1},a_{2})\,da_{1}da_{2}

subject to the normalization condition
\iint p\,da_{1}da_{2}=1
and the above logarithmic mean constraints.

Construct the Lagrangian functional:

\mathcal{L}=-\iint p\ln p\,d\mathbf{a}+\lambda_{0}(\iint p\,d\mathbf{a}-1)+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\lambda_{i}(\iint p\ln a_{i}\,d\mathbf{a}-\mu_{i})

Taking the variational derivative with respect to
p
and setting it to zero:

\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta p}=-\ln p-1+\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}\ln a_{1}+\lambda_{2}\ln a_{2}=0

Solving gives the distribution form:

p(a_{1},a_{2})=\exp(\lambda_{0}-1)\cdot a_{1}^{\lambda_{1}}\cdot a_{2}^{\lambda_{2}}

Let
\alpha_{i}=\lambda_{i}+1
, and write the normalization constant explicitly. Considering the domain
0<a_{i}\leq A_{i}^{\max}
, the normalization integral requires
\alpha_{i}>0
for convergence. Calculating the normalization constant
C
:

1=C\int_{0}^{A_{1}^{\max}}a_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}da_{1}\int_{0}^{A_{2}^{\max}}a_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-1}da_{2}\implies C=\frac{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}{(A_{1}^{\max})^{\alpha_{1}}(A_{2}^{\max})^{\alpha_{2}}}

Finally, we obtain the joint probability density function of technical coefficients as
independent truncated power law distributions
:

p(a_{1},a_{2})=\frac{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}{(A_{1}^{\max})^{\alpha_{1}}(A_{2}^{\max})^{\alpha_{2}}}a_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}a_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-1},\quad 0<a_{i}\leq A_{i}^{\max}

(1)

2.4
Economic Interpretation of Distribution Parameters

The derived distribution has several key characteristics worth interpreting from an economic perspective:

•

Independence:
The form
p(a_{1},a_{2})=f_{1}(a_{1})f_{2}(a_{2})
indicates that, under the maximum entropy benchmark, the technical efficiencies of the two inputs are statistically independent. This implies no systematic ”skill-biased” correlation exists (unless additional correlation constraints are introduced).

•

Shape Parameter
\alpha_{i}
:
The parameter
\alpha_{i}
determines the shape of the distribution.

–

When
\alpha_{i}
is small (close to 0), the distribution is not only skewed towards 0 (the high-efficiency end) but also has a thick tail, implying the existence of many extremely efficient ”superstar” firms.

–

When
\alpha_{i}
is large, the distribution concentrates towards
A_{i}^{\max}
(the low-efficiency end), meaning technological disparities are small, with most firms clustered near the technologically backward edge.

Therefore,
\alpha_{i}
can be viewed as a measure of
inverse technical heterogeneity
or as reflecting some social-historical state of technology diffusion and monopoly power.

Thus far, we have established the micro-foundations via first principles. The next section will show how macro regularities emerge when these micro agents, following a power law distribution, are aggregated in the market.

3
From Micro Behavior to Macro Emergence

Having established that micro technical coefficients follow a truncated power law distribution, this section examines the collective behavior of these heterogeneous production units under a market price mechanism. We will prove that through statistical aggregation, the combination of the micro-level technology distribution and market selection naturally gives rise to a macro production function with constant factor share characteristics.

3.1
Market Selection of Micro Agents

Consider a representative production unit characterized by its technical coefficient vector
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2})
and fixed-factor capacity
\phi
. Assume
\phi
and
\mathbf{a}
are independent, and the aggregate fixed-factor stock in society is
K=\int\phi\,dF(\phi)
.

Facing market prices
(p_{0}=1,p_{1},p_{2})
, the production unit faces a binary choice:

•

If unit profit
\pi(\mathbf{a})=1-a_{1}p_{1}-a_{2}p_{2}\geq 0
, produce at full capacity: output
y=\phi
, input demand
x_{i}=a_{i}\phi
.

•

If
\pi(\mathbf{a})<0
, shut down: output and inputs are 0.

Therefore, on the
(a_{1},a_{2})
plane, active production units must lie within the triangular region bounded by the budget constraint line
a_{1}p_{1}+a_{2}p_{2}=1
and the coordinate axes:

\mathcal{D}(p)=\{(a_{1},a_{2}):a_{1}\geq 0,a_{2}\geq 0,a_{1}p_{1}+a_{2}p_{2}\leq 1\}

(2)

Assumption 1 (Effective Constraint Equilibrium):
We assume the economy is in a ”frontier state,” meaning market prices are sufficiently high (or technology sufficiently advanced) such that the budget constraint line lies entirely within the rectangle defined by the physical technological upper bounds. Mathematically:
1/p_{i}<A_{i}^{\max}
.

Economic Meaning:
This assumption implies that all technologies eliminated by the market (those outside
\mathcal{D}(p)
but physically feasible) are eliminated due to
economic inefficiency
, not physical impossibility. This ensures the integration region is a regular simplex.

3.2
Statistical Aggregation and Constant Shares

Define macro aggregate output
Y
and aggregate variable inputs
X_{i}
as the sum over all active production units. Using the law of large numbers, the expected values of these aggregates can be expressed as integrals over the distribution density:

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=K\iint_{\mathcal{D}(p)}p(a_{1},a_{2})\,da_{1}da_{2}

(3)

\displaystyle X_{i}

\displaystyle=K\iint_{\mathcal{D}(p)}a_{i}p(a_{1},a_{2})\,da_{1}da_{2}

(4)

Substituting the derived distribution
p(a_{1},a_{2})\propto a_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}a_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-1}
. To simplify calculations, let
y=Y/K
and
x_{i}=X_{i}/K
denote averages per unit of fixed factor. Using the variable substitution
t_{i}=a_{i}p_{i}
, the integration region transforms into the standard simplex
t_{1}+t_{2}\leq 1
.

After standard integration (using Dirichlet integral or Beta function properties, detailed in the Appendix), we obtain the following explicit solutions:

y=C_{0}\cdot p_{1}^{-\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{-\alpha_{2}}

(5)

x_{i}=C_{i}\cdot p_{i}^{-1}\cdot p_{1}^{-\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{-\alpha_{2}}

(6)

where
C_{0},C_{i}
are constants depending only on the parameters
\alpha
and
A^{\max}
.

From this, we can compute a key dimensionless quantity in macroeconomics—
Factor Income Shares
:

S_{1}=\frac{p_{1}X_{1}}{Y}=\frac{p_{1}x_{1}}{y}=\frac{C_{1}}{C_{0}}=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1}

(7)

S_{2}=\frac{p_{2}X_{2}}{Y}=\frac{p_{2}x_{2}}{y}=\frac{C_{2}}{C_{0}}=\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1}

(8)

This is a striking result:
Factor shares
S_{i}
are constants completely independent of prices
p_{1},p_{2}
.
Regardless of fluctuations in factor prices, as long as the micro technical distribution maintains its power-law characteristic, the macro distribution proportion remains stable. This is precisely the defining feature of the Cobb-Douglas production function.

3.3
Emergence of the Cobb-Douglas Function

Using duality theory, we can invert the factor demand functions to derive the production function. From the constancy of
S_{i}
, we can solve for prices:

p_{i}\propto\frac{Y}{X_{i}}

Substituting this into the output equation (
5
)
Y\propto p_{1}^{-\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{-\alpha_{2}}
, after rearrangement we obtain:

Y=\mathcal{A}\cdot X_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}X_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}K^{\gamma_{K}}

(9)

where the macro exponents
\gamma
have a one-to-one correspondence with the micro parameters
\alpha
:

\gamma_{1}=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1},\quad\gamma_{2}=\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1},\quad\gamma_{K}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1}

(10)

and constant returns to scale holds:
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{K}=1
.

3.4
Economic Interpretation of Macro Emergence

The above derivation reveals the deep structure of macro regularities:

1.

Origin of Exponents:
The macro output elasticity
\gamma_{i}
does not, as neoclassical theory claims, represent the marginal product of some single technology. Instead, it is a function of the micro distribution shape parameter
\alpha_{i}
. A smaller
\alpha_{i}
(greater micro technical heterogeneity, fatter-tailed distribution) corresponds to a smaller factor share
\gamma_{i}
. This indicates that macro distribution proportions are determined by the degree of
heterogeneity
within the system’s technology.

2.

Source of Returns to Scale:
In our model, micro units have fixed-proportion (Leontief) technology; there is no smooth factor substitution. The macro substitution elasticity of 1 (C-D form) arises entirely from adjustment on the
extensive margin
—as relative prices change, previously unprofitable firms become profitable and enter the market (or vice versa), thereby changing the overall input-output ratio.

3.

Nature of Quasi-Rents:
The term
K^{\gamma_{K}}
appears, meaning the fixed factor
K
receives a constant share
\gamma_{K}
of output. Under perfect competition assumptions, this income does not originate from ”waiting” or ”capital services” but from
scarcity rent
. Due to capacity constraints and the presence of many highly efficient (low-cost) firms in the distribution, these firms inevitably earn positive excess profits in a market where the marginal price is determined by less efficient firms. Macro ”capital income” is essentially the statistical aggregate of these micro rents.

Thus far, we have proven that the Cobb-Douglas production function is a statistical emergence of truncated power law distributions under market mechanisms. This provides a simple mathematical foundation for the critique of capital fetishism in the next section.

4
A Mathematical Dissection of Capital Fetishism — A Dialogue with Marx

In
Capital
, Marx systematically elaborated ’commodity fetishism,’ revealing the reification of social relations in the commodity form. Later scholars extended this critique to the realm of capital, forming the concept of ’capital fetishism’ to analyze the mystification of the capital valorization process. It profoundly reveals the specific illusion of the capitalist mode of production: social relations between people are concealed and appear as natural properties between things. For a long time, this critique remained primarily at the philosophical and qualitative analysis level. The axiomatic system established in this study, by demonstrating the statistical emergence mechanism of macro regularities, provides a preliminary mathematical footnote to Marx’s critique of fetishism.

4.1
Parameters as Social Relations and Exponents as Natural Attributes

In our micro model, the shape of the technical coefficient distribution is determined by shape parameters
\alpha_{i}
. These seemingly dry statistical parameters actually condense profound social-historical content.

\alpha_{i}
measures the degree of heterogeneity and concentration of technical efficiency in the economic system. What determines a society’s
\alpha
value? It depends on the restrictions on technology diffusion by intellectual property regimes, the shaping of labor skills by the education system, the control over R&D direction by monopoly capital, and the inducement of technology choice (e.g., machine substitution for labor) by the balance of class forces. In other words,
\alpha_{i}
is the ”statistical index” of production relations and class power balance in a specific historical stage. It is historical, social, and mutable.

However, when we derive the macro Cobb-Douglas function
Y=\mathcal{A}X_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}X_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}K^{\gamma_{K}}
through statistical aggregation, this social-historicity undergoes a qualitative ”phase transition.”

In the macro function,
\gamma_{i}=\alpha_{i}/(\sum\alpha+1)
is interpreted as the ”output elasticity” of the factor. In the neoclassical context, this is treated as a technical attribute akin to a physical law, as if capital
K
and labor
L
naturally combine to produce output like hydrogen and oxygen combining to produce water according to a fixed chemical formula.
This is a typical ”naturalization” process: social relations full of tension and conflict (
\alpha
), through the black box of the aggregation mechanism, are transformed into cold, seemingly eternal technical parameters (
\gamma
). This is precisely the completed form of fetishism at the level of mathematical logic — a specific historical product of social relations is disguised as a trans-historical natural law.

4.2
Differential Rent and the Illusion of Capital Productivity

Marxist political economy strictly distinguishes between ”value creation” and ”value distribution.” This distinction is vividly reflected in the
\gamma_{K}
term (the share of the fixed factor) in our model.

In mainstream economics textbooks,
\gamma_{K}
represents the marginal product of fixed capital, i.e., the ”contribution” of fixed capital as a production factor to output. However, returning to our micro-foundations, the source of this share is entirely different.
Our model shows that the fixed factor
K
receives returns not because it ”produces” anything (the micro production function is Leontief; the fixed factor itself does not directly enter the production process but merely provides a capacity constraint), but because of its
scarcity
and
excludability
.

Specifically, due to technological differences (the
\alpha
distribution), the market price is determined by the marginal, inefficient firms (zero-profit condition). This allows all intra-marginal firms with more efficient technology to earn excess profits. In nature, this profit is equivalent to the
differential rent
described by Ricardo and Marx.
The macro
K^{\gamma_{K}}
is essentially the statistical sum of these micro differential rents.

Therefore, this model reveals a grand theoretical ”appropriation”:
rent
arising from private ownership and exclusive possession of the means of production is, in the form of the Cobb-Douglas function, repackaged as
productivity
stemming from factor function. This can be understood as a mathematical rendition of Marx’s critique of the ”Trinity Formula” (capital-interest, land-rent, labor-wage): projecting the social roots of distribution relations onto the technical roots of the production process.

4.3
Lossy Compression of Information and the Concealment of Social Relations

From an information theory perspective (echoing Axiom 3, the Maximum Entropy Principle), the process of establishing a macro production function is essentially a process of
information compression
.

In this process, detailed information (entropy) about which micro individual exploits whom, who monopolizes technology, who is being eliminated, is ”averaged out.” The macro function retains only the most macroscopic statistical features of the system. This compression is so successful that it is mathematically extremely concise (Cobb-Douglas form) and empirically extremely robust (constant shares).

However, this success is precisely the source of its deceptive nature. It not only compresses data but also ”compresses” social contradictions. When an economist points to the Cobb-Douglas function and says, ”Look, this is the law of economic operation,” they are actually presenting a ”holographic photo” stripped of all social power relations.
The value of this axiomatic system lies in its reverse engineering (from macro back to micro distribution), breaking the myth of this hologram. It reminds us that behind those smooth isoquants lie uneven class terrain and technological divides.

In summary, this model does not deny the existence of macro regularities but reshapes the understanding of these regularities from a Marxist perspective: the Cobb-Douglas function is not a reflection of capitalist production technology but the fetishized projection of capitalist production relations (as a statistical distribution) within the system of commodity market exchange.

5
The Logical Dilemma of Capital Aggregation — A Dialogue with the Cambridge School

The ”Cambridge Capital Controversy” that erupted in the 1960s, which questioned the production function, is also worth pondering. The Cambridge (UK) School, represented by Joan Robinson and Piero Sraffa, sharply challenged the neoclassical synthesis represented by Samuelson and Solow: In a world with heterogeneous capital goods, what exactly is
K
in the aggregate production function
Y=F(K,L)
?

5.1
Circular Reasoning and a Price-Independent Capital Aggregate

The core of the Cambridge critique lies in the problem of ”reverse causality” or ”circular reasoning.” Neoclassical theory attempts to explain the interest rate (profit rate) by taking the partial derivative
r=\partial F/\partial K
of the production function. This requires
K
to be a physically aggregateable quantity independent of the interest rate. However, real-world capital is heterogeneous (e.g., machine tools, factories, software). To aggregate them into a single
K
, prices (interest rates) must be used for valuation.
Thus, a logical closure emerges:
To determine the interest rate, one must first know the capital aggregate; to determine the capital aggregate, one must first know the interest rate.
Sraffa (1960) further proved that, due to the ”reswitching of techniques” phenomenon, there isn’t even a monotonic relationship between capital value and the interest rate, which completely undermines the logical foundation of the neoclassical production function.

This axiomatic system provides a unique perspective to address this dilemma. Our model deliberately avoids the trap of ”capital value aggregation” from the outset:

1.

Return to Physical Quantities:
In our micro-foundations,
K
is strictly defined as the
physical stock
of the fixed factor (e.g., number of machines, area of land, or specific capacity units), not as a value quantity.
\phi
is the capacity parameter,
K
is the integral of capacity. This is an engineering quantity, independent of the price system.

2.

Emergence of Value Quantities:
In the macro relation we derived,
Y=\mathcal{A}X^{\gamma}K^{\gamma_{K}}
, although
K
appears formally, its ”price” (rental rate
r
) is not given exogenously but is endogenously determined by the model (the residual after subtracting variable costs from total output).

Therefore, this model is logically self-consistent; it severs the circular ”defining capital via interest rates” and demonstrates that, based on purely physical aggregation, the macro economy can still exhibit Cobb-Douglas-like regularities.

5.2
The Production Function as a Parable: Acknowledging the Rupture Between Micro and Macro

However, this evasion of the aggregation problem comes at a cost, a cost that precisely confirms the deeper judgment of the Cambridge School.

Joan Robinson (1953) famously called the production function a ”powerful parable.” She argued that using smooth curves to depict the production process, while providing some intuition, conceals the discreteness and irreversibility of micro technology choices.

The conclusions of our model further elaborate Robinson’s point.
Although we derived
Y=K^{\gamma_{K}}(\dots)
at the macro level, making it appear as if there is a continuously differentiable technical relationship between
K
and
Y
, no such relationship exists in our micro world. Micro firms use fixed-proportion (Leontief) technology; there is no smooth substitution between capital and labor.
The ”substitution elasticity of 1” observed at the macro level arises entirely from changes in the entry, exit, and composition of firms on the
extensive margin
.

This means the Cambridge School is correct:
The macro production function is not an enlarged version of the micro production function.
The macro function is a statistical object describing the
state distribution
of the system, not the
technical blueprint
of a single firm. When we say ”capital increases, marginal product decreases,” this is merely a statistical metaphor; what actually happens is: more capital entering leads to price competition, forcing less efficient (lower-rent) marginal firms into the market, pulling down the average rate of return.

5.3
Theoretical Compromise on Reswitching

It must be honestly noted that while this model solves the static aggregation problem, it still has limitations in dealing with Sraffa’s dynamic challenge of ”reswitching.”
Our axiomatic system (Maximum Entropy Principle) assumes a unimodal, smooth probability distribution (power law). This implicitly assumes that technology choice is ”ordered” in some sense. In Sraffa’s world, as the interest rate changes, the optimal technique may jump back and forth between labor-intensive and capital-intensive types; this non-monotonicity is smoothed out in our single-parameter distribution.

This suggests that the reason the Cobb-Douglas function is so prevalent empirically might be precisely because the complexity (entropy) of the real economy is high enough to drown out those pathological ”reswitching” phenomena in statistical averaging. In this sense, while this model does not fully account for the logical possibilities explored by the Cambridge School, it explains why the neoclassical ”parable” can be an effective empirical approximation under the law of large numbers.

In summary, dialogue with the Cambridge School leads us to recognize that the Cobb-Douglas function derived in this model should be seen as a
”statistical validity”
rather than
”logical truth.”
It solves the static physical aggregation problem but does not resurrect the neoclassical single-capital theory; instead, it confirms the ontological rupture between macro and micro.

6
The Inevitability of Statistical Identity — A Dialogue with Shaikh

If the Cambridge debate revealed the logical cracks in the production function, then Anwar Shaikh’s critique attempts to destroy its empirical foundation. Shaikh (1974) proposed the famous ”humbug” hypothesis, using numerical simulations to show that even in an economy with completely chaotic technology and no marginal productivity law whatsoever, as long as the data satisfies certain accounting identities, the fitted production function will still exhibit a perfect Cobb-Douglas form. This critique, like pulling the rug from underfoot, renders all empirical defenses based on high
R^{2}
pale.

6.1
From ”Algebraic Disguise” to ”Statistical Necessity”

Shaikh’s core argument is based on the national income accounting identity:
Y\equiv wL+rK
. He proves that as long as the wage share
wL/Y
and capital share
rK/Y
remain relatively stable over the sample period, regression analysis on
Y,L,K
will necessarily yield a Cobb-Douglas function with exponents close to these shares. In other words, the so-called production function estimation is merely a complex tautological expression of the identity itself.

This axiomatic system fully accepts Shaikh’s argument and attempts to answer a question Shaikh did not explore in depth:
Why do factor shares remain stable in reality?

Shaikh’s simulation relies on the empirical premise of ”stable shares” without providing a micro-mechanism for their generation. Our model, using the Maximum Entropy Principle, fills this gap, thereby elevating Shaikh’s ”algebraic coincidence” to a
”statistical necessity.”

According to Axiom 3, in an economic system full of heterogeneity and incomplete information, the most probable distribution (i.e., the maximum entropy distribution) of technical coefficients must be a power law. And as we proved in Part III, once micro technologies follow a power law distribution, the macro factor shares
S_{i}=\alpha_{i}/(\sum\alpha+1)
obtained through market competition aggregation are strictly constant, depending only on the shape parameters of the distribution.
This means that the share stability observed by Shaikh is not accidental but a statistical steady-state characteristic of complex system evolution. Therefore, the Cobb-Douglas form is indeed a ”disguise” of the identity, but this disguise is a necessary, not accidental, manifestation of the system in its maximum entropy state.

6.2
Complete Demystification of ”Technological Determinism”

Mainstream economics often interprets the good fit of the production function as evidence for ”technological determinism,” i.e., that economic growth is driven by specific technical parameters (e.g.,
\alpha\approx 0.3
). Shaikh’s critique aims to shatter this illusion, and our model provides micro-mechanistic support for this demystification.

In our framework, the macro exponent
\gamma
(i.e., the
\alpha
in empirical regressions) is determined by the micro distribution parameter
\alpha
. This means that when an economist regresses a stable production function, they are capturing not a physics-like technical constant (e.g., the gravitational constant) but a
social-statistical distribution characteristic
.

Consider an extreme counterfactual experiment: if government policies drastically change the rules of technology diffusion, or changes in union power reshape wage bargaining structures, causing the shape parameter
\alpha
of the micro technology distribution to shift. In that case, although the underlying physical technology (how machines operate) remains unchanged, the observed ”production function exponent” at the macro level would change accordingly.
This strongly supports Shaikh’s point: production function parameters lack ”structural invariance.” They are not exogenous iron laws of technology but endogenous institutional and statistical outcomes.

6.3
The Cobb-Douglas Function as a ”Null Hypothesis”

Shaikh once joked that the Cobb-Douglas function is the ”panacea of economics.” Based on the findings of this model, we can reformulate this view in more rigorous statistical terms:
The Cobb-Douglas function should be regarded as the ”null hypothesis” in macroeconomic analysis.

In statistical physics, the maximum entropy distribution represents the ”natural state” of a system without additional constraints. Similarly, in economics, if we observe macro data conforming to the Cobb-Douglas form, it does not mean we have verified some special neoclassical growth theory but merely that we have verified the economic system is in a statistical equilibrium state of
”minimum information, maximum randomness.”

Only when data significantly
deviates
from the Cobb-Douglas form (e.g., showing significant CES characteristics) does it suggest the presence of some powerful constraint within the system (e.g., drastic institutional change, technological blockade, or disequilibrium shock) forcing the technology distribution away from the natural maximum entropy state.
Thus, Shaikh’s critique is elevated within our framework: the ubiquity of the Cobb-Douglas function is not because it is profoundly correct but because it represents the most featureless, least informative statistical baseline. Treating it as a profound economic law precisely confuses ”signal” with ”noise.”

In summary, the dialogue with Shaikh ultimately leads us to an epistemological turning point: the task of macroeconometrics should not be to ”discover” the production function (because that is merely the statistical projection of an identity) but to investigate the institutional and historical forces behind the parameters (i.e., the distributional form) that determine the identity.

7
Conclusion and Outlook

A core task of macroeconomics is to build a bridge between micro complexity and macro regularity. For a long time, the Cobb-Douglas production function, as the keystone of this bridge, has stood firm empirically while teetering over an abyss of theoretical foundations. This paper attempts, by introducing the Maximum Entropy Principle from statistical physics combined with the intrinsic scale invariance of the system, to construct an axiomatic micro-foundation, providing an explanation for this ancient and mysterious macro regularity while also critically reconstructing its ontological status.

The theoretical model of this paper shows that macro characteristics such as constant factor shares and constant returns to scale do not originate from micro firms universally adopting a specific technical blueprint but are
statistical necessities
of heterogeneous technology distributions under information constraints. When the micro system satisfies the axiom of scale invariance, the Maximum Entropy Principle drives technical coefficients to exhibit a truncated power law distribution; and market competition, as a screening mechanism, transforms this micro disorder into macro order, naturally giving rise to a Cobb-Douglas aggregate function.

This finding has dual significance. On a
constructive
level, it provides a solid micro-foundation for neoclassical growth models that does not rely on a ”representative agent” or ”value aggregation of capital,” solving part of the logical puzzle of the Cambridge Capital Controversy. On a
critical
level, it reveals that the aggregate production function is essentially a
lossy compression
of micro information. In this compression process, micro-level technological heterogeneity, extensive margin adjustments of market entry and exit, and the social-historical relations embedded in distribution parameters are all ”smoothed” into seemingly natural marginal productivity laws.

Therefore, this paper supports and deepens Shaikh’s ”humbug” hypothesis and Marx’s ”fetishism” critique. We prove that the so-called ”technical law” is actually an algebraic manifestation of a social statistical regularity; the so-called ”factor contribution” is actually the institutionalized distribution of scarcity rent. The aggregate production function is not only a tool of economic analysis but also a carrier of ideology—it disguises the social production relations of a specific historical stage as eternal, unchanging natural technical laws.

Of course, as a highly abstract theoretical attempt, this model has limitations. We assume a static distribution form and do not consider the dynamic complexity of reswitching; we assume the exogeneity of parameters
\alpha
and do not endogenize the feedback mechanisms of technological progress and class struggle on distribution shape. Future research could attempt to introduce stochastic processes to dynamically evolve distribution parameters.

In summary, the research paradigm of economics can move beyond excessive pursuit of determinism and reductionism toward an emergentist perspective closer to real-world complexity. Traditional neoclassical theory often takes the rational optimization of micro individuals as its ontological basis, attempting to derive macro regularities through aggregation. However, the analysis in this paper shows that the aggregate production function is not a simple scaling up of micro behavior but a statistical result
emerging
from the interaction of heterogeneous agents under institutional constraints and market selection. The stability presented by the Cobb-Douglas function does not stem from some deep technical constant but from the distributional characteristics naturally formed by a complex system under specific constraints.

Therefore, the understanding of the aggregate production function should shift from ontology to emergence, i.e., no longer viewing it as a direct and faithful reflection of micro production technology but as a macro approximation that is statistically stable yet conceals micro heterogeneity and the structure of social relations. This shift in perspective helps us morely recognize the conditions behind economic regularities and the historicity of economic laws, thereby maintaining necessary caution and openness in theory and policy.

Appendix A
Mathematical Derivation Details

This appendix details the derivation of the technical coefficient distribution (Maximum Entropy Principle) and the calculation of macro aggregation integrals (application of Beta functions) from the main text.

A.1
Variational Derivation of the Maximum Entropy Distribution

We seek the joint probability density function
p(a_{1},a_{2})
that maximizes the Shannon entropy over the domain
\mathcal{D}=\{(a_{1},a_{2})|0<a_{i}\leq A_{i}^{\max}\}
:

H[p]=-\iint_{\mathcal{D}}p(a_{1},a_{2})\ln p(a_{1},a_{2})\,da_{1}da_{2}

subject to the following constraints:

1.

Normalization:
\iint_{\mathcal{D}}p\,d\mathbf{a}=1

2.

Logarithmic mean constraints (scale invariance):
\iint_{\mathcal{D}}p\ln a_{i}\,d\mathbf{a}=\mu_{i},\quad i=1,2

Construct the Lagrangian functional
\mathcal{L}[p]
:

\mathcal{L}=-\iint p\ln p\,d\mathbf{a}+\lambda_{0}\left(\iint p\,d\mathbf{a}-1\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\lambda_{i}\left(\iint p\ln a_{i}\,d\mathbf{a}-\mu_{i}\right)

Taking the variational derivative with respect to
p
and setting it to zero:

\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta p}=-(\ln p+1)+\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}\ln a_{1}+\lambda_{2}\ln a_{2}=0

Solving:

\ln p=(\lambda_{0}-1)+\ln(a_{1}^{\lambda_{1}})+\ln(a_{2}^{\lambda_{2}})

That is:

p(a_{1},a_{2})=e^{\lambda_{0}-1}a_{1}^{\lambda_{1}}a_{2}^{\lambda_{2}}

Let
C=e^{\lambda_{0}-1}
be the normalization constant, and
\alpha_{i}=\lambda_{i}+1
. Determine constant
C
via the normalization condition:

1=C\int_{0}^{A_{1}^{\max}}a_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}\,da_{1}\int_{0}^{A_{2}^{\max}}a_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-1}\,da_{2}

Computing the definite integrals (assuming
\alpha_{i}>0
for convergence):

\int_{0}^{A_{i}^{\max}}a_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}\,da_{i}=\frac{(A_{i}^{\max})^{\alpha_{i}}}{\alpha_{i}}

Substituting back:

C=\frac{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}{(A_{1}^{\max})^{\alpha_{1}}(A_{2}^{\max})^{\alpha_{2}}}

Therefore, the maximum entropy distribution is a truncated power law distribution:

p(a_{1},a_{2})=\frac{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}{(A_{1}^{\max})^{\alpha_{1}}(A_{2}^{\max})^{\alpha_{2}}}a_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}a_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-1}

(11)

A.2
Macro Aggregation and Beta Function Integrals

A.2.1
Integration Region and Variable Substitution

Macro average output
y=Y/K
is defined as:

y=\iint_{\mathcal{D}(p)}p(a_{1},a_{2})\,da_{1}da_{2}

where the integration region (under effective constraint equilibrium) is the simplex:

\mathcal{D}(p)=\{(a_{1},a_{2}):a_{1}\geq 0,a_{2}\geq 0,a_{1}p_{1}+a_{2}p_{2}\leq 1\}

Perform variable substitution: let
t_{1}=a_{1}p_{1}
,
t_{2}=a_{2}p_{2}
. Then
a_{i}=t_{i}/p_{i}
, and the Jacobian determinant is
J=\frac{\partial(a_{1},a_{2})}{\partial(t_{1},t_{2})}=\frac{1}{p_{1}p_{2}}
.
The new integration region becomes the standard simplex:
\Delta=\{(t_{1},t_{2}):t_{i}\geq 0,t_{1}+t_{2}\leq 1\}
.

A.2.2
Calculation of Average Output

Substituting the distribution function:

y=C\iint_{\Delta}\left(\frac{t_{1}}{p_{1}}\right)^{\alpha_{1}-1}\left(\frac{t_{2}}{p_{2}}\right)^{\alpha_{2}-1}\frac{1}{p_{1}p_{2}}\,dt_{1}dt_{2}

Rearranging constant and price terms:

y=C\cdot p_{1}^{-\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{-\alpha_{2}}\iint_{\Delta}t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}t_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-1}\,dt_{1}dt_{2}

Computing the double integral:

I=\int_{0}^{1}t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}\left[\int_{0}^{1-t_{1}}t_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-1}dt_{2}\right]dt_{1}

Inner integral:

\int_{0}^{1-t_{1}}t_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-1}dt_{2}=\frac{(1-t_{1})^{\alpha_{2}}}{\alpha_{2}}

Outer integral (using the Beta function definition
B(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}u^{x-1}(1-u)^{y-1}du
):

I=\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}\int_{0}^{1}t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}(1-t_{1})^{\alpha_{2}}dt_{1}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}B(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}+1)

Using Beta function property
B(x,y+1)=\frac{y}{x+y}B(x,y)
yields a more symmetric form (optional, for reference).

A.2.3
Calculation of Average Factor Demands

For the demand for the first factor
x_{1}
:

x_{1}=\iint_{\mathcal{D}(p)}a_{1}p(a_{1},a_{2})\,da_{1}da_{2}

After substitution, the integrand gains an extra factor
a_{1}=t_{1}/p_{1}
:

x_{1}=C\iint_{\Delta}\frac{t_{1}}{p_{1}}\left(\frac{t_{1}}{p_{1}}\right)^{\alpha_{1}-1}\left(\frac{t_{2}}{p_{2}}\right)^{\alpha_{2}-1}\frac{1}{p_{1}p_{2}}\,dt_{1}dt_{2}

Rearranging:

x_{1}=C\cdot p_{1}^{-(\alpha_{1}+1)}p_{2}^{-\alpha_{2}}\iint_{\Delta}t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}t_{2}^{\alpha_{2}-1}\,dt_{1}dt_{2}

Note the exponent of
t_{1}
becomes
\alpha_{1}
. Repeating the integration process:

I_{x1}=\int_{0}^{1}t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\frac{(1-t_{1})^{\alpha_{2}}}{\alpha_{2}}dt_{1}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}B(\alpha_{1}+1,\alpha_{2}+1)

A.3
Proof of Constant Factor Shares

Compute the income share of the first factor
S_{1}=\frac{p_{1}x_{1}}{y}
.
Substituting the previous results, the price terms
p_{1}^{-\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{-\alpha_{2}}
and constant
C
cancel between numerator and denominator, leaving only the ratio of the integrals:

S_{1}=\frac{p_{1}\cdot Cp_{1}^{-(\alpha_{1}+1)}p_{2}^{-\alpha_{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}B(\alpha_{1}+1,\alpha_{2}+1)}{Cp_{1}^{-\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{-\alpha_{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}B(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}+1)}

Simplifying:

S_{1}=\frac{B(\alpha_{1}+1,\alpha_{2}+1)}{B(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}+1)}

Using the Beta function recurrence property
B(x+1,y)=\frac{x}{x+y}B(x,y)
, with
x=\alpha_{1},y=\alpha_{2}+1
, yields:

S_{1}=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}+(\alpha_{2}+1)}=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1}

Similarly:

S_{2}=\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1}

Since
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
are distribution constants,
S_{1},S_{2}
are constants, independent of prices. Q.E.D.
