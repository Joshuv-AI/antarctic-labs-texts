Letter regarding “Thigh muscle pump”
Authors: Massimo Cappelli, Raffaele Molino Lova, Mauro Pinelli, Maurizio Lombardi, Claude Franceschi
Published: 2026

In the aforementioned paper, the authors reiterate the concept, already expressed in a previous paper,1 that during ambulation, there is no anterograde flow within the greater saphenous vein (GSV), and that the emptying of the superficial lower limb veins occurs into the deep lower limb veins through the perforator veins in the toe-off phase of the stride cycle.

However, the paper has several critical issues:

(1)The catheters used in the study have a tip opening, and, being oriented from the knee to the groin, in the case of a possible GSV anterograde flow, the pressure measured, (ie, the total pressure) decreases during the flow, as it results from the algebraic sum of the lateral pressure and the dynamic pressure, which in this case has a negative value;

(2)External transducers, compared with catheter-tip transducers, show a remarkably higher inertia and, as a consequence, a remarkably lower frequency response, along with a huge number of artifacts, due to the movement or to air bubbles in the circuit, and a more marked damping effect. Accordingly, external transducers are probably not suitable to accurately detect the quickly repeating venous pressure changes during ambulation;

(3)Authors take for granted that the presence of a gradient between the GSV and the intramuscular vein necessarily entails the presence of a flow directed from the GSV to the intramuscular vein, which is not true. In fact, a gradient can generate a flow only in the presence of hemodynamically active physical connections between the two veins, specifically large-caliber perforator veins. On the contrary, in normal conditions, subjects show just a few perforator veins and with a very small caliber, as perforator veins play the role of a compensatory mechanism for the superficial lower limb veins (eg, when they are overloaded by the saphenous femoral junction incompetence or in physiological conditions, such as thermoregulation and prolonged physical exercise);

(4)Our personal observations using the B-flow ultrasound technology during treadmill ambulation have shown, qualitatively, a GSV maximum flow peak during the heel strike phase and during the very beginning of the toe-off phase of the stride cycle. Although treadmill ambulation is not perfectly comparable to physiological walking, as the progression is due to the treadmill movement and not to the contraction of hip extension muscles (mainly the gluteus muscles), this observation is a direct demonstration of the presence of an anterograde flow in the GSV even during ambulation and not only during dynamic tests. Further, after the surgical closure of the saphenous femoral junction, it is a quite common finding to detect the appearance of thigh perforator veins showing an anterograde flow, which is an indirect demonstration of the presence of an anterograde GSV flow, responsible for the increase in the GSV systolic pressure that results in the dilatation of the perforator veins;

(5)GSVs in healthy subjects are small and particularly reactive, due to the higher number of smooth muscular cells and to the richer sympathetic innervation; however, in the paper, there is no mention of whether the GSV was checked for caliber changes due to spasm before and after the introduction of the 18-gauge venous catheters.
