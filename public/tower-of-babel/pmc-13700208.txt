Prenatal COVID-19 pandemic-related traumatic stress and risk of child neurodevelopmental disorders: a cohort study
Authors: Luke P Grosvenor, Meredith Anderson, Yinge Qian, Stacey E Alexeeff, Jennifer L Ames, Paul Ashwood, Kathryn K Ridout, Robert Yolken, Judith Van de Water, Lisa A Croen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic was a prolonged period of traumatic stress with potentially lasting health impacts, especially for vulnerable groups including pregnant individuals. PTS was measured using the 10-item PTS Scale and modeled as a continuous total symptom score (range: 0–10) and binary exposure (high, ≥ 9; low, < 9). We prospectively followed children through July 2025 and linked to sociodemographic and medical characteristics, including NDD diagnoses, in KPNC electronic records. Background

The first three years of the COVID-19 pandemic represented a prolonged period of traumatic stress with potentially lasting health impacts. Adults in the U.S. experienced significant increases in symptoms of anxiety and depression [1], and vulnerable populations including pregnant women were at particularly increased risk for psychosocial distress [2, 3]. Exposure to SARS-CoV-2 infection in utero has been linked to a range of pregnancy and birth outcomes including increased risk of pre-term birth [4, 5], low birth weight [5, 6], lower APGAR scores [4, 6], and neurodevelopmental disorder (NDD) diagnoses [7–14]. One major shortcoming of studies of the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on child outcomes has been the limited duration of follow up—only one has followed children beyond 37 months of age [14], and the majority examined outcomes in children under 18 months of age, far below the median age of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) diagnosis in the U.S [15]. In addition, very little is known about child outcomes following prenatal exposure to pandemic-related changes other than SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Stress related to adverse life events experienced during pregnancy has been associated with greater severity of autism-related traits and greater risk of NDD diagnoses [16–19]. For example, higher number of cumulative adverse events was associated with more severe social communication impairments at age 6 years [16] and children born to mothers who experienced severe natural disasters during pregnancy were more likely to be diagnosed with ASD [17–19]. Pandemic-related traumatic stress (PTS) is a psychosocial exposure comprising stress reactions above and beyond the general psychological distress experienced with pandemic-related disruptions to daily life, such as school and work closures [20]. Given prenatal stress related to adverse events has been linked to a range of child neurodevelopmental outcomes, exposure to PTS during pregnancy could plausibly be associated with greater risk of NDD diagnoses. Whether associations of prenatal PTS and NDD may be independent of postnatal factors associated with altered child neurodevelopment (e.g., postpartum depression [21, 22], breastfeeding duration [23]) should also be considered.

Fetal sex may play an important role in moderating associations between prenatal exposures and risk of child NDD given the strong evidence for a male-to-female ASD prevalence ratio of approximately four-to-one [15]. Prenatal psychological stressors have been shown to influence pregnancy and neonatal outcomes in male and females differently [24], and elevated prenatal perceived stress has been associated with greater severity of motor and language deficits among male children only [25, 26]. Sex differences in risk of NDD diagnosis have also been demonstrated for prenatal exposures with potentially shared mechanisms including obesity, asthma, and gestational diabetes [27, 28] as well as SARS-CoV-2 infection [11]. Several prior studies of prenatal stress and child neurodevelopmental outcomes have not conducted or reported on sex-stratified analyses [16–19, 29, 30], likely due to small sample sizes and limited statistical power.

The objective of this study was to examine associations between prenatal PTS and risk of NDD diagnosis in children up to 5 years of age, and to evaluate effect modification by child sex. We hypothesized that higher prenatal pandemic-related traumatic stress would be associated with greater risk of child NDD diagnoses and that associations would differ by child sex.

Methods

Study setting and population

We conducted a survey among members of the Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC) integrated healthcare delivery system who were pregnant and ≥ 12 weeks gestation at the time the survey was collected, between June 2020 and September 2022. Eligible participants were identified by a positive pregnancy test result or scheduled prenatal visit documented in the electronic health record (EHR) [31]. We included mothers in this study who completed all 10 questionnaire items of the PTS Scale [32] and their children from single-gestation births who survived to at least age 3 months and were prospectively followed through June 2025. The KPNC Institutional Review Board approved all study procedures and participants provided electronic informed consent prior to participation. This study followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) reporting guidelines for cohort studies.

Data collection

The 26-question KPNC COVID-19 Pregnancy Survey was administered online and asked participants about the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on their health, health-related behaviors, and health care (31). The survey was adapted from the NIH Environmental Influences on Children’s Health Outcomes COVID-19 Questionnaire [33]. Data on sociodemographic and medical characteristics were obtained from linkage with the KPNC electronic health record for all survey respondents.

Pandemic-related traumatic stress (PTS)

Prenatal PTS was measured using the 10-item PTS Scale, which was developed with the express purpose of measuring an individual’s response to traumatic stress induced by the COVID-19 pandemic and associated social isolation related to mitigation actions and has been described and validated previously [20, 32] (Additional File 1: Table S1). The PTS Scale measures traumatic stress more generally and over a longer period than “acute” stress, which is defined as a reaction occurring within 1 month after a potentially life-threatening exposure such as the COVID-19 pandemic [20, 32, 34]. Prenatal PTS was measured as both a continuous total symptom score (range: 0–10), with one point counted for each of the 10 items rated as a 3 or greater on a 5-point Likert scale, as well as a binary measure of traumatic stress produced by dichotomizing the PTS total symptom score with scores of ≥ 9 indicating high PTS. We examined both continuous and binary exposures because posttraumatic stress symptoms can cause significant functional impairment regardless of whether they exceed the threshold for an indication of posttraumatic stress disorder [35].

Child neurodevelopmental outcomes

Children were prospectively followed for neurodevelopmental outcomes through June 30, 2025. Outcome groups included children diagnosed with any NDD (including ASD), ASD only, or no neurodevelopmental disorders by the end of the follow-up period. NDD was determined by the presence of at least one diagnosis code in the child’s electronic health record for any of ASD, motor delay, speech delay, learning disorder, other behavioral disorders, cognitive disorders, or cerebral palsy. A complete list of ICD diagnosis codes is presented in Additional File 1: Table S2. ASD was determined by the presence of at least one International Classification of Disease (ICD) diagnosis code in the child record (ICD-10 code F84.x). As standard of care, KPNC implements a universal child developmental screening program using the Developmental Milestones Questionnaire from the validated Survey of Well-Being of Young Children (SWYC) [36] at the 18-month well-child visit and The Parent’s Observation of Social Interactions (POSI) [37] at the 18- and 24-month well-child visit. Children were diagnosed at a KPNC ASD evaluation center by a multidisciplinary team using a standardized protocol, including the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule [38] or by 1 or more developmental behavioral pediatricians, child psychiatrists, pediatric neurologists, or general pediatricians.

Covariates

Maternal sociodemographic and clinical characteristics extracted from EHR included age at delivery (years), race and ethnicity (self-reported as Asian, Black, Hispanic, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, Multiracial, Native American, Unknown, or White), insurance status (government or commercial), pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), pre-pregnancy depression (presence of a diagnosis in the year prior to the start of pregnancy), pregnancy start date (the last menstrual period [LMP] date in the EHR) and gestational age in weeks at completion of the COVID-19 Pregnancy Survey (the difference between the pregnancy start date and date of survey completion). History of SARS-CoV-2 infection (before or during pregnancy) was based on both survey response indicating an infection confirmed or likely confirmed by a healthcare provider, cheek swab, or blood test, as well as on polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection data extracted from the mother’s electronic health records. At KPNC, SARS-CoV-2 PCR testing started in March 2020 but was limited to symptomatic individuals. Testing was expanded to asymptomatic individuals in April 2020, and to all individuals who were admitted for delivery beginning December 1, 2020. Child covariates extracted from electronic records included birth year (2020, 2021, or 2022) and biological sex (male or female). We also calculated the ratio of number of completed well-child visits (extracted from EHR) to duration of follow-up time and compared across exposure groups to account for any differences in care utilization that may influence results.

Statistical analysis

We described maternal and child characteristics overall and across levels of binary PTS exposure using chi-square tests for categorical and t-tests for continuous variables. We fitted separate Cox proportional hazards regression models to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for associations between each PTS exposure (continuous and binary) and each outcome (all NDD and ASD). Outcomes were coded as binary variables indicating the time-to-event (days) for whichever occurred first between a clinical diagnosis in the child record, loss to follow-up, or the end of the follow-up period. Children were followed from 3 months of age until the date of the first NDD diagnosis, the date they left the KPNC health system, death, or end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. The following series of models were run: Crude model, unadjusted; Model 1, adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics (maternal age, race and ethnicity, insurance type, gestational age at survey completion and child birth year and sex); Model 2, additionally adjusted for history of SARS-CoV-2 infection and pre-pregnancy BMI; and Model 3, additionally adjusted for pre-pregnancy depression to account for confounding by potentially co-occurring psychosocial exposures. Variables included as covariates in statistical models were selected based on past literature and associations with exposure and outcomes observed in our sample. To account for censoring, we also estimated the unadjusted cumulative incidence rates of NDD and ASD by 3 years for each value of the PTS score using the Kaplan-Meier method. To contextualize the hazard ratios of NDD and ASD associated with PTS scores, we calculated the cumulative incidence rates and 95% CIs from fully adjusted Cox models using the curve_cont() function in the contsurvplot R package [39] and provided translations of the absolute risk for those with high and low PTS scores. We evaluated effect modification by child sex by performing stratified analyses and testing for two-way interactions between prenatal PTS and child sex and evaluated whether timing of PTS measurement with respect to the start of the pandemic modified associations between PTS and NDD outcomes, using models stratified by year and 6-month intervals of survey completion. We performed subgroup analyses to estimate adjusted associations of prenatal PTS with each of the distinct outcomes included in the composite NDD outcome, where sample sizes permitted. To evaluate whether observed associations between PTS and NDD were independent of postnatal factors, we performed supplemental analyses additionally adjusting for postpartum depression diagnosis (any diagnosis from birth to end of child follow-up) or breastfeeding status (yes/no) at 6 months postpartum, identified from maternal and child health records. We were missing breastfeeding data for 1,364 mother-child pairs (9.8%) and therefore reran Model 3 among the subsample with non-missing breastfeeding data to compare associations with those observed in the full sample prior to additionally adjusting for breastfeeding status. All tests were two-sided and evaluated using a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Results

Sample characteristics

Out of 115,179 pregnant individuals who were invited to participate in the survey between June 2020 and September 2022, 21,376 (18.6%) responded and 13,859 (64.8%) of those had complete exposure and covariate data and were of single gestation births (Additional File: Figure S1). Among this final cohort of 13,859 mother-child pairs, 21.1% of mothers identified as Asian, 3.0% Black, 19.6% Hispanic, 4.6% Multiracial, < 1.0% as Native American or Pacific Islander, 49.0% identified as White, and 1.7% were of Other or Unknown race (Table 1). The mean (SD) PTS total symptom score was 3.9 (2.6) and 722 (5.2%) mothers had high PTS (symptom score ≥ 9) (Additional File 1: Figure S2). Mothers with high PTS were younger at delivery, had a higher mean pre-pregnancy BMI and a greater proportion had pre-pregnancy depression or postpartum depression compared to those with low PTS. The mean (SD) ratio of well-child visits to years of follow-up in the full sample was 6.22 (4.50) and did not differ comparing children born to mothers with low versus high prenatal PTS (low PTS: 6.22 [4.48], high PTS: 6.15 (4.70), P-value = 0.67) (Table 1). Compared to mothers of children without NDD, the mean [SD] PTS score for mothers of children with NDD or ASD was significantly higher (No NDD: 3.8 [2.6], NDD: 4.1 [2.6] ASD: 4.3 [2.8]) and the proportion of those who had high binary PTS was significantly greater (No NDD: 4.9%, NDD: 6.3%, ASD: 7.4%) (Additional File 1: Table S3).

Pandemic-related traumatic stress associations with NDD

Higher continuous PTS score was associated with greater risk of NDD in the fully adjusted model (HR = 1.02 per 1-point increase in PTS score, 95% CI= [1.01–1.04] and HR = 1.11 [1.05–1.22] per 5-point increase) (Table 2). This HR translated to an adjusted cumulative incidence rate (95% CI) of NDD by 3 years of 21.79 (20.43–23.22) for children born to mothers with a PTS score of 8 and 19.88 (19.10–20.70) for those with a PTS score of 3. Similarly, high PTS (score ≥ 9) was associated with greater risk of NDD in all models (HR = 1.18 [1.01–1.37] in fully adjusted Model 3) compared to those with PTS score < 9.

Higher continuous PTS score was also associated with greater risk of ASD after adjusting for all covariates (HR = 1.04 [1.01–1.07] per 1-point increase in PTS and HR = 1.21 [1.04–1.40] per 5-point increase) (Table 2). This HR translated to an adjusted cumulative incidence rate (95% CI) of ASD by 3 years of 6.12 (5.29-7.00) for children born to mothers with a PTS Score of 8 and 5.12 (4.62–5.69) for those with a PTS score of 3. High PTS was associated with greater risk of ASD in crude models (HR = 1.46 [1.09–1.96]), but fully adjusted models did not reach statistical significance (HR = 1.20 [0.89–1.62]) (Table 2). The unadjusted cumulative incidence rates of NDD and ASD by 3 years of follow-up for each value of prenatal PTS score are presented in Additional File: Table S4. Subgroup analyses revealed there were no differences in associations of either continuous or binary PTS compared with each of the other distinct outcomes included in the composite NDD outcome (Additional File 1: Table S5).

In supplemental analyses of postnatal factors, associations between either continuous or binary prenatal PTS and NDD risk were materially identical compared to those from Model 3 after additionally adjusting for postpartum depression (continuous PTS: NDD HR = 1.03 [1.01–1.04], ASD HR = 1.04 [1.01–1.08]; binary PTS: NDD HR = 1.22 [1.04–1.42], ASD HR = 1.23 [0.92–1.66]). Adjusted associations of PTS and NDD risk among the subsample of 12,495 pairs with non-missing breastfeeding data were also materially identical compared to those observed in the full sample and after adjusting for breastfeeding status remained identical to Model 3 for continuous PTS (NDD HR = 1.02 [1.01–1.04]; ASD HR = 1.04 [1.01–1.07]) and attenuated only slightly for binary PTS (NDD HR = 1.17 [1.00-1.37]; ASD HR = 1.19 [0.88–1.60]) (Additional File 1: Table S6).

Effect modification by child sex and exposure measurement timing

There was no evidence for effect modification by child sex; stratified associations between both the PTS symptom score as well as high PTS and child neurodevelopmental outcomes were similar for females and males (NDD: HRFemales= 1.20 [0.92–1.56], HRMales= 1.17 [0.96–1.41]; interaction P-value = 0.78; ASD: HRFemales= 1.31 [0.73–2.34], HRMales= 1.17 [0.82–1.65]; interaction P-value = 0.60) (Table 3) (Fig 1). There were also no differences in associations between either continuous or high PTS and risk of NDD or ASD diagnosis after stratifying by timing of PTS measurement using either 12- or 6-month intervals (Table 4).

Discussion

Among this large cohort of mother-child pairs recruited and followed within an integrated health system, higher prenatal PTS was associated with greater risk of child NDD diagnoses. Small yet significant increases in risk were observed between continuous PTS scores and both NDD and ASD, while high PTS was associated with an almost 20% greater risk of any child NDD diagnosis, after accounting for SARS-CoV-2 infection and pre-pregnancy depression status. There was no evidence for sex differences in these associations, and associations did not differ by timing of PTS measurement with respect to the start of the pandemic.

Comparisons with past findings

Very few studies have examined relationships between pandemic-related exposures other than SARS-CoV-2 infection and child NDD diagnoses [8, 40] and ours is the first to investigate associations with PTS. One previous study linked birth during the first years of the pandemic—regardless of exposure to SARS-CoV-2 infection in utero—to worse ASD screening outcomes in motor and personal–social domains, suggesting factors other than infection such as pandemic-related stress may influence child neurodevelopmental outcomes [8]. However, this study followed infants for developmental outcomes for only 6 months after birth and included a relatively small sample of 272 mother-child pairs. A separate study compared cohorts of 18-month-old infants born in the years before and after the start of the pandemic and reported no differences in the proportion screening positive for ASD risk, irrespective of SARS-CoV-2 infection status [40]. While this suggests prenatal pandemic exposure may not be associated with greater likelihood of ASD, that study only compared rates across time periods, rather than examining associations with a specific pandemic-related exposure such as traumatic stress. The authors also noted their use of ASD screening measures to ascertain outcomes, rather than actual NDD and ASD diagnoses as reported here, as a key limitation and stressed the importance of longer-term monitoring of children born during the pandemic.

Our findings are consistent with those from past studies linking prenatal adverse life events and higher levels of perceived stress (unrelated to the pandemic) to greater severity of autism-related traits and risk of ASD diagnosis [16–19, 26, 41]. The order of magnitude of associations observed in our data (an about 20% greater risk of NDD) is comparable to past studies of prenatal adverse events and perceived stress, for example reporting a 10% greater risk of ASD diagnosis [26] or a 90% greater risk of a child having above-threshold autism-related traits [41]. Our results should also be considered in the context of two recent studies that reported no link between prenatal stress and child neurodevelopmental outcomes [42, 43]. One analysis of over 50,000 mother-child pairs from the Norwegian Mother, Father, and Child Cohort (MoBA) found that associations between a higher number of adverse life events (ALEs) in pregnancy and greater risk of ASD were attenuated and no longer significant among sibling controls, suggesting unmeasured or familial confounding explained associations [42]. In a separate MoBA sample and using a Mendelian randomization approach, the same research group concluded there was no direct causal link between prenatal perceived stress and ASD risk [43]. These studies examined associations with composite, general measures of prenatal perceived stress [42, 43] that fundamentally differ from our study of prenatal PTS. The distribution of other social and environmental stressors likely differs between Norway and Northern California as well as between pandemic and non-pandemic periods and could further explain differences in our findings.

Sex differences and potential mechanisms

Contrary to our hypothesis, we did not observe any differences in associations between prenatal PTS and risk of NDDs by child sex. Sex-stratified associations were of similar magnitude and direction compared to those observed among males and females combined, and stratified estimates were no longer statistically significant most likely because of reduced precision among smaller samples. Two prior studies reported that associations between prenatal stress and neurodevelopmental or cognitive outcomes remained significant only among males after stratifying by sex but did not find a statistically significant difference between males and females [25, 26]. For example, prenatal psychological distress was associated with significantly lower cognitive and motor development scores among males only [25]. Most studies of the relationship between prenatal stress and likelihood or severity of neurodevelopmental outcomes, however, have not reported on potential sex differences [16–19, 29, 30] and further research is warranted.

Consistent with the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease hypothesis [44], stress-induced maternal immune activation (MIA) [45, 46] and associated dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis [47, 48] are possible mechanisms underlying the associations observed in our sample. MIA can be induced by non-infectious stimuli including prenatal stress, which has been linked to higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines [49]. This could impact fetal neurodevelopment by down-regulating placental enzymes essential for protecting the fetus from harmful levels of maternal glucocorticoids such as cortisol [50], which have been consistently linked to elevated general perceived and pandemic-related prenatal stress [51, 52]. However, recent studies found no evidence for direct effects of higher cortisol levels on infant birthweight and child cognitive outcomes [43] and suggested the intrauterine environment may not play a causal role in associations between prenatal stress measured by elevated cortisol levels and child outcomes [53]. These contrasting findings could be accounted for by differences in types of stressors examined across these studies. Additional research is needed to better characterize the effects of prenatal stressors by type and severity, including traumatic stress related to future public health emergencies such as the COVID-19 pandemic.

PTS and co-occurring psychosocial exposures

Incidence of anxiety and depression increased due to the COVID-19 pandemic [33, 54] and it is therefore possible that unmeasured maternal psychosocial exposures may have influenced our findings. Disentangling contributions of prenatal stress and depressive symptoms presents a considerable challenge, as it is not always clear the extent to which co-occurring conditions or symptoms represent distinct constructs. To account for potential confounding by closely related psychosocial exposures, we controlled for pre-pregnancy depression diagnosed in the year preceding pregnancy onset and found prenatal PTS remained significantly associated with greater risk of NDD. Notably, continuous PTS scores have demonstrated low to moderate correlations with continuous measures of depressive and anxiety symptoms, suggesting prenatal PTS represents something related to, but distinct from, acute stress or increases in depression and anxiety resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic [20].

Strengths and limitations

Few research settings were capable of conducting large-scale studies of the intergenerational health impacts of COVID-19 pandemic. We enabled prospective follow-up for neurodevelopmental outcomes among a large sample of children by rapidly surveying a cohort of sociodemographically diverse pregnant women within KPNC within just weeks of the start of the pandemic. Prenatal PTS was measured using a well-validated, psychometrically robust measure [20]. Our well-characterized sample includes outcomes ascertained from a setting of universal early developmental screening and diagnoses made with gold-standard instruments rather than screening or self-report measures and is generally representative of the insured population of Northern California [55]. The large sample provided sufficient power to study ASD as a separate outcome from NDD broadly, though statistical power was comparatively limited for this subgroup.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. Selection bias may have been introduced by studying only mothers who chose to take the survey and had complete exposure data, and may have impacted generalizability. A previous analysis of these data found respondents compared to non-respondents were more likely to be White, older, live in lower deprivation neighborhoods, and did not differ by history of SARS-CoV-2 infection [31]. The median age of children at the end of follow up was 3.2 years. This is comparable to the median age of ASD diagnosis in the California region reported by the CDC Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring Network [15] and misclassification of children yet to be diagnosed would have biased our observed associations toward the null. We did not examine risk of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) because most ADHD diagnoses are made after 6 years of age [56] and children in our sample were followed up to 5 years. Future studies should examine risk for this and other neurodevelopmental or behavioral outcomes. We were not able to examine the potential mediating or modifying role of postnatal lifestyle factors (e.g., parenting style) and it is worth noting that postnatal factors such as postpartum depression and breastfeeding occur after the prenatal PTS exposure and could potentially lie on a causal pathway with NDD. The supplemental analyses may therefore be over adjusted, and the results are presented primarily to evaluate robustness of the observed associations. We only measured PTS at one time in pregnancy and were unable to determine when exactly during pregnancy the PTS occurred or was most severe, since the PTS Scale was developed to evaluate ongoing traumatic stress reactions without defined time bounds. Given the lack of universal screening and testing for SARS-CoV-2 infection, we may have under-ascertained maternal infection. We are not able to make any inferences regarding causality given the observational nature of our study and results could be influenced by unmeasured confounders, for example prenatal perceived discrimination or shared familial genetic or environmental factors. Finally, the KPNC population is representative only of Northern California and findings may not generalize to areas in the U.S. with different sociodemographic compositions.

Conclusions

In this large prospective cohort study of mothers and their children born in the first three years of the pandemic, higher prenatal PTS was associated with greater risk of NDD. Behavioral interventions for event-related prenatal traumatic stress could therefore be explored as a means to improve neurodevelopmental and behavioral outcomes in children. Associations did not vary by child sex or by timing of traumatic stress measurement during the pandemic. Continued follow-up of children born in the early years of the COVID-19 pandemic, including studies accounting for potential confounding by shared familial or environmental factors, is required to understand the intergenerational health impacts of pandemic-related exposures on neurodevelopmental outcomes.
