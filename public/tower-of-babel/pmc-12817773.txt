Exploring nurse–patient mutuality in inflammatory bowel disease: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Daniele Napolitano, Silvia Cilluffo, Alessia Marini, Maura Lusignani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), including Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), require long-term, multidisciplinary care. Within this model, the nurse–patient relationship is essential, yet little is known about mutuality—defined as shared trust, collaboration, and reciprocity. This study assessed nurse–patient mutuality in IBD using the Nurse–Patient Mutuality in Chronic Illness (NPM-CI) scale.MethodsA multicenter, cross-sectional study was conducted in Italian IBD centers. Adult outpatients with confirmed IBD completed the NPM-CI patient version, a 20-item validated tool assessing three dimensions: developing and going beyond, being a point of reference, and deciding and sharing care. Descriptive statistics were used.ResultsAmong 453 patients, 449 (94%) completed the NPM-CI. The mean total score was high (81.2 ± 13.3). UC patients reported higher scores than CD patients. The highest-rated items concerned respect for privacy and attention to well-being, while lower scores reflected limited emotional sharing and clarification of information from other professionals.ConclusionsThis is the first study to evaluate nurse–patient mutuality in IBD using a validated tool. Findings highlight the central role of IBD nurses in building therapeutic relationships. Enhancing emotional communication and shared decision-making could further strengthen mutuality and improve care outcomes.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), which primarily include ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), are chronic conditions characterized by persistent inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract [1]. These diseases typically present with symptoms such as diarrhoea, abdominal pain, anaemia, and fatigue during active phases. The clinical course of IBD is often progressive and marked by unpredictable relapses and remissions [2, 3].

In the last two decades, newly industrialized countries in Asia, Latin America, and the Middle East have reported a rapid increase in IBD incidence, with rates in some areas exceeding 10 cases per 100,000 persons per year. This trend is associated with westernization-related factors such as urbanization, dietary changes, and increased antibiotic exposure. By contrast, in Europe and North America, where incidence had risen sharply in the 20th century, recent data show stabilizing or even declining trends. However, prevalence remains high, exceeding 0.3% of the population in many countries [2, 4].

IBD conditions due to a relapsing-remitting disease course led patients to long courses of treatment with pharmacological and surgical approaches [5, 6]. A multidisciplinary approach is necessary for these patients, as they require a care pathway shared among different healthcare professionals [7, 8]. A personalized, individualised care path tailored to the patient’s specific objectives is necessary; therefore, an effective relationship is required [9, 10].

The concept of mutuality has been studied over the past three decades [11] and described as a relationship between individuals based on reciprocity [12], trust [13] and sharing [14]. This dyadic approach can be contextualised in a care between patient-nurse or a patient and a caregiver [15–20].

IBD represents a particularly suitable setting for exploring mutuality due to its complex and fluctuating course. Patients often face uncertainty, emotional burden, and repeated interactions with healthcare providers, making relational continuity and shared decision-making essential elements of care. Unlike acute or episodic conditions, IBD management spans long periods and includes multidisciplinary support, where the nurse often becomes a stable point of reference [21, 22].

Within this framework, the development of mutuality between nurse and patient may enhance communication, trust, and engagement, supporting not only adherence to treatment but also emotional coping. Despite its relevance, mutuality has not been extensively studied in the context of IBD. Addressing this gap, our study aims to evaluate nurse–patient mutuality in individuals with IBD, applying a validated tool grounded in a conceptual framework developed by Cilluffo et al. (2022), which defines mutuality as a multidimensional process encompassing “developing and going beyond,” “being a point of reference,” and “deciding and sharing care” [17].

Recently, it has been suggested that mutuality affects certain patient outcomes, such as self-care [23]. The complexity of the IBD care pathway, along with the rising incidence rates of these diseases, confronts healthcare providers with the need to develop a multidisciplinary, individualised care approach for each patient [24, 25]. Within this multidisciplinary framework, the role of the IBD nurse has become increasingly central. As an advanced practice nurse with specific expertise in IBD, the nurse coordinates care, supports patient education, promotes treatment adherence, and acts as a primary point of contact across different stages of the disease trajectory. This professional figure plays a key role in identifying patient needs, preventing complications, and providing emotional and relational support, thus facilitating patient engagement and continuity of care [21, 26]. Through regular and person-centred interactions, the IBD nurse can foster a therapeutic relationship based on trust and mutual understanding, which is especially important in the context of chronic, fluctuating diseases such as IBD [27]. Studies have been done on mutuality between nurse and patient in multiple chronic conditions, but are absent in the IBD context [23].

Aim of the study

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate nurse-patient mutuality in patients with IBD.

Methods

Design

A multicentre, cross-sectional, observational study was conducted on a cohort of outpatients diagnosed with IBD [28]. The study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [29].

Settings and sample

Patient sampling was recruited from IBD units distributed across Italy. Specifically, nine IBD Units participated in the study, covering Northern (3), Central (3), and Southern Italy (3). All participating centers are tertiary referral units with established multidisciplinary teams including gastroenterologists, surgeons, and specialized IBD nurses. This distribution ensures geographic representativeness of the Italian IBD population and reflects different organizational models of care. To be eligible for this study, patients had to be over 18 years of age, have a diagnosis of IBD and be able to understand both written and spoken Italian. Additionally, these patients were required to have received care at clinics willing to participate in the study. Patients who had received a diagnosis of IBD within the 12 months before the study and those who had undergone IBD-related surgery within the 6 months preceding the study’s initiation were excluded [30]. Individuals with another severely symptomatic chronic illness and any psychiatric disorders were also excluded from the study.

Instruments

Data were collected using a self-administered instrument, the Nurse-Patient Mutuality in Chronic Illness (NPM-CI) scale [31], in its patient version, along with a questionnaire that captured socio-demographic and clinical characteristics. Patient variables included age, gender, body mass index (BMI), education level, occupational status, type of pathology, years since IBD diagnosis, biological therapy, frequency of urgent care visits in the past year, weekly frequency of visits to the IBD center, and years of follow-up at the reference center.

The authors propose that the NPM-CI scale can effectively measure the mutuality between nurses and patients, thereby enhancing the understanding of the mutuality process, as emphasized in the conceptual framework by Cilluffo et al. and includes the three dimensions of mutuality developing and going beyond, point of reference, and deciding and sharing care [17].

The items assess aspects of mutuality, that is, a mutual relationship between the patient and nurse, based on trust, respect, and attention in care. Furthermore, through this relationship, proper therapeutic education can occur through personalised patient information and care, leading the patient to make informed decisions about their chronic condition.

The NPM-CI scale, which has been developed and validated in its Italian version, consists of 20 items with a 5-point Likert scale and has two mirror versions, one for patients and one for nurses [31]. The NPM-CI scale is a brief and easy-to-use tool. Both versions of the instrument demonstrated three dimensions of the framework through Exploratory Factor Analysis. In the dimension “developing and going beyond”, are 4 items included, in the dimension “point of reference”, there are 7 items, and in “deciding and sharing care”, there are 9 items. The patient version demonstrated internal consistency, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, with a value of 0.90 for the total scale. Additionally, the test-retest reliability was assessed via the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), yielding a value of 0.97 (95% CI 0.91–0.99; p < 0.001).

Data collection

Nurses administered the questionnaires during outpatient visits. Data were collected between April and June 2024. Participants were selected consecutively, in the order of their access to the identified setting. After being informed about the study and signing consent, data were collected to evaluate the sampling criteria. The sample was then given the questionnaires to complete, and the administrators remained available for any questions or doubts. The administration was conducted in dedicated rooms, which allowed for privacy during compilation and provided participants with the opportunity to ask questions if they had difficulty understanding the items. The administration required about 10 min for each participant.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed with descriptive statistics, mean and standard deviations (M ± SD) or medians and quartiles for quantitative variables, and frequencies and percentages for qualitative data. Skewness and kurtosis for each item and total score were assessed, with skewness < 2 and kurtosis < 7 (29). Normality testing of the study variables was conducted using the Shapiro Wilk test (p > 0.01). Data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) v. 29.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Socio-demographical and clinical characteristics

A total of 453 patients were enrolled in the study, of whom 449 (94%) fully completed the NPM-CI questionnaires. The sample was composed of 51% males, with a mean age of 43.5 ± 16.4 years and a mean BMI of 23.9 ± 4.66. Diagnoses were evenly distributed between CD (N 223, 49.2%) and UC (N 230, 50.8%). Most participants had completed at least high school (N 349, 77.1%) and were employed (N 287, 63.4%). Over half (N 257, 56.7%) had been living with IBD for 1–10 years, and the vast majority (N 417, 92.1%) were receiving biological therapy. In the previous year, 306 (67.5%) had not required any emergency care. Weekly follow-up at the IBD Unit was most reported as twice weekly (N 214, 47.2%), and (N 275, 60.7%) had been followed at their current center for 1–5 years. Results are presented in Table 1.

Mutuality scores

All scores were above 3 on average, with an overall mean of 81.2 (SD 13.3). Male patients had an average score of 80.4 (SD 13.6) on the NPM-CI scale, while female patients averaged 82 (SD 13).

If we divide the average total score of the NPM-CI scale by type of IBD, patients with CD scored an average of 78.9 (SD 14.1), while patients with UC had an average score of 83.5 (SD 12.2).

Dividing the scores of the NPM-CI scale into the three dimensions of mutuality(17), ‘developing and going beyond’ had an average score of 13.1 (SD 2.04), ‘point of reference’ had an average of 35.4 (SD 4.75), and ‘deciding and sharing care’ had an average of 32.7 (SD 8.09).

The item with the highest score was “The nurse considers the patient’s privacy during care,” with a score of 4.74 (SD = 0.620). This also has the value of asymmetry toward the higher lower values (-2.93) and the highest Kurtosis (9.89). Other items with higher scores were “The nurse pay attention to the patient’s well-being during care”, 4.67 (SD 0.631), “The nurse maintains health commitments made with the patient”, 4.54 (SD 0.784), “The nurse expresses trust in you”, 4.51 (SD 0.720) and “You put trust at the basis of the relationship with the nurse”, 4.51 (SD 0.707).

Lower scores were obtained by items as “You ask the nurse for clarification on the information given by other health professionals”, 3.35 (SD 1.27) and “You share emotions with the nurse”, 3.36 (SD 1.26). This last item has symmetry values close to zero (-0.299).

The results of the Shapiro-Wilk test (all with p < 0.001) indicate that the data do not follow a normal distribution for any parameter. Results are illustrated in Table 2.

Discussion

In this study, nurse–patient mutuality was measured in a sample of IBD patients. To our knowledge, this is the first study investigating mutuality in this population. These findings offer novel insights into the relational dimension of nursing care in IBD and contribute to expanding the conceptual and empirical understanding of mutuality in chronic illness management. Our results reveal a high level of perceived mutuality among IBD patients, with an average mean total score of 81.2 out of 100, suggesting a well-established relational bond between patients and IBD nurses across multiple centres. A comparison with previous studies on patients with multiple chronic conditions shows that individuals with a single chronic condition, such as IBD, exhibit higher levels of mutuality [32]. This may be related to the easier management of one single condition compared to multiple ones. Female patients reported higher mutuality scores than male patients, in contrast to previous findings on gender differences [32, 33].

The average age of our sample was lower than that of other populations where the NPM-CI has been administered [32]. High mutuality among older patients has been linked to greater health needs and more frequent interactions with healthcare professionals, which in turn can strengthen the relational bond [32]. The analysis showed consistently high scores across all three dimensions of the NPM-CI: “developing and going beyond” (84.2), “being a point of reference” (85.7), and “deciding and sharing care” (66.1). “Developing and going beyond” reflects the strong presence of reciprocal trust [12, 13], communication, and collaboration within the nurse–patient dyad—the theoretical cornerstones of mutuality. In “being a point of reference,” patients recognized the nurse as a partner and co-participant in their therapeutic journey [17, 34, 35]. Mutuality allows for an equal relationship, preventing patients from feeling subordinate, and paves the way for “deciding and sharing care,” where patients are empowered to choose the therapeutic and educational plan that best suits them [23, 34]. The highest scoring items were related to privacy, attention to well-being, and perceived trust in the nurse–patient relationship, consistent with findings from heart failure and stroke populations [20, 36]. The lowest scores involved emotional sharing and clarification of information provided by other professionals, which may indicate discomfort in addressing emotionally charged issues or lack of opportunity during clinical encounters. This gap mirrors broader evidence that emotional aspects of care are often undervalued compared to biomedical tasks [38]. Differences in scores between UC and CD patients may reflect the more unpredictable course and surgical burden of CD, which can fragment continuity of care [27, 37].

The results of our study align with a growing body of literature highlighting the key role of IBD nurses in enhancing outcomes, satisfaction, and engagement. IBD nurses are now recognized as integral members of multidisciplinary teams, responsible not only for clinical monitoring but also for psychological support, education, and empowerment strategies [38, 39]. Their role extends beyond routine care and includes fostering therapeutic alliances that facilitate adherence and self-management, particularly in patients facing complex regimens [37, 40]. Evidence suggests that nurse-led care models in IBD contribute to reduced emergency admissions, better symptom management, and improved patient knowledge and satisfaction [41], and it is also consistent with the mutuality conceptual framework [17]. A systematic review confirmed that IBD self-management interventions are more effective when coordinated or delivered by trained nurses [42].

Moreover, patients with access to abundant nurse staffing reported higher self-efficacy and fewer limitations in daily life [37]. These findings reinforce that mutuality is not only a relational quality but also a clinical asset mediating measurable outcomes. International studies further demonstrate that IBD patients value continuity and trust as central to their care experience [43].

Our findings indicate that patients rely on IBD nurses as trustworthy providers of personalized care [44–46]. This highlights the importance of IBD nurses in fostering strong relational bonds. As IBD treatment pathways become increasingly complex, nurses are well-positioned to act as relational anchors in multidisciplinary teams [22, 25, 37]. Training in communication, emotional intelligence, and shared decision-making is essential to maintain high levels of mutuality. Lower scores in emotionally intensive aspects suggest a need for targeted interventions, particularly during active disease or care transitions.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. Firstly, it included a large sample of patients across multiple IBD Units in Italy, enhancing the generalizability of findings within the national context. While these results are representative of the Italian IBD care model, broader generalization to other countries should be made cautiously, as healthcare systems, nurse-to-patient ratios, and organizational models of IBD care may differ. Secondly, we used a psychometrically validated instrument, the NPM-CI scale, that allows a structured and theory-based measurement of mutuality. Thirdly, data collection procedures ensured patient privacy and minimized potential response bias, thereby strengthening the reliability of the findings. However, several limitations must be acknowledged.

The cross-sectional design does not allow for causal inferences regarding the impact of mutuality on patient outcomes. Longitudinal studies are needed to explore whether high mutuality predicts improvements in adherence, self-care behaviors, or clinical indicators over time.

Additionally, our study assessed mutuality solely from the patient’s perspective. Including the nurse version of the NPM-CI scale in future research could offer a more comprehensive understanding of the relational dynamic. It is also essential to recognize that cultural and systemic variables—such as nurse-to-patient ratios, regional care models, and institutional support for nursing roles—may influence mutuality. These factors warrant further investigation and could explain differences across settings and patient subgroups.

Future research and clinical implications

Given the recognition of IBD nurse specialists in international guidelines [21, 25], research should explore how investment in nurse staffing influences both efficiency and relational quality. Policy efforts should promote standardized nurse-led pathways and include relational metrics in quality evaluations. Mutuality should also be integrated into professional education, ensuring that IBD nurses are equipped to act as relational anchors within multidisciplinary teams [45].

Conclusion

This study is the first to investigate the nurse-patient mutuality concept in IBD context using the NPM-CI scale. Our findings demonstrate a high level of perceived mutuality across all three dimensions, developing and going beyond, being a point of reference, and deciding and sharing care, highlighting the quality of relational care provided by IBD nurses in Italian outpatient settings. These results confirm that the IBD nurse plays a crucial role in establishing a therapeutic relationship founded on trust, collaboration, and personalized care. The high scores observed reflect the nurses’ strong relational and professional engagement. On the other hand, lower scores in emotionally sensitive items suggest that aspects such as emotional disclosure and communication across healthcare providers may still represent areas of vulnerability that deserve focused interventions. Overall, mutuality emerges as a valuable process indicator in the context of chronic illness care. Given its importance, mutuality should be further explored in longitudinal studies and integrated into professional training and quality evaluation frameworks. Future research should be focused on the association with patient’s, nurse’s and dyadic outcomes. Integrating mutuality into IBD care may lead to a more responsive, patient-centred healthcare model in which the therapeutic alliance becomes a core driver of quality and continuity of care.
