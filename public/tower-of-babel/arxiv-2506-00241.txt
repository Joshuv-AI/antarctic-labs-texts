Designing AI Tools for Clinical Care Teams to Support Serious Illness Conversations with Older Adults in the Emergency Department CCS: Human-centered computing Empirical studies in HCI CCS: Applied computing Health care information systems CCS: Computing methodologies Artificial intelligence
Menglin Zhao, Zhuorui Yong, Ruijia Guan, Kai-Wei Chang, Adrian Haimovich, Kei Ouchi, Timothy Bickmore, Bingsheng Yao, Dakuo Wang, Smit Desai

Abstract
. Serious illness conversations (SICs), discussions between clinical care teams and patients with serious, life-limiting illnesses about their values, goals, and care preferences, are critical for patient-centered care. Without these conversations, patients often receive aggressive interventions that may not align with their goals. Clinical care teams face significant barriers when conducting serious illness conversations with older adult patients in Emergency Department (ED) settings, where most older adult patients lack documented treatment goals. To understand current practices and identify AI support opportunities, we conducted interviews with two domain experts and nine ED clinical care team members. Through thematic analysis, we characterized a four-phase serious illness conversation workflow (identification, preparation, conduction, documentation) and identified key needs and challenges at each stage. Clinical care teams struggle with fragmented EHR data access, time constraints, emotional preparation demands, and documentation burdens. While participants expressed interest in AI tools for information synthesis, conversational support, and automated documentation, they emphasized preserving human connection and clinical autonomy. We present design guidelines for AI tools supporting SIC workflows that fit within existing clinical practices. This work contributes empirical understanding of ED-based serious illness conversations and provides design considerations for AI in high-stakes clinical environments.



1.
Introduction

When patients live with serious illnesses like cancer or cardiovascular disease, the serious illness conversations (SICs) they hold with clinical care teams (i.g, physicians and nurses) become critically important
(
Ouchi et al. 2019
;
Hamidpour et al. 2014
)
. These SICs serve as pivotal moments where clinical care teams work to understand what matters most to patients. Specifically, this refers to patients’ fundamental values, which reflect what matters most to patients in the context of serious illness (e.g., the desire to be present for grandchildren’s milestones
(
Naik et al. 2016
)
), their treatment goals (e.g., pursuing curative treatment versus focusing on comfort care), and their specific care preferences (e.g., preferences about life-sustaining treatments or location of care)
(
Shilling et al. 2024
)
. Rather than simply discussing medical options, well-conducted SICs help patients feel heard, strengthen the therapeutic alliance, and create a foundation for patient-centered care that reflects patients’ own priorities.

The stakes of SICs become particularly acute in Emergency Department (ED) settings, where patients often arrive at an inflection point in their illness trajectory
(
Ouchi et al. 2019
)
. Here, urgent decisions about ICU admission or hospice referral must be made under intense time pressure, yet these choices may fundamentally shape the entire trajectory of a patient’s subsequent care. Because of this urgency, a timely and meaningful SIC in the ED can be essential for helping patients clarify their values and goals when facing potentially life-altering treatment decisions. When done effectively, these conversations not only improve patient satisfaction with their care
(
Ouchi et al. 2017
)
but also enhance the quality of patients’ end-of-life experiences by ensuring the care they receive truly reflects their individual preferences and values
(
Paladino et al. 2023
;
Hachem et al. 2025
;
Geerse et al. 2019
)
.

Older adults aged 65 and above represent both the largest group needing SIC and the population least likely to receive them
(
Wilson et al. 2020
)
. These patients account for up to one-quarter of all ED visits, totaling 156 million visits annually in the U.S.
(
Samaras et al. 2010
;
Pines et al. 2013
)
, yet often arrive with care needs that differ significantly from those of younger patients
(
Aminzadeh and Dalziel 2002
)
. The urgency becomes clear when considering that three-fourths of these patients will die within six months
(
Wilson et al. 2020
)
, making the ED visit a critical moment for understanding their priorities and wishes. Despite this pressing need, up to 99% of older adult patients in the ED lack documented treatment goals and care preferences
(
Ouchi et al. 2017
)
. This documentation gap has significant consequences for patient care. Without understanding what matters most to patients, clinical care teams often default to aggressive interventions (e.g., chemotherapy, ventilation, or intubation

(
Wright et al. 2008
)
) that may not align with patients’ values and preferences. For instance, older adult patients who prioritize comfort may receive intubation in the ICU, which can cause distressing sensations of suffocation
(
Knudsen et al. 2015
)
, contributing to physical and psychological distress during an already challenging time
(
Wright et al. 2008
)
, resulting in a condition that some older adults consider "worse than dying"
(
Rubin et al. 2016
)
. Additionally, patients without documented preferences experience higher rates of anxiety and depression
(
Detering et al. 2010
)
, while receiving treatments that cost nearly double those of less aggressive approaches
(
Teno et al. 2002
)
. These outcomes highlight the importance of developing effective approaches to facilitate SIC in emergency care settings.

Despite the importance of SIC, clinical care teams face significant barriers that prevent meaningful conversations with older adult patients in emergency settings. Research by
Prachanukool et al. 2022
examining 332 older adult patients found that 63% did not experience SIC in the ED, highlighting the gap between need and practice. This gap stems from multiple interconnected challenges that clinical teams encounter in the ED environment. Time constraints represent a primary barrier, as EDs can admit up to 120 patients per day
(
Jones et al. 2008
)
, severely limiting care teams’ capacity to conduct thorough SIC with each patient facing serious illness
(
Adeyemi et al. 2024
)
. Beyond time pressures, many clinical care teams struggle with uncertainty about how to appropriately initiate these sensitive conversations
(
Ouchi et al. 2019
)
, creating hesitation that further reduces the likelihood of SIC occurring. Additionally, prognostic uncertainty compounds these challenges, as clinical care teams often remain uncertain about patients’ conditions and tend toward optimism about patient outcomes
(
Christakis et al. 2000
)
, which can delay recognition of when SIC becomes necessary and hinder timely intervention. Existing frameworks designed to support SIC, such as the Serious Illness Conversation Guide (SICG)
(
Bernacki et al. 2015
)
and the Jumpstart Guide
(
Curtis et al. 2023
)
, offer structured approaches but present their own limitations in the ED context and have proved insufficient
(
Grudzen et al. 2025
)
. These tools require substantial time investment, with SICG conversations averaging over 20 minutes
(
Mandel et al. 2023
)
, and provide static guidance that cannot adapt to the diverse clinical presentations of older adult patients in emergency care. Furthermore, these frameworks focus primarily on supporting clinicians during the conversation itself rather than integrating with the broader clinical workflow, making them poorly suited for the fast-paced, high-workload environment that characterizes emergency departments.

SIC is a form of patient-provider communication.
Beyond the context of SIC, the Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) community has proposed many technical solutions that we may draw inspiration from.
For example,
Ryu et al. 2023
built an interactive system to record patients’ care preferences, and those preferences are then shared with clinical care teams, while
Yang et al. 2024
designed a Large Language Model-powered system to support asynchronous communication between older adults and healthcare providers. However, there is limited research that focuses on exploring technological solutions to support clinical care teams’ workflow of SIC in high-stakes and time-sensitive ED environments. Recognizing this gap, there have been increasing calls from the medical community to leverage AI technologies to support the workflow of SIC. For example,
Manz et al. 2020
showed that AI-generated mortality predictions with behavioral nudges to clinical care teams can improve SIC frequency. Building on this foundation,
Chua et al. 2022
proposed conversational agents (CAs) to collect patients’ information and care preferences before having SIC with clinical care teams to maximize the efficiency of SIC. They further noted that AI can streamline the SIC documentation process
(
Chua et al. 2022
)
, which could save clinical care teams’ time and further increase the efficiency of SIC.

To respond to these calls, we aim to explore specific AI opportunities to better support these critical conversations.
Although prior studies have identified barriers to conducting SIC from the clinical care teams’ perspective
(
Shilling et al. 2024
)
, these findings are often described at a high level and lack the granular, workflow-specific insights necessary for designing effective AI support systems.
To address the gap, our study seeks to explore two research questions (RQs):

(1)

RQ1: What challenges and needs do clinical care teams face in their workflow of conducting serious illness conversations with older adult patients in the emergency department?

(2)

RQ2: What design considerations should inform the development of AI tools to support clinical care teams in their workflow of conducting serious illness conversations?

Given that SIC in the ED is highly contextual and emergent, with no unified evidence-based workflow currently established, we adopted a multi-phase approach to systematically understand both current practices and future opportunities for AI support. We began with formative expert interviews involving two highly skilled clinician-researchers, each with extensive experience in both practicing and studying SIC in emergency settings. These interviews aimed to surface implicit workflow stages, identify variability in current practices, and explore potential AI intervention points within SIC processes. Through this initial phase, we derived a four-step workflow framework (identification, preparation, conduction, and documentation) and developed a series of text-based AI technology probes that served as concrete examples for discussing AI capabilities with practitioners. This foundational understanding informed our development of a structured interview guide for subsequent interviews with clinical care team members working in ED environments. Our interview guide systematically aligned workflow-related questions with each stage and decision point in the SIC process, enabling us to probe practitioners’ specific needs, challenges, and perspectives on where AI might provide meaningful support. During interviews with clinical care team members who had limited familiarity with AI technologies, we utilized our text-based technology probes to elicit their perceptions of AI support and gather insights about potential applications within their SIC workflows. Through this comprehensive approach, we developed design guidelines that address the unique challenges clinical care teams face when conducting SIC in the fast-paced, time-constrained ED environment. This paper makes the following key contributions:

•

A detailed characterization of clinical care teams’ workflow for conducting SICs with older adult patients in emergency departments
, derived from expert interviews and validated through practitioner interviews, revealing four key stages and associated challenges.

•

An empirical analysis of the specific needs, challenges, and opportunities clinical care teams encounter at each stage of the SIC workflow
, based on interviews with nine emergency department practitioners, highlighting critical pain points in time-constrained environments.

•

Design guidelines for AI tools supporting SICs in emergency care
, informed by practitioner feedback on where and how AI can meaningfully augment clinical workflows without disrupting patient care.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Serious Illness Conversations With Older Adult Patients in Emergency Department

Older adult patients with serious illness face a high risk of mortality and often experience reduced quality of daily functioning and overall quality of life
(
Kelley and Bollens-Lund 2018
)
.
According to a survey, three-quarters of older adults aged 65 and older live with at least one type of serious illness
(
Statista Research Department 2022
)
.
For this population, conducting SICs is essential to enable treatment plans that align with patients’ values, goals, and care preferences
(
Houben et al. 2014
;
Silveira et al. 2010
)
.
However, many older adults have never participated in SIC
(
Prachanukool et al. 2022
)
, and up to 99% lack any documented goals or values in their EHR
(
Ouchi et al. 2017
)
.
This combination of low SICs uptake and incomplete documentation can impede patient
-
centered care and increase the frequency and duration of unnecessary hospitalizations
(
Klingler et al. 2016
)
.
Therefore, there is a pressing need to conduct SICs.

Conducting SICs with older adults in the ED environment is especially important
(
Prachanukool et al. 2022
)
.
Many initial treatment plans are formulated in the ED as the ED often serves as the primary entry point into the hospital system
(
Ouchi et al. 2019
)
.
Thus, integrating SICs at this stage and conducting them effectively can profoundly influence patients’ care trajectories (i.e., ICU admission to hospice referral)
(
Wilson et al. 2020
)
.
Among all ED visits, older adults account for approximately one-quarter of the visits
(
Galvin et al. 2017
)
, and half of the older adult patients in the ED are in the last month of life, and three-fourths are within six months of their death
(
Wilson et al. 2020
)
.
For high-risk, clinically unstable, and vulnerable older adults in the ED, conducting SICs can lead to less aggressive medical care near the end of the patient’s life (e.g., mechanical ventilation or resuscitation), reduce stress among surrogates, and a higher likelihood that care aligns with patients’ wishes
(
Ouchi et al. 2017
)
.

However, clinical care teams rarely conduct SICs, with less than one-third of older adults engaged in SICs
(
Clark et al. 2018
)
.
Clinical care teams face significant challenges in conducting SICs.
One major challenge is that clinical care teams often have substantial time constraints in the ED
(
Chandar et al. 2017
)
.
For example, some physicians and nurses in the ED reported high workloads and understaffing, which limited their ability to engage in lengthy, emotionally charged conversations
(
Adeyemi et al. 2024
)
.
In addition, clinical care teams often lack a clear, actionable protocol for initiating SICs
(
Ouchi et al. 2019
)
, which can leave them unsure how to begin and worried that broaching the topic might trigger negative emotional responses from patients.
Furthermore, clinical care teams are sometimes uncertain about prognostic, and tend to err on the side of optimism, which can delay SICs and, ultimately, critical clinical decision-making
(
Christakis et al. 2000
)
.

Several frameworks and programs have been developed to support conducting SICs.
The Serious Illness Conversation Guide (SICG) is the widely validated framework to provide open-ended questions to help clinical teams navigate SICs
(
Bernacki et al. 2015
)
.
In parallel, the Serious Illness Care Program (SICP) introduces communication training and embeds reminders to improve both the quality and frequency of SICs
(
Paladino et al. 2020
;
Paladino et al. 2023
)
.
However, the framework and the program reveal notable usability and implementation gaps.
In terms of SICG, care providers required a long time to complete the SIC (20 minutes and 53 seconds)
(
Mandel et al. 2023
)
, which may be impractical to implement in the time-constrained ED.
As for SICP, it includes many components, but not all of them are necessary in the time-constrained setting of the ED.

The ineffectiveness of the aforementioned interventions suggests a need for exploring novel approaches to support clinical care teams in their workflow of conducting SICs in the ED.
To better support them, it is essential to understand their workflow, as well as the needs and challenges clinical care teams face in their workflow.
However, there is limited research on these aspects.
Thus, these gaps raise the RQ1 in this paper.

2.2.
Technological Interventions in Supporting Patient-Provider Communications

Communication between patients and clinical care teams plays a crucial role in providing patient-centered care
(
Sunnerhagen and Francisco 2013
)
.
Effective patient-provider communication enhances patient engagement in their care process
(
Butz et al. 2007
)
, reinforces positive future expectations
(
Wright et al. 2004
)
, and improves their mental health status
(
Wong et al. 2019
)
.
To better support the communication, various technological interventions have been explored
(
Ryu et al. 2023
;
Ramesh et al. 2024
;
Seljelid et al. 2021
;
Ryu et al. 2023
;
Bascom et al. 2024
)
.
For example, the online platform BodyDiagrams enables patients to visually mark the intensity and timing of their pain on a body map, facilitating communication of pain experiences between patients and healthcare providers
(
Jang et al. 2014
)
.

Ramesh et al. 2024
designed a data visualization dashboard to support collaborative review of recovery progress between stroke rehabilitation patients and clinical care teams.
In addition,
Cox et al. 2025
designed a mobile application to support patients’ families in providing better care by delivering guidance and information from ICU clinical care teams.

Another line of research explored technologies to help clinical care teams elicit patients’ care preferences and goals of care
(
Sudore et al. 2017
;
Berry et al. 2021
;
Curtis et al. 2023
)
.
For example, Living Voice is an online website to help patients document their care preferences (i.e., whether they receive mechanical ventilation or feeding tubes), and the result can be shared online with clinical care teams
(
Vital Decisions 2024
)
.

Foong et al. 2024
designed five value-elicitation prototype tools to elicit patients’ values and goals through their informal caregivers.
These tools are typically designed for low-pressure care settings.
In these settings, patients have more time to reflect on their care preferences and goals with clinical care teams to make decisions without urgency.
In contrast, the ED is a high-stakes and time-constrained environment.
Older adults in the ED may experience sudden health deterioration, which makes it critical to conduct SICs in order to guide the treatment plans.

Recent advancements in AI present promising pathways to support clinical care teams in streamlining communication and sharing decisions
(
Bartle et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2024
)
.
For instance,
Yang et al. 2024
introduced a home-based telehealth application system (Talk2Care) utilizing Large Language Models (LLMs) to support asynchronous communication between older adults and healthcare providers.
Moreover,
Hao et al. 2024
built an AI system that supports older adult cancer patients in expressing their preferences, raising questions, and engaging in treatment decision discussions with their cancer care clinicians during consultations.
Although these studies explored AI in supporting patient-provider communication, few of them have examined using AI-based tools to support clinical care teams in SICs with older adult patients in the ED environment.
To bridge the gap, our work aims to explore the AI opportunities in this unique and important scenario.

2.3.
AI Technologies in Supporting the SICs

Current research explored some AI-based solutions to support clinicians in their workflow of conducting SICs.
For example, one group of researchers developed an AI algorithm to predict patients’ mortality rates.
The mortality rate helps clinicians save a large amount of time in identifying high-risk patients
(
Avati et al. 2018
)
and conduct the SIC with them.
Another group of researchers increased the frequency of conducting the SIC by sending the message of AI mortality risk predictions and a reminder about considering initiating SICs to oncology clinicians
(
Manz et al. 2020
)
.
In addition, Hachem et al.
(
Hachem et al. 2025
)
developed a dashboard to help track clinicians’ SICs training progress and facilitate documentation of the SICs.
Moreover, researchers explored the potential of using natural language processing to help clinical care teams find information on previous SICs in patients’ EHRs
(
Lindvall et al. 2022
)
.

Another line of research proposed an AI-human collaborative workflow for conducting the SIC
Chua et al. 2022
.
Before the SIC, they suggested using CAs to collect the needed patients’ information to better prepare for SICs.
After the SIC, they highlighted the potential of using NLP to improve both the efficiency and quality of SICs documentation.
Given AI’s potential to evaluate communication skills in clinical settings
(
Ryan et al. 2019
)
, they also proposed leveraging AI to analyze previous SICs and provide feedback to clinical care teams.
Although there are calls and research on leveraging AI to support clinical care teams to conduct SICs, we still have a limited understanding of whether and how clinical care teams want AI to support them, especially in the high-stakes, high-risk, and time-constrained ED environment. This gap becomes the foundation to validate the RQ2 in this paper.

3.
Formative Expert Interviews

To inform RQ1 and RQ2, we conducted formative interviews with two emergency physicians who specialize in SICs within ED settings and conduct research at the intersection of emergency medicine and palliative care. These expert interviews served multiple critical purposes in our research design. Given the absence of standardized workflows for SICs in emergency departments, with practices varying widely across institutions and individual clinical care team members
(
Paladino et al. 2021
;
Chua et al. 2022
)
, we needed to first understand the range of approaches, adaptations, and tensions that characterize these conversations in high-pressure clinical environments. The complexity and ethical sensitivity inherent in SICs required expert guidance to identify clinical norms, recognize potential conversational breakdowns, and surface meaningful opportunities for AI intervention. These insights proved essential for defining the critical moments and decision points that warranted deeper investigation in our broader study. Additionally, the expert interviews informed the development of our semi-structured interview guide for subsequent semi-structured interviews with nine clinical care team members, ensuring that our questions were grounded in domain-appropriate language and clinical logic. This methodological approach enabled us to systematically explore the challenges, needs, and AI opportunities that clinical care teams encounter throughout their SICs workflows while maintaining clinical authenticity and relevance.

3.1.
Study Participants

We recruited two highly experienced clinical experts (hereafter referred to as EX1 and EX2) through the professional networks of domain experts on our research team using convenience sampling
(
Sedgwick 2013
)
.

Both experts have substantial experience conducting serious illness communication with older adult patients in the emergency department.

They possess deep knowledge of the broader care team’s workflow for initiating these conversations and are familiar with the potential and limitations of AI technologies in clinical contexts.

EX1 is an emergency physician and home hospital physician as well as a nationally recognized clinical researcher. He serves as a clinical standard-setter for serious illness conversations in emergency medicine. In addition, he specializes in palliative care interventions for older adults with serious illness and leads a network of 400 emergency physicians focused on geriatrics and palliative care.
EX2 is an emergency physician and researcher working on developing AI-driven, automatable screening tools to identify older adults in the ED at high risk of six-month mortality. His research focuses on leveraging EHR data and automation to improve screening, SICs, and care delivery for older adults, particularly those with dementia or near the end of life.

3.2.
Study Procedure

Figure 1.
In this paper, we conducted a two-stage study. First, we carried out semi-structured interviews with two SIC experts to derive a four-step workflow for conducting the SICs in the ED. We also developed a set of text-based AI probes, which were subsequently used in interviews with nine clinical care team members. Based on the interviews with the clinical care teams, we addressed RQ1 and RQ2 by identifying the key needs and challenges in the SICs process, as well as AI opportunities and concerns. Finally, we derived design guidelines grounded in our findings.

We conducted two semi-structured interviews via Microsoft Teams, each lasting approximately 60 minutes. At the start of each session, the experts were invited to describe a recent experience conducting a serious illness conversation with an older adult in the emergency department. We asked follow-up questions about how they elicited patients’ goals and values, how those informed treatment decisions, how they collaborated with other care team members, and what tools or documentation practices they currently use.

Next, the experts walked us through the typical steps of their serious illness conversation workflow, highlighting key decision points and communication challenges. We then explored their perspectives on how AI tools might support these workflows, including features they would find valuable and concerns related to trust, reliability, and integration into clinical practice.
Insights from these interviews were synthesized into a set of text-based probes that illustrated AI support opportunities (e.g., surfacing patient values, suggesting next conversational steps). These probes were later used in subsequent interviews with members of the clinical care teams in EDs to prompt reflection and concrete feedback on potential AI functionality. This approach aligns with design research practices that prioritize participant-driven input while offering light scaffolding when exploring emerging technologies.

Experts were asked not to disclose any personally identifiable information during the interviews. With their consent, interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed. We conducted thematic analysis
(
Braun and Clarke 2021
;
Braun and Clarke 2012
)
to derive key themes, focusing on: (1) the overall workflow for conducting serious illness conversations in the emergency setting, and (2) expert perspectives on how AI tools could support this work.
Two members of the research team independently coded the transcripts to identify meaningful segments. Codes were iteratively grouped into themes, and discrepancies were resolved through collaborative discussion to finalize a consensus set of findings.

3.3.
Findings

3.3.1.
Clinical Care Teams’ Workflow of Conducting SICs With Older Adults in the ED

Based on the initial interviews, the SIC workflow in the ED can be categorized into four main phases:
Identification
,
Preparation
,
Conduction
, and
Documentation
. In the ED, SICs are usually initiated and led by physicians and, in a few cases, by nurses, though depending on clinical needs and staff availability, social workers, physician assistants, and lay care workers may also be involved. Patients, their surrogates, and family members serve as the central participants throughout this process.

During the
Identification
phase, the clinical team determines whether a patient is appropriate for a SIC by recognizing key triggers that signal the need for values-based care discussions. These triggers include patient physiological status, advanced age, repeated ED visits, or a mismatch between the patient’s condition and the documented code status. For example, EX2 identifies high-risk patients through patterns of repeated ED visits and observable signs of decline, while EX1 flags cases where the treatment intent appears palliative but the patient remains listed as Full Code. The identification process draws from two primary information sources: direct clinical observation (including the patient’s presentation, triage assessment, functional status, previously expressed values and goals, and acute condition) and the Electronic Health Record (particularly past visit history, treatment notes, and current code status). When these sources collectively suggest that the current care plan may not reflect the patient’s overall goals and values, the team moves forward with initiating SICs.

After they identified a patient who needs SICs, the
Identification
phase begins.
Preparation is typically brief but critical for setting the foundation for meaningful conversation.
There are two parts in this stage: mental and logistical preparation. The logistical part is about when and where to hold the conversation, and who should be present is guided by clinical judgment, the urgency of the patient’s condition, and the available resources in the ED. Beyond these practical considerations, this phase also encompasses a moment of internal readiness, as clinical care team members prepare themselves mentally and emotionally to engage in what is often a difficult but critically important discussion. This dual preparation helps ensure that clinical care teams can navigate the complex terrain of the SIC with appropriate sensitivity and clinical grounding.

The clinical care team then enters the
Conduction
phase of the conversation, which represents the core interaction where values and goals are explored and care plans are aligned. This phase typically begins with an opening question to the patient or family that introduces the topic and initiates the dialogue in a sensitive manner. The care team first assesses what the patient and family understand about the current condition and treatment, offering any necessary clarifications or explanations to ensure a shared understanding of the clinical situation. The conversation then shifts to the heart of SICs: exploring the patient’s goals, values, and acceptable trade-offs, as well as identifying any signs of decline or distress that may influence decision-making. Throughout this exploration, the care plan is dynamically adapted based on the patient’s or family’s stated preferences and priorities. The overarching aim of this phase is to reach a shared understanding of the patient’s condition and care priorities, ultimately proposing a plan of care that authentically aligns with what the patient values most.

Following SICs, the clinical care team enters the
Documentation
phase, where they record the outcomes of the conversation, including the patient’s goals, values, and care preferences, in the EHR. This documentation serves multiple critical functions: it provides a reference for other care providers involved in the patient’s ongoing treatment, ensures continuity of care across different clinical encounters, and creates a lasting record of the patient’s expressed wishes that can guide future medical decisions. Beyond formal documentation, this phase may also include verbal encouragement for the patient to arrange follow-up with a specialist or another care team, depending on their condition and specific care needs. The quality and accessibility of this documentation become particularly important in the ED setting, where multiple providers may be involved in a patient’s care and where the insights gained from SICs must be effectively communicated to ensure goal-concordant care continues beyond the emergency visit. 3.3.2.
AI Opportunities in Supporting the Clinical Care Teams’ Workflow

After two experts talked about the workflow of conducting SICs in the ED, they showed a high interest in integrating AI into their workflow.
There are mainly two aspects related to the AI opportunities: generating questions based on patient responses in real time, and getting feedback to improve skills in conducting SICs.
During SICs, EX2 mentioned that while they have access to a guideline with suggested questions, the volume of questions in the guideline makes it difficult to select the most appropriate ones under time pressure.
So both experts mentioned that using an AI assistant to listen to both clinical care team members and patient interactions during SICs and providing real-time question suggestions, such as what can be asked next, could be highly beneficial.
Moreover, EX2 mentioned that after SICs, he does not receive any feedback about the SICs he had.
Therefore, it is hard to improve the skill in conducting SICs.
Thus, he expressed a need for AI-generated feedback on how he phrased questions during the last SIC and what could be improved, which could help him refine his communication skills for future SICs.

4.
Interviews with Clinical Care Teams

Based on the formative expert interviews, we found that there are four steps in the clinical care team’s workflow of conducting SICs with older adults in the ED.
In the subsequent semi-structured interviews, we talked with broader clinical care teams to gain a deep understanding of the challenges and needs that they face in each step, as well as their perceptions towards the AI tools in supporting them in their workflow.

4.1.
Study Participants

The participants in this study were clinical care team members who practice in the ED and have experience conducting SICs with older adults. While they do not specialize in or conduct research on serious illness communication, they routinely engage in these conversations as part of their clinical responsibilities. Their work environment is high-pressure and fast-paced, often involving complex cases and rotating day and night shifts, which makes their availability limited.
To capture a holistic understanding of the interprofessional workflow and communication dynamics involved in SICs, we intentionally recruited nine U.S.-based clinical care team members across diverse roles, including two nurses and seven physicians.
Participants were identified through the professional networks of our expert interviewees and recruited using snowball sampling—a commonly used method for accessing highly specialized clinical populations.

4.2.
Study Procedure

We conducted 30-minute semi-structured interviews remotely via Microsoft Teams.
Participants were first asked to describe a past experience conducting an SIC with an older adult in the ED.
Next, they discussed the challenges and needs they encounter at each stage (identification, preparation, conduction, and documentation) of the SIC workflow.
Participants were then asked to share the frameworks and technologies they currently use to support the SICs process.
Finally, we explored their perspectives on potential opportunities for AI tools.
To support participant reflection on the role of AI in SICs, we drew on insights from our expert interviews to develop text-based AI support examples.
These were not part of the interview protocol per se, but were used only if participants struggled to articulate scenarios on their own.
Our intention was not to test these scenarios or restrict the conversation but rather to use them as reflective scaffolds—cognitive prompts that could help participants generate or refine their own ideas.
Participants were encouraged to respond however they wished: expanding, critiquing, reframing, or proposing entirely different possibilities.
This approach reflects early-stage, exploratory design research practices where speculative but grounded examples can support participants’ imagination while preserving openness and participant agency
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
.
The interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed with the participants’ consent.

After the interviews, we used both inductive and deductive thematic analysis approaches
(
Fereday and Muir-Cochrane 2006
)
to derive key themes from our transcripts.
Two researchers in our team first independently employed the inductive thematic analysis approach to analyze two transcripts. They identified meaningful quotes, categorized these quotes into codes based on the challenge and need in each step of the SIC workflow.
Then, they resolved discrepancies among the codes and came up with key themes.
After the discussion, they used deductive thematic analysis to group similar codes and leveraged inductive thematic analysis to identify new codes.
They then discussed again and reached a consensus about the codes.
Finally, they grouped the codes into themes in each step of the SIC workflow.
To transparently report our findings, we indicate the prevalence of codes and themes across participants using the following terminology: "a few" refers to up to 20% of participants, "some" indicates 21-50%, "most" represents 51-80%, and "nearly all" denotes 80% or more of participants.

5.1.
Gaps in Real-Time Alignment Between Communication Needs and EHR

When clinical care teams admit older adult patients with serious illness to the ED, nurses start a triage to evaluate patient health conditions.
During triage, nurses attempt to gather important information such as patients’ health history and their chief complaint for the current visit. However, this information-gathering process faces a significant challenge, as not all patients provide the necessary details to support accurate assessment of their condition. P2 illustrated this challenge by describing a patient who reported no health issues when questioned directly about their condition. Unable to obtain critical information from the patient interview, P2 turned to the EHR system to review the patient’s medical history and discovered that the patient actually had stage IV cancer.

“Maybe something doesn’t seem quite right…from what you’re telling me, it sounds like they’re in pretty good health, but I’m looking at this patient and saying this patient is not in good health, so what’s missing?”
(P2)

While some patients are able to communicate effectively with clinical care teams in the ED, others present significant communication barriers that complicate information gathering. Some patients may arrive at the ED in a coma or otherwise incapacitated, making them unable to communicate their medical history or current condition to the clinical care team. When patients lack the capacity to communicate, clinical care teams typically turn to patients’ families or surrogates (people who make medical decisions on behalf of a patient when the patient is unable to express their wishes or make decisions themselves) to obtain essential patient information. However, P1 noted that high-risk patients are sometimes unaccompanied by family members, creating additional challenges in determining whether aggressive treatments should be administered.

“And oftentimes you get older individuals who don’t have family members, or they can’t be identified, and it makes the treatment sometimes trickier because they can’t tell the story.”
(P1)

5.2.
Clinical Care Teams Have Difficulties Accessing and Utilizing the EHR Data Prior to SICs in the ED

Many participants (P1, P4, P5, P7, P8, P9) noted that when they identified life-threatening clinical indicators, such as intracranial hemorrhage, newly diagnosed malignancy, or frequent ED revisits, they recognized that the patients were at elevated risk of mortality.
If patients have a high risk of mortality, it normally means they need to receive aggressive treatment to save their lives.
Thus, clinical care teams would check the EHR to find whether patients had SICs previously, and to access documentation related to patients’ goals of care and treatment preferences, including DNI (i.e., do Not Intubate) and DNR (i.e., do Not Resuscitate).
This information is essential since it informs subsequent clinical decision-making.

“We’re worried that we may hit a point very quickly, if not immediately where we are considering doing some kind of invasive procedure that they may or may not want.”
(P8)

Many participants (P1, P4, P5, P7, P8, P9) noted that when they identified life-threatening clinical indicators such as intracranial hemorrhage, newly diagnosed malignancy, or frequent ED revisits, they recognized that patients were at elevated risk of mortality. High mortality risk typically necessitates aggressive treatment interventions to preserve life. However, before proceeding with such interventions, clinical care teams must first determine whether these treatments align with patients’ previously expressed preferences. This clinical decision point prompts teams to search the EHR for evidence of prior serious illness conversations and to access documentation related to patients’ goals of care and treatment preferences, including DNI (Do Not Intubate) and DNR (Do Not Resuscitate) orders. This information is essential for informing subsequent clinical decision-making, particularly in situations where aggressive interventions may conflict with patient values and preferences.

“Talking about code status (DNI and DNR) is one small piece of that [SIC], but that can actually be shockingly hard to find in most EHRs or EMRs…it’s usually not flagged in an obvious way… So it’s like mentioned in a progress note, but there might be thousands of progress notes.”
(P8)

However, accessing reliable patient preference information presents multiple challenges. Even when clinical care teams locate relevant documentation in the EHR system, a few participants (P3, P7) reported that patients’ goals of care and treatment preferences may have changed over time without corresponding updates to their records. P7 additionally noted that conflicting documentation within the EHR sometimes creates further confusion about patient wishes. In other circumstances, some participants (P1, P4, P7, P8) encountered limited information availability altogether. For example, when P7 searched for specific care preference keywords, the EHR returned only "not addressed," providing insufficient information to support clinical decision-making. These information access challenges led a few participants (P3, P7) to emphasize that having access to accurate and up-to-date patient information would be highly beneficial, as such information helps clinical care teams improve patient condition assessment and supports decisions about whether SICs is needed.

“The information documented in the chart may not be accurate, the goals of care may have changed, and haven’t been updated. Sometimes there’s conflicting notes, and understanding what’s right and current can be helpful.”
(P3)

5.3.
Contextual Logistical and Emotional Constraints Undermining Preparation for Values-Based Emergency Care

When clinical care teams identify the need to conduct SICs with older adult patients, they must first prepare logistically, yet they face several interconnected challenges in this preparation phase. Some participants (P4, P7, P8, P9) mentioned that finding an appropriate moment to conduct SICs proves difficult due to the high patient volume and fast-paced environment of the ED. This time pressure forces clinicians to be more selective about which patients receive SICs, as P7 noted. Beyond timing, the sensitive nature of SICs topics often catches patients and their family members unprepared, making some participants (P3, P4, P6, P8, P9) emphasize the importance of finding private and quiet spaces to create calm and undisturbed environments that improve SICs effectiveness. However, securing private space presents its own challenge due to overcrowded ED conditions, with P8 describing how some patients are placed in hallways amid noise and chaos while others remain in waiting rooms for 10 to 15 hours.

“I think in the emergency setting, it is usually [challenging] like physical, like [finding] time and space [to conduct the SIC]. So like I said, you know, even finding private care space sometimes is challenging. Having the time to have these conversations is really challenging.”
(P4)

When clinicians do secure time and space for SICs, some participants (P1, P3, P4) reported wanting to identify who else patients want present during the conversation. If key family members or surrogate decision-makers are not physically present, clinical care teams often call them to ensure everyone remains informed. However, surrogates and caregivers are not always available when needed. In cases where patients cannot communicate and no documented care preferences exist, clinical care teams typically proceed with aggressive treatment to preserve life.

Beyond logistical preparation, the mental and emotional preparation for SICs presents even greater complexity. Many participants (P1, P2, P4, P5, P6, P9) mentioned that preparing mentally for SICs is particularly difficult, especially for those with limited prior experience. A few participants (P4, P6) noted that SICs sometimes occur spontaneously without advance preparation time. P9 explained that conducting SICs requires creating an "emotional container" to manage the negative emotions that patients and families often experience during these conversations. However, establishing this emotional readiness and preparing for both expected and unexpected emotional responses proves challenging, leading P6 to observe that most of his colleagues feel uncomfortable initiating SICs.

“The emotional piece is much more complex, and it’s why this issue has such great difficulty within dissemination and adoption.”
(P5)

Participants identified training modules (P1, P6) and real-life practice (P1, P2, P7) as beneficial for better SICs preparation. However, beyond formal training approaches, personal experience significantly affects clinicians’ perceived difficulty in conducting SICs mentally. P1 described how his experience providing long-term care for his parents helps him relate to patients and family members during difficult times. Conversely, P5’s loss of his father to cancer a few years ago makes SICs more emotionally challenging, particularly when conducting conversations with older adult cancer patients.

“That next year or two, I was emotionally different than I was before. These conversations were more emotional for me. I was more connected to the conversation as I just lost my father. Even I might tear up during these conversations when historically throughout my career, I never would cry or show emotion like that… You know how this hits home, you know, a clinician who lost a parent to cancer to go talk to a cancer patient at end of life might be extremely difficult, but maybe for someone else [it] might not be difficult.”
(P5)

5.4.
Supporting Patient Sense-making and Emotional Engagement During SICs Conduction

When clinical care teams finally secure the time and space to conduct SICs with older adult patients in the ED, they encounter three primary challenges: communicating undesirable and unexpected health conditions, building connection and trust within compressed timeframes, and making clinical decisions when patients cannot participate in conversations.
Delivering difficult diagnoses presents significant communication challenges, as some participants (P3, P4, P7, P8) found it particularly hard to communicate devastating health conditions such as cancer diagnoses to older adult patients and their families. This difficulty often stems from a substantial gap between clinical reality and patient or family perceptions, where patients may believe they are in good health or families maintain unrealistic recovery expectations. When clinicians deliver such news, patients and families are frequently shocked and struggle to accept the information. To address this challenge, a few participants noted that providing additional contextual information (P2, P9) and calibrating communication approaches based on patient understanding (P2) prove essential for more effective SICs.

“I think it’s often challenging when the goals of care don’t necessarily align with reality. And there’s an expectation that the patient’s gonna recover from a condition that’s essentially not recoverable. Or the expectations of the family or that there’s going to be some type of sort of miraculous cure when that’s not going to happen, that can be challenging.”
(P3)

Beyond information delivery, some participants (P2, P5, P6, P9) emphasized that building meaningful connections with patients is essential for facilitating effective SICs. P2 described how she builds connections by finding common topics to initiate conversations, such as discussing patients’ favorite sports teams or their children. This personalized approach helps clinical care teams recognize that they are treating the person, not merely the disease, thereby creating more positive experiences during difficult conversations.

“Because if you don’t make a connection, then your nursing is not personal to them (the patients). And then you feel like you’re not treating their them personally, you’re just treating cancer or you’re just treating a heart attack.”
(P2)

However, establishing trust and connection in the ED environment presents unique challenges. Some participants (P2, P5, P8) noted that while connection-building enables two-way communication where patients actively listen and respond, clinical care teams face significant disadvantages in the ED setting. P5 explained that, unlike other departments where providers may develop relationships over time, ED clinicians often meet patients for the first time under high-pressure circumstances, making trust establishment particularly difficult.

“We’re at a significant disadvantage in the emergency department as I am not their primary care doctor. I have not cared for them for a decade, you know, and earned all their trust and faith. They are meeting me at point blank, you know, and they have to develop faith and trust.”
(P5)

To compensate for these constraints, care teams employ various rapid rapport-building strategies, such as asking personal questions about where patients live or involving family members in conversations when appropriate, as P6 noted.
The third major challenge occurs when older adult patients cannot participate conversationally due to conditions such as unconsciousness or breathing difficulties before or during their ED stay. Most participants (P1, P2, P4, P5, P7, P8, P9) identified this as a particularly complex situation. While P5 and P9 noted that patients are always the preferred participants for SICs, when patients cannot engage, clinical care teams must quickly identify alternative decision-makers, such as surrogates or family members, to support clinical decision-making. When working with surrogates, care teams attempt to understand patient values through family insights, focusing on what matters most to patients and their quality of life preferences. However, this process becomes complicated when multiple family members have varying levels of decision-making authority, as P4 described. The situation becomes even more challenging when patients arrive without any family members, as P1 noted, potentially hindering SICs’ conduct and delaying critical treatment decisions.

“you get older individuals who don’t have family members, or they can’t be identified, and it makes the treatment sometimes trickier because they can’t tell the story.”
(P1)

5.5.
Inadequate Handoff of Patient Values and Goals from Emergency Clinicians to Primary Teams

Following SICs’ completion, all participants document conversation summaries in the EHR system, yet this documentation process creates significant workflow burdens and communication challenges. A few participants (P5, P8) noted that documentation consumes substantial time, with P8 explaining that no effective method exists for documenting SICs during active shifts. The high ED workload leaves clinical care teams with limited time for documentation, forcing many to stay late after shifts and use unpaid hours to complete this essential task.

“A lot of what you do is looking through a ton of data, trying to synthesize it, seeing the patient, addressing too many things in too little time, and then trying to document it and then do it, you know, 30 or 40 more times in a shift. And so many of us end up staying late or working unpaid hours, finishing charts because we care, and the system takes advantage of us.”
(P8)

Despite these time constraints, documentation remains critically important for two interconnected reasons: facilitating communication with primary care providers and enabling care continuity across clinical encounters. Regarding primary care communication, a few participants (P1, P4) emphasized the need to connect with patients’ established care providers to support clinical decision-making. However, this communication proves challenging due to the complexity of provider networks within EHR systems. P1 described the difficulty of identifying appropriate contacts among numerous listed healthcare providers, including physicians, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, and residents. Even when clinical care teams identify the correct provider, timing mismatches often prevent connection, particularly when ED teams work night shifts while other providers remain unavailable.

“You’ll open up a patient’s chart and there’ll be so many healthcare providers, physicians, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, residents. You can’t. You don’t know who to call, and if you do, they’re often not on calls.”
(P1)

The second critical function of documentation involves enabling care continuity, as a few participants (P8, P9) noted that documentation allows subsequent providers to continue SICs processes. P5 emphasized that SICs represent an ongoing, evolving conversation rather than a singular ED event, making it essential to document SICs’ progress and ensure accessibility for other care providers. However, this continuity goal is compromised by inconsistent documentation practices. P9 observed that clinical care teams document SICs content in various EHR locations: some create separate ED progress notes, others include information in ED final notes, and some use advance care planning (ACP) notes. This variability creates accessibility problems, as documentation not entered as ACP notes is unlikely to be revisited or seen by future providers, potentially compromising clinical care teams’ ability to continue or follow up on SICs.

“I’ve seen variability where I’ve seen some people document it as a separate ED progress note. I’ve seen some people document as an ACP note. And then I’ve also seen some people just document it as part of the ED course that then populates into the final ED provider note.”
(P9)

5.6.
Integrating AI into the SIC workflow in the ED

5.6.1.
Opportunities for Integrating AI into the SIC workflow in the ED

When participants discussed potential AI integration opportunities, they expressed strong interest across three areas that directly address the workflow challenges identified earlier: pre-conversation information synthesis for identification and preparation, real-time conversation support for conducting SICs, and post-conversation documentation assistance.
Building on the EHR access challenges identified in Section
5.2
, participants recognized AI’s potential to synthesize and summarize patient information more effectively. P3 and P4 suggested that AI could extract and summarize key information from extensive health records, helping clinical care teams quickly understand patient health conditions and identify individuals who matter most to patients, thereby improving preparation for serious illness conversations. Some participants (P3, P4, P7, P8) specifically expressed the need for rapid access to previous serious illness conversation summaries, as these conversations often build upon earlier discussions but remain scattered across different EHR locations. However, preferences for information presentation varied, with P9 preferring to view full conversation text with key information highlighted rather than condensed summaries, emphasizing the importance of maintaining clinical nuance and context.

“Anyone who’s previously documented, like if there was a goal of care button, I could click and it would go through everything that’s been documented on a patient’s chart. Because maybe they had a conversation with a social worker a few weeks ago or their primary care doctor, and I just don’t have the time to click through every note.”
(P4)

Beyond information preparation, some participants (P4, P5, P6) envisioned AI providing real-time support during conversations through ambient listening capabilities. P6 explained that conducting serious illness conversations places clinical care teams in uncomfortable territory where maintaining natural dialogue flow proves challenging, particularly for those with limited experience. He suggested that AI could recommend contextually appropriate follow-up questions based on ongoing conversation content, helping to sustain meaningful dialogue and more effectively elicit patient care preferences and goals of care. This real-time support addresses the communication challenges identified in Section
5.4
, where clinical care teams struggle to navigate sensitive topics while building trust under time pressure.

P5 emphasized another critical dimension of real-time AI support: tracking conversation progress across the patient’s care trajectory. He noted that serious illness conversations conducted in the ED represent merely the initial step in ongoing dialogues that continue throughout a patient’s illness journey. Since comprehensive conversations can extend to an hour, but ED time constraints severely limit coverage of all necessary topics, a few participants (P8, P9) mentioned that ED-focused conversations must prioritize short-term goals, such as determining patient preferences for aggressive treatment to support immediate clinical decision-making in high-risk and high-stakes environments. Because of this temporal limitation, P5 emphasized that AI could ambient listen to conversations and record progress, enabling subsequent care providers to understand which topics were addressed and which require attention in future encounters, supporting the delivery of goal-concordant care.

“And you know, you guys (researcher) define what those six things [that needed to be gotten done in SICs] are. I can only maybe accomplish one or two of them [in the ED]. The AI recognizes it and is able to inform the next provider. ’Hey, [the previous care provider] was only able to get through one and two, you know, consider focusing on three, four, and five’.”
(P5)

Addressing the substantial documentation burden identified in Section
5.5
, most participants (P1, P3, P4, P5, P7, P8, P9) expressed strong interest in AI-generated conversation summaries that could significantly reduce the time currently spent on unpaid charting work. This need is particularly important given that serious illness conversations can be lengthy, and not all documented information is directly relevant to patient goals and values. P3 suggested that AI could distill lengthy conversations to focus specifically on patient goals and values, filtering out clinically irrelevant content that often consumes documentation time. Beyond summarization, P1 proposed that AI could generate appropriate documentation prompts to guide efficient record creation, providing clinical care teams with structured frameworks for capturing essential conversation elements. In this way, AI could not only assist clinical care teams by generating first-draft summaries of serious illness conversations but also support them in efficiently documenting and revising key points from conversations. P8 estimated that AI handling approximately 80% of documentation tasks could save nearly half of the working hours currently spent on post-shift charting, time that could instead be redirected to seeing more patients.

“But if AI could offload even say 80 percent of that (documentation) and then the other 20 percent you’re tweaking and verifying the transcript or, you know, the output, that would save close to half of my working hours that I could actually be spending with seeing more patients.”
(P8)

However, participants also expressed important concerns about AI summarization accuracy and clinical appropriateness. Some participants (P3, P4, P7, P9) worried that AI might emphasize clinically irrelevant information while missing critical details that experienced clinicians would immediately recognize as significant. P4 and P9 noted that AI-identified important content might not align with what clinical care teams consider most clinically relevant, potentially leading to inappropriate clinical responses. AI may overemphasize irrelevant content while failing to adequately highlight the information most relevant to clinical care teams. For example, P4 explained that while a patient might mention "chest pain" during conversation, this reference may not constitute clinically significant chest pain requiring intervention. However, if AI documents this mention without proper clinical context or interpretation, it could inappropriately trigger comprehensive cardiac workups and unnecessary resource utilization. These accuracy concerns led some participants (P3, P7, P9) to emphasize the critical importance of clinician review and verification processes before finalizing any AI-generated summaries, ensuring that automated assistance enhances rather than compromises clinical decision-making.

“Although the final sign off is going to be clinician reviewing the summary of what AI note taking would be, right? … We still have to read it again, make sure that’s actually all accurate.”
(P7)

5.6.2.
Concerns About Integrating AI into the SIC workflow in the ED

While participants expressed enthusiasm for AI support opportunities, they also articulated significant concerns about integrating AI into serious illness conversations, focusing primarily on preserving human connection and ensuring trustworthy implementation in sensitive clinical contexts.
Some participants (P1, P3, P5, P6, P9) expressed concerns that AI technology could diminish the essential humanity of SICs. The communication between clinical care teams and patients represents more than a structured information exchange—it constitutes "a human moment" (P5) that is "deeply personal" (P3). While AI assistance might facilitate smoother workflows and more structured questioning, participants worried that clinical care teams could lose their authentic connection with patients, with several emphasizing that patients might perceive AI-assisted conversations as less genuine or heartfelt.

“if there’s a perception that the physician is just using an electronic tool to ask [the] right questions, that’s not really sort of coming from the heart, that could be negatively perceived.”
(P3)

Beyond concerns about authenticity, some participants (P1, P4, P5, P6, P9) worried that AI integration might create distracting and disrespectful interactions when clinical care teams attempt to build connections with patients. They emphasized that meaningful conversations require complete attention and direct eye contact, viewing frequent computer interaction as potentially undermining the respect and presence that serious illness conversations demand. P6 captured this sentiment by noting that these conversations deserve face-to-face engagement without computer screen barriers.

“And I just think these conversations are like worthy of someone sitting down with you face to face, not having a computer screen in front of them.”
(P6)

Trust and credibility concerns represent another significant barrier to AI adoption in serious illness conversations. Some participants (P1, P5, P7) emphasized the necessity of thorough data validation before integrating AI into their workflows, recognizing that these deeply human and sensitive moments require clinical care teams to earn patient and family trust. Beyond fundamental privacy considerations emphasizing patient permission and opt-out rights, participants identified factors such as data validation and colleague endorsement as critical when evaluating AI tools for sensitive care conversations. P1 explained that his willingness to adopt AI tools would depend heavily on the credibility of the recommending source and the scientific rigor underlying the technology. This concern reflects the high stakes of serious illness conversations, where trust between clinical care teams and patients forms the foundation for meaningful dialogue about values, goals, and care preferences.

6.
Discussion

6.1.
Design Implications

Based on our interviews with nine clinical care team members, we identified significant challenges and needs in their workflow of conducting SICs, revealing both opportunities for AI support and critical design constraints that must be carefully balanced. The time-sensitive, high-risk, and high-stakes environment of the ED creates unique considerations for integrating AI into clinical workflows, particularly given the competing demands we observed between efficiency needs and concerns about preserving human connection. Our findings reveal a fundamental tension in clinical care teams’ perspectives on AI integration. While participants in Section
5.6.1
expressed strong interest in AI support for information synthesis, conversation guidance, and documentation assistance, those same participants and others in Section
5.6.2
emphasized concerns about technology potentially diminishing the essential humanity of these deeply personal conversations. This tension suggests that effective AI systems must be designed with careful attention to preserving clinical autonomy and maintaining the intimate nature of serious illness conversations.

Several key design considerations emerge from this analysis that must guide AI system development in this sensitive clinical context. First, any AI tools must prioritize non-intrusive implementation that allows clinical care teams the flexibility to opt in or out based on their individual comfort levels and specific conversation dynamics. Second, given the severe time constraints in ED environments, AI interactions must be streamlined and seamlessly integrated into existing workflows rather than creating additional cognitive burden. Third, solutions should be lightweight and easily accessible, such as mobile phone applications with passive modes, to minimize potential negative impacts and distractions during sensitive conversations. Finally, our findings align with
Zhang et al. 2024
’s work in high-risk clinical settings, confirming that clinical care teams envision AI as a physician-facing assistant that augments human capabilities rather than making independent clinical decisions or judgments. These considerations inform four design guidelines that address the specific workflow challenges identified in our study while respecting the complex balance between efficiency needs and the preservation of meaningful human connection in serious illness conversations.

DG1: Synthesize and summarize critical patient information to support identification and preparation
. As identified in Section
5.2
, clinical care teams struggle to efficiently access fragmented patient information scattered across EHR systems, particularly previous serious illness conversation documentation, medical history, code status, and surrogate contact details. This information fragmentation creates significant barriers during the time-critical identification and preparation phases, where clinicians must rapidly assess whether patients would benefit from serious illness conversations while simultaneously gathering context for meaningful dialogue. To address this challenge, AI systems should automatically extract, synthesize, and summarize key critical patient information (code status, primary serious illness, previously expressed goals and values, and even mortality prediction) in easily consumable formats that integrate seamlessly into existing clinical workflows. Prior research has demonstrated the feasibility of such approaches, including AI-driven mortality prediction systems that help identify high-risk patients
(
Avati et al. 2018
)
, and interventions that use AI predictions to prompt serious illness conversations
(
Manz et al. 2020
)
. Rather than replacing clinical judgment, such systems should serve as intelligent information organizers that help clinical care teams quickly develop comprehensive patient understanding. Implementation should prioritize information hierarchy (highlighting most clinically relevant details first), provide direct links to source documentation for verification, and offer both summary and detailed views to accommodate different clinician preferences and time constraints.

DG2: Provide conversational support during SICs conduction using ambient technologies for smooth dialogue flow and care continuity
. Based on Section
5.6.1
, clinical care teams face dual challenges during serious illness conversations: maintaining natural dialogue flow while ensuring comprehensive coverage of essential topics within ED time constraints. Many clinicians, particularly those with limited experience, struggle to navigate these sensitive conversations smoothly, while the fragmented nature of ED care means that subsequent providers often lack awareness of what topics were previously addressed. To address these interconnected challenges, AI systems should offer conversational support that assists clinical care teams in conducting effective serious illness conversations while tracking progress for care continuity. This support could include ambient listening capabilities that passively monitor conversations (with explicit patient consent) to provide context-aware question suggestions that help maintain conversation flow, as well as progress tracking that identifies which conversation elements have been covered for future provider reference. Such ambient recording approaches are already being adopted in clinical practice, with EHR systems such as Epic

Implementation must prioritize non-intrusive design that preserves the intimate nature of these conversations, offering support through subtle interfaces that clinicians can choose to engage with or ignore based on their comfort level and the specific conversation dynamics.

DG3: Streamline documentation through automated conversation summarization while preserving clinical autonomy
. As mentioned in Section
5.6.1
, clinical care teams face substantial documentation burdens that force many to work unpaid hours after shifts, with comprehensive serious illness conversation documentation consuming significant time that could be redirected to direct patient care. The challenge is compounded by the need to distill lengthy conversations into clinically relevant summaries that capture essential patient goals, values, and care preferences while filtering out less relevant content. To address this challenge, AI systems should automatically generate structured conversation summaries that highlight key clinical information, provide suggested documentation prompts, and offer editable drafts that clinical care teams can review and modify. Recent research in Large Language Models (LLMs)
(
Agaronnik et al. 2025
)
and NLP
(
Lindvall et al. 2022
)
have demonstrated their suitability for this task. These technologies can process conversational content, identify clinically relevant information (e.g., values and goals, and advanced care directives), and generate structured summaries in formats and standards consistent with existing documentation standards. Implementation must prioritize clinical autonomy by ensuring transparency in AI-generated content, maintaining clear links to source conversation segments for verification, and providing intuitive editing interfaces that allow clinicians to correct, expand, or restructure summaries based on their clinical judgment and documentation requirements.

DG4: Support skill development through personalized feedback based on conversation analysis
. Clinical care teams mentioned in Section
5.3
that training and experience with serious illness conversations help them conduct these conversations more effectively and comfortably. However, most participants noted the difficulty of mental and emotional preparation, with many colleagues feeling uncomfortable initiating serious illness conversations due to limited experience. Moreover, in Section
5.6.1
, clinical care team members expressed interest in receiving AI-generated feedback about their performance in serious illness conversations to improve their communication skills over time. To address this need, AI systems should analyze recorded serious illness conversations to provide personalized feedback and suggestions that help clinical care teams refine their communication approaches. Such systems could identify specific areas for improvement in conversation flow, question phrasing, and patient engagement techniques based on previous conversation recordings. This approach builds on previous research proposing AI-human collaborative workflows for serious illness conversations
(
Chua et al. 2022
)
, which highlighted AI’s potential to analyze conversation content and provide feedback to clinical care teams, leveraging demonstrated capabilities for evaluating communication skills in clinical settings
(
Ryan et al. 2019
)
. Implementation should provide constructive feedback that builds clinician confidence and comfort levels, ultimately improving their preparation and effectiveness in conducting future serious illness conversations within the time-constrained ED environment.

6.2.
Limitations

This study is based on the individual experiences of healthcare professionals and experts, including two domain experts and nine frontline clinicians (ED physicians and nurses). Despite the relatively small sample size, it reflects the practical challenges of recruiting emergency clinicians who work in unpredictable and high-pressure ED environments. However, most participants were from the same geographic region and affiliated with large, resource-rich healthcare systems. This sampling composition may introduce bias and limit the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Including clinicians from smaller institutions, different resource settings, or other geographic areas could yield more diverse and comprehensive insights.

In addition, the study focused mainly on clinical staff, meaning perspectives from other relevant stakeholders, such as social workers, patients, and family members, were not represented, limiting the diversity of viewpoints regarding SICs process. Moreover, the participants varied in their knowledge of AI, their personal experience with AI tools, and the extent to which AI has been integrated into their clinical settings. These differences may have influenced how they described workflows and interpreted the potential of AI in clinical care. A systematic assessment of the digital and AI tools currently used in these settings would offer clearer context for interpreting participants’ responses. Finally, data collection was limited to semi-structured interviews. This method may not fully capture tacit challenges, implicit norms, embedded routines, or nuanced practices that shape everyday clinical workflows. Incorporating on-site observations could have provided a more comprehensive understanding of real-world clinical processes.

7.
Conclusion

This study investigates the practical challenges clinical care teams face when conducting SICs with older adult patients in emergency department settings and identifies opportunities for AI support grounded in current clinical workflows. Through interviews with two domain experts and nine frontline clinicians, we characterize a four-phase model of SICs process—identification, preparation, conduct, and documentation—and systematically examine barriers encountered at each stage. Our findings reveal that fragmented and unstructured EHR data significantly impedes clinicians’ ability to access prior SICs content and documented patient preferences, disrupting care continuity while increasing both documentation burden and communication overhead. Clinical care teams frequently conduct these sensitive conversations under substantial physical and temporal constraints, often lacking adequate privacy and coordination support within the fast-paced ED environment. Beyond logistical challenges, SICs carry considerable emotional labor as clinicians must simultaneously manage patient and family distress while navigating their own emotional responses, typically without sufficient preparation time or institutional support structures. Despite these significant constraints, participants expressed strong interest in AI tools that could synthesize relevant patient information, provide structured conversational guidance, and streamline documentation processes. However, they emphasized that effective AI systems must maintain interpretability and preserve emotional authenticity without compromising the essential human connection central to these conversations. Grounded in frontline clinician perspectives, this study surfaces critical workflow challenges in ED-based SICs and identifies key requirements for AI systems that can help clinicians access patient values, manage emotional demands, and maintain care continuity across fragmented workflows while reducing cognitive load and documentation burden.
