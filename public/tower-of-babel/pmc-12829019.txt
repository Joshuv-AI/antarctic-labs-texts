A retail audit of mosquito control products in Bidibidi refugee settlement, northwestern Uganda
Authors: Kaci D McCoy, Esther Nakyaze, Samantha W Tsang, Amy A Ellis, Mary Grace Alwano, Olive D’Mello, Suzanne Van Hulle, Steve A Harvey, Danielle Piccinini Black, Jarvice Sekajja
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUganda remains one of the highest malaria burden countries globally, with refugee populations disproportionately affected. While government-led initiatives, such as free distribution of insecticide-treatesd nets (ITNs) and indoor residual spraying, are key to the vector control response, limited data exist on the role of retail markets in providing complementary mosquito control products (MCPs) in refugee settings.MethodsAs part of the Advancing Evidence for the Global Implementation of Spatial Repellents project, we conducted a retail audit across 61 refugee and host community villages in Bidibidi refugee settlement area in northwestern Uganda. The audit consisted of two phases: a mapping of all retail outlets (n = 828) and a cross-sectional survey of a sub-set of retail outlets currently stocking or that had previously stocked MCPs in the six months prior to the mapping (n = 252) to characterize MCP availability, sales, supply chains, and perceived profitability. Data were collected using electronic structured questionnaires and analysed through descriptive statistics for quantitative and thematic coding of qualitative responses.ResultsAmong the outlets surveyed, 65.4% currently sell MCPs. Incense sticks with insecticide were stocked most (56.4% of outlets), followed by adult ITNs (29.1%) and non-insecticidal incense sticks (27.3%). Most outlets restocked products at least monthly, mainly sourcing MCPs from local wholesale suppliers. Respondents reported seasonal spikes in sales and demand during rainy months, and they identified perceived profitability and community health benefits as key motivators for stocking MCPs. However, limited capital, unreliable supply chains, and lack of product knowledge were identified as barriers.ConclusionThe availability, selection, and perceived profitability of MCPs suggests a viable market for MCPs and a gap in protection or preference that consumers are looking to fill in the Bidibidi refugee settlement area. These findings highlight the potential of engaging the private sector in humanitarian contexts to inform the strategic introduction of new tools, such as spatial repellents.Keywords: Malaria, Spatial repellent, Mosquito control, Retail audit, Retail outlets, Insecticide‐treated mosquito nets, Aerosol sprays, Mosquito coils, Topical repellents, Uganda, Refugee settlement

Background

In 2023, globally, there were an estimated 263 million malaria cases in 83 malaria endemic countries; an increase of 11 million cases compared with 2022. With nearly 12 million cases in 2023, Uganda was the third highest contributor worldwide [1]. Despite substantial progress in malaria control and prevention, the disease remains highly endemic throughout Uganda, where it accounted for 32.1% of all reported hospital outpatient visits and 26.2% of hospital admissions in 2023 [2]. As Africa’s largest refugee-hosting country, Uganda housed approximately 1.6 million refugees as of early 2024, primarily from South Sudan and the Democratic Republic of the Congo [3]. Among refugee populations, malaria represents an even greater health burden, accounting for nearly half (43%) of reported morbidity [4, 5] and a prevalence rate of 35.1% for children under 5 years mainly because of limited access to preventative measures like insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS), and challenges with healthcare access and treatment [6].

In alignment with WHO priorities, Uganda centres vector control as a key malaria prevention measure and has consequently mainstreamed malaria vector control in several major health policies [7–9]. Mass and routine ITN distribution and focused IRS make up the backbone of Uganda’s vector control strategy [10–13], including in refugee settlement areas like Bidibidi. In Yumbe District, where the Bidibidi refugee settlement is located, mass ITN campaigns have been conducted every three years, with the most recent campaign completed in 2023. During the 2023 national campaign, 28.2 million ITNs were distributed to 11,070 parishes in 136 districts [14]. In addition, ITNs are continuously distributed through health facilities, to pregnant women attending their first antenatal care visit and children receiving their third dose of DPT-HepB-Hib (diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis (whooping cough), hepatitis B, and Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) [10, 11]. While studies have shown the effectiveness of using ITNs in chronic humanitarian settings, they have also highlighted a need to evaluate and adopt new interventions for malaria control in these contexts, particularly in acute emergency settings [15]. In addition to ITNs, the Ugandan government, in partnership with the U.S. government's President’s Malaria Initiative (PMI), has implemented IRS since 2009. Recently, during the 2022–2024 spray campaigns, IRS interventions have focused on the highly endemic areas in northern and eastern Uganda, including Yumbe [16]. Notably, in 2025, the World Health Organization issued a conditional recommendation for spatial repellents as a complementary vector control tool alongside ITNs in areas with ongoing malaria transmission [9]. While evidence gaps still remain about their potential to protect people outdoors or in humanitarian emergencies [17], spatial repellents, like the Mosquito Shield™ evaluated under the broader Advancing Evidence for the Global Implementation of Spatial Repellents (AEGIS) project under which this study was conducted [18], have been identified as a potential complementary tool which may be suitable for emergency and humanitarian settings [15, 19].

Beyond the Government of Uganda’s directly implemented mosquito control efforts, mosquito control products (MCPs) are available for purchase through the private market, including but not limited to mosquito coils, mosquito sticks, aerosol sprays, topical repellents, mosquito traps, and mosquito killer bulbs. Retail outlets are known to be key suppliers of MCPs in many countries [20–24], and anecdotal evidence from Uganda indicates that there has been a general increase in private sector sales of MCPs, especially from pharmacies and retail shops [10]. However, previous studies examining the role of Uganda’s private sector in malaria control have largely focused on retail sales of therapeutics and diagnostics rather than MCPs [25–28]. Data specific to Uganda’s refugee settings are even more limited.

To generate evidence related to retail sales of MCPs in the refugee setting of Uganda, we conducted a retail audit within the Bidibidi refugee settlement area in Northern Uganda. This retail audit was conducted as part of the AEGIS programme and aimed to understand the current types of MCPs available and purchased through retail outlets; the channels through which the MCPs are obtained; customer preferences as perceived by respondents; and the factors that influence demand for MCPs in refugee settlements in Uganda. Better understanding the landscape of MCPs within humanitarian contexts could guide future implementation of new vector control interventions, such as spatial repellents.

Methods

Study site and population

The retail audit was conducted in the Bidibidi refugee settlement in Yumbe district in northwestern Uganda from May to July 2024. This settlement borders South Sudan and the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) and is one of the largest refugee settlements operating globally [29, 30]. Established in August 2016, Bidibidi spans over 800 square kilometers and is home to over 200,000 refugees as of January 2025 with majority of the population being women and children [31]. The settlement consists primarily of semi-permanent structures made from local materials, such as clay bricks and grass thatched roofs [29]. Structures typically have open eaves and often lack window coverings, which have been identified elsewhere as key mosquito entry points to indoor spaces [32, 33]. The settlement is organized into five zones, each divided into clusters and further subdivided villages, with a refugee governance structure mirroring Uganda's local council structure [34, 35]. For this study, data were collected from Zones 1–4 and nearby host communities. "Host communities" in this context are the local populations within the area where refugees have been settled which neighbor the refugees and share community resources and amenities. Crop production is the main source of livelihoods for both refugees and host communities. Bidibidi typically experiences two rainy seasons—March–June and August–November– although rainfall is becoming more difficult to predict [36]. The area receives a total annual average rainfall of about 800 mm and 1200 mm, with average temperatures ranging from 20 °C to 35 °C [37].

Core mosquito control interventions, including ITNs and IRS, are the main free mosquito control interventions implemented by the government of Uganda. The most recent mass ITN distribution was conducted in December 2023, prior to data collection for this study. This distribution covered an average of 75% of households in Yumbe District, falling short of the targeted coverage rate of 95% [38]. To be considered “covered,” a household received two ITNs for the entire household’s use. Assuming one ITN protects two individuals, when taking into consideration the average household size of 7 individuals in Bidibidi settlement [39], there likely remains a gap in individual ITN coverage. IRS has been implemented annually in three consecutive rounds since 2022, with an administrative coverage of 95.8% for the most recent IRS campaign conducted in December 2024 [40].

Study procedures

The retail audit considered retail spaces within the 61 refugee and host villages in Zones 1–4 that had been mapped out for the parent AEGIS Uganda operational research. The audit consisted of two key steps: the mapping phase and the survey phase. During mapping, study enumerators recorded GPS coordinates for each outlet in 61 villages throughout Zones 1–4, plus shop name, names and telephone numbers of the shop contact, whether or not the outlet was currently stocking or had previously stocked MCPs, and, if they were not currently stocking MCPs, if they would in the future. Outlets were categorized as retail shops, drug shops/pharmacies, market stalls, market stools, wholesale shops, or other. For the purposes of this retail audit, “drug shop” and “pharmacy” were used interchangeably: shops within the study area sold both over-the-counter items and prescriptions, such as anti-malarial medications. Market “stalls” were defined as larger, more permanent stands within market areas. Market “stools” were defined as smaller, semi-permanent stands within or near market areas or roadsides that are typically set up in the same places but removed at the end of the day. Wholesale outlets were not eligible to be included in the survey, as the primary focus of the study was to identify outlets that provide services directly to the final consumer.

From the mapping, a representative sample of eligible retail outlets in the study area was drawn for inclusion in the survey phase, via stratified random sampling by zone. An outlet was eligible to participate in the survey if it currently sold an MCP or had sold an MCP within the previous six months, as determined through the outlet mapping. A sample of 250 retail outlets was targeted for the survey based on precedent from related audits in Kenya and Sri Lanka, where this number was sufficient to achieve saturation [21, 41].

Data collection

In the mapping phase, information gathered included the name and GPS coordinates of each outlet, its zone and village, and the type of retail outlet to understand geographic distribution and market coverage. Additional data captured whether the outlet currently sells, has sold in the past six months, or intends to sell mosquito control products in the future, along with reasons for their intentions. Contact details such as the name, position, and phone number of the contact person at the shop were also recorded.

In the survey phase, a trained team of enumerators administered a structured questionnaire consisting of closed- and open-ended questions uploaded on an electronic tablet. The survey collected detailed information to understand the retail dynamics, sales patterns, and supply chain of MCPs. The survey captured the role of the person interviewed and the types of products sold in each shop to establish context. It explored whether outlets currently sell MCPs, which types they stock, how long they have sold them, and which products customers purchase most frequently. Seasonal trends were assessed by asking about periods when sales surge or decline and the reasons behind these patterns. To understand market competition and consumer behavior, respondents were asked about alternative MCPs available outside their shop, how consumers obtain them, and whether customers buy MCPs intentionally or incidentally while shopping for other items. For shops not currently selling such products, questions focused on past sales, reasons for discontinuation, and types of products previously sold. The survey also gathered supply chain and business information, including stock purchasing frequency, last restocking date, quantities purchased, sales volumes, remaining stock, profit perceptions, and future sales intentions. Finally, respondents were asked about procurement practices—how products are obtained, delivery or pickup sources, payment arrangements, and supplier details—to map distribution networks and identify barriers or opportunities in product availability and sales.

The study enumerators were fluent in English and the local languages of Lugbara Ti and Juba Arabic, and they also had previous experience in qualitative and quantitative data collection. Enumerators were trained on the consent process, research ethics, data collection instruments, and data management before beginning data collection.

After pre-testing the survey with 38 retail outlets not included in the study, the team clarified phrasing and survey flow to finalize the questionnaire. Study documents (i.e., informed consent forms and questionnaires) were available in English, Lugbara Ti, and Juba Arabic; each respondent indicated which language they preferred. Only individuals who were 18 years and older and who had knowledge of the retail outlets’ sales (e.g., owners, managers, supporting family) were eligible to participate in the survey. To be interviewed, respondents for the survey indicated that they were familiar with decision-making for the outlet; owners, spouses, and managers were prioritized for participation.

This study was approved by the Mildmay Ethics Review Committee, the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health Institutional Review Board, and the WHO Ethics Review Committee.

Data management and analysis

Both mapping and survey data were captured via CommCare (v. 2.53.1, Dimagi, Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA) and exported to Microsoft Excel (v. 2501, Microsoft 365). Data were cleaned and validated, and if qualitative data were collected in Juba Arabic or Lugbara Ti, they were translated to English. All analyses were conducted in Excel, including descriptive analysis of frequency counts and percentages and weighted average rank (sum of item rank positions ÷ frequency of item mentions) to determine consumer preferences of MCPs based on retailer perceptions. To analyse qualitative data, four team members created codes based on the survey questions and responses; the codes included reasons for sales surges and dips, factors affecting demand, and rationale for why a seller intended to continue or had stopped selling MCPs. A subset (15%) of responses were coded by four team members to identify and resolve any differences in coding and to finalize the codebook. After finalizing the codebook, the team members coded the full set of qualitative data. Codes were then collated into themes for inclusion in this report.

Results

Mapping and survey: characteristics of respondents interviewed

A total of 828 retail outlets were mapped. Following the mapping, 252 retail outlets were surveyed (Fig. 1, Table 1). The types of retail outlets listed and surveyed in all zones differed by location, with most outlets being located in the trading or market centers. Most outlets stocked a limited range of food items (bread, beans, cooking oil, salt, sugar, tea leaves, food spices, tomatoes, onions), and basic necessities (soap, jerricans, cell phones, headphones, radio receivers, matches, toilet paper, soft-drinks, biscuits, and small-denomination mobile phone air-time cards).

Outlets during mapping and survey that currently sold, previously sold, or would sell MCPs in the future

In the retail audit survey, 165 (65.4%) of the 252 outlets surveyed were currently selling MCPs. Of these, more than three quarters were retail shops, with the remaining outlets including market stalls (n = 15; 9.1%), market stools (n = 6; 3.6%), and drug shops and others represented (n = 8; 4.8%) each. Overall, 87 outlets had previously sold MCPs, with retail shops again representing slightly more than half of this group (n = 53; 60.9%). Other outlets included market stalls (n = 8; 9.2%), market stools (n = 8; 9.2%), and drug shops (n = 11; 12.6%). “Other” had smaller representation at (n = 7; 8.1%) among those who had previously sold MCPs.

Mapping: reasons for selling MCPs in the future

Participants were asked to share what factors influenced their willingness to sell MCPs in the future using open-ended questions. Most respondents pointed to capital as a key factor influencing their decision to sell MCPs in the future. Many expressed willingness to do so once their capital base expands.

The second most common reason (next to capital) was the perceived usefulness of MCPs, particularly in relation to malaria prevention. These respondents highlighted the health benefits and social importance of the products, noting that selling MCPs could contribute to community health goals. Below is one response:

“I will sell the mosquito control products because people can easily access, buy, and use the mosquito control products to control the spread of malaria” Respondent, Refugee community, Owner, Retail shop.

Other reasons mentioned as factors influencing the future sale of MCPs included perceived consumer demand for MCPs—which some respondents tied to the rainy season—and perceived profitability of selling MCPs. For example, respondents noted that the rainy season brought a surge in malaria cases, creating a temporary spike in demand for MCPs; they also noted the opposite for the dry season.

“During the rainy periods breeding of mosquitoes is high thus demand for mosquito control products” Respondent, Host community, Salesperson, Market stall.

Despite a reported intent to stock MCPs in the future, many respondents indicated that the initial investment required to purchase MCPs and the ongoing operational costs posed a significant challenge.

“If I am able to get enough capital to put in the business and bring a variety of items for sale, I will be able to bring the mosquito control products” Respondent, Host community, Owner, Retail shop.

Other respondents mentioned that they needed to first secure a reliable supply chain for MCPs.

“Currently the supplier who used to bring the mosquito net no longer comes around and I do not have his contact to ask why he is not coming nowadays. He used to come every month but since February this year he has not come around.” Respondent, Refugee Community, Owner, Market stall.

Some respondents also mentioned that they might be willing to sell MCPs in the future if they had better knowledge and training about MCPs to feel more confident in their ability to sell the products. Other outlets were interested in selling MCPs to expand their product range. By adding MCPs to their inventory, they reported a perception that diversification could enable them to reach a broader customer base.

Mapping: reasons for not selling MCPs in the future

Respondents not willing to sell MCPs in the future identified several factors. The primary reason was a lack of capital. Another concern for some respondents was the current scope of their business, as these focused on selling specific items and MCPs were not an option to be considered. For example, several of the outlets primarily selling food products that were unwilling to sell MCPs commonly reported concern that mixing MCPs with food items could cause contamination, negatively impact their brand, or interfere with their core business of food sales.

“I sell food products only, what if I start selling these [mosquito control] products and they contaminate the food items I am selling?” Respondent, Refugee community, Owner, Retail shop

Some respondents identified lack of knowledge as a reason for not stocking MCPs in the future: outlets that reported not being familiar with MCPs or how they functioned reported hesitancy in selling them. These respondents requested further support to understand MCP uses, benefits, and marketing strategies.

A major barrier to stocking MCPs was perceived profitability, and how demand, seasonality, consumer purchasing power, and competition influence the economics surrounding this. Perceived limited demand for MCPs was identified by some respondents as a reason they were not willing to sell MCPs in the future, as they reported there was not enough customer interest to justify stocking the products. Additionally, a few outlets indicated concern that malaria seasonality could affect sales (i.e., that sales would increase during the rainy season but decrease in the dry season), and that the product might not generate a consistent profit throughout the year. Others identified a lack of consumer purchasing power that was perceived to limit profitability of MCPs. Some respondents reported concern about competition from other outlets or organizations providing similar products; a few respondents specifically identified government vector control strategies (i.e., ITN distributions and IRS campaigns) as factors that reduced consumer demand for MCPs purchased via retail spaces. With these free alternatives readily available, respondents indicated concern that there might not be enough consumer demand to justify selling MCPs.

“Not enough market for mosquito control products and low demand because of free net distribution by the government” Respondent, Refugee community, Salesperson, Market stall

Other factors reported to reduce respondents' willingness to sell MCPs included perceived challenges related to the supply chain. Respondents identified difficulties in ensuring steady availability of products and avoiding stockouts as barriers to committing to selling MCPs.

Survey: inventory and sales trends for MCPs

Of the 165 surveyed outlets selling MCPs on the day of the survey, 93 (56.4%) stocked incense sticks with insecticide (Table 3). Adult ITNs were stocked in 48 outlets (29.1%). Incense sticks without insecticide were stocked or sold in 45 outlets (27.3%). Other available but less commonly stocked MCPs included mosquito coils, adult UTNs, topical repellents, aerosols, child ITNs, and child UTNs. 65 (39.4%) outlets sold 2 or more MCPs on the day of the survey, with the most commonly paired items being incense sticks with insecticide (n = 22) and mosquito coils and incense sticks with insecticide and adult ITNs (n = 15).

When asked to rank which MCPs they perceived as selling most frequently in other shops, respondents identified incense sticks followed by adult ITNs and mosquito coils; the MCPs perceived to be purchased least frequently were child UTNs and child ITNs (Table 3).

Survey: restocking practices

Respondents reported procuring nearly all MCPs at least monthly (Table 4): incense sticks with or without insecticide, mosquito coils, topical repellents, and aerosol insecticide sprays were typically replenished every month, with a number of respondents restocking the MCPs every two weeks or as often as weekly. Some MCPs, including adult ITNs and MCPs classified under “Other,” were typically restocked after a couple of months (> a month). When asked where MCPs were acquired, most respondents reported picking up MCPs from wholesale shops (rather than having these items delivered). MCPs were typically picked up or delivered from wholesale shops located nearby in the settlement area or in Yumbe town, with a few also sourcing from nearby districts such as Koboko, or Ugandan cities further away, like Arua or Kampala, or locations in South Sudan. A few respondents noted that they had procured ITNs informally from community members, who had received the ITNs from the mass distribution. Payments for nearly all items were made upfront in cash.

Survey: profit adequacy and future stocking intentions

Most respondents who stocked incense sticks with insecticide (n = 65; 69.9%) reported that the profits were adequate (Table 5). In addition, respondents selling other MCPs such as incense sticks without insecticide, adult ITNs, topical repellents, child ITNs, and child UTNs indicated that the profit margins were adequate. Most respondents stocking incense sticks with insecticide (n = 90; 96.8%) expressed their intention to continue stocking these products. Similarly, nearly all the respondents dealing with other MCPs, such as adult ITNs and topical repellents, planned to continue stocking these items in the future.

Period and seasonal variation in sales for MCPs

The vast majority of outlets (n = 154; 93.3%) reported a surge in MCP sales, predominantly in the months of March, April, August, and September, which align with the long and short rainy seasons in Yumbe. Many respondents also specifically linked the rainy season—and sales surge—with an increase in the number of mosquitoes during these months. Other reasons for increased sales included other season-related factors, such as an increase in vegetation, long grass, and stagnant water, which respondents identified as breeding places for mosquitoes.

Similarly, most outlets (n = 148; 89.7%) reported that they experienced a dip in MCP sales, most notably in January, February, May, November and December, which respondents linked with the dry season and fewer mosquitoes. Respondents mainly attributed this reduction to high temperatures, a lack of bush areas, and no stagnant water, which they reported reduced mosquito breeding. Respondents also identified vector control measures from the government as periodic factors leading to a sales dip. Most specifically mentioned the free ITN distribution and IRS campaigns which took place a few months prior to the survey, in December 2023.

Discussion

This Uganda retail audit assessed the availability, supply chain, and factors influencing sales trends and demand for MCPs within Bidibidi refugee settlement, Yumbe District in Uganda. Although the Mosquito Shield™ spatial repellent is not presently intended for retail sale, understanding the types of MCPs that are already being used within the existing retail market for MCPs can help guide introduction and product positioning of new MCPs, including spatial repellents. This study adds to the relative paucity of information available on retail sales of MCPs, especially within humanitarian settings.

Overall, the data highlighted that retail outlets sell a considerable variety of MCPs, and that retail shops play a notable role in the sale of MCPs in this refugee settlement context. This exploration of retail outlets’ contributions to vector control echoes existing literature that has highlighted the important role of the retail sector in malaria and vector control across various settings [25, 42].

Incense sticks with insecticide were the most frequently stocked or sold MCP; this was identified by respondents as their own top-selling MCP as well as their perception of what others sold most commonly. Although there are limited efficacy data available on the use of incense sticks for vector control, the popularity of incense sticks in this context echoes findings from Yadav et al. [43], who found that incense sticks (not necessarily those with insecticide) are often preferred because they are relatively affordable, related to religious beliefs, have a pleasant smell, easy to use, and culturally familiar. These results also parallel those from another retail audit conducted in Busia, Kenya as part of the larger AEGIS program that identified mosquito coils, which employ a similar delivery format to reduce vector-human contact (i.e., are a type of spatial repellent that is burned, releasing chemicals to affect mosquito behavior and provide protection outside of when core vector control tools are in use), as the most predominant product available [21]. The predominance of insecticide-treated incense sticks rather than coils in this context may be due to logistics—respondents reported supply chains through neighboring South Sudan, and incense is notably a major internationally-traded commercial item produced along the border of Sudan and South Sudan [44]. The popularity may also be due to cultural preference, as respondents often reported that incense sticks (insecticide-treated or not) were burned throughout the year for cultural purposes or for a good scent.

Though less commonly stocked, the availability of other MCPs, such as mosquito coils, adult UTNs, topical repellents, aerosols, and child-specific ITNs and UTNs, highlights a diverse range of products available to consumers and reflects what others have noted as the commercial and public health value of these products within humanitarian settings [45]. This is emphasized by the reported “adequate” profitability of MCPs by most respondents.

The dominance of spatial repellent-type products—incense sticks with or without insecticide and mosquito coils—underscores the potential for introducing other MCPs which provide protection in similar spaces and settings (i.e., outside of the protection of core vector control interventions). This adds to the findings from other studies under the AEGIS Kenya project, in which community members spoke to the utility of spatial repellents for use in similar spaces, although this work is yet to be published. That consumers are prioritizing purchasing these goods despite a very limited discretionary income [46] suggests a preference or need for these MCPs. Additionally, the popularity of these products could relate to the relative cost of the MCPs, as items providing protection for a shorter time such as incense sticks and mosquito coils require a smaller initial investment than those such as ITNs.

The steady restocking practices observed in this study—with most MCPs being restocked more often than monthly—reinforces the idea that MCPs are not only profitable but also frequently purchased despite high distribution of MCPs from government vector control programs (i.e., ITNs and IRS). These results aligned with previous research, demonstrated the profitability of and demand for complementary vector control products in retail markets [21]. When asked if they would continue stocking MCPs in the future, nearly all respondents in this study responded positively. Even among respondents not currently selling MCPs, a large percentage indicated interest in selling them in the future. The steady restocking practices, combined with adequate profit margins and a reported commitment to or interest in stocking MCPs in the future, suggest that MCPs do and will continue to play a crucial role in the local retail landscape.

While interest in selling MCPs in the future is high, respondents highlighted key barriers to stocking or selling MCPs. The responses highlight that available capital is a significant factor, with many respondents stocking MCPs with a relatively low unit cost, such as incense sticks, and fewer respondents stocking MCPs with a higher cost, such as ITNs or aerosol sprays. Beyond having sufficient capital, the scope of the business itself was also a factor that influenced a participant’s decision to stock MCPs. Participants also identified a lack of knowledge about MCPs and a challenging supply chain (e.g., frequent stock-outs, long distances to suppliers). This echoes previous research, which determined main barriers for refugees in Uganda running general retail stores being limited capital—financial, human, natural, physical, or social—and limited access to markets [46]. However, many participants identified Yumbe town (approximately 15 km west of Bidibidi refugee settlement) or villages within the settlement as their primary source of their wholesale goods. The central role of Yumbe town as a wholesale distribution hub is particularly noteworthy. Yumbe town’s central position may reduce costs and maximize product availability for respondents when stockouts and transportation barriers can be overcome. This finding is consistent with supply chain models that emphasize the role of a central hub for regenerative supply chains [47].

Seasonality of sales was the most identified factor affecting demand or sales of MCPs, both as a barrier during the dry season or when government programs were recent and as an enabler in the rainy season or after protection from government provided tools waned. This seasonality in sales itself was identified as a barrier to a few participants, who expressed hesitancy in stocking MCPs when sales would not be constant throughout the year. Enabling factors identified by respondents included a sense of usefulness or community good, providing protection to the community by supplying MCPs appears to be a strong motivator, and training or knowledge about the products. This desire for training was also expressed in previous research on livelihoods within refugee settlements in Uganda, which reported both a desire for entrepreneurship and high interest in training and skills development for non-crop production activities [48]. Future introductions of vector control products in similar contexts should consider designing community engagement activities which incorporate the social good of MCPs and training events on their storage, use, and benefit for maximum impact, especially if engaging the private sector.

Study limitations

The study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. Firstly, the cross-sectional design provides a snapshot of the market at a point in time, which limits the ability to estimate long-term trends or changes in consumer behavior and retailer practices. While the availability of new products and the fact that respondents are stocking them suggest potential for market growth, responses requesting capital, training, and other forms of support may have been influenced by the implementing organization’s presence in Yumbe for over ten years and its role in humanitarian services. ITN availability may have been overrepresented at this point in time, as the audit was conducted approximately six months after a mass ITN distribution campaign. While it is unknown how many government-distributed ITNs may have reached the retail market, this reflects a known risk of product loss or diversion associated with mass distribution campaigns. Relatedly the availability of insecticide sticks without insecticide may have been underreported in the audit, as participants themselves were asked to identify the MCPs in stock, which may have resulted in different interpretations of what was considered to be an MCP (e.g., an incense stick without insecticide could be viewed as either an MCP or not, depending on the individual). Additionally, the study did not specifically inquire about participants' willingness to adopt or promote Mosquito Shield™ or capture information on non-purchased methods, such as the use of lemongrass and other local plants known to be used for mosquito control. The survey was unable to determine product pricing or sales volumes for MCPs and also did not capture this data on other common household products, which limits our ability to assess the relative market share or economic significance of MCPs. As the study primarily focused on the retail space, further research is needed to explore the vector control landscape and market dynamics more comprehensively, including consumer perspectives, post-distribution monitoring, alternative products or practices in use, and the potential impact of new products such as Mosquito Shield™ being introduced into the market.

Conclusions

The availability, selection, and perceived profitability of MCPs suggests that there is a viable market for MCPs in the Bidibidi refugee settlement area. This indicates that demand exists for MCPs that supplement the protection provided by ITNs and IRS, and that these products appear to be purchased even within communities that have very limited discretionary income. Incense sticks with insecticide were the most commonly sold MCP, likely due to a combination of stable logistics, affordability, and cultural relevance. The spectrum of other products available, including incense sticks without insecticide, adult and child ITNs, mosquito coils, adult and child UTNs, and topical repellents, indicate a healthy market for MCPs and, potentially, a gap in protection or preference that consumers are looking to fill. Future research should consider consumer purchasing trends and preferences to validate retailer perspectives and better guide introduction of new vector control tools in similar contexts.
