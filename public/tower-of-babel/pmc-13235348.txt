Effectiveness of buffered and non-buffered lignocaine and articaine in children aged 6–12 years undergoing invasive dental procedures: A randomized clinical trial
Authors: Shweta Kajjari, Akanksha Prakash Dumbare, Sharvari Itankar, Vidyavathi Patil, Shivayogi M Hugar, Snehal Kumthekar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective pain control is fundamental to successful pediatric dental care. However, intraoral injections cause significant anxiety, due to burning sensations from the acidic pH of anesthetic solutions. Buffering with sodium bicarbonate raises pH closer to physiological levels, reducing pain and accelerating anesthesia onset.AimTo evaluate the effectiveness of buffered and non-buffered lignocaine and articaine in children aged 6 to 12 years undergoing invasive dental procedures in terms of pain perception on injection, onset of action, and duration of action.Materials and methodsThis was an in vivo, four-arm, concurrent, randomized clinical trial. Eighty healthy children requiring buccal and palatal infiltration meeting inclusion criteria were enrolled and randomized into four groups (n = 20) namely, Group I (2% Non-buffered Lignocaine), Group II (4% Non-buffered Articaine), Group III (2% Buffered Lignocaine) and Group IV (4% Buffered Articaine). Clinical parameters evaluated included pain perception (Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale and Sound–Eye–Motor scale), onset of anesthesia and duration of action.ResultBuffered local anesthetic groups demonstrated significantly lower subjective and objective pain scores compared to non-buffered groups. Buffered articaine group showed significantly lower pain perception, the fastest onset of anesthesia, and the longest duration of anesthetic effect followed by buffered lignocaine.ConclusionAmong the four groups evaluated, buffered articaine demonstrated consistently lower subjective and objective pain scores, more rapid onset of anesthesia, and prolonged duration of action. These findings indicate that buffering, particularly when used in combination with articaine, enhances anesthetic efficacy and improves patient comfort during pediatric dental procedures.Keywords: Buffered articaine, Buffered lignocaine, Local anesthesia, Pediatric dentistry

Introduction

Effective pain control is crucial for ensuring successful procedures and optimal patient management in pediatric dentistry.1 Various adjuncts such as distraction techniques, anxiolytic medications, and sedative agents can assist in managing pain during procedures, but local anesthesia continues to be the primary method for pain prevention and control.2

Among local anesthetic agents used in dentistry, lidocaine has traditionally been considered the gold standard. In recent years, 4% articaine has become widely favored for its superior ability to diffuse through both soft and hard tissues.3,4 Its proven efficacy and favorable safety profile make it a preferred agent for dental procedures requiring profound anesthesia, such as pulp therapy and extractions.3

However, intraoral injections are considered one of the most anxiety-inducing experiences for patients, especially children in the dental environment.2 The incorporation of a vasoconstrictor, most commonly epinephrine, into local anesthetic solutions is intended to enhance the duration of anesthesia by decreasing local blood perfusion and limiting systemic diffusion from the site of administration. To ensure chemical stability and prolong the shelf life of the vasoconstrictor, these anesthetic formulations are maintained at an acidic pH, typically around 3.5 to 4.5. However, the acidic nature of the solution is responsible for the burning or stinging sensation frequently reported by patients during injection, which is particularly significant in pediatric patients and may negatively influence behavior, cooperation, and overall treatment acceptance.6 Consequently, there has been growing interest in modifying local anesthetic solutions to improve patient comfort without compromising efficacy. One such approach is buffering with sodium bicarbonate, which increases the pH of local anesthetic solution.5,6 It brings it closer to the body's physiological pH. Thus, causes less burning and stinging sensation. It has shown better efficacy in various studies. Several studies have assessed the efficacy of buffered local anesthetics in adult populations. Evidence in pediatric patients remains limited, despite differences in pain perception and tissue response in pediatric patients. Furthermore, no previous study has simultaneously compared buffered and non-buffered formulations of both articaine and lignocaine within a single study design.

Therefore, this randomized clinical trial was aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of buffered and non-buffered lignocaine and articaine in children undergoing invasive dental procedures in terms of pain perception on injection, onset of action, and duration of action.

This was an in vivo, four arm, concurrent, randomized clinical study carried out over a duration of six months (September 2025 to February 2026) on the patients reporting to the outpatient Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry. Preparation of buffered lignocaine

•Buffered lignocaine was prepared immediately prior to administration under aseptic conditions. Buffering was achieved by combining sodium bicarbonate with lignocaine in a 1:10 vol ratio. A commercially available 30-mL vial of 2% lignocaine hydrochloride containing 1:200,000 epinephrine (Neon Laboratories Ltd., India) was used. To this vial, 3 mL of 8.4% sodium bicarbonate solution (Neon Laboratories Ltd., India) was added slowly. Preparation of buffered articaine

•The buffered articaine solution was prepared immediately before use under sterile conditions. From a standard 1.8 mL dental cartridge of 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine (Septanest®, Septodont, France), 0.18 mL of the anesthetic solution was withdrawn using an insulin syringe. An equivalent volume (0.18 mL) of 8.4% sodium bicarbonate solution (SODAC®, Neon Uttaranchal Biotech Ltd., India) was then introduced into the cartridge. Following replacement, the cartridge was gently inverted several times to achieve adequate mixing of the buffered solution prior to administration.8

Procedure

The planned procedure was explained to both the patients and their parents or guardians by the principal investigator. A detailed case history was subsequently recorded.

A skin prick test with the allotted anesthetic agent by a subcutaneous injection on the forearm was performed. Children who tested negative for the allergy test were included in the study. After drying the site of intraoral injection using sterile gauze, approximately 0.2 mL of topical anesthetic was applied. After a minute buccal infiltration was performed by administering 1.5 mL of the study solution and palatal infiltration using 0.2–0.3 mL of the local anesthetic solution. The injection rate was standardized at approximately 1 mL/min across all groups.

Evaluation of pain

Measurement of onset of action

The onset of action (recorded in minutes) was measured in a standardized manner from the time of needle insertion to the appearance of subjective and objective symptoms of anesthesia.

•Subjective symptoms were checked by asking the child for feeling of numbness or tingling sensation.

Duration of anesthesia

The duration of action was assessed at the subsequent visit by recording the time at which the patient first experienced pain or the time at which a rescue analgesic was consumed, whichever occurred earlier.

Following anesthesia and outcome assessment, the planned dental procedure was completed under standard operating protocols.

Statistical analysis

The results obtained were tabulated in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Corp.) and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 22.0, Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive analysis was conducted on the data, where frequency distributions, percentages, mean and standard deviation were computed. The Chi-square test was used to evaluate associations between categorical variables. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since Wong Baker Faces Pain Scale and Sound–Eye–Motor scores were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were applied using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Mann–Whitney U test for pairwise comparisons. Variables with normal distribution (subjective onset time, objective onset time, and duration of anesthesia) were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The study sample consisted of a total of 80 participants, distributed equally among four groups (n = 20 each). All infiltration procedures were uneventful and well tolerated by the participants, with no reported complications. Overall, the study population consisted of 46 males (57.5%) and 34 females (42.5%). Normality assessment using the Shapiro–Wilk test demonstrated that Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale scores and SEM scores did not follow a normal distribution across the four groups (p < 0.05). Therefore, non-parametric statistical tests were performed for these variables. Subjective onset time, objective onset time, and duration of action followed normal distribution (p > 0.05) and parametric tests were performed.

Pain upon injection

Intergroup comparison of subjective pain scores assessed using the Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale demonstrated a statistically significant difference when analyzed with the Kruskal–Wallis test (p = 0.0055).

Pairwise analysis of subjective pain scores using the Mann–Whitney U test indicated significantly lower pain scores in the buffered lignocaine group compared with the lignocaine group (p = 0.0411). A more pronounced reduction in pain was seen in the buffered articaine group relative to the lignocaine group (p = 0.0008). In addition, pain scores in the buffered articaine group were significantly lower than those in the articaine group (p = 0.0256). No significant differences were found between lignocaine and articaine (p = 0.1478), between articaine and buffered lignocaine (p = 0.5162), or between buffered lignocaine and buffered articaine (p = 0.1478) (Table 1).

However, no statistically significant difference was observed among the remaining comparisons (Table 3).

Discussion

Pain during local anesthetic injection is an anxiety provoking aspect of dental treatment for children. The acidic pH (approximately 3.5–4.5) of commercially available local anesthetic solutions is a major contributor to the burning or stinging sensation experienced during injection. The principle of buffering local anesthetic solutions is derived from the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation. Buffering with sodium bicarbonate increases the pH approximately to 7.2. Buffering reduces the hydrogen ion concentration in the tissues and increases the number of the non-ionized base form of the anesthetic. This enhances membrane permeability and nerve penetration, leading to reduced pain perception.11 The overall study population comprised 46 males (57.5%) and 34 females (42.5%), and no attempt was made to balance the groups with respect to gender.

The findings of the present study demonstrated that buffering local anesthetic solutions significantly enhanced their clinical performance, particularly in the case of 4% articaine. Buffered articaine consistently showed superior results across all evaluated parameters, including reduced subjective and objective pain scores, faster onset of anesthesia, and prolonged duration of anesthetic action when compared to three other groups.

In the present study, pain assessment both subjectively and objectively revealed significantly lower pain scores in the buffered articaine group. These findings are in accordance with those reported by Amorim et al.,12 who observed reduced injection pain with buffered articaine during maxillary infiltrations. Similarly, Dhake et al.6 reported decreased pain perception with buffered articaine in children undergoing extraction of primary maxillary molars. However, some previous studies have reported contradictory findings, particularly when buffered anesthetics were used for mandibular blocks or in adult populations. These variations may be due to differences in injection technique, anatomical site, bone density, and age-related pain perception. The maxilla, being more porous and vascular than the mandible, allows easier diffusion of anesthetic solutions, which may further enhance the benefits of buffering during maxillary infiltrations.

A significantly faster onset of anesthesia was observed in the buffered articaine group, both subjectively and objectively. This finding can be explained by the increased availability of the non-ionized form of articaine following buffering, allowing quicker diffusion through nerve membranes. While articaine is already known for its superior diffusibility due to its thiophene ring structure, buffering appears to further potentiate this property, resulting in a clinically meaningful reduction in onset time.

Buffered articaine group showed significantly prolonged duration of anesthesia. This may be attributed to more efficient nerve penetration and sustained anesthetic effect due to improved pharmacodynamic properties following buffering. Prolonged duration of anesthesia is particularly beneficial in pediatric dentistry, as it ensures adequate pain control throughout the procedure and reduces the likelihood of intraoperative discomfort.

The findings of our study are consistent with evidence from the systematic review by Tirupathi et al.,5 which demonstrated that sodium bicarbonate buffered local anesthetic solutions significantly reduce pain on injection and hasten the onset of action when compared with non-buffered formulations. The review further highlighted that articaine benefits more from buffering than lignocaine due to its superior tissue diffusibility, particularly during maxillary infiltrations. These observations strongly support the present results, wherein buffered articaine showed the lowest pain scores, fastest onset, and longest duration of anesthesia.

The present study possesses several methodological strengths that enhance the validity and clinical relevance of its findings. The use of a randomized clinical trial design with four parallel groups minimizes selection bias and allows for a robust comparison between buffered and non-buffered formulations of both lignocaine and articaine within a single study framework. Standardization of the clinical protocol, including uniform injection technique, controlled rate of administration, and consistent preparation of buffered solutions, helped reduce operator-dependent variability. Furthermore, both subjective (Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale) and objective (Sound–Eye–Motor scale) measures of pain assessment strengthens the reliability of pain evaluation. The use of a blinded evaluator further reduces the risk of assessment bias.

Despite the favorable outcomes, certain practical limitations of buffered anesthetic solutions must be acknowledged. The preparation of buffered anesthetics requires increased time, precision, and aseptic handling, which may be challenging in demanding clinical environments. Furthermore, buffered solutions have shorter shelf life compared to commercially available formulations, requiring their use immediately following preparation.

The present study was limited by its relatively short duration and single-center design. Additionally, children with special health care needs and those presenting with acute infections were excluded, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. The study employed a single-blind design, and operator blinding was not feasible, which may introduce potential performance bias. Future studies involving larger sample sizes, and inclusion of diverse clinical scenarios such as inflamed tissues or different injection techniques are recommended to further validate the benefits of buffered anesthetic solutions in pediatric dentistry.

Conclusion

Buffered local anesthetic formulations provided better efficacy compared to non-buffered solutions in pediatric maxillary molar infiltrations. Among the four groups, buffered articaine consistently resulted in lower subjective and objective pain scores, faster onset of anesthesia, and longer duration of action. These findings suggest that buffering, particularly when combined with articaine, enhances anesthetic efficacy and patient comfort in pediatric dental procedures.
