Flexibility and Degeneracy around a theorem of Thurston
Alexander Nolte

Abstract
. We give two flexible and degenerate constructions related to a theorem of Thurston. First, we produce geodesic segments for Thurston’s asymmetric metric on Teichmüller space 𝒯 ⁡ ( S g ) \mathcal{T}(S_{g}) that remain geodesics after adding arbitrary ε \varepsilon -Lipschitz noise to all but one Fenchel-Nielsen coordinate. Then, for all 2 < n ≤ 3 ​ g − 3 2<n\leq 3g-3 we construct open sets in 𝒯 ​ ( S g ) n \mathcal{T}(S_{g})^{n} for which the limit cones of the corresponding representations in PSL 2 ​ ( ℝ ) n \mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{n} are cones over explicit finite-sided polyhedra. Each construction is as degenerate as possible and has applications to the basic structure and local non-rigidity of the involved objects.



In this paper, we identify and study degenerate phenomena in two constructions that compare lengths of geodesics in hyperbolic surfaces.
The two constructions are as follows.

We construct two families of oddities: geodesic segments for

Both are related to the following peculiar theorem of Thurston:

Theorem A

(Thurston,
[

)
.

On the complement of a closed set of Hausdorff codimension

The generic stability of optimizers in Thm.

For an adjacent example, no such stability is present when optimizing extremal length ratios on surfaces

We mention that Thm.

13

24

We prove our results by developing methods that allow one to conclude, among other things, that a given simple closed curve optimizes

in classes of examples.
The stability underlying Thm.

appears for us as leniency in the estimates in our proofs, and allows us to carry out quite direct arguments that would otherwise be hopeless.
Our constructions are as degenerate as possible in precise senses.
The degeneracies are rather dramatic; for instance, we prove that there are isometric embeddings of the
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
metric on
[0,1]^{2}
into

One of the points of this paper is to contribute some new understanding of what is happening in the generic case of Thurston’s theorem.
The idea of both our constructions is to see the stability of optimizers in Thm.

in the geometric isolation of simple closed geodesics in hyperbolic surfaces.
This is different from Thurston’s proof of Thm.
A

We present our results together with the hope of bringing out their common underlying idea and their relation to Thurston’s work.

Our two methods are different, and each has settings where we can use it to prove more than the other.
Broadly speaking, our construction of geodesics for

is analytic and is useful in analyzing very close together hyperbolic structures.
On the other hand, our method for limit cones uses coarser estimates from Gromov hyperbolicity.
It can be used to prove stronger conclusions, but requries more separation between the involved surfaces.

1.2.
Limit Cones

Our main results on limit cones give a class of
n
-tuples of Fuchsian representations with the surprising property that their limit cone can be computed explicitly.
These examples have the further counter-intuitive property that their limit cones are cones over finite-sided polyhedra, despite being defined with infinitely many rays.

Theorem E

(Polyhedral Limit Cones)
.

Let
n\geq 3
and let
n\leq k\leq 3g-3
.
Then there is a nonempty open set
U
of
\mathcal{T}(S_{g})^{n}
so that for any
(\rho_{1},...,\rho_{n})\in U
the limit cone
\mathcal{L}(\rho(\pi_{1}S_{g}))

\rho:\pi_{1}S_{g}\to\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{n}
is the cone over a
k

P_{\rho}
.

The polyhedra
P_{\rho}
and open sets
U
are explicitly described in Fenchel-Nielsen coordinates.

Polyhedra are the most degenerate convex bodies in the sense that they are completely determined by finitely many points.
Their appearance here is a manifestation of a degeneracy phenomenon in which finitely many curves dominate all others in families of length-ratio optimization problems, as in Thm.
A
.
This finiteness is responsible for the tractability of a full description of these limit cones.

1.3.
Non-rotating length

Our limit cones are verified through a uniform estimate on how lengths of arbitrary closed curves distort between pinched and untwisted hyperbolic surfaces (Thm.
H

We hope this estimate will be of broader use, and explain it here.

Roughly, we use combinatorial data to correct lengths of closed curves for contributions from twisting, with a simple scheme that is asymptotically accurate for curves with large spiraling.

1.5.
Remarks on Proofs

We conclude by discussing proofs of Thms.
C
and
E
.
Of the two, the harder proof is of Thm.
E
.
The way that both proofs were found was through the intuition that Thurston’s Theorem
A
says that when simple closed curves win optimization problems for length-ratios on hyperbolic surfaces, they typically win with room to spare.
A practical theme of our proofs is that this phenomenon leads to the viability of rather direct methods of verifying optimizers in related problems.
One manifestation of this is that our proofs are almost entirely based on classical hyperbolic geometry.

1.5.1.
Thurston Geodesics

On Thurston geodesics, the conceptual point behind Thm.
C
is an elementary construction of explicit Lipschitz-optimal maps on hyperbolic cylinders that have their Lipschitz constants dampen out as one moves away from the core.
The key contrast between these model maps and Thurston’s construction
[
44
]
is that our model maps only maximally stretch the core curve, while in Thurston’s examples the maximally stretched set contains no isolated curve.
We note that essentially these model maps are used near boundaries of hexagons in the geodesics of
[
39
]
and
[
40
]
.

The remainder of the proof is to actually construct Lipschitz maps between the relevant hyperbolic surfaces that extend these model maps and do not increase the Lipschitz constant.
This is made realistically feasible by Lang-Schroeder’s Theorem
[
30
]
on Lipschitz extensions in negative curvature (see also
[
21
]
), which reduces the construction of such maps to getting sufficient control on derivatives of geometric quantities in well-pinched hyperbolic surfaces.
Everything involved is expressed in terms of hyperbolic trigonometric functions, and the necessary estimates are neither unexpected nor pleasant.
The heart of this part is in getting estimates that work when the involved surfaces are very close; for less close-together surfaces more flexible techniques are useful, c.f. for instance
[
10
]
or Part II of this paper.

The use of Lang-Schroeder’s theorem
[
30
]
in our construction of geodesics is the main technical distinction from the methods of
[
36
,
39
,
40
,
44
]
and allows us to obtain much more flexibility in our geodesics by removing the need to be completely explicit in our optimal Lipschitz maps away from the maximally stretched lamination.

1.5.2.
Limit Cones

The limit cones of Thm.
E
are the convex hulls of Jordan projections of short curves in a pants decomposition, and the computation of limit cones is deduced directly from Thm.
H
, which we discuss the proof of here.

We emphasize that the main difficulty of Thm.
H
is working with all closed curves.
Indeed, the simple case of Thm.
H
is quite similar to a proposition of Mirzakhani
[
35
, Prop. 3.5]
, which has a considerably simpler proof.

The first step in proving Thm.
H
is to get combinatorial data out of closed geodesics and bound the shape of geodesics in well-pinched and untwisted hyperbolic metrics with this data.
The basic idea is to encode the shape of geodesics in the orders of endpoints of curves they intersect in the Gromov boundary of
\pi_{1}S
, which is independent of the Fuchsian representation.
This basic method is known, e.g.
[
31
]
.
What appears to be new here is that we consider intersections with two families of
intersecting
curves, and use the structure encoded in the
difference
between the orders of the corresponding sets on the top and bottom of the circle at infinity.

Our systems of curves are the pants curves in
\mathscr{P}
and the seams curves
\mathscr{H}
; their union cuts
X\in\mathcal{T}(S)
into hexagons.
This choice of curves has a few pleasant features.
First, the data we obtain fairly cleanly separates the combinatorics of geodesics as they cross between pants from their behavior within pairs of pants.
Next, the data within pairs of pants from seams intersections is convenient for encoding the structure of segments of closed geodesics in individual pairs of pants.
There, our basic combinatorial scheme is inspired by the set-up of the analysis of Chas-McMullen-Phillips
[
12
]
of closed geodesics in pairs of pants.

With the structure of geodesics constrained, we split up an arbitrary closed geodesic
\gamma
into three regimes of behavior based on a threshold
T
for combinatorial twisting data: segments crossing between pants, segments within a single pair of pants that twist
T
times around one pants curve, and segments in a single pair of pants without
T
-twisting segments around a pants curve.
Each type of segment can then be analyzed.
At its heart, the key point of this method is that
6
6

6

When
\gamma
is not entirely contained in a well-pinched pair of pants. Otherwise, a modified proof works.
after sufficient pinching we can guarantee a lower bound on the non-rotational contributions to the length of
\gamma
that grows linearly in the number of segments in our decomposition.
These length guarantees allow us to convert additive uncertainty in lengths of segments to multiplicative uncertainty, as appears in the statement of Thm.

Part I: Thurston Geodesics

We first construct geodesics for

Twisting coordinates are handled on cuff regions, using a quantitative dampening out Lipschitz constants of our model maps away from cylinders’ cores.
The relevant technical points are about the asymptotic behavior of shapes of hyperbolic surfaces while curves are pinched, which can be understood with hyperbolic trigonometry.

Section §

Within §

Model Maps on Cylinders

3.1.
Models

For any
a_{2}>a_{1}>0
and hyperbolic cylinders
\mathcal{A}_{a_{1},R_{1}}

\mathcal{A}_{a_{2},R_{2}}

R_{1}\geq R_{2}
define the

by sending curves of of constant distance from core curves to each other, linearly in distance from core curves and preserving crossing geodesics orthogonal to the boundaries, arranged so that

is orientation-preserving.

Variations on this map are used throughout our construction of geodesics for Thurston’s metric, both as parts of Lipschitz-optimal maps and as a technical tool.
The following collects the ways in which these model maps are Lipschitz optimal.
These maps have the curious property that both they and their inverses are Lipschitz optimal.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
a_{2}>a_{1}>0
and
R_{1}\geq R_{2}

,

(2)

f^{-1}_{a_{1},a_{2}}

g:\mathcal{A}_{a_{2},R_{2}}\to\mathcal{A}_{a_{1},R_{1}}

The Lipschitz constant of

is given by

(3.1)

\displaystyle\max\left\{\frac{R_{1}}{R_{2}},\,\frac{a_{1}\cosh(R_{1})}{a_{2}\cosh(R_{2})}\right\}.

(3)

Suppose additionally that
R_{1}/R_{2}\geq(a_{1}\cosh(R_{1}))/(a_{2}\cosh(R_{2}))

Proof.

We have

The target curve
\eta_{a_{2},(R_{2}/R_{1})r}

Similarly, if

is any Lipschitz map
(\mathcal{A}_{a_{2},R_{2}},\partial\mathcal{A}_{a_{2},R_{2}})\to(\mathcal{A}_{a_{1},R_{1}},\partial\mathcal{A}_{a_{1},R_{1}})
then we obtain a bound
\mathrm{Lip}(g)\geq(a_{1}\cosh(R_{1}))/(a_{2}\cosh(R_{2}))
by examining the outer boundary curve.
By examining the restriction of
g
to the crossing, boundary orthogonal geodesics in
\mathcal{A}_{a_{2},R_{2}}
we obtain the bound
\mathrm{Lip}(g)\geq R_{1}/R_{2}
.
So
f_{a_{1},a_{2}}^{-1}
is also Lipschitz optimal, with Lipschitz constant given by Eq. (
3.1
).
Finally, if Eq. (
3.1
) is given by
R_{1}/R_{2}
, then equality implies
g
maps boundary orthogonal geodesics to each other linearly in arc length.
Combining the equality case considerations now gives (
3
).
∎

3.2.
Away from the core

A key property of these model maps is that their Lipschitz constants “dampen” away from the core, as follows.
Fix a constant
\delta_{*}>0
, and take cylinders to have boundary hypercycle of length
\delta_{*}
.
For
0<a_{1},a_{2}<\delta_{*}
and
0<D<\min(R_{a_{1}},R_{a_{2}})
, let
L(a_{1},a_{2},D)
denote the optimal Lipschitz constant of the restriction of the stretch map
f_{a_{1},a_{2}}:\mathcal{C}_{a_{1}}\to\mathcal{C}_{a_{2}}
to a
D
-neighborhood of the long boundary component of
\mathcal{C}_{a_{1}}
.
In this subsection we prove:

Proposition 3.2

(Lipschitz Damping)
.

Fix
\delta_{*}>0
and let
D_{0}>0
,
T_{0}>0
and
\varepsilon>0
be given.
Then there is a
a_{0}>0
so that for any
0\leq l<a_{0}
and
|t|<T_{0}
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle 0\leq\log\mathrm{L}(l,e^{t}l,D_{0})\leq\varepsilon t.

3.2.1.
Height Distortion

When contracting rather than expanding the length of core curves and holding boundary lengths constant, we must control the distortion of the heights of cylinders.
To this end, for
l,t>0
let
R_{l}(t)=\arccosh(\delta_{*}l^{-1}e^{t})
be the height of the one-sided collar with core of length
le^{-t}
and non-geodesic boundary curve of length
\delta_{*}
.
Our normalization is so that
R_{l}(t)
is increasing in
t
.
The basic estimate here is:

Lemma 3.3

(Height Distortion)
.

Let
T_{0}>0
be fixed.
Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there is a
a_{0}>0
so that for all
a<a_{0}
and
0\leq t\leq T_{0}
we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle 0\leq\log\frac{R_{a}(t)}{R_{a}(0)}\leq\varepsilon t.

Furthermore, we may also arrange for
0\leq\frac{d}{dt}\frac{R_{a}(t)}{R_{a}(0)}\leq\varepsilon
for all
a,t
as above.

The following observation from calculus, which follows from the bound
\log(1+x)\leq x
for
x\geq 0
is helpful for proving inequalities of this form.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f\in C^{1}[0,t]
be positive and
f^{\prime}(x)\in[0,C]
for
x\in[0,t]
. Then
\log\frac{f(t)}{f(0)}\leq\frac{|t|C}{f(0)}.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

The lower bound is trivial.
For the upper bound, note that the denominator
R_{a}(0)=\arccosh(\delta_{*}a^{-1})
grows unboundedly large as
a\to 0
while the numerator’s derivative remains uniformly bounded as
a
grows small:

\lim_{a\to 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{0}]}\frac{d}{dt}R_{a}(t)=\lim_{a\to 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{0}]}\frac{d}{dt}\arccosh(\delta_{*}a^{-1}e^{t})=\lim_{a\to 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{0}]}\frac{\delta_{*}a^{-1}e^{t}}{\sqrt{(\delta_{*}a^{-1}e^{t})^{2}-1}}=1.

Then apply Lemma
3.4
.
The final remark is proved with the same estimates.
∎

3.2.2.
Offset distortion

Let
\gamma_{0}
be a geodesic in
\mathbb{H}
and fix
\delta>0
.
Take two perpendicular geodesics
\sigma_{i}
and
\sigma_{f}
to
\gamma_{0}
at distance
x<\delta
apart, let
\eta_{0,x}
be the hypercycle segment parallel to
\gamma_{0}
of arc-length
\delta
bounded by
\sigma_{i}
and
\sigma_{f}
.
For
t\in\mathbb{R}
let
\eta_{t,x}
be the hypercycle parallel to
\gamma_{0}
at oriented distance
t
from
\eta_{0,x}
along
\sigma_{i}
away from
\gamma_{0}
.
It is helpful to have an expression for the arc-length
\ell(\eta_{t,x})
in terms of
\delta
,
t
, and
x
:

Lemma 3.5

(Offset Distortion)
.

For fixed
\delta>0
, there is a real-analytic function
g_{\delta}(x)
defined for
x\in(-\delta,\delta)
with
g(0)=g^{\prime}(0)=0
so that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\ell(\eta_{t,x})

\displaystyle=\delta e^{t}+\sinh(t)g_{\delta}(x)

Note that the expression on the left of (
3.5
) is only defined for
x>0
but the expression on the right is defined for
x\in(-\delta,\delta)
.

Proof.

For
x>0
, the curve
\eta_{t,x}
has constant distance
\arccosh\left(\frac{\delta}{x}\right)+t
from
\gamma_{0}
, with length

\displaystyle x\cosh\left(\arccosh\left(\frac{\delta}{x}\right)+t\right)

\displaystyle=\delta\cosh(t)+x\sinh\left(\arccosh\left(\frac{\delta}{x}\right)\right)\sinh t

\displaystyle=\delta\cosh(t)+\sinh(t)\left(\delta-x\right)^{1/2}(\delta+x)^{1/2}.

The first few terms of the Taylor expansions in
x
for the square root terms are

\displaystyle\left(\delta-x\right)^{1/2}\left(\delta+x\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\delta^{1/2}-\frac{x}{2\delta^{1/2}}-\frac{x^{2}}{8\delta^{3/2}}\right)\left(\delta^{1/2}+\frac{x}{2\delta^{1/2}}-\frac{x^{2}}{8\delta^{3/2}}\right)+O(x^{3})

\displaystyle={\delta}-\frac{x^{2}}{2\delta}+O(x^{3}).

Note the order
1
cancellation.
The
-x^{2}/(2\delta)+O(x^{3})
term here is real-analytic in a neighborhood of
0
; write it as
g_{\delta}(x)
.
We see that this
g_{\delta}
satisfies the desired properties and

\displaystyle\ell(\eta_{t,x})

\displaystyle=\delta\cosh(t)+\sinh(t)(\delta+g_{\delta}(x))=\delta e^{t}+\sinh(t)g_{\delta}(x).

∎

3.2.3.
Lipschitz Damping

We now prove the Lipschitz Damping Proposition
3.2
.

Proof of Prop.
3.2
.

The estimates break into cases on whether
t\geq 0
or
t<0
.
For
t<0
, the proof of Prop.
3.1
shows that

does not increase the length of any curve of constant distance to the core curve, and so Lemma
3.3
shows that for sufficiently small

we have

0\leq\log\mathrm{L}(a,e^{t}a,D_{0})\leq\log\frac{R_{a}(-t)}{R_{a}(0)}\leq\varepsilon t.

For
t>0
, the map
f_{a,e^{t}a}
has
||Df_{a,e^{t}a}(v)||\leq||v||
for
v
tangent to geodesics orthogonal to the core curve.
The proof of Prop.
3.1
also shows that for tangent vectors
v
to curves
\eta_{r}
of constant distance

from the core curve that
||Df_{a,e^{t}a}(v)||/||v||
is monotonically decreasing in

.
As any length-minimizing geodesic between two points in the
D_{0}
-neighborhood of the long boundary component is contained in the
(D_{0}+\delta/2)
-neighborhood of the boundary component, let
v
be a tangent vector of norm

to the curve of distance

from the long boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{a}
.
From the Offset Distortion Lemma

, to deduce the desired bound from Lemma
2.2
, the quantity to estimate to control

is:

\displaystyle\log\mathrm{L}(a,e^{t}a,D_{0})\leq\log\frac{\delta\exp(-\frac{R_{a}(-t)}{R_{a}(0)}(D_{0}+\delta/2))+\sinh(-\frac{R_{a}(-t)}{R_{a}(0)}(D_{0}+\delta/2))g_{\delta}(e^{t}a)}{\delta\exp(-(D_{0}+\delta/2))+\sinh(-(D_{0}+\delta/2))g_{\delta}(a)}.

Lemma
3.4
reduces this to noting that for
t\in[0,T_{0}]
, the function

F(t,a)=\delta\exp\left(-\frac{R_{a}(-t)}{R_{a}(0)}(D_{0}+\delta/2)\right)+\sinh\left(-\frac{R_{a}(-t)}{R_{a}(0)}(D_{0}+\delta/2)\right)g_{\delta}(e^{t}a)

has a uniform lower bound on

for
a
in a sufficiently small neighborhood of
0
and showing that
\frac{d}{dt}F(t,a)
tends to
0
uniformly on
[0,T_{0}]
as

.
This is seen directly by taking the derivative, applying Lemma
3.3
, and using that
g_{\delta}(0)=g^{\prime}_{\delta}(0)=0
. ∎

4.
Maps between Polygons

The other component of our construction is of Lipschitz extensions away from core curves.
The general method is to cut up the complements of collars into hexagons with alternating geodesic and hypercycle arc boundary segments, to map these hexagons to each other in a controlled way, then to use Lang-Schroeder’s theorem to extend to interiors.

Throughout this section, fix a
\delta_{*}>0
small enough to be comfortably less than the
\delta
-hyperbolicity constant of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

4.1.
Shorts

with lengths
2a_{1},2a_{2},2a_{3}

If hypercycles of length

\delta_{*}
-shorts

.
Note that
\delta_{*}
-shorts are well-defined when

The pair of pants

-short hexagons
in

For

The goal of this subsection is to prove the following quantitative geometric convergence of the shorts

to

(Shorts Maps)
.

(2)

4.1.1.
Preliminaries and Pentagons

Label the sides of
H_{\delta_{*}}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
by
\beta_{1},\zeta_{3},\beta_{2},\zeta_{1},\beta_{3},\zeta_{2}
in cyclic order so that
\zeta_{i}
is a geodesic segment opposite a hypercycle arc
\beta_{i}
for all

.
See Figure

Write the lengths as

for
\beta_{i}
and
c_{i}
for

for

.
In particular,
\delta_{*}/2=b_{i}
for
i=1,2,3

Write the sides of the full hexagon that correspond to the seams of the pants

as

for

In this paragraph we prove:

Lemma 4.2
.

As functions of
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
, the lengths

(i=1,2,3)
admit real-analytic extensions to a neighborhood of

in

The point for our uses later is that this uniformly bounds the partial derivatives of

on a neighborhood of
(0,0,0)

Lemma
4.2
is proved with elementary hyperbolic trigonometry and calculus by finding a formula for

that makes the claim clear.
We explain the details as there is some delicacy: the obvious formulas for

are as sums of positive and negative terms that grow unboundedly as

.
So showing extension across

We focus on

are symmetric.
The argument here is greatly simplified by splitting hexagons into right pentagons.
Let us set notation.
The hexagon
H
may be split uniquely along a geodesic

and

Figure 2
.
Labels on a hexagon and one of the pentagons it is split into.

Let us take

and

In particular,

From uniqueness of this splitting, the values of

We focus on

Hyperbolic trigonometry
[

gives the identities
a_{1,K}+a_{1,L}=a_{1}
,
\sinh a_{1,K}\sinh t=\cosh a_{2},

\sinh\alpha_{1,L}\sinh t=\cosh a_{3}

From these identities and hyperbolic function addition formulas, one derives the identity
F(a_{1,K},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=0
with

(4.1)

\displaystyle F(a_{1,K},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=\sinh(a_{1,K})\left(1+\frac{\cosh a_{1}\cosh a_{2}}{\cosh a_{3}}\right)-\cosh(a_{1,K})\left(\frac{\sinh a_{1}\cosh a_{2}}{\cosh a_{3}}\right),

with

well-defined on all of

.
Note that the

is non-vanishing at

.
We remark that one may explicitly compute the derivatives of

As

for

Write

and

Proposition

Lemma 4.3
.

c_{1,K}
is the restriction of a real-analytic function of
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
defined on a neighborhood of
0
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the claim for

Using addition formulas for hyperbolic trigonometric functions,

\displaystyle\sinh c_{1,K}

\displaystyle=\sinh(c_{1,K}^{\prime}-\arccosh(\delta_{*}/2a_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{*}\sinh(c_{1,K}^{\prime})}{2a_{2}}-\cosh(c_{1,K}^{\prime})\left(\frac{\delta_{*}}{2a_{2}}-1\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\delta_{*}}{2a_{2}}+1\right)^{1/2}.

The first few terms of the Taylor polynomial for the square root terms are

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\delta_{*}}{2a_{2}}-1\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\delta_{*}}{2a_{2}}+1\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2a_{2}}\left(\delta_{*}^{1/2}-\frac{a_{2}}{\delta_{*}^{1/2}}-\frac{a_{2}^{2}}{2\delta_{*}^{3/2}}\right)\left(\delta_{*}^{1/2}+\frac{a_{2}}{\delta_{*}^{1/2}}-\frac{a_{2}^{2}}{2\delta_{*}^{3/2}}\right)+O(a_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{*}}{2a_{2}}-\frac{a_{2}}{\delta_{*}}+O(a_{2}^{2}).

The

; write it as

.
Combining this with the identity

we see

\displaystyle\sinh c_{1,K}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{*}\sinh c_{1,K}^{\prime}}{2a_{2}}-(\sinh c_{1,K}^{\prime}+e^{-c_{1,K}^{\prime}})\left(\frac{\delta_{*}}{2a_{2}}-\frac{a_{2}}{\delta_{*}}+g(a_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\cosh a_{1,K}\left(\frac{a_{2}\delta_{*}^{-1}+g(a_{2})}{\sinh a_{2}}\right)+e^{-c_{1,K}^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\delta_{*}}{2a_{2}}+\frac{a_{2}}{\delta_{*}}+g(a_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\cosh a_{1,K}\left(\frac{a_{2}\delta_{*}^{-1}+g(a_{2})}{\sinh a_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\sinh a_{2}}{\cosh a_{1,K}+\sqrt{\cosh^{2}a_{1,K}+\sinh^{2}a_{2}}}\left(\frac{\delta_{*}}{2a_{2}}+\frac{a_{2}}{\delta_{*}}+g(a_{2})\right).

The claim now follows from the analyticity of

and
a_{1,K}

∎

4.1.2.

Definition 4.6

For

Lemma 4.7
.

For any
\delta_{*}<1

is the restriction of a real-analytic function defined on a neighborhood of

in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

The requirement

Proof.

Computing the holonomy

\displaystyle M(a)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\cosh(a/2)&-\exp(\arccosh(\delta_{*}/a))\sinh(a/2)\\

Part II: Limit Cones

Almost all of this part is spent proving Thm.
H
, and the final relevant statement is Prop.
6.31
.
Then polyhedrality of the limit cones we consider is deduced quickly.

We begin with a brief outline.
The basic idea is to break up a closed geodesic into its intersections with pairs of pants, then analyze contributions to its length from a few different factors.
It is useful to group these contributions to length into three classes, by the order of how they distort between distinct pinched, barely twisted, marked hyperbolic surfaces
X,Y
:

(1)

The spiraling of geodesics around core curves
\gamma_{i}
of long collars.
The ratios of length contributions from segments of this type behave on the order of
[\ell_{X}(\gamma_{i})/\ell_{Y}(\gamma_{i})]^{\pm 1}
.

(2)

Portions of geodesics that correspond to excursions deep into collars or crossings of collars, corrected to remove spiraling contributions.
The ratios of length contributions from this type of behavior are no greater in order than
[\log(\ell_{X}(\gamma_{i}))/\log(\ell_{Y}(\gamma_{i}))]^{\pm 1}
.

(3)

Portions of geodesics that stay in thick parts of pants.
The ratios of length contributions from this type of behavior are bounded by uniform constants.

We mention that properly identifying and partitioning into these cases and setting thresholds for consideration are major points.
A crucial feature of this decomposition is that we can arrange that a curve picks up a definite amount of length whenever it is considered for contributions of the form (
1
).
This allows us to convert
additive
errors in estimates coming from
\delta
-hyperbolicity into uniformly bounded
multiplicative
errors in the contributions of type (
2
) and (
3
).

In order to exhibit the desired phenomena, we need tight enough estimates so that this behavior is separated out.
The estimation procedure is also complicated by the need for our estimates to be uniform across
all
isotopy classes of closed (not necessarily simple) curves and for the estimates to hold across a fairly large family of hyperbolic structures.
Note that though working only with simple closed curves is possible in the case of
n=2
due to work of Thurston
[
44
, §3]
, Thurston’s proof of this breaks when
n>2
.

Section §
5
gives the basic estimates on hyperbolic lengths and geodesics in annuli used in our combinatorialization.
Then §
6
combinatorializes the lengths of geodesics on pinched surfaces: this is by far the longest section in this part.
In §
6.1
we set basic notation and recall fundamentals.
In §
6.2
we collect invariant data and use this data to constrain the shape of geodesics in pinched surfaces: §
6.2.1
describes the data and its basic properties, §
6.2.2
and §
6.2.3
use this data to encode twisting, and §
6.2.4
uses this data to give well-behaved partitions of geodesics.
Then §
6.3
finishes the combinatorialization scheme and deduces the relevant length estimates.
Combinatorialization of lengths of crossing segments is done in §
6.3.1
, the decomposition of segments of our partition that stay in a pair of pants and its basic properties is established in §
6.3.2
, and the main estimate is finally deduced in §

5.
Basic Length Estimates

We begin by giving definitions and basic estimates from hyperbolic geometry of annuli that will be used throughout this part.

5.1.
Twisting in Annuli

Let us begin with a notion of twisting.
For
a>0
, let
\mathcal{A}_{a}^{\prime}
be as in §
2.1.1
above, and write the core geodesic of
\mathcal{A}_{a}^{\prime}
as
\beta_{a}
.
There is a canonical closest-point projection to
\beta_{a}
from
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{a}
.
For any curve
\gamma
in
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{a}
, define the
basic rotation

\bar{r}(\gamma)
as the net (real, positive) number of rotations around
\beta_{a}
that the projection of
\gamma
to
\beta_{a}
makes.

A notion that is less well-defined but has properties that are useful for invariance arguments is as follows.
Let
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
be orthogonal geodesics to
\beta_{a}
in
\mathcal{A}_{a}^{\prime}
spaced exactly half-way apart and let
\gamma
be a curve in
\mathcal{A}_{a}^{\prime}
that is monotone after projection to
\beta_{a}
, such as a geodesic in
\mathcal{A}_{a}^{\prime}
.
We define the
synthetic rotation

|r|_{\sigma}(\gamma)\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
of
\gamma
with respect to
\sigma
to be half the number of intersections of
\gamma
and
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
.
Synthetic rotation is well-defined to within
1/2
, regardless of the choice of
\sigma
.

A basic bound on rotation from hyperbolic geometry that we shall use is:

Lemma 5.1

(Thick Twisting Bound)
.

Fix
\delta_{*}>0
. Then for all
\varepsilon<\delta_{*}
sufficiently small, there exists a
C=C(\varepsilon,\delta_{*})<\infty
with the following property.

Let
\alpha
be any geodesic segment in
\mathcal{C}_{a}^{\prime}
with
0\leq a\leq\varepsilon
entirely contained in the complement of
\mathcal{C}_{a}
, i.e.
\alpha
is contained in the part of
\mathcal{C}_{a}^{\prime}
foliated by hypercycles of length at least
\delta_{*}
.
Then the basic rotation of
\alpha
is no more than
C
.

We remark that
C(\varepsilon,\delta_{*})
is not large in practice for large
\delta_{*}
.
For instance, in the hyperbolic cusp
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\prime}
with
\delta_{*}=1
, the sharp constant is
C=2
.

Proof.

It is helpful to lift this setup to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.
One sees that the basic rotation of
\alpha
is always finite because the geodesic containing
\alpha
does not share an endpoint with the lift of the core geodesic
\beta_{a}
(or the cusp endpoint if
a=0
).
Let
\alpha(0)
be a lift of the point in
\alpha
that makes the deepest excursion into
\mathcal{C}_{a}
, and let
\sigma
be the geodesic in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
orthogonal to
\beta_{\alpha}
and containing
\alpha(0)
.
By cutting
\alpha
at
\alpha(0)
, it suffices to show the claim for geodesic rays that start at their deepest excursion into
\mathcal{C}_{a}^{\prime}
.

For these rays, working in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
also shows that the total rotation is monotone increasing when
\alpha
is pushed deeper into
\mathcal{C}_{a}
by a loxodromic transformation preserving
\sigma
.
Since
\alpha(0)
is the point of deepest excursion of
\alpha
, the segments
\alpha
and
\sigma
have an oriented angle of no more than
\pi/2
pointing in the direction away from
\beta_{a}
on
\sigma
.
Basic hyperbolic geometry also shows that the total rotation of a geodesic ray through
\alpha(0)
and with such an angle in
[0,\pi/2]
is monotone increasing under increasing the angle up to
\pi/2
with an elliptic transformation that holds
\alpha(0)
fixed.

So it suffices to prove the claim for geodesic rays
\alpha
tangent to the hypercycle
\nu_{\delta_{*}}
of length
\delta_{*}
, and to prove this it suffices to consider complete geodesics
\alpha
tangent to
\nu_{\delta_{*}}
.
The synthetic rotation of
\alpha
is then given by the number of orbits of
0\in\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
under powers of
M(a)
that are contained in
[-1,1]
, where
M(a)
is the holonomy of the core curve of
\mathcal{C}_{a}
, normalized so that the tangent vector to
\nu_{\delta_{*}}
at
\alpha(0)
is
(i,1)
in the upper half-plane model of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.
This is in fact the very same Möbius transformation that appeared in Lemma
4.7
of Part I and is written in Eq.
4.2
).
The claim follows from the expression of Eq. (
4.2
) and the unifom comparability of basic rotation and synthetic rotation.
∎

5.2.
Spiraling estimates

We now turn to estimating lengths of geodesics that spiral around the core of a hyperbolic annulus.

5.2.1.
Non-crossing estimates

We begin with geodesic segments in cut-off cylinders.
We fix a
\delta_{*}\in(0,1]
and take cut-off cylinders and annuli to have hypercycle boundaries of length
\delta_{*}
.

The object of interest to us here is the quantity, for a geodesic
\alpha_{a,t}
with boundary on the long boundary component of
\mathcal{C}_{a}
and with total basic rotation
t
,

\mathcal{L}_{a}(t)=\ell(\alpha_{a,t})-ta.

The goal of this paragraph is to get a useful bound on
\mathcal{L}_{a}(t)
(Prop.
5.3
).

There is a maximal
s(a,t)>0
so that
\alpha_{a,t}
intersects the hypercycle
\eta_{s(a,t)}
.
Define the
excursion depth

\mathcal{E}(a,t)
of
\alpha_{a,t}
to be the distance between
\eta_{s(a,t)}
and the long boundary component of
\mathcal{C}_{a}
.
Recall that
R_{a}
is the distance between the boundary components of
\mathcal{C}_{a}
and
R_{a}=\arccosh(\delta_{*}/a)
.

We estimate
\mathcal{E}(a,t)
through the following construction.
Lift to the universal covering of
\mathcal{C}_{a}
, then place two geodesics
\sigma_{i}
and
\sigma_{f}
orthogonal to the lift
\widetilde{\beta}_{t}
of the core geodesic spaced
ta
apart.
Call
\beta_{a,t}
the cut out segment of
\widetilde{\beta}_{t}
.
See Figure
3
.
The resulting shape has four corners, cyclically ordered as
p_{i},p_{f},P_{f},P_{i}
with
p_{i}
and
p_{f}
on the lift of the core geodesic.
Then a lift
\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t}
of
\alpha_{a,t}
is the geodesic between
P_{i}
and
P_{f}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{Q}(a,t)
the hyperbolic quadrilateral with sides
\beta_{a,t},\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t},\sigma_{i},
and
\sigma_{f}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{P}(a,t)
the quadrilateral obtained by splitting
\mathcal{Q}(a,t)
in half by a geodesic segment
\nu_{a,t}
orthogonal to the midpoint
b
of
\beta_{t}
.
Then
\mathcal{E}(a,t)=R_{a}-\ell(\nu_{a,t})
.

It seems worthwhile to note that this construction gives an analytic expression for
\mathcal{E}(a,t)
, as
\mathcal{P}(a,t)
is a hyperbolic quadrilateral with three right angles.
Such quadrilaterals are known as
Lambert quadrilaterals
, and have well-understood analytic expresions for side lengths, e.g.
[
4
]
.
From these, one sees:

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(a,t)=R_{a}-\arctanh\left(\frac{\tanh(R_{a})}{\cosh(ta)}\right).

In particular,
\mathcal{E}(a,t)
is monotone increasing in
t
and real-analytic.

We have found coarse estimates from
\delta
-hyperbolicity to generally be more useful than Eq. (
5.1
) in proving the estimates we shall need below.
Let
\delta_{\mathbb{H}}
be the optimal Gromov hyperbolicity constant of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.
The basic estimates here are:

Lemma 5.2

(Excursion Estimates)
.

Fix
\delta_{*}>0
and let
\mathcal{O}>0
be given.
Then there are finite positive constants
C_{\ref{good-len-approx}}
and
C_{\ref{C-excursion-error}}
with only
C_{\ref{C-excursion-error}}
depending on
\mathcal{O}
so that for any
0<a<\delta_{*}
:

(1)

|2\mathcal{E}(a,t)-\mathcal{L}_{a}(t)|\leq C_{\ref{good-len-approx}}
for all
t\geq 0
,

(2)

|\mathcal{E}(a,t)-\log t|\leq C_{\ref{C-excursion-error}}
for
1\leq t\leq 4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}/a+\mathcal{O}
,

(3)

R_{a}-2\delta_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\mathcal{E}(a,t)\leq R_{a}
for
t\geq 4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}/a
.

Figure 3
.
The basic set-up after lifting to the universal cover.

Proof.

We retain the above notation.
For Claim (
1
), consider the two right triangles formed in
\mathcal{P}(a,t)
after adding the geodesic segment
\zeta_{a,t}
from
P_{i}
to the midpoint
b
of
\beta_{a,t}
.
The two triangles meet along the long side
\zeta_{a,t}
.
A basic fact from hyperbolic trigonometry (which follows, for instance, from the law of cosines) is that the length of the long side of a right hyperbolic triangle is within a uniform constant
C_{\ref{C-triangle}}
of the sum of the shorter side lengths.
Because
\ell(\nu_{a,t})=R_{a}-\mathcal{E}(a,t)
, we then have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}(a,t)-\mathcal{L}_{a}(t)/2|

\displaystyle=|R_{a}-\ell(\nu_{a,t})-(\ell(\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t})-ta)/2|

\displaystyle\leq|R_{a}+ta/2-\ell(\zeta_{a,t})|+|\ell(\zeta_{a,t})-(\ell(\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t})/2+\ell(\nu_{a,t}))|

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{\ref{C-triangle}}.

This proves (
1
).

Claim (
2
) is the most involved.
We prove it by showing that the strategy of going down to a hypercycle of fixed length, crossing between
\sigma_{i}
and
\sigma_{f}
on that hypercycle, and going back up to the top is not very inefficient in comparison to
\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t}
in this time range.
The main point is getting a uniform upper bound on
\ell(\eta_{s(a,t)})
in this time range, which then determines a good length of hypercycle to go down to.
The argument is a bit complicated by this strategy not being well-defined for times that are too large or too small relative to
a
, which makes the edges of the time range here need to be handled with separate considerations.

Let us begin.
We claim that there is a
C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}
so that
\ell(\eta_{s(a,t)})\leq C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}
for all
a<\delta_{*}
and
1\leq t\leq 4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}/a+\mathcal{O}
.
We do case analysis on whether or not
\mathcal{E}(a,t)\leq R_{a}-2\delta_{\mathbb{H}}
.

Note first that if
\mathcal{E}(a,t)\geq R_{a}-2\delta_{\mathbb{H}}
and
ta\leq 4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}+\mathcal{O}a
then
\ell(\eta_{s(a,t)})\leq(4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}+\mathcal{O}\delta_{*})\cosh(2\delta_{\mathbb{H}})
.
This handles the case
\mathcal{E}(a,t)\leq R_{a}-2\delta_{\mathbb{H}}
.

So assume that
\mathcal{E}(a,t)<R_{a}-2\delta_{\mathbb{H}}
.
Let
p_{m}
be the midpoint of
\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t}
and let
r_{m}
be the intersection point of
\sigma_{i}
and
\eta_{s(a,t)}
.
Let
\mathcal{I}(a,t)
be the all-right quadrilaterial with three geodesic sides formed by
\nu_{a,t}
, arcs of
\beta_{a,t}
and
\sigma_{i}
, and half of
\eta_{s(a,t)}
.

We prove the desired bound by applying Gauß-Bonnet to
\mathcal{I}(a,t)
.
As
\mathcal{I}(a,t)\subset\mathcal{P}(a,t)
and
\mathcal{P}(a,t)
is a geodesic quadrilaterial with three right angles, the area
A(a,t)
of
\mathcal{I}(a,t)
is bounded above by
\pi/2
.
As
\mathcal{E}(a,t)<R_{a}-2\delta_{\mathbb{H}}
, the geodesic curvature
\kappa(a,t)
of
\eta_{s(a,t)}
satisfies
\kappa(a,t)\geq\tanh(2\delta_{\mathbb{H}})
.
Now because
\mathcal{I}(a,t)
has four right angles and three geodesic sides, Gauß-Bonnet gives

-A(a,t)=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\eta_{s(a,t)}}\kappa(a,t).

The bounds on these quantities then show that
\pi/\tanh(2\delta_{\mathbb{H}})
is an upper bound for
\ell(\eta_{s(a,t)})
under these hypotheses.
So with
C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}=\max\{\pi/\tanh(2\delta_{\mathbb{H}}),(4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}+\mathcal{O}\delta_{*})\cosh(2\delta_{\mathbb{H}})\}
, we have
\ell(\eta_{s(a,t)})\leq C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}
for all
t\in[1,t\delta_{\mathbb{H}}/a+\mathcal{O}]
.

We next observe that as
ta\leq 4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}+\mathcal{O}\delta_{*}
, Claim (
1
) shows that
\ell(\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t})/2
and
\mathcal{E}(a,t)
differ by at most an
(a,t)
-independent additive constant, and so it suffices to prove the claim for
\ell(\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t})/2
.
Finally, we note that using the very coarse estimate
0\leq\ell(\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t})\leq t\delta_{*}
, we can adjust
C_{\ref{C-excursion-error}}
to make the desired bound trivial for
t<C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}/\delta_{*}
, and henceforth assume
t\geq C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}/\delta_{*}
.

Write the depth of the hypercycle with boundary length
C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}
as
D(a,t)
.
Using the curve obtained by going down to this hypercycle, going over, then going back up as a competitor for
\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t}
gives the bound
\ell(\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t})/2\leq D(a,t)+C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}/2
.
On the other hand, as
C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}
bounds
\ell(\eta_{s(a,t)})
above and the length of hypercycles is monotone in distance from the core, we have
\mathcal{E}(a,t)\geq D(a,t)
.
Comparing the estimates this gives on
d(P_{i},\beta_{a,t})
and
d(p_{m},\beta_{a,t})
, we then get
\ell(\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t})/2\geq D(a,t)
.
So
|D(a,t)-\ell(\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t})/2|\leq C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}/2
.

We now estimate
D(a,t)
.
It is a technically useful remark that
C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}\geq 4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}+\mathcal{O}\delta_{*}
, though it would be trivial to arrange this if it were not already the case.
By definition,
C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}=\cosh(R_{a}-D(a,t))ta
, so that
D(a,t)=R_{a}-\arccosh(C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}/ta)
.
This expression is valid for all
t
under consideration because
C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}\geq 4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}+\mathcal{O}\delta_{*}
.
Now, using the definition of
R_{a}
and the bound
|\arccosh(x)-\log(x)|\in[0,\log 2)
for
x\in[1,\infty)
,

\displaystyle|D(a,t)-\log t|

\displaystyle=|\arccosh(\delta_{*}/a)-\arccosh(C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}/ta)-\log t|

\displaystyle\leq|\log(\delta_{*}/a)-\log(C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}}/ta)-\log t|+\log 4

\displaystyle\leq|\log(\delta_{*}/C_{\ref{C-hypercycle-bound}})|+\log 4,

and Claim (
2
) follows.

Finally, for Claim (
3
), observe that in this case
\ell(\beta_{a,t})\geq 4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}
.
As
\mathcal{Q}(a,t)
is a quadrilateral, every point
p\in\partial\mathcal{Q}(a,t)
is within the
2\delta_{\mathbb{H}}
-neighborhood of the sides of
\mathcal{Q}(a,t)
not containing
p
.
So
p_{m}
is within a
2\delta_{\mathbb{H}}
-neighborhood of
\beta_{a,t}
, and the claim follows.
∎

The main proposition of this subsection is now:

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\delta_{*}>0
be fixed, and let
\tau>0
and
C>1
be given.
Then there are positive constants
\varepsilon_{*}
and
T_{0}
so that for all
a_{1}\leq a_{2}<\varepsilon_{*},t_{1}>T_{0}
, and
|t_{2}-t_{1}|\leq\tau
,

C^{-1}\leq\frac{\mathcal{L}_{a_{1}}(t_{1})}{\mathcal{L}_{a_{2}}(t_{2})}\leq C\frac{\log(a_{1})}{\log(a_{2})}.

Proof.

What happens is that
\mathcal{L}_{a_{1}}(t_{1})
and
\mathcal{L}_{a_{2}}(t_{2})
are approximately the same size until
\mathcal{L}_{a_{2}}(t_{2})
approaches its supremum, which is around
\log a_{2}
.
Then
\mathcal{L}_{a_{1}}(t_{1})
keeps growing until it approaches its supremum, which is around
\log a_{1}
.
Decreasing
\varepsilon_{*}
and increasing
T_{0}
makes these estimates become arbitrarily multiplicatively accurate.

Let us now be precise.
Let
\tau>0
and
\delta_{*}\in(0,1]
be fixed.
We begin by arranging
T_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{*}
so that the necessary estimates can be made below.
So take
T_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{*}
so that with the constants suppiled by Lemma
5.2
with
\mathcal{O}=2\tau
:

(1)

C^{-1/6}\log t<\log t-\max(C_{\ref{C-excursion-error}},C_{\ref{good-len-approx}}/2)
and
C^{1/6}\log t>\log t+\max(C_{\ref{C-excursion-error}},C_{\ref{good-len-approx}}/2)
for all
t\geq T_{0}-\tau
,

(2)

C^{-1/6}\log t\leq\log(t-\tau)\leq\log(t+\tau)\leq C^{1/6}\log t
for all
t>T_{0}-\tau
,

(3)

C^{-1/6}|\log a|\leq R_{a}\leq C^{1/6}|\log a|
for all
0<a<\varepsilon_{*}
,

(4)

(R_{a}+x)/R_{a}\in[C^{-1/6},C^{1/6}]
for all
0<a<\varepsilon_{*}
and
|x|<2\delta_{\mathbb{H}}
,

(5)

(|\log a|+x)/|\log a|\in[C^{-1/6},C^{1/6}]
for all
0<a<\varepsilon_{*}
and
|x|<\log(4\delta_{\mathbb{H}})
.

Now fix
a_{1}\leq a_{2}<\varepsilon_{*}
and let
t_{1},t_{2}
satisfy the hypotheses.
Note first that that using (
1
), Lemma
5.2
, and monotonicity of
\mathcal{E}(a,t)
in
t
shows that for
i=1,2
,

(5.2)

\displaystyle C^{-1/6}2\mathcal{E}(a_{i},t_{i})\leq\mathcal{L}_{a_{i}}(t_{i})\leq C^{1/6}2\mathcal{E}(a_{i},t).

We now do case analysis on the size of
t_{1}
to apply the various estimates in the regimes of behaviors in Lemma
5.2
to
\mathcal{E}(a,t)
.
Suppose first that
t_{1}\leq 4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}/a_{2}+\tau
.
Then using (
1
) above and Lemma
5.2
.(
2
) shows for
i=1,2
,

\displaystyle C^{-1/6}\log(t_{i})\leq\log(t_{i})-C_{\ref{C-excursion-error}}\leq\mathcal{E}(a_{i},t_{i})\leq\log(t_{i})+C_{\ref{C-excursion-error}}\leq C^{1/6}\log(t_{i}).

Combining this with (
2
) and Eq. (
5.2
), in this time range

\displaystyle C^{-1}\leq C^{-2/3}\frac{\log(t_{1})}{\log(t_{2})}\leq\frac{\mathcal{L}_{a_{1}}(t_{1})}{\mathcal{L}_{a_{2}}(t_{2})}\leq C^{2/3}\frac{\log(t_{1})}{\log(t_{2})}\leq C.

Next suppose that
4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}/a_{2}+\tau\leq t_{1}
, so that
t_{2}\geq 4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}/a_{2}
.
Then by Lemma
5.2
.(
3
) and assumptions (
3
) and (
4
) we have the following estimate on
\mathcal{E}(a_{2},t_{2})
:

(5.3)

\displaystyle C^{1/6}|\log a_{2}|\geq R_{a_{2}}\geq\mathcal{E}(a_{2},t_{2})\geq R_{a_{2}}-2\delta_{\mathbb{H}}\geq C^{-1/6}R_{a_{2}}\geq C^{-1/3}|\log a_{2}|.

For
(a_{1},t_{1})
, we break into two cases.
If
t_{1}\geq 4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}/a_{1}
, then arguing as in Eq. (
5.2
) above
C^{1/6}|\log a_{1}|\geq\mathcal{E}(a_{1},t_{1})\geq C^{-1/6}|\log a_{1}|
.
Next, suppose
t_{1}\leq 4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}/a_{1}
.
Then arguing as in Eq.
5.3
) above and using the constraint
t_{1}\geq 4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}/a_{2}
,

\displaystyle C^{1/6}|\log a_{1}|\geq R_{a_{1}}\geq\mathcal{E}(a_{1},t_{1})\geq C^{-1/6}\log t_{1}\geq C^{-1/6}(|\log a_{2}|+\log(4\delta_{\mathbb{H}}))\geq C^{-1/6}|\log a_{2}|.

Combining these estimates, in all cases

\displaystyle C^{-1}=C^{-1}\frac{\log a_{2}}{\log a_{2}}\leq\frac{\mathcal{L}_{a_{1}}(t_{1})}{\mathcal{L}_{a_{2}}(t_{2})}\leq C\frac{\log a_{1}}{\log a_{2}}.

5.2.2.
Crossing Estimates

We now estimate the length of geodesics in
\mathcal{A}_{a}^{\prime}
that cross through the cylinder.
The estimates are much simpler than in the non-crossing case.

In this paragraph, change notation so that
\alpha_{a,t}
is a geodesic in
\mathcal{A}_{a}^{\prime}
between the boundary components of basic rotation
t
.
In analogy to before, define

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{a}(t)=\ell(\alpha_{a,t})-ta.

In this setting, something stronger than Prop.
5.3
is true:

Lemma 5.4
.

There is a constant
C_{\ref{C-crossing}}
so that
2R_{a}-C_{\ref{C-crossing}}\leq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{a}(t)\leq 2R_{a}
for all
t\geq 0
and
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

Once again lift to the universal covering of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{a}
and examine a lift
\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t}
of
\alpha_{a,t}
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.
Let
\beta_{a,t}
be a segment of the lift of the core curve of length
ta
, with orthogonal geodesic segments
\sigma_{i},\sigma_{f}
of length
2R_{a}
at its endpoints.
Then
\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t}
connects one side of
\sigma_{i}
to the other side of
\sigma_{f}
, and by symmetry meets
\beta_{a,t}
at its midpoint
b
.
The curve
\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t}
is the union of the long sides of two right geodesic triangles formed with halves of
\sigma_{i},\sigma_{f}
, halves of
\beta_{a,t}
, and halves of
\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t}
.

Because the long side of a right triangle in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is uniformly close in length to the sum of the other sides, we obtain a bound of
\ell(\alpha_{a,t})\geq 2R_{a}+ta-C_{\ref{C-crossing}}
for a uniform
C_{\ref{C-crossing}}
.
Using the curve that goes down along
\sigma_{i}
to
\beta_{a,t}
then across
\beta_{a,t}
, then down
\sigma_{f}
as a competitor for
\widetilde{\alpha}_{a,t}
gives the complementary bound
\ell(\alpha_{a,t})\leq 2R_{a}+ta
.
∎

6.
Length Combinatorialization

We now turn to combinatorializing the lengths of curves.
The final statement is Prop.
6.31
, which has slightly more information than Thm.
H
.
We begin by introducing the families of hyperbolic surfaces and decompositions we consider (§
6.1
).
We then describe appropriately invariant combinatorial data induced by isotopy classes of curves with respect to this decomposition, and show that these combinatorial data determine the basic shape of a closed geodesic in the family of surfaces we consider to within certain bounds (§
6.2
).
Finally, we prove our desired combinatorialization (§

6.1.
Basic Constructions

We begin by setting notation for cutting surfaces into hexagons, and giving reminders on how to systematically encode geodesics in pairs of pants.

6.1.1.
Hexagon Decompositions

We recall the relevant systems of curves.
Let us begin by setting terminology on pants decompositions. Let
\mathscr{P}=\{\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{3g-3}\}
be the curves in pants decomposition of
S
.
Write the corresponding pants as
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},...,P_{2g-2}\}
.

Definition 6.1

(Untwisted Locus)
.

Let
\mathscr{U}(\mathscr{P})
be the subset of
\mathcal{T}(S)
consisting of hyperbolic structures that have
0
twist parameters along all curves in
\mathscr{P}
.

For
\varepsilon>0
let
\mathscr{U_{\varepsilon}(P)}
be the collection of
X\in\mathscr{U(P)}
so that all length parameters are less than
\varepsilon
, and for
\varepsilon>0
let
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
be the collection of (marked) isometry classes of hyperbolic pants with geodesic boundaries of lengths not exceeding
\varepsilon
.

Each pair of pants
P_{j}
has three distinct boundary curves and is uniquely split by three geodesic arcs
\nu_{i,j}
(
i=1,2,3
) orthogonal to the boundary components into two right hyperbolic hexagons
H^{\pm}_{j}
.
For
X\in\mathscr{U}(\mathscr{P})
, the curves
\nu_{i,j}
glue together into simple closed geodesics
\nu_{1},...,\nu_{l}
on
X
.
Denote this collection of simple geodesics by
\mathscr{H}
.

The complementary regions for
\mathscr{P}\cup\mathscr{H}
in general

are hexagons; for
X\in\mathscr{U}(\mathscr{P})
the complementary regions are right hexagons.
Accordingly, let us define:

Definition 6.2
.

For
X\in\mathscr{U(\mathscr{P})}
, the pair
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{H})
is a
hexagon system
on
X
.

To simplify statements of invariance properties of combinatorial data induced by a hexagon system, it is useful to restrict to pants decompositions with the following property.

Definition 6.3
.

A pants decomposition
\mathscr{P}
is

if it is embedded and every curve
\nu\in\mathscr{H}
has connected intersection with each pair of pants.

It is an exercise to show that convenient pants decompositions exist.
We henceforth only consider convenient pants decompositions.

We shall often fix a
\delta_{*}>0
chosen to be sufficiently small that the following holds.
For any
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta_{*}}
, the hypercycle arcs of length
\delta_{*}
around each boundary component of
P
are embedded, and cutting off the boundary components along these arcs leaves a topological pair of pants
P_{\delta_{*}}
.
Following Part I, we call
P_{\delta_{*}}
the
\delta_{*}
-shorts
in
P
.

6.1.2.
Geodesics in pairs of pants

We have found the technical framework used by Chas-McMullen-Phillips in
[
12
]
useful for encoding closed geodesics on a pair of pants, and describe it in this paragraph.
The case of non-closed geodesics requires only small modification.

Let
\widetilde{G}=\langle x,y,z\mid x^{2}=y^{2}=z^{2}=e\rangle
, and let
G
be the subgroup generated by
(a,b,c)=(xy,yz,zx)
.
It is a free group on two generators, with presentation
\langle a,b,c\mid abc=e\rangle
.

Every word in
\widetilde{G}
may be uniquely put into
reduced form
, i.e. with no letter appearing twice consecutively.
For
w\in\widetilde{G}
(or
G
), write
\mathrm{len}(w)
as the length of the reduced form of
w
in
\widetilde{G}
.
A word is
cyclically reduced
if
w
has different starting and ending letters.
One says that two cyclically reduced words
w_{1},w_{2}
are
equivalent
if
w_{1}
is conjugate to
w_{2}
and in this case writes
w_{1}\sim w_{2}
.
A
run of length
n
in a word
w\in G
is an appearance of
(xy)^{\pm n}
,
(yz)^{\pm n}
or
(zx)^{\pm n}
in a reduced form of
w
(or cyclically reduced form, depending on the context).

Let
P
be a pair of pants.
Label the three seams of
P
by
x,y
and
z
.
Denote by
\mathcal{G}_{o}
the collection of closed (not necessarily primitive) oriented geodesics in
P
that are not peripheral.
Denote by
\mathcal{G}_{-}
the collection of geodesic segments between fixed reference points in opposite right hexagons in
P
and
\mathcal{G}_{\upsilon}
the collection of geodesic segments starting and ending at fixed reference points in the same hexagon.
By writing down the ordered intersections of such segments with seams, we get maps from these collections of geodesics to reduced words in the cases of
\mathcal{G}_{\upsilon}
and
\mathcal{G}_{-}
and to equivalence classes of cyclically reduced words in the case of
\mathcal{G}_{o}
.
It follows from basic hyperbolic geometry (lift to the universal cover) that these maps are bijections after basic constraints are adjoined:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{o}

\displaystyle\cong\left\{{\text{cyclically reduced }w\in\widetilde{G}}\text{ so }w\not\sim a^{n},b^{n},c^{n}\,\,(n\in\mathbb{Z})\right\}/\sim,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\upsilon}

\displaystyle\cong\{\text{reduced }w\in\widetilde{G}\text{ with }\mathrm{len}(w)\equiv 0\pmod{2}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{-}

\displaystyle\cong\{\text{reduced }w\in\widetilde{G}\text{ with }\mathrm{len}(w)\equiv 1\pmod{2}\}.

6.2.
Combinatorial Data from Curves

Our task in this subsection is to give concrete senses in which geodesics look similar in their realizations across similarly shaped hyperbolic surfaces.
The source of invariance that we use is the existence of boundary conjugations of
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
between Fuchsian groups.
In analogy to the classification of simple closed curves
[
20
]
, our data for closed curves is obtained in terms of intersections with geodesics adapted to a pants decomposition.

The basic initial observation is:

Lemma 6.4

(Simple Intersection Patterns)
.

Let
\mathscr{S}=\{\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{n}\}
be a finite collection of disjointly realizable simple closed curves and let
\gamma
be an oriented closed curve in
S
.
Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be two marked hyperbolic structures on
S
and let
\gamma_{i}^{X_{j}}
and
\gamma^{X_{j}}
be the geodesic realizations of these curves on
X_{j}
(

).
Let
(c_{k})^{X_{j}}_{k\in I_{j}}
(with
I_{j}=\emptyset
or
\mathbb{Z}
) be the sequence of indices of
\gamma_{c_{k}}^{X_{j}}
that
\gamma^{X_{j}}
intersects in order, with respect to a base-point on
\gamma^{X_{j}}
.

Then there is an order-preserving bijection
I_{1}\to I_{2}
so that after applying this bijection,
(c_{k})^{X_{1}}=(c_{k})^{X_{2}}
.

Proof.

Fix
j\in\{1,2\}
and take geodesic representatives on
X_{j}
, then pick a lift of
\gamma^{X_{j}}
to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
with endpoints
(x_{i},x_{f})\in\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
.
Let
x_{f}
be the positive-time endpoint of
\gamma^{X_{j}}
.
One obtains orientations on each connected component
U_{1},U_{2}
of
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}-\{x_{i},x_{f}\}
by pointing the sides towards
x_{f}
.
Note that a geodesic
\eta
in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
intersects
\gamma^{X_{j}}
if and only if the endpoints of
\eta
are in opposite connected components of
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}-\{x_{i},x_{f}\}
.

Let
{\mathscr{S}}_{j}^{\prime}
be the collection of lifts of elements of
\mathscr{S}_{i}
in
X_{j}
to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
whose endpoints are on different connected components of
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}-\{x_{i},x_{f}\}
.
Note that
{\mathscr{S}}_{j}^{\prime}
is countable and obtains a total order from the orientation of
U_{i}
.
This order separates points because
\mathscr{S}
consists of closed curves and

is closed.
That the geodesics in
\mathscr{S}
are disjointly realizable implies that this ordering is independent of the choice of connected component

.

Note that this construction is independent of the choice of lift of

in the sense that changing the lift changes all relevant constructions by a covering transformation of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, which induces an order-preserving bijection between the relevant sets
{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}_{j}
.

Now if

there is an equivariant homeomorphism
\xi:\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}\to\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
conjugating uniformizing groups for
X_{j}
and
X_{i}
and taking the endpoints of the lift of
\gamma^{X_{j}}
to the endpoints of a lift of
\gamma^{X_{i}}
.
As the boundary map is a homeomorphism and takes endpoints of lifts of elements of
\mathscr{S}
with respect to
X_{j}
to endpoints of lifts of elements of
\mathscr{S}
with respect to
X_{i}
, we obtain an order-preserving bijection between
{\mathscr{S}}_{j}^{\prime}
and
{\mathscr{S}}_{i}^{\prime}
.
One sees that the sequences
(c_{k})^{X_{j}}
are obtained from
{\mathscr{S}}_{j}^{\prime}
by listing the curves that elements of
{\mathscr{S}}_{j}^{\prime}
are lifts of in order, so the claim is proved.
∎

Lemma
6.4
makes the following well-defined.

.

Let

be a closed geodesic in

have all their length coordinates less than

Second,

This can be arranged with Lemma

So let
\rho

For the remaining containment, let

Then by our arrangement of constants, Prop.

\mathcal{C}_{L_{\rho}}

Because cones are semigroups and

As
\gamma

We now handle twisting.
Fix a
\rho

Let

Denote the factor representations of

Let

From Wolpert’s Cosine Formula

7.1.
Finite type surfaces

We briefly describe analogues in the finite-type case.
As in Part I, the statements to Thms.
E
and
F
are modified for surfaces
S_{g,p,b}
with
p
punctures,
b

with the maximum number
\kappa(S_{g,p,b})=3g-3+2b+p

Cor.

For surfaces
S_{g,0,b}
with boundary, these results are deduced by isometrically embedding

into a closed surface and applying Prop.
6.31

.

For surfaces with punctures, it is important to note that the proof of Prop.
6.31
does not directly work due to cusps interfering with the combinatorial structure we use.
However, there is a work-around to this for the desired statements: open up punctures to boundary components, apply Prop.
6.31
, and take a limit as the boundaries are pinched.

We now sketch the analogue of Thm.
E
in the same notation as its proof; the others are similar.
For untwisted
\rho_{t}\in\mathcal{T}(S_{g,p,b})^{n}
under consideration that are deformations of a given
\rho

\rho_{t}
as a limit of
\rho_{t,k}\in\mathcal{T}(S_{g,0,p+b})

\rho_{t}
.
Take a
C
so that
\mathcal{H}_{L_{\rho}}(C)
is contained in the interior of
\mathcal{C}_{L_{\rho}}
.
From continuity of Jordan projections,
\lambda(\rho_{t}(\gamma))=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lambda(\rho_{t,k}(\gamma))
for any
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(S_{g,p,b})
.

Now analyze
\lambda(\rho_{t}(\gamma))
for a fixed
\gamma
.
Apply Prop.
6.31
to
\lambda(\rho_{t,k}(\gamma))
, note that contributions from rotation around the opened cusps become small compared to the size of
\lambda(\rho_{t,k}(\gamma))
as
k
grows large, and take the limit.
That Prop.
6.31
is uniform across all
X,Y\in\mathscr{U}_{\varepsilon}(\mathscr{P})
and curves
\gamma
implies that there is a
\gamma
-independent
a>0
so that
\lambda(\rho_{t}(\gamma))
is the sum of an element of
\mathcal{C}_{L_{\rho}}
and an element of
\mathcal{H}_{L_{\rho}}(C)
for all
t<a

\gamma\in\pi_{1}(S_{g,p,b})

The point is that the values of
k

\gamma

\lambda(\rho_{t}(\gamma))
is
\gamma

This now implies
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t}(\pi_{1}(S_{g,p,b})))=\mathcal{C}_{L_{\rho}}
for all

Twisting is handled as in the closed case.
