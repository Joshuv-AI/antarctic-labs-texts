Chromosome-scale genomes and population resequencing resolve subgenome diversity and halophyte adaptation in Salicornia

ABSTRACT
Amid escalating water scarcity and groundwater depletion, halophytes such as Salicornia (Amaranthaceae) represent valuable models for extreme salt tolerance and hold promise for saltwater-based agriculture. Here, we show chromosome-scale genome assemblies for six Salicornia species, revealing four distinct subgenomes, reconciling our assemblies with two existing reference genomes (S. ramosissima UK and S. europaea China), correcting chromosome numbering and orientation. Comparative analyses across ploidy levels demonstrate genome expansion in North American lineages driven by Gypsy retrotransposons, and lineage-specific expansions of two gene families implicated in stress metabolism. Phylogenetic and population-structure analyses of a global resequencing panel of 318 accessions resolve interspecific relationships and establish curated germplasm collections for future crop breeding. Genetic analyses uncover a contrasting population-genetic signal on chromosome 6A between two species, highlighting an OSCA calcium-permeable channel gene as a candidate locus for osmotic adaptation. Together, these resources establish a genomic framework for Salicornia that supports evolutionary studies of halophyte adaptation and crop development.

Introduction

Water security is increasingly under threat worldwide, with 21 of the 37 major aquifers exceeding sustainable limits1. Compounding the crisis, soil salinisation now affects an estimated 30% of irrigated lands globally2, jeopardising crop productivity and food security. Despite extensive efforts to improve the salt tolerance of crops, gains in yield stability under saline conditions have been modest3. To enable agriculture in water-insecure regions, a paradigm shift is needed—toward the domestication and cultivation of edible halophytic plants capable of thriving in saltwater-based systems3.

Salicornia L., (Amaranthaceae; Salicornioideae), commonly referred to as glasswort or samphire, comprises succulent halophytes cultivated for their edible shoots (sea asparagus)4, and for seeds that are rich in nutrients, proteins and oils that are an excellent source of fats5,6. Most annual Salicornia species are widely distributed across four continents7, while related genera show more restricted distributions, including Sarcocornia (predominantly perennial, with a broad intercontinental range) and Tecticornia (comprising both annual and perennial species, largely endemic to Australia). These taxa occur across coastal salt marshes and inland salt lakes, and can be grown on saline land using seawater, without competing for freshwater or arable land. Salicornia, used generally to describe this taxon, tolerate salinities (>200 mM NaCl; ~12,000 ppm) lethal to most plants8, in part through tissue-level Na⁺ sequestration- referred to as tissue ion tolerance8,9.

Members of the Salicornioideae rank among the most salt-tolerant angiosperms, alongside other highly tolerant relatives within the Amaranthaceae such as Suaeda and Haloxylon. These taxa occupy environments characterised by multiple, overlapping stresses, including high and fluctuating salinity, osmotic stress, ion toxicity, nutrient limitation, and periodic inundation and hypoxia, conditions typical of coastal marshes and inland salt flats10,11. Their extreme tolerance is underpinned by a suite of morphological, anatomical and biochemical adaptations, including reduced leaves forming a succulent sheath around the stem node to limit transpiration, shallow root systems optimised for nutrient uptake, the accumulation of compatible solutes, and water-rich succulent tissues that buffer osmotic stress. Salicornioideae species are Na⁺ accumulators rather than Na⁺ excluders, possessing large vacuoles that enable efficient intracellular ion compartmentalization8.

Despite its ecological and agronomic importance, the taxonomy and evolutionary history of the Salicornioideae remain poorly resolved. Salicornia is thought to have originated in Eurasia, evolving from the perennial Sarcocornia lineage12, and subsequently underwent independent polyploidization events in Eurasia and North America, giving rise to distinct tetraploid lineages13. Although genomic resources for Salicornia have expanded rapidly, ranging from a draft diploid assembly (S. europaea8) and a chromosome-scale assembly of both a diploid (S. ramosissima Woods14) and a tetraploid (S. europaea15), uncertainty persists over how these references relate to local species. Broad taxonomic labels (for example, S. europaea) have been applied to morphologically similar but genetically distinct taxa across wide geographic ranges, contributing to long-standing confusion in species delimitation13. A phylogenetic study using four molecular markers clarified intergeneric relationships within the subfamily, though the limited sampling within Salicornia, including the absence of East Asian and Middle Eastern accessions, left relationships among several tetraploid lineages unresolved7.

Chromosome-scale Salicornia genome assemblies for tetraploid accessions have increased rapidly15,16. However, these resources were generated independently, often without cytogenetic validation, subgenome resolution, or consistent phylogenetic placement. As a result, it remains unclear whether existing reference genomes are representative of local species, particularly in polyploid lineages where subgenomes have not been systematically distinguished. Earlier taxonomic efforts based on limited molecular markers resolved intergeneric relationships but lacked the resolution to address species boundaries and subgenome origins within Salicornia. Comparative-genomic and population-genomic approaches provide powerful tools for resolving the genetic basis of environmental adaptation at gene and genome scales. However, the limited availability of high-quality genomic resources, unresolved subgenome structure, and persistent taxonomic ambiguity have historically constrained the application of these approaches in the Salicornioideae.

Here, we generate chromosome-scale genome assemblies for four diploid and two tetraploid Salicornia species spanning major continents, resolve their polyploidization history and subgenome architecture, and standardise subgenome nomenclature across published and newly generated assemblies. Integrating comparative and population genomic analyses across a globally representative resequencing panel, we clarify phylogenetic relationships and identify lineage-specific structural and copy-number variations underlying salinity-related adaptation. Together, these resources establish a standardised, phylogenetically informed genomic framework for Salicornia that enables robust cross-study comparison and advances evolutionary and comparative analyses of halophyte adaptation.

Four diploid genomes were assembled to 518–570 Mb, whereas the two tetraploid genomes were assembled to 1171 Mb for Salicornia sp. France and 2040 Mb for S. bigelovii (Table 1). Scaffolding anchored an average of 93.8% of contigs into nine chromosomes in diploids and 18 chromosomes in tetraploids, producing highly contiguous assemblies (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2). Omni-C contact maps displayed clear chromosome-scale interaction patterns consistent with expected karyotypes (Supplementary Fig. 2), which were independently validated by cytogenetic analyses (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Assembly quality metrics indicated reference-grade genomes. All assemblies achieved Long Terminal Repeat Assembly Index (LAI18) scores > 10 and Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) completeness values averaging 98.6% (Table 1). Base-level accuracy was further supported by Merqury quality values (QV > 55 for all assemblies). Telomeric repeats (CCCTAAA/TTTAGGG) were identified at 84% of chromosome ends, indicating near-complete chromosomal reconstruction (Table 1).

Gene annotation integrated Iso-Seq, RNA-seq and protein-homology evidence, resulting in an average of 23,008 protein-coding genes in diploids and 42,858 genes in tetraploids, with annotation BUSCO completeness ranging from 96% to 99% (Table 1). Mapping of Illumina short reads back to the assemblies yielded a mean alignment rate of 99.25% (Supplementary Table 2). Variant calling using Illumina data from independently sequenced accessions revealed expected polymorphisms among accessions, whereas mapping Illumina data from the accession used for genome assembly to its corresponding assembly revealed negligible polymorphism (error rate < 0.002%), confirming high assembly accuracy (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Data 2 and 3).

Published Salicornia genomes, including S. depressa16, Chinese tetraploid S. europaea15, and diploid S. ramosissima14, were examined for subgenome assignment and chromosome numbering. Salicornia ramosissima and tetraploid S. europaea showed consistent chromosome numbering and orientation, whereas S. depressa exhibited discrepancies (Supplementary Table 3). Therefore, we harmonised our assemblies with S. ramosissima and Chinese S. europaea, standardising chromosome ordering and orientation (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Figs. 5–7). Structural changes were observed between diploid S. ramosissima (A subgenome) and the C subgenome of Salicornia sp. France, reflecting divergence. Despite numbering and orientation issues, S. depressa showed substantial homology with another United States accession, S. bigelovii, with B–B and A–D subgenome correspondence, as further validated by short-read alignments (Supplementary Note 1). In addition, BUSCO ortholog analysis against Chenopodium quinoa indicated that the published S. depressa16 chromosome numbering was not fully supported (Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Note 1), and we suggest that future assemblies harmonise chromosome numbering and orientation to ensure consistency across Salicornia genomes.

Together, these results establish a set of highly contiguous, well-validated chromosome-scale genomes that provide a robust foundation for subgenome-resolved comparative and population genomic analyses across Salicornia.

Subgenome partitioning using k-mer-based sequence composition (SubPhaser) separated the 18 chromosomes of both S. bigelovii and Salicornia sp. France into two distinct subgenomes (Supplementary Fig. 8). Phylogenetic analysis of coding sequences (CDSs) resolved four distinct genomic lineages within the genus, designated A, B, C, and D (Fig. 2b). Salicornia sp. France comprised A and C subgenomes, whereas S. bigelovii carried B and D subgenomes.

In Salicornia sp. France, an apparent discrepancy between cytogenetic and assembly-based chromosome counts prompted further investigation. Genome analysis identified a 45S rDNA locus located centrally on chromosome 9C, a region showing incomplete chromatin condensation (Supplementary Fig. 9a–d). Targeted Oligo-FISH using chromosome-arm-specific probes and a 45S rDNA probe confirmed that this region caused the visual separation of a single chromosome into two segments, explaining the apparent discrepancy (Supplementary Fig. 9e). These data unambiguously establish Salicornia sp. France as a tetraploid with 2n = 36 chromosomes.

Having resolved karyotype structure, we examined large-scale structural variation across these Salicornia genomes using S. europaea (Israel) as a reference. We identified multiple species-specific chromosomal translocations, including a chromosome 3-8 translocation in S. persica ssp. iranica and three independent translocations distinguishing S. bigelovii from diploid taxa (Fig. 2c), all validated by oligo-painting FISH (Supplementary Fig. 10). Genes flanking all breakpoint regions were retained, indicating that these rearrangements reflect genome reorganisation rather than gene loss.

Polyploid evolution and repeat-driven genome expansion in Salicornia

Polyploidy and transposable element (TE) proliferation are major contributors to plant genome expansion19,20. Comparative repeat annotation across four diploid and two tetraploid Salicornia genomes revealed marked species-specific differences (Fig. 2d). Expansion of Gypsy long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons was the primary driver of genome enlargement in S. bigelovii, accounting for 41.6% of its genome (Supplementary Table 5). In contrast, no comparable subgenome expansion was detected in Salicornia sp. France, indicating divergent post-polyploid evolutionary trajectories.

Phylogenomic analysis across 17 representative Amaranthaceae species placed Salicornia divergence from other family lineages at ~10.1 million years ago (MYA) (Fig. 3a). Synonymous substitution (Ks) analysis of syntenic gene pairs revealed distinct polyploidization histories for the two tetraploid lineages (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 11). Salicornia sp. France exhibited a recent duplication peak at Ks ≈ 3.1 × 10⁻², whereas S. bigelovii showed a more ancient peak at Ks ≈ 5.2 × 10⁻², consistent with independent polyploidization events (Fig. 3b). All diploid species shared an older whole-genome triplication signal (Ks ≈ 1.28–1.58), corresponding to an ancient eudicot-wide event21,22 (Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12). All diploid species also displayed a recent Ks peak among intraspecies paralogous gene pairs (Supplementary Fig. 11). Using a lineage-specific synonymous substitution rate of 6.27 × 10⁻³ substitutions per site per MYA, we dated tetraploid formation to ~4.2 MYA for S. bigelovii and ~2.5 MYA for Salicornia sp. France (Fig. 3b). Ancestral karyotype reconstruction revealed striking chromosomal conservation across the genus, with protein-coding gene order largely preserved and major rearrangements confined to S. bigelovii and S. persica ssp. iranica (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Note 2). Together, these results indicate that polyploid Salicornia genomes have evolved through independent duplication events followed by lineage-specific repeat dynamics, superimposed on an unusually stable chromosomal framework23 (Supplementary Fig. 13 and see Supplementary Note 2).

Comparative-genomic analyses reveal extensive structural variation and gene-family turnover in Salicornia

In addition to polyploidization and karyotype evolution, pairwise whole-genome comparisons revealed pervasive structural variation across the genus Salicornia (Supplementary Table 6). Pairwise comparisons relative to the S. europaea (Israel) reference genome identified 3803 structural variants (SVs) in S. persica ssp. iranica, 7561 in S. sinus-persica and 7378 in S. brachiata (Supplementary Table 6). By contrast, differentiation between the A and C subgenomes of S. sp. France was markedly higher, with the SubC-SubA comparison identifying 19,088 SVs. Hierarchical assignment of SVs to gene models indicated that most variants were intergenic (~79–84%), with few intronic (~11–16%) and exonic (~3–5%) variants (Supplementary Table 7). GO enrichment analysis of SV-overlapping genes revealed both shared and species-specific functional biases in diploid comparisons. A core set of enriched terms was shared among all three species and was associated with ion transport, membrane-related processes and metal-ion homeostasis (Fig. 3c). Species-specific enrichments included phosphate/arsenate transport and salt-response terms in S. persica ssp. iranica, metal-ion sequestration and intracellular homeostasis in S. sinus-persica, and cation transmembrane transport in S. brachiata (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Data 4–6). Collectively, these data highlight gene families implicated in fundamental cellular systems for maintaining ionic balance across membranes, which are also required for survival in saline environments.

Gene-family expansion and contraction analysis identified 46 orthogroups significantly expanded in the Salicornia genus relative to the set of species included in this study (including sampled Amaranthaceae species and Arabidopsis thaliana), (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 14). Expanded families were enriched for genes encoding solute transporters, including major facilitator superfamily (MFS) and amino acid-auxin permease (AAAP) transporters, as well as pathways related to nitrogen and phosphorus uptake, protein homeostasis and signalling, photosystem-II repair, ribosome biogenesis and DNA replication (Supplementary Fig. 15a and Supplementary Data 7). We also examined families encoding ion transport-related proteins known to be central to salt-stress responses, including the Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger (NHX) family (three classes), the Slow-type anion channel-associated (SLAC/SLAH) family (three clades), high-affinity K⁺ transporters (HKT), Calcineurin B-Like proteins (CBL) and Chloride Channels (CLC) (Supplementary Figs. 16–20). Salicornia did not exhibit a marked increase in copy number for any of these families relative to the other Amaranthaceae species analysed (Supplementary Tables 8–10).

In addition to transporter- and stress-associated families, we identified expansions in several metabolic gene families linked to specialised metabolism and stress adaptation. Members of the Cupin_1 superfamily showed copy-number expansion in all members of the halophytic genera Salicornia, Suaeda and Haloxylon examined, suggesting a shared evolutionary response among Amaranthaceae lineages (Fig. 3a). The Cupin superfamily encompasses a diverse set of enzymes involved in redox reactions, metal binding and stress-related metabolism24, and expansion of the poorly characterised clade 1 and potential relevance to high-shoot sodium accumulation and tolerance merit further exploration (Fig. 3a).

Chalcone synthase (CHS) genes underwent expansion exclusively within the Salicornia genus (Fig. 3a). The CHS homologue AT5G13930 (CHS/TT4) encodes a key enzyme in flavonoid biosynthesis (Arabidopsis tt4 null mutants lack flavonoids)25. CHS/TT4 is required for UV-B tolerance, with tt4 mutants lacking CHS being hypersensitive to UV-B irradiation, demonstrating a role for CHS/flavonoids in abiotic-stress protection26.

To test whether these lineage-expanded copies respond to salt stress in a Salicornia-specific manner, we compared DEG calls from two published RNA-seq datasets within Amaranthaceae: S. bigelovii shoots after 6 weeks of 600 mM NaCl8 and Amaranthus hypochondriacus leaves after a 14-d stepped salinity regime27-two species that contrast sharply in Na⁺ accumulation capacity. Among the S. bigelovii CHS orthologs, no copy reached DEG statistical significance (Padj < 0.05) in either subgenome, whereas both A. hypochondriacus CHS orthologs were statistically significant DEGs (Padj < 0.001). For the Cupin 1 family, only one S. bigelovii ortholog (SaBi02252, B subgenome) reached DEG statistical significance (Padj < 0.001), and no A. hypochondriacus ortholog was statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 15b, c). Thus, the salt-accumulating S. bigelovii has remodelled these lineage-expanded families, in both copy number and salt response, different to the non-accumulating A. hypochondriacus.

The k-mer-based neighbour-joining (NJ) phylogeny revealed clear separation of major evolutionary lineages within Salicornia (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 21, see Supplementary Note 2 for further discussion). Two deeply divergent tetraploid clades were resolved: a lineage comprising Salicornia sp. France together with S. procumbens and S. veneta, and a lineage corresponding to S. bigelovii from the Americas. Within S. bigelovii, accessions from the United States and Mexico clustered tightly, consistent with geographic origin.

Diploid taxa formed well-supported clades corresponding broadly to species and region. Middle Eastern diploids grouped into distinct lineages including S. sinus-persica and S. persica ssp. iranica from Saudi Arabia, and a separate S. europaea lineage from Israel. S. europaea accessions from the United Arab Emirates clustered with S. sinus-persica, while Egyptian accessions formed a small subclade within Israeli S. europaea (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 22), highlighting ongoing taxonomic ambiguity within nominal S. europaea. Smaller collections from China and the Netherlands formed a distinct subclade clustering with S. brachiata from India (Fig. 4); given limited sampling (Supplementary Data 1), this pattern likely reflects divergence from Middle Eastern S. europaea rather than close affinity among these regions.

Principal component analysis (PCA) supported the phylogenetic results (Supplementary Fig. 22). The primary axis of variation (PC1, 33%) separated tetraploids, with AC-subgenome Salicornia sp. France positioned opposite BD-subgenome S. bigelovii. Diploid accessions occupied intermediate positions, showing closer affinity to AC tetraploids. Outgroup taxa (Tecticornia spp.) also mapped between the two tetraploid clusters (Supplementary Fig. 22). Together, these analyses reveal complex population structure shaped by ploidy, geography and historical divergence, underscoring the need for genome-informed curation, increased sample size and standardised taxonomic frameworks within Salicornia.

Nomenclature of Salicornia sp. France and relationship with a published S. europaea tetraploid

Salicornia germplasm collected from the Étang de Thau lagoon (France) are locally classified as S. europaea (INPN CD_NOM identifier 119881, https://inpn.mnhn.fr/), yet phylogenomic analysis places them in a distinct sister clade to S. procumbens, separate from diploid S. europaea (Fig. 4). Although these French accessions share chromosome number and overall subgenomic composition with other tetraploids (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 9e), they form a coherent and genetically distinct lineage (data link available in Supplementary Note 3).

To assess their relationship to the recently published Chinese tetraploid S. europaea genome15, we mapped short reads from the French tetraploid and other accessions to the Salicornia T2T reference (Supplementary Fig. 23a, b and Supplementary Data 8). French tetraploid reads aligned almost exclusively to a single subgenome, whereas the second subgenome showed negligible representation (Supplementary Fig. 23). In contrast, tetraploid S. bigelovii showed low overall alignment, with reads primarily mapping from its D subgenome, indicating a more distant relationship (Supplementary Data 8 and Supplementary Fig. 23a). These comparisons demonstrate that one subgenomic component of the French tetraploid, here designated the C subgenome, is not represented in the published Chinese tetraploid assembly.

Based on its phylogenetic distinctness, subgenome composition, and lack of correspondence to existing reference genomes, we designate the French tetraploid as ‘Salicornia sp. France’. This tetraploid represents a distinct species and provides a valuable genomic resource, including a subgenome not present in existing references.

Read-depth maps, ploidy confirmation, and A-subgenome relationships

Read-depth mapping of all accessions to an in silico octaploid reference (combined two tetraploid genomes) confirmed unambiguous subgenome assignments across the collection (Supplementary Fig. 24; further analyses are presented in Supplementary Note 4). Diploid accessions mapped exclusively to the A subgenome, whereas tetraploids mapped to either AC or BD subgenome combinations, validating ploidy and genome composition. The read-mapping profiles demonstrate clear subgenome differentiation; for instance, S. bigelovii (SalD_109) reads mapped predominantly to the B and D subgenomes, whereas Salicornia sp. France (SalD_100) reads aligned almost entirely to the A and C subgenomes, with negligible cross-mapping (≤2%) to alternative subgenomes (Supplementary Fig. 25). These HISAT2-based subgenome-specific alignment patterns demonstrate high mapping fidelity, with negligible cross-subgenome and homoeologous alignments (≤2%), supporting subgenome assignment and the robustness of downstream genomic analyses (Supplementary Fig. 25).

Closely related genera (Tecticornia and Sarcocornia) showed negligible alignment, indicating that their genomes are distinct from Salicornia (Supplementary note 4). Within diploid S. europaea, accessions from Israel, the United Arab Emirates, and Egypt formed genetically distinct clades (Fig. 4), consistent with substantial geographic structure and suggesting unresolved species boundaries.

To examine the origin of the A subgenome in tetraploid Salicornia sp. France, diploid and tetraploid accessions were mapped to the A-subgenome chromosomes and analysed using ~1.4 million high-quality SNPs (Supplementary Fig. 26a, b and Supplementary Data 9). Further description of SNP filtering and mapping is provided in Supplementary Note 5. Phylogenetic analysis placed the tetraploid accessions in a distinct clade, separate from all diploid species. No diploid lineage showed sufficiently high alignment or phylogenetic proximity to be identified as a direct progenitor, indicating that the A subgenome likely derives from an unsampled or extinct diploid lineage (Supplementary Fig. 26a, b and Supplementary Data 9).

SNP genotyping, population structure and gene flow in diploid Salicornia

Genome-wide SNP analysis of all diploid Salicornia identified ~10.5 million high-quality biallelic variants after filtering (Supplementary Fig. 27a, b and Supplementary Note 5). SNP density and allele-frequency spectra varied across lineages, with diploids showing an excess of rare variants, consistent with population subdivision and demographic history (Supplementary Fig. 27). In contrast, the tetraploid Salicornia sp. France exhibited a more uniform SNP distribution and a higher proportion of intermediate-frequency alleles across its A subgenome, indicating distinct evolutionary dynamics relative to diploids (Supplementary Fig. 27e, f).

Population-structure analysis revealed pronounced genetic differentiation and admixture among diploid taxa (Fig. 5a; further analyses provided in Supplementary Note 6). At low K, S. sinus-persica clustered closely with S. europaea from the United Arab Emirates, whereas S. europaea from Israel and Egypt showed stronger affinity to S. persica ssp. iranica. Considerable gene flow was detected between S. europaea populations from Israel and Egypt, whereas introgression between S. sinus-persica and S. europaea (Israel) was weaker but detectable (Supplementary Table 11). Genetic diversity among diploid Salicornia species

Genome-wide estimates of nucleotide diversity (π) and Nei’s index revealed pronounced contrasts among diploid Salicornia (further analyses provided in Supplementary Note 7). Coastal populations showed substantially higher diversity than inland populations, with S. europaea from the United Arab Emirates and S. sinus-persica having the highest π and Nei’s index, whereas S. persica ssp. iranica-the only inland population sampled, displayed extremely low diversity (Supplementary Table 12). This low diversity could be due to a recent founder event or a strong demographic bottleneck in this population, which inhabits an inland salt lake that is geographically isolated from coastal seed-dispersal routes. Environmental salinity at the inland site was broadly comparable to that of coastal habitats (electrical conductivity (EC) ≈ 55 dS m⁻¹ in the inland salt lake vs ≈60 dS m⁻¹ in Red seawater; water Na ≈ 1.2% vs 1.2–3.5%), suggesting that factors such as population size, isolation, and demographic history, rather than salinity alone, are more likely to explain the observed disparity.

In addition, the allele-frequency spectrum of S. persica ssp. iranica based on 2,785,227 biallelic SNPs (Supplementary Fig. 27c) is characterized by an excess of rare alleles and a marked deficit of alleles at intermediate and high minor-allele frequencies (MAF) (Supplementary Fig. 27d). Such a skewed spectrum is consistent with a recent demographic expansion following a severe reduction in effective population size, a pattern commonly associated with founder events but also potentially arising from bottlenecks followed by recovery. Without explicit demographic modelling, these alternative scenarios cannot be conclusively distinguished.

Patterns of population differentiation mirrored these trends. Pairwise FST values indicated low differentiation between S. europaea (UAE) and S. sinus-persica, moderate differentiation between S. europaea (Israel) and S. persica ssp. iranica, and strong differentiation among most other diploid comparisons (Supplementary Table 13). Together, these results demonstrate that diploid Salicornia diversity is structured by geography, habitat type, and demographic history (Supplementary Table 13, Fig. 5a, and Supplementary Note 7).

Genome-wide Tajima’s D and detection of a candidate OSCA region

Genome-wide scans of Tajima’s D revealed distinct allele-frequency patterns between tetraploid species, Salicornia sp. France and S. procumbens, with the most pronounced contrast occurring on chromosome 6A (Supplementary Fig. 28a and Supplementary Figs. 29a, 30a, 31a). Within this interval (~33–36 Mb), S. procumbens populations from the North Sea/Wadden Sea exhibited predominantly negative Tajima’s D values, whereas Salicornia sp. France showed a strongly positive Tajima’s D peak (Supplementary Fig. 28a and Supplementary Fig. 31a).

To ensure that the uneven sample sizes between the two species (N = 71 for Salicornia sp. France vs N = 12 for S. procumbens) did not skew our statistics, we performed a down-sampling control experiment. Randomly down-sampling the Salicornia sp. France population into independent subsets matching the sample size of S. procumbens (N = 12) and re-running Tajima’s D scans consistently reproduced identical, contrasting distribution patterns on chromosome 6A. This confirms that the observed divergence at this locus is a robust biological signal rather than an artefact of sample size imbalances or uneven sampling density.

This genomic interval contains an OSCA-family gene (ortholog of At4g02900) (Supplementary Fig. 28a, b and peptide FASTA sequence in Supplementary Data 10). This gene family play a key role in osmotic-stress-induced calcium signalling28,29. To provide environmental context for this region, we examined metadata collected at the sampling sites, including soil EC. The Mediterranean Étang de Thau exhibited high soil EC at the time of seed collection (EC: 23.9 dS m⁻¹), whereas sites from the North Sea (1.6 dS m⁻¹) and the Wadden Sea (5.8 dS m⁻¹) showed substantially lower salinity levels at the time of collection (Supplementary Data 1). In addition, the Étang de Thau experiences strong seasonal fluctuations in salinity and temperature30.

Genome-wide nucleotide diversity in AC-subgenome tetraploids

A genome-wide comparison of nucleotide diversity (π) revealed that S. procumbens exhibits overall higher genetic diversity than Salicornia sp. France (Supplementary Figs. 29b and 30b). In Salicornia sp. France, nucleotide diversity is largely uniform across all 18 chromosomes. In contrast, S. procumbens shows higher genome-wide nucleotide diversity for A-subgenome chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 29a). This widespread distribution suggests relatively high standing genetic variation and a more heterogeneous genomic landscape across the A subgenome. A localised increase in nucleotide diversity is observed on chromosome 6A around ~33–36 Mb, for which π of Salicornia sp. France exceeds that of S. procumbens. This region coincides with contrasting Tajima’s D signals between the two species, and encompasses the OSCA/CSC1-like locus (Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29).

Local FST across the chromosome 6A OSCA locus

To further characterise genetic differentiation across the chromosome 6A interval encompassing the OSCA locus (approximately 33–36 Mb), we performed a sliding-window FST analysis (10-kb windows, 5-kb steps) using balanced population sizes (12 individuals per species). Genetic differentiation varied across this interval, with a broad region of elevated FST transitioning into a localised reduction in differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 32). The OSCA/CSC1-like locus lies within this transition, where FST shifts from relatively elevated to lower values. Together with the contrasting Tajima’s D and nucleotide diversity patterns, these results identify the OSCA region as a distinctive population-genetic interval between the two species.

Reference-independent validation and structural variation at chromosome 6A

To rule out assembly artefacts or multi-copy cross-mapping within the draft Salicornia sp. France reference, we re-mapped all whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data against an independent diploid S. ramosissima genome. Tajima’s D and contrasting peaks at chromosome 6A were recapitulated (Supplementary Figs. 28a and 29), demonstrating that reference choice does not alter the underlying population dynamics. Genomic-coverage-depth analysis revealed distinct SVs around the target region. When mapped against Salicornia sp. France, a small, fixed deletion at ~30 Mb was ubiquitously present across both species (genome coverage maps at Dryad Repository). However, at the 33–36 Mb region harbouring the OSCA locus, SV was polymorphic, in that S. procumbens individuals lacked deletions entirely, whereas Salicornia sp. France individuals exhibited a segregating presence/absence mosaic. High-resolution local haplotype tracking across the OSCA exons (~35.8 Mb on S. ramosissima) showed that S. procumbens maintains a uniform, fixed haplotype block consistent with a selective sweep (Supplementary Fig. 28b). In contrast, Salicornia sp. France preserves a hyper-variable mosaic of reference, alternative, and heterozygous alleles interspersed with localised deletion tracks (Supplementary Figs. 28b and 29b).

Discussion

In this study, we combined chromosome-scale genome assemblies with population-scale resequencing to resolve the evolutionary history, genomic architecture, and adaptive diversity of the extreme halophyte genus Salicornia. Evolutionary analysis of our high-quality reference genomes resolved four subgenomes, A, B, C, and D, within the tetraploid Salicornia species (Fig. 2); however, a close extant diploid relative could be confidently identified only for subgenome A (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 24). We assembled high-quality reference genomes for two tetraploid species, Salicornia sp. France and S. bigelovii. S. bigelovii possesses an enlarged genome associated with substantial proliferation of LTR retrotransposons (Table 1 and Fig. 2). Extant diploid relatives corresponding to the B and D subgenomes could not be identified in this study, meaning that the timing of divergence between subgenome A and the B/D progenitors could not be inferred. These inferences therefore reflect the lineages sampled in this study and provide a robust evolutionary framework for the major diploid and tetraploid Salicornia groups examined. Broader genomic sampling across additional Salicornioideae genera from multiple continents will be required to fully resolve deeper evolutionary relationships within the subfamily, test the generality of this model, and identify potential progenitors of the B and D subgenomes.

The identification of an extant diploid lineage corresponding to subgenome A enabled a focused comparison of genetic diversity across diploid Salicornia taxa. Specifically, the A genome of S. europaea, S. sinus-persica, and S. persica ssp. iranica exhibits substantial variation in genetic diversity (Supplementary Table 12). A similar pattern is observed in other wild crop relatives; for example, in wild wheat, the A genome carried by diploids Triticum urartu and T. monococcum has high genetic diversity. High genetic diversity likely allows Salicornia populations to exist across steep environmental gradients and to respond plastically to fluctuating conditions, a pattern consistent with the extensive ecological breadth and population structure of our resequencing panel (Figs. 1 and 4 and Supplementary Data 1) and that was previously reported across the genus by Kadereit et al.13.

To examine how this diversity is structured and filtered by local selective pressures following polyploid formation, we analysed population-genetic patterns within the A subgenome of tetraploid Salicornia accessions. Comparisons of A sub-genome diversity across diploid and tetraploid species revealed pronounced genome-wide variation in SNP density and rare-variant distributions, consistent with differences in demographic histories and population structure (Supplementary Fig. 27). Within chromosome 6A, S. procumbens and Salicornia sp. France exhibited contrasting patterns of Tajima’s D and nucleotide diversity, together with localised variation in population differentiation surrounding the OSCA/CSC1-like locus (Supplementary Figs. 28 and 32). Independent remapping to the S. ramosissima reference genome and local haplotype analyses recovered comparable patterns, indicating that the signal is robust to reference genome choice. The sampled populations occupy environmentally distinct coastal habitats, with S. procumbens occurring in relatively stable, lower-salinity environments and Salicornia sp. France inhabiting a more variable, higher-salinity Mediterranean lagoon. Given the established role of OSCA-family genes in osmotic stress signalling, these environmental differences provide ecological context for the observed population-genetic patterns.

Although the coincidence of contrasting population-genetic patterns with environmental differences is consistent with the known biological function of the OSCA/CSC1-like gene, the present data do not distinguish among alternative evolutionary mechanisms underlying the broader genomic region. Similar patterns may arise through balancing selection, directional selection, demographic history, ancestral polymorphism, historical introgression, or local structural variation. Consequently, we conservatively interpret the chromosome 6A OSCA region as a candidate adaptive genomic interval exhibiting a distinctive population-genetic signature rather than as definitive evidence for any specific mode of selection. Future studies integrating functional characterisation, environmental association analyses, and structural variant detection will be required to resolve the evolutionary processes shaping this region.

To place patterns of local adaptation in a broader evolutionary context, we examined population structure and gene flow across Salicornia using a large, globally representative resequencing panel. The population phylogenetic grouping conducted in this study used a k-mer-based approach, distinguishing it from traditional alignment-based methods. Previous population-genomic studies were limited by smaller sample sizes and fewer species31–33. Given the varying ploidy levels among the species studied, we opted for a k-mer approach rather than SNP calling across the entire collection. As a result, our population-genomics analysis provides improved delineation among Salicornia lineages and provides preliminary resolution of relationships with Tecticornia, and Sarcocornia. We anticipate that expanded reference genome resources for these related genera will further refine taxonomic and evolutionary relationships within the Salicornioideae.

Middle Eastern Salicornia species were well represented in the resequencing panel, allowing assessment of gene flow among regional lineages. Minimal introgression was detected between S. sinus-persica (United Arab Emirates) and S. europaea (Israel), consistent with long-term isolation and an ancient divergence. In contrast, we observed strong introgression signals between S. europaea populations from Israel and Egypt (Supplementary Table 11), indicating substantial, relatively recent gene flow, suggesting that these populations belong to a closely related or recently differentiated gene pool. Thus, while geographically proximate S. europaea populations appear to exchange genetic material, the S. europaea lineage from the United Arab Emirates has maintained a deeper and more persistent divergence from the Mediterranean S. europaea complex.

Persistence of the chromosome 6A Tajima’s D signal across more than one independent reference genome indicates that it is not a technical and assembly-related artefact of mapping. However, a chromosome-scale reference genome is not yet available for S. procumbens; reads from this species were therefore mapped to a congeneric reference (Salicornia sp. France or S. ramosissima), which may introduce mapping bias for this population specifically. The co-localisation of an elevated Tajima’s D and structural presence/absence variations at the ~35 Mb genomic region warrants careful interpretation. In complex polyploid plant genomes, segregated structural variants, including hemizygous deletions, can influence read alignment and genotype calling, potentially affecting allele-frequency-based statistics such as Tajima’s D.

Alternatively, if the observed genotype calls reflect genuine biological variation rather than technical artefacts, the elevated local heterozygosity in Salicornia sp. France relative to the surrounding chromosomal background (Supplementary Figs. 28b and 31b) suggests the maintenance of multiple haplotypes within this genomic interval. Given the known role of the OSCA/CSC1-like gene in osmotic stress signalling and the contrasting environmental conditions occupied by the two populations, this pattern is biologically intriguing. However, the present data do not distinguish among alternative evolutionary mechanisms that could generate this signature, including balancing selection, directional selection, demographic history, ancestral polymorphism, historical introgression, or the effects of local structural variation. Consequently, we conservatively interpret the chromosome 6A OSCA region as a candidate adaptive genomic interval requiring further validation rather than as evidence for any specific selective process. Resolving the evolutionary basis of this region will require larger population samples, particularly from S. procumbens, together with high-depth targeted resequencing or long-read phased assemblies. Integrating these data within a pangenome framework will enable accurate characterisation of structural variants and haplotype architecture across the region, helping to distinguish technical artefacts from genuine evolutionary processes.

These population-scale patterns provide important insight into long-standing taxonomic ambiguities within the Salicornia genus. Diploid S. europaea from Israel and the United Arab Emirates bear higher genetic diversity than most other species examined. S. europaea from Israel and Egypt were genetically closer to S. persica ssp. iranica, whereas S. europaea from the United Arab Emirates clustered more closely with S. sinus-persica (Fig. 5); the latter reclassification of species is supported by more common use of this species name in the United Arab Emirates and neighbouring regions including Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, and Qatar34–36. Together, these findings highlight the need for increased attention on taxonomic resolution and a standardised classification of Salicornia germplasm worldwide to support genetic research and conservation efforts of species growing in at-risk ecosystems37,38.

Genomic insights also underscore the vulnerability of Salicornia populations to habitat loss. Salicornia spp. are classified as taxa of high conservation priority in Saudi Arabia39 and resurveys of sites documented in 2017 revealed that only two of six populations persisted after four years36, with losses along the eastern coast largely attributable to industrial and residential development. This pattern is particularly concerning given that inland Salicornia communities, such as S. persica iranica, harbour lower genetic diversity (Supplementary Table 12) than coastal communities such as S. sinus-persica (Saudi Arabia) or S. europaea (United Arab Emirates), suggesting reduced adaptive capacity in already fragmented inland habitats. While salinity levels are broadly similar, other factors - such as population size, isolation, and historical bottlenecks may contribute to this disparity. Together, these patterns of genetic diversity and population vulnerability highlight both the urgency and opportunity for genomics-informed intervention in Salicornia.

This study reveals that Salicornia germplasms worldwide exhibit considerable genetic diversity, with distinct species maintaining substantial genetic distances, positioning them as valuable resources for selective breeding and hybridisation. Given the limited breeding efforts in Salicornia to date, considerable potential exists for crop development through structured and genomics-informed breeding programs. However, cross-species hybridisation has historically been challenging13, a pattern that aligns with our own observations, and may be attributable to strong inbreeding promoted by floral-organ traits such as early anther dehiscence in diploids or the close anther positions favouring inbreeding in the polyploids. The resolution of AACC and BBDD tetraploid genome architectures presented here provides a framework for understanding the genetic and subgenomic bases of these reproductive barriers and supports genomics-assisted breeding strategies for trait improvement, including marker-based selection with compatible gene pools and targeted gene editing approaches where cross-species barriers exist.

The genomic resources generated for Salicornia now rival those established for quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa), the first allopolyploid Amaranthaceae crop with chromosome-scale reference and diversity-panel assemblies40,41. As in quinoa, we observe clear subgenome differentiation in Salicornia: homoeologous collinearity, divergence in repeat and gene content, and structural variation between subgenomes. The comparable depth and breadth of genomic resources in Salicornia therefore lay the groundwork for trait-mapping efforts analogous to those already underway in quinoa.

To facilitate integration of existing and future genomic datasets, we also standardised subgenomes nomenclature across Salicornia. Previous studies labelled the two subgenomes of tetraploid S. europaea (China) and tetraploid S. depressa as A and B; however, phylogenetic analyses based on homologous proteins indicate that these subgenomes are not evolutionarily equivalent (Supplementary Fig. 33). By reconstructing phylogenies using four diploid and two allotetraploid genomes, we reassigned subgenome designations according to clade membership (Supplementary Fig. 33). This unified nomenclature will facilitate data integration, comparative analyses and community-wide progress in Salicornia genomics and breeding. By harmonising chromosome numbering and orientation across Salicornia genomes, we aim to reduce confusion in future research and facilitate consistent usage of these assemblies in the community. Corrected subgenome assignments and standardised chromosome frameworks (Supplementary Note 1) provide a reference for comparative analyses, breeding, and functional genomics, ensuring that structural and orientation discrepancies do not hinder downstream studies.

Our comparative genomic analyses reveal lineage-specific remodelling of ion-transport-related gene families across Salicornia and related halophytes. We detected an Amaranthaceae-specific SLAC clade II (when compared to Arabidopsis; Supplementary Fig. 20), convergent expansion of Cupin_1 across high shoot-sodium-accumulating halophytes (Fig. 3a), and a Salicornia-specific increase in Chalcone synthase (CHS/TT4) copy number (Supplementary Fig. 34). Together, these patterns suggest that core physiological challenges posed by saline environments, such as ion homeostasis, redox balance and protective specialised metabolism, are primarily addressed through reorganisation and amplification of conserved stress-response pathways rather than entirely novel mechanisms. However, copy-number expansion alone does not demonstrate functional relevance, as some expansions may reflect neutral genomic turnover. Discriminating adaptive from neutral variation will require targeted functional analyses. Such focused tests will prioritise candidates for deeper mechanistic investigations and help clarify evolutionary routes to extreme salt tolerance. Future functional validation of these candidate genes will involve the use of heterologous systems such as yeast8, as well as the development of Salicornia-specific genetic tools.

In summary, our chromosome-scale genome assemblies, together with phylogenomic and population-structure profiling, chart the genomic and genetic landscape within Salicornia. These resources facilitate future functional studies of salinity adaptation and establish a framework for genomics-enabled improvement of Salicornia. In the context of escalating freshwater scarcity and increasing seawater intrusion into groundwater and low-lying agricultural regions, salt-tolerant crops such as Salicornia offer a promising path towards food security resilience for coastal and delta-dwelling communities1,42.

Methods

Plant materials

A global germplasm collection of 318 accessions was used for this study, sourced from both wild locations and seed banks from 13 countries (Supplementary Data 1, see Data Availability). Six accessions were maintained as single-seed descent inbreds and chosen for genome assemblies, whilst the entire panel was resequenced using WGS. Each accession was assigned a unique identifier (e.g. ‘SalD_#’ where # represents a number).

For accessions originating from France (FR), Saudi Arabia (KSA), Egypt (EG), the Netherlands (NL), Mexico (MX), China (CH), and the Republic of Korea (ROK), seeds were collected directly from individual wild plants, and corresponding DNA samples were generated from progeny plants derived from those accessions (Supplementary Data 1). For accessions obtained from external collections or collaborators (United States, United Arab Emirates, Israel, Australia, India and Italy), seed lots frequently exhibited morphological segregation during propagation, suggesting that some original collections may have contained mixed maternal lineages. To reduce this heterogeneity, individual plants were isolated during propagation and treated as independent accessions for downstream analyses. Where possible, these lines were advanced through one generation of selfing prior to DNA extraction and WGS (Supplementary Data 1).

Salicornia species were grown for total RNA isolation in a hydroponic ebb and flow growth system in a salt treatment (200 mM NaCl) in the KAUST greenhouse (22°18'31.1“ʻ N 39°06'27.8″ E). Seeds were germinated according to the protocol available on protocols.io (https://doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.kqdg394xpg25/v1). Seedlings were transplanted to the hydroponic system according to the protocol described on protocols.io (https://doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.n2bvjb4rpgk5/v1). Whole shoots and roots were collected from two individual plants (42 days after transplanting into the hydroponic units) whilst bract tips were collected at early anthesis.

DNA used for WGS was isolated from young stems collected from Salicornia spp. plants grown to 20 cm height in a pot (10 × 8 cm) with a mixture of soil (three parts Stender potting soil: one part sand) and watered with diluted natural seawater at a salinity of 20 gkg−1 prepared by diluting natural seawater with deionised water.

Genome assembly and validation

The amount of nuclear DNA content in Salicornia, Sarcocornia and Tecticornia species was measured by flow cytometry according to Salazar et al.8 and further detailed in Supplementary Note 8. High-molecular-weight DNA was isolated from young secondary stems of a single plant following a 48-h dark treatment and using a modified CTAB protocol (see Supplementary Note 9). Subsequently, HiFi libraries were constructed and sequenced on a PacBio Sequel II system in circular consensus sequencing (CCS) mode (Supplementary Note 9). Omni-C libraries were constructed from young secondary stems of a single plant following a 48-h dark treatment using the Dovetail Omni-C Kit for plant tissues. Library preparation and sequencing were performed as described by Athiyannan et al.43.

Contig-level assemblies for six Salicornia species were generated by using hifiasm (v2.28) with default parameters44 (Supplementary Note 10). Omni-C contact maps were then generated using the 3d-dna pipeline45 (v180922), followed by manual inspection and correction using Juicebox (v2.17.00)45. Salicornia sp. France was scaffolded using the HapHic pipeline (v1.0.5) with default parameters46. Omni-C contact maps were generated using the HapHic pipeline (v1.0.5) with default parameters46. Chromosome identifiers were assigned following the numbering and orientation of the Salicornia ramosissima reference assembly14.

BUSCO (v5.7.1) with the Viridiplantae database was used to assess genome completeness47. QUAST (v5.2.0) was used to calculate N50, N90, and other parameters48. LAI in LTR_Finder (v1.0.7) was used to evaluate genome assembly quality49. The TeloExplorer function from quarTeT (v1.2.0) was used to find telomeric repeats50. Genome-quality assessment details are provided in Supplementary Note 10.

Oligomers specific for individual chromosome arms were identified in the reference genome sequence of diploid S. europaea (Israel) using the Chorus v1.1 pipeline51, and further details are provided in Supplementary Note 11. 45S rDNA probe was also prepared. Fluorescence in situ hybridisation and image-analysis details are provided in Supplementary Note 11. Probe sequences for all oligo-FISH libraries are provided in Supplementary Data 11.

Salicornia europaea (Israel) was employed as the reference genome to map the tetraploid genomes of S. bigelovii and Salicornia sp. France using D-GENIES (v1.5.0) to identify paired homologous chromosomes in these species52. Further subgenome classification steps are provided in Supplementary Note 12.

Genome annotation

Iso-Seq libraries were prepared from whole shoots of vegetative-stage plants and bract tips with flowers collected at anthesis-stage plants of six Salicornia species. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) and treated with the DNA-free™ DNA Removal Kit (Invitrogen) to remove residual genomic DNA. Library preparation followed the PacBio Iso-Seq™ Express Template Preparation protocol41, and sequencing was performed on the Sequel II System at Novogene Co., Ltd.

For the Iso-Seq analysis of the six Salicornia genomes, HiFi reads were produced using the CCS application (v6.4.0) with the continuous long read subread dataset and default parameters. Subsequently, the Iso-Seq tool (v4.0.0) (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/IsoSeq) was employed to trim primer sequences and polyadenylation tails, yielding de novo full-length non-chimeric (FLNC) transcripts. These FLNC transcripts were then merged with RNA-seq data, and duplicate sequences were removed prior to downstream mapping and annotation.

Salicornia europaea (Israel) and S. bigelovii RNA-seq data were sourced from the publicly available database PRJNA7338928. Raw sequencing reads were aligned to the respective S. europaea (Israel) or S. bigelovii reference genomes using HISAT2 (v2.2.1)53. The SAM output files were then converted into BAM format using samtools (v1.16.1), and transcript assemblies were generated from these alignments via Trinity (v2.15.1) using the genome_guided_bam model54,55.

The Extensive de novo TE Annotator (EDTA) pipeline (v2.0.0), RepeatModeler (v2.0.1), and RepeatMasker (v4.1.2) were employed for repeat sequence identification in our six Salicornia genomes56,57. These repeat sequences were soft masked in our genomes and then used for gene model predictions. The BRAKER3 pipeline, including GeneMark-ETP and AUGUSTUS with integrated transcript and protein sequences, was used for gene-model predictions58. The longest representative protein sequences from BRAKER3 results were input to BUSCO (v5.7.1) with the Viridiplantae database used to assess annotation completeness47. For gene functional annotation, functional domains and potential GO terms, the longest representative protein sequence of each gene was used as a query for the eggNOG-mapper (v2) online tool by using NCBI NR, Swissprot, GO, KEGG, and Pfam databases59.

Synteny plots among the six Salicornia genomes, excluding gene-based collinearity, were generated using GenomeSyn (v1.41)60. In this analysis, extension sequences were segmented into 500-kb fragments to facilitate subsequent collinearity assessments. Genome alignments were then executed with minimap2 (v2.21) using its default parameters.

Phylogenetic analysis

To infer the phylogenetic position of Salicornia within Amaranthaceae and to resolve relationships within the genus, we selected a representative set of species spanning major lineages of the family, see Supplementary Note 13 for species details.

Orthologous groups and orthologs across genomes were identified using OrthoFinder (v2.4.0) with default parameters, and gene families were clustered accordingly61. A species tree was constructed based on high-confidence 1140 single-copy orthologous genes using RAxML (v8.0.0)62. Divergence times were estimated with MCMCTREE (v1.0) and calibrated according to the TIMETREE database63. Convergence of the MCMCTREE analyses was assessed in Tracer (v1.7.2) by inspecting trace plots and effective sample sizes (Supplementary Fig. 35).

Subgenome phylogenomic analysis

To reconstruct phylogenetic relationships within the genus Salicornia, we adopted a hierarchical outgroup strategy. Arabidopsis thaliana was included as the distant outgroup to maintain consistency with the family-level analysis (Fig. 2B), as it provides an appropriate evolutionary distance for rooting and possesses one of the most comprehensive and well-curated genome annotations currently available. Spinacia tetrandra was additionally included as a closely related outgroup, because it represents the closest available telomere-to-telomere (T2T) genome assembly within Amaranthaceae outside of the genus Salicornia, providing a high-quality near-outgroup for resolving fine-scale relationships within the genus.

To assess the similarity of these genomes, we identified 4977 single-copy orthologous genes shared among S. tetrandra, A. thaliana, S. brachiata, S. sinus-persica, S. europaea (Israel), S. persica ssp. iranica, Salicornia sp. France subgenomes A and C, and S. bigelovii subgenomes B and D. For each genome, only one representative CDS per gene was retained, corresponding to the longest CDS, to avoid redundancy caused by alternative splicing.

Single-copy orthologous groups were inferred using OrthoFinder (v2.4.0) based on predicted gene models, and orthogroups containing exactly one gene copy in each genome were selected for downstream analyses. The CDSs corresponding to these single-copy orthologs were extracted and used directly for multiple sequence alignment.

Multiple sequence alignments (MSA) were performed at the nucleotide level using the CDS of each single-copy ortholog. CDS alignments were generated with MAFFT (v7.505) using nucleotide-alignment mode and default parameters. To minimise the influence of poorly aligned or ambiguous regions, each CDS alignment was trimmed using trimAl in automated mode. The remaining high-quality CDS alignments were concatenated into a single supermatrix for phylogenetic inference. A partition file defining one partition per gene was generated to allow for gene-wise heterogeneity in substitution patterns. Species trees were reconstructed from the concatenated CDS supermatrix using IQ-TREE (v3.0.1) under a partitioned nucleotide model, with the best-fit substitution model for each partition selected by ModelFinder. Branch support was assessed using 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates.

To complement this nucleotide-level analysis and to control for potential frame-shift artefacts, we additionally performed a codon-aware reconstruction of the same 4977 single-copy orthogroups. Protein multiple-sequence alignments produced by OrthoFinder (MAFFT, default parameters) were back-translated to codon-aware CDS alignments using PAL2NAL v14. Ambiguously aligned regions were filtered with trimAl v1.5 with the -automated1 option, with the trimming projected onto codon coordinates through the -backtrans option (-ignorestopcodon enabled) to strictly preserve codon integrity. Seven orthogroups in which the CDS could not be unambiguously back-translated to the protein were excluded, leaving 4970 codon-aware alignments that were concatenated into a supermatrix of 7,442,829 bp across 10 taxa. Phylogenetic inference was performed with IQ-TREE (v3.0.1) under the best-fit model selected by ModelFinder (GTR + F + R10, BIC), and branch support was assessed by 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates together with 1000 SH-aLRT replicates. As a further cross-validation, a parallel reconstruction was performed directly on the concatenated protein supermatrix (2,480,943 amino-acid sites, Q.plant+F + R6, BIC). The codon-aware and protein-only reconstructions both recovered a topology identical to that obtained from the direct CDS alignment (Supplementary Fig. 36), with maximal support (UFBoot = 100, SH-aLRT = 100) at all internal nodes.

Whole-genome triploidization and divergence-event analysis

Syntenic blocks were identified from homologous gene pairs, and synonymous substitution rates (Ks) between collinear genes were estimated using the WGDI pipeline (v0.4.5)64. Collinear blocks corresponding to polyploidy and divergence events were classified and distinguished by median Ks values between homologous genes. Gaussian mixture modelling of Ks distributions was performed using WGDI (v0.4.5), and the Ks values used for plotting were estimated with the Nei and Gojobori method by WGDI (v0.4.5). Ks distribution plots were generated using custom Python scripts available at GitHub (https://github.com/supermanwasd/Salicornia-genome).

Gene-family identification and phylogenetic analysis

Gene-family members were identified using a combined homology-based strategy integrating sequence-similarity searches and profile Hidden Markov models (HMM) (v3.1b2). Protein sequences from each species were queried against curated reference sets for A. thaliana and Oryza sativa using BLASTP (v2.14.1), and conserved protein domains characteristic of each family were detected using HMMER with Pfam profiles.

Only genes supported by both BLASTP similarity and HMMER domain evidence were retained as confident family members. The identified protein sequences from all species were extracted and merged into a single dataset, with species-specific prefixes added to sequence identifiers.

Multiple-sequence alignment of the gene-family proteins was performed using MUSCLE (v5.3). Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using a maximum-likelihood approach implemented in IQ-TREE (v3.0.1), and branch support was evaluated using both ultrafast bootstrap and SH-aLRT tests. The resulting phylogeny was used to assess gene family expansion, contraction, and evolutionary relationships across species.

Gene-family expansion and contraction analysis

Gene-family expansion and contraction analyses were performed based on orthogroup gene counts and a time-calibrated species phylogeny. Orthogroups and their corresponding gene copy numbers across species were inferred using OrthoFinder (v2.4.0). A species tree derived from divergence-time estimation was used as the input phylogeny after removal of annotation metadata and normalisation of branch lengths.

Gene-family evolutionary dynamics were analysed using CAFE5 (v1.1)65, which models gene gain and loss across a phylogenetic tree under a stochastic birth–death process. The orthogroup gene-count matrix and the processed species tree were used as inputs to infer expanded and contracted gene families along each lineage. Ancestral gene copy numbers were reconstructed, and lineage-specific expansion and contraction events were identified based on the inferred evolutionary model.

Differential gene-expression analysis

RNA-seq reads were quality-trimmed with Trimmomatic (v0.39)66, aligned to the corresponding reference genome with HISAT2 (v2.2.1)53 and counted per gene with featureCounts (Subread v2.0.4)67. Differential expression was tested with DESeq2 (v1.42.0)68 independently within each species against its own controls, with multiple-testing correction by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Genes with total raw counts < 10 across all samples were filtered out. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2 fold-change| > 0.585 (≥ 1.5-fold).

Structural-variant and gene-ontology enrichment analysis

Structural variants (SVs) were identified from pairwise whole-genome comparisons among Salicornia species and subgenomes using SyRI (v1.5.5)69. Only SVs with a length ≥ 50 bp were retained, and variants were classified as deletions, inversions (including inverted translocations and inverted duplications), translocations and duplications.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed to investigate the functional characteristics of the target gene set. GO annotations were obtained from eggNOG-mapper(v 2.1.12)59, and only genes with assigned GO terms were retained for downstream analysis. GO term enrichment was conducted using the clusterProfiler (v 4.10.1)70 package in R, based on a custom gene-to-GO mapping derived from the eggNOG annotations (Supplementary Note 14).

Ancestor karyotype construction and MapMan enrichment

For ancestor karyotype construction, see Supplementary Note 15. Predicted protein sequences from each genome assembly were functionally annotated using the Mercator4 web server and assigned to MapMan4 (Mercator4) functional BINs. Enrichment analysis was performed using Mercator4 with false discovery rate correction. A statistical-significance threshold of FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 was applied to report enriched BINs.

Sequencing, k-mer and phylogeny, genome coverage, SNP identification, and population structure

Whole-genome resequencing libraries were generated from young shoot tissue using PCR-free Illumina TruSeq chemistry and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE150), targeting 6–12× coverage. Reads were quality filtered using fastp (further details provided in Supplementary Note 16). Populationgenetic structure was assessed using complementary k-mer-based and SNP-based approaches.

A presence/absence k-mer matrix was generated for all accessions using the KGWASMatrix workflow71, enabling genetic clustering, principal-component analysis (PCA), and phylogenetic inference (Supplementary note 16). Accessions showing discordance between genetic clustering and passport information were re-evaluated and taxonomically curated. An NJ phylogenetic tree and PCA were constructed following established k-mer-based methods. The NJ phylogenetic tree was generated using a k-mer-based matrix of presence/absence (1/0) variation, employing the clustering approach described in refs. 72,73 (Supplementary Note 17).

To assign ploidy and subgenomic composition, reads were mapped to an in silico octoploid reference comprising the AC and BD subgenomes of tetraploid Salicornia assemblies. Only uniquely mapped concordant reads were retained to control for cross-subgenome and homoeologous mapping to non-target subgenomes, and genome-wide normalised read depths were then plotted74. Normalised read-depth profiles were used to infer chromosome copy number and subgenome identity (further descriptions and analyses provided in Supplementary Note 17).

For SNP-based analyses, diploid accessions were mapped to the S. europaea (Israel) reference genome, variants were called using BCFtools54, and population structure was inferred using the LEA75 package in R (details provided in Supplementary Note 18).

SNP calling was also performed for other groups of species as needed (Supplementary Table 14). For instance, to validate the assemblies, we called SNPs in S. sinus-persica and S. europaea (Israel), genotyped all diploids accessions, and computed assembly error rates on S. sinus-persica and S. europaea (Israel), respectively. Then, the filtered SNPs identified with S. europaea (Israel) were also used to dissect the population structure and estimate other population parameters such as pairwise FST, Nei’s index and D-statistics. Similarly, to study potential ancestral relations between tetraploid A-subgenome Salicornia sp. France and A-subgenome diploids, variants were also called in tetraploid Salicornia sp. France A-subgenome chromosomes.

Sequence reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT253, retaining only uniquely mapped concordant pairs via SAMtools54 filtration to minimise multi-mapping biases. Variant calling was performed using Bcftools54. Because analyses were restricted to uniquely mapped reads against the corresponding subgenome reference, genotypes of allotetraploids were also treated as diploids during subgenome-specific variant calling.

Raw variants were filtered in a multi-step pipeline. Initial filtration using bcftools retained variants satisfying QUAL ≥ 30, INFO/DP ≥ 20, and 0.01 < AF < 0.99. Per-sample sequencing coverage was evaluated during quality control and averaged approximately 6–12× across the dataset; therefore, no additional per-sample depth filtering was applied. An in-house script was subsequently applied to filter for minor allele frequency (MAF > 0.02), missing data (<20%), and heterozygosity (<20%), unless otherwise specified for a particular downstream analysis. Only high-confidence, biallelic SNPs passing all criteria were retained for downstream analyses.

Assessment of genetic diversity and population differentiation

Genetic diversity within diploid Salicornia populations was assessed using multiple complementary metrics, including Nei’s diversity index76, nucleotide diversity (π), and the number of segregating sites. Analyses focused on three well-sampled diploid groups: S. sinus-persica, S. europaea (Israel), and S. persica ssp. iranica. Variant subsets were extracted from filtered VCF files using VCFtools (v0.1.17), and diversity statistics were calculated on a genome-wide basis (details provided in Supplementary Note 19).

Population differentiation among diploid groups was quantified using pairwise FST estimates calculated between all population pairs. Both window-based and genome-wide mean FST values were derived to assess patterns of genetic divergence. Detailed filtering criteria, window sizes, and implementation parameters are provided in the Supplementary Note 19.

A-genome diploid and AC tetraploid phylogenomics

To assess the genomic relationship between A-subgenome diploids and AC tetraploids, we mapped accessions from both diploid and AC tetraploids exclusively onto the A subgenome chromosomes of the tetraploids using HISAT2 alignment53 and compared overall and unique-concordant mapping statistics. We then called variants using BCFtools as described above and constructed an NJ phylogenetic tree to identify the probable A-subgenome donor to tetraploid Salicornia sp. France.

Tajima’s D and nucleotide diversity (π)

Tajima’s D statistics77 were calculated and compared between two tetraploid taxa, Salicornia sp. France and S. procumbens. These taxa cluster within the same phylogenetic clade but originate from contrasting coastal environments: the Étang de Thau (France) and the Wadden/North Sea (Netherlands), respectively (Supplementary Data 1). Tajima’s D was computed using non-overlapping 50-kb windows with vcftools78, allowing genome-wide comparison of allele-frequency patterns between the two environments. We also conducted a genome-wide nucleotide-diversity (π) assessment between these groups and plotted both statistics using ggplot2 in R (R Core Team, 2024). Tajima’s π and haplotype blocks within a candidate selection region on chromosome 6A were analysed in two tetraploid species (Salicornia sp. France and S. procumbens) using variants called against the Salicornia sp. France reference genome. To mitigate reference bias or structural assembly collapses, the region was independently reanalysed using A-subgenome SNPs called against an external S. ramosissima assembly14. Comparing both datasets allowed us to determine whether the observed signature was driven by structural variation or true differential selection between the two environmental regimes.

Introgressions in Middle Eastern Salicornia

Introgression among Salicornia lineages sourced from the Middle East was assessed using Patterson’s D statistic and the f4-ratio implemented in Dsuite79, with S. sinus-persica used as the outgroup for tests involving S. europaea from Israel, Egypt and S. persica ssp. iranica. Similarly, S. persica ssp. iranica was used as the outgroup in reciprocal tests among S. europaea and S. sinus-persica.
