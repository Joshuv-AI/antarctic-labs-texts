Botensilimab (Fc-enhanced anti–CTLA-4 antibody) plus balstilimab (anti–PD-1 antibody) in patients with treatment-refractory ovarian cancer
Authors: Rebecca Porter, Bruno Bockorny, Bradley R Corr, Daruka Mahadevan, Breelyn A Wilky, Anthony B El-Khoueiry, Andrea J Bullock, Navid Hafez, Justin Moser, Anastasia Martynova, Justin Stebbing, Bishnu Joshi, Sean Bourdelais, Chloe Delepine, Dhan Chand, Wei Wu, Benny Johnson, Joseph E Grossman, Steven J O’Day, Ursula A Matulonis, Michael S Gordon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with platinum-resistant/refractory ovarian cancer (PROC) experience suboptimal outcomes, highlighting an immediate need for novel therapies. This phase 1b study investigated the safety and efficacy of botensilimab (BOT), a fragment crystallizable (Fc)-enhanced anti–cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) antibody with differentiated mechanisms of action from first-generation CTLA-4 inhibitors, plus balstilimab (BAL; anti–programmed cell death protein 1 antibody), in an expanded cohort of patients with treatment-refractory ovarian cancer.MethodsBOT was administered intravenously at 1 mg/kg or 2 mg/kg every 6 weeks in combination with BAL intravenously at 3 mg/kg every 2 weeks (up to 2 years). The primary objectives were to assess safety and tolerability. Efficacy end points included objective response rate (ORR), duration of response (DOR), and progression-free survival (PFS) by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V.1.1. Overall survival (OS) was an exploratory end point.ResultsOverall, 44 patients were evaluable for safety (with a median of 3 prior lines of therapy; median follow-up 9.6 months (range, 0.6—36.6)), and 35 for efficacy. The most common treatment-related adverse event was diarrhea/colitis (43%; 16% grade 3) with no treatment-related deaths. RECIST-confirmed ORR was 23% (8/35; 95% CI 10% to 40%; one complete (CR), seven partial responses (PRs)) and clinical benefit rate (CR, PR, or stable disease ≥24 weeks) was 31% (11/35; 95% CI 17% to 49%). Median DOR was 9.7 months (95% CI 2.8 to not reached (NR)), median PFS was 2.8 months (95% CI 1.4 to 5.5), median OS was 14.8 months (95% CI 12.1 to NR), and 12-month OS was 75% (95% CI 55% to 86%). Immune phenotypic analyses and biomarker data revealed significantly higher FcγRIIIA+CD11c+ cells and higher programmed death-ligand 1 expression in responding patients, a strong association between T-cell infiltrated tumors and clinical benefit, and differences in immune architecture across histologic subtypes.ConclusionThe BOT/BAL combination demonstrated deep, durable responses and complete remissions in patients with treatment-refractory ovarian cancer where no standard treatments are currently available. RECIST under-represented clinical benefit with 11 patients achieving prolonged/clinically meaningful stable disease (or better) for ≥24 weeks. Toxicities were manageable and reversible. The encouraging clinical activity of BOT/BAL in heavily pretreated patients, as well as biomarker associations, warrants further investigation of this combination.Keywords: Ovarian Cancer, Biomarker, Combination therapy, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Immunotherapy

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

In recurrent ovarian cancer, prior immunotherapy trials using cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4)/programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitors have shown limited efficacy with no approvals, and treatment options remain limited for patients with platinum-resistant/refractory ovarian cancer (PROC).

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

The combination of a fragment crystallizable (Fc)-enhanced CTLA-4 antibody (botensilimab) with PD-1 blockade (balstilimab) produced notable and durable responses in heavily pretreated patients with treatment-refractory ovarian cancer (mostly PROC), with enhanced activity compared to historical immunotherapy data. Biomarker analyses showed that T-cell infiltrated phenotypes were associated with clinical benefit. Immune-mediated treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs; most commonly, diarrhea/colitis) were manageable and reversible with steroids and/or tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) inhibitors.

HOW MIGHT THIS STUDY AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These results support further randomized studies of botensilimab/balstilimab for treatment-refractory ovarian cancer, potentially filling an unmet clinical need; the identified immune biomarkers may inform future patient selection strategies; and successful management of immune-mediated TRAEs with TNF-α inhibitors could improve the safety of CTLA-4 inhibitor combinations across oncology practice.

Ovarian cancer results in nearly 13,000 annual deaths in the USA and 200,000 globally.1 2 For patients with recurrent or persistent ovarian cancer, single-agent therapies blocking programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or its ligand, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) have demonstrated modest objective response rates (ORRs) in larger clinical studies (8–10%) with median progression-free survival (PFS) near 2 months,3 4 improving to 3.9 months when combined with the cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) inhibitor, ipilimumab.5 Other approaches have combined immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) with chemotherapy or small molecules including poly-ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors and/or anti-angiogenic agents.36,11 Results thus far have been disappointing, although certain rare subgroups may benefit.11 Several prognostic factors have been examined, such as the presence of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells within the epithelium of ovarian cancers, which portend a more favorable prognosis.12,14

Botensilimab (BOT) is a multifunctional anti–CTLA-4 antibody engineered with an enhanced fragment crystallizable (Fc) IgG1 region that promotes superior binding to activating Fc gamma receptors (FcγRs) and recruitment of novel FcγR-dependent mechanisms, unlike conventional anti–CTLA-4 antibodies (eg, ipilimumab or tremelimumab).15 This enhanced interaction improves the engagement of T cells with antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and natural killer cells, resulting in optimal T-cell priming, activation, memory formation and myeloid activation.15 16 Additionally, BOT promotes depletion of regulatory T cells via antibody-dependent-cellular cytotoxicity and phagocytosis mechanisms.15 Balstilimab (BAL) is a fully human IgG4 monoclonal antibody that binds with high affinity to PD-1, completely blocking PD-1–PD-L1/L2 interactions and demonstrating pharmacological activity comparable to approved PD-1 inhibitors.17 18 The combination of BOT plus BAL leverages distinct and complementary mechanisms of action. CTLA-4 blockade enhances T-cell priming and activation during the initial immune response by strengthening the T-cell–APC immune synapse through FcγR engagement, relieving inhibitory signals and modulating regulatory T-cell function, while PD-1 blockade reinvigorates tumor-infiltrating T cells and restores their effector function.19 These complementary effects with CTLA-4 plus PD-1 blockade can result in robust antitumor immunity and represent an approach that may overcome the immunosuppressive barriers that are characteristic of ovarian cancer.19 20

Previously reported results from a phase 1b, open-label study of BOT alone or in combination with BAL have demonstrated promising clinical activity across various immune “cold” and treatment-refractory cancers, including those that are unresponsive to first-generation PD-1/CTLA-4 inhibitors, such as microsatellite-stable metastatic colorectal cancer (MSS mCRC)21 and sarcomas,20 among others.15 22

Here, we report the results of BOT/BAL in a cohort of patients with treatment-refractory, largely platinum-resistant/refractory ovarian cancer (PROC).

Methods

Study design and participants

The C-800–01 study is an open-label, phase 1b, multicenter study evaluating the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of BOT±BAL (NCT03860272). Patients with treatment-refractory ovarian cancer were enrolled and treated between April 1, 2019, and November 8, 2023, at nine sites across the USA. The full study design, dose escalation, and results from the MSS mCRC and sarcoma cohorts have been previously reported.21 23

Eligible patients had a confirmed diagnosis of recurrent ovarian cancer for which no standard therapy was available or where standard therapies previously failed. Patients were aged ≥18 years with measurable disease per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors V.1.1 (RECIST V.1.1) and had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0 or 1 with adequate end-organ function. Prior ICI was permitted. Full eligibility criteria are in the published protocol (online supplemental file 3).

Procedures

During the dose escalation period, BOT was given once every 3 (Q3W) or 6 weeks (Q6W) at dose levels ranging from 0.1 to 3 mg/kg (administered intravenously), while BAL was given intravenously once every 2 weeks (Q2W) at 3 mg/kg, both for up to 2 years. In the dose expansion period, BOT was given at 1 mg/kg or 2 mg/kg Q6W in combination with BAL 3 mg/kg Q2W. Patients were permitted to crossover from BOT monotherapy to the combination and to continue treatment beyond progression when clinically appropriate. The C-800–01 study is now closed to enrollment.

End points

The primary end point was to assess safety and toxicity (occurrence of dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) in the dose escalation period) and has been reported previously; a maximum tolerated dose was not reached (NR).21 BOT doses up to 2 mg/kg were considered safe.21 Secondary end points included severity and duration of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and laboratory abnormalities (according to the National Cancer Institute’s Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) V.5.0), ORR (per cent patients with complete or partial response (CR or PR)), duration of response (DOR; minimum time from first observation of CR or PR to the time of progressive disease (PD) or death; whichever occurred first), disease control rate (DCR; per cent patients with CR, PR, or stable disease (SD) for ≥6 weeks) and PFS (the interval from the date of first dose until the earliest date of PD or death; whichever occurred first). All responses were evaluated per RECIST V.1.1 based on investigator assessment. Imaging was performed Q6W (±3 days) from the first treatment dose. Exploratory end points included overall survival (OS; interval from date of first dose until death due to any cause) and clinical benefit rate (CBR; per cent patients with best response of CR, PR, or SD for ≥24 weeks). Post hoc subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate efficacy in patients with platinum-refractory/resistant (platinum-free interval (PFI) ≤6 months), which also included patients with primary platinum-refractory disease (disease progression on first exposure to platinum therapy or ≤1 month following completion of treatment), and patients with platinum-sensitive disease (PFI >6 months). PFI was defined as the time from the last dose of the latest line of platinum therapy to the date of disease progression or relapse.

Statistical analysis

The data cut-off date was December 5, 2024. The safety analysis set (SAS) included all patients who received at least one dose of study drug (n=44). The efficacy evaluable set included all treated patients who had at least one post-baseline 6-week tumor imaging scan (n=35). Trial results (means, medians, ranges and measures of variability) were summarized with descriptive statistics and qualitative variables with counts and percentages.

Confirmation of response was required for a best response of a CR or PR. For patients with a confirmed CR or PR, DOR was summarized using Kaplan-Meier methods, as was PFS and OS for all patients. Median time to event(s) and 95% CIs for medians were provided as required. Safety end points were tabulated by dose level, using descriptive statistics, the severity of adverse events graded using NCI-CTCAE whenever possible.

Correlative studies

Multiplex immunofluorescence and image analysis

The multiplex immunofluorescence assay was performed on pretreatment formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue biopsies collected using a Leica Bond automated stainer (Leica Biosystems) with multiple immune and tumor markers (online supplemental table 1A-E) using Opal multiplex immunofluorescence (Akoya Biosciences), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The optimal conditions and antibody staining order were determined using chromogenic single-plex (3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB)) and fluorescence monoplex staining methods. Each primary antibody was paired with a specific Opal fluorophore, as shown in online supplemental table 1A-E. After staining with all markers, slides were incubated with spectral 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and mounted with coverslips using ProLong Gold mounting media (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA). Images were acquired on the PhenoImager Fusion system and processed with Phenochart and INFORM software for spectral unmixing. Image analysis was performed using HALO software (Indica Labs), including cell segmentation and phenotyping. T-cell infiltration classification of the tumor microenvironment (TME) was assessed by analyzing CD3+CD8+ T-cell distribution in whole-slide images scanned at 20× magnification using the HALO image analysis platform (Indica Labs). The infiltration analysis module was used to quantify T-cell densities (cells/mm²) within tumor and stromal compartments. Tumors were classified into T-cell infiltrated, excluded, or desert phenotypes based on the spatial distribution of T cells following criteria established in prior studies across multiple cancer types: infiltrated (abundant and homogenous distribution of T cells in both tumor and stroma), excluded (high stromal but absent intratumoral T cells), and desert (low to absent T cells in both compartments).

Tumor mutational burden

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was determined through local clinical testing performed at designated reference laboratories, as per standard practice at each site. All TMB assessments and analyses were conducted using tumor-based validated next-generation sequencing panels, with results reported as mutations per megabase (mut/Mb).

Cytokine analysis

Blood samples for cytokine analysis were obtained from consenting patients under Institutional Review Board-approved protocols. Pretreatment plasma was prepared from blood collected in NaHep tubes and processed at clinical sites immediately. Plasma aliquots were stored at −80°C. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels in plasma were measured using the Meso Scale Discovery V-PLEX Plus Proinflammatory Panel 1 electrochemiluminescence assay (K15049G) performed by Precision for Medicine (Frederick, Maryland, USA).

Tumor PD-L1 immunohistochemistry

PD-L1 protein expression was evaluated in pretreatment biopsy specimens using PD-L1 immunohistochemistry 28–8 pharmDx qualitative immunohistochemical assay (Dako/Agilent, Cat# SK005) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed prior to PD-L1 detection, and tumor presence was examined by a certified pathologist. Tissue samples with <100 tumor cells per slide were considered non-evaluable (NE). PD-L1 expression in all evaluable samples was assessed by combined positive score (CPS). Tumor non-relevant areas (normal and necrotic tissues), tissue and staining artifacts were excluded from the analysis. Samples stained more than 6 months after sectioning were excluded.

Results

Patients

As of the data cut-off, 44 patients with treatment-refractory ovarian cancer were treated and evaluable for safety: 7 during the dose-escalation period and 37 during the dose-expansion period. Of the 44 patients, 35 had at least one post-baseline 6-week imaging scan and were efficacy-evaluable (figure 1). Nine patients who were treated were not evaluable for efficacy due to clinical progression, consent withdrawal, death, or due to adverse events ahead of their first post-baseline scan.

CONSORT diagram of the C-800–01 study and all treated patients with treatment-refractory ovarian cancer (n=44). BAL, balstilimab; BOT, botensilimab; CONSORT, Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials; SAS, safety analysis population.

The SAS (n=44) was consistent with general demographics of patients with treatment-refractory ovarian cancer (table 1): median age was 66 years (range, 37–79) with ECOG performance status of 0 (50%) or 1 (50%). Patients were heavily pretreated and received a median of 3 prior lines of therapy (range 1–14), including a platinum doublet (100%), bevacizumab (77%), PARP inhibitors (59%), PD-(L)1 (14%), and prior mirvetuximab soravtansine-gynx (5%). No patients received prior CTLA-4. Twenty-seven per cent of patients were platinum sensitive, 55% were platinum resistant, and 18% had primary platinum-refractory disease; 73% of patients were in the platinum refractory/resistant subgroup. The most common histologic subtype was high-grade serous ovarian cancer (82%) followed by clear cell (11%), carcinosarcoma (5%) and endometrioid adenocarcinoma (2%). Twenty-one patients received BOT at 1 mg/kg plus BAL, 22 received 2 mg/kg BOT plus BAL, and 1 patient crossed over from 0.1 mg BOT monotherapy Q3W to 1 mg/kg BOT/BAL. In patients who were tested, 35% (8/23) had BRCA1/2 mutations and nearly all were MSS (n=41; no data was available for three patients). Few patients had TMB tested locally (n=13), and only one patient (1/13, 8%) had a high TMB (>10 mutations mut/Mb). Most patients with PD-L1 testing performed (n=25) were PD-L1 positive (CPS≥1%; 21/25, 84%).

Safety

In the SAS (n=44), two patients had events qualifying as DLTs that occurred during the dose escalation phase. One patient who initially received BOT monotherapy had a grade 2 maculopapular rash causing dose delay (qualifying as a DLT) but was able to resume therapy with prolonged SD and eventually crossed over to BOT/BAL combination therapy where PR was observed. The second patient initially received combination therapy and had a DLT of grade 4 thrombocytopenia, which resolved with treatment but precluded re-challenge with BOT.

All patients in the SAS (n=44) experienced at least one TEAE (68% grade ≥3; table 2; online supplemental table 2) and 89% experienced at least one treatment-related adverse event (TRAE; 41% grade ≥3) with the most common TRAEs being diarrhea/colitis (43%; 16% grade 3), fatigue (36%; 7% grade 3), and nausea (36%; 2% grade 3). Grade 4 TRAEs occurred in one patient each: autonomic neuropathy, acute kidney injury, and thrombocytopenia, while no treatment-related deaths were reported. Pneumonitis (2% grade 2) and immune-mediated myocarditis (2% grade 3) were infrequent, while hypophysitis was not reported in this cohort. Nineteen patients (43%) discontinued BOT and/or BAL due to a TRAE; the most common TRAEs leading to discontinuation were immune-mediated enterocolitis (n=6; 14%) and colitis (n=5; 11%).

Overall, 73% of patients experienced potential immune-mediated TRAEs of any grade (online supplemental table 3; defined as related to treatment with the patient receiving steroids and/or immunosuppressants or endocrine replacement for any reason during the event; analyzed using grouped terms defined in the table footnote). The most common immune-mediated TRAE was diarrhea/colitis, occurring in 43% of patients (23% grade 3), followed by skin events (27%; 5% grade 3), nausea (20%; 2% grade 3), and hepatitis (11%; 2% grade 3). Only one grade 4 event (immune-mediated thrombocytopenia) was observed and no grade 5 immune-mediated TRAEs were reported.

Tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) inhibitors (such as infliximab) were permitted for steroid-sensitive (ie, responded to steroids) patients to resolve diarrhea/colitis, shorten the duration of steroids, and to allow for retreatment.24 Patients who received TNF-α inhibitors were permitted to continue study drug if toxicity was resolved to grade <2. If a patient had a grade 3 immune-mediated TRAE related to BOT but not related to BAL, they could continue treatment with BAL. Additionally, to continue treatment, only prednisone doses ≤10 mg/day orally were permitted. Other immune-mediated TRAEs were managed according to guidelines at the investigator’s discretion.24 25 Among the 20 patients with immune-mediated diarrhea/colitis who received steroids and/or TNF-α inhibitors, 14 received both agents, 5 received steroids alone, and 1 received TNF-α inhibition alone. Most patients (65%) experienced immune-mediated diarrhea/colitis more than once, with none still receiving steroids and/or immunosuppressants. All cases of immune-mediated diarrhea/colitis resolved.

Efficacy

Only RECIST-confirmed investigator-assessed responses are reported in the efficacy evaluable set (n=35; table 3, figure 2A waterfall plot). Overall, eight patients achieved a response (ORR 23%; 95% CI 10% to 40%) including one CR and seven PRs. Responders included five patients with a high-grade serous histology, one with clear cell carcinoma, one with mixed histology (clear cell with endometrioid adenocarcinoma), and one with endometrioid adenocarcinoma. DCR was 60% (95% CI 42% to 76%), CBR was 31% (95% CI 17% to 49%), and median DOR was 9.7 months (95% CI 2.8 to NR; figure 2B spider plot). Responses ranged from 1.4+ to 17.5+ months, with one responder remaining in DOR follow-up as of the data cut-off. Median PFS was 2.8 months (95% CI 1.4 to 5.5) (figure 3A), median OS was 14.8 months (95% CI 12.1 to NR), and 12-month OS rate was 75% (95% CI 55% to 86%; figure 3B). Median follow-up was 9.6 months (range 0.6–36.6); calculated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method, median follow-up was 14.6 months (95% CI 9.2 to 21.2).

Benefits of therapy were not confined to RECIST responses. Eleven patients had SD (or better; per CBR) of at least 24 weeks including 1 patient out to 36 weeks and another out to 60 weeks. Additionally, two responders (one CR and one PR) had isolated/limited PD and were treated beyond progression. As of the data cut-off, treatment-free intervals were emerging; one responder had not received any new systemic therapy (treated for ~3 months beyond PD) and the other had not received any since 2023 (treated for ~13 months beyond PD), respectively.

Post hoc analyses among efficacy-evaluable patients with platinum-refractory/resistant (n=25) or platinum-sensitive (n=10) ovarian cancer demonstrated similar responses between these subgroups (online supplemental figure 1). Among the four efficacy-evaluable patients with primary platinum-refractory disease (those who had disease progression during first exposure to platinum therapy or ≤1 month following completion of treatment), there were three responders (two PRs and one CR; one SD).

Correlative studies

Histological and immune phenotyping revealed distinct patterns of T-cell infiltration across ovarian cancer histological subtypes (figure 4A,B) that are classified into three phenotypes: T-cell infiltrated, T-cell excluded, and T-cell desert as previously described.26 Frequencies of CD3+CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and CD3+CD4+ helper T cells appeared elevated in patients with non-high-grade serous (HGS) tumors compared with those with HGS histology (figure 4C,D), although this difference did not reach statistical significance. However, a significant difference in the immune infiltration pattern distribution was noted between HGS and non-HGS tumors, with non-HGS tumors showing a higher frequency of T-cell infiltration and lower frequencies of T-cell exclusion and desert phenotypes compared with HGS tumors (figure 4E; p=0.0004). The presence of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS), identified by the co-localization of CD20+, CD3+, HLA-DR+, and CD11c+ cells (figure 4F), were significantly more prevalent in tumors with non-HGS histology (69%) compared with HGS tumors (figure 4G, p<0.0001). These structures, indicative of organized local immune responses, were predominately observed in tumors with the T-cell infiltrated phenotype.

Analysis of immune phenotypes revealed significant associations with clinical responses to therapy (online supplemental figure 2). Patients experiencing CR/PR/SDs predominantly had tumors classified as T-cell infiltrated compared with patients with PD, who primarily exhibited T-cell excluded and desert phenotypes (online supplemental figure 2A; p<0.0001). Although survival differences among infiltration groups did not reach statistical significance, T-cell infiltrated patients showed a trend toward improved outcomes (online supplemental figure 2B; HR=0.31, p=0.12). TLS presence significantly correlated with improved clinical outcomes (online supplemental figure 2C; p<0.0001), although survival benefit was not statistically significant (online supplemental figure 2D; HR=0.56, p=0.25). Further analysis of immune cell populations demonstrated significantly higher frequencies of CD56+ pan-CK– cells (online supplemental figure 2E; p=0.04), CD11c+ cells (online supplemental figure 2F; p=0.002), CD86+ cells (online supplemental figure 2G; p=0.03), and FcγRIIIA+CD11c+ cells (online supplemental figure 2H; p=0.0212) in patients with CR/PR/SDs compared with PD. Despite these differences, survival analysis stratified by FcγRIIIA+CD11c+ cell percentage did not demonstrate statistically significant prognostic implications (online supplemental figure 2I; HR=0.65, p=0.55).

PD-L1 expression was evaluated using the 28–8 (research use only (RUO)) assay. The overall cohort had a median CPS of 6 (range, 0–65). Stratification by histology revealed significantly higher PD-L1 expression in non-HGS patients (median CPS, 35; range, 10–65) compared with HGS patients (median CPS, 3; range, 0–30; Mann-Whitney test, p=0.0015) (online supplemental tables 4 and 5). PD-L1 expression was significantly higher in CR/PR/SD patients compared with PD patients (online supplemental figure 2J; p=0.02), though survival did not differ significantly based on CPS score stratification (online supplemental figure 2K; p=0.43). PD-L1 CPS positively correlated with densities of CD3+CD8+ (online supplemental figure 2L; R=0.60, p=0.0006), CD3+CD4+ (online supplemental figure 2M; R=0.62, p=0.0004), CD11c+ (online supplemental figure 2N; R=0.48, p=0.0079), and CD20+ cells (online supplemental figure 2O; R=0.69, p<0.0001). These findings suggest that PD-L1 expression is closely associated with immune cell infiltration in the TME.

Analysis of systemic inflammatory markers revealed that plasma IL-6 levels tended to be lower in patients with CR/PR/SDs compared with those with PD, although this difference was not statistically significant (p=0.32; online supplemental figure 2P; online supplemental tables 4 and 5) and not associated with survival outcomes (online supplemental figure 2Q; HR=0.44, p=0.17).

TMB was assessed in 13 patients, with a median of 4 mut/Mb (range, 1–11 mut/Mb). Only one patient (8%) had a TMB >10 mut/Mb (TMB=11 mut/Mb), which was observed in a tumor of clear cell/endometrioid adenocarcinoma histology with SD (online supplemental tables 4 and 5).

At baseline, cancer antigen 125 (CA-125) level was reported for 39 patients. The overall CA-125 median was 233 U/mL (range, 7–6,464), with 29/39 (74%) of the patients showing elevated (>35 U/mL) CA-125. Patients with HGS histology had higher levels than non-HGS patients (median of 324 U/mL and 80 U/mL respectively; online supplemental tables 4 and 5). On treatment, patients with CR/PRs and three patients with SD showed reductions in CA-125 levels, whereas the remainder of patients with SD and PD showed elevated levels (online supplemental figure 2R).

Discussion

In this phase 1b C-800–01 trial expansion cohort, BOT/BAL demonstrated promising efficacy in heavily pretreated patients with treatment-refractory ovarian cancer. All patients received a prior platinum doublet and most were platinum-resistant/refractory, with a majority receiving prior bevacizumab. High-grade serous histology was the most common, followed by a smaller proportion of clear cell tumors. Despite these unfavorable characteristics, the BOT/BAL combination yielded a confirmed ORR of 23%, prolonged SD (or better) of 31%, and continued benefit beyond PD in some patients, while maintaining a manageable safety profile.

While previous PD-1/CTLA-4 trials in recurrent ovarian cancer have been conducted, no immunotherapies have been approved. An early phase 1 study of ipilimumab and nivolumab with a similar heavily pretreated, platinum-resistant population to this study reported an ORR of ~10%.27 Another phase 2 trial of ipilimumab and nivolumab in a potentially more favorable population with more platinum-sensitive patients, fewer prior lines of therapy and more patients with the clear cell histology demonstrated a 31% ORR.5 In that trial, clear cell histology was associated with a 5.2-fold greater likelihood of response and has previously been associated with higher TMB.5 28 29 Additionally, durvalumab plus tremelimumab showed an ORR of ~3% in unselected platinum-resistant ovarian cancers.30 Thus, compared with historical data, the BOT/BAL combination may confer enhanced activity even in “immune-cold” ovarian cancers such as relapsed/refractory high-grade serous, which historically has been associated with limited ICI response.

Post hoc analyses among patients with platinum-refractory/resistant or platinum-sensitive disease demonstrated responses across both subgroups. Of note, this C-800–01 ovarian cancer expansion cohort included four efficacy-evaluable patients with primary platinum-refractory disease (comprising one CR, two PRs, and one SD), a population with historically poor prognosis that is often excluded from later phase trials in ovarian cancer.31,34 Given the small sample size and non-randomized design of this phase 1b trial, these observations should be interpreted with caution; however, these findings suggest that BOT/BAL may provide durable clinical benefit even among heavily pretreated and platinum-refractory patients, including those with primary platinum resistance.

Mirvetuximab soravtansine-gynx, an antibody-drug conjugate targeting folate receptor-alpha (FRα), was recently approved for PROC based on a 32% ORR (n=104).35 A randomized study found that the ORR with mirvetuximab was associated with improvements in both PFS and OS.34 In contrast, immunotherapy trials involving CTLA-4 inhibitors often demonstrate modest ORRs with greater effects on OS than PFS. In such trials where only a minority of patients benefit, PFS commonly underestimates clinical benefit.36 Despite mirvetuximab’s recent approval, options remain limited for those who progress or whose tumors lack adequate FRα expression, as evidenced by several patients who previously received mirvetuximab who enrolled on this trial, and BOT/BAL may fill this unmet need for some patients.

Across immuno-oncology, there has been a paradigm shift away from high-dose steroids with prolonged tapers, which may negatively impact tumor control, toward early intervention with TNF-α blockade in steroid-sensitive settings to minimize time without treatment (allowing for rapid tapers).24 37 38 This management strategy has been employed across this trial and resulted in a manageable and reversible safety profile for BOT/BAL. In patients with treatment-refractory ovarian cancer, specifically, there were no new safety signals for BOT/BAL and the most common TRAE was diarrhea/colitis (43%; 16% grade 3). Previous data has shown that the presence of immune-mediated adverse events (including high-grade events), such as diarrhea/colitis, correlates positively with response, and patients who discontinue therapy early may have durable benefit.39,41 Further use of this management strategy will improve the safety of CTLA-4 inhibitors, novel immunotherapeutic agents, and combination approaches once more broadly adapted.

Biomarker analyses provided valuable insights into the immune contexture associated with response to BOT/BAL therapy. Patients exhibiting clinical benefit (CR/PR/SD) predominantly displayed a T-cell infiltrated phenotype, marked by elevated densities of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, CD4+ helper T cells, and TLSs. Notably, the presence of FcγRIIIA+CD11c+ cells was significantly higher in patients experiencing clinical benefit, consistent with BOT’s Fc-enhanced mechanism of action that promotes novel FcγR-dependent immune activation; however, this did not demonstrate statistically significant prognostic implications.15 While PD-L1 expression was not associated with improved survival outcomes, expression positively correlated with increased immune cell infiltration and was significantly higher in responders compared with non-responders, though its prognostic value requires further validation. Additionally, reductions in CA-125 levels were observed in responders and some patients with SD, supporting its role in monitoring response to treatment. Conversely, baseline plasma IL-6 levels and TMB showed limited prognostic value. Overall, no definitive predictive biomarkers emerged in this trial, consistent with previous reports, highlighting the need for further biomarker refinement to optimize patient selection and enhance the therapeutic efficacy of BOT/BAL in treatment-refractory ovarian cancer.

The current study has several limitations. As is common in phase 1 studies, patient selection may not have been optimal, showcased by early censoring of time-based end points, including nine patients who were not efficacy evaluable. The median PFS of only 2.8 months reflects the inclusion of patients with rapid progression who did not benefit from immunotherapy. Given the response kinetics, it may be critical to select patients for immunotherapy trials without evidence of rapid clinical decline and who can be assessed at multiple time points. In addition, the study population was relatively small and heterogeneous, including a mixture of patients with platinum-sensitive, platinum-resistant, and platinum-refractory disease, which complicates interpretation of the overall efficacy results.

Overall, the BOT/BAL combination demonstrated deep, durable responses and complete remissions in heavily pretreated patients with mostly PROC, with manageable safety. Considering the void of currently available therapies for this patient population, these results warrant further investigation in randomized studies.
