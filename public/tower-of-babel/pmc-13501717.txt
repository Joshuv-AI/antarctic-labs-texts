Factors associated with the engagement in meaningful activity and occupational balance in institutionalised older adults: a prospective observational cohort study
Authors: Rafael Prieto-Moreno, Encarnación Martín-Franco, Marta Mora-Traverso, Rocio Pozuelo-Calvo, Patrocinio Ariza-Vega, Miguel Martín-Matillas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults living in institutional settings often face barriers to maintaining engagement in meaningful activities, which may compromise occupational balance, autonomy and overall well-being. Although a wide range of activity programmes is typically offered, the factors influencing meaningful engagement and occupational balance in these environments remain poorly understood.MethodsAn observational one-year cohort study was conducted across four nursing homes and day centres in Andalusia (Spain). A total of 144 institutionalised older adults were assessed at baseline and at 12-month follow-up. Engagement in meaningful activities was measured using the Engagement in Meaningful Activities Survey (EMAS). Occupational balance was assessed using the Spanish version of the Occupational Balance Questionnaire (OBQ-E). Functional status was assessed with the Functional Independence Measure (FIM) with frailty being assessed through the Fried’s criteria. Participation in daily living, cognitive, leisure, physical and sensory and community activities was recorded. Paired t-tests, simple linear regressions and hierarchical linear regression models were used to examine change over time and predictors of the two main outcomes.ResultsFunctional status, frailty and cognitive engagement appear to be key determinants of meaningful participation and occupational balance in. The predominance of highly structured leisure activities within current institutional programmes may limit opportunities for meaningful engagement. These findings highlight the potential value of co-designed, person-centred activity programmes better aligned with interests, values, and functional abilities of the institutionalised older adults to support more meaningful ageing in institutional settings.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the need to review the type and range of activities offered in institutional settings, reducing time devoted to highly structured leisure activities and promoting more enriching experiences. To do so, experience-based co-design approaches involving institutionalised older adults and professionals in these settings are essential to develop person-centred programmes to enhance engagement in meaningful activities and occupational balance. Future research should include controlled designs and mixed methods to better capture the complex interplay between health status, contextual factors and activity engagement in institutionalised settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07753-8.Keywords: Frailty, Functional status, Institutional settings, Institutionalised older adults, Meaningful activity engagement, Nursing homes, Occupational balance

Background

The aging of the global population represents one of the most significant demographic and social transformations of the 21st century [1]. By 2030, 1 in 6 people will be aged 60 years or over, a proportion projected to double by 2050 [2]. This demographic shift is particularly evident in Europe, which is experiencing a rapid increase in its older population, leading to significant societal and healthcare challenges [3]. In Spain, a significant subset of this demographic, about 4.2% of those aged 65 and above, resides in institutional settings, a figure that increases to 9.6% among individuals over 80 years old [4]. This institutionalised population requires particular attention from healthcare and social services due to their complex needs, which demand substantial resources and careful planning [5]. In fact, according to the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), older adults with functional dependence generate an average annual care cost ranging from €15,000 to €30,000 per person [6]. Although institutionalisation can provide continuous care, it may also disrupt established habits, social roles and daily routines, potentially compromising the quality of life [7].

The transition to institutional living often brings a significant loss of autonomy and a reduction in opportunities for meaningful engagement, which are crucial for maintaining quality of life [7]. Taking part in meaningful activities is known to be essential for healthy aging because it supports physical, mental and emotional well-being [8]. For institutionalised older adults, however, access to and engagement in these activities can be severely limited due to factors such as staffing constraints, lack of personalised programs, physical barriers, or a focus on basic care rather than a holistic well-being [9]. When older adults are deprived of opportunities to engage in activities that align with their interests, values and past roles, they may experience boredom, apathy, depression and a further decline in functional abilities [10, 11]. Therefore, encouraging and helping institutionalised older adults to take part in activities that matter to them is crucial in these places to improve their quality of life, give them a sense of purpose and reduce the negative effects of institutionalisation [12].

Within this context, the concept of occupational balance emerges as a critical, but often forgotten, part of well-being for older adults living in institutional settings [13]. Occupational balance means feeling like your daily activities are varied, meaningful and fit well with what you need, values and capacities [13]. It encompasses a harmonious distribution of activities across different domains, such as self-care, productivity, leisure and rest, preventing an imbalance that could lead to stress, boredom, or a lack of fulfilment [14]. For institutionalised older adults, achieving and maintaining occupational balance can be particularly challenging due to rigid routines, limited choices and environments that may not support individual hobbies and interests [13]. A deficit in occupational balance can impact mental health, physical function and overall life satisfaction, highlighting the importance of interventions aimed at restoring a sense of control and purpose through a varied and personalised occupational repertoire [13, 15]. Despite the importance of these constructs, several research gaps remain. First, most previous studies examining participation or occupational balance have been community-based, usually involving younger older adults with different environmental and occupational contexts compared to institutionalised settings [16, 17]. Consequently, how these factors interact within the specific constraints of nursing homes remains poorly understood. Second, there is a lack of evidence-based research specifically conducted in Spain, particularly in regions like Andalusia, where the institutionalised population presents a distinct profile of advanced age and high functional dependence [4]. Furthermore, while existing literature often focuses on specific diseases, there is limited longitudinal evidence exploring how broader clinical markers [18–20], such as frailty and functional status, act as primary predictors of meaningful engagement and balance over time. This study addresses these gaps by providing novel evidence through a one-year prospective cohort design. Thus, the aim of this study is to analyse the factors influencing engagement in meaningful activities and occupational balance among institutionalised older adults.

Methods

Study design

The present study is an observational cohort study, conducted following the guidelines established by the Helsinki Declaration and Law 14/2007 on Biomedical Research. The Ethics Committee of the Research Centre of Granada (CEI-GRANADA) and the University of Granada approved this study (number: 3928/CEIH/2023). The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. Institutionalised older adults were recruited in four different day centres and nursing homes in Andalucía (southern Spain): Los Tulipanes Day Care Unit, La Zubia Day Care Unit and Nursing Home (Granada), Vitalia Bahía San Fernando Nursing Home (Cádiz) and Geriatric XXI “El Balcón de Cullar Vega” Day Care Unit and Nursing Home (Granada).

Participants

Between January 1st, 2024 and February 28th, 2025, a total of 144 institutionalised older adults from the aforementioned centres who met the inclusion criteria were recruited and followed for one year. The inclusion criteria were: (i) residing in or regularly attending one of the centres included in this study and (ii) understanding and accepting participation in this study by signing the informed consent form. The exclusion criterion was (i) having severe cognitive impairment (Pfeiffer Test score > 8).

Data collection

Data were collected through structured face-to-face questionnaires and performed based tests conducted at two time points: the baseline assessment (between January and February 2024) and the follow-up assessment one year later. Sociodemographic information included age, sex and educational level, among others. Clinical and functional data were obtained using validated assessment tools and were supplemented with information extracted from the medical records of the older adults. In addition, information on the types of activities performed was collected through structured interviews with participants and formal caregivers and complemented by the centres’ activity records. Activity engagement was quantified by recording participation (yes/no), frequency (days per week and days per month) and duration (minutes per session). Activities were classified into five categories: daily living, cognitive, physical and sensory, leisure and community activities following standard occupational therapy frameworks, as presented in Table 1. To ensure a robust analysis, specific programs were aggregated into these broader categories; for instance, the ‘daily living’ category included programs such as feeding, oral hygiene and dressing.

The profile of institutionalised older adults attending nursing homes and day centres in Andalusia is characterised by advanced age, mostly over 80 years and a high prevalence of moderate to severe functional dependence [21]. Most institutionalised older adults require assistance with basic activities of daily living and frequently present multiple chronic conditions, including cardiovascular diseases and cognitive impairment. Women constitute around 70% of this population, consistent with recent national demographic reports. Admissions are frequently influenced by caregiver burden or social isolation [6, 21].

Outcomes

Engagement in meaningful activities

Measured through the Engagement in Meaningful Activities Survey (EMAS), a quick and easy-to-use self-report tool consisting of 12 items in 4 categories to assess the frequency of engagement in meaningful daily activities [22]. The score is obtained by adding up the responses (from 1 = almost never to 4 = always) for the 12 items. The score ranges from 12 to 48 points, with a higher score representing greater engagement in meaningful activities. The scale has demonstrated good internal consistency, validity with a Cronbach’s α of 0.89 [23].

Occupational balance

Measured through the Occupational Balance Questionnaire, Spanish version (OBQ-E), a tool that includes a series of questions to determine their perception of the balance between the activities they carry out in their daily lives and the time they spend on each of them [24]. It has 13 items, each of which is scored from 0 to 5 depending on whether the person agrees or disagrees with the statements. 0 means ‘Completely disagree’ and 5 means ‘Completely agree’. At the end of the questionnaire, the score that can be obtained is from 0 to 65 points. The higher the score, the greater the person’s occupational balance. The reliability of this questionnaire corresponds to a Cronbach’s α of 0.87 [25].

Functional status

Assessed using the Functional Independence Measure (FIM) [26]. A widely used instrument for evaluating the level of independence in activities of daily living. The FIM consists of 18 items, including items related with development of Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) (e.g., self-care or transfers) as well as social and cognitive items (e.g., communication). The total FIM score range is between 18 and 126 points with higher scores indicate a higher level of independence. This allowed for the examination of changes over time, providing insight into the participant’s functional trajectories across the year. The psychometric properties of the FIM scale are adequate [26].

Frailty

It was assessed using Fried’s criteria, which consider frailty as the presence of at least three of the five key indicators: unintentional weight loss, muscle weakness, exhaustion, slow walking speed and low physical activity level [20]. Unintentional weight loss was defined as a decrease of ≥ 4’5 kg or ≥ 5% of body weight over the previous year. Muscle weakness was assessed using handgrip dynamometry, applying sex and body mass index (BMI) specific thresholds. Exhaustion was identified through self-reported feelings of “everything being an effort” during the past week, as per Fried’s original criteria [20]. Slow walking speed was measured by recording the time taken to walk 4.6 m (approximately 15 steps), applying sex- and height-specific thresholds [27]. Finally, low physical activity was evaluated using the Minnesota Leisure Time Physical Activity Questionnaire, estimating weekly energy expenditure in kilocalories [28]. Classifying participants according to the lowest sex-specific quintile, following the original criteria and the Spanish short version validation [29]. The total score ranges from 0 to 5, with participants considered frail if they met three or more criteria, pre-frail if they met one or two and robust if they met none.

Statistical analysis

The normal distribution of the data was checked with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Descriptive characteristics of the sample are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) or frequency and percentage when appropriate. Mean differences between pre-post outcomes measurement of the older adults included in this study were measured with the Paired-Samples T Test. Independent samples t-tests were conducted to explore potential differences between participants from nursing homes and day care centres in post-intervention outcomes. Simple linear regressions were performed to examine the influence of age, sex, frailty, functional status and participation in various intervention activities during the stay in the institutional settings, focusing on performance in daily living, cognitive function, leisure, physical and sensory abilities and community participation, on post-assessment outcomes of engagement in meaningful activities and occupational balance. Variables that were significant in the simple regressions (p < 0.20) were retained for subsequent analysis. A hierarchical multiple regression was then performed with the significant predictors, entered in different steps based on the conceptual model of determinants developed for this study (Fig. 1). This framework articulates the theoretical pathways between capacity determinants and occupational outcomes, providing the rationale for the hierarchical approach. First, age, sex and baseline values of the outcomes were included as confounders to account for initial inter-individual differences and improve the estimation of associations at follow-up. Second frailty, functional status and engagement in the aforementioned activities were added. This order of entry allows for isolating the predictive impact of functional independence, identified as a primary enabler and specific activity types on the two independent outcomes: meaningful engagement (EMAS) and occupational balance (OBQ-E). All analyses were performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, IBM Corporation version 25.0; Armonk, NY) and the level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Regarding differences in assessments of engagement in meaningful activities, occupational balance, frailty and functional status, it was observed that the mean score for engagement in meaningful activities (EMAS) increased from 34.93 (8.31) to 36.03 (8.05) points, with a mean difference of -1.10 (8.80) points (p = 0.162). Regarding occupational balance (OBQ), the mean score decreased from 41.82 (14.52) to 41.65 (12.44) points, with a non-significant mean difference of 0.17 (12.98) points (p = 0.886). In terms of frailty (Fried scale), the mean score decreased from 2.74 (1.13) to 2.52 (0.95) points, with a mean difference of 0.21 (1.04) points (p = 0.022). Specifically, between the first and second assessment, there was a change in the distribution of frailty levels among participants, as the majority of participants (60.4%) were classified as frail (i.e., those with a total score of 3 or more on the Fried scale), while 38.2% were pre-frail (i.e., those with a total score of 1 or 2) and only 1.4% were non-frail (i.e., those with a total score of 0). In the subsequent assessment, the proportion of frail individuals decreased to 54.8%, while the percentage of pre-frail individuals increased to 42.9% and the proportion of non-frail individuals increased to 2.4%. Although the change in mean score reached statistical significance, the effect size was small (d = 0.21) and the observed shifts in frailty categories were minimal and bidirectional, suggesting no clinically meaningful change in frailty status. In addition, functional status (FIM) decreased from a mean of 97.42 (24.39) to 94.12 (24.09) points, with a mean difference of 3.30 (11.30) (p = 0.001). No significant differences between settings (nursing homes vs. day care centres) were found for EMAS (p = 0.95). OBQ (p = 0.81) or functional status (FIM; p = 0.80). However, nursing home participants showed higher frailty scores (FRIED; p = 0.031).

Discussion

Engagement in meaningful activities and occupational balance did not show statistically significant changes over time. Despite the statistical significance observed in frailty and functional status, the magnitude of the changes was small and unlikely to be clinically meaningful. Moreover, engagement in meaningful activities was positively predicted by participation in cognitive activities and baseline engagement and negatively associated with frailty, participation in activities of daily living, and occupational and leisure activities. Occupational balance was positively predicted by sex and functional status, and negatively associated with participation in occupational and leisure activities, with baseline occupational balance as a strong predictor [20, 26].

The participants’ profile in our study is consistent with previous research conducted in institutionalised settings, which typically report mean ages ranging from 84 to 87 years [19, 30]. The predominance of women in our sample is also consistent with existing evidence, which attributes higher rates of institutionalisation among women to greater longevity, multimorbidity and economic vulnerability, as well as higher mortality rates among men [19, 31] These demographic characteristics are important to consider, as they may influence how institutionalised older adults engage with occupational opportunities, with implications for the design and tailoring of activity programmes. Nevertheless, most previous studies examining participation or occupational balance have been community-based studies, usually involving younger older adults, with different environmental and occupational contexts compared to institutionalised settings [16, 17] .

Despite the different activities offered in the institutionalised settings in this study, no statistically significant changes were observed in either participation in meaningful activities or occupational balance along the follow-up. Although frailty improved and functional status declined significantly over time, these changes were small and unlikely to be clinically meaningful. These findings may suggest that the activities offered were not sufficiently aligned with person-centred approaches [32–34]. While such activities may help prevent functional decline and frailty, they do not necessarily foster a sense of personal growth, an essential component of meaningful engagement [35]. Therefore, continued participation in activities that lack personal relevance may lead to reduced psychological well-being and a decline in quality of life [36]. In this context, a co-creation process for programme design is recommended, developing a partnership between residents, families and staff to improve outcomes and satisfaction [37–39]. Such process can be structured into phases, such as understanding the issue, exploring ideas, prototyping solutions and testing them, ensuring that those with lived experience are central to both design and evaluation. This approach aligns with participatory and experience-based co-design frameworks that emphasise collaborative partnerships between stakeholders in healthcare and long-term care improvement [39, 40].

Considering the factors influencing engagement in meaningful activities, our study underscores the relevance of including cognitive activities in institutional programmes. This is supported by previous findings in community-dwelling older adults, where participation in instrumental and cognitively demanding leisure activities has been associated with better executive function, attention, functional status and overall health in older adults, whereas low-demand activities show only weak associations [41–43]. Interestingly, frailty and greater participation in activities of daily living were negatively associated with engagement in meaningful activities. This may suggest that, within institutional settings, participation primarily driven by care needs or routine functional demands does not necessarily translate into a greater sense of meaningful engagement. Similarly, participation in occupational and leisure activities may not always reflect personally meaningful or self-directed occupations, particularly in highly structured institutional environments. These findings may reinforce the importance of promoting opportunities for meaningful, self-directed, and cognitively stimulating occupations within institutional settings.

Our findings further support that differences in occupational balance are partly shaped by individual characteristics, with women and individuals with higher functional levels exhibiting better occupational balance, along with those reporting lower engagement in leisure activities [44]. This finding is consistent with previous literature relating sex, longevity and career trajectories to patterns of occupational engagement in older people [31]. Furthermore, we found that participation in occupational and leisure activities negatively influenced occupational balance. Although this finding may appear counterintuitive, several explanations should be considered. First, institutional settings may overrepresent certain types of leisure activities, particularly passive or highly structured activities, at the expense of other meaningful occupations such as productive, social, or self-directed activities [45–47], possibly due to organisational routines and limited staff or material resources within institutional settings [46, 48]. Therefore, greater participation in leisure activities may not necessarily reflect a more balanced occupational pattern. Second, this association may also reflect reverse causality, whereby individuals with lower occupational balance engage more frequently in leisure activities available within the institution. In addition, some conceptual overlap between participation domains and occupational balance measures cannot be ruled out. These findings highlight the potential importance of promoting greater active participation, shared responsibility, and mutual learning in the design of institutional programmes tailored to the needs and preferences of older adults [37, 49]. However, given the observational design of this study, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require further conceptual and empirical investigation. Taken together, these findings suggest that institutional activity programmes may help maintain certain aspects of health and functioning, while being insufficient to enhance meaningful engagement and occupational balance.

Limitations of the study

This study presents several limitations to be considered when interpreting the results. First, the observational design without a control group limits the ability to establish causal inferences from the observed changes. Future randomised controlled trials, including both experimental and control group, are needed to rigorously examine the effectiveness of such interventions. Second, the influence of centre-specific contextual factors may have introduced variability in the results, as these factors have been identified as key determinants of meaningful engagement and occupational balance in institutionalised settings [49]. Third, the use of self-report scales may have introduced self-report bias, as responses depend on institutionalised older adult’s self-perceptions and may not fully reflect actual participation levels [36]. In addition, variability in participant availability and engagement throughout routine residency activities may have influenced the findings; however, these factors were not formally assessed. Finally, the representativeness of the sample is limited to institutional settings in a specific region of southern Spain, which reduce the generalisability of the findings to other contexts. Despite these limitations, this study provides novel evidence on an underexplored population and contributes to the development of person-centred interventions that promote meaningful ageing in institutional settings.

Strengths and implications of the study

Among other reasons, the strengths of this study lie in its longitudinal and prospective design, which allows for the analysis of how variables evolve and the identification of predictive factors with greater reliability than cross-sectional studies. It focuses on a vulnerable and understudied population, highly dependent elderly people in care homes, thereby filling a significant gap in the literature. Furthermore, it offers an innovative perspective by identifying that not all institutional activities are equally beneficial, distinguishing between non-tailored activities and those that foster meaningful engagement. Finally, the study is of great clinical relevance, as it proposes a person-centred care approach and incorporates contextualised evidence from the Spanish setting, specifically the region of Andalusia, thereby contributing to knowledge within this specific context.

Conclusion

This study highlights that engagement in meaningful activities and occupational balance did not improve despite of the variety of activities offered to the institutionalised older adults included. Older adults with higher functional status and those who participated in cognitive activities showed better engagement in meaningful activities, whereas women, those with higher functional status and lower participation in leisure activities showed better occupational balance. These findings suggest the need to critically analyse the type and range of activities offered in institutional settings, reconsider the amount of time devoted to highly structured leisure activities and promote more enriching and valuable experiences for institutionalised older adults. To this end, the experience-based co-design approaches involving all stakeholders (i.e., older adults, caregivers, social and health professionals) are essential to develop and refine activities programmes of institutionalised settings. This would contribute to achieving a person-centred approach that enhances engagement in meaningful activities and occupational balance. Future studies should incorporate controlled designs and mixed methods to better capture the associations found in the present study and the complex interplay between health status, contextual factors, activity engagement and occupational balance in institutionalised settings.
