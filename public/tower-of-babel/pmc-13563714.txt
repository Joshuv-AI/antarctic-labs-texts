Temporal dissociation between endocrine and psychological responses across an official boxing bout in national-level male boxers
Authors: Bilal Bayram, Melek Makaracı, Zarife Pancar, Yücel Makaracı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile cortisol and testosterone track rapid physiological adaptations to competition, psychological states like anxiety and anger are driven by cognitive appraisal and context. This study examined concurrent neuroendocrine and emotional responses in boxing to determine whether hormonal shifts mirror athletes’ subjective stress.MethodsTwenty national-level male boxers (age: 19.5 ± 1.4 years, range: 18–21 years; sports experience: 8.5 ± 1.8 years) competing in an official national tournament were assessed using a repeated-measures design. Salivary cortisol and testosterone (sCor and sTes) were collected at three standardized time points (pre-, mid-, and post-competition), whereas competitive anxiety (cognitive, somatic, and self-confidence) and state anger were evaluated immediately before and after each bout using the Competitive State Anxiety Inventory-2 and the State-Trait Anger Expression Inventory.ResultssTes increased significantly during mid-competition, whereas sCor peaked post-competition (both p < .001, ηp² = 0.96). Cognitive and somatic anxiety increased significantly from pre- to post-competition (both p < .001, large effect sizes [dz = 3.54 and 1.07, respectively]), whereas self-confidence and state anger remained unchanged (both p > .05). No significant associations were found between hormonal reactivity and changes in anxiety or anger (all p > .05).ConclusionHormonal and psychological responses to competition followed distinct temporal patterns, with no evidence that acute endocrine changes directly reflected athletes’ subjective emotional responses. These findings suggest that physiological biomarkers and self-reported psychological measures provide complementary rather than interchangeable information about competitive stress. Accordingly, athlete monitoring in combat sports may benefit from integrating both physiological and psychological assessments. This study extends current knowledge by concurrently characterizing endocrine and psychological responses during official boxing competition.

Introduction

Competition represents a fundamental feature of sport that challenges athletes to demonstrate and develop their physical and psychological capacities while striving for successful performance [1]. In sporting settings, competition is a multidimensional process that challenges not only physical performance but also psychological resilience [2]. Exposure to competitive stressors elicits a range of physiological and psychological responses in athletes [3]. Real competition environments, in particular, provide ecologically valid conditions for examining the neuroendocrine and emotional reactions elicited by these stressors [4].

Combat sports place substantial psychophysiological demands on athletes due to their high physical intensity, pronounced competitive pressure, and strong performance expectations [5]. Among these sports, boxing requires athletes to deliver controlled punches while simultaneously defending against incoming strikes [6]. Official boxing bouts consist of a predetermined number of rounds (typically 3–12), each characterized by short bursts of high-intensity, intermittent efforts [7]. Consequently, the repeated anaerobic demands and competitive pressure inherent to boxing contribute to significant accumulations of physiological and psychological stress [8].

Studies investigating hormonal responses to combat sport competitions indicate that both real and simulated bouts act as potent stressors on the endocrine system [8–10]. Within this context, cortisol and testosterone are frequently examined as key markers of neuroendocrine responses to competitive situations [11, 12]. Cortisol is a steroid hormone synthesized in the adrenal cortex that increases in response to physical and psychological stressors and is widely used as an objective indicator of stress [13]. Testosterone, on the other hand, is a steroid hormone that has been linked to competitive contexts and dominance-related behaviors, although its influence on behavior is complex, context-dependent, and not directly deterministic [14]. Evidence suggests a reciprocal regulatory relationship between cortisol and testosterone, whereby elevated cortisol may dampen the influence of testosterone on competitive behaviors, while increases in testosterone may correspond with competitive intensity and bout outcomes [15, 16]. However, it is important to note that circulating concentrations of cortisol and testosterone do not directly translate into immediate behavioral outputs.

Psychological processes, including stress and anxiety, are closely linked to endocrine responses elicited during competition [17, 18]. These endocrine responses occur rapidly in response to competitive stressors but may primarily reflect physiological mobilization rather than immediate modulation of higher-order cognitive or emotional states. Accordingly, saliva-based hormone analyses have become a widely accepted method in sports science for evaluating training load, competition stress, recovery status, and overall physiological readiness [19]. Nevertheless, salivary cortisol and testosterone should be interpreted as peripheral markers of endocrine activity rather than direct indicators of central neural processes or behavioral states. Exercise-induced reductions in plasma volume can transiently elevate measured hormone concentrations, which may overestimate endocrine activation if not carefully contextualized [20]. This consideration is particularly relevant in high-intensity, intermittent sports such as boxing.

Emotional factors, particularly anxiety and anger, represent additional components that significantly influence athletes’ responses during competition [21]. Among these emotional responses, state anger is of particular interest in boxing because athletes are repeatedly exposed to physical confrontation, perceived threat, and situations requiring rapid emotional regulation under competitive pressure. Unlike general affect or perceived arousal, state anger reflects a specific transient emotional response that may influence attentional focus, decision-making, and behavioral control during direct opponent interactions. Although evidence regarding competition-induced changes in state anger remains inconsistent [22], examining this construct alongside competitive anxiety may provide complementary insight into the emotional responses elicited during real boxing competition. Athletes’ cognitive and emotional reactions are shaped by dynamic interactions between personal characteristics and situational demands, through which behavior emerges from person–environment interplay [23]. Within this framework, increases in anxiety before and during competition are expected and may alter both performance outcomes and accompanying hormonal patterns [24]. Although findings regarding anger responses remain inconsistent across sports, acute increases in anger have been reported in highly competitive contact sport settings, suggesting that state anger may represent an important psychological response during competition [25, 26]. Accordingly, hormonal responses to competition may not necessarily parallel athletes’ subjective emotional responses, as endocrine and psychological changes can follow partly independent patterns despite occurring concurrently [27, 28].

Despite growing evidence that competitive situations elicit both endocrine and psychological responses, these responses have rarely been examined concurrently during official boxing competition. Consequently, it remains unclear whether acute endocrine reactivity parallels athletes’ subjective emotional responses under real competitive conditions. To address this gap, this study aimed to investigate competition-induced psychophysiological responses in national-level male boxers by examining changes in salivary cortisol and testosterone concentrations together with psychological responses, including cognitive anxiety, somatic anxiety, self-confidence, and state anger, before, during, and after competition. Rather than assuming a direct causal relationship, this study examined the associations between acute hormonal responses and changes in athletes’ emotional responses. We hypothesized that (i) sCor and sTes levels during competition would be significantly higher than pre-competition levels, (ii) post-competition state anger scores would be elevated compared with pre-competition scores, and (iii) competition-induced changes in salivary cortisol and testosterone would not be significantly associated with changes in state anger or competitive anxiety.

Methods

Study design

The present study employed a field-based, naturalistic, and repeated-measures design in which athletes’ psychophysiological stress responses were monitored during actual competitive bouts. Data collection was conducted during an elimination tournament used to select national team members. Potential candidates from the national pool were invited to this tournament, where they competed in single bouts determined by a randomized fixture. This procedure ensured an objective and ethically sound competition environment and provided an ecologically valid setting for examining neuroendocrine responses during real, high-stakes matches organized by a responsible manager affiliated with the national federation. Neuroendocrine and psychological responses were assessed using saliva samples and standardized questionnaires, respectively, under standardized experimental conditions designed to minimize the influence of potential confounding factors, as detailed below.

Participants

A minimum required sample size of 20 participants was calculated through an a priori power analysis conducted with G*Power 3.1 software, assuming an alpha level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.80 [29]. Accordingly, twenty national-level male boxing athletes (age: 19.5 ± 1.4 years, range: 18–21 years; height: 1.76 ± 0.1 m; body mass: 65.9 ± 12.4 kg; body mass index: 21.3 ± 3.4 kg/m2; sports experience: 8.5 ± 1.8 years) were voluntarily recruited for the study. All athletes were actively competing in national championships organized by the National Boxing Federation and also participated in international tournaments. Participants competed in the relevant senior men’s weight categories in accordance with World Boxing regulations. The participants’ measured body mass ranged from 52 to 100 kg. Each boxer competed in one official bout against an opponent from the same weight category, resulting in a total of 10 bouts.

None of the participants reported any acute or chronic injuries, neurological disorders, or other health conditions that could adversely affect their performance during the study. At enrollment, all subjects were medically cleared and injury-free, as formally verified by their respective club managers. The use of performance-enhancing substances or medications and irregular training attendance were considered exclusion criteria.

Subjects were fully informed about the study procedures, potential risks, and benefits, and each provided both verbal and written informed consent prior to participation. The study protocol adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Social and Humanities Research Ethics Board of Karamanoğlu Mehmetbey University (approval ID: 09-2024/226).

Procedures and competition setting

All bouts in the elimination tournament were conducted in accordance with international boxing regulations and were overseen by an official referee. The tournament followed a single-elimination (knockout) format, whereby athletes were eliminated after a loss (“lose and exit”), and therefore competed in one bout within the scope of this study. Each match consisted of three 3-minute rounds with 1-minute rest intervals. The structure was intentionally designed to promote maximal competitive effort and to minimize tactical pacing strategies that could prolong the bout. None of the matches ended in a knockout. Because all bouts resulted in a clear win/lose outcome, the competitive context substantially increased the perceived difficulty, importance, and overall stress imposed on the athletes.

Each competition was conducted individually, athletes did not observe other matches, nor were they provided with information about opponents or match outcomes. All bouts were completed within the same competitive session on the same day. Each athlete contributed data from a single bout only (i.e., their first and only bout within the study context); therefore, no selection or randomization of bouts was required. To evaluate neuroendocrine changes (sCor and sTes) during the competition, saliva samples were collected at three time points: (i) pre-competition (immediately prior to warm-up, ~ 30 min before), (ii) mid-competition (after the second round), and (iii) post-competition (approximately 5 min after the bout). The mid-competition sampling point was selected after the second round to capture acute endocrine responses during active competition while allowing sufficient time for saliva collection during the scheduled one-minute rest interval without interfering with the conduct of the bout. This approach is consistent with previous investigations examining hormonal responses during combat sport competitions [30, 31].

In addition, athletes completed the State-Trait Anger Expression Inventory (STAXI) [32] and the Competitive State Anxiety Inventory-2 (CSAI-2) [33] at time points synchronized with saliva collection immediately before and after the competition. In this context, sCor and sTes were used as physiological indicators of stress, whereas state anger and competitive anxiety were assessed as psychological responses to competitive demands, allowing for the examination of both physiological and subjective components of stress.

Although match outcomes (win/loss) were recorded, they were not included in the primary analyses. This decision was based on the study’s aim to examine general psychophysiological responses to competitive stress rather than performance determinants. Additionally, the relatively small sample size and potential inter-individual variability in opponent characteristics and bout dynamics limited the statistical power and interpretability of outcome-based comparisons.

Given the sensitivity and logistical challenges of collecting physiological and psychological data during a real competition, the first author (BB) was solely responsible for data acquisition. He coordinated with all athletes prior to the tournament and managed the entire procedure, including collecting saliva samples (pre-, mid-, and post-match) and administering questionnaires. Saliva collection

The saliva collection protocol was verbally explained to all athletes beforehand to prevent procedural errors and minimise potential bias. The sampling procedures followed the protocol described by Aydemir et al. [11]. To minimize potential confounding influences on salivary hormone concentrations, several standardization procedures were implemented. Athletes were instructed to maintain their habitual sleep schedule, obtain their usual night’s sleep, and wake between 08:00 and 09:00 a.m. on the day of competition. They were also instructed to refrain from caffeine-containing beverages for at least 12 h before competition, maintain their usual hydration status, avoid strenuous exercise during the 24 h preceding the tournament, and refrain from eating and drinking (except water) for at least 60 min before each saliva collection. Tooth brushing before saliva collection was also prohibited to avoid blood contamination. The first author (BB) reinforced compliance with these standardized pre-competition instructions through regular communication with all athletes prior to the tournament. Because all athletes competed under identical competition conditions and saliva samples were collected according to a standardized timeline, potential variability associated with circadian fluctuations in salivary hormone concentrations was minimized.

Saliva samples were obtained by the passive drool method. Athletes first allowed saliva to accumulate in their mouths, with their chins tilted slightly downward. They then gently expelled the pooled saliva into 15-mL Falcon polypropylene tubes. Each sample tube was pre-labelled with the athlete’s study ID and sampling time point (pre-, mid-, and post-competition) and distributed to each athlete before the data-collection began. All saliva samples were collected within a standardized time window (12:00 p.m.–5:00 p.m.) during the same competition day to minimize potential circadian variation in hormone concentrations. All saliva samples were collected during the tournament and temporarily stored in a portable insulated cooler containing ice packs until transport to the biochemistry laboratory, located in the same city. Samples were stored at − 80 °C immediately upon arrival. The interval between the first sample collection and storage at − 80 °C did not exceed approximately 4 h [34].

Assessment of state anger and competition anxiety

State anger was assessed using the national adaptation of STAXI [32]. The State Anger subscale measures the intensity of angry feelings experienced at the present moment. Participants responded using the national adaptation’s 4-point Likert scale ranging from “not at all” to “very much so,” with higher scores indicating greater levels of state anger. Scores were calculated by summing all item responses, yielding a total score between 10 and 40. The internal consistency of the scale in the present study was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.85).

Athletes also completed the national adaptation of the CSAI-2 to assess competitive anxiety [33]. The CSAI-2 consists of 27 items across three subscales, cognitive anxiety, somatic anxiety, and self-confidence, with nine items per subscale. Items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very much so). Subscale scores ranged from 9 to 36, with higher scores reflecting greater cognitive anxiety, greater somatic anxiety, or greater self-confidence. The internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) values for the current study were as follows: cognitive anxiety (α = 0.84), somatic anxiety (α = 0.88), and self-confidence (α = 0.89).

Biochemical analysis

All biochemical analyses were conducted in a certified biochemistry laboratory. Frozen saliva samples were thawed at room temperature and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min to remove cellular debris; only the clear supernatant was used for hormonal analysis. sCor and sTes concentrations were measured using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Bioassay Technology Laboratory Company, Shanghai, China). All samples were analysed in duplicate, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis. Concentrations were calculated according to the standard curve constructed using standard values and were expressed as µg/dL for sCor and ng/dL for sTes.

Hormone concentrations were measured using commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reported analytical performance characteristics of the assays, including intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation, analytical sensitivity, and measuring range, complied with the manufacturer’s specifications.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as mean (X̄) and standard deviation (SD). Normality testing with the Shapiro–Wilk procedure showed that all dependent variables were normally distributed. A repeated-measures ANOVA was performed to evaluate differences in sCor and sTes across the three time points (pre-, mid-, and post-competition). When a significant main effect was observed, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons were conducted to identify pairwise differences. Additionally, paired-samples t-tests were used to compare state anger scores and competitive anxiety levels between pre-competition and post-competition measurements. Effect sizes (Cohen’s dz) were calculated using the formula dz = t / √n, which is recommended for within-subject (paired) designs, and were interpreted according to conventional thresholds, with values around 0.20 considered small, around 0.50 medium, and 0.80 and above considered large. To further examine the psychophysiological coupling between hormonal reactivity and emotional responses, delta (Δ) scores were calculated as the difference between the mid- and pre-competition values for the hormonal markers (ΔCort and ΔTest) and between the post- and pre-competition values for the psychological outcomes (ΔCognitive Anxiety and ΔSomatic Anxiety). Pearson correlation analyses were then conducted to assess the associations between peak hormonal reactivity (Δ from pre- to mid-competition) and subsequent changes in state anger and competitive anxiety (Δ from pre- to post-competition). Statistical significance was set at an alpha level of 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS (Version 27; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate competition-induced neuroendocrine responses and emotional changes in national-level athletes during an actual boxing competition. Our findings demonstrate robust elevations in both sCor and sTes across competitive phases, with the most pronounced increase in sTes during mid-competition and sCor peaking post-competition. These endocrine shifts were accompanied by significant increases in cognitive and somatic anxiety from pre- to post-competition, however, peak hormonal reactivity did not correlate with changes in state anger or competitive anxiety, suggesting that neuroendocrine activation and subjective emotional states may be regulated by partially distinct mechanisms during real competitive encounters. Collectively, these results provide valuable insights into the hormonal and emotional responses of national-level boxers to genuine competitive stress.

The presence of a competitive environment serves as a potent stressor for athletes, eliciting physiological adjustments that prepare the organism to cope with imminent physical and psychological demands [4]. Our hormonal analyses revealed distinct temporal patterns for sCor and sTes across the competitive bout. sCor exhibited a progressive rise from pre-competition, peaking post-competition, consistent with cortisol’s well-established role as an acute marker of metabolic and psychological load during high-intensity combat sports [8, 35]. However, this increase should be interpreted with caution. In addition to activation of the HPA axis, acute elevations in circulating hormone concentrations during and after high-intensity exercise may partially reflect exercise-induced reductions in plasma volume (i.e., hemoconcentration), rather than solely increased secretion. Therefore, the observed post-competition increase in sCor likely represents a combined effect of physiological stress, metabolic demand, and fluid shifts occurring during the bout. Accordingly, post-competition elevations in cortisol may reflect the integrated influence of psychological arousal, perceived threat, accumulated fatigue, and cognitive demands, although the relative contribution of these factors cannot be determined within the present study design. This pattern is broadly consistent with previous literature demonstrating increases in cortisol following intense athletic engagement [36, 37].

Similarly, studies conducted across different sporting disciplines have consistently shown significant increases in cortisol levels immediately before or during competition [11, 38, 39]. In tournament-style combat settings, post-bout cortisol concentrations have consistently been reported to exceed pre-bout levels [40]. However, as in the present study, these responses should be interpreted cautiously because they may reflect the combined influence of HPA axis activation, metabolic stress, exercise-induced fluid shifts, and other physiological responses to intense competition rather than endocrine activation alone. Notably, the high-impact, full-contact nature of combat sports, leads to particularly pronounced cortisol secretion, with levels often remaining elevated for an extended period after exertion has ceased. This delayed recovery underscores that competitive stress is not merely phasic but can exert extended physiological consequences. However, it is important to acknowledge that the physiological stress elicited by competition does not always translate into measurable changes in cortisol secretion [41]. In this regard, factors such as sport-specific characteristics, bout duration and continuity, contact intensity, and individual adaptive capacity may play a decisive role in shaping cortisol responses.

While sCor displayed its maximum response post-competition, sTes followed a distinct temporal trajectory, peaking during mid-competition. This divergence highlights the fundamentally different biological functions of the two hormones. Cortisol primarily reflects acute stress reactivity and metabolic mobilization, functioning as a key component of the HPA axis response to physical and psychological challenges [13]. In contrast, testosterone responses observed during competition should be interpreted as context-dependent physiological changes influenced by exercise intensity, metabolic demand, and competitive exposure. Although testosterone has been associated with dominance- and aggression-related behaviors in competitive settings, these relationships are complex and should not be interpreted as direct or exclusive indicators of behavioral responses [42]. Accordingly, the mid-competition increase in sTes likely reflects the combined physiological demands of intense exercise and the broader competitive context. Although psychological factors such as competitive engagement, motivation, and outcome expectancy may contribute to this response, the relative contribution of these factors cannot be determined from the present study. The relatively low pre-competition testosterone concentrations may reflect the absence of intense physical exertion before the bout, although interactions between the HPA and HPG axes may also contribute to this pattern. Supporting this interpretation, Casto and Edwards [43] reported increases in sTes during competition among football players, followed by a decline within 30 min post-match. Similarly, Edwards and Kurlander [42] observed linear increases in sTes during warm-up and throughout competition in volleyball players and tennis players. However, competition-related changes in testosterone should not be interpreted solely as a consequence of competitive intensity, but rather as the result of multiple interacting physiological and psychological factors that may differ between individuals [44].

Although testosterone has been associated with dominance- and status-related behaviors in competitive settings [45], this relationship does not necessarily imply corresponding changes in subjective emotional states such as anger. However, contrary to this theoretical expectation and to our hypotheses, state anger did not change significantly in this study, indicating a possible dissociation between neuroendocrine reactivity and observable emotional responses during real-world boxing performance. It is also worth noting that physical exercise itself is one of the most effective strategies for managing anger, because it promotes neurotransmitter synthesis, particularly of serotonin, which plays a key inhibitory role in anger and aggression [46]. Moreover, the relationship between competition and anger remains inconsistent across studies [22], which is likely attributable to differences in type of sport, competitive structure, and contextual demands [47]. Aggressive tendencies in sports have also been linked to broader sociocultural patterns of violence within specific disciplines, suggesting that anger expression may depend on factors beyond immediate physiological arousal [18]. Given this complexity in anger regulation, examining other emotional domains provides valuable insight into how athletes psychologically respond to competitive stress. Although significant changes were observed in both endocrine and psychological responses during competition, their temporal patterns and associations differed. Specifically, cognitive and somatic anxiety increased significantly from pre- to post-competition, whereas no significant changes were observed in state anger. Furthermore, neither cortisol nor testosterone responses were significantly associated with changes in anxiety, suggesting that endocrine and psychological responses may represent partially distinct aspects of the competitive stress response. The observed increases in cognitive and somatic anxiety highlight the anticipatory and reactive psychological burden experienced by athletes in real combat situations [48]. This pattern aligns with previous research showing that competitive environments, particularly in high-intensity or contact sports, reliably elevate athletes’ anxiety levels as they navigate performance demands, uncertainty, and outcome expectations [47, 49, 50]. However, as mentioned above, state anger did not change significantly, suggesting that anger may be less sensitive to acute competitive demands or more effectively regulated by experienced athletes. These findings suggest that competitive anxiety and state anger may respond differently to the demands of boxing competition, although the present measurement schedule does not allow direct comparison of their temporal dynamics.

Consistent with the study hypotheses, no significant associations were observed between hormonal reactivity and changes in anxiety or anger, despite clear hormonal and psychological changes across the competition. Neither sCor reactivity (Δpre→post) nor sTes reactivity (Δpre→mid) was correlated with changes in cognitive or somatic anxiety. This lack of association suggests that circulating hormone concentrations may not directly reflect subjective emotional states during acute competition. Rather, emotional responses may be influenced by cognitive appraisal, prior experience, and situational context, which may operate independently of acute peripheral endocrine fluctuations. This dissociation is consistent with previous evidence indicating that physiological and subjective stress responses do not always covary in competitive settings [51]. Taken together, these findings indicate that concurrent changes in hormonal and emotional variables may follow distinct trajectories during competition, although causal relationships cannot be inferred from the present design. Rather than indicating a direct hormone–emotion coupling, emotional responses in athletes may be more strongly associated with contextual and cognitive factors than with acute hormonal fluctuations [52]. Physiological stress responses and subjective psychological responses should not be considered interchangeable during real boxing competition. Therefore, athlete monitoring should integrate both physiological biomarkers and psychological assessments rather than relying on either approach alone. Such an integrated approach may better support individualized athlete monitoring, recovery planning, and psychological preparation in combat sports.

It should also be acknowledged that the present study employed a field-based observational design without a non-competitive comparison condition (e.g., a standardized training session). Consequently, although the observed hormonal and psychological responses occurred during official competition, the relative contributions of competitive psychological stress and the physiological demands of high-intensity exercise cannot be distinguished. Therefore, the observed responses likely reflect the combined influence of physical exertion and competitive stress.

From an applied perspective, the present findings suggest that endocrine markers and self-reported psychological measures provide complementary rather than interchangeable information about athletes’ responses to competition. Consequently, practitioners should avoid relying solely on hormonal measurements to infer athletes’ psychological state during competition. Instead, integrating physiological markers with validated psychological assessments may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of competitive stress, thereby supporting individualized athlete monitoring, recovery planning, and psychological preparation in combat sports. Furthermore, the findings highlight the importance of adopting a multidimensional monitoring approach that considers both biological and psychological responses when evaluating athletes’ readiness and adaptation to competitive demands.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, sCor and sTes were collected at fixed time points, which may not fully capture rapid fluctuations in endocrine responses throughout the competitive bout. Second, the absence of a non-competitive comparison condition (e.g., a standardized training session) prevents distinguishing the relative contributions of competitive psychological stress and the physiological demands of intense exercise. Although competition outcomes (win/loss) were recorded, they were not included in the primary analyses. Consequently, the present study could not determine whether hormonal or psychological responses differed according to competitive outcome. Given the relatively small sample size and potential variability in opponent characteristics and bout dynamics, such analyses would have been underpowered and should be addressed in future research. Third, although participants were instructed to maintain their habitual sleep schedule and dietary routine before competition, these factors were not objectively monitored; therefore, some inter-individual variability related to recovery status, sleep quality, or nutritional intake cannot be excluded. Fourth, psychological responses were assessed using self-report questionnaires, which may have been influenced by social desirability or athletes’ reluctance to disclose their true emotional state during competition. Finally, the inclusion of only national-level male athletes limits the generalizability of the findings to female boxers and athletes of different competitive standards.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a temporal dissociation between neuroendocrine and emotional responses during real boxing competition in national-level athletes. While sCor and sTes exhibited clear phase-dependent reactivity, competitive anxiety increased independently of hormonal changes. These findings suggest that endocrine responses and subjective emotional states may operate through partially independent mechanisms under competitive stress. From a practical perspective, the present results do not support the use of isolated hormonal measurements to infer athletes’ psychological state during competition. Instead, they reinforce the importance of integrating physiological and psychological assessments when monitoring competitive stress. Collectively, these findings emphasize that physiological and psychological responses to competition should be monitored as complementary dimensions rather than interchangeable indicators of competitive stress. This integrated approach may help coaches, sport psychologists, and sport scientists better understand athletes’ readiness, recovery needs, and responses to high-pressure competitive environments. Future research should examine these relationships using larger samples, more frequent measurements, and consideration of contextual factors such as competition outcome and athlete level to better understand their relevance for performance and applied practice.
