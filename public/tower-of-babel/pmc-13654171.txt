Multi-sequence magnetic resonance imaging of a Brucella-infected abdominal aortic aneurysm: a rare case description
Authors: Xiaofang Chen, Shibo Li, Xiaoyan Ma, Xingguang Liu, Xing Zhou
Published: 2026

Brucella-infected abdominal aortic aneurysm (BIAAA) is a rare but potentially life-threatening cardiovascular complication of brucellosis. The role of comprehensive magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) evaluation in this setting remains underutilized. We report a case of BIAAA in a patient without livestock exposure, highlighting multi-sequence MRI findings that may inform the clinical diagnosis and management of this rare condition.

A 55-year-old male was admitted to Gansu Provincial Hospital on August 19, 2025, with progressively worsening low back and abdominal pain for 10 months. He denied livestock contact; however, his sister had been diagnosed with brucellosis 10 months earlier. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee(s) and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this article and the accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) performed on August 21, 2025 (Figure 1) revealed a 52 mm × 56 mm saccular aneurysm near the inferior mesenteric artery with intraluminal heterogeneous low-density material and a periaortic low-density shadow involving the left psoas major muscle, suggestive of infection.

A multidisciplinary team evaluated the risk of stent-graft infection. Given the presence of active infection, surgery was delayed, strict blood pressure control was implemented, and triple antibiotic therapy (doxycycline, rifampicin, and gentamicin) was initiated on August 22, 2025, to stabilize the lesion. On August 26, 2025, MRI (Figure 2) combined with magnetic resonance angiography (MRA; Figure 3) revealed a saccular aneurysm with patchy T2-weighted imaging hyperintense signals along the aneurysm wall. An irregular thick-walled cystic lesion posterior to the left aspect of the aneurysm induced perilesional exudation extending into the left psoas muscle and paravertebral space. Contrast T1-weighted imaging demonstrated enhancement of the aneurysm wall and cyst, alongside heterogeneous enhancement of the surrounding soft tissues, without typical Brucella spondylotic lesions in the lumbar spine.

On August 28, 2025, the patient developed hematemesis (approximately 1,500 mL) with hypotension (65/42 mmHg). Emergency CTA (Figure 4A) showed increased aneurysm volume and expanded periaortic inflammation with a high risk of rupture. Emergency endovascular repair with stent-graft implantation and branch artery embolization was performed on the same day, with intraoperative digital subtraction angiography (Video 1). The bleeding was considered multifactorial in origin, with potential factors including antibiotic-related mucosal injury, stress-related mucosal bleeding, and an aorto-enteric fistula; however, CTA showed no definitive evidence of the latter.

Laboratory evaluation on admission showed a white blood cell count of 11.2×109/L (76.3% neutrophils), an interleukin-6 level of 21.62 pg/mL, and a procalcitonin level of 0.091 ng/mL. The Brucella antibody titer determined by tube agglutination testing was 1:400 (++) (Figure S1). On September 18, 2025, non-contrast computed tomography revealed an intact stent graft, reduced aneurysm size (≈18 mm), and resolved inflammation (Figure 4B). The patient’s symptoms improved, and the inflammatory markers normalized by the 4-week follow-up.

BIAAA accounts for less than 5% of cardiovascular complications of brucellosis (1,2). Its pathogenesis involves three main pathways: secondary infection from adjacent foci, hematogenous direct infection of the aortic wall, and infection of a pre-existing aneurysm (3). The abdominal aorta is the most commonly affected site (49%), likely due to low blood flow and a predisposition to thrombus formation (4,5). The patient had no history of livestock exposure or contact with sick animals; however, his sister had been diagnosed with brucellosis 10 months earlier. It is possible that the two siblings shared a common environmental or foodborne source of infection.

CTA serves as the first-line imaging tool for aneurysm evaluation, accurately depicting aneurysm size, intraluminal thrombus, and the risk of rupture (6). MRI provides superior soft tissue contrast and enables supplementary evaluation of perianeurysmal abscesses and spinal involvement (7) as demonstrated in the present case; however, head-to-head comparative analyses of these two modalities remain limited. Most existing studies on BIAAA have focused on CTA (6,8,9), while MRI has rarely been used (3,5,7). Even when MRI was used in previous literature, the examinations were primarily limited to MRA for the morphological assessment of aneurysms, without thorough evaluation of soft tissue or spinal lesions. In our case, MRI clearly delineated the scope of periaortic soft tissue inflammation and excluded lumbar spondylitis, offering critical clues for identifying the infectious source and guiding treatment planning. Nevertheless, diffusion-weighted imaging and apparent diffusion coefficient sequences were not included in our MRI protocol, limiting comprehensive evaluation of infectious lesions, abscesses and fluid collections.

From a clinical perspective, BIAAA should be considered in patients presenting with persistent low back and abdominal pain and elevated inflammatory markers, especially those with a personal or family history of brucellosis. The initial evaluation may include ultrasound and CTA (4), and MRI can be added to better define soft tissue and spinal involvement. For patients at high risk of rupture, emergency endovascular repair is recommended as the first-line treatment (10). An MRI-inclusive approach may provide a more comprehensive assessment of BIAAA.
