Beyond E-FAST: Using POCUS to Suggest the Bullet’s Injury Path of a Thoracoabdominal Gunshot – Case Report
Authors: Carlos Augusto Metidieri Menegozzo, Paula Ferrada, Felipe Kfouri, Sérgio Henrique Bastos Damous, Edivaldo Massazo Utiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transmediastinal traumatic injuries challenge trauma surgeons due to their potential to involve thoracic and abdominal cavities, requiring rapid assessment for timely interventions. This case report describes a 27-year-old male with a thoracoabdominal gunshot wound, emphasizing the potential role of ultrasound in emergency settings. The patient presented with diminished left-sided breath sounds and stable hemodynamics. Physical examination revealed an entry wound at the sixth intercostal space. With portable X-ray unavailable, ultrasound detected left hemothorax, pneumothorax, and pneumopericardium. The sonographic findings suggested the bullet’s trajectory and helped further management. CT confirmed findings, with esophagoscopy and thoracoscopy identifying minor esophageal and pericardial injuries. Discharged three days post-injury, the patient highlights ultrasound’s utility in resource-limited settings despite lower sensitivity compared to CT.

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic transmediastinal injuries present significant challenges to trauma surgeons due to their potential involvement of both the abdominal and thoracic cavities. Rapid and accurate assessment of bullet trajectory is crucial for identifying life-threatening injuries and guiding timely interventions. While computed tomography (CT) is the gold-standard imaging modality due to its high sensitivity for traumatic injuries, it is time-consuming and may not always be readily available. The extended focused assessment with sonography for trauma (e-FAST) has become a valuable tool in trauma evaluation, offering a noninvasive, rapid method for identifying critical injuries despite its lower sensitivity.[1] This case report highlights the effectiveness of ultrasound in diagnosing pneumopericardium and suggesting bullet pathways, underscoring its critical role in emergency management.

CASE REPORT

This case report follows the CARE guidelines,[2] and informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication. A 27-year-old male presented with a gunshot wound to the left thoracoabdominal region. On initial assessment, he exhibited diminished breath sounds on the left side, with pulse oximetry at 93% on room air. His blood pressure was 110/83 mmHg, and his heart rate was 110 bpm. Neurological examination revealed no deficits. Physical examination identified a through-and-through wound on the left forearm, an entry wound at the sixth intercostal space along the left anterior axillary line, and no identifiable exit wound.

Given the unpredictable trajectory of the bullet and the risk of multiple internal injuries, an e-FAST examination was performed in the trauma bay. Portable X-ray was unavailable at the time of the patient’s assessment. E-FAST evaluation revealed a left hemothorax [Figure 1], pneumothorax, and no free intraperitoneal fluid. The subxiphoid window demonstrated focal hyperechoic mobile images with reverberation artifacts resembling A-lines, partially obscuring the heart chambers [Figure 2 and Video 1], findings consistent with pneumopericardium, suggesting a mediastinal passage of the bullet. No pericardial fluid or signs of tamponade were found. In addition, bedside ultrasound identified a small right pleural effusion and basal B-lines and a hyperechoic image in the liver parenchyma [Figure 3]. This liver finding was further examined during the secondary assessment with ultrasound depicting a hyperechoic linear strip, which could suggest that the bullet continued its trajectory to the right side, causing right pleural injury and liver damage.

Due to the patient’s hemodynamic stability, a contrast-enhanced CT scan was performed, confirming the sonographic findings and bullet’s transmediastinal path [Figure 4]. Subsequent esophagoscopy revealed a small posterior wall hematoma [Figure 5]. A minimally invasive laparoscopy of the posterior esophageal wall identified a minor muscular tear without mucosal violation. Thoracoscopic evaluation revealed a residual hemothorax and a small pericardial laceration without associated cardiac injury [Figure 6]. The pericardial injury was left open, and the patient had an uneventful recovery, being discharged 3 days postinjury.

DISCUSSION

Traumatic transmediastinal injuries are among the most complex and life-threatening challenges faced by trauma surgeons. Gunshot wounds can cause severe damage to both the abdominal and thoracic cavities. Determining the bullet’s trajectory is essential for identifying potentially fatal injuries that require urgent intervention.

Although chest and abdominal radiographs can help infer the bullet’s path, they have lower sensitivity for detecting thoracoabdominal injuries compared to other imaging modalities.[3] E-FAST serves as a rapid screening tool during the primary survey, complementing physical examination to identify life-threatening injuries, but it is not a substitute for comprehensive imaging like CT for definitive trajectory mapping. In our case, e-FAST detected left pneumothorax, bilateral pleural effusions, and signs of pneumopericardium. Further ultrasound assessment during the secondary survey depicted the hyperechoic strip in the liver parenchyma. Although the findings could suggest the projectile’s passage, CT is superior for assessing bullet trajectory and can provide additional insights into potential vascular and organ injuries.[34] A 2000 prospective study evaluating stable patients with transmediastinal injuries demonstrated the benefits of CT in guiding bullet trajectory assessment and minimizing unnecessary invasive procedures.[4] A more recent study from 2023 reported that CT scans for mediastinal injuries had an overall sensitivity of 75%, specificity of 99.1%, and accuracy of 98.6%.[5] Table 1 provides the diagnostic performance of different modalities for pneumothorax, hemothorax, pneumopericardium, and bullet’s trajectory.

In this case, portable radiography was unavailable at the time of admission, and the trauma team proceeded without chest and abdominal radiographs. In such scenarios, e-FAST plays a critical role in evaluating penetrating trauma, allowing for the identification of life-threatening injuries and guiding appropriate resuscitation measures.[89] While conventional FAST is effective for assessing the abdominal cavity and pericardial space, incorporating pulmonary windows in e-FAST significantly enhances its utility in trauma settings.[1011] Although e-FAST has lower sensitivity, it demonstrates high specificity for detecting injuries in the torso.[12] In this case, while primarily aimed at detecting thoracoabdominal injuries, ultrasound also helped to suggest the bullet’s injury path, aiding in bedside clinical decision-making. However, it is important to highlight that the current e-FAST protocol is not designed, recommended, or validated to evaluate the bullet’s trajectory or specific solid organ injuries. Such signs might be depicted during a more comprehensive ultrasound evaluation. In this case, while the ultrasound was able to suggest the bullet’s injury path, the CT scan confirmed the suspicion and provided further details.

Two critical sonographic signs in this case suggested the bullet’s injury path. The first was pneumopericardium, a rare but significant finding seldom described in sonographic literature. Key indicators include a hyperechoic strip along the pericardium with reverberation artifacts resembling A-lines, similar to those seen in lung ultrasound [Figure 2]. The second significant finding was a hyperechoic strip within the liver parenchyma [Figure 3], commonly seen in solid organ contusions. In our case, the sonographic findings of injury to both lungs and pneumomediastinum suggested the bullet’s injury path from the left to the right side. These findings aided in hypothesizing the bullet’s trajectory, facilitating timely clinical decision-making.

Despite its advantages, ultrasound has several limitations, particularly in detecting traumatic injuries compared to CT.[13] These limitations are more pronounced when evaluating structures such as the diaphragm, retroperitoneum, mediastinum, and bowel, for which the CT has superior diagnostic accuracy. A previous study found that pelvic fluid, pericardial fat pad, and thoracic views were associated with false-negative ultrasound results.[14] In addition, pneumopericardium and hemothorax can reduce the sensitivity of ultrasound for detecting cardiac injuries.[15] However, no studies have specifically analyzed the diagnostic accuracy of ultrasound in detecting pneumopericardium. Air artifacts, operator dependency, and reduced visualization of gas-filled structures like the bowel or mediastinum can lead to false negatives.[1416] Similarly, ultrasound evaluation of pneumomediastinum is uncommon due to the artifacts produced by gas accumulation, which can obscure interpretation.[16]

When assessing transmediastinal injuries, ultrasound should be used to detect thoracic and abdominal signs of trauma, considering the variable trajectories of penetrating injuries. Signs such as the “lung point” and the “heart sign” can be used to confirm pneumothorax.[17] Moreover, the M-mode can display the “barcode sign,” confirming the absence of lung sliding, especially when it is not readily appreciated. In our case, the heart sign was absent, but pneumothorax was confirmed by the presence of the lung point. We did not use the M-mode because the absence of lung sliding was easily observed on B-mode, avoiding unnecessary delays. A meta-analysis by Netherton et al. evaluated the sensitivity of ultrasound in detecting pneumothorax, pericardial effusion, and intraperitoneal free fluid in both blunt and penetrating trauma cases, reporting sensitivities of 69%, 91%, and 74%, respectively.[11] These findings highlight the limitations of ultrasound in definitively ruling out these injuries. Moreover, no studies have evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of ultrasound for pneumopericardium or pneumomediastinum, and data on other imaging modalities for detecting these conditions are also limited.

While CT remains the gold standard for mapping bullet paths and detecting traumatic injuries due to its superior sensitivity, ultrasound offers rapid and valuable diagnostic insights and serves as an effective screening tool in critical situations.[13] In resource-limited settings where CT may not be available, understanding the role and limitations of ultrasound in trauma evaluation is essential, especially for critically ill patients. Although most trauma protocols recommend CT for stable patients, ultrasound should be systematically included as an adjunct to the physical examination when CT is unavailable. This case underscores the utility of ultrasound in diagnosing life-threatening conditions and suggesting bullet injury paths, offering vital guidance for further management.[18] This case highlights the need for further research into ultrasound’s diagnostic accuracy for rare findings such as pneumopericardium and pneumomediastinum. Future studies should explore standardized protocols for ultrasound in transmediastinal injuries and assess its reliability across operator skill levels. Clinically, this case supports integrating ultrasound into trauma protocols, particularly in resource-limited settings where CT is unavailable, provided trauma teams are trained to recognize its limitations.
