Genome-wide association study dissects the genetic architecture of kernel rate and its component traits in maize
Authors: Wenlong Li, Haiyang Duan, Xinyu Sun, Jihong Zhang, Yuehua Zhao, Jiawen Zhao, Hongyu Chen, Xiaoyang Chen, Dong Ding, Jihua Tang, Jianbing Yan, Xuehai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKernel rate (KR), the ratio of kernel to ear weight, serves as a key metric of assimilate partitioning efficiency and determines single-ear yield stability under high planting densities. Despite its critical role in modern intensive agriculture, the genetic architecture of KR remains largely elusive. Here, we performed a genome-wide association study (GWAS) for KR and its component traits—ear weight (EW), kernel weight (KW), and cob weight (CW)—using a diverse panel of 502 maize inbred lines across seven environments. Phenotypic profiling revealed substantial variation and high heritability. Leveraging 10.77 million high-quality SNPs, we identified 202 significant associations, which were refined into 106 quantitative trait loci (QTL), each explaining 5%-12% of the phenotypic variance. Notably, four QTL hotspots exhibited co-located across multiple traits and environments, indicative of pleiotropic regulation. Integration of gene annotation with transcriptomic profiles narrowed these loci to 308 genes. Subsequent haplotype analysis prioritized two high-confidence candidates: ZmKR1 (encoding pentatricopeptide repeat protein, PPR, essential for kernel development) and ZmKR2 (encoding meiotic recombination 11b, a DNA repair component). Specifically, favorable haplotype (Hap1) of ZmKR1 and ZmKR2 (Hap1) were significantly associated with elevated KR. Furthermore, evolutionary analysis revealed that these two genes have been selected at domestication. Our findings provide comprehensive genetic dissection of KR and its components, offering fundamental insights into the regulation of assimilate partitioning and delivering valuable gene resources for breeding maize varieties with improved yield stability under high-density conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08986-w.Keywords: Maize, Kernel rate, Genetic architecture, High-density planting, Modern breeding

Introduction

Maize (Zea mays L.) stands as a preeminent global cereal crop, indispensable for food, feed, and industrial uses, and its sustained yield improvement is a cornerstone of global food security [1]. To meet escalating demand, increasing planting density has become the predominant strategy for boosting yield per unit area. However, this approach introduces a fundamental physiological challenge: intense inter-plant competition for resources culminates in reduced ear size, thereby threatening single-plant productivity [2]. This constraint makes the efficiency of assimilate partitioning—the process by which a plant directs photosynthetic products to grains versus structural components—a critical determinant of yield under dense canopy. Owing to its extensive genetic diversity and rich natural variation [3], maize has long served as a model species in genetic and genomic research [4].

Kernel yield improvement under high-density planting is closely associated with traits such as kernel rate (KR), ear weight (EW), and cob weight (CW) [2]. KR, the ratio of kernel weight (KW) to EW, serves as a direct and quantifiable measure of this partitioning efficiency [5, 6]. The agronomic significance of KR is powerfully demonstrated by decades of empirical breeding. In China, for instance, concerted phenotypic selection has driven a remarkable increase in the KR of modern maize hybrids, from approximately 85% in older varieties to over 92% in contemporary elite varieties. This trait reflects the plant’s efficiency in allocating photosynthetic products to harvestable grains rather than structural cob materials—a particularly critical adaptation under high-density stress, where reduced ear size makes efficient resource partitioning even more crucial for yield stability. Traits such as ear length (EL) and KW significantly influence KR, making it a crucial determinant of ear productivity, especially in dense planting systems where plants must optimize grain filling within smaller ears. Consequently, varieties with high KR values are particularly valuable for improving grain yield potential under high-density conditions [7], as they can better compensate for the reduction in ear size through improved grain filling efficiency.

Although numerous studies have investigated yield-related traits such as EL, kernel row number (KRN), EW, and KW using linkage mapping approaches [8–10], the genetic basis of KR remains elusive, especially in the context of high-density planting where ear size reduction occurs. For example, researcher mapped 12 QTL for KRN using a maize F2:3 population derived from a cross between V54 (low KRN) and Lian87 (high KRN), each explaining 1.40% to 14.95% of the phenotypic variance across four environments [11]. Similarly, Li et al. (2011) identified a KR-related QTL, qKR-4-1, from a set of 258 recombinant inbred lines (RILs) [7]. However, these studies relied mainly on biparental linkage analysis, which is labor-intensive, limited in resolution, and can only detect bi-allelic variation [12]. Furthermore, despite the agronomic importance of KR for high-density planting, particularly its role in mitigating yield loss from smaller ears, no genes regulating this trait have been cloned to date. Thus, systematic analysis of natural variation in KR remains an unmet need, particularly for breeding maize adapted to high-density conditions.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) based on linkage disequilibrium (LD) offer higher resolution and throughput than traditional linkage mapping and have proven effective in dissecting the genetic architecture of complex quantitative traits [13, 14]. Recent advances have seen the integration of multiple statistical approaches to enhance the reliability of GWAS findings. For instance, An et al. (2020) employed seven different models combined with whole-genome prediction to identify seven stable genomic regions controlling kernel row number (KRN) across multiple environments, demonstrating the power of multi-locus models in dissecting the genetic architecture of yield components [15]. The power of GWAS can be further amplified by integrating it with complementary omics technologies. Jia et al. (2020) and Wan et al. (2023) both combined GWAS with transcriptomic analysis to pinpoint candidate genes; the former identified Zm00001d047468 as a regulator of grain drying rate, while the latter uncovered 31 genes associated with kernel nutritional quality, including those governing protein and oil content [16, 17]. Beyond these approaches, high-resolution mapping has also proven valuable, enabling the dissection of complex physiological processes such as kernel moisture dynamics [18] and facilitating the discovery of specific functional genes. For instance, a GWAS on 291 inbred lines identified 59 significant SNPs associated with maize yield and yield components, 14 of which overlapped with previously mapped QTL [19]. In a larger-scale analysis, Zhou et al. (2021) employed 1.25 million SNPs to identify 421 significant associations for 40 protein spots, ultimately defining 46 non-redundant protein QTL [20]. Beyond statistical associations, GWAS has also facilitated the cloning of functional genes; for example, Luo et al. (2022) successfully cloned YIGE1, a gene associated with increased ear length and grain yield, demonstrating the power of this approach for gene discovery [21].

Given the close relationship between KR and grain yield, uncovering its genetic basis is critical for maize improvement. In this study, we performed a genome-wide association analysis using 10.77 million high-quality SNPs to investigate KR and its components (EW, CW, KW) in 502 diverse maize inbred lines evaluated across seven environments. The objectives of this study were to: (1) characterize the phenotypic variation and heritability of these traits; (2) identify genomic regions and candidate genes associated with KR; (3) discover favorable haplotypes contributing to higher KR; and (4) investigate the selection signatures of key candidate genes during maize domestication and improvement. Our findings aim to advance the understanding of KR genetics and provide a foundation for breeding high-yielding maize varieties adapted to high-density planting conditions.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials and field design

The association mapping panel consisted of 502 maize inbred lines capturing diverse genetic backgrounds from temperate, subtropical, and tropical. Phenotypic evaluations were conducted across seven provinces in China during 2011 and 2012: Chongqing (11CQ, 12CQ), Yunnan (11YN), Hainan (11HN, 12HN), and Henan (11HB, 12HB). The field trials followed a randomized complete block design with single-replicate plots [22]. Each plot comprised a single 2.5 m row with 0.6 m inter-row spacing, resulting in a planting density of approximately 67,500 plants ha− 1. Standard agronomic practices were applied at each location to minimize environmental heterogeneity. Detailed pedigree and origin information for the panel is provided in Table S1.

Phenotypic evaluation and statistical analysis

Phenotypes were measured on five air-dried ears per plot. KW = EW - CW, and KR = KW/EW. While EW and CW data were obtained from Xiao et al. (2016), KW and KR were calculated for this study [22]. Phenotypic data for EW and CW were obtained from a previous study, while KW and KR were calculated accordingly. Best linear unbiased predictions (BLUPs) for KW and KR across environments were estimated to account for environmental variance and enhance the robustness of subsequent analyses. A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29.0.2.0) to assess the effects of genotype, environment, and their interaction on each trait. BLUP values for KW and KR across environments were estimated using a linear mixed-effects model implemented in the R package lme4 [23]. All four traits (EW, CW, KW, KR), measured in each individual environment along with their corresponding BLUPs, were included in the subsequent genome-wide association analysis. Traits were systematically named by combining the trait abbreviation with the year and location code (e.g., KR_11YN for kernel rate measured in Yunnan in 2011).

Genome-wide association study

Genotyping was performed using a high-quality dataset of 10.77 million single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) aligned to the B73 reference genome (RefGen_v4). These SNPs were obtained from the association panel via whole-genome resequencing at approximately coverage, followed by strict quality control including a minor allele frequency (MAF) cutoff of > 0.05 [27, 28]. GWAS was conducted for all traits using a Mixed Linear Model (MLM) in TASSEL 5.2.8 [29], which incorporates population structure (Q matrix) and kinship (K matrix) to control for spurious associations. The Q matrix was derived using STRUCTURE v2.2 with [30], while the K matrix was calculated according to Loiselle [31]. Considering that our association panel comprises 1.25 million SNPs, many of which are in linkage disequilibrium (LD), a threshold of P ≤ 1/En (En is the effective number of independent markers) was used as the criterion for determining significant marker-trait associations [32]. The genome-wide significance threshold was set at . Significant QTLs were defined as 100-kb genomic regions centered on the peak SNPs (± 50 kb). This window size was selected based on previous studies in maize using the same association panel and SNP dataset [33, 34]. All annotated genes within these intervals were considered candidate genes [35]. Manhattan and quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots were generated using the “CMplot” package in R 4.3 [36].

QTL naming and colocalization analysis

A unified numbering system was applied to name QTL for each trait. All QTL identified for a given trait were collectively sorted: first by chromosome number (1 to 10), and then by the physical position of the peak SNP in ascending order on each chromosome. The resulting ordered list was assigned sequential serial numbers (1, 2, 3…), which were incorporated into the final QTL name following the format “q” + [Trait abbreviation] + “-” + [Serial number]. For example, “qKR-1” represents the first QTL in this genome-wide ordered list for kernel rate.

Two distinct approaches were used to identify co-localized QTLs: (1) comparing QTL for the same trait across different environments to identify stable loci, and (2) comparing QTL for different traits within the same environment to detect potential pleiotropic regions. The QTL was considered colocalized if their 100-kb confidence intervals overlapped or if they shared an identical peak SNP. Genomic regions showing evidence of colocalization were given high priority for subsequent candidate gene investigation.

Candidate gene prediction

To identify potential candidate genes, we first retrieved the genome annotation of the maize B73 RefGen_v4 from MaizeGDB (http://www.maizegdb.org). For each QTL region, a 100-kb genomic interval (50 kb upstream and downstream of the peak SNP) was examined to identify all annotated genes located within these loci. Functional annotation of the identified genes was then performed to elucidate their potential biological roles related to the four studied traits.

To enhance the accuracy of gene prediction, we performed comparative analyses with homologous genes in Arabidopsis thaliana and Oryza sativa. Furthermore, we obtained gene expression data of the B73 inbred line across various developmental stages from the qTeller database (https://qteller.maizegdb.org) [37]. Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) values. We specifically focused on genes identified through GWAS analysis and extracted their FPKM values during kernel development stages. Additionally, expression data from the ZEAMAP database (https://db.cngb.org/zeamap/) were utilized to further prioritize candidate genes based on their expression patterns in tissues of KR and its components. Genes exhibiting the highest expression levels in these specific tissues were considered strong candidates for further analysis as potential regulators of KR and its components in maize.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses

To elucidate the biological functions and metabolic pathways associated with candidate genes, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses by using the OmicShare platform (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/). Significantly enriched terms and pathways were identified using a hypergeometric test corrected for False Discovery Rate (FDR < 0.05). These analyses provided systematic insights into the biological processes and molecular mechanisms regulating KR and its component traits.

Haplotype analysis

For each candidate, all polymorphic sites located within the gene and the 2-kb upstream promoter region were extracted from the 10.77 M SNP dataset. A candidate gene-based association analysis was conducted using a Mixed Linear Model (MLM). SNPs exceeding a significance threshold of were selected as tagging SNPs to construct haplotypes. Haplotype groups represented by fewer than 10 inbred lines were excluded. Phenotypic variation among haplotypes was evaluated using a two-step statistical approach: One-way ANOVA was first applied to assess global differences; upon detecting significance , pairwise comparisons were conducted using Student’s -test. All statistical analyses were performed in R 4.3.1, and phenotypic distributions were visualized using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2.

Selective sweep analysis

To decode the genomic footprints of selection during domestication and modern maize improvement [38], we performed selective sweep analysis usingFstmethod [1] by combining genotype data (SNP panel) of 505 moderninbred lines, Mexicana and Parviglumis were sourced from Chen [39]. Following established classification criteria [40], these lines were divided into three subgroups based on release period: CN1960s & 70s (9 lines), CN1980s & 90s (80 lines), and CN2000s & 10s (4 lines). SNPs within a 10 Mb genomic region (spanning 5 Mb upstream and downstream of each candidate gene) were scanned using a sliding window approach with 500 kb window size and 60 kb step size. Results were visualized using Excel to identify genomic regions exhibiting signatures of selection.

Genome-wide association analysis of KR and its component traits

To select the optimal statistical framework, we evaluated quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots across all environments. The Q and K models exhibited high false detection rate (spurious associations), whereas the mixed linear model (Q + K) effectively corrected for population structure and kinship (Figure S2 and Figure S3). Consequently, the Q + K model was adopted for all subsequent analyses. Using threshold of , we identified 202 significant SNPs associated with the four traits, CW: 83, KW: 55, EW: 47, KR: 17 (Fig. 2 and Figure S4). Based on the LD decay (~ 50 kb), these associations were consolidated into 106 distinct QTL, with individual QTL explaining 5.24% to 12.63% of the phenotypic variation. The distribution of these loci revealed distinct patterns. CW was associated with 33 QTL (involving 83 unique SNPs), predominantly located on chromosome 4, with the peak signal explaining 9.02% of the phenotypic variation. Similarly, EW (31 QTL; 47 SNPs) and KW (32 QTL; 55 SNPs) showed hotspots on chromosome 1 and 10, explained 9.72% and 9.87% of the phenotypic variation, respectively. In contrast, KR was controlled by fewer loci; only 10 QTL was detected (clustered mainly on chromosome 4), with the strongest association explaining 8.93% of the phenotypic variation (Table S3).

Co-localization analysis

Ten of 106 QTL exhibited colocalization across different traits, suggesting the existence of pleiotropic loci or tightly linked gene clusters coordinately regulating yield components. Such as qKW-29/qEW-30, qKW-2/qEW-1, and others detailed in Fig. 3 and Table S3. Focusing on these ten high-priority regions, we screened functional candidates and identified 18 genes (Table S3). Notably, partial annotations of the co-localized genes are associated with grain development. A particularly promising candidate is Zm00001d009538, which encodes a late embryogenesis abundant (LEA) protein. It is speculated to be related to grain development. LEA proteins primarily function to safeguard cellular structures and macromolecules - including enzymes, functional proteins, and membrane integrity—against damage induced by abiotic stresses such as dehydration, drought and extreme temperatures. Other functional categories identified in these colocalized regions included heat shock factors (e.g., HSP40), stress-responsive enzymes (e.g., GST, NCED3/ABA), and transcriptional regulators. These genes likely ensure developmental stability and optimize the seed maturation environment through their roles in stress adaptation, transcriptional control and protein synthesis, suggesting their potential importance in the coordinated regulation of multiple kernel yield-related traits. The co-localization and transcriptome analyses indicated that the majority of the 17 candidate genes (e.g., Zm00001d029272 and Zm00001d002234) were highly expressed during the late stages of seed development, particularly in the embryo, endosperm, and pericarp tissues at various time points after pollination (Figure S5 and Table S4).

Candidate gene analysis

Mining the kb flanking regions of the 106 QTL identified 308 genes, 114 of which were functionally annotated (Table S3). These genes were distributed across traits as follows: CW (40), KW (40), EW (25), and KR (9). Functional enrichment analyses (GO and KEGG) revealed that these genes were significantly enriched in transcriptional regulation, stress responses, and metabolic pathways (Fig. 4a and 4b). Specifically, key molecular function included DNA-binding transcription factor activity and ATP binding, while the nucleus was the predominant cellular component. KEGG pathways further highlighted roles in DNA repair, plant hormone signal transduction, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis. Collectively, these functional enrichments suggest a multi-layered regulatory mechanism: hormone signaling likely coordinates the source-sink transport essential for kernel biomass accumulation, while the specific transcription factors and metabolic genes orchestrate the transition from cell division to the rapid synthesis of storage compounds, ultimately determining the final yield traits. To refine the potential candidate, we implemented a multi-tiered prioritization strategy integrating functional annotation, cross-species homology, and spatiotemporal expression profiling during kernel development. This approach prioritized five high-confidence candidates: Zm00001d033447, Zm00001d042039, Zm00001d049471, Zm00001d052036, and Zm00001d049474. Of particular relevance to KR, two candidates emerged as top targets. Zm00001d042039 (encoding pentatricopeptide repeat protein, PPR) is known to be essential for kernel development (named empty pericarp5, emp5), while Zm00001d049471, encodes meiotic recombination 11b, a DNA repair component. Furthermore, the KR-associated peak SNP chr4.s_128427966 was physically linked to two additional functional genes: Zm00001d050885, implicated in cell wall synthesis, and Zm00001d050886, involved in chloroplast gene expression. However, expression profiling indicated that both genes are minimally expressed in kernels during kernel filling (15–25 DAP) (Table S4). Moreover, their functional annotations implicate them in vegetative rather than reproductive development, offering no clear connection to the assimilate partitioning logic underlying KR. Therefore, we designated Zm00001d042039 and Zm00001d049471 as key candidates, and we named them ZmKR1 and ZmKR2, respectively.

Linkage disequilibrium and haplotype analyses

The peak SNPs exhibiting association with ZmKR1 and ZmKR2 showed localized linkage disequilibrium (Fig. 5a and Fig. 5c). Haplotype analysis of the prioritized candidate genes identified significant phenotypic associations for ZmKR1 and ZmKR2. For ZmKR1, three major haplotypes were identified, Hap1 (126 lines), Hap2 (94 lines), and Hap3 (90 lines) (Table S5). These haplotypes showed significant associations with kernel rate (KR) in the 11HN. Pairwise comparison revealed significant difference between Hap1 and Hap2 (P = 1.34 × 10− 6) and between Hap1 and Hap3 (P = 1.38 × 10− 5), with Hap1 representing the favorable haplotype associated with higher KR (Fig. 5b). For ZmKR2, three haplotypes were identified: Hap1 (384 lines), Hap2 (91 lines), and Hap3 (28 lines) (Table S6). Significant association with KR were observed in the 11YN. Statistical analysis showed significant difference between Hap1 and Hap2 (P = 1.83 × 10− 6) and Hap2 with Hap3 (P = 0.03) (Fig. 5d). These results indicated that Hap1 as elite haplotypes for ZmKR1 and ZmKR2, provides valuable targets for marker-assisted selection in maize breeding program aimed at improving KR.

Selective sweep analysis reveals selection signatures during maize domestication and breeding

Selective sweep analysis using the Fst method identified selection signals near the candidates ZmKR1 and ZmKR2 during maize domestication and breeding (Table S7). We found that these two genes underwent moderate selection during domestication, but showed weak or no selection during modern maize breeding and regional adaptation (temperate Vs. tropical/subtropical). It is highly likely that favorable alleles of these major-effect genes regulating KR were selected and nearly fixed early during maize domestication. As a result, during the relatively short period of modern maize improvement (approximately 60 years), these loci lacked sufficient genetic variation to exhibit detectable selection signals among contemporary subpopulations. Collectively, KR has been improved through maize breeding, the duration of modern maize improvement is brief, merely decades, which is negligible compared to the far longer domestication process. This study highlights that the major-effect genes regulating KR were selected during domestication, underscoring the lasting impact of domestication genes on yield enhancement (Fig. 6).

Discussion

KR represents a critical component of harvest index, determining maize yield potential under high-density [6]. While modern breeding has optimized KR via phenotypic selection, the specific genetic mechanisms driving this assimilate partitioning remain uncharacterized. Here, we present comprehensive genetic analysis of KR, resolving 106 QTL and pinpointing two priority candidate genes validated by haplotype and evolutionary analyses. By elucidating the genetic basis of KR, this study establishes essential genetic targets for precision breeding strategies aimed at breaking current yield barriers.

Model comparison validated the Q + K model’s efficacy in controlling population structure, aligning with best practices for complex trait(s) dissection in maize [41, 42]. More importantly, dissecting KR in the context of its components—EW, CW, and KW—enabled us to uncouple the genetic basis of assimilate partitioning from that of vegetative biomass accumulation. The coexistence of pleiotropic hotspots (e.g., qKW-29/qEW-30) and KR-specific loci demonstrates that KR is shaped by both general developmental regulators and specific allocation factors. This insight offers a precise strategy for molecular breeding: targeting KR-specific loci allows for the improvement of harvest index independent of ear size, optimizing yield potential under high-density planting conditions.

We identified two high-confidence candidates that influence KR through divergent biological pathways. ZmKR1, essential for kernel development [43], showed strong haplotype-phenotype correlations, directly implicating its natural variation in the enhancement of partitioning efficiency. More intriguingly, ZmKR2 highlights a previously underappreciated link between genome maintenance and reproductive success. Functioning within the DNA damage response machinery [44], MRE11B is hypothesized to safeguard the genomic integrity of rapidly dividing endosperm cells. Superior alleles of MRE11B may thus bolster kernel set by buffering against replication stress induced by environmental fluctuations.

We also identified supporting candidates involved in stress adaptation and cellular regulation. For instance, the qKW-2 region harbors a heat stress transcription factor (Zm00001d029270) and a cell-cycle-related isomerase (Zm00001d029272), suggesting mechanisms for thermotolerance and growth regulation [45, 46]. Finally, while GALT1 and MTERF2 were physically linked to the significant SNP chr4.s_128427966, their low transcript abundance in kernels implies they likely contribute to KR indirectly, possibly by modulating source strength (e.g., photosynthesis or cell wall integrity) rather than sink strength.

The estimation that approximately 1,200 genes were involved in maize domestication underscores the complexity of yield trait evolution, making the identification of key selected genes particularly valuable for targeted breeding [47]. Selective sweep analysis using Fst method revealed the evolutionary trajectories for our candidate genes. ZmKR1 and ZmKR2 have not been subject to selection during modern maize improvement and breeding, but they have been selected over the millennia-long domestication process. This evolutionary divergence supports a complementary breeding approach: utilizing ZmKR1 favorable haplotypes for immediate genetic gain while exploring ZmKR2 variation for enhanced yield stability. This evolutionary context, combined with their complementary functions, suggests that the elite haplotypes of both genes could simultaneously capture modern breeding gains and ancient adaptation advantages. Further analysis has proven invaluable for identifying functional variants and elucidating their phenotypic effects [42]. Our results demonstrate that the superior haplotypes of both ZmKR1 and ZmKR2 are significantly associated with higher KR, suggesting that increasing their frequency in breeding materials could enhance KR substantially.

This study establishes a comprehensive genetic framework for understanding and improving KR in maize. We demonstrate that KR is regulated through a coordinated network involving both specialized partitioning genes and general developmental stabilizers. The identification of ZmKR1 and ZmKR2 as complementary regulators, with verified haplotype effects, provide concrete targets for marker-assisted selection. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the candidate genes identified in this study require further functional validation through transgenic approaches. Future efforts will focus on characterizing the molecular mechanisms of ZmKR1 and ZmKR2 and evaluating their elite haplotypes in diverse breeding populations to accelerate KR improvement.

Conclusion

This study establishes a comprehensive framework for the genetic improvement of Kernel Rate (KR), a key determinant of harvest index. We mapped 106 QTLs to reveal that assimilate partitioning is governed by genetic mechanisms distinct from those controlling vegetative biomass. We highlight ZmKR1 and ZmKR2 as critical regulators that modulate KR through developmental coordination and genomic stability, respectively. Crucially, the evolutionary trajectories of these genes suggest they harbor significant natural variation underutilized in elite germplasm—effectively a ‘hidden reservoir’ for further yield gains. Our findings thus provide concrete targets for marker-assisted selection, enabling the design of varieties capable of maximizing reproductive output under the pressures of high-density planting.
