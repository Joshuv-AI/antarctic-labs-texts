Moral distress in emergency nurses: a qualitative systematic review and meta-synthesis protocol
Authors: Yu Zhang, Jiajun Liu, Fengling Dai, Tao Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionMoral distress is a significant challenge in contemporary nursing practice, posing a substantial threat to nurses’ well-being and patient safety. Nurses in the emergency department are considered a high-risk group for experiencing this distress due to their unique working environment. Although numerous qualitative studies have explored this issue, a systematic synthesis of this fragmented evidence is notably absent. This qualitative meta-synthesis aims to integrate existing evidence to construct a comprehensive conceptual framework of the experiences, processes and coping mechanisms related to moral distress among emergency nurses.Methods and analysisThis study will be a qualitative systematic review and meta-synthesis, adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols statement and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology. A systematic search will be conducted in international and Chinese databases, including PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, CNKI, etc. All peer-reviewed qualitative studies exploring the first-person experiences of emergency nurses will be included. Two independent reviewers will perform study selection, data extraction and methodological quality appraisal using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. Data synthesis will employ a theory-integrated meta-aggregation approach, systematically mapping findings onto the Stress, Appraisal, and Coping Theory to construct a nuanced conceptual framework that explains the dynamic process of moral distress. Confidence in the synthesised findings will be assessed using the ConQual approach.Ethics and disseminationAs this study is a secondary analysis of published data, ethical approval is not required. The findings will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presentations at academic conferences.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251041396.Keywords: Nurses, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, Emergency Departments, MEDICAL ETHICS, Nursing research

Introduction

As a core challenge in contemporary nursing practice, moral distress is a global issue that poses a severe threat to nurses, healthcare systems and patient safety.1 2 It was first defined by Jameton in 1984 as the profound psychological distress experienced when an individual knows the right moral action to take but is prevented from carrying it out by internal or external constraints.3 Moral distress is typically rooted in structural and situational ethical conflicts, primarily stemming from the inadequate allocation or misallocation of medical and human resources, tension between institutional policies and clinical ethical judgement, and communication barriers within multidisciplinary teams or with patients and their families.4 5 Far from being a transient emotion, this erosive experience can accumulate and produce the lasting trauma of moral residue, inducing severe professional burnout and turnover intention.6,8 Concurrently, it can be accompanied by other negative states such as compassion fatigue, hypervigilance and impaired analytical ability, which ultimately impact nursing decision-making and quality of care, thereby increasing the risk of adverse events.9,11

As a critical hub in global healthcare systems, the emergency department (ED) is characterised by high intensity, a fast pace, high unpredictability and constant time pressure.12 13 As the first point of contact for critically ill patients, ED nurses are tasked with the initial identification and management of conditions such as major trauma and disaster triage, requiring them to make rapid and optimal clinical judgments and nursing decisions within extremely limited windows of information and time.14 Concurrently, a steady increase in patient volume in the ED leads to overcrowding and prolonged waiting times, often preventing patients from receiving optimal admission and referral due to inequitable resource allocation.15 Faced with a lack of understanding from patients and their families regarding treatment processes and decisions, 79.39% of ED nurses have encountered both physical and non-physical violence,16 17 compelling them to struggle with emotional and ethical conflicts and often preventing them from executing the optimal course of action due to external pressures. This confluence of factors causes ED nurses to face more significant moral distress than their counterparts in other specialties; a 2024 study showed that 71% of ED nurses reported experiencing moral distress frequently,18 which in turn exacerbates nurse turnover.19

To alleviate this situation, a deep understanding of the subjective experiences and coping strategies of ED nurses in moral distress is crucial, serving as a key prerequisite for designing and implementing clinical guidelines, educational programmes and organisational-level ethical support interventions aimed at improving their well-being.20 The core of qualitative research lies in its deep exploration of the phenomena experienced and articulated by participants, which provides a multilayered and profound depiction of the complex landscape of ED nurses’ moral distress, thereby effectively supplementing the findings of quantitative research.21 Early phenomenological and focus group studies have portrayed the burdensome nature of this experience, describing it as a ‘heavy burden’ that nurses must carry,22 and have revealed the profound ‘identity conflict’ and ‘the mechanism of emotional compartmentalisation’ that nurses experience amid the conflict between advocating for patients and adhering to physicians’ orders.23 In recent years, the COVID-19 pandemic has greatly exacerbated this trauma, in some cases leading to an even deeper loss of identity as a nurse and a sense of despair.24 In response to these dilemmas, which often originate from organisational structures and systems, nurses have developed coping strategies such as positive reframing and peer support,25 yet their ultimate workplace engagement remains closely linked to factors such as professional autonomy and personal resilience.26

However, these valuable insights, which respectively focus on core experiences, the impact of major events and coping mechanisms, remain fragmented and lack conceptual integration; a comprehensive meta-synthesis on this topic has yet to be conducted. A meta-synthesis of qualitative findings can integrate the breadth of participant experiences and rich descriptive data to generate profound insights that transcend any single study. Through such a rigorous synthesis, we aim to distill a conceptual framework capable of explaining the dynamic interplay between the experiential process, core conflicts and coping strategies. By moving beyond descriptive themes will provide a theoretical foundation for the design of interventions and significantly deepen the understanding of this phenomenon.27 28

Study aims

Therefore, this study aims to systematically review, integrate and reinterpret the existing qualitative evidence on moral distress in ED nurses through a systematic review and meta-synthesis in order to answer the following core research questions:

Based on the first-hand accounts of ED nurses, what are the core sources and specific contexts of their moral distress, and what is the experience of this moral distress?

By addressing these questions, this study expects to provide comprehensive and experience-based evidence to inform the formulation of supportive policies, the development of interventions and the optimisation of clinical management guidelines, ultimately contributing to the improvement of the professional health and ethical practice environment for ED nurses globally.

Methods

Methodology

This protocol for a qualitative meta-synthesis has been developed in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 statement29 30 (see online supplemental additional file 1), and the 2024 Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guideline for qualitative evidence synthesis.31

This protocol was successfully registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) in April 2025, with the registration number CRD420251041396. The execution period for this study is anticipated to be from 26 February 2026 to 26 April 2026. To ensure full compliance with the prospective principle, all formal literature searching, screening and data extraction will commence only after this protocol has been accepted and published in a peer-reviewed journal. Should any amendments to the protocol be necessary prior to its formal publication, we will submit a modification request via the PROSPERO platform, detailing the changes and their rationale to ensure all revisions are traceable and auditable.

Search strategy

The databases to be searched will include English-language databases: PubMed (MEDLINE), CINAHL, Embase, Web of Science, PsycINFO and Scopus; as well as Chinese-language databases: CNKI, WanFang Data and VIP Database.

This study will follow the three-step search strategy recommended by JBI.32 The first step will involve a preliminary, limited search of the PubMed and CINAHL databases, followed by an analysis of text words contained in the titles and abstracts and the subject headings used in relevant articles (eg, MeSH terms, CINAHL Headings). For the second step, a comprehensive formal search will be conducted across all included databases using all keywords and subject headings identified from step 1. In the third step, the reference lists of all finally included articles, covering publications from database inception to our final search date, will be manually searched, and citation tracking will be used to supplement and identify any other potentially missed literature. The search strategy will be finalised by the research team in consultation with and with the assistance of a medical librarian. A preliminary exploratory phase will help familiarise the team with the existing literature, which will aid in optimising search parameters, identifying key MeSH terms and keywords, and improving the search strategy (see online supplemental additional file 2). Furthermore, as the phenomenon of moral distress may be described in the literature using confounding terminology, a search strategy limited solely to the term ‘moral distress’ could potentially miss a substantial body of relevant literature.33 34 This protocol must explicitly disclose this challenge, and the research team will integrate studies that, while not explicitly using the term ‘moral distress’, contain descriptions consistent with Jameton’s classic definition.3

Type of phenomena of interest

This meta-synthesis will focus on the first-hand experiences of moral distress among ED nurses, including the value conflicts they face, their psychological conflicts and their subjective perceptions and meaning-making throughout this process.

Type of outcomes studied

This study will comprehensively analyse various elements related to moral distress in ED nurses. This includes its triggers and typical contexts, as well as the range of outcomes resulting from these experiences, such as psychological and physiological responses and behavioural coping strategies.

Screening procedure

The research team comprises four members (R1, R2, R3 and R4) who have undergone systematic training in systematic review methodology. Initially, one reviewer (R1) will conduct the literature search across all databases. All retrieved citations will be imported into EndNote X9 (V.19.3.3) for duplicate removal, after which the citation details will be imported into the JBI SUMARI system36 (JBI SUMARI, Adelaide, Australia: JBI) for unified management, assessment and review. Prior to formal screening, all team members will independently assess 25 titles and abstracts against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. This calibration exercise aims to ensure consistency in understanding the criteria among the screeners (see online supplemental additional file 3). Once an agreement rate of 90% is achieved, two randomly assigned reviewers will proceed to independently assess 10% of the titles and abstracts.37 38 The formal full-text screening phase will be conducted independently by two reviewers based on the inclusion criteria, with reasons for exclusion meticulously recorded (see online supplemental additional file 4). If potentially eligible studies have missing or unclear information, we will contact the authors via email to request additional information, setting a 3-week response deadline; if no response is received within this period, the study will be excluded. Any disagreements arising at both stages of screening will be resolved through discussion; if consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will act as an arbitrator. This assessment will determine if each study meets all predefined criteria, including (1) study design, (2) participant characteristics, (3) the phenomenon explored and (4) the outcomes assessed. The selection process will be described in a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram (see figure 1), and all reasons for exclusion will be recorded and reported in an appendix.

Quality appraisal

The review team will conduct a critical appraisal of all included studies. Each article will be independently assessed by two team members (R1 and R2) for methodological quality and risk of bias using the JBI 2020 Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. This process aims to systematically review multiple dimensions of each study, including its philosophical underpinnings, the congruity between its research objectives and methodology, methods of data collection and analysis, interpretation of results, logical coherence and ethical considerations. Any disagreements regarding the appraisal will be resolved by a third reviewer acting as an arbitrator.

Reviewers will use four options for their assessment: ‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘unclear’ or ‘not applicable’ (see online supplemental additional file 5). The ‘unclear’ option will be selected when a study provides information but lacks specific detail. Any disagreements between the initial reviewers (R1 and R2) will first be resolved through discussion; if a consensus cannot be reached, the third and fourth reviewers (R3 and R4) will be consulted to make a final decision.

While there are differing views on whether low-quality studies should be excluded, they can still provide valuable, authentic insights into the phenomenon even if reported inadequately.39 Therefore, all eligible studies will be included regardless of their quality; their quality will be considered during the synthesis process rather than serving as grounds for exclusion. A subsequent sensitivity analysis will be conducted by temporarily removing studies rated as having a ‘high risk of bias’ to assess their potential impact on the final synthesised conclusions.

Data extraction

Data will be extracted independently by two reviewers using a pre-piloted, standardised form (see online supplemental additional file 6). The form’s architecture is structured to build a comprehensive dataset moving from general study descriptors to the specific qualitative findings for synthesis. The process begins with documenting essential study identifiers for tracking and appraisal, such as author, publication year, country of origin and study design. The extraction then proceeds to chart key variables necessary for a multilevel contextual analysis, organised across three distinct levels: (a) the participant, with a specific focus on professional experience. Experience data will be described according to professional seniority and developmental level. Primary classification will be guided by the qualitative stages of Benner’s framework: novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient and expert.40 This determination will be based on an interpretive synthesis of descriptive evidence within the primary studies. Should qualitative data prove insufficient for this classification, reported years of ED experience will be used as a proxy indicator of professional stage. The interpretation will follow the conceptual progression described by Benner (from novice to expert), ensuring flexibility and contextual sensitivity across studies; (b) the practice environment, including the type of ED ranging from academic trauma centres to community hospitals and any reported data on staffing or resource availability; and (c) the individual’s history, encompassing any described prior encounters with ethically challenging events or participation in resilience-building training. The process culminates in the extraction of the core qualitative data for the meta-synthesis, comprising the authors’ interpretive conclusions, all emergent themes and illustrative participant quotes.

Data synthesis

This study will follow the JBI three-step meta-aggregation procedure for data synthesis,41 employing a theory-integrated approach. Initially, the process will be data driven and inductive: two independent reviewers (R1 and R2) will extract all relevant findings from the included studies, which consist of the authors’ interpretive conclusions and supporting participant quotes. These findings will be grouped into categories based on similarity in meaning to ensure the synthesis is thoroughly grounded in the primary data.

Following this initial aggregation, Lazarus and Folkman’s Stress, Appraisal, and Coping Theory42 will be integrated to guide the meta-synthesis process rather than being used solely for post hoc interpretation. The established categories will be systematically mapped onto the core constructs of the theory. Specifically, categories describing situational or organisational pressures will be aligned with environmental demands, those reflecting nurses’ emotional and cognitive evaluations will correspond to primary and secondary appraisal and those concerning behavioural or emotional regulation will be aligned with problem-focused and emotion-focused coping. By systematically and iteratively linking the inductively derived categories with the theoretical constructs, the synthesis will develop an explanatory conceptual framework that elucidates how moral distress is experienced, appraised and coped with by ED nurses.

To capture potential variations within the framework, we will conduct an interpretive analysis focused on participant experience. Where the primary studies provide sufficient detail, we will systematically compare findings from nurses with different levels of professional experience, such as contrasting early-career nurses with more senior counterparts, to explore potential differences in stressors, appraisal and coping strategies across career stages. The extracted data on the practice environment will also serve as a key interpretive layer, contextualising the synthesised findings and explaining variations across different clinical settings. Finally, information on prior experiences and training will be analysed to explore how these factors may influence different forms of resilience and coping within the conceptual framework.

To ensure a systematic and transparent analysis process, this study will use NVivo V.15.2 software to assist with data management. At each stage of data extraction, categorisation and synthesis, the team will resolve disagreements through regular consensus meetings. Concurrently, the research team will maintain a reflexive stance, continually examining the potential influence of their own presuppositions on the interpretation of the data. If interstudy heterogeneity is too high to permit a meaningful thematic aggregation, a narrative synthesis approach will be employed to present the results.

Sensitivity analysis

A post-synthesis sensitivity analysis will be conducted after the initial synthesis is complete to assess the robustness of our findings. Studies rated as having a ‘high risk of bias’ during the quality appraisal stage will be temporarily excluded from the analysis. Subsequently, we will re-examine the main synthesised findings to determine if the removal of these studies significantly alters the conclusions or diminishes the richness of the final conceptual framework. This process will enable us to transparently assess the impact of lower-quality studies on the overall synthesised results and to report on the credibility and stability of our final conclusions.

Confidence in cumulative evidence

This study will use the ConQual approach to assess the confidence of the final synthesised findings.43 This method begins by assigning an initial high level of confidence to all findings, which is then systematically downgraded based on an assessment of the two core elements of dependability and credibility. The assessment of dependability is based on whether the research process meets five key criteria, with the rating being downgraded by one to two levels if an insufficient number of criteria are met. Credibility is assessed based on the goodness of fit between the researcher’s interpretation and the original data; the rating is downgraded by one to four levels depending on whether the individual findings that constitute the synthesised result are ‘unequivocal’, ‘equivocal’ or ‘unsupported’. Following this process, each synthesised finding will ultimately be assigned a final confidence level of high, moderate, low or very low and presented in a summary of findings table.

Patient and public involvement

While this study does not involve direct recruitment of participants, we have engaged with key stakeholders (ED nurses) to ensure the relevance and applicability of our research questions. During the protocol development phase, we conducted informal consultations with a panel of senior ED nurses (n=5). They provided critical feedback on the scope of our research questions and affirmed the importance of synthesising evidence on this topic for both novice and experienced nurses alike. We plan to present our preliminary synthesised findings to this advisory panel to obtain their insights on the interpretation of the themes, ensuring our conclusions remain grounded in real-world clinical practice. The research findings will also be disseminated in formats that are readily comprehensible to ED nurses.

Ethics and dissemination

This study is a secondary analysis of published qualitative data and does not involve the collection of individual private data. However, the ethical approval and informed consent processes of the included primary studies will be verified. This systematic review protocol has been registered with PROSPERO (http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO). The findings of this study will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presentations at academic conferences.

Discussion

This qualitative meta-synthesis aims to systematically integrate the current qualitative evidence on moral distress in ED nurses, thereby constructing a conceptual framework capable of explaining their experiential process, core conflicts and coping mechanisms. At present, despite growing attention to this issue, the fragmented nature of the existing evidence limits a holistic understanding of this complex phenomenon. More importantly, the academic community has explicitly called for a move beyond simple descriptions of moral distress towards developing and validating theoretical or conceptual frameworks that can explain its underlying mechanisms, in order to provide a more solid theoretical foundation for interventions.44 Our study is a direct response to this academic call.

It is noteworthy that existing qualitative evidence syntheses on moral distress in nurses tend to focus on other populations or settings. For example, a scoping review on intensive care unit (ICU) nurses systematically identified four core factors contributing to their distress: ‘a sense of powerlessness in decision-making’, ‘complex end-of-life care situations’, ‘ineffective teamwork’ and the ‘personal characteristics of the nurse’.45 Another meta-synthesis on nursing students at the start of their careers revealed how distress manifests during the initial stages of professional socialisation, with core conflicts stemming from ‘inadequacy and lack of autonomy’, a sense of powerlessness when faced with the ‘unprofessional behaviour of senior’, and ‘medical resource constraints’.46 However, as early research has indicated, moral distress in the ED is more ‘environment-driven’ than ‘event-driven’, rooted in unpredictable workflows, resource limitations and systemic issues, which presents a fundamental difference from other specialties.22 The predicament of ED nurses is unique: it neither resembles the relatively stable critical care patterns of the ICU nor can it be attributed to the inexperience of nursing students. Instead, they are seasoned professionals but frequently face having their professional autonomy stripped away amidst chaos and high pressure. Therefore, a qualitative meta-synthesis specifically targeting moral distress in ED nurses is particularly urgent and important for revealing the full landscape of distress in this unique ‘battlefield’.

We anticipate that the findings of this study will have significant practical implications at multiple levels. First, it will provide robust evidence for clinical managers and policymakers. As noted in a systematic review of interventions for moral distress, although various intervention models currently exist, their study designs often lack scientific rigour, leaving the evidence base for their effectiveness unsubstantiated.20 The review also emphasises that due to the highly complex and individualised nature of the causes and manifestations of moral distress, interventions that can respond to individual needs are particularly important. Therefore, by constructing a comprehensive framework of moral distress in ED nurses, the primary value of this study is its capacity to provide a solid qualitative evidence base for the design of future interventions. These in-depth qualitative findings will help ensure that future intervention tools, such as facilitated discussions or multidisciplinary rounds, can genuinely address the unique ethical root causes faced by ED nurses, rather than merely alleviating superficial psychological distress.

Furthermore, on an individual level, this study will contribute valuable material for nursing education and professional development. Research indicates that enhancing nurses’ moral resilience is a key strategy for mitigating the negative effects of moral distress.47 By deeply analysing the successful coping experiences of nurses, this study will provide experience-based, specific and context-rich content for the development of training programmes aimed at cultivating and enhancing moral resilience. This is of particular importance for reducing the high turnover intention among ED nurses, as a recent integrative review has confirmed that moral distress is one of the most significant predictors of burnout and turnover intention among nurses.19 Ultimately, this can provide them with practical tools to cope with this inherent professional challenge.

A key methodological strength of this research protocol lies in its rigour and theoretical depth. The protocol not only strictly adheres to recognised systematic review standards such as JBI and PRISMA-P but also innovatively plans to use Lazarus and Folkman’s Stress, Appraisal, and Coping Theory as an analytical lens during the data synthesis phase. This theoretical perspective will enable us to systematically examine the dynamic process of moral distress, rather than merely listing a series of static stressors and coping behaviours, thereby facilitating a deeper understanding of how nurses’ subjective appraisals shape their distress experiences and behavioural responses. Methodological discussions in qualitative research also support such theory-driven synthesis as an effective pathway for advancing knowledge and theory-building.48

We also acknowledge that this study has some inherent limitations. First, our search is limited to specific Chinese and international databases, which may lead to the omission of other important studies. Second, the exclusion of grey literature may cause us to miss some valuable data. However, we believe that through a rigorous synthesis of existing high-quality, peer-reviewed literature, this study can still provide the most comprehensive and in-depth evidence base to date for a field that is widely recognised as under-researched. Its findings are expected to guide the direction of future intervention studies and will have significant theoretical and practical implications for improving the professional well-being of ED nurses globally and for maintaining a high-quality, ethical practice environment.
