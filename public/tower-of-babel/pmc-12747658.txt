Uniform and High Li+ Transporting Polymer Electrolytes for Stable and Long‐Cycle‐Life Lithium Metal Batteries: Current Status and Future
Authors: Puji Lestari Handayani, Gihyeon Kim, Ahyeon Cho, Yongha Jeon, Naehoon Kim, Sangwon Kim, Siyeon Ahn, Huiju Jeong, U Hyeok Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolid polymer electrolytes (SPEs)‐based lithium metal batteries (LMBs) are at the forefront of next‐generation energy storage, offering remarkable energy density and safety. Despite their high potential, the practical use and commercialization of LMBs encounter two significant challenges: inadequate interfacial stability and low critical current density (CCD). One promising approach to tackle the problems is by designing an advanced SPE that simultaneously ensures uniform and high lithium‐ion transport. Achieving uniform ion transport is key to minimizing concentration gradients that lead to dendrite formation, while selective lithium‐ion conduction prevents anion accumulation, thereby improving interfacial stability. This review comprehensively examines recent progress in the development of uniform lithium‐ion transporting polymer electrolytes (ULPEs) and high lithium‐ion conducting polymer electrolytes (HLPEs). By combining the structural uniformity of ULPEs with the enhanced conductivity of HLPEs, uniform‐high lithium‐ion transporting polymer electrolytes (UHLPEs) have emerged as a promising class of materials capable of simultaneously ensuring high interfacial stability and supporting elevated CCD in practical LMBs. Key molecular design strategies, along with insights into ionic conductivity, electrochemical performance, and interfacial behavior, are systematically reviewed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the way to achieve high CCD, enhanced safety, and extend cycle life.Keywords: uniform lithium transport, high lithium transport, solid‐state battery, interfacial stability, polymer electrolytes

Introduction

As the global community grapples with the urgent need to mitigate CO2 emissions and reduce carbon footprints, the transition to green energy and the enhancement of energy efficiency are now in the spotlight. This shift presents significant opportunities in the field of next‐generation energy storage research, particularly in the domain of lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs).[ 1 ] Due to their high specific energy, lightweight design, and excellent cycling performance, LIBs have been widely used in various consumer electronics, electric vehicles (EVs), and even aerospace applications.[ 2 , 3 , 4 ] However, the rapidly growing demand for even higher energy density has exposed the limitations of conventional graphite‐based anodes, prompting a shift toward lithium metal anodes (LMAs). With an ultra‐high theoretical specific capacity of 3860 mAh g−1 and the lowest reduction potential of −3.04 V versus standard hydrogen electrodes (SHE), LMAs are widely regarded as the most promising anode material for next‐generation lithium metal batteries (LMBs).[ 5 , 6 ]

To unlock the full potential of LMBs in a safe and reliable manner, however, the electrolyte system must also evolve beyond conventional formulations. Commercial LIBs predominantly utilize conventional liquid electrolytes (LEs), which are generally composed of a combination of organic carbonate solvent and lithium salts. However, a significant drawback associated with these solvents is their intrinsic flammability and volatility, which pose considerable safety risks, particularly under abusive conditions like internal short circuits or overcharging. Additionally, the use of LEs in lithium metal anodes leads to non‐uniform lithium deposition, manifesting as “mossy” or “needle‐like” lithium dendrite. These needle‐like dendrites can penetrate the separator, causing an internal short circuit that leads to cell failure and potentially results in thermal runaway. The growth of dendrites is fundamentally linked to the non‐uniform distribution of Li+ ions at the anode surface, a phenomenon exacerbated by electrolyte diffusion limitation at high current densities.[ 7 ] A short circuit causes a rapid and uncontrolled temperature increase, initiating a chain reaction of heat accumulation and gas release from the decomposing electrolyte. This cascading effect ultimately culminates in combustion or even explosive reactions. Moreover, the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formed in LEs is often unstable and brittle. This instability can lead to the SEI thickening and becoming ineffective, resulting in rapid degradation of battery performance. The continual breakdown and reformation of the SEI consume electrolyte, leading to a loss of active material and ultimately shortening the battery's cycle. These challenges underscore the urgent need for safer and more robust electrolyte systems. Solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs) have garnered significant attention as viable alternatives to LEs, owing to their inherent non‐flammability, mechanical integrity, lightweight, and design flexibility.[ 8 , 9 ] SPEs not only eliminate leakage risks but also offer broad electrochemical windows and compatibility with flexible form factors, making them attractive for integration into advanced battery architectures.[ 10 ]

Recent progress in composite and engineered SPEs has demonstrated tremendous improvements in ionic conductivity, mechanical durability, and electrochemical stability over a wide temperature range.[ 11 , 12 , 13 ] These attributes make them not just theoretical prospects but tangible solutions on the path to commercialization. However, a critical performance bottleneck remains: the low critical current density (CCD), which often limits the performance of all‐solid‐state lithium metal batteries (ASSLMBs). CCD itself is defined as the maximum current density (mA cm−2) that a battery can sustain before lithium dendrite‐induced short circuiting leads to catastrophic cell failure.[ 14 , 15 ] A high CCD is essential for enabling fast charging and achieving the high energy throughput required in real‐world applications such as EVs. Yet, while most laboratory‐scale all‐solid‐state LMBs operate at CCDs of ≈1–2 mA cm− 2, practical systems demand at least 4 mA cm− 2.[ 15 ] This requirement aligns with the guidelines established by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 62660‐2:2018) and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO 12405‐4:2018), which stipulate that electric vehicle batteries must maintain a high‐rate charge/discharge capability, typically defined by CCD exceeding 4 mA cm−2, while remaining operationally stable across a wide temperature range of −20 to 55 °C. In addition, the United States Department of Energy (US DOE) has established a performance target of achieving 80% state of charge (SOC) within 15 min.[ 15 , 16 , 17 ] This aggressive target continues to fuel intensive global research efforts aimed at overcoming the kinetic and interfacial limitations of current battery technologies.

Although recent advancements in SPEs have enabled CCD exceeding > 3 mA cm−2, maintaining such high current densities over extended cycling remains a significant challenge. At high plating/stripping rates, lithium deposition becomes uneven, leading to dendrite generation, unstable SEI formation, and continuous electrolyte consumption. Reinforcement strategies, such as crosslinking or incorporating composite fillers, enhance mechanical strength to block dendrite penetration.[ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ] However, these improvements often come at the cost of reducing segmental mobility and ionic conductivity. Moreover, CCD performance frequently deteriorates in practical full cells with high cathode loading, lean electrolytes, high temperatures, or high voltages. These limitations highlight the inherent trade‐offs among ionic conductivity, interfacial stability, and mechanical stiffness, reflecting the difficulty of simultaneously achieving high CCD and long cycle stability in LMBs.

Beyond CCD, achieving high energy density and extended cycle life hinges on the stability of interfaces at both the anode and cathode. For instance, despite significant progress, ASSLMBs still suffer from high interfacial resistance due to the solid nature of SPEs and limited chemical compatibility, which often result in poor physical contact with rough electrode surfaces, leading to high interfacial resistance. When interfacial stability is compromised, continuous reactions can degrade the electrolyte, resulting in the accumulation of dead lithium, the formation of excessively thick solid electrolyte interphases (SEIs), and increased cell impedance. These degradation pathways not only reduce coulombic efficiency but also accelerate capacity fading. A stable and well‐engineered interface, by contrast, enhances the reversibility of lithium plating/stripping, suppresses side reactions, and ensures consistent ionic transport, thereby promoting a long cycle life.[ 22 ] Even the most advanced polymer electrolytes and electrode materials are rendered ineffective without stable interfaces, highlighting the necessity of concurrent innovations in both electrolyte composition and interface engineering. One promising approach to address both the CCD and interfacial challenges lies in the design of polymer electrolytes that simultaneously ensure uniform and selective lithium‐ion transport (Figure 1 ). Recent cryo‐electron microscopy (EM) studies reveal that solid/vitrified electrolytes form an ultrathin SEI layer ≈10 nm, which is significantly thinner than the SEI layer typically observed in LEs (≈20 nm). This thinner and denser interphase is more effective at suppressing uncontrolled side reactions and mitigating impedance build‐up, thereby preserving interfacial stability and sustaining battery performance.[ 23 ]

Illustration of unlocking high‐performance LMBs: Solid Polymer Electrolyte (SPE) engineered for uniform lithium flux and exceptional interfacial stability paves the way for a wide electrochemical stability window, enhanced lithium‐ion transference number (tLi+), high critical current density, and remarkable long‐term cycling stability in lithium metal batteries (LMBs).

Uniform ion transport minimizes concentration gradients and inhibits localized dendrite nucleation, while selective Li⁺ conduction suppresses anion accumulation and interfacial instability. These design principles form the basis for a new class of materials referred to as Uniform and High Li⁺ Transporting Polymer Electrolytes (UHLPEs), which synergistically combine features from the design to consistently regulate Li+ flux and maximize ionic conductivity and transference number, simultaneously. This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of recent advances in the design and development of UHLPEs for lithium metal batteries. We begin by clarifying the distinctions between Uniform Li⁺‐Transporting Polymer Electrolytes (ULPEs) and High Li⁺‐Conducting Polymer Electrolytes (HLPEs), then explore the synergy achieved in UHLPEs that combine both characteristics. Emphasis is placed on molecular design strategies, ionic conductivity, electrochemical performance, and interfacial behavior. Finally, we outline key future directions for advancing UHLPEs toward commercialization, highlighting their potential to unlock safe, high‐performance lithium metal battery systems for next‐generation energy storage.

Challenges Concerning Conventional Polymer Electrolytes (CPEs)

Conventional polymer electrolytes (CPEs), though offering key advantages such as processability and enhanced safety over liquid electrolytes, face critical challenges that hinder their application in practical lithium metal batteries (LMBs). These challenges can be broadly categorized into three main areas: bulk material limitations, inefficient ion transport, and interfacial issues at the electrode–electrolyte interfaces. In Section 2.1, we discuss the intrinsic issues faced by conventional CPEs in the context of LMBs, including low ionic conductivity, limited lithium‐ion transference number (tLi+), narrow electrochemical stability windows, and insufficient mechanical strength to suppress lithium dendrites. Additionally, severe interfacial incompatibility with both the lithium metal anode (LMA) and high‐voltage cathodes further degrades performance, leading to capacity fading and reduced cycling stability. Section 2.2 highlights the fundamental differences between dual‐ion polymer electrolytes (DIPEs) and single‐ion polymer electrolytes (SIPEs). DIPEs, which rely on the transport of both Li⁺ cations and their corresponding anions, suffer from concentration polarization and dendrite‐inducing space charge effects due to the dominance of anion mobility. In contrast, SIPEs aim to immobilize the anion to enhance Li⁺ selectivity and mitigate dendrite formation. However, despite their promise, SIPEs present trade‐offs such as reduced overall ionic conductivity and remain limited by the intrinsic sluggishness of lithium‐ion transport in polymer matrices. Through this section, we aim to identify the root causes of performance degradation in CPEs and establish a foundational understanding for the design of next‐generation systems with improved ionic transport, interfacial stability, and mechanical robustness, topics that will be further explored in the following sections.

Ion Transport and Interfacial Limitations in CPEs for LMBs

The development of polymer electrolytes began with the pioneering work of Armand, who demonstrated the viability of polyethylene oxide (PEO) mixed with lithium salts as a solid polymer electrolyte (SPE) for lithium‐ion batteries.[ 24 ] This innovative approach paved the way for advancements in ASSLMBs.[ 25 ] Despite extensive research into alternative polymer matrices for SPEs, including polyacrylonitrile (PAN), poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate (PEGMEMA), poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF), and poly(vinylene carbonate) (PVC), these polymer‐based CPEs still face several persistent challenges hindering their commercialization (Table 1 ).[ 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 ] As shown in Figure 2 , one of the most critical issues is their low ionic conductivity relative to liquid electrolytes (≈10−3 S cm−1), fundamentally constraining their rate performance and energy output. The inherently low bulk ionic conductivity of CPEs primarily arises from the semi‐crystalline nature of the polymer matrices. Crystalline domains act as rigid barriers that restrict polymer chain mobility, thereby impeding the segmental motion required for efficient Li+ transport. In PEO‐based CPEs, ionic conduction is predominantly governed by the flexibility and segmental dynamics of the ethylene oxide (EO) chains, which enable the coordination and migration of Li+ ions within the amorphous regions.

In addition to the intrinsic limitations of bulk CPEs, interfacial challenges between CPEs and electrode materials also play a crucial role in limiting the performance and longevity of LMBs. These interfacial issues manifest on both the LMA side and on the cathode side. On the LMA side, issues such as poor solid–solid contact, volume fluctuation, and limited chemical compatibility lead to high interfacial resistance and non‐uniform lithium deposition, which promotes dendrite growth. The insufficient chemical stability of polymer electrolytes further triggers parasitic reactions with the lithium anode, increasing interfacial resistance and dendrite formation. Furthermore, the inherently low shear modulus of CPEs, typically well below the critical threshold (1 – 10 GPa) needed to mechanically suppress dendrite growth, fails to effectively constrain dendrite propagation. This inadequacy leads to the formation of electrically isolated dead lithium and promotes continuous electrolyte decomposition.[ 35 ] Conversely, at the cathode side, insufficient interfacial contact and oxidative degradation of CPE hinder ion transport, reduce active material utilization, and restrict the integration of high‐voltage cathodes.[ 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 ] Above 4 V, PEO‐based electrolytes are prone to electrochemical oxidation, releasing gases (e.g., H2, CH4, CO, etc.) through dehydrogenation of the polymer. This process generates strong acidic species, which react with lithium metal to evolve hydrogen gas and accelerate polymer breakdown. Furthermore, the cathode volume shrinks at high states of charge, inducing interfacial delamination from the PEO electrolyte, intensifying electrochemical polarization, and facilitating lithium salt decomposition within the system. Collectively, these bulk and interfacial limitations contribute to rapid capacity degradation, poor cycling stability, and hinder the commercial viability of CPEs in next‐generation LMBs.

Ion Transport Engineering in CPEs: Dual‐Ion Versus Single‐Ion Polymer Electrolytes

Most CPEs are dual‐ion polymer electrolytes (DIPEs), which are typically prepared by dissolving lithium salts in a polymer matrix. In these systems, both lithium cations and salt‐derived anions contribute to ionic conductivity. However, due to the strong ion–dipole interactions between Li+ and the polar functional groups of the polymer chains, the mobility of Li+ is significantly hindered compared to that of the accompanying anions. As a result, the tLi+ in DIPEs is typically low (< 0.4), highlighting that a large fraction of the ionic current is carried by anions rather than lithium ions. This anion‐dominated transport leads to the formation of concentration gradients during cycling. As lithium cations and anions migrate in opposite directions, ions accumulate near the electrode/electrolyte interfaces, causing concentration polarization. This effect not only restricts the operational voltage window but also promotes interfacial instability and degradation over repeated cycles.[ 40 ]

Concentration polarization is also a key factor in the formation of lithium dendrites in DIPE‐based systems. According to Chazalviel's model (Figure 3a), ramified metallic electrodeposits from diluted salts arise from cation depletion near the electrode surface during electrochemical reduction. This depletion, occurring when the rate of ion consumption at the electrode exceeds their supply by diffusion, causes the formation of a space charge region. Once this localized electric field at the interface surpasses a critical threshold (after τ), ion migration becomes the dominant transport mechanism within the space–charge region. Lithium ions begin to deposit non‐uniformly, particularly at protruding tips of the growing metal structures, which then accelerates the deposition rate. This interplay between the electric field and deposition dynamics fosters the rapid growth of the metal deposit in the space charge region, leading to intricate ramified structures.[ 42 ] Furthermore, the Chazalviel model described the evolution of ionic concentration profiles near electrode surfaces, showing that the dendrite growth velocity (vd ) is directly proportional to the electric field (E 0) and the anion mobility (µ a ), as expressed by the equation of vd = −µ a E 0. This relationship highlights that dendritic growth is governed by electric field‐driven anion migration, where the mobility of anions plays a critical role in determining the degree of ion depletion and the onset of inhomogeneous lithium deposition (Figure 3b). Moreover, Brissot and colleagues expand this model to explain dendritic growth in two distinct regimes: at low current densities, deposition inhomogeneity is primarily determined by surface morphology and kinetics, whereas at high current densities, dendrite formation is initiated by ion depletion and polarization effects. Their experiment with PEO‐based electrolytes found that, at low current density, a needle‐like type of dendrite gradually grew at the tip as the electrodeposition time increased, before eventually piercing the electrolyte. In contrast, at high current density, the dendrites exhibited a tree‐like morphology, which is highly non‐uniform with extended growth due to the concentration gradient near the electrode (Figure 3c).[ 43 ] These findings highlight the importance of suppressing anion mobility to promote uniform lithium deposition and mitigate dendritic growth.

To address these issues, single‐ion polymer electrolytes (SIPEs) have emerged as a promising alternative. In conventional SIPEs, the anions are chemically tethered to the polymer backbone, allowing only lithium ions to move freely, thereby increasing tLi+ toward unity. By minimizing concentration polarization, SIPEs promote uniform lithium deposition and suppress dendrite growth, which are crucial factors for achieving long cycle life and supporting fast charging applications (Figure 3d).[ 44 ] Consequently, this design also effectively prevents dendrite growth, thereby extending the battery's lifespan. However, despite their potential, conventional SIPEs, particularly those incorporating carboxylate (─COO−) anion groups, exhibit low room‐temperature ionic conductivity (typically below 10−5 S cm−1), due to strong ionic interactions with Li+. These interactions raise the glass transition temperature (Tg ), thereby hindering polymer segmental motion and ion transport.[ 46 ] Furthermore, the anodic stability of the carboxylate‐based SIPEs in the presence of 4 V‐class cathode materials raises significant concerns for their practical implementation in high‐voltage LMBs.[ 47 , 48 ] As an alternative, sulfonate (─SO3 −) anion groups have been widely investigated as tethered anions in conventional SIPEs. Due to their lower binding energy with Li+, sulfonate‐based SIPEs generally offer higher ionic conductivity compared to their carboxylate counterparts.[ 49 , 50 ] However, this conductivity still falls short for high‐rate battery applications. Moreover, their poor mechanical integrity prevents the formation of self‐standing membranes, hindering comprehensive electrochemical evaluation under practical cell configurations.[ 48 ] A further critical drawback is their limited interfacial compatibility with lithium metal anodes; in particular, sulfonate‐based SIPEs struggle to form robust, inorganic‐rich SEI layers, which are vital for suppressing dendrite growth and achieving long‐term cycling stability.[ 51 ]

Recent advances in SIPEs have underscored the importance of sulfonylimide‐based anion groups (─SO2N(−)SO2─) in increasing ionic conductivity. Their large conjugated structure effectively delocalizes the negative charge, consequently weakening its electrostatic interaction with Li+ and improving ion mobility.[ 52 ] This inherent property, combined with the plasticizing effect, significantly enhances the ionic conductivity of sulfonylimide‐based SIPEs[ 53 ] compared to conventional anions. To further boost interfacial stability and conductivity, molecular engineering of the terminal groups has been explored. For instance, replacing traditional ─CF3 end groups with fluorinated sulfonyl (─FSO2) moieties has shown superior interfacial compatibility with lithium metal, primarily due to the formation of stable interfacial chemistries enabled by fluorine‐containing species.[ 54 , 55 ] More recently, cyano (─CN) and borate (─B─) functional groups have gained attention as alternative end groups, aiming to enhance the electrochemical window and Li⁺ transference number while minimizing parasitic side reactions.[ 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 ] A comparison of representative anionic structures and their key features is summarized in Figure 4 .

Nevertheless, several critical challenges remain. A persistent trade‐off exists between ionic conductivity and mechanical robustness. Polymers with high chain mobility may exhibit good conductivity but lack sufficient mechanical integrity, while mechanically strong systems often restrict ion transport. Many SIPEs also suffer from narrow ESWs, limiting their compatibility with high‐voltage cathodes. Furthermore, conventional SIPEs frequently form chemically unstable and mechanically fragile SEI layers that are rich in organic components. These layers are prone to excessive swelling and growth during cycling, resulting in continuous electrolyte consumption and reduced coulombic efficiency. To overcome these multifaceted limitations, unconventional SIPE designs have been proposed. These include hybrid SIPEs formed by grafting anionic monomers onto inorganic nanostructures, which simultaneously provide mechanical reinforcement and ion transport pathways. Another promising approach involves incorporating anion receptors or Lewis acidic sites that selectively suppress anion mobility, thereby further increasing tLi+. Engineering Design of Next‐Generation SPEs

Overcoming the limitations inherent in CPEs requires an integrated engineering strategy that addresses both bulk transport properties and interfacial stability. Recent advances in material chemistry and electrolyte architecture have led to the emergence of next‐generation SPEs, designed with tailored functionalities to meet the stringent requirements of high‐performance LMBs. As shown in Figure 5 , we categorize the design strategies for advanced SPEs into two primary frameworks: Uniform Li+‐Transporting Polymer Electrolytes (ULPEs), which focus on creating spatially homogeneous Li⁺ transport pathways to suppress concentration gradients and enhance interfacial stability, and High Li+‐Conducting Polymer Electrolytes (HLPEs), which aim to maximize the bulk ionic conductivity by enhancing the mobility of Li⁺ while minimizing the role of anion transport. While these two approaches address different aspects of SPE performance, ULPEs targeting ion distribution and deposition uniformity, and HLPEs focusing on rapid ion transport, they are not mutually exclusive. A third emerging category, termed Uniform and High Li+‐Transporting Polymer Electrolytes (UHLPEs), seeks to synergistically integrate both design principles to simultaneously achieve uniform ion flux and high ionic conductivity. The following Sections (3.1 and 3.2) will explore the fundamental concepts, structural design strategies, and representative material systems underpinning ULPEs and HLPEs, respectively, laying the groundwork for the development of UHLPEs in subsequent discussions.

Schematic illustration of three next‐generation SPEs: ULPE, HLPE, and UHLPE, highlighting their distinct lithium‐ion transport characteristics and mechanisms. ULPEs are optimized for uniform Li+ flux and deposition to suppress dendrites and ensure long‐term stability, but often suffer from limited Li+ conductivity. HLPEs aim to maximize Li+ transference number for fast ion transport and high‐rate performance, though they may still face issues with interfacial instability. UHLPEs synergistically integrate both strategies to achieve both uniform Li+ distribution and high mobility, offering the best of both worlds in terms of cycle life and rate performance—albeit with greater design complexity.

Uniform Li+ Transporting Polymer Electrolytes (ULPEs)

Non‐uniform Li+ flux is one of the primary causes of lithium dendrite formation in LMBs, often exacerbated by unstable SEI growth and local volume fluctuations at the LMA. Dendritic propagation not only shortens cycle life and reduces capacity retention but also elevates safety risks by increasing the likelihood of internal short circuits and thermal runaway. Therefore, achieving a spatially uniform distribution of lithium ions is critical to suppressing dendritic growth and enhancing interfacial stability.[ 71 , 72 ] To address these challenges, recent advances in polymer electrolyte design have focused on regulating ion flux distribution at both the molecular and mesoscale levels. Among the most promising strategies are: 1) multidirectional and continuous Li⁺ transport pathway engineering, 2) nano‐to‐macro phase separation for ion‐conduction channel alignment, and 3) zwitterion‐enabled Li⁺ coordination sites for selective transport. These approaches aim to facilitate homogeneous lithium ion movement, suppress local ionic concentration gradients, and promote uniform lithium deposition, thereby enabling stable and long‐lasting LMB operation.

Multidirectional and Continuous Li⁺ Transport Pathway Engineering

Creating a multidirectional and continuous Li+ pathway within the polymer matrix is one of the powerful strategies for creating ion channels to enhance ionic conductivity and regulate Li+ flux. This advanced design strategy employs the strategic construction of 2D or 3D ion channels by incorporating various types of materials, including organic fillers [e.g., covalent organic frameworks (COFs) and metal–organic frameworks (MOFs)], inorganic fillers (e.g., LLZO and LLZTO), as well as silica aerogel (SA) frameworks. Incorporating these fillers into polymer matrices, or alternatively infusing polymer electrolytes into porous SA frameworks, enables the formation of continuous Li+ conduction channels. These 2D/3D pathways effectively decouple ion transport from the segmental motion of the polymer backbone, enhancing ionic conductivity and uniformity of Li+ flux.

Organic frameworks have emerged as highly effective fillers for constructing multidirectional and continuous Li⁺ transport pathways in ULPEs by tuning the interaction of anions via Lewis acid–base interactions. Their ordered porous structures, tunable functionality, and chemical versatility enable both enhanced ionic conductivity and improved interfacial stability. For instance, Shaw et al. incorporated 2D pyrazine‐linked and imine‐linked COFs into PEO polymer matrices to introduce electron‐rich and polar sites that strongly interact with Li+ ions, thus promoting efficient Li+ migration through the polymer matrix.[ 73 ] Building upon this, Shi and colleagues functionalized MOF with vinyl groups to create 3D crosslinking with poly(ethylene glycol)diacrylate (PEGDA) and pentaerythritol tetraacrylate (PETEA). This crosslinked framework enhanced mechanical integrity while the Lewis acidic MOF surfaces suppressed PEO crystallinity and electrostatically absorbed TFSI− anions, promoting uniform lithium flux distribution in SPE.[ 74 ] The incorporation of 3D crosslinked networks with vinyl‐functionalized zeolitic imidazolate framework‐8 (ZIF‐8) resulted in a mechanically robust ULPE, exhibiting a tensile strength of 1.85 MPa and a high room‐temperature ionic conductivity of 6.52 × 10−4 S cm−1, while maintaining stable cycling performance for over 2000 h at 0.1 mA cm−2 (Figure 6a).

Alongside the progress in organic filler frameworks, inorganic fillers have also shown considerable potential in tackling challenges related to Li+ transport processes. Inorganic fillers, particularly garnet‐type ceramics such as Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO) and its doped variants (e.g., LLZTO), have been widely explored as efficient Li+‐conductive frameworks in SPEs and gel polymer electrolytes (GPEs). Their high room‐temperature ionic conductivity and rigid structure make them well‐suited for constructing 3D continuous ion‐conduction pathways. For example, Tu and co‐workers integrated LLZO nanowires (LLZO‐w) into PVDF‐based GPEs through a functional superposition strategy.[ 75 ] The addition of butyl acrylate and PEGDA yielded an elastic network that enabled uniform dispersion of LLZO‐w, forming an interwoven 3D conductive architecture that greatly enhanced Li+ mobility across the electrolyte (Figure 6b). Expanding on structural design, Zeng et al. developed a micropatterned 3D composite polymer electrolyte via direct 3D printing technology, generating vertically aligned Li+ transport channels. This tailored architecture significantly reduced ionic resistance and polarization during repeated cycling processes, demonstrating the potential of additive manufacturing for electrolyte design (Figure 6c).[ 76 ] Additionally, Mai and co‐workers developed an in situ polymerization approach to coat garnet‐type LLZTO fillers with a conformal polymer layer (Figure 6d).[ 77 ] This engineered interface effectively mitigated the formation of interfacial space charge layers and reduced electron localization, thereby improving Li+ transport continuity and compatibility at the filler‐polymer boundary

Silica‐based frameworks, particularly silica aerogels, have also gained attention as promising inorganic scaffolds for constructing continuous Li⁺ transport pathways while enhancing mechanical rigidity. Owing to their ultrahigh porosity, large surface area, and low density, silica aerogels can serve as effective hosts for polymer electrolytes, enabling well‐dispersed ion channels that suppress dendritic growth and improve electrochemical stability. For instance, one notable approach utilized a mechanically reinforced silica aerogel network combined with PEO‐based GPEs to construct a lightweight yet robust skeleton for ion conduction. The highly porous structure of the aerogel permitted substantial infiltration of polymer electrolyte, facilitating enhanced Li⁺ transport throughout the composite and improving ion distribution homogeneity at the Li interface (Figure 6e).[ 78 ]

Taken together, these studies reveal that the superior performance of framework‐based SPEs is coming from the synergistic effect between ionic transport regulation and structural reinforcement. Organic framework, which is able to tune Li+ ‐anion interactions and create directional conduction pathways, while inorganic fillers such as garnet or silica aerogel enhance modulus and mitigate stress at the Li interface. 3D crosslinked design formed by MOFs or LLZO enables continuous Li+ highways, eliminates space–charge build up, and maintains uniform flux. In essence, frameworks act dually as ion‐channel engineers and mechanical stabilizers, paving the way for ULPEs with high ionic conductivity, dendrite resistance, and long cycle stability.

Nano‐to‐Macro Phase Separation for Ion‐Conduction Channel Alignment

Tailoring phase‐separated morphologies across multiple length scales, from the nano‐ to macroscopic‐domains, is a powerful strategy to simultaneously achieve uniform ion transport and mechanical stability in SPEs. Unlike traditional chemically modified polymer networks, phase separation enables the formation of dual‐functional architectures in which soft ion‐conductive and rigid elastomeric domains coexist in a continuous, interconnected framework.[ 79 , 80 , 81 ]

Among various phase separation strategies, polymerization‐induced phase separation (PIPS) has emerged as one of the most versatile and effective. It enables the spontaneous formation of bicontinuous structures during the curing process by adjusting the miscibility between ionic components and polymer precursors. These bicontinuous networks allow for continuous Li⁺ conduction while reinforcing mechanical resilience.[ 82 ] For example, White et al. demonstrated that increasing LiTFSI concentration delays phase separation, affecting domain growth and pore formation due to enhanced monomer–polymer solubility, ultimately influencing the ionic network connectivity (Figure 7a).[ 83 ] Building upon this, Kim and co‐authors developed a plastic‐crystal‐embedded elastomer electrolyte (PCEE) via PIPS. Figure 7b illustrates the mechanism of the formation of bicontinuous PCEE using butyl acrylate (BA) as the elastomer phase and succinonitrile (SN) plastic crystal as the ion‐conducting phase. By fine‐tuning the BA:SN ratio, a bicontinuous 3D morphology was achieved. This precise control over morphology influences the domain size and connectivity of each phase, which in turn significantly impacts the Li+ transport properties within the ion‐conducting phase. The resulting PCEE with a bicontinuous 3D network structure exhibited an impressive ionic conductivity of over 1 mS cm−1 at 20 °C.[ 84 ] To further enhance interfacial stability and salt confinement, Kim et.al ingeniously introduced hydrophobic fluorinated monomers, which allow for the synthesis of poly(trifluoroethyl acrylate). This immiscibility‐driven design led to the preferential localization of Li salts within the continuous, interconnected SN‐rich phase, thereby enhancing electrochemical performance and promoting the formation of LiF‐rich SEI layers (Figure 7c).[ 85 ] For subzero temperature applications, the same group developed a fluorinated version of PCEE (F‐PCEE) by incorporating a high content of fluorinated moieties using 2,2,3,4,4,4‐hexafluorobutyl acrylate (HFBA) as the elastomer matrix (Figure 7d). In this system, Li+ ions are primarily confined within the SN‐rich phase, which significantly enhances both ionic conductivity and mechanical robustness. The inclusion of fluorinated segments facilitates the formation of a LiF‐rich SEI layer, contributing to excellent cell stability under subzero conditions. Moreover, the conformational dynamics of the SN component, particularly the trans‐gauche isomerism, enable efficient Li+ hopping via ligand exchange mechanisms, resulting in a high tLi+. An optimized SN content (fSN = 0.54) supports the formation of an ideal bicontinuous network (tortuosity, τ = 1.5–3.0) with superior mechanical strength (≈250% elongation), providing a balanced platform for high‐performance solid‐state operation.[ 71 ]

As an alternative to conventional PIPS systems, Li and co‐authors introduced the fragment‐separated epoxy polymer electrolytes (FSEEs) strategy to enhance the uniform distribution of Li+ and stabilize LMBs during the plating/stripping process. Their approach involves incorporating rigid epoxy‐based segments into the PEO polymer matrix, effectively disrupting its crystallinity and promoting segmental motion of polymer chains. This structural modification increases the inter‐chain spacing and facilitates enhanced Li⁺ diffusion via improved polymer chain relaxation dynamics. As a result, the electrolyte exhibited a notable increase in CCD, from 0.4 to 0.8 mAh cm− 2 at 45 °C, demonstrating improved uniformity in lithium plating and stripping behavior (Figure 7e).[ 86 ] Both PIPS and FSEE exemplify how phase separation, driven by thermodynamic immiscibility or structural segmentation, can yield bicontinuous or microphase‐separated morphologies that optimize ion conduction pathways. These engineered morphologies not only support uniform Li⁺ flux and enhanced mechanical durability but also lay the groundwork for scalable, flexible, and high‐performance SPE platforms suitable for next‐generation LMBs.

In essence, the success of SPE phase‐separation lies in its ability to decouple ion transport from mechanical reinforcement. The bicontinuous network establishes percolating Li+ highways within soft domains, while the rigid phase acts as mechanical anchors that suppress volume change and dendrite penetration. Crucially, the domain connectivity and tortuosity dictate Li+ hopping dynamics and salt solvation, which are directly related to cycling stability. This structure–property correlation highlights that the rational control of phase separation design is emerging as a unifying design for UPLEs, bridging ionic conductivity, dendrite suppression, and practical durability in LMBs.

Zwitterion‐Enabled Li+ Coordination Sites for Selective Transport

Zwitterions, featuring both positively and negatively charged functional groups within a single molecular structure, offer distinct advantages in SPE design.[ 87 ] Their high dielectric constants, intrinsic ion pair dissociation ability, and anionic mobility suppression make them ideal candidates for enhancing Li⁺ selectivity and transport efficiency. By tailoring zwitterionic structure and network architecture, researchers have achieved improved ionic conductivity, elevated tLi+, and stable interfacial behavior in LMBs.[ 88 ]

To exploit these advantages, molecular‐level design of zwitterionic frameworks has become a key strategy. For instance, Fu et al. incorporated polyhedral oligomeric silsesquioxane (POSS) into polymeric zwitterionic ionic liquids (PZILs), along with 1‐(3‐(N‐(perfluoroalkylsulfonyl) aminosulfonyl) propyl)‐1‐vinylimidazolium (VICF), to effectively suppress TFSI− mobility via ion‐dipole interactions and steric confinement (Figure 8a). The dipole–dipole physical crosslinking between zwitterionic moieties also contributed to enhanced mechanical stability of the electrolyte.[ 89 ] Moreover, the introduction of VICF reduces the activation energy Ea for ion conduction, thereby enhancing the ionic conductivity.

Beyond molecular design, network‐level structuring has further improved performance. Lai and colleagues introduced a flexible polymeric mesh architecture, inspired by a fishing net motif, using 2‐(2‐ethoxyethoxy)ethyl acrylate (EOEOEA) as a flexible crosslinker. This approach alleviated the rigidity of polyzwitterions and, combined with carboxybetaine methacrylate (CBMA) zwitterions, enabled effective Li⁺ flux regulation and suppression of TFSI− transport, achieving cycling stability >5500 h at 0.1 mA cm− 2 (Figure 8b).[ 90 ] In another approach, Yin et al. developed a zwitterionic copolymer matrix using trimethylolpropane ethoxylate triacrylate (ETPTA) for mechanical integrity and incorporated methacryloyloxyethylphosphorylcholine (MPC) for ion selectivity (Figure 8c).[ 91 ] The phosphorylcholine group, featuring a phosphate‐based anion, served as a low‐binding‐energy Li⁺ coordination site, while quaternary ammonium groups restricted TFSI− mobility through Coulombic interaction, enhancing both tLi+ and interfacial compatibility that leads to a high discharge capacity of 160.2 mAh g−1 and remarkable capacity retention reaches 96%.

A significant breakthrough was achieved by Du et al., who reported zwitterionic covalent organic framework nanosheets (ziCOFNs) designed to precisely modulate Li⁺ transport and interfacial charge dynamics.[ 92 ] These 2D frameworks possess negatively charged side chains and zwitterionic micropores, which provide distinct Li⁺ migration pathways and facilitate efficient desolvation. Acting as ion regulators, ziCOFNs promote uniform interfacial charge distribution during electrodeposition, thus effectively suppressing Li dendrite formation. The implementation of ziCOFNs enabled an LFP full cell to retain 84.6% capacity after 700 cycles at 2C, while the symmetric cell demonstrated outstanding stability for over 6500 h at 3 mA cm− 2 (Figure 8d).

Zwitterion‐enabled electrolytes leverage molecular polarity, ion selectivity, and interfacial compatibility to create highly efficient and stable ion transport environments. Through rational design, ranging from molecular architecture to mesoscopic confinement frameworks, these systems offer a promising route to achieving uniform Li+ flux, suppressed dendrite growth, and long‐term cycling stability in solid‐state lithium metal batteries. Ultimately, the succession of zwitterion‐based electrolytes lies in their multi‐functional design. Their inherent ability to create an ion‐dipole superhighway for Li+ transport. The internal electric fields produced by the zwitterionic groups act as mobile lithium ion acceptors, actively guiding the cations along the polymer network while simultaneously repelling the counter‐anions. This dual‐action mechanism is what enables the simultaneous achievement of high ionic conductivity and high transference numbers, a critical trade‐off that has long plagued CPEs.

High Li+ Conducting Polymer Electrolytes (HLPEs)

While achieving uniform lithium‐ion transport is essential for suppressing interfacial instability and dendritic growth, ensuring high lithium‐ion conductivity is equally crucial to enable fast‐charging and high‐rate operation in next‐generation solid‐state batteries. However, conventional polymer electrolytes typically suffer from low lithium transference numbers (tLi+ < 0.4) and limited room‐temperature ionic conductivities, which restrict their practical performance and energy output. To overcome these intrinsic transport limitations, recent materials engineering efforts have focused on three strategic directions. 1) Anion‐suppressing single‐ion polymer electrolytes for enhanced Li⁺ selectivity; by immobilizing anionic species on the polymer backbone or within hybrid frameworks, these systems suppress anion mobility, thereby enhancing lithium‐ion selectivity (high tLi+) and mitigating concentration polarization. 2) Polymer‐in‐salt electrolytes (PiSEs); inverting the traditional salt‐in‐polymer ratio, PiSEs utilize ion‐rich compositions to create continuous ionic percolation networks that facilitate superionic conduction, especially at ambient or sub‐ambient temperatures. 3) Ion–dipole coordination engineering for accelerated Li⁺ transport; leveraging strong ion–dipole interactions between lithium ions and tailored polar functional groups, this approach enhances lithium‐ion dissociation and mobility within polymer matrices. The following subsections examine each of these three engineering strategies in detail, highlighting recent advancements, representative materials systems, and their impact on lithium transport efficiency in solid polymer electrolytes.

Anion‐Suppressing Single‐Ion Polymer Electrolytes for Enhanced Li⁺ Selectivity

One of the most direct and effective strategies to enhance lithium‐ion selectivity in polymer electrolytes is the immobilization of anions through covalent tethering, thereby forming single‐ion polymer electrolytes (SIPEs). This approach suppresses anion mobility, increases the lithium transference number (tLi+), and enables more efficient and stable lithium‐ion transport. Three representative anionic frameworks, sulfonylimide‐, boron‐, and emerging nitrile‐based SIPEs, are summarized below.

Sulfonylimide‐type SIPEs are among the most extensively investigated due to their high charge delocalization and low coordination strength to Li⁺, which promote facile lithium dissociation and high tLi+. For instance, taking advantage of the simplicity and efficiency of thiol‐ene click chemistry, Bresser and coworkers prepared a polysiloxane‐based SIPE via facile one‐step thiol‐ene click polymerization using sulfonylimide monomer.[ 93 ] In this approach, poly(mercaptopropyl) methylsiloxane (PMMS) reacts with the vinyl group of lithium 1‐[3‐(methacryloyloxy)‐(propylsulfonyl)‐1‐(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (LiMTFSI), resulting in the covalent immobilization of the anionic species (Figure 9a). The successful immobilization of LiMTFSI resulted in SIPE exhibiting a high lithium‐transport number while maintaining stability during plating/stripping, sustaining operation for over 550 h at a current density of 0.3 mA cm− 2.

In a quest to address the trade‐off between mechanical strength and ionic conductivity, Choi et al. designed a hybrid and composite material by grafting vinyl‐functionalized lithium 1‐[3‐(methacryloyloxy)‐(propylsulfonyl)‐1‐(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (LiMTFSI) onto a silica aerogel framework. This approach leverages the dual functionality of interconnected silica nanoparticles as both mechanical scaffolds and efficient ion‐conduction channels.[ 63 ] This engineering design facilitated fast lithium‐ion migration, resulting in high ionic conductivity and a high tLi+ of 0.84. The resulting SIPE also demonstrated remarkable electrochemical stability across a broad temperature range (−10 – 40 °C), and sustaining lithium plating/stripping for over 800 h at 0.2 mA cm− 2 at room temperature (Figure 9b).

Beyond the sulfonylimide‐based system, researchers have extensively explored alternative chemistries to immobilize anions, with sp3 boron‐based SIPEs being notable for their weak coordination with lithium and ability to reduce anion mobility via Lewis acid–base interactions.[ 59 , 94 , 95 , 96 , 97 ] Wang and co‐workers designed a flexible boron‐based SIPE (B‐SIPE) with intrinsic self‐healing capability, enabled by ester transesterification chemistry to address defects caused by internal dendrite protrusion and external mechanical stress.[ 98 ] This B‐SIPE incorporates a charge‐delocalized sp3 boron‐based anion, lithium bis(5‐aminosalicylic acid) borate (LiB5AB), in combination with EO‐containing segments. The dynamic and reversible boronic ester bonds rapidly reform upon contact at fractured interfaces, facilitating autonomous self‐healing (Figure 9c). As a result, B‐SIPE exhibits outstanding self‐healing performance, achieving high Coulombic efficiency and a remarkable 93.5% capacity retention over 500 cycles, demonstrating long‐term cycling stability.

Nitrile‐functionalized polymer systems are also being explored as a potential anion‐immobilizing platform due to their high polarity, strong dipole–ion interactions, and compatibility with lithium salts. Cyanamide anion‐based SIPEs may offer unique benefits such as enhanced salt dissociation, improved thermal stability, and potential for plastic crystal‐like phase behavior.[ 58 , 99 ] For example, Choi and colleagues designed a class of weak‐binding single‐ion gel polymer electrolytes (WSGPEs) by integrating a weak‐binding ionic monomer of lithium [(cyano)(4‐styrenesulfonyl)imide] (LiCSI) with the zwitterionic monomer 3‐(1‐vinyl‐3‐imidazolio)propanesulfonate (VIPS) (Figure 9d).[ 62 ] The cyanamide anion within LiCSI effectively reduced Li+ cation binding energy, while the substantial dipole moment of VIPS facilitated efficient ion dissociation. Remarkably, the presence of the cyano group in LiCSI enables the WSGPEs to achieve a high ESW of 4.7 V.

In essence, suppressing anion mobility through covalent anchoring and delocalization of charge is an effective route to achieving high lithium‐ion transference numbers and enhanced ionic conductivity. By immobilizing the anion, the SIPEs system eliminates the detrimental effects of concentration polarization and space charge build‐up at the electrode/electrolyte interfaces, which is the primary reason behind dendrite formation and battery degradation. Furthermore, the molecular‐level design of the tethered anions directly influences promoting a uniform ion flux and a more stable inorganic‐rich SEI that can withstand the mechanical stress of the repeated plating and stripping process. Additionally, sulfonylimide‐based systems are well established and continue to evolve through structural hybridization, while boron‐based and emerging nitrile‐based systems provide additional versatility in electrolyte design. Together, these anion‐suppressing strategies form a critical foundation for building next‐generation HLPEs with superior electrochemical performance and cycling durability.

Polymer‐in‐Salt Electrolytes (PiSEs): Ion‐Rich Systems for Superionic Conduction

PiSEs represent a paradigm shift in electrolyte design, reversing the conventional salt‐in‐polymer configuration by incorporating a high content of lithium salt, often exceeding 50 wt% and reaching up to 70–90 wt%, into a polymer matrix that serves primarily as a structural scaffold or plasticizer.[ 100 , 101 , 102 , 103 , 104 ] This ion‐rich architecture decouples Li⁺ transport from polymer chain segmental motion, enabling unique conduction mechanisms through the salt‐rich domains. As a result, PiSEs offer multiple advantages: suppressed polymer crystallinity, weakened Li⁺‐polymer interactions, elevated lithium transference number (tLi+), enhanced ionic conductivity (> 10−3 S cm−1), reduced interfacial resistance, expanded electrochemical stability window, and high thermal stability.[ 103 , 104 ] The accelerated transport of lithium‐ion within PiSE is facilitated by multiple pathways: (pathway 1) Li+ migration along aggregated ion clusters embedded within the polymer matrix, (pathway 2) the formation of [Li(solvent)x]+ cluster with residual solvent molecules, assisting in Li+ conduction, and (pathway 3) continuous conduction pathways formed by coordinated interactions between lithium salts and the polymer backbone (Figure 10 ).[ 105 ] To fully harness these advantages, various research groups have explored diverse strategies, ranging from optimizing salt chemistry to incorporating composites to harness these PiSE benefits.

Multiple Li+ pathways in polymer‐in‐salt electrolytes (PiSEs).

To address inherent challenges in PiSEs regarding crystallinity, Chen and co‐workers utilized a crystallization‐resistant asymmetric salt, lithium (fluorosulfonyl)(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiFTFSI), within a cationic poly(ionic liquid) matrix to prepare a PiSE with 90 mol% Li salt.[ 106 ] The system (type III shown in Figure 11a) demonstrated a transition from decoupled to coupled ion motion, with a low glass transition temperature and suppressed crystallization, thereby enhancing both ionic conductivity and tLi+. Beyond salt design, additive strategies have also proven effective to address the crystallinity issue. Xiong and colleagues introduced heptafluorobutyric anhydride (HFA) as an additive in their PiSE system.[ 28 ] This incorporation produces nanometric aggregates (n‐AGGs), creating a well‐connected porous pathway for rapid Li+ transport. Additionally, the low LUMO energy level of HFA promotes the formation of a stable LiF‐rich SEI layer (Figure 11b). Expanding on strategies that create specific ion transport pathways, Wang and colleagues developed a PVDF‐HFP‐based PiSE incorporating LLZTO as an active filler. Within this PiSE, the residual solvent of DMF plays a crucial role by complexing Li+ to generate Li(DMF)x+ clusters.[ 107 ] These solvated structures facilitate ion conduction by moving along the PVDF‐HFP polymer chains. The introduction of LLZTO creates dual Li+ transport pathways, while the significant amount of Li‐salt addition forms a triple transport pathway. This engineered design material leads to a high CCD of the electrolyte with 3.2 mA cm−2.

Rather than simply creating transport pathways, some researchers focus on optimizing Li+ dissociation and managing ion aggregation. Lin et al. designed a comparable single‐ion polymer‐in‐salt electrolyte (SIPiSE) featuring a perfluorosulfonimide lithium moiety with an alternating sequence, thereby enhancing compatibility with LiTFSI (Figure 11c). Upon reaching threshold concentration, this SIPiSE not only provides a high, uniformly distributed Li+ ions, but also, critically, the predominant ionic species transitions from contact ion pairs (CIPs) to percolating ionic nanoaggregates (p‐AGGs).[ 108 ] This transition was evidenced by Raman spectroscopy, which revealed a shift in the spectral peak associated with the solvation structure of CIPs peaks from 744 cm−1 to a peak at 749 cm−1 that corresponds to AGGs. Furthermore, the anionic configuration of bis(fluoroalkylsulfonyl)imides within SIP and LiTFSI significantly enhances Li+ dissociation (inverse Haven ratio = 0.7) and enables a uniform and dense distribution of p‐AGGs.

Equally critical to high Li+ transport is ensuring robust interfacial stability for extended battery life. Lian and colleagues employed a supramolecular strategy incorporating poly(methyl vinyl ether‐alt‐maleic anhydride) (PME) in conjunction with a dual‐Li salt of lithiated polyformaldehyde (LiPVFM)/LiTFSI, for enhanced interfacial stability and a high tLi+.[ 100 ] This high tLi+ 0.62 arises from three key mechanisms: i) LiPVFM acts as a single anion monomer, immobilized anion, and reduces space charge regions; ii) strong dual‐Li salt interactions suppress TFSI− anion diffusion; and iii) the polarity of the PME restricts anion mobility. Additionally, the presence of carboxylic acid groups enhances interfacial adhesion, contributing to the long‐term cycling stability of over 1000 h at 0.2 mA cm−2 (Figure 11d).

Consequently, PiSEs represent a compelling platform for achieving superionic conduction by inverting the conventional salt‐to‐polymer ratio. Through strategic salt selection, nanostructure engineering, additive incorporation, and composite integration, researchers have effectively reduced crystallinity, enhanced lithium ion dissociation, and facilitated multiple Li⁺ transport pathways. These design innovations collectively enable PiSEs to deliver high ionic conductivity, elevated Li⁺ transference numbers, and superior interfacial stability, making them a promising class of electrolytes for high‐performance LMBs.

Ion–Dipole Coordination Engineering for Accelerated Li⁺ Transport Interaction

Ion–dipole interactions are crucial for optimizing Li+ transport in electrolytes, significantly influencing both lithium salt dissociation and ion mobility within the polymer matrix. These interactions not only facilitate the dissociation of salt but also modify the local chemical environment around lithium salts and polar polymers. This understanding drives the development of highly or locally concentrated electrolytes, which aim to reduce free solvent molecules and mitigate solvent decomposition. By modifying the thermodynamic properties, such as lowering the HOMO energy level of solvents and polymers, these electrolytes enhance oxidative stability and improve compatibility with high‐voltage cathode materials.[ 109 , 110 ]

To harness ion–dipole interactions, researchers have employed various strategies to leverage these interactions. For instance, Lai et al. investigated an organic cation strategy to enhance the properties of perfluoropolymer electrolytes, specifically focusing on strong Li+–solvent interactions to improve Li+ transport and mechanical properties.[ 111 ] Their MD simulations revealed that integrating ionic liquid into the polymer matrix (PBM‐IL) creates significant ion–dipole interactions. These interactions, while constraining ion migration, simultaneously expand polymer chain spacing, ultimately facilitating faster Li+ movement (Figure 12a). They also found that adding lithium salt to PBM‐IL altered the ionic liquid distribution, profoundly affecting ionic mobility and interaction dynamics.

Building on several types of interactions, Choi and colleagues developed advanced multiple‐bridge composite elastomer electrolytes (CEEs). These CEEs combine ion‐rotating dipole interactions, ion‐anchoring dipole interactions, and hydrogen bonding.[ 112 ] Notably, the ion–dipole interactions (Li+ — N≡C‐) and hydrogen bonding interactions (TFSI− — N‐H) increase the dipole moment and lower the binding energy. This effectively mitigates side reactions and diminishes anion concentration polarization (Figure 12b). Moreover, this group also developed a dynamic nanophase using N‐methylacetamide (NMA)‐based deep eutectic solvent (DES).[ 31 ] This novel approach leveraged ion–dipole interactions between the polar group of NMA, lithium acrylate (LiA), and poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate (PEGM) to form polymerizable deep eutectic monomers (PDEMs) (Figure 12c). The resulting nanophase enhances the safety of the electrolyte by preventing leakage and ensuring strong electrode/electrolyte interfacial contact during cycling. The resulting PDEM‐based SIPE exhibits a high tLi+ of 0.86 and a wide ESW of 5.0 V. Building on similar principles of optimizing ion–dipole interactions, Li and coworkers made significant advancements by incorporating adiponitrile (ADN) into the PVDF‐HFP matrix.[ 113 ] As displayed in Figure 12d, the strong binding energy and bond dipole moment of 11.8 × 10−3 Cm of ADN—Li+ facilitate Li+ dissociation and liberate Li+ from polymer chains, enabling the cell to maintain stable operation at a high C‐rate.

Beyond enhancing bulk electrolyte properties, recent research highlights the critical role of optimizing residual solvents through ion–dipole interactions to improve electrode/electrolyte compatibility. Wang et al. exemplified this by employing an ion–dipole interaction to stabilize residual solvent into a thermodynamically and kinetically stable structure. This strategy mitigates electrochemical instabilities and resolves issues related to solvent incompatibility with electrodes (Figure 12e).[ 114 ] Their work demonstrated that the electron‐withdrawing additive LiDFOB enhances the electropositive nature of Li+, boosting its affinity for solvents. This modulation of the local environment promotes competitive coordination of free solvents within an anion‐dominated solvation structure. The resulting PVDF‐LATP‐LiTFSI‐LiDFOB‐LiBF4 (PLLDB) electrolyte enables the cell to operate at a high C‐rate of 5C for over 2000 cycles.

Collectively, ion–dipole coordination engineering provides a powerful strategy to promote salt dissociation, regulate solvation structure, and tailor ion transport dynamics within polymer matrices. Beyond simply promoting dissociation, the core mechanistic advantage of ion–dipole engineering lies in its ability to sculpt the local coordination environment of lithium ions. The ion–dipoles design offers dynamic, weak‐binding sites that act as an ion‐hopping superhighway. These tailored interactions enable Li+ ions to rapidly transfer from one coordination site to another with a lower activation energy. By fine‐tuning local interactions at the molecular level, this approach significantly enhances both bulk ionic conductivity and interfacial compatibility, which are essential for advancing high‐performance lithium metal batteries.

Uniform and High Li+ Transporting Polymer Electrolytes (UHLPEs)

Achieving both uniformity and efficiency in lithium transport is a fundamental requirement for next‐generation polymer electrolytes. While strategies focused solely on either uniform Li⁺ flux or enhanced transport kinetics have shown partial success, integrating spatially homogeneous ion pathways with advanced ion‐conduction mechanisms has emerged as a synergistic and promising direction. Such integration not only ensures consistent Li⁺ flux across the electrode–electrolyte interface but also maximizes ionic conductivity, transference number, and electrochemical stability, key attributes for long‐life, high‐rate lithium‐metal batteries.

One representative example is the lignin‐based SIPE developed by Zhu and co‐workers, which features a 3D nanofiber ionic‐bridge network embedded in an ultrathin (≈13.2 µm) electrolyte matrix (Figure 13a).[ 115 ] This architecture significantly shortens the ion transport distance and promotes uniform Li⁺ deposition through interconnected ionic bridges. As a result, the SIPE demonstrated stable cycling over 6000 h at 0.5 mA cm− 2, while supporting high current densities up to 0.76 mA cm− 2. Expanding on structural innovation, Choi et al. designed a zwitterionic single‐ion conductor integrated with a silica framework (SF‐ZSIC), which simultaneously incorporates a high‐modulus solvating matrix, a polar zwitterionic domain, and a well‐defined ion‐hopping medium (Figure 13b).[ 116 ] The silica skeleton acts as a mechanical barrier (elastic modulus ≈0.9 GPa), effectively suppressing dendrite growth, while the zwitterion‐rich domains ensure uniform Li⁺ flux and enhanced tLi+. This multifunctional SF‐ZSIC achieved a critical current density (CCD) of 2.6 mA cm− 2 and stable Li symmetric cell cycling for over 4000 h at 0.1 mAh cm− 2. In a related effort, Nan and colleagues employed LiMTFSI with vinyl ethylene carbonate (VEC) and succinonitrile (SN) to optimize ion–dipole interactions, specifically targeting C═O···Li+ and C≡N···Li+ coordination (Figure 13c).[ 117 ] The resultant single‐ion polymer conductor (SIPC) exhibited exceptional Li⁺ selectivity (tLi+ = 0.93) and robust cycling stability across a broad temperature range (−20 – 90 °C), attributed to finely tuned polymer–ion interactions. From a molecular design perspective, Wu et al. introduced a hyperbranched supramolecular polyether composite electrolyte (PHMP) with ion‐solvation cages and a semi‐rigid cross‐linked structure (Figure 13d).[ 118 ] The densely branched ether‐oxygen functionalities provide abundant coordination sites and disrupt crystallinity, thereby facilitating rapid ion transport (≈10−4 S cm−1 at room temperature). Simultaneously, the cage‐like solvation environment promotes Li⁺ dissociation and the formation of a LiF‐rich SEI, enabling dendrite‐free lithium plating/stripping for over 1600 h at 1 mAh cm− 2.

The development of UHLPEs combines the strengths of uniform Li⁺ flux regulation with high ionic conductivity by leveraging advanced structural designs (e.g., nanofiber networks, inorganic frameworks, and hyperbranched architectures) and tailored molecular interactions (e.g., zwitterion coordination and ion‐solvation cages). Ultimately, the true innovation of UHLPEs lies in their ability to create a feedback loop between bulk transport and interfacial stability. By fundamentally altering the SEI process through their two synergistic properties, uniform Li+ flux and fast Li+ transport enabled by anion immobilizing, UHLPE facilitates the formation of a multilayered, robust SEI layer. This is particularly important because the formation of a brittle and unstable SEI, particularly one rich in LiF, remains a significant challenge in LMBs. Such brittle layers are prone to cracking due to non‐uniform lithium deposition and large volume changes during cycling, leading to continuous SEI rupture and reformation.

To overcome these limitations and realize stable long‐term cycling, the architecture of UHLPEs is specifically engineered to regulate interfacial reactions while promoting controlled lithium plating behavior. Unlike the CPEs, UHLPEs are engineered to ensure a homogenous distribution of Li+ ions at the electrode surface. The uniform ion flux leads to a smooth and dense lithium deposition, avoiding the formation of “hot spots” that trigger dendrite growth. This uniform plating distributes mechanical stress evenly, enabling the formation of a thin and stable SEI rather than a thick and brittle one. Furthermore, in UHLPEs, particularly in single‐ion conductors, the anion is covalently grafted to the polymer backbone, preventing its migration toward the electrode interface and avoiding reductive decomposition. This structural feature significantly suppresses the excessive formation of LiF and other inorganic‐rich species that typically dominate the SEI in conventional electrolytes. Instead, the resulting SEI is thinner, more compliant, and often exhibits an organic–inorganic hybrid character that better accommodates electrode volume changes without fracturing. Ultimately, the elegant coupling of enhanced bulk transport and optimized interfacial behavior, achieved through the design of UHLPEs, enables high‐rate performance, improved electrochemical stability, and extended cycle life, thereby addressing key limitations of CPEs for future‐generation energy storage.

Strategies for Improving Interfacial Stability and CCD for Practical LMBs with UHLPEs

In SPE‐based LMBs, low ionic conductivity and interfacial instability are inherently interconnected challenges. Insufficient interfacial contact, arising from poor wetting, mechanical mismatch, or chemical incompatibility, results in elevated interfacial resistance, which severely hinders Li⁺ transport and diminishes the effective ionic conductivity. These interfacial deficiencies not only compromise energy efficiency but also accelerate degradation processes, including dendrite growth, parasitic side reactions, and interfacial delamination. Consequently, addressing these issues in isolation is often ineffective; instead, a holistic approach that simultaneously considers both bulk transport and interfacial behavior is essential for practical performance improvements. In the previous section, we discussed the development of ULPEs, HLPEs, and their integrated form, UHLPEs, which combine homogeneous Li⁺ flux with high ion conductivity. However, to realize their full potential in practical devices, UHLPEs must also function as interfacial stabilizers, capable of accommodating mechanical stress, suppressing dendrite growth, and promoting robust and long‐lived interfacial chemistry. This section focuses on how advanced UHLPE materials and processing innovations can be used to simultaneously enhance interfacial stability and critical current density (CCD), two key requirements for fast‐charging, long‐cycling solid‐state batteries. We categorize recent strategies into the following three approaches. 1) Artificial Solid Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) Engineering: Pre‐ or post‐treatment of Li metal surfaces and cathodes with functional coatings or interfacial modifiers to reduce reactivity, improve contact, and stabilize Li plating/stripping. 2) In Situ Polymerization‐Driven Interfacial Integration: Engineering UHLPEs through in situ gelation or polymerization at the electrode interface to form conformal, defect‐free interfacial contact that accommodates volume changes and suppresses void formation. 3) Self‐Healing‐Assisted Mechanical Interface Stabilization: Incorporating dynamic polymer networks into UHLPEs enables autonomous healing of interfacial cracks or defects, thereby enhancing mechanical integrity and sustaining stable long‐term interfacial contact. Together, these interfacial design strategies provide a holistic framework for maximizing the benefits of UHLPEs in realistic battery conditions, offering a path toward safer, more durable, and high‐rate‐compatible lithium metal batteries.

Artificial SEI Engineering

The stability and performance of LMBs are profoundly affected by the properties of the interface between the electrodes and the electrolyte. A key interfacial feature is the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), which forms spontaneously on the lithium anode due to its low electrochemical potential.[ 119 ] This SEI acts as a protective barrier, preventing continuous electrolyte decomposition and regulating Li⁺ transport across the interface. By suppressing parasitic reactions and stabilizing the electrode–electrolyte interface, the SEI plays a vital role in enhancing battery efficiency and longevity. The SEI forms from the electrochemical reduction and decomposition of electrolyte components (such as solvents, salts, and anions) at the surface of the lithium metal anode. This process typically initiates within the Helmholtz double layer, where the electrochemical potential drives the parasitic reaction.[ 120 ] The resulting insoluble decomposition products accumulate on the anode surface, gradually forming the SEI layer. Advanced operando characterization techniques, such as soft X‐ray absorption spectroscopy, have provided crucial insights into the dynamic formation process of the SEI.[ 121 ] These studies reveal a sequential deposition process, where inorganic species form first, followed by the accumulation of organic components. This results in a distinct bilayer structure: the inner region (closer to the electrode) is predominantly rich in inorganic components, such as LiF, while the outer region (facing the electrolyte) typically contains a higher concentration of organic species (Figure 14 ).

Schematic showing the morphology of the multilayer SEI, featuring inorganic‐rich inner layers (LiF, Li2O, Li2CO3, Li3N) and organic‐rich outer layers that accommodate volume changes and promote fast ion transport across the SEI.

Inorganic components within the SEI generally exhibit superior mechanical and chemical properties, such as high shear and Young's modulus, as well as enhanced chemical stability.[ 122 ] Common examples include lithium fluoride (LiF), lithium oxide (Li2O), lithium nitride (Li3N), and lithium carbonate (Li2CO3). Among them, LiF is particularly notable due to its excellent thermodynamic stability with respect to lithium, low solubility, strong passivation abilities, high surface energy, and superior electronic insulation (Table 3 ). These properties synergistically promote uniform Li+ transfer and deposition, which is pivotal for effectively suppressing lithium dendrite growth.[ 122 , 123 , 124 ] Additionally, LiF's wide band gap (8.9 eV) helps electronically isolate the electrolyte from lithium, thereby mitigating continuous electrolyte decomposition and further SEI formation. Li2CO3, Li3N, and Li2O also contribute positively to ion conduction due to their low Li⁺ diffusion barriers, further aiding dendrite suppression. In contrast, organic species (ROLi and ROCO2Li) tend to exhibit poor mechanical and chemical stability.[ 125 ] Studies have reported a negative correlation between the proportion of organic components in the SEI and the reversibility of lithium plating/stripping, as well as the coulombic efficiency.[ 126 ] This is primarily due to their higher solubility in the electrolyte, which leads to continuous SEI dissolution and reformation, consuming additional electrolyte and causing the SEI to thicken progressively over cycling. Furthermore, organic components are prone to excessive swelling, which correlates strongly with lower CE; cells with highly swollen SEI exhibit faster capacity degradation and more pronounced Li+ depletion.[ 23 ] Conversely, SEI with a higher inorganic content shows reduced swelling and enhanced passivation capability. While SEI formation is an inevitable consequence of lithium's high reactivity, the naturally formed (native) SEI often presents significant challenges. In battery research, a clear distinction is frequently drawn between the desired properties of an “ideal SEI” and the shortcomings of the “native SEI”. Ideally, an SEI should possess excellent electronic insulation, superior mechanical properties, high electrochemical stability, uniform and stable thickness, and excellent Li+ conductivity. In practice, however, the native SEI is often chemically heterogeneous, mechanically fragile, uneven, and unstable in the presence of reactive electrolytes.

To circumvent the inherent limitations of native SEI layers, the concept of artificial SEI (a‐SEI) engineering has emerged as a strategic solution. Unlike the spontaneous, in situ formation of native SEI, an a‐SEI is a deliberately constructed interfacial layer formed on the LMA prior to cell assembly. This proactive strategy aims to produce a uniform, ion‐permeable, and mechanically robust interphase that suppresses dendrite growth, mitigates undesirable side reactions, and enables regulated, homogenous Li+ flux. By precisely tuning the composition and morphology of the SEI, artificial layers offer improved structural integrity and enhanced electrochemical stability, thereby extending battery cycle life. For example, Gao and colleagues developed an in situ‐formed Li3PO4/polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) protective layer, designed to accommodate volume changes, inhibit dendrite propagation, and maintain intimate contact with the LMA surface (Figure 15a).[ 127 ] In this configuration, Li3PO4 provides continuous Li⁺ transport pathways, while the flexible PVA matrix enhances mechanical adaptability, collectively contributing to stable interfacial properties and improved cycling performance. Ding and coworkers further advanced this concept by engineering a dynamic supramolecular elastomer (DSE) interface layer using ion–dipole interactions (Figure 15b).[ 128 ] The DSE consists of soft segments that facilitate rapid ion transport and hard segments with lithiophilic sites that promote inorganic‐rich SEI formation. This adaptive polymer network accommodates dynamic volume changes during cycling and supports stable Li deposition, enabling symmetric cell operation for over 6000 h at 1 mAh cm−2. Additionally, Meng et al. developed a single‐ion conducting artificial SEI layer via in situ UV polymerization (Figure 15c).[ 129 ] The resulting network forms a uniform, chemically stable interphase that effectively shields the Li anode from side reactions and reduces electrolyte consumption. This design supports long‐term cycling stability, with the cell maintaining stable operation for over 1200 h at 5 mAh cm− 2. A particularly effective strategy involves forming inorganic‐rich SEI layers, especially those containing LiF, due to LiF's strong lithiophobicity, chemical inertness, and mechanical strength. In a notable example, Qiao et al. fabricated an artificial SEI comprising LiF, Sn, and Sn–Li alloy (Figure 15d).[ 130 ] This hybrid SEI not only suppresses dendrite growth and prevents dead lithium formation but also facilitates uniform ion transport. The inclusion of LiF enables consistent Li⁺ flux and reversible lithium plating beneath the SEI, allowing the cell to cycle stably for more than 2000 h. These advances collectively demonstrate that rationally designed a‐SEIs play a pivotal role in enhancing battery efficiency, lifespan, and safety.

A key advantage of in situ polymerization lies in its adaptability to the dynamic environment of LMBs. Unlike rigid, preformed SPEs that often lose contact during volume fluctuations from lithium plating/stripping, leading to delamination and increased interfacial resistance, an in situ polymerized protective layer can maintain intimate, flexible contact. This continuous interfacial contact not only accommodates volume changes but also facilitates the formation of a more uniform and stable SEI, which is critical for prolonged cycling stability.[ 137 ] Overall, in situ polymerization enables the creation of seamless, low‐resistance interfaces, enhances physical and electrochemical compatibility, and simplifies battery fabrication. These advantages position it as a scalable and practical route toward high‐performance lithium metal batteries.

Self‐Healing‐Assisted Mechanical Interface Stabilization

The understanding of how SPEs suppress dendrites has evolved significantly. While early models suggested that a high shear modulus was necessary to physically block dendrites, more recent research emphasizes that properties such as toughness, ductility, and compliance are equally important. Compliant and plastically deformable interfaces and self‐healing bonding capabilities can significantly improve voltage hysteresis and extend cycle life. This design approach helps maintain a more coherent electrolyte/electrode interface with a more planar electrodeposited lithium morphology. The development of SPE, which possesses self‐healing properties, will bring significant benefits for enhancing the performance of LMBs. This design facilitates high interfacial stability and improved CCD, which consequently leads to prolonged cycle stability in LMBs. One promising strategy involves incorporating active indium fluoride (InF3) additives at the lithium anode side. This active additive can dynamically repair the electrode interface by facilitating the formation of a LiF‐rich SEI and Li–In alloys via an in situ process.[ 138 ] Furthermore, the in situ polymerization of a high dielectric constant poly(vinyl ethylene carbonate) (PVEC) generates high interfacial contact with electrode materials. This process accelerates the formation of LiF and Li–In alloys, further enhancing interfacial stability and critical current density. The second approach is to develop a supramolecular network that leverages quadruple hydrogen bonding from 2‐(3‐(6‐methyl‐4‐oxo‐1,4‐dihydropyrimidin‐2‐yl)ureido)ethyl methacrylate (UPyMA) combined with weak‐binding lithium 4‐styrenesulfonyl(phenylsulfonyl)imide (SSPSILi) to establish a self‐healing single‐ion conducting polymer electrolyte.[ 139 ] The unique quadruple hydrogen bonding within the supramolecular polymer enables self‐healing properties without the need for external stimuli. Meanwhile, the delocalized sulfonylimide anions promote the dissociation of Li+, further enhancing the ionic conductivity, safety, and overall performance of the electrolyte. Lastly, employing non‐covalent interaction of ─CH3 — CF3 within an aprotic ethyl acrylate (EA) and LiMTFSI polymer matrix contributes to self‐healing properties and chemical stability at the lithium metal anode surface.[ 140 ] The exploration of self‐healing polymer electrolytes has garnered significant attention in the scientific community, resulting in a substantial number of scientific reports on this topic (Table 5 ).

Summary and Future Outlook

Summary

The development of SPEs is essential for enabling LMBs that offer both high energy density and intrinsic safety, key requirements for next‐generation energy storage systems. However, the practical implementation of SPE‐based LMBs faces two fundamental and interrelated challenges: i) achieving fast and selective lithium‐ion transport and ii) ensuring stable, dendrite‐free interfaces. Addressing these coupled issues requires the integration of transport‐ and interface‐focused design strategies. In this review, we systematically examined recent advances in the engineering of SPEs, with particular emphasis on their ionic transport characteristics and interfacial behavior. To provide a clear framework, we classified state‐of‐the‐art SPEs into three functional categories based on their design principles and performance objectives:

Uniform Li+ Transporting Polymer Electrolytes (ULPEs), designed to ensure spatially homogeneous Li+ flux. Key approaches include the construction of multidirectional and continuous ion‐conductive pathways (e.g., 2D/3D network alignment), phase‐separated morphology control for anisotropic channel formation, and zwitterionic functionalization for selective coordination and transport. ULPEs typically achieve moderate ionic conductivity in the range of 10−4–10−3 S cm−1 at room temperature, coupled with relatively wide ESW, high mechanical strength, and moderate lithium transference number (≈0.4–0.6).

Future Directions

The future of next‐generation energy storage systems hinges not only on advancements in material chemistry but also on the comprehensive integration of various multifaceted elements. Bridging the gap between laboratory research and real‐world implementation of lithium metal batteries (LMBs) with solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs), particularly uniform and high lithium‐ion transporting polymer electrolytes (UHLPEs), requires progress across multiple fronts, including advanced manufacturing, digital tools, characterization, and scalable design.

Advanced Manufacturing and Form Factor Innovation: To enable the practical adoption of SPE‐based LMBs, future designs must align with scalable and manufacturing‐compatible formats. While conventional battery architectures, such as cylindrical, pouch, and prismatic cells, currently dominate the industry,[ 145 , 146 , 147 ] emerging manufacturing technologies, including 3D and 4D printing, offer transformative opportunities for producing compact, energy‐dense, and application‐specific battery systems.[ 76 , 148 ] These advanced fabrication techniques not only enhance safety and improve energy and power density,[ 149 , 150 ] but also allow unprecedented customization of battery shapes and sizes. Such design flexibility is particularly advantageous for seamless integration into diverse electronic products, optimizing internal space usage and boosting overall device performance. Embracing these innovations is essential for building sustainable, high‐performance energy storage systems and addressing the escalating global demand for clean and adaptable power solutions.

AI‐Driven Design and Simulation: The second aspect is the consideration of the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) in the context of LMBs. These technologies are transforming sustainable practices within the LMB industry by functioning as virtual laboratories. With the use of computational simulations and machine learning, researchers can dramatically minimize the reliance on expensive physical experiments, thereby accelerating the discovery and optimization of SPEs. Advanced methods such as Density Functional Theory (DFT) and Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations provide highly accurate predictions regarding solvation structures, oxidation stability, and reaction pathways.[ 151 , 152 , 153 , 154 ] Additionally, machine learning models are adept at analyzing extensive datasets generated from pilot‐scale and industrial manufacturing processes, unlocking insights that were previously difficult to attain.[ 155 ]

A notable example of ML implementation is electrolyte design, as reported by Zhao and colleagues, who screened a dataset of ≈10 000 organic solvents based on key descriptors such as dielectric constant, dipole moment, and the energy gap between HOMO and LUMO levels. By integrating advanced ML models (XGBoost, SISSO, and CGCNN), the study successfully identified five promising solvents for SPEs. These predictions were experimentally validated through the fabrication of new SPE formulations, which demonstrated a wide ESW (4.5 V), high ionic conductivity, and enhanced interfacial compatibility with lithium metal. The optimized SPEs enabled long‐term cycling stability, achieving 86.7% capacity retention after 500 cycles in Li||LFP cells, and 98.7% retention after 200 cycles in Li||NCM91 cells at a high current rate of 2C.[ 156 ] Building on these data‐driven methodologies, Jiang and co‐workers expanded the ML toolkit by incorporating density functional theory (DFT) and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) to investigate the electrolyte–electrode interface at the atomic scale. Their study focused on 23 representative solvents, analyzing properties such as redox potentials, adsorption energies, and charge transfer behavior on lithium metal surfaces. Employing multiple linear regression (MLR) and deep neural networks (DNN), they mapped the decomposition tendencies of solvent–salt pairs and predicted the resultant SEI composition. This approach offers a mechanistic understanding of how molecular‐level interactions shape interfacial stability, enabling rational design of electrolytes with tailored SEI‐forming behavior.[ 157 ] More recently, Wang and colleagues introduced a comprehensive ML framework that combines unsupervised clustering and supervised learning with DFT calculations and graph convolutional neural networks (GCNNs) to discover new polymer electrolytes with high performance at low temperatures.[ 158 ] Guided by ML‐driven screening, they formulated a novel class of ionic polymer electrolytes (IPEs) by pairing selected ionic liquids with a liquid‐crystalline polyelectrolyte, poly(2,2′‐disulfonyl‐4,4′‐benzidine terephthalamide) (PBDT), and lithium salts. These IPEs maintained mechanical robustness (modulus > 200 MPa) over a wide temperature range from –50 °C to 300 °C, ensuring thermal stability and safety. Notably, the Li|IPE|Li symmetric cells achieved an impressive critical current density (CCD) of 6 mA cm−2 at 80 °C, marking a significant milestone in the development of high‐rate, dendrite‐suppressing polymer electrolytes. This progression exemplifies how ML‐guided design is transforming electrolyte discovery from conventional trial‐and‐error methods to predictive, high‐throughput strategies aimed at overcoming key limitations in lithium metal batteries. This in silico approach not only streamlines research and development but also represents a paradigm shift that is vital for overcoming the remaining challenges associated with SPEs.

In‐Depth Characterization Techniques: The third critical aspect is the advanced characterization of solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs). Techniques such as cryo‐transmission electron microscopy (cryo‐TEM), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), time‐of‐flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF‐SIMS), and X‐ray microscopy provide invaluable insights into the intricate behavior of SPEs.[ 159 , 160 , 161 ] These advanced characterization methods facilitate a deeper understanding of SPE dynamics, enabling the rational design of polymer chemistries aimed at mitigating dendrite formation, enhancing ionic conductivity, and maintaining stable interfaces. Furthermore, these techniques yield direct information that is essential for optimizing scalable manufacturing processes. By adopting such comprehensive and sophisticated characterization strategies, manufacturers can ensure that even small samples of SPE films meet stringent requirements for safety, electrochemical performance, and cost‐effectiveness, criteria that are vital for the successful commercialization of lithium metal batteries (LMBs).

UHLPEs represent a next‐generation SPE platform capable of overcoming the long‐standing barriers of lithium metal batteries. By combining uniform ion flux, high ionic conductivity, and robust interfacial adhesion, UHLPEs pave the way toward safer, high‐energy, and fast‐charging LMBs. To transition from a laboratory to an industry scale, UHLPEs must meet specific quantifiable performance targets. Table 6 outlines the key parameters that must be achieved for scaled‐up practical applications, providing a clear benchmark for future research and development. Moving forward, a synergistic approach that integrates rational molecular design, real‐time diagnostics, AI‐guided discovery, and scalable manufacturing will be essential for unlocking the full potential of UHLPEs in commercial applications. The future of energy storage lies in such cross‐disciplinary convergence, uniting materials science, engineering, and digital innovation.
