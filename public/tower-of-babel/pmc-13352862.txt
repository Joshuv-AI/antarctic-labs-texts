Hydrogel delivery platform of engineered apoptotic vesicles for ischemic stroke therapy
Authors: Dongdong Xiao, Yin Shen, Jinlu Gan, Shuang Li, Xiaobing Jiang, Haijun Wang, Qiangping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemic stroke, induced by the occlusion of cerebral blood vessels, represents a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality. Although thrombolytic therapy and mechanical thrombectomy are cornerstone treatments for restoring cerebral perfusion, their clinical effectiveness is severely constrained by a narrow therapeutic window. This study presents a reactive oxygen species (ROS) - responsive hydrogel platform loaded with engineered apoptotic vesicles (apoVs) designed to simultaneously target multiple post-stroke pathological processes. Proteomic analyses reveal that apoV isolated from apoptotic neural stem cells are enriched in proteins associated with angiogenesis and neuroprotection. In vitro investigations and in vivo animal experiment further demonstrate that the hydrogel can alleviate oxidative stress, promote angiogenesis and protect nerve injury by releasing hydrogen sulfide (H2S) - functionalized apoV in the infarct area. In summary, this hydrogel platform holds considerable therapeutic promise by transforming the hostile post-stroke microenvironment into a regeneration-permissive niche, thereby fostering endogenous tissue repair.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04519-7.Keywords: Stroke, Hydrogel, ApoVs, H2S

Introduction

Stroke refers to an acute focal neurological dysfunction of cerebrovascular etiology, with clinical manifestations including hemiparesis, dysarthria, sensory disorders, aphasia, and visual impairment [1]. It remains the second leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for over 6 million deaths annually and leaving 33 million people living with disability [2, 3]. Notably, while global stroke incidence has risen by 15.03%, a study reports a striking 100.64% increase in China [4]. As a major threat to public health, cerebral ischemic stroke, mainly triggered by thrombosis or embolism, is recognized as the most prevalent and severe form of stroke. Clinical data indicate that its 30-day mortality rate reaches 45%, and over 65% of survivors develop moderate-to-severe disabilities [1].

In ischemic stroke, delayed intervention and suboptimal postoperative outcomes often lead to disability, profoundly impairing patients′ daily functioning and their families′ well-being [5]. Due to the narrow treatment window (within 4.5 h after onset), potential risk of bleeding, and rupture of the blood-brain barrier, the current thrombolytic therapy only benefits about 5% of patients [3, 5, 6]. Meanwhile, the emerging mechanical approaches like endovascular thrombectomy have also faced the possibility of narrow treatment time windows (within 6 h after stroke) and equipment- and surgery-related complications, resulting in only 6% of patients benefiting from this treatment [5]. Consequently, there is an urgent demand for the development of effective therapeutic strategies capable of restoring and regenerating injured cerebral tissues [3].

A comprehensive understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms underlying stroke is crucial for enhancing the precision and efficacy of therapeutic interventions. Specifically, the diminished supply of oxygen and glucose following ischemic stroke triggers the release of excitatory neurotransmitters and the initiation of excitotoxicity. This cascade disrupts neuronal ion homeostasis and induces mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, resulting in excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and other detrimental cellular events [7]. Following ischemic stroke onset, microglia become hyperactivated and undergo immune polarization, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), thereby exacerbating neuronal tissue damage [8]. These pathological reactions trigger a cascading effect, resulting in cerebral tissue damage and even mortality. Conventional therapeutic strategies targeting a single mechanism or signaling pathway have been demonstrated to be inadequate for the comprehensive management of stroke [9]. For example, the clinical translation of neuroprotective agents targeting inflammation, oxidative stress, and excitotoxicity caused by stroke has failed in the past few decades [10]. Given the complex mechanism of the ischemic cascade, this study proposes a multi-targeted therapy strategy based on neural stem cell-derived apoptotic vesicles (apoVs) and hydrogen sulfide for treating ischemic stroke and improving patients’ long-term prognosis.

Apoptosis is a fundamental metabolic process critical for physiological homeostasis, during which membrane blebbing generates apoptotic bodies that form apoVs with defined intracellular cargo distribution [11]. As a distinct subset of extracellular vesicles (EVs), apoVs carry a diverse array of bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids, microRNAs, and DNA [11]. Accumulating evidence indicates that apoVs play critical roles in modulating immune responses and promoting tissue repair and regeneration across multiple organs [11–14]. However, in ischemic stroke therapy, the precise mechanisms by which apoVs promote neurological recovery, angiogenesis, and inflammatory regulation remain incompletely understood. In addition, H2S is an endogenous gasotransmitter that regulates a series of physiological and pathological processes in the brain [15, 16]. In hypoxic/ischemic conditions and neurodegenerative disorders, increasing evidence suggests that H2S alleviate microglial activation, pro-inflammatory cytokine production and oxidative stress response [15, 17–21]. As an organic thiol-dependent H2S-releasing compound, 2-aminopyridine-5-thiocarboxamide (APTC) possesses a relatively conserved release mechanism. Nevertheless, its therapeutic potential is constrained by the poor aqueous solubility, high clearance rates, and inherent toxicity [22]. With the aim of surmounting these limitations, APTC was first conjugated to the 1,2-Distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-polyethylene glycol (DSPE-PEG) and subsequently anchored onto the surface of apoVs via hydrophobic interactions.

In this study, we first isolated apoVs from apoptotic neural stem cells (NSCs) using the classical gradient centrifugation method and employed proteomic analyses to characterize their protein composition relative to that of EVs (Scheme 1A). Subsequently, we engineered apoVs with APTC surface modification and demonstrated in vitro that this modification did not significantly compromise their H2S-releasing capacity or intrinsic biological activity. To avoid rapid clearance of the engineered apoV after injection into the infarct area, we constructed a ROS responsive hydrogel sustained-release platform (Scheme 1B and C). The hydrogel has a suitable elastic modulus and exhibits a 3D structure that mimics the properties of the natural extracellular matrix within the central nervous system (CNS) [23]. The hydrogel is capable of extending throughout the irregularly shaped stroke cavity to support cellular infiltration and shield encapsulated therapeutics from rapid cerebrospinal fluid–mediated clearance [24, 25]. Furthermore, it has been well-documented that hydrogels can deliver a broad spectrum of bioactive molecules to the target brain region and serve as a tunable matrix for the axonal outgrowth of endogenous or transplanted cells, thereby facilitating tissue regeneration [5, 26, 27]. By integrating APTC-modified apoV with a ROS-responsive hydrogel system, this study presented a highly promising strategy for the sustained delivery of therapeutic engineered vesicles into stroke lesions. Furthermore, it established a translatable platform that could be extended to the treatment of other CNS injuries and neurodegenerative diseases. The combined approach holds substantial potential to improve therapeutic outcomes in ischemic stroke, addressing critical limitations of current interventions and advancing the development of next-generation regenerative therapies for brain diseases.

Results and discussion

Characterizations of NSCs-apoVs

We first isolated NSCs from the brain tissues of prenatal mice brain cortex (Fig. 1A). These NSCs formed typical spherical neurospheres in vitro and expressed two established NSC markers, Nestin and Sox2 (Fig. 1B) [28, 29]. Subsequently, NSCs neurospheres were treated with staurosporine (STS) to induce apoptosis, and apoVs were isolated through a gradient centrifugation protocol (Fig. 1C). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that the apoVs expressed two apoptosis-related protein markers, including C1q, and phosphatidylserine (PS) exposed on the outer membrane leaflet which was detected by annexin V binding (Fig. 1D and E) [30]. Following co-incubation with mouse hippocampal neurons (HT22 cells), fluorescence microscopy revealed efficient internalization of DiI-labeled apoVs (Fig. 1F).

Although EVs derived from viable NSCs have been extensively studied, apoVs released during apoptosis process of NSCs remain less characterized. The molecular composition of apoVs is of particular research interest, as these vesicles carry a diverse repertoire of functional proteins that may influence recipient cell behavior [11]. Cargo sorting into apoVs is a cell type–dependent process, suggesting that apoVs originating from different parental cells may harbor distinct proteomic profiles and, consequently, exhibit differential biological activities [12]. In this study, proteome sequencing was employed to identify 19,705 proteins in apoVs, of which 294 were differentially expressed proteins, with 156 significantly upregulated in apoVs compared to the EVs derived from viable NSCs (Fig. 1G). We also found that some pro-repair related proteins, such as Netrin-1, Stathmin, P-Akt and Endoglin were significantly increased in apoVs, while some pro-inflammatory proteins, such as MCP3, FOXO3, CCL5 and Galectin-3 were reduced in apoVs (Fig. 1H).

Based on functional annotations using the Gene Ontology (GO) database, the upregulated proteins were categorized into three domains: “Cellular Component (CC),” “Molecular Function (MF),” and “Biological Process (BP),” indicating the presence of various functional proteins in apoVs (Fig. 1I). Enrichment analysis revealed that apoVs are enriched with regulatory proteins that could regulate multiple biological functions, including “oxidoreductase activity”, “cell-cell adhesion”, “cell adhesion molecule binding”, “brain development” and “angiogenesis” (Fig. 1I). Furthermore, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed to explore the pathways involved in the upregulated proteins. The results showed that these proteins are associated with “PPAR signaling pathway,” and “NF-kappa B signaling pathway” in the inflammation response and oxidative stress regulation category; “VEGF signaling pathway” in the angiogenesis category; “ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling” in the neuroprotection category; and “oxidative phosphorylation” in the metabolic regulation category (Fig. 1J). These results indicated that apoVs are enriched with functional proteins highly relevant to cellular plasticity responds to environmental signals, cellular behavior, regeneration and metabolic regulation.

Preparation and characterization of apoV@APTC

To enhance the biological function of apoVs, APTC was first covalently conjugated with DSPE-PEG-NHS via the reaction of its amino group with NHS. Consequently, DSPE-PEG-APTC could insert into the membrane of apoVs through hydrophobic insertion, forming apoV@APTC. The hydrophobic insertion relies on the hydrophobic interaction between amphiphilic molecules and the lipid bilayer, which has been widely used for the surface modification of EVs, as amphiphilic conjugates do not interfere with their biological functions [31]. The structure of DSPE-PEG-APTC was characterized by 1H-NMR spectroscopy. The newly appearing proton peaks at 8.5–9.0 ppm compared to DSPE-PEG-NHS indicated the successful conjugation of APTC to DSPE-PEG-NHS (Fig. 2A). Additionally, the new absorption peaks in the UV absorption spectrum of DSPE-PEG-APTC, corresponding to APTC, further confirmed this (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, apoVs exhibiting typical vesicular structures with a particle size greater than 100 nm were observed via TEM (Fig. 2C). Following DSPE modification, a core–shell structural configuration became apparent. Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) analysis revealed that the average hydrodynamic diameter of apoVs was slightly smaller than that of apoV@APTC (Fig. 2D). The modification significantly increased the zeta potential of apoVs from − 36.5 mV to -11.2 mV (Fig. 2E). These results demonstrated the successful modification of DSPE-PEG-APTC into the apoV membrane at the physicochemical level.

Favorable biocompatibility is essential for in vivo biomaterial applications, as it ensures minimal cytotoxicity, avoids adverse immune activation or tissue damage, and thereby enables safe integration into the biological milieu [6]. To investigate the cytotoxicity, apoV@APTC was incubated with HT22 cells at various concentrations (Fig. 2F). Within a treatment concentration of 160 µg/mL, apoV@APTC exhibited no obvious effect on HT22 cells with a cell viability rate above 86% for a duration of 24 h (Fig. 2G). However, at the concentrations exceeding 320 µg/mL, apoV@APTC gradually demonstrated cytotoxic effects on HT22 cells (Fig. 2H). Thus, the treatment concentration of 160 µg/mL of apoV@APTC was chosen for subsequent cellular experiments. We further clarified that the loading of DSPE-PEG-APTC on the surface of apoptotic vesicles did not affect the uptake efficiency of apoptotic vesicles by HT22 cells (Fig. S1). In addition, the observed co-localization of DiI-labeled apoV@APTC with lysosomes clearly demonstrated successful intracellular uptake, providing definitive evidence for the internalization of apoV@APTC (Fig. S2).

APTC has been investigated for its application in the treatment of diseases such as myocardial infarction [32], but its clinical application is limited by poor aqueous solubility, rapid clearance, and toxicological concerns [22]. To overcome these limitations, APTC was coupled with DSPE and further inserted into the apoVs surface. In this study, glutathione (GSH) which played a vital role in the plasma was utilized as the representative activator for measuring H2S [33]. The H2S release profiles were determined by the methylene blue assay (Fig. 2I). The results demonstrated that the H2S-releasing capacity of APTC was not significantly affected after conjugation with DSPE. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 2J, apoV@APTC exhibited a faster release rate under acidic conditions (pH 6.5) compared to neutral conditions (pH 7.4). Additionally, the intracellular H2S levels in HT22 cells were assessed using the Washington State Probe-1 (WSP-1) staining technique [34]. As shown in Fig. 2K, cells treated with APTC and apoV@APTC displayed strong green fluorescence, corroborating the presence of H2S within cells.

To clarify the potential mechanism underlying the effects of apoV@APTC, oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD)-injured neurons were harvested following apoV@APTC treatment for subsequent bioinformatics analysis, with untreated OGD-induced neurons set as the control group. Through analysis of the resulting volcano plot derived from transcriptomic profiling, 207 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected after treatment with the apoV@APTC, with 98 being upregulated and 109 downregulated (Fig. S3A). Notably, this observation demonstrated a distinct discrepancy between the OGD-induced neuron group and the apoV@APTC-treated group. The results of GO enrichment analysis indicated enhanced expression of genes implicated in biological regulation, developmental process, homeostatic process and transcription regulator process. The identified upregulated genes are linked to the processes of homeostasis preservation and neurovascular regeneration modulation (Fig. S3B).

Subsequently, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed, revealing that the upregulated target genes were significantly enriched in several key signaling pathways, including the cGMP-PKG signaling pathway, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and the neurotrophin signaling pathway (Fig. S3C). It was well known that cGMP-PKG signaling pathway integrated diverse intra- and extracellular signals to precisely modulate cellular behaviors and regulate core biological processes, including proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and stress adaptation. The nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolic pathway were crucial for maintaining the intracellular NAD⁺/NADP⁺ homeostasis. Through the dynamic cycle of “synthesis-utilization-regeneration”, it provided essential coenzymes for core physiological processes such as energy metabolism, DNA repair, and cellular signal transduction, and was closely associated with tissue damage repair. The neurotrophin signaling pathway activated downstream cascades through the binding of ligands such as nerve growth factor and brain-derived neurotrophic factor to Trk receptors, thereby regulating neuronal survival, differentiation, and synaptic plasticity.

In conclusion, the transcriptomic analysis revealed that the OGD-induced neurons treated with apoV@APTC displayed gene expression profiles conducive to inhibiting inflammatory response and cell apoptosis, as well as promoting neurovascular regeneration.

Preparation and characterization of apoV@APTC loaded hydrogel

Although apoV@APTC with H2S-releasing capability shows promising therapeutic potential for ischemic brain injury, the clinical translation remains limited by its inherent instability under complex physiological conditions. To address this challenge, we developed an injectable ROS-responsive hydrogel for the delivery of apoV@APTC to ischemic brain tissue (Fig. 3A and B). Specifically, HA-ADH was first synthesized via an EDC/NHS coupling reaction. The successful conjugation of ADH onto HA was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 3C), which showed characteristic methylene proton signals, N-acetyl methyl peaks, and ester methylene protons (1.5–2.5 ppm) derived from the grafted ADH [35]. In parallel, a ROS-sensitive linker (TK-NHS) was synthesized by activating TK-COOH using EDC and NHS. The 1H NMR spectrum revealed newly emerged proton signals around 2.8 ppm corresponding to the -NHS groups [36], confirming the successful synthesis of the TK-based crosslinker (Fig. 3D). The NHS groups of the crosslinker were capable of reacting with the hydrazide groups on HA-ADH to form covalent bonds under clinically relevant mild conditions. The hydrogel exhibited good injectability and moldability, allowing it to be easily extruded through a 27G needle and injected into the stroke cavity (Fig. 3B).

To optimize the formulation of the injectable hydrogel for ischemic brain injury treatment, we systematically assessed the gelation behavior of the two precursor solutions across varying volume ratios, with particular emphasis on gelation kinetics, rheological properties, and morphological characteristics. Interestingly, the gelation rate was found to decrease with increasing TK-NHS content (Fig. 3E). The fastest gelation time (< 40 s) was achieved when the HA-ADH/TK-NHS ratio was 0.6. In contrast, gelation times increased to about 2, 4, and 5 minutes when the HA-ADH/TK-NHS ratios were set at 1, 2, and 4, respectively. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) imaging of the freeze-dried hydrogels revealed a relatively uniform porous architecture, which could provide sufficient anchoring sites for in situ delivery of apoV@APTC (Fig. 3F). Notably, the surface pore structure became more regular with increasing TK concentration, likely due to enhanced crosslinking density. Rheological analysis further demonstrated that the storage modulus (G’) consistently exceeded the loss modulus (G’’) across all formulations, indicating the formation of stable hydrogel networks (Fig. 3G). Importantly, G’ values showed an inverse correlation with the HA-ADH/TK-NHS ratio. At an angular frequency of 1 rad/s, the hydrogel formulated with a HA-ADH/TK ratio of 2 exhibited a G’ value of 173 Pa, closely matching the reported elastic modulus of normal brain tissue (~ 189 Pa) [37]. Based on these findings, the hydrogel formulation with an HA-ADH/TK ratio of 2 was selected for subsequent studies, as it recapitulated key biomechanical properties of native brain tissue while providing favorable gelation kinetics and structural integrity.

Among emerging therapeutic strategies, hydrogels show considerable promise for neural repair and regeneration in ischemic stroke, owing to their capacity to enhance therapeutic efficacy via injectable, targeted delivery of drugs and cells to affected brain regions [5]. The primary requirements for hydrogels used in the treatment of ischemic stroke are excellent biocompatibility and low cytotoxicity [5]. The in vitro biocompatibility of the hydrogels was initially assessed in HT22 cells. The live/dead assays indicated high cell survival and minimal dead cells in the hydrogel, suggesting low cytotoxicity and favorable cytocompatibility (Fig. 3H and I). As indicated by the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 3J), hydrogel did not exhibit any appreciable cytotoxicity to HT22 cells after 24 h of incubation, with cell viability exceeding 95%. The above results suggested that the hydrogel had no adverse effect on cell growth and survival.

When used as a carrier for drugs or bioactive molecules, the swelling ratio and degradation rate of hydrogels are critical parameters that significantly influence therapeutic efficacy in ischemic stroke treatment. Hydrogels crosslinked with TK-NHS exhibited a distinct oxidative-responsive degradation behavior (Fig. 3K). Exposure to an oxidizing microenvironment simulated by H2O2 significantly accelerated their degradation rate, indicating that the degradation kinetics were closely correlated with ROS levels (Fig. 3L). The in vitro release results of apoV@APTC indicated that, compared to physiological conditions without H2O2, a significantly accelerated release profile was observed under conditions containing 5 mM H2O2, further confirming the ROS-responsive nature of our hydrogel system (Fig. S4). In terms of the swelling properties, the swelling ratio of hydrogels increased by 20% after 24 h of swelling in PBS (Fig. 3M).

To demonstrate the hydrogel platform’s capability for sustained release and enhanced retention of apoVs within the infarct region, we injected free apoV-DiI solution and apoV-DiI-loaded hydrogels into the mouse infarct cavity, respectively. Three days post-injection, the fluorescence signal of the free apoV-DiI solution was barely detectable, whereas the hydrogel group still exhibited a strong fluorescence intensity (Fig. S5). Concurrently, when the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel was injected into the periphery of the infarct cavity, residual hydrogel material was still observed after four weeks (Fig. S6). In summary, encapsulating apoV@APTC within the hydrogel prevents its rapid clearance and prolongs its retention time in the ischemic region.

The apoV@APTC loaded hydrogel reduced OGD-induced apoptosis and oxidative stress in HT22 cells

Neurons in the ischemic core are highly susceptible and undergo rapid cell death within minutes following the onset of ischemia [38]. Consequently, neuroprotection aimed at maximizing the preservation of viable neurons in the ischemic penumbra has emerged as a central therapeutic objective in ischemic stroke management [39]. In this study, the HT22 cell was employed as a model system to investigate the neuroprotective effects of apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel. To assess the ROS scavenging capacity of the hydrogel, intracellular ROS levels were measured using the 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe following OGD. As expected, OGD treatment induced a marked elevation in ROS production in HT22 cells compared to the control group (Fig. 4A and S7). The level of ROS in the hydrogels with H2S-releasing function treated groups was significantly reduced, and more than 80% ± 8.3% was eliminated.

The effect of apoV@APTC loaded hydrogel on apoptosis and oxidative stress induced by oxygen-glucose deprivation in HT22 cells. (C-F) UV-vis absorbance spectra change and (G-J) scavenging activity of DPPH, ABTS, •OH and •O2− of 0, 25, 50, 100, 200, and 500 µg/mL apoV@APTC (n = 3)

Mitochondrial membrane integrity is an early indicator of apoptosis, and we assessed changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) using the JC-1 fluorescent probe [8]. The results showed a reduction in red fluorescence and an increase in green fluorescence in HT22 cells following OGD, indicative of ΔΨm dissipation and the initiation of early apoptotic processes. Notably, these two APTC-loaded hydrogels remarkably reversed the aforementioned trend, with a higher ΔΨm detected in contrast to the OGD-induced group (Fig. 4B). Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) serves as the primary energy currency for cellular functions, with mitochondria generating ATP via oxidative phosphorylation to fuel essential processes including proliferation, biosynthesis, and signal transduction. Therefore, the apoV@APTC loaded hydrogel protected mitochondrial function and increased cellular ATP production of HT22 cells. This provided energy supply for cells on the verge of apoptosis and enhances cellular viability (Fig. S8). Furthermore, H2S acts as a reducing agent that alleviates oxidative stress through direct scavenging of ROS/RNS and indirect modulation of redox-related enzymes and signaling pathways, endowing the hydrogel with protective effects against ROS-induced cellular damage [40].

Oxidative stress during ischemic injury severely impair neuronal viability, making rapid ROS scavenging a critical strategy to mitigate acute ischemia-reperfusion damage and preserve neural tissue [41]. To quantify the free-radical scavenging efficacy of apoV@APTC, we employed a set of established assays traditionally used to measure antioxidant activity, including the 2,2′-Azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) radical cation (ABTS•) and 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl radical (DPPH•). As shown in Fig. 4C, D, G and H, the scavenging rates of these two free radicals could reach 66.1%±7.2%, and 62.3%±5.4%, respectively at 500 µg/mL of apoV@APTC. Furthermore, apoV@APTC exhibited outstanding ROS scavenging performance (Fig. 4E, F, I and J), including superoxide anions (•O2−) and hydroxyl radicals (•OH).

The apoV@APTC loaded hydrogel could promote angiogenesis

Blood vessels support neuronal function by delivering oxygen and nutrients and subsequently removing metabolic waste—processes essential for maintaining healthy brain activity. Given their critical role in neurovascular units, angiogenesis-based therapies have gained increasing recognition in the clinical management of cerebral ischemia [42]. Considering the angiogenic-related proteins significantly enriched in the proteomic profiling of apoptotic vesicles. In vitro tube formation assays indicated that Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs) cultured with the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel constructed more complete vascular networks relative to the OGD treatment groups (Fig. 5A). Quantitative analysis showed that the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel group significantly elevated tube numbers, total tube length, and branching points compared with the other groups, indicating robust apoV-mediated angiogenesis (Fig. 5B and D).

As shown in Fig. 5E and G, EdU staining revealed that both apoV-loaded and APTC-loaded hydrogels enhanced HUVEC proliferation compared to the OGD group, with the apoV-loaded hydrogel exhibiting a more pronounced pro-proliferative effect. Vinculin, a key structural protein essential for cell adhesion and spreading, serves as a critical component of focal adhesions [43, 44]. As shown in Fig. 5F, immunofluorescence staining for adhesion-related protein Vinculin was performed on cells after different treatment. Compared to the OGD group, the apoV@APTC loaded hydrogel group exhibited the strongest Vinculin fluorescence intensity, reflecting the most notable difference (Fig. 5H).

Vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) and its receptors (VEGFRs) form the central signaling axis governing angiogenesis, orchestrating endothelial cell proliferation, migration, differentiation, and vascular lumen formation through precise molecular interactions and signal transduction to ensure adequate tissue perfusion. As shown in Fig. S9, immunofluorescence staining for VEGFA and VEGFR2 was performed following different treatments, revealing that the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel group exhibited the strongest fluorescence intensity for both markers compared to the OGD and other control groups. And treatment with Gel/apoV@APTC significantly increased VEGF secretion in HUVECs compared to the other groups (Fig. S10). In summary, our findings demonstrated that the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel significantly promoted endothelial cell maturation and angiogenic activity in vitro.

The apoV@APTC loaded hydrogels induced the polarization of microglial toward the anti-inflammatory phenotype

Microglia, the primary resident immune cells of the central nervous system, act as the first line of defense following neural injury; after ischemic stroke, they degenerate within the infarct core but proliferate actively in the peri-infarct region [25]. Clinically, the long-lasting neuroinflammatory environment subsequent to ischemic stroke is a major driver of adverse outcomes. Chronically activated microglia secrete cytotoxic molecules that exacerbate inflammation and promote the loss of newly born neurons, thereby impeding neurological recovery [45]. Accordingly, mitigating microglia-mediated neuroinflammation after stroke is likely critical for recovery-focused therapeutic strategies [25, 45–47]. Activated microglia typically exhibit a characteristic amoeboid morphology, featuring an enlarged cell body and multiple short, thickened processes [45].

To further detect the effects of apoV@APTC loaded hydrogel on microglia, Iba1 staining was conducted on day 7 post-treatment in our experiments (Fig. 6A and D). Under normal physiological conditions, Iba1⁺ microglia typically display a resting phenotype, characterized by small cell bodies and highly ramified processes [25]. In the photothrombotic (PT) group, there was a marked elevation in the quantity of Iba1+ microglia within the peri-infarct area. In contrast, transplantation of the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel not only markedly reduced the density of Iba1⁺ microglia but also restored the complexity of their ramified processes, collectively indicating that intracavitary delivery of the hydrogel after stroke exerts a sustained anti-neuroinflammatory effect.

(B) Immunofluorescence staining of the microglia marker Iba-1 and CD86 on day 7 after treatment (n = 6). (C) Immunofluorescence staining of Iba-1 and CD206 on day 7 after treatment (n = 6). (D) Quantitative analysis of the Iba1 mean area. (E) Quantitative analysis of the CD206 relative fluorescence intensity. (F) Quantitative analysis of the CD86 relative fluorescence intensity

Under normal physiological conditions, microglia maintain a resting state but are capable of polarizing into two distinct phenotypes: the M1 and M2 phenotypes [6]. Following stroke, microglia rapidly polarize toward the M1 phenotype, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines and mediators that create a neurotoxic, pro-inflammatory microenvironment, thereby exacerbating neuronal injury and hindering tissue repair [6]. By comparison, M2 microglia exert protective effects by producing and secreting neurotrophic factors, thereby supporting tissue repair processes and safeguarding neuronal integrity in pathological contexts [21]. To distinguish the two functional phenotypes of microglia, Iba1/CD86 and Iba1/CD206 co-staining were utilized in this study to label M1 and M2 subsets, respectively [21]. As shown in Fig. 6B and F, microglia in the peri-infarct region of the PT group predominantly adopted the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. In contrast, treatment with the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel significantly suppressed CD86 expression and effectively promoted microglial polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype (Fig. 6C and E). Interestingly, the APTC-loaded hydrogel group demonstrated a superior inhibitory effect on CD86 expression compared to the apoVs-loaded hydrogel group. qPCR results also revealed that the expression of the CD86 was significantly downregulated in the Gel/apoV@APTC treatment group compared to the control group (Fig. S11). Conversely, the expression of the anti-inflammatory marker CD206 was markedly upregulated in the Gel/apoV@APTC treatment group (Fig. S11). This phenotypic shift indicated that the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel fostered a more conducive microenvironment for tissue recovery by suppressing the inflammatory response in ischemic lesions. The findings suggested that the hydrogel modulated the brain′s inflammatory response, likely through the combined antioxidant effects of H2S and the neuroprotective properties of apoVs, to promote microglial polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and accelerate brain repair.

The apoV@APTC loaded hydrogels induced neuroprotection

Following apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel treatment, we observed a pronounced attenuation of oxidative stress in the peri-infarct brain tissue, as evidenced by three complementary assays (as shown in Fig. S12): dihydroethidium (DHE) staining, 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) immunohistochemistry, and malondialdehyde (MDA) quantification. DHE fluorescence intensity, a measure of superoxide anion accumulation, was significantly reduced by over 50% in the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel-treated group compared to PT group, indicating effective scavenging of ROS. Consistently, 8-OHdG-positive cells, which reflect oxidative DNA damage, were markedly decreased, suggesting enhanced genomic protection. Furthermore, MDA levels, a well-established biomarker of lipid peroxidation, were substantially lower in apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel-treated animals, confirming mitigation of membrane lipid oxidation. Collectively, these findings provide robust in vivo evidence that the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel exerts potent antioxidant effects by simultaneously suppressing ROS generation, preserving DNA integrity, and inhibiting lipid peroxidation in the ischemic brain microenvironment.

To further assess neuronal functional recovery after ischemic stroke, we performed Nissl staining on brain tissue sections on day 7 to visualize Nissl bodies. Nissl staining of PT mice brain tissue showed sparse, disordered nerve fibers and damaged pyramidal cells (blurred contours, Nissl body loss), indicating severe neuronal injury post-ischemic stroke (Fig. 7A). Conversely, treatment with hydrogel loaded with either apoV or APTC partially attenuated the disruption of cortical nerve fibers and the loss of Nissl bodies in brain slices. Notably, apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel administration in mice led to more organized neuronal fibers, minimal vacuolation, reduced neuronal swelling, and a marked increase in Nissl bodies within the infarcted region. To gain further insight into the underlying mechanisms, we evaluated apoptotic cell death in brain tissue by performing TUNEL staining 7 days post-treatment (Fig. 7B). Notably, compared with the PT group, treatment with Gel/apoV, Gel/APTC, or Gel/apoV@APTC resulted in a significant reduction in the proportion of TUNEL-positive cells (Fig. 7D). These findings indicated that apoVs and APTC exerted neuroprotective and antioxidant effects by partially attenuating neuronal apoptosis and oxidative stress, underscoring their potential in alleviating ischemic brain injury. It was plausible to hypothesize that the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel promoted neuronal morphological reconstruction by restoring structural integrity and enhancing the regenerative capacity of damaged neurons, thereby facilitating functional recovery after neural injury.

In order to explore the morphological changes of neurons in mice treated with different hydrogels, Golgi staining was used to measure the density of dendritic spines, total dendritic branches and dendritic length of neurons in the penumbra region (Fig. 7C). Our findings demonstrated that PT stroke mice displayed a significant reduction in dendritic spine density, total dendritic length, and neurite branching complexity in neurons within the peri-infarct cortical region at 28 days post-ischemia. Particularly, treatment with the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel significantly attenuated these structural deficits, preserving dendritic spine density, dendritic length, and neurite branching in the peri-infarct cortex (Fig. 7E and G).

We then tested the in vivo biocompatibility of the hydrogel loaded with apoV@APTC at 7 days post-injection. As shown in Fig. S13, there were no abnormal tissues around the hydrogel implantation site even after hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining. Similarly, there was no significant increase in the infiltration of CD3-positive T cells or CD68-positive macrophages in the brain tissue around the hydrogel. These data demonstrated that empty hydrogel or hydrogel loaded with apoV@APTC did not induce a significant immune reaction in the brain. Furthermore, histopathological analysis by H&E staining revealed no apparent signs of organ toxicity in major organs, indicating favorable systemic biocompatibility of the treatment (Fig. S14).

Ischemic stroke disrupts synaptic integrity and neural circuitry, leading to neuronal dysfunction; synaptic repair and regeneration are thus pivotal for functional recovery. As key functional proteins located on the presynaptic and postsynaptic membranes respectively, vesicular glutamate transporter 1 (vGluT1) and postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95) regulate the loading and release of excitatory neurotransmitters as well as the assembly of postsynaptic signal complexes. They play an indispensable role in synaptic reconstruction, restoration of signal transmission, and neuronal survival following neural injury, with their expression levels and functional states directly influencing the efficacy of repair. As shown in Fig. S15 and S16, immunofluorescence staining for MAP2/vGluT1 and MAP2/PSD95 were performed on neurons after different treatment. The apoV@APTC loaded hydrogel group exhibited the strongest vGluT1 and PSD95 fluorescence levels, compared to the other groups, demonstrating significant neural repair effects by apoV@APTC loaded hydrogel.

The apoV@APTC loaded hydrogels could promote angiogenesis after stroke

The aforementioned studies have confirmed the pro-angiogenic effect of hydrogels in vitro, and we further investigated the effect of hydrogels on regulating angiogenesis in vivo. To assess whether the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel promoted cerebral perfusion recovery, laser speckle contrast imaging was used to evaluate regional blood flow within and around the stroke cavity at 28 days post-ischemia. The color-coded images revealed that marked cerebral hypoperfusion was evident in the infarcted hemisphere of the PT group (Fig. 8A). In contrast to the PT group, Gel/apoV@APTC implantation in mice resulted in a significant increase in blood flow (Fig. 8D). Collectively, our results demonstrated that the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel promoted therapeutic recovery by stimulating angiogenesis and revascularization, thereby facilitating restoration of cerebral blood flow in the infarct region.

Angiogenesis, a key process in ischemic stroke recovery, constitutes the formation, differentiation, and maturation of new blood vessels. Both experimental and clinical studies have shown that inducing angiogenesis reduces infarct volume, enhances neuronal survival, and supports restoration of neurovascular network function [25, 42]. As a marker of angiogenesis, CD31 staining was used to examine the effects of apoV@APTC loaded hydrogel on angiogenesis [24]. Quantitative analysis showed no statistically significant difference in microvasculature components between the PT group and the Gel group (Fig. 8B). A significant increase in CD31 expression was observed in the Gel/apoV group and Gel/apoV@APTC group, indicating robust in vivo angiogenesis and the establishment of a favorable regenerative environment conducive to nerve repair (Fig. 8E and F).

Furthermore, the newly formed vasculature was initially immature, highly permeable, and disorganized. It subsequently matured through the deposition of a basement membrane and the establishment of pericyte and astrocyte end-feet coverage [48]. To assess the potential of dual-functional hydrogels (with anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic properties) in promoting neovascular maturation, we performed double immunofluorescence staining on tissue sections using antibodies against CD31 and the pericyte marker PDGFR-β (Fig. 8C) [49]. Quantitative analysis revealed that the apoV loaded hydrogel and apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel significantly enhanced pericyte coverage on neovasculature in the infarct area compared to other groups, demonstrating improved vascular maturity and structural integrity (Fig. 8G). Thus, in vivo angiogenesis assays confirmed that the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel promoted robust maturation and organization of nascent vasculature, thereby facilitating revascularization and repair of ischemic tissue.

The apoV@APTC loaded hydrogels enhanced functional recovery after stroke

Recovery of neurological functions, particularly cognitive and sensorimotor abilities, is a critical endpoint in evaluating ischemic stroke therapies. To assess the neuroprotective effects of the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel, representative behavioral tests were performed, including the Morris water maze, beam balance, rotarod, cylinder, and grid-walk tests.

To assess potential deficits in spatial learning and memory, we used the Morris water maze test, which is highly sensitive to these cognitive impairments [21]. As illustrated in Fig. 9A, treatment with either Gel/apoV or Gel/APTC resulted in a significant improvement in learning, evidenced by shorter escape latencies compared to the PT group. The Gel/apoV@APTC group exhibited the most pronounced preservation of learning ability, with a greatly shortened escape latency (Fig. 9B). During the probe trial (platform removed), both Gel/apoV and Gel/APTC treatments effectively rescued spatial memory deficits. Notably, mice in the Gel/apoV@APTC group spent significantly more time in the target quadrant, indicating superior memory retention (Fig. 9C). The improved performance in the Morris water maze test suggests a potential link to the protective effects of the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel against hippocampal damage following ischemic stroke.

Moreover, evaluation of sensorimotor function via the beam balance test, the rotarod, cylinder and grid-walk test. In the beam balance test, apoV@APTC loaded hydrogel treatment significantly decreased the time to cross beam (Fig. 9D). The rotarod test further confirmed a significant improvement in motor coordination following treatment with the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel, as evidenced by a markedly prolonged latency to fall (Fig. 9E). The cylindrical enclosure is designed to elicit vertical rearing behavior, during which mice naturally explore the walls with their forelimbs. Following a stroke, unilateral nerve damage results in a significant asymmetry in forelimb use, with a strong preference for the non-impaired limb [6]. As shown in Fig. 9F, mice treated with Gel/apoV@APTC exhibited a significantly lower forelimb asymmetry score in the cylinder test compared to all other groups. Corroborating these motor improvements, the grid-walk test revealed a reduction in the foot fault rate following treatment with Gel/apoV or Gel/APTC (Fig. 9G).

Collectively, the neurobehavioral outcomes demonstrate that the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel significantly mitigated ischemic brain injury. This therapeutic effect was achieved through the concerted actions of scavenging ROS to foster angiogenesis, coupled with direct neuroprotection via the suppression of neuronal apoptosis and neuroinflammation. The marked improvement in both cognitive and sensorimotor functions highlights the potential of apoV@APTC loaded hydrogel in the enhancement of functional recovery after stroke. These results collectively demonstrate the significant neuroprotective efficacy of the apoV@APTC-loaded hydrogel against ischemic injury, and our findings substantiate its promising potential for clinical translation.

Over the past decade, exosomes and microvesicles have been extensively explored in clinical trials; however, their clinical translation has been markedly hindered by labor-intensive isolation procedures and limited production yields [50]. By comparison, apoptotic vesicles (apoVs) can be obtained more readily and in substantially higher quantities, highlighting their considerable potential as a clinically applicable class of extracellular vesicles.

Consistent with this premise, apoVs have recently attracted increasing attention as candidates for translational and clinical applications. In 2023, Mao et al. conducted a single-center, prospective, two-arm clinical trial to assess the safety and therapeutic efficacy of a gelatin sponge-loaded apoV complex in promoting bone regeneration after mandibular third molar extraction (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05971342) [51]. In that study, allogeneic umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived apoVs were incorporated into a gelatin sponge scaffold, thereby facilitating functional alveolar bone repair.

Despite these encouraging advances, several important challenges still need to be addressed before apoVs can be more broadly translated into clinical practice. Notably, the methods used to induce apoV generation vary considerably, and such differences may profoundly influence their composition and biological activity. This variability underscores the urgent need for standardized protocols covering apoV induction, isolation, phenotypic characterization, and quantitative assessment. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that distinct apoV subpopulations may exert divergent therapeutic effects through different molecular mechanisms. Therefore, a deeper understanding of apoV heterogeneity will be essential for optimizing their therapeutic efficacy and accelerating their future clinical translation.

Conclusion

Ischemic stroke is driven by a cascade of interrelated pathological events, rendering single-target interventions often ineffective. To address this complexity, we developed an injectable ROS-responsive hydrogel platform co-loaded with apoVs and APTC. Unlike conventional delivery systems that passively release therapeutics, our platform leverages the elevated ROS microenvironment of the ischemic brain as a trigger for on-demand, sustained release of engineered apoVs, thereby enabling adaptive, multi-mechanistic intervention. In vitro, treatment with apoV@APTC loaded hydrogels significantly reduced ROS accumulation and attenuated neuronal apoptosis in HT22 cells subjected to OGD. In a murine model of ischemic stroke, intracerebral delivery of the hydrogel led to prolonged retention at the lesion site, concomitant with reduced oxidative stress markers, suppressed neuroinflammation, and enhanced neuronal survival. Critically, during the subacute and recovery phases, animals treated with apoV@APTC loaded hydrogels exhibited marked improvements in sensorimotor function and spatial learning, correlating with increased neuroprotection and angiogenesis.

Notably, this work represents a strategic integration of bioactive cell-derived vesicles with a pathology-responsive biomaterial, distinguishing it from prior standalone EVs therapies or static H₂S donors. The hydrogel not only protects apoVs from rapid clearance but also couples their regenerative potential to the disease activity itself—offering a self-regulating therapeutic paradigm with strong translational relevance for stroke and other ROS-driven neurological disorders. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that the apoV@APTC hydrogel platform exerts multifaceted protective effects against cerebral ischemia, providing a promising foundation for future clinical development.
