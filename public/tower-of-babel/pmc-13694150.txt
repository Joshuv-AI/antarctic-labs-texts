Legal and Ethical Considerations of Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging in India
Authors: Vineet Upadhyaya, Vibhav Mithal, Amit Gupta, Krithika Rangarajan, Devasenathipathy Kandasamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence is being deployed in radiology faster than the rules and regulations required to govern it. In India, this is further complicated by disparities in access to healthcare and a data protection legal framework that is in a transition phase until it becomes fully operational on May 13, 2027. Understanding these legalities is no longer optional for doctors who are in practice, using AI in the clinic, or participating in the development of AI.MethodsThis is a narrative review drawing on existing articles, Indian laws, and current national governance frameworks related to the use of AI in medical imaging in India.ResultsThis review discusses the ethical and legal considerations that come with using AI today. We discuss algorithmic bias, the economic divide that limits AI access, the lack of transparency and explainability of black-box models, the threat to radiology residents from automation bias, and post-deployment model drift. On the legal side, we discuss the requirements of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, regarding informed consent and hospitals as data fiduciaries, the classification of AI tools as Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), and Indian governance frameworks, including the Indian Council of Medical Research Ethical Guidelines for AI in Biomedical Research and Healthcare, 2023, and the Strategy for Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare for India (SAHI), 2026.ConclusionThe review concludes with a practical good-practice checklist that gives radiologists an illustrative set of questions to help retain the critical mindset required at each stage of AI adoption. As AI use cases vary by context, specific guidance for your own use case is recommended.

Introduction

India finds itself at a pivotal stage in the progression of its healthcare system. It is rapidly transitioning from physical to digital modes, making the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) more feasible than ever. Its large and diverse population has healthcare needs that differ from those of other countries; hence, a planned and tactful implementation is required to achieve a meaningful impact.

AI has the potential to transform several areas of the healthcare workflow, particularly from a public health perspective. AI holds the promise of reducing the administrative workload of healthcare workers, such as documentation and scheduling, making healthcare delivery more efficient and enabling the digitization of healthcare services by reducing the time spent on delivery and giving them more time for direct patient care. 1 2 The earlier generation of one-task-specific AI models has been particularly effective for specific tasks in radiology. Newer foundational and generative models hold the promise of understanding multiple tasks and providing multimodal capability.

Several AI applications have been studied in large-scale clinical trials; some such applications are mentioned here. AI has shown promise in triaging patients with critical conditions, such as intracranial hemorrhage on non-contrast head CT, as evidenced by a trial comparing two AI triage platforms across 4,081 scans, which reported a sensitivity of 94.4% and a specificity of 97.4%. 3 AI models for pneumothorax screening on chest radiographs, which facilitate faster reporting, have also been found to be effective. 4 The Mammography Screening with Artificial Intelligence (MASAI) trial 5 demonstrated that AI-assisted mammography screening had a non-inferior interval cancer rate, higher sensitivity, and the same specificity as standard double reading while reducing the radiologist's reading workload. AI models have also proven useful in personalizing and optimizing diabetes management. 6 These tools are excellent for augmenting a healthcare professional's clinical expertise, upskilling healthcare workers, and upscaling the extent of coverage that can be provided.

AI also plays an important role in strengthening health systems management. It can be used to support resource planning, supply chain management, and personnel allocation, thereby increasing the efficiency of healthcare schemes. It has also been effectively used to accelerate healthcare research, particularly in drug development and clinical trial design. In the public sector in India, AI has been integrated into national health initiatives and clinical decision support systems in the eSanjeevani program to generate differential diagnoses across over 282 million consultations. 7 Prominent examples of use in the Indian public health system include Cough against TB 8 and MadhunetrAI, 9 which have been used to screen patients for tuberculosis and diabetic retinopathy, respectively, and are particularly useful in resource-limited settings where specialists are not available. AI has also been used as a fraud-detection tool for the Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY scheme. 7 A recent study on the use of generative AI showed that simulated training of residents on generated mammograms led to a significant improvement in their reporting accuracy. 10 AI has also been used for patient education, such as the simplification of radiology reports to aid patient understanding in oncology 11 and translation of radiology reports into Hindi. 12

The nationwide implementation of these AI solutions is largely uneven due to infrastructure limitations, and adoption needs to evolve to address several constraints, including defining areas where economic benefit can be clearly demonstrated and further developing the regulatory landscape.

To mitigate risks from AI systems, India's approach to AI governance balances innovation with regulation, adopting the law “plus” approach. 13 Risks from AI systems are either mitigated through laws such as the Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act or through “plus” mechanisms, which may include policies, frameworks, and standards. Illustratively, Indian Government policies that support AI governance and/or growth of the AI ecosystem include the IndiaAI mission, the Digital Information Security in Healthcare Act (DISHA, which was not separately implemented, but rather subsumed into broader health data protection legislation), 14 and the Strategy for Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare for India (SAHI). This review is intended as a primer on the ethical challenges, legal considerations, and approaches to addressing them that doctors, particularly radiologists, must be aware of when venturing into the development, deployment, or use of healthcare AI.

Methodology

The purpose of this review is to identify and summarize the governance frameworks, policies, and literature relating to the legal and ethical considerations of artificial intelligence in medical imaging in India. Electronic databases such as PubMed and Google Scholar were searched for terms such as “artificial intelligence,” “radiology,” “India,” “medical imaging,” “legal,” and “ethics.” Resources from Indian legislation, governmental notifications, Central Drugs Standard Control Organization (CDSCO) regulations, Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) policies, and others were also incorporated into the review.

India versus Global Approach, Landscape of AI in Health in India

The emergence of AI in healthcare has pushed countries worldwide to create regulatory frameworks that ensure the safe and ethical use of these technologies without stifling innovation. Various approaches have been tried by different nations. The European Union, through its AI Act, and South Korea, via national legislation, have implemented risk-based regulatory frameworks that categorize systems by risk level and impose stricter requirements for higher-risk applications, especially healthcare-related models. At the federal level, the United States has provided flexible guidance from healthcare regulators rather than comprehensive legislation. Singapore and Canada, on the other hand, integrate existing laws in the regulatory realm. However, through a dedicated AI law lens, they follow principle-based governance models that rely on core values such as transparency, fairness, and accountability. Countries such as France, which may follow the EU AI Act, have also put infrastructure, including research collaborations and partnerships, in the limelight, which provides easy access to data and bolster the research and development ecosystem that may enable AI adoption. 7 India, with a vast and diverse population, requires a meticulously planned AI policy to adequately address its healthcare landscape. India has developed a digital health infrastructure and policies that foster the integration of AI into its healthcare sector.

A core component of this is the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, which has created digital health identifiers, interoperable health records, and consent-based data-sharing mechanisms that have laid the groundwork for gathering the basic data required by these systems. 15 India's AI Governance Guidelines (2025) state that the existing laws, such as the DPDP Act and sectoral regulations, are sufficient for mitigating most AI-related risks and that a separate AI law, enacted in many Western countries, is not required in India at present. 7 16

At the same time, recent reports from July 2026, indicate that this position may change, with India considering a separate regulatory framework for AI. 17

The Indian healthcare workforce is diverse, comprising physicians, nurses, allied health workers, Community Health Officers (CHOs), Accredited Social Health Activists, and others. Their training is now being structured to include AI and digital health. The National Board of Examinations in Medical Sciences recently launched an online training program on AI in Medical Education to provide early exposure to the use of AI in clinical settings, research, education, and diagnostics. 7 In addition, an institution-level approach is also being simultaneously pursued through the designation of Centers of Excellence in AI in Health in various tertiary care hospitals and technical institutions.

As AI technologies continue to grow and evolve, integrating these somewhat fragmented approaches to AI into a cohesive framework is expected to ensure the safe, ethical, and effective adoption of AI in the Indian healthcare system. This is especially true for the field of radiology, which has been quick to adopt AI technologies due to its pre-existing digital and pattern-recognition-based nature.

Ethical Considerations

In radiology, we are rapidly moving into, or have at least partially implemented, systems where AI prepares preliminary reports, gives automated suggestions, determines priorities in worklists, or, in some extreme cases, performs autonomous readings. While the advent and implementation of AI in medical imaging have presented numerous ways to improve diagnostic accuracy and efficiency, they also raise ethical concerns that are particularly important in the Indian healthcare system, in part due to large disparities in healthcare affordability and accessibility.

Algorithmic Bias

Unequal access to healthcare across the country can lead to the skewed training and validation of AI models. It is important to recognize that AI is a data-driven process. Most AI models are trained on data from high-income settings, thereby propagating and amplifying bias, since they have not encountered patterns present in low-income settings. 18 19 Algorithmic bias develops when models are trained on skewed, non-representative datasets. Algorithmic amnesia is another problem that can occur in the Indian scenario, where models trained on international data are fine-tuned for Indian data. Here, the machine may “forget” underlying patterns, while overfitting to the patterns on which they are fine-tuned. 20

Ensuring the model generalizes to a variety of patient populations involves training the model on the entire spectrum of clinical data. Doing this requires large-scale centralization of health data. A possible workaround to this is the concept of “federated learning.” 21 Federated learning is an approach in which the AI model is trained across multiple sites without transferring patient data across institutions. Instead, the model is sent to various hospitals and trained on locally obtained data, such as scans on PACS. The model's updated parameters are then shared with the central server, thus obviating the need to share patient data itself and increasing data safety. It is stated that each “site” in the above multi-site training would have to comply separately with the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, described in more detail in Section 5.

Another layer of complexity is added due to the lack of standardization in India's healthcare ecosystem. Most AI models are trained and validated in tightly controlled and uniform environments, but there is a large and varied distribution of technology in India, ranging from cutting-edge MRI units in urban tertiary care centers to older generation X-ray machines in rural primary health centers. Acquisition protocols and calibration also vary widely based on location, which could lead to poor performance of AI models trained on standardized datasets. Thus, independent external validation and calibration should be carried out by institutions prior to deploying an AI model.

The Economic Divide and Distributive Justice

Given that the Indian healthcare system is already full of inequities, the concern that the adoption of AI in medical imaging will worsen these divides is not unwarranted. High-tech commercial AI imaging resources engineered to help with diagnostic reporting, workflow optimization, and other functions are largely available only in well-funded private hospitals in large metro cities, whereas government and municipal hospitals and primary health centers more often than not are unable to use these technologies, not only due to monetary restrictions but also due to the lack of sufficiently trained personnel.

The principle of distributive justice, which emphasizes the fair allocation of resources, requires that AI technologies be integrated into the public sector through government schemes, subsidies, and public–private partnerships to ensure that advances in healthcare reach the country's entire population. Integrating AI into existing systems, such as the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, could be one way to work toward this goal.

Transparency and Explainability

Many AI systems, such as those used in radiology, operate as “black boxes,” i.e., they produce diagnoses without explaining how the conclusions were reached. This is the opposite of how a radiologist works – as they are trained to explain why certain imaging features point toward or against a diagnosis. A clinician would be unable to assess the validity of an AI system's diagnosis without a sound explanation, thereby reducing trust in the model. 22 23 It also makes the clinician less accountable in high-stakes scenarios. 24

It is recommended that any AI model used in healthcare must be explainable, as it would influence patients' diagnoses and treatment outcomes. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) aims to make AI outputs easily understandable by providing visual explanations, such as heatmaps that highlight abnormal areas in a scan that lead to a particular diagnosis. A physician can then independently verify the output and rule out artefactual errors, thereby increasing trust. 22 23 XAI models are still limited by the potential to generate plausible but unfaithful explanations that may not reliably reflect the model's underlying true reasoning. Therefore, at this stage, they can only be deployed as a clinical aid for physicians, rather than as standalone decision-making tools. 22 23

Automation Bias

Automation bias is the tendency to agree with AI-generated outputs rather than with one's own clinical opinion. With regard to radiology, this bias is important from both a physician training and a patient safety standpoint.

Wherever AI-assisted reporting is available, radiologists may start signing off on these algorithmic diagnoses without adequately questioning them. This leads to errors of commission, in which incorrect AI suggestions are accepted, and errors of omission, in which abnormalities missed by AI are not sought by the radiologist. 25 26 These errors are likely to be increased in high-workload departments, in which the radiologist has less time to look through each scan. 26 As reliance on AI-generated pre-reads increases, this is likely to become a major problem. Thus, with an awareness of automation bias, radiologists need to carefully consider which areas of the radiology workflow they want to adopt AI in.

The effect of such automated pre-reads is particularly striking when resident training is considered. Residents may develop an over-reliance on AI models and may not adequately develop their own pattern recognition and critical thinking skills. 26 Thus, in the future, when these residents become fully trained radiologists, their ability to correct reports already generated by AI may be seriously hampered, and academic hospitals must consider this possibility as they incorporate AI into their clinical workflows. If AI is to be adopted, mitigating the risk of automation bias may involve designing methods of human oversight (that do not further amplify automation bias), along with appropriate technical, organizational, and governance safeguards.

Illustratively, radiology training programs should address this problem early by incorporating AI literacy and education on cognitive biases. They should teach their residents how and when AI-made decisions should be questioned and overridden. Radiologists should be trained to serve as clinical auditors and to use AI as a supportive tool, not as the final decision-maker. 7

Post-Market Surveillance and Algorithmic Drift

To maintain the safety and efficiency of AI models post-deployment, it is essential to conduct regular post-market surveillance. AI tools can change over time and experience “drift,” in which their performance changes over time due to variations in data distribution, patient demographics, and disease prevalence. It is therefore necessary to maintain continuous monitoring to detect drift and recalibrate the model as needed.

As a guiding light for mitigating the described ethical concerns, SAHI has incorporated these seven principles to create a framework that emphasizes equity, transparency, accountability, and public trust. It states that AI should serve as a complement to a doctor's clinical expertise and not a replacement for it. This is particularly important in radiology, where AI systems can assist with diagnostic decision-making to some extent. This requires strict adherence to ethical principles as a prerequisite. SAHI recommends implementing adequate measures to ensure that all healthcare AI models are trained on datasets that are appropriately representative of India's diverse population, disease profiles, and primary care settings. Otherwise, bias would be introduced into the models, particularly against the historically underserved and marginalized groups of society, depriving them of much-needed medical care and harming public trust. 7

SAHI describes a “lifecycle-based” approach to governance, which is an end-to-end process from the initial decision to apply AI in healthcare to the monitoring and adaptation of the model to prevent data drift and to its eventual retirement. This is in line with the Total Product Lifecycle approach emphasized by the US FDA which includes postmarket surveillance and algorithm updates. 29 These measures are necessary to maintain clinical safety and public trust.

Guidelines for Fixing Liability

The distribution of liability in AI-assisted healthcare (or generally across the AI value chain) is not yet legally defined in medical imaging. Errors in AI-assisted medical imaging may arise from algorithmic bias, algorithmic amnesia, automation bias, etc. The errors may also be a result of the role that the different actors, such as developers, deployers, vendors, healthcare institutions, or clinicians, play across the AI value chain. This creates a loophole in terms of whom to hold accountable as it can be unclear whether the radiologist's interpretation was wrong or the AI's reasoning was flawed. This leads to an accountability issue in which no single person/model can be held responsible for adverse outcomes. 24 Acknowledging this aspect, India's AI Governance Guidelines, 2025, under the accountability pillar, recommend “ Adopt a graded liability system based on the function performed, level of risk, and whether due diligence was observed, and greater transparency is required about how different actors in the AI value chain operate and their compliance with legal obligations .” 16

Till the vexed issue of liability allocation is solved, in practice, it would be prudent for radiologists, who are ultimately end-users of AI in the AI value chain, to view AI as decision support tools and be aware of which tool they are using, for what purpose, and how exactly AI will augment their work. This information would enable them to have complete clarity on their role. If, on the other hand, radiologists directly deploy AI tools in their businesses, as entrepreneurs, then their role in the AI value chain changes, and they require appropriate advice.

Legal Considerations

This section describes the legal frameworks that underlie AI implementation as they relate to healthcare( Table 2 ).

Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, Read with Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025

If a healthcare AI system processes digital personal data , a key legal obligation is compliance with the Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023, which, read in conjunction with the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025, governs the processing of digital personal data of Indian citizens and its use in AI systems. The DPDP Act was passed by Parliament on August 11, 2023, and the DPDP Rules were notified on November 14, 2025. This brought the DPDP framework partially into effect. It is set to take full effect on May 13, 2027. The period between November 14, 2025 and May 13 th , 2027, is an intentional transition period provided by the Government of India for compliance with the DPDP Act. 30 31 32

For context, the DPDP Act acknowledges a data principal's (e.g., a patient) rights over their personal data and safeguards the processing of their digital personal data . The DPDP Act also imposes obligations (Section 8) 31 on data fiduciaries, i.e., entities that are processing the patient's personal data (Section 2[i]). 31 Personal data is any data of an individual by which the individual can be identified, e.g., a name, an email address, etc. The DPDP Act applies to personal data in digital form, and also includes the processing of a patient's personal data even if the said patient is outside India if such digital personal data is being used “in connection with any activity related to the offering of goods or services within the territory of India ” (Section 3). Therefore, if an Indian citizen patient's personal data who is based abroad, is being used for an activity related to a service being offered in India, the DPDP Act will apply. Because AI systems may be trained using personal data in digital form, if there is such an intersection between digital personal data and AI systems, the DPDP Act and Rules have to be complied with by data fiduciaries. Data fiduciaries include hospitals, diagnostic centers, and healthcare institutions. Moreover, the Central Government, in its discretion, may notify any data fiduciary as a Significant Data Fiduciary, based on an assessment of certain factors such as the volume and sensitivity of personal data processed. If an entity qualifies as a Significant Data Fiduciary, it has additional obligations, which include appointing an independent data auditor to carry out a data audit and undertaking a periodic Data Protection Impact Assessment.

In terms of data fiduciaries' obligations, the DPDP Act permits the processing of a patient's personal data either on the basis of consent or on the basis of certain legitimate uses (e.g., processing digital personal data in the case of a medical emergency involving a threat to life, or an immediate threat to health) where consent would not be needed. Therefore, data fiduciaries must obtain the consent of patients or assess if the processing of personal data qualifies as a “legitimate use” where consent may not be needed. If consent is needed, then informed consent is required from all patients before data collection and processing. The patients must be informed whether their imaging data will be used for the training or validation of AI algorithms, and patients have many rights under the DPDP Act, including the right to withdraw their consent at any time. Moreover, whether a patient's consent is taken or a patient's personal data is processed under a legitimate use, in both scenarios, a data fiduciary has legal obligations. These include ensuring that personal data is complete, accurate, and consistent; protecting personal data in its possession from a breach by adopting reasonable security safeguards (e.g., data security measures such as encryption of personal data); erasing personal data (subject to certain exceptions) if a request for withdrawal is received; and establishing a mechanism to redress grievances of Data Principals. There are further obligations upon data fiduciaries to follow if personal data of children is processed. 33

The DPDP Act imposes significant penalties. A failure to observe reasonable security safeguards to prevent a personal data breach may lead to a fine of up to Rs 250 crores; a failure to observe additional obligations in relation to children may lead to a fine of Rs 200 crores; and a breach of additional obligations by a significant data fiduciary may attract fines of up to Rs 150 crores.

An exemption from the application of the DPDP Act is given in Section 17 (2) (b) of the DPDP Act, which allows the use of patient data for research, archival, or statistical purposes, but this data must not be used to make any decision for the patient. Rule 16 of the DPDP Rules, 2025 provides further information on how this exemption may be applied. 32 Finally, personal data in non-digital form, processed for any personal or domestic purpose, and/or made or caused to be made publicly available by a Data Principal or by any person who is under a legal obligation, is excluded from the DPDP Act. The DPDP Act does not define “publicly available” and what qualifies as “publicly available” is currently a matter of interpretation. This exemption, however, applies only to the DPDP Act and does not obviate the need for ethics committee approval, institutional governance, cybersecurity safeguards, and compliance with any applicable rules.

Regulatory Clearances: SaMD and CDSCO

AI-enabled models that qualify as medical devices need regulatory clearance for use. Similar to the US FDA and the CE Mark, the CDSCO is the nodal body in India that issues regulatory licenses.

AI-enabled diagnostic models are classified as “Software as a Medical Device” (SaMD), software designed for medical purposes and not as a physical medical device. In India, these may fall under the Medical Device Rules, 2017, which divide them into four risk classes, with each higher class attracting greater legal and ethical scrutiny. Typical AI applications in radiology fall into risk categories B or C. 34

It is reasonable to assume that the implementation of these good practices, some of which may be outside a radiologist's day-to-day job, might require interdisciplinary teams that radiologists may need to work with. However, as radiologists are going to be using the AI tool at the interface with the patient, they must be aware of what to look for. This voluntary good-practice risk-mitigation approach aligns with India's approach. India's AI Governance Guidelines acknowledge both current laws and voluntary approaches such as industry frameworks (NASSCOM Playbook); ISO standards (e.g., ISO 42001 AI Management System Standard); and TEC standards (for Bias Assessment) as ways to mitigate risks from AI systems. 16

Future Directions

As AI in radiology rapidly evolves, its scope may soon be broadened by large language models and multimodal AI systems, which are capable of integrating imaging with lab parameters and clinical data. Adaptive models may emerge that can keep updating themselves continuously on local data and improve their performance over time. Radiologists and institutions should keep in mind that the legal and ethical standards governing the use of such AI models will also continue to evolve and mature with time.

Conclusion

The Indian healthcare AI ecosystem is rapidly evolving, with a growing regulatory framework and increasing public and private sector investment. For radiologists, the challenge lies in ensuring the safe, ethical, and accountable use of AI that complies with the legal system. There are multiple existing frameworks, such as the DPDP Act, SAHI, and ICMR Ethical Guidelines, etc., which have been discussed in this review. The onus is on individual physicians and institutions to be well informed and ensure adequate oversight.
