Embryo dysplasia in obese broiler breeders reduces hatchability: Comparison of high and low abdominal fat lines
Authors: Haixu Jiang, Bohan Cheng, Jia Zhao, Jiawen Yang, Shouzhi Wang, Zhiyong Su, Yumao Li, Peng Luan, Li Leng, Hui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hatchability is a key indicator of reproductive performance in broiler breeders. Obesity in broiler breeder hens results in a significant reduction in hatchability. Maternal obesity can impair embryonic development in mammals. However, whether maternal obesity reduces hatchability by compromising embryonic development remains unclear in birds. This study used the hens from 24th generation of the Northeast Agricultural University broiler lines divergently selected for abdominal fat content (NEAUHLF), along with their corresponding hatching eggs. Hatchability, embryo malposition rate, embryo malformation rate, and embryonic development profiles were compared between the two lines. The results showed that hatchability of the fat line was significantly lower than that of the lean line (72.39% vs. 63.64%, P < 0.05), whereas embryonic malposition (20.64% vs. 26.59%, P = 0.049) and malformation rates (0.27% vs. 7.29%, P < 0.001) were significantly higher in the fat line. Further analysis showed that embryos from the fat line were developmentally delayed compared with those from the lean line at stages HH2, HH9, HH24, HH40, and HH43. The present study generates embryonic development profiles for genetically fat and lean broilers, highlighting that maternal obesity in poultry reduces hatchability by inducing embryonic malposition, malformation, and stage-specific developmental delay. These findings provide embryological insights into the causes of obesity-induced decline in hatchability.

Introduction

Hatchability as a key indicator of reproductive performance in broiler breeders, is closely related to the economic benefits of the broiler industry. Embryonic abnormalities, including malformations, malpositions, and developmental retardation, significantly reduce hatchability (Bloom and Buss, 1966; Pearson and Herron, 1982; Fasenko et al., 2001). Obesity in broiler breeders has also been shown to reduce hatchability significantly (Zhang et al., 2018). The eggs derived from obese hens require a prolonged incubation period compared to those from normal hens, suggesting that maternal obesity may impede embryonic development (Bednarczyk et al., 1984). However, it remains unclear whether the reduced hatchability observed in obese broiler breeder hens is caused by abnormalities in embryonic development.

Studies in mammals indicate that parental obesity induces embryonic developmental delays. Compared with controls, high-fat diet-induced obese mice display a significant reduction in blastocyst rate and a significant retardation of development at both the 2-cell and blastocyst stages (Binder et al., 2012; Finger et al., 2015). Similarly, genetically obese pigs have been reported to show slower early embryonic development than their normal pigs (Gonzalez-Anover et al., 2011). Retarded embryonic development has also been observed in high-fat-diet-induced obese mares and in obese rabbit dams (Picone et al., 2011; D’Fonseca et al., 2021). Birds represent a key model organism, occupying an important evolutionary position between reptiles and mammals, and are widely used in studies of genetics, developmental biology, and related biological disciplines (Furlong, 2005; Siegel et al., 2006). However, the relationship between maternal obesity and embryonic development in avian species remains largely unexplored.

The Northeast Agricultural University broiler lines divergently selected for abdominal fat content (NEAUHLF) serve as a well-characterized genetically obese broiler model (Zhang et al., 2020). Our previous work demonstrated that hatchability in the fat line was lower than in the lean line across 14 to18 generations (G14 to G18) (Zhang et al., 2018). The aim of this study was to determine whether maternal obesity in poultry affects hatchability by impairing embryonic development, using the NEAUHLF as an obese model. In this study, hens from the G24 of NEAUHLF, along with their corresponding hatching eggs, were used to compare hatchability, embryonic malposition, embryonic malformation, and overall embryonic development. This study provides embryological insights into the causes of obesity-induced decline in hatchability.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The use of experimental animals in this study followed the "Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals" issued by the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People's Republic of China (Approval No. 2006-398). All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and the Biosafety Committee of the Northeast Agricultural University (Harbin, China).

Experimental animals and trait measurements

The broiler breeder hens used in this study were obtained from the NEAUHLF. These NEAUHLF lines have undergone divergent selection since 1996 based on abdominal fat percentage (AFP) values and very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) concentrations. Briefly, the G0 generation of NEAUHLF was derived from the same grandsire line originating from the Arbor Acres (AA) broiler. It was then divided into 2 lines based on their VLDL concentrations at 7 weeks of age. From G1 to G24, birds from each line were reared in 2 hatches, with ad libitum access to feed and water. Plasma VLDL concentrations were measured for all male birds at 7 weeks of age, and AFP of the male birds in the first hatch was measured following slaughter at 7 weeks of age. Sibling birds from families with lower (lean line) or higher (fat line) AFP than the population mean were selected as breeding candidates. Selection also considered body weight (BW) at 7 weeks of age and plasma VLDL concentration in males from the second hatch, as well as egg production in females from both hatches. The detailed selection protocol has been previously described (Guo et al., 2011).

The hatching eggs used in this study were collected from 320 broiler breeder females from the G24 of NEAUHLF. All birds were maintained under identical environmental conditions according to the AA Parent Stock Pocket Guide (Aviagen, 2024). Broiler breeder males were provided ad libitum access to feed and water from 0 to 7 weeks of age and were subjected to feed restriction from 8 to 53 weeks of age. Broiler breeder females were provided ad libitum access to feed and water from 0 to 2 weeks of age and were subjected to feed restriction from 3 to 53 weeks of age. At 53 weeks of age, the broiler breeder females were subjected to a 12-hour fasting period and then slaughtered (lean line: 116 females; fat line: 154 females). BW was recorded before slaughter; following slaughter, abdominal fat weight (AFW) was measured, and AFP (AFP = AFW/BW × 100%) was calculated.

Experimental design

At 42 weeks of age, hens from fat and lean lines were artificially inseminated, and hatching eggs were collected (lean line: 1,904 eggs; fat line: 1,895 eggs). Of these, 1,684 lean line eggs and 1,675 fat line eggs were used to evaluate hatching performance. For the evaluation of hatching performance, 300 eggs from AA broilers were used as controls. After incubation, dead embryos from fat and lean lines were collected to assess embryonic malposition and malformation. Additionally, according to Hamburger and Hamilton (1951) staging system (HH1 to HH45), 220 eggs from each line were randomly allocated into 45 groups, ensuring three available biological replicates in each group, for construction of the embryonic development atlas. At 53 weeks of age, the broiler breeder hens (lean line: 116 hens; fat line: 154 hens) were slaughtered, and BW, AFW, and AFP were recorded.

Analysis of hatching performance

To compare hatchability among the fat line, lean line, and commercial AA broilers. Artificial insemination was carried out in fat line and lean line broiler breeder females at 42 weeks of age, after which hatching eggs were collected (fat line: 1,675 eggs; lean line: 1,684 eggs). In parallel, 300 hatching eggs from AA parent stock were obtained from Yinong Poultry Industry Co., Ltd. (Harbin, China). All eggs were incubated under identical conditions at Shanhe Hatchery (Harbin, China). Incubation conditions were maintained as follows: 37.8°C and 60% relative humidity from embryonic day 1 to day 18, and 37.5°C and 70% relative humidity from day 19 to day 21. Eggs were turned every 2 hours. The candling was performed on incubation day 11 to record number of infertility eggs. On incubation day 21, the numbers of hatched chicks, healthy chicks, and infertile eggs were recorded. Fertility (number of fertile eggs / total number of eggs), hatchability of fertile eggs (number of hatched chicks/number of fertile eggs), hatchability of setting eggs (number of hatched chicks / total number of eggs set), and percentage of healthy chicks (number of healthy chicks / total number of hatched chicks) were calculated.

Quantification of embryonic malposition and malformation

Following completion of the aforementioned hatch protocol, all dead embryo eggs from the fat and lean lines were collected. The eggs containing blood rings or the presence of a small, undifferentiated embryo were classified as early embryonic death (Scott and Mackenzie, 1993). Each egg was opened at the blunt end, and the embryo was extracted. Embryos were categorized based on previously established criteria for malposition and malformation (Purohit et al., 1974; Fineman and Schoenwolf, 1987; Drake et al., 2006). Embryonic malposition rate (number of malpositioned embryos / total number of dead embryos) and embryonic malformation rate (number of malformed embryos / total number of dead embryos) were calculated.

Construction of the embryonic development atlas

Following artificial insemination of the fat and lean line broiler breeders, 220 fresh hatching eggs per line were collected and incubated in a Brinsea Ova-Easy-380 incubator (Brinsea, FL, UK). Incubation conditions were maintained as follows: 37.8°C and 60% relative humidity from embryonic day 1 to day 18, and 37.5°C and 70% relative humidity from day 19 to day 21. Eggs were turned every 2 hours. Morphological characteristics corresponding to each stage of the Hamburger and Hamilton (1951) staging system (HH stages) were used as reference criteria to construct embryonic development profiles for the two lines. At each HH stage (HH1-HH45), three healthy embryos per line were sampled as biological replicates, and their morphological features and developmental status were recorded.

For stages HH1–HH6, developmental progression was assessed based on the extent of the embryonic shield, primitive streak formation (initial, intermediate, and definitive streak), head process, and head fold. For stages HH7–HH14, developmental progression was assessed based on somite number, neural fold fusion, neural tube closure, head flexure, body rotation, and amniotic extension. For stages HH15–HH20, somite development, degree of head flexure, extent of body rotation, length of amniotic extension, size of wing and leg buds, ocular pigmentation, and allantoic morphological changes were used as indicators for embryonic developmental assessment. For stages HH21–HH24, developmental progression was evaluated using maxillary morphology and the length-to-width (L/W) ratios of wing and leg buds. For stages HH25–HH30, developmental progression was assessed based on elbow and knee joint differentiation, digit/toe morphology, and the number of nictitating membranes and scleral papillae. For stages HH31–HH35, developmental progression was evaluated using scleral papilla number, eyelid shape, and digit/toe differentiation. For stages HH36–HH39, developmental progression was assessed based on comb development, eyelid shape, beak length, and third toe length. For stages HH40–HH45, developmental progression was assessed based on beak length and third toe length.

Embryos were isolated as follows: the blunt end of each egg was opened, the yolk was gently transferred into ice-cold PBS, and the membrane covering the embryo was cut along the edge of the embryo and then peeled off. Embryos at stages HH1-HH30 were placed directly onto glass slides and imaged using a Leica M165FC stereomicroscope (Leica, HE, Germany). Embryos at stages HH31–HH45 were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24–48 h, rinsed with 70% ethanol, and stored at room temperature before imaging with a digital single-lens reflex camera (Li et al., 2019). Morphometric parameters, including wing and leg bud length and width, beak length, and third-toe length, were quantified using ImageJ v1.8.0 (NIH, MD, USA) (Hamburger and Hamilton, 1951). Image processing was performed using Adobe Photoshop CC 2019 (Adobe, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

For comparisons of BW, AFW, and AFP between the fat and lean line broiler breeder hens, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test was applied to assess normality, and Levene's test was used to evaluate homogeneity of variance. Data conforming to a normal distribution were analyzed by a two-tailed independent-samples t-test; otherwise, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Differences in fertility, hatchability of fertile eggs, hatchability of setting eggs, and percentage of healthy chicks among the fat line, lean line, and AA line were analyzed using chi-square tests, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons. Pairwise comparisons of embryonic malformation rate and embryonic malposition rate between the fat and lean lines were performed using chi-square tests. To compare embryonic wing- and leg-bud length-to-width ratios, embryonic beak length, and third-toe length between the fat and lean lines, Levene's test for equality of variances was first conducted to assess homogeneity of variance, followed by two-tailed independent-samples t-tests for inter-group comparisons. All data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM, NYC, USA); P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and P < 0.01 was considered highly significant.

At each HH stage, three morphologically normal embryos per line were analyzed as biological replicates. Inter-line comparisons were performed only when all three embryos within a given line exhibited consistent morphological features.

Discussion

Abnormal embryonic development significantly reduces hatchability (Bloom and Buss, 1966; Pearson and Herron, 1982; Fasenko et al., 2001). Our previous studies in G14–G18 of the NEAUHLF showed that hatchability was significantly lower in the fat line compared with the lean line (Zhang et al., 2018). This observation led to the hypothesis that broiler-breeder obesity reduces hatchability by inducing embryonic abnormalities. Using broiler breeder hens from G24 of NEAUHLF and their corresponding hatching eggs, this study confirmed that hatchability remains lower in the fat line. The fat line also shows significantly higher rates of embryonic malformation and malposition, along with delayed embryonic development at multiple stages. These findings indicate that obesity in broiler breeder hens diminishes hatchability through the induction of developmental anomalies.

When hatchability was compared among the fat line, lean line, and commercial AA broilers, both the fat and lean lines showed significantly lower hatchability of fertilized eggs and hatchability of setting eggs than the AA broiler (Table 2). Previous studies have shown that inbreeding significantly increases late embryonic mortality and reduces hatchability (Byerly et al., 1934). Marchesi et al. (2018) used genomic re-sequencing and runs of homozygosity (ROH) to estimate inbreeding in a non-sib-mated broiler line selected for 17 generations, and a high coefficient was obtained (FROH = 0.202 in G16), indicating that long-term selection can result in significant inbreeding even when inbreeding-avoidance protocols are implemented. The NEAUHLF population used in this study has undergone 24 generations of artificial selection, which may have led to inbreeding. We previously obtained pedigree and genome resequencing data from G23 of NEAUHLF and revealed high ROH-based inbreeding coefficients in both lines (fat line FROH = 0.223; lean line FROH = 0.227; unpublished data). It is proposed that inbreeding depression may contribute to the reduced hatchability observed in both selected lines relative to the AA broilers.

Increased incidence of malformation and malposition is closely associated with reduced hatchability (Pearson and Herron, 1982). In this study, the fat line showed significantly higher rates of malformation and malposition among dead embryos compared with the lean line (Table 3). These rates also exceeded those reported for Marshall broilers (malformation 5.11–6.72%; malposition 1.48–2.13%) (Pearson and Herron, 1982). These findings indicate that obesity may predispose broiler embryos to malformation and malposition.

Evidence from human and rodent models indicates that maternal obesity increases the risk of embryonic defects, including neural-tube, cardiovascular, and caudal anomalies (Kappen, 2013; Alcala et al., 2021), with neural-tube defects (NTDs) being particularly prevalent (Shaw et al., 1996). The neural tube, which serves as the primordium of the central nervous system, is formed through the fusion of the neural folds; failure of this process results in severe brain and craniofacial malformations (Kappen, 2013). Campbell et al. (1987) demonstrated that delayed neural-tube closure results in embryonic malformation in chickens. In this study, the development of the primitive streak and neural folds was delayed in fat-line embryos at HH2 and HH9 (Fig. 1; Appendix Table S1), indicating postponed neural-tube closure and a corresponding increase in NTDs. These findings suggest that obesity in broiler breeder hens may elevate embryonic malformation, particularly NTDs, through delayed neural-tube closure, reducing hatchability. The molecular mechanisms responsible for this delay remain to be clarified.

Embryonic malposition is generally attributed to a developmental delay that prevents the embryo from reaching the correct position before rotation is complete. Such malposition can impair gas exchange in late-stage embryos, resulting in embryonic death and a reduction in hatchability (Robertson, 1961; Wilson et al., 2003). This study showed that fat line embryos developed more slowly than lean line embryos at HH2, HH9, HH24, HH40, and HH43 (Appendix Table S1 and S2). These findings indicate that this developmental delay predisposes fat line embryos to malposition during late incubation, thus reducing hatchability.

Normal embryonic development is a key determinant of hatchability. Delayed embryonic development has been shown to significantly reduces hatchability in Japanese quail (Hyankova and Starosta, 2012). In this study, systematic morphological comparisons were employed between fat- and lean-line embryos at each developmental stage. At HH2 and HH9, fat-line embryos lagged behind lean-line embryos (Fig. 1; Appendix Table S1). The primitive streak and Hensen’s node are critical structures established during early avian development. The primitive streak defines the anterior–posterior axis, and its cells serve as precursors of the neural folds (Iimura et al., 2007; Voiculescu, 2020). Hensen’s node, the thickened anterior extremity of the streak, acts as a signaling center and provides progenitor cells for somite formation (Waddington, 1933; Kirby et al., 2003). At HH2, formation and elongation of both the primitive streak and Hensen’s node were delayed in fat-line embryos (Fig. 1; Appendix Table S1), likely contributing to the postponed neural-fold and somite development observed at HH9. The formation of these structures is closely associated with the number and status of blastoderm cells (Pokhrel et al., 2017). During blastulation, cells divide and migrate to form the three germ layers that give rise to all embryonic tissues (Spratt and Haas, 1967; Veini and Bellairs, 1983; Serrano Najera and Weijer, 2020). Studies in obese mouse models indicate that parental obesity increases gamete oxidative stress, impairs cleavage and differentiation of zygote, elevates the proportion of apoptotic cells in the blastocyst, and disrupts allocation of cells to the epiblast and inner cell mass, ultimately delaying further development (Finger et al., 2015; Han et al., 2018; Andreas et al., 2019). Studies in broilers have shown that accumulated oxidative stress products in hatching eggs directly impair early embryonic development (Tompkins et al., 2023). Elevated oxidative stress in broiler embryos triggers the accumulation of lipotoxic intermediates, such as ceramides and diacylglycerols, which directly induce lipotoxic injury (Surugihalli et al., 2019). This lipotoxic damage further causes mitochondrial injury, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, and mitochondrial dysfunction in stem cells from tissues and organs during embryonic development, leading to apoptosis and ultimately resulting in embryonic dysplasia (Ali et al., 2026). We hypothesized that maternal obesity may impair blastocyst cell status and number via oxidative stress, lipid toxicity and mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby delaying the formation of the primitive streak and Hensen's node and resulting in retarded early embryonic development in the fat line. Future studies are warranted to compare lipid and ROS levels in embryos from the fat and lean lines to verify this hypothesis.

At HH24, HH40, and HH43, development of the wing and leg buds and digital morphology was slower in fat-line embryos (Fig. 2, Fig. 4), and both third-toe length and beak length were significantly reduced (Appendix Table S2), indicating delayed skeletal development. In the chick embryo, the limb skeleton is established through chondrogenic and osteoblastic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cell populations beneath the ectoderm, and ossification drives longitudinal growth (Davey et al., 2018; Andreas et al., 2019). It was reported that parental obesity in rats modifies DNA methylation and histone modification patterns in limb mesenchymal stem cells, reducing their osteogenic potential and impairing skeletal development (Chen et al., 2012, 2022). This study proposes that a comparable epigenetic disruption of osteogenic differentiation may occur in mesenchymal stem cells of fat-line limb buds, resulting in delayed skeletal growth.

Maternal obesity can influence embryonic development by altering the transfer of nutrients to the embryo (Alvarez et al., 2020). Studies in humans, pigs, and sheep have shown that placentas from obese females exhibit abnormal fatty acid metabolism and nutrient transport, leading to markedly elevated free fatty acid concentrations in the embryo and consequent developmental impairment (Alvarez et al., 2020; Tian et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2010). For broilers, all nutrients required for embryonic development are derived entirely from the yolk and albumen within the hatching egg (Hartmann et al., 2003). Cahane et al. (1986) reported that hatching eggs from lean hens had greater egg weight, higher albumen dry matter content, and higher hatchability than those from fat hens. We speculate that the differences in embryonic development and hatchability between the fat and lean lines may be related to the nutritional composition of the hatching eggs. Future comparative studies on egg nutrient composition between the fat and lean lines could verify this hypothesis.

Jones et al. (2024) reported that cold-treated zebrafish embryos showed developmental delay specifically at 18 and 30 hours post-fertilization (hpf), while Alawathugoda et al. (2025) observed neural developmental retardation in high-fat-diet-induced obese rats only at embryonic days E14.5 and E18.5. These findings indicate that developmental retardation is not continuous but occurs in discrete intervals. This study revealed a similar pattern: fat-line embryos lagged behind lean-line embryos only at HH2, HH9, HH24, HH40, and HH43. These stages correspond to key developmental events, including Hensen’s node formation, neural-fold and somite formation, limb-bud formation, and digit formation (Charrier and Teillet, 1999; Tickle, 2015; Rothstein and Simoes-Costa, 2023). Oxidative stress-induced cellular damage is a recognized cause of developmental delay (Surai et al., 2016; Saputra et al., 2024), and maternal obesity is known to increase embryonic oxidative stress (Finger et al., 2015; Han et al., 2018). We propose that the episodic retardation observed in fat line embryos reflects transient repair responses to oxidative insults encountered at these critical developmental checkpoints. Following successful passage through each checkpoint, embryonic development in the fat line may proceed at a normal or potentially accelerated rate until the next key node is reached.

Conclusion

This study generates embryonic development profiles for genetically fat and lean broilers, highlighting that maternal obesity in poultry reduces hatchability by inducing embryonic malposition, malformation, and stage-specific developmental delay. These findings provide embryological insight into the causes of obesity-associated decline in hatchability and offer a reference framework for examining the relationship between maternal obesity and embryonic development in other species. Future studies integrating biochemical, molecular, or physiological mechanisms are needed to fully elucidate how maternal obesity leads to embryonic development dysplasia and reduced hatchability.
