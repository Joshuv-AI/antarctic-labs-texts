Numerical and Experimental Investigation of High-Pressure Hydrogen Jet Dispersion from Leakage at 70 MPa
Authors: Zhi Zhang, Shishuai Nie, Yujie Lin, Xinqi Zhang, Qi Cao, Kang Yu, Yi Liu, Anfeng Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-pressure hydrogen storage has become the mainstream technical route for fuel cell vehicles due to its high energy density and favorable economic efficiency, with operating pressures gradually increasing from 35 to 70 MPa. However, the complex shock wave structures formed during high-pressure leakage pose significant challenges to numerical simulation. Existing CFD models based on incompressible assumptions or low Mach number conditions are prone to numerical oscillations, nonconvergence, or even computational failure when dealing with the strong nonlinearity and solution discontinuities induced by intense shock waves. Conventional numerical schemes often face a dilemma: low-dissipation schemes may introduce nonphysical oscillations, whereas high-dissipation schemes tend to excessively smooth shock structures, leading to distorted flow characteristics and reduced prediction accuracy. In this study, a numerical model for high-pressure hydrogen jet diffusion is established by considering the functional relationship between the influence range of shock waves at discontinuities and the time step. Validated through 70-MPa schlieren experiments, the model predicts the Mach disk position with an error of 8.7%. The research indicates that local low-pressure zones as low as 25,000 Pa appearing in the flow field significantly reduce the critical autoignition temperature of hydrogen, and the flammable range is primarily concentrated at the wrinkled edges of the hydrogen cloud and near-wall “air entrapment zone.” These findings provide a quantitative basis for optimizing the placement of leak detection sensors and designing explosion venting solutions.

Introduction

The safety of hydrogen energy is one of the critical challenges hindering its widespread commercialization and utilization. Due to advantages such as high volumetric energy density, low loss rate, and cost-effectiveness, high-pressure hydrogen storage is extensively employed in hydrogen production plants, refueling stations, and hydrogen fuel cell vehicles. , For example, the high-pressure hydrogen storage tanks used in mass-produced fuel cell vehicles by Toyota Motor Corporation can reach pressures as high as 70 MPa. , However, the high-pressure storage approach also introduces risks of hydrogen leakage, and hydrogen storage systems typically undergo repeated refueling cycles, making the containers highly susceptible to fatigue damage under long-term cyclic loading. , Fundamentally different from leakage at atmospheric pressure, high-pressure hydrogen leakage involves an extremely complex transient physical process. Its most distinctive characteristic is the formation of an under-expanded jet and a series of intricate shock structures near the leakage source. , These shocks not only cause rapid expansion and mixing of hydrogen in the air but also pose significant challenges to conventional numerical simulation methods. Among these, the most critical issue is numerical divergence caused by shocks in the region close to the leakage source.

To address the aforementioned challenges, researchers and experts have conducted extensive studies. Li et al. conducted three-dimensional numerical simulations of high-pressure hydrogen jets at a Reynolds number on the order of 105 using the astroFoam solver developed based on OpenFOAM. By employing high-resolution shock-capturing schemes, refined mesh, and implicit time-advancement techniques, they successfully addressed numerical challenges caused by strong shock waves, high shear, and complex turbulent structures. The study revealed that, compared to nitrogen jets, hydrogen jets exhibit higher dispersion capacity and nozzle exit velocity due to their lower molecular weight, resulting in a unique flow field structure. Hourri et al. simulated the centerline concentration decay of horizontal hydrogen jets using Fluent software. They found that buoyancy effects cause significant bending of the gas cloud, influencing concentration decay behavior. In the momentum-dominated region, the behavior of hydrogen jets conforms to classical similarity laws. Asahara et al. employed highly robust numerical methods such as AUSMDV to successfully resolve numerical difficulties caused by strong shocks and rapid expansion in under-expanded hydrogen jet simulations, effectively avoiding computational divergence. They captured Mach disk structures and periodic high-concentration hydrogen clouds, revealing a correlation between concentration and velocity distributions. Zhang et al. proposed a hydrogen jet velocity prediction method based on concentration measurements. Using PIV technology and CFD simulations for validation, they demonstrated the derivation of flow velocity distribution from centerline mass fraction, providing a practical solution for risk assessment. Mohammadpour et al. combined large eddy simulation (LES) and proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) to achieve efficient simulation of low-temperature hydrogen leakage. Their research showed that fire risk assessment based on 10-mode POD results was highly consistent with LES results (deviation of 2.1%–3.3%). Yao et al. compared the characteristics of low-temperature hydrogen and methane jets using a laser Raman scattering system. They developed a specialized denoising algorithm and a two-component PDF model, providing a theoretical basis for risk assessment of low-temperature hydrogen storage systems.

As a result, most numerical models lack experimental benchmarks highly consistent with real engineering conditions, restricting their practical applicability and reliability.

To address these issues, this study establishes a functional relationship between the influence range of shock discontinuities and the time step and proposes a high-precision shock-capturing algorithm with dynamically optimized numerical dissipation coefficients based on wave speed and time step. By adaptively adjusting the dissipation coefficient, the algorithm overcomes the difficulty of conventional schemes in simultaneously achieving stability and resolution in strong discontinuity regions. Meanwhile, high-precision schlieren experiments are conducted under the 70-MPa ultrahigh-pressure condition, and the numerically predicted Mach disk locations are quantitatively compared with experimental measurements, providing solid data support for the effectiveness and engineering applicability of the proposed algorithm. Based on the experimentally validated numerical model, the distributions of velocity, pressure, and species concentration near the leakage source are systematically analyzed. The significant effect of local low-pressure zones on the autoignition sensitivity of hydrogen is revealed, which can provide theoretical and technical support for the safety design and risk prevention of high-pressure hydrogen storage systems.

High-Pressure Hydrogen Jet Dispersion Model

Fundamental Assumptions

Considering the low density and viscosity characteristics of hydrogen, any leakage in a green hydrogen production facility will lead to rapid mixing with air, forming a flammable gas cloud. This significantly alters the fluid dynamics and thermophysical properties within the affected area. The hydrogen leakage and dispersion process is accompanied by transient changes in the distribution of motion and physical properties among fluid components in space. This section establishes a mathematical model describing this complex flow process by analyzing the mass transfer, momentum exchange, and energy variation laws of the mixed gas and its components during hydrogen leakage. The fundamental assumptions are as follows.(1)The mixture of hydrogen and air undergoes an unsteady three-dimensional flow in space, with the flow parameters of each component treated as continuous and differentiable functions in both spatial and temporal domains.(2)Hydrogen exhibits high-speed jet motion near the leakage source. The pressure difference between the high-pressure storage tank and the atmospheric pressure exerts a far greater influence on the motion parameters of the hydrogen-air mixture than body forces such as buoyancy and gravity. Therefore, the effect of body forces on fluid motion is neglected.(3)After discretization of the governing equations, any physical quantity field is continuous within individual grid cells and piecewise continuous across the computational domain.

High-Precision Capture Algorithm for Strong Discontinuities in High-Pressure Hydrogen Jet Flow Fields

This study employs the finite volume method to achieve transient calculations of the thermophysical properties and flow characteristics of the gas mixture within the computational domain. Specifically, the solution domain is first discretized into a series of control volumes. The overall evolution of the domain is then indirectly characterized by the flow and physical parameters at the center of each grid cell.

Since the pressure in hydrogen storage tanks is typically much higher than atmospheric pressure, the jet velocity near the leakage source may approach or even exceed the speed of sound, leading to strong discontinuities in physical quantities. Consequently, the consideration of weak solutions becomes necessary. Traditional methods based on Taylor series expansion (e.g., central discretization schemes) fail in such scenarios, and higher-order expansions tend to exacerbate numerical oscillations near discontinuities.

To address this issue, Godunov-type numerical schemes construct a piecewise constant distribution of physical quantities at time level tn , leverage the hyperbolic nature of the Navier–Stokes equations to solve the Riemann problem between tn and tn +1, and subsequently update the physical quantities at tn +1 . This approach ultimately computes the numerical fluxes at cell interfaces, thereby avoiding numerical oscillations near discontinuities.

In essence, the Lax–Friedrichs method is a Godunov-type scheme that accounts for the influence range of shocks during the time interval [tn , tn +1 ]. However, it introduces excessive numerical dissipation, which can lead to significant inaccuracies in the solution when grid resolution is coarse or time steps are small. To overcome this limitation, this work introduces a functional relationship between the influence range of shocks at discontinuities and the time step size. An optimal numerical dissipation coefficient is determined based on the wave speed and time step, and the Kurganov and Tadmor method is applied to construct the numerical fluxes.

This algorithm decouples the acoustic modes and material transport modes in compressible flow, achieving synchronous advancement of various physical quantities through the construction of special flux formats in a single step, thereby improving computational efficiency while ensuring shock-capturing accuracy. On the other hand, this algorithm does not involve the iterative corrections and convergence processes required for pressure–velocity coupling terms in algorithms like SIMPLE, thus avoiding divergence phenomena caused by improper relaxation factor selection and significantly reducing sensitivity to time step size.

It should be noted that although the explicit-implicit hybrid algorithm adopted in this study decouples the acoustic and material transport modes, avoiding the iterative process of pressure–velocity coupling and thereby improving single-step computational efficiency and enhancing robustness, its stability is still constrained by the CFL condition associated with the explicitly treated part. In the high-pressure jet simulations of this study, the extremely high flow velocities in the near field become the primary factor limiting the maximum time step size. In contrast, fully implicit or fully coupled methods (such as those based on the SIMPLE family or PISO algorithm), while requiring multiple iterations per time step, allow for significantly larger time steps and even steady-state calculations. However, for high-pressure jet problems involving strong transient shock evolution, excessively large time steps can smear important flow details, which is detrimental to accuracy. The algorithm in this paper, while strictly adhering to the CFL condition, updates all physical quantities simultaneously within a single step. It trades a smaller time step for high-fidelity capture of transient processes. The overall computational cost is acceptable for the near-field shock and turbulent mixing problems that are the focus of this study, with clear advantages in accuracy. Future research could explore dual time-stepping methods based on fully implicit schemes to further relax stability restrictions.

Experimental Study on 70-MPa High-Pressure Hydrogen Jet

This study systematically conducted schlieren experiments on 70-MPa high-pressure hydrogen jets, aligning with the maximum pressure-bearing capacity of current international commercial onboard hydrogen storage cylinders (70 MPa level). The experiments focused on analyzing the release characteristics of high-pressure hydrogen under sudden leakage conditions, including key features such as jet development processes and shock wave formation mechanisms. Complete flow field data covering the initial to stable development stages of the jet were obtained. These high-precision experimental measurements provide a reliable validation benchmark for the numerical model of high-pressure hydrogen jet diffusion established in this study and offer critical data support for the safety design of high-pressure hydrogen systems.

Experimental Setup

As shown in Figure (a), the experiment primarily utilized the 140-MPa high-pressure injection system at the Haiyang Experimental Base of SINOPEC Safety Engineering Research Institute Co., Ltd., a Luwei schlieren system, XEN-5320 concentration sensors, and various types of nozzles. The 140-MPa high-pressure injection system enabled a jet pressure of 70 MPa, the schlieren system allowed precise measurement of the structure and velocity of the high-pressure hydrogen jet field, and the concentration sensors provided accurate measurement of the hydrogen concentration along the jet axis.

Schematic diagram of the schlieren experimental system and experimental procedure.

As shown in Figure (b), a 5 W LED light source was employed as the point light source during the experiment. The beam emitted from the point light source passed through an aperture to form a diverging light path. A mirror (Mirror 1) was installed 3 m from the aperture to convert the light path into a parallel beam after reflection. Variations in light and dark patterns caused by density gradients of the released gas were observed and recorded within the parallel beam region. Another mirror (Mirror 2) was positioned behind the observation area, and a blade was horizontally placed 3 m from Mirror 2 (i.e., at the focal point of Mirror 2). The parallel beam was focused onto the blade after reflection by Mirror 2, and the height of the blade could be adjusted to modulate the light intensity received by the camera, thereby altering the contrast of the schlieren images. A high-speed camera was installed behind the blade to capture the schlieren images and synchronously transmit the data to a computer.

Model-Experimental Comparison Results

To validate the accuracy of the numerical model, the near-field shock structures predicted by the simulation are compared with the schlieren experimental results under the same working conditions. A circular orifice nozzle with a diameter of d = 1.0 mm is used in the experiments. Hydrogen is discharged from the storage tank into the ambient atmosphere (ambient pressure 101325 Pa) at a stagnation pressure of 70 MPa. A high-speed schlieren system is applied to record the flow images when the jet reaches a quasi-steady state. Accordingly, the numerical simulation employs exactly the same geometric dimensions, boundary conditions, and initial parameters.

As shown in Figure , the axial position of the Mach disk from the nozzle exit observed experimentally is approximately 46.8 mm, while the position predicted by the numerical simulation is approximately 51.3 mm, yielding a relative error of about 8.7%. Possible reasons for this error mainly include the following aspects: First, although the finite volume method and TVD scheme with the Van Leer limiter employed in the numerical simulation effectively suppress oscillations, they inevitably introduce a certain degree of numerical dissipation, which may cause a slight shift in the shock wave position. Second, the simulation assumes hydrogen as an ideal gas and adopts the k-ε turbulence model, whereas subtle differences may exist between the real gas effects (such as intermolecular forces) of actual high-pressure hydrogen and the instabilities of the jet boundary layer compared to the model’s simplified assumptions, also affecting the precise prediction of the shock wave position.

Comparison of simulated shock structures and experimental schlieren images.

Nevertheless, the relative error of 8.7% is well within the tolerance range permitted for engineering risk analysis. Moreover, the simulation results successfully capture the formation location of the Mach disk and the overall morphology of the shock waves, demonstrating high agreement with the flow field characteristics observed experimentally. Therefore, the high-resolution shock-capturing algorithm established in this paper can effectively simulate the near-field jet behavior of 70-MPa high-pressure hydrogen leaks, providing a reliable numerical tool for subsequent predictions of flammable cloud ranges and optimization of sensor placement.

Numerical Simulation Analysis of 70-MPa High-Pressure Hydrogen Jet

Based on the high-pressure hydrogen jet diffusion numerical algorithm established in Sections –2.2.3, this study focuses on the 70-MPa high-pressure hydrogen leakage scenario, with emphasis on analyzing key physical processes such as the evolution of under-expanded jet structures, shock wave formation and development, turbulent mixing characteristics, and flammable cloud distribution. The numerical simulation successfully captured typical flow field structures, including the formation of Mach disks, wave characteristics in the jet expansion zone, and the influence of vortex ring structures on the mixing process. It also revealed the significant impact of local low-pressure zones and wrinkled edges of hydrogen clouds on combustion sensitivity.

Initial Conditions

In this section, numerical simulations and analyses are conducted for the typical scenario of 70-MPa high-pressure hydrogen leakage into the atmosphere through a micro-orifice. The computational domain is a two-dimensional axisymmetric model as shown in Figure , with a radial dimension of 0.2 m and an axial length of 1.0 m to ensure sufficient development of the jet. The diameter of the leakage orifice (nozzle) is set as d = 1.0 mm, which is consistent with the dimension adopted in the experimental validation in Chapter 5. The left side of the computational domain is set as the pressure inlet boundary representing the leakage source with a total pressure of 70 MPa; the right and upper sides are set as pressure outlet boundaries with a pressure of 101,325 Pa (ambient atmosphere); and the axis is set as the symmetry boundary. To avoid numerical divergence caused by directly imposing the extremely high pressure of 70 MPa at the inlet, an inlet section with a length of 1 m is arranged between the leakage orifice and the ambient environment. The initial pressure of this inlet section is set as 70 MPa, and its pressure and velocity distributions can dynamically adjust with the evolution of the downstream flow field, allowing the upstream propagation of pressure waves, so as to physically smooth the sharp pressure gradient between the high-pressure region and the ambient environment in a more reasonable manner and improve computational stability. The computational mesh is locally refined near the leakage orifice and downstream regions where shock waves may occur, with a minimum mesh size of 0.02 mm. At the initial moment, the entire computational domain is filled with stationary air at a pressure of 0.1 MPa and a temperature of 300 K.

Analysis of Jet Flow Field Characteristics

Figure shows the distribution pattern of the jet velocity field. Combined with Figure (c), it can be observed that the fluid rapidly expands and its velocity sharply increases after being ejected from the nozzle due to pressure release, forming a hypersonic flow with a Mach number far greater than 1. Simultaneously, the pressure and temperature significantly decrease (as shown in Figure ). Subsequently, a normal shock wave, known as a Mach disk, forms at the end of the expansion zone. The airflow pressure increases to atmospheric pressure while the velocity decreases, transitioning to subsonic flow.

Computational domain for numerical simulation of 70-MPa high-pressure hydrogen jet.

Contour plots of key parameter distributions for 70-MPa high-pressure hydrogen jet numerical simulation.

Figure (b) shows the velocity distribution along the radial direction AB. It can be seen that the velocity generally follows a Gaussian distribution along the jet radial direction, but two symmetric secondary peaks form on either side of the main velocity peak. The reason for this lies in the shear instability during gas release (i.e., the velocity difference between the high-speed jet and the surrounding stationary or low-speed fluid). The boundary layer curls and evolves into large-scale, orderly ring-shaped vortex structures. Each vortex ring induces motion in the surrounding fluid. Inside the vortex ring structure (closer to the jet axis), the rotation induces a radial velocity away from the axis, which limits the development of the axial velocity. Outside the vortex ring structure (farther from the axis), the rotation induces a radial velocity toward the axis, which increases the velocity of the mixed fluid at the boundary positions. This process is the core mechanism of jet mixing with the surrounding fluid. Through this induction, vortex rings continuously “entrain” external fluid into the jet, causing the jet width to gradually expand and the axial velocity to gradually decay.

Analysis of Jet Mixing/Combustion and Explosion Characteristics

As shown in Figure , during the expansion and jetting of high-pressure hydrogen in the channel, significant pressure fluctuations and even local low-pressure zones appear in the flow field due to complex wave system structures and turbulent mixing. Figure (b) shows that the minimum pressure drops to 25,000 Pa, which is below atmospheric pressure. This phenomenon has important safety implications: existing research indicates that when the pressure drops below 50,000 Pa, the critical autoignition temperature of hydrogen begins to decrease. , For instance, G.R. Astbury et al. noted in their review that a pressure reduction significantly lowers the ignition temperature of hydrogen, meaning that hydrogen is more easily ignited by hot surfaces in low-pressure environments. Under atmospheric pressure (101.3 kPa), the autoignition temperature of a stoichiometric hydrogen-air mixture is 773–850 K, whereas at low pressures of 20–50 kPa, ignition can occur at temperatures as low as 620 K (347 °C). Zhang et al. experimentally investigated the lower flammable limit (LFL) of hydrogen–air mixtures in the pressure range of 50–250 kPa, and clearly concluded that a decrease in initial pressure reduces the LFL of hydrogen. This indicates that hydrogen is more prone to forming flammable mixtures and undergoing deflagration under low pressures. This implies that the occurrence of local low-pressure regions significantly increases the combustion sensitivity of hydrogen–air mixtures.

In the hydrogen volume fraction distribution (Figure (a)), distinct wrinkled structures can be clearly observed at the edges of the hydrogen cloud, which is a direct manifestation of turbulent mixing and reflects the nonuniform mixing characteristics of hydrogen and air. Additionally, the flow structure near the wall is particularly complex: the hydrogen cloud compresses the adjacent air to form a laminar thin layer, while in some areas, the gas is trapped between the hydrogen cloud and the wall, forming an “air entrapment zone.” The laminar thin layer may inhibit heat and mass dispersion, leading to local overheating; meanwhile, the trapped air zone, due to hydrogen penetration and pressure oscillations, is more prone to forming locally concentrated combustible mixtures that meet ignition conditions.

Figure further extracts the combustible range where the hydrogen volume fraction is between 4% and 75%. The results show that this region is mainly concentrated near the wrinkled edges of the hydrogen cloud and the near-wall air entrapment zone. Combined with the above analysis, the pressure drop lowers the ignition threshold, the wrinkled hydrogen cloud and near-wall structures amplify local concentration disturbances, and the coupling of density and temperature accelerates energy and mass transport. The synergistic effect of these factors greatly increases the possibility of the mixture transitioning from “combustible” to “highly flammable.” Therefore, in practical engineering applications, the risk of autoignition can be reduced by optimizing channel geometry (avoiding concave corner structures that can trap gas, adopting smooth wall transitions to reduce vortex scale) and regulating flow conditions (adding flow control devices or optimizing jet parameters to suppress large-scale wrinkles and reduce the intensity of turbulent mixing).

Conclusion

The high-pressure hydrogen leakage jet dispersion process involves complex under-expanded jet structures, shock wave evolution, and turbulent mixing phenomena, placing extremely high demands on numerical simulation methods. To address the numerical oscillations and divergence issues caused by strong discontinuities near the leakage source, this study developed a high-precision algorithm based on an adaptive relationship between the shock influence range and the time step. By introducing flux limiters and optimizing the numerical dissipation coefficient, the algorithm effectively achieves accurate capture of the coupled physical processes of shock waves and turbulence, demonstrating excellent stability and precision. Schlieren experiments of a 70-MPa high-pressure hydrogen jet were conducted, and the results show that this method can accurately capture key flow field structures such as Mach disks, aligning well with experimental measurements.
