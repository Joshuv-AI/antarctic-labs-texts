A Cross-Sectional Study: Dentists’ Knowledge, Attitudes, and Perceptions of the Role of Plasma-Rich Protein and its Applications in Tooth Movement Rate
Authors: I Bevin Shaga, Bharathi Elangovan, Acchuthakrishnan, J Devika, K. Anitha, SMM Moulvi, Nagappan Nagappan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:This study aimed to assess dentists’ perceptions and knowledge of plasma-rich protein and uses in rate of tooth movement.Material and Methods:This online survey, which is cross-sectional in nature, is being done among dental care providers. The 500 participants’ self-administered questionnaires on PRP in orthodontic tooth mobility. The statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) (V 22.0). The frequency distribution was calculated.Results:The survey revealed that, 452 (90.4%) were aware of the PRP preparation process, and 400 (20%) were aware of the use of plasma-rich protein (PRP) to accelerate tooth movement. Regarding the primary PRP ingredients and the PRP procedure for quickening tooth movement in orthodontics, 66.4% had given the right responses. The majority of dentists were sufficiently knowledgeable about both invasive and non-invasive methods for quickening the movement of teeth.Conclusion:The study’s conclusions demonstrated that dental professionals were aware of the most recent developments in dental PRP application and its protocols, but additional research has to be done to educate everyone about these recent developments. Future experimental research on PRP in dental practices will be necessary in order to further develop our awareness of it.

INTRODUCTION

Patients often refuse orthodontic treatments because they find them to be inconvenient, which is a big deterrent. The levelling and correcting of dental crowding is one such laborious and common therapy that can take up to eight months.[1] Adult individuals are now seeking orthodontic procedures at a growing rate. As a result, orthodontic tooth movement is accelerating to address longer treatment times.[2]

Marx (2004)[3] described platelet-rich plasma as a “autologous concentration of platelets in a small volume of plasma.” PRP has 5% of RBCs, 1% of WBCs, and 94% of platelets, whereas peripheral blood contains 6% of platelets, <1% of white blood cells, and 94% of red blood cells.[4] PRP can be prepared using a variety of technologies, and several authors have employed different techniques to synthesise PRP. It is made by centrifuging a patient’s complete blood twice: First to separate the components of the patient’s blood, and again to produce the final product, platelet-rich plasma, which is a main source in autologous growth factors.[5] The higher concentration of several growth factors present in platelet-rich plasma is responsible for its broad therapeutic applications in dentistry. PRP has been shown to contain the following growth factors (GFs): Transforming growth factors-β. (TGF-β), insulin growth factor-1, fibroblast growth factor, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and vascular endothelial growth factor.[6]

According to a study by Ahmed El-Timamy[7] on the influence of platelet-rich plasma on the rate of orthodontic tooth movement, PRP showed a promising ability to increase the rate of tooth movement when injected within the initial two months of the orthodontic therapy. If PRP injections are given frequently to maintain a constant rate of increasing tooth movement, more study is required. Even though it’s the newest technology, not many dentists are familiar with it. Although the current study shows that dental professionals have an appropriate degree of information, more has to be done to inform people about the most recent advancements in PRP treatment for rapid orthodontic tooth movement.

METHODOLOGY

The study design involved recruiting a sample of general and specialty dentists practicing in Tamil Nadu for a cross-sectional descriptive study. 500 people make up the sample size (95% power and 5% alpha error), which was established based on earlier publications of related studies. Participants in the study were dentists who were practicing in Tamil Nadu and held dental degrees such as a Bachelor of Dental Surgery, Master of Dental Surgery, or Doctor of Philosophy. Participants were those who were willing to offer informed consent and were interested in taking part. People who were unwilling to offer informed consent and had no interest in participating were not allowed to participate. April 2024 to July 2024 was the planned period for data collecting. Using an online Google Form, a pretested, self-administered questionnaire was used to gauge the use of PRP in accelerated tooth movement among dentists. After receiving a brief explanation of the study’s goals and objectives through the use of an information documents, the dentist used an online Google Form to express their informed consent. Only completely completed questionnaire forms were included in the study.

RESULTS

Complete data were entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, and the SPSS software (Version 18.0) was used to analyse the frequency distribution data (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Of the 500 dentists in the study, 285 participants (57%) were men and 215 participants (43%) were women. Around 500 dentist involved in the research, 216 (43.2%) had experience ranging from 0 to 10 years, 182 (36.4%) from 10 to 20 years, and 102 (20.4%) from 20 to 30 years in the dentistry field. Of the 500 participants in the research, 466 (93.2%) were aware that PRP can help accelerate tooth movement, while 34 (6.8%) were unaware of this. Among the 500 study subjects, 452 (90.4%) aware of the procedure for PRP preparation, whereas 48 (9.6%) had no idea about the standardisation. 32 (66.4%) had provided the appropriate answers on the main PRP components and the PRP process in terms of accelerating the movement of teeth in orthodontics.

When participants are asked if they are aware of any other techniques for accelerating tooth movement, of the 500 study participants, 307 (61.4%) participants were aware of invasive procedures such as corticotomy, piezocision, and micro-osteoperforations, while 100 (20%) dentists were aware of non-invasive procedures such as low-level laser therapy, cyclic forces, photobiomodulation, and biomechanical procedures (e.g., self-ligating brackets). The remaining 93 (18.6%) dentists were unaware of any methods.

DISCUSSION

This study established to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of 500 Indian dentists on the use of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) to accelerate orthodontic tooth movement. At first, the focus was on experience and demographic data among the 500 dentists that took part in the research. The majority of the 216 dentists are young (43.2%) and had between 0 and 10 years of experience.

In the study, 48 participants (9.6%) were unaware of the standardisation, while 452 (90.4%) were aware of the PRP preparation process. PRP has proven to have a broad range of applications and advantages across all dental fields of study.[8] Prior studies have demonstrated that PRP preparation techniques have evolved into a range of approaches with the shared objective of standardising the PRP preparation procedure. In the early stages of PRP preparation, 10 mL of 10% calcium chloride and 10,000 units of bovine thrombin were combined to activate PRP.[9] Because of the demonstrated coagulopathy caused by the cross-reactivity of human factor V antibodies with anti-bovine factor V antibodies, activation with bovine thrombin is no longer recommended. Consequently, the use of autologous thrombin or calcium chloride alone was recommended.[10]

Leukocyte PRP (L-PRP) injection was employed by Nakornnoi et al. in their study as a means of accelerating OTM. At every observation time, the L-PRP group’s rate of OTM increased cumulatively in comparison with the control group, reaching a 1.2-fold higher rate on the day 21.[11] This discrepancy was caused by leukocytes in the L-PRP, which in the early stages of OTM cause a burst release of pro-inflammatory cytokines that act as an initiator for molecular and cellular processes.[12]

While 100 dentists (20%) were aware of non-invasive procedures like low-level laser therapy, cyclic forces, photobiomodulation, and biomechanical procedures (e.g., self-ligating brackets), 307 (61.4%) participants were aware of invasive procedures like corticotomy, piezocision, and micro-osteoperforations. Ninety-three dentists (18.6%) were not aware of any techniques. Nevertheless, non-invasive techniques do not result in this kind of alveolar bone loss. Biological supplements, such as vitamin D metabolites, relaxin hormones, and prostaglandin cytokines, are thus utilised to attain commensurately effective biological responses from the least invasive techniques. However, employing allogenic goods or additional hormones can result in unwanted systemic adverse effects and necessitate regular injections of the supplement.[13141516]

According to this study, dental practitioners knew that PRP might be used to accelerate tooth movement, but they were still unaware of the criteria, ethical concerns, and collecting procedure. Dental practitioners must learn how to utilise them effectively, and patients must be made aware of them, in order for their use in dentistry to be possible.

CONCLUSION

Although the study’s findings showed that dental professionals understood the current advancements in dental PRP application and its protocols, there is still a need to increase public awareness of these breakthroughs. It was shown that injecting platelet-rich plasma during the levelling and aligning phase of orthodontic treatment may expedite the movement of teeth in those patients. The vast majority of research participants said they would like to attend conferences or programs related to CDE in the near future. This could be explained by the increasing number of studies and applications in the sector, as well as a potential lack of comprehensive knowledge on dental PRPs. To improve knowledge about PRP in dental practices, several experimental studies will be required in the future.
