Prime One-Sided Ideals in Principal Ideal Domains
Masood Aryapoor

Abstract
This article investigates various notions of primeness for one-sided ideals in noncommutative rings, with a focus on principal ideal domains.



1
Introduction

The concept of primeness in ring theory has long served as a cornerstone for understanding the structural properties of rings and their ideals. While the classical notion of prime ideals in commutative rings is well-established, extending this idea to noncommutative settings introduces a rich landscape of possibilities and challenges.
This article explores various generalizations of prime ideals to the realm of one-sided ideals, particularly within the framework of principal ideal domains (PIDs).

In Section
2
, we begin by briefly revisiting foundational definitions such as completely prime and prime two-sided ideals, and then review four distinct notions of primeness for one-sided ideals: extremely prime, completely prime, structurally prime, and weakly prime. We examine each notion through rigorous definitions, illustrative examples, and key theorems that highlight their algebraic significance and interrelations. Section
3
provides several descriptions of one-sided primness for principal ideal domains. In particular, the results demonstrate how properties such as irreducibility,
invariance, and boundedness govern the behavior of left ideals in PIDs. Our findings indicate that the notions of extremely prime, structurally prime and weakly prime ideals are primarily governed by the presence of invariant and bounded elements, whereas the concept of completely prime one-sided ideals is more closely associated with irreducibility. Although our primary focus is on PIDs, it is worth noting that some of our results hold for the more general class of 2-firs.

In this article, all rings are assumed to be associative and unital, but not necessarily commutative.

2
Prime One-Sided Ideals

In this section, we review several concepts of primness for both two-sided ideals and one-sided ideals. For early foundational work on these concepts, see the article by Duan
[
7
]
.

2.1
Prime ideals

We begin by a short discussion about the notion of primeness for two-sided ideals. Recall that a proper two-sided ideal
{\swabfamily p}
in a commutative ring
R
is called prime if for
a,b\in R
,
ab\in{\swabfamily p}
implies
a\in{\swabfamily p}
or
b\in{\swabfamily p}
, or equivalently, the quotient ring
R/{\swabfamily p}
is a domain. Generalizing this definition to the noncommutative case leads to the following definition, which was already pointed out by H. Fitting in 1935
[
9
]
:

Definition 2.1
.

An ideal
{\swabfamily p}
in a ring
R
is said to be
completely prime
if
{\swabfamily p}\neq R
, and for
a,b\in R
,
ab\in{\swabfamily p}
implies
a\in{\swabfamily p}
or
b\in{\swabfamily p}
, or equivalently, the quotient ring
R/{\swabfamily p}
is a domain.

In
[
20
]
, N. H. McCoy commented that this notion is not useful since “a noncommutative ring seldom contains very many completely prime ideals”. For example, a simple ring with zero-divisors has no completely prime ideals. However, reduced rings (i.e., rings without nonzero nilpotent elements) have enough completely prime ideals in the sense that the intersection of all the completely prime ideals in a reduced ring is the zero ideal. More generally, completely prime ideals can be used to characterize reduced ideals. Recall that a two-sided ideal
{\swabfamily A}
in a ring
R
is called
reduced
if, for
a\in R
and
n\geq 1
,
a^{n}\in{\swabfamily A}
implies
a\in{\swabfamily A}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

An ideal
{\swabfamily A}
of a ring
R
is reduced iff
{\swabfamily A}
can be written as an intersection of completely prime ideals in
R
.

Proof.

The case
{\swabfamily A}=\{0\}
is due to Andrunakievich and Ryabukhin
[
2
]
(For other proofs of this result, see
[
12
]
and
[
15
, Theorem 12.7]
). The general case follows immediately by applying the special case to the ring
R/{\swabfamily A}
. See also
[
15
, p. 195]
.
∎

We now turn to the standard definition of a prime ideal:

Definition 2.2
.

An ideal
{\swabfamily p}
in a ring
R
is said to be
prime
if
{\swabfamily p}\neq R
, and for ideals
{\swabfamily A},{\swabfamily B}
in
R
,
{\swabfamily A}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
implies
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
or
{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
.

In
[
20
]
, N. H. McCoy initiated the systematic study of this notion and related concepts. In particular, he obtained several characterizations of prime ideals
[
20
, Theorem 1]
. It is evident that every completely prime ideal is a prime ideal. Moreover, any ring has at least one prime ideal as every maximal ideal is prime. An important result is that a two-sided ideal
{\swabfamily C}
in a ring
R
is semiprime (i.e.,
{\swabfamily A}^{2}\subseteq{\swabfamily C}
implies
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily C}
, for every ideal
{\swabfamily A}
of
R
) iff
{\swabfamily C}
can be written as an intersection of prime ideals. For a detailed discussion of prime ideals and related concepts, we refer the reader to Lam’s book
[
15
, Chapter 4]
.

Remark 2.1
.

We have mentioned two of the most studied notions of prime ideals in the noncommutative setting. However, other notions of prime ideals also appear in the literature; for example, the notion of a strongly prime ideal, which is motivated by the concept of pseudo-valuation rings
[
11
]
. For another generalization of primeness, see
[
22
]
.

2.2
Extremely Prime Left Ideals

The first notion of a prime one-sided ideal is obtained by generalizing the concept of a completely prime ideal:

Definition 2.3
.

A left ideal
{\swabfamily p}
in a ring
R
is said to be
extremely prime
if
{\swabfamily p}\neq R
, and and for
a,b\in R
,
ab\in{\swabfamily p}
implies
a\in{\swabfamily p}
or
b\in{\swabfamily p}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Some authors use the term “completely prime” instead of “extremely prime”. Here, we prefer the phrase “extremely prime”, suggested by Reyes
[
23
]
, and reserve the former phrase for another notion, introduced and studied by Reyes
[
23
]
.

A disadvantage of this concept is that there exist rings that have no completely prime one-sided ideals. Examples of such rings are simple rings with nontrivial idempotents, e.g. matrix rings over division rings, (for a proof of this, see
[
23
, Proposition 2.4]
). It is worth noting that the condition defining the notion is left-right symmetric, which does not reflect the fact that it concerns one-sided ideals. Although the notion of an extremely prime left ideal does not seem particularly useful, we remark that in
[
3
]
, Andrunakievich utilized this notion to derive a criterion for determining whether a ring is isomorphic to a subdirect product of division rings.

2.3
Completely Prime Left Ideals

In
[
23
]
, M.M. L. Reyes introduced and studied a new notion of a prime one-sided ideal, which he termed “completely prime”. He argued that completely prime one-sided ideals control the “one-sided structure of a ring”. Let us first review the concept of a completely prime left ideal.

Definition 2.4
.

A left ideal
{\swabfamily p}
in a ring
R
is said to be
completely prime
if
{\swabfamily p}\neq R
, and for
a,b\in R
,
ab\in{\swabfamily p}
and
{\swabfamily p}b\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
imply
a\in{\swabfamily p}
or
b\in{\swabfamily p}
.

To demonstrate that the concept of a completely prime one-sided ideal is a suitable tool for studying the one-sided structure of a ring, Reyes proved, among other things, a noncommutative version of Cohen’s Theorem (
[
23
, Theorem 3.8]
):

Theorem 2.2
.

A ring
R
is left noetherian iff every completely prime left ideal of
R
is finitely generated.

He also obtained a description of division rings using the notion of a completely prime left ideal (see
[
23
, Proposition 2.11]
):

Proposition 2.3
.

A nonzero ring
R
is a division ring iff every proper left ideal of
R
is completely prime.

Every maximal left ideal is completely prime
[
23
, Corollary 2.10]
. In particular, any nonzero ring has at least one completely prime left ideal.
It is clear that if a left ideal is extremely prime then it is completely prime. However, a completely prime left ideal may not be extremely problem, as shown in the following example.

Example 2.1
.

Let
D
be a division ring and
M_{2}(D)
be the ring of
2\times 2
-matrices over
D
. It is easy to see that
M_{2}(D)
has no extremely prime left ideals (see also
[
23
, Proposition 2.4]
). Note that any maximal left ideal of
M_{2}(D)
is completely prime.

For more results regarding completely prime one-sided ideals, see
[
24
]
. For a brief survey of notions of primeness for one-sided ideals, see also Section 8 in the reference.

2.4
Structurally Prime Left Ideals

The notion of a prime ideal, as presented in Definition
2.2
, can be naturally extended to left ideals as follows:

Definition 2.5
.

A left ideal
{\swabfamily p}
in a ring
R
is said to be
structurally prime
if
{\swabfamily p}\neq R
, and for left ideals
{\swabfamily A},{\swabfamily B}
in
R
,
{\swabfamily A}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
implies
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
or
{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Some authors use the phrase “prime one-sided ideals” in place of “structurally prime one-sided ideals”. To emphasize the dependence of this notion on the structure of one-sided ideals, we adopt the term “structurally prime”.

It is evident that a two-sided ideal is structurally prime as a left ideal iff it is prime as a two-sided ideal. The following proposition gives several characterizations of structurally prime left ideals, which are similar to the familiar ones for prime two-sided ideals:

Proposition 2.4
.

For a left ideal
{\swabfamily p}\subseteq R
, the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

{\swabfamily p}
is structurally prime.

(2)

For
a,b\in R
,
RaRb\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
implies
a\in{\swabfamily p}
or
b\in{\swabfamily p}
.

(3)

For
a,b\in R
,
aRb\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
implies
a\in{\swabfamily p}
or
b\in{\swabfamily p}
.

(4)

For a two-sided ideal
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq R
and a left ideal
{\swabfamily B}\subseteq R
,
{\swabfamily A}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
implies
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
or
{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
.

(5)

For a two-sided ideal
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq R
and an element
b\in R
,
{\swabfamily A}b\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
implies
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
or
b\in{\swabfamily p}
.

Proof.

The proof of
(1)\implies(2)\implies(3)\implies(1)
is straightforward. It is clear that (1) implies (4). To prove
(4)\implies(1)
, suppose
{\swabfamily A}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
for some left ideals
{\swabfamily A},{\swabfamily B}
in
R
. For the ideal
{\swabfamily A}R
and the left ideal
{\swabfamily B}
, we have

({\swabfamily A}R){\swabfamily B}={\swabfamily A}(R{\swabfamily B})={\swabfamily A}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}.

If (4) holds, then either
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily A}R\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
or
{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
, which implies (1). The easy proof of (4)
\iff
(5) is left to the reader.
∎

Definition
2.5
appears to have been first introduced by Koh in
[
13
]
, where it was used to define and study the concept of the “right dimension of a ring.” Shortly after, in
[
21
]
, Michler presented the notion and attributed it to Lambek. Michler also proved the following noncommutative version of Cohen’s Theorem:

Theorem 2.5
.

A ring
R
is left noetherian iff every structurally prime left ideal of
R
is finitely generated.

In
[
18
]
, Michler and Lambek studied structurally prime one-sided ideals in the context of the torsion theory at a prime ideal. In particular, they
proved the following result
[
18
, Proposition 2.1]
:

Proposition 2.6
.

A left notherian ring
R
is simple iff every left ideal of
R
is structurally prime.

It is clear that any extremely prime left ideal is structurally prime (see the third part of Proposition
2.4
). It is easy to show that any maximal left ideal is structurally prime. In particular, a structurally prime left ideal may not be extremely prime as there exist rings in which no maximal left ideal is extremely prime (see Example
2.1
). The following example shows that a structurally prime left ideal may not be completely prime as a left ideal.

Example 2.2
.

Let
D
be a division ring and
M_{2}(D)
be the ring of
2\times 2
-matrices over
D
. The zero ideal in
M_{2}(D)
is structurally prime by Proposition
2.6
. However, the zero ideal is not completely prime as a left ideal because
M_{2}(D)
has zero-divisors.

The next example provides a left ideal that is completely prime, but not structurally prime.

Example 2.3
.

Let
D
be a division ring. Consider the ring
R
of all lower triangular matrices in
M_{2}(D)
. It is easy to check that

{\swabfamily p}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&a\end{bmatrix}:\,a\in D\right\}

is a completely prime left ideal of
R
. The left ideal
{\swabfamily p}
is not structurally prime since

\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}R\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\subseteq{\swabfamily p},

but

\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\notin{\swabfamily p}\text{ and }\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\notin{\swabfamily p}.

2.5
Weakly Prime Left Ideals

As the final notion of primeness for one-sided ideals reviewed in this paper, we consider the concept of weakly prime one-sided ideals. This notion relates to the notion of a “structurally prime left ideal” in the same way that the concept of a “completely prime left ideal” relates to the notion of an “extremely prime left ideal”.

Definition 2.6
.

A left ideal
{\swabfamily p}
in a ring
R
is said to be
weakly prime
if
{\swabfamily p}\neq R
, and for left ideals
{\swabfamily A},{\swabfamily B}
in
R
,
{\swabfamily A}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
and
{\swabfamily p}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
imply
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
or
{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
.

This notion was introduced by Koh in
[
14
]
, where the notion was termed “prime left ideal” and the following results were obtained:

Theorem 2.7
.

A ring
R
is left noetherian iff every weakly prime left ideal of
R
is finitely generated.

Theorem 2.8
.

A left notherian ring
R
is simple iff every left ideal of
R
is weakly prime.

It is evident that every structurally prime left ideal is weakly prime. It is easy to prove that every completely prime left ideal is weakly prime. Therefore, Theorem
2.7
follows from either Theorem
2.2
or Theorem
2.5
. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that in
[
14
]
, Koh formulated and proved his results for rings that are not necessarily unital.

In
[
25
, Proposition 1.1 and Proposition 1.3]
, van der Walt provided several characterizations of weakly prime one-sided ideals. However, he did not observe that his notion coincides with Definition
2.6
, as evidenced by the fact that he reproved one direction of Theorem
2.8
while stating that his result strengthened Theorem
2.8
. Let us give a more complete version of van der Walt’s characterizations of weakly prime ideals.

Proposition 2.9
.

For a left ideal
{\swabfamily p}\subseteq R
, the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

{\swabfamily p}
is weakly prime.

(2)

For left ideals
{\swabfamily A},{\swabfamily B}
in
R
both containing
{\swabfamily p}
,
{\swabfamily A}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
implies
{\swabfamily A}={\swabfamily p}
or
{\swabfamily B}={\swabfamily p}
.

(3)

For left ideals
{\swabfamily A},{\swabfamily B}
in
R
,
({\swabfamily A}+{\swabfamily p})({\swabfamily B}+{\swabfamily p})\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
implies
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
or
{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
.

(4)

For left ideals
{\swabfamily A},{\swabfamily B}
in
R
,
{\swabfamily A}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
and
{\swabfamily p}\subseteq{\swabfamily A}
imply
{\swabfamily p}={\swabfamily A}
or
{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
.

(5)

For left ideals
{\swabfamily A},{\swabfamily B}
in
R
,
({\swabfamily A}+{\swabfamily p}){\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
implies
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
or
{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
.

(6)

For
a,b\in R
,
(a+{\swabfamily p})R(b+{\swabfamily p})\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
implies
a\in{\swabfamily p}
or
b\in{\swabfamily p}
.

(7)

For a two-sided ideal
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq R
and a left ideal
{\swabfamily B}\subseteq R
,
{\swabfamily A}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
and
{\swabfamily p}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
imply
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
or
{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
.

(8)

For
a,b\in R
,
aRb\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
and
{\swabfamily p}Rb\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
imply
a\in{\swabfamily p}
or
b\in{\swabfamily p}
.

Proof.

The equivalences (2)
\iff(3)\iff(4)\iff(5)\iff
(6) are proved in
[
25
, Proposition 1.1]
. To prove (1)
\implies
(2), suppose
{\swabfamily A}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
for some left ideals
{\swabfamily A},{\swabfamily B}
containing
{\swabfamily p}
. Then,
{\swabfamily p}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily A}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
. If
{\swabfamily p}
is weakly prime, then
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
or
{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
, proving (2). To prove (5)
\implies
(1), suppose that
{\swabfamily A}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
and
{\swabfamily p}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
for some left ideals
{\swabfamily A},{\swabfamily B}
in
R
. It follows that

({\swabfamily A}+{\swabfamily p}){\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily A}{\swabfamily B}+{\swabfamily p}{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}.

If (5) holds, then either
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
or
{\swabfamily B}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
, which implies (1). The proof of (1)
\iff
(7) is similar to the proof of (1)
\iff

. The equivalence of (6) and (8) is immediate.
∎

It is evident that for two-sided ideals, the concept of a weakly prime left ideal coincides with the concept of a structurally prime left ideal. For left ideals that are not two-sided, the following result gives more characterizations of weakly prime left ideals.

Proposition 2.10
.

For any left ideal
{\swabfamily p}
in a ring
R
that is not two-sided, the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

{\swabfamily p}
is weakly prime.

(2)

For a left ideal
{\swabfamily A}
,
{\swabfamily p}{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
implies
{\swabfamily A}\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
.

(3)

For
b\in R
,
{\swabfamily p}Rb\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
implies
b\in{\swabfamily p}
.

Proof.

For a proof, see
[
25
, Proposition 1.3]
.
∎

As a simple consequence of Proposition

, we see that a proper left ideal
{\swabfamily p}
in

is necessarily weakly prime. Here is an example of a left ideal which is weakly prime, but not completely prime as a left ideal:

Example 2.4
.

The zero ideal in a matrix ring over a division ring is structurally prime, hence weakly prime. However, the zero ideal in a matrix ring over a division ring is not completely prime as a left ideal. See Example

.

A weakly prime left ideal may not be structurally prime, as shown in the following example.

Example 2.5
.

Consider the ring

{\swabfamily p}
in Example

{\swabfamily p}
is not structurally prime.

2.6
More Characterizations of Primeness

This part gives more characterizations of the reviewed notions of primeness in terms the notion of a left quotient ideal by an element. For a left ideal
I
and an element
a
in a ring
R
, the
left quotien ideal
of
I
by
a
is defined as

(I:a)=\left\{x\in R\,|\,xa\in I\right\}.

It is easy to see that
(I:a)
is always a left ideal. The largest two-sided ideal in a one-sided ideal
I
is denoted by
\operatorname{id}(I)
. The following proposition provides characterizations of the four notions of primeness for one-sided ideals in terms of
(I:a)
and
\operatorname{id}(I)
. The easy proof is left to the reader.

Proposition 2.11
.

For any proper left ideal
{\swabfamily p}
in a ring
R
, the following statements hold:

(1)

{\swabfamily p}
is extremely prime iff
({\swabfamily p}:b)\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
for all
b\in R\setminus{\swabfamily p}
.

(2)

{\swabfamily p}
is completely prime iff
({\swabfamily p}:b)={\swabfamily p}
for all
b\in R\setminus{\swabfamily p}
such that
{\swabfamily p}\subseteq({\swabfamily p}:b)
.

(3)

{\swabfamily p}
is structurally prime iff
\operatorname{id}(({\swabfamily p}:b))\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
for all
b\in R\setminus{\swabfamily p}
.

(4)

{\swabfamily p}
is weakly prime iff
\operatorname{id}(({\swabfamily p}:b))\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
for all
b\in R\setminus{\swabfamily p}
such that
{\swabfamily p}R\subseteq({\swabfamily p}:b)
.

3
Prime Left Ideals in Principal Ideal Domains

In this section, we study the four notions of one-sided primeness, mentioned in the previous chapter, in the context of principal ideal domains.

3.1
Prinicipal Ideal Domains

For the reader’s convenience, we briefly review some necessary concepts regarding principal ideal domains. Most of the material is standard and can be found in, for example,
[
6
]
. A domain in which all left ideals and all right ideals are principal is called a
principal ideal domain
, or PID for short. In what follows, let
R
be a PID.

An element
a\in R
is called a
right
(resp.,
left
)
factor
of
b\in R
if
b\in Ra
(resp.,
b\in aR
). A left factor
a
of
b
is called
proper
if
a\notin bR
. An element
a\in R
is called a
factor
of
b
if
b=ras
for some
r,s\in R
. An element
a\in R
is called a

right
(resp.,
left
)
associate
of
b\in R
if
b=au
(resp.,
b=ua
) for some unit
u\in R
, that is,
aR=bR
(resp.,
Ra=Rb
).
An element
a\in R
is called an
associate
of
b\in R
if
a=ubv
for some units
u,v\in R
.
Elements
a,b\in R
are called
similar
iff
R/Ra
and
R/Rb
are isomorphic as left
R
-modules. It is known that the notion of similarity is left-right symmetric, that is,
a,b\in R
are similar iff
R/aR
and
R/Rb
are isomorphic as right
R
-modules. A non-unit
a\in R
is said to be
irreducible
(or an
atom
) if a relation
a=bc
in
R
implies that
b
or
c
is a unit. Every PID is a UFD in the following sense: a nonzero element
a\in R
can be written as a product of atoms, and furthermore, if
a=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}=b_{1}\cdots b_{m}
are two factorizations of
a
into atoms, then
m=n
and there is a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
such that
a_{i}
is similar to
b_{\pi(i)}
for all
i
.

A relation
ax=by
in
R
is called a
left
(resp.,
right
)
comaximal relation
if
aR+bR=R
(resp.,
Rx+Ry=R
). A comaximal relation is a relation that is both left and right comaximal. The following result describes similar elements in terms of comaximal relations in PIDs (we include a proof, as the author was unable to locate a suitable reference in the existing literature):

Proposition 3.1
.

In any PID
R
, elements
a,b\in R
are similar iff they satisfy a comaximal relation
ax=yb
in
R
.

Proof.

First, let
a,b\in R
be similar, i.e.,
R/Ra
and
R/Rb
are isomorphic as left
R
-modules. It follows from
[
6
, Proposition 1.3.6]
that there exists
x\in R
such that
Rb+Rx=R
and
(Rb:x)=Ra
. We have
ax=yb
for some
y\in R
. To prove that
ax=yb
is a comaximal relation, we need to show that
aR+yR=R
. We have
aR+yR=dR
for some
d\in R
, implying
a=da_{1},y=dy_{1}
for some
a_{1},d_{1}\in R
. The relation
ax=yb
simplifies to
a_{1}x=y_{1}b
. Since
a_{1}\in(Rb:x)=Ra
,
d
must be a unit because
a_{1}\in Ra
and
a\in Ra_{1}
. This completes the proof of the forward direction.

Conversely, let
ax=yb
be a comaximal relation in
R
. It is easy to verify that the map

r+Ra\mapsto rx+Rb

is an isomorphism between
the left
R
-modules
R/Ra
and
R/Rb
, i.e.,
a,b\in R
are similar, and we are done.
∎

A nonzero element
a\in R
is called
invariant
if
Ra=aR
. It is easy to see that a nonzero element
a\in R
is invariant iff
aR\subseteq Ra
iff
Ra\subseteq aR
. Every nonzero ideal of
R
is of the form
Ra=aR
for some invariant element
a\in R
, which is unique up to (right or left) associates. For every
a\in R
and ideal
I
in
R
,
I\subseteq Ra
iff
I\subseteq aR
(proof: if
Rb=bR\subseteq Ra\implies\exists r,\,b=ra\implies\exists s,\,rb=bs=ras\implies b\in aR\implies I\subseteq aR
). An element
a\in R
is called
bounded
if
Ra
contains a nonzero ideal, or equivalently,
aR
contains a nonzero ideal. For a bounded element
a
, a bound
a^{*}
of
a
is defined to be a generator of the largest ideal contained in
Ra
. The element
a^{*}
is unique up to associates, called the
bound
of
a
. Note that
Ra^{*}
is also the largest ideal contained in
aR
. If
a^{*}
is the bound of
a
, then

Ra^{*}=a^{*}R=Ann(\frac{R}{Ra})=Ann(\frac{R}{aR}).

In particular, similar elements have the same bound. It is easy to show that an element
a\in R
is bounded iff
ras
is invariant for some nonzero elements
r,s\in R
.

3.2
Extremely Prime Left Ideals in PIDs

In this part, we give a description of extremely prime left ideals of a PID. In what follows, let
R
be a PID. Since
R
is a domain, the zero ideal is extremely prime. The following proposition describes nonzero extremely prime left ideals of
R
.

Proposition 3.2
.

A nonzero left ideal
Ra
in
R
is extremely prime iff
a
is irreducible and invariant.

Proof.

First, suppose that
Ra
is a nonzero extremely prime left ideal of
R
. Using the fact that
R
is (left and right) noetherian, we can write
a=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}
, where
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in R
are irreducible. Since
Ra
is extremely prime,
a_{i}\in Ra
for some
i
. Since
a_{i}
is irreducible and
a
is a non-unit,
a
must be irreducible.
Fix
c\in R\setminus Ra
. Then
(Ra:c)=Rb
for some nonzero element
b\in R
. Since
bc\in Ra
and
c\notin Ra
, we must have
b\in Ra
. It is easy to verify that the map

x+Rb\mapsto xc+Ra

is an isomorphism between
the left
R
-modules
R/Ra
and
R/Rb
, i.e.,
a,b
are similar. In particular,
b
is irreducible, and since
b\in Ra
, we conclude that
(Ra:c)=Ra
. This proves that
aR\subseteq Ra
, implying that
a
is invariant. This completes the proof of the forward direction.

Conversely, suppose that
Ra
is a nonzero left ideal such that
aR=Ra
and
a
is irreducible. Let
bc\in Ra
and
c\notin Ra
. We need to show that
b\in Ra
. Since
a
is irreducible and
c\notin aR
, we see that
cR+aR=R
. Multiplying by
b
, we obtain
bcR+baR=bR
. Then

b\in bR=bcR+baR\subseteq RaR+RaR=Ra.

This proves that

Ra
is extremely prime, completing the proof of the reverse direction.
∎

As a consequence of the proposition, we note that any extremely prime left ideal of a PID is a completely prime two-sided ideal, and vice versa.

The structure of invariant elements in skew polynomial rings is well understood, thanks to the work of Cauchon
[
5
]
, Carcanague
[
4
]
, Lam and Leroy
[
17
]
, among others. We give some examples using their results.

Example 3.1
.

Let
D[t]
denote the ring of polynomials in one variable over a division ring
D
. It is well-known that
D[t]
is a PID. It is easy to see that a monic polynomial in
D[t]
is invariant iff it is central. By Proposition
3.2
, a nonzero left ideal
I
in
D[t]
is extremely prime iff
I=D[t]f(t)
, where
f(t)
is a central irreducible polynomial. In the case where
D=\mathbb{H}
is the division ring of quaternions over
\mathbb{R}
, a nonzero left ideal
I
in
\mathbb{H}[t]
is extremely prime iff
I=\mathbb{H}[t](t-a)
for some
a\in\mathbb{R}
. This is a consequence of the fundamental theorem of algebra for quaternions
[
15
, Thoerem 16.14]
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
D[t;\sigma]
denote the ring of skew polynomials associated to an automoprhism
\sigma
of a division ring
D
. It is well-known that
D[t;\sigma]
is a PID. If
\sigma
is of infinite inner order, then every invariant elements of
D[t;\sigma]
is of the form
at^{m}
for some
a\in D\setminus\{0\}
and
m\geq 1
. It follows that
D[t]t
is
the only nonzero extremely prime left ideal of
D[t;\sigma]
provided that
\sigma
is of infinite inner order.

3.3
Completely Prime Left Ideals in PIDs

We begin with a definition that is needed to describe completely prime one-sided ideals of PIDs. A nonzero element
a
in a ring
R
is said to be
c-reducible
if
a
can be written as
a=bb^{\prime}=c^{\prime}c
for some nonunits
b,b^{\prime},c^{\prime},c\in R
such that
b
is similar to
c
. An element that is not c-reducible will be called
c-irreducible
.

As the zero ideal is completely prime as a left ideal in any domain, we only need to deal with nonzero left ideals. The following result gives a description of nonzero completely prime left ideals of PIDs.

Proposition 3.3
.

A nonzero left ideal
Ra
of a PID
R
is completely prime iff
a
is c-irreducible.

Proof.

We prove the contrapositive of the statement, that is,
a
is c-reducible iff
Ra
is not completely prime. First,
assume that
a
is c-reducible.
a=bb^{\prime}=c^{\prime}c
for some nonunits
b,b^{\prime},c^{\prime},c\in R
such that
b
is similar to
c
. By Proposition
3.1
, there exists a comaximal relation
cx=yb
in
R
. We have
c(xb^{\prime})=ybb^{\prime}\in Ra
. Moreover,
(Ra)xb^{\prime}=Rc^{\prime}cxb^{\prime}=Rc^{\prime}ybb^{\prime}\subseteq Ra
. Since
c\notin Ra
and
xb^{\prime}\notin Ra
, we conclude that
Ra
is not completely prime. This completes the proof of the forward direction.

Conversely, suppose that
Ra
is not completely prime. Then, there exist
b,c\in R\setminus Ra
such that
bc\in Ra
and
(Ra)c\subseteq Ra
. It follows that
b\in(Ra:c)
and
a\in(Ra:c)
. We have
(Ra:c)=Rb_{0}
for some
b_{0}\in R
. Therefore,
a=b^{\prime}b_{0}
for some
b^{\prime}\in R
. Since
Ra+Rc=Rc_{1}
for some
c_{1}\in R
, we see that
a=c_{2}c_{1}
and
c=xc_{1}
for some
c_{2},x\in R
such that
Rx+Rc_{2}=R
. It follows from
(Ra:c)=Rb_{0}
that
b_{0}c=y_{0}a
for some
y_{0}\in R
. Therefore,
b_{0}xc_{1}=b_{0}c=ya=yc_{2}c_{1}
, implying
b_{0}x=y_{0}c_{2}
. We have
b_{0}=db_{1}
and
y_{0}=dy
for some
d,b_{0},y
such that
b_{1}R+yR=R
. Since
b_{1}x=yc_{2}
,
Rx+Rc_{2}=R
, and
b_{1}R+yR=R
, it follows from Proposition
3.1
that
b_{1},c_{2}
are similar. Since
a=(b^{\prime}d)b_{1}=c_{2}c_{1}
, we conclude that
a
is c-reducible. Note that
b^{\prime}d,b_{1},c_{2},c_{1}
are nonunits, thanks to the assumption
b,c\in R\setminus Ra
.
∎

Since the notion of c-irreducibility is left-right symmetric, it follows from Proposition
3.3
that
Ra
is a completely prime left ideal iff
aR
is a completely prime right ideal.

It is clear that every irreducible element is c-irreducible. The converse does not hold, as shown in the following example:

Example 3.3
.

Let
\frac{d}{dx}
denote the derivative on the ring
\mathbb{R}(x)
of rational functions over
\mathbb{R}
. Consider the skew polynomial ring
R=\mathbb{R}(x)[t;1,\frac{d}{dx}]
. It is known that
R
is a PID. Set
f=(t+x)t\in R
. If
f=(t+a(x))(t+b(x))
is a factorization of
f
in
R
, then
b(x)
must satisfy the Bernoulli equation
y^{\prime}+xy=y^{2}
whose only rational solution is
y=0
. It follows that the element
f
has a unique factorization
f=(t+x)t
into monic nonconstant polynomials. It is easy to verify that
t+x
and
t
are not similar in
R
. Therefore,
f
is c-irreducible, but not irreducible. For another example of a c-irreducible element that is not irreducible, see
[
1
, Proposition 4.5]
.

Under certain assumptions, a c-irreducible element must be irreducible:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
a\in R
be an element in a PID. Assume that
a
has a non-unit bounded factor. Then
a
is c-irreducible iff it is irreducible.

Proof.

We only need to prove the forward direction. Let
a=p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\in R
be a factorization of a c-irreducible element
a
into irreducible elements. Since
a
has a bounded factor, some
p_{i}
is bounded. Let
p_{i_{1}},\dots,p_{i_{m}}
be all the factors that are similar to
p_{i}
. It follows from Lemma 6.2.5 in
[
6
, p. 344]
that

a=(p_{i_{1}}\cdots p_{i_{m}})b=cb^{\prime},

where
(p_{i_{1}}\cdots p_{i_{m}})
and
b^{\prime}
are similar (see also the discussion following the lemma in Cohn’s book
[
6
, p. 344]
). Since
a
is c-irreducible,
m=1
and
a
must be similar to
p_{i}
, implying that
a
is irreducible.
∎

Following Feller
[
8
]
, we call a PID
bounded
if every non-unit element in the PID is bounded. It is easy to show that a PID
R
is bounded iff every atom in
R
is bounded. Consequently, a PID is bounded iff it is not (left, or equivalently, right) primitive. For a detailed study of the notion of “boundedness” for arbitrary rings, see
[
10
, Chapter 1]
. As a direct consequence of Proposition
3.4
, we present the following result.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
R
be a bounded PID. Then,
a\in R
is c-irreducible iff it is irreducible. In other words, in any bounded PID, a nonzero left ideal is completely prime as a left ideal iff it is maximal as a left ideal.

We now give some examples of bounded PIDs.

Example 3.4
.

Let
D
be a division ring. It is known that
D[t]
is a bounded PID if every square matrix over
D
is algebraic over the center of
D

[
10
, Theorem 17.2.]
. In particular, if
D
is centrally finite, i.e., is of finite dimension over its center, then
D[t]
is a bounded PID.

Example 3.5
.

Let
\delta
be a derivation on a field
k
of characteristic
p>0
. If
\delta
is nilpotent, then
R=k[t;\delta]
is a bounded PID
[
10
, Exercise 9D]
. It can also be proved using the results in the next example.

Example 3.6
.

For the terminology used in this example, we refer the reader to
[
19
]
. A skew polynomial ring
D[t;\sigma,\delta]
over a division ring
D
is left primitive iff
D[t;\sigma,\delta]
satisfies one of the following conditions: (1)
D[t;\sigma,\delta]
is simple; (2)
D[t;\sigma,\delta]
is not simple and no positive power of
\sigma
is an inner automorphism; (3)
D[t;\sigma,\delta]
is not simple, a positive power of
\sigma
is an inner automorphism, and there exist
n\geq 1
and a square matrix
A\in M_{n}(D)
such that
A
is not algebraic over the center of
D

[
19
, Theorem 3.26]
. Since boundedness and nonprimitiveness are equivalent for PIDs, we see that
D[t;\sigma,\delta]
is a bounded PID iff
D[t;\sigma,\delta]
is not simple, some positive power of
\sigma
is an inner automorphism, and every square matrix over
D
is algebraic over the center of
D
. As an example, if
\sigma:k\to k
is an automorphism of finite order over a field
k
, then

is a bounded PID. In particular, a left ideal of

is completely prime iff it is a maximal left ideal. For another proof of the last statement, see
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Example 3.7
.

In this example, we assume that the reader is familiar with the work of Lam and Leroy
[
16
]
. Let
D[t;\sigma,\delta]
be a skew polynomial ring over a division ring. Suppose that the
(\sigma,\delta)
-conjugacy class of an element
a\in D
is
(\sigma,\delta)
-algebraic. Then, any polynomial
f(t)
of degree at least
2
that has
a
as a right root is necessarily c-reducible. This is because, by Corollary 6.3 in
[
16
]
,
f(t)
must have a left root
b
that is
(\sigma,\delta)
-conjugate to
a
. Note that
t-a
, a proper left factor of
f(t)
, and
t-b
, a proper right factor of
f(t)
, are similar. In particular, for such an element
f
, the left ideal
D[t]f(t)
is never completely prime as a left ideal.

3.4
Structurally Prime Left Ideals in PIDs

To give a characterization of structurally prime left ideals in a PID, we need to review some concepts. Recall that an invariant non-unit
a\in R
is called
Inv-irreducible
(or an
Inv-atom
) if
a=bc
, where
b,c\in R
are invariant, implies that either
b
or
c
is a unit. It is easy to see that
a\in R
is Inv-irreducible iff
Ra
is a maximal ideal
[
6
, Theorem 6.2.4 (i)]
. It is also known that an element
a\in R
in a PID is Inv-irreducible iff
a
is the bound of a bounded atom
b
, in which case
a
is a product of similar bounded atoms
[
6
, Theorem 6.2.4 (ii), (iii)]
. A nonzero non-unit is called
left totally unbounded
if it has no non-unit bounded left factors.

Proposition 3.6
.

A nonzero left ideal
Ra
of a PID
R
is structurally prime iff
a
is left totally unbounded or a factor of an Inv-atom.

Proof.

To prove the forward direction, suppose that
Ra
is structurally prime. If
a
is left totally bounded, there is nothing to prove. Assume that
a
is not left totally bounded. We have
a=pb
, where
p
is a bounded irreducible left factor of
a
. Let
c=rp
be the bound of
p
. Then
cRb=R(rp)b\subseteq Ra
. Since
Ra
is structurally prime and
b\notin Ra
, we see that
c\in Ra
, i.e.,
a
is a factor of the bound of an atom, which is Inv-reducible by
[
6
, Theorem 6.2.4 (iii)]
. This completes the proof of the forward direction.

Conversely, suppose that
a
is left totally unbounded or a factor of an Inv-irreducible element. Suppose
bRc\subseteq Ra
for some nonzero
b,c\in R
. To prove that
Ra
is structurally prime, we need to show that
b\in Ra
or
c\in Ra
. We have
Ra+Rc=Rx
for some
x\in R
. It follows that
a=a_{1}x,c=c_{1}x
for some
a_{1},c_{1}\in R
such that
Ra_{1}+Rc_{1}=R
. Multiplying by
b
, we obtain

bR=bRa_{1}+bRc_{1}\subseteq Ra_{1}.

It follows that
a_{1}
is bounded. If
a
is left totally bounded,
a_{1}
must be a unit, and consequently,
c\in Ra
. We may, therefore, assume that
a
is a factor of an Inv-atom
p
and
a_{1}
is a non-unit. Let
d
be the bound of
a_{1}
. Note that
b\in Rd
. Since
a\in a_{1}R
,
p
belongs to
Rd
. Since
p
is Inv-irreducible, we see that
Rd=Rp
, hence
b\in Rd=Rp\subseteq Ra
. This completes the proof.
∎

As a direct consequence of Proposition
2.4
, we see that a nonzero two-sided ideal of a PID
R
is prime as a two-sided ideal iff it is a maximal two-sided ideal.

Example 3.8
.

Let
D
be a division ring with center
k
. It is easy to see that a monic polynomial
f\in D[x]
is invariant iff
f\in k[x]
. Moreover, a nonconstant polynomial
f\in k[x]
is Inv-irreducible iff
f
is irreducible as an element of
k[x]
. By Proposition
2.4
, a nonzero left ideal
D[x]f(x)\neq D[x]
is structurally prime, iff
f(x)
is not a factor of a nonzero polynomial in
k[x]
, or
f(x)
is a factor of an irreducible polynomial in
k[x]
. In the case where
D=\mathbb{H}
is the division ring of quaternions, the center of
\mathbb{H}[x]
is
\mathbb{R}[x]
, and a nonconstant polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x]
is Inv-irreducible iff it has degree
1
or
f(t)=t^{2}+rt+s
, where
r^{2}<4s
.

In general rings, a completely prime ideal may not be structurally prime (see Example
2.3
). This is, however, true for PIDs:

Proposition 3.7
.

A completely prime left ideal in any PID is structurally prime

Proof.

Let
Ra
be a nonzero completely prime ideal in a PID
R
. By Proposition
3.4
,
a
is either irreducible or totally unbounded. It follows from Proposition
3.6
that
Ra
is structurally prime. ∎

The converse of Proposition
3.7
does not hold, as demonstrated by the following example.

Example 3.9
.

The left ideal
{\swabfamily p}=\mathbb{H}[t](t^{2}+1)
is structurally prime since the invariant element
t^{2}+1
is Inv-irreducible. However,
{\swabfamily p}
is not completely prime because
(t-i)(t+i)\in{\swabfamily p}
and
{\swabfamily p}(t+i)\subseteq{\swabfamily p}
, but
t-i,t+i\notin{\swabfamily p}
. For another example, see
[
1
, Example 1]
.

3.5
Weakly Prime Left Ideals in PIDs

In general, a two-sided ideal is weakly prime as a left ideal iff it is structurally prime as a left ideal. Therefore, we restrict our attention to left ideals that are not two-sided.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
Ra
be a left ideal in a PID
R
that is not two-sided. Then
Ra
is weakly prime iff
a
has no non-unit invariant factors. In other words, a left ideal in a PID that is not two-sided is weakly prime iff it is not contained in a proper two-sided ideal.

Proof.

We prove the contrapositive of the statement, that is,
a
has a non-unit invariant factor iff
Ra
is not weakly prime. First, assume that
a
has a non-unit invariant factor
b
. It follows that
a=bx
for some
x\in R
, implying
aRx\subseteq bRx=Rbx=Ra
. Since
x\notin Ra
, it follows from Proposition
2.10
(see Part (3)) that
a
is not weakly prime.

Conversely, suppose that
Ra
is not weakly prime. Since
Ra
is not two-sided, by Part (3) of Proposition
2.10
, there exists
b\in R\setminus Ra
such that
aRb\in Ra
. We can write
a=cx
and
b=dx
for some
b,d,x\in R
such that
Rc+Rd=R
. Note that
aRd\subseteq Rc
. Since
b\notin Ra
,
c
must be a non-unit. Furthermore, we have

aR=aRc+aRd\subseteq Rc.

It follows that
c
is a bounded element whose bound is a factor of
a
, completing the proof.
∎

We end the article with the following example.

Example 3.10
.

Let
D
be a division ring with center
k
. Any left ideal of
D[x]
that is not two-sided is of the form
D[x]f(x)
, where
f(x)
is a monic nonconstant polynomial not contained in
k[x]
. For such a left ideal,
D[x]f(x)
is weakly prime iff
f(x)
does not have nonunit factos belonging to
k[x]
. In the case where
D=\mathbb{H}
is the division ring of quaternions, the reader can check that the left ideal
\mathbb{H}[x](x-j)(x-i)
is weakly prime, but not structurally prime.
