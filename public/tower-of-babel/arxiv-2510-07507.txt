Adaptive Control Allocation for Underactuated Time-Scale Separated Non-Affine Systems
Daniel M. Cherenson, Dimitra Panagou

Abstract
Many robotic systems are underactuated, meaning not all degrees of freedom can be directly controlled due to lack of actuators, input constraints, or state-dependent actuation. This property, compounded by modeling uncertainties and disturbances, complicates the control design process for trajectory tracking. In this work, we propose an adaptive control architecture for uncertain, nonlinear, underactuated systems with input constraints. Leveraging time-scale separation, we construct a reduced-order model where fast dynamics provide virtual inputs to the slower subsystem and use dynamic control allocation to select the optimal control inputs given the non-affine dynamics. To handle uncertainty, we introduce a state predictor-based adaptive law, and through singular perturbation theory and Lyapunov analysis, we prove stability and bounded tracking of reference trajectories. The proposed method is validated on a VTOL ( VTOL ) quadplane with nonlinear, state-dependent actuation, demonstrating its utility as a unified controller across various flight regimes, including cruise, landing transition, and hover.

Introduction

Many robotic systems, including aerial robots
[

]
, underwater robots
[

are underactuated. In this paper, we consider a broad class of uncertain underactuated systems where the mapping from control inputs to

Some systems exhibit time-scale separation, meaning a subset of the system states evolve more quickly than the rest. Exploiting this property allows the use of reduced-order models, in which the fast subsystem reference is treated as a virtual input to the slow system, simplifying the overall control problem by augmenting the control input space. Singular perturbation theory provides formal treatment and analysis of time-scale separated control systems
[
5
]
.

Adaptive control provides a framework for handling uncertainties in dynamical systems by adjusting controller parameters online to maintain stability and tracking performance without requiring exact knowledge of the system
[
6
]
. While adaptive methods have been successfully applied to certain underactuated systems, much of the existing work has focused on control-affine manipulators with relatively simple actuation structures or without input constraints
[
3
,
7

8

[

[

The combination of underactuation, non-affine-in-control dynamics, and time-scale separation in adaptive control of a general class of systems remains largely unexplored.

In this paper, we propose an adaptive control architecture for uncertain, underactuated nonlinear systems that uses a time-scale separated control strategy. Our contributions are as follows:
1) We design an adaptive control architecture for uncertain, non-affine in control, underactuated nonlinear systems with redundant, constrained control inputs using a time-scale separation property and dynamic control allocation, 2) We prove stability and bounded tracking error of a reference trajectory using singular perturbation theory and Lyapunov analysis, and 3) We demonstrate the algorithm on a

II
Preliminaries

In this paper, we form a closed-loop three-time-scale model for the control of an uncertain underactuated system:

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=f(t,x,z,u,\epsilon),\quad x(0)=\xi(\epsilon)

(1a)

\displaystyle\epsilon\dot{z}

\displaystyle=g(t,x,z,u,\epsilon),\quad z(0)=\eta(\epsilon)

(1b)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\dot{u}

\displaystyle=p(t,x,z,u,\epsilon),\quad u(0)=\upsilon(\epsilon)

(1c)

where
0<\epsilon\ll 1
is a parameter that represents the time-scale separation between the three subsystems. That is,

evolves at a rate
\epsilon

\epsilon

\xi(\epsilon)

\eta(\epsilon)

\upsilon(\epsilon)
are smooth functions of
\epsilon
. The fast time scale is

and the fastest time scale is
t^{\prime\prime}=\frac{t^{\prime}}{\epsilon}=\frac{t}{\epsilon^{2}}
. The system is in standard form if the algebraic equation

has at least one isolated real root
u=q_{i}(t,x,z)
. We drop the subscript

j
.

The three-time-scale model is equivalent to two nested two-time-scale models. In the fastest time-scale

, let

. The boundary layer subsystem is given by
\frac{\operatorname{d}}{\operatorname{d}t^{\prime\prime}}v=p(t,x,z,v+q(t,x,z),0)
. Similarly, let

. The boundary layer in the fast time-scale

is given by
\frac{\operatorname{d}}{\operatorname{d}t^{\prime}}y=g(t,x,y+h(t,x),q(t,x,y+h(t,x)),0)
. The reduced system is the result of substituting the roots

and

1a

\dot{\bar{x}}=f(t,x,h(t,x),q(t,x,h(t,x)),0)
. By applying Tikhonov’s Theorem
[
15

, we can show that the reduced slow subsystem
\bar{x}(t)
is a uniform approximation to the actual
\epsilon
-dependent solution to (
1a

x(t,\epsilon)-\bar{x}(t)=O(\epsilon)
, where a vector function

is said to be

if there exist positive constants

such that
\left\|f(t,\epsilon)\right\|\leq k\epsilon\quad\forall\epsilon\in[0,\epsilon^{*}],~\forall t\in[t_{1},\infty)
.

Consider a MIMO nonlinear system in controllable canonical form with respect to a control input function

\displaystyle\dot{z}=A_{z}z+B_{z}\tau_{z}(x,z,u)

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0&1\\
0&0&\cdots&0&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(3)

We assume that the full states

and
z
are measured. Input constraints are encoded by the polytope
\mathcal{U}=\{u:Cu\leq c\},C\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{c}\times m},c\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{c}}
where
n_{c}>0
is the number of individual input constraints. We further partition

into
d

, where each

,

, and we will focus on

, which will become a virtual control input to the slow system.
The combined state dynamics are
\dot{\chi}=A_{\chi}\chi+B_{\chi}\tau(x,z,u),
where
A_{\chi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(n_{x}+n_{z})\times d(n_{x}+n_{z})}
is a block diagonal matrix of

and

, and the same applies for
B_{\chi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d(n_{x}+n_{z})\times n_{x}+n_{z}}
.

The slow-fast state partition aids in the control design process, where singular perturbation theory confirms that treating the fast state as a control input to the slow state
DOF
is valid. We further write the combined control input function as
{\tau(x,z,u)=[\tau_{x}(x,z,u)^{\top},\tau_{z}(x,z,u)^{\top}]^{\top}.}
We approximate the function
\tau:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}+n_{z}}
as the sum of a known component
\tau_{0}
and a linearly-parameterized uncertainty
\phi(x,z,u)W

\phi:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{w}

is a constant vector of

. The resulting form of

\displaystyle\tau(x,z,u):=\tau_{0}(x,z,u)+\phi(x,z,u)W+\delta(x,z,u),

(4)

where
\delta(x,z,u)\in R^{n_{x}+n_{z}}
is a non-parametric uncertainty. Although
\delta
is not estimated, we assume a known bound, denoted as
\delta_{\max}=\sup_{x,z,u}\left\|\delta(x,z,u)\right\|
.

In our proposed approach, we design an estimator for
W

\widehat{W}
in the controller to represent the current estimated
\tau
, denoted
\hat{\tau}
for brevity:

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}=\hat{\tau}(x,z,u;\widehat{W}):=\tau_{0}(x,z,u)+\phi(x,z,u)\widehat{W}.

(5)

Note that we do not maintain an estimate of
\delta
or use the bound

in our control design. We only use it for robustness analysis. The error between

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}:=\tau-\hat{\tau}=\phi(x,z,u)\widetilde{W}+\delta(x,z,u),

IV
Method

Block diagram of the proposed adaptive control architecture. The gray blocks are user-specified. The blue and green blocks are detailed in

Tracking arbitrary trajectories for all
DOF
is impossible because (
2
) is underactuated. Using a reduced-order model facilitates control design, since the fast state
z
can be treated as a control input to the slow state
x
. To establish stability and bounded tracking, we apply singular perturbation theory, which requires exponential stability of the tracking error system. This is difficult to show with an adaptive controller, so instead, we introduce a state predictor and modify the adaptation law for
\widehat{W}
to reduce prediction error. A high-level controller reduces the error between the predictor and the reference trajectory, while the control allocation algorithm selects
u
to realize the high-level command. Ultimately, the error between the slow state
x
and the reference
x^{r}(t)
is shown to be bounded.

To cast the closed-loop system in the form of
eqs.

1a
,
1b
and
1c
, we employ a non-traditional adaptive architecture. Exponential stability of
x(t)-x^{r}(t)
cannot be proven directly due to non-parametric uncertainty
\delta
and lack of persistence of excitation. Instead, by controlling predictors
\hat{x}
and
\hat{z}
rather than the true states, we remove the uncertainty from the closed-loop dynamics. Inspired by
[
9
]
, our approach has two steps: (i) adapt parameters to reduce
\hat{x}(t)-x(t)
, and (ii) apply high-level feedback to reduce
\hat{x}(t)-x^{r}(t)

Our contributions to the architecture in

1
are the high-level controller and control allocation blocks, along with theoretical guarantees of stability and bounded tracking.

IV-
1
Reduced System Reformulation

We seek to write the control function
\tau
in a form that makes it surjective with respect to an augmented control input
\bar{u}
. Here, we define
\bar{u}
as the original control input
u
augmented by a command for the fast state
z
such that the new form of
\tau
is surjective. This step relies on the assumption that the fast state can be controlled much more quickly than the slow state. We apply singular perturbation theory to the final closed-loop system to verify that our approach is valid and gives the desired stability and boundedness properties.

The following example of a planar quadrotor shows that although the combined state model is underactuated due to input constraints, the partitioning of the slow and fast states results in a fully-actuated reduced system.

Example 1

(Planar Quadrotor with Pusher Propeller)
.

Consider a simplified planar quadrotor with two vertical propellers
u_{1},u_{2}
and one lateral pusher propeller
u_{3}
. The slow state
x=\begin{bmatrix}p_{x}&v_{x}&p_{y}&v_{y}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
and fast state
z=\begin{bmatrix}\theta&\omega\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
. The simplified control function
\tau
is

\displaystyle\tau(x,z,u)=\begin{bmatrix}R(\theta)\begin{bmatrix}u_{3}\\
-u_{1}-u_{2}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\\
u_{1}-u_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(7)

where
R(\theta)\in\mathbb{SO}(2)
. The map
\tau(x,z,\cdot)
is not surjective because of the input constraints. In a hover where
x=0,z=0
, there does not exist a

such that
\tau=\begin{bmatrix}-0.1,0,0\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
because
u_{3}
cannot be negative, as the pusher propeller can only produce force in one direction. If, however, we assume that the fast subsystem settles to the command
z^{c}=\begin{bmatrix}\theta^{c}&0\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
instantaneously, where
\theta^{c}
is a virtual control input, then there exists an augmented control
\bar{u}=\begin{bmatrix}u^{\top}&\theta^{c}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
that achieves the desired
\tau
. The virtual control input
\theta
is the reference signal to the fast dynamics, which must be sufficiently fast for the reduced model assumption to be valid.

Formalizing this notion, let
\vartheta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}}
be a vector of fast system commands. We treat the fast state
z
as a virtual input to the slow state dynamics by assuming the fast state instantaneously achieves its command
z^{c}=\begin{bmatrix}\vartheta^{\top}&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
. The reduced-order system model is
\dot{x}=A_{x}x+B_{x}\hat{\tau}_{x}(x,z^{c},u)
. We now define the augmented control input
\bar{u}=\begin{bmatrix}u^{\top}\vartheta^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\bar{\mathcal{U}}:=\mathcal{U}\times\Theta=\{\bar{u}:\bar{C}\bar{u}\leq\bar{c}\}
where
\bar{C}
and
\bar{c}
encode the augmented polytopic input constraints on the virtual input. The reduced control function is

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}_{x}(x,\bar{u}):=\hat{\tau}_{x}(x,z^{c},u).

(8)

It is required that the fast state
z
be fully-actuated to track the command
\vartheta
. Formally, we assume the following:

Assumption 1
.

\bar{\tau}_{x}(x,\cdot):\bar{\mathcal{U}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
and
\hat{\tau}_{z}(x,z,\cdot):\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}}
are surjective
\forall x\in\mathcal{X},z\in\mathcal{Z},\widehat{W}\in\mathcal{W}
.

We will use this reduced system for control design of the high-level controller of the slow system.

2
State Predictor

We introduce state predictors
\hat{x}
and
\hat{z}
to place the relevant time-scale separated dynamics into a form to which singular perturbation theory applies.
Figure

1
depicts the state predictor below the plant dynamics because they have similar inputs
u

and
z/\hat{z}
. The slow state predictor
\hat{x}
is a series-parallel model defined by the following update law:

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{x}}=A_{x}x+B_{x}\hat{\tau}_{x}(x,z,u)-A_{s_{x}}(x-\hat{x}),

(9)

where
A_{s_{x}}
is a Hurwitz matrix in the form

\displaystyle A_{s,x}=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{n_{x}}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&I_{n_{x}}\\
\lx@intercol\hfil[-K_{s_{x}}]\hfil\lx@intercol\end{bmatrix},

(10)

where
K_{s_{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}k_{s_{x},1}&k_{s_{x},2}&\cdots&k_{s_{x},d}\end{bmatrix}\otimes I_{n_{x}}
with each element non-negative. The

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{z}}=A_{z}z+B_{z}\hat{\tau}_{z}(x,z,u)-A_{s_{z}}e_{s_{z}}.

(11)

The combined predicted state is
\hat{\chi}=[\hat{x}^{\top}\hat{z}^{\top}]^{\top}
, and we define

. The dynamics of

are

\displaystyle\dot{e}_{s}=A_{s}e_{s}+B\Phi(x,z,u)\widetilde{W}+\delta(x,z,u),

(12)

where

. Next, we introduce the error between the reference trajectory

and the predicted
\hat{x}
. The dynamics of the reference trajectory are given by the linear system
\dot{x}^{r}=A_{x}x^{r}+B_{x}r(t)
, where

is the derivative of the

.
The tracking error dynamics are

\displaystyle\dot{e}_{x}

\displaystyle=A_{x}x+B_{x}\hat{\tau}_{x}(x,z,u)-A_{s_{x}}e_{s_{x}}-A_{x}x^{r}-B_{x}r(t)

\displaystyle=A_{x}e_{x}+B_{x}(\hat{\tau}_{x}(x,z,u)-r(t)-K_{s_{x}}e_{s_{x}}).

3
High-Level Controller

We now design a high-level controller for the slow states,
\pi_{x}(t,x,\hat{x}):\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
, and for the fast states,
\pi_{z}(\vartheta,z,\hat{z}):\Theta\times\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}}
. In
Figure

1
, the high-level controller lies between the reference trajectory and control allocation blocks.

The goal is to choose the control input
u
and fast state command
\vartheta
such that the dynamics of
e_{x}
are exponentially stable in the form
\dot{e}_{x}=A_{r_{x}}e_{x}
, where
A_{r_{x}}
is of the same form as
A_{s_{x}}
and
K_{s_{x}}
is replaced by
K_{r_{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}k_{r_{x},1}&k_{r_{x},2}&\cdots&k_{r_{x},d}\end{bmatrix}>0

The resulting slow state high-level control law

is

\displaystyle\pi_{x}(t,\hat{x},x)=r(t)-K_{s_{x}}(x-\hat{x})-K_{r_{x}}(\hat{x}-x^{r}(t)).

(13)

The reduced-order model assumption requires that

. The desired equilibrium of the fast state predictor must then be
\hat{z}_{1}=\vartheta-e_{s_{z},1}
. Hence, the desired dynamics of
\hat{z}
are
\dot{\hat{z}}=A_{r_{z}}\hat{z}+B_{z}k_{r_{z},1}(\vartheta-e_{s_{z},1}),
where
A_{r_{z}}
is the fast state reference matrix, which has the same structure as

with the last

rows as
-K_{r_{z}}
, and

is the first element of

. Here, we introduce the parameter
\epsilon

as the time-scale separation between the slow and fast reference systems. In the next section, we use this specific form of

to put the fast system into singularly-perturbed standard form.
The fast reference matrix is defined as

\displaystyle A_{r_{z}}=\frac{1}{\epsilon}T(\epsilon)^{-1}\bar{A}_{r_{x}}T(\epsilon),

(14)

where
\bar{A}_{r_{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{dn_{z}\times dn_{z}}
has the same entries as

and

The resulting fast state feedback control constraint is then

\displaystyle\pi_{z}(z,\hat{z},\vartheta)=k_{r_{z},1}(\vartheta-(z_{1}-\hat{z}_{1}))-K_{s_{z}}(z-\hat{z})-K_{r_{z}}\hat{z}.

(15)

With the high-level controllers
\pi_{x}
and
\pi_{z}

and virtual input

that achieves
\hat{\tau}_{x}=\pi_{x}
and
\hat{\tau}_{z}=\pi_{z}
.

IV-
4
Control Allocation and Parameter Adaptation

The reduced system may be overactuated due to the additional virtual control inputs, i.e., the
\bar{u}
that achieves the desired
\hat{\tau}_{x}=\pi_{x}
and
\hat{\tau}_{z}=\pi_{z}
is not unique, which results in a control allocation problem. In
Figure

1
, the control allocation block acts as an inner loop controller, taking commands from the high-level controller and producing control inputs. The following time-varying control allocation problem returns the optimal
\bar{u}^{*}
that minimizes the cost
J
achieves the high-level control commands:

\displaystyle\min_{\bar{u}\in\bar{\mathcal{U}}}\quad

\displaystyle J(t,x,z,\bar{u})

(16a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\pi_{x}(t,x,\hat{x})=\bar{\tau}_{x}(x,\bar{u})

(16b)

\displaystyle\pi_{z}(\vartheta,z,\hat{z})=\hat{\tau}_{z}(x,z,u).

(16c)

In addition to penalizing the real control inputs

, the deviation between the external reference
z_{1}^{r}(t)
and the optimal command
\vartheta
is penalized, and is augmented with log-barrier functions to encode the input constraints
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{U}
as
J(t,x,z,\bar{u})=J_{u}(x,z,u)+\alpha(\vartheta-z_{1}^{r}(t))^{2}-\beta\sum_{i}\log(-C_{i}\bar{u}+c_{i})
, where
\alpha>0
,
0<\beta\ll 1
,
\bar{C}_{i}
is the
i
-th row of
\bar{C}
and
\bar{c}_{i}
is the
i
-th element of
\bar{c}
.

The Lagrangian of (

VII
Conclusion

We have presented an adaptive control framework for uncertain, non-affine in control, underactuated nonlinear systems. Our approach leverages a time-scale separation property together with dynamic control allocation. Using singular perturbation theory and Lyapunov analysis, we established stability guarantees and bounded tracking error of a reference trajectory. Finally, we demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed method on a
VTOL
quadplane with highly nonlinear dynamics and state-dependent levels of actuation.

Future work could involve adaptation to time-varying external disturbances, potentially applying modern machine learning paradigms such as meta learning. Another potential direction is using adaptation algorithms that guarantee exponential stability without persistence of excitation, removing the state predictor and simplifying the architecture.
