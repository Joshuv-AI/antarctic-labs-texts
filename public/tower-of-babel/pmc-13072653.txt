Effect of tactile–kinesthetic stimulation on breastfeeding performance among preterm neonates in a neonatal intensive care unit
Authors: Selvi S., Valarmathi V., Subash S., Helen Shaji J. C.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPreterm birth, which means the baby is born before the 37th week of pregnancy, is very often linked to a later start in breastfeeding and to feeding problems because the baby’s neuromotor system is not yet fully coordinated. Early sensory interventions like tactile-kinesthetic stimulation (TKS) can potentially improve the feeding skills and physiological stability of preterm babies.MethodsThis research, a quasi-experimental pre-test and post-test control group design, was carried out in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) of a tertiary care hospital. Forty eligible neonates were recruited using purposive sampling and allocated alternately to the experimental group (n = 20) and control group (n = 20). Neonates who met the inclusion criteria, such as gestational age 28–37 weeks, postnatal age 11–28 days at the time of data collection, birth weight 800 g to 1500 g, and delivery through either normal vaginal delivery or lower segment caesarean section (LSCS), were included in the study. The intervention was carried out by the researcher, who was a proficient neonatal stimulator. The study group underwent 30 min of tactile, kinesthetic stimulation, which included gentle stroking and passive leg movements, thrice a day for seven successive days before each breastfeed. On the other hand, the control group was given standard NICU care without any additional stimulation. Breastfeeding performance was assessed using the Preterm Infant Breastfeeding Behavior Scale (PIBBS). Data were analysed using paired t-tests and independent t-tests with SPSS software. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsBefore the intervention, the mean breastfeeding performance score was 4.80 ± 1.64 in the experimental group and 5.10 ± 1.50 in the control group, indicating that there was no significant difference between the two groups (P > 0.05). Following the intervention, the mean breastfeeding performance score in the experimental group substantially rose to 14.60 ± 1.57, while the control group experienced only a minor rise to 6.20 ± 1.45. The enhancement in breastfeeding performance in the experimental group was statistically significant when compared to the control group (t = 32.19, P < 0.001).ConclusionInitially, breastfeeding scores between the two groups were equivalent. Nevertheless, after tactile, kinesthetic stimulation, the experimental group showed a substantial enhancement in breastfeeding ability compared to the control group.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Preterm birth, defined as birth before 37 completed weeks of gestation, remains a major global health challenge because of the high risk of fetal and neonatal morbidity. and mortality being the major associated problems, along with long-term neurodevelopmental impairment [1, 2]. The inability to establish efficient breastfeeding is the most common problem in preterm infants [3, 4]. Due to undeveloped neuromotor coordination, premature babies are unable to efficiently perform the integrated acts of sucking, swallowing, and breathing, thus causing a delay in the weaning of tube feeding and initiation of breastfeeding directly and elevating the risk of feeding intolerance and poor weight gain [5, 6].

Early sensory and developmental care interventions have become more and more recognized as necessary parts of neonatal care [7]. Tactile stimulation, massage therapy, and skin-to-skin contact as intervention methods have been proven to lead to the improvement of physiological stability, enhancement of vagal tone, gastric motility promotion, and weight gain support [8]. These physiological changes are essential tasks that must be accomplished to have successful oral feeding and breastfeeding [9]. Moreover, oral stimulation procedures featuring peri-oral stroking and non-nutritive sucking have made significant contributions in lessening the period of time to become independently feeding orally [10]. Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) has been widely reported in the literature, and the effect is simply the earlier initiation and longer duration of exclusive breastfeeding, along with better thermoregulation and oxygenation [11, 12].

Tactile–kinesthetic stimulation (TKS), which involves structured stroking combined with passive limb movements, is a simple, low-cost, and non-pharmacological intervention widely used in neonatal intensive care settings [13]. Tactile–kinesthetic stimulation is one of the ways to regulate the autonomic nervous system. Besides that, it also increases gastric motility and, lastly, helps preterm neonates grow sufficiently by promoting weight gain [14]. Most of the previous research in this field, however, has mainly been centred around secondary outcomes, including weight gain and tolerance to enteral feeding. Breastfeeding-related outcomes such as latch quality, efficiency of sucking, duration of feeding, and behavioural readiness have been comparatively understudied [15].

Very few studies focus exclusively on breastfeeding behaviours and performances, particularly using standardised assessment tools; moreover, the results concerning direct breastfeeding success and exclusivity are still contradictory [2, 16]. It is crucial to fill this gap, especially in neonatal intensive care units, where delayed lactation and poor breastfeeding outcomes are typical for preterm neonates [17, 18].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of structured tactile–kinesthetic stimulation on breastfeeding performance among preterm neonates admitted to the NICU. Evidence from this study may support neonatal nurses and caregivers in integrating simple, developmentally supportive interventions into routine clinical practice to improve feeding competence, facilitate early nutrition, and enhance both short- and long-term health outcomes.

Hypotheses

H1

The mean breastfeeding performance scores of preterm neonates who receive tactile–kinesthetic stimulation differ significantly from those who receive routine care.

H2

A statistically significant relationship exists between selected demographic and clinical variables and the mean change in breastfeeding performance scores among preterm neonates.

Methodology

This study used a quasi-experimental pre-test, post-test control group design to evaluate the influence of tactile-kinesthetic stimulation (TKS) on breastfeeding efficiency of preterm babies who are admitted to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) of a SRM tertiary care teaching hospital. The independent variable was tactile–kinesthetic stimulation (TKS), and the dependent variable was breastfeeding performance measured using the Preterm Infant Breastfeeding Behaviour Scale (PIBBS). The purpose of this research was to check if well-organised tactile stimulation can be a means to enhance infants’ feeding readiness, rooting, latching, sucking, and swallowing behaviors.

The study population was preterm neonates with a gestational age of 28 to 37 weeks, postnatal age of 11 to 28 days, and birth weights ranging from 800 g to 1500 g. Neonates delivered through normal vaginal delivery or lower segment cesarean section (LSCS) and admitted to the NICU in an incubator, radiant warmer, or phototherapy unit were considered for the study. Exclusion criteria were neonates with critical illness, congenital anomalies, a history of surgical intervention, skin-related disorders, or those who had sedative medications. Parents had to be able to read and speak either English or Tamil for effective communication during data collection.

The sample size was calculated using the formula for comparison of two means with a 5% significance level and 80% power.

Based on preliminary observations and the sample size formula, a minimum of 18 neonates per group was required. The pilot study was conducted with 40 neonates to check the study procedures, intervention delivery, and data collection methods. To make sure of the feasibility and to include a buffer for attrition, the sample size was 40 neonates (20 in each group) recruited through a non-probability purposive sampling method. In order to minimize the chance of contamination, participants were allocated alternately to study and control groups.

Intervention was based on the particular TKS, which was provided by the researcher, who is a qualified neonatal nurse. The intervention was administered by a trained neonatal nurse to ensure consistency in the technique and timing of stimulation. Also, the parents were not given TKS training so as not to interfere with the study. All the neonates of the study group got TKS three times a day (9–11 am, 1–3 pm, and 5–7 pm) for seven days in a row, and each session was 30 min long. TKS consisted of light, even strokes of the scalp, back, arms and legs, after which limbs were moved passively by bending and straightening the joints. Safety was ensured by not touching areas of the body with central catheters or other vascular devices.

Both groups of infants kept receiving their usual treatment and care in the NICU, including standard gentle touching, in order to neutralize the effect of an additional touch. TKS treatment sessions were timed apart from other usual care so that there would be no interference, and the times of intervention would be restricted and recognised.

The Preterm Infant Breastfeeding Behavior Scale (PIBBS) measured preterm infant breastfeeding behavior. The PIBBS is a standardized observational scale that measures the breastfeeding skills of preterm neonates. It quantifies the infant feeding behavior such as rooting, areolar grasp, latching, sucking pattern, and swallowing during the act of breastfeeding. A high score on this scale means better breastfeeding performance.

Data on demographic and clinical characteristics such as age, sex, birth order, birth weight, Apgar score, head circumference, chest circumference, and length were obtained using a structured, content- validated tool.

The BPIBBS was used to assess breastfeeding behaviour. It measures readiness to feed, rooting, latching, sucking, and swallowing. Scoring is from 0 to 18, and the interpreting categories are poor (0–5), moderate [6–10], good [11–14], and excellent [15–17]. Evaluations were done 15–20 min after each TKS session by a NICU nurse trained and blinded to the group allocation [19].

The project got the ethical clearance through the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC No.: 8517/2023). Analysis of the data was performed through SPSS version 26. Baseline characteristics were summarized by descriptive statistics; paired t-tests were used to determine within-group pre- and post-test differences, Independent t-tests were used to identify between-group differences and to examine the association between selected demographic and clinical variables and breastfeeding outcome scores. P < 0.05 was the threshold for statistical significance [20].

Results

Most of the neonates in both groups were 11–15 days old, and the females were the dominant participants. The majority of neonates had a birth weight of 1201–1500 g and an Apgar score of 7–8. Anthropometrical parameters such as head circumference, chest circumference, and length were also similar in both groups (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study evaluated the effectiveness of tactile–kinesthetic stimulation (TKS) administered by a trained neonatal nursing investigator on breastfeeding outcomes among preterm neonates and examined the association between selected demographic and clinical variables and breastfeeding performance. The findings demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in breastfeeding scores in the experimental group compared with the control group, indicating that structured tactile–kinesthetic stimulation can substantially enhance feeding behaviour in preterm neonates.

In addition, Significant associations were observed between breastfeeding outcomes and demographic factors such as age and gender, as well as clinical parameters including birth weight, Apgar score, head circumference, chest circumference, and length. These findings suggest that both intrinsic physiological maturity and contextual family-related factors influence breastfeeding success in this vulnerable population [22, 23].

The clinical variables, particularly chest circumference and length, are among those that may have the greatest impact on neonatal growth; moreover, they have a big impact on respiratory stability and developmental maturity. In order to be able to breastfeed, preterm neonates should have good coordination between the three actions of sucking, swallowing, and breathing. Besides these, the infant should be able to breathe well, have good neuromuscular control, and have enough energy left. Birth weight and Apgar score have been widely used as markers of a baby’s stabilising physiological conditions and getting used to life outside the womb right after birth [10]. Head circumference is a measure of brain development and thus has an impact on the baby’s capability of coordinating oral motor functions, whereas chest size is a measure of thoracic growth and respiratory ability, which is necessary for continuous feeding [24]. The underlying hypothesis of this study was that preterm neonates demonstrating greater physiological and anthropometric maturity would achieve significantly higher breastfeeding performance scores compared to less mature neonates and would respond more effectively to tactile–kinesthetic stimulation [25]. The major associations found in this study confirm the developmental and physiological explanation [26].

The experimentally observed enhancement in breastfeeding scores in the experimental group is most likely due to the direct physiological impact of tactile–kinesthetic stimulation [12]. Soft, methodical tactile stimulation along with passive limb movement can potently rejuvenate the central nervous system, regulate the infant’s neurobehavioral state, and support the autonomic functioning in preterm babies [27]. These changes at the physiological level probably result in better synchronisation of sucking, swallowing, and breathing in the infant, which leads to more effective feeding [15]. Preterm babies usually have feeding problems because their nerve-muscle system is still underdeveloped and they are not able to feed for long periods; therefore, tactile–kinesthetic stimulation helps to improve their sensory and motor integration and makes the baby more ready for oral feeding. The results of this study provide evidence that a well-planned developmental stimulation program can enhance breastfeeding skills if it is performed at a physiologically stable time in the NICU environment [28, 29]. One of the merits of this research is the quasi-experimental pretest, posttest control group design, which substantially increases internal validity and allows for the evaluation of intervention effectiveness; the employment of a standardized breastfeeding evaluation tool, which increases measurement reliability; partial blinding through separate outcome assessment, which lessens observer bias; as well as the consideration of multiple demographic and clinical factors, which enables a detailed analysis of the determinants influencing breastfeeding performance [8]. Moreover, the fact that the study was carried out in an actual NICU environment makes the findings highly clinically relevant and translatable [30].

On the other hand, a few drawbacks should be recognised. Non-randomised purposive sampling may lead to selection bias. The relatively small number of participants and the study being based at a single centre limit the generalizability of the results. Moreover, the researchers mainly concentrated on the early breastfeeding outcomes and did not check if breastfeeding was continued for a long time after discharge. Along with that, the psychosocial, maternal, and cultural factors that impact breastfeeding practices were not delved into in great detail. These aspects could affect both the interpretation and external validity of the study results. Nevertheless, the tactile–kinesthetic stimulation regimen is not only practicable but also repeatable in other similar NICUs as long as nursing staff are trained, standard operating procedures are followed, and there are support systems at the institutional level. Additional multicenter randomised controlled trials (RCTs) with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm the findings, establish greater causality, and enhance the generalizability of the results.

Limitations

There are a number of limitations to this study. First of all, we employed a quasi-experimental design, which might complicate the unambiguous establishment of causal relations between tactile-kinesthetic stimulation and breastfeeding performance. Moreover, the sample was quite limited (n = 40), and the setting was a single tertiary care NICU. The small sample size and single setting may make it difficult for the findings to be applicable to other contexts. Besides that, the study only evaluated breastfeeding performance in the short run during NICU stay while the long-term breastfeeding results after discharge were not measured. On top of that, maternal and psychosocial factors affecting breastfeeding were hardly taken into consideration. Therefore, larger sample sizes, multicenter studies, and longer follow-up periods are some of the options for future studies that can be used to verify these results.

Conclusion

The study demonstrated that preterm neonates who received structured tactile–kinesthetic stimulation showed significantly improved breastfeeding performance compared with those receiving routine care. This simple developmental care intervention may enhance feeding readiness and breastfeeding success among preterm infants.
