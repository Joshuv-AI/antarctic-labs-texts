RAGE phosphorylation by CK2 in human epithelial cell model: Modulation of connexin 43 expression
Authors: Karen Coste, Anne-Marie Hesse, Geoffroy Marceau, Odile Filhol, Claude Cochet, Céline Freton, Loïc Blanchon, Vincent Sapin, Corinne Belville
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) is a membrane protein involved in many diseases linked to epithelial dysfunction. Its activation mechanism remains unclear because RAGE lacks intrinsic kinase activity depending on other kinases for phosphorylation. Using an epithelial amniotic cell model (FL cells), researchers combined mass spectrometry, phosphorylation assays, microscale thermophoresis to show that RAGE intracellular domain binds to the α subunit of protein kinase CK2, leading to the phosphorylation of RAGE at serine 400. Ser400Ala mutation preserves CK2α binding but disrupts downstream signaling, altering phosphorylation of the transcription factors CREB (Ser133) and c-Jun (Ser63) after AGE ligands stimulation. In silico analyses identify binding sites for these two transcription factors in the connexin 43 promoter/UTR, a known RAGE target, associated with the overexpression of its mRNA. Overall, these findings highlight that phosphorylation at serine 400 is essential for RAGE signaling and transcriptional regulation in response to AGE ligands.

Introduction

Cellular processes are numerous, complex, and interdependent, requiring harmonious coordination to achieve optimal functions. They are regulated by both exogenous and endogenous factors, involving numerous intracellular signaling pathways. Among those potential actors is the transmembrane receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), a well-known receptor implicated in sterile inflammatory diseases, such as metabolic disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and cancers. At the cellular level, the action of the RAGE is associated with essential cellular processes, including inflammation, migration, proliferation, autophagy, apoptosis,1,2,3 and epithelial-mesenchymal transition.4

The RAGE is a glycoprotein of 404 amino acids belonging to the immunoglobulin superfamily. It consists in a long extracellular domain (342 aa) responsible for ligand binding, consisting of a variable V and two constant (C1 and C2) regions, a transmembrane helix domain, and a short cytoplasmic tail (43 amino acids) responsible for intracellular signaling transduction.5,6 The RAGE binds to a wide array of ligands, with high affinity binding requiring receptor multimerization. The most well-known of these are the damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) family, including glycated proteins, such as advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), S100 proteins, HMGB1, and β-amyloids.7 The accumulation of AGEs and their specific binding to the RAGE are central to the pathogenesis responsible for various chronic inflammatory diseases. In particular, the AGEs-RAGE complex activates multiple signaling pathways and leads to cellular dysfunction and disease progression, making it a critical target for therapeutic intervention.8 Unlike many membrane receptors with intrinsic kinase activity capable of directly phosphorylating proteins within signaling cascades, the RAGE lacks a kinase domain. Sakagushi et al. first described the phosphorylation of the RAGE in its intracytoplasmic part at Ser391 by PKCζ upon ligand binding.9 This phosphorylation event facilitates the recruitment of adaptor proteins, such as Dia-1, TIRAP, and MyD88, propagating the signal and activating downstream signal pathways, including RHO/ROCK, Rac1/Cdc42, P38/MAPK, Pi3K/AKT, JNK, Ras/ERK 1/2, JAK, JNK/MAPK, JAK/STAT, and transcription factors, such as NF-kB, AP-1, STAT3, and EGR-1.10,11

Protein kinase CK2 is a ubiquitous kinase that phosphorylates protein substrates on serine or threonine residues. It is composed of two catalytic subunits, α (391 amino acids) and/or α' (350 amino acids, lacking the C-terminal domain), which share a conserved catalytic domain, and two regulatory subunits β (215 amino acids), responsible for substrate recognition and protein stability. The predominant form of CK2 is a tetrameric complex consisting of two catalytic subunits (αα or αα’) and two regulatory subunits (ββ), which use ATP or GTP as phosphate donors.12,13 Notably, the catalytic subunits are constitutively active and can independently phosphorylate substrate without the β subunit.14 Despite a 90% sequence identity, CK2α and CK2α’ exhibit differential tissue distribution and activity during cell-cycle phases. With the ability to phosphorylate over 300 substrates, CK2 is a major contributor to the human phosphoproteome.15 It plays a pivotal role in diseases, such as diabetes, neurodegenerative disorders, and cancers, given its involvement in fundamental biological processes, such as cell growth, survival, and intracellular signaling.15,16 Such dysregulations are already described in different human epithelial pathophysiological models, such as colonic epithelial cells participating in acute colitis pathogenesis, renal proximal tubule epithelial cells responsible for fibrosis, and the coordinated adhesion and migration of mammary epithelial cells.17,18,19

As previously described, CK2 and RAGE are independently involved in common pathophysiological inflammatory processes like neurodegenerative disorders, vascular diseases, and cancers. Moreover, they are co-expressed in the same tissue as in the epithelial part of amniotic membranes during parturition, supporting the use of the FL amnion—derived cell line model.20 RAGE could be a novel CK2 substrate? Here, a combination of in silico and in vitro analyses using epithelial cell line FL reveals that in the presence of AGEs ligands, CK2 binds to and phosphorylates the RAGE at Ser400. We show that a Ser400Ala mutation in the RAGE co-activates several specific intracellular kinases and c-Jun/CREB transcription factors, leading to the overexpression of CX43 mRNA, a RAGE target gene.

For this, FL cells transfected with GFP-RAGE were subjected to immunoprecipitation using an anti-GFP antibody, and a kinase assay was then performed in the presence of recombinant CK2 and [32P]-ATP. We showed a band corresponding to the phosphorylated RAGE that drastically reduced in the presence of the specific CK2 inhibitor CX-4945 (Figure 3A). In silico analysis identified three potential targets at serine 391, 399, 400, and one at threonine 401 within the RAGE intracellular tail (Figure 3B). Additionally, the sequence alignment illustrated in Figure 3B showed that these serine residues were highly conserved across species, highlighting their potential functional importance in RAGE signaling. To verify these predictions and identify the specific phosphorylation sites, mass spectrometry analysis was performed on FL cells treated or not treated with the specific CK2 inhibitor CX-4945. The results confirmed the phosphorylation of the RAGE at serine 391, 399, and 400. Since the three mono-phosphorylated peptides were nearly co-eluted, parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) analysis was conducted to validate the presence of all three forms. PRM provided high quality spectra with high scores and confidences in the localization of the phosphorylated sites (Figure 3C top). The extracted ion chromatogram of the y13 fragments from the PRM acquisition of the mono-phosphorylated peptide confirmed the elution of the serine 391 phosphorylated form at 11.5 min, preceding the elution of the serine 399 and serine 400 phosphorylated forms at 11.9 min. Since these two forms were co-eluted, we used the y7 fragments to distinguish the signals between the serine 399 and serine 400 in order to quantify each form separately. To assess the impact of the inhibitor, we measured the areas of specific fragments for each phosphorylated form (y13 m/z 623.2889 Th for Ser391, y7 m/z 675.3420 Th for Ser399, and y7 m/z 755.3084 Th for Ser400) in samples treated or not treated with CX-4945. The extracted ion area of the y13 fragment from the PRM acquisition of the unphosphorylated peptide was also measured for normalization and to correct for variation in peptide input levels. Notably, CX-4945 treatment led to a 59% reduction in the phosphorylation level of Ser400 compared to the untreated condition (Figure 3C bottom). In light of these results, we decided to focus on Ser400 in the following experiments.

RNA and protein expression levels of WT-RAGE (RAGE-GFP) and the mutants were quantified in FL cells. The mRNA levels of RAGE-GFP and Δcyto-RAGE-GFP were higher than GFP alone (empty plasmid), and a trend toward increased expression was observed for Ser400Ala-GFP (adjusted p value = 0.0812). To address the question of why the deletion mutant was less expressed, we performed TUNEL assays. As shown in Figure 4C, enhanced apoptosis in FL cells expressing the Δcyto-RAGE-GFP mutant may explain its decreased expression.

We then verified that mutations did not suppress the cell membrane addressing signal of the RAGE. Immunofluorescence and confocal analysis revealed that GFP labeling was present at the plasma membrane for RAGE-GFP and the mutants, unlike GFP alone, which localized to the intracellular compartment (Figure 4D left). Surface biotin labeling also confirmed the presence of Δcyto-RAGE in plasma membranes, albeit at reduced levels (Figure 4D right). These results indicate that all the mutants were correctly expressed and targeted to the plasma membrane of the FL cells.

Two hydrogen bonds were identified, the first between E19 of the RAGE and Y50 of CK2α with a length of 2.364 Å, and the second between E23 of the RAGE and K77 of CK2α with a length of 2.802 Å (Figure 5B). In addition, analysis revealed the presence of two hydrophobic interactions, one between W2 of the RAGE and L41, I69 of CK2α, and another between L27 of the RAGE and K49, K74, and Y50 of CK2α (Figure 5C). Three regions of electrostatic interactions were also identified—the first between E26 of the RAGE and K77, L178, N189, and R191 of CK2α (Figure 5D left); the second between E11 of the RAGE and R47 of CK2α, as well as between E19 of the RAGE and K49 and R195 of CK2α (Figure 5D middle); and the third between E22 of the RAGE and R195 of CK2α, and between E23 of the RAGE and L178 of CK2α (Figure 5D right).

To further investigate this protein-protein interaction, microscale thermophoresis (MST) was used to evaluate the affinity of the RAGE extracted from FL cells with recombinant CK2α or CK2β because the classical RAGE-CK2 complex could not be successfully demonstrated by immunoprecipitation, likely due to limitations related to our antibodies. This analysis confirmed the absence of binding between the RAGE and CK2β (Figure 5E top), whereas the RAGE interacted with CK2α with an EC50 value of 5 μM (Figures 5E bottom and 5F). Furthermore, the Ser400Ala mutation did not affect this interaction (Figure 5F).

Impact of Ser400Ala mutation on RAGE intracellular signaling

To evaluate the cellular effect of Ser400 phosphorylation of the RAGE on AGEs-induced intracellular pathways, we analyzed the phosphorylation status of multiple kinases in FL lysates using a proteome profiler phosphokinase array. In this analysis, the relative levels of phosphorylation of 37 proteins were evaluated, and we retained the data, which gave identical results between 3 independent experiments, as illustrated in Figure S2. RAGE Ser400Ala mutant upregulates connexin 43 RNA

A previous work identified connexin 43 (CX43), a gap junction structural protein, as a RAGE target gene in corneal epithelial cell lines (HCE).2 In FL cells, RAGE knockdown by siRNA resulted in a moderate reduction in CX43 mRNA expression, however confirming this relationship (Figure 6C). Interestingly, compared to cells expressing RAGE-GFP, CX43 mRNA levels were elevated in Ser400Ala-GFP-expressing cells after 24 h of AGEs ligand treatment (Figure 6D).

To reinforce the importance of RAGE phosphorylation for CX43 transcriptional regulation, we investigated the presence of response elements for c-Jun and CREB, two transcription factors differentially phosphorylated in the presence of Ser400Ala, using bioinformatic studies with the open-access database JASPAR 2024 (Figure 6E). The results revealed two consensus sequences (relative score = 1) for the TPA response element (TRE)/AP1 (TGA(C/G)TCA) at positions −47 to −41 and −1004 to −997, where dimers c-Jun/JunB, c-Fos/c-Jun, and BATF/c-Jun could hybridize. Moreover, regarding the lower relative score (close to 0.950), other dimers such as Fra-1/c-Jun and Fra-2/c-Jun could interfere at positions −1004 to −997. Dimers c-Jun/c-Jun could interact at three other regions (−474 to −462, −1004 to −991, and −1282 to −(269) but with 0.904–0.931 relative scores (Table S1). No consensus c-AMP response element (CRE, TGACGTCA) was found, instead, variant sites (CREB∗) were identified at positions +162 to +169 (TGAAGTCA, relative score = 0.894), −1013 to −1006 (TGAGGCCA, relative score = 0.890), and −2273 to −2266 (TGATTTCA, relative score = 0.880) (Table S2).

Discussion

Activation of RAGE is a complex process that plays an important role in several biological responses including inflammation, oxidative stress, and cell migration. Similar to cell surface receptors, RAGE signaling depends on its oligomerization state. Li et al. demonstrated that heparin sulfate-induced RAGE oligomerization is essential for RAGE signaling. This molecule, present in the extracellular matrix, maintains the RAGE in an active oligomeric complex at the cell surface through electrostatic interactions with its VC1 extracellular domain.22 This complex is stabilized by the C2 extracellular domain and the transmembrane region.23 High affinity binding of AGEs—products of the Maillard reaction implicated in pathologies such as diabetes—to the V domain also stabilizes the preformed oligomeric complex and triggers signaling cascades.6,24 This activation is enhanced by the phosphorylation of serine 391 in its intracellular domain by PKCζ, a process dependent on AGEs concentrations9 and required for activation of the inflammatory cascade.25

Given the limited understanding of RAGE activation mechanisms, we decided to characterize them in an epithelial cell model derived from the human amnion from fetal membranes. The RAGE plays a crucial role in sterile inflammation and mesenchyme-epithelial transition, two mechanisms implicated in the premature rupture of fetal membranes when they are exacerbated.26 Moreover, our previous findings showed that in amnion at term, the RAGE and CK2 are co-expressed and exhibit a similar epithelial cellular localization, leading to the use of the epithelial-derived amniotic cell model.20 We hypothesized that CK2 could phosphorylate RAGE in the intracellular domain. This is supported by bioinformatic analysis, which identified potential CK2-dependent phosphorylation sites, such as Ser391, Ser399, Ser400, and Thr401. To study in vitro RAGE signaling, we overexpressed GFP-tagged RAGE (RAGE-GFP) in the FL cell line and confirmed its functionality, leading to the activation of NF-ΚB. Mass spectrometry analyses confirmed the phosphorylation of serine 391 and led to the discovery of new phosphorylation sites at serine 399 and 400 in the intracellular domain. The short cytosolic tail of the RAGE is crucial for downstream signaling. Indeed, truncated RAGE isoforms lacking its full cytoplasmic domain (Δcyto-RAGE) or the splice variant of RAGE (ΔICD) act as dominant-negative mutants.27 While capable of ligand binding, these isoforms disrupt interactions with intracellular protein adaptors, preventing signaling pathway activation.28 Moreover, as already described for RAGE ΔICD in rat C6 glioma cells ΔICD,27 we found that the expression of Δcyto-RAGE in FL cells is correlated with enhanced cellular apoptosis, demonstrating that the RAGE has an anti-apoptotic activity in these cells. In contrast to the N-terminal region of the intracellular domain, which is folded into α-turns and positively charged, facilitating interactions with Dia-129 or Erk-1/2,30 serine 391 is located in the negatively charged central domain and has the ability to interact with the adaptor proteins TIRAP or MyD88 in the presence of AGEs. Serine 400, situated in the unstructured C-terminal region of the cytoplasmic tail, plays a distinct role.

Among selective CK2 inhibitors, CX-4945 is primarily used in fundamental research as a potent ATP competitive inhibitor of CK2 and in cancer therapy under the name Silmitasertib. Using this latter, we demonstrated that serine 400 phosphorylation is CK2 dependent, with no modification for serine 391 (which its known to be phosphorylated by PKCζ9) and serine 399. Serine 400 phosphorylation occurs within a non-consensus CK2 site lacking an acidic residue at the +3 position on the C-terminal side, as previously described for Serine 389 of p53.31 The folding of RAGE could position a cluster of aspartate residues located N-terminal to Serine 400 in an appropriate orientation, thereby promoting its phosphorylation. Moreover, CK2 is well documented to phosphorylate non-consensus sites, particularly within intrinsically disordered regions or in the presence of distal acidic clusters. Consistent with this, phosphorylation of this site was strongly reduced upon pharmacological inhibition, and was efficiently catalyzed by recombinant CK2 in vitro. These observations support CK2 as the responsible kinase despite the absence of a canonical motif.

In silico cytoplasmic RAGE-CK2α or cytoplasmic RAGE-CK2β complex structure analyses were also carried out. These showed that the CK2β regulatory subunit does not interact with the RAGE, while the CK2α catalytic subunit binds to this latter. Indeed, in silico molecular modeling suggests that the negatively charged glutamic acids 19, 23, and 26 of the RAGE bind to the positively charged lysines 74, 77, and 79 and arginines 191 and 195 of CK2α by an array of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions. These results are particularly interesting because these basic residues have already been identified in two distinct regions (74–83 and 191–198) implicated in substrate recognition32 and in negative regulation by the CK2β subunit.33 Recent technologies like microscale thermophoresis (MST) are particularly advantageous for studying dynamic, transient, or weak kinase-substrate interactions because it requires no antibodies, preserves native binding and provides quantitative measurements—unlike classical immunoprecipitation. MST assays confirmed the RAGE-CK2α interaction characterized by an EC50 of 5 μM.

Functional studies of Ser400 phosphorylation revealed its importance in guiding downstream signaling. Our findings show that while the Ser400Ala mutation did not affect CK2α binding or RAGE membrane addressing, it altered intracellular RAGE signaling upon AGE stimulation. Specifically, Ser400Ala-GFP expression led to changes in phosphorylation levels of key protein kinases, such as RSK1/2, ERK1/2, Lyn, AKT, and two transcription factors: CREB (Ser133) and c-Jun (Ser63), which share the basic leucine zipper (bZIP) motif. These last play a key role in the specific DNA binding to response element sequence and to the dimerization of the transcription factors. These observations suggest that the phosphorylation at Ser400 may be important to guide the activation of target genes and associated cellular processes. Indeed, the mutation of Ser400 to Ala in the RAGE has the same functional consequence as disrupting the interaction between the C-terminal domain of the RAGE and the adaptor protein Dia-1 using RAGE229 (N-[4-[7-Cyano-4-(4-morpholinylmethyl)-2-quinolinyl]phenyl]acetamide), a pharmacological inhibitor that has been shown to decrease the inflammatory response in diabetic mice.34

Moreover, it has been shown that phosphorylation is increased by different stimuli, such as an increase of glucose concentrations in inflammatory diseases or following oxidative stress.35 Similarly, in rats subjected to a high fat and high sugar liquid diet, a rich source of AGEs, RAGE229 was found to activate CREB phosphorylation and prevent diet-induced anhedonia.36

Phosphorylation of c-Jun at Ser63 was also increased in cells expressing the RAGE mutant Ser400Ala. This bZIP can bind to different partners (Jun, Fos, Maf, and ATF) to form a homo- or a heterodimeric-complex called AP-1 factor transcription.37 Among MAPKs, c-Jun NH2-terminal kinase (JNK) and p38 MAPK are the main kinases described for phosphorylate c-Jun at Ser63 and Ser73 in its N-terminal transactivation domain, potentiating its capacity to activate transcription.38 In this study, we describe an increase of c-Jun phosphorylation at Ser63 after transfection of the RAGE Ser400 mutant, which was independent of the phosphorylation of JNK1/2/3 (Thr83/Tyr185, Thr221/Tyr183) and p38 MAPK (Thr180/Tyr182). These results have also been described in mouse epithelial cells, where reduced activation of JNK and p38 MAPK was not associated with inconsistent phosphorylation of c-Jun at Ser63 and Ser73, suggesting that other kinases are also responsible as mediators for c-Jun activation.39

To understand the impact of the Ser400Ala-RAGE mutant on signaling pathways in the FL cell line model, we assessed the expression of CX43, a well-established RAGE target protein2 involved in gap junctions, particularly in promoting cell-cell adhesion.40 As previously reported,41 we confirmed that the siRNA-mediated reduced expression of the RAGE lead to a significant, although moderate, decrease in CX43 expression in FL cells, validated CX43 as a RAGE target gene. Forced expression of the Ser400Ala RAGE mutant induced a 2-fold increase of CX43 mRNA. Our data, in an epithelial model, are in accordance with previous studies showing that CX43 expression was positively regulated by CREB and c-Jun (in the heterodimer AP1 complex) in rat nucleus cardiomyocytes and in the myometrium.42

To determine whether c-JUN and/or CREB might be involved in this upregulation, we performed an in silico analysis of transcription factor-binding sites in the CX43 promoter (−2812 to +187). Our analysis revealed that AP1 could bind to the CX43 promoter at two consensus TRE/AP1 sites (TGA(C/G)TCA) (−47 to −41 and −1004 to −997), implicating dimers c-Jun/JunB, c-Fos/c-Jun, and BATF/c-Jun. Geimonen et al. confirmed by mutagenesis experiments that the proximal AP1 site (−47 to −41) plays a crucial role in the regulation of CX43 expression in primary human myometrial cells treated with tetradecanoyl phorbol acetate (TPA). The increase in CX43 protein levels was concomitant with an increase in c-Jun and c-Fos levels.43 However, based on supershift and overexpression experiments, only c-Jun was shown to bind to this proximal AP1 site, either as a homodimer or as a heterodimer associated with other FOS family members or other transcription factors, such as CREB/ATF, in the same cellular model.44

The CX43 promoter lacks a canonical CRE (TGACGTCA) consensus site for a CREB homodimer but contains a degenerated sequence without the core CGTCA motif. Since this sequence differs from the AP1/TRE by only a single nucleotide insertion, an overlap could generate a new response element shared by both CREB and AP1, potentially affecting CX43 transcription. This type of interaction has been previously described. Indeed, CREB and c-Jun can bind together to common response elements such as CRE2/AP1, modulating StAR gene expression in MA-10 mouse Leydig tumor cells.45

In conclusion, our in vitro mechanistic study highlights the critical role of CK2 in RAGE phosphorylation. Limitations of the study

In summary, these results revealed that Ser400Ala plays an important role in RAGE signaling in an amniotic epithelial cell line model. It would be interesting to confirm these findings in other epithelial cell lines derived from human fetal membranes or in primary cultures of amniocytes after delivery. Furthermore, comparing epithelial cells from other organs or different cell types would help determine whether this mechanism is specific to fetal membranes or a more ubiquitous physiological phenomenon.

We also show that protein kinase CK2 is composed of two catalytic subunits, CK2α and CK2α’, in FL cells. In this study, we focused on the CK2α subunit (the most described in the literature) and demonstrated that it phosphorylates the C-terminal domain of RAGE. What about the other catalytic subunit, CK2α’, which has a shorter structure of 41 amino acids? Could this size difference influence its affinity for binding to RAGE?

We confirmed using a siRNA approach that CX43 is a RAGE target gene. However, the observed knockdown was moderate in FL cells which are difficult to transfect. These results should therefore be confirmed by using another human epithelial amniotic cell line.

Using a human phospho-kinase array, we identified four cytosolic kinases and two transcription factors that were differentially expressed after RAGE-GFP or Ser400Ala transfection. Further work is needed to clarify how these kinases activate c-Jun and/or CREB, ultimately leading to Cx43 mRNA expression. Moreover, the CX43 promotology analyses would help to identify the true binding sites of CREB or c-Jun.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell line

Human amnion epithelial cells (FL cells) from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, CCL-62) were cultured in DMEM/F12 + GlutaMAX™-I (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Eurobio), 100 units/ml of penicillin, 100 μg/ml of streptomycin, and 0.25 μg/ml of amphotericin B (Eurobio). Cells were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The cells are tested to be free of mycoplasma contamination by PCR method (Eurofins Genomics).

Method details

Cell treatments

Twenty-four hours after transfection, FL cells were treated with AGEs (100 μg/ml, Abcam) for 24 h in DMEM/F12 + GlutaMAX™-I for the study of mRNA expression and 48 h for the protein.

CX-4945 is in solution in DMSO in initial concentration at 5 mM and stored at -20°C. One day after transfection of FL cells, the serum was deprived and replaced with AGEs (100 μg/ml, Abcam) for 18 h before treatment with CX-4945 (Abmole Bioscience Inc) at 5 μM (final concentration) for 6 h. The control phosphorylation in the absence of the inhibitor was treated by the same amount of DMSO (0.1% DMSO) and AGEs.

RAGE mutant DNA constructs

Expression vectors pCMV6-AC-tGFP and RAGE cDNA (NM_001136) cloned in 5′ of the C-terminal turbo-GFP (tGFP) were obtained from OriGene. This latter was used as a template for site-directed mutagenesis to generate Ser400Ala-GFP. Briefly, site-directed mutagenesis by PCR was performed using the following primers: F: 5′ GAGGCAGGCGAGAGTGCTACTGGAGGGCCT 3′,

R: 5′ AGGCCCTCCAGTAGCACTCTCGCCTGCCTC 3′, and Phusion™ High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Twenty μl of the PCR product was digested with Dpn I (New England Biolabs) for 3 h at 37°C to eliminate template DNA. One μl of the latter was used to transform into TOP10 competent E. coli bacteria (Invitrogen).

Human Δcyto-RAGE from pcDNA3 (Addgene) was amplified by PCR using primers with the following restriction sites: F-Sgf I: 5′ GCGATCGCATGGCAGCCGGAACAGCAG 3′, R-Mlu I: 5′ ACGCGTCCGCCTTTGCCACAAGATG 3′. After digestion with these enzymes (New England Biolabs), products were cloned into Sgf I/Mlu I digested pCMV6-AC-tGFP.

DNA constructs were checked after DNA sequencing (Eurofins Genomics).

Transfection

The FL cells were plated in six-well plates (12.105 cells/well). At 80–90% of confluence, the cells were transiently transfected using the Lipofectamine® 3000 Transfection Kit (Invitrogen) according to the protocol with 2 μg of each expressing GFP plasmid. SiRNA transfections were performed as described in Gross et al.2 After different times of culture according to the protocol used, GFP cells, total RNA and proteins were collected.

Quantitative RT-PCR

The cells were harvested and the total RNA was extracted from transfected FL cells using the Nucleospin RNA (Macherey-Nagel) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After quantification with a DS-11Fx spectrophotometer (DeNovix), cDNA was obtained from 1 μg of RNA using a SuperScript™ IV First-Strand Synthesis System for RT-PCR (Invitrogen). Quantitative RT-PCR reactions were carried out using a LightCycler® 480 (Roche) and the Power SYBR® Green Master Mix (Roche). Primer details are listed in Table 1. Transcripts were quantified using a standard curve method using LightCycler® 480 Software release 1.5.1.62. All the steps followed the MIQE guidelines.48 Two human housekeeping genes (RPLP0 and RPS17) were used for normalization in each individual sample. The ratio of gene interest transcript/geometric mean was used to calculate relative expression changes. Western blot

The FL cells were lysed in a RIPA buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH = 7.5 (Invitrogen), 150 mM NaCl (Merck), 1% Nonidet P40 (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.5% sodium deoxycholate (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 mM EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.1% SDS (Euromedex), 1x Complete Protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) for 30 min at 4°C. After measuring the protein concentration, protein extracts were prepared in Laemmli sample buffer containing 3% (V/V) of β-mercaptoethanol and heated at 95°C for 5 min. Thirty μg of total proteins were separated on 4–15% Mini-PROTEAN TGX Stain-Free gel electrophoresis (Bio-Rad) in Tris/Glycine/SDS running buffer. Gels were activated using UV illumination according to the manufacturer’s instructions and imaged to verify protein loading and separation. Proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes using Trans-Blot® Turbo™ (Bio-Rad) for 7min at 1.3A and 25V. Membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat dry milk in TBS-Tween 20 (0.1%) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. After washing in TBS-Tween 20, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence and visualized with Clarity Western ECL substrate (Bio-Rad) using the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System and Image Lab software (version 6.0.1. build 34; Bio-Rad). The relative protein ratio was normalized with the total protein loaded per well20 using the stain-free signal.20 Data were extracted from at least 3 independent experiments, each performed in one technical replicate.

Confocal microscopy

Transfected FL cells were grown onto coverslips, fixed in paraformaldehyde (4% in PBS, Electron Microscopy Sciences), blocked with PBS 1X (Euromedex), TritonX-100 0.1% (Euromedex) and SVF 10% (Eurobio). The cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with the indicated primary antibodies. A secondary antibody was incubated for 2 h on slides, followed by Hoechst nuclear staining (Invitrogen) for 20 min. The cells were examined under an LSM800 confocal microscope (Zeiss) and the application Zen 2.3 (blue edition). For negative controls, samples were incubated without the primary antibody.

NF-κB luciferase assay

The FL cells were co-transfected with 1 μg pNF-κB-luc (Agilent) in combination with 1 μg RAGE-GFP or 1 μg GFP plasmid (negative control) with 50 ng of pRL-TK Renilla luciferase plasmid (Promega). After washing in PBS 1x, the cells were lysed for 20 min under rocking in 500 μl of passive lysis buffer (Promega). Twenty μl were analyzed for Firefly and Renilla luciferase activity according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Dual Luciferase reporter assay system, Promega) using a Sirius luminometer (Berthold). Transfection efficiencies were normalized to the Renilla luciferase activities in the corresponding wells and reported to the GFP condition. Data were extracted from 5 independent experiments, each performed in two technical replicates.

In silico analysis of potential sites of RAGE phosphorylation

Putative sites of RAGE phosphorylation were screened using the public database GPS.6 (https://gps.biocuckoo.cn/online.php), PhosphosSitePlus (https://www.phosphosite.org), or dbPTM (https://biomics.lab.nycu.edu.tw/dbPTM).

Immunoprecipitation

FL cells were grown on a 10 cm plate (7.106 cells/plate). Forty-eight hours after RAGE-GFP constructs transfection, the cells were lysed in 50 mM Tris-HCl (Invitrogen), 150 mM NaCl (Merck), 1% Triton X-100 (Euromedex), and 1x Complete Protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) for 30 min at 4°C. After centrifugation (10 min, 8000 rpm, 4°C) and protein assay (BCA protein assay kit, Pierce), 1 mg total protein was immunoprecipitated with 3 μg anti-tGFP (OriGene) coated with superparamagnetic nanoparticles conjugated with protein A and G (Master IP, Ademtech) for 30 min at room temperature in AutoMag solution (Ademtech). Immunoprecipitates were washed four times at room temperature for 30 sec with 50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Triton X-100, and 1x Complete Protease inhibitor cocktail. Beads were collected in 40 μl kinase buffer (mass spectrometry analysis) or eluted in glycine buffer 50 mM, pH = 3 (Euromedex) and neutralized with 1M Tris-HCl pH = 9.

In vitro RAGE phosphorylation by CK2

RAGE immobilized on GFP magnetic beads (Master IP, Ademtech) after immunoprecipitation was incubated for 25 min at 30°C with 30 ng of purified recombinant CK2α49 in a 20 μl final volume of kinase buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8 (Invitrogen), 5 mM MgCl2 (Sigma-Aldrich), 100 mM KCl (Sigma-Aldrich), 10 mM β-Mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich), and 50 μM ATP (Sigma-Aldrich)) and 2.5 μCi of 32P-γ-ATP (NEN). Reactions were stopped by adding reducing sample buffer, separated by SDS–PAGE, and analyzed using autoradiography.

Mass spectrometry–based proteomic analyses

After immunopurification, proteins were solubilized in Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad) and separated on a 4–12% NuPAGE gel (Invitrogen) before R-250 Coomassie blue staining. The gel bands were manually excised and digested using modified Trypsin (sequencing grade, Promega) as previously described.50 The dried extracted peptides were resuspended in 5% Acetonitrile (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.1% Trifluoroacetic acid (Honeywell Research) and analyzed using online nanoliquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) (Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano and Q-Exactive HF, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were sampled in a 300 μm 5 mm PepMap C18 precolumn (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated in a 75 μm 250 mm C18 column (Reprosil-Pur 120 C18-AQ, 1.9 μm, Dr. Maisch HPLC GmbH).

The nano-LC method consisted of a 60 min multilinear gradient at a flow rate of 300 nl/min, ranging from 5 to 41% Acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid (Supelco). For data-dependent acquisition, the spray voltage was set at 2 kV, and the heated capillary was adjusted to 270°C. Survey full-scan MS spectra (m/z = 300–2000) were acquired with a resolution of 120,000 after the accumulation of 3 x 106 ions (maximum filling time 100 ms). The 20 most intense ions were fragmented by higher-energy collisional dissociation after the accumulation of 5 x 105 ions (maximum filling time: 200 ms).

For targeted proteomics, analyses were performed in PRM mode. The acquisition method combined two scan events corresponding to a full scan event and a time-scheduled PRM event targeting one of the 13 precursor ions selected in ±4.5 min elution time windows (based on DDA analysis, Table S3). Survey full-scan MS spectra (m/z = 300–2000) were acquired with a resolution of 120,000 after the accumulation of 3 x 106 ions (maximum filling time: 200 ms). PRM spectra were acquired with a resolution of 30,000 after the accumulation of 1 x 106 ions (maximum filling time: 400 ms). MS and MS/MS data were acquired using the Xcalibur software package (Thermo Scientific).

Mass spectrometry–based proteomic data processing

Data were processed automatically using the Mascot Distiller software (version 2.7.1.0, Matrix Science). Peptides and proteins were identified using Mascot (version 2.8.3) through concomitant searches against Uniprot (Homo Sapiens taxonomy, Release 2025_01), a classical contaminants database (homemade), a database containing GFP-RAGE sequences, and their corresponding reversed databases. Trypsin/P was chosen as the enzyme, and two missed cleavages were allowed. Precursor and fragment mass error tolerances were set to 10 ppm and 20 ppm, respectively. The peptide modifications allowed during the search were: cysteine carbamidomethylation (fixed); acetyl (protein N-terminal, variable); methionine oxidation (variable); and serine, threonine, and tyrosine phosphorylation (variable). The Proline software (version 2.3.1)51 was used to merge DDA results.

After combination, the results were filtered to conservation of rank 1 peptide-spectrum match (PSM) with a minimal length of 6 and a minimal score of 25. Peptide-spectrum matching (PSM) score filtering was then optimized to reach a false discovery rate (FDR) of PSM identification below 1% by employing the target decoy approach. A minimum of one specific peptide per identified protein group was set. The validated DDA dataset was used to select the ions to follow in PRM mode. The PRM data were processed with the same parameters in Mascot and Proline. Finally, the validated DDA and PRM results were combined before ProteomeXchange submission.46 For quantification purposes, fragment areas were extracted from the PRM data using Xcalibur.

Cell-surface biotinylation

Cell-surface biotinylation of FL cells was carried out as described in.52 Briefly, 48 hours after transfection, the cells were washed with PBS 1x (containing 0.1 mM CaCl2 (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 mM MgCl2 (Sigma-Aldrich)) and exposed to EZ-link™ Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin (Thermo Scientific)) for 2h at 4°C. The biotin excess was quenched by washing the cells with PBS 1x containing 0.1 mM CaCl2, 1mM MgCl2, 15 mM glycine (Euromedex). After lysis, immunoprecipitation, elution, and electrophoresis of the complexes and transfer onto a nitrocellulose membrane, RAGE-GFP constructions were detected with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin (dilution 1/500, Jackson ImmunoResearch). Signals were acquired with a Clarity Western ECL substrate (Bio-Rad) using the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System and Image Lab software (version 6.0.1. build 34; Bio-Rad).

TUNEL analysis

TUNEL experiments were performed on transfected FL cell coverslips fixed for 15 min with 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and washed with PBS 1x. After permeabilization of the cells with cold ethanol (Carlo-Erba) at 70% for 1 h at 4°C, endogenous peroxidase was blocked in 0.3% hydrogen peroxide (VWR) in PBS 1x for 30 min. The cells were incubated with 0.3 U/μl terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 6.7 μM biotin-11-dUTP (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 2.67 μM dATP (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase buffer for 45 min at 37°C. To stop the enzymatic reaction, the cells were washed with PBS 1x. Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin (dilution 1/500, Jackson ImmunoResearch) was added onto the slides for 25 min. After washing with PBS 1x, the signal was amplified with TSA conjugated to Alexa Fluor™ 555 (Invitrogen) diluted to 1/200 for 10 min. Nuclei were labeled with Hoechst solution (1μg/ml, Invitrogen) for 15 min. The cells were viewed under confocal microscopy for TUNEL positivity. Nine fields were observed for each experiment. Three independent experiments were performed, Results are expressed as the number of TUNEL-positive cells for GFP+ cells.

RAGE-CK2 complex structure prediction

The three-dimensional protein complex structure of human RAGE interacting with CK2α and CK2β was predicted using Alphafold 2.0 that was available on the ColabFold version 1.5.553 with the default parameters set as msa_mode: MMseqs2 (UniRef+Environmental) and other default settings. The local interaction score (LIS) and area (LIA) were calculated using the opensource GitHub (https://github.com/flyark/AFM-LIS). The CK2-RAGE 3D structure and environment were predicted using UCSF ChimeraX version 1.7.1 (https://www.rbvi.ucsf.edu/chimerax).

Microscale thermophoresis (MST)

RAGE-GFP, Ser400Ala-GFP, and GFP plasmids were transfected in FL cells. After lysis in RIPA buffer, supernatants were brought into contact with increasing concentrations of recombinant CK2β or CK2α47 in the kinase buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (Invitrogen), 50 mM KCl (Sigma-Aldrich), 10 mM MgCl2 (Sigma-Aldrich), pH 7.5). Samples were loaded into standard glass capillaries (Nanotemper) and analyzed at 25°C by MST using a Monolith NT.115 Pico instrument (NanoTemper). To determinate the EC50, NanoTemper software (MO.Affinity Analysis v2.3) was used. Data were extracted from 2 independent experiments, each performed in one technical replicate.

Flow cytometry analyses

Twenty-four hours after transfection of RAGE-GFP constructs in six-well plates, cells were washed, dissociated by trypsination, and centrifuged at 100 g for 10 min at room temperature. The pellets were re-suspended in Facs buffer (DMEM without red phenol (Gibco) supplemented with 0.5% BSA (Euromedex), 25 mM Hepes 1 M (Gibco), 5 mM MgCl2 (Sigma-Aldrich)). After cell counting, 2.106 cells were resuspended in 1 ml DMEM in Facs buffer supplemented with 0.01 mg/ml DNAse I (Sigma-Aldrich) and analyzed using a FACSMelody Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences).

Human phospho-kinase array

Six hours after the transfection, cells were growth in DMEM/F12 + GlutaMAX™-I without fetal bovine serum for 18 h followed by AGEs (100 μg/ml, Abcam) stimulation for 15min in the same medium.

Cells were then lysed using the supplied buffer, and 300 μg of total protein lysate was hybridized to each array of the Proteome Profiler Human Phospho-Kinase Array (R&D Systems, Bio-Techne) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Signals were detected using the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad), and Image Lab software (version 6.0.1. build 34; Bio-Rad) was used to quantify the pixel intensity. The average of the signal intensity of the negative control was subtracted for each spot. Involvement of phosphokinases in gene ontology/biological processes was assessed based on their -log10(p-value) using g:Profiler (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost, version e112_eg59_p19_25aa4782). Data were extracted from 3 independent experiments, each performed in two technical replicates.

Prediction of factor transcription response elements on the CX43 promoter

Identification of CREB and c-Jun putative transcription factor recognition sites in the CX43 promoter obtained from Ensembl (ENST00000282561.4) was conducted using Jaspar2024 (https://jaspar.elixir.no).

Quantification and statistical analyses

The results were analyzed using PRISM software (version 10.4.0) (GraphPad Software Inc.). The quantitative data are presented as the medians ± interquartile ranges. Non-normally distributed data between two independent groups were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney test, and to study more than two independent groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was performed followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test.
