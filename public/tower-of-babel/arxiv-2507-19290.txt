Query Efficient Structured Matrix Learning
Noah Amsel, Pratyush Avi, Tyler Chen, Feyza Duman Keles, Chinmay Hegde, Christopher Musco, Cameron Musco, David Persson

Abstract
We study the problem of learning a structured approximation (low-rank, sparse, banded, etc.) to an unknown matrix 𝐀 \bm{\mathbf{A}} given access to matrix-vector product (matvec) queries of the form 𝐱 → 𝐀𝐱 \bm{\mathbf{x}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{x}} and 𝐱 → 𝐀 𝖳 ​ 𝐱 \bm{\mathbf{x}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{x}} . This problem is of central importance to algorithms across scientific computing and machine learning, with applications to fast multiplication and inversion for structured matrices, building preconditioners for first-order optimization, and as a model for differential operator learning. Prior work focuses on obtaining query complexity upper and lower bounds for learning specific structured matrix families that commonly arise in applications. We initiate the study of the problem in greater generality, aiming to understand the query complexity of learning approximations from general matrix families. Our main result focuses on finding a near-optimal approximation to 𝐀 \bm{\mathbf{A}} from any finite-sized family of matrices, ℱ \mathcal{F} . Standard results from matrix sketching show that O ⁡ ( log ⁡ | ℱ | ) O(\log|\mathcal{F}|) matvec queries suffice in this setting. This bound can also be achieved, and is optimal, for vector-matrix-vector queries of the form 𝐱 , 𝐲 → 𝐱 𝖳 ​ 𝐀𝐲 \bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{y}} , which have been widely studied in work on rank- 1 1 matrix sensing. Surprisingly, we show that, in the matvec model, it is possible to obtain a nearly quadratic improvement in complexity, to O ~ ​ ( log ⁡ | ℱ | ) \tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|}) . Further, we prove that this bound is tight up to log-log factors. Via covering number arguments, our result extends to well-studied infinite families. As an example, we establish that a near-optimal approximation from any linear matrix family of dimension q q can be learned with O ~ ​ ( q ) \tilde{O}(\sqrt{q}) matvec queries, improving on an O ⁡ ( q ) O(q) bound achievable via sketching techniques and vector-matrix-vector queries.

Introduction

What can we learn about an unknown matrix
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
from multiplying it by a few vectors? This basic question has been central to numerical linear algebra for decades, underlying work on Krylov subspace methods
[
Saad, 2003
,
Saad, 2011
]
, matrix sketching
[
Woodruff, 2014
,
Drineas and Mahoney, 2016
]
, randomized numerical linear algebra
[
Weiße et al., 2006
,
Martinsson and Tropp, 2020
]
, and more. In its most general form, the task is to interact with an
n\times n
matrix
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
in an adaptive way, issuing matrix-vector product queries (“matvec queries”) of the form:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{x}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{x}}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{or}\hskip 20.00003pt\bm{\mathbf{x}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{x}},

where
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be chosen to be any vector. We consider the setting where we can issue multiple matvec queries
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{m}
, and each
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
can be chosen adaptively depending on the results of the previous queries,
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{i-1}
.

Understanding the matvec query complexity of linear algebraic problems is critical in applications where
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
is only accessible as an implicit operator (algorithms that work in this setting are called “matrix-free”) or when the goal is to avoid explicit operations on the entries of
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
to take computational advantage of the natural parallelism of matrix-vector multiplication. In recent years, we have seen a flurry of success in proving better upper and lower bounds (often matching) on the matvec query complexity of central problems like linear system solving
[
Braverman et al., 2020
]
, trace estimation
[
Meyer et al., 2021
,
Woodruff et al., 2022
,
Epperly et al., 2024
,
Chen et al., 2024
]
, eigenvalue estimation
[
Simchowitz et al., 2018
,
Chen et al., 2021
,
Braverman et al., 2022
,
Musco et al., 2025
,
Bhattacharjee et al., 2025
]
, matrix function computation
[
Chewi et al., 2024
]
, and much more
[
Sun et al., 2019
,
Meyer et al., 2025
]
.

1.1
Matrix Approximation

One of the most important problems studied in the matvec query model is that of learning a near-optimal approximation to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
from a given “hypothesis class” of structured matrices,
\mathcal{F}
. For example,
\mathcal{F}
could be all rank-
k
matrices or all tridiagonal matrices. We are interested in this problem in the
agnostic learning
setting, where
\bm{\mathbf{A}}

is not
assumed to lie in
\mathcal{F}
, nor to be generated from some “true” matrix in
\mathcal{F}
, e.g., by adding i.i.d. noise. Formally, we consider:

Problem 1
.

For an unknown
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and a matrix family
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
(a hypothesis class), find
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\gamma\cdot\inf_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}

for some approximation factor
\gamma\geq 1

\bm{\mathbf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}
as possible.

1

We assume
\bm{\mathbf{A}}

\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}

\mathcal{F}

The matvec query model is relevant in this setting because, on modern hardware, matvecs are an inherently efficient way to access

A familiar setting is when

Beyond Specific Matrix Families.

It is natural to ask if a more general theory exists for the query complexity of approximation from a given hypothesis class,
\mathcal{F}
. In supervised learning, a rich theory based on VC dimension, Pollard pseudodimension, fat-shattering dimension, etc. helps categorize the complexity of learning functions from arbitrary hypothesis classes
[
Pollard, 1990
,
Alon et al., 1997
,
Anthony and Bartlett, 1999
,
Vapnik, 2000
]
. We ask:

Does a general theory exist to characterize the matvec query complexity of structured matrix approximation?

This question is interesting because
1
differs in important ways from standard supervised learning. A first observation is that data collection can be active and adaptive, although there has been significant work on active learning
[
Settles, 2009
]
. More interesting is the fact that the output corresponding to any query/data example
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is itself an
n
dimensional vector,
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{x}}
(or
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{x}}
). We might therefore hope to learn
more
from each example than in a setting with scalar-valued outputs (informally, up to
n
times more). One such setting with scalar outputs is the well-studied rank-
1
matrix sensing problem
2
2

2

This model is called “rank-
1
” sensing because
\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{y}}
is equivalent to the Frobenius inner product between
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
and the rank-
1
matrix
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
from compressed sensing, in which the goal is to find an approximation to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
, but the matrix is only accessible via queries of the form
\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{y}}

[
Zhong et al., 2015
,
Dasarathy et al., 2015
,
Kueng et al., 2017
]
. From here on, we refer to this setting as the “vector-matrix-vector” query model.

Indeed, for many of the families mentioned above, there is a lot to be gained from the multidimensional output. Rank-
k

O(nk)

\Omega(nk)

O(s)

In both of these examples, matvec queries allow for a dramatic

[

Amsel et al., 2025b

1.2
Our Contributions

In this work, we take a step towards understanding the power of matvec queries for structured matrix-approximation in greater generality by considering arbitrary
finite families
of matrices (i.e., with finite size
|\mathcal{F}|)
. Such families require
\log(|\mathcal{F}|)
bits (i.e., parameters) to represent, and it can be shown using standard techniques from matrix sketching that it is always possible to solve
1
with constant approximation factor
\gamma
using
O(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
matvec queries (see
Section

2.1
for more details). Indeed, this bound can also be achieved in the more restrictive vector-matrix-vector (i.e., rank-
1
matrix-sensing) setting, with queries of the form
\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{y}}
. In that setting, the bound is optimal (see
Appendix

A
).

Upper Bound.
Our main result shows that
O(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
is far from optimal for matvec queries. By taking advantage of the multidimensional output of such queries, we give a nearly quadratic improvement in query complexity
for any finite
\mathcal{F}
. In particular, in
Section

2
we prove:

Theorem 1

(Finite Family Approximation)
.

There is an algorithm (
Algorithm

4
) that, for any finite matrix family
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with size
|\mathcal{F}|
and any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, uses
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|}/\epsilon^{2})
matrix-vector product queries
3
3

3

We use
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
to suppress polylog factors in the input argument and the failure probability of the algorithm.

with
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}
and finds, with high probability, an approximation
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying:

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}<(3+\epsilon)\cdot\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}.

The algorithm achieving
Theorem

1
strongly relies on the ability to query both
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}
. It is not hard to prove a lower bound of
\Omega(\log|\mathcal{\mathcal{F}}|)
if we can only query
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
on one side (see
Appendix

A
). In addition to querying on both sides, our algorithm selects queries adaptively – the best known non-adaptive method achieves complexity
O(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
. However, we have no lower bounds showing that adaptivity is necessary.

Roughly speaking, we obtain
Theorem

1
by efficiently simulating an iterative sketching method that solves
1
using
{O}(\log|\mathcal{F}|)

one-sided
queries. Described in
Section

2
, the idea is to multiply
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
on the right with a random matrix,
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
, with
\tilde{O}(1)
columns at each iteration, and to eliminate any matrix
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}
for which
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
is large. Using standard results on how well the sketch
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
approximates
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
, it is easy to show that this approach eliminates all matrices that are not good approximations to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
after
\tilde{O}(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
rounds.

Our main idea is to use an additional left-sketch,
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}
, to identify what fraction of bad approximations
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}
a particular set of right queries,
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
, will eliminate. Importantly, we can do so without ever computing
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
directly – we only use information in the sketch
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
, which can be computed using our left sketch alone, and no additional matvec queries on the right. We show a dichotomy: either we find that
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
eliminates a very large fraction of bad approximations, in which case we issue the queries and continue. Otherwise, if
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
would remove too few bad approximations (i.e., it provides less information about
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
), we prove that it is possible to simulate the result of issuing
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
using the sketch
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
. Balancing these two cases gives our improvement from
O(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
to
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
.

Lower Bound.

A natural question is if the bound from
Theorem

1
can be improved. For one, our current techniques only achieve a constant factor approximation; it would be interesting to push the result to achieve approximation factor
1+\epsilon
. We discuss this and other open problems in
Section

5
.
Nevertheless,
we show that significantly improving the dependence on
|\mathcal{F}|
in
Theorem

1
is not possible in general for finite matrix families. In
Section

4
we prove:

Theorem 2

(Finite Family Lower Bound)
.

Any algorithm that solves
1
with constant probability for general finite matrix families
\mathcal{F}
and approximation factor
\gamma
requires
\Omega(\sqrt{\log|F|/\log\gamma})
matvec queries.

Setting
\gamma=O(1)
,
Theorem

2
shows that the dependence on
\log|\mathcal{F}|
in
Theorem

1
cannot be improved except by log-log factors.
The “hard” family used to prove this result is simple: let
\mathcal{F}
be a discrete net over the set of all
n\times n
matrices with bounded norm. Not surprisingly, we require
\Omega(n)=\Omega(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
matvec queries to learn any meaningful approximation from this family: essentially, we need to read all of
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
.
Our lower bound can also be proven using the more realistic hard instance of constant-rank butterfly matrices, which are commonly used to approximate non-uniform Fourier transforms and other operators
[
Liu et al., 2021
]
. Such matrices require
\tilde{O}(n)
parameters to represent, but our best-known butterfly approximation algorithms require
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})
matvecs queries
[
Li and Yang, 2017
]
. As discussed further in
Section

4
, our lower bound implies that this is optimal.

Beyond Finite Families.

In addition to being a natural starting point for work on general matrix families, one reason to study finite families is that upper bounds immediately imply bounds for infinite families that can be appropriately discretized (i.e., are well-approximated by a finite family). Using standard arguments, we show that
Theorem

1
immediately implies that a near optimal approximation from any family with covering number bounded by
\Gamma
can be computed with
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log\Gamma})
queries. We conclude new results for well-studied infinite families. For example, there has been interest in approximation via
linearly parameterized families
with dimension
q
, meaning that any matrix in the family can be written as
c_{1}\bm{\mathbf{P}}_{1}+\ldots+c_{1}\bm{\mathbf{P}}_{q}
, where
\bm{\mathbf{P}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{P}}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are a fixed set of base matrices and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{q}\in\mathbb{R}
are arbitrary scalar coefficients
[
Eldar et al., 2020
,
Halikias and Townsend, 2023
,
Otto, 2023
]
. Linearly parameterized families include sparse families like banded and block diagonal matrices, displacement-structured matrices like Toeplitz, circulant, and Hankel matrices, and more. In
Section

3
we prove:

Corollary 1

(Linear Family Approximation)
.

For any linearly parameterized matrix family,
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, with dimension
q
and any
\alpha,\epsilon\in(0,1)
, it is possible to learn
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying, with high probability,

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq(3+\epsilon)\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{L}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}+\alpha\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\|_{\mathsf{F}},

using
\tilde{O}\left({\sqrt{q\log(1/\alpha)}}/{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
matrix-vector product queries with
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}
.

Observe that, due to our need to fix a scale for covering
\mathcal{L}
,
Corollary

1
is not a pure multiplicative error result: we incur additive error
\alpha\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
. While the query complexity depends only logarithmically on
\alpha

(1+\epsilon)
multiplicative error can be obtained with
O(q/\epsilon)
queries – i.e., with a quadratically worse dependence on
q
. In particular, leveraging subspace embedding results for Kronecker structured matrices
[
Ahle et al., 2020
]
, it can be shown that it suffices to return

\bm{\mathbf{G}}

O(q/\epsilon)
samples of entries from
\bm{\mathbf{A}}

[
Chen and Price, 2019
]
, which can be obtained in the weaker vector-matrix-vector model.
Using similar techniques to those used in proving the lower bound in
Theorem

2

\sqrt{q}

Corollary

Additional Related Work

While we believe our work is the first to address

Low-Rank Approximation.

One of the most familiar settings is when
\mathcal{F}

that
\Omega(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
one-sided matvec queries are still needed to learn a generic finite family
\mathcal{F}
(i.e., quadratically worse than what is possible via
Theorem

1
with two-sided queries). This gap is intuitive: let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite net over the set of matrices whose first row is an arbitrary unit vector, and whose remaining rows are all zero. This family has size
2^{O(n)}
, but we require
n
one-sided queries to learn a meaningful approximation – we essentially need to read each entry in
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
’s first row. If two-sided queries are allowed, we could multiply the first standard basis vector by
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}
to learn the first row with a single query.

Finally, regardless of the specific query model studied, many of the works above focus on the
non-agnostic
or
exact recovery
setting, where
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
is assumed to be contained in our family
\mathcal{F}
, i.e.
\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}=0
. The goal is to exactly identify
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
– see e.g.,
[
Halikias and Townsend, 2023
]
. For many matrix classes, the easier exact recovery problem has been tackled first, preceding results in the agnostic setting. In a preliminary version of this paper
[
Amsel et al., 2025a
]
, we prove exact recovery results for arbitrary finite families using an algorithm that is similar, but simpler, than the one used to prove
Theorem

1
.

2
Finite Family Matrix Approximation

In this section, we establish our main result: an
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
query algorithm for computing a near-optimal approximation to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
(i.e, for solving
1
) from any finite family
\mathcal{F}
. Our proof is based on an efficient “simulation” of a natural one-sided query algorithm that only interacts with
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
via right matrix-vector products of the form
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{x}}
, and which requires
{O}({\log|\mathcal{F}|})
queries. Indeed, this is the best possible for a one-sided algorithm (see
Appendix

A
).

We begin by first introducing and analyzing this one-sided algorithm in
Section

2.1
. We then show how to simulate the method with quadratically fewer two-sided queries in
Section

2.2
. In both of these sections, we assume we have knowledge of an upper bound,
M
, on the cost of the optimum solution,
\mathrm{OPT}=\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
. Our error bounds will initially have a dependence on
M
, which we will later remove in
Section

2.3
, by showing how to efficiently identify a value of
M
with
\mathrm{OPT}\leq M\leq(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{OPT}
for any
\epsilon>0
.

2.1
One-Sided Algorithms

The one-sided method that we simulate differs a bit from the most obvious approach, which we describe first.

The standard approach.

An optimal one-sided algorithm for solving
1
for finite matrix families can be obtained immediately from prior work on Frobenius norm sketching, or equivalently, stochastic trace estimation (i.e., the Hutchinson-Girard trace estimator)
[
Hutchinson, 1990
,
Girard, 1987
,
Clarkson and Woodruff, 2009
,
Avron and Toledo, 2011
,
Rudelson et al., 2013
,
Roosta-Khorasani and Ascher, 2015
,
Cortinovis and Kressner, 2022
]
. In particular, a standard result, following, e.g., from the Hanson-Wright inequality
[
Rudelson et al., 2013
]
is the following:

Fact 1

(Frobenius-Norm Sketching)
.

For any
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
and
\ell\geq c\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^{2}
for a fixed constant
c
, let
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\in\{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell}}\}^{n\times\ell}
have i.i.d. scaled Rademacher entries. Then for any matrix
\bm{\mathbf{C}}
with
n
columns, with probability
>1-\delta
,

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq(1+\epsilon)\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}.

(1)

To obtain a one-sided solution to
1
, we simply use
1
to estimate the error of
every
possible
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}
by setting
\bm{\mathbf{C}}=\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}
. In particular, we can return:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\in\argmin_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}.

(2)

Computing (
2
) only requires
\ell
right matrix-vector products of the form
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{Ax}}
. Moreover, if we set
\delta^{\prime}=\delta/|\mathcal{F}|
, and use
\ell=O(\log(1/\delta^{\prime})/\epsilon^{2})=O(\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
matvecs, by a union bound, we have that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
is a
(1\pm\epsilon/3)
-approximation to
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}

for all

\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}
. It follows that
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{OPT}
for a constant
c
with high probability.
We conclude:

Claim 1
.

For any finite matrix family
\mathcal{F}
and
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, there is an algorithm that solves
1
with approximation factor
\gamma=1+\epsilon
and success probability
>1-\delta
using
O(\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
one-sided matvec queries of the form
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{x}}
.

As discussed, it is possible to achieve the same bound as in
1
using just vector-matrix-vector queries of the form
\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{y}}
.
We prove this in
Appendix

A
, where we also show that the bound is essentially tight, for both one-sided matvec queries and vector-matrix-vector queries.

We briefly note that one might hope to improve the dependence on
\epsilon
in
1
using improvements to the Hutchinson-Girard estimator like Hutch++ and related techniques
[
Meyer et al., 2021
,
Persson et al., 2022
,
Epperly et al., 2024
,
Jiang et al., 2024
]
. However, all of these methods require matrix-vector multiplications with
(\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}})^{\mathsf{T}}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}})
. It is unclear how to compute this in a query-efficient manner, since we would need to compute
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{x}}
for all
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}
.

An alternative iterative approach.
While simple, it is not clear how to simulate the above approach with a smaller number of two-sided queries. Instead, we consider a closely related alternative method based on
iteratively refining

\mathcal{F}
. At every step of the algorithm, we use a very small sketch
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
to remove a large fraction of candidate approximations from
\mathcal{F}
until, after roughly
O(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
rounds, we converge on a near-optimal approximation.

This approach is formalized as
Algorithm

1
. The method performs
\log|\mathcal{F}|/\log\log|\mathcal{F}|
iterations of refinement, using a sketch of size
O(\log\log|\mathcal{F}|)
in each iteration, for a total query complexity of
O(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
, matching the standard approach of
1
.

Algorithm 1

One-Sided Iterative Candidate Refinement

1:

Finite matrix family
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
, accuracy parameter
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, upper bound
M\geq\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}=\mathrm{OPT}
.

2:

Matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq(1+\epsilon)\cdot M
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

3:

Initialize candidate set
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\mathcal{F}
.
Set
\ell=\frac{c}{\epsilon^{2}}\log(\log|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)
for sufficiently large constant
c
. Set
T=\frac{\log|\mathcal{F}|}{\log\log|\mathcal{F}|}
.

4:

for

i=1,\ldots,T

do

5:

Draw
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\in\{-1/\sqrt{\ell},1/\sqrt{\ell}\}^{n\times\ell}
with i.i.d. scaled Rademacher entries.

6:

Compute
\bm{\mathbf{Z}}=\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
.
\triangleright
Requires
\ell
matvec queries.

7:

Let
\mathcal{C}_{i}=\left\{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{C}_{i-1}:\|\bm{\mathbf{Z}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq(1+\epsilon/2)\cdot M\right\}
.

8:

return
Any remaining
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{C}_{T}
.

Note that
Algorithm

1

assumes knowledge of an upper bound
M
on the cost of the optimal solution
\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}=\mathrm{OPT}
. Using a standard binary search method, we will show how to efficiently compute an accurate upper bound in
Section

2.3
, which leads to an algorithm with a relative error guarantee. As written,
Algorithm

1
guarantees error at most
(1+\epsilon)M\geq(1+\epsilon)\mathrm{OPT}
.

The proof of correctness is straightforward. We make two observations. First, consider any fixed
\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{*}\in\argmin_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
. Via the standard Frobenius norm sketching bound of
1
, as long as the constant
c
is set large enough in
3
, at each iteration of the loop, we have that the estimate
\|\bm{\mathbf{Z}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{*}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}=\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{*}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
is a
(1\pm\epsilon/2)
multiplicative approximation to
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{*}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
with probability at least
1-\delta/\log|\mathcal{F}|
. By a union bound, this holds at every iteration with probability at least
1-\delta
. So, with high probability,
\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
survives the check at
7
in all iterations, and
\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{T}
. In other words, the optimal solution remains as a candidate for output in
8
.

Therefore, we obtain an accurate solution
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}
with
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq(1+\epsilon)\cdot M
as long as we can ensure that
\mathcal{C}_{T}

contains no “bad” matrices
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
for which
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}>(1+\epsilon)\cdot M
. To argue that this is indeed the case, let
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{F}
denote this set of “bad” matrices with
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}>(1+\epsilon)\cdot M
. Fix a particular
{\bm{\mathbf{B}}}\in\mathcal{B}
. Again invoking
1
, as long as the constant
c
is chosen sufficiently large in
3
, at each iteration
i
,
\|\bm{\mathbf{Z}}-{\bm{\mathbf{B}}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}>(1-\epsilon/6)\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-{\bm{\mathbf{B}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}>(1-\epsilon/6)\cdot(1+\epsilon)\cdot M>(1+\epsilon/2)\cdot M

with probability at least
1-{\delta}/{\log|\mathcal{F}|}
. I.e.,
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
is eliminated at
7
with probability at least
1-{\delta}/{\log|\mathcal{F}|}
in each iteration. Therefore, after
T=\frac{\log|\mathcal{F}|}{\log\log|\mathcal{F}|}
iterations, the probability that
{\bm{\mathbf{B}}}
remains in the candidate set is at most
({\delta}/{\log|\mathcal{F}|})^{\frac{\log|\mathcal{F}|}{\log\log|\mathcal{F}|}}<\frac{\delta}{|\mathcal{F}|}
.
Via a union bound, since
|\mathcal{B}|<|\mathcal{F}|
, no
{\bm{\mathbf{B}}}\in\mathcal{B}
remains in
\mathcal{C}_{T}
with probability at least
1-\delta
, and thus we always return
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\notin\mathcal{B}
.
After noting that
Algorithm

1
uses
\ell\cdot T=O\left(\frac{\log(\log|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{\log|\mathcal{F}|}{\log\log|\mathcal{F}|}\right)=O\left(\frac{\log|\mathcal{F}|\cdot\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
queries,
we conclude:

Claim 2
.

For any finite matrix family
\mathcal{F}
,
Algorithm

1
with inputs
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
and
M\geq\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}=\mathrm{OPT}

solves
1
with error
\gamma=(1+\epsilon)\cdot\frac{M}{\mathrm{OPT}}
and success probability
>1-\delta
using
{O}(\log|\mathcal{F}|\cdot\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
one-sided matvec queries of the form
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{x}}
.

As mentioned, in
Section

2.3
we will show how to compute

with

, giving a
1+\epsilon
relative error guarantee for
Algorithm

1
after plugging into
2
.

2.2
Near Quadratic Improvement via Two-Sided Simulation

In this section, we show how to use matvecs with both
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}
to solve
1
with just
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
queries. Our approach is based on an efficient “simulation” of
Algorithm

1
from the previous section.
We first sketch the main ideas behind this simulation in
Section

2.2.1
.
We then formally present the approach as
Algorithm

2
and analyze it in
Section

2.2.2
, yielding
Theorem

3
. Combining
Theorem

3
with an efficient method for estimating an accurate upper bound
M\geq\mathrm{OPT}
, given in
Section

2.3
, yields our main result,
Theorem

1
.

2.2.1
Proof Overview

For simplicity of asymptotic notation, we assume throughout this proof sketch that both our error parameter
\epsilon
and the failure probability
\delta
are fixed constants. We also suppress all
\log\log|\mathcal{F}|
factors with
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
notation, and write
\log|\mathcal{F}|/\log\log|\mathcal{F}|=O(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
.

Recall that the goal of
Algorithm

1
is to continually shrink the candidate set
\mathcal{C}
until it no longer contains any “bad” matrices that are not good approximations to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
. In the proof of
2
, we argue that for every block of
\ell={O}(\log\log|\mathcal{F}|)
right queries, we reduce the set of bad candidates by an
O(1/\log|\mathcal{F}|)
factor, eliminating all bad candidates after
\log|\mathcal{F}|/\log\log|\mathcal{F}|
iterations. This leads to an overall query complexity of
{O}(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
.

To obtain an algorithm that uses only
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
queries, we need to cut down the set of candidates far more aggressively, ideally by an
O(1/2^{\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|}})
factor with each block of queries.
To do so, our simulation
leverages the fact that each iteration of
Algorithm

1
falls under one of two cases, depending on the random sketching matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
:

Case 1.

First, if we get lucky,
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
might be a highly “productive” sketch. In particular,
recalling that are targeting constant error in this proof sketch, we could have that, for a constant
c
,

\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}>cM
for all but an
O(1/2^{\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|}})
fraction of
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
. In this case, we can simply issue the right queries
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
and remove a large fraction of remaining candidates.

Case 2.

Alternatively, if we are not so lucky in our choice of
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
, there could be
many candidates

\bm{\mathbf{B}}
for which
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq cM
(at least an
\Omega(1/2^{\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|}})
fraction).
However, we can take advantage of this situation to filter the candidate set
\mathcal{C}_{i}

with no right queries at all
.
In particular, suppose hypothetically that we knew that a certain matrix
\bm{\mathbf{Y}}
had
\|\bm{\mathbf{Y}}-\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq cM
, we could use it as a proxy, discarding candidates
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
for which
\|\bm{\mathbf{Y}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\geq 2cM
, without needing to compute
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
and thus make any right queries to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
.
By the triangle inequality, the discarded matrices would all have
\|\bm{\mathbf{A\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\geq cM
, and we would still successfully eliminate a large fraction of bad candidates with this proxy rule.

Our key insight is that, if we find ourselves in Case 2, it is easy to construct such a
\bm{\mathbf{Y}}
, since there are many candidates
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
for which
\bm{\mathbf{Y}}=\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
is close to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
.
In particular, we just need to find a suitable “representative”,
\bm{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
such that
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq cM
.
To do so, we uniformly sample roughly
q=O(2^{\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|}})
candidates
\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{q}
from
\mathcal{C}_{i}
. By the assumption of Case 2, at least an
\Omega(1/q)
fraction of the candidates in
\mathcal{C}_{i}
have
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq cM
. So, with high probability, we will have at least one such candidate in our sample. We can use Frobenius norm sketching (
1
) with
left
queries to identify this representative candidate. That is, we sample a left sketching matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}
with
\tilde{O}(\log q)=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
columns and check the value of
\|\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
for each
j=1,\ldots,q
. By a union bound over our
q
samples, this estimate will be a constant factor approximation to
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
with high probability for all
\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{j}
, and thus enough to identify our representative
\bm{\mathbf{R}}\in\{\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{q}\}
.

Importantly, the above process requires
no right queries to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
, since we can compute
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}
using left queries, and then just multiply this matrix by
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
to compute
\|\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
.
With our representative
\bm{\mathbf{R}}
in hand, we can filter out candidates where
\|\bm{\mathbf{R\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{B\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
is large, eliminating a large fraction of bad candidates using this proxy rule (i.e., all but a
O(1/\log|\mathcal{F}|)
fraction as in
Algorithm

1
) with high probability.

Putting the cases together.

The above case analysis suggests the following algorithm:
We first precompute the left sketch
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}
using a total of
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
left queries. This sketch will be used to search for a representative
\bm{\mathbf{R}}
in each of the
O(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
iterations of
Algorithm

1
.
At each iteration, we draw a
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}
and determine if it falls under Case 1 or Case 2. This determination is made by searching for a representative
\bm{\mathbf{R}}
. If none is found, we conclude we are in Case 1.
In Case 1, we issue
\ell=\tilde{O}(1)
right queries and eliminate all but an
O(1/2^{\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|}})
fraction of the remaining candidates. We can encounter this case at most
O(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
times before removing all candidates, and thus will issue at most
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
right queries in total.
In Case 2, we issue no new queries but still eliminate all but a
O(1/\log|\mathcal{F}|)
fraction of bad candidates, meaning that we still terminate after at most
O(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
iterations.
Regardless, we issue at most
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
right and left queries in total, and identify a constant factor solution to
1
with high probability.

Independence Across Iterations.

The description above captures the key idea behind our
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
query algorithm. However, it ignores a key issue:
to obtain the claimed complexity, we need to
reuse
the same left sketching matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}
across all iterations of the algorithm.
However, for iteration
i>0
, the candidate set
\mathcal{C}_{i}
could depend on
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}
, since it may depend on the choice of representative
\bm{\mathbf{R}}
at an earlier iteration, which is identified using the left sketch
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}
. Hence, the set of randomly sampled potential representatives,
\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{q}
may depend on
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}
too.
Due to this loss of independence, we cannot directly argue that
\|\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
is a good approximation to
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
for each
\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{j}\in\{\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{B}}_{q}\}
as required to identify a good representative
\bm{\mathbf{R}}
.

To avoid this issue, we slightly modify the procedure. Instead of drawing a single right sketch for each iteration, we draw
r=O(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
sketches for each iteration,
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{r}
.
We then attempt to find representatives
\bm{\mathbf{R}}^{1},\ldots\bm{\mathbf{R}}^{r}
(via sampling as described above) for each of these sketches.
If we fail to find a representative for a particular
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{j}
, it means that
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{j}
lands in Case 1, and we can issue these queries to filter the candidate set down to just an
O(1/2^{\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|}})
fraction of its size.
Otherwise, if we succeed in finding a representative for
every
sketch
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{r}
, we can use these representatives to filter the candidate set for free according to
all of these sketches at once
.
For
r=O(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
, this process filters out all the remaining bad candidates with high probability in a single shot, and the algorithm terminates.
The benefit of this approach is that in Case 1, the set of candidates that survives the filtering depends on
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}
only through the choice of the sketching matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{j}
for which we could not find a representative. Since there are just
r=O(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
sketching matrices to choose from at each iteration, we can control the number of candidate sets that we might possibly want to sketch with
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}
and use a union bound to guarantee that the sketch is accurate for all possible such candidate sets. In Case 2, while we use representatives
\bm{\mathbf{R}}^{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{R}}^{r}
that depend on
\bm{\mathbf{\Psi}}

2.3
Estimating the Optimal Error

We next show how to obtain an accurate upper bound

Overview of Approach.

Our approach starts by showing that a simple one-sided sketching algorithm using
O(\log(1/\delta))
matvecs can efficiently find, with probability
>1-\delta
,
M_{\rm init}
satisfying the very coarse (but still multiplicative) error bound
\mathrm{OPT}\leq M_{\rm init}\leq 6|\mathcal{F}|\cdot\mathrm{OPT}

Starting with

, we then run a binary search procedure to identify a more accurate upper bound on
\mathrm{OPT}
.
In particular, we consider
O(\log|\mathcal{F}|/\epsilon)
possible bounds:

for
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,\lceil\log_{(1+\epsilon)}(6|\mathcal{F}|)\rceil\}
. The smallest of these bounds is

Claim 3

Proof.

The upper bound follows directly from Markov’s inequality: it is well-known that

3
Bounds for Infinite Families Via Covering

We next extend our results on finite family matrix approximation from
Section

2
to infinite families, which comprise most structured matrix families of interest in practice. We first state a generic result for any family
\mathcal{F}
with finite
covering number
, which follows essentially immediately from our main result on finite families,
Theorem

1
.

We then give an application to
linear matrix families
, which are a common class of infinite families, including diagonal, tridiagonal, and banded matrices, other matrices with fixed sparsity patterns, Toeplitz matrices, Hankel matrices, and more. We show that a near-optimal approximation from a linear family of dimension
q
can be obtained with
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{q})
matvecs, improving on an
O(q)
bound achievable via one-sided sketching techniques or vector-matrix-vector queries. It is not hard to see that both of these bounds are nearly optimal via the same lower bound approach used for finite families in
Section

4
.

We start with a standard definition for the covering number of a matrix family
\mathcal{F}
, see e.g.,
[
Vershynin, 2018
]
.

Definition 1

(Covering Number)
.

Consider a family of matrices

. We say that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{F}

\alpha
-cover
of

if, for all
\bm{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathcal{F}

\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
such that
\|\bm{\mathbf{X}}-\bm{\mathbf{Y}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\alpha

\alpha
-covering number
of
\mathcal{F}
is the minimum cardinality of any
\alpha
-cover of
\mathcal{F}
.

We remark that an
\alpha
-cover is also frequently referred to as an
\alpha
-net in the literature. With this definition in hand, we are ready to give our result for infinite families with bounded covering numbers.

Theorem 4
.

Consider any
\alpha>0
. There is an algorithm that, for any matrix family

\alpha

\Gamma_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq(3+\epsilon)\cdot\inf_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}+(3+\epsilon)\alpha.

4
Lower bound

We now argue that the dependence on
\log|\mathcal{F}|
in
Theorem

1
is essentially tight: there exist finite families where
\Omega(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
queries are required to solve

even to a coarse approximation factor. We begin by stating a more detailed version of
Theorem

2
below:

Theorem
2
.

For any
n>0
, any approximation factor
\gamma\geq 1.1
, and any
k\leq c^{\prime}n^{2}\log\gamma
for a universal constant
c^{\prime}
, there
is a finite family of matrices
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
\log|\mathcal{F}|=O(k)
and a distribution over
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that, if
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}
is the output of any algorithm that makes fewer than
\frac{c\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|}}{\sqrt{\log\gamma}}
matvec queries to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}
for a universal constant
c>0
,

\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\geq\gamma\cdot\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}},

with probability
\geq 9/10
(over the randomness in
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
and possibly the algorithm).
In particular,
\Omega(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
matvec queries are necessary to achieve any constant approximation factor
\gamma
with probability
\geq 1/10
.

Note that the dependence on
1/\sqrt{\log\gamma}
in
Theorem

2
is optimal: in
3
, we show how to find a
\gamma=O(|\mathcal{F}|)
-approximate solution using just
O(1)=O(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|/\log(\gamma)})
matvec queries.

Before proving
Theorem

2
, we first present several technical lemmas that will be used in the argument. As discussed in
Section

1.2
, the idea behind the lower bound is simple: we let
\mathcal{F}
be a sufficiently fine net over all matrices that are non-zero on the top
z\times z
block of
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
and zeros elsewhere. We will have
\log|\mathcal{F}|=O(z^{2})
. To solve
1
, we essentially must recover all of
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
’s top
z\times z
block, which requires
\Omega(z)=\Omega(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|})
queries.

To formalize this argument, we need to chose a distribution over inputs
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
that is sufficiently anti-concentrated so that its best approximation in
\mathcal{F}
is hard to learn. There are many possible options here – we will let
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
be a Wishart matrix (i.e.,
\bm{\mathbf{A}}=\bm{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}^{\mathsf{T}}
where
\bm{\mathbf{G}}
has i.i.d. Gaussian entries), since the distribution of these matrices after querying with a set of possibly adaptively chosen matvecs is well understood. In particular, we will use the follow result:

Lemma 1

(
Lemma 13
in
[
Braverman et al., 2020
]
)
.

Let
\bm{\mathbf{A}}=\bm{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}^{\mathsf{T}}
, where
\bm{\mathbf{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
has i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
entries. For any
m\leq n
, consider any set of adaptively chosen queries
\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i}
is drawn from a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}
that is a function of
\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i-1}
and the responses
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i-1}
. We can construct a matrix
\bm{\mathbf{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n-m}
with orthonormal columns and a matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\Delta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, both of which are functions only of
\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{m}
and
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{m}

such that

\bm{\mathbf{A}}=\bm{\mathbf{\Delta}}+\bm{\mathbf{V}}\bm{\mathbf{W}}\bm{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}},

\bm{\mathbf{W}}=\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{G}}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{G}}}^{\mathsf{T}}
for

\mathcal{N}(0,1)

\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{m}
and
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{m}

Lemma 2
.

There exists a constant

such that if

each have i.i.d.

and

then for any

It also follows as a special case of the more general Lemma 1 of

\bm{\mathbf{u}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{v}}
in that lemma to be standard basis vectors.

Application to Butterfly Matrices.

While the hard instance of
Theorem

2
may seem somewhat artificial, it is actually closely related to structured matrix classes that arise commonly in applications. In particular, the bound easily implies a lower bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
matvecs for approximation from the class of constant-rank butterfly matrices, which have been considered in many recent works
[
Li et al., 2015
,
Li and Yang, 2017
,
Liu et al., 2021
]
. This lower bound matches the best known upper bound upper bound of
[
Li and Yang, 2017
]
up a
\log n
factor.

Corollary 2
.

For
n=2^{k}
for integer
k>0
, let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the class of rank-
1
butterfly matrices
[
Li et al., 2015
]
. For any
\gamma>1.1
, solving
1
for
\mathcal{F}
to accuracy
\gamma
for any
\gamma\geq 1.1
with probability
\geq 1/10
requires
\Omega(\sqrt{n}/\sqrt{\log\gamma})
queries.

We refer the reader to
[
Li and Yang, 2017
]
for a complete definition of butteryfly matrices. We use the fact that rank-
1
butterly matrices are equivalent to the family of matrices with
complimentary rank-
1
structure
. In short, a matrix
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
has complimentary rank-
1
if, for all
i,j<\log_{2}(n)
, given a partition of
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
into a grid of blocks of size
2^{i}\times 2^{j}
, every block has rank
1
. With this definition in place, we can prove the claim:

Proof.

Consider the family
\mathcal{F^{\prime}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
where the top-left entry of every block in a partition of the matrix into
n
,
\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}
blocks is arbitrary, and all other entries are zero. It is not hard to see that any matrix in this family has complimentary rank-
1
since any block in a partition into
2^{i}\times 2^{j}
blocks has at most a single non-zero column. I.e.,
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is a subset of the family of rank-
1
butterfly matrices,
\mathcal{F}
.

Moreover, it is not hard to see that, up to a permutation of row and column indices, this family is equivalent to the set of matrices that take arbitrary non-zero values on their top
\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}
principal submatrix and are
0
anywhere else. Now, let
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
be an appropriate row/column permutation of the hard case of
Theorem

2
, a matrix whose top
\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}
block is a Wishart matrix
\bm{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}^{\mathsf{T}}
for
\bm{\mathbf{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}}
. Any algorithm for finding an optimal approximation to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
in
\mathcal{F}
must exactly recover
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
since
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
. Thus, any such algorithm yields an algorithm, with the same query complexity, for finding an optimal approximation to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
in any other family, including in the finite
\alpha
-cover considered in
Theorem

2
. Thus, such an algorithm must make
\Omega(\sqrt{n}/\sqrt{\log\gamma})
queries, yielding the theorem.
∎

5
Open Problems

We conclude by highlighting a few open problems that would be interesting to address in follow-up work. First, for finite families, we conjecture that it is possible to obtain a
(1+\epsilon)
approximation with
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|}/\mathrm{poly}(\epsilon))
queries, improving on the
(3+\epsilon)
bound achieved by
Theorem

1
. We would also like to understand the roll of adaptivity – is it possible that the same bound is achievable even if the query vectors
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{m}
are chosen all at once, or with just a few rounds of adaptivity? If not, can we prove a lower bound? Our current algorithm issues a non-adaptive set of left queries to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}
, but our right queries to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
are chosen adaptively. Currently, the best known non-adaptive method (discussed in
Section

2
) requires
\tilde{O}(\log|\mathcal{F}|/\epsilon^{2})
queries; that is it takes no advantage of having access to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}
.

We are also interested in obtaining improved bounds for linearly parameterized families. We conjecture that
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{q}/\mathrm{poly}(\epsilon))
queries suffice to achieve a pure
(1+\epsilon)
relative error approximation for any linearly parameterized family with dimension
q
. Eliminating the additive
\alpha\cdot\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}
from our
Corollary

1
will seemingly require a different approach that does not rely on a black-box reduction to finite families.

Finally, beyond query complexity, it is interesting to consider computational complexity. The runtime of our current algorithms scale as
\tilde{O}(|\mathcal{F}|)
. While this is essentially optimal if we do not assume any additional structure about
\mathcal{F}
(we must at least read the
|\mathcal{F}|
matrices in the family), we could hope to do significantly better if
\mathcal{F}
has more structure – e.g., has a compact parameterization, or is a discretization of a simply described infinite family. For example, a concrete question is if it is possible to match the
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{q})
query complexity of
Corollary

1
for dimension
q
linear families with an algorithm that runs in
\mathrm{poly}(n,q)
time.

) is a near quadratic improvement on the best achievable bound using either one-sided matvec queries, or vector-matrix-vector queries (which are strictly weaker than one-sided queries).

In this section we first show that a bound of
O(\log|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
is achievable in the vector-matrix-vector query model, matching the bound of
1
for the one-sided query model. We then show that
\Omega(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
queries are necessary in the one-sided query model, and in turn the weaker vector-matrix-vector query model, even to achieve a weak approximation bound with small constant probability.

A.1
Upper Bound for Vector-Matrix-Vector Queries

We prove the following upper bound for finite family matrix approximation in the vector-matrix-vector model:

Claim 5
.

There is an algorithm that, for any finite matrix family
\mathcal{F}
,
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, and
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
accessible via vector-matrix-vector queries of the form
\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{y}}
, issues
O(\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
such queries and, with probability
>1-\delta
, returns
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying:

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq(1+\epsilon)\cdot\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}.

Proof.

As with
1
, the result follows by using a Frobenius norm approximation result like
1
, and union bounding over all matrices
\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}
for
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}
. However, the Frobenius norm approximation method must be implementable with just vector-matrix-vector queries. We show that this is possible using a standard “median-trick” style argument. Consider independent
\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
with i.i.d. Rademacher entries. We will show that, for any matrix
\bm{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
(\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{C}}\bm{\mathbf{y}})^{2}
is an unbiased estimator for
\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}
with bounded variance. In particular, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[(\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{C}}\bm{\mathbf{y}})^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i,j}x_{i}y_{j}C_{ij}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\left[x_{i}^{2}y_{i}^{2}C_{ij}^{2}\right]+\sum_{i\neq k,j}\mathbb{E}\left[x_{i}x_{k}y_{j}^{2}C_{ij}C_{kj}\right]+\sum_{i,k\neq j}\mathbb{E}\left[x_{i}^{2}y_{k}y_{j}C_{ij}C_{ik}\right]+\sum_{i\neq k,j\neq\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[x_{i}x_{k}y_{j}y_{\ell}C_{ij}C_{k\ell}\right].

Observe that all terms except those in the first sum are
0
since
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{y}}
are independent and
\mathbb{E}[x_{i}x_{j}]=0
whenever
i\neq j
(and analogously
\mathbb{E}[y_{i}y_{j}]=0
). Thus, using that
\mathbb{E}[x_{i}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[y_{i}^{2}]=1
for all
i
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{C}}\bm{\mathbf{y}})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\left[x_{i}^{2}y_{j}^{2}C_{ij}^{2}\right]=\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

(23)

Further, again dropping all ‘cross’ terms that include quantities of the form
x_{i}x_{j}
or
y_{i}y_{j}
for
i\neq j
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[(\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{C}}\bm{\mathbf{y}})^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{C}}\bm{\mathbf{y}})^{4}\right]=\sum_{i,k,j,\ell}\mathbb{E}[x_{i}^{2}x_{k}^{2}y_{j}^{2}y_{\ell}^{2}C_{ij}^{2}C_{k\ell}^{2}]=\sum_{i,k,j,\ell}C_{ij}^{2}C_{k\ell}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i,j}C_{ij}^{2}\right)^{2}=\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{4}.

(24)

We can reduce the variance of
\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{C}}\bm{\mathbf{y}}
via averaging. Let
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{m}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
and
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{m}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
be independent vectors with i.i.d. Rademacher entries. Let
S(\bm{\mathbf{C}})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{C}}\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{i})^{2}
.
Then, by (
23
) and (
24
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S(\bm{\mathbf{C}})]=\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\,\text{ and }\,\mathrm{Var}[S(\bm{\mathbf{C}})]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{4}.

If we set
m=\frac{1}{36\epsilon^{2}}
, then we have
\mathrm{Var}[S(\bm{\mathbf{C}})]\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{36}\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{4}
. Thus, by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\displaystyle\Pr[|S(\bm{\mathbf{C}})-\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{3}\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}]\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}[S(\bm{\mathbf{C}})]}{(\epsilon/3)^{2}\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{4}}=\frac{1}{4}.

(25)

Now, let
S_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{C}}),\ldots,S_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{C}})
be independent copies of
S(\bm{\mathbf{C}})
, where
t=c\log(1/\delta)
for a large enough universal constant
c
. By (
25
), in expectation, at least
3t/4
of these estimators satisfy
|S_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{C}})-\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}|<\frac{\epsilon}{3}\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}
. By a standard Chernoff bound, great than
t/2
satisfy this bound with probability at least
1-\delta
. Thus, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

(1-\epsilon/3)\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq\mathrm{median}(S_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{C}}),\ldots,S_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{C}}))\leq(1+\epsilon/3)\|\bm{\mathbf{C}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

Setting
\delta^{\prime}=\delta/|\mathcal{F}|
and considering all
\bm{\mathbf{C}}
of the form
\bm{\mathbf{C}}=\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}
for
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}
, we can apply a union bound to claim that, with probability at least
1-\delta
, simultaneously for all
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have:

(1-\epsilon/3)\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq\mathrm{median}(S_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}),\ldots,S_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}))\leq(1+\epsilon/3)\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

Thus, if we set
\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}=\arg\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{median}(S_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}),\ldots,S_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}))
, we have

\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq\frac{(1+\epsilon/3)}{(1-\epsilon/3)}\cdot\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+\epsilon)\cdot\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2},

with probability at least
1-\delta
, as desired.

Computing
S_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}),\ldots,S_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}})
for all
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}
requires just
m\cdot t=O(\log(1/\delta^{\prime})/\epsilon^{2})=O(\log(|\mathcal{F}|/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
vector-matrix-vector queries with
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
, giving the claimed query complexity.
∎

A.2
Lower Bound for One-Sided Queries

We next show that the bounds of
1
and
5
are optimal in their dependence on
\log|\mathcal{F}|
: in the one-sided matvec query model, and in turn the vector-matrix-vector query model,
\Omega(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
queries are necessary to find even a coarse approximation from any finite matrix family. In particular, we show:

Theorem 5
.

For any
n>0
, any approximation factor
\gamma\geq 1.1
, and any
k\leq c^{\prime}n\log\gamma
for a universal constant
c^{\prime}
, there
is a finite family of matrices
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
\log|\mathcal{F}|=O(k)
and a distribution over
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that, if
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}
is the output of any algorithm that makes fewer than
\frac{c\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{F}|}}{\sqrt{\log\gamma}}
matvec queries to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
(and none to
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}}
) for a universal constant
c>0
,

\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\geq\gamma\cdot\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}},

with probability
\geq 9/10
(over the randomness in
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
and possibly the algorithm).
In particular,
\Omega(\log|\mathcal{F}|)
one-sided matvec queries are necessary to achieve any constant approximation factor
\gamma
with probability
\geq 1/10
.

We remark that, similar to
Theorem

2
, the dependence on
1/\log\gamma
in
Theorem

5
is optimal, since in
3
, we show how to find a
\gamma=O(|\mathcal{F}|)
-approximate solution using just
O(1)=O(\log|\mathcal{F}|/\log(\gamma))
one-sided matvecs.

The idea behind
Theorem

5
is simple. As in the proof of
Theorem

2
, we can focus on the case when
k=c^{\prime}n\log\gamma
. We let
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
be distributed so that its first row is a random Gaussian vector and the rest of its rows are all zero.
\mathcal{F}
will consist of matrices that are also only nonzero on their first row, each corresponding to a vector from a sufficiently fine cover (see
Definition

1
) for a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The radius of the ball is chosen so that it contains the first row of
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
with high probability. The accuracy of the cover will depend inversely on
\gamma
, so
\log|\mathcal{F}|=O(n\log\gamma)
.
To solve
1
, one needs to find a vector in the cover that is close to the first row of
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
. However, each matvec query
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{x}}
only gives a single linear measurement of this row. It can thus be shown that
\Omega(n)=\Omega(\log|\mathcal{F}|/\log\gamma)
such measurements are necessary to find any reasonable approximation to the row.

Our formal proof will use two basic lemmas about Gaussian vectors, stated below. Throughout, we use the notation
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{C}})
to denote a Gaussian distribution with mean
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and covariance
\bm{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. We use
\bm{\mathbf{I}}_{n}
to denote the
n\times n
identity matrix, dropping the subscript when the dimension is clear from context. We use
\bm{\mathbf{0}}
to denote the all zero vector of length
n
.

Lemma 3
.

(Gaussian anti-concentration)

Let
\bm{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be drawn from
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{I}})
. For any fixed
\bm{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any
\epsilon>0
,

\Pr[\|\bm{\mathbf{g}}-\bm{\mathbf{a}}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon]\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{4\pi}\left(\frac{e\epsilon^{2}}{n}\right)^{n}}

Proof.

The probability density function of
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{I}})
is upper bounded by
\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}
. Thus,

\Pr[\|\bm{\mathbf{g}}-\bm{\mathbf{a}}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon]\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\cdot\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{B}(\epsilon,\bm{\mathbf{a}})),

where
\mathcal{B}(\epsilon,\bm{\mathbf{a}})
is an
\ell_{2}
ball of radius
\epsilon
around
\bm{\mathbf{a}}
. We have

\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{B}(\epsilon,\bm{\mathbf{a}}))=\frac{\pi^{n/2}\epsilon^{n}}{\Gamma(n/2+1)}\leq\frac{\pi^{n/2}\epsilon^{n}}{\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{n/2}}\left(\frac{n/2}{e}\right)^{n/2}},

where the inequality follows from Stirling’s approximation to the Gamma function.
Thus, we have:

\displaystyle\Pr[\|\bm{\mathbf{g}}-\bm{\mathbf{a}}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon^{n}}{\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{n}}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n/2}}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{4\pi}\left(\frac{e\epsilon^{2}}{n}\right)^{n}}.\qed

Without loss of generality we can assume that any (possibly adaptive) matvec algorithm only issues queries
\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{m}
that are that are unit norm and orthogonal to each other; see e.g.,
[
Sun et al., 2019
]
. In partilcuar, the query responses for a protocol that issues arbitrary queries
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{m}
can be exactly computed from the responses of a protocol that instead issues queries that from a nested orthonormal basis for
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{m}
– i.e., from a matrix
\bm{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
where, for any
i
, the first
i
columns span
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
. Such a basis could be obtained by orthogonalizing
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
on-the-fly against all prior queries.

With this simplifying assumption in mind, and recalling that our target matrix
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
will be chosen to be non-zero only in its first row, our problem will thus reduce to approximating that row (which is chosen to be a random Gaussian vector) given access to the inner products with a set of orthonormal queries. We make the following claim about the distribution of the row given the outcomes of any adaptively chosen set of orthonormal queries:

Lemma 4
.

Let
\bm{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be drawn from
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{I}})
. For any
m\leq n
, consider any set of adaptively chosen, unit norm and mutually orthogonal query vectors
\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{m}
, where
\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i}
is drawn from a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}
that is a function of
\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i-1},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i-1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}}
.
Let
\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
have
\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{m}
as its columns and let
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{m}=\bm{\mathbf{g}}-\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}}
.
The distribution of
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{m}
conditioned on the outcomes of

\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{m},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}}
is
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}})
.

Proof.

The proof is inductive. For
i\leq m
, define
\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{i}:=[\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i}]
and
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{i}:=(\bm{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}})\bm{\mathbf{g}}
.
Inductively assume that the distribution of
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{i}
conditioned on

\{\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}}\}
is
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}})
.
Since the distribution of
\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i+1}
is a function of
\{\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}}\}
, it follows that
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{i}
is still distributed as
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{i}\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}})
when conditioned on
\{\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i+1}\}
.
By rotational invariance of the Gaussian distribution, we can thus write:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{i}=z\cdot\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i+1}+(\bm{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{i+1}\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{i+1}^{\mathsf{T}})\bm{\mathbf{h}},

where
\bm{\mathbf{h}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{I}})
,
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and
\bm{\mathbf{h}},z
are independent from each other and from
\{\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i+1}\}
.
Observing that
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{i+1}=(\bm{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i+1}\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i+1}^{\mathsf{T}})\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{i}
, we thus that, conditioned on
\{\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i+1}\}
,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{i+1}

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{i+1}\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{i+1}^{\mathsf{T}})\bm{\mathbf{h}}

and

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i+1}^{T}\bm{\mathbf{g}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i+1}^{T}\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{i+1}=z.

Since
\bm{\mathbf{h}}
and
z
are conditionally independent, we conclude that
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{i+1}
is still distributed as
(\bm{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{i+1}\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{i+1}^{\mathsf{T}})\bm{\mathbf{h}}
even conditioned on all
i+1
queries and responses,
\{\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i+1},\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{i+1}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}}\}
, as required.

For the base case,
\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}
is independent of
\bm{\mathbf{g}}
so,
(\bm{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}})\bm{\mathbf{g}}
is distributed as
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\bm{\mathbf{q}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}})
.
∎

Using
Lemmas

3
and
4
, we can now prove
Theorem

5
.

Proof of
Theorem

5
.

First note that it suffices to prove the lower bound for
\log|\mathcal{F}|=O(n\log\gamma)
. To obtain a lower bound for smaller choices of
\mathcal{F}
, we simply embed the hard instance described below in the top left
n\times n
block of a larger matrix, setting all values not in that block to zero, both in
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
and in the matrices in
\mathcal{F}
.

As discussed, we choose
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to have its first row distributed as
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{I}})
and all other rows equal to
\bm{\mathbf{0}}

— i.e.,
\bm{\mathbf{A}}=\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\mathsf{T}}
for
\bm{\mathbf{g}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{I}})
, where
\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{1}
is the first standard basis vector.
For constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
to be set later, let
\alpha=\frac{c_{1}\sqrt{n}}{\gamma}
and let
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
be an
\alpha
-cover (see
Definition

1
) of the ball
\mathcal{B}(\sqrt{c_{2}n})=\{\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|\bm{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{c_{2}n}\}
. Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the set of matrices generated by taking an element of
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
and placing it in the first row, with all other rows set to
0
. I.e.,
\mathcal{F}=\{\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}:\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}\}
. It is well known (c.f.
[
Vershynin, 2018
, Corollary 4.2.13]
) that we can construct
\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}
with
|\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}|=\left(\frac{2\sqrt{c_{2}n}}{\alpha}+1\right)^{n}=\exp(O(n\cdot\log\gamma))
and thus
\log|\mathcal{F}|=\log|\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}|=O(n\cdot\log\gamma)
. Note that here we use the assumption that
\gamma>1.1
to ensure that
\log(2\gamma\sqrt{c_{2}}/c_{1}+1)=O(\log\gamma)
.

Upper bound on the optimal error.

By a standard Chi-squared concentration bound, if
c_{2}
is set large enough, then
\bm{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathcal{B}(\sqrt{c_{2}n})
with probability at least
19/20
.
Assume that this event holds. Then by the definition of an
\alpha
-cover:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{F}}\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\alpha=\frac{c_{1}\sqrt{n}}{\gamma}.

(26)

Lower bound on algorithm’s error.

By
Lemma

4
, the distribution of
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
conditioned on any
m
adaptively chosen matvec queries of the form
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\rightarrow\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{x}}
is
\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{1}\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}
, where
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{m}
is drawn from
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}})
and
\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is an orthonormal basis for the query vectors. That is, conditioned on the outcomes of the queries,
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{m}
is distributed as
\bm{\mathbf{Z}}\bm{\mathbf{h}}
where
\bm{\mathbf{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n-m)}
is an orthonormal basis for which
\bm{\mathbf{Z}}\bm{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathsf{T}}=\bm{\mathbf{I}}-\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\bm{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{h}}
is distributed as
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{I}}_{n-m})
.
For any fixed vector
\bm{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have:

\displaystyle\Pr[\|\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{m}-\bm{\mathbf{a}}\|_{2}\leq c_{1}\sqrt{n}]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[\|\bm{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{m}-\bm{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{a}}\|_{2}\leq c_{1}\sqrt{n}]=\Pr[\|\bm{\mathbf{h}}-\bm{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{a}}\|_{2}\leq c_{1}\sqrt{n}]

where the first inequality uses that
\bm{\mathbf{Z}}
has orthonormal columns and so
\|\bm{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{m}-\bm{\mathbf{Z}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\mathbf{a}}\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{m}-\bm{\mathbf{a}}\|_{2}
.
Invoking
Lemma

3
with
\epsilon=c_{1}\sqrt{n}
and choosing
c
small enough that
m\leq\frac{c\log|\mathcal{F}|}{\log\gamma}\leq\frac{n}{2}
, we have that, for sufficiently small
c_{1}
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\|\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{m}-\bm{\mathbf{a}}\|_{2}\leq c_{1}\sqrt{n}\right]\leq\sqrt{\frac{n-m}{4\pi}\left(\frac{ec_{1}^{2}n}{n-m}\right)^{n-m}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{n-m}{4\pi}\left(2ec_{1}^{2}\right)^{n-m}}\leq\frac{1}{20}.

Since the output
\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}
of any algorithm only depends on the
m
queries and responses, we thus have that
\|\bm{\mathbf{A}}-\bm{\mathbf{\tilde{B}}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}\geq c_{1}\sqrt{n}
with probability
\geq 19/20
