Elucidating the astringency perception mechanism of protocatechuic acid, a key astringent component in black rice, based on polyphenol–salivary protein interaction
Authors: Qian Huang, Zijun Liu, Wangyang Shen, Weiping Jin, Guoyan Yang, Wenjing Huang, Jinling Li, Bin Li, Cheng Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Black rice is rich in bran layer polyphenols, which enhance bioactivity but may reduce its consumer acceptability due to astringency. Polyphenol–salivary protein aggregation is the molecular basis of astringency. In this study, a saliva-induced precipitation approach with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry was used to identify protocatechuic acid (PA) as the key astringent component in black rice, accounting for 68% of total precipitated polyphenols. With α-amylase as a representative, the PA–salivary protein interaction was analyzed using fluorescence spectroscopy, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), and molecular docking. Fluorescence spectroscopy showed a quenching constant (Kq) of 7.8451 × 1011 L⋅mol−1⋅s−1, and ITC revealed a binding constant (K) of 2.65 × 103 L/mol with 1.32 binding sites, indicating the interaction was characterized by strong, spontaneous, noncovalent binding, static quenching, and multiple binding sites. Atomic force microscopy, contact angle, and small amplitude oscillatory shear rheology revealed that PA increased salivary film surface roughness (contact angle from 36.2° to 43.3°) and weakened viscoelasticity, thereby facilitating film rupture and loss of lubrication underlying astringency perception through tactile stimulus pathway. Molecular dynamics simulations further revealed stable PA binding to human astringency receptor human taste 2 receptor member 16 (hTAS2R16), indicating an additional astringency perception gustatory pathway.

Introduction

Black rice is a widely consumed pigmented cereal with a characteristic dark purple to black appearance, which is mainly attributed to the enrichment of polyphenols in the bran layer (Kraithong et al., 2025; Saloni et al., 2025; Thilavech et al., 2025). Black rice and its derived products are commonly known for antioxidant capacity and potential health benefits owing to their high content of bioactive phytochemicals (Das et al., 2023; Gul et al., 2025). However, polyphenols are key determinants of food sensory quality, which can exhibit astringency that may reduce consumer acceptance (Delimont et al., 2017; Soares et al., 2018).

Astringency is commonly referred to as a dry, puckering, and rough complex oral sensation (Huang and Xu, 2021; Zhou et al., 2024). It is not generated by a single component but results from interactions between food constituents and the oral environment, particularly whole saliva (Mu and Chen, 2023; Soares et al., 2018). Polyphenols are representative astringent substances in plant-derived foods, and several complementary mechanisms have been proposed to explain polyphenol astringency perception (Laguna and Sarkar, 2017; Sarkar and Krop, 2019; Silva et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2025). Polyphenols bind salivary proteins and induce aggregation, thereby reducing lubricating proteins, disrupting or rupturing the salivary film, and increasing oral friction. The formed aggregates may also directly contact oral surfaces, further enhancing friction and reducing lubrication. In addition to this physicochemical explanation, astringent compounds can interact with oral epithelial cells and membrane components, thereby contributing to astringent sensation through cellular responses. Moreover, astringency has been linked to trigeminal pathways, in which astringent substances trigger mechanosensory and chemosensory signals (Canon et al., 2018).

Although different viewpoints exist, interactions between polyphenol and salivary proteins are widely considered a central event connecting the food matrix to astringency perception (Manjón et al., 2024; Morzel et al., 2022). Among the salivary proteins most frequently reported to be involved in astringency perception are proline-rich proteins (Ammam et al., 2026), mucins (Torres-Rochera et al., 2023), and α-amylase (Zhou et al., 2024). These proteins differ in structure and function, but all may contribute to the oral drying and roughing sensations associated with astringency through their interactions with phenolic compounds. The phenomenon of polyphenols aggregating and precipitating with salivary proteins occurs through a multi-stage process: first, the formation of compact soluble protein–polyphenol complexes through noncovalent interactions; then, crosslinking into larger, insoluble structures and eventual precipitation as polyphenol concentration increases (Deng et al., 2022; Huynh et al., 2026). The insoluble aggregation formed through the combination of polyphenols and salivary proteins leads to dry and sticky saliva and reduces the lubricity (Lei et al., 2022). The aggregates are also directly exposed to the oral surface, thereby increasing friction (Rudge et al., 2021). Thus, astringency is perceived through the tactile stimulus pathway. Additionally, the aggregation of salivary proteins can rupture the salivary film. Astringent substances can directly stimulate oral epithelial cells and sensory neurons, contributing to astringency perception through the gustatory pathway (Huang and Xu, 2021; Pires et al., 2020).

Although the polyphenolic composition in black rice has been extensively discussed in the context of nutrition, the key contributors to astringency and the molecular mechanisms underlying astringency perception remain unclear. In this study, a saliva-induced precipitation approach combined with liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) was used to identify the predominant polyphenol associated with salivary protein aggregation, and an electronic tongue was used to determine its astringency threshold. Then, the interaction between the key astringent component and representative salivary protein was investigated using fluorescence spectroscopy and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), and the binding mode was examined using molecular docking. Subsequently, fluorescence microscopy, atomic force microscopy (AFM), contact angle analysis, and small amplitude oscillatory shear (SAOS) rheology were used to evaluate the effects of the key astringent component on salivary protein aggregation and salivary film properties. Finally, the interaction potential between the key astringent component and astringency-related receptors was assessed using molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation. The results revealed the dominant astringent component enriched from black rice. This study further provides new insights into its astringency perception mechanism from the tactile stimulus pathway and the gustatory pathway.

Materials and methods

Materials

Black rice was purchased from Heilongjiang Cangtianran Ecological Agriculture Co., Ltd. (Harbin, China). Protocatechuic acid (PA) (≥97%) and α-amylase (9.4 U/mg) were sourced from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Rhodamine B was supplied by Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Saliva collection

All volunteers were nonsmokers, nondrinkers, and free from oral diseases. Because of the strict health criteria, only six volunteers (three men and three women, aged 22–24 years) could be enrolled in this study, leading to a limitation in the volume of saliva samples collected. Fresh, unstimulated saliva was collected as we previously described (Zhou et al., 2025). The saliva samples were collected between 10:00 and 11:00 a.m. Volunteers were instructed to refrain from eating or drinking for at least 2 h prior to collection.

Extraction of black rice polyphenol

Black rice was mechanically crushed and passed through a 60-mesh sieve. The black rice powder (15.0 g) was extracted with 75 mL 100% hexane twice to remove lipid. The lipid-free residue was extracted in a 1:20 mass-to-liquid ratio of a mixed solvent (acetone/water/acetic acid = 70/29.5/0.5 (v/v/v)) for 1 h with shaking at room temperature (Qi et al., 2019). Following centrifugation (5000 g, 4 °C, 20 min), the supernatant was lyophilized to obtain black rice polyphenol powder.

Analysis of polyphenols in the precipitate of black rice polyphenol and saliva mixture

Black rice polyphenol powder was dissolved in PBS solution (10 mmol/L, pH 6.8), and the concentration of the resulting solution was set to 4.0 mg/mL. Then, a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of the black rice polyphenol solution and saliva was prepared. Following a 15 min incubation at 25 °C, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min to obtain the precipitate.

Polyphenols in the precipitate of black rice polyphenol–saliva mixture were analyzed using LC–MS/MS (Hangzhou Yanqu Information Technology Co., Ltd., China). First, 50 mg of the sample was extracted with 0.5 mL in 80% methanol (containing 0.2% VC). Liquid chromatography analysis was performed using an ultra-performance liquid chromatography instrument (Vanquish, Thermo, USA) equipped with a Waters HSS T3 column (50 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm). Mobile phases A was 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution and B was 0.1% formic acid acetonitrile solution. The flow rate was 0.3 mL/min. The column temperature was 40 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The elution gradient was as follows: 0 min A/B (90:10, v/v), 2 min A/B (90:10, v/v), 6 min A/B (40:60, v/v), 9 min A/B (40:60, v/v), 9.1 min A/B (90:10, v/v), and 12 min A/B (90:10, v/v). Here, the time periods with the same elution gradient ratio were used to balance the elution. Mass spectrometry data were acquired using a high-resolution mass spectrometer (Q Exactive, Thermo, USA). The sheath and auxiliary gas flows were 40 arb and 10 arb, respectively; the ion spray voltage was −2800 V; the temperature was 350 °C; and the ion transfer tube temperature was 320 °C. Data were acquired in full scan-ddMS2 mode under negative ionization, with a scan m/z range of 100–900 (Glauser et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2019).

Analysis of proteins in the precipitate of black rice polyphenol and saliva mixture

The black rice polyphenol solutions (1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 mg/mL) were mixed with saliva at a 1:1 vol ratio to prepare the polyphenol–saliva mixed samples. Then, 1.0 mL of the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min to obtain the precipitate. The precipitate was resuspended with 60 μL PBS solution. The molecular weights of proteins in the supernatant and precipitate were determined by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) as we previously described (Zhou et al., 2025). The LC–MS/MS identification were performed as we previously described. (Zhou et al., 2025).

Electronic tongue measurement

The astringency of PA aqueous solutions was measured using a SA402B electronic tongue (Insent Intelligent Sensor Technology Inc., Japan). The test time was 30 s. The concentrations of PA solutions were set to 62.5, 125, 250, 500, and 1000 μg/mL.

Analysis of the interaction between PA and α-amylase

Fluorescence spectroscopy

The binding of PA with α-amylase was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-4600, HITACHI, Japan). The mixture was prepared by mixing 3 mL of α-amylase solution with 0.2 mL of PA solution. The concentration of α-amylase solution was 0.5 mg/mL, and the concentrations of PA solutions were 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mg/mL, respectively. The slit width was set to 5 nm (Li et al., 2022).

ITC

ITC was measured at 25 °C using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC microcalorimeter (MicroCal Inc., USA). Forty μL of PA solution (12 mmol/L) was sequentially injected into a 200 μL titration cell containing α-amylase solution (7.6 μmol/L). The titration was performed using 19 successive injections of 2 μL, and the first 0.5 μL injection was excluded from the analysis. Each injection lasted 60 s, with a 90 s interval between successive injections. The stirring speed was set at 750 rpm. A PA solution (12 mmol/L) titration of PBS solution (10 mmol/L, pH 6.8) was used for the calibration experiment, and the heat released during the calibration was subtracted from the raw data (Zhou et al., 2024).

Molecular docking

The crystal structure of α-amylase (PDB code 1JXJ) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). The two-dimensional SDF structure file of PA was obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Then, molecular docking of the ligand compounds with the protein active sites was performed using Schrödinger software as we previously described (Zhou et al., 2024).

Analysis of the astringency perception mechanism of PA

Determination of turbidity and particle size

The PA–saliva mixed samples were prepared by mixing equal volumes of PA solution and saliva to achieve the desired concentrations. The concentrations of PA in samples were 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mg/mL. Turbidity was measured using a spectrophotometer (UV-1100, MAPADA, China) at 600 nm. Turbidity = 100−T%, where T is transmittance (Liu et al., 2023; Zhan et al., 2018). A Malvern Zetasizer Nano-ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK) was used to determine the particle sizes of the mixed samples (Guo et al., 2025).

AFM

Equal volumes of saliva and PA solution were mixed, resulting in final PA concentrations of 0, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mg/mL in the mixed samples. The ultrasonically dispersed mixed samples were deposited onto a silicon wafer and dried. Atomic force microscope (Dimension® Icon™, Bruker Inc., Germany) was employed in tapping mode to examine the surface morphologies of the samples, using a scan area of 10 μm × 10 μm and a scan frequency of 1 Hz (Zhou et al., 2025).

Three-phase contact angle measurement

Three-phase contact angle was measured at 25 °C using a drop shape analysis system (JY-PHA, Chengde Youte Testing Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Homogeneous films were prepared by depositing the mixed samples on glass slides, which were dried at room temperature. Then, 2 μL of Milli-Q purified water was placed onto the film surface and photographed after 20 s. The Laplace–Young equation was applied to fit the profile data of the droplet. All measurements were triplicated (Guo et al., 2025).

SAOS rheology

SAOS tests were conducted to characterize the rheological properties of the mixed samples using an AR-500 dynamic rheometer (TA Instruments Inc., USA) with a 40 mm plate geometry at 25 °C, applying a frequency of 0.1 Hz and a strain of 2% (Zhou et al., 2024).

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted in Desmond under the OPLS4 force field and SPCE solvation, with the protein–ligand complex immersed in a cubic water box. The system charge was neutralized by adding 0.15 mol/L of Na+ and Cl− ions. Energy minimization (steepest descent, 50,000 steps) and NVT/NPT equilibration (50,000 steps, heavy-atom restraints, 300 K, 1 bar) were performed, followed by a 100 ns unrestrained simulation. Trajectory analysis and animation were conducted using Maestro 14.3 (Xia et al., 2024).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS statistical software V22.0 (IBM, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation of three replicates. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Identification of the key astringent component in black rice

The polyphenol-induced aggregation of salivary proteins was the molecular basis of polyphenol astringency. When the black rice polyphenol solution was mixed with saliva, it accelerated salivary protein aggregation. After the sample was centrifuged, a large amount of purple precipitate was collected (Fig. 1A). The polyphenols in the precipitate, which facilitated the aggregation of salivary proteins, were the key astringent components in black rice. A total of 32 polyphenolic components were detected in the precipitate of black rice polyphenol and saliva mixture (Table S1). The content of PA was the highest (390.7731 ± 14.1858 ng/mg), accounting for 68% of the total polyphenolic components. Determination of the astringency threshold is an iterative process. Based on the astringency thresholds of other polyphenols, a test concentration of 1000 μg/mL was selected initially, then successively halved the concentration until no astringency response was detected. The astringency intensities of a series of PAs at different concentrations were measured using an electronic tongue. The electronic tongue correlates with human sensory evaluation by using sensor arrays and pattern recognition to mimic gustatory perception. A comprehensive review by Cho and Moazzem (2022) concluded that electronic tongue systems can serve as effective rapid assessment tools, often detecting subtle changes that human panels cannot perceive. Wang et al. (2025) demonstrated strong correlations between electronic tongue measurements and human sensory scores for astringent compounds including procyanidin and epigallocatechin gallate. However, the electronic tongue cannot fully replicate complex tactile sensations. In the absence of qualified sensory panels, published correlation models provide reasonable predictions of relative astringency intensity. As shown in Table 1, the astringency intensity gradually decreased as the PA concentration decreased. When the concentration was lower than 125 μg/mL, the electronic tongue was unable to detect astringency intensity. The astringency threshold was taken as the geometric mean of the lowest perceptible concentration and the next hypothetical lower level (Paissoni et al., 2018). Therefore, the astringency threshold of PA calculated in this study was 88.4 μg/mL, which was lower than that of epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG, 183 μg/mL) (Rossetti et al., 2008), indicating that PA had a strong astringency. Notably, the astringency threshold of PA was determined in water, which differs to some extent from the actual oral environment. The easily precipitated proteins in saliva were also identified using SDS–PAGE coupled with a mass spectrometry method. As shown in Fig. 1B, the bands of proteins in saliva were mainly distributed in 55, 26, and 10–17 kDa. As the concentration of black rice polyphenol gradually increased, the band luminosity of proteins in supernatant became progressively lighter, while that of proteins in precipitate became progressively darker. This phenomenon indicates that the concentration of black rice polyphenol is positively correlated with the degree of salivary protein aggregation. Mass spectrometry analysis identified three proteins in the precipitate: α-amylase (55 kDa), lipocalin 1 (26 kDa), and cystatin S (10–17 kDa). The band luminosity of α-amylase was the brightest, indicating that α-amylase was the most abundant and dominant protein in the precipitate. Additionally, α-amylase is one of the major proteins in human saliva and has been repeatedly reported to participate in polyphenol-induced aggregation. Its clear abundance and availability make it suitable for fluorescence spectroscopy, ITC, and molecular docking, allowing the interaction between PA and salivary proteins to be characterized in a reliable and interpretable manner. Therefore, α-amylase was used as an example to investigate the interaction between PA and salivary proteins. Compared with previous studies (Zhou et al., 2024, 2025), it was found that both PA and EGCG can induce the aggregation and precipitation of salivary proteins, including α-amylase, lipocalin 1, and cystatin S, with the precipitation becoming more pronounced as the concentration increases. Among these, α-amylase is the common major target protein of both polyphenols, suggesting that they may contribute to oral astringency through similar protein pathways. Moreover, saliva is known to contain a variety of proteins that can participate in polyphenol aggregation. However, in this study, only three of the most obvious SDS-PAGE bands were selected for analysis, resulting in the missing of other salivary proteins. In the future, it is necessary to expand the identification of salivary proteins involved in PA aggregation to improve the representativeness of the mechanism.

Evaluation of the interaction between PA and α-amylase

Several methods are used for evaluating polyphenol–protein interactions. In this study, fluorescence spectroscopy, ITC, and molecular docking were used to quantify the α-amylase-binding potential of PA. The effect of varying PA concentrations on the fluorescence emission spectrum of α-amylase is illustrated in Fig. 2A. The fluorescence intensity of α-amylase at 350 nm regularly decreased with increasing PA concentration, and no shift of the maximum emission peak was observed. This phenomenon indicated that the binding of PA to α-amylase triggered fluorescence quenching. Similar phenomena of the interaction between α-amylase and procyanidins of grape seed have been reported in previous studies (Ferrer-Gallego et al., 2012). Furthermore, a molecular docking study was performed to determine the binding sites and binding modes of PA with α-amylase. As shown in Fig. 2C, the amino acid residues, such as TYR151, LEU162, and VAL234 in α-amylase, interacted hydrophobically with PA. The ligand also formed two hydrogen bonds with GLU233, one hydrogen bond and a salt bridge with LYS200, and a π–π bond with HIS201.

Effects of PA on salivary film

The study of the interaction between PA and α-amylase revealed a strong affinity between PA and salivary proteins. The addition of PA led to an increasing formation of white turbidity in saliva (Fig. 3A), indicating that PA promoted salivary protein aggregation. The aggregates were further examined using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano-ZS. The aggregation of salivary proteins increased the surface roughness of salivary film. The changes in the surface morphology of salivary film after the addition of PA were examined using AFM. As shown in Fig. 4A, the surface roughness of the salivary film significantly increased with the PA concentration. Additionally, the three-phase contact angle reflected the surface structure of the saliva film. The contact angle of a liquid on a smooth surface is relatively stable, but if the surface has minute roughness, the contact angle may be amplified (Lei et al., 2022; Razavifar et al., 2025). The natural salivary film surface was hydrophilic, and its three-phase contact angle was measured to be 36.2° (Fig. 4B). As the amount of PA added increased, the contact angle continuously increased, indicating that the rough structure of the salivary film surface was more pronounced. The surface roughness of the salivary film increased as high concentrations of PA induced the aggregation of salivary proteins into large aggregates.Fig. 4(A) Atomic force microscopy images of the protocatechuic acid (PA)–saliva mixed samples. Due to their high surface activity, salivary proteins spontaneously adsorb to the air–liquid interface, exhibiting a “solid-like” behavior characterized by the formation of a highly elastic surface film (Rossetti et al., 2008). Due to the ability of PA to cause aggregation of salivary proteins, it will inevitably affect the elasticity of the salivary film. As shown in Fig. 5, the G′ value of saliva was higher than the G″ value throughout the experiment, indicating the formation of the elastic gel network structure of saliva (Proctor et al., 2005; Zhou et al., 2025). However, the G′ and G″ values of saliva decreased with increasing PA concentration (0.5 and 1.0 mg/mL), indicating that the aggregation of salivary proteins disrupted the elastic gel network structure of saliva. Notably, when the concentration of PA was 2.0 mg/mL, the G′ value became lower than the G″ value after 1.75 min. As the concentration of PA increased, excessive aggregation of salivary protein led to precipitation and stratification of saliva. Only when the PA concentration reached 2.0 mg/mL, a crossover point where G′ became lower than G″ appeared. It indicated that the transition from elastic behavior to viscous behavior occurred. The salivary film plays a protective role on the oral surface. When this film is intact, free PA cannot directly contact the astringency receptors. The loss of elasticity caused the salivary film to be easily ruptured, thereby enabling PA direct access to the astringency receptors and resulting in the perception of astringency. Moreover, compared with EGCG, PA and EGCG can both disrupt the elastic gel network structure of the salivary film at high concentrations by inducing excessive aggregation of salivary proteins. However, they exhibit significant differences at low concentrations: PA exerts a disruptive effect at all concentrations tested, whereas EGCG enhances the network structure at low concentrations. Additionally, PA induces a transition from elastic to viscous behavior (G′ < G″) at high concentrations, a phenomenon not observed with EGCG. Molecular docking mainly focuses on the static interaction between ligand molecules and target proteins, while molecular dynamics simulation focuses on the dynamic behavior of molecular systems (Horstmann et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2023). These two methods are usually used in combination. The molecular docking results revealed that the astringency receptor hTAS2R16 exhibited the most stable binding with PA. As a result, further dynamics simulation of the binding of PA to the preferred astringent receptor was performed to confirm and deeply understand the interaction mechanism. A 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation of PA and hTAS2R16 was performed, and their molecular dynamics trajectories were analyzed. Root mean square deviation (RMSD) is used to evaluate simulation stability and result reliability. A larger change in the RMSD value indicates a greater amplitude and instability of the molecular skeleton (Liu et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 6A, the RMSD values of PA and hTAS2R16 were relatively stable after 70 ns, indicating that the PA and hTAS2R16 molecules tended to be stable in the late stage of binding. Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) quantifies residue-level fluctuations in the protein backbone, where peaks correspond to segments with enhanced conformational dynamics (Xia et al., 2024). Conclusions

The interaction between polyphenols and salivary proteins is the main contributor to astringency, arising from changes in oral lubrication and direct receptor-related stimulation following disruption of the salivary film. In this study, PA was identified as the principal astringent polyphenol in black rice by combining saliva-induced precipitation with LC–MS/MS, providing direct evidence that PA is the dominant component associated with salivary protein aggregation. PA showed strong astringency and bound spontaneously to salivary proteins through noncovalent interactions with multiple binding sites, with α-amylase serving as a representative target protein. More importantly, the main novelty of this study is the proposal of a dual-pathway mechanism for black rice astringency centered on PA. PA facilitated salivary protein aggregation, increased the surface roughness of salivary film, and weakened the elastic gel network, thereby increasing the likelihood of salivary film rupture and the consequent loss of lubricity. With increasing concentration, the salivary film ruptured from the excessive aggregation of salivary proteins, and the astringent receptors were exposed. PA also passed through the ruptured salivary film and directly stimulated astringent receptors to produce astringency. Astringency was thus perceived through both the tactile stimulus and gustatory pathways. The molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation studies revealed that PA stably bound to hTAS2R16 astringency receptor protein, supporting a potential gustatory contribution to astringency in addition to the tactile pathway. These findings show that PA induces astringency both indirectly through salivary film destabilization and directly through receptor activation, providing a comprehensive understanding of polyphenol-mediated oral sensation. Furthermore, these findings provide a clearer molecular basis for improving the sensory quality of black rice products and suggest practical strategies for astringency control, such as reducing free PA, limiting its interaction with salivary targets, or enhancing oral lubrication.
